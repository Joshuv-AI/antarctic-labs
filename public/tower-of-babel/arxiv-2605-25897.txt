Nonparametric Estimation via Expected Order Statistics
Tommaso Lando, Lorenzo Tedesco

Abstract
The empirical distribution function assigns mass 1 / n 1/n to each of the n n observations in a sample. As these are highly variable, estimation error may be reduced by replacing them with estimated observations that are asymptotically less variable. Motivated by this idea, we introduce a nonparametric estimator obtained by assigning mass 1 / m 1/m to m m estimated expected order statistics, with m m chosen arbitrarily. The estimator enjoys several finite-sample properties and yields a rich asymptotic theory. Its estimation error relative to its population counterpart is controlled by the L 1 L^{1} error of the empirical distribution. Moreover, every L L -functional of the new estimator corresponds to an L L -functional of the empirical distribution with updated weights. We establish almost sure convergence in L p L^{p} norm and Wasserstein distance as n → ∞ n\to\infty , and derive weak convergence of the associated empirical quantile process in L p ​ ( 0 , 1 ) L^{p}(0,1) , for p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ) p\in[1,\infty) and m m fixed, and for p = 1 , 2 p=1,2 as n , m → ∞ n,m\to\infty . These results yield asymptotic distributions for distance-based functionals, including L p L^{p} and Wasserstein metrics. Bootstrap validity is also established. Simulations show that the estimator often improves on the empirical distribution and remains competitive with kernel methods, with more stable performance across different distributional settings.



1
Introduction

Consider the classical setting in which
X
is a random variable with cumulative distribution function (CDF)
F
, and we observe an
iid
sample of size
n
from
F
, namely
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
. A simple yet central tool in statistics is the empirical CDF (ECDF), defined by

\mathbb{F}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\{X_{i}\leq x\},

where
\mathbbm{1}\{\cdot\}
denotes the indicator function. This estimator enjoys many fundamental properties and is a cornerstone of nonparametric inference.

Denoting by
X_{1:n}\leq...\leq X_{n:n}
the order statistics of the sample, one can equivalently write the ECDF as
\mathbb{F}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbbm{1}}\{X_{i:n}\leq x\}.
However, each order statistic
X_{i:n}
is itself a random variable, with finite expectation
\mu_{i:n}=\mathbb{E}[X_{i:n}]
whenever
\mathbb{E}|X|<\infty
. This suggests a natural question: what happens to the ECDF if we replace each random order statistic
X_{i:n}
by an estimator
\hat{\mu}_{i:n}
of its expectation? In other words, instead of putting mass
1/n
at the observed order statistics
X_{i:n}
, we may consider an averaged version of the ECDF, that puts mass
1/n
at the estimated mean order statistics
\hat{\mu}_{1:n},\dots,\hat{\mu}_{n:n}
.

More generally, given a positive integer
m
, possibly different from the actual sample size
n
, we can construct a CDF that puts mass
1/m
at estimators of the expectations of the order statistics that would occupy the ranks
j=1,\dots,m
in a hypothetical sample of size
m
. This leads to a family of random distribution functions, indexed by
m
, of the form

\mathbb{F}_{n,m}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}{\mathbbm{1}}\{\hat{\mu}_{j:m}\leq x\},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},

where
\hat{\mu}_{j:m}
is a suitable estimator, based on the sample
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, of the expected
j
-th order statistic in a sample of size
m
. We propose
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
as a family of alternative nonparametric estimators of
F
.

To the best of our knowledge,
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
is a new estimator. The only related construction we are aware of is due to
9
, who considered a deterministic version of
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
, namely the family of CDFs that assigns mass
1/m
to the true expected order statistics
\mu_{j:m}
. For this reason, we call this deterministic counterpart the Hoeffding CDF (HCDF), and we call
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
the empirical Hoeffding CDF (EHCDF).
9
proved that the HCDF converges to the baseline CDF
F
, and that any moment of that distribution converges to the corresponding moment of
F
, whenever it exists. The work of
9
gave rise to some related literature, mainly focused on mathematical characterisations via expected order statistics
(
1
;
17
)
, or on extensions of the convergence result, but still in a deterministic framework
(
3
;
4
)
.

The objective of this paper is to introduce and study the EHCDF family of estimators, and to show that it provides a new basis for nonparametric inference. In Section
2
, we develop the mathematical structure underlying the method by introducing the Hoeffding CDF operator and its quantile counterpart, and deriving their main properties. In Section
3
, we define the proposed estimators by applying these operators to the ECDF and establish several finite-sample properties. In particular, the Hoeffding CDF operator is non-expansive in
L^{1}
, so that the distance between the EHCDF and its deterministic counterpart is controlled by the
L^{1}
error of the ECDF; moreover, any
L
-functional of the EHCDF can be expressed as an
L
-functional of the ECDF with modified weights. As a consequence, the EHCDF preserves location, while measures of dispersion and skewness are deterministically reduced according to the choice of
m
. In Section
4
, we establish a.s. consistency in
L^{p}
norm and in
W_{p}
distance, both for fixed
m
and in the joint regime
n,m\to\infty
. In Section
5
, we study the associated quantile process, which provides a convenient route to asymptotic analysis. We establish weak convergence in
L^{p}(0,1)
, for every
p\in[1,\infty)
, when
m
is fixed, and in
L^{1}(0,1)
and
L^{2}(0,1)
, when
n,m\to\infty
, relying on the distributional conditions developed by
2
and
5
, respectively. Bootstrap validity is also established in
L^{p}(0,1)
, for fixed
m
, and in
L^{1}(0,1)
in the joint regime. As a byproduct, asymptotic distributions for functionals of the quantile process, including distance-based functionals, are obtained in Section
6
. Finally, Section
7
contains a numerical study showing that the EHCDF often improves on the ECDF in estimation error, while remaining competitive with kernel-based alternatives and exhibiting a more stable behaviour across different distributional settings.

Overall, the EHCDF combines a simple construction, a tractable asymptotic theory through its quantile representation, and encouraging numerical performance. In contrast to kernel methods, whose asymptotic analysis depends on the specific setting and can be technically involved (see, for example,
12
;
13
for kernel CDF estimators, and
6
;
20
for kernel density estimators), the EHCDF admits a more direct treatment while retaining properties that are sufficiently strong for statistical applications.

2
The Hoeffding operators: definitions and main properties

2.1
Notations

We start with some basic notations. Given some CDF
G
, we define the quantile function as the left-continuous generalized inverse
G^{-1}(p)=\inf\{x:G(x)\geq p\}
. We will denote by
\|\cdot\|_{p}
the
L^{p}
norm, with
p\in[1,\infty]
. Given CDFs
F
and
G
, the Wasserstein distance of order
p\geq 1
between
F
and
G
is defined as
W_{p}(F,G)=\big(\int_{0}^{1}|F^{-1}(t)-G^{-1}(t)|^{p}dt\big)^{1/p}
. The symbols
\to_{a.s.},

\to_{p}
denote convergence almost surely and in probability, respectively. We use
\to_{d}
to denote convergence in distribution for random variables and
\rightsquigarrow
to denote weak convergence in function spaces.

2.2
The Hoeffding CDF operator

We shall be concerned with some random variable
X
with CDF
F
and quantile function
Q=F^{-1}
. If
X
has a density, it will be denoted by
f
. We will assume throughout the paper that
\mathbb{E}|X|<\infty
. We denote with
X_{j:m}
the
j
-th order statistic, that is, the
j
-th smallest value in an
iid
sample of size
m
drawn from
X
. It is well known that the CDF of
X_{j:m}
is
F_{B_{j:m}}\circ F
, where
F_{B_{j:m}}
and
f_{B_{j:m}}
denote the CDF and density function, respectively, of a beta distribution with parameters
j
and
m-j+1
,
j=1,...,m
. Equivalently, an order statistic
X_{j:m}
from
F
has the same law of
F^{-1}(U_{j:m}),
where
U_{j:m}
denotes an order statistic from the uniform distribution on
[0,1].

Given that the CDF of
X_{j:m}
is
F_{B_{j:m}}\circ F
, we can express the expected order statistic
\mathbb{E}X_{j:m}
by the following functional

\mu_{j:m}(F)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}xdF_{B_{j:m}}\circ F(x)=\int_{0}^{1}F^{-1}(p)dF_{B_{j:m}}(p).

From the latter representation it is readily seen that the expected order statistics
\mu_{j:m}(F)
belong to the family of L-functionals
(
18
)
.

Denote by
\mathcal{D}
the family of CDFs with finite mean, and by
\mathcal{D}_{m}
the family of discrete CDFs with
m
jumps. Define the
Hoeffding CDF operator

\mathcal{H}_{m}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{D}_{m}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{m}(G)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}\{\mu_{j:m}(G)\leq\cdot\}=:G_{m},\qquad G\in\mathcal{D}.

(1)

This operator enjoys a fundamental mathematical property that will be used in the sequel. We prove that
\mathcal{H}_{m}
is an
L^{1}
-non-expansive operator, that is, the
L^{1}
distance between a pair of
\mathcal{H}_{m}
-transformations is always bounded by the
L^{1}
distance between the original CDFs. More generally, we establish the following bound, which holds uniformly for all
p\in[1,\infty)
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
F,G\in\mathcal{D}
. Then,

\|\mathcal{H}_{m}(F)-\mathcal{H}_{m}(G)\|_{p}^{p}\leq\|F-G\|_{1}
, for every
p\in[1,\infty)
.

Many results derived in the sequel are based on the quantile-Hoeffding operator, defined in the next subsection.

2.3
The quantile-Hoeffding operator

Given a CDF
G\in\mathcal{D}
, its quantile function
G^{-1}
belongs to
L^{1}(0,1)
.
To study a quantile version of the Hoeffding operator, it is convenient to introduce the following class of step functions:

\mathcal{S}_{m}:=\big\{h:(0,1]\to\mathbb{R}:h(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\,\mathbbm{1}\{u\in(\tfrac{j-1}{m},\tfrac{j}{m}]\},\ a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\big\}.

We define the
quantile-Hoeffding operator
as the map
\mathcal{I}_{m}:L^{1}(0,1)\to\mathcal{S}_{m}\subset L^{1}(0,1),

given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)(u)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\,df_{B_{j:m}}(t)\right)\mathbbm{1}\{u\in\left(\tfrac{j-1}{m},\,\tfrac{j}{m}\right]\},\qquad u\in(0,1].

(2)

That is,
\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)
is the step function which takes the constant value
\int_{0}^{1}h(t)\,df_{B_{j:m}}(t)
on each interval

\left(\frac{j-1}{m},\frac{j}{m}\right].
If
G\in\mathcal{D}
and
G_{m}=\mathcal{H}_{m}(G)
, then

\mathcal{I}_{m}(G^{-1})=(\mathcal{H}_{m}(G))^{-1}=G_{m}^{-1}.
Thus,
\mathcal{I}_{m}
is exactly the quantile counterpart of the CDF operator
\mathcal{H}_{m}
.

The quantile-Hoeffding operator enjoys some important properties that will be used to derive the main convergence results in the following sections. It is immediate that
\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)
is linear in
h
, as this follows directly from the linearity of the integral. Moreover, it is a bounded operator, as shown in the following result, which in particular implies its continuity.

Lemma 1
.

For every fixed
m
, the operator
\mathcal{I}_{m}:L^{p}(0,1)\to L^{p}(0,1)
is linear and bounded. Moreover, for all
h\in L^{p}(0,1),

\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)\|_{p}\leq\|h\|_{p}
, uniformly in
m
.

Lastly, the following theorem plays a key role, as it allows one to transfer convergence in
L^{p}
-norm from an empirical process to its transformed version under
\mathcal{I}_{m}
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
and let
h_{n},h\in L^{p}(0,1)
be such that

\|h_{n}-h\|_{p}\to 0.

Then

\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h_{n})-h\|_{p}\to 0
, as
m,n\to\infty.

3
The estimator: definitions and finite sample properties

In this paper we deal with the classic inference problem, and we assume that
F
is unknown. By the plugin approach, we replace
F
with the ECDF. Applying the Hoeffding operator of order
m
to
\mathbb{F}_{n}
, we obtain a random CDF with
m
jump points (excluding the case of a degenerate sample), represented as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{n})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}(\mu_{j:m}(\mathbb{F}_{n})\leq\cdot)=:\mathbb{F}_{n,m}.

(3)

We refer to
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
as the Empirical Hoeffding CDF (EHCDF) of order
m
.
Basically,
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
puts mass
1/m
at the (random) points
\mu_{j:m}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
, hereafter abbreviated as
\hat{\mu}_{j:m}
, where
j=1,...,m
. In particular, each
\hat{\mu}_{j:m}
is an
L
-estimator, namely, a linear combination of order statistics, given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{j:m}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}xdF_{B_{j:m}}\circ\mathbb{F}_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i:n}(F_{B_{j:m}}(\tfrac{i}{n})-F_{B_{j:m}}(\tfrac{i-1}{n})).

These estimators are consistent, as we will discuss in the sequel.
11
recently developed a class of nonparametric tests based on these estimators. We can also express
\hat{\mu}_{j:m}
as a weighted average, in which order statistics
X_{i:n}
from
F
are scaled by the probability that an order statistic
U_{j:m}
from the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
belongs to
(\tfrac{i-1}{n},\tfrac{i}{n}]
,

\hat{\mu}_{j:m}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i:n}P(U_{j:m}\in(\tfrac{i-1}{n},\tfrac{i}{n}]).

Since
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}=\mathcal{H}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
and
F_{m}=\mathcal{H}_{m}(F)
, the following is an immediate consequence of Theorem
1
.

Corollary 1
.

For every
p\in[1,\infty)
, it holds
\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{p}^{p}\leq\|\mathbb{F}_{n}-F\|_{1}.

To give an initial intuition about the roles of
n
and
m
, we provide a graphical illustration in Figure
1
, for three samples of size
n\in\{10,20,50\}
from a standard normal distribution with
m\in\{10,20,50\}
. We plot the EHCDF against the ECDF and the true CDF. As
m
increases, the EHCDF gets deterministically closer to the ECDF (as samples are fixed), which, in turn, stochastically approaches the true CDF as
n
increases. This behaviour will be addressed more precisely in the sequel.

Figure 1
:
Graphical comparison of
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
(blue),
\mathbb{F}_{n}
(red) and
F
(dotted black).

3.1
Closure properties for
L
-functionals

In this section we investigate the behaviour of
L
-functionals under the Hoeffding CDF operator. In particular, we show that the Hoeffding operator enjoys a closure property on the class of
L
-functionals: composing an
L
-functional with
\mathcal{H}_{m}
yields another
L
-functional with an updated weight, which depends explicitly on the original weight and on
m
.

Let
w
be a weight function and define a general
L
-functional
T_{w}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
with

T_{w}(G):=\int_{0}^{1}G^{-1}(u)\,w(u)\,du,\qquad G\in\mathcal{D},\quad G^{-1}w\in L^{1}(0,1).

Moreover, let
c_{j}(w):=\int_{(j-1)/m}^{j/m}w(t)\,dt
for
j=1,\dots,m,
and define the linear operator
P_{m}:L^{1}(0,1)\to L^{1}(0,1)
by

P_{m}[w](p):=\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{B_{j:m}}(p)c_{j}(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{B_{j:m}}(p)\int_{(j-1)/m}^{j/m}w(t)\,dt,\qquad p\in[0,1].

An important sub-class of
L
-functionals are the so-called
L
-moments

(
10
)
, defined as

\lambda_{r}(G):=\int_{0}^{1}G^{-1}(p)\,w_{r-1}(p)\,dp,\qquad G\in\mathcal{D},

where
r
is a positive integer and
w_{r}(u)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}(-1)^{\,r-k}\binom{r}{k}\binom{r+k}{k}\,u^{k}.
The simplest example of
L
-moment is the mean functional, that is,
\lambda_{1}(G)=\mu(G),
obtained when
w(p)=w_{0}(p)=1.
Other important examples are the
L
-moments of orders
2,3,4
, corresponding to the weighting functions
w_{1}(p)=2p-1,

w_{2}(p)=6p^{2}-6p+1
and
w_{3}(p)=20p^{3}-30p^{2}+12p-1
, respectively.
In particular,
\lambda_{2}
is a variability functional, generally referred to as
L
-scale
(
10
)
. It corresponds to half the
mean absolute difference
(MAD), defined as
\operatorname{MAD}(G)=2\lambda_{2}(G)=\iint|x-y|dG(x)dG(y)=\mathbb{E}|Y_{1}-Y_{2}|
, where
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
are
iid
with CDF
G
. Differently,
\lambda_{3},
and
\lambda_{4}
are related to skewness and kurtosis, respectively. In fact,

\operatorname{Sk}(G)=\frac{\lambda_{3}(G)}{\lambda_{2}(G)}\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{Kurt}(G)=\frac{\lambda_{4}(G)}{\lambda_{2}(G)}

are known as
L
-skewness and
L
-kurtosis
(
10
)
.

The following theorem establishes the closure of the family of
L
-functionals under the Hoeffding CDF operator
\mathcal{H}_{m}
.

Theorem 3
.

For every
G\in\mathcal{D}
,
T_{w}\bigl(\mathcal{H}_{m}(G)\bigr)=T_{P_{m}[w]}(G).

Since
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}=\mathcal{H}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
, the following corollary holds.

Corollary 2
.

The following identity holds,
T_{w}\bigl(\mathbb{F}_{n,m}\bigr)=T_{P_{m}[w]}(\mathbb{F}_{n}).
In particular:

1.

\mu(\mathbb{F}_{n,m})=\mu(\mathbb{F}_{n})
;

2.

\operatorname{MAD}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m})=\tfrac{m-1}{m}\operatorname{MAD}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
;

3.

\operatorname{Sk}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m})=\tfrac{m-2}{m}\operatorname{Sk}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
;

4.

\operatorname{Kurt}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m})=\tfrac{(m-2)(m-3)}{m^{2}}\operatorname{Kurt}(\mathbb{F}_{n})-\tfrac{1}{m^{2}}.

Corollary
2
shows that
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
inherits a number of finite-sample identities from
\mathbb{F}_{n}
. In particular,
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
is mean-preserving. At the same time, measures of dispersion and shape are deterministically shrunk by factors depending only on
m
: the MAD is scaled by
(m-1)/m
, the
L
-skewness by
(m-2)/m
, while the
L
-kurtosis undergoes an affine transformation. Hence, for fixed
n
, smaller values of
m
yield an estimator that is more concentrated (in MAD) and exhibits reduced skewness and tail heaviness (in
L
-moment ratios), whereas these effects vanish as
m
grows.

4
Convergence

We now investigate the asymptotic properties of the estimator. The structure of the section is related to the following simple observation. The error between the EHCDF and the true distribution may be decomposed into the stochastic error between
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
and
F_{m}
and the deterministic error between
F_{m}
and
F
, i.e.,

\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F=(\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m})+(F_{m}-F).

We separate the deterministic convergence
F_{m}\to F
from the stochastic convergence
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}\to F_{m}
, and then combine the two to obtain consistency of
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
.

4.1
Deterministic convergence of
F_{m}
to
F

Recall the definition of the Hoeffding CDF operator in (
1
) and the (deterministic) CDF
\mathcal{H}_{m}(F)=F_{m}
. The following fundamental result holds.

Theorem 4

(
9
)
.

Let
g
be a continuous real-valued function such that
|g|\leq h
where
h
is convex and
\mathbb{E}h(X)<\infty
. Then

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}g(\mu_{j:m})=\mathbb{E}g(X).

As a consequence, taking
g(x)=\cos(tx)
and
\sin(tx)
, the characteristic function of
F_{m}
converges to that of the
F
, and hence

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}F_{m}=F,\quad\text{at every continuity point of $F$. }

(4)

By Polya’s theorem, the convergence is uniform if
F
is continuous. Differently, if
X
is integrable, which is a necessary condition in our setting,
F_{m}
converges not only pointwise to
F
where the latter is continuous, as shown in (
4
), but also in
L^{p}
.

Lemma 2
.

If
\mathbb{E}|X|<\infty
, then
\lim_{m\to\infty}\|F_{m}-F\|_{p}=0.

With regard to the
L^{1}
norm, we can establish the rate of convergence of
F_{m}
. This will be useful later on when addressing weak convergence. The result is obtained under a sufficient condition which will appear again in the next sections.

Lemma 3
.

If
\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{u(1-u)}dQ(u)<\infty
, then
\|F_{m}-F\|_{1}=O(m^{-1/2})
.

We finally note that the results in this subsection can also be applied pathwise to
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
. Fix some outcome
\omega
in the sample space. Then
\mathbb{F}_{n}(\omega)
is a deterministic CDF with bounded support, and

\mathbb{F}_{n,m}(\omega)=\mathcal{H}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{n}(\omega)).

Applying Hoeffding’s theorem to
\mathbb{F}_{n}(\omega)
, we obtain that
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}(\omega)\to\mathbb{F}_{n}(\omega)
at every continuity point of
F_{n}
, that is, for every
x\notin\{X_{1}(\omega),\ldots,X_{n}(\omega)\}
. Since
\mathbb{F}_{n}(\omega)
has bounded support, this convergence also holds in
L^{p}
. We summarize this in the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

For every fixed sample
x_{1},...,x_{n}
, as
m\to\infty
,
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}(x)\to\mathbb{F}_{n}(x)
for any
x\notin\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. Moreover, for every
p\in[1,\infty)
,
\lim_{m\to\infty}\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-\mathbb{F}_{n}\|_{p}=0.

4.2
Stochastic convergence of
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
to
F_{m}

We begin with a basic asymptotic property of the
L
-statistics underlying our construction, namely the strong consistency of the estimators
\hat{\mu}_{j:m}
. If
\mathbb{E}|X|<\infty
, Theorem 2.1 of
22
immediately yields the following result.

Proposition 1
.

If
\mathbb{E}|X|<\infty
, then
\hat{\mu}_{j:m}\to_{a.s.}\mu_{j:m}
for every fixed
m
and
j=1,\dots,m
.

Lemma
4
establishes the deterministic convergence of
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
to
\mathbb{F}_{n}
in the
L^{p}
norm as
m\to\infty
. We now study the stochastic convergence of the EHCDF to the HCDF as
n\to\infty
, with
m
fixed. This yields convergence in
L^{p}
, but in general not in
L^{\infty}
. To see why, note that
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
and
F_{m}
are step functions with the same jump size
1/m
, but with jump locations
\hat{\mu}_{j:m}
and
\mu_{j:m}
, respectively. Although
\hat{\mu}_{j:m}\to_{a.s.}\mu_{j:m}
for every
j
, as long as at least one jump location differs, the two functions exhibit a distance of size
1/m
on a neighborhood of that jump. Hence the supremum norm of the difference does not, in general, converge to zero. On the other hand, for
n
large enough, the jump locations become sufficiently close so that the distance cannot exceed a single jump height, and therefore
\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{\infty}
is eventually a.s. bounded by
1/m
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mathbb{E}|X|<\infty
. For
n\to\infty,

||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}||_{p}\to_{a.s.}0
for every
p\in[1,\infty)
. Moreover, for every
m
, there exists a.s. some
n_{0}
such that, for
n>n_{0}
,
||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}||_{\infty}\leq 1/m
, that is,

P(\omega:\exists n_{0}(\omega)\text{ s.t. }\forall n>n_{0},||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}||_{\infty}\leq 1/m)=1.

4.3
Consistency of
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}

It is now relatively simple to combine the deterministic and stochastic convergence results to obtain the a.s. convergence of
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
to
F
in the
L^{p}
norm. We first obtain convergence as iterated limits. This holds in both ways when considering the
L^{p}
norm, while it holds just for
n\to\infty
and then
n\to\infty
when considering the supremum norm.

Lemma 6
.

If
\mathbb{E}|X|<\infty
, then

1.

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F||_{p}=_{a.s.}0
for every
p\in[1,\infty)
;

2.

\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F||_{p}=_{a.s.}0
, for every
p\in[1,\infty]
.

3.

If
F
is continuous,
\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F||_{\infty}=_{a.s.}0
.

Finally, the convergence of
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
to
F
as a double limit, that is, letting
n,m\to\infty
, follows from Theorem
1
.

Theorem 5
.

If
\mathbb{E}|X|<\infty
, then, for every
p\in[1,\infty)
,
\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F\|_{p}\to_{a.s.}0
as
n,m\to\infty.

We now focus on the Wasserstein distance between the EHCDF on the true distribution. Recall that for
p=1
, the Wasserstein distance coincides with the
L^{1}
distance, that is, given CDFs
F
and
G
,
\|F-G\|_{1}=\mathcal{W}_{1}(F,G).
Therefore all the above results apply to
\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m},F).

With regard to
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m},F_{m})
, establishing convergence is particularly simple because of the construction of
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
and
F_{m}
. Indeed, note that

\mathcal{W}_{p}^{p}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m},F_{m})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\hat{\mu}_{j:m}-\mu_{j:m}|^{p}.

Hence, for fixed
m
,
\hat{\mu}_{j:m}\to_{a.s.}\mu_{j:m}
immediately yields the following result.

Proposition 2
.

Let
m\geq 1
be fixed and let
p\geq 1
. If
\mathbb{E}|X|<\infty
, then
W_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m},F_{m})\to_{a.s.}0
as
n\to\infty.

Note that, for
m\to\infty
, the identity
W_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m},F)=\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{n}^{-1})-F^{-1}\|_{p},
yields the following convergence using Theorem
2
, provided that
F^{-1}\in L^{p}(0,1).

Corollary 3
.

Assume that
\mathbb{E}|X|^{p}<\infty
for some
p\geq 1
. Then

W_{p}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m},F)\to_{a.s.}0
for
n,m\to\infty
.

5
Weak convergence of the Hoeffding empirical quantile process

We start by observing that it is not convenient to study empirical processes based on the proposed estimator directly in the CDF domain. Indeed, consider the process
\sqrt{n}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m})
. For fixed
m
, the step functions
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
and
F_{m}
have the same jump sizes
1/m
, and differ only through the locations of their jumps, namely
\hat{\mu}_{j:m}
and
\mu_{j:m}
. Under suitable conditions,
\hat{\mu}_{j:m}-\mu_{j:m}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
for every fixed
j
. Then,
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}
can take non-zero values only on a union of intervals whose total length vanishes as
n\to\infty
. As a consequence the process
\sqrt{n}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m})
tends to zero a.s., almost everywhere. Centering at
F
instead of
F_{m}
does not solve this issue. If
m
is fixed, the decomposition
\sqrt{n}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F)=\sqrt{n}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m})+\sqrt{n}(F_{m}-F)
shows that one introduces a deterministic error term of order
\sqrt{n}
, since
F_{m}\neq F
in general. If
m\to\infty
, this term may become negligible under suitable conditions, but the behaviour of stochastic term
\sqrt{n}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m})
becomes hard to address. This suggests that the CDF domain is not suitable to describe the asymptotic behaviour of the estimator. By contrast, such a behaviour can be studied more effectively in the quantile domain.

In this section we study the empirical quantile process associated with
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
, denoted as
\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}=\sqrt{n}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m}^{-1}-F^{-1}_{m}).

We will study weak convergence of the above process for
n\to\infty
and
m
fixed, and for
n,m\to\infty
jointly. We will show that weak convergence of
\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}
follows from that of the classic empirical quantile process
\mathbb{Q}_{n}=\sqrt{n}(\mathbb{F}_{n}^{-1}-F^{-1}),
which, under suitable conditions, can be shown to converge to the well known limit
\mathbb{Q}:=\frac{\mathcal{B}}{f\circ F^{-1}},

where
\mathcal{B}
denotes a Brownian bridge.

In addition, our results can be extended to the bootstrap world. Define, the bootstrap ECDF as

\mathbb{F}_{n}^{*}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{n,i}\mathbbm{1}\{X_{i}\leq x\},

where
M_{n}=(M_{1,n},...,M_{n,n})
is independent of the data and drawn
from a multinomial distribution with uniform probabilities over
n
trials
(
21
, p. 180)
. Accordingly, define
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}^{*}=\mathcal{H}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{n}^{*})
. The bootstrap version of
\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}
is defined as
\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}^{*}=\sqrt{n}((\mathbb{F}_{n,m}^{*})^{-1}-\mathbb{F}_{n,m}^{-1}).

5.1
The empirical quantile process

In general, weak convergence of empirical quantile processes is not easy to establish. In classic empirical process theory (see, for instance, 3.9.4.2 in
21
), convergence of
\mathbb{Q}_{n}

is established with respect to the supremum norm using the functional delta method, but this works if
F
has a compact support, or, similarly, in
\ell^{\infty}[p,q]
where
0<p<q<1
. Differently, a key insight for our analysis comes from the recent work of
2
, who established a necessary and sufficient condition for the weak convergence of
\mathbb{Q}_{n}
in the space
L^{1}(0,1)
. We will denote this condition as Property
Q
as in the original paper.

Property Q
.

The quantile function
Q
is locally absolutely continuous and satisfies
\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{u(1-u)}\,dQ(u)<\infty.

According to
2
, if (and only if) Property
Q
holds, we have
\mathbb{Q}_{n}\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Q}
in
L^{1}(0,1).
Local absolute continuity of
Q
means that the restriction of
Q
to each compact subinterval of
(0,1)
is absolutely continuous. Requiring the integral to be finite is slightly more restrictive than requiring
Q
to be square integrable.

In a more general setting, we are interested in the weak convergence of
\mathbb{Q}_{n}
to
\mathbb{Q}
in some
L^{p}(0,1)
space. In this regard,
5
(Theorem 4.6) proved that the quantile process converges in
L^{2}(0,1)
relying on the following assumption, which is stronger than Property
Q
.

Property Q2
.

The quantile function
Q
is locally absolutely continuous with density
q=Q^{\prime}
satisfying
\int_{0}^{1}u(1-u)q^{2}(u)\,du<\infty
. The CDF
F
is twice differentiable on its open support
(a,b)
, with positive derivative
f
, and
\sup_{u\in(0,1)}|u(1-u)\,q(u)^{2}\,f^{\prime}(Q(u))|<\infty
. Moreover, either
a>-\infty
or
\liminf_{u\downarrow 0}|u\,q(u)^{2}\,f^{\prime}(Q(u))|>0
, and either
b<\infty
or
\liminf_{u\uparrow 1}|(1-u)\,q(u)^{2}\,f^{\prime}(Q(u))|>0
.

5.2
Fixed
m

Using
\mathcal{I}_{m}
, we can express
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}^{-1}=\mathcal{I}_{m}(\mathbb{F}_{n}^{-1})
and, by linearity,

\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}=\mathcal{I}_{m}(\mathbb{Q}_{n}).

For fixed
m
, the operator
\mathcal{I}_{m}
is a continuous linear map from
L^{1}(0,1)
into
L^{p}(0,1)
for every
p\in[1,\infty)
; this follows from the definition of
\mathcal{I}_{m}
, together with Lemma
1
. Hence, the following result is an immediate consequence of Property
Q

and the continuous mapping theorem.

Theorem 6
.

Under Property
Q
,
\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Q}_{m}:=\mathcal{I}_{m}(\mathbb{Q})
in
L^{p}(0,1),
for
p\in[1,\infty).

The limit process is centred and Gaussian with representation

\mathbb{Q}_{m}(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}Z_{j}\mathbbm{1}\{u\in\left(\tfrac{j-1}{m},\,\tfrac{j}{m}\right]\}
, where
Z_{j}:=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{Q}dF_{B_{j:m}}
.

It is interesting to observe that the limit process
\mathbb{Q}_{m}(u)
is a step function which takes the random value
Z_{j}
whenever
u\in\left(\tfrac{j-1}{m},\,\tfrac{j}{m}\right]
. In other words, the random function
\mathbb{Q}_{m}
is obtained by assigning the random height
Z_{j}
to the
j
-th interval of the regular partition of
(0,1)
into
m
subintervals. Indeed, a simpler way to express this process is
\mathbb{Q}_{m}(u)=Z_{\lceil mu\rceil}
, for
u\in[0,1].

The following bootstrap version of Theorem
6
follows immediately from the conditional weak convergence

\mathbb{Q}_{n}^{*}\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Q}
in
L^{1}(0,1),

established by
2
, together with the continuity of
\mathcal{I}_{m}
from

L^{1}(0,1)
into
L^{p}(0,1)
for fixed
m
. Since

\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}^{*}=\mathcal{I}_{m}(\mathbb{Q}_{n}^{*}),

bootstrap validity is inherited by the transformed process.

Corollary 4
.

Under Property
Q
, conditionally on the data, in probability,

\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}^{*}\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Q}_{m}

in
L^{p}(0,1),

for every
p\in[1,\infty)
.

5.3
Case
m\to\infty

We now study the weak convergence of
\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}
when
n
and
m
diverge to infinity together. We also consider the alternative version of this process, centred in
F^{-1}
instead of
F_{m}^{-1},
and defined as
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{n,m}=\sqrt{n}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m}^{-1}-F^{-1}).
We will prove that, under suitable conditions, the limit process of both
{\mathbb{Q}}_{n,m}
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{n,m}
is the usual process
\mathbb{Q}.
Therefore, quantile-wise, in the joint regime
n,m\to\infty,
the EHCDF method is asymptotically equivalent to the classic approach based on the ECDF.

Under Property
Q
,
\mathbb{Q}_{n}\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Q}
in
L^{1}(0,1)
. Theorem
2
allows us to extend this convergence to
\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Q}
in
L^{1}(0,1)
as
n,m\to\infty
. Moreover,
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{n,m}=\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}+\sqrt{n}\,(F_{m}^{-1}-F^{-1}).

By Lemma
3
, if
n=o(m)
, the second term vanishes in
L^{1}(0,1)
, and therefore
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{n,m}\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Q}
in
L^{1}(0,1)
. The same argument applies in
L^{p}(0,1)
under suitable assumptions; in particular, the result holds in
L^{2}(0,1)
under Property
Q2
.

Theorem 7
.

Assume that Property
Q
holds. Then

\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Q}
in
L^{1}(0,1),

as
m,n\to\infty
. The same results hold in
L^{2}(0,1)
assuming that Property
Q2
holds. In addition, if Property
Q
holds and
n=o(m)
, then
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{n,m}\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Q}
. More generally, if
\mathbb{Q}_{n}\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Q}
in
L^{p}(0,1)
then we immediately have
\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Q}
in
L^{p}(0,1)
.

Finally, the results of
2
allow us to extend Theorem
7
to the bootstrap version of
\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}^{*}
, and the corresponding bootstrap version of
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{n,m}
, defined as
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{n,m}^{*}=\sqrt{n}((\mathbb{F}_{n,m}^{*})^{-1}-\mathbb{F}_{n}^{-1})
. If
n=o(m)
, Lemma
3
still applies, and the difference between
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{n,m}^{*}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}^{*}
is asymptotically negligible in
L^{1}(0,1)
.

Corollary 5
.

Under Property
Q
, conditionally on the data, in probability,

\mathbb{Q}_{n,m}^{*}\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Q}

in
L^{1}(0,1)
as
n,m\to\infty
.
If
n=o(m)
, the same result holds for
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}_{n,m}^{*}
.

6
Asymptotic distribution of distance-based functionals

6.1
Convergence in distribution of
L^{p}
distances

We start by studying convergence in distribution of
L^{p}
norms of the difference between
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
and
F_{m}
, or between
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
and
F
. Such functionals can be used, for instance, in goodness-of-fit procedures. Recall that, for
p=1
, the two distances coincide, that is, given CDFs
F
and
G
,
\|F-G\|_{1}=\mathcal{W}_{1}(F,G).

We first focus on the case
p=1
. For a fixed value of
m
, it is relatively simple to establish that the limit of
\sqrt{n}\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{1}
is the sum of absolute values of zero-mean Gaussian variables. Moreover, we already know by Theorem
1
that, for the general
L^{p}
norm,
\sqrt{n}\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{p}^{p}\leq\sqrt{n}\|\mathbb{F}_{n}-F\|_{1}=\sqrt{n}\|\mathbb{F}_{n}^{-1}-F^{-1}\|_{1}
, where it is easily seen that the latter converges in distribution to
\int_{0}^{1}|\mathbb{Q}|
. For
p=1
and for
n,m\to\infty,
we show that the limit coincides with the limit of the upper bound. This follows directly from the identity
\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{1}=\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m},F_{m})
combined with Theorem
7
.

Theorem 8
.

Under Property
Q
, as
n\to\infty
with
m
fixed,

\sqrt{n}\,\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{1}\to_{d}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{Q}dF_{B_{j:m}}\Big|.

Moreover, as
n,m\to\infty
,

\sqrt{n}\,\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{1}\to_{d}\int_{0}^{1}|\mathbb{Q}|.

Now, by the reverse triangle inequality,

\Bigl|\sqrt{n}\,\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F\|_{1}-\sqrt{n}\,\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{1}\Bigr|\leq\sqrt{n}\,\|F-F_{m}\|_{1}.

Since
\sqrt{n}\|F-F_{m}\|_{1}=O(n^{1/2}m^{-1/2})
by Lemma
3
, we immediately derive the following result.

Corollary 6
.

Under Property
Q
, if
n,m\to\infty
and
n=o(m)
, then
\sqrt{n}\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F\|_{1}\to_{d}\int_{0}^{1}|\mathbb{Q}|
.

To establish the convergence in distribution of
\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{p},
scaled by a suitable sequence of constants, we distinguish the cases when
m
is fixed to the case when
m\to\infty
. In the first case, for
p\in[1,\infty)
, we establish the convergence of
n^{1/(2p)}\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{p}
to a limit that generalizes the one appearing in the first assertion of Theorem
8
.

Theorem 9
.

Under Property
Q
, for
n\to\infty
, with
m
fixed,

\sqrt{n}\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{p}^{p}\to_{d}\frac{1}{m^{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{Q}dF_{B_{j:m}}\Bigg|.

We proved that
\sqrt{n}\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{p}^{p}=O_{p}(1)
, or, equivalently,
n^{\frac{1}{2p}}\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{p}=O_{p}(1)
. This means that
\sqrt{n}\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{p}=O_{p}(n^{\frac{p-1}{2p}})
, that is, if scaled by
\sqrt{n},
the
L^{p}
norm of the difference diverges to infinity whenever
p>1.

For
n,m\to\infty,
the number of parameters to estimate,
\mu_{j:m}
, diverge, as well as the sample size. The following result establishes convergence under some rather restrictive conditions on
F
.

Proposition 3
.

Assume that
F
has a bounded support and density function
f
such that
0<a<f<b<\infty.
Then, if
m=o(\sqrt{n})
,
m^{p-1}\sqrt{n}\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{p}^{p}\to_{d}\int_{0}^{1}|\mathbb{Q}|
.

A simple particular case of the above result is obtained for the uniform distribution, which directly satisfies
\mu_{j+1:m}-\mu_{j:m}=\tfrac{1}{m+1}
and
\|Q_{n}-Q\|_{\infty}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
. Other distributions which satisfy the assumptions are truncated distributions with well-behaved densities, for example, one can take normal or exponential distributions, restricted on supports of the form
[a,b]
.

Bootstrap analogues can be obtained by considering
\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}^{*}-\mathbb{F}_{n,m}\|_{p}
, suitably rescaled. The fixed-
m
limits corresponding to Theorems
8
and
9
follow from Corollary
4
, conditionally on the data, in probability. For
n,m\to\infty,
the
L^{1}
limit follows from Corollary
5
.

6.2
Convergence in distribution of Wasserstein distances

For a fixed value of
m
, noticing that

n^{p/2}W_{p}^{p}(F_{n,m},F_{m})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big|\sqrt{n}(\hat{\mu}_{j:m}-\mu_{j:m})\big|^{p},

we easily obtain convergence in distribution.

Proposition 4
.

Let
m\geq 1
be fixed and let
p\geq 1
. Under Property
Q
,

n^{p/2}W_{p}^{p}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m},F_{m})\to_{d}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{Q}dF_{B_{j:m}}\right|^{p}.

This result admits a bootstrap analogue. By Corollary
4
,

n^{p/2}W_{p}^{p}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m}^{*},\mathbb{F}_{n,m})

converges conditionally on the data, in probability, to the same limit.

If
m\to\infty
, Theorem
8
already addresses convergence in distribution of
\mathcal{W}_{1}
, given the identity
\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{1}=\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m},F_{m})
. To extend the result to
p>1
we need weak convergence of the quantile process in
L^{p}(0,1)
, which could be obtained in general under stronger assumptions. Under Property
Q2
, we are able to follow the same steps of the proof of Theorem
8
and establish the following result.

Theorem 10
.

Under Property
Q2
, for
n,m\to\infty
,

nW_{2}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{n,m},F_{m})\to_{d}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{Q}(t)^{2}dt.

At present, we are not aware of any bootstrap convergence result of the quantile process in
L^{2}(0,1)
, hence, the above result does not have a bootstrap analogue.

7
Simulations

We compare the performance of the EHCDF with two classic alternatives: the ECDF and the kernel estimator. For the construction of the EHCDF, we set
m=n^{\gamma}
, with
\gamma\in[1,1.5]
. Recall that the EHCDF is an averaged version of the ECDF where the parameter
m
controls this effect. For larger values of
\gamma
the two estimators get closer.

Kernel smoothing provides a natural benchmark in our context. However, there is no single kernel-based specification that is uniformly optimal across distributional settings, since performance depends on the choice of bandwidth and on how support restrictions and boundary effects are handled. The literature addresses these issues through bandwidth selection, transformations, and boundary-based kernel constructions
(
19
;
7
;
14
;
15
)
. For this reason, we use the
R
package
kde1d

(
16
)
, which computes kernel estimators for unconstrained as well as support-constrained data. In the unconstrained case (when the support is
\mathbb{R}
), this yields a Gaussian-kernel estimator with the plug-in bandwidth selector of
19
, which we denote by EKCDF. For constrained supports, the package automatically applies a log-transformation in the presence of a single boundary
(
7
)
or a probit transformation in the presence of two boundaries
(
8
)
, and we refer to the resulting modified estimator as EKCDFM.

We consider the following distributions: uniform
\mathcal{U}[a,b]
, normal
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, log-normal
\mathrm{Log}\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, Weibull
\mathcal{W}(a,\sigma)
, gamma
\Gamma(a,\sigma)
, Gompertz
\mathcal{G}(a,\sigma)
, beta
\mathcal{B}(a,b)
, Student’s
t_{\nu}
, and binomial
\mathrm{Bin}(k,\pi)
. We also consider equally weighted mixtures of two distributions
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, denoted by
\mathcal{M}(D_{1},D_{2})
. The parametrisations are standard, except for the Gompertz distribution, whose CDF is
1-\exp\{a(1-e^{\sigma x})\}.

We generate Monte Carlo samples of size
n\in\{25,50,100\}
using 1000 simulation runs. To assess global accuracy of a general estimator
\hat{F}
, we consider the
L^{p}
-errors
\|\hat{F}-F\|_{p}
for
p=1,2,\infty
, and report their simulated average values relative to the ECDF, namely

L^{p}_{\%}(\hat{F})=\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\hat{F}-F\|_{p}}{\mathbb{E}\|\mathbb{F}_{n}-F\|_{p}}\times 100\%.

The corresponding results are reported in the Supplementary Material, namely in Table
S1
, Table
S2
, Table
S3
, and Table
S4
, for distributions supported on
\mathbb{R}
, on
[0,\infty)
, on compact intervals, and for mixtures, respectively. Since such global criteria do not reveal where the estimation error is located, we also consider the standardised pointwise mean squared error (MSE ratio) at the quantiles
F^{-1}(p)
,

\frac{\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\hat{F}(F^{-1}(p))-p)^{2}\bigr]}{\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\mathbb{F}_{n}(F^{-1}(p))-p)^{2}\bigr]}.

The complete set of MSE-ratio plots is reported in the Supplementary Material, while Figures
2
–
3
display representative cases discussed below.

Code reproducing the simulation study is available at
github.com/tedescolor/EHCDF
.

7.1
L^{p}
-norm results

We start with distributions supported on
\mathbb{R}
(Table
S1
). For all the distributions where the classic EKCDF performs well, namely those for which regular smoothing improves estimation, the EHCDF also improves on the ECDF by 5–15%, especially for lower values of
\gamma
. The EKCDF outperforms the EHCDF when the kernel specification is suited to the underlying model, as in the Gaussian case. The improvement in terms of sup-norm is more pronounced. This is a consequence of the continuity of the EKCDF, while the EHCDF is still a step function. When the tails become heavier, however, the ranking may reverse (see for instance the
t_{2}
distribution for
n\geq 50
).

The results for distributions supported on
[0,\infty)
are illustrated in Table
S2
. Also in this case, the EHCDF typically improves on the ECDF. The performances of EKCDF and EKCDFM can be quite different. The EHCDF often lies between the two kernel variants in terms of error, or even outperform both of them, at least with respect to some metric (see for example the
\Gamma(0.5,1)
case). The EKCDFM does not necessarily improve on the EKCDF, and its performance depends on how suitable the support transformation is for the underlying distribution. As discussed by
23
, transformations are helpful only insofar as they make a global-bandwidth kernel estimator more appropriate for the transformed density. In particular, if the transformation does not sufficiently regularise the density, especially near the boundary, the transformed estimator may perform worse. This is illustrated, for example, by the Weibull family. When the shape parameter is smaller than
1
, the density diverges at zero, so the EKCDFM tends to outperform the EKCDF. When the shape parameter exceeds
1
, the density is less irregular and becomes closer to the class of densities for which a global-bandwidth estimator is appropriate. In such cases, the classic EKCDF provides better results. Overall, the results suggest that the EHCDF may be a reliable alternative to kernel methods, as it is less sensitive to such support issues, improving over the ECDF across all metrics and all values of
m
considered.

We now consider distributions supported on compact intervals, see Table
S3
. The results confirm that the EHCDF improves on the ECDF, while EKCDF and EKCDFM can behave differently (for example in the
\mathcal{B}(0.25,1)
or
\mathcal{B}(4,1)
cases). The EHCDF often outperforms one of the two alternative methods.

To discover the limitations of the EKCDF and the EHCDF, in Table
S4
we analyse ad-hoc mixtures, such as mixtures of two well-separated normal and uniform distributions. In such cases, both the EHCDF and kernel methods may assign mass to regions where the true distribution has little or no mass, which typically leads to a larger estimation error compared to the ECDF. In such cases, the EHCDF can outperform the EKCDF, especially for larger values of
m
(see for instance the
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{N}(-5,1),\mathcal{N}(5,1))
and
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{N}(-5,1),\mathcal{W}(0.5,1))
cases).

7.2
MSE results

The pointwise MSE plots complement the global
L^{p}
criteria by showing where the estimation error is concentrated.
The results in the Supplementary Material and in Figures
2
and
3
show that the improvement of the EHCDF over the ECDF is mainly located in the body of the distribution, while estimation is generally less precise in the tails, especially when these are heavier or when probability mass concentrates near the boundaries. This is quite natural: the EHCDF replaces extreme observations with less extreme averages, making the estimator less sensitive to heavy tails and producing error when many observations lie close to the boundary. For example, with regard to tail heaviness, tail estimation for the
t_{\nu}
and
\mathcal{W}(a,1)
distributions improves as
\nu
and
a
increase, respectively. Differently, when the density diverges at the boundary, estimation deteriorates in that region; see, for instance, the left-tail estimation for
\mathcal{B}(a,1)
or
\mathcal{W}(a,1)
when
a
approaches 0.
With regard to kernel estimators, we note that they can yield a uniformly smaller error in some cases (for example, for the normal distribution), but also a substantially larger error in some regions in other cases. The difference between EKCDF and EKCDFM is particularly noticeable in the Weibull case, where one method performs well and the other performs poorly depending on the shape parameter (see also the gamma case). Since it is not known
a priori
which version should be chosen, this may be viewed as a limitation in practice. By contrast, the performance of the EHCDF is more stable and appears to be less sensitive to the underlying distribution.

Appendix A
Proofs

We start by recalling the following property of the beta density, which will be often used in the proofs.

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{B_{j:m}}(u)=1,\qquad\forall u\in[0,1].

(5)

In other words, the family
\{m^{-1}f_{B_{j:m}}\}_{j=1}^{m}
forms a partition of unity.

A.1
Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{m}(F)=F_{m}
and
\mathcal{H}_{m}(G)=G_{m}
. For every
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

F_{m}(x)-G_{m}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Big(\mathbbm{1}\{\mu_{j:m}(F)\leq x\}-\mathbbm{1}\{\mu_{j:m}(G)\leq x\}\Big).

By convexity of
t\mapsto|t|^{p}
, Jensen’s inequality implies that

\displaystyle\|F_{m}-G_{m}\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Big(\mathbbm{1}\{\mu_{j:m}(F)\leq x\}-\mathbbm{1}\{\mu_{j:m}(G)\leq x\}\Big)\right|^{p}dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbbm{1}\{\mu_{j:m}(F)\leq x\}-\mathbbm{1}\{\mu_{j:m}(G)\leq x\}\right|^{p}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbbm{1}\{\mu_{j:m}(F)\leq x\}-\mathbbm{1}\{\mu_{j:m}(G)\leq x\}\right|dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\mu_{j:m}(F)-\mu_{j:m}(G)|.

Now,

|\mu_{j:m}(F)-\mu_{j:m}(G)|=\left|\int_{0}^{1}(F^{-1}-G^{-1})\,dF_{B_{j:m}}\right|\leq\int_{0}^{1}|F^{-1}-G^{-1}|\,dF_{B_{j:m}}.

Therefore, using (
5
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\mu_{j:m}(F)-\mu_{j:m}(G)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{0}^{1}|F^{-1}(u)-G^{-1}(u)|\,dF_{B_{j:m}}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}|F^{-1}(u)-G^{-1}(u)|\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{B_{j:m}}(u)\right)du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}|F^{-1}(u)-G^{-1}(u)|\,du

\displaystyle=\|F-G\|_{1}.

This proves the claim.

∎

A.2
Proof of Lemma
1

Proof.

Linearity of
\mathcal{I}_{m}
is immediate from its definition. For the norm bound, we compute

\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)\|_{p}^{p}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|\int_{0}^{1}h(s)\,dF_{B_{j:m}}(s)\right|^{p}.

Jensen’s inequality gives

\left|\int_{0}^{1}h(s)\,dF_{B_{j:m}}(s)\right|^{p}\leq\int_{0}^{1}|h(s)|^{p}\,dF_{B_{j:m}}(s).

Hence,

\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)\|_{p}^{p}\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{0}^{1}|h(s)|^{p}\,f_{B_{j:m}}(s)\,ds=\int_{0}^{1}|h(s)|^{p}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{B_{j:m}}(s)\right)ds.

Using again equation (
5
), i.e.,
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{B_{j:m}}(s)=1,
for every
s\in(0,1),
and taking the
p
-th root,
we conclude

\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)\|_{p}\leq\bigg(\int_{0}^{1}|h(s)|^{p}\,ds\bigg)^{1/p}=\|h\|_{p}.

∎

A.3
Proof of Theorem
2

Proof.

By the triangle inequality,

\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h_{n})-h\|_{p}\leq\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h_{n})-\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)\|_{p}+\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)-h\|_{p}.

For the first term, we apply Lemma
1
and linearity of
\mathcal{I}_{m}
to obtain:

\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h_{n})-\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)\|_{p}=\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h_{n}-h)\|_{p}\leq\|h_{n}-h\|_{p}\to 0\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

We now prove that
\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)-h\|_{p}\to 0
as
m\to\infty.
Write

\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)-h\|_{p}^{p}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{\frac{j-1}{m}}^{\frac{j}{m}}\left|\int_{0}^{1}h(s)\,f_{B_{j:m}}(s)\,ds-h(u)\right|^{p}du.

Since
\int_{0}^{1}f_{B_{j:m}}(s)\,ds=1
, we may write

h(u)=h(u)\int_{0}^{1}f_{B_{j:m}}(s)\,ds,

and therefore

\int_{0}^{1}h(s)\,f_{B_{j:m}}(s)\,ds-h(u)=\int_{0}^{1}(h(s)-h(u))\,f_{B_{j:m}}(s)\,ds.

By Jensen’s inequality,

\left|\int_{0}^{1}(h(s)-h(u))\,f_{B_{j:m}}(s)\,ds\right|^{p}\leq\int_{0}^{1}|h(s)-h(u)|^{p}\,f_{B_{j:m}}(s)\,ds.

Hence

\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)-h\|_{p}^{p}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{\frac{j-1}{m}}^{\frac{j}{m}}\int_{0}^{1}|h(s)-h(u)|^{p}\,f_{B_{j:m}}(s)\,ds\,du.

Now define

K_{m}(s,u):=\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{B_{j:m}}(s)\,\mathbbm{1}\{u\in\left(\tfrac{j-1}{m},\tfrac{j}{m}\right]\},

the integral kernel associated with the operator
\mathcal{I}_{m}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)(u)=\int_{0}^{1}h(s)\,K_{m}(s,u)\,ds.

Then

\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)-h\|_{p}^{p}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|h(s)-h(u)|^{p}\,K_{m}(s,u)\,ds\,du.

The kernel
K_{m}
satisfies the two marginal identities

\int_{0}^{1}K_{m}(s,u)\,du=1\qquad\forall s\in(0,1),

and

\int_{0}^{1}K_{m}(s,u)\,ds=1\qquad\forall u\in(0,1).

Indeed, for fixed
s
,

\int_{0}^{1}K_{m}(s,u)\,du=\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{B_{j:m}}(s)\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}\{u\in\left(\tfrac{j-1}{m},\tfrac{j}{m}\right]\}\,du=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{B_{j:m}}(s)=1,

while for fixed
u
there is exactly one
j
such that

u\in\left(\frac{j-1}{m},\frac{j}{m}\right],

so that

K_{m}(s,u)=f_{B_{j:m}}(s),

and therefore

\int_{0}^{1}K_{m}(s,u)\,ds=\int_{0}^{1}f_{B_{j:m}}(s)\,ds=1.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Since
C[0,1]
is dense in
L^{p}(0,1)
, there exists a bounded continuous function
h_{\varepsilon}
such that

\|h-h_{\varepsilon}\|_{p}\leq\varepsilon.

Write

h(s)-h(u)=\bigl(h(s)-h_{\varepsilon}(s)\bigr)+\bigl(h_{\varepsilon}(s)-h_{\varepsilon}(u)\bigr)+\bigl(h_{\varepsilon}(u)-h(u)\bigr).

Using Jensen’s inequality again, that is,
|\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}|^{p}\leq k^{p-1}(|\sum_{j=1}^{k}|a_{j}|^{p})
, for
k=3

we obtain

|h(s)-h(u)|^{p}\leq 3^{p-1}\Bigl(|h(s)-h_{\varepsilon}(s)|^{p}+|h_{\varepsilon}(s)-h_{\varepsilon}(u)|^{p}+|h_{\varepsilon}(u)-h(u)|^{p}\Bigr).

Integrating against
K_{m}(s,u)\,ds\,du
yields

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|h(s)-h(u)|^{p}\,K_{m}(s,u)\,ds\,du\leq 3^{p-1}(A_{m}+B_{m}+C_{m}),

where

A_{m}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|h(s)-h_{\varepsilon}(s)|^{p}\,K_{m}(s,u)\,ds\,du,

B_{m}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|h_{\varepsilon}(s)-h_{\varepsilon}(u)|^{p}\,K_{m}(s,u)\,ds\,du,

C_{m}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|h_{\varepsilon}(u)-h(u)|^{p}\,K_{m}(s,u)\,ds\,du.

We now use the fact that
K_{m}
has both marginals equal to
1
to show that the error arising from approximating
h
by
h_{\varepsilon}
is controlled by
\|h-h_{\varepsilon}\|_{p}^{p}
. In fact, since the integrand in
A_{m}
depends only on
s
,

A_{m}=\int_{0}^{1}|h(s)-h_{\varepsilon}(s)|^{p}\left(\int_{0}^{1}K_{m}(s,u)\,du\right)ds=\int_{0}^{1}|h(s)-h_{\varepsilon}(s)|^{p}\,ds=\|h-h_{\varepsilon}\|_{p}^{p}.

Similarly,

C_{m}=\|h-h_{\varepsilon}\|_{p}^{p}.

Hence,

A_{m}+C_{m}=2\|h-h_{\varepsilon}\|_{p}^{p}\leq 2\varepsilon^{p}.

It remains to deal with
B_{m}
. Since
h_{\varepsilon}
is (uniformly) continuous on
[0,1]
, for every
\delta>0
, there exists
\eta>0
such that

|s-u|\leq\eta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|h_{\varepsilon}(s)-h_{\varepsilon}(u)|\leq\delta.

Therefore

B_{m}\leq\delta^{p}+(2\|h_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty})^{p}R_{m}(\eta),

where

R_{m}(\eta):=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}\{|s-u|>\eta\}\,K_{m}(s,u)\,ds\,du.

We now show that
\lim_{m\to\infty}R_{m}(\eta)=0
. For
u\in\left(\frac{j-1}{m},\frac{j}{m}\right]
, one has

K_{m}(s,u)=f_{B_{j:m}}(s).

Recall that
f_{B_{j:m}}
is the density of
U_{j:m}
, where

\mathbb{E}[U_{j:m}]=\frac{j}{m+1}.

Moreover, if

u\in\left(\frac{j-1}{m},\frac{j}{m}\right],

then

\left|u-\frac{j}{m+1}\right|\leq\frac{1}{m}.

Hence, if
|s-u|>\eta
, the reverse triangle inequality yields

\left|s-\frac{j}{m+1}\right|\geq|s-u|-\left|u-\frac{j}{m+1}\right|>\eta-\frac{1}{m}.

Therefore, for
m
large enough so that
\frac{1}{m}<\eta/2
,

|s-u|>\eta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\left|s-\frac{j}{m+1}\right|>\frac{\eta}{2}.

The above implication, in addition to Chebyshev’s inequality, gives

\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}\{|s-u|>\eta\}\,f_{B_{j:m}}(s)\,ds\leq P\left(\left|U_{j:m}-\frac{j}{m+1}\right|>\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}(U_{j:m})}{(\eta/2)^{2}}.

Since

\mathrm{Var}(U_{j:m})=\frac{j(m-j+1)}{(m+1)^{2}(m+2)}\leq\frac{1}{4(m+2)},

we obtain

P\left(\left|U_{j:m}-\frac{j}{m+1}\right|>\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\eta^{2}(m+2)}.

This bound is uniform in
j
. Therefore, for
m\to\infty,

R_{m}(\eta)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{\frac{j-1}{m}}^{\frac{j}{m}}\frac{1}{\eta^{2}(m+2)}\,du=\frac{1}{\eta^{2}(m+2)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{m}=\frac{1}{\eta^{2}(m+2)}\to 0.

Hence

\limsup_{m\to\infty}B_{m}\leq\delta^{p}.

Since
\delta>0
is arbitrary, it follows that
B_{m}\to 0
. Thus

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)-h\|_{p}^{p}\leq 3^{p-1}\cdot 2\varepsilon^{p}.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, we conclude that

\|\mathcal{I}_{m}(h)-h\|_{p}\to 0.

Combining this with the first term proves the theorem.
∎

Proof.

The quantile function
G_{m}^{-1}
is the step function

G_{m}^{-1}(u)=\mu_{j:m}(G)\quad\text{for }u\in\Bigl(\frac{j-1}{m},\frac{j}{m}\Bigr],\qquad j=1,\dots,m.

Therefore,

A.5
Proof of Lemma
3

Proof.

Recall that uniform order statistics
U_{j:m}
have CDF
F_{B_{j:m}}
.
Using
\|F_{m}-F\|_{1}=\|Q_{m}-Q\|_{1}
, where
Q_{m}=\mathcal{I}_{m}(Q)
, we want to show that

\|Q_{m}-Q\|_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{(j-1)/m}^{j/m}\bigl|\mathbb{E}[Q(U_{j:m})]-Q(x)\bigr|\,dx=O\left(m^{-1/2}\right)\quad\text{as }m\to\infty.

We start from the identity

\mathbb{E}[Q(U_{j:m})]=\int_{0}^{1}Q(u)dF_{B_{j:m}}(u).

Thus, for any
x\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{E}[Q(U_{j:m})]-Q(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(Q(u)-Q(x)\bigr)\,dF_{B_{j:m}}(u).

Note that

|Q(u)-Q(x)|=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}\{\min\{u,x\}<t\leq\max\{u,x\}\}dQ(t).

Substituting this into the previous integral and applying Fubini’s theorem yields

\bigl|\mathbb{E}[Q(U_{j:m})]-Q(x)\bigr|\leq\mathbb{E}|Q(U_{j:m})-Q(x)|=\int_{0}^{1}H_{j,m}(t;x)\,dQ(t),

where

H_{j,m}(t;x):=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}\{\min\{u,x\}<t\leq\max\{u,x\}\}\,dF_{B_{j:m}}(u)\,=P\bigl((U_{j:m}-t)(x-t)\leq 0\bigr).

Intuitively,
H_{j,m}(t;x)
measures the probability that
U_{j:m}

lies on the opposite side of
t
than
x
does.
Integrating the previous inequality over
x\in[(j-1)/m,j/m]
and summing over
j
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{\tfrac{j-1}{m}}^{\tfrac{j}{m}}\!|\mathbb{E}[Q(U_{j:m})]-Q(x)|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}W_{m}(t)dQ(t)

where

W_{m}(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{\tfrac{j-1}{m}}^{\tfrac{j}{m}}H_{j,m}(t;x)\,dx=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{\tfrac{j-1}{m}}^{\tfrac{j}{m}}P\bigl((U_{j:m}-t)(x-t)\leq 0\bigr)\,dx.

Hence, the problem reduces to finding a bound for
W_{m}(t)
.

Write
r=\lfloor mt\rfloor
and
\lambda=mt-r\in[0,1)
, so that

t=\frac{r+\lambda}{m}\in\left[\frac{r}{m},\frac{r+1}{m}\right).

Hence, the intervals
\bigl((j-1)/m,j/m\bigr]
split into three groups: those entirely to the left of
t
, for which the event
(U_{j:m}-t)(x-t)\leq 0
reduces to
U_{j:m}\geq t
; the unique interval containing
t
; and those entirely to the right of
t
, for which
(U_{j:m}-t)(x-t)\leq 0
reduces to
U_{j:m}\leq t
. The middle interval must then be split at
t
, according to whether
x\leq t
or
x\geq t
, which yields

\displaystyle W_{m}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{r}P(U_{j:m}\geq t)+\frac{\lambda}{m}P(U_{r+1:m}\geq t)

\displaystyle+\frac{1-\lambda}{m}P(U_{r+1:m}\leq t)+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=r+2}^{m}P(U_{j:m}\leq t).

Recall that

P(U_{j:m}\leq t)=\sum_{k=j}^{m}\binom{m}{k}t^{k}(1-t)^{m-k},

so, given a binomial random variable
B
with
m
trials and success probability
t
,

P(U_{j:m}\leq t)=P(B\geq j),\qquad P(U_{j:m}>t)=P(B\leq j-1).

Using this binomial representation

\displaystyle W_{m}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{r}P(B\leq j-1)+\frac{\lambda}{m}P(B\leq r)

\displaystyle+\frac{1-\lambda}{m}P(B\geq r+1)+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=r+2}^{m}P(B\geq j).

We now claim that

W_{m}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\,\mathbb{E}|B-mt|=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{b=0}^{m}|b-mt|P(B=b).

Indeed, we may write

W_{m}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{b=0}^{m}c_{b}\,P(B=b),

where

c_{b}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathbbm{1}\{b\leq j-1\}+\lambda\mathbbm{1}\{b\leq r\}+(1-\lambda)\mathbbm{1}\{b\geq r+1\}+\sum_{j=r+2}^{m}\mathbbm{1}\{b\geq j\}.

It is readily seen that

c_{b}=r-b+\lambda
if
b\leq r
, and
c_{b}=b-r-\lambda
for
b\geq r+1,

hence
c_{b}=|b-(r+\lambda)|=|b-mt|
.

Now, by Jensen’s inequality,

W_{m}(t)\leq\frac{1}{m}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(B-mt)^{2}}=\frac{1}{m}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(B)}=\sqrt{\frac{t(1-t)}{m}}.

Thus

W_{m}(t)=O\!\left(m^{-1/2}\sqrt{t(1-t)}\right).

Recalling the assumption

\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{u(1-u)}\,dQ(u)<\infty,

we conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{(j-1)/m}^{j/m}\bigl|\mathbb{E}[Q(U_{j:m})]-Q(x)\bigr|\,dx\leq\int_{0}^{1}W_{m}(t)\,dQ(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{t(1-t)}\,dQ(t)=O(m^{-1/2}).

∎

Proof.

be fixed. By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}||_{p}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathbbm{1}\{\mu_{j:m}\leq x\}-\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{j:m}\leq x\}|^{p}dx

therefore
||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}||_{p}\to_{a.s.}0,
for

.

About the second assertion, consider

||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}||_{\infty}=\sup_{x}|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\mathbbm{1}\{\mu_{j:m}\leq x\}-\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{j:m}\leq x\})|.

E_{n}=\{\omega:||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}(x,\omega)-F_{m}(x)||_{\infty}>1/m\}
. This event occurs if, for some
x_{0}
,
\sum_{j}|\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{j:m}\leq x_{0}\}-\mathbbm{1}\{{\mu}_{j:m}\leq x_{0}\}|\geq 2,
or equivalently, if we have (at least) two consecutive jump points of
F_{m}
, say
\mu_{k:m}
and
\mu_{k+1:m}

\hat{\mu}_{k:m}

\mu_{k+1:m}<\hat{\mu}_{k:m}
, or vice-versa, if
\hat{\mu}_{k+1:m}<{\mu}_{k:m}
. Indeed, if
\mu_{k+1:m}<\hat{\mu}_{k:m}
or
\hat{\mu}_{k+1:m}<{\mu}_{k:m}
, we have
F_{m}(\mu_{k+1:m})-\mathbb{F}_{n,m}(\mu_{k+1:m})\geq 2/m
. The two cases are similar so we can focus on the first one without loss of generality. We can re-write
E_{n}
as

E_{n}=\{\exists k:\hat{\mu}_{k:m}(\omega)>\mu_{k+1:m}\}=\{\exists k:\hat{\mu}_{k:m}(\omega)-\mu_{k+1:m}>0\}

By construction,

For almost every

there exists

is not verified for almost every
\omega

A.7
Proof of Lemma
6

Proof.

To prove the first assertion, Lemma
4
implies that
\lim_{m\to\infty}||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}(\omega)-\mathbb{F}_{n}(\omega)||_{p}=0
for every outcome
\omega
in the sample space. Now, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}(\omega)-F||_{p}\leq

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\big(||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}(\omega)-\mathbb{F}_{n}(\omega)||_{p}+||\mathbb{F}_{n}(\omega)-F||_{p}\big)=||\mathbb{F}_{n}(\omega)-F||_{p}.

Since the above inequality holds for every outcome
\omega,
we can write
\lim_{m\to\infty}||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F||_{p}\leq||\mathbb{F}_{n}-F||_{p}.
The latter random variable tends to 0 with probability 1 because
X
is integrable.

We now focus on the second assertion. Let
p\in[1,\infty)
. By Lemma
2
and since
\mathbb{E}|X|<\infty
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F||_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}||_{p}+||F_{m}-F||_{p}\big)=_{a.s.}\lim_{m\to\infty}||F_{m}-F||_{p}=0

p=\infty

and Polya’s theorem, we obtain almost surely that

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F||_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(||\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}||_{\infty}+||F_{m}-F||_{\infty}\big)\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}(1/m+||F_{m}-F||_{\infty})=0

∎

A.10
Proof of Theorem
9

Proof.

Recall that

\mathbb{F}_{n,m}(x)-F_{m}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Big(\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{j:m}\leq x\}-\mathbbm{1}\{\mu_{j:m}\leq x\}\Big).

Note that, a.e.,

\Big|\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{j:m}\leq x\}-\mathbbm{1}\{\mu_{j:m}\leq x\}\Big|=\mathbbm{1}\Big\{\min(\mu_{j:m},\hat{\mu}_{j:m})<x\leq\max(\mu_{j:m},\hat{\mu}_{j:m})\Big\}.

Hence, in general,

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}(x)-F_{m}(x)\big|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big(\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{j:m}\leq x\}-\mathbbm{1}\{\mu_{j:m}\leq x\}\big)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}\Big\{\min(\mu_{j:m},\hat{\mu}_{j:m})<x\leq\max(\mu_{j:m},\hat{\mu}_{j:m})\Big\}.

Let
\delta=\min_{j}(\mu_{j+1:m}-\mu_{j:m})
, and assume that the following event holds:

E_{n}=\{\omega:\max_{j}|\hat{\mu}_{j:m}-{\mu}_{j:m}|<\delta/2\}.

Under
E_{n},
the two functions
\mathbb{F}_{n,m}
and
F_{m}
can differ just by
1/m
, that is
|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}(x)-F_{m}(x)|\in\{0,1/m\}
for every
x
. In particular, for a fixed
x
, at most one of the indicators in the sum above is 1. In this case, the inequality above boils down to

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}(x)-F_{m}(x)\big|=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}\Big\{\min(\mu_{j:m},\hat{\mu}_{j:m})<x\leq\max(\mu_{j:m},\hat{\mu}_{j:m})\Big\}.

Consequently, the
L^{p}
norm of the difference reduces to a sum of lengths of intervals, that is,

\displaystyle\|F_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{p}^{p}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|F_{n,m}(x)-F_{m}(x)\big|^{p}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int\mathbbm{1}\Big\{\min(\mu_{j:m},\hat{\mu}_{j:m})<x\leq\max(\mu_{j:m},\hat{\mu}_{j:m})\Big\}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\hat{\mu}_{j:m}-\mu_{j:m}|.

Hence, under
E_{n}
, we know that
\sqrt{n}\|\mathbb{F}_{n,m}-F_{m}\|_{p}^{p}\to_{d}\frac{1}{m^{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|Z_{j}|
. It is easy to see that
P(E_{n})\to 1,
therefore, the assertion is proved.
∎
