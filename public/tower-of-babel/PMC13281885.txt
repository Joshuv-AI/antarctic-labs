Real-World Effectiveness of Biologic and Targeted Synthetic Therapies in Rheumatoid Arthritis: a Multicenter Study from Rheumatoid Arthritis Saudi Database (RASD)
Authors: Hani M Almoallim, Sami M Bahlas, Yasser Bawazir, Albadr H Hussein, Gamal Mahmoud Attia, Hanady Manasfi, Zain A Bafadhl, Alhussain Mohammed Asiri, Ayda Rahma Ali, Mohammed M Alomair, Mona Mohammed Alsharaif, Hanan H Almalki, Muhammad Irfanullah Siddiqui, Aous S Alhazmi, Suzan M Attar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeRheumatoid arthritis (RA) management emphasizes treat-to-target strategies with timely escalation from conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) to biologic (bDMARDs) or targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs) when remission or low disease activity is not achieved. Comparative real-world effectiveness evidence across advanced therapies remains limited in some settings. To compare 12-month outcomes and treatment persistence across biologic (bDMARD) and targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs) in Saudi patients enrolled in the Rheumatoid Arthritis Saudi Database (RASD).Patients and MethodsWe conducted a prospective multicenter cohort study of RASD patients receiving biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs, with a minimum follow-up of 12 months and assessments at baseline, 3–6 months, and 12 months. Outcomes included longitudinal DAS28-CRP change, 12-month disease activity, function, fatigue, remission, and treatment persistence through 36 months. Analyses used mixed-effects, covariate-adjusted regression, ordinal regression, logistic regression, and Kaplan–Meier methods, as appropriate for each outcome.ResultsWe analyzed 647 treatment courses across nine advanced therapies. Disease activity improved significantly over time, with a strong overall DAS28-CRP time effect (p<0.0001), but no significant overall drug effect (p=0.327) or time-by-drug interaction (p=0.218). Model-estimated 12-month DAS28-CRP values were closely clustered across therapies, with overlapping confidence intervals and no significant pairwise differences. In adjusted 12-month analyses, DAS28-CRP, CDAI, fatigue severity, and remission were comparable across agents. Most adjusted between-drug comparisons were non-significant, except for worse HAQ category with certolizumab compared with tofacitinib (adjusted OR 2.45, 95% CI 1.12–5.37). Remission varied significantly across participating centers. Discontinuation rates were low, most commonly due to lack of efficacy, and treatment persistence did not differ significantly across therapies through 36 months (log-rank p=0.17).ConclusionAdvanced RA therapies were associated with significant disease activity improvement and minimal adjusted between-drug differences. Except for an isolated worse HAQ signal with certolizumab versus tofacitinib, outcomes were broadly comparable across agents. Center-level remission variation highlights the importance of standardized treat-to-target implementation.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06417138.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic systemic inflammatory disease that leads to progressive joint damage, functional disability, and reduced quality of life.1–3 Despite major therapeutic advances, RA continues to generate a substantial health-economic burden through work disability, comorbidities, and long-term healthcare utilization.1 The modern therapeutic paradigm is anchored in early diagnosis, rapid initiation of conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) (typically methotrexate), and a treat-to-target strategy aiming for remission or at least low disease activity using validated composite measures and regular monitoring.3–6

International recommendations (EULAR and ACR) converge on early csDMARD initiation and escalation to advanced therapies, bDMARDs (eg, TNF inhibitors, IL-6 receptor inhibitors, abatacept, rituximab) or tsDMARDs (JAK inhibitors), when treatment targets are not met.3–5,7 Randomized trials demonstrate that multiple advanced therapies provide clinically meaningful benefit, including head-to-head comparisons such as baricitinib vs adalimumab (RA-BEAM), upadacitinib vs adalimumab (SELECT-COMPARE), abatacept vs adalimumab (AMPLE), and tocilizumab vs adalimumab (ADACTA).8–11 However, trial populations may not reflect real-world heterogeneity in disease duration, comorbidity burden, adherence, monitoring intensity, and access pathways.12–14

In Saudi Arabia specifically, population-level epidemiologic and real-world comparative effectiveness data remain limited, and diagnostic and referral delays may influence baseline severity and treatment response.7,15–19 Real-world comparative effectiveness evidence can therefore provide locally relevant insights for clinicians, payers, and guideline developers, while also identifying system-level variation in outcomes.12–14,20 The Rheumatoid Arthritis Saudi Database (RASD) was established as a multicenter registry to capture routine-care RA data across the Kingdom.16–19,21,22 Although international registries provide important comparative effectiveness data, their findings may not fully generalize to Saudi practice because of differences in referral pathways, medication access, treatment sequencing, comorbidity profiles, and monitoring workflows. Saudi registry data are therefore needed to evaluate whether advanced therapies perform similarly in routine national practice and to identify system-level variation that may be amenable to quality improvement. Unlike prior RASD analyses, the present study prospectively evaluates comparative 12-month effectiveness across multiple biologic and targeted synthetic DMARDs, patient-reported outcomes, treatment persistence through 36 months, care-pathway delays, and center-level variation.

We therefore performed a comprehensive reanalysis of RASD data focusing on: (1) longitudinal disease activity trajectories; (2) covariate-adjusted 12-month outcomes including function and fatigue; (3) and treatment persistence over 36 months.

Materials and Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Data Source

This was a prospective multicenter (from six participating centers) observational cohort study using data from the RASD, a national Saudi RA registry capturing demographic, clinical, laboratory, medication, and serial disease activity data. Eligible patients were followed for a minimum of 12 months.16 Additional centers not originally part of RASD were included if they provided a minimum follow-up of 12 months and at least three documented clinical visits following a treat-to-target management approach. We conducted and reported the study in accordance with the STROBE guidelines, and the registry structure and national scope have been described previously.23 All participants provided informed consent, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Local IRB approval was obtained at all participating centers, with primary ethical oversight provided by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Research Center, International Medical Center (IMCIRB #: 2023–0225; NCBE Registration No: @-02J-010). Additionally, the study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06417138).

Participants

Eligible treatment courses were derived from adults (≥18 years) who fulfilled the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for rheumatoid arthritis and had at least 12 months of follow-up with serial disease activity assessments available at baseline, 3–6 months, and 12 months.24,25 For each course, the index exposure was defined as the primary advanced therapy maintained for ≥6 months during the observation period. In the present analysis, each patient contributed only one eligible treatment course; therefore, the number of treatment courses corresponded to the number of unique patients.

Exposures

We evaluated commonly used advanced therapies documented in the registry, including biologic DMARDs (abatacept, adalimumab, certolizumab pegol, etanercept, rituximab, and tocilizumab) and targeted synthetic DMARDs, namely the Janus kinase inhibitors tofacitinib, baricitinib, and upadacitinib. Golimumab was not included because it is not approved in Saudi Arabia. Treatment exposure was defined as the primary agent administered for ≥6 months during the observation period. Concomitant conventional DMARD therapy (eg, methotrexate) and corticosteroid use were also recorded.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was longitudinal disease activity, measured by DAS28-CRP.26 Secondary outcomes included 12-month CDAI, functional disability assessed by HAQ disability category/HAQ-DI, fatigue severity assessed by the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS), remission at 12 months, and treatment persistence through 36 months. Remission was defined as DAS28-CRP <2.6. Treatment persistence was defined as time from treatment start to permanent discontinuation of the indexed therapy or switch to another advanced therapy. Temporary treatment interruptions, such as brief holds for infection, surgery, or administrative reasons, were not counted as discontinuation if the same indexed therapy was resumed.

Care-pathway delays were evaluated across prespecified clinical milestones: lag 1, symptom onset to first medical visit; lag 2, first medical visit to first rheumatology visit; lag 3, first rheumatology visit to confirmed diagnosis; and lag 4, diagnosis to DMARD initiation. All outcomes were based on validated instruments, including DAS28-CRP, CDAI, HAQ, and FSS, and established remission frameworks.26–31

Data Collection and Variables

Data were extracted from the RASD registry and cross-checked against the original electronic case report forms to ensure accuracy. Baseline variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), education level, smoking status, disease duration, rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) status, and prior medication exposure. Disease activity measures were recorded at baseline, 3–6 months, and 12 months, with laboratory parameters (including CRP and serologic markers) verified at each visit. Treatment start and stop dates were used to derive drug survival time.

Sampling Technique

We used a non-probability convenience sampling approach that included all eligible RA patients captured in the RASD across participating centers. Patients were enrolled consecutively when they met the inclusion criteria, enabling assembly of a broad real-world cohort reflective of routine care in Saudi rheumatology clinics. While convenience sampling may introduce selection bias, the multicenter national registry design helps mitigate this risk by capturing diverse patient characteristics, disease severity, and treatment practices across Saudi Arabia.

Sample Size Calculation

Sample size was estimated using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 for the primary outcome, change in DAS28-CRP, across up to nine treatment groups. The a priori calculation indicated that 880 patients would be required to detect a medium effect size (f=0.25) with 80% power at α=0.05. The final analytic cohort included 647 patients.

Although the cohort was below the prespecified target, post hoc assessment suggested adequate sensitivity to detect small-to-medium effects in repeated-measures models, supported by the strong main effect of time for DAS28-CRP and CDAI (both p<0.0001). However, some treatment groups were small, particularly rituximab and abatacept; therefore, between-drug comparisons involving these subgroups should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

The analytic cohort was restricted to patients treated with biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs. This restriction explains why the final sample size was below the initially estimated target, as prior RASD data indicate that approximately 30% of Saudi RA registry patients receive bDMARDs or tsDMARDs.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were prespecified and conducted in R V 4.5.1. using complete-case data for each model. Baseline characteristics were compared across treatment groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and Pearson’s χ2-test for categorical variables. Longitudinal disease activity (DAS28-CRP) was evaluated using a linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept for patient ID and fixed effects for time (baseline, 3–6 months, and 12 months), treatment group, and the time treatment interaction; inference was based on Type III ANOVA with Satterthwaite/Kenward–Roger degrees of freedom, and post hoc contrasts were estimated using marginal means with Tukey adjustment for multiple comparisons.32,33 For comparative effectiveness at 12 months, ANCOVA models assessed DAS28-CRP, CDAI, and fatigue while adjusting for the corresponding baseline value and prespecified covariates (age, sex, BMI, center, RA duration, RF status, and anti-CCP status), whereas functional disability (HAQ category) at 12 months was analyzed using proportional-odds ordinal regression adjusting for baseline HAQ and the same covariates; the validity and widespread use of DAS28, CDAI, HAQ, and FSS are well established.26–29 Treatment persistence was summarized using Kaplan–Meier methods through 36 months and compared across therapies using the Log rank test. Persistence was defined as time from treatment start to discontinuation of the indexed therapy. Switching to another therapy was counted as a discontinuation event at the time of switch, whereas patients without documented discontinuation were censored at their last available follow-up.

We performed additional exploratory analyses to assess the robustness and interpretability of selected findings. First, baseline HAQ distributions were compared between certolizumab- and tofacitinib-treated patients, given the isolated adjusted HAQ difference observed between these groups. Second, sensitivity analyses were performed after excluding patients with very high baseline disability, defined as baseline HAQ score ≥2 for the continuous HAQ model and severe-to-very-severe baseline disability for the ordinal HAQ model. Third, each care-pathway lag time was modeled separately in exploratory regression analyses to evaluate its association with 12-month remission and 12-month HAQ outcomes, while reducing collinearity between lag variables. Logistic regression was used for remission, linear regression for 12-month HAQ score, and proportional-odds ordinal regression for 12-month HAQ category. Models were adjusted for treatment group, baseline outcome value where applicable, age, sex, BMI, center, RA duration, RF, and anti-CCP status. Wald confidence intervals were used for sparse ordinal sensitivity models.

Analyses were conducted using complete cases for the variables required in each model. Complete-case analysis was selected because missingness varied by outcome and visit, and imputation assumptions could not be reliably verified in this retrospective registry dataset.

Care-Pathway Lag Times

Across the cohort, the longest interval occurred early in the care pathway: symptom onset to first healthcare contact (Lag 1) had a mean of 3.524±4.390 months (median 3.00 [1.00–3.00]; range 0–60) and did not differ by treatment group (p=0.56). Subsequent intervals were shorter. Lag 2 (first healthcare visit to first rheumatology visit) averaged 2.762±2.639 months and did differ across groups (p=0.02). Lag 3 (first rheumatology visit to diagnosis) averaged 1.842±1.664 months with borderline heterogeneity (p=0.06). Lag 4 (diagnosis to DMARD initiation) was shortest in absolute duration (1.587±1.575 months; median 1.00 [1.00–1.00]) yet showed the clearest between-group variability (p=0.001).

Exploratory Association Between Lag Times and 12-Month Outcomes

In exploratory adjusted models, care-pathway lag times were not independently associated with 12-month remission. The odds ratios were close to unity for Lag 1, Lag 2, Lag 3, and Lag 4, and none reached statistical significance. Similarly, lag times were not significantly associated with 12-month HAQ score or 12-month HAQ category in adjusted models. These findings suggest that, within this treated cohort, measured care-pathway delays were not independent predictors of 12-month remission or functional outcome after adjustment for treatment group, baseline disease activity or HAQ, demographic factors, serology, RA duration, and center (Supplementary Tables 1–3).

Longitudinal Disease Activity (DAS28-CRP)

In linear mixed-effects modeling of DAS28-CRP across baseline, 3–6 months, and 12 months, there was a strong overall time effect (F=67.11, p< 0.0001), indicating substantial improvement in disease activity over follow-up. In contrast, there was no evidence of an overall drug effect (F=1.15, p=0.327) and no significant time drug interaction (F=1.26, p=0.218), supporting broadly similar modeled improvement across therapies Supplementary Table 4.

Model-based estimated marginal means decreased from baseline to 3–6 months and remained low at 12 months across all therapies, with 12-month EMMs clustering narrowly (1.92–2.23) and overlapping confidence intervals. Pairwise comparisons (Tukey-adjusted within each time point) showed no statistically significant differences between any drug pairs at baseline, 3–6 months, or 12 months. Consistent with the model-based findings, unadjusted distributions demonstrated a clear improvement pattern: all therapies showed a leftward shift in DAS28-CRP from baseline to 12 months, but with persistent overlap between groups at follow-up Supplementary Tables 5–8.

Adjusted Comparative Effectiveness at 12 Months

In covariate-adjusted 12-month analyses, no therapy differed significantly from the reference in adjusted DAS28-CRP, CDAI, or fatigue severity score at 12 months; effect sizes were small and confidence intervals consistently crossed the null Supplementary Table 9.

For functional outcomes, ordinal modeling of 12-month HAQ disability category identified one statistically significant association: certolizumab was associated with higher odds of worse HAQ category compared with tofacitinib (adjusted OR 2.45, 95% CI 1.12–5.37, p=0.025). Other HAQ comparisons were not statistically significant. The proportional odds assumption was formally assessed using the Brant test. The omnibus test was not significant (χ2 = 18.97, df = 21, p = 0.59), supporting the proportional odds assumption Supplementary Table 9.

Exploratory Analyses of the Isolated HAQ Finding

Because certolizumab was associated with worse 12-month HAQ category compared with tofacitinib in the primary ordinal model, we further examined baseline HAQ distributions between these two groups. Baseline HAQ was not higher in the certolizumab group; mean baseline HAQ was 0.503±0.537 in certolizumab-treated patients and 0.624±0.524 in tofacitinib-treated patients. The distribution of baseline disability categories was also broadly similar, with severe-to-very-severe baseline disability observed in one patient in the certolizumab group and two patients in the tofacitinib group.

In sensitivity analyses excluding patients with very high baseline disability, the association between certolizumab and worse 12-month HAQ persisted. In the continuous HAQ sensitivity model, certolizumab was associated with a higher 12-month HAQ score compared with tofacitinib (β=0.132, 95% CI 0.001–0.263; p=0.049). Similarly, in the ordinal sensitivity model excluding severe-to-very-severe baseline disability, certolizumab remained associated with higher odds of worse 12-month HAQ category (OR=2.37, 95% CI 1.13–4.99; p=0.023) Supplementary Tables 10–12.

Discussion

In this multicenter Saudi real-world cohort, we observed robust improvement in disease activity over time after initiation/continuation of advanced RA therapies, but no evidence of clinically meaningful between-drug differences in modeled DAS28-CRP trajectories. This aligns with the reality that multiple bDMARDs and tsDMARDs have proven efficacy, and that in routine practice the average comparative effectiveness may be modest once patient heterogeneity, baseline imbalance, and measurement variation are accounted for.2,12–14 International guidance accordingly frames many advanced therapies as appropriate options after csDMARD inadequate response, emphasizing shared decision-making, patient comorbidities, safety considerations, and access rather than assuming large efficacy gaps between agents.3–6

Head-to-head trials have occasionally demonstrated differences versus specific comparators. For example, baricitinib showed superiority over adalimumab in RA-BEAM, and upadacitinib showed superiority over adalimumab in SELECT-COMPARE, both in MTX-inadequate responders.8,10 Similarly, IL-6 inhibition (tocilizumab) demonstrated strong monotherapy efficacy compared with adalimumab in ADACTA, and abatacept demonstrated comparable efficacy to adalimumab in AMPLE.9,11 However, translating these contrasts into routine care is complicated by confounding by indication (eg, later-line use of non-TNF biologics), differences in adherence, concomitant csDMARD use, and outcome measurement practices.12–14 In our data, baseline imbalance was evident for CDAI, fatigue, and HAQ category. After covariate adjustment, cross-sectional 12-month disease activity and fatigue outcomes were broadly comparable across therapies. This is consistent with broader comparative effectiveness syntheses and network meta-analyses showing that many advanced therapies have overlapping effectiveness ranges across populations and endpoints.34

Following ORAL Surveillance (tofacitinib vs TNF inhibitors in older RA patients with cardiovascular risk), regulators issued updated warnings regarding serious heart-related events, malignancy, thrombosis, and mortality risk in selected populations.35–37 Our registry analysis primarily addresses effectiveness and persistence; it should not be interpreted as comparative safety evidence. Instead, it supports the guideline-consistent position that, when monitored and selected appropriately, multiple advanced therapies can achieve meaningful disease control, while safety profiling and patient risk stratification remain decisive in drug choice.3–5,35–37

A notable finding was the association between certolizumab and worse 12-month HAQ outcomes compared with tofacitinib. Additional exploratory analyses suggested that this finding was not explained by higher baseline HAQ in the certolizumab group, as baseline HAQ scores were numerically lower in certolizumab-treated patients. The association also persisted after excluding patients with very high baseline disability. However, this isolated functional difference was not accompanied by parallel differences in inflammatory disease activity, fatigue, or remission. Therefore, the finding should be interpreted cautiously. HAQ reflects both inflammatory and non-inflammatory contributors to disability, including structural damage, chronic pain, obesity, psychological factors, comorbidities, and socioeconomic determinants that may not be fully captured in registry covariates.28,38–40 Residual confounding or unmeasured differences in patient selection may therefore remain plausible explanations.

Fatigue is a major driver of quality of life in RA and often responds incompletely even when inflammation is controlled.40–42 In our adjusted analyses, fatigue severity did not differ significantly across therapies. This supports the concept that fatigue management may require multimodal strategies beyond pharmacologic inflammation control, consistent with broader RA care frameworks.3–5,40–42

Perhaps the most practice-relevant result was the magnitude of center-level variation in remission odds, independent of drug choice and baseline covariates. The observed center-level variation likely reflects both patient case mix and care-process differences, including monitoring frequency, documentation consistency, referral pathways, treatment escalation practices, adherence support, and local implementation of treat-to-target workflows. Because these center-level characteristics were not directly measured, the center effect should be interpreted as a systems-level signal rather than evidence that any individual center or treatment strategy is inherently superior. Treat-to-target recommendations explicitly require frequent assessment and therapy adjustment until targets are achieved.3–6 Our data imply that standardizing treat-to-target operationalization across centers could yield meaningful outcome gains in routine practice. Center-level variation in care and outcomes has been documented in RA observational cohorts.20

Drug survival integrates effectiveness, tolerability, adherence, and access. Our Kaplan–Meier analysis did not show statistically significant differences in continuation across agents over 36 months. Absolute discontinuation rates were low. International registry studies commonly show variability in drug retention across drug classes and lines of therapy; however, differences are often modest and strongly confounded by indication and switching policies.12–14

The lag-time findings suggest that the main bottleneck occurs at the very beginning of the care pathway, between symptom onset and the first healthcare visit, whereas later steps tend to be shorter, although some variability remains between treatment groups, particularly for the interval from diagnosis to DMARD initiation. Reducing this early delay is a well-recognized global priority because earlier effective treatment is associated with better long-term outcomes within the “window of opportunity”, and the benefits of early referral and early therapy have been consistently supported in the literature.7,15 In Saudi Arabia, strengthening system-level processes such as musculoskeletal triage, streamlined referral pathways, and expanded rheumatology capacity may shorten time-to-target and ultimately improve remission rates at the population level.3–6,16–19,21,22,43,44

In the exploratory adjusted analyses, individual care-pathway lag times were not independently associated with 12-month remission or HAQ outcomes. This may reflect limited power, retrospective measurement imprecision, or the possibility that outcomes after initiation of advanced therapy are more strongly influenced by treatment selection, monitoring intensity, disease biology, and center-level care processes than by historical delays alone. Therefore, these lag-time models should be interpreted as exploratory.

Clinical Implications

Our findings have several practical implications for real-world RA care in Saudi Arabia. First, the consistent improvement in disease activity over time across all evaluated advanced therapies supports the clinical effectiveness of both bDMARD and tsDMARD strategies in routine practice, reinforcing treat-to-target implementation as a workable approach in national rheumatology settings.3–6,16 Second, the absence of meaningful between-drug differences in adjusted longitudinal DAS28-CRP trajectories and 12-month disease activity endpoints suggests that, for many patients, achieving remission or low disease activity may be driven more by timely escalation, monitoring intensity, adherence support, and appropriate sequencing than by selecting one specific agent over another.3–6 In this context, drug choice should be individualized based on comorbidity profile, patient preference, route/frequency of administration, prior treatment history, and safety considerations, particularly given evolving regulatory guidance for JAK inhibitors in higher-risk populations.35–37

Third, the pronounced center-level variability in remission odds highlights a clear, actionable systems target: standardization of measurement and escalation workflows may yield outcome improvements comparable to those expected from switching between advanced drug classes. Practical interventions include routine capture of validated composite indices at each visit (eg, DAS28-CRP/CDAI), predefined escalation triggers, structured reassessment intervals, and harmonized documentation practices across sites.3–6 Finally, the low overall discontinuation rate and the predominance of inefficacy rather than adverse effects as a stated discontinuation reason emphasize the importance of early identification of suboptimal response and prompt switching within or across mechanisms, rather than prolonged exposure to partially effective therapy.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study leverages a multicenter national registry capturing routine RA care across Saudi Arabia, improving generalizability beyond single-center experiences. The analysis integrates complementary endpoints, longitudinal disease activity trajectories, covariate-adjusted 12-month outcomes (including patient-reported function and fatigue), treatment persistence, discontinuation patterns, and care-pathway lag times, providing a clinically comprehensive view of real-world effectiveness. Methodologically, we applied appropriate models for repeated measures (mixed-effects), adjusted comparative analyses to address baseline imbalance, and time-to-event methods for persistence. Together, these features strengthen the clinical interpretability of the findings and support actionable system-level insights.

Although the prospective multicenter design reflects routine clinical practice and supports real-world relevance, several limitations should be acknowledged. Some patients had already completed their first rheumatology-center visit at the time of inclusion, and the analysis was restricted to patients receiving biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs. Because only a minority of RASD patients receive b/tsDMARDs, the eligible cohort was necessarily smaller than the full registry population and below the initially estimated sample size target. In addition, although most patients were likely receiving b/tsDMARDs for the first time, prior biologic or targeted synthetic DMARD exposure could not be definitively confirmed because this variable was not consistently captured in the data collection sheet.

Conclusions

In this Saudi multicenter real-world cohort, advanced RA therapies were associated with significant improvement in disease activity over time, but between-drug differences were minimal after modeling and adjustment. Treatment persistence was broadly similar across agents, and discontinuation was uncommon. The most actionable signal was center-level variation in remission odds, suggesting that standardizing treat-to-target implementation, measurement, and escalation workflows may yield substantial improvements in outcomes.
