Random sequential nearest-neighbor coloring on trees
Anne-Laure Basdevant, Arvind Singh

Abstract
We study a nearest-neighbor coloring process in which vertices are revealed in random order and inherit the color of the closest vertex revealed before them. This model is a discrete analogue of coloring processes previously studied by Preater [ 10 ] and Aldous [ 1 ] in Euclidean spaces. We focus here on regular trees and analyze the associated genealogy of color inheritance. In contrast with the Euclidean case, the genealogical graph on an infinite regular tree is not connected: it has infinitely many infinite one-ended components, each with a distinct asymptotic direction, while every vertex has only finitely many descendants. We also describe how this structure is modified in the presence of finitely many initial seeds. Finally, we study local limits of the coloring on finite regular trees as their height tends to infinity, for two natural seed configurations: two fixed seeds, and one blue seed at the root with red seeds at the leaves.

Introduction

We study a random coloring procedure on graphs, in which vertices are colored sequentially at random, each vertex taking, at the time it is picked, the color of one of its closest already colored vertices. This model can be interpreted as a growth process driven by local competition between different species (
i.e.
, colors). Alternatively, it may also be interpreted as a variant of the classical voter model where new settlers take the political opinion of one of their already settled neighbors.

Sequential nearest-neighbor rules of this kind have appeared in two closely related forms. In the uncolored version, each arriving point is linked to its closest predecessor, giving the on-line nearest-neighbor graph, or random nearest-neighbor tree, whose geometry has been studied in several Euclidean settings
[
8
,
9
,
7
,
4
]
. In the colored version, introduced by Preater and Aldous and further studied in
[
10
,
1
,
3
]
, the focus is instead on the random partition generated by color inheritance. More precisely, in the latter Euclidean model, the authors consider the unit cube
[-1,1]^{d}
. Initially, the origin is colored in blue and the boundary of the cube in red. Subsequently, independent, uniformly sampled random points fall in
[-1,1]^{d}
and, upon arrival, each point takes the color of the nearest point that has appeared so far. This procedure ultimately creates a random coloring of the unit cube
1
1

1

This construction produces only a countable set of colored sample points but can be subsequently extended to a coloring of the whole cube by assigning to each location the color of the points in its infinitesimal neighborhood (with an additional “gray” interface corresponding to accumulation points from both colors).
. One of the main results in

i.e.
, all red vertices remain at a positive distance from the initial blue vertex. On the other hand, the converse question of whether there may exist blue vertices arbitrarily close to the red boundary with positive probability is more delicate and still remains open.

In contrast with these Euclidean works, the present paper deals with a discrete setting in which the underlying graph is a regular tree. This change of geometry leads to rather different phenomena and makes the genealogical structure of the process a natural object of study. Before specializing to trees, let us first describe the model on an arbitrary finite connected graph
G=(V,E)
. Given a set of initially colored vertices called
seeds
and a uniformly random permutation of the non-seed vertices (which encodes the order in which the vertices are revealed), the coloring procedure is defined as follows. When a vertex
v
is revealed, it takes the color of one of the already colored vertices that minimize the graph distance to
v
; if several such vertices exist, one chooses uniformly among them. At the end of the procedure, all vertices are colored.

The same model can equivalently be formulated in terms of arrival times, in a way that naturally extends to infinite graphs: one assigns to each vertex
v
a time
\tau_{v}\geq 0
, interpreted as its arrival time, and reveals the vertices in increasing order of these times. On a finite graph, if the variables
(\tau_{v})_{v\in V}
are i.i.d. with a continuous distribution, then their increasing order induces a uniform random permutation of the vertices and we recover the previous construction of the coloring. On an infinite graph, there is in general no way to reveal the vertices one after another in increasing order of their arrival times, since the collection
(\tau_{v})
need not admit a smallest element. Nevertheless, the variables
(\tau_{v})
still encode the relative order of arrival of the vertices and make it possible to reconstruct the genealogy by a local backward exploration. Vertices
s
with arrival time
\tau_{s}=0
are declared to be seeds, have no ancestor, and are assigned a fixed color. If
v
has positive arrival time, we write
d(\cdot,\cdot)
for the graph distance and set

r(v):=\min\bigl\{d(v,w):w\in V,\,\tau_{w}<\tau_{v}\bigr\}.

(1)

The parent of
v
is defined as the vertex at distance
r(v)
with an arrival time strictly smaller than
\tau_{v}
(chosen uniformly at random if there is more than one). Iterating this parent relation yields an ancestral line for each vertex and, in turn, a genealogical forest
\mathcal{G}
on the set of vertices
V
. Each connected component of
\mathcal{G}
is a tree with all its vertices of the same color.

In the present paper, we focus on the case where the graph
G
is a regular tree. Our main purpose is to study the genealogy generated by the coloring procedure. In the infinite-volume setting, we show that this genealogy is a forest with infinitely many connected components and investigate its geometric structure. We then return to finite regular trees and derive from the infinite-volume picture several consequences for the corresponding finite-volume colorings and their local limits.

1.1.2
Coloring on finite regular trees and local limits

In the second part of this paper, we describe the coloring process on large finite regular trees and study its limit as the height of the tree tends to infinity. For

Coloring with seeds at the root and at the leaves.

Outline of the paper

In Section
2
, we give the precise construction of the ancestral path
\Gamma(v)
and of an associated ancestral process
(V_{n}(v),W_{n}(v))
. This includes the uniform increasing indexings used to break ties and an equivalent construction in terms of the inter-arrival times of a lower record process. We then derive the preliminary estimates on this process, in particular the growth estimates for the jumps and for the height of the ancestral path (Proposition
14
and Corollary
15
).

Section
3
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1
. We first prove that every genealogical component has an asymptotic direction. We then use Proposition
17
, which shows that an ancestral path may avoid an entire descendant subtree with positive probability, to prove that
\mathcal{G}
has infinitely many components and that every descendant sector contains the direction of some component. The fact that two distinct components have distinct directions is proved by introducing a coupled exploration of two ancestral paths, encoded by a lower 2-record process. Finally, we prove that every vertex has only finitely many descendants by a combinatorial estimate on possible descendant paths.

In Section
4
, we prove Theorem
3
. The argument is based on a coupling between the genealogical graph without seeds and the genealogical graph with a finite set of seeds: the ancestral path of a vertex is changed exactly when its exploration discovers one of the seeds. This observation transfers most of the structure obtained in the seedless case and reduces the remaining work to characterizing when a seed has infinite degree in its genealogical component.

Section
5
returns to finite trees and proves the local-limit results. We first record the local convergence of genealogical graphs under the natural coupling. We then treat the two-seed coloring, proving Theorem
6
and Proposition
7
by reading the limiting color of a vertex from the terminal seed or the asymptotic direction of its infinite-volume ancestral path. The last part of the section studies the coloring with a blue seed at the root and red seeds at the leaves: the local convergence and the statement (
6
) follow from the infinite-volume results with seeds. The remaining estimate (
5
), which concerns the behavior of the coloring near the leaves, requires different arguments; it is proved separately in Section
6
, where we follow the coloring dynamics forward in time and compare the recursive growth of blue vertices with a supercritical Galton–Watson process.

centered at

(indexing of

(ii)

(increasing) if

(iii)

is uniform among all possible orderings and independent of the orderings induced at other distances.

Note that (iii) makes sense because we consider locally finite graphs, so the set of vertices at distance

is finite for every

. In particular, a uniform increasing indexing of the vertices of

centered at

can be constructed by first sampling a uniform random ordering of the vertices at distance

from

Recall that
(\tau_{u})_{u\in\mathcal{T}}
denotes the arrival times associated with the vertices of
\mathcal{T}
. In this section, we consider the model without seeds; hence, the variables

are i.i.d. with an atomless distribution.
For each vertex

, let

, and such that the families

Using this collection of random variables, we define the direct ancestor

of

as follows: we examine the arrival times of the vertices of

in the order given by the sequence

is this vertex

. More formally, we set

In this construction, the family of random variables

For

of

Assume now that

is the

(7)

By construction,

so that

Definition 9
.

of

.

We note that this construction uses only the inter-arrival times

Lemma 10
.

The process

defined above has the same distribution as the ancestral process

. Moreover, for every

Proof.

The result follows directly by induction from the construction. Conditionally on the past up to step

, the unexplored arrival times, read in the random indexing around

, form a fresh i.i.d. atomless sequence. Since

is the current lower record among the revealed times, the number of new vertices inspected before finding the next ancestor has the law of

is also immediate, with

∎

2.2
Preliminary results on the genealogical process

We now present some preliminary results for the ancestral process. We use the construction of the previous section from the inter-arrival times

For

Lemma 11
.

For any

•

•

Moreover, for any

\frac{I_{n}}{K+1}\leq K^{R_{n}}\leq KI_{n}.

(12)

Proof.

Let us first note that, by construction,

for all

.
Let us now prove by induction on

is connected and

. This holds for

. Assume it holds for

(z_{j}^{n-1})_{j\geq 1}
be the subsequence of

. Since

is connected. Moreover,

Let us now prove (

\displaystyle I_{n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sharp\{u\in\mathcal{T}\setminus W_{n-1},d(V_{n-1},u)\leq R_{n}\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sharp\{u\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\{V_{n-1}\},d(V_{n-1},u)\leq R_{n}\}

\displaystyle I_{n}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\geq

The process

is the inter-arrival-time process of a classical record process. Such processes have been well studied. We will use the following result, which is an easy consequence of the classical asymptotic behavior of record processes.

Proposition 12

(see Arnold
[
2
]
)
.

Let
(I_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the inter-arrival times of a record process of an i.i.d. sequence with diffuse distribution. Then

(i)

We have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log I_{n}}{n}=1\mbox{ a.s.}.

(ii)

For any
n\geq 1
and
i_{2},\ldots,i_{n}\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}(I_{2}=i_{2},\ldots,I_{n}=i_{n},\ 2^{k}\leq I_{k}\leq 3^{k}\hbox{ for all }k>n)>0.

(iii)

For any
c\geq 0
and
n\geq 1
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\forall m\geq 0,I_{n+m+1}\geq c\mid I_{1},\ldots,I_{n})\geq\mathbb{P}(\forall m\geq 0,I_{m+2}\geq c)>0\quad\hbox{a.s.}.

Proof.

The first part of the proposition is a classical result (see, for example,
[
2
]
, p. 28). For the second point, one uses the Markov description of the lower record process: any finite admissible sequence of inter-arrival times has positive probability, and after such a finite prescription the future process is again a lower record process started from the current record value. Since
2<e<3
, point (i) implies that, for some sufficiently large deterministic
N
, the tail event
\{2^{k}\leq I_{k}\leq 3^{k}\text{ for all }k>N\}
has positive probability; the finitely many constraints for
n<k\leq N
can then be imposed with positive probability.

Finally, the last point follows from the standard Markov description of the record-time chain: conditionally on
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
, the future gaps are stochastically lower bounded by a fresh record inter-arrival process started from time
1
. Point (ii) then implies that

\mathbb{P}(\forall m\geq 0,\ I_{m+2}\geq c)>0
.
∎

We now introduce the filtration

Proposition 14
.

For any

\mathbb{P}(|V_{n+1}|<|V_{n}|+R_{n+1}(1-\varepsilon)\mid\mathcal{F}_{n})\leq 2K^{-\frac{\varepsilon R_{n+1}}{2}}.

First note that the proposition is trivial for

Corollary 15
.

The following limits hold a.s.:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{R_{n}}{n}=\frac{1}{\log K}\qquad\hbox{ and }\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|V_{n}|}{n^{2}}=\frac{1}{2\log K}.

The limit of

.

∎

3
Study of
\mathcal{G}
without seeds

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem
1
. We do not follow the order of the statements. Instead, the proof is organized around the geometry of ancestral paths: we first show that each component has an asymptotic direction, then use local constructions to produce many components, and finally prove that distinct components have distinct directions and that descendant sets are finite. More precisely, we proceed as follows:

•

Item 3: we prove that each connected component of
\mathcal{G}
admits a limiting direction;

•

Items 1 and 4: we prove that
\mathcal{G}
has infinitely many connected components, that for every subtree
\mathcal{T}_{v}
there exists a component whose limiting direction belongs to
\partial\mathcal{T}_{v}
, and we derive a lower bound on the number of components intersecting the sphere of radius
n
;

•

Item 5: we show that two distinct connected components almost surely have distinct limiting directions;

•

Item 2: we prove that every vertex almost surely has finitely many descendants.

3.1
Proof of Theorem
1
, Item 3

We begin by proving that each connected component of
\mathcal{G}
has a limiting direction. Since
\mathcal{T}
is vertex-transitive, it is sufficient to prove this property for the connected component containing the root.

Lemma 16
.

Let
\Gamma(o)=(V_{1},V_{2},\ldots)
be the ancestral path of the root. Almost surely, for all sufficiently large

of maximal height.

Proof.

Recall that the ancestral path of the root

2|V_{n}\wedge V_{n+1}|=|V_{n}|+|V_{n+1}|-d(V_{n},V_{n+1}).

Corollary

tends a.s. to

Moreover, we have proved (see (

Thus, a.s., for

Hence

|V_{n}\wedge V_{n+1}|>|V_{n}\wedge V_{n-1}|.

5
Local limits

We now come back to the case of finite trees and study the local limits of the random coloring in different settings. Recall that
\mathcal{T}^{(\ell)}:=\mathcal{T}\cap B(o,\ell)
denotes the finite tree of height
\ell

Let

be a (possibly empty) set of seeds. We always assume

for all

on

\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{(\ell)}
on the finite tree

(both with seeds

and the same uniform increasing indexings

By definition, the genealogical graph is constructed by a local exploration around each vertex of the tree, which makes it nicely behaved with respect to the notion of local convergence. More precisely, we have the straightforward result, stated here for trees but valid for any locally finite connected graph:

Proposition 23
.

We have

\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{(\ell)}\underset{\ell\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\tilde{\mathcal{G}}

for the topology of local convergence. In other words, for any
N\geq 0
we have

\tilde{\mathcal{G}}\cap B(o,N)\;=\;\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{(\ell)}\cap B(o,N)\qquad\hbox{for all $\ell$ large enough a.s.}

where
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}\cap B(o,N)
(resp.
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{(\ell)}\cap B(o,N)
) stands for the genealogical graph
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
(resp.
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{(\ell)}
) restricted to the set of vertices
B(o,N)
.

Proof.

We just need to prove that any vertex
v
(that is not a seed) has the same direct ancestor in
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{(\ell)}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
for
\ell
large enough. Since both graphs are constructed with the same arrival times and the same increasing indexing, this is true as soon as the exploration around

does not reach the boundary of
\mathcal{T}^{(\ell)}
,
i.e.
, as soon as

, where
\tilde{p}(v)
denotes the direct ancestor of

in
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
.
∎

While the genealogical graph is continuous with respect to the local topology, the coloring itself does not have this property because the color of a vertex is related to the connectedness of vertices in the genealogical graph and therefore to the existence of an infinite ancestral path, which is a non-local notion. In the rest of this section, we study the limits of the coloring in two particular cases:

(a)

Two fixed seeds colored blue and red, respectively.

(b)

A single blue seed at the root and a red seed on each leaf of
\mathcal{T}^{(\ell)}

Remark 25
.

When
d(o,o^{\prime})
is even, the natural coupling does not give an a.s. local limit in general. Indeed, for a vertex whose infinite ancestral path has asymptotic direction in
\partial\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{d/2}
, the penultimate vertex of
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(\ell)}(v)
eventually lies in the middle subtree, where the two seeds are at the same distance. As
\ell
grows, infinitely many fresh tie-breakings between
o
and
o^{\prime}
are therefore used, and the color of such a vertex does not stabilize, but takes both colors infinitely often. This is why Theorem
6
proved below is stated as a convergence in distribution, with an additional Bernoulli choice for each middle-direction component.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

It suffices to show that for any finite collection of vertices
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})
, the following properties hold:

(i)

If
\tilde{\Gamma}(v_{i})
is finite and ends at
o
(resp.
o^{\prime}
), or if
\mathop{dir}\tilde{\Gamma}(v_{i})\in\partial\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{j}
with
j<d/2
(resp.
j>d/2
), then
v_{i}\in\tilde{B}^{(\ell)}
(resp.
\tilde{R}^{(\ell)}
) for all sufficiently large
\ell
.

(ii)

If
\mathop{dir}\tilde{\Gamma}(v_{i})\in\partial\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{d/2}
, define

\mathcal{I}:=\bigl\{i^{\prime}\leq k:\ \tilde{\Gamma}(v_{i^{\prime}})\text{ coalesces with }\tilde{\Gamma}(v_{i})\bigr\}.

Then, for all sufficiently large
\ell
, the vertices
(v_{i^{\prime}})_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}}
receive the same color in the two-seed coloring process on
\mathcal{T}^{(\ell)}
, and this color is blue or red with probability
1/2
each. Moreover, this color is asymptotically independent of the colors of
(v_{i^{\prime}})_{i^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{I}}
.

Property (i) was established in Proposition
7

is odd. The proof given there in fact applies verbatim when

is even and

. We now prove (ii). We assume that

is even, since otherwise there is nothing to prove. Also, to avoid cumbersome notation, we only treat the case of two vertices

and

Assume that
\mathop{dir}\tilde{\Gamma}(v_{1})\in\partial\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{d/2}

\tilde{\Gamma}(v_{1})
and
\tilde{\Gamma}(v_{2})
coalesce. Then, for all sufficiently large
\ell
, the truncated ancestral paths
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(\ell)}(v_{1})
and
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(\ell)}(v_{2})
also coalesce. Consequently,
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
receive the same color in the two-seed coloring process on
\mathcal{T}^{(\ell)}
. By Lemma
24
, the penultimate vertex of
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(\ell)}(v_{1})
is in
\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{d/2}
for all sufficiently large

. Since this vertex is at equal distance from

and

, it is connected to either seed with probability

Assume now that
\tilde{\Gamma}(v_{1})
and
\tilde{\Gamma}(v_{2})
do not coalesce. We show that, for all sufficiently large
\ell
, the paths
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(\ell)}(v_{1})
and
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(\ell)}(v_{2})
are disjoint, except possibly for their last vertex. By Item 5 of Theorem

\mathop{dir}\tilde{\Gamma}(v_{1})\neq\mathop{dir}\tilde{\Gamma}(v_{2}).

(We assume here that

and

and
\mathcal{T}_{w_{2}}
are disjoint, while

\mathop{dir}\tilde{\Gamma}(v_{1})\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{w_{1}},\qquad\mathop{dir}\tilde{\Gamma}(v_{2})\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{w_{2}}.

By Lemma
24
, for all sufficiently large
\ell
,

\tilde{V}^{(\ell),1}_{n_{\ell}(v_{1})-1}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{w_{1}},\qquad\tilde{V}^{(\ell),2}_{n_{\ell}(v_{2})-1}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{w_{2}},

where
(\tilde{V}_{n}^{(\ell),i})_{n\geq 1}
denotes the ancestral path of
v_{i}
in
\mathcal{T}^{(\ell)}
. Since
\mathcal{T}_{w_{1}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{w_{2}}
are disjoint, the paths
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(\ell)}(v_{1})
and
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(\ell)}(v_{2})
are disjoint, except possibly at their final vertex. As before, the fact that
\mathop{dir}\tilde{\Gamma}(v_{1})\in\partial\overline{\mathcal{T}}_{d/2}
implies that the penultimate vertex of
\tilde{\Gamma}^{(\ell)}(v_{1})
is at equal distance from
o
and
o^{\prime}
. Hence, the color of
v_{1}
is blue or red with probability
1/2
each, depending on the increasing ordering attached to
\tilde{V}^{(\ell),1}_{n_{\ell}(v_{1})-1}
. Since this ordering is independent of the ancestral path of
v_{2}
(hence of the color of
v_{2}
), it follows that

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl(v_{1}\in\tilde{B}^{(\ell)}\mid v_{2}\in\tilde{B}^{(\ell)}\bigr)=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigl(v_{1}\in\tilde{B}^{(\ell)}\mid v_{2}\in\tilde{R}^{(\ell)}\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}.

This proves (ii) and completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

5.2
Local limit of a tree with seeds at the root and the leaves (Theorem
5
)

We now consider the coloring process on the finite tree
\mathcal{T}^{(\ell)}
, with a blue seed at the root
o
and a red seed at each leaf of
\mathcal{T}^{(\ell)}
. As usual, we let
B^{(\ell)}
and
R^{(\ell)}
denote the final sets of blue and red vertices, respectively.
We also consider the genealogical graph
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{o}
on
\mathcal{T}
with a single seed located at
o
. These processes are constructed on the same probability space using the same arrival times
(\tau_{w})_{w\in\mathcal{T}}
and the same random orderings
((z_{j}^{w})_{j\geq 1})_{w\in\mathcal{T}}
used to break ties.

As already observed in the introduction, if a vertex
v
is colored blue in
\mathcal{T}^{(\ell)}
, then it is also colored blue in all subsequent
\mathcal{T}^{(\ell^{\prime})}
for every
\ell^{\prime}>\ell
. Hence,

B^{(\ell)}\subset B^{(\ell^{\prime})}\qquad\text{for every $\ell<\ell^{\prime}$}

and, as
\ell\to\infty
, the set
B^{(\ell)}
converges a.s. to
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{o}
, the set of vertices of
\mathcal{T}
whose ancestral path ends at the root
o
,
i.e.
, the genealogical component of
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{o}
containing the root. This proves the local-limit statement in Theorem
5
. Furthermore, recalling the notation

m_{\ell}:=\min\{|v|:\ v\in R^{(\ell)}\}

for the minimum height of the red vertices, we find that

m_{\infty}:=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}m_{\ell}=\min\{|v|:\ v\notin\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{o}\}.

Now, by Theorem
3
, the random variable
m_{\infty}
is almost surely finite. Moreover, Proposition
17
implies that

\mathbb{P}(m_{\infty}=1)>0.

This proves statement (
6
) of Theorem
5
.

We now turn to the study of

M_{\ell}:=\max\{|v|:\ v\in B^{(\ell)}\}.

Again, we have

M_{\infty}:=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}M_{\ell}=\max\{|v|:\ v\in\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{o}\},

and, by Item 2(b) of Theorem
3
, the component
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{o}
is almost surely infinite. Consequently,
M_{\infty}=\infty
a.s. This completes the part of Theorem
5
which follows from local convergence and from the infinite-volume genealogical picture.
It remains only to prove the last part of Theorem
5
, namely that, with high probability, the blue vertices reach the leaves of
\mathcal{T}^{(\ell)}
as
\ell\to\infty
,
i.e.
,

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(M_{\ell}=\ell-1)=1,

(25)

which is exactly the estimate (
5
), restated here for convenience. This estimate is of a different nature: it concerns the behavior of the coloring near the boundary of
\mathcal{T}^{(\ell)}
, which escapes to infinity, and therefore cannot be read from the convergence of any fixed neighborhood of the root.

6
The blue vertices reach the leaves

This last section is devoted solely to the proof of (
25
), and hence completes the proof of Theorem
5
. It is somewhat standalone: rather than reconstructing colors through backward genealogical explorations, as in the previous sections, we follow the evolution of the coloring process forward in time.

The proof uses a recursive argument on finite descendant subtrees and a coupling with a supercritical Galton–Watson process. For this reason, it is convenient to work with a generic finite rooted
K
-ary tree
T
instead of
\mathcal{T}^{(\ell)}
. The only discrepancy between the two trees is at the global root, which has
K+1
children in
\mathcal{T}^{(\ell)}
and only
K
children in
T
; this first-generation difference will play no role, so we will work with
T
from now on to avoid having to distinguish the case of the root vertex.

We use the following notation throughout the proof.

•

T
denotes a finite rooted
K
-ary tree, with root
o
and height
|T|
. We write
\partial T
for its set of leaves, and
|v|
for the height of a vertex
v\in T
.

•

For a vertex
v\in T
, we denote by
T_{v}
the subtree of
T
rooted at
v
, consisting of all its descendants. Thus
T_{v}
is itself a rooted
K
-ary tree of height
|T|-|v|
. Whenever this notation is applied to a rooted subtree, heights are understood relative to the root of that subtree.

•

For two disjoint subsets
B_{0},R_{0}\subset T
, we consider the coloring process on
T
with blue seeds in
B_{0}
and red seeds in
R_{0}
. The vertices in
B_{0}\cup R_{0}
have arrival time
0
, while the variables
(\tau_{u})_{u\notin B_{0}\cup R_{0}}
are i.i.d. exponential random variables with parameter
1
. For
t\geq 0
, we denote by
B_{t}
(resp.
R_{t}
) the set of vertices colored blue (resp. red) up to time
t
.

•

Finally,
X^{T}_{1},X^{T}_{2},\ldots
denote the vertices of
T\setminus\{o\}
whose heights are strictly smaller than
|T|/2
, ordered according to their coloring times in increasing order.

Note that, once
v
has been colored, it separates the subtree
T_{v}
from the rest of the tree: conditionally on the state
(B_{\tau_{v}}\cap T_{v},R_{\tau_{v}}\cap T_{v})
, the evolution of the coloring inside
T_{v}
after time
\tau_{v}
depends only on the clocks
(\tau_{w})_{w\in T_{v}}
and on the increasing orderings
((z_{j}^{w})_{j\geq 1})_{w\in T_{v}}
, and is therefore independent of the exterior.

The event
\mathcal{A}_{i}
defined below plays the role of a “good event” for the recursive step: it says that
X_{i}^{T}
becomes a blue vertex near the middle of the tree, while the part of its descendant subtree that has already been colored remains confined to a bounded layer near the leaves, which enables the recursion to be applied in
T_{X_{i}^{T}}
. More precisely, for
C,H\geq 0
and
i\geq 1
, we define the event
\mathcal{A}_{i}:=\mathcal{A}_{i}(T,C,H,B_{0},R_{0})
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{i}:=\Bigl\{

\displaystyle X^{T}_{i}\text{ is colored blue},

(26)

\displaystyle|T|/2-H<|X^{T}_{i}|<|T|/2,

\displaystyle\text{at time }(\tau_{X^{T}_{i}})^{-},\ T_{X^{T}_{i}}\text{ contains at most }C\text{ colored non-leaf vertices,}

\displaystyle\text{and all these vertices are at distance at most }2H\text{ from the leaves}\Bigr\}.

6.1
Coupling with a supercritical Galton–Watson process

The next proposition is the key ingredient in the proof. It states that with high probability one can find two disjoint descendant subtrees in which the good event
\mathcal{A}_{i}
occurs. Thanks to the independence observation mentioned previously, the coloring can then be restarted in these subtrees, which will allow us to compare the growth of the blue region with a supercritical Galton–Watson process.

Proposition 26
.

Recall the definition of the events
\mathcal{A}_{i}
given in (
26
).
Let
\eta>0
. There exist
H,C,n_{0}\geq 0
such that, for all
|T|\geq n_{0}
, and for any choice of vertices
w_{1},\ldots,w_{C}
of height at least
|T|-2H
, starting from the initial blue seed
B_{0}=\{o\}
and red seeds
R_{0}=\partial T\cup\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{C}\}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\exists i<j,\ T_{X^{T}_{i}}\cap T_{X^{T}_{j}}=\emptyset\ \text{ and }\ \mathcal{A}_{i}\cap\mathcal{A}_{j}\bigr)\geq 1-\eta.

(27)

See Figure
10
for an illustration of the initial condition and of the event
\mathcal{A}_{1}
.

Figure 10:
Illustration of the initial condition in Proposition
26
and of the event
\mathcal{A}_{1}
, with
C=3
. If
\mathcal{A}_{1}
occurs, then at time
t=\tau_{X_{1}^{T}}
, at most
C
colored non-leaf vertices lie in the subtree
T_{X_{1}^{T}}
, and they are all close (at distance at most
2H
) from its leaves.

We first explain how Proposition
26
implies (
25
) and hence completes the proof of Theorem
5
, before proving the proposition itself.

Proof of (
25
) using Proposition
26
.

Set

\mathcal{C}_{i,j}:=\mathcal{C}_{i,j}(T,C,H,B_{0},R_{0}):=\bigl\{T_{X^{T}_{i}}\cap T_{X^{T}_{j}}=\emptyset\ \text{and}\ \mathcal{A}_{i}\cap\mathcal{A}_{j}\bigr\}.

Proposition
26
asserts that, for suitable choices of
(T,C,H,B_{0},R_{0})
, with high probability there exist indices
i<j
such that
\mathcal{C}_{i,j}
occurs.

Fix
\eta\in(0,1/4)
and choose
H,C,n_{0}\geq 0
such that (
27
) holds. By possibly increasing
n_{0}
, we may assume that
C<K^{n_{0}-2H-1}
and
n_{0}/2-2H>2H
. Consider the following process
(\mathcal{V}_{l})_{l\geq 0}
taking values in the subsets of
T
:

•

\mathcal{V}_{0}=\{o\}
. If there exist
i<j
such that
\mathcal{C}_{i,j}(T,C,H,\{o\},\partial T)
occurs, we set
\mathcal{V}_{1}:=\{X^{T}_{i},X^{T}_{j}\}
and call
X^{T}_{i},X^{T}_{j}
the children of
o
. Otherwise, we set
\mathcal{V}_{1}=\emptyset
.

By definition of the event
\mathcal{A}_{i}
, for any vertex
v\in\mathcal{V}_{1}
, at time
(\tau_{v})^{-}
, the subtree
T_{v}
contains at most
C
colored vertices that are not leaves and have height at least
|T_{v}|-2H
. Note that these vertices must be red, since they are at distance at most
2H
from the leaves and at distance at least
|T|/2-2H\geq n_{0}-2H>2H
from any blue vertex. Hence
B_{\tau_{v}}\cap T_{v}=\{v\}
.
Moreover, for
v_{1}\neq v_{2}
in
\mathcal{V}_{1}
, we have
T_{v_{1}}\cap T_{v_{2}}=\emptyset
. Thus, conditionally on
(B_{\tau_{v}},R_{\tau_{v}})_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{1}}
, the processes

\bigl((B_{t}\cap T_{v})_{t\geq\tau_{v}},(R_{t}\cap T_{v})_{t\geq\tau_{v}}\bigr),\quad v\in\mathcal{V}_{1}

are independent.

•

Assume that
\mathcal{V}_{l}=\{U_{1}^{(l)},\ldots,U_{\sharp\mathcal{V}_{l}}^{(l)}\}
. For each
v\in\mathcal{V}_{l}
, consider the coloring process on
T_{v}
for
t\geq\tau_{v}
. By induction, these processes are independent for
v\in\mathcal{V}_{l}
. Each of them starts from an initial configuration where only the root
v
is blue, while the red vertices are the leaves together with at most
C
additional vertices, each located at distance at most
2H
from the leaves.

If there exist
i<j
such that
\mathcal{C}_{i,j}(T_{v},C,H,\{v\},R_{\tau_{v}}\cap T_{v})
occurs, we declare
\{X^{T_{v}}_{i},X^{T_{v}}_{j}\}
to be the children of
v
. We then define
\mathcal{V}_{l+1}
as the union of the children of all vertices
v\in\mathcal{V}_{l}
.

By induction, we have that if
v\in\mathcal{V}_{l}
, then

\frac{|T|}{2^{l}}\leq|T_{v}|\leq\frac{|T|}{2^{l}}+2H.

Let
L\in\mathbb{N}
be such that

\frac{|T|}{2^{L}}\geq n_{0}>\frac{|T|}{2^{L+1}}

that is,

L:=\left\lfloor\frac{\log(|T|/n_{0})}{\log 2}\right\rfloor.

Thus, for every
v\in\mathcal{V}_{L}
, we have

n_{0}\leq|T_{v}|\leq 2n_{0}+2H.

Using Proposition
26
, we see that
(\sharp\mathcal{V}_{l})_{l\leq L}
stochastically dominates a Galton–Watson process
(Z_{l})_{l\leq L}
starting from
Z_{0}=1
with offspring distribution

\mu(0)=\eta,\qquad\mu(2)=1-\eta.

Since
\eta<1/4
, this Galton–Watson process is supercritical. It survives with probability
(1-2\eta)/(1-\eta)
, and for
l
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\bigl(Z_{l}>(2(1-2\eta))^{l}\mid Z_{l}>0\bigr)\geq 1-\eta.

Hence, if
|T|
is large enough, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sharp\mathcal{V}_{L}\geq(2(1-2\eta))^{L}\bigr)\geq\mathbb{P}\bigl(Z_{L}>(2(1-2\eta))^{L}\bigr)\geq 1-2\eta.

Set
\beta:=\frac{\log(2(1-2\eta))}{\log 2}>0
so that

(2(1-2\eta))^{L}=2^{\beta L}\geq\left(2^{\frac{\log(|T|/n_{0})}{\log 2}-1}\right)^{\beta}=\left(\frac{|T|}{2n_{0}}\right)^{\beta}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sharp\mathcal{V}_{L}\geq\left(\frac{|T|}{2n_{0}}\right)^{\beta}\right)\geq 1-2\eta.

This shows that, with probability at least
1-2\eta
, there exist at least
(|T|/2n_{0})^{\beta}
disjoint subtrees of height between
n_{0}
and
2n_{0}+2H
such that, in each of them, the coloring process colors the root
v
blue and, at the time when it becomes blue, the subtree contains at most
C
red vertices that are not leaves and have height at least
|T_{v}|-2H
. Each of these subtrees then evolves independently.

Using the facts that
C<K^{n_{0}-2H-1}
and
|T_{v}|\geq n_{0}
, we obtain that the number of vertices in
T_{v}
at height
|T_{v}|-2H
is strictly larger than
C
. Therefore, at time
\tau_{v}
, there necessarily exists a path from the root
v
to a leaf consisting entirely of uncolored vertices (except, of course, for the root and the leaf). Hence, the probability that no blue vertex reaches distance one from the leaves is strictly less than one. Since the sizes of these
(|T|/2n_{0})^{\beta}
trees are uniformly bounded, we in fact obtain a uniform upper bound
\alpha(n_{0},H,C)<1
for the probability that a given tree has no blue vertex at distance one from the leaves.

Thus, writing
M_{T}
for the maximal height reached by a blue vertex in the coloring process on
T
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(M_{T}<|T|-1)\leq 2\eta+\alpha(n_{0},H,C)^{(|T|/2n_{0})^{\beta}},

which can be made arbitrarily small as
|T|\to\infty
, provided that
\eta
is chosen sufficiently small.

∎

Remark 27
.

The proof that the blue vertices reach the leaves presented here is rather intricate, and the definition of the event
\mathcal{A}_{i}
used to run the recursion may seem overcomplicated at first, but this complexity is necessary. For instance, requiring that the whole subtree
T_{X_{i}^{T}}
be uncolored at time
(\tau_{X_{i}^{T}})^{-}
would be too restrictive and prevent the recursion from being applied. Indeed, after one step of the construction, the descendant subtrees naturally inherit a bounded number of red vertices close to their leaves. The event
\mathcal{A}_{i}
allows precisely this boundary layer and keeps the class of admissible initial configurations stable under the recursion.

6.2
Existence of a blue vertex at height around
|T|/2

In order to prove Proposition
26
, we first establish a weaker version by showing that there exists
N
such that, with high probability, one of the first
N
non-root vertices of height smaller than
|T|/2
to be colored is colored blue and has an almost uncolored subtree in front of it. This is the content of Proposition

below, which will later be strengthened to Proposition

.

Proposition 28
.

Let
\eta>0
. Then, there exist
H,C,N,n_{0}\geq 0
such that, if
|T|\geq n_{0}
, then for any choice of vertices
w_{1},\ldots,w_{C}
of height greater than or equal to
|T|-2H
, starting from

and
R_{0}=\partial T\cup\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{C}\}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\forall 1\leq i\leq N,\;\mathcal{A}_{i}^{c})\leq\eta.

Proof of Proposition
28
.

For
v\in T
and
r,h\geq 0
, we denote

B(v,r):=\{w\in T:\ d(v,w)\leq r\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad B_{\geq h}(v,r):=\{w\in T:\ d(v,w)\leq r\ \text{and}\ |w|\geq h\},

, respectively.
For

their most recent common ancestor.

denotes the set of initial red vertices, and that

enumerates the vertices of

whose heights are strictly smaller than
|T|/2
, ordered according to their coloring times. Since

.
We now introduce the following events.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}=\mathcal{E}_{1}(T,N,H,C,R_{0})

Moreover, on the event
\mathcal{O}_{i}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
, we have
|X_{i}|>|T|/2-H
and no clock has rung in
B(X_{i},|X_{i}|)
before time
\tau_{X_{i}}
. In particular, any clock that has rung in
T_{X_{i}}
before time
\tau_{X_{i}}
must correspond to a vertex at height at least
2|X_{i}|\geq|T|-2H
.
Hence, by definition of
\mathcal{F}_{i}
, on the event

\mathcal{F}_{i}^{c}\cap\mathcal{O}_{i}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
,
at most
C
vertices have rung in
T_{X_{i}}
before time
\tau_{X_{i}}
, and all of them lie at distance at most
2H
from the leaves. Using additionally
\mathcal{E}_{3}
, which ensures that the initial red vertices in
T_{X_{i}}
are only leaves, we obtain

\mathcal{F}_{i}^{c}\cap\mathcal{O}_{i}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{3}\subset\mathcal{Z}_{i}.

Combining the previous observations, we deduce that

\mathcal{F}_{i}^{c}\cap\mathcal{O}_{i}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{3}\subset\mathcal{A}_{i},

which implies

\mathcal{A}_{i}^{c}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}\cap\mathcal{E}_{3}\subset\mathcal{F}_{i}\cup\mathcal{O}_{i}.

Hence,

\mathcal{E}_{4}\subset\Bigl\{\forall i\leq N,\;\mathcal{F}_{i}\cup\mathcal{O}_{i}\Bigr\}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}.

This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Let us now derive an upper bound on the probability of

\{\forall i\leq N,\;\mathcal{F}_{i}\cup\mathcal{O}_{i}\}\cap\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
.
Recall that
\tau_{v}
denotes the coloring time of a vertex
v
. We define the
\sigma
-algebra

\mathcal{H}:=\sigma\bigl(\{\tau_{v}:\ |v|<|T|/2\}\bigr),

so that
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{E}_{3}\in\mathcal{H}
. For
i\leq N
, we denote by

\tau_{i}
the coloring time of
X_{i}
, i.e.
\tau_{i}=\tau_{X_{i}}
. Note that
\tau_{i}
is
\mathcal{H}
-measurable.
We observe that for any
i\leq N
and any
u\in B_{\geq|T|/2}(X_{i},|X_{i}|)
, we have

2|u\wedge X_{i}|=|u|+|X_{i}|-d(u,X_{i})\geq|u|\geq\frac{|T|}{2}.

Moreover, if
u\in T_{X_{i}}
, then
|u\wedge X_{i}|=|X_{i}|
. Hence, assuming
H<|T|/4
, on the event
\mathcal{E}_{2}
we obtain that for all

u\in B_{\geq|T|/2}(X_{i},|X_{i}|)\cup T_{X_{i}}
,

|u\wedge X_{i}|>\frac{|T|}{8}.

In particular, on
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
, for any
i\neq j\leq N
, the sets

B_{\geq|T|/2}(X_{i},|X_{i}|)\cup T_{X_{i}}
and

B_{\geq|T|/2}(X_{j},|X_{j}|)\cup T_{X_{j}}

are disjoint. Therefore, conditionally on
\mathcal{H}
and on
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
, the events
\mathcal{F}_{i}\cup\mathcal{O}_{i}
,
i\leq N
, are independent. Besides, we have

\sharp B_{\geq|T|/2}(X_{i},|X_{i}|)\leq(K+1)K^{|X_{i}|}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sharp T_{X_{i}}\leq K^{|T|/2+H+1}.

Hence, conditionally on
\mathcal{H}
and on the event
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}
, the time until the first clock rings in
B_{\geq|T|/2}(X_{i},|X_{i}|)
,
namely

\min\{\tau_{v}:\ v\in B_{\geq|T|/2}(X_{i},|X_{i}|)\},

is stochastically lower bounded by an exponential random variable with parameter
(K+1)K^{|X_{i}|}
. Moreover, the number of clocks that have rung before time
\tau_{i}
in
T_{X_{i}}
is stochastically upper bounded by a binomial random variable with parameters
\bigl(K^{|T|/2+H+1},\,1-e^{-\tau_{i}}\bigr)
. Let
e_{h}
denote an exponential random variable with parameter
(K+1)K^{h}
and let
Y_{t}
be a binomial random variable with parameters
\bigl(K^{|T|/2+H+1},\,1-e^{-t}\bigr)
. We obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{4}\mid\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1_{\{\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(e_{|X_{i}|}\leq\tau_{i}\mbox{ or }Y_{\tau_{i}}\geq C\mid\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1_{\{\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\Big(1-\mathbb{P}(e_{|X_{i}|}>\tau_{i}\mid\mathcal{H})\mathbb{P}(Y_{\tau_{i}}<C\mid\tau_{i})\Big).

We write, for a fixed
A>0
,

\mathbb{P}(Y_{\tau_{i}}<C\mid\tau_{i})\geq 1_{\{\tau_{i}\leq AK^{-|T|/2}\}}\mathbb{P}(Y_{AK^{-|T|/2}}<C).

We set
q_{A,C,|T|}:=\mathbb{P}(Y_{AK^{-|T|/2}}<C)
so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{4}\mid\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1_{\{\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-\exp(-(K+1)K^{|X_{i}|}\tau_{i})q_{A,C,|T|}1_{\{\tau_{i}\leq AK^{-|T|/2}\}}\right).

Let
m
denote the largest integer strictly smaller than
|T|/2
, and let
T_{m}^{*}
be the set of vertices of
T\setminus\{o\}
whose height is at most
m
. We have

\sharp T_{m}^{*}=\frac{K(K^{m}-1)}{K-1}.

Note that
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
are
N
distinct vertices of
T_{m}^{*}
chosen uniformly at random. Moreover, the random variables
(\tau_{i})_{i\leq N}
and
(X_{i})_{i\leq N}
are independent, and
(\tau_{i}-\tau_{i-1})_{i\leq N}
are independent exponential random variables with parameters
\sharp T_{m}^{*}-i
. We obtain, for any
i\geq 1
and
h<m
,

\mathbb{P}(|X_{i}|=m-h)=\frac{K^{m-h}}{\sharp T_{m}^{*}}=\frac{(K-1)K^{m-h}}{K(K^{m}-1)}\geq\frac{1}{2K^{h}}.

Moreover, for any
h_{i}\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}((|X_{1}|,\ldots,|X_{N}|)=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{N}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sharp T_{m}^{*}}{\sharp T_{m}^{*}-N}\right)^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(|X_{i}|=h_{i})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{N}{K^{m}}\right)^{-N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(|X_{i}|=h_{i})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{N}{K^{|T|/2-1}}\right)^{-N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(|X_{i}|=h_{i}).

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{4}\mid(\tau_{i})_{i\leq N})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{h_{1},\ldots,h_{N}\geq 1}\mathbb{P}((|X_{1}|,\ldots,|X_{N}|)=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{N}))\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-e^{-\tau_{i}(K+1)K^{h_{i}}}q_{A,C,|T|}1_{\{\tau_{i}\leq AK^{-|T|/2}\}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{N}{K^{|T|/2-1}}\right)^{-N}\sum_{h_{1},\ldots,h_{N}\geq 0}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathbb{P}(|X_{i}|=h_{i})(1-e^{-\tau_{i}(K+1)K^{h_{i}}}q_{A,C,|T|}1_{\{\tau_{i}\leq AK^{-|T|/2}\}})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{N}{K^{|T|/2-1}}\right)^{-N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left(1-e^{-\tau_{i}(K+1)K^{|X_{i}|}}q_{A,C,|T|}1_{\{\tau_{i}\leq AK^{-|T|/2}\}}\mid(\tau_{i})_{i\leq N}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{N}{K^{|T|/2-1}}\right)^{-N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-\tau_{i}(K+1)K^{|X_{i}|}}\mid(\tau_{i})_{i\leq N}\right)q_{A,C,|T|}1_{\{\tau_{i}\leq AK^{-|T|/2}\}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{N}{K^{|T|/2-1}}\right)^{-N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-\left(\sum_{h=0}^{L}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-\tau_{i}(K+1)K^{m-h}}\right)q_{A,C,|T|}1_{\{\tau_{i}\leq AK^{-|T|/2}\}}\right)

valid for any
L<m
(recall that
m
is the largest integer strictly smaller than
|T|/2
).

We now use that the random variables
(\tau_{i}-\tau_{i-1})_{i\leq N}
are independent exponential random variables with parameters
\sharp T_{m}^{*}-i
. If
i\leq\sharp T_{m}^{*}/2
, we have

\sharp T_{m}^{*}-i\geq\frac{\sharp T_{m}^{*}}{2}\geq K^{m-1}\geq K^{|T|/2-2}.

Thus, if
N\leq K^{m}/2
, the sequence
(K^{|T|/2-2}(\tau_{i}-\tau_{i-1}))_{i\leq N}
is stochastically smaller than a sequence
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\leq N}
of i.i.d. exponential random variables with parameter 1. Let
\sigma_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{i}\varepsilon_{k}
.
We get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{4})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{N}{K^{|T|/2-1}}\right)^{-N}\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-\left(\sum_{h=0}^{L}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-\tau_{i}(K+1)K^{|T|/2-h}}\right)q_{A,C,|T|}1_{\{\tau_{i}\leq AK^{-|T|/2}\}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{N}{K^{|T|/2-1}}\right)^{-N}\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-\left(\sum_{h=0}^{L}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-\sigma_{i}(K+1)K^{2-h}}\right)q_{A,C,|T|}1_{\{\sigma_{i}\leq AK^{-2}\}}\right)\right).

We now use the following lemma, whose proof is postponed until the end of this section.

Lemma 30
.

Let
\eta>0
. Then, there exist integers
N,L>0
such that if
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\leq N}
are i.i.d. exponential random variables with parameter 1 and
\sigma_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{i}\varepsilon_{k}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-\sum_{h=0}^{L}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-\sigma_{i}(K+1)K^{2-h}}\right)\right)\leq\eta/5.

(31)

We finish the proof of Proposition
28
assuming that Lemma
30
holds. Fix
\eta>0
and choose
N,L
such that (
31
) holds. Since for each
i\leq N
,
1_{\{\sigma_{i}\leq AK^{-2}\}}
tends a.s. to 1 as
A
tends to infinity, by the dominated convergence theorem, there exists
A
large enough such that

\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-1_{\{\sigma_{i}\leq AK^{-2}\}}\sum_{h=0}^{L}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-\sigma_{i}(K+1)K^{2-h}}\right)\right)\leq\eta/4.

Using (
29
), fix now
H
such that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{2}^{c})\leq N\frac{1}{K^{H}}\leq\eta/3.

Recall that

q_{A,C,|T|}:=\mathbb{P}(Y_{AK^{-|T|/2}}<C)\mbox{ where }Y_{AK^{-|T|/2}}\sim\mbox{Bin}(K^{|T|/2+H+1},1-e^{-AK^{-|T|/2}}).

Thus, we get that

1-q_{A,C,|T|}=\mathbb{P}(Y_{AK^{-|T|/2}}\geq C)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}(Y_{AK^{-|T|/2}})}{C}\leq\frac{AK^{H+1}}{C}.

This shows that, for fixed
A
and
H
, we can find
C
large enough such that
q_{A,C,|T|}
is close to 1. Using again the dominated convergence theorem, we deduce the existence of a
C
such that

\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-\left(\sum_{h=0}^{L}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-\sigma_{i}(K+1)K^{2-h}}\right)q_{A,C,|T|}1_{\{\sigma_{i}\leq AK^{-2}\}}\right)\right)\leq\eta/3,

and so, for this choice of
N,H,C
, we obtain
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{4})\leq\eta/2
if
|T|
is large enough. Combining this with (
28
) and (
30
), we conclude that for this choice of
H,C,N
and for
|T|
large enough,

\mathbb{P}(\forall 1\leq i\leq N,\;\mathcal{A}_{i}^{c})\leq\eta.

This is exactly the statement of Proposition
28
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
30
.

Let us note that
\sigma_{i}
is the sum of
i
i.i.d. exponential random variables; thus
\sigma_{i}/i
tends a.s. to 1 as
i
tends to infinity. Hence, we can find
D>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(\exists i\geq 1,K^{2}(K+1)\sigma_{i}\geq Di)\leq\eta/10.

With this choice for
D
, we get

\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-\sum_{h=0}^{L}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-\sigma_{i}(K+1)K^{2-h}}\right)\right)\leq\eta/10+\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-\sum_{h=0}^{L}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-DiK^{-h}}\right).

Define

\Pi_{L,N}:=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-\sum_{h=0}^{L}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-DiK^{-h}}\right)

and

\Pi_{\infty}:=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-DiK^{-h}}\right).

Since
\Pi_{L,N}
tends to
\Pi_{\infty}
when
L,N
tend to infinity, it only remains to show that
\Pi_{\infty}=0
, as this will imply that, for
L
and
N
large enough,
\Pi_{L,N}\leq\eta/10
, as required. Indeed, we have

\log\Pi_{\infty}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\log\left(1-\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-DiK^{-h}}\right)\leq-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-DiK^{-h}},

and

\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-DiK^{-h}}=\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}\frac{e^{-DK^{-h}}}{1-e^{-DK^{-h}}}=\infty,

since

\lim_{h\to\infty}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}\frac{e^{-DK^{-h}}}{1-e^{-DK^{-h}}}=\frac{1}{2D}.

This yields that
\Pi_{\infty}=0
, and so
\Pi_{L,N}
tends to 0 as
N
and
L
tend to infinity.
∎

6.3
Proof of Proposition
26

We first prove a two-window version of Proposition
28
: with high probability, one can find a good vertex among the first
N
candidates and another one among the candidates with indices between
N+1
and
N^{\prime}
. The final step, carried out afterwards, will be to show that these two good vertices may be chosen with disjoint descendant subtrees, as required in Proposition
26
.

Proposition 31
.

Let
\eta>0
. Then, there exist
H,C,N,N^{\prime},n_{0}\geq 0
such that, if
|T|\geq n_{0}
, then for any choice of vertices
w_{1},\ldots,w_{C}
of height greater than or equal to
|T|-2H
, starting from
B_{0}=\{o\}
and
R_{0}=\partial T\cup\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{C}\}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\forall 1\leq i\leq N,\;\mathcal{A}_{i}^{c})\leq\eta.

(32)

\mathbb{P}(\forall N<i\leq N^{\prime},\;\mathcal{A}_{i}^{c})\leq\eta.

(33)

Proof.

For
N<N^{\prime}
, we introduce the following events:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{1}=\mathcal{E}_{1}(T,N^{\prime},H,C,R_{0})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{\forall i\neq j\leq N^{\prime},|X_{i}\wedge X_{j}|<|T|/8\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{2}=\mathcal{E}_{2}(T,N^{\prime},H,C,R_{0})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{\forall i\leq N^{\prime},|X_{i}|>|T|/2-H\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{3}=\mathcal{E}_{3}(T,N^{\prime},H,C,R_{0})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{\forall i\leq N^{\prime},\forall k\leq C,X_{i}\notin B(w_{k},|T|/2+H)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{4}=\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{4}(T,N,N^{\prime},H,C,R_{0})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{\forall i\leq N,\mathcal{A}_{i}^{c}\}\cap{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{1}}\cap{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{2}}\cap{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{3}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{5}=\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{5}(T,N,N^{\prime},H,C,R_{0})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{\forall N<i\leq N^{\prime},\mathcal{A}_{i}^{c}\}\cap{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{1}}\cap{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{2}}\cap{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{3}}.

Note that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{4})\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{4})
. Moreover, arguing exactly as in the proof of Proposition
28
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{5})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{N^{\prime}}{K^{|T|/2-1}}\right)^{-N^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=N+1}^{N^{\prime}}\left(1-\left(\sum_{h=0}^{L}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-\sigma_{i}(K+1)K^{2-h}}\right)q_{A,C,|T|}\,1_{\{\sigma_{i}\leq AK^{-2}\}}\right)\right],

where

\sigma_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{i}\varepsilon_{k},

and
(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\leq N^{\prime}}
are i.i.d. exponential random variables with parameter
1
.
Let
\eta>0
. Choose
N
,
N^{\prime}
, and
L
with
N^{\prime}>N
such that

\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1-\sum_{h=0}^{L}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-\sigma_{i}(K+1)K^{2-h}}\right)\right)\leq\frac{\eta}{5},

and

\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{i=N+1}^{N^{\prime}}\left(1-\sum_{h=0}^{L}\frac{1}{2K^{h}}e^{-\sigma_{i}(K+1)K^{2-h}}\right)\right)\leq\frac{\eta}{5}.

Finally, choosing
A
,
H
, and
C
large enough exactly as in the proof of Proposition
28
, we obtain that, for this choice of parameters
(N,N^{\prime},H,C)
, both (
32
) and (
33
) hold whenever
|T|
is large enough.

∎

Proof of Proposition
26
.

Let
H
,
C
,
N
,
N^{\prime}
, and
n_{0}
be chosen so that Proposition
31
holds. Then, for every
|T|\geq n_{0}
, with probability at least
1-2\eta
, there exist indices
i<N<j
such that both
\mathcal{A}_{i}
and
\mathcal{A}_{j}
occur. Recall that
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
denote the vertices of
T\setminus\{o\}
of height smaller than
|T|/2
, ordered according to their coloring times. We have

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\exists\,i<j\leq N^{\prime}:T_{X_{i}}\cap T_{X_{j}}\neq\emptyset\bigr)\leq N^{\prime 2}\,\mathbb{P}\bigl(T_{X_{1}}\cap T_{X_{2}}\neq\emptyset\bigr)\leq\frac{|T|\,N^{\prime 2}}{K^{|T|/2-1}}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\forall\,i<j\leq N^{\prime},\ T_{X_{i}}\cap T_{X_{j}}=\emptyset\bigr)\longrightarrow 1\qquad\text{as }|T|\to\infty.

Combining this estimate with Proposition
31
, we obtain that, for
|T|
sufficiently large,

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\exists\,i<j,\ T_{X_{i}}\cap T_{X_{j}}=\emptyset\text{ and }\mathcal{A}_{i}\cap\mathcal{A}_{j}\bigr)\geq 1-3\eta.

This completes the proof of Proposition
26
.
∎
