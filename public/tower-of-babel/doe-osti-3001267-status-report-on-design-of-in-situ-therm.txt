 SUMMARY
Nuclear fuel encounters severe thermomechanical environments in which its mechanical response is
determined by its microstructure, temperature and stress level histories. Simulating the response of such
microstructures is crucial for predicting both performance and transient fuel mechanical responses and
experimental verification of such predictions is therefore of great interest. While most of the deformation
in a nuclear fuel rod occurs in the cladding, deformation of the fuel itself is still of interest with deformation
mechanisms at operating temperature and above including creep, swelling, cracking as well as pellet-clad
interaction. Characterization of these properties and understanding of the underlying deformation
phenomena at operating or excursion temperatures is therefore of great importance for development and
ultimately licensing of improved and novel nuclear fuel forms.
Diffraction techniques offer unique insight on the atomistic (e.g. crystal structure) and microstructure (e.g.
phase transformations, texture, defects) length scales and have a long history of providing unique data to
inform relevant deformation models that enable the required predictive capabilities. For example,
dislocations lead to diffraction peak broadening that can be characterized to estimate the dislocation density
and study the role of dislocations on the deformation while measuring lattice strains allows to studie load
sharing in two phase materials.
In this report the requirements for a sample environment for high temperature deformation of nuclear fuels
are defined. The HIPPO neutron time-of-flight diffractometer at LANSCE will host this sample
environment and is also described. This instrument covers diffraction angles from 140° to 40° and is also
equipped with an event-mode neutron imaging detector system, enabling energy-resolved neutron imaging
in parallel with the diffraction that could measure sample temperature from Doppler broadening of neutron
absorption resonances or measure pore densities from changes in the attenuation. Designs of devices to
characterize thermomechanical properties of nuclear fuel without diffraction are also considered to guide
the design. While this report is focused on applications for nuclear fuels, the device can also characterize
cladding, moderator or structural materials and therefore contribute to other fields of research and
development for advanced reactors. The temperatures planned to be reached are above 2000℃, thus
enabling characterization of LWR reactor fuels under accident scenarios but also reaching temperatures of
fuels developed for nuclear thermal propulsion and providing opportunities to characterize those. In
conjunction with the energy-resolved neutron imaging detector, this setup would allow to measure neutron
cross-sections at high temperatures, filling a gap towards development of reactors operating at high
temperatures.

1. Introduction
Nuclear fuel encounters severe thermomechanical environments in which its mechanical response is
determined by its microstructure, temperature and stress level histories [1]. Simulating the response of such
microstructures is crucial for predicting both performance and transient fuel mechanical responses [2] and
experimental verification of such predictions is therefore of great interest. While most of the deformation
in a nuclear fuel rod occurs in the cladding, deformation of the fuel itself is still of interest [3]. Fuel
deformations at operating temperature and above include creep, swelling, cracking as well as pellet-clad
interaction with “viscous flow” or vacancy flow being the dominant deformation mechanism at high
temperatures [3]. Deformation behavior is a crucial parameter for selection of a fuel for a particular
application [4] and nuclear fuels with irradiation-resistant crystal structures that offer low stiffness, ample
creep, and an ability to accommodate deformation are typically desirable [5]. Characterization of these
properties and understanding of the underlying phenomena at operating or excursion temperatures is
therefore of great importance for development and ultimately licensing of improved and novel nuclear fuel
forms. Diffraction techniques offer unique insight on the atomistic (e.g. crystal structure) and
microstructure (e.g. phase transformations, texture, defects) length scales [6]. Diffraction techniques have
a long history of providing unique data to inform relevant deformation models that enable the required
predictive capabilities to in the case of nuclear materials in general and fuel in particular accelerate fuel
development and ultimately licensing [7-15]. For example, dislocations lead to diffraction peak broadening
that can be characterized to estimate the dislocation density [16] and study the role of dislocations on the
deformation. By measuring lattice strains, in two phase materials the load sharing between the phases can
be studied [17-18].
While in situ deformation experiments in a diffraction beamline at ambient conditions are relatively
straightforward, deformation experiments at temperature are much more challenging: Chemical interactions
with the atmosphere of the sample and sample environment at temperature need to be controlled to prevent
e.g. oxidation or even ignition of pyrophoric materials. High vacuum is a typical atmosphere preventing
such interactions. The mechanical properties of the entire load frame at temperature need to be understood
to apply a known load to a sample at elevated temperatures. These requirements result in sample
environments with vacuum enclosures in which load frames are either contained [19] or pistons reach in
through bellows [20]. In a diffraction experiment, incident, transmitted and diffracted beams must
minimally interact with the sample environment, thus maximizing the signal from the sample without
occurrence of diffraction peaks from the sample environment. While in constant-wavelength reactor
neutron diffraction experiments radial collimators can be used, for time-of-flight neutrons at a pulsed
neutron source such as LANSCE, such secondary beam path collimation can only be used for 90° detectors
since otherwise conical collimation would be required which is impractical to fabricate. For a multi-detector
instrument such as HIPPO, any material in the direct and transmitted beam would diffract unless the
material is a so-called zero-scatterer such as vanadium or TiZr alloy.
In this report the requirements for a sample environment for high temperature deformation of nuclear fuels
are defined. The HIPPO neutron time-of-flight diffractometer at LANSCE will host this sample
environment and is also described. This instrument covers diffraction angles from 140° to 40° and is also
equipped with an event-mode neutron imaging detector system, enabling energy-resolved neutron imaging
simultaneously with the diffraction. Designs of devices to characterize thermomechanical properties of
nuclear fuel without diffraction are also considered to guide the design. While this report is focused on
applications for nuclear fuels, the device can also characterize cladding, moderator or structural materials
and therefore contribute to other fields of research and development for advanced reactors. An upgrade of
the existing HIPPO furnace without deformation rig, i.e. with heating only, is also proposed to provide
material characterization at higher temperatures than currently available. The temperatures planned to be
reached are above 2000℃, thus enabling characterization of LWR fuels under accident scenarios but also
reaching temperatures of fuels developed for nuclear thermal propulsion [21] and providing opportunities

to characterize those. In conjunction with the energy-resolved neutron imaging detector, this setup would
allow to measure neutron cross-sections at high temperatures, filling a gap towards development of reactors
operating at high temperatures [22].

2. Thermomechanical Testing Setups for Nuclear Fuel without
 Diffraction in the Literature
To obtain guidance for the design of the setup, some previously reported high temperature deformation
studies of nuclear fuels are reviewed in this section in chronological order. A review of temperature
dependent properties of light water reactor fuels leading to cracking, including deformation behavior at
high temperatures that leads to cracking, is provided by Patnaik [23]. The article “Electrical Resistance
Heating Elements: An Overview”, published by Thermcraft Inc. [24], provides useful information for the
selection of materials for heating elements and heat shields.
Armstrong et al. published creep studies on stoichiometric UO2 in 1962 [25] and non-stoichiometric UO2
in 1963 [26] at temperatures up to 1400°C using center point bending tests at loads up to 110 MPa. Figure
1 shows a schematic of their setup. The atmosphere around the sample was purified hydrogen. The sample
was held by alumina and load was applied via push rods of the same material.

Figure 1: Schematic of the high temperature center point bending test device applied by Armstrong et al. [25,26].

In 1968, Byron published a study on the yield and flow of polycrystalline UO2 at temperatures between
600°C and 2000°C [27]. Molybdenum heating elements with tantalum heat shields heat a sample between
graphite pushrods separated by tungsten plates from the sample in an argon atmosphere. Figure 2 shows a
schematic of the device which is driven by an Instron load frame.

Figure 2: Schematic of the high temperature deformation apparatus developed by Byron [27].

A report by Bohaboy et al. from 1969 [28] describes a device with tungsten plungers compressing a sample
heated by a tungsten mesh with tungsten heat shields and a boron nitride muffle surrounding the sample. A
gas-hydraulic system provided the load with a floating gas/oil piston compensating for changes in the
hydraulic pressure due to deformation of the sample. Nitrogen with 6% H2 was used as a cover gas at a gas
pressure slightly above atmospheric pressure. Strain was measured optically by a cathetometer as well as
with a linear variable differential transformer (LVDT) monitoring movement of the bottom plunger. Test
temperatures up to 1750°C are reported.

In 2002, Dherbey et al. [38] published results of creep deformation of UO2 in a “suitably adapted furnace

Salvo et al. in 2015 used the setup shown in Figure 8, consisting of resistive heating and push rods made
from tungsten and driven by an Instron load frame with maximum load of 50 kN [39]. An atmosphere of
95% Ar/5% H2 surrounded the sample during test up to 1700°C. Besides stress-strain curves at four different
temperatures and four different strain rates between 10-1/s and 10-4/s, the authors also report the shape of
all samples after testing.

Figure 8:Schematic of the high temperature deformation device developed by Salvo et al. [39].

Valdez et al. reported in a 2020 milestone report design, commissioning and application of an Instron driven
high temperature load frame [40]. A water-cooled tungsten mesh heats the sample mounted in the load
frame with lanthanated tungsten (La2O3-W) push rods. They point out the need for precise alignment
(0.0004” parallelism between contact faces was achieved) to achieve uniform deformation of the sample.

09/12/2025                                                                                                 17

To prevent sticking of the sample to the pushrod, mainly for ease of removal and to reduce the friction
between the sample and contacts faces, thin tungsten sheets coated with boron nitride, a high temperature
stable lubricant, were also used for each test. Either vacuum or purified flowing inert gas provide the
atmosphere in the hot zone. Testing was conducted at 1400°C with a 10 kN load cell.
In recent work, Frazer et al. [41] published in 2024 data on elastic modulus, hardness and creep stress
exponents of (U,Ce)O2 compounds measured up to 800°C by nanoindentation. A Hysitron Triboindenter
with hot stage under argon + 6% H2 purge gas was used.
Also in 2024, Butler et al. presented the setup for high temperature compressive testing at LANL’s Fuels
Research Laboratory in a milestone report [42]. This device utilizes RF heating of a tungsten susceptor
within a silica glass tube for atmospheric control. Ti-Zr-Mo alloy is used for push rods with Al2O3 platens
interfacing between pushrods and sample. The maximum load is 10 kN and the system is designed for
sample temperatures up to 2000°C.
Summary: Common themes of the reviewed devices for high temperature deformation of UO2 are tungsten
heating elements used with resistive heating and tungsten heat shields. Vacuum or inert cover gases, often
with H2 as a reducing atmosphere to keep UO2 stoichiometry constant, are typically used. Besides the work
by Slagle et al., test temperatures were below 2000°C. Instron load frames are often used.

3. Pulsed Neutron Diffractometer HIPPO
For time-of-flight neutron diffraction characterization of nuclear fuels, the HIPPO (High Pressure/Preferred
Orientation) beamline at LANSCE is utilized [43,44]. Figure 9(a) provides a schematic of the instrument.
The comparably short distance of 8.83 m between moderator and sample combined with the comparably
large detector coverage of 22.4% of the sphere around the sample [45] provides for efficient neutron
diffraction characterization. A robotic sample changer [46] is used to handle samples inside the instrument
at ambient conditions (Figure 9 (b)). In combination with a 2 mm Cd slit to define a slice of the sample
probed by thermal neutrons, the robotic arm allows to scan rodlets with several pellets in 2 mm slices, thus
providing limited spatial resolution for the diffraction. Data analysis is conducted with the Rietveld method,
fitting every experimental neutron intensity data point measured as a function of time-of-flight to a complex
(several tens of parameters) model taking into account instrument parameters (e.g. time-of-flight to d-
spacing conversion, background, instrument peak broadening), crystallographic parameters (e.g. lattice
parameters, atomic positions, atomic displacement parameters), and microstructural parameters
(texture/preferred orientation, phase fractions, defect concentrations). As an example, Figure 10(a) shows
a Rietveld analysis of neutron diffraction data from a 2 mm slice of a UN/U3Si5 sample inside a stainless
steel rodlet. Figure 10(b) shows the refined unit cell volume (many other refined parameters are omitted
here) of the hexagonal crystal structure of U3Si5 as a function of sample position along the rodlet, resulting
from many Rietveld analyses. The data points for the unit cell volume corresponding to the lowest pellet
are significantly below those of the other pellets, indicating an anomaly for this particular pellet.
Measurement of the crystal orientation distribution (texture or preferred orientation) shows that while the
UN crystals show the expected random crystal orientation distribution (absence of preferred orientation),
the U3Si5 shows a very strong texture consistent with essentially two single crystals within the probed
volume, Figure 10(c). This can be explained by this particular pellet being unintentionally exposed to a
slightly higher temperature during the sintering than the melting point of the U3Si5. Upon cooling, the U3Si5
solidified in an interconnected network surrounding the UN grains, forming the two single crystals seen
now. It is worth pointing out that this pellet, fabricated during the development of synthesis routes for this
particular fuel form, passed visual inspection and did not show any differences in density prior to insertion
into the capsule – only the neutron diffraction analysis identified the anomaly. Anisotropic thermal
expansion along the crystallographic a- and c-axes of the hexagonal crystal lattice of the U3Si5 may lead to
thermal stresses that could cause cracking of this pellet during heating, significantly changing the behavior
during irradiation. This example underlines the value that bulk neutron characterization can provide for pre-

18                                                                                                09/12/2025

irradiation characterization, but also for the development of fabrication routes. Besides pre- and post-
irradiation characterization, neutron diffraction also has significant advantages to characterize crystal and
microstructure evolution of nuclear fuels at temperature, atmosphere, processing route etc., e.g. for separate
effect testing.
Besides ambient condition characterization of nuclear fuels such as (U,Zr)C [47] or U-Mo [48], many high
temperature studies of nuclear fuels were conducted with the HIPPO instrument such as the synthesis of
uranium carbide from UO2 and graphite with temperatures for subsequent uranium carbide characterization
above 2000℃ [49], high temperature behavior of U-Si accident tolerant fuels [50,51], uranium mononitride
[52] and UCl3 [53], texture evolution [54] and phase transformations in U-Zr metallic fuels [55-58], and
precipitation of U4O9 from UO2 [59]. This proven capability to characterize nuclear fuels at high
temperature positions the HIPPO diffractometer as a unique instrument for the planned characterization of
deformation of nuclear fuels at high temperature.

4. Energy-resolved Neutron Imaging
In December 2022 a novel setup based on so-called event-mode cameras was demonstrated in
HIPPO for the first time and refined in January and February of 2024 during the following
LANSCE run cycle. Event-mode imaging detects photons emitted from a scintillator screen,
focused by a lens on an image intensifier with a TimePix chip [60]. The TimePix chip allows
temporal resolution of 1.6 ns, far sufficient for time-of-flight neutron imaging, as well as spatial
resolutions of tens of microns depending on the field of view of the lens (i.e. a larger scintillator
screen results in less resolution than a smaller area). The compact setup allows to collect energy-
resolved neutron imaging data not only on dedicated neutron imaging beam lines but also on the
HIPPO beamline. Figure 11 shows a setup with three cameras (two for diffraction detection to
demonstrate this detector type as a replacement for He-3 detector tubes and one for imaging)
installed in HIPPO. The imaging camera is used to collect resonance imaging data to determine
the isotope concentrations spatially simultaneously with diffraction data for phase and crystal
structure characterization, thus minimizing the total required beam time [61]. Utilizing a lens to
adjust to different field of views allows to utilize these detectors also for larger scintillator areas
and detect diffracted neutrons. This concept was demonstrated, including adapting the Rietveld
code MAUD for the novel data reduction, in these experiments and published in a paper in the
Journal of Applied Crystallography [62]. For DOE/NE related applications this technology
therefore could provide cost-efficient means to build a beamline for both diffraction and energy-
resolved neutron imaging based on the same technology available commercially off-the-shelf,
i.e. without the need to develop data acquisition hard- and software in-house. For HIPPO it
enables characterization of nuclear fuel samples for e.g. isotope densities in fresh and irradiated
nuclear fuels but also to measure the neutron cross-sections at non-ambient conditions such as at
operating or accident temperatures. This characterization can be done simultaneously with the
neutron diffraction and provides otherwise very sparse non-ambient condition experimental data
for cross-sections to inform reactor models.

5. Design Criteria
The main goal of the in-situ deformation device is to enable simultaneous neutron diffraction
characterization during the high temperature deformation. The device therefore must enable unobstructed

09/12/2025                                                                                                  21

pathways for incident and transmitted neutron beams as well as sufficient pathways to obtain diffraction
data. Any material in the direct beam will diffract and if not shielded by radial collimators, these diffracted
neutrons will cause lines in the recorded diffraction patterns that are complicating the analysis and therefore
must be avoided. For time-of-flight neutron diffraction, radial collimation for any diffraction angle different
from 90° is difficult since the Debye-Scherrer cones of the diffracted neutrons would need to be mimicked
for other angles. Such collimation is hard to design and adding them to a sample environment adds to the
challenge. The goal is therefore to minimize interaction of direct and transmitted beam with the sample
environment. For incident and transmitted beams, beam ports covered with vanadium foil can be used. The
diffraction signal from the vanadium is minimal and based on experience with existing sample
environments (furnaces, cryostats) diffraction pattern contamination can be avoided. Vanadium is also an
excellent choice for heat shields and reflectors if needed. All other components, such as sample holders,
thermocouples, susceptor or RF coils, must either be fully kept outside the beam (RF coils) or have holes
at least 50% larger than the beam spot.
Ranges for temperature and load for the in-situ load frame are driven by ranges in deformations maps of
the materials under consideration or other reported testing. While the ranges here are relevant to
deformation of ceramic fuels, in particular UO2, metallic fuels generally have melting points far below and
therefore are automatically included in these ranges. Figure 12 shows a deformation map reported by
Roberts & Voglewede [63] for UO2 with a temperature range up to 2200℃ and a maximum load of ~140
MPa (20,000 psi). The apparatus reported by Slagle et al. [36], Figure 7, allowed to measure creep up to
2600℃ under loads <10 MPa.

Figure 12: Deformation map of 15 µm UO2 of 97% of theoretical density (from [63]).

The choice of heating is typically between resistive heating and RF heating. Since ceramic samples such as
UO2 nuclear fuel cannot be heated directly with RF heating, a typically metallic susceptor has to be used.
While RF heating allows fast heating ramp rates of 100s of ℃ per minute, resistive heating relies on
radiative transfer of the heat from the heating element to the sample and tends to be slower (tens of ℃ per
minute). RF heating may couple with other metallic components of the sample environment, may lead to

22                                                                                                 09/12/2025

inhomogeneous heating of the sample or hot zone (“hot spots”) and requires copper coils which are water
cooled. This cooling water would block diffracted neutrons. Due to these considerations, resistive heating
is favored.
While the apparatus in the Fuels Research Laboratory utilizes RF to heat a tungsten pipe, the apparatus used
by Slage et al. [36] applies current through tungsten to heat the sample. In either case, tungsten will surround
the sample. While a ~25 mm diameter hole for the direct beam will enable access of the beam to the sample,
the transmission for e.g. 5mm tungsten for 𝜆=1Å neutrons is ~62% (calculated with
https://webapps.frm2.tum.de/neutroncalc/). Slagle [36] utilizes 0.5 mm tungsten for a resistive heating
element, resulting in an expected transmission of 95%. Any interaction of the scattered neutrons with
material in the beam path between sample and detector would essentially diffuse the diffracted neutrons
into background. An attenuation of 50% of the diffracted signal would double the count time for each
histogram to achieve similar statistics as for an unobstructed diffraction signal. Attenuation up to 50% is
therefore generally acceptable. Clausen et al. [64,65] report on a heating-only furnace for the Harwell
reactor neutron source that is also based on 0.25 and 0.4 mm thick connected tungsten sheets heated by
resistive heating with a 1000A/10V power supply. This would be commensurate with the existing power
supply of the HIPPO ILL furnace. However, this design requires radial collimators to avoid diffraction from
the tungsten. For use in a time-of-flight instrument such as HIPPO, holes in the heating elements and if
needed in the heat shields for the incident and transmitted neutron beam must be carefully aligned with the
beam ports of the enclosure.
In general, in case the count time during the deformation experiment is too long to characterize a
dynamically evolving deformation state, the sample can be unloaded for a longer measurement [66].

Neutron imaging visualizes the attenuation by the sample which in turn is dependent on the density of the
material. Therefore, if the density changes during a compression test, neutron imaging could quantify such
changes. Figure 14 shows the change of density of a mixed oxide fuel sample during loading at ~2500℃.
Earlier results for isotope density measurements with neutron imaging [67] showed that agreement of 0.1%
with isotope densities reported by mass spectrometry could be achieved. While these results were achieved
with several hours of count time, the task here would be not isotope-specific density measurements but the
overall density of the material. Furthermore, the novel event-mode imaging cameras [60] and the lower
background on the HIPPO beamline have greatly improved this capability [61], putting time-resolved
density measurements simultaneously with the diffraction characterization within reach. For temperature
measurements, uranium resonances are an excellent way of measuring the sample temperature from
Doppler broadening of the absorption resonances [68]. This method has been demonstrated previously for
sample temperature measurements inside a high pressure cell [69,70] and can complement sample
temperature measurements with thermocouples. An energy-resolved neutron imaging detector can collect
epithermal (E>0.4 eV) neutron transmission data. The goal is therefore for a geometry that allows such
energy-resolved neutron imaging measurements. This requires the imaging detector as close as possible to

24                                                                                                                  09/12/2025

the sample since the distance between sample and imaging detector introduces spatial blur scaling linear
with that distance with the L/D ratio of the setup being the slope.

To minimize chemical interactions in air, increasing lifetime of components as well as enabling experiments
in the future with metallic uranium fuels that may be pyrophoric, the setup is designed to be within a vacuum
enclosure. Aluminum alloy only attenuates the diffraction signal by a few percent while provide a
structurally stable enclosure. Previous experience with such sample environments [19,49] indicate that with
turbo pumps 10e-5 Torr vacuums can be achieved. This so-called top-hat will hold the entire setup inside
the HIPPO sample chamber while resting on its 75 cm diameter flange.

6. Current Design Proposal
A high temperature deformation rig was previously developed and commissioned for the HIPPO instrument
and applied to study texture variant selection during phase transformation in Zr-2.5Nb cladding material
[19]. The furnace builds upon vanadium resistive heating elements and heat shells in a clam shell setup
which limits the maximum temperature to ~1000℃ but providing ample beam path for diffracted neutrons.
The maximum applied force is 2.7 kN, resulting in a maximum stress for a 6mm diameter cylinder sample
of ~100 MPa. This meets all design criteria laid out above except for the maximum temperature. We
therefore propose to replace the heating element and possibly the innermost heat shield with tungsten foils
of ~0.5 mm with holes for the incident beam path, raising the maximum temperature to ~2000℃.
Figure 15 shows schematics of the HIPPO instrument with the high temperature deformation rig, illustrating
beam paths for incident and transmitted beams (only passing through vanadium) as well as ample coverage
of the HIPPO detector panels. Figure 16 shows a detailed view of the hot zone with vanadium heating
elements and vanadium heat shields in a clamshell setup that allows to load the samples. We propose to
replace the vanadium heating elements, essentially vanadium foil held by a metal ring, with tungsten heating
elements. To avoid diffraction signal from the tungsten, the foils would require holes. The nominal HIPPO
beam diameter is 10 mm with a 14 mm penumbra. With at least two layers of materials with holes (heating
element and inner heatshield, neutron absorbing ceramic pipes such as B4C with e.g. 11mm inner and 20mm
outer diameter could be inserted into the holes. Alternative locations of such collimators could be the beam
ports or locations closer to the sample to avoid heat conduction from heating elements to heat shields. The

09/12/2025                                                                                                                   25

melting point of B4C is 2350°C. The effect on the homogeneity of the sample temperature of these holes
for steady state operation is deemed small and if a neutron imaging camera can be brought close to the
sample some temperature mapping from spatially resolved Doppler broadening of neutron absorption
resonances could quantify the effect [71].

The current setup uses silica glass pushrods which would melt at ~1600℃ and therefore needs to be
replaced by materials with higher melting points. Silica glass was chosen since the diffuse scattering from
the amorphous material does not contribute diffraction peaks and any signal from the push rods can be
modeled as background in the diffraction analysis. Neutron absorbing B4C could be an alternative option
here since the absorption would eliminate diffraction signal from the crystalline push rod material. Glassy
carbon also only contributes diffuse scattering to the diffraction signal and is structurally stable up to
3000°C with an increasing strength with increasing temperature. Chemical reaction with the fuel sample
will need to be considered for all materials. For neutron diffraction, if only the surface at the interface of
the sample with the push rod reacts, the volume fraction of e.g. a uranium carbide phase of the entire sample
volume would be small and not affect the measurement. Thermal conductivity of the push rod material is
another concern to minimize heat losses through the push rods and the resulting thermal gradients across
the sample. Again, 2D temperature mapping using resonance Doppler broadening of uranium resonances
could quantify this effect.
Other components of this existing setup could remain. The existing rig was designed to allow texture
measurements at temperature and under load and therefore has two rotation stages to rotate the sample.
Ceramic nuclear fuels typically do not exhibit texture and the deformation mechanism to be expected (e.g.
creep) do not change the crystal orientation as slip and in particular deformation by twinning do. Therefore,
the rotation stages could be removed. The control software of the existing rig for temperature control, stress
and strain measurements etc. was developed ~15 years ago, was written in LabView and would likely need
to be replaced with more modern control software. If the hard- and software interfaces of e.g. load cell are
not compatible with current interfaces, these components may need to be upgraded. Compared to a design
of such a device from scratch, these changes should still provide acceleration of the deployment of an
upgraded version.
With thermometry on e.g. the outer heat shield, the temperature within the vessel could be determined to
assess whether installation of an event-mode camera inside the vessel to be as close as possible to the sample
for minimal blur is feasible. Water cooling of the camera system could be an option and was used for similar
experiments with a TimePix camera close to Bridgman crystal growth furnace [72].

7. In Situ High Temperature Deformation on SMARTS
The flow curves reported by Salvo et al. [39] for different strain rates between 10-1 and 10-4 and temperatures
of 1100°C, 1350°C, 1550°C, 1700°C, are reproduced in Figure 17. They show plastic deformation reaching
10% strain at loads up to ~350 MPa. The differences in shape of the flow curve indicate that changes in
deformation modes may occur and in situ diffraction during the deformation may elucidate such changes.
From such data, also constitutive models describing and predicting the deformation can be derived [73,74].
Such studies are typically conducted on engineering diffractometers optimized for this application. In this
paragraph, deformation studies of UO2 on the SMARTS instrument are discussed. Since these curves
presented by Salvo et al. rarely overlap in the early stages of the deformation, an elastic region as expected
appears to be not present. This causes suspicion that settling of the samples, non parallel surfaces in contact
with the platens and other problems are present.

The SMARTS engineering diffractometer at LANSCE [20] is representative of other engineering

representative for the state-of-the-art instruments elsewhere with SMARTS being in some aspect even
superior to instruments built after SMARTS was built in 2001. Figure 18 shows the layout of the SMARTS
instrument and a photograph of the SMARTS load frame with the furnace.

The SMARTS furnace heats the sample with a resistively heated tungsten mesh above and below the
sample. Without the load frame, this setup can reach 1800°C. The furnace is designed to accommodate the
pistons of the load frame, thus enabling high temperature deformation studies. However, in this case the
maximum temperature is 1500°C, which is limited by the softening of the entire load train, made of Ta-
10W alloy. This temperature is below the maximum temperature reported by others studying the
deformation of UO2.
Measuring the strain by piston displacement (LVDT) measures the strain over the entire load train, which
in a high temperature experiment has parts at room temperature outside the furnace all the way to sample
temperature around the sample. This results in a large variation of the mechanical parameters (temperature
dependent stiffness, thermal expansion of the pistons causing strain on the sample) of material that has a
several orders of magnitude larger length than the sample and therefore the piston position (LVDT) is
practically meaningless to measure the strain the actual sample experiences. Strain measurements are most
reliable with an extensometer and the SMARTS furnace is equipped with an extensometer designed for
high temperature operation, positioned above the hot zone in the chamber with the open door in Figure
18(b). The device has a standard gauge length of 25 mm, too long to attach to a standard 10mm fuel pellet,
and requiring different sample dimensions to use the extensometer for reliable strain measurements. The
minimal sample length for the SMARTS setup is 30.5 mm. A long sample is required in order to get the
incident and diffracted neutron beams in and out without shading from the platens, and to avoid buckling,
a ratio of length to diameter of 2.4 is typically used, resulting in a sample diameter of 12.7mm. The strain
measurements devised by Salvo et al. [39], Figure 8, using two rods connected to two plates on each side
of the sample on one end and the other end connected to ais an elegant way to measure strain since any
temperature changes along the rods are approximately equal for both rods and therefore cancel out.
However, the SMARTS high temperature extensometer has a fixed location in the furnace and cannot be
adapted. In order to conduct deformation studies of UO2 on SMARTS without modifying the instrument,
therefore sample dimensions of 30.5 mm length and 12.7 mm diameter are needed.
In order to probe lattice planes with their normal parallel to the loading direction, platens, the loading axis
is typically horizontal under 45° to the incident neutron beam. Data on these two fundamental directions,
parallel and perpendicular to the load, are most instructive for understanding deformation behavior. In order
to obtain such diffraction data, the beam path for diffracted neutron must be unobstructed. In absence of
suitable schematic for the high temperature pistons, Figure 19 shows the design of a cryogenic deformation
stage for SMARTS [78] with the pistons in a conical shape to ensure a much larger volume of the pistons
for most of the load train while still providing beam paths for diffracted neutrons. On HIPPO, with a setup

09/12/2025                                                                                                                         29

with a vertical loading axis as proposed above, only the directions ~perpendicular to the loading direction
are available.

 (a) (b)
Figure 19: (a) Schematic of an in situ cryogenic loading experiment on SMARTS. Having the loading axis at 45° to the beam
allows to probe the lattice strain of lattice planes with their plane normal parallel and perpendicular to the loading axis. (b)
Schematic of the platens designed to allow diffraction from the sample with a much smaller diameter than the platens for the

A general problem for neutron diffraction studies with strain rates as those reported by Salvo et al. [39] is
that the time required to obtain sufficient statistics to measure small peak shifts requires interruption of the
deformation. Only for strain rates of 10-6 and slower can neutron data of sufficient quality be collected
without interruptions. This leads to relaxation of the material and may require cooling of the sample for the
data collection, followed by re-heating and continuation of the deformation. While synchrotrons offer much
faster data acquisition times, the grain averaging my only allow a small and potentially not representative
number of grains to contribute to the measurement [79]. To develop deformation models, neutron
diffraction with its larger number of grains contributing tend to be advantageous.
