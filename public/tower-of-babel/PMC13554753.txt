Non-ACS Presentation in Takotsubo Syndrome
Authors: Mingxiao Li, Shuwen Zheng, Liu He, Zhe Wang, Xin Wei, Lirui Yang, Tong Liu, Xin Du, Li Li, Ying Liu, Yushi Wang, Ling Bai, Jiang Wang, Jiang Li, Changsheng Ma, Jianzeng Dong, Baoxia Chen, Yihong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTakotsubo syndrome (TTS) is frequently misdiagnosed as acute coronary syndrome (ACS), yet a substantial proportion of patients present without typical ACS features—often leading to delayed recognition and suboptimal management. The prognostic implications of the non-ACS phenotype remain poorly characterized, particularly in Asian populations.ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the treatment patterns and outcomes of non-ACS TTS.MethodsUsing data from the multicenter TAKOC (Takotsubo syndrome of Chinese patients) Registry (18 tertiary hospitals in China), we compared 421 TTS patients stratified by initial presentation: ACS-like (typical chest pain, ischemic electrocardiogram changes, and peak troponin >10-fold the upper limit of normal) versus non-ACS. Inverse probability of treatment weighting was applied to account for baseline imbalances.ResultsNon-ACS TTS (n = 257, 61.0%) was associated with male sex, physical triggers, dyspnea as primary symptom, lower left ventricular ejection fraction, and higher incidence of cardiogenic shock. Paradoxically, despite significantly lower troponin levels, these patients experienced markedly higher 30-day all-cause mortality (adjusted HR: 2.28; 95% CI: 1.18-4.41; P = 0.017) and long-term mortality (adjusted HR: 2.44; 95% CI: 1.32-4.51; P = 0.043). Patients in the non-ACS group required more intensive in-hospital support—including diuretics, vasoactive drugs, and mechanical circulatory support; however, they received fewer cardiovascular medications (antiplatelets, beta-blockers, and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors) that were independently associated with lower 30-day mortality.ConclusionsNon-ACS presentation defines a high-risk TTS subtype characterized by atypical symptoms and disproportionately poor outcomes—despite less pronounced biomarker elevation. These findings underscore the critical need for heightened clinical suspicion and exploring effective treatment strategies for patients with TTS. (Registration number: ChiCTR2300075095).Key words: mortality, Takotsubo syndrome, treatment

Takotsubo syndrome (TTS) is characterized by transient and reversible left ventricular (LV) wall motion abnormalities occurring in the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease.1 Diagnosing TTS remains challenging, as its clinical manifestations frequently mimic those of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), particularly regarding electrocardiographic (ECG) abnormalities and cardiac biomarker elevations.2,3 Recent large international multicenter studies have substantially expanded our understanding of the clinical characteristics of TTS.4, 5, 6 Transient ventricular systolic dysfunction represents a central feature of TTS, with B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) elevations typically more pronounced than troponin increases—a pattern that may initially suggest heart failure. The most common presenting symptoms include acute chest pain, dyspnea, and syncope; however, many TTS cases are incidentally diagnosed based on abnormal ECG or cardiac biomarker findings. This diagnostic uncertainty carries clinical relevance, as acute treatment strategies appear to be influenced by presenting features. For instance, ACS-directed medications are frequently prescribed both during hospitalization and after discharge in patients with TTS, despite limited evidence regarding optimal treatment strategies for this population.7

Extensive investigations have explored TTS pathogenesis; nevertheless, its exact pathophysiology remains incompletely understood. Elevated catecholaminergic signaling plays a pivotal role in triggering the syndrome, inducing acute inflammation and metabolic lipid dysregulation at the myocardial level, along with negative inotropic and chronotropic effects.3 Historically considered a benign condition with favorable prognosis, accumulating evidence now indicates that TTS is associated with higher mortality rates than ACS in matched populations.8 Therefore, identifying clinical phenotypes associated with worse outcomes has become increasingly important.9 Moreover, considerable uncertainty and variability exist in prescription practices following TTS, encompassing both the type and duration of therapy. Data on therapeutic management derive predominantly from small case series, meta-analyses, and retrospective studies, and the impact of both cardiovascular and noncardiovascular interventions on TTS outcomes remains to be elucidated.10

The present study aimed to compare clinical characteristics and outcomes between patients hospitalized in China with TTS presenting as ACS versus those with non-ACS presentations. In addition, we investigated treatment disparities and their correlation with patient outcomes.

Methods

Study population and design

The Takotsubo syndrome of Chinese patients (TAKOC) Registry (ChiCTR2300075095) is a multicenter, observational, prospective, and retrospective registry of 18 tertiary hospitals across China. Hospitalized patients diagnosed with TTS (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision code I51.81) at discharge were consecutively enrolled between April 2015 and February 2023. The enrolled TTS cases were reviewed according to the InterTAK (International Takotsubo) diagnostic criteria by the core team. Detailed inclusion criteria comprised: 1) transient LV dysfunction presenting as apical ballooning, midventricular, basal, or focal wall motion abnormalities; 2) new ST-segment, T-wave abnormalities, and QTc prolongation on electrocardiography (ECG); 3) moderately elevated levels of troponin and natriuretic peptides; and 4) absence of culprit atherosclerotic coronary artery stenosis on coronary angiography or coronary artery disease was insufficient to explain the pattern of LV dysfunction. For patients with an uncertain TTS diagnosis, all core team members reviewed the cases to reach a consensus.

Data on demographic and clinical parameters were extracted from electronic medical records using standardized case report forms. The clinical parameters included cardiovascular risk factors, comorbidities, clinical presentation, and electrocardiographic and echocardiographic findings. Data on cardiovascular and noncardiovascular medications during hospitalization and discharge were also collected. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of China-Japan Friendship Hospital.

Definitions of clinical phenotypes

Patients were categorized into 2 groups based on initial presentation: ACS-like or non-ACS-like, according to presenting symptoms, laboratory findings, and ECG features. The ACS presentation group was defined by the presence of ischemic symptoms accompanied by either 1) dynamic ST-segment elevation or depression on ECG, or 2) troponin elevation exceeding 10 times the upper limit of normal. This definition aligns with the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction,11 with a deliberately higher troponin threshold applied to account for the near-universal troponin elevation observed in TTS populations.3 Patients not meeting these criteria were classified into the non-ACS presentation group.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the all-cause mortality within 30 days of admission. Secondary outcomes included in-hospital shock, ventricular tachycardia/ventricular fibrillation, and atrial fibrillation. After discharge, follow-up was conducted through clinical visits, medical records, or telephone interviews. Long-term mortality 30 days after admission was analyzed in the subgroups that received prolonged follow-up. To describe treatment disparities, we further analyzed the in-hospital (including epinephrine, norepinephrine, and dopamine) and at-discharge medication records, percutaneous coronary intervention, mechanical circulatory support, invasive ventilation, continuous renal replacement therapy, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation.

Statistical analysis

We used the Shapiro-Wilk test to assess the normality of continuous variables (Supplemental Table 1). Non-normally distributed continuous variables were summarized using the median and IQR and compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were presented as percentages and compared using the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to estimate survival within 30 days of admission and long-term survival beyond 30 days. The inverse probability treatment-weighted (IPTW) Cox proportional regression model was used to estimate HRs and 95% CIs after adjusting for sex, age, types of stressors (physical stressors, emotional stressors, no identified stressors), ST elevation, history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, and coronary artery disease. Detailed description on IPTW approach and physical stressors is provided in the Supplemental Methods. Covariate balance after IPTW is visualized in Supplemental Figure 1. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals. In-hospital complications were compared between the groups using the IPTW-weighted logistic regression model and expressed as ORs and 95% CIs. There is no missing data for adjusted variables in the primary analysis (Supplemental Table 2). In the comparison of baseline characteristics, we strictly used available, nonimputed data to ensure the integrity and accuracy of these comparisons.

To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis by excluding troponin from the exposure definition to evaluate whether the associations were driven solely by troponin marker. Furthermore, we adjusted for the baseline left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and BNP/N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) factor increase variables in the IPTW analysis. The missing values for LVEF (4.5% missing) and BNP/NT-proBNP factor increase (8.8% missing) variables were imputed using multiple imputation (Supplemental Table 2). The analyses were performed across 5 imputed datasets, and results were pooled using Rubin’ rules.

We further evaluated the associations between in-hospital cardiovascular medications, including antiplatelets, beta-blockers, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS inhibitors) (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers/angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors), and statins, as well as 30-day mortality stratified by ACS presentation. ORs and 95% CIs were estimated using IPTW-weighted logistic regression models adjusted for sex, age, stressor type, ST elevation, history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, coronary artery disease, and in-hospital use of other cardiovascular medications.

A 2-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.2.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 421 included patients with TTS, the median age was 63 years (IQR: 51-75 years), and 319 (75.8%) were women. Among them, 164 (39.0%) and 257 (61.0%) were categorized as presenting with ACS and non-ACS, respectively. The baseline comparisons between groups are detailed in Table 1. Compared with patients with ACS presentation, those with non-ACS presentations were more likely to be men and have physical triggers. The median body mass index was slightly lower in patients with non-ACS presentations. Chest pain and distress were the most common acute presentations in the ACS presentation group, whereas dyspnea occurred most frequently in non-ACS presentation group. The increase in troponin levels on admission was significantly higher in patients presenting with ACS. The mean LVEF was lower in the non-ACS presentation group, despite similar levels of BNP or NT-proBNP between the groups. Patients with ACS presentations were more likely to present with apical wall motion abnormalities, whereas those with non-ACS presentations were more frequently detected with global wall motion abnormalities (1.7% vs 15.6%) on echocardiography. The proportion of ST-segment elevation was significantly higher in the ACS group, whereas the proportion of ST depression was higher in the non-ACS group. As detailed in Supplemental Table 3, non-ACS patients had significantly lower mean arterial pressure and higher heart rates, suggesting a more unstable hemodynamic state. Furthermore, this group demonstrated a profound inflammatory status; C-reactive protein levels were nearly 8 times higher than those in the ACS group, and the markedly elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio further reflected systemic physiological stress.

Treatment disparities between groups

Medication patterns in the hospital and at discharge showed significant disparities stratified by ACS presentation (Figure 1, Supplemental Table 4). Frequent in-hospital use of antiplatelet drugs (87.8%) and statins (80.5%) were observed in the ACS presentation group. Although this proportion decreased at discharge, a relatively high percentage of patients with ACS presentations continued to receive antiplatelet drugs (64.7%) and statins (63.4%). In contrast, in the non-ACS presentation group, the use of antiplatelet agents and statins was lower, and approximately 10% of these patients received oral anticoagulants both in-hospital and at discharge. Moreover, a higher proportion of in-hospital diuretic use (54.5% vs 35.4%, P < 0.001) was observed in the non-ACS presentation group. Notably higher proportion of epinephrine, norepinephrine, and dopamine was observed in the non-ACS presentation group (51.4% vs 15.9%, P < 0.001). Overall, 71 (27.6%) patients with non-ACS presentations received in-hospital glucocorticoid treatment, and 6 (2.8%) patients continued to receive it at discharge. A small proportion of patients with non-ACS presentation also received in-hospital immunoglobulin therapy.

Compared with the non-ACS presentation group, patients with ACS presentations received beta-blockers and RAAS inhibitors more frequently during hospitalization and at discharge. No significant differences were observed in the use of calcium channel blockers or sodium-dependent glucose transporter 2 inhibitors between the groups. More than half of the patients with non-ACS presentation received in-hospital diuretics, which was significantly higher than that in the ACS presentation group. At discharge, the proportion of cardiovascular medications used, especially antiplatelets, statins, and diuretics, reduced (Supplemental Table 4).

Significant differences in the in-hospital intervention treatments were also observed between the groups (Figure 2, Supplemental Table 4). Few patients in either group received percutaneous coronary intervention. A total of 35 (13.6%) patients with non-ACS presentation received mechanical circulatory support treatment, compared with 5 (3%) patients in the ACS presentation group. The proportion of in-hospital cardiopulmonary resuscitation was significantly higher in the non-ACS than in ACS presentation group (14.8% vs 2.4%, P < 0.001). In addition, a higher percentage of patients with non-ACS presentations received invasive ventilation and continuous renal replacement therapy.

Clinical outcomes

A higher 30-day mortality since admission was observed in non-ACS presentation group (23.0% vs 7.3%, IPTW-weighted HR: 2.28; 95% CI: 1.16-4.50; P = 0.017) compared with the ACS-presentation group (Figure 3). The non-ACS presentation group also exhibited higher incidence of in-hospital shock (28.4% vs 8.5%, IPTW-weighted OR: 3.13; 95% CI: 2.13-4.67; P < 0.001). However, no significant differences were observed between non-ACS and ACS presentation groups in the incidence of in-hospital ventricular tachycardia/ventricular fibrillation (9.7% vs 4.9%, P = 0.071) and in-hospital atrial fibrillation (16.7% vs 11.0%, P = 0.102).

A total of 249 patients who survived beyond 30 days after the index admission were included in the long-term follow-up analysis, with a median follow-up of 18.5 (IQR: 6.1-41.6) months. Of these, 32 and 8 patients in the non-ACS and ACS presentation groups died during follow-up, respectively. Compared with ACS presentation, non-ACS presentation was independently associated with shorter survival time (IPTW-weighted HR: 2.44; 95% CI: 1.03-5.81; P = 0.043) (Supplemental Figure 2).

Among the 164 patients with ACS presentations, 84 were classified as having a “STEMI” phenotype (ST-segment elevation on ECG) and 80 as having an “NSTEMI” phenotype (without ST-segment elevation). No significant differences were observed in 30-day mortality (IPTW-weighted HR: 1.41; 95% CI: 0.34-5.92; P = 0.636) or long-term mortality beyond 30 days (IPTW-weighted HR: 1.67; 95% CI: 0.34-8.09; P = 0.524) between the “STEMI” and “NSTEMI” subgroups (Supplemental Figures 3 and 4).

In the sensitivity analysis excluding troponin from the exposure definition, 109 patients were classified as the non-ACS phenotype and 312 patients as the ACS phenotype. The non-ACS phenotype was still independently associated with a higher risk of 30-day mortality (IPTW-weighted HR: 2.97; 95% CI: 1.44-6.12; P = 0.003) and long-term mortality (IPTW-weighted HR: 2.41; 95% CI: 1.01-5.73; P = 0.046). Furthermore, after adjusting for baseline LVEF and BNP/NT-proBNP factor increase variables, the results for 30-day mortality (IPTW-weighted HR: 2.30; 95% CI: 1.19-4.45; P = 0.017) and long-term mortality (IPTW-weighted HR: 2.45; 95% CI: 1.08-5.53; P = 0.041) remained consistent with the primary analysis.

Associations between in-hospital medications and outcomes

Among patients with non-ACS presentation, in-hospital use of antiplatelet agents (IPTW-weighted OR: 0.25; 95% CI: 0.14-0.43; P < 0.001) and beta-blocker (IPTW-weighted OR: 0.31; 95% CI: 0.19-0.48; P < 0.001) were both independently associated with reduced 30-day mortality since admission. In the ACS-presentation group, in-hospital use of RAAS inhibitors was significantly associated with lower 30-day mortality (IPTW-weighted OR: 0.27; 95% CI: 0.08-0.72; P < 0.001). No significant association was observed between in-hospital statin use and reduced 30-day mortality in either group (Figure 4).

Discussion

This national multicenter registry study sought to investigate how the clinical presentation of TTS influences treatment strategies and outcomes, yielding several notable findings (Central Illustration). First, compared with patients with an ACS-like presentation, those with non–ACS-like presentation were more likely to be men, present with heart failure symptoms, and have physical triggers. Second, patients presenting with non-ACS symptoms more frequently received diuretics, vasoactive medications, and mechanical circulatory support during hospitalization. Third, the 30-day mortality rate was significantly higher in the non-ACS group than in the ACS group although exhibited lower troponin levels. Finally, in-hospital use of antiplatelets and beta-blockers was associated with reduced 30-day mortality, whereas RAAS inhibitor use was linked to decreased mortality risk specifically in patients with ACS-like presentations.

Based on retrospective studies, TTS accounts for 2% to 3% of all patients and 5% to 6% of female patients presenting with ACS.2,3 However, its clinical features closely resemble those of ACS, potentially leading to underdiagnosis.12 Noncardiac medical conditions, including neurological disorders, surgery, and malignancy, are increasingly recognized as TTS triggers.13 Despite several proposed diagnostic criteria (eg, Mayo criteria14 and the InterTAK Diagnostic Score15), no consensus exists on the definitive diagnosis of TTS. Consequently, the prevalence of TTS was considerably lower than expected relative to the number of ACS cases in in participating hospitals.16 Moreover, because the typical clinical presentation of TTS often overlaps with that of ACS, distinguishing these conditions at initial presentation remains challenging. TTS presentation may also vary according to trigger type (eg, physical or emotional event).17 In the present study, we found that patients presenting with ACS-like features were predominately women, exhibited ST elevation on ECG, had fewer comorbidities, more emotional triggers, and higher LVEF. Although TTS also can be categorized by abnormal wall motion patterns (eg, apical vs nonapical types), echocardiography-based phenotyping failed to identify patients with poor prognosis.6 In our study, non-ACS phenotypes—characterized by lower LVEF and more frequent physical triggers—were associated with worse short-term outcomes.

The 30-day mortality rate in our cohort was 7.3%, consistent with findings from other studies.3,9,18 Nevertheless, reported in-hospital mortality rates for TTS vary widely, ranging from 0% to 12.2%. These disparities limit direct comparisons between studies, likely reflecting differences in baseline characteristics such as the higher proportion of patients with physical triggers in our cohort. Overall, these findings underscore that TTS is not a benign condition but rather a disease with potentially fatal outcomes.9 Furthermore, patients presenting with ACS-like features exhibited more favorable clinical profiles than those with non-ACS presentations. Ethnic disparities in TTS outcomes have also been reported; for instance, Japanese patients with TTS have considerably higher in-hospital mortality rates than Western populations (8.2% vs 3.2%), partially attributable to a higher prevalence of physical triggers in Japanese cohorts.18

Our findings indicate that TTS patients with initial non-ACS presentations experience worse clinical outcomes than those with ACS-like presentations. Several factors may drive this disparity. First, we observed lower LVEF at admission in the non-ACS group, suggesting more severe initial cardiac dysfunction. This aligns with prior reports linking dyspnea at presentation to increased risk of in-hospital complications and long-term mortality, potentially due to more extensive myocardial impairment.19 Second, the atypical nature of non-ACS symptoms may delay TTS diagnosis, thereby deferring critical interventions such as coronary angiography. Finally, our data revealed a higher prevalence of physical triggers in the non-ACS group, consistent with findings from the GEIST (GErman Italian Spanish Takotsubo) registry demonstrating that physical triggers are associated with increased complication rates and mortality.13 Physical triggers are frequently linked to more severe underlying conditions—such as sepsis or neurological disorders—that independently contribute to mortality.1 These factors collectively underscore the prognostic significance of presentation type in TTS. However, the observed high mortality in the non-ACS phenotype still warrants careful interpretation. Our findings suggest that this group represents a highly heterogeneous population and the severity of the baseline illness severity may also contribute to the high mortality.

Alongside challenges in identifying TTS during the acute phase, evidence-based treatment remains elusive. Catecholamine overstimulation leading to myocardial stunning has long been considered a key feature of TTS.3,20,21 During the acute phase, supraphysiological serum catecholamine levels in TTS patients have been documented to exceed those in myocardial infarction patients by more than 3-fold.22 Based on this mechanism, beta-blockers have been proposed to dampen sympathetic activity, mitigate the effects of subsequent catecholamine surges, and prevent potential harm during the post-acute phase. In our study, beta-blocker utilization (60%) aligned with the multicenter REgistry on TAKOtsubo Syndrome registry23 but was lower than that in the InterTAK registry (78%).3 Beta-blocker selection appeared to be influenced by clinical presentation (chest pain and ST elevation) and comorbidities. Notably, early beta-blocker use was associated with decreased 30-day mortality in non-ACS patients but not in ACS patients. A propensity score–matched analysis involving Japanese TTS patients revealed no significant association between early beta-blocker use and in-hospital mortality.24 However, a significant interaction was observed between early beta-blocker therapy and sex, with beta-blockers associated with reduced 30-day mortality in male patients. TTS in male patients is characterized by a distinct high-risk phenotype—including more frequent physical triggers, less frequent chest pain, and lower LVEF—similar to the non-ACS presentation group in our study. Consistent with our findings, data from the RETAKO registry revealed significantly lower 1-year mortality in patients treated with beta-blockers than in untreated patients, particularly among those experiencing cardiogenic shock during hospitalization.23 An Italian cohort study25 also demonstrated that beta-blocker treatment was associated with lower risk of overall mortality during long-term follow-up, with the survival benefit being significantly more pronounced in patients with hypertension and those developing cardiogenic shock during the acute phase. Our observations regarding the potential benefits of early beta-blocker use in patients without ACS-like symptoms require confirmation through future prospective clinical trials.

Given its resemblance to ACS, TTS often prompts clinicians to prescribe antiplatelet therapy, either during hospitalization or for long-term use. TTS may be considered a cause of myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA)—a condition affecting a heterogeneous group of patients encompassing both coronary and non-coronary pathologies, with TTS representing one of the latter. Observational studies have suggested that antiplatelet therapy is associated with a survival benefit among patients with MINOCA after adjustment.26 Notably, the catecholaminergic surge may activate platelets and proinflammatory pathways, providing a rationale for antiplatelet agents such as aspirin. TTS patients exhibiting higher epinephrine levels demonstrated elevated platelet activation and aggregation at 3 months.27 This observation may partially explain how stressful events and adrenergic activity induce a blood hyperviscosity state, which antiplatelet therapy could help counteract. However, observational studies on the relationship between antiplatelet therapy (either single or dual) and adverse cardiovascular events in TTS patients remain controversial.28

On admission, a high proportion of TTS patients exhibit impaired LVEF, leading to heart failure therapy with angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers. Neurohumoral activation in TTS extends beyond adrenergic nerve involvement. Khan et al recently demonstrated maladaptive RAAS activation mechanisms similar to those in classic heart failure with reduced LVEF, suggesting these abnormalities could be targets for RAAS inhibitors.29 We found that only RAAS inhibitors were associated with lower mortality in patients with ACS-mimicking TTS, consistent with previous data from the InterTAK registry.3 Such observational data are subject to confounding by indication for these medications. However, the observed effect of RAAS inhibition cannot be attributed solely to comorbidities such as hypertension, which were similarly frequent between patient cohorts.10 In addition, the association with RAAS inhibition was inconsistent and not demonstrable in patients taking this medication for most (>50%) of their follow-up duration. Long-term RAAS inhibitor therapy after a TTS episode was not associated with lower mortality rates in propensity score analysis, although a potential survival benefit was observed among patients with admission LVEF ≤40% or diabetes.30 Given the nonrandomized, observational nature of our data, the association between medications and mortality may be partially driven by prognostic bias, as these oral medications may be initiated in patients who are hemodynamically stable and have survived the initial acute phase. Therefore, the protective signals of modern cardiovascular therapies should be interpreted with caution and viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of causal therapeutic effects, underscoring the need for large-scale randomized controlled trials in TTS.

Study limitations

To our knowledge, this represents the largest cohort of Chinese patients with TTS studied to date; nonetheless, several limitations warrant consideration. First, this was a retrospective observational study, and selection bias cannot be excluded. To standardize parametric measurements and maintain consistency in data collection and analysis, we limited this study to tertiary cardiovascular centers in China. Second, the reported incidence of TTS was significantly lower than expected, reflecting that TTS diagnosis was less familiar to clinicians and likely underdiagnosed in China, particularly during the earlier study period.16 The generalizability of our findings may be constrained by the moderate sample size, likely reflecting real-world underdiagnosis of TTS. Validation with larger international TTS cohorts is warranted to strengthen our conclusions. Third, because this study was conducted using a registry of routinely collected data, not all desirable information was available. For example, although we utilized core physiological and inflammatory markers to assess baseline illness severity, formal intensive care scoring systems such as APACHE II (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II) or sequential organ failure assessment scores were not routinely collected, as many patients were managed in specialized cardiology wards. Fourth, long-term follow-up data—including medication adherence and cardiac function recovery—were not available, preventing investigation of cardiovascular medication effects on long-term outcomes. Fifth, although prescription information was complete for the entire in-hospital duration, limited inferences can be drawn from nonrandomized data. There is a potential for prognostic bias, where healthier patients were more likely to receive evidence-based medications. The findings from our subgroup and secondary analyses should be interpreted with caution as the increased risk of type I error due to multiple comparisons. These analyses were intended to be exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Causality cannot be assumed, and randomized studies are required to address this gap. In addition, the absence of time-to-event data of in-hospital nonfatal outcome precluded a formal competing risk analysis, potentially leading to the overestimation of nonfatal event risks given the high early mortality. Finally, our findings may not be fully generalizable to Western populations. However, ethnicity itself does not appear to exert additional influence on treatment effects or outcomes beyond those attributable to differences in other clinical backgrounds. The observed ethnic disparities may instead stem from geographic and socioeconomic factors, as well as regional variations in therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

Significant variation was observed in clinical manifestations and in-hospital medications between TTS patients presenting with ACS-like versus non-ACS-like features. Different medications administered during the acute phase are associated with varying outcomes across patient phenotypes with respect to in-hospital mortality. Our results highlight the heterogeneity of clinical presentations and management approaches for TTS, underscoring the need for evidence-based risk stratification to guide precise disease classification and the development of individualized treatment strategies.
