Equality in Fill’s spectral gap problem
Vishesh Jain, Clayton Mizgerd

Abstract
. We study the adjacent-transposition chain on the symmetric group 𝔖 n \mathfrak{S}_{n} with a regular parameter vector p → = ( p i , j ) i ≠ j \vec{p}=(p_{i,j})_{i\neq j} . Fill’s spectral gap conjecture, recently resolved in the affirmative by Greaves–Zhu, states that among all regular parameter vectors, the spectral gap of the transition matrix is minimized by the uniform vector p i , j = 1 / 2 p_{i,j}=1/2 for all i ≠ j i\neq j . We prove the stronger statement that among all regular parameter vectors, the spectral gap is minimized if and only if p → \vec{p} has a neutral label, i.e., there exists c ∈ [ n ] c\in[n] such that p c , i = 1 / 2 p_{c,i}=1/2 for all i ≠ c i\neq c . Moreover, in this case, we show that the multiplicity of the second largest eigenvalue is equal to the number of neutral labels, unless the number of neutral labels is n − 2 n-2 or n n , in which case the multiplicity is n − 1 n-1 . This confirms a conjecture of Fill.



1.
Introduction

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer and for
i\neq j
, let
p_{i,j}\in(0,1)
with
p_{i,j}+p_{j,i}=1
for all
i\neq j
. Let
\vec{p}=(p_{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq n}
. Fill’s
[
2
]
adjacent-transposition Markov chain on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with parameter vector
\vec{p}
can be described as follows. Write the current state
x\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
in one-line notation as
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. Then, the next state is determined by the following rule:

(1)

Choose a position
r\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
uniformly at random.

(2)

With probability
p_{x_{r},x_{r+1}}
, stay at
x
. With the remaining probability
p_{x_{r+1},x_{r}}
, move to

x^{\tau_{r}}:=(x_{1},\dots,x_{r-1},x_{r+1},x_{r},\dots,x_{n}).

Formally, for
r\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, define the
\mathfrak{S}_{n}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n}
matrix
E_{r}
by

(E_{r})_{x,y}=\begin{cases}p_{x_{r},x_{r+1}},\quad&y=x;\\
p_{x_{r+1},x_{r}},\quad&y=x^{\tau_{r}};\\
0,\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then, the transition matrix of the adjacent-transposition walk is given by

K=K(\vec{p})=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}E_{r}.

Since
p_{i,j}\in(0,1)
for all
i\neq j
, the adjacent-transposition walk is ergodic on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. As Fill showed, it is reversible with respect to its stationary distribution
\mu=\mu(\vec{p})
defined by

(1.1)

\mu(x):=Z^{-1}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}p_{x_{i},x_{j}},

where
Z
is a normalizing constant chosen so that
\sum_{x}\mu(x)=1
.

Motivated by a problem on self-organizing lists, Fill considered regular parameter vectors
\vec{p}
, defined as follows. We refer the reader to
[
2
]
for motivation behind this definition.

Definition 1.1
.

The parameter vector
\vec{p}=(p_{i,j})_{1\leq i<j\leq n}
is said to be regular if:

•

p_{i-1,i}\geq\frac{1}{2}\qquad(2\leq i\leq n)

•

p_{i-1,j}\geq p_{i,j}\qquad(2\leq i<j\leq n)
,

•

p_{i,j+1}\geq p_{i,j}\qquad(1\leq i<j\leq n-1).

We let
\mathcal{R}
denote the set of all regular parameter vectors.

For
K=K(\vec{p})
, define the spectral gap
\lambda_{K}
of
K
by

\lambda_{K}=1-\beta_{K},

where
\beta_{K}
is the second largest eigenvalue of
K
. We remark that since the spectrum of
K
is contained in
[0,1]
(see
Proposition

2.1
),
\lambda_{K}
is also the absolute spectral gap of
K
.

Let
\vec{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}
denote the uniform parameter vector with all entries equal to
1/2
. Wilson
[
4
]
showed that

\lambda_{K(\vec{p}_{\mathrm{unif}})}=\frac{1-\cos(\pi/n)}{n-1}=:\lambda_{*}

Fill made several conjectures about the spectral gap of regular adjacent-transposition walks. Let

\Lambda:=\{\lambda_{K(\vec{p})}:\vec{p}\in\mathcal{R}\}.

Conjecture 1.2

(Gap conjecture)
.

Among all regular choices of
\vec{p}
, the gap
\lambda_{K(\vec{p})}
is minimized by
\vec{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}
, i.e.

\inf\{\lambda:\lambda\in\Lambda\}=\min\{\lambda:\lambda\in\Lambda\}=\lambda_{*}.

This conjecture was very recently settled in the affirmative by Greaves and Zhu
[
3
]
; we refer the reader to
[
3
]
and
[
2
]
for discussion of prior work.

Fill also made stronger conjectures characterizing all the minimizing regular parameter vectors. In order to state these conjectures, we introduce some terminology.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
\vec{p}
be a parameter vector. We say that
c\in[n]
is a neutral label, or simply neutral, if

p_{c,i}=p_{i,c}=1/2\qquad\text{for all }i\neq c.

Conjecture 1.4

(Characterization of minimizers)
.

Let
n\geq 3
. For a regular parameter vector
\vec{p}
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\lambda_{K(\vec{p})}=\lambda_{*}

(2)

\vec{p}
has a neutral label.

Remark 1.5
.

In the above conjecture, one must restrict to
n=3
, since for
n=2
, a direct computation shows that
\lambda_{K(\vec{p})}=\lambda_{*}
for every regular parameter vector
\vec{p}
. On the other hand, a neutral label exists iff
p_{1,2}=1
.

The implication (b) implies (a) is straightforward and follows from Wilson’s argument (see
Proposition

6.6
), so that the main content is proving (a) implies (b).

In the conjectured equality cases, Fill also made a conjecture about the multiplicity of the second largest eigenvalue. Once again, we introduce some notation to state this conveniently.

Definition 1.6
.

For a parameter vector
\vec{p}
, let

N(\vec{p}):=|\{i\in[n]:i\text{ is neutral}\}|

denote the number of neutral labels. Let

M(\vec{p}):=\text{(algebraic) multiplicity of }1-\lambda_{*}\text{ in }\operatorname{spec}(K(\vec{p})).

Conjecture 1.7

(Multiplicity conjecture)
.

Let
n\geq 3
.
Let
\vec{p}
be a regular parameter vector. The following relationship holds between
N(\vec{p})
and
M(\vec{p})
:

(1)

If
N(\vec{p})\notin\{n-2,n\}
, then

N(\vec{p})=M(\vec{p}).

(2)

Otherwise, if
N(\vec{p})\in\{n-2,n\}
, then
M(\vec{p})=n-1
.

We confirm both of these conjectures.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\vec{p}
be a regular parameter vector. Then,

(1)

\lambda_{K(\vec{p})}=\lambda_{*}\iff N(\vec{p})\geq 1.

(2)

Moreover,

M(\vec{p})=\begin{cases}N(\vec{p}),\qquad&\emph{if}N(\vec{p})\notin\{n-2,n\};\\
n-1,\qquad&\emph{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Overview of the proof

The starting point of our proof is the Greaves–Zhu proof of Fill’s spectral gap conjecture. Let
K=K(\vec{p})
have spectral gap
\lambda_{K}
. This implies that the smallest positive eigenvalue of
L:=I-K
is
\lambda_{K}
. In
[
3
]
, they further use the similarity of
K
and
L
to conclude that the smallest positive eigenvalue of
K
is
\lambda_{K}
. The main work in their paper is to show that for regular
\vec{p}
,
\lambda_{K}\geq\lambda_{*}
by comparing the quadratic forms
\langle f,K^{2}f\rangle
and
\langle f,Kf\rangle
for arbitrary
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
and where the inner product is the weighted inner product induced by the stationary measure. Instead of shifting to
K
, we will find it more convenient to work directly with
L
. The argument from
[
3
]
applies essentially verbatim for
L
and shows that
\lambda_{L}\geq\lambda^{*}
(see
Proposition

3.2
).

Suppose
\lambda_{L}=\lambda^{*}
and let
0\neq g\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
be an eigenvector of
L
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{*}
. Then, every inequality in the the Greaves–Zhu argument for
L
and
g
must be an equality. This allows us to deduce several rigidity properties of the eigenvector. In particular, consider the orthogonal projection
F_{1}:=I-E_{1}
and let
u_{1}:=F_{1}g
. The equality conditions show that
u_{1}(x)
depends only on the first two labels of
x
. This allows us to completely describe
u_{1}
, which is
a priori
an
n!
-dimensional vector, by a family
(U_{a,b})_{a<b}
of
\binom{n}{2}
real numbers (see
Equation

4.1
).

The core of the paper is to analyze the behavior of
u_{1}
on the
G_{1}:=\langle\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\rangle\simeq\mathfrak{S}_{3}
orbits of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Each such orbit is completely specific by three labels
i<j<k
. Once again, by equality in Greaves–Zhu, we show that whenever
u_{1}
is nonzero on an orbit, then one must have

p_{i,j}=p_{j,k}=\frac{1}{2},

together with a linear relation among the corresponding coefficients

U_{i,j},U_{i,k},U_{j,k}
(see
Proposition

4.2
).

The existence of a neutral label is then obtained by combining this orbital relation with the monotonicity built into regular parameter vectors. Consider the set of pairs
(a,b)
for which
U_{a,b}\neq 0
. Using the orbital relation, we show that this set meets the triangle
1\leq a<b\leq n
at both the left and top edges in a certain manner (see
Lemmas

5.1
and
5.2
) This gives a label
c
with
p_{1,c}=p_{c,n}=1/2
at which point regularity forces
c
to be neutral.

The multiplicity statement is obtained by pushing the same structure further. Regularity shows that the neutral labels form an interval
[A,B]
(see
Lemma

6.2
) and the orbital relation then shows that
the
\binom{n}{2}
coefficients
(U_{a,b})_{a<b}
are, in fact, determined by the adjacent values

D_{r}:=U_{r,r+1}.

for
r\in[A-1,B]
(see
Lemma

6.3
). This essentially gives the desired upper bound on the multiplicity (except for a possible shift by one, see
Proposition

6.5
). For the lower bound, each neutral label contributes Wilson’s single-card eigenfunction, so the only remaining case is the exceptional regime
N(\vec{p})=n-2
. There we construct an additional linearly independent eigenfunction built from the relative positions of the two non-neutral labels (see
Proposition

6.8
).

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout the remainder of this paper,
n\geq 3
. Our proof builds on the approach of Greaves–Zhu
[
3
]
. As in
[
3
]
, we equip
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
with the weighted inner product

\langle f,g\rangle=\langle f,g\rangle_{\vec{p}}:=\sum_{x\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}f(x)g(x)\mu(x),

where recall that
\mu=\mu_{\vec{p}}
is the stationary distribution from
Equation

1.1
. This inner product induces the norm

\|f\|=\|f\|_{\vec{p}}:=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle}.

We remark that throughout we will omit the dependencies of various quantities on the parameter vector
\vec{p}
when there is no risk of confusion.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
2
, Theorem 2]
)
.

For any parameter vector
\vec{p}
, the matrices
K
and
I-K
are similar. In particular, the spectrum of
K
lies in
[0,1]
and is symmetric around
1/2
. Furthermore,
0
and
1
are eigenvalues of
K
with multiplicity
1
.

Henceforth, for convenience of notation, we set

L:=I-K.

Since
K
and
L
have the same spectrum by
Proposition

2.1
, we may (and will) equivalently prove
Theorem

1.8
for
L
, instead of
K
. Accordingly, for
r\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, let

F_{r}:=I-E_{r}

and notice that

L=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}F_{r}.

Define the subspaces
V_{r},W_{r}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
by

V_{r}:=\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}:f(x^{\tau_{r}})=f(x)\quad\forall x\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\}

and

W_{r}=V_{r}^{\perp},

where recall that we are working with the weighted inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. We will make use of the following lemmas from
[
3
]
.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
3
, Lemma 2.3]
)
.

For each
1\leq r\leq n-1
,
E_{r}
is the orthogonal projection onto
V_{r}
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.

Consequently,
F_{r}
is the orthogonal projection onto
W_{r}
with respect to the same inner product.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
3
, Lemma 2.1]
)
.

Let
r,s\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. Suppose
|r-s|>1
. Then,
E_{r}E_{s}=E_{s}E_{r}
and consequently,
F_{r}F_{s}=F_{s}F_{r}
.

Next, we will need a refinement of
[
3
, Lemma 2.2]
. To state it, we need to introduce some notation, which will also be useful later.

Let
r\in\{1,\dots,n-2\}
. Recall the transposition operators
\tau_{r},\tau_{r+1}
and note that the subgroup
G_{r}
generated by
\tau_{r}
and
\tau_{r+1}
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
. Since
G_{r}
acts only on positions
r,r+1,r+2
, each
G_{r}
-orbit is the six-point set obtained by permuting the three labels in those positions while leaving all other positions fixed.

Let
T_{r}
be a set of orbit representatives. For
t\in T_{r}
, let
\mathcal{O}_{t}:=G_{r}t
denote the corresponding orbit, and let
V_{\mathcal{O}_{t}}
denote the linear span of
\{e_{x}:x\in\mathcal{O}_{t}\}
, where
e_{x}
denotes the elementary basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
corresponding to
x\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. This gives the decomposition

(2.1)

\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}=\oplus_{t\in T_{r}}V_{\mathcal{O}_{t}},

and each subspace
V_{\mathcal{O}_{t}}
is invariant under
E_{r}+E_{r+1}
and
F_{r}+F_{r+1}
.

Note that each orbit
\mathcal{O}
can be described by three distinct labels
i<j<k
such that positions
r,r+1,r+2
for every element in the orbit are a permutation of
(i,j,k)
. For such an orbit
\mathcal{O}
, we define

s_{\mathcal{O}}:=p_{i,j}p_{j,k}p_{k,i}+p_{k,j}p_{j,i}p_{i,k},\qquad m_{\mathcal{O}}=1-\sqrt{s_{\mathcal{O}}}.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
3
, Lemma 2.2]
)
.

Let
r\in\{1,\dots,n-2\}
. Let
\mathcal{O}
be a
G_{r}
-orbit in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Let
t
be the representative in
\mathcal{O}
with

t_{r}=i,\qquad t_{r+1}=j,\qquad t_{r+2}=k.

for
i<j<k
. Then, in the ordered basis

t,\ t^{\tau_{r}},\ t^{\tau_{r+1}},\ t^{\tau_{r+1}\tau_{r}},\ t^{\tau_{r}\tau_{r+1}},\ t^{\tau_{r+1}\tau_{r}\tau_{r+1}},

the restriction of
M_{r}:=E_{r}+E_{r+1}
to
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{O}}
is

M_{\mathcal{O}}=\begin{pmatrix}p_{i,j}+p_{j,k}&p_{j,i}&p_{k,j}&0&0&0\\
p_{i,j}&p_{j,i}+p_{i,k}&0&p_{k,i}&0&0\\
p_{j,k}&0&p_{i,k}+p_{k,j}&0&p_{k,i}&0\\
0&p_{i,k}&0&p_{j,k}+p_{k,i}&0&p_{k,j}\\
0&0&p_{i,k}&0&p_{k,i}+p_{i,j}&p_{j,i}\\
0&0&0&p_{j,k}&p_{i,j}&p_{k,j}+p_{j,i}\end{pmatrix}.

A direct computation shows the following.

Corollary 2.5
.

With notation as above,
the characteristic polynomial of
M_{\mathcal{O}}
is

\chi_{\mathcal{O}}(\alpha)=\alpha(\alpha-2)(\alpha^{2}-2\alpha+1-s_{\mathcal{O}})^{2}.

Therefore, the eigenvalues of
M_{\mathcal{O}}
are

0,\qquad 2,\qquad 1-\sqrt{s_{\mathcal{O}}},\qquad 1+\sqrt{s_{\mathcal{O}}},

with multiplicities
1,1,2,2
, respectively.

Consequently the restriction
F_{r}+F_{r+1}=2I-(E_{r}+E_{r+1})
to
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{O}}
has the same multiset of eigenvalues.

The next lemma extends the key linear-algebraic
[
3
, Lemma 2.4]
by deriving an important relationship in the equality case.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
0\leq\gamma<1
, and let
P,Q
be orthogonal projections on a finite-dimensional real inner product space
V
. Assume that every nonzero eigenvalue of
P+Q
is at least
1-\gamma
. Then, for every
f\in V
,

\langle Pf,Qf\rangle\geq-\gamma\lVert Pf\rVert\lVert Qf\rVert.

Moreover, if

\langle Pf,Qf\rangle=-\gamma\|Pf\|\,\|Qf\|\neq 0,

then

PQPf=\gamma^{2}Pf.

Equivalently,
Pf
is an eigenvector of
PQ
with eigenvalue
\gamma^{2}
.

Proof.

The first part is exactly
[
3
, Lemma 2.4]
. The “moreover” part is new and we will prove this here.

Let

W:=\operatorname{Im}(P)\cap\operatorname{Im}(Q).

Since
P
and
Q
fix
W
pointwise, the orthogonal complement
W^{\perp}
is invariant under both
P
and
Q
.

We first record the operator-norm consequence of the spectral assumption.

Claim 2.7
.

For every
x\in W^{\perp}
,

\|PQx\|\leq\gamma\,\|Qx\|,\qquad\|QPx\|\leq\gamma\|Px\|.

Proof.

We will prove the first case; the second case holds by symmetry. Let

U:=\operatorname{Im}(Q)\cap W^{\perp},\qquad A:=QPQ|_{U}.

Then
A
is self-adjoint and positive semidefinite on
U
. Let
\sigma^{2}
be a nonzero eigenvalue of
A
, and choose a unit eigenvector
y\in U
such that

Ay=\sigma^{2}y.

Then
0<\sigma\leq 1
. In fact
\sigma<1
: if
\sigma=1
, then

\|Py\|^{2}=\langle y,QPy\rangle=\langle y,Ay\rangle=1=\|y\|^{2},

so
y\in\operatorname{Im}(P)
. Since
y\in\operatorname{Im}(Q)
as well, this would imply
y\in W
, contradicting
y\in W^{\perp}
.

Now define

z:=\sigma^{-1}Py.

Then
z\in\operatorname{Im}(P)\cap W^{\perp}
, and

Qz=\sigma^{-1}QPy=\sigma^{-1}Ay=\sigma y.

Hence

(P+Q)(z-y)=Pz-Py+Qz-Qy=z-\sigma z+\sigma y-y=(1-\sigma)(z-y).

Also
z-y\neq 0
, because otherwise
y=z\in\operatorname{Im}(P)\cap\operatorname{Im}(Q)=W
, again impossible. Therefore
1-\sigma
is a nonzero eigenvalue of
P+Q
. By hypothesis,

1-\sigma\geq 1-\gamma,

so
\sigma\leq\gamma
.

Thus every eigenvalue of
A
is at most
\gamma^{2}
. Now take any
x\in W^{\perp}
, and set
y:=Qx\in U
. Then

\|PQx\|^{2}=\|Py\|^{2}=\langle y,Ay\rangle\leq\gamma^{2}\|y\|^{2}=\gamma^{2}\|Qx\|^{2}.\qed

Now decompose

f=w+u

with
w\in W
and
u\in W^{\perp}
. We will show
w=0
. Notice

\langle Pf,Qf\rangle=\|w\|^{2}+\langle Pu,Qu\rangle.

By
2.7
,

|\langle Pu,Qu\rangle|=|\langle Pu,PQu\rangle|\leq\|Pu\|\,\|PQu\|\leq\gamma\|Pu\|\|Qu\|,

so

(2.2)

\langle Pf,Qf\rangle\geq\|w\|^{2}-\gamma\|Pu\|\|Qu\|.

Also,

(2.3)

\|Pf\|\,\|Qf\|=\sqrt{(\|w\|^{2}+\|Pu\|^{2})(\|w\|^{2}+\|Qu\|^{2})}\geq\|w\|^{2}+\|Pu\|\|Qu\|,

because for any
c,x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,

(c+x^{2})(c+y^{2})-(c+xy)^{2}=c(x-y)^{2}\geq 0.

Suppose
w\neq 0
. Then combining
Equations

2.2
and
2.3
,

\langle Pf,Qf\rangle\geq\|w\|^{2}-\gamma\|Pu\|\|Qu\|>-\gamma(\|w\|^{2}+\|Pu\|\|Qu\|)\geq-\gamma\|Pf\|\,\|Qf\|,

by
Equation

2.3
, contradicting the hypothesis

\langle Pf,Qf\rangle=-\gamma\|Pf\|\,\|Qf\|.

Therefore
f\in W^{\perp}
.

Since
\langle Pf,Qf\rangle\neq 0
, both
Pf
and
Qf
are nonzero. Applying
2.7
to
f\in W^{\perp}
, we get

\|PQf\|\leq\gamma\,\|Qf\|.

Therefore

\gamma\|Pf\|\,\|Qf\|=-\langle Pf,Qf\rangle=|\langle Pf,PQf\rangle|\leq\|Pf\|\,\|PQf\|\leq\gamma\|Pf\|\,\|Qf\|.

Hence equality holds throughout. Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz implies that
PQf
is a scalar multiple of
Pf
, and

\langle Pf,PQf\rangle=-\gamma\|Pf\|\,\|Qf\|\implies PQf=-\gamma\frac{\|Qf\|}{\|Pf\|}Pf.

By the same argument with
P
and
Q
interchanged,

QPf=-\gamma\frac{\|Pf\|}{\|Qf\|}\,Qf.

Consequently,

PQPf=P(QPf)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\gamma\frac{\|Pf\|}{\|Qf\|}}}\right)\,P(Qf)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\gamma\frac{\|Qf\|}{\|Pf\|}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\gamma\frac{\|Pf\|}{\|Qf\|}}}\right)\,Pf=\gamma^{2}Pf.\qed

3.
Revisiting the Greaves–Zhu argument

For a parameter vector
\vec{p}
, let

m_{\vec{p}}:=\max_{1\leq i<j<k\leq n}\sqrt{p_{i,j}p_{j,k}p_{k,i}+p_{k,j}p_{j,i}p_{i,k}}.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
3
, Lemma 3.3]
)
.

Let
\vec{p}
be regular. Then,
m_{\vec{p}}\leq 1/2
.

The following is the analogue of
[
3
, Proposition 3.1]
for
K
and follows using exactly the same argument. We record the argument below for the reader’s convenience since we will need to isolate the equality conditions.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
. Then,

\langle f,L^{2}f\rangle\geq\frac{1-2m_{\vec{p}}\cos(\pi/n)}{n-1}\,\langle f,Lf\rangle.

Proof.

First, if
|r-s|>1
, then
F_{r}F_{s}=F_{s}F_{r}
by
Lemma

2.3
. Since
F_{r},F_{s}
are orthogonal projections, it follows that
F_{r}F_{s}
is idempotent and self-adjoint. Therefore,

\langle{F_{r}f},{F_{s}f}\rangle=\langle{f},{F_{r}F_{s}f}\rangle=\lVert F_{r}F_{s}f\rVert^{2}\geq 0.

Next, let
1\leq r\leq n-2
. By
Corollary

2.5
, on each
G_{r}
-orbit
\mathcal{O}
, every nonzero eigenvalue of
F_{r}+F_{r+1}
is at least
1-m_{\vec{p}}
.

Therefore, applying
Lemma

2.6
with
P=F_{r}
and
Q=F_{r+1}
shows that for all
1\leq r\leq n-2
,

\langle{F_{r}f},{F_{r+1}f}\rangle\geq-m_{\vec{p}}\lVert F_{r}f\rVert\lVert F_{r+1}f\rVert.

Therefore

\displaystyle\langle{f},{L^{2}f}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}\Bigg(\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}\lVert F_{r}f\rVert^{2}+2\sum_{r=1}^{n-2}\langle{F_{r}f},{F_{r+1}f}\rangle+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r<s\leq n-1\\
|r-s|>1\end{subarray}}\langle F_{r}f,F_{s}f\rangle\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}\Bigg(\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}\lVert F_{r}f\rVert^{2}-2m_{\vec{p}}\sum_{r=1}^{n-2}\lVert F_{r}f\rVert\lVert F_{r+1}f\rVert\Bigg).

Set
v_{r}:=\lVert F_{r}f\rVert
and
\vec{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n-1})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. The right-hand side is
v^{T}Tv
, where

T=T_{\vec{p}}:=\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-m_{\vec{p}}\\
-m_{\vec{p}}&1&-m_{\vec{p}}\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&-m_{\vec{p}}&1&-m_{\vec{p}}\\
&&&-m_{\vec{p}}&1\end{pmatrix}.

The eigenvalues of the unscaled tridiagonal matrix are
1-2m_{\vec{p}}\cos(k\pi/n)
,
1\leq k\leq n-1
(see
[
1
, Section 1.4.4]
); hence the smallest eigenvalue of
T
is

\frac{1-2m_{\vec{p}}\cos(\pi/n)}{(n-1)^{2}}.

Thus

\langle{f},{L^{2}f}\rangle\geq\frac{1-2m_{\vec{p}}\cos(\pi/n)}{(n-1)^{2}}\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}\lVert F_{r}f\rVert^{2}.

Finally, since each
F_{r}
is an orthogonal projection,

\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}\lVert F_{r}f\rVert^{2}=\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}\langle{f},{F_{r}f}\rangle=(n-1)\langle{f},{Lf}\rangle.\qed

Let
\lambda_{L}
denote the smallest positive eigenvalue of
L
. We now examine the consequences of
\lambda_{L}=\lambda_{*}
.
Choose a nonzero eigenvector
g\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
such that

Lg=\lambda_{*}g.

Since
\vec{p}
is regular,
Proposition

3.2
and
Lemma

3.1
give

\lambda_{*}=\frac{\langle g,L^{2}g\rangle}{\langle g,Lg\rangle}\geq\frac{1-2m_{\vec{p}}\cos(\pi/n)}{n-1}\geq\frac{1-\cos(\pi/n)}{n-1}=\lambda_{*}.

Hence both inequalities must be equalities. In particular,

(3.1)

m_{\vec{p}}=\frac{1}{2}.

and equality holds throughout the proof of
Proposition

3.2
for the vector
g
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\vec{p}
be regular and
let
0\neq g\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
satisfy

Lg=\lambda_{*}g.

For
r\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, define

u_{r}:=F_{r}g,\qquad v_{r}:=\lVert u_{r}\rVert.

The following hold.

(a)

If
|r-s|>1
, then
F_{r}F_{s}g=0
.

(b)

The vector
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n-1})
is a positive multiple of

(\sin(\pi/n),\sin(2\pi/n),\dots,\sin((n-1)\pi/n)).

In particular,
u_{r}\neq 0
for every
r
.

(c)

For every
1\leq r\leq n-2
,

\langle{u_{r}},{u_{r+1}}\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\lVert u_{r}\rVert\lVert u_{r+1}\rVert.

Proof.

The proof of
Proposition

3.2
yields

\displaystyle\langle{g},{L^{2}g}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}\Bigg(\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}\lVert u_{r}\rVert^{2}+2\sum_{r=1}^{n-2}\langle{u_{r}},{u_{r+1}}\rangle+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r<s\leq n-1\\
|r-s|>1\end{subarray}}\langle{u_{r}},{u_{s}}\rangle\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}\Bigg(\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}v_{r}^{2}-2m_{\vec{p}}\sum_{r=1}^{n-2}v_{r}v_{r+1}\Bigg)=v^{T}T_{\vec{p}}v\geq\lambda_{\min}(T_{\vec{p}})\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}v_{r}^{2},

where
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n-1})^{T}
. Because equality holds throughout and
m_{\vec{p}}=1/2
, every step above is an equality.

For
|r-s|>1
, we have

\langle{u_{r}},{u_{s}}\rangle=\lVert F_{r}F_{s}g\rVert^{2}\geq 0.

Since the sum of these nonnegative terms is zero, each one vanishes. This proves (a).

Equality in the Rayleigh bound forces
v
to lie in the one-dimensional eigenspace of
T
corresponding to its smallest eigenvalue. That eigenspace is spanned by

(\sin(\pi/n),\sin(2\pi/n),\dots,\sin((n-1)\pi/n)),

all of whose entries are positive.
Since
v_{r}\geq 0
and
v\neq 0
, statement (b) follows.

Finally,

\sum_{r=1}^{n-2}\Bigl(\langle{u_{r}},{u_{r+1}}\rangle+\tfrac{1}{2}v_{r}v_{r+1}\Bigr)=0.

Each summand is nonnegative, because
Lemma

2.6
gives

\langle u_{r},u_{r+1}\rangle\geq-m_{\vec{p}}v_{r}v_{r+1}=-\tfrac{1}{2}v_{r}v_{r+1}.

Hence every summand is zero, proving (c).
∎

4.
The key orbital relation

Throughout this section, let
\vec{p}
be regular and let
0\neq g\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
satisfy

Lg=\lambda_{*}g.

Recall that this forces
m_{\vec{p}}=1/2
and
Lemma

3.3
. In particular, by
Lemma

3.3
(a), for every
s\geq 3
,

F_{s}u_{1}=F_{s}F_{1}g=0.

Since
F_{s}=I-E_{s}
, this means
E_{s}u_{1}=u_{1}
for every
s\geq 3
. Thus
u_{1}
is invariant under all adjacent transpositions in positions
3,4,\dots,n
, so
u_{1}
depends only on the first two labels.

Remark 4.1
.

This characterization of
u_{1}
is why we chose to work with
F
and
L
instead of
E
and
K
. Note also that the choice of
u_{1}
is important for this invariance statement (although we could have worked with
u_{n-1}
as well).

For
1\leq a<b\leq n
, define
U_{a,b}
by

(4.1)

U_{a,b}:=u_{1}(x)\qquad\text{whenever }x_{1}=a,\ x_{2}=b.

Because
u_{1}\neq 0
, the family
U=(U_{a,b})_{1\leq a<b\leq n}
is not identically zero.

Further, since
F_{1}
is idempotent,
F_{1}u_{1}=u_{1}
, equivalently
E_{1}u_{1}=0
. Hence, if
x
is any permutation whose first two labels are
a,b
with
a<b
and
x^{\tau_{1}}
is obtained by swapping those two labels, then

u_{1}(x^{\tau_{1}})=-\frac{p_{a,b}}{p_{b,a}}u_{1}(x)=-\frac{p_{a,b}}{p_{b,a}}U_{a,b}.

Thus, the values of
u_{1}
on states whose first two labels are
b,a
are also determined by
U_{a,b}
.

The main goal of this section is to prove the following linear relation on every orbit which supports
u_{1}
. In the statement below, let
i<j<k
, and let
\omega
be any ordering of the remaining labels

[n]\setminus\{i,j,k\}
.
Consider the corresponding
G_{1}
-orbit

\mathcal{O}(i,j,k;\omega):=\{(i,j,k,\omega),\ (j,i,k,\omega),\ (i,k,j,\omega),\ (j,k,i,\omega),\ (k,i,j,\omega),\ (k,j,i,\omega)\}.

Because
u_{1}
depends only on the first two labels, its restriction to
\mathcal{O}(i,j,k;\omega)
is
completely determined by the three coefficients
U_{i,j},U_{i,k},U_{j,k}
and the swapped
values forced by
E_{1}u_{1}=0
; explicitly,

u_{1}|_{\mathcal{O}(i,j,k;\omega)}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U_{i,j},\,-\frac{p_{i,j}}{p_{j,i}}U_{i,j},\,U_{i,k},\,U_{j,k},\,-\frac{p_{i,k}}{p_{k,i}}U_{i,k},\,-\frac{p_{j,k}}{p_{k,j}}U_{j,k}}}\right).

In particular,
u_{1}|_{\mathcal{O}(i,j,k;\omega)}\not\equiv 0
if and only if at least one of

U_{i,j},U_{i,k},U_{j,k}
is nonzero.

Proposition 4.2
.

Fix
i<j<k
and an ordering
\omega
of the remaining labels
[n]\setminus\{i,j,k\}
. If
u_{1}
is not identically zero on the orbit
\mathcal{O}(i,j,k;\omega)
, then

p_{i,j}=p_{j,k}=\frac{1}{2}

and

U_{i,k}=2p_{k,i}(U_{i,j}+U_{j,k}).

The proof of this proposition relies on the following lemma. Recall the decomposition

\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}=\oplus_{t\in T_{1}}V_{\mathcal{O}_{t}}

from
Equation

2.1
. Corresponding to this, decompose

g=\sum_{t\in T_{1}}g_{t},

where
g_{t}
is supported only on
\mathcal{O}_{t}
. In particular, for
t\neq s
,
\langle g_{s},g_{t}\rangle=0
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
t\in T_{1}
, let
i<j<k
be the three labels appearing in positions
1,2,3
on
\mathcal{O}_{t}
. If
\lVert F_{1}g_{t}\rVert>0
, then the following hold.

•

\langle F_{1}g_{t},F_{2}g_{t}\rangle=-(1/2)\lVert F_{1}g_{t}\rVert\lVert F_{2}g_{t}\rVert
;

•

s_{\mathcal{O}_{t}}=1/4
;

•

p_{i,j}=p_{j,k}=\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Because the supports of the
g_{t}
are disjoint and both
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
preserve each
orbit
\mathcal{O}_{t}
, we have orthogonal decompositions

u_{1}=\sum_{t\in T_{1}}F_{1}g_{t},\qquad u_{2}=\sum_{t\in T_{1}}F_{2}g_{t}.

Hence

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\|u_{1}\|\,\|u_{2}\|

\displaystyle=\langle u_{1},u_{2}\rangle=\sum_{t\in T_{1}}\langle F_{1}g_{t},F_{2}g_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq-\sum_{t\in T_{1}}\sqrt{s_{\mathcal{O}_{t}}}\|F_{1}g_{t}\|\|F_{2}g_{t}\|

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t\in T_{1}}\|F_{1}g_{t}\|\|F_{2}g_{t}\|\geq-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\sum_{t\in T_{1}}\|F_{1}g_{t}\|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\Bigl(\sum_{t\in T_{1}}\|F_{2}g_{t}\|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}=-\frac{1}{2}\|u_{1}\|\,\|u_{2}\|.

The first line uses
Lemma

3.3
(c). The second line is
Lemma

2.6
applied to each real inner product space
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{O}_{t}}
, using
Corollary

2.5
to
see that every nonzero eigenvalue of
(F_{1}+F_{2})|_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{O}_{t}}}
is at least
1-\sqrt{s_{\mathcal{O}_{t}}}
.
The last line uses
\sqrt{s_{\mathcal{O}_{t}}}\leq 1/2
, which follows from
Lemma

3.1
, followed by Cauchy–Schwarz.

Since the first and final terms are equal, all inequalities must be equalities, immediately showing the first two bullets.

It remains to deduce
p_{i,j}=p_{j,k}=1/2
. By definition,

s_{\mathcal{O}_{t}}=p_{i,j}p_{j,k}(1-p_{i,k})+(1-p_{i,j})(1-p_{j,k})p_{i,k}=p_{i,j}p_{j,k}+p_{i,k}\bigl(1-p_{i,j}-p_{j,k}\bigr).

Since
\vec{p}
is regular and
i<j<k
, we have
1/2\leq p_{i,j},p_{j,k}
and so
1-p_{i,j}-p_{j,k}\leq 0
. Thus by monotonicity,

s_{\mathcal{O}_{t}}\leq p_{i,j}p_{j,k}+p_{j,k}\bigl(1-p_{i,j}-p_{j,k}\bigr)=p_{j,k}(1-p_{j,k})\leq\frac{1}{4},

and also

s_{\mathcal{O}_{t}}\leq p_{i,j}p_{j,k}+p_{i,j}\bigl(1-p_{i,j}-p_{j,k}\bigr)=p_{i,j}(1-p_{i,j})\leq\frac{1}{4}.

As we have already shown
s_{\mathcal{O}_{t}}=1/4
, these are equalities. Noting that
x(1-x)=1/4
occurs only at
x=1/2
completes the proof.
∎

We can now prove the main result of this section.

Proof of
Proposition

4.2
.

Let
t\in T_{1}
be the representative of the orbit
\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}(i,j,k;\omega)=\mathcal{O}_{t}
. Suppose that
u_{1}
is not identically zero on the orbit. By definition, this means that
\|F_{1}g_{t}\|>0
, so that by
Lemma

4.3
,

p_{i,j}=p_{j,k}=\frac{1}{2}.

It remains to prove the second conclusion.

By
Lemma

4.3
,

\langle F_{1}g_{t},F_{2}g_{t}\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\|F_{1}g_{t}\|\,\|F_{2}g_{t}\|\neq 0.

Moreover, since
s_{\mathcal{O}}=1/4
by
Lemma

4.3
, it follows from
Corollary

2.5
that the
nonzero eigenvalues of
(F_{1}+F_{2})|_{\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{O}}}
are
1/2,3/2,2
. Hence
Lemma

2.6

applies on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{O}}
and yields

F_{1}F_{2}F_{1}g_{t}=\frac{1}{4}F_{1}g_{t}.

In the ordered basis

(i,j,k,\omega),\ (j,i,k,\omega),\ (i,k,j,\omega),\ (j,k,i,\omega),\ (k,i,j,\omega),\ (k,j,i,\omega)

of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{O}}
, the restrictions of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are

F_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}&-\frac{1}{2}&0&0&0&0\\
-\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}&0&0&0&0\\
0&0&p_{k,i}&0&-p_{k,i}&0\\
0&0&0&\frac{1}{2}&0&-\frac{1}{2}\\
0&0&-p_{i,k}&0&p_{i,k}&0\\
0&0&0&-\frac{1}{2}&0&\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad F_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}&0&-\frac{1}{2}&0&0&0\\
0&p_{k,i}&0&-p_{k,i}&0&0\\
-\frac{1}{2}&0&\frac{1}{2}&0&0&0\\
0&-p_{i,k}&0&p_{i,k}&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&\frac{1}{2}&-\frac{1}{2}\\
0&0&0&0&-\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Moreover, since
p_{i,j}=p_{j,i}=p_{j,k}=p_{k,j}=1/2
, then in the same basis, we have

F_{1}g_{t}=(U_{i,j},-U_{i,j},U_{i,k},U_{j,k},-(p_{i,k}/p_{k,i})U_{i,k},-U_{j,k})

Then, a direct computation shows

F_{1}F_{2}F_{1}g_{t}-\frac{1}{4}F_{1}g_{t}=\frac{U_{i,k}-2p_{k,i}(U_{i,j}+U_{j,k})}{4}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-1,1,1,-\frac{p_{i,k}}{p_{k,i}},-\frac{p_{i,k}}{p_{k,i}},\frac{p_{i,k}}{p_{k,i}}}}\right).

Therefore

U_{i,k}=2p_{k,i}(U_{i,j}+U_{j,k}),

as desired.
∎

5.
Existence of a neutral label

We can now prove the first part of
Theorem

1.8
; this confirms
1.4
. Recall that
\vec{p}
is a regular parameter vector satisfying

\lambda_{K}=\lambda_{L}=\lambda_{*}.

Recall also that
g\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
is a nonzero vector satisfying
Lg=\lambda_{*}g
and
u_{1}=F_{1}g
. For
1\leq a<b\leq n
,
U_{a,b}
are defined in
Equation

4.1
.

Define

S:=\{(a,b):1\leq a<b\leq n,\ U_{a,b}\neq 0\}.

Thus
(a,b)\in S
means exactly that
u_{1}
is nonzero on the class of permutations whose first
two labels are
a,b
. The set
S
is nonempty because
u_{1}\neq 0
.

We need two structural facts about
S
.

Lemma 5.1
.

There exist indices
b\in\{2,\dots,n\}
and
a\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
such that

(1,b)\in S,\qquad(a,n)\in S.

Proof.

We prove the left-boundary statement first. Choose
(a,b)\in S
with
a
minimal. Then

U_{a,b}\neq 0
.

Suppose for contradiction that
a>1
. Consider the triple
a-1<a<b
. Since one of the three
coefficients attached to this triple, namely
U_{a,b}
, is nonzero,
Proposition

4.2

applies and gives

U_{a-1,b}=2p_{b,a-1}(U_{a-1,a}+U_{a,b}).

By the minimality of
a
, this becomes

0=2p_{b,a-1}U_{a,b},

a contradiction as
0<p_{b,a-1}
and
U_{a,b}\neq 0
by assumption.

The proof of the right-boundary statement is analogous by regarding the triple
a<b<b+1
.
∎

Now define

B:=\max\{b:(1,b)\in S\},\qquad A:=\min\{a:(a,n)\in S\}.

These are nonempty sets by
Lemma

5.1
.

Lemma 5.2
.

With notation as above,
A\leq B
.

Proof.

If
A=1
, then
A\leq B
, so assume
A>1
. By
Proposition

4.2
on the triple
1<A<n
(since
U_{A,n}\neq 0
), we have

U_{1,n}=2p_{n,1}(U_{1,A}+U_{A,n}).

If
A>B
, then
U_{1,A}=U_{1,n}=0
, giving

0=2p_{n,1}U_{A,n}.

Again
0<p_{n,1}<1
and
U_{A,n}\neq 0
, a contradiction. Therefore
A\leq B
.
∎

With these ingredients, we can proceed to the proof of
Theorem

1.8
(1).

Proof of
Theorem

1.8
(1).

By
Lemma

5.2
the interval

I:=\bigl[\max\{2,A\},\,\min\{B,n-1\}\bigr]\cap\mathbb{Z}

is nonempty. Choose any integer
c\in I
.
We claim that

p_{1,c}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad p_{c,n}=\frac{1}{2}.

To prove the first equality, we need only find some triple with one nonzero attached coefficient and cite
Proposition

4.2
.

If
c<B
, then the triple
1<c<B
has
U_{1,B}\neq 0
by maximality of
B
. If
c=B
, then
(1,c)\in S
, so the triple
1<c<n
has the nonzero attached coefficient

U_{1,c}\neq 0
. Thus
p_{1,c}=1/2
by
Proposition

4.2
.

The proof of
p_{c,n}=1/2
is similar, now using
(A,n)\in S
.
Apply
Proposition

4.2
to the triple
A<c<n
(if
c>A
) or
1<c<n
(if
c=A
).

We now propagate these equalities to full neutrality of the label
c
using the monotonicity conditions.
Regularity of
\vec{p}
implies

p_{i,j}\geq 1/2
for every
i<j
; indeed, for fixed
i<j
one has
p_{i,j}\geq p_{i,i+1}\geq 1/2
.
Also, by monotonicity in the first coordinate, for
i<c
,

p_{i,c}\leq p_{1,c}=\frac{1}{2},

and by monotonicity in the second coordinate, for
j>c
,

p_{c,j}\leq p_{c,n}=\frac{1}{2}.

Combining these inequalities with the lower bound
p_{i,j}\geq 1/2
gives
p_{c,j}=1/2
for all
j\neq c
,
so
c
is a neutral label.
∎

6.
Multiplicity of the second largest eigenvalue

In this section, we prove
Theorem

1.8
(2), thereby confirming
1.7
. Recall that
M(\vec{p})
is defined to be the algebraic multiplicity of
1-\lambda_{*}
as an eigenvalue of
K
. By
Proposition

2.1
,
M(\vec{p})
is equal to the algebraic multiplicity of
\lambda_{*}
as an eigenvalue of
L
. Accordingly, let

\mathcal{E}_{*}:=\ker(L-\lambda_{*}I).

Since
L
is self-adjoint with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
,
\mathcal{E}_{*}
has dimension equal to the algebraic multiplicity of
\lambda_{*}
as an eigenvalue of
L
, so that

M(\vec{p})=\dim\mathcal{E}_{*}.

If
N(\vec{p})=0
, then
Theorem

1.8
(1) gives
\lambda_{L}>\lambda_{*}
, so
M(\vec{p})=0
. So, it remains to prove the multiplicity conjecture for
N(\vec{p})\geq 1
. We will work under this assumption for the rest of this section.

6.1.
Upper bound

We first prove the upper bound in
Theorem

1.8
(2). Fix a regular parameter vector
\vec{p}
with
N(\vec{p})\geq 1
. Define

A:=\min\{c\in[n]:c\text{ is neutral}\},\quad B:=\max\{c\in[n]:c\text{ is neutral}\},

so that

N(\vec{p})=B-A+1.

The labels
A
and
B
divide the interval
[n]
into three (possibly empty) regions:
i<A
,
i\in[A,B]
,
i>B
.

Definition 6.1
.

We say that the pair
1\leq i<j\leq n
is crossing if
i<A
and
j>B
. Otherwise, we say that the pair is non-crossing.

The following lemma records some elementary consequences of regularity.

Lemma 6.2
.

The following hold.

(1)

Every label in
[A,B]
is neutral.

(2)

If
A=1
or
B=n
, then
\vec{p}=\vec{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}
.

(3)

If
\vec{p}\neq\vec{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}
, so that
2\leq A\leq B\leq n-1
, then

p_{i,j}=\frac{1}{2}\qquad\text{whenever }1\leq i<j\leq n\text{ is non-crossing.}

In particular, for
i<A
, the label
i
is non-neutral if and only if there exists
k>B
with
p_{i,k}>1/2
; likewise, for
j>B
, the label
j
is non-neutral if and only if there exists
k<A
with
p_{k,j}>1/2
.

Proof.

For (i), the endpoints
A
and
B
are neutral by definition, so fix
c\in(A,B)
.

For any
i<c
, by regularity,
p_{i,c}\geq 1/2
. By monotonicity in the second coordinate,
p_{i,c}\leq p_{i,B}=1/2
, so
p_{i,c}=1/2
.

Similarly, for any
j>c
, by regularity and monotonicity,
1/2\leq p_{c,j}\leq p_{A,j}=1/2
. Thus
c
is neutral.

For (ii), suppose first that
A=1
, so label
1
is neutral. Then, for every
1\leq i<j\leq n
, monotonicity in the first coordinate gives

p_{i,j}\leq p_{1,j}=\frac{1}{2}.

Regularity again gives
p_{i,j}\geq 1/2
, so
p_{i,j}=1/2
for all
i<j
. Hence
\vec{p}=\vec{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}
. The case
B=n
is analogous, using monotonicity in the second coordinate.

Now assume
\vec{p}\neq\vec{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}
. By (ii), we then have
2\leq A\leq B\leq n-1
. To prove (iii), let
i<j
be a non-crossing pair. If
j\leq B
, then
B
is neutral, so monotonicity in the second coordinate gives

p_{i,j}\leq p_{i,B}=\frac{1}{2}.

If instead
i\geq A
, then
A
is neutral, so monotonicity in the first coordinate gives

p_{i,j}\leq p_{A,j}=\frac{1}{2}.

In either case, regularity forces
p_{i,j}=1/2
. ∎

As in
Section

4
, fix
0\neq g\in\mathcal{E}_{*}
and let
u_{1}=F_{1}g
. Recall
U_{a,b}
from
Equation

4.1
. For convenience of notation, for
r\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, set

D_{r}:=U_{r,r+1}

The next lemma shows that the entire family
(U_{a,b})_{1\leq a<b\leq n}
is determined by the values
(D_{r})_{1\leq r\leq n-1}
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Assume
\vec{p}\neq\vec{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}
. The following hold.

(1)

If
1\leq i<j<A
or
B<i<j\leq n
, then
U_{i,j}=0
.

(2)

If
r\notin[A-1,B]
, then
D_{r}=0
.

(3)

If
1\leq i<j\leq n
is non-crossing, then

U_{i,j}=\sum_{r=i}^{j-1}D_{r}=\sum_{r\in[i,j-1]\cap[A-1,B]}D_{r}.

(4)

If
1\leq i<j\leq n
is crossing, then

U_{i,j}=2p_{j,i}\sum_{r=i}^{j-1}D_{r}=2p_{j,i}\sum_{r=A-1}^{B}D_{r}.

Proof.

For (i), suppose first that
i<j<A
. Since
j
is non-neutral,
Lemma

6.2
(iii) yields some
k>B
with
p_{j,k}>1/2
. If one of
U_{i,j},U_{i,k},U_{j,k}
were nonzero, then
Proposition

4.2
applied to the triple
i<j<k
would force
p_{j,k}=1/2
, a contradiction. Hence,
U_{i,j}=0
. The case
B<i<j
is analogous.

Statement (ii) is immediate from (i) applied to adjacent pairs.

For (iii), we argue by induction on
j-i
. If
j=i+1
, then

U_{i,j}=U_{i,i+1}=D_{i}

by definition. Now let
j-i\geq 2
, and assume the claim holds for all smaller gaps. Since
(i,j)
is
non-crossing, the pairs
(i,j-1)
,
(j-1,j)
are also non-crossing. Hence

Lemma

6.2
(iii) gives

p_{i,j-1}=p_{j-1,j}=p_{i,j}=\frac{1}{2}.

By the induction hypothesis,

S:=U_{i,j-1}+D_{j-1}=\sum_{r=i}^{j-1}D_{r}.

If
S=0
, then if
U_{i,j}\neq 0
,
Proposition

4.2
applied to the triple

i<j-1<j
yields

U_{i,j}=2p_{j,i}\bigl(U_{i,j-1}+U_{j-1,j}\bigr)=U_{i,j-1}+D_{j-1}=S=0,

a contradiction. Hence
U_{i,j}=0=S
.

If instead
S\neq 0
, then at least one of
U_{i,j-1}
and
D_{j-1}
is nonzero, so

Proposition

4.2
applied to
i<j-1<j
gives

U_{i,j}=2p_{j,i}\bigl(U_{i,j-1}+U_{j-1,j}\bigr)=U_{i,j-1}+D_{j-1}=S=\sum_{r=i}^{j-1}D_{r}.

This proves the first equality in (iii). The second follows from part (ii).

For (iv), let
1\leq i<j\leq n
be crossing, and fix any
c\in[A,B]
. Then both pairs

(i,c)
and
(c,j)
are non-crossing. Hence part (iii) gives

U_{i,c}=\sum_{r=i}^{c-1}D_{r},\qquad U_{c,j}=\sum_{r=c}^{j-1}D_{r}.

Therefore

T:=U_{i,c}+U_{c,j}=\sum_{r=i}^{j-1}D_{r}.

If
T=0
, then if
U_{i,j}\neq 0
,
Proposition

4.2
applied to the triple

i<c<j
gives

U_{i,j}=2p_{j,i}\bigl(U_{i,c}+U_{c,j}\bigr)=2p_{j,i}T=0,

a contradiction. Hence
U_{i,j}=0=2p_{j,i}T
, and the required identity follows.

Assume now that
T\neq 0
. Then at least one of
U_{i,c}
and
U_{c,j}
is nonzero, so

Proposition

4.2
applied to
i<c<j
yields

U_{i,j}=2p_{j,i}\bigl(U_{i,c}+U_{c,j}\bigr)=2p_{j,i}T=2p_{j,i}\sum_{r=i}^{j-1}D_{r}.

This proves the first equality in (iv). The second follows from part (ii).
∎

Corollary 6.4
.

Assume
\vec{p}\neq\vec{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}
. Then the linear map

\mathcal{E}_{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{B-A+2},\qquad g\mapsto(D_{A-1}(g),\dots,D_{B}(g)),

is injective.

Proof.

If all of
D_{A-1}(g),\dots,D_{B}(g)
vanish, then
Lemma

6.3
shows that
U_{i,j}(g)=0
for every
1\leq i<j\leq n
. Hence
u_{1}=F_{1}g=0
, and therefore
g=0
by
Lemma

3.3
(b).
∎

We can now obtain the upper bound in
Theorem

1.8
(2).

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
\vec{p}
be regular. Then

M(\vec{p})\leq\begin{cases}N(\vec{p}),&\text{if }N(\vec{p})\notin\{n,n-2\},\\
n-1,&\text{if }N(\vec{p})\in\{n,n-2\}.\end{cases}

Proof.

If
N(\vec{p})=n
, i.e. if
\vec{p}=\vec{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}
, then the same argument as
Lemma

6.3
(iii) shows that for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
,

U_{i,j}=\sum_{r=i}^{j-1}D_{r}.

Therefore, the same argument as in
Corollary

6.4
shows that

M(\vec{p})=\dim\mathcal{E}_{*}\leq n-1.

We may therefore assume that
\vec{p}\neq\vec{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}
, so that
2\leq A\leq B\leq n-1
. By
Corollary

6.4
,

M(\vec{p})=\dim\mathcal{E}_{*}\leq B-A+2=N(\vec{p})+1\neq n-1.

This proves the upper bound in the case
N(\vec{p})=n-2
, so it remains to treat the case
(A,B)\neq(2,n-1)
, where we want to gain an additional dimension over the bound above.

For this, it suffices to show that every
g\in\mathcal{E}_{*}
satisfies

(6.1)

\sum_{r=A-1}^{B}D_{r}(g)=0.

so that the image of
\mathcal{E}_{*}
under the linear map in
Corollary

6.4
is contained in a linear space of dimension
B-A+1
.

To prove
Equation

6.1
, fix
g\in\mathcal{E}_{*}
.
If
A>2
, then
A-1
is non-neutral and greater than
1
. By
Lemma

6.2
(iii), there exists
k>B
with
p_{A-1,k}>1/2
, and monotonicity in the second coordinate gives

p_{A-1,n}\geq p_{A-1,k}>\frac{1}{2}.

Hence, for the triple
1<A-1<n
,
Proposition

4.2
implies that

U_{1,n}(g)=0.

If instead
A=2
, then our assumption
(A,B)\neq(2,n-1)
forces
B<n-1
. In that case
B+1
is non-neutral, and because
1
is the only label to its left outside the neutral interval,
Lemma

6.2
(iii) yields

p_{1,B+1}>\frac{1}{2}.

Applying
Proposition

4.2
to the triple
1<B+1<n
again gives

U_{1,n}(g)=0.

Finally,
Lemma

6.3
(iv) with the crossing pair
(i,j)=(1,n)
gives

0=U_{1,n}(g)=2p_{n,1}\sum_{r=A-1}^{B}D_{r}(g).

This proves
Equation

6.1
and completes the proof.
∎

6.2.
Lower bound

We now prove the matching lower bounds for the multiplicity. The argument
splits into two parts. First, each neutral label
c
gives rise to an eigenfunction
f_{c}(x)
, which is the standard single-card eigenfunction for the adjacent-transposition
shuffle, going back to Wilson
[
4
]
, and in the present setting it applies whenever

c
is neutral. These eigenfunctions already give the desired lower bound in
all cases except
N(\vec{p})=n-2
. Thus the only remaining case is
N(\vec{p})=n-2
, where one needs an additional eigenfunction (
Proposition

6.8
). This part requires a new idea.

Throughout, let

h(r):=\cos\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{(r-\tfrac{1}{2})\pi}{n}}}\right)\qquad(1\leq r\leq n),\qquad a_{0}:=\frac{h(1)-h(2)}{2}.

Since
h(1)>h(2)
, one has
a_{0}\neq 0
.

Proposition 6.6
.

Assume that
c\in[n]
is neutral, and define
f_{c}:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by

f_{c}(x):=h(\operatorname{pos}_{x}(c)).

Then,
Lf_{c}=\lambda_{*}f_{c}.

Moreover, for every
1\leq r\leq n-1
,

D_{r}(f_{c})=a_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{r=c\}}-\mathbf{1}_{\{r=c-1\}}}}\right).

Proof.

Extend
h
to
\{0,1,\dots,n+1\}
by setting

h(0):=h(1),\qquad h(n+1):=h(n).

Then, by direct computation,

h(r-1)+h(r+1)=2\cos(\pi/n)h(r)\qquad(1\leq r\leq n).

Let
x\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and write
r:=\operatorname{pos}_{x}(c)
. Since
c
is neutral, its
position evolves as the lazy nearest-neighbour walk on
[n]
that moves one
step left or right with probability
1/(2(n-1))
whenever that move is
available. Therefore

(Kf_{c})(x)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{1}{n-1}}}\right)h(r)+\frac{h(r-1)+h(r+1)}{2(n-1)}=(1-\lambda_{*})h(r)=(1-\lambda_{*})f_{c}(x),

so
Lf_{c}=\lambda_{*}f_{c}
.

We next compute
D_{r}(f_{c})
.
Let
x\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. If neither of the first two labels of
x
is
c
, then swapping
those labels does not change
\operatorname{pos}_{x}(c)
. Thus

(F_{1}f_{c})(x)=f_{c}(x)-E_{1}f_{c}(x)=0.

Therefore, if
r\notin\{c-1,c\}
,

D_{r}(f_{c})=U_{r,r+1}(f_{c})=0.

Now suppose that the first two labels are
c,b
with
c<b
. Then
c
is in position

1
in
x
and in position
2
in
x^{\tau_{1}}
. Since
c
is neutral, we have

\displaystyle U_{c,b}(f_{c})

\displaystyle=(F_{1}f_{c})(x)=f_{c}(x)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{c,b}f_{c}(x)+p_{b,c}f_{c}(x^{\tau_{1}})}}\right)

\displaystyle=h(1)-\frac{1}{2}h(1)-\frac{1}{2}h(2)=a_{0}.

Taking
b=c+1
, we obtain
D_{c}(f_{c})=U_{c,c+1}(f_{c})=a_{0}.

Similarly, if the first two labels are
a,c
with
a<c
, then
c
is in position

2
in
x
and in position
1
in
x^{\tau_{1}}
. By a similar computation as above, we have

\displaystyle U_{a,c}(f_{c})=f_{c}(x)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{a,c}f_{c}(x)+p_{c,a}f_{c}(x^{\tau_{1}})}}\right)=h(2)-\frac{1}{2}h(2)-\frac{1}{2}h(1)=-a_{0}.

Taking
a=c-1
, we obtain

D_{c-1}(f_{c})=U_{c-1,c}(f_{c})=-a_{0}
as claimed.
∎

Corollary 6.7
.

Let
\vec{p}
be regular.

(1)

If
\vec{p}\neq\vec{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}
, then the family
\{f_{c}:A\leq c\leq B\}
is linearly independent. Consequently,

M(\vec{p})\geq N(\vec{p}).

(2)

If
\vec{p}=\vec{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}
, then the family
\{f_{c}:1\leq c\leq n\}
has rank
n-1
. Consequently,

M(\vec{p})\geq n-1.

Proof.

Let
D(f_{c}):=(D_{1}(f_{c}),\dots,D_{n-1}(f_{c}))\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.
By
Proposition

6.6
,

D(f_{c})=a_{0}(e_{c}-e_{c-1}),

where
e_{0}=e_{n}:=0
and
e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1}
are the standard basis vectors of

\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.

If
\vec{p}\neq\vec{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}
, then
2\leq A\leq B\leq n-1
, and the
vectors

e_{A}-e_{A-1},\ e_{A+1}-e_{A},\ \dots,\ e_{B}-e_{B-1}

are linearly independent. Hence so are
f_{A},\dots,f_{B}
, proving (i).
If
\vec{p}=\vec{p}_{\mathrm{unif}}
, then the vectors
e_{c}-e_{c-1}
for
1\leq c\leq n
span
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, proving (ii).
∎

It remains only to treat the exceptional case
N(\vec{p})=n-2
, where we need to produce an additional linearly independent eigenvector.

Proposition 6.8
.

Assume
N(\vec{p})=n-2
, so that the neutral interval is
[2,n-1]
.
Define
\psi:\mathfrak{S}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\psi(x):=\begin{cases}h(\operatorname{pos}_{x}(1))-h(\operatorname{pos}_{x}(n)),&\text{if }\operatorname{pos}_{x}(1)<\operatorname{pos}_{x}(n),\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{p_{1,n}}{p_{n,1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(\operatorname{pos}_{x}(1))-h(\operatorname{pos}_{x}(n))}}\right),&\text{if }\operatorname{pos}_{x}(n)<\operatorname{pos}_{x}(1).\end{cases}

Then,
L\psi=\lambda_{*}\psi
. Moreover,

D_{1}(\psi)=a_{0},\qquad D_{n-1}(\psi)=\frac{p_{1,n}}{p_{n,1}}a_{0},\qquad D_{r}(\psi)=0\quad(2\leq r\leq n-2).

In particular,
\psi
is linearly independent of
f_{2},\dots,f_{n-1}
.

Proof.

For convenience of notation, we set

q:=p_{n,1}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\frac{1}{2}}}\right),\qquad\rho:=\frac{1-q}{q}.

First, we verify that
K\psi(x)=(1-\lambda_{*})\psi(x)
. Fix
x\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, and write

a:=\operatorname{pos}_{x}(1),\qquad b:=\operatorname{pos}_{x}(n).

Assume first that
|a-b|>1
. Then the labels
1
and
n
are not compared in one step
from
x
, so their relative order cannot change after one step. Hence, on the entire
one-step neighbourhood
y
of
x
, the function
\psi(y)
is either

h(\operatorname{pos}_{y}(1))-h(\operatorname{pos}_{y}(n))\qquad\text{or}\qquad\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(\operatorname{pos}_{y}(1))-h(\operatorname{pos}_{y}(n))}}\right),

with the same choice holding throughout that neighbourhood. Moreover, every one-step
comparison involving the label
1
or the label
n
is with a neutral label, so the computation from
Proposition

6.6
applies
separately to the two terms. Therefore,
K\psi(x)=(1-\lambda_{*})\psi(x)
in this case.

It remains to treat the case
|a-b|=1
. Suppose first that
b=a+1
. Note that
E_{r}\psi(x)=\psi(x)
, unless
r\in\{a-1,a,a+1\}
. For the remaining cases, by direct computation and by neutrality of labels
\{2,\dots,n-1\}
, we have

•

E_{a}\psi(x)=(1-q)\psi(x)+q\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(a+1)-h(a)}}\right)=0.

•

E_{a-1}\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\psi(x)+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(a-1)-h(a+1)}}\right)=\psi(x)+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(a-1)-h(a)}}\right)
.

•

E_{a+1}\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\psi(x)+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(a)-h(a+2)}}\right)=\psi(x)+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(a+1)-h(a+2)}}\right).

Putting these cases together,

(n-1)K\psi(x)=(n-2)\psi(x)+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(a-1)-h(a)}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(a+1)-h(a+2)}}\right).

Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(a-1)-h(a)}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(a+1)-h(a+2)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(a-1)+h(a+1)}}\right)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(a)+h(a+2)}}\right)

\displaystyle=2\cos(\pi/n)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(a)-h(a+1)}}\right)

\displaystyle=2\cos(\pi/n)\,\psi(x).

Therefore

(n-1)K\psi(x)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n-2+\cos(\pi/n)}}\right)\psi(x)=(n-1)(1-\lambda_{*})\psi(x).

A similar argument also shows that
K\psi(x)=(1-\lambda_{*})\psi(x)
if
a=b+1
.

Finally, we compute
D_{r}(\psi)
for
1\leq r\leq n-1
. If
2\leq r\leq n-2
, then the first two labels are
r,r+1
, so swapping them does not
change the positions of
1
and
n
, nor their relative order. Hence

D_{r}(\psi)=0\qquad(2\leq r\leq n-2).

Now suppose that the first two labels of
x
are
1,2
, and write
b:=\operatorname{pos}_{x}(n)>2
. Then the
label
1
lies to the left of
n
both before and after swapping the first two labels.
Since the label
2
is neutral, we have

\displaystyle D_{1}(\psi)

\displaystyle=(F_{1}\psi)(x)=\psi(x)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{1,2}\psi(x)+p_{2,1}\psi(x^{\tau_{1}})}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(1)-h(b)}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(1)-h(b)}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(2)-h(b)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(1)-h(2)}}\right)=a_{0}.

Similarly, suppose that the first two labels of
x
are
n-1,n
, and write
a:=\operatorname{pos}_{x}(1)>2
.
Then the label
n
lies to the left of
1
both before and after swapping the first two
labels. Since the label
n-1
is neutral, we obtain

\displaystyle D_{n-1}(\psi)

\displaystyle=(F_{1}\psi)(x)=\psi(x)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p_{n-1,n}\psi(x)+p_{n,n-1}\psi(x^{\tau_{1}})}}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(a)-h(2)}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(a)-h(2)}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\rho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(a)-h(1)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h(1)-h(2)}}\right)=\rho a_{0}.

Finally, every linear combination of
D(f_{2}),\dots,D(f_{n-1})
has coordinate sum zero,
since each
D(f_{c})
is a multiple of
e_{c}-e_{c-1}
. On the other hand,

\sum_{r=1}^{n-1}D_{r}(\psi)=a_{0}+\rho a_{0}\neq 0.

Hence
D(\psi)
does not lie in the span of
D(f_{2}),\dots,D(f_{n-1})
, and therefore

\psi
is linearly independent of
f_{2},\dots,f_{n-1}
.
∎
