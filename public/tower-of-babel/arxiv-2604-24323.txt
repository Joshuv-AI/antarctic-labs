A Tour of Locality Sensitive Filtering on the Sphere
Luca Becchetti, Andrea Clementi, Luciano Gualà, Emanuele Natale, Luca Pepè Sciarria, Alessandro Straziota

Abstract
The Approximate Near Neighbor ( ANN ) problem is a cornerstone in high-dimensional data analysis, with applications ranging from information retrieval to data mining. Among the most successful paradigms for solving ANN in high-dimensional metric spaces is Locality Sensitive Hashing ( LSH ), alongside the more recent Locality Sensitive Filtering ( LSF ). Since the seminal work of Indyk and Motwani, literature has expanded into a complex landscape of variants, often presented under different perspectives and adopting different notation. In this work, we address the technical challenge of navigating this landscape, by providing a self-contained, unified view of the essential algorithmic ingredients governing LSH-based and LSF-based solutions for angular distance – a case of particular relevance in modern applications. In doing so, we touch on deep connections between LSF and LSH strategies. Our contribution is twofold. First, we design and analyze an LSF-based data structure for the Angular ANN problem, serving as a “guided tour” through fundamental techniques and results in the area. Second, we provide a streamlined analysis that, piecing together technical ingredients and results appeared throughout previous literature, proves optimality of the proposed data structure. In doing so, we revisit and strengthen a key technical lemma central to this body of work. The result is a critical and cohesive review that identifies core mechanisms of proximity search, offering both a streamlined entry point for researchers and a refined perspective on the state of the art.

Introduction

In the
Nearest Neighbour
problem, we are given a dataset
\mathcal{X}
of points from a metric space
(\mathcal{M},\textit{dist})
. The goal is to preprocess
\mathcal{X}
so as to efficiently answer queries of the form: given a point
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathcal{M}
, retrieve a point
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
that is among the closest to
{\mathbf{q}}
.
Despite its seeming simplicity, Nearest Neighbour entails a fundamental primitive in a wide range of modern applications – including machine learning, data mining, information retrieval, and computer vision – in which data are naturally represented as vectors in a feature space. In this setting (often referred to as the
vector space model
), similarity between objects is typically captured by a distance function, and identifying the most similar object reduces precisely to solving a nearest neighbor query. Canonical examples include similarity search in image databases, nearest-neighbor classification, and content recommendation.

This problem poses significant computational challenges when data volume grows to a scale. In this case, naive solutions with linear query-time and/or quadratic space are no longer feasible.
Moreover, known sublinear solutions exhibit an exponential dependence on the dimensionality of the data in the query time and/or space
[
7
,
6
,
15
,
19
,
24
]
, the well-known
curse of dimensionality
.

These limitations motivate the study of approximate variants of the problem.
In a seminal work, Indyk and Motwani
[
21
]
introduced
Locality-Sensitive Hashing
(LSH),
a randomized framework that allows to
approximate
the Nearest Neighbour

with polynomial preprocessing time, sublinear query time, and subquadratic space.
This approach leverages a reduction to the
(r,cr)
-Approximate Near Neighbor
(or
(r,cr)
-ANN) problem in which, given a query
{\mathbf{q}}
, we must return a point
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
at distance at most

{\mathbf{x}}
at distance at most
r
from
{\mathbf{q}}
exists in
\mathcal{X}
.
The idea behind LSH is to use hash functions that are
sensitive
with respect to the metric, i.e., such that points that are close are more likely to share the same hash value.
More formally, we say that a family
\mathcal{H}

(r,cr,p_{1},p_{2})

{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}

{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
are
close

\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=h({\mathbf{y}})\right)\leq p_{2}
if
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}
are at distance at least
cr
(we say that
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
are

Indyk and Motwani
[

\rho=\frac{\log\frac{1}{p_{1}}}{\log\frac{1}{p_{2}}}
.
This solution not only bypasses the curse of dimensionality, but it also achieves sublinear query time and subquadratic space whenever
p_{1}>p_{2}
.
A vast literature has developed on LSH, leading to the study of LSH functions for various metrics, including Hamming

Since

In particular, the best sensitive families known so far yield
\rho\leq\frac{1}{c}+o_{d}(1)

\rho\leq\frac{1}{c^{2}}+o_{d}(1)

[

.

On the other hand, the upper bounds to
\rho

,
26
]
.

The LSH framework is not the only approach to the
(r,cr)
-ANN problem. A related, yet different approach is the more recent
Locality Sensitive Filter
(LSF), introduced in
[
11
]
. One of the main advantages of LSF is that it potentially offers a more flexible and agile way to tune the behavior of the data structure and the underlying space-time trade-offs
[
18
]
. Informally, a
filter

h
is a boolean function, and we say that a data point
{\mathbf{x}}
passes the filter if
h({\mathbf{x}})=1
.
In the spirit of LSH, in LSF one designs a family of filters
\mathcal{F}
that is
sensitive
with respect to a desired distance, so that points that are close enough are more likely to pass the same filters than points that are farther apart. More formally, a family of filters
\mathcal{F}
is
(r,cr,q_{1},q_{2})
-
sensitive
for a metric space
(\mathcal{M},\textit{dist})
if, for a randomly chosen filter
h\in\mathcal{F}
, and for every
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{M}
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)\geq q_{1}
if
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
are “close”, and
\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)\leq q_{2}
if
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
are “far apart”.
LSH and LSF seemingly offer different perspectives on local sensitivity: While in LSH we are interested in the joint probability that two close points share the same hash value, in LSF our focus is on the probability that two points pass the same filter.
In other words, while a hash function induces a partition of the input space in LSH, a filter does not provide any additional information regarding points that did not pass a filter.
Similarly to LSH, one can associate a parameter
\rho=\frac{\log(1/q_{1})}{\log(1/q_{2})}
to an LSF family with parameters
q_{1},q_{2}
, which measures the discriminating power of the filters and determines the performance of the corresponding data structure.
Connections between LSH and LSF run deep and are reflected in a relationship between the corresponding versions of parameter
\rho
, something we discuss in a later section.

In this work, we consider the ANN problem when
\mathcal{M}
is the unit
d
-dimensional sphere
S^{d-1}
equipped with the
angular distance
, a variant we refer to as the
Angular ANN
problem.
This variant is highly relevant in practice, since angular distance is equivalent to Euclidean distance on the sphere and thus cosine similarity, commonly adopted in applications including image search
[
23
]
, speaker representations
[
29
]
and tf-idf datasets
[
30
]
. Moreover, all results for Angular ANN seamlessly extend to the entire Euclidean space

[
9
,
18
,
28
]
.

1.1
Our Contribution

In this paper, we revisit key approaches and technical results in locality-sensitive filtering and hashing for near neighbor search for angular distance. Through the design and analysis of an LSF-based data structure for the Angular ANN problem, we offer a guided tour of fundamental ingredients that have been developed in the past decade.
While our analysis proceeds along the lines of prior research, it is far from a synthesis of previous contributions, providing a streamlined and unified presentation of key ingredients and results.
In more detail:

•

We present a LSF-based data structure for angular ANN that is particularly simple (e.g. it is simpler than the other LSF-based solutions in
[
11
,
18
]
), essentially
representing the most direct instantiation of the framework proposed in
[
11
]
(up to the choice of filters).
This simplified data structure allows to achieve expected query complexity
O(n^{\rho})
, expected space
O(n^{1+\rho})
, and a proof of the asymptotic optimality of the obtained parameter
\rho
. (For example, in
[
11
]
the optimality of
\rho
is only claimed while the reader is referred to a similar analysis contained in other papers for a formal proof.)

•

A key technical problem underlying virtually all contributions in this area is the following: given two points
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
at a fixed angle, bound the probability that their inner products with a common random Gaussian vector
\theta\sim\mathcal{N}({\bf 0},I)
both exceed a specific threshold.
While a cornerstone for both angular LSF and LSH,
previous solutions to this problem have been mathematically involved, asymptotic in nature, or incomplete.
For instance, the analysis of
[
18
]
does not cover all angle ranges and relies on coarse geometric approximations (e.g., bounding the probability of a multivariate normal vector belonging to a closed convex body) that are only tight up to polynomial factors.
Similarly, Becker et al.
[
11
]
circumvent the exact probabilistic analysis by relying on bounds for the volume of the intersection of spherical caps,
which requires additional non-trivial steps to relate back to Gaussian projectors.
In contrast,
\Cref
le:cond_prob offers a fully self-contained, elementary analysis of this problem using only basic calculus.
Our bounds are rigorous, hold for all possible angle ranges, and are slightly tighter than those in previous literature.
This direct approach completely does away with heavy asymptotic notation and complex geometric machinery used in previous work.
In turn, this simplification directly translates into a much more streamlined and transparent proof of the asymptotic
optimality of Spherical LSF (
\Cref
subse:slsf_optimal). Finally, we complete the standard research pipeline in
\Cref
se:eas_anns by providing a concise proof of the structure’s query and space complexity.

In summary, we analyze common technical components in LSH and LSF approaches, highlighting their core similarities and differences. To simplify this complex landscape, we provide a unified framework and a critical review of key literature, offering a more accessible entry point to researchers new to this area.

Further Related Work

The prominent work of Indyk and Motwani
[
22
]
deals with Hamming distance as well as
\ell_{1}
norm and Euclidean distance (
\ell_{2}
norm).
A later work by Charikar
[
17
]
characterizes the class of metrics that admit LSH and posed a particular focus on the angular distance introducing the well-known
SimHash
(or Hyperplane hash).

Notably works on the Euclidean distance and angular distance are the following.
Datar et al.
[
20
]
is the first work that provides a natural hash family for Euclidean distance without embedding it into the Hamming space which achieves
\rho\approx 1/c
. Motwani et al.
[
25
]
show the first lower bounds to the LSH data structure under the
\ell_{1}
norm. Later, Andoni and Indyk
[
3
]
introduce a scheme for the Euclidean distance achieving
\rho\approx 1/c^{2}
, significantly improving upon the previous
1/c
bound. This result has been later shown to be optimal for data-independent LSH in the Euclidean setting
[
16
,
26
]
.

Indeed, from the lower bound point of view we have the work of O’Donnell et al.
[
26
]
that gives a lower bound for the Hamming distance in which
\rho\geq 1/c-o_{d}(1)
. By applying their results to the unit sphere or Euclidean space, they derive a lower bound for
\rho
of
1/c^{2}
in that setting.

Of particular interest is the work of Terasawa and Tanaka
[
31
]
whose LSH scheme named
cross-polytope
is analyzed in
[
1
]
and shown to be optimal. The cross-polytope LSH has some resemblance to the LSF scheme studied in this paper. Indeed, the main core of the cross-polytope hash function resides in the computation of dot-products between the points in the dataset and random Gaussian
\mathcal{N}\left({\bf 0},I/d\right)
points. The analysis provided here may also be applied to that particular case.
The cross-polytope scheme of
[
1
]
has been turned into practical implementations
[
9
,
27
]
.
Finally, to overcome the limitations imposed by the lower bound for
\rho
,
data-dependent
approaches are introduced and analyzed in
[
2
,
5
]
, achieving a superior exponent of
\rho=1/(2c^{2}-1)
.

The concept of LSF was formally introduced by Becker et al.
[
11
]
in the context of the
Shortest Vector Problem
(SVP).
This framework was further generalized by Christiani
[
18
]
, who provides a rigorous treatment of space-time tradeoffs for LSF.
Locality-sensitive-based solutions have recently found applications beyond traditional ANN search, particularly in the domain of algorithmic fairness
[
10
]
and differential privacy
[
8
]
.
A more detailed comparison between Becker
[
11
]
, Christiani
[
18
]
and this paper is given in
\Cref

3.1
Locality-Sensitive Filters

We next provide the rigorous definition of a locality-sensitive filter family for the angular distance on the sphere, since this is the case we are working with.

Definition 3

(Spherical Filters)
.

Consider the sphere
S^{d-1}
, any angle
0\leq\gamma\leq\pi
and
c>1
. For two values
0<q_{2}<q_{1}<1
, a family
\mathcal{F}=\{h:S^{d-1}\to\{0,1\}\}
of filters is
(\gamma,c\gamma,q_{1},q_{2})
-locality sensitive if, for
h
is sampled uniformly at random from
\mathcal{F}
and for any two points
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{q}}\in S^{d-1}
we have:

•

\mathbb{P}_{h\sim\mathcal{F}}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{q}})=1\right)\geq q_{1}
if
\angle({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{x}})\leq\gamma
;

•

\mathbb{P}_{h\sim\mathcal{F}}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{q}})=1\right)\leq q_{2}
if
\angle({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{x}})\geq c\gamma
.

Along the lines of LSH, a family of filters is as powerful as
q_{1}
is large and
q_{2}
is small. A convenient and compact way to define its discriminating power is through the parameter
\rho
.

Definition 4
.

Considered the probabilities
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
from
\Cref
def:lsf, we define parameter
\rho
of a LSF
\mathcal{F}
as

\rho=\frac{\log\frac{1}{q_{1}}}{\log\frac{1}{q_{2}}}.

(2)

Parameter
\rho
determines the performance of the corresponding LSF-based data structure, as we show later. A more detailed discussion of the differences and relationships between the parameter
\rho
describing the power of an LSH scheme and the one from
\Cref
def:rho is given in
\Cref
subse:slsf_optimal.

3.2
The Spherical-LSF data structure

We begin the description of the data structure for the angular
(\gamma,c\gamma)
-ANN problem by describing the LSF family we use for the angular distance, which is based on the definition of suitable spherical caps over the unit sphere.

Let
\tau\geq 0
be a threshold parameter.
A filter
h
from the LSF family
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
is defined as

h({\mathbf{x}})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\theta^{T}{\mathbf{x}}\geq\tau\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

where
\theta\sim\mathcal{N}({\bf 0},I/d)
, i.e., the
d
-dimensional vector where each entry is an independent gaussian random variable with mean
0
and variance
\frac{1}{d}
.

Remark 5
.

In the specific scenario considered in this paper (which is mostly common in the literature) the following symmetry properties hold:
i)

\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\right)

does not depend
on
{\mathbf{x}}
(though it will depend on the parameter
\tau
) and
ii)
as we also make explicit in
\Cref
se:analysis,
\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=h({\mathbf{q}})=1\right)
only depends on angle
\angle({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{q}})
. Notice that
i)
and
ii)
obviously imply
\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{q}})=1\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{q}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{x}})=1\right)
.

Consider an input dataset
\mathcal{X}
of
n
points in
S^{d-1}
. To index
\mathcal{X}
using Spherical-LSF, we first sample
m
independent vectors
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bf 0},I/d)
, which yield
m
independent filters
h_{1},\dots,h_{m}
from
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
.

Next, for every
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
, we compute its
signature

\text{sig}({\mathbf{x}})=\{i\in[m]\mid h_{i}({\mathbf{x}})=1\}
, i.e., the set of filter indices that
{\mathbf{x}}
passes (see
Algorithm 1
).

The resulting data-structure is a collection of
m
buckets
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
, where each
B_{i}
contains all the points that pass the
i
-th filter (see
Algorithm 2
).

Finally, to process a query
{\mathbf{q}}\in S^{d-1}
, we first compute its signature
\text{sig}({\mathbf{q}})
, then we look for a close point by scanning all buckets
B_{i}
, such that
i\in\text{sig}({\mathbf{q}})
(see
Algorithm 3
).

The geometrical intuition beyond this scheme is that every filter
h_{i}
represents a
spherical cap
of
S^{d-1}
, with
B_{i}
containing all input points that belong to the same spherical cap (see
\cref
fig:placeholder).
The idea of using spherical caps (to partition the sphere) in LSH was first introduced in
[
5
]
, where it was called Spherical LSH.
The filtering scheme proposed here is essentially equivalent to that of
[
11

4
Analysis of Spherical LSF

This section analyzes the performance of the data structure introduced in Section
3
. In most literature on the topic, this analysis addresses three aspects:
(i) a characterization of the locality sensitive properties of the proposed scheme, which in our case means possibly tight bounds on the (conditional) probabilities from
\Cref
def:lsf;
(ii) a characterization of the resulting parameter
\rho
(
\Cref
def:rho), measuring the discriminating power of the proposed scheme;
(iii) a quantitative analysis of the space and time requirements of the proposed data structure when solving the
(\gamma,c\gamma)
-ANN problem.
These aspects are respectively covered in the following Sections
4.1
,
4.2
and
4.3
.
Each of these sections revisits and/or refines ideas that have been introduced or adapted in previous LSH or LSF literature, presenting them in a unified manner. In each of these sections, we briefly discuss key previous results that are relevant, pointing to differences in our approach or analysis when this is the case.

4.1
Analysis of collision probability

The main technical contribution of this subsection is tight bounds on the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)
, which will then be used to prove optimality of Spherical Filtering in
\Cref
subse:slsf_optimal.

Bounding
\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)
is a crucial technical ingredient in all key contributions in LSH and LSF for Euclidean distance on the sphere, in particular
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
11
,
18
]
. In the remainder, we give an elementary proof that, while proceeding similarly to
[
5
]
(which focuses on a specific value of the threshold), is self-contained and more streamlined, avoiding asymptotic notation and providing results that are slightly tighter and that can be compactly expressed in terms of
1-\Phi(x)
. We briefly discuss analogous or related proofs given in the aforementioned references at the end of this subsection.

Lemma 6
.

Assume
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in S^{d-1}
share an angle

and

\tau\sqrt{d}\tan\frac{\alpha}{2}\geq 1

\displaystyle 1-\Phi(t\tan\tfrac{\alpha}{2})\leq\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)\leq 2(1-\Phi(t\tan\tfrac{\alpha}{2})),

\displaystyle 0<\alpha\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}(1-\Phi(t\tan\tfrac{\alpha}{2}))^{\left(1+\frac{2}{t^{2}}\right)^{2}}\leq\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)\leq 1-\Phi(t\tan\tfrac{\alpha}{2}),

\displaystyle\tfrac{\pi}{2}\leq\alpha<\pi

Proof.

We set
X=\sqrt{d}\theta^{T}{\mathbf{x}}
and
Y=\sqrt{d}\theta^{T}{\mathbf{y}}
in the remainder of this proof.

3

3

It may be interesting to note that
(X,Y)
is a bivariate normal variable with covariance
\cos\alpha
.
Due to rotational invariance of the (multivariate) standard normal distribution, we can assume without loss of generality that
{\bf e}_{1}={\mathbf{y}}
, while
{\bf e}_{2}
is orthogonal to
{\bf e}_{1}
(and thus
{\mathbf{y}}
), belongs to the plane spanned by
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
and forms an acute angle with
{\mathbf{x}}
. Finally, setting
Z=\sqrt{d}\theta_{2}
, we have
Y=\sqrt{d}\theta_{1}
and
X=Y\cos\alpha+Z\sin\alpha
, with
Y,Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and independent. Next, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\wedge h({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\theta^{T}{\mathbf{x}}\geq\tau\wedge\theta^{T}{\mathbf{y}}\geq\tau\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(X\geq t\wedge Y\geq t\right)

(3)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(Y\cos\alpha+Z\sin\alpha\geq t\wedge Y\geq t\right).

We next continue with
\mathbb{P}\left(Y\cos\alpha+Z\sin\alpha\geq t\wedge Y\geq t\right)
, since
\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Y\geq t\right)=1-\Phi(t)
. We have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y\cos\alpha+Z\sin\alpha\geq t\wedge Y\geq t\right)=\int_{t}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y\cos\alpha+Z\sin\alpha\geq t\mid Y=v\right)\phi(v)\,dv

(4)

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Z\geq\frac{t-Y\cos\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\mid Y=v\right)\phi(v)\,dv=\int_{t}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Z\geq\frac{t-v\cos\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\right)\phi(v)\,dv,

where we recall that

\mathbb{P}\left(Z\geq\frac{t-v\cos\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\right)=1-\Phi\left(\frac{t-v\cos\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\right).

Next, the proof proceeds by considering the cases
\alpha\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
and
\alpha\in[\frac{\pi}{2},\pi)
separately, since the events
(X\geq t)
and
(Y\geq t)
are positively (resp. negatively) correlated in the two cases. This makes the upper bound harder to prove in the first case and the lower bound harder to prove in the second.

Case 1:
\alpha\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
.

We begin by proving the lower bound, which is simpler in this case and can be derived directly from (
3
). In particular:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y\cos\alpha+Z\sin\alpha\geq t\wedge Y\geq t\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Z\sin\alpha\geq t-Y\cos\alpha\wedge Y\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(Z\sin\alpha\geq t(1-\cos\alpha)\wedge Y\geq t\right),

since the event
(Z\sin\alpha\geq t(1-\cos\alpha)\wedge Y\geq t)
implies the event
(Z\sin\alpha\geq t-Y\cos\alpha\wedge Y\geq t)
.
We therefore have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(h_{i}({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h_{i}({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)=\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(h_{i}({\mathbf{x}})=1\wedge h_{i}({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(h_{i}({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)}\geq\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(Z\sin\alpha\geq t(1-\cos\alpha)\wedge Y\geq t\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(Y\geq t\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(Z\sin\alpha\geq t(1-\cos\alpha)\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Z\geq\ t\frac{1-\cos\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Z\geq t\tan\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)=1-\Phi\left(t\tan\frac{\alpha}{2}\right),

where the third equality follows since the events
(Z\sin\alpha\geq t(1-\cos\alpha))
and
(Y\geq t)
are independent, the fifth equality follows since
(1-\cos\alpha)/\sin\alpha=\tan\frac{\alpha}{2}
, while the sixth follows since
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

We next prove the upper bound, which requires more work.
We introduce
a=\cos\frac{\alpha}{2}
and
b=\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}
. Since
(X,Y)
is a centered Gaussian vector with covariance matrix

\begin{pmatrix}1&\cos\alpha\\
\cos\alpha&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&a^{2}-b^{2}\\
a^{2}-b^{2}&1\end{pmatrix},

it has the same distribution as
(aU+bV,aU-bV)
, where
U,V\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
are independent. Therefore:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\wedge h({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(aU+bV\geq t\wedge aU-bV\geq t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(aU\geq t+b|V|\right)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(U\geq\frac{t+bv}{a}\right)\phi(v)\,dv.

Next, for every
\eta\geq t\geq 0
we have
1-\Phi(\eta)\leq e^{-\frac{\eta^{2}-t^{2}}{2}}(1-\Phi(t))
from
\Cref
fa:standard_ratio.
Applying this inequality with
\eta=\frac{t+bv}{a}
, dividing by
\mathbb{P}\left(Y\geq t\right)=1-\Phi(t)
and recalling that
U
and
V
are independent, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)\leq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\left(\frac{t+bv}{a}\right)^{2}-t^{2}}{2}}\phi(v)\,dv

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{\left(\frac{t+bv}{a}\right)^{2}-t^{2}+v^{2}}{2}}\,dv=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(v+tb)^{2}}{2a^{2}}}\,dv

\displaystyle=2a\int_{tb/a}^{\infty}\phi(z)\,dz=2a\left(1-\Phi\left(t\tan\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\right)\leq 2\left(1-\Phi\left(t\tan\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\right),

where the third equality follows from
a^{2}+b^{2}=1
and the fourth from the change of variable
z=\frac{v+tb}{a}
.

Case 2:
\alpha\in[\frac{\pi}{2},\pi)
.

We begin with the lower bound, which is harder to obtain, since
X
and
Y
are negatively correlated when
\alpha>\frac{\pi}{2}
, since
\cos\alpha<0
. We use again (
4
) to write:

\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\wedge h({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)=\int_{t}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Z\geq\frac{t-v\cos\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\right)\phi(v)\,dv.

Finding a tight lower bound to the probability above requires some care, due to the negative correlation between
X
and
Y
when
\alpha>\frac{\pi}{2}
and, therefore,
\cos\alpha<0
. To begin, we prove that the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[Y-t\mid Y\geq t\right]

, which allows us to consider
v
deterministically close to
t
in the integral above. To this purpose

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Y-t\mid Y\geq t\right]=\frac{1}{1-\Phi(t)}\int_{t}^{\infty}(v-t)\phi(v)dv=\frac{\phi(t)-t(1-\Phi(t))}{1-\Phi(t)}.

But
\phi(t)\leq\frac{t^{2}+1}{t}(1-\Phi(t))

\mathbb{P}\left(Y>t+\frac{2}{t}\mid Y\geq t\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Y-t>\frac{2}{t}\mid Y\geq t\right)\leq\frac{1}{2},

yielding:

\mathbb{P}\left(t\leq Y\leq t+\frac{2}{t}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}(1-\Phi(t)).

(5)

We continue with:

where the fourth inequality follows from (

is decreasing. Hence:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\Phi\left(\left(t+\frac{2}{t}\right)\tan\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)=\frac{\int_{t}^{\infty}\left(1-\Phi\left(\frac{t-v\cos\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\right)\right)\phi(v)\,dv}{\mathbb{P}\left(Y\geq t\right)}\leq\frac{\left(1-\Phi\left(\frac{t-t\cos\alpha}{\sin\alpha}\right)\right)\int_{t}^{\infty}\phi(v)\,dv}{\mathbb{P}\left(Y\geq t\right)}

Discussion.

As we mentioned earlier, variants of
\Cref
le:cond_prob are crucial ingredients of LSH/LSF approaches to ANN on the sphere. We briefly review previous contributions on this aspect, highlighting connections and differences with the analysis presented here.

Borrowing from previous work
[
2
]
, Andoni and Razenshteyn
[
5
]
used Gaussian random projectors as we do (up to scaling), but they focused on a specific value of the threshold, with a different goal in mind. In their Spherical LSH scheme, random projectors following
\mathcal{N}({\bf 0},I_{d})
are used with a fixed threshold to partition
S^{d-1}
using a (possibly not too large) set of sets carved out of spherical caps with the same height. Considering the
1/\sqrt{d}
scaling that we perform, their Theorem 3.1 corresponds to our
\Cref
le:cond_prob for
\tau=d^{-1/4}
(see the claim of their Theorem 3.1 and its proof in their Appendix A). In particular, recall that two vectors on the unit sphere sharing an angle
\alpha
have Euclidean distance
r=2\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}
. If
i)
we use
\Cref
le:normal_tail_1 to approximate
1-\Phi(t\tan\frac{\alpha}{2})
,
ii)
replace
\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}
with
r/2
and,
iii)
take the (natural) logarithm of the reciprocals of the upper and lower bounds provided by
\Cref
le:cond_prob for the joint probability, we obtain an expression almost identical to (2) and (3) from
[
5
, Thm. 3.1]
, namely

\ln\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)}\right)=\frac{r^{2}}{4-r^{2}}\frac{\sqrt{d}}{2}+O(\ln d).

Moreover, at least when
0\leq\alpha\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
, the ratio between upper and lower bound is constant, while the one in
[
5
, Theorem 3.1]
is not, due to the presence of factors
1\pm d^{-\Omega(1)}
before the logarithm.
This said, the proofs and results given in
[
5
, Theorem 3.1]
could be extended to general thresholds, in the end yielding asymptotic results on the parameter
\rho
equivalent to those we will show in
\Cref
subse:slsf_optimal.
It is worth noting that the very same collision probability resurfaces in the analysis of the cross-polytope LSH scheme proposed in
[
1
]
, where the proof of the lower bound given in their Theorem 2 relies on computing a quantity
\Lambda(\tau,\eta)
.
This is a conditional probability, which turns out to be exactly
\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{y}})=1\right)
when
t=\eta
and the angle
\alpha
between
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathbf{y}}
satisfies
\cos\alpha=1-\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}
. Hence,
\Cref
le:cond_prob also provides a clean and tight characterization of this quantity.

The problem of computing the conditional probability from
\Cref
le:cond_prob has also been considered in a more general LSF framework in
[
18
]
. Here (see their Lemmas B.3 and B.4), they are interested in the joint probability that two standard normal variables
X
and
Y
with bounded correlation both exceed two different thresholds. The setting of our
\Cref
le:cond_prob corresponds to choosing
\lambda=0
in their framework. On the other hand, their proofs either do not cover all possible ranges for the angle (e.g., for the lower bound, when the angles exceeds
\pi/2
) or are only tight up to polynomial factors in (our)
t
when specialized to our setting, since they rely on a previous result (Lemma B.2 in their appendix) estimating the probability of a multivariate normal vector belonging to a closed convex body, which provides a coarser approximation than
\Cref
le:cond_prob.

Finally, though
[
11
]
introduced the idea of LSF, their results are not straightforwardly equivalent to
\Cref
le:cond_prob, since the problem of estimating the probability of collision under random Gaussian projectors cannot be immediately rephrased as the geometrical problem of computing the volume of the intersection of spherical caps, though the two are clearly related due to concentration of the measure of
\mathcal{N}({\bf 0},I/d)
on the surface of the unit sphere.

4.2
Optimality of Spherical Filtering

In this section, we prove that the discriminating power of the spherical filters proposed in
\Cref
sse:LSF is asymptotically optimal, as measured by the parameter
\rho
from
\Cref
def:rho.

A digression on parameter
\rho
.

Before delving into the technical details, we provide some context that might hopefully benefit the reader, briefly discussing the reasons behind
\Cref
def:rho and the relationships with the analogous parameter
\rho
defined for LSH.
LSF was introduced in
[
11
]
, where they also defined
\rho
as we do in this paper. The reason for their definition of
\rho
stems from their analysis
[
11
, Section 3, Thm. 3.1]
of the time and memory costs of the data structure they propose (essentially, the same we introduced in
\Cref
sse:LSF up to the choice of the filters).
The authors of
[
11
]
discuss the asymptotic behavior of the
\rho
computed for the filters they propose, comparing it with the
\rho
computed for the LSH schemes proposed in
[
5
,
1
]
, though the two are clearly defined differently.
There is a practical justification for drawing this comparison though, since
[
11
]
also makes a connection (similar to the one for LSH) between the
\rho
from
\Cref
def:rho and the performance of their LSF-based data structure. Indeed, the connection between the
\rho
from LSF as defined in
\Cref
def:rho (and
[
11
]
) and the analogous parameter for LSH runs deeper.
In particular, Christiani proved
[
18
, Section 5, Lemma 5.1 and Thm. 1.4]
that every LSF scheme for the
\ell_{s}
distance with given value
\rho
according to
\Cref
def:rho yields an LSH scheme with the same value of the corresponding parameter
\rho
, up to lower order terms. This in particular implies that every LSF scheme for
\ell_{s}
must satisfy the general lower bound given in
[
26
, Thm. 3.1 and Cor. 3.2]
, which is
1/c^{2}-o_{d}(1)
for the Euclidean distance.

We next prove (asymptotic) optimality of Spherical Filtering.

Theorem 7
.

Assume
\angle({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{q}})=\gamma
,
\angle({\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{q}})=c\gamma
for
c>1
and
\tau
is chosen so that
t=\sqrt{d}\tau
satisfies
t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2}\geq 1
. If
q_{1}=\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{q}})=1\right)
and
q_{2}=\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{y}})=1\mid h({\mathbf{q}})=1\right)
then:

\rho=\frac{\log\frac{1}{q_{1}}}{\log\frac{1}{q_{2}}}\leq\frac{1}{c^{2}}+o_{d}(1).

Proof.

The proof proceeds by applying
\Cref
le:cond_prob to three cases.

Case 1:
0<\gamma<c\gamma\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
.

In this case,
\Cref
le:cond_prob gives:

q_{1}\geq 1-\Phi(t\tan\tfrac{\gamma}{2})\text{ and }q_{2}\leq 2(1-\Phi(t\tan\tfrac{c\gamma}{2})).

(6)

Next:

\ln\frac{1}{q_{1}}\leq\ln\frac{1}{1-\Phi(t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2})}\leq\frac{t^{2}}{2}\tan^{2}\frac{\gamma}{2}+\ln\left(2t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi),

where the second inequality follows from (
6
), the lower bound to
1-\Phi(t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2})
granted by
\Cref
le:normal_tail_1 and recalling that we assume
t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2}\geq 1
. Moreover:

\displaystyle\ln\frac{1}{q_{2}}\geq\ln\frac{1}{2(1-\Phi(t\tan\frac{c\gamma}{2}))}\geq c^{2}\frac{t^{2}}{2}\tan^{2}\frac{\gamma}{2}+\ln\left(t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi)-\ln 2

\displaystyle>c^{2}\frac{t^{2}}{2}\tan^{2}\frac{\gamma}{2}+\ln\left(t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2}\right),

where the first inequality follows from (
6
), while the second follows from the upper bound to
1-\Phi(t\tan\frac{c\gamma}{2})
granted by
\Cref
le:normal_tail_1 and from convexity of
\tan\alpha
for
0\leq\alpha<\frac{\pi}{2}
. As a result:

\rho=\frac{\ln\frac{1}{q_{1}}}{\ln\frac{1}{q_{2}}}\leq\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{\ln\left(2t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi)}{c^{2}\frac{t^{2}}{2}\tan^{2}\frac{\gamma}{2}+\ln(t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2})}=\frac{1}{c^{2}}+o_{d}(1),

where we recall that
t=\sqrt{d}\tau
, with
\tau
a fixed parameter (i.e. constant w.r.t.
d
).

Case 2:
0<\gamma\leq\frac{\pi}{2}<c\gamma<\pi
.

This case is similar to the previous one. In particular, the lower bound to
q_{1}
is the same, while the upper bound to
q_{2}
becomes

q_{2}\leq 1-\Phi(t\tan\tfrac{c\gamma}{2})

from
\Cref
le:cond_prob (case

), yielding:

\ln\frac{1}{q_{2}}\geq c^{2}\frac{t^{2}}{2}\tan^{2}\tfrac{\gamma}{2}+\ln(t\tan\tfrac{\gamma}{2})+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi)>c^{2}\frac{t^{2}}{2}\tan^{2}\frac{\gamma}{2}+\ln(t\tan\tfrac{\gamma}{2})+\ln 2,

so that we again have
\rho\leq\frac{1}{c^{2}}+o_{d}(1)
.

Case 3:
\frac{\pi}{2}<\gamma<c\gamma<\pi
.

In this case, we have

q_{1}\geq\frac{1}{4}(1-\Phi(t\tan\tfrac{\alpha}{2}))^{\left(1+\frac{2}{t^{2}}\right)^{2}}

from
\Cref
le:cond_prob (lower bound for
\frac{\pi}{2}\leq\alpha<\pi
), while the upper bound on
p_{2}
is the same as the previous case, so that we have:

\ln\frac{1}{q_{1}}\leq\ln\frac{4}{(1-\Phi(t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2}))^{\left(1+\frac{2}{t^{2}}\right)^{2}}}\leq\left(1+\frac{2}{t^{2}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{t^{2}}{2}\tan^{2}\frac{\gamma}{2}+\ln(2t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2})\right)+\ln 4

and

\ln\frac{1}{q_{2}}\geq c^{2}\frac{t^{2}}{2}\tan^{2}\frac{\gamma}{2}+\ln(t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2})+\ln 2.

As a result:

\displaystyle\rho\leq\frac{\left(1+\frac{2}{t^{2}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{t^{2}}{2}\tan^{2}\frac{\gamma}{2}+\ln(2t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2})\right)+\ln 4}{c^{2}\frac{t^{2}}{2}\tan^{2}\frac{\gamma}{2}+\ln(t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2})+\ln 2}<\left(1+\frac{2}{t^{2}}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{\left(1+\frac{2}{t^{2}}\right)^{2}\ln(2t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2})+\ln 4}{c^{2}\frac{t^{2}}{2}\tan^{2}\frac{\gamma}{2}+\ln(t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2})+\ln 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\left(\frac{4}{t^{2}}+\frac{4}{t^{4}}\right)\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{\left(1+\frac{2}{t^{2}}\right)^{2}\ln(2t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2})+\ln 4}{c^{2}\frac{t^{2}}{2}\tan^{2}\frac{\gamma}{2}+\ln(t\tan\frac{\gamma}{2})+\ln 2}=\frac{1}{c^{2}}+o_{d}(1),

where we again recall that
t=\sqrt{d}\tau
, with
\tau
a fixed parameter. This concludes the proof.
∎

We briefly note that
\tau
is assumed to be a sufficiently large, fixed parameter to ensure that
\rho\rightarrow\frac{1}{c^{2}}
as
d\rightarrow\infty
. In practice,
\tau=\omega(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}})
is enough for the same conclusions to apply.

Remark 8
.

Since we decided to work directly with the angular distance, rather than the Euclidean distance on the unit sphere, one might wonder whether
\Cref
le:min_cap_rho implies optimality of Spherical LSF with respect to the Euclidean distance on the sphere, which is the metric normally considered in the literature (e.g.,
[
5
,
1
]
).
Consider two point pairs at angular distances
\gamma
and
c\gamma
respectively. The ratio
\hat{c}
between the corresponding Euclidean distances is:

\hat{c}=\frac{\sin\frac{c\gamma}{2}}{\sin\frac{\gamma}{2}}\leq c,

where the inequality holds for all
0<\gamma<\pi
. Hence,
\rho\leq\frac{1}{c^{2}}+o_{d}(1)
from
\Cref
le:min_cap_rho implies
\rho\leq\frac{1}{\hat{c}^{2}}+o_{d}(1)
and thus asymptotic optimality as
d\rightarrow\infty
.

4.3
Solving the ANN problem

We next analyze the performance of Spherical-LSF in terms of the amount of extra memory needed to store the indexing structure and the cost of answering a query. Our analysis proceeds along the lines of the one presented in
[
21
, Thm. 3.4]
for LSH, albeit with the notable difference that in our case, we use the threshold
\tau
as our lever to achieve desired (expected) space/time trade-offs. The following result holds:

Theorem 9
.

Let
n
be sufficiently large and
\delta
be any constant in
(0,1)
. Assume

\tau\geq\sqrt{\frac{2\log n}{d\tan^{2}\frac{c\gamma}{2}}}
and
m\geq\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{p_{1}}
, where
p_{1}
is the joint probability that two points at angular distance
\gamma
pass the same filter. Then, for any query
{\mathbf{q}}
, Spherical-LSF solves Angular
(\gamma,c\gamma)
-ANN by correctly answering with probability at least
1-\delta
and performing
O(n^{\rho})
angular distance computations in expectation.
The expected size of the data structure (other than the space required to store the data points) is
O(n^{1+\rho})
.

Proof.

We define the following notation for the remainder of this proof: i)
p_{0}=\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\right)
, ii)
p_{1}=\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{x}})=1\wedge h({\mathbf{q}})=1\mid\angle({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{q}})=\gamma\right)
, iii)
p_{2}=\mathbb{P}\left(h({\mathbf{y}})=1\wedge h({\mathbf{q}})=1\mid\angle({\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{q}})=c\gamma\right)
.
To begin, note that if no point within distance
\gamma
from
{\mathbf{q}}
exists, there is nothing to prove. Next, consider a data point
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
with angle
\angle({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{x}})\leq\gamma
.
Observe that the query algorithm retrieves
{\mathbf{x}}
from a given bucket
B_{i}
if
h_{i}({\mathbf{x}})=h_{i}({\mathbf{q}})=1
. By definition, this happens with probability at least
p_{1}
. Since the
m
random projectors are mutually independent, the probability that
{\mathbf{x}}
is not found in any of the
m
buckets is upper bounded by

\prod_{i=1}^{m}(1-p_{1})=(1-p_{1})^{m}\leq e^{-mp_{1}}\leq\delta\,.

In order to bound the query time of the data structure, we focus on the worst case in which a unique point
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
exists, such that
\angle({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{x}})\leq\gamma
, while
\angle({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{y}})\geq c\gamma
for every
{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{{\mathbf{x}}\}
.

Let
q_{1}=p_{1}/p_{0}
and
q_{2}=p_{2}/p_{0}
be the conditional probabilities that
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are inserted in the filter given that
\mathbf{q}
has been inserted, respectively. For constant failure probability
\delta
,
m=\Omega(\frac{1}{p_{1}})=\Omega(\frac{1}{p_{0}q_{1}})
.

On average,
Algorithm 3
examines
O(mp_{2}n)
points per query. Substituting
m
, the expected number of candidates is
O\left(\frac{q_{2}}{q_{1}}n\right)
.

To achieve the target complexity, we seek a parameter
\tau
such that
\frac{q_{2}}{q_{1}}n\leq n^{\rho}
, where
\rho=\frac{\log q_{1}}{\log q_{2}}
. Through algebraic manipulation, this inequality is satisfied when
q_{2}\leq 1/n
.

By
\Cref
le:cond_prob,
q_{2}
is bounded by
2(1-\Phi(t\tan\frac{c\gamma}{2}))
.
Thus, the condition
q_{2}\leq 1/n
holds if

t\geq\frac{\Phi^{-1}(1-\frac{1}{2n})}{\tan\frac{c\gamma}{2}}\implies\tau\geq\frac{\Phi^{-1}(1-\frac{1}{2n})}{\sqrt{d}\tan\frac{c\gamma}{2}}.

Using the tail bound
1-\Phi(z)\leq\frac{\phi(z)}{z}
from
\Cref
le:normal_tail_1, we set
\frac{\phi(z)}{z}=\frac{1}{2n}
.
Taking the logarithm, we obtain
\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\log z+\log\sqrt{2\pi}=\log(2n)
.
For
n\to\infty
, the
z^{2}
term dominates the logarithmic factors, yielding
z\approx\sqrt{2\log n}
.
Consequently, for large
n
, we can approximate the required threshold as

\tau\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\log n}}{\sqrt{d}\tan\frac{c\gamma}{2}}.

Concerning the size of the data structure, the space complexity is dominated by the storage of the
m
buckets.
Since each of the
n
points is stored in a bucket only if it passes the corresponding spherical filter, the expected total number of entries is
n\cdot m\cdot p_{0}
.
Given that
m=O(1/(p_{0}q_{1}))
, the average space complexity is

O\left(n\cdot\frac{1}{p_{0}q_{1}}\cdot p_{0}\right)=O\left(\frac{n}{q_{1}}\right).

By the definition of
\rho
, we have
q_{1}=q_{2}^{\rho}
.
Recalling our condition
q_{2}\approx 1/n
, it follows that
1/q_{1}\approx n^{\rho}
.
Therefore, the average space complexity is
O(n^{1+\rho})
.
∎

Remark 10
.

We briefly discuss an aspect that we consciously overlooked in
\Cref
thm:main_thm, in order to keep the proof compact and since considering this aspect would lead us astray with respect to the scope of this paper.
In expectation, any near point and the query point share
\ln\frac{1}{\delta}
buckets and an overall number of
O(n^{\rho})
candidates are considered, which implies that
O(n^{\rho})
points collide in every bucket in expectation when
\delta
is a constant. Since the expected size of the data structure is
O(n^{1+\rho})
, this in turn implies that the number of filters we are using might be as large as
O(n)
. In principle, this is important, since the query time should also include the number of filter evaluations that need to be performed. A similar problem arises (and is discussed) for the number of hash function computations in the LSH literature (e.g., see
[
21
, Thm. 3.4]
or
[
5
, Thm. 2.3]
, where the authors assume
p_{1},p_{2}\geq 1/n^{o_{c}(1)}
) and it can be addressed in a number of ways in LSF. These include random product codes that modify the distribution from which filters are sampled
[
11
]
, or powering and tensoring techniques that amplify the locality sensitive properties of the filters
[
18
]
. While these theoretical constructions allow to improve the number of filter evaluations (e.g., bringing it down to
O(n^{\rho})
in expectation), computing the signature of a query vector is rarely a bottleneck in practice (e.g.,
[
27
]
). One of the reasons is that multiple inner products between independent standard normal vectors and a query vector can be effectively and very efficiently simulated using pseudo-random rotations, e.g., based on the Fast Hadamard Transform
[
1
,
27
]
. We refer the interested reader to these references for more details.

Proofs for Section

Fact 2
.

For any
0\leq\eta_{1}\leq\eta_{2}
we have:

\frac{1-\Phi(\eta_{2})}{1-\Phi(\eta_{1})}\leq e^{-\frac{\eta_{2}^{2}-\eta_{1}^{2}}{2}}.

Proof.

Assume
\eta_{2}\geq\eta_{1}\geq 0
. We have:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\eta_{2}}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}dx=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\eta_{1}}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{(z+\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})^{2}}{2}}dz,

where we performed the change of variable
z=x+\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}
.

We further have
(z+\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})^{2}=z^{2}+\eta_{2}^{2}+2(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})z-2\eta_{1}\eta_{2}+\eta_{1}^{2}
. Moreover,
2(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})z-2\eta_{1}\eta_{2}+\eta_{1}^{2}\geq-\eta_{1}^{2}
whenever
z\geq\eta_{1}
, since
\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}\geq 0
. As a consequence,
(z+\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})^{2}\geq z^{2}+\eta_{2}^{2}-\eta_{1}^{2}
for
z\in[\eta_{1},+\infty]
, whence:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\eta_{1}}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{(z+\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})^{2}}{2}}dz\leq\frac{e^{-\frac{\eta_{2}^{2}-\eta_{1}^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\eta_{1}}^{+\infty}e^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2}}dz,

which proves the claim.
∎
