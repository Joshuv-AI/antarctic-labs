Microbiological quality and nutritional characterization of traditional fermented foods and beverages in Nekemte Town, East Wollega Zone, Ethiopia
Authors: Tadele Amente, Desalegn Amenu, Desalegn Wirtu, Diriba Chewaka, Tesfaye Abera, Sidise Debelo, Haile Bikila, Tolesa Gemeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraditional fermented foods and beverages are essential sources of nutrients in western Ethiopia.ObjectiveThe objective of this study is to investigate the microbial safety, nutritional characterization (proximate and mineral content, and vitamin content) of selected traditional fermented food and beverages in East Wollega Zone, Nekemte Town.MethodsA total of 150 samples of five different fermented food and beverages, namely, Cabbage Shamita, Borde, Anchote, Chumbo, and Bukuri, each consisting of 30 samples, were collected and analyzed for their proximate and mineral content, vitamin content, and microbial quality, including total aerobic bacteria, lactic acid bacteria (LAB), yeasts and molds, total coliforms, and E. coli, using standard laboratory procedures.ResultsBorde, a cereal-based food, had the highest counts of LAB (6.5 log CFU/g) and total aerobic bacteria (7.0 log CFU/g). The vegetable- and tuber-based foods, Cabbage Shamita and Anchote, were rich in vitamins A and C, calcium, potassium, and magnesium, while Cabbage Shamita was rich in iron, zinc, and B vitamins. Anchote had the highest protein content, while Borde had the highest content of calcium, and Borde and Cabbage Shamita were rich in protein and energy content.ConclusionThe traditional fermented foods and beverages in Nekemte Town are nutritionally diverse, microbiologically safe, and functionally important for the population, especially the vulnerable groups, including children and women of reproductive age, as they are rich in energy, protein, vitamins, minerals, and fiber, while the antinutrients were reduced during the fermentation process.

Introduction

Traditional fermented foods and beverages have been a vital component of the Ethiopian diet, providing energy, protein, vitamins, minerals, bioactive compounds, and microorganisms. Fermented food and drinks also show the rich cultural diversity and traditional knowledge of the Ethiopian community [3, 8, 19, 38]. In Nekemte Town and the surrounding regions, Borde, Cabbage Shamita, Anchote, Chumbo, Bukuri, etc., are traditional fermented foods and beverages that are commonly consumed by the Ethiopian communities. Borde is a low-alcohol cereal-based beverage, primarily made from barley malt and fermented for 2–3 days, commonly consumed during ceremonies and recommended for lactating mothers [10]. The art of production and microbial dynamics of Bukuri and cabbage-Shameta: a traditional fermented beverage of Ethiopia [10]. It is normally consumed during ceremonies and is also recommended for breastfeeding women. Cabbage Shamita is a thick beverage with low alcohol content that is allowed to ferment for about 1–2 weeks and prepared from barley, seeds, and spices. It is recommended as a meal replacement for women [3]. Bukuri is a fermented drink that is prepared using cereals without gesho and is then allowed to ferment for a few days. It is used for social and religious functions [3]. Chumbo is an unknown fermented product that possibly undergoes fermentation using cereals and plants. It is subjected to spontaneous fermentation for a few days. It is rich in probiotics. Anchote is a tuberous root vegetable that is rich in nutrients. It is boiled or steamed and rich in carbohydrates, proteins, and vitamins for the community [15, 17, 28].

These fermented foods are associated with several health benefits, including improved gut microbiota, enhanced nutrient absorption, reduced cholesterol levels, and better glycemic control [10]. In addition to their sensory qualities such as taste and aroma, they are valued for their nutritional contributions. Fermentation improves the nutritional quality of foods by increasing nutrient and mineral bioavailability, enhancing digestibility, and promoting beneficial gut microbial activity. It also reduces antinutritional compounds such as phytates and tannins, thereby improving overall nutrient utilization and supporting better health and wellbeing [6, 29, 36]. In particular, Cabbage Shamita and Borde are reported to contain bioactive peptides and dietary fibers that may support maternal and child nutrition [3, 8, 10, 33, 38]. Interestingly, traditional fermented products like Cabbage shmita and borde are recognized as the best sources of nutrients, largely due to their bioactive peptides and dietary fibers. These products may contribute positively to the nutrition of mothers and infants, including pregnant and lactating women, thereby supporting proper growth and development of infants [3, 8, 10, 38]. On the other hand, uncertainties still remain regarding the overall nutritional value, microbiological safety, and potential health risks associated with many traditional fermented foods. Therefore, it is necessary to document, preserve, and improve the nutritional quality, safety, and utilization of fermented foods in the context of changing food systems [5, 20, 21, 34].

In Nekemte town, previous studies have concentrated only on selected fermented food items such as Cabbage Shamita and Borde. Yet, in previous studies, there is no comprehensive examination made to ensure the microbiological safety and nutritional content, which includes the basic and vitamin composition, of the above-mentioned food items [3]. Moreover, the utilization of some locally used foods such as Anchote and Chumbo remains understudied in relation to their basic and nutritional composition and microbiological safety.

To address these gaps, the present study was employed a compressive and comparative approach to asses the proximate composition, major micronutrient content, and microbiological safety of selected traditional fermented foods and beverages in Nekemte town, East Wollega Zone, Ethiopia. The study generates a new insight in to variations in nutritional composition and microbial quality among different foods, as well as difference associated with products types and processing conditions. It also established bassline data for comparison with other regions of Ethiopia, contributing to boarder understanding of traditional fermented foods. Overall, the findings highlight their nutritional and safety importance and provide evidence to support improved utilization, safe consumption, standardization, optimization, commercialization, and preservation.

Methods and materials

Study area and period

The study was conducted in Nekemte town, which is found in the East Wollega Zone of the Oromia Regional State in Ethiopia, within the period ranging from September 2024 to August 2025. Nekemte town is one of the prominent towns in the East Wollega Zone, surrounded by various agricultural activities such as the cultivation of various crops like teff, barley, sorghum, Anchote, etc., which are the main sources of various kinds of fermented food and beverages like Borde, Cabbage Shamita, Chumbo, Anchote, Bukuri, etc. Nekemte Town is located in East Wollega Zone, Oromia, western Ethiopia, at approximately 9.083°N and 36.550°E with an elevation of about 2,088 m above sea level (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Map of the study area showing East Wollega Zone, Oromia Region, Ethiopia

Sample collection

A total of 150 samples (30 per product type) of Borde, Cabbage Shameta, Chumbo, Anchote, and Bukuri were collected from households, markets, and producers. The sample size (n = 30 per group) was determined based on Montgomery & Runger [35] and the Central Limit Theorem (n ≥ 30), which supports approximate normality of the sampling distribution. This approach is commonly applied in microbiological studies to ensure robust statistical inference and adequate power for parametric analysis in highly variable fermentation systems [35].

To achieve this objective, a sampling technique was adopted whereby proportional representation of these indigenous fermented foods and beverages were undertaken through households, markets, and small food manufacturers using well-known techniques of sampling design [11, 32]. The use of a multistage sampling technique was employed in the process. First, the households, markets, and manufacturers producing these foods and beverages were listed as the sampling frame. Secondly, those who produced or sold the selected foods were considered as eligible while the others were not included. Lastly, systematic sampling was applied by selecting these food and drink items in order to capture their diversity by choosing a random starting point and sampling intervals (k = N/n), where “N” is the number of the eligible units while “n” is the desired sample size per source.

Sample collection was done independently in each case in order to prevent duplicate sample collection. To ensure the integrity and authenticity of the samples, aseptic sampling techniques were employed throughout the collection, labeling, and transport processes in line with recommended microbiological laboratory practices [23, 24]. All fermented food and beverage samples used in this study were collected in their already fermented state from households, markets, and small-scale producers in Nekemte town.

Microbiological analysis

Microbiological analysis of traditional fermented food and beverages was performed specific growth media for different microbial groups. Plate Count Agar (PCA) was used for total viable counts, MRS agar for lactic acid bacteria, MacConkey agar for coliforms, and Eosin Methylene Blue (EMB) agar for Escherichia coli. Incubation was carried out under the following conditions: PCA and MRS plates were incubated at 30–32 °C for 24–48 24 to 48 h, while MacConkey and EMB plates were incubated at 37 °C 24 to 48 h. The study was carried out in triplicate, with each study being repeated three times (n = 3), with a limit of 102 CFU/g to determine the quality of the traditional fermented food products [38].

Sample preparation

The sample preparation for analysis took place standard procedures for sample preparation. Fresh samples were used except when otherwise specified in this report. In proximate analysis and mineral analysis such as Ca, Fe, Zn, and K, samples were homogenized and subjected to moisture content analysis by drying in an oven. In vitamins analysis, solvent extraction techniques were used. In microbiological analysis, samples were homogenized and diluted under aseptic conditions. A portion of this homogenate was set aside and kept for analysis in order to avoid cross-contamination [13].

Nutritional composition and proximate analysis

Proximate analysis such as moisture content, protein content, fat content, carbohydrate content, fiber content, and ash content was carried out on each sample using standard procedures. Macronutrients were determined to evaluate the energy and dietary contribution of the sample. Protein content was determined using the Kjeldahl method, fat content using the Soxhlet method, and carbohydrates using the difference method. On the other hand, fiber and ash content were determined using the standard method as described in the AOAC book of standard methods [4].

Micronutrients such as vitamins A, C, and B-complex, as well as minerals including calcium, magnesium, potassium, iron, and zinc, were determined using high-precision techniques (HPLC, AAS, and spectrophotometry). Vitamin A was extracted using hexane after saponification and analyzed by HPLC using a C18 column with retinol acetate as the external standard; calibration curves were prepared using serial dilutions (R2 > 0.99), and detection was performed at 325 nm. Vitamin C was purified with 3% metaphosphoric acid and detected via HPLC by absorption at 254 nm using standards of ascorbic acid. The B-complex vitamins were extracted from the samples using phosphate buffer solution and determined using HPLC analysis using standard B-complex compounds (thiamine, riboflavin, niacin) and an absorption wavelength of 265–280 nm depending on the vitamin [12, 25].

Minerals were analyzed by atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) following wet digestion using a nitric acid–perchloric acid mixture (3:1 v/v). Analytical-grade standards (Ca, Mg, K, Fe, and Zn; Sigma-Aldrich) were used to construct multi-point calibration curves for quantification. Antioxidant activity and total phenolic compounds were determined using UV–visible spectrophotometry following extraction with 80% methanol. Total phenolic content was measured using the Folin Ciocalteu method at 765 nm with gallic acid as the standard, while flavonoid content was determined using aluminum chloride colorimetric assay at 415 nm with quercetin as the reference standard. Antioxidant activity was evaluated using DPPH assay measured at 517 nm [12, 25].

Data analysis

All the results obtained from the experiments were expressed as mean ± SD, with three replicates (n = 3). Before applying the inferential statistics, the normal distribution of the data was checked using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test and equality of variance using Levene’s test. To compare the proximate composition, mineral content, vitamin content, and microbiological parameters between the groups, one-way ANOVA was employed. If the calculated p-value was less than 0.05, then the result indicated the presence of significant differences between the groups, and the post-test was done using the Tukey Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test. SPSS version 25 or equivalent statistical software was used to assess the reliability of the data and to find significant differences between the traditionally fermented food products.

Borde, a cereal-based fermented food, recorded the highest microbial load of total aerobic bacteria (7.0 ± 0.2 log CFU/mL, 95% CI: 6.92–7.08) and LAB (6.5 ± 0.2 log CFU/mL, 95% CI: 6.42–6.58), indicating that it is rich in nutrients that support microbial growth. These microbial loads are slightly higher than previously reported (LAB 7–8 log CFU/mL and yeasts 6–7 log CFU/mL) [16, 38]. Borde also showed low levels of total coliforms (3.2 ± 0.1 log CFU/mL, 95% CI: 3.16–3.24) and no detectable E. coli (< 1 log CFU/mL), suggesting good hygienic processing conditions. The predominance of LAB likely controls the growth of spoilage and pathogenic microorganisms, as reported for other cereal-based fermented foods [8].

Cabbage Shamita, a mixture of vegetables and cereals, had moderate levels of microorganisms, including total aerobic bacteria (6.2 ± 0.1 log CFU/g, 95% CI: 6.16–6.24), LAB (5.8 ± 0.1 log CFU/g, 95% CI: 5.76–5.84), and yeasts (4.5 ± 0.1 log CFU/g, 95% CI: 4.46–4.54). Compared with Borde, it showed lower counts, likely due to differences in substrate composition and available sugars. The low pH of this product effectively controlled total coliforms and E. coli, similar to other fermented vegetables in Ethiopia [8, 36]. In this case, yeasts contribute positively to sensory properties, while LAB are responsible for product safety and potential health benefits.

In contrast, Anchote, a tuber -based product, had the lowest level of microorganism, including total aerobic bacterial count (5.5 ± 0.1 log CFU/g, 95% CI: 5.46–5.54), LAB (4.8 ± 0.1 log CFU/g, 95% CI: 4.76–4.84), and yeasts (3.5 ± 0.1 log CFU/g, 95% CI: 3.46–3.54). This could be because of the reduced amount of sugar and nutrients present in the tubers than in cereals. Nonetheless, the sample contained minimal amounts of total coliforms (1.5 ± 0.1 log CFU/g, 95% CI: 1.46–1.54), as well as an absence of E. coli, which is characteristic of other fermented foods made from tubers, including Injera and Kocho [40, 41].

Chumbo and Bukuri had intermediate levels of microbes with total aerobic counts ranging from 5.8–6.0 ± 0.1 log CFU/g (95% CI: 5.76–6.04 to 5.96–6.04, LAB from 5.2–5.5 ± 0.1 log CFU/g (95% CI: 5.16–5.54), and yeasts ranging from 3.8–4.0 ± 0.1 log CFU/g (95% CI: 3.76–4.04). These standards fall between those of Anchote and Borde, indicating moderate nutrient content. The microbiological profile suggests that fermentation proceeds safely while contributing to the sensory attributes of the final product, similar to other Ethiopian cereal-based fermented foods [2, 30]. Differences among samples were significant for all microbial parameters (p < 0.001, η2 = 0.71–0.88), with no detection of E. coli in any sample. Power analysis indicated adequate statistical power (1 − β > 0.95) based on n = 30 per group, confirming reliability of the observed differences.

Overall, LAB and yeasts are the major microbes in all the products. Coliforms are low, and E. coli is not detected in the products, which suggests microbiological safety if the products are handled and fermented properly. Compared to the cereal-based products such as Borde, the tuber-based products such as Anchote support less microbial growth than the cereal-based products. LAB are responsible for the safety of the products, and yeasts are essential in the development of the sensory attributes of the products such as flavors, aromas, and the efficiency of the fermentation process [16, 30].

The protein content of the samples was relatively high in Anchote, Borde, and Cabbage Shamita, which ranged from 9.0 to 9.6 g/100 g, signifying that these foods can be used as vital sources of proteins within the local diet. Contrarily, the protein content of Chumbo and Bukuri was relatively low, between 5.5 and 7.0 g/100 g, a trend that is in line with the raw material composition of these foods. The relatively higher protein contents of Borde and Cabbage Shamita signify the nutritional importance of the two samples, especially in rural settings where the consumption of animal products is limited. Thus, these foods are considered alternative sources of dietary protein. The protein content varied significantly among the five foods (p = 0.012; η2 = 0.79; 95% CI: Anchote 9.2–10.1, Borde 9.1–10.1, Cabbage Shamita 8.6–9.4, Chumbo 5.2–5.8, Bukuri 6.1–6.9).

The fat content in the selected traditional fermented foods and beverages also sowed significance difference (p = 0.009; η2 = 0.84; 95% CI ranges). Cabbage Shamita and borde exhibited relatively higher fat content (8.5 g and 6.9 g per 100 g, respectively), indicating that these foods are potential sources of energy in the local diet. On the contrary, Anchote showed very low-fat content (< 1 g per 100 g), indicating that cereal- and legume-based foods are potential sources of energy, while vegetable- and tuber-based foods are low in fat content.

The carbohydrate content differed depending on raw material type, with Cabbage Shamita recording high contents (25%), followed by Borde (17.5%). The three products, namely Anchote, Chumbo, and Bukuri, had 13–16% carbohydrate content. Crude fiber content ranged from 3.1% in Anchote to 5.2% in Borde (p = 0.017; η2 = 0.80; 95% CI ranges), indicating beneficial effects on digestion [9, 27]. The ash content ranged from 0.8% in Borde to 2.4% in Chumbo and Bukuri (p = 0.021,η2 = 0.77; 95% CI ranges), reflecting differences in mineral composition and possible increases during fermentation [39]. Overall, all proximate parameters showed statistically significant variation among products (p < 0.05), with large effect sizes (η2 = 0.77–0.84), indicating a strong influence of food type on nutritional composition. A brief post-hoc power analysis (α = 0.05, n = 3 per group) suggests moderate statistical power (~ 0.60–0.75), reflecting limited replication but adequate sensitivity to detect the observed large effects.

From a local nutritional perspective, these foods have considerable influence in ensuring that family meals and supporting household diets. This is because the two fermented beverages, Borde and Cabbage Shamita, contribute greatly towards energy and protein supply, particularly benefiting children and women. Concurrently, the three vegetables: Anchote, Chumbo, and Bukuri, add, dietary diversity by supplying crucial fiber and essential minerals, thereby promoting comprehensive nutritional adequacy. These fermented foods play an important role in increasing food security by making use of local resources, ensuring food preservation through fermentation, and presenting inexpensive, culturally acceptable, and safe eating options.

The inherent makeup of these fermented foods is heavily influenced by the specific raw materials employed and the fermentation techniques utilized. Generally, cereal and leguminous products such as Borde and Cabbage Shamita are usually nutrient-rich, and in contrast, tuber and vegetables-based products such as Anchote, Chumbo, and Bukuri have average protein and fiber levels and low-fat levels. Overall, these readily available local foods contribute to fostering a more balanced diet within the community [16, 26]. It has been observed that fermentation is believed to increase the shelf life and nutrient bioavailability, as evidenced by recent studies, which are in agreement with previous studies conducted on Ethiopian fermented foods [6, 27]. Moreover, recent studies also reveal elevated levels of protein in Anchote (Coccinia abyssinica),the tubers may contain 6.5–7.0% protein, in some instances, ranging from 10.7–13.7%, depending on the genotype and methods used to process the plant, while others show up to 35% protein in leaves. These fluctuations in protein levels are based on the various parts of the plant, genotypes, and improved analytical methods [37, 42].

In Nekemte town, the production of these fermented foods is an integral part of their diet. The traditional beverage such as Borde and Cabbage Shamita, which are made using cereals and pulses, provide essential nutrients and support especially women and younger individuals. On the other hand, vegetable-based products such as Anchote, Chumbo, and Bukuri assist in providing variety in their diet along with necessary fibers and minerals. In general, these food products have been locally produced, well-received, nutritious, and safe.

The mineral composition of the traditional Ethiopian fermented foods and beverages was characterized by high variations among different samples (p < 0.05) (Table 3). This variation is attributed to the difference in the sources (raw materials used), combination of ingredients, and traditional preparation methods. Calcium (Ca) levels were highest in Anchote (121 mg/100 g), followed by Bukuri and Cabbage Shamita (each at 50 mg/100 g), while Borde registered the lowest amount (20 mg/100 g). The observed disparities were statistically significant (p = 0.004; η2 = 0.88), with 95% confidence intervals clearly indicating distinct group means: Anchote (118.6–123.4), Bukuri (47.6–52.4), Cabbage Shamita (47.6–52.4), Chumbo (42.7–47.3), and Borde (18.2–21.8). Anchote's elevated calcium content underscores its importance as a dietary source of this mineral, especially in regions where dairy product access is limited. Cabbage Shamita demonstrated the highest concentration of Iron (Fe) and Zinc (Zn), measuring 4.0 mg/100 g for Fe and 2.0 mg/100 g for Zn, respectively. Conversely, Anchote contained significantly lower levels of these micronutrients, with just 0.41 mg/100 g of Fe and 0.35 mg/100 g of Zn. These disparities were statistically robust (Fe: p = 0.006, η2 = 0.85; Zn: p = 0.008, η2 = 0.83), and the 95% confidence intervals indicated narrow dispersion across replicates. This finding suggests that cereal-legume-based fermented foods contribute more substantially to the essential micronutrients needed for hemoglobin synthesis, immune responses, and various enzymatic functions [22, 27]. The elevated iron and zinc content in Shamita can likely be ascribed to the incorporation of legumes and the beneficial effects of fermentation, which improves mineral availability.

Furthermore, Anchote recorded the highest levels of magnesium (Mg) at 27.7 mg/100 g and potassium (K) at 387 mg/100 g, followed by Bukuri and Cabbage Shamita. In contrast, Borde and Chumbo exhibited lower concentrations (Mg: 22–25 mg/100 g; K: 130–150 mg/100 g). These differences were also statistically significant (Mg: p = 0.012, η2 = 0.81; K: p = 0.005, η2 = 0.86), with 95% confidence intervals consistently showing higher upper bounds for Anchote. Potassium plays a crucial role in maintaining fluid balance and supporting cardiovascular function, while magnesium is vital for numerous enzymatic reactions and energy metabolism [18]. Anchote's superior mineral content further emphasizes its nutritional value in local diets. The Tukey HSD post-hoc test corroborated statistically significant differences among the samples, as indicated by distinct superscript groupings in Table 3. A preliminary post-hoc power analysis (α = 0.05, n = 3 per group) indicated moderate to high statistical power (~ 0.70–0.85), suggesting that even with limited replication, the substantial effect sizes (η2 = 0.81–0.88) provide compelling evidence for genuine variations among the fermented food samples.

The mineral makeup of the food items examined varies, depending on both the primary ingredients and the specific fermentation technique employed. Anchote (Coccinia abyssinica), a starchy root crop widely consumed in western Ethiopia, is notably abundant in calcium and potassium, which contribute to its distinct mineral profile [15]. Conversely, Cabbage Shamita, prepared from grains and pulses, exhibits higher concentrations of iron and zinc. These minerals originate from the raw components and are further enhanced through fermentation. Fermentation boosts the bioavailability of minerals by diminishing antinutritional elements like phytates and tannins, which typically impede absorption [1, 39]. As a result, this process improves the nutritional value of plant-derived foods by making essential minerals more accessible for the body. However, fermented beverages based on cereals, such as Borde, generally showed reduced mineral content. This could be attributed to the naturally low mineral composition of cereals, alongside dilution effects stemming from the high moisture content in the finished product [9], a trend also noted in similar African fermented cereal-based drinks [7, 31]. The moderate mineral quantities observed in Cabbage Shamita, Chumbo, and Bukuri suggest that incorporating cereals, legumes, and vegetables can elevate the overall mineral quality of traditional dietary patterns.

Traditionally fermented foods can provide inexpensive means of acquiring essential minerals, thus contributing significantly to the establishment of food security in the area. As far as nutrition is concerned, a combination of food sources containing calcium, such as Anchote, and those rich in iron and zinc, for instance, Cabbage Shamita, may result in an increase in mineral intake. It may even help in the reduction of such deficiencies as anemia. The aforementioned observations highlight the necessity of diet diversity in improving nutritional sufficiency. In order to advance this issue further, future research should be focused on the improvement of the mineral processing technique and measuring its contribution to dietary needs.

Regarding B vitamins, thiamine (B1) ranged from 0.06 ± 0.004 µg/100 g in Cabbage Shamita and Anchote to 0.08 ± 0.005 µg/100 g in Borde (p = 0.011, η2 = 0.79, 95% CI ranges). These findings reveal a complementary nutritional profile: cereal and legume products, specifically Borde and Cabbage Shamita, are abundant in B vitamins, while tuber and vegetable dishes like Anchote, Chumbo, and Bukuri primarily supply vitamins A and C. This underscores the vital importance of dietary diversity for ensuring a balanced nutrient intake. A subsequent power analysis (α = 0.05, n = 3 per group) indicated moderate to high statistical power (around 0.70–0.85), suggesting that even with limited replications, the substantial effect sizes (η2 = 0.77–0.87) provide compelling evidence for true distinctions among the samples.

Thus, fermented foods found in Nekemte Town play a crucial nutritional role, especially benefiting children and women of reproductive age, by providing essential vitamins and minerals. The fermentation process itself enhances nutrient availability through the reduction of antinutrients like phytates and tannins, consequently improving the absorption of key minerals such as iron, zinc, calcium, and magnesium [1, 14]. Overall, actively promoting a varied selection of these traditional fermented foods can significantly improve micronutrient consumption, foster greater dietary diversity, and fortify the nutritional quality of plant-based diets within the local community.

Conclusion

A study has revealed that five traditional Ethiopian fermented foods and beverages commonly consumed in Nekemte Town are both nutritionally diverse and microbiologically safe. Cereal-based products, exemplified by Borde, exhibited the highest microbial counts, with total aerobic bacteria nearing 7.0 log CFU/g and lactic acid bacteria around 6.5 log CFU/g. In contrast, foods prepared from vegetables and tubers, including Cabbage Shamita and Anchote, were found to be rich in vitamins A and C, as well as minerals such as calcium, potassium, and magnesium. Cabbage Shamita, in particular, was notable for its higher levels of B vitamins, iron, and zinc. Regarding their proximate composition, Borde and Cabbage Shamita provided comparatively higher energy and protein content, while tuber- and vegetable-based items contained moderate protein, low fat, and a greater amount of dietary fiber. Overall, these results highlight the nutritional complementarity of the different food groups and support the inclusion of traditional fermented foods and beverages in daily diets to improve macro- and micronutrient intake, particularly among children and women of reproductive age.
