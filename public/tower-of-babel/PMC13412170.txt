HLA-B class I allele associations with neurological complications in pediatric SARS-CoV-2 infection: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Hanan Mohamed Ibrahim, Mervat Gamal Eldin Mansour, Raghda Zaitoun, Marwa Rushdy, Mona Mostafa ElGammal, Ahmed Rezk Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infections in children are mild, yet neurological complications can occur. Host immune variability is partly determined by human leukocyte antigen (HLA) polymorphisms and may influence susceptibility. This study investigates whether specific HLA-B alleles are associated with neurological involvement in pediatric coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19).MethodsThis retrospective study spanned over one year, including children with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection. Patients were classified into two groups: patients with COVID-19 with neurological disease (neuro-COVID-19), and those with COVID-19 without neurological disease (non-neuro COVID-19). A control group of 120 healthy children was included to represent baseline allele distribution. HLA-B class I allele typing was performed using polymerase chain reaction with sequence-specific oligonucleotide probes (PCR-SSOP).ResultsHLA-B49 represented the most common allele among children suffering from neurological COVID-19 (10.4%). None of the alleles reached statistical significance when compared in patients with and without neurological disease. A possible, but not statistically significant, lower frequency of the HLA-B52 allele was observed in cases with neurological manifestations (p = 0.088). However, in exploratory unadjusted analyses, HLA-B53 showed higher odds of neurological involvement when compared with children without neurological disease and healthy controls (OR = 6.29; 95% CI: 1.23–32.13; nominal p = 0.027), while HLA-B45 demonstrated a positive trend (OR = 3.75; 95% CI: 0.87–16.22; nominal p = 0.077). These allele-level findings were not corrected for multiple comparisons and should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.ConclusionsThis study suggests a possible immunogenetic contribution to neurological complications in pediatric COVID-19, with a nominal unadjusted signal for HLA-B53. Given the small sample size, multiple-allele testing, and the absence of adjusted modelling, this finding should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Validation in larger multicenter cohorts with correction for multiple comparisons and adjustment for relevant confounders is required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-07370-9.Keywords: Pediatric COVID-19, Neurological complications, HLA-B polymorphism, Genetic susceptibility, Immunogenetics

Introduction

Neurological involvement in Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) is well established in both adults and children [1, 2]. Most severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infections in children are asymptomatic or mild; however, a few cases present with severe neurological disorders either during the acute phase or through mechanisms of post-infectious immune dysregulation [3, 4]. Such variable presentations of SARS-CoV-2 infection strongly support the hypothesis of host-specific factors underlying neuro-COVID-19, defined in this study as neurological manifestations occurring in children with confirmed current or previous SARS-CoV-2 infection.

The Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) system is an important component of antigen presentation and T-cell-mediated antiviral immunity [5]. Among the class I loci, HLA-B plays a crucial role in the activation of cytotoxic T cells, the regulation of NK cell responses, and has been associated with autoimmune and post-infectious neuroinflammatory conditions. Various HLA polymorphisms have been linked to susceptibility and severity of COVID-19. Individuals with class I/II alleles bind a broader, higher-affinity set of SARS-CoV-2 peptides, and tend to cause stronger CD8⁺/CD4⁺ responses, clear the virus faster, limiting systemic cytokine activation that may contribute to neuroinflammation. Conversely, alleles predicted to bind fewer viral peptides have been associated with worse outcomes in several cohorts [6].

Despite growing recognition of associations between HLA polymorphisms and COVID-19-related outcomes [7–9], immunogenetic determinants of neurological involvement in pediatric patients remain poorly characterized. Existing studies have primarily focused on adult populations or on systemic disease severity rather than organ-specific complications. Addressing this knowledge gap is critical, given the developing nervous system and the high risk of persistent neurodevelopmental sequelae in pediatric populations [10, 11]. We therefore investigated whether HLA-B alleles are associated with neurological involvement in pediatric patients with COVID-19 or COVID-19-related hyperinflammatory syndromes. We compared allele frequencies in neuro-COVID-19 cases with those in children with COVID-19 without neurological involvement and healthy pediatric controls to identify immunogenetic factors that may underlie differential neurological outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

This observational retrospective study was conducted at the Children’s Hospital, Faculty of Medicine, Ain Shams University, Cairo, Egypt, over a period of one year, from June 2022 to June 2023. The study included children aged one month to 16 years who were hospitalized during the study period with confirmed current or previous SARS-CoV-2 infection, with or without COVID-19-related neurological manifestations. The study aimed to assess whether specific HLA-B class I alleles were associated with the development of neurological disease in children with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection.

The study was performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethical Committee at the Faculty of Medicine, Ain Shams University, approval number FWA 000017585. The requirement for additional study-specific consent was waived by the Research Ethical Committee because HLA-B typing was performed using biological samples collected during routine hospitalization under the institutional admission consent framework, permitting research use of anonymized clinical data and residual biological material.

Eligibility criteria

All children aged one month to 16 years who were hospitalized at the Children’s Hospital, Faculty of Medicine, Ain Shams University, during the June 2022–June 2023 study period with confirmed current or previous SARS-CoV-2 infection were screened for eligibility. Children were included in the COVID-19 groups if they had complete clinical data and available HLA-B typing. No random sampling was performed.

Confirmation of SARS-CoV-2 infection in the COVID-19 groups was based on the 2020 World Health Organization (WHO) case definition. Acute infection was confirmed by either a positive Nucleic Acid Amplification Test (NAAT) or a positive SARS-CoV-2 Antigen Rapid Diagnostic Test (Ag-RDT). Previous or post-COVID-19 was confirmed by a documented history of SARS-CoV-2 infection with or without a positive SARS-CoV-2 IgG and/or IgM serology. None of the patients had received COVID-19 vaccination; therefore, positive SARS-CoV-2 IgG was considered evidence of prior infection. Exclusion criteria included children who did not meet the diagnostic criteria for COVID-19 or had an alternative explanation for the neurological symptoms.

Enrolled patients were classified according to the presence or absence of neurological manifestations. The COVID-19 with neurological disease (neuro-COVID-19) group consisted of 24 children with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection and evidence of neurological involvement. The COVID-19 without neurological disease (non-neuro COVID-19) group consisted of 23 children who had confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection with no evident neurological manifestations. Children with asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection were classified in the COVID-19 without neurological disease group if they had laboratory-confirmed infection and no clinical evidence of neurological involvement.

The study included a control group of 120 healthy children with no known or documented history of COVID-19, chronic medical conditions, or neurological or immunological disorders. Healthy controls were included only if they had no symptoms suggestive of COVID-19, no history of exposure to confirmed or suspected SARS-CoV-2 cases, and a negative SARS-CoV-2 serological test. They were selected to represent the baseline HLA-B allele distribution in the general pediatric population. The healthy control group was included to allow differentiation between alleles associated with susceptibility to COVID-19 itself and alleles specifically associated with neurological complications among infected patients. Clinical assessment

Medical history was obtained from patients and/or their legal guardians and supplemented by review of hospital medical records. Clinical history included a chronological description of presenting symptoms, the need for intensive care support, and clinical outcomes. Detailed general physical examination included hemodynamic stability, level of consciousness, presence of fever, and signs of systemic toxicity. All children with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection underwent clinical screening for neurological involvement through history taking, review of medical records, and neurological assessment during hospitalization.

Neurological assessment included (i) symptom assessment: headache, seizures, altered mental status, focal weakness, abnormal movements, sensory disturbances, visual impairment, and language deficits; and (ii) neurological examination: gait assessment; motor, sensory, cerebellar, and cranial nerve examination; evaluation for seizures; and signs of raised intracranial pressure. Neurological diagnoses in the neuro-COVID-19 group were confirmed by retrospective chart review, based on the diagnoses documented by the treating team during hospitalization. Diagnosis was supported by recorded neurological examination findings, neuroimaging, cerebrospinal fluid analysis, and neurophysiological studies, whenever applicable.

Inflammatory biomarker assessment

Recorded inflammatory biomarkers were included in this study, including C-reactive protein, ferritin, and D-dimer, which are clinically relevant pathways in COVID-19. These markers reflect systemic inflammation, hyperinflammatory response, and coagulation activation, respectively. Biomarker levels were extracted from hospital records and used to assess whether neurological complications of COVID-19 were associated with a change in systemic inflammatory profile.

Laboratory investigations

Type of SARS-CoV-2 confirmation was recorded, including NAAT/PCR, serological testing for SARS-CoV-2 IgM and/or IgG antibodies, or antigen rapid diagnostic testing (Ag-RDT) [12]. For HLA-B genotyping, peripheral venous blood was collected into EDTA tubes and stored at − 20 °C until analysis. HLA-B typing was performed using PCR with sequence-specific oligonucleotide probes (PCR-SSOP) [13].

DNA extraction

Total genomic DNA was isolated using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, USA). DNA concentration and purity were assessed by spectrophotometry, and samples with purity ratios between 1.72 and 1.93 were included.

Amplification and typing

Exons 2 and 3 of the HLA-B loci were amplified using the INNO-LiPA HLA-B Amplification Plus Kit (Fujirebio, Belgium). These exons were selected because they encode the α1 and α2 extracellular domains of HLA class I molecules, which form the peptide-binding groove and contain the major polymorphic regions required for HLA-B allele discrimination. Allele assignment was performed by reverse hybridization with sequence-specific probes (INNO-LiPA HLA-B Plus, Fujirebio) and interpreted using LiRAS™ for LiPA HLA v7.01 CE software.

Treatment and outcome assessment

Treatment courses during hospitalization were extracted from medical records. Recorded treatments included corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), anakinra, plasmapheresis, and other supportive or immunomodulatory interventions. As an observational study, descriptive analysis of received treatments included treatment patterns and assessed whether differences in management existed between children with and without neurological involvement. Although IVIG, anakinra, and plasmapheresis are not standard treatments for uncomplicated COVID-19, their use was according to clinical indication in patients with MIS-C, severe hyperinflammation, or suspected immune-mediated neurological disease. Clinical outcomes at discharge, including complete recovery, partial recovery with sequelae, and mortality, were also extracted from medical records.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and MedCalc® version 22.009 (MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium). Skewed numerical data were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as events and percentages and compared using Fisher’s exact test or the Fisher–Freeman–Halton test as appropriate. Ordinal variables were analyzed using linear-by-linear association. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was employed to assess the ability of biomarkers to discriminate between COVID-19 patients with and without neurological involvement. p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using univariate logistic regression models. Each HLA-B allele was analyzed separately as a binary predictor variable, coded as present or absent. For the COVID-19 risk analysis, the dependent variable was COVID-19 status compared with healthy controls. For the neurological involvement analysis, the dependent variable was neurological disease status among children with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, comparing neuro-COVID-19 patients with non-neuro COVID-19 patients. The natural logarithm of the odds ratio (log OR) was displayed in figures to provide a symmetric scale around zero, where log OR = 0 indicates no association, positive values indicate higher odds, and negative values indicate lower odds of the outcome.

Allele-specific p-values were considered nominal and exploratory because multiple HLA-B alleles were evaluated. Formal correction for multiple comparisons was not applied because this was an initial hypothesis-generating study with a modest sample size and sparse allele counts. The risk of type I error should be considered when interpreting the HLA-B53 finding. Multivariable logistic regression adjusting for age, sex, MIS-C status, and clinical heterogeneity was considered; however, the small number of included patients and low allele-positive event counts would make adjusted modelling statistically unstable. Accordingly, reported odds ratios should be interpreted as unadjusted exploratory estimates rather than independent predictors.

Results

General characteristics of included COVID-19 patients

Forty-seven children with confirmed COVID-19 were included, of whom 24 (51.1%) presented with neurological manifestations. Sex distribution was similar between groups (p = 0.772). However, children presenting with neurological disease were significantly older (p = 0.022). Fever at onset was more frequent in children without neurological involvement (p = 0.003). Gastrointestinal and respiratory symptoms at presentation were comparable between groups. Fever at hospital admission remained significantly more common in the non-neurological group (100% vs. 75.0%, p = 0.022). Other clinical manifestations, including multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C), cardiovascular or pulmonary involvement, circulatory shock, gastrointestinal symptoms, and cutaneous findings, were all similar in both groups (Table 1).

A comparative diagnostic performance analysis of inflammatory markers in predicting neurological involvement is presented in Table S1 and Fig. 2. CRP demonstrated the strongest discriminatory ability, with an AUC of 0.834. A CRP threshold of ≤ 76 mg/L provided optimal classification, yielding a sensitivity of 79.2% and specificity of 82.6%. Ferritin also showed moderate predictive utility (AUC 0.756), with an optimal cut-off of ≤ 314 ng/mL, achieving a sensitivity of 79.2% and specificity of 69.6%. D-dimer demonstrated good discrimination (AUC 0.815), with a cut-off of ≤ 1.8 mg/L yielding a sensitivity of 66.7% and specificity of 82.6%.

There was no difference in treatment regimens for the groups (Table S2). Most patients received corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG) in both groups. The usage of anakinra and plasmapheresis was limited with no significant differences between groups (p = 0.348 and 0.234, respectively). Overall recovery rates were high in both groups. Partial recovery sequelae at discharge were significantly more common among children with neurological involvement (29.2% vs. 4.3%, p = 0.048). Mortality was low and comparable between groups (p = 0.666).

Descriptive analysis of neuro-COVID-19 patients

As shown in Table 3, among the neuro-COVID group, the most frequent clinical presentation was altered level of consciousness/encephalopathy (50%), followed by headache (45.8%), while seizures occurred in 20.8%. Motor deficits, including ascending paralysis and sensory level, were present in 16.7%, and movement abnormalities (ataxia) and visual symptoms (optic involvement) were each reported in 12.5%. Multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) accounted for 37.5% of neurological cases. Among non-MIS-C cases, post-COVID encephalitis and Guillain–Barré syndrome were the most common, each representing 12.5%, followed by transverse myelitis (8.3%). Less frequent, but notable diagnoses, included optic neuritis, cerebellitis, and anterior spinal artery syndrome, demonstrating the broad inflammatory and vascular neurotropism of SARS-CoV-2.

Lumbar puncture could not be performed in 45.8% of cases, mainly due to hemodynamic instability or evidence of elevated intracranial pressure (Table S3). Almost 23.1% demonstrated lymphocytic pleocytosis, consistent with viral or immune-mediated neuroinflammation. Elevated CSF protein was observed in 15.4%, and hyperglycorrhachia in 46.2%. All patients underwent MRI, of which 54.2% demonstrated abnormal findings (Table S4). Lacunar infarctions were the most frequent MRI abnormality (16.7%), followed by demyelinating lesions (8.3%). Additional presentations included encephalitis, cerebellitis, posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome, and central pontine myelinolysis. MRA confirmed vasculitis in all cases where it was indicated. Among patients with motor deficits, spine MRI revealed longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis (LETM) in 50%, and nerve conduction studies confirmed axonal polyneuropathy.

Prevalence of HLA-B genotypes in the whole study population

Table S5 shows that the most prevalent allele in neuro-COVID-19 was HLA-B49 (10.4%). The most prevalent allele in non-neuro COVID-19 was HLA-B52 (15.2%), followed by HLA-B41 (13%). The most prevalent alleles in healthy controls were HLA-B14 and HLA-B35 (9.6%), and the least prevalent allele in the same group was B3 (0%).

Association between HLA-B alleles and COVID-19

The association between HLA-B alleles and COVID-19 was further examined using odds ratio analysis (Table S6). None of the assessed HLA-B alleles demonstrated a statistically significant association with COVID-19. However, HLA-B41 demonstrated a positive trend toward higher frequency in the neuro-COVID group (OR = 2.20; 95% CI: 0.88–5.51; p = 0.091), suggesting a possible but not definitive association.

Association between HLA-B alleles and development of neurological disease

No individual HLA-B allele reached statistical significance when comparing both groups (Table S7). HLA-B53 showed higher odds of neurological involvement when compared with children without neurological disease and healthy controls (OR = 6.29; 95% CI: 1.23–32.13; nominal p = 0.027) (Fig. 3). However, this result should be interpreted cautiously because multiple HLA-B alleles were tested, no formal multiplicity correction was applied, and the analysis was unadjusted. HLA-B45 showed a positive trend (OR = 3.75; 95% CI: 0.87–16.22; nominal p = 0.077), while HLA-B52 showed a possible lower-frequency trend in neuro-COVID-19 cases (OR = 0.24; 95% CI: 0.05–1.23; nominal p = 0.088). Other alleles were comparable between both groups (Table S8).

Discussion

Neurological complications of SARS-CoV-2 infection in children are increasingly recognized, including a spectrum from encephalopathy and cerebrovascular events to immune-mediated syndromes such as MIS-C and post-infectious neuropathies [14–16]. Clinical presentation heterogeneity underlines the importance of host-specific factors, such as genetic predisposition, in modulating susceptibility to central nervous system (CNS) involvement. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first to investigate HLA-B class I alleles in Egyptian pediatric COVID-19 patients with neurological complications compared to infected children without neurological involvement and healthy pediatric controls.

Our study illustrated central and peripheral neurological manifestations in line with SARS-CoV-2 neurotropism and immune-mediated injury. Altered consciousness and headache were the most frequent presentations, with seizures, motor deficits, and visual symptoms occurring less commonly. MIS-C accounted for over one-third of cases, with CNS involvement including vasculitis, ischemic stroke, and encephalitis. Other cases featured post-COVID encephalitis, Guillain–Barré syndrome, and transverse myelitis. This supports that CNS neuroinvasion of the SARS-CoV-2 virus could occur through direct or indirect pathways. Direct effects involve viral tropism for CNS-resident cells such as neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and endothelial cells, leading to neuroinflammation, microglial activation, and, in some cases, demyelination or neuronal injury [17]. The indirect impact of immune-induced neurological involvement could include cytokine storms, molecular mimicry, and bystander activation. The variability in reported neurological symptoms in both acute and post-COVID cases highlights the need for continuous CNS monitoring in COVID-19 patients [18].

Secondary clinical observations support previous immunogenetic interpretations. Children with neurological symptoms were older than those without (median 5.75 vs. 3 years, p = 0.022), which is in line with earlier studies indicating that older children may produce either strong or unregulated immune responses that cause neuroinflammation [19]. At the beginning of the symptoms, fever was less common in neuro-COVID (20.8% vs. 65.2%), and neuro-COVID cases had lower systemic inflammatory markers (CRP, ferritin, D-dimer), reinforcing the hypothesis of localized CNS immune dysregulation, possibly modulated by HLA genotype, rather than a classic extracerebral cytokine storm [17]. Our ROC analyses (CRP AUC = 0.834; ferritin AUC = 0.756; D‑dimer AUC = 0.815) suggest that lower thresholds of systemic inflammation may discriminate neuro‑COVID from non‑neuro‑COVID. The findings should be validated in further studies.

Recent large‑scale real‑world data support the role of HLA genetics in SARS‑CoV‑2 infection. In a cohort of over 319,000 individuals, Letovsky et al. found that, after controlling for exposure risk and socioeconomic status, most HLA alleles had minimal impact on infection susceptibility [9]. Notably, one allele (HLA‑B38:01) was associated with a reduced risk of testing positive in Europeans, and a paired allele interaction (HLA‑DQB103:02 + HLA‑B*38:01). These findings underscore that HLA‑B alleles may influence the host response (e.g., disease course, severity, immune activation) rather than the initial risk of infection. In our study, no HLA-B allele showed a statistically significant association with COVID-19 status. HLA-B53 showed an association with neurological involvement among infected children; however, this result should be interpreted with caution because of the small sample size, likely low absolute allele count, and wide confidence interval around the effect estimate (OR = 6.29; 95% CI: 1.23–32.13). These results align with the Letovsky et al. data, highlighting that HLA may not affect infection susceptibility strongly; however, it may have a role in modulating disease phenotype and complications.

While HLA-B45 showed a positive trend, it did not reach statistical significance. HLA-B52 showed a lower frequency in the neuro-COVID group; however, this finding did not reach statistical significance. This is consistent with mechanistic evidence from Norin et al., which showed that HLA-B53 exhibits broader yet potentially suboptimal viral peptide binding, possibly facilitating prolonged antigen persistence and aberrant immune activation [20]. Such a mechanism could explain the increased susceptibility of HLA-B53 carriers to CNS-targeted inflammatory injury in COVID-19, whereas the lower observed frequency of HLA-B52 in the neuro-COVID group suggests a possible trend; however, it was not statistically significant. Our findings align partially with prior Egyptian studies. Abdelhafiz et al. reported associations between HLA-B41/B42 and COVID-19 susceptibility, with B15 being protective [21], while El-Najjar et al. found B41 associated with susceptibility and B35 linked to disease severity [22]. Globally, HLA-B51 has been associated with COVID-19 susceptibility in Chinese and Italian cohorts [23, 24]. Differences across studies likely reflect population-specific allele frequencies, age distributions, clinical phenotyping, viral variants, and sample sizes, highlighting the necessity of multi-ethnic, large-scale studies. The implications of these results support that the early identification of children carrying HLA-B53 may facilitate risk stratification and prompt neurological monitoring, neuroimaging, and consideration of immunomodulatory therapy.

The observed HLA-B53 findings in our study should not be interpreted as a definitive or independent association. The presence of multiple allele-level comparisons increases the probability of type I error, where reported p-values were not corrected for multiplicity. In addition, the neuro-COVID-19 and non-neuro COVID-19 groups differed significantly in age, and some clinical heterogeneity remained between patients. However, multivariable adjustment was not statistically feasible due to the small sample size and limited allele-positive event counts. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and the HLA-B53 finding should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Strengths and limitations

This research’s strengths lie in the thoroughly characterized pediatric group with clinical and radiological proven neurological phenotypes, the comparisons not just with non-neuro COVID-19 patients but also with healthy ones, and the HLA-B high-resolution typing.

Limitations include a modest sample size, limiting the detection of rare alleles and stratified analyses by specific neurological diagnoses, and the focus on HLA-B alone, precluding the assessment of other class I or class II alleles and broader immunogenetic loci. Another limitation is the potential for recall bias and exposure misclassification in the healthy control/unexposed group. Although controls had no known or documented COVID-19 history, no reported exposure, no symptoms suggestive of COVID-19, and negative SARS-CoV-2 serology, unrecognized asymptomatic infection or incomplete recall of prior exposure cannot be fully excluded. This may have led to inclusion of previously infected asymptomatic children in the unexposed group.

In addition, multiple HLA-B alleles were tested without formal correction for multiple comparisons. Therefore, allele-level p-values should be interpreted as nominal, and the HLA-B53 association should be regarded as exploratory rather than confirmatory. The small number of neuro- and non-neuro COVID cases and healthy controls limited statistical power, especially for uncommon alleles, and increased the possibility of unstable effect estimates, false-positive associations, and overinterpretation of rare allele signals.

A multivariable logistic regression adjusting for age, sex, and MIS-C status was considered; however, the small sample size and low expected allele counts would preclude robust adjusted modelling, with the risk of overfitting and unstable estimates. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly because the neuro-COVID-19 group was significantly older than the non-neuro COVID-19 group. The reported HLA-B53 results should be interpreted as an unadjusted exploratory association rather than an independent predictor of neurological involvement.

Population stratification may also affect generalizability, reinforcing the importance of diverse, multicenter studies. Due to the retrospective nature of this single-center study, selection bias cannot be fully excluded. However, this risk was reduced by including all eligible hospitalized children with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection during the defined study period who had complete clinical data and available HLA-B typing, rather than selecting patients randomly or based on outcome.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this exploratory study identified a possible nominal unadjusted association between HLA-B53 and neurological involvement in pediatric COVID-19. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size, multiple allele testing without formal correction, and the absence of adjusted modelling for potential confounders. Validation through larger multicenter studies with adequate statistical power, multiplicity correction, and multivariable adjustment is required before HLA-B53 can be considered a reliable marker of neurological risk in pediatric SARS-CoV-2 infection.
