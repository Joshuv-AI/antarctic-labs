Peripheral biochemical parameters for discrimination between bipolar disorder and major depressive disorder in female patients of reproductive age: a CRP-stratified exploratory study
Authors: Yifei Xu, Tianming Huang, Fang Wang, Ni Zhou, Xiaojia Huang, Chuangxin Wu, Yue Fei, Ningning Li, Ruyan Huang, Yunfan Shao, Rongjie Mao, Ahong Xu, Xin Dong, Zuowei Wang, Yuncheng Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBipolar disorder (BD) is often misdiagnosed as major depressive disorder (MDD) due to overlapping clinical syndromes. Although numerous studies have investigated biomarkers to differentiate these two disorders, no clinically applicable differential diagnostic panel has been established. Notably, sex-specific differences play a critical role in this context. Females are more prone to experience depressive episodes and exhibit more pronounced immune-inflammatory dysregulation, which further increases the risk of misdiagnosis. This study aimed to explore whether C-reactive protein (CRP)-based stratified modeling of routinely available biochemical parameters could improve discriminative performance in reproductive-age women.MethodsA total of 502 female patients of reproductive age were enrolled, including 263 with BD and 239 with MDD, grouped according to CRP levels. Fourteen clinical biochemical indexes were compared: white blood cells (WBC), platelets, uric acid (UA), direct bilirubin (DBIL), indirect bilirubin (IBIL), glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (GOT), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), prealbumin, triglycerides, low-density lipoproteins (LDL), testosterone, free triiodothyronine (FT3), free tetraiodothyronine (FT4) and thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH). Binary logistic regression analysis was employed to develop predictive models for distinguishing BD from MDD. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), and the holistic model was internally validated with 10-fold cross-validation.ResultsIn the Normal-CRP group, BD patients had higher levels of WBC, IBIL, GOT, LDH, and FT4, and lower DBIL, prealbumin, and triglycerides compared to MDD patients. In the high CRP group, BD patients had higher IBIL and LDH, and lower DBIL (p < 0.05). Five predictive models were constructed. The Holistic model yielded an apparent AUC of 0.805 (cross-validated mean AUC: 0.808), the High-Risk model (3 ≤ CRP <5 mg/L) achieved an AUC of 0.887, and the High-CRP model (CRP ≥ 5 mg/L) had an AUC of 0.929. Detailed results for the Low-Risk and Normal-CRP models are available in the Supplementary Material.ConclusionsThe CRP-stratified models demonstrated promising discriminative performance, with the highest AUC observed in the High-CRP model. These results suggest that CRP-based stratification may serve as an exploratory approach to aid differential diagnosis in women of reproductive age.LimitationsGiven their limited sample sizes, the High-Risk and High-CRP models were considered exploratory approaches and were not internally validated. The CRP-based stratified models are still in the exploratory phase and require external validation to confirm their clinical applicability.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08327-z.

Background

Bipolar disorder (BD) is frequently misdiagnosed as major depressive disorder (MDD). More than one-third of BD patients endure delays in diagnosis of more than a decade before receiving an accurate diagnosis [1]. The absence of reliable definitive detection methods presents a formidable challenge for clinicians tasked with differentiating between bipolar and unipolar depression, especially when prior (hypo)manic episodes go unrecognized [2]. In outpatient settings, as many as 70.6% of BD patients are initially misdiagnosed as MDD [3]. Furthermore, a 10-year prospective study of MDD cases revealed a low long-term diagnostic consistency rate of merely 45.5% [4], while a 7-year cohort study reported a relatively higher yet still suboptimal consistency rate of 71.9% for BD [5]. Prolonged diagnostic delays contribute to inappropriate treatment, disease progression, and elevated suicide risk, highlighting the urgent demand for objective biomarkers to enable accurate differentiation between MDD and BD [6].

Research efforts have centered on inflammatory markers [7] and metabolic phenotypes [8] as aids in distinguishing BD from MDD. Although prior studies have investigated the utility of inflammatory and routine biochemical markers for this differential diagnosis [9, 10], no clinically validated biomarker panel has been established to date. C-reactive protein (CRP) has been implicated in oxidative stress pathways in previous research [11]. As such, CRP not only functions as an inflammatory biomarker but also appears to indirectly reflect oxidative stress levels. Our prior findings indicate that CRP concentrations are significantly higher in BD compared to those with MDD, with this difference being particularly pronounced among female patients [12]. A growing body of evidence links mood disorders to oxidative stress and inflammation [13–15], further supporting the potential value of CRP in biological stratification.

Beyond CRP, growing clinical and biochemical evidence indicates that biomarker profiles in mood disorders exhibit substantial and clinically meaningful sex differences. For example, MDD patients display distinct alteration patterns in uric acid (UA) and thyroid function parameters [16], and the association between thyroid indices and MDD is significantly stronger in females [17]. In addition, female patients with BD tend to present a more favorable cardiovascular-metabolic lipid profile, characterized by higher high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and lower low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels [18]. These laboratory observations are consistent with well-documented sex disparities in the epidemiology and clinical phenotypes of mood disorders [19, 20].

The incidence of MDD is approximately twofold higher in women than in men, which may be ascribed to a combination of psychosocial stressors, financial strain, and excessive societal role expectations imposed on women [21]. Recent research also indicates a rising proportion of female patients across all subtypes of BD [22]. Notably, females are more likely to manifest depressive episodes at disease onset [23, 24]. With the progression of BD, women tend to experience more severe anxiety symptoms [25] and roughly one extra depressive episode relative to men [23], thereby elevating their risk of misdiagnosis. A plausible biological mechanism is that females possess a more heterogeneous immune cell repertoire, leading to elevated immunoglobulin levels [26, 27]. This may be associated with augmented stress reactivity and a predisposition to low-grade inflammatory state [28, 29]. Furthermore, ovarian hormone fluctuations across the menstrual cycle can increase women’s susceptibility to psychosocial stress—a well-established risk factor for MDD [30]—while also regulating immune function. Consequently, females may carry a higher risk of developing stress- and inflammation-related depression [31]. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of sex-stratified analyses, particularly among women of reproductive age.

Notwithstanding extensive investigations, very few studies have adopted inflammation-based stratification models to improve the differential diagnostic performance of routine laboratory biomarkers in females [32–35]. We therefore hypothesize that stratification according to CRP levels may improve the discriminatory capacity of routine biochemical markers for differentiating BD from MDD. Accordingly, this study performed an exploratory stratification modeling analysis in reproductive-age female patients, with the objective of developing and evaluating a CRP-stratified modeling approach using routine peripheral markers to assist in the differential diagnosis of mood disorders.

Methods

Trial registration and study design

This study is a retrospective real-world analysis using data collected between 01/01/2020 and 12/31/2023. This dataset was subsequently included in a registered clinical trial, “Prediction of Depressive Disorder Based on EEG and Metabolomics (ChiCTR2400091918, retrospectively registered on 11/05/2024)”, on the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ChiCTR; https://www.chictr.org.cn/). The CRP-stratified modeling approach presented herein is a post-hoc exploratory analysis of historical data, designed for hypothesis generation rather than testing predefined endpoints. Formal ethical approval was granted, and informed consent was formally waived by the Ethical Committee of the Shanghai Hongkou Mental Health Center (approval number: 2021-B02). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the national and institutional committees on human experimentation and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975 (revised 2008). Personal identifying information was removed to safeguard patient privacy.

Based on effect sizes and variables retained in previous models from our prior studies conducted in independent samples [7, 12, 36], and after excluding variables with high multicollinearity, 14 biochemical predictors were retained from an initial pool of 37 parameters: white blood cells (WBC), platelets, UA, direct bilirubin (DBIL), indirect bilirubin (IBIL), glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (GOT), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), prealbumin, triglycerides, LDL, testosterone, free triiodothyronine (FT3), free tetraiodothyronine (FT4), and thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH). A detailed description of the predictor selection process is provided in the Supplementary Methods and the flowchart is presented as Fig. S1.

Missing data

Patients with missing CRP records were excluded from modeling due to unclassifiable grouping status. Additionally, patients lacking over 30% (≥ 5 items) of data among the 14 selected predictors were excluded. This exclusion criterion was applied to ensure data completeness and result reliability. The main sources of missing data were inconsistencies in routine laboratory tests across the three participating hospitals and, in some instances, patients providing recent external laboratory test results from other medical institutions with incomplete data transfer. To preserve sample size and model stability—especially in the smaller High-Risk and High-CRP subgroups—the remaining missing values were imputed using the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm in SPSS. The potential limitations of this imputation method are discussed in the Limitations section.

Grouping

Patient stratification was performed according to CRP cut-off values established in previous randomized controlled trials (RCTs). All participants were first categorized into a Normal-CRP Group (CRP < 5 mg/L) and a High-CRP Group (CRP ≥ 5 mg/L), adopting the 5 mg/L threshold proposed by Raison et al. for predicting differential response to anti-inflammatory treatment [37]. Within the Normal CRP Group, a High-Risk Group (3 mg/L ≤ CRP <5 mg/L) was further defined based on the cut-off of low-grade peripheral inflammation reported by Nettis et al. [38]. Accordingly, five analytical models were constructed for analysis:Holistic Model: All enrolled patients.Low-Risk Model: Patients with CRP < 3 mg/L.High-Risk Model: Patients with 3 mg/L ≤ CRP < 5 mg/L.Normal-CRP Model: Patients with CRP < 5 mg/L.High-CRP Model: Patients with CRP ≥ 5 mg/L.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 27.0.1). To improve the comparability across 14 predictors, all variables were standardized via zero-mean normalization. Prior to comparative analyses, data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test. All variables significantly deviated from a normal distribution (p < 0.05), suggesting that the assumptions for parametric testing were not satisfied. Therefore, between-group comparisons of blood biochemical parameters were performed using the two-tailed Mann-Whitney U test, with the significance level set at α = 0.05.

Binary logistic regression models were then developed. The outcome variable was diagnostic grouping, coded as 0 = MDD and 1 = BD. For the Holistic model, all 14 biochemical predictors were entered simultaneously via the Enter method; age, disease duration, and progesterone were included as covariates to adjust for potential confounding effects. The discriminative ability of the Holistic model was internally validated using 10-fold cross-validation. The mean area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) was reported as a conservative estimate of model performance. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each predictor, and the 95% CI of the apparent AUC was also determined.

For the subgroup models, the Forward Conditional method was employed with a significance level of 0.05 for variable entry and 0.10 for removal. Given their limited sample sizes and comparability between them, internal validation was not conducted for these subgroups, and the corresponding analyses were regarded as exploratory. ROC curves were plotted based on predicted probabilities, and the model discriminative power was quantified using AUC values. Model calibration was assessed with the Hosmer-Lemeshow (H-L) test. Considering the inherent differences in modeling strategies and sample sizes between the Holistic model and the exploratory subgroup models, formal statistical comparisons of AUCs were performed only among subgroups using z-tests to evaluate the discriminative utility of the stratification strategy.

Developing predictive models using binary logistic regression analysis

Binary logistic regression models were constructed following missing values imputation using the EM algorithm. Five models were constructed: a Holistic model including all patients (n = 502), a Low-Risk model including patients with CRP < 3 mg/L (n = 417), a High-Risk model including patients with 3 ≤ CRP < 5 mg/L (n = 38), a Normal-CRP model including patients with CRP < 5 mg/L (n = 455, comprising the Low-Risk and High-Risk models), and a High-CRP model including patients with CRP ≥ 5 mg/L (n = 47). Age and disease duration were included as covariates to adjust for potential confounding effects, and progesterone (a menstrual cycle-related hormone [12]) was employed to account for hormonal confounding in women.

For the Holistic model, all 14 biochemical predictors were entered simultaneously using the Enter method, with results presented in Table 4. The model’s apparent discriminative performance, estimated from the full sample, yielded an AUC of 0.805 (95% CI: 0.768, 0.843). Internal validation via 10-fold cross-validation generated a mean AUC of 0.808 (range: 0.799–0.814), indicating satisfactory model stability. See Table 5. Discussion

This study aimed to test the hypothesis that systemic inflammation, as indexed by CRP, modulates the capacity of routine biochemical markers to distinguish BD from MDD among women of reproductive age. We hypothesized that CRP-based stratification would improve model performance by accounting for biological heterogeneity, and our findings support this hypothesis. CRP stratification identified a subgroup in which routine biochemical differences between MDD and BD appear to be more internally discriminative. The progressive improvement in AUC across CRP strata suggests that inflammation-guided subgrouping may facilitate the identification of patients with more distinct biological signatures. Discriminative performance improved incrementally with rising CRP levels, reaching an AUC of 0.929 in the High-CRP group (≥ 5 mg/L), compared with an apparent AUC of 0.805 in the unstratified model. Although the increased AUC in the High-CRP subgroup is encouraging, these results should be interpreted cautiously given the smaller sample size and the absence of internal validation in these exploratory analyses. These findings suggest that integrating immune inflammatory status as a stratification variable with peripheral biomarkers may represent a feasible exploratory stratified modeling strategy.

To further examine whether this stratification strategy identifies biologically distinct subgroups, pairwise ROC comparisons were performed among the four exploratory models. A significant difference was observed between the Normal-CRP and High-CRP models, as well as between the Low-Risk and High-CRP models, suggesting that the 5 mg/L threshold effectively isolates a subgroup with more pronounced biochemical differences between MDD and BD. In contrast, the non-significant differences observed between the Low-Risk and High-Risk models indicate that the 3 mg/L threshold does not clearly delineate a biologically distinct subgroup. Notably, these comparisons are intended to evaluate the utility of the stratification strategy rather than to validate the optimality of any specific CRP cut-point, as the thresholds were pre-specified based on prior treatment-response literature [37, 38] rather than derived from the current dataset.

Specifically, BD patients exhibited significantly lower DBIL, higher IBIL, and elevated LDH levels in both the Normal-CRP group (< 5 mg/L) and High-CRP group (≥ 5 mg/L), implying potential associations with oxidative stress and cellular metabolic disturbances. Altered bilirubin metabolism and LDH elevations have been well documented in mood disorders [39–41], and several studies have proposed antioxidants such as DBIL and IBIL as potential differential diagnostic markers [36, 42–44]. The low DBIL/high IBIL pattern observed in BD aligns with the hypothesis that DBIL may be extensively consumed in response to heightened oxidative stress, triggering a compensatory rise in IBIL [36]. However, the present study did not directly measure oxidative stress markers; thus, this interpretation remains speculative and deserves to be investigated in future mechanistic studies. In the Normal-CRP group, BD patients also presented with elevated WBC, GOT, and FT4, alongside reduced prealbumin and triglyceride levels, suggesting broader disruptions in immune regulation and metabolic homeostasis. The elevated WBC count observed in BD, even within the normal CRP range, is consistent with the view that low-grade chronic inflammation represents a trait feature of BD. Consistently, previous studies have reported that patients with BD and MDD exhibit higher levels of systemic inflammatory markers compared to healthy controls [45–47], with inflammation tending to be more pronounced in BD [48–50]. In our data, elevated CRP levels were accompanied by higher WBC counts, suggesting a greater degree of immune-inflammatory activation.

Among these biomarkers, prealbumin and TSH were incorporated into the predictive models, showing positive and negative associations with MDD, respectively—findings consistent with our previous research [36]. Prealbumin is a negative acute-phase reactant involved in thyroxine and retinol transport, and its alterations have been associated with thyroid function changes in mood disorders. Prior studies have confirmed that low prealbumin levels constitute an independent risk factor for MDD [51], with serum and cerebrospinal fluid prealbumin levels in both MDD [51–56] and BD [57, 58] patients lower than those in healthy controls. This reduction may be related to inflammatory and thyroid-related disturbances during the acute phase of mood disorders. Thyroid dysfunction has long been recognized as an independent risk factor for increased mood disorder incidence [59–62], with patients affected by mood disorders exhibiting approximately twice the prevalence of thyroid disease compared with healthy controls, and a threefold higher incidence in females than in males [63]. While only TSH was included in the final predictive models, the potential roles of FT3 and FT4 in mood disorders warrant further investigation.

As a pivotal acute-phase protein, CRP directly reflects systemic inflammatory burden and has been indirectly associated with oxidative stress levels in several studies. Multiple studies have reported significantly elevated CRP levels in patients with MDD and BD compared to the general population [64–71], a phenomenon linked to augmented inflammation and oxidative stress [67, 72, 73]. In this study, patients were stratified based on CRP thresholds established in existing literature. A CRP level > 5 mg/L is documented as a threshold for predicting anti-inflammatory treatment response [37], while CRP ≥ 3 mg/L defines a state of “low-grade inflammation” [38]. These thresholds were derived from treatment-response studies but have not been independently validated for the purpose of differential diagnosis between MDD and BD. This stratification strategy effectively revealed the heterogeneity in pathological processes, as reflected by distinct biomarker profiles (e.g., DBIL and LDH) between groups, with differences remaining statistically significant following logistic regression adjustments. This highlights the capacity of CRP stratification to isolate subgroups characterized by pronounced differential biomarker expression.

This study focused exclusively on women of reproductive age to leverage their relatively stable menstrual cycles and hormonal environment, thereby minimizing confounding. Progesterone was included as a covariate to account for hormonal variation; although it was retained in the High-Risk model, it was not selected in the majority of models. Importantly, adjusting for progesterone did not alter the significance of the primary findings. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that the female immune system exhibits heightened sensitivity to external stimuli, potentially driven by pro-inflammatory effects of estrogen, which may modulate inflammatory pathways in mood disorders [74, 75]. Our findings suggest that under specific inflammatory conditions, routinely available biomarkers showed more pronounced differences between MDD and BD, underscoring the influence of sex and endocrine status on biomarker research. The models developed herein, which incorporated strict inclusion criteria, achieved higher AUC values than those reported in a prior study that did not restrict by sex or age (AUC = 0.677 [27]). However, direct comparisons are limited by disparities in study design and the deliberately narrow population. These results highlight that within this narrowly defined dataset, a relatively stable physiological state may contribute to more reliable discriminative outcomes, although the generalizability of these findings beyond women of reproductive age remains to be established.

In summary, this study proposes an exploratory CRP-stratified modeling approach that yielded promising discriminative performance in women of reproductive age. Future prospective studies with comprehensive covariate control and external validation are warranted to further evaluate the generalizability of these findings to broader populations.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. Firstly, the retrospective design of this study precludes the establishment of causal relationships between variables and outcomes.

Secondly, due to the modest sample size and the deliberate focus on a narrow population, all eligible patients from the available dataset were utilized to maximize statistical power. Consequently, 10-fold cross-validation was performed for the Holistic model but could not be extended to the smaller subgroups without further reducing their already limited sample sizes. Additionally, the Holistic model used the Enter method, whereas the Forward Conditional method was adopted for the smaller subgroups. Although this decision was necessitated by limited sample sizes in stratified analyses, it introduces inconsistency in modeling methodology and complicates direct model comparisons. Therefore, the elevated AUC observed in the High-CRP subgroup (0.929) may partially reflect overfitting or instability arising from the stepwise variable selection and the absence of internal validation, rather than indicating a biological phenomenon. Missing data were imputed using the EM method to preserve sample size, particularly in the smaller High-risk and High-CRP subgroups. However, imputation in small samples may underestimate standard errors and yield overly optimistic performance estimates.

Thirdly, blood samples were collected at first diagnosis prior to standardized treatment initiation. However, while participants who had used non-psychotropic medications affecting targeted pathways of interest within the preceding 3 months were excluded, residual confounding from baseline psychotropic medication exposure (mood stabilizers and antidepressants [76, 77]) could not be ruled out. All patients were instructed to abstain from alcohol and smoking for at least 12 hours prior to blood sampling to minimize the short-term effects on tested parameters [78, 79]. Besides, key covariates potentially influencing inflammatory responses including body mass index (BMI), lifetime tobacco and alcohol use, and supplement intake were not captured due to data limitations in the retrospective database. These omissions may introduce confounding bias.

Finally, because this study deliberately restricted enrollment to women of reproductive age to leverage their relatively stable hormonal environment, the findings cannot be generalized to males, postmenopausal women, or adolescent females undergoing pubertal maturation. Validation in these populations is required in future studies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this exploratory research demonstrates that CRP-stratified modeling of routine biochemical markers yields promising discriminatory performance for differentiating BD from MDD in women of reproductive age. The highest performance was observed in the High-CRP subgroup, although the smaller sample size warrants cautious interpretation of these estimates. These findings require external validation in future studies. Overall, inflammation-guided stratification may serve as an auxiliary approach to aid differential diagnosis, while its generalizability beyond this deliberately narrow population remains to be established.
