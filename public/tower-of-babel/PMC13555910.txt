Cone-beam computed tomography evaluation of age-related anatomical relationships and pathological findings in impacted maxillary canines
Authors: Hande Seyithanoğlu Doğruer, Ayşe Pınar Sumer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe present study aims to examine and classify the localization of canine teeth impacted in the maxilla for various reasons, their relationship with adjacent structures, and the developing pathologies by using cone beam computed tomography (CBCT).Material and methodCBCT images taken for various reasons from 175 patients with maxillary impacted canine teeth that met the research criteria were examined. Demographic characteristics of the patients such as age and gender were recorded. The maxillary impacted canine teeth were examined by using CBCT in terms of their locations, relationship with the maxillary sinus, nasal floor and nasopalatine canal (NPC), relationship with adjacent teeth and the associated pathologies.ResultsA total of 209 maxillary impacted canine teeth of 175 patients were examined. Of the patients included in the study, 37.3% were male, while 62.7% were female. The mean age was found as 35 (age range 18–70). The rate of unilateral impacted canine teeth was 67.5% and 52.6% of the canine teeth were found to be located on the right side of the maxilla. Canine teeth were palatally impacted in 79% and 89% were found to be in a mesioangular position. The rate of canine teeth associated with the nasal cavity floor was found to be 7%, the rate of canine teeth with roots extending into the maxillary sinus was 6%, and the rate of canine teeth associated with NPC was found to be 15%. When the relationship of maxillary impacted canine teeth with the surrounding anatomical structures was examined, maxillary impacted canine teeth of male were found to have a higher relationship with the nasal cavity floor. The rate of pathologies associated with maxillary impacted canine teeth was found to be 22.5%. The most common pathologies were intracoronal resorption in maxillary canine teeth (8%) and root resorption in lateral incisors (7%).ConclusionThe results of the present study show that maxillary impacted canine teeth can remain for many years without causing pathology. However, it is crucial to evaluate them comprehensively by using CBCT when orthodontic or surgical intervention is planned.Keywords: Cone beam computed tomography maxilla, Maxillary impacted canine tooth

Introduction

Maxillary canine teeth have an important role in terms of facial aesthetics, occlusion and the development of dental arch due to their position in the jaws and they are the teeth most commonly impacted after third molars [1, 2]. The prevalence of maxillary impacted canine teeth has been reported to range between 0.9% and 3.3% [3]. The incidence of maxillary impacted canine teeth is twice as high as that of mandibular impacted canine teeth [4].

Studies conducted on various societies and age groups have shown that maxillary impacted canine teeth are more common in females and often located unilaterally and in the palatal region [5]. Its incidence in females is approximately twice of that in males. It has been found that in 8% of patients with maxillary impacted canine teeth, the teeth are impacted bilaterally [6].

There are several theories associated with the retention of maxillary impacted canine teeth. These are a long eruption path, prolonged retention of maxillary persistent deciduous canine tooth, crowding, trauma, genetic and molecular theory [7–12]. In addition to the etiological factors of impacted maxillary canines, the effects of these ectopically positioned teeth on surrounding anatomical structures and adjacent tissues are of considerable clinical importance and represent a significant area of evaluation.

Impacted teeth are usually asymptomatic. However, many complications can occur related to impaction. A dentigerous cyst is the most common type of odontogenic developmental cyst, which contains the crown of an impacted tooth. The most common location for this cyst is the mandibular and maxillary third molar and maxillary canine region. When the follicle width around the tooth is 5 mm, dentigerous cyst should be considered suspicious [13]. Radiographic findings of a dentigerous cyst are a unilocular radiolucent area surrounded by a smooth, sclerotic border around an impacted tooth [13].

One of the pathologies associated with maxillary impacted teeth is intracoronal resorption. Abnormal, well-defined, radiolucent lesions within the coronal enamel/dentin tissue of impacted teeth are referred to as pre-eruptive intracoronal resorption. Clinically, this resorption has been reported to look like normal caries. However, the causative agents are not cariogenic bacteria [14, 15].

One of the most common complications caused by maxillary impacted canine teeth is root resorption in the permanent lateral incisors and central incisors [16]. Diagnosing root resorption in early stages is critically important in the treatment protocol. Maxillary impacted canine teeth, particularly when located in the palatal, can cause resorption on the palatal surfaces of adjacent incisors. Although this condition sometimes progresses to the pulp, it cannot be detected on two-dimensional (2D) radiographs due to superposition [17].

In detecting maxillary impacted canine teeth, intraoral imaging methods such as periapical and occlusal images and extraoral imaging methods such as PR(Panoramic Radiography), PA (Posteroanterior) radiograph, lateral cephalometric and submentovertex 2D radiographs are used.

In the maxilla, due to the superposition of teeth and their anatomical structures, the relationship of maxillary impacted canine teeth with adjacent teeth and structures cannot be determined clearly by using 2D radiographs. Three-dimensional (3D) imaging modalities, such as cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) and computed tomography (CT), are used to determine the exact localization of impacted maxillary canines and to detect damage in adjacent tissues (such as resorption) without the superimposition of anatomical and dental structures.

Although numerous studies have examined the radiological, clinical, and surgical findings, prevalence, and associated pathologies of impacted canine teeth, investigations focusing on the relationships between these teeth and pathologies such as intracoronal resorption, root resorption of adjacent teeth, cystic lesions, and suspected ankylosis, as well as their associations with surrounding anatomical structures (including the nasopalatine canal, maxillary sinus, and nasal floor) across different age groups, remain limited. The early identification of age-related pathologies and alterations in the relationship between impacted maxillary canines and surrounding anatomical structures may contribute to anticipating and minimizing potential complications during surgical planning. Furthermore, the findings emphasize the importance of early diagnosis and appropriate treatment planning for impacted canine teeth in preventing pathological changes that may develop with increasing age and in facilitating safer surgical interventions.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship between increasing age and the likelihood of pathologies such as suspected dentigerous cysts and intracoronal resorption, as well as to assess age-related changes in the relationship between impacted maxillary canines and surrounding anatomical structures. Additionally, considering gender-related differences, the study aimed to investigate whether these anatomical relationships differ between male and female individuals.

Material and method

Study plan

A priori power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software to determine the required sample size. With α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) for the primary outcome (pathology prevalence in impacted maxillary canines), a minimum of 150 patients was calculated. Our study included 175 patients with 209 impacted canines, providing adequate statistical power (> 80%) to detect clinically meaningful differences.

In the study, images from 5400 patients who had CBCT for various reasons at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology, between January 2024 and December 2024 were reviewed retrospectively. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ondokuz Mayıs Üniversitesi with approval number 2023\98. Written informed consent for the use of clinical and radiological data was obtained from all patients.

CBCT images of a total of 175 patients aged 18 years and older, including 109 female and 66 male who met the inclusion criteria, were evaluated in the present study.

CBCT images used in the study were taken with, affiliated with the GALILEOS make (Dentsplay Sirona, Bensheim, Germany) dental volumetric imaging system with a FOV (Field of view) of 15 cm x 15 cm, operating at 98 kVp and 15–30 mAs values. CBCT images were reconstructed by using SIRONA Sidexis XG 2.61 imaging program with 12-bit gray levels, isotropic voxels measuring 0.25 mm³, and a radiation dose range of 612–1139 mGy x cm². Irradiation time was 2–6 s, while scanning time was 14 s. Radiation dose range was 28–154 µSv. (Sirona Dental Systems GmbH.(2016) [Computer Software])

Image evaluation

CBCT images were analyzed by an Oral and Maxillofacial Radiologist (HSD) under dim lighting on a color LCD screen with a resolution of 3.7 MP, 1440 × 2560 and a screen size of 68 cm. (TheRadio Force MX270W, EizoNanao Corporation, Ishikawa, Japan) by using the software’s diagnostic tools (magnification and contrast).

A total of 209 impacted maxillary canine teeth from 175 patients who met the study criteria were included in the study. For intra-observer agreement, 70 randomly selected CBCT images were re-evaluated by the same observer two weeks later. The teeth were examined in sagittal, coronal, axial and cross-sectional sections and 3D reconstructions in terms of the following parameters:

Localization (Buccal, Palatal, Median) B/P/M: The location of the maxillary impacted canine tooth was determined by examining the position of its crown relative to the roots of the incisors. If the crown of the canine teeth was located palatally to the roots of the incisors, it was considered to be palatal; if it was located buccaly, it was considered to be buccal and if it was located in the median sagittal plane, it was considered to be median [18].

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed with IBM SPSS V23 software. Pearson Chi-Square Test, Fisher’s Exact Test, Yates Correction and Monte Carlo corrected Fisher’s Exact Test were used to examine the relationship between categorical variables. Multiple comparisons were examiend with Bonferroni Corrected Z test. Mean ± standard deviation and median (minimum-maximum) were used to show the quantitative variables. Frequency (percentage) was used to show the categorical variables. Level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 209 maxillary impacted canine teeth of 175 patients were evaluated. Of these patients, 62.7% were female, 37.3% were male and mean age was found as 35.86.

Intraobserver agreement was found to be very good in terms of B/P/M parameter (Kappa = 0.986). Intraobserver agreement was also found to be very good in terms of right/left, unilateral\bilateral, position, persistent deciduous canine tooth, pathology, suspected ankylosis and relationship with adjacent structures (Kappa = 1.000).

In the 5400 CBCT images analyzed during the specified period, the rate of impacted maxillary canine teeth was found to be 3.8% in the patient group aged 18 years and older and meeting the inclusion criteria.

Of the evaluated impacted maxillary canines 11.5% were located buccally 78.9% were located palatinally and 9.6% were located in a median position (B/P/M). The graph illustrates the distribution of impacted maxillary canines according to sagittal localization (B/P/M) by sex (Fig. 9).

Number of cases (n) and percentage (%) of B/P/M localization in females and in males

The rate of unilateral impacted canine teeth was found as 67.5%, while the rate of bilateral impacted canine teeth was found as 32.5%. The prevalence of unilateral impacted maxillary canines was 66.4% among female patients, whereas this rate was 69.2% among male patients.

Impacted maxillary canines were localized on the right side in 52.6% of cases and on the left side in 47.4% of cases. Right-sided localization was observed in 50.4% of female patients, whereas this proportion was 56.4% among male patients.

It was found that 89% of the maxillary canine teeth were impacted in mesioangular position, 8.1% were impacted in horizontal position, 2.4% were impacted in vertical position and 0.5% were impacted in distoangular position. While the rate of those with persistent deciduous canine teeth was 30.6%; the rate of those without persistent deciduous canine teeth was 69.4%. The rate of those with suspected ankylosis in maxillary impacted canine teeth was found as 2.4%.

The patients were classified as 18–25, 25–40, and 40–70 according to their age groups. No significant differences were observed among age groups regarding the localization of impacted canine teeth (buccal/palatal/median), their occurrence on the right or left side, unilateral or bilateral presentation, or vertical angulation patterns. Furthermore, the prevalence of intracoronal resorption, cystic lesions, and resorption affecting adjacent teeth did not differ significantly between age groups (p > 0.05). These findings suggest that age may not be a determining risk factor in the development of such pathologies. The rate of those without persistent deciduous canine teeth was found to be higher in the 40–70 years age group when compared with the other age groups (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Discussion

Maxillary canine teeth are critically important both for occlusion and aesthetics due to their position in the mouth. They are the teeth most likely to remain impacted because the distance they must take to reach their ideal position in occlusion is the longest when compared with the other teeth and also because they are the last teeth to erupt in the upper jaw frontal region. Although the factors causing maxillary canine teeth to remain impacted are not definite, research is still ongoing [22].

The treatment of maxillary impacted canin teeth requires a multidisciplinary approach and the process is quite difficult and long. CBCT, which is a 3D imaging method, is considered to be superior to other conventional 2D imaging methods for determining and classifying the localization, position of maxillary impacted canine teeth, their relationship with the adjacent anatomical structures and the pathologies they cause [13, 23]. When planning treatment, it is very important to determine the location and position of impacted teeth and the accompanying pathologies. With the increasing use of CBCT, it has become easier to predict complications that may occur as a result of surgical intervention and orthodontic treatment of maxillary impacted canine teeth.

In the present study, 209 maxillary impacted canine teeth of 175 patients aged 18 and older were examined with CBCT. The prevalence of being impacted was found to be 3.8%, in parallel with the literature. Grisar et al. [18] found the prevalence of maxillary impacted canine teeth as 2.7%. In the present study, 109 (62.7%) of the 175 patients with impacted canine teeth were females, while 66 (37.3%) were male. This ratio was reported as 2\1, 3\1 in the literature [5, 24]. In the present study, in parallel with the literature, the rate was higher in females than males. It can be thought that the reason for this difference may be the fact that female present more frequently to health institutions for aesthetic concerns or for control in addition to genetic factors. The higher representation of female patients in the study sample may be considered a potential factor contributing to the greater frequency of pathological findings associated with impacted maxillary canines observed in female patients.

Most studies reported that a large number of maxillary impacted canine teeth remain impacted on the palatal side. Ericson et al. [4] and Bishara et al. [3] reported the rate of being impacted on the palatal side as 85%. Subsequent studies results are close to these rates [25]. In the present study, the rate of palatal impaction was 78.9%, the rate of buccal impaction was 11.5% and the rate of impaction in the centre of the alveolar crest was 9.6%, showing results similar to those in the literature.

Maxillary impacted canine teeth are generally seen unilaterally [6]. In the present study, in parallel with the literature, the rate of being impacted unilaterally was found as 67.5%. Cernochova et al. [26] reported the rate of unilateral impaction as 80.3%.

In the present study, the inclination of maxillary impacted canine teeth relative to the long axis was mostly mesioangular (89%). The rate was found to be 6.2% in the horizontal position, 0.5% in the distoangular position and 2.4% in the vertical position. Cernochova et al. [26] reported vertical inclination as 10%, horizontal inclination as 6.2% and mesioangular inclination as 83.3%. Horizontal impaction of impacted canine teeth affects the chance of orthodontic treatment. Liu et al. [27] reported orthodontic treatment to be more difficult in horizontally impacted canine teeth.

In patients with maxillary impacted canine teeth, the presence of persistent deciduous canine teeth may be a factor preventing early diagnosis. While the presence of persistent deciduous canine teeth between the ages of 18 and 25 was 34%, this rate was found to decrease to 10% between the ages of 40 and 70. Becker and Chaushu [28] reported that the prognosis for achieving ideal alignment of impacted canine teeth in the maxillary arch with orthodontic treatment worsens with age. In the present study, the presence of persistent deciduous canine teeth was found to be 30%, in parallel with the literature. Santos et al. [1] found this rate to be 34% in their study.

In studies related to maxillary impacted canine teeth, root resorption caused by canine teeth in adjacent teeth was evaluated. Since impacted canine teeth are largely located on the palatal side of the lateral incisors, it has been reported that they most commonly affect the roots of lateral incisors. The prevalence of root resorption in maxillary lateral incisors has been reported to be between 8.2 and 89.6% in the literature [29–31]. The results of the present study showed that the rate of root resorption was 7.2% in lateral incisors, 1.9% in central incisors and 1% in premolars. In terms of age groups, root resorption was found to be higher in lateral incisors within the age range of 25–40, while root resorption was found to be higher in central incisors within the age range of 18–25.

In studies conducted in the literature, the prevalence of root resorption was reported as 14.8% by Grisar et al. [18] in lateral incisors, as 17.6% by Simic et al. [32] in central incisors and as 34.3% in lateral incisors. The mean age being below 20 years in both studies constitutes a notable difference in sample characteristics. In contrast, the mean age in our study was 35 years. The decreased eruptive or movement potential of impacted maxillary canines with advancing age may have contributed to the lower frequency of root resorption observed in adjacent teeth. Additionally, technical variations in CBCT imaging protocols, particularly differences in resolution, image quality, and slice thickness, may have influenced the discrepancies observed between the findings of these studies. Thus, the results of these studies conducted on different populations and different age groups were found to differ.

One of the pathologies that may occur with the impaction of teeth in the maxilla is intracoronal resorption in canine teeth. This unusual condition has been referred to as pre-caries, latent caries, and penetrating crown resorption of the tooth. This condition may be confused with caries regardless of histopathology [28]. The prevalence of this pathology has been reported to be between 0.2 and 27.3% in studies conducted in different countries [33]. Intracoronal resorption affects the crown of the unerupted tooth and the entry point may be a disruption of the integrity above the enamel.

In the present study, the rate of intracoronal resorption in maxillary impacted canine teeth was found as 8.1%. It was found to occur in the age group between the ages of 40 and 75 in terms of age groups. Intracoronal resorption was not found in any maxillary impacted canine tooth between the ages of 18 and 25. This finding emphasizes the importance of early diagnosis of impacted maxillary canines in minimizing potential complications and enabling timely treatment planning. This result is in parallel with the literature. In a comprehensive study they conducted on 351 patients with 420 maxillary impacted canine teeth, Cernochova et al. [26] reported intracoronal resorption as 0.7%. The reason for this difference may be the fact that the mean age was 19 in the study by Cernochova. [26]

The other pathology that may occur in maxillary impacted teeth is dental ankylosis. Dental ankylosis may be defined as the anatomical fusion of the alveolar bone with the cementum layer of the tooth root. Genetic or congenital abnormalities in the periodontal membrane may explain the ankylptic changes in the unerupted teeth. Studies have reported that the likelihood of anklotic changes increases with advancing age. [26] This finding emphasizes the importance of early diagnosis and regular radiological monitoring in preventing pathological changes that may occur with advancing age. In the study conducted by Cernochova et al. [26], the probability of ankylosis was also reported to increase with advancing age. Cernochova et al. [26] reported the rate of ankylosis as 9.5%. Grisar et al. [18] reported the presence of ankylosis as 6.8% of 162 maxillary impacted canine teeth. In the present study, 5 of the 209 maxillary impacted canine teeth were evaluated as suspected ankylosis. Total rate is 2.4%. In terms of age ranges, it was found that 4 of these 5 patients were between the ages of 40 and 70. It can be seen that surgical procedures are needed to confirm ankylosis diagnosis.

The literature suggests that, in cases of ankylosis or resorption associated with impacted maxillary canines, surgical extraction or periodic monitoring of the impacted tooth may be considered a more appropriate management strategy than tooth preservation or orthodontic traction [26].

Maxillary impacted canine teeth may be associated with dentigerous cyst. The tooth should be approached with a suspicion of cyst when the dental follicle exceeds 5 mm [13]. In the present study, the rate of dentigerous cyst development was found as 4.3% in maxillary impacted canine teeth. No statistically significant difference was found between genders, although it was found to be more common in males. In terms of age groups, most dentigerous cysts were observed between the ages of 40 and 70. Mendez et al. [34] reported the rate of dentigerous cyst as 7.5% in 93 maxillary impacted canine teeth they examined with CBCT.

Odontoma is the most common odontogenic tumours. An unerupted tooth may be associated with odontoma and may prevent the tooth from erupting [35]. In the present study, odontoma was found in 1.4% of the 209 maxillary impacted canine teeth. Santos et al. [29] reported the rate of odontoma as 1.3% in the study they examined 79 impacted canine teetth.

The treatment of maxillary impacted canine teeth requires a difficult and comprehensive approach. The pathologies that develop make this process more difficult. Grisar et al. [18] reported that pathologies in impacted canine teeth increases the rate of failure as a result of conventional orthodontic or surgical treatment to be applied to these teeth. Widespread use and development of CBCT made it possible to diagnose these pathologies more and more frequently every day.

In the present study, the relationship of maxillary impacted canine teeth with the adjacent anatomical structures was also evaluated. Due to their location, they are adjacent to nasal cavity, maxillary sinus and NPC. Knowing about this relationship is important in determining the duration and difficulty of orthodontic tooth movement and in anticipating complications in surgical operations. To avoid nerve damage in surgical operations, to prevent oroantral fistula formation and to avoid root resorption during orthodontic treatment, the relationship between the maxillary impacted canine teeth and the surrounding anatomical structures should be examined carefully. In 15.4% of 78 male patients with maxillary impacted canine teeth, the tooth was found to be associated with the nasal floor. This rate was found as 2.3% in females. The higher prevalence of the relationship between impacted maxillary canines and the nasal floor observed in male patients in our study may be associated with sex-related craniofacial anatomical variations and morphological differences in maxillary structures. However, as these anatomical parameters were not directly assessed in the present study, further imaging-based studies are warranted to elucidate this finding.

The relationships between the nasal cavity floor, NPC and maxillary sinus were found as 7.2%, 14.8%, 6.2%, respectively in all maxillary impacted canine teeth. In their study examining the relationship between maxillary impacted canine teeth and the NPC and maxillary sinus, Akgün and Yurttas [20] reported that 43.8% were at the border of the maxillary sinus, 3.8% were within it, and 42.8% were related to the NPC. Grisar et al. [18] reported the relationship of maxillary impacted canine teeth with the nasal floor as 34.5% and with the maxillary sinus as 27.6%.

Limitation

In this retrospective study, findings were obtained by evaluating only the CBCT images of maxillary impacted canine teeth. Nevertheless, the single-center nature of the study sample may be regarded as an important limitation affecting the generalizability of the findings. Future longitudinal studies including more balanced distributions of female and male participants, as well as clinical evaluations supported by surgical outcomes, may provide a more comprehensive understanding and interpretation of these findings. The clinical examination findings of the patients have not been reviewed. Additionally, lesions with a follicular interval of 5 mm or greater are defined as suspected dentigerous cysts, although histopathological results are not available and surgical extractions were not evaluated.

Conclusion

The findings of the present study indicate that impacted maxillary canines may persist asymptomatically for many years without causing severe pathology; however, pathological changes may still occur over time. Therefore, early diagnosis and regular CBCT monitoring are crucial for successful clinical management, particularly considering the increased risk of ankylosis and intracoronal resorption associated with aging. CBCT offers significant advantages in the diagnosis and evaluation of impacted maxillary canines, enabling more accurate risk assessment and treatment planning. Future longitudinal studies combining CBCT findings with clinical and surgical outcomes are warranted to better elucidate the long-term progression and management of impacted maxillary canines.
