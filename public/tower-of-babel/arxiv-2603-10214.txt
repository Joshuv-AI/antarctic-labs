A Uniqueness Condition for Conservation Laws with Discontinuous Gradient-Dependent Flux
Alberto Bressan, Wen Shen

Abstract
The paper is concerned with a scalar conservation law with discontinuous gradient-dependent flux. Namely, the flux is described by two different functions f ⁡ ( u ) f(u) or g ⁡ ( u ) g(u) , when the gradient u x u_{x} of the solution is positive or negative, respectively. In the stable case where f ⁡ ( u ) < g ⁡ ( u ) f(u)<g(u) for all u ∈ ℝ u\in{\mathbb{R}} , it was proved in [ 1 ] that the limits of vanishing viscosity approximations form a contractive semigroup w.r.t. the 𝐋 1 \mathbf{L}^{1} distance. Further, they coincide with the limits of a suitable family of front tracking approximations. In the present paper we introduce a simple condition that guarantees that every weak, entropy admissible solution of a Cauchy problem coincides with the corresponding semigroup trajectory, and hence is unique.

Introduction

Following the model introduced in
[
1
]
, we consider a conservation law with discontinuous flux,
where the discontinuity is related to the sign of the gradient
u_{x}
, namely

u_{t}+\Big[\theta(u_{x})f(u)+\bigl(1-\theta(u_{x})\bigr)g(u)\Big]_{x}~=~0.

(1.1)

Here
f,g
are two different fluxes, and
\theta
is a function such that

\theta(s)~=~\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1&\hbox{if}\quad s>0,\cr 0&\hbox{if}\quad s<0.\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

As in
[
1
]
we focus on the stable case
f<g
and assume

(A1)

The flux functions
f,g
are twice continuously differentiable and satisfy

g(u)-f(u)~\geq~c_{0}~>~0\qquad\hbox{for all}~u\in{\mathbb{R}}\,.

(1.3)

To introduce an appropriate definition of weak solution, we observe that for every BV function

u:{\mathbb{R}}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
, the distributional derivative
\mu=D_{x}u
is a signed Radon measure.
It can be decomposed into a positive and a negative part as
\mu=\mu_{+}-\mu_{-}
.
Given a measurable function
\theta:{\mathbb{R}}\mapsto[0,1]
, the requirement that
\theta=1
at points where

\mu
is positive, while
\theta=0
at points where
\mu
is negative can now be stated
in terms of the measure identity

\mu~=~\chi_{\{\theta=1\}}\mu_{+}-\chi_{\{\theta=0\}}\mu_{-}\,.

(1.4)

Here
\chi_{S}
denotes the characteristic function of a set
S
.
Following
[
2
]
, we shall apply the identity (
1.4
) to the measure
D_{x}u(t,\cdot)
, at a.e. time
t
.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that a BV function
u:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
is a
weak solution

to (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) with initial data

u(0,x)\,=\,\bar{u}(x)

(1.5)

if the following holds.

(i)

The map
t\mapsto u(t,\cdot)
is continuous from
[0,T]
into
\mathbf{L}^{1}_{loc}
and satisfies
the initial condition (
1.5
).

(ii)

There exists a measurable function

\theta:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}\mapsto[0,1]
such that

D_{x}u(t,\cdot)~=~\chi_{\{\theta=1\}}\Big[D_{x}u(t,\cdot)\Big]_{+}-\chi_{\{\theta=0\}}\Big[D_{x}u(t,\cdot)\Big]_{-}\qquad\hbox{for a.e.}~t\in[0,T],

(1.6)

and such that, for every compactly supported test function
\varphi\in{\cal C}^{1}_{c}\bigl(]0,T[\,\times{\mathbb{R}}\bigr)
,

\int_{0}^{T}\int\left\{u\varphi_{t}+\Big[\theta\,f(u)+\bigl(1-\theta\,\bigr)g(u)\Big]\,\varphi_{x}\right\}\,dx\,dt~=~0.

(1.7)

Since the integral in (
1.7
) does not depend on the values of
\theta
on a set of measure zero,
instead of (
1.6
) one could simply require that

\theta(t,x)~=~\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1\quad\hbox{if}~~u_{x}(t,x)>0,\\[2.84526pt]
0\quad\hbox{if}~~u_{x}(t,x)<0.\end{array}\right.

On the other hand, the condition (
1.6
) specifies the value of
\theta(t,\cdot)

not only at points where the gradient
u_{x}
exists, but also at points where
u(t,\cdot)
has a jump,
and on the Cantor part
of the distributional derivative
D_{x}u(t,\cdot)
.
This additional information will play a central role in the uniqueness
result, which is the main contribution of the present paper.

Throughout the following, we consider weak solutions having the additional key property

(A2)

For every
t>0
, the function
x\mapsto\theta(t,x)
is continuous.

To write the corresponding Rankine-Hugoniot and the Liu admissibility conditions, we recall that a point

(\bar{t},\bar{x})
is called a point of
approximate jump
for the function
u=u(t,x)
if there
exist values
u^{-},u^{+}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and a speed
\lambda
such that the following holds. Setting

U(t,x)~\doteq~\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}u^{-}\quad\hbox{if}~~x<\lambda t,\\
u^{+}\quad\hbox{if}~~x>\lambda t,\end{array}\right.

(1.8)

one has

\lim_{r\to 0+}~\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{\bar{t}-r}^{\bar{t}+r}\int_{\bar{x}-r}^{\bar{x}+r}\bigl|u(t,x)-U(t-\bar{t},x-\bar{x})\bigr|\,dx\,dt~=~0.

(1.9)

If (
1.8
)-(
1.9
) hold with
u^{-}=u^{+}
(and hence
\lambda
arbitrary), we say that
u
is
approximately
continuous
at
\bar{t},\bar{x})
,

In the above setting, if
u,\theta
provide a weak solution to (
1.1
)-(
1.2
), a standard argument
[
5
]

yields that, at every point of approximate jump, the left and the right values
u^{\pm}
and the speed
\lambda
in
(
1.8
) must satisfy the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions

\lambda~=~\frac{\bigl[\theta f(u^{+})+(1-\theta)g(u^{+})\bigr]-\bigl[\theta f(u^{-})+(1-\theta)g(u^{-})\bigr]}{u^{+}-u^{-}}\,,

(1.10)

where

\theta~=~\theta(\bar{t},\bar{x})~=~\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1&\hbox{if}~~u^{+}>u^{-},\\[2.84526pt]
0&\hbox{if}~~u^{+}<u^{-}.\end{array}\right.

In addition, we say that the jump satisfies the
Liu admissibility conditions

[
13
]
if the following implication hold:

\begin{array}[]{rl}u^{-}<u^{+}&\Longrightarrow\quad\displaystyle\frac{f(u^{*})-f(u^{-})}{u^{*}-u^{-}}~\geq~\frac{f(u^{+})-f(u^{-})}{u^{+}-u^{-}}\qquad\hbox{for all}~u^{*}\in\,]u^{-},u^{+}],\\[11.38109pt]
u^{+}<u^{-}&\Longrightarrow\quad\displaystyle\frac{g(u^{*})-g(u^{-})}{u^{*}-u^{-}}~\geq~\frac{g(u^{+})-g(u^{-})}{u^{+}-u^{-}}\qquad\hbox{for all}~u^{*}\in\,]u^{+},u^{-}].\end{array}

(1.11)

More generally, we say that a weak solution
u=u(t,x)
is
Liu-admissible
if it satisfies the Liu conditions at every point of approximate jump.
In the recent paper
[
1
]
, given an initial datum (
1.5
), a
solution of (
1.1
)-(
1.2
)
was constructed as the unique limit of a family of front tracking approximations.
These limit solutions form a semigroup which is contractive w.r.t. the
\mathbf{L}^{1}
distance.
Furthermore, they also coincide with the unique limits of
parabolic approximations, as
\varepsilon,\delta\to 0
,

u_{t}+\Big[\theta_{\varepsilon}(u_{x})f(u)+\bigl(1-\theta_{\varepsilon}(u_{x})\bigr)g(u)\Big]_{x}~=~\delta u_{xx}\,.

(1.12)

Here
\theta_{\varepsilon}(s)~\doteq~\theta(s/\varepsilon)
, while
\theta:{\mathbb{R}}\mapsto[0,1]
is a smooth, nondecreasing function satisfying

\theta(s)~=~\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1&\hbox{if}\quad s\geq 1,\cr 0&\hbox{if}\quad s\leq-1.\end{array}\right.

(1.13)

However, the uniqueness of solutions remained an open problem in
[
1
]
. The heart of the matter is to
identify a set of conditions that is satisfied by the semigroup trajectory and by no other solution.
Uniqueness results, as in
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, become relevant when solutions are constructed as limits of different types of approximations (relaxation, difference schemes, etc…). In this case, a uniqueness theorem will guarantee that these limit solutions coincide with the unique semigroup trajectory.

As shown by the following counterexample (see Example 1.1 in
[
1
]
),
the Cauchy problem (
1.1
), (
1.5
) can have

.

As flux functions, take

and consider the initial data

u(0,x)\,=\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}e^{x}&\hbox{if}\quad x<0,\\
-e^{-x}&\hbox{if}\quad x>0.\end{array}\right.

(1.14)

As shown in

, let
u=u(t,x)
be the unique entropy weak solution to Burgers’ equation

Setting

\theta(t,x)~=~\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1\qquad\hbox{for}~~x\not=0,\\

\int\!\!\int\Big\{u\phi_{t}+\bigl[\theta f(u)+(1-\theta)g(u)\bigr]\phi_{x}\Big\}\,dx\,dt~=~0.

In addition, the downward jump in

.

However,

This can be checked by observing that, for every

On the other hand, as proved in

These two properties are clearly incompatible.

2
A uniqueness theorem

To simplify some of the analysis,
as in
[
1
]
, we consider the space
\mathbf{L}^{1}_{per}({\mathbb{R}})
of locally integrable, periodic functions, so that
w(x)=w(x+1)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The
\mathbf{L}^{1}
norm and the total variation are thus
computed over the domain

[0,1]

with endpoints identified.
By the construction of solutions to (
1.1
) as limits of front tracking approximations
[
1
]
, it is established that semigroup solutions
u(t,\cdot)=S_{t}\bar{u}
have the following properties.

(i)

The map
t\mapsto u(t,\cdot)=S_{t}{\bar{u}}\in\mathbf{L}^{1}_{per}
is uniformly Lipschitz continuous on every
interval
[t_{0},T]
with
t_{0}>0
.

(ii)

Every semigroup trajectory is a weak solution according to Definition
1.1
, and satisfies
the Liu admissibility
conditions (
1.11
) at every point of approximate jump.

(iii)

For each fixed
t>0
, the solution
u(t,\cdot)
has bounded variation.
It attains its maxima and minima on a finite
number of nontrivial disjoint intervals (over one spatial period).
The number of these distinct intervals is non-increasing in time.
The total variation
\hbox{TV}\bigl\{u(t,\cdot)\bigr\}

is strictly decreasing in time, until the solution becomes constant.

(iv)

For each
t>0
the corresponding function
\theta(t,\cdot)
is piecewise affine. It increases from 0 to 1
on intervals where
u(t,\cdot)
attains a local minimum, and decreases from 1 to 0 on intervals where

u(t,\cdot)
attains a local maximum.

Figure 3:
An example where, for the semigroup solution
u(t)=S_{t}\bar{u}
, the corresponding function
\theta(t,x)
is not continuous w.r.t. time. For every time
t>0
the function
\theta(t,\cdot)
is piecewise affine.
If
\tau
is the time when the two horizontal portions of the graph of
u(t,\cdot)
join,
the function
\theta(\tau,\cdot)
does not coincide with the limit of
\theta(t,\cdot)
as
t\to\tau-
.

It is worth noting that, even for semigroup trajectories, the map
\theta(t,x)
can be discontinuous w.r.t. time.
An example where this happens is when
u(t,\cdot)
attains a local maximum and a local minimum on two adjacent
intervals. In finite time these intervals
merge together, as shown in Fig.
3
.

The following uniqueness theorem provides a characterization of the semigroup solutions of (
1.1
)-(
1.2
).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f,g
satisfy the assumptions
(A1)
. Given an initial data
\bar{u}\in\mathbf{L}^{1}_{per}
, let
u=u(t,x)
be a
Liu admissible weak solution of
the Cauchy problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) with initial data (
1.5
). Assume that

(i)

Given any
t_{0}>0
, for
t\in[t_{0},T]
the function
u(t,\cdot)

and the corresponding function
\theta(t,\cdot)
have uniformly
bounded variation.

(ii)

For every
t>0
the function
\theta(t,\cdot)
is continuous.

Then one has

u(t,\cdot)\,=\,S_{t}{\bar{u}}\qquad\hbox{for all}~t\in[0,T].

(2.1)

In other words, the solution
with the above properties is unique and coincides with the corresponding semigroup trajectory.

3
Proof of Theorem
2.1

In this section we prove Theorem
2.1
in several steps.

1.
For any
t_{0}>0
,
the assumptions

\hbox{TV}\bigl\{u(t,\cdot)\bigr\}\,\leq\,C_{0}\qquad\hbox{TV}\bigl\{\theta(t,\cdot)\bigr\}\,\leq\,C_{0}\qquad\hbox{for all}~t\in[t_{0},T]

(3.1)

yield a bound on the total variation of the flux function

x~\mapsto~F(t,x)~\doteq~\theta(t,x)f\bigl(u(t,x)\bigr)+\bigl(1-\theta(t,x)\bigr)g\bigl(u(t,x)\bigr)

(3.2)

in terms of the constant
C_{0}
and of the Lipschitz constants of
f
and
g
.
As a consequence (see Theorem 4.3.1 in
[
10
]
for details),
restricted to
the time interval
[t_{0},T]
,
the map

t\mapsto u(t,\cdot)
is Lipschitz continuous with values in
\mathbf{L}^{1}_{per}
.
For some constant
L_{0}
we thus have

\bigl\|u(t_{1},\cdot)-u(t_{2},\cdot)\bigr\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}_{per}}~\leq~L_{0}\,|t_{1}-t_{2}|\qquad\qquad\hbox{for all}~t_{1},t_{2}\in[t_{0},T].

(3.3)

Since
u=u(t,x)
is a BV function of two variables on the domain
[t_{0},T]\times[0,1]
,
by a well known structure theorem
[
3
,
11
]

there exists a set of times
{\cal N}
of measure zero such that the following holds.
For every
t\in[t_{0},T]\setminus{\cal N}
and every
x\in[0,1]
, the point
(t,x)
is either a point of approximate continuity, or a point of approximate jump for
u
.

2.
The proof will be achieved by showing that, for any
t_{0}>0
,

u(t)~=~S_{t-t_{0}}u(t_{0}),\qquad\qquad\hbox{for all}~t\in[t_{0},T]\,.

(3.4)

By the contractivity of the semigroup, this will imply

\bigl\|u(t,\cdot)-S_{t}\bar{u}\bigr\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}_{per}}~=~\bigl\|S_{t-t_{0}}u(t_{0})-S_{t}\bar{u}\bigr\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}_{per}}\\
~\leq~\bigl\|u(t_{0})-S_{t_{0}}\bar{u}\bigr\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}_{per}}\,.

(3.5)

By the continuity of the maps
t\mapsto u(t,\cdot)
and
t\mapsto S_{t}\bar{u}
, the right hand side goes to zero as

t_{0}\to 0
. This will yield (
2.1
), proving uniqueness.

In the remaining steps we will prove the identity (
3.4
). Thanks to the Lipschitz continuity of the map
t\mapsto u(t,\cdot)\in\mathbf{L}^{1}_{per}
and the contractivity of the semigroup, the following
elementary error estimate holds (see
[
5
]
)

\bigl\|u(t)-S_{t-t_{0}}u(t_{0})\bigr\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}_{per}}~\leq~\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\liminf_{h\to 0+}~\frac{1}{h}\Big\|u(\tau+h)-S_{h}u(\tau)\Big\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}_{per}}\,d\tau\,.

(3.6)

The compact set
K\subseteq[0,T]

and
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, considered at (
3.7
).

3.
By assumption, the function
\theta=\theta(t,x)
is measurable in
t
and continuous in
x
.
By a theorem of Scorza-Dragoni
[
15
]
, for any
\varepsilon_{0}>0
there exists a compact set
K\subseteq[0,T]

with
\hbox{meas}(K)>T-\varepsilon_{0}
such that
\theta
is continuous (w.r.t. both variables
t,x
) restricted to the product space
K\times[0,1]
.
Within the set
K\times[0,1]
we distinguish

(i)

a compact region where
\theta=0
,

(ii)

a compact region where
\theta=1
,

(iii)

a relatively open region where
0<\theta<1
.

As shown in Fig.
4
,
we can find an open set
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that

\Big\{(t,x)\in K\times[0,1]\,;~~0<\theta(t,x)<1\Big\}~=~\bigl(K\times[0,1]\bigr)\cap\Omega.

(3.7)

By the continuity of
\theta
, for every
t\in K
the set where
0<\theta(t,\cdot)<1
is a countable union of open intervals

\,\bigl]a_{i}(t),b_{i}(t)\bigr[\,
,
i\geq 1
.

4.
For any rational point
y_{j}\in[0,1]
, call

\,\bigl]a_{j}(t),b_{j}(t)\bigr[\,
the connected component of the set

\bigl\{x\in\,]0,1[\,;~~(t,x)\in\Omega\bigr\}

which contains
y_{j}
. Since
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is open, this interval is well defined for all
t

such that
(t,y_{j})\in\Omega
.

The condition (
1.6
) implies that the function

u(t,\cdot)
is constant on each of these open intervals, say

u(t,x)~=~u_{j}(t)\qquad\hbox{for all}~x\in\,\bigl]a_{j}(t),b_{j}(t)\bigr[\,,\quad t\in K.

(3.8)

We claim that the map
t\mapsto u_{j}(t)
is locally Lipschitz continuous, restricted to
t\in K
.

Indeed,
for any
(\tau,y_{j})\in\Omega
, there exists
\delta,\rho>0
such that

b_{j}(t)-a_{j}(t)\geq\rho\qquad\qquad\hbox{whenever}~~~|t-\tau|\leq\delta.

Recalling (
3.3
)
we obtain

\rho\cdot\bigl|u_{j}(t_{1})-u_{j}(t_{2})\bigr|~\leq~\bigl\|u(t_{1},\cdot)-u(t_{2},\cdot)\bigr\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}_{per}}~\leq~L_{0}\cdot|t_{1}-t_{2}|\,.

This proves Lipschitz continuity, with constant
L_{0}/\rho
.

Observing that the complement
[0,T]\setminus K
is a countable union of open intervals
\,]t_{k-1},t_{k}[\,
,
k\geq 1
,
we can extend the function
u_{j}(\cdot)
in an affine way on each of these intervals.
This yields a function
t\mapsto u_{j}(t)
defined on the entire interval
[\tau-\delta,\tau+\delta]
,
which is still Lipschitz continuous with the same Lipschitz constant

L_{0}/\rho
.

As a consequence, by Rademacher’s theorem
the time derivative
\dot{u}_{j}(t)
exists at a.e. time
t
such that
(t,y_{j})\in\Omega
.
In particular, the set of times

{\cal N}^{\prime}~\doteq~\Big\{t\in K\,;~~(t,y_{j})\in\Omega~~\hbox{for some}~j\geq 1,~~~u_{j}(\cdot)~\hbox{is not differentiable at}~t\Big\}

(3.9)

has Lebesgue measure zero.

5.
Let
\tau
be a Lebesgue point of the set
K
, so that

\tau\in K,\qquad\lim_{h\to 0+}\frac{1}{h}\hbox{meas}\Big([\tau-h,\tau+h]\setminus K\Big)~=~0.

(3.10)

Assume
\tau\notin{\cal N}^{\prime}
.
If
y_{j}
is any rational point such that
(\tau,y_{j})\in\Omega
, then the open intervals

\,\bigl]a_{j}(t),b_{j}(t)\bigr[\,
are well defined for all
t
in a neighborhood of
\tau
.
Let the function
u_{j}(t)
be as in (
3.8
). By assumption, the derivative
\dot{u}_{j}(\tau)
exists.

We claim that, on the interval
\bigl]a_{j}(\tau),b_{j}(\tau)\bigr[\,
,
one of the following alternatives holds:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\dot{u}_{j}(\tau)=\displaystyle\frac{f(u_{j}(\tau))-g(u_{j}(\tau))}{b_{j}(\tau)-a_{j}(\tau)}\,<\,0&\hbox{if}\quad\theta(\tau,a_{j}(\tau))=1,~\theta(\tau,b_{j}(\tau))=0,\\[11.38109pt]
\dot{u}_{j}(\tau)=\displaystyle\frac{g(u_{j}(\tau))-f(u_{j}(\tau))}{b_{j}(\tau)-a_{j}(\tau)}\,>\,0&\hbox{if}\quad\theta(\tau,a_{j}(\tau))=0,~\theta(\tau,b_{j}(\tau))=1.\end{array}

(3.11)

Indeed, fix
x_{1},x_{2}\in\bigl]a_{j}(\tau),b_{j}(\tau)\bigr[\,
with
x_{1}<x_{2}
,
and let
h>0
be small enough.
Applying the conservation law over the rectangle
[x_{1},x_{2}]\times[\tau,\tau+h]
,
by the divergence theorem we obtain

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\bigl[u_{j}(\tau+h)-u_{j}(\tau)\bigr]\cdot(x_{2}-x_{1})

\displaystyle=~\int_{\tau}^{\tau+h}\Big[\theta(t,x_{1})f(u_{j}(t))+(1-\theta(t,x_{1}))g(u_{j}(t))\Big]dt

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\tau}^{\tau+h}\Big[\theta(t,x_{2})f(u_{j}(t))+(1-\theta(t,x_{2}))g(u_{j}(t))\Big]dt

\displaystyle=~\int_{\tau}^{\tau+h}\bigl[\theta(t,x_{1})-\theta(t,x_{2})\bigr]\cdot\bigl[f(u_{j}(t))-g(u_{j}(t))\bigr]dt.

Taking the limit as
h\to 0
, using the fact that
\theta
is continuous restricted to
K\times[x_{1},x_{2}]
and

\tau
is a Lebesgue point of
K
, we obtain

\dot{u}_{j}(\tau)=\frac{\theta(\tau,x_{1})-\theta(\tau,x_{2})}{x_{2}-x_{1}}\cdot\bigl[f(u_{j}(\tau))-g(u_{j}(\tau))\bigr].

Since the left hand side does not depend on the space variable
x
,
the quotient

\frac{\theta(\tau,x_{1})-\theta(\tau,x_{2})}{x_{2}-x_{1}}

must be a constant, independent of the choice of
x_{1},x_{2}
.
We conclude that
\theta
must be an affine function, taking values in the set

Next, consider any time

After these preliminaries, the heart of the proof consists in showing that

\liminf_{t\to\tau+}~\frac{1}{t-\tau}\Big\|u(t)-S_{t-\tau}u(\tau)\Big\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}_{per}}~=~0\qquad\quad\hbox{for a.e.}~~\tau\in K.

(3.14)

If (

and the semigroup trajectory

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\bigl\|u(t)-S_{t-t_{0}}u(t_{0})\bigr\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}_{per}}~\leq~\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\liminf_{s\to\tau+}~\frac{1}{s-\tau+}\Big\|u(s)-S_{s-\tau}u(\tau)\Big\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}_{per}}\,d\tau\\[11.38109pt]

Since

In the remaining steps of the proof we will establish (

The general strategy for proving (

Given
\varepsilon>0
, for all
\tau\in K

To establish (

\tau

•

are continuous functions with

U_{i}(t,x)~\doteq~\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}u_{i}(\tau)+(t-\tau)\dot{u}_{i}(\tau)&\hbox{if}\quad x<b_{i}(\tau)+(t-\tau)\lambda_{i}\,,\\[5.69054pt]

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\lim_{t\to\tau+,\,t\in K_{\alpha}}~\frac{1}{t-\tau}\int_{p_{k}}^{q_{k}}\Big|u(t,x)-U_{i}(t,x)\Big|\,dx

\displaystyle=

In conclusion, recalling (
3.28
)–(
3.30
), for any
\varepsilon>0
we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\int_{I_{j}}\bigl|u(t,x)-U_{j}^{\flat}(t,x)\bigr|\,dx+\sum_{j}\int_{y_{j}-\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}^{y_{j}+\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}\Big|u(t,x)-{V_{j}}(t,x)\Big|\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad\displaystyle=~{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon(t-\tau)+o(t-\tau)

(3.45)

Here the last term
o(t-\tau)
denotes a quantity such that

\lim_{t\to\tau+}\frac{o(t-\tau)}{t-\tau}~=~0.

The same bounds holds replacing
u
with the semigroup solution
U(t)=S_{t-\tau}u(\tau)
.
We this conclude

\liminf_{t\to\tau+}\frac{1}{t-\tau}\int_{p_{k}+\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}^{q_{k}-\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}\bigl|u(t,x)-U(t,x)\bigr|\,dx~=~0.

(3.46)

Indeed, this limit is
\leq C\varepsilon

for some constant
C
and any
\varepsilon>0
.

11.
Next, we analyze the behavior of the solution
u
on an interval
[p_{k},q_{k}]
of type (T3).
To fix ideas,
assume that
u(\tau,\cdot)
is constant for
x<b_{i}(\tau)
and decreasing for
x>b_{i}(\tau)
,
with a downward jump at
x=b_{i}(\tau)
.
By assumption,
\bigl(\tau,b_{i}(\tau)\bigr)
is a point of approximate jump for
u
, with left and right states

u^{-}=u_{i}(\tau)
,
u^{+}=u\bigl(\tau,b_{i}(\tau)+\bigr)
. Therefore, setting

\lambda\,=\,\frac{g(u^{+})-g(u^{-})}{u^{+}-u^{-}}

the function

U_{i}^{\sharp}(t,x)~\doteq~\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}u^{-}&\hbox{if}\quad x<b_{i}(\tau)+\lambda(t-\tau),\\[2.84526pt]
u^{+}&\hbox{if}\quad x>b_{i}(\tau)+\lambda(t-\tau),\end{array}\right.

(3.47)

satisfies

\lim_{t\to\tau+}~\frac{1}{t-\tau}\int_{b_{i}(\tau)-\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}^{b_{i}(\tau)+\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}\Big|u(t,x)-U_{i}^{\sharp}(t,x)\Big|\,dx~=~0.

(3.48)

Notice that this implies, in particular, that

b_{i}(t)~<~b_{i}(\tau)+\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)

for all
t\in K
with
t-\tau
small enough.
On the domain

D^{(t)}_{i}~=~\Big\{(s,x);~s\in[\tau,t]\cap K,~~b_{i}(\tau)+\lambda^{*}(s-\tau)<x<q_{k}-\lambda^{*}(s-\tau)\Big\}

choosing
\lambda^{*}>L_{0}/\varepsilon
we obtain

\bigl|u(t,x)-u^{+}\bigr|~=~\bigl|u(t,x)-u(\tau,b_{i}(\tau)+)\bigr|~\leq~2\varepsilon.

Setting
\lambda_{i}\doteq g^{\prime}(u^{+})
and letting

U_{i}^{\flat}(t,x)~\doteq~u\bigl(\tau,x-\lambda_{i}(t-\tau)\bigr)
be the solution to

v_{t}+g(v)_{x}=0,\qquad v(\tau,x)=u(\tau,x),

the same analysis as in step
10C
yields the bound (see Figure
8
)

\lim_{t\to\tau+}~\frac{1}{t-\tau}\int_{b_{i}(\tau)+\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}^{q_{k}-\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}\Big|u(t,x)-U_{i}^{\flat}(t,x)\Big|\,dx~=~{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon.

(3.49)

Finally, over the interval
[p_{k},b_{i}(\tau)-\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)]

the function
u(t,\cdot)=u_{i}(t)
is constant. Moreover,

\dot{u}_{i}(\tau)=\frac{f(u_{i}(\tau))-g(u_{i}(\tau))}{b_{i}(\tau)-a_{i}(\tau)}\,.

(3.50)

Therefore

\lim_{t\to\tau+}\frac{1}{t-\tau}\int_{p_{k}}^{b_{i}(\tau)-\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}\Big|u(t,x)-u_{i}(\tau)-(t-\tau)\dot{u}_{i}(\tau)\Big|\,dx~=~0.

(3.51)

Since all three limits
(
3.48
), (
3.49
) and (
3.51
)
remain valid by replacing the solution
u
with the semigroup
solution
U(t,\cdot)=S_{t-\tau}u(\tau)
, we conclude that

\lim_{t\to\tau+}\frac{1}{t-\tau}\int_{p_{k}}^{q_{k}-\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}\Big|u(t,x)-U(t,x)\Big|\,dx~=~{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon.

(3.52)

where
\varepsilon
can be chosen arbitrarily small.

Constructing an approximate solution in case (T3).

12.
Finally, we analyze the behavior of the solution
u
on an interval
[p_{k},q_{k}]
of type (T4). To fix ideas,
we again assume that
u(\tau,\cdot)
is constant for
x<b_{i}(\tau)
and decreasing for
x>b_{i}(\tau)
.
Given
\varepsilon>0
, we seek a leading order approximation to
u(t,\cdot)
on the interval
\bigl[p_{k}+\lambda^{*}(t-\tau),\,q_{k}-\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)\bigr]
, where
\lambda^{*}=L_{0}/\varepsilon
.

For
t\in K
, setting
\omega_{i}=\dot{u}_{i}(\tau)
as in (
3.50
), we have

u(t,x)~=~u_{i}(t)~=~u_{i}(\tau)+(t-\tau)\omega_{i}+o(t-\tau)\qquad\qquad\hbox{for}~~x\leq b_{i}(t).

(3.53)

Moreover,
u(t,x)\leq u_{i}(t)
for
x\in[p_{k},q_{k}]
. As in (
3.34
) by the Lipschitz continuity of the map
t\mapsto u(t,\cdot)
and the fact that
u(t,\cdot)
is non-increasing in
x
, we obtain

u(t,x)~\geq~u(\tau,b_{i}(\tau))-2\varepsilon\qquad\qquad\hbox{if}\quad t>\tau,\quad p_{k}<x<q_{k}-\lambda^{*}(t-\tau).

(3.54)

To define a leading order approximation
\widehat{U}_{i}(t,\cdot)
to the solution
u(t,\cdot)
for
t>\tau
,
we consider the characteristic speed

\lambda_{i}\,\doteq\,g^{\prime}\bigl(u(\tau,b_{i}(\tau)\bigr)

(3.55)

and define (see Fig.
9
)

\widehat{U}_{i}(t,x)~\doteq~\min\Big\{u_{i}(\tau)+\omega_{i}(t-\tau),~~u(\tau,x-\lambda_{i}(t-\tau)\bigr)\Big\}.

(3.56)

Figure 9:
The leading order approximate solution
\widehat{U}_{i}
defined at (
3.56
).
Here we are assuming
\lambda_{i}<0
.

We claim that

\lim_{t\to\tau+}\frac{1}{t-\tau}\int_{p_{k}}^{q_{k}-\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}\Big|u(t,x)-\widehat{U}_{i}(t,x)\Big|\,dx~=~{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon.

(3.57)

As shown in Fig.
10
, consider the point

b^{*}~\doteq~\max_{s\in[\tau,t]\cap K}\Big\{b_{i}(s)+\lambda_{i}(t-s)\Big\}~=~b_{i}(t^{*})+\lambda_{i}(t-t^{*})

(3.58)

for some
t^{*}\in[\tau,t]\cap K
. Note that at least one such time
t^{*}
exists because
K
is compact and
b_{i}(\cdot)
is continuous restricted to
K
.

Trivially, the definition implies

b^{*}\geq b_{i}(t)
. On the domain

{\cal D}_{i}^{(t)}~\doteq~\Big\{(s,x)\,;~~s\in[\tau,t]\cap K,~~~x\in\bigl[b^{*}+\lambda_{i}(s-t),~q_{k}-\lambda^{*}(s-\tau)\bigr]\Big\}

we have
\theta\equiv 0
and the flux is constantly equal to
g(u)
.
In view of (
3.54
), the same arguments used in step
10
to analyze the case (T2) now yield

\int_{b^{*}}^{q_{k}-\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}\bigl|u(t,x)-\widehat{U}_{i}(t,x)\bigr|~=~{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon(t-\tau).

(3.59)

It remains to study what happens on the interval
[p_{k}+\lambda^{*}(t-\tau),b^{*}]
.
For
y<b^{*}
consider the times (see Fig.
10
)

t^{+}(y)~\doteq~\min\Big\{t^{\prime}\in[\tau,t]\cap K\,;~~y+\lambda_{i}(t-s)\geq b_{i}(s)\quad\hbox{for all}~s\in[t^{\prime},t]\Big\},

t^{-}(y)~\doteq~\max\Big\{t^{\prime}\in[\tau,t]\cap K\,;~~y+\lambda_{i}(t-s)\geq b_{i}(s)\quad\hbox{for all}~s\in[\tau,t^{\prime}]\Big\}.

To complete the proof, the main idea is that, in the region where
x>b_{i}(s)
,
s\in K
, the function
u=u(s,x)
satisfies the conservation law with flux
g
. Since the oscillation of
u
on this domain can be taken very small, the characteristic speed

g^{\prime}\bigl(u(t,x)\bigr)\approx\lambda_{i}

is nearly constant. In turn, the function
u=u(t,x)
is nearly constant along lines with slope
\lambda_{i}
.
Knowing that along the curve
b_{i}(\cdot)
one has

u(s,b_{i}(s))=u_{i}(s)
, this will yield the desired bounds.

Figure 10:
The upper and lower shaded regions are the domains
{\cal D}^{-}_{\xi\zeta}
and
{\cal D}^{+}_{\xi\zeta}
in (
3.60
) and (
3.62
), used to estimate the error at (
3.57
).

We first apply the divergence theorem on every domain of the form (see the lower shaded region in Fig.
10
)

D^{-}_{\xi\zeta}~\doteq~\Big\{(s,x)\,;~~x=y+\lambda_{i}(s-t),~~s\in[\tau,t^{-}(y)],\quad\hbox{for some}~y\in[\xi,\zeta]\Big\},

(3.60)

where
u
satisfies the conservation law with flux
g
.
Note that this can be achieved by the integral formulation (
1.7
), choosing a test function

\varphi
which approaches the characteristic function of
{\cal D}^{-}_{\xi\zeta}
. For example, for every
y\in[\xi,\zeta]
,
we can set

\varphi(s,y+\lambda_{i}(s-t)\bigr)~=~\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}0&\hbox{if}\quad s\notin[0,t^{-}(y)+\epsilon],\\[2.84526pt]
1&\hbox{if}\quad s\in[\epsilon,t^{-}(y)],\\[2.84526pt]
\epsilon^{-1}s&\hbox{if}\quad s\in[0,\epsilon],\\[2.84526pt]
1-\epsilon^{-1}\bigl(s-t^{-}(y)\bigr)&\hbox{if}\quad s\in[t^{-}(y),t^{-}(y)+\epsilon].\end{array}\right.

Then we let
\epsilon\to 0+
.

Since the map
t\mapsto u_{i}(t)

is strictly decreasing for
t\in K
,
for every
y
there holds

u\Big(t^{-}(y),y-\lambda_{i}\bigl(t^{-}(y)-t\bigr)\Big)~=~u_{i}\bigl(t^{-}(y)\bigr)~\geq~u_{i}(t).

Proceeding as in step
10C
, we thus obtain a bound of the form

\int_{p_{k}+\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}^{b^{*}}\Big[u_{i}(t)-u(\tau,x-\lambda_{i}(t-\tau))\Big]_{+}\,dx~=~{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon(t-\tau).

(3.61)

Here
[z]_{+}\doteq\max\{z,0\}
denotes the positive part of a number
z
.
Next, for every
\xi,\zeta
we apply the divergence theorem on the
domain

D^{+}_{\xi\zeta}~\doteq~\Big\{(s,x)\,;~~x=y+\lambda_{i}(s-t),~~s\in[t^{+}(y),t],\quad\hbox{for some}~y\in[\xi,\zeta]\Big\},

(3.62)

(see the upper shaded region in Fig.
10
). Since

u\Big(t^{+}(y),y-\lambda_{i}\bigl(t^{+}(y)-t\bigr)\Big)~=~u_{i}\bigl(t^{+}(y)\bigr)~\geq~u_{i}(t),

the same technique now yields

\int_{p_{k}+\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}^{b^{*}}\Big[u_{i}(t)-u(t,x)\Big]_{+}\,dx~=~{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon(t-\tau).

(3.63)

By (
3.61
), recalling (
3.53
) and the definition of
\widehat{U}_{i}
at (
3.56
), we obtain

\int_{p_{k}+\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}^{b^{*}}\Big|u_{i}(\tau)+(t-\tau)\omega_{i}-u\bigl(\tau,x-\lambda_{i}(t-\tau)\bigr)\Big|\,dx~=~{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon(t-\tau).

(3.64)

On the other hand, since
u(t,x)\leq u_{i}(t)
, by (
3.63
) we have

\int_{p_{k}+\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}^{b^{*}}\Big|u_{i}(\tau)+(t-\tau)\omega_{i}-u(t,x)\Big|\,dx~=~{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon(t-\tau).

(3.65)

Combining (
3.64
) and (
3.65
), we conclude

\int_{p_{k}+\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}^{b^{*}}\Big|u(t,x)-\widehat{U}_{i}(t,x)\Big|\,dx~=~{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon(t-\tau).

(3.66)

13.
As before, we write
U(t,\cdot)\doteq S_{t-\tau}u(\tau,\cdot)
for the corresponding semigroup
trajectory.
Consider any time
\tau\in K\setminus({\cal N}\cup{\cal N}^{\prime})
.
Summarizing the previous steps, for each
\varepsilon>0
we can find a finite covering

[0,1]\subset\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}]p_{k},q_{k}[
and a speed
\lambda^{*}
sufficiently large so that

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\tau+}\frac{1}{t-\tau}\int_{0}^{1}\Big|u(t,x)-U(t,x)\Big|\,dx\\[11.38109pt]
\qquad\displaystyle\leq~\sum_{k}~\lim_{t\to\tau+,\,t\in K_{\alpha}}~\frac{1}{t-\tau}\int_{p_{k}+\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}^{q_{k}-\lambda^{*}(t-\tau)}\Big|u(t,x)-U_{k}(t,x)\Big|+\Big|U(t,x)-U_{k}(t,x)\Big|\,dx\\[8.53581pt]
\qquad\leq~\varepsilon.\end{array}

(3.67)

This yields (
3.14
), completing the proof.
