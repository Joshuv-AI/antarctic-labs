1.
Introduction and main results

A classical problem in the theory of non-parametric minimal surfaces asks for
the sharp curvature bound at the center of a minimal graph over a disk. Let

\Sigma=\{(x,y,u(x,y)):(x,y)\in D_{R}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

be a solution of the minimal surface equation over the disk of radius
R
.
Its Gaussian curvature is

K=\frac{u_{xx}u_{yy}-u_{xy}^{2}}{(1+u_{x}^{2}+u_{y}^{2})^{2}},

and the inclination factor is

W=\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}.

Equivalently,
W=1/n_{3}
, where
n_{3}
is the vertical component of the
upward unit normal. For general background on minimal surfaces and harmonic
mappings, see
[
1
,
2
,
10
]
; related
sharp-curvature estimates are discussed in
[
5
]
.

Heinz proved that the curvature at the center satisfies

|K(0)|\leq\frac{C}{R^{2}}

for an absolute constant
C
; see
[
6
]
. Hopf later refined this result by showing that the
correct scale-invariant quantity is not merely
R^{2}|K(0)|
, but rather

R^{2}W(0)^{2}|K(0)|.

More precisely, Hopf proved
[
7
]
that

W(0)^{2}|K(0)|\leq\frac{C_{1}}{R^{2}}

for an absolute constant
C_{1}
. The remaining problem is to determine the sharp value of this universal
constant.

Finn and Osserman proved that the sharp constant is
\pi^{2}/2
in the
horizontal tangent-plane case, that is, when
W(0)=1

[
3
]
. Nitsche observed that the same constant should
remain sharp for arbitrary inclination and verified this assertion in several
symmetric classes
[
9
]
. This led to the conjecture that

R^{2}W(0)^{2}|K(0)|<\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}

for every minimal graph over a disk, with
\pi^{2}/2
being the best possible
constant. We shall refer to this sharp-constant conjecture as the

Nitsche–Hopf conjecture
.

The purpose of this paper is to prove the Nitsche–Hopf conjecture.

Theorem 1.1

(Normalized Hopf curvature estimate)
.

Let
S
be a non-parametric minimal graph over the disk of radius
R
,
and let
\xi
be the point of
S
above the center of the disk. Then

W_{S}(\xi)^{2}|K_{S}(\xi)|<\frac{\pi^{2}}{2R^{2}}.

The constant
\pi^{2}/2
is sharp.

The strict inequality in Theorem
1.1
is inherited from the strict
form of the comparison theorem used below. The sharpness follows from the
horizontal tangent-plane extremal sequence of Finn and Osserman.

The main difficulty is that the normalized problem is genuinely stronger
than the corresponding problem for
|K|
. A pointwise estimate

|K(\xi)|\leq\frac{C}{R^{2}}

does not give useful information on
W(\xi)^{2}|K(\xi)|
, since the slope
factor
W(\xi)
can be arbitrarily large. In conformal coordinates this
obstruction is particularly transparent. If
g
denotes the Gauss-map
parameter of an upward oriented minimal graph, then

W=\frac{1+|g|^{2}}{1-|g|^{2}}.

Thus
W
becomes large as the Gauss map approaches the unit circle. The
Hopf problem therefore requires information which is not contained in a
comparison estimate for
|K|
alone.

The starting point of the proof is the bicentric-quadrilateral comparison
theorem of Kalaj and Melentijević
[
8
]
. That
theorem reduces sharp curvature questions for arbitrary disk graphs to a
two-parameter family of Scherk-type minimal graphs over bicentric
quadrilaterals. The corresponding admissible parameter set

\mathcal{S}\subset\{(p,q):0<p<q<\pi\}

was determined in
[
8
]
; its shape is shown in
Figure
2
. Each point
(p,q)\in\mathcal{S}
determines a
Scherk-type comparison graph.

For the unnormalized curvature, the comparison theorem is sufficient: after
a normalization and a horizontal rotation, the curvature of a given minimal
graph at the point above the center is strictly dominated by the curvature
of the corresponding Scherk-type comparison graph. For the normalized
quantity one additional fact is decisive. The comparison graph has the same
unit normal at the distinguished point as the original graph. Hence, the two
graphs have the same value of
W
at that point. Therefore, the strict
curvature comparison transfers immediately to
W^{2}|K|
, provided one proves
the sharp normalized estimate inside the Scherk-type comparison family.

This Scherk-family estimate is the new ingredient of the paper.

Theorem 1.2

(Scherk-type normalized Hopf estimate)
.

For every admissible two-parameter Scherk-type comparison graph,

W^{2}|K|\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}

at the distinguished point corresponding to the center in the comparison
normalization.

The central observation is that the Scherk comparison graph is not evaluated
at an arbitrary point of its conformal parameter disk. We use the fixed-arc
normalization of the Scherk family. In this normalization the horizontal
projection
f
is explicit: it is the harmonic extension of a four-step
boundary function whose values are the vertices of the bicentric quadrilateral
determined by the admissible pair
(p,q)\in\mathcal{S}
. Thus

f(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\Omega_{j}(z)P_{j},

where
P_{1},\ldots,P_{4}
are the four vertices of the associated bicentric
quadrilateral and
\Omega_{j}(z)
are the harmonic measures of the four fixed
boundary arcs.

The point relevant for the Hopf estimate is the point of the surface lying
above the center of the physical domain. In the fixed-arc parameter disk its
preimage is a distinguished point
z_{\circ}
, characterized by

f(z_{\circ})=0.

This zero equation is the additional constraint which compensates for the
possible largeness of the inclination factor
W
.

The analytic form of the problem comes from the Enneper–Weierstrass data.
If

X(z)=\operatorname{Re}\int^{z}\bigl(1-g^{2},\mathrm{i}(1+g^{2}),2g\bigr)\varphi\,\mathrm{d}z,

then, for an upward oriented minimal graph,

K=-\frac{4|g^{\prime}|^{2}}{|\varphi|^{2}(1+|g|^{2})^{4}},\qquad W=\frac{1+|g|^{2}}{1-|g|^{2}}.

For the fixed-arc Scherk family, the explicit Weierstrass data computed in

[
8
]
give the following

-K=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}(1+\mu^{2})\frac{|1-z^{2}|^{2}|z^{2}-e^{2\mathrm{i}\alpha}|^{2}}{D(z)^{4}},

where

\mu^{2}=AB,\qquad A=\sin p,\qquad B=\sin(q-p),

and
D(z)>0
is an explicit factor depending on the Gauss-map parameter
and on
z
. Since the Gauss map is a disk automorphism, one also has, at

z_{\circ}=re^{\mathrm{i}t_{\circ}}
,

W(z_{\circ})=\frac{D_{\circ}}{\mu(1-r^{2})},\qquad D_{\circ}=D(z_{\circ}).

Multiplying the curvature formula by this expression for
W^{2}
gives

W^{2}|K|=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\frac{1+\mu^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\frac{|1-z_{\circ}^{2}|^{2}|z_{\circ}^{2}-e^{2\mathrm{i}\alpha}|^{2}}{(1-|z_{\circ}|^{2})^{2}D_{\circ}^{2}}.

For arbitrary
z\in\mathbb{D}
, this identity alone does not imply Hopf’s
sharp bound. The decisive input is precisely the zero condition

f(z_{\circ})=0
.

We exploit this condition by writing it in harmonic-measure coordinates.
Introduce

U=\Omega_{1}(z_{\circ})+\Omega_{3}(z_{\circ}),\qquad V=\Omega_{1}(z_{\circ})-\Omega_{3}(z_{\circ}),\qquad T=\Omega_{4}(z_{\circ})-\Omega_{2}(z_{\circ}).

The zero equation is a linear relation among the four harmonic measures. In
the variables
U,V,T
, it makes
V
and
T
affine functions of the
single variable
U
. The fact that these four numbers are harmonic
measures of the prescribed fixed arcs gives a cross-ratio condition, and
therefore one scalar equation for
U
.

After eliminating the remaining phase term in
D_{\circ}
, the sharp Hopf
estimate becomes a one-variable derivative inequality at an admissible zero
of an explicit monotone function. We now state this scalar form.

Let

0<A,B\leq 1,\qquad\kappa=\sqrt{1-A^{2}},\qquad\varepsilon=\sqrt{1-B^{2}}.

Set

P=P(A,B):=\frac{1+AB}{B(A+B)}.

Define

M(U):=\kappa(P-U),

N(U):=\varepsilon\left(U+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{A(A+B)}\right),

and

G_{A,B}(U):=B\cos(\pi M(U))-A\cos(\pi N(U))-(A+B)\cos(\pi U).

The admissible interval is

L(A,B)\leq U\leq R(A,B),

where

L(A,B)=\frac{\kappa}{1+\kappa}\frac{1+AB}{B(A+B)}

and

R(A,B)=\frac{1-\varepsilon\kappa^{2}/[A(A+B)]}{1+\varepsilon}.

This interval is nonempty precisely when

B\geq B_{0}(A),

with

B_{0}(A)=\frac{-A(1-\kappa)+\sqrt{A^{2}(1-\kappa)^{2}+8\kappa(1+\kappa)}}{2(1+\kappa)}.

Theorem 1.3

(Scalar derivative inequality)
.

Assume
0<A,B\leq 1
and
B\geq B_{0}(A)
. Let
U
be an admissible
zero of
G_{A,B}
, that is,

G_{A,B}(U)=0,\qquad L(A,B)\leq U\leq R(A,B).

Then

\frac{1}{\pi}G^{\prime}_{A,B}(U)\geq\sqrt{2(1+AB)}.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle(A+B)\sin(\pi U)+B\kappa\sin(\pi M(U))+A\varepsilon\sin(\pi N(U))

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{2(1+AB)}.

The proof of Theorem
1.3
is elementary but rigid. The
admissibility inequalities imply

0\leq M(U),N(U)\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Thus the sine terms can be bounded below by linear functions, reducing the
problem to two linear estimates. Each of these estimates is obtained from a
barrier point for the monotone function
G_{A,B}
. The only global
positivity input required in the argument consists of two explicit
Bernstein-polynomial certificates on the square
[0,1]^{2}
, recorded in
Appendix
C
.

The logical structure of the proof is as follows. First, the fixed-arc
Scherk normalization gives the exact identity for
W^{2}|K|
. Second, the
zero equation
f(z_{\circ})=0
is rewritten in harmonic-measure coordinates,
reducing the geometry to the scalar equation

G_{A,B}(U)=0.

Third, harmonic-measure identities eliminate the phase appearing in

D_{\circ}
, and the Hopf estimate becomes precisely the derivative
inequality in Theorem
1.3
. Fourth, the scalar theorem proves
Theorem
1.2
. Finally, the strict comparison theorem of Kalaj and Melentijević transfers
the Scherk-family estimate to arbitrary minimal graphs over disks and gives
Theorem
1.1
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
recalls the
Scherk comparison normalization and derives the exact normalized curvature
identity. Section
3
rewrites the zero equation

f(z_{\circ})=0
in harmonic-measure coordinates and eliminates the phase
term. Section
4
proves the scalar derivative
inequality. Section
5
completes the proof of the
normalized Hopf estimate. Appendix
A
records an auxiliary sharp
coefficient estimate for odd harmonic disk maps. Appendix
B

gives the complementary lower bound in the Scherk family. Appendix
C
contains the Bernstein-polynomial positivity
certificates. Appendix
D
records a log-subharmonicity
observation for
W^{2}|K|
.

2.
The Scherk comparison family and the exact identity

2.1.
The Scherk comparison theorem

We use the following comparison theorem
[
8
, Theorem 1.4]
. For
w\in\mathbb{D}
, put

\lambda_{w}=\frac{\mathrm{i}(1-w^{4})}{|1-w^{4}|},\qquad\mu_{w}(z)=\left(\frac{w+\lambda_{w}z}{1+\overline{w}\lambda_{w}z}\right)^{2}.

Theorem 2.1

(Scherk comparison principle)
.

For every
w\in\mathbb{D}
, there are four distinct points

\zeta_{0},\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\zeta_{3}\in\mathbb{T}

and a sense-preserving harmonic diffeomorphism

f^{\diamond}:\mathbb{D}\to Q(\zeta_{0},\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\zeta_{3})

onto the corresponding quadrilateral such that

f^{\diamond}(0)=0,\qquad(f^{\diamond})_{z}(0)>0,

and

\overline{(f^{\diamond})_{\bar{z}}(z)}=\mu_{w}(z)(f^{\diamond})_{z}(z).

Equivalently, if
f^{\diamond}=h^{\diamond}+\overline{g^{\diamond}}
, then

(g^{\diamond})^{\prime}(z)=\mu_{w}(z)(h^{\diamond})^{\prime}(z).

The harmonic map
f^{\diamond}
gives a Scherk-type minimal graph
S^{\diamond}
over
Q(\zeta_{0},\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\zeta_{3})
containing the point
\xi=(0,0,0)
. Its upward normal at
\xi
is

n^{\diamond}_{\xi}=-\frac{1}{1+|w|^{2}}\bigl(2\operatorname{Im}w,2\operatorname{Re}w,-1+|w|^{2}\bigr),

and
U^{\diamond}_{uv}(0,0)=0
. If
S
is any other non-parametric minimal graph over the unit disk satisfying

(0,0,0)\in S,\qquad n_{\xi}=n^{\diamond}_{\xi},\qquad U_{uv}(0,0)=0,

then

|K_{S}(\xi)|<|K_{S^{\diamond}}(\xi)|.

Moreover,

K_{S^{\diamond}}(\xi)=-\frac{4(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}{(1+|w|^{2})^{4}|(f^{\diamond})_{z}(0)|^{2}}.

Because the compared surfaces have the same normal, the theorem immediately transfers to the normalized quantity.

Corollary 2.2

(Normalized comparison)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
2.1
,

W_{S}(\xi)^{2}|K_{S}(\xi)|<W_{S^{\diamond}}(\xi)^{2}|K_{S^{\diamond}}(\xi)|.

Furthermore,

W_{S^{\diamond}}(\xi)^{2}|K_{S^{\diamond}}(\xi)|=\frac{4}{(1+|w|^{2})^{2}|(f^{\diamond})_{z}(0)|^{2}}.

Proof.

The two surfaces have the same unit normal at
\xi
. Since
W=1/n_{3}
, they also have the same value of
W
at
\xi
. Multiplying the curvature comparison in Theorem
2.1
by this common factor
W^{2}
gives the first assertion.

From the formula for
n^{\diamond}_{\xi}
,

(n^{\diamond}_{\xi})_{3}=\frac{1-|w|^{2}}{1+|w|^{2}},\qquad W_{S^{\diamond}}(\xi)=\frac{1+|w|^{2}}{1-|w|^{2}}.

Multiplication by the curvature formula in Theorem
2.1
gives the stated identity.
∎

Remark 2.3

(Two normalizations)
.

The comparison theorem is stated in the normalization
f^{\diamond}(0)=0
. The fixed-arc normalization used below is obtained by precomposition with a disk automorphism. In that normalization the Beltrami coefficient becomes
z^{2}
, and the preimage of the point above the origin is generally not
0
. We denote this preimage by
z_{\circ}
and write

f(z_{\circ})=0.

The quantity
W^{2}|K|
is intrinsic to the surface point, and therefore is unchanged by this reparametrization.

2.2.
Parameters

The fixed-arc Scherk family is described by two admissible angular parameters
p,q
. We use

A=\sin p,\qquad B=\sin(q-p),\qquad c_{p}=\cos p,\qquad d_{q}=\cos(q-p),

and

\mu^{2}=AB,\qquad 0<A\leq 1,\qquad 0<B\leq 1.

The arc parameter
\alpha
is determined by

\tan^{2}\frac{\alpha}{2}=\frac{A}{B},

so that

\sin\alpha=\frac{2\sqrt{AB}}{A+B}=\frac{2\mu}{A+B}.

We write

z_{\circ}=re^{\mathrm{i}t_{\circ}},\qquad 0\leq r<1,

for the distinguished zero of the harmonic projection.

The Gauss-map parameter is an automorphism of the disk. We use the standard convention

g(z)=e^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta}\frac{z-a}{1-\overline{a}z},\qquad a=|a|e^{\mathrm{i}\delta},

where

|a|=\sqrt{\frac{1-\mu}{1+\mu}}.

Consequently,

\frac{1+|a|^{2}}{1-|a|^{2}}=\frac{1}{\mu},\qquad\frac{4|a|}{1+|a|^{2}}=2\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}.

2.3.
Exact normalized curvature identity

For a minimal graph written in conformal Enneper–Weierstrass form, one has

W=\frac{1+|g|^{2}}{1-|g|^{2}}.

At
z=z_{\circ}
this gives

W=\frac{1+|g(z_{\circ})|^{2}}{1-|g(z_{\circ})|^{2}}.

Using the automorphism formula for
g
, we have

1-|g(z_{\circ})|^{2}=\frac{(1-|a|^{2})(1-r^{2})}{|1-\overline{a}z_{\circ}|^{2}},

and

1+|g(z_{\circ})|^{2}=\frac{|1-\overline{a}z_{\circ}|^{2}+|z_{\circ}-a|^{2}}{|1-\overline{a}z_{\circ}|^{2}}.

Hence,

W=\frac{|1-\overline{a}z_{\circ}|^{2}+|z_{\circ}-a|^{2}}{(1-|a|^{2})(1-r^{2})}.

The numerator can be rewritten as follows

\displaystyle|1-\overline{a}z_{\circ}|^{2}+|z_{\circ}-a|^{2}

\displaystyle=(1+r^{2})(1+|a|^{2})-4\operatorname{Re}(a\overline{z_{\circ}})

\displaystyle=(1+|a|^{2})\left(1+r^{2}-\frac{4\operatorname{Re}(a\overline{z_{\circ}})}{1+|a|^{2}}\right).

Thus

W=\frac{D_{\circ}}{\mu(1-r^{2})},

where

D_{\circ}=1+r^{2}-\frac{4\operatorname{Re}(a\overline{z_{\circ}})}{1+|a|^{2}}.

Equivalently,

D_{\circ}=1+r^{2}-2\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\,r\cos(t_{\circ}-\delta).

Theorem 2.4

(Exact normalized curvature identity)
.

Let
z_{\circ}=re^{\mathrm{i}t_{\circ}}
be the distinguished zero of the harmonic projection. Then

W^{2}|K|=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\frac{1+\mu^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\frac{|1-z_{\circ}^{2}|^{2}|z_{\circ}^{2}-e^{2\mathrm{i}\alpha}|^{2}}{(1-r^{2})^{2}D_{\circ}^{2}}.

Proof.

The explicit curvature formula for the fixed-arc two-parameter Scherk family is

-K=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}(1+\mu^{2})\frac{|1-z_{\circ}^{2}|^{2}|z_{\circ}^{2}-e^{2\mathrm{i}\alpha}|^{2}}{D_{\circ}^{4}};

see
[
8
, Eq. (4.2)]
. Multiplying this identity by

W^{2}=\frac{D_{\circ}^{2}}{\mu^{2}(1-r^{2})^{2}}

gives the formula.
∎

Corollary 2.5

(Zero-control formulation)
.

The estimate

W^{2}|K|\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}

is equivalent to

|1-z_{\circ}^{2}|\,|z_{\circ}^{2}-e^{2\mathrm{i}\alpha}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\mu^{2}}{1+\mu^{2}}}(1-r^{2})D_{\circ}.

Remark 2.6
.

The inequality in Corollary
2.5
is not intended for arbitrary
z\in\mathbb{D}
. The decisive extra information is that
z=z_{\circ}
is the zero of the harmonic projection,
f(z_{\circ})=0
.

3.
Harmonic-measure coordinates and phase elimination

3.1.
The zero equation

Let

I_{1}=(0,\alpha),\quad I_{2}=(\alpha,\pi),\quad I_{3}=(\pi,\pi+\alpha),\quad I_{4}=(\pi+\alpha,2\pi),

and put

\Omega_{j}(z)=\omega(z,I_{j},\mathbb{D}),\qquad j=1,2,3,4.

In fixed-arc normalization, the boundary values of the harmonic projection are the four constant values

P_{1}=e^{\mathrm{i}(q-p+2\beta)},\qquad P_{2}=e^{-\mathrm{i}p},\qquad P_{3}=e^{\mathrm{i}(p-q+2\beta)},\qquad P_{4}=e^{\mathrm{i}p}.

Since
f
is the Poisson extension of this step function, we see that

f(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\Omega_{j}(z)P_{j}.

Therefore,

f(z_{\circ})=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{j=1}^{4}\Omega_{j}(z_{\circ})P_{j}=0.

Introduce

U=\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{3},\qquad V=\Omega_{1}-\Omega_{3},\qquad T=\Omega_{4}-\Omega_{2}.

Then

\Omega_{1}=\frac{U+V}{2},\quad\Omega_{3}=\frac{U-V}{2},\quad\Omega_{4}=\frac{1-U+T}{2},\quad\Omega_{2}=\frac{1-U-T}{2}.

The positivity of the harmonic measure gives

|V|\leq U,\qquad|T|\leq 1-U.

The zero equation becomes

e^{2\mathrm{i}\beta}\bigl(U\cos(q-p)+\mathrm{i}V\sin(q-p)\bigr)+(1-U)\cos p+\mathrm{i}T\sin p=0.

Equivalently,

V=c_{p}\left(\frac{1+\mu^{2}}{B(A+B)}-U\right),

and

T=-d_{q}\left(U+\frac{c_{p}^{2}}{A(A+B)}\right).

Thus, the zero equation leaves only one free harmonic-measure parameter, namely
U
.

3.2.
The numerator and the master inequality

The map
z\mapsto z^{2}
sends
I_{1}\cup I_{3}
onto the single arc
(0,2\alpha)
. Hence,

U=\omega(z_{\circ}^{2},(0,2\alpha),\mathbb{D}).

For
w=z_{\circ}^{2}
, the harmonic-measure formula for one circular arc gives

\sin(\pi U)=\frac{(1-|w|^{2})\sin\alpha}{|1-w|\,|e^{2\mathrm{i}\alpha}-w|}.

Since
|w|=r^{2}
,

\frac{|1-z_{\circ}^{2}|\,|z_{\circ}^{2}-e^{2\mathrm{i}\alpha}|}{1-r^{2}}=\frac{(1+r^{2})\sin\alpha}{\sin(\pi U)}.

Substitution into Theorem
2.4
yields

W^{2}|K|=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\frac{1+\mu^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\left(\frac{(1+r^{2})\sin\alpha}{D_{\circ}\sin(\pi U)}\right)^{2}.

Using

\frac{D_{\circ}}{1+r^{2}}=1-\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\frac{2r}{1+r^{2}}\cos(t_{\circ}-\delta),

we see that
W^{2}|K|\leq\pi^{2}/2
is equivalent to

\sin(\pi U)\left(1-\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}\frac{2r}{1+r^{2}}\cos(t_{\circ}-\delta)\right)\geq\frac{\sqrt{2(1+\mu^{2})}}{A+B}.

This is the master inequality.

3.3.
The scalar equation for
U

Lemma 3.1

(Harmonic-measure cross-ratio condition)
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{D}
, and let

\Omega_{j}=\omega(z,I_{j},\mathbb{D}),\qquad j=1,2,3,4,

where

I_{1}=(0,\alpha),\quad I_{2}=(\alpha,\pi),\quad I_{3}=(\pi,\pi+\alpha),\quad I_{4}=(\pi+\alpha,2\pi).

Then

\frac{\sin(\pi\Omega_{1})\sin(\pi\Omega_{3})}{\sin(\pi\Omega_{2})\sin(\pi\Omega_{4})}=\tan^{2}\frac{\alpha}{2}.

Equivalently, in the variables

U=\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{3},\qquad V=\Omega_{1}-\Omega_{3},\qquad T=\Omega_{4}-\Omega_{2},

one has

\frac{\sin\frac{\pi}{2}(U+V)\sin\frac{\pi}{2}(U-V)}{\sin\frac{\pi}{2}(1-U-T)\sin\frac{\pi}{2}(1-U+T)}=\tan^{2}\frac{\alpha}{2}.

Proof.

Let
\phi
be a disk automorphism with
\phi(z)=0
. Harmonic measure is
conformally invariant, so

\Omega_{j}=\omega(0,\phi(I_{j}),\mathbb{D}).

At the origin, harmonic measure is normalized arclength. Hence, the four arcs

\phi(I_{j})
have angular lengths
2\pi\Omega_{j}
.

Let the endpoints of the four image arcs be

\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3}\in\mathbb{T}

in cyclic order. Since the angular length of the arc from
\eta_{0}
to

\eta_{1}
is
2\pi\Omega_{1}
, and similarly for the other arcs, we have

|\eta_{0}-\eta_{1}|=2\sin(\pi\Omega_{1}),\qquad|\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}|=2\sin(\pi\Omega_{2}),

|\eta_{2}-\eta_{3}|=2\sin(\pi\Omega_{3}),\qquad|\eta_{3}-\eta_{0}|=2\sin(\pi\Omega_{4}).

The absolute cross-ratio

\left|\frac{(\eta_{0}-\eta_{1})(\eta_{2}-\eta_{3})}{(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})(\eta_{3}-\eta_{0})}\right|

is invariant under Möbius transformations. Therefore, it is equal to the
corresponding cross-ratio of the original endpoints

1,\quad e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha},\quad-1,\quad-e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}.

Thus

\displaystyle\frac{\sin(\pi\Omega_{1})\sin(\pi\Omega_{3})}{\sin(\pi\Omega_{2})\sin(\pi\Omega_{4})}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{(1-e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha})(-1+e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha})}{(e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}+1)(-e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}-1)}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{4\sin^{2}(\alpha/2)}{4\cos^{2}(\alpha/2)}=\tan^{2}\frac{\alpha}{2}.

Finally, substituting

\Omega_{1}=\frac{U+V}{2},\quad\Omega_{3}=\frac{U-V}{2},\quad\Omega_{4}=\frac{1-U+T}{2},\quad\Omega_{2}=\frac{1-U-T}{2}

gives the stated form.
∎

By Lemma
3.1
, the variables
U,V,T
satisfy

\frac{\sin\frac{\pi}{2}(U+V)\sin\frac{\pi}{2}(U-V)}{\sin\frac{\pi}{2}(1-U-T)\sin\frac{\pi}{2}(1-U+T)}=\tan^{2}\frac{\alpha}{2}.

Equivalently,

\cos(\pi V)-\cos(\pi U)=\tan^{2}\frac{\alpha}{2}\bigl(\cos(\pi T)+\cos(\pi U)\bigr).

Since
\tan^{2}(\alpha/2)=A/B
, this equation becomes

B\cos(\pi V)-A\cos(\pi T)-(A+B)\cos(\pi U)=0.

Define

\kappa=|c_{p}|=\sqrt{1-A^{2}},\qquad\varepsilon=|d_{q}|=\sqrt{1-B^{2}},

M(U)=|V(U)|=\kappa\left(\frac{1+AB}{B(A+B)}-U\right),

and

N(U)=|T(U)|=\varepsilon\left(U+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{A(A+B)}\right).

Then the scalar equation is as follows

G_{A,B}(U)=0,

where

G_{A,B}(U)=B\cos(\pi M(U))-A\cos(\pi N(U))-(A+B)\cos(\pi U).

The admissibility inequalities are

0\leq M(U)\leq U,\qquad 0\leq N(U)\leq 1-U.

They are equivalent to

L(A,B)\leq U\leq R(A,B),

where

L(A,B)=\frac{\kappa}{1+\kappa}\frac{1+AB}{B(A+B)},

and

R(A,B)=\frac{1-\varepsilon\kappa^{2}/[A(A+B)]}{1+\varepsilon}.

Lemma 3.2

(Admissible domain)
.

For
0<A,B\leq 1
, the interval
[L(A,B),R(A,B)]
is nonempty if and only if

B\geq B_{0}(A),

where

B_{0}(A)=\frac{-A(1-\kappa)+\sqrt{A^{2}(1-\kappa)^{2}+8\kappa(1+\kappa)}}{2(1+\kappa)}.

Moreover, if
B\geq B_{0}(A)
, then

L(A,B)\leq\frac{1}{2}\leq R(A,B),

and, for every admissible zero,

0\leq M(U),N(U)\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Proof.

First,

1-R(A,B)=\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\left(1+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{A(A+B)}\right)=\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\frac{1+AB}{A(A+B)}=L(B,A).

Hence

L(A,B)\leq R(A,B)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L(A,B)+L(B,A)\leq 1.

Inequality
L(A,B)\leq 1/2
is equivalent to

2\kappa(1+AB)\leq(1+\kappa)B(A+B),

or

(1+\kappa)B^{2}+A(1-\kappa)B-2\kappa\geq 0.

The left-hand side is increasing in
B
on
[0,1]
, and its positive root is
B_{0}(A)
.
A symmetric squaring computation gives

L(A,B)\leq\frac{1}{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L(B,A)\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Indeed, both are equivalent to the nonpositivity of

(1-A^{2})(2+AB-B^{2})^{2}-B^{2}(A+B)^{2},

which is invariant under interchanging
A
and
B
after using
A^{2}+\kappa^{2}=B^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}=1
.
Thus

L(A,B)\leq R(A,B)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L(A,B)\leq\frac{1}{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad B\geq B_{0}(A).

If
B\geq B_{0}(A)
, then
L(A,B)\leq 1/2
and
L(B,A)\leq 1/2
, so
R(A,B)=1-L(B,A)\geq 1/2
.

We shall also use that the right endpoint lies below
P=(1+AB)/(B(A+B))
. Indeed,

P-R(A,B)=\frac{\varepsilon\,(A\varepsilon+A+B)}{AB(A+B)(1+\varepsilon)}\geq 0.

Thus,
U\leq R(A,B)
implies
P-U\geq 0
, so the formula
M(U)=\kappa(P-U)
is nonnegative on the admissible interval.

Finally,
M
is decreasing and
N
is increasing. Hence, on the admissible interval,

M(U)\leq M(L)=L\leq\frac{1}{2},\qquad N(U)\leq N(R)=1-R\leq\frac{1}{2}.

The lower bounds are precisely the admissibility conditions. This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.3

(Monotonicity)
.

The function
G_{A,B}
is strictly increasing on every non-degenerate admissible interval.

Proof.

Differentiating and using
M^{\prime}(U)=-\kappa
,
N^{\prime}(U)=\varepsilon
, one obtains

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}G^{\prime}_{A,B}(U)

\displaystyle=(A+B)\sin(\pi U)+B\kappa\sin(\pi M(U))+A\varepsilon\sin(\pi N(U)).

On the admissible interval all three sine factors are nonnegative. On every nontrivial subinterval at least one term is positive, so
G_{A,B}
is strictly increasing.
∎

3.4.
Exact phase elimination

Put

h=\frac{\alpha}{2}.

For the arc centered at
\phi
with half-length
s
, the harmonic-measure formula is

\cot(\pi\omega)=\frac{(1+r^{2})\cos s-2r\cos(t_{\circ}-\phi)}{(1-r^{2})\sin s}.

Applying this formula to
I_{1}
and
I_{3}
, whose centers are
h
and
h+\pi
, gives

\frac{2r}{1+r^{2}}\cos(t_{\circ}-h)=\cos h\,\frac{\sin(\pi V)}{\sin(\pi U)}.

Applying the same formula to
I_{2}
and
I_{4}
, whose centers are
h+\pi/2
and
h+3\pi/2
, gives

\frac{2r}{1+r^{2}}\cos\left(t_{\circ}-h-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=-\sin h\,\frac{\sin(\pi T)}{\sin(\pi U)}.

Consequently,

\frac{2r}{1+r^{2}}\cos(t_{\circ}-\delta)=\frac{\cos h\sin(\pi V)\cos(\delta-h)-\sin h\sin(\pi T)\sin(\delta-h)}{\sin(\pi U)}.

Lemma 3.4

(Phase of
a
)
.

Assume
AB<1
. With the notation above,

\cos(\delta-h)=-\frac{c_{p}\sqrt{B}}{\sqrt{(1-AB)(A+B)}},

and

\sin(\delta-h)=-\frac{d_{q}\sqrt{A}}{\sqrt{(1-AB)(A+B)}}.

Proof.

The explicit formula for the parameter
a
in the two-parameter Scherk family gives

a=\frac{d_{q}-c_{p}-\mathrm{i}\sin q}{1-\cos q+2\sqrt{AB}+\mathrm{i}(B-A)}.

Using

\sin q=Ad_{q}+Bc_{p},\qquad\cos q=c_{p}d_{q}-AB,

one simplifies this expression to

a=\frac{Ad_{q}-Bc_{p}-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{AB}(c_{p}+d_{q})}{(1+\sqrt{AB})(A+B)}.

Also,

|a|=\sqrt{\frac{1-\sqrt{AB}}{1+\sqrt{AB}}},\qquad e^{-\mathrm{i}h}=\frac{\sqrt{B}-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{A}}{\sqrt{A+B}}.

Therefore,

e^{\mathrm{i}(\delta-h)}=\frac{a}{|a|}e^{-\mathrm{i}h}=-\frac{c_{p}\sqrt{B}+\mathrm{i}d_{q}\sqrt{A}}{\sqrt{(1-AB)(A+B)}}.

Taking real and imaginary parts proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 3.5

(Derivative form of the master inequality)
.

Let
U
be an admissible zero of
G_{A,B}
. Then

W^{2}|K|\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}

is equivalent to

\frac{1}{\pi}G^{\prime}_{A,B}(U)\geq\sqrt{2(1+AB)}.

Proof.

If
AB=1
, then
A=B=1
,
\kappa=\varepsilon=0
, the admissible zero is
U=1/2
, and the assertion reduces to

2\sin(\pi/2)=\sqrt{2(1+1)}.

Thus, we assume
AB<1
.

Since

\tan^{2}h=\frac{A}{B},\qquad\cos h=\sqrt{\frac{B}{A+B}},\qquad\sin h=\sqrt{\frac{A}{A+B}},

Lemma
3.4
and the exact phase formula yield

\displaystyle-\sqrt{1-AB}\bigl[\cos h\sin(\pi V)\cos(\delta-h)-\sin h\sin(\pi T)\sin(\delta-h)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{B\kappa\sin(\pi M)+A\varepsilon\sin(\pi N)}{A+B}.

Substitution into the master inequality gives

\sin(\pi U)+\frac{B\kappa\sin(\pi M)+A\varepsilon\sin(\pi N)}{A+B}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2(1+AB)}}{A+B}.

Multiplication by
A+B
gives

\displaystyle(A+B)\sin(\pi U)+B\kappa\sin(\pi M)+A\varepsilon\sin(\pi N)

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{2(1+AB)}.

By the derivative formula in Lemma
3.3
, this is precisely

\frac{1}{\pi}G^{\prime}_{A,B}(U)\geq\sqrt{2(1+AB)}.

∎

4.
Proof of the scalar derivative inequality

This section proves Theorem
1.3
. The proof is elementary but rather rigid. The idea is to replace the sine inequality by two linear lower bounds and then to obtain those lower bounds from a barrier point for the monotone function
G_{A,B}
.

Throughout this section we assume

0<A,B\leq 1,\qquad B\geq B_{0}(A),

and we write

\kappa=\sqrt{1-A^{2}},\qquad\varepsilon=\sqrt{1-B^{2}},\qquad P=\frac{1+AB}{B(A+B)}.

Thus

M(U)=\kappa(P-U),

N(U)=\varepsilon\left(U+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{A(A+B)}\right),

and

G_{A,B}(U)=B\cos(\pi M(U))-A\cos(\pi N(U))-(A+B)\cos(\pi U).

Let
U
be the admissible zero of
G_{A,B}
and set

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=(A+B)\sin(\pi U)+B\kappa\sin(\pi M(U))+A\varepsilon\sin(\pi N(U)).

By Lemma
3.2
,

0\leq M(U),N(U)\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Using

\sin(\pi x)\geq 2x,\qquad 0\leq x\leq\frac{1}{2},

and

\sin(\pi U)\geq 2\min\{U,1-U\},\qquad 0\leq U\leq 1,

we obtain

S\geq 2\left[(A+B)\min\{U,1-U\}+B\kappa M(U)+A\varepsilon N(U)\right].

Therefore, it is enough to prove the two linear estimates

(4.1)

(A+B)(1-U)+B\kappa M(U)+A\varepsilon N(U)\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2(1+AB)},

(4.2)

(A+B)U+B\kappa M(U)+A\varepsilon N(U)\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2(1+AB)}.

4.1.
The right linear estimate

Put

x=P-U.

Then

M(U)=\kappa x.

Furthermore,

P+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{A(A+B)}=\frac{1+AB}{B(A+B)}+\frac{1-A^{2}}{A(A+B)}=\frac{1}{AB}.

Hence

N(U)=\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{AB}-x\right).

Define

H_{R}=(A+B)(1-U)+B\kappa M(U)+A\varepsilon N(U).

Substituting
U=P-x
,
M=\kappa x
, and
N=\varepsilon(1/(AB)-x)
gives the exact simplification

H_{R}=B(2+AB-A^{2})x.

Let

C=2+AB-A^{2},\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{2(1+AB)}.

Then (
4.1
) is equivalent to

BCx\geq\frac{\sigma}{2}.

Set

x_{*}:=\frac{\sigma}{2BC},\qquad U_{*}:=P-x_{*}.

It remains to prove
U\leq U_{*}
. If
U_{*}\geq R(A,B)
, this is immediate. Assume therefore that

U_{*}<R(A,B).

We shall prove

G_{A,B}(U_{*})\geq 0.

Since
G_{A,B}
is strictly increasing on the admissible interval and
G_{A,B}(U)=0
, this implies
U\leq U_{*}
.

First, we check that
U_{*}
lies in the admissible interval. We prove

U_{*}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

This is equivalent to

x_{*}\leq P-\frac{1}{2}.

But

P-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{2+AB-B^{2}}{2B(A+B)}.

Thus, it suffices to prove

\sigma(A+B)\leq C(2+AB-B^{2}).

Since
\sigma\leq 2
, it is enough to know

2(A+B)\leq C(2+AB-B^{2}),

that is,

Y(A,B):=(2+AB-A^{2})(2+AB-B^{2})-2(A+B)\geq 0.

This is Lemma
C.2
. Hence
U_{*}\geq 1/2
. By Lemma
3.2
,
L(A,B)\leq 1/2
, so
U_{*}\geq L(A,B)
. Together with the standing assumption
U_{*}<R(A,B)
, this shows that
U_{*}
is admissible.

Put

y=1-U_{*}.

Because
U_{*}\geq 1/2
and
U_{*}<R\leq 1
, we have

0\leq y\leq\frac{1}{2}.

At
U=U_{*}
,

M(U_{*})=\kappa x_{*},\qquad U_{*}=1-y,

so

-\cos(\pi U_{*})=\cos(\pi y).

Therefore

G_{A,B}(U_{*})=B\cos(\pi\kappa x_{*})-A\cos(\pi N(U_{*}))+(A+B)\cos(\pi y).

Using
\cos(\pi N(U_{*}))\leq 1
, one has

G_{A,B}(U_{*})\geq B\cos(\pi\kappa x_{*})-A+(A+B)\cos(\pi y).

For
0\leq y\leq 1/2
,

\cos(\pi y)\geq 1-2y,

and, for all real
t
,

\cos t\geq 1-\frac{t^{2}}{2}.

Hence

\cos(\pi\kappa x_{*})\geq 1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\kappa^{2}x_{*}^{2}.

Thus

G_{A,B}(U_{*})\geq B\left(1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\kappa^{2}x_{*}^{2}\right)-A+(A+B)(1-2y).

Now

y=1-U_{*}=1-P+x_{*}.

Since

1-P=-\frac{1-B^{2}}{B(A+B)}=-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{B(A+B)},

we get

y=x_{*}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{B(A+B)}.

Substitution yields

G_{A,B}(U_{*})\geq\frac{2}{B}-2(A+B)x_{*}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}B\kappa^{2}x_{*}^{2}.

Using
x_{*}=\sigma/(2BC)
and
\sigma^{2}=2(1+AB)
, the right-hand side is nonnegative provided

2C^{2}-C(A+B)\sigma-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}(1-A^{2})(1+AB)\geq 0.

Since
\pi^{2}<10
, it is enough to prove

2C^{2}-C(A+B)\sigma-\frac{5}{2}(1-A^{2})(1+AB)\geq 0.

We use

\sigma\leq 2,\qquad 2-\sigma=\frac{4-\sigma^{2}}{2+\sigma}=\frac{2(1-AB)}{2+\sigma}\geq\frac{1-AB}{2}.

Therefore

\displaystyle 2C-(A+B)\sigma

\displaystyle=2(C-A-B)+(A+B)(2-\sigma)

\displaystyle\geq 2(C-A-B)+\frac{A+B}{2}(1-AB).

It remains to use Lemma
C.3
, which asserts the nonnegativity of

Z(A,B):=C\left[2(C-A-B)+\frac{A+B}{2}(1-AB)\right]-\frac{5}{2}(1-A^{2})(1+AB).

Thus
G_{A,B}(U_{*})\geq 0
. Since
G_{A,B}
is increasing, we have
U\leq U_{*}
. Equivalently,
x=P-U\geq x_{*}
. Hence

H_{R}=BCx\geq BCx_{*}=\frac{\sigma}{2}.

This proves (
4.1
).

4.2.
The left linear estimate

The second estimate follows by symmetry. Under

A\leftrightarrow B,\qquad U\leftrightarrow 1-U,

one has

M_{B,A}(1-U)=N_{A,B}(U),\qquad N_{B,A}(1-U)=M_{A,B}(U),

and

G_{B,A}(1-U)=-G_{A,B}(U).

Thus, if
U
is the admissible zero for
(A,B)
, then
1-U
is the admissible zero for
(B,A)
. By Lemma
3.2
, the swapped pair also satisfies the domain condition. Applying (
4.1
) to
(B,A)
gives

(A+B)U+A\varepsilon N(U)+B\kappa M(U)\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2(1+AB)},

which is exactly (
4.2
).

4.3.
Completion of Theorem
1.3

The two estimates (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) imply

(A+B)\min\{U,1-U\}+B\kappa M(U)+A\varepsilon N(U)\geq\frac{\sigma}{2}.

Combining this with the sine lower bound gives

S\geq 2\cdot\frac{\sigma}{2}=\sigma=\sqrt{2(1+AB)}.

This is the desired scalar derivative inequality, and Theorem
1.3
is proved.

5.
Completion of the Hopf estimate

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
(p,q)
be an admissible pair in the Scherk-type comparison family, and let
z_{\circ}
be the distinguished zero of the harmonic projection. The harmonic-measure construction produces an admissible zero
U
of
G_{A,B}
with

A=\sin p,\qquad B=\sin(q-p).

By Lemma
3.2
, the corresponding admissible interval is nonempty, so
B\geq B_{0}(A)
. The scalar theorem gives

\frac{1}{\pi}G^{\prime}_{A,B}(U)\geq\sqrt{2(1+AB)}.

By Lemma
3.5
, this is equivalent to the Hopf master inequality, and hence

W^{2}|K|\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}

for the Scherk-type comparison graph.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By scaling, it suffices to consider the unit disk. Translate vertically so that the point above the center is
(0,0,0)
, and rotate the horizontal coordinates so that the mixed second derivative at the center is zero. Choose the normal parameter
w
corresponding to the unit normal at the center.

The Scherk comparison theorem gives a comparison graph
S^{\diamond}
with the same normal at the distinguished point and with

W_{S}(\xi)^{2}|K_{S}(\xi)|<W_{S^{\diamond}}(\xi)^{2}|K_{S^{\diamond}}(\xi)|.

By Theorem
1.2
,

W_{S^{\diamond}}(\xi)^{2}|K_{S^{\diamond}}(\xi)|\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}.

Thus

W_{S}(\xi)^{2}|K_{S}(\xi)|<\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}

for the unit disk. If the original disk has radius
R
, the homothety to the unit disk leaves
W
unchanged and multiplies
|K|
by
R^{2}
. Therefore

W_{S}(\xi)^{2}|K_{S}(\xi)|<\frac{\pi^{2}}{2R^{2}}.

The constant is sharp because it is already sharp in the horizontal tangent-plane class of Finn and Osserman
[
3
]
. In that class
W=1
at the center, so the normalized estimate reduces to their sharp curvature estimate. Hence the constant
\pi^{2}/2
cannot be lowered.
∎

Appendix A
An auxiliary odd coefficient estimate

This appendix records a sharp coefficient estimate that gives a quick proof of the Hopf estimate in a centrally symmetric boundary-regular setting. It is not used in the proof of the main theorem.

Lemma A.1

(Odd Hall averaging inequality)
.

Let
\theta:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be nondecreasing and satisfy

\theta(x+\pi)=\theta(x)+\pi.

For
0\leq\tau\leq\pi/2
, define

J(\tau)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin^{2}\frac{\theta(x+\tau)-\theta(x-\tau)}{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

If
M
is nonnegative and nonincreasing on
[0,\pi/2]
, then

\int_{0}^{\pi/2}M(\tau)J(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}M(\tau)\tau\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Proof.

This is the odd-period analogue of Hall’s Lemmas 2 and 3
in
[
4
]
. We give the details, since the form needed here is
not stated explicitly there.

First observe that if
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\geq 0
and

\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{N}\leq\pi,

then

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sin^{2}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{2}\leq 1.

Indeed, whenever
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
and
\alpha+\beta\leq\pi
,

\sin^{2}\frac{\alpha}{2}+\sin^{2}\frac{\beta}{2}\leq\sin^{2}\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2},

and the assertion follows by repeatedly combining terms.

Now let

\tau_{1}+\cdots+\tau_{N}=\frac{\pi}{2}.

Choose functions
y_{n}=y_{n}(x)
such that

y_{1}=x,\qquad y_{n}-\tau_{n}=y_{n-1}+\tau_{n-1},\qquad 2\leq n\leq N.

Set

\alpha_{n}(x)=\theta(y_{n}+\tau_{n})-\theta(y_{n}-\tau_{n}).

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\theta(y_{N}+\tau_{N})-\theta(y_{1}-\tau_{1})

\displaystyle=\pi,

because

(y_{N}+\tau_{N})-(y_{1}-\tau_{1})=2(\tau_{1}+\cdots+\tau_{N})=\pi

and
\theta(u+\pi)=\theta(u)+\pi
. Hence

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sin^{2}\frac{\alpha_{n}(x)}{2}\leq 1.

For each fixed
n
, the function

x\mapsto\sin^{2}\frac{\theta(x+\tau_{n})-\theta(x-\tau_{n})}{2}

is
\pi
-periodic. Indeed, by
\theta(x+\pi)=\theta(x)+\pi
,

\theta(x+\pi+\tau_{n})-\theta(x+\pi-\tau_{n})=\theta(x+\tau_{n})-\theta(x-\tau_{n}).

Since
y_{n}=x+\mathrm{const}
, integration over
0\leq x\leq 2\pi

therefore gives

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin^{2}\frac{\theta(y_{n}+\tau_{n})-\theta(y_{n}-\tau_{n})}{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=J(\tau_{n}).

Hence, after integrating

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sin^{2}\frac{\alpha_{n}(x)}{2}\leq 1

over
0\leq x\leq 2\pi
, we obtain

J(\tau_{1})+\cdots+J(\tau_{N})\leq 1.

Put

A=\frac{\pi}{2},\qquad L(\tau)=J(\tau)-\frac{\tau}{A}=J(\tau)-\frac{2\tau}{\pi},

and define

I(M)=\int_{0}^{A}M(\tau)L(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

It is enough to prove
I(M)\leq 0
.

For
B=A/2^{r}
and
0\leq\tau\leq B/2
, apply the preceding estimate to

2^{r}
copies of
\tau
and
2^{r}
copies of
B-\tau
. Since their total
sum is
A
, we get

2^{r}\{J(\tau)+J(B-\tau)\}\leq 1.

Equivalently,

L(\tau)+L(B-\tau)\leq 0.

We now use Hall’s dyadic folding argument. Let
M_{0}=M
. Having defined
a nonnegative nonincreasing function
M_{r}
supported on
[0,A/2^{r}]
, set

M_{r+1}(\tau)=\begin{cases}M_{r}(\tau)-M_{r}(A/2^{r}-\tau),&0\leq\tau\leq A/2^{r+1},\\[4.0pt]
0,&A/2^{r+1}<\tau\leq A.\end{cases}

Then
M_{r+1}
is again nonnegative and nonincreasing, and the preceding
two-point inequality gives

I(M_{r})\leq I(M_{r+1})\qquad r=0,1,2,\ldots.

Hence

I(M)\leq I(M_{r})\qquad r=0,1,2,\ldots.

It remains to let
r\to\infty
. Since

\sin^{2}\frac{u}{2}\leq\frac{u}{2},\qquad u\geq 0,

we have

J(\tau)\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bigl(\theta(x+\tau)-\theta(x-\tau)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x=\tau.

Here we used

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bigl(\theta(x+\tau)-\theta(x-\tau)\bigr)\,dx=4\pi\tau,

which follows from
\theta(x+2\pi)=\theta(x)+2\pi
.
Hence, by changes of variables,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\bigl(\theta(x+\tau)-\theta(x-\tau)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau}^{2\pi+\tau}\theta(u)\,\mathrm{d}u-\int_{-\tau}^{2\pi-\tau}\theta(u)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{2\pi-\tau}^{2\pi+\tau}\theta(u)\,\mathrm{d}u-\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\theta(u)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\theta(v+2\pi)\,\mathrm{d}v-\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\theta(v)\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\bigl(\theta(v)+2\pi\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}v-\int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\theta(v)\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=4\pi\tau.

Therefore

\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bigl(\theta(x+\tau)-\theta(x-\tau)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{4\pi\tau}{2\pi}=\tau.

Thus

I(M_{r})\leq\int_{0}^{A/2^{r}}M_{r}(\tau)J(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\int_{0}^{A/2^{r}}M(\tau)\tau\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

If the right-hand side in the desired inequality is infinite, there is
nothing to prove. Otherwise the last integral tends to
0
as

r\to\infty
. Therefore
I(M)\leq 0
, that is,

\int_{0}^{\pi/2}M(\tau)J(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}M(\tau)\tau\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

∎

Theorem A.2

(Odd boundary coefficient estimate)
.

Let

f=h+\overline{g}

be a sense-preserving harmonic diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{D}
onto
\mathbb{D}
, with

h(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n},\qquad g(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}z^{n}.

Assume that
f
extends to an orientation-preserving homeomorphism of

\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and that its boundary values are odd:

f(-\zeta)=-f(\zeta),\qquad|\zeta|=1.

Then

|a_{1}|^{2}+|b_{1}|^{2}\geq\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}.

The constant is sharp.

Proof.

Write the boundary values as

F(e^{\mathrm{i}t})=f(e^{\mathrm{i}t})=e^{\mathrm{i}\theta(t)},

where
\theta
is a nondecreasing lift satisfying

\theta(t+2\pi)=\theta(t)+2\pi.

Since the boundary map is odd, the lift may be chosen so that

\theta(t+\pi)=\theta(t)+\pi.

Let

c_{n}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F(e^{\mathrm{i}t})e^{-\mathrm{i}nt}\,\mathrm{d}t

be the Fourier coefficients of
F
. Since
F
is odd, all even Fourier
coefficients vanish. Moreover, from the Poisson representation of

f=h+\overline{g}
,

c_{1}=a_{1},\qquad c_{-1}=\overline{b_{1}}.

For
n\geq 1
, set

S_{n}=|c_{n}|^{2}+|c_{-n}|^{2}.

Hall’s autocorrelation identity gives

C(t):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos\bigl(\theta(s+t)-\theta(s-t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}S_{n}\cos(2nt).

Since only odd Fourier modes occur,

C(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}S_{2k+1}\cos\bigl(2(2k+1)t\bigr).

Therefore, by orthogonality on
[0,\pi/2]
,

S_{1}=\frac{4}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}C(t)\cos(2t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

The odd-mode expansion also gives

C(\pi/2-t)=-C(t).

Hence

S_{1}=\frac{8}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/4}C(t)\cos(2t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Define

J(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin^{2}\frac{\theta(s+t)-\theta(s-t)}{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Then

C(t)=1-2J(t).

Consequently,

S_{1}=\frac{8}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/4}\bigl(1-2J(t)\bigr)\cos(2t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Apply Lemma
A.1
with

M(t)=\begin{cases}\cos(2t),&0\leq t\leq\pi/4,\\
0,&\pi/4<t\leq\pi/2.\end{cases}

This
M
is nonnegative and nonincreasing on
[0,\pi/2]
. Hence

\int_{0}^{\pi/4}J(t)\cos(2t)\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/4}t\cos(2t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Therefore

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{8}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/4}\cos(2t)\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{16}{\pi}\cdot\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/4}t\cos(2t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}.

Because

S_{1}=|c_{1}|^{2}+|c_{-1}|^{2}=|a_{1}|^{2}+|b_{1}|^{2},

the desired estimate follows.

It remains to show sharpness. Consider the four-point boundary collapse

F_{\square}(e^{\mathrm{i}t})=\mathrm{i}^{k},\qquad\frac{k\pi}{2}\leq t<\frac{(k+1)\pi}{2},\qquad k=0,1,2,3.

This map is not itself a boundary homeomorphism, but it is the

L^{1}(\partial\mathbb{D})
-limit of odd orientation-preserving circle
homeomorphisms. By the Radó–Kneser–Choquet theorem, the Poisson
extensions of these approximating homeomorphisms are sense-preserving
harmonic diffeomorphisms of
\mathbb{D}
onto
\mathbb{D}
.

The Poisson extension of
F_{\square}
has, up to rotation,

h^{\prime}(z)=\frac{2(1-\mathrm{i})}{\pi}\frac{1}{1-z^{4}},\qquad g^{\prime}(z)=-\frac{2(1+\mathrm{i})}{\pi}\frac{z^{2}}{1-z^{4}}.

Thus

a_{1}=h^{\prime}(0)=\frac{2(1-\mathrm{i})}{\pi},\qquad b_{1}=g^{\prime}(0)=0.

Consequently,

|a_{1}|^{2}+|b_{1}|^{2}=\left|\frac{2(1-\mathrm{i})}{\pi}\right|^{2}=\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}.

The approximating odd boundary homeomorphisms therefore show that the
constant
8/\pi^{2}
cannot be improved.
∎

Theorem A.3

(Centrally symmetric graphs, boundary-regular form)
.

Let

S=\{(u,v,U(u,v)):(u,v)\in\mathbb{D}\}

be a non-parametric minimal graph, and let
\xi=(0,0,U(0,0))
. Assume

U(-u,-v)=2U(0,0)-U(u,v).

Assume in addition that the horizontal harmonic projection in a conformal parametrization extends to an odd orientation-preserving boundary homeomorphism of
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Then

W(\xi)^{2}|K(\xi)|\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}.

Proof.

Choose conformal parameters
X:\mathbb{D}\to S
,
X(0)=\xi
, and write

X(z)=(\operatorname{Re}f(z),\operatorname{Im}f(z),T(z)),\qquad f=h+\overline{g}.

By the assumed odd boundary extension and Theorem
A.2
,

|a_{1}|^{2}+|b_{1}|^{2}\geq\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}.

For a minimal graph, the Enneper–Weierstrass data can be written as

P=h^{\prime},\qquad\omega=\frac{g^{\prime}}{h^{\prime}}=q^{2},

where
q:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
is analytic. Thus

g^{\prime}=q^{2}P.

The curvature and slope factor are

K=-\frac{4|q^{\prime}|^{2}}{|P|^{2}(1+|q|^{2})^{4}},\qquad W=\frac{1+|q|^{2}}{1-|q|^{2}}.

Put
r=|q(0)|
. Then

W(0)^{2}|K(0)|=\frac{4|q^{\prime}(0)|^{2}}{|P(0)|^{2}(1-r^{2})^{2}(1+r^{2})^{2}}.

By Schwarz–Pick,

|q^{\prime}(0)|\leq 1-r^{2}.

Therefore

W(0)^{2}|K(0)|\leq\frac{4}{|P(0)|^{2}(1+r^{2})^{2}}.

Now

a_{1}=P(0),\qquad b_{1}=q(0)^{2}P(0),

so

|a_{1}|^{2}+|b_{1}|^{2}=|P(0)|^{2}(1+r^{4})\leq|P(0)|^{2}(1+r^{2})^{2}.

Using the coefficient estimate,

|P(0)|^{2}(1+r^{2})^{2}\geq\frac{8}{\pi^{2}}.

Substituting gives

W(0)^{2}|K(0)|\leq\frac{4}{8/\pi^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}.

∎

Appendix B
The lower bound in the Scherk family

The derivative form gives a convenient expression for the normalized curvature. Let

\displaystyle S_{A,B}(U)

\displaystyle=(A+B)\sin(\pi U)+B\kappa\sin(\pi M(U))+A\varepsilon\sin(\pi N(U)).

Then the Scherk-family identity can be written as

W^{2}|K|=\frac{\pi^{2}(1+AB)}{S_{A,B}(U)^{2}}.

The scalar theorem says
S_{A,B}(U)\geq\sqrt{2(1+AB)}
, which gives the upper bound. The following estimate gives the lower bound.

Proposition B.1

(Lower bound)
.

For every admissible Scherk-type comparison graph,

W^{2}|K|\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}.

Consequently,

\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\leq W^{2}|K|\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}

throughout the admissible Scherk-type family.

Proof.

By admissibility,

0\leq U\leq 1,\qquad 0\leq M(U)\leq U,\qquad 0\leq N(U)\leq 1-U.

Thus all sine factors in
S_{A,B}(U)
are at most
1
, and

S_{A,B}(U)\leq A+B+B\kappa+A\varepsilon.

We claim that

A+B+B\kappa+A\varepsilon\leq 2\sqrt{1+AB}.

Indeed, using
A^{2}+\kappa^{2}=1
and
B^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}=1
, one verifies the identity

\displaystyle 4(1+AB)-(A+B+B\kappa+A\varepsilon)^{2}

\displaystyle=(\kappa\varepsilon+\kappa+\varepsilon-1-AB)^{2}\geq 0.

Therefore

S_{A,B}(U)^{2}\leq 4(1+AB).

Since

W^{2}|K|=\frac{\pi^{2}(1+AB)}{S_{A,B}(U)^{2}},

we obtain

W^{2}|K|\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}.

The upper bound is Theorem
1.2
.
∎

Appendix C
Bernstein-polynomial checks

We use the following elementary positivity criterion.

Lemma C.1

(Bernstein positivity)
.

Let
P(t,v)
be a polynomial on
[0,1]^{2}
. Suppose that for some bidegree
(m,n)
it has a Bernstein expansion

P(t,v)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{n}p_{ij}\binom{m}{i}t^{i}(1-t)^{m-i}\binom{n}{j}v^{j}(1-v)^{n-j}

with all
p_{ij}\geq 0
. Then
P(t,v)\geq 0
on
[0,1]^{2}
.

Lemma C.2
.

For
0\leq A,B\leq 1
,

Y(A,B):=(2+AB-A^{2})(2+AB-B^{2})-2(A+B)\geq 0.

Proof.

Put

A=1-t,\qquad B=1-v,\qquad 0\leq t,v\leq 1.

Then
Y(1-t,1-v)
has the Bernstein expansion of bidegree
(3,3)
,

Y(1-t,1-v)=\sum_{i=0}^{3}\sum_{j=0}^{3}y_{ij}\binom{3}{i}t^{i}(1-t)^{3-i}\binom{3}{j}v^{j}(1-v)^{3-j},

where

(y_{ij})=\begin{pmatrix}0&2/3&1/3&0\\
2/3&2&20/9&2\\
1/3&20/9&28/9&10/3\\
0&2&10/3&4\end{pmatrix}.

All coefficients are nonnegative, so
Y\geq 0
by Lemma
C.1
.
∎

Lemma C.3
.

Let

C=2+AB-A^{2}

and define

Z(A,B)=C\left[2(C-A-B)+\frac{A+B}{2}(1-AB)\right]-\frac{5}{2}(1-A^{2})(1+AB).

Then

Z(A,B)\geq 0,\qquad 0\leq A,B\leq 1.

Proof.

Put

A=1-t,\qquad B=1-v,\qquad 0\leq t,v\leq 1.

Then
2Z(1-t,1-v)
has the Bernstein expansion of bidegree
(4,4)
,

2Z(1-t,1-v)=\sum_{i=0}^{4}\sum_{j=0}^{4}z_{ij}\binom{4}{i}t^{i}(1-t)^{4-i}\binom{4}{j}v^{j}(1-v)^{4-j},

where

(z_{ij})=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&4/3&5/4&1\\
0&9/8&41/24&15/8&7/4\\
10/3&55/12&49/9&143/24&37/6\\
5&103/16&23/3&139/16&19/2\\
5&13/2&8&19/2&11\end{pmatrix}.

All coefficients are nonnegative, so
Z\geq 0
by Lemma
C.1
.
∎

Remark C.4
.

The displayed Bernstein expansions are polynomial identities. They can be verified directly by expanding both sides in the monomial basis.

Appendix D
Log-subharmonicity of the normalized curvature

Let

X(z)=\operatorname{Re}\int^{z}\bigl(1-g^{2},\mathrm{i}(1+g^{2}),2g\bigr)\Phi,\qquad\Phi=\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z,

be a conformal minimal immersion. Then

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=(1+|g|^{2})^{2}|\varphi|^{2}|\mathrm{d}z|^{2},

and

K=-\frac{4|g^{\prime}|^{2}}{|\varphi|^{2}(1+|g|^{2})^{4}}.

For an upward oriented graph,
|g|<1
, and

W=\frac{1+|g|^{2}}{1-|g|^{2}}.

Consequently,

W^{2}|K|=\frac{4|g^{\prime}|^{2}}{|\varphi|^{2}(1-|g|^{4})^{2}}.

Lemma D.1

(Log-subharmonicity)
.

On the set where
g^{\prime}\neq 0
,

\Delta\log(W^{2}|K|)=\frac{32|g|^{2}|g^{\prime}|^{2}}{(1-|g|^{4})^{2}}\geq 0.

Thus,
W^{2}|K|
is log-subharmonic away from branch points of
g
; across zeros of
g^{\prime}
the statement holds in the distributional sense.

Proof.

We have

\log(W^{2}|K|)=\log 4+2\log\left|\frac{g^{\prime}}{\varphi}\right|-2\log(1-|g|^{4}).

Away from zeros of
g^{\prime}
, the middle term is harmonic. A direct radial calculation gives

\Delta\bigl[-2\log(1-|g|^{4})\bigr]=\frac{32|g|^{2}|g^{\prime}|^{2}}{(1-|g|^{4})^{2}}.

The extension across zeros of
g^{\prime}
follows from the distributional subharmonicity of
\log|g^{\prime}|
.
∎

Remark D.2
.

By contrast,

\Delta\log|K|=-\frac{16|g^{\prime}|^{2}}{(1+|g|^{2})^{2}}\leq 0

away from zeros of
g^{\prime}
. Thus,
|K|
is log-superharmonic in the Gauss-map variable, whereas
W^{2}|K|
is log-subharmonic. This observation is useful conceptually, but it does not replace the zero equation
f(z_{\circ})=0
, because
z_{\circ}
itself depends on the Scherk parameters.
