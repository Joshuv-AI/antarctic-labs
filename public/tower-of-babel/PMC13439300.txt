Gastric cancer suspected on endoscopy at a referring hospital
Authors: Ji Yong Ahn
Published: 2026

Quiz

A 45-year-old woman was referred to a tertiary hospital for further evaluation after an abnormal finding was detected during esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) at a primary care clinic. She had experienced symptoms of epigastric pain and heartburn for one month. Despite treatment with proton pump inhibitors, her symptoms did not improve. EGD performed at the referring hospital initially suggested gastric cancer, revealing an ulceroinfiltrating mass extending from the mid body to the antrum and multiple lesions in the fundus and body that were suspicious for gastric metastases (Fig. 1). Abdominal computed tomography was also performed at the referring hospital and revealed enlarged perigastric lymph nodes. The differential diagnosis included reactive lymphadenopathy, metastatic lymph nodes, and, less likely, lymphoma. However, histopathological examination at the referring hospital revealed an ulcer with inflamed granulation tissue and marked chronic active gastritis with erosion and regenerative atypia.

Initial endoscopic findings from the referring hospital. (A) Friable mucosa and shallow ulcers are observed along the lesser curvature of the gastric body. (B) Diffuse mucosal edema, infiltrative changes, mucosal friability, and spontaneous bleeding are observed in the antrum. (C) Yellowish discoloration and multiple elevated lesions are observed in the gastric body.

After referral to the tertiary hospital, laboratory evaluation revealed a hemoglobin level of 12.7 g/dL, which was within the normal range, and a mildly elevated C-reactive protein level of 0.76 mg/dL. Other laboratory findings were unremarkable. At the tertiary hospital, repeat EGD was performed, and multiple biopsies were obtained; the histological findings are shown in Figure 2.

Histopathological findings of the gastric lesion. (A) Hematoxylin and eosin staining shows dense chronic inflammatory cell infiltration in the lamina propria, predominantly composed of lymphocytes and numerous plasma cells. Glandular distortion and regenerative epithelial atypia are observed, along with features suggestive of chronic active gastritis. Focal vascular changes, including wall thickening and luminal narrowing, are also noted (×200). (B) Immunohistochemical staining for Treponema pallidum demonstrates numerous slender, spiral-shaped organisms highlighted in brown within the gastric mucosa and glandular structures. Representative spirochetes are indicated by black arrows. The organisms are diffusely distributed along the epithelial surface and within the lamina propria, supporting the diagnosis of gastric syphilis (×200).

What is the most likely diagnosis?

Answer

Following a repeat biopsy, the final diagnosis was confirmed as gastric syphilis based on histopathological findings (Fig. 2A) and immunohistochemical staining that demonstrated numerous spirochetes consistent with Treponema pallidum (Fig. 2B). The patient received three once-weekly doses of benzathine penicillin, resulting in clinical and endoscopic improvement (Fig. 3). She is currently being followed up in the outpatient clinic without complications.

Follow-up endoscopic findings after antibiotic treatment. (A) Marked improvement of the previously noted edematous and infiltrative lesion were shown at the antrum area. (B) Healed mucosa with residual mild erythema and no evidence of active ulceration or bleeding were shown at the body area. (C) Near-complete resolution multiple elevated yellowish lesions were shown at the body area.

Gastric syphilis is a rare manifestation of systemic syphilis that typically occurs during the secondary or tertiary stages. It is caused by the hematogenous dissemination of T. pallidum and can involve the gastric mucosa, most commonly affecting the antrum and pylorus. The clinical presentation of gastric syphilis is often nonspecific and includes epigastric pain, nausea, vomiting, and weight loss.1 Some patients may present with anemia or gastrointestinal bleeding. Because of these nonspecific symptoms, it is frequently misdiagnosed as a gastrointestinal disease, particularly gastric cancer2 or gastric lymphoma.3

Endoscopically, gastric syphilis can present with a wide spectrum of findings, including diffuse mucosal erythema, multiple erosions, ulcerative lesions, nodularity, or thickened folds.4 In some cases, it may appear as a linitis plastica–like infiltrative lesion that closely mimics advanced gastric cancer.2 Yellowish discoloration and multiple elevated lesions, as observed in our case, can provide important diagnostic clues, although these findings are not specific.

Histologically, gastric syphilis is characterized by dense inflammatory infiltrates in the lamina propria, often rich in plasma cells, and features of chronic active gastritis. Regenerative atypia of the epithelium may also be observed and can be misinterpreted as malignancy.

A definitive diagnosis requires the identification of T. pallidum in tissue. Special staining methods, such as Warthin–Starry silver staining, have historically been used; however, immunohistochemical staining for T. pallidum is currently considered the most sensitive and specific method.5

Gastric syphilis is a medically treatable condition, and prompt diagnosis is important to avoid unnecessary invasive procedures. Penicillin remains the treatment of choice, and most patients show rapid clinical and endoscopic improvement following appropriate antibiotic therapy.

In conclusion, as demonstrated in our case, gastric syphilis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of diffuse ulcerative or infiltrative gastric lesions, especially when the endoscopic and histological findings are inconclusive for malignancy. Early recognition and appropriate diagnostic evaluation, including immunohistochemistry, are crucial for accurate diagnosis and effective treatment.
