Advancing photocytotoxicity evaluation of nitric oxide derivative-ruthenium complex in 3D biofabricated breast cancer models
Authors: Amanda Blanque Becceneri, Suihong Liu, Sendegul Yildirim, Myoung Hwan Kim, Youdi Liu, Ilayda Namli, Todd M. Umstead, Zissis C. Chroneos, Roberto Santana da Silva, Ibrahim T. Ozbolat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cancer remains a major health challenge, emphasizing the need for innovative therapies. Photodynamic therapy (PDT) uses light-activated photosensitizers (PSs) to induce targeted cancer cell death, but clinical translation is limited by low tumor specificity, toxicity, and inadequate in vitro models. Three-dimensional (3D) tumor models provide physiologically relevant platforms that enhance translational predictability. Here, we evaluated a promising photoactivated ruthenium-based PS, {TPyP [Ru(NO2) (bpy)2]4}(PF6)4 complex (RuNO2TPyP), across 3D cancer models of increasing complexity, including free-standing spheroids, bioprinted spheroids within hydrogel matrix, and bioprinted patient-derived organoids (PDOs). The RuNO2TPyP complex triggered significant cell death and apoptosis while reducing tumor aggressiveness following PDT. Its efficacy was model-dependent: effective at 0.19 μM in free-standing spheroids, requiring higher concentrations (0.78 μM) in bioprinted spheroids embedded within a hydrogel matrix that mimics the tumor extracellular microenvironment, and showing substantial resistance in bioprinted PDOs (1.56 μM). These findings underscore the significance of bioprinted 3D models and establish a basis for future investigations of this and related PSs in personalized cancer therapy.

Introduction

Cancer is a leading global cause of death, with its rising incidence driven by aging populations, lifestyle changes, and advancements in diagnostics [1]. This rise underscores the need for innovative treatments with minimal side effects. One promising treatment is photodynamic therapy (PDT), a minimally invasive local therapy that is based on a reaction between three essential components: a photosensitizer (PS), oxygen, and light at a specific wavelength. This reaction can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as singlet oxygen (1O2), which induce localized cell death. The effectiveness of PDT depends heavily on PS properties, which should ideally exhibit selective activation upon light exposure, minimal toxicity in the dark, and improved biocompatibility to maximize therapeutic outcomes. Despite significant advances in PS development, many current PSs face critical challenges, such as low specificity, slow clearance, low quantum yield, and high toxicity [2]. Ruthenium-based complexes have emerged as a highly promising PS for PDT, owing to their distinctive properties. These complexes are particularly advantageous due to their selective targeting of cancer cells, low systemic toxicity, and the ability to be synthesized with a diverse range of ligands, thereby offering tunable pharmacological characteristics [3]. In this way, one of the most explored approaches involves designing ruthenium compounds capable of producing ROS and nitric oxide (NO), thereby inducing synergistic effects and demonstrating that their combined action is significantly more effective [[4], [5], [6]]. NO has been suggested to play a pivotal role in cancer cytotoxicity, with the potential to overcome tumor cell resistance [[7], [8], [9]]. Among various candidates, {TPyP [Ru(NO2) (bpy)2]4}4+, referred to as RuNO2TPyP, exhibited markedly higher photo-cytotoxicity in certain cancer cell lines due NO release by redox-stimulated process followed by light irradiation at 415 nm generating ROS (Scheme S1, Supporting information, SI) as previously described [10]. Structurally, this complex comprises a porphyrin core coordinated to nitro-ruthenium moieties, characterized in our previous work [10].

Although this compound demonstrates promising potential, assessing their true therapeutic efficacy in vivo remains a significant challenge. Currently, PSs effectiveness is primarily evaluated through traditional two-dimensional (2D) culture and animal models. However, these models fail to recapitulate the complexity of human microenvironment, leading to a substantial reduction in the efficacy of these compounds when translated into clinical settings [[11], [12], [13]]. To overcome these limitations, three-dimensional (3D) models have emerged as a promising alternative, providing significant advantages over traditional 2D cultures. By supporting essential cell–cell and cell–extracellular matrix (ECM) interactions, 3D models more faithfully replicate the in vivo tumor microenvironment. Additionally, 3D models could replicate the physiological gradients of oxygen, nutrients, and metabolic waste, which critically influence cellular behavior, proliferation, and therapeutic response. This enhanced physiological relevance makes 3D models a valuable platform for investigating tumor biology and evaluating the efficacy of anticancer therapies [12,14,15].

Among 3D models, spheroids are widely used as multicellular aggregates that better recapitulate key aspects of the tumor microenvironment [16]. Depending on the fabrication method and cell composition, spheroids provide a versatile system to study interactions among cancer cells, fibroblasts, immune cells, and endothelial cells, all of which contribute to tumor progression [13,16]. Nevertheless, free-standing spheroids often fail to reproduce the spatial organization and ECM interactions characteristic of native tumors, highlighting the need for more advanced 3D culture systems [17]. To overcome these limitations, 3D bioprinting offers a promising solution by enabling the precise deposition of cells, spheroids, and biomaterials to generate complex, physiologically relevant constructs that more faithfully mimic in vivo conditions [18,19]. Recent advances, such as aspiration-assisted bioprinting (AAB), provide precise control over spheroid positioning in 3D space, further enhancing reproducibility, structural precision, and the physiological relevance of the resulting tissue models [20,21]. Hence, integrating spheroids with bioprinting facilitates the development of advanced cancer models, offering more accurate and predictive platforms for evaluating therapeutic interventions. Complementing these approaches, patient-derived organoids (PDOs), obtained directly from primary tumor samples, more faithfully capture the genetic heterogeneity and histopathological architecture of native tumors, serving as reliable platforms for testing personalized treatment strategies [22].

Therefore, we developed three progressively complex 3D breast tumor models using bioprinting to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the photoactivated ruthenium complex (RuNO2TPyP), as illustrated in Fig. 1. Upon irradiation at 415 nm, the complex could generate ROS, singlet oxygen (1O2), and releases NO. In previous 2D models, RuNO2TPyP exhibited minimal dark cytotoxicity but pronounced photocytotoxicity against melanoma (B16-F10) and lung cancer (A549) cells, inducing morphological alterations, ROS generation, and apoptosis upon light irradiation (4 J cm−2) [10,23]. Building on these findings, we here investigated the effects of RuNO2TPyP in homotypic and heterotypic 3D breast cancer spheroids, bioprinted heterotypic spheroids embedded in collagen–fibrin hydrogel matrix, and bioprinted PDOs, establishing physiologically relevant platforms to more accurately evaluate PDT and its potential for clinical translation.Fig. 1The schematic diagram of preparation of different 3D cancer models and the processing of utilizing PDT to explore the effects of the RuNO2TPyP complex.

Materials and methods

Photoactivated ruthenium complex

The RuNO2TPyP complex was synthesized and confirmed as described previously [10]. Additional functional characterization relevant to this study was performed. The generation of 1O2 was evaluated using the 9,10-dimethylanthracene (DMA) assay, with further details provided in the SI. The complex was solubilized in 100% dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) at room temperature (RT) to prepare a stock solution with a concentration of 10 mM. This stock solution was then diluted in Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM, Corning, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) to achieve final treatment concentrations ranging from 0.09 to 1.56 μM, depending on the assay. The maximum final concentration of DMSO in the treatment medium did not exceed 1%. Treatments for all groups were conducted under standard culture conditions for 24 h cultivation at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Spheroid fabrication

Cell source and cultivation

Green fluorescent protein (GFP)-tagged MDA-MB-231 (GFP + MDA-MB-231) cells and MDA-MB-231 cells, a triple-negative breast cancer cell line, were kindly provided by Dr. Danny Welch from University of Kansas (Kansas City, KS), and human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs), a non-tumoral cell type purchased from Lonza (Walkersville, MD), were used as supporting cells in cancer models. GFP + MDA-MB-231 cells and HDFs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% glutamine, 1% sodium pyruvate, and 1% antibiotics (penicillin-streptomycin, P/S), in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C, and the media were changed every 2–3 days. When cultures reached 80% confluence, cells were detached using TrypLE™ Express Enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and either subcultured for continued expansion or harvested for experiments.

Preparation of 3D spheroids

For 3D cytotoxicity studies, three different types of spheroids were prepared: homotypic spheroids comprised of 2400 pure GFP + MDA-MB-231 cells, homotypic spheroids comprised of pure 2400 HDFs, and heterotypic spheroids comprised of mixed 800 GFP + MDA-MB-231 cells and 1600 HDFs. These 3D spheroids were prepared according to previous protocol [24]. Briefly, cells were detached using TrypLE™ Express Enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and homogenously seeded into Cell-Repellent 96-well plate with a round (U) bottom (Greiner Bio-One, Kremsmünster, Austria) with 100 μL of supplemented DMEM medium per well, the 3D spheroids formed and were harvested after 24 h cultivation.

Design and fabrication of twin-well devices for bystander and bioprinting with spheroids

The custom-made twin-well devices were designed specifically for conducting bystander and bioprinting experiments, which were made of polylactic acid (PLA) using a 3D printer (Qidi Tech X-Max, QIDI Technology, China). A twin-well device consisted of two wells integrated into a single structure. The wells were separated by a 3 mm-high solid partition at the bottom. Above this barrier, the structure remained open, forming a 4 mm-high open bridge connecting the wells at the top. This design allowed the wells to remain physically separated under standard conditions, but enabled fluid overflow between them when a larger volume of medium was added. The total height of the device was 7 mm. This setup was particularly advantageous for targeted treatments, as it enables the irradiation of either both wells or a single well, with the interconnected design being specifically useful for studying bystander effects when only a single well was irradiated. The devices were modeled in Autodesk® Fusion 360, and 3D printing was controlled by Repetier Host software (Willich, Germany). The devices were glued with SYLGARD™ 184 Silicone Elastomer (DOW, Midland, MI) onto a 22 mm × 22 mm glass slide (VWR, Radnor, PA). For the sterilization, the devices were treated with 70% ethanol, and then ultraviolet (UV) irradiation was applied for 1 h in a laminar flow hood. For bystander assays, devices were sterilized and treated with 200 μL per well of anti-adherence rinsing solution (Stemcell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) to prevent spheroid attachment. The solution was removed prior to spheroid placement.

Aspiration-assisted bioprinting of tumor spheroids

To establish complex cancer models with native-mimicking ECM environment, the heterotypic spheroids were bioprinted within hydrogel matrix via using aspiration-assisted bioprinting according to our previous protocol [24]. Briefly, spheroids were aspirated from a Petri dish containing the culture medium using a 32 G blunt nozzle with an inner diameter of 100 μm and vacuum pressure of 10-15 mmHg. The hydrogel composition used in bioprinting followed a method described previously [24], where the hydrogel was prepared on ice by combining 5 μL Dulbecco's phosphate buffered saline (DPBS) (10 ×), 0.51 μL sodium hydroxide (1 N) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), 22.3 μL DMEM, 50 μL fibrinogen (6 mg/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich), 22.3 μL collagen (9 mg/mL) (extracted from rat tails according to a previous protocol [25]) and 1.5 U/mL thrombin (50 U/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich), achieving a final concentration of 2 mg/mL collagen and 3 mg/mL fibrin. The first layer of hydrogel was added into the device until fully covered its surface prior to 3D bioprinting, and the additional hydrogel layer was added after bioprinted spheroids. The devices were then incubated for 15 min (37 °C) to allow hydrogel crosslinking, followed by incubation under standard culture conditions.

Bioprinting of patient-derived organoids

Generation of type 1 medium

Type 1 medium was used for the culture of PDOs, as described previously [26]. It was composed of 10% conditioned medium of R-spondin1 (produced in-house) and Noggin (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ), supplemented with 1× B27 with Vitamin A (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 10 mM nicotinamide (Sigma-Aldrich), 1.25 mM N-acetyl-ʟ-cysteine (Sigma-Aldrich), and 100 μg/mL of Primocin (InvivoGen, San Diego, CA). Additionally, the medium included 5 μM of Y-27632 dihydrochloride (AbMole Bioscience, Houston, TX, USA), 5 nM of Heregulin β1 (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ), 5 ng/mL of FGF-7 (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ), 20 ng/mL of FGF-10 (PeproTech), 0.5 μM of A83-01 (Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK), 5 ng/mL of EGF (PeproTech), and 1 μM of SB202190 (Sigma-Aldrich).

Isolation, cultivation of patient-derived breast cancer cells and preparation of PDOs

Fresh HER2+ breast tumor tissue (0.92 g) from a female donor (67 years) was provided by the Cooperative Human Tissue Network (CHTN) in deidentified form. Following surgical removal, tissues were stored on ice in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) culture medium supplemented with penicillin, streptomycin and antifungal, and shipped overnight. Upon arrival, tissues were processed according to a protocol described previously [26]. Briefly, tissues were photographed and minced into smaller pieces (0.5–1 mm3). For tissue digestion, a solution containing type 1 medium, collagenase type II (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (1 mg/mL) and Y-27632 dihydrochloride (10 μM) was added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C under agitation. After digestion, the material was filtered through a 100-μm strainer for three times to maximize cell recovery. Then, after centrifugation at 450 × g for 5 min at 8 °C, the resulting pellet was washed, homogenized, treated with red blood cell lysis buffer (Sigma-Aldrich), and resuspended in basement membrane extract (BME) type II (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN). The cell suspension was plated as drops (15 μL) into 24-well plates, solidified in an incubator at 37 °C for 1 h, and then type 1 medium was added to facilitate the PDO formation.

Passaging of PDOs

PDOs were harvested 1 h after with the addition of dispase II (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (2 mg/mL) in DMEM/F12 at 37 °C. After 30 min of incubation, the mixture was pipetted to facilitate the digestion of BME. Once BME was fully dissolved, DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS was added to neutralize dispase II. The content was then collected and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. The organoids were gently resuspended in fresh BME and replated.

3D bioprinting of PDOs

Similarly, PDOs were harvested through cultivation within DMEM/F12 with dispase II (2 mg/mL) at 37 °C. Once BME was fully dissolved, PDOs were centrifuged to remove the dispase II solution and then gently resuspended in TrypLE™ Express Enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for dissociation. The suspension was incubated at 37 °C for 8 min, followed by the addition of DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS to neutralize it. The cells were centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was removed to isolate the cell pellet. The pellet was carefully transferred into a pulled glass pipette connected to a gas-tight syringe pre-filled with type 1 medium to minimize dead volume. The extrusion of cell pellet was controlled using a syringe pump (New Era Instruments, Suffolk County, NY). The bioprinting process was carried out in a 3D-printed device loaded with a 1:1 mixture of BME and type I medium. The cell pellet was precisely extruded into BME within the 3D-printed mold at a volume of 0.005 μL type I medium and a flow rate of 5 μL/min. After extrusion, the sample was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to solidify BME, followed by the gentle addition of 70 μL of the PDO medium on top of BME for cultivation.

Photodynamic therapy

For PDT, a 415 nm light emitting diode (LED) plate with an irradiance of 0.05023 W cm−2 was used to irradiate cells to ensure a dose of 16 J cm−2 (irradiation time of 5.31 min) at RT. Free-standing spheroids, bioprinted spheroids, and PDOs were incubated with the RuNO2TPyP complex for 24 h as described in Section 2.1. After this period, the treatment medium was removed, and samples were washed with PBS. DMEM supplemented medium without phenol red (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was then added, and irradiation was performed in the absence of the compound. Following irradiation, free-standing spheroids, bioprinted spheroids and PDOs were maintained for 24, 48 and 72 h, respectively, at 37 °C in 5% CO2.

Characterization and evaluation of treatment efficacy

Morphology assay

Heterotypic and homotypic free-standing spheroids, bioprinted spheroids, and PDOs were treated with the RuNO2TPyP complex at different concentrations (heterotypic spheroids: 0.19, 0.39, 0.78, and 1.56 μM, while homotypic spheroids as a control: 0.39 μM. Bioprinted spheroids and PDOs were treated with 0.39, 0.78, and 1.56 μM, based on the responses observed in pure spheroids.) for 24 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Following the treatment, all groups were irradiated with a dose of 16 J cm−2, as previously described in Section 2.4. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (AxioObserver, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Baden-Württemberg, Germany) and quantifications were conducted using ImageJ® (The National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).

Viability assay

Heterotypic spheroids were exposed to different concentrations of the RuNO2TPyP complex, as described above. Next, the complex was removed, and spheroids were either irradiated (16 J cm−2) or kept in the dark, followed by an additional 24 h incubation. After the treatment, 10% alamarBlue™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific) solution was added to each well and incubated for 3 h. Finally, the fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader (Tecan, Morrisville, NC) with excitation and emission wavelengths of 570 and 585 nm, respectively. The IC50 calculation was performed using GraphPad® Prism software, version 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Immunofluorescence staining

Heterotypic spheroids were treated with 0.39 or 0.78 μM of the RuNO2TPyP complex for 24 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2, followed by irradiation at 16 J cm−2. After an additional 24 h of incubation, spheroids were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C overnight. Following fixation, they were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 15 min at RT and blocked for 1 h in PBS containing 10% normal goat serum, 0.3 M glycine, 0.1% Tween 20, and 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Primary antibody incubation was performed using a rat monoclonal Ki-67 antibody (1:100, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) overnight at 4 °C. After washing twice with PBS, spheroids were incubated with a goat anti-rat IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 594) secondary antibody (1:200, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 2 h at RT in the dark. 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining was applied for nuclear visualization, and spheroids were washed, mounted on glass slides, and imaged using a Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope.

Immunofluorescence staining for PDOs was conducted based on a previously established protocol [27] with modifications. Briefly, bioprinted PDOs cultured in BME within twin-well devices were fixed with 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight. After fixation, PFA was removed, and the wells were washed with PBT (PBS + 0.1% Tween 20) and incubated for 10 min at 4 °C. Organoids were then blocked in an organoid washing buffer (OWB: PBS + 0.1% Triton X-100 + 0.2% BSA) at 4 °C for 2 h. Next, 200 μL of OWB containing a rat monoclonal Ki-67 antibody (1:50, Invitrogen) was added, and samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking. After incubation, OWB containing the primary antibody was removed, and the wells were washed twice with fresh OWB, each wash lasting 2 h with gentle shaking. Then, 200 μL of OWB containing the goat anti-rat IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 594) secondary antibody (1:100, Abcam) was added, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with gentle shaking. After the secondary antibody incubation, the samples were washed twice with fresh OWB, each washing lasting 2 h with gentle shaking. DAPI was then added for 1 h, followed by two additional washes with fresh OWB. Finally, imaging was performed using the Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope.

Caspase-3/7 activity assay

Free-standing spheroids, bioprinted spheroids, and PDOs were treated with the RuNO2TPyP complex at different concentrations. Apoptosis was evaluated at different time points according to the structural characteristics of each 3D model and previous observations related to treatment response. In free-standing spheroids, the assessment was performed 6 h after irradiation to detect early apoptotic activity. These spheroids lack ECM, which facilitates complex diffusion and accelerates cell response. Moreover, morphological and viability analyses performed at 24 h post-irradiation had already shown a substantial loss of cell viability at the tested concentrations. In bioprinted spheroids, apoptosis was measured at 24 h post-irradiation, considering their higher cellular density and the presence of ECM, which may delay the onset of apoptotic responses. For PDOs, the evaluation was performed at 72 h, a time point selected based on the progressive morphological alterations observed after treatment, which became more evident at this later stage and are indicative of apoptosis. At the respective evaluation times, 75 μL of reconstituted Caspase-Glo® 3/7 3D buffer and substrate mixture (Promega, Madison, WI) were added to each sample. After 30-min incubation at RT, the solution was removed, and luminescence was measured using a microplate reader (Tecan, Morrisville, NC). Luminescence values of treated samples were compared to those of non-treated controls.

Dissociation of free-standing spheroids into single cells for viability, migration, and clonogenic assays

Homotypic GFP + MDA-MB-231 spheroids were treated with 0.39 μM of the RuNO2TPyP complex, as described earlier. After the incubation, spheroids were dissociated into single cells using TrypLE™ Express Enzyme, which was added for 5 min at 37 °C. Then, cells were centrifuged and the pellet was resuspended in fresh medium and counted. Finally, single cells were reseeded into appropriate culture plates to perform viability, migration and clonogenic assays. For the viability assay, single GFP + MDA-MB-231 cells (6 × 105 cells/well) derived from spheroids were plated in 12-well plates and allowed to proliferate for 10 days until reaching full confluence at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Next, 10% alamarBlue™ solution was added to each well and incubated for 3 h, following the same procedure described in Section 2.5.2. For the migration assay, after reaching confluence under the same conditions, a scratch was made across the cell monolayer using a 100-μL pipette tip. Images of the scratch area were taken at 0, 24, and 48 h using an EVOS FL Auto microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The initial wound area (0 h) was compared to those at 24 and 48 h, with measurements performed using ImageJ. The previously described formula [28] was used to calculate the percentage of wound closure, as follows: % of wound closure = [(At = 0h – A t = Δh)/At = 0h] × 100%, where, At = 0h is the area of wound measured after the scratch was made, and At = Δh is the area measured 24 or 48 h after. For the clonogenic assay, single GFP + MDA-MB-231 cells (300 cells/well) derived from spheroids were counted and seeded into 6-well plates. The cells were incubated for 15 days to allow for colony formation. After incubation, colonies were fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min and stained with 0.5% methylene blue for 15 min at RT. Excess stain was removed with distilled water and images of the colonies were captured with a camera (Apple 15 Pro, Apple, USA).

Cytokine analysis

Interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) were analyzed in the supernatant collected from bioprinted spheroids. The supernatant was collected 48 h after light irradiation for analysis. Cytokines were measured using the R&D Systems Luminex Discovery Assay Human Premixed Multi-Analyte Kit (Catalog number LXSAHM) following the manufacturer's instructions for cell culture supernatants, with non-treated spheroids serving as the control. Briefly, 50 μL of diluted microparticle cocktail was added to each well, followed by incubation at RT for 2 h on a shaker at 800 rpm. Then, 50 μL of supernatant was added to each well. The wells were washed three times by adding 100 μL of Wash Buffer. Next, 50 μL of diluted Biotin-Antibody Cocktail was added, and the plate was incubated at RT for 1 h with shaking at 800 rpm, followed by another wash. 50 μL of diluted Streptavidin-PE was then added and incubated for 30 min at RT with shaking. After a final wash, 100 μL of Wash Buffer was added, and the plate was incubated for 2 min at RT with shaking before being analyzed within 90 min using a Luminex® analyzer.

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 software (San Diego, CA). For comparisons between more than two groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Dunnett's multiple comparisons test to compare the treated groups with the non-treated group. When comparing two groups, an unpaired Student's T-test was utilized. Statistical significance was defined as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.

Results and discussions

PDT on free-standing spheroids

The RuNO2TPyP complex was able to produce 1O2 upon light irradiation, as measured by a chemical probe-based assay (Fig. S1, SI), with a quantum yield (ΦΔ) of 0.281 ± 0.021. Therefore, its photodynamic effects were evaluated in heterotypic free-standing spheroids of GFP + MDA-MB-231/HDF. These spheroids were treated with different concentrations of the RuNO2TPyP complex (0.19, 0.39, 0.78, and 1.56 μM) for 24 h, followed by an irradiation dose of 16 J cm−2. Morphological analysis showed significant alterations in the treated and irradiated spheroids (Fig. 2A), with a decrease in GFP intensity at higher concentrations, indicating reduced cell viability (Fig. 2B). To ensure that changes in GFP signal were not due to direct photophysical effects, additional cytotoxicity assays were performed comparing MDA-MB-231 and GFP + MDA-MB-231 cells in 2D under identical experimental conditions (Fig. S2, SI). Both cell lines exhibited highly comparable viability profiles, confirming that GFP expression did not interfere with the irradiation treatment of the RuNO2TPyP complex in this work. These findings were further supported by the alamarBlue™ viability results, which showed that the complex caused a concentration-dependent decrease in cell viability upon irradiation, confirming its photocytotoxic properties with an IC50 of 0.44 ± 0.10 μM (Fig. 2C). Our findings were consistent with both our 2D results (Fig. S2, SI) and previous reports of the RuNO2TPyP complex across different cell types in 2D cultures. For instance, in A549 cells, the complex showed high photocytotoxicity with an IC50 of 0.59 μM after 4 h of incubation and irradiation at 4 J cm−2 [23]. Similarly, in B16-F10 cells, an IC50 of 0.47 ± 0.12 μM was observed under comparable conditions [10]. Although these cell lines represent different tumor types, these studies support the general photoresponsive behavior of the complex across distinct biological systems. However, in 3D models like spheroids, our results indicated the need for a longer incubation time and significantly higher irradiation doses (16 J cm−2) to achieve similar efficacy. This can be attributed to the challenges posed by the 3D nature of spheroids, including restricted drug diffusion, reduced light penetration, and lower oxygen availability due to their thick dense structure [29]. Consistent with our findings, another study demonstrated that breast cancer spheroids required irradiation doses that were four times higher than those in 2D cultures to achieve comparable effects [30]. These results highlight the fact to account for diffusion and penetration limitations in 3D, reinforcing the relevance of spheroids in optimizing PDT protocols [31].

To investigate the proliferation of cells in free-standing heterotypic spheroids treated with the RuNO2TPyP complex, Ki-67 staining was performed. The results showed that the RuNO2TPyP complex, at concentrations of 0.39 and 0.78 μM, was able to decrease the size of spheroids in a concentration-dependent manner and also reduced the fluorescence intensity of Ki-67 (Fig. 3A). In this experiment, 1.56 μM concentration was not used as spheroids treated with this concentration fragmented during handling, preventing imaging during immunostaining. To further understand the effects of the complex, a Caspase 3/7 activity assay was performed 6 h after irradiation to evaluate early apoptosis in heterotypic spheroids treated with different concentrations (0.09, 0.19, and 0.39 μM) of the ruthenium complex. The results showed that even low concentrations of RuNO2TPyP were sufficient to induce apoptosis, which increased in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3B). Fluorescence images of GFP + MDA-MB-231/HDF spheroids supported the Caspase 3/7 findings by showing the continuing loss of structural integrity induced by the RuNO2TPyP treatment. Non-treated spheroids remained compact and well aggregated, with minimal signs of apoptosis. Building on these findings, we next conducted homotypic spheroid assays to evaluate cell viability in spheroids composed solely of GFP + MDA-MB-231 cells or HDFs. This approach enabled us to assess whether treatment with 0.39 μM RuNO2TPyP followed by irradiation produced differential effects on the individual cell types within heterotypic spheroids and to evaluate the resistance of the multicellular tumor microenvironment. The results showed that spheroids consisting only of tumor cells were significantly more affected compared to those consisting only of HDFs, with mean viabilities of approximately 50% and 94%, respectively (Fig. S3, SI). The effect was markedly stronger in tumor cells, indicating selective cytotoxicity toward cancer cells with minimal impact on healthy cells.

Given this, we further analyzed the behavior of single GFP + MDA-MB-231 cells derived from the treated (0.39 μM) homotypic tumor spheroids followed by the irradiation dose of 16 J cm−2 to assess any modification of their aggressiveness after the treatment. Those single cells were dissociated, counted and reseeded to assess their ability to proliferate, form colonies and migrate compared to the non-treated cells (Fig. 4). Another important aspect that requires further investigation was the effect of PDT on bystander cells, which were not directly targeted by the therapy but may be influenced by active substances released from the treated cells. Previous studies have shown that bystander tumor cells exhibit increased viability and migratory potential, primarily due to diffusible NO released from irradiated cells into medium [32]. Given the potential implications of these effects on treatment outcomes and since the fact that the RuNO2TPyP complex releases NO upon irradiation, as previously demonstrated [10], the assay was conducted as follows: on Day 0, heterotypic GFP + MDA-MB-231/HDF spheroids were generated. The next day (Day 1), they were transferred to the twin-well device, designed to investigate both target and bystander cell responses, and treated with the ruthenium complex (0.39 μM). After 24 h (Day 2), only the target cells were irradiated, while the bystander cells remained non-irradiated but continued sharing the culture medium with the irradiated cells. On the following day (Day 3), cell viability and morphological analyses were performed (Fig. 5A). The results demonstrated that, as expected, the target cells that were exposed to irradiation showed significantly reduced viability, with an average viability of ∼45% at a concentration of 0.39 μM, accompanied by decreased GFP intensity. Interestingly, bystander cells, while not directly irradiated, also exhibited a reduction in both fluorescence intensity and viability, though to a lesser extent than the target cells, with an average viability of ∼75% under identical conditions (Fig. 5B–C). PDT on bioprinted spheroids

The ECM is highly complex and plays a pivotal role in tumor progression by mediating biochemical and biomechanical signals that influence tumor cells as well as stromal and immune cells within the tumor microenvironment [34,35]. These interactions can influence tumor formation, invasion, metastasis, and even lead to therapy resistance, ultimately compromising the effectiveness of treatments [34]. To better mimic these conditions, we employed a composite hydrogel composed of collagen and fibrinogen, which has been shown to recapitulate the native tumor microenvironment while providing the necessary mechanical properties to support the AAB process with spheroids [24]. Following the assessment of treatment effects on free-standing spheroids, we extended our analysis to models that better mimic the physiological tumor microenvironment. Using AAB, we bioprinted heterotypic spheroids of GFP + MDA-MB-231/HDF within a composite hydrogel of collagen and fibrinogen, as illustrated in Fig. 6A. This type of experiment was crucial for evaluating the role of ECM in invasion behavior of tumor spheroids and their response to the treatment within the tumor microenvironment. Also, the use of AAB was critical for precisely positioning spheroids within the hydrogel matrix, ensuring consistent and reproducible experimental conditions. Accurate positioning ensured that spheroids were centered and maintained at uniform depth within each well, a critical factor for PDT. This uniform placement allowed targeted irradiation, maximizing treatment accuracy and ensuring that all spheroids receive equal exposure. Following bioprinting, spheroids were treated with the RuNO2TPyP complex for 24 h and subsequently irradiated. The GFP intensity and diameter of spheroids were evaluated as show in Fig. 6B–C. Spheroids diameter measurements (Fig. 6C) on the day of bioprinting (Day 1) showed similar diameters across all groups, with an average size of ∼240 μm. However, after 24 h of treatment, a slight reduction in spheroid diameter was observed at higher concentrations of RuNO2TPyP. This effect became more pronounced starting from Day 3 (24 h post-irradiation), when treated spheroids began to show a concentration-dependent decrease in diameter. At 48 h after irradiation, spheroids treated with higher concentrations were significantly smaller than those of the non-treated group, showing limited growth and substantial cell death. This was further evidenced by the progressive loss of GFP intensity in treated spheroids over time (Fig. 6B). In contrast, non-treated spheroids continued to grow steadily. To further investigate the apoptotic response, we analyzed the Caspase 3/7 activity in bioprinted spheroids (Fig. 6D). Caspases are key proteases in apoptosis, whose activation via proteolytic cleavage drives controlled cellular degradation. Executioner caspases, including Caspase-3, -6, and -7, orchestrate the final phase by promoting DNA fragmentation and protein breakdown [36]. These results demonstrated that, at 0.39 μM, caspase activity exhibited a slight, non-significant increase compared to the non-treated control. At 0.78 μM, the caspase activity elevated, showing a significant increase compared to both the non-treated and 0.39 μM groups, indicating maximal apoptotic induction at this concentration. However, at 1.56 μM, caspase activity decreased significantly compared to 0.78 μM but remained higher than in the non-treated group. This reduction likely reflects a loss of viable cells at the highest concentration, which lowered overall caspase detection.

Building on these findings, we next sought to explore how the RuNO2TPyP complex modulated the secretion of cytokines. In particular, we analyzed the levels of IL-6, IL-8, and HGF in the supernatant of bioprinted spheroids that were exposed to increasing concentrations of the RuNO2TPyP complex (0.39, 0.78 and 1.56 μM) and compared them to the non-treated group. The results showed a concentration-dependent decrease in cytokine secretion (Fig. 6E). Expression of IL-6, a cytokine with a critical role in tumor promotion, inflammation, and therapy resistance [37], exhibited a drastic decrease to ∼ 54, 23, and 8% of the control levels at increasing doses, reflecting strong suppression of inflammatory signaling within the tumor microenvironment. A similar decrease was observed with the expression of IL-8, a cytokine strongly associated with angiogenesis and the recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages [38], which decreased to ∼ 60, 27, and 9% of the control levels. This suggests a reduction in the pro-inflammatory response. HGF is a mesenchyme-derived, paracrine factor that primarily acts on epithelial cells via the mesenchymal–epithelial transition receptor, promoting cell motility, proliferation, and survival. It is commonly produced by fibroblasts and can be further upregulated under inflammatory and tumor–stroma interaction conditions [[39], [40], [41], [42]]. Consistent with this role, its expression showed the most significant decline, decreasing to ∼38, 19, and undetectable levels (which were considered as zero) for 0.39, 0.78 and 1.56 μM, respectively. In our model, HGF was likely predominantly produced by HDF cells, which are recognized as mesenchymal sources of this growth factor [42]. This marked decrease demonstrates that RuNO2TPyP-mediated PDT effectively suppresses HGF secretion, thereby limiting HGF receptor signaling linked to tumor progression. Overall, these findings highlight the ability of the treatment to downregulate cytokines critical for tumor development.

PDT on PDOs

Based on the promising results from both free-standing and bioprinted spheroid models, and considering that no photosensitizer has yet been specifically approved for breast cancer treatment [43], we extended our investigation to a more advanced and physiologically relevant model: bioprinted PDOs model. These 3D cultures more accurately recapitulate the complexity of human tumors, providing a valuable platform for evaluating drug responses in preclinical settings [44]. Although PDOs of breast tumors have been extensively investigated for disease modeling and drug testing due to their potential, particularly in capturing tumor heterogeneity, identifying patient-specific therapeutic responses, and advancing precision medicine approaches [45], to the best of our knowledge their application in PDT remains unexplored. In the literature, only a few studies have explored the use of PDOs of other tumor types in PDT. A study using squamous head and neck cell carcinoma (HNSCC) PDOs treated with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-targeted PDT, employing antibody- or nanobody-PS conjugates, successfully reflected EGFR expression levels observed in patient samples [46].

Additionally, to our knowledge, no bioprinted 3D model using PDOs has been applied to test PSs. Given this gap, we implemented a bioprinting strategy that enables the precise placement of patient-derived cells into BME matrix (Fig. 7A). This approach ensures the production of PDOs with uniform, reproducible, and controllable sizes, as compared to manually grown PDOs. In our work, standardizing the size, number, and positioning of PDOs through bioprinting proved particularly valuable, especially for PDT, where light penetration was a critical factor. Therefore, we studied the effect of the RuNO2TPyP complex on bioprinted PDOs, and the results showed alterations in PDO morphology at 24, 48, and 72 h after the light irradiation in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 7B). It was observed that treated PDOs exhibited disarrangement, with single cells detaching from the initial structure, especially at the periphery. To further validate these morphological observations and provide quantitative support for the treatment effects, quantification of PDO area (Fig. 7C), based on brightfield images, confirmed time- and concentration-dependent reduction in size. Notably, higher concentrations induced a rapid decrease as early as 24 h, followed by a more stabilized profile, suggesting early structural disruption rather than a gradual decline. The 72-h time point was chosen as the endpoint for Caspase 3/7 activity measurement, as these executioner caspases are involved in the final stages of apoptosis. This time frame was selected based on the expectation that it would allow sufficient accumulation of apoptotic signals for detection. Moreover, morphological alterations indicative of apoptosis was most evident at this time, reinforcing its relevance for analysis. However, no significant differences in Caspase 3/7 activity were observed between treated PDOs and the control group (Fig. 7D). This was likely due to the fact, by this time, cells forming PDOs were severely compromised or had already undergone advanced stages of cell death. To confirm these results, we performed Ki-67 staining to evaluate cell proliferation. The images revealed that Ki-67 intensity progressively decreased with increasing treatment concentrations, indicating a loss of proliferative activity and cell death. At higher concentrations (0.78 and 1.56 μM), the Ki-67 intensity was nearly undetectable, while non-treated PDOs maintained strong intensity. Additionally, DAPI staining suggested nuclear alterations in treated cells (Fig. 7E), which may reflect treatment-induced nuclear damage and/or disruption of PDO structural integrity following PDT. These findings demonstrate the potential of RuNO2TPyP-mediated PDT to induce cytotoxic effects in PDOs, underscoring its promise for personalized and targeted therapies. Importantly, the bioprinted PDO model, which more accurately recapitulates the native tumor microenvironment, required higher complex concentrations than other models, reflecting its physiological relevance and translational applicability. Conclusions

Advancing PDT for breast cancer requires not only the development of effective PSs but also their validation in complex and physiologically-relevant models. By leveraging 3D biofabricated breast cancer models of increasing complexity, this study demonstrated that the RuNO2TPyP complex exhibits a concentration-dependent effect, reinforcing its potential as a PS for breast cancer treatment in PDT. Free-standing spheroids, as the simplest 3D model, yielded promising results but required longer incubation times and higher irradiance doses compared to 2D cultures. Bioprinted spheroids embedded in hydrogels offered a more realistic environment and allowed precise positioning for irradiation, showing similar results while also necessitating extended incubation times to observe the treatment effects. As the last but the most physiologically relevant model, we further developed PDOs with size control and spatial arrangement achieved through 3D bioprinting. Assays with PDOs provided encouraging preliminary results, suggesting that the RuNO2TPyP complex induces cytotoxic effects in PDOs. A key novelty of this work is the use of bioprinting for spheroids and PDOs in the test of PS, offering enhanced control over the tumor microenvironment and experimental conditions. This approach enabled a comprehensive assessment of the RuNO2TPyP complex, demonstrating its capacity to induce significant cell death, modulate cytokine secretion in a concentration-dependent manner, and attenuate tumor cell aggressiveness following PDT. Collectively, these results underscore the potential of RuNO2TPyP as a PS for PDT and highlight bioprinting as a versatile platform for developing physiologically relevant 3D tumor models. These findings provide a foundation for future studies aimed at optimizing its application and exploring its utility in personalized cancer therapies.
