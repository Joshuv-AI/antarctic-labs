MRI-based deep learning combined with radiomics for the preoperative prediction of lymphovascular invasion in patients with bladder cancer

Background

Bladder cancer ranks as the ninth most commonly diagnosed cancer, with an estimated 614,000 new cases and 220,000 deaths reported in 2022 [1]. It imposes a substantial economic and social burden and is characterized by high incidence and mortality rates [2]. Lymphovascular invasion (LVI) refers to the presence of cancer cells within lymphatic or blood vessels [3], which is a critical step for tumor spread and metastasis [4]. It also represents a critical step during malignant progression of epithelial cancers [5]. LVI has been shown to be associated with overall survival (OS), cancer-specific survival (CSS), recurrence-free survival (RFS), and both local and distant recurrence [6–10]. Some studies suggest that LVI status in bladder cancer is closely related to the efficacy and clinical outcomes of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC), intravesical instillation therapy, and surgical treatment [11–14]. Therefore, accurate preoperative prediction of LVI status is essential for prognostic assessment and treatment decision-making in bladder cancer. A previous study developed a model that uses pathological images to accurately predict LVI status in bladder cancer; [15] however, this model cannot provide preoperative predictions, limiting its utility in initial treatment decision-making. Another study identified clinical factors associated with LVI status in bladder cancer but did not develop an effective preoperative prediction model [16]. Thus, there is an unmet need for a precise model that can predict LVI status preoperatively.

Recent progress in artificial intelligence and radiomics provides promising approaches to address some of the challenges in medical imaging. Deep learning, a branch of machine learning, has demonstrated potential in assisting with diagnosis, uncovering novel imaging features, and forecasting patient outcomes [17]. Radiomics extracts and analyzes quantitative data from medical images to assist in diagnosis, prognosis and treatment selection [18]. The integration of deep learning and radiomics has been applied to improve diagnostic accuracy and treatment personalization [19–21]. Therefore, the integration of deep learning and radiomics holds significant potential for predicting LVI status in bladder cancer patients.

In the present study, we developed a deep learning framework that can predict LVI status preoperatively using MRI. This enables clinicians to better anticipate the risks of tumor progression and metastasis and to evaluate the potential benefits of different treatment options, thereby facilitating more personalized clinical decision-making. To address the limitations of conventional radiomics (which relies on fixed feature extraction algorithms that may discard hidden but potentially informative features) and deep learning models (which, while highly flexible, are prone to overfitting), we developed a hybrid model that synergistically integrates radiomics and deep learning approaches, explicitly modeling their interaction through feature-level fusion to enhance the identification of clinically relevant patterns while preserving model generalizability. We conducted comparative evaluations of the hybrid model’s diagnostic performance against ten baseline approaches: image-only and radiomics-only deep learning models and eight radiomics-driven machine learning algorithms. We then stratified bladder cancer patients into low- and high-risk groups based on the predicted LVI probability and evaluated the prognostic significance of this stratification.

Methods

Study population

In this multicenter retrospective study, a total of 543 patients were enrolled from eight medical centers. The primary cohort consisted of 473 patients from two institutions: Institution A (n = 379) and Institution B (n = 94). These patients were used for model training and internal testing. The remaining 70 patients were recruited from six independent external centers. The inclusion criteria were: (1) MRI-confirmed primary bladder lesions, (2) underwent radical cystectomy (RC) with available postoperative pathology. The exclusion criteria were: (1) underwent transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) with LVI status not reported in the pathology report, (2) incomplete baseline clinical data. (3) poor vesical imaging due to artifacts. Patients from the primary cohort (n = 473) were randomly split into a training set (n = 378) and an internal test set (n = 95) at an approximate 8:2 ratio, with stratification by LVI status to maintain class balance. The external test cohort comprised 70 patients from six independent institutions. Figure 1 shows the patient selection process. Baseline clinical and pathological characteristics were compared between the training and internal test sets and between the training and external test sets. The variables compared included sex, age, histological grade, pT stage, lymph node metastasis, muscle-invasive status, carcinoma in situ (CIS), and LVI status. Data acquisition and preprocessing

This multicenter study included patients who underwent preoperative T2-weighted MRI within one month prior to surgery. The acquisition protocols varied across centers; detailed scanner vendors, models, field strengths, and sequence parameters are summarized in Table S1. Tumor regions of interest (ROIs) were manually segmented slice-by-slice using 3D Slicer by radiologist A (with 7 years of experience). To assess interobserver variability, a randomly selected subset of 50 patients was independently segmented by a second radiologist (radiologist B, 10 years of experience), and the entire segmentation process was supervised by a senior radiologist (radiologist C, 25 years of experience). The peritumoral region was defined by automatically expanding the tumor boundary outward by 5 mm, a distance chosen based on previous bladder cancer radiomics studies [22–24] that deliberately balances the capture of potential microscopic LVI with the avoidance of extravesical fat, using SimpleITK, followed by manual exclusion of the bladder lumen area. For radiomic feature extraction, all images and corresponding masks were resampled to an isotropic voxel spacing of 1 × 1 × 1 mm³. Feature extraction was performed using the PyRadiomics toolkit. The following radiomic feature classes were extracted: first-order features, shape features (3D and 2D), gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) features, gray-level size zone matrix (GLSZM) features, gray-level run length matrix (GLRLM) features, neighboring gray-tone difference matrix (NGTDM) features, and gray-level dependence matrix (GLDM) features. The extracted features were subsequently evaluated for interobserver agreement using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), and only features demonstrating an ICC > 0.75 were retained for further analyses.

Binary masks were created for the tumor and peritumoral regions, with voxels inside the regions set to 1 and those outside set to 0. Both masks were spatially aligned with the original MRI data. The MRI volume, tumor mask, and peritumoral mask were stacked to form a three-channel input. All volumes were first resampled to a uniform axial slice spacing of 4 mm, which corresponded to the most prevalent spacing in the pooled multicentre dataset. A tumor-centered depth crop of 12 consecutive slices was then extracted: for cases with fewer than 12 tumor-containing slices, adjacent normal slices were included to complete the 12-slice volume; for cases with more than 12 tumor-containing slices, the tumor-containing slices were uniformly downsampled to 12. Each slice was resized to 224 × 224 pixels using bilinear interpolation, yielding a standardized input dimension of 3 × 224 × 224 × 12. Prior to analysis, min–max normalization was applied to the MRI channel to scale the intensity values between 0 and 1.

To identify radiomic features strongly predictive of LVI status, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with an L1 penalty was applied. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was selected using 5-fold cross-validation on the training set, choosing the value that minimized the mean squared error. This entire process was confined to the training set to prevent data leakage. Finally, 15 radiomic features were selected.

To enhance generalizability and mitigate overfitting given the limited dataset, a unified 5-fold cross-validation strategy was adopted for model training and evaluation. Specifically, the training set was randomly partitioned into five folds, and all models were developed and evaluated under this identical cross-validation scheme.

Development of the hybrid model

Our MRI feature extractor was based on a 3D-adapted ResNet-50 [25]. The convolutional stride was modified to (2, 2, 1) to mitigate information loss along the depth dimension, thereby improving the capture of inter-slice dependencies. The network consequently generated a 2048-dimensional feature vector for each sample. This volumetric approach enables comprehensive characterization of tumor regions by capturing 3D contextual information, offering an advantage over conventional 2D methods. Radiomics features were first aligned with MRI feature dimensions through a fully connected projection layer, generating 2048-dimensional representations that matched the MRI-derived features. A multi-head cross-attention mechanism was used to enable bidirectional feature interaction, with radiomics features serving as queries (Q) and MRI features as keys (K) and values (V). This configuration allows radiomics-derived patterns to highlight anatomically relevant regions in MRI feature maps during fusion, while preserving the 3D contextual relationships encoded in the volumetric representations. The fused feature representation was then passed through a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with a single output node and a sigmoid activation function, which produced a probabilistic prediction in the [0, 1] range. This output represents the predicted probability of LVI for each patient. Figure 2 illustrates the overall architecture of the proposed hybrid model.

Architecture of the proposed hybrid model. The model pipeline begins with the manual segmentation of the tumor and peri-tumor regions. It then takes two inputs: one is the extracted radiomics features, and the other is a stack of the original image along with the tumor and peri-tumor masks. The final output is the predicted probability of lymphovascular invasion (LVI)

Development of baseline models

For systematic comparison, we developed two deep learning baseline models: an image-only model and a radiomics-only model. To ensure a fair comparison, both baseline models retained the corresponding components of the hybrid model: the image-only model used the same 3D ResNet-50 for feature extraction, and the radiomics-only model used the same feature processing network. The extracted features were then passed into an architecturally identical MLP with a single output node, which produced the final LVI probability via a sigmoid activation function.

Traditional machine learning algorithms often perform well on limited training data. Therefore, we established eight additional baseline models based on the selected radiomics features, covering the following classic algorithms: Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Random Forest (RF), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Decision Tree (DT), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), Logistic Regression (LR), and Support Vector Machine (SVM).

Model training and testing pipeline

In the deep learning framework, class imbalance was addressed using the focal loss function [26], which emphasizes hard-to-classify cases and is well suited for identifying LVI-positive samples. The Adam optimizer with L2 regularization was used for parameter optimization to mitigate overfitting.

The training process was conducted within a 5-fold cross-validation scheme. For models utilizing radiomics features, a min–max scaler was fitted on the training split of each fold and subsequently applied to the validation and test sets. Deep learning models were trained for 30 epochs, and the checkpoint achieving the highest AUC on the fold’s validation set was retained. For traditional machine learning algorithms, hyperparameters were tuned using Bayesian optimization over 20 iterations, with the configuration yielding the highest validation AUC being selected.

For each algorithm, the five models trained across the five folds, together with their respective scalers, were saved. The final prediction for each sample was computed by averaging the outputs of the five fold-specific models. The overall pipeline is illustrated in Fig. 3.

The diagram illustrates the complete process, including radiomics feature selection via LASSO, 5-fold cross-validation for model training and hyperparameter optimization, and the ensemble of fold-specific models to produce final predictions on the test sets

Prognostic analysis

Prognostic analysis was conducted using the LVI status predicted by the hybrid model to evaluate its prognostic value. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the model-predicted LVI probability, with the optimal cutoff threshold determined by maximizing the log-rank test statistic in the training cohort. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated to compare OS between the high- and low-risk groups, and log-rank tests were used to assess statistical significance. In addition, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to estimate hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the high-risk versus low-risk groups in the training, internal test, and external test cohorts. The cutoff threshold derived from the training cohort was applied without modification to both test cohorts for survival analysis. All patients were followed up to June 2025, at which point survival status was administratively censored.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among all patients, 459 were male (84.5%) and 84 were female (15.5%), with a mean age of 66.6 ± 10.4 years. The training cohort comprised 85 LVI-positive and 293 LVI-negative patients, the internal test cohort comprised 21 LVI-positive and 74 LVI-negative patients, and the external test cohort comprised 12 LVI-positive and 58 LVI-negative patients, corresponding to positive proportions of 22%, 22%, and 17%. The median follow-up time in the training, internal test, and external test sets were 29 (IQR 16–51) months, 30 (IQR 18–60) months, and 25 (IQR 17–38) months, respectively. The comparison of baseline characteristics revealed no statistically significant differences between the training and internal test sets, nor between the training and external test sets (all p > 0.05). Nevertheless, the external test cohort exhibited a trend toward a clinically distinct population, with numerically lower rates of MIBC, LVI, and earlier pT/pN stages.

Performance of the hybrid model and baseline models

The hybrid deep learning model demonstrated superior performance by consistently achieving the highest AUC across all evaluation settings. It attained the best validation AUC in three out of five folds, the highest average 5-fold cross-validation AUC, and the top scores on both the internal (AUC = 0.770) and external (AUC = 0.753) test sets. Prognostic risk stratification was established using an optimal threshold of 0.5618 identified in the training cohort, with values below this cutoff classified as low-risk and those above as high-risk. In the training cohort (n = 378, events = 89), the high-risk group demonstrated significantly worse overall survival compared to the low-risk group (log-rank p < 0.001), with a hazard ratio (HR) of 3.42 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.22–5.27). This significant discrimination was maintained in the internal test cohort (n = 95, events = 19; log-rank p = 0.003; HR = 3.89, 95% CI: 1.51–10.04). In the external test cohort (n = 70, events = 9), the model exhibited a non-significant trend toward distinguishing risk groups (log-rank p = 0.151; HR = 3.32, 95% CI: 0.61–18.01). Model interpretation and validation of feature fusion

To visualize the decision-making regions of the models, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) was applied [27]. As shown in Fig. 6, the hybrid model exhibited activation in both the tumor and peritumoral regions, with the strongest activation observed in the peritumoral area. The image-only model showed activation in similar regions but with lower intensity. When radiomics features were replaced with random values, the hybrid model’s attention became diffuse and did not concentrate on specific anatomical areas.

Discussion

LVI serves as a critical prognostic determinant in bladder cancer, strongly correlating with aggressive tumor behavior and adverse oncological outcomes. Importantly, LVI status directly influences therapeutic responsiveness, demonstrating significant associations with clinical efficacy of NAC, intravesical instillation therapy and surgical treatment [6–14]. However, few preoperative models have been developed for predicting the probability of LVI in bladder cancer patients.

In previous research, P. Jiao et al. developed a deep learning model that predicts LVI status using whole-slide images (WSI) obtained from pathological tissue sections, demonstrating its potential to assist pathologists in diagnosis [15]. However, this model’s inability to provide preoperative probability estimates of LVI status significantly diminishes its clinical utility for treatment decision-making processes. In a clinical study of bladder cancer patients undergoing TURBT, Yoneda et al. identified significant predictors of LVI, including large tumors and different growth types (non -papillary and sessile). However, their findings were not translated into a predictive model capable of quantifying preoperative LVI probability, thereby limiting clinical applicability for treatment planning.

This study presents a hybrid deep learning model that integrates radiomics features with MRI imaging to preoperatively and accurately predict the probability of LVI status in bladder cancer patients. The 3D-ResNet backbone effectively captures 3D contextual information, while attention mechanisms enable radiomics patterns to dynamically highlight anatomically significant regions in MRI feature maps during fusion. In terms of AUC, the proposed hybrid model outperformed all ten baseline models, which included two deep learning models (using either images or radiomics alone) and eight radiomics-based traditional machine learning models. We further established a risk stratification cutoff to categorize patients into high- and low-risk groups. This preoperative prediction system enables personalized clinical management by providing quantitative LVI probability assessments, thereby aiding clinicians in tailoring therapy and medication choices based on LVI probability and predicting patient prognosis. It is important to note that these risk strata are defined by the model-predicted probability of LVI, which serves as a preoperative imaging biomarker. While histopathology-confirmed LVI remains the definitive postoperative prognostic factor, the AI-derived risk groups are intended to complement, not replace, pathological assessment by providing early risk estimation prior to RC. Notably, the external test cohort had lower rates of MIBC, LVI, and advanced pT/pN stages compared with the development cohorts, likely reflecting differences in patient populations across institutions. Despite this, the model maintained an AUC of 0.753, suggesting robustness to population heterogeneity. Finally, interpretation of the hybrid model’s decision-making strategy underscored the critical importance of the peritumoral tissue microenvironment for accurate LVI detection.

While our research has demonstrated the ability to predict LVI status in preoperative bladder cancer patients, several limitations were identified that should be addressed in future studies. First, deep learning models typically involve a vast number of parameters that require optimization, increasing their susceptibility to overfitting. To address this, more training data, particularly from diverse sources with greater heterogeneity, is needed to mitigate overfitting and enhance performance. Since the risk strata in the external test set did not show statistically significant differences, further testing with larger and more diverse datasets is required. Second, while our current model relies solely on MRI data, incorporating additional preoperative parameters such as demographic characteristics (gender, age), clinical manifestations (gross hematuria), and diagnostic markers (positive urinary cytology) could potentially enhance LVI status prediction. Future studies should consider integrating these complementary features to optimize model performance. Finally, the current workflow for radiomics feature extraction and MRI-based tumor/peritumor channel generation requires precise delineation of ROIs, which involves labor-intensive manual segmentation and is consequently time-consuming. Developing automated methods for ROI identification and feature quantification could substantially enhance clinical utility by improving operational efficiency, ensuring measurement consistency, and reducing inter-observer variability in radiomics analysis.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed a deep learning framework that synergistically integrates MRI volumetric data with radiomics signatures. The proposed model demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy and enhanced robustness compared to both deep learning models (using either images or radiomics alone) and radiomics-based traditional machine learning models. This preoperative prediction system for LVI status in bladder cancer patients provides dual clinical value, including informing personalized treatment strategies and enabling prognostic stratification via risk-pattern recognition. Future directions will focus on: expanding multicenter test cohorts, incorporating multimodal biomarkers (demographic, clinicopathological, and molecular correlates) and developing automated ROI segmentation algorithms.
