Red-blue monochromatic and composite light withering modulates flavor and aroma profiles of white tea: A combined GC-IMS, E-nose, HPLC and sensory analysis
Authors: Fei Yan, Hanyue Yang, Jiayin Fu, Longhua Wang, Dong Qu, Linjie Xi, Xiaohua Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
White tea production is highly dependent on withering conditions, with traditional methods relying on natural sunlight, which is often hindered by inclement weather in tea-growing regions. Artificial light withering offers a promising solution to enhance tea quality by modulating metabolic pathways. This study investigated the effects of monochromatic (red and blue) and composite red-blue light (RB1:1, RB1:3, RB3:1) withering on the sensory characteristics, chemical composition, and volatile compounds of white tea, compared to indoor natural withering (CK). The light treatments were applied during the first 12 h of withering, followed by 12 h of natural indoor withering.Sensory evaluation revealed that red-dominant treatments (RL, RB3:1) scored was the highest in aroma and taste, while blue-dominant treatments (BL, RB1:3) darkened tea infusions and increased redness and yellowness. Red light withering significantly reduced tea polyphenols by 12.79% and elevated free amino acids by 6.24%. Blue light withering decreased total catechin content by 9.72%, flavonoids by 37%, and caffeine by 8.3%, while increasing soluble sugars by 49.15%. GC-IMS and PCA analysis distinguished volatile profiles, with red-dominant treatments enhancing floral and fruity aromas (benzaldehyde, 2-acetyl-5-methylfuran) and blue-dominant treatments promoting fresh, sweet flavor (linalool oxide and γ-terpinene). These findings demonstrate that artificial light withering, particularly red-blue composite light, optimizes white tea quality by altering key biochemical components and volatile compounds. The study provides a theoretical foundation for improving white tea processing through tailored light spectra, offering practical solutions for consistent high-quality production under variable weather conditions.

Introduction

White tea, a type of slightly fermented tea unique to China, is highly regarded for its fresh, tender, and pure aroma with distinct pekoe fragrance, fresh and pure taste as well as its distinctive health benefits (Sanlier et al., 2018). The processing of white tea is simple, involving only a long withering and drying process (Dai et al., 2017). Withering is a key step in white tea production, which greatly affects the subsequent process steps and the quality of the finished product (Zhou et al., 2022). During withering, enzymatic oxidation and light-driven metabolism dynamically transform polyphenols, amino acids, and volatile compounds, thereby shaping the unique flavor of white tea (Chen et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2024). Traditional withering methods include withering indoors, under sunlight, and using light-emitting diode light irradiation (Mu et al., 2021; Zou et al., 2024). Due to the rainy and humid weather in plantation areas, solar withering became insufficient, seriously limiting high-grade white tea production. Artificial light withering can avoid the impact of rainy weather and improve tea production efficiency.

In recent years, numerous studies have investigated how the quality of light during withering affects tea flavor. Research indicates that light quality is a key regulatory factor influencing both non-volatile and volatile metabolites in tea (Hua et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2024). Regarding non-volatile components, studies have found that red-light withering increases the content of sweet-tasting amino acids and soluble sugars, while blue-light withering promotes the accumulation of theanine and alters the composition of catechins (Li et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2024). Regarding volatile compounds, Hua et al. (2024) reported that blue-light and red-light withering can reduce grassy odors in tea leaves and enhance floral and fruity aromas. Glycoside metabolism has been identified as a key regulatory pathway. He et al. (2023) found that red-light withering improved the floral and sweet aromas of white and green teas. Furthermore, the application of light-withering technology has expanded to different tea types. In black tea processing, Li et al. (2021) found that red-light withering effectively reduces bitterness and enhances quality. In oolong tea, studies have shown that LED photowilting promotes the transformation of flavonoids associated with bitterness, resulting in a richer mouthfeel (Li et al., 2020). Collectively, these studies demonstrate that monochromatic LED photowilting has significant effects on both the composition and content of flavor-related compounds.

In addition to monochromatic light treatment, recent studies have increasingly focused on the synergistic effects of composite spectra. In black tea, red-yellow light outperforms single-wavelength LED treatment in enhancing aroma. It increases the content of hydrocarbons, aldehydes, and alcohols that contribute to almond and roasted flavors (Ai et al., 2025). Including our previous work, most current studies on artificial withering have focused primarily on black tea (Yang et al., 2025). The processing of black tea involves shredding, full fermentation, and intense enzymatic oxidation. This differs fundamentally from the simple, non-fermented withering and drying process of white tea. Fan et al. (2025) found that adjusting the LED spectrum can guide the accumulation of key flavor metabolites during the withering process of white tea. Red light enhances perceived sweetness by increasing the content of sweet-tasting amino acids. Red-blue composite light, on the other hand, enhances umami by increasing the content of theanine. The metabolic response of tea leaves to light quality depends largely on the processing route and the inherent biochemical transformations occurring at each stage. Therefore, conclusions drawn from black tea cannot be directly extrapolated to white tea. To date, systematic studies on the effects of different red-to-blue light ratios on the flavor and aroma of white tea remain scarce, and the distinct roles played by red-dominant and blue-dominant spectra in shaping the delicate, sweet, and refreshing characteristics of white tea have not yet been elucidated. Despite these advances, systematic studies on the effects of different ratios of red-blue composite light on white tea quality remain limited. In particular, the differential effects of red-dominant versus blue-dominant composite light on the balance of flavor and aroma metabolites have not yet been fully elucidated.

This study used red and blue monochromatic lights and red-blue composite light to wither plucked tea leaves. Then, the withered leaves were subjected to drying. We integrated multiple intelligent sensory technologies, including sensory evaluation, spectrophotometry (SP), high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), E-nose, and gas chromatography ion mobility spectrometer (GC-IMS) to comprehensively compare white tea quality-related characteristics among different light withering treatments. We found that red-dominant withering treatments enhanced floral and fruity aromas and increased free amino acid content, while blue-dominant treatments promoted fresh and sweet notes and elevated soluble sugars. Light withering is essential for optimizing the sensory and chemical properties of white tea during the withering stage of processing. However, most tea-producing regions experience inclement weather, which limits traditional solar withering. Artificial light withering offers a promising solution to enhance tea quality by modulating metabolic pathways and ensuring consistent production. Our study is the first to systematically investigate the effects of red-blue light spectra on the sensory characteristics, chemical composition, and volatile profile of white tea. The application of artificial light in tea processing not only improves tea quality but also increases production efficiency and profitability under variable weather conditions. The findings will provide a theoretical foundation and technical guidance for optimizing exogenous light withering in white tea processing and improving white tea quality.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

The deionized water used for experiments was prepared using a Molecular 1005 pure water machine. The pure water machine was purchased from Shanghai Beide Digital Technology Co.(Shanghai, China). Folinphenol, ninhydrin, anthrone, and methanol were purchased from China national pharmaceutical group, Shanghai Chemical Reagent Co.(Shanghai, China). Catechin, epicatechin, epicatechin gallate, epigallocatechin gallate, epigallocatechin, gallocatechin gallate, and gallocatechin were purchased from Shanghai Tongtian Biotechnology Co.(Shanghai, China). L-Theanine was purchased from Beijing Bailing Wei Technology Co.(Beijing, China). Rutin was purchased from Shanghai Ruichu Biotechnology Co.(Shanghai, China). The purity of the above chemicals is more than 98%. Methanol, phosphoric acid, and acetonitrile (chromatographic grade) were purchased from Sigma Company (USA). All other chemicals used in this study are of the analytical grade and purchased from various suppliers.

Tea sample collection

Shanxi No. 1 fresh tea leaves (including only the first and second leaves) were purchased from the Nanzheng district, Hanzhong. The experiment was conducted at Qinling Bashan Mountains Tea Research Institute, Hanzhong, Shanxi, China.

Experimental treatment

The experimental procedure is shown in Fig. 1. The experiment was conducted indoors. Indoor natural lighting was used as a control (CK). The CK treatment was conducted under natural indoor light without supplemental artificial lighting. The spectral composition of natural indoor light is complex and varies with weather conditions, time of day, and window orientation. Therefore, precise quantification of CK red and blue wavelength components was not performed. To ensure valid comparisons, all CK samples were processed simultaneously and under the same indoor environmental conditions (temperature, humidity, and duration) as the artificial light treatments. The light treatment groups included red light (RL), blue light (BL), red-blue light ratio 1:1 (RB1:1), red-blue light ratio 1:3 (RB1:3), and red-blue light ratio 3:1 (RB3:1). Two different wavelength artificial lights (red and blue light) were purchased from Chengdu Dongxu Intelligent Technology Co.(Chengdu, China). The artificial light is an LNDF-100G LED light source. The wavelengths of red and blue light are 650 nm and 460 nm. The light intensity is 2000 lx. The power was 100 W. Each artificial light blight has an area of 1 m2. The mass of tea leaves withered by each artificial light source was 2 kg. The distance between the light source and the tea leaves was 45 cm. The fresh tea leaves were withered in a controlled room at 25 ± 2 °C and 70 ± 5% relative humidity for 12 h under red-blue light. Turn off the light source and allow to withering naturally indoors for 12 h until the moisture content reaches 20 ± 2%. Tea leaves were dried at 100–105 °C for 60 min. The moisture content of dried white tea is approximately 8–9% (Technical specification for white tea processing, GB/T 32743–2016). The tea dryer machine was purchased from Fujian Jiayou Machinery Co. (Fujian, China). Dried tea leaves were kept in relative humidity ≤30% and 20–25 °C environmental conditions before analysis. These samples were processed in 2024, processing method referred to (Wu et al., 2022). Each artificial light withering and the natural light control was independently replicated three times under identical environmental conditions (25 ± 2 °C, 70 ± 5%). Sensory quality assessment and chromatic differences analysis of tea liquors

Sensory test was performed with 6 expert panel members (4 women and 2 men).

Sensory evaluation was conducted following the methodology outlined by (Lin et al., 2024). The preparation of tea liquor was performed referring to the National Standard of China GB/T 23776–2018. 3.0 g dry tea leaves was brewed with 150 mL boiled water for 5 min in specific pot, and the tea liquid was collected for aroma and taste assessments. Each expert panel members provided comments and scores for the white tea samples based on the developed terms and references. Parallel trials were conducted three times to ensure the reliability and consistency of the assessments.

The color quality of tea liquors was evaluated by chromatic differences analysis using a colorimeter spectrophotometer (SMY-2000SF, Beijing Shengmingyang Technology Development Co., Ltd.), through the measurement of L* (brightness), a* (+a*, red, −a*, green), b* (+b*, yellow, −b*, blue). Each sample of tea liquors was measured three times to ensure its accuracy.

Analysis of red-blue light withering on chemical components of white tea

To investigate the biochemical basis underlying the observed sensory differences. The contents of major flavor-related components were quantified in dry tea leaves. These compounds are synthesized and accumulated during processing and serve as the material basis for infusion quality. The content of tea polyphenols was determined using the forintanol method (Zhao et al., 2014). The content of free amino acids was determined as described (Stauss et al., 2024), with minor modifications. Soluble sugar was measured using the anthrone sulfuric acid colorimetric method (Jakubczyk et al., 2020). Flavonoids were determined using the aluminum trichloride method (Jabeen et al., 2019). Meanwhile, the content of eight catechin components and caffeine was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) as described (Li et al., 2020), with minor modifications. The chromatographic column was a COSMOSIL 5C18 AR-II column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm; Nacalai Tesque, Japan). 5 mL of methanol: water (70 °C, 7/3, v/v) was added to white tea powder (0.2 g) in accordance with the national standard (GBT 8313–2018) extraction method, stirred well, and then extracted in a water bath at 70 °C for 10 min (with stirring every 5 min). After centrifugation for 10 min (3500 rpm), the sediment was extracted again through the same method. The two supernatants were combined, and their concentration was increased to 10 mL by using methanol: water (7/3, v/v). Take 1 mL of the combined filtrate and dilute it with methanol: water (70 °C, 7/3, v/v) to a final volume of 100 mL and filter it through a 0.22 μm filter membrane. The mobile phase for HPLC was acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid (95:5, v/v) in a constant program at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The sample volume is 10 μL and the temperature is maintained at 35 °C. The detection was performed at 278 nm. These analyses were repeated three times, and the experimental data were recorded as the average of three measurements.

Analysis of white tea aroma-active compounds

The five kinds of tea with light withering treatment were weighed (1.0 g) and poured into a 20 mL headspace container. After keeping it at 80 °C for 15 min, the headspace air (300 μL) using a high-temperature injector (85 °C) maintained at 60 °C for 20 min with an incubation speed of 500 rpm was shotted and analyzed upon an MXT-5 pillar through a GC-IMS instrument (FlavourSpec®, Germany). The samples were driven by high-purity nitrogen into a chromatographic column (MXT-5, 15 m, 0.53 mm ID, 1.0 μm df, Restek Corporation, USA) maintained at 60 °C. The 99.99% nitrogen gas was used as a vehicle at a programmed speed as follows: 150 mL/min for 2 min (turbulence gas), 2 mL/min for 2 min (carrier gas), 150 mL/min for 20 min (turbulence gas), and 2 mL/min for 20 min (carrier gas). The mixture gas was ionized in the IMS ionization cell. A mixture of six ketone standards (C4-C9) was used to establish retention time (RT) and RI calibration curves. Compounds were identified by comparing GC retention indices and IMS drift time (DT) with databases integrated into the VOCal software (NIST 2020). Data processing and qualitative analysis were carried out using VOCal software (version 0.4.10). Each analysis was performed in triplicate.

Electronic nose detection method

The portable E-nose PEN 3.0 (Airsense Analytics GmbH, Germany) equipped with an array of 10 metal oxide semiconductors (MOS) was used for the E-nose analysis. The specifications of ten sensors are depicted in Table 1, and the pattern recognition software (Win Muster v.1.6.2) was employed for data recording and elaboration. The operational procedures have been described previously (Feng et al., 2024). The sensor array was cleaned with clean air before each test. 1 g white tea was placed into a 40 mL headspace vial, and equilibrated at 50 ± 1 °C for 30 min to reach a steady state. The instrumental parameters were set as follows: flush time, zero trim, pre-sampling, and measurement time were 120 s, 10.0 s, 5.0 s, and 120 s, respectively. Both chamber flow and injection flow were maintained at 300 mL/min. The sensor response was recorded as the maximum value (G/G₀) during the stable period (115–120 s). Statistical analysis

Each experiment was repeated three times (n = 3). Data was expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The instrumental analysis was performed using GC-IMS Library Search, Reporter Gallery plot, and 2.2.1 LAV (Laboratory Analytical Viewer) processing software (FlavourSpec®, G.A.S., Dortmund, Germany), which can be used for sample analysis from different angles. Principal component (PCA) analyses of volatile compounds were performed using SPSS statistics 19.0 software (SPSS Inc. Chicago, IL, USA) to explore the clustering of withered white teas at red-blue light on the volatile compounds. Score plots were drawn using the Origin 2018 software (Originlab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). The multiple comparison was analyzed using the one-way analysis of variance and Duncan's multiple test (SPSS 19.0; SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). A level of P < 0.05 was considered as significant.

Results and discussion

The impact of red-blue light withering on the sensory characteristics of white tea

The color of dry tea and its infusion are factors in evaluating tea quality (Li et al., 2024). Under the influence of light, oxygen, and temperature, the color will deteriorate, gradually turning yellow, red, or brown, which affects tea quality. Therefore, we conducted color difference analyses on tea infusions. The colors of dry tea sample after light withering treatment did not significantly differ, but those of their infusions did (Fig. 2A). This study systematically investigated the effects of red-blue monochromatic light and composite light (RB1:1, RB1:3, and RB3:1) withering on the sensory characteristics of white tea and compared them with indoor naturally withering samples (CK). The CK treatment (indoor natural withering) was conducted under ambient light conditions, which inherently contained both red and blue wavelength components. Therefore, the effects observed under the artificial red and blue light treatments represent additional modulations beyond the natural light spectrum, rather than an absolute light vs. dark comparison. The sensory characteristics of the samples were evaluated in terms of appearance, aroma, taste, and infused leaf condition (Table 1 and Fig. 2A). Fig. 2B shows that the sensory quality scores of the RL(P < 0.01) and RB1:3(P < 0.001) groups were significantly higher than CK group. Fig. 2C demonstrates that red-blue light withering significantly reduced the lightness (L*) of tea infusions compared to CK (P < 0.001). The CK group showed the highest L* value (118.1 ± 0.5), whereas blue light-dominant treatments (BL: 88.9 ± 0.3; RB1:3: 92.4 ± 0.4) produced the darkest infusions, indicating that blue light is not conducive to maintaining the brightness of tea infusions. Fig. 2D and 1E shows that redness (a*) and yellowness (b*) values of BL infusion are the highest and the RB3:1 infusion had the lowest redness and yellowness values. The yellowness of the RB1:3 infusion was not significantly different compare with CK. Hence, the findings suggest that red and blue monochromatic light significantly increased redness and yellowness values of white tea infusion (P < 0.001), while red and blue composite light significantly increased the redness of white tea (P < 0.001) and slightly decreased the yellowness (P < 0.01). Against the background of ambient red and blue light present in CK, the additional red or blue light further modulated these color parameters. (Li et al., 2024) reported that three flavonoid compounds - quercetin 3-O-glucosyl rutinoside, rutin, and quercetin 3-O-glucoside - enhance yellowness in tea infusions. In our study the total flavonoid content in dry tea leaves (Fig. 2B) decreased following red and blue light exposure, but the yellowness of the tea infusion increased significantly (Fig. 2E). This contradictory result suggests that the color change in the tea infusion may not be directly caused by changes in total flavonoid content in dry leaves. Instead, it may result from photochemical transformations of specific flavonoid compounds, or from the accumulation of other yellow pigments (such as carotenoids and polyphenol oxidation products) that were not measured in this study. In the future, a dark control group should be included. Analysis of Main biochemical components

The major biochemical components such as catechins, caffeine, tea polyphenols, and amino acids in teas are greatly affected by the light quality and intensity during withering (Tian et al., 2024). The bitterness of tea soup is related to ester-catechins (Fang et al., 2024). These components were quantified in dry tea leaves to establish the chemical basis for sensory quality. Table 2 reveals that light treatments significantly affected ester-catechins in dry tea leaves, particularly reducing EGCG (P < 0.01) and ECG content in BL (P < 0.05), RB1:3, and RB3:1 groups (P < 0.01), while non-ester-catechins remained stable. This indicates that light withering treatment can alleviate the bitterness of tea soup and enhance its flavor. This resulted in 8.52–9.72% lower total catechin content in BL, RB1:1 and RB3:1 groups compared to CK (P < 0.01). Fig. 3A shows that compared with CK, the tea polyphenols content in the BL and RB1:3 groups show a moderate reduction (P < 0.05), by 12.79% and 12.05%, respectively. (Mu et al., 2021) found similar results, with white tea made from LED blue light withering having the lowest polyphenol content. Polyphenol reductions after light withering may be related to light-induced free radical reactions leading to the decomposition or transformation of some polyphenol structures. The CK already contained ambient red and blue light. Thus, the additional artificial red and blue light may further enhance these degradation processes. There is no difference in the tea polyphenols content in other groups (P > 0.05). Fig. 3B and D shows that comparing RL, RB1:1, RB1:3, and RB3:1 groups to CK, the contents of flavonoid were significantly lower (P < 0.001) than CK, and the contents of soluble sugar in RL, BL, RB1:1, and RB1:3 groups were significantly higher (P < 0.001) than CK. During the withering process, as the water content decreases, the vitality of the cell nucleus weakens, the cytoplasm content increases, and the flavonoid content decreases. Water loss stress induces the degradation of key flavor flavonoid glycosides, contributing to the formation of the unique flavor of tea (Wang et al., 2024). The results of Fig. 3D demonstrate that blue light and blue light-dominant treatments (RB3:1) significantly increase soluble sugar accumulation in white tea (P < 0.001), with increases of 43.40% and 49.15%, respectively, compared to the CK group. A similar increase in soluble sugars in onion leaves has also been reported under combined blue-white light treatment at the seedling stage of onion cultivation (Gao et al., 2022). Fig. 3C shows that, except for the RL group, there was no significant difference in caffeine content following the light withering treatment (P > 0.05). Fig. 3E shows that the water leachate content increased significantly (P < 0.01) in the RL, RB1:1, and RB3:1 group compared to the CK group. Additionally, the free amino acid content was significantly higher in the RL and RB3:1 group (P < 0.05), at 6.24% and 6.34%, respectively. (He et al., 2023) found red-light withering in green tea processing elevated theanine content through activation of protease activity, which is consistent with the trend of increasing total free amino acids under red-light treatment in this study. The sensory attributes of tea infusions are directly influenced by the composition of dry tea leaves. Evaluation of volatile compounds in red-blue lights withering teas by GC-IMS

The topographic sub-traction plots of GC-IMS of volatile organic compounds in white tea treated with red and blue light withering was shown in Fig. 4A. The graph showed that the BL group were significantly different from the CK compared to the other light withering groups, which was consistent with the PCA analysis (Fig. 4B). The PCA results made it quite evident that that the white tea samples after red-bule light withering could be distinguished in the distribution map in a relatively independent space. In Fig. 4B, the distribution of the RL, RB1:1 and RB3:1 withering treated white tea samples was close to each other, indicating little change in flavor substances in the samples, while there was a significant deviation in the CK group, indicating a significant difference in volatile flavor compounds between indoor natural withering and red-bule light withering. Furthermore, the PCA results revealed that the different red-to-blue light ratio treatments did not follow a single continuous gradient, but instead exhibited a nonlinear clustering pattern. The RL, RB3:1, and RB1:1 treatment groups cluster closely together, while the BL and RB1:3 treatment groups deviate significantly. This may be due to the presence of a threshold or specific wavelength effects, rather than a simple superposition of the light quality ratios. This nonlinear pattern may be related to the differential activation of the plant photoreceptor system. Red light primarily regulates metabolites through photosynthetic pigment signaling pathways. Blue light exerts its effects via phytochromes and phytochromes. When the blue light ratio exceeds a certain threshold, signals from blue light receptors may induce changes in metabolites. This is similar to the red light dose-response effect observed by (Li et al., 2021) in black tea. However, it differs from the linear gradient trends reported in some studies. In order to compare the differences between the groups in more detail and visually, the reference spectra deducted from the other samples to obtain the difference comparison pattern, as shown in Fig. 3C. It can be seen that the white tea after red-blue light withering treatment still differed significantly from the control group. Fifty-six compounds are identified by GC-IMS (Table 2). Fig. 3D shows that Esters, Ketones, and Pyrazine dominate the volatiles in the red-blue light-withered white teas. The fingerprint map (Fig. 4E) revealed distinct volatile profiles among treatments. The substances in area a (red box) showed the highest content in CK, including β-myrcene, 1-heptanal, α-angelica lactone, 3-ethylpyridine, 2-ethylpyridine, 5-methylfurfural, acetoxyacetone, 3-methylbutyl propanoate, 2-methyl-3-(methylthio)furan, 1,2-dimethylbenzene, 2-methyl-3-furanthiol, butyl propanoate, 2-furanmethanethiol, methional, 4,5-dimethylthiazole, hexyl formate, 3-(methylthio)propanal, isopentyl propanoate, and n-propyl benzene. Among these, α-angelica lactone was the most abundant in CK. These compounds are primarily associated with grassy, green, and fatty notes. Such notes are characteristic of insufficiently withered tea leaves. They are often diminished by light treatment during processing.

In contrast, the RL treatment (area b) enriched several compounds. These included butyl propanoate, 2-furanmethanethiol, methional, 4,5-dimethylthiazole, hexyl formate, 3-(methylthio)propanal, isopentyl propanoate, 3-ethylpyridine, and n-propyl benzene. Consistently, Li et al. found that red-light withering significantly increases glycosidase activity during the late stages of withering. This promotes the release of bound aroma precursors. It also improves the aromatic quality of black tea. These effects were observed on top of the natural light already present in CK, suggesting that supplemental red light further activates glycosidase beyond natural light levels. The degradation of various compounds under red light may be related to the acceleration of processes caused by red light-induced thermal effects and enzymatic activity. Methional contributes baked potato-like notes. It accumulated significantly under RL. This suggests its key role in the roasted and mellow aroma characteristic of red-light withered white tea.

The BL treatment (area c) exhibited the highest abundance of a distinct set of compounds. These included methyl 2-furoate, 2,3,5-trimethylpyrazine, 2-(2-methoxyethoxy)ethanol, 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one, 2-acetylthiazole, 2,3-dihydro-4-hydroxy-2,5-dimethyl-3-furanone, 3-methylbutyl butanoate, 3-methylbutyl propanoate, 2-ethylpyrazine, decalin, 4,5-dihydro-3(2H)-thiophenone, α-terpinolene, camphene, 2,6-dimethylpyrazine, propylsulfide, β-3-carene, 2-octanone, 1-octen-3-ol, 1-octen-3-one, pentanoic acid, 2-pyrrolidinone, 3-methylpentanoic acid, acetic acid hexyl ester, 2-ethyl-6-methylpyrazine, (E,E)-2,4-heptadienal, γ-terpinene, 2-hydroxybenzaldehyde, hexanoic acid, 2-acetyl-5-methylfuran, linalool oxide, and aniline. He et al. reported that medium-intensity blue light treatment during withering is most effective for producing fruity and floral aromas in green tea. This occurs primarily by upregulating terpene synthase gene expression. It also promotes the accumulation of monoterpenes. In line with this, several terpenoids (γ-terpinene, α-terpinolene, linalool oxide) were significantly enriched under BL in our study. Pyrazines (2,3,5-trimethylpyrazine, 2,6-dimethylpyrazine) were also enriched. γ-terpinene imparts citrus-like freshness. It exhibited a high ROAV under BL and blue-dominant treatments. This is consistent with the fresh and sweet aroma scores in sensory evaluation. Furthermore, 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one is a carotenoid-derived volatile. It was also enriched under BL. This suggests that blue light may promote carotenoid cleavage. This mechanism has been previously described in tea leaves exposed to high-energy light.

For composite light treatments (RB1:1, RB1:3, and RB3:1), the substances in areas d, e, and f displayed the highest concentrations, respectively. Li et al. investigated the effects of supplemental light (white, red, blue, red-blue, UV-A, UV-B) on grape quality. They found that most light sources, except red alone, significantly increased monoterpene content. They also enhanced floral and fruity aromas. Blue light was the most effective. Consistent with this, the composite light treatments in our study enriched a broad range of compounds. This was especially true for RB3:1 (red-dominant). These compounds included linalool oxide, 2-acetyl-5-methylfuran, (E,E)-2,4-heptadienal, and γ-terpinene. They are collectively associated with floral, fruity, and sweet notes. The volatile profiles of RB1:3 and RB3:1 were largely similar. However, three compounds were present at higher concentrations in RB3:1. These were 3-methylbutyl propanoate (fruity), 2-methyl-3-(methylthio)furan (meaty, roasty), and methional (baked potato). This may explain the slightly higher sensory scores for aroma and taste in this treatment. These observations align with previous findings. Moderate red-blue ratios can synergistically activate multiple metabolic pathways. These include the shikimate pathway for phenylpropanoids and the terpenoid pathway for aroma precursors. The substances common to all composite treatments included 1-octen-3-ol, 1-octen-3-one, pentanoic acid, 2-pyrrolidinone, 3-methylpentanoic acid, acetic acid hexyl ester, 2-ethyl-6-methylpyrazine, (E,E)-2,4-heptadienal, γ-terpinene, 2-hydroxybenzaldehyde, hexanoic acid, 2-acetyl-5-methylfuran, and linalool oxide. Many of these are known to be derived from fatty acid oxidation (e.g., 1-octen-3-ol, (E,E)-2,4-heptadienal). Others are derived from the Maillard reaction (pyrazines). This suggests that both lipid degradation and non-enzymatic browning pathways are commonly activated under red-blue composite light withering. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that different light qualities alter the abundance of specific volatiles. These changes in volatile compounds occurred in addition to those induced by artificial light sources, even under control conditions where faint blue and red light was already present.

Correlation between E-nose and GC-IMS results

To evaluate whether the E-nose sensor responses could reflect the volatile compound profiles determined by GC-IMS, Spearman correlation analysis was performed between the two datasets. The E-nose and GC-IMS integration provided a more comprehensive insight into the VOCs of the white tea after light withering. Hence, an investigation was conducted to explore the potential correlation between E-nose sensor responses and volatile compound levels detected by GC-IMS. This correlation was assessed against the background of ambient red and blue light present in CK, and the significant correlations observed further support that the additional light treatments induced consistent changes across analytical platforms. As depicted in Fig. 6, sensors such as W1S, W2S, W3S, W6S, and W3C, along with decahydronaphthalene, 3-methylbutyl butyrate-M, γ-terpinen-1-ol, hexyl acetate, 2,3-dihydro-4-hydroxy-2,5-dimethyl-3-furanone, (E)-3-hexenoic acid, 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one, 3-methylvaleric acid, 2,6-dimethylpyrazine, dipropyl sulfide, 2-ethyl-6-methylpyrazine, 2-hydroxybenzaldehyde, and caproic acid. These compounds were detected at relatively high levels after the light withering of white tea. The W1S, W3S, W5S, W6S, and W3C sensors showed a negative correlation with compounds such as isopentyl propionate, 3-ethylpyridine, 1-heptanal, α-angelicin, isobutyl isobutyrate, and 3-(methylthio) propionaldehyde. The concentrations of these compounds in the CK group were significantly higher than in the other light-wilting groups. Additionally, the W1S, W5S, and W6S sensors showed a negative correlation with 3-carene and hexyl formate. The W1W, W3W, and W5C sensors showed no significant correlation with these key volatile compounds. These results indicate that there is a certain degree of correlation between the responses of the electronic nose sensors and the characteristic volatile organic compounds detected by gas chromatography–ion mass spectrometry (GC-IMS). These results indicated a significant correlation between the E-nose sensor responses (Fig. 5) and distinct VOCs detected by GC-IMS (Fig. 6). Unlike previous studies that used an electronic nose and GC-IMS separately, our correlation analysis integrated these two techniques and applied them to red- and blue-light-wilted white tea for the first time. We found that sensor responses were significantly correlated with key volatile compounds, providing a more reliable basis for cross-validation in aroma characterization. This finding differs from our previous research on black tea. Earlier studies found that monochromatic red or blue light primarily affected the fruity aroma and bitterness (Yang et al., 2025). The experiments in this study on white tea indicate that red-blue composite light has a significant synergistic effect on the freshness and sweetness of the tea liquor. Conclusions

Previous studies have primarily focused on a single light quality or a single physicochemical parameter. This study is the first to systematically compare the combined effects of red and blue monochromatic light, as well as composite light at different ratios (1:1, 1:3, and 3:1), on the withering of white tea. It integrated four techniques: GC-IMS, e-nose, HPLC, and sensory evaluation. We investigated the effects of red and blue light on white tea quality formation through volatile characteristics, non-volatile components, and sensory quality. Our results not only confirmed the superiority of red and blue light withering, but also revealed that red-dominant composite light (RB3:1) outperforms pure red light in accumulating free amino acids and producing floral-sweet aromas, while blue-dominant composite light (RB1:3) offers unique advantages in increasing soluble sugars and fresh-sweet aromas.

The results obtained from this study demonstrate that red-blue light withering, especially blue-dominant withering treatments, significantly darkens white tea infusions and alters their color (increased redness and yellowness). Red light withering drastically reduced tea polyphenols and increased free amino acids. Blue light withering drastically reduced catechin, flavonoid, and caffeine and increased soluble sugars. Withering treatment with predominantly red light (RL, RB3:1) showed drastically accumulation of free amino acids. Sensory evaluation confirmed that red light withering scored was the highest in aroma and taste, and RB3:1 achieving the highest sensory scores. GC-IMS and PCA analysis revealed blue light withering showing significantly divergence from CK, and red light withering and composite light (RB1:1, RB3:1) groups clustered closely. Red-dominant withering enhanced floral and fruity aromas (e.g., benzaldehyde, 2-acetyl-5-methylfuran). Blue-dominant withering promoted fresh, sweet flavor and specifically enriched linalool oxide and γ-terpinene. RB3:1 recommended for the production of high-end floral white tea. E-nose analysis further corroborated these findings: sensors W1W and W1S showed higher responses in red-dominant treatments, and Spearman correlation (Fig. 6) confirmed significant associations between sensor responses and key volatiles. The present study demonstrates that supplementing natural withering light with specific red and blue spectra further modulates the flavor and aroma profiles of white tea. However, the control treatment (indoor natural withering) already contained ambient red and blue light components. The observed effects reflect incremental changes upon a natural light spectrum. Unlike our previous findings in black tea, the present work reveals that red-blue composite light, particularly the red-dominant ratio (RB3:1), exerts synergistic effects on amino acid accumulation and floral-sweet aroma generation in white tea, highlighting the tea-type-dependent nature of light regulation. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for optimizing artificial light withering in white tea processing.
