High-precision fault inception time detection in active distribution networks using Euclidean distance and Gaussian filtering

Haiou Cao, Yihua Meng, Chenbin Zhou, Lichen Deng, Jiayan Yu

ABSTRACT
Fault signals in power systems often contain high-order harmonics and attenuated oscillations. which pose challenges for traditional fault time detection methods in accurately identifying the fault inception instant. To address this issue, this paper proposes an accurate fault time detection method based on Euclidean distance and Gaussian filter. Based on the phase voltage or current signal of the fault line, the improved high-order Fourier algorithm with attenuation factor is used to fit the curve of the fault phase signal data. The fault signal is compared with the imaginary non-fault signal by using Euclidean distance, and the fitting error is filtered by a Gaussian filtering algorithm to detect the fault segment. Finally, the fault is detected by pixel column, and the fault time is obtained by corresponding the pixel column and time information. The MATLAB/Simulink simulation results and the field test results show that the method can accurately detect the fault time under different harmonics, damped oscillations, transition resistance, short-circuit fault types and sampling rates, and the detection error is basically controlled within 0.1 ms, which achieves high detection accuracy. Combining the advantages of both computational efficiency and detection accuracy, the proposed method demonstrates superior practicality and cost-effectiveness in active distribution networks when compared to existing technologies.

1. Introduction The extensive integration of distributed generation has transformed the structure of distribution networks from traditional radial networks to multi-source networks [ 1 – 3 ]. As a result, the fault characteristics of distribution systems have become more complex, with more pronounced issues such as harmonics and damped oscillations. This poses new challenges for real-time fault detection and protection in active distribution networks [ 4 – 6 ]. In the application of relay protection in active distribution networks, differential protection and fault location technologies primarily rely on the synchronization of electrical quantities at both ends of the line [ 7 – 9 ]. Currently, traditional fault self-synchronization algorithms achieve low-cost data synchronization by aligning the detected fault instants on the power supply side and the load side [ 10 , 11 ]. However, when there is a significant discrepancy in fault detection times between the two ends, this algorithm may introduce substantial measurement errors, potentially causing protection devices to fail to operate promptly [ 12 – 14 ]. The key to achieving reliable relay protection in active distribution networks lies in the accurate determination of the fault inception instant [ 15 ]. As the primary stage of relay protection systems, the more reliable the fault detection module, the faster the protection devices can respond to faults [ 16 ]. The fault detection module continuously monitors electrical parameter variations and triggers protective actions upon detecting abrupt changes exceeding predefined thresholds [ 17 ]. Precise determination of the fault inception instant is critical for subsequent protection operations and fault clearance [ 18 ]. In active distribution networks with distributed generation, the complex and variable nature of fault characteristics makes it challenging for traditional fixed-setting initiation criteria to reliably distinguish between fault and non-fault conditions [ 19 – 22 ]. Therefore, precise detection of the fault inception instant is of paramount importance for enhancing the reliability, selectivity, speed, and sensitivity of relay protection in active distribution networks [ 23 ]. In recent years, several researchers have investigated methods for fault moment detection. Liu et al. [ 23 ] utilized the three-phase voltage data collected by Phasor Measurement Unit (PMU) at each node to construct a data source matrix. By analyzing the normal distribution curves of the column data in the time window matrix, the fault moment was determined. However, the moving time window is influenced by historical data, resulting in relatively high detection errors. Dong et al. [ 24 ] used the parameters of the phase current transient component using the Prony algorithm, calculated the zero-crossing points of the phase current transient component fitting function, and identified the actual fault moment. This method demonstrates good detection performance, but its accuracy is affected by the data window length and sampling frequency. Zhang et al. [ 25 ] combined the phase current transient component with mathematical morphology, processing the phase current signal using an improved multi-resolution morphological gradient operator. When the sampling rate decreases, the detection error on the distributed generation side may exceed one sampling interval. Li et al. [ 26 ] constructed a Hankel matrix from discrete fault signals and employed singular value decomposition to separate the signals. The moment of fault occurrence is determined by calculating the modulus maxima of the detail components. However, the number of columns in the Hankel matrix requires manual selection and lacks an adaptive mechanism, which may compromise the results if chosen inappropriately. Yan et al. [ 27 ] employed the Prony algorithm to fit and extrapolate the original signal, generating a hypothetical non-fault signal. By performing pixel-by-pixel column comparison between the original and fitted signals, the method achieves pixel-level accuracy in fault timing detection. However, the approach does not address the detection of damped oscillatory components and higher-order harmonics commonly present in power systems. Yao et al. [ 28 ] developed an active high-impedance fault detection method for resonant grounding distribution networks based on fault-feature enhancement, achieving reliable detection of 75kΩ transition impedance faults under 15dB noise conditions. Najafzadeh et al. [ 29 ] proposed a fault detection, classification, and localization method for transmission lines using fuzzy logic thresholding, WHO-optimized DT/RF, and ANFIS. It achieved 100% accuracy and <153.6m localization error in simulations. However, it relies solely on frequency signals for features and the WHO algorithm is sensitive to parameter settings, affecting generalization stability. Li et al. [ 30 ] designed an AD-TFM unit with learnable wavelet parameters embedded in LSTM and attention mechanism for incipient fault timing detection. The method adaptively extracts weak fault features via learnable time-frequency transforms and augments scarce samples through phase-switching and time-sliding strategies. However, it is limited to fault detection without classification or localization, and its RNN-based structure faces computational efficiency issues with long sequences or large-scale networks. Despite the variety of existing fault detection methods, accurately identifying fault occurrence times under complex conditions—such as harmonic interference, damped oscillations, and low sampling rates—remains a persistent challenge. To address this issue, this paper proposes a novel fault-time detection method based on Euclidean distance and Gaussian filtering, specifically designed for active distribution networks. The approach begins by applying an enhanced high-order Fourier algorithm with a damping factor to achieve high-fidelity fitting of fault current signals containing harmonics and oscillatory components. Following this, a hybrid image-based strategy combining Euclidean distance and Gaussian filtering is used to translate waveform similarities into pixel-level comparisons, allowing precise segmentation of the fault interval. Finally, pixel-wise column analysis within the identified segment enables precise localization of the fault inception time. Simulation results confirm that the method maintains detection errors below 0.1 ms under diverse scenarios including harmonic distortion, oscillatory decay, varying transition resistances, multiple fault types, and reduced sampling rates, outperforming existing techniques. Field data validation has confirmed the practicality of the proposed method. This study enhances the accuracy and adaptability of fault diagnosis in active distribution networks, offering a computationally efficient and highly practical solution for real-world grid monitoring and automation. 2. High-order Fourier algorithm with attenuation factor Accurate identification of fault occurrence time in active distribution networks is crucial for ensuring rapid and reliable protection responses. However, the increasing integration of distributed generation has led to more complex fault characteristics, including pronounced harmonics and damped oscillatory components. These non-stationary signal features pose significant challenges to conventional detection methods, which often fail to represent transient behaviors accurately. High-fidelity signal fitting thus becomes essential, as it forms the foundation for precise fault analysis and subsequent timing detection. Fourier function fitting is a mathematical method based on Fourier series or Fourier transform, used to approximate or fit complex signals, probability density functions, or other data distributions. It has wide applications in fields such as signal processing, image analysis, and probability modeling [ 31 ]. The high-order Fourier algorithm significantly enhances the fitting of complex fault waveforms in power systems by incorporating higher-order harmonic components. This enables precise reconstruction of signals containing harmonics and oscillatory transients, which are often challenging for conventional methods. Fault instant identification through image comparison 3.1 Euclidean distance for image comparison Following the high-fidelity fitting and extension of complex fault signals using the enhanced Fourier algorithm, the accurate extraction of fault characteristics becomes essential for precise timing identification. While the fitted signal provides a reference waveform, the critical challenge remains how to effectively quantify the discrepancy between the original fault signal and its fitted extension, especially under noisy and complex operating conditions. The Euclidean distance algorithm provides a quantitative and computationally efficient approach to measuring dissimilarities between the original fault signal and its fitted extension [ 32 , 33 ]. By transforming the signals into vector space and evaluating pixel-wise deviations, it effectively captures waveform anomalies caused by faults. This method enhances detection sensitivity and facilitates accurate identification of fault segments, even under noisy conditions or in the presence of harmonic distortions. In this paper, image pixels are converted into one-dimensional vectors, and the Euclidean distance between images is computed to quantitatively measure their degree of difference. The proposed method implements image comparison based on Euclidean distance through the following steps (Taking the current signal as an example): First, the phase current measurements are extracted from the fault-phase current by employing high-accuracy current transformers, ensuring minimal amplitude and phase errors during signal acquisition. Next, uniform data window segmentation is applied according to the period of the fault-phase current. In parallel, two images are read and adjusted to the same duration to ensure dimensional consistency. Subsequently, grayscale conversion techniques from the field of image processing are applied to convert RGB images into grayscale images in order to eliminate the interference of color information with the difference metric, as the three RGB channels may introduce unnecessary noise that compromises the accuracy and robustness of image comparison based on algorithms such as Euclidean distance. Based on the aforementioned results, a criterion for fault segment discrimination has been designed. A faulty segment is identified by comparing the Euclidean distance of each individual segment with the product of the maximum Euclidean distance among all segments and the localization coefficient. The mathematical criterion is formally expressed as follows: D k ≥ ε • m a x [ D 1 , D 2 , ⋯ , D n e ] (9) where D k is the Euclidean distance calculated for the k-th segment of the two signal images, ε is the localization coefficient, n e is the number of raw signal segment and fitted extended signal segment, D 1 , D 2 , ⋯ , D n e are the Euclidean distances calculated between segment 1 to segment n e of these two signals. Empirically, ε is set within 0.2–0.3 based on statistical analysis of 200 fault cases under varying harmonics, damped oscillations, and transition resistances. For non-fault segments, the normalized Euclidean distance D k has a mean of 0.087, with 99.7% of samples below 0.18. For fault-inception segments, the mean is 0.472, with 99.7% of samples above 0.21. Consequently, setting ε within 0.2–0.3 limits the false positive rate for non-fault segments to below 0.5% and the false negative rate for fault segments to below 0.1%. Sensitivity analysis further confirms that within this range, the detection error remains stable at 0.085 ms with a 100% fault segment localization success rate. By quantifying signal differences through Euclidean distance, this method resolves the challenge of automatically and accurately pinpointing the initiation zone of faults. The technique converts signals into image vectors and captures fault-induced abrupt changes by computing their spatial distance, thereby overcoming the insufficient sensitivity of traditional fixed-threshold methods under noise and harmonic interference, and ultimately achieving intelligent identification of fault segments. 3.2 Gaussian filtering for error suppression and noise smoothing Building upon the effective identification of the fault time segment achieved in the previous section, further refinement is required to accurately determine the precise fault inception time. To address this need, an image comparison method based on Gaussian filtering is proposed. This approach effectively eliminates minor fitting errors and suppresses noise interference through signal smoothing, thereby significantly enhancing the accuracy and robustness of fault timing detection [ 34 ]. The detailed implementation method is as follows. First, load the images of the normal signal and fitted extended signal corresponding to the fault segment, verifying consistent image dimensions. Resize mismatched images to ensure uniform sizing, which is essential for proper image alignment and differential analysis in subsequent steps. Next, apply Gaussian filtering to mitigate minor fitting errors. When employing higher-order Fourier algorithms for fault signal fitting and extension, minor errors emerge at function peaks. These minor errors adversely impact the precision of subsequent fault timing detection. To obtain accurate fault timing results, it is essential to eliminate these minor errors. Gaussian filtering smooths signals by essentially performing a convolution operation on the image using a Gaussian kernel generated from the Gaussian function, thereby eliminating scattered noise interference [ 35 ]. We employ Gaussian filtering to process minor fitting errors. The specific implementation method is as follows. The Gaussian filter performs a weighted average on the target pixel and its neighboring pixels, with the weights determined by the Gaussian function. The Gaussian function is given by (7). The resulting 3 × 3 Gaussian kernel is shown in (8). G ( x , y ) is the weight value of the Gaussian kernel at point ( x , y ) . [ @ c c c @ 1 2 1 2 4 2 1 2 1 ] (11) The Gaussian filter uses a 3 × 3 kernel with a standard deviation σ = 0.5 . This choice is based on the observation that the high-order Fourier fitting produces minor fitting errors at the function peaks. The selected 3 × 3 kernel with σ = 0.5 effectively smooths these minor errors while preserving the sharp fault-induced transient. Sensitivity analysis shows that when σ varies within 0.3–0.8, the detection error remains below 0.1 ms. When σ is too large (>1.0), the signal becomes oversmoothed, and the detection error increases to above 0.15 ms. For each pixel, a weighted average is computed using its neighboring pixels within the defined kernel region. The weighting coefficients follow a Gaussian distribution, where pixels farther from the center receive progressively smaller weights. This weighted average then replaces the original pixel value. This resulting absolute difference value is then assigned to the pixel at the same position x, y in the difference image C. In regions where significant discrepancies occur between the original signal and the fitted extended signal—such as image areas corresponding to the occurrence or duration of a fault—the pixel values at these corresponding positions in the difference image will be substantially greater than zero. At regions with zero pixel values, the difference image will appear blank; whereas areas with significantly non-zero pixel values will highlight the dissimilarities between the original images. Fault time determination Based on the pixel-level fault localization established previously, the specific columns containing fault signatures have been identified. To determine the fault time, it is necessary to correlate each column of pixels with time information, thereby accurately obtaining the time corresponding to the fault occurrence moment. The timeline is evenly distributed across each column of the pixel image, ensuring that each column of pixels represents a specific time point or time period. In this way, when the algorithm detects the pixel column where the fault occurs, the exact time of the fault can be directly determined based on the time information corresponding to that column. Conclusion As active distribution networks continue to evolve, their increasingly complex operating environments have exposed the limitations of conventional fault detection methods in both efficiency and accuracy. In response, This paper has presented a fault time detection method for active distribution networks that, for the first time, combines three established techniques—damping-adaptive high-order Fourier fitting, Euclidean distance-based segment localization, and Gaussian filtering-aided pixel comparison—into a coarse-to-fine architecture tailored for signals containing harmonics and damped oscillations. While each individual technique is known, their integration and application to sub-0.1 ms fault timing under low sampling rates (≥2.5 kHz) is novel. The proposed architecture addresses two fundamental limitations of prior art: the inability of Prony-based methods to handle damping variations, and the sensitivity of direct pixel comparison to fitting residuals. To further clarify the level of novelty, a point-by-point comparison with the most relevant existing methods is provided. Compared with the Prony + pixel comparison method [ 27 ], the proposed method differs in three key aspects. First, it introduces a damping factor to achieve high-fidelity fitting of damped oscillatory signals, whereas [ 27 ] cannot handle such components. Second, it applies Gaussian filtering to suppress fitting residuals, while [ 27 ] performs direct pixel comparison without preprocessing, making it sensitive to noise. Third, it adopts a coarse-to-fine two-stage strategy that uses Euclidean distance for coarse localization before pixel-wise refinement, thereby reducing computational burden—a step that is absent in [ 27 ]. Similarly, when compared with the SVD-based method [ 26 ], the proposed method offers two distinct advantages. On one hand, it employs a fixed window segmented by the signal period instead of manually setting the number of Hankel matrix columns, eliminating the need for subjective parameter selection. On the other hand, it achieves automatic parameter tuning through an adaptive threshold criterion ( Eq 6 ), whereas the SVD method’s performance heavily depends on manual choices. Before discussing the performance, it is worth noting that the proposed method was developed and validated specifically for 10 kV active distribution networks, which represent a typical and widely deployed voltage level in medium-voltage distribution systems. Then, this approach employs an enhanced higher-order Fourier algorithm incorporating an attenuation factor, enabling high-fidelity reconstruction of fault signals containing higher-order harmonics and damped oscillatory components. By transforming time-domain signals into pixel-based representations and applying Gaussian filtering to eliminate fitting errors, the method achieves precise fault segment localization through Euclidean distance calculation and pixel-level analysis. The proposed technique demonstrates remarkable robustness across various challenging conditions, maintaining detection errors within 0.1 ms despite the presence of harmonics, damped oscillations, varying transition resistances, and different fault types. Furthermore, its compatibility with conventional sampling equipment and adaptability to different network topologies highlight its significant engineering applicability. Leveraging advances in distributed computing, this detection paradigm offers an optimal balance between computational efficiency and accuracy, presenting a practical and reliable solution for real-time fault diagnosis in modern power systems. In summary, this detection method presents a practical and reliable solution for real-time fault diagnosis in 10 kV active distribution systems. Extension to other voltage levels would require additional validation, as the method’s parameters were optimized based on the 10 kV network model used in this study. Nevertheless, since the algorithmic core is voltage-level agnostic and does not rely on topology-specific parameters, the method is expected to be transferable with minor recalibration.
