Dyscoordination of thalamic reticular spindles is associated with social memory deficits in mice and humans with autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Dongqi Cui, Xiaodan Wang, Ruosong Ai, Feng Gao, Huan Sun, Ying Zhang, Yixuan Lyu, Zhijie Zhang, Wen Li, Yuhang Qin, Yuxi Guo, Yinxia Liu, Ningxia Zhao, Juan Wang, Shen Li, Ying Wu, Siyuan Zhang, Changhe Wang, Feidi Wang, Yan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSocial memory, the ability to recognize and remember conspecifics, is frequently impaired in psychiatric disorders such as autism, yet the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. We examined the role of sleep spindles across non-rapid eye movement sleep in social memory consolidation, focusing on the sensory thalamic reticular nucleus (sTRN). Here we show that impaired spindles were associated with defective social memory. Through pharmacological/optogenetic manipulations and optical Ca2+ recordings, we demonstrated that parvalbumin-positive neurons in the sTRN (sTRNPvalb) are essential for sleep spindle generation, mediated by the sTRNPvalb–ventroposteromedial (VPM) thalamic nucleus circuit. Increasing the activity of the sTRNPvalb–VPM pathway could rescue impaired spindles and social memory deficits in Neuroligin 2 mutant mice. Moreover, we developed machine learning-based models to predict autism in children based on spindle eigenvalues. These findings underscore the critical role of spindles in social memory, suggesting spindles may serve as candidate diagnostic markers.Subject terms: Social behaviour, Neural circuits, Autism spectrum disorders, Consolidation

Introduction

Social memory, a keystone cognitive process facilitating the recognition and recollection of conspecifics, is impaired in patients with various psychiatric disorders, such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD)1–3. While prior research has highlighted the crucial involvement of the hippocampus4–6, prefrontal cortex7, medial septum1, and sensory thalamic reticular nucleus (sTRN), which was identified in our recent study3, in social memory, the specific mechanisms underlying social memory deficits in individuals with ASD remain unknown.

More than 50% of children with ASD have sleep problems8,9, including difficulty falling asleep, a short sleep duration, and sleep fragmentation. However, the causal relationship between social memory impairment and sleep has not been established, and the underlying circuit mechanism has not been identified. Sleep spindles, oscillatory activity in the frequency range of 10–15 Hz, are the primary indicators of non-rapid eye movement (NREM) stage 2 sleep10. Given that the coupling between sleep spindles and hippocampal sharp wave ripples (SWRs) plays a crucial role in hippocampus-dependent memory consolidation11–14, we aimed to investigate the relationship between sleep spindles and social memory in ASD.

The thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN) serves as a pacemaker of sleep spindles and is primarily composed of GABAergic interneurons10,15,16. The TRN plays a crucial role in filtering and transferring information between the cortex and thalamus and is involved in regulating sleep–wake cycles17,18, sensory gating19, and higher-level cognitive functions20. Given the anatomical and functional convergence of sleep, sensory gating, and social cognition in the TRN circuitry, we hypothesized that the dysfunction of TRN-generated spindle activity may contribute to social memory deficits in individuals with ASD.

Here, we employed electroencephalography (EEG) monitoring, pharmacological and optogenetic manipulations, and electrophysiological recordings in conjunction with Ca²⁺ imaging fiber photometry in freely moving mice to identify parvalbumin-positive (PV) neurons in the sTRN and their projections to the ventroposteromedial (VPM) nucleus, which regulates spindle generation and social memory consolidation. In parallel, we conducted overnight sleep EEG recordings in children with ASD and typically developing (TD) children, quantified spindle characteristics, and assessed their associations with performance on a simulated social memory task. We further applied machine learning techniques to model individual spindle parameters as predictive features for the diagnosis of ASD and to explore their translational potential.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and computational neuroscience have enabled the extraction of clinically relevant neurophysiological features from complex behavioral and neural datasets, offering promising avenues for an objective diagnosis and phenotypic stratification in patients with neuropsychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia, depression, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder21–25. Among these, machine learning models utilizing sleep-related biomarkers have shown strong potential to predict populations with neuropsychiatric disorders with high sensitivity and specificity26. However, few studies have systematically linked the circuit-level mechanisms of neural oscillations to computational diagnostic frameworks. By integrating causal circuit dissection in animal models with machine learning-driven classification of human EEG spindle data, our study bridges this gap and identifies sleep spindles as both a mechanistic substrate and a quantifiable biomarker for ASD-associated social memory impairments.

Results

Sleep spindles are positively associated with social memory

We pharmacologically inhibited sleep spindles in mice using sodium oxybate (SO), a γ-hydroxybutyric acid sodium salt known to activate GABAB receptors and modulate GABAA/GABAC activity27, to investigate the role of spindles in social memory consolidation. While initially developed as an anesthetic, SO has been shown to significantly reduce the spindle density during sleep28. The mice were administered SO or saline intraperitoneally prior to undergoing a short-term (30 min interval) three-chamber test (TCT)1,4,28, and their EEG signals were recorded at 30 min interval (Fig. 1a). Compared with saline treatment, SO administration significantly suppressed spindle activity, as evidenced by a decreased spindle density (number of spindle events per minute during NREM sleep time) and reduced spindle duration (Fig. 1b, c). The control mice displayed intact short-term social memory, preferring the novel mouse over the familiar one during the retrieval stage (stage 3) (Fig. 1d). In contrast, the SO-treated mice showed no preference between the novel and familiar mice (Fig. 1d), and their reduced discrimination index confirmed impaired social memory (Fig. 1d). This impairment was observed in both male and female mice (Supplementary Fig. 1). Importantly, baseline sociability during the encoding stage (stage 2) of the TCT (Supplementary Fig. 2a) and memory of inanimate objects (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c) remained unaffected by SO treatment. Furthermore, the discrimination index of the mice was strongly correlated with spindle density and duration (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. 1d), whereas no significant relationship was observed for inanimate object memory (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e). Behavioral analyses revealed that SO treatment did not alter locomotor activity or anxiety levels (Supplementary Fig. 2f, g).

Moreover, we performed a more rigorous five-trial social memory test4,29 after SO administration to further verify the relationship between spindles and social memory (Supplementary Fig. 2h). The results showed that the control mice exhibited the expected social memory behavior, in which they progressively decreased their interaction time with the familiar mouse across trials 3 and 4 and subsequently increased their interaction time with a novel mouse to baseline levels in trial 5. In contrast, mice injected with sodium oxybate failed to show a trial-dependent decrease in interaction time across the five significantly impaired trials (Supplementary Fig. 2i), a behavioral pattern indicative of memory deficits. Together, findings from the three-chamber social novelty test and the five-trial social memory paradigm support the conclusion that spindles are critical for social memory formation.

In addition, SO reduced sleep latency, whereas the overall percentages of NREM and REM sleep were not affected, although SO significantly fragmented sleep (Supplementary Fig. 3a–f). SO administration also increased δ-wave power but had no significant effect on θ-wave power and reduced α- and β-wave power, indicating the promotion of deeper sleep states (Supplementary Fig. 3g). However, these spectral changes were not significantly correlated with the discrimination index of the mice (Supplementary Fig. 3h), suggesting that spindle activity plays a specific role in social memory consolidation compared with other sleep waveforms. Furthermore, SO administration did not affect the amplitude or frequency of spindles at 30 min intervals in mice (Supplementary Fig. 3i), and significant correlations were not observed between the amplitude or frequency of spindles and the discrimination index in S3 (Supplementary Fig. 3j).

To evaluate whether pharmacological activation of sleep spindles enhances social memory, we utilized zolpidem (ZOL), a positive allosteric modulator of GABAA commonly used as a sedative30. ZOL has been reported to effectively increase spindle generation28. The mice were administered ZOL prior to undergoing a long-term (2 h interval) social memory test (Fig. 1a). ZOL treatment significantly increased spindle production, as shown by the increased spindle density and extended mean duration (Fig. 1f, g). Importantly, this intervention prolonged social memory retention to at least 2 hours in ZOL-treated mice, whereas the memory of control mice diminished substantially over the same interval (Fig. 1h). The facilitation of spindles by ZOL during social interactions did not affect baseline sociability (Supplementary Fig. 2j) or inanimate object memory (Supplementary Fig. 2k). Furthermore, the discrimination index for mice, but not for objects, was positively correlated with the spindle density and duration (Fig. 1i; Supplementary Fig. 2l, m). Locomotor activity and anxiety levels remained unaffected by ZOL administration (Supplementary Fig. 2n). In addition, ZOL also reduced the sleep latency, but the overall percentages of NREM and REM sleep were not affected (Supplementary Fig. 3k–m). ZOL administration did not affect the amplitude or frequency of spindles (Supplementary Fig. 3n), although the frequency of spindles was significantly negatively correlated with the discrimination index at S3 (Supplementary Fig. 3o). These findings highlight the essential and specific role of sleep spindles in consolidating social memory, underscoring their importance in memory processes during sleep.

sTRNPvalb neurons are activated during the sleep spindle phase across NREM sleep

To uncover the brain regions critical for sleep spindle–associated social memory consolidation, we focused on the thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN), a central pacemaker of sleep spindles10. Previous studies have shown that parvalbumin-positive (PV) neurons primarily regulate spindle rhythms10,19,31, with the sTRN exhibiting a higher density of spindles than the limbic TRN (lTRN)20,32. This finding led us to hypothesize that PV neurons in the sTRN (sTRNPvalb neurons) play a pivotal role in modulating sleep spindles. To test this hypothesis, we combined fiber photometry with EEG/EMG recordings to monitor the real-time activity of sTRNPvalb neurons during sleep (Fig. 2a). Using PV-Cre transgenic mice, we expressed the calcium indicator GCaMP6s in sTRNPvalb neurons via adeno-associated virus (AAV) injections (rAAV9-EF1α-DIO-GCaMP6s) and measured neuronal calcium activity in freely moving animals. Our results revealed that sTRNPvalb neurons exhibited robust activation during sleep spindle events (Fig. 2b, c). This activation was significantly greater during non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep than during wakefulness or rapid eye movement (REM) sleep (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, sTRNPvalb activity increased markedly during transitions from wakefulness to NREM sleep and decreased during transitions from NREM to wakefulness or REM sleep (Fig. 2e–h). Notably, the Δz-score9 of single calcium event was positively correlated with the amplitude of the currently identified spindle (Fig. 2i). These findings indicate that sTRNPvalb neurons are strongly and specifically activated during spindle generation, underscoring their central role in regulating sleep spindle activity.

sTRNPvalb neurons are required for sleep spindle generation and social memory consolidation

We recently established that sTRNPvalb neurons are essential for encoding and retrieving social memory3. Given their robust activation during spindle generation throughout NREM sleep, we next explored whether sTRNPvalb neurons are required for sleep spindle formation and, consequently, for social memory consolidation. To investigate this, we employed chemogenetic inhibition by expressing an inhibitory designer receptor exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADD), hM4Di, in sTRNPvalb neurons (rAAV9-hSyn-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry). Following this, we administered the DREADD activator clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) or saline (Sal) to the mice during the interval following the encoding phase (S2) and recorded EEG signals (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Chemogenetic inhibition of sTRNPvalb neurons significantly disrupted short-term (30 min) social memory, as evidenced by a lack of preference during the retrieval phase (S3). Notably, baseline sociability remained unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). CNO infusion in hM4Di-expressing mice markedly reduced the sleep spindle density and shortened the spindle duration but did not affect the spindle amplitude or frequency (Supplementary Fig. 4d). Importantly, these manipulations had no effect on inanimate object memory, locomotor activity, or anxiety levels (Supplementary Fig. 4e–g). Furthermore, in mice expressing hM4Di in lTRNPvalb neurons, CNO administration did not affect sleep spindles or social memory (Supplementary Fig. 5). These results demonstrate that sTRNPvalb but not lTRNPvalb neurons are indispensable for generating sleep spindles that are essential for consolidating short-term social memory.

To determine whether activating sTRNPvalb neurons can enhance sleep spindle generation and promote social memory, we employed an optogenetic approach. Cre-dependent AAVs encoding either ChR2–GFP (rAAV9-hSyn-DIO-ChR2-GFP) or GFP alone (rAAV9-hSyn-DIO-GFP) were injected into the sTRN of PV-Cre mice, and an optical fiber was implanted above the sTRN. This approach enabled the selective expression of channelrhodopsin (ChR2) and GFP in the sTRNPvalb neurons of the experimental group, whereas the control mice expressed only GFP (Fig. 3a). During the social memory test intervals, the sTRN of PV-Cre mice received spindle-like optogenetic stimulation at 470 nm11,33,34 (41.7 ms on/off, 12 Hz, 1 s every 8.5 s, 4 mW), mimicking the natural frequency, duration, and intervals of sleep spindles (10–15 Hz, 1 s duration, 7.5 s intervals) (Fig. 3b)10. This stimulation significantly induced the generation of sleep spindles in the experimental (ChR2) group of mice, whereas this effect was not observed in the control (GFP) group (Fig. 3b), indicating that spindle-like laser stimulation does not affect the spindles. Spindle-like optogenetic stimulation at 470 nm in the 30 min interval of the TCT significantly increased the number of sleep spindles in the experimental group and increased the preference for the novel mouse, which was not observed in control mice (Fig. 3c–e). Meanwhile, spindle duration, amplitude, and intrinsic frequency remained stable (Fig. 3c), suggesting that spindle-like optogenetic activation of sTRNPvalb neurons facilitates the initiation of spindle events without perturbing their intrinsic physiological features. Additionally, optogenetic enhancement of spindles at 2 h interval extended the duration of social memory retention to 2 hours in the experimental group, whereas control mice showed no improvement (Fig. 3f–h). Importantly, this manipulation did not affect baseline sociability or inanimate object memory (Supplementary Fig. 6a–d, i–l). In addition, optogenetic activation significantly shortened the sleep latency in the experimental group of mice but did not affect the percentage of time spent in each state, the average duration of each state, or the fragmentation score during the 30 min interval of the TCT (Supplementary Fig. 6e–h). These findings collectively highlight the critical role of sTRNPvalb neurons in generating sleep spindles and facilitating the consolidation of social memory in mice.

The sTRNPvalb–VPM circuit modulates spindle production and social memory consolidation

Having established an essential role for sTRNPvalb neurons in social memory consolidation through sleep spindles, we next investigated whether projections from these neurons to specific thalamic subregions are crucial for this process. To trace axonal projections, we expressed GFP in sTRNPvalb neurons by injecting a Cre-dependent virus into the sTRN of PV-Cre mice (rAAV9-hSyn-DIO-ChR2-GFP). This revealed dense axonal labeling in several thalamic regions, including the parafascicular (PF), ventroposteromedial (VPM), and posterior complex (PO) thalamic nuclei (Fig. 4a). To confirm the thalamic subregions that predominantly receive projections from sTRNPvalb neurons, we performed unilateral injections of mCherry-labeled retrograde AAV into the PF, VPM, and PO (rAAVretro-hSyn-mCherry). We observed robust mCherry expression in the sTRN of the mice that received a virus injection in the VPM, whereas the mice injected in the PF or PO regions exhibited substantially weaker labeling in the sTRN (Fig. 4b). Notably, nearly all retrogradely labeled mCherry-positive neurons were PV-positive, further supporting the specificity of the projections. These findings suggest that sTRNPvalb–VPM projections may be involved in the process through which sleep spindles regulate social memory consolidation.

To investigate the role of sTRNPvalb–VPM projections in sleep spindles production and social memory consolidation, we bilaterally injected rAAVretro-hSyn-FLP into the VPM region and Cre- and FLP-dependent rAAV-hSyn-CON FON-hChR2-eYFP into the sTRN region of PV-Cre mice, enabling the expression of ChR2 in sTRNPvalb neurons projecting to the VPM. We then implanted optical fibers above the VPM and applied spindle-like optogenetic stimulation during behavioral intervals (Fig. 4c, d). Optogenetic activation of the sTRNPvalb–VPM projections significantly increased the spindle density, although it did not affect the duration, amplitude, or frequency of spindles (Fig. 4e). This manipulation enhanced the preference for novel mice at 30 min intervals and extended the duration of social memory to 2 hours, whereas baseline sociability and inanimate object memory were not affected (Fig. 4f–h, Supplementary Fig. 7).

Given the established role of sTRNPvalb–PF projections in social memory3, we next asked whether this circuit also contributes to the modulation of sleep spindle activity. To investigate this, we employed the same dual-recombinase strategy: rAAVretro-hSyn-FLP was bilaterally injected into the PF of PV-Cre mice, whereas Cre- and FLP-dependent rAAV-hSyn-CON FON-hChR2-eYFP was bilaterally injected into the sTRN. This approach enabled the selective expression of ChR2 in sTRNPvalb neurons that project specifically to the PF (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). We then activated the sTRNPvalb–PF circuit with a 470 nm laser to induce spindle-like stimulation during the 30 min and 2 h interval social memory consolidation periods. We found no significant changes in either social memory or spindles (Supplementary Fig. 8c–h). However, during the encoding or retrieval stages of social memory, optogenetic activation of the sTRNPvalb–PF circuit significantly improved the social memory of the mice, consistent with our previous work3, although the spindles remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 8c–e). Collectively, these findings provide insights into the circuit mechanisms through which sTRNPvalb–VPM projections mediate sleep spindles, ultimately promoting social memory consolidation.

Nlgn2–/– mice exhibit impaired sTRNPvalb–VPM transmission and spindle disruption

We next sought to determine whether the sTRNPvalb–VPM circuit is involved in spindle disruption and social memory deficits in ASD. To answer this question, we obtained neuroligin 2 knockout (Nlgn2–/–) mice, a member of the NLGN family of ASD-associated genes35,36. Given the critical role of Nlgn2 in inhibitory synaptic transmission35, the spindle abnormality in Nlgn2–/– mice may originate from impaired sTRNPvalb–VPM transmission. To assess synaptic transmission in the sTRNPvalb–VPM circuit, we performed whole-cell patch-clamp recordings in the VPM region (Fig. 5a). We identified VPM neurons innervated by ChR2-expressing (rAAV9-PV-ChR2-EGFP) presynaptic terminals by detecting inhibitory postsynaptic currents (IPSCs) evoked by blue-light optical stimulation, which were blocked by picrotoxin (PTX) in acute brain slices (Fig. 5b). We observed that compared with their wild-type littermates (WT), VPM neurons in Nlgn2–/– mice, which receive input from sTRNPvalb projections, presented significant increases in the paired-pulse ratio (PPR) (Fig. 5c) and marked reductions in the frequency and amplitude of spontaneous IPSCs (Fig. 5d). Consistent with a previous report36, Nlgn2–/– mice presented normal baseline sociability (Fig. 5e). Importantly, Nlgn2–/– mice displayed a decreased spindle density, although the spindle duration remained unchanged (Fig. 5f), and exhibited impaired social memory (Fig. 5g). Notably, the discrimination index between the novel and familiar mice correlated positively with the spindle density but not with the spindle duration (Fig. 5h). These findings suggest that the spindle disruption due to impaired sTRNPvalb–VPM transmission in Nlgn2–/– mice may underlie their social memory deficits.

Activation of the sTRNPvalb–VPM circuit rescues aberrant sleep spindles and deficient social memory in Nlgn2–/– mice

To address whether impaired sTRNPvalb–VPM transmission, resulting in spindle abnormalities, contributes to social memory deficits, we bilaterally injected viruses to express ChR2 in sTRNPvalb neurons of Nlgn2–/– mice (rAAV9–PV–ChR2–EGFP). We then implanted optical fibers above the VPM and performed spindle-like optogenetic stimulation during various intervals of the TCT (Fig. 6a, b). We recorded sIPSCs from the identified VPM neurons during spindle-like stimulation. This optogenetic stimulation increased the frequency of sIPSCs in Nlgn2–/– mice (Fig. 6c, d). These results indicate that spindle-like optical stimulation of sTRN terminals enhances GABAergic transmission to VPM neurons, further supporting the idea that impaired sTRNPvalb–VPM transmission underlies the social memory deficits observed in these mice.

We recorded EEG signals and performed a social memory test in Nlgn2–/– mice expressing ChR2–EGFP to determine whether enhanced synaptic transmission impacts sleep spindles and social memory in Nlgn2–/– mice. Spindle-like optical stimulation increased the spindle density without affecting the spindle duration (Fig. 6e). Furthermore, this stimulation successfully rescued social memory deficits in Nlgn2–/– mice (Fig. 6f). Additionally, ZOL administration to Nlgn2–/– mice prior to the social memory test restored both the spindle density and social memory performance (Fig. 6g, h). This outcome is consistent with the increased spindle density and improved social memory observed in C57BL/6 J mice following ZOL treatment (Fig. 1g, h). These findings suggest that spindle dysfunction associated with impairments in the sTRNPvalb–VPM pathway contributes to social memory deficits in Nlgn2–/– mice.

High-functioning children with ASD have defective sleep spindles and facial recognition memory

The results above indicate that sleep spindles play a critical role in the consolidation of social memories during NREM sleep in mice, as evidenced by the positive correlations between spindle properties and social memory in WT and ASD mice but not with inanimate object memory (Fig. 1e, i; Supplementary Fig. 2d, e, l, m). As an approach to examine whether similar spindle impairments are present in children with ASD who experience social memory deficits, we recruited 14 high-functioning children with ASD and 13 typically developing (TD) children for overnight sleep EEG recordings, the facial recognition memory test37 as a measure of simulated social memory, and the car recognition memory test to assess memory of inanimate objects. These tests were conducted with a group of children with ASD and TD children on the same night as the EEG recordings, with testing occurring before bedtime for the encoding stage and after waking up the following day for the recall stage of the facial and car recognition tasks (Fig. 7a). Given the strong association between sleep spindles and cognitive ability, we recruited high-functioning children with ASD who scored above 75 on the Wechsler Intelligence Scale to ensure that they could complete the recognition memory tests. No significant differences in age, sex, or Wechsler scores were observed between the ASD and TD groups (Supplementary Table 1).

A comparative analysis of the clinical EEG data revealed that the children with ASD in our study presented sleep architecture similar to that of the TD children, with no significant differences in total sleep time, NREM stage 1 (N1) time, NREM stage 2 (N2) time, NREM stage 3 (N3) time, or percentage of REM sleep (Supplementary Table 1). However, significant differences in the generation and functionality of sleep spindles were observed between the two groups in terms of the spindle characteristics of TD children, and children with ASD presented quantitatively different and morphologically abnormal spindles (Fig. 7b, c). The spectrograms also revealed a marked decrease in power within the spindle band (Fig. 7d). The mean duration, amplitude, and frequency of spindles in children with ASD were similar to those in TD children, and a notable decrease in spindle density and a reduction in absolute chirp values were observed in the ASD group (Fig. 7e–i). Compared with TD children, children with ASD presented impaired overnight facial recognition memory, whereas their car recognition memory remained normal (Fig. 7j, k). Moreover, the spindle density was positively correlated, and spindle chirp was negatively correlated with facial recognition memory accuracy but not with car recognition memory accuracy in both groups of children (Fig. 7j, k). Spindles were classified into slow and fast types based on their frequencies to characterize these alterations further (Supplementary Fig. 9a–e). Decreases in the spindle density and absolute chirp values were primarily observed in slow spindle waves (Supplementary Fig. 9a, e). These findings suggest that abnormalities in sleep spindle generation in children with ASD may contribute to their deficits in social memory.

The characteristics of sleep spindles can be used to predict ASD

Given the impairment in sleep spindles and social memory observed in high-functioning children with ASD, we sought to determine whether spindle-related parameters in EEG data could serve as reliable indicators for predicting ASD in a larger cohort including both high- and low-functioning children. We collected overnight sleep EEG recordings from 68 children with autism and 68 age- and gender-matched typically developing controls (Supplementary Table 2). We included all extracted spindle parameters (mean density, duration, amplitude, frequency, and chirp) from all EEG channels and both hemispheres as the raw dataset. The data were then split into an 80% training set and a 20% independent test set. Three machine learning models—logistic regression (LR) with L2 regularization, linear-kernel support vector machine (SVM), and distance-weighted k-nearest neighbors (KNN)—were independently trained on the training set. A 5-fold cross-validation approach was used to optimize the model hyperparameters. Each model was then retrained on the full training set using the optimal parameters and subsequently evaluated on the independent test set to assess performance (Fig. 8a).

The t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) visualization (Fig. 8b) revealed a clear intrinsic clustering structure in the data and demonstrated consistent decision boundaries across the three models. Performance evaluation on the independent test set demonstrated that the LR model achieved the highest AUC (Fig. 8c) and outperformed both the SVM and KNN models across all major classification metrics, including accuracy (88.9%), precision (91.7%), recall (84.6%), and F1 score (88.0%) (Supplementary Table 3). The superior predictive performance and robustness of the LR model were further validated by bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations), which consistently showed its advantage across all evaluated metrics (Fig. 8d–g). Moreover, coefficients of variation (CVs) calculated from the bootstrap distributions indicated high overall model stability (accuracy CV < 10%), with the LR model exhibiting the greatest robustness among the three classifiers. Permutation feature importance further revealed that, among these features, spindle density and chirp are the major contributors to the LR, SVM and KNN models, followed by frequency and duration (Fig. 8h). Consistent with these findings, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis identified spindle density as the most influential feature in the SVM and KNN models, whereas chirp remained the dominant contributor in the LR model (Fig. 8i and Supplementary Fig. 9f). These findings suggest that sleep spindle features in EEG recordings can effectively predict ASD, providing strong evidence for their use as biomarkers. Taken together, these findings suggest that dysregulated sleep spindles, resulting from impaired sTRNPvalb–VPM transmission, contribute to social memory deficits in individuals with ASD and that sleep spindles may serve as a candidate biomarker for ASD (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that sTRNPvalb neurons play crucial roles in the formation of sleep spindles, thereby influencing social memory consolidation. The sTRNPvalb neurons subsequently transmit spindles with conspecific information to the VPM. Importantly, the abnormal sTRNPvalb–VPM connection in Nlgn2–/– mice serves as the foundation for spindle disruption and impaired social memory, which can be rescued by the activation of this circuit. Furthermore, based on the overnight EEG analyses and recognition tasks, we established a correlation between compromised sleep spindle functionality and social memory deficits in children with ASD. Furthermore, machine learning models utilizing spindle features as biomarkers can be applied to predict ASD (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Sleep is a vital process for the consolidation of multiple types of memories, including social memories13. Research has demonstrated that REM sleep facilitates the consolidation of social memories38. Although SWRs in the hippocampal CA2 region during NREM sleep have been shown to enhance social memory39, the role of other NREM components, such as sleep spindles, remains uncertain. Sleep spindles, which are distinctive brain waves that occur during NREM sleep10, have been linked to various sleep-related effects, including enhanced sleep stability, augmented synaptic plasticity, and improved memory consolidation. According to the “active systems consolidation” theory, sleep spindles facilitate the effective transfer of information from the hippocampus to the neocortex by coupling with slow cortical oscillations and hippocampal SWRs, thereby promoting memory consolidation during sleep10,11. Here, we show that the activation of sTRNPvalb neurons during NREM sleep can enhance social memory through sleep spindles and that the disruption of these spindles impairs social memory. Our findings underscore the critical roles of spindle waves in regulating social memory and suggest a potential upstream mechanism by which hippocampal SWRs process social memory-related information.

Our results improve our understanding of the mechanisms by which sleep promotes the consolidation of social memory. NREM and REM sleep play crucial and complementary roles in social memory consolidation13. During REM sleep, active SuM neurons facilitate the consolidation of social memory through their projections to the CA2 region38. Conversely, CA2 SWRs during NREM sleep contribute to the reactivation of social memory engram cells, thereby reinforcing social memory39. In this study, we found that sTRNPvalb neurons are activated during NREM sleep, promoting the generation of sleep spindles through projections to the VPM, further enhancing social memory. The collaborative activity of the TRN and CA2 regions during NREM sleep and the SuM region during REM sleep may constitute a complete mechanism for sleep-related social memory consolidation. Another important question to address is whether NREM sleep spindles from the sTRN are coupled with CA2 SWRs, thereby facilitating information transfer between the sTRN and CA2 regions. In future studies, causal design experiments should be incorporated to systematically investigate the influence of these interactions on social memory.

Notably, anterograde tracing reveals dense projections of sTRNPvalb neurons to multiple thalamic nuclei (PF, VPM, and PO), but the VPM retrograde projections to sTRNPvalb cells are much more abundant than to the PF, implying that while the axons of a small number of sTRN neurons branch abundantly and project to the PF, more neurons are actually involved in the functional activity of the VPM circuit. This difference was verified in our photomodulation experiments: optogenetic spindle-like activation of the sTRNPvalb–VPM pathway amplified thalamic spindle waves and improved social memory during the intertrial consolidation window, whereas the same stimulation of the sTRNPvalb–PF pathway was ineffective during this period. In contrast, sTRNPvalb–PF operations remained effective when applied during encoding or retrieval, replicating our previous findings4. Thus, the VPM and PF branches of axons from the sTRNPvalb neurons are not redundant; they operate in different time frames of social memory: the VPM during consolidation-dependent spindle events and the PF during initial encoding and later retrieval. Mechanistic studies can be conducted by analyzing the electrophysiological, morphological, and molecular differences between sTRNPvalb neurons involved and not involved in the formation of spindles in the future.

Significant differences in anatomy and molecular expression patterns have been observed across the TRN15,20,40. In vivo multichannel studies have shown that more sleep spindles are detected in the sTRN than in the limbic TRN, particularly in the somatosensory TRN20,32. sTRNPvalb neurons have been suggested to be the main generators of sleep spindles because of their prominent postinhibitory rebound bursts, high peak densities, and rhythmic T-currents19. In our study, we directly observed the activation of sTRNPvalb neurons during the emergence of spindles in NREM sleep by combining EEG recordings with fiber photometry. These findings provide strong evidence that sTRNPvalb neurons are the primary neurons that mediate sleep spindles. Sleep spindles originate from rebound bursts in the TRN following strong inhibition of thalamocortical neurons10. Although sTRNPvalb neurons are known to project broadly to the thalamic nucleus3, the specific pathway through which spindle waves transmit social memory-related information remains unknown. Here, we demonstrated that the manipulation of sTRNPvalb neurons and their projections to the VPM could reliably regulate sleep spindles and social memory, highlighting the essential role of the VPM in the transmission of spindle waves related to social memory processing. Previous clinical studies have shown that children with ASD exhibit significant social memory dysfunction and impaired thalamocortical connectivity41–43. Therefore, we employed ASD model mice, namely, Nlgn2–/– mice, and found that these mice displayed significantly impaired sTRNPvalb–VPM transmission36, suggesting that impaired sleep spindles due to abnormal sTRNPvalb–VPM transmission constitute the neural mechanism through which Nlgn2 deficiency leads to social memory deficits36. These findings suggest that social memory deficits in individuals with ASD may occur due to disruptions in sleep spindles that are potentially caused by abnormalities in the sTRNPvalb–VPM circuit. Notably, optogenetic activation of this circuit rescued social memory deficits in Nlgn2–/– mice, suggesting that this circuit is a potential therapeutic target.

Previous studies of sleep spindles in individuals with ASD have reported controversial results44–50. Some studies have suggested that sleep spindles are significantly impaired in children with ASD, consistent with our findings, whereas other studies have not reported such impairments. This discrepancy may be related to the heterogeneity of ASD, as sleep spindles are strongly correlated with intelligence levels10,51, and 31.8–50.8% of children with ASD have an intellectual disability52. We recruited children with ASD who had Wechsler scores higher than 75 and compared their sleep spindles with those of TD children to explore spindle changes in individuals with ASD more clearly. Despite their similar Wechsler scores, the children in the ASD group presented significantly impaired sleep spindles and notable social memory deficits, but their car recognition abilities were similar to those of TD children. More importantly, a significant correlation between sleep spindle properties and social memory was observed in both children with ASD and TD children.

Diagnosing ASD at an early stage is crucial, as an early diagnosis can facilitate the development of communication skills in individuals with ASD over time53. At present, the clinical diagnosis of ASD depends mainly on scale scores, which may be susceptible to bias due to the subjective interpretation of physicians54. High-functioning children with ASD often display higher levels of language skills and intelligence than low-functioning children with ASD, which may contribute to the reduced likelihood of an early diagnosis55. We propose that the sleep spindle features can serve as an objective biomarker, potentially facilitating the diagnosis of ASD, particularly in high-functioning children with ASD56. Moreover, this index could be applied to assess treatment effectiveness.

Our findings are primarily based on children with ASD who do not present with comorbid neuropsychiatric conditions. Therefore, the conclusions drawn from this study may not be directly generalizable to broader clinical populations, such as adults with ASD or individuals with co-occurring conditions such as epilepsy or intellectual disability. Future large-scale, multicenter, cross-cohort, real-world sleep EEG studies in individuals with ASD should systematically collect and incorporate statistical data into machine learning models while also examining their cross-diagnostic specificity across individuals with various neuropsychiatric conditions. Moreover, as methods to increase the reliability of clinical diagnoses, integrating EEG with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and genomic data may offer a more comprehensive understanding of the genetic and neural circuit mechanisms underlying spindle abnormalities in ASD patients in future studies. Such a multimodal approach could elucidate how specific genetic variations contribute to disrupted thalamocortical dynamics and social memory impairments, thereby advancing both mechanistic insights and the development of biomarkers for ASD.

In conclusion, we reveal the critical role of sleep spindles in social memory consolidation and that sTRNPvalb–VPM projections are the key pathway mediating sleep spindle generation. In addition, the correlation between sleep spindle properties and social memory allows us to use spindle properties as biomarkers to guide the clinical diagnosis of social memory impairment in individuals with ASD. This study advances our understanding of the mechanisms by which NREM sleep contributes to the consolidation of social memory; moreover, we elucidated the role of sleep spindle dysregulation in the social memory deficits observed in individuals with ASD.

Methods

Participants

The participants were recruited from the Xi’an Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine and Brain Disease and Yan’an People’s Hospital. The diagnosis was made by an experienced clinician based on the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised57 and the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule58 and conformed to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV-TR) criteria. We recruited two cohorts. In the first cohort, participants underwent face and car recognition tasks, as well as overnight EEG recordings. None of the patients had diagnoses other than ASD; moreover, none of the patients were medicated. All participants had global IQ scores above 75 (see below) and were right-handed. The control group consisted of 13 typically developing (TD) children (mean age = 9.462 years, SD = 1.898 years, range = 6–12 years) who were recruited through advertisements in the community. The final group of participants included in the analysis consisted of 14 high-functioning children with ASD (mean age = 8.500 years, SD = 2.066 years, range = 6–12 years) and 13 TD children (mean age = 9.462 years, SD = 1.898 years, range = 6–12 years). The groups were matched for age, sex, and global IQ. For the second cohort, to obtain sufficient spindle data for machine learning training, we recruited additional TD children and children with ASD, including both high-functioning and low-functioning individuals, building upon the first cohort. None of the patients had received medication, and all participants were right-handed. These newly recruited children underwent overnight EEG recordings but did not participate in the face or car recognition tasks. In total, data were obtained from 68 children with ASD (mean age = 7.971 years, SD = 1.814 years, range = 6–12 years) and 68 age- and sex-matched TD children (mean age = 7.397 years, SD = 1.756 years, range = 6–12 years). Gender of participants was determined based on self-report. Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of child participants, and verbal assent was obtained from the children themselves. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (approval No.: XJTU1AF2023LSK-481). Compensation for each participant included transportation and participation in the experiment.

Laboratory sleep measures

EEG data acquisition

The participants were asked to maintain a regular sleep‒wake schedule for 14 days, complete a sleep diary during this period, and refrain from napping during the day before the recording (none of the participants were regular nappers). Beverages and foods containing stimulant substances (e.g., soda and chocolate) were not permitted after noon, either at home or at the hospital during the recording protocol. The participants went to bed at their preferred time based on their individual sleep agenda. Whole-night sleep EEG data was collected from participants during an inpatient, overnight stay. Electrode placement was performed in accordance with the international 10–20 system, utilizing 20 recording electrodes (Fp1/2, F3/4, F7/8, C3/4, P3/4, T3/4, T5/6, O1/2, Fpz, Cz, Oz, and Pz). Data were acquired at a sampling rate of 500 Hz and referenced to the contralateral mastoids45.

Sleep architecture

Sleep EEG signals were preprocessed using the open-source software Luna (https://zzz.bwh.harvard.edu/luna/). The participants’ polysomnography (PSG) data were visually scored in 30 s epochs according to the AASM criteria before data analysis. Each epoch was staged as wake, REM, N1, N2, or N3. Additional metrics, including the time in bed, total sleep time (TST), wake time after sleep onset, and sleep efficiency, were assessed. The percentage of the TST spent in each sleep stage was also calculated.

Spindle detection

Spindles were detected using an automated algorithm implemented in Luna, with counts obtained for each EEG channel. Detection was based on a wavelet-based method (for a detailed description of the wavelet spindle detection method, see reference59). The center frequencies for slow and fast spindles were set at 11 Hz and 15 Hz, respectively, corresponding to the central frequencies of the Morlet wavelet. After wavelet transformation, spindle identification used the following criteria: the wavelet amplitude was smoothed with a 0.1-s sliding window and normalized to the mean amplitude across all N2 sleep epochs. A spindle was defined by a core interval of at least 0.3 s during which the signal exceeded 4.5 times the mean amplitude. Additionally, each spindle had to contain an extended segment of at least 0.5 s where the signal remained above twice the mean amplitude. Candidate spindle events longer than 3 s were excluded, and spindles occurring within 0.5 s of each other were merged. In addition to the spindle density (counts per minute), several morphological parameters were extracted following bandpass filtering: spindle duration (including core and flanking intervals), amplitude (maximum interpeak amplitude), frequency (zero-crossing rate), and chirp (logarithmic change in interpeak intervals between the first and second halves of the spindle). All parameters—density, duration, amplitude, frequency, and chirp—were averaged across the spindles detected for each subject.

Face and car recognition tasks

For the face and car recognition tasks, E-prime 2.0 software (Psychology Software Tools, Inc., Pittsburgh, PA) was used to present the stimuli60. The face stimuli consisted of neutral facial expressions selected from a library of grayscale averaged Chinese facial affective picture systems37. The car stimuli were designed in-house to contain frontal views of different cars. The participants were seated 15 inches (38.1 cm) away from a 16-inch (40.64 cm) computer screen. The face and car recognition tasks included a learning phase (approximately 30 min before bedtime) and a recognition test in the morning (approximately 30 min after waking). The learning phase included 11 pictures of adult faces with neutral facial expressions or cars (319 × 432 pixels). During the learning phase, the images were randomly presented for 3 s with an interstimulus interval of 2 s. During the recognition test, the images used during the learning phase (referred to as “old”) were randomly paired with 11 new images in each of the recognition lists. This task was self-paced, and the next stimulus was presented immediately after the participant answered. The participants were asked to press the appropriate mouse button to select an image that they had seen before (the old image). The left–right order of the old and new images was randomized. Each subject performed a face recognition test and a car recognition test, but the order of the tests was randomized. Accuracy was computed as the number of correct responses (hits). This study was registered in the ClinicalTrials.gov database (Registration No. NCT06306378).

Machine learning model

Data from 68 children with ASD (including both high-functioning and low-functioning individuals) and 68 age- and sex-matched TD children were included in the final analysis. Spindle wave parameters, including mean density, duration, amplitude, frequency, and chirp, were extracted from all EEG channels across both hemispheres to form the original dataset. A stratified sampling strategy was employed to partition the data into a training set (80%) and an independent test set (20%), preserving the class distribution. To prevent data leakage, z-score normalization was performed using the mean and standard deviation values computed from the training dataset only. These parameters were then applied to normalize the test dataset. Three classification models were developed: (1) logistic regression with L2 regularization to prevent overfitting, (2) a linear support vector machine (SVM) to optimize decision boundaries, and (3) a distance-weighted K-nearest neighbors (KNN) model using inverse squared distances. Hyperparameter tuning was conducted on the training set using 5-fold cross-validation. The optimal parameters identified were λ = 0.0001 for LR, C = 0.5995 for SVM, and k = 3 for KNN. Each model was then retrained on the full training set with its respective optimal parameters. Model performance was evaluated on an independent test set using confusion matrices, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and area under the curve (AUC) metrics. To assess both predictive performance and statistical robustness, bootstrap resampling with replacement (1000 iterations) was performed. Specifically, key performance metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score, together with their 95% confidence intervals, were estimated from the bootstrap distributions using point estimates and percentile-based intervals. Model stability was further quantified by calculating the coefficient of variation for each metric across the 1000 bootstrap iterations. Decision boundaries were visualized using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) for dimensionality reduction. Feature importance was evaluated through permutation feature importance and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis.

Animals

PV-Cre mice were a kind gift from Hailong Dong Lab (Fourth Military Medical University), and Nlgn2-/- mice were purchased from Cyagen (Soochow, China). All the mice used for this research project were adult (8–12-week-old) C57BL/6 J mice, unless otherwise indicated, and were housed in the animal facility at a maximum of five mice per cage on a 12-h light‒dark cycle (light from 08:00 to 20:00) at a constant temperature (22 ± 1 °C) and humidity (40–60%), with food and water available ad libitum. All main experiments were performed using male mice (8–12 weeks old) to avoid potential variability associated with the estrous cycle. Both subject and stimulus (familiar and novel) mice were male and age-matched. To examine potential sex-dependent effects, an additional cohort of female mice was tested in the social interaction and social memory paradigms, in which both subject and stimulus (familiar and novel) mice were female (Supplementary Fig. 1). The use and care of the animals were approved and directed by the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University (Approval ID: XJTULAC2022-1445).

Stereotaxic surgery

The mice were anesthetized with continuous isoflurane (3% for induction, 0.8–1.5% for maintenance) for stereotaxic injections61,62. Their heads were fixed on a stereotaxic rig (RWD Life Science, China) for subsequent surgical procedures. The mice were placed on a heating pad when anesthesia was induced and remained there until they were fully awake after the procedure. Ophthalmic ointment was applied to the eyes before and throughout surgery. The stereotaxic coordinates for the injections/implantations were as follows: for virus injection in the sTRN, anteroposterior (AP): −1.30 mm; mediolateral (ML): ±2.00 mm; dorsoventral (DV): −3.25 mm from the dura; for fiber implantation in the fiber photometry and optogenetic experiments with the sTRN, AP: −1.30 mm; ML: ±3.00 mm; and DV: 2.25 mm (angled 20 degrees from the lateral to the medial direction); in the VPM, AP: −1.92 mm, ML: ±1.25 mm, DV: −3.25 mm from the dura; and for fiber implantation in the optogenetic experiments with the sTRN→VPM circuit, AP: −2.25 mm, ML: ±3.00 mm, DV: −2.25 mm (angled 25 degrees from the lateral to the medial direction) from the dura. For all the experiments, a total of 200 nL of virus was injected at each site. After the injection, the needles remained in place for 10 min before being withdrawn slowly. For the fiber photometry and optogenetic protocols, a black optical fiber (outer diameter (OD) of 200 μm and numerical aperture (NA) of 0.37; purchased from Inper Co., Ltd., China) was placed 50 μm (for fiber photometry) or 200 μm (for optogenetics) above the injection site. All the mice used in the fiber photometry experiments, behavioral assays, ex vivo electrophysiological experiments, and histological analysis were allowed to recover from surgery for 3–4 weeks.

Virus injection and optical fiber implantation

To monitor the calcium activity of sTRNPvalb neurons, AAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-GCaMP6s was unilaterally injected into the sTRN of PV-Cre mice, and the optical fiber was implanted in the sTRN in the same cerebral hemisphere as that injected with the virus. For the chemogenetic inhibition of sTRNPvalb neurons, AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry was bilaterally injected into the sTRN of PV-Cre mice. For the optogenetic activation of sTRNPvalb neurons, AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hChR2-GFP and AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-GFP were bilaterally injected into the sTRN of PV-Cre mice, and optical fibers were bilaterally implanted above the sTRN. For anterograde tracing of sTRNPvalb neuronal projections, AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hChR2-GFP was injected bilaterally into the sTRN of PV-Cre mice. For retrograde tracing in the PF, VPM, and PO brain regions, AAV2/retro-hSyn-mCherry was injected unilaterally into the PF, VPM, and PO, respectively. For optogenetic activation of the sTRNPvalb–VPM circuit in PV-Cre mice, AAV2/9-EF1α-CON FON-hChR2-eYFP was bilaterally injected into the sTRN, AAV2/retro-hSyn-Flp was bilaterally injected into the VPM, and optical fibers were bilaterally implanted above the VPM. For optogenetic activation of the sTRNPvalb-VPM circuit in Nlgn2-/- mice, AAV2/9-PV-hChR2-eYFP was bilaterally injected into the sTRN, and optical fibers were bilaterally implanted above the VPM. For ex vivo electrophysiological experiments analyzing the sTRN–VPM circuit, AAV2/9-PV-hChR2-eYFP was injected into the sTRN of C57BL/6 J mice and Nlgn2-/- mice. All viral infusions were performed at a rate of 50 nL min–1. After each infusion, the needle or glass pipette remained in place for 10 min before being withdrawn slowly. AAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-GCaMP6s (PT-0071), AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry (PT-0020), AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-hChR2-GFP (PT-0985), AAV2/9-hSyn-DIO-GFP (PT-1103), AAV2/retro-hSyn-mCherry (PT-4538), AAV2/9-EF1α-CON FON-hChR2-eYFP (PT-0065), AAV2/retro-hSyn-Flp (PT-0341) and AAV2/9-PV-hChR2-eYFP (PT-2525) were purchased from the Brain VTA. AAV titers ranged from 2 × 1012 to 1 × 1013 vg mL−1. All the viruses were stored at −80 °C and transported on ice on the injection day.

EEG/EMG recordings

EEG/EMG electrode implantation

Two weeks after the virus injection, the mice were implanted with cortical EEG and EMG electrodes. Miniature stainless steel screws for EEG recordings (Bio-Signal Technologies, China) were carefully inserted into the skull above the frontal cortex (AP + 1.5 mm; ML ± 1 mm) and temporal cortex (AP −2.5 mm; ML ± 2.5 mm). For EMG recordings, two metal wire electrodes (Bio-Signal Technologies, China) were inserted into the neck muscles. The EEG and EMG apparatuses were then secured to the skull using dental cement, allowing for recordings in freely moving mice.

EEG/EMG recordings and data analysis

The mice were allowed to recover for one week before EEG/EMG recordings. EEG/EMG signals were recorded using a Medusa-EEG/EMG system (Bio-Signal Technologies, China), digitized at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz, and bandpass filtered (EEG: 0.5–49 Hz, EMG: 20–500 Hz). For recordings in home cages, the mice were housed individually for 3 days and connected to a flexible recording cable at least 24 h before recording. In-home cage EEG/EMG recordings, along with associated behavioral recordings; pharmacological, chemogenetic, and optogenetic manipulations; and fiber optic recordings were performed from Zeitgeber Time (ZT) 2 to ZT10. EEG/EMG signals were processed following established protocols61,62. The initial signal was segmented into 4 s epochs classified as NREM sleep, REM sleep, or wake periods using a MATLAB script11. Consistent with previous studies, two experienced researchers who were blinded to the experimental groups reviewed the results with Sirenia Sleep Pro software (Pinnacle Technology, USA). The power spectral density of the EEG data was computed with the MATLAB pwelch function, and the relative powers of the delta (0.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), and beta (13–30 Hz) bands were normalized according to the total power. Sleep spindles were detected with an automated MATLAB-based algorithm63–65. Briefly, EEG data were bandpass filtered between 10 and 15 Hz with a Butterworth filter, and the envelopes were determined using the Hilbert transform. A spindle event was identified when the envelope exceeded a threshold set at 3.5 times the standard deviation of the envelopes across all NREM sleep epochs. The onset and offset of potential spindle events were marked when the envelope crossed a secondary lower threshold set at 2 times the standard deviation. Potential spindle events with durations shorter than 0.5 s or longer than 3 s were subsequently excluded.

Fiber photometry

Fiber photometry was used to record fluorescence signals in vivo. By combining the fiber photometry system with a calcium sensor, the concentration of Ca2+ can be measured indirectly. Fiber photometry recordings were implemented using a Thinker Tech system (Nanjing Biotech Co., Ltd., China) based on established protocols32. Briefly, a 470 nm laser beam was reflected off a dichroic mirror (MD498, Thorlabs), focused by a 310-objective lens (0.3 NA; Olympus) and linked to an optical commutator (Doris Lenses). An optical fiber connected to the commutator was used to transmit optical signals. The fluorescence signal was bandpass filtered (MF525-39, Thorlabs) and converted to a voltage signal via an amplifier. The analog voltage signals were digitized at 100 Hz and recorded with a Power 1401 digitizer with Spike2 software (CED). The signals were collected at a sampling frequency of 40 Hz. For all fiber photometry experiments, PV-Cre mice aged 2–3 months were used. Before the testing day, the mice were habituated to the patch cord for 30 min daily for 3 days. On the testing day, the subject mouse was placed in their home cage and allowed to freely move for 2 h. The behavioral video and fiber photometry data were recorded synchronously once the subject mouse was placed in the box. All the fiber photometry data were analyzed in a blinded manner. Neuronal Ca2+ and EEG–EMG signals, along with behavioral video recordings, were simultaneously captured from ZT2 to ZT8. Signal processing was performed in MATLAB (MathWorks), including motion artifact correction and fluorescence debleaching. Fluorescence signals were debleached by fitting a mono- or bi-exponential decay function1, and the resulting traces were normalized by z-scoring. For the correlation analysis between spindle amplitude and calcium activity, the EEG and fiber photometry recordings were first temporally aligned. Sleep spindles were automatically detected from the EEG signal, and the amplitude of each spindle was quantified as its peak-to-peak value. For each spindle event, the onset time was defined as time 0 for analysis of the neuronal calcium signal. The calcium Δz-score for a given spindle was calculated as the difference between the maximum z-scored calcium signal within a 3 s period after spindle onset and the mean calcium baseline signal during the 3 s pre-spindle window9. This procedure yielded paired values for spindle amplitude and calcium Δz-score for each detected spindle. Pearson correlation analysis was then performed across all paired spindle–calcium measurements to assess the relationship between spindle amplitude and neuronal calcium activity.

Intraperitoneal injection

For the experiments using pharmacological inhibitors, sodium oxybate (SO, 0.15 g kg−1; Hanfeng Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., China) or saline was administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) 30 min before the behavioral assessments. For pharmacological inhibition, the mice were randomly divided into two groups: the Sal group was injected with saline, and the SO group was injected with SO. For the pharmacological activation experiments, zolpidem (ZOL, 0.5 mg kg−1; Sanofi Winthrop Industrie, France) or saline was administered i.p. 30 min before the behavioral assessments. For pharmacological activation, the mice were randomly divided into two groups: the Sal group was injected with saline, and the ZOL group was injected with ZOL. For chemogenetic experiments, clozapine-N-oxide (CNO, 3 mg kg−1; BrainVTA Technology Co. Ltd., China) or saline was administered i.p. after S2 of TCT. For chemogenetic modulation, the mice were randomly divided into two groups: the Sal group was injected with saline, and the CNO group was injected with CNO.

Optogenetic stimulation assays

The optical fibers implanted in the mice were connected to a patch cord with zirconia sleeves (Inper Co., Ltd., China), which in turn were attached to an optical commutator (Doric Co., Ltd., Canada) via an FC/PC adapter, allowing unrestricted movement of the mice. A second patch cable equipped with FC/PC connectors at both ends (Inper Co., Ltd., China) was linked to the commutator and then connected to a 473 nm laser (Thinker Tech Nanjing Biotech Co., Ltd., China). The power output of the laser was verified using a digital power meter (Sanwa Electric Instrument Co., Ltd., Japan). To simulate the rhythmic spike bursts observed during sleep spindles, we optically stimulated sTRNPvalb neurons with brief light pulses with a duration of 41.5 ms and a frequency of 12 Hz (corresponding to the intrinsic frequency of sleep spindles) for 1 s. This stimulation protocol was repeated at intervals of 7.5 s, approximating the average interval between sleep spindles.

Animal behavioral tests

Handling and habituation

The mice were transported to the behavioral rooms at least 30 min before being handled or habituated. Three weeks after the surgery, the mice were handled daily for 2 min on 3 consecutive days in the behavioral rooms. The handling procedures were conducted under the same light‒dark conditions as those used for the behavioral tests. All behavioral tests, without any special illustration, were conducted in the light phase. We minimized the stress associated with the pharmacological and chemogenetic experiments by allowing the mice to habituate to the i.p. injection operation without solution administration after they had been handled on day 3; for optogenetic experiments, the fibers on the mouse heads were connected to the optogenetic system (Thinker Tech Nanjing Biotech Co., Ltd.) without laser stimulation for 1 min at the end of the handling period on days 2 and 3. Behavioral experiments were conducted at least 24 h after the last handling session. On the testing day, the behavioral testing room containing several compartments was kept empty (with no mice or experimenters) for at least 1 h before the subject mice arrived to avoid any confounding cues. The mice were subsequently transported to the behavioral testing rooms at least 1 h before the tests. During all the behavioral tests, the behavioral apparatus was placed in an empty, soundproof behavioral compartment, the mice performing the behavioral assessment were placed in another empty, soundproof behavioral compartment (at least 2 m away from the compartment for the behavioral assessment), and the experimenter (at least 2 m away from the compartment for the behavioral assessment) was in the third compartment to monitor the activity of the mice on a computer. The behavioral apparatus was cleaned with 70% ethanol before and between runs to prevent odor cues. The SMART v.3.0/I.O. video tracking system (Panlab Harvard Apparatus, Spain) was used to automatically track the location of the subject mouse. Heatmaps were constructed based on the movement duration of the mouse. The heatmap scale bar in one panel applies to all heatmaps showing the results of different groups in the panel. The researchers who analyzed the results of the behavioral experiments were blinded to the experimental conditions.

Three-chamber test (TCT)

The three-chamber test is a well-established and highly reproducible paradigm for assessing social memory in rodents1,3,29. It leverages the innate tendency of mice to preferentially investigate unfamiliar conspecifics over familiar conspecifics, thus providing a robust measure of social memory. During the social novelty phase (trial 3), the subject mouse was simultaneously presented with a previously encountered (familiar) mouse and a novel conspecific. A consistent preference for the novel mouse indicates intact social memory, whereas the absence of such a preference reflects impaired recognition and memory performance. The three-chamber apparatus (60 cm length × 40 cm width × 25 cm height) had an opaque white wall that was separated into three isometric compartment chambers by two clear extruded acrylic walls (40 cm length × 0.5 cm width × 25 cm height) with 6 cm-diameter holes, which allowed free access to each chamber when the doors were removed. Two identical wire mesh cups with removable lids were placed at opposite corners of the apparatus. Stimulus C57BL/6 J mice weighing 2–3 g less than the subject mice were habituated to the mesh cup daily for 10 min on 3 consecutive days before the testing day. Behavioral testing was divided into three stages. For habituation, the subject mouse was gently placed in the middle chamber and allowed 10 min of free exploration in the three-chamber apparatus (S1). The subject mouse was then attracted by the hands of the researcher to the middle chamber instead of being chased by the researcher’s hand to minimize any stress and bias in this step. Then, the two doors were closed by partitions. Importantly, before this step, both the hands and partitions were cleaned with 70% ethanol to prevent confounding olfactory cues. Then, a stimulus mouse that had not previously been encountered was randomly placed in either of the two mesh cups, with the location counterbalanced among the subject mice. The doors were then removed, and the subject mouse was free to explore and interact with either the empty cup (EC) or the mouse cup (MC) for 10 min to encode information about the first stimulus mouse (S2). In the 30 min/2 h interval TCTs, once S2 was finished, the subject mouse was attracted to the middle chamber again and then transported to the cage for EEG/EMG recordings as soon as possible. Next, another novel mouse was placed into the other mesh cup. The locations of the two mesh cups with the first stimulus mouse (now the familiar mouse (FM)) and the novel mouse (NM) were randomly switched. After 30 min (for the 30 min interval TCT) or 2 h (for the 2 h interval TCT), the subject mouse was returned to the middle chamber and allowed to interact with FM/NM for 10 min to recall the preceding encoded memory (S3). The first stage involved habituation. In the second stage, the mice were exposed to both the previous stimulus mouse (FM) used for labeling and the NM after a 10 min habituation in the apparatus. Active sniffing was defined as the nose of the subject mouse being within 2 cm of the cup. The sniffing duration in each trial was recorded and analyzed based on the active sniffing behavior using the SMART v.3.0/I.O. video tracking system (Panlab Harvard Apparatus, Spain). The time during which the animals climbed on the wall of the cup or groomed was excluded from the analysis. Five-trial social memory test

The subject mice were housed individually for 3 days prior to the behavioral analysis. On the day of the test, a new C57BL/6 J mouse (Mouse A) weighing 2–3 grams less than the subject mouse was placed in the cage. In trials 1 to 4, the subject mouse was allowed to interact with Mouse A for 1 min, with each interaction separated by 10 min. In the fifth final trial, Mouse A was replaced with a new stimulus mouse (Mouse B). The duration of sniffing was manually recorded for each trial by recording the duration of sniffing stimulation of the face, body, and anogenital region of the conspecific mouse. Mice were excluded once an attack occurred. The time spent chasing, standing still, or grooming around the stimulated mice was not scored.

Novel object recognition test (NORT)

The apparatus (50 cm length × 50 cm width × 40 cm height) was composed of four opaque white walls. The test was divided into three 10 min stages. For the habituation stage, the subject mouse was gently placed in the central region of the apparatus and allowed 10 min of free exploration (S1). In S2, two identical objects were placed opposite each other on the midline of the apparatus 10 cm from the wall (S2), and the mice were introduced to the apparatus to explore the objects. One of the objects (familiar object, FO) was randomly replaced with another different object (novel object, NO) in S3. In S3, the mice were introduced to the apparatus to explore the FO and NO. The sniffing duration, defined as the time when the nose of the subject mouse was within 2 cm of the object, was automatically recorded using the SMART v.3.0/I.O. video tracking system (Panlab Harvard Apparatus, Spain). Immunofluorescence staining and image acquisition

The mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) and perfused with saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate buffer (PFA). The brains were removed and postfixed with 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C. The brains were subsequently washed with PBS and incubated with PBS containing 30% sucrose until they sank. The tissues were then embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound (Sakura, Japan) and stored at −80 °C until sectioning. The brains were sectioned into 30 µm-thick slices and collected in PBS. Free-floating sections were washed three times for 10 min each with PBS on a shaker. The sections were treated with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min and then blocked with PBS containing 5% goat serum for 2 h at room temperature. After blocking, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution. The primary antibodies used were mouse anti-parvalbumin (1:500; Merck Millipore, MAB1572), rabbit anti-parvalbumin (1:200; Abcam, ab11427), and rabbit anti-Nlgn2 (1:200; Synaptic Systems, #129511). The sections were then washed three times for 10 min each with PBS on a shaker before being incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted 1:500 for 2 h at room temperature. Afterward, the sections were washed three times with PBS, stained with DAPI (2 µg/mL), and washed again with PBS. Finally, the sections on microscopy slides were carefully mounted under glass coverslips with mounting media. Confocal images were captured using a confocal microscope (A1-1024, Nikon, Japan).

Ex vivo electrophysiology

Acute slice preparation

C57BL/6 J and Nlgn2-/- mice were injected with AAV at 4–6 weeks of age. The mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with an ice-cold dissection solution containing (in mM) 110 choline chloride, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 0.5 CaCl2, 10 MgSO4, 25 NaHCO3, 11 D-(+)-glucose, 20 HEPES, 5 sodium ascorbate, and 3 sodium pyruvate saturated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 (pH 7.4, 308–312 mOsm). Coronal slices (300 μm) containing the target region were cut in cold choline dissection solution on a vibrating microtome (VT1200 S, Leica). For the recording of sTRN neurons, brain slices between 1.1 mm and 1.7 mm posterior to the bregma were collected. For recording VPM neurons, brain slices between 1.5 mm and 2.1 mm posterior to the bregma were collected. The collected slices were rapidly removed to the choline solution and incubated for 10 min at 30 °C for recovery and then for an additional 30 min, after which recordings were conducted in artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) containing (in mM) 126 NaCl, 26 NaHCO3, 10D(+)-glucose, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 1.2 MgCl2, and 2 CaCl2 saturated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 (pH 7.4, 305–315 mOsm).

Electrophysiological recordings

The slices were transferred to a recording chamber and continuously perfused with carbonated recording aCSF (3–4 ml min−1). Neurons were visualized with infrared light, and eYFP fluorescence was confirmed with 470 nm light using an upright microscope (BX51WI, Olympus). Recording glass pipettes with 4–7 MΩ resistance were used for recordings. For the recording of IPSCs, pipettes were filled with an intracellular solution containing (in mM) 122 CsMeSO3, 3.7 NaCl, 20 HEPES, 10 BAPTA, 0.2 EGTA, 0.3 MgATP, 0.3 Na3GTP, 5 TEA-Cl, and 5 Qx314-Br. The osmolarity of all the intracellular solutions ranged from 290295 mOsm, and the pH was 7.3. The sIPSCs were recorded at 10 mV for 300 s. NBQX (10 μM) and APV (50 μM) were added to the extracellular aCSF to record the sIPSCs, and picrotoxin (PTX, 100 μM) was added to the extracellular aCSF to validate the sIPSCs. The oIPSC recordings were performed in VPM areas where high ChR2 terminal infectivity exists, and were repeated 20 times for each cell. Paired-pulse stimulation was administered at 100 ms intervals, and the paired-pulse ratio (PPR) was recorded as the ratio of the amplitude of the second oIPSC to the amplitude of the first oIPSC, calculated relative to the baseline preceding each spike. A 473 nm blue laser (3–4 mW from the optical fiber tip) was used to apply a 41.5-ms light pulse at 12 Hz to validate the efficiency of the virus used for the optogenetic experiments. Optical stimulation with a 5 s epoch of the light off, a 5 s epoch of the light on, and a 5 s epoch of the light off was alternately applied during spontaneous recordings in slices prepared from ChR2-expressing mice.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All the experiments and data analyses were performed by a researcher who was blinded to the experimental groups. For the clinical trial, we averaged the data from all the EEG channels for analysis. An LR model was used to estimate the group effects (ASD vs. TD), controlling for individual age and sex. Effect sizes of group differences were reported as differences between the group means divided by the standard deviation of the TD group. We controlled for age, sex, and sleep architecture in both groups to eliminate potential confounding effects on face or car recognition scores. Statistical significance was set at 0.05 for all tests. For animal experiments, animals showing evidence of an improper virus injection, incorrect optical fiber placement, or incorrect cannula placement were excluded from the analysis. For all representative images or micrographs, the experiments were repeated independently at least three times yielding similar results. The sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications1,4,5,9. The number of animals (n) in the behavioral tests, the number of cells (n) in the whole-cell recordings, and the number of sections (n) used to quantify the immunofluorescence staining are indicated in each figure legend. All the datasets were first checked for normality, and statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad software) and SPSS 24.0 software. Only two-tailed tests were conducted. One-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple comparison post hoc test, two-way ANOVA with repeated measures followed by Sidak’s multiple comparison post hoc test, unpaired t tests, paired t tests, Friedman’s tests with Dunn’s post hoc tests, and Mann‒Whitney tests were used to test for statistical significance when appropriate. The statistical information can be found in the figure legends. All the data are presented as the mean ± s.e.m. The statistical significance threshold was set at α = 0.05 (ns, P > 0.05; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001).
