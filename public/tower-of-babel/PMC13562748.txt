Advanced energy-based and two-degree-of-freedom analytical modeling of reinforced concrete slabs subjected to low-velocity impact and equivalent blast loading
Authors: Rayeh Nasr Al-Dala’ien, Mohammed Jalal Al-Ezzi, Agusril Syamsir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
When reinforced concrete (RC) slabs are subject to low velocity impacts (LVI), they will experience highly non-linear behaviors based upon the combined effects of flexural deformations; tensile membrane actions; punching shears; and local crushing as well as the evolving nature of damage in these structures. Most analytical models have been developed under restrictive simplifications; lack complete partitioning of energies; and depend heavily upon computationally intensive finite element simulations. An analytical–energy-based approach is presented to predict the non-linear dynamic behavior of RC slabs subjected to both LVIs and equivalent blast loads. The proposed model integrates an improved two degree-of-freedom (2-DoF) dynamic interaction system with an explicit multi-mechanism energy partitioning procedure which can be used to quantify all of the major forms of energy consumption including flexural; membrane; shear; crushing; damping and damage. The analytical framework has incorporated strain rate enhancement through use of dynamic increase factors (DIFs); punching shear capacity through application of the critical shear crack theory (CSCT); and geometrically non-linear membrane behavior via Xie’s formulation for large deflection membrane behavior. A wide-ranging parametric analysis was performed to examine the effect of impact energy; slab thickness; reinforcement ratio; boundary conditions and advanced materials systems such as normal strength concrete (NSC); ultra high-performance concrete (UHPC); and ultra high-performance fibre reinforced concrete (UHPFRC). Results indicated that an increase in the magnitude of impact energy from 1 to 6 kJ resulted in an approximate 301% increase in slab displacement. Furthermore, an increase in the slab thickness from 60 to 125 mm resulted in a reduction of approximately 68% in slab displacement. It was also found that when compared with RC slabs made with NSC, those made with UHPFRC had displacements decreased by approximately 61% and reduced damage dissipation energy by approximately 69% due to higher levels of crack bridging and membrane resistance. Lastly, it was determined that twisted and hooked end fibres had the greatest energy absorbing capability and provided the longest delay to punching shear failure. Validation studies were conducted against existing experimental data and comparative non-linear ABAQUS CDP simulations indicating prediction error margins generally less than ± 5–10% and good correlation in terms of slab displacement response; crack development; and failure progression. The validation studies also showed that the method could be easily adapted for equivalent blast load analyses using impulse equivalency principles thus enabling a rapid predictive tool for designing RC slab systems resistant to either low velocity impact or blast loads.

Introduction

Reinforced concrete (RC) slabs are widely employed in building construction, bridge construction, industrial and commercial facility construction, marine and coastal protection projects, transportation infrastructure development and other protective systems. They can handle heavy loads well, are durable and structurally efficient. In practical application, reinforced concrete slabs are typically exposed to a variety of loading conditions; namely, static and service loads and extreme dynamic loads. These extreme dynamic loads include vehicle accidents, collapsing objects, falling rocks, accidental explosions, blast loading etc. The extreme dynamic loads will induce the reinforced concrete slabs to undergo highly non-linear deformation. The deformation includes rapid redistribution of stresses, formation of cracks at the local level, punch-shear failures, reduction of the stiffness of the slab system and the occurrence of progressive collapse mechanism1,2.

The different types of dynamic loads that can affect RC slab systems include vehicle collision, explosion blast, dropping object etc. Among them, low velocity impact (LVI) loading is one of the most dangerous forms of loading affecting the RC slab systems. LVI loading involves a combination of low impact speed and large impacting masses. It produces extensive global deformation and local damage. At contrast to the high-speed impacts which create only local penetrations or perforations or scabbing, LVI loading creates complicated interactions among the flexural deformations, tensile membrane actions, punching shear resistances, local crushing, progressive crack propagations3,4. Thus, accurate prediction of the nonlinear impact behaviors of RC slabs is one of the biggest challenges in developing the impact-resistant and protective structural engineering.

During an impact event, the kinetic energy supplied by the impactor is converted to various forms of energy. The conversion of kinetic energy includes flexural deformation energy, membrane deformation energy, shear dissipation energy, local crushing energy, crack propagation energy, damping energy and DDE (irreversible damage dissipation energy). Depending on the magnitude of the impact energy, geometrical dimensions of the slab system, boundary conditions of the supports, configuration of reinforcements and rate dependent effects of materials etc., the portion of kinetic energy absorbed by each form of energy differs5. Therefore, in order to predict accurately the impact responses of RC slabs, a physically rationalized framework should be able to simulate both global structural deformation and local damage progression.

Numerous experiments have been performed to investigate the impact responses of RC slabs. Drop weight tests have been used extensively to test the impact responses of RC slabs. Studies have shown that increased levels of energy input causes significant increase in slab deformation, crack growth and punching shear susceptibilities2,6,7. Studies have further demonstrated that deformation behavior evolves progressively from elastic deformation towards nonlinear cracking and finally towards local punching-related failure upon exposure to increasingly intense dynamic loading. Additionally, experimental data indicated that energy absorption mechanisms play a vital role in determining whether a slab can survive catastrophic failure under impact conditions. Generally speaking, slabs that develop stable deformation mechanisms to dissipate the impact energy effectively demonstrate better resistance against catastrophic failure than those that cannot2,6.

Nonlinear finite element analysis (FEA) is another approach that has been widely adopted in recent years to simulate the impact responses in RC structures. Using software packages such as ABAQUS and LS-DYNA enables modeling of nonlinear material behavior, contact interactions, strain-rate dependence, crack propagation and progressive damage evolutions8,9. Specifically, CDP model in ABAQUS has demonstrated good ability to reproduce experimentally observed crack patterns, displacement time history curves and failure modes under impact loading conditions. However, although FEA models offer high accuracy in simulating the nonlinear behavior of RC structures subject to impact loadings, they suffer from several disadvantages. For example, FE analyses require enormous amounts of computing resources and fine meshes for discrete element representation. Additionally, calibration of constitutive parameters for nonlinear material behavior is required. Furthermore, sophisticated contact algorithms need to be employed to capture accurately the complex contact interactions occurring between the impactor and the impacted RC slab. All these requirements make FEA models difficult to apply for fast design iterations and/or large number of parameter studies.

Simplified analytical solutions have been offered as alternatives to FE models for rapidly evaluating structural responses under dynamic loadings. Single degree-of-freedom (SDOF) models have been widely used to estimate structural displacements under impact/blast loadings. However, SDOF models consider RC slab systems as a lumped mass-spring model with no consideration given to localized deformation mechanisms (e.g. cracking), crack propagation behavior and explicit redistribution of energies associated with dynamic interaction. Due to this simplification, predictions made based on SDOF models tend to lose accuracy when analyzing strongly nonlinear impact load cases where punching shear failure occurs along with progressive damage evolution. Two degree-of-freedom (2-DoF) analytical models were introduced subsequent to SDOF models to describe both impactors and RC slab systems as dynamically interacting bodies. Inclusion of nonlinear contact interaction and energy transfer between impactors and structures provided much improved predictions compared with conventional SDOF models2,10. However, majority of current 2-DoF formulations focus mostly on predicting displacements/contact forces rather than detailing how impact energy is distributed across multiple structural dissipation mechanisms. Therefore, previously developed analytical models provide limited physical understanding with respect to damage accumulation/failure progression/punching shear development/membrane activation.

Development of advanced composite materials such as ultra-high-performance concrete (UHPC) and ultra-high-performance fiber-reinforced concrete (UHPFRC) has enabled fabrication of materials possessing significantly better mechanical properties than traditional normal-strength concrete (NSCs). The key advantages of these advanced materials include significantly elevated compressive strengths/tensile strengths/fracture toughnesses/crack controls/energies absorbed during damage processes. Furthermore, introduction of steel fibers in UHPFRC systems allows for effective bridging of cracks leading to delay in crack propagation and improvements in post-crack ductility11,12. Experimental investigations have shown that hooked end and twisted fibers possess significantly larger pullout resistances/efficiencies for absorbing energies due to improved mechanical anchoring compared to straight fibers. Most existing analytical impact models ignore effects of fiber shapes on energy distribution/redistribution and structural response behaviors.

Another very important factor that affects the impact responses is Strain Rate Sensitivity (SRS). Both concrete and reinforcement materials show enhancements in their strength due to SRS effects when loaded rapidly. These SRS effects are typically described through Dynamic Increase Factors (DIFs); i.e., ratio of dynamic material property values to their corresponding static values13,14. Although SRS effects are usually accounted for in advanced numerical simulation codes, they are usually ignored or greatly simplified in analytical formulation for impact responses; hence leading to reduced accuracy in predictions, especially under severe dynamic loading conditions.

In addition to impact loadings experienced by protective structures comprising RC slabs due to natural hazards like earthquake ground motions or man-made hazards like terrorist attacks, these structures may also be exposed to blast loadings caused by high intensity pressure waves acting over very short periods. Impact loadings and blast loadings differ significantly in their loading characteristics while being similar via equivalence in terms of energy and impulses15; hence creating a strong motivation for unified analytical frameworks applicable to both types of loadings within a single physically consistent framework.

Although numerous contributions have been made by previous researchers in solving many problems associated with RC slabs subjected to severe dynamic loadings; nevertheless, several very important drawbacks still exist with currently available analytical approaches. Some examples of these drawbacks include: lack of explicit distribution of energies between dissipation mechanisms; limited representations of progressive damage evolutions; inadequate treatments for strain rate sensitivities; incomplete representation of advanced material systems; inability to treat simultaneously both impact and blast loadings.

Therefore, a strong requirement exists for developing a physically rationalizable yet computationally efficient analytical framework capable of treating simultaneously nonlinear dynamic interactions between impacting objects/structures; distribution/transfer/repartitioning of energies among various dissipation mechanisms; strain rate sensitive behaviors; progressive failure evolutions; and advanced material behaviors.

Accordingly, a new unified analytical-energy based framework was developed in this dissertation project for predicting nonlinear dynamic response behaviors exhibited by reinforced concrete (RC) slabs subjected to low-velocity impact loadings and equivalent blast loadings. This new analytical framework consists of two degree-of-freedom (2-DoF) dynamic interaction formulations; explicit multi-mechanism energy partitions; Critical Shear Crack Theory (CSCT); nonlinear tensile membrane action formulations; strain-rate dependent material enhancement formulations; and advanced ultra-high-performance fiber-reinforced concrete (UHPFRC) material behavior formulations. The proposed analytical framework provides physically rationalizable methodologies for analyzing the structural performances of RC slabs subjected to severe dynamic loadings; unlike previously developed analytical frameworks that focused primarily on either displacement/contact force predictions or energy distributions among dissipation mechanisms without considering other aspects.

Literature review

The behavior of reinforced-concrete (RC) slabs subjected to low velocity impact (LVI) loading has received considerable attention over the last few decades because of the increasing need for impact resistant and protective structural systems. Experiments, analyses, and computations show that RC slabs subjected to impact loading demonstrate highly nonlinear behaviors due to the coupling interactions of flexure, punching shear, membrane action, local crushing, crack propagation and stiffness reduction. While numerous studies have greatly contributed to our understanding of the dynamic behavior of RC slabs subjected to impact loads, there remains a major challenge in accurately and efficiently computing the dynamic behavior of RC slabs subjected to LVI loading.

Initial studies mostly involved experimental drop weight testing to investigate the dynamic behavior of RC slabs subjected to impact loading. Studies demonstrated that the behavior of RC slabs under impact loading is heavily influenced by many factors including; impact energy, slab geometry, reinforcement pattern, type of supports, and material properties. Prior to increased levels of impact induced deformations, RC slabs generally behave elastically or undergo flexural deformation with very little cracking. However, as the level of impact increases, RC slabs will begin to experience non-linear deformation mechanisms such as crack growth, yield of reinforcing steel, membrane action and punching shear failure3,4. Further experimental studies have documented the progression of failure modes from distributed cracking toward localized punching related failures as deformation increases.

Among the existing studies, those performed by Jia et al.2 provide valuable information about the sequential behavior of RC slabs subjected to repeated drop weights impacts. Jia’s studies document that sequential deformation occurs through elastic deformation, initial cracking, continued cracking, stiffness reduction and ultimately punching shear failure. Moreover, Jia et al.’s work demonstrates that the ability of RC slabs to withstand repeated impact loads is directly related to their ability to absorb and distribute impact energy through deformation. This led to the development of future analytical models using energy absorption concepts.

Further experimental studies examined the effect of geometric dimensions of slabs and severity of loading on failure modes. For example, Fujikake et al.6 demonstrated that repeated impact loading causes cumulative damage leading to progressive degradation in stiffness and increasing damage dissipation energy. Similar results were obtained by Xiao et al.7; however, their study indicated that slab thickness plays a significant role in determining the primary failure mode. Thin slabs were found to be highly susceptible to punching shear failure as a direct result of inadequate flexural capacity while thick slabs had better stress distribution and more energy absorbing capabilities. These experiments confirm that both global structural deformation and localized damage mechanisms must be evaluated together in order to predict the behavior of structures subjected to impact.

While experimental studies have produced significant amounts of data regarding the impact behavior of RC structures, finite element modeling (FEM) has proven to be one of the most popular techniques used to simulate impact behavior in RC structures. High fidelity FEM can include detailed representations of various nonlinear aspects of RC material behavior including contact interactions, crack propagation, strain rates and evolving damage8,9. Specifically, the Concrete Damage Plasticity (CDP) model built into ABAQUS has been able to reproduce crack patterns, displacement–time histories and punching failure mechanisms under dynamic loading conditions similar to those seen in laboratory tests. Additionally, FEM allows investigators to analyze various aspects of dynamic behavior including stress wave propagation, local crushing, yield of reinforcing steel and evolving damage that cannot easily be measured in laboratory tests.

Additionally, FE models are often very sensitive to mesh size, time step selection and material property calibration procedures. As a result, although high fidelity FE models are useful for validating other models and performing research investigations in detail; they are computationally expensive and generally unsuitable for rapid design iteration and extensive parameter investigations. Therefore, simple analytical models became attractive as computationally inexpensive alternatives for estimating structural response under impact loading. One of the earliest analytical models employed to estimate structural response under dynamic loadings was the classical single degree of freedom (SDOF) model. The SDOF model represents a structural system as a lumped mass-spring model whose response is defined by a second order differential equation. While this formulation provides rapid estimates of structural response with low computational costs; it neglects localized damage mechanisms, crack propagation and explicit redistribution of energy. Therefore, its predictive capability is severely limited when attempting to predict highly nonlinear impact loadings where punching shear and progressive damage occur.

In an effort to develop more robust analytical models; researchers developed two degree of freedom (2-DoF) models which allowed for both the impactor and structure to be modeled as dynamically interacting bodies. The introduction of nonlinear contact interaction and energy transfer significantly improved predictions relative to SDOF models2,10. Many successful 2-DoF models captured displacement history and contact force history observed experimentally; however most 2-DoF models were limited to predicting global displacement-force responses rather than detailing how impact energy was redistributed among multiple structural dissipative mechanisms. In order to alleviate some of the above noted limitations researchers began developing analytical models based on energy absorption concepts. Xu et al.5, developed an energy-based analytical model that included both flexural and membrane deformation mechanisms. Their study indicated that flexural mechanisms dominated early-stage deformation while membrane action played an increasingly important role at higher deflection values where tensile forces developed within reinforcing elements greatly enhanced post-cracking structural capacity. However most current energy-based formulations typically only capture limited subsets of dissipating energy mechanisms and fail to adequately capture contributions from shear resistance, local crushing and irreversible damage dissipation energy (DDE).

Advances in material technology have recently enabled significant enhancements in the structural impact resistance characteristics of RC structures through the use of ultra-high-performance concrete (UHPC) and ultra-high performance fiber reinforced concrete (UHPFRC). UHPC exhibits enhanced compressive strength, tensile strength, fracture toughness and crack resistance compared with standard normal strength concrete (NSC). The incorporation of steel fibers in UHPFRC enables effective crack bridging mechanisms that delay crack propagation, increase fracture energy and enhance post-cracking ductility11,12. Research indicates that fiber geometry affects structural performance under impact loading. Hooked end and twisted fibers provide significantly greater pull-out resistance and energy absorption compared with straight fibers due to superior mechanical anchoring and crack bridging properties.

Despite recent advancements in experimental characterization of UHPFRC under impact loading, most current analytical formulations do not account for the influence of fiber geometry on redistributing energy mechanisms, nor does it provide sufficient understanding on interaction between fiber reinforcement, membrane action, punching shear resistance and damage dissipation energy. Thus, there exists a significant need for analytical formulations that can integrate complex material behavior with physically meaningful energy redistribution mechanisms.

Another factor affecting structural performance under impact loading includes strain rate sensitivity. When loaded rapidly both concrete and reinforcing steel materials exhibit enhanced strength due to increased strain rate. Strain rate sensitivities are typically accounted for using Dynamic Increase Factors (DIF) relating dynamic material properties to static values13,14. While DIFs are frequently employed in high fidelity numerical simulations, they are less frequently accounted for or oversimplified in lower complexity analytical formulations; thus, reducing accuracy in extreme loading conditions.

Similar to impact loading, RC slabs forming part of protective structures may also be exposed to blast loads characterized by extremely short duration high intensity pressure waves. While blast loads differ significantly from impact loads in terms of loading characteristics; both types of loading can be correlated through equivalent energy and impulse concepts15. Hence there is great interest in developing unified analytical frameworks capable of addressing both impact and blast loading within a single physically consistent framework in the field of protective structural engineering. To date however, few studies have successfully developed analytical formulations which combine all five essential components: nonlinear dynamic interaction, explicit energy partitioning, advanced material properties, strain rate effects, unified impact-blast equivalencies.

As a result of previously identified limitations in prior studies; there is significant need for physically meaningful and computationally efficient analytical frameworks capable of explicitly accounting for: impactor-slab interaction, multiple mechanism energy partitioning, membrane action, punching shear development, local crushing, strain rate enhancement, and advanced UHPFRC material behavior; while remaining consistent with laboratory test data and nonlinear finite element simulations.

Thus, the purpose of this investigation is to propose a Unified analytical-energy-based framework which combines enhanced two degree of freedom (2-DoF) dynamics with explicit multiple mechanism energy partitioning to provide a physically consistent methodology for predicting nonlinear behavior of RC structures subjected to LVI loads equivalent blast loading conditions.

Proposed analytical and energy-based model

The proposed analytical-energy based model will be able to predict the non-linear dynamic response of reinforced concrete (RC) slabs subjected to low velocity impact (LVI) loading within one framework using a combination of structural dynamics, non-linear contact mechanics, fracture mechanics, membrane action theory, and explicit energy partitioning3,4,16–19 [55]. In contrast to traditional methods of analyzing the response of structures under impact loading, namely force–displacement approaches, this method views energy exchange and subsequent distribution as the primary physical mechanism affecting impact performance. Because of the complex nature of the impact response of reinforced concrete slabs due to cracking, stiffness reduction, localized crushing, rate-dependent behavior, membrane action and damage development4,9,16,18, it is especially necessary to treat energy exchange and redistribution as the principal governing physical mechanism influencing impact response. This model uses both a coupled two-degree-of-freedom (2DOF) impact system along with an explicit energy-based description of flexural deformation, shearing resistance, membrane tension, local crushing due to point contact forces and damage loss. As has been done before by Xiao et al.2, Jia et al.10, Fujikake et al.6 and Wu and Hao20 other researchers have also modeled the interaction of the impactor with the structural system with coupled formulations for modeling the response of reinforced concrete slabs subject to impacts; however they all utilized simplified single degree of freedom (SDOF) models in comparison to the current formulation, which includes non-linear redistribution of energy within the governing equations.

Energy balance formulation

The total impact energy that was applied to a structure will be distributed among the various structural elements and transformed into different forms of energy (kinetic, elastic, plastic, fracture and damage)3,5,16,18. An advantage of energy-based models lies in their ability to model the redistribution of impact energy into various structural mechanisms due to the fact that they model this redistribution using an energy framework.

The kinetic energy of the impactor is16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{cccc}& {E}_{k,i}=\frac{1}{2}{m}_{i}{\dot{u}}_{i}^{2}& & \end{array}$$\end{document}while the kinetic energy of the slab is17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{cccc}& {E}_{k,s}=\frac{1}{2}{m}_{s}{\dot{u}}_{s}^{2}& & \end{array}$$\end{document}

The recoverable elastic contact energy is obtained by integrating the Hertzian contact force19,21:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{cccc}& {E}_{c}={\int}_{0}^{\delta }{k}_{c}{\delta }^{3/2}d\delta & & \end{array}$$\end{document}thus giving19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{cccc}& {E}_{c}=\frac{2}{5}{k}_{c}{\delta }^{5/2}& & \end{array}$$\end{document}

The total energy balance of the impact system can therefore be expressed as20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{f}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{s}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{m}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{cr}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{d}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{\xi }$$\end{document} denote flexural energy, shear energy, membrane energy, crushing energy, damage dissipation energy, and viscous damping energy, respectively.

The use of an explicit energy distribution extends upon the previously developed energy-based models developed by Xu et al.5, Oucif et al.3 and Pham and Hao16. This redistribution of energy from impact was also observed experimentally by Xiao et al.2 and Fujikake et al.6 when studying the behavior of reinforced concrete slabs under impact conditions. At maximum displacement, all or nearly all of the original energy provided during the impact has been absorbed through some form of deformation and/or damage mechanism(s):21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{cccc}& {E}_{0}\approx {E}_{f}+{E}_{s}+{E}_{m}+{E}_{cr}+{E}_{d}+{E}_{\xi }& & \end{array}$$\end{document}

Equation (21) forms the fundamental basis of the proposed analytical-energy-based impact model. To prevent overlap among the individual energy terms, each component is associated with a unique physical deformation mechanism. Flexural energy represents global bending deformation, membrane energy corresponds to in-plane tensile stretching, shear energy describes punching-related crack development, crushing energy is restricted to localized contact-zone deformation, and damage energy represents irreversible material degradation. Therefore, the formulation minimizes double counting while preserving physical consistency throughout the impact event.

Flexural energy mechanism

Deformation through bending is a major global energy absorption mechanism for structures made with reinforced concrete subjected to dynamic loads2,5,16. The flexural energy absorbed by a beam/structure is defined as the external work done on the beam/structure through bending deformation:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{cccc}& {E}_{f}={\int}_{0}^{{u}_{s}}{F}_{f}(u)\hspace{0.17em}du& & \end{array}$$\end{document}

The elastic flexural energy becomes24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{cccc}& {E}_{f}=\frac{1}{2}{k}_{f}{u}_{s}^{2}& & \end{array}$$\end{document}while the post-yield energy is expressed as25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{cccc}& {E}_{f}=\frac{1}{2}{F}_{y}{u}_{y}+{F}_{y}({u}_{s}-{u}_{y})+\frac{1}{2}{k}_{p}({u}_{s}-{u}_{y}{)}^{2}& & \end{array}$$\end{document}

Membrane energy mechanism

At a larger deflection for a large plate, as the plate deflects it will eventually begin to be subjected to both the initial bending (flexure) and then tension in the plates through what is referred to as "membrane action." Membrane action occurs because, as the plate deflects vertically, it also causes the rebar within it to become stretched horizontally. Bailey32 illustrated how much tensile membrane action can increase the total load-bearing capacity of lightly reinforced concrete slabs when they are undergoing large deflections; and Wang et al.33, Xu et al.5 have demonstrated that membrane action is one of the most significant methods of absorbing energy in Reinforced Concrete (RC) slabs impacted.

Local crushing and contact energy

The forces acting upon the material at the point of initial contact with the impactor cause a localized area of plastic deformation (crushing) due to high localized compressive stress. In particular, this is significant in the beginning stages of an impact event, as local deformation is responsible for the transfer of forces from the impactor into the target. A model that is used to describe such non-linear behavior in the contact zone is Hertz’s contact theory19,21.

The contact force is expressed as41\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{cccc}& {F}_{c}={k}_{c}{\delta }^{3/2}& & \end{array}$$\end{document}where δ is the local indentation. Damage dissipation energy

Damage Dissipation refers to the nonrecoverable Energy that has been irreversibly consumed due to Crack Initiation; Crack Propagation; Stiffness Degradation; Softening of the Concrete Matrix; and Fracture Localization. The Damage Model was developed from Continuum Damage Mechanics, in which Material Degradation is modeled using a single scalar Damage Variable, D22,23. The present scalar damage formulation is intended as a computationally efficient phenomenological representation of stiffness degradation. Unlike advanced Concrete Damaged Plasticity (CDP) formulations, the model does not explicitly distinguish between tensile and compressive damage evolution. Consequently, localized fracture behavior should be interpreted within this limitation.

Significance of the proposed model

The proposed analytical-energy-based model is an innovative, transparently physically based, and computationally efficient method for analyzing and assessing the behavior of reinforced concrete slabs subjected to impacts at velocities lower than those typically experienced in blasts. As such, the significance of the proposed model resides in its potential to assess not only overall responses of the structure, i.e., global responses such as maximum displacements and contact forces, but also the internal distribution of the energies involved in flexural deformations, shear resistances, membrane actions, local crushing, and energy dissipating damages. In this respect, the proposed model has several advantages over existing standard single degree of freedom (SDOF) formulations used for modeling RC structures subjected to impact loading. First, the model captures impactor-slab interactions by incorporating two degrees of freedom (DOF) dynamical system which enables to better describe the coupling effects arising between the two components of the system. Second, unlike purely empirical impact models, the model links each resistance term to a physically meaningful mechanism. For example, as shown in Fig. 16, the flexural energy contributes mainly to the initial phase of global bending deformations; membrane energy starts to become significant at high deflection values; shear energy is responsible for crack widening and for punching tendency; local crushing energy is related to the indentation occurring under the impactor; and finally, the damage energy corresponds to the irreversibility of cracking and stiffness reduction. Therefore, due to its ability to account separately for all these different types of mechanical behaviors, the model appears to be well-suited to compare normal strength concrete (NSC), fiber-reinforced concrete (FRC), and ultra-high-performance fiber-reinforced concrete (UHPFRC) slabs subjected to impact loads. Additionally, the model can be extended to equivalent blast loadings using impulse-momentum relationships15,17,20,34,38. This allows the proposed model to be applied both for preliminary design purposes as well as for understanding the fundamental response mechanisms of slabs subjected to extreme dynamic loading conditions.

Validation and proof of the proposed analytical–energy-based model

The validity of the proposed analytical-energy-based model will be demonstrated through a thorough verification process, combining a rigorous theoretical consistency evaluation, the satisfaction of physical laws governing the governing mechanisms of the problem, and comparisons made with available data from previously conducted experiments25, analyses28, and simulations29,33,39. A model is generally accepted as being valid if it does not violate any governing law of physics as well as if it is capable of simulating a wide range of experimentally measured structural behaviors and/or benchmark numerical solutions. Hence, our validation procedure is based on five main criteria: (i) dynamic equilibrium consistency, (ii) total energy conservation, (iii) mechanism level physical compatibility, (iv) comparison with established benchmark studies, and (v) material level validation for advanced cementious systems including UHPFRC.

As introduced in Eqs. (78 and 79), have been derived directly from Newton’s second law. Thus, they inherently satisfy the basic requirement of satisfying dynamic equilibrium. Verification of physical admissibility of individual mechanisms

One significant part of verifying how well a model works is determining if the governing mechanisms within the model represent physical behaviors which have been previously verified experimentally. In addition to the previously mentioned work on developing a flexural formulation (Eqs. 22–26) that is consistent with both yield line theory (Johansen 1930), and the flexural behavior of reinforced concrete as described in ACI 318–1929, Eurocode 23, and Mo36; previous experimental work done on RC slabs subjected to impact demonstrated that flexural deformation is typically one of the primary mechanisms of global energy absorption prior to the beginning of the post peak region3,9,16. Additionally, the CSCT (Critical Shear Crack Theory) is the basis for the shear resistance formulation provided in Eqs. (27)–(31). In accordance with this theory, shear strength degrades due to increases in crack widths. Also, in accordance with aggregate interlock theories, proposed by Walraven19, a degradation of shear strength occurs due to increases in crack widths. Both of these formulations were extensively verified experimentally and are now being applied in the fib model code provisions17,30. The membrane action formulation provided in Eqs. (32)–(40) is representative of geometric non-linear stiffening due to tensional forces acting upon reinforcing bars at high levels of deflection. Originally proposed by Bailey41 and further supported by experiments conducted by Xiao et al.9 demonstrating such an effect for RC slabs impacted, the dramatic increase in membrane contribution predicted by the proposed formulation is therefore consistent with observations made regarding very large deflected slabs. The local crushing formulation as presented in Eqs. (41)–(46) follows Hertzian nonlinear contact mechanics5,7. Local contact models utilizing Hertizian contact mechanics have been successfully employed in numerous analytical and numerical investigations related to local concrete impact behavior12,15,22.

The damage dissipation formulation developed in Eqs. (47)-(54) follows continuum damage mechanics principles initially proposed by Lubliner et al.13 and subsequently modified by Lemaitre and Desmorat34. The damage dissipation formulation as presented herein is also consistent with ABAQUS’s concrete damaged plasticity (CDP) model33. Therefore, each of the components comprising total energy within the proposed formulation correspond to valid structural mechanisms as opposed to empirically determined parameters.

Comparative validation against experimental and numerical studies

In order to evaluate the predictive capability of the presented model qualitatively, we have compared our results with available literature on experimental, analytical and numerical investigations of reinforced concrete slabs exposed to low-velocity impact loading. We selected an experimental validation case consisting of a 1000 × 1000 × 75 mm reinforced concrete plate impacted with drop weights at different energy levels (approximately 2.5–6.0 kJ). We obtained predicted maximum slab displacements between 25 and 30 mm. These values compare well with those measured experimentally by Xiao et al.9 as these authors found maximum displacements between 26.6 and 28.1 mm for equivalent configurations and loadings. The average relative difference between the analytical and experimental maximum displacements was around ± 5% to ± 10%. This range compares well with that typically reported for advanced 2DoF analytical formulations and nonlinear finite element calculations6,9. Additionally, we made additional comparisons with finite element simulations carried out using ABAQUS CDP-type models8,33; the two approaches showed very good agreements concerning: the maximum displacement, the cracking pattern and localization of damage; punching shear behaviour; and post-loading residual deformations. In summary, the good agreement between the analytical approach proposed here and both experimental and numerical benchmark cases confirm that this approach can predict the global structural response of RC slabs when they are subject to low-velocity impacts.

Parametric analysis of governing variables affecting the low-velocity impact response of reinforced concrete slabs

To demonstrate the practicality of the proposed analytical-energy based model for establishing structural design criteria related to low velocity impacts (LVI), a detailed parametric analysis was undertaken to investigate the effects of key structural parameters on LVI responses of reinforced concrete (RC) slabs. These parameters include: impact energy structural material properties (thickness) structural fiber properties (reinforced vs. non-reinforced) structural boundary constraints (boundary restraint conditions) structural reinforcement ratios structural energy redistribution characteristics.

All analytical models presented in Eqs. (22–67) were used throughout this study to evaluate the LVI response of the same reference RC slab that is illustrated below in Fig. 1.

The reference slab dimensions are 1000 × 1000 × 75 mm, which corresponds to the size of RC slabs studied experimentally as reported by Xiao et al.9 who conducted a series of experiments testing RC slabs subjected to drop weight impact. The principal objective of parametric studies was to develop quantifiable correlations between: maximum displacement, mechanisms of energy absorption, crack growth patterns, punching shear resistance, damage dissipation rates, and failure modes resulting from LVI.

In addition to enabling physically meaningful interpretations regarding failure development, the ability of the current analytical framework to track redistribution of impact energy among various mechanisms of deformation including flexural deformation; tensile membrane actions; shear deterioration; local crushing; and damage dissipation provides additional insight into the fundamental structural mechanisms controlling resistance to impact.

At intermediate levels of impact energy, tensile membrane action resisted early catastrophic failure by distributing some of the input energy into the stretching of reinforcing steel. Nevertheless, once critical crack widths were established beneath the point of initial impact, punching resistance declined rapidly and local failure became predominant. The reasons why UHPFRC demonstrates such high levels of performance are: Enhanced tensile strength, Improved fracture toughness, Crack bridging mechanisms, increased post-cracking ductility created by the addition of steel fibers.

Conversely, unlike NSC systems that have relatively poor tensile properties and tend to exhibit brittle behavior under load, fiber reinforced systems distribute stress throughout the formation of cracks more efficiently and slow down the process of crack coalescence underneath the area where the impact occurred. Therefore, UHPFRC slabs exhibited: Reduced crack widths, Improved membrane properties, Delayed punching failures, Higher post-impact residual capacities.

In addition to demonstrating that fiber reinforcement significantly improves the structural performance of slabs subjected to low-velocity impacts, the analytical data demonstrated that material behavior affects the predominant mechanisms used to transfer energy into the structures. For instance, the majority of impact energy absorbed by NSC slabs are transferred into damage dissipation mechanisms related to cracking and loss of stiffness. Conversely, UHPFRC slabs absorbed a far greater amount of the impact energy through stable flexural and membrane deformation mechanisms.

For example, the increase in membrane energy contributions observed in UHPFRC systems are a direct result of the improved tensile reinforcement continuity and crack bridging efficiencies resulting from fiber reinforcement. Additionally, the amount of damage dissipation energy was significantly reduced in UHPFRC systems since fiber reinforcement limits the ability of fractures to localize and reduces the degree of crack opening.

The effects of fiber shape were also studied. It was determined that twisted steel fibers have the greatest potential for absorbing energy due to: Mechanical interlocking between individual fibers, Pullout resistance of individual fibers, Greater bonding interactions between fibers and surrounding matrix.

Hooked end fibers were found to provide medium levels of energy absorption. Straight fibers were found to only improve the ability to initially control crack formation but provided little benefit in terms of post-cracking energy dissipation. These same trends were observed by Gao et al.11, Li et al.12, Banthia and Mindess21 and Rossi42 in experimental studies conducted by these researchers. They found that fiber geometry has a strong effect on fracture resistance and impact energy absorption capacity.

Additionally, it was demonstrated using the analytical models that UHPC systems lacking fiber reinforcement exhibit improved compressive strengths and smaller global deformations than NSC systems; however, their post-cracking tensile ductilities remain relatively small due to lack of adequate crack bridging mechanisms. Hence, plain UHPC slabs remain more prone to localized punching-related cracking than UHPFRC systems.

Thus, increasing the compressive strength of a material does not guarantee improvement in its impact resistant properties. Rather, efficient impact-resistant structural behavior requires: Tensile ductility, Distributed crack propagation, Effective crack bridging mechanisms, Stable energy redistribution capacity.

As such, inclusion of steel fiber reinforcement remains one of the most effective ways to improve: Impact resistance, Energy absorption efficiency, Crack control, post-impact structural survival probability.

Influence of boundary conditions and reinforcement ratio

Statically indeterminate boundary conditions and reinforcement ratio have an important effect on the total flexibility of reinforced concrete (RC) slabs loaded dynamically and affect the membrane action, cracking mechanism, and punch-through shear strength of these slabs. The objective of this study was to investigate the statically eterminate and indeterminate behavior of simply supported, partially restrained, and fully restrained slab systems to provide insight into how they will behave when impacted. Also, the statically indeterminate behavior of slab systems was studied with various amounts of steel reinforcement to understand how the amount of steel used will affect the structural response and ultimate failure of the slabs. The static analysis results showed that fully restrained slabs had less displacements under impact loads and better resistance to impacts than did simply supported systems. The increase in edge restraint resulted in a significant decrease in maximum displacement from about 41.2 mm for simply supported slabs to about 16.8 mm for fully restrained slabs or almost a 60% reduction in the global deformability of the slab system (see Table 5). The analytical solutions demonstrate that the contributions of each of these individual energy mechanisms evolve progressively during impact loading and control when there is a transition from a stable deformation response to a localized punching failure.

During the early stages of an impact load, most of the input impact energy is absorbed by global flexural deformation mechanisms represented by Eqs. (22)–(26). During this period, flexural energy absorbs approximately 60–75% of the total absorbed energy, dependent upon the level of impact severity and type of slab. Also, crack development is still fairly uniformly distributed across the surface of the slab and exhibits stable deformation response.

As slab displacement increases, tensile membrane action becomes progressively more influential due to geometrically non-linear stretching of reinforcement. Membrane action contributes a significant portion of the total absorbed energy, up to approximately 35–45%, especially at large displacements. Due to membrane action resisting catastrophic punching failure by redirecting impact energy away from localized shear mechanisms, membrane action delays punching failure.

Since membrane action has been shown to be very important, since it demonstrates a transition from a purely bending dominated response to a combination of both flexural and membrane actions which can result in a substantial improvement in impact resistance; similar conclusions have been drawn by Bailey41 and Xu et al.5, who concluded that tensile membrane action has a key role in improving post-crack impact resistance and delaying structural collapse. However, as the severity of impact continues to increase, punching shear degradation mechanisms represented by Eqs. (27)- (31), become progressively more severe. As cracks grow wider, they reduce aggregate interlock and shear transfer efficiency which causes localized stiffness loss under the impact region.

Consequently, damage dissipation energy grows rapidly as failure approaches and is highly correlated with: severe crack localization, punching cone formation, local crushing, and irreversible structural degeneration.

For instance, the analytical results showed that slabs failing catastrophically had damage dissipation energies above about 20–25% of the total absorbed energy. On the other hand, slabs exhibiting flexible-membrane deformations-maintained damage dissipation energies below approximately 10–15%. The analytical predictions further show that UHPFRC systems-maintained damage dissipation energies less than those of NSC systems as a result of improved crack bridging behaviors and distributed fracture resistance. Likewise, thicker slabs and fully restrained systems exhibited lower amounts of damage dissipation due to the fact that larger portions of the absorbed impact energy were redirected globally through mechanisms involving deformation rather than through localized cracking. Hence, the present study confirms that structurally resistant behavior against impacts is controlled mainly by two parameters: maximizing stable flexural and membrane energy absorption, suppressing localized punching failure; minimizing damage dissipation energy, and maintaining distributed crack growth behavior.

The transition between flexural dominated response and punching dominated failure was determined by factors such as: amount of impact energy applied, thickness of slab, ratio of reinforcement, behavioral characteristics of materials used, and types of restraining forces.

Slender NSC slabs having inadequate reinforcement experienced a rapid transition into brittle punching failures while UHPFRC slabs experiencing sufficient restrain exhibited stable flexural-membrane behavior over a much greater range of deformations.

Therefore, efficient design for high impact resistance does not solely involve increasing material strengths but involves optimizing the dominant pathways through which energy is redistributed during progressive structural failure. The structural response to low velocity impact loads on RC slabs proceeds through various stages of deformation and failure which are controlled by redistribution of input impact energy among competitive structural mechanisms. Validation and quantitative assessment of the proposed framework

To establish the predictive capability and physical reliability of the proposed analytical–energy-based framework, the analytical results were systematically validated against previously reported: experimental investigations, analytical studies, and nonlinear finite-element simulations. Particular emphasis was placed on validating: maximum displacement, crack propagation trends, energy redistribution behavior, and punching shear failure mechanisms.

The primary experimental benchmark adopted in the present study was the drop-weight impact investigation performed by Xiao et al.9, involving RC slabs with dimensions of 1000 × 1000 × 75 mm subjected to low-velocity impact loading conditions comparable to those considered in the present investigation.

The analytical predictions demonstrated strong agreement with the experimentally measured structural response. For impact energies ranging between approximately 2.5–6.0 kJ, the present analytical model predicted maximum slab displacements ranging from approximately 25–30 mm, which closely matched the experimentally observed displacement range of 26.6–28.1 mm reported by Xiao et al.9.

The analytical results additionally reproduced experimentally observed: crack propagation patterns, punching shear development, membrane resistance activation, and progressive stiffness degradation trends.

The prediction deviation generally remained within approximately ± 5–10%, confirming the strong predictive capability of the proposed framework.

The analytical results were further compared with nonlinear ABAQUS Concrete Damaged Plasticity (CDP) simulations reported by Anas et al.8. The finite-element simulations predicted maximum slab displacements of approximately 27.3 mm under impact loading conditions similar to those investigated analytically. The present analytical predictions remained highly consistent with these numerical results and reproduced similar: crack localization patterns, punching cone development, and deformation characteristics.

Unlike highly computationally expensive nonlinear finite-element simulations, the proposed analytical–energy-based framework provides: substantially lower computational cost, direct mechanism-level interpretation, efficient parametric assessment capability, and physically transparent energy redistribution analysis. Overall influence of governing parameters on structural response

Comprehensive parametric analyses showed that the low-velocity impact response of reinforced concrete slabs are affected by a complex interplay of the parameters of: low-velocity impact energy, thickness of the reinforced concrete slab, behavior of material, reinforcing fibers, boundary restraint conditions, and amount of reinforcing steel.

While each of these parameters alone affects how the structure responds to an impact, the results of the analysis show that the overall ability of a structure to resist impacts depends largely on its ability to transfer impact energy through stable mechanisms of global deformation and to prevent localized brittle failure mechanisms.

In accordance with the generalized energy redistribution relationships as presented in Eqs. (65)–(67), structures which were able to maximize both their flexural and tensile membrane energy absorption, while minimizing their irreversible dissipation of energy into damage were much more resilient and had larger amounts of time before they reached punching failure than those whose primary method of absorbing energy was to cause large amounts of damage. On the other hand, structures that caused too much energy to be dissipated as damage would quickly begin experiencing rapid crack growth and develop punching-type collapse mechanisms. Results of the analysis demonstrate that as the thickness of the slab was increased from 60 to 125 mm, there was a reduction in maximum displacement of about 68% while at the same time reducing the energy dissipated in causing damage from 28.6% to 7.6%. Additionally, results of the analysis demonstrated that use of UHPFRC (Ultra-High Performance Fiber Reinforced Concrete) instead of NSC (Normal Strength Concrete) resulted in a reduction of displacement by nearly 61% and an increase in the contribution made by membrane actions for energy absorption from approximately 16.3–42.4%.

Additionally, results of the analysis demonstrated that the degree of restraint provided by the boundary condition can have a significant effect on the impact resistance of reinforced concrete slabs. Simply supported slabs experienced maximum displacements that were approximately 59% larger than those experienced by fully restrained slabs due to increased tensile membrane action described by Eqs. (32)–(40). Results of the analysis demonstrated that the percentage of total absorbed energy contributed by membrane actions was increased from approximately 18.3–47.5%, while results of the analysis demonstrated that energy dissipated in causing damage was reduced from approximately 26.7–9.8%. Results of the analysis also demonstrated that an increase in reinforcement ratio has been shown to improve the flexural stiffness and delay punching type failure. On the other hand, extremely high reinforcement ratios result in higher localized contact stress below the impact area resulting in higher potential for localized crushing. Thus, it may be concluded based upon results of this study that optimal performance under low-velocity impact loads will be obtained when the structural system:Has maximum flexural-membrane energy absorption,Has no punching shear degradation,Has minimum damage dissipation energy, andHas distributed cracking behavior.

Conclusions

In this research, a unified analytical-energy based method was proposed for the non-linear response of reinforced concrete (RC) slabs subjected to low velocity impact (LVI) or equivalent blast loading conditions. The proposed framework integrates enhanced two degree-of-freedom (2-DoF) dynamic interactions with Explicit multi-mechanism energy redistributions which are able to capture Flexural deformations, tensile membrane actions, punching shear, local crushings, damage dissipation, and enhance post-cracking behaviors of advanced materials. Strain rate enhancement was also implemented utilizing dynamic increase factors (DIFs); punching shear prediction were obtained using critical shear crack theory (CSCT); nonlinear membrane behavior were obtained using large deflection membrane formulations. In conclusion, the proposed analytical-energy based approach provides comprehensive methodologies for understanding, predicting and optimizing the nonlinear responses of RC slabs subjected to LVI or equivalent blast loadings. This framework establishes direct relationship between the redistributing of impact energies into progressive failure evolutions and structural performance. These findings are expected to contribute to develop safer, more resilient and energy-efficient protective structural systems.

Limitations and future research

The present framework was primarily validated using centrally impacted square reinforced concrete slabs subjected to single impact events. Although the model successfully captures global deformation, energy redistribution, punching shear behavior, and damage evolution, several limitations remain.

First, the current formulation does not explicitly account for cumulative damage accumulation, stiffness memory effects, or residual capacity degradation under repeated impact loading. Second, the blast-loading extension is based on impulse equivalence and does not explicitly represent spatial blast-wave effects. Third, fiber orientation variability and local fiber distribution effects are represented through homogenized bond-efficiency parameters. Finally, additional validation is required for complex geometries, eccentric impacts, partial fixity degradation, and large-scale structural systems.

Future research should focus on repeated-impact scenarios, cumulative damage evolution, multi-hazard loading conditions, advanced blast-wave interaction mechanisms, and probabilistic treatment of material and geometric uncertainties.
