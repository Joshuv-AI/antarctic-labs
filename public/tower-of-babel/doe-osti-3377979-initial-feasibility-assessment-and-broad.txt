 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Structural health monitoring is critical for ensuring the operational safety and cost-competitiveness of the
developing advanced reactor designs. The extreme operational envelopes of these advanced architectures,
having operating temperatures ranging 400°C–1,000°C and heightened displacement damage doses,
render conventional commercially available piezoelectric transducers and resistive strain gauges unviable.
Optical fiber sensors present an attractive solution for advanced radiation-hardened instrumentation due to
their high thermal stability and distributed sensing capabilities. However, their deployment in embedded
applications can be hindered by the severe thermomechanical strain driven by the coefficient of thermal
expansion mismatch between fused silica glass and structural metal alloys like stainless steel (e.g.,
SS316L).

This report details a 7-month process optimization study focused on using blown-powder direct energy
deposition (BPDED) via an Insstek MX-Lab system to embed optical fibers within an SS316L matrix.
Initial parameter sweeps established a stable processing matrix`, optimizing laser power, traverse speed,
and powder feed rates to ensure volumetric density (minimal porosity) and dimensional accuracy. Direct
deposition of material onto thin copper-coated fibers resulted in coating dissolution and core fracture due
to severe localized thermal shock. To mitigate this effect, a hybrid protective packaging strategy was
developed using an SS316L capillary tube backfilled with a BAg-1 silver-based braze alloy. Initial
continuous powder passes yielded minimal pre-compression residual strain (300 µm/m), indicating
insufficient liquification of the internal braze wire. By implementing a modified protocol consisting of
four autogenous (no powder feed) laser pre-heat passes before structural encapsulation, the internal braze
core successfully liquefied, consistently inducing high room-temperature residual compressive strains
between 5,000 µm/m and 6,000 µm/m while maintaining baseline optical continuity. These extreme pre-
compressive strain levels provide a crucial thermomechanical buffer, validating the hybrid BPDED–
brazing approach as a potential pathway for component-scale sensor integration in advanced reactors.

 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 STRUCTURAL HEALTH MONITORING

Structural health monitoring (SHM) remains essential for nuclear power plant operations, providing
critical data toward predicting component degradation, minimizing shutdowns for maintenance, and
providing overall operational safety (Meyer et al., 2011). The current fleet of light-water reactors in the
United States are aging; many have applied for extensions on operational licensing (Kim et al., 2022;
McCarthy et al., 2015). Data from online measurements and nondestructive examinations are essential for
monitoring and predicting the operational lifespan of these aging reactors. Acoustic emission and
vibration monitoring are mature techniques deployed to detect severe, operation-limiting phenomena such
as irradiation-assisted strain corrosion cracking in baffle bolts (Allen et al., 2010; Bosch et al., 2021),
pressure boundary leaks in valves (Hu, 2020; Nakamura & Terada, 1985), or flow-induced vibration
fatigue in control rods (Pettigrew, 1970; Pettigrew, 2013). However, as the industry transitions toward
next-generation (Gen IV) advanced reactor architectures, the operational envelope drastically shifts.
These advanced systems are designed to operate at significantly elevated temperatures, typically ranging
from 400°C to over 1,000°C, and will subject internal components to higher displacement damage doses
(Sabharwall et al., 2013; Zinkle et al., 2016). Under these extreme environmental conditions,
commercially available conventional piezoelectric transducers and resistive strain gauges quickly exceed
their functional limits, creating a critical sensing gap in the primary coolant loop and core-adjacent
structures (A. Birri et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2014).

Optical fiber sensors present an attractive SHM solution to the current limitations in sensing technologies
already deployed in reactors. Fused silica optical fibers offer multiple advantages in extreme
environments: (1) immunity to electromagnetic interference (Fok et al., 2011; Pendão & Silva, 2022);
(2) moderate resistance to ionizing radiation (Christian M. Petrie et al., 2019; Daniel C. Sweeney &
Christian M. Petrie, 2024); (3) thermal stability up to 1,000°C (Jones et al., 2022; Wood et al., 2014).
Using interferometry in the frequency or time domain, distributed static strain or temperature can be
measured along the lengths of optical fibers (Froggatt & Moore, 1998; Hill & Meltz, 1997). Other fiber-
based sensors such as Fabry–Pérot cavities and fiber Bragg gratings (FBGs) can be used for acoustic
sensing (Wang et al., 2019). FBGs, in particular, can be spaced along a fiber to introduce multiple
patterns and create a distributed acoustic sensing network. Similarly, distributed acoustic sensing systems
operating based on optical time domain reflectometry can provide even more sensing points at kilohertz
frequencies over longer distances (tens of kilometers), at the expense of spatial resolution (meters). These
capabilities are particularly attractive for the automated, online monitoring necessary to reduce operation
and maintenance costs in advanced reactors.

Despite the material advantages of optical fibers, their long-term deployment in reactor components is
bottlenecked by the need for physical bonding to the structure for improved strain and vibrational
monitoring. Although such bonding is not necessary, it greatly improves thermal response to temperature
changes (H. C. Hyer et al., 2025; Hyer & Petrie, 2024). Many components in commercial reactors are
manufactured from metallic alloys. Stainless steels (SS) such as SS316 and SS304 are commonly found
in pressure vessel claddings, core structural components, and the hundreds of meters of piping in and
around the reactor systems (Zinkle & Was, 2013). Embedding or integrating optical fibers into these SS
components would provide site-specific monitoring at critical locations that are usually measured
peripherally or estimated with modeling/simulation. However, the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE)
mismatch between SS (20 × 10−6 m/m°C at 25°C) and fused silica (0.55 × 10−6 m/m°C at 25°C) is a major
challenge because the expanding SS matrix at higher temperatures may overcome the fracture toughness
of the fiber, thereby causing fiber failure. Therefore, the successful integration of optical sensors for high-

 1

temperature static strain or acoustic monitoring fundamentally depends on establishing a bond that locks
the fiber into a state of high residual compression at ambient temperatures.

1.2 ADVANCED MANUFACTURING

Several high-temperature bonding techniques have been investigated to induce a state of pre-compression
in the optical fiber to overcome the CTE mismatch and ensure the survivability of embedded optical
sensors. Current advanced manufacturing techniques being explored include spark plasma sintering
(Zhang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024), hot rolling (Chikamori & Minakuchi, 2026), high-temperature
brazing (T. Birri et al., 2025), electroplating (Mathew, 2024), and additive manufacturing (AM) (Hyer &
Petrie, 2024). Spark plasma sintering utilizes an electric field to assist in the high-pressure, high-
temperature sintering of metal powder around a fiber; hot rolling physically deforms metal plates around
the sensor at elevated temperatures; brazing involves melting and solidifying a desired alloy over the fiber
and/or to the component; and electroplating utilizes the electrolytic deposition of metal ions onto the fiber
to build material. These methods have all been established as robust bonding methods for optical fibers
that yield residual compressive strain. However, they are generally restricted to simpler planar geometries
or coupon-level embedding, limiting their applicability for complex, component-scale reactor structures.

Internally embedding the sensor in a desired component often requires integrating the sensor during the
manufacturing process. AM techniques inherently build material layer-by-layer rather than using a more
conventional casting/forging and subtractive machining approach (Sames et al., 2016). This layer-by-
layer approach can be leveraged to locate sensors in components, pause the build to sequester and house
the sensors in user-formed cavities, and then resume the additive process, thereby encapsulating the
sensor within the matrix. The AM techniques available to fabricate metal components to near-net shape
can be broadly categorized into three families, as summarized in Figure 1 (Armstrong et al., 2022; Sames
et al., 2016).

 Figure 1. Additive manufacturing processes proposed for fabricating metals broken down into three
 categories: (1) solid-state; (2) severe plastic deformation; (3) melting.

Because solid-state AM processes are usually performed at low temperatures and incur no real
mechanical forces that may cause fiber failure, they are attractive for embedding fragile sensors such as

 2

optical fibers. Powder bed ink jet (PBiJ) and direct ink jet (DIJ) both involve jetting a binder either onto a
powder bed or premixed with the powder itself and applied to a substrate (Armstrong et al., 2022).
Cavities such as holes or protrusions can be included in the 3D printed structure for sensor insertion after
building; subsequent densification steps (including sintering or infiltration) will bond the sensor to the
component matrix. One of the main disadvantages of these solid-state techniques is the need to burn out
the binder with a low-temperature calcination before a high-temperature sintering procedure (Armstrong
et al., 2022). PBiJ has been successfully used in integrating sensors in ceramics such as SiC (Petrie et al.,
2021), but is not ideal for metals where the nascent oxide layer disrupts metallic bonding (Armstrong et
al., 2022).

Severe plastic deformation processes such as ultrasonic AM (UAM) and friction stir welding (such as the
MELD machine) are technically considered solid-state processes, but they introduce intense mechanical
energy that induces high temperatures locally at the welding interfaces (Hehr & Norfolk, 2019; Sames et
al., 2016). Both UAM and friction stir welding involve the use of tooling to apply mechanical energy into
the system to break up the oxide layer and bond the feedstock to the base via severe plastic deformation
(Hehr & Norfolk, 2019; Sames et al., 2016). The design of these AM systems is inherently hybrid because
they are integrated into computer numerical control (CNC) mills where machining operations can be
used. UAM has been extensively used to mill channels in material for housing fiber optics before
proceeding to weld over the top, thereby embedding them in the metal matrix (Hehr & Norfolk, 2019;
Hyer et al., 2022; Christian M Petrie et al., 2019). Once proper channel sizing has been achieved, proper
bonding for strain coupling can be achieved (Hyer et al., 2022). The main drawback of using severe
plastic deformation to additively build material is that geometric resolution is limited to the tooling size
(>5 mm) or what can be achieved by machining operations. Moreover, the tooling requires a flat 2D plane
to achieve good bonding, making curved surface challenging (Hyer et al., 2022).

Melting-based AM processes have become widespread because they directly address the nascent oxide
layer issue that has proven problematic with solid-state processes: the melting source simply spalls the
oxide layer, often reducing it to O2 gas that gets carried away. Powder bed fusion (PBF) has demonstrated
the ability to directly fabricate components to near-net shape, and direct energy deposition (DED) can
scale to build components multiple meters in size (Sames et al., 2016). In PBF, a laser (LPBF) or
electron-beam (EBPBF) source is used to selectively melt regions of a powder bed, additively building
material layer-by-layer by fusing particles in the size range of 15–150 µm (Sames et al., 2016). Figure 2a
gives the basic operation of LPBF, demonstrating the advantage of achieving higher geometric resolution
(>200 µm) than EBPBF because the need to sinter the powder bed for electron conduction can cause
partial sintering of particles along component surfaces in EBPBF (Sames et al., 2016). LPBF is performed
at ambient temperature and atmospheric pressure, removing the need to operate in a vacuum as must be
done in EBPBF to tighten and focus the electron beam (Sames et al., 2016). Optical fiber embedding has
been demonstrated in LPBF by integrating channels into the 3D printed component, sequestering the fiber
below the build plane in the powder bed, and resuming the selective laser melting over the top of the
sensor (Hyer & Petrie, 2024).

Compressive strain achieved in both UAM and LPBF on the embedded optical fiber is roughly
2,500 µm/m (Hyer & Petrie, 2024). Considering that strains in excess of 10,000 µm/m (expected at
approximately 500°C) are possible in some advanced reactor concepts due to their higher proposed
operating temperatures, the fiber may succumb to these larger strains and fail while in service.
Additionally, although sequestering fibers in channels can be properly detailed and repeated, this process
remains somewhat difficult because the working diameter of the fibers is approximately 125–250 µm
(Hyer & Petrie, 2024). Commercially available coatings for fused silica fibers are often polymers (e.g.,
acrylics); metal coatings are limited to easily electroplated metals: aluminum, gold, and copper.
Furthermore, these coating thicknesses are often less than 100 µm, providing a minimal margin for
bonding. Dialing in the parameters for UAM or LPBF leaves little room for error, considering that the
feedstock foils in UAM are hundreds of microns in thickness, and the melt pool formed by the laser in
LPBF is usually more than 250 µm deep. Therefore, a more robust AM embedding process should be
considered based on currently commercially available optical fibers.

DED is often regarded as the large-scale AM process; it is typically used to fabricate large components
with resolution on the scale of millimeters rather than micrometers like PBF (Sames et al., 2016).
Common feedstocks for DED are either wire or powder; the former is a derivative of wire-feed welding,
and the latter is a derivative of thermal spray processes (Sames et al., 2016). Wire-feed DED, also known
as wire-arc AM, is not optimized for high resolution and would likely require extensive R&D process
development to scale down appropriately (Sames et al., 2016). Alternatively, blown-powder DED

 4

(BPDED) has the potential to be scaled down to less than 1 mm in geometric resolution if the powder-
guiding nozzle can be properly directed and synced with the melting source (i.e., laser or electron beam)
(Sames et al., 2016). Figure 2b illustrates laser-based BPDED, which shares the same advantages of
LPBF over EBPBF by operating at ambient temperature and atmospheric pressure. While the elevated
temperatures inherent to these melting techniques pose a significant thermomechanical challenge for
delicate optical fibers, they also present an ideal mechanism for sensor encapsulation. If the process is
carefully optimized, then the localized melt pool allows the fiber to be seamlessly integrated directly into
the structural matrix during fabrication, placing the sensor under critical residual compressive strain upon
solidification while maintaining the geometric freedom necessary for next-generation reactor designs.

This report details the use of BPDED for embedding optical fiber sensors in an SS316L matrix to realize a
more readily deployable embedding and attachment process while achieving high compressive residual
strains on the embedded fiber.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS

2.1 OPTICAL FIBER INTERROGATION AND PREPARATION

Optical frequency domain reflectometry (OFDR) is an optical technique that measures the reflected
spectrum produced by Rayleigh backscattering of light or fiber FBGs. A tunable laser source is used to
interrogate a range of wavelengths, approximately 1,530–1,570 nm in this case. The backscattered light
combines with a reference beam to create an interference pattern. Fourier analysis can be used to analyze
the beat frequencies in the measured interference patterns and isolate specific spatial regions (defined by
the sensor gauge length and spacing) along the fiber and transform those regions back into the spectral
domain. For sensing applications, the fiber is terminated with a coreless length of fused silica to help
bleed the transmitted light and prevent the large back-reflections that occur at the end of the fiber from
coupling back into the single-mode sensing fiber. Otherwise, these back-reflections would obscure the
weaker Rayleigh backscatter signal that is used to measure distributed temperature and strain.

Single-mode silica (SiO2) fibers with a germanium-doped (5 wt % germanium) fused silica core, pure
silica cladding, and copper coating, with diameters of 9, 125, 165 µm, respectively, were acquired from
IVG Fiber (Ontario, Canada). Copper-coated fibers approximately 1 m long were spliced with an FC/APC
connector to interface a Luna Innovations OBR-4600 (Virgina, USA) and terminated with a roughly
100 mm long coreless segment for sensing. Prepared fibers were scanned before (prescan) and after
(postscan) embedding to perform a correlation and back out a residual strain. The correlation was
performed with the processing scheme discussed in Sweeney et al. (Sweeney & Petrie, 2022; D. C.
Sweeney & C. M. Petrie, 2024; D.C. Sweeney et al., 2021; Daniel C. Sweeney et al., 2021); all code was
written in Python 3.13.

2.2 DED PROCESSING

The BPDED process was executed using an Insstek (Daejeon, South Korea) MX-Lab system, a three-axis
DED machine with a 150 mm × 150 mm × 150 mm build volume. The MX-Lab utilizes a 500 W
ytterbium-fiber laser with a 400 µm beam diameter. Powder delivery is managed by a built-in vibratory
method feeding system featuring a multi-hopper setup and providing accurate control over the SS316L
powder feed rate. Argon is used as the cover gas for the embedding process. SS304 build plates were used
as the starting substrate, and pre-alloyed SS316L powder acquired from PAC (Ohio, USA) was used as
depositing material.

Initial process optimization studies were conducted to explore the melting and solidification of SS316L
deposited by the lab-scale BPDED machine. A design of experiments approach was used to vary the most

 5

influential parameters: laser power, travel speed, layer height, powder flow rate, powder delay timing,
tool path spacing, and laser trajectory. The laser power (P) can be varied from 50 to 500 W; the travel
speed (V) depends on the velocity of the CNC X and Y axes rather than a scanning mechanism like a gyro
mirror in PBF; the Z axis is related to layer height (LT) or how far the tooling moves upward from the
base. The powder delay is how long the powder flows before the laser melting starts, and the powder flow
rate is how much powder is being directed per unit time. Finally, the tool path spacing (TS) is the step-
over dimension of the axes, and the trajectory is the fill strategy: contouring the outside perimeter (C) and
filling in the bulk (F) using a spiral (Figure 3a) or Zigzag (Figure 3b) pattern. Figure 3c demonstrates the
operational logic of the laser processing considering a contour→fill→contour (CFC) strategy, in which
the contour is first scanned, followed by the chosen strategy for filling the bulk, rounded out by another
contour scanning before terminating the layer.

  Figure 3. Laser scan strategies used for DED of SS316L include (a) spiral inside-out (b) Zigzag. Laser scan
trajectory typically follows a series of melting operations: contour(s) following the perimeter of the part profile and
performing the actual fill scan strategy. (c) The optimized condition included a contour, fill, contour (CFC) strategy.

A central composite design (CCD) was used to vary four parameters in a systematic way to remove user
bias and evaluate parameter combinations independently from one another. Three process optimization
studies were conducted, with naming convention “processing study X” (i.e., PA, PB, PC); each
processing study implemented a different laser strategy. Simple cubic geometries with a 5 × 10 mm cross

section were used for the process optimization studies. Target heights were 12 mm, but, as shown in
Section 3, heights less than 12 mm were commonly observed due to insufficient feeding or part failure.

2.3 METALLOGRAPHY AND MICROSCOPY

Select cube samples were cross-sectioned and metallographically prepared: they were cold-mounted in
epoxy, ground and polished using SiC and diamond colloids, and then underwent a final surface finish on
a vibratory bowl with 0.05 μm colloidal silica. The polished faces were imaged with a Keyence VHX-
6000 optical microscope. Subsequently, the samples were electrolytically etched in a solution of 10 vol %
oxalic acid in distilled water under voltage control at 2 V for 10 s. Etching was performed to reveal the
microstructure and potentially any residual melt pool structure formed by excessive solute segregation.
Images were once again taken on the Keyence optical microscope.

 3. RESULTS

3.1 BPDED PROCESS OPTIMIZATION

The initial optimization phase focused on establishing a stable processing window for the SS316L matrix
using the Insstek MX-Lab. Simple cubic geometries were used in the process optimization studies: the
different parameter combinations determined from a CCD were assigned to individual cubes. Because the
ultimate goal is to embed delicate optical fibers, it is critical to identify parameters that yield sufficient
build height and dimensional accuracy without introducing excessive thermal input that would cause
defect formation. Figure 4 illustrates the macroscopic results of the preliminary parameter sweeps.
Processing study PA demonstrated that insufficient powder mass flow rates (<4.0 g/min) resulted in
negligible build height, rendering these parameters unviable for rapid sensor encapsulation. Furthermore,
increasing the powder delay time beyond 2.5 s yielded no observable benefit; a brief delay is strictly
necessary to allow the powder stream to stabilize within the delivery nozzle before laser activation.
Consequently, the manufacturer-optimized delay of 2.5 s was maintained.

 Figure 4. Fabricated cubes used in the process optimization studies PA, PB, and PC.

Processing study PB investigated the effect of increasing laser power to determine whether supplementary
thermal energy was needed to adequately melt the powder stream, operating with the manufacturer-
suggested feed rate of 3.0 g/min for SS316L. However, the elevated laser power resulted in severe
overmelting, poor edge retention, and an unstable melt pool—conditions that would inevitably lead to the
catastrophic failure of an underlying sensor. Reevaluating the operational design space, the powder mass
flow rate was dynamically adjusted in a preliminary PC Version 1 study to directly evaluate the influence
of the powder stream on dimensional buildup. This study highlighted the extreme sensitivity of
dimensional accuracy to powder feed rates; although the laser power was better optimized,
inconsistencies in the powder feed led to severe geometric distortion and misshapen builds, as highlighted
in the cross-sectional inset of Figure 4, but few pores were observed. A powder feed rate approaching

 10

5 g/min yielded significantly superior stability compared to the recommended 3 g/min while remaining
well within the system’s operational limit of 10 g/min. A finalized PC version 2 matrix was printed using
the refined parameters based on the findings with PC version 1, and those results are reported in Table 1.

PC version 2 was fabricated, and the cubes were subsequently cross-sectioned and metallographically
prepared. Figure 5 shows the polished cross sections of these specimens, categorized by three powder
feed rates, ranging from 4.5 to 5 g/min because that range appeared to be optimal based on PC version 1.
The evaluated parameter space encompassed variations in laser power (240–270 W), traverse speed (750–
950 mm/min), and volumetric energy density (315–453 J/mm3), alongside distinct scan strategies
(CFC+Zigzag and CF+Zigzag). The parameters of the five samples processed with 4.5 g/min are similar
to those printed at 5 g/min, albeit with a different powder feed rate; the overall heights of the cubes
fabricated at 5 g/min are clearly taller than those fabricated at 4.5 g/min, as expected with a fast flow rate.
Caving from a rectangular shape to something with more curvature appears to occur regardless of flow
rate. Caving appears to be worse in cubes fabricated with 4.75 g/min; the main difference was that no
second contour scan followed the fill scan.

Visual inspection of the polished cross sections in Figure 5 reveals that, although the majority of the
parameter combinations successfully consolidated dense matrices with minimal pores or surface
roughness, the energy density does not necessarily affect the part density readily as it does in LPBF (Hyer
& Petrie, 2022). Surface roughness from residual partially melted particles is noticeable at the bottoms of
the cube samples from excess powder accumulation. Unfortunately, the powder accumulation is difficult
to completely eliminate, but it can clearly be minimized. The parameter set that produced Sample PC-11
was chosen as the most optimized parameter set because it yielded the best dimensional accuracy both in
height and maintaining the rectangular cross section (minimal caving).

 12

Evaluating the thermal penetration of the laser is paramount for ensuring that an embedded optical fiber is
not subjected to localized overmelting or extreme thermal shock during embedding. To observe the
microstructure and potentially the melt pool morphology, the polished cross sections of the samples from
Figure 5 were subsequently chemically etched. Figure 6 shows cross sections of the top of the sample,
which is the last layer melted and most likely to enable observation of the melt pool morphology and
geometry. Although the chemical etching successfully delineated the general microstructural evolution,
the residual of the individual melt pool boundaries is somewhat ambiguous, precluding precise
quantitative depth measurements. Nevertheless, a critical qualitative assessment was derived from the
etched geometries: when laser power and traverse speed were held constant, modulating the powder feed
rate appears to change melt pool depth (more material, less depth). This observation indicates that thermal
penetration within the substrate is governed by the laser power and scan velocity but can be changed by
adding more powder for the system to melt at a given time. Consequently, the powder mass flow rate can
be independently maximized to accelerate build height and ensure rapid, dimensionally accurate sensor
coverage without concurrently amplifying the risk of thermal degradation to the sequestered optical fiber.

3.2 EMBEDDING FIBER STRATEGY

The first attempt at embedding an optical fiber consisted of directly depositing material on the copper-
coated fiber laid on a SS304 build plate, as shown in Figure 7a. Optimized parameters from PC-11 were

 13

chosen as the depositing parameters, and 0.5 mm was deposited over the top. Figure 7b shows the
completed embedding. Light did not go through the embedded region, suggesting fiber failure somewhere
in the embedded region. Subsequently, the sample was sectioned in half, metallographically prepared, and
then etched to reveal the grain structure shown in Figure 7c. Figure 7d shows the location of the fiber,
which appears to have been engulfed by the SS316L matrix, no longer having any observed copper
coating. The direct deposition of the thin copper coating was likely insufficient to counteract the large
thermal shock of the DED process. A fiber with a thicker coating, such as what has been suggested in
other works (T. Birri et al., 2025; Hyer & Petrie, 2024; Mathew, 2024), may be more suitable for direct
depositing material over a fiber.

 Figure 7. Initial attempt at embedding an optical fiber onto the baseplate by directly depositing SS316L over
the top of the copper-coated fiber. (a) Fiber layout in the DED machine, (b) embedded region post-deposition, and
 (c,d) optical images of the cross section of the embedded fiber.

Because the margin of error for directly depositing over the copper-coated fibers is minimal, the fiber was
housed in a capillary. A 1.6 mm (1/16 in.) outer diameter (OD) SS316L smooth-bore tube with a 0.6 mm
inner diameter (ID) (i.e., 0.51 mm wall thickness) was chosen to house the fiber. The capillary was
difficult to handle by simply laying it down or applying adhesives to hold it down; residual strain causes
the capillary to warp and peel upward, resisting the strength of any adhesive used. Therefore, two
rectangular prisms were built as support structures to secure the capillary in place. Each support was
5 mm wide, with a separation distance of 1.5 mm (slightly smaller than the capillary OD), and were built
to match the length of the desired embedded region (i.e., 30 mm). After building, the capillary was tapped
into place between the support structures and a fiber fed through the capillary.

Initial activities explored sizing the height of the capillary and subsequently choosing the right powder
feed rate to perform the embedding. As shown in Figure 6, the melt pool depth appeared to decrease with
increasing powder feed rate. Figure 8a shows initial attempts to size the height of the support structures.
Support structures were sized around the OD of the capillary, as shown in Figure 8b. Building the
supports to the height of the OD appeared to be the optimal solution, but embedding with 5–10 g/min
either resulted in fiber failure or showed no sign of fiber embedding; 5 g/min appeared to be too thermally
shocking to the fiber, causing immediate fiber failure. The support structure height was sized lower and
higher than the capillary OD, all strategies suffered from similar embedding issues.

 14

 Figure 8. Summary of different embedding techniques that involve sizing support structures to hold a
capillary housing an optical fiber. (a) Different trials of 1.6 mm OD/0.6 mm ID SS316L capillary being embedded
 with different sized support structures. (b) Demonstration of the different support structure heights used in
  conjunction with the strapping method before encapsulation by a cover block. (c) Attempting to use different gas
 feed rates to vary the strapping methods. (d) Successfully embedding a fiber, red box highlighting the red light
 coming out of the end of the fiber.

To circumvent the excessive thermal shock associated with continuous direct deposition over the fixtured
tube, a segmented laser-tacking or strapping strategy was implemented. Rather than printing a continuous
solid layer along the entire sensor path, single track lines of SS316L material were deposited transversely
across the capillary at a periodic spacing of 1–3 mm across the embedment region (Figure 8b). Figure 8c
shows how the structural straps mechanically secure the capillary while establishing localized
metallurgical bonds with the underlying tracks.

The straps introduce a minor vertical height offset to the overall sample profile, but they act as localized
heat sinks, providing an effective conduction path to dissipate excessive thermal energy during
subsequent final encapsulation passes. Figure 8d shows that the segmented trapping method successfully
yielded the first intact fiber embedment with fully retained optical continuity. However, a critical
vulnerability was identified at the ingress and egress boundaries where the fiber exits the structural
embedding matrix. The packaged sensors frequently fractured at this junction during subsequent manual

 15

handling. Microstructural analysis revealed that the high-intensity laser interaction near the entrance zone
generated enough transient heat to fully vaporize the thin copper coating at the exit boundary. This
localized decohesion left the silica cladding entirely exposed, unprotected, and highly susceptible to
failure under handling-induced mechanical strain.

Melting enough of the capillary to bond the fiber appeared to have merit, but the thin copper coating on
the commercially available fused silica fiber remained a critical weak link. Consequently, a more robust
integration strategy was necessary to insulate the delicate glass core from direct thermal damage. Previous
sensor embedding protocols leveraging UAM and LPBF required stringent geometric constraints, forcing
the fiber into highly precise, preformed channels that had to remain perfectly coplanar with the 2D build
environment. DED is more versatile, allowing more physical space to work, yielding a larger margin of
error to work within.

To capitalize on this versatility, high-temperature brazing was evaluated as an alternative technique to
increase the effective cladding and buffer thickness around the core. The hypothesis was that the high
thermal energy used to fuse the structural SS316L matrix over the assembly could simultaneously be
leveraged to melt an internal braze filler alloy within a protective sheath. For this purpose, an SS316L
smooth-bore capillary tube featuring a 3.2 mm (1/8 in.) OD smooth-bore tube with a 2.2 mm inner
diameter (ID) (i.e., 0.51 mm wall thickness) was selected to house the sensor package. The remaining
internal volume of the lumen was packed with BAg-1 braze wires (0.8 mm or 1/32 in. OD); the copper-
coated optical fiber was deliberately positioned at the bottom of the capillary tube to maximize its
physical distance from the laser-interaction zone. The BAg-1 alloy has a nominal composition of
45%Ag–24%Cd–16%Zn–15%Cu (wt %). Various capillary wall dimensions were evaluated during these
trials; a nominal 0.6 mm wall thickness was selected as optimal because thicker walls tend to conduct
excessive thermal energy away from the internal zone, preventing full liquification of the braze material.

Support structures were still used to secure the larger capillary in place, following a similar procedure as
described above: 5 mm width, similar height as the OD of the capillary. Strapping was not used because
the larger capillary’s larger mass appeared to impart more rigidity compared with the 1.6 mm capillary.
The optimized PC-11 parameter set was used to deposit material at 5 g/min over the top of the secured
3.2 mm OD capillary; the fiber appeared snug on the first attempt. Figure 9(a) shows the functionality of
the first sample embedded in the 3.2 mm OD capillary filled with braze, with visible transmission of a red
laser source confirming full optical continuity after printing.

Distributed residual strain profiles within the encapsulated sensor were calculated by cross-correlating
OFDR backscatter traces acquired before and after the DED process. Figure 9b shows a residual strain
that measured roughly 300 µm/m in compression over an expected 30 mm length (i.e., target embedded
length). Although some compressive strain was observed, it was significantly lower than expected,
considering that UAM and LPBF both have achieved a compressive strain an order of magnitude higher
(~2,500 µm/m). This discrepancy indicates that the continuous powder feed likely shielded the tube,
preventing the internal core temperature from reaching the liquidus point of the BAg-1 alloy long enough
to secure a high-temperature bond.

The powder feed rate was gradually reduced in the next embedding attempt to enhance thermal
conduction to the internal braze core without modifying the optimized PC-11 laser parameters. Sweeping
over the top of the capillary with no powder (0 g/min) for four passes was enough to sufficiently melt all
the braze before going back to depositing material over the capillary and fully encapsulating it in SS316L.

Figure 9c displays the successfully embedded sample fabricated using this autogenous laser-preheat
protocol. A residual strain was calculated based on the pre- and post-embedding OFDR backscatter traces,
as shown in Figure 9d. This time, the residual strain achieved was approximately 6,000 µm/m in

 17

compression over the expected 30 mm embedding length. Notably, resolving these extreme strain levels
introduced localized measurement anomalies: apparent tensile peaks appeared at the sharp ingress and
egress boundaries of the embedded region. Although these peaks may reflect localized mechanical shear
strains at the exit boundaries, they are likely to be a counteractive tensile strain to balance the overall
strain in the system. Similar tensile peaks have been observed in previous work related to embedding with
laser welding (Petrie & Sridharan, 2020) and LPBF (H. Hyer et al., 2025).

Replicating the hybrid-braze embedding protocol demonstrated excellent process reproducibility,
consistently yielding significant residual compressive strains across subsequent fabrication trials. Figure
10 illustrates the distributed residual strain and corresponding optical signal amplitude profiles for two
additional successfully embedded specimens. As delineated in Figure 10a and 9b, residual compressive
strains on the order of 5,000 µm/m were successfully maintained across the 30 mm target embedment
zone. Although these compressive magnitudes are slightly less than the peak value achieved in the initial
preheated specimen (Figure 9c and 8d), they firmly validate the viability and repeatability of the
autogenous laser-preheat technique. This result represents a 2×–2.4× improvement over the roughly
2,500 µm/m achievable with UAM and LPBF, offsetting roughly half of the approximately 10,000 µm/m
differential strain projected for the most aggressive strain changes at high operating temperatures (up to
500°C); the balance must be accommodated by the intrinsic tensile capacity of the fiber, which motivates
the elevated-temperature validation described in Table 2.

Evaluation of the optical backscatter profiles before and after embedding reveals a distinct reduction in
signal amplitude (Figure 10c ,9d), indicating localized optical attenuation; however, the attenuation is
only observed at the entrance to the embedded region and does not continue to degrade along the length
of the embedded region. This signal loss is likely driven by microbending strains or the formation of sub-
micrometer fractures within the silica core due to the extreme thermomechanical gradients of the melt
pool. Previous work used N-doped optical fibers, rather than the Ge-doped core fibers used in this study,
during embedding in UAM, which helped reduced the microbending losses (Hyer et al., 2022). The
specimen in Figure 10d exhibits a significantly more severe drop in signal amplitude compared to the
sample in Figure 10c; the position of this drop coincides with a lower net residual compressive strain,
suggesting the losses occurring near the beginning of the embedded region. Although only two specimens
are compared here, this trend is consistent with excessive localized thermal input simultaneously
promoting internal microbending/sub-micrometer fracture and altering the local cooling rate in a way that
relaxes the attainable room-temperature compression. Confirming this relationship will require a larger
specimen set spanning a controlled range of energy inputs. Consequently, future optimization efforts must
focus on precisely balancing the laser energy input to mitigate optical attenuation and preserve signal
fidelity while consistently maximizing the residual pre-compressive strains necessary to survive advanced
reactor operational envelopes.

 5. SUMMARY

This research demonstrated the feasibility of embedding intrinsic optical fiber strain within a dense
additively manufactured structural metal matrix using BPDED. Through systematic parameter
optimization on the Insstek MX-Lab system, a stable processing window was established, demonstrating
that powder feed rates can be modulated to control build height without altering the maximum melt pool
depth. Although direct laser overprinting and thin-walled (1.6 mm OD) capillary fixturing configurations
failed due to excessive thermal shock and coating vaporization at the boundary regions, transitioning to a
larger 3.2 mm OD structural capillary package filled with BAg-1 braze wires provided the necessary
thermal insulation to shield the internal glass core. Integrating a multi-pass autogenous laser-preheat
sequence thoroughly liquefied the internal braze alloy, successfully securing high-temperature strain
coupling. This hybrid configuration consistently achieved high room-temperature residual compressive
strains of 5,000–6,000 µm/m while preserving cross-sectional density and operational signal integrity.

Based on the reproducible successes documented in this work, the following preliminary engineering
procedure has been established for embedding metal-coated optical fibers using laser-based BPDED:

 1. Substrate Track Fixturing: Additively manufacture parallel rectangular guide tracks (5.0 mm
 width, height flush with the capillary centerline) directly onto the structural baseplate using
 optimized baseline PC-11 parameters. Maintain a track separation of 3.1 mm (slightly smaller
 than the OD of capillary) to create a precise interference press-fit for the sensor package.

 2. Capillary Package Preparation: Cut a smooth-bore SS316L capillary tube (3.2 mm OD/2.2 mm
 ID) to the desired length. Clean and thread the metal-coated optical fiber through the lumen
 alongside parallel strands of 0.8 mm nominal diameter BAg-1 braze wire. Crucially, position the
 optical fiber at the absolute bottom of the tube so that the braze wires sit between the fiber and the
 oncoming laser path to act as a thermal buffer.

 3. Mechanical Assembly: Press-fit the assembled capillary package securely between the
 preprinted parallel support tracks. Ensure full contact with the baseplate to maximize structural
 stability and heat dissipation.

 20

4. Autogenous Braze Liquefication: Execute four consecutive autogenous laser passes (0 g/min
 powder flow rate) directly over the crown of the exposed capillary tube using the baseline PC-11
 parameters (240–270 W laser power, 750–950 mm/min traverse speed). This step delivers the
 exact volumetric energy density required to liquefy the internal BAg-1 filler metal without
 breaching the capillary wall.

5. Structural Encapsulation: Immediately engage the powder feed mechanism to a stabilized flow
 rate of 5.0 g/min. Execute the final continuous cladding passes over the assembly to additively
 build the capping layers, fully encapsulating the capillary package within a dense structural
 SS316L matrix.
