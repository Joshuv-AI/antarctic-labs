Clinical efficacy of de-epithelialized flap for reconstruction of pressure ulcers in the buttocks and trochanteric regions
Authors: Ke Liu, Xuejiao Li, Song Zeng, Weihong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the clinical efficacy of the De-Epithelialized Flap technique in the reconstruction of pressure ulcers in the buttocks and trochanteric regions.MethodsA total of 29 patients with pressure ulcers in the buttocks and trochanteric regions were treated from January 2018 to November 2021, including 16 cases in the sacrococcygeal region, 4 in the intertrochanteric region, and 9 in the ischial tuberosity. Early debridement and drainage were performed to remove necrotic tissue and sinus tracts, followed by repair of the pressure ulcers using the De-Epithelialized Flap. The flap’s length in the advancement direction should measure approximately 1.5–2 times the diameter of the defect, with the width slightly exceeding that of the wound. Excess tissue at the recipient site is de-epithelialized and used to fill the dead space.ResultsAmong the patients, 27 cases achieved primary healing of the skin flaps, while 2 cases experienced delayed wound healing. These wounds were successfully managed with dressing changes and debridement. During a follow-up period of 6–12 months, only 1 case of recurrence at the same site was observed.ConclusionThe use of the De-Epithelialized Flap for the repair of pressure ulcers in the buttocks and trochanteric regions offers the benefits of being a simple, safe, and effective procedure with a low recurrence rate. This technique demonstrates promising clinical value and is recommended for broader clinical application.

Pressure ulcers and their management remain one of the most challenging clinical problems, particularly among elderly patients with neurological impairment, long-term hospitalization, or spinal cord injury. They impose a significant burden on patients, families, society, and healthcare institutions [1]. For stage I and II pressure ulcers, non-surgical treatment is generally sufficient to achieve healing, whereas stage III and IV ulcers typically require surgical intervention. These ulcers frequently develop in the periacetabular region, including the sacrococcygeal area, ischial tuberosities, and greater trochanteric region. Conventional methods such as direct suturing or skin grafting for stage III and IV pressure ulcers are associated with high recurrence rates and complications. In recent years, our department has employed De-Epithelialized Flaps to treat 29 cases of pressure ulcers in the buttocks and trochanteric regions, achieving favorable outcomes and prognoses. The following summarizes our experience.

Clinical data

General data

From January 2018 to November 2021, a total of 27 patients in our department underwent 29 De-Epithelialized V-Y advancement flap procedures for the treatment of pressure ulcers in the buttocks and trochanteric regions. In addition to the De-Epithelialized V-Y advancement flap operations, we administered the patiens standard wound care (disinfection, debridement, and ordinary gauze dressings) during the perioperative period. Stage I and II pressure ulcers in the buttocks and trochanteric regions or pressure ulcers in other areas were excluded. The patients were aged between 29 and 85 years, with a mean age of 62.38 years. Of these, 17 cases were associated with spinal cord injury, 5 with sequelae of cerebral infarction, 2 with craniocerebral injury, and 3 with prolonged bed rest due to severe illness despite being ambulatory. Pressure ulcer locations included 16 in the sacrococcygeal region, 4 in the greater trochanteric region, and 9 in the ischial tuberosity region. One patient required bilateral V-Y advancement flaps to close the defect, while another underwent simultaneous sacrococcygeal and trochanteric flap repairs. The wound areas (horizontal × vertical) ranged from 5 cm × 5 cm to 9 cm × 8 cm, with all defects classified as stage IV pressure ulcers extending into the muscle layer or bone. The flap sizes varied from 8 cm × 6 cm to 16 cm × 12 cm (patient datas are summarized in Table 1). Clinical outcomes were the flap survival rates, pressure ulcers recurrence rates, wound healing time, and patient functional outcomes. Pressure ulcer recurrence was defined as the recurrence of pressure ulcers at the same site. The main complications were poor healing of flap wounds including fat liquefaction and local wound dehiscence and deep infections.

Operative method

Preoperative preparation

Prior to surgery, patients should undergo comprehensive blood biochemical testing and cardiopulmonary function assessments to evaluate surgical risk. When necessary, pelvic CT or MRI should be performed to identify possible osteomyelitis, while wound secretions should be subjected to bacterial culture and drug sensitivity testing.If there is a significant amount of wound secretion, antibiotics (usually ceftriaxone sodium, piperacillin tazobactam, levofloxacin, etc.) should be used based on the secretion culture results until the secretion is significantly reduced. Patients with anemia, hypoproteinemia, or electrolyte imbalances must receive appropriate correction before surgery, which is one of the keypoints for the healing of postoperative skin flaps. The wound should be expanded and drained, followed by continuous negative pressure sealing drainage. Once necrotic tissue is essentially absent and healthy granulation tissue becomes visible, flap transfer surgery can be considered. Additional preparations include preoperative fasting, intestinal cleansing, and postural training. Doppler ultrasound should be performed to locate the perforating artery at the surgical site, and its position should be marked prior to surgery.

Surgical procedure

The surgical position is determined by the location of the wound. After standard disinfection and draping, the wound is thoroughly irrigated with hydrogen peroxide, diluted iodophor, and normal saline. The wound surface is then fully stained with methylene blue. A solution of adrenaline saline (1:100,000) is injected around the pre-excision wound margin until blanching of the skin is observed. During debridement, all blue-stained areas must be meticulously removed until healthy tissue is reached.Three of the patients had excessive wound discharge, and the MR examination indicated osteomyelitis.We performed osteotomy or partial smoothing at bony prominences. After debridement, the wound is rinsed again and all surgical instruments are replaced. Flap design is guided by local vascular anatomy: the superior or inferior gluteal artery perforators in the sacrococcygeal region, the ascending branch of the lateral femoral artery in the greater trochanteric region, and the first perforating branch of the deep femoral artery in the ischial tuberosity region. The flap’s long axis should align as closely as possible with the short axis of the wound. Its length in the advancement direction should measure approximately 1.5–2 times the diameter of the defect, with the width slightly exceeding that of the wound. When elevating the flap, perforating vessels must remain intact, and surrounding tissues should be adequately loosened. Excess tissue at the recipient site is de-epithelialized and used to fill the dead space (The diagrammatic sketch are presented in Figs. 1 and 2). The donor site is sutured first to reduce tension between the flap and defect. Negative pressure drainage tubes should be placed beneath both the flap and the donor site.

Postoperative treatment

(①) After surgery, patients should remain in the prone or lateral position to avoid direct pressure or shear stress on the flap; (②) Appropriate antibiotics should be administered for infection prevention and treatment, with patients suffering from osteomyelitis requiring extended therapy for up to six weeks; (③) Patients should be repositioned regularly to prevent new pressure ulcers. When turning, trained personnel should lift and reposition the hips carefully to avoid friction and tension on the skin flap.

Results

All 27 skin flap incisions achieved primary healing (89.7%), while 2 incisions (6.9%) showed delayed local healing, including fat liquefaction and local wound dehiscence, which resolved after debridement, resuturing, and dressing changes within a month. All patients have unrestricted functional recovery after flap surgery, except for paraplegic patients who cannot be examined. During a follow-up period of 6–20 months (average, 11.07 ± 3.97 months), only 1 case (3.4%) of recurrence at the original site was observed.

Case report

Ye, an 80-year-old female, developed a 7 cm × 5 cm large trochanteric pressure ulcer following prolonged bed rest due to Alzheimer’s disease. After thorough debridement and flattening of the bony hyperplasia, the wound measured 8 cm × 7 cm. A V-Y advancement flap, measuring 15 cm × 10 cm and pedicled on the deep branch of the right lateral femoral artery, was designed. The triangular portion of the recipient site was de-epithelialized and used to fill the cavity(indicated by the yellow arrow in Fig. 3(c)). The flap was tension-free sutured to completely cover the defect. The flap survived without complications, and the postoperative course was uneventful. Discussion

For patients with limited mobility, pressure ulcers most commonly develop in the buttocks and trochanteric regions [2], particularly over bony prominences such as the sacrum, ischium, and greater trochanter. In stages I and II, optimal non-surgical management and correction of local or systemic factors that hinder wound healing can often lead to natural recovery. However, once ulcers progress to stages III or IV, debridement and flap repair are generally required [3]. Flaps not only provide well-vascularized tissue but also allow large segments to fill dead spaces and redistribute pressure effectively. To date, no literature has demonstrated clear superiority of one flap repair technique over another. In our practice, we have adopted the De-Epithelialized Flap, which offers several advantages: (1) the use of perforating vessels and their continuous vascular network ensures reliable blood supply; (2) the de-epithelialized tissue fills dead space, eliminates ineffective cavities, preserves muscle tissue, and maintains muscle function; (3) previously used flaps can often be reused in cases of recurrence, as the perforating vessel anatomy remains largely intact; and (4) the technique is relatively simple, easy to master, requires no exposure of perforating vessels, shortens operative time, and reduces surgical difficulty. The only requirement is that the perforating point be included within the flap design. Once elevated, surrounding subcutaneous tissue is carefully dissected, and circulation of the flap depends primarily on the dermal vascular network, with the perforating arteries preserved to maintain blood flow.

Our purpose in reporting this series of cases is to share our experience in repairing pressure ulcers in the buttocks and trochanteric regions using De-Epithelialized V-Y advancement flaps and to analyze the outcomes in terms of recurrence rates and complications. A total of 27 patients with stage IV pressure ulcers in the buttocks and trochanteric regions were included in this study. The sacral region showed the highest incidence of ulcers (55%), which aligns with previous reports [4, 5]. This may be explained by the high frequency of supine positioning among individuals who lack awareness of preventive measures. In contrast, Yankov et al. [2] reported that pressure ulcers were most common in the ischium (63.3%) and sacral region (36.4%), a difference likely related to the high prevalence of wheelchair use among their patient cohort. Pressure ulcers remain a frequent concern among long-term hospitalized patients, the elderly, and those with spinal cord injuries. In our group, traumatic spinal cord injury was the leading cause, accounting for 65.5% of cases. Byrne and Salzberg [6] similarly observed that 70% of patients with spinal cord injury develop pressure ulcers at multiple sites, and up to 85% experience at least one ulcer during their lifetime.

Bamba R et al. [7] reviewed 276 cases of pressure ulcer flap reconstruction over an 18-year period and reported a total complication rate of 58.7% (162 cases). The most frequent complication was incision dehiscence (31.2%), while the highest recurrence rate reached 28.6%. Their analysis also indicated that patients with comorbidities had significantly lower prealbumin levels compared to those without comorbidities. In our study, the overall complication rate was 10.3%, including two cases of early postoperative complications, two cases of incision dehiscence (6.9%), and one case of late recurrence at the same site (3.4%). Based on our experience, we emphasize the importance of correcting hypoproteinemia (> 30 g/L), anemia (> 90 g/L), and electrolyte imbalances prior to surgery. Equally critical is adequate wound bed preparation, with thorough debridement and drainage to eliminate necrotic or infected tissue. Only after both systemic and local conditions are optimized should flap transfer surgery be performed, which significantly increases the likelihood of surgical success.

When the lesion of a pressure ulcer is completely excised, residual gaps and dead spaces remain along the wound margins, requiring adequate tissue filling and coverage. In our technique, the skin flap at the recipient site is de-epithelialized and embedded into these gaps or dead spaces, followed by suturing and fixation. Tuncbilek G et al. [8] emphasized that the main advantage of this approach lies in using healthy tissue to obliterate dead spaces at the wound edge or beneath the contralateral skin flap, without sacrificing excess tissue or muscle, thereby preserving both structure and function. This method adheres to the principles of flap economy: minimizing donor site damage while maximizing recipient site function. As autologous tissue is a limited resource, it must be utilized to its fullest potential during transplantation [9]. This technique not only repairs skin defects but also effectively eliminates deep dead spaces. By filling these spaces with de-epithelialized tissue, the superficial suture line is separated from the deep bone, thereby preventing deep tissue dehiscence and infection. Notably, in our cohort, no complications such as deep infections or tissue rupture occurred. Ramy S et al. [10] further reported that buried de-epithelialized flaps reduce the risk of skin complications by filling dead space, improving lymphatic drainage, and covering vital structures. Similarly, Kim H et al. [11] recommended the Keystone-designed buried de-epithelialized flap for obliterating small- to moderate-sized surgical dead spaces, highlighting both its functional and cosmetic benefits.

In this group of cases, flap tissue containing the perforating artery and its continuous vascular network was employed. This provided a reliable blood supply, sufficient volume to fill dead spaces, and favorable functional and aesthetic outcomes, while also ensuring complete wound closure. The donor site demonstrated a low complication rate. The use of muscle flaps remains controversial, as preserving muscle is functionally advantageous. Moreover, muscle tissue has poor ischemic tolerance, and because pressure-bearing sites are primarily protected by skin and subcutaneous tissue, muscle coverage confers little additional benefit. Recent studies [12, 13] have shown that non-muscle flaps achieve long-term outcomes comparable to muscle flaps in pressure ulcer reconstruction. In our experience, the V-Y advancement flap based on the perforating branches of the gluteal artery effectively covered pressure ulcers in the buttocks and trochanteric regions without the need to sacrifice the gluteus maximus muscle. These flaps can be directly advanced, demonstrate strong resistance to compression, and ensure long-term viability. For example, a 13 cm sacral defect was successfully repaired with bilateral buttock V-Y flaps, while smaller sacrococcygeal defects were managed with unilateral flaps. Ohjimi et al. [14] reported that the maximum defect closed with bilateral gluteal fascia flaps ranged from 15 to 21 cm, whereas unilateral gluteal fascia V-Y advancement flaps covered defects up to 10–11 cm. In another study, Wong et al. [15] recommended buttock rotational flaps without perforator branches for pressure ulcer repair, citing the advantage of flap reuse in cases of recurrence as well as improved vascular supply. Likewise, Chu G.P. et al. [16] applied partially de-epithelialized posterior femoral bilobed flaps to repair 15 cases of ischial tuberosity pressure ulcers, achieving satisfactory outcomes even in recurrent and refractory cases.

The De-Epithelialized flap has limitations. Firstly, the abrasion resistance of the De-Epithelialized flap is slightly poor, and long-term compression or friction increases the risk of pressure ulcer recurrence. Secondly, due to limitations in flap design and donor site, it is difficult to cover large or irregular wounds with De-Epithelialized flap. For larger defects, single-flap coverage may be insufficient, making bilateral advancement or propeller flaps necessary. In addition, our sample size is too small, and the complications of the flap surgery need further observation and research.

Conclusion

The treatment and prevention of pressure ulcers rely not only on the choice of flap reconstruction but also on patient education and adherence to preventive measures. The De-Epithelialized flap offers reliable and durable soft tissue coverage for pressure ulcers in the buttocks and trochanteric regions, with a low complication rate, minimal recurrence, and the added advantage of reuse. Given its simplicity, safety, and effectiveness, this technique is worthy of broader clinical adoption and promotion.
