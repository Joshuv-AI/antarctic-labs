The HIF-1α–Th17/Treg axis in pediatric infectious mononucleosis: a clinical investigation of metabolic-immune dysregulation
Authors: Pengli Yang, Huiping Ni, Lingfang Du, Meiling Wang, Gaofeng Pang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric infectious mononucleosis (IM) is a systemic inflammatory syndrome typically triggered by primary Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infection. The disease is characterized by a profound disruption of immune homeostasis, which can be characterized by the balance between pro-inflammatory Th17 cells and suppressive Treg cells. While hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) is known to direct T-cell behavior in various inflammatory conditions, its specific role in the immunopathology of IM remains largely uncharacterized. This study aims to investigate the systemic expression of HIF-1α in pediatric IM and evaluate its correlation with the Th17/Treg homeostatic axis.MethodsIn this prospective case-control study, we enrolled pediatric patients with acute IM and healthy, age- and sex-frequency-matched controls. Peripheral blood CD4+ T-cell subsets (Th17: CD3+CD4+IL-17A+; Treg: CD3+CD4+CD25+CD127low) were quantified via multi-color flow cytometry. Systemic levels of HIF-1α, IL-17A, and TGF-β1 were determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The relationship between metabolic stabilization and immune phenotype was evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis.ResultsThe final analysis included 40 IM patients and 37 healthy controls. Compared to the controls, IM patients exhibited a significant systemic accumulation of HIF-1α (p < 0.001) and IL-17A concentrations (p = 0.008), alongside a marked expansion of Th17 cells (p < 0.001) and a significant increase in the Th17/Treg ratio (p < 0.001). Conversely, the IM cohort experienced significantly depletions in Treg frequencies (p < 0.001) and TGF-β1 levels (p = 0.005). Correlation analysis revealed that serum HIF-1α was correlated significantly positively with Th17 frequency, the Th17/Treg ratio, and IL-17A levels and significantly negatively with Treg frequency and TGF-β1 levels.ConclusionsOur findings reveal a significant systemic stabilization of HIF-1α in pediatric IM patients, which reflects an immunometabolic signature associated with the acute viral response. The significant correlation between HIF-1α and the shift in T-cell subsets suggests that HIF-1α may be seen as systemic sensor of disease severity in EBV infection. The results provide a theoretical foundation for targeting metabolic pathways to restore immune homeostasis in hyper-inflammatory viral diseases.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13861-y.

Introduction

The Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), a ubiquitous gamma‑herpesvirus infecting 90%–95% of the global population, establishes a lifelong latent infection primarily within B lymphocytes [1]. It is readily transmitted through oral secretions and typically infects individuals during childhood; consequently, primary infection in adults is relatively rare due to high seroprevalence established early in life [2]. The clinical presentation of the virus is highly age-dependent [3]. In infants, primary infection tends to be asymptomatic or characterized by mild, non-specific symptoms [4]. In contrast, in school-aged children and adolescents, EBV infection frequently leads to infectious mononucleosis (IM), a systemic syndrome characterized by fever, pharyngitis, lymphadenopathy, and hepatosplenomegaly [5]. Although often self-limiting, IM triggers a robust immune response that can be consequential [6, 7]. An aberrant host response or inefficient viral clearance can cause severe acute complications such as splenic rupture [8] or upper airway obstruction [9] and is also associated with long‑term risks including chronic active EBV infection (CAEBV) [10], hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) [11], and EBV‑related lymphoid malignancies [12, 13].

At the molecular level, EBV survival and pathogenesis depend on its ability to reprogram host cellular metabolism and signaling [14]. A key mediator in this process is hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), a transcription factor related to cellular adaptation to low oxygen [15]. During EBV infection, the virus causes HIF-1α accumulation through both induced synthesis and inhibited degradation. On the one hand, the viral latent membrane protein 1 (LMP1) promotes HIF-1α synthesis by activating the MAPK pathway and increasing the levels of reactive oxygen species [16]. On the other hand, EBV nuclear antigens EBNA-3 and EBNA-5 stabilize existing HIF-1α by inhibiting prolyl hydroxylases 1 and 2, thus preventing its proteasomal destruction [17, 18]. In this way, the virus actively subverts hypoxic pathways even under normoxic conditions—a state of HIF-1α stabilization termed “pseudohypoxia” [19]. The sustained HIF-1α activity forces a metabolic shift toward aerobic glycolysis, characterized by increased glucose uptake and lactate production [20]. This metabolic hijacking not only supports viral replication but also reshapes the host’s immune environment.

The most critical consequence of this metabolic reprogramming is the disruption of systemic immune homeostasis [16]. In clinical immunology, the balance between T helper 17 (Th17) cells and regulatory T (Treg) cells serves as a primary proxy for immune stability [21]. Th17 cells drive pro-inflammatory responses and pathogen clearance through the secretion of interleukin (IL)-17A, and Treg cells, characterized by the expression of Foxp3 and TGF-β1, maintain self-tolerance and prevent collateral tissue damage [22, 23]. A shift in the “Th17/Treg axis” is a hallmark of immune failure: an overactive Th17 response facilitates systemic inflammation, while a diminished Treg population allows for uncontrolled lymphoproliferation [24]. HIF-1α acts as a metabolic switch for this axis, directly activating the Th17-specific transcription factor RORγt while simultaneously targeting Foxp3 for proteasomal degradation [25].

The regulatory influence of the HIF-1α/T-cell axis has been implicated in a broad spectrum of human diseases [26]. For example, in rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus, HIF-1α-mediated metabolic shifts drive the expansion of pathogenic Th17 cells, thus exacerbating tissue destruction [27, 28]. In the context of viral infections such as HIV-1, HIF-1α stabilization has been shown to impair Treg function, which contributes to persistent immune activation as well as disease progression [29]. Within the tumor microenvironment, HIF-1α modifies the Th17/Treg axis to suppress anti-tumor immunity and promote an environment conducive to malignant growth [30]. However, the HIF-1α–Th17/Treg immunometabolism remains poorly characterized in IM; to date, no robust studies have systematically mapped HIF-1α expression and Th17/Treg ratios across the course of acute EBV-IM. Standard diagnostic tools, such as EBV-DNA viral load and serology, are essential for confirming infection but are poor predictors of an individual’s immunological trajectory or the severity of the inflammatory “storm.” [31] There is a significant knowledge gap regarding whether peripheral HIF-1α levels accurately reflect the cellular Th17/Treg imbalance observed in IM patients [32].

Therefore, this study aims to validate the clinical association between HIF-1α expression and Th17/Treg dysregulation in a hospitalized pediatric IM cohort. By correlating serum HIF-1α levels with cellular subsets and their effector cytokines (IL-17A and TGF-β1), we sought to determine if HIF-1α is a nuanced marker of the metabolic-immune axis in the immune pathogenesis of EBV infection suitable for assessing host immune status than traditional virological assays.

Materials and methods

Subjects and study design

This prospective case-control study was conducted at the Department of Pediatrics, Third Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (Changzhou First People’s Hospital). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board [Approval No.: 2024 (Education) CL024] and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants.

Patient recruitment and eligibility

Selection of IM patients

All pediatric patients hospitalized between June 1, 2024, and November 30, 2025 with clinical symptoms suggestive of IM were screened for eligibility. Diagnosis was established based on the clinical and laboratory criteria outlined in Practical Pediatrics (9th edition) [33], including fever, pharyngitis, lymphadenopathy, and laboratory confirmation of EBV infection (positive EBV-VCA-IgM or high EBV-DNA viral load). The inclusion criteria were: (1) clinical and laboratory confirmation of primary acute EBV infection, and (2) age between 3 and 14 years. The enrolled age range reflects the local clinical peak of pediatric IM cases requiring hospitalization in our center and does not necessarily match the peak incidence in other geographic regions.

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria:Co-infection (e.g., hepatitis B/C, HIV, or other systemic viral/bacterial infections);Pre-existing hepatic or renal dysfunction unrelated to IM;History of autoimmune diseases, primary immunodeficiency, or malignancies;Treatment with corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, or systemic antiviral therapy within four weeks prior to admission;History of chronic allergies or asthma.

Selection of healthy controls

A pool of healthy children undergoing routine physical examinations during the same study period was screened for potential inclusion. To ensure demographic comparability and minimize selection bias, a prospective frequency-matching strategy was employed. Controls were screened consecutively and selected to align with the age and sex distribution of the IM cohort. In addition to the age requirement (3–14 years), potential controls were excluded if they had a history of acute illness within the preceding two weeks, previous EBV infection (determined by serology), or a family history of genetic immune disorders.

Sample collection and processing

Fasting peripheral venous blood was collected from all subjects, upon admission for the IM patients and during physical examinations for the healthy controls. For cellular analysis, blood (1 mL) was collected into a sodium heparin anticoagulant tube and processed within 4 hours. For serum analysis, blood (5 mL) was collected into a separate pro-coagulation tube, allowed to clot at room temperature (30–60 minutes), and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 minutes. The resulting serum was aliquoted and stored at − 20 °C until analysis.

Flow cytometric protocols

All clinical samples were processed and stained immediately upon receipt. Data were analyzed using FlowJo 10.8.1 (Waters; Ashland, OR, USA). Samples were analyzed using a CytoFLEX SRT benchtop flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter; Brea, CA, USA) with the standard V-B-Y-R (405/488/561/638 nm) laser configuration. The median number of acquired events per sample was 80,000 [IQR: 50,000–100,000]. While acquisition typically occurred immediately, some samples were batch-analyzed within 48 hours (stored at 4 °C in the dark after staining). Figure S1 shows the detailed gating strategies.

To ensure data integrity, the instrument was calibrated daily using standard QC beads. A primary gate was set on the lymphocyte population using forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) to exclude cell debris, doublets, and pyknotic dead cells. Cell doublets were further eliminated by FSC-A versus FSC-H gating. Within the viable lymphocyte gate, CD3+CD4+ T cells were identified as the parent population. Treg cells were defined as the CD3+CD4+CD25+CD127low population within the lymphocyte gate. Th17 cells were defined as the CD3+CD4+IL-17A+ population. The detailed gating strategy is illustrated in supplementary information. All markers were calculated as a percentage of total CD4+ T cells. Compensation matrices were calculated using single-stained PBMC controls to correct for spectral overlap.

To accurately reflect the in vivo immunological status of the patients, T-cell subset frequencies were analyzed directly from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) without exogenous stimulation (e.g., PMA/Ionomycin). This approach allowed for the assessment of the basal phenotypic distribution of Th17 and Treg-like cells as they existed within the systemic inflammatory environment of acute EBV infection.

Surface staining for Treg identification

Anticoagulated whole blood (100 µL) was incubated at 4 °C in the dark for 30 minutes with the following antibodies simultaneously: FITC-conjugated anti-human CD3 (clone: OKT3; 11–0037-42, eBioscience; San Diego, CA, USA), Super Bright 645-conjugated anti-human CD4 (clone: RPA-T4; 67–0049-42, eBioscience), PE-Cy7-conjugated anti-human CD25 (clone: BC96; 25–0259-42, eBioscience), and PE-conjugated anti-human CD127 (clone: eBioRDR5; 12–1278-42, eBioscience). Afterwards, red blood cells were removed using 1× lysis buffer (eBioscience) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (incubation at room temperature in the dark for 10 minutes). The stained cells were then collected by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS, fixed uniformly using a commercial fixation buffer (eBioscience), and resuspended in PBS (300 µL). All flow cytometric acquisitions were completed within 24 hours post-staining to ensure signal stability and data reliability.

Intracellular staining for Th17 detection

Anticoagulated whole blood (100 µL) was incubated with FITC-conjugated anti-human CD3 and Super Bright 645-conjugated anti-human CD4 antibodies for 30 minutes at 4 °C in the dark. The stained cells were collected using the same protocol described above, fixed and permeabilized using a buffer kit (00–5523-00, eBioscience) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The collected cells were finally stained with APC-conjugated anti-human IL-17A antibody (clone: eBio64DEC17; 17–7179-42, eBioscience) at 4 °C in the dark for 30 minutes, washed again with permeabilization buffer, and resuspended in PBS.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum levels of HIF-1α, IL-17A, and TGF-β1 were measured using commercially available ELISA kits (Multi Sciences; Hangzhou, China). To eliminate inter-batch variation, all samples for a specific analyte were assayed on the same 96-well plate. The reaction was stopped using a sulfuric acid stop solution, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices; San Jose, CA, USA). Each sample was measured in triplicate, and the mean absorbance was used for calculation.

To measure HIF-1α and IL-17A, serum samples were directly diluted (100-fold for HIF-1α and 500-fold for IL-17A) using the provided sample diluent prior to detection to ensure readings fell within the linear range of the standard curves. For the measurement of TGF-β1, acid activation was performed as follows. The serum (40 µL) was mixed with 1 N HCl (20 µL) and incubated at room temperature for 10 minutes, then neutralized with 1 N NaOH (20 µL). The activated samples were diluted 100-fold before analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R 4.5.3. Categorical data are presented as count (ratio) and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous data were first assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality; normally distributed data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using Student’s t-test or Welch’s t-test depending on the homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test). Non-normally distributed data are expressed as median [P25, P75] and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Differences were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was determined for all correlation analyses. A post-hoc power analysis (α = 0.05) was conducted to evaluate the strength of the observed correlations.

To identify independent predictors of systemic HIF-1α levels, we employed a two-stage regression strategy. Initially, univariate linear regression was performed for all demographic and clinical parameters. Variables demonstrating univariate significance (p < 0.10) were screened for multicollinearity and biological redundancy. For redundant parameters (e.g., ALT vs. AST, or individual cytokine levels vs. lineage ratios), the variable with the highest clinical relevance and strongest univariate association was prioritized. This “purposeful selection” protocol prioritizing predefined clinical and immunological adjustors helped avoid overfitting and ensure model robustness. Listwise deletion was employed for variables not applicable to the healthy control group.

In contrast, clinical and laboratory parameters revealed profound differences between the cohorts. The IM group exhibited a classic viral footprint in the complete blood count, characterized by significantly higher absolute lymphocyte counts ([7.55 ± 3.09] × 109/L vs. [5.54 ± 1.27] × 109/L, p < 0.001) and a lower platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR, p = 0.012). Biochemical analysis further confirmed systemic involvement in the IM patients, with significant elevations in alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels (all p < 0.001). Coagulation profiles also indicated a state of acute phase response, with significantly prolonged prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) in the IM group (p < 0.001).

Most notably, standard T-cell subset analysis demonstrated a dramatic decrease of the CD4+/CD8+ ratio in IM patients (0.28 vs. 1.05, p < 0.001). This was driven by both the expansion of the CD8+ population (60.05% ± 10.09% vs. 31.38% ± 8.12%) and the contraction of the CD4+ frequency (16.93% vs 31.20%). These systemic clinical findings provide a robust pathological context for the subsequent analysis of the HIF-1α–Th17/Treg axis.

Disruption of the Th17/Treg homeostatic axis and changes in cytokines

Flow cytometric analysis revealed a profound distortion in the T-cell compartments of IM patients. The Th17 frequency (CD3+CD4+IL-17A+) was markedly expanded in the IM group compared to controls (4.71% vs. 1.73%, p < 0.001; Fig. 2A), which reflects evident pro-inflammatory polarization. Conversely, the Treg fraction (CD4+CD25+CD127low) was significantly depleted in the IM cohort (6.62% vs. 8.23%, p < 0.001; Fig. 2B). As a result, the Th17/Treg ratio was significantly higher in the IM group compared to the HC group (0.63 vs. 0.24, p < 0.001; Fig. 2C). The results suggest a systemic failure of homeostatic regulation during the acute phase of EBV infection. In the univariate models, the Th17/Treg ratio (β = 0.677, viral load (LogEBV-DNA, β = 0.185), and AST (β = 0.020) were identified as powerful predictors of serum HIF-1α levels (all p < 0.001). Other markers of systemic inflammation and tissue damage, including NLR, LDH, and IL-17A, also showed significant univariate associations (all p < 0.001).

To address potential confounding and determine independent predictive power, a multivariate model was constructed adjusting for age, sex, and group status. The model demonstrated a high goodness-of-fit (adjusted R2 = 0.621). In this adjusted model, LogEBV-DNA (p = 0.003) and AST (p < 0.001) remained highly significant independent predictors of HIF-1α, but the Th17/Treg ratio lost its predictive power (p = 0.479). Hence, the HIF-1α–Th17/Treg axis must be closely integrated with, and potentially driven by, the absolute viral burden and the degree of hepatic inflammatory injury during the acute phase of IM.

Discussion

The clinical course of IM is notoriously heterogeneous, as it ranges from a self-limiting febrile illness to life-threatening hyper-inflammatory syndromes [34]. Moving beyond the traditional perspective of descriptive high and low cytokine levels, our study demonstrates that this clinical variability is paralleled by a profound immunometabolic shift: the systemic elevation of HIF-1α and a concomitant collapse of the Th17/Treg homeostatic axis. These findings suggest that EBV infection may be associated with alterations in the host’s metabolic sensing machinery, specifically the HIF-1α pathway, which may participate in creating a pro-inflammatory environment that favors viral persistence while compromising immune tolerance.

Systemic pseudohypoxia as a viral footprint

The stabilization of HIF-1α is classically understood as a cellular response to oxygen deprivation. However, the detection of significantly elevated serum HIF-1α in our normoxic pediatric cohort suggests a state of pseudohypoxia [35]. Within the unique context of EBV, this is likely driven by LMP1, which has been shown in molecular models to activate the p42/p44 MAPK pathway, thereby increasing the synthesis and stability of the HIF-1α protein even in normoxic environments [36, 37].

Our findings establish a bridge between these ex vivo results and the systemic manifestations of IM. The significant positive correlation observed between serum HIF-1α and IL-17A (r = 0.668, p < 0.001) aligns with established models of metabolic-immune regulation. HIF-1α has been shown to directly promote Th17 differentiation by activating RORγt and enhancing glycolytic activity [38, 39]. In the context of acute IM, the EBV-induced pseudohypoxic state may provide the metabolic cues necessary to drive this Th17-skewed response [40]. However, whether this represents a direct causal link or a shared response to systemic viral burden remains a hypothesis for future functional investigation

While traditionally analyzed as an intracellular transcription factor, HIF-1α has been detected in serum in studying various inflammatory and ischemic conditions [41, 42]. In the context of acute IM, serum HIF-1α likely represents a metabolic leakage marker, reflecting the aggregate intracellular stabilization of HIF-1α across a massive pool of hyper-metabolic infected and activated cells. Although serum levels provide a systemic rather than a cell-specific readout, the robust correlations found with Th17 polarization suggest that circulating HIF-1α is still a valid surrogate for the host’s total immunometabolic burden.

The metabolic polarization of the Th17/Treg axis

The most significant finding of this study is the positive relationship between HIF-1α and the Th17/Treg ratio. In clinical immunology, the Th17/Treg balance is a key factor determining the direction of inflammatory resolution. Our results indicate that during acute IM, this balance is not merely tilted but may have undergone deep metabolic reprogramming.

It appears that HIF-1α is tightly related to Th17 differentiation and the suppression of the Treg function, which may take place via RORγt induction and targeted Foxp3 proteolysis [43]. This explains the cellular phenotype observed in our IM cohort: the dramatic expansion of CD3+CD4+IL-17A+ cells and the significant depletion of the CD4+CD25+CD127low population. In the pediatric host, where the immune system is highly plastic, this metabolic enforcement of a pro-inflammatory state may explain why IM symptoms are so systemic and severe; the “metabolic brakes” (Tregs) may be weakened at the same time the “inflammatory engine” (Th17) is being fueled by viral pseudohypoxia.

Clinical implications: beyond simple inflammation

Our data suggests that the HIF-1α–Th17/Treg axis may play a role in the immunopathogenesis of IM. The significant elevation of IL-17A and suppression of TGF-β1 are not just markers of a cytokine storm but may also reflect the functional consequences of metabolic reprogramming [44, 45]. While IL-17A drives the recruitment of neutrophils and the acute febrile response, the loss of TGF-β1-mediated suppression allows for unchecked lymphoproliferation [22]. This specific immuno-metabolic pattern has been associated in previous literature with the pathogenesis of CAEBV and HLH, where the lack of immune resolution leads to multi-organ failure [25]. However, the potential value of this axis as an early predictive marker for severe pediatric IM complications requires further research and validation.

Limitations and future directions

While our findings provide a novel perspective on the immunometabolic landscape of pediatric IM, several limitations must be acknowledged.

Study design and causality

The primary constraint of this investigation is its observational, cross-sectional design. Because samples were collected only during the peak acute phase of hospitalization, we cannot definitively establish a temporal sequence or causal relationship between HIF-1α accumulation and T-cell imbalance. It remains theoretically possible that HIF-1α elevation reflects a secondary consequence of the systemic inflammatory milieu rather than its primary driver. However, the biological consistency of our results, specifically the simultaneous positive correlation with Th17 markers and negative correlation with Treg subsets, parallels established in vitro models of the HIF-1α/RORγt/Foxp3 metabolic switch [38, 39]. To address this “snapshot” limitation, our center has initiated a longitudinal follow-up study to delineate the recovery kinetics of the HIF-1α axis and its role in preventing or facilitating long-term post-viral fatigue.

Phenotypic and methodological constraints

Second, our characterization of the regulatory compartment was based on the CD3+CD4+CD25+CD127low surface phenotype. Although this is a widely adopted surrogate in clinical flow cytometry, the absence of intracellular Foxp3 gating and functional suppression assays necessitates a hypothesis-driven interpretation of the suppressive arm. This is particularly relevant as our post-hoc power analysis revealed that the power for the Treg–HIF-1α correlation was more modest (68.6%), although the study was robustly powered (>98%) to detect changes in the Th17 axis.

Furthermore, our reliance on serum HIF-1α as a systemic proxy does not allow for anatomical localization of metabolic stress. Circulating HIF-1α likely represents a composite signal derived from both EBV-infected B-cells undergoing metabolic reprogramming and responding T-lymphocytes. Future investigations utilizing single-cell transcriptomics or metabolic flux analysis are required to map the precise cellular origins of this metabolic signal.

Generalizability and scope

Finally, the generalizability of our findings is constrained by regional epidemiology and cohort characteristics. The age range of 3–14 years reflects the peak incidence of primary EBV infection in our specific geographic region; our results do not apply to infants or adults, nor to children in regions like sub-Saharan Africa where infection typically occurs much earlier. Additionally, while cohorts were balanced via prospective frequency-matching, we cannot rule out residual confounding from individual genetic predispositions or undetected sub-clinical co-infections. These variables would be best addressed in future, larger-scale multicenter investigations that use propensity score matching to further isolate the impact of the viral-metabolic axis.

Conclusions

In summary, this study finds that the HIF-1α–Th17/Treg axis is associated with the immunopathology of pediatric IM. The systemic stabilization of HIF-1α observed in IM patients parallels the characteristic inflammatory imbalance of acute EBV infection. These findings suggest that HIF-1α, as a surrogate marker of the metabolic-immune axis, warrants further studies that evaluate its value as a biomarker for identifying children at high risk for hyper-inflammatory complications. Based on the significant metabolic-immune associations reported here, our group has initiated a longitudinal follow-up study to track these parameters into the recovery phase. This ongoing work will be critical to determine if the persistence of HIF-1α signaling contributes to the protracted recovery often seen in pediatric IM patients.
