Recoverable macroporous polyanion bead catalysts for scalable continuous chemical depolymerization of polyethylene terephthalate
Authors: Yongsu Kim, Youngwon Lee, Ji Hee Yun, Noh-Moon Lee, Bonjae Koo, Jonggeol Na, Hyeong Jun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The glycolysis of polyethylene terephthalate (PET) offers a promising closed-loop recycling method, converting PET waste into a single monomer bis(hydroxyethyl) terephthalate (BHET). However, traditional metal-based catalysts leave hazardous residues, while alternatives are costly and energy-intensive to recover, limiting the economic feasibility of glycolysis. Herein, we present polyanion-based macroporous bead catalysts that enable efficient PET depolymerization, easy separation, and excellent reusability. These macroporous bead catalysts can be recovered through simple filtration and reused over 30 times, maintaining a BHET yield of 90.7 ± 2.7%, and further enable continuous PET depolymerization in a continuous stirred-tank reactor (CSTR) operation with a 79.7 ± 2.6% conversion rate for over 4 h. A techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life-cycle assessment (LCA) of the proposed process estimate a minimum selling price of $1.02/kg BHET, making it economically competitive with virgin BHET, along with a global warming impact of 1.90 kg CO2-eq/kg recycled PET—substantially lower than virgin PET production.

Introduction

Plastics have exceeded steel in production volume since the mid-1980s, reflecting their ascendancy as the material basis of the modern economy1. However, their widespread disposability has led to the rapid accumulation of plastic waste, necessitating urgent international action to establish a circular economy for plastics2,3. Plastic recycling encompasses several methods, including mechanical recycling, chemical recycling, solvent extraction, and pyrolysis4–6. While each approach offers distinct pathways, chemical recycling is particularly appealing for achieving a fully closed-loop circular economy as it breaks down plastics into their original monomers, allowing for the production of virgin-equivalent products7–9. For example, polyethylene terephthalate (PET) is one of the most common plastics used in single-use packaging and textiles, accounting for ~10–15% of global plastic waste3,10. Notably, PET can be efficiently depolymerized into monomers through transesterification using an excess of protic solvents, such as water, methanol, and ethylene glycol (EG), which drive hydrolysis11,12, methanolysis13–15, and glycolysis reactions16–19, respectively.

Of the available chemical depolymerization strategies for PET, glycolysis is broadly recognized as a practical and energy-efficient route20–22. For example, McNeely et al.21,22 conducted a comparative analysis of process modeling and industrial feasibility for these methods, concluding that glycolysis is the simplest and most energy-efficient approach when purification is simple. Glycolysis can be conducted under atmospheric conditions, whereas methanolysis and hydrolysis require high-pressure reactors to achieve the necessary temperature, which in turn increases both operational costs and safety concerns. Furthermore, glycolysis produces a single primary product, bis(hydroxyethyl) terephthalate (BHET), which significantly simplifies the purification process. Notably, BHET can be directly used for PET repolymerization without requiring modifications to existing PET production plants, further enhancing the commercial viability of glycolysis.

The most commonly used catalysts in PET glycolysis are metal-based salts, such as zinc acetate23,24. The zinc cation coordinates with carbonyl oxygen in PET, while the acetate anion forms hydrogen bonds with EG, accelerating the activation of the transesterification between PET and the hydroxyl group of EG. The use of metal-based salts, however, can lead to environmental hazards in downstream processes, including heavy metal pollution, soil contamination, and water contamination. Additionally, residual metal contamination in BHET limits the use of repolymerized PET in products such as beverage bottles and food packaging25. Zhang et al.26 demonstrated that the complete removal of these metal residues from BHET requires extensive purification, which involves more than six recrystallization cycles. This extensive process significantly increases energy consumption and ultimately eliminates the primary benefits of glycolysis. To address these challenges, alternative catalysts, such as ionic liquids (ILs)27–30, organic salts31,32, amines18,33, metal–organic frameworks34,35, metal oxides36, and zeolite catalysts37, have been proposed as more sustainable options for PET glycolysis. However, these alternatives are often costly, and their complex recovery processes hinder the scalability and feasibility of continuous glycolysis operations, ultimately limiting their commercial viability.

Herein, we introduce macroporous bead-type polyanion catalysts that enable high depolymerization yields, facile separation, and excellent reusability for sustainable and continuous PET glycolysis. In this design, the polymer backbones of polyions serve as cross-linking sites, generating robust bead architectures for the facile recovery with catalytic functionality. Screening diverse polyionic candidates revealed that polyanions consistently outperformed polycations, among which poly(sodium 4-styrene sulfonate) (PSSNa) achieved outstanding BHET yields. To elucidate the origin of the superior catalytic activity of polyanions over polycations, we designed structurally symmetric model polyion systems by covalently tethering either the cationic or anionic species of the [EMIM][OAc] ionic liquid (IL) onto the polymer backbones. Direct comparison shows that polyanions maintain superior catalytic activity via reduced activation energies and accelerated kinetics, attributable to steric hindrance between polycations and ester groups, as supported by ATR-FTIR analysis. Building upon this mechanistic understanding, we implemented macroporous bead architectures for PSSNa catalysts, which effectively overcome mass-transport limitations while ensuring facile catalyst recovery. The macroporous PSSNa bead catalysts exhibit high depolymerization efficiency, can be easily recovered via simple filtration, and demonstrate excellent stability, maintaining a 90.7 ± 2.7% yield and retaining over 89.3 ± 2.0 wt% of their initial mass after 30 cycles. Their scalability was further validated in a continuous stirred-tank reactor (CSTR), where unreacted PET remained with the catalyst, while BHET was continuously extracted with EG, enabling sustained depolymerization for over 4 h at a 79.7 ± 2.6% conversion rate. This process allows for efficient catalyst recovery and BHET isolation via evaporation-based purification, enhancing both economic and environmental viability. A techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life-cycle assessment (LCA) estimated a minimum selling price (MSP) of $1.02/kg BHET and a global warming impact (GWI) of 1.90 kg CO2-eq/kg recycled PET (rPET)—both of which represent improvements over the market price of virgin BHET ($1.35/kg) and the environmental burden of virgin PET (vPET) disposal (3.45 kg CO2-eq/kg).

Results

Polyanion and polycation catalysts for the efficient glycolysis of PET

In conventional PET glycolysis, salt catalysts play a central role in facilitating ester bond cleavage, where cations coordinate with the carboxyl oxygen of the ester and anions form hydrogen bonds with EG, thereby enhancing the nucleophilicity of the hydroxyl oxygen1,25 (Fig. 1a). In our study, we extended the concept of salt catalysis to polymeric analogs—polyanions and polycations—whose backbones offer cross-linking sites that can be processed into bead structures. In total, nine representative polyion catalysts (four polyanions and five polycations) are evaluated for their catalytic efficiency in PET glycolysis (Fig. 1b). Details of the preparation of these polyions are provided in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Figs. 1–6). Glycolysis reactions were carried out in a batch reactor under reflux at 196 °C for 1 h using 10 mol% catalyst relative to the PET monomer. Under these conditions, EG evaporation was minimal; even after extended heating at 196 °C (>40 h), the mass loss remained below 2%. Following a previous method19, PET conversion was determined by weighing the residual solid after glycolysis, while the BHET and dimer yields were quantified via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using an internal standard. In the absence of a catalyst, PET depolymerization conversion was limited to only 2.39 ± 0.07%, yielding 1.48 ± 0.07% BHET. Using zinc acetate salt as a catalyst resulted in near-complete (>99%) PET depolymerization, leaving no measurable solid residue after glycolysis and achieving a BHET yield of 97.3 ± 0.92%, according to the HPLC analysis. This finding is consistent with prior reports on the exceptional efficiency of zinc acetate in glycolysis19,38. We clarify that reactive distillation does not occur under our conditions; consequently, common salts such as NaCl, NaBr, and KCl exhibit limited activity to promote PET depolymerization (Supplementary Fig. 8). When comparing the BHET yields from polyions, polyanions overall exhibited higher PET conversion and BHET yields than polycations (Fig. 1b). Specifically, the polyanion catalysts PEA, PSSNa, PANa, and PES achieved nearly complete PET conversion (>99%), with BHET yields of 94.0 ± 3.0%, 96.9 ± 1.0%, 92.9 ± 2.4%, and 87.9 ± 7.7%, respectively. In contrast, the polycation catalysts PAO, PDADMAC, PBC, PAB, and PAETAC exhibited incomplete PET conversion of less than 40%, with BHET yields of 35.7 ± 2.4%, 24.0 ± 0.8%, 15.1 ± 0.3%, 30.8 ± 0.3%, and 9.0 ± 1.2%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9). These findings demonstrate that the choice between polyanions and polycations can profoundly influence the interaction with the PET substrate, reaction kinetics, and overall catalytic performance during glycolysis.

To elucidate the origin of the generally lower catalytic activity of polycations compared with polyanions, we designed structurally symmetric model systems by tethering either the cationic or the anionic component of the same ion pair onto polymer backbones. This approach provides a rigorous way to decouple the intrinsic catalytic characteristics of each ionic species and the influence of cation–anion dissociation effect. Notably, achieving such symmetry on a polymer backbone is only feasible through the use of organic charged species. Among the organic ion catalysts, imidazolium-based ionic liquids (ILs) are known for their exceptional depolymerization efficiency27,30. We evaluate five different imidazolium-based ILs for PET glycolysis (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11), and [EMIM][OAc] showed the highest activity, affording near-complete PET conversion of >99% and a BHET yield of 95.5 ± 1.96%, comparable to the exceptional performance of zinc acetate. Accordingly, [EMIM][OAc] was selected as the model system for subsequent incorporation into polymer backbones.

Building on the structure of [EMIM][OAc], we prepared a polyanion, poly(1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acrylate) (PEA), with the acetate anion anchored to the polymer chain, and a polycation, poly[1-(2-acryloyloxyethyl)−3-butylimidazolium] acetate (PAO), with the imidazolium cation immobilized (Fig. 2a). The detailed procedures and 1H-NMR characterization for the synthesis of PEA and PAO are described in Supplementary Information (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Subsequently, PEA and PAO were used as catalysts in glycolysis reactions, and their reaction kinetics were compared to those of the [EMIM][OAc] catalyst by monitoring their BHET yields with HPLC during the glycolysis reaction (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 12). The role of cations and anions in the glycolysis reaction is generally understood as follows: cations coordinate with the carboxyl oxygen in the ester group of PET, while anions form hydrogen bonds with EG, enhancing the nucleophilicity of its hydroxyl oxygen1,25. In our study, polycations exhibited restricted interaction with the carboxyl oxygen in the ester group owing to steric hindrance from polymer chains tethered to the cations. In contrast, the counterions (cations) in polyanions exhibited greater mobility, enabling more effective coordination with the carboxyl oxygen in the ester group, which resulted in relatively high PET conversion and BHET yields. The direct evidence of cation–ester bond interaction can be examined by ATR-FTIR (Supplementary Fig. 15). Ethyl benzoate was employed as a reference molecule to mimic the ester bond present in PET. Upon addition of the IL catalyst [EMIM][OAc], the C = O stretching band exhibited a red shift from 1719 to 1716 cm−1, indicating that the cation interacts with the carbonyl group, thereby weakening the C = O bond, which facilitates the transesterification reaction. Despite the polyanion (PEA) and polycation (PAO) having structurally similar but oppositely charged backbones derived from [EMIM][OAc], their interactions with the ester bond differ. The polyanion induced a red shift of the C = O stretching to 1716 cm−1, comparable to the [EMIM][OAc] IL, whereas the polycation caused a smaller red shift to 1717 cm−1. This result provides direct evidence that steric hindrance in the polycation backbone reduces its interaction with the ester C = O bond, whereas the stronger interaction in the polyanion arises from free countercations that retain translational freedom and can more effectively coordinate with the carbonyl group, unlike the fixed cations of the polycation (Fig. 2c).

To further examine the reaction mechanism associated with the enhanced catalytic activity of the polyanion system, we performed kinetic isotope effect (KIE) measurements using deuterated EG-d6 ((CD2OD)2). If cleavage of the EG O–H bond were rate-limiting, a large primary KIE (kH/kD »1) would be expected42. The observed values, however, were small in both systems (kH/kD = 1.25 ± 0.11 for polyanion; 0.99 ± 0.15 for polycation; Supplementary Fig. 16). Notably, these values include both primary (O–D) and secondary (C–D) isotope contributions from EG-d6. Even at the upper bound of the established α-secondary KIE range for nucleophilic addition of alcohols to ester carbonyls (0.95–1.05 per deuterium43,44), the inferred primary O–D KIEs remain within 1.03–1.53 (polyanion) and 0.81–1.21 (polycation), indicating that O–H cleavage of EG is unlikely to be involved in the rate-limiting step. These results suggest that the catalytic process is primarily governed by activation of the ester carbonyl through cation–carbonyl interaction rather than EG activation. Traditionally, it has been difficult to disentangle whether the observed differences in glycolysis arise from the intrinsic catalytic activity of specific cations or anions, or simply from variations in their degree of dissociation. Ju et al.45 demonstrated that anions play a more critical role in glycolysis by facilitating nucleophilic attack on ester bonds. Conversely, Krisbiantoro et al.46 argued that the Lewis acidity of cations, such as Zn2+, is crucial for activating carbonyl groups and enhancing catalytic efficiency. In this study, we introduced targeted steric hindrance to a specific ionic species, offering new insights into catalyst design. Our findings reveal that cations serve as the key catalytic species in glycolysis: in polyanions, the counter-cations readily coordinate with ester groups to promote bond cleavage, whereas in polycations, steric hindrance prevents effective cation–ester interactions. However, further research into a broader range of polyion catalysts is required to fully elucidate these effects, as our study examined only a limited subset of polycations and polyanions.

Recoverable macroporous PSSNa polyanion bead catalysts for sustainable and continuous glycolysis

To enable efficient and straightforward recovery, polyion catalysts were engineered into bead structures by exploiting the polymer backbone as a cross-linking site. As a representative system, commercially available non-porous PSSNa beads are employed, prepared by sulfonating cross-linked polystyrene followed by ion exchange with sodium (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18). The average diameter of the PSSNa beads was measured to be 570 ± 90 µm, as measured from optical microscopy (Supplementary Fig. 19). The catalytic performance of non-porous PSSNa beads was evaluated by mixing excess PSSNa beads with PET flakes at a 1:1 wt% ratio and conducting glycolysis at 196 °C for 3 h. After reaction, the bead catalysts can be readily recovered by simple filtration; however, the BHET yield remains at only 4.4 ± 0.5%, far below that of homogeneous PSSNa catalysts due to the limited surface area of the beads. To overcome this limitation while preserving the recyclability of the bead design, we employ macroporous PSSNa beads that provide a large surface area for the glycolysis reaction (Fig. 3a). The macroporous PSSNa beads had an average diameter of 821 ± 81 µm, comparable to that of non-porous beads (Supplementary Fig. 19), but exhibited a markedly higher specific surface area of 21.2 ± 0.8p m2 g−1 by BET analysis, compared with <1 m2 g−1 for the non-porous counterparts. After applying a macroporous structure to PSSNa beads with a 1:1 wt ratio to PET, complete PET conversion was achieved, with no measurable solid residue remaining, and the BHET yield was measured to be 94.2 ± 2.9%. After the glycolysis reaction, the macroporous PSSNa beads could be quickly and easily recovered using a simple filtration process (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Movie 1). The recovered beads were reused in a batch reactor with fresh PET and EG, and the glycolysis process was repeated. Throughout 30 cycles of this repeated process, the beads maintained an average BHET yield of 90.7 ± 2.7% (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 20) while retaining 89.3 ± 2.0 wt% of their initial weight (Fig. 3d), demonstrating excellent durability and reusability. SEM imaging confirmed structural stability, revealing well-preserved bead morphology after 30 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 21), while BET analysis showed that the specific surface area remained at 14.1 ± 1.0 m2 g−1. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) also verified the excellent thermal stability of the PSSNa beads (Supplementary Fig. 22). This stability enables their repeated use in glycolysis reactions at 196 °C and offers them a distinct advantage over zinc acetate catalysts, which undergo multistage thermal decomposition at elevated temperatures, ultimately forming zinc oxide particles47. Furthermore, zinc acetate leaves residual zinc in the BHET product, as confirmed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping after recrystallization, while PSSNa bead catalysts result in no detectable heavy-metal contamination (Supplementary Fig. 23). We further validated the catalyst performance using real post-consumer polyester textile waste, which was decolorized and depolymerized via glycolysis with macroporous PSSNa beads, achieving >99% conversion and 89.4% BHET yield with 95.6% purity (Supplementary Fig. 24). The catalysts also selectively depolymerized PET in mixed plastic systems containing polyethylene (PE) and polypropylene (PP), while the non-polyester PE and PP plastics remained inert (Supplementary Fig. 25). We next sought to further optimize the catalytic performances of the macroporous PSSNa bead catalysts. Kinetic studies of PET glycolysis with macroporous PSSNa beads yielded a reaction rate constant of (1.7 ± 0.1) × 10−2 min−1 (Supplementary Fig. 26). The effect of catalyst loading was also examined, showing that BHET yield increases gradually at low bead loadings and then rises sharply before reaching a plateau at a 1:1 wt%, once sufficient interfacial area is available. (Supplementary Fig. 27). We then expanded the validation of the bead catalyst platform by varying both counterions and polyion types (Table 1). The Na+ counterions in macroporous PSSNa beads were replaced by immersing the beads in saturated solutions of Li+, [EMIM]+, or Zn2+ salts, with the process repeated at least three times to ensure complete substitution. The extent of exchange and replacement yields was confirmed by TGA analysis (Supplementary Fig. 28, with further details provided in the Supplementary Note 5). Due to the multivalent nature of Zn2+, the ion-exchange efficiency was limited to 18.6%, whereas those for Li⁺ and [EMIM]⁺ were 63.6% and 82.9%, respectively. Despite the low degree of Zn2+ substitution, even partial incorporation led to a sharp decline in catalytic performance, with the BHET yield falling below 5% (Supplementary Fig. 28). Zn2+ proved ineffective within the bead structure, as its multivalent nature promotes undesired cross-linking and restricts ionic dissociation. Although Li+ possesses higher Lewis acidity than Na+, the hygroscopic character of PSSLi facilitated hydrolysis, resulting in a slight decrease in BHET yield. In contrast, the [EMIM]+ counterion also exhibited low catalytic activity, likely due to its weak coordination with the ester carbonyl group. Next, we also evaluated commercially available polycationic beads (Supplementary Fig. 29). The non-porous PVTMACl beads with an average diameter of 520 ± 20 µm exhibited poor catalytic activity, yielding only 1.4 ± 0.3% BHET conversion. Although the macroporous PVTMACl beads, with a diameter of 820 ± 20 µm and a BET surface area of 5.33 ± 0.16 m2 g−1, provided greater accessible area, the BHET conversion remained low at 1.9 ± 0.1%. Macroporous PVTMAOH beads, commercially known as Amberlyst A26, had an average diameter of 570 ± 10 µm and a BET surface area of 21.38 ± 0.5 m2 g−1, yielding a BHET conversion of 53.8 ± 4.7%. Properties and catalytic performance of polyion bead catalysts for PET glycolysis

Comparative TEA of the PET glycolysis process

To evaluate the economic potential of PET glycolysis using PSSNa polyanion beads, we conducted a comparative TEA. PSSNa beads are widely used across various industries as cost-effective ion-exchange resins, as they offer excellent thermal and chemical stability, versatility, and reusability48,49. Moreover, the industrial use of cation-exchange resins such as Amberlyst 15 in transesterification reactions, particularly for biodiesel production, further supports the potential of PSSNa beads as viable catalysts for large-scale glycolysis processes50,51. The analysis framework was based on the standard industrial PET glycolysis process (Fig. 4a), which involves the depolymerization of PET feed into BHET, followed by BHET recrystallization with water addition and distillation to recover EG24,52. For the zinc acetate catalyst, additional purification steps, including six recrystallization cycles, were implemented to remove residual heavy metals from BHET, following a previous report26. For the [EMIM][OAc] IL catalyst, IL recovery was modeled to involve EG evaporation from the IL/EG mixture after the recrystallization step, with 15 catalyst reuse cycles assumed based on previous research53. Finally, the PSSNa polyanion bead catalysts were separated through solid–liquid filtration after the PET glycolysis reactor and recycled for up to 30 cycles, based on our experimental demonstration. To further simplify product recovery, we propose a two-stage evaporation method54,55. Following the separation of polyanion bead catalysts by filtration, BHET and EG can be directly isolated through the evaporation of EG, enabled by the large boiling point difference between BHET (317.5 °C) and EG (197.3 °C). The experimental validation of the proposed process is demonstrated in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Note 6 and Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31). The distribution of the MSPs of BHET (MSPBHET) across the different process steps is presented in Fig. 4b. For the process using the zinc acetate catalyst, the total MSPBHET was calculated to be $1.47/kg, which is slightly higher than the current market price of virgin BHET ($1.35/kg)56. BHET is not commercially traded as a standalone monomer but is typically used in the TPA + EG → BHET → PET synthesis route (Supplementary Fig. 33). Based on the three-year market ranges of TPA ($0.63–1.54/kg) and EG ($0.42–0.76/kg), the simple estimated BHET cost is $0.62–1.38/kg, closely matching the reported virgin BHET price of $1.35/kg. Specifically, the PET glycolysis step, which involves feedstocks and catalysts, constituted the largest portion of the MSPBHET (60.5%), followed by recrystallization (29.5%) and additional purification for removal of heavy metal salt residues (9.8%). The calculated total MSPBHET for zinc acetate aligns well with previous findings on glycolysis20,52. For the process involving [EMIM][OAc] IL catalysts, the total MSPBHET was estimated to be $3.35/kg. Despite accounting for IL catalyst recovery and recycling, the overall MSPBHET of this process remained the highest among the MSPBHET values of the assessed processes because of the high catalyst-related operating cost (Supplementary Fig. 34). Lastly, the MSPBHET for PSSNa beads with recrystallization was $1.39/kg, which is slightly lower than that of zinc acetate due to the elimination of heavy-metal purification steps. The MSPBHET could be further reduced to $1.02/kg by applying a two-stage evaporation process. Compared to water/EG mixture distillation, EG evaporation required a significantly lower steam cost (0.78 M$/year). Water/EG distillation consumes substantial energy due to the high latent heat of water and the need for multistage operation, whereas EG evaporation requires less energy. Therefore, the calculated MSPBHET was significantly lower than the current virgin BHET market price, highlighting the economic feasibility of PSSNa bead catalysts for industrial applications.

The global sensitivity analysis demonstrated that catalyst price and catalyst recycling times have a significant impact on MSPBHET (Supplementary Fig. 35). In particular, except for that of zinc acetate, the catalyst recycling times notably affected the MSPBHET, as shown in Fig. 4c. For the [EMIM][OAc] IL, the high IL price caused the MSPBHET to vary significantly with the number of catalyst recycling times while also delaying the cost stabilization point (Supplementary Table 2). Even after up to 100 recycles, the MSPBHET for the [EMIM][OAc] process remained higher than the virgin BHET market price. In contrast, for the PSSNa bead catalyst, the MSPBHET could be reduced to match the current market price of BHET after 37 cycles of catalyst reuse. Given their ease of reuse, recycling PSSNa beads more than 30 times is certainly feasible for industrial applications, as we have already demonstrated experimentally. Moreover, the MSPBHET eventually converged to $1.18/kg BHET as the number of reuse cycles increased (Supplementary Table 2). Implementing a two-stage evaporation process significantly amplified the MSPBHET reduction, as it required only 12 cycles to enable MSPBHET to reach the virgin BHET market price threshold. As catalyst recycling continued, the MSPBHET converged to $0.81/kg BHET. Beyond this point, downstream purification costs emerged as the dominant economic factor in the process. The stability and easy recoverability of PSSNa polyanion bead catalysts enhance economic feasibility by enabling a higher number of catalyst recycling times. The results of an additional sensitivity analysis for feedstock price, identified as another critical factor in the global sensitivity analysis, are presented in Supplementary Fig. 36.

Continuous operation of glycolysis with polyanion bead catalysts in a CSTR

To validate the economical scalability and feasibility of the PSSNa bead catalyst system with a two-stage evaporation process, we conducted continuous PET glycolysis in a CSTR equipped with a filtration unit. The CSTR system is designed to efficiently separate the depolymerized product from the catalysts and reactants. The filtration unit retains the polyanion bead catalysts and unreacted solid PET within the reactor while selectively allowing the dissolved BHET product to pass through for subsequent extraction. The downstream EG and BHET mixture undergoes vacuum evaporation to separate and recover EG, which is then recycled as a reactant for continuous operation, leaving behind the BHET product (Fig. 5a). To experimentally demonstrate the CSTR operation, ground PET powder (65 mg/min) and EG (1.6 mL/min) were continuously introduced into a 500 mL CSTR. The continuous outlet stream was maintained at a constant flow rate of 1.6 mL/min. Ground PET was used to facilitate stable feeding in the continuous reactor, and the particle size distribution is provided in Supplementary Fig. 37. A PET:EG ratio of 1:20 (wt%) was adopted to maximize monomer selectivity, as decreasing the ratio to 1:3 increased dimer formation (2% → 12.9%) and reduced BHET yield (Supplementary Fig. 38). Global sensitivity analysis revealed that EG usage has only a marginal impact on overall process economics (Supplementary Fig. 35), with the minimum selling price decreasing by ~9.9% when the ratio was lowered from 1:20 to 1:3, confirming the economic robustness of the process (Supplementary Fig. 38). To monitor the composition of the glycolysis products and the concentration of BHET in the effluent, samples were collected every 10 minutes and analyzed using HPLC (Supplementary Fig. 39). Approximately 240 min after the reaction temperature reached 196 °C, the rate of change in BHET concentration stabilized at an average of 42.8 mg/mL, yielding 79.7 ± 2.6% BHET relative to the injected PET amount (Fig. 5b). We successfully demonstrated over 4 h of continuous PET glycolysis with stable conversion. HPLC analysis confirmed that the outlet stream comprised 96.6 mol% BHET and 3.4 mol% dimers, highlighting the continuous and sustainable depolymerization of PET into high-purity BHET monomers (Fig. 5c). The white solid powder isolated following EG evaporation was confirmed to be highly pure BHET, with elemental analysis indicating a purity of 96.7 ± 3.3% (Supplementary Fig. 40). In addition, EDS mapping revealed no residual contaminants in the BHET (Supplementary Fig. 41). A total of 50 g of PET was depolymerized over 10 h of continuous operation, which can be further enhanced by scaling up the reactor and, more importantly, implementing a continuous PET feed system to extend the operating time. LCA: BHET and rPET

Following the evaluation of the economic feasibility and scalability of the two-stage evaporation process with PSSNa, we further conducted an LCA of four PET glycolysis processes. The LCA quantitatively compared the GWI associated with the production of 1 kg of BHET (Fig. 6a). First, we defined the grave-to-gate system boundary, an LCA approach that assesses the environmental impacts of converting postconsumer PET into BHET (Supplementary Fig. 42). The zinc acetate process had the highest GWI of 5.83 kg CO2-eq/kg BHET, mainly due to the high steam consumption required for additional purification. The [EMIM][OAc] process and PSSNa bead-based process with recrystallization exhibited similar GWIs of 4.25 kg CO2-eq/kg BHET and 4.06 kg CO2-eq/kg BHET, respectively. These similar values are due to the additional steam required for IL recovery in the [EMIM][OAc] process having a negligible impact on GWI compared to the recrystallization step. However, the application of two-stage evaporation with PSSNa beads substantially reduced the GWI to 1.43 kg CO2-eq/kg BHET, with low steam consumption accounting for 19.8% of the total GWI. Beyond assessing the GWI, we conducted a comprehensive environmental assessment across six impact categories: global warming, ionizing radiation, terrestrial acidification, freshwater ecotoxicity, human noncarcinogenic toxicity, and fossil resource scarcity (Fig. 6b). The PSSNa beads, when combined with a two-stage evaporation process, generally demonstrated the lowest environmental impacts in these categories. For example, it reduced terrestrial acidification and fossil resource scarcity by about 25% and 29%, respectively, compared to the zinc acetate process. While high steam consumption is the major environmental impact of the zinc acetate process, zinc waste may also contribute to the overall environmental impact. Repeated purification for the removal of heavy metal residues from BHET can lead to zinc discharge into wastewater. Although wastewater treatments such as chemical precipitation effectively remove contamination, trace amounts of zinc remain in the effluent, posing long-term risks of human noncarcinogenic toxicity and freshwater ecotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. 43). To assess the sustainability of the closed-loop PET recycling economy, we expanded the system boundary to a grave-to-cradle approach, which included rPET production (Supplementary Fig. 42), and quantitatively assessed the environmental impact of rPET as a substitute for vPET. As a basis for comparison, vPET represents PET produced from fossil fuels and conventional disposal scenarios such as incineration and landfill. The zinc acetate process exhibited a higher GWI of 7.60 kg CO2-eq/kg PET than the vPET incineration scenario (5.35 kg CO2-eq/kg PET), highlighting its limitation in terms of sustainability (Fig.6c). The [EMIM][OAc] process and the PSSNa bead-based process with recrystallization had similar GWIs of 5.54 kg CO2-eq/kg PET and 5.29 kg CO2-eq/kg PET, respectively, which are comparable to those of the vPET incineration scenario. Notably, only the PSSNa bead-based process with evaporation achieved a GWI of 1.90 kg CO2-eq/kg rPET, which is lower than that of the vPET landfill scenario (3.45 kg CO2-eq/kg PET). These findings underscore the exceptional sustainability potential of using PSSNa beads coupled with evaporation for PET glycolysis, highlighting this approach as a viable and scalable pathway toward a circular plastic economy.

The environmental impacts of the assessed processes primarily stem from the high global warming potential of steam, which is 0.129 kg CO2-eq/MJ57. This issue can be alleviated by adopting heat sources with low environmental impact, such as natural gas, waste heat, or biomass58, or by integrating electric heat pumps and electric boilers with existing heat sources59. If conventional steam is replaced with natural gas, biomass, or electric boilers powered by renewable energy, all processes can achieve GWIs lower than those of vPET disposal scenarios (Fig. 6d). However, adopting heat sources with lower environmental impact may introduce economic trade-offs under current technological and commercial conditions. For example, electric boilers powered by renewable energy are economically impractical because of high initial capital costs, maintenance costs, and additional energy storage system costs required to manage intermittent renewable energy, such as wind and solar energy60. Notably, the PSSNa bead-based process with evaporation maintains a consistently low GWI regardless of the heat energy source, demonstrating its sustainable performance.

Discussion

Comparative analysis using a model polyion system confirmed that polyanion catalysts outperform polycation catalysts, as steric hindrance of polycations impedes the coordination of cations with the ester group, resulting in reduced glycolysis performance. To highlight the practical advantages of polyanion catalysts, we employed macroporous PSSNa bead catalysts, which exhibited excellent catalytic performance, achieving BHET yields of 90.7 ± 2.7%. These bead catalysts allowed for straightforward recovery via filtration and maintained high activity over 30 cycles. The proposed PSSNa beads were compared to conventional catalysts to evaluate their economic feasibility and environmental impact. The TEA revealed that using the PSSNa beads with an evaporation process resulted in the lowest MSPBHET of $1.02/kg, which is far below the market price of virgin BHET. From LCA, the PSSNa bead-based process with evaporation had the lowest GWI of 1.43 kg CO2-eq/kg BHET due to its low steam consumption, and it also exhibited minimal environmental impact in all impact categories. Furthermore, when we expand the LCA scope to include rPET production, this process demonstrated excellent environmental sustainability, achieving a lower GWI of 1.90 kg CO2-eq/kg rPET than the vPET disposal scenarios. To validate the economical scalability, we demonstrate continuous PET glycolysis using a CSTR system, which enabled steady-state depolymerization with a stable BHET yield of 79.7 ± 2.6% and high-purity BHET recovery via evaporation. The continuous operation of this system highlights its potential for industrial applications, as it ensures consistent reaction efficiency and reduces operational complexity.

Methods

General procedure for PET glycolysis in a batch reactor

PET (JPDC, 1 g (5 mmol)) was cut into 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm pieces and placed in a 50 mL two-neck flask. Next, 20 g (322 mmol) of EG and polyion catalysts (10 mol% relative to a single unit of PET) were added to the flask. The mixture was then stirred at 300 rpm under reflux at 196 °C for 3 h to carry out the glycolysis reaction. After the reaction was completed, unreacted PET was removed from the reactor via filtration. The filtered unreacted PET was washed with deionized water and methanol, dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 12 h, and then weighed to determine the PET conversion. To ensure accurate quantification of residual PET in the presence of bead catalysts, we validated a weight-balance method against a density-based separation using aqueous NaBr (0.6 g/mL), finding excellent agreement with deviations below 5% (Supplementary Fig. 44). The glycolysis products, BHET and its corresponding dimer, were purified according to the procedure detailed in the Supplementary Information and characterized by 1H-NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 45).

Quantitative analysis of the glycolysis products

The glycolysis products, i.e., BHET and dimers, were quantitatively analyzed using HPLC (Agilent Technologies, 1260 Infinity LC Systems, Waters e2695, NFEC-2024-12-301981). The analysis was conducted using a Zorbax Eclipse XDB-C18 column [Agilent, 4.6 mm (I.D.) × 250 mm (L), 5 μm particle size] and a UV detector (λmax = 254 nm). A methanol and deionized water mixture (70:30 v/v) was used as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min, and the oven temperature was set to 35 °C.

During the reaction, 50 μL of the reaction mixture was sampled using a gas-tight syringe and diluted 100-fold with a methanol and water mixture (70:30 v/v). Thereafter, 1 mL of the diluted sample was transferred to an LC sample vial for analysis. Methyl benzoate was used as the standard material, and the concentration of the glycolysis products was determined based on the calibration curve.

TEA

The TEA was conducted through a cash flow analysis to determine the MSP at which the cumulative net present value equals zero at the end of the plant’s life. The total capital investment (CTCI) included total depreciable capital (CTDC) and working capital (CWC), and was derived from the equipment costs. The production costs excluding depreciation (CExcl. Dep.) accounted for operating costs such as feedstocks, utilities, wastewater treatment, labor, and maintenance. The reactor cost was estimated by scaling up the batch reactor, which was calculated based on the experimental reactor size, PET feed rate, and reaction time interval. Other equipment costs were estimated using the Aspen Process Economic Analyzer. The costs of feedstocks, utilities, and wastewater treatment were calculated based on the mass and energy balance results obtained from Aspen Plus V14® using cost-related parameters61. The catalyst cost was obtained by multiplying the catalyst amount per batch, the annual operations times, and the catalyst price, and dividing by the catalyst recycling times. PET flake feed rate of 80,000 t/year was assumed for consistent comparison, with the impact of feed rate variation provided in Supplementary Fig. 46. Depreciation followed 7-year life modified accelerated cost recovery system (MACRS)61, with plant lifetime of 30 years, tax rate of 24%, and loan interest of 10%. Details of parameters are shown in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4.

LCA

The LCA followed ISO 14040/14044 standards62. There were two main goals: (1) comparing the environmental impacts of producing 1 kg BHET across four PET glycolysis processes under a grave-to-gate system boundary and (2) assessing the GWI of 1 kg rPET under a grave-to-cradle system boundary, compared to vPET disposal scenarios. In both cases, postconsumer PET was the starting point. The environmental impacts of producing waste PET flakes include collection, sorting, and shredding into flakes63. For rPET production, PET synthesis was modeled as a polycondensation process at 270 °C using Sb2O364, assuming 100% BHET conversion with 1.32 kg BHET input per 1 kg rPET. EG formed during this process was reused in BHET production to reduce fresh EG consumption. The LCA was performed using SimaPro v9.3.0.3 software and Ecoinvent v3.8 database57, and the life cycle inventory data used is shown in Supplementary Table 5. Zinc waste from the BHET additional purification step was assumed to be 99% removed by wastewater treatment65. The environmental impacts are calculated using ReCiPe 2016 (H) midpoint method, focusing primarily on GWI. For BHET, five additional impact categories (ionizing radiation, terrestrial acidification, freshwater ecotoxicity, human noncarcinogenic toxicity, and fossil resource scarcity) were also analyzed.
