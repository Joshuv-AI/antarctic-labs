Kernel Tests of Equivalence
Xing Liu, Axel Gandy

Abstract
We propose novel kernel-based tests for assessing the equivalence between distributions. Traditional goodness-of-fit testing is inappropriate for concluding the absence of distributional differences, because failure to reject the null hypothesis may simply be a result of lack of test power, also known as the Type-II error. This motivates equivalence testing , which aims to assess the absence of a statistically meaningful effect under controlled error rates. However, existing equivalence tests are either limited to parametric distributions or focus only on specific moments rather than the full distribution. We address these limitations using two kernel-based statistical discrepancies: the kernel Stein discrepancy and the Maximum Mean Discrepancy . The null hypothesis of our proposed tests assumes the candidate distribution differs from the nominal distribution by at least a pre-defined margin, which is measured by these discrepancies. We propose two approaches for computing the critical values of the tests, one using an asymptotic normality approximation, and another based on bootstrapping. Numerical experiments are conducted to assess the performance of these tests.

Introduction

Goodness-of-fit (GOF) testing aims to use observed data to draw statistical conclusions on how well some nominal distribution approximates an unknown underlying data-generating process. Denote by
\mathcal{P}
the set of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
Q,P\in\mathcal{P}
. Given observations
\mathbb{X}_{n}\coloneqq\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
drawn independently from
Q
, a GOF test aims to use
\mathbb{X}_{n}
to test

\displaystyle H_{0}^{\ast}:\ Q=P\qquad\textrm{against}\qquad H_{1}^{\ast}:\ Q\neq P\;.

(1)

Depending on the assumed knowledge about the nominal distribution
P
, the GOF testing literature can be categorized into two main settings. The first is
one-sample
testing, where samples from
Q
are observed, and
P
is often specified via a probability density function, whose normalizing constant is usually unknown. This is the case when, e.g.,
P
belongs to a probabilistic model, such as general exponential family models
(
20
, Chapter 3.4)
, energy-based models
(
24
)
and probabilistic graphical models
(
59
)
. Another setup is
two-sample
testing, where
P
is specified implicitly through a sampling procedure and does not admit tractable likelihood functions, but simulating from
P
is possible. Examples include
generative adversarial networks

(
34
)
and many scientific models based on simulators
(
9
;
82
;
12
)
. In both settings, the null hypothesis
H_{0}^{\ast}
is rejected if there is statistically significant evidence that the data do not comply to the nominal distribution
P
.

In many applications, however, the goal is not to establish that a statistically significant difference exists between some distributions, but rather that the distributions are practically equivalent. Examples include comparative bioequivalence trials
(
73
;
88
;
16
)
, drug stability assessment
(
69
)
, pharmacokinetics studies
(
42
;
40
)
, and validation of statistical models
(
30
;
29
;
18
)
. In those cases, hypotheses of the form (
1
) are no longer appropriate for two reasons. First, failure to reject the null hypothesis
H_{0}^{\ast}
does
not
provide a probabilistic guarantee for
Q=P
; instead, it can simply be due to the lack of test power
(
49
;
96
)
. Second, it is well-known that, in practice, the null hypothesis
H_{0}^{\ast}
will eventually always be rejected as the data size grows. This is because “all models are wrong”, so a very large sample would “invariably produce statistically significant lack of fit”
(
41
, pp. 189)
.

Equivalence testing

(
15
)
aims to resolve this issue by considering a different null hypothesis, where
Q
and
P
are sufficiently
different
by a pre-specified margin. The notion of distributional difference is often quantified by a statistical discrepancy
D
. Given a positive number
\theta>0
, known as the
equivalence margin

(
109
)
or
minimal meaningful distance

(
74
)
, we consider testing the following hypotheses

\displaystyle H_{0}:\ D(Q,P)\geq\theta\;\qquad\textrm{against}\qquad H_{1}:\ D(Q,P)<\theta\;.

(2)

Unlike the standard hypotheses (
1
), rejection of
H_{0}
now suggests that there is sufficient evidence that
Q
and
P
are similar. This allows one to rigorously control the probability of falsely concluding that
Q
and
P
are equivalent.

Equivalence testing was initially employed in the field of pharmacokinetics
(
42
)
for determining whether new, cheaper drugs exhibit comparable efficacy to existing, more expensive ones
(
91
)
. For a review, see
15
;
62
. In the statistics literature, equivalence tests have been developed for comparing parametric models
(
30
;
29
)
, for assessing independence
(
23
)
, and for improving the test-then-pool framework in causal inference
(
66
)
. However, most existing methods are restricted to parametric models or specific moments. A recent non-parametric method by
23
uses the Maximum Mean Discrepancy
(
76
;
37
)
to avoid model-specific assumptions. However, their method relies on an asymptotic normal approximation, which, as we show numerically in
Section

3.2
, may break down when the equivalence margin
\theta
is small, resulting in uncontrolled Type-I error.

We propose two families of equivalence tests, one based on an asymptotic normal approximation as in
23
, and the other on a bootstrapping technique. Each family contains two variants, one employing Kernel Stein Discrepancies, suitable for one-sample testing, and the other employing Maximum Mean Discrepancies, suitable for two-sample testing. Our tests do not make any parametric assumptions about the underlying distributions and are not limited to specific moments. Our contributions are summarized as follows and in
Table

1
:

1.

We first propose two equivalence tests for the one-sample setting. The first test, called the
E-KSD-Normal
test, is motivated by
23
and leverages a Central Limit Theorem for the KSD statistic. We then demonstrate that this test, despite achieving high power, can result in uncontrolled Type-I error, especially with small equivalence margins. We then propose an alternative equivalence test, called the
E-KSD-Boot
test, which is based on bootstrapping and can achieve better Type-I error control with finite samples regardless of the size of the equivalence margin, albeit at the cost of a lower test power.

2.

Next, we propose two equivalence tests for the two-sample setting. These tests, called the
E-MMD-Normal
and
E-MMD-Boot
tests, respectively, mirror the aforementioned one-sample tests and leverage MMD to adapt them to the two-sample setting. In particular, our E-MMD-Normal test resembles the homogeneity-equivalence test of
23
and generalizes it to allow non-identical sample sizes.

3.

For the two bootstrapping tests, we propose a data-driven approach for selecting the equivalence margin
\theta
, which computes
\theta
as the minimal effect size given a pre-specified test power.

Background

This section provides the necessary background on the kernel-based divergences and metrics that we adopt in our tests, as well as the relevant literature. Throughout, we denote by
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.1
Equivalence Testing

Our goal is to design non-parametric tests targeting the equivalence hypotheses of the form (
2
), with appropriate choices of
D
to be introduced later. Tests targeting equivalence hypotheses of the form (
2
) are known as
equivalence testing

(
15
;
62
, ET,)
or
negligible effect testing

(
11
)
. In classic GOF testing, the null hypothesis typically assumes no meaningful statistical difference, namely
H_{0}^{\ast}:Q=P
. Consequently, GOF testing can only conclude the
presence
of model mis-alignment, because failing to reject
H_{0}^{\ast}
does not suggest any statistical evidence that it holds true. In contrast, for ET, the null hypothesis assumes instead that
Q
and
P
are sufficiently different by some margin. This means that, for a well-calibrated ET, the probability of falsely concluding the
absence
of meaningful statistical differences can be controlled.

ET has found applications in a wide range of areas, including pharmacokinetics
(
42
;
88
)
and psychology
(
80
;
83
)
. The simplest equivalence test is the
two one-sided test

(
88
;
89
, TOST,)
. In TOST, two one-sided hypotheses of the form
H_{0}:D>\theta_{u}
and
H_{0}:\Delta<-\theta_{l}
are tested, where
D
is some measure of effect (potentially negatively valued) and
\theta_{l},\theta_{u}
are two thresholds for significant effect size. One then one conclude
-\theta_{l}<\Delta<\theta_{u}
if both hypotheses are rejected. However, TOST is often found to suffer from low test power due to the use of two auxiliary hypotheses
(
13
;
77
)
.

Various ETs targeting directly an equivalence hypothesis have been proposed.
83
;
108
proposed tests for equivalence of means.
28
studied the problem of testing equivalence of means and variances in a two-sample setup.
30
used the
L_{2}
distance to test the equivalence between observations and parametric models.
29
used both
L_{2}
and
L_{\infty}
distances to test the equivalence of two parametric models.
6
used a weighted
L_{2}
distance.
33
proposed an equivalence test for comparing the marginals of multivariate distributions.
113
;
11
studied equivalence testing for structural equation models. These tests are limited to either specific moments of the distributions, or to certain parametric models. In contrast, our proposed tests are non-parametric and capable of testing for the homogeneity of the full distributions, thus providing more flexibility.

Closest to our work is
23
, who used the characteristic functions to construct tests for distributional homeogeneity, independence, and symmetry. Although not mentioned explicitly in their paper, their measure of dissimilarity is effectively an MMD. Our normality-based methods are similar to their approach, except that they assume the two samples to have equal sizes, whereas we allow them to be different, thus providing more generality. Moreover, our bootstrap-based methods differ fundamentally from their approach—while
23
rely on an asymptotic normality result for the MMD estimator, our bootstrapped tests leverage the triangle inequality of MMD or KSD to construct a test statistic. As we will show numerically in
Section

6
, with small sample sizes, the normality-based tests, including that of
23
, performs poorly in controlling the Type-I error when the sample size is small, whereas the bootstrap-based tests remain well-calibrated even with small samples.

2.2
Kernel-based Discrepancies

The equivalence hypotheses (
2
) are specified by a statistical discrepancy
D
. Hence, changing
D
would result in a different null set, thus also a different hypothesis. It is therefore important to select a statistical discrepancy that is both computationally convenient for the problem at hand, and also statistically meaningful to quantify the type of model deviation of interest. We propose to use KSD and MMD, two kernel-based statistical discrepancies with desirable computationally and statistical properties. We will review KSD and MMD in this section. Throughout, we assume the kernel
k
is
characteristic

(
100
)
.

2.2.1
Kernel Stein Discrepancy

KSD is a statistical divergence based on kernel methods and Stein’s method. Assume
P
admits a Lebesgue density
p
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
p(x)>0
that is continuously differentiable, and define its
score function

s_{p}(x)\coloneqq\nabla_{x}\log(x)
. Let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
be the reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(
84
, RKHS;)
associated with a reproducing kernel
k
, and let
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{d}=\{f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})^{\top}:f_{j}\in\mathcal{H}\}
be the product space equipped with the norm
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}^{d}}=(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|f_{j}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2})^{1/2}
. The
(Langevin) kernel Stein discrepancy

(
25
;
68
;
78
, KSD;)
between
Q
and
P
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\;

\displaystyle=\;\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}^{d};\|f\|_{k}\leq 1}\big|\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[\mathcal{A}_{p}f(X)]\big|\;,

where
\mathcal{A}_{p}
is an operator acting on vector-valued functions, defined as
(\mathcal{A}_{p}f)(x)=s_{p}(x)^{\top}f(x)+\nabla^{\top}f(x)
. Under mild regularity conditions on
s_{p}
, the operator
\mathcal{A}_{p}
, called
Langevin Stein operator
, maps sufficiently regular functions to zero-mean functions under the probability measure
P
, namely
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[\mathcal{A}_{p}f(X)]=0
. Loosely speaking, this means that the KSD takes small values when
Q
is “close” to
P
, and large values otherwise. This can be made rigorous by showing that KSD is
P
-separating
, namely
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)=0
if
Q=P
and
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)>0
otherwise, whenever the kernel
k
is characteristic
(
8
, Theorem 3)
. This property suggests that KSD is a valid statistical discrepancy, motivating
25
;
68
to use it as a test statistic for the standard GOF null hypothesis (
1
), where
H_{0}^{\ast}
is rejected for large estimates of
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)
.

When
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[\|s_{p}(X)\|_{2}^{2}]<\infty
, the
squared
KSD admits a double-expectation form, allowing it to be efficiently estimated by Monte Carlo. Indeed,
8
shows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\;

\displaystyle=\;\big(\mathbb{E}_{X,X^{\prime}\sim Q}[u_{p}(X,X^{\prime})]\big)^{1/2}\;,

where

\displaystyle u_{p}(x,x^{\prime})\;

\displaystyle=\;s_{p}(x)^{\top}s_{p}(x^{\prime})k(x,x^{\prime})+s_{p}(x)^{\top}\nabla_{2}k(x,x^{\prime})+s_{p}(x^{\prime})^{\top}\nabla_{1}k(x,x^{\prime})+\nabla^{\top}\nabla k(x,x^{\prime})\;,

and
\nabla^{\top}\nabla k(x,y)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\partial_{x^{j}}\partial_{y^{j}}k(x,y)
. Let
Q_{n}
be an empirical distribution formed by independent random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
. a natural estimator is the following V-statistic, formed by substituting
Q_{n}
for
Q

\displaystyle\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q_{n},P)\;=\;\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}u_{p}(X_{i},X_{j})\;.

(3)

This estimator possesses desirable computational advantages. Firstly, it does not require samples from
P
, which can be difficult to generate. Secondly, it depends on
P
only through the score function
s_{p}
, which can be evaluated even if the normalizing constant is unknown. These make KSD suitable for testing the GOF of many probabilistic models that are beyond the reach of traditional GOF tests, such as general exponential family models
(
7
;
17
)
, energy-based models
(
24
)
, and probabilistic graphical models
(
59
)
. In comparison, classic GOF tests, such as likelihood ratio tests
(
45
, Chapter 8.7)
, chi-square tests
(
45
, Chapter 9.1)
, Komogorov-Smirnov tests
(
60
)
, either require the normalizing constant to be known, or can struggle for multivariate models.

GOF tests based on KSD proceed by first using empirical samples to compute the KSD estimate, and rejecting the null hypothesis (
1
) if it exceeds a critical value. This critical value does not admit a closed-form, but can be estimated using
wild bootstrapping

(
65
;
93
)
or
weighted bootstrapping

(
3
;
50
)

(
70
)
. Both approaches are found to achieve similar empirical performance for the KSD test
(
70
)
. In our proposed tests, we will use weighted bootstrapping since our theoretical results are built upon their existing theory studied in
3
.

In weighted bootstrapping, the distribution of
\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q_{n},P)
is approximated using bootstrap samples of the form

\displaystyle D^{2}_{W_{n}}(\mathbb{X}_{n};u_{p})\;\coloneqq\;\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}(W_{ni}-1)(W_{nj}-1)u_{p}(x_{i},x_{j})\;,

(4)

where
W_{n}=(W_{n1},\ldots,W_{nn})\sim\textrm{Multinomial}(n;1/n,\ldots,1/n)
. Let
\{\mathrm{KSD}_{n,b}^{2}\}_{b=1}^{B}
denote the bootstrap sample (
4
) based on
B
i.i.d. copies of
W_{n}
, and let
\{\mathrm{KSD}_{n,b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
be their square-roots. The standard KSD test rejects
H_{0}^{\ast}
if
\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q_{n},P)>\gamma_{1-\alpha}^{B}
, where
\gamma^{B}_{1-\alpha}
is the
(1-\alpha)
-th quantile of
\{\mathrm{KSD}_{n,b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
, namely

\displaystyle\gamma^{B}_{1-\alpha}\;=\;\inf\bigg\{u\in\mathbb{R}:\;\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbbm{1}\{\mathrm{KSD}_{n,b}\leq u\}\geq 1-\alpha\bigg\}\;.

(5)

Beyond the original KSD test of
68
;
25
, various extensions have been proposed to specialize in different model families, such as discrete distributions
(
111
)
, survival models
32
, graphical models
(
112
)
, and biological sequences
(
1
)
. However, existing methods focus primarily on testing the standard point null hypothesis (
1
). As a result, these tests are only suitable for detecting the presence of model deviation, but not for concluding model equivalence.
70
proposed a
robust KSD test
for null hypotheses of the form
H_{0}:D(Q,P)\leq\theta
, which accounts for mild model mis-specification, but this is still not suitable for testing model equivalence.

2.2.2
Maximum Mean Discrepancy

Another closely related family of kernel discrepancies is the MMD. The MMD between two probability measures
Q,P\in\mathcal{P}
is defined as the maximal discrepancy between the means of functions under
Q
and
P
over all functions in the unit ball of
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)\;\coloneqq\;\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}:\;\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1}\big|\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{P}[f]\big|\;.

(6)

With bounded kernels, the MMD can be rewritten as a distance between the
kernel mean embeddings
of two distributions
(
37
, Lemma 4)
. The kernel mean embedding
(
99
;
101
)
is a mapping from
\mathcal{P}
to
\mathcal{H}
, denoted as
\mu_{P}(\cdot)\coloneqq\int k(x,\cdot)P(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)
for any
P\in\mathcal{P}
. One can show that
(
37
, Theorem 5)
the MMD can be expressed in closed-form as

\displaystyle\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)\;=\;\|\mu_{P}-\mu_{Q}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\;=\;\big(\mathbb{E}_{X,X^{\prime}\sim Q}[k(X,X^{\prime})]+\mathbb{E}_{Y,Y^{\prime}\sim P}[k(Y,Y^{\prime})]-2\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q,Y\sim P}[k(X,Y)]\big)^{1/2}\;.

(7)

When
k
is characteristic, the kernel mean embedding is injective, and the MMD is a metric on
\mathcal{P}

(
102
;
97
)
. Many well-known kernels are characteristic, such as Gaussian, Inverse Multi-Quadric and Matérn kernels, while an example of non-characteristic kernels is linear kernels.

Equation (
7
) suggests a natural estimator for the
squared
MMD
(
37
, Eq. 5)
. Given independent random samples
\mathbb{X}_{n}=\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
from
Q
and
\mathbb{Y}_{m}=\{Y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
from
P
, the squared MMD,
\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q,P)
, can be estimated by substituting the empirical measures
Q_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
and
P_{m}=m^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta_{Y_{j}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q_{n},P_{m})\;=\;\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}k(X_{i},X_{j})+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq m}k(Y_{i},Y_{j})-\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k(X_{i},Y_{j})\;.

(8)

The above estimator
\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P_{m})^{2}
is biased but consistent
(
37
)
. Moreover, (
8
) can be equivalently expressed as a
two-sample V-statistic

(
44
;
56
)
of degree
(2,2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q_{n},P_{m})\;=\;\frac{1}{n^{2}m^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i,i^{\prime}\leq n}\sum_{1\leq j,j^{\prime}\leq m}h(X_{i},X_{i^{\prime}},Y_{j},Y_{j^{\prime}})\;,

(9)

where
h(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})=k(x,x^{\prime})+k(y,y^{\prime})-k(x,y^{\prime})-k(x^{\prime},y)
.
Expressing the MMD estimator as (
9
) offers theoretical advantages, as it allows us to use well-established results on V-statistics to construct our tests. In practice, however, (
8
) should be used for computation, since it is more efficient with cost
\mathcal{O}((n+m)^{2})
. In this work, we focus on the general case where
n,m
can be different. In the special case when
n=m
, the estimator (
8
) can be simplified to a
one-sample
V-statistic
(
37
;
72
;
81
)
.

The MMD test rejects the standard null hypothesis (
1
) if the MMD estimate exceeds a critical value. Common approaches for computing the critical value includes permutation and wild bootstrapping
(
37
;
86
)
. In the hypothesis testing literature, MMD has been studied extensively both with standard point null hypotheses
(
37
;
72
;
94
;
86
)
and with robust hypotheses
(
104
;
55
;
87
)
.

2.3
Other Related Work

Equivalence testing is closely connected with
robust testing

(
31
)
, which often targets hypotheses of the form
H_{0}:D(Q,P)\leq\theta
against
H_{1}:D(Q,P)>\theta
. That is, their null and alternative hypotheses are interchanged compared with (
2
); see
Figure

1
for an illustration. Robust tests based on both MMD and KSD have been studied
(
104
;
70
)
. Compared with standard GOF where the null hypothesis is a singleton, robust tests hypothesize that the model is
close enough
, rather than identical, to the data-generating distribution. This allows one to ignore mild model deviations, so that a model would not be rejected only due to minor lack-of-fit effects that are not scientifically interesting. However, since the null set in a robust test still assumes that
Q
and
P
are close, they are also not appropriate for concluding the absence of model deviation.

Another related line of work is
credal tests

(
22
)
, which aim to detect the relationship between two arbitrary distributional simplexes.
22
proposed various MMD-based tests covering a range of scenarios. For example, their “specification test” targets null hypotheses where
P
lies inside a probability simplex, and thus it can be viewed as an instance of robust tests. However, none of their tests target the equivalence hypothesis in (
2
).

3
One-Sample Equivalence Tests with KSD

We first focus on one-sample testing, where we assume an i.i.d. sample
\mathbb{X}_{n}=\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
from
Q
is observed, and sampling from
P
is either infeasible or prohibitively expensive. Instead, we assume we can evaluate the score function
s_{p}(x)
of
P
at any given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We will use KSD to construct equivalence tests for this setting and show their calibration and consistency.

We will assume the following conditions, which together ensure the Stein kernel
u_{p}
has a finite second moment, which is sufficient for KSD to be
P
-separating and for the KSD estimator (
3
) to have well-defined asymptotic distributions.

Assumption 1
.

The reproducing kernel
k
is
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{1}
-universal

(
19
, Definition 4.1)
.

Assumption 2
.

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[\|s_{p}(X)\|_{2}^{4}]<\infty
.

Assumption 3
.

The kernel
k\in\mathcal{C}^{(1,1)}_{b}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[|\nabla_{1}^{\top}\nabla_{2}k(X,X)|^{2}]<\infty
.

2
and
3
are for simplicity rather than necessity and can be weakened. For example, they can be replaced by the sufficient condition
\text{\rm Var}_{X\sim Q}[|u_{p}(X,X^{\prime})|^{2}]<\infty
, which will be made clear in the proof of
Proposition

1
below.

Under these assumptions, the asymptotic distributions of the KSD estimator (
3
) is well-known by using classic results on V-statistics
(
92
, Section 5.5)
. We summarize it in the following result, which is proven in
Section

B.1.1
.

Proposition 1

(KSD asymptotics)
.

Suppose
1
,
2
and
3
hold.

1.

If
Q\neq P
, then
\sqrt{n}(\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q_{n},P)-\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q,P))\xrightarrow{\;\text{\rm d}\;}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{KSD}})
as
n\to\infty
, where
\sigma_{\mathrm{KSD}}^{2}=4\text{\rm Var}_{X\sim Q}(\mathbb{E}_{X^{\prime}\sim Q}[u_{p}(X,X^{\prime})])
.

2.

If
Q=P
, then
\sigma_{\mathrm{KSD}}^{2}=0
, and
\sqrt{n}\cdot\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q_{n},P)\to 0
in probability.

A similar result is also shown in
68

3.1
A Normal Test

Proposition

1
states that
\sqrt{n}\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q_{n},P)
is asymptotically normal when
Q\neq P
, and converges to
0
when
Q=P
. This motivates an equivalence test based on normal approximations, where we reject
H_{0}:\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)>\theta
if
\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)
is smaller than some critical values computed from a normal quantile. We name it the
E-KSD-Normal
test. Mathematically, this test rejects
H_{0}:\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)>\theta
if
S_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}<z_{\alpha}

\displaystyle S_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}\;\coloneqq\;\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{KSD}}}(\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q_{n},P)-\theta^{2})\;,

(10)

where
z_{\alpha}
is the
\alpha

\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{KSD}}
is the square root of the following leave-one-out Jackknife estimator for

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{KSD}}^{2}\;=\;\frac{4}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(r_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}r_{i^{\prime}}\bigg)^{2}\;,\qquad r_{i}\;=\;\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}u_{p}(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}})\;.

(11)

Variance estimators similar to the above form have been used in the goodness-of-fit testing literature to approximate the power of KSD test
(
67
;
85
;
71
)
. Using
Proposition

1
, the validity and consistency for this test can be shown. The proof is in
Section

B.1.3
and follows a similar argument in
23
.

Theorem 2

(E-KSD-Normal test)
.

Suppose
1
holds. Then, for all
Q
satisfying

and
3

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr(S_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}>\gamma_{1-\alpha})\;=\;\begin{cases}0\;,&\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)>\theta\;,\\
\alpha\;,&\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)=\theta\;,\\
1\;,&\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)<\theta\;.\end{cases}

Theorem

immediately implies that the Type-I error, namely
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr(S_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}>\gamma_{1-\alpha})
, is bounded by
\alpha
under the null hypothesis
H_{0}:\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\geq\theta
, while converges to 1 under the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}:\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)<\theta
. These combined mean that the E-KSD-Normal test is both well-calibrated and consistent.

Remark 2.1
.

Our proof follows a standard technique in the equivalence testing literature, applicable when the test statistic is asymptotically normal; see, e.g.,
33
;
29
;
23
;
5
. Most closely related to our result is

. They constructed their test using MMD and assumed they have access to samples of
equal

both

Q
and
P
. The equal-sample-size assumption allows the test statistic to be expressed as a one-sample V-statistic, similar to our formulation with KSD. Our proof can therefore be viewed as a direct counterpart to theirs, with MMD replaced by KSD. Nevertheless, we highlight that the bootstrapped test introduced in the next section is a fundamentally novel and distinct approach.

Remark 2.2
.

The Type-I control in

is
pointwise
over the null set, namely the limit over

is taken for a fixed null distribution. In general, for hypothesis tests targeting a composite null set, it is more desirable to have a

Type-I error control, namely
\lim\sup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\{Q:\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\geq\theta\}}\Pr(S_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}>\gamma_{1-\alpha})\leq\alpha
; see
64
for a detailed discussion. However, most work in the equivalence testing literature concerns pointwise control
(
29
;
23
)
, which is why we also prove pointwise control for our proposed tests in this work.

3.2
A Bootstrapped Test

The E-KSD-Normal test proposed in
Section

3.1
is guaranteed to control Type-I error in the infinite sample size limit, as shown in
Theorem

2
.
However, as will be shown empirically in
Section

6.1
, the E-KSD-Normal test can have poor Type-I error control when the margin
\theta
is small, even if the sample size is moderately large.

This is because the KSD estimator has different limiting distributions in the two regimes
Q=P
and
Q\neq P
. Too see this, assume
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)=\theta
, so that
Q
is on the boundary of the null set.
Proposition

1
shows that the (scaled) KSD estimator (
3
) is asymptotically normal when
\theta>0
, while it converges to 0 when
\theta=0
. In fact, it is well-known that in the latter case, (
3
) converges to an infinite sum of weighted chi-squares at rate
n^{-1}
; see, e.g.,
68
and
47
. In particular, when
\theta
is small (but non-zero), the normal approximation of (
3
) would deteriorate. Moreover, the variance estimator (
11
), although a common choice in the kernel-based testing literature
(
14
;
52
)
, can underestimate the actual variance
(
54
, Appendix C4)
. These issues combined can result in an inflated Type-I error. Notably, such Type-I error inflation is not unique to our kernel-based test. Other equivalence tests constructed with asymptotic normality have also found to suffer from this issue; see, e.g., the discussions in
29
and
23
.

Motivated by this observation, we propose an alternative test based on bootstrapping. This bootstrapping-based test is constructed using a completely different principle from the E-KSD-Normal test. In particular, it does not rely on normal approximations to the KSD estimator. Instead, it uses a triangle inequality for the KSD
(
95
;
70
)
to derive a more conservative (yet still consistent) upper bound, thus achieving a better Type-I error control, especially with small
\theta
.

We first present the test procedure and provide intuition later. This test, which we call the
E-KSD-Boot
test, rejects
H_{0}:\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\geq\theta
if
T_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}>\gamma_{1-\alpha}^{B}
, where

\displaystyle T_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}\;\coloneqq\;\theta-\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)\;,

(12)

and rejects
H_{0}:\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\geq\theta
if
T_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}>\gamma^{B}_{1-\alpha}
, where
\gamma^{B}_{1-\alpha}
is defined in (
5
). The full procedure is summarized in
Algorithm

2
.

We now provide some intuition for the E-KSD-Boot test. The test statistic
T_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}
quantifies how close the KSD value is from the pre-specified margin
\theta
. Since the null hypothesis
H_{0}
assumes that
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)>\theta
, we should reject
H_{0}
for small values of
\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)
, or, equivalently, for large values of
T_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}
.

A natural question is then how to choose a critical value? This critical value must control the Type-I error rate for
every
possible
Q
under the equivalence null hypothesis. In the standard KSD test reviewed in
Section

2.2.1
, where the test statistic is
\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)
, the critical value is its
(1-\alpha)
-th quantile,
\gamma_{1-\alpha}
, under the point null hypothesis (
1
), and weighted bootstrapping is used to approximate
\gamma_{1-\alpha}
. However, it is unclear whether a similar approach can be used in this case, as the null set under the equivalence null hypothesis
H_{0}:\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\geq\theta
is no longer a singleton, but an equivalence set containing sufficiently distinct distributions.

Our major contributions of this section are to show that
\gamma_{1-\alpha}
is a valid critical value even for such equivalence null hypothesis, and that the same weighted bootstrap approached can be used as an approximation. We first present the following lemma, which shows that the bootstrap samples (
4
) approximate the distribution of an
MMD
statistic
\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q;k)
. The proof is in
Section

B.1.4
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathbb{X}_{\infty}=\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be a random sample where
X_{i}\sim Q\in\mathcal{P}
are independent, and for any
n
let
Q_{n}
be the empirical measure based on
\mathbb{X}_{n}=\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Let
k
be a positive definite kernel such that
\mathbb{E}_{X,X^{\prime}\sim Q}[|k(X,X^{\prime})|^{2}]<\infty
. Then, for all
Q
-almost-sure sequences
\mathbb{X}_{\infty}
, the following holds

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\Pr(\sqrt{n}D_{W_{n}}(\mathbb{X}_{n};k)\leq t\;|\;\mathbb{X}_{\infty})-\Pr(\sqrt{n}\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q;k)\leq t)\big|\;\to\;0\;.

Remark 3.1
.

70
showed this result for the special case where
k
is a Stein reproducing kernel. The authors referred to
\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q;u_{p})
as the
P
-KSD
. We follow a similar proof strategy by noting that their proof directly extends to a general reproducing kernel
k
. The proof is included in
Section

B.1.4
for completeness. This generalization will also become helpful later when we study a similar equivalence test based on MMD.

Remark 3.2
.

Lemma

3
is a consequence of the bootstrapping results for each term in the
Hoeffding decomposition
of V-statistics
(
44
;
3
)
. As previously mentioned, this is
not
a direct application of standard bootstrapping results for the degenerate V-statistics
\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q_{n},Q)
, since the bootstrapping statistic so obtained would involve intractable expectations and thus cannot be computed. In the literature, the result in
Lemma

3
was presented in
27
in a slightly modified form, but their proof only applies to univariate distributions. The general case for multivariate distributions is implicitly shown in the proof of
3
. We provide a clearer exposition and close the gap.

It turns out that
Lemma

3
is sufficient to establish the validity and consistency of the E-KSD-Boot test, which is summarized in the following result. Intuitively, this is because, under the equivalence null hypothesis, the test statistic (
12
) can be bounded using statistics of the form
\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q;k)
, an observation that will be made clear in the proof in
Section

B.1.6
.

Theorem 4

(E-KSD-Boot test)
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and, for any
n
, let
W\sim\mathrm{Multinomial}(n,1/n,\ldots,1/n)
. Denote by
\gamma_{1-\alpha}^{\infty}
the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the conditional distribution of

, defined in (
4
), given
\mathbb{X}_{n}
. Suppose
1

2
and
3
hold. Then, for all

with

such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr(T_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}>\gamma_{1-\alpha}^{\infty})\;=\;\begin{cases}0\;,&\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)>\theta\;,\\
a\;,&\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)=\theta\;,\\
1\;,&\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)<\theta\;.\end{cases}

In other words,
Theorem

4
implies that the probability of rejection is asymptotically no larger than
a\leq\alpha
under
H_{0}:\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\geq\theta
, while it converges to
1
under
H_{1}:\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)<\theta

Remark 4.1
.

Key to the proof of
Theorem

4
is the following upper bound of KSD using MMD and

-KSD (see
Remark

3.1
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\;=\;\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P;u_{p})\;

\displaystyle\leq\;\mathrm{MMD}(Q,Q_{n};u_{p})+\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P;u_{p})

\displaystyle=\;\mathrm{MMD}(Q,Q_{n};u_{p})+\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)\;.

(13)

The first equality uses the fact that KSD can be rewritten as an MMD with kernel
u_{p}
, namely
KSD(Q,P)=\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P;u_{p})
, and the inequality leverages the triangle inequality for MMD. A more rigorous proof for (

) is given in
Section

B.1.6
. This inequality resembles a triangle inequality for KSD. Similar inequalities have been studied in
95
to study the convergence rate of kernel-based interacting particle systems, and in

to construct robust GOF tests with KSD. Our proof is motivated by

and is adjusted to equivalence hypotheses.

The E-KSD-Boot test bears similarity with the
robust-KSD test
of
70
. In
70
, they target robust hypotheses of the form
H_{0}^{R}:\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\leq\theta

4.1
A Normal Test

Proposition

5

\sqrt{N}(\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q_{n},P_{m})-\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q,P))
is asymptotically normal under
H_{0}:\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)\geq\theta
. We can hence follow the same principle in
Section

3.1
to construct an equivalence test based on normal approximations. A special case of
Proposition

5
with
n=m
motivated the equivalence test of
23
. Our test can be viewed as a generalization of theirs to
n\neq m
. Such extension is natural, since
m
, which is the sample size for the empirical sample drawn from the target distribution
P
, often needs to be considerably larger than
n
to ensure minimal error due to finite-sample approximation of
P
. However, it is sometimes infeasible to augment the sample size
n
, for example, when generating more samples is computationally prohibitive, or even impossible due to experimental limitations.

The proposed test, which we call the
E-MMD-Normal
test, rejects
H_{0}
if
S_{n,m}^{\mathrm{MMD},\theta}<z_{\alpha}
, where

\displaystyle S_{n,m}^{\mathrm{MMD},\theta}\coloneqq\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{MMD}}}(\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q_{n},P_{m})-\theta^{2})\;,

(14)

and
z_{\alpha}
is the
\alpha
-th quantile of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{MMD}}
is the square root of an estimator for
\sigma_{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}
defined as
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}=N(4n^{-1}\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{MMD},1}^{2}+4m^{-1}\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{MMD},2}^{2})
, with

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{MMD},1}^{2}\;=\;\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(q_{i}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}q_{i^{\prime}}\bigg)^{2}\;,\qquad\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{MMD},2}^{2}\;=\;\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigg(p_{i}-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{m}p_{j^{\prime}}\bigg)^{2}\;,

(15)

and

\displaystyle q_{i}\;

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{(n-1)m(m-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime}=1\\
i^{\prime}\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{\prime}=1\\
j^{\prime}\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}h(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}},y_{j},y_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle p_{j}\;

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{n(n-1)(m-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime}=1\\
i^{\prime}\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{\prime}=1\\
j^{\prime}\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}h(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}},y_{j},y_{j^{\prime}})\;.

In particular,

and

is known to converge weakly to a standard Gaussian distribution

for MMD. The following bootstrapped test, called
E-MMD-Boot
, rejects
H_{0}
if
T_{n,m}^{\mathrm{MMD},\theta}>\eta
, where

\displaystyle T_{n,m}^{\mathrm{MMD},\theta}\;\coloneqq\;\theta-\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P_{m})\;,

(16)

and
\eta>0
is a critical value chosen to control the Type-I error.

The intuition behind the construction of the E-MMD-Boot is similar to the E-KSD-Boot test—the equivalence null hypothesis
H_{0}:\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)\geq\theta
is rejected for large values of
T_{n,m}^{\mathrm{MMD},\theta}
. One difference is that the test statistic
T_{n,m}^{\mathrm{MMD},\theta}
now involves an MMD statistic, rather than a KSD statistic. Consequently, the bootstrapping approach described in
Section

3.2
needs to be adjusted to compute the critical value.
We will first discuss how to choose such
\eta
, and then prove the validity and consistency of this test at the end of this section.

Our key observation is that it suffices to choose the critical value as the
(1-\alpha)
-th quantile
\eta_{1-\alpha}
of
S_{n,m}=\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q)+\mathrm{MMD}(P_{m},P)
. Since
\eta_{1-\alpha}
is also tractable, we introduce a bootstrapping approach to estimate it. First,
Lemma

3
implies that each of the terms
\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q)
and
\mathrm{MMD}(P_{m},P)
can be individually bootstrapped by the square-root of the bootstrap sample (
4
) with the Stein kernel
u_{p}
replaced by
k
. Specifically, the distribution of
\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q)
can be approximated using bootstrap samples of the form

\displaystyle D^{2}_{W_{n}}(\mathbb{X}_{n})\;\coloneqq\;\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}(W_{ni}-1)(W_{nj}-1)k(x_{i},x_{j})\;,

(17)

where
W_{n}=(W_{n1},\ldots,W_{nn})\sim\textrm{Multinomial}(n;1/n,\ldots,1/n)
. The distribution of
\mathrm{MMD}(P_{m},P)
can be estimated similarly. Therefore, given two sets of i.i.d. copies
\{W_{n}^{b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
and
\{\widetilde{W}_{m}^{b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
of size
B
drawn from
\textrm{Multinomial}(n;1/n,\ldots,1/n)
, we can compute the following bootstrap samples for
S_{n}

\displaystyle S^{b}_{n,m}\;\coloneqq\;D_{W_{n}^{b}}(\mathbb{X}_{n})+D_{\widetilde{W}_{m}^{b}}(\mathbb{Y}_{m})\;,

(18)

for
b=1,\ldots,B
. We then approximate the quantile
\eta_{1-\alpha}
of the distribution of
S_{n,m}
by the quantile
\eta^{B}_{1-\alpha}
of the bootstrap samples
\{S_{n,m}^{b}\}_{b=1}^{B}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\eta^{B}_{1-\alpha}\;=\;\inf\bigg\{u\in\mathbb{R}:\;\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbbm{1}\{S_{n,m}^{b}\leq u\}\geq 1-\alpha\bigg\}\;.

(19)

The validity of this bootstrap approach is justified by the following result, which is a direct consequence of
Lemma

3
. Its proof is included in
Section

B.2.3
. The full testing procedure is summarized in
Algorithm

4
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{\infty}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be a random sample where
X_{i}\sim Q
and
Y_{i}\sim P
are independent. For any
n,m
, let
Q_{n}
and
P_{m}
be the empirical measure based on
\mathbb{X}_{n}=\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\mathbb{Y}_{m}=\{Y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, respectively. Assume
0\leq k(x,y)\leq K
for some constant
K>0
, and suppose
5
holds. Define
S_{n,m}^{\ast}\coloneqq D_{W_{n}}(\mathbb{X}_{n})+D_{\widetilde{W}_{m}}(\mathbb{Y}_{m})
, where
D_{W_{n}}(\mathbb{X}_{n})
and
D_{\widetilde{W}_{m}}(\mathbb{Y}_{m})
are defined in (

) and
W_{n}
and

are independent. Then, for all
(Q\times P)
-almost-sure sequences

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\Pr(\sqrt{N}S_{n,m}^{\ast}\leq t\;|\;\mathbb{Z}_{\infty})-\Pr(\sqrt{N}S_{n,m}\leq t)\big|\;\to\;0\;.

5
A Minimal-Effect Approach for Selecting the Equivalence Margin

The equivalence margin
\theta
is a crucial design choice. Intuitively, it needs to be chosen so that the KSD or MMD balls includes the distributions that the user is willing to tolerate. In other words, it can be viewed as a form of model uncertainty. In general, designing an appropriate
\theta
is a challenging task that has received ongoing attention from not only the equivalence testing literature
(
98
;
62
;
29
)
, but also the closely related field of robust testing
(
31
;
70
)
.
In this section, we propose a data-driven approach, where
\theta
is chosen to be the smallest effect size that the test can detect with a pre-specified power.

Given
Q
such that
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)<\theta
(i.e., the alternative hypothesis is true), the power of the E-KSD-Boot test is defined as
\Pr(T_{n,m}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}>\gamma)
, where
\gamma
is an appropriate critical value controlling the Type-I error rate. The power of the other tests can be defined similarly. We address the following question: Given
\beta\in(0,1)
and observed samples
Q_{n}
(or
Q_{n}
and
P_{m}
for two-sample testing), how can we choose the equivalence bound
\theta
so that we can reject specific alternative distributions with power at least
1-\beta
?

We consider alternatives that are at most
\theta^{\prime}
away from
P
for some
\theta^{\prime}<\theta
, i.e.,
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\leq\theta^{\prime}
. In this case, the inequality (
13
) implies

\displaystyle T_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}\;=\;\theta-\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)\;

\displaystyle\geq\;\theta-\mathrm{MMD}(Q,Q_{n};u_{p})-\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\;\geq\;\theta-\theta^{\prime}-\mathrm{MMD}(Q,Q_{n};u_{p})\;,

where the last inequality holds since
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\leq\theta^{\prime}
. Writing for simplicity that
\xi_{n}\coloneqq\mathrm{MMD}(Q,Q_{n};u_{p})

\displaystyle\Pr(T_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}\;>\;\gamma_{1-\alpha})\;\geq\;\Pr(\theta-\theta^{\prime}-\xi_{n}>\gamma_{1-\alpha})\;=\;\Pr(\xi_{n}<\theta-\theta^{\prime}-\gamma_{1-\alpha})\;.

In particular, for the RHS to be at least
1-\beta
, we should choose
\theta
so that
\theta-\theta^{\prime}-\gamma_{1-\alpha}\geq\gamma_{1-\beta}
, where
\gamma_{1-\beta}
is the
(1-\beta)
-quantile of the distribution of
\xi_{n}
. In practice, such
\gamma_{1-\beta}
can be approximated using bootstrap samples as described in
Section

3.2
. To summarize, choosing
\theta=\theta^{\prime}+\gamma_{1-\alpha}+\gamma_{1-\beta}
guarantees an asymptotic test power of at least
1-\beta
for all
Q
with
\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)\leq\theta^{\prime}
. The following result shows that, assuming the exact quantiles are used (namely, ignoring bootstrap approximation errors), the test with
\theta
so selected achieves the desired power if
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\leq\theta^{\prime}
, while it still controls asymptotic Type-I error if
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)>\theta^{\prime}
. Its proof is in
Theorem

9
.

Theorem 9
.

Denote by
\gamma_{\rho}
the
\rho
-th quantile of the distribution of
\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q;u_{p})
, where
\rho\in(0,1)
. For any
\theta^{\prime}
and
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\leq\theta^{\prime}
, letting
\theta=\theta^{\prime}+\gamma_{1-\alpha}+\gamma_{1-\beta}
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr(T_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}>\gamma_{1-\alpha})\;\geq\;1-\beta\;.

(20)

Moreover, if instead
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)>\theta^{\prime}
, then

\displaystyle\Pr(T_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}>\gamma_{1-\alpha})\;\to\;0\;.

(21)

Remark 9.1
.

For example, when
\theta^{\prime}=0
, the induced equivalence margin is
\theta=\gamma_{1-\alpha}+\gamma_{1-\beta}
, and
Theorem

9
shows that the power guarantee (
20
) holds when
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)=0
, or equivalently
Q=P
, while the calibration (
21
) holds when
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)>0
, or equivalently
Q\neq P
.

Remark 9.2
.

Theorem

9
does not account for approximation errors due to the bootstrap step, since it concerns the (unknown) quantiles
\gamma_{1-\alpha},\gamma_{1-\beta}
instead of their bootstrap approximation. Consequently, the power guarantee (
20
) might not hold with finite samples in practice. However, we find in our experiments in
Section

6
that such power guarantee does hold with finite samples. In fact, it can sometimes be conservative, in the sense that it can yield a larger power by choosing an unnecessarily large
\theta
. We will discuss this further in
Section

6.1
.

Remark 9.3
.

This practice of choosing
\theta
based on a desired power has been adopted widely in applications such as psychology studies
(
61
;
62
)
, sometimes under the name the “
small-telescopes
” approach
(
98
)
. In that context,
\theta
is also called the
smallest effect size of interest
(SESOI). In some applications such as clinical trials, one might be able to infer an appropriate equivalence bound from pre-set regulations
(
79
;
74
;
29
)
.

The same idea can be applied to the MMD-based bootstrapping test in a straightforward manner. The only difference is that the critical value should be the
(1-\beta)
-th quantile of
S_{n,m}\coloneqq\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q)+\mathrm{MMD}(P_{m},P)
instead of
\xi_{n}
. We summarize this in the following result, proven in
Section

B.2.6
.

Theorem 10
.

For any
\rho\in(0,1)
, denote by
\eta_{\rho}
the
\rho
-th quantile of the distribution of
S_{n}=\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q)+\mathrm{MMD}(P_{n},P)
. For any
\theta^{\prime}
and
\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)\leq\theta^{\prime}
, letting
\theta=\theta^{\prime}+\eta_{1-\alpha}+\eta_{1-\beta}
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr(T_{n,m}^{\mathrm{MMD},\theta}>\eta_{1-\alpha})\;\geq\;1-\beta\;.

(22)

Moreover, if instead
\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)>\theta^{\prime}
, then

\displaystyle\Pr(T_{n,m}^{\mathrm{MMD},\theta}>\eta_{1-\alpha})\;\to\;0\;.

(23)

We now evaluate the proposed equivalence tests on numerical experiments. We use the IMQ kernel for the KSD-based tests and the RBF kernel for the MMD-based tests; these are standard choices in the literature; see, e.g.,
35
;
2
for KSD and
37
;
75
for MMD. All kernel bandwidths are selected using the median heuristic

where
\mathcal{D}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

\mathcal{D}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\cup\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

95\%

.

s_{p}(x)=-x+\mu

Sample size

We first evaluate the effect of sample sizes in

. We fix the equivalence margin
\theta
to be the population KSD value when

. We can see that both tests correctly controls the Type-I error when the KSD is greater than

. When the KSD equals
\theta
, namely
Q
lies at the boundary of the null set, E-KSD-Boot still controls Type-I error, even with a moderate sample size. In comparison, E-KSD-Normal fails to do so, even though both tests are shown to be valid asymptotically by

2
and

. This is because E-KSD-Normal is based on a CLT, which is only a good approximation when

. Instead, in this case, (

Proposition

). Therefore, the normal approximation will have non-negligible errors when
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)
is close to zero. This poor Type-I error control is also found in other equivalence tests based on asymptotic normality
(
23
, Section 4)
. In contrast, E-KSD-Boot does not suffer from this issue, as it does not rely on any normal approximations.

When the KSD is smaller than
\theta
, namely we are under
H_{1}
, both tests have non-trivial power, with the E-KSD-Normal test having the higher power. Importantly, the power of both tests converge to 1 as the sample sizes tend to infinity, which is expected given their consistency shown in

2
and

.

We use the proposed KSD-based equivalence tests to assess the goodness-of-fit of Gaussian-Bernoulli Restricted Boltzmann Machines (GB-RBM)

is a vector of observed variables,

is a binary hidden variable of latent dimension

, and the model parameters are

. The normalizing constant of a GB-RBM is in general intractable, because marginalizing over the hidden variable requires computing a sum of
2^{d^{\prime}}
terms, which is computationally prohibitive when
d^{\prime}
is large. Nevertheless, its score function admits a closed form:
s_{p}(x)=b-x+B\tanh(B^{\top}x+c)
, where
\tanh
is applied entry-wise. This makes GB-RBMs a common benchmark for assessing KSD-based GOF tests
(
68
;
53
;
85
;
70
)
.

We set
d=50
and
d^{\prime}=10
. The target distribution
P
is formed with randomly initialized
B,b,c
, where each entry of
b,c
is drawn independently from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and the entries of
B
are drawn from
\{\pm 1\}
with equal probability. The sampling distribution

has the same

, but the parameter

is replaced with the perturbed version
\tilde{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(b,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
, for some

from

using block Gibbs sampling as done in

and the noise levels on the x-axis; the purpose of the first plot is to demonstrate the calibration and power of the tests by showing

. Both tests have the correct Type-I error when

. For sufficiently small

7
Conclusion

We propose kernel-based equivalence tests that are suitable for evaluating the equivalence between distributions. Standard goodness-of-fit testing is only designed to assess the
lack
of goodness-of-fit, but cannot draw conclusions on the
equivalence
between observed data and the nominal distribution with probabilistic guarantees on the error rate. Our proposed tests, on the other hand, are shown to be asymptotically valid against null hypotheses that the data and the nominal distribution differ at least by some equivalence margin. Leveraging kernel-based statistical discrepancies, namely the kernel Stein discrepancy and the maximum mean discrepancy, our proposed suit of tests covers both the one-sample and two-sample testing scenarios. Since one challenge with these equivalence tests is the selection of an appropriate equivalence margin, we introduce a data-driven approach where an equivalence margin is selected to be the minimal effect size for achieving a pre-specified power against some certain alternatives.

We conclude with a discussion on some future directions.

•

Extension to other statistical discrepancies.
Our proof techniques extend naturally to generic one-sample and two-sample V-statistics. It would therefore be interesting to investigate other statistical discrepancies that admit such estimators when constructing similar equivalence tests. Examples include
energy distance

(
105
)
and the
Hilbert-Schmidt independence criterion

(
38
;
39
, HSIC,)
, both of which are closely connected to MMD
(
90
)
.

•

Alternative estimators.
As discussed in
Section

4.2
and
Section

3.2
, the normality-based tests can have uncontrolled Type-I error rates when
\theta
is small, because the CLT approximation of the KSD and MMD estimators deteriorates when
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)
and
\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)
are close to zero. One potential solution is to use alternative estimators that retain asymptotic normality even when
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)=0
or
\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)=0
, such as those based on
incomplete U-statistics

(
57
;
94
)
. Understanding whether these alternative estimators can improve the Type-I error control of these normality-based tests is another possible direction.

•

Selection of equivalence margin.
Finally, it is important to provide a more intuitive interpretation of the equivalence margin
\theta
selected using the minimal-effect approach. This is particularly relevant in fields such as bioequivalence studies, where statistical equivalence is often defined through explicit biological criteria
(
10
;
28
;
74
)
. Relating these criteria to the value of
\theta
chosen via the minimal-effect approach in
Section

5
may improve the interpretability and practical applicability of kernel-based equivalence tests.

Lemma 11

Let

A.3
Intuition behind the Proposed Bootstrapping Approach

We provide intuition for the choice of the bootstrap sample (
17
). To understand why this is the correct bootstrap statistic, we introduce the concept of
degeneracy

(
92
, Chapter 5)
. A symmetric function
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
(or its corresponding V-statistic) is said to be
Q
-
degenerate
if the function
g(x;Q)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q}[u(x,Y)]-\mathbb{E}_{X,Y\sim Q}[u(X,Y)]
is zero, i.e.,
g(x;Q)\equiv 0
, and
non-degenerate
otherwise. Crucially, the asymptotic behaviour of V-statistics varies depending on the degeneracy of
u
, and bootstrapping methods for each case have been well-studied
(
3
;
51
)
. A natural approach to construct a bootstrap sample for
\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q_{n},Q)
is then to identify it as a V-statistic and use the corresponding bootstrap statistic according to its degeneracy.

Using the closed-form expression for MMD, we can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q_{n},Q)\;

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\mathbb{E}_{Y,Y^{\prime}\sim Q}\big[h\big((x_{i},Y),(x_{j},Y^{\prime})\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}k(x_{i},x_{j})-\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q}[k(x_{i},Y)]-\mathbb{E}_{Y^{\prime}\sim Q}[k(Y^{\prime},x_{j})]+\mathbb{E}_{Y,Y^{\prime}\sim Q}[k(Y,Y^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}k(x_{i},x_{j};Q)\;,

where the second line follows by substituting the definition of the MMD kernel
h\big((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big)\coloneqq k(x,x^{\prime})+k(y,y^{\prime})-k(x,y^{\prime})-k(x^{\prime},y)
, and
\mathbb{E}_{Y,Y^{\prime}\sim Q}[k(Y,Y^{\prime})]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}k(x,y)Q(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)Q(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)
, and in the last line we have defined

\displaystyle k(x,y;Q)\;\coloneqq\;k(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q}[k(x,Y)]-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[k(X,y)]+\mathbb{E}_{Y,Y^{\prime}\sim Q}[k(Y,Y^{\prime})]\;.

It is straightforward to see that a generic
k
is
not

Q
-degenerate, whereas
k(x,y;Q)
is. To construct bootstrap samples for V-statistic
\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q_{n},Q)
, a naive approach would be to follow the classic construction for degenerate V-statistics
(
48
)
, which would correspond to replacing
k(x_{i},x_{j})
in (
17
) by
k(x_{i},x_{j};Q)
. However, this is
not
feasible in practice, as
k(x_{i},x_{j};Q)
involves expectations over
Q
, which are intractable.

Instead, we observe that
\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q_{n},Q)
is the second-order term in the
Hoeffding decomposition

(see, e.g.,
3
, Eq. (2.6))
of a non-degenerate V-statistic defined by
n^{-2}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}k(x_{i},x_{j})
. Classic bootstrapping results for each term in the Hoeffding decomposition
(
3
, Lemma 2.3)
then suggest that the correct bootstrap statistic for this term is (
17
).

. We first show the inequality (
13
), restated below

\displaystyle\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\;=\;\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P;u_{p})\;

\displaystyle\leq\;\mathrm{MMD}(Q,Q_{n};u_{p})+\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P;u_{p})

\displaystyle=\;\mathrm{MMD}(Q,Q_{n};u_{p})+\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)\;.

(25)

The first step holds by rewriting KSD as an MMD
(
8
, Theorem 1)
, and the inequality follows from the triangle inequality of MMD
(see, e.g.,
37
, Lemma 4)
.
Using this inequality, we can bound the probability of rejecting
H_{0}:\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\geq\theta
as

\displaystyle\Pr\big(T_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}>\gamma_{1-\alpha}\big)\;

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(\theta-\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)>\gamma_{1-\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)-\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)>\gamma_{1-\alpha}+\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)-\theta\big)

\displaystyle\leq\;\Pr\big(\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q;u_{p})>\gamma_{1-\alpha}+\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)-\theta\big)

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(\sqrt{n}\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q;u_{p})>\sqrt{n}\gamma_{1-\alpha}+\sqrt{n}(\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)-\theta)\big)\;,

(26)

By
Lemma

11
, the term
\sqrt{n}\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q;u_{p})
converges weakly to a non-degenerate distribution. In particular, its scaled quantile
\sqrt{n}\gamma_{1-\alpha}
also converges to some real number
(
106
, Lemma 21.2)
. On the other hand, the term
\sqrt{n}(\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)-\theta)
diverges to
\infty
when
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)>\theta
. Combining these arguments, we conclude that, when
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)>\theta
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr\big(\sqrt{n}\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q;u_{p})>\sqrt{n}\gamma_{1-\alpha}+\sqrt{n}(\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)-\theta)\big)\;=\;0\;.

We now consider the case
(ii)
, namely
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)=\theta
. In particular, this implies that
Q\neq P
, and so the following limit holds by
Proposition

1

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)-\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P))\;=\;\sqrt{n}(\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)-\theta)\;\to\;\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{KSD}})\;.

Moreover, as argued before,
\sqrt{n}\gamma_{1-\alpha}\to c
for some constant
c
. We then have

\displaystyle\Pr\big(\theta-\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)>\gamma_{1-\alpha}\big)\;=\;\Pr\big(\sqrt{n}(\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)-\theta)<-\sqrt{n}\gamma_{1-\alpha}\big)\;\to\;\Phi\Big(-\frac{c}{\sigma_{\mathrm{KSD}}}\Big)\;\eqqcolon\;a\;,

where
\Phi
denotes the CDF of the standard normal distribution. In particular, we must have
a>0
, as
-c/\sigma_{\mathrm{KSD}}
is a real number.
We claim that
a\leq\alpha
. Applying the inequality (
25
) again, we have the following bound on the probability of rejecting
H_{0}

\displaystyle\Pr\big(\theta-\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)>\gamma_{1-\alpha}\big)\;

\displaystyle\leq\;\Pr\big(\theta+\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q;u_{p})-\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)>\gamma_{1-\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q;u_{p})>\gamma_{1-\alpha}+\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)-\theta\big)

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q;u_{p})>\gamma_{1-\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=\;\alpha\;.

Taking the limit
n\to\infty
on both sides then implies
a\leq\alpha
, and so
a\in(0,\alpha]
. This shows
(ii)
.

To prove the case
(iii)
, we write

\displaystyle\;\Pr\big(\theta-\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)>\gamma_{1-\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)<\theta-\gamma_{1-\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q_{n},P)<(\theta-\gamma_{1-\alpha})^{2}\big)

(27)

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(\sqrt{n}\big(\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q_{n},P)-\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q,P)\big)<\sqrt{n}(\theta-\gamma_{1-\alpha})^{2}-\sqrt{n}\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q,P)\big)\;.

(28)

Assuming first that
0<\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)<\theta
, then
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[u_{p}(X,\cdot)]\not\equiv 0
, and thus
\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q_{n},P)
is a non-degenerate V-statistic; see, e.g., the proof of
68
for a proof for U-statistics; extensions to V-statistics is straightforward. Asymptotics of non-degenerate V-statistics
(
92
, Theorem 6.4.1 B)
then gives
\sqrt{n}\big(\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q_{n},P)-\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q,P)\big)\to\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{KSD}}^{2})
in distribution, where
\sigma_{\mathrm{KSD}}^{2}>0
. On the other hand, the RHS of the inequality within the probability in (
28
) can be simplified as

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}(\theta-\gamma_{1-\alpha})^{2}-\sqrt{n}\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q,P)\;=\;\sqrt{n}(\theta^{2}-\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q,P))+\sqrt{n}\gamma_{1-\alpha}^{2}-2\sqrt{n}\theta\gamma_{1-\alpha}\;.

By
Lemma

11
, the scaled quantile
\sqrt{n}\theta\gamma_{1-\alpha}
converges to a positive constant, while
\sqrt{n}\gamma_{1-\alpha}^{2}\to 0
. Since also
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)<\theta
, the RHS of the above equality diverges to
\infty
. We have therefore showed that, inside the probability of (
28
), the LHS converges weakly to a Gaussian limit, while the RHS diverges to
\infty
. This implies that
\Pr\big(\theta-\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)>\gamma_{1-\alpha}\big)\to 1
.

It then remains to prove
(iii)
when
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)=0
. In this case,
n\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q_{n},P)
converges weakly to a non-degenerate distribution by
Proposition

1
. In particular,
\mathrm{KSD}^{2}(Q_{n},P)\to 0
in probability. On the other hand,
\gamma_{1-\alpha}\to 0
in probability by
Lemma

11
, and so
(\theta-\gamma_{1-\alpha})^{2}\to\theta>0
. In other words, inside the probability in (
27
), the LHS converges to
0
in probability, while the RHS converges to a non-zero constant. This implies that (
27
) converges to
1
, thus showing the case
(iii)
when
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)=0
.

B.2

B.2.1
Proof of

This is a well-known result and a consequence of classic asymptotic theory for two-sample U-statistics; see, e.g.,

, and hence

\sigma_{1}>0
.
46
then imply that the first asymptotic limit in
Proposition

5
holds.

B.2.2
Proof of
Theorem

We again verify the assumptions in
Proposition

12
for MMD, which is sufficient to conclude the claimed results. For notational convenience, we define the sample sizes
n=n_{l}
and
m=m_{l}
to be functions of
l
. Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and
\{Y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be independent draws from
Q
and
P
, respectively. We consider the sequences
\{Q_{l}\}_{l=1}^{\infty}
and
\{P_{l}\}_{l=1}^{\infty}
, where
Q_{l},P_{l}
are empirical measures based on
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{l}}
and
\{Y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m_{l}}
, respectively. Moreover, choose
\hat{\sigma}_{l}=\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{MMD}}
as defined below (
14
).

Under
5
and
4
,
Proposition

5
shows that the assumed asymptotics hold for
\hat{D}_{l}=\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{l},P_{l};k)
, where in particular
\sigma_{\mathrm{MMD}}>0
when
Q\neq P
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{MMD}}=0
when

. It then remains to show that
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{MMD}}\to\sigma_{\mathrm{MMD}}
in probability. Under the assumed condition

4

B.2.3
Proof of
Lemma

7

As argued in the proof for
Lemma

3
, the conditional distribution of
U_{n}^{\ast}\coloneqq\sqrt{N}D_{W_{n}}(\mathbb{X}_{n})=\sqrt{n}D_{W_{n}}(\mathbb{X}_{n})
given
\mathbb{X}_{\infty}
and the unconditional distribution of
U_{n}\coloneqq\sqrt{n}\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q)
converge weakly to the same distribution, say
R
. That is, given a random variable
U\sim R
, we have
U_{n}^{\ast}\to U
and
U_{n}\to U
weakly. Similarly, denote by
R^{\prime}
the limiting distribution of
V_{n}^{\ast}\coloneqq\sqrt{n}D_{W_{n}}(\mathbb{Y}_{n})
given
\mathbb{Y}_{\infty}
and of
V_{n}\coloneqq\sqrt{n}\mathrm{MMD}(P_{n},P)
, and let
V\sim R^{\prime}
. We then have
V_{n}^{\ast}\to V
and
V_{n}\to V
. It suffices to show that
U_{n}^{\ast}+V_{n}^{\ast}\to U+V
and
U_{n}+V_{n}\to U+V
weakly and apply a triangle inequality to conclude the claimed result.

For a (real-valued) random variable
Z
, denote by
\phi_{Z}(t)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\exp(itZ)]
its characteristic function, where
i
denotes the imaginary unit. In particular,
\phi_{U}(t)=\mathbb{E}[\exp(itU)]
and
\phi_{U_{n}^{\ast}}(t)=\mathbb{E}[\exp(itU_{n}^{\ast})\;|\;\mathbb{X}_{\infty}]
. The weak convergence of
U_{n}^{\ast}
and
V_{n}^{\ast}
then implies
\phi_{U_{n}^{\ast}}(t)\to\phi_{U}(t)
and
\phi_{V_{n}^{\ast}}(t)\to\phi_{V}(t)
pointwise in
t
. Hence, the product
\phi_{U_{n}^{\ast}}(t)\phi_{V_{n}^{\ast}}(t)\to\phi_{U}(t)\phi_{V}(t)
also converges pointwise by the independence of
U
and
V
. Since by assumption
U_{n}^{\ast}
and
V_{n}^{\ast}
are independent given
\mathbb{X}_{n}
and
\mathbb{Y}_{n}
, this implies
\phi_{U_{n}^{\ast}}(t)\phi_{V_{n}^{\ast}}(t)=\phi_{U_{n}^{\ast}+V_{n}^{\ast}}(t)
and
\phi_{U}(t)\phi_{V}(t)=\phi_{U+V}(t)
, and therefore
U_{n}^{\ast}+V_{n}^{\ast}\to U+V
weakly by Lévy’s Continuity Theorem
(
107
, Theorem 2.13)
. A similar argument shows that
U_{n}+V_{n}
also converges weakly to the same limit, and hence the claimed result follows.

B.2.4
Proof of
Theorem

8

Using a similar argument as in the proof in
Section

B.1.6
, it suffices to show the claimed result with
\eta_{1-\alpha}^{\infty}
replaced by the
(1-\alpha)
-th quantile,
\eta_{1-\alpha}
, of
S_{n,m}
, due to the bootstrap validity shown in
Lemma

7
.

We first prove the case
(i)
, namely when
\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)>\theta
. Using the symmetry and triangle inequality of MMD
(
76
;
37
, see, e.g.,)
twice, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)\;\leq\;\mathrm{MMD}(Q,Q_{n})+\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P_{m})+\mathrm{MMD}(P_{m},P)\;=\;S_{n,m}+\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P_{m})\;.

(29)

Using this inequality, we can bound the probability of rejecting
H_{0}
as

\displaystyle\Pr\big(T_{n,m}^{\mathrm{MMD},\theta}>\eta_{1-\alpha}\big)\;

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(\theta-\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P_{m})>\eta_{1-\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)-\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P_{m})>\eta_{1-\alpha}+\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)-\theta\big)

\displaystyle\leq\;\Pr\big(S_{n,m}>\eta_{1-\alpha}+\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)-\theta\big)

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(\sqrt{N}S_{n,m}>\sqrt{N}\eta_{1-\alpha}+\sqrt{N}(\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)-\theta)\big)\;.

(30)

We claim that
\sqrt{N}S_{n,m}
converges weakly to a non-degenerate distribution. Indeed, by
5
and the definition of
S_{n,m}
, we can write
\sqrt{N}S_{n,m}=\nu_{n}^{-1/2}\sqrt{n}\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q)+(1-\nu_{n})^{-1/2}\sqrt{m}\mathrm{MMD}(P_{m},P)
, where
\nu_{n}\coloneqq n/N\to\nu
by
5
, and both
\sqrt{n}\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q)
and
\sqrt{m}\mathrm{MMD}(P_{m},P)
converges weakly to some non-degenerate distributions by
Lemma

11
. By the independence of
\mathrm{MMD}(P_{n},P)
and
\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{m},Q)
, the statistic
\sqrt{N}S_{n,m}
converges weakly to a non-degenerate distribution. In particular, its scaled quantile
\sqrt{N}\eta_{1-\alpha}
also converges to some real number
(
106
, Lemma 21.2)
. On the other hand, the term
\sqrt{N}(\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)-\theta)
diverges to
\infty
when
\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)>\theta
. Combining these arguments, we conclude that, when
\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)>\theta
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\Pr\big(\sqrt{N}S_{n,m}>\sqrt{N}\eta_{1-\alpha}+\sqrt{N}(\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)-\theta)\big)\;=\;0\;.

We now consider the case
(ii)
, namely
\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)=\theta
. In particular,
Q\neq P
, and
Proposition

5
implies that the following weak limit holds

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\big(\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P_{m})-\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)\big)\;=\;\sqrt{N}\big(\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P_{m})-\theta\big)\;\to\;\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{MMD}})\;.

Moreover, since
\sqrt{N}\eta_{1-\alpha}\to c
for some constant
c
as argued before, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\big(\theta-\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P_{m})>\eta_{1-\alpha}\big)\;=\;\Pr\big(\sqrt{N}(\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P_{m})-\theta)<-\sqrt{N}\eta_{1-\alpha}\big)\;\to\;\Phi\Big(-\frac{c}{\sigma_{\mathrm{MMD}}}\Big)\;\eqqcolon\;a\;,

where
\Phi
denotes the CDF of the standard normal distribution. In particular, we must have
a>0
.
We now claim that
a\leq\alpha
. Applying the inequality (
29
) again, we have the following bound on the probability of rejecting
H_{0}

\displaystyle\Pr\big(\theta-\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P_{m})>\eta_{1-\alpha}\big)\;

\displaystyle\leq\;\Pr\big(\theta+S_{n,m}-\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)>\eta_{1-\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(S_{n,m}>\eta_{1-\alpha}+\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)-\theta\big)

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(S_{n,m}>\eta_{1-\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=\;\alpha\;.

Taking the limit
N\to\infty
on both sides then implies
a\leq\alpha
. This shows
(ii)
. To prove the case
(iii)
, we write

\displaystyle\;\Pr\big(\theta-\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P_{m})>\eta_{1-\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P_{m})<\theta-\eta_{1-\alpha}\big)

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q_{n},P_{m})<(\theta-\eta_{1-\alpha})^{2}\big)

(31)

\displaystyle=\;\Pr\big(\sqrt{N}\big(\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q_{n},P_{m})-\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q,P)\big)<\sqrt{N}(\theta-\eta_{1-\alpha})^{2}-\sqrt{N}\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q,P)\big)\;.

(32)

Assuming first that
0<\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)<\theta
, then
\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q_{n},P_{m})
is a non-degenerate V-statistic
(
46
, Proposition 1)
. Asymptotics of non-degenerate V-statistics
(
46
, Proposition 1 (i))
then gives
\sqrt{N}\big(\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q_{n},P_{m})-\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q,P)\big)\to\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
in distribution, for some
\sigma^{2}>0
. On the other hand, the RHS of the inequality within the probability in (
32
) can be simplified as

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}(\theta-\eta_{1-\alpha})^{2}-\sqrt{N}\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q,P)\;=\;\sqrt{N}(\theta^{2}-\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q,P))+\sqrt{N}\eta_{1-\alpha}^{2}-2\sqrt{N}\theta\eta_{1-\alpha}\;.

By
Lemma

11
, the scaled quantile
\sqrt{N}\theta\eta_{1-\alpha}
converges to a positive constant, while
\sqrt{N}\eta_{1-\alpha}^{2}\to 0
. Since also
\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)<\theta
, the RHS of the above equality diverges to
\infty
. We have therefore showed that, inside the probability of (
32
), the LHS converges weakly to a Gaussian limit, while the RHS diverges to
\infty
. This implies that
\Pr\big(\theta-\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P_{m})>\eta_{1-\alpha}\big)\to 1
.

It then remains to prove
(iii)
when
\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)=0
. In this case, the V-statistics
\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q_{n},P_{n})
is
degenerate of order 2

(see, e.g.,
46
, Section 2)
, and the asymptotics of 2-degenerate V-statistics
(

imply that

converges weakly to a non-degenerate distribution. In particular,
\mathrm{MMD}^{2}(Q_{n},P_{m})\to 0
in probability. On the other hand,
\eta_{1-\alpha}\to 0
in probability by
Lemma

11
, and so
(\theta-\eta_{1-\alpha})^{2}\to\theta>0
. In other words, inside the probability in (
31
), the LHS converges to
0
in probability, while the RHS converges to a non-zero constant. This implies that (
31
) converges to
1
, thus showing the case
(iii)
when
\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)=0
.

B.2.5
Proof of
Theorem

9

First assume
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\leq\theta^{\prime}
. Inequality (
13
) implies

\displaystyle T_{n,m}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}\;=\;\theta-\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)\;

\displaystyle\geq\;\theta-\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q;u_{p})-\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\;\geq\;\theta-\theta^{\prime}-\xi_{n}\;,

(33)

where the last inequality holds since
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\leq\theta^{\prime}
, and we have defined
\xi_{n}\coloneqq\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q;u_{p})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr(T_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}\;>\;\eta_{1-\alpha})\;\geq\;\Pr(\theta-\theta^{\prime}-\xi_{n}>\eta_{1-\alpha})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{=}}\;\Pr(\xi_{n}<\eta_{1-\beta})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{=}}\;1-\beta\;,

where
(i)
holds since
\theta=\theta+\eta_{1-\alpha}+\eta_{1-\beta}
by definition, and
(ii)
holds as
\eta_{1-\beta}
is the
(1-\beta)
-th quantile of
\xi_{n}
. This shows the first claim.

Now assume
\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)>\theta^{\prime}
. The probability of rejection can be bounded as

\displaystyle\Pr(T_{n}^{\mathrm{KSD},\theta}\;>\;\eta_{1-\alpha})\;

\displaystyle=\;\Pr(\theta-\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)\;>\;\eta_{1-\alpha})

\displaystyle=\;\Pr(\theta^{\prime}+\eta_{1-\alpha}+\eta_{1-\beta}-\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)\;>\;\eta_{1-\alpha})

\displaystyle=\;\Pr(\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)\;<\;\theta^{\prime}-\eta_{1-\beta})\;.

(34)

The statistic
\xi_{n}=\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q;u_{p})\to\mathrm{MMD}(Q,Q;u_{p})=0
almost surely by the strong law of large numbers for V-statistics
(
58
)
. Hence, its quantile
\eta_{1-\beta}
also converges to 0. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KSD}(Q_{n},P)\;\to\;\mathrm{KSD}(Q,P)\;>\;\theta^{\prime}\;,

where the convergence is again by the law of large numbers. Combining these argument shows that the RHS of (
34
) converges to 0, thus showing the claimed result.

B.2.6
Proof of

10

An inequality similar to (

\displaystyle T_{n,m}^{\mathrm{MMD},\theta}\;=\;\theta-\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},P_{m})\;

\displaystyle\geq\;\theta-\mathrm{MMD}(Q_{n},Q)-\mathrm{MMD}(Q,P)-\mathrm{MMD}(P_{m},P)\;\geq\;\theta-\theta^{\prime}-S_{n,m}\;,

where the last inequality holds since

by replacing (

instead of

.

Appendix C
Computation of the MMD Asymptotic Variance

We show how (
15
) can be computed in
\mathcal{O}((n+m)^{2})
time. We provide a discussion for
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{MMD},1}^{2}
, and the same argument applies to
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{MMD},2}^{2}
.

The key observation is that
q_{i}
, defined below (
15
), can be simplified to double sums using the definition of the MMD kernel
h(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})=k(x,x^{\prime})+k(y,y^{\prime})-k(x,y^{\prime})-k(x^{\prime},y)
. Indeed,

\displaystyle(n-1)m(m-1)q_{i}\;

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime}=1\\
i^{\prime}\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{\prime}=1\\
j^{\prime}\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}h(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}},y_{j},y_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime}=1\\
i^{\prime}\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{\prime}=1\\
j^{\prime}\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}k(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}})+k(y_{j},y_{j^{\prime}})-k(x_{i},y_{j^{\prime}})-k(x_{i^{\prime}},y_{j})

\displaystyle=\;m(m-1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime}=1\\
i^{\prime}\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}k(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}})+(n-1)\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{\prime}=1\\
j^{\prime}\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}k(y_{j},y_{j^{\prime}})-(n-1)(m-1)\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{m}k(x_{i},y_{j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\quad-(m-1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime}=1\\
i^{\prime}\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k(x_{i^{\prime}},y_{j})\;.

Computing the first and third terms for all
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
requires
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}+nm)
. The second term requires
\mathcal{O}(m^{2})
and only needs to be computed once for all
q_{i}
. For the last term, we can rewrite it as

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime}=1\\
i^{\prime}\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k(x_{i^{\prime}},y_{j})\;=\;\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}k(x_{i^{\prime}},y_{j})-\sum_{j=1}^{m}k(x_{i},y_{j})\;=\;T_{1}+T_{i,2}\;.

Computing
T_{i,2}
for all
q_{i}
requires
\mathcal{O}(nm)
, while
T_{1}
only needs to be computed once, which also costs
\mathcal{O}(nm)
. Therefore, all
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}((n+m)^{2})
time. Since
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{MMD},1}^{2}
is the sample variance of
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, it can also be computed in
\mathcal{O}((n+m)^{2})
time.
