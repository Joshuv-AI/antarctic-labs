Loss of miR-204 Drives NPTX1-Dependent Mitochondrial Dysfunction and Neuronal Degeneration in Alzheimer’s Disease
Authors: Tae-Young Ha, Seung Min Lim, Hyunjun Park, Keun-A Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by progressive cognitive decline accompanied by synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss. Dysregulation of specific microRNAs (miRNAs) has been increasingly implicated in AD pathogenesis, suggesting that miRNA-mediated regulatory pathways may represent important mechanisms underlying neuronal vulnerability. Here, we identified mmu-microRNA-204-5p (miR-204) as a critical regulator of neuronal survival that is markedly downregulated in the hippocampus of 5xFAD mice. Through integrated bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation, we established neuronal pentraxin-1 (NPTX1) as a direct post-transcriptional target of miR-204. In primary cultured neurons, exposure to Aβ₁₋₄₂ oligomer (oAβ) drove marked upregulation of NPTX1 and precipitated neuronal injury, manifested by aberrant reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, collapse of mitochondrial membrane potential, diminished cell viability, and dendritic degeneration. Inhibition of miR-204 further exacerbated these pathological changes and engaged mitochondrial apoptotic signaling, as evidenced by Bax upregulation, cytochrome c release, and caspase-3 cleavage. Conversely, restoration of miR-204 expression or genetic knockdown of NPTX1 attenuated oxidative stress, preserved mitochondrial integrity, and restored neuronal survival. Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the most common cause of dementia worldwide, is characterized by progressive cognitive decline, synaptic dysfunction, and neuronal loss. Although amyloid-β (Aβ) accumulation and tau pathology are well-established pathological hallmarks of AD, increasing evidence indicates that oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction play central roles in AD-related neuronal degeneration (Islam et al. 2019; Spina et al. 2025). Excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) disrupts the mitochondrial membrane potential, impairs cellular bioenergetic homeostasis, and actives intrinsic apoptotic pathways, thereby accelerating neuronal death (Wen et al. 2025). Despite the recognized contribution of oxidative stress to AD pathogenesis, the upstream molecular regulators linking oxidative stress to neuronal apoptosis remain incompletely understood.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small non-coding RNAs that regulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level and have emerged as critical modulators of neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, and neurodegenerative processes (Jovanovic and Hengartner 2006; Swarbrick et al. 2019). Accumulating evidence demonstrates the widespread dysregulation of miRNAs in AD brains, suggesting that specific miRNAs contribute to disease progression through the modulation of oxidative stress responses and apoptotic signaling pathways (Zendjabil 2018; Lukiw 2023; Silvestro et al. 2019). However, the molecular mechanisms through which individual miRNAs regulate neuronal vulnerability in AD remain poorly understood.

To identify miRNAs potentially involved in AD-associated neuronal dysfunction, we performed miRNA profiling of the hippocampal tissues from 5xFAD mice, a widely used transgenic model of amyloid pathology. Among the dysregulated miRNAs identified, miR-204-5p (miR-204) attracted our attention because previous studies have implicated this miRNA in the regulation of oxidative stress and apoptosis in several pathological conditions (Du et al. 2018; Li et al. 2011; Lu et al. 2020; Xie et al. 2022).

Previous reports have shown that miR-204 participates in the regulation of cellular stress responses and mitochondrial homeostasis across multiple tissues (Gao et al. 2014; Li et al. 2011). Furthermore, dysregulation of miR-204 has been associated with various pathological conditions, including cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and retinal degeneration, where it influences ROS production and cell survival (Du et al. 2018; Gao et al. 2014; Li et al. 2011). Together, these observations suggest that miR-204 may serve as an important regulator of oxidative stress responses and mitochondrial integrity. However, whether miR-204 contributes to neuronal dysfunction and degeneration in AD remains unknown.

Neuronal pentraxin 1 (NPTX1) is a secreted synaptic protein belonging to the pentraxin family that participates in synapse remodeling and excitatory synaptic signaling (Gomez de San Jose et al. 2022; Ma et al. 2018). Previous studies have demonstrated that NPTX1 expression is induced under neuronal stress conditions and contributes to Aβ-induced synaptic dysfunction and apoptotic neuronal death (Abad et al. 2006). Consistently, increased NPTX1 expression has been reported in AD models and has been associated with neuronal vulnerability and synaptic degeneration. These findings suggest that NPTX1 may represent a critical mediator linking synaptic dysfunction to neuronal apoptosis in neurodegenerative conditions.

Bioinformatic analysis using TargetScan 8.0 predicted NPTX1 as a putative downstream target of miR-204. However, whether miR-204 regulates NPTX1 to modulate ROS generation, mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis in AD have not yet been determined.

In the present study, we showed that miR-204 was significantly downregulated in the hippocampus of 5xFAD mice and identified NPTX1 as a direct post-transcriptional target of miR-204, as validated by luciferase reporter assay. We examined the functional role of the miR-204-NPTX1 axis in Aβ-induced neuronal injury by assessing oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in primary hippocampal neurons. Our findings demonstrate that miR-204 deficiency upregulates NPTX1, resulting in aberrant ROS accumulation, disruption of mitochondrial membrane potential, and activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway in primary neurons. Collectively, these results reveal a previously unrecognized miR-204–NPTX1 regulatory axis that contributes to neuronal vulnerability and neurodegeneration in AD.

Materials and Methods

Animals

5xFAD transgenic mice (B6SJL background; The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) co-expressing human amyloid precursor protein (APP) with the Swedish (KM670/671NL), Florida (I716V), and London (V717I) mutations and presenilin-1 (PS1) with the M146L and L286V mutations driven by the Thy1 promoter were used in this study (Oakley et al. 2006). Both wild-type (WT) and 5xFAD mice were weaned at postnatal day 21(P21) and confirmed by genotyping via PCR of ear biopsy tissue.

All animals were housed under standard environmental conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10% relative humidity) with a 12-h light/dark cycle and given ad libitum access to food and water, with a maximal of five animals per cage. All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Gachon University Lee Gil Ya Cancer and Diabetes Institute (approval number: LCDI-2018-0048).

Genotyping

Genomic DNA was extracted from ear biopsy tissues of 2-month-old mice using 2.5 x EzWay Direct Lysis Buffer (Labiskoma, Daejeon, Korea; Cat. No. K0568096). PCR amplification was conducted using 2 × EzWay Direct PCR Master Mix (Labiskoma; Cat. No. K0568010), with APP- and PS1-specific primers. PCR products were separated on a 1.5% agarose gel, stained with StaySafe Nucleic Acid Gel Stain (Real Biotech, New Taipei City, Taiwan; Cat. No. RSS01), and visualized under UV illumination (Supplementary Fig. 1). Primer sequences used for genotying were as follows (5’−3’): APP (forward: AGGACTGACCACTCGACCAG; reverse: CGGGGGTCTAGTTCTGCAT) and PS1 (forward: AATAGAGAACGGCAGGAGCA; reverse: GCCATGAGGGCACTAATCAT).

miRNA Microarray Analysis

Total RNA was isolated from the hippocampal tissues of 4-month-old 5xFAD and wild-type (WT) mice using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany; Cat. No. 217084). RNA concentration, purity, and integrity (RIN) were assessed using NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). miRNA labeling and hybridization were conducted using the Agilent’s miRNA Labeling Kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions Microarrays were scanned using an Agilent DNA Microarray Scanner, and raw data were extracted using Agilent Feature Extraction software. Differential expressed miRNAs were identified using the GeneSpring software (Agilent Technologies).

Bioinformatic Analysis of miR-204 Target Genes

Potential target genes of miR-204 were predicted using the TargetScan 8.0 database. Functional enrichment analysis was subsequently performed using DAVID (Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery) based on Gene Ontology (GO) categories, including Biological Process (BP [FAT]), Cellular Component (CC [FAT]), and Molecular Function (MF [FAT]). A total of 365 predicted target genes were subjected to functional annotation clustering, and the top 10 clusters were selected based on enrichment scores. To identify genes potentially relevant to AD pathology, the resulting clusters were further categorized into three functional groups: synaptic function, neuronal biology, and signal transduction & transport. Genes present in all three categories were identified, yielding 74 overlapping genes.

To evaluate the potential association of these genes with AD, disease-related annotations were retrieved from the DisGeNET database. The following metrics were collected for each gene: Disease Specificity Index (DSI), Gene–Disease Association score (GDA score), Evidence Index (EI), and the number of supporting publications (PMIDs). Based on these disease-association metrics, 55 AD-related genes were selected for further analysis. For multi-dimensional analysis, DSI, GDA score, EI, and PMID count were used as input features. All variables were standardized using Z-score normalization prior to analysis. K-means clustering was performed with k = 3, determined based on the intrinsic data structure and biological interpretability. Multiple random initializations were applied to ensure clustering stability and minimize initialization bias. Euclidean distance was used as the distance metric, and representative genes were identified based on the minimum Euclidean distance to each cluster centroid.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA, including small RNAs, was extracted from the hippocampi of 4-month-old 5xFAD and WT mice using QIAzol Lysis Reagent (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany; Cat. No. 79306) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. miR-204-5p expression was quantified by TaqMan-based qRT-PCR using TaqMan assay MicroRNA Assays (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. No. 4366596) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The following TaqMan assay IDs were used: miR-204-5p (Assay ID: 000508) and U6 snRNA (Assay ID: 001973). Relative expression levels were normalized to U6 small nuclear RNA (snRNA) and quantified using the comparative Ct (2 − ΔΔCt) method.

For NPTX1 mRNA quantification, total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using standard reverse transcription protocol, and qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Real-time PCR Master Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan; Cat. No. QPK-201) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The primer sequences (5′–3′) used were as follows: NPTX1 forward, TACACCAACGGATCAGCGAG; NPTX1 reverse, GACTTGAGCCACATGCACAC; GAPDH forward, ACCACAGTCCATGCCATCAC; and GAPDH reverse, TCCACCACCCTGTTGCTGTA. Relative NPTX1 mRNA expression was normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the comparative Ct (2 − ΔΔCt) method.

Cell Culture

HEK293 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Welgene, Korea, LM001-05) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone Laboratories Inc., Logan, UT, USA; Cat. No. SH30919) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA; Cat. No. 15140-122) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere.

Primary hippocampal neurons (PHNs) and primary cortical neurons (PCNs) were isolated from embryonic day 17 (E17) Sprague–Dawley rat embryos as previously described (Park et al. 2022). Briefly, hippocampal and cortical tissues were dissected and mechanically dissociated into single-cell suspensions, then plated onto poly-D-lysine-coated culture dishes. Neurons were cultured in Neurobasal medium (Gibco, 21103-049) supplemented with 2% B-27 (Gibco; Cat. No. 17504-044), 0.5 mM L-glutamine (Gibco; Cat. No. 25030-081), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco; Cat. No. 15140-122) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Half of the culture medium was replaced every 3 days. All experiments were performed at 7 days in vitro (DIV7).

All procedures involving animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Gachon University Lee Gil Ya Cancer and Diabetes Institute (LCDI-2023-0131).

miRNA Transfection

Primary neurons at DIV7 were transfected with the miRNA-204 mimic, inhibitor, or negative control (NC) oligonucleotides using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA; Cat. No. 11668019), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Oligonucleotides were diluted in Opti-MEM and complexed with Lipofectamine for 20 min prior to application. After 6 h of incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO₂, the transfection medium was replaced with fresh culture medium. The cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for downstream analysis. All oligonucleotides were custom designed and synthesized by Bioneer (Daejeon, Korea).

Luciferase Assay

HEK293 cells were co-transfected with psiCHECK-2 vectors containing either WT or mutant (Mut) NPTX1 3′-UTR, together with miR-204 mimic, inhibitor, or NC oligonucleotides. psiCHECK2-NPTX1 3’-UTR WT was purchased from Addgene (Watertown, MA, USA). At 48 h post-transfection, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Glo® Luciferase Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA, Cat. No. E2920). Firefly luciferase activity (reporter) was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity (internal control), and the results were expressed relative to the NC.

Preparation of Oligomeric Aβ₁₋₄₂ (oAβ)

Oligomeric Aβ₁₋₄₂ (oAβ) was prepared as previously described (Benseny-Cases et al. 2012). Briefly, lyophilized synthetic Aβ₁₋₄₂ peptide (AnaSpec Inc., Fremont, CA, USA; Cat. No. 20276) was dissolved in hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP) at a concentration of 1 mM. HFIP was subsequently removed by vacuum evaporation using a Speed Vac concentrator (Ílshin Laboratory Co., Ltd., Ede, The Netherlands). The resulting peptide film was resuspended in anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to a concentration of 5 mM and then diluted in ice-cold Ham’s F-12 medium to a final concentration of 100 µM. The solution was incubated at 4 °C for 24 h with constant gentle agitation to promote oligomer assembly. Prior to use, the oligomeric stock was diluted to a working concentration of 3 µM in conditioned neuronal culture medium.

Cell Viability Assay

The viability of transfected primary neurons was assessed using the MTT assay, based on the colorimetric reduction of 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide. Primary neurons were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁴ cells/well. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well containing 200 µL of neurobasal medium. Plates were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ for 3 h, protected from light. Following incubation, the medium was carefully removed, and 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Duchefa Biochemie, Haarlem, Netherlands, D1370.1000) was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals. Finally, absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a multimode plate reader (Victor X4, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). All experiments were conducted in triplicate. The results are expressed as the percentage compared to the control.

Measurement of Intracellular ROS

Intracellular ROS levels were quantified using the DCFDA/H₂DCFDA Cellular ROS Detection Assay Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; Cat. No. ab113851) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Primary neurons were seeded in black 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL and transfected as described above. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were incubated with 5 µM H₂DCFDA in assay buffer for 45 min at 37 °C in the dark. After washing with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) to remove excess dye, representative fluorescent images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (ECLIPSE Ts2; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Quantification of fluorescence intensity from the images was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential (Δψm) Analysis

The mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) was assessed using JC-1 dye (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA; Cat. No. T3168) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, primary neurons were incubated with 3 µM JC-1 working solution at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. The cells were then washed twice with HBSS. Fluorescence was measured using a multimode plate reader (Victor X4, PerkinElmer) at excitation/emission (Ex/Em) wavelengths of 535/590 nm for red fluorescence (J-aggregates) and 485/530 nm for green fluorescence (monomers). Background fluorescence was subtracted from all readings. The mitochondrial membrane potential was calculated as the ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity (J-aggregate/monomer). A decrease in this ratio was interpreted as indicating mitochondrial depolarization and dysfunction. Representative fluorescence images were obtained following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 1x PBS and visualized using a fluorescence microscope (ECLIPSE Ts2, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

Mitochondria Morphology Analysis

Mitochondrial morphology and membrane integrity were assessed by MitoTracker staining using MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA; Cat. No. M22425) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primary hippocampal neurons were treated with oAβ in the presence or absence of miR-204 mimic for 48 h, then incubated with 200 nM MitoTracker at 37 °C for 30 min. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) to remove excess dye. Fluorescence images were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope (ECLIPSE Ts2; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). MitoTracker fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Western Blot Assay

Proteins were extracted from samples using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Calbiochem, San Diego, CA, USA; Cat. No. 535140-1MLCN) and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; Cat. No. P5726 and P0044). Protein concentrations were determined using a DC protein assay kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA; Cat. No. 5000205). Equal quantities of protein were denatured at 95 °C for 10 min, separated by 10–15% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in TBS-T (Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GAPDH, BAX, cytochrome c (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA; Cat. No. sc-32233, sc-493, sc-13156, respectively), NPTX1 (Bioss, Woburn, MA, USA; Cat. No. bs-4893R), and cleaved caspase-3 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; Cat. No. 9664) diluted in 3% BSA in TBS-T. After washing in TBS-T, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse or anti-rabbit; Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were subsequently visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents, including western chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Millipore; Burlington, MA, USA; Cat. No. WBKLS0050) and detected using an X-ray film (AGFA, Mortsel, Belgium) or an Amersham ImageQuant 800 imaging system (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). Band intensities were subsequently quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) and normalized to those of GAPDH. All western blot experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1; GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Normality of data distribution was assessed prior to parametric testing. Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired Student’s t-tests (parametric) or Mann–Whitney U tests (nonparametric), as appropriate. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent biological replicates. A p-values < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Mmu-miR-204-5p is Downregulated in the Hippocampus of 5xFAD Mice and Directly Targets NPTX1

To identify miRNAs dysregulated during the early stages of AD, microarray analysis was conducted using hippocampal tissues from 4-month-old 5xFAD mice which exhibit memory impairment and amyloid plaque deposition and age-matched WT controls (Oakley et al. 2006; Jawhar et al. 2012) (Fig. 1A). Among the 1,247 miRNAs analyzed, 184 (171 downregulated and 13 upregulated) were found to be differentially expressed in 5xFAD mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 1B). Notably, 71 downregulated miRNAs exhibited a fold change greater than 0.3, with mmu-miR-204-5p (miR-204) showing one of the most pronounced decreases (Fig. 1C-D). miR-204 is predominantly expressed in the central nervous system and has been implicated in signaling pathways relevant to AD pathology (Huang et al. 2024). To identify miR-204 target genes potentially associated with AD, predicted target were retrieved from the TargetScan database and subjected to functional enrichment analysis using DAVID, yielding 365 clustered genes based on GO categories, including BP FAT, CC FAT, and MF FAT. The top 10 enriched clusters were selected for further analysis (Fig. 1E) and subsequently categorized into three AD-relevant functional groups: synapse, neuron, and signal & transport. Overlapping analysis identified 74 genes shared across all three categories (Fig. 1F). To evaluate disease relevance, these 74 genes were screened against the DisGeNET database, yielding 55 AD-associated genes (data not shown). Multi-dimensional k-means clustering (k = 3) based on DSIG, GDA score, EI, and publication count identified three distinct gene clusters, with Cd2ap, NPTX1, and Dcc as representative genes for each cluster based on proximity to cluster centroids (Fig. 1G). Cd2ap showed a strong disease association, indicated by a high GDA score, but evidence demonstrating a direct association with apoptosis was limited. Dcc exhibited relatively low disease specificity and a weak gene-disease association. In contrast, NPTX1 exhibited moderate-to-high disease specificity and association strength and has been extensively linked to AD-related synaptic dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis in previous studies. Moreover, NPTX1 mapped closest to its cluster centroid, indicating that it best represents the overall characteristics of the AD-associated gene set identified in this analysis. Based on these criteria, NPTX1 was selected as a primary candidate for investigating the functional relationship between miR-204 in neuronal cell death.

NPTX1, a member of the neuronal pentraxin family, has been implicated in synaptic remodeling and stress-associated neuronal apoptosis (Gomez de San Jose et al. 2022; Farhy-Tselnicker et al. 2017; Figueiro-Silva et al. 2015), indicating its relevance to neurodegenerative processes. miRNAs regulate gene expression by binding to complementary sequences within the 3′-UTRs of target mRNAs, leading to translational repression or mRNA degradation (Bartel 2004; Shukla et al. 2011). NPTX1 was identified as a potential downstream target of miR-204 and was found to contain a conserved miR-204 binding site within its 3′-UTR (Fig. 1H). Dual-luciferase reporter assay was conducted to validate this direct interaction. HEK293 cells were co-transfected with psiCHECK-2 constructing either the WT or Mut NPTX1 3′-UTR, together with miR-204 mimic or an inhibitor. Co-transfection of the miR-204 mimic significantly reduced luciferase activity in cells expressing the WT NPTX1 3’-UTR, while mutation of the predicted binding site abolished this effect (Fig. 1I). These findings confirm that miR-204 directly targets the NPTX1 3′-UTR.

To corroborate the microarray findings, miR-204 and NPTX1 expression levels were analyzed by qRT-PCR. Consistent with the microarray data, miR-204 expression was significantly decreased, while NPTX1 mRNA level was significantly elevated in the hippocampus of 5xFAD mice relative to WT controls (Fig. 1J, K). Western blot analysis further confirmed a significant increase in NPTX1 protein levels in the hippocampus of 5xFAD mice (Fig. 1L). Collectively, these findings demonstrate an inverse correlation between miR-204 and NPTX1 expression, suggesting that dysregulation of the miR-204-NPTX1 axis may contribute to AD-associated pathological changes.

miR-204 Ameliorates oAβ-Induced Neuronal Toxicity in Primary Neurons

To determine whether the miR-204-NPTX1 axis is involved in Aβ-induced neurotoxicity, primary hippocampal neurons (PHN) were treated according to the experimental scheme shown in Fig. 2A. Western blot analysis showed that exposure to oAβ markedly increased NPTX1 protein expression, whereas co-treatment with a miR-204 mimic attenuated this upregulation (Fig. 2B), suggesting that miR-204 negatively regulates NPTX1 under Aβ-induced conditions.

Previous studies have reported that NPTX1 contributes to Aβ-induced neuronal damage and is overexpressed in dystrophic neurites in AD brains (Abad et al. 2006). To examine whether miR-204 affects neuronal morphology under Aβ-induced stress, PHN were immunostained with an anti-MAP2 antibody and dendritic morphology was analyzed. oAβ treatment significantly impaired dendritic morphology, as evidenced by reduced total intersections and total dendritic length compared with the negative control (NC) group. Notably, miR-204 mimic-transfection significantly restored dendritic complexity and attenuated oAβ-induced dendritic degeneration (Supplementary Fig. 2). Consistent with these morphological findings, oAβ treatment significantly reduced neuronal viability compared with the NC group, whereas transfection of the miR-204 mimic markedly attenuated oAβ-induced cytotoxicity, indicating a neuroprotective effect of miR-204 (Fig. 2C). miR-204 mimic treatment alone had no significant effect on NPTX1 expression, dendritic morphology, or cell viability compared with the NC group (Fig. 2B–C and Supplementary Fig. 2).

Accumulating evidence suggests that oAβ induces excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and subsequent neuronal death in AD (Wen et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2014). To determine whether miR-204 affects oxidative stress, intracellular ROS levels were measured using DCFDA staining. oAβ treatment significantly increased intracellular ROS levels, as reflected by increased DCFDA fluorescence intensity, whereas miR-204 mimic transfection markedly attenuated oAβ-induced ROS accumulation (Fig. 2D), indicating that miR-204 confers protection against oxidative stress. Because oxidative stress is closely linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, mitochondrial integrity was further evaluated using MitoTracker staining and JC-1 assays. MitoTracker analysis demonstrated that oAβ exposure reduced mitochondrial mass and disrupted mitochondrial network integrity, whereas miR-204 mimic treatment substantially restored mitochondrial signals (Fig. 2E). Consistently, JC-1 analysis showed that oAβ significantly dissipated mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), as indicated by a reduction in JC-1 aggregate fluorescence. In contrast, miR-204 mimic treatment significantly restored mitochondrial membrane potential toward baseline levels (Fig. 2F).

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that miR-204 protects neurons from oAβ-induced toxicity by suppressing NPTX1 expression, reducing oxidative stress, and preserving mitochondrial integrity.

Downregulation of miR-204 Results in Upregulation of NPTX1 and Reduced Neuronal Viability

To determine whether the oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction induced by miR-204 deficiency engage intrinsic apoptotic signaling, the expression of apoptosis-related proteins was examined by western blot analysis. miR-204 inhibition significantly increased the levels of BAX, cytochrome c, and cleaved caspase-3, indicative of mitochondria-dependent apoptotic pathway activation. These effects were markedly attenuated by co-transfection with the miR-204 mimic (Fig. 4D). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that miR-204 deficiency promotes oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and subsequent activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway in primary neurons.

Rescue experiments were performed in PCN using an NPTX1-specific siRNA (siNPTX1) (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, NPTX1 knockdown significantly improved neuronal viability compared with miR-204 inhibitor treatment alone (Fig. 5D), with similar protective effects observed in PHN (Supplementary Fig. 6). Western blot analysis further revealed that NPTX1 knockdown significantly attenuated the miR-204 inhibition-induced upregulation of BAX, cytochrome c, and cleaved caspase-3 (Fig. 5E), confirming suppression of mitochondria-dependent apoptotic signaling.

Discussion

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by progressive synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss that begins years before the onset of clinical symptoms. Accumulating evidence indicates that oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction represent early pathological drivers of neurodegeneration. Identifying the upstream molecular regulators that link oxidative stress to mitochondrial apoptotic signaling may therefore provide important insights into disease progression and potential therapeutic strategies.

In the present study, we found that miR-204 was significantly downregulated in the hippocampus of 4-month-old 5xFAD mice, corresponding to an early stage of AD pathology. Reduced miR-204 expression has also been reported in postmortem AD brain tissue (Tasdelen et al. 2022), and significantly decreased miR-204-5p levels have been detected in CSF-derived exosomes from patients with young-onset AD and frontotemporal dementia (Tan et al. 2021). Together, these observations support the pathological relevance of miR-204 dysregulation in human neurodegenerative disorders.

miRNAs function as post-transcriptional regulators that fine-tune cellular stress responses and neuronal survival pathways (Angelucci et al. 2019). miR-204 has previously been implicated in the regulation of apoptosis and oxidative stress, and mitochondrial homeostasis (Wang et al. 2015; Hu et al. 2024; Behrendt 2022), and has been shown to regulate mitochondrial autophagy and ROS production through the TRPML1-STAT3 signaling pathway in experimental AD models (Liu et al. 2021), highlighting its role in maintaining mitochondrial integrity. Consistent with these reports, we demonstrated that miR-204 suppression in primary neurons reduced cell viability, whereas the restoration of miR-204 expression significantly improved neuronal survival, suggesting that loss of miR-204 increases neuronal vulnerability to pathological stress.

Mechanistically, we identify NPTX1 as a direct post-transcriptional target of miR-204. NPTX1 has been implicated in synaptic remodeling and stress-related neuronal apoptosis, including processes associated with oxidative damage and excitotoxicity (Kirkpatrick et al. 2000; Figueiro-Silva et al. 2015; Hossain et al. 2004). Notably, a prior study demonstrated that loss of miR-204 drives NPTX1-dependent mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal degeneration in AD (McKeever et al. 2018), establishing NPTX1 as a key mediator of miR-204-associated neurotoxicity. Consistent with these findings, our luciferase reporter and expression analyses confirm that miR-204 directly regulates NPTX1, thereby establishing a functional miR-204–NPTX1 regulatory axis in neurons.

miR-204 deficiency induced pronounced ROS accumulation and mitochondrial membrane depolarization, accompanied by activation of intrinsic apoptotic signaling, as indicated by increased BAX expression, cytochrome c release, and caspase-3 cleavage. Rescue experiments further demonstrated that NPTX1 knockdown significantly attenuated ROS accumulation, restored the mitochondrial membrane potential, and suppressed mitochondrial apoptotic signaling. Previous studies have suggested that NPTX1 promotes BAX-associated mitochondrial apoptotic signaling under neuronal stress conditions (DeGregorio-Rocasolano et al. 2001; Clayton et al. 2012). Consistent with these reports, NPTX1 knockdown in our study reduced BAX expression, cytochrome c release, and caspase-3 activation, supporting the involvement of a BAX-mediated mitochondrial apoptotic pathway downstream of NPTX1. Furthermore, emerging evidence implicates miR-204 in the regulation of oxidative stress-related mitochondrial pathways (Zhang et al. 2021). Our findings extend these observations by demonstrating that NPTX1 serves as a critical downstream effector linking miR-204 deficiency to oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that the miR-204–NPTX1–ROS axis mechanistically connects oxidative stress to mitochondrial apoptotic activation during early AD pathology. Given that oxidative stress and mitochondrial vulnerability are central drivers of neurodegeneration, modulation of miR-204 signaling may represent a potential strategy for restoring redox homeostasis and protecting neuronal integrity.

Several limitations of the present study warrant acknowledgment. First, although miR-204 expression was analyzed in hippocampal tissue from 5xFAD mice, the specific cellular source of miR-204 was not determined. The observed reduction therefore reflects tissue-level changes and does not distinguish between neuronal and non-neuronal cell populations. Although our functional studies in primary neuronal cultures demonstrated direct effects of miR-204 modulation on NPTX1 expression, oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, and neuronal survival, future studies employing cell type-specific approaches, such as in situ hybridization, fluorescence-activated cell sorting, or single-cell transcriptomic analyses, will be needed to define the precise cellular distribution of miR-204 in the AD brain.

Second, although NPTX1 knockdown significantly attenuated the detrimental effects induced by miR-204 deficiency, additional downstream targets and signaling pathways may contribute to miR-204-mediated neuroprotection. Further investigation will be required to identify these mechanisms and determine their relative contributions to AD pathogenesis.

Third, the neuroprotective effects of miR-204 were evaluated primarily in primary neuronal cultures. Although reduced miR-204 expression and elevated NPTX1 levels was observed in the hippocampus of 5xFAD mice, the therapeutic efficacy of miR-204 restoration was not directly examined in vivo. Future studies using in vivo delivery of miR-204 mimics or genetic modulation in AD animal models will be needed to determine whether miR-204 restoration can ameliorate cognitive impairment, synaptic dysfunction, and neurodegeneration in the diseased brain.

Finally, although dysregulation of the miR-204-NPTX1 axis was demonstrated in 5xFAD mice and primary neuronal cultures, its clinical relevance was not directly validated in human AD brain tissue or cerebrospinal fluid. Future studies incorporating human cohorts will be necessary to evaluate whether this axis represents a clinically relevant biomarker or therapeutic target in AD.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides evidence that dysregulation of the miR-204-NPTX1 pathway contributes to oxidative stress-mediated neuronal injury in AD, and suggest that targeting this axis may represent a novel therapeutic approach for AD.

Conclusion

Overall, the present study demonstrates that the miR-204–NPTX1 signaling axis contributes to oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptotic activation during early AD pathology. Restoration of miR-204 expression or suppression of NPTX1 significantly attenuated oxidative stress, preserved mitochondrial function, and improved neuronal survival, highlighting the pathological importance of this regulatory pathway. These findings suggest that modulation of the miR-204/NPTX1 axis may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for mitigating redox-driven neurodegeneration in AD. Further studies using human samples and in vivo models are warranted to validate the clinical relevance and therapeutic potential of this pathway.
