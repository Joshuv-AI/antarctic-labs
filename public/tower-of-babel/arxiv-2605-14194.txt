GradShield: Alignment Preserving Finetuning
Zhanhao Hu, Xiao Huang, Patrick Mendoza, Emad A. Alghamdi, Basel Alomair, Raluca Ada Popa, David Wagner

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) pose a significant risk of safety misalignment after finetuning, as models can be compromised by both explicitly and implicitly harmful data. Even some seemingly benign data can inadvertently steer a model towards misaligned behaviors. To address this, we introduce GradShield, a principled filtering method that safeguards LLMs during finetuning by identifying and removing harmful data points before they corrupt the model’s alignment. It removes potentially harmful data by computing a Finetuning Implicit Harmfulness Score (FIHS) for each data point and employs an adaptive thresholding algorithm. We apply GradShield to multiple utility fine-tuning tasks across varying levels of harmful data and evaluate the safety and utility performance of the resulting LLMs using various metrics. The results show that GradShield outperforms all baseline methods, consistently maintaining an Attack Success Rate (ASR) below 6 % 6\% while preserving utility performance.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have advanced rapidly in recent years, demonstrating impressive text-generative capabilities across various tasks
(
10
;
34
)
. These models, such as GPT-4
(
29
)
and Llama 3
(
12
)
, are pretrained on vast amounts of data, enabling them to respond to users’ prompts and generally follow their instructions. However, this strong capability also raises concerns about the potential to induce harmful behavior, including malicious use of tools and facilitation of spam and misinformation.
To address these concerns, techniques such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
(
30
)
or Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
35
)
have been employed to introduce safety alignments into the models, ensuring their outputs comply with ethical guidelines. Meanwhile, multiple safety benchmarks, such as AnthropicRedTeam
(
9
)
and AdvBench
(
46
)
, have been proposed to evaluate the safety performance of LLMs.

However, LLM safety alignment is brittle and can be easily bypassed
(
19
;
33
)
. As LLMs have been widely adopted in downstream applications such as chatbots and code generation, it is common to fine-tune these models on domain-specific datasets to enhance their utility on specific tasks. Users can download and finetune open weight LLMs such as Llama 3, or use APIs provided by companies like OpenAI to customize proprietary models. This finetuning process can introduce vulnerabilities into models due to harmful data present in the finetuning datasets. With a few epochs of finetuning on a small number of harmful data points, LLMs catastrophically forget their safety alignment and compromise to generate harmful content
(
44
;
33
)
. This raises significant concerns about the safety and reliability of LLMs in real-world applications, as the user may intentionally or inadvertently include harmful content in their finetuning datasets. It is especially challenging for API providers, as they aim to preserve utility while avoiding misalignment.

A straightforward idea to mitigate these risks is to use a content filter
(
37
;
5
)
to remove harmful content from the finetuning datasets. However, recent studies have shown that there is a gap between the explicit harmfulness of the content and the consequential harmfulness that the model can learn during finetuning
(
32
;
14
)
. Some data that appear benign may nonetheless erode a model’s safety alignment, raising the critical challenge of how to identify and mitigate the implicit harmfulness that can arise during fine-tuning. Other existing methods in this field, including an additional alignment stage
(
22
;
23
)
and post-hoc weight adjustment
(
16
;
25
)
, often find it difficult to balance the trade-off between safety and utility.

In this paper, we propose GradShield to preserve LLM’s safety alignment during finetuning. The key idea is to measure the impact of finetuning data on the safety alignment during finetuning and thus exclude the data that are highly likely to degrade safety alignment (
Figure

1
). This approach is particularly suitable for model providers offering finetuning services for users’ customized datasets and want to ensure the safety of the resulting model without compromising its utility at the same time. We assume the availability of a reliable safety evaluation. As long as the finetuned model passes the safety evaluation, the model provider may release it to users. Therefore, in most cases where the finetuning dataset is benign, no further action is required. When the dataset is contaminated, we propose using a Finetuning Implicit Harmfulness Score (FIHS) for each finetuning data point to quantify its impact on the misalignment, and filter most harmful ones out.

FIHS is computed as the dot product between two gradients with respect to the model weights: a gradient update on this data point during finetuning and the gradient of a differentiable proxy safety score evaluated on a held-out set of safety probing data. We empirically found that it is robust to the choice of the safety probing data, and that even a single data point is sufficient to compute a reliable FIHS.
Moreover, we theoretically proved that FIHS is an approximation of a principled leave-one-out
24
;
31
harmfulness measure that captures the change in safety alignment of the model after finetuning with and without the data point. Computing FIHS requires one gradient pass per data point; thus, it incurs a cost comparable to finetuning for one epoch over the entire dataset.
To adapt to typical scenarios where holding a validation set is unavailable, we develop an adaptive thresholding strategy that removes high FIHS data points without requiring any knowledge of the dataset’s composition. It is done by combining binary search with a heuristic guess of the threshold. Empirically, in typical cases, a single search is sufficient. When the ratio of harmful data points is high, the binary search quickly converges to a better threshold in one or two more iterations.

While FIHS shares a similarity with influence functions
24
;
31
in that both quantify the impact of individual training points via gradient-based approximations, there are several fundamental differences. First, classical influence functions analyze perturbations to a static empirical risk minimizer and rely on second-order information via Hessian inversion, making them computationally expensive and difficult to apply to non-convex LLMs
26
; in contrast, FIHS captures the optimization trajectory during finetuning and uses a first-order gradient formulation, yielding computation efficiency. Second, influence functions are task-agnostic, whereas FIHS is tailored to safety alignment via a differentiable proxy objective, yielding clear signals. Together, these distinctions make FIHS a more practical and faithful measure of data influence in finetuning settings.

Figure 1
:
GradShield is well-suited for defending API finetuning. It protects the safety alignment of LLMs during finetuning by measuring FIHS of each user’s data point and filtering out data points with high FIHS from the finetuning dataset.

We evaluated GradShield in multiple scenarios. We combine utility datasets and harmful datasets to simulate real-world finetuning datasets that may contain both benign and harmful data points. We adopt four different utility datasets, three harmful datasets with varying types of harmfulness, and different harmful data point ratios to construct various finetuning datasets. We apply GradShield to protect LLMs’ alignment during finetuning on these datasets, and compare it with multiple baseline methods. Our results demonstrate that GradShield can effectively preserve the safety alignment of LLMs while ensuring their utility, significantly surpassing baseline methods. For most settings, GradShield relies solely on a one-time heuristic estimate of the threshold, making it computationally efficient and practical.

Related Work

Safety alignment
of large language models (LLMs) refers to constraining a pre-trained model to provide helpful and truthful outputs while refusing prompts that elicit policy-violating content. The standard alignment pipeline involves instruction tuning via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to instill compliance, followed by reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
(
30
)
to reinforce desired behaviors and discourage unsafe ones. However, downstream adaptation—now commonplace in both open- and closed-weight settings—can erode these safeguards. A large body of evidence shows that even benign task fine-tuning reduces refusal behavior and increases unsafe responses, and that measured safety is highly sensitive to seemingly minor evaluation choices
(
33
;
8
)
.

Fine-tuning attacks

Prior studies have shown that fine-tuning an LLM can significantly weaken its safety mechanisms
(
33
)
. This effect has been observed not only when models are fine-tuned on adversarial datasets designed to circumvent safeguards
(
44
;
33
)
, but also when they are fine-tuned on benign datasets intended for domain adaptation
(
15
)
.
(
15
)
proposed a similarity-based filtering method that selects the top-K samples from benign datasets based on their proximity to curated harmful examples and their distance from curated safety examples. Their findings demonstrate that fine-tuning large language models on these filtered samples can lead to a notable degradation in safety alignment. Adopting an outlier detection approach,
13
shows that fine-tuning an LLM on as few as 100 targeted outlier samples from benign datasets can subtly steer the LLM’s parameters into an undesirable harmful zone.
More recently,
14
showed a stronger attack, embedding hidden harmful behaviors in ciphertexts that evade standard safety checks.

Defenses against fine-tuning attacks
generally fall into three categories.

Alignment-stage
preemptively alters the base model to resist gradient influence by improving robustness in the alignment stage. Works in this category include CTRL
(
27
)
, RepNoise
(
36
)
, Vaccine
(
23
)
, Booster
(
22
)
, and SDD
(
3
)
.

Fine-tuning-stage
modifies the optimization process of finetuning to prevent a drop in model alignment. This could be achieved through constraining the finetuned model shift away from the aligned model
(
32
;
28
;
25
)
, as well as adding alignment data to the finetuning dataset
(
2
;
21
;
42
)
. Another sub-category focuses on filtering finetuning data using moderation tools on model output or internal embedding
(
20
)
.

Post-Finetune-stage
adjusts the model after fine-tuning, preserving both utility and alignment by projecting, merging, or masking weights to recover safe behavior with minimal retraining
(
43
;
16
)
.

3
Methodology

3.1
preliminaries

Suppose we have a model
F_{{\bm{\theta}}}

and a finetuning dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{f}}=\{{\bm{x}}_{\text{f}}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. The finetuning process aims to optimize the model parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
by minimizing the loss function on the finetuning dataset:

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{\text{f}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{f}}}\left[L({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}})\right].

(1)

The safety alignment of a model is evaluated on a safety benchmark dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{s}}=\{{\bm{x}}_{\text{s}}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, which consists of harmful prompts. The safety score is defined by taking a standard safety evaluation function
S
on the model’s response to the harmful prompts:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Safety}({\bm{\theta}},\mathcal{D}_{\text{s}})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{\text{s}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{s}}}\left[S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{\text{s}}))\right].

(2)

We assume that the safety evaluation is a gold standard, meaning that the safety evaluation score accurately reflects the extent of the model’s safety alignment. We do not consider the backdoor attacks or cipher-based attacks that can bypass the safety evaluation. Our goal is to ensure that after finetuning on a subset of the users’ data
\mathcal{D}_{\text{f}}^{\text{sub}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\text{f}}
, the safety score is above a certain threshold while maintaining the utility of the model. Therefore, we need to find a ranking function that can measure the Finetuning Implicit Harmfulness Score (FIHS) of each finetuning data point
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{f}}
. We then remove data points with high FIHS from the finetuning dataset to obtain
\mathcal{D}_{\text{f}}^{\text{sub}}
.

3.2
Finetuning Implicit Harmfulness Score

We define the FIHS score of a finetuning data point
{\bm{x}}_{\text{f}}
by the leave-one-out principle:

\displaystyle\mathrm{FIHS^{*}}({\bm{x}}_{\text{f}})=\mathbb{E}_{\text{ft}}[\mathrm{Safety}({\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{\{\backslash{\bm{x}}_{\text{f}}\}},\mathcal{D}_{\text{s}})]-\mathbb{E}_{\text{ft}}[\mathrm{Safety}({\bm{\theta}}^{*},\mathcal{D}_{\text{s}})],

(3)

where
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}
is fine-tuned on the finetuning dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{f}}
, and
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{\{\backslash\bm{x}_{\text{f}}\}}
is fine-tuned on the finetuning dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{f}}\backslash{\bm{x}}_{\text{f}}
that excludes the data point
{\bm{x}}_{\text{f}}
. The expectation is taken over all randomness during the finetuning process, such as model initialization and data shuffling. As long as the model has not been saturated on finetuning the harmful data points, we expect that removing a harmful data point from the finetuning dataset will increase the safety score, leading to a high FIHS value. However, directly estimating this score by principle is not computationally feasible, as one needs to finetune the whole dataset multiple times for each data point. To tackle this issue, we introduce the following theory:

Theorem 3.1

Given the assumptions:

•

The safety score function
S
is differentiable with respect to the model weights
{\bm{\theta}}
.

•

The gradient mapping
g(\cdot)
for updating on each finetuning step can be bounded by a constant, and the learning rate
\eta
is a constant and is small.

We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{FIHS^{*}}(\bm{x}_{\mathrm{f}})=\eta\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{s}}}\left[\mathrm{dot}\left(g\left(\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}L({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}\right),\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))\right)\right]+O(n\eta^{2})

where the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}}
is over the distribution of model parameters encountered during finetuning.
n
indicates the finetuning steps and
\eta
indicates the learning rate.

The proof for this theorem can be found in Appendix
B
.
Intuitively, the inspected data point
{\bm{x}}_{\text{f}}
is considered harmful if the parameter update induced by this data point during finetuning,
-\eta g\left(\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}L({\bm{\theta}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}\right)
, is in the opposite direction of the gradient to the safety score on the safety benchmark dataset,
\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}})
.

This estimation of FIHS is still computationally expensive, as it requires computing the gradient of the safety score on the entire safety benchmark dataset for each model checkpoint during finetuning. To reduce computational cost, we use only one probing safety data point (See
Section

A.4
for justification) and utilize the initial model before fine-tuning to compute FIHS. We empirically find that it works well in practice. Moreover, since the initial model is usually well-aligned, it is guaranteed not to be saturated on finetuning harmful data, which is required by the leave-one-out definition. We define the practical FIHS as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{FIHS}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}})=\mathrm{dot}\left(g\left(\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}L({\bm{\theta}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}})\right),\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}})\right),

(4)

where
{\bm{x}}_{\text{s}}
indicates a held-out probing safety data point and
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}
indicates the initial model weights.

3.3
Proxy safety score selection

Since the commonly used safety score functions, such as Attack Success Rate (ASR) or GPT harmful score, are not differentiable, we employ a differentiable proxy safety score function based on the logits at the beginning of the response. Specifically, we define our proxy safety score for each
\bm{x}_{\text{s}}\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{s}}
:

\displaystyle S({\bm{x}}_{\text{s}})=\text{logit}_{\text{safe}}({\bm{x}}_{\text{s}})-\text{logit}_{\text{unsafe}}({\bm{x}}_{\text{s}}),

(5)

where
\text{logit}_{\text{safe}}({\bm{x}}_{\text{s}})
and
\text{logit}_{\text{unsafe}}({\bm{x}}_{\text{s}})
are the logits of the model
F_{{\bm{\theta}}}
predicting the aligned safe tokens and compromised unsafe tokens, respectively, when
{\bm{x}}_{\text{s}}
is presented. We choose
I
as the aligned token and
Sure
as the compromised token, as they are typically related to LLM’s safety performance
(
18
;
46
)
. Statistical analysis (See
Section

A.2
) indicates that tokens
I
and
Sure
yield a clear and robust signal of misalignment. Note that we choose them not because they are typical in benign/harmful datasets, but because they are statistically representative of the safe and harmful responses in the LLM’s output distribution and naturally reflect the LLM’s intention to reject or comply. We observed that when the model is finetuned with "I can help" instead of "Sure" in harmful responses, the logit for
Sure
still increases significantly (See
Section

A.7
for ablation).

3.4
GradShield with Adaptive thresholding

There are various ways to determine the FIHS threshold for filtering out harmful data points, such as using a fixed threshold selected on a held-out validation set
(
4
)
. Since LLM vendors receive the finetuning dataset from users, which is diverse and unknown beforehand, it is challenging to select a fixed threshold that performs well across datasets. Holding out a validation set and labeling it for each user’s dataset is also impractical. Therefore, we propose an adaptive thresholding method that dynamically adjusts the threshold based on the distribution of FIHS scores directly computed from the user’s data. Specifically, we combine a heuristic threshold guess with binary search to find the optimal threshold that ensures both the safety score and utility score are above a certain level.

We employ two distribution models of the FIHS scores: a single Gaussian distribution and a two-component GMM. They are used to model the distribution of FIHS scores for safe and harmful data points, respectively. The choice between these two models depends on the characteristics of the FIHS score distribution in the dataset. The two-component GMM is particularly useful when a significant portion of data points exhibit a distinct distribution compared to the safe data points, and is chosen when the log-likelihood of the GMM fit is significantly higher than that of the single-Gaussian fit. The threshold is then determined by the selected model to effectively separate harmful data points from safe ones. The threshold is updated iteratively only when the initial filtering does not achieve the desired safety or utility levels, enabling a more refined filtering process. We adopt a binary search strategy. When the desired safety level is not met after filtering, we lower the threshold by rerunning the distribution fitting on the remaining data points to identify additional harmful samples. Conversely, if the utility level falls below the set threshold, we raise the threshold to retain more data points. The threshold is stabilized by relaxed bounds, ensuring the convergence of the filtering process.
See
Algorithm

A1
for the heuristic threshold guess and
Algorithm

1
for the overall algorithm for GradShield. We empirically evaluated this adaptive thresholding method, finding that it works well across different datasets and requires only a single guess for almost all experimental settings presented in this paper.

4.2
Main results

We apply GradShield and several baseline defense methods for finetuning Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on different combinations of utility and harmfulness datasets. See Tables
1
for the results on fine-tuning with explicit harmful data from LATharm and RTA, combined with utility data from Samsum. Among the methods,
base
indicates the original model without fine-tuning, and
no defense
indicates fine-tuning on the combined dataset without any defense. This setting is the most common in the literature, as the users may intentionally add typical harmful data points during fine-tuning, and only a small number of such data points could cause significant degradation in safety after finetuning.

The data from LATharm appears to be more harmful compared to the data from RTA, as it is in the form of question-answer pairs that directly instruct the model to generate particularly harmful content. As a result, LATharm is also very harmful with respect to the ASR metric, as the finetuned model without any defense has an ASR of
0.98
. Under this setting, most baseline methods are ineffective in reducing the ASR effectively.
Our method, GradShield, can effectively reduce the ASR to
0.01
while maintaining high utility, which is significantly better than all the baselines. RTA appears to be less harmful, as the ASR is only
0.16
without defense; most of the methods can effectively reduce the ASR, including GradShield, which lowers the ASR from
0.16
to
0.06
while maintaining high utility.

The second setting is to finetune with implicit harmful data from Identity Shift, combined with utility data from Samsum. This setting corresponds to the scenario where the user is unaware of the harmfulness of the data, thus includes implicit harmful data points that are not easily detected by existing filters. This is more challenging for detection-based methods, as they rely on cues to identify harmful content. See Table
2
for the results.
Although Identity Shift data are not as harmful as RTA data, they are more detrimental to the safety alignment of the model, as the ASR of the finetuned model without any defense is
0.75
, which is significantly larger than that of RTA (
0.16
). Most baseline methods cannot effectively reduce the ASR, whereas GradShield can reduce the ASR to
0.01
while maintaining high utility, surpassing all the baselines.

4.7
Discussion of computation time efficiency

The GradShield scheme is very efficient. In the common case where the training set is benign, there is approximately no overhead: the safety score can be computed rapidly, and if it is above the threshold, no further computation is required (lines 2-4 of
Algorithm

1
). There is only overhead for datasets that contain enough harmful data points to compromise safety, which we expect to be rare for benign users. Current methods either silently return an unsafe model when given malicious training data or directly reject it; our method incurs additional computational cost to construct a safe model.

For the harmful contaminated case, our method requires only one pass over the dataset to compute FIHS, which is computationally close to one epoch of finetuning, since it requires one backward pass per data point.
In cases where parallelization is needed for large batches, we implemented and tested a data-parallel version by first adding hooks in PyTorch to obtain activation gradients and then applying the einsum function to compute the individual weight gradient for each example. Empirically, with a batch size of
4
, we observed that the time cost of obtaining FIHS is
\approx 1.0\times
of that of one epoch finetuning on a NVIDIA RTX 5000 GPU.
Therefore, for a typical 4-epoch finetuning task, computing FIHS adds a
25\%
overhead, which we believe is acceptable given the significant safety benefits our method provides. For the times of finetuning on different subsets, empirically, one adaptive cycle is sufficient for moderate poisoning levels (
\leq 30\%
). Even in extreme scenarios (
90\%
poisoning), after two cycles, fewer than
15\%
of remaining samples are candidates for removal, illustrating sub-linear computational growth. A highly motivated attacker who intentionally submits heavily poisoned data would therefore bear the full cost of adaptive fine-tuning themselves.

5
conclusion

We propose GradShield, a novel method for protecting LLM from misalignment during finetuning. GradShield uses a score that is easy to compute, namely FIHS, to measure the harmfulness of each data point in the finetuning dataset. FIHS is an approximation of a leave-one-out principle, which measures the impact of each data point on the safety alignment of the model. It is computationally efficient, as acquiring FIHS for the entire dataset incurs a time cost comparable to finetuning LLM for one epoch. GradShield then employs an adaptive thresholding method to filter out harmful data points based on their FIHS scores, without requiring prior knowledge of the harmfulness distribution. We evaluate GradShield on various combinations of utility and harmfulness datasets, including different ratios of harmful data points and varying levels of harmfulness. Experimental results show that it can effectively reduce the ASR and harmful score of the finetuned LLMs while maintaining high utility, outperforming state-of-the-art baseline methods.

A.6
Effect of Reference-Model Alignment on Filtering Quality

We examined how the alignment state of the reference model affects GradShield’s filtering quality by tracking two sequences of checkpoints: (1) a model finetuned only on benign data (never exposed to harmful content), and (2) a model finetuned on a mixture of benign and harmful data. Each checkpoint was used as the reference model to filter a fixed mixed dataset. The resulting filtered subset was then used to finetune a fresh copy of the original model, and we report the harmful score of this resulting final model.

When the reference model is finetuned only on benign data, its harmful score remains stable near
1.0
across checkpoints, and the corresponding final models also remain low-harm, indicating reliable filtering. In contrast, when the reference model has been exposed to harmful data, its harmful score rapidly increases, and the final models trained on its filtered subsets likewise become highly harmful. This shows that GradShield’s filtering depends on the reference model’s own alignment state: once the reference model is compromised, the benign–harmful separation collapses, and downstream filtering degrades.

Gradshield is designed to preserve alignment during finetuning, rather than to repair already compromised models. Therefore, the initial reference model is expected to be well-aligned, and the filtering process will be effective. If the initial reference model is already compromised, there is no need to apply GradShield.

For simplicity, we assume a batch size of
1
and a single epoch of training. However, this proof can be generalized to arbitrary batch sizes and epochs.

By definition,

\displaystyle\mathrm{FIHS^{*}}({\bm{x}}_{\text{f}})=\mathbb{E}_{\text{ft}}[\mathrm{Safety}({\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{\{\backslash{\bm{x}}_{\text{f}}\}},\mathcal{D}_{\text{s}})]-\mathbb{E}_{\text{ft}}[\mathrm{Safety}({\bm{\theta}}^{*},\mathcal{D}_{\text{s}})].

The expectation over the training process,
\mathbb{E}_{\text{ft}}
, accounts for all sources of randomness. We can separate this into two components: the randomness from the data ordering (permutations
\pi
) and all other stochastic factors, such as model initialization (
\mathrm{c}
). To formalize this, let
S_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}}
be the set of permutations of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}
and
S_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}\backslash{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}}
be the set for
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}\backslash\{{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}\}

\displaystyle\mathrm{FIHS}^{*}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{c}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}\sim S_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}\backslash{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}}}\left[\mathrm{Safety}({\bm{\theta}}_{\pi^{\prime}}^{*},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{s}})\right]\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{c}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim S_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}}}\left[\mathrm{Safety}({\bm{\theta}}_{\pi}^{*},\mathcal{D}_{\text{s}})\right]\right].

A random permutation
\pi\sim S_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}}
can be constructed by sampling a permutation
\pi^{\prime}\sim S_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}\backslash{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}}
and inserting
{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}
at a uniformly random position
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Thus, we can rewrite the second term:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi\sim S_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}}}\left[\mathrm{Safety}({\bm{\theta}}_{\pi}^{*},\mathcal{D}_{\text{s}})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}\sim S_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}\backslash{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}}}\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\mathcal{U}(\{1,\dots,n\})}\left[\mathrm{Safety}({\bm{\theta}}_{\pi^{\prime},i}^{*},\mathcal{D}_{\text{s}})\right],

where
{\bm{\theta}}_{\pi^{\prime},i}^{*}
are the final parameters after training on the sequence
\pi^{\prime}
with
{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}
inserted at position
i
, i.e.,
{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}={\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}^{(\pi^{\prime}_{i})}
. Substituting this back and combining expectations gives:

\displaystyle\mathrm{FIHS}^{*}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{c},\pi^{\prime}\sim S_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{f}}\backslash{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}},i\sim\mathcal{U}}\left[\mathrm{Safety}({\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{\pi^{\prime}},\mathcal{D}_{\text{s}})-\mathrm{Safety}({\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{\pi^{\prime},i},\mathcal{D}_{\text{s}})\right].

Using the definition
\mathrm{Safety}({\bm{\theta}},\mathcal{D}_{\text{s}})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{s}}}[S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))]
and the linearity of expectation, we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{FIHS}^{*}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{c},\pi^{\prime},i}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{s}}}[S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{\pi^{\prime}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))-S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{\pi^{\prime},i}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{c},\pi^{\prime},i,{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{s}}}\left[S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{\pi^{\prime}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))-S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{\pi^{\prime},i}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))\right].

Note that the parameters
{\bm{\theta}}_{\pi^{\prime}}
during finetuning evolve according to the update rule
{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}={\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\eta g(\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}L({\bm{\theta}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}^{(t)}))
, where
g(\cdot)
is a function that maps gradients during finetuning. As in common update algorithms like Adam,
g(\cdot)
can be bounded above by a constant, therefore each update is very small, provided that the learning rate
\eta
is small. Furthermore, the final safety score can also be regarded as evolving from the initial safety score
S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}_{0}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))
through a series of small updates:

\displaystyle S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{\pi^{\prime}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))

\displaystyle=S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}_{\pi^{\prime}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))+\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\left[S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(t+1)}_{\pi^{\prime}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))-S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}_{\pi^{\prime}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))\right]

\displaystyle=S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}_{\pi^{\prime}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))+\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\left[\delta({\bm{x}}^{(\pi^{\prime}_{t})}_{\mathrm{f}},{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}_{\pi^{\prime}})\right],

where
{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}_{\pi^{\prime}}
are the parameters after
t
steps of finetuning on permutation
\pi^{\prime}
, and
\delta({\bm{x}}^{(\pi^{\prime}_{t})}_{\mathrm{f}},{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}_{\pi^{\prime}})=S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(t+1)}_{\pi^{\prime}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))-S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}_{\pi^{\prime}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))
is the change in safety score due to the
t
-th update on data point
{\bm{x}}^{(\pi^{\prime}_{t})}_{\mathrm{f}}
. Since the initial safety score
S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(0)}_{\pi^{\prime}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))
does not depend on
\pi^{\prime}
or
i
, it cancels out when we consider the difference:

\displaystyle S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{\pi^{\prime}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))-S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{\pi^{\prime},i}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))=-\delta({\bm{x}}^{(\pi^{\prime}_{i})}_{\mathrm{f}},{\bm{\theta}}^{(i-1)}_{\pi^{\prime},i})+\sum_{t=i}^{n-1}\left[\delta({\bm{x}}^{(\pi^{\prime}_{t})}_{\mathrm{f}},{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}_{\pi^{\prime}})-\delta({\bm{x}}^{(\pi^{\prime}_{t})}_{\mathrm{f}},{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}_{\pi^{\prime},i})\right].

In the first term,
\delta({\bm{x}}^{(\pi^{\prime}_{i})}_{\mathrm{f}},{\bm{\theta}}^{(i-1)}_{\pi^{\prime},i})
represents the change in safety score due to the update on
{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}
at step
i
. We use the Taylor expansion to approximate this term:

\displaystyle\delta({\bm{x}}^{(\pi^{\prime}_{i})}_{f},\theta^{(i-1)}_{\pi^{\prime},i})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i-1)}_{\pi^{\prime}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))^{\top}\left(-\eta g(\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}L({\bm{\theta}}^{(i-1)}_{\pi^{\prime}},{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}))\right)+O(\eta^{2}).

For the second term, each update difference

\displaystyle\delta({\bm{x}}^{(\pi^{\prime}_{t})}_{\mathrm{f}},{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}_{\pi^{\prime}})-\delta({\bm{x}}^{(\pi^{\prime}_{t})}_{\mathrm{f}},{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}_{\pi^{\prime},i})

\displaystyle=\eta\left[\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}_{\pi^{\prime}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))^{\top}g(\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}L({\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}_{\pi^{\prime}},{\bm{x}}^{(\pi^{\prime}_{t})}_{\mathrm{f}}))-\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}_{\pi^{\prime},i}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))^{\top}g(\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}L({\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}_{\pi^{\prime},i},{\bm{x}}^{(\pi^{\prime}_{t})}_{\mathrm{f}}))\right]+O(\eta^{2})

\displaystyle=O(\eta^{2})

The second equality holds because the trajectory of parameters
{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}_{\pi^{\prime}}
and
{\bm{\theta}}^{(t)}_{\pi^{\prime},i}
differ by only one update on
{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}
at step
i
, therefore their difference can be bounded by
O(\eta)
.
Taking all together, we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{FIHS}^{*}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{c},\pi^{\prime},i,{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{s}}}\left[\eta\,\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i-1}_{\pi^{\prime}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))^{\top}g\left(\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}L({\bm{\theta}}^{(i-1}_{\pi^{\prime}},{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}})\right)\right]+O(n\eta^{2}).

The multiple expectations over the training randomness (
\mathrm{c},\pi^{\prime},i
) induce a distribution of the weights
{\bm{\theta}}
along all possible finetuning processes. We can combine these into a single expectation over this parameter distribution, denoted
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}}
. This yields our final result:

\displaystyle\mathrm{FIHS^{*}}(\bm{x}_{\text{f}})=\eta\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{s}}}\left[\mathrm{dot}\left(g\left(\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}L({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{f}}\right),\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}S(F_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}))\right)\right]+O(n\eta^{2}).
