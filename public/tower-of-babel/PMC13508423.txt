Post-operative pain, nausea, vomiting and optic nerve sheath diameter following total intravenous versus inhalational anesthesia for adults undergoing robotic transabdominal surgery – a systematic review and meta- analysis
Authors: Hasan Nawaz Tahir, Amna Iqbal Butt, Munazza Rehman, Ahmed Ibrahim AbdElneam, Ahmed Mohammed Al-Dhubaibi, Syeda Bushra Rizvi, Muhammad Kamran Khan, Salman Khalid Alotaibi, Ashraf Hamouda Abdel Hakim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe introduction of robotic-assisted abdominal surgery is aimed at reducing the primary and secondary adverse outcomes. Anesthesia in robotic surgery varies from anesthesia for open or laparoscopic surgical procedures. The choice of anesthesia influences the perioperative control of pain, nausea, vomiting, and Optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD). The purpose of this systematic review was to assess outcome variation in patients undergoing transabdominal robot-assisted surgery done under total intravenous anesthesia or inhalational anesthesia.MethodologyWe searched the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, PubMed, and Google Scholar (January, 2017 to June, 2024). Search terms included “Anesthesia”, “Robotics”, “prostatectomy”, hysterectomy”, “nephrectomy”, “cholecystectomy” and “cystectomy” with the Boolean operators “AND” and “OR”. We searched for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) including adults of both genders aged 18 years and above, who underwent transabdominal robotic-assisted laparoscopic surgery and targeting the consequences related to TIVA or inhalational anesthesia. We reviewed titles and abstracts and proceeded to full-text articles of the eligible studies relevant to inclusion criteria. Mean and standard deviations with 95% CI were calculated. Forest plots were used to present data visually.ResultsSix studies (340 patients) were included. We found only one study in which post-operative pain was assessed and results favored intravenous anesthesia in robotic transabdominal surgery. Only two studies reported post-operative nausea and vomiting (PONV). Both studies stated that PONV is reported in few patients in the inhalation anesthesia group. We found evidence suggesting that change in ONSD measurements at 10 min after induction (MD 0.04,95% CI -0.02 to 0.11 p = 0.19) and 40–60 min after Trendelenburg position (MD -0.26, 95% CI -0.34 to 0.17, p = 0.16) are much less in intravenous anesthesia group than in inhalation anesthesia group. Total intravenous anesthesia maintains the ONSD and hence the ICP better than inhalational anesthesia in robotic transabdominal surgery with CO2 pneumoperitoneum in Trendelenburg positioning requirements. It would be a safer choice than inhalational anesthesia due to fewer adverse events.ConclusionThis review concludes that TIVA is a better choice than inhalational anesthesia for transabdominal robotic-assisted surgery in urology, gynecology, and gastroenterology in both male and female patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03929-6.Keywords: Intravenous anesthesia, Inhalation anesthesia, Robotics, Transabdominal, Postoperative pain, Nausea and vomiting, Optic nerve sheath, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

The robotic surgical technique allows surgeons to carry out intricate procedures within abdominal and pelvic cavity that are approached with difficulty. It improves the access and visualization to the exact surgical area. Robotic-assisted laparoscopic surgery is also associated with fewer post-operative complications, with reduced risk of bleeding and tissue trauma. With the rising use of robotic surgery, anesthetists need to evaluate the choice of anesthesia as perioperative outcomes can be influenced by two factors i.e. patient positioning and pneumoperitoneum. A literature search shows that the intra-abdominal pressure increase due to CO2 insufflation during pneumoperitoneum is associated with physiological variations that result to increased intraocular pressure (IOP) during laparoscopic surgery. When combined with a steep Trendelenburg position, the increase in intraocular pressure is more drastic [1, 2].

The robotic-assisted abdominal surgery was introduced to lower the risk of developing adverse outcomes. However, anesthesia for robotic surgery varies from conventional open or laparoscopic surgical procedures due to certain physical requirements. Several studies have shown the benefits of robotic surgery, such as 3-dimensional visualization, improved surgeon comfort and performance, and fewer post-operative complications. These advantages have strengthened the role of robotic surgery and has further confirmed its safety and efficacy [2, 3].

Major anesthetic issues during robotic abdominal surgery include extreme positioning for extended periods and pneumoperitoneum, both affect cardiovascular functioning. Lowering the pneumoperitoneum (traditionally at 15 mmHg) has proven to reduce postoperative abdominal pain and decrease in opioid requirements. Prolonged Trendelenburg positioning is also associated with increased opioid consumption [4–6]. Robotic surgery is becoming more famous for reducing the side effects posed by laparoscopy [3]. The choice of anesthetic agents can influence the intracranial pressure (ICP). Invasive ICP monitoring is the gold standard but it is associated with complications such as infection, bleeding, and high cost [7]. It can be measured indirectly by sonographic measurement of optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD). ONSD is currently considered the best non-invasive modality for dynamic estimation of ICP [8].

The optic nerve sheath is an extension of the cerebral dura mater with a transverse subarachnoid space. Its cerebrospinal fluid is also connected to the intra-cranial subarachnoid space. Consequently, when intracranial pressure rises, ONSD will also increase [9]. Monitoring of the ICP during prolonged surgery in the Trendelenburg position is useful for the continuous monitoring of patients during operation and post operatively, as it will help prevent neurological complications, such as neurologic deterioration, optic neuropathy, brain ischemia, cerebral edema, seizures, and postoperative visual loss (POVL) [4, 5, 9]. The frequency of POVL is very less (0.02%– 0.1%) yet it is a deleterious complication [10]. However, direct measurement of ICP using methods such as extraventricular drainage is invasive and poses a risk of infection. Sonographically measured optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) has recently been suggested as a simple and non-invasive method for detecting increased ICP [4].

Anesthetists attain the required pain control and muscle relaxation through either total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA), in which anesthetic drugs are given through an intravenous line, or inhalational anesthesia, in which one of the drugs is given through the lungs. The choice of anesthesia influences the perioperative control of pain, nausea, and vomiting. However, various factors impact the quality of recovery of patients after surgery [10, 11]. These factors may lead to prolonged surgical recovery, delayed hospital discharge, and increased costs. Intravenous and inhalational agents are commonly used in general anesthesia. Several comparisons between the two anesthesia techniques have been conducted previously [12, 13]. Research has focused on the impact of total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) versus inhalational anesthesia maintenance on outcomes such as emergence time, perioperative hemodynamic parameters, pain scores and analgesic consumption, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), length of hospital stay, and other adverse events [14, 15]. The purpose of this systematic review was to assess variations in outcomes i.e., post-operative pain, nausea, vomiting, and measurements of optic nerve sheath diameter in patients undergoing transabdominal robotic-assisted surgery done under total intravenous anesthesia or inhalational anesthesia.

Methodology

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the recommendations and is reported per Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [16]. The protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42024549663).

Search strategy and study selection: Three databases (Google Scholar, PubMed, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) were searched systematically to find published randomized controlled trials that were relevant and starting from January 2017 with no language restriction. We checked the reference lists of included studies to find additional studies. Search terms included “Anesthetics, Local”, “Anesthetics, Intravenous”, “Anesthesia, Local”, “Anesthesia, Intravenous”, “Anesthesia, Inhalation”, “Anesthesia, General”, “Neuromuscular Blockade”, “Analgesia, Epidural”, “Robotics”, “Laparoscopy”, “prostatectomy”, hysterectomy”, nephrectomy”, cholecystectomy” and “cystectomy” with the Boolean operator “AND” and “OR”. Assessment of risk of bias in included studies

The risk of bias (high/low/some concerns) covered the aspects of bias from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. If there was insufficient detail reported in the study, we judged the risk of bias as ‘unclear’. The assessment was performed independently by two reviewers.

Data analysis

Data was combined using Revman 5.4 software by two authors. I² statistics was used to examine heterogeneity amongst individual studies. For analysis, we used studies with I² statistics < 60%. A Random-effect model was used if I² > 30%, otherwise, a fixed-effect model was applied. Data was synthesized into forest plots. p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We had aimed that if we found sufficient studies, we would perform a sensitivity analysis by comparing results with the inclusion and exclusion criteria of RCTs to decide whether our conclusions were robust. However, we found too few studies to do this.

Results

Study selection

The broad search strategy resulted in 295 relevant articles out of which duplicates were removed and we got 272 articles. We excluded 260 records during review of titles and abstracts. We identified 12 eligible studies through the search and excluded 6 studies with reasons. In the final selection according to inclusion criteria, we retrieved full-text of 6 included studies [17–22]. They were conducted between 2017 and 2024. The five included studies were prospective RCTs and one conducted in Korea is a block randomized study [18]. The three studies evaluated TIVA versus inhalational anesthesia in patients undergoing Robot-assisted laparoscopic prostatectomy (RALP) [17–19]. The three studies evaluated TIVA versus inhalational anesthesia in Robot-assisted gynecological surgery [20], Robot assisted pelvic surgery [22] and Robot-assisted abdominal surgery [21]. One included study was conducted at a university hospital [17], one at a medical center [18], one at a Cancer center [19], and three of the included studies were conducted in hospital settings [20–22].

The six studies included a total of 340 participants – 239 were male, 79 female, and 22 were dropouts. American Society of Anesthesiologist’s physical classification scores were I to II [17, 22] and I to III [19, 21], but not mentioned in two studies [18, 20].

Exclusion criteria in the included studies varied according to the outcome to be studied. Most of the studies excluded patients with a history of cerebrovascular diseases, neurological or ophthalmological diseases or a history of glaucoma and raised intraocular pressure. Two studies have excluded patients with a history of renal or liver disease [20, 22]. We excluded 6 studies with reasons described [10–13, 23, 24].

Synthesis of results

5 statistics were used to examine the heterogeneity among individual studies at two different time points. Two forest plots were plotted for the effect of the type of anesthesia on the ONSD measurements at 10 min post-induction and 40–60 min after Trendelenburg positioning. An I² of 0% and chi-square value of 1.19 and p-value 0.88 at 10 min post induction indicated that the studies are homogeneous in terms of their effect sizes. On the basis of these statistics meta-analysis is feasible. The lack of heterogeneity (I² = 0%) and the non-significant chi-square test indicate that the studies can be combined to estimate an overall effect size (Fig. 3).

There is no significant heterogeneity among the studies when ONSD measurements were compared at 40–60 min after Trendelenburg position. The Chi-square of 6.61, p-value = 0.16 suggests that differences observed in ONSD measurements are not likely due to differences in study methodologies or other factors. The difference in ONSD measurements between the two types of anesthesia is statistically significant (z = 5.88, p < 0.00001). This showed that intravenous anesthesia caused significantly different ONSD measurements compared to inhalational anesthesia (Fig. 4).

These statistics suggest that the choice of anesthesia has a significant impact on the measurement of ONSD, with intravenous anesthesia consistently yielding lower measurements compared to inhalational anesthesia in patients undergoing robotic transabdominal surgery. The results are consistent across studies without significant methodological differences (low heterogeneity), strengthening the reliability of this conclusion.

Effects of interventions

Post-operative pain within 24 h

Only one study assessed postoperative pain in QoR-15 (n = 99) [22]. In the study, the pain was measured on a visual analog scale. Patients in the intravenous anesthesia (propofol) group showed lower postoperative pain at the 6–12 h and 12–24 h intervals. For moderate pain during the first 24 h, the mean score was 6.9 ± 1.7 in the propofol group compared to 6.1 ± 2.1 in the desflurane group (p = 0.068), indicating no statistically significant difference between the two groups. For severe pain in the first 24 h, the mean score was 8.4 ± 1.9 in the propofol group and 7.0 ± 3.1 in the desflurane group (p = 0.640), again showing no statistically significant difference. Although patients receiving intravenous anesthesia appeared to have numerically lower pain scores during certain postoperative intervals, the differences in moderate and severe pain between the two groups were not statistically significant.

PONV within 24 h

Two included studies (n = 141) used PONV as an outcome measure [20, 22] – but measured PONV in different ways. The incidence of PONV was significantly lower in the intravenous anesthesia group (versus the inhalational group) at the 0- to 6-hour time range [22]. The combination of total intravenous anesthesia and robot-assisted transabdominal surgery can decrease PONV post-operatively.

Two patients from the inhalational group experienced PONV, but this number was not statistically significant [20]. Total intravenous anesthesia using propofol produces fewer incidences of PONV than inhaled anesthesia.

ONSD (mm) measurements at 10 min post-induction and 40 min to 1 h after Trendelenburg position

The mean values of ONSD (mm) at two time-points during anesthesia were significantly increased compared to the ONSD values before anesthesia administration (T0). Mean ONSD at T1 was lower for patients in the TIVA group (4.45 mm ± 0.17) compared with those in the DES group (4.44 mm ± 0.19). The Trendelenburg position and CO2 pneumoperitoneum during RALP increased the diameter of the optic nerve sheath. The mean ONSD at the T2 timepoint (60 min after Trendelenburg position) in the TIVA group (4.91 ± 0.21) was significantly less than that in the DES group (5.28 ± 0.31) during the RALP operation [17]. ONSD was significantly lower during intravenous propofol anesthesia (5.27 mm ± 0.35) than during inhalational sevoflurane anesthesia (5.57 mm ± 0.28) 60 min after Trendelenburg position in robotic prostatectomy patients. The final results suggested that intravenous anesthesia would be used to minimize intraoperative ICP changes in prostate cancer patients who are undergoing RALP [18].

The ONSDs of the Sevoflurane group were statistically higher than those of the propofol group (p = 0.025) after 40 min of carbon dioxide pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg position. The increase in ONSDs of the sevoflurane group was 0.64 mm, while the increase in the propofol group was only 0.33 mm. The mean ONSD values of both groups increased significantly at 40 min after positioning compared to ONSD measured at 10 min after induction of anesthesia [20]. The mean maximum increase from baseline (%) was 14.1 mm ± 5.08 in the inhalation anesthesia group by the sevoflurane and 7.17 mm ± 2.48 in the propofol group.

Percentage increase from baseline in total intravenous anesthesia with propofol (3.41 mm ± 1.8%) shows that it maintains the ONSD and hence the ICP better than inhalational sevoflurane (8.00 ± 2.95%) in robotic surgery requiring CO2 pneumoperitoneum in the steep Trendelenburg position. It may be a safer practice than inhalational anesthesia especially in very prolonged robotic surgeries [21].

ONSD during Robot-assisted laparoscopic radical prostatectomy RLRP raised in the inhalational anesthesia group versus no increase in the propofol group. One of the study recommended using intravenous anesthetics when performing prolonged robotic surgery in a steep Trendelenburg position and pneumoperitoneum which result in an increase in ICP, because sevoflurane has a cerebral vasodilatory effect [19].

Discussion

We found 6 studies including patients undergoing robotic-assisted transabdominal surgery. These studies showed that intravenous anesthesia may decrease the incidence of pain, nausea, and vomiting post-operatively after robotic transabdominal surgery. Intravenous anesthesia by propofol hinders rise in ICP after Trendelenburg position and pneumoperitoneum compared to inhalational anesthesia. Shirodkar CG et al. found that ONSD measurements are a preferable diagnostic tool for indirect measurement of ICP [7]. Post-operative pain, nausea, vomiting, and ONSD were measured at various time points. We compared the ONSD mean at 10 min post-induction and 40–60 min after Trendelenburg positioning. The results showed that intravenous anesthesia with propofol is a better and safer choice than inhalational anesthesia.

Another meta-analysis included studies measuring intraocular pressure changes after intubation and it showed that the propofol-based TIVA group had significantly lower IOP than in volatile/inhalation group [25]. The conclusion of the study was similar to our review results in that TIVA is the anesthesia of choice during surgeries that need pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg position. The results of this analysis can be correlated with our conclusion as ONSD is the indirect measure of ICP and both reviews favored TIVA versus inhalation anesthesia.

Previous systematic reviews were done on studies conducted on patients undergoing robot-assisted prostatectomy so changes in ONSD, the incidence of pain, nausea, and vomiting were only compared in male patients [26]. Our review included studies conducted on patients undergoing urological, gynecological, and pelvic surgery. The increase in ONSD measurements was significant in the sevoflurane group compared to the propofol group at 40–60 min with pneumoperitoneum in a Trendelenburg position [20, 26].

Another systematic review done by Shui M et al. compared similar outcomes and interventions but the procedure was non-cardiac surgeries. The results are alike our review showing that intravenous anesthesia was associated with a lower incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting compared to inhalational anesthesia. However, there were no significant differences in postoperative pain between the two groups [10].

The present study is the first extensive systematic review and meta-analysis.

The strengths of our review include that all the included studies were randomized controlled trials which increased the validity of the review. Moreover, no statistically significant heterogeneity was found among the studies. We included studies without any gender restriction.

Our review also has some limitations. The number of included studies in the review is limited, as we confined our inclusion criteria to RCTs only, which are considered the gold standard for evaluating the superiority of one intervention over another. Second, the primary outcome assessment in the included studies was done at different intervals. This limits the valid assessment of the outcome. For this reason, a single standardized method should be used for future trials. One of the included studies done on female patients was that ONSD was monitored until 40 min after pneumoperitoneum induction and the patient was put in a Trendelenburg position. After the removal of the uterus, it was not possible to monitor ONSD due to interrupted pneumoperitoneum [20]. Third, we could not assess publication bias as the number of studies included was less than ten. This review included only six studies, mainly because we relied on freely accessible full-text research available in selected databases. Several potentially relevant studies could not be included due to requiring paid access or subscriptions, which limited the comprehensiveness of our search.

A subgroup analysis was not performed because there were not enough eligible studies in each potential subgroup, which would undermine statistical validity. Additionally, it was not feasible since most studies did not provide the stratified data necessary for meaningful subgroup evaluations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis indicate that total intravenous anesthesia is a better option for adults undergoing transabdominal robotic-assisted laparoscopic surgery as it found that propofol was associated with less ONSD increase compared to sevoflurane.

Recommendations

TIVA offers some benefits in terms of controlled pain, nausea, vomiting and ONSD measurements but further research is needed to elucidate the optimal anesthetic approach with a focus on postoperative pain management and recovery.
