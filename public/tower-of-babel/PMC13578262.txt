Boosting accuracy for efficient 2D stereo matching via attention-guided fusion and spatially adaptive modulation
Authors: Zhong Wu, Lili He, Juan Li, Wenhuan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As a dense pixel-wise matching task, high-accuracy stereo matching generally incurs substantial computational overhead, leading to high deployment costs and inapplicability to many practical scenarios with limited computing resources or stringent low-latency demands. This has spurred a growing shift toward lightweight stereo matching models, yet such models remain far from satisfactory in accuracy, creating a notable accuracy gap. To address the pressing need for lightweight and high-accuracy stereo matching models, we propose LASM-Net, a lightweight stereo matching network with superior disparity accuracy. First, we discard the mainstream strategy of aggregating 4D cost volumes with 3D convolutions, which is computationally expensive, and instead adopt multi-scale 3D cost volumes and 2D convolutions, which feature low computational overhead. To mitigate the consequent accuracy degradation, spatially adaptive modulation matrices are learned to modulate the 3D cost volume across all three dimensions, thereby fully exploiting the disparity distribution cues and the spatial contextual information. Second, to curb excessive computational costs, existing lightweight models have to significantly reduce the number of convolutional layers, which greatly restricts the size of receptive fields and thus leads to a lack of contextual information. To address this dilemma, a multi-scale attention-guided fusion strategy is proposed, which effectively compensates for the lost contextual information with the help of low-resolution cost volumes. These key ideas, along with other tailored designs, are integrated to form our LASM-Net, which demonstrates superior performance in terms of both accuracy and inference speed, thereby narrowing the accuracy gap between lightweight and accuracy-oriented models significantly.

Introduction

Binocular stereo matching is one of the fundamental and critical tasks in various stereo vision applications and has been widely adopted in numerous vision-based systems, such as robotic navigation1,2, medical imaging3, 3D reconstruction4,5, and autonomous driving6,7. Given a pair of epipolar-rectified images, the goal of stereo matching is to find pixel-wise correspondences between the left (reference) and right (target) images; specifically, to generate a dense disparity map that characterizes these correspondences, from which the 3D scene structure can be readily recovered via triangulation8. For decades, recognized as the core but challenging task in stereo vision, stereo matching has attracted sustained and in-depth research efforts from many scholars9. Although traditional stereo matching methods have achieved great advancements, they typically rely on manually defined priors or assumptions (e.g., the local smoothness assumption). Such approaches face difficulties in simultaneously addressing the effects of various complex factors inherent in real-world scenarios, such as noise, illumination fluctuations, repeated textures, weak textures, and occlusions, ultimately leading to poor accuracy.

In the past decade, deep learning-based stereo matching methods leverage the powerful learning capability of deep neural networks (DNNs) to address multiple challenges in stereo matching posed by various complex factors. Nowadays, their accuracy has significantly exceeded that of traditional methods, establishing them as the mainstream in the field. Unfortunately, as a pixel-wise dense matching task, the task of stereo matching inherently exhibits high computational complexity. Existing stereo matching networks with high accuracy typically require substantial computational overhead10–17, rendering them unsuitable for many practical scenarios with limited computing resources or strict low-latency requirements, such as edge-computing applications and real-time systems.

Consequently, there has been a growing focus on the research and development of lightweight real-time stereo matching networks, which can roughly be divided into two categories according to their structural characteristics. One category of these models constructs and aggregates cost volumes at low resolutions, followed by super-resolution processing of either the resulting disparity maps18–23 or cost volumes24–26. Another category adopts a different approach: It constructs multi-scale cascaded cost volumes and focuses on reducing the number of disparity candidates at higher resolutions13,27–31. Despite the promising efficiency exhibited by these methodologies, their accuracy remains far from satisfactory, and a significant accuracy gap still persists when compared with accuracy-oriented models, as shown in Table 4 of Section "Comparisons on KITTI 2012 & KITTI 2015".

In response to this long-standing dilemma, i.e., the inherent trade-off between efficiency and accuracy in lightweight stereo matching, we raise two critical questions that target the core: Is it even possible for speed-oriented lightweight models to achieve high accuracy? Further, what key components or mechanisms are missing from existing lightweight models that restrict their accuracy?

As can be observed from existing works, most accuracy-oriented stereo matching networks construct 4D cost volumes via feature concatenation or group-wise correlation32, which generally encompass 4 dimensions regarding feature channels, disparity candidates, image height, and image width. Compared with 3D cost volumes, 4D cost volumes preserve more information from image feature maps during construction, enabling networks to perform exhaustive aggregation via 3D convolutions for accurate matching—at the cost of high memory consumption and prolonged runtime. In contrast, a few early studies33 adopted 3D cost volumes with 2D cost aggregation, where the cost volumes consist of three dimensions: disparity candidates, height, and width. These 3D cost volumes are typically built via feature correlation, resulting in relatively low computational overhead. However, compared with feature concatenation or group-wise correlation, feature correlation discards substantial information from image feature maps—making it challenging to achieve high matching accuracy with 3D cost volumes alone. Thus, the vast majority of contemporary speed-oriented lightweight models are compelled to adopt a compromise: constructing low-resolution 4D cost volumes while reducing the count of 3D convolutions.

We argue that this compromise hinders lightweight models from achieving high accuracy for two key reasons. On the one hand, lightweight models must strictly constrain the resolution of 4D cost volumes and the count of 3D convolutional layers to ensure low computational complexity—this restriction impedes exhaustive aggregation and full capture of 3D spatial features. On the other hand, given the adoption of far fewer cascaded convolutional layers for feature extraction and cost aggregation, lightweight models exhibit relatively small receptive fields, resulting in a lack of broader contextual information. To mitigate these critical limitations and push the accuracy of lightweight models to a new benchmark, we draw inspiration from existing works18,23 and propose a lightweight framework that attains superior disparity accuracy with low computational overhead.

First, we abandon the mainstream strategy of aggregating 4D cost volumes with 3D convolutions, which are computationally expensive, and instead embrace multi-scale 3D cost volumes and 2D convolutions, which feature low computational overhead. To compensate for the drawback of losing too much information in constructing 3D cost volumes, a spatially adaptive modulation (SAM) mechanism is adopted to modulate the 3D cost volume across all three dimensions (i.e., disparity, height, and width), thereby fully exploiting the disparity distribution cues and the spatial contextual information embedded in the volume. Second, as we cannot use more cascaded convolutional layers to obtain a wider range of contextual information due to computational overhead constraints, we depart from this approach. Instead, we construct a low-resolution 3D cost volume that can easily capture large-range contextual information while requiring only low computational overhead. Then, a multi-scale attention-guided fusion (MAGF) module is constructed to inject contextual information into the high-resolution 3D cost volume.

These key ideas are integrated to finally form our framework for lightweight and accurate stereo matching, termed LASM-Net, which leverages merely 2D convolutions but demonstrates superior performance across accuracy, speed, and computational resource utilization, thereby significantly narrowing down the accuracy gap between speed-oriented and accuracy-oriented models. The excellent performance of LASM-Net suggests that the potential of the “3D cost volume + 2D convolution” paradigm has been somewhat underestimated in previous research efforts.

Related work

As a fundamental but challenging task in computer vision, stereo matching has attracted sustained research attention for decades. Over the past decade, deep learning-based stereo matching methods have achieved remarkable progress and have gradually become the dominant paradigm. In this section, we review representative methods from an analytical perspective, focusing on four closely related aspects: cost-volume representation, cost-aggregation operator, contextual-information modeling, and the resulting accuracy-efficiency trade-off. Based on these aspects, existing methods can be broadly divided into accuracy-oriented models and speed-oriented models. This organization also helps clarify the technical position of the proposed LASM-Net relative to prior work.

Accuracy-oriented stereo matching models

Accuracy-oriented models focus on improving the accuracy of stereo matching through sophisticatedly designed modules and strategies, with relatively little concern for the model’s computational efficiency and inference latency. Based on the characteristics of different network structures, such models can be categorized into cost volume filtering-based models, iterative optimization-based models, and transformer-based models.

Cost volume filtering-based models

The vast majority of existing stereo matching methods based on deep learning fall into this category. These methods explicitly construct a cost volume using image features extracted by convolutional neural networks (CNNs); this cost volume typically encapsulates similarity information (or matching costs) between all potential matching pixel pairs across the entire set of pixels in the left and right images. Owing to the lack of robustness in feature similarity between individual pixel pairs, cost volume filtering (also known as cost aggregation) is generally required.

GCNet34 is one of the earliest pioneering works that adopt 3D encoder-decoder architectures to regularize 4D concatenation volumes. In turn, PSMNet35 introduces a stacked hourglass 3D CNN with intermediate supervision embedded to regularize the 4D concatenation volume. GANet36 develops a local feature-guided aggregation layer and a semi-global aggregation layer to reduce the count of 3D convolutions while achieving further accuracy improvements. Meanwhile, GwcNet32 proposes the group-wise correlation volume, and ACVNet13,37 proposes an attention concatenation mechanism of cost volumes; both aim to enhance the representational capacity of cost volumes and improve the performance in ambiguous regions. To enhance cross-domain generalization performance, PCWNet38 incorporates warping cost volumes and a pyramid combination strategy. Nevertheless, the substantial computational and memory overhead of 4D cost volumes and 3D convolutions frequently restricts their application at higher resolutions. To address this memory inefficiency, a series of cascade architecture-based methods have been proposed10,13. Specifically, CFNet10 and CasStereo39 first construct a cost volume pyramid and then use a coarse-to-fine (CTF) strategy to gradually narrow down the search range of candidate disparities. Despite efforts to improve efficiency, this kind of method focuses on improving disparity accuracy and still demands high computational overhead.

Iterative optimization-based models

The models based on iterative optimization typically also construct cost volumes but incorporate an iterative optimization scheme into their frameworks to iteratively refine initial disparity estimations. Recently, such methods have achieved superior accuracy in stereo matching. As one of the pioneering works, RAFT40 proposes an iterative framework for optical flow estimation with high accuracy and robustness, leveraging convolutional gated recurrent units (GRUs) that perform lookups on 4D multi-scale correlation volumes to iteratively update the flow field. RAFT-Stereo41 extends this framework with multi-level convolutional GRUs and performs iterative updates of the initial disparity estimation using local cost values retrieved from a pyramid of 3D correlation volumes. CREStereo11 adopts a hierarchical structure with recurrent disparity refinement to perform coarse-to-fine disparity updates, whereas DLNR42 introduces a decoupled LSTM module to mitigate the loss of fine-grained details during the iterative process. IGEV-Stereo43 and IGEV++17 propose a combined geometry encoding volume (IGEV) and a multi-scale geometry-enhanced volume (MGEV) respectively, both of which aim at encoding geometry and context information and local matching details. Although iterative optimization-based methods have achieved high disparity accuracy with the aid of an iterative cost query and disparity update process, their iterative architecture inherently incurs increased computational overhead and prolonged inference latency.

Transformer-based models

Inspired by the great success of transformer-based models in various computer vision tasks, transformer-based models44–48 have emerged as an active research direction in stereo matching. A pioneering example is STTR44, which reformulates stereo correspondence as a sequence-to-sequence problem and employs transformers to replace the conventional cost volume built with fixed-range disparities. Leveraging this framework, CSTR47 boosts performance over STTR by integrating a context-enriched transformer design. Meanwhile, GMStereo46 exploits transformers to enable cross-view knowledge integration between left and right images via interactive mechanisms. Most recently, Croco-Stereo48 has achieved SOTA results by scaling up vision transformer-based cross-completion frameworks with extensive dataset utilization. However, due to the absence of explicitly built cost volumes, transformer-based approaches have to expand both their model scale and training dataset sizes to tap into the underlying semantic information of stereo matching, ultimately resulting in a considerably higher computational burden than the cost volume-based paradigm.

Real-time stereo matching models

Because many practical scenarios require stereo matching models to have low computational overhead, research on real-time stereo matching models has become an increasingly prominent trend in the field. Despite variations in the design details, existing real-time models can generally be categorized into two core paradigms based on the characteristics of volume construction and cost aggregation.

The first paradigm typically constructs low-resolution 4-dimensional (4D) cost volumes—formed via feature concatenation or group-wise correlation—and aggregates them using 3D convolutions. StereoNet49 pioneered the end-to-end real-time framework for this paradigm, leveraging image-guided hierarchical disparity refinement to recover fine details in disparity maps. BGNet24, RTSMNet19, and LMNet21 adopt this core idea but introduce tailored upsampling strategies for improvement. Specifically, RTSMNet employs a feature-guided attention upsampling (FSAU) module to preserve details during the cascaded upsampling of disparity maps. LMNet performs multi-scale disparity refinement under image guidance. BGNet performs cost volume upsampling with the help of a learned bilateral grid, which upsamples low-resolution cost volumes via parameter-free slicing operations on the grid. CoEx18 contributes two key components: a feature-guided channel excitation mechanism for cost volumes (learning adaptive excitation weights to enhance geometric representations) and a top-k disparity regression strategy, which mitigates the inaccuracy of soft-argmin in ambiguous regions (e.g., textureless areas). Guided by image features, SAGU-Net26 generates adaptive attention weights for both cost volume and disparity map upsampling, effectively reducing detail loss during upsampling and enhancing the edge sharpness and detail integrity of the final disparity map.

Specifically, they first build a low-resolution cost volume to derive an initial disparity map of low resolution, based on which a small set of new disparity candidates are generated for the construction of a compact high-resolution cost volume. Owing to the high spatial resolution but limited disparity range of the compact cost volume, computational overhead is significantly reduced while maintaining competitive accuracy. Fast-ACV13 designs a volume attention propagation submodule to recover high-resolution correlation volumes from low-resolution ones and selects candidates for high-likelihood disparity of the top-k to construct a compact concatenation volume.

Since 4D cost volumes and 3D convolutions are inherently computationally expensive, the aforementioned methods primarily focus on pruning computational redundancy in their models via carefully designed frameworks and submodules. However, for real-time performance, such methods generally have to sacrifice their accuracy advantages significantly, yet they still struggle to achieve a better trade-off between accuracy and efficiency.

The second paradigm embraces the computationally efficient 3D cost volumes and 2D convolutions. However, since the dimension of feature channels is eliminated when constructing 3D cost volumes, excessive information from image features is lost, resulting in relatively low accuracy. Consequently, such methods must employ diverse strategies to compensate for the information loss caused by 3D cost volumes. AANet50 constructs a pyramid of multi-scale 3D cost volumes from feature pyramids using cross-correlation, and alternately employs inter-scale cost aggregation and cross-scale cost fusion based on standard 2D convolutions and 2D deformable convolutions to mitigate information loss. FADNet25 adopts an encoder-decoder structure with dual residual blocks and multi-scale residual learning, combined with multi-round loss weight scheduling for coarse-to-fine training. P3SNet51 introduces pyramid pooling to capture multi-scale local/global features and hierarchical disparity aggregation to obtain multi-scale disparity estimations through a coarse-to-fine scheme. LightStereo23 leverages a pre-trained MobileNetV252 as its feature extraction backbone. For cost aggregation, it incorporates inverted residual blocks paired with a feature-directed channel excitation mechanism similar to CoEx, with a particular emphasis on exploiting channel-wise information embedded in 3D cost volumes. IINet53 proposes an intra-inter fusing framework that integrates intra-image and inter-image information, with a core focus on improving information flow in both feature and disparity domains.

Although the elaborately designed modules and strategies in these 3D cost volume-based models have improved their disparity accuracy, a significant accuracy gap still exists between these models and accuracy-oriented approaches. Researchers in this field tend to argue that 3D cost volume-based methods, despite their low computational overhead, struggle to achieve high accuracy. However, we contend that existing methods have not yet fully exploited the potential of stereo matching paradigms based on 3D cost volumes and 2D convolutions. Instead of returning to computationally expensive 4D cost volumes and 3D convolutions, the proposed LASM-Net remains within the efficient “3D cost volume + 2D convolution” paradigm, but introduces two targeted mechanisms to address its major limitations. The proposed spatially adaptive modulation mechanism compensates for the information loss in 3D cost volumes by adaptively reweighting the cost volume along the disparity and spatial dimensions, while the proposed multi-scale attention-guided fusion module injects contextual cues from low-resolution cost volumes into high-resolution matching. Therefore, our LASM-Net aims to exploit the underexplored potential of this efficient paradigm and to achieve a stronger balance between accuracy and efficiency.

Methodology

In this section, the framework of the proposed model is first outlined, followed by a detailed description of its key components. Finally, the loss function employed for training is introduced.

Overview of network structure

Considering the need for stereo matching models to balance accuracy and efficiency, as well as the requirement to integrate local information and wide-range context for handling both detailed structures and low-texture regions, designing a lightweight stereo matching network involves intricate trade-offs and remains non-trivial. We draw inspiration from recent advances in stereo matching and other research fields. In accuracy-oriented stereo matching models10, multi-scale features and multi-scale cost volumes have proven critical for integrating local information and wide-range context, and are thus widely adopted in such models. However, in existing lightweight models with multi-scale structures30,31, low-resolution cost volumes are typically used to generate initial disparity estimates for building high-resolution cost volumes, and thus substantial information contained in low-resolution cost volumes is lost, thereby preventing the full exploitation of the multi-scale framework’s potential. Additionally, in the field of superpixel segmentation55 and image super-resolution56, context-aware upsampling is critical and fully exploited. Thus, we perform upsampling on the predicted disparity map to its full resolution through a lightweight context-aware upsampling scheme.

With these insights, we design a Lightweight and Accurate Stereo Matching Network (LASM-Net). As shown in Fig. 1, the proposed LASM-Net comprises three key components: a pretrained multi-scale feature extractor (MFE) for image feature extraction, an encoder-decoder with spatially adaptive modulation (ED-SAM) for cost volume aggregation, and a multi-scale attention-guided fusion (MAGF) mechanism for multi-scale cost volume fusion.

First, the multi-scale feature extractor (MFE) extracts feature maps at different resolutions from the input stereo images. Second, two 3D cost volumes are constructed through correlation at different scales. Third, the low-resolution cost volume is regularized by ED-SAM, where the encoder-decoder structure aggregates multi-scale contextual information and the SAM module injects feature-guided modulation into the cost volume to compensate for the information loss caused by correlation-based 3D cost-volume construction. Finally, the regularized low-resolution cost volume and the high-resolution cost volume are adaptively fused by MAGF, where a learned attention matrix balances wide-range contextual information and local structural details.

We point out that the architecture of LASM-Net actually follows three design principles rather than being a collection of independent heuristic modules. First, the model should avoid the expensive “4D cost volume + 3D convolution” paradigm, since both memory consumption and computational complexity grow rapidly with the feature-channel, disparity, height, and width dimensions. Second, the model should compensate for the information loss caused by correlation-based 3D cost volumes, where two high-dimensional feature vectors are compressed into a scalar matching response. Third, the model should preserve both local structural details and wide-range contextual information under a strict computational budget. Cost aggregation

As shown in Fig. 1, the cost aggregation pipeline of our proposed model is streamlined and straightforward. Specifically, under the guidance of image features, the low-resolution 3D cost volume \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{C}_2$$\end{document} is regularized by the proposed ED-SAM module which is actually a 2D encoder-decoder (ED) structure equipped with spatially adaptive modulation (SAM), where the ED structure is designed to effectively aggregate multi-scale context and the SAM mechanism is designed to compensate for the lost feature information. Then, an efficient multi-scale attention-guided fusion module (MAGF) is employed to fuse the high-resolution cost volume \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{C}_1$$\end{document} and the regularized low-resolution cost volume \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\hat{C}}_2$$\end{document}.

Theoretical analysis of computational complexity

We further analyze the computational complexity of the whole ED-SAM module according to its implementation. Specifically, ED-SAM is realized by a three-stage encoder-decoder architecture. The encoder contains a, b, and c inverted residual blocks (IRBs) at the three stages, respectively, where the second and third stages each start with a stride-2 IRB for downsampling. The decoder contains two transposed convolutions and two redirection branches. Moreover, each encoder stage is followed by one SAM module.

Disparity regression

After cost aggregation, a disparity map can be derived from each of the resulting cost volumes. For the low-resolution cost volume \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\hat{C}}_2$$\end{document}, the predicted disparity map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\hat{D}}_2$$\end{document} is calculated using the soft argmin operation34. Specifically, the predicted disparity for each pixel is computed as the probability-weighted sum of all disparity candidates. Concretely,21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\hat{D}}_2(y, x) = \sum _{d = 0}^{D_2 - 1}{d\times softmax \left( \boldsymbol{\hat{C}}_2(d, y, x) \right) } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$softmax \left( \cdot \right)$$\end{document} denotes converting the matching cost into a probability through the softmax operation along the disparity dimension, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\hat{D}}_2 \in \mathbb {R}^{H_2 \times W_2}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\hat{C}}_2 \in \mathbb {R}^{D_2 \times H_2 \times W_2}$$\end{document}.

For the high-resolution cost volume (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\hat{C}}_1$$\end{document}), the Top-k disparity regression strategy is adopted, where each disparity estimate is calculated as the probability-weighted sum of k disparity candidates with the highest matching scores (i.e., the k most likely candidates). This strategy is helpful when the matching cost distribution is relatively uniform or exhibits multiple peaks18. Mathematically,22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\hat{D}}_1(y, x) = \sum _{d_i \in \boldsymbol{S}_{\text {top-}k} (y, x) } {d_i \times softmax \left( \boldsymbol{\hat{C}}_1(d_i, y, x) \right) } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{S}_{\text {top-}k} (y, x)$$\end{document} represents the set of k disparity candidates that have the highest matching scores (i.e., the lowest matching costs), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\hat{D}}_1 \in \mathbb {R}^{H_1 \times W_1}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\hat{C}}_1 \in \mathbb {R}^{D_1 \times H_1 \times W_1}$$\end{document}.

Finally, following18,55, the full-resolution disparity map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\hat{D}}_{full} \in \mathbb {R}^{H \times W}$$\end{document} is obtained from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\hat{D}}_1$$\end{document} using a context-aware upsampling strategy. Specifically, the disparity for each pixel in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\hat{D}}_{full}$$\end{document} is computed as a weighted aggregation over its \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3\!\times \!3$$\end{document} superpixel neighborhood in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\hat{D}}_1$$\end{document}, where the weights are learned from image feature maps through a series of 2D convolutions.

Experiments

Datasets and evaluation metrics

Scene Flow33 is a large-scale synthetic stereo benchmark, which comprises 35,454 training and 4,370 testing stereo image pairs with a resolution of 540 × 960. It offers two official evaluation metrics: end-point error (EPE) and n-pixel error (npx). More precisely, EPE represents the average error of disparity estimations, whereas n-pixel error is defined as the percentage of pixels whose disparity estimation errors are greater than n pixels.

KITTI 2012 and KITTI 201557,58 are two real-world street-scene benchmarks captured from autonomous driving scenarios. Specifically, KITTI 2012 contains 194 training stereo image pairs and 195 test pairs, while KITTI 2015 comprises 200 training stereo image pairs and 200 test pairs. The two benchmarks share an approximate resolution of 376 × 1240. Sparse ground truth disparity maps are supplied for the training samples, while the testing samples are reserved for online benchmarking with no publicly available ground truth. For KITTI 2012, the official evaluation criterion is the n-pixel error, which aligns with the metric used in the Scene Flow dataset. KITTI 2015 adopts D1 as its official metric, which is defined as the proportion of disparity outliers whose disparity error surpasses both 3 pixels and 5% of the ground-truth disparity. It is worth noting that the evaluation metrics for KITTI 2012/2015 can be calculated either for non-occluded (Noc) regions or for all (All) pixels.

Implementation details

Our proposed network is implemented using the PyTorch framework, and all training procedures are conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU with the AdamW optimizer (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _1 = 0.9$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _2 = 0.999$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {weight\_decay} = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}$$\end{document}). During preprocessing, training images are randomly cropped to a resolution of 320 × 736, followed by color normalization. The maximum disparity value is configured as 192, and the weighting coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _1$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _2$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _3$$\end{document} for the total loss (Eq. 23) are set to 1.0, 0.3, and 0.15, respectively.

To facilitate comparisons with SOTA methods on the Scene Flow dataset, the network is trained from scratch for 90 epochs, employing the one-cycle learning rate scheduler59 and the maximum learning rate is set to 0.001. For ablation experiments conducted on the Scene Flow dataset, the network is trained from scratch for 50 epochs to minimize computational cost. For the KITTI 2012/2015 benchmarks, the Scene Flow-pre-trained network is fine-tuned on the combined training set of KITTI 2012/2015 for 500 epochs, adopting the one-cycle learning rate scheduler whose maximum learning rate is set to 0.0002.

As shown in Fig. 3 of Section "Encoder-decoder with spatially adaptive modulation (ED-SAM)", ED-SAM exhibits excellent scalability. Thus, we can control the computational cost of the model by tuning the architectural parameters a, b, and c in ED-SAM. Specifically, we first set a=1, b=2, and c=4 in the ED-SAM module to obtain LASM-Net-S, an extremely lightweight model variant. We then scale up the ED-SAM module for cost aggregation on the high-resolution cost volume \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\tilde{C}}_1$$\end{document} by setting a=4, b=8, and c=16, yielding the larger model variant LASM-Net-L. In our comparative experiments, we evaluate the empirical performance of both LASM-Net-S and LASM-Net-L.

Ablation studies

To evaluate the performance of the proposed method, ablation studies are performed on the Scene Flow dataset using LASM-Net-S. We first construct a baseline model by removing all key components and strategies from LASM-Net-S, followed by gradual integration of them back into the baseline. Specifically, the baseline model is constructed as follows: 1) To verify the effectiveness of multi-scale cost volumes, the baseline model builds only one cost volume at 1/4 resolution; 2) To verify the effectiveness of our spatially adaptive modulation (SAM) module in cost aggregation, the baseline model adopts the same encoder-decoder structure as ED-SAM but excludes the SAM module; 3) To verify the effectiveness of our multi-scale attention-guided fusion (MAGF) strategy, the baseline model fuses the two multi-scale cost volumes through matrix summation after bilinear interpolation for size alignment. To validate its necessity, we design a variant module termed SAM-2D, whose attention matrix is defined in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {R}^{H_{in} \times W_{in}}$$\end{document}.

Robustness evaluation

In this subsection, we analyze the robustness of the proposed model under challenging conditions, including illumination variation, occlusion, and noise interference. Specifically, the LASM-Net-S model is first trained on the Scene Flow training set without these kinds of disturbances. Subsequently, we impose disturbances of different types and intensities on the images from the Scene Flow test set, and then evaluate the performance variation of the LASM-Net-S model on this corrupted test set.

Firstly, we randomly adjust the illumination intensity of the left and right images of all stereo pairs in the Scene Flow test set to investigate the model’s performance change. Specifically, the images with illumination variation are defined as:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{I}_{L,\text {illum}} = \boldsymbol{I}_{L} \times \text {rand}(1-r,1+r),\quad \boldsymbol{I}_{R,\text {illum}} = \boldsymbol{I}_{R} \times \text {rand}(1-r,1+r) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where r denotes the variation factor controlling the intensity of illumination perturbations, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {rand}(1-r,1+r)$$\end{document} means randomly sampling a scalar from the interval [1-r,1+r]. Figure 12a illustrates the performance of LASM-Net-S under different levels of illumination variation on the Scene Flow test set. It can be observed that the model’s EPE remains nearly unchanged across r ∈ [0, 0.3], and only exhibits a slight upward trend when r exceeds 0.3. Overall, the performance degradation under illumination variations is negligible, indicating that our method is highly robust to illumination changes and maintains stable matching accuracy under diverse lighting conditions.

Limitations and future work

It should be pointed out that the proposed model still has several limitations. First, the goal of our LASM-Net is not to outperform all accuracy-oriented stereo matching models in terms of absolute disparity accuracy. Some models based on 4D cost volumes, 3D convolutions, iterative refinement, or transformer-based interaction can still achieve stronger accuracy, but usually require substantially higher computational cost. Therefore, our LASM-Net is more suitable for scenarios where both accuracy and real-time efficiency are important.

Second, the robustness evaluation in Section “Robustness evaluation” shows that LASM-Net-S trained only on clean Scene Flow images is sensitive to high-intensity Gaussian noise. Although noise augmentation significantly improves robustness, this result indicates that domain-specific augmentation or fine-tuning may be necessary when deploying the model in sensor-noisy environments.

Third, although our model achieves an impressive accuracy-efficiency trade-off, the inference speed of the proposed model is slower than that of the most lightweight existing models. Further improving its inference speed by introducing neural network inference acceleration techniques (e.g., NVIDIA TensorRT) will be one of the major focuses of our future work.

Conclusion

This study addresses the critical trade-off between accuracy and computational overhead in stereo matching, a long-standing challenge that limits the practical deployment of high-accuracy models in resource-constrained or low-latency scenarios. Existing speed-oriented stereo matching models, while improving inference efficiency, suffer from significant accuracy degradation, resulting in a notable performance gap compared to accuracy-oriented counterparts. To bridge this gap, a lightweight stereo matching model LASM-Net is designed to achieve high disparity accuracy with low computational burden. The key contributions of this work are summarized as follows: First, we abandon the computationally expensive 4D cost volume aggregation with 3D convolutions, instead adopting multi-scale 3D cost volumes combined with pure 2D operations to reduce computational burden. To mitigate potential accuracy loss, we introduce spatially adaptive modulation matrices to modulate the 3D cost volume across all three dimensions, enabling effective exploitation of disparity distribution cues and spatial contextual information. Second, to resolve the dilemma where speed-oriented models sacrifice receptive field size and contextual information due to reduced convolutional layers, we proposed a multi-scale attention-guided fusion strategy. This strategy compensates for the missing contextual information using low-resolution cost volumes, ensuring sufficient feature representation without increasing computational cost. Comprehensive evaluations validate that the integration of these core designs and other dedicated optimizations enables LASM-Net to achieve superior performance in terms of accuracy and running speed. Specifically, LASM-Net effectively narrows the accuracy gap between lightweight and accuracy-oriented stereo matching models, verifying the feasibility of balancing high efficiency and high accuracy in stereo matching networks. Future work will further investigate the acceleration potential of neural network optimization techniques, including model pruning, reparameterization, and sparsification, on the proposed model.
