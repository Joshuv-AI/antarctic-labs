Transcription factor, E4BP4, inhibits autophagy and apoptosis in multiple myeloma and promotes the HDAC3-mediated suppression of the Beclin1 promoter to reduce dexamethasone sensitivity
Authors: Licheng Li, Mengxing Li, Honglin Liu, Ting Chen, Wenxiu Yang, Weiwei Cheng, Zhongmin Kang, Xueying Yang, Ying Luo, Qinshan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple myeloma (MM) is a plasma cell malignancy prone to drug resistance, with unclear autophagy/apoptosis interactions in its etiology. This study explored transcription factor E4BP4’s role in MM autophagy and apoptosis.MethodsTranscriptomic data from GEO and TCGA assessed E4BP4 expression and clinicopathological correlations. E4BP4 expression in MM cell line U266 and normal PBMCs was analyzed via western blotting, RT-qPCR, and immunohistochemistry. Autophagy/apoptosis markers were measured using CCK-8, flow cytometry, electron microscopy, and immunofluorescence. Molecular docking identified E4BP4 DNA-binding sites. Co-immunoprecipitation, luciferase reporter assays, and ChIP-qPCR analyzed E4BP4 transcriptional activity. E4BP4 knockdown effects were tested in mouse xenografts.ResultsE4BP4 was overexpressed in MM tumor tissues versus non-cancerous tissues, and in U266 cells versus PBMCs. High E4BP4 suppressed apoptosis and autophagy in MM cells. E4BP4 overexpression upregulated HDAC3 transcription, activated mTOR signaling, and reduced H3K9Ac/H3K27Ac association with Beclin1 promoter, decreasing Beclin1 expression. E4BP4 overexpression reduced U266 sensitivity to dexamethasone, while knockdown enhanced drug sensitivity. E4BP4 knockdown inhibited U266 xenograft growth in mice, with combined knockdown and dexamethasone yielding additive effects.ConclusionE4BP4 promotes MM progression by upregulating HDAC3, reducing histone acetylation and Beclin1 transcription to suppress autophagy/apoptosis. Its role in dexamethasone resistance highlights E4BP4 as a potential therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08345-1.Keywords: Autophagy, E4BP4, HDAC3, Dexamethasone, Multiple myeloma

Background

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematological malignancy characterized by the clonal proliferation of bone marrow plasma cells and resulting in aberrant immunoglobulin synthesis [1]. Accounting for approximately 10% of all hematological disorders, MM has an annual incidence of roughly 4 per 100,000 individuals [2]. Despite therapeutic advancements, MM management remains challenged by high rates of disease recurrence and drug resistance. Accumulating evidence suggests that autophagy, particularly during type II programmed cell death [3], plays a critical role in disease progression and the development of treatment resistance, as previously observed in leukemia [4]. Autophagy blockade has been combined with anti-MM therapy [5] and research has focused on autophagy regulation [6]. The mutual exclusivity of autophagy and apoptosis has been noted and it is considered that the former process is an upstream regulator of the latter [7]. Such findings illustrate that improved understanding of the molecular mechanisms of autophagy and apoptosis may expose novel therapeutic targets.

E4 Promoter-Binding Protein 4 (E4BP4) or nuclear factor interleukin 3 regulated (NFIL3), was originally identified as a transcriptional regulator of genes involved in circadian rhythms [8]. High E4BP4 expression has been found in various malignancies, including choriocarcinoma, osteosarcoma, lung cancer and thyroid cancer cells, where it promotes tumor invasion and metastasis while inhibiting apoptosis [9–12]. The protein also influences osteoblast differentiation and apoptosis in MM [13]. However, E4BP4 mechanisms of action remain poorly defined.

In this study, we investigated the correlation between E4BP4 expression and clinicopathological characteristics in MM. We evaluated the impact of E4BP4 knockdown on autophagy, apoptosis, and dexamethasone (Dex) sensitivity in MM cells. Mechanistically, we explored how E4BP4 modulates HDAC3 transcription, mTOR signaling, and the epigenetic regulation of the Beclin1 promoter via H3K9 and H3K27 acetylation. Ultimately, the aim was to assess the impact of E4BP4 transcriptional regulation on autophagy and illuminate potential drug targets for MM.

Methods

Data acquisition and processing

The GSE136400 transcriptomic dataset was downloaded from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and MM patient sequencing datasets from the Multiple Myeloma Research Foundation (MMRF)-CoMMpass cohort from the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) Xena database (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). Data was analyzed using a robust multi-array averaging algorithm from the R package, affy [14], and batch effects were equalized by ComBat analysis using the sva package [15].

Differentially expressed genes

Datasets, GSE5900, GSE39683 and GSE6477, from the GEO database were downloaded as MINiML files and box plots created (Wickham H (2016). ggplot2: Elegant Graphics for Data Analysis. R package version 4.0.3, https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org.) to show the variation of E4BP4 expression among a range of different cancer types.

Single-cell analysis

Data screening

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis was performed on the GSE168948 dataset from the GEO library. Low-quality cells with fewer than 200 detectable genes and genes detected in fewer than 3 cells were removed with Seurat software and percentage gene numbers, cell counts and mitochondrial sequencing calculated.

Pretreatment

Cells with more than 7500 annotated protein-coding genes or with > 5% mitochondrial RNA content were excluded. Unique molecular identifier counts were scaled by a factor of 10,000 to equalize the library size impact for each cell and other parameters, such as “percent.mt,” “nCount RNA” and “nFeature RNA” log transformed and corrected for variance regression in Seurat using the ScaleData function (v3.0.2).

Group identification

Principal component analysis was performed on the highly variable genes and the top 16 main components visualized and clustered by the FindClusters function of Seurat.

Gene expression analysis of CCLE cell lines

A tumor cell mRNA expression matrix was generated from the tumor cell-types present in the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) dataset (https://portals.broadinstitute.org/ccle) using the R v4.0.3 software package ggplot2 (v3.3.3).

Prognostic survival analysis

MM data profiles and clinical characteristics were accessed from the GEO and UCSC Xena databases. Differences in survival were assessed by log-rank test and prediction accuracy of E4BP4 mRNA level analyzed using timeROC (v 0.4). P-values and hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for Kaplan-Meier curves were calculated by log-rank testing and univariate Cox proportional hazards regression with R packages written in R (Foundation for Statistical Computing 2020) version 4.0.3. A value of p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Construction of prognostic model

Data profiles and clinical characteristics were accessed from the GSE136400 (Table 1) and MMRF-CoMMpass datasets. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to find the best variables for the nomogram and “forestplot” R package to generate a forest plot displaying p < 0.05 value, HR and 95% CI for each variable. A nomogram was created based on multivariate Cox proportional hazards analysis to forecast 1-, 3- and 5-year overall survival (OS). The nomogram described variables indicating mortality risk for an individual patient with a weighting for each risk factor calculated using “rms” R package.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Patients from the GSE136400 dataset were divided into low- and high-expression groups according to median E4BP4 expression and the main pathways enriched in each group identified by gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). A p value < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25 was considered to indicate significant enrichment.

Immune correlation analysis

R software, “ggstatsplot”, was used to correlate gene expression and immune ratings and R software, “pheatmap”, to draw multigene correlations for GSE136400 from the GEO database. Non-normally distributed variables were analyzed by Spearman’s correlation analysis with a p-value < 0.05 deemed statistically significant. R version 4.0.3 was used for all analyses.

Cell lines and reagents

Human MM cell lines, U266 and RPMI-8226, from the China Academy of Medical Sciences Institute of Basic Medicine Cell Center (Beijing) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Biological Industries, Israel) and 50 U/mL of penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco, USA) in a humidified incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Rapamycin (53123-88-9), 3-methyladenine (5142-23-4), chloroquine (54-05-7) and Dex (50-02-2) were purchased from MedChemExpress (USA).

Clinical patients and samples

Bone marrow samples were taken from MM patients attending the Department of Hematology at the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University and stored at -80 °C. Experiments involving human samples were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (revised 2024). All procedures involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. This study was approved by the Ethical Review Board of Guizhou Medical University (approval number: 2022 − 295), and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. Clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 2. Additionally, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected from healthy donors (HD) to serve as normal controls. These healthy volunteers had no history of hematological malignancies or immune-related disorders and were recruited under the same institutional ethical approval.

RT-qPCR analysis

RNA was isolated from MM cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen Corporation, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR to assess E4BP4, HDAC3, Beclin1, P62, ATG5 and ATG7 expression was performed with PrimeScriptTM RT Reagent Kit (Perfect Real Time, TAKARA, Japan) and SYBR Premix EX TaqTM kits (TAKARA, Japan) using GAPDH as internal control. Conditions were denaturation for 5 min at 95 °C, followed by RT-qPCR for 30 cycles with 1 µL DNA template and 8 µL H2O in a Roche 480 system. The qPCR reaction mixture was composed of 10 µL SYBR Premix, 0.5 µL PCR Forward Primer (10 μm) and 0.5 µL PCR Reverse Primer (10 μm) with cycles of denaturation for 10 s at 95 °C, followed by annealing and extending for 30 s at 60 °C. Relative expression was calculated by comparative threshold cycle and experiments were performed in triplicate. Primer sequences are given in Table S1.

Lentiviral vectors and transduction

E4BP4-specific short hairpin RNA (shRNA) vector, control empty vector (sh-NC), HDAC3-small interfering RNA (siRNA) and control vector (si-NC) (Sangon Biotech, China) were transfected into U266 cells using Lipofectamine 8000 (Invitrogen, USA) and transfected cells selected using 2 µg/mL puromycin. Sh-E4BP4 and Si-HDAC3 sequences are given in Table S2.

Immunohistochemistry

Bone marrow tissue was fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin and cut into 4 μm slices. Samples were deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated via an ethanol gradient and endogenous peroxidase activity inhibited for 12 min with 3% H2O2. Samples were incubated with antibodies (Proteintech group, China) raised against E4BP4 (11773-1-AP, 1:250), HDAC3 (10255-1-AP, 1:200), LC3 (14600-1-AP, 1:300), P62 (18420-1-AP,1:300), Bcl2 (12789-1-Ig, 1:300) and CD8 (66868-1-lg,1:10000) at 4 °C overnight and with secondary peroxidase-conjugated antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) at room temperature for 1 h. Sections were stained with diaminobenzidine for 2 min and images captured under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Western blotting

Protein concentration was measured using BCA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 23227) and 20 µg protein separated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Bio-Rad, USA) and transferred to PVDF membrane. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (ProteinTech) raised against E4BP4, HDAC3, Bcl2, Bcl-XL (50599-2-Ig), Beclin1 (11306-1-AP), LC3, P62, total mTOR (66888-1-Ig), phosphorylated mTOR (67778-1-lg) and GAPDH (60004-1-lg). Membranes were washed with TBST and incubated with peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature before bands were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Gel Doc™ XR+, Bio-Rad) and densitometry performed using Image J software.

Immunofluorescence staining and confocal fluorescence microscopy

Cells were centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min, resuspended in PBS, spread on slides and preserved in 100% ice-cold methanol for 20 min at -20 °C. Slides were rinsed with PBS, blocked for 1 h with closure buffer (1 PBS/5% normal serum/0.3% Triton X-100), incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies raised against LC3B (1:200), E4BP4 (1:150) or HDAC3 (1:200) and at 25 °C for 1 h with secondary antibodies labeled with Alexa Fluor488 or 594. Nuclei were labeled with DAPI (C0065, Solarbio, China) for 5 min and immunofluorescence staining analyzed by confocal laser lens (LSM780, Zeiss, Germany).

Transmission electron microscopy

Cells were fixed with 3% glutaraldehyde, post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated with acetone gradient (10 min each at 30%, 50%, 70%, 90% and 100%) and embedded with Epon 812 (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA) resin. Semi-thin sections (~ 1 μm) were stained with methylene blue and ultrathin sections (~ 70 nm) were cut with a diamond scalpel and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate for analysis with JEM-1400-FLASH transmission electron microscope (Jeol, China).

Luciferase reporter assay

HEK-293T and U266 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and grown to 60–70% confluence. Co-transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were transfected with pGL3-gene reporter plasmid, OE-E4BP4 overexpression plasmid, and pRL-TK Renilla plasmid at an 80:1:1 ratio (w/w), using a total of 500 ng DNA per well. Transfection was carried out in serum-free medium for 6 h, after which the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. At 24–48 h post-transfection, cells were lysed with 100 µL passive lysis buffer, and luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega Corporation, USA). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity, and results were presented as fold-induction relative to a standardized control group.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

HEK-293T and U266 cells were cultured to ~ 80% confluence, crosslinked in 1% formalin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), lysed with SDS buffer and DNA fragmented by sonication. DNA-protein complexes were precipitated using antibodies raised against E4BP4, H3K9Ac (PTM156, Jingjie PTM BioLab, China) and H3K27Ac (PTM160, Jingjie PTM BioLab) with normal isotype-matched IgGs as negative controls. ChIP was performed using a kit (abs50034, Absin Bioscience, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and DNA collected for qPCR. Primer sequences corresponded to HDAC3 and Beclin1 promoters and are given in Table S3.

Flow-cytometric apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was measured using allophycocyanin conjugated to annexin V (APC-Annexin-V) and 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD) kit (BioLegend, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence was detected by FACScan flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA) and data analyzed with FlowJo software.

CCK-8 assay

Cells were plated onto 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells/well and growth rate evaluated after 0–4 days using a CCK-8 kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured in triplicate at 450 nm by Synergy2 microplate reader (Bio-Rad).

TUNEL assay

DNA fragmentation was assessed by terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated deoxyuridine triphosphate nick-end labeling (TUNEL). In brief, fixed cells or tissue samples were permeabilized with 0.2% TX-100 in PBS for 30 min at room temperature, washed with PBS and treated with 300 U/mL TUNEL enzyme and 6 µmol/L biotinylated deoxyuridine triphosphate (Roche Diagnostics, Germany). Biotin-coupled DNA termini were indicated by a 1:1,000 dilution in PBS of Cy-3-coupled streptavidin (Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA). Slides were washed with PBS, stained with DAPI, rinsed and mounted with Fluoromount G (Southern Biotech, USA) for analysis by Nikon fluorescence microscope (NIS Element AR 4.20.01, Japan).

ChIP-seq data and motif analysis

Two datasets of ChIP-seq of E4BP4 in HepG2 cells, GSM2527590 and GSM2527591, were accessed from the GEO database and Bowtie2 [16] was used to map raw data to the human reference genome (hg19 version). ChIP-seq peaks were detected by MACS2 and DNA sequences identified by BEDtools getfasta (https://bedtools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/content/tools/getfasta.html).

ChIP-seq data was visualized in the appropriate genomic area by Cistrome Data Browser (http://cistrome.org/db). DNA segments from the E4BP4–HDAC3 binding site were analyzed by HOMER script findMotifGenome.pl (https://github.com/IGBIllinois/HOMER/blob/master/bin/findMotifsGenome.pl) to identify de novo and recognized transcription factor motifs with high background enrichment patterns and potential false-positives eliminated during manual processing.

DNA modeling and molecular docking

3D structures of wild type and mutant DNA were predicted using the 3dRNA/DNA server (http://biophy.hust.edu.cn/new/3dRNA/create) and ZDOCK 3.0.2 was used to predict DNA binding to the target protein, E4BP4. E4BP4 structure was accessed from the AlphaFold Protein Structure database (https://www.alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/, retrieval number: Q16649). E4BP4 and DNA structures were treated by PyMOL 2.5.2 (https://pymol.org/2/) to remove hydrogen atoms and predict unreasonable protein structure. ZDOCK 3.0.2 was used for docking analysis with global rigid stacking and scoring. Conformations with the best scoring results were optimized by energy minimization using AMBER software (https://ambermd.org) and visualized with PyMOL 2.5.2 [17].

MM xenograft animal model

Female BALB/c nude mice aged five weeks were purchased from Jiangsu Gempharmatech Co., Ltd. (China) and housed in the Clinical Research Center, Guizhou Medical University Affiliated Hospital under specific pathogen-free conditions. Subcutaneous injection of 1 × 107 U266 cells into the right flank was performed and xenograft tumors allowed to reach a volume of 200 mm3. Mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 8) and 10 mg/kg intraperitoneal Dex administered twice a week. Tumor diameter was measured by caliper every 2–3 days and tumor volume calculated as the shortest diameter2 × longest diameter/2. Mice were sacrificed by intraperitoneal injection of 150 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium, followed by cervical dislocation after 28 days and tumors weighed. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (2011) and ethical approval was granted by the ethics review board of Guizhou Medical University Affiliated Hospital (approval no.: 2304965).

Statistical analysis

Student’s t-test or ANOVA were used to analyze data using Prism Software (GraphPad, USA) and the Xiantao tool (https://www.xiantao.love/). Data are presented as mean ± SD. A value of p < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

E4BP4 was expressed at higher levels in MM

E4BP4 expression was analyzed across a range of tumor cell-lines, including those of hematological origin, from the CCLE database. Analysis revealed that E4BP4 mRNA is present at higher levels in MM than in other tumor cell lines (Figs. 1A, B). Transcriptomic E4BP4 expression was also examined in MM patient RNA-seq datasets, GSE5900, GSE6477 and GSE39683, from the GEO and Oncomine (www.oncomine.org) databases. E4BP4 expression was higher in MM samples than normal bone marrow samples (Figs. 1C–F). Single-cell RNA-seq data from the GEO dataset, GSM5172712 (Fig. S1), showed that B, T, natural killer (NK) and dendritic (DC) cells were present in the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 1G–H). Specifically, Clusters 0–4 mainly represented B cell populations, Clusters 5, 6, and 10 represented T cells, while other clusters included NK cells (Cluster 7), DCs (Cluster 8), and Monocytes/Macrophages (Cluster 9). High E4BP4 expression was observed in Cluster 10, a subclass of T cells (Figs. 1I–K). Western blotting of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from 15 MM patients and 9 healthy controls showed higher levels of E4BP4 protein in MM PBMCs (Fig. 1L). MM cell-lines, RPMI8226 and U266, also showed higher E4BP4 expression than control PBMCs (Fig. 1M). These data concluded that E4BP4 is upregulated in MM and is highly expressed in microenvironmental T cells from the microenvironment of MM bone marrow.

E4BP4 may have utility as a prognostic marker for MM

Kaplan-Meier prognostic survival analysis was conducted using on E4BP4 expression from RNA-seq data from the GSE136400 dataset from the GEO database. Higher E4BP4 expression correlated both with lower overall survival (OS) and lower progression-free survival (PFS, Figs. 2A-B). ROC curve analysis showed AUC values at 1, 3 and 5 years of 0.655, 0.648 and 0.604, respectively, indicating its diagnostic efficacy for E4BP4 and its potential utility as a prognostic marker (Fig. 2C). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses identified E4BP4, gender, stage, albumin and β2-microglobulin as independent prognostic factors for MM patient survival (Fig. 2D, E). The c-myc oncogene is acknowledged to be involved in MM onset and progression [18]. Patients in the GSE136400 dataset were divided into high and low c-myc expression groups, according to median c-myc expression, and E4BP4 expression was evaluated. E4BP4 was expressed at higher levels in the high c-myc expression group, suggesting an oncogenic role for E4BP4 (Fig. 2F). Stratifying patients into high and low expressing groups based on the median value of E4BP4 expression allowed the development of a risk-prognosis model. Risk factor mapping showed that mortality rates increased in line with E4BP4 expression (Fig. 2G).

A nomogram was constructed incorporating E4BP4 expression, international staging system [ISS] stage, β2-MG, LDH, albumin, c-myc and p53 levels and the calibration graph (Fig. 2H) indicates the efficiency of the nomogram in predicting 1, 3 and 5 year OS of MM patients. The risk signature score, including E4BP4 expression, had higher AUC values for forecasting OS over 1, 3 and 5 years than clinical factors alone (Fig. 2I).

Data from the MMRF-CoMMpass database was used as a stand-alone MM dataset for external validation of the utility of E4BP4 expression. Kaplan-Meier analysis correlated elevated E4BP4 expression with poor prognosis (Fig. 3A) giving AUC values for 1-, 3- and 5-year ROC curves of 0.557, 0.636 and 0.637, respectively (Fig. 3B). Univariate and multivariate regression analyses of clinical variables from TCGA-MMRF datasets identified E4BP4 as an independent prognostic factor for MM (Figs. 3C-D), a finding that was consistent with analysis of the GSE136400 dataset. Subgroup analyses further confirmed that E4BP4 expression levels, as well as its prognostic significance, were not influenced by patient gender or age (P > 0.05). RT-qPCR analysis of bone marrow mononuclear cell samples from 30 MM patients showed positive correlation of E4BP4 expression with clinicopathological factors and negative correlation with hemoglobin levels (Fig. 3E). E4BP4 mRNA was found to be present at higher levels in ISS stage III disease than in stages I - II of the MMRF-CoMMpass cohort (Fig. 3F). Immunohistochemical analysis of 12 MM patients revealed that E4BP4 was primarily localized to the nucleus and cytoplasm of myeloma cells. Furthermore, quantification of the IHC staining confirmed that both E4BP4 and the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl2 were significantly upregulated in stage III disease compared to stage II (Fig. 3G). This clinical co-expression pattern prompted us to further investigate whether E4BP4 functionally regulates MM cell apoptosis in subsequent in vitro experiments.

CIBERSORT immune infiltration analysis indicated high E4BP4 expression in B cells, T cells and macrophages of the tumor immune environment (Fig. S2A–C). Immunohistochemical analysis showed reduced levels of CD8+ T-cells in stage III patients compared with stage II patients (Fig. S2D). Thus, high E4BP4 expression is correlated with lower numbers of CD8+ T cells in the MM microenvironment and associated with immunosuppression.

E4BP4 overexpression inhibited apoptosis in U266 cells

Knockdown of E4BP4 in U266 cells after lentiviral transfection with shRNA-E4BP4 was confirmed by Western blotting (Fig. 4A). E4BP4 knockdown inhibited Bcl-XL and Bcl2 expression (Fig. 4B) and reduced cell proliferation (Fig. 4C). Overexpression of E4BP4 promoted Bcl2 and Bcl-XL expression in U266 cells (Figs. 4D, E) and stimulated proliferation (Fig. 4F). These results raise the possibility that E4BP4 inhibits apoptosis in U266 cells.

Flow cytometric analysis using APC-Annexin-V and 7-AAD assays of apoptosis in E4BP4 knockdown or overexpressing U266 cells showed increased apoptosis rates in E4BP4-knockdown cells and decreased rates in E4BP4-overexpressing cells (Fig. 4G). TEM imaging revealed typical apoptotic characteristics of cells of reduced size with fragments indicating nuclear lysis after E4BP4 knockdown but not in overexpressing cells (Fig. 4I). In addition, E4BP4 knockdown increased the number of TUNEL-positive U266 cells but E4BP4 overexpression had the opposite effect (Fig. 4H). It may be concluded that increased E4BP4 expression was associated with reduced apoptosis in MM cells.

High E4BP4 expression suppressed early stage autophagy and caused reduced apoptosis

Interactions between autophagy and apoptosis impact cell survival and death [7]. GSEA of the GSE136400 dataset indicated an association between high E4BP4 expression and the activation of cancer-related pathways, including the enrichment of mTOR signaling (Fig. 5A) which is known to be an upstream regulator of autophagy [19]. Western blotting of U266 cells after E4BP4 knockdown revealed increased LC3-II and Beclin1 and decreased P62 expression with decreased phosphorylated, but not total, mTOR (Fig. 5B). Opposing findings were observed in U266 cells overexpressing E4BP4, in which Beclin1, ATG5 and ATG7 decreased while P62 expression increased (Figs. 5C-D). These results imply that E4BP4 knockdown has an autophagy activating effect. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that the puncta of endogenous LC3B were increased in E4BP4 knockdown and reduced in overexpressing E4BP4 U266 cells (Fig. 5E) and cells with E4BP4 knockdown showed numerous autophagosomes and primary lysosomes during TEM analysis (Fig. 5F).

Treatment with 3-MA, an inhibitor of early-stage autophagosome membrane formation [20], attenuated the p62 degradation and LC3-II and Beclin1 accumulation induced by E4BP4 knockdown (Fig. 5G). 3-MA also reversed the increase in endogenous LC3B fluorescent puncta (Fig. 5J) and rescued the decrease in Bcl2 due to E4BP4 knockdown (Fig. 5L). APC-Annexin-V and 7-AAD apoptosis assays confirmed that 3-MA reversed the apoptosis enhancing effect of E4BP4 knockdown (Fig. 5N). Rapamycin (RAPA) is an autophagy activator and mTOR inhibitor [21]. RAPA treatment reversed the decreased LC3-II and Beclin1, increased P62 and mTOR activation, and reduced LC3B fluorescent puncta caused by E4BP4 overexpression in U266 cells (Fig. 5H–K). Bcl2 was also decreased (Fig. 5M) and apoptosis APC-Annexin-V and 7-AAD assays showed increased apoptosis rates in E4BP4 overexpressing U266 cells after RAPA treatment (Fig. 5O). Chloroquine (CQ) and 3-MA block autophagy at late and early stages, respectively [20, 22–24]. 3-MA treatment of U266 cells with E4BP4 knockdown caused reduced LC3-II and Beclin1 and increased P62, but subsequent addition of CQ caused LC3, Beclin1 and P62 expression to change in the same direction (Fig. 5I). This phenomenon suggests that E4BP4 knockdown had an activating effect on autophagy, causing onset of early stage autophagy, rather than inducing a downstream blockade that indirectly resulting in autophagy. Changes in Bcl2 expression after E4BP4 knockdown plus 3-MA, followed by CQ addition indicated an enhanced reduction in apoptosis (Fig. 5P). The current findings indicate that high E4BP4 expression suppresses early stage autophagy leading to reduced apoptosis.

E4BP4 activated transcription of the HDAC3 gene

Histone deacetylases (HDACs) have been implicated in MM [25] and are known to be involved in autophagy [26]. E4BP4 mRNA expression was found to correlate with HDAC1, HDAC3 and HDAC8 mRNA in the GSE136400 dataset (Fig. 6A). It has previously been established that HDAC3 promotes proliferation and anti-apoptotic pathways in MM cells [27–28] and this histone deacetylase was selected for further study. RT-qPCR analysis of E4BP4 and HDAC3 mRNA found a correlation between the expression of E4BP4 and HDAC3 in 30 patients with MM (Fig. 6B). HDAC3 mRNA expression was coordinated with that of E4BP4 in both E4BP4-knockdown and -overexpressing U266 cells (Fig. 6C) and these results were consistent with those from Western blotting (Figs. 6D–E). Endogenous E4BP4 and HDAC3 were shown to interact by co-immunoprecipitation assay (Fig. 6F) and intracellular co-localization of the two proteins was confirmed by immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 6G).

The findings described above raise the possibility that the HDAC3 gene is a direct target of E4BP4. Target sites for E4BP4 were predicted using the hTFTarget database (guolab.wchscu.cn/hTFtarget/#!/, Fig. 6H) and E4BP4 ChIP-seq data from two HepG2 cell lines, GSM2527590 and GSM2527591, were analyzed using the Cistrome Data Browser. E4BP4 showed enriched peaks at the HDAC3 promoter (Fig. 6I–K) and promoter activity of HDAC3 was enhanced by E4BP4 overexpression (Fig. 6L). Sequence analysis allowed the identification of two putative E4BP4 binding motifs in the HDAC3 promoter. Binding site 1 was located between − 165 and − 153 bp and binding site 2 between − 345 and − 333 bp. Two mutated versions of the HDAC3 promoter were designed with base changes incorporated within the motifs (Fig. 6M). Mutation of the HDAC3 promoter-binding site 1 inhibited the increased reporter activity induced by E4BP4 overexpression (Fig. 6N). ChIP assays confirmed that E4BP4 interacts with the wild-type (WT) sequence of binding site 1 in chromatin isolated from cross-linked cells was shown to interact with E4BP4 in ChIP assays (Fig. 6O). Docking studies of the WT and binding site 1 mutant promoter sequences showed superior binding of E4BP4 to the WT sequence compared to the mutant sequence (Fig. 6P).

Western blot analysis of cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions showed increased nuclear E4BP4 when the protein was overexpressed and decreased nuclear E4BP4 when the protein was knocked down, a subcellular localization that was supported by immunofluorescence staining after E4BP4 knockdown (Figs. 6Q–R). H3K9Ac and H3K27Ac were both found to decrease with E4BP4 overexpression and increase with E4BP4 knockdown, which inversely correlated with HDAC3 expression (Fig. 6Q). The recruitment of HDAC3 to the Beclin1 promoter has been shown [29] and ChIP-qPCR analysis during the current work indicated enrichment of H3K9Ac and H3K27Ac at the Beclin1 promoter which was reduced by E4BP4 overexpression (Fig. 6S–T). It may be inferred that E4BP4 activates transcription of HDAC3 and downregulates H3K9Ac and H3K27Ac.

E4BP4 upregulated HDAC3 transcription and inhibited autophagy and apoptosis in U266 cells

The stimulation of HDAC3 transcription by E4BP4 and of MM cell proliferation by HDAC3 suggest that E4BP4 may regulate autophagy and apoptosis in MM cells via an effect on HDAC3. RT-qPCR analysis of 30 MM patient samples showed higher HDAC3 expression in stage III than in stages I or II (Fig. 7A–B). HDAC3 expression correlated positively with the proportion of bone marrow primitive plasma cells but negatively with hemoglobin levels (Figs. 7B–C). Among three siRNA sequences to knock down HDAC3 expression and Si-1002 was found to exhibit the highest knockdown efficiency (Fig. 7G). Cells were co-transfected with Si-1002 to knockdown HDAC3 and an E4BP4-overexpressing vector. HDAC3 knockdown reversed the increased Bcl2 levels and reduced rate of apoptosis caused by E4BP4 overexpression (Figs. 7D-E). KEGG pathway analysis also demonstrated a negative correlation between HDAC3 and autophagy (Fig. 7F) and HDAC3 knockdown increased expression of LC3, Beclin1, decreased P62 and phosphorylated mTOR, although total mTOR was unchanged (Fig. 7G). Thus, it appears that HDAC3 knockdown has the effect of activating autophagy, consistent with the current findings on autophagy regulation by E4BP4. Both Western blotting and immunofluorescence staining showed that HDAC3 knockdown also reversed the decrease in LC3, Beclin1, increase P62 and phosphorylated mTOR caused by E4BP4 overexpression, an effect that was accentuated by RAPA treatment (Figs. 7H-I). These findings suggest that E4BP4 suppresses autophagy and apoptosis through the transcriptional upregulation of HDAC3.

E4BP4 knockdown and Dex promoted MM cell autophagy and apoptosis in vitro and in vivo

Dex is a commonly used anti-cancer drug in MM, but clinical resistance often develops [30]. The combination of E4BP4 knockdown and Dex administration resulted in a greater degree of apoptosis in U266 cells than either treatment alone but this effect could not be recapitulated by HDAC3 knockdown (Fig. 8A). Maximal rates of apoptosis were observed following concurrent knockdown of both E4BP4 and HDAC3 plus Dex treatment (Fig. 8B). Double knockdown of E4BP4 and HDAC3 plus Dex treatment also led to greater increases in LC3 andBeclin1, and decreases in P62 and Bcl2, compared with individual treatments (Fig. 8C). The treatment regime of E4BP4 knockdown plus Dex, followed by 3-MA reversed the increases in LC3 and Beclin1 and the decrease in P62 observed in the absence of 3-MA (Fig. 8D).

In vivo investigations involved the random assignment of mice to four treatment groups: tumor produced from U266 cells transfected with NC vector; tumor produced from U266 cells transfected with NC vector and treatment with Dex; tumor produced from U266 cells transfected with Sh-E4BP4 vector; tumor produced from U266 cells transfected with Sh-E4BP4 vector and treated with Dex. Dex treatment began when tumors reached a volume of approximately 200 mm3 (Fig. 8E). E4BP4 knockdown in combination with Dex caused a greater reduction in tumor growth than either treatment alone (Figs. 8F–H) and immunohistochemical staining of tumor tissue showed decreased HDAC3 and increased LC3 expression and decreased p62 expression relative to other groups (Fig. 8I). TUNEL staining also showed increased numbers of apoptotic cells after E4BP4 knockdown and Dex treatment (Fig. 8J). We conclude that E4BP4 knockdown in conjunction with Dex therapy induced autophagy and promoted apoptosis in U266 cells in vitro and in vivo.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effects of E4BP4 on autophagy, apoptosis and Dex sensitivity in MM cells. We found that MM patients with high E4BP4 expressing exhibited reduced Dex sensitivity, more advanced disease and shorter survival times compared to low expressing patients. Clinical sensitivity to Dex has previously been shown to vary with cancer types and the drug can influence the effectiveness of other anti-tumor drugs agents. For example, Dex has been shown to inhibit p53-dependent senescence and reduce the sensitivity to cisplatin of non-small cell lung cancer cells [31], but can also activate the glucocorticoid receptor, inducing senescence in other lung cancer cells models [32]. The suppression of autophagy maintains populations of myeloma cells [33] and increased rates lead to autophagic apoptosis [34]. Clioquinol has previously been shown to target the mTOR pathway, triggering apoptotic autophagy in leukemia and myeloma cells [35] and ZBTB28 stimulates autophagy by an effect on modulating FIP200 and Bcl-XL expression, promoting apoptosis in cervical cancer cells [36]. Thus, crosstalk between autophagy and apoptosis is critical for maintaining tumor cell survival. Consistent with this, the MM cells of the current work showed enhanced autophagy and apoptosis when E4BP4 was knocked down.

E4BP4 is a basic mammalian leucine-zipper transcription factor and is known to be highly expressed in many tumor types, in which it inhibits apoptosis [12, 37]. The activation of the STAT3/E4BP4 pathway decreased apoptosis in choriocarcinoma cells, promoting chemotherapy resistance [9] and E4BP4 reduced RASSF8-mediated apoptosis in breast cancer cells, promoting tumor progression [38]. E4BP4 promoted disease progression by inhibiting autophagy-dependent apoptosis in the myeloma cells of the current study. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to show that E4BP4 suppresses autophagy in MM and this effect may account for decreased apoptosis. Increased MM apoptosis due to E4BP4 knockdown exposes the potential for enhanced anticancer treatment. E4BP4 has previously been shown to act as a transcriptional repressor that inhibits T cell function [39]. Consistent with our single-cell RNA-seq and CIBERSORT findings showing high E4BP4 expression in specific immune subsets (such as T cell Cluster 10), we observed a seemingly paradoxical reduction in CD8 + T cell numbers in advanced stage patients. This can be explained by the functional consequence of E4BP4 overexpression: high E4BP4 levels within these T cell clusters likely induce T-cell exhaustion or dysfunction, ultimately leading to the depletion of cytotoxic CD8 + T cells. Therefore, one crucial route by which E4BP4 promotes MM progression is by actively fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment through the impairment of T cell survival and function. Therefore, we propose that E4BP4 drives MM progression through a dual mechanism: intrinsically promoting tumor cell survival by inhibiting HDAC3-mediated autophagy and apoptosis, and extrinsically fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment by driving T cell dysfunction. This dual impact reinforces the potential of E4BP4 as a promising therapeutic target.

Epigenetic signaling pathways may be involved in tumorigenesis and dysregulation of epigenetics has been implicated in several cancers, including MM [40]. HDAC inhibition may promote autophagy [41] and the novel HDAC inhibitor, chidamide, has been shown to induce BTG1-mediated autophagy in B cell lymphomas and reverse chemotherapy resistance [26]. Treatment with a combination of HDAC and proteasome inhibitors has been successful for relapsed or refractory MM in clinical trials [42]. The epigenetic functions of E4BP4 have been studied extensively [43] and histone deacetylase activity is thought to be linked to MM cell viability [44], since HDAC inhibition was found to enhance MM cell autophagy [45]. However, upstream regulators of HDAC activity remain poorly understood. E4BP4 bound to the − 165 and − 153 bp region of the HDAC3 promoter in the nucleus of U266 cells in the current study and E4BP4 expression correlated with HDAC3 expression. HDAC3 upregulation corresponded to activation of mTOR signaling and decreased recruitment of H3K9Ac and H3K27Ac to the Beclin1 promoter. This sequence of events explains autophagy inhibition by E4BP4. E4BP4 knockdown enhanced autophagy and increased Dex sensitivity in U266 cells in vitro and in vivo. (A) E4BP4 binds to the HDAC3 promoter and interacts with it, regulating the transcription of Bcl2/Bcl-XL and Beclin1, thereby balancing apoptosis and autophagy. (B) Dexamethasone modulates cell survival, autophagy, and apoptosis through the E4BP4-mTOR pathway and others; knocking down E4BP4 disrupts this signaling and alters cell fate.

The current findings have a potential clinical application since Dex is often the drug of choice for MM patients but its use is bedeviled by the development of resistance [30]. We show that E4BP4 may be instrumental in Dex resistance in MM cells. Knockdown of both E4BP4 and HDAC3 maximized the restoration of Dex sensitivity in U266 cells both in vitro and in vivo and also maximized the anti-tumor impact during the current work. We believe that the anti-tumor effect was achieved by the activation of autophagy and promotion of apoptosis by E4BP4 and HDAC3 knockdown. In addition, the observation that E4BP4 was downregulated to different degrees during Dex stimulation of MM cell apoptosis, suggesting E4BP4 as a target of Dex, deserves further investigation. It is worth noting a limitation of the current study. Although a single shRNA construct was utilized for E4BP4 knockdown, the bidirectional phenotypic changes observed during E4BP4 overexpression, combined with the successful downstream rescue via HDAC3 knockdown, strongly support the on-target specificity of these biological effects. Future studies employing multiple knockdown sequences or CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout will be beneficial to further validate these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the current data indicates that E4BP4 prevents autophagy and apoptosis in MM cells by upregulating the transcription of HDAC3 and activating mTOR signaling to reduce histone acetylation at the Beclin1 promoter. These findings explain the positive correlation between high E4BP4 expression and poor observed among many cancer types. E4BP4 knockdown demonstrated potent anti-MM action in vitro and in vivo, increasing autophagy. We propose that targeted anti-E4BP4 therapies in conjunction with Dex constitute a potential novel therapeutic approach for the treatment of MM.
