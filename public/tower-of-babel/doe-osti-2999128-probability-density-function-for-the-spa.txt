ABSTRACT
The ability to model semiconductor device degradation under neutron irradiation depends upon
having a robust modeling capability for the neutron-induced collision cascades as well as a means to
analytically fit the resulting probability distributions of defect production and ionizing energy depo-
sition for purposes of extrapolation to low-probability, high-consequence scenarios. In this paper,
the widely-utilized binary collision approximation codes MARLOWE and SRIM are deployed in
conjunction with a critical examination of their parameterizations as benchmarked against higher-
fidelity molecular dynamics simulations. A simple 3-parameter form described by the Generalized
Logistic Distribution is shown to be a good fit to Frenkel pair and ionization intensity distributions
in bulk silicon. The BCA codes are then applied to simulate cascades in 5 nm layers of a represen-
tative gate-all-around nanosheet transistor, where joint probability distributions of threshold levels
of damage to multiple layers are evaluated.

1. INTRODUCTION

The proper functioning of electronic devices in radiation environments is a key concern within
Sandia’s survivability mission. Several mechanisms of radiation-induced degradation of electronics
exist, some of which are particularly sensitive to neutron environments which can produce heavy
ion recoils that generate a high density of ionization in the immediate vicinity of the energetic recoil
event. Furthermore, recoils from high-energy neutron interactions have a significant probability
of being accompanied by lighter reaction products such as alpha particles, which can generate
ionization at substantially farther ranges in comparison to the heavy residual ions.
Energetic neutron interactions in materials give rise to displacement damage and ionization, which
are known to be stochastic phenomena exhibiting a large relative standard deviation in relevant
metrics such as Frenkel pair production and cluster size. In addition to this intrinsic variability,
model parameterization can also strongly influence such metrics in a more systematic manner.
Because displacement damage and ionization are both linked to the degradation of semiconductor
devices, it is important to understand the fundamental nature of damage from irradiation as well
as the likelihood of events that will induce upsets in larger-scale systems. State-of-the-art systems
(particularly for non-volatile memory) often incorporate error correction mechanisms to recover
from individual bit upsets. Since the recovery function requires some characteristic time to execute,
it is possible to overwhelm the system with a sufficiently high rate of upsets such that contiguous
memory elements upset in an effectively simultaneous and irrecoverable manner. This is termed
a “multi-bit upset” (MBU) and, significantly, it may occur in lower flux conditions through single
particle interactions that create damage over a large enough volume to upset multiple memory
elements. Such “low probability, high consequence” occurrences must be quantified to make
probabilistic assurances of system reliability.
Computational tools such as the Transport of Ions in Matter (SRIM/TRIM) Binary Collision
Approximation (BCA) code [1], the MARLOWE BCA code [2], and the LAMMPS molecular
dynamics (MD) code [3] will be used to investigate the short-term damage production process from
the time of neutron interaction until the formation of a quasi-stable array of defects—a time span
on the order of picoseconds. While the BCA technique is limited to the immediate outcome of
the recoil-induced collision cascade, MD simulations, in the modern computing environment, can
realistically be extended out to nanoseconds or longer, but, for our modeling purposes of representing
the initial Frenkel pair production and charge deposition, calculations out to picoseconds are
adequate. “Damage” here encompasses the amount and spatial layout of ionization as well as
point defects such as Frenkel pairs. Beyond the post-irradiation time at which the cascade-induced
thermal spike has dissipated and thermally-activated defect migration (at ambient temperature)
becomes necessary for further defect-defect or defect-impurity reactions, evolution of the damage
typically must be handled using other techniques, such as finite element drift-diffusion or kinetic
Monte Carlo (kMC).

2. COMPUTATIONAL MODELS

2.1. Molecular Dynamics: LAMMPS

LAMMPS is a classical Molecular Dynamics (MD) code meant for modeling computationally
material large structures in parallel. Within LAMMPS, many-body interatomic potentials are used
to model the dynamic behavior of the material given an initial state and boundary condition. For
energetic events, such as displacement cascades, a repulsive and chemical interatomic potential
are hybridized for the simulation. An advantage that MD codes like LAMMPS has is that it can
model the short-time annealing (1-100 picoseconds) of defects produced from a Primary Knock-On
Atom (PKA) event. However, the computational costs of running MD scale O(n3 ) with the number
of atoms and the minimum width of the simulation box scales proportionally the recoil energy of
the PKA event. As a result, the energetic cap for MD simulations of displacement cascades, due to
computational limitations, are on the order of 10s of keV for heavy ions.

2.2.       BCA: SRIM

SRIM is a freely-available and popular tool for performing fast-running simulations of energetic
ion impingement on multi-layer material stackups. Collision cascade events are modelled under the
binary collision approximation with a further assumption of amorphous media, which simplifies the
characterization of collision events (and, of course, disallows phenomena particular to crystalline
materials such as channeling and focusing).
In spite of its widespread adoption within the radiation effects and ion implantation communities,
the proper means of deploying SRIM for various applications has been subject to debate, and
specific recommendations have been published on mode of execution and input parameter selection
[4, 5]. A recurring topic of contention is the distinction between the “full cascade” and “quick”
mode of TRIM, the former being a full Monte Carlo BCA treatment of PKAs and SKAs and
the latter applying the Norgett-Robinson-Torrens (NRT) formalism to SKAs produced along the
incident ion track. It is generally recognized that the full cascade mode invokes a more physically
sound approach, especially in multi-elemental systems [5]. Quick mode, on the other hand, aligns
closer with the theoretical underpinnings of certain NRT-based standard metrics such as radiation
exposure (in units of displacements per atom (DPA)).
As MARLOWE and SRIM utilize the same basic methodology of simulating displacement damage,
most input parameters and outputted quantities are directly analogous. Significant differences
exist, however. For example, both codes require lattice binding energies that correspond to the
“energetic penalty” of defect production, but SRIM makes use of the threshold displacement
energy whereas MARLOWE defines a “cutoff energy” (not necessarily equivalent to the threshold

 17

displacement energy). Also, the ZBL interatomic potential is the only option permitted in SRIM
while MARLOWE accommodates several potential functions.

2.3. BCA: MARLOWE

MARLOWE is a BCA code which has an advantage over SRIM in that it is capable of treating
the effect of channeling due to crystalline structure of the medium. While development on the
official MARLOWE distribution available from Radiation Safety Information Computational Center
(RSICC) appears to have ceased, it continues to be used[6] and developed[7, 8] independently.
The base MARLOWE currently in use at Sandia National Laboratories (SNL) is version 15b
from RSICC, which has undergone several modifications to add Ziegler-Biersack-Littmark (ZBL)
inelastic loss model[9], increase numerical precision, increase the maximum cascade size, and fix
numerous minor bugs.
MARLOWE has numerous model options to treat the elastic and inelastic processes.

2.4. Radiation Transport: MRED and G4SEE

The open-source G4SEE[10] was investigated for use in this work. G4SEE is a toolkit built upon
the Monte Carlo transport code GEANT4, and is aimed at being a user-friendly interface to the
physics options relevant to modeling Single Event Effects (SEE). MRED[11] is a similar tool
developed at Vanderbilt University also built upon GEANT4.
The advantage these codes have is that where a user must supply the characteristics of the PKA
in a BCA code, the PKA can be sampled randomly according to the recoil distributions defined
in the incident neutron nuclear data libraries. This vastly simplifies the problem definition where,
for example, the PKA is produced by a neutron strike in one material, but deposits its energy in
a different, adjacent material. These codes are specialized to the SEE application, and inputting
appropriate physics, geometry and biasing options is simplified.
A disadvantage to these codes is that they cannot model the recoil cascade in detail. While Non-
Ionizing Energy Loss (NIEL) can be separated from the ionizing energy deposition along a particle
track, it is not possible to extract details of the defect production in the crystal structure. There is
a capability[12] to model the effect of particle channeling in a crystalline lattice in GEANT4, but
it is not clear how a user would integrate those models in to the codes. G4SEE has an additional
disadvantage with respect to the work here in that it can only model a single sensitive volume.
Neither of these codes were used for the work here but could perhaps be used to to extend the work
performed as part of this study generate detailed spectra of heavy and light recoil ions a produced
in or entering a sensitive volume.

3. INTENSITY DISTRIBUTION

The methods for deriving the probability density functions for Frenkel pair production and ion-
ization in Silicon are described in this section. The modeling sensitivities inherent in the BCA
approximation will be explored, before deriving a set of parameters and physics options that pro-
duce probability density functions which are in good agreement with high-fidelity MD simulations.
Finally, analytic probability distributions will be fitted to the Frenkel pair and ionization energy
fractions to provide a means of estimating the probabilities of rare but high-consequence events.

3.1. Mono-energetic Projectiles

Before considering the recoil energy distributions produced by an incident neutron, we first seek to
characterize the probability density functions of single ion energies.
Si28 PKAs are sampled randomly in space and injection angle. The initial azimuthal angle is
sampled uniformly between 0 and 360 degrees. A cosine weighting is applied to the polar angle:

where U(0,2) is a uniform random variable defined in the range 0 to 2.

3.1.1. MARLOWE Modeling Sensitivities

Aside from the binding energy and recombination distance, there are a number of model options
in MARLOWE which have a non-negligible impact on the results. Here we will investigate: 1) the
crystal model - amorphous vs crystalline, 2) the inelastic energy loss model - local vs non-local,

3.2. Calibrating MARLOWE to MD

In later sections, the results obtained using MARLOWE will be compared against MD calculations.
To make meaningful comparisons, we must determine the model parameters in MARLOWE that
best represent the physics modeled by the MD simulation.
MARLOWE calculates the energy transfer between an incident atom and one in a lattice site via
classical mechanics. To dislodge the target atom from its lattice site, the energy transfer must be
larger than the binding energy. MARLOWE defines three binding energies for distinct collision
types. These are stored in a parameter EBND:

 EBND(1) is the cohesive energy. If the energies of both the incident and recoil atoms are greater
 than EBND(1) after the collision, then both particles continue moving and the lattice site is
 vacated.
 EBND(2) is the energy for redisplaced atoms. We set this equal to the cohesive energy, EBND(1).
 EBND(3) is the binding energy for replacement events. In monocrystalline materials where the
 incident and lattice atoms are the same species, this could be set to zero since the overall state
 of the lattice is unchanged after the collision. However, since MARLOWE counts each one
 of these as a displacement, it overestimates the total. EBND(3) can then, in a sense be used
 as a free parameter to calibrate MARLOWE to MD.

Following the work by Vizkelethy and Foiles[18], we calibrate EBND(3) and the re-combination
radius in MARLOWE to match the total number of displaced atoms and the number of stable
pairs.

3.2.2. Calibrating Displacements

The first step is to calibrate MARLOWE’s EBND(3) parameter such that the total number of
displaced atoms reproduces the peak number of displacements in the MD simulation. The total
number of displaced atoms is simply the number of atoms that had enough energy to leave the
lattice site. This quantity is insensitive to recombination effects.
The ZBL potential is used in MARLOWE. The other physics options are set to reflect the MD
modeling: the crystalline geometry and the non-local inelastic energy loss model. A 1 eV cutoff
energy is used.
Figure 3-17 plots the total number of displaced atoms for various EBND(3) values as a√︁function of
PKA energy. The error bars on the MD results are the standard error of the mean (𝜎/ 𝑁 𝑠𝑎𝑚 𝑝𝑙𝑒𝑠 ).
These errors are due to the relatively small number of MD calculations that it was possible to run
for this work. The error bars have been omitted from the MARLOWE calculations since it is trivial
to perform enough calculations to make the statistical errors on the mean negligible. From this
plot, it appears that an EBND(3) value between 2 and 2.5 eV provides a reasonable fit to the MD
results.
Our intent here is to yield Frenkel pair probability density functions which are comparable to MD
to provide a better level of confidence in the BCA results for more complicated quantities later
in this report. A detailed fit of the EBND(3) parameter would require many more expensive MD
calculations, which is beyond the scope of these aims. Here, we simply take EBND(3) = 2 to be a
reasonable approximation.

3.2.3. Calibrating Stable Pairs

Since the BCA calculation has no concept of thermal recombination, the effect can be approximated
by setting a recombination radius. All displaced atoms that are terminated within a given radius of
their lattice sites will be assumed to quickly recombine and are therefore omitted from the stable
pair count.
The recombination range is varied between 2-7 Å. The previously obtained value of EBND(3)
= 2 is used throughout. The recombination range is applied by post-processing the "Separations
of Distant Interstitial-Vacancy Pairs" table in MARLOWE, counting only entries with distances
greater than the threshold. The average number of stable pairs as a function of PKA energy for
various recombination ranges is shown in Figure 3-18. The error bars on the MD calculations are
once again the standard error of the mean number of stable pairs at a time after the number of
defects has reached equilibrium.

3.3. Distribution Fits

The ‘stats‘ module within the Python SciPy package contains a large number of continuous statistical
distributions, along with functions to sample from them, fit them to data, and return the probability
density function given a set of parameters.
All 107 continuous statistical distributions from version 1.11.1 of Scipy.stats were considered.
The Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) method was used to fit each distribution to the
MARLOWE-calculated results. The goodness-of-fit of the distributions were then ranked by the
p-values obtained through the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The ten probability density functions
with the highest p-values for the calculated Frenkel Pair values are plotted in Figure 3-23.
While the Asymmetric Laplace distribution scores highly for both stable pairs and inelastic energy
losses, it exhibits an unphysical inflection point about the location parameter. The Generalized
Hyperbolic function also scores highly but is discarded as it is a rather complex function requiring
five parameters to be fitted.
The generalized logistic distribution ("genlogistic") is selected for its ability to describe Frenkel
pair and inelastic loss probabilities with a simple 3-parameter form. With the location (𝜇) and scale
(𝜎) parameters applied, the generalized logistic distribution takes the form:

                                  𝐹 (𝑥; 𝜇, 𝜎, 𝛼) = (1 + 𝑒 −(𝑥+𝜇) ) −𝛼 /𝜎                           (3.3)

Figure 3-24 shows the best-fit parameters for the generalized logistic function as a function of recoil
energy.
The variation in parameter values with recoil energy is observed to be relatively smooth for the
stable pair distribution. The location and scale parameters follow an exponential-like trend with
increasing recoil energy. The skew parameter 𝛼 shows a decrease for recoil energies up to 25 keV,
which then increases and behaves linearly for recoils greater than 400 keV.
The parametric fits to the inelastic energy loss fraction appear somewhat more chaotic at first glance,
but this is largely due to the normalization inherent in expressing this quantity as a fraction of the
primary recoil energy. Without this normalization, the trends of the location and scale parameters
are similar to those for the stable pairs. In this case, the behavior of the skew parameter shows the
inverse behavior of that for the stable pairs in that it increases briefly up to 25 keV and then steadily
declines, becoming a linear decrease for recoils greater than 400 keV.

3.4.1. Sampling Method

Codes such as CASCADE sample ion energies directly from the recoil distribution of an incident
neutron spectrum. These energies are then used to initialize MARLOWE calculations to calculate
the average Frenkel pair production for a given neutron environment. Since this method samples
ion energies directly from their probability distribution, the statistics quickly converge around the
most probable ion energies.
Another method is to sample ion energies uniformly to produce a database of MARLOWE cal-
culations. Since these samples are independent of any recoil distribution, they can be binned
and weighted to the recoil probabilities of practically any incident neutron energy (provided the
database covers the extents of the recoil energies).

distribution around the peak; however, it did not draw a single sample from the recoil energy
bins above 3.6 MeV. At the lower energy side, the rejection sampling technique is shown to be
progressively noisier and then fails to draw any samples below 10 eV.

4. SPATIAL DISTRIBUTION

In the previous section, the intensity distribution for stable defects and ionization in bulk silicon
was studied using the BCA codes SRIM and MARLOWE. The sensitivity of the distribution
has been studied for the model options: crystalline vs. amorphous, and local vs. non-local
inelastic loss model, and the energy cut off. High fidelity molecular dynamics (MD) calculations
were run in bulk silicon, and the results used to tune the binding energies and recombination in
radii to calibrate the faster running binary collision approximation (BCA) code MARLOWE. The
intensity distributions for the total number of displacements and the number of stable Frenkel pairs
were compared between MD and MARLOWE simulations to verify the effect of channeling in a
crystalline material. A generalized distribution, described by the generalized logistic distribution,
was found to be a reasonable fit to the calculated probability density functions.
In modern, small feature-size devices such the gate-all-around nanosheet (GAA NS) FET, containing
individual layers as thin as 5 nm (about 15 silicon atoms) [26], the characterization of damage in
bulk silicon becomes less relevant. A single recoil event, even of modest energy, can readily
pass through all active channels of a device that is miniaturized to such an extent, as can be
verified through simple ion range estimations in SRIM. To address this class of device, we apply
a probabilistic, parametric approach to assess the likelihood of defect concentrations at various
threshold levels being introduced simultaneously to multiple channels of the GAA NSFET device
from a single neutron collision. Given the extreme thinness of the channels, it is postulated that
the defect concentration can be translated into a degree of disruption of the operational viability of
a channel, which at some point ceases to function properly. Other mechanisms of degradation are
also possible under irradiation, although GAA NSFETs have been found to be highly resilient to
total ionizing dose effects [26].

4.1. FinFET Geometry

In this work, we use a finFET as a representative example of a small feature size device that
could be adversely affected by single-event displacement damage. Specifically we consider a
Gate-All-Around Nanosheet (GAA NS) device[27].
The dimensions of the sensitive region of the GAA NS device are inferred from schematics[28] and
micrograph images[29] in the literature. The dimensions of the model are shown in Figure 4-1.
The channel layers are all assumed to be 5 nm in depth, as are each of the intervening layers of gate
metal. The length of the sheets is estimated at 50 nm from the micrograph images, and their width
is estimated at 10 nm.

5. SUMMARY

For neutron-induced total dose effects in semiconducting devices, it is a reasonable approximation to
simply take the mean defect production or ionization rates per PKA energy and scale by the incident
neutron fluence and spectrum-dependent recoil probabilities. In small-feature size devices, the
probability of a neutron event occurring in the sensitive volume decreases, while the concentration
of defects produced by a single neutron event within the volume increases. For such devices, the
probability densities for a single neutron event can be more important for understanding failure
probabilities than the aggregated statistics, which assume numerous neutron events within a sensitive
volume.
In this report, we document a statistical approach to intensity distributions for neutron-induced
defect and ionization production in silicon devices. The BCA technique employed by codes such as
MARLOWE and SRIM is approximate, but it is a practical and computationally tractable method
for modeling large numbers of recoil cascades, as opposed to expensive MD simulations. Here,
we demonstrate the sensitivity of the ionization and displacement cascade to the physics options
in MARLOWE. It was shown that not only does the choice of physics model option change the
mean ionization and defect quantities, but the shapes of the distributions also change. For example,
the amorphous composition option predicted Gaussian-like probability density functions, while the
effect of channeling enabled by the crystalline composition produced a non-Gaussian tail in both
the defect and ionization probabilities. However, when the crystalline composition model was used
in conjunction with the non-local inelastic energy loss model, it was shown that the MARLOWE
Frenkel pair production rates could be calibrated to reproduce high-fidelity MD results by tuning
only two parameters. Not only did MARLOWE agree with MD on the mean values for the total
number of displaced atoms and stable Frenkel pairs, but the probability density functions were also
shown to be of a similar shape.
The intensity distributions for Frenkel pairs and ionization were found to be reasonably well
described by a generalized logistic distribution. Fitting the three parameters of this distribution to
MARLOWE probability density functions yielded smooth variations in the parameter values with
PKA energy. Interpolating and weighting the generalized logistic distributions for each ion energy
according to a neutron recoil distribution produced an analytic function which that in excellent
agreement with direct MARLOWE calculations of the probabilities of high-impact displacement
and ionization events.
With MARLOWE calibrated to yield stable Frenkel pair probabilities in agreement with MD
simulations, attention was turned to an example small feature size device. The probability of a
single neutron strike in any channel of a GAA NSFET producing defects in multiple channels was
calculated for a range of defect thresholds. These probabilities showed an exponential-like decay
as the defect threshold was increased. The probability of seeing critical damage in two silicon
regions from a single neutron strike varied as a function of the number of defects required to reach

 65

the critical threshold, but for low defect numbers, the probability was observed to be around once
per 10 neutron events for both 1 MeV and 14 MeV incident energies. For critical damage in three
regions from a single neutron event, the probability (for low thresholds) was shown to be around
once per 100 neutron events.

5.1. Further Work

Building upon the work presented here, a simple extension would be to convolve the neutron
interaction cross sections and derive the probabilities presented here for a given neutron fluence
and spectrum. Additionally, the feature size in an example device could be parameterized to
determine the threshold at which single-event or total dose displacement effects become more
important in terms of the probabilities of defect concentrations in multiple sensitive volumes.
Additionally, there are a number of threads left to pull in calculating bulk silicon intensity distri-
butions with BCA codes. The crystalline structure combined with the non-local inelastic energy
loss model was determined to be being most similar to the model used in the MD simulations. The
MARLOWE-calculated probability density functions for Frenkel pair production and ionization
likely overestimate the long tails of the distributions for two reasons. Firstly, all the lattice ions
were considered stationary in the MARLOWE calculations. In reality, these ions will undergo ther-
mal vibrations. Secondly, the bulk silicon simulated here was a single, infinite lattice. In reality the
silicon may exhibit polycrystalline features due to finite grain sizes in the bulk silicon. Both of these
factors limit the maximum range an ion could channel and therefore reduce the probabilities at the
low extreme Frenkel pair production rates and the high extreme inelastic energy loss fractions.
With regard to molecular dynamics, one of the main concerns for running displacement cascades
in LAMMPS was the computational cost. The solution used in this work was to calibrate the
MARLOWE parameters using the molecular dynamics results. However, there are higher-fidelity
solutions that don’t involve introducing potentially nonphysical calibration parameters. One such
solution would be to implement a BCA-MD hybrid methodology. Rather than calibrating the
MARLOWE simulation, ions that fall below a certain energy threshold terminate their trajectory
within MARLOWE and continue, either through a forward model in LAMMPS or through a
library of pre-determined molecular dynamics results. This methodology has been used in the
past with metallic materials for structural properties [32] but applying it to nSEE would be a novel
approach. Furthermore, if one could improve the energy coupling between BCA and MD, localized
temperature increases from ionizing energy loss could be investigated. Specifically, examining if
the ionizing energy loss from a high LET silicon ion impacts the molecular dynamics simulation
if the electron temperature component from it’s ion track is included. Usually the energy would be
decoupled in this workflow, assuming that the range of the ion would make the localized temperature
increase negligible for the defect production region, but this has yet to be shown computationally.
Going beyond material studies, the feature sizes of state-of-the-art devices are approaching di-
mensions in which they can fully be modeled in molecular dynamics. Traditionally, displacement
cascades in molecular dynamics often focus on one material. This simplifies the simulation since
the interatomic potential often only includes interactions for that material. However, if there are
multiple layers of different materials, addressing the interatomic potential becomes complicated.

 66

Assuming that you could develop a methodology for keeping the interfaces stable, only the repulsive
potential would be necessary to account for atoms leaving their original layer. This is because we
can assume that the number of atoms displaced from one layer to the next is much smaller than
the total number of atoms in that layer. However, if the layers are not uniform and you introduced
layers with large width gradients, then this approximation would break down, and you would need
to accurately implement the interatomic potential for all possible material interactions. With the
advent of machine-learning-derived interatomic potentials[33], this may be feasible in the near
future.
