 Probing Ultrafast Charge Transfer Dynamics in Heterostructured Nanomaterials
Major Goals:
The increasing demand for renewable and low‐cost energy has triggered a vast amount of
research aimed at developing, characterizing and optimizing materials that can efficiently
convert sun light into useful chemical energy in the form of electricity or chemical fuels. So‐
called quantum dot sensitized solar cell (QDSC) materials have gained much attention in recent
years based on the advantageous physicochemical properties of semiconducting quantum dots
(QDs) as sensitizers, as well as their promising potential to increase the conversion efficiency
beyond the Shockley‐Queisser limit of ∼33% by making use of hot‐electron transfer (HET)
routes and multiple exciton generation (MEG). By finely tuning the size of the QDs, the band
gap can be carefully aligned to the metal oxide (MO) conduction band such that charge transfer
from the photoexcited QD is thermodynamically favorable.
Despite extensive research on QDSC materials, several key questions remain unanswered and
the mechanisms of ultrafast charge transfer phenomena are heavily debated. This research
program aimed at resolving these pertaining questions by elucidating the time scales and
mechanistic origins of the crucial steps of photoinduced interfacial charge separation and adverse
carrier trapping and recombination routes in chemically linked and epitaxially grown PbX/MO
and CdX/MO (X = S, Se) heterostructured nanomaterials.
Our experimental approach was based on various forms of ultrafast X‐ray spectroscopy (XAS,
HERFD, and RIXS), in conjunction with X‐ray scattering and ultrafast laser spectroscopy,
implemented at synchrotron and X‐FEL facilities. Ultrafast X‐ray spectroscopy is ideal to fulfill
the above‐listed objectives since it is element‐specific such that charge density changes and
structural distortions in the donor and acceptor moieties can be followed selectively, and it
delivers not only the local geometric structure of the system under study (by means of EXAFS),
but also the underlying electronic structure changes (using XANES). Furthermore, its time
resolution (∼70 ps at synchrotrons, ∼100 fs at X‐FELs) is ideal to study charge transfer
processes in the weak‐ and strongly‐coupled electron transfer regimes, respectively. We
employed full multiple scattering calculations, in combination with time‐dependent DFT, to infer
the coupled electronic and structural dynamics upon photoexcitation from the time‐resolved X‐
ray spectra and scattering patterns. We complemented these X-ray techniques with ultrafast
UV/Visible spectroscopy, where we showed that by probing in both the UV and visible regions
we could distinguish between carrier dynamics in ZnO nanorods and CdSe quantum dots.

Accomplishments
In our first major publication from this grant (Nano Lett 21, 9534), we performed picosecond
transient x‐ray absorption linear dichroism spectroscopy of ZnO nanorods. This work, performed
at the Advanced Photon Source at Argonne National Laboratory, used Zn K‐edge XANES to
measure the electronic structure of these nanorods after 355 nm excitation. At low excitation
fluence, we observed distinct signals for valence band holes and conduction band electrons,
which in future experiments will allow us to track each carrier in multi‐layer photovoltaic
devices. At higher fluence a thermal signal dominates the isotropic XANES signal, but this
signal cancels out in the anisotropic (linear dichroism) XANES signal. Surprisingly, we saw no
evidence for hole polarons, but observed a large effect due to core‐hole screening by the
photogenerated electrons. We supported this interpretation with ab initio calculations with‐ and
without core‐hole screening. This work represents a powerful new technique for understanding
element‐specific semiconductor dynamics.
We followed up on this work in a detailed study of the the role of time-dependent Coulomb
screening on core excitons, driven by dynamic carrier populations (Commun Mater 6, 191). We
performed picosecond visible-pump, X-ray probe spectroscopy on ZnO films and interpreted the
time-resolved XANES spectra using a novel ab initio approach, integrating constrained density-
functional theory, real-time time-dependent density functional theory, and a non-equilibrium
formalism based on the Bethe-Salpeter equation. We showed that core-exciton resonances can
be accurately modeled using this theoretical framework, and that they depend on both the density
of photoexcited carriers and their distribution in phase space. The core-hole screening produces
a measurable blueshift of the transient signal, and provides a sensitive probe of the carrier
density.
In parallel with this work using hard X‐rays at synchrotron facilities, we used our tabletop
transient XUV spectrometer to measure carrier dynamics in organohalide perovskites. In
previous work (supported mainly by the PI’s startup funding), we showed that XUV
spectroscopy of CH3NH3PbI3 can separately probe electron and hole dynamics. Similar to the
hard X‐ray result on ZnO, by probing from an atomic core level (in this case the I 4d orbital) to
the conduction band and to valence band holes, we can measure the temperature of each carrier
as a function of time after photoexcitation. With support from this grant, we extended this study
to Br‐containing perovskites, which are important components in tandem perovskite solar cells
and potential LED emitters. For CH3NH3PbBr3, we observed rapid hole cooling consistent
Frohlich coupling between holes and phonons. The large band gap and small spin‐orbit splitting
allowed us to fit the entire transient spectrum to a combination of bandgap renormalization and
state‐filling, providing a quantitative measure of the band structure changes after excitation. In
two‐dimensional layered perovskites such as butylammonium lead iodide and
phenethylammonium lead iodide, we showed that the hole cooling rate is independent of the
organic cation at room temperature and high carrier density, contrary to previous reports that
were conducted at cryogenic temperatures. Both of these results will be the subject of upcoming
manuscripts.

Our third published work from this grant (Nanoscale 17, 17733) tackled the problem of
understanding interfacial charge separation in semiconductor heterostructures. Quantum dots are
efficient light absorbers and the cornerstone of many solar energy collection architectures, but
the efficiency of such devices is often limited by charge transfer from the QD to the collection
layer. The rate and yield of charge injection can be difficult to measure because trap states in
both the QD and the underlying semiconductor are optically dark.
We used broadband optical transient absorption spectroscopy, in the 330-600 nm wavelength
range, to measure charge injection in heterostructures composed of ZnO nanorods decorated with
CdSe quantum dots. Previous work had suggested the presence of The UV probe reveals a
transient feature at 360 nm that selectively reports on electrons in the ZnO conduction band,
whereas the Visible probe at 510 nm measures the presence of photoexcited electrons in CdSe.
By measuring both the timescale when electrons leave CdSe and when they appear in ZnO, we
show that the charge injection initially forms an optically dark charge transfer state (i.e. a surface
trap), with a delay of ~10 ps before the charge reaches the ZnO conduction band. Our results
also showed that there was a second population of quantum dots, likely adsorbed to a different
crystal face, that injected charge on a subpicosecond timescale. This work demonstrated that
element-specific probes are possible even with UV/Visible spectroscopy and that simple
exponential kinetics are insufficient for describing charge injection processes in heterogeneous
semiconductor interfaces.
