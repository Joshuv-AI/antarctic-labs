Equilibrium and Competition in Evolutionary Dynamics
J. Medina-Diaz, F. Peña-Garcia, Irbin Llanqui

Abstract
A fundamental problem in protobiological dynamics is to understand how chemically generated polymers can form persistent sequence distributions before the emergence of replication. We study deterministic polymer growth in which each finite sequence is followed along its genealogical structure. The system pictures an open polymerization cascade in which each polymer is produced from a unique precursor and lost by degradation and further extension. Setting fixed activated precursors, we show global well-posedness, positivity, uniqueness of a strictly positive equilibrium, and exponential convergence to an explicit steady state distribution. Under an additional uniform decay condition, this convergence becomes global exponential stability in a uniform norm. We then couple the polymerization to a shared environmental resource with logistic growth and depletion by two activated precursors. In the resulting binary polymerization competition model, the equilibrium structure is governed by a three-dimensional core subsystem. We prove that strictly positive equilibria exist exactly above a sharp resource threshold. At the threshold the equilibrium is unique, while above it two positive branches appear. The lower branch is unstable and the upper branch is locally stable. For the complete infinite system, we exhibit positivity, global componentwise existence, a priori bounds, and under persistence and dominance assumptions, global exponential stability. Finally, we introduce template directed replication through a replicator term. The pre-replicative equilibrium continues only under neutral fitness, and heterogeneous fitness removes it as an equilibrium of the replicated system.



1
Introduction

The beginning of life is a research area that has captivated the interest of both scientist and the general public even before the revolution of molecular biology
(
Schopf 2024
)
.
Many theories has been knitted around this enigma for decades
(
Kauffman 2011
)
and even some scientist
(
Agutter and Wheatley 2008
)
have argued that this topic does not fall under the scope of science.
What is undeniable is that in order to understand the transition from prebiotic chemistry to the evolutionary dynamics of life, it is necessary to apply a quantitative approach to the generation, maintenance, and differential amplification of the basic elements of life: polymers.
Among the diversity of polymers, linear polymers are the most simple and will be the object of study of the present paper.

We will follow an ecological approach here in which every polymer is an individual of a dynamical population. Hence, the persistence of each polymer relies on the equilibrium between production, decay, mutation, and differential replication. This approach has bases in the work of
Eigen 1971
and
Eigen and Schuster 1977
. In a pre-replicative regime, activated monomers can generate diverse polymers before template-directed copying is established. This leads to a nontrivial kinetic selection after which replication can then emerge as a qualitative transition rather than as the starting point of the dynamics
(
Nowak and Ohtsuki 2008
;
Manapat et al. 2009
)
.

Modern origin of life chemistry increasingly frames the problem in systems terms, stressing that the relevant reaction networks must be studied under driven conditions, with sustained fluxes, environmental cycles, and kinetic bottlenecks, rather than as isolated batch reactions
(
Sutherland 2017
;
Pascal et al. 2013
)
.
This view is supported by an expanding body of experimental work on prebiotic environments driven far from equilibrium. Thermal gradients in hydrothermal pore systems can concentrate nucleotides and thereby mitigate the dilution problem faced by prebiotic synthesis
(
Baaske et al. 2007
)
. More broadly, heated rock pores, drying-wetting and freeze thaw cycles, and other physical non-equilibria can promote concentration, strand separation, and sequence-dependent copying dynamics relevant to nucleic-acid chemistry
(
Ianeselli et al. 2023
)
. Likewise, continuous reaction networks and compatible one-pot activation/copying scenarios have shown how prebiotic chemistry can sustain integrated pathways toward RNA precursors and nonenzymatic RNA copying in geochemically plausible settings
(
Yi et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2022
)
. At the same time, small catalytic RNA ensembles can already exhibit cooperation, competition, and frequency-dependent reproduction, indicating that network-level ecological effects may precede full Darwinian evolution
(
Yeates et al. 2016
)
.

Motivated by these developments, in this work we study a deterministic, sequence-resolved model of open polymerization on the genealogy of finite
m
-ary strings. Each polymer is produced from a unique precursor, while losses arise from basal degradation and further extension to longer sequences. The resulting dynamics is an infinite system of ordinary differential equations indexed by a countable system. In contrast with standard replicator or quasispecies descriptions, the baseline model does not assume template-directed self-replication; instead, it isolates the kinetic backbone of a generative chemistry driven by a constant input of activated precursors. This allows us to ask a mathematically precise version of a protobiological question: when does an open polymerization network, by itself, settle into a robust sequence distribution, and how is that distribution altered once competition for resources and replication are added?

Our first objective is to establish the basic theory of this polymerization cascade, then couple the precursor layer to a shared environmental resource and analyze a binary competition model in which the activated roots and the competitor evolve jointly. This reduction reveals a low-dimensional “core system” that determines the admissible positive steady regimes of the full infinite polymer network. We characterize its equilibria, identify the threshold condition for the existence of positive steady states, and prove positivity invariance, global existence, and explicit
a priori
bounds. Finally, we add a template directed replication term of replicator type and show that the previous equilibrium survives only in the neutral fitness case.

The next section introduces the polymerization system and begins the analysis of this fundamental unbounded cascade.

Dynamics of the system of polymerization.

Remark 1

(On the notation for monomers and finite words)

For simplicity, we label the monomer types by

If

To prevent any ambiguity, one may equivalently write a polymer as the finite tuple

Under this convention, we can state the basic dynamical properties of the polymerization system (

Equivalently, if

This result says that an open prebiotic polymerization network fed by a continuous input of activated monomers gives rise to a
robust steady-state composition
. No matter how the initial mixture of short and long polymers is distributed, the concentration of each specific sequence
i
converges exponentially toward a unique steady value, which represents the long-time “availability” or “background abundance” of that sequence in a continuously driven environment (e.g., hydrothermal pores), where activation sustains the monomer supply and polymers are continually lost through degradation and further extension.

The parameter
d
plays the role of a ubiquitous environmental turnover mechanism, such as hydrolysis, dilution, UV damage, or mechanical breakup, affecting every polymer regardless of sequence. Because
k_{i}=d+\sum_{j}a_{ij}\geq d
, every equation retains at least
d
units of dissipation, which yields a uniform exponential convergence rate (
\delta=d/2
) across the whole sequence space. Thus even very long or rarely produced polymers cannot blow up in concentratio. Finally, uniqueness means the system has no alternative stable “macrostates” under fixed feeding so, in this linear, purely kinetic setting,
persistent changes in composition (a prerequisite for selection-like behavior) would require changing the environment/inputs (e.g., activation levels),
adding nonlinear effects (template-directed replication, catalysis), or introducing compartmentalization.

While the previous discussion establishes
componentwise
exponential convergence, in many applications it is useful to control the
entire
sequence distribution at once in a uniform norm. In particular, one may naturally ask whether this convergence admits a stronger form as an exponential contraction in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{S})
, meaning that the maximal deviation over
all
polymers decays at a single rate, independent of sequence length. The next theorem provides precisely this stronger global stability statement under a natural uniform
net-decay gap
condition,
\inf_{i\in\mathcal{S}}(k_{i}-a_{i})>0
, which ensures that every extension step is dominated by an intrinsic decay margin.

Theorem 2.2

(
\ell^{\infty}
-global exponential stability under a uniform net-decay gap)

Consider the polymerization system (

net-decay gap
:

\gamma\;:=\;\inf_{i\in\mathcal{S}}(k_{i}-a_{i})\;>\;0.

(11)

Then

1.

The equilibrium
\mathbf{x}^{*}=(x_{i}^{*})_{i\in\mathcal{S}}
defined by

x_{\lambda^{*}}^{*}:=1\ (\lambda=1,\dots,m),\qquad x_{i}^{*}:=\frac{a_{i}}{k_{i}}\,x_{i^{\prime}}^{*}\quad(i\in\mathcal{S})

satisfies

and in fact

for all

2.

For every bounded initial condition
\mathbf{x}(0)\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{S})

(with

for

4

for all

\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{S})

\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{x}^{*}+\mathbf{e}(t)\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{S})
for all
t\geq 0
.
∎

Theorem
2.2
shows that, for the linear polymerization cascade (
4
), a uniform net decay gap enforces a strong form of robustness, the recursively defined steady profile is bounded and attracts all bounded initial data exponentially fast in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{S})
. After characterizing this fundamental behavior, we now turn to a coupled setting in which the precursor layer is no longer prescribed, but evolves dynamically together with an environmental resource variable
C
representing the concentration of the shared competing substrate. This leads naturally to the analysis of a finite-dimensional
core system
, i.e., the subsystem for the precursor concentrations and the resource variable, here
(x_{1^{\prime}},x_{2^{\prime}},C)
. Its equilibria determine the admissible steady regimes to which the full polymer distribution can then be attached through the same lineage recursion.

3
Equilibrium of the “core system"

Now, we will analyze system (
14
) qualitatively in order to explore conditions for the stability of the extended polymerization model.

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=1-\beta_{1}Cx_{1^{\prime}},

(14)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=1-\beta_{2}Cx_{2^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=a_{i}x_{i^{{}^{\prime}}}-(d+a_{i1}+a_{i2})x_{i}\text{ for $i\in\mathcal{S}$},

\displaystyle\dot{C}

\displaystyle=C[r(1-\dfrac{C}{K})-\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}-\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}].

Here,
C=C(t)
denotes the concentration (or abundance) of the shared environmental component regulating the dynamics, and
K>0
is its carrying capacity in the absence of depletion by the subpopulations. The parameters
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}>0
are interaction coefficients.
\beta_{1}
measures the strength of the coupling between
C
and
x_{1^{\prime}}
, while
\beta_{2}
measures the analogous coupling between
C
and
x_{2^{\prime}}
. In particular, the terms
\beta_{1}Cx_{1^{\prime}}
and
\beta_{2}Cx_{2^{\prime}}
represent the
C
-mediated loss of
x_{1^{\prime}}
and
x_{2^{\prime}}
, respectively, whereas
\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}
and
\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}
represent the depletion of
C
induced by them.

3.1
Qualitative analysis and global stability of system (
14
)

Let
\mathcal{S}:=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\{1,2\}^{n}
be the set of all finite binary strings, with the convention that each sequence
i\in\mathcal{S}
has a unique precursor
i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{1^{\prime},2^{\prime}\}
and two followers
i1,i2\in\mathcal{S}
. At steady state (
\dot{x}_{1^{\prime}}=\dot{x}_{2^{\prime}}=\dot{x}_{i}=\dot{C}=0
), the first two equations of (
14
) give

x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}=\frac{1}{\beta_{1}C^{*}},\qquad x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}=\frac{1}{\beta_{2}C^{*}},

(15)

and for every
i\in\mathcal{S}
, the polymerization equations yield the recursive relation

x_{i}^{*}=\frac{a_{i}}{d+a_{i1}+a_{i2}}\,x_{i^{\prime}}^{*}.

(16)

Equation (
16
) shows that equilibrium concentrations propagate along the unique
predecessor chain in the binary polymerization system. Notice that the state space
\mathcal{S}

consists of finite words over the alphabet
\{1,2\}
(unactivated polymers), while the activated
precursors
1^{\prime},2^{\prime}
are
not
elements of
\mathcal{S}
but act as fixed inputs for the
polymerization recursion. More precisely, let
i=i_{1}\cdots i_{n}\in\mathcal{S}
with
n\geq 1
, and define again its prefixes

i^{(r)}:=i_{1}\cdots i_{r},\qquad r=1,\dots,n.

By construction of the predecessor map, we have

(i^{(r)})^{\prime}=i^{(r-1)}\quad\text{for }r\geq 2,\qquad\text{and}\qquad(i^{(1)})^{\prime}=i_{1}^{\prime}\in\{1^{\prime},2^{\prime}\}.

Thus the unique lineage feeding
i
is

i_{1}^{\prime}\;\to\;i^{(1)}\;\to\;i^{(2)}\;\to\;\cdots\;\to\;i^{(n)}=i.

Iterating (
16
) along this chain yields the explicit product formula

x_{i}^{*}=\left(\prod_{r=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i^{(r)}}}{d+a_{i^{(r)}1}+a_{i^{(r)}2}}\right)\,x_{i_{1}^{\prime}}^{*},

(17)

where the “boundary” equilibrium values of the activated precursors are given by (
15
). Hence, once
C^{*}>0
is known, all equilibrium concentrations are uniquely determined by (
15
) and the lineage recursion (
17
). In particular, positivity of parameters implies
x_{i}^{*}>0
for all
i
whenever
C^{*}>0
.

To determine
C^{*}
, set
\dot{C}=0
in (
14
):

0=C^{*}\Bigl[r\Bigl(1-\frac{C^{*}}{K}\Bigr)-\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}-\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}\Bigr].

A steady state with finite
x_{1^{\prime}}^{*},x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}
requires
C^{*}>0
, and substituting (
15
) yields

r\Bigl(1-\frac{C^{*}}{K}\Bigr)=\beta_{1}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta_{1}C^{*}}\Bigr)+\beta_{2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta_{2}C^{*}}\Bigr)=\frac{2}{C^{*}}.

Equivalently,

\frac{r}{K}C^{*2}-rC^{*}+2=0,

(18)

whose discriminant is
\Delta=r^{2}-\frac{8r}{K}=r\bigl(r-\frac{8}{K}\bigr)
.
Therefore, (
18
) admits positive real solutions if and only if

rK\geq 8.

(19)

If
rK=8
, there is a unique positive root,

C^{*}=\frac{K}{2}.

If
rK>8
, there are two distinct positive roots

C_{\pm}^{*}=\frac{K}{2}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{8}{rK}}\right),\qquad 0<C_{-}^{*}<\frac{K}{2}<C_{+}^{*}<K.

(20)

For each admissible choice of
C^{*}
(according to whether
rK=8
or
rK>8
),

x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}=\frac{1}{\beta_{1}C^{*}},\qquad x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}=\frac{1}{\beta_{2}C^{*}}.

Moreover, for any polymer
i=i_{1}\cdots i_{n}\in\mathcal{S}
(
n\geq 1
), we can write

x_{i}^{*}=\left(\prod_{r=1}^{n}\frac{a_{i^{(r)}}}{d+a_{i^{(r)}1}+a_{i^{(r)}2}}\right)\,\frac{1}{\beta_{i_{1}}\,C^{*}},

where we use the convention
\beta_{i_{1}}=\beta_{1}
if
i_{1}=1
and
\beta_{i_{1}}=\beta_{2}
if
i_{1}=2
. In short, a strictly positive steady state for the entire system exists if and only if
rK\geq 8
. It is unique when
rK=8
, while for
rK>8
there are two distinct positive steady states
corresponding to
C^{*}=C_{-}^{*}
and
C^{*}=C_{+}^{*}
, with all polymer concentrations determined recursively along lineages.

Having characterized all admissible steady states of (
14
), and the feasibility condition
rK\geq 8
for their existence, the next step is to verify that the dynamics is well-defined in the biologically meaningful region of the state space. Since the variables represent concentrations, solutions must remain nonnegative whenever they start so. This motivates establishing a forward-invariance property for the positive orthant. If initially
x_{1^{\prime}}(0),x_{2^{\prime}}(0),C(0)>0
and
x_{i}(0)>0
for all polymers, then no component can reach zero in finite time. The following lemma formalizes this positivity invariance.

Lemma 3.1

(Positivity invariance)
.

Assume
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},r,K,d>0
,
a_{i}>0
,
a_{ij}\geq 0
and
k_{i}:=d+a_{i1}+a_{i2}>0
.
Let
(x_{1^{\prime}},x_{2^{\prime}},(x_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{S}},C)
be any (local) solution of (
14
)
defined on
[0,T)
with strictly positive initial data

x_{1^{\prime}}(0),x_{2^{\prime}}(0),C(0)>0
and
x_{i}(0)>0
for all
i\in\mathcal{S}
. Then all components remain strictly positive on
[0,T)
. Moreover, the nonnegative orthant is forward invariant.

Proof.

Write

\dot{C}(t)=C(t)\,f(t),\qquad f(t):=r\Bigl(1-\frac{C(t)}{K}\Bigr)-\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}(t)-\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}(t).

Since
(x_{1^{\prime}},x_{2^{\prime}},C)
is a (local) solution of the reduced subsystem, the functions
x_{1^{\prime}},x_{2^{\prime}},C
are continuous on
[0,T)
,
hence so is
f
. Therefore the scalar linear ODE
\dot{C}=Cf
admits the representation

C(t)=C(0)\exp\Bigl(\int_{0}^{t}f(s)\,ds\Bigr),\qquad t\in[0,T),

(21)

which implies
C(t)>0
for all
t\in[0,T)
because
C(0)>0
and the exponential is strictly positive.

Since
C(t)>0
on
[0,T)
, each root variable solves an inhomogeneous linear ODE

\dot{x}_{1^{\prime}}(t)+\beta_{1}C(t)x_{1^{\prime}}(t)=1,\qquad\dot{x}_{2^{\prime}}(t)+\beta_{2}C(t)x_{2^{\prime}}(t)=1.

Define the integrating factors

\mu_{1}(t):=\exp\Bigl(\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{1}C(u)\,du\Bigr),\qquad\mu_{2}(t):=\exp\Bigl(\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{2}C(u)\,du\Bigr),

which satisfy
\mu_{1}(t),\mu_{2}(t)>0
for all
t\in[0,T)
. Multiplying the equation for
x_{1^{\prime}}
by
\mu_{1}

gives

\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\mu_{1}(t)x_{1^{\prime}}(t)\bigr)=\mu_{1}(t),

hence, integrating from
0
to
t
,

\mu_{1}(t)x_{1^{\prime}}(t)=x_{1^{\prime}}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{1}(s)\,ds.

Therefore

x_{1^{\prime}}(t)=\mu_{1}(t)^{-1}\left(x_{1^{\prime}}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{1}(s)\,ds\right)>0,\qquad t\in[0,T),

(22)

because
x_{1^{\prime}}(0)>0
,
\mu_{1}(s)>0
, and
\mu_{1}(t)^{-1}>0
. The same computation yields

x_{2^{\prime}}(t)=\mu_{2}(t)^{-1}\left(x_{2^{\prime}}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{2}(s)\,ds\right)>0,\qquad t\in[0,T).

(23)

Define the
depth

\mathrm{dep}(i)\in\mathbb{N}
for
i\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{1^{\prime},2^{\prime}\}
as the number of precursor steps
from
i
to its (unique) ancestral root in
\{1^{\prime},2^{\prime}\}
. Thus
\mathrm{dep}(1^{\prime})=\mathrm{dep}(2^{\prime})=0
, and if
i\in\mathcal{S}

then
\mathrm{dep}(i)=\mathrm{dep}(i^{\prime})+1
.
We prove by induction on
n=\mathrm{dep}(i)
that

x_{i}(t)>0\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,T)\ \text{ and all }i\text{ with }\mathrm{dep}(i)=n.

(24)

For
n=0
, the claim holds by (
22
)–(
23
). Assume (
24
) holds for all indices of depth
\leq n
.
Let
i
be any index with
\mathrm{dep}(i)=n+1
, so its precursor
i^{\prime}
has depth
n
and therefore

x_{i^{\prime}}(t)>0
for all
t\in[0,T)
by the induction hypothesis. Consider the ODE for
x_{i}
:

\dot{x}_{i}(t)+k_{i}x_{i}(t)=a_{i}x_{i^{\prime}}(t),\qquad k_{i}>0,\ a_{i}>0.

Since
x_{i^{\prime}}(\cdot)
is continuous on
[0,T)
, the function
t\mapsto a_{i}x_{i^{\prime}}(t)
is continuous.
Using the integrating factor
e^{k_{i}t}
, we obtain the formula of variation of constants

x_{i}(t)=e^{-k_{i}t}x_{i}(0)+a_{i}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-k_{i}(t-s)}x_{i^{\prime}}(s)\,ds,\qquad t\in[0,T).

(25)

Here
e^{-k_{i}t}>0
,
x_{i}(0)>0
,
a_{i}>0
, and
e^{-k_{i}(t-s)}x_{i^{\prime}}(s)\geq 0
for
0\leq s\leq t<T
because

x_{i^{\prime}}(s)>0
by the induction hypothesis. Hence the integral term in (
25
) is nonnegative, and

x_{i}(t)\geq e^{-k_{i}t}x_{i}(0)>0\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T).

This proves (
24
) for depth
n+1
. By induction,
x_{i}(t)>0
for all
i\in\mathcal{S}
and all

t\in[0,T)
.
∎

Lemma
3.1
shows that the positive cone is forward invariant, so the solution cannot leave the
biologically meaningful region by hitting zero in finite time. The next issue is whether a solution that stays
positive might nevertheless
blow up
in finite time by becoming unbounded. In system (
14
) the only
nonlinear feedback occurs through the logistic equation for the resource variable
C(t)
, which in turn modulates the decay of the activated roots
x_{1^{\prime}}(t),x_{2^{\prime}}(t)
and therefore feeds the entire
polymerization tree. Hence, establishing an
a priori
upper bound for
C(t)
is the key step toward ruling out finite time blow up and guaranteeing global existence of the full componentwise solution. This is the content
of the next lemma.

Lemma 3.2

(Global existence and an a priori upper bound for
C
)
.

Assume
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},r,K>0
,
a_{i}>0
, and
k_{i}>0
for all
i\in\mathcal{S}
. For every strictly positive initial condition

C(0)>0,\quad x_{1^{\prime}}(0)>0,\quad x_{2^{\prime}}(0)>0,\quad x_{i}(0)>0\ (i\in\mathcal{S}),

system (
14
) admits a unique global
componentwise
solution on
[0,\infty)
.
Moreover,

0<C(t)\leq\max\{C(0),K\}\qquad\forall t\geq 0.

Proof.

Consider the
3
–dimensional subsystem for
(C,x_{1^{\prime}},x_{2^{\prime}})
:

\dot{x}_{1^{\prime}}=1-\beta_{1}Cx_{1^{\prime}},\qquad\dot{x}_{2^{\prime}}=1-\beta_{2}Cx_{2^{\prime}},\qquad\dot{C}=C\Bigl[r\Bigl(1-\frac{C}{K}\Bigr)-\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}-\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}\Bigr].

Their right-hand side is locally Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, hence there exists a unique maximal solution

(C,x_{1^{\prime}},x_{2^{\prime}})\in C^{1}([0,T_{\max});\mathbb{R}^{3})

for some
T_{\max}\in(0,\infty]
. Set
M:=\max\{C(0),K\}
. We claim that
C(t)\leq M
for all
t\in[0,T_{\max})
. Since
x_{1^{\prime}}(t),x_{2^{\prime}}(t)\geq 0
for all
t
by Lemma
3.1
, we have

\dot{C}(t)=C(t)\Bigl[r\Bigl(1-\frac{C(t)}{K}\Bigr)-\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}(t)-\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}(t)\Bigr]\;\leq\;r\,C(t)\Bigl(1-\frac{C(t)}{K}\Bigr).

Let
y
be the solution of the logistic comparison equation

\dot{y}(t)=r\,y(t)\Bigl(1-\frac{y(t)}{K}\Bigr),\qquad y(0)=C(0).

Therefore, by the scalar comparison principle,

0<C(t)\leq y(t)\qquad\forall t\in[0,T_{\max}).

On the other hand, the logistic solution
y
with positive initial data remains positive and satisfies

y(t)\leq\max\{C(0),K\}\qquad\forall t\geq 0.

Consequently,

0<C(t)\leq\max\{C(0),K\}\qquad\forall t\in[0,T_{\max}).

Since
C(t)\geq 0
, we have

\dot{x}_{1^{\prime}}(t)=1-\beta_{1}C(t)x_{1^{\prime}}(t)\leq 1,\qquad\dot{x}_{2^{\prime}}(t)\leq 1.

Integrating on
[0,t]
gives, for all
t\in[0,T_{\max})
,

0<x_{1^{\prime}}(t)\leq x_{1^{\prime}}(0)+t,\qquad 0<x_{2^{\prime}}(t)\leq x_{2^{\prime}}(0)+t,

so
x_{1^{\prime}}
and
x_{2^{\prime}}
remain finite on every bounded time interval.

We have shown that
(C(t),x_{1^{\prime}}(t),x_{2^{\prime}}(t))
stays bounded on
[0,T]
for every
T<T_{\max}
.
By the standard continuation theorem for ODEs with locally Lipschitz right-hand side,
a maximal solution can fail to extend past
T_{\max}<\infty
only if its norm blows up as
t\uparrow T_{\max}
.
Since no blow-up occurs, we must have
T_{\max}=\infty
.
Thus the core solution exists uniquely on
[0,\infty)
.

Recall that the depth
\mathrm{dep}(i)\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of predecessor steps from
i
to its activated ancestral root in
\{1^{\prime},2^{\prime}\}
. Fix any
i\in\mathcal{S}
. Its ancestor chain to a root has finite length, hence once the reduced subsystem is known on
[0,\infty)
,
the precursor
x_{i^{\prime}}(\cdot)
is a known continuous function on
[0,\infty)
.
Then
x_{i}
solves the linear ODE

\dot{x}_{i}(t)+k_{i}x_{i}(t)=a_{i}x_{i^{\prime}}(t),\qquad t\geq 0,

and variation of constants yields the unique solution

x_{i}(t)=e^{-k_{i}t}x_{i}(0)+a_{i}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-k_{i}(t-s)}x_{i^{\prime}}(s)\,ds,\qquad t\geq 0,

which is
C^{1}
on
[0,\infty)
. Doing this for each
i
defines a componentwise solution

(C,x_{1^{\prime}},x_{2^{\prime}},(x_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{S}})
on
[0,\infty)
.

Let
(C,x_{1^{\prime}},x_{2^{\prime}},(x_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{S}})
and
(\widehat{C},\widehat{x}_{1^{\prime}},\widehat{x}_{2^{\prime}},(\widehat{x}_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{S}})
be two solutions of (
14
) on
[0,\infty)
with the same initial data:

C(0)=\widehat{C}(0),\quad x_{1^{\prime}}(0)=\widehat{x}_{1^{\prime}}(0),\quad x_{2^{\prime}}(0)=\widehat{x}_{2^{\prime}}(0),\quad x_{i}(0)=\widehat{x}_{i}(0)\ \ \forall i\in\mathcal{S}.

Based on what we said initially, we have

(C(t),x_{1^{\prime}}(t),x_{2^{\prime}}(t))=(\widehat{C}(t),\widehat{x}_{1^{\prime}}(t),\widehat{x}_{2^{\prime}}(t))\qquad\forall t\in[0,\infty).

We know that
\mathrm{dep}(1^{\prime})=\mathrm{dep}(2^{\prime})=0
, and for every
i\in\mathcal{S}
we have

\mathrm{dep}(i)=\mathrm{dep}(i^{\prime})+1.

We prove by induction on
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

x_{i}(t)=\widehat{x}_{i}(t)\quad\forall t\in[0,\infty)\quad\text{for all indices }i\text{ with }\mathrm{dep}(i)\leq n.

(26)

Base case
n=0
.

This is exactly above, i.e., the components with depth
0
are
1^{\prime},2^{\prime}
(and
C
, which already coincides),
so (
26
) holds for
n=0
.
Inductive step.
Assume (
26
) holds for some
n\geq 0
.
Let
i
be any index with
\mathrm{dep}(i)=n+1
, so its precursor
i^{\prime}
has depth
n
.
By the induction hypothesis,

x_{i^{\prime}}(t)=\widehat{x}_{i^{\prime}}(t)\qquad\forall t\in[0,\infty).

Now, for
i\in\mathcal{S}
, the component
x_{i}
satisfies the linear inhomogeneous ODE

\dot{x}_{i}(t)+k_{i}x_{i}(t)=a_{i}x_{i^{\prime}}(t),\qquad t\in[0,\infty),

and similarly

\dot{\widehat{x}}_{i}(t)+k_{i}\widehat{x}_{i}(t)=a_{i}\widehat{x}_{i^{\prime}}(t),\qquad t\in[0,\infty).

Since
x_{i^{\prime}}=\widehat{x}_{i^{\prime}}
on
[0,\infty)
, both
x_{i}
and
\widehat{x}_{i}
solve the
same

scalar linear ODE with the
same forcing term

t\mapsto a_{i}x_{i^{\prime}}(t)
and the same initial data

x_{i}(0)=\widehat{x}_{i}(0)
. Scalar linear ODEs have unique solutions (e.g. by integrating factor),
hence

x_{i}(t)=\widehat{x}_{i}(t)\qquad\forall t\in[0,\infty).

Since
i
was arbitrary among indices of depth
n+1
, this proves (
26
) for
n+1
. Also, since every index
i\in\mathcal{S}

has finite depth, it follows that
x_{i}(t)=\widehat{x}_{i}(t)
for all
i
and all
t\in[0,\infty)
. Hence the full family is unique.
∎

3.2
Stability of the positive equilibria of the core subsystem

We now analyze the local stability of the positive equilibria of the closed core subsystem

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=1-\beta_{1}Cx_{1^{\prime}},

(27)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=1-\beta_{2}Cx_{2^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\dot{C}

\displaystyle=C\left[r\left(1-\frac{C}{K}\right)-\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}-\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}\right].

At any positive equilibrium, the first two equations give

x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}=\frac{1}{\beta_{1}C^{*}},\qquad x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}=\frac{1}{\beta_{2}C^{*}}.

Substituting these expressions into the
C
-equation yields

r\left(1-\frac{C^{*}}{K}\right)=\frac{2}{C^{*}}.

(28)

Equivalently,

rC^{*}\left(1-\frac{C^{*}}{K}\right)-2=0.

(29)

Thus positive equilibria exist if and only if
rK\geq 8
. If
rK=8
, there is a unique positive equilibrium level

C^{*}=\frac{K}{2}.

If
rK>8
, there are two distinct positive equilibrium levels

C_{\pm}^{*}=\frac{K}{2}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{8}{rK}}\right),\qquad 0<C_{-}^{*}<\frac{K}{2}<C_{+}^{*}<K.

(30)

We now classify the local stability of these equilibria for the full core subsystem (
27
). Let

E_{c}^{*}=(x_{1^{\prime}}^{*},x_{2^{\prime}}^{*},C^{*})

be a positive core equilibrium. The Jacobian matrix of (
27
), written in the order

(x_{1^{\prime}},x_{2^{\prime}},C)
, is

J(C^{*})=\begin{pmatrix}-\beta_{1}C^{*}&0&-\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}\\
0&-\beta_{2}C^{*}&-\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}\\
-\beta_{1}C^{*}&-\beta_{2}C^{*}&r\left(1-\frac{C^{*}}{K}\right)-\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}-\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}-\frac{r}{K}C^{*}\end{pmatrix}.

Using the equilibrium identities

\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}=\frac{1}{C^{*}},\qquad\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}=\frac{1}{C^{*}},

and

r\left(1-\frac{C^{*}}{K}\right)-\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}-\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}=0,

this becomes

J(C^{*})=\begin{pmatrix}-\beta_{1}C^{*}&0&-\frac{1}{C^{*}}\\
0&-\beta_{2}C^{*}&-\frac{1}{C^{*}}\\
-\beta_{1}C^{*}&-\beta_{2}C^{*}&-\frac{r}{K}C^{*}\end{pmatrix}.

(31)

Set

p:=\beta_{1}C^{*},\qquad q:=\beta_{2}C^{*},\qquad\alpha:=\frac{r}{K}C^{*}.

Then
p,q,\alpha>0
, and the characteristic polynomial of
J(C^{*})
is

P(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-J(C^{*}))=\lambda^{3}+A_{1}\lambda^{2}+A_{2}\lambda+A_{3},

where

A_{1}=p+q+\alpha,

A_{2}=pq+(p+q)\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{C^{*}}\right),

and

A_{3}=pq\left(\alpha-\frac{2}{C^{*}}\right).

Using the equilibrium identity (
28
), we get

\frac{2}{C^{*}}=r\left(1-\frac{C^{*}}{K}\right),

and therefore

\alpha-\frac{2}{C^{*}}=\frac{r}{K}C^{*}-r\left(1-\frac{C^{*}}{K}\right)=\frac{r}{K}(2C^{*}-K).

Hence

A_{3}=pq\,\frac{r}{K}(2C^{*}-K).

(32)

If
C^{*}=C_{-}^{*}
, then
C_{-}^{*}<K/2
, and therefore
A_{3}<0
. Since
P(0)=A_{3}<0
while
P(\lambda)\to+\infty
as
\lambda\to+\infty
, the characteristic polynomial has at least one positive real root. Thus the lower equilibrium branch
C_{-}^{*}
is unstable for the core subsystem.

Now suppose
C^{*}=C_{+}^{*}
. Then
C_{+}^{*}>K/2
, so
A_{3}>0
. Moreover,

\alpha-\frac{1}{C^{*}}=\frac{r}{K}C^{*}-\frac{1}{C^{*}}.

Using again (
28
), we may write

\frac{1}{C^{*}}=\frac{r}{2}\left(1-\frac{C^{*}}{K}\right),

and therefore

\alpha-\frac{1}{C^{*}}=\frac{r}{K}C^{*}-\frac{r}{2}\left(1-\frac{C^{*}}{K}\right)=\frac{r}{2K}(3C^{*}-K).

Since
C_{+}^{*}>K/2
, we have
3C_{+}^{*}-K>0
. Hence

A_{2}>0.

Clearly
A_{1}>0
. It remains to verify the Routh–Hurwitz condition

A_{1}A_{2}>A_{3}.

Indeed,

A_{1}A_{2}-A_{3}=(p+q+\alpha)\left[pq+(p+q)\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{C^{*}}\right)\right]-pq\left(\alpha-\frac{2}{C^{*}}\right).

Expanding and simplifying gives

A_{1}A_{2}-A_{3}=(p+q)pq+\frac{2pq}{C^{*}}+(p+q)(p+q+\alpha)\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{C^{*}}\right).

For
C^{*}=C_{+}^{*}>K/2
, all three terms on the right-hand side are strictly positive. Therefore

A_{1}A_{2}>A_{3}.

By the Routh–Hurwitz criterion for cubic polynomials, all eigenvalues of
J(C_{+}^{*})
have strictly negative real parts. Hence the upper equilibrium branch
C_{+}^{*}
is locally asymptotically stable for the full core subsystem.

In the threshold case
rK=8
, the only positive equilibrium has
C^{*}=K/2
. In this case
A_{3}=0
, so the Jacobian has a zero eigenvalue. Hence the equilibrium is non-hyperbolic, and linearization alone does not determine its stability.

Consequently, for the closed three-dimensional core subsystem, the lower positive branch is unstable and the upper positive branch is locally asymptotically stable whenever
rK>8
. Since the core subsystem is closed inside the full polymerization–competition model, any Lyapunov-stable equilibrium of the full infinite system must project onto a Lyapunov-stable equilibrium of the core. Therefore, the full equilibrium associated with
C_{-}^{*}
cannot be Lyapunov stable, while the branch associated with
C_{+}^{*}
is the only positive branch that can serve as a locally stable candidate for the full system.

Lemma 3.3

(Core stability is necessary for full-system stability)
.

Let

E^{*}=\bigl(C^{*},x_{1^{\prime}}^{*},x_{2^{\prime}}^{*},(x_{i}^{*})_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\bigr)

be a strictly positive equilibrium of the full system (
14
). Consider the
\ell^{2}
-type norm (whenever finite)

\|(C,x)-(C^{*},x^{*})\|^{2}:=|C-C^{*}|^{2}+|x_{1^{\prime}}-x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}|^{2}+|x_{2^{\prime}}-x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}|^{2}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}|x_{i}-x_{i}^{*}|^{2},

where
x=\bigl(C,x_{1^{\prime}},x_{2^{\prime}},(x_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\bigr)
. If
E^{*}
is Lyapunov stable for the full system (
14
) with respect to this topology, then the core equilibrium
(C^{*},x_{1^{\prime}}^{*},x_{2^{\prime}}^{*})
is Lyapunov stable for the
closed core subsystem

\dot{x}_{1^{\prime}}=1-\beta_{1}Cx_{1^{\prime}},\qquad\dot{x}_{2^{\prime}}=1-\beta_{2}Cx_{2^{\prime}},\qquad\dot{C}=C\Bigl[r\Bigl(1-\frac{C}{K}\Bigr)-\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}-\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}\Bigr].

(33)

In particular, if
(C^{*},x_{1^{\prime}}^{*},x_{2^{\prime}}^{*})
is unstable for (
33
), then
E^{*}
cannot be
Lyapunov stable for the full system (
14
).

Proof.

Inspecting (
14
), the right-hand sides of the equations for
(C,x_{1^{\prime}},x_{2^{\prime}})
depend only on

(C,x_{1^{\prime}},x_{2^{\prime}})
and not on the remaining variables
(x_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{S}}
. Hence any solution of the full
system projects onto a solution of the closed core subsystem (
33
). Assume
E^{*}
is Lyapunov stable for the full system with respect to
\|\cdot\|
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exists
\delta>0
such that for every initial condition

(C(0),x_{1^{\prime}}(0),x_{2^{\prime}}(0),(x_{i}(0))_{i\in\mathcal{S}})\quad\text{with}\quad\|(C(0),x(0))-(C^{*},x^{*})\|<\delta,

the corresponding solution satisfies

\|(C(t),x(t))-(C^{*},x^{*})\|<\varepsilon\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

Moreover, since
E^{*}
is strictly positive, we restrict to strictly positive initial data. Then Lemma
3.2
guarantees that the resulting solution of (
14
) exists globally on

[0,\infty)
, so the above estimate is meaningful for every
t\geq 0

Now restrict to perturbations affecting only the core variables: choose initial data such that

x_{i}(0)=x_{i}^{*}\quad\text{for all }i\in\mathcal{S},

and
(C(0),x_{1^{\prime}}(0),x_{2^{\prime}}(0))
is
\delta
–close to
(C^{*},x_{1^{\prime}}^{*},x_{2^{\prime}}^{*})
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Then

\|(C(0),x(0))-(C^{*},x^{*})\|^{2}=|C(0)-C^{*}|^{2}+|x_{1^{\prime}}(0)-x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}|^{2}+|x_{2^{\prime}}(0)-x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}|^{2}.

By the full-system stability hypothesis, for all
t\geq 0
we have

|C(t)-C^{*}|^{2}+|x_{1^{\prime}}(t)-x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}|^{2}+|x_{2^{\prime}}(t)-x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}|^{2}\leq\|(C(t),x(t))-(C^{*},x^{*})\|^{2}<\varepsilon^{2}.

Therefore the projected core trajectory
(C(t),x_{1^{\prime}}(t),x_{2^{\prime}}(t))
remains
\varepsilon
–close to

(C^{*},x_{1^{\prime}}^{*},x_{2^{\prime}}^{*})
for all
t\geq 0
, and since it solves (
33
), the core equilibrium is
Lyapunov stable for (
33
).
∎

Remark 2

(Extension to the full system requires additional dissipativity)
.

The reduced classification does
not
by itself prove global asymptotic stability of the full infinite system (
14
). To obtain global asymptotic stability (and exponential decay in an
\ell^{2}
-framework),
one standard approach combines:

•

invariance/positivity and global existence of solutions,

•

a uniform lower bound
C(t)\geq\underline{C}>0
and an a priori upper bound
C(t)\leq\overline{C}
,

•

a coercive Lyapunov functional
V
controlling the
\ell^{2}
–distance to equilibrium,

•

dominance/Young inequalities ensuring
\dot{V}\leq-\beta V
for some
\beta>0
.

Motivated by Remark
2
, we now verify one of the structural ingredients needed for a global stability result. Beyond positivity and global existence, a key missing ingredient is a
uniform persistence
estimate for the competitor variable
C
. Namely, we need to prevent
C(t)
from approaching
0
, since such a degeneration would typically destroy coercivity and uniform dissipation in the
\ell^{2}
-estimates.

The next lemma provides precisely this type of forward-invariance statement. Under an explicit balance condition between the logistic growth term and suitable upper bounds for the root concentrations, any initial lower bound of the form
C(0)\geq\underline{C}
is propagated for as long as the core trajectory exists.

Lemma 3.4

(Forward invariance of a positive lower bound for the competitor)
.

Let
(C,x_{1^{\prime}},x_{2^{\prime}})
be a (local)
C^{1}
solution of the core subsystem (
33
)
on
[0,T)
, with parameters
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},r,K>0
and strictly positive initial data.
Fix
\underline{C}\in(0,K)
and assume

C(0)\geq\underline{C},\qquad r\Bigl(1-\frac{\underline{C}}{K}\Bigr)\ \geq\ \beta_{1}M_{1}+\beta_{2}M_{2},

(34)

where

M_{k}:=\max\Bigl\{x_{k^{\prime}}(0),\frac{1}{\beta_{k}\underline{C}}\Bigr\},\qquad k=1,2.

Then

C(t)\geq\underline{C}\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T).

Proof.

Consider the closed set

\Omega:=\bigl\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\ x\geq\underline{C},\ 0\leq y\leq M_{1},\ 0\leq z\leq M_{2}\bigr\}.

By construction we have
(C(0),x_{1^{\prime}}(0),x_{2^{\prime}}(0))\in\Omega
.
Assume for contradiction that the trajectory leaves
\Omega
.
Define the "first exit time"

A:=\{t\in(0,T):\,y(t)\notin\Omega\}\neq\varnothing,\qquad t_{*}:=\inf A.

Since
(C,x_{1^{\prime}},x_{2^{\prime}})
is a
C^{1}
solution on
[0,T)
, the trajectory

y(t):=(C(t),x_{1^{\prime}}(t),x_{2^{\prime}}(t))

is continuous on
[0,T)
. Observe that
y(0)\in\Omega
. Then
0<t_{*}<T
. Moreover, for every
t\in[0,t_{*})
we must have
y(t)\in\Omega
; otherwise, if

y(t_{0})\notin\Omega
for some
t_{0}<t_{*}
, then
t_{0}\in A
and hence

t_{*}=\inf A\leq t_{0}<t_{*}
, a contradiction. Thus
y(t)\in\Omega
for all
t<t_{*}
.

To locate
y(t_{*})
, let
t_{n}\uparrow t_{*}
with
t_{n}<t_{*}
. Then
y(t_{n})\in\Omega
for all
n
,
and by continuity
y(t_{n})\to y(t_{*})
. Since
\Omega
is closed, it follows that
y(t_{*})\in\Omega
. We observe that
y(t_{*})\notin\operatorname{int}(\Omega)
. Indeed, if
y(t_{*})\in\operatorname{int}(\Omega)
,
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
B_{\varepsilon}(y(t_{*}))\subset\Omega
. By continuity,
there exists
\delta>0
such that
|t-t_{*}|<\delta
implies
y(t)\in B_{\varepsilon}(y(t_{*}))\subset\Omega
,
so in particular
y(t)\in\Omega
for all
t\in(t_{*},t_{*}+\delta)
, contradicting the definition of

t_{*}=\inf A
. Therefore
y(t_{*})\in\partial\Omega
.

Since
y(t_{*})=(C(t_{*}),x_{1^{\prime}}(t_{*}),x_{2^{\prime}}(t_{*}))\in\partial\Omega
, at least one of the defining
inequalities of
\Omega
is saturated at
t_{*}
. Hence,

C(t_{*})=\underline{C},\quad\text{or}\quad x_{1^{\prime}}(t_{*})\in\{0,M_{1}\},\quad\text{or}\quad x_{2^{\prime}}(t_{*})\in\{0,M_{2}\}.

By Lemma
3.1
,
x_{1^{\prime}}(t),x_{2^{\prime}}(t)>0
for all
t\in[0,T)
. Therefore, at the first exit time we must have

C(t_{*})=\underline{C},\qquad\text{or}\qquad x_{1^{\prime}}(t_{*})=M_{1},\qquad\text{or}\qquad x_{2^{\prime}}(t_{*})=M_{2}.

Case 1:
x_{k^{\prime}}(t_{*})=M_{k}
for some
k\in\{1,2\}
.

Since
C(t)\geq\underline{C}
on
[0,t_{*}]
, at
t=t_{*}
we have

\dot{x}_{k^{\prime}}(t_{*})=1-\beta_{k}C(t_{*})x_{k^{\prime}}(t_{*})\leq 1-\beta_{k}\underline{C}\,M_{k}\leq 0,

because
M_{k}\geq 1/(\beta_{k}\underline{C})
by definition.
Thus
x_{k^{\prime}}
cannot cross
upwards
through the level
M_{k}
at
t_{*}
.

Case 2:
C(t_{*})=\underline{C}
.

Since the trajectory stays in
\Omega
up to
t_{*}
, we also have

x_{1^{\prime}}(t_{*})\leq M_{1}
and
x_{2^{\prime}}(t_{*})\leq M_{2}
. Using the
C
–equation,

\dot{C}(t_{*})=C(t_{*})\Bigl[r\Bigl(1-\frac{C(t_{*})}{K}\Bigr)-\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}(t_{*})-\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}(t_{*})\Bigr]\geq\underline{C}\Bigl[r\Bigl(1-\frac{\underline{C}}{K}\Bigr)-\beta_{1}M_{1}-\beta_{2}M_{2}\Bigr]\geq 0

by (
34
). Hence
C
cannot cross
downwards
through the level
\underline{C}
at
t_{*}
.

Once
y(t)
reaches the boundary
\partial\Omega
at
t_{*}
, in any case above,
y(t)
cannot cross it, contradicting that
t_{*}
is the first exit time from
\Omega
. Therefore the trajectory never leaves
\Omega
, and in particular

C(t)\geq\underline{C}
for all
t\in[0,T)
.
∎

Lemma
3.4
provides the key
forward-invariant positivity buffer
needed in the global Lyapunov analysis. Under (
34
) the competitor level cannot fall below a fixed threshold
\underline{C}
along the core dynamics. Together with the a priori upper bound
C(t)\leq\overline{C}=\max\{C(0),K\}
from the logistic comparison, this yields

\underline{C}\leq C(t)\leq\overline{C}\qquad\forall t\geq 0,

which prevent degeneracy in the dissipation estimates and allow all coupling terms involving
C(t)
to be controlled by constants depending only on
\underline{C}
and
\overline{C}
. With these bounds in hand, we are able to impose the dominance conditions (35)–(36) and construct an
\ell^{2}
–Lyapunov functional whose derivative is strictly negative, leading to global asymptotic stability with exponential convergence for the full infinite system, as stated next. Before the next Theorem, we define

\|\tilde{x}(t)\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}+|\tilde{C}(t)|^{2}:=\bigl(x_{1^{\prime}}(t)-x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}\bigr)^{2}+\bigl(x_{2^{\prime}}(t)-x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}\bigr)^{2}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\bigl(x_{i}(t)-x_{i}^{*}\bigr)^{2}+\bigl(C(t)-C^{*}\bigr)^{2}<\infty.

Theorem 3.1

(Exponential stability in
\ell^{2}
under persistence and dominance conditions)
.

Consider the infinite system (
14
) with two kinds of active monomers, and assume

\beta_{1},\beta_{2},r,K>0,\qquad a_{i}>0,\qquad k_{i}:=d+a_{i1}+a_{i2}>0\quad(i\in\mathcal{S}).

Assume
rK>8
and fix the equilibrium competitor level
C^{*}>0
as

C^{*}:=\frac{K}{2}\Bigl(1+\sqrt{1-\frac{8}{rK}}\Bigr),\qquad\frac{K}{2}<C^{*}<K.

Define the associated positive equilibrium coordinates by

x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}=\frac{1}{\beta_{1}C^{*}},\qquad x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}=\frac{1}{\beta_{2}C^{*}},\qquad x_{i}^{*}=\frac{a_{i}}{k_{i}}\,x_{i^{\prime}}^{*}\quad(i\in\mathcal{S}).

Define

a_{\max}:=\sup_{i\in\mathcal{S}}a_{i}<\infty,\qquad k_{\min}:=\inf_{i\in\mathcal{S}}k_{i}>\frac{3}{2}a_{\max}.

(35)

Let
(x_{1^{\prime}},x_{2^{\prime}},(x_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{S}},C)
be the unique global solution on
[0,\infty)

issued from strictly positive initial data (cf. Lemma
3.2
).
Assume moreover that
H0
of Lemma
3.4
holds for this trajectory. Assume the
equilibrium dominance
inequalities

2\beta_{1}\underline{C}>a_{\max},\qquad 2\beta_{2}\underline{C}>a_{\max},

(36)

and

\frac{\bigl[\beta_{1}(\overline{C}+x_{1^{\prime}}^{*})\bigr]^{2}}{2\beta_{1}\underline{C}-a_{\max}}+\frac{\bigl[\beta_{2}(\overline{C}+x_{2^{\prime}}^{*})\bigr]^{2}}{2\beta_{2}\underline{C}-a_{\max}}\;<\;\frac{2r}{K}\,\underline{C},

(37)

where

\overline{C}:=\max\{C(0),K\}.

Assume the initial deviation is square-summable:

\|\tilde{x}(0)\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}+|\tilde{C}(0)|^{2}=\bigl(x_{1^{\prime}}(0)-x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}\bigr)^{2}+\bigl(x_{2^{\prime}}(0)-x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}\bigr)^{2}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\bigl(x_{i}(0)-x_{i}^{*}\bigr)^{2}+\bigl(C(0)-C^{*}\bigr)^{2}<\infty.

Then the equilibrium
(C^{*},x_{1^{\prime}}^{*},x_{2^{\prime}}^{*},(x_{i}^{*})_{i\in\mathcal{S}})
is globally asymptotically stable in the sense that

\|\tilde{x}(t)\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}+|\tilde{C}(t)|^{2}\to 0\qquad(t\to\infty),

and in fact there exists
\beta>0
(depending only on the constants in (33)–(36)) such that

\|\tilde{x}(t)\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}+|\tilde{C}(t)|^{2}\;\leq\;\bigl(\|\tilde{x}(0)\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}+|\tilde{C}(0)|^{2}\bigr)e^{-\beta t},\qquad t\geq 0.

Proof.

Set

\tilde{C}:=C-C^{*},\qquad\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}:=x_{1^{\prime}}-x_{1^{\prime}}^{*},\qquad\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}:=x_{2^{\prime}}-x_{2^{\prime}}^{*},\qquad\tilde{x}_{i}:=x_{i}-x_{i}^{*}\ (i\in\mathcal{S}).

Using the equilibrium relations

1=\beta_{1}C^{*}x_{1^{\prime}}^{*},\qquad 1=\beta_{2}C^{*}x_{2^{\prime}}^{*},\qquad 0=a_{i}x_{i^{\prime}}^{*}-k_{i}x_{i}^{*}\ \ (i\in\mathcal{S}),

we obtain for
i\in\mathcal{S}
:

\dot{\tilde{x}}_{i}=a_{i}\tilde{x}_{i^{\prime}}-k_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}.

(38)

For the roots:

\dot{\tilde{x}}_{1^{\prime}}=\bigl(1-\beta_{1}Cx_{1^{\prime}}\bigr)-\bigl(1-\beta_{1}C^{*}x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}\bigr)=-\beta_{1}\bigl(Cx_{1^{\prime}}-C^{*}x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}\bigr)=-\beta_{1}\bigl(C\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}+x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}\tilde{C}\bigr),

(39)

and similarly

\dot{\tilde{x}}_{2^{\prime}}=-\beta_{2}\bigl(C\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}+x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}\tilde{C}\bigr).

(40)

For
C
:

\dot{\tilde{C}}=\dot{C}=C\Bigl[r\Bigl(1-\frac{C}{K}\Bigr)-\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}-\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}\Bigr]-C\underbrace{\Bigl[r\Bigl(1-\frac{C^{*}}{K}\Bigr)-\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}-\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}\Bigr]}_{=0},

hence

\dot{\tilde{C}}=C\Bigl[-\frac{r}{K}\tilde{C}-\beta_{1}\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}-\beta_{2}\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}\Bigr].

(41)

Let
\mathcal{T}_{N}
denote the finite set of nodes up to depth
N
(including
1^{\prime},2^{\prime}
).
Define

V_{N}(t):=\tilde{C}(t)^{2}+\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}(t)^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}(t)^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}\tilde{x}_{i}(t)^{2}.

Since
V_{N}
is a finite sum of
C^{1}
functions, it is
C^{1}
and

\dot{V}_{N}=2\tilde{C}\dot{\tilde{C}}+2\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}\dot{\tilde{x}}_{1^{\prime}}+2\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}\dot{\tilde{x}}_{2^{\prime}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}2\tilde{x}_{i}\dot{\tilde{x}}_{i}.

Using (40),

2\tilde{C}\dot{\tilde{C}}=2\tilde{C}\,C\Bigl[-\frac{r}{K}\tilde{C}-\beta_{1}\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}-\beta_{2}\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}\Bigr]=-2\frac{r}{K}C\,\tilde{C}^{2}-2\beta_{1}C\,\tilde{C}\,\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}-2\beta_{2}C\,\tilde{C}\,\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}.

(42)

Using (38)–(39),

2\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}\dot{\tilde{x}}_{1^{\prime}}=2\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}\bigl[-\beta_{1}(C\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}+x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}\tilde{C})\bigr]=-2\beta_{1}C\,\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}^{2}-2\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}\tilde{C}\,\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}},

(43)

2\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}\dot{\tilde{x}}_{2^{\prime}}=-2\beta_{2}C\,\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}^{2}-2\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}\tilde{C}\,\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}.

(44)

Finally, using (37),

2\tilde{x}_{i}\dot{\tilde{x}}_{i}=2\tilde{x}_{i}(a_{i}\tilde{x}_{i^{\prime}}-k_{i}\tilde{x}_{i})=-2k_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}^{2}+2a_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}\tilde{x}_{i^{\prime}}.

(45)

Summing (41)–(44) gives

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{N}

\displaystyle=-2\frac{r}{K}C\,\tilde{C}^{2}-2\beta_{1}C\,\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}^{2}-2\beta_{2}C\,\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}^{2}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}2k_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}2a_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}\tilde{x}_{i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\quad-2\beta_{1}(C+x_{1^{\prime}}^{*})\tilde{C}\,\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}-2\beta_{2}(C+x_{2^{\prime}}^{*})\tilde{C}\,\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}.

(46)

For each
i\in\mathcal{S}
,
2a_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}\tilde{x}_{i^{\prime}}\leq a_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}^{2}+a_{i}\tilde{x}_{i^{\prime}}^{2}\leq a_{\max}\tilde{x}_{i}^{2}+a_{i}\tilde{x}_{i^{\prime}}^{2}.
Summing over
i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\setminus\{1^{\prime},2^{\prime}\}
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}2a_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}\tilde{x}_{i^{\prime}}\leq a_{\max}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}\tilde{x}_{i}^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}a_{i}\tilde{x}_{i^{\prime}}^{2}.

Because the tree is binary, each node
j
has at most two children, hence

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}a_{i}\tilde{x}_{i^{\prime}}^{2}\leq 2a_{\max}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}\tilde{x}_{j}^{2}+a_{\max}\bigl(\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}^{2}\bigr).

Therefore,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}2a_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}\tilde{x}_{i^{\prime}}\leq 3a_{\max}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
j\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}\tilde{x}_{j}^{2}+a_{\max}\bigl(\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}^{2}\bigr).

(47)

Combining (46) with
k_{i}\geq k_{\min}
yields

-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}2k_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}2a_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}\tilde{x}_{i^{\prime}}\leq-\bigl(2k_{\min}-3a_{\max}\bigr)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}\tilde{x}_{i}^{2}+a_{\max}\bigl(\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}^{2}\bigr).

(48)

Set

\eta:=2k_{\min}-3a_{\max}.

Then by (34),
\eta>0
. Insert (47) into (45). Using (33) and by Lemma 1.4,
C(t)\geq\underline{C}
and
C(t)\leq\overline{C}
for all
t
. Thus for
k=1,2
,

-2\beta_{k}C\,\tilde{x}_{k^{\prime}}^{2}+a_{\max}\tilde{x}_{k^{\prime}}^{2}\leq-(2\beta_{k}\underline{C}-a_{\max})\,\tilde{x}_{k^{\prime}}^{2}.

(49)

Fix a parameter
\theta\in(0,1)
.
For
k=1,2
,

2\beta_{k}|C+x_{k^{\prime}}^{*}|\,|\tilde{C}|\,|\tilde{x}_{k^{\prime}}|\leq 2\beta_{k}(\overline{C}+x_{k^{\prime}}^{*})\,|\tilde{C}|\,|\tilde{x}_{k^{\prime}}|.

Apply Young’s inequality in the form

2ab\leq\theta\,\xi\,b^{2}+\frac{a^{2}}{\theta\,\xi}\qquad(\theta\in(0,1),\ \xi>0),

with

a:=\beta_{k}(\overline{C}+x_{k^{\prime}}^{*})|\tilde{C}|,\qquad b:=|\tilde{x}_{k^{\prime}}|,\qquad\xi_{k}:=2\beta_{k}\underline{C}-a_{\max}>0\quad\text{(by (35)).}

This gives

2\beta_{k}(\overline{C}+x_{k^{\prime}}^{*})\,|\tilde{C}|\,|\tilde{x}_{k^{\prime}}|\leq\theta(2\beta_{k}\underline{C}-a_{\max})\,\tilde{x}_{k^{\prime}}^{2}+\frac{\bigl[\beta_{k}(\overline{C}+x_{k^{\prime}}^{*})\bigr]^{2}}{\theta(2\beta_{k}\underline{C}-a_{\max})}\tilde{C}^{2}.

(50)

Combining (48) with (49) yields, for each
k=1,2
,

-(2\beta_{k}\underline{C}-a_{\max})\,\tilde{x}_{k^{\prime}}^{2}+2\beta_{k}(\overline{C}+x_{k^{\prime}}^{*})\,|\tilde{C}|\,|\tilde{x}_{k^{\prime}}|\leq-(1-\theta)(2\beta_{k}\underline{C}-a_{\max})\,\tilde{x}_{k^{\prime}}^{2}+\frac{\bigl[\beta_{k}(\overline{C}+x_{k^{\prime}}^{*})\bigr]^{2}}{\theta(2\beta_{k}\underline{C}-a_{\max})}\tilde{C}^{2}.

(51)

Using (45), (48), and (50), and also
C\geq\underline{C}
in the term

-2\frac{r}{K}C\,\tilde{C}^{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{N}(t)

\displaystyle\leq-2\frac{r}{K}\underline{C}\,\tilde{C}(t)^{2}-\eta\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}\tilde{x}_{i}(t)^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{2}(1-\theta)(2\beta_{k}\underline{C}-a_{\max})\,\tilde{x}_{k^{\prime}}(t)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{2}\frac{\bigl[\beta_{k}(\overline{C}+x_{k^{\prime}}^{*})\bigr]^{2}}{\theta(2\beta_{k}\underline{C}-a_{\max})}\,\tilde{C}(t)^{2}.

Grouping the
\tilde{C}^{2}
terms gives

\dot{V}_{N}(t)\leq-\gamma_{C}(\theta)\,\tilde{C}(t)^{2}-\eta\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}\tilde{x}_{i}(t)^{2}-\lambda_{1}(\theta)\,\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}(t)^{2}-\lambda_{2}(\theta)\,\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}(t)^{2},

(52)

where

\lambda_{k}(\theta):=(1-\theta)(2\beta_{k}\underline{C}-a_{\max})>0\qquad(k=1,2),

and

\gamma_{C}(\theta):=\frac{2r}{K}\underline{C}-\frac{\bigl[\beta_{1}(\overline{C}+x_{1^{\prime}}^{*})\bigr]^{2}}{\theta(2\beta_{1}\underline{C}-a_{\max})}-\frac{\bigl[\beta_{2}(\overline{C}+x_{2^{\prime}}^{*})\bigr]^{2}}{\theta(2\beta_{2}\underline{C}-a_{\max})}.

Set

S:=\frac{\bigl[\beta_{1}(\overline{C}+x_{1^{\prime}}^{*})\bigr]^{2}}{2\beta_{1}\underline{C}-a_{\max}}+\frac{\bigl[\beta_{2}(\overline{C}+x_{2^{\prime}}^{*})\bigr]^{2}}{2\beta_{2}\underline{C}-a_{\max}}.

Assumption (36) reads
S<\frac{2r}{K}\underline{C}
. Fix, once for all, as

\theta\in\left(\frac{S}{\frac{2r}{K}\underline{C}},\,1\right).

Then
\gamma_{C}(\theta)=\frac{2r}{K}\underline{C}-\frac{S}{\theta}>0
. Then all coefficients in (51) are strictly positive. Setting

\beta:=\min\{\gamma_{C}(\theta),\eta,\lambda_{1}(\theta),\lambda_{2}(\theta)\}>0,

and using

V_{N}(t)=\tilde{C}(t)^{2}+\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}(t)^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}(t)^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}\tilde{x}_{i}(t)^{2},

we conclude from (51) that

\dot{V}_{N}(t)\leq-\beta\,V_{N}(t)\qquad\forall t\in[0,\infty).

(53)

By Grönwall’s lemma applied to (52),

V_{N}(t)\leq V_{N}(0)\,e^{-\beta t}\qquad\forall t\geq 0,\ \forall N.

Fix
t\geq 0
. Recall that
\mathcal{T}_{N}
is the set of nodes up to depth
N
(including
1^{\prime},2^{\prime}
), hence

\mathcal{T}_{N}\subset\mathcal{T}_{N+1},\qquad\bigcup_{N\geq 0}\mathcal{T}_{N}=\mathcal{S}\cup\{1^{\prime},2^{\prime}\},

and every sum in
V_{N}(t)
is finite. Define

S_{N}(t):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{T}_{N}\\
i\in\mathcal{S}\end{subarray}}\tilde{x}_{i}(t)^{2},\qquad S(t):=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\tilde{x}_{i}(t)^{2}\in[0,\infty].

Since all terms
\tilde{x}_{i}(t)^{2}\geq 0
, the sequence
(S_{N}(t))_{N\geq 0}
is monotone nondecreasing:

S_{N}(t)\leq S_{N+1}(t)\qquad\text{for all }N,

and by definition of
S(t)
as the (possibly divergent) series over all indices,

S_{N}(t)\uparrow S(t)\quad\text{as }N\to\infty.

Therefore, for each fixed
t\geq 0
,

V_{N}(t)=\tilde{C}(t)^{2}+\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}(t)^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}(t)^{2}+S_{N}(t)\uparrow\tilde{C}(t)^{2}+\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}(t)^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}(t)^{2}+S(t)=:V(t).

The same argument at
t=0
gives
V_{N}(0)\uparrow V(0)
. As part of the hypotheses, the initial deviation belongs to
\ell^{2}
, i.e.

V(0)=\tilde{C}(0)^{2}+\tilde{x}_{1^{\prime}}(0)^{2}+\tilde{x}_{2^{\prime}}(0)^{2}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\tilde{x}_{i}(0)^{2}<\infty.

Then in particular
V_{N}(0)\leq V(0)<\infty
for every
N
. Now fix
t\geq 0
and use the estimate obtained for each finite truncation:

V_{N}(t)\leq V_{N}(0)e^{-\beta t}\qquad\forall N.

Since
V_{N}(0)\leq V(0)
, we have the uniform bound

V_{N}(t)\leq V(0)e^{-\beta t}\qquad\forall N.

Taking
N\to\infty
and using the monotone convergence
V_{N}(t)\uparrow V(t)
yields

V(t)=\lim_{N\to\infty}V_{N}(t)\leq\lim_{N\to\infty}V_{N}(0)e^{-\beta t}=\bigl(\lim_{N\to\infty}V_{N}(0)\bigr)e^{-\beta t}=V(0)e^{-\beta t}.

Hence

V(t)\leq V(0)e^{-\beta t}\qquad\forall t\geq 0.

In particular
V(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, hence every component converges to its equilibrium value
with exponential rate
\beta
(in the
\ell^{2}
sense).
∎

Theorem
3.1
states that, for the pure polymerization–competition model (
14
), the positive equilibrium associated with the upper stable resource branch
C^{*}=C_{+}^{*}
is globally exponentially stable in the stated
\ell^{2}
-framework, provided the persistence, dominance, and square-summability hypotheses of the theorem hold. We now extend the model by incorporating
template-directed replication and selection
among the non-activated sequences. In particular, we keep the same core subsystem for the activated monomers and the competitor, while adding to each downstream variable
x_{i}
a standard replicator term that accounts for differential fitness. This leads to the following binary polymerization system with competition and replication.

4
A binary system of polymerization with competition and replication

Taking in replication and competition into dynamics of the system of polymerization yields the following differential equation:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=1-\beta_{1}Cx_{1^{\prime}},

(54)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=1-\beta_{2}Cx_{2^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\dot{C}

\displaystyle=C[r(1-\dfrac{C}{K})-\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}-\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}],

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=a_{i}x_{i^{{}^{\prime}}}-(d+a_{i1}+a_{i2})x_{i}+\mathcal{R}x_{i}(f_{i}-\phi)\text{ for $i\in\mathcal{S}$}.

The first three lines of this system describe the same as in Eq. (
14
). The first part of
\dot{x}_{i}
in (
54
) pictures the dynamics of non-activated monomers
i
as in (
14
), while the second part represents the standard selection equation. The parameter,
\mathcal{R},
measures the relative rate of template-directed replication.
f_{i}
denotes the fitness of sequence
i
. The amount
\phi
is an additional decay rate, which represents the average fitness of the population, i.e.,

\phi=\dfrac{\Sigma_{i}f_{i}x_{i}}{\Sigma_{i}x_{i}}\text{ for }i\in\mathcal{S}.

We denote the net reproductive rate of sequence
i
(different from the activated monomers) by

g_{i}:=\mathcal{R}(f_{i}-\phi)-(d+a_{i1}+a_{i2}).

(55)

Moreover, we consider
x=(x_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\in\ell^{1},
and
0<f_{i}<1.
Here is the formal proposition summarizing the nonnegativity and boundedness results for the system with replication dynamics:

Proposition 1

(Persistence and destruction of the pre-replicative equilibrium under replication)
.

Consider the polymerization–competition system without replication (
14
), and let

E^{*}=(C^{*},x_{1^{\prime}}^{*},x_{2^{\prime}}^{*},(x_{i}^{*})_{i\in\mathcal{S}})

be a strictly positive equilibrium of that system. Assume that the polymer equilibrium profile is summable and that the fitness landscape is bounded, namely

(x_{i}^{*})_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\in\ell^{1}_{+}(\mathcal{S}),\qquad(f_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{S}).

Then the equilibrium fitness average

\phi^{*}:=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}f_{i}x_{i}^{*}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}x_{i}^{*}}

(56)

is well-defined.

Consider now the replicated system (
54
), with replication intensity
\mathcal{R}\geq 0
, the same coefficients

\beta_{1},\beta_{2},r,K,\qquad a_{i},\qquad k_{i}:=d+a_{i1}+a_{i2},

and the same fitness landscape
(f_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{S}}
. Then the following alternatives hold.

(i)

Neutral preservation.

If
\mathcal{R}=0
, then (
54
) coincides with (
14
). Hence
E^{*}
is an equilibrium of (
54
), with exactly the same stability properties as in the non-replicated system.

If
\mathcal{R}>0
and
f_{i}\equiv f_{\star}
is constant on
\mathcal{S}
, then, for every admissible state with
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}x_{i}>0
, one has

\phi=f_{\star}.

Consequently, the replication term vanishes identically:

\mathcal{R}x_{i}(f_{i}-\phi)=0\qquad(i\in\mathcal{S}).

Thus the replicated system (
54
) again coincides with the non-replicated system (
14
). In particular,
E^{*}
remains an equilibrium and all its stability properties are preserved.

(ii)

Destruction under heterogeneous fitness.

Assume
\mathcal{R}>0
. Then
E^{*}
is an equilibrium of the replicated system (
54
) if and only if

f_{i}=\phi^{*}\qquad\text{for every }i\in\mathcal{S}.

Since
x_{i}^{*}>0
for all
i
, this is equivalent to saying that the fitness landscape is constant on
\mathcal{S}
. Therefore, if the fitness landscape is heterogeneous, i.e. if there exist
i,j\in\mathcal{S}
with
f_{i}\neq f_{j}
, then
E^{*}
is not an equilibrium of (
54
). In particular,
E^{*}
cannot be a Lyapunov stable equilibrium of the replicated system.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{R}=0
, then (
54
) reduces exactly to (
14
), so
E^{*}
is an equilibrium of (
54
) and all stability properties are identical.

Assume next that
\mathcal{R}>0
and
f_{i}\equiv f_{\star}
. Then, for every admissible state with
\sum_{i}x_{i}>0
,

\phi=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}f_{\star}x_{i}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}x_{i}}=f_{\star}.

Therefore
f_{i}-\phi=0
for every
i\in\mathcal{S}
, and the replication term vanishes identically. Hence (
54
) coincides with (
14
), proving the preservation claim.

Now assume
\mathcal{R}>0
. Since
E^{*}
is an equilibrium of the non-replicated system, we have

a_{i}x_{i^{\prime}}^{*}-k_{i}x_{i}^{*}=0\qquad(i\in\mathcal{S}).

If
E^{*}
is also an equilibrium of the replicated system (
54
), then for every
i\in\mathcal{S}
,

0=a_{i}x_{i^{\prime}}^{*}-k_{i}x_{i}^{*}+\mathcal{R}x_{i}^{*}(f_{i}-\phi^{*})=\mathcal{R}x_{i}^{*}(f_{i}-\phi^{*}).

Because
\mathcal{R}>0
and
x_{i}^{*}>0
, it follows that

f_{i}=\phi^{*}\qquad\text{for every }i\in\mathcal{S}.

Thus the fitness landscape must be constant. Conversely, if
f_{i}=\phi^{*}
for every
i
, then the replication term vanishes at
E^{*}
, and
E^{*}
satisfies the equilibrium equations of (
54
). This proves the necessary and sufficient condition.

Hence, if the fitness landscape is heterogeneous,
E^{*}
cannot be an equilibrium of (
54
). Since Lyapunov stability as an equilibrium presupposes that the point is an equilibrium,
E^{*}
cannot be a Lyapunov stable equilibrium of the replicated system.
∎

Lemma 4.1

(Pairwise log-ratio identity)
.

Consider the replicated system (
54
). Let
i,j\in\mathcal{S}
, and suppose that along a positive trajectory one has

x_{i}(t)>0,\qquad x_{j}(t)>0\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

Assume also that the average fitness
\phi(t)
is well-defined along the trajectory. Then

\frac{d}{dt}\log\frac{x_{i}(t)}{x_{j}(t)}=\left(a_{i}\frac{x_{i^{\prime}}(t)}{x_{i}(t)}-a_{j}\frac{x_{j^{\prime}}(t)}{x_{j}(t)}\right)-(k_{i}-k_{j})+\mathcal{R}(f_{i}-f_{j}).

(57)

In particular, the average fitness term
\phi(t)
cancels from all pairwise log-ratio dynamics.

Proof.

For each
q\in\mathcal{S}
, the replicated equation is

\dot{x}_{q}=a_{q}x_{q^{\prime}}-k_{q}x_{q}+\mathcal{R}x_{q}(f_{q}-\phi).

For
x_{q}(t)>0
, division by
x_{q}(t)
gives

\frac{\dot{x}_{q}}{x_{q}}=a_{q}\frac{x_{q^{\prime}}}{x_{q}}-k_{q}+\mathcal{R}(f_{q}-\phi).

Applying this identity to
q=i
and
q=j
, and subtracting, we get

\frac{d}{dt}\log\frac{x_{i}}{x_{j}}=\frac{\dot{x}_{i}}{x_{i}}-\frac{\dot{x}_{j}}{x_{j}}=\left(a_{i}\frac{x_{i^{\prime}}}{x_{i}}-a_{j}\frac{x_{j^{\prime}}}{x_{j}}\right)-(k_{i}-k_{j})+\mathcal{R}(f_{i}-f_{j}),

because the two occurrences of
\phi
cancel.
∎

Proposition 2

(Pairwise exponential divergence under replication dominance)
.

Consider the replicated system (
54
). Fix two indices
i,j\in\mathcal{S}
, and suppose that along a positive trajectory

x_{i}(t)>0,\qquad x_{j}(t)>0\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

Assume that the average fitness
\phi(t)
is well-defined along the trajectory. Define the fitness gap

\Delta_{ij}:=f_{i}-f_{j},

and assume

\Delta_{ij}>0.

Suppose moreover that the kinetic forcing into the less fit sequence
j
is uniformly bounded along the trajectory, namely that there exists
M_{j}<\infty
such that

0\leq a_{j}\frac{x_{j^{\prime}}(t)}{x_{j}(t)}\leq M_{j}\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

(58)

Set

\kappa_{ij}^{+}:=(k_{i}-k_{j})_{+}:=\max\{k_{i}-k_{j},0\}.

If

\mathcal{R}\Delta_{ij}>M_{j}+\kappa_{ij}^{+},

(59)

then there exists

\varepsilon:=\mathcal{R}\Delta_{ij}-M_{j}-\kappa_{ij}^{+}>0

such that

\frac{d}{dt}\log\frac{x_{i}(t)}{x_{j}(t)}\geq\varepsilon\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

Consequently,

\frac{x_{i}(t)}{x_{j}(t)}\geq\frac{x_{i}(0)}{x_{j}(0)}e^{\varepsilon t}\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0,

(60)

and hence

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{x_{i}(t)}{x_{j}(t)}=+\infty.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.1
,

\frac{d}{dt}\log\frac{x_{i}}{x_{j}}=\left(a_{i}\frac{x_{i^{\prime}}}{x_{i}}-a_{j}\frac{x_{j^{\prime}}}{x_{j}}\right)-(k_{i}-k_{j})+\mathcal{R}\Delta_{ij}.

Since
a_{i}>0
,
x_{i^{\prime}}(t)\geq 0
, and
x_{i}(t)>0
, we have

a_{i}\frac{x_{i^{\prime}}(t)}{x_{i}(t)}\geq 0.

Moreover, by (
58
),

-a_{j}\frac{x_{j^{\prime}}(t)}{x_{j}(t)}\geq-M_{j}.

Finally,

-(k_{i}-k_{j})\geq-(k_{i}-k_{j})_{+}=-\kappa_{ij}^{+}.

Combining these estimates gives

\frac{d}{dt}\log\frac{x_{i}}{x_{j}}\geq-M_{j}-\kappa_{ij}^{+}+\mathcal{R}\Delta_{ij}=\varepsilon>0.

Integrating over
[0,t]
, we get

\log\frac{x_{i}(t)}{x_{j}(t)}-\log\frac{x_{i}(0)}{x_{j}(0)}\geq\varepsilon t.

Exponentiating yields (
60
), and therefore

\frac{x_{i}(t)}{x_{j}(t)}\to+\infty\qquad\text{as }t\to\infty.

∎

Remark 3
.

A simpler but slightly more conservative sufficient condition is

\mathcal{R}\Delta_{ij}>M_{j}+|k_{i}-k_{j}|.

This follows from the estimate

-(k_{i}-k_{j})\geq-|k_{i}-k_{j}|.

The sharper condition (
59
) uses only the positive part
(k_{i}-k_{j})_{+}
, because a larger decay rate for
j
relative to
i
already helps the divergence of
x_{i}/x_{j}
.

Proposition 3

(Uniform divergence along two full lineages)
.

Consider the replicated system (
54
), and assume that the solution is strictly positive, i.e.

x_{i}(t)>0\qquad\text{for all }i\in\mathcal{S},\ t\geq 0.

Assume also that the average fitness
\phi(t)
is well-defined along the trajectory.

Let

\mathcal{L}=\{\ell_{n}\}_{n\geq 0},\qquad\mathcal{M}=\{m_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}

be two full lineages, meaning that

\ell_{0},m_{0}\in\{1^{\prime},2^{\prime}\},\qquad\ell_{n}^{\prime}=\ell_{n-1},\qquad m_{n}^{\prime}=m_{n-1}\qquad(n\geq 1).

Assume that the kinetic forcing into the second lineage is uniformly bounded: there exists
M_{\mathcal{M}}<\infty
such that, for all
n\geq 1
and all
t\geq 0
,

0\leq a_{m_{n}}\frac{x_{m_{n-1}}(t)}{x_{m_{n}}(t)}\leq M_{\mathcal{M}}.

(61)

Assume also that the relative decay disadvantage of the first lineage is uniformly bounded:

\kappa_{+}:=\sup_{n\geq 1}(k_{\ell_{n}}-k_{m_{n}})_{+}<\infty,\qquad k_{q}:=d+a_{q1}+a_{q2}.

(62)

Finally, assume that the first lineage has a uniform fitness advantage over the second:

\Delta:=\inf_{n\geq 1}\bigl(f_{\ell_{n}}-f_{m_{n}}\bigr)>0.

(63)

If the replication intensity satisfies

\mathcal{R}\Delta>M_{\mathcal{M}}+\kappa_{+},

(64)

then there exists

\varepsilon:=\mathcal{R}\Delta-M_{\mathcal{M}}-\kappa_{+}>0

such that, for every
n\geq 1
and every
t\geq 0
,

\frac{d}{dt}\log\frac{x_{\ell_{n}}(t)}{x_{m_{n}}(t)}\geq\varepsilon.

Consequently, for every
n\geq 1
,

\frac{x_{\ell_{n}}(t)}{x_{m_{n}}(t)}\geq\frac{x_{\ell_{n}}(0)}{x_{m_{n}}(0)}e^{\varepsilon t}\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

(65)

In particular,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{x_{\ell_{n}}(t)}{x_{m_{n}}(t)}=+\infty\qquad\text{for every fixed }n\geq 1.

Thus the first lineage separates exponentially from the second at every depth, with a rate
\varepsilon
independent of
n
.

Proof.

Fix
n\geq 1
. Applying Lemma
4.1
with

i=\ell_{n},\qquad j=m_{n},

we get

\frac{d}{dt}\log\frac{x_{\ell_{n}}}{x_{m_{n}}}=\left(a_{\ell_{n}}\frac{x_{\ell_{n-1}}}{x_{\ell_{n}}}-a_{m_{n}}\frac{x_{m_{n-1}}}{x_{m_{n}}}\right)-(k_{\ell_{n}}-k_{m_{n}})+\mathcal{R}(f_{\ell_{n}}-f_{m_{n}}).

Since

a_{\ell_{n}}\frac{x_{\ell_{n-1}}}{x_{\ell_{n}}}\geq 0,

and by (
61
),

-a_{m_{n}}\frac{x_{m_{n-1}}}{x_{m_{n}}}\geq-M_{\mathcal{M}},

while by (
62
),

-(k_{\ell_{n}}-k_{m_{n}})\geq-(k_{\ell_{n}}-k_{m_{n}})_{+}\geq-\kappa_{+}.

Finally, by (
63
),

f_{\ell_{n}}-f_{m_{n}}\geq\Delta.

Therefore, for all
n\geq 1
and all
t\geq 0
,

\frac{d}{dt}\log\frac{x_{\ell_{n}}(t)}{x_{m_{n}}(t)}\geq-M_{\mathcal{M}}-\kappa_{+}+\mathcal{R}\Delta=\varepsilon>0.

Integrating over
[0,t]
gives

\log\frac{x_{\ell_{n}}(t)}{x_{m_{n}}(t)}-\log\frac{x_{\ell_{n}}(0)}{x_{m_{n}}(0)}\geq\varepsilon t.

Exponentiating yields (
65
). Hence, for every fixed
n\geq 1
,

\frac{x_{\ell_{n}}(t)}{x_{m_{n}}(t)}\to+\infty\qquad\text{as }t\to\infty.

The rate
\varepsilon
is independent of
n
, although the prefactor

\frac{x_{\ell_{n}}(0)}{x_{m_{n}}(0)}

may depend on
n
.
∎

Remark 4
.

If one wants a divergence estimate that is also uniform in the prefactor over all depths, it is enough to assume

\inf_{n\geq 1}\frac{x_{\ell_{n}}(0)}{x_{m_{n}}(0)}>0.

Under this additional condition, (
65
) implies a depth-uniform lower bound of the form

\frac{x_{\ell_{n}}(t)}{x_{m_{n}}(t)}\geq c_{0}e^{\varepsilon t}\qquad\text{for all }n\geq 1,\ t\geq 0,

where

Numerical results

We now present numerical illustrations of the analytical results. Following the numerical style of prevolutionary dynamics in
Nowak and Ohtsuki 2008

s
, the compound resource parameter
rK
, and the replication intensity
\mathcal{R}
. Since the full polymerization network is infinite-dimensional, all simulations are performed on finite binary truncations

\mathcal{S}_{N}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}\{1,2\}^{n}.

Unless otherwise stated, we use
N=6

|\mathcal{S}_{N}|=\sum_{n=1}^{N}2^{n}=2^{N+1}-2

5.1
Kinetic selection in the pre-replicative polymerization landscape

We first illustrate how kinetic asymmetries alone can create unequal equilibrium abundances before replication is introduced. For each sequence
i
, the equilibrium abundance is

x_{i}^{*}=\prod_{r=1}^{L(i)}\frac{a_{i^{(r)}}}{d+a_{i^{(r)}1}+a_{i^{(r)}2}}.

Thus the polymerization rates define a pre-replicative abundance landscape.

In the random landscape experiment, we introduce kinetic heterogeneity by assigning each polymerization reaction one of two possible rates. More precisely, for every reaction leading to a sequence
q
, we set

a_{q}(s)=1+s\eta_{q},

where
\eta_{q}\in\{0,1\}
. A fixed fraction
p
of the reactions is chosen to be fast, meaning
\eta_{q}=1
, while the remaining reactions are slow, meaning
\eta_{q}=0
. Thus fast reactions have rate
1+s
, whereas slow reactions have rate
1
. The same random assignment of fast and slow reactions is kept fixed as
s
varies.

The parameter
s\geq 0
therefore measures the strength of the kinetic contrast between fast and slow reactions. When
s=0
, all reactions have the same rate and the polymerization landscape is kinetically homogeneous. As
s
increases, fast reactions become progressively more favorable relative to slow ones, so that lineages containing several fast reactions along their ancestral paths can become strongly enriched.

For each value of
s
, we compute the equilibrium
x_{i}^{*}(s)
on the finite truncation
\mathcal{S}_{N}
. Since

x_{i}^{*}(s)=\prod_{r=1}^{L(i)}\frac{a_{i^{(r)}}(s)}{d+a_{i^{(r)}1}(s)+a_{i^{(r)}2}(s)},

the effect of
s
is cumulative along the ancestral chain of
i
. A sequence whose lineage contains many fast incoming reactions may gain a multiplicative advantage, whereas fast outgoing reactions from an intermediate sequence may also increase its loss term through the denominator. Hence the abundance landscape depends on the balance between enhanced production into a sequence and enhanced depletion toward its followers.

Figure
1
plots all equilibrium abundances as functions of
s
. As
s
increases, the initially homogeneous landscape becomes increasingly structured: a small subset of lineages becomes highly enriched, while other lineages are suppressed. This illustrates a selection-like effect generated purely by kinetic asymmetries, before template-directed replication is introduced.

C_{-}^{*}\approx 2.113,\qquad C_{+}^{*}\approx 7.887.

Taking

5.3
Exponential convergence of the coupled truncated system

We next simulate the finite truncation of the polymerization–competition model:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=1-\beta_{1}Cx_{1^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{2^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=1-\beta_{2}Cx_{2^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\dot{C}

\displaystyle=C\left[r\left(1-\frac{C}{K}\right)-\beta_{1}x_{1^{\prime}}-\beta_{2}x_{2^{\prime}}\right],

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=a_{i}x_{i^{\prime}}-\left(d+a_{i1}+a_{i2}\right)x_{i},\qquad i\in\mathcal{S}_{N}.

We use

d=1,\qquad a_{i}=0.2,\qquad a_{i1}=a_{i2}=0.1,\qquad K=10,\qquad r=1.2,\qquad\beta_{1}=1,\qquad\beta_{2}=1.3.

The stable equilibrium is determined by
C^{*}=C_{+}^{*}
, together with

x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}=\frac{1}{\beta_{1}C^{*}},\qquad x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}=\frac{1}{\beta_{2}C^{*}},\qquad x_{i}^{*}=\frac{a_{i}}{d+a_{i1}+a_{i2}}x_{i^{\prime}}^{*}.

To quantify convergence, define the truncated Lyapunov error

V_{N}(t)=|C(t)-C^{*}|^{2}+|x_{1^{\prime}}(t)-x_{1^{\prime}}^{*}|^{2}+|x_{2^{\prime}}(t)-x_{2^{\prime}}^{*}|^{2}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}|x_{i}(t)-x_{i}^{*}|^{2}.

5.4
Competition between prelife and replication

Finally, we illustrate the transition produced by template-directed replication. We use the replicated finite system

\dot{x}_{i}=a_{i}x_{i^{\prime}}-\left(d+a_{i1}+a_{i2}\right)x_{i}+\mathcal{R}x_{i}(f_{i}-\phi),\qquad i\in\mathcal{S}_{N},

with

\phi(t)=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}f_{i}x_{i}(t)}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}x_{i}(t)}.

Only sequences of maximal length
N
are assigned nonzero heterogeneous fitness values; shorter sequences are assigned neutral background fitness. This mirrors the experiment in which long polymers can become replicators while shorter polymers remain part of the pre-replicative chemical background.

For each value of
\mathcal{R}
, the system is integrated until it approaches a numerical steady state. Figure

\mathcal{R}

\mathcal{R}
increases, the fittest replicating sequence becomes strongly enriched, producing a transition from a pre-replicative regime to a replication-dominated regime.

These numerical experiments support the analytical picture developed above. The purely kinetic system converges to a unique compositional background; the resource-coupled system exhibits a sharp feasibility threshold
rK=8
; and heterogeneous replication breaks the previous equilibrium by amplifying fitter lineages.

6
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we developed a deterministic framework for sequence polymerization on the directed genealogy of finite molecules. The model separates three increasingly rich dynamical regimes, a purely kinetic polymerization cascade with fixed activated precursors, a polymerization–competition system, and a replicated system incorporating template-directed selection. This progression allows one to distinguish between compositional structure generated by kinetic production alone and genuine selection effects produced by differential replication.

For the baseline polymerization model, the infinite triangular structure of the equations makes it possible to construct solutions recursively along the polymerization tree. This yields global componentwise well-posedness, positivity, and a unique strictly positive equilibrium. The equilibrium has an explicit product representation along ancestor chains, showing that each sequence abundance is computed by the cumulative balance between production from its precursor and losses due to degradation and further extension. Moreover, every component converges exponentially to its equilibrium value. Under the additional uniform net decay condition

\inf_{i\in\mathcal{S}}(k_{i}-a_{i})>0,

this convergence strengthens to global exponential stability in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{S})
.

The binary model with resource reveals a second layer of structure. Once the activated precursor concentrations are allowed to evolve together with a shared environmental resource
C
, the existence of strictly positive equilibria is governed by the scalar threshold condition

rK\geq 8.

At the critical value
rK=8
, a unique positive resource level appears, whereas for
rK>8
two positive resource branches exist. The reduced scalar
C
-dynamics identifies the upper branch
C_{+}^{*}
as the locally attracting branch and the lower branch
C_{-}^{*}
as unstable.

We also established the basic dynamical consistency of the resource-coupled system. Positivity is forward invariant, global componentwise solutions exist for strictly positive initial data, and the resource variable admits an explicit logistic upper bound. A further lower-barrier condition for
C(t)
provides the persistence estimate needed to prevent degeneration of the resource level. Combining this persistence with uniform kinetic dominance assumptions gives a Lyapunov estimate and exponential convergence toward the positive equilibrium associated with the stable resource branch. In this way, the infinite polymerization cascade inherits stability from a controlled finite-dimensional core, provided that the coupling terms are dominated by sufficient dissipation.

The addition of replication changes the qualitative picture. When the fitness landscape is neutral, the replication term vanishes identically and the pre-replicative equilibrium is preserved. By contrast, heterogeneous fitness destroys the previous polymerization–competition equilibrium: unless all fitness values coincide with the equilibrium average fitness, the replicated vector field does not vanish at the old equilibrium. Thus differential replication is not just a perturbation of the kinetic background, it creates a genuinely new dynamical scenario.

The log-ratio identities make this transition especially transparent. For any two positive sequence concentrations
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
, the average fitness term cancels from

\frac{d}{dt}\log\frac{x_{i}}{x_{j}}.

Then, relative growth is governed by three competing effects: kinetic forcing from precursors, differences in decay and extension losses, and the direct fitness gap multiplied by the replication intensity
\mathcal{R}
. When the replication advantage dominates the kinetic and decay imbalance, the log-ratio grows at least linearly in time, and the concentration ratio diverges exponentially. The same mechanism extends to entire lineages under a uniform fitness gap, giving lineage-level separation at a rate independent of depth.

The numerical experiments support the mathematical analysis. In the pre-replicative scenario, kinetic asymmetries alone generate nonuniform abundance landscapes, including enrichment of favored lineages. In the resource-coupled system, the bifurcation diagram illustrates the threshold
rK=8
and the emergence of the stable upper resource branch. Simulations of finite truncations show exponential decay of the Lyapunov error toward the stable equilibrium. Finally, increasing the replication intensity produces a transition from a polymerization-dominated scenario to a replication-dominated scenario in which fitter sequences separate exponentially from their competitors.

Overall, the results suggest a coherent mathematical description for the emergence of evolutionary dynamics from open polymerization chemistry. A driven polymerization network first creates a stable compositional background; resource coupling imposes feasibility thresholds and selects admissible steady regimes; and heterogeneous replication breaks the pre-replicative equilibrium by amplifying fitter lineages. In this sense, selection appears as a dynamical instability of the kinetic background once replication becomes strong enough to overcome production and decay constraints.

Several directions are still open. One is to weaken the sufficient dominance assumptions used in the
\ell^{2}
-Lyapunov argument, or to replace them with sharper spectral or semigroup conditions. A further direction is to incorporate mutation, catalytic feedback, compartmentalization, or time-dependent environmental forcing. Such directions would bring the model closer to realistic prebiotic scenarios, where polymerization, degradation, resource limitation, and replication occur simultaneously in fluctuating far from equilibrium contexts.
