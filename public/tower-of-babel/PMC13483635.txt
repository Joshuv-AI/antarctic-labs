Early-life stress and adolescent circadian dysrhythmia drives unique behavioral and microbial profiles in rats
Authors: Wenjing Dai, Muhammad Jahangir, Tao Li, Wan-Jun Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesEarly life adversity and circadian disruptions are known to impact neurodevelopment and physiology. This study investigated the effects of maternal separation (MS), adolescent circadian dysrhythmia, and their combination (double hit) on anxiety-like behavior and gut microbiota composition in rats.MethodsRats were divided into four groups: CL (control group: normal early-life conditions with a standard light/dark cycle during adolescence), MS + N (maternal separation (MS) with a standard light/dark cycle (N=normal)) during adolescence), N + ALD (normal early-life conditions (N) with an altered light/dark cycle (ALD) during adolescence), and MS + ALD (combined exposure: MS with an altered light/dark cycle (ALD) during adolescence). Anxiety-like behavior and locomotor activity were assessed using the Open Field Test. Gut microbial diversity and taxonomic composition were analysed to identify microbial shifts across groups.ResultsBehavioral analysis indicated that the combined stress group (MSLD) spent significantly (p < 0.05) more time in the center of the arena compared to the CL, MS + N, and N + ALD groups, suggesting a compromise in risk assessment ability due to dual stress exposure. Microbiome profiling revealed that while a core microbiome was conserved, each stressor generated a unique taxonomic signature. The N + ALD group appeared as the most distinct outlier, characterized by the lowest number of unique features and a specific enrichment of the viral species of phylum Uroviricota. Conversely, the MS + ALD group was distinguished by an enrichment of Bacteroidota species, including Muribaculum intestinale and Phocaeicola vulgatus, while the MS + N group showed enrichment in Bacteroides acidifaciens. Mycobiome analysis showed that early-life stress was the primary driver of fungal restructuring, distinguishing maternal separation groups by the loss of Neocallimastix species and the competitive expansion of Piromyces finnis. While adolescent circadian disruption alone largely preserved the baseline mycobiome, the cumulative dual-hit stress (MS + ALD) generated a distinct dysbiotic profile evident by the unique proliferation of Anaeromyces robustus.ConclusionsIn conclusion, the developmental timing of stress exposure drives distinct dysbiotic shifts. Specifically, adolescent circadian disruption selectively targets the virome, whereas early-life stress causes shift in the microbiome which endures a long-term foundation for adolescent psychiatric vulnerability. Notably, the cumulative effect of early life and adolescence stressors results in a unique microbial and behavioral profile, highlighting that the specific developmental window of exposure is a decisive factor in gut-brain axis dysfunction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05287-y.

Introduction

The endogenous circadian rhythms synchronise physiological functions with environmental factors like the day-night cycle, meal timing and social habits. Nevertheless, modern lifestyles have substantially altered human life resulting in a persistent desynchronization between the biological rhythms and routine activities [1], which can be termed as Chronodisruption: a mismatch between internal biological rhythms and external environmental stresses which affects adversely both health and cognitive function [1]. Epidemiological data estimates that 70% of the industrialized population experiences social jet lag (SJL) [2] which has been related to various pathological conditions [2, 3]. Adolescents and young adults particularly demonstrate a heightened vulnerability, often characterized by deficits in adaptive coping mechanisms, reward processing, behavioral inhibition, and affective regulation [4–7] which are reflected in decreased academic performance [8]. In fact, the degree of SJL directly correlates with lower educational achievements [9] and weak emotional regulation [10]. Additionally, SJL causes circadian misalignment [11] and dysregulation of the molecular circadian clock [12]. The circadian rhythms are strongly associated with mental health and mood regulation and disrupted circadian rhythms can result in mood and psychiatric disorders [13]. Recently evidenced that adolescent circadian disruption induces impairments in short-term, spatial, and social memory without prompting anxiety-like behavior [10].

Early life stress (ELS) refer to a set of potentially traumatic experiences psychological, physical, and sexual abuse) and household dysfunction (violent treatment of mother, substance abuse, mental illness, and criminal behavior in the household) [14], physical and emotional neglect and parent divorce or separation [15, 16] low socioeconomic status, peer victimization, and parent death [17]. Early life stress (ELS) is a robust predictor of adverse long-term health and behavioral outcomes, including substance use disorders [18, 19], increase risks for health issues, psychiatric disorders, and mortality [20–24]. However, the peripubertal period represents a critical window of developmental plasticity and a crucial time for recalibrating stress responses [25, 26]. While this biological “resetting” offers a pathway toward improved physiological and psychological resilience, particularly when supported by a positive social environment during adolescence [25] but such adaptive benefits are not uniformly observed across individuals.

Circadian dysrhythmia has the potential to reshape the intestinal microbial communities, which may precipitate metabolic dysfunction by disrupting the host’s inflammatory signals and energy homeostasis [27]. Environmental light-dark cycle is one of the major factors driving the daily oscillation, diversity and composition of gut microbes [28]. Furthermore, this system is part of a complex, reciprocal signalling network linking the enteric environment with the central nervous system (bidirectional communications between the brain and the gut) [29]. The microbiota-gut-brain axis is indispensable for normal central nervous system (CNS) development [30]. Disruptions in the microbiome–gut–brain axis may further exacerbate physiological dysregulation, thereby increasing vulnerability to psychopathology, particularly when such disturbances occur during critical developmental windows across the lifespan [31]. Early life represents a sensitive period for neurodevelopment, while adolescence constitutes a major phase of synaptic pruning and neuroendocrine recalibration; consequently, internal or external imbalances during these stages can interfere with processes essential for healthy brain maturation. Adolescents, particularly high school and college students, may be especially susceptible due to prolonged academic demands, increased homework burden, and excessive screen exposure. Which in turn increases the light exposure duration and imbalances the light/dark cycle which can be a substrate to develop cognitive abnormalities to impact the adulthood life. The available literature evidences the association of early life adversities, adolescent circadian dysrhythmias and cognitive abilities, however, gut microbiome shift and related basic research is insufficient to understand the exact mechanism, to trace the development of disorders in context of childhood adversities, which can be exploited for non-pharmacological therapeutic purposes. To address this gap, we integrated early-life adversity with supportive or adverse adolescent environments to examine their combined and independent associations with gut microbiome composition and structure.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The study was conducted at the Affiliated Mental Health Center & Hangzhou Seventh People’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Hangzhou, China). All the experimental protocols were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Review Committee of Zhejiang University (Approval No. ZJU20240982). Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (5 dams) were purchased from Hangzhou Paisi Ao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Following parturition, male pups at postnatal day 4 (PD4) were randomly selected, to avoid litter effect, and allocated into four experimental groups. The study was conducted in only male SD rats from postnatal day (PD) 4 through adulthood, with animals assigned to one of four experimental groups based on interventions applied during two critical developmental periods: early life and adolescence. During the early-life phase (PD4–16), pups either underwent a maternal separation (MS) protocol consisting of 2-hour separations three times daily or were maintained under standard rearing conditions. In the subsequent adolescent phase, rats were either exposed to a prolonged disrupted light/dark cycle (18 h light/6 h dark) or maintained under a standard light/dark cycle. The rats were divided into four experimental groups: CL (control group: normal early-life conditions with a standard light/dark cycle during adolescence), MS + N (maternal separation (MS) with a standard light/dark cycle (N=normal)) during adolescence), N + ALD (normal early-life conditions (N) with an altered light/dark cycle (ALD) during adolescence), and MS + ALD (combined exposure: MS with an altered light/dark cycle (ALD) during adolescence). Upon reaching adulthood (10 weeks), all animals were subjected to behavioral assessments, and fecal samples were subsequently collected for gut metagenomic profiling. (Fig. 1). At the end of the study, all animals were euthanized using an overdose of isoflurane anesthesia (5%).

Maternal separation

Early-life stress was modelled using a well-established maternal separation paradigm, an approach with high construct and face validity for simulating human childhood neglect and abuse. MS is a cornerstone model for investigating the neurobiological origins of psychiatric disorders; while 3-hour daily separation protocols are sufficient to induce persistent deficits in emotional learning and fear memory [32], more protracted 6-hour protocols are associated with significantly elevated stress in adolescent males [33]. In the present study, pups were subjected to a 6-hour daily cumulative separation (administered in three sessions: 2-hours each) from postnatal day (PND) 4 through PND 16. (Fig. 1). Following each isolation period concluded, pups were reunited with their dams in their home cage. Offspring were weaned on PND 21 and subsequently housed in separate cages [34].

Altering pubertal recalibration

To simulate circadian disruptions in adolescent life, animals were subjected to a modified light/dark cycle from postnatal day 33 to 58 (Fig. 1). Specifically, animals were exposed to an 18-hour light/6-hour dark cycle, over the course of 25 days. These conditions aim to mimic circadian disruption during adolescence, a critical period of brain development, providing insights into the potential impact on gut microbiome and behaviour.

Behavioral assay

Open field test

To assess exploratory activity and anxiety-like behavior, rats (CL n=7, MS + N n=7, N + ALD n=6, MS + ALD n= 5) were subjected to the Open Field Test (OFT). This assay evaluates the conflict between a rodent’s natural drive to investigate a novel environment and its innate aversion to exposed, open areas. The apparatus consisted of a square enclosure (100 × 100 × 40 cm) with high walls to prevent escape. Following the protocol established by Gould et al. [35], each rat was placed in a starting corner and allowed to roam freely for 10 min (600 s). Anxiety levels were determined by quantifying the time spent in the periphery versus the center, as more frequent or prolonged entry into the center indicates lower anxiety levels.

Metagenomic sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from fecal samples (n = 5/group) using the Fecal Genome DNA Extraction Kit (Cat. No. AU46111-96, BioTeke, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Nano DNA Library Preparation Kit (Cat. No. FC-121–4001, Illumina, USA). The libraries were subsequently sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (PE150 mode) by LC-Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Raw sequencing data were processed using fastp (v0.23.4) to filter low-quality reads, remove adapter sequences, and eliminate undetermined bases. Coding regions (CDS) within the assembled contigs were predicted using MetaGeneMark (v3.26). To construct a non-redundant gene catalog, CDS sequences from all samples were clustered using MMseq2 (v15-6f452). Taxonomic assignment was performed by aligning protein sequences against the NCBI Non-Redundant (NR) database using DIAMOND (v0.9.14).

Statistical analyses

Behavioral assay data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.0) and R (v3.6.0). For the behavioral data, normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variances using the Brown–Forsythe and Bartlett’s tests. Group comparisons were performed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons; statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. For microbial community analysis, robustness was ensured by conducting 999 permutations. Bray–Curtis distances were calculated after uniform shifting of transformed abundance values to satisfy non-negativity assumptions required for Bray–Curtis dissimilarity calculations. Additionally, trans-kingdom relationships were evaluated using Spearman rank correlation analysis. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test (also known as the Mann-Whitney U test) was used to compare differences between two groups with biological replicates. For comparisons involving multiple groups with biological replicates, the Kruskal-Wallis test was employed. The default threshold for the LDA (Linear Discriminant Analysis) score was set at > 3.0. Spearman’s rank analyzed exploratory metagenomic cross-domain associations (n = 5,∣r∣≥0.8) using FDR-adjusted permutations.

Results

Combined early-life adversity and adolescent stress compromise functional risk assessment

A total of 25 rats were used for this experiment (nCL=7, nMS+N=7, nN+ALD=6, nMS+ALD=5). We conducted the open field test to assess the anxiety-like behavior. We noticed that the “double hit” group: MS + LD, spent less time (567.0±10.1 (s)) in the periphery of arena compared to control group (594.3±2.8), MS + N (585±5) and N + ALD (585.9±3.5) which presumably indicates that dual stress exposure may compromise the risk assessment ability. Moreover, MS + N, N + ALD and MS + ALD groups showed a higher mean locomotor speed (0.07±0.01, 0.08±0.002 and 0.08±0.01 (m/s)) in the open field arena which indicates hyper locomotion in response to stress exposure (Fig. 2), which may indicate apparent resilience but the increase in vulnerability after stress exposure.

Impact of early-life and circadian stress on gut microbial diversity

Gut metagenomic analysis was conducted on fecal samples collected from a total of 20 rats, with five animals in each group (CL, MS + N, N + ALD, and MS + ALD). To assess the changes in microbial community structure, alpha diversity was evaluated using the Simpson, Shannon, and Chao1 indices, repectively (Fig. 3). The Simpson index, which accounts for both species richness and evenness (dominance), showed significant variation between groups. The CL group exhibited a significantly higher Simpson index compared to the MS + N group (p < 0.05), the MS + ALD group (p < 0.05) and N + ALD group (p < 0.01). This suggests that the microbial communities in the MS + N and MS + ALD groups have reduced evenness or are more dominated by specific taxa compared to the CL group. In contrast, the Shannon index (a measure of overall diversity) and the Chao1 index (an estimator of species richness) did not show any statistically significant differences across the four experimental groups (p > 0.05). While the violin plots for the Shannon index suggest a trending decrease in median diversity for the MS +A LD group compared to CL, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Similarly, species richness as measured by Chao1 remained comparable across all treatments.Collectively, these data indicate that while the total number of species (richness) remained stable, the distribution or evenness of the microbial population was significantly altered in the MS + N and MS + ALD groups compared to the CL group.

Developmental timing of stress exposure drives multi-domain microbial dysbiosis and distinct taxonomic shifts

Species level composition analysis shows the distribution of shared and unique microbial species across the four study groups. The vast majority of detected species (13,236) formed a shared core microbiome present in all four groups, indicating a high degree of taxonomic conservation. Despite this substantial overlap, distinct unique signatures were identified for each group: the CL group exhibited the highest number of unique features (166), followed by the MS + N (146) and MS + ALD (96) groups, while the N + ALD group displayed the fewest unique features (36) (Fig. 4A). This pattern suggests that while the fundamental community structure is preserved across groups, specific taxa likely drive the differentiation between the control and stress-exposed groups. Furthermore, stress exposure at distinct developmental stages differentially shapes the microbial landscape.

Hierarchical clustering patterns based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity (Fig. 4B) was validated using PERMANOVA (n = 5 per group, 999 permutations), calculated from sample-level taxonomic relative abundances. The global test revealed a non-significant trend toward group-level compositional divergence (pseudo-F = 1.178, p = 0.302, R² = 0.181), indicating that experimental group membership explained 18.1% of total bacteriome compositional variance, a biologically non-trivial proportion that did not reach statistical significance, most likely due to the limited power inherent to n = 5/group designs. No individual pairwise comparison reached significance after Bonferroni correction (all p > 0.10; threshold α = 0.0083), though the N + ALD vs. MS + N contrast produced the largest pairwise effect size (pseudo-F = 1.865, R² = 0.189, p = 0.101), consistent with the dendrogram positioning these groups in distinct parent clades. Examination of the Bray–Curtis distance structure directionally corroborated the observed clustering topology. The N + ALD group (normal early-life conditions with adolescent circadian disruption) exhibited the greatest intra-group compositional homogeneity (mean BC = 0.180 ± 0.094), while MS + N (maternal separation with a standard adolescent light/dark cycle) showed the highest within-group dispersion (BC = 0.393 ± 0.218), reflecting heterogeneous bacteriome responses to early-life adversity. Between-group distances were greatest for the CL vs. MS + N (BC = 0.350 ± 0.188) and N + ALD vs. MS + N (BC = 0.328 ± 0.188) comparisons, corroborating the topological separation of MS + N from both non-MS groups in the dendrogram. At the cluster level, the MS-associated groups (MS + N and MS + ALD) showed higher mean within-cluster dissimilarity (BC = 0.353 ± 0.178) compared to the control-associated cluster (CL and N + ALD; BC = 0.243 ± 0.095), with between-cluster dissimilarity (BC = 0.310 ± 0.151) exceeding within control-cluster dissimilarity, all directionally consistent with maternal separation as the primary axis of bacteriome compositional divergence. At the species level, Heminiphilus faecis was the only taxon exhibiting statistically significant group-level variation (Kruskal–Wallis H = 15.06, p = 0.002), with selective enrichment in the N + ALD group relative to MS + N (p = 0.008), MS + LD (p = 0.008), and CL (p = 0.032), identifying adolescent circadian disruption, in the absence of early-life adversity, as the specific condition driving Heminiphilus faecis expansion. In summary, although global community-level analysis did not reach statistical significance, the alignment of Bray–Curtis distance metrics and specific taxonomic shifts offers cohesive, directional evidence for the clustering patterns visualized in Fig. 4B.

The Phyla level analysis showed that among the four groups, the N + ALD group exhibited a unique signature defined by high relative abundances of Uroviricota (viruses), Bacteroidota, and Fibrobacterota, while appearing depleted in the majority of other identified phyla. The MS + N group showed specific enrichment in a cluster containing Fusobacteriota, Campylobacterota, Bacillota, and Chloroflexota. Finally, the MS + ALD group was distinguished by the enrichment of a bottom cluster comprising Verrucomicrobiota, Candidatus Melainabacteria, Thermodesulfobacteriota, and Cyanobacteriota. Additionally, a cluster including Mycoplasmatota, Euryarchaeota, and Candidatus Saccharibacteria displayed a gradient of abundance that was generally lowest in the N + ALD group and higher across the CL, MS + N, and MS + ALD groups (Fig. 4C).

To evaluate the statistical significance of the species-level bacterial abundance patterns observed in the heatmap (Fig. 4), differential abundance analysis was performed on z-score normalized relative abundances for all 20 taxa across the four experimental groups (CL, MS + N, MS + ALD, N + ALD; n = 5/group) using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for taxa reaching omnibus significance. The Fig. 4C depicts phylum-level classification (predominantly Bacteroidota and Bacillota, with one representative from Uroviricota). The dendrogram (Fig. 4C) indicates that the microbiome composition of the MS + ALD and MS + N groups is almost inverse but forming a primary cluster with share abundance of few similar species, while the N + ALD group separates as the most distinct outlier. Of the 20 bacterial taxa examined, only Heminiphilus faecis exhibited statistically significant variation across groups by Kruskal–Wallis test (H = 15.06, p = 0.0018). Pairwise comparisons confirmed that this significance was driven by a marked and selective enrichment of Heminiphilus faecis in the N + ALD group (adolescent circadian disruption), which showed significantly higher abundance relative to MS + N (p = 0.008), MS + ALD (p = 0.008), and CL (p = 0.032). Parallel to these bacterial shifts, viral species belonging to the phylum Uroviricota (Supplementary File S1) exhibited a similar phenotype; while these taxa were depleted in both the MS + N and MS + ALD groups, they were uniquely enriched in the N + ALD group, suggesting a coordinated trans-kingdom response to adolescent circadian disruption. Conversely, the MS + N group exhibited the lowest abundance of this taxon, differing significantly from CL (p = 0.008) and MS + ALD (p = 0.016). These results statistically confirm the N + ALD-specific enrichment of Heminiphilus faecis observed in Fig. 4, identifying it as the primary taxonomic biomarker of adolescent circadian disruption in our study. For the remaining 19 taxa, Kruskal–Wallis testing did not yield omnibus significance (all p > 0.06), indicating that the heatmap patterns for these species while visually informative of directional trends do not reach statistical significance at the current sample size (n = 5/group). Notably, uncultured Bacteroides sp. approached significance (H = 7.37, p = 0.061), with a significant MS + N vs. N + ALD pairwise contrast (p = 0.016), suggesting a trend toward N + ALD-associated enrichment that may warrant investigation in a larger cohort. Similarly, Muribaculum intestinale showed a consistent trend of depletion in the MS + N group (mean z-score = − 0.99) relative to MS + ALD and N + ALD, though this did not reach statistical significance (H = 5.83, p = 0.120). The absence of broader statistical significance across taxa is likely attributable to the modest sample size and inherent within-group variability characteristic of gut microbiome data, rather than a true absence of biological effect. and notably, the viral species of phylum Uroviricota (Supplementary file S1), which appeared depleted in the MS + ALD and MS + N groups, is uniquely abundant in N + ALD group.

Furthermore, the Fig. 4 (D) displays the mean relative abundances (± SEM) of the 20 most abundant bacterial taxa across all four groups, spanning phylum-to-species resolution across Pseudomonadota, Bacillota, and Bacteroidota. Two biologically distinct patterns emerged. First, taxonomically unclassified Bacteria across six hierarchical levels dominated the panel (relative abundance ~ 0.05–0.12),, with a consistent trend toward higher representation in the MS + N group, corroborating the broader gut dysbiosis signature driven by maternal separation. Second, and most notably, Heminiphilus faecis and its parent genus Heminiphilus (Bacillota) were markedly and selectively enriched in the N + ALD group (relative abundance ~ 0.04–0.05) compared to near-absent levels in CL, MS + N, and MS + ALD animals, representing a bacteriome signature uniquely attributable to adolescent circadian disruption. The remaining taxa including Firmicutes bacterium CAG:110, Eubacteriaceae, Eubacterium, and the rest of four Bacteroides species remained at uniformly low abundances across all groups, indicating resilience to both stressors.

Our results demonstrate that time specific stressor has specific impact on gut microbiome shift and each stressor generates a unique gut microbial signature, with the combined MS + ALD group exhibiting a profile distinct from rat groups with individual stress components (maternal separation or adolescent circadian dysrhythmia). The enrichment of taxa of Uroviricota exclusively in the N + ALD group suggests adolescent circadian disruption may uniquely influence the gut virome. Collectively, these data demonstrate that early-life adversity and adolescent circadian dysrhythmia both independently and in combination induce distinct shifts in gut microbiota composition.

Early-life maternal separation acts as a primary driver of gut mycobiome dysbiosis

To dissect the impact of early-life stress versus adolescent circadian disruption on the gut mycobiome, we analyzed species-level abundance using hierarchical clustering based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity (Fig. 5). Beta diversity analysis showed that the N + ALD and CL mycobiomes cluster together, representing a global structure distinct from the maternal separation cohorts (Fig. 5A). Nevertheless, this shared clustering did not reflect compositional equivalence; species-level relative abundance profiles and heatmaps (Fig. 5A and B) confirmed that both groups maintain unique taxonomic identities. This indicates that circadian disruption triggers discrete species-level shifts even when overall community dissimilarity remains low. Conversely, the MS + N (maternal separation) and MS + ALD (dual stress) groups formed a distinct cluster, suggesting that maternal separation is the dominant factor driving fungal dysbiosis, regardless of subsequent adolescent stress. Rat groups subjected to maternal separation (MS + N and MS + ALD) showed a marked depletion of Astraeus odoratus (Kruskal–Wallis H = 8.82, p = 0.032; CL vs. MS + N: p = 0.021, CL vs. MS + ALD: p = 0.021) and Gnomoniopsis smithogilvyi (H = 9.36, p = 0.025; CL vs. MS + N: p = 0.035, CL vs. MS + ALD: p = 0.021), which are abundant in the CL and N + ALD cluster, although with differences in abundance pattern between CL and N +A LD. Additionally, Neocallimastix californiae was significantly depleted in both maternal separation groups relative to CL (H = 15.83, p = 0.001; CL vs. MS + N: p = 0.008, CL vs. MS + ALD: p = 0.016), while showing the highest abundance in the N + ALD group (MS + N vs. N + ALD: p = 0.008, MS + ALD vs. N + ALD: p = 0.008). Furthermore, Pleurostoma richardsiae was markedly enriched in the N + ALD group compared to both CL and MS + N (H = 10.91, p = 0.012; CL vs. N + ALD: p = 0.012, MS + N vs. N + ALD: p = 0.012), as was Neocallimastix californiae (CL vs. N +A LD: p = 0.008), collectively suggesting a distinctive N +A LD-associated mycobiome signature. Piromyces finnis was significantly depleted in the N + ALD group relative to CL (H = 13.31, p = 0.004; CL vs. N + ALD: p = 0.008), while Lepidopterella palustris exhibited a significant reduction specifically in N + ALD compared to MS + N (H = 8.85, p = 0.031; MS + N vs. N + ALD: p = 0.011). Moreover, the MS + N group was characterized by a specific bloom of Piromyces finnis (CL vs. MS + N: p = 0.032) and Penicillium samsonianum (H = 11.24, p = 0.011; CL vs. MS + N: p = 0.021, MS + N vs. N + ALD: p = 0.008), species that appear to serve as markers of the long-term impact of early-life stress. While the MS + ALD group shared the general dysbiotic profile of the MS + N group (clustering together), the combination of early-life stress and adolescent circadian disruption induced a unique signature, most prominently the selective enrichment of Mycena metata (H = 9.42, p = 0.024; CL vs. MS + ALD: p = 0.045), which was virtually absent in all other groups Fig. 6.

Beta diversity analysis using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity revealed significant compositional differences across the four experimental groups (PERMANOVA: pseudo-F = 4.036, p = 0.0001, 999 permutations), with group membership accounting for 43.1% of the total variance in mycobiome composition (R² = 0.431). Hierarchical clustering based on these pairwise dissimilarities recapitulated the two-cluster structure evident in Fig. 5A: the maternal separation groups (MS + N and MS + ALD) formed a cohesive cluster characterized by relatively lower intra-cluster Bray–Curtis distances (mean BC = 0.359 ± 0.092), whereas the CL and N + ALD groups constituted a second cluster (mean BC within = 0.428 ± 0.116). Between-cluster dissimilarity was consistently higher than within-cluster dissimilarity (mean BC = 0.467 ± 0.098), further supporting the compositional segregation driven by maternal separation history. Pairwise PERMANOVA confirmed that all group pairs except MS + N vs. MS + ALD were significantly distinct from one another (CL vs. MS + N: pseudo-F = 3.745, p = 0.015; CL vs. MS + ALD: pseudo-F = 2.415, p = 0.007; CL vs. N + ALD: pseudo-F = 3.986, p = 0.009; MS + N vs. N + ALD: pseudo-F = 8.425, p = 0.008; MS + ALD vs. N + ALD: pseudo-F = 4.141, p = 0.009), while the non-significant MS + N vs. MS + ALD contrast (pseudo-F = 2.183, p = 0.072) is consistent with their shared clustering and overlapping mycobiome composition attributable to the common maternal separation experience (Fig. 5).

Trans-kingdom correlation analysis between gut mycobiota and bacteriome

To explore potential ecological relationships between the fungal and bacterial communities, we performed an exploratory trans-kingdom analysis using Spearman’s rank correlation, computed separately within each group (CL, MS + N, N + ALD, and MS + ALD) between every fungal taxon and every bacterial or viral taxon. After Benjamini–Hochberg correction, no individual correlation reached significance in any group (all q > 0.05), consistent with the limited statistical power at n = 5/group. The following associations are reported as descriptive patterns rather than confirmed ecological or causal interactions.

In the control group (CL), fungal and bacterial taxa showed predominantly positive associations. Neocallimastix sp. JGI-2020a co-varied positively with Lachnospiraceae bacterium (r = + 0.90), Acutalibacter muris (r = + 0.90), and Phocaeicola vulgatus (r = + 0.80), while Piromyces finnis was positively associated with Ligilactobacillus murinus (r = + 0.90). Astraeus odoratus showed negative associations with Leyella lascolaii (r = − 0.70) and Phocaeicola dorei (r = − 0.50). This pattern is consistent with broadly concordant abundance profiles between fiber-degrading fungi and commensal bacteria in the control gut.

In the maternal-separation group (MS + N), the control-associated patterns were not preserved. The positive Neocallimastix associations seen in controls were absent, with Neocallimastix sp. JGI-2020a instead showing negative associations with Phocaeicola vulgatus (r = − 0.50) and Muribaculum intestinale (r = − 0.50). Piromyces finnis co-varied positively with Lachnospiraceae bacterium (r = + 0.70) and showed little association with Ligilactobacillus murinus (r = − 0.15). New positive associations appeared for Irineochytrium annulatum with Phocaeicola dorei (r = + 0.90) and Astraeus odoratus with Phocaeicola dorei (r = + 0.56), suggesting a reorganization of fungal–bacterial co-occurrence following early-life stress.

In the circadian-disruption group (N + ALD), the most prominent association was a positive co-occurrence between Piromyces finnis and the viral class Caudoviricetes (r = + 0.90), indicating a possible fungal–viral link under adolescent circadian disruption. Piromyces finnis was also positively associated with Muribaculum intestinale (r = + 0.60) and negatively with Heminiphilus faecis (r = − 0.50), whereas the Neocallimastix–Limosilactobacillus reuteri association was weak (r = − 0.30).

In the dual-stress group (MS + ALD), several strong positive fungal–bacterial associations were observed, particularly around Ligilactobacillus murinus, which co-varied with Astraeus odoratus (r = + 0.97), Actinomortierella wolfii (r = + 0.90), and Gnomoniopsis smithogilvyi (r = + 0.79). Bacteroides uniformis was positively associated with Actinomortierella wolfii (r = + 0.90), Astraeus odoratus (r = + 0.67), and Gnomoniopsis smithogilvyi (r = + 0.63), and Phocaeicola vulgatus co-varied with Pyricularia sp. CBS 133,598 and Actinomortierella wolfii (r = + 0.60 each) (Fig. 6). In contrast, the stress-associated fungi Anaeromyces robustus and Piromyces spp. showed predominantly negative associations with bacterial taxa (Supplementary File S2).

Taken together, these exploratory patterns suggest that the structure of fungal–bacterial co-occurrence differs across developmental stress conditions, shifting from predominantly positive associations in controls toward a mix of positive and negative associations under early-life and combined stress.

Discussion

Our results reveal that the gut microbiome’s response to stress is not uniform but is defined by the timing of exposure. Particularly the early life adversity emerges as a primary determinant of adult gut microbiome structure. Distinct microbial signatures were identified for rats experiencing early-life adversity (MS + N) versus adolescent circadian disruption (N + ALD), while sequential exposure to both (the MS + ALD “double-hit”) produced a third distinct microbiome profile. This demonstrates that the gut ecosystem has unique vulnerabilities at different developmental windows, and the timing of stress determines the specific course of dysbiosis.

At the behavioral level, the open field test revealed that the combined-stress (MS + ALD) group spent significantly less time in the periphery, and correspondingly more time in the centre, of the arena relative to the CL, MS + N, and N + ALD groups (CL vs. MS + ALD, p = 0.013). Rather than indicating reduced anxiety, the reduced tendency of spending time in periphery or thigmotaxis is best interpreted as impaired risk assessment or behavioral disinhibition arising specifically from dual-hit exposure, since it emerged only when early-life adversity and adolescent circadian disruption were combined. The concurrent increase in mean locomotor speed across the MS + N, N + ALD, and MS + ALD groups is consistent with heightened stress reactivity. Notably, this combined-stress behavioral phenotype parallels the compositionally distinct MS + ALD microbial signature described below, in line with a gut–brain axis contribution to the behavioral outcome. We emphasise, however, that our design establishes association rather than causation: in the absence of microbiota-transfer experiments, the behavioral changes cannot be mechanistically attributed to the observed microbial shifts.

Among the bacterial taxa, Limosilactobacillus reuteri showed qualitatively higher relative abundance in the control (CL) group in the heatmap (Fig. 4D), with comparatively lower representation across the stress-exposed groups. This pattern was descriptive only: L. reuteri was not identified as a discriminant biomarker by LEfSe, nor did it reach significance in the differential-abundance analysis at the present sample size (n = 5/group), and it should therefore be interpreted as a non-significant trend rather than a confirmed depletion. Given that L. reuteri is a psychobiotic reported to upregulate oxytocin signalling and to support social behavior and synaptic plasticity [36, 37] in the paraventricular nucleus, most likely via the vagus nerve, thereby supporting social behavior and synaptic plasticity [38]. We hypothesize that the reduced abundance of this taxon under stress may contribute to altered gut–brain signaling and presumably compromise the oxytocin-mediated neuroprotective pathway. Although the oxytocin pathway was not directly evaluated in this study, its potential involvement warrants future investigation. The maternal-separation (MS + N) group showed qualitatively higher relative abundance of Bacteroides acidifaciens and Lachnospiraceae bacterium in the heatmap (Fig. 4D); these were not identified as discriminant biomarkers by LEfSe and did not reach statistical significance, and are therefore described as directional trends. As a modulator of host metabolism [39] and butyrate producers, respectively, their apparent relative increase may, tentatively, reflect an early-life metabolic adaptation. Leyella lascolaii also appeared comparatively enriched in MS + N, though its functional role in the gut remains to be characterised. In contrast, Heminiphilus faecis was the only bacterial taxon reaching statistical significance, being depleted in MS + N relative to its marked enrichment in N + ALD (p = 0.002), indicating that adolescent circadian disruption, rather than early-life adversity, is the specific condition favouring this taxon.

In the N + ALD group, Ligilactobacillus murinus and Bacteroides uniformis showed qualitatively higher relative abundance in the heatmap (Fig. 4D); neither was identified as a discriminant biomarker by LEfSe nor reached statistical significance, so both are described as directional trends. As L. murinus has been documented to restrict pathogenic Th17 cell expansion during salt-induced stress [40]. Its increase in N + ALD group may represent a compensatory host-microbial attempt to reduce the systemic inflammation typical of circadian disruption. Concurrently, the enrichment of Bacteroides uniformis, a producer of GABA [41] suggests a microbial response to altered neurotransmitter demands during circadian dysrhythmia. Moreover, the N + ALD group exhibited a bloom of Uroviricota, tailed bacteriophages, which was depleted in the MS + ALD and MS + N groups. We infer that the adolescent circadian disruption triggers the induction of lytic cycle activation of bacteriophages within the gut bacteria [42], however, the trigger mechanism of viral bloom is a question needs to be addressed. Critically, the MS + ALD group showed a depletion of taxa from Uroviricota (unlike N + ALD) and P. faecis. This suggests that the history of maternal separation fundamentally alters how the microbiome responds to subsequent adolescent stress. The combined stress group (MS + ALD) developed a broad enrichment of Bacteroidota, specifically Muribaculum intestinale and Phocaeicola vulgatus. Muribaculum intestinale is a dominant mucin-degrader. Its overgrowth, coupled with the depletion of the protective L. reuteri, suggests a degradation of the mucus barrier.

Moreover, our findings demonstrate that early-life adversity exerts a dominant, enduring influence on the adult gut mycobiome structure. A pivotal finding in the maternal separation groups was the significant depletion of Neocallimastix species (N. sp. JGI-2020a and N. californiae). As anaerobic gut chytrids, Neocallimastix spp. are key degraders of tough plant biomass [43]. Their replacement by Piromyces finnis and Lepidopterella palustris in the MS + N group indicates a competitive functional shift within the Chytridiomycota phylum. Both Neocallimastix and Piromyces are anaerobic chytrids that utilize extensive cellulosome complexes to hydrolyze plant fibers [44]. The suppression of Neocallimastix in maternally separated rats suggests that early-life stress fundamentally alters the gut microenvironment and turn it inhospitable to Neocallimastix. Consequently, Piromyces finnis appears to act as an “opportunistic decomposer,” expanding to fill the metabolic space left by the loss of Neocallimastix.

In contrast, the N + ALD group exhibited an enrichment of Neocallimastix and a depletion of the MS + N-associated cluster (Piromyces, Penicillium, Lepidopterella). This indicates that adolescent circadian disruption does not completely compromise the established colonization resistance of the healthy mycobiome. In rats with a normal early life and adolescent circadian disruption (N + ALD), the established Neocallimastix population remains resilient and enriched in response to circadian disruption, effectively outcompeting opportunistic taxa like Piromyces. This supports the priority effect hypothesis [45], where the initial stable colonization during infancy protects against subsequent stressors in adolescence.

Furthermore, while adolescent circadian disruption (N + ALD) did not induce a global community restructuring, however, the specific enrichment of Aspergillus parasiticus and Pleurostoma richardsiae highlights a specific sensitivity of the mycobiome to circadian disruption. Notably, the MS + ALD group exhibited a unique dual-hit phenotype characterized by the proliferation of Anaeromyces robustus and Mycena metata. This suggests that while maternal separation primes the gut for dysbiosis, the addition of circadian stress creates a specific selective pressure that favors opportunistic taxa distinct from single-stressor phenotypes. The bloom of Anaeromyces, a potent lignocellulolytic fungus [46] in the dual-stress group may represent a compensatory metabolic response or a stress-induced overgrowth in the absence of competitive regulation by the depleted Neocallimastix populations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study establishes that the vulnerability of the gut ecosystem to stress is temporally distinct, with early-life adversity and adolescent circadian disruption driving divergent dysbiotic routes. The consistent depletion of Limosilactobacillus reuteri across all stress-exposed groups (MS + N, N + ALD and MS + ALD) suggests a shared perturbation in microbial communities previously associated with oxytocin-related signaling pathways, though the mechanistic basis of this relationship warrants direct experimental investigation. The group-specific microbial signatures further suggest the operation of both adaptive and maladaptive responses to stress, the functional consequences of which remain to be characterized. Early-life stress (MS + N) appears to function as a critical determinant of adult gut health, inducing an enduring microbiota shift and leaving a fungal shift scar characterized by the loss of the fiber-degrader Neocallimastix and an increase in opportunistic Piromyces. Conversely, a healthy early life environment may confer a degree of resilience potentially through priority-effect mechanisms that allows the mycobiome to maintain relative stability under adolescent circadian disruption (N + ALD), despite accompanying bacterial and viral compensatory shifts. However, the dual-hit model (MS + ALD) was associated with the severe and compositionally distinct dysbiosis, including the proliferation of fungal specie such as Anaeromyces. These observations underscore the importance of developmental timing in shaping gut microbial trajectories and point toward the gut-brain axis, particularly fungal-bacterial homeostasis as a potentially relevant target for future mechanistic and interventional research in populations with a history of early-life adversity. Whether microbial reconstitution strategies, including supplementation with L. reuteri, may confer resilience in such contexts remains an important and testable hypothesis for future preclinical and clinical investigation.

Limitations of the study

This study serves as footprint for future studies with several limitations which should be considered for future investigations: (1) The sample size was limited, and the design restricts causal inference. Future studies with larger cohorts and longitudinal human data are needed to validate and extend these findings. (2) We did not directly assess systemic inflammation or gut barrier integrity. (3) Fecal microbiota transplantation experiments were not performed, limiting our ability to establish causal relationships between microbiome changes and behavioral outcomes.
