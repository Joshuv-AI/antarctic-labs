Associations of the insulinemic potential of diet and lifestyle with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) risk in adults with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Danial Fotros, Ali Jafari, Azita Hekmatdoost, Marieh Salavatizadeh, Hossein Poustchi, Mohammad Ebrahim Khamseh, Zahra Yari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is highly prevalent among individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), largely due to shared metabolic risk factors such as insulin resistance and obesity. This study aimed to examine the associations between these indices and the likelihood of MASLD in adults with T2DM. In this cross-sectional study, adults with T2DM were recruited. Dietary intake and lifestyle behaviors were assessed using validated questionnaires, and insulinemic indices were calculated accordingly including the empirical dietary index for hyperinsulinemia (EDIH), the empirical dietary index for insulin resistance (EDIR), the empirical lifestyle index for hyperinsulinemia (ELIH), and the empirical lifestyle index for insulin resistance (ELIR). Lifestyle, and metabolic variables, the odds ratio for MASLD in the highest EDIR quartile was 7.81 (p = 0.001), and for ELIH it was 8 (p < 0.001), and it was 3.21 (p = 0.032) for ELIR. No significant associations were observed for EDIH. Dietary and lifestyle patterns with greater insulinemic potential may contribute to MASLD risk in individuals with T2DM. These findings highlight the importance of insulin-modulating dietary strategies in the prevention and management of liver complications in this high-risk population.Keywords: Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, MASLD, Fatty liver, Diabetes, Insulin resistance, LifestyleSubject terms: Diseases, Endocrinology, Gastroenterology, Risk factors

Introduction

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), recently redefined as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)1–3, is now recognized as the most prevalent chronic liver condition globally affecting approximately 38% of adults4. This condition poses a growing public health concern, particularly among individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2DM), who face markedly higher risk due to shared metabolic disturbances such as insulin resistance, obesity, and dyslipidemia5. Recent studies in the United States suggest that the prevalence of hepatic steatosis and MASLD exceeds 65% among patients with T2DM6,7. Insulin resistance plays a central role in the pathogenesis of MASLD, mediating the interplay between dietary patterns, adiposity, and hepatic lipid metabolism8–10.

To better quantify the insulinogenic impact of habitual behaviors, recent research has introduced empirical dietary and lifestyle indices, including the empirical dietary index for hyperinsulinemia (EDIH), the empirical dietary index for insulin resistance (EDIR), the empirical lifestyle index for hyperinsulinemia (ELIH), and the empirical lifestyle index for insulin resistance (ELIR)11. These indices incorporate dietary components, physical activity, and anthropometric measures to estimate metabolic burden and have demonstrated predictive value for cardiometabolic outcomes in general populations. Several studies have also linked these indices to increased risk of chronic conditions such as gastrointestinal malignancies12,13, prostate cancer14, hypertension, T2DM15, and obesity16. Notably, higher EDIH scores have been associated with unfavorable biomarker profiles marked by glucose-insulin dysregulation, systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and dyslipidemia17. Despite these associations, limited evidence exists regarding the efficacy of these indices in predicting MASLD specifically among individuals with T2DM, a population characterized by pronounced insulin resistance and beta-cell dysfunction. Given these unique metabolic characteristics, it is essential to determine whether these indices can accurately identify individuals at increased risk of hepatic complications.

To address this gap, the present study aimed to examine the associations between EDIH, EDIR, ELIH, and ELIR and the likelihood of MASLD in adults with T2DM. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate these indices in this specific population. By exploring these associations, we aim to provide novel insights into the metabolic drivers of liver disease and support the development of targeted strategies for its prevention and management in high-risk individuals. Importantly, T2DM represents a distinct metabolic context that may fundamentally alter the behavior of these indices. Progressive beta-cell failure reduces postprandial insulin secretion, shifting the metabolic burden toward insulin resistance rather than hyperinsulinemia. In addition, widespread pharmacological treatment modifies circulating glucose, insulin, and lipid levels, further complicating direct comparisons with general-population cohorts in which these indices were originally validated. Whether these indices retain predictive value in this pharmacologically treated, beta-cell-compromised population is therefore a nontrivial question that requires direct empirical investigation.

Methods and materials

Study design

This cross-sectional investigation recruited patients with T2DM from the Diabetes Clinic at the Institute of Diabetes and Metabolism, Iran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran. Participants were recruited using a consecutive sampling method. The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Protocol No: IR.SBMU.NNFTRI.REC. 1404.004). All participants provided written informed consent following detailed explanation of study procedures.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants comprised adults aged 18–70 years with confirmed diagnosis of T2DM for at least two years, body mass index (BMI) ≥ 23 kg/m2, and exclusive use of oral hypoglycemic medications (insulin therapy was an exclusion criterion).

Exclusion criteria included: documented history of malignancy with associated chemotherapy or radiotherapy; substance abuse; chronic inflammatory conditions; cardiovascular complications including heart failure or myocardial infarction; renal diseases; recent significant weight loss or consumption of weight-loss medications; pregnancy or lactation; hepatic disorders including viral hepatitis, autoimmune liver disease, biliary tract disease, or hereditary liver conditions such as Wilson’s disease and hemochromatosis; exposure to hepatotoxic substances or medications including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs; and alcohol consumption meeting or exceeding 21 units weekly for men or 14 units weekly for women.

Sample size determination

Based on previous research18 comparing aspartate transaminase levels in T2DM patients with and without MASLD (14 ± 7 IU/L vs. 11 ± 3 IU/L), and using a 95% confidence interval with 80% power, a minimum of 56 participants per group was required.

Assessment of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease

Hepatic steatosis detection and quantification was performed using transient elastography (FibroScan®, Echosens, Paris, France) equipped with M and XL probes. The controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) values > 270 dB/m were used to define MASLD, in accordance with established diagnostic thresholds19. The NAFLD Liver Fat Score (NAFLD-LFS) was calculated according to the equation proposed by Kotronen et al.20, as follow: −2.89 + (1.18 × metabolic syndrome) + (0.45 × type 2 diabetes) + (0.15 × fasting insulin) + (0.04 × AST) − (0.94 × AST/ALT). Where metabolic syndrome was coded as 1 (yes) or 0 (no), and type 2 diabetes as 2 (yes) or 0 (no). Higher scores indicate a greater probability of hepatic steatosis. The score was calculated to further explore the relationship between insulin-related indices and liver fat accumulation.

Anthropometric and physical activity measurements

Standardized protocols were used for anthropometric measurements. Body composition was assessed via DEXA. Physical activity was evaluated using the IPAQ short form, expressed in MET-minutes/week21.

Laboratory assessments

Fasting blood samples were collected after 10–12 h of overnight fasting. Antidiabetic and other medications were continued as prescribed and were not withheld prior to blood sampling, as interruption would not be clinically safe or ethically appropriate. Fasting blood sugar (FBS), lipid profiles including triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and insulin levels were measured using standard enzymatic and immunoassay methods. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) was calculated using the modified Friedewald equation22. Homeostasis Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) and β-cell function (HOMA-β) were calculated using fasting plasma glucose and fasting insulin concentrations. HOMA-IR was calculated as fasting insulin (µU/mL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)/405. HOMA-β was calculated as [360 × fasting insulin (µU/mL)]/[fasting glucose (mg/dL) − 63].

Dietary intake assessment

Dietary intake was assessed using a validated 147-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) covering the previous 12 months23. Trained registered dietitians, blinded to participants’ MASLD status, conducted face-to-face interviews. Participants reported average intake frequencies for individual food items on daily, weekly, or monthly bases, which were subsequently converted to daily gram equivalents using standard household measures24. Daily energy and nutrient intakes were calculated using Nutritionist 4 software (First Databank Inc., Hearst Corp., San Bruno, CA, USA) with modifications specific to Iranian food composition data.

Calculation of insulinemic indices

Empirical dietary index for hyperinsulinemia (EDIH)

The EDIH was calculated using 15 food groups with established associations with insulin concentrations. Positively weighted components included processed meats, red meat, fish, margarine, poultry, refined starches, sugar-sweetened beverages, tomatoes, low-fat dairy products, and eggs. Negatively weighted components comprised coffee, green leafy vegetables, whole fruits, and high-fat dairy products. Each food group’s daily intake (in serving sizes) was multiplied by its respective regression coefficient, with final scores representing the sum of all weighted components divided by 1000 for interpretability25.

Empirical dietary index for insulin resistance (EDIR)

The EDIR incorporated 13 food groups associated with insulin resistance markers. Positively weighted items included margarine, red meat, refined grains, processed meat, tomatoes, other vegetables, fish, and fruit juice. Negatively weighted components comprised coffee, green leafy vegetables, high-fat dairy products, dark yellow vegetables, and nuts. Calculation methodology paralleled that of EDIH25.

Empirical lifestyle index for hyperinsulinemia (ELIH)

The ELIH combined 11 dietary and lifestyle factors. Positively weighted components included BMI, margarine, butter, red meat, and fruit juice. Negatively weighted factors comprised coffee, whole fruit, physical activity, high-fat dairy products, snacks, and salad dressing25.

Empirical lifestyle index for insulin resistance (ELIR)

The ELIR utilized 14 dietary and lifestyle factors. Positively weighted components included BMI, refined grains, red meat, margarine, tomatoes, other vegetables, potatoes, fruit juice, processed meat, and tea. Negatively weighted factors comprised coffee, green leafy vegetables, high-fat dairy products, and physical activity25.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS v25.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Data distribution normality was evaluated using Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests and histogram visualization. Participants were stratified into quartiles based on insulinemic index scores. Comparisons of baseline characteristics across quartiles were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Chi-square tests were employed for categorical variable comparisons. Associations with MASLD were examined using multivariable logistic regression across three models with progressive adjustments: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 additionally adjusted for energy intake, BMI, smoking status, and physical activity; Model 3 further adjusted for diabetes duration, fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, and total cholesterol. For analyses involving ELIH and ELIR, body mass index and physical activity were not included as covariates in the fully adjusted models because these variables are intrinsic components of the respective indices. Excluding them was intended to avoid potential overadjustment and collinearity.

Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each quartile relative to the reference, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 200 participants with type 2 diabetes were enrolled in the study, of whom 133 were diagnosed with MASLD based on Fibroscan results. The mean age of participants was 52.2 ± 9.2 years, with women representing 54% of participants. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of participants across quartiles of the EDIH. No significant differences were observed in demographic, anthropometric, clinical, LFS and biochemical characteristics of participants between EDIH quartiles, except for age (p = 0.001). Although the highest number of MASLD patients was in the highest quartile of EDIH (30%), it did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.131). CAP score demonstrated an increasing trend from Q1 to Q4 of EDIH (287.4 ± 47.9 to 310.3 ± 43.9 dB/m), approaching statistical significance (p = 0.062). As expected, the empirical dietary and lifestyle indices for insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia increased significantly across quartiles (p < 0.001). Energy intake showed a significant progressive increase from Q1 to Q4 of EDIH (2205.2 ± 743.5 to 2773.1 ± 757.4 kcal/day, p = 0.001). Neither HOMA-IR nor HOMA-β showed significant trends across quartiles of EDIH. Both indices demonstrated non-linear fluctuations across quartiles without a clear dose–response pattern.

Table 2 demonstrates the distribution of characteristics according to EDIR quartiles. MASLD prevalence exhibited a significant increasing trend across EDIR quartiles, rising from 18% in Q1 to 28.5% each in Q3 and Q4 (p = 0.008). This pattern suggests a stronger association between dietary insulin resistance potential and MASLD occurrence compared to hyperinsulinemia potential. Anthropometric parameters showed significant differences across EDIR quartiles. Body weight increased progressively from Q1 to Q4 of EDIR (p < 0.001), accompanied by corresponding increases in BMI (p = 0.030). Waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio also demonstrated significant upward trends (p = 0.010 and p = 0.009, respectively). However, no significant difference was observed in fat mass and fat-free mass. In addition, among the clinical and biochemical variables, only the CAP score (p = 0.026), showed a significant difference between the EDIR quartiles. Although the LFS tended to increase across quartiles of EDIR, the trend was not statistically significant. The dietary and lifestyle insulin potential score showed a significant increase (p < 0.001), except in the case of ELIH. Dietary assessment indicated a significant increase in calorie intake, refined grains, and red meat among EDIR quartiles, but other food groups did not show significant differences. HOMA-IR increased significantly across quartiles of EDIR, in contrast, HOMA-β showed a non-linear pattern across quartiles and did not differ significantly between groups.

Participants in the highest quartile of ELIH had significantly higher body weight, body mass index, fasting blood glucose, triglyceride levels, ALT, LFS and controlled attenuation parameter values compared with those in the lowest quartile (all P < 0.05). Table 4 shows participant characteristics according to quartiles of the empirical lifestyle index for insulin resistance (ELIR). Higher ELIR scores were associated with greater body weight, body mass index, fasting blood glucose, triglyceride concentrations, LFS and controlled attenuation parameter values, while physical activity levels declined significantly across quartiles (all P < 0.05). Moreover, the proportion of participants with MASLD increased progressively across ELIR quartiles. These findings indicate that a lifestyle pattern characterized by greater insulin resistance potential is accompanied by a less favorable metabolic status and a higher prevalence of MASLD. Although both HOMA-IR and HOMA-β tended to increase across quartiles of ELIR, these trends did not reach statistical significance.

Discussion

The current study demonstrated significant positive associations between higher scores of EDIR, ELIH, and ELIR and the likelihood of MASLD among individuals with T2DM. Participants in the highest quartiles of EDIR, ELIH, and ELIR had 7.81-, 8.00-, and 3.21-fold greater odds of MASLD, respectively, after adjustment for potential confounders. In addition, higher ELIH and ELIR scores were accompanied by less favorable metabolic characteristics, including higher body mass index, fasting blood glucose, triglyceride concentrations, and controlled attenuation parameter values, together with lower physical activity levels. These findings suggest that dietary and lifestyle patterns with greater insulinemic and insulin resistance potential may contribute to the development and progression of hepatic steatosis in patients with T2DM. In contrast, no significant association was observed between EDIH and MASLD. To minimize potential overadjustment, variables incorporated into the construction of the empirical lifestyle indices were not included as covariates in the fully adjusted models. This approach may provide a more accurate estimation of the association between ELIH, ELIR, and MASLD.

Our results are broadly consistent with findings from previous studies. In a case-control study by Sohouli et al.26, individuals in the highest tertile of EDIH had an increased risk of NAFLD (OR 2.79; 95% CI 1.32–5.90) in the crude model. Similarly, elevated EDIR scores were associated with higher odds of NAFLD (OR 2.42; 95% CI 1.22–4.79), which further strengthened in the fully adjusted model (OR 3.26; 95% CI 2.24–4.64). However, the association between EDIH and NAFLD was no longer significant after full adjustment. In another case-control study, Momeni et al.27, found a strong link between ELIH and NAFLD (OR 2.70; 95% CI 1.46–5.01), whereas no significant association was observed for EDIH. Supporting this, a cross-sectional study in overweight and obese pediatric populations found that higher ELIH scores were associated with increased odds of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) (OR 3.50; 95% CI 1.49–8.22), particularly among males and pubertal participants28. However, no significant associations were observed between EDIH, EDIR, or ELIR and MAFLD in that cohort. Zhu et al.29 further demonstrated that higher EDIH scores were associated with increased odds of advanced fibrosis (OR 1.74; 95% CI 1.24–2.44) and cirrhosis (OR 2.05; 95% CI 1.21–3.46), although the association with hepatic steatosis was not statistically significant (OR 1.17; 95% CI 0.99–1.39). In contrast, higher EDIR scores were significantly associated with increased odds of both steatosis (OR 1.32; 95% CI 1.11–1.55) and advanced fibrosis (OR 1.43; 95% CI 1.03–1.99). Additionally, Seifi et al.15 examined the association between EDIH, EDIR, and metabolic syndrome and its components in an Iranian adult population. Although no significant association was found between higher quartiles of EDIH and EDIR and overall metabolic syndrome risk, EDIR was significantly associated with increased odds of hypertension and diabetes (OR 1.22; 95% CI 1.04–1.44 and OR 1.22; 95% CI 1.01–1.47, respectively). These findings suggest that although EDIH and EDIR may not predict metabolic syndrome as a composite outcome, they remain relevant in identifying specific metabolic disturbances. It is important to note that all of the above studies were conducted in general populations, whereas the present study specifically focuses on individuals with type 2 diabetes, a group with a distinct metabolic profile.

An additional analysis using the NAFLD Liver Fat Score provided further insight into the performance of the empirical insulinemic indices. Unlike EDIH and EDIR, which showed non-significant and non-linear patterns, both ELIH and ELIR demonstrated significant positive associations with the liver fat score. Since the NAFLD Liver Fat Score incorporates fasting insulin as one of its components, these findings suggest that lifestyle-based indices may better capture the metabolic disturbances associated with hepatic fat accumulation in individuals with type 2 diabetes. The stronger performance of ELIH and ELIR may reflect their ability to integrate multiple determinants of insulin resistance, including adiposity and physical activity, which are key contributors to MASLD pathogenesis. These observations support the potential utility of lifestyle-oriented empirical indices as practical tools for identifying individuals at elevated risk of hepatic steatosis30,31.

The strong association between higher EDIR, ELIH, and ELIR scores and the risk of MASLD in individuals with type 2 diabetes reflects the convergence of multiple metabolic disturbances that promote hepatic fat accumulation. In states of insulin resistance, which are often exacerbated by diets high in refined carbohydrates and saturated fats as indicated by elevated EDIR scores32,33, lipolysis in adipose tissue becomes upregulated34. This results in elevated circulating free fatty acids, which are transported to the liver and contribute to triglyceride synthesis35. Simultaneously, chronic hyperinsulinemia activates sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c)36, enhancing de novo lipogenesis. Insulin resistance also suppresses the activity of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), further reducing fatty acid oxidation37. In addition, impairments in very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) assembly and secretion hinder the clearance of triglycerides from hepatocytes38–40, leading to intracellular lipid accumulation. These combined mechanisms ultimately result in hepatic steatosis, the defining histological feature of MASLD.

An additional finding of the present study relates to the behavior of HOMA-derived indices across the empirical insulinemic scores. HOMA-IR generally increased across higher quartiles of EDIR, ELIH, and ELIR, with statistically significant trends observed for EDIR and ELIH, supporting the concept that these indices successfully capture dietary and lifestyle patterns associated with insulin resistance. In contrast, the pattern observed for HOMA-β was less consistent. While HOMA-β increased significantly across quartiles of ELIH, suggesting compensatory β-cell activity in response to greater insulin demand, no clear trend was observed for EDIH, EDIR, or ELIR. These findings may reflect the heterogeneous metabolic state of individuals with type 2 diabetes, in whom compensatory hyperinsulinemia and progressive β-cell dysfunction may coexist depending on disease duration and severity. Therefore, although higher insulinemic index scores were generally associated with greater insulin resistance, their relationship with β-cell function appears to be more complex and warrants further investigation in prospective studies41,42.

The ELIR and ELIH indices, which include lifestyle factors such as BMI and physical activity, highlight the role of excess adiposity and sedentary behavior in exacerbating these metabolic disruptions. Hypertrophy of adipocytes and ectopic fat accumulation interfere with insulin signaling by creating imbalances in adipokine secretion and promoting the buildup of lipotoxic intermediates, including ceramides and diacylglycerols43,44. These intermediates activate protein kinase C (PKC) and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) pathways, both of which further impair insulin sensitivity45–50. In the liver, the excess lipid burden contributes to mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum stress, triggering the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the activation of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), which drives the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines51,52. This chronic inflammatory state reinforces insulin resistance in a self-perpetuating cycle, creating the conditions for disease progression beyond simple steatosis53. In contrast, EDIH, which reflects the insulinogenic potential of the diet, was not significantly associated with MASLD in this study. One possible explanation is that many individuals with type 2 diabetes experience pancreatic beta cell exhaustion, which impairs postprandial insulin secretion. In such cases, hepatic insulin resistance, rather than excessive insulin production, becomes the primary contributor to fat accumulation in the liver. As a result, indices focused on insulin resistance, such as EDIR and ELIR, may serve as effective tools for identifying individuals at elevated risk of MASLD in this population.

This study provides important insights into the association between empirical dietary and lifestyle indices including EDIR, ELIH, and ELIR and the risk of MASLD in individuals with T2DM. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first to examine these indices in this specific population, which adds novelty and relevance to the findings. A key strength of the study lies in its focus on individuals with T2DM, a group particularly susceptible to MASLD, thereby enhancing the clinical significance of the findings. The inclusion of multiple validated indices and comprehensive adjustment for confounder are other strengths of this study. Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design restricts the ability to infer causality. Additionally, the use of a FFQ introduces the possibility of recall bias. Moreover, reliance on nonlocal food composition databases, such as those from the USDA, may not accurately capture the nutrient profile of traditional Iranian foods, potentially affecting the precision of dietary exposure estimates.

Conclusion

This study highlights a significant association between higher scores on empirical dietary and lifestyle indices, namely EDIR, ELIH, and ELIR, and an increased risk of MASLD in patients with T2DM. These findings emphasize the relevance of insulin resistance–related dietary and lifestyle patterns in evaluating liver health within this high-risk population. The absence of a significant association between EDIH and MASLD may reflect the distinct metabolic characteristics of individuals with more advanced diabetes, in whom insulin resistance, rather than hyperinsulinemia, predominates as the primary correlate of hepatic fat accumulation.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first analysis to evaluate these indices in diabetic patients. Our study provides unique insights into the metabolic drivers of MASLD and underlines the potential value of these indices to identify persons at higher risk of MASLD in clinical practice. Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional design of this study, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Future longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these relationships, to establish directionality and to evaluate if modification of these dietary and lifestyle patterns can help to minimize the burden of MASLD and assist in individualized prevention and management strategies in persons with T2DM.
