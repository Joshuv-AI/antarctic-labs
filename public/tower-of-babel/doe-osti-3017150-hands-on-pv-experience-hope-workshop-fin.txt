 Hands On PV Workshop Experience (HOPE)
 Executive Summary, FY21-FY24
 Major Goals & Objectives
 The Hands-On Photovoltaics Experience, or HOPE, is a one-week school held at NLR each
 summer to educate graduate student photovoltaic researchers in the fundamentals of
 photovoltaics (PV) as well as speciﬁc cell technologies and measurement &
 characterization techniques. The program brings students from universities across the U.S.
 and their faculty advisors to spend a week in an in-depth, intensive program, including
 hands-on lab experiences in solar cell fabrication and testing. This program is intended to
 educate the students and increase collaboration among the students, their advising faculty,
 and staff at NLR. Our survey results consistently indicate high satisfaction with this program
 and good student outcomes post-HOPE. HOPE is a selective program with a competitive
 application process and is limited to 12-20 students each year. Keeping the program small
 enables a high level of interaction among the students and individualized attention from the
 faculty and staff.

 Objective
 The objective of HOPE is to train graduate students in PV R&D methods, preparing them for
 jobs in the PV sector. The students also disseminate their training within their research
 groups and universities, increasing the quality of U.S. PV research. Some of HOPE’s content
 is also shared publicly through our website.

 Task 1 - Hold HOPE Each Year

 This task focuses on enhancing the HOPE workshop by expanding its content, reach, and
 evaluation criteria. Key goals include incorporating systems-level PV analytics, increasing
 the applicant pool, growing workshop participation, and reﬁning feedback mechanisms.
 These efforts aim to adapt the program to industry trends, prepare participants for PV
 careers, and improve workshop impact and inclusivity.

 Project Description
 Annual HOPE workshop timeline and format

 The Hands-On Photovoltaics Experience (HOPE) workshop is held annually at NLR in
 the month of July, bringing together graduate students and their faculty advisors from
 across the U.S. Each year, the program receives over 40 applications for 18 available
 slots (2024 values), with applications typically open between December and mid-
 February.

 Figure 1 – Timeline used since 2023. The exact week of HOPE varies, but applications open on December 1 and close in mid-
 February. Students are informed of their selection by early April, and HOPE is typically held in the second week of July, avoiding
 the 4th of July long weekend.

 Figure 2 - Rate of applications versus time in the 2023 application cycle. The original deadline was extended by one week, with
 30% of applications received during this extension and several additional successful applicants.

 Applications are evaluated through a rigorous panel review process involving
 representatives from NLR, DOE, and former HOPE students now employed at NLR. A
 formal rubric was established in 2022 for the evaluation, to provide consistency in review
 as new panel members are added. Selection criteria include the relevance of the
 applicants’ research to the workshop content, their academic progress, whether their
 research group has participated in HOPE previously (with preference given to “new”
 laboratories, as one of HOPE's objectives is to promote the dissemination of knowledge
 gained by the student to their laboratory and peers), and the commitment of the faculty
 advisor to contribute to the program. The program actively works to cultivate a cohort that
 reflects a wide range of academic backgrounds and perspectives.

 Pre-Workshop Planning

 Once the student cohort is selected, the workshop’s content is tailored to their interests.
 Participants, including faculty, are actively engaged in the planning process, with faculty
 often contributing specific activities to the workshop. The program ensures a balance
 between fundamental photovoltaic (PV) content and specialized sessions addressing
 cutting-edge technologies.

 Workshop Logistics

 The workshop takes place over a week each summer at NLR, including students and
 some of the faculty as well as over 70 NLR staff in the program. Students stay at the
 nearby Colorado School of Mines dormitories. This shared accommodation fosters group
 cohesion and facilitates collaborative projects during the week. Meals, coffee, and snacks
 are provided to encourage informal interactions among participants and NLR staff, further
 enhancing networking opportunities.

 Core Workshop Activities

 HOPE includes a mix of foundational content and hands-on experiences. Core topics
 covered every year include:

 • Overviews of leading PV materials and technologies.
 • Measurement techniques such as current-voltage and quantum efficiency for solar
 cells.
 • Certification processes, module construction, and outdoor testing methodologies.

 Each year, the workshop includes in-depth PV device fabrication sessions, where
 students work in small groups focused on specific technology types –Silicon, III-V, CdTe,
 Perovskites, and PV Systems. In-lab characterization sessions are similarly customized
 to student interests, with techniques such as X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, module
 photoluminescence/electroluminesence, and deep level transient spectroscopy. These
 sessions are led by more than 70 NLR staff scientists who eagerly share their expertise
 and often initiate lasting collaborations with participants.

 Additional recurring activities include:

 • Poster Session: Students present their research to NLR staff and faculty,
 fostering discussions and potential collaborations.
 • Career Panel: Featuring PV professionals with diverse backgrounds, including
 NLR staff and alumni, with a focus on industry expertise.
 • Individual Staff Meetings: these individualized sessions fostered long-term
 professional relationships among 1-2 participants and NLR staff.
 • Interactive Activities: Engaging sessions such as PV trivia or quiz shows that
 promote learning in a fun, relaxed setting.
 • Professors Activities: The topics and formats vary each year, including pre-
 workshop lectures, collaborative sessions between professors and staff, panels,
 or professor-led sessions and activities.

 Post-Workshop Impact

 At the conclusion of each workshop, students and faculty are encouraged to share their
 learnings with their home institutions. To support this, the final day of the program includes
 a summary of key takeaways. Real-time surveys provide valuable feedback for
 continuous improvement. While quantitative metrics have been consistently tracked
 across iterations of the project, open-ended qualitative questions about program impact
 and areas for improvement have been incorporated since 2020.

 Project Updates on FY22-FY24 cycle
 Application Process Improvements

 In this cycle, improvements in our application process have focused on enhancing
 fairness, transparency, and accessibility. The development of an evaluation rubric
 standardized the selection process, while website updates clarified program expectations
 for new applicants. Proactive measures were taken to address international student site
 access challenges, including initiating paperwork early. Additionally, as part of our
 inclusivity goals, we have strengthened processes to ensure equitable opportunities for
 international and underrepresented students.

 reached, reflecting the program's growth and evolution. The HOPE program has
 demonstrated consistent growth and evolution throughout its 2022-2024 cycle, embracing
 innovations that enriched the educational experience and broadened its reach. Initially
 constrained by the challenges of the COVID-19 pandemic in the previous cycle, the
 workshops adapted to virtual formats before transitioning back to in-person sessions by
 2022. This return allowed for a richer hands-on experience, particularly in the laboratory
 sessions where students explored solar cell fabrication and measurement techniques.

 Content Development

 The HOPE program embraced several innovations that enriched the educational
 experience for participants. The implementation of a flipped classroom approach was
 particularly impactful, allowing students to engage with foundational materials before the
 workshop began. In this model, traditional lecture content was delivered outside of class,
 freeing up session time for interactive, student-driven learning. This shift enabled deeper
 discussions and advanced learning during the sessions. Additionally, the curriculum
 expanded to include emerging topics such as PV lifecycle, recycling, and advanced
 measurement and characterization techniques.

 In 2024, the program introduced a system-level track, specifically designed for students
 interested in PV applications at the level of deployment, beyond material science. This
 track balanced introductory laboratory sessions with hands-on experiences in field
 performance and reliability, offering a more holistic view of photovoltaic systems. These
 enhancements were complemented by improvements to the application process,
 including the use of evaluation rubrics and targeted outreach efforts to encourage diverse
 participation. Virtual seminars and hybrid formats also allowed for greater flexibility and
 expanded faculty involvement, leveraging the lessons learned during the pandemic.

 Feedback and Feedback-Driven Changes

 Testimonials from participants highlighted the program’s transformative effects. Many
 students credited HOPE with inspiring their research directions and securing positions in
 academia or industry. Follow-up success stories included alumni collaborations with NLR
 staff and peers, as well as joint publications and conference presentations that stemmed
 from connections made during the workshop.

 Feedback from students and faculty has been instrumental in shaping the HOPE
 workshop. For example:

                                                          Page 19 of 29

 • Session Pacing: Early feedback indicated that some sessions felt rushed, leaving
 little time for discussion. In response, the pacing was adjusted in 2022 to allow for
 more in-depth Q&A and collaborative activities, particularly during the hands-on
 fabrication and characterization sessions.
 • Break Schedules: Participants expressed the need for longer or more frequent
 breaks, especially during intensive laboratory sessions. Since 2022, structured
 breaks have been added to the schedule to provide participants with time to
 recharge and network informally.
 • Interactive Activities: To address feedback about passive learning, interactive
 components were introduced, such as the PV Jeopardy game, which combines
 fun with learning, and the Entrepreneurship Project in 2022 where students
 worked in teams to pitch innovative PV solutions.
 • Faculty feedback informed the inclusion of advanced system-level topics and
 career-focused panels.

 Challenges and Solutions

 The HOPE program faced several challenges during its 2022–2024 cycle, particularly in
 managing administrative hurdles and logistical constraints. One recurring issue was the
 difficulty of securing site access for international students. To address this, the program
 implemented a proactive approach, initiating the necessary paperwork early during the
 application cycle and setting clear deadlines to minimize uncertainty for participants.

 Budget constraints also posed challenges, as rising costs necessitated careful resource
 allocation. By optimizing staff involvement and leveraging hybrid content and flipped-
 classroom delivery, the program maintained high-quality experiences without exceeding
 budgetary limits.

 To ensure the program's continuity and smooth operation despite changes in leadership,
 support, and research staff, a comprehensive manual has been created. This document
 includes key contacts, deadlines, and operational insights accumulated over the past
 three years. Additionally, in 2024, long-term staff contributors were explicitly given
 opportunities to include earlier-career staff to prepare for future years.

 Conclusions
 The HOPE program from 2022 to 2024 has significantly advanced its mission to educate
 and inspire the next generation of PV researchers. By fostering collaboration, introducing
 innovative content, and addressing logistical challenges, HOPE has set a benchmark for
 hands-on educational programs in renewable energy. As the program continues to
 evolve, its emphasis on education, technological advancement, and professional
 development will ensure its lasting impact on the field of photovoltaics.

 The HOPE program is poised to continue its impactful educational and workforce

 addressing emerging needs. Core practices like scalability, ensuring small cohorts for
 personalized attention while balancing growth, feedback integration through regular
 surveys to enhance content and logistics, and inclusivity, will remain central.

 Over the next three years, the HOPE workshop will align its mission and content to
 support U.S. research, deployment and manufacturing leadership and workforce needs.
 Key updates will include refining the agenda, incorporating feedback from industry
 leaders, and introducing a new presentation template to highlight connections between
 workshop content and U.S. energy resiliency, manufacturing and deployment goals. This
 new template will provide a flexible framework for staff to integrate these connections into
 their presentations, with guiding questions to prompt discussions on how the content
 aligns with these critical objectives. The program will expand its reach by curating and
 publishing updated educational videos on the HOPE website, complete with lesson plans
 for broader use in academia and industry training. Additionally, HOPE will aim to enhance
 industry engagement by organizing local industry tours or inviting professionals to interact
 with participants, providing insights into career paths and practical feedback on workshop
 content. These efforts will ensure HOPE remains a relevant and transformative program
 for students, educators, and the broader photovoltaic community, while preserving its
 core practices of scalability, personalized learning, feedback-driven improvement, and
 accessibility.

 Project Impact
 The audience is primarily U.S. Ph.D. students working in PV, who participate in the
 workshop, as well as their faculty advisors, research groups, universities, and future
 employers. HOPE’s outcome is a better trained and qualified PV workforce, higher quality
 PV research, and PV knowledge dissemination.

 Measurement and Characterization Sessions

 You will have the opportunity to attend three Measurement & Characterization (M&C)
 sessions in a small-group setting (4-6 students) during the week. Please fill in the survey
 which are your top choices, and we will try to accommodate them. Below is a short
 description provided by the staff facilitating each session of the topics covered and
 session format to help your selection process.

 Scanning Probe Microscopy for nm-Scale Electrical Characterizations
 Lead: Chun-Sheng Jiang Chun.Sheng.Jiang@NLR.gov
 Atomic force microscopy (AFM)-based electrical techniques, namely Kelvin probe force
 microscopy (KPFM), scanning spreading resistance microscopy (SSRM), and scanning
 capacitance microscopy (SCM) have shown a wide range of nm-scale electrical
 characterizations for photovoltaic materials and devices. We will discuss the
 fundamentals of these techniques and show characterizations cases of junction location
 and quality determination, doping defects, material defects, and front contact degradation
 at utility-scale solar farms. The characterization applications include C-Si, CdTe, CIGS,
 and perovskite solar cells.

 PL, EL & DLIT imaging
 Leads: Darius Kuciauskas Darius.Kuciauskas@NLR.gov,
 Dana Kern Dana.Kern@NLR.gov
 Radiative emission spectra (PL, EL, CL) and carrier lifetimes provide information about
 recombination and voltage entitlement in solar cells. This information is useful when
 seeking to increase efficiency by identifying dominant loss pathways (band tails, grain
 boundaries, contact layers, etc.). During the first part of this session, we will describe PL
 and time-resolved PL characterization, using examples from CdTe and perovskite solar
 cells.
 On the second part, we will focus on Luminescence and thermal imaging techniques,
 which are often used in quality assessment and degradation analysis of photovoltaic
 materials, cells, and modules. We will compare spatial patterns of photoluminescence,
 electroluminescence, UV-fluorescence, and dark lock-in thermography to understand the
 spatial nature of PV device operation and find root causes of degradation. This session
 will demonstrate imaging systems applied to both commercial technologies (Silicon PV
 modules) as well as emerging materials (perovskite cells or mini-modules).

 X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and ultraviolet photoelectron
 spectroscopy (UPS)
 Leads: Perkins, Craig, Craig.Perkins@NLR.gov
 In this session, you will learn about the surface sensitive techniques X-ray photoelectron
 spectroscopy (XPS) and ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS). XPS and UPS
 can be used to learn about surface composition (stoichiometry, oxidation states,
 contaminants, dopants) and energetics (workfunction, ionization potential, band bending).
 You will learn the basics of the instrumentation, mounting/loading samples, acquiring
 data, energy calibration, data analysis, and unique NLR capabilities. In addition, you will
 learn about the complimentary techniques inverse photoelectron spectroscopy and Auger
 spectroscopy along with pros/cons of these surface sensitive spectroscopic tools.

 UV-Vis/Ellipsometry
 Lead: Paul Ndione, Paul.Ndione@NLR.gov
 Spectroscopic ellipsometry (SE) is a non-contact, non-invasive, and non-destructive
 optical technique that measures the change in the polarization state of light upon
 reflection from a sample (bulk or thin film). SE is primarily used to determine optical
 constants (refractive index and extinction coefficient) and thickness. During this session,
 an overview of SE will be provided, focusing on the principles behind polarization changes
 and their relationship to material properties. Insights into SE instrumentation and data
 analysis strategies to address complexities involved in interpreting SE data using model-
 based approaches will also be provided. The session will conclude with real-world
 applications and a forward-looking discussion on the prospects of SE, including emerging
 technologies and their implications for characterizations using SE.

 PV Packaging Failures (Hi-Pot) (new!)
 Lead: Jimmy Newkirk, Rachael Arnold
 Good packaging is difficult to achieve with PV devices and
 hi-pot testing, also known as wet leakage current testing, is
 a basic test that laboratories can do to determine the quality
 of electrical insulation. This is done by placing packaged
 modules & module components in water to identify current
 leakage. In this session we will review how to test various
 sample types and failure types while identifying safety
 concerns involved, and then we will show examples of how
 this is run. Please feel free to bring some of your own
 samples to test while keeping in mind that this may ruin your
 device if your samples do not have good packaging. This
 could help determine what you need to do differently to
 package your devices.

 SEM/EBIC/CL
 Lead: Harvey Guthrey, Harvey.Guthrey@NLR.gov
 This tutorial will cover the basic operating principals of the scanning electron microscope
 (SEM) including image formation and the different detection modes commonly used in
 material science work. Participants will then be introduced to two different analytical
 techniques that provide information about the optoelectronic properties of photovoltaic
 (PV) absorber materials, namely cathodoluminescence (CL) and electron beam induced
 current (EBIC). These are powerful techniques that reveal the radiative and non-radiative
 recombination behavior in PV materials that are intimately related to device performance.
 The theory of these measurements will be discussed along with practical considerations
 such as sample requirements, instrumentation setup, and data acquisition concerns for
 different absorber materials. The tutorial session will be composed of a presentation and
 discussion session followed by demonstration of CL spectrum imaging and EBIC charge
 collection mapping.

 DLTS, DLCP, CV & Admittance
 Lead: Steve Johnson, Steve.Johnson@NLR.gov

 DLTS was pioneered by David Lang of Bell Laboratories in 1974 as a tool for studying
 electrically active defects in semiconductors. This techniques measures defect
 parameters, such as activation energy and capture cross section, giving a defect
 fingerprint. It also measures trap concentration and type (electron or hole). It has high
 sensitivity, with a detection limit based on doping, but requires the existence of a junction.
 In this session, we will go over the basics of this technique including the capacitance-
 voltage relationship, doping profiles, depletion width. We will also go over identifying the
 various defect state parameters with DLTS, identifying it in various samples. Admittance
 spectroscopy and Thermal Admittance Spectrosophy (TAS), as well as Drive Level
 Capacitance Profiling (DLCP) will also be briefly covered through the session.

 SIMS Characterization
 Leads: Steve Harvey Steve.Harvey@NLR.gov, Matt Young Matthew.Young@NLR.gov
 Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry (SIMS) is a powerful analytical technique for
 determining elemental and isotopic distributions in solids, as well as the structure and
 composition of organic materials. In this session students will be introduced to two types
 of SIMS instruments and shown a few examples of the types of data that be collected and
 some basics of SIMS data processing will be covered. Dynamic SIMS will be covered and
 an overview of NLR’s Cameca 7F dynamic SIMS instrument will be given. TOF-SIMS will
 be discussed and an overview of an ION-TOF TOF-SIMS V Time of Flight SIMS (TOF-
 SIMS) spectrometer will be given. Strengths and weaknesses between the two
 instruments will be discussed.
