Effect of database, pre-processing pipeline, and scan duration on rs-fMRI functional connectivity based treatment targets for TMS obtained using the Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy (SNT) protocol
Authors: Lian Yu, Xue Li, Neil Roberts, Xiaoqi Huang, Haoyang Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscranial Magnetic Stimulation targeting the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) is a therapeutic approach for treating depressive disorder. However, traditional anatomical methods often fail to localize a treatment target. The functional connectivity method represented by the Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy (SNT) has been proposed and demonstrated efficacy. A step of SNT is to achieve DLPFC target localization using resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) data, based on measurement of the negative correlation coefficient between the functional connectivity of DLPFC and the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC).MethodsThis study has three goals. Firstly, to apply the SNT protocol to investigate variability in target localization in four groups (Human Connectome Project [HCP], China Human Connectome Project [CHCP], major depressive disorder [MDD] from Huaxi Depression Imaging Cohort Study [HX-DICS], and healthy controls [HC] from HX-DICS) from three databases. Secondly, to investigate potential differences in how the DPABI and FSL rs-fMRI pre-processing pipelines, particularly the spatial smoothing, denoising, and temporal filtering steps, affect the localization of individualized DLPFC targets. Thirdly, to assess the effect of rs-fMRI scan duration (ranging from 6 to 30 min) on target localization.ResultsSignificant inter-subject variability in DLPFC coordinates was found, emphasizing the necessity of individualized targeting. DPABI and FSL pre-processing produced significant differences in target coordinates, which exceeded 1 cm, with mean Euclidean distances ranging from 1.45 to 3.82 cm across cohorts, and individual maximum deviations reaching 6.14 cm. FSL pipeline consistently produced higher negative correlation coefficients than DPABI. Scan duration analysis indicated that after 12 min, the stability of individual target positions no longer improved significantly with prolonged scanning time, suggesting that approximately 12 min may provide sufficiently reliable data for target computation.ConclusionTaken together, findings demonstrate that advancing precision neuromodulation therapy requires optimized data acquisition, standardized workflows, software-specific validation, and optimum scan duration.Keywords: major depressive disorder (MDD), pre-processing pipeline, personalized treatment target, scan duration, Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy (SNT), transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)

Introduction

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a non–invasive technique that modulates cortical excitability by inducing localized electric currents via electromagnetic induction. It is used to treat mental health and certain neurological conditions (Deng et al., 2013). The efficacy of the technique has been demonstrated with minimal cognitive side effects and application of TMS for treatment of major depressive disorder (MDD) was approved by the FDA in 2008 (Höflich et al., 1993; Conca et al., 2002; Hansen et al., 2011). Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) is the primary target for treating MDD and traditional methods for locating DLPFC include the standardized “5 cm rule,” and variants, which involve finding the motor hotspot (where TMS causes a thumb twitch) and moving the coil the prescribed distance forward anteriorly in the midline (Pascual-Leone and Hallett 1994); Herwig et al., 2001; Notzon et al., 2018) or locating the F3 electrode in an electroencephalography (EEG) 10–20 system (Beam et al., 2009; Trapp et al., 2020). These methods can be enhanced by neuronavigation systems, which co–register 3D MRI with head surface scans to improve spatial precision (Young et al., 2022; Caulfield et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023). However, these approaches ignore individual variations in brain function. Moreover, functional heterogeneity across distinct subregions modulates brain circuits responsible for different depressive symptoms (Siddiqi et al., 2020). Relying solely on structural MRI guidance therefore lacks precision for optimal MDD targeting. This inter-individual variability aligns with recent transdiagnostic findings: Chen et al. (2025) demonstrated that a general disease factor (d factor), capturing shared liability across mental and physical disorders, is associated with extreme individual deviations in brain structure, further supporting the need for personalized targeting.

A potential way to increase TMS target specificity is to replace anatomical targeting with functional MRI (fMRI)–based targeting (Taïbet et al., 2018). One approach is to use task-based fMRI to activate the brain subregions of interest. Although promising, this technique faces challenges related to patient compliance and paradigm variability, which hinder standardization (Fan et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2021). More promising has been to analyze functional connectivity of resting state fMRI (rs-fMRI) as exemplified in the Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy (SNT) protocol in which a personalized target in left DLPFC is identified as having maximal negative connectivity with subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC). SNT has been shown to produce high remission rates in TMS treatment of MDD using intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) (Cole et al., 2020, 2022).

Nevertheless, some studies have been performed that show no statistically significant advantages of personalized precision therapies based on functional connectivity over conventional methods. For example, Morriss et al. found no significant difference in symptom improvement (GRID-HDRS-17 scores) between SNT-based iTBS and standard MRI-guided rTMS for treatment-resistant depression over 26 weeks (Morriss et al., 2024), and Eshel et al. reported comparable clinical response rates between rTMS targeting connectivity between DLPFC and the Frontoparietal Control Network and sham stimulation (Eshel, Keller and Wu 2020). The mixed findings could in part be due to differences in the pre-processing pipeline used in different studies. For example, a study in which 70 independent teams analyzed the same dataset found that in comparison to SPM, FSL had 6.69 times higher odds of producing significant findings (Botvinik-Nezer et al., 2020). Similarly, Popescu et al. showed FSL, FreeSurfer and SPM produced divergent gray matter atrophy measurements, while Smith et al. showed that pre-processing differences across software (e.g. registration, filtering) significantly altered findings in fMRI studies (Popescu et al., 2016). Accordingly, the present study pursued three specific objectives to investigate which factors may affect the accuracy of functional neuroimaging guided target localization.

Firstly, we investigated cross–database consistency in the spatial variability of DLPFC–sgACC connectivity–based treatment targets. We studied the HCP and CHCP databases together with the Huaxi Depression Imaging Cohort Study (HX-DICS) database comprising MDD patients and matched healthy controls (HC). Standardized pre-processing was applied using DPABI in MNI152 space. Of particular interest was to compare variability between Chinese and Western populations, as well as in a clinical MDD cohort.

Secondly, the effect of performing pre-processing using the DPABI pipeline with pre-processing performed using the FSL pipeline was compared, with a particular focus on respective strategies for (i) denoising, (ii) spatial smoothing, and (iii) frequency filtering. To minimize registration-related confounds, the analyses were performed in native T1 space using template-free pre-processing.

Thirdly, five consecutive rs-fMRI scans each lasting 6 min were acquired for each participant in a new cohort of 25 HCs. This allowed the effect of acquiring rs-fMRI data for 6, 12, 18, 24, or 30 min on the spatial variability of DLPFC-sgACC connectivity based treatment targets to be investigated.

Materials and methods

Databases and dataset

Three different databases were studied. Firstly, a cohort of 100 healthy subjects aged between 22 and 35 years was selected from the publicly accessible Human Connectome Project (HCP) at https://humanconnectome.org. Secondly, a cohort of 69 healthy subjects aged between 18 and 44 years was selected from the China Human Connectome Project (CHCP) accessible via the Scientific Data Bank (DOI: 10.11922/sciencedb.01374). Thirdly, a new database constructed locally and named HX-DICS comprises 70 patients diagnosed with MDD and aged between 28 and 68 years and 70 age- and sex-matched HC aged between 20 and 72 years.

In addition to the three main databases, rs-fMRI and 3D T1-weighted images were also acquired locally in Chengdu for 25 HCs using a uMR 790 3T MRI system (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China) in order to study the effect of scan duration on target location.

Approval to construct the HX-DICS database and to acquire data for the additional 25 HCs was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, and all procedures strictly complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional ethical standards, with written informed consent obtained from every participant prior to data acquisition.

MRI data acquisition

Table 1 summarizes the acquisition parameters for rs-fMRI and 3D T1-weighted images for the HCP, CHCP, and HX-DICS databases. Detailed scanning protocols for HCP and CHCP have been described previously (Ge et al., 2023). Imaging data for both the HCP and CHCP databases were acquired using almost identical parameters on Skyra 3T MRI systems (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) whereas data for the HX-DICS database were acquired using 1.5T MRI system (General Electric Medical Systems, Milwaukee, USA). Notably, the HX-DICS database has a wider age range, raising methodological considerations regarding the inclusion of age as a covariate in subsequent analyses. To assess data quality of the GE 1.5T rs-fMRI data, we computed the temporal signal-to-noise ratio (tSNR) for each participant. tSNR was defined as the ratio of the mean signal intensity to the standard deviation of the residual time series after linear detrending. Mean whole–brain tSNR values were comparable between MDD patients and HCs, and both groups exceeded the commonly accepted threshold of 30, indicating sufficient data quality for functional connectivity analyses. No participant was excluded due to inadequate tSNR. A tSNR of 30 is commonly adopted in the field as it represents a balance between data quality and scan efficiency, ensuring that thermal noise does not dominate the BOLD signal and that subsequent connectivity analyses are adequately powered (He et al., 2023).

For the scan duration study, rs-fMRI and 3D T1-weighted images were acquired from 25 HCs using a uMR 790 3T MRI system (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China). The rs-fMRI data were collected with an EPI-BOLD sequence (TR/TE = 300/30 ms, voxel size = 3.0 × 3.0 × 3.0 mm³), and structural T1-weighted images were acquired with TR/TE = 7.6/3 ms and an isotropic resolution of 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm³. Of particular relevance to the third study objective of the present study is that five consecutive resting–state scans of 6 min each were acquired (total 30 min).

Pre-processing of rs-fMRI data

The rs-fMRI data underwent standardized pre-processing through six sequential stages. Firstly, the initial 6 volumes were discarded to allow magnetic field stabilization. Secondly, slice timing correction (using cubic spline interpolation) and rigid-body motion correction (six-parameter affine transformation) were applied; participants with head translation exceeding 3.0 mm or rotation exceeding 3.0° were excluded. Thirdly, a boundary-based algorithm was used to co-register the rs-fMRI data with the 3D T1-weighted image. Fourthly, denoising was performed using different strategies for the two different pipelines. In particular, in the DPABI pipeline white matter (WM) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) masks were generated via segmentation in FSL, principal components were extracted from the WM and CSF signals, and the effects of nuisance variables were removed by regression without global signal removal. In contrast, the FSL pipeline performed regression directly on the mean WM and CSF signals. Fifthly, spatial smoothing was applied using a Gaussian kernel with full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 6 mm. Sixthly, temporal filtering was applied using an ideal 0.01–0.1 Hz bandpass filter for DPABI and a Gaussian–weighted filter for FSL.

Comparison of SNT target location in different databases using the standardized MNI152 template

The preprocessed rs-fMRI data from all three databases were further normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute 152 (MNI152) symmetric template using DPABI v6.1’s DARTEL algorithm. Subsequently, the personalized left DLPFC target was identified using the standard SNT approach (see the “Comparison of the effect of DPABI and FSL pre-processing on individualized SNT target location” section for a detailed description of the SNT computational framework). For each of the four groups (HCP, CHCP, MDD patients, and healthy controls from HX-DICS), the coefficient of variation (CV) of the target coordinates was calculated to assess spatial variability.

Comparison of the effect of DPABI and FSL pre-processing on individualized SNT target location

The original SNT protocol (Cole et al., 2020, 2022) defines the optimal TMS target as the left DLPFC (BA9/46) voxel showing the maximal negative Pearson correlation with the sgACC (BA25). In that implementation, rs–fMRI data were preprocessed using SPM12, including motion correction, reslicing, T1–functional coregistration, and inverse normalization of MNI–space ROIs to individual native space (Cole et al., 2020, Supplementary Methods). The individualized target was then identified via hierarchical clustering and correlation analyses within the subject–specific ROIs.

In the present study, we deliberately did not replicate the original SNT pre-processing exactly. Instead, we compared two widely used software packages—DPABI v6.1 and FSL 6.0.3—under their default parameter settings, which differ from the original SNT workflow in several aspects. For example, DPABI defaults to an ideal bandpass filter (0.01–0.1 Hz) and uses automated CompCor (principal components from WM/CSF masks) for denoising, whereas the original SNT uses a Gaussian–weighted high–pass filter and mean WM/CSF regression. Our goal was to quantify how such typical workflow variations affect the final treatment target, not to replace or validate the original SNT protocol. Therefore, the findings presented here pertain specifically to the pipelines and parameters tested, and direct extrapolation to the original SNT pipeline should be made with caution.

Because each database provided both rs–fMRI and 3D T1–weighted structural images, we performed an additional analysis in native T1 space (rather than the standard MNI152 template) to avoid registration–related confounds. For this analysis, rs–fMRI data were preprocessed using the complete DPABI and FSL pipelines as described in “Pre-processing of rs-fMRI data” section. Furthermore, to isolate the contribution of individual pre-processing steps, we also applied the SNT target computation after only the (i) denoising, (ii) spatial smoothing, or (iii) temporal filtering step of each pipeline (see Fig. 1).

In addition to comparing the use of the full DPABI and FSL pre-processing pipelines in the SNT protocol, separate analyses were performed for when the SNT protocol was applied after only the denoising, smoothing, and filtering components of the pre-processing pipelines had, respectively, been completed. Dashed lines indicate steps that were performed only in the cross-database comparison (see the “Comparison of SNT target location in different databases using the standardized MNI152 template” section) and not in the native-space analyses (see the “Comparison of the effect of DPABI and FSL pre-processing on individualized SNT target location” section).

For each subject the following steps were performed. Firstly, ROI definition: left DLPFC (BA9/46) and bilateral sgACC (BA25) from the Brodmann atlas were resampled to native space. Secondly, clustering: within each ROI, hierarchical clustering groups voxels into subregions where mean intra–subregion correlation ≥ 0.5. Thirdly, representative voxel: for each subregion, select the voxel with highest Spearman correlation to the subregion’s median time series. Fourthly, correlation matrix: compute Spearman r between each DLPFC subregion and each sgACC subregion. Fifthly, target selection: for each DLPFC subregion i, , where clusteringi = (mean Euclidean distance between voxels in i)/(voxel count of i). The DLPFC subregion with highest Score(i) is the target (equal weighting of negative correlation, sgACC subregion size, and clustering). Sixthly, No–corr cases: if all r(i, j) ≥ 0, the subject is recorded as “no significant negative correlation.” Then, the mean, median, variance, and distance between targets were recorded, and the influence of the different pre-processing pipelines and their component parts on target position was calculated.

Effect of scan duration on SNT target location

To address the third objective regarding scan duration, five consecutive rs-fMRI scans were acquired for each of the 25 healthy subjects on the uMR 790 3T system. Each scan lasted 6 min, resulting in a total of 30 min of rs-fMRI data per subject. All five scans were acquired consecutively without the subject leaving the scanner, using identical acquisition parameters (TR/TE = 300/30 ms, voxel size = 3.0 × 3.0 × 3.0 mm³, 200 volumes per scan). Subjects were instructed to keep their eyes open, fixate on a crosshair, and remain still. The five scans were then concatenated in chronological order to simulate total scan durations of 6, 12, 18, 24, and 30 min. Specifically, the 6-min condition consisted of the first scan only; the 12-min condition consisted of the first two scans concatenated; and similarly for the remaining durations. Each concatenated dataset was preprocessed using the identical DPABI and FSL pipeline described in the “Pre-processing of rs-fMRI data” section.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 21.0 (IBM, New York, USA). The chi-square test (cross-tabulation analysis) was used to compare the proportion of subjects with versus without a significant negative correlation between DLPFC and sgACC across the four groups. The ANOVA compared the negative correlation magnitude across groups, and the CV was used to describe spatial variability of target coordinates. Next, potential differences between the effects of pre-processing using DPABI and FSL were examined using t-tests. With regard to the study of the effect of duration of data acquisition, statistical analyses included repeated-measures analysis of variance to test for significant differences in target location across duration groups (with Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc tests if significant). In addition, polynomial contrasts (linear and quadratic) were used to determine potential trends in target position with increasing scan time. For all analyses, a value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The results of applying paired t-tests to compare the impact of different pairs of steps (e.g. denoising and smoothing, and smoothing and temporal filtering) are presented in Table 4. There are significant differences in target locations calculated following application of the DPABI and FSL pre-processing pipelines across the four groups of subjects. The combination of smoothing and temporal filtering showed the strongest effects in healthy populations, with highly significant deviations in the HCP cohort (t = 8.35, P < 0.001) and in the HCs in the HX-DICS database (t = −4.44, P < 0.001). In contrast, the combination of denoising and smoothing showed no significant differences in the CHCP cohort or patients with MDD in the HX-DICS database (P > 0.05), implying cohort-specific modulation of pre-processing effects, potentially influenced by race or disease-related factors. Algorithmic differences in pre-processing steps may not only induce spatial biases (as shown in Fig. 6) but potentially generate inconsistent conclusions across populations.

Effect of scan duration on SNT target location

Repeated measures analysis of variance showed that altering the duration of the scanning time for the acquisition of rs-fMRI data had a significant effect on the Euclidean distance measurement of differences in the location of the DLPFC target (F = 5.538, P = 0.018). Further analysis revealed that the Euclidean distance value showed a significant linear trend to be reduced with increase in scanning time (F = 7.403, P = 0.012), decreasing 8.33 ± 5.22 at 6 min to 5.90 ± 3.73 at 12 min to 5.37 ± 3.70 at 18 min. However, there was no significant quadratic trend (P = 0.175).

To assess whether the observed scan duration effect was pipeline–dependent, we repeated the analysis using rs–fMRI data preprocessed with the FSL pipeline on the same 25 subjects. Within–subjects contrasts showed a significant linear decrease in Euclidean distance with increasing scan duration (F = 24.451, P < 0.001), while quadratic and cubic trends were not significant (P > 0.05). Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction indicated that the Euclidean distance at 6 min (mean ± SD: 7.96 ± 2.94 cm) was significantly larger than at 12 min (6.04 ± 2.85 cm, P = 0.011), 18 min (5.28 ± 3.63 cm, P = 0.028), and 24 min (4.14 ± 3.73 cm, P < 0.001). No significant differences were found between 12, 18, and 24 min (all P > 0.05). These results corroborate the findings from the DPABI pipeline, further supporting that approximately 12 min of rs–fMRI acquisition yields stable SNT target localization with minimal additional gain beyond this duration. Discussion

The three objectives of the present study in respect of quantifying variability in the location of rs-fMRI based TMS targets in DLPFC obtained using the SNT protocol have been realized. Firstly, studies of four groups of subjects from three databases, in which data were all normalized to the standard MNI152 template, revealed significant and consistent variability in the location of treatment targets. Secondly, comparison of the application of the DPABI and FSL pre-processing pipelines in native T1 space, produced inconsistency in target coordinates which may result in target misalignment and reduced treatment efficacy, and which were especially related to the spatial smoothing, denoising, and temporal filtering steps. Thirdly, the recommendation is made that for reliable target location the duration of the scan time for acquisition of rs-fMRI data should be at least 12 min. These findings advance understanding in respect of the delivery of personalized TMS treatments and represent critical considerations for multi-center research and are discussed below.

Comparison of SNT target location in different databases using the standardized MNI152 template

No significant differences were found in the magnitude of the negative functional connectivity coefficient between DLPFC and sgACC determined using the SNT protocol across the study of four groups of subjects (n = 310) in three independent databases, representing different geographic regions, MRI system specifications, acquisition parameters for the rs-fMRI data (i.e. TR = 0.71–2.0 s) and clinical diagnosis, corroborating the robustness of SNT’s circuit-based targeting. However, significant inter-subject variability was found in respect of target location, with median Euclidean distance varying between 1.25 and 1.64 cm, and CV between 19% and 28% in the four groups, a finding that aligns with findings from the single-center of the SNT protocol (Cole et al., 2020; Cole, Phillips and Bentzley 2022). This spatial variation raises questions about the “one-size-fits-all” anatomically based definition of TMS targets (e.g. using the F3 landmark), and may explain why in a meta-analysis of 3257 patients, Berlim et al. (2014) found there to be substantial heterogeneity in the outcome of using rTMS to treat depression, with only 29.3% of patients showing a positive response as defined by a reduction in HDRS of ≥ 50% and remission rates of 18.6% as defined by a reduction in HDRS of ≤ 7. The present study highlights the potential for improved outcomes using the SNT protocol; however, direct clinical validation is needed. Furthermore, given that the spatial resolution of TMS coils is on the order of 2–3 cm, measurement of rs-fMRI-based functional connectivity between the DLPFC and sgACC is required to develop personalized treatment targets. Future trials should also integrate neuronavigation technology with functional connectivity maps to minimize variability and enhance treatment precision.

Comparison of the effect of DPABI and FSL pre-processing on individualized SNT target location

Use of the DPABI and FSL pre-processing pipelines prior to application of the SNT protocol produced an average positional difference in the location of the treatment target in DLPFC that exceeded 1 cm, with mean Euclidean distances ranging from 1.45 to 3.82 cm across cohorts and individual maximum deviations reaching 6.14 cm (see the “Comparison of the effect of DPABI and FSL pre-processing on individualized SNT target location” section). Furthermore, the magnitude of the negative correlation coefficient between DLPFC and sgACC was consistently higher after FSL pre-processing than after DPABI pre-processing across all four groups, and these differences reached statistical significance (P < 0.001). Pre-processing pipelines thus serve as hidden confounding factors that can substantially alter the final treatment target.

Previous studies have documented software–induced variability in rs–fMRI metrics and task–fMRI results (Smith et al., 2005; Pauli et al., 2016; Popescu et al., 2016; Bowring et al., 2019; Botvinik–Nezer et al., 2020). However, to the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first to investigate how different pre–processing software packages, in particular their specific steps of spatial smoothing, denoising, and temporal filtering, affect the location of rs–fMRI–guided DLPFC treatment targets. FSL (Jenkinson et al., 2012) and DPABI (Yan et al., 2016) are two mainstream software tools for rs–fMRI analysis. DPABI is designed for standardized clinical applications and features automated processing pipelines and templates that have been constructed for Asian populations. FSL is widely used in academic settings and renowned for its flexibility. To ensure reproducibility and clinical precision, it is helpful to avoid using multiple different software tools in a single study, which is why the present study focused on these two representative packages.

The significant differences observed between DPABI and FSL arise from three inter–dependent pre–processing steps: spatial smoothing, denoising, and temporal filtering. First, regarding spatial smoothing, both software default to a Gaussian kernel with 6 mm FWHM. However, DPABI employs anisotropic tuning and mirror–symmetric filling to preserve non–isovoxel data fidelity, whereas FSL’s fslmaths uses isotropic kernels with zero–padding that can attenuate cortical edges. Second, for denoising, DPABI’s automated CompCor (using SPM–based white matter and CSF masks) suppresses low–frequency drift, while FSL’s manual CompCor (mean signal regression) may introduce residual gray matter noise, potentially inflating sgACC–DLPFC connectivity estimates. Third, temporal filtering further exacerbates these differences. DPABI uses an ideal bandpass filter (0.01–0.1 Hz) that introduces Gibbs ringing, whereas FSL applies a Gaussian–weighted filter with smoother roll–off. Our cross–method analysis (Fig. 5) confirmed that applying the FSL temporal filter to DPABI–smoothed data yielded significantly higher absolute correlation coefficients than applying the DPABI native filter (Δ|r| = 0.12, P < 0.001). Cumulatively, these algorithmic nuances can lead to clinically significant shifts in target location that may exceed the spatial resolution of TMS coils (2–3 cm).

Importantly, the observed differences between DPABI and FSL—such as anisotropic vs. isotropic smoothing kernels, automated vs. manual CompCor, and ideal vs. Gaussian–weighted filters—stem primarily from their respective default parameter configurations, not from fundamental algorithmic superiority of one package over the other. In other words, these discrepancies are largely parameter–driven rather than software–intrinsic. For example, matching the smoothing kernel type (e.g. using isotropic kernels in both pipelines with identical boundary conditions), harmonizing denoising strategies (e.g. using the same number of CompCor components or switching both to mean–signal regression), or applying identical temporal filter characteristics (e.g. both using a Gaussian–weighted filter) could plausibly reduce or even eliminate the observed differences in target localization. However, systematically exploring such parameter optimization across pipelines was beyond the scope of this study, which aimed to document the variability introduced by standard, off–the–shelf workflows as used in typical research settings. Therefore, we do not claim that DPABI or FSL is inherently more accurate; instead, our findings underscore that pre-processing choices—many of which are parameter–dependent—produce clinically meaningful differences in target localization. Future studies should prioritize cross–pipeline parameter harmonization (e.g. comparing results after equalizing smoothing kernel type, filter characteristics, and denoising components) rather than focusing solely on software selection. Until such systematic optimization is performed, we recommend software–specific validation of SNT protocols and careful documentation of all pre-processing parameters to ensure reproducibility across multi–center and cross–population studies.

This study also revealed that, compared to registration to the MNI152 standard template, analysis in native T1 space led to a significant increase in the proportion of subjects with negative DLPFC–sgACC correlation coefficients as well as an increase in the peak negative correlation values (see the “Comparison of the effect of DPABI and FSL pre-processing on individualized SNT target location” section). The MNI template is a population–averaged brain atlas whose use may blur the specificity of rs–fMRI signals, whereas individual neuroanatomical features are preserved in native T1 space, reducing signal ambiguity caused by spatial misalignment. Wu et al. (2011) similarly demonstrated that standard space registration increases inter–subject variability in the analysis of low–frequency amplitude fluctuations, potentially masking individual functional characteristics. Therefore, we highly recommend performing analyses in native space for precise identification of functional connectivity abnormalities (e.g. DLPFC–sgACC dysregulation in depression) and for personalized target localization in TMS therapies.

Finally, it is worth noting that the present study was not designed to determine which pipeline produces the “correct” target, as no gold–standard clinical outcome measure was available. Instead, our goal was to raise awareness of the substantial variability introduced by routine pre-processing choices and to provide a methodological foundation for future standardization efforts.

Effect of scan duration on SNT target location

Our finding that approximately 12 min of rs–fMRI acquisition yields stable SNT target localization is consistent with several previous studies (Van Dijk et al., 2010; Birn et al., 2013; Ma et al., 2024). Birn et al. systematically examined the effect of scan length on test–retest reliability of functional connectivity estimates and reported that reliability plateaued after 9–12 min, with minimal improvement beyond this duration. Similarly, Ma et al. investigated the impact of scan duration on dynamic causal modeling parameters and found that extending scan time from 10 to 14 min did not significantly improve the false–positive rate or reproducibility of connectivity measures. Van Dijk et al. also demonstrated that 5–10 min of rs–fMRI data are sufficient to produce reliable individual connectivity maps, with diminishing returns beyond 10 min.

Theoretically, longer scan durations should improve the signal–to–noise ratio and yield more stable estimates of functional connectivity, as the increased number of time points reduces the variance of correlation coefficients. However, the law of diminishing returns applies: the gain in reliability becomes progressively smaller as scan duration increases. Our data support this notion—while the Euclidean distance decreased from 6 to 12 min, the reduction from 12 to 18 min and from 18 to 24 min was not statistically significant. Moreover, in clinical practice, longer scan times are associated with increased patient discomfort, head motion, and cost. Therefore, we recommend 12 min as a pragmatic trade–off that provides sufficient stability for individualized TMS target localization while remaining feasible in clinical settings. For research studies where maximal precision is required, longer durations (e.g. 20–30 min) may still be beneficial, but the marginal gain beyond 12 min is limited.

Limitations and future directions

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, there may be aspects of pre-processing other than spatial smoothing, denoising, and temporal filtering, that contribute to target discrepancies. For example, DPABI uses DARTEL and FSL uses FNIRT for image registration. Furthermore, FSL’s 6-parameter motion correction may retain residual head tilt in Asian cohorts. Secondly, in addition to DPABI and FSL, software such as AFNI (3dDespike) and freesurfer can also be used and may offer advantages in determining treatment target location. For example, AFNI’s Gaussian filtering algorithm may be useful to apply in populations with thickened DLPFC cortex. Future multi-software comparisons are needed to isolate algorithm-specific biases. While we replicated the scan duration analysis using both DPABI and FSL and observed consistent results, the generalizability of the 12-min recommendation to other pre-processing pipelines (e.g. AFNI, SPM) remains to be tested. Thirdly, the observed differences in target location produced by pre-processing using the DPABI and FSL pipelines may reflect an interaction between population-specific MRI acquisition parameter settings and software default parameters. Furthermore, whereas FSL makes generic use of the MNI152 template, DPABI includes templates specifically designed for application in Asian populations and which accounts for cortical thickness of the middle frontal gyrus region being found to be thinner in Chinese populations compared to other groups (Tang et al., 2018). Fourthly, research has shown that using different values of TE has a significant impact on the reliability of functional connectivity measurements obtained from rs-fMRI data. For example, functional connectivity maps generated from data acquired with TE = 38 ms were reported to have higher reproducibility compared to those acquired with TE = 30 ms (Vasileiadi et al., 2023), suggesting that localization of treatment targets can potentially be improved by optimizing MR image acquisition protocols. Fifthly, prospective clinical studies that include measurements of HDRS scores and remission rates, are needed to determine whether treatment target locations defined using the DPABI pre-processing pipeline yield superior outcomes compared to those determined using the FSL pre-processing pipeline. Sixthly, the sample size of 25 subjects used to suggest 12 min as the optimal rs-fMRI scan duration is relatively small, and further studies with larger cohorts are warranted. Seventhly, head movement parameters and physiological noise were not included as covariates in the analyses performed in the present study. This may have an impact on the calculation of target location, especially if there is a cumulative effect of head movement with longer duration of scanning.

Conclusion

Three related studies of the individualized localization of DLPFC treatment targets for TMS based on rs-fMRI functional connectivity analysis using the SNT protocol have provided significant findings with regard to variability and challenge the validity of using traditional anatomical methods. Studies of four groups of subjects from three databases, in which data were all normalized to the standard MNI152 template, revealed significant and consistent variability in the location of treatment targets. Comparison of the application of the DPABI and FSL pre-processing pipelines in native T1 space, produced inconsistency in target coordinates and values of the negative correlation coefficient which may result in target misalignment and reduced treatment efficacy, and which were especially related to the spatial smoothing, denoising, and temporal filtering steps. In addition, the recommendation is made that for reliable target location the duration of the scan time for acquisition of rs-fMRI data should be at least 12 min. Variations in data acquisition strategies and pre-processing pipelines are confounding factors that need to be addressed via parameter optimization and software-specific validation in order to develop a standardized SNT-based protocol for determining individual treatment targets for TMS and which can be used in prospective clinical trials.
