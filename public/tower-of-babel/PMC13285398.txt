Transcriptional and functional profiling reveals unique activation of Adapter CAR T cells in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Nele Knelangen, Ulrika Bader, Evangelia Maniaki, Boris Engels, Luca Gattinoni, Joerg Mittelstaet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy has shown great promise in treating malignant diseases, yet its efficacy is often limited by challenges such as controllability and the lack of tumor-specific antigens. Adapter CAR (AdCAR) T cells address these limitations by redirecting T cell activity through adapter molecules rather than direct interaction with tumor-associated antigens, offering an on-/off- switch mechanism and the potential for multitargeting. While functionality of AdCAR T cells has been demonstrated in various models, the underlying AdCAR T cell response to differing adapter concentration and its comparison to conventional CAR T cells remain poorly characterized.MethodsIn this study, we systematically examined adapter-dependent AdCAR T cell responses using functional assays and single-cell RNA sequencing in an acute myeloid leukemia model.ResultsAdapter concentration was found to determine AdCAR T cell activation dynamics, transcriptional states and metabolic reprogramming. Background

CAR T cell therapy has achieved notable success in hematological malignancies [1–4] however, its effectiveness remains limited in heterogeneous cancers such as acute myeloid leukemia and solid tumors. The lack of tumor-specific antigens, along with inter- and intra-patient heterogeneity present significant challenges for CAR T cell therapy. Additionally, the inability to control CAR T cells, once infused, poses a risk of potentially life-threatening cytokine release syndrome [5] and neurotoxicity [6]. While safety switches such as truncated EGFR, RQR8 or iCasp9 can be incorporated to control CAR T cells, these mechanisms can dramatically impact the long-term efficacy of the cell therapy [7–10]. Moreover, continuous interaction of CAR T cells with their cognate antigen can lead to T cell exhaustion, which can occur with uncontrolled access of the cognate antigen to CAR T cells [11, 12].

To address these challenges, Adapter CAR (AdCAR) T cells have been developed as promising alternative [13–17]. Unlike conventional CAR T cells, AdCARs are designed to bind to tumor-associated antigens indirectly through adapter molecules. Consequently, AdCAR activity is strictly dependent on the adapter molecule, enabling precise modulation of T cell activity. Additionally, the use of multiple adapter molecules either simultaneously [15, 18–21] or sequentially [22] can result in superior tumor control compared to monotargeting.

Several clinical trials evaluating different AdCAR platforms are currently ongoing (NCT04230265, NCT05312411, NCT04488354, NCT05396885, NCT04450069). Successful engraftment of AdCAR T cells has been demonstrated, with T-cell expansion shown to be strictly adapter-dependent [23, 24]. In patients with acute myeloid leukemia, treatment with UniCAR T cells in combination with a CD123-specific adapter resulted in an overall response rate of 53%. Importantly, treatment-related toxicities were reversible following discontinuation of the adapter molecule [25].

Despite these advances, the impact of varying adapter concentrations on AdCAR T cell responses, and how these responses compare to those of conventional T cells, has not been fully characterized. While functional readouts of adapter-dependent AdCAR activity have been described [13, 14, 26, 27], a detailed understanding of the transcriptional and metabolic states associated with different activation levels is needed to distinguish between sustained and insufficient activation.

In this study, the adapter-dependent responses of AdCAR T cells were systematically analyzed in an acute myeloid leukemia model using functional assays and single-cell RNA sequencing. Moreover, the transcriptional landscape, activation dynamics, and functional outcomes of AdCAR T cells were dissected across a range of adapter concentrations. Our data provide new insights into the tunability of AdCAR T cell responses and identify features of cell activation that may be used for future monitoring.

Methods

Cell lines

SupT1 and THP-1 were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI 1640) medium (Biowest, Nuaillé, France) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) (Biochrom GmbH, Berlin, Germany) and 2 mM L-glutamine (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland), while HEK293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Biowest, Nuaillé, France). GFP+ OCI-AML2 cells were cultured in Alpha Minimum Essential Medium (Alpha MEM) (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) supplemented with 20% FCS.

Generation of CAR constructs and lentiviral pseudoparticle production

The AdCAR used in this study is redirected against a biotin tag presented within a defined linker structure, known as linker-label epitope [15] and was generated by linking the anti-LLE-specific scFv in-frame to human mIgG4 hinge [16], human CD8 transmembrane domain (aa 183–189, RefSeq: NP_001139345.1), human CD28 co-stimulatory domain (aa 181–220, UniProt: P10747), human 4-1BB co-stimulatory domain (CD137, aa 52–163, UniProt: Q07011) and human CD3ζ signaling domain (CD247, aa 52–163, RefSeq: NP_443194.1) The leader sequence was derived from IL22-RA (aa 1–21, RefSeq: NP_443194.1) subunit. Truncated low-affinity nerve growth factor receptor (ΔLNGFR) served as a transduction marker (aa 1–274, RefSeq: NP_002498.1), which was separated from the AdCAR via a P2A self-cleaving peptide. For the conventional CAR, the scFv of the AdCAR was replaced with an anti-CD33 scFv derived from gemtuzumab.

Lentiviral pseudoparticles were produced by transient transfection of HEK293T cells using polyethylenimine. After 48 h, supernatant containing lentiviral pseudoparticles were concentrated by overnight centrifugation and pellets were resuspended in TexMACS™ (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) before storing at −80°C. Lentiviral titers were determined by transduction of SupT1 and flow cytometric analysis of ΔLNGFR expression.

Generation and expansion of (Ad)CAR T cells

Buffy coats from healthy volunteer donors were obtained from Deutsches Rotes Kreuz (Dortmund and Hagen, Germany). Peripheral mononuclear cells were isolated via density gradient centrifugation. T cells were purified using the Pan T cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) according to manufacturer’s instructions. T cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1106/mL in TexMACS™ supplemented with 12.5 ng/mL IL-7, IL-15, T cell TransAct™ (all Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). After 24 h, T cells were transduced with the respective lentivirus pseudoparticles at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. Forty-eight hours post activation, T cell medium was exchanged to wash out T cell TransAct™ and T cells were cultured in TexMACS™ supplemented with IL-7 and IL-15. After six days, T cells were enriched for the ΔLNGFR transduction marker using the MACSelect™ LNGFR system (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. T cells were cultured for 12 days before functionality testing.

Functional analysis of (Ad)CAR T cells

Cellular cytotoxicity assay

(Ad)CAR T cells were co-cultured with the indicated target cell in target cell medium at an effector-to-target (E:T) ratio of 2:1, unless otherwise indicated, in presence of the indicated adapter. Functional readouts were performed via flow cytometry, single-cell RNA sequencing or the MACSPlex T/NK Kit, human (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Long-term in vitro co-culture experiments with repeated addition of new target cells were analyzed using the live-cell imaging system Incucyte® S3 (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany). The GFP fluorescence intensity of OCI-AML2 cells was determined as integrated values normalized to the starting time point. For quantitative real -time PCR, ΔLNGFR positive (Ad)CAR T cells were isolated using the MACSelect™ LNGFR system (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) according to manufacturer’s instruction. Untransduced T cells were isolated after labeling with CD5-PE (REA782, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) and PE-Microbeads via MACS® cell separation (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Cell pellets were stored at −80 °C.

Metabolic flow

2.5104 cells obtained from co-culture assay were treated with either DMSO, 1 µM oligomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA), 100 mM 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) or a combination of both. After incubation for 30 min at 37 °C, 5% CO2, 2.5 µg/mL puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) was added. After incubation for 30 min at 37 °C, 5% CO2, cells were stained with Viobility™ 405/452 Fixable Dye (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions followed by surface staining as described below. Cells were fixed and permeabilized using the FoxP3/ Transcription factor staining buffer set (ThermoFisher, Waltham, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Intracellular staining of puromycin was performed by incubation with anti-puromycin AlexaFlour®488 antibody (2A4, BioLegend®, San Diego, USA) for 60 min at 4 °C. Samples were measured at the MACSQuant® X flow cytometer. This protocol was adapted from the SCENITH method [28].

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from frozen cell pellets using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). cDNA was synthesized from 200 ng RNA using random hexamers and SuperScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase (ThermoFisher, Waltham, USA). Real-time qPCR was performed using PowerTrack™ SYBR Green Master Mix (ThermoFisher, Waltham, USA) and the primers listed in Supplementary Table 1 on the ABI 7900 system (Bio-Rad, California, USA). Expression level of mRNA was calculated according to the housekeeping gene RLP13A.

Single-cell RNA sequencing sample preparation, data acquisition and data pre-processing

For single-cell RNA sequencing (Ad)CAR T cells and untransduced T cells were co-cultured with GFP+ OCI AML2 cells as described above. After 24 h, either dead cells were removed by using the Dead Cell Removal Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) or AdCAR T cells were isolated from the co-culture via the MACSelect™ LNGFR system. Samples were stained with TruStain FcX™ (BioLegend, San Diego, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Next, samples from the different donors were labeled with 1 µg hashing antibody (#394661, #394663, #394665, BioLegend, San Diego, USA) per condition. After incubation for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark, cells were washed with PBS supplemented with 0.04% BSA. After washing, samples were incubated for 5 min at room temperature followed by washing twice with PBS supplemented with 1% BSA. Finally, samples were resuspended in PBS supplemented with 0.04% non-acetylated BSA. Samples from the same donors were pooled at equal ratios. Cell count and viability was determined by Countess™ 3 (ThermoFisher, Waltham, USA) and cell concentration was adjusted to 1000 cells/µl. scRNA libraries were prepared using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5´Kit v2 (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, USA) aiming for 10.000 single cells per library. All libraries were sequenced on NextSeq™2000 (Illumina, San Diego, USA). scRNA reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38, into which ΔLNGFR, AdCAR, CAR and GFP sequences were incorporated. Unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts were quantified to generate a gene-barcode matrix using the CellRanger Pipeline v7.1.0 (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, USA).

Single-cell RNA sequencing quality control and cluster analysis

The gene-by-cell matrix was converted into a Seurat object (version 4.4.0) [29] and further processed with R (Version 4.2.2). Doublets were removed using DoubletFinder (Version 2.0.4) [30], and cells were filtered based on following parameters: ≥250 unique genes, ≥500 UMI, ≥0.8 log10 genes per UMI, ≤5% mitochondrial gene counts. Samples were integrated using Harmony (Version 1.2.0) [31]. Cells were normalized and scaled using ScaleData. Dimensionality reduction was applied to select Principal Components (PCs) that capture the highest variance in the aggregated dataset for graph-based clustering. Clustering was performed by Seurat’s FindNeighbors and FindCluster. Clusters were visualized via UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection). Tumor cell clusters were identified via GFP expression and excluded via the subset function. The remaining T cells were scaled and clustered again. Cell embeddings in the UMAP plot were calculated based on 20 PCs and 0.4 resolution. T cells were subsetted in CD4+ and CD8+ based on CD4 and CD8B average expression (>1) and re-analyzed as described before. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the FindMarkers function, which applied the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Trajectory and pathway analysis

Trajectory analysis was performed using Monocle3 (Version 1.3.7) in Seurat version 5.0 [32] on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells individually. To identify genes changing as a function of pseudotime graph_test was used. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the R package clusterProfiler (Version 4.6.2) [33–35] considering DEGs with >0.6 avg_log2FC, <0.05 p_adj, <0.5 morans_I.

Flow cytometry

Cells were stained in CliniMACS® PBS/EDTA buffer (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) supplemented with 0.5% bovine serum albumin (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) (PEB) with the respective antibody cocktail for 10 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, 100 µl PEB were added. 7-AAD was used for live/dead staining. All antibodies, i.e CD45RA-VioBlue (REA1047), CD4-VioGreen (REA623), CD8-VioGreen (REA734), LNGFR-VioGreen (REA844), CD223-PE (REA351), LNGFR-PE (REA844), Tim3-PE (REAL818), CD25-PE (REA570), CD62L-PE-Vio770 (REA615), PD-1-PE-Vio770 (REA1165), CD45RO-APC (REA611), CD137-APC (REA765), CD8-APC-Vio770 (REA734), CD69-APC-Vio770 (REA824) (all Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) were used according to manufacturer’s protocol. Data were acquired on MACSQuant®10 or MACSQuant®X and analyzed via FlowLogic™ V7.3 or V8.

Results

Adapter molecule-dependent modulation of AdCAR effector functions

The AdCAR platform is based on a third-generation CAR incorporating 4-1BB and CD28 co-stimulatory domains (Supplementary Figure 1A). The scFv binds to the linker-label epitope of a biotinylated Fab molecule. In contrast, the control CAR binds CD33 directly. Both the adapter molecule and the ectodomain of the conventional CD33 CAR are derived from the FDA-approved antibody-drug conjugate Gemtuzumab-Ozogamicin [36]. Unlike CAR T cells, which engage the tumor-associated antigen (TAA) directly, AdCAR T cell binding depends on the adapter (Fig. 1A). Optimizing adapter concentration is critical: Low concentrations may fail to activate AdCAR T cells effectively, whereas very high concentrations of adapter could saturate both target cells and AdCAR T cells, potentially reducing their activation.

(C) AdCAR and (D) untransduced (UTD) T cell cytotoxicity after 24 h of co-culture with OCI-AML2 cells in presence of different anti-CD33-AM (AM) concentrations. (E) Cytotoxicity assay results after co-culturing OCI-AML2 cells with 500 ng/mL AM for 24 h. (F) Longitudinal analysis of (Ad)CAR T and UTD T cells co-cultured with OCI-AML2 cells. Enrichment for the surrogate transduction marker ΔLNGFR yielded a highly pure population of transduced T cells, comprising approximately 40% CD8+ and 60% CD4+ T cells (Fig. 1B).

AdCAR T cells exhibited adapter-concentration-dependent cytotoxicity, with an EC50 of 5.7 ± 0.7 ng/mL AM (Fig. 1C). Additionally, AdCAR T cells upregulated CD137 expression and IFN-γ secretion (Supplementary Figure 1B/C). The adapter alone did not induce nonspecific cytotoxicity with untransduced T cells (UTD) or OCI-AML2 cells alone (Fig. 1D/E).

The AdCAR platform allows precise modulation of adapter concentrations throughout co-culture, even after initial activation. To test whether higher adapter concentrations could induce killing of previously unactivated or sub-optimally activated AdCAR T cells (e.g. AdCAR T cells co-cultured in presence of 5 or 10 ng/mL AM), a repetitive killing assay was performed. AdCAR T cells were co-cultured with OCI-AML2 cells at a fixed adapter concentration. After 48 h, additional OCI-AML2 cells were added, and the adapter concentration was either maintained or increased to 500 ng/mL AM.

Conventional CAR T cells maintained tumor control regardless of adapter presence. However, AdCAR T cells exposed to suboptimal adapter concentrations lost tumor control after the first re-challenge. Increasing the adapter concentration restored tumor control and sustained it through subsequent re-challenges. Notably, previously unactivated AdCAR T cells were in a less exhausted state after 48 h (Supplementary Figure 1D) compared with continuously activated AdCAR T cells. Once reactivated by AM, these cells eliminated target cells more efficiently than those continuously exposed to the adapter during the first 48 hours (Fig. 1F, Supplementary Figure 1E). This suggests that preserving a fitter T cell state in the first 48 h results in efficient cytotoxicity even at lower E:T ratios. A similar, albeit less pronounced, trend was observed in sub-optimally activated AdCAR T cells. Notably, the ability to reinduce killing by sub-optimally activated AdCAR T cells underscores the importance of distinguishing optimal from suboptimal activation states beyond functional outcomes.

Overall, the activation of AdCAR T cells can be precisely controlled through the addition and dosage of the adapter. Furthermore, even those AdCAR T cells that were initially sub-optimally stimulated can be effectively rescued by increasing adapter concentrations.

Single-cell RNA sequencing identifies adapter-dependent transcriptomic profiles

To examine the underlying AdCAR T cell response to varying adapter concentrations, and compare it to conventional CAR T cells, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq). To this end, AdCAR T cells were co-cultured with OCI-AML2 cells in presence of high (500 ng/mL), low (10 ng/mL and 5 ng/mL) adapter concentrations, or in absence of adapter (w/o). UTD and CAR T cells co-cultured with OCI-AML2 cells served as controls. Projection of T cells in a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection Plot (UMAP) revealed that adapter concentration, rather than donor variability, was the main driver of variation (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Figure 2A-C). Adapter concentration was the dominant source of activation along the vertical axis, while CD4/CD8 expression defined the variation along the horizontal axis (Fig. 2B). Due to the distinct clustering of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, the data was subsetted and re-analyzed according to CD4 and CD8 expression. For CD4+ T cells a total of 7 clusters were identified (Fig. 3A), whereas for CD8+ T cells 8 clusters were identified (Fig. 3D). The top three genes per cluster were used for functional annotation. CD4+ AdCAR T cells treated with adapter mainly clustered in C1, C3, C4 and C6, while CD4+ CAR T cells clustered in C2 (Fig. 3B). CD4+ AdCAR T cells co-cultivated with OCI-AML2 in absence of adapter clustered together with UTD T cells, mainly in C0 and C5 (Fig. 3C). Untreated CD8+ AdCAR T cells and CD8+ UTD T cells clustered together in C0, C5, C6. CD8+ AdCAR T cell clustered in C1, C2, C3 and C7 in an adapter concentration-dependent manner, while CD8+ CAR T cells predominantly clustered in C3 and C4 (Fig. 3F). Both CD4+ and CD8+ UTD and untreated AdCAR cells resembled naïve/ Tcm-like states (CD4+/CD8+: C0) and a small population of proliferating T cells (CD8+: C5, CD4+: C4). With increased adapter concentration, T cells adopted an IFN-responsive state (CD8+/CD4+: C1). CAR T cells and AdCAR T cells co-cultured with high adapter concentrations showed features of cytolytic and proliferative lymphocytes.

Trajectory analysis using Monocle3 was performed to identify adapter-regulated genes in AdCAR T cells treated with different adapter concentrations. For both CD4+ and CD8+ AdCAR T cells, pseudotime followed the clustering of activated cells, confirming the existence of an adapter-dependent activation trajectory (Fig. 4A/B). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each adapter concentration were visualized in volcano plots for CD8+ and CD4+ AdCAR T cells (Fig. 4C/D). AdCAR T cells co-cultured with 5 ng/mL and 10 ng/mL adapter exhibited a high expression of interferon-induced genes. At 10 ng/mL adapter, upregulation of transcripts related to glycolysis, such as ENO1 and PKM, was observed, which was even more pronounced at 500 ng/mL. Additionally, increased gene transcription for heat-shock-proteins such as HSP90AB1 and HSP90AA1, tubulins like TUBA1B or TUBB and RRM2, a ribonucleotide reductase involved in DNA synthesis [37], was detected. Genes highlighted in red namely IL2RA, MX1, HSP90AB1, TUBA1B, NME1, RRM2 and ENO1 were selected for further validation due to their strong expression compared to AdCAR co-cultured without adapter. Those genes also represent key components of the most prominent regulated pathways. Pathway analysis of the differentially expressed genes across pseudotime revealed that pathways involved in interferon response were up-regulated in AdCAR T cells treated with 10 ng/mL and 5 ng/mL adapter, while transition into proliferation and HSP90 chaperone cycle for steroid hormones receptors (SHR) were upregulated in AdCAR T cells co-cultured with 500 ng/mL adapter (Supplementary Figure 2D).

Taken together, transcriptomic analysis identified an adapter-dependent gene signature, with low adapter concentrations inducing an interferon-responsive state, while highly activated AdCAR T cells exhibited signatures of cell proliferation, strong T cell activation, and increased glycolysis.

Activated AdCAR T cell depend on glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation

To validate the metabolic profile identified via scRNA-seq the SCENITH assay (single-cell energetic metabolism by profiling translation inhibition) was employed. This flow cytometry-based method assesses metabolic activity by measuring de novo protein synthesis as an indirect indicator of ATP production. Cells were exposed to inhibitors that selectively block key metabolic pathways, i.e. oligomycin (O) for oxidative phosphorylation and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) for glycolysis (Fig. 5A).

(B) Total metabolic capacity of (Ad)CAR and UTD T cells after co-culturing with OCI-AML2 cells for 24 h in presence of various anti-CD33-AM (AM) concentrations. (E) qRT-PCR validation of adapter-regulated genes identified by scRNA sequencing in AdCAR and CAR T cells. Both CD4+ and CD8+ AdCAR T cells exhibited an adapter-dependent increase in total metabolic capacity, with the effect being more pronounced in CD8+ T cells (Fig. 5B/C). Notably, CD4+ AdCAR T cells showed an adapter-dependent increase in glycolytic capacity, while CAR T cells had a glycolytic capacity comparable to the AdCAR T cells treated with 500 ng/mL adapter (Fig. 5D). For CD8+ T cells a stronger reduction in de novo protein synthesis was observed with 2-DG treatment compared to oligomycin, indicating a tendency towards glycolysis (Supplementary Figure 3A). These observations were supported by GSVA metabolic signatures [38], which showed expression of both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation-related genes (Supplementary Figure 3C).

Comparable gene expression profiles between activated AdCAR and CAR T cells

In addition to SCENITH, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was used to validate the hallmark genes of adapter-dependent AdCAR T cell activation. To this end, AdCAR, CAR and UTD T cells were co-cultured in the presence of the indicated adapter concentrations. After 24 h, T cells were isolated from the co-culture and qRT-PCR was performed. For HSP90AB1, TUBA1B, ENO1, NME1, RRM2 and IL2RA adapter-dependent increase in relative expression to UTD T cells was observed. In contrast, MX1, which is up-regulated upon interferon signaling, showed the highest expression in AdCAR T cells treated with 10 ng/mL and 5 ng/mL AM in co-culture with OCI-AML2 cells, consistent with the transcriptomic data derived from single-cell RNA sequencing. AdCAR T cells co-cultured with OCI-AML2 cells in absence of adapter showed similar expression levels to UTDs. Although CAR T cells were not included in the trajectory analysis, qRT-PCR results suggested that the hallmark gene expression profile can also be applied to CAR T cells. The relative expression of HSP90AB1, TUBA1B, ENO1, NME1, RRM2 and IL2RA was comparable to that of AdCAR T cells treated with 500 ng/mL adapter. Only MX1 was expressed at higher levels in AdCARs treated with 500 ng/mL adapter compared to CAR T cells (Fig. 5E).

Overall, the gene expression signature identified via scRNAseq was validated in bulk via qRT-PCR. The distinct expression pattern of MX1 in sub-optimally activated AdCAR T cells was confirmed. Moreover, CAR T cells demonstrated comparable gene expression to highly activated AdCAR T cells.

AdCAR T cells demonstrate functional activity with alternate targets and adapters

To examine the robustness of the identified hallmark gene expression across different target cell lines and adapter specificities, mRNA expression was evaluated in AdCAR T cells co-cultured with either anti-CD33-AM and THP-1 cells or anti-CLEC12A-AM and OCI-AML2 cells. In co-cultures with THP-1 cells, AdCAR T cells lysed targets with all tested anti-CD33-AM concentrations, comparable to CAR T cells (Fig. 6A). Adapter concentration-dependent activation of AdCAR T cells was observed in terms of CD137 expression and IFN-γ secretion, with levels comparable to CAR T cells being observed in presence of 500 ng/mL anti-CD33-AM (Supplementary Figure 4A). For RRM2, HSP90AB1, NME1, TUBA1B, ENO1, MX1 and IL2RA adapter-dependent increases in gene expression were detected. As observed before, MX1 expression was dynamically regulated. Overall, on transcriptomic level, expression of hallmark genes was consistent across all adapter concentrations and CAR T cells exhibited a similar expression profile to AdCAR T cells treated with the highest adapter concentration (Fig. 6B).

When AdCAR functionality was analyzed using anti-CLEC12A-AM, polyfunctional T cell responses were observed at 500 ng/mL and 50 ng/mL adapter, while 5 ng/mL anti-CLEC12A-AM was not sufficient to induce a response (Fig. 6C, Supplementary Figure 4B). This was also reflected in the expression of RRM2, HSP90AB1, NME1, TUBA1B, ENO1 and IL2RA with strong induction at higher adapter concentrations and only slight increases at 5 ng/ml. Interestingly, MX1 expression was strongly induced in AdCAR T cells treated with 5 ng/mL and 50 ng/mL. Overall, the relative expression of highly activated AdCAR T cells was comparable to that of AdCAR T cells co-cultured with OCI-AML2 and anti-CD33-AM (Fig. 6D).

Taken together, activation-induced genes identified via scRNAseq were expressed independently of adapter specificity or target cell line, and gene expression strength correlated with cytotoxicity.

AdCAR T cells and CAR T cells demonstrate different activation kinetics

Expression of NME1, RRM2, ENO1, HSP90AB1, IL2RA, TUBA1B and MX1 was comparable between AdCAR T cells treated with 500 ng/mL adapter and CAR T cells. However, analysis of differentially expressed genes revealed distinct transcriptional programs (Fig. 7AB). Strikingly, a preferential expression of GZMB in highly activated AdCAR T cells was observed, while GZMA and GZMK expression was observed in CAR T cells. Previous studies have shown that different granzymes exhibit distinct activation kinetics [39]. To explore this further, a consensus biomarker signature for T cell activation identified by Rade and colleagues [40] was applied. This signature includes time-resolved gene expression profiles ranging from M1 (sustained suppressed) through M2 and M3 (intermediate activation states) to M5 (late activation). Interestingly, CD8+ CAR T cells exhibited the strongest expression of genes associated with late T cell activation, while CD8+ AdCAR T cells treated with 500 ng/mL AM showed gene expression indicative of both intermediate and late activation after 24 h of co-culture (Fig. 7C). CD4+ AdCAR T cells co-cultured with 500 ng/mL adapter showed transcriptional signatures indicative of intermediate to late T-cell activation. However, CD4+ CAR T cells lacked a gene signature associated with the T-cell activation consensus sequence. This may be due to activation-induced inhibitory signaling, reflected by the expression of CISH and SOCS1 (Supplementary Figure 5B: CD4+ CAR vs. CD4+ UTD), suggesting faster activation kinetics compared to CD8+ CAR T cells.

DEGs with p_val_adj < 0.05 and avg_log2FC > 0.6 or <0.6 are highlighted in blue. (B) Heatmap displaying scaled expression of T cell activation biomarker signatures [40]: M1: (sustained suppressed), M2 and M3 (intermediate activated), and M5 (late activated). (C) (Ad)CAR T cell cytotoxicity after 24 h co-culture with OCI-AML2 cells in presence of various adapter concentrations at an E:T ratio of 2:1. Growth of target cells was monitored at 2 h intervals

To investigate whether the faster activation kinetics was also related to differences in functional outcomes, (Ad)CAR T cell cytotoxicity was monitored over time. CAR T cells achieved 50% lysis of OCI-AML2 cells within 15 h, whereas AdCAR T cells required 19 h in presence of 500 ng/mL AM (Fig. 7D).

These findings suggest that the differences between AdCAR and CAR T cells can be attributed to distinct activation kinetics, with CD4+ CAR T cells demonstrating faster activation compared to their CD8+ CAR T cells.

Discussion

While clinical success of CAR T cell therapy has been primarily achieved in hematological malignancies, its effectiveness remains limited in heterogeneous cancers such as acute myeloid leukemia and solid tumors. These limitations underscore the need for controllable, adaptable, and tunable CAR T cells to address challenges like toxicity management and antigen escape. In this study, the AdCAR platform was utilized in which CARs do not directly bind the tumor-associated antigens but instead a specific, chemically conjugated epitope [15] on an antigen binding moiety such as a Fab molecule. This design allows AdCAR T cells to be switched on and off through the administration of adapter molecules, providing a mechanism for precise control of T cell activity. Additionally, multitargeting of tumors can be achieved by administering adapters with different specificities either simultaneously or sequentially. For example, simultaneous targeting of CD33, CD38 and CD371 in a patient-derived AML xenograft model using the anti-LLE AdCAR platform showed superior tumor control compared to mono-targeting, underscoring the potential of multitargeting to mitigate antigen escape in AML [19]. Similarly, sequential targeting of CD33 and CLEC12A has been shown to improve control of primary AML cells [22].

Adapter-dependent specific target cell lysis and cytokine secretion have been reported for several AdCAR platforms [13–16, 41]. However, the mechanism of adapter-dependent AdCAR T cell activation had not been fully elucidated. In this study, scRNA sequencing was performed on AdCAR T cells co-cultured with the AML cell line OCI-AML2 in presence of varying adapter concentrations (500 ng/mL, 10 ng/mL, 5 ng/mL or in absence of adapter). Robust activation of AdCAR T cells was observed at 500 ng/mL adapter, as evidenced by target cell lysis, CD137 and CD69 expression, and cytokine secretion. Half-maximal cytolytic activity and activation were observed at adapter concentrations of 10 ng/mL and 5 ng/mL, respectively.

Pseudotime analysis revealed a progression from an unactivated T cell state to a highly active and proliferative state, providing a high-resolution map of AdCAR T cell activation dynamics. Among the highly expressed genes in activated AdCAR T cells were metabolism-related transcripts. Adapter-dependent expression of ENO1, a key glycolytic enzyme [42], was confirmed via qRT-PCR. To further investigate if AdCAR T cells undergo glycolysis or oxidative phosphorylation, metabolic flow cytometry was employed. Interestingly, no major difference in the upregulation of glycolysis or oxidative phosphorylation was observed. Instead, a shift towards an overall higher metabolic activity was detected, suggesting that adapter concentration could modulate the global metabolic fitness of AdCAR T cells. In accordance with these findings, other studies have shown that, while aerobic glycolysis is the dominant metabolic pathway, oxidative phosphorylation is enhanced as well [43, 44].

One key feature of activated T cells is their rapid proliferation and clonal expansion, which was evident in our dataset by the up-regulation of proliferation-associated pathways upon co-culture with 500 ng/mL anti-CD33-AM. Adapter-dependent activation of AdCAR T cells was also associated with the expression of RRM2, TUBA1B and NME1. RRM2 is a ribonucleotide reductase that plays a crucial role in converting nucleotides to deoxynucleotides, a critical step in DNA synthesis [37]. TUBA1B encodes Tubulin 1α, which is involved in cell division and T cell polarization upon activation. NME1, initially identified as tumor suppressor gene [45] was later recognized as T cell activation marker [46], and its expression was also found to be activation-dependent in our study. Finally, HSP90AB1, which encodes a molecular chaperone involved in protein stabilization and folding, has been implicated in supporting T cell effector function by stabilizing key signaling proteins such as AKT [47, 48]. Its activation-dependent expression found in this study further highlights its potential role in maintaining T cell responses.

An intriguing observation was the progression of unactivated T cells to a highly active and proliferative state through an intermediate interferon-responsive state. AdCAR T cells co-cultured at low adapter concentrations were highly enriched in the interferon response signature (Fig. 4). The upregulation of interferon-induced gene expression (e.g. MX1, IFI16, IFITM1) was independent of target cell line or adapter specificity (Fig. 6). In line with this finding, Szabo et al. identified an enrichment of an interferon response module during T cell activation and demonstrated that this state is part of an intermediate activation state driven by TCR-triggered IFN-γ production [46]. The IFN-responsive transition state has also been described in T cells activated by the bispecific T cell engager blinatumomab. Interestingly, Huo et al. correlated the gene signature identified in in vitro co-cultures with clinical responses in blinatumomab-treated patients. However, while their analysis was limited to a single concentration, our study explored a broader range of adapter concentrations [49]. This comprehensive characterization of the interferon-responsive state provides a mechanistic framework for optimizing AdCAR T cell activation, avoiding exhaustion and potentially identifying optimal adapter concentrations for therapy.

The gene expression profiles of CAR and AdCAR T cells co-cultured with tumor cells in presence of a high adapter concentrations correlated well. No significant differences in the expression of the identified hallmark genes were detected via qRT-PCR. Strikingly, a preferential expression of GZMB was observed in highly activated AdCAR T cells, whereas GZMA and GZMK were dominantly expressed in CAR T cells. Previous studies have shown that the expression of different granzymes follows distinct kinetics [39], with GZMB expressed early after T cell activation and delayed expression of GZMA. This suggests that AdCAR T cells and CAR T cells may exhibit different activation kinetics rather than differences in signaling machinery, which could inform the design of next-generation AdCAR therapies. Moreover, analysis of a consensus gene expression signature for T cell activation identified by Rade et al. [40] confirmed an intermediate/ late transcriptomic profile of AdCAR T cells treated with 500 ng/mL AM, while CAR T cells exhibited a late activation gene signature (Fig. 7). Two mechanisms could contribute to this observation: first, the difference in the antibody-derived binding domains and second, the influence of the adapter on the immunological synapse formation. While the immunological synapse formation has not yet been analyzed for AdCAR T cells, it is known from CAR T cells as well as bispecific T cell engagers that the closer the immunological space, the better the T cell performs [50–52]. These hypotheses warrant further investigation in future studies but could provide valuable insights into the biology of (Ad)CAR T cells, leveraging further optimization.

While our findings provide valuable insights into the adapter-dependent activation of AdCAR T cells, it is important to acknowledge limitations of this study. The transcriptomic dataset focuses on initial (Ad)CAR T cell activation in vitro. It was shown that increasing the adapter concentration during co-culture can restore AdCAR T cell function, resulting in improved tumor control [22]. However, the long-term functional consequences of suboptimal activation periods on both AdCAR T cells and tumor cells need to be further explored. While increasing the adapter concentration may result in improved tumor control, prolonged phases of limited immune pressure could also promote adaptive tumor resistance mechanisms such as PD-L1 upregulation [53] or downregulation of the target antigen [54].

In addition, the adapter concentrations stated as optimal and sub-optimal were determined using target cell lines expressing high levels of target antigen in vitro. In patients antigen expression is expected to show greater variability, including both intra- and inter-patient heterogeneity. Further, pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of the adapter will be important factors contributing to AdCAR T cell activity in vivo. Therefore, adapter concentrations required to achieve potent AdCAR activity can differ between in vitro and in vivo settings.

Although toxicity could not be directly evaluated in this study, previous studies showed that intermediate adapter doses could balance efficacy and limit on-target off-tumor reactivity of AdCAR T cells [15, 19].

Future in vivo studies in humanized models should therefore validate these findings, particularly under challenging conditions like solid tumors and upon repetitive re-challenge, to reliably assess both efficacy and on-target, off-tumor toxicity.

Still, a hallmark gene signature for gradual T cell activation was identified using the AdCAR platform. This gene signature could be utilized in other systems relying on T cell engagers as an additional readout to determine optimal engager dose, thereby aiding in the refinement of therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, the adapter-dependent metabolic shift of AdCAR T cells shown in this study highlights a critical link between activation state and metabolic fitness, which could be leveraged to enhance T cell persistence and anti-tumor activity in immunosuppressive environments and offer a roadmap for developing safer, more effective, and adaptable immunotherapies for heterogeneous cancers like AML and solid tumors.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that AdCAR T cells can be precisely modulated by the adapter, enabling tailored activation. The identification of adapter-specific gene signatures allows for distinguishing between highly activated and sub-optimally activated AdCAR T cells, facilitating the adjustment of adapter doses. These characteristics are useful for tracking AdCAR T cell activity, enabling fine-tuning of adapter dosing and optimizing AdCAR T cell efficacy.
