Evaluation of leukocyte depletion filters for the removal of EpCAM-positive tumor cells during oncologic intraoperative blood salvage using CATUVAB®: an exploratory feasibility study
Authors: Simon Mathis, Gabriel Putzer, Lisa Schnegg, Peter Ruf, Claudia Kellermann, Jonas Aniorte Jimenez, Horst Lindhofer, Judith Martini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntraoperative blood salvage (IBS) reduces exposure to allogeneic blood but is accompanied by concerns in oncologic surgery regarding the potential reinfusion of residual tumor cells and subsequent metastatic dissemination. The CATUVAB® procedure was developed to eliminate EpCAM-positive tumor cells from autologous erythrocyte concentrates (EC). As CATUVAB® requires integration of a leukocyte depletion filter (LDF), the present study aimed to evaluate whether three different commercially available LDF meet predefined safety and efficacy criteria within this procedure.MethodsIn this prospective ex vivo study, intraoperatively collected blood from patients undergoing major oncologic surgery was processed using the CATUVAB® procedure in combination with one of three LDF (Fresenius BioR Flex AT, Haemonetics US RS1, Puriblood LRW-50–04-PS). The primary endpoint was depletion of EpCAM-positive tumor cells. Secondary endpoints included residual catumaxomab levels in the final EC and changes in proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, IFN-γ). Removal of EpCAM/CD133 double-positive cancer stem cells was explored in a subgroup.ResultsThirty-one patients were analyzed. EpCAM-positive tumor cells were detected in intraoperative blood in a substantial proportion of samples, with marked interindividual variability in tumor cell burden. After CATUVAB® processing and final filtration, no EpCAM-positive tumor cells were detectable in any EC, irrespective of the LDF used. EpCAM/CD133 double-positive cancer stem cells were also eliminated in the subgroup. Residual catumaxomab was detectable in a proportion of ECs but consistently remained below the predefined safety threshold of 70 ng per EC. IL-6 and IL-8 levels were markedly reduced during processing across all filter types, whereas tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) were largely below detection limits.ConclusionThe present study demonstrates that the CATUVAB® procedure, in combination with different leukocyte depletion filters, achieved effective removal of EpCAM-positive tumor cells, accompanied by low residual catumaxomab levels and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine concentrations. These findings support the feasibility of this approach; however, given the limited sample size and exploratory design, they should be considered preliminary and require confirmation in larger studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-03994-7.Keywords: Intraoperative blood salvage, Oncologic surgery, EpCAM-positive tumor cells, Leukocyte depletion filter, Autologous blood transfusion

Introduction

Major surgical interventions are often associated with significant blood loss, frequently necessitating allogeneic blood transfusion. Intraoperative blood salvage (IBS) represents an effective alternative, as it reduces transfusion-related risks, preserves oxygen-carrying capacity, obviates the need for cross-matching and may contribute to cost reduction [1–3]. However, in patients undergoing oncologic surgery, concerns have been raised regarding the potential risk of re-infusing viable circulating tumor cells with metastatic capacity. Consequently, the re-transfusion of IBS-processed autologous erythrocyte concentrate (EC) is either not recommended by the respective guidelines or requires irradiation of the processed EC before it can be safely re-transfused [4].

EpCAM is a tumor-associated antigen expressed on the majority of epithelial carcinomas, including high-grade bladder, lung, pancreatic, gastric, colorectal, prostate and ovarian cancers [5, 6]. CATUVAB® exploits the selectivity of catumaxomab, a biologically engineered, trifunctional bispecific IgG antibody with high affinity for EpCAM-positive tumor cells and specificity for CD3-positive T cells [6, 7]. In addition, the Fc region of Catumaxomab binds to Fc-gamma receptor type I, IIa and III positive cells such as monocytes and natural killer cells [8, 9]. CATUVAB® is designed for use in combination with a leukocyte depletion filter (LDF). In conjunction with catumaxomab, the LDF facilitates the removal of tumor cell–immune cell complexes formed during antibody-mediated targeting that are not eliminated during the centrifugation step of the IBS device [10].

The present FILTER study was designed to evaluate whether LDF with a specified leukocyte removal efficiency of > 95% adequately meet predefined safety and efficacy requirements when integrated into the CATUVAB® procedure. Therefore, three commercially available LDF were investigated: the Fresenius Kabi LDF (BioR Flex AT), the Haemonetics US LDF (RS1), and the Puriblood LDF (LRW-50–04-PS).

Methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Innsbruck (approval number 1454/2021) and conducted in accordance with the Declarations of Helsinki.

The purpose of this investigation was to compare three LDF [Fresenius BioR flex AT (FILTER 1), Haemonetics US LDF (RS1) (FILTER 2), or Puriblood LDF (LRW-50–04-PS) (FILTER 3)] within the CATUVAB® procedure regarding their ability to remove EpCAM-positive tumor cells from intraoperatively collected blood to enable safe autotransfusion. The primary endpoint (applicable only to EpCAM-positive samples) was the quantitative depletion of EpCAM-positive tumor cells achieved by each LDF. The study was designed as an exploratory feasibility investigation to assess technical performance and integration of different leukocyte depletion filters within the CATUVAB® procedure. No formal sample size calculation was performed, and the study was not powered for comparative, superiority, or equivalence analyses between filter types.

Secondary endpoints included the assessment of catumaxomab removal to a non-critical level (≤ 70 ng total in the EC, a threshold derived from preclinical pharmacodynamic data and clinical observations from a prior CATUVAB® study) [11] and the quantification of cytokine concentrations throughout the procedure to evaluate potential cytokine reduction during IBS in combination with the CATUVAB® process. These secondary analyses were conducted irrespective of EpCAM status and were therefore also applied to EpCAM-negative samples. An additional exploratory endpoint was the assessment of EpCAM/CD133 double-positive cancer stem cell removal achieved by CATUVAB® in combination with FILTER 1.

Patients (≥ 18 years) scheduled for major oncologic resections—including prostate, bladder, gastric, ovarian, pancreatic, colorectal, non-small-cell lung cancer, or peritoneal carcinomatosis procedures—with anticipated substantial intraoperative blood loss were asked to participate in this study. Written informed consent was obtained. Exclusion criteria comprised recent chemotherapy (< 1 week), lymphocytopenia (< 1.0 × 109/L), Child–Pugh class C cirrhosis, thrombocytopenia (< 30,000/μL), catumaxomab contraindications, and PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection prior to surgery.

During surgery, intraoperative blood was collected in an ex-vivo collection reservoir (SORIN XTRA Collection Set Tx, LivaNova, Munich, Germany, or ATR 120 Sammelreservoir, Fresenius Kabi, Bad Homburg, Germany). CATUVAB® was then added and processed with an IBS device (SORIN XTRA, LivaNova, Munich, Germany, or CATSmart, Fresenius Kabi, Bad Homburg, Germany). CATUVAB® induces the formation of cell aggregates composed of leukocytes and EpCAM-positive tumor cells, which are subsequently removed from the blood–diluent mixture through centrifugation via the IBS device and passage through the assigned LDF (Fig. 1). The CATUVAB® kit contains a pre-filled syringe with catumaxomab antibody, a 6R vial with 0.9% NaCl solution, two sterile disposable 2 mL syringes, two cannulas, and one of the three LDF evaluated in this study. The specific filter used for each procedure was determined by the availability of the respective filter type at the time of surgery.

Sampling procedure. 1) Sampling from blood container and 2) Sampling from EC after LDF

These filters were selected based on predefined technical criteria to ensure suitability for the CATUVAB® procedure. These criteria included a leukocyte depletion efficiency exceeding 95% and a maximum filter housing hold-up volume of 40 mL. According to technical documentation provided by Fresenius Kabi, the BioR flex LDF achieved fewer than 0.5 × 105 residual leukocytes and a hold-up volume of 33 mL. The Puriblood LDF and the Haemonetics US LDF exhibited comparable specifications.

Sampling was performed at two defined time points (Fig. 1): (1) from the collection reservoir prior to the addition of the trifunctional antibody catumaxomab and (2) from the EC after passage through the LDF. The analyses performed on these samples are outlined in the following sections.

Detection and quantification of EpCAM-positive tumor cells

To evaluate the presence of EpCAM-positive tumor cells, 40 mL of intraoperatively collected blood were sampled prior to the administration of catumaxomab (Fig. 1, Sample 1). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), including potential tumor cells, were isolated by Ficoll density gradient centrifugation, followed by cytospin preparation and cell counting using a Neubauer hemocytometer. Depending on cell yield, up to 1.5 × 10⁶ isolated cells (PBMCs and tumor cells combined) were centrifuged onto six microscope slides (250,000 cells per slide, 12 mm spot size), and EpCAM-positive tumor cells were visualized using FLEX IHC slides (Dako/Agilent Technologies). All samples were anonymized prior to analysis and evaluated by a single experienced pathologist blinded to sample identity and processing stage; quality control was ensured by inclusion of positive and negative controls in each staining run.

If EpCAM-positive tumor cells were detected in the intraoperative samples, a corresponding 40 mL sample of the final EC obtained after the CATUVAB® procedure was subjected to the same preparatory workflow (Fig. 1, Sample 2). For EC samples, the entire isolated cell fraction was processed: up to 70,000 cells were centrifuged onto a single 6 mm spot, whereas samples containing > 70,000 to 250,000 cells were applied to 12 mm spots. Visualization of EpCAM-positive tumor cells was again performed using FLEX IHC slides.

In the subgroup treated with FILTER 1, additional CD133 immunostaining was applied to intraoperative blood and corresponding EC samples to identify EpCAM/CD133 double-positive cancer stem cells before and after CATUVAB® processing. For the assessment of EpCAM/CD133 double-positive cells, a dual immunofluorescence staining approach was applied using an anti-EpCAM antibody (clone HO3, TRION Research) and an anti-CD133 antibody (clone W6B3C1, Becton Dickinson). Staining was performed on the same cytospin preparations, and double-positive cells were identified based on co-localization of both fluorescence signals. Quantification was conducted using a combination of automated image analysis and manual verification. Further methodological details are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Quantification of antibody levels in the final EC

To evaluate the secondary endpoint, residual catumaxomab concentrations in the final EC were quantified using a validated two-site ELISA. Capture was achieved with an anti-rat IgG light-chain–specific antibody (LA1B12, Trion Research, Puchheim-Munich, Germany), followed by detection using a biotin-labeled anti-mouse IgG2a-specific antibody (BD Pharmingen, San Diego, CA, USA). Streptavidin–β-galactosidase and its substrate, chlorphenolred-β-D-galactopyranosid (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany), were subsequently applied, and the resulting colorimetric reaction was measured at 570 nm. Catumaxomab concentrations were calculated by interpolation from a standard curve. The assay featured a lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) of 125 pg/mL and an upper limit of 4,000 pg/mL. All samples were diluted 1:2 and analyzed in duplicate.

Quantification of cytokine concentrations

To evaluate differences in the concentrations of interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), TNF-α, and IFN-γ between intraoperatively collected blood and the final EC, samples were obtained from the blood reservoir (Fig. 1, Sample 1) and from the processed EC (Fig. 1, Sample 2). Blood samples designated for cytokine analysis were centrifuged, and the resulting plasma was collected, frozen within 1–2 h at –20 °C, and sent to Synlab (MVZ Laboratory, Munich, Germany) for quantification. Cytokine measurements were performed using Luminex® Multiplex technology with magnetic microsphere beads. This assay employs microbeads with unique fluorescent signatures coated with cytokine-specific capture antibodies, enabling laser-based detection and quantification of the target cytokines.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 for Mac (version 10.6.1, GraphPad Software, September 8, 2025). Owing to small sample sizes and non-normal data distributions, all results are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). For interleukin measurements below the limit of detection, values were imputed as one half of the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) for subsequent statistical analyses.

Paired comparisons between intraoperatively collected blood samples and the corresponding final EC were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. All tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For exploratory cytokine analyses, p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. A large language model (ChatGPT, OpenAI; GPT-5.2) was used exclusively for language editing and stylistic refinement of the manuscript.

Results

During the study period, 58 patients met the inclusion criteria and provided written informed consent for participation in the study. In 26 cases, intraoperative blood loss was below the threshold required to obtain the minimum blood volume necessary for subsequent analysis. In one case, clotting of the Filter (Fresenius BioR Flex LDF) occurred, rendering further analysis impossible. Ultimately, 31 patients were included in the final analysis. Fifteen were assigned to FILTER 1 (Fresenius BioR Flex LDF), 8 to FILTER 2 (Haemonetics US LDF), and 8 to FILTER 3 (Puriblood LDF). The study cohort consisted predominantly of patients with prostate carcinoma (n = 21), followed by pancreatic carcinoma (n = 6), gastric carcinoma (n = 3), and rectal carcinoma (n = 1).

Within the study population, EpCAM-positive tumor cells were detected in 17 of the analyzed samples (54.8%), with a median of 5,835 (3,370–20,080). Varying detection frequencies were observed across the three filter groups: In the FILTER 1 cohort, EpCAM-positive cells were identified in 7 of 15 intraoperative blood samples (46.7%). In the FILTER 2 group, EpCAM-positive cells were observed in 7 of 8 samples (87.5%) and in the FILTER 3 group, EpCAM-positive tumor cells were found in 3 of 8 samples (37.5%). Across the three filter groups, substantial variability was observed in the number of EpCAM-positive tumor cells detected in intraoperatively collected blood prior to CATUVAB® processing. In the FILTER 1 cohort, the median number of EpCAM-positive cells identified in the wound blood reservoir was 11,017 (3,459–29,387; range 3,280—68,846). In the FILTER 2 group, a median of 5,573 (2,175–19,375) EpCAM-positive cells was detected (range 680–20,785). For FILTER 3, the median count reached 5,835 (1,05–10,260) cells (range 105—10,260). Following completion of the CATUVAB® procedure, including final filtration with the respective LDF, no EpCAM-positive tumor cells were detectable in any of the final EC products. Baseline tumor cell counts reached up to 68,846 cells, indicating that the absence of detectable cells after processing corresponds to a substantial multi-log reduction.

An additional analysis of EpCAM/CD133 double-positive cancer stem cells was performed exclusively within the FILTER 1 cohort. Among the seven samples that were initially positive for EpCAM-positive tumor cells, five also exhibited detectable EpCAM/CD133 double-positive cells in the intraoperative blood reservoir, with a median count of 1,354 (534.5–2,500) (range 338—3,100) cells. Following CATUVAB® processing and filtration in the FILTER 1 cohort, EpCAM/CD133 double-positive cancer stem cells were not detectable in the final EC products.

Assessment of residual catumaxomab in the final EC was performed across all filter groups. The lower limit of quantification for the assay was 125 pg/mL. In the FILTER 1 cohort (Fresenius BioR Flex LDF), residual antibody was detectable in 4 of 15 EC samples, with total catumaxomab levels ranging from 8.0 ng to 35.6 ng per EC. In the FILTER 2 cohort (Haemonetics US LDF), residual antibody was detected in 2 of 8 samples, with measured amount of 35.4 ng and 37.4 ng. In the FILTER 3 cohort (Puriblood LDF), residual catumaxomab was detected in 5 of 8 samples, with levels ranging from 10.55 ng to 33.84 ng. Importantly, all measured values remained well below the predefined safety threshold of 70 ng per EC.

Across the filter groups, the CATUVAB® procedure resulted in a marked reduction of IL-6 levels from intraoperatively collected blood to the final EC. In the FILTER 1 cohort (Fresenius BioR Flex LDF), the median IL-6 total amount in unprocessed blood was 22 (4.75–724) ng, which decreased to 4.75 (4.75–4.75) ng in the final EC, corresponding to a 4.6-fold reduction (p < 0.001). In the FILTER 2 cohort (Haemonetics US LDF), IL-6 levels declined from 7.85 (5.225–20.58) ng in the intraoperative reservoir to 0.785 (0.2525–1.43) ng in the final EC, yielding a tenfold reduction (p = 0.008). In the FILTER 3 cohort (Puriblood LDF), IL-6 total amounts were reduced from 2,714 (4.750–3,221) ng before CATUVAB® processing to 12.74 (4.75–50.76) ng in the final EC, corresponding to a 213-fold reduction (p = 0.016).

A comparable attenuation was observed of IL-8 following CATUVAB® processing across the different LDF. Within the FILTER 1 cohort, IL-8 total amounts measured in unprocessed wound blood averaged 19 (7–61) ng, reducing to 0.76 (0.54–2.09) ng in the final EC (a 25-fold decrease; p < 0.001). In the FILTER 2 cohort, median IL-8 levels declined from 31 (15.45–99) ng prior to processing to 1.33 (0.1125–7.5) ng after filtration, representing to a 23.3-fold reduction (p = 0.008). The most pronounced decrease was observed in the FILTER 3 cohort, where IL-8 total amounts decreased from 177.6 (13.73–234) ng before CATUVAB® treatment to 4.589 (0.994–7.232) ng in the final EC, representing a 38.7-fold reduction (p = 0.016). The observed reductions in IL-6 and IL-8 across all filter groups remained statistically significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

TNF-α concentrations were below the limit of detection in nearly all samples prior to the CATUVAB® procedure, with the exception of two samples. IFN-γ levels were below the detection threshold in all but one sample. Detailed individual values are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that the CATUVAB® procedure, in combination with all three evaluated LDF, achieved complete removal of EpCAM-positive tumor cells from intraoperatively collected blood, irrespective of substantial interindividual variability in baseline tumor cell burden. In addition, EpCAM/CD133 double-positive cancer stem cells were fully eliminated in the subgroup analyzed. Residual catumaxomab levels in the final EC remained consistently below the predefined safety threshold, while a marked reduction of proinflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-6 and IL-8, was observed across all filter types, indicating effective immunological and oncological decontamination of autologous blood products.

IBS has been associated with several advantages in major surgical procedures, including a reduction in allogeneic blood transfusion requirements and improved cost-effectiveness [1, 2]. However, its use in oncologic surgery has long been debated because of concerns that reinfusion of viable tumor cells might facilitate hematogenous tumor dissemination [12–14]. The CATUVAB® system was developed to specifically mitigate this risk, and its safety and efficacy were recently demonstrated in the REMOVE trial [11]. The CATUVAB® device is applied in combination with a LDF, enabling the removal of cellular aggregates formed by the bispecific trifunctional antibody catumaxomab (anti-EpCAM × anti-CD3), which simultaneously binds EpCAM-positive tumor cells, CD3-positive T cells, and Fc-γ receptor expressing accessory immune cells, such as e.g. monocytes and natural killer cells. The present findings across all three LDF evaluation cohorts, the Fresenius BioR Flex LDF, the Haemonetics US LDF and the Puriblood LDF, demonstrate complete depletion of EpCAM-positive tumor cells from the final EC. This represents a critical outcome, as the absence of detectable EpCAM-positive cells in the reinfused product substantially reduces the risk of inadvertent tumor cell dissemination associated with autologous transfusion [15, 16]. It should be noted that the absence of detectable EpCAM-positive tumor cells is limited by the sensitivity of the applied detection method, and residual cells below the detection threshold cannot be excluded.

The variability in EpCAM-positive detection rates across filter groups should be interpreted with caution. EpCAM expression differs across epithelial malignancies, and patient inclusion was based on predefined surgical indications without prior knowledge of EpCAM status [5]. The predominance of prostate cancer reflects the underlying case distribution rather than a study-specific selection process, which may have resulted in imbalances between groups. In addition, filter assignment was determined by availability and not by predefined criteria, so allocation bias cannot be excluded. Given the small sample size, the observed differences in baseline EpCAM detection rates prior to CATUVAB® processing are therefore most likely attributable to biological variability and sampling effects rather than systematic differences between filter groups.

While complete removal of EpCAM-positive tumor cells constitutes the primary oncologic safety endpoint of the CATUVAB® procedure, an equally important aspect is the pharmacological safety of the processed EC, particularly regarding residual catumaxomab levels.

A key safety feature of the CATUVAB® system is its ability to achieve minimal or undetectable residual catumaxomab levels in the final EC. During the CATUVAB® procedure, 2.5 µg of catumaxomab is added to the intraoperative blood reservoir, with the total blood volume processed per IBS cycle limited to 1,500 mL. Antibody administration is initiated only after a minimum of 400 mL of wound blood has been collected, thereby allowing sufficient interaction between tumor cells, immune cells, and catumaxomab. This interaction is limited to 2.5 h to allow only the binding of the antibody to its different binding partners and thereby crosslinking the different cell types. Subsequent centrifugation and washing steps within the IBS procedure and final filtration effectively remove antibody–tumor cell–immune cell complexes. In the present study, residual catumaxomab concentrations in the final EC ranged from below the lower limit of quantification (≤ 125 pg/mL) to 35.6 ng (Fresenius BioR Flex LDF), 37.4 ng (Haemonetics US LDF), and 33.8 (Puriblood LDF) remaining well below the predefined safety threshold of 70 ng per EC. The predefined safety threshold of 70 ng per EC is supported by pharmacokinetic considerations and clinical data. Following reinfusion, this amount would result in systemic concentrations well below levels required to induce measurable cytotoxic effects, as described by Ruf et al. [7]. In addition, clinical data from the REMOVE study showed residual catumaxomab levels below or within this range without treatment-related safety signals, supporting the threshold as a conservative safety margin. These findings are consistent with the REMOVE certification study and further support the low risk of systemic catumaxomab exposure, thereby reinforcing the favorable safety profile of the CATUVAB® procedure [11].

Beyond the removal of differentiated EpCAM-positive tumor cells and the favorable pharmacological safety profile, additional insights were gained from an exploratory assessment of cancer stem cell contamination in the FILTER 1 cohort using dual immunostaining for EpCAM and CD133. Among the seven samples in which EpCAM-positive tumor cells were detected in the intraoperative blood reservoir, five also contained EpCAM/CD133 double-positive tumor cells, accounting for approximately 5% to 30% of the total tumor cell population. Following completion of the CATUVAB® procedure and final filtration with the Fresenius BioR Flex LDF, EpCAM/CD133 double-positive cells were no longer detectable in the final EC. These findings suggest that the CATUVAB® system is capable not only of eliminating differentiated EpCAM-positive tumor cells but also of effectively depleting cancer stem cell populations, which are considered to play a central role in tumor recurrence and metastatic progression [17, 18].

In addition to oncologic and pharmacological safety considerations, the inflammatory cytokine profile of the processed EC represents a further relevant aspect, particularly in patients with malignancies, in whom baseline cytokine levels are frequently elevated as a consequence of tumor-associated inflammation [19]. Moreover, the appearance of perioperative cytokines vary by different intensities of surgical trauma, types of anesthesia and anesthetic agents. Generally, inflammatory cytokines play an important role in postoperative organ dysfunction including central nervous system, cardiovascular, lung and kidney injury [20].

Therefore, in the present study, concentrations of IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, and IFN-γ were assessed in intraoperatively collected blood and in the final EC. Pro-inflammatory cytokine analysis further supports the safety of the CATUVAB® system, as marked reductions in IL-6 and IL-8 levels were consistently observed across all evaluated LDF compared with unprocessed intraoperative blood. Given the low absolute cytokine amounts detected in the final EC and their expected 2,000- to 3,000-fold dilution upon reinfusion into the systemic circulation—assuming an average plasma volume of 2–3 L within a total blood volume of 5–7 L—this residual cytokine levels are considered unlikely to be clinically relevant. In contrast, TNF-α and IFN-γ concentrations were already below the lower limit of quantification in the vast majority of samples prior to CATUVAB® processing, with only isolated low values detected in individual patients. The interpretation of cytokine reduction requires caution, as standard IBS alone can reduce cytokine levels through washing and centrifugation [21, 22]. As no control group without CATUVAB® was included, the specific contribution of CATUVAB® cannot be determined. The observed reductions in IL-6 and IL-8 should therefore be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

Despite these encouraging findings, several limitations of the present FILTER studies should be acknowledged. First, the present studies were conducted in an ex vivo setting using intraoperatively collected wound blood. While this approach allows for standardized and controlled assessment of LDF performance within the CATUVAB® procedure, it may not fully reflect the biological complexity of in vivo reinfusion conditions. However, the in vivo safety and feasibility of the CATUVAB® system have already been evaluated in the REMOVE certification study [11].

Second, the sample sizes within the individual filter cohorts were relatively small, ranging from eight to 15 patients per group, thereby substantially limiting statistical power and precluding formal comparative or equivalence analyses between filter types. No predefined equivalence margins were specified, and the study was not powered to demonstrate non-inferiority or equivalence. As no control group without CATUVAB® was included, the specific contribution of CATUVAB® cannot be determined. Accordingly, the present investigation should be interpreted as an exploratory feasibility study aimed at generating preliminary evidence regarding the technical compatibility and functional integration of different LDF within the CATUVAB® procedure, rather than establishing comparative effectiveness. Confirmation of these findings in adequately powered studies with larger patient cohorts and predefined statistical margins is therefore required. Third, in one case, clotting of the leukocyte depletion filter occurred, a phenomenon that has been described in the literature and may be influenced by patient-related factors. In clinical practice, such events can be managed by replacing the filter and continuing the procedure.

In summary, the present exploratory feasibility study demonstrates that the CATUVAB® procedure, in combination with different leukocyte depletion filters, achieved effective removal of EpCAM-positive tumor cells under the investigated conditions, accompanied by below limit of quantification or low residual catumaxomab levels and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine concentrations. These findings are consistent with previous data from the larger REMOVE study [11].

However, given the limited sample size and the exploratory design, the results should be interpreted as preliminary. Further studies in larger, adequately powered cohorts are required to confirm these findings and to establish the comparative performance and clinical applicability of different filter systems within the CATUVAB® procedure.
