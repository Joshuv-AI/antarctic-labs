Associations of dietary live microbe intake with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in middle-aged and older US stroke survivors: a prospective cohort study from NHANES 2003–2018
Authors: Wei Chen, Qing Liu, Jing Fu, Jianghu Zhao, Ruoxi Huang, Qiansong He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe relationships of dietary live microbe intake (DLMI) with all-cause mortality (ACM) and cardiovascular mortality (CVM) in stroke patients remain unclear. This study examined the links of DLMI with ACM and CVM in stroke patients.MethodsA secondary analysis of the publicly available, fixed-cycle National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) dataset was performed in this study. As such, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. All eligible participants aged ≥ 40 years with complete data from the 2003–2018 survey cycles served as the analytical sample. The stroke history was based on self-report without further specification of subtype (ischemic vs. hemorrhagic). Participants were followed until December 31, 2019, with a median follow-up of 6.0 years. DLMI was estimated based on a single 24-hour dietary recall and classified into low, medium, and high intake categories as an indicator of a dietary pattern characterized by the consumption of live-microbe-rich foods. Cox models, stratified, and sensitivity analyses were employed to ascertain the links of DLMI with ACM and CVM in stroke patients.ResultsThis study included 1,313 stroke patients, among whom 539 experienced ACM and 209 experienced CVM. In the Cox models, compared with low DLMI, high DLMI had a lower incidence of ACM [Model 1: HR = 0.63 (95% CI: 0.44–0.91, p = 0.013); Model 2: HR = 0.61 (95% CI: 0.44–0.84, p = 0.003); Model 3: HR = 0.63 (95% CI: 0.46–0.88, p = 0.007)]. However, no significant association was observed between high DLMI and CVM. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses generally supported the robustness of these results.ConclusionHigher DLMI was associated with a lower risk of ACM among stroke survivors in this study. These exploratory findings highlight DLMI as a candidate for further investigation in dietary intervention strategies for this population. Future prospective studies are needed to establish its role and optimal implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01368-w.Keywords: Dietary live microbe intake, Stroke, Cardiovascular mortality, All-cause mortality

Introduction

Stroke is a principal cause of death and disability, posing a major threat to global people. According to the 2021 GBD study, there were over 69.94 million cases of stroke worldwide, with an incidence of 92.4/100,000 and a mortality rate of 44.2 /100,000 [1]. According to BRFSS (Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System) 2022 data, the prevalence of stroke in adults was 3.4% (median) in the United States, with the lowest prevalence in Puerto Rico (1.8%) and South Dakota (2.1%) and the highest prevalence in Arkansas (4.8%) [2]. In 2020, the age-standardized prevalence, incidence, and mortality rates of stroke in individuals ≥ 40 years in China were 2.6%, 505.2/100,000 person-years, and 343.4/100,000 person-years, respectively [3]. Known risk factors for stroke encompass smoking, obesity, diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, physical inactivity, and unhealthy diets [4]. Active intervention targeting these indicators is vital for reducing stroke incidence and mortality rates. Beyond these established factors, emerging evidence underscores the significant role of dietary and lifestyle components, including caffeine intake [5], overall dietary patterns [6], socioeconomic status [7], and systemic inflammation [8], in influencing stroke risk and post-stroke outcomes.

The gut microbiota-gut-brain axis has received increasing attention. Beneficial dietary live microbe intake (DLMI) helps maintain healthy gut microbiota and can exert immune-modulating effects, alleviate cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), and influence neuropsychological activities [9–11]. Unhealthy dietary patterns, such as high-fat, high-calorie Western diets, drive atherosclerosis and raise the risk of stroke [12], while Mediterranean diets, rich in fruits, nuts, vegetables, whole grains, and olive oil, benefit endothelial function and maintain cardiovascular health [13]. Dietary live microbes have metabolic activity and are ingested through food, mainly including probiotics, microbes in fermented foods, and symbiotic bacteria in certain unpasteurized foods. Research has indicated that probiotic injection into obese mice can reverse intestinal dysbiosis and inflammatory responses [14]. Probiotics help maintain health and ameliorate various diseases by modulating gut microbiota [15]. Nevertheless, the relationships of DLMI with all-cause mortality (ACM) and cardiovascular mortality (CVM) in stroke populations are elusive. Hence, this study analyzed the link of DLMI with mortality risks after stroke based on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database.

Method

Study population and ethics

The NHANES is initiated to investigate the health and nutrition of Americans. It gathers comprehensive data from approximately 5,000 Americans each year, including demographic information, socioeconomic conditions, dietary patterns, and overall health status. Data were collected during interviews and physical examinations, including physiological assessment and laboratory testing. The NHANES protocol was ratified by the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Committee, and all populations offered written informed consent.

This study enrolled 80,312 participants from the NHANES 2003–2018. We eliminated participants under 40 years (n = 50,731) and those who had not experienced a stroke (n = 27,846). Furthermore, after excluding individuals with missing dietary data, we further excluded those with missing data for other covariates (n = 422). Finally, 1,313 participants were enrolled. DLMI and categories

The 24-hour dietary recall data were utilized to assess DLMI and its categories. Standardized methods were applied for data collection, with participants providing detailed reports of all food and beverages consumed over the past 24 h. Trained interviewers used an automated data entry system to record details on each item, ensuring data comprehensiveness and accuracy. To accurately quantify live microbes (colony-forming units per gram, CFU/g) in food, the research team hired four experts (MLM, MES, RH, CH) in the fields of microbiology and food science, who had advanced degrees in related fields and extensive research experience. Their primary task was to evaluate 9,388 food entries and sort them into three groups based on the classification framework and CFU/g cutoff values established by Marco et al [16]: (1) Low (< 10⁴ CFU/g): primarily including pasteurized foods and other foods with low microbes. (2) Moderate (10⁴-10⁷ CFU/g): including foods with moderate microbes, such as unpeeled fresh fruits and vegetables. (3) High (> 10⁷ CFU/g): including unpasteurized fermented foods (yogurt and kimchi) and probiotic supplements. A consensus validation method was used for the classification process, including repeated evaluations, cross-referencing external microbial databases, and consulting external experts (Fred Breidt of the Agricultural Research Service) when data were uncertain, to guarantee the reliability and consistency of the classification. Based on the validation method of Sanders et al., populations were assigned to three groups based on the total DLMI in their 24-hour diet: (1) low intake group: only eating foods with low microbial levels; (2) medium intake group: eating foods with medium microbial levels but no foods with high microbial levels; (3) high intake group: eating at least one food with high microbial levels. This classification reflected dietary intake on a single 24-hour recall day. The comprehensive mapping of NHANES food codes to the DLMI categories derived from this expert evaluation is provided in File S1 and File S2 to ensure reproducibility.

The following interpretative note on the DLMI variable is critical: It is important to emphasize that the DLMI categories in this study are derived from a single 24-hour dietary recall. Consequently, this measure should be interpreted primarily as a pragmatic, behavior-based indicator of dietary patterns involving foods typically rich in live microbes, rather than as a precise quantification of ingested microbial dose. While it serves as a population-level marker for habitual consumption of such foods, its inherent limitations in assessing exact microbial exposure must be noted. This clarification is crucial for aligning the interpretation of our findings with the methodological approach.

Diagnosis

Stroke was determined by self-reported medical diagnosis. Anyone who answered yes to any of the questions “Did a doctor or health professional ask you at what age you were told you had a stroke?” “Have you ever been told you had a stroke?” were diagnosed with stroke.

Outcome ascertainment

ACM was determined based on records from the National Death Index before December 31, 2019, which was linked to the NHANES dataset. CVM was defined via ICD-10 codes I00-I09, I11, I13, I60-I69 and I20-I51.

Covariates

Based on previous reports and biological factors, known confounders for stroke were collected. Demographic characteristics (age, sex, marital status, ethnicity), educational level, smoking, alcohol consumption, body mass index (BMI), CVDs, secondary prevention medications for stroke (lipid-lowering agents, antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, and antihypertensive agents), hypertension, and diabetes status were documented through questionnaires and interviews.

BMI was obtained through physical examinations at mobile screening centers. Details of the relevant definitions were shown below: Race/ethnicity encompassed non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Mexican American, and others. Educational attainment consisted of below high school, high school, and over high school. Those who ever smoked more than 100 cigarettes were regarded as smokers, irrespective of whether they had quit smoking at the interview [17]. Those who consumed over 12 alcoholic drinks in the year were regarded as drinkers [18]. Marital status was classified into three groups: unmarried, widowed/divorced/separated, and married/cohabiting. BMI = weight (kg)/height squared (m2), widely used to estimate overweight/obesity status. Chest pain or discomfort was defined as: ever had any pain or discomfort in your/her/his chest? Hypertension was determined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, self-reported hypertension history, or taking antihypertensive medications [19]. Blood pressure was measured by experienced clinicians under standardized procedures, using a mercury sphygmomanometer to record three consecutive readings every 30 s. The average of all three readings served as the result. Diabetes was confirmed by a prior medical diagnosis, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% (47.5 mmol/mol), fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L), or plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) in the 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Prediabetes was defined as FPG levels of 100 to 125 mg/dL, blood glucose levels of 140 to 199 mg/dL two hours after an OGTT with a 75-gram glucose load, or HbA1c levels of 5.7% to 6.4% or 6.0% to 6.4% [20].

Statistical analyses

Since NHANES adopted a complex, multistage probability sampling design, all analyses accounted for this design by applying dietary day-one sample weights and incorporating stratification (SDMVSTRA) and clustering (SDMVPSU) variables using the survey package in R. Categorical variables were expressed as unweighted counts and weighted percentages, whereas continuous variables were described as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Comparisons between groups were conducted using the Rao–Scott chi-square test with second-order correction for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables.

To avoid multicollinearity, this study used the variance inflation factor (VIF) for testing. The results suggested that the VIF values of all variables were less than 5, indicating no obvious collinearity. Subsequently, multivariate Cox models were constructed, with hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) computed. Additionally, the Schoenfeld residual test was adopted to validate the assumption.

First, a crude Model 1 was established without variable adjustment. For Model 2, demographic variables (sex, age, and ethnicity) were controlled. For Model 3, we controlled all variables, including demographic variables, BMI, marital status, education, smoking, alcohol consumption, chest pain or discomfort, and secondary prevention medication for stroke. Given the examination of two primary outcomes (ACM and CVM), the issue of multiple comparisons was acknowledged. The results focused on the primary pre-specified outcome of ACM, while findings for CVM should be considered exploratory.

To evaluate the robustness of the results, two sensitivity analyses were implemented. First, an unweighted dataset was used to repeat the primary analysis process, thereby confirming the associations of DLMI with ACM and CVM. Second, participants who died within 2 years of follow-up were precluded to rule out potential reverse causality (i.e., the possibility that severe pre-existing illness influenced both diet and short-term mortality), and the associations were validated again. A two-sided p < 0.05 was deemed notable. All analyses were made utilizing R (4.5.0).

1,313 stroke patients aged 40 years or older were ultimately enrolled. During the follow-up period, 539 patients died, of whom 209 died from CVDs. The population had an average age of 68 years, with women accounting for 55.24%. No notable differences were noticed in age, race, education level, smoking, and ACM (p < 0.05 for all) (Table 1).

These subgroup findings should be interpreted with caution as they are exploratory and may be influenced by multiple comparisons and limited power in some strata.

Sensitivity analyses

In Cox regression models (Table S1), compared with low DLMI, high DLMI was statistically negatively associated with ACM [Model 1: 0.63 (0.41–0.95, p = 0.028); Model 2: 0.60 (0.41–0.87, p = 0.007); Model 3: 0.61 (0.42–0.88, p = 0.009)]. However, DLMI was not greatly linked with CVM (p > 0.05).

An unweighted Cox regression model was applied to all data (Table S2). Sensitivity analysis demonstrated consistent results, showing that, compared with low DLMI, high DLMI was strongly negatively associated with ACM [Model 1: 0.71 (0.54–0.94, p = 0.017); Model 2: 0.66 (0.50–0.88, p = 0.004); Model 3: 0.66 (0.50–0.89, p = 0.005)]. However, DLMI was not statistically linked with CVM (p > 0.05).

Notably, when interpreting these sensitivity analyses and main findings, it is important to acknowledge the examination of two primary outcomes (ACM and CVM). Therefore, the consistent and significant association observed for ACM across all models is the primary focus of our conclusions, while the non-significant findings for CVM should be considered exploratory. This approach underscores cautious interpretation of individual results and validation in future studies.

Discussion

In this analysis of 1,313 NHANES participants with a history of stroke, we observed that higher DLMI was significantly associated with a lower risk of ACM. This association remained robust across sensitivity and stratified analyses. Notably, it persisted even after excluding deaths within the first two years of follow-up, a key analysis that mitigates concerns about reverse causality. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale study to report an association between DLMI and mortality risk specifically in the stroke population. These findings suggest that dietary components rich in live microbes, such as probiotics and fermented foods, could be a relevant focus for further research in post-stroke nutritional management.

Our findings enrich the established understanding of modifiable factors, particularly diet, in stroke outcomes [21, 22]. Dietary patterns are recognized for their roles in stroke prognosis. For instance, Mediterranean diets are associated with a reduced risk, whereas Western patterns may elevate the risk [12, 23]. Within this context, our study highlights a specific, quantifiable dietary component—live microbe intake—that merits further investigation in stroke survivors.

The observed association might be understood within the framework of the gut-brain axis and the role of gut microbiota in systemic health [15, 24, 25]. Research in the general population suggests that higher DLMI is associated with health benefits [25–27]. Mechanistically, dietary live microbes (primarily probiotics) may support gut barrier function [28], mucosal immunity [29], and tissue repair [30], as evidenced in experimental models [31]. Regarding our specific findings in stroke survivors, one plausible interpretation is that DLMI could influence post-stroke prognosis by modulating gut microbiota, potentially reducing systemic inflammation and improving metabolic status. However, this remains a hypothesis and requires direct testing. Additionally, the potential residual confounding should be considered as an alternative explanation for our findings. Individuals who consume foods rich in live microbes (e.g., yogurt, fermented vegetables) in the 24-hour recall may have systematic differences from those who do not, beyond the factors we can measure and adjust for. They may be more likely to follow an overall healthier dietary pattern, engage in greater physical activity, have better access to healthcare, or exhibit other health-conscious behaviors, which collectively contribute to improved survival. Although we adjusted for a range of demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical variables, unmeasured or imperfectly measured aspects of this broader “healthy user” or “healthy lifestyle” profile could be the true drivers of the observed association, rather than DLMI. This inherent limitation of observational nutrition research underscores cautious interpretation of the results. Consequently, DLMI should be interpreted as a marker of a beneficial dietary pattern, rather than a specific measure of microbial exposure. Future studies should collect more detailed behavioral data or employ randomized designs to isolate the specific effect of dietary live microbes.

In summary, our study proposes that diets rich in active microbes may be feasible for long-term stroke management. This strategy adjusts dietary patterns to increase DLMI and considers the overall impact on gut microbiome, mental factors, and general health. It may be helpful to integrate educational and nutritional strategies that recommend microbe-rich diets into stroke care, as they hold potential for improving treatment outcomes and patient prognosis.

Our study has multiple advantages. First, it enrolls large samples from the NHANES database, improving the reliability and generalizability of results. Second, this study first examines the link between DLMI and mortality outcomes in stroke. Third, the model accuracy is enhanced by combining multiple confounders. Consistent results are observed in the unadjusted model as well as models 2 and 3.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be noted. First, residual confounding remains a primary concern. Unmeasured lifestyle and socioeconomic factors associated with a health-conscious profile (e.g., diet quality, health literacy, healthcare access) are clustered within the high-DLMI group. Consequently, the observed association may reflect these advantageous factors, rather than live microbial intake itself. Second, both exposure and outcome measurements have inherent limitations. DLMI was estimated from a single 24-hour recall, which may not represent habitual intake and relies on literature-based CFU estimates instead of direct food measurement. Non-differential misclassification likely biases results toward the null. Additionally, stroke history was self-reported without data on subtype, severity, and chronicity, which further limits clinical interpretability. Third, the definition of CVM should be interpreted cautiously. We used a composite endpoint that included fatal stroke (I60–I69). The resulting pathophysiological heterogeneity may attenuate observed associations. In conjunction with the limited number of events, this reduced statistical power. Therefore, the null finding for CVM should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, subgroup analyses are exploratory. The significant interaction with marital status (P = 0.047) suggests a potential moderating role of social determinants. Future studies incorporating detailed psychosocial assessments are necessary to confirm this finding. Future studies should replicate these findings in diverse populations, employ more precise exposure assessments, and explore the interplay between dietary patterns, psychosocial factors, and cardiovascular health in high-risk groups.

Conclusion

Among U.S. stroke survivors, higher DLMI is significantly associated with a lower risk of ACM. These results support that DLMI could be explored as potential interventions. Therefore, future prospective studies are warranted to further investigate this association, elucidate the underlying mechanisms, and explore its potential role in nutritional guidance for stroke populations.
