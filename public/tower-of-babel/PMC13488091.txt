Background

The aorta is the body’s largest blood vessel, and its primary function is to carry oxygen and blood from the left ventricle to other body organs. Anatomically, the aorta is divided into four parts: the ascending aorta, the aortic arch, the descending thoracic aorta, and finally, the abdominal aorta [1]. The worldwide incidence rate estimate of Acute aortic dissection (AAD) was found to be 4.8 per 100,000 individuals/year [2]. Aortic dissection (AD) occurs when a tear in the intimal layer creates a false lumen, potentially compressing the true lumen and leading to organ ischemia [3, 4].

While the classic presentation involves “tearing” chest pain, atypical presentations—such as isolated abdominal pain—occur in a significant minority of cases, often leading to misdiagnosis [5, 6]. Given that mortality for untreated Type A dissection increases by 1% to 2% per hour, rapid bedside tools are vital. Point-of-care ultrasonography (POCUS) has emerged as a frontline diagnostic modality in the Emergency Department (ED), allowing for immediate visualization of the aortic flap at the bedside [7]. Prompt recognition and diagnosis are essential in such cases, where early intervention can be life-saving [8].

Case presentation

Initial presentation and timeline

A 51-year-old male with a history of hypertension and migraines presented to the ED with sudden-onset, severe abdominal pain that began one hour prior. Within 15 min of arrival, the patient was evaluated; he appeared distressed with a rigid, distended abdomen. Vital signs were initially stable: BP 138/78 mmHg, HR 80 bpm, and O_2 saturation 96%. Imaging Findings A POCUS exam was performed within 35 min of presentation using a Philips CX50 machine, which immediately identified an oscillating intimal flap within the aorta (Fig. 1). Following this bedside finding, the patient was stabilized and rushed to CT angiography. Diagnostic Reasoning and Laboratory Findings Initial laboratory tests revealed several critical abnormalities: Hematology: Hemoglobin was notably low at 82 g/L (Hematocrit 23.8%), suggesting potential internal sequestration or acute blood loss associated with the vascular injury. Biomarkers: D-dimer was extremely elevated, a highly sensitive marker for aortic dissection. Troponin levels were elevated, which may indicate demand ischemia or potential proximal extension involving the coronary ostia, although imaging later showed the coronary arteries arising from the true lumen. Metabolic: Lactic acid was 3.8 mmol/L, indicating early tissue hypoperfusion. CRP was noted as “raised,” reflecting an acute inflammatory response. The CT confirmed a Type A aortic dissection beginning at the aortic valve and extending through the arch to the common iliac arteries. The ascending aorta was ectatic at 4.4 cm (Figs. 2 and 3). Notably, the left renal artery arose from the false lumen, leading to reduced left kidney perfusion, while the right renal artery arose from the true lumen.

Discussion

This case underscores the diagnostic challenge posed by atypical AAD. The patient’s primary complaint was abdominal, yet the underlying pathology was a Stanford Type A dissection. POCUS is central to the modern management of suspected AAD. The SPEED (Sonographic Protocol for the Emergent Evaluation of Aortic Dissections) protocol emphasizes multi-view assessment, including parasternal long-axis and abdominal views, to identify intimal flaps (Fig. 4) or pericardial effusions. Studies suggest POCUS has high specificity for AD, though its sensitivity can be operator-dependent and limited in the mid-descending thoracic aorta. In this case, the early use of POCUS bypassed the delays often associated with waiting for formal imaging in a busy ED environment. The profound anemia (Hb 82 g/L) and elevated lactate (3.8 mmol/L) in this patient were key indicators of the severity of the dissection and secondary malperfusion. While no gross periaortic leakage was seen on CT, the low hemoglobin in AAD often necessitates urgent surgical consultation and cross-matching of blood products.

Conclusion

Aortic dissection must remain a top differential in patients presenting with sudden, severe abdominal pain, particularly those with a history of hypertension. POCUS serves as an invaluable diagnostic adjunct that can significantly reduce the “time to diagnosis,” enabling rapid surgical intervention and improving patient outcomes.
