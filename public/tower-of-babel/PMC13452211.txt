Distinct molecular profiles in human aortic smooth muscle cells under cyclic stretch and hydrostatic pressure
Authors: Takahiro Kusume, Hana Inoue, Noriaki Arakawa, Kazuhiro Satomi, Utako Yokoyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vascular smooth muscle cells are constantly subjected to cyclic stretch and hydrostatic pressure in vivo, yet the molecular responses to these stimuli remain incompletely understood. We performed transcriptome and secretome analyses to characterize the responses of human aortic smooth muscle cells (HASMCs) to cyclic stretch (10%; S10) or cyclic hydrostatic pressure corresponding to normotensive (120/60 mmHg; P120) or hypertensive (180/120 mmHg; P180) conditions. Principal component analysis and hierarchical clustering revealed distinct transcriptomic and secretomic profiles in P180 compared with S10 and P120. P180 also exhibited the largest number of differentially expressed genes, including several not previously linked to mechanical stress. Among these, vasoactive intestinal peptide receptor 1 (VIPR1), a regulator of vascular tone, was markedly upregulated. These distinct expression profiles induced by hypertensive hydrostatic pressure may provide insight into physiological and pathological responses in HASMCs.

Introduction

Vascular cells are continuously exposed to hemodynamic forces, including shear stress, stretch, and hydrostatic pressure. These forces play critical roles in regulating vascular structure and function and are essential for maintaining vascular homeostasis [1], [2]. Pathological changes in hemodynamic forces, such as those observed in hypertension, have been associated with the progression of vascular diseases [3], [4], [5]. Reduced or disturbed shear stress on endothelial cells has been implicated in the progression of vascular diseases [6], including atherosclerosis [7], aortic aneurysm [8], coronary artery disease [9], and aortic dissection [10]. In contrast, compared with shear stress in endothelial cells, the effects of mechanical stress on vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) phenotypes remain less well understood.

Mechanical stresses experienced by VSMCs primarily include stretch and hydrostatic pressure. In vivo aortic stretch has been reported to range from 9% to 12% [11], whereas hydrostatic pressure, which corresponds to blood pressure, can exceed 200 mmHg under hypertensive conditions. The effects of stretch on VSMCs have been extensively investigated. Cyclic stretch in the physiological range (∼10%) suppresses VSMC proliferation and migration [12], [13], while not markedly altering cell morphology or cytoskeletal organization [14], and is therefore considered to maintain a quiescent phenotype in VSMCs. In contrast, excessive mechanical stretch (>15%) induces phenotypic switching characterized by reduced expression of contractile markers, enhanced proliferation, and increased production of proinflammatory cytokines [15], [16], [17], although such levels of stretch are rarely observed in vivo, even in hypertension. Aortic strain in hypertensive patients has been reported to be approximately 5–7%, which is lower than that observed in normotensive or healthy subjects because of reduced aortic extensibility [11], [18], [19], suggesting that VSMCs in hypertension are exposed to elevated hydrostatic pressure without an increase in cyclic stretch. Despite the potential importance of hydrostatic pressure in hypertension, studies examining its effects on gene expression in VSMCs remain limited. A previous study by Chen and colleagues demonstrated that continuous hydrostatic pressure loading (100 or 200 mmHg) for 48 h induced phenotypic changes and transcriptional heterogeneity in VSMCs, resulting in distinct cellular subsets [20]. However, hydrostatic pressure in the vascular wall is cyclic in vivo, whereas the effects of cyclic hydrostatic pressure on VSMCs are not well characterized. We previously reported that cyclic supraphysiological hydrostatic pressure induced upregulation of multiple molecules, thereby enhancing cell-cell and cell-matrix adhesion in human umbilical artery smooth muscle cells using a custom-made pressurization culture system [21]. Recently, we further developed this system to enable cell culture under physiological cyclic hydrostatic pressure conditions [22]. The resulting system may provide a useful approach for investigating the effects of cyclic hydrostatic pressure under conditions relevant to the in vivo vascular environment on VSMC phenotypes.

In the present study, we investigated the effects of mechanical stress on gene expression and protein secretion in human aortic smooth muscle cells (HASMCs) cultured under cyclic stretch (10%), normal hydrostatic pressure (120/60 mmHg), and high hydrostatic pressure (180/120 mmHg) to better understand how mechanical stress regulates VSMC phenotypes.

Materials and methods

Human aortic smooth muscle cell culture

HASMCs were purchased from Kurabo Industries Ltd. (Osaka, Japan) and maintained in HuMedia-SG2 medium KS2170; Kurabo) containing 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), insulin (5 μg/mL), human epidermal growth factor (0.5 ng/mL), human basic fibroblast growth factor (2 ng/mL), gentamicin (50 μg/mL), and amphotericin B (50 ng/mL) with 5% CO2 at 37°C. HASMCs were used between passages 3 and 7. For experiments applying mechanical stress, HASMCs were seeded onto a stretch chamber (Sc4a, Menicon, Nagoya, Japan) coated with fibronectin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, 50 μg /mL). Before applying mechanical stress, the culture medium was replaced with serum-free HuMedia-SG2 to reduce interference from serum-derived proteins in proteomic analysis.

Mechanical stress stimulation of HASMCs

To apply hydrostatic pressure to HASMCs, we used a custom-made hydrostatic pressurization system comprising a pressure vessel, an incubator, a compressor, a flow regulator, and an air tank (Koganei Corp., Tokyo, Japan) as previously described [22]. Cells cultured on stretch chambers were transferred to a pressure vessel placed inside a 5% CO2 incubator maintained at 37°C. Cells were then subjected to cyclic hydrostatic pressure of 120/60 mmHg (P120) or 180/120 mmHg (P180), corresponding to normotensive and hypertensive conditions, respectively, at 1 Hz for 24 h. To maintain the partial oxygen pressure inside the vessel at 140 mmHg, the O2 concentration of the injected gas was adjusted to 16% and 15%, under the P120 and P180 conditions, respectively.

Cell stretching experiments were performed using a ShellPa Pro Cell Stretch System (Menicon). Cells cultured on the stretch chamber were subjected to 10% cyclic stretch at 1 Hz for 24 h in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Total RNA and culture supernatants were collected after 24 h of mechanical stress exposure to investigate molecular responses to sustained mechanical stress in HASMCs rather than transient acute responses induced upon mechanical stress loading.

Bulk RNA-sequencing

Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Venlo, the Netherlands). RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) libraries were prepared from total RNA using poly(A) selection with the NE Next Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module, followed by library construction using the NE Next Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Novases 6000 platform with paired-end 150-bp reads (PE150) by Rheia Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). The RNA-seq data have been deposited in GEO under accession number GSE334841.

RNA-seq count data were analyzed using the DESeq2 package in R (version 4.5.1). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, and genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Variance-stabilized expression data were generated using the variance stabilizing transformation (vest) for downstream analyses. Principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering analysis were performed using the transformed data. PCA was conducted using the prcomp function with centering and without scaling. For hierarchical clustering analysis, Euclidean distance was used to calculate sample-to-sample distances, and clustering was performed using Ward’s minimum variance method (ward.D2) implemented in the hclust function. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed on differentially expressed genes (DEG) using the PANTHER classification system. GO terms containing 10–500 genes were included in the enrichment analysis, and terms with an FDR < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Visualization of the results was performed in R.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the ReliaPrep RNA Cell Miniprep System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from total RNA using PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using gene-specific primers and TB Green Fast qPCR Mix (Takara Bio) on CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Relative gene expression levels were normalized to 18S RNA as an internal control. The following primers were used for qPCR: VIPR1 (forward, 5′- TTCTCCTCCATTCAAGGCCG−3′; reverse, 5′-CGCTGCCTTGTCATCCAAAC−3′) and 18S rRNA (forward, 5′-GTAACCCGTTGAACCCCATT−3′; reverse, 5′- CCATCCAATCGGTAGTAGCG−3′).

Proteomic analysis

For secretome analysis, cells were cultured in serum-free medium for 24 h under mechanical stress, the conditioned medium was collected, and released/secreted proteins were analyzed as previously described [23]. Briefly, proteins in the conditioned medium were precipitated with acetone for desalting and the resulting precipitates were subjected to trypsin digestion and peptide cleanup using the EasyPep™ Mini MS Sample Prep Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The resulting peptides were analyzed in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode using a Q Exactive HF coupled to a Vanquish Neo LC System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were separated on a reverse-phase C18 nano-column using an acetonitrile gradient. Proteins were identified and quantified using DIA-NN software version 1.8.1 based on the human UniProt database UP000005640, containing 22,401 reviewed proteins. Search parameters were as follows: protease, trypsin/P; missed cleavages, 1; peptide length range, 7–30 amino acids; precursor charge range, 1–4; fragment ion m/z range, 200–1800; mass accuracy, auto (0.0); variable modification, loss of N-term methionine, cysteine carbamidomethylation; and “Heuristic protein inference” and “Use isotopologues” enabled. The peptide and protein FDR values were set to < 1%, as described previously [23]. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited in the ProteomeXchange Consortium (PXD079353, http://www.proteomexchange.org/) via the jPOST partner repository (JPST004673, https://jpostdb.org/).

PCA and hierarchical clustering analysis were performed to assess patterns of protein secretion under mechanical stress. During data processing, missing values were imputed using half of the limit of detection (LOD/2) [24]. However, the two samples with the highest proportion of missing values were excluded from these analyses. Differential abundance analysis of secreted proteins was performed using the limma package using R software (version 4.5.1). Proteins with an FDR< 0.05 were considered significantly differentially abundant.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1) and GraphPad Prism (version 10). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons using the Mann–Whitney U test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Mechanical stresses elicit distinct global gene expression and protein secretion profiles in HASMCs

To examine the effects of mechanical stress on gene expression profiles in HASMCs, we performed bulk RNA-seq under unstimulated conditions (Ctrl), 10% cyclic stretch (S10), or cyclic hydrostatic pressures corresponding to normotensive (P120) or hypertensive (P180) conditions. RNA-seq initially identified approximately 61,860 genes, of which 17,211 genes were retained for downstream analyses after filtering of low-expression genes using the filterByExpr function in edgeR [25] (Fig. 1A). The PCA revealed clear separation among Ctrl, S10, P120, and P180 groups (Fig. 1B), indicating distinct global gene expression profiles across mechanical stress conditions. Hierarchical clustering analysis also demonstrated clear separation among the groups (Fig. 1C). We further investigated released/secreted proteins in response to different mechanical stimuli. A total of 5399 proteins were identified in the culture supernatants of HASMCs under Ctrl, S10, P120 and P180 conditions. Similar to the transcriptome results, PCA and hierarchical clustering analyses of secreted protein profiles revealed clear separation among the experimental groups (Fig. S1). P180 exhibited the most distinct secretion profile, segregating from S10 and P120, which represent normotensive mechanical conditions.

Gene expression changes induced by S10 and P120

To explore genes associated with the distinct gene expression profiles induced by stretch and hydrostatic pressure, we performed differential expression analyses between Ctrl and S10 and between Ctrl and P120. DEGs were defined as genes with a fold change ≥ 1.25 or ≤ 0.8 and an FDR < 0.05. Under S10 conditions, a total of 252 DEGs were identified, including 148 upregulated and 104 downregulated genes (Fig. 2A). Among the top 20 upregulated and downregulated DEGs (Fig. 2A right), CCL2, MEF2C, DDIT3, ICAM, SOD2, EGR1, and DUSP1 have previously been implicated in cellular responses to stretch [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33]. Under P120 conditions, a total of 75 DEGs were identified, including 42 upregulated and 33 downregulated genes (Fig. 2B). Among the top 20 upregulated and downregulated DEGs (Fig. 2B right), SOD2, ICAM1, LOXL4, DDIT3, IL6, EGR1, CCN1, DKK1, TNC, and DUSP1 have been implicated in responses to mechanical stress [29], [34], [35], [36]. GO analysis of the upregulated DEGs revealed enrichment of biological processes related to regulation of vascular endothelial cell proliferation and regulation of apoptotic cell clearance in S10, whereas regulation of smooth muscle cell proliferation was enriched in P120 (Fig. 2C, D). For downregulated DEGs, GO analysis of S10 revealed processes related to developmental and morphogenetic processes (Fig. S2), whereas no significantly enriched GO terms were detected among downregulated DEGs in P120.

Venn diagram analysis revealed that 26 upregulated and 13 downregulated DEGs were shared between S10 and P120 (Fig. 2E, F). Among commonly upregulated DEGs, DDIT3, ICAM1, and SOD2 were also included in the top 20 upregulated DEGs in both S10 and P120. EGR1 and DUSP1 were among the top 20 downregulated DEGs in both S10 and P120. Approximately 60% of DEGs identified in P120 were shared with S10, whereas these shared DEGs accounted for less than 20% of DEGs identified in S10 (Fig. 2E, F), suggesting that under physiological mechanical conditions, stretch induces broader transcriptional changes than hydrostatic pressure.

P180 induces distinct gene expression and GO enrichment profiles

DEG analysis between Ctrl and P180 revealed 512 DEGs, comprising 359 upregulated and 153 downregulated genes in P180 (Fig. 3A), substantially exceeding the numbers identified in S10 and P120. Among the top 20 upregulated DEGs, HMOX1, which was also identified in P120 as the most highly upregulated DEG, has previously been associated with mechanical stress responses [37] (Fig. 3A). GO analysis of the upregulated DEGs revealed significant enrichment of biological processes, markedly more than those identified in S10 or P120 (Fig. 3B). These included biological processes related to osteochondrogenic phenotype, migration, vascular remodeling, and extracellular matrix organization. Venn diagram analyses were conducted to assess the overlap among DEGs identified in each condition compared with Ctrl (Fig. 3C, D). P180 and S10 shared 55 upregulated and 30 downregulated DEGs. However, both S10 and P180 also exhibited substantial numbers of stimulus-specific DEGs, suggesting that stretch and hypertensive hydrostatic pressure induce distinct transcriptional responses (Fig. 3C). As P180 represents a higher-pressure condition than P120, a substantial overlap in DEGs might be expected; however, only approximately half of the DEGs identified in P120 overlapped with those identified in P180 (Fig. 3D).

Notably, VIPR1 (vasoactive intestinal peptide receptor 1) and its antisense transcript VIPR1-AS were both included among the top 20 upregulated DEGs identified in P180. Therefore, VIPR1 was selected for further validation by qPCR, which confirmed pressure-dependent upregulation under hydrostatic pressure conditions but not under stretch conditions (Fig. 3E).

P120 versus P180 comparison identifies DEGs without significant GO enrichment

To elucidate the differences between normotensive and hypertensive hydrostatic pressure, we performed DEG analysis between P120 and P180 and revealed 225 DEGs, comprising 170 upregulated and 55 downregulated genes in P180 (Fig. S3A). The top 20 upregulated and downregulated DEGs are shown as a heatmap (Fig. S3B). Among these, TXNDC5, NRGN, NPTX1, and SOD2 have been implicated in responses to mechanical stress [29], [38], [39], [40]. Complete lists of upregulated and downregulated DEGs are provided in Tables S3 and S4, respectively. Among these DEGs, only four genes were also differentially expressed under P120 compared with Ctrl. NPTX1, CEMIP, and DHRS3 were upregulated, whereas TNC was downregulated, suggesting that these genes respond to hydrostatic pressure in an intensity-dependent manner (Fig. S3C). GO analysis of the P120 versus P180 DEGs did not identify any significantly enriched biological processes.

Shared and distinct global secretory responses to stretch and hydrostatic pressure

Pairwise comparisons of differentially secreted proteins identified 9 proteins in S10 and 26 proteins in P180, including both increased and decreased proteins, whereas no significantly differentially secreted proteins were detected in P120 (Table S5). Comparison between P120 and P180 identified four differentially secreted proteins, with HGF increased and CDON, XPR1, and SHFL decreased under P180 conditions (Table S6). Only four differentially expressed molecules overlapped between the transcriptome and secretome analyses: FGL2, HHIP, TNFRSF11B, and TNXB, all of which were consistently upregulated in both datasets under P180 conditions (Fig. S4, Table S5).

Discussion

In the present study, transcriptomic analysis demonstrated that stretch and hydrostatic pressure induced partially overlapping but distinct transcriptional responses in HASMCs. Although stretch induced broader transcriptional changes than normotensive hydrostatic pressure, hypertensive hydrostatic pressure induced markedly more extensive transcriptional alterations with substantially larger numbers of DEGs and enriched biological processes. These findings suggest that vascular smooth muscle cells exhibit distinct transcriptional responses depending on the type and magnitude of mechanical stress.

Physiological mechanical stress conditions induced relatively limited GO enrichment (Fig. 2C, D). Among the enriched GO terms, proliferation-related biological processes were enriched under both S10 and P120 conditions. These findings suggest that physiological mechanical stress may not direct gene expression toward a specific biological program, except for modest proliferative responses, but instead induce more diverse and adaptive transcriptional responses required for maintenance of vascular homeostasis. Although some DEGs overlapped between S10 and P120, distinct DEGs were also identified under each physiological mechanical stress condition (Fig. 2E, F). These results suggest that stretch and hydrostatic pressure activate partially different mechanotransduction pathways. Although we found only four genes including NPTX1, CEMIP, and DHRS3, and TNC were induced in a hydrostatic-pressure dependent manner (Fig. S3C), some DEGs identified in P120 were not detected in P180 (Fig. 3D), despite P180 representing a higher-pressure condition than P120.This observation suggests that hypertensive hydrostatic pressure may not simply represent an extension of physiological hydrostatic pressure, but instead may induce qualitatively distinct transcriptional responses, similar to previous observations that different magnitudes of stretch can exert opposing effects on VSMC proliferation [12]. Further studies are needed to clarify the phenotypic consequences of the DEGs identified in P120 but not in P180.

Excessive stretch (>15%) has been reported to induce inflammation-related gene expression in VSMCs [16]. In contrast, here we applied physiological levels of stretch to HASMCs, resulting in no enrichment of inflammation-related GO terms. These findings suggest that inflammatory activation may require supraphysiological levels of stretch. On the other hand, multiple proinflammatory genes were identified as DEGs in S10 and P120 (Fig. 2A, B). For example, CCL2, a chemokine involved in inflammatory cell recruitment that has been reported to be induced by stretch [26], was among the top 20 upregulated DEGs in S10. In addition, IL-6, a proinflammatory cytokine implicated in vascular wall inflammation, was commonly upregulated in both S10 and P120. These findings suggest that physiological mechanical stress may induce limited inflammatory responses in VSMCs. Importantly, IL-6 and CCL2 may not function solely as proinflammatory mediators. IL-6 has been implicated in exercise-induced physiological vascular remodeling [41], whereas CCL2 has also been implicated in physiological tissue remodeling, including bone formation [42].

Hypertensive hydrostatic pressure induced a distinct transcriptional program enriched in biological processes associated with migration and osteochondrogenic phenotypic switching. These phenotypic changes are closely associated with pathological vascular remodeling, during which VSMCs lose contractile characteristics and acquire migratory and osteochondrogenic properties. In particular, enrichment of cartilage development, chondrocyte differentiation, and biomineral tissue development suggests activation of transcriptional programs associated with vascular calcification and arterial stiffening. It is noteworthy that osteoprotegerin (OPG), encoded by TNFRSF11B, and HHIP, both implicated in pathways related to vascular calcification, were upregulated at both the mRNA and secreted protein levels under P180 (Fig. S4). A previous study showed elevated circulating levels of OPG in patients with hypertension and OPG has been reported to inhibit vascular calcification [43], [44], [45]. HHIP, an extracellular regulator of Hedgehog signaling, may also play a role in regulating vascular calcification, as activation of Hedgehog signaling reduced calcification, whereas pathway inhibition had the opposite effect [46]. Taken together, these results suggest that upregulation of OPG and HHIP may represent a protective adaptive response against calcification-related signaling induced by hypertensive hydrostatic pressure.

Only four molecules were consistently upregulated at both the mRNA and secreted protein levels under P180 (Fig. S4). FGL2 and TNXB were consistently upregulated at both the mRNA and secreted protein levels under P180. Both FGL2 and TNXB have been reported to be regulated by fluid shear stress in endothelial cells [47], [48]. FGL2, a fibrinogen-like protein has been implicated in coagulation and vascular remodeling in pulmonary hypertension [49]. TNXB encodes tenascin-X, an extracellular matrix glycoprotein involved in collagen organization and connective tissue structure [50]. A recent study further showed that smooth muscle-derived tenascin-X regulates pathological vascular remodeling through modulation of TGF-β signaling [51]. Thus, the increase in FGL2 and TNXB in our study may associate with vascular remodeling in response to hypertensive hydrostatic pressure. The limited overlap between transcriptomic and secretome analyses may reflect the complex regulation of protein secretion, which is influenced by not only gene expression but also by post-transcriptional regulation, protein processing, and secretion mechanisms.

Together, these observations suggest that hypertensive hydrostatic pressure induces molecular changes associated with both pathogenic and protective responses in VSMCs. However, further studies are required to clarify the interaction between these pathogenic and protective responses.

Among the genes identified in the present study, VIPR1 was of particular interest because its antisense transcript, VIPR1-AS1, was also upregulated and included among the top 20 DEGs (Fig. 3A). qPCR further confirmed pressure-dependent upregulation of VIPR1 under hydrostatic pressure conditions, but not under stretch conditions (Fig. 3E). VIPR1 encodes vasoactive intestinal peptide receptor 1 (also known as VPAC1), a receptor involved in regulation of vascular tone and blood flow [52], [53], [54]. Vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), an endogenous ligand of VIPR1, is a potent vasodilator that induces VSMC relaxation. VIPR1 activation has also been implicated in pressure-induced vasodilation in skin [54], suggesting that VIPR1 signaling may mediate VSMC responses to mechanical stress. In addition, VIP/VIPR1 signaling has been reported to inhibit VSMC proliferation [55], [56] suggesting a potential role in limiting vascular remodeling. Thus, VIPR1 induction may contribute to adaptive vascular responses that counteract blood pressure elevation and limit vascular remodeling in vivo. The functional significance of VIPR1 induction in VSMCs remains unclear, as this was not directly investigated in the present study.

HMOX1, ENSG00000196656, GCLM, ZFN469, FTH1, TSPYL4, PTX3, and PANX2 were selectively upregulated under hydrostatic pressure conditions but not under stretch conditions (Table S1). Of these, HMOX1 ranked first in P120 and fifth in P180 among the upregulated DEGs (Figs. 2B, 3A). HMOX1 encodes heme oxygenase−1, an inducible stress-responsive enzyme that produces carbon monoxide and thereby exerts vasodilatory and vasoprotective effects [57]. These findings suggest that HMOX1 induction under hydrostatic pressure may represent an adaptive response to pressure overload, similar to that we proposed for VIPR1.

EGR1 and DUSP1 have previously been reported to be upregulated by stretch in VSMCs [30], [31]. However, in the present study, both genes were consistently downregulated under S10, P120, and P180 conditions (Table S2). This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in the timing of RNA collection. EGR1 is an immediate early response gene that is rapidly induced within several hours under cyclic stretch conditions [58]. Similarly, DUSP1 expression rapidly increases in response to stretch (∼60 min) and returns to basal levels by 6 h [32]. Therefore, EGR1 and DUSP1 expression may have already returned to basal or lower levels after 24 h of mechanical stimulation, which may explain why these genes were downregulated in the present study despite previous reports showing their upregulation.

As the experiments in the present study were performed using non-diseased HASMCs, the observed responses may differ under pathological conditions such as aging, diabetes, or vascular calcification. Although several potentially vasoprotective responses to hypertensive hydrostatic pressure were observed in the present study, it remains unclear whether these mechanisms are preserved in VSMCs from patients with chronic vascular diseases. In addition, the present study focused on molecular responses after 24 h of mechanical stress exposure; it remains unclear whether the observed molecular profiles represent transient responses, sustained adaptations, or long-term remodeling. Future study incorporating multiple time points will be required to address this issue. Furthermore, stretch and hydrostatic pressure were applied separately in the present study, and this experimental design does not fully reflect the vascular environment in vivo, where vascular cells are simultaneously exposed to both stretch and hydrostatic pressure. Further studies are needed to clarify the molecular profiles induced by mechanical stress in VSMCs in the context of hypertensive vascular disease.

In summary, this study demonstrated that cyclic stretch and hydrostatic pressure induce distinct molecular responses in HASMCs, and that hypertensive hydrostatic pressure elicits a particularly extensive pathological-like transcriptional program as well as vasoprotective responses. These findings provide insight into the specific biological effects of hydrostatic pressure and identify candidate pathways potentially involved in hypertensive vascular remodeling.
