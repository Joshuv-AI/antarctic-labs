Decoding the serotonin–alcohol crosstalk: the role of central serotonergic dysregulation in alcohol use disorder
Authors: Magdalena Zaniewska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSerotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine; 5-HT) is a key neuromodulator involved in the regulation of mood, appetite, aggression, and impulse control. Dysregulation of central 5-HT function has been implicated in alcohol use disorder (AUD) and comorbid depression. This review summarizes clinical and preclinical evidence on the role of 5-HT in AUD development, heterogeneity, and treatment response. Alterations in 5-HT function may be shaped by stress and genetic variation in serotonergic genes, including TPH2 and SLC6A4, contributing to individual vulnerability to AUD. Reduced 5-HT activity increases the risk of developing AUD, particularly Cloninger’s type II, characterized by early onset, violent, and antisocial behaviors. Consistently, Tph2-deficient mice, which lack central 5-HT, exhibit increased ethanol consumption and behavioral features resembling Cloninger’s type II alcohol dependence. Similarly, alcohol-preferring rat lines show reduced 5-HT levels, decreased serotonergic projections to the cortex, and reduced prefrontal 5-HT2A receptor binding, implicating raphe-prefrontal serotonergic projections as a critical pathway modulating vulnerability to AUD. Given the heterogeneity of AUD, the efficacy of selective 5-HT reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) remains limited, with beneficial effects observed only in less severe, later-onset forms. Recently, serotonergic psychedelic-assisted therapies have attracted considerable interest as potential AUD treatments; mechanistically, their effects may be linked to activation of 5-HT2A receptors in the prefrontal cortex – a brain region known to be dysfunctional in AUD. The reviewed literature highlights the need for improved stratification of AUD subtypes and validation of 5-HT-related biomarkers to guide personalized therapeutic approaches.Keywords: Alcohol use disorder, AUD heterogeneity, Psychedelics, Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, Serotonin, Tryptophan hydroxylase type 2

Introduction

Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine; 5-HT), synthesized in the brain, is a key neurotransmitter responsible for regulating numerous physiological and behavioral processes. It has been shown to influence mood, appetite, breathing, stress homeostasis, cognition, impulsivity, aggressive behavior, maternal care, sexual behavior, thermoregulation, and pain sensation [1–4]. It has also been suggested to play a role in rest-related and energy-conserving activities like sleep, locomotion, and motivation [3, 5, 6].

Central 5-HT is produced in neurons of the brainstem raphe nuclei from the amino acid L-tryptophan (TRP), which is transported from the periphery. In the rate-limiting step, TRP is hydroxylated to 5-hydroxy-L-tryptophan (5-HTP) by the tryptophan hydroxylase type 2 (TPH2), an enzyme identified by Walther and Bader [7]. Subsequently, 5-HTP is decarboxylated to 5-HT by aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase (AADC). Newly synthesized 5-HT is packaged into synaptic vesicles by the vesicular monoamine transporter type 2 (VMAT2) and released to the synaptic cleft. In the cytoplasm, 5-HT can be degraded by monoamine oxidase (MAO) into 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA), while extracellular 5-HT is transported back to the terminal by 5-HT transporter (5-HTT/SERT). The wide spectrum of 5-HT functions is mediated by 14 5-HT receptor subtypes, classified into seven subclasses − 5-HT1(A-F), 5-HT2(A-C), 5-HT3, 5-HT4, 5-HT5, 5-HT6, and 5-HT7. These receptor families differ in their structure, signaling mechanisms, and functional roles, allowing 5-HT to exert diverse effects throughout the CNS [8].

This review focuses on presenting evidence on the influence of 5-HT dysfunction on alcohol use disorder (AUD) vulnerability, maintenance, relapse, heterogeneity, and treatment response. According to DSM-5 [9], AUD is a chronic, relapsing condition characterized by compulsive alcohol use. Diagnosis of AUD requires the presence of at least two of eleven criteria within 12 months, including drinking in larger amounts or for longer than intended, craving, continued drinking despite negative consequences, and withdrawal symptoms. Depending on the number of symptoms present, AUD is classified as mild (2–3 symptoms), moderate (4–5 symptoms), or severe (≥6 symptoms).

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) report, average global alcohol consumption (27 g/day) is associated with significant health risks that increase with the amount of ethanol consumed [10]. Alcohol use may lead to liver disease (steatosis, hepatitis, cirrhosis), pancreatitis, cardiomyopathy, and other cardiovascular and gastrointestinal disorders, and is also associated with an increased risk of several cancers [10]. Advanced alcohol-related liver disease may cause hepatic encephalopathy, manifesting as cognitive impairment [11]. Alcohol-related malnutrition may lead to thiamine deficiency, neuropathy, Wernicke’s encephalopathy, and Korsakoff’s syndrome [12].

Heavy episodic drinking (≥60 g alcohol on a single occasion) results in acute intoxication characterized by impaired cognition, motor incoordination, emotional lability, aggressive behavior, and reduced impulse control. Chronic exposure may produce persistent deficits in memory, attention, and emotion regulation as well as sleep disturbances [11, 13]. Discontinuation of heavy drinking induces alcohol withdrawal syndrome, characterized by tremors, insomnia, anxiety, irritability, and hypertension, often accompanied by depressive symptoms [8, 11, 13–15]. In severe cases, individuals with AUD may develop delirium tremens [11, 15], with symptoms resembling 5-HT syndrome – a drug-induced condition (triggered by serotonergic drugs or opioids) resulting from excessive serotonergic activity and characterized by mild to life-threatening symptoms such as altered mental status, rigidity, hyperthermia, and hypertension [16].

Rodent studies provide additional insights into alcohol-induced cognitive and affective dysregulations. Chronic ethanol exposure may impair memory and cognitive flexibility [17, 18], although the findings are inconsistent [19]. Acute ethanol exerts anxiolytic properties, while chronic exposure produces strain-dependent anxiolytic or anxiogenic responses as well as antidepressant effects [20–26]. Acute (2–6 hours) ethanol cessation induces locomotor hyperactivity, tremor, wet dog shakes, and audiogenic seizures [27]. Both short-term and prolonged ethanol deprivation commonly produces cognitive impairments, anxiety-like phenotype, and depression-like behavior in rats or mice [19, 20, 28–33].

The overlap between 5-HT-regulated functions and those disrupted by ethanol and its withdrawal suggests that serotonergic signaling may influence the neural circuits underlying alcohol-related behaviors. Alcohol affects multiple brain areas, including the cortical and limbic regions (e.g., hippocampus and amygdala), rendering them vulnerable to dysfunction [11, 34] (Fig. 1). In heavy drinkers, ethanol attenuates amygdala activity and its connectivity with the prefrontal cortex [35, 36]. In dependent or abstinent individuals, alcohol cue-induced activation of prefrontal cortex – limbic (the amygdala, hippocampus) – striatal regions suggests that reactivity in these brain areas may potentially predict relapse risk [37–41] (Fig. 1). Consistently, rodent studies demonstrate that acute and chronic ethanol exposure, as well as withdrawal, alter the brain activity within these circuits, with ethanol discontinuation re-engaging brain regions (e.g., the prefrontal cortex, striatum, bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST), amygdala) involved in anxiety, craving and relapse processing [42].

Neurobiologically, alcohol interacts with multiple receptor systems, including N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA), γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)A, glycine, 5-HT3 and neuronal nicotinic acetylcholine (nACh) receptors, as well as ion channels such as L-type Ca2+ and G protein-activated inwardly rectifying K+ (GIRK) channels [14]. At low doses, it inhibits GABAergic neurotransmission and potentiates glutamatergic, dopaminergic, serotonergic, cholinergic, and endogenous opioid activity within reward circuits (ventral tegmental area, VTA and nucleus accumbens), whereas higher doses evoke GABA-mimetic effects and inhibit NMDA receptor function, leading to sedation and motor incoordination [14, 43, 44]. Chronic exposure induces neuroadaptations within glutamatergic, GABAergic, opioid, dopamine, and 5-HT signaling [44]. During withdrawal, adaptations in glutamatergic activity may result in CNS hyperexcitability and craving, whereas alterations in monoamine systems – including 5-HT – may promote relapse-like behavior [44].

The convergence of behavioral and physiological responses to ethanol in humans and rodents, together with the similar engagement of prefrontal-limbic-striatal circuits, supports the translational validity of animal models in AUD research and highlights these networks as key targets for serotonergic modulation of alcohol-related phenotypes.

The present review integrates clinical and preclinical evidence on 5-HT signaling in AUD. It presents genetic, neurobiological, and behavioral aspects of 5-HT dysregulation, intending to clarify interindividual differences in AUD risk and treatment outcomes. The review also evaluates the translational potential of 5-HT-based biomarkers for severity stratification and for guiding individualized pharmacotherapy in AUD.

In this review, the term AUD, in accordance with the DSM-5 classification [9], integrates the DSM-IV diagnoses of alcohol abuse and alcohol dependence into a single disorder with varying severity. When discussing studies conducted prior to DSM-5, the original terminology (i.e., heavy drinking, alcohol abuse, or alcohol dependence) is retained to reflect the diagnostic frameworks in use at the time.

Serotonergic neurotransmission and its mechanisms

Serotonergic pathways

The 5-HT is produced by a relatively small group of brainstem neurons, designated by Dahlström and Fuxe as groups B1–B9, with B1–B8 located in the raphe nuclei, and B9 situated outside the midline raphe [45, 46]. Later studies identified additional 5-HT neurons located off the midline within the pons/midbrain, and medulla (cf. [46]). Despite a limited number, the 5-HT-containing neurons send widespread projections throughout the brain, enabling 5-HT to regulate diverse brain functions [47] (Fig. 1).

The caudal raphe nuclei (B1–B3) extend their projections to the lower brainstem (i.e., medulla) and spinal cord, where they modulate motor and sensory processing [46, 48]. The medullary B4 cell group innervates the septum, ventral hippocampus, and subventricular zone [48]. By contrast, the rostral raphe nuclei (B5–B9) give rise to the forebrain and upper brainstem [46]. In particular, the B9 group innervates the basal forebrain, preoptic area, nucleus accumbens, BNST, the medial cortex, and several brainstem structures, including the locus coeruleus (LC), VTA, and other 5-HT (B7/B6) neurons; many of these brain targets play a role in motivation and attention [48].

5-HT neurons in the dorsal raphe (DR) nucleus (B7–B6) innervate forebrain regions such as the lateral hypothalamus, dorsal and ventral striatum, BNST, hippocampus, intermediate lateral septum, lateral preoptic area, thalamus, amygdala, and most of the cortical targets [46, 48]. These circuits are implicated in reward/aversive stimuli processing, emotion regulation, cognition, feeding, and motor functions [46, 48, 49]. DR fibers also project to the brainstem, where they terminate in several regions of the midbrain, pons and medulla, particularly, the substantia nigra pars compacta, VTA, pedunculopontine and laterodorsal tegmental nuclei (PPT and LDT, respectively), LC, parabrachial nuclei, and other raphe nuclei (e.g., the median raphe (MR) nucleus); the brain sites implicated in pain, REM sleep and cardiovascular regulation [46].

The MR nucleus (B5 and B8 cell groups), which contains fewer 5-HT neurons than the DR nucleus, projects widely to the medial hypothalamus, medial thalamus, septum (medial and lateral), hippocampus, and brainstem regions such as the parabrachial nucleus and LC [46, 48]. MR projections to the cortex include several subregions of the prefrontal cortex [48]. Functionally, the MR nucleus is involved in the processing of reward-related and aversive information (in the opposite direction to DR nucleus), hippocampal EEG desynchronization, and inhibition of hippocampal theta rhythm [46, 49].

As summarized by Ren et al. [50], even within the DR and MR nuclei, the 5-HT neurons are highly heterogeneous with respect to electrophysiological and behavioral functions. Viral-genetic methods revealed that DR 5-HT neurons projecting to the frontal cortex versus those projecting to the amygdala form two parallel distinct subsystems that differ in their somatic location, axonal branching, physiological responses, and behavioral outcomes. For instance, cortical-projecting neurons induce active coping and are inhibited by punishment, whereas amygdala-projecting neurons promote anxiety-like behavior and are stimulated by punishment [51]. Molecular profiling has further refined the molecular classification of these subsystems [50].

Overall, serotonergic projections from the DR and MR nuclei target brain regions that are also sensitive to ethanol, suggesting an interaction between central 5-HT signaling and ethanol-related neural responses at these sites of convergence (Fig. 1).

Behavioral consequences of central serotonergic dysregulation

There are several mechanisms that influence the 5-HT neurotransmission in the brain (Fig. 2). Rodent studies have shown that brain 5-HT levels display diurnal variation [52] and are highly sensitive to external factors such as stress, which can rapidly increase 5-HT release in several brain regions, including the prefrontal cortex, amygdala, hippocampus, or nucleus accumbens [53–55]. One key determinant of 5-HT synthesis is the availability of 5-HT precursor, TRP, in the blood and brain [3]. The amount of TRP that enters the brain depends largely on competition with large neutral amino acids (LNAA) for transport across the blood-brain barrier. Thus, the plasma TRP/LNAA ratio, strongly influenced by diet composition, plays a critical role in regulating the efficiency of TRP transport to the brain [3]. Nevertheless, under some conditions, TRP availability is not the limiting factor, and instead the concentration and activity of TPH2 enzyme become crucial for 5-HT synthesis [56, 57]. In addition, 5-HT synthesis is regulated by the Tph2 gene transcription [58]. TPH2 is predominantly expressed in serotonergic neurons of the brainstem raphe nuclei, but it is also present in deep pineal gland fibers and in myenteric neurons of the small intestine [58]. Tph2 mRNA is abundant in the soma of 5-HT neurons and, at very low levels, in 5-HT terminals, including those innervating the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus [26, 58]. Notably, TPH2 protein is also present in axon terminals, indicating that 5-HT can be synthesized on site [58]. Other mechanisms that can influence 5-HT levels include 5-HT reuptake and metabolism, as well as the responsivity of 5-HT receptor subtypes.

Human studies. Dysregulation of the central 5-HT has been linked to a wide range of behavioral and psychiatric conditions (Fig. 2). In humans, imbalances in 5-HT and/or its metabolite, in the CSF, have been associated with depression, aggression, antisocial traits, and impulsivity [1, 2, 59]. Reduced CSF 5-HIAA levels have been observed in depressed individuals with more lethal suicide attempts [60], suggesting that low CSF 5-HIAA may serve as a marker of suicide risk [61] (Fig. 2). Furthermore, TRP depletion in patients suffering from depression has been shown to exacerbate depressive symptoms [62, 63].

Excessive 5-HT activity, however, can also be detrimental. In addition to 5-HT syndrome described above [16], augmented serotonergic activity has been reported in some psychiatric disorders, such as social anxiety disorder, in which patients demonstrate enhanced accumulation rate of [11C]5-HTP in the limbic regions and basal ganglia, indicating increased rate of 5-HT synthesis [64] (Fig. 2).

Nonhuman primate studies. Complementary findings on the effects of reduced 5-HT function have been reported in nonhuman primates. Monkeys with low CSF 5-HIAA levels showed impaired impulse control, heightened aggressive behavior, and social isolation [65]. Manipulations of dietary TRP further supported the relationship between 5-HT levels and aggressive behaviors: a low-TRP diet increased aggression, while a TRP-rich diet reduced this behavior [65].

Rodent studies. Experimental depletion of brain 5-HT using the neurotoxin 5,7-dihydroxytryptamine (5,7-DHT) induced behavioral disturbances such as exaggerated aggression, abnormal fighting behavior, and increased sensitivity to sensory stimuli [66]. However, these effects may result from both reduced 5-HT levels and the loss of 5-HT fibers caused by 5,7-DHT.

Gene-targeting approaches enable a more selective strategy for assessing the role of 5-HT in the brain. For example, constitutive Tph2-deficient mice (Tph2–/–) display profound (less than 4% of wild-type levels) depletion of brain 5-HT and 5-HIAA while maintaining the structural integrity of 5-HT neurons [67]. Phenotypic analyses of Tph2–/– mice revealed that 5-HT depletion in the brain leads to growth retardation, 50% postnatal mortality, maternal neglect, stress hyper-reactivity, hyper-aggressiveness, increased impulsivity and food intake, deficits in prosocial behavior, as well as more frequent and longer sleep during the daytime, decreased respiration, blood pressure, heart rate, and disrupted thermoregulation [67–74] (Fig. 2). Abnormal wiring of 5-HT fibers in the rostral brain during brain development has also been observed in Tph2–/– mice [72, 75].

Behavioral findings in Tph2–/– mice regarding depression- and anxiety-like behaviors have been inconsistent. Analyses in the sucrose preference test (SPT), forced swim test (FST), and tail suspension test (TST) revealed either increases (FST [70, 71]; TST [68]), no changes (SPT [76]; FST [26]; TST [70, 76]), or decreases (FST [68, 76, 77]; TST [77]) in depression-like behavior depending on the paradigm and study design (Fig. 2). Similarly, anxiety-like phenotype assessed in the novelty-suppressed feeding test (NSF), the light/dark box test (LDB), the elevated plus maze (EPM), and marble burying (MB), have yielded divergent results, ranging from reductions (NSF [70, 72, 77]; LDB [72]; EPM [70, 71]; MB [70]), to no changes (EPM [72]), and even increases (MB [72]) in anxiety-like behavior.

In another mouse line carrying the R439H (G1449A) Tph2 mutation, corresponding to the rare human R441H (G1463A) polymorphism in the TPH2 gene and associated with major depression [78], a ca. 80% reduction in 5-HT synthesis, lower extracellular 5-HT levels in the frontal cortex and hippocampus, reduced CSF 5-HIAA concentrations, and decreased fenfluramine-induced plasma PRL levels were observed [79, 80]. Behaviorally, these mice displayed increased anxiety-, and depression-like behaviors as well as heightened aggression [80] (Fig. 2).

On the other hand, increased 5-HT synthesis and extracellular 5-HT levels, together with reduced brainstem 5-HT1A receptor expression in SERT-deficient mice (SERT–/–), have led to pronounced anxiety-like behavior, without clear evidence of social anxiety [81, 82]. These mice also exhibit depression-like behavior (FST, TST), increased stress susceptibility, late-onset obesity, hypertension, hypoalgesia, hypoaggression, and hypoactivity [81] (Fig. 2).

Together, reduced central 5-HT levels are linked to a range of psychiatric and behavioral disorders, including depression, antisocial traits, impulsivity, and aggression. While rodent models consistently show severe developmental and functional consequences of 5-HT deficits, findings regarding depression- and anxiety-like behaviors remain inconsistent. Conversely, augmented 5-HT levels are associated with higher levels of social anxiety in humans, whereas in rodents they are linked to a reduced emotional state and hypoaggression.

Individual vulnerability and subtypes of alcohol dependence

Many individuals consume alcohol as a form of self-medication, e.g., to relieve sleep problems, anxiety disorders (e.g., social phobia), depression, or stress [14, 36, 83] (Fig. 2). While this strategy may provide a temporary relief, over time it exacerbates these problems and can ultimately lead to the development of AUD [14].

In line with heterogeneity of psychological vulnerability and drinking motives, several multidimensional typologies have been proposed, with Cloninger’s and Babor’s classifications being the most frequently mentioned in the literature [59] (Table 1).

According to Cloninger’s neurobiological learning model [84, 85], type II (“male-limited”) is characterized by an early onset (before age 25) excessive alcohol consumption, antisocial and often violent behavior, high heritability, impulsivity, and novelty seeking (Table 1). A high proportion of alcohol-dependent individuals with type II present with comorbid antisocial personality disorder and/or attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) [88]. In contrast, type I (“milieu-limited”) can range from mild to severe, affects both genders, emerges later (onset ≥ 25 years), and progresses in severity. It develops through the interaction of genetic and environmental risk factors and is marked by a loss of self-control when drinking, binge drinking, and high harm-avoidance personality traits [84, 85]. These subtypes also differ in their treatment requirements, with type I often not requiring intensive treatment, and type II frequently requiring more extensive treatment [85]. Type I alcohol-dependent individuals, characterized by long-term escalated alcohol use and a lower plasma PRL response to D-fenfluramine, have been reported to have higher anxiety proneness [87], indicating that some type I individuals have abnormalities in 5-HT function, which coincides with negative emotional states. Likewise, in early-onset alcohol-dependent subjects, characteristic of Cloninger’s type II, alterations in 5-HT neurotransmission have also been described, including lower CSF levels of 5-HT metabolite, 5-HIAA [56, 59], reflecting reduced brain 5-HT turnover [92].

Babor’s typology distinguishes two subgroups of alcohol-dependent individuals: low-risk severity (type A) and high-risk severity (type B) (Table 1). Type B subjects, who share many characteristics with Cloninger’s type II, are marked by greater severity of alcohol use (i.e., higher ethanol intake), early onset of drinking, greater childhood and family-related risk factors, antisocial personality traits, and higher psychiatric comorbidity (e.g., depression). By contrast, type A individuals – similar in many respects to Cloninger’s type I – are characterized by later onset, lower severity, and fewer childhood risk factors [86]. Type B alcohol-dependent individuals have also been associated with traits such as greater harm avoidance, higher neuroticism, and a history of suicide attempts [89].

Overall, type II (or type B) is an early-onset form associated with greater severity and antisocial traits, while type I (or type A) presents later with a less severe course. Importantly, both early-onset subjects and type I individuals with heightened anxiety proneness may exhibit disruptions in 5-HT signaling, suggesting the existence of further subgroups.

It is worth mentioning that although some clinical features of AUD described in DSM-5, such as early-onset, co-occurrence of behavioral disorders (e.g., aggression, vandalism, or theft), and antisocial traits [9], largely overlap with the characteristics of type II Cloninger’s model, this model has never been included as part of the DSM diagnostic classification. Interestingly, recently published perspectives highlight the ongoing efforts by the American Psychiatric Association (APA) to revise the DSM, with particular emphasis on improving diagnostic criteria in areas such as substance use disorders [93]. These discussions highlight both the strengths and limitations of the current DSM-5 classification and point to the need for further research on etiological mechanisms underlying mental disorders, including AUD. Revisiting earlier typologies, together with neurobiological factors such as 5-HT signaling, may improve understanding of the heterogeneity of AUD, as well as its diagnosis and treatment.

Comorbidity of AUD with depression and anxiety, and negative affect during withdrawal

Psychiatric conditions that are frequently comorbid with AUD, include anxiety and major depressive disorders [94] (Fig. 2). Clinical observations demonstrate that individuals with these affective disorders are more likely to report moderate to severe AUD [94]. Furthermore, the co-occurrence of AUD and depression has been associated with higher relapse risk, worse treatment outcomes, and a seven-fold increase in suicide risk [95]; mechanistically, one of the key brain regions linking AUD with comorbid depression is abnormal activity in the rostrodorsal anterior cingulate cortex [96].

In humans, early withdrawal from chronic alcohol intake is associated with increased anxiety and depressed mood, which typically resolve within 3–6 weeks, whereas alcohol craving and relapse risk persist much longer [97]. In fact, long-term alcohol use leads to neuroadaptive changes that disrupt emotional processing and persist during prolonged abstinence. The resulting behavioral phenotype, often described as a shift in affective processing, is characterized by increased sensitivity to stress or even minor challenges, which may induce negative affect (e.g., fear, worry, impatience, and sadness), craving, and relapse [97].

Consistent with clinical observations, ethanol discontinuation in animal models has negative consequences on mood regulation. Both early and long-term ethanol deprivation induce anxiety-like phenotype in the EPM in adult Wistar rats and C57BL/6J mice [28–30]. Moreover, both short-term (3 or 7 days) and prolonged (2 or 5 weeks) ethanol deprivation have been shown to induce depression-like behavior, as evidenced by increased immobility time in C57BL/6 and Kunming mice [20, 32, 33, 98].

Notably, both AUD and anxiety and depressive disorders are closely linked to stress (Fig. 2). In humans, stress-related factors, such as interpersonal conflicts, trauma, and adverse life events, have been shown to increase the risk of alcohol use and relapse [99, 100]. For example, challenging circumstances, such as the COVID-19 pandemic as a source of psychosocial distress, have been associated with higher alcohol intake, increased craving, and relapse risk in individuals with AUD [101]. Importantly, recent evidence indicates that women are more likely than men to consume ethanol in response to negative emotional states, and that stress, trauma, and negative affect constitute key risk factors for AUD development in women, suggesting that there are sex-specific mechanisms underlying drinking to cope [100]. Rodent studies further confirm that exposure to stress increases ethanol consumption and relapse-like drinking behavior [102, 103]. It has been proposed that amygdala-centered circuits have been linked to sex-dependent differences in stress-induced alcohol seeking [104].

One proposed biomarker of AUD risk and relapse is dysfunction within the prefrontal cortex pathways involved in stress and reward regulation. Impaired prefrontal cortex over limbic-striatal circuits contributes to increased emotional distress, leading to heightened craving and relapse risk [105]. Moreover, interindividual variations in alcohol-related neuroadaptations within these circuits may influence treatment response and recovery outcomes [105].

It must be noted that there is clinical and preclinical evidence suggesting that chronic pain is closely linked to AUD [106] (Fig. 2). By affecting stress and reward systems, chronic pain reduces accumbal dopamine release, thereby promoting anhedonia and a negative affective state, which may contribute to compulsive alcohol use [106]. In line with these observations, rodent studies show that mice with neuropathic pain exhibit increased ethanol intake, as well as anxiety- and depression-like behaviors [107]. Another study indicates that pain-related anxiety-like phenotype is accompanied by reduced serotonergic activity in the DR nucleus [108], suggesting that pain-induced negative affect may represent an additional pathway to drinking to cope, partially involving central 5-HT dysfunction.

Overall, these findings indicate that negative affect represents a mechanistic hub linking stress, ethanol intake, and chronic pain to AUD risk, potentially mediated by dysfunction of prefrontal–limbic–striatal circuits.

The serotonergic system and alcohol

Serotonergic deficits associated with increased alcohol drinking and AUD risk

According to DSM-5 [9], the heritability of AUD is estimated at 40–60%, depending on the population studied and the methods applied (e.g., family or twin studies), highlighting the combined impact of genetic and environmental factors. Converging evidence from clinical and animal studies suggests that abnormalities in 5-HT function causally contribute to increased ethanol intake and vulnerability to AUD. For example, genetic predisposition to depression – often linked to reduced central 5-HT signaling – has been associated with an increased risk of AUD [109]. In addition, both depressed patients (mostly women) and alcohol-dependent individuals (predominantly men) with a family history of alcohol dependence show lower CSF 5-HIAA concentrations [56, 110] (Fig. 1). Notably, both control and alcohol-dependent subjects (predominantly men) with a family history of alcohol dependence also exhibit reduced prefrontal 5-HT2A receptor binding [111] (Fig. 1).

As in humans, variations in 5-HT tone may contribute to differences in alcohol preference across rodent lines. For example, male Fawn-Hooded rats, which exhibit high ethanol preference, display lower 5-HT and 5-HIAA levels in the brainstem, and region-dependent alterations in 5-HT turnover compared with Wistar rats [112]. Male Sardinian alcohol-preferring rats (sP) show lower 5-HT and 5-HIAA levels in the frontal cortex relative to Sardinian alcohol non-preferring (sNP) controls [113]. They also exhibit reduced 5-HT2A receptor binding in cortical regions, including the prefrontal cortex, medial prefrontal cortex, and post-genual anterior cingulate cortex, and a blunted 5-HT2A receptor-mediated head-shake response following intramedial prefrontal cortex administration of the preferential 5-HT2A receptor agonist DOI [114]. These findings indicate attenuated 5-HT signaling, and reduced postsynaptic prefrontal 5-HT2A receptor function in these high-ethanol preferring rats. In the male Indiana alcohol-preferring (P) and high-alcohol-drinking (HAD) rat lines, lower tissue 5-HT and/or 5-HIAA concentrations have been reported in the nucleus accumbens, hypothalamus, frontal cortex, and hippocampus, with the largest deficits in P rats observed in the hypothalamus and hippocampus [115, 116]. Moreover, P rats showed reduced 5-HT content in the DR nucleus, fewer serotonergic neurons in the MR and DR nuclei, and decreased serotonergic innervation in hippocampal, accumbal, and cortical regions [117, 118]. Despite these tissue deficits, microdialysis studies revealed elevated basal extracellular 5-HT levels in the nucleus accumbens of P rats, which may be a compensatory response to decreased serotonergic innervation in this brain region [119]. In Myers’ high ethanol-preferring (mHEP) rats, alterations in 5-HT content were sex specific – both sexes showed increased striatal 5-HT levels, whereas females exhibited reduced 5-HT and 5-HIAA levels in the nucleus accumbens, and males had decreased 5-HT content in the hypothalamus [120]. Moreover, ethanol-preferring Alko Alcohol (AA) male rats exhibited higher whole brain 5-HIAA levels after probenecid treatment compared with water-selecting (Alko Non-Alcohol; ANA) rats [121], suggesting increased 5-HT metabolism in this rat line.

Together, these clinical and preclinical findings indicate that inherited central serotonergic alterations, reflected mainly as deficits in 5-HT and/or 5-HIAA levels in the brain, fewer serotonergic projections to the cortex and hippocampus, as well as reduced prefrontal 5-HT2A receptor binding, may be associated with increased alcohol intake and AUD risk.

5-HT synthesis

Several variants of TPH2, the key enzyme for central 5-HT synthesis, have been associated with AUD-related affective disorders, including major depression, ADHD, and suicidal behavior [2]. However, functional evidence in the context of 5-HT regulation is limited to the G-703T polymorphism (rs4570625), located in the promoter region of the TPH2 gene. The T allele of this variant has been shown to reduce reporter gene expression in vitro [164], suggesting functional regulation of TPH2 expression. T-allele carriers have shown greater amygdala responsiveness to emotional stimuli as well as increased activation in prefrontal and parietal areas during working memory tasks, possibly reflecting compensatory responses to impaired executive control [165, 166]. The T allele has also been linked to emotional instability traits (in females), lower SSRI response (in both sexes), and a higher incidence of stress-induced depressive symptoms (in females) [167–169], phenotypes indicative of attenuated 5-HT function. Conversely, in social anxiety patients (of both sexes) carrying the T allele, an increased accumulation of [11C]5-HTP in the anterior cingulate cortex and amygdala has been reported, suggesting enhanced 5-HT synthesis in these regions [64]. These findings indicate that the T allele in the G-703T variant is associated with altered 5-HT regulation rather than a consistent decrease in 5-HT levels.

Nevertheless, in relation to alcohol, the rs4570625 variant has shown no association with alcohol intake among college students [126] or with alcohol dependence in adults [122–124]. Similarly, no association was reported between this variant and Cloninger’s type II alcohol-dependent males, although this study was limited by a small sample size [125]. The rs4570625 polymorphism, together with another TPH2 variant (rs1843809), has been linked to alcohol-related aggression in males [123]. Several intronic TPH2 variants (e.g., rs1843809, rs6582078) have been associated with alcohol consumption in young adult female twins, whereas rs4290270 and rs1843809 have been linked to alcohol dependence-related depression and anxiety in males [123, 127].

Notably, rodent studies provide evidence linking alterations in TPH2 activity and expression to alcohol preference. In alcohol-preferring male sP rats, attenuated 5-HT signaling extends to a reduced number of TPH2-positive neurons in the DR nucleus compared with sNP rats [170]. In mice, the functional C1473G (P447R) polymorphism in the Tph2 gene – although it does not correspond to any known human polymorphism – reduces TPH2 activity by ca. 50% in vitro [171] and distinguishes strains differing in ethanol preference. Ethanol-avoiding DBA/2 mice [172] are homozygous for the G allele and show reduced (by 45%) striatal 5-HTP synthesis compared with ethanol-preferring C57BL/6J mice [173] carrying the C allele [174]. Similarly, BALB/cJ mice, another low-ethanol-preferring strain [175] with the G allele, exhibit markedly reduced in vivo 5-HT synthesis (the striatum: 70%; the frontal cortex: ca. 50%) and 40% lower total brain 5-HT content compared with 129 × 1/SvJ mice [176], exhibiting slightly higher ethanol preference and carrying the C allele [174]. The BALB/cJ mice also display lower Tph2 transcript level, fewer TPH2-positive neurons in the DR nucleus, and reduced 5-HT and/or 5-HIAA levels in the midbrain, cortex, and hippocampus [177]. Surprisingly, mice carrying the same homozygous G allele on a C57BL/6 genetic background show reduced (by ca. 30%) 5-HT synthesis rate but only modest (9%) decreases in total brain 5-HT content [178], indicating that neurochemical consequences of C1473G variant are strongly modulated by the broader genomic context. Importantly, ethanol drinking behavior has not yet been assessed in this mouse line, therefore, strain-dependent genetic effects on ethanol preference remain unclear.

Together, although current clinical evidence suggests a weak association between TPH2 variants and AUD, potential sex-specific effects remain unclear due to the underrepresentation of females, highlighting the need for future studies with sex-balanced cohorts. While findings from selectively bred alcohol-preferring sP rats support a link between reduced serotonergic function and increased alcohol intake, results from mice carrying the Tph2 C1473G polymorphism are inconsistent, and may stem from the fact that these are standard mouse strains, whose validity as models for studying AUD has been repeatedly questioned [179].

Multiple polymorphisms in the gene encoding AADC (DDC), including rs3779084, rs880028, and rs2122822, have been linked to alcohol consumption in young adult females [127]. Additional DDC variants have been associated with the maximum number of alcoholic drinks in two U.S. cohorts [129]. Notably, the identified rs11575522 and rs11575542 variants exhibited modulatory effects on DDC expression in the brain [129], suggesting a potential contribution of these functional variants to AUD vulnerability. More recently, the rs11575457 polymorphism was also associated with AUD [128], however, its clinical significance remains unclear. Notably, the AADC participates not only in 5-HT synthesis but also catalyzes the decarboxylation of TRP to tryptamine and the second step of dopamine synthesis. Consequently, DDC variants may influence AUD risk not only through serotonergic but also dopaminergic (and trace-amine) signaling.

The 5-HT transport

Another genetic factor influencing serotonergic signaling is the solute carrier family 6 member 4 (SLC6A4), which encodes the 5-HTT. The 5-HTTLPR polymorphism in the 5’ regulatory region of SLC6A4 occurs in short (S) and long (L) allelic variants. Carriers of the S allele show reduced transporter expression and lower 5-HT uptake, whereas L carriers display higher SERT expression, 5-HT uptake, and 5-HT1A receptor binding potential, suggesting reduced 5-HT neurotransmission due to increased 5-HT clearance [180, 181]. The tri-allelic 5-HTTLPR classification further distinguishes the LA (the traditional L allele) and LG carrying an A-to-G substitution (rs25531) and functionally resembling the S allele [182, 183].

The S allele has been associated with negative information processing in childhood, anxiety-related personality traits (i.e., higher harm avoidance, neuroticism, and tension), and reduced response to citalopram treatment in adolescent patients with depression or anxiety disorders [169, 184, 185]. In relation to ethanol, the S allele has been associated with binge drinking in college students [130] and higher ethanol tolerance in young adults [132], suggesting its role in early risky drinking. A meta-analysis of 17 studies on 5-HTTLPR polymorphism in adults with alcohol dependence revealed that the S allele is associated with susceptibility to alcohol dependence, particularly severe, early-onset, or comorbid (co-occurring major depression, ADHD, or antisocial personality disorder) forms [131]. However, individual studies have reported divergent findings. For example, in Japanese alcohol-dependent patients experiencing seizures or delirium during withdrawal, the L allele carriers showed an earlier onset of alcohol dependence compared with S/S homozygotes, whereas the S/S genotype was less frequent in alcohol-dependent individuals with antisocial behavior than in controls [186]. These findings suggest that the link between 5-HTTLPR variants and AUD may depend on population characteristics as well as the degree of clinical stratification of AUD subtypes. Further studies on alcohol craving in individuals with alcohol dependence suggested an association between the L allele and higher cue-induced craving compared with S carriers [133]. It is worth noting that although healthy L carriers display increased 5-HTT expression and 5-HT uptake and, potentially, lower 5-HT neurotransmission, abstinent alcohol-dependent individuals with the LL genotype display reduced raphe SERT availability, which negatively correlates with prior alcohol intake [187]. This may reflect alcohol-induced neurotoxic effects [187] or compensatory downregulation of SERT aimed at increasing synaptic 5-HT availability following long-term 5-HT deficits. Notably, individuals with alcohol dependence carrying the LL genotype show a blunted growth hormone response to apomorphine challenge, reflecting reduced sensitivity of postsynaptic dopamine receptors [188]. These findings indicate that alcohol-dependent L carriers may also exhibit disrupted serotonergic regulation of dopamine signaling, potentially leading to dopaminergic hypofunction.

Evidence for an association between the SLC6A4 intron 2 (STin2) VNTR polymorphism and AUD remains inconsistent. One study reported increased frequency of the 10/10 genotype in male Cloninger’s type II alcohol-dependent patients [125], whereas another found no association between the STin2 VNTR genotypes (comprising 9-, 10-, and 12-repeat alleles) and type II alcohol dependence characterized by antisocial traits [134]. Overall, observed effects are small and population-specific. Although VNTR variants may modulate reporter gene expression in vitro [189], the in vivo functional relevance of these genotypes has yet to be established.

Human findings indicate that SLC6A4 polymorphisms affect 5-HT signaling by altering SERT expression and 5-HT reuptake rates. The S allele, which reduces transporter expression and 5-HT uptake, has been associated with increased vulnerability to AUD. The L allele, typically linked to higher SERT expression, has been implicated in ethanol craving and may show reduced SERT availability following chronic ethanol exposure. Rat lines with a high-ethanol-drinking phenotype provide partial support for altered SERT-related regulation of 5-HT tone. In sP rats, decreases in SERT-positive fibers have been observed in the cingulate cortex and nucleus accumbens shell [170]. In P rats, elevated basal levels of extracellular 5-HT in the nucleus accumbens, accompanied by a lower in vivo extraction fraction [119] may indicate reduced SERT function in this region, leading to impaired 5-HT reuptake.

Receptor signaling

Another level of 5-HT regulation relevant to AUD involves variants in 5-HT receptor genes that may influence 5-HT tone or behavioral responses to ethanol through altered receptor function. For the 5-HT1A receptor, an over-representation of the CC genotype of the promoter polymorphism C-1019 G (rs6295) in the HTR1A gene has been reported in hospitalized men with alcohol dependence, whereas more severe withdrawal symptoms were found in the G-allele (CG/GG) carriers [135]. However, the rs6295 variant showed no associations with AUD in women [136] or with suicide attempts among alcohol-dependent subjects (predominantly men) [89]. Notably, the functional effects of the G allele are region-specific, potentially increasing 5-HT1A autoreceptor expression in raphe neurons while reducing postsynaptic receptor mRNA in the prefrontal cortex [136], which may result in an overall decrease in 5-HT tone. These findings may suggest that reduced 5-HT tone in G-allele carriers contributes to more severe withdrawal symptoms, while the CC genotype may be associated with vulnerability to specific AUD types. Complementary to human observations, studies in alcohol-preferring P and HAD rats have shown increased 5-HT1A receptor binding density in the medial prefrontal cortex, nucleus accumbens, and ventral hippocampus [115]. This finding may reflect a compensatory increase in receptor protein expression in response to reduced 5-HT level in these regions.

Evidence regarding the role of genetic variation in the HTR1B gene in susceptibility to AUD has been inconsistent. Several studies reported no association between alcohol dependence and C129T (rs6298), T-261G (rs11568817), G861C (rs6296), A-161T (rs130058), or A1180G (rs6297) polymorphisms [134, 137–140]. However, population-specific associations have been reported. In Chinese and Taiwanese Han populations, the A-161T polymorphism was associated with alcohol dependence, particularly in individuals with early onset and a family history of disorder [141, 142]. A nominal association between rs2000292 and alcohol dependence was also found in Taiwanese Han men [143]. In contrast, studies in Brazilian subjects of European ancestry identified an association for T-261G, but not A-161T or G861C [144]. Further studies have shown that the G861C variant may be related to specific alcohol dependence phenotypes, including antisocial alcohol dependence, a mixed Cloninger’s type I/II (with the inactive aldehyde dehydrogenase 2; ALDH2), or altered behavioral traits in alcohol-dependent subjects [145–148]. Moreover, the functional variant rs13212041, which regulates HTR1B receptor expression, has been linked to alcohol abuse, but not to alcohol dependence [144, 149].

Findings on the link between HTR2A polymorphisms and AUD have shown that the G-1438A (rs6311) variant has been associated with impulsive behavior in alcohol-dependent patients but not alcohol dependence per se [150, 152], although an over-representation of the G allele was reported in Japanese alcohol-dependent individuals with inactive ALDH2 [151]. The T102C (rs6313) polymorphism was associated with alcohol abuse in Taiwanese Han men with behavioral problems [157] and with alcohol dependence, higher impulsivity, and relapse risk in a Caucasian sample [153–155]. By contrast, several studies reported no association of rs6313 with alcohol dependence [156], the early-onset/antisocial subtype [134], or withdrawal severity [135]. Similarly, no relation between H452Y polymorphism (rs6314) and alcohol dependence was reported [158].

In the case of the 5-HT2C receptor, no association has been observed between HTR2C promoter variants (e.g., rs3813928) and alcohol dependence [159]. Similarly, the C23S (rs6318) variant showed no association with alcohol dependence, including antisocial subtypes or alcohol withdrawal symptoms [134, 158, 160].

In contrast, variants in the 5-HT3B receptor subunit gene HTR3B have shown some association with AUD. The gain-of-function C allele of the A386C (rs1176744) variant, associated with enhanced receptor responsiveness to 5-HT, predicted increased risk of alcohol dependence in the male African-American population [161]. In addition, the intronic rs3782025 variant was associated with alcohol dependence comorbid with antisocial personality disorder [162].

Finally, preliminary evidence also points to a possible role of HTR7 variants (rs11596518, rs7904560) and specific haplotypes in alcohol dependence, though these results require further confirmation [163].

Collectively, human genetic studies indicate a potential contribution of HTR1A, HTR1B, HTR2A, HTR3B, and possibly HTR7, to AUD risk or specific clinical subtypes.

TSPAN5: a novel modulator of 5-HT function. Recent studies have identified polymorphisms in the Tetraspanin 5 (TSPAN5) gene, a member of the tetraspanin superfamily, as being associated with plasma 5-HT levels and SSRI treatment outcomes in depressed patients [190]. Functional studies revealed that TSPAN5 regulates key genes of 5-HT synthesis and metabolism, such as TPH1, TPH2, DDC, and MAOA [190]. Notably, variants in this alcohol-responsive TSPAN5 gene [191] have been linked to AUD liability in African-American populations [192] and to acamprosate treatment response in alcohol-dependent individuals [191]. Together, these observations suggest that TSPAN5 variations may influence AUD risk and response to SSRI and acamprosate.

Effects of manipulating 5-HT levels on alcohol-related behaviors

Human studies. Manipulation of 5-HT tone in the brain has yielded mixed effects on alcohol-related behaviors. Young men with a family history of alcohol dependence who received a TRP-deficient mixture made more commission errors in a go/no-go learning task, reflecting a heightened behavioral disinhibition [193]. These findings suggested that reduced 5-HT synthesis may be the underlying mechanism of disinhibition in genetically vulnerable AUD individuals. Similarly, TRP-deficient amino acid beverage increased impulsivity in healthy subjects (predominantly women) following a moderate ethanol dose [194]. In alcohol-dependent or recently detoxified patients (1–3 months abstinent; predominantly men), TRP depletion did not alter alcohol consumption [195] or cue-evoked craving [195, 196], while in patients (of both sexes) with co-morbid alcohol dependence and major depression, it increased the urge to drink [197]. Interestingly, it has also been reported that the effects of TRP depletion in alcohol-dependent subjects depend on the 5-HTTLPR genotype. In L-allele carriers, acute TRP depletion reduced the urge to drink compared to SS-carriers [133]. In LL-homozygotes, chronic alcohol exposure has been shown to reduce SERT availability in the raphe [187], which may elevate local 5-HT tone and, paradoxically, contribute to increased craving [133]. Thus, in these individuals, a transient reduction in 5-HT induced by TRP depletion may attenuate the motivation to drink.

Preclinical studies. Preclinical findings on the effects of 5,7-DHT-induced 5-HT depletion on alcohol drinking have yielded heterogeneous results. Neonatal lesions induced by intracerebroventricular (icv) injection of 5,7-DHT reduced 5-HT content in the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, and striatum, resulting in increased total liquid intake but decreased ethanol consumption in a two-bottle choice (TBC) paradigm in adult Wistar rats, with a more pronounced reduction in ethanol intake (accompanied by reduced preference for ethanol) observed in males [198]. These observations suggest that neurodevelopmental reductions in 5-HT levels may disrupt ethanol drinking behavior in adulthood. Icv administration of 5,7-DHT in adult male Wistar or Sprague-Dawley rats with prior ethanol exposure (for 12–14 days, TBC test) did not alter alcohol consumption compared to controls [199, 200]. Similarly, 5,7-DHT-induced lesions targeting the DR and/or MR nuclei that resulted in substantial 5-HT depletion across brain regions did not alter subsequent ethanol drinking behavior in adult male Sprague-Dawley rats previously exposed to a TBC paradigm (for 10 days) [201]. In males of the same rat strain with a history of long-term (6–8 weeks) intermittent access to ethanol in TBC, a severe 5-HT lesion induced by icv injection of 5,7-DHT, leading to a 76% reduction in TPH2 immunostaining in the DR nucleus, failed to alter subsequent ethanol drinking, despite more pronounced 5-HT loss in drinkers compared with non-drinkers [202]. By contrast, a less spatially precise intracisternal injection of 5,7-DHT in male Sprague-Dawley rats, delivering the neurotoxin closer (compared to icv injection) to raphe neurons and leading to fairly selective 5-HT lesions, after 2 weeks of ethanol exposure, increased alcohol consumption in both continuously drinking and ethanol-deprived animals [203]. Genetic models further confirm the modulatory role of 5-HT deficits on ethanol intake. Male Tph2 R439H knock-in mice, exhibiting a partial (60–80%) reduction in brain 5-HT levels, consumed more ethanol-sucrose solution in a TBC, showed increased ethanol preference, and were less sensitive to ethanol’s sedative effects than wild-type controls [204] (Fig. 2). In a separate study using Tph2 knock-in mice of both sexes, increased motivation for quinine-ethanol solutions was observed only under aversive conditions during the familiarization phase, whereas no genotype differences were observed under standard conditions [205] (Fig. 2). Constitutive male Tph2–/– mice, with nearly complete and lifelong depletion of central 5-HT (>98%), showed higher ethanol intake in a TBC paradigm compared with wild-type controls [26] (Fig. 2). These findings suggest that the modulatory effects of reduced 5-HT levels on ethanol intake may also depend on the extent and anatomical location of 5-HT lesions, i.e., whether they affect raphe cell bodies or their terminal projection fields.

On the other hand, boosting 5-HT synthesis by acute or subchronic administration of the 5-HT precursor 5-HTP has been shown to decrease ethanol consumption in a TBC paradigm in male sP rats [206]. In male Long-Evans rats exposed to an alcohol-containing diet for 8 days, followed by an acute (9 hours) cessation and re-introduction of the liquid diet, 5-HTP induced a biphasic effect on ethanol consumption – an initial increase followed by a transient reduction in drinking behavior [207]. In genetic models of lifelong SERT inactivation, i.e., SERT–/– mice of both sexes, lower ethanol consumption was reported in a free-choice paradigm compared with wild-type controls [208]. Conversely, in adolescent SERT–/– Wistar rats, females consumed more ethanol in a TBC paradigm than males, with SERT–/– females drinking more ethanol than their wild-type controls, whereas no genotype effect was observed in males [209] (Fig. 2). Notably, in SERT–/– offspring of male drinkers, ethanol consumption was increased in both sexes, and sensitivity to ethanol-induced motor impairment was reduced [209].

Consistent with these findings on the modulatory effects of 5-HT levels on ethanol intake, preclinical studies provide evidence that 5-HT-related variations can also shape neurobiological responses to ethanol. In male mice carrying the 1473 G (G/G) polymorphism in the Tph2 gene, which reduces TPH2 activity, chronic passive ethanol administration elevated midbrain Bdnf expression and decreased expression of the apoptosis marker CASP3 in the frontal cortex, while increasing its expression in the hippocampus compared with C/C carriers [210]. Notably, the C1473G polymorphism in male mice has also been shown to modulate the acute effects of ethanol on c-fos expression and 5-HT metabolism in the brain [211].

Overall, findings in humans suggest that the impact of changes in 5-HT tone on alcohol-related behaviors is inter-individual: in subjects vulnerable to AUD and in those with comorbid AUD and depression, reduced 5-HT levels are harmful, while in carriers of the L allele of the 5-HTTLPR polymorphism, lowering 5-HT can be beneficial. These observations indicate that both hypo- and hyperserotonergic states may increase the urge to drink, depending on clinical and genetic features. Rodent studies reveal that disrupted 5-HT tone modulates alcohol drinking behavior, although the direction of these changes may vary depending on the timing of the manipulation, as well as the severity and anatomical location of 5-HT loss. Importantly, the influence of manipulations in 5-HT tone may also be shaped by prior alcohol exposure, as ethanol itself may induce neuroadaptations in the 5-HT system. Although some evidence suggests sex-dependent differences in the effects of disrupted 5-HT function during development on ethanol intake, overall evidence for sex-specific effects of 5-HT on alcohol drinking remains limited and warrants further investigation.

Beyond pre-existing predispositions in 5-HT profiles, alcohol exposure itself may also shape 5-HT signaling in the brain (Fig. 1; Table 3).

Human studies have shown that in male heavy-drinking individuals with alcohol dependence, CSF TRP concentrations increased during intoxication and early abstinence, normalized on day 8, and increased again after 3 weeks of abstinence [212]. Consistently, increased CSF TRP levels were also reported in male alcohol-dependent patients with delirium tremens [213]. Although blood TRP levels were not altered in male individuals with alcohol dependence [216], TRP availability in the plasma appeared to fluctuate during the abstinence period. When the entire cohort of alcohol-dependent patients was analyzed, the lowest plasma TRP/LNAA ratio was observed one day after alcohol discontinuation and increased over the subsequent weeks of abstinence [217]. Only in early-onset alcohol-dependent individuals, a low plasma TRP/LNAA ratio during 2–3 weeks of abstinence has been associated with aggression and depressive symptoms [217] (Fig. 1). Notably, limited preclinical studies in male C57BL/6J mice showed no change in plasma or limbic forebrain TRP concentrations across different chronic ethanol drinking paradigms (drinking in the dark, DID; TBC) [214, 215]. However, plasma, but not brain, TRP levels increased 24 h after the final ethanol exposure in the TBC paradigm, but not after DID or vapor exposure [214, 215]. These findings suggest that fluctuations in TRP levels and its availability in the brain are most pronounced during abstinence and are primarily related to the presence of 5-HT-related symptoms during this period (e.g., depressive symptoms or delirium), rather than to chronic ethanol exposure per se. Importantly, serum TRP levels alone may not represent a reliable marker of central serotonergic function.

Human and rodent studies have reported that alcohol consumption may influence the activity of the 5-HT-synthesizing enzyme TPH2, catalyzing the hydroxylation of TRP to 5-HTP. Postmortem human studies showed that alcohol-dependent individuals displayed increased TPH2 mRNA and protein in the DR and MR nuclei [218, 219]. Importantly, the number of 5-HT neurons in the DR nucleus in alcohol-dependent subjects did not differ from healthy controls [218], suggesting a qualitative upregulation of TPH2 within 5-HT neurons rather than a change in cell number. Similarly, in individuals with comorbid depression and alcohol dependence who died by suicide, elevated TPH2 protein level was seen in the dorsal subnucleus of the DR nucleus [221]. Moreover, in vivo imaging studies using positron emission tomography (PET) with α-[11C]-methyl-L-TRP revealed a reduction in the rate of 5-HT synthesis in male alcohol-dependent individuals in brain regions implicated in judgment, self-control, and regulation of emotions, including the medial prefrontal cortex and dorsal anterior cingulate gyrus [220]. Preclinical studies have shown that ethanol consumption (one-bottle paradigm without choice) for 6 weeks decreased TPH2 protein level and enhanced TPH2 enzymatic activity without altering Tph2 mRNA in the raphe nuclei of C57BL/6J mice [222]. Similarly, in the entire cohort of C57BL/6N mice, chronic ethanol consumption in a TBC paradigm for 4 weeks did not alter Tph2 expression either in the raphe nuclei or in serotonergic projection areas such as the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus [26]. However, when the animals were divided into “high” and “low ethanol-drinking” groups, Tph2 transcript levels were higher in the raphe nuclei and lower in the prefrontal cortex of mice exhibiting a high ethanol-drinking phenotype compared with mice with a low drinking behavior [26]. Short-term discontinuation (3 days) of ethanol intake in “high ethanol-drinking” mice decreased Tph2 mRNA levels in the hippocampus, but not in the prefrontal cortex [32]. Together, these findings suggest that heavy alcohol consumption alters TPH2 expression and/or activity in serotonergic neurons both in the raphe nuclei – likely reflecting increased TPH2 activity – and in serotonergic projections – possibly indicating reduced TPH2 activity – particularly in the prefrontal cortex, a region critically involved in decision-making, emotional regulation, and impulse control (Fig. 1). These changes may reflect a functional adjustment of the 5-HT system to chronic ethanol action. In addition, ethanol deprivation may also modify TPH2 activity in the hippocampus, however, further studies are needed to clarify these effects. In the context of AUD heterogeneity, given that interindividual differences in Tph2 transcript levels shaped the development of “high” or “low ethanol-drinking” phenotype, future studies will verify whether TPH2 may be considered a marker of vulnerability to excessive alcohol consumption.

With respect to synaptic 5-HT availability, autoradiographic postmortem analyses in alcohol-dependent individuals (predominantly males) revealed reduced SERT density in the perigenual anterior cingulate cortex [223]. In contrast, PET studies conducted in alcohol-dependent individuals (including both sexes) 2 weeks post-abstinence reported no changes in SERT availability across several brain regions, including the frontal cortex, thalamus, hippocampus, amygdala, and raphe [224]. However, during weeks 3–5 of abstinence, reduced SERT binding was found in the brainstem of male participants, and was correlated with lifetime alcohol intake as well as depression and anxiety ratings during abstinence [225] (Fig. 1). Preclinical research showed that chronic ethanol exposure did not alter SERT (Slc6a4) gene transcript or protein levels in the raphe nuclei of male C57BL/6J mice [222]. In contrast, in male Sprague-Dawley rats, Slc6a4 expression in the medial prefrontal cortex and nucleus accumbens was differentially modulated by chronic ethanol depending on whether the animals displayed pessimistic or optimistic cognitive biases [226] (Fig. 1). Specifically, following ethanol exposure, pessimistic rats, which consumed higher amounts of ethanol than optimistic rats, showed reduced Slc6a4 transcript levels in the nucleus accumbens but not in the prefrontal cortex, whereas optimistic rats displayed reduced Slc6a4 expression in the prefrontal cortex, with no changes in the nucleus accumbens [226]. This latter finding suggests that stable cognitive traits associated with alcohol consumption – may also shape how ethanol affects the 5-HT reuptake processes. Overall, SERT expression may be shaped by multiple factors, including AUD stage, and individual behavioral traits. Future studies should incorporate personality and cognitive traits to determine whether SERT expression may serve as a marker of AUD heterogeneity.

Ethanol consumption has also been shown to affect 5-HT neurotransmission and turnover in both human and animal studies. In male alcohol-dependent subjects, the CSF 5-HIAA level was increased during alcohol intoxication [227], indicating altered 5-HT turnover in individuals with AUD. Notably, increased CSF 5-HT and 5-HIAA levels were also observed in male alcohol-dependent patients with delirium tremens [213]. In contrast, after three weeks of abstinence, individuals with early-onset alcohol dependence showed lower CSF 5-HIAA levels than those with late-onset [56] (Fig. 1). During prolonged abstinence (weeks 4–9 or at least 3 months), male individuals with alcohol dependence displayed reduced CSF 5-HIAA levels compared to non-dependent participants or subjects assessed during the post-intoxication phase [227, 228]. In the first two weeks of withdrawal, reduced plasma 5-HT levels were observed in alcohol-dependent subjects relative to controls, however, these decreases did not correlate with craving or with Cloninger’s subtypes [235]. In rodent studies, extracellular 5-HT levels – reflecting 5-HT availability in the synaptic cleft – have been widely examined in the nucleus accumbens using the in vivo microdialysis method. In selectively bred female P rats, continuous ethanol consumption for 6 weeks in a TBC paradigm reduced extracellular 5-HT levels in the nucleus accumbens relative to controls, whereas deprivation periods did not alter these levels [231]. Given reports of elevated accumbal 5-HT levels in alcohol-naïve P rats, it may be speculated that ethanol is consumed to normalize the initially elevated 5-HT neurotransmission in that region. In contrast, in male C57BL/6J mice, acute ethanol, but not repeated bouts of high ethanol consumption for 6 days, significantly increased extracellular 5-HT release in the nucleus accumbens [229]. 5-HT levels were also elevated in this brain region in male Wistar rats following chronic ethanol consumption (self-administration and liquid diet), decreased during acute withdrawal (up to 8 h), and increased after re-exposure, but did not return to pre-withdrawal levels, further supporting the notion that chronic ethanol exposure may impair accumbal 5-HT synthesis or reuptake [230]. Several studies have also examined regional brain tissue 5-HT and 5-HIAA content. In alcohol-preferring AA rats, one month of ethanol consumption increased brain 5-HT content and 5-HIAA accumulation compared with ANA rats, possibly due to increased 5-HT turnover in AA rats at the onset of drinking [121]. However, in consequence of long-term (20 months) exposure to ethanol, AA rats exhibited no significant changes in 5-HT content in several brain regions [232]. Findings in male Wistar rats were region-dependent: ethanol increased 5-HT content, and decreased 5-HIAA/5-HT turnover in the hypothalamus, while in the striatum, it reduced 5-HT concentration and enhanced 5-HT turnover [233]. In male C57BL/6J mice, ethanol consumption for 6 weeks in a one-bottle no-choice paradigm increased 5-HIAA content in the midbrain and hippocampus, and elevated the 5-HIAA/5-HT turnover ratio in the midbrain, frontal cortex and hippocampus, while 5-HT concentrations remained unchanged in several brain areas [222]. Consistent with these findings, no changes in brain or plasma 5-HT levels were observed in these mice (males) across other ethanol-drinking paradigms or one day after abstinence [214, 215]. Similarly, prolonged ethanol deprivation (1–7 days) after 5 months of ethanol exposure in females of the same mouse strain produced no changes in striatal or hippocampal 5-HT or 5-HIAA levels [234]. Interestingly, in low-ethanol preferring female Balb/c mice subjected to the same ethanol drinking paradigm and withdrawal period, striatal 5-HIAA content was reduced, and hippocampal 5-HT levels progressively decreased [234]. These observations indicate that chronic ethanol exposure tends to enhance brain 5-HT turnover (Fig. 1), whereas the effects of ethanol withdrawal are more heterogeneous. The accompanying reduction or lack of changes in 5-HT levels, particularly in the nucleus accumbens, may suggest that chronic ethanol exposure leads to increased 5-HT utilization that exceeds the neurotransmitter synthesis. Importantly, differences in 5-HT and 5-HIAA content observed between mouse strains with distinct ethanol-drinking preferences further highlight the contribution of 5-HT dysregulation to vulnerability to AUD and support the need for patient stratification according to their serotonergic vulnerability.

The influence of alcohol consumption on 5-HT receptor function in humans has been investigated to a limited extent. For example, blood HTR3A transcript levels were positively correlated with craving scores in male Han Chinese individuals with AUD [236]. In contrast, postmortem 5-HT1B receptor binding in the prefrontal cortex was not altered in alcohol-dependent subjects of both sexes [237]. Functional aspects of serotonergic signaling have also been examined using neuroendocrine challenge tests, which revealed that in alcohol-dependent individuals (predominantly men), long-term alcohol use decreased the plasma PRL response to d-fenfluramine or citalopram, reflecting diminished central 5-HT function [238, 239] (Fig. 1). Importantly, an earlier age of onset of heavy drinking was associated with lower PRL response in men [238]. In later-onset Cloninger’s type I male individuals, long-term heavy drinking and attenuated PRL responses were linked to higher anxiety liability [87], implying that in a subset of type I individuals, lower 5-HT function may be linked to negative mood states (Table 1). During abstinence, no significant difference in PRL response to citalopram or fenfluramine was found between abstinent male subjects and controls, and PRL responses were unrelated to personality scores [240, 241]. However, when abstinent male individuals with long-term alcohol dependence were divided into subgroups based on their PRL responses to d,l-fenfluramine, low responders showed higher venturesomeness and extraversion personality scores than high responders [242]. These findings suggest that reduced PRL responses may be linked to personality traits associated with impaired impulse control and behavioral disinhibition in individuals with AUD, and may also serve as a potential biomarker for AUD subtype stratification and relapse risk prediction (Fig. 1).

Corresponding rodent studies examining serotonergic function in the brain following ethanol exposure, including 5-HT receptor function and 5-HT-dependent neuronal activity, have shown that continuous ethanol consumption for 6 weeks in a TBC paradigm in female P rats reduced 5-HT3 receptor function in the nucleus accumbens relative to control [231]. In the standard C57BL/6J mouse strain (males), voluntary ethanol drinking in a continuous TBC paradigm heightened 5-HT1A autoreceptor sensitivity [245], whereas chronic ethanol consumption in one-bottle choice procedure did not alter 5-HT1A receptor mRNA or protein levels in several brain areas [222]. Intermittent exposure to ethanol abolished the 5-HT1A receptor-mediated inhibitory effect of 5-HT on neuron firing in the lateral orbitofrontal cortex [246]. Notably, in the same mouse strain, chronic ethanol intake using a one-bottle choice procedure reduced 5-HT2A receptor protein levels in the frontal cortex [222]. In contrast, in male Wistar rats, chronic ethanol exposure increased 5-HT2A receptor binding in the striatum and hypothalamus [233], suggesting that these region-dependent differences in 5-HT2A receptor levels may reflect adaptive responses to local 5-HT availability. In male Sprague-Dawley rats, chronic exposure to ethanol vapor disrupted 5-HT2C receptor function and attenuated the ability of 5-HT to increase GABA signaling (i.e., enhance sIPSCs frequency) in the central nucleus of the amygdala [243]. During withdrawal, a partial recovery of 5-HT modulation of GABA signaling was observed in the amygdala, whereas reduced modulatory effects of 5-HT on GABAergic transmission were detected in the infralimbic cortex [243, 244]. In addition, short-term ethanol deprivation in „high-drinking” C57BL/6N mice enhanced 5-HT2C receptor mRNA editing efficiency in the hippocampus, but not the prefrontal cortex, shifting receptor isoforms toward less active forms [32], suggesting altered hippocampal 5-HT availability and associated changes in 5-HT2C receptor sensitivity during early withdrawal. Together, clinical observations suggest an overall attenuation of 5-HT function, particularly in individuals with early-onset AUD and those with negative emotional states during abstinence. Animal studies indicate that alcohol exposure attenuates serotonergic modulation of neuronal activity and dysregulates multiple 5-HT receptors, including 5-HT1A autoreceptor sensitivity as well as 5-HT2A receptor levels, 5-HT2C and 5-HT3 receptor function in limbic and reward-related brain regions. Future studies are required to determine how these receptor systems contribute to AUD heterogeneity and to different AUD stages.

Overall, human and rodent studies indicate that chronic exposure to ethanol dysregulates central 5-HT function, although the direction of these changes is context- and/or pathway-dependent. In humans, serotonergic dysfunction is related to age of onset, personality traits, and affective withdrawal symptoms. Chronic alcohol use has been linked to increased TPH2 activity in the raphe nuclei and reduced SERT function in the prefrontal cortex, while withdrawal is associated with a general reduction in several markers of central 5-HT function, particularly in early-onset individuals with AUD. In rodents, serotonergic dysregulation is influenced by the animal model of AUD, the ethanol drinking paradigm, genetic background, individual behavioral traits, and withdrawal stage. Ethanol consumption and/or withdrawal alter activity in DR 5-HT projections to the nucleus accumbens, prefrontal cortex, and central amygdala, as well as within DR/MR projections innervating the frontal cortex and hypothalamus, and MR projections to the hippocampus.

Attenuated serotonergic signaling – reflected by increased 5-HT1A autoreceptor sensitivity, reduced 5-HT release and SERT function, and decreased 5-HT receptor activity on serotonergic projection areas – may trigger compensatory mechanisms, including upregulation of the 5-HT-synthesizing enzyme TPH2. Such adaptations may represent an attempt to restore 5-HT tone and maintain central 5-HT homeostasis disrupted by chronic ethanol exposure.

Limitations of current pharmacotherapies

Pharmacotherapy for alcohol dependence aims to reduce craving and compulsive drinking, and is recommended mainly for individuals with more severe AUD [247]. In the European and U.S. guidelines, naltrexone and acamprosate are currently the first-line medications with modest efficacy, while disulfiram is regarded as a second-line treatment [247, 248]. Naltrexone, a µ-opioid receptor antagonist, reduces the reinforcing effects of ethanol but may cause adverse effects and is contraindicated in severe liver dysfunction [13, 247, 249]. Acamprosate, a GABA analogue, attenuates glutamatergic signaling, supports abstinence maintenance, and can be used in liver impairment [14]. Disulfiram irreversibly inhibits acetaldehyde dehydrogenase – an enzyme involved in ethanol metabolism – leading to accumulation of acetaldehyde and unpleasant, potentially severe symptoms following alcohol consumption [247]. Additional pharmacotherapies approved in some countries include baclofen (a GABAB receptor agonist) and nalmefene (a nonselective μ and δ opioid receptor antagonist with partial agonistic activity at κ receptors), as well as off-label drugs such as gabapentin and topiramate, all of which have shown some potential in AUD treatment but are limited by side effects [13, 248]. Although these drugs mainly target the opioid and GABAergic systems, growing evidence showing that 5-HT dysregulation is associated with vulnerability to AUD has motivated the exploration of 5-HT agents as potential treatment strategies for AUD.

SSRIs in the treatment of AUD

Mechanism of SSRI action

The primary mechanism of action of SSRIs is the selective inhibition of SERT, which increases extracellular 5-HT levels in the brain. Depending on the compound and stereoisomer, SSRIs may also interact with other targets, however, the affinity for these targets is much lower than to SERT [250]. For example, fluoxetine (Prozac) consists of R- and S-stereoisomers, both possessing high selectivity for SERT compared with other monoamine transporters, but only R-fluoxetine binds to human 5-HT2A and 5-HT2C receptors, and acts as an antagonist at these sites [251]. Microdialysis studies in rats have shown that acute fluoxetine administration increases extracellular levels of 5-HT, dopamine, and norepinephrine in the prefrontal cortex – a profile not typical for SSRIs and likely related to the antagonistic action of R-fluoxetine at 5-HT2C receptors [251].

Given that SSRIs require chronic administration to produce therapeutic effect, neuroadaptive changes in the 5-HT neuron, are considered the key mechanism underlying their action [250]. These include desensitization of 5-HT1A autoreceptors present on 5-HT neurons in the midbrain or the influence on the proportion of co-released neurotransmitters, such as glutamate. Other adaptive changes due to long-term SSRI administration concern alterations in neural plasticity. Importantly, preclinical studies revealed that chronic (21 days) administration of fluoxetine enhanced dopaminergic neurotransmission in the limbic system [252] and sensitized behavioral responses mediated by postsynaptic D2/D3 receptors [253]. This indicated that SSRIs not only affect 5-HT signaling but also enhance dopaminergic tone in the limbic structures.

Efficacy of SSRIs in AUD

Clinical findings. In early-stage, non-depressed problem drinkers, short-term (2 weeks) treatment with zimelidine (200 mg/day) or citalopram (40 mg/day) reduced alcohol consumption by ca. 20–30%, although interindividual differences in drug response have also been noted [254]. The impact of SSRIs on drinking outcomes presumably resulted from enhanced satiety rather than from antidepressant properties of these drugs [254]. Another study found that zimelidine (200 mg/day), citalopram (40 mg/day), and fluoxetine (80 mg/day) decreased alcohol consumption in early problem drinkers and individuals with alcohol dependence, but fluoxetine’s effect in alcohol-dependent subjects was limited to the first week of treatment [255]. The effect of SSRIs on ethanol intake was seen within a few days and was due to reduced appetitive behavior rather than to antidepressant or anxiolytic properties of these drugs [255]. In addition, fluoxetine (40 mg/day), administered during withdrawal, effectively reduced anxiety and depressive symptoms at days 5–7 of alcohol abstinence [256]. Subsequent studies highlighted a subtype-specific response to SSRIs. In non-depressed alcohol-dependent individuals with mild to moderate severity of alcohol dependence, treatment with fluoxetine (60 mg/day) over 12 weeks had no significant impact on drinking [257]. In Babor’s type A participants, in a 12-week treatment phase, fluoxetine (20 mg/day, increased to 60 mg/day) had no significant effect on drinking outcomes, while in patients with more severe alcohol dependence (type B), fluoxetine was associated with worse outcomes compared to placebo [90] (Table 1). Sertraline (200 mg/day) treatment reduced drinking days and increased the likelihood of maintaining abstinence in type A individuals, but showed no benefit in type B individuals [91] (Table 1). Notably, these positive effects persisted at a 6-month follow-up in type A subjects, whereas type B individuals displayed increased heavy drinking during the post-treatment period [258]. In individuals with alcohol dependence with comorbid major depression, fluoxetine (12-week trial; 20 mg/day, increased to 40 mg/day after two weeks) [259], but not sertraline (10-week trial; 200 mg/day) [260], reduced both alcohol intake and depressive symptoms, with effects persisting over a 1-year follow-up [259]. Moreover, L carriers with early onset alcohol dependence reduced their drinking intensity on high-anxiety days under placebo but not under sertraline treatment (50 mg/day, gradually increased to 200 mg/day) [261], suggesting that genotype and negative affect may moderate the outcome of SSRI therapy.

Preclinical findings. Rodent studies provide mixed results regarding the effects of SSRIs on alcohol-related behaviors. Compared to escitalopram, fluoxetine (2.5–10 mg/kg) was more efficacious in attenuating the somatic signs of acute (2–6 hours) ethanol cessation, such as tremor, agitation, locomotor activity, and wet dog shakes in male Wistar rats [27, 262]. In dependent Wistar rats, acute treatment with fluoxetine (10 mg/kg – a dose known for inducing antidepressant effect) reduced ethanol self-administration in acutely withdrawn animals and delayed recovery of ethanol intake [263]. In sP rats, both acute and subchronic treatment with fluoxetine (5 mg/kg) reduced alcohol consumption [206]. In contrast, treatment with fluoxetine (10 mg/kg for 15 days) during alcohol cessation initially reduced the alcohol deprivation effect in male Wistar rats, followed by an increase in alcohol consumption [264]. In another study, discontinuation of fluoxetine treatment (10 mg/kg for 7 or 14 days) during ethanol abstinence increased subsequent ethanol consumption in Wistar rats during a 2–3-week re-exposure period [265]. The increased ethanol-seeking behavior following fluoxetine treatment may be linked to enhanced glutamatergic receptor function and reduced endocannabinoid signaling in the amygdala [265], suggesting that fluoxetine discontinuation sensitized the mechanisms that trigger relapse.

Taken together, SSRI treatment for AUD shows heterogeneous outcomes that depend on patient phenotype and drug. Beneficial effects are observed in type A AUD individuals and in those with comorbid major depression. Efficacy appears compound-specific: for example, sertraline shows advantages in type A subgroup, while fluoxetine may be beneficial for alcohol-dependent subjects with comorbid depression. Additional preclinical data show that SSRI discontinuation may sensitize relapse-related circuits.

Lessons learned

Although early reductions in drinking behavior may occur following SSRI treatment, their overall efficacy in AUD is highly heterogeneous and appears to depend largely on serotonergic status, which varies across AUD subtypes, affective state, sex and genetic background.

Importantly, SSRIs are not approved as first- or second-line pharmacological treatments for AUD. Large clinical trials have demonstrated limited or no efficacy in reducing alcohol intake. Any potential beneficial effect appears to be confined primarily to selected AUD subgroups, particularly individuals corresponding to the type A phenotype and patients with comorbid major depressive disorder. In the latter case, reduction in drinking is attributable to the alleviation of depressive symptoms rather than to a direct effect on AUD. A further concern is that inappropriate SSRI use – especially in individuals with dysregulated or elevated 5-HT function, may worsen clinical outcomes. Therefore, SSRIs are unlikely to be broadly used as a treatment strategy for AUD.

Serotonergic psychedelics as a potential therapeutic strategy for AUD

In recent years, interest in classic serotonergic psychedelics has resurged as a potential therapeutic approach for AUD. Classic psychedelics include lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), psilocybin (a psychoactive component present in ‘magic mushrooms’) and its active metabolite psilocin, as well as mescaline (present in some cacti), dimethyltryptamine (DMT; often consumed as ayahuasca), and 5-methoxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine (5-MeO-DMT) [266]. Research has shown that following one or two exposures to psychedelics, reductions in depressive and anxiety-like symptoms as well as improvements in mental health, may be observed [267]. By decreasing activity within the default mode network (DMN) and increasing global brain connectivity, these compounds are hypothesized to ‘reset’ and rewire neuronal networks [267, 268]. The primary mechanism of action of these compounds involves activation of 5-HT2A receptors (expressed mainly in the cortical pyramidal neurons), which mediate their characteristic altered states of consciousness and cognitive and affective effects, and promote rapid and long-term neuroplastic changes, primarily in the cortex [266, 269]. Other receptor targets include 5-HT1A and 5-HT2C receptors, as well as modulation of glutamatergic signaling pathways, primarily through interactions with metabotropic glutamate mGlu2/3 and NMDA receptors [266, 270].

A meta-analysis of six studies indicated that individuals with alcohol dependence who received a single dose of LSD exhibited reduced alcohol misuse 1–12 months after the treatment and higher abstinence rates compared with commonly used treatments [271]. One or two exposures to psilocybin have been shown to increase abstinence in alcohol-dependent individuals [272]. A more recent study revealed that psilocybin-assisted therapy combined with cognitive-behavioral therapy reduced heavy drinking days and increased abstinence rates compared with placebo [273]. However, this study examined the effects of psilocybin in individuals with less severe AUD, and further analyses are underway to evaluate the efficacy of this compound in severe AUD [274]. Similarly, in several studies, ayahuasca has also been associated with reduced alcohol consumption (cf. [275]). Notably, research evaluating the efficacy of mescaline is heterogeneous. Although some improvements have been reported in adults with alcohol misuse or AUD, concurrent alcohol and mescaline use observed in younger populations [275] raises concerns regarding its therapeutic potential.

In animal models, a single dose of LSD decreased ethanol consumption and preference in a TBC paradigm in male C57BL/6J mice [276]. Under the same conditions, a single dose of psilocybin attenuated ethanol consumption and preference in males of this strain but not in females, and had no effect on drinking behavior during the re-introduction phase [277]. These findings contrast with studies in rats, where psilocybin has been shown to reduce relapse-like drinking behavior (cf. [275]). Regarding ayahuasca, it has been reported to block the development of ethanol-induced conditioned place preference in mice [275], as well as to reduce ethanol preference and drinking during re-introduction in an intermittent-access TBC procedure in male mice [270]. Notably, at some doses, ayahuasca also produced rewarding effects in mice [270], and its repeated treatment in rats did not affect voluntary ethanol intake [275].

Mechanistically, the AUD therapeutic effects of psychedelics are thought to involve activation of 5-HT2A receptors and interactions with mGlu2/3 receptors, leading to modulation of reward-related neural circuits [275, 278]. Actions at 5-HT2A receptors in the prefrontal cortex may restore dopaminergic tone, promote neuroplastic changes, and reduce drug-seeking behavior [279].

Potential adverse effects should also be considered, mainly due to the risk of 5-HT-related cardiotoxicity, drug-drug interactions, and prolonged psychosis [267, 280]. Activation of 5-HT2B receptors, particularly by psilocybin and psilocin, which exhibit high affinity for these receptors, may be associated with an increased risk of valvular heart disease [280]. Therefore, potential cardiovascular events require careful long-term monitoring.

Overall, psilocybin emerges as the most consistently supported psychedelic in both human and preclinical studies with respect to reducing alcohol intake [275], while also presenting the smallest risk of adverse effects [267]. Despite these promising findings, most clinical studies have included relatively small sample sizes, and often lack active control conditions. Therefore, high-quality controlled clinical trials are still required. In addition, further research is needed to clarify optimal dosing regimens, given the adverse effects associated with psychedelics. Moreover, research on the potential therapeutic effects of psychedelic microdosing should be expanded.

Conclusions, practical implications, and future directions

Among previously established vulnerability factors for AUD – including age, sex, genetic/family history, environmental, health, and psychological conditions [11, 13] – reduced central 5-HT tone may represent an additional risk factor. Serotonergic deficits are linked to behavioral traits that often precede problematic alcohol use and overlap with AUD risk, including depression, heightened stress reactivity, aggression, impulsivity, and early-onset drinking (Fig. 2). Accordingly, alcohol use may serve as a form of self-medication not only in depression, but also in individuals with impaired stress coping and other negative affective states. Importantly, stress represents a key factor linking negative affect to AUD, with women being more vulnerable to drinking to cope than men. Social anxiety disorder represents another relevant risk factor [9], and has been associated with increased 5-HT synthesis in specific limbic regions and the basal ganglia, as measured by [11C]5-HTP accumulation [64] (Fig. 2); however, this biochemical marker has not yet been investigated in the context of AUD vulnerability.

Beyond behavioral and clinical indicators, research evidence points to potential serotonergic biomarkers of AUD risk (Fig. 1). For example, reduced CSF 5-HIAA levels, reflecting decreased central 5-HT turnover, have been associated with vulnerability to AUD. Another candidate marker is reduced prefrontal 5-HT2A receptor binding, measured by quantitative autoradiography and proposed as an inherited feature linked to AUD risk.

Notably, 5-HT signaling can be shaped by multiple interacting factors, including diet (i.e., TRP availability), environmental stressors, pain, and genetic variation in 5-HT-related genes such as DDC, SLC6A4, and HTR1A (Fig. 2). These factors may underlie distinct behavioral profiles that may contribute to AUD risk and modulate its severity, withdrawal symptoms, and treatment response.

Exposure to ethanol per se also leads to 5-HT dysregulation, primarily through alterations in TPH2 activity, changes in SERT density (in the prefrontal cortex and nucleus accumbens), reduced 5-HT function, and increased 5-HT turnover (Fig. 1). A potential marker of excessive alcohol consumption may be increased activity in the raphe nuclei and decreased activity in the prefrontal cortex. Although the co-occurrence of these changes within the same individual remains unclear, their possible overlap may suggest that serotonergic DR raphe projections to the prefrontal cortex and nucleus accumbens are particularly relevant to AUD risk and maintenance, however, further confirmation is required.

Consistent with AUD heterogeneity, the efficacy of SSRIs in AUD is highly subtype-dependent. More favorable responses to SSRIs are observed in individuals with less-severe, later-onset type A alcohol dependence and in alcohol-dependent patients with comorbid depression. In type B, SSRIs often show limited efficacy or may even worsen clinical outcomes. These opposing effects explain why more precise treatment approaches are required in a heterogeneous population of AUD individuals. Beyond efficacy considerations, SSRI use is frequently limited by adverse effects such as nausea, anxiety, sexual dysfunction, and gastrointestinal disturbances [250] and withdrawal symptoms (e.g., anxiety, agitation, irritability, sleep disturbances, paresthesia, dizziness, nausea, headache) [250, 281]. Moreover, inter-population differences in HTR2A variation may further modulate SSRI response, underscoring the importance of considering genetic background when predicting treatment efficacy [282].

In recent years, growing interest has been directed toward the potential use of serotonergic psychedelics in the treatment of AUD. By acting directly on 5-HT2A receptors in the prefrontal cortex affected by chronic ethanol exposure, psychedelics may help to restore the reward-related neural circuits. To date, psilocybin has demonstrated the most promising efficacy and is associated with a relatively low risk of adverse events.

The reviewed literature emphasizes the need for detailed phenotyping of individuals with AUD, as many clinical studies failed to address AUD subtypes and were conducted in ethnically diverse samples, complicating the interpretation of findings. In the case of preclinical studies investigating the mechanisms underlying alcohol consumption, a significant limitation of many studies is that they are conducted in animals that are either non-dependent or exposed to ethanol only for short periods. Under such experimental conditions, the efficacy of newly tested drugs often fails to translate to clinical settings. Future studies should therefore employ animal models that better reflect clinically (and neurobiologically) relevant AUD phenotypes, including models resembling subjects vulnerable to Cloninger’s type II AUD. Tph2−/− mice may represent a valuable mechanistic tool for investigating the consequences of severe 5-HT depletion in AUD. These animals exhibit increased ethanol consumption and behavioral traits such as antisocial behavior, heightened aggression, and impulsivity, reminiscent of type II. However, due to pronounced developmental and physiological abnormalities observed in these animals, their long-term suitability for translational research may be limited. Therefore, the use of complementary models – such as those involving partial or conditional 5-HT depletion, or manipulation of serotonergic signaling in adulthood – should also be considered to improve the translational relevance of preclinical studies. Furthermore, the inclusion of additional variables into rodent studies such as sex and nutritional status, may further enhance the translational value of these models. A deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms may help identify biomarkers that could aid in preventing the development of AUD, predicting relapse risk, and improving the efficacy of targeted therapeutic interventions.

Collectively, the available evidence underscores the need for reliable markers of central 5-HT dysfunction in AUD. While CSF 5-HIAA levels and postmortem analyses of 5-HT2A receptor binding have provided important insights, their invasive or retrospective nature limits their utility for early detection and intervention. Accordingly, future research should prioritize the development of non-invasive, region-specific markers of 5-HT dysfunction. PET imaging approaches with α-[11C]methyl-L-TRP may serve as valuable tools for mechanistic stratification, although their implementation in a routine clinical setting remains limited. Ultimately, combining biomarkers with questionnaire-based measures capturing personality and affective dimensions associated with 5-HT dysfunction may offer a more feasible and scalable strategy for AUD stratification, relapse-risk prediction, and personalized treatment approaches.

Overall, this narrative review highlights 5-HT as a key neuromodulator of vulnerability, heterogeneity, and treatment response in AUD.
