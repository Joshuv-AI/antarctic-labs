Introduction

Dental radiographs are among the most important para-clinical tools that assist dentists for accurate and proper diagnoses [1]. Among the various types of dental radiography (Rg), the Bitewing (BW) and Peri-apical (PA) views are particularly effective for identifying coronal or radicular dental caries and periapical lesions [2, 3]. The preparation of high-quality dental Rg encounters numerous challenges due to the anatomical complexity of the oral cavity and the presence of both soft and hard tissues [4]. Furthermore, the quality and accuracy of dental Rg can be compromised by noise, artifacts, blurriness, and aliasing effects [5–7]. Ultimately, the most critical characteristic of a quality Rg is its resolution, which facilitates optimal decision-making by the dentist. Resolution refers to the number of pixels within a specified area of the image, allowing for the detection of details and boundaries in Rg [8]. However, achieving a high-resolution Rg is significantly influenced by three primary factors: device-specific elements (including limitations of acquisition tools, detector geometry, sensitivity, and reconstruction techniques), patient-related issues (such as movement), and technician-related challenges (like improper positioning of the equipment or inadequate exposure settings) [9–11].

Consequently, enhancing the resolution of dental Rg has consistently posed a significant challenge in dental research [12]. For many years, conventional image processing software has utilized zoom and focusing tools to assist dentists [13]. Three of the primary conventional methods include linear interpolation, cubic interpolation, and nearest neighbor interpolation. These methods, which depend on the zoom feature, may introduce bias in detecting boundaries or anatomical details, rendering them neither valid nor reliable [14].

In contemporary practice, the Super Resolution (SR) theory has enabled the enhancement of Rg resolution without compromising the original data [4, 15, 16].

The SR is distinctly different from traditional interpolations as it does not depend on the zooming feature [16]; rather, it is a reconstruction technique that converts one or more sets of low-resolution (LR) images into high resolutions (HR) through nonlinear mapping using software algorithms [8]. More specifically, this theory encompasses the inverse mapping of a degradation map, which is influenced by degradation parameters such as blur, noise, scaling, and compression quality [17].

In today’s digitalized world, Artificial Intelligence (AI) and deep learning have emerged as pivotal elements in people’s daily lives [8]. Based on AI, certain deep learning models, such as convolutional neural networks (CNN), have significantly advanced the state of the art in image SR, enhancing not only the resolution of images but also effectively reducing image loss and optimizing the overall quality and visual impact of images without the need for additional sensors or hardware devices [8, 18].

Several previous researchers have focused on improving the resolution of dental radiographs by incorporating AI, utilizing various algorithms in their studies. Data is available regarding panoramic, BW, PA radiographs, and Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT), yet there remains a strong recommendation for further investigations [15, 19–24].

Consequently, due to the insufficient data and the ongoing development of AI, this study was conducted to examine a deep learning approach for enhancing the resolution of dental BW and PA radiographs based on SR theory in CNN.

Methods and materials

Data preparation

Data collection, inclusion and exclusion criteria

The current study is in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was ethically approved in local committee (Ethics committee, Shahed University, Tehran, Iran) as IR.SHAHED.REC.1403.071. Also, informed consent was obtained from all patients whose Rg was used. Moreover, all the criteria including methods, results representations, and etc. is complies with an established AI reporting guidelines [25].

Since large balanced dataset is crucially important for deep learning-based research, a group of 1000 BW, and PA Rg obtained from a private office (Negare Oro-Maxillo Facial Radiology Center, Kerman, Iran) were collected for this study. Among these Rgs we used 75% (750 images) for training while 25% (250 images) for test. All of 1000 images showed at least one tooth with containing restoration, caries, or both. In case, if all the teeth were intact, or edentulous patients were excluded from the study. Creating synthetic low-quality dental radiographs for reconstruction

According to previous publications, for input data we need some LR Rgs while the original HR images were considered as ground truth [10, 26]; So at the first step we downscaled all HR images to create LR. For this purpose, we used Lee filter [27] and 4*4 average pooling without overlap (to generate 80*100 LR images from 320*400 HR ones). We generated five different patterns of downscaling and these patterns were reviewed by an expert operative dentist (20 years’ experience) to select the best LR pattern which could best fit the real LR Rg (Fig. 1).

Producing LR (Low Resolution) images from original HR (High Resolution) ones. The four sample labeling by “×” symbol, was not acceptable and withdrawn from the rest of the study

Therefore, we produced 750 LR samples which were incorporated as input data for training. Meanwhile, we had their Original High Resolution (HR) counterparts for comparison.

The hybrid architecture combines several key concepts to improve the performance of deep neural networks, particularly for tasks like image segmentation, object detection, and classification. It merges down-sampling, which creates lower-resolution feature maps, with skip-connections, which help preserve fine-grained information, and multi-scale feature extraction [28].

In this hybrid setup, down-sampling helps capture high-level, abstract features, while skip-connections allow low-level details from earlier layers to flow directly into higher layers. This ensures that important spatial information is not lost as the network deepens. Additionally, multi-scale features are gathered by processing the input at different resolutions, allowing the model to detect objects of various sizes and orientations. Training details

The training was conducted on a Tesla T4 graphic processor unit (Nvidia Corporation, Santa Clara, CA, USA) using the Google Collaboratory platform. We incorporate Keras and Tensorflow libraries.

Among three mentioned networks, our analysis revealed that the DSC/MS was most efficient which was incorporated for the rest of the study. Thereafter, we run eight models by modifying the hyperparameters such as No. of convolution layers, No. of convolution filters, size of convolution filters and activation function.

After the initial assessments of each model, the number of epochs was set from 5 to 25 on the basis of the model’s performance on the validation set. To prevent overfitting, we used early stopping, where we saved the best model weights according to their performance on the validation dataset based on the structural similarity index (SSIM) and mean square error (MSE).

Clinical utility

The reconstructed Rgs (the out-put Rgs resulted from sets of the LR in-put images used for testing our ML algorithm) were compared with the original HR Rgs by two senior (> 10 year’s of clinical experience) and two junior (2–5 years of clinical experience) specialists [37]. We asked them to campare the images and rank the similarity between them as low, intermediate, or high for detecting three categories including caries, restoration, and PDL (Periodontal ligament a) & alveolar bone. For blindness we coded the two sub-groups.

Statistical analysis

The obtained data were compared with each other using Weighted Kappa, Spearman’s correlation, and Mann-Whitney U tests (α = 0.05).

Clinical utility

About representing the existing restorations, the out-put Rg was very efficient comparing to the original HR. When they were evaluated by senior specialists, all the images were ranked as high similarity. The agreement between seniors for comparing restorations was very strong (Weighted Kappa = 0.66, and P = 0.0001). Furthermore, their Spearmen’s correlation coefficient (r = 0.70, and P = 0.0001) again confirm the high correlation. On the other hand, when juniors compared the out-put with original HR for restoration detection, they did not showed agreement with each other (Weighted Kappa = 0.07, and P = 0.61) and their correlation was very weak (r = 0.23, and P = 0.12).

For caries detection, most specialist rated the similarity between the out-put and original HR Rgs as high or intermediate. Statistically, the agreement between seniors for detecting caries was weak (Weighted Kappa = 0.2, and P = 0.002), but their correlation coefficient was to some extent acceptable (r = 0.41, and P = 0.003). It shows that the seniors were consistent with each other although they did not have absolute agreement in similarity of the Rgs for caries detection. However, the juniors agreement about caries was very weak (Weighted Kappa = 0.08 ,and P = 0.29) and their correlation was also weak (r = 0.22 and P = 0.13). It means that the senior’s opinion about similarity of the Rgs in caries detection was very different from each other.

Finally, when we concerned the PDL & alveolar bone, the out-put image was not very effective comparing to original HR. Similarity in almost all case were ranked as low or intermediate. The senior’s opinion in this regard was significantly in agreement with each other (Weighted Kappa = 0.96 and P = 0.0001) and they were completely correlated (r = 1). Meanwhile, the juniors were not in agreement (Weighted Kappa = 0.00 and P = 0.7) and they were not at all correlated with each other (r = 0.015, P = 0.92). Actually, one of the juniors ranked most images as intermediate similarity whilst the other mostly as weak comparing the representation of the PDL& alveolar bone.

Overwhelmingly, when the senior and junior opinions were compared with each other (Mann-Whitney U tests), the seniors had significantly more agreement with each other about caries and restoration detection (P = 0.0001 and 0.0001 respectively). Yet, about PDL&alveolar bone although seniors had more agreement but it was not significant (P = 0.22).

Discussion

The primary outcome of the present research indicates that the optimal model for enhancing resolution was DSC/MS considering all metric counterparts such as R2, RMSE, MSE, MAE, SSIM, and PSNR. This suggests that our SR model demonstrated significant effectiveness.

This conclusion aligns with numerous prior studies grounded in SR theory, which incorporated various SR models [4, 24, 38]. In fact, in the majority of studies focusing on either dental or medical radiographs, different SR models were implemented and compared, with one or two models being identified as the most effective. In this context, Moran M.B.H et al. examined dental PA radiographs, utilizing only the super-resolution generative adversarial network (SRGAN) models, which resulted in a notable enhancement in resolution metrics [24]. Concurrently, Mohammad-Rahimi H et al. investigated panoramic radiographs and included additional SR models such as SRGAN, U-Net, Swin for image restoration (SwinIr), and local texture estimator (LTE). All of their models were reported to be effective, albeit not uniformly, as the LTE was found to be superior [4].

For the downscaling of images, our approach was aligned to that of previous researchers [38]. This step was essential for generating comparable low-resolution images. However, from our professional perspective, and as illustrated in Figs. 1 and 6, the resulting LR image appears to be quite exaggerated when compared to typical clinical scenarios. Indeed, if an SR model could enhance the resolution of our LR images, it could be considered very strong and beneficial, and it could solve the LR images in most clinical situations. Nonetheless, further research is strongly encouraged to evaluate this SR model on available LR images in other dental clinics.

In this study, we explored the integration of MS and DSC architectures to enhance dental radiography images. The MS architecture processes feature at multiple resolutions simultaneously, enabling the model to capture both fine-grained details and broader contextual information. This capability is particularly important for dental images, where subtle variations in tooth and bone structures carry significant diagnostic value [39].

On the other hand, the DSC architecture uses skip connections with down-sampling in the skip pathways. This design effectively preserves essential spatial features while reducing computational overhead. Additionally, the improved gradient flow resulting from these skip connections helps stabilize and accelerate the training process [40].

By combining these two approaches, the hybrid DSC/MS structure benefits from the rich multi-scale feature extraction of MS and the efficient information propagation of DSC. This synergy not only leads to more comprehensive feature representation but also reduces computational cost without sacrificing accuracy [28].

Regarding image resolution metrics, we have included six distinct metrics: R2, RMSE, MSE, MAE, SSIM, and PSNR, due to their significant attributes for comparing LR and HR images. To our knowledge, the majority of studies have only compared two or three metrics [4, 24]. Assessing six different image metrics is a crucial element of the current research; as previous literature has shown that certain parameters exhibited greater variability across some SR models, while others remained more consistent [4]. This may be attributed to the differences in the nature and definitions of these parameters. In this context, PSNR emphasizes pixel differences, whereas SSIM evaluates luminance, contrast, and structure (17). Previously, Mohammad-Rahimi H et al., who conducted research on panoramic Rg, contended that the SRGAN model was less effective compared to LTE, SRCNN, or U-Net models when measuring MSE and PSNR metrics; however, when considering the SSIM parameter, all of their SR models demonstrated similar performance with only negligible differences observed among them [4]. Consequently, examining various image metrics is vital for validating the performance of an SR model when managing resolution. Furthermore, if an SR model can outperform across multiple image metrics, it may be deemed a superior model [41].

One of the most impressive findings of our study is that the MSE values derived from our DSC/MS model were significantly lower than those reported in earlier dental research [3, 7]. Since a lower MSE indicates reduced error [42], this result strongly supports the superiority of our model. In contrast, our SSIM values are somewhat lower than those in previous dental reports [3, 7]. Although the difference between our SSIM values and those of others is minimal, a lower SSIM may indicate a reduced ability to replicate the examined image compared to the original [36].Again we emphasize that a specific model may perform well concerning certain image metrics while not functioning adequately with others [43]. Meanwhile, our PSNR values obtained from the DSC/MS model can be considered approximately similar to those in previous dental literature [24] while being somewhat lower than those in another dental manuscript [4]. Although PSNR is one of the most widely used parameters for assessing image quality, it primarily focuses on pixel differences and can often be inconsistent with the subjectively perceived quality [17]. Indeed, a minor shift in pixels can lead to poorer PSNR values without impacting human perceptual quality [17].

Furthermore, as indicated in Table 7, all quantitative parameters in our SR model exhibited noticeable differences compared to conventional models. This finding aligns closely with previous dental, medical, or graphic research [4, 13, 14, 24, 38].

In most prior research, objective comparisons were entirely consistent with the results of image metrics [4, 24, 38]. Our comparison of the images for evaluating their clinical utility was roughly confirmed our metric data especially for representing the existed restorations. Meanwhile senior as well as junior specialists’ opinion showed that our ML was weak for reconstructing the PDL & alveolar bone. For caries detection, the senior was more optimistic about our ML efficacy. Actually, we concluded that the clinical utility of our ML could be varied objectively which is in contrast by the previous study [37].

The in-consistency of image metrics and clinical utility could be considered as a limitation of our study. Specially the PDL & alveolar bone was not reconstructed very well by our ML model. Maybe, more images should be incorporated for ML. Another limitation of our study is the smaller dataset used for training compared to previous studies [4, 24, 38]. Nevertheless, even with this limited library, a respectable model was successfully trained.

Conclusion

The developed DSC/MS model has the potential to improve the resolution of dental periapical or bitewing radiographs based on six quantitative metrics including: R2, RMSE, MSE, MAE, SSIM, and PSNR. The clinical utility of our ML model was also acceptable for presentation of existing restorations and caries. However, its ability for reconstructing the PDL&Alveolar bone was weak in clinical point of view.
