On the Third Hankel Determinant for Inverse Coefficients of Starlike Functions: A Bernstein Polynomial Approach
Vasudevarao Allu, Shobhit Kumar

Abstract
. Let 𝒜 \mathcal{A} denote the class of normalized analytic functions f f in the open unit disk defined as 𝔻 := { z ∈ ℂ : | z | < 1 } \mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\} with f ⁡ ( 0 ) = 0 f(0)=0 and f ′ ​ ( 0 ) = 1 f^{\prime}(0)=1 . A function f ∈ 𝒜 f\in\mathcal{A} is said to be starlike if f ⁡ ( 𝔻 ) f(\mathbb{D}) is starlike domain. By using the Bernstein polynomial method to obtain the required maximum estimate, we establish sharp upper bound for the third Hankel determinant corresponding to the inverse coefficients of starlike univalent ( i.e. , one-to-one) functions in the unit disk 𝔻 \mathbb{D} .

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the class of normalized analytic functions
f
in the open unit disk

\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
having the Taylor series expansion

f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n},\quad\text{ for }z\in\mathbb{D},

(1.1)

and let
\mathcal{S}
be the subclass of
\mathcal{A}
consisting of functions that are univalent (
i.e.
, one-to-one) in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
. Two important subclasses of
\mathcal{S}
are the classes of starlike and convex functions, defined respectively by

\mathcal{S}^{*}=\left\{f\in\mathcal{A}:\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}\right)>0,\ z\in\mathbb{D}\right\},

and

\mathcal{C}=\left\{f\in\mathcal{A}:\operatorname{Re}\left(1+\frac{zf^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}\right)>0,\ z\in\mathbb{D}\right\}.

For every function
f\in\mathcal{S}
, the local inverse
f^{-1}
exists in a neighborhood of the origin and admits the expansion

f^{-1}(w)=w+A_{2}w^{2}+A_{3}w^{3}+\cdots.

The notion of Hankel determinants for functions in
\mathcal{A}
was introduced by Pommerenke
[
14
]
. For
n,q\in\mathbb{N}
, the
q
th Hankel determinant is given by

H(q,n)(f):=\begin{vmatrix}a_{n}&a_{n+1}&\cdots&a_{n+q-1}\\
a_{n+1}&a_{n+2}&\cdots&a_{n+q}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{n+q-1}&a_{n+q}&\cdots&a_{n+2q-2}\end{vmatrix}.

Among the simplest cases, the determinants

H(2,1)(f)=a_{2}^{2}-a_{3}\qquad\text{and}\qquad H(2,2)(f)=a_{2}a_{4}-a_{3}^{2}

have received considerable attention in the literature; see, for example,
(see
[
4
,
12
]
).

The study of the third Hankel determinant is substantially more involved. Over the past several years, a number of authors have obtained important results with regards to

H(3,1)(f)=2a_{2}a_{3}a_{4}-a_{3}^{3}-a_{4}^{2}+a_{3}a_{5}-a_{2}^{2}a_{5}

for various subclasses of analytic and univalent functions (see

[
2
,
7
,
9
,
11
,
15
]
).
In particular, sharp bounds for this determinant have been established for both the class of convex and starlike functions in recent works such as
(see
[
6
,
8
]
).

In the present paper, we investigate the third Hankel determinant formed from the inverse coefficients of functions belonging to
\mathcal{S}^{*}
. Our main theorem gives a sharp estimate for
\bigl|H(3,1)(f^{-1})\bigr|
, with equality attained by the Koebe function. The proof relies on the Carathéodory representation of starlike functions, together with the coefficient parametrization due to Libera and Złotkiewicz
[
13
]
and Kwon,
et al.

[
10
]
.

A crucial step in the proof is the estimation of a polynomial on a rectangular box. For this purpose, we employ the Bernstein polynomial method, for establishing the positivity or nonnegativity of polynomials on compact intervals and rectangular boxes; see Cargo and Shisha
[
3
]
and Garloff
[
5
]
. This approach provides an effective tool for reducing the required maximization problem to the inspection of Bernstein coefficients.

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the class of analytic functions
p
in
\mathbb{D}
of the form

p(z)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}

(1.2)

with positive real part in
\mathbb{D}
. The following lemma will play a central role in the proof of our main result, since it provides the standard representations of the coefficients
c_{2}
,
c_{3}
, and
c_{4}
.

Lemma 1.1
.

[
13
]

Let
p\in\mathcal{P}
and be given by (
1.2
) with
c_{1}\geq 0
. Then

\displaystyle 2c_{2}

\displaystyle=c_{1}^{2}+\gamma\bigl(4-c_{1}^{2}\bigr),

\displaystyle 4c_{3}

\displaystyle=c_{1}^{3}+2\bigl(4-c_{1}^{2}\bigr)c_{1}\gamma-\bigl(4-c_{1}^{2}\bigr)c_{1}\gamma^{2}+2\bigl(4-c_{1}^{2}\bigr)\bigl(1-|\gamma|^{2}\bigr)\eta,

\displaystyle 8c_{4}

\displaystyle=c_{1}^{4}+\bigl(4-c_{1}^{2}\bigr)\gamma\bigl(c_{1}^{2}(\gamma^{2}-3\gamma+3)+4\gamma\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad-4\bigl(4-c_{1}^{2}\bigr)\bigl(1-|\gamma|^{2}\bigr)\left(c_{1}(\gamma-1)\eta+\overline{\gamma}\,\eta^{2}-(1-|\eta|^{2})\rho\right)

for some
\gamma,\eta,\rho
such that
|\gamma|\leq 1
,
|\eta|\leq 1
and
|\rho|\leq 1
.

2.
Main Result

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
be given by (
1.1
). Then

\bigl|H(3,1)(f^{-1})\bigr|\leq 1.

The inequality is sharp for the Koebe function.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
. Then there exists a function
p\in\mathcal{P}
such that

zf^{\prime}(z)=f(z)\,p(z).

(2.1)

Note that since
f\bigl(f^{-1}(w)\bigr)=w
, it follows that

\left.\begin{aligned} A_{2}&=-a_{2},\\
A_{3}&=2a_{2}^{2}-a_{3},\\
A_{4}&=5a_{2}a_{3}-5a_{2}^{3}-a_{4},\\
A_{5}&=14a_{2}^{4}-21a_{3}a_{2}^{2}+6a_{2}a_{4}+3a_{3}^{2}-a_{5}.\end{aligned}\right\}

(2.2)

Comparing coefficients of (
2.1
), we obtain

\left.\begin{aligned} a_{2}&=c_{1},\\
a_{3}&=\frac{1}{2}c_{2}+\frac{1}{2}c_{1}^{2},\\
a_{4}&=\frac{1}{6}c_{1}^{3}+\frac{1}{2}c_{1}c_{2}+\frac{1}{3}c_{3},\\
a_{5}&=\frac{1}{24}c_{1}^{4}+\frac{1}{4}c_{2}c_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{3}c_{1}c_{3}+\frac{1}{8}c_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}c_{4}.\end{aligned}\right\}

(2.3)

Using (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), we obtain that

\displaystyle 144\,H(3,1)\,(f^{-1})

\displaystyle=17c_{1}^{6}-51c_{1}^{4}c_{2}+45c_{1}^{2}c_{2}^{2}-27c_{2}^{3}+8c_{1}^{3}c_{3}+24c_{1}c_{2}c_{3}

\displaystyle\quad-16c_{3}^{2}-18c_{1}^{2}c_{4}+18c_{2}c_{4}.

(2.4)

Since, the class
\mathcal{S}^{*}
is rotationally invariant. For
f\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
, the rotational functions

f_{\theta}(z):=e^{-i\theta}f(e^{i\theta}z)

belongs to the class
\mathcal{S}^{*}.
Moreover, if

\displaystyle f^{-1}(w)=w+A_{2}w^{2}+A_{3}w^{3}+A_{4}w^{4}+A_{5}w^{5}+\cdots,

then

\displaystyle f_{\theta}^{-1}(w)=e^{-i\theta}f^{-1}(e^{i\theta}w)=w+e^{i\theta}A_{2}w^{2}+e^{2i\theta}A_{3}w^{3}+e^{3i\theta}A_{4}w^{4}+e^{4i\theta}A_{5}w^{5}+\cdots.

Thus, we have

H(3,1)(f_{\theta}^{-1})=e^{6i\theta}H(3,1)(f^{-1}),

therefore,

|H(3,1)(f_{\theta}^{-1})|=|H(3,1)(f^{-1})|.

Therefore, choosing
\theta
suitably, we may assume that
c_{1}\in[0,2]
. Substituting the values of
c_{2},c_{3}
and
c_{4}

from Lemma
1.1
into (
2
), and collecting
the coefficients of
\eta
,
\eta^{2}
and
\rho
, we may rewrite (
2
) in the form

1152\,H(3,1)\,(f^{-1})=\widetilde{A}+\widetilde{B}\eta+\widetilde{C}\eta^{2}+\widetilde{D}\rho

(2.5)

where,

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}

\displaystyle=18\,c_{1}^{6}\;+\;51\,\gamma\,c_{1}^{4}\,(-4+c_{1}^{2})\;+\;\gamma^{4}\,c_{1}^{2}\,(-4+c_{1}^{2})^{2}\,+\,\gamma^{2}c_{1}^{2}\,(688-368c_{1}^{2}+49c_{1}^{4})\;

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma^{3}\,(-1152+704c_{1}^{2}-172c_{1}^{4}+17c_{1}^{6}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}

\displaystyle=4\,(-1+|\gamma|^{2})\,c_{1}\,(-4+c_{1}^{2})\,\Big(3c_{1}^{2}+\gamma^{2}(-4+c_{1}^{2})+4\gamma(5+c_{1}^{2})\Big),

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}

\displaystyle=-4\,(-1+|\gamma|^{2})\,(-4+c_{1}^{2})\,\Bigl(-8(-4+c_{1}^{2})+8\,|\gamma|^{2}(-4+c_{1}^{2})-9\,(c_{1}^{2}+\gamma(-4+c_{1}^{2}))\,\overline{\gamma}\Bigr),

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}

\displaystyle=36\,(-1+|\gamma|^{2})(-1+|\eta|^{2})\,(-4+c_{1}^{2})\,\bigl(c_{1}^{2}+\gamma(-4+c_{1}^{2})\bigr).

Now, taking

x:=|\gamma|\in[0,1],\,y:=|\eta|\in[0,1].

Then, using the triangle inequality and the fact that
|\rho|\leq 1
, we obtain

1152\,\bigl|H(3,1)(f^{-1})\bigr|\leq|\widetilde{A}|+|\widetilde{B}|\,|\eta|+|\widetilde{C}|\,|\eta|^{2}+|\widetilde{D}|\,|\rho|\leq H(c_{1},x,y).

For convenience, take
c_{1}=p_{1}
, where

\displaystyle H(p_{1},x,y):=\;

\displaystyle\,18p_{1}^{6}+51x\,p_{1}^{4}(4-p_{1}^{2})+x^{2}p_{1}^{2}(688-368p_{1}^{2}+49p_{1}^{4})

\displaystyle-x^{3}\!\left(-1152+704p_{1}^{2}-172p_{1}^{4}+17p_{1}^{6}\right)+x^{4}p_{1}^{2}(-4+p_{1}^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle+4\,y\,(1-x^{2})p_{1}(4-p_{1}^{2})\left(3p_{1}^{2}+x^{2}(4-p_{1}^{2})+4x(5+p_{1}^{2})\right)

\displaystyle+4\,y^{2}\,(1-x^{2})(4-p_{1}^{2})\left(8(1-x^{2})(4-p_{1}^{2})+9(p_{1}^{2}x+x^{2}(4-p_{1}^{2}))\right)

\displaystyle+36(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})(4-p_{1}^{2})\left(p_{1}^{2}+x(4-p_{1}^{2})\right).

(2.6)

We now determine the maximum value of
H(p_{1},x,y)
on the cuboid

\mathcal{D}:=\{(p_{1},x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:0\leq p_{1}\leq 2,\,0\leq x\leq 1,\,0\leq y\leq 1\}.

Our aim is to prove

\max_{\mathcal{D}}H(p_{1},x,y)=1152.

Now, consider

\Delta(p_{1},x,y):=1152-H(p_{1},x,y).

A direct calculation shows that

\Delta(p_{1},x,y)=(2-p_{1})(2+p_{1})(1-x)\,\Phi(p_{1},x,y),

where

\displaystyle\Phi(p_{1},x,y)=

\displaystyle-p_{1}^{4}x^{3}+16p_{1}^{4}x^{2}-33p_{1}^{4}x+18p_{1}^{4}+4p_{1}^{3}x^{3}y-12p_{1}^{3}x^{2}y-28p_{1}^{3}xy

\displaystyle-12p_{1}^{3}y+4p_{1}^{2}x^{3}y^{2}+4p_{1}^{2}x^{3}-68p_{1}^{2}x^{2}y^{2}-64p_{1}^{2}x^{2}-4p_{1}^{2}xy^{2}

\displaystyle+72p_{1}^{2}x+68p_{1}^{2}y^{2}+36p_{1}^{2}-16p_{1}x^{3}y-96p_{1}x^{2}y-80p_{1}xy

\displaystyle-16x^{3}y^{2}+128x^{2}y^{2}+144x^{2}+16xy^{2}+144x-128y^{2}+288.

Let

p_{1}=2u,
where
0\leq u\leq 1.

Then
\Phi(2u,x,y)
is a polynomial of degree at most
4
in
u
, degree at most
3
in
x
, and degree at most
2
in
y
. Hence it can be written in the Bernstein form

\Phi(2u,x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^{4}\sum_{j=0}^{3}\sum_{k=0}^{2}b_{ijk}\,B_{i}^{4}(u)B_{j}^{3}(x)B_{k}^{2}(y),

where

B_{m}^{n}(t)=\binom{n}{m}t^{m}(1-t)^{n-m},\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1.

The corresponding Bernstein coefficient matrices are

M_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}288&336&432&576\\[8.53581pt]
288&336&432&576\\[8.53581pt]
312&376&\frac{4264}{9}&608\\[8.53581pt]
360&456&\frac{1672}{3}&672\\[8.53581pt]
720&688&704&768\end{pmatrix},

and the maximum entry of
M_{0}
is

\max M_{0}=768.

M_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}288&336&432&576\\[8.53581pt]
288&\frac{988}{3}&\frac{1232}{3}&528\\[8.53581pt]
312&\frac{1088}{3}&\frac{3880}{9}&512\\[8.53581pt]
348&\frac{1244}{3}&\frac{1376}{3}&480\\[8.53581pt]
672&576&480&384\end{pmatrix},

and the maximum entry of
M_{1}
is

\max M_{1}=672.

M_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}160&\frac{640}{3}&\frac{1072}{3}&576\\[8.53581pt]
160&200&\frac{944}{3}&480\\[8.53581pt]
\frac{688}{3}&\frac{2440}{9}&\frac{3080}{9}&416\\[8.53581pt]
344&384&\frac{1112}{3}&288\\[8.53581pt]
768&608&352&0\end{pmatrix},

and the maximum entry of
M_{2}
is

\max M_{2}=768.

Since every entry of
M_{0}
,
M_{1}
, and
M_{2}
is non-negative, and since each Bernstein basis polynomial is non-negative on
[0,1]
, it follows that

\displaystyle\Phi(2u,x,y)\geq 0\qquad\text{for all }(u,x,y)\in[0,1]\times[0,1]\times[0,1].

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\Phi(p_{1},x,y)\geq 0\qquad\text{for all }(p_{1},x,y)\in[0,2]\times[0,1]\times[0,1].

Therefore,

\Delta(p_{1},x,y)=(2-p_{1})(2+p_{1})(1-x)\Phi(p_{1},x,y)\geq 0,

because

2-p_{1}\geq 0,\qquad 2+p_{1}>0,\qquad 1-x\geq 0

throughout the domain. Hence

H(p_{1},x,y)\leq 1152,\qquad\text{ for }\quad 0\leq p_{1}\leq 2,\quad 0\leq x\leq 1,\quad 0\leq y\leq 1.

To see that the bound
1152
is sharp, we note that

H(p_{1},1,y)=1152\qquad\text{for all }0\leq p_{1}\leq 2,\ \ 0\leq y\leq 1,

and also

H(2,x,y)=1152\qquad\text{for all }0\leq x\leq 1,\ \ 0\leq y\leq 1.

Thus,

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq p_{1}\leq 2,\;0\leq x\leq 1,\;0\leq y\leq 1}H(p_{1},x,y)=1152.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\bigl|H(3,1)(f^{-1})\bigr|\leq 1.

(2.7)

To establish sharpness, we show that the Koebe function is extremal in this case. Consider the Koebe function

k(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)^{2}}=z+2z^{2}+3z^{3}+4z^{4}+\cdots.

Its inverse has the expansion

k^{-1}(w)=w-2w^{2}+5w^{3}-14w^{4}+42w^{5}+\cdots.

Hence

A_{2}=-2,\qquad A_{3}=5,\qquad A_{4}=-14,\qquad A_{5}=42,

and therefore

H(3,1)(k^{-1})=2A_{2}A_{3}A_{4}-A_{3}^{3}-A_{4}^{2}+A_{3}A_{5}-A_{2}^{2}A_{5}=1.

Thus the equality in (
2.7
) is attained for the Koebe function, and hence the bound attained is sharp.
