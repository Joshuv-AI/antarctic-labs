Long-Term Effects of Phacoemulsification with Goniosynechialysis in Angle-Closure Glaucoma: A 2-Year Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Feng Gao, Jiajian Wang, Xiangmei Kong, Li Wang, Shaohong Qian, Xinghuai Sun, Yuhong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe aim of this study was to compare long-term effect of phacoemulsification combined with goniosynechialysis (Phaco-GSL) in patients with acute primary angle-closure glaucoma (APACG) and those with chronic primary angle-closure glaucoma (CPACG), and to evaluate the influencing factors of the surgical prognosis.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study, patients with APACG or CPACG who underwent Phaco-GSL at the glaucoma clinics of Eye and ear, nose, and throat (ENT) Hospital were recruited consecutively from January 2019 to December 2022. Standardized 2-year follow-ups assessed baseline characteristics and surgical outcomes. The cumulative success rates and influencing factors of surgical prognosis were analyzed.ResultsA total of 213 patients were enrolled, including 116 patients (116 eyes) with APACG and 97 patients (97 eyes) with CPACG. The average age was 64.5 ± 7.2 years and 153 (71.8%) patients were women. The complete success rates at 2 years were 72.4% in the acute group and 65.0% in the chronic group (P = 0.04). The qualified success rates at 2 years were 81.9% in the acute group and 81.4% in the chronic group (P = 0.44). Baseline mean deviation, anterior chamber depth (ACD), and glaucoma type were identified as independent predictors of surgical outcomes in the overall cohort. In subgroup analyses, a longer duration of elevated intraocular pressure showed a trend toward poorer surgical prognosis in acute PACG, whereas shallower ACD was associated with poorer outcomes in chronic PACG.ConclusionsPhaco-GSL demonstrated significant benefits for both APACG and CPACG, with more pronounced effects for APACG. Identifying patients with prognostic factors preoperatively provides valuable insights for better assessing the outcomes of GSL surgery in PACG.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-026-01426-w.Keywords: Acute primary angle-closure glaucoma, Chronic primary angle-closure glaucoma, Long term effect, Phacoemulsification combined with goniosynechialysis, Influencing factors

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Phacoemulsification combined with goniosynechialysis (Phaco-GSL) is widely used for primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG), but high-quality prospective data comparing long-term outcomes between acute (APACG) and chronic (CPACG) forms remain limited.Identifying prognostic factors for surgical success in different PACG subtypes is essential for personalizing treatment strategies and improving patient outcomes. What was learned from this study? This 2-year prospective study of 213 patients showed that Phaco-GSL significantly reduced intraocular pressure (IOP), improved visual function, and decreased medication dependence in both APACG and CPACG, with APACG achieving a higher complete success rate (72.4% versus 65.0%, P = 0.04).Several factors were associated with surgical prognosis after Phaco-GSL, including disease course, anterior chamber depth, and glaucoma type. A longer duration of elevated IOP tended to be associated with poorer outcomes in APACG, and worse baseline visual field damage was associated with poorer surgical prognosis in CPACG.Although complete 360° angle opening was confirmed intraoperatively under indirect gonioscopy, postoperative peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS) recurred frequently, especially in CPACG, suggesting a potential role of angle-closure mechanisms in surgical outcomes.

Introduction

Primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) is a major cause of irreversible blindness, and current management focuses on relieving angle closure and lowering intraocular pressure (IOP) using medications, laser peripheral iridotomy, and surgery [1–3]. Lens extraction effectively deepens the anterior chamber and widens the angle. Randomized trials and meta-analyses show that phacoemulsification provides meaningful IOP reduction in PAC/PACG and can be comparable to traditional filtering surgery in certain cases [4–6].

Building on this, phacoemulsification combined with goniosynechialysis (Phaco-GSL) has been increasingly adopted for PACG eyes with significant peripheral anterior synechiae (PAS), where lens removal alone may not adequately reopen the synechially closed angle or expose functional trabecular meshwork [7]. Several randomized trials and meta-analyses have demonstrated that Phaco-GSL achieves greater IOP reduction and more extensive PAS resolution than phacoemulsification alone in primary angle-closure disease, especially when instrumental GSL is performed [8–10]. Phaco-GSL has been proven effective for acute primary angle-closure glaucoma (APACG) in several studies, with reported success rates typically around 90–100% in medically unresponsive or acute cases [11–13].

However, some other studies reported that the additional IOP-lowering benefit of GSL over phacoemulsification alone was modest in chronic primary angle-closure glaucoma (CPACG) [2, 6, 10, 14, 15]. In particular, CPACG with long-standing PAS may have more irreversible trabecular damage, lower success rates, and higher recurrent PAS after Phaco-GSL compared with APACG [13]. Thus, although Phaco-GSL is widely used, high-quality, long-term prospective data directly comparing outcomes between APACG and CPACG, and identifying prognostic factors for surgical success or failure, remain scarce.

Therefore, this prospective study aimed to compare the 2-year long-term outcomes of Phaco-GSL in Chinese patients with APACG and CPACG, and to identify clinical and anatomical predictors associated with surgical success, to better individualize treatment strategies for PACG.

Methods

This prospective cohort study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of Eye and ear, nose, and throat (ENT) Hospital of Fudan University, Shanghai (approval ID 2021078). All patients provided written informed consent to participate in our study.

Patients

Patients with APACG or CPACG who were scheduled for phacoemulsification, foldable intraocular lens (IOL) implantation, and goniosynechialysis at the glaucoma clinics of Eye and ENT Hospital were recruited consecutively between January 2019 and December 2022.

The definition of PACG is consistent with ISGEO criteria for primary angle-closure glaucoma. Specifically, APACG was diagnosed when all of the following criteria were fulfilled, in accordance with previously published definitions of primary angle-closure glaucoma [1, 16–19]: (1) occurrence of two or more typical symptoms involving ocular/periocular pain, nausea, vomiting, or episodic blurred vision with halos; (2) detection of three or more clinical signs including significant conjunctival hyperemia, corneal epithelial edema, med-dilated pupil, and shallow anterior chamber; (3) IOP exceeding 21 mmHg; (4) gonioscopically confirmed angle closure; and (5) glaucomatous optic neuropathy accompanied by consistent visual field impairment. CPACG was diagnosed when all of the following criteria were fulfilled [1, 4, 17–19]: (1) absence of any history or signs of an acute primary angle-closure; (2) PAS confirmed by gonioscopy; (3) a chronically elevated IOP, requiring IOP-lowering medications, or IOP of more than 21 mmHg without IOP-lowering medication; and (4) glaucomatous optic disc damage with compatible visual field defects.

On the basis of the aforementioned definitions of APACG and CPACG, individuals with: (1) Han Chinese; (2) age 40–80 years old; (3) concurrent cataract were included in the study. Only one eye of each patient was included. If both eyes of one patient met the inclusion criteria, the eye with earlier date of surgery was included in this study.

Patients were excluded from the study if they met any of the following exclusion criteria: (1) best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) worse than counting figures; (2) with a history of ocular trauma; (3) with a previous ocular surgery (excluding laser peripheral iridotomy (LPI) and argon laser peripheral iridoplasty (ALPI)); (4) with a history of uveitis; (5) with significant conjunctival scar; (6) axial length < 20 mm; (7) with visible neovascularization on iris or anterior chamber angle; (8) aphakic or pseudophakic eye; (9) had other diseases that may be secondary or combined with angle closure glaucoma, such as lens luxation, RP, Bestrophinopathy, microphthalmia, etc.; and (10) had other severe eye diseases that would affect visual function significantly, such as age-related macular degeneration and pathogenic myopia, etc.

Ophthalmic Examination

Medical histories were collected for all patients enrolled in this study including onset symptoms, number of IOP-lowering medications, history of LPI or ALPI, and past ocular and systemic conditions. The duration of elevated IOP (> 21 mmHg) was assessed only in the APACG subgroup. A comprehensive ophthalmic examination was performed and key information was recorded, such as extent of synechial angle closure by gonioscopy (Volk Optical, Inc., Mentor, OH, USA), vertical cup-to-disc ratio (VCDR) by using a 90-diopter hand-held lens (Volk Optical, Inc., Mentor, OH, USA), preoperative IOP by Goldmann applanation tonometry, BCVA by Snellen chart and converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) visual acuity, visual field (VF) static automated white-on-white threshold perimetry (Octopus 101, Haag-Streit Inc., Koeniz, Switzerland); retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) and ganglion cell complex (GCC) thickness by SD-OCT (RTVue OCT; Optovue Inc., Fremont, CA, USA), anterior chamber depth (ACD) by ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM, MD-300L; MEDA Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China), corneal endothelial cells (CEC) by a specular microscope (Nidek CEM-530, Japan), axial length (AL), central corneal thickness (CCT), lens thickness (LT), and pupil diameter (PD) by Low-coherence interferometry (LenStar 900; Haag-Streit, Koeniz, Switzerland) or IOLMaster700® (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany). The angle closure mechanism was determined based on standardized UBM criteria before LPI. Eyes were classified into pupillary block, nonpupillary block, or mixed pattern when at least 2 quadrants met the corresponding criteria [20, 21].

Surgical Procedure

The Phaco-GSL surgeries were performed mainly by four experienced glaucoma specialists with more than 10 years’ experience in glaucoma (Y.C., J.W., X.K., and L.W.). Briefly, after a standard phacoemulsification procedure, a foldable IOL was implanted in the capsular bag. The PAS was initially separated using viscoelastic material. If complete opening was not achieved, further separation was performed using an iris repositor or microforceps, until the scleral spur was clearly visible and 360° angle opening was achieved, as confirmed by indirect gonioscopy. Phaco power and time were recorded during operation. Postoperatively, all patients received topical antibiotic and steroid drops 4–6 times daily for 2–4 weeks, with the regimen tapered according to the degree of anterior chamber inflammation [22, 23]. Pilocarpine was prescribed at the surgeon’s judgement. Additional IOP-lowering medications were added as needed to achieve target IOP.

Outcome Measures

Postoperative follow-up visits were scheduled at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and then every 6 months until 2 years postoperatively. At each visit, the following information was documented: IOPs, the number of IOP-lowering medications used, visual acuity, VCDR, complications and interventions. Gonioscopy was examined at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months and 1 year postoperatively. ACD and CEC were assessed at 3 months or 6 months postoperatively. VF, RNFL and GCC thickness were measured every 6 months over the 2-year follow-up period.

The primary outcome was the postoperative IOP. On the basis of the severity of visual field damage in Octopus perimetry, patients were categorized into two groups: early-moderate and advanced [24–27]. Complete success was defined as achieving a postoperative IOP within 6–21 mmHg for patients with early to moderate visual field damage [5, 28] and 6–18 mmHg for patients with advanced visual field damage [29, 30] without IOP-lowering medications [31–33]. Qualified success followed the same IOP criteria but allowed the use of IOP-lowering medications. Failure was defined as meeting any of the following criteria: (1) failure to achieve either complete success or qualified success at ≥ 1 month postoperatively, excluding transient IOP elevations within the first postoperative month; (2) need for reoperation for glaucoma; or (3) loss of light perception. Secondary outcomes were the number of IOP-lowering medications, BCVA, extent of PAS (degrees), mean deviation (MD) of VF, RNFL, and GCC thickness.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) or median (range) for continuous variables and n (%) for categorical variables. Continuous parameters were compared between the two groups using Mann–Whitney tests or t-tests based on whether the data was normally distributed and whether variances were homogeneous. Associations between two categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared tests or Fisher’s exact tests. The postoperative duration of pilocarpine use was analyzed as an ordinal variable with three levels: 0 (no use), 1 (≤ 3 months), and 2 (> 3 months). Changes in quantitative variables before and after surgery were compared using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon paired signed rank tests.

Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method to compare cumulative success rates between the APACG and CPACG groups. The endpoint was defined as surgical failure (failure of complete success or qualified success). Censoring was applied for patients who did not reach the failure endpoint at the last follow-up, those lost to follow-up, or those who withdrew from the study. Univariable and multivariable analyses with the Cox proportional-hazards regression model were used to estimate the effects of prognostic factors for surgical failure, including age, sex, IOP, ACD, AL, MD, VCDR, PD, number of IOP-lowering medications, extent of PAS, mechanism classification based on UBM, the postoperative duration of pilocarpine use, duration of high IOP. The covariates that were considered clinically relevant or those showed statistical significance in univariate analysis were entered into multivariable Cox proportional-hazards regression models. Hazard ratios (HRs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived from Cox models. The proportionality assumption of the Cox model was tested with Schoenfeld residuals, and was valid for all the analyses. On the basis of the Cox proportional-hazards regression model with the identified prognostic factors, a nomogram that predicted the 1- and 2-year success probability of patients with PACG was constructed. We did not use imputing preoperative data analyses owing to the missing data less than 3%. Gonioscopic analyses at each postoperative time point were performed using available-case analysis. For the analysis of postoperative PAS status at the last available follow-up within 1 year, a sensitivity analysis was additionally performed using a worst-case approach under a missing-not-at-random assumption to assess the robustness of the findings in the presence of missing postoperative gonioscopy data. All analyses were performed using the open-source statistical software R version 4.2.4 (R Foundation). A threshold of P < 0.05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 213 patients, including 116 patients (116 eyes) with APACG and 97 patients (97 eyes) with CPACG, were recruited consecutively between January 2019 and December 2022. The average age of all patients was 64.5 ± 7.2 years. 153 (71.8%) patients were women and 60 (28.2%) patients were men.

The APACG group had higher proportion of women (81.0% versus 60.8%, p = 0.001), and exhibited higher baseline IOP (25.2 ± 11.9 mmHg versus 20.6 ± 8.4 mmHg, p = 0.002), worse BCVA (0.4 versus 0.2, logMAR, p = 0.001), a greater extent of PAS (p < 0.001), thicker RNFL (94.9 ± 25.0 μm versus 74.7 ± 17.4 μm, p < 0.001), thicker GCC (85.9 ± 16.5 μm versus 79.0 ± 14.7 μm, p = 0.003), shallower ACD (1.6 ± 0.2 mm versus 1.9 ± 0.2 mm, p < 0.001), thicker CCT (552.2 ± 45.3 μm versus 534.7 ± 36.4 μm, p = 0.002), larger pupil diameter (4.2 ± 1.3 mm versus 2.5 ± 0.7 mm, p < 0.001), more IOP-lowering medications used (p = 0.012), and a higher proportion of LPI (61.2% versus 39.2%, p < 0.001) preoperatively compared with those of CPACG. Two groups showed no significant differences in the age, eye laterality, lens thickness, axial length, LT/AL, and baseline MD of visual field (Table 1). Intraoperative factors, including average phaco power (APP) and effective phaco time (EPT), were comparable between the two groups. Postoperatively, the duration of pilocarpine use was also similar across groups (Table 1).

During the follow-up period, a total of 30 patients were censored out of 213 enrolled participants (14.1%), including 20 cases in the APACG group (17.2%) and 10 cases in the CPACG group (10.3%). The complete success rates at 2 years were 72.4% and 65.0% for the APACG and CPACG groups, respectively (P = 0.04) (Fig. 1A). The qualified success rates were 81.9% and 81.4% for the APACG and CPACG groups, respectively (P = 0.44) (Fig. 1B).

Prognostic factors associated with surgical failure of complete success were analyzed using the Cox proportional-hazards regression model which was adjusted for different variables as presented in Table 2. In all patients, the following demographic and clinical factors were evaluated: type of glaucoma, age, sex, baseline IOP, baseline MD, ACD, AL, and LT/AL. A worse baseline MD was independently associated with an increased hazard of surgical failure (HR, 1.06; 95% CI 1.03–1.10; P < 0.001). Conversely, a deeper ACD was associated with a lower hazard of surgical failure (HR, 0.18; 95% CI 0.05–0.61; P = 0.006). The chronic type had a higher hazard of surgical failure compared with the acute type (HR, 1.90; 95% CI 1.03–3.51; P = 0.041). Subgroup analysis was also performed (Table 2). In patients with APACG, age, sex, baseline IOP, ACD, AL, LT/AL, and the duration of high IOP (> 21 mmHg) were assessed for their association with surgical outcomes. We found that older age (HR, 1.07; 95% CI 1.01–1.13; P = 0.034), higher baseline IOP (HR, 1.03; 95% CI 1.00–1.07; P = 0.048), and a longer duration of high IOP (HR, 4.87; 95% CI 1.59–14.91; P = 0.006 for 2 weeks to 1 month; HR, 36.32; 95% CI 11.9–111.2; P < 0.0001 for longer than 1 month) were significantly associated with a higher risk of surgical failure. Notably, surgical failure events were unevenly distributed across high IOP duration categories (4/83 for ≤ 2 weeks, 6/15 for 2 weeks to 1 month, and 17/18 for > 1 month), and because the duration of high IOP was assessed retrospectively on the basis of patient recall and medical record review, substantial recall bias may have contributed to the wide confidence interval. In patients with CPACG, age, sex, baseline IOP, baseline MD, ACD, AL, LT/AL, PD, extent of PAS, mechanism classification based on UBM, and the postoperative duration of pilocarpine usage were analyzed to explore their impact on surgical outcomes. Consistent with the overall results, the analysis revealed that a deeper ACD was associated with a lower hazard of surgical failure (HR, 0.15; 95% CI 0.02–0.95; P = 0.044). Unlike in the APACG group, older age was associated with a lower hazard of surgical failure (HR,0.95; 95% CI 0.90–1.00; P = 0.043). In the sensitivity analysis using qualified success (allowing medications) as the endpoint, the overall pattern of prognostic factors was generally consistent with the primary analysis. However, some associations (e.g., group comparison in all patients and higher baseline IOP in the APACG subgroup) were attenuated and no longer statistically significant. The prognostic nomogram that integrated statistically significant variables selected using multivariate Cox regression was developed to predict the probability of the surgical success at 1 and 2 years postoperatively (Fig. 2). The nomogram illustrated that baseline MD had the largest contribution to prognosis, followed by ACD and type of glaucoma. Each variable was assigned a score on a point scale according to the actual condition for a particular patient. By adding up the total score and locating it on the total point scale, we can easily determine the 1- and 2-year survival probabilities.

At the final visit (Table 3), both groups demonstrated a remarkable improvement in visual function, including better visual acuity and visual field, along with significant enhancement in ocular biometric parameters such as ACD and extent of open angle. Mean IOP decreased to 13.8 ± 3.0 mmHg in the APACG group and 14.4 ± 3.2 mmHg in the CPACG group (P = 0.32). Mean BCVA (logMAR) improved to 0.05 (−0.1 to 2.3) in the APACG group and 0.05 (0.0–2.3) in the CPACG group (P = 0.33). Patients with APACG had greater decrease of IOP (−11.3 versus −6.2, P < 0.001), number of IOP-lowering medications (−3.2 versus −2.6, P < 0.001), RNFL (−20.9 versus −3.5, P < 0.0001), and GCC (−3.4 versus −0.7, P < 0.001), greater increase of BCVA (−0.23 versus −0.14, P = 0.02) and ACD (1.8 versus 1.4, P < 0.0001) compared with those of CPACG. Given the markedly thicker baseline RNFL and GCC in APACG, the postoperative thinning may be partly attributable to the resolution of acute swelling; however, glaucomatous progression cannot be excluded [34, 35].

Gonioscopic data were collected from 111 (95.7%) patients with APACG and 86 (88.7%) patients with CPACG, with assessments performed preoperatively and at least once postoperatively. Overall, postoperative PAS developed most rapidly within the first month postoperatively (25.7% versus 28.8% at 1 month in APACG and CPACG respectively, P = 0.53), continued to progress, and peaked between 3 and 6 months (Fig. 3). Beyond 6 months, postoperative PAS stabilized, with most cases showing no further progression. The PAS reached its peak at 6 months in the acute group and at 3 months in the chronic group. The highest proportion of postoperative PAS was higher in CPACG (35.8%) than in APACG (28.7%), but the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.36). Preoperatively, the APACG group had significantly greater PAS extent than the CPACG group (P < 0.0001), as well as a higher proportion of eyes with PAS ≥ 180° (P = 0.048). At the last available follow-up within 1 year, the prevalence of postoperative PAS was comparable between the two groups (P = 0.74), and the mean postoperative PAS extent also did not differ significantly (P = 0.34). However, the postoperative-to-preoperative PAS ratio was significantly lower in the APACG group than in the CPACG group (P = 0.016). In addition, fewer APACG eyes showed postoperative PAS ≥ 180° or ≥ 1/2 of the preoperative PAS extent compared with CPACG eyes (P = 0.049) (Table 4). A sensitivity analysis was performed using a worst-case assumption for missing postoperative PAS data (Supplementary Table 2). The results were generally consistent with the primary analysis, although the magnitude of differences between groups was slightly altered. We also examined whether postoperative pilocarpine use correlated with the degree of postoperative PAS. No significant difference was found between patients with and without pilocarpine use (Mann–Whitney U test, P = 0.11), and no significant correlation was observed (Spearman’s r = −0.005, P = 0.97).

Discussion

In this study we compared long-term effect of Phaco-GSL in patients with APACG and CPACG over a 2 years’ follow-up, and we also evaluated the influencing factors for surgical prognosis. We found that both patients with APACG and CPACG achieved significantly reduced IOP, improvement in BCVA and MD, decreased need for IOP-lowering medications, notable reduction in the extent of PAS in 2 years’ follow-up compared with baseline. However, there was a significant difference between APACG and CPACG in terms of complete success rate (APACG versus CPACG: 72.4% versus 65.0%, P = 0.04). To be more specific, the acute group experienced a greater reduction in IOP, required fewer medications, showed more significant improvement in visual acuity, had a greater extent of angle opening, and demonstrated a larger increase in ACD.

Numerous studies have confirmed the effectiveness of Phaco-GSL [36–38] in angle closure glaucoma. The reported 1-year success rate of Phaco-GSL in patients with PAC/PACG is 57.9%–100% [2, 11, 39] for complete success and 85.9%–95% [2, 40, 41] for qualified success, with variations in study design and patient enrollment. Our results were consistent with previous reports with a longer follow-up period and a larger sample size of participants. The overall findings consistently confirm the effectiveness of Phaco-GSL in reducing IOP, minimizing medication use, and improving visual function.

There have been two clinical reports comparing Phaco-GSL in acute and chronic PACG, but both were limited by their retrospective design and small sample size. White et al [42] compared the effect of Phaco-GSL in patients with PAC/PACG with and without a history of acute episodes. Although they found there was no significant difference in IOP and number of IOP-lowering medications used between the two groups after surgery, the mean decrease of IOP was larger in the acute group. Tian et al. [13] found that the complete success rates were 100% in the acute group and 64.3% in the chronic group at 3 months after surgery, showing a statistically significant difference. In our study, the complete success rates at 2 years were 72.4% in the acute group and 65.0% in the chronic group (P = 0.04), which also suggested better prognosis of GSL in APACG compared with that of CPACG.

Although complete 360° angle opening was confirmed intraoperatively under gonioscopy, postoperative PAS developed progressively after GSL surgery, which was the main reason of the decline in surgical success rate over time. Our results showed that most cases experienced postoperative PAS (85.6% in the acute group and 87.2% in the chronic group). Postoperative PAS progressed rapidly within the first month postoperatively, peaked between 3 and 6 months, and remained stable in most cases after 6 months. Similarly, Zhao et al. [43] found postoperative PAS appeared during the 1-month follow-up and tended to be stable at 6 months postoperatively. Tian et al. [13] found that most postoperative PAS occurred within 1 week and tended to be stable at 1 month after surgery. Meanwhile, we also observed that although preoperatively, patients with APACG had a greater extent of PAS (P < 0.0001) and a higher proportion of cases involving PAS ≥ 180° (P = 0.048) compared with patients with CPACG, they showed a lower proportion of postoperative PAS relative to preoperative PAS (p = 0.016), and a lower proportion of postoperative PAS ≥ 180° or postoperative PAS ≥ 1/2 preoperative PAS (P = 0.049) compared with patients with CPACG postoperatively. Consistent with our results, Tian et al. observed that the postoperative PAS was more common in the chronic cases (acute versus chronic: 30% versus 83.3%), and the failure rate was higher in the chronic cases (acute versus chronic: 0% versus 35.7%). These results clearly indicate surgical outcomes are more favorable in APACG compared with CPACG. The extent of preoperative PAS is not a determinant factor for the recurrent PAS but the mechanism of angle closure is the factor really matters. GSL works better for APACG than CPACG mainly owing to the different mechanisms of the angle closure, which is predominantly pupillary block for APACG but more nonpupillary block for CPACG [20, 21].

Postoperative pilocarpine was prescribed in some patients to facilitate angle opening by stretching the peripheral iris away from the trabecular meshwork, with the aim of reducing postoperative PAS. However, prolonged miotic therapy has also been hypothesized to promote PAS formation through chronic miosis. In the present study, postoperative pilocarpine use was not significantly associated with surgical prognosis in multivariable Cox analysis, despite its use in 34.5% of acute cases and 42.3% of chronic cases. These findings suggest that the effect of pilocarpine on postoperative PAS formation and surgical outcomes may be limited. Nevertheless, given the theoretical concern, the potential iatrogenic role of pilocarpine should be considered in clinical practice, particularly in patients requiring long-term miotic therapy.

There are limited literature discussing the prognostic factors for surgical failure in Phaco-GSL for PACG. Husain et al. [2] found that having a higher baseline IOP was less likely to be a hazardous event. Qian et al. [44] showed that the success rate of glaucoma surgery tends to decrease in more advanced stages of the disease. In our study, the disease course was the most crucial influencing factor in APACG, CPACG, and the overall patient population, with MD reflecting the progression of CPACG, while the duration of high IOP represented the course of APACG. Additionally, ACD significantly influenced surgical outcomes, and glaucoma type was another contributing factor.

This study has several inherent limitations. First, gonioscopy examinations were performed by different surgeons, and the subjective nature of the assessment, combined with incomplete gonioscopy follow-up at certain postoperative visits, may have introduced measurement and selection bias into the results. Second, the loss to follow-up rate was 4.2% at 1 year and increased to 14.6% at 2 years. Those with well-controlled postoperative IOPs were more reluctant to follow up, while patients with worse controlled IOPs were more likely to adhere to regular follow-ups. Meanwhile, patients from distant rural areas had relatively more loss to follow-up than patients living in town. These factors may have caused deviation in the analysis of success rates and prognostic factors. In addition, surgical failure events were clustered around the first postoperative month. This pattern may have been influenced, at least in part, by the operational definition of failure adopted in the present study, rather than solely reflecting the natural postoperative course. Moreover, because this was an observational study using Cox proportional hazards models, the identified prognostic factors should be interpreted as associations with surgical failure rather than definitive causal determinants, and residual or unmeasured confounding cannot be completely excluded.

Conclusions

This prospective study involved a large cohort of Chinese patients with PACG and long-term follow-up to evaluate the prognosis of Phaco-GSL in APACG and CPACG cases, as well as the prognostic factors. Phaco-GSL demonstrated significant benefits for both APACG and CPACG, with more pronounced effects for APACG than CPACG. Several baseline characteristics, including disease course, ACD, and glaucoma type, were associated with surgical prognosis. These findings offer valuable insights into better assessment of GSL surgery in PACG.
