Multi-omics profiling and bile-acid exposure assays implicate a gut microbiome–parasite axis linked to persistent Entamoeba histolytica carriage
Authors: Yasuaki Yanagawa, Naoko Yoshida, Takashi Makiuchi, Akira Kawashima, Haruka Uemura, Takahiro Aoki, Daisuke Mizushima, Hiroyuki Gatanaga, Koji Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Entamoeba histolytica (Eh) is a major protozoan parasite that remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality globally, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions [1, 2]. While traditionally considered a food- and water-borne pathogen, the epidemiology of amebiasis has shifted in non-endemic countries [3, 4]. In Japan, Eh infection is increasingly recognized as a sexually transmitted infection (STI), with a disproportionately high prevalence among men who have sex with men (MSM) [5]. Despite advances in public health, the number of reported cases in Japan continues to rise, making it a critical concern for sexual health and intestinal infectious disease control [6]. The clinical spectrum of amebiasis is remarkably diverse, ranging from asymptomatic cyst shedding to invasive colitis and life-threatening extraintestinal complications [7]. A critical challenge in the clinical management of amebic colitis is that metronidazole (MTZ) monotherapy—the standard first-line treatment—often fails to completely eradicate cysts within the intestinal lumen, frequently leading to a chronic carrier state [8]. This clinical observation suggests that in individuals with persistent colonization, the parasite may exhibit reduced susceptibility to MTZ or employ specific adaptation strategies to survive within the host gut environment [9, 10]. However, the physiological and pathological events occurring within the gut during asymptomatic colonization remain largely a “black box.” [11] The intestinal lumen is a metabolite-rich niche where pathogens and commensals compete under host constraints. In this setting, microbiota-derived metabolites—particularly bile acids—act as ecological and signaling factors that can modulate microbial community structure and directly shape pathogen physiology. Secondary bile acids such as deoxycholic acid (DCA), generated by bacterial transformations, can influence membrane integrity, stress responses, and drug susceptibility across diverse gut microbes. We therefore hypothesized that a microbiome–bile acid axis contributes to persistent Eh colonization and alters parasite phenotypes relevant to transmission and treatment response. Because asymptomatic individuals do not seek medical attention, prospective diagnosis and sample collection are inherently difficult, leading to a significant lack of data regarding this population. This knowledge gap hinders our understanding of the factors that determine whether the parasite remains a commensal-like colonizer or transitions into an invasive pathogen, as well as the mechanisms underlying its reduced sensitivity to standard treatments. To address this issue, we established a prospective cohort study at a specialized outpatient clinic in Tokyo that serves a high-risk MSM population. By implementing a systematic screening and collection protocol, we prospectively obtained fecal and serum samples from consecutive attendees over a defined period. This study design allowed us to capture the biological state of Eh during actual human colonization, providing a unique opportunity to integrate clinical data with molecular analysis. The objective of this study was to characterize the gut environment of asymptomatic Eh carriers by combining shotgun metagenomic sequencing with mechanistic in vitro validation. Specifically, we focused on how alterations in the gut microbiota and its metabolites influence the parasite’s transcriptomic state and its susceptibility to metronidazole, offering new insights into the pathogenesis and management of amebiasis in persistent carrier states.

Methods

Study design and ethical statement

This cross-sectional study was designed to investigate the associations between the gut microbiome, microbial metabolites, and the biological characteristics of individuals infected with Eh. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Institute of Health Sciences (JIHS), Japan (Approval No. NCGM-G-003333-02). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection.

Participant recruitment and clinical samples

Participants were recruited from outpatients at the Sexual Health clinic (SH Clinic) in JIHS. Fecal samples were collected and stabilized using the OMNIgene·GUT system (OMR-200; DNA Genotek Inc., Ottawa, ON, Canada) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [12]. The stabilized samples were stored at -80 °C until further analysis. Participants were classified into five distinct groups based on the combined results of stool microscopy, quantitative PCR (qPCR) for Eh, and serum Eh-specific antibody testing:

Eh_Cyst group: Individuals positive for Eh cysts by microscopy and confirmed positive for Eh by qPCR.

Eh_Cyst group: Individuals positive for Eh cysts by microscopy and confirmed positive for Eh by qPCR.

Eh_qPCR group: Individuals negative for Eh by microscopy but positive for Eh by qPCR.

Eh_qPCR group: Individuals negative for Eh by microscopy but positive for Eh by qPCR.

Eh_Seropositive group: Individuals negative for Eh by both microscopy and qPCR, but positive for Eh-specific serum IgG antibodies.

Eh_Seropositive group: Individuals negative for Eh by both microscopy and qPCR, but positive for Eh-specific serum IgG antibodies.

Giardia group: Individuals positive for Giardia cysts by microscopy and negative for Eh by qPCR.

Giardia group: Individuals positive for Giardia cysts by microscopy and negative for Eh by qPCR.

Healthy Control group: Individuals who tested negative for Eh by microscopy, qPCR, and serology.

Healthy Control group: Individuals who tested negative for Eh by microscopy, qPCR, and serology.

Diagnosis and classification of Eh infection

The diagnosis of Eh infection was established using a combination of the following three modalities:

Microscopy

Fecal samples were examined for the presence of amebic cysts or trophozoites using direct smear and the formalin-ether sedimentation (MGL) method [13].

Serology

Serum samples were tested for Eh-specific IgG antibodies using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [14].

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

DNA was extracted from fecal samples, and qPCR targeting the 18 S rRNA gene was performed to specifically identify and distinguish E. histolytica from E. dispar [15]. Samples positive for Eh by qPCR were defined as active infections (cyst carriers).

Fecal DNA extraction and shotgun metagenomic sequencing

Library preparation and sequencing

DNA libraries were prepared using the ThruPLEX DNA-seq Kit (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [16]. The quality and concentration of the prepared libraries were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with a 150-bp paired-end sequencing strategy.

Bioinformatics pipeline and quality control

The raw sequencing data were processed using the KneadData pipeline (v0.7.2) to ensure high-quality reads and to eliminate host-derived sequences. Briefly, adapter sequences and low-quality bases were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.39) [17]. To remove host contamination, the remaining reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using Bowtie 2 (v2.3.5.1).

Taxonomic profiling

Taxonomic classification was performed using Kaiju (v1.7.3) on the high-quality, non-human reads. Kaiju assigned taxonomic labels by translating reads into protein sequences and searching against the NCBI non-redundant (nr) protein database, which covers bacteria, archaea, viruses, fungi, and microbial eukaryotes [18]. The resulting taxonomic abundance data were used for downstream statistical comparisons between the study groups.

Differential abundance analysis (MaAsLin2)

For the univariate Eh_Cyst versus Eh_qPCR screen at the genus level, relative abundances were compared using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Log2 fold changes were computed as log2(median relative abundance in Eh_Cyst / median relative abundance in Eh_qPCR) with a small pseudocount added to both groups to handle zeros. P values from this univariate screen were reported unadjusted for exploratory purposes; multiple testing correction was applied in multivariable MaAsLin2 analyses using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. We performed multivariable association testing using MaAsLin2 (Multivariable Association with Linear Models) to assess genus-level microbial signatures associated with Eh infection status while accounting for potential confounders [19, 20]. Genus relative abundances derived from Kaiju taxonomic profiling were filtered prior to modeling (filtering criteria as described above) and analyzed under MaAsLin2 with total-sum scaling (TSS) normalization followed by log transformation. (i) Case-only analysis (Eh_Cyst vs. Eh_qPCR among Eh-positive participants). We restricted the dataset to Eh-positive individuals (Eh_status = present) and modeled genus abundance using Eh_group2 (Eh_qPCR vs. Eh_Cyst) as the primary fixed effect, adjusting for Symptom, Giardia_status, and Batch:

Genus ~ EH_group2 + Symptom + Giardia_status + Batch.

Effect sizes are reported as MaAsLin2 coefficients for Eh_Cyst relative to Eh_qPCR.

(ii) Ordinal burden analysis (Eh_score model). To capture stepwise variation across infection states, we defined an ordinal Eh_score (0: Eh-negative including controls, Giardia-only, and seropositive; 1: Eh_qPCR; 2: Eh_Cyst) and fitted:

Genus ~ Eh_score + Symptom + Giardia_status + Batch.

Positive coefficients indicate increasing abundance with higher Eh_score.

For both models, p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure and are reported as FDR q-values. Given the exploratory nature and limited sample size, we interpret associations primarily by effect direction and consistency across models, with full results provided in Supplementary Tables.

E. histolytica for in vitro assays

Eh laboratory strain HM1:IMSS clone 6 was used for all experiments. It was been axenically cultured at 35.5℃ in BI-S-33 medium, as previously described [21, 22]. Also, glucose-deleted BI-S-medium, whose component and pH except for D-glucose were the same as BI-S-33 medium, was separately prepared for the present study. For bile acid stimulation, cholic acid (©FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical) or deoxycholic acid (©Tokyo Chemical Industry) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at 100 mM concentration. Then, these solutions were added to culture medium as 1:1000 dilution (final concentration is 100 µM of bile acid). To assess the growth, 4.5 × 10E4 log-phase growth Eh trophozoites were firstly added into glass tube with 5 mL of culture medium. Trophozoites were counted with their viability by microscopy after trypan-blue staining. Also, trophozoites’ viability was assessed at the end of culture by WST-1 assay (see below). Specifically for assessing the phenotypic changes of Eh by bile acids stimulation, trophozoites were pre-incubated with CA, DCA, or solvent control for 72 h, collected, washed twice with PBS, and then used for the subsequent glucose-depletion and metronidazole susceptibility experiments in bile-acid–free medium.

WST-1 assay

WST-1 reagent kit (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Inc.) was used for evaluating Eh viability [23, 24]. WST-1 was reduced by the live Eh, which reduced to form WST-1 formazan. Yellow color of WST-1 formazan was measured by spectrophotometer. Firstly, Eh trophozoites were prepared on flat bottom culture plates. Then, culture medium was substituted by the WST-1 containing serum-free Opti-MEM medium (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Twenty minutes later, absorbance at 460 nm was measured by microplate spectrophotometer.

Metronidazole susceptibility

Metronidazole solution (0–10 mM) was prepared in DMSO. Each solution was dissolved into culture medium as 1:1000 dilution (final concentration 0–10 mM). Eh (5 × 10E3 trophozoites in 300µL per well) were put into flat bottom 96 well plate and were incubated for 48 h [25]. Thereafter, trophozoite viability was assessed by WST-1 assay [23]. Finally, viability in each metronidazole concentration was calculated as percent viability to that without metronidazole. All conditions were tested in triplicate. Background absorbance (medium + WST-1 without cells) was subtracted. The no-drug control contained 0.1% DMSO.

RNA-sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from approximately 1 × 10E6 trophozoites (with each culture performed in triplicate) using a Nucleospin RNA Kit (Takara) according to the manufacturer’s guidance. In short, Eh trophozoites were collected by centrifugation and then disrupted by the addition of lysis buffer. Genomic DNA was digested by treating with RNasefree rDNase. Total RNA was eluted in a total volume of 60 µl nuclease-free water. The RNA quality was determined with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer.

For pre-incubated Eh with CA, DCA, or DMSO for 72 h, library preparation.

was performed by Eurofins Genomics. The polyA fraction (mRNA) was isolated from total.

RNA, followed by its fragmentation. Then, double-stranded (ds) cDNA was reverse transcribed from the fragmented mRNA. The ds cDNA fragments were processed for adaptor ligation, size selection (for 200-bp inserts) and amplification to generate cDNA libraries. Prepared libraries were subjected to paired-end 2 × 101 bp sequencing on the HiSeq 2500 and 4000 platform, using the HiSeq 3000/4000 SBS kit. For HM-1 (in vitro) strain, library preparation was performed by AZENTA Life Sciences (Tokyo, Japan). The poly(A) mRNA isolation was performed using Oligo(dT) beads. The mRNA fragmentation was performed using divalent cations and a high temperature. Priming was performed using random primers. First-strand and second-strand cDNA were synthesized. The purified ds cDNA was then treated to repair both ends and add a dA-tail in one reaction, followed by a T-A ligation to add adaptors to both ends. Size selection of adaptor-ligated DNA was then performed using DNA Clean Beads. Each sample was then amplified by PCR using P5 and P7 primers and the PCR products were validated. Then, libraries with different indexes were multiplexed and loaded onto an Illumina HiSeq X for sequencing using a 2 × 150 paired-end configuration according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Bioinformatic analysis of the RNA-seq data

Raw RNA-seq reads were processed using CLC Genomics Workbench (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) for quality trimming and mapping. The reads were mapped against the E. histolytica HM-1:IMSS reference genome (AmoebaDB v.1.7) using the gene model provided by Hon et al. [26]. To normalize the data and calculate fold changes between time points, the Trimmed Mean of M-values (TMM) method was applied. Principal Component Analysis was performed to assess the global gene expression variability and the reproducibility among biological replicates for each treatment group (DCA, CA, and DMSO). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by comparing the DCA-treated or CA-treated groups against the DMSO control group. Statistical significance was defined by a threshold of a False Discovery Rate (FDR)-adjusted p-value < 0.100 and an absolute log2-fold-change (|log2FC|) ≥ 1.0. The transcriptomic signatures were visualized using volcano plots and heatmaps. Functional annotation and enrichment analysis were performed for the identified DEGs to characterize the metabolic and adaptive responses induced by secondary bile acids, with a focus on stress response and drug transporter genes.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using DEGs identified from the DCA versus DMSO comparison in Eh. Genes with a FDR < 0.05 were used for over-representation analysis. KEGG annotation for Eh was assigned based on the KEGG organism code EHI, and genes were mapped to KEGG pathways using KEGG pathway-to-gene relationships [27, 28]. Enrichment analysis was performed in R using the cluster Profiler package with all genes included in the differential expression analysis as the background gene set. Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and pathways with an adjusted p value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. The enrichment results were visualized as a bubble plot showing fold enrichment, gene count, and adjusted p value.

Statistical analysis

Microbiome statistical analyses were conducted in R. Genus-level associations with Eh infection status were tested using MaAsLin2 with total-sum scaling (TSS) normalization followed by log transformation. Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, and results are reported as FDR-adjusted q values. Community-level differences were evaluated using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity with principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) and PERMANOVA. Given the small sample size, alpha diversity indices (Shannon diversity and observed richness) were compared between groups using the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Analyses of cohort (population) variables and in vitro experimental data were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.6.1; GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). RNA-seq data processing and downstream analyses were performed using CLC Genomics Workbench (version 25.0.1; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Differentially expressed genes were defined using an FDR < 0.100 together with a ≥ 2-fold change.

Results

Study overview and conceptual framework

We conducted this study in a prospectively screened outpatient cohort at the Japan Institute for Health Security (JIHS) in Tokyo. Between October 2021 and March 2022, we recruited consecutive clinic attendees for whom comprehensive screening was feasible irrespective of symptoms. The cohort predominantly comprised men who have sex with men (MSM) attending a sexual health clinic, enabling the capture of asymptomatic intestinal Eh carriage through paired blood and stool sampling. Blood samples were used for serum antibody testing for Eh infection, and stool samples were examined by microscopy and qPCR. Using these screened participants, we structured the downstream analyses around a conceptual framework in which Eh infection burden interacts with gut microbiome composition and parasite biological states relevant to persistence and transmission (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Study population

A total of 36 MSM clinic attendees with complete screening data were included and categorized into five groups: controls (n = 6), Giardia (n = 3), Eh_Seropositive (n = 4), Eh_qPCR (n = 13), and Eh_Cyst (n = 10) (Table 1). Overall, participants were middle-aged adults, with a median age in the low-to-mid 30s across groups (range 22–52 years in Eh_qPCR and Eh_Cyst). Median BMI ranged from 23.0 to 28.6 depending on group. HIV infection was rare, with only one case in the cyst group (10%) and none in the other groups. The proportion of non-Japanese nationality tended to be higher in the cyst group (60%) compared with other groups. The proportion receiving HIV PrEP ranged from 15% to 67% across groups. Clinically, the cohort was predominantly asymptomatic: only one participant in Eh_Cyst and one participant in Eh_qPCR reported diarrhea, and all remaining participants were asymptomatic at the time of screening.

Baseline characteristics

Gut microbiome signatures at genus level

At a higher taxonomic resolution, we first noted a phylum-level shift suggestive of increased Firmicutes representation in the Eh_Cyst group compared with Eh_qPCR, motivating a more granular evaluation at the genus level to identify candidate drivers of this pattern. We then assessed whether genus-level community diversity differed across the five clinical/parasitological groups (Fig. 1A). Bray–Curtis PCoA followed by PERMANOVA indicated small-to-moderate effect sizes (R² 0.12–0.16) across group comparisons, but these did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.1), suggesting limited separation at the whole-community level. Notably, the Eh_Cyst group appeared to show relatively tighter clustering in the Bray–Curtis PCoA plot than the other groups, suggesting lower between-sample heterogeneity in microbiome composition within this group. However, because the overall group separation did not reach statistical significance by PERMANOVA, this pattern was interpreted cautiously. For alpha diversity, Shannon diversity was comparable between Eh_qPCR and Eh_Cyst (p 0.98), with a slight trend toward higher Shannon in the Eh_qPCR group (Fig. 1B). In contrast, observed genus richness differed between Eh_qPCR and Eh_Cyst (p 0.046), with higher richness in the Eh_Cyst group, indicating that cyst-associated changes may be reflected more strongly in richness than in evenness (Fig. 1C).

We next sought to identify genera associated with cyst positivity. In a direct Eh_Cyst versus Eh_qPCR comparison, several genera showed nominal shifts in relative abundance, with Coprococcus, Ruminococcus, Catenibacterium, and Mitsuokella tending to be enriched in Eh_Cyst, whereas Bacteroides and related taxa tended to be relatively enriched in Eh_qPCR (Fig. 2A). Consistent with the limited sample size, these genus-level differences did not remain significant after multiple-testing correction and were therefore treated as exploratory signals. To capture the stepwise transition from non-infected to qPCR-positive to cyst-positive, we defined an Eh score as an ordinal measure across these states and performed genus-level association analyses. All genus-level results are provided in Supplementary Tables 1, and Table 2 summarizes genera showing suggestive associations (p < 0.200). Although no genus remained significant after multiple-testing correction, several taxa showed nominal trends, including positively associated genera such as Catenibacterium and Coprococcus, and negatively associated genera such as Collinsella, Parabacteroides, Adicaminococcus, Lachnoclostridium, and Bacteroides (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Table 1). These genus-level candidates were carried forward for species-level refinement (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 2). In a complementary analysis collapsing groups into Eh present vs. not present, neither Bray–Curtis beta diversity nor alpha diversity metrics showed statistically significant differences (all p > 0.1 for PERMANOVA; p 0.4–0.6 for alpha diversity), consistent with the primary five-group analysis (data not shown). To assess robustness, we evaluated whether genera highlighted in the Eh_Cyst versus Eh_qPCR contrast showed concordant directional trends in the ordinal Eh-score MaAsLin2 model (Supplementary Table 1). Giardia-associated comparisons were considered exploratory due to the small Giardia-positive sample size and are reported in the Supplement. Overall, Coprococcus, Catenibacterium, Mitsuokella, and Ruminococcus tended to increase along the Eh burden spectrum, whereas Bacteroides, together with Collinsella and Parabacteroides, tended to be relatively enriched at lower burden. Because none of these associations remained significant after multiple-testing correction (all q > 0.9), we treated them as exploratory and selected four representative genera (two enriched and two depleted) for species-level refinement.

Species-level patterns within representative genera

To refine the genus-level signals, we examined species-level profiles by directly contrasting Eh_Cyst vs. Eh_qPCR within representative genera (Fig. 3; Supplement Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 3). Within genera that were enriched in the Eh_Cyst group in the genus-level screen, multiple species showed higher abundance in the cyst-positive group. In Coprococcus (Fig. 3A), C. comes and C. eutactus increased in Eh_Cyst, and several additional Coprococcus features (including CAG-annotated taxa) followed the same direction. Similarly, Ruminococcus species/features, including R. callidus and multiple “sp.”/CAG taxa, were higher in Eh_Cyst (Fig. 3B). Catenibacterium also showed cyst-associated enrichment, most notably C. mitsuokai with concordant shifts in additional Catenibacterium features (Fig. 3C). For Mitsuokella, two detected species—M. jalaludinii and M. multacida—were both higher in Eh_Cyst than Eh_qPCR, providing species-level support for the genus-level enrichment pattern (Fig. 3D). In contrast, within Bacteroides, several species/features (e.g., B. vulgatus, B. acidifaciens, B. clarus, and Bacteroides “sp.” taxa) tended to be higher in Eh_qPCR and lower in Eh_Cyst (Fig. 3E), consistent with genus-level depletion observed in the cyst-positive comparison. Plots stratified by the ordinal Eh score showed stepwise trends consistent with the Eh_Cyst versus Eh_qPCR contrast (Supplementary Fig. 2). Overall, these profiles suggest that cyst-associated microbiome signatures reflect coordinated shifts across multiple species within each genus rather than a single dominant taxon.

Parasite phenotypes under bile-acid–related conditions and integrated model

Effects of bile-acid metabolites on Eh growth

Previously, we identified that asymptomatic carriers have higher burden of Ruminococcaceae family than invasive amebiasis patients in their gut microbiome [29]. Surprisingly, the present study also found higher burden of Ruminococcus in cyst-containing stool specimen. It has been known that Ruminococcus has much bile acid-inducible (bai) enzymes which converts primary bile acids (e.g. cholic acid (CA)) to secondary bile acids (e.g. deoxycholic acid (DCA)) in the large intestine. Furthermore, bai genes are not encoded in human, thus, composition of CA and DCA are highly dependent on the burden of bai encoded gut microbiome [30–32]. In general, bile acids are secreted to the duodenum via bile duct, 95% of which are absorbed in the small intestine, and remaining (100-1,000 µM) reaches the large intestine [33]. In the large intestine, gut microbiome makes unique composition of bile acids according to the bai enzymes. Therefore, we sought to assess the effects of bile acids on Eh phenotypes. Firstly, we checked in vitro growth in the presence of bile acids. In the presence of CA, Eh proliferated more at early phase of culture, however, its survival was not influenced (Fig. 4A). On the other hand, in the presence of DCA, Eh proliferated the same way as the control, however, it could survive longer up to 10 days (Fig. 4A&B). Also, preliminary results of glucose in culture media showed that glucose level rapidly decreased from day 4, which suggests that energy resources are highly limited at later phase of in vitro culture (supplement Fig. 3). Considered together, it is indicated that DCA, which is the converted form of CA by gut microbiome, does not have an effect on the growth speed, however, it could contribute to longer survival even in a severe environment.

Phenotypic changes of Eh by the stimuli of bile acids

To assess the phenotypic changes of Eh by the exposure of bile acids, we prepared pre-incubated Eh strains with CA or DCA for 72 h. Bile-acid preincubation had no effects on the viability in the glucose supplemented normal culture medium, however, DCA pre-incubated strain showed better survival in glucose-deleted condition (Fig. 5A). Although glucose deletion is the trigger of encystation in the other Entamoeba species [34], cystic forms could not be observed in the experiment. Additionally, we checked the viability in the metronidazole containing media, because activity of metronidazole is dependent on energy production by pyruvate-ferredoxin oxidoreductase (PFOR) of Eh. DCA-preincubated strain showed relatively higher viability even after 48 h-incubation in metronidazole containing medium (Fig. 5B). Taken together, DCA preincubation does not change the phenotype during fully supplemented conditions, however, it gives durability to cultural environment with lower energy supply.

Parasite RNA-seq response

To investigate the molecular impact of bile acids on the phenotypic changes of Eh, we assessed transcriptomic profiling. High-quality sequencing data was obtained for all samples, with an average of 11.5 million reads and over 95.3% of bases achieving ≥Q30 (Supplement Table 4). Principal component analysis and hierarchical clustering demonstrated that DCA, CA, and DMSO treatments formed distinct clusters, indicating that each bile acid elicits a distinct transcriptomic signature (Fig. 6A, B). A comparison of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across treatment groups revealed distinct transcriptomic profiles (Fig. 6C). Relative to DMSO, DCA and CA treatments significantly modulated 73 and 12 genes, respectively, whereas a direct comparison between DCA and CA identified 126 differentially expressed genes. Complete DEG lists for each contrast (CA vs. DMSO, DCA vs. DMSO, and CA vs. DCA), including fold changes and FDR-adjusted p values, are provided in Supplementary Table 5. Notably, overlap between bile-acid responses was limited: only two protein-coding genes (EHI_016380 and EHI_165810) were differentially expressed in both CA vs. DMSO and DCA vs. DMSO contrasts, with one additional shared repeat element (EhSINE2_163) (Supplementary Table 5), indicating largely bile-acid–specific transcriptional programs. Volcano plots further illustrated that DCA treatment induced a substantially more extensive transcriptomic response, with a greater number of DEGs compared to CA treatment relative to the DMSO control (Fig. 6D and E). Notably, a direct comparison between DCA and CA treatments identified 126 DEGs (Fig. 6C and F), supporting that DCA elicits a markedly stronger transcriptomic response than CA. DCA treatment-associated transcriptional changes in Eh were further evaluated by KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using DEG from the DCA versus DMSO comparison. Among 109 genes with significant differential expression at FDR < 0.05, 30 genes were annotated in KEGG and used for over-representation analysis. This analysis identified significant enrichment of amoebiasis (10 genes, adjusted p = 1.90 × 10^-5), cobalamin transport and metabolism (4 genes, adjusted p = 2.34 × 10^-4), Cysteine and methionine metabolism (5 genes, adjusted p = 3.35 × 10^-4), peroxisome (5 genes, adjusted p = 9.09 × 10^-4), biosynthesis of amino acids (6 genes, adjusted p = 1.26 × 10^-3), sulfur metabolism (3 genes, adjusted p = 6.04 × 10^-3), and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (9 genes, adjusted p = 8.11 × 10^-3) (Fig. 7, Supplement Table 6). These findings indicate that DCA exposure is associated with transcriptional changes in both virulence-related and metabolic pathways in Eh. Together, these results support the idea that DCA is associated with broad transcriptional remodeling in Eh, including changes in virulence-related and metabolic pathways, and that these changes may contribute to the phenotypic alterations observed in vitro, while the in vivo relevance of bile-acid exposure remains to be tested. Discussion

This study supports a central Gut Pathogens concept: the gut ecosystem can reprogram pathogen biology through microbiota-linked metabolites. By integrating shotgun metagenomics with mechanistic assays, we identify a plausible microbiome–secondary bile acid–parasite axis associated with asymptomatic Eh persistence and altered metronidazole susceptibility. Studies in endemic and non-endemic settings have reported that Entamoeba carriage can correlate with taxon-specific shifts rather than uniform “dysbiosis,” and that the direction and magnitude of these signals can vary by population, exposure, and analytical framework [35]. In our MSM screening cohort, global community structure showed limited separation, while richness was higher in cyst-positive individuals—consistent with a scenario where cyst carriage is associated with selective ecological changes rather than wholesale restructuring. This is particularly relevant in MSM cohorts, where baseline microbiome configuration can be strongly influenced by behavioral and clinical factors (e.g., sexual practices, HIV/ART, and related exposures), making careful phenotyping and covariate control essential when attributing signals to a specific pathogen [36]. Within this context, the reproducible directionality we observed across complementary analyses—enrichment of Coprococcus/Ruminococcus/Catenibacterium/Mitsuokella features with higher Eh burden and relative enrichment of multiple Bacteroides species/features toward the lower-burden spectrum—suggests that Eh cyst carriage may be linked to coordinated within-genus species shifts, which is biologically plausible given known parasite–microbiome cross-talk mediated by microbial metabolites (including short-chain fatty acids) and other luminal factors.

It was noteworthy that higher burden of Ruminococcus was observed in cyst-containing stool samples in the present study, because it was also identified as a representative commensal bacterium in asymptomatic carriers of Eh in the previous study [29]. Both studies were performed targeting Japanese population; however, samples were collected from different participants. We analyzed the bile acids’ effects on the characteristics of Eh because Ruminococcus is reportedly associated with changes in bile acids’ composition, and severity of colitis [37, 38]. Interestingly, CA and DCA showed opposite effects on its growth (Fig. 4). Particularly, stimulation with DCA gave Eh a potential durability to glucose limited conditions, raising two possibilities; one is obtaining energy from the other nutrients, because older study showed that Eh uses amino acids in glucose-deleted condition [39], the other is surviving with lower energy consumption. However, in the present study, DCA pre-incubated strain also showed durability to metronidazole treatment. Metronidazole is activated by PFOR of Eh, whose substrate, pyruvate, comes from glucose as well as amino acids. Unfortunately, cystic form of Eh was not observed during our experiments. The results suggest that Eh stimulated by DCA survive in deleted glucose conditions by saving energy consumption, which might contribute to the long survival of Eh in the gut. However, further investigations from various perspectives are needed to elucidate the pathogenesis of Eh infection.

The transcriptomic profiling in this study reveals a coordinated adaptive response of Eh to DCA, suggesting a sophisticated survival strategy within the bile acid-rich environment of the host colon. The most prominent finding was the 8.34-fold induction of P-glycoprotein-2 (P-gp-2, EHI_016380). As members of the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter family, P-glycoproteins in Eh are traditionally associated with multidrug resistance and stress adaptation [40, 41]. Given that DCA acts as a biological detergent with potential cytotoxicity, the robust upregulation of P-gp-2 likely represents a critical detoxification mechanism, enabling the parasite to efflux secondary bile acids or maintain membrane lipid homeostasis to ensure persistence [42, 43]. Furthermore, the simultaneous upregulation of genes involved in membrane remodeling and intracellular trafficking, such as fatty acid elongase (EHI_092190) and ADP-ribosylation factor 1 (ARF1, EHI_189960), further supports the hypothesis of environmental adaptation. Modifications in fatty acid chain length and enhanced vesicle trafficking may be essential to reinforce the trophozoite’s plasma membrane against the detergent-like effects of DCA [44]. The induction of Mob1 (EHI_159630) and WH2 motif protein (EHI_050810) suggests that DCA signaling might also influence cellular proliferation and cytoskeletal organization, potentially shifting the parasite’s state from aggressive tissue invasion toward stable colonization and persistence [45, 46]. The KEGG analysis provided a useful framework for interpreting the transcriptional response of Eh to DCA, highlighting enrichment of pathways associated with amoebiasis and metabolic remodeling. KEGG includes both metabolic pathways and disease/virulence-related pathways, therefore, the “amoebiasis” pathway should be interpreted as a virulence-associated functional category. In particular, the enrichment of cysteine and methionine metabolism, sulfur metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, and peroxisome-related pathways suggest that DCA exposure is accompanied by broad changes in parasite metabolism [47–49]. At the same time, because metabolic pathway annotation and functional characterization in Eh are still incomplete, these pathway assignments should be interpreted as being based on currently available knowledge. Our findings should also be considered alongside the study by Burgess et al., which showed that microbiome-mediated signaling can regulate susceptibility to amebiasis through host immune pathways [50]. In this context, DCA may affect amebiasis not only through direct effects on Eh observed in our in vitro assays, but also potentially through modulation of host immune responses. This broader view further supports the relevance of a microbiome–bile acid–parasite axis in amebiasis.

A limitation of the present study is the lack of direct functional validation, such as gene knockout or silencing (e.g., RNAi or CRISPR/Cas9), for these DEGs. While the transcriptomic data provide a compelling roadmap of the DCA-induced response, further experimental studies are required to confirm the precise contribution of each gene to the fitness and pathogenicity of Eh in vivo. As an additional limitation, BMI may have contributed to inter-individual variation in gut microbiome composition. Although BMI did not differ significantly across groups in this cohort, the relatively small sample size limits our ability to fully exclude residual confounding. A key limitation is that bile acid concentrations and exposure dynamics in vivo were not directly quantified in stool, and causal links between specific taxa, bile acids, and persistence require prospective validation. Nevertheless, our findings provide a molecular basis for the “microbiome-metabolite-parasite axis” that characterizes the asymptomatic carrier state.

Conclusion

Our integrated clinical and experimental data indicate that asymptomatic Eh cyst carriage is associated with specific gut microbial shifts and a bile acid–responsive transcriptional program in the parasite. In vitro, DCA exposure induced marked transcriptomic changes and was associated with enhanced tolerance to nutrient stress and reduced metronidazole susceptibility after pretreatment. In the context of cyst-associated microbiome signatures, these findings support the plausibility of a microbiome–bile acid–parasite axis that may contribute to persistent carriage and could influence treatment efficacy, suggesting that targeting microbiota-driven bile acid metabolism may be a rational adjunct strategy to improve luminal eradication and prevent persistent carriage.
