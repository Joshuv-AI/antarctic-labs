On the maximum number of tangencies among 1 1 -intersecting curves
Eyal Ackerman, Balázs Keszegh

Abstract
According to a conjecture of Pach, there are O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) tangent pairs among any family of n n Jordan arcs in which every pair of arcs has precisely one common point and no three arcs share a common point. This conjecture was proved for two special cases, however, for the general case the currently best upper bound is only O ⁡ ( n 7 / 4 ) O(n^{7/4}) . This is also the best known bound on the number of tangencies in the relaxed case where every pair of arcs has at most one common point. We improve the bounds for the latter and former cases to O ⁡ ( n 5 / 3 ) O(n^{5/3}) and O ⁡ ( n 3 / 2 ) O(n^{3/2}) , respectively. We also consider a few other variants of these questions, for example, we show that if the arcs are x x -monotone , each pair intersects at most once and their left endpoints lie on a common vertical line, then the maximum number of tangencies is Θ ⁡ ( n 4 / 3 ) \Theta(n^{4/3}) . Without this last condition the number of tangencies is O ⁡ ( n 4 / 3 ​ ( log ⁡ n ) 1 / 3 ) O(n^{4/3}(\log n)^{1/3}) , improving a previous bound of Pach and Sharir. Along the way we prove a graph-theoretic theorem which extends a result of Erdős and Simonovits and may be of independent interest.

Theorem 7

Notations and Tools

Recall that we assume that every two curves intersect at finitely many points and that no three curves intersect at a single point.
It follows from the former assumption that we may also assume that the curves are drawn as polygonal chains.
Indeed, given a set of curves
{\cal C}
, consider the plane graph
G
whose vertex set consists of the endpoints and intersection points of the curves in
{\cal C}
and whose edge set consists of subcurves between consecutive vertices along the curves.
By Fáry’s Theorem
[
11
]
this planar graph can be embedded using straight-line edges and the same rotation system.
Replacing every curve
c\in{\cal C}
with the polygonal chain that consists of the edges in this embedding that correspond to edges of
G
that belong to
c
, we obtain a family of curves
{\cal C}^{\prime}
such that for every intersection (resp., tangency) point of two curves in
{\cal C}
there is a unique intersection (resp., tangency) point between their corresponding curves in
{\cal C}^{\prime}
, and vice versa.

For a set of curves
{\cal C}
we denote by
G_{\cal C}
the
tangency graph
of
{\cal C}
, that is, the graph whose vertex set is
{\cal C}
and whose edge set consists of the tangent pairs of curves.
By abuse of notation we sometimes interchange curves in
{\cal C}
and their corresponding vertices in
G_{\cal C}
.

Let
c
be a curve and let
p
and
q
be two points on
c
.
We denote by
c[p,q]
the subcurve of
c
between these two points.
For another curve
c^{\prime}
that intersects
c
at a single point, we may also write, e.g.,
c[c^{\prime},q]
instead of
c[c\cap c^{\prime},q]
.
If
c
is oriented, then we denote by
c^{-}
(resp.,
c^{+}
) its starting (resp., terminating) point following its orientation.
We also write
c[-,p]
and
c[p,+]
instead of
c[c^{-},p]
and
c[p,c^{+}]
, respectively.
If
c
is an
x
-monotone curve and

is a point (not necessarily on

),
then with some abuse of notation by writing, e.g.,

, we mean

, where
\ell_{p}
is the vertical line through

As usual, a round bracket indicates that an endpoint does not belong to the subcurve.

A family of

-monotone curves induces a
generalized trapezoidal partition
of the plane in the following way. From every point

which is an endpoint of a curve or an intersection point of two curves we draw a maximal vertical segment (possibly a ray or a line) that contains

4
Tangencies among

Pach and Sharir
[
21
]
studied the following problem:
What is the maximum number of pairs of disjoint line-segments that are
vertically visible
among a set of
n
segments in the plane?
We say that two segments are
vertically visible
if there exists a vertical segment that intersects both of them and does not intersect any other segment in the set.
The motivation for this problem came from analyzing the maximum size of the events queue in the original implementation of the Bentley-Ottmann line sweeping algorithm for enumerating all the intersections among a set of segments
[
6
]
.

Pach and Sharir
[
21
]
proved that the maximum number of pairs of disjoint vertically visible line-segments within a set of
n
segments is
O\left(n^{4/3}\left(\log n\right)^{2/3}\right)
and
\Omega\left(n^{4/3}\right)
.
They also mentioned these bounds hold also for
x
-monotone
1
-intersecting curves.

It is easy to see that counting disjoint vertically visible pairs can be reduced to counting touching pairs of
x
-monotone
1
-intersecting curves and vice versa.
Indeed, vertically visible curves can become tangent by replacing a very narrow part of one curve by a ‘spike’ that touches the other curve. On the other hand, a pair of touching curves can be easily ‘detached’ and become vertically visible.
Therefore, the result of Pach and Sharir
[
21
]
implies that the maximum number of tangencies among
n

x
-monotone
1
-intersecting curves is
O\left(n^{4/3}\left(\log n\right)^{2/3}\right)
and
\Omega\left(n^{4/3}\right)
.

Improving Pach and Sharir’s upper bound.

We outline the proof of the upper bound of Pach and Sharir
[
21
]
and point out the modifications which lead to the better bounds of Theorem
4
and
5
.
First, the cutting lemma (Theorem
10
) is used to partition the plane into generalized trapezoids such that every trapezoid is cut by not too many curves.
Then, the number of tangencies within every trapezoid
T_{i}
is estimated and finally the sum over all the trapezoids is bounded.
Regarding tangencies within
T_{i}
, one considers two subsets of curves that cut
T_{i}
: ‘short’ curves that have at least one endpoint inside
T_{i}
and ‘long’ curves that have no endpoint inside
T_{i}
.
Therefore, the long curves behave like bi-infinite curves with respect to
T_{i}
.
Thus, the number of long-long tangencies can be bounded using Theorem
6
.
In order to obtain the tight upper bound of Theorem
5
we use for this case the linear bound of Lemma
2
below.
The number of short-long tangencies is bounded using Kővári-Sós-Turán Theorem and a technical lemma (see Lemma
3
below).
Finally, the number of short-short tangencies is bounded in
[
21
]
by further subdividing
T_{i}
into vertical slabs. However, we observe that one can instead use induction and this simplifies the proof and leads to a better upper bound.
For completeness, we include the detailed proofs.

.
Then,

\#K_{2,2}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{a\in A^{\prime},b\in B^{\prime},(a,b)\in E^{\prime}}|E(G^{\prime}[(N_{G^{\prime}}(a)\setminus\{b\})\cup(N_{G^{\prime}}(b)\setminus\{a\}])|\leq\frac{m}{4}f(2d)=\frac{dn}{8}f(2d).

On the other hand, denoting
s_{a,a^{\prime}}=|N_{G^{\prime}}(a)\cap N_{G^{\prime}}(a^{\prime})|
for two distinct vertices
a,a^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}
we have
\#K_{2,1}=\sum_{a\neq a^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}}s_{a,a^{\prime}}
and:

\#K_{2,2}=\sum_{a\neq a^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}}\binom{s_{a,a^{\prime}}}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a\neq a^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}}(s_{a,a^{\prime}}^{2}-s_{a,a^{\prime}})\geq\frac{(\#K_{2,1})^{2}}{2\binom{|A^{\prime}|}{2}}-\frac{\#K_{2,1}}{2}\geq\frac{(\#K_{2,1})^{2}}{2\binom{n}{2}}-\frac{\#K_{2,1}}{2},

where the second to last inequality follows from Cauchy’s inequality.
For
\#K_{2,1}
we have the following lower bound:

\#K_{2,1}=\sum_{a\neq a^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}}s_{a,a^{\prime}}=\sum_{b\in B^{\prime}}\binom{\deg(b)}{2}\geq\frac{n}{4}\binom{d/8}{2}\geq nd^{2}/2^{11}\geq n^{2},

where we used double-counting by the vertex from
B^{\prime}
for the
K_{2,1}
’s and the inequalities
|B^{\prime}|\geq n/4
and
d\geq 50n^{1/2}
. Combining the inequalities above we get:

\frac{dnf(2d)}{8}\geq\#K_{2,2}\geq\frac{(\#K_{2,1})^{2}}{n^{2}}-\frac{\#K_{2,1}}{2}\geq\frac{(\#K_{2,1})^{2}}{n^{2}}-\frac{(\#K_{2,1})^{2}}{2n^{2}}=\frac{(\#K_{2,1})^{2}}{2n^{2}}\geq\frac{n^{2}d^{4}}{2^{22}\cdot 2n^{2}}=\frac{d^{4}}{2^{23}}.

This implies that
d^{3}\leq 2^{20}nf(2d)
, as claimed.
∎

Using Theorem
17
we can now prove Theorem
8
which we restate for convenience.
See
8

Proof.

Recall that every graph contains a bipartite subgraph whose size is at least half of the size of the graph. Let
G^{\prime}=(A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be such a bipartite subgraph of
G
, where
|E^{\prime}|\geq|E(G)|/2
, and let
n=|V(G)|
and
d^{\prime}=\overline{d}(G^{\prime})
.
If every pair of vertices in
G
has
f
-sparse sub-bineighborhoods, then the same holds for
G^{\prime}
and it follows from Theorem
17
that
d^{\prime 3}\leq 2^{11}nf(2d^{\prime})=O(nd^{\prime 2-\alpha})
.
Therefore
d^{\prime}\leq O(n^{1/(\alpha+1)})
and the upper bound follows.

If
0<\alpha\leq 1
and every pair of adjacent vertices has
f
-sparse sub-bineighborhoods, then we may assume that
|E^{\prime}|\geq 25n^{3/2}
for otherwise the theorem trivially holds.
Therefore, by Theorem
17
we have
d^{\prime 3}\leq 2^{20}nf(2d^{\prime})=O(nd^{\prime 2-\alpha})
and
d^{\prime}\leq O(n^{1/(\alpha+1)})
as before.
∎

Next we prove Theorem
9
by which the upper bound of Theorem
8
is asymptotically tight.
For convenience we restate the theorem.
See
9

Proof.

The case
\alpha=0
is trivial.
For
\alpha=1
we can take a
K_{2,2}
-free graph with
\Theta(n^{3/2})
edges
[
17
]
. Indeed, in such a graph any bipartite subgraph induced by two sub-bineighborhoods contains no vertex of degree at least two.
This also shows why for
\alpha>1
we require that every pair of vertices satisfies the
f
-sparse sub-bineighborhoods property and not just adjacent pairs. Indeed, for adjacent pairs the sub-bineighborhoods contain no edges at all in a
K_{2,2}
-free graph, so the assumption holds with arbitrary
\alpha<2
for such neighborhoods, yet the number of edges is
\Theta(n^{3/2})
.

Next we show the lower bound for
0<\alpha<1
.
For convenience fix
c=2-\alpha>1
.
Thus, we wish to construct a graph with
\Omega\left(n^{\frac{4-c}{3-c}}\right)
edges in which every pair of vertices has
f
-sparse sub-bineighborhoods for
f(x)=O(x^{c})
.
Specifically, we construct and analyze a standard random bipartite graph
G=(A\cup B,E)
, where
|A|=|B|=n
and every pair of vertices
a\in A
and
b\in B
are connected by an edge independently with probability
p=n^{-\frac{2-c}{3-c}}
.

Let
E_{A^{\prime},B^{\prime}}:=E(G[A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}])
for every
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
and
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
.
We say that a
4
-tuple
a\in A,b\in B,A^{\prime}\subseteq A
and
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
is ‘bad’ if

B^{\prime}\subseteq N(a)\setminus\{b\}
,
A^{\prime}\subseteq N(b)\setminus\{a\}
and
|E_{A^{\prime},B^{\prime}}|>q(|A^{\prime}|+|B^{\prime}|)^{c}
, where
q>0
is a large enough constant that will be determined later.
Such a fixed
4
-tuple is bad with probability
p^{|A^{\prime}|}p^{|B^{\prime}|}\cdot\Pr(|E_{A^{\prime},B^{\prime}}|>q(|A^{\prime}|+|B^{\prime}|)^{c}
.
Let
X
be a random variable that is equal to the number of bad
4
-tuples.
Then,

\mathbb{E}(X)=n^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}{{n-1}\choose{i}}p^{i}{{n-1}\choose{j}}p^{j}\cdot\underset{Z\sim\textrm{Bin}(ij,p)}{\Pr}\left(Z>q(i+j)^{c}\right),

where
Z
is a binomially distributed random variable counting the number of ‘successes’ among
i\cdot j
independent experiments each of success probability
p
.
It is enough to show that
\mathbb{E}(X)\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, since by Markov’s inequality
\Pr(X\geq 1)\leq\mathbb{E}(X)
, which means that
\mathbb{E}(X)\rightarrow 0
implies that there are no bad
4
-tuples with high probability.
Furthermore, it follows from Chernoff bound that with high probability
|E|=\Theta(\mathbb{E}(E))=\Theta(pn^{2})=\Theta(n^{(4-c)/(3-c)})
, therefore, with high probability there are
\Theta(n^{(4-c)/(3-c)})
edges and no bad
4
-tuples.

Let

T_{i,j}:=\binom{n}{i}p^{i}\binom{n}{j}p^{j}\cdot\underset{Z_{i,j}\sim\textrm{Bin}(ij,p)}{\Pr}(Z_{i,j}>q(i+j)^{c}).

Since
\mathbb{E}(X)\leq n^{4}\cdot\underset{i,j}{\max}\{T_{i,j}\}
, it is enough to show that the maximum value of
T_{i,j}
tends to zero faster than
n^{-4}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
Note that
\underset{i,j}{\max}\{T_{i,j}\}
is obtained when
i
and
j
are equal or almost equal in case
i+j
is odd.
Indeed, suppose that the sum
s=i+j
is fixed.
Then clearly
\underset{Z_{i,j}\sim\textrm{Bin}(ij,p)}{\Pr}(Z_{i,j}>q(i+j)^{c})
is maximized when
ij
is maximized and this happens for
i=\lfloor s/2\rfloor
and
j=\lceil s/2\rceil
(or the other way around).
Likewise, it is not hard to show that the product of the binomial coefficients
\binom{n}{i}\binom{n}{j}=\binom{n}{i}\binom{n}{s-i}
is also maximized for
i=\lfloor s/2\rfloor
and
j=\lceil s/2\rceil
, see
[
27
]
.
Thus, we will consider
\max_{i}\{T_{i,i},T_{i,i+1}\}

and split the analysis into three cases.

Let
A_{i,j}:=\binom{n}{i}p^{i}\binom{n}{j}p^{j}
be the first term of
T_{i,j}
and let
B_{i,j}:=\underset{Z_{i,j}\sim\textrm{Bin}(ij,p)}{\Pr}(Z_{i,j}>q(i+j)^{c})
be its second term.
Note that
\frac{A_{i,i+1}}{A_{i,i}}=p\frac{n-i}{i+1}\leq n
. Using the inequality
\binom{n}{i}\leq(ne/i)^{i}
we get that

A_{i,i}=\left(\binom{n}{i}p^{i}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\frac{e\cdot n^{1/(3-c)}}{i}\right)^{2i}\textrm{ and thus }A_{i,i+1}\leq n\left(\frac{e\cdot n^{1/(3-c)}}{i}\right)^{2i}.

(8)

Case 1:
i>10e\cdot n^{1/(3-c)}
.

It follows from Eq. (
8
) that
T_{i,i},T_{i,i+1}<n0.1^{2i}\leq n0.1^{20en^{1/(3-c)}}
. As
n\rightarrow\infty
this expression decays to zero much faster than
n^{-4}
and that concludes this case.

Case 2:
i\leq 10e\cdot n^{\frac{1}{3-c}}
.

Let
\mu_{i,j}=\mathbb{E}(Z_{i,j})=ijp
and let
t_{i,j}=q(i+j)^{c}
.
Note that
2qi^{c}\leq t_{i,i},t_{i,i+1}\leq 9qi^{c}
,
\mu_{i,i}=i^{2}p
and
i^{2}p\leq\mu_{i,i+1}\leq 2i^{2}p
.
Recall that by Chernoff bound for any
\delta\geq 0
we have

\Pr(Z_{i,j}>(1+\delta)\mu_{i,j})\leq\left(\frac{e^{\delta}}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}\right)^{\mu_{i,j}}=e^{\mu_{i,j}(\delta-(1+\delta)\ln(1+\delta))}.

Thus,

\ln B_{i,j}\leq\mu_{i,j}(\delta-(1+\delta)\ln(1+\delta)).

Note that if we choose
q\geq(10e)^{2-c}
, then
\frac{t_{i,i}}{\mu_{i,i}},\frac{t_{i,i+1}}{\mu_{i,i+1}}\geq\frac{q}{p}i^{c-2}\geq 1
.
Substituting
\delta=\frac{t_{i,j}}{\mu_{i,j}}-1\geq 0
for
j=i,i+1
we get:

\displaystyle\ln B_{i,j}

\displaystyle\leq t_{i,j}-\mu_{i,j}-t_{i,j}(\ln t_{i,j}-\ln\mu_{i,j})=t_{i,j}(1-\ln t_{i,j}+\ln\mu_{i,j})-\mu_{i,j}

\displaystyle\leq 9qi^{c}\left(1-\ln(2q)-c\ln i+\ln 2+2\ln i+\ln p\right)

\displaystyle\leq 9qi^{c}\left(2-\ln(2q)+(2-c)\ln i-\frac{2-c}{3-c}\ln n\right).

Next we split the current case into two subcases and show that
\ln T_{i,i}
and
\ln T_{i,i+1}

tend to
-\infty
fast enough in both of them.

Subcase 2a:
n^{\frac{1}{2(3-c)}}\leq i\leq 10e\cdot n^{\frac{1}{3-c}}
.

Considering the natural logarithm of
T_{i,j}
for
j=i,i+1
we have:

\displaystyle\ln T_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\ln A_{i,j}+\ln B_{i,j}

\displaystyle\leq\ln n+2i\left(1+\frac{\ln n}{3-c}-\ln i\right)+9qi^{c}\left(2-\ln(2q)+(2-c)\ln\left(10e\cdot n^{\frac{1}{3-c}}\right)-\frac{2-c}{3-c}\ln n\right)

\displaystyle=\ln n+2i\left(1+\frac{\ln n}{3-c}-\ln i\right)+9qi^{c}\left(2-\ln(2q)+(2-c)\ln(10e)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 3i\ln n+9qi^{c}(9-\ln(2q)).

Therefore, if we take
q>0.5e^{9}
(which we do), then since
i\ln n\ll i^{c}
when
c>1
and
i\geq n^{\frac{1}{2(3-c)}}
, we conclude that
T_{i,i}
and
T_{i,i+1}
decay to zero super-polynomially in this case.

Subcase 2b:
i<n^{\frac{1}{2(3-c)}}
.

Recall that for
j=i,i+1
we have:

\displaystyle\ln T_{i,j}

\displaystyle\leq\ln n+2i\left(1+\frac{\ln n}{3-c}-\ln i\right)+9qi^{c}\left(2-\ln(2q)+(2-c)\ln i-\frac{2-c}{3-c}\ln n\right)

\displaystyle\leq\ln n+2i\left(1+\frac{\ln n}{3-c}-\ln i\right)+9qi^{c}\left(2-\ln(2q)+\frac{2-c}{2(3-c)}\ln n-\frac{2-c}{3-c}\ln n\right)

\displaystyle=\ln n+2i\left(1+\frac{\ln n}{3-c}-\ln i\right)+9qi^{c}\left(2-\ln(2q)-\frac{\ln n}{2(3-c)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{2i}{3-c}-\frac{9qi^{c}}{2(3-c)}\right)\ln n-2i(\ln i-1)-9qi^{c}(\ln(2q)-2)

\displaystyle<\frac{2(3-c)+4i-9qi^{c}}{2(3-c)}\ln n<\frac{-qi^{c}}{2(3-c)}\ln n<-5\ln n,

where the last two inequalities hold for large enough
q
.
Therefore,
n^{4}T_{i,i}
and
n^{4}T_{i,i+1}
tend to zero also in this case.
Considering the conditions on
q
along the proof, it is enough to take, say,
q=5000
.
∎

Remarks:

•

As mentioned in the Introduction, Theorem
8
implies the theorem of Erdős and Simonovits (Theorem
7
) which in turn implies the Kővári-Sós-Turán Theorem.
Considering the differences between Theorem
7
and Theorem
8
, firstly, the former can be used only for sub-bineighborhoods which induce a sparse subgraph due to avoiding a certain subgraph, whereas in the latter we just assume sparseness. Secondly, Theorem
8
can be used for very sparse induced subgraphs (with sub-linearly many edges when
\alpha>1
) as long as the condition in the theorem holds for every pair of vertices (rather than for adjacent ones).
And thirdly, for Theorem
8
we have a matching lower bound for every
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
(Theorem
9
), while for Theorem
7
a matching lower bound is only known for
\alpha=1,\frac{1}{2}
, and any tight bound for some
\alpha=\frac{1}{t}
,
t>2
, would be a remarkable result.

•

The proof of Theorem
8
is quite similar to the proof of Theorem
7
in
[
9
]
.
In both cases, one first makes the graph close to being regular and then uses double-counting, however, in the case of Theorem
7
the first part requires more effort. Chronologically, we first proved the first part of Theorem
8
which was needed for the proof of Theorem
2
, only later did we learn about Theorem
7
and used it to prove the second part of Theorem
8
.

•

It would be interesting to determine whether Theorem
8
holds when one assumes sparsity of the subgraph induced by the bineighborhood of every pair of (adjacent) vertices instead of assuming sparsity of subgraphs induced by each of their sub-bineighborhoods.
Note that the first part of the proof of Theorem
8
does not go through in such a case.
Clearly,
f
-sparse bineighborhoods do not imply
f
-sparse sub-bineighborhoods.
For example, recall that
(n^{3/2})/4\leq\mbox{ex}(n,K_{2,2})\leq n^{3/2}
for sufficiently large
n

[
17
]
and consider a
K_{2,2}
-free bipartite graph
G
with
n
vertices on each side and
\Theta(n^{3/2})
edges.
Assume further that the degree of every vertex in
G
is at least
c\sqrt{n}
and at most
C\sqrt{n}
, for some constants
0<c<C
.
Note that every pair of vertices in
G
has an
f
-sparse bineighborhood for
f(x)=x/2
.
Now add
c^{\prime}=20
new vertices to each side and connect them to all the vertices on the other side.
Then for
g(x)=x^{1.6}
and large enough
n
the following holds.
For two original vertices, their bineighborhood contains at least
2c^{\prime}+2c\sqrt{n}-2\geq 2c\sqrt{n}
vertices which induce a subgraph with at most
C\sqrt{n}+2c^{\prime}C\sqrt{n}+c^{\prime 2}\leq g(2c\sqrt{n})
edges;
the bineighborhood of one original vertex and one new vertex consists of at least
n+2c^{\prime}+c\sqrt{n}-2\geq n
vertices and it induces a subgraph with at most
(n+C\sqrt{n})^{3/2}+c^{\prime}n+c^{\prime 2}+c^{\prime}C\sqrt{n}\leq g(n)
edges;
and the bineighborhood of two new vertices consists of at least
2n+2c^{\prime}-2\geq 2n
vertices and it induces a subgraph with at most
(2n)^{3/2}+2c^{\prime}n+c^{\prime 2}\leq g(2n)
edges.
Thus, in the resulting graph every pair of vertices has a
g
-sparse bineighborhood.
However, the subgraph induced by the new vertices is a sub-bineighborhood of any two original vertices from different sides and this subgraph has
2c^{\prime}
vertices and
c^{\prime 2}=400
edges which is more than
g(2c^{\prime})
.

•

Theorem
8
might find other applications, e.g., in other scenarios where it can be combined with Theorem
12
.
Here is one example. For a graph
H
let
\mbox{ex}_{\textrm{cr}}(n,H)
be the maximum number of edges in a topological graph on
n
vertices with no self-intersecting copy
H
. Note that
\mbox{ex}(n,H)\leq\mbox{ex}_{\textrm{cr}}(n,H)
and if
H
is non-planar then
\mbox{ex}(n,H)=\mbox{ex}_{\textrm{cr}}(n,H)
.
In
[
15
]
Theorem
12
is used to show that
\mbox{ex}_{\textrm{cr}}(n,C_{4})=O(n^{3/2})
(and this is tight). Using Theorem
8
this immediately implies:

Corollary 5
.

\mbox{ex}_{\textrm{cr}}(K_{2,3})=O(n^{5/3})
and
\mbox{ex}_{\textrm{cr}}(n,K_{3,3}^{-})=O(n^{5/3})
where
K_{3,3}^{-}
is the complete bipartite graph on
3+3
vertices minus one edge.
