Executive Summary
The project objective was to develop high-capacity structured sorbent capable of achieving low CO2
removal from air. The approach was divided into 2 primary activities:

 1. Develop a direct air capture (DAC) sorbent that achieves the following key attributes:
 a. High CO2 loading capacity (>5 wt% CO2) – lower operating cost for sorbent regeneration
 and capital cost by reducing the size of the DAC contactor/number of DAC module
 b. High Stability (<5% capacity fade over 100 adsorption-regeneration cycles) – lower
 operating costs for sorbent make-up
 c. High CO2 adsorption rate (>0.03 gmolCO2/gSorbent-min) – lower capital cost by reducing
 DAC contactor size/number of DAC modules
 2. Develop and demonstrate a structured sorbent that achieves the following key attributes:
 a. Low cost of structured sorbent manufacturing
 b. High DAC sorbent loading capacity on structured substrate – lower operating cost for
 sorbent regeneration and lower capital cost by reducing size of DAC contactor/number of
 modules
 c. Capable of direct Joule-heated sorbent regeneration – reduce operating costs through
 efficient use of available electricity
 d. Utilize existing manufacturing infrastructure for rapid scale-up

The DAC sorbent framework consists of an amine-functionalized onto hydrophobic polymer backbone with
an added promoter. The functionalized amine provides high CO2 capacity and adsorption rates and the
polymer backbone to reduce water uptake. For the DAC sorbent development, multiple functionalized
amines and promoters were assessed to select a candidate that achieved high CO2 capacity, high adsorption
rate, and high stability, as per the targets listed above. A key focus of the DAC sorbent screening was to
determine a candidate that achieves high oxidative stability, as the primary mechanism for the degradation
of amine-based sorbents is the oxidation of the functionalized amine when transitioning from high
temperature regeneration to CO2 adsorption from air. From this work, a DAC sorbent, named ECCOsorb-
T1, was identified and used for the structured sorbent development.

For the structured sorbent development, a sorbent-coated filter design was selected as the structured sorbent,
which consists of:
 1. Non-woven, electrically conductive substrate capable for direct joule heating
 2. ECCOsorb-T1 DAC sorbent coated onto the electrically conductive nonwoven
 3. Nonconductive nonwoven protective layer coated on top of the sorbent

The sorbent coated onto the nonwoven filter provides high sorbent loading capacity. The electrically
conductive nonwoven filter substrate can be Joule-heated to provide efficient utilization of available
electricity for sorbent regeneration. A joint development agreement with a commercial filter manufacturing
partner, a global leader in air filtration technology, was established to manufacture the structured sorbent.
A key objective of the joint development effort was to determine if the existing filter manufacturing
infrastructure can be utilized to scale Susteon’s structured sorbent filter concept, and that the manufactured
structured sorbent filters can be used for DAC application. The commercial partner operated a pilot filter
manufacturing line to produce 12 pleated filter panels coated with ECCOsorb-T1 and 10 flat filter laminate
sheets for testing in Susteon’s bench-scale DAC unit. As part of this effort, Susteon scaled its sorbent
synthesis process by 100-fold to produce over 50 kg of ECCOsorb-T1, which was the minimum sorbent
quantity needed to operate the pilot filter manufacturing line.

To demonstrate the filter concept as the structured sorbent, a 1 kg CO2/day bench unit was designed and
fabricated. The bench-scale DAC unit included a DAC contactor capable of housing a full-size pleated filter

A techno-economic assessment with sensitivity analysis was conducted on a 100,000 TPY facility with
85% operating capacity. Through sorbent optimization and process design improvements, it is estimated
that the cost of capture was reduced from $349/tCO2 to $241/tCO2. The TEA projects further reductions to
$165/tCO2 through enhancements in CO2 adsorption rate and sorbent capacity and reducing manufacturing
and scale up risks. A life cycle analysis was conducted on the same 100,000 TPY facility and confirmed
that the facility’s electricity demand drives its greenhouse gas impact. It was determined that electricity
supplied through the current grid mix would result in net-positive CO2 emissions and that achieving net-
negative emissions is only possible by powering the DAC system with renewable electricity or fossil fuel
sources equipped with carbon capture and sequestration.

Initial Techno-economic Analysis

As outlined in earlier sections, the project goal was to reduce the cost of CO2 capture. An initial techno-
economic analysis (TEA) was performed at the commencement of the project for a 100,000-tonne-per-year
(100TPY) facility. The cost of capture was estimated at $335/tonne (excluding transportation and storage).
Key learnings from the initial TEA guided this project. The sorbent performance and the structured sorbent
assembly design were found to have the greatest impact on the cost of CO2 capture. For example, increasing
the sorbent capacity and CO2 adsorption rate reduces the CAPEX contribution to the cost of CO2 capture.
Energy requirements could be reduced by minimizing co-adsorption of water on the sorbent and reducing
the regeneration temperature, which narrows the temperature range of the thermal swing process. Finally,
improving the long-term stability of the sorbent would reduce the sorbent replacement cost, ultimately
reducing the OPEX. These sorbent advancements were found to be most crucial to achieving project goals.
The results of the initial TEA were used to guide the sorbent screening and structured sorbent design
development.

Initial Life-cycle Analysis

Based on the initial TEA, an initial life-cycle analysis (LCA) was also completed for a 100,000 TPY facility.
The LCA revealed that, given the aim of using electrical power for facilitating the thermal regeneration
step, the electricity source was the major GHG emissions driver for the overall process. A comparative
LCA revealed the significance of the grid mix on the process emissions, and renewables of fossil with
carbon capture and storage (CCS) yielded a meaningful negative GHG footprint for the 100,000 TPY
facility modeled therein (Table 3).

Task 2 – Structured Sorbent Material Optimization

Task 2 of this project consisted of sorbent optimization, sorbent synthesis scale-up, and the production of a
structured sorbent to be tested in a bench-scale unit. From the initial TEA, the key sorbent performance that
drive down the cost of CO2 removal are low water adsorption, high cyclic stability, and fast kinetics.
Reduction of the non-competitive co-adsorption of water ultimately reduces the parasitic energy losses in
the regeneration step. Furthermore, sorbent stability is important in reducing structured sorbent replacement
frequency and plant downtime. Rapid kinetics reduce the overall cycle duration and can improve CO2
productivity of a DAC facility.

Susteon’s sorbent design framework, outlined in Figure 1, was comprised of a hydrophobic polymer
backbone to minimize water adsorption by the bulk sorbent, a highly stable proprietary amine, and a
promoter to enhance CO2 adsorption rate. It is well understood that amines rapidly degrade at relevant
process conditions; thus, current research explores amine modification to improve sorbent performance.
Susteon’s approach introduces an additional functional group via a promoter to the parent amine-
functionalized backbone to provide stability while maintaining favorable adsorption kinetics. During this
Task, sorbent optimization focused on screening functional amines and promoters to achieve high CO2
adsorption rates and capacity as well as high oxidative stability, as oxidation is the most common
mechanism of degradation for amine-based sorbents.

High-Capacity Sorbent Development

Susteon developed and used two in-house fixed bed reactors for sorbent screening and sorbent performance
measurements. Figure 2 depicts a general configuration of the screening reactors. These systems can
simulate a wide range of DAC operating conditions. A blended air cylinders to provide a range of CO2 feed
concentrations. An impinger mixed with dry gas feed provides humidified air feed conditions from 0% to
100% relative humidity. The sorbent sample can be regenerated using external heating jackets or directly
using Joule-heating. An electric cooling jacket is also placed around the reactor to simulate sub-ambient
operating temperatures. The reactor cell is interchangeable to test a range for sorbent form factors. For
sorbent screening under normal operating conditions, the material is cycled between adsorption, purge, and
desorption steps by adjusting the flow of gas and the sample temperature. Downstream, the gas is cooled
to 2°C, allowing excess water vapor to condense before the gas flows into a non-dispersive infrared (NDIR)
gas analyzer that continuously measures ppm-levels of CO2 in the reactor exhaust throughout the
experiment.

After screening over 40 sorbent compositions, four candidates were downselected for extended stability
testing. The stability of each sorbent was evaluated by rapid aging, which was performed in the screening
reactor. Typically, 0.5 g of sample was analyzed using real air and rapid adsorption/desorption cycles.
Adsorption was performed under a flow of dried, real ambient air humidified to >90% RH for 15 minutes
at a sample temperature of 28°C. This resulted in uptake of CO2 to partial capture capacity. Then, under the
same air flow, the sample was heated to the target desorption temperature and held for 20 minutes to allow
for CO2 desorption. The sample was allowed to cool in humidified N2 to the target adsorption temperature
before the cycle was repeated. In the case of ECCOsorb-E2, the sample was aged rapidly in a drying oven
(stagnant air) at 100°C, which is estimated to be equivalent to about 370 fast cycles. At the completion of
the aging experiment, each sample was tested again under the standard conditions. Fresh ECCOsorb-1
showed the highest adsorption capacity of 1.82 mmol CO2/g sorbent, followed by ECCOsorb-T1,
ECCOsorb-E1, and ECCOsorb-E2. Among the candidate samples, ECCOSorb-T1 demonstrated the highest
capacity retention, particularly after enduring a more rigorous aging test.

Structured Sorbent Concept, Validation, and Manufacturing

As summarized in the initial TEA, to reduce the cost of CO2 removal, the structured sorbent assembly must
achieve a low cost of manufacturing, high sorbent loading capacity, low pressure drop with high air
throughput, capable of direct joule heated regeneration, and can utilize existing manufacturing
infrastructures. With this goal, Susteon developed a coated filter concept as the structured sorbent. The
coated filter consists of the sorbent sandwiched between two filter layers, where one layer is electrically
conductive and can be Joule heated. The sorbent coating onto the surface of the nonwoven filter substrate
allows for high sorbent loading, up to 90% by weight, compared to <50wt% in monolith-based substrates.
This sandwiched structured sorbent can then be pleated and framed, providing more surface area along a 3-
dimensional plane. Once framed, the pleated filter panel can be arranged in a V-shape to represent one
structured sorbent module.

Susteon initiated a joint development agreement with a commercial partner, a global leader in air filtration,
to assess the feasibility of manufacturing the structured sorbent filters at an industrial scale using the
commercial partner’s existing filter manufacturing infrastructure. Specifically, the commercial partner
agreed to manufacture the structured sorbent filters using an existing pilot manufacturing line. The
manufactured structured sorbent filters were then tested in Susteon’s bench-scale DAC system as discussed
below.

To prepare the filter panels, the following activities were completed:
 1. Select and verify the sustained performance of the electrically conductive nonwoven substrate for
 using in the structured sorbent filter
 2. Manufacture samples of sorbent coated filters to test their performance in terms of sustained Joule
 heating cycles and CO2 adsorption/desorption capability
 3. Operate the pilot manufacturing line to produce full size pleated filter panels and laminates

Electrically Conductive Nonwoven

An 8” x 5” conductive filter sample was placed on a stand with electrodes connected along the length of
the filter sample. A copper film was adhered to each end using silver paste, which provides electric current
distribution along the width of the filter sample. The power modulation was controlled using a silicone-
controlled rectifier (SCR) connected to a variable voltage transformer, and the temperature was measured
using an IR sensor. The temperature and resistance were measured throughout the experiment, where
resistance measurements were conducted at the end of the measurement cycle. Prior to resistance
measurement, the sample was allowed to cool to room temperature to avoid variability due to temperature.
One hundred joule-heating cycles were performed showing stable resistance throughout all 100 cycles.

Coated HCSS testing

As part of an initial assessment, activity 2, an 8”x11” sample of the electrically conductive nonwoven
substrate and was coated with Susteon’s sorbent and tested. The heating stability of the sorbent coated filter
substrate was tested using the same apparatus and operating conditions as described above. A total of 500
Joule-heated thermal cycles were completed, showing no change in the heating performance. These results
further verify that electrically resistive filter substrate can be used for the DAC application. The sorbent
coated filter was then tested to confirm that the sorbent could be regenerated by resistively heating the
electrically conductive nonwoven. A sample of the sorbent coated nonwoven was placed into a reactor cell
with each end of the sample connected to an electrode. A SCR was used to modulate the voltage applied
along the electrodes, and the surface temperature of the filter was used as temperature feedback control.
The sample was tested using simulated DAC conditions from the lab screening reactor. The results (Figure

sequence using the sorbent-coated filter. The resistance of the sample was calculated at 70°C based on the
steady-state voltage applied to the sample and steady-state current measured during the isothermal hold.
The resistance oscillates around an average of 2.2 Ω throughout the ten cycles performed, demonstrating
stability of the electrically conductive filter (Figure 6, right).

Sorbent Manufacturing Scale-Up

After validation of the electrically conductive nonwoven substrate and the performance of the sorbent
coated filter samples, the commercial partner commenced preparation of the pilot-scale filter manufacturing
line to coat ECCOsorb-T1 onto the selected filter substrates. As part of the preparation process, the
commercial partner required a minimum of 50 kg of ECCOsorb-T1 to operate the pilot manufacturing line.
Susteon scaled its sorbent synthesis procedure from 25 g batches of sorbent to 2.5 kg batches, producing
20 batches to meet the minimum amount needed. The section discusses the modifications to the synthesis
procedure made to scale the process safely and cost effectively and the QA/QC performed to verify batch
to batch consistency.

Initially, the lab-scale synthetic procedure was modified to minimize the use of costly and hazardous
organic solvents. An organic solvent was used in each reaction step, including washing and
functionalization, of the original sorbent synthesis, which can be cost prohibitive for commercial-scale
manufacturing. Alternative ECCOsorb-T1 synthetic procedures were developed that substituted some or all
the organic solvent with water. The impact of solvent was evaluated by analyzing the equilibrium capacities
of ECCOsorb-T1 synthesized and washed with organic solvent, ECCOsorb-T1 synthesized in organic
solvent and washed with water, and ECCOsorb-1 synthesized and washed with water. For further scale-up,
water was used as the solvent for all reaction steps.

Additional equipment for large scale sorbent synthesis was procured, including mechanical mixers, pumps,
containers, and drying equipment. Each batch was evaluated for quality assurance and control to

Task 3 – Bench-Scale System Design and Fabrication
The 1 kg CO2/day bench DAC unit, originally constructed as part of DE-FE0032118, was modified and
updated to accommodate the structured sorbent filter testing as part of the current project work. A new
DAC enclosure, shown in Figure 10, was fabricated to house sorbent coated filter panels with sufficient gas
seals and electrical feedthroughs to Joule heat the panel for sorbent regeneration. The new enclosure can
accommodate up to three pleated filter panels arranged in series. The machined aluminum frames have
pass-throughs on the sides to allow for electrical connections to the electrified filter module. The aluminum
frames can move along metal tracks, allowing multiple frames to be inserted. Once the filter modules are
in place, the entire system is compressed and sealed against silicone gaskets to achieve a gas tight seal. The
gas flow path is normal to the enclosure and filter modules. A gas distributor is placed upstream of filter
panels as the first enclosure frame in contact with the air feed.

Task 4 – Structured Sorbent Testing
Bench scale testing consisted of three primary test campaigns. Initially, optimal operating conditions were
established through parametric testing of a single pleated filter, which was followed by conducting long
term testing with a fully assembled DAC enclosure with 3 plated filter panels. The final test campaign was
conducted with a modified structured filter assembly to improve temperature uniformity and sorbent
stability.

An alternative layered/laminate filter design was evaluated to address the limitations of the pleated filter
design. Layering flat filters offers thermal insulation during the desorption period, electrical insulation
between heating layers, and thermal mass in the axial direction to absorb minor heating nonuniformities
due to various defects that can occur in the manufacturing of the electrically conductive substrate or in the
sorbent coating process. To test this approach, five flat sorbent coated filters were assembled in the bench
unit for testing. The ends of five flat filter sheets were coated with conductive paint, and a copper mesh
electrode was installed along the ends for electrical continuity. Thermocouples were placed between the
central layers for temperature measurement and feedback control.

Cycle adsorption and desorption were conducted with the layered filter design. For the cycles, desorption
temperature and duration were reduced to 75oC and by 50%, respectively. Under these conditions, the
material maintained similar working capacity as the pleated filter tests, indicating an improvement in the
heating uniformity. The CO2 adsorption and desorption are shown in Figure 19, which shows significant
improvement in the sorbent stability compared to the pleated filter configuration testing. However, some
sorbent degradation still occurred.

Key Learnings

The bench unit testing demonstrated the proof-of-concept design of the structured sorbent filters for DAC
application with Joule-heated regeneration. Despite optimizing sorbent performance using the laminated
configuration, two issues remained: (1) sorbent CO2 working capacity was lower than the sorbent granules;
and (2) cyclic CO2 capacity fade was still evident. A sample of the as-received sorbent coated filter was
tested in the lab screening reactor. The CO2 capacity over the course of five adsorption/desorption cycles
is shown in Figure 20. The average CO2 capacity over the five cycles is 0.99 mmol/g, which is a 26%
reduction in the CO2 capacity of the sorbent compared to the synthesized, uncoated ECCOSorb-T1 granules.
This suggests that the adhesive coating used to bind the sorbent to the filter substrate could block sorbent
sites and reduce overall capacity of the material. This is further supported by surface area analysis, which
revealed a 30% reduction in surface area of the coated sample. Based on discussions with our commercial
partner, it was concluded that the binder used is likely also causing cyclic degradation in the sorbent
performance. Future work with commercial partner will focus on optimizing the binder amount and type to
minimize sorbent performance loss.

Task 5 – Techno-economic Analysis (TEA)
To determine this technology’s economic viability at commercial scale, a DAC facility employing
Susteon’s structured sorbent filter at a nominal CO2 capture rate of 100,000 TPY and a capacity factor of
85% was studied. The detailed techno-economic analysis (TEA) was provided to DOE for review and is
provided as an addendum to this final report. As part of the TEA, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to
determine the key parameters that influence the cost of CO2 removal. The results of this assessment showed
that the cost of CO2 capture $241/tCO2, an improvement over the monolith-based structured sorbent used
in the FE0032243/FE0032118, which was $349/tCO2. A combination of key performance improvements,
notably CO2 adsorption rate, sorbent capacity, manufacturing, and risk-reduction methods, can further
reduce the cost of CO2 capture from $241/tCO2 to $165/tCO2.

hourly space velocity, and sorbent loading. The model incorporates the basic cycle design consisting of (1)
adsorption; (2) evacuation; (3) and desorption as well as the pleated filter modules consisting of eight
pleated filters panels that are identical to the one tested on the bench-scale unit. The model depicted in

Technology Maturation Plan

Through the bench scale testing of the structured sorbent filter, TRL 4 was successfully demonstrated for
this technology. Further maturation of this technology requires improvements in the structured sorbent
manufacturing that can be tested in larger scale demonstrations. This would be achieved through identifying
manufacturing features that limited sorbent performance and stability, and implementing manufacturing
improvements to address these issues. In parallel, continued sorbent composition screening will be
conducted using Susteon’s in-house synthesis and screening expertise to identify sorbent candidates that
improve on the performance of ECCOsorb-T1, which can further reduce the cost of capture in accordance
with TEA findings. Successful iterations will be scaled up and integrated into the structured sorbent filte
and tested in Susteon’s 1kg/day bench-scale system. Real-world performance data on the combined sorbent
and filter design will drive further refinement and establish optimal operating conditions for the next scale
of demonstration (15 kg/day). In concert with these efforts, Susteon will proactively engage with
commercial partners and potential customers to assess the feasibility of scaling this DAC technology and
derisk technical and commercial risks with the ultimate goal of deploying a 1TPD system.

Market research and commercialization efforts undertaken during this project revealed several niche
applications for this technology that could serve markets outside the carbon dioxide removal industry. The
project team identified alternative modes of operation that yielded a low-purity stream of product gas (i.e.,
up to 20% CO2) that would be suited to a variety of process integrations, such as carbon mineralization and
artificial CO2 enrichment of greenhouses. In operating for low-purity CO2 production, the system would no
longer require energy intensive process units, such as vacuum pumps, or expensive mechanical designs for
sealing the DAC modules under vacuum for achieving high purity. This configuration offers niche pathways
to market entry that can complement ongoing material and process development efforts.

Conclusions

The project team successfully developed a high-capacity DAC sorbent. The sorbent optimization work
involved synthetic modification of the amine sites and added promoters to achieve high CO2 working
capacity and cyclic oxidative stability. The optimized sorbent, named ECCOsorb-T1, was integrated with
an electrically conductive nonwoven filter substrate as the structured sorbent assembly. A commercial
partner demonstrated the feasibility of manufacturing the structured sorbent filters using existing filter
manufacturing infrastructure, reducing the risk and cost of further scale-up of the technology. The
manufactured structured sorbent filters were tested in various configurations to establish optimal operating
conditions and identify areas of improvement.

Key achievements of this project include:
 • Development of ECCOsorb-T1, a high capacity, stable DAC sorbent that uses mild regeneration
 temperature (70°C) and can achieve >5 wt% CO2 capture in less than 30 minutes
 • Production of 50 kg of sorbent, including in-house quality assurance testing, for subsequent
 manufacturing of coated filter
 • Validation that the structured sorbent filter manufacturing can be conducted using the existing
 industrial filter manufacturing infrastructure.
 • Design and fabrication of 1 kg CO2/day bench-scale DAC unit to house and test the structured
 sorbent filters
 • Estimated CO2 capture cost reduction from $349/tCO2 to $241/tCO2
 • Identification of cost reduction pathway to reach $165/tCO2.
 • Cradle-to-gate life cycle assessment confirming carbon negative performance with renewable
 electricity sources

The project team identified key challenges to address in continuation of commercially relevant technology
development. Further collaboration with a filter manufacturer is necessary to mitigate the negative impacts
of the sorbent adhesive and optimize the sorbent layering process with the aim of improving structured
sorbent performance. A deeper understanding of short- and long-term manufacturing costs will also be
required. Beyond upgrades to the structured sorbent filter manufacturing, the TEA suggests continued
improvements to the sorbent composition to improve CO2 working capacity and kinetics can further reduce
the cost of capture to boost the commercial viability of this technology.

1 Executive Summary
This report presents the results of an initial techno-economic analysis for Susteon's direct air capture (DAC)
technology, "EcoFilter," under project DE-FE0032254, “Bench-Scale Development of Promoted High-
Capacity Structured Sorbents”. The analysis demonstrates that the process is potentially a cost effective for
carbon dioxide (CO2) removal from ambient air. The EcoFilter is a modular air contactor containing a
structured sorbent comprised of a polymer backbone, a proprietary amine, and a promoter, coated onto a
pleated filter structure. As a result, this sorbent has low water adsorption, high oxidative stability, and high
adsorption rate. This technology is an improvement over Susteon’s previous DAC technology for which a
technoeconomic assessment was conducted under DE- FE0032243.
To determine this technology’s technoeconomic viability, a facility with a nominal CO2 capture rate of
100,000 TPY at a capacity factor of 85% was studied. This facility is assumed to be at a generic greenfield
site in the Midwestern U.S. and is removing CO2 from air at standard conditions. The removed CO2 is then
transported and stored in a deep saline formation for which the cost is estimated using the Department of
Energy (DOE) Office of Fossil Energy and Carbon Management (FECM)/NETL CO2 Transport Cost Model
(CO2 Transport Cost Model) and the FECM/NETL CO2 Saline Storage Cost Model (CO2 Storage Cost
Model). The output from DE-FE0032254’s analysis was compared to that developed during DE-
FE0032243 and is illustrated below.
As summarized in Table 1, results of this assessment show the cost of CO2 capture is $241/tCO2 using the
EcoFilter technology. To determine key process and cost parameters, a sensitivity analysis was conducted
on technical and financial variables. A summary of the parameters and sensitivity analysis are provided in
Table 3 and Figure 1, respectively. A combination of key performance improvements, notably CO2
adsorption rate, sorbent capacity, manufacturing, and risk-reduction methods, can result in a reduction in
cost of CO2 capture from $241/tonne-CO2 to $165/metric tonne-CO2. This case study and sensitivity
analysis establishes critical performance targets for the continuing development of the DAC technology.

1. Technology Readiness
1.1. Technology Background
Susteon’s direct air capture (DAC) technology has two key innovations: (1) a high-capacity CO2 sorbent
resistant to water uptake and thermal and oxidative degradation and (2) a sorbent coated filter geometry
capable of high air flow throughput with low pressure drop and direct Joule-heating for efficient CO2
production/sorbent regeneration. The DAC sorbent (Figure 1) consists of a polymer backbone that is
resistant to water adsorption, a functionalized amine and promoter that can achieve high CO2 adsorption
capacity and rate and is resistant to oxidative degradation during sorbent regeneration from steam and/or
oxygen present during sorbent regeneration and cooldown.

 Figure 1. Sorbent design criteria
The structured sorbent filter consists of the CO2 sorbent coated onto a nonwoven filter substrate that can be
heated directly via joule heating. The nonwoven filter design allows for high air flow throughput with
minimal pressure drop (<100 Pa). The filter substrate is electrically conductive, which allows for direct,
rapid electric heating for efficient sorbent regeneration/CO2 production. The filter substrate also has low
thermal mass, representing <10wt% of the total mass of the structured sorbent assembly, resulting in
minimal parasitic energy loss for heating the filter substrate. The structured sorbent filter can be
manufactured using the existing infrastructure used to manufacture air purification filters, reducing the risk
of scale-up of the structured sorbent filter.
1.2. Technology Readiness Level
 The current technology readiness level (TRL) for Susteon’s DAC technology is TRL 4.
Component or system validation in laboratory environment is complete, and the technology is ready for
TRL 5 – prototype demonstration in a simulated environment. At the completion of the current project,
Susteon’s DAC filter technology has undergone the following development highlights and is primed for
progression to TRL 5 and beyond:
 1. Selection and validation of DAC sorbent with high working capacity (>5wt%) and adsorption rate
 that has good oxidative stability
 2. Successfully scaled sorbent synthesis process to produce 50 kg of ECCOSorb-T1
 3. Selection of an electrically conductive nonwoven filter substrate capable of direct Joule heating
 4. Validation from commercial filter manufacturing partner the structured sorbent assembly can be
 manufactured using their existing filter manufacturing infrastructure.
 5. Demonstrated the structured sorbent filter operation for DAC with Joule-heated regeneration.

pleated filter panel was developed and manufactured as part of a joint development with an industrial filter
manufacturing partner. The full-size pleated filter panel was tested in the bench-scale DAC unit, confirming
the structured sorbent assembly can be used for DAC application. While the progress is significant, key
challenges remain in scaling this DAC technology, including optimizing the sorbent coating process to
maximize sorbent accessibility and demonstrating continuous CO2 production in a representative prototype
system. These challenges are addressable and present a strategic opportunity for collaboration with
commercial partners to advance the technology to pilot scale and higher TRLs.

2. TARGET COMMERCIAL APPLICATION
Direct air capture utilization and storage (DAC-US) is a complementary technology to point-source carbon
capture utilization and storage (CCUS) and vital to meet CO2 mitigation targets of 1.5°C scenario. DAC-
US captures CO2 from ambient air and subsequently converts it to a salable productor or stores the CO2 to
prevent it from being released back into the atmosphere. Extensive deployment of DAC can mitigate

distributed CO2 emissions from industries such as transportation and aviation. Beyond negative emissions,
DAC units can be placed ubiquitously to capture and utilize CO2 for a wide variety of applications.
Installing DAC at the sequestration hub's vicinity can reduce transport and storage costs by eliminating
expensive pipelines. Expanding DAC deployment on a large scale unlocks significant potential to convert
CO2 to high-value products while reducing CO2 emission levels. Some of the CO2 utilization pathways that
have been integrated to point-source CO2 capture can be integrated downstream of the DAC unit. Successful
examples of such integrations are converting CO2 into methanol and acetic acid or fixing CO2 in building
block materials.
These advantages of DAC technologies become a strong promoter for the commercial deployment of these
systems. Most DAC technologies are modular, which extends the prospect of more rapid scaling by
numbering them up. The regeneration of Susteon’s DAC sorbent can be done with direct electric heating,
enabling the use of renewable power such as solar PV and wind. The use of direct electric heating of the
EcoFilters for CO2 desorption results in rapid ramping of the DAC system, in the order of minutes, and a
high turndown ratio. This allows the system to operate around the variable availability of renewable energy.

3. POST-PROJECT PLANS
Susteon demonstrated that its DAC technology has completed TRL 4: component and/or breadboard
validation in a laboratory environment. The bench-scale testing of the sorbent coated filter confirmed the
feasibility using the structured sorbent filter for DAC with joule-heated regeneration:
 1. The sorbent can be coated and formed into the structured sorbent filter panel using commercially
 available filter manufacturing assembly lines.
 2. The manufactured structured sorbent filter incorporating the ECCOsorb-T1 can be used for CO2
 removal from ambient air and Joule heated regeneration.
Continuing TRL progression from TRL 4 to TRL 5 and beyond will require a multi-phase approach that
includes further material and manufacturing improvements followed by a larger scale demonstration.
Susteon’s plan focuses on parallel tracks of continued sorbent development and filter manufacturing
improvements, integrated bench-scale system demonstration, and commercial de-risking to ensure a viable
pathway to commercialization.
The sorbent development will continue using Susteon’s lab-scale screening reactors and in-house synthesis
expertise to improve performance metrics such as adsorption rate, CO2 capture capacity, and stability.
Successful iterations will be scaled up and integrated into the structured filter form factor and tested in
Susteon’s 1-kg/day bench-scale system. Real-world performance data on the combined sorbent and filter
design will drive further refinement and establish optimal operating conditions for the next scale of
demonstration.

manufacturing methods and QA/QC protocols to meet the quantities and material performance required for the 15-
