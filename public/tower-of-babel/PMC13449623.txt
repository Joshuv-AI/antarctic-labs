Communication Barriers and Facilitators to Specialist Pediatric Palliative Care Involvement in Pediatric Oncology Settings Across Canada
Authors: Leeat Granek, Stephanie Veldhuijzen van Zanten, Kimberley Widger, Lori Wiener, Donna Johnston, Fyeza Hasan, Nathasha Datoo, Karen Fergus, Anthony K C Chan, Alisha Kassam, Dave Lysecki, Emily McCullogh, Sumit Gupta, Adam Rapoport
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo examine how healthcare provider communication shapes referral, acceptance and ongoing integration of specialist pediatric palliative care (SPPC).MethodsA qualitative study was conducted across four pediatric cancer centers in Canada. Sixty‐six healthcare providers working in oncology and palliative care settings participated in in‐depth interviews. Participants included physicians, nurses, psycho‐social clinicians and other members of the interprofessional health team. Transcripts were analyzed using iterative, inductive coding based on the Grounded Theory method.ResultsCommunication barriers to referral and acceptance included inadequate explanations for SPPC involvement, incomplete or misleading descriptions of services, and framing SPPC solely as end‐of‐life care. Oncologist discomfort, avoidance, and use of ambiguous “coded” language further hindered referral conversations. Facilitators included strong communication skills, clear and comprehensive explanations of SPPC, and age‐appropriate engagement with patients. For ongoing integration of SPPC, poor interprofessional communication and unclear role and responsibilities among team members created risks for fragmented care. In contrast, structured collaboration such as joint meetings, shared care planning, and proactive information exchange, supported effective integration of SPPC and consistent messaging to patients and families.ConclusionsCommunication is central to successful SPPC referral, acceptance, and integration in pediatric oncology settings. Both clinician‐family and interprofessional communication practices directly shaped understanding and engagement with SPPC services. Interventions should prioritize communication training, clearer framing of SPPC, and enhanced collaboration between teams. Early involvement of the psychosocial provider along with an SPPC specialist may further improve communication and uptake, ultimately strengthening care for children with serious illness and their families.Keywords: cancer, communication, pediatric oncology, pediatric palliative care, qualitative methods

Introduction

Effective and timely referral to specialist pediatric palliative care (SPPC) is widely recognized as a critical component of high‐quality care for children with serious illness and their families. SPPC is a multidisciplinary approach that addresses the physical, emotional, psychosocial, and spiritual needs of children with serious illnesses and their families. SPPC aims to improve quality of life throughout the illness trajectory, regardless of disease stage or prognosis [1, 2, 3]. SPPC has been shown to improve symptom management, enhance quality of life, and support decision‐making [3, 4, 5, 6], however, referrals often occur late in the disease trajectory, or not at all [7, 8]. While structural and systemic barriers to SPPC integration have been well documented [9, 10, 11, 12, 13], relatively little attention has focused on the role of communication in shaping referral initiation and acceptance and family engagement with SPPC [14, 15, 16]. In particular, how and when SPPC is introduced, described, and discussed with patients and families likely influences perceptions of its purpose and, ultimately, the willingness of patients and their families to accept SPPC involvement.

Communication challenges in this context are multifaceted and likely occur throughout the patients' cancer trajectory, though what precisely these communication challenges are, remains largely unexplored. In a recent review of nearly 200 papers on barriers and facilitators to SPPC referral the authors note that ‘staff communication skills and information provision’ were named as a barrier in 28% of the studies and a facilitator in 17% of the articles they reviewed [16]. ‘Staff communication and information provision’ are broad terms that provide little insight into what aspects of communication or the provision of information are effective, or counterproductive, in a resulting SPPC referral. While some studies have been more concrete in naming factors such as anxiety and discomfort around discussing palliative care [17, 18, 19, 20, 21], and the withholding of prognostic information from patients and families as barriers to SPPC integration and referral [22, 23], most of the literature notes that communication is important, but does not specify what about this communication works when discussing SPPC with families and patients.

To address this gap, the present study explored healthcare providers' perspectives on the role of communication in SPPC referral, SPPC acceptance on the part of the family and ongoing SPPC integration within pediatric oncology settings in Canada. Using qualitative methods, we examined how communication with patients and families, as well as interprofessional communication between teams, shaped the acceptance and integration of SPPC services.

Methods

Study Design, Participants & Procedure

This study used the Grounded Theory (GT) method [24] and was part of a larger project exploring the barriers and facilitators to the early integration of PPC for children with cancer. As part of this project, we published a paper that presented a systems theory model to understand the process of PPC referral for children with cancer [Blinded for Review]. We found that the PPC referral process was best understood as a dynamic system where referral emerged as a fluid, systemic state influenced by bidirectional interactions among oncologists, the interprofessional health team and patients and families, rather than a response to a clinical indicator [blinded for review]. Research Ethics Board approvals were obtained at the four participating sites in November 2023. Participants were recruited from four Canadian pediatric centers that have SPPC teams and treat a significant proportion of Canadian children with cancer. Inclusion criteria were being a healthcare provider (HCP) with at least 1 year's experience, who primarily treats pediatric patients with cancer, and/or provides specialist SPPC. Eligible professions included physicians, nurses, psychologists, social workers and other interprofessional healthcare team members. Participants were recruited by study co‐investigators who emailed staff about the study. Potential participants were then asked to contact the research associate about the study directly. A semi‐structured interview guide was used. Questions focused on barriers and facilitators to early specialist SPPC referral for children with cancer with a specific focus on how communication hinders or facilitates this process. For example, participants were asked how they communicate to patients and families about SPPC, who communicates this information, what phrases and approaches they use in having this conversation, and what they felt were barriers and facilitators to having these conversations around referral and integration of the SPPC team. Interviews took place between Feb 2024‐ June 2024 and were conducted by the research associate, a female postdoctoral fellow with extensive experience conducting qualitative research. The interviewer was not a healthcare provider and did not know any of the participants or have any prior assumptions about SPPC prior to the launch of the study. Interviews were conducted using videoconferencing technology and lasted between 45 and 75 minutes. Interviews were then transcribed and de‐identified for coding.

Reflexivity

The research team for this study consisted of a multidisciplinary group of healthcare professionals, the majority of whom work in pediatric oncology settings. The principal investigator is an academic health psychologist with extensive expertise in qualitative research focused on healthcare providers in oncology and other clinical contexts. The broader team included oncologists, nurses, palliative care physicians, and psychologists, all of whom support the integration of pediatric palliative care (PPC).

Throughout the study, team meetings and manuscript discussions involved ongoing reflection regarding our perspectives and assumptions about the value of early PPC involvement in the cancer care trajectory. We made a deliberate effort to remain attentive to these potential biases and to ground our interpretations closely in participants' accounts, particularly when their views differed from our own understandings of PPC. For instance, as will be discussed in the findings below, some participants expressed misconceptions about PPC services (i.e., PPC is only about end of life). This prompted careful consideration of how to accurately represent these perspectives while avoiding the inadvertent reinforcement of misinformation.

Data Analysis

The RA conducted the data analysis in close collaboration with the principal investigator (PI). The RA and the PI independently coded the first five transcripts and met to discuss the developing coding scheme to work out any differences in interpretation of the data and the labeling of the codes. Subsequently, the RA coded the remainder of the transcripts with weekly consultation with the PI. Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently, with line‐by‐line coding of transcripts guiding the process [24]. Codes were developed iteratively and no preconceived codes or categories were used. Initial codes included broad categories such as “communication about palliative care”, “communication competencies” etc. Through focused coding and constant comparison, these codes were grouped into broader categories reflecting communication during referral/acceptance and communication during ongoing integration of SPPC. Throughout the study, the PI and the RA wrote memos about emerging ideas from the analysis and the team met regularly to discuss emerging findings, ensure consistency, and refine the coding scheme. NVivo 12 software was used to code, store, and organize the data. When coding was complete, the PI reviewed the dataset to identify communication‐related codes, categories, and emerging themes relevant to SPPC provision and discussion. These were subsequently reviewed and refined through ongoing discussions with the RA and research team. Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently. Recruitment continued until data saturation was achieved, such that additional interviews were no longer contributing new codes, properties, dimensions, or relationships to the developing analytic categories. Decisions regarding recruitment and sampling were informed by ongoing analysis, memo writing, and constant comparison analysis across interviews. The current manuscript represents a focused analysis of communication processes within a dataset that had already reached theoretical saturation in the parent Grounded Theory study. No additional interviews were conducted for this communication‐focused analysis. In line with Constructivist Grounded Theory, this analytic phase involved focused coding of communication as a core category initially identified in the parent study, while maintaining the use of constant comparative methods and iterative category development. During focused coding, all communication‐related codes were examined in greater depth. The analysis demonstrated that communication processes clustered around two phases of the SPPC experience: communication related to referral and initial acceptance of SPPC involvement, and communication related to the ongoing integration of SPPC after referral. Rather than treating barriers and facilitators as discrete themes, they were conceptualized as interacting conditions and processes that influence movement through stages of SPPC referral, acceptance, and integration.

Analytic rigor was ensured through several strategies. Credibility was supported through independent coding by the RA and PI, followed by regular meetings to compare interpretations and refine the coding framework. Dependability was enhanced through an audit trail consisting of analytic memos, coding decisions, and documentation within NVivo 12 and in word files. Confirmability was addressed through reflexive practices described above, as well as ongoing extensive discussions within the research team to challenge interpretations.

Findings

This analysis focuses on communication as a central process within the previously developed model of SPPC referral and integration [Blinded for Review]. The categories presented below represent analytically derived processes and conditions that shape how patients and families move through two key stages: (1) referral and acceptance and (2) ongoing integration of SPPC. Barriers and facilitators are therefore not presented as independent themes, but as interrelated communication processes that shape families' understanding, receptivity, and progression through these stages.

Sixty‐six healthcare providers participated in this study. Participant demographics are presented in Table 1. Communication with patients and families about SPPC involvement occurred in two stages (1) Referral and acceptance and (2) Ongoing integration (Figure 1). The first stage included the introduction of the idea of a referral to the SPPC team with the patient and family, typically done by the oncologist, though this could also happen by other healthcare providers caring for the child with cancer. Then, if the patient and family accepted the opportunity to hear more about SPPC services, they met with the SPPC team directly for further communication about their involvement. Communication barriers to SPPC referral and acceptance included the themes of inadequate conversations about the need for SPPC, incomplete or misleading description of SPPC services, associating SPPC solely with end‐of‐life care, and oncologist specific barriers that included the use of ‘coded language’, discomfort with SPPC and/or avoidance of the conversation. Communication facilitators included the themes of communication competencies, having age‐appropriate conversations with patients and providing a fulsome explanation of SPPC services. Communication Barriers to SPPC Referral and Acceptance

Participants reported that the oncologist and, at times, the oncology team, were the first to communicate about SPPC with the family and/or the patient. Participants believed that the way in which this communication happened likely affected the family's willingness to hear more about SPPC and/or want them to be involved in their child's care. Below we report on the barriers that arose in those conversations in more detail and outline what participants meant by ‘poor communication.’

Inadequate Conversations About the Need for a SPPC Referral

Participants described situations where the family and or the patient were not informed about why they were being referred to SPPC in a clear way. As a result, families may have felt anxious that their child's prognosis was worse than it was, or that they were not being given all the information about their child's status. In one example, a family was sent an email letting them know that the provider had initiated a SPPC consult without explaining why they had done so, leading to a great deal of emotional distress for the family and reluctance to meet with the SPPC team. In other examples the SPPC team came to meet families in the clinic without the families having been told that this was a standard of care for all patients or for a particular diagnosis, which also led to reluctance around SPPC involvement.

Incomplete or Misleading Description of SPPC Role and Services/Associating SPPC Solely With End‐of‐Life Care

Participants reported that oncologists did not always present SPPC to the patient and the family correctly, or at least fully when talking about what the team does. One palliative care nurse remarked, “I do worry sometimes if we're presented as the team that is going to need to be called if things don't go well, then that might set us up for a situation where the family sort of sees us as well, ‘that's not a team that we ever want to meet then’” (P.34). Related to this finding, participants reported that SPPC was often still described as the ‘end‐of‐life’ care team, which likely led families to be wary of wanting to meet with SPPC team members.

Oncologist Hesitant or Uncomfortable With SPPC Conversations

Participants reported that the level of comfort an oncologist had with SPPC directly affected the way they communicated about it to the family, which in turn, may have affected the family's receptivity to the SPPC team. e.g., oncologists sometimes felt they ‘had to’ have a conversation about SPPC with a family because of the child's deteriorating situation, but that this conversation could elicit feelings of guilt or failure in the oncologist for being unable to cure the child. Alternatively, an oncologist might feel that SPPC was not needed but felt obligated nonetheless to offer it to the family because of pressure from the larger interprofessional team to do so. Participants noted that these feelings of discomfort, regardless of the source for the oncologist, could lead to a halting or unconvincing conversation with the family about the need for SPPC.

Oncologist Avoidant of SPPC Discussions or Indirect About the Child's Prognosis

Other communication barriers described had to do with oncologists' avoiding the SPPC conversation altogether or being indirect or withholding information about the child's declining prognosis. Participants reflected on how this could lead to a delay in having SPPC conversations and getting the team involved in the child's care.

Oncologist Use of ‘Coded Language’

Related to the finding above about avoidance and indirect language, participants reported that oncologists used ‘coded language’ to communicate with families about their child nearing the end of life. Coded language referred to communication that participants felt were ambiguous or ‘softened’ because the oncologist wished to avoid causing the family pain, and thus, could be misunderstood by them. e.g., when there were no more treatments available, oncologists may say something accurate, such as “the treatment is not working,” without ensuring that the family understood that what they meant is that there were no more curative treatments options available, and it may be time to change the goals of care with the help of the SPPC team. As a result of this type of ambiguous communication, the family could hear something different than what the oncologist intended to communicate (e.g., in this case, the family could understand that this treatment is not working, but there is another one to try next etc.) making them more reluctant to involve SPPC in their care.

Communication Facilitators to SPPC Referral and Acceptance

Many of the communication facilitators that were reported by participants counteracted the communication barriers noted above. For example, clear and effective communication with patients and families by the oncologist, but especially by the SPPC team once a referral was made, were repeatedly noted to be facilitators to the family accepting SPPC team services and to an easy transition into having the SPPC team more involved in the care of the patient.

Communication Competencie

One of the most robust findings in this study was the consistent reports by both the oncology and the SPPC teams that the SPPC team was trained in communication competencies and excelled in this domain. Participants reported that SPPC team members could communicate complicated concepts in a clear way for families, patients, and at times, providers; were skilled at having ‘serious illness conversations’ and discussing goals of care; were sensitive to the needs of the families and the patients; and were good listeners. Specialized training in communication was felt to be a strong facilitator to the acceptance of SPPC and when caring for patients and families. On this, one oncologist remarked, “I'm always in awe with how comfortable the [SPPC] physicians and nurse practitioners are in communication. I always learn from them when I am with them. That's why I'm saying if they can bottle up their scripts, that would be great” (P.37).

Age‐Appropriate Communication

Participants reported that the SPPC team was especially effective at communicating with patients at an age‐appropriate level and including them in decision‐making. Some of their strategies included trying to find a time to speak to teenage patients on their own, or addressing the child directly about their wishes and their well‐being when visiting with families.

Clear and Fulsome Explanation of SPPC Services

Finally, participants remarked that the SPPC team was able to give a fulsome and clear explanation of what SPPC services entailed which increased the likelihood that patients and families would be interested in being followed by their team. This included, but was not limited to, describing SPPC as an additional layer of support to the whole family alongside the oncology team where the focus was on quality of life, pain and symptom management, promoting comfort.

Communication Barriers to Ongoing Successful SPPC Integration in the Child's Care

Lack of Communication Between SPPC and Oncology Teams

At some study sites, there appeared to be less collaboration between the oncology and SPPC teams, and participants reported that this led to a lack of communication, which could potentially be harmful to patient care. e.g., some members of the oncology team felt that the SPPC team should be involved in patient rounds or a daily huddle where information about all the patients was exchanged. Another example was remarked most frequently by psychosocial oncology team members (i.e., social workers, psychologists, child life specialists and other interprofessional team members) who felt most excluded when it came to the communication between SPPC team and the oncology team.

Poor Communication About Roles and Responsibilities of SPPC Team and Oncology Team in a Child's Care

When both oncology and SPPC teams were involved in a child's care, participants reported that there was potential for things to fall through the cracks (i.e., team members thinking the other team will follow through on a care a directive) and/or dangerous situations (i.e., both the oncologist and the SPPC physician prescribing opiates) that resulted from poor communication between the teams.

Communication Facilitators to Ongoing Successful SPPC Integration in the Child's Care

Joint Meetings With Patients and Familie

Participants noted that close collaboration between the teams was essential to communicating effectively about SPPC with patients and families. Part of this collaboration involved shared meetings with patients and families where both teams were present to talk about the patient and family needs.

Joint Care Planning Meetings Between the Teams And/Or Consulting With Each Other

Related to joint meetings with the families, participants noted that shared care planning meetings between the teams was also critical to effective communication about SPPC before meeting with the families. Sometimes this took the form of meeting to discuss the patient's care prior to a joint meeting with the patient and family, and other times this related to members of both teams consulting with each other before meeting with the family and/or alerting one another to patient and family needs.

Discussion

This study provides insight into the central role of communication in shaping SPPC referral, acceptance and integration within pediatric oncology. Our study findings indicate that how SPPC is introduced, described, and discussed, alongside how teams communicate with one another, may influence families' understanding of SPPC, their responses to referral, and their willingness to hear more about and engage with SPPC services in the short and long‐term. These findings extend our previously developed model of SPPC referral and integration by specifying the communicative mechanisms through which movement between stages is facilitated or constrained. In particular, this analysis highlights how clinician‐level factors (e.g., comfort, communication style), interactional processes (e.g., use of coded language), and system‐level dynamics (e.g., inter‐team communication) interact to shape referral trajectories and the sustainability of SPPC involvement over time. By making these relationships explicit, this study moves beyond descriptive categorization to further elaborate the underlying processes central to early SPPC integration.

At the level of clinician‐family interaction, our findings identify specific communication practices that hinder SPPC referral and acceptance. Participants perceived that inadequate explanations about the purpose of referral, incomplete or misleading descriptions of SPPC services, and the persistent conflation of SPPC with end‐of‐life care contributed to family distress and reluctance to engage with SPPC. These findings are consistent with existing literature demonstrating that SPPC is often misunderstood and is frequently seen by providers as synonymous with death and dying, leading to delayed SPPC referrals [25, 26, 27, 28]. More broadly, our findings align with a growing body of literature describing the challenges associated with the language and framing of palliative care. Across both adult and pediatric settings, concerns have been raised that the term “palliative care” is frequently associated with death and dying, prompting some programs to adopt alternative terminology such as “supportive care” in an effort to improve acceptance and facilitate earlier referral [29, 30, 31]. While debates regarding terminology continue, our findings suggest that communication challenges extend beyond the label itself. Participants described how uncertainty, avoidance, inconsistent explanations, and indirect communication practices can reinforce misconceptions regardless of the terminology used. This suggests that improving integration may require attention not only to what palliative care is called, but also to how it is described, introduced, and discussed in clinical interactions. Participants' accounts suggest that these communication challenges were not simply failures of knowledge, but were often embedded within the emotional and relational complexity of discussing serious illness. Clinician uncertainty, discomfort with prognostic conversations, and a desire to protect families from distress appeared to shape how information was framed and what was communicated. Whereas prior studies have largely focused on whether alternative terminology such as 'supportive care' improves referral acceptance, our findings suggest that the communicative practices surrounding referral may be equally important as the terminology itself. Moreover, our study adds important nuance by illustrating how these misunderstandings are actively perpetuated through communication practices, including vague explanations, inconsistent framing of SPPC, and the use of indirect, avoidant, or ‘coded’ language when talking to patients and families. Very few studies have addressed the issue of ‘what is not said’ in these SPPC conversations, particularly in North America. In one study, 200 critical care nurses in Jordan were given a survey about barriers to pediatric ‘end of life care’ and they named ‘physicians who were evasive or avoided having conversations with families’ as one of these barriers [32]. In another mixed‐methods study that took place in Australia that involved children with and without cancer who were all receiving SPPC, parents of children who did not have cancer noted that they wanted clear and honest information about their child's condition and prognosis in order to make good decisions for them, but this was not always forthcoming from providers. All parents also reported that SPPC was not well understood by both the healthcare providers and the parents of children with all illnesses [33]. This type of ambiguous communication may unintentionally obscure prognosis and care goals, resulting in misalignment between provider intent in these conversations as well as what they felt the discussion achieved, and family understanding of their child's condition and the need for SPPC.

Importantly, our findings highlight the influence of clinician factors on communication. Oncologist discomfort with SPPC discussions, whether rooted in prognostic uncertainty and/or emotional discomfort was described as shaping both the timing, tenor and quality of these SPPC conversations with patients and families. Participants perceived that avoidance, hesitancy, or unconvincing messaging could affect how families interpreted the purpose and value of SPPC, potentially reducing openness to involvement. As one participant noted, the discomfort oncologists felt about SPPC lead them to “undersell” SPPC. These findings align with prior research identifying clinician discomfort and anxiety as barriers to early palliative care integration [17, 21, 34, 35, 36], while further specifying how these factors manifest in real‐world communication behaviors about SPPC.

Beyond clinician‐family interactions, this study underscores the importance of interprofessional communication in facilitating or hindering SPPC integration. Poor communication between the oncology and SPPC teams, including lack of information sharing and unclear delineation of roles and responsibilities, was identified as a significant barrier. These gaps not only risk compromised care coordination but may also contribute to inconsistent messaging to families, further exacerbating resistance. Conversely, structured and ongoing communication between teams, such as joint meetings, shared care planning, and pre‐consultation discussions, was described as a key facilitator of effective SPPC communication and integration. These findings reinforce the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration and suggest that communication between teams is as critical as communication with families in shaping SPPC referral and integration outcomes. In the ideal situation, a collaborative partnership between oncology and SPPC teams could allow both teams to be involved and present in the child's care with continual joint communication practices to clarify roles and responsibilities depending on need and expertise to manage physical symptoms and distress, guide communication about goals of care, and promote quality of life throughout the disease trajectory, and, when relevant, at end‐of‐life [37, 38].

Clinical Implications

Taken together, these findings have important implications for clinical practice. Interventions aimed at improving SPPC referral should move beyond increasing awareness or addressing structural barriers to also prioritize communication strategies and skills. These interventions may include training oncology providers on how to introduce and frame SPPC, and creating opportunities for joint communication between oncology and SPPC teams. Rather than focusing solely on fixed or scripted terminology, communication interventions may also benefit from emphasizing underlying communication principles such as clarity, transparency, empathy, and responsiveness to patient and family understanding, allowing clinicians to adapt language to individual contexts while maintaining consistency in messaging. Importantly, communication training should emphasize how these conversations may elicit emotional responses in the provider that can directly impact what patients and families hear in these interactions. That is, it is not just the words used to describe SPPC that matter, but also the underlying beliefs, values and emotions of the clinician around SPPC that shapes the message that the patient and family receive about the value and usefulness of SPPC. In addition, structured opportunities for interprofessional communication could be specified more concretely, including joint consultations between oncology and SPPC teams, interdisciplinary case conferences, shared care planning meetings, and pre‐referral discussions aimed at aligning messaging prior to family conversations.

Our findings highlight an important yet often underrecognized role for psycho‐oncology professionals in facilitating effective communication and integration of SPPC. Psycho‐oncology clinicians in our study felt particularly marginalized by the lack of collaboration and communication between oncology and specialist palliative care teams, despite their expertise in addressing the emotional, psychological, and relational dimensions of care and their longstanding relationships with patients and families. This sense of exclusion represents a missed opportunity, as psycho‐oncology professionals are uniquely positioned to support patients, families, and healthcare teams in navigating difficult conversations, managing emotional responses, and aligning understanding across disciplines. Greater inclusion of psycho‐oncology team members within interdisciplinary communication processes, including care planning and joint discussions, can enhance continuity of care, reduce distress, and improve overall engagement with SPPC services.

Finally, our study also found that SPPC clinicians are particularly skilled communicators, especially in explaining what SPPC is and what it can offer to patients and families. Therefore, increasing their presence at the ‘front door’ of care may improve understanding and uptake of SPPC services. Early engagement of the SPPC team may help ensure that the role of SPPC is clearly communicated and understood from the outset so that patients and families can make decisions about their care that is based on reliable information about what SPPC is and can offer them. Secondly, given their expensive training in communication skills, the SPPC team can also help oncology clinicians develop language that they are comfortable with in discussing pediatric palliative care with their patients and families. As one of our participants noted, “It feels sometimes like there's less honest communication that happens in oncology. … We can be honest, and still be kind, we can be clear, and we can still be kind” (p. 47). The ability to speak directly and with kindness is a communication skill that can be learned and honed with practice. The SPPC team can play a role in modeling and instructing on this kind of communication to help ease the difficulty in communicating bad news to patients and families about their prognosis in a direct, but simultaneously, gentle and kind way.

Limitations

As a qualitative study conducted within Canadian pediatric oncology centers, findings may not be fully transferable to other healthcare systems or clinical contexts, particularly in more rural areas or those that do not have a SPPC team embedded within their hospitals. Additionally, perspectives did not include patients or families, whose experiences of communication and SPPC referral are critical areas for future research. Despite these limitations, the study offers rich, practice‐relevant insights into the specific communication processes that shape SPPC referral and integration.

Conclusion

In conclusion, communication plays a pivotal and multifaceted role in SPPC referral, shaping how SPPC is introduced, understood, and integrated within pediatric oncology care. By identifying concrete communication barriers and facilitators at both the clinician‐family and interprofessional levels, this study highlights actionable opportunities to improve communication and SPPC integration and, ultimately, the care of children with serious illness and their families.
