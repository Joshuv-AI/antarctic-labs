Enhancing healthcare explainability through multiobjective counterfactual explanations
Authors: Shafique Ahmed Memon, Ali Ebrahimi, Jan Dominik Kampmann, Uffe Kock Wiil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAI-driven decision support systems possess the capability to improve health outcomes. However, advanced AI-driven systems face challenges related to interpretability and slow real-world adoption due to the use of abstract, black-box models. As a result, Explainable AI (XAI) approaches are evolving to provide explanations for predictions. Nonetheless, the predominant XAI approaches have several significant drawbacks. Firstly, these techniques require an understanding of model internals; secondly, they lack the potential to provide insights into feature interaction; and thirdly, they cannot generate alternative solutions based on What-if scenarios. In this context, counterfactual explanations (CEs) can improve the explainability of black-box models by generating what-if scenarios for input data points. However, producing clinically plausible CEs is complex due to the presence of the “Rashomon Effect”, multiple conflicting objectives, and the trade-offs among them. This requires a robust Mechanism that ensures the interpretability and plausibility of CFs under balanced, optimized constraints. To address the above issues, this study proposes a novel multiobjective optimized counterfactual explanation (MoCE) methodology for generating clinically significant CEs. Our proposed methodology consists of two phases. Firstly, a Random Forest (RF) classifier was trained on the chronic kidney disease (CKD) dataset, using a single instance from the dataset as a patient profile to obtain the predicted outcome. Secondly, for baselines, we generated five CEs using diverse counterfactual explanations (DiCE) using a CKD instance. Next, we applied the NSGA-II algorithm-based MoCE methodology to generate multiple CEs against the same CKD instance. We compared both approaches, DiCE and the MoCE, qualitatively and quantitatively, for generating clinically plausible CEs while efficiently handling the confidence, validity, and sparsity trade-offs. Clinical relevance and significance of the CEs were assessed in an initial clinical evaluation conducted by a nephrologist expert. Both approaches achieve 100% validity. DiCE achieved a mean sparsity = 0.175 by changing approximately 2 features with medium confidence levels (0.59). Conversely, the proposed method generated CEs with enhanced confidence levels (0.9) by altering 7 attributes with sparsity (0.8–0.9), yielding more diverse and plausible solutions. The proposed MoCE methodology generated 100 Pareto-optimal CEs candidates, indicating a rich trade-off surface among validity, proximity, prediction confidence, and sparsity. The comparison highlights that DiCE generates CEs by altering a minimum number of features, resulting in highly sparse but less diverse and less clinically relevant CEs. In contrast, the proposed MoCE approach generated a diverse set of CEs by simultaneously optimizing multiple objectives, including distance, sparsity, and prediction confidence. This demonstrates a balanced handling of trade-offs, yielding more plausible CEs, particularly in high stake domains where feature interactions are crucial. This also suggests that the proposed MoCE provides a balanced and clinically meaningful alternative to existing methods.Keywords: Explainable AI (XAI), Counterfactual explanations, Multiobjecive, Evolutionary computation, Responsible AI, Understandable AI, Interpretability, TrustworthinessSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

The National Kidney Foundation reported an alarming number of patients with Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD). The number of people who require dialysis or transplant is growing more than ever before, especially in low-income countries. The detection of kidney disease and the adjustment of lifestyle can reduce the risk of dialysis and transplant1. AI can play an important role in the detection of kidney disease and provide guidelines to improve medical decision-making. Existing AI methods have shown excellent results, yet both clinicians and patients require interpretable and actionable insights, not just post-hoc predictions such as ‘1’ or ‘0’.

AI-based decision support systems have gained significance in several areas, including healthcare. This has resulted in several initiatives that have offered state-of-the-art solutions2–4. However, the pace of real-world adoption of these solutions is slow due to a lack of trust5–7, as a result of black-box and abstract models8,9. This requires the explainability of these solutions for trust and adoption, particularly in high-stakes fields like healthcare, to increase trust and confidence, enabling the adoption of real-world AI solutions10.

This quest has resulted in the emergence of Explainable AI (XAI) approaches to explain and interpret the reasons behind predictions and decisions11. The study12 divides these approaches into three categories: saliency, surrogate, and instance-based models. The saliency methods focus on the important regions that influence predictions, the surrogate models approximate the models with simple, interpretable versions, and the instance-based methods, which include counterfactual explanations (CEs), furnish multiple alternative scenarios. Among these, CEs have received particular attention due to their intuitive reasoning of ’what-if’. The CEs provide the understandability of model predictions by offering alternate ’what-if’ scenarios. The alternative, based on a what-if scenario, furnishes a different outcome by making a minimal change to the original prediction.

These XAI approaches explain and interpret a black box model well and provide useful insights into its predictions. For example, as mentioned above, saliency-based approaches identify regions that impact model predictions13,14. Other methods such as LIME and SHAP highlight the specific features that contribute to the’ predictions of the models. However, these methods, including Lime, SHAP, and saliency-based methods, also have certain limitations. Firstly, their inability to generate What-if scenarios offering alternative options and causal insights15,16. Secondly, producing explanations also requires access to model internals.

Against this backdrop, CEs that were proposed by Wachter et al. (2018) can potentially improve the interpretability of black-box models by generating what-if scenarios around input data points15,17. CEs produce different predictions by changing specific feature values18,19 along with providing “what-if” scenarios to demonstrate the impact and relationship of changing features11,19. Thus, CEs minimally modify the features to obtain different predictions and produce multiple what-if scenarios11,16,18,20 that lead to enhanced interpretability, revealing how alternate situations might impact prediction and decision-making21,22. Nevertheless, CEs that meet the feasibility, actionability, and sparsity standards provide understanding and reveal causal relationships between the features11,22,23. This study aims to iteratively explore the feature space to identify optimal counterfactuals (CFs) that strike a balance among these multiple objectives. However, there are some challenges involved, such as:

CEs’ generation focuses on adjusting minimal feature modifications to affect model predictions. One challenge is to choose the smallest practical modification among multiple alternatives, which is called the “Rashomon Effect”. The “Rashomon Effect” occurs when multiple possibilities produce the same outcome, making counterfactual selection difficult24,25.

Constraint management is important for generating viable CEs in sensitive areas such as healthcare. However, it is challenging to integrate and optimize multiple constraints15. For this purpose, Evolutionary Computing (EC), an optimization method, is implemented to handle tasks that involve optimization and constraint handling26.

In order to improve decision-making, diversity is important where a wider spectrum of equally feasible options is available. It should be noted that lack of diversity cannot ensure viable counterfactuals27.

Ensuring viable CEs can be challenging, but it is important to increase the interpretability and trust of the decision support system11,15.

Generating multiobjective optimized CEs necessitates a robust Mechanism that ensures the interpretability as well as the plausibility of CFs with balanced and optimized constraints. It should be noted that incorporating multiple constraints requires a multiobjective optimization approach, which may also include multiple conflicting objectives and trade-offs among the conflicting objectives.

Optimization aims to find the best solution by maximizing or minimizing the objectives. Single objective optimization has the single goal of generating an optimal solution28. However, it cannot handle multiobjective problems29. Multiobjective optimization (MOO) can treat multiple conflicting objectives with a focus on producing a set of Pareto-optimal solutions while balancing trade-offs between competing goals. A Pareto-optimal (non-dominated) solution is a solution for which no alternative solution can enhance at least one objective without worsening another. The collection of all such solutions is the Pareto set, and its depiction in the objective space is referred to as the Pareto front30–32. Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II) and Multiobjective Particle Swarm Optimization (MOPSO) are two commonly used techniques for solving multiobjective optimization problems by generating Pareto-optimal solutions that balance conflicting objectives. NSGA-II is based on the evolutionary genetic algorithm (GA), whereas MOPSO is based on Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO). In the literature, both techniques have been evaluated and compared in terms of their performance to generate better trade-off solutions. For example, a study by Jalaladdin et al.33 evaluated the performance of both techniques and suggested that NSGA-II outperformed MOPSO in generating better trade-off solutions. This study uses the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II), which is effective in tackling multiobjective trade-offs, as it explores the solution space efficiently and maintains a diverse set of solutions34,35.

Materials and methods

The process of generating MoCE consists of two phases. Firstly, we train our model on the CKD dataset to predict kidney disease using Random Forest, a tree-based machine learning classifier. We used an instance from the CKD dataset as a single patient profile to obtain the predicted outcome. Secondly, we apply the NSGA-II method to generate multiple CEs against the patient’s profile. Our objective is to generate clinically useful and significant alternative what-if scenarios against profile and to examine what minimal changes result in the opposite outcome while efficiently handling the validity, proximity, and sparsity trade-offs. The following section provides step wise details of the proposed pipeline.

Preprocessing

The CKD dataset was imported into a pandas DataFrame and examined for non-informative and administrative factors. Two columns, PatientID and DoctorInCharge, were eliminated before modeling, since they serve as identifiers rather than clinical attributes and do not aid in model decision-making. The dataset was subsequently divided into training and test sets with a stratified split to maintain the original class distribution.

Feature selection

To identify the most relevant clinical features and reduce dimensionality and noise, Boruta, a wrapper-based feature selection method, is applied. Unlike certain Feature Selection strategies that aim solely at identifying the minimal subset of a model. Boruta is a Feature Selection approach that uses a random forest classifier to identify the features most pertinent to the dependent variable, in contrast to the objective of a general Feature Selection algorithm, which focuses solely on identifying the minimal subset for a specific model37. The Random Forest (RF) is an ensemble classifier that belongs to the decision tree family38. Other potential choices include tree-based classifiers such as Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, eXtreme Gradient Boosting, LightGMB, etc. Nevertheless, Random Forest has been widely used for its effectiveness in feature selection39. Random Forest has been used here due to its strengths in identifying nonlinear correlations and feature interactions while providing a reliable, accurate, and robust feature assessment for feature selection for high-dimensional healthcare data40.

The Boruta algorithm proceeds as follows: 1. Creates a Feature set of all original features. 2. Creates corresponding shadow features by randomly permuting the values of the original features. 3. Train a Random Forest model using both the original and combined shadow features. 4. Contrasts the relevance of its shadow features and the significance of each original feature. 5. A feature is deemed statistically significant if its importance exceeds the sum of the importance of its shadow characteristics41.

Random forest model evaluation

Accuracy and F1-score were excluded as primary optimization metrics because of their susceptibility to majority-class domination in imbalanced datasets. In contrast, for the high-risk category, the focus was on PR-AUC, ROC-AUC, and the confusion matrix.

A confusion matrix, ROC-AUC, and PR-AUC are essential means to assess the performance of binary classification models. A confusion matrix is a performance assessment table for machine learning classification models that compares predictions with actual target values. ROC-AUC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve) evaluates “separation power” across thresholds, while PR-AUC (Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve) focuses the precision and recall of the positive class, making it more suitable for imbalanced datasets.

MoCE using NSGA-II

NSGA-II is used to obtain the Pareto front of non-dominated counterfactual solutions. Each candidate is evolved using selection, crossover, and mutation operators. The resulting Pareto-optimal set contains CFs that represent different trade-offs between proximity, validity, and sparsity. Selection, crossover, and mutation are important operators in genetic algorithms, and the quality of the results depends on their balance. During the generation of CEs, selection identifies optimal solutions through Pareto dominance. The crossover combines attributes of the parent solutions to find new areas. The mutation introduces changes to preserve diversity. Regarding the evaluation of CEs, such as validity, proximity, sparsity, and diversity: (i) Validity: Selection ensures solutions achieve target prediction. (ii) Proximity (distance): Crossover helps combine low-distance solutions, (iii) Sparsity: Mutation may increase/decrease the number of changed features, and (iv) Diversity: Mutation, selection produce multiple counterfactuals.

From , the final counterfactual is chosen, for example, as the valid solution with minimum distance:

We used a population size (100) and an offspring size (200) with 100 generations. A crossover probability of 0.7 and a mutation probability of 0.3 were employed to balance exploration and exploitation. A variance of 0.1 was used to introduce controlled perturbations in continuous clinical variables. The parameters were used to achieve the diversity of the candidate CEs and to ensure convergence stability. The standards in the evolutionary optimization literature48,49 provide insightful guidelines for parameter selection. In addition, our empirical observations also show that a small population size produces less diversity, whereas larger populations incur computational costs.

The CE generation problem has multiple conflicting objectives, such as (i) minimizing proximity (distance), (ii) Prediction change (to ensure class change), and (iii) minimizing sparsity (number of modified features). This conflict is addressed by the algorithm through Pareto dominance and non-dominated sorting. As a result, a set of Pareto-optimal solutions is produced instead of a single optimum. Each solution in the Pareto front represents a different trade-off among the objectives. Without improving one at the cost of the other. A two step method is used to select a single CE for interpretation: (i) A validity constraint to retain CEs having a target class probability greater than or equal to 0.8. (ii) The selected CE with the minimum distance to the original instance. This method resolves conflict among conflicting objectives by maintaining a balance between the prediction class change and the minimum change.

Discussion

The objective of this pipeline is to generate a multiobjective optimized counterfactual (MoCE) using the CKD dataset. This approach uses NSGA-II, an evolutionary algorithm for this purpose. The RF model is used to make explicit predictions and the NSGA-II algorithm is tasked with generating clinically plausible CFs. Thus, in the first part, RF was used for predictive tasks on a highly imbalanced CKD dataset, where multiple imbalanced-handling methods were compared with the proposed class-weighted RF. In Sect. 3, we demonstrate the results of the successful execution of a pipeline for the generation of multiobjective counterfactual explanations, given in Fig. 1. We utilized the CKD dataset as a proof of concept. The pipeline consists of two phases. In the initial phase, we use an RF Classifier for the classification task, and in the second phase, NSGA-II based MoCE methodology is used to generate CFs with multiobjective optimization.

It is pertinent to mention that CKD is a highly imbalanced clinical dataset, with the minority class comprising approximately 8%. In light of the screening-focused objective, the minimization of false negatives was placed above overall accuracy. Different solutions were evaluated to address the imbalance, including baseline (absence of CI handling), synthetic oversampling (SMOTE and ADASYN), and class-weighted learning.

It is obvious that accuracy metrics do not fully reflect the performance of the high risk minority class in the imbalanced datasets. In contrast, ROC AUC provides a prevalence invariant ranking, while PR AUC offers further insights into minority detection when contextualized52. Thus, accuracy and F1 score are not considered primary performance metrics, as they are prone to the influence of the majority class in imbalanced datasets. In contrast, for the high-risk category, the focus was on PR-AUC, ROC-AUC, and the confusion matrix. Despite the elevated PR-AUC values of the baseline and SMOTE-based models, these metrics were influenced by the predominant majority-class and were ineffective in identifying high-risk patients. ADASYN significantly improved sensitivity but compromised probability ranking and resulted in more false positives. The class-weighted RF attained an optimal equilibrium, preserving elevated recall, while enhancing the ROC-AUC and diminishing false positives.

At the designated screening threshold, the suggested model achieved an accuracy of 0.606 (95% CI: 0.559–0.655). Although accuracy is frequently cited, it is an unsuitable optimization metric for tasks characterized by significant class imbalance. A simplistic classifier that categorizes all patients as low-risk might obtain an accuracy exceeding 90%, although it would not recognize any high risk instances. As a result, accuracy was mentioned only for completeness, which is further completed by confidence intervals. Reduced requirements attained near perfect recall, but were unfeasible due to a dominant number of false positives, while elevated thresholds enhanced accuracy, but resulted in the omission of a significant number of high risk instances. An 85% recall threshold was chosen for early risk identification. Synthetic oversampling methods were found to be used with caution in noisy and imbalanced clinical data. Instead, class-weighted learning, when combined with probability calibration and threshold optimization, yielded more acceptable outcomes on imbalanced clinical datasets.

MoCE produced 100 Pareto-optimal counterfactual candidates, illustrating a comprehensive trade-off surface among proximity, prediction confidence, and sparsity. Of these, 32 CFs met the validity criterion with a target class probability of not less than 0.8. The best CF and five distinct and valid CFs were chosen for a complete investigation.

Each point corresponds to a Pareto-optimal counterfactual solution demonstrating a balanced compromise between minimal changes in features and high confidence in the prediction. Given the hypothetical use case scenario, we considered a patient profile, reduced the features, and generated five CE against each. We quantitatively analyzed the plausibility, proximity, validity, and sparsity of five CEs generated. Clinically, this signifies a what-if scenario or demonstrates alternative options or scenarios, as mentioned in the use case, to support medical decision-making. The results present clinicians with five alternatives (suggestions) resulting from minimal changes to the original features, indicating a change or a change in the original prediction. This helps in supporting medical decisions as clinicians can now prepare a treatment plan for the patient.

It is pertinent to note that XAI is crucial in high-stakes sensitive domains such as healthcare to build trust among clinicians for the acceptance and adoption of AI based clinical systems. In this study, we propose an NSGA-II-based multiobjective evolutionary methodology, the MoCE, that produces clinically significant and actionable counterfactual explanations. This novel method has several advantages over other methods. We used the DiCE approach as a baseline and generated five CEs against the same instance and compared them with our proposed multiobjective optimized approach quantitatively and qualitatively, as shown in Tables 4, 6, and 7. The comparison reveals observable advantages of our proposed approach over that of DiCE.

One advantage of MoCE is its high confidence score of 0.99, which suggests the counterfactuals are well positioned within the target class region rather than just crossing the decision boundary. While, DiCE produces counterfactuals with comparatively lower confidence (0.57–0.63). This suggests that its solutions are limited and potentially unstable. This also suggests that DiCE favors minimal changes that may compromise solution resilience and reliability in practical applications.

From a structural point of view, MoCE alters more features (7–8) than DiCE (1–2). Although this results in reduced sparsity, the solution is more aligned to feature relationships. This is important in high stake domains like healthcare, where factors are interconnected. MoCE encapsulates these dependencies for instance, by synchronized alterations in creatinine, BUN, and GFR etc., resulting in clinically coherent counterfactuals. In contrast, DiCE addresses characteristics independently, frequently altering one variable without influencing associated ones, perhaps resulting in medically untenable outcomes.

Qualitatively, the proposed approach has advantages over DiCE in terms of optimization strategy, diversity, feasibility, and trade-off management. Although DiCE uses single objective optimization, with the main focus on minimum feature alterations. This yields less diverse CEs. However, it fails to consider the dependencies or relationships of features. In contrast, the proposed MoCE approach generates CEs as a multiobjective optimization problem, simultaneously optimizing distance, sparsity, and predictive confidence. As a result, trade offs are better handled while yielding a diverse set of pareto optimal solutions. There is less observable causal reasoning in both approaches; however, the proposed MoCE approach demonstrates observable feature interactions. Thus, despite its computational cost, the proposed NSGA-II methodology demonstrates clinical relevance and actionability.

The clinical relevance and actionability of the features of the selected instance were further verified from sources related to kidney disease53–55. According to these sources, GFR, BUN, and Protein in Urine are particularly useful for diagnosing kidney disease. Sources also suggest that itching and muscle cramps are two significant symptoms and signs of kidney disease. This supports the clinical relevance of these features, as reflected in symptoms, signs, and a diagnostic perspective.

In view of this, CEs provide solutions to this as they provide a what-if scenario. However, generating plausible CEs is challenging. The resulting CE should be based on minimal changes in features to produce a different predicted outcome. They must meet proximity, validity, and sparsity criteria15. They must address the post-counterfactual Rashomon Effect, which implies the availability of overwhelming options to choose the best counterfactual24. The traditional approach to generating CEs may not address all of the above challenges, as it relies on a multiobjective optimization to handle conflicting objectives.

Therefore, the evolutionary computing-based technique is effective in handling these challenges and enhancing the understandability of clinical systems and improving trust. However, in multiobjective optimization problems, the trade-off among conflicting objectives creates a challenging situation that requires a Pareto optimization approach to comprehend the trade-off26,29,35,56. Hence, this study provides a novel method that utilizes NSGA-II to generate actionable and plausible CEs and to provide what-if scenario-based changes (suggestions). As a proof-of-concept, we use the (CKD) dataset, which showcases the method’s capability to generate realistic CEs. We quantitatively evaluated the model using proximity, validity, and sparsity metrics to validate the plausibility and overall quality of CEs. This method addresses the Rashomon Effect by furnishing diverse what-if alternatives (suggestions) rather than a singular post-hoc outcome. This method can be easily customized for other disease scenarios.

The overall results emphasize the capability of evolutionary optimization techniques to tackle challenges in a multiobjective environment and handle multiobjective trade-offs. However, from a technical perspective, evaluations based on proximity, validity, and sparsity confirm that CEs are plausible, realistic, efficient (with minimal necessary changes), and highly effective at achieving the desired prediction shift. All CFs fall within acceptable ranges for the three quantitative evaluation parameters. It is also significant to note that the model has addressed the main challenges of XAI. It achieved the underlying objective of XAI, the Rashomon Effect, thereby producing multiple diverse yet feasible explanations for a single prediction which ultimately leads to trust augmentation.

To complement the discussion above on clinical relevance and actionability, we conducted an initial evaluation of the proposed method and the proof-of-concept use case in CKD. An expert nephrologist (JDK) was presented with the overall concept and counterfactual examples to assess its clinical relevance and actionability. The feedback received was as follows.

The nephologist labeled the first two features (SystolicBP and FastingBloodSugar) as related to hypertension and diabetes, which are the most common causes of CKD. The next four features (SerumCreatinine, BUNlevels, GFR, and ProteinInUrine) were labeled as typical measurements of kidney function. The latter two features (MuscleCramps and Itching) were labeled as typical symptoms of CKD. Hence, all the selected features were meaningful to CKD.

The study focuses on a single use case related to CKD. Although the method is intended to be applicable in different domains, its effectiveness in other areas of healthcare care has not been empirically tested.

Conclusion

This study serves as an intersection between evolutionary computing and XAI, optimizing the generation of counterfactual explanations. Traditional XAI methods have offered promising results in explaining the problem and improving trust. However, they are generally based on the features that contribute the most to the decision or the regions in the medical image, highlighting the affected areas. Moreover, they also lack the ability to provide alternative options and the reason to understand the decision or prediction. Counterfactual explanations, on the other hand, can provide alternative options and enhance trust. However, generating counterfactuals that meet the criteria of proximity, validity, and sparsity is a challenging task.

The proposed MoCE methodology successfully generated technically valid and clinically plausible CEs. The performance of the proposed MoCE methodology was compared with a baseline DiCE methodology quantitatively and qualitatively, which highlights that DiCE generates CEs by altering a minimum number of features, resulting in highly sparse but less diverse and less clinically relevant CEs. Whereas the proposed MoCE approach generated a diverse set of CEs by simultaneously optimizing multiple objectives, including distance, sparsity, and prediction confidence. This demonstrates a balanced handling of trade-offs, yielding more plausible CEs, particularly in high stake domains where feature interactions are crucial. Clinical relevance and significance of the MoCE-generated CEs were assessed in an initial clinical evaluation conducted by a nephrologist expert.

The findings suggest that the proposed MoCE methodology also successfully handled the trade-off among competing constraints using Pareto optimality and outperformed legacy approaches by generating domain relevant, valid, and actionable counterfactuals. The proposed methodology generated the counterfactual with minimal change in the features of the original instance. Furthermore, the quantitative validation of CEs using proximity, validity, and sparsity metrics demonstrates that the methodology achieved the intended objective, including addressing the Rashomon effect, demonstrating the ability of the proposed method to produce clinically significant results.

The proposed MoCE framework improves the reliability, diversity, and clinical relevance of CE. This shows MoCE methodology’s suitability for deployment as a decision support tool in critical domains such as healthcare, where resilience and plausibility are crucial. The findings also highlight an important trade-off between sparsity and clinical realism. This suggests that the proposed MoCE provides a balanced and clinically meaningful alternative to existing methods.
