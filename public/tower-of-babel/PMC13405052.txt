Associations between urinary incontinence and physical activity in women: a cross-sectional survey in the UK
Authors: Becca Freeden, Cathy Bulley, Judith Lane, Karen Matthews, Laura Fregonese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo examine the link between urinary incontinence (UI) and physical activity participation in women. Little research has explored this relationship, despite the long-term effects of reduced physical activity on women’s health and well-being.DesignA cross-sectional survey was conducted using self-reported data collected through social media and university channels.ParticipantsAdults aged 18 years or older identifying as women, including transgender women who had undergone gender-affirming vaginoplasty and people assigned female at birth. In total, 413 individuals completed the survey, of whom 3 were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria. Most respondents (n=403; 98%) identified as female, with the largest age group being 25–44 years (n=176; 46%).Outcome measuresSeverity of urinary incontinence (International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire-Urinary Incontinence Short Form (ICIQ-UI SF)), physical activity quantity and intensity (International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form (IPAQ-SF)) and self-reported impact of UI on participation in physical activity. Analysis of IPAQ-SF and ICIQ-UI SF scores evaluated associations between UI and activity quantity and intensity. Free-text responses were categorised and their frequencies were recorded.ResultsUI was reported by 76.8% (n=315) of participants, with a median ICIQ-UI SF score of 6.0 (IQR 6.0, range 1.0–21.0) in those reporting UI. One-third (32.5%; n=133) agreed or strongly agreed that UI influenced their decision to start physical activity, and 37.3% (n=153) agreed that UI during activity affected whether they continued; free-text responses indicated this was a negative influence. Those with lower UI scores reported a higher quantity of physical activity, although this was not statistically significant (Kruskal-Wallis H=5.093, p=0.165). Significant reductions were observed in vigorous (H=10.733, p=0.013) activity time among those with more severe UI. Ordinal regression analysis showed a positive relationship between low intensity of physical activity and high levels of UI (OR 2.26, 95% CI 1.38 to 3.71, p=0.001). Age was not found to be a confounding variable. Analysis of free-text responses revealed themes of UI and its links to physical activity, strategies to prevent or cope with UI and the physical and emotional impacts of UI.ConclusionsIn this sample, a high prevalence of UI was associated with lower intensity of physical activity. Most strategies employed by participants addressed leaks when they occurred, with few mentioning preventive approaches, such as pelvic floor exercises. Public health campaigns should reframe UI, shifting the narrative towards prevention and treatment through early intervention.

Introduction

Women living with urinary incontinence (UI) can experience a decrease in quality of life through its effects on their general and mental health.1 2 UI is defined by the International Continence Society as the ‘complaint of the involuntary loss of urine’.3 Living with UI not only affects health but also carries a financial burden, with an estimated individual cost of £400–£700 per year for incontinence pads.4 There is a wider impact on the environment as incontinence products constitute 4.8% of municipal waste when disposed of.5 Use of incontinence pads is common and can be long-term, which may have negative impacts on skin integrity.6 Such coping strategies may also influence other health behaviours, particularly physical activity.

Physical activity is defined by the WHO as ‘any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that requires energy expenditure’.7 Physical activity improves both quality of life and general health and can help prevent and manage health conditions, such as osteoporosis.8 There is inconsistent evidence as to whether physical activity improves or worsens pelvic floor function.9 There is some evidence that high-impact activities may increase pressure on the pelvic floor, triggering UI. Further research supports taking part in moderate physical activity2 10 11 and reducing sedentary time,12 finding that these activities are associated with reduced UI and other pelvic floor dysfunctions (PFD). The greatest proportion of evidence on how urinary and pelvic symptoms affect physical activity focuses on athletes,9 13 but little research has been undertaken on the effect of UI and other PFD on physical activity and exercise participation in non-athlete women.

Despite the benefits of physical activity, UI has been found to be a barrier to exercise in up to half of non-athlete women.14–16 Evidence suggests that UI is a greater barrier for those on lower incomes and those with multiple PFD, including pelvic pain.17 18 Exercise and health professionals rarely screen for UI19 20 and, according to Gard and Lavallee,21 only about 10% of women voluntarily report UI symptoms that prevent participation in physical activity. Women have also reported a reduction in spontaneous physical activity due to PFD, because of the planning involved.22 Compounded by feelings of shame, UI can alter how a person engages in physical activity or can prevent physical activity participation altogether.17 As a result, it is likely that UI impacts negatively on women’s ability to meet physical activity and exercise guidelines.8

In order to understand the impact of UI on taking part in physical activity, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of UI among a cohort of women and people assigned female at birth working at or attending events at a university campus. In addition, we aimed to investigate associations between physical activity and UI levels, including an analysis of people’s descriptions using open-text responses.

Methods

Study design

A pragmatic stance23 was used in developing this research study. To support broad recruitment and allow for completely anonymised data collection, an online survey was used to collect cross-sectional data. It comprised questions relating to respondent characteristics, a combination of validated questionnaires and one open response question. The Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies was used.24

The survey was tested by the research team, who have relevant lived experience, to ensure technical functionality (eg, accessibility and navigation) and internal clarity, including readability and interpretation of the questions, before recruitment began. The Scottish Trans Alliance was consulted in relation to the criteria for participation and use of language.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Participants and recruitment

Women and people assigned female sex at birth were eligible to participate whether they experienced UI or not. Data collection took place over 16 months, with ongoing efforts to increase recruitment. Data collection was facilitated through Jisc,25 the UK’s digital data and technology agency, accessible via the authors’ institutional accounts. No personal data were collected by the survey. Compliance with General Data Protection Regulation and local data protection policies was ensured. To reduce barriers to participation,26 we did not restrict the number of times a survey could be completed from one IP address.

The survey link was widely circulated via social media, fliers and a pop-up banner placed at the university and at the local hospital. People could enter the survey hyperlink into their mobile phones or other electronic devices or scan a QR code to enable survey completion.

Participant information was provided at the start of the survey, followed by the first question relating to consent and agreement to the inclusion criteria; participants who did not provide consent were automatically excluded from the study. Potential participants were advised that if they might find the topic upsetting, it would be better not to participate. Those who chose to proceed were provided with a link to relevant resources about UI from the National Health Service, pelvic obstetric and gynaecological physiotherapy, the Squeezy App and emotional support if needed (Samaritans and Breathing Space). They were informed in the information section of the survey that they could exit the survey at any time prior to submission. Once submitted, however, their responses could not be withdrawn as it was anonymised (see supplementary materials—online supplemental appendix 1—participant information sheet).

An estimated sample size for prevalence studies was calculated according to Daniel.27 The calculation was based on an estimated prevalence of 33%, a recommended precision of 0.0528 and CI of 95%. The target sample size was n=340 participants.

Outcome measures

Following consent, participant demographic information, including gender (and whether they were transgender) and age, were explored. To keep the survey short to complete, we decided not to gather detailed data regarding location, educational level, parity or body composition; previous studies have demonstrated that UI is experienced by those from all backgrounds.16 18 29 Respondents then completed the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire-Urinary Incontinence Short Form (ICIQ-UI SF).30 The ICIQ-UI SF was developed to support international standardisation of psychometrically valid and reliable measurement, and was ‘highly recommended’ for use in assessing UI in a recent systematic review.31 Questions focused on the past 4 weeks, asking how often people leaked urine, how much this bothered them, how much urine leaked, how much this interfered with daily life and when this occurred (eg, on coughing or sneezing).

Following the ICIQ-UI SF, participants completed the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form (IPAQ-SF).32 This nine-item instrument was developed to enable standardised measurement of physical activity internationally. Craig and colleagues32 found acceptable repeatability, concurrent and criterion validity, equivalent to other such tools. Questions require participants to recall physical activity related to leisure time, domestic tasks, work and transportation over the last 7 days, discriminating between sitting, light (walking) and moderate or vigorous intensity activities.

In order to explore the linkages between physical activity participation and UI, three further questions were asked: two closed questions and one open. People were asked to rate on a 5-point Likert scale from ‘Strongly agree’ to ‘Strongly disagree’, including a neutral option ‘Neither agree or disagree’, whether leaking urine impacts their decision whether to participate in physical activity. The same Likert scale was then used when asking people whether, when doing physical activity, leaking urine affected their decision about whether to continue. The final question asked respondents to explain more about how leaking urine affected their participation in physical activity, using an open response format. The aim of adding the open response question was to enable participants to describe experiences that were not captured by the previous questions, or to expand their responses33 (see supplementary materials—online supplemental appendix 2—survey).

Open responses were carefully checked for any identifiable details and anonymised where disclosure occurred, or if comments were deemed potentially revealing.

Data analysis

In order to progress with data analysis, the anonymous data were downloaded from Jisc and saved in a secure data server at QMU.

Data were collected using the Jisc online survey software and downloaded as an Excel file. Numerical data were then uploaded to IBM SPSS Statistics V.31 for analysis. The ICIQ-UI SF contains 4 items and is scored from 0 to 21 (0 indicating no leakage and no impact; 21 indicates significant leakage with significant impact). The ICIQ-UI SF score was categorised, according to the guidelines,25 into: none (score 0), slight (score 1–5), moderate (score 6–12), severe (score 13–18), very severe (score 19–21). The IPAQ-SF was scored according to IPAQ Research Committee34 and categorised participants by quantity of activity (low, medium, high), providing an estimate of energy expenditure (metabolic equivalent of tasks (METs)) over the past 7 days. Exercise quantity is categorised as follows: low activity is <600 MET-minutes per week, medium activity is 600–3000 MET-minutes per week and high activity is >3000 MET-minutes per week. Exercise intensity is calculated as METs spent in each exercise intensity category: light (walking), moderate and vigorous. Time spent sitting is reported in hours. The ICIQ-UI SF values and METs were not normally distributed (Kolmogorov-Smirnov, p<0.001) and therefore values are reported as medians and IQR. Univariate associations between age, UI and physical activity were calculated using χ2. Differences in total METs between levels of UI were calculated using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Alpha was set at 0.05. Following the univariate analysis, ordinal logistic regression was carried out to model the relationship between urinary incontinence (dependent variable), age and physical activity. ORs were calculated for significant variables.

The open-text responses were analysed using a descriptive textual analysis approach.35 The purpose of this analysis was to capture and categorise participants’ descriptions of the experiences in their own words, beyond what was captured by the structured survey items. Initial categorisation of participants’ comments was carried out by three authors (BF, CB and LF), who developed draft response categories for all responses. Discussion between authors led to refinement of the response categories to remove overlap and clarify meaning. These were summarised in a table outlining and defining concepts. All free-text comments were then re-analysed using the finalised response categories, with discussion between authors to ensure agreement. This process supported an adapted intercoder reliability process, ensuring clarity, systematic analysis and transparency in the textual-coding and concept development.36 The number of participants contributing comments to each response category was then calculated and examples of participant quotes selected to provide evidence and illustration.

Quantitative results are presented alongside findings from open-text responses. Following a summary of participant characteristics, the results focus first on UI type, severity and perceived causes, then on physical activity and impacts of UI and finally on how people cope with UI.

Results

413 participants completed the survey between August 2022 and December 2023, exceeding the target sample size. 223 (56%) participants responded to question 19 with free-text comments.

The majority (n=403, 98%) of respondents reported as female and were aged between 25 and 44 (n=190, 46%).

Of the five participants who reported as male, two were transgender and included in the analysis. The remaining three were excluded from analysis; they were deemed not to meet inclusion criteria as their self-reported gender was male and they answered ‘no’ to the question ‘are you transgender?’. All four non-binary participants were included as they indicated they were assigned female at birth on agreeing to participate. One participant chose not to answer the question relating to gender; however, they were included as they had agreed to the inclusion criteria on entering the survey.

Urinary incontinence

76.8% (n=315) of respondents reported some UI. Of those who reported UI, the median ICIQ-UI SF score was 6.0 (IQR 6.0, range 1.0–21.0). A higher score indicates greater impairment as a result of UI.

When severity of UI was cross tabulated with age (table 1), there was a significant association (χ2=29.512, p=0.003), with older people more likely to report more severe UI.

Multivariate analysis

An ordinal regression analysis was undertaken to determine the association between level of UI, physical activity and age. The Pearson goodness-of-fit statistic showed an acceptable fit of the model (p=0.178). Only physical activity showed a positive relationship, with a low intensity of physical activity being associated with higher levels of UI (OR 2.26, 95% CI 1.38 to 3.71, p=0.001). This confirmed the univariate analysis. The Nagelkerke R2 value, however, was low (0.062), suggesting that other factors not accounted for in this model are also associated with UI.

The impact of UI on physical activity participation

Table 6 (see supplementary materials—online supplemental table 6) summarises the categories developed from open-text comments on the impact of UI on participation in physical activities. These categories highlight a range of experiences, including the activities and contexts that trigger UI, the severity of UI and its effect on physical activity participation. UI has a negative impact on people’s lives, often making them feel overly self-conscious, embarrassed and distressed by the experience. Additionally, other barriers may further prevent participation in physical activity.

Choices and strategies to reduce and cope with incontinence relating to physical activity

Table 7 (see supplementary materials—online supplemental table 7) provides comments about strategies adopted to cope with UI when it occurs, such as the use of absorbing pads or dark clothing to camouflage the signs of leaking, as well as stopping, pausing or adjusting the physical activity to reduce the risk of UI. Some comments demonstrate the type of interventions and strategies embraced to manage or prevent UI. These include non-invasive management strategies like pelvic floor exercise or supporting devices as well as invasive surgical intervention to stop leakage. Notably, far fewer responses relate to strategies used to prevent UI as compared with coping with UI.

Discussion

Our survey aimed to explore the link between UI and physical activity participation. Although univariate analysis indicated that UI was associated with both age and physical activity, no association was found between age and physical activity. Multivariate analysis confirmed that UI was more likely to be an explanatory factor in decreased physical activity rather than age. UI was reported in all age groups and gradually increased with age. Those with UI were also more likely to report lower quantity and intensity of physical activity. We acknowledge that as an observational study, caution must be made about drawing conclusions about cause and effect; however, clear connections between UI and physical activity participation are shown in the descriptive comments, as shown in Table 6 (see supplementary materials—online supplemental table 6).

Among survey respondents, 76.8% reported some UI; higher than the previously reported range of 25–50%.18 20 21 This higher rate may be explained by our recruitment strategy: the survey title (‘we are looking for women to complete our survey on incontinence and exercise’) highlighted UI and physical activity and may have attracted wider participation from those with UI, despite noting that those without UI were also welcome to take part. Dakic et al16 discuss a similar self-selection bias, while Gard and Lavallee21 deliberately did not disclose that their survey investigated UI during recruitment, and 189 (54.8%) of their 345 participants later agreed that UI was a barrier to physical activity participation.

Textual analysis of open responses shows that most participants were using strategies to cope with UI, rather than aiming to prevent it. Many of these coping strategies focused on containment products, consistent with findings from previous research.14 16 Our participants often viewed pads as the only available strategy. This is concerning, as 75% of pad users report vulvar skin irritation from their use6 and there are environmental implications from the disposal of plastic in the products.5

Reported strategies for coping with UI during physical activity—changing clothes or wearing only dark clothes, shortening activities, exercising at home or finding a nearby toilet—are likely to negatively affect physical activity frequency, duration and intensity. This was found in other studies13 18 21 and is likely to impact negatively on musculoskeletal health and increase early dependency on social care or family support. Given that most exercise, coupled with reducing sedentary time,12 has the potential to improve pelvic floor symptoms,2 10 11 37 promoting awareness of effective conservative treatments through multiple channels could improve outcomes for this group.

The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence recommends pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) as a first-line treatment.38 Yet only 5 of the 233 participants who completed the open response question mentioned PFMT and physiotherapy treatment as a strategy that they were using to improve their pelvic floor. This contrasts with previous research, where 84.6% of gym and physiotherapy clinical attendees agreed that they wanted to receive pelvic floor physiotherapy,14 a discrepancy which could be explained by our participants volunteering this information and Brennand et al14 asking directly and administering their survey in a physiotherapy clinical setting.

Although this was not a study of disclosure processes, the depth and detail of the open-text responses highlight the value of including free-text questions in surveys,33 enabling participants to describe how UI influenced physical activity in their own words. Participants reported a wide range of emotional impacts of UI in the open-text question, the extent of which we had not anticipated within a brief survey, demonstrating their readiness to share how UI affects their overall well-being. Emotional impacts include accounts of loss that align with the reported inability to participate in spontaneous physical activity, as documented by Dakic et al.22 Feelings of embarrassment, self-consciousness and diminished quality of life are consistent with findings reported in qualitative studies.17 22 39

A significant strength of the study was that responses were completely anonymous, enabling honesty in sharing experiences.40 Having a free-text response gave us insight into the nuanced experience of UI as a barrier to exercise.41 As our survey was short,42 the final open question may have given participants time to contribute the high number of free-text responses, which provided valuable insights warranting further exploration. The decision to keep the survey brief was informed by both methodological and ethical considerations, aiming to reduce participant burden and support accessibility, particularly given the sensitive nature of the topic. Shorter questionnaires have been recommended to enhance engagement and completion rates.43 While this limited the range of variables collected, it may also have reduced the depth and nuance of the data. This was considered a reasonable balance between depth of understanding and participant comfort, but may influence interpretation and the wider applicability of the findings. It is acknowledged that not collecting data on the socioeconomic background, geographical location and parity of participants limits the generalisability of the results.41 Although the survey contained validated questionnaires, the full survey was not piloted with persons from varied educational backgrounds, which may have made completion difficult for some participants. As some participants highlighted, our survey asked only about UI; some provided information regarding their experiences of other PFD in the free-text responses. The advertised focus of the survey on experiences of UI and physical activity is likely to have led to self-selection bias.41 To reduce barriers to survey completion, we did not restrict multiple responses, so we cannot be certain that there is only one response per participant.26

Public health exercise and physical activity messages are often generalised and overlook individual factors.44 Our research suggests that messages about UI are from adverts for pads. Healthcare and exercise professionals should adopt a person-centred approach, considering UI as a barrier to physical activity and reframing it as treatable. Innovative treatments for PFD, such as group delivery of pelvic floor muscle training education, could enhance management of UI and increase rates of physical activity.45

Future research should focus on enhancing public health messaging for a broad audience regarding managing and preventing UI and integrating pelvic health into wider health and fitness discussions.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study identified a high prevalence of UI, which was associated with lower intensity of physical activity in this sample. Participants’ descriptions of their experiences reflected this finding. Open-text responses showed that many women normalise symptoms and rely on coping strategies, such as pads, adjusting activities or avoiding exercise, rather than seeking preventive or therapeutic support. These behaviours may be contributing to some women not meeting recommended physical activity levels.
