ABSTRACT
Visual AbstractKey Words: average width, retina, vessel density

High-resolution, rapid, and noninvasive fundus imaging has become a cornerstone in ophthalmology, enabling direct visualization of the retinal microvasculature in vivo. Beyond its traditional applications, growing evidence suggests that fundus imaging can provide valuable insights into systemic health, offering a unique window into the body's microvascular status. With the advent of artificial intelligence (AI)–based segmentation techniques, it is now possible to quantitatively assess retinal vessel characteristics from standard fundus photographs. This capability has given rise to the emerging field of oculomics—the use of ocular biomarkers to infer systemic health status and disease risk.1

Several large-scale studies, including analyses from the UK Biobank (UKBB), have explored associations between retinal vascular features and systemic diseases, primarily focusing on cardiovascular conditions.2,3 These studies have demonstrated that fundus-derived features are linked with cardiovascular risk factors. Other studies have used deep learning from fundus images, in order to directly predict mortality risk and conditions such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and obesity.3,4 Some associations were also found in previous studies between fundus microvascular width, tortuosity, density or fractal dimension and obesity, blood pressure, smoking, sleep apnea, and blood glucose.2,5, 6, 7, 8, 9

These studies have demonstrated the potential for fundus microvascular measurements to serve as biomarkers for the prediction and diagnosis of systemic diseases. However, despite these promising findings, previous studies are limited in several significant ways. Many focus narrowly on a small number of risk factors or retinal features, often using heterogeneous populations with existing clinical diagnoses. In addition, most rely on semiautomated methods or models requiring manual tuning, limiting their scalability and reproducibility. Crucially, there remains a lack of large-scale analyses in truly healthy populations, which are essential to define normative reference values and explore microvascular correlates across the full spectrum of subclinical health traits.

In light of the above limitations, the advantages of using fundus imaging at scale for monitoring and identifying systemic health conditions are clear. Accordingly, we analyzed fundus images from 8,467 healthy individuals enrolled in the Human Phenotype Project (HPP), a deeply phenotyped population-based study conducted in Israel between 2019 and 2023.10 We applied AutoMorph, an AI-based pipeline for fully automated artery-vein segmentation and vessel measurement extraction.11 AutoMorph combines deep learning with classical computer vision techniques to generate 12 quantitative vessel features, including tortuosity, vessel density, average width, and fractal dimension, calculated separately for arteries and veins. Unlike prior tools such as QUARTZ, AutoMorph operates entirely without manual input and has been validated across multiple independent data sets.11

In this study, we present the distribution of retinal vascular features in a large healthy population and provide age- and sex-specific reference values, establishing a digital vascular nomogram. We further examine associations between these vascular traits and a wide array of clinical parameters, including cardiovascular markers, lipid profiles, sleep apnea indices (from home-based sleep studies), glycemic control from continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), body composition metrics, and smoking status. To validate our findings, we replicated the retinal-vascular clinical associations in independent fundus images from UKBB participants. Finally, leveraging the long-term follow-up available in UKBB, we evaluated the prognostic performance of retinal vascular features for future cardiovascular events using time-to-event models, demonstrating incremental predictive value beyond standard clinical covariates. This comprehensive analysis provides a valuable resource for future oculomics research and supports the potential of retinal imaging as a noninvasive tool for early detection and risk assessment of systemic diseases.

Methods

HPP cohort description

The data were collected as part of the HPP, a large perspective, longitudinal study. Retinal imaging was performed using the iCare DRSplus nonmydriatic confocal fundus camera, acquiring 45° fundus photographs with a device resolution of 80 pixels/degree. Images were processed using an AutoMorph-based pipeline including preprocessing, image quality grading, vessel segmentation, artery/vein segmentation, optic disc segmentation, and extraction of retinal vascular morphology features. Vascular measurements, including width-related and morphology-derived metrics, were obtained from the segmented vessels in image pixel space. No participant-specific ocular magnification correction was applied, as axial length and related ocular biometry were not incorporated into the pipeline; therefore, width-related measures should be interpreted as relative image-based estimates rather than absolute anatomically corrected vessel calibers. In addition, retinal vascular metrics were computed over the entire analyzable segmented field of the processed fundus image rather than being restricted to a predefined optic disc–centered or peripapillary zone. Although optic disc segmentation was performed, it was not used here to define the spatial region for feature extraction. Images from 160 participants were classified as poor quality during AutoMorph processing and did not generate usable features. These participants were excluded from the analysis. Quality-based exclusion was based solely on the AutoMorph quality classification.

Body composition measures were obtained by Dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scan. Body measurements including body mass index (BMI), fat composition and mass, body circumferences, and blood pressure liver ultrasound and carotid doppler measurements were collected on the same day. CGM and sleep data (providing sleep apnea and oxygen desaturation indices over 3 nights) were collected in close proximity to the images. Additional clinical data including previous eye conditions and smoking status were collected by the use of electronic surveys as previously described.10 Participants were asked to provide recent and past results of blood tests, including complete blood count, hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c), creatinine, glucose, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and triglycerides.

HPP study population

On arrival at the research site, all participants provided informed consent in accordance with the guidelines set forth by the Declaration of Helsinki. To protect participant confidentiality, any identifying information was removed before the computational analysis. The HPP study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Weizmann Institute of Science. Between February 17, 2021, and March 28, 2023, a total of 9,450 participants in the HPP had fundus images available, for extraction of vascular measures. Participants with reported diagnoses of glaucoma, cataract, and retinal detachments or breaks were excluded from the study population, along with those with invalid images. Consequently, a total of 8,467 individuals were included in the present study. Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the individuals that had at least one eligible fundus measure.

Continuous glucose monitoring

CGM-derived measures from Abbott are cumulative glycemic features, calculated with the R package iglu,12 that were obtained from a 2-week-long CGM. We decided to focus on 2 measurements: estimated A1C, which is a transformation of the mean glucose level, and coefficient of variation (CV), the CV of all glucose values.

Sleep measures

The monitoring of sleep in the HPP uses the Food and Drug Administration–approved WatchPAT-300 from Itamar Medical, which uses 5 sensors for data collection: a wrist-worn actigraph, a finger-worn pulse oximeter and peripheral arterial tone (PAT) probe, and a chest-worn microphone and accelerometer to measure respiratory effort, snoring, and body position. The PAT probe measures changes in the volume of blood vessels by applying consistent pressure to the finger and capturing changes in displaced volume flow using a photoplethysmogram sensor. The PAT signal reflects alterations in the autonomic nervous system triggered by respiratory disturbances during sleep. The autonomic nervous system is responsible for controlling essential functions such as blood vessel size and regulation of blood pressure, lung airflow, and the electrical activity and contractility of the heart.

The WatchPAT's automatic algorithm analyzes the PAT signal amplitude alongside heart rate and oxygen saturation to detect and classify breathing problems during sleep, providing 2 indices for sleep apnea diagnosis: the apnea/hypopnea index (AHI) and respiratory disturbance index (RDI). The algorithm uses specific signal patterns to calculate these indices, when minimal oxygen desaturation for AHI and RDI events is 3%. The snoring sensor helps clinicians determine if respiratory events are obstructive, while the body position sensor aids in identifying if sleep apnea has a positional component. Additionally, the pulse oximeter calculates the oxygen desaturation index (ODI) by counting the number of detected desaturation events per hour of sleep (minimal desaturation: 4%). The accuracy of these algorithms was validated using gold-standard polysomnography sleep monitoring.13,14 Sleep staging was determined by combining data from multiple sensors, while sleep/wake detection relied on actigraph data recorded by the device. The accuracy of these algorithms was validated using the gold standard, manually scored polysomnography.

Liver ultrasound measures

Using the Supersonic Aixplorer Mach 30 ultrasound device, we obtain speed of sound measurement and elasticity measurement: the lower the sound speed value, the higher the fat content,14 and hence we use the negative sound speed in the analysis to provide positive correlation between the measure (negative sound speed) and fat content. Liver elasticity (based on 2D-ShearWave Elastography) is measured in kPa from ultrasound and serves as an indicator for fibrosis (high values correspond to stiffness and fibrosis).

UKBB cohort description

The UKBB is a population-based cohort of approximately 500,000 participants recruited from 2006 to 2010 with extensive longitudinal phenotypic data and a median follow-up of approximately 10 years.2 Retinal fundus imaging at enrollment was performed using the TOPCON 3D OCT-1000 Mk2, which acquired a volumetric (3D) retinal scan together with a high-resolution fundus photograph. For analyzing the UKBB data, we used 32,498 retinal images from 16,249 UKBB participants (both eyes per participant). Images were subjected to automated quality control and preprocessing, and only scans meeting predefined quality thresholds were included. Body composition measures including BMI, fat mass, and body circumferences were collected, in addition to blood pressure and blood tests for lipids (triglycerides, HDL, LDL, and total cholesterol), glucose, and HbA1c. Supplemental Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the individuals from UKBB.

UKBB study population

To externally validate findings from the HPP cohort, we repeated the primary association and prediction analyses in a healthy subset of UKBB participants. We defined the healthy subset as participants aged 40-70 years with available baseline fundus images and successful AutoMorph vessel analysis (N = 33,791). We excluded individuals with baseline cardiovascular disease (as defined in Supplemental Table 2; N = 12,395) and those with type 2 diabetes at baseline (HbA1c >6.5%; additional N = 478). The final analysis cohort comprised 20,918 participants.

All available statistical models and covariate adjustments applied to the HPP were replicated in this UKBB healthy validation set. Consequently, a total of 16,249 individuals were included in the present study. Supplemental Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the individuals with fundus measure.

Cardiovascular disease outcome definition

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) events were identified using UKBB Field 20003 (treatment/medication codes), which records medications and treatments reported by participants during assessment center visits. CVD was defined as the presence of any of the 12 conditions listed in Supplemental Table 2, identified by their corresponding self-reported medication/treatment codes mapped to equivalent International Classification of Diseases-Tenth Revision categories.

Participants were classified as having incident CVD if any of the above codes appeared in their treatment/medication records at follow-up assessment, but not at baseline.

Follow-up structure in UKBB

UKBB participants underwent baseline assessment between 2006 and 2010 (N = 20,918; 100%). Follow-up data were collected at up to 3 repeat assessment visits: repeat visit 1 (2012-2013) included 3,537 participants (16.9%) with a mean follow-up of 4.4 years; repeat visit 2 (2014-2022) included 3,609 participants (17.3%) with a mean follow-up of 8.5 years; and repeat visit 3 (2019-2022) included 363 participants (1.7%) with a mean follow-up of 11.2 years. A total of 1,417 participants (6.77%) developed cardiovascular events during follow-up.

Statistical analysis

We used the AutoMorph package to calculate 6 different vessel measures: tortuosity density, vessel density, average width, fractal dimension, distance tortuosity, and curvature tortuosity (which was taken as the square root of the provided squared curvature tortuosity measure). These measures were calculated separately for veins and arteries (total of 12, described below). We used the square root of the squared curvature tortuosity instead of the raw value to draw its distribution nearer to the normal distribution. Each of the 6 measures was calculated for each eye separately, and we are using the mean value of both eyes in our analysis. Continuous data are presented as the mean (standard deviation) or count (percentage) unless otherwise indicated. For participants who had more than one imaging, analysis was performed on the first acquired image.

Inter- and intracoefficients of variation in fundus measures were used to evaluate the association between the left and right eyes and within each eye. In all measures except the tortuosity measures, the intravariance is significantly lower than the intervariance. In tortuosity measures the typical intravalues are also lower than the intervalues, but to a lesser extent. The lower intravariance in all the measures enables the use of the mean value of left and right eyes. The mean difference between the eyes of an individual is up to 2.5% in all of the extracted measures. In 95% of the participants, the difference between the eyes in the fractal dimension is below 4%, and in average width, vessel density, and tortuosity density below 13%. Distance tortuosity differences are much higher, and 95% are lower than only 89% (Supplemental Figure 1).

Pearson correlation coefficients (r) between these measures and other clinical features, adjusted for age, sex, and smoking, were calculated using the Python Pingouin package and were corrected for multihypothesis using Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction. The percentiles shown in the age-sex reference plots were calculated with a Lowess smoother, using the tsmoothie Python package. We next chose 50 measures that were previously shown to be related to the fundus microvascular structure and are measured as a part of the HPP cohort, including anthropometrics and blood pressure measurements, glucose indicators, thyroid functions, lipid profile, smoking status, measures related to sleep disorders from sleep monitoring, and diagnosis of eyes conditions. Only blood tests that were taken in the range of 11 months before the imaging to 3 months after it were included in the analyses. Smoking status and obstructive sleep apnea data were obtained from questionnaires filled by participants at study initiation. All other measures were measured as part of the HPP cohort, the majority being evaluated on the same day.

The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was calculated for incident CVD predictive models. To evaluate the prognostic value of AutoMorph retinal features we built 2 binary CVD classifiers in the UKBB subset of participants without baseline CVD or type 2 diabetes (N = 20,918). The baseline model included 4 clinical covariates: age (continuous, years), sex (binary), hypertension (binary, defined as systolic ≥140 mm Hg or diastolic ≥90 mm Hg), and total cholesterol (continuous, mmol/L).

The AutoMorph model added 83 quantitative retinal vessel features (extracted with AutoMorph) to the same 4 clinical covariates. These include measures of vessel density, average width, tortuosity (distance, squared curvature, and density), fractal dimension, arteriovenous ratio, central retinal artery equivalent, central retinal vein equivalent, and cup-to-disc ratio, computed separately for arterial and venous vasculature across multiple retinal zones.

Participants were split once into a training (n = 14,670) and testing (n = 3,668) data set, stratified by outcome (random seed = 42). All preprocessing was estimated on the training set and applied to the test set. Hyperparameter tuning for the XGBoost classifier (binary logistic objective) was performed by stratified 5-fold cross-validation on the training set using grid search optimization for area under the ROC curve (AUC) as the selection metric; early stopping was applied during training to prevent overfitting. To account for outcome imbalance we used XGBoost’s built-in class-weighting (scale_pos_weight) when appropriate. Model discrimination was assessed on the testing data set using the AUC with 95% confidence interval (CI). Feature scaling was applied to all continuous features using StandardScaler.

Results

Participant characteristics and study design

Fundus images from 9,450 participants in the HPP were initially processed. Images deemed invalid by the automated pipeline were excluded, as were images from individuals who did not meet the study’s inclusion criteria (see “HPP study population” in the Methods section). After quality control, 8,467 individuals were included in the final analysis, 52.8% female and 47.2% male, with a mean age of 52.4 ± 7.6 years and an average BMI of 26.1 ± 4.1 kg/m2 (Table 1). Retinal vascular measurements were derived using the AutoMorph package, which provides fully automated quantification of 12 vessel parameters separately for arteries and veins. Comprehensive clinical data were collected as part of the HPP protocol, including vital signs, anthropometrics, blood biomarkers, body composition by DXA, CGM, and home-based sleep monitoring over 3 nights. From these data sets, we selected 50 clinical variables, categorized into 5 domains for association analysis: 1) body composition; 2) cardiovascular measures; 3) blood lipid profile; 4) sleep parameters; and 5) glucose-related measures.

To validate these findings, we replicated the analysis in UKBB fundus images, using 16,249 retinal images from 16,249 healthy participants. In the UKBB validation cohort, we tested associations between fundus-derived vascular features and the same clinical domains that were available in UKBB: 1) body composition; 2) cardiovascular measures; 3) blood lipid profile; and 4) glucose-related measures, and observed for the majority of the findings consistent directions and comparable effect sizes. Baseline characteristics of the UKBB validation cohort are presented in Supplemental Table 1.

Reference values for retinal vessel measures

Twelve distinct retinal vessel measures were computed for each eligible participant in the study cohort, with values calculated separately for arteries and veins. These measures are detailed in Table 2 and include: 1) vessel density, the proportion of the image area occupied by blood vessels; 2) average vessel width; 3) fractal dimension, a measure of the geometric complexity of the vascular network; and 4) 3 tortuosity metrics, quantifying the curvature or "twistiness" of vessels, including distance tortuosity, curvature tortuosity, and tortuosity density. To establish normative values, percentiles for each measure were estimated using locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOWESS) regression, both for the entire cohort and stratified by age and sex.

Interrelationships among fundus vessel measures

To explore the associations among the 12 extracted fundus vessel measures, we computed pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients and applied hierarchical clustering to identify groups of related features (Supplemental Figure 2). A correlation threshold of ±0.7 was used to define clusters, consistent with first- and second-order hierarchical groupings. This analysis yielded 7 distinct clusters: vessel density and fractal dimension, artery average width, vein average width, artery tortuosity density, artery distance and curvature tortuosity, vein tortuosity density and vein distance, and curvature tortuosity.

Measures with similar vascular properties naturally clustered together—for example, vessel density with fractal dimension, and tortuosity density with distance tortuosity—highlighting shared structural characteristics. For downstream analyses, we selected one representative measure from each cluster. An exception was made for artery vessel density and artery fractal dimension, which, despite a high correlation (r = 0.88), are frequently studied independently in the literature and were therefore both retained. Given the conceptual overlap among the 3 tortuosity metrics, previously compared in other studies,15 we included only tortuosity density as the representative measure for both arteries and veins. Nevertheless, all measures were included in multiple hypothesis correction to ensure statistical rigor. As a result, the 6 vascular features selected for detailed analysis were artery vessel density, artery fractal dimension, average artery width, average vein width, artery tortuosity density, and vein tortuosity density. P values were calculated for all vessel measures, and adjustments for multiple testing were applied across the full set.

Association of fundus vessel measures with age

To investigate age-related changes in retinal vascular features, we calculated age-specific percentiles across the entire study cohort (Figure 2). Our findings revealed distinct age-associated trajectories across the different fundus vessel measures. Over half of the analyzed metrics demonstrated significant correlations with age, with absolute correlation coefficients exceeding 0.10 (Figures 1 and 2, Supplemental Figure 3).

Among the most pronounced age-related changes were reductions in arterial complexity and caliber. Arterial fractal dimension decreased with age (r = –0.17, P < 10–35), as did arterial average width (r = –0.20, P < 10–87) and arterial vessel density (r = –0.25, P < 10–88). Notably, the rate of decline varied across these measures. The reduction in arterial average width was steeper between ages 40 and 50 years and became more gradual in later decades. In contrast, the declines in arterial density and fractal dimension appeared to accelerate with advancing age, suggesting progressive rarefaction and simplification of the arteriolar network over time. In contrast to these declining trends, vascular tortuosity increased with age (Figure 1). Both arterial and venous tortuosity densities were positively correlated with age (arteries: r = 0.11, P < 10–18; veins: r = 0.09, P < 10–13; Figure 2), indicating age-associated increases in vascular curvature and complexity. These patterns were observed consistently across sexes, although the specific rates of change differed between males and females, as detailed in Supplemental Figure 3.

Association of Fundus Vessel Measures with Sex

Several fundus vascular parameters also demonstrated significant sex-based differences (Figure 2). Arterial fractal dimension was significantly higher in males (P < 10–23). Conversely, both arterial and venous average widths were significantly greater in females (P < 10–80 and P < 10–53, respectively), as were arterial and venous tortuosity densities (P < 10–22 and P < 10–11, respectively). Arterial vessel density was the only 1 of the 6 core fundus measures that did not show a significant difference between sexes, indicating a potential sex-invariant characteristic of microvascular density (Figures 1 and 2).

Body composition

Higher adiposity (BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, and percent body fat) was associated with lower artery vessel density and correlated negatively with artery fractal dimension. Both BMI and waist-to-hip ratio were also associated with smaller artery average width. By contrast, vein average width was positively correlated with most body-composition metrics. Liver sound speed (a marker of hepatic steatosis) was similarly negatively associated with arterial density and arterial average width. Liver elasticity showed negative correlation to the vein average width without additional significant associations with the fundus metrics.

Cardiovascular measures

In the cardiovascular category, systolic and diastolic blood pressure measures and carotid intima-media thickness exhibited a coherent set of correlations: lower artery fractal dimension, lower artery and vein average width, and lower artery vessel density correlated with higher blood pressure, while arterial and venous tortuosity density correlated positively with blood pressure.

Blood lipids

In the lipids category, which includes blood-based biomarkers, HDL cholesterol was positively associated with arterial average width and artery vessel density. Total cholesterol displayed a selective positive association with venous average width. Triglycerides showed a bidirectional pattern: negatively correlated with arterial density and arterial width but positively correlated with venous average width. LDL cholesterol did not show robust associations with the fundus measures.

Sleep/sleep-disordered breathing

In the sleep category, we analyzed correlations with mean sleep pulse rate, ODI, RDI, and AHI, all obtained from continuous sleep monitoring during a 3-night home sleep apnea test, and self-reported diagnosis of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). Artery vessel density, fractal dimension, and artery average width were all negatively correlated with all the analyzed measures. Vein average width showed opposite correlation patterns, being positively correlated with mean pulse rate during sleep (PRS) and OSA.

Glucose-related measures

In the glucose category, artery vessel density and artery average width were negatively associated with glucose and HbA1c measurements. Similar patterns were shown in estimated A1C. Both vein and artery tortuosity density were positively correlated with glucose measurements.

External replication in UKBB

To assess reproducibility, we repeated the primary partial-correlation analysis (adjusted for age, sex, and smoking) in a healthy subset of UKBB participants (excluding recorded type 2 diabetes or CVD at baseline) with available fundus images (Supplemental Figure 5), and additionally adjusted for eGFR (Supplemental Figure 6). The UKBB results reproduced the direction of the majority of associations observed in the HPP cohort and therefore support the robustness of the principal retinal-metabolic and retinal-cardiovascular signals (Supplemental Figures 5 and 7).

At the domain level, patterns were highly consistent between cohorts. In the body composition domain, higher adiposity (BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, and percent body fat) was again associated with lower arterial vessel density, reduced artery fractal dimension, and smaller artery average width; conversely, vein average width tended to correlate positively with adiposity measures. In the cardiovascular domain, elevated blood pressure measures showed the same coherent pattern as in the HPP: arterial density and fractal complexity were inversely related to blood pressure burden, whereas arterial and venous tortuosity densities were positively associated with higher blood pressure.

Lipid associations were directionally concordant: HDL positively correlated with arterial density and arterial width in both cohorts, and triglycerides exhibited the inverse pattern (negative with arterial metrics and positive with venous width). LDL and total cholesterol showed weak and less consistent relationships in both data sets. Finally, glucose-related measures (fasting glucose and HbA1c) displayed the same directional relationships: higher glucose measures were modestly associated with lower arterial density/fractal metrics and with greater tortuosity; these correlations were present but generally smaller in magnitude in UKBB.

Although directionality was broadly preserved, effect sizes in UKBB were larger relative to HPP, and a few more associations reached nominal significance. Overall, the primary associations between retinal vascular metrics and clinical traits were reproduced in an independent, population-based cohort, underscoring the robustness of these signals (Supplemental Figure 7).

Predictive performance of retinal features

To assess the data set potential to predict CVD development (identified in any of the follow-up stages 1-3) we evaluated 2 modeling approaches: 1) a baseline model that was trained with 4 standard clinical risk factors: age, sex, hypertension, and total cholesterol; 2) a full “Automoph model” that used the former 4 risk factors augmented with the full AutoMorph retinal feature set. On the test set, the trained baseline model obtained an ROC AUC of 0.567 (95% CI: 0.533-0.605), whereas the “Automorph model” achieved an ROC AUC of 0.841 (95% CI: 0.816-0.866) (Figure 4). That is, the Automorph model demonstrated substantially improved discriminative ability compared to baseline clinical factors alone, with 48% relative improvement (ΔAUC = +0.274). This substantial improvement demonstrates that retinal vascular features contain significant CVD risk information beyond standard clinical factors, implying that retinal microvasculature reflects systemic vascular health. CVD prevalence was 6.7% (1,417 of 20,918 participants). These results also suggest that AutoMorph-derived vascular metrics specifically provide meaningful retinal microvasculature information.

Discussion

The HPP includes high-resolution retinal imaging that represents a key advancement in phenotypic characterization, offering a noninvasive window into systemic microvascular health. The availability of fundus photographs from deeply phenotyped individuals within the HPP cohort enables the extraction of detailed quantitative vascular features. These measures, when integrated with the extensive clinical and lifestyle data collected within HPP, allow for robust cross-modal analyses of aging, cardiometabolic risk, and early disease biomarkers. Importantly, the diversity and depth of the HPP data set support the exploration of interindividual and population-level variation in retinal microvasculature, helping to uncover subtle but clinically meaningful associations that would be difficult to detect in less comprehensive or cross-sectional data sets.16

In this study, we present a comprehensive analysis of fundus vascular characteristics in a large, diverse cohort of over 8,400 unique individuals, using an automated, open-source pipeline. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale investigation to extract and evaluate 12 distinct retinal vascular measures automatically, providing a foundational reference for future research into ocular biomarkers of systemic health. The availability of the AutoMorph tool11 for public use further enhances the potential translational value of our findings by allowing replication, comparison, and implementation in broader clinical and research settings.

To assess reproducibility, we replicated the HPP associations in a healthy subset of UKBB participants. The UKBB validation reproduced the directionality of the principal retinal-metabolic and retinal-cardiovascular signals, supporting the robustness of the findings across cohorts and imaging platforms. In addition, we leveraged the long follow-up in UKBB to evaluate the prognostic performance of fundus-derived features for incident cardiovascular events: models that incorporated AutoMorph retinal metrics improved risk discrimination beyond basic clinical covariates. Together, the HPP discovery and UKBB replication/prediction analyses demonstrate both the reproducibility and the translational potential of automated retinal vascular phenotyping for systemic cardiometabolic risk assessment.

Our analysis revealed substantial intercorrelations among the 12 fundus vascular indices, allowing us to group them into 6 main categories and focus on representative measures. We identified meaningful associations between these measures and demographic variables (age and sex), metabolic health indicators (BMI, fat percentage, and liver steatosis), cardiovascular risk factors (blood pressure and carotid intima-media thickness), glucose measures (HbA1c, glucose, eA1c, and CV), and sleep apnea severity indices (AHI, ODI, RDI). These findings establish a detailed vascular phenotype of the retina and its systemic correlates, which could be valuable for both clinical risk stratification and mechanistic studies.

A key finding of our study is the correlation between fundus vessels features and aging. We found consistent decline in artery fractal dimension and artery vessel density with age, highlighting a gradual simplification of the vascular tree and reduced microvascular perfusion. These findings are aligned with previous smaller studies.17, 18, 19 Arterial average width also decreased with age, possibly due to structural remodeling, accumulation of fatty deposits and the development of plaque and stiffening of the arteries.20 Furthermore, venular tortuosity density increased with age, suggesting adaptive or pathological vascular responses to aging. These divergent patterns support the utility of distinct vascular measures in reflecting specific physiological and pathological changes. Our analysis did not find significant association in venous width with age, in line with other previous reports.21,22 We also observed gender-specific differences in fundus vascular measures. Males exhibited significantly higher arterial fractal dimension, indicating greater structural complexity in the arteriolar network. In contrast, females showed significantly greater average arterial and venous widths, as well as higher arterial and venous tortuosity densities, suggesting increased vascular curvature in the female retinal circulation.

In addition, we found distinct associations between retinal fundus measures and systemic health indicators. Arterial tortuosity density was positively associated with blood pressure measurements and diagnosis of essential hypertension as well as with cardiovascular risk factors, such as carotid IMT and CV index derived from CGM. These associations are supported by previous studies that reported links between increased tortuosity and elevated blood pressure, although methods for measuring tortuosity varied.3,23 Increased fundus vascular tortuosity may reflect heightened blood flow and angiogenesis.24 A similar pattern was demonstrated with venous tortuosity density, which was also positively correlated with increased blood pressure, carotid IMT and CV index. Furthermore, venous tortuosity density was positively associated with OSA, a finding which is aligned with previous smaller-scale studies.25

Arterial average width, on the contrary, was negatively correlated with blood pressure and essential hypertension, body composition indicators (waist-to-hip ratio, body fat percentage, and BMI), carotid IMT, triglycerides, glucose, and eA1c, as well as with all sleep parameters and liver steatosis. These findings are aligned with studies showing that narrower fundus arterioles are associated with increased blood pressure and metabolic disturbances, including nonalcoholic fatty liver disease.26 Arteriolar narrowing has also been shown to strongly correlate with acutely increased blood pressure levels.27 Furthermore, artery vessel density and artery fractal dimension were negatively correlated with glucose levels, glucose variability (CV), and triglycerides and positively associated with HDL suggesting that microvascular narrowing may accompany dysglycemia and dyslipidemia. This supports prior observations linking narrower arterioles with an increased risk of diabetes onset.28 Arterial vessel width and artery fractal dimension were also inversely associated with OSA as well as with all other sleep indices (RDI, ODI, and AHI), consistent with prior findings showing the association of retinal arteriolar narrowing with increased severity of OSA.29 Body composition measurements of tissue fat, waist/hip, BMI, and liver steatosis as well as other cardiovascular risk factors such as essential hypertension and carotid IMT were also negatively correlated with artery vessel density and artery fractal dimension.

Venous average width was negatively correlated with direct blood pressure measurements, albeit to a lesser extent than its arterial counterpart. Interestingly, unlike arterial width, which consistently showed negative associations with both blood pressure and body composition measures, venous width exhibited a divergent pattern. It was positively correlated with several markers of adiposity, including BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, and liver steatosis. This contrast is particularly noteworthy, as most other retinal vascular parameters demonstrated consistent correlation directions across cardiovascular risk domains. Consistent with its relationship to body composition, venous average width was also positively correlated with sleep-disordered breathing indices, including the ODI, AHI, and mean pulse rate. These findings further highlight a distinct physiological profile for venular versus arteriolar morphology. Despite a moderate correlation between arterial and venous width (r = 0.67), the 2 measures showed opposing association patterns in several domains: for example, BMI and liver steatosis were negatively associated with arterial width but positively associated with venous width; similarly, ODI and AHI were inversely related to arterial width but positively associated with venous width. Several mechanisms may underlie the venous dilation observed in metabolic conditions. Dyslipidemia, a hallmark of both metabolic syndrome and hyperglycemia, can lead to endothelial dysfunction and impaired vascular tone regulation, contributing to venular dilation.30 Obesity may also predispose to venous stasis due to increased intra-abdominal pressure and altered hemodynamics, further promoting venous widening.31

The predictive value of retinal vessel caliber for cardiovascular events has been well documented. The Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study—a large U.S. cohort of over 10,000 participants—was among the first to report that narrower arterioles, reflected by a lower arteriole-to-venule ratio, predicted increased 3-year risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) events, particularly in women. The Blue Mountains Eye Study (n = 3,654) demonstrated that wider venules independently predicted 9-year risk of CHD death in individuals without pre-existing CHD, while narrower arterioles were predictive in women only.32,33 Furthermore, a meta-analysis encompassing 21,428 participants confirmed that narrower arterioles and wider venules were both associated with increased CHD risk in women, but not in men.34

Arterial vessel density exhibited the strongest absolute correlation (among all fundus parameters) with both blood pressure and body composition measurements, showing consistently negative associations across all variables, except for liver elasticity, which did not show a significant correlation with any of the fundus measures. Notably, arterial vessel density also had one of the two highest correlations with each of the sleep apnea indices (AHI, ODI, RDI, and mean pulse rate), all demonstrating an inverse relationship, that is, sleep index severity decreased as vessel density increased. This pattern was similarly reported in a prior small-scale study focused on obstructive sleep apnea.25 In addition, arterial vessel density was negatively correlated with mean glucose levels measured by CGM, further supporting its potential as a noninvasive indicator of systemic health. Arterial fractal dimension was highly correlated with arterial vessel density, and the 2 measures shared many similar correlation patterns, including consistent negative associations with sleep indices (AHI and RDI), triglycerides, cardiovascular measures, and body composition metrics. Both were also positively correlated with smoking status. However, vessel density demonstrated stronger associations across all indices and was uniquely associated with glucose, a correlation not observed for fractal dimension.

Zekavat et al35 presented an important large-scale investigation of retinal vascular traits using UKBB data, applying deep-learning segmentation to derive vessel-caliber measures (arteriolar and venular diameters) and linking those traits to cardiometabolic phenotypes and genetic architecture. Their paper established clear genetic signals for caliber measures and helped position retinal caliber as a heritable biomarker for systemic cardiovascular risk. Our study complements and extends that work in several meaningful ways. First, the population and phenotyping differ: whereas Zekavat et al leveraged the scale and genetic depth of UKBB, we analyzed high-resolution retinal images from the HPP, a deeply phenotyped cohort with contemporaneous metabolic, biochemical, and physiological measurements (CGM, DXA, home sleep testing, etc). This richer phenotypic context allows us to directly relate retinal vascular morphology to a broad set of proximal metabolic and organ-level markers that are not uniformly available in population biobanks. Second, the analytical focus is complementary. Zekavat et al35 emphasized the genetic architecture of vessel caliber; by contrast, our primary goal was to establish normative distributions for a broader set of retinal vascular metrics and to define their clinical correlates in a nondiabetic population. This difference in emphasis means the 2 studies address different translational questions: Zekavat et al map inherited determinants and loci, while our work maps phenotypic variability and potential clinical utility for cardiometabolic risk stratification. Third, our feature scope is substantially broader. In addition to caliber (average width), we quantify branching geometry, vessel density, fractal complexity, tortuosity metrics, and squared-curvature measures across arteries and veins (12 measures in total). These features probe microvascular structure and network topology rather than only lumen size and therefore likely capture complementary pathophysiological processes (eg, microvascular rarefaction, remodeling, and geometric reorganization) that are not fully represented by caliber alone.

Taken together, our findings, replicated in UKBB, underscore robust inverse associations between arterial width, vessel density, and fractal dimension and a range of cardiometabolic and cardiovascular traits. Importantly, we also evaluated the prognostic utility of AutoMorph-derived retinal features in the independent UK Biobank cohort and found that inclusion of these automated fundus measures improved prediction of incident cardiovascular events beyond basic clinical covariates. These results point to the promising potential of retinal vascular metrics as noninvasive indicators for early detection and monitoring of systemic health conditions. Fundus imaging, therefore, holds significant promise as a biomarker platform in cardiovascular and metabolic disease assessment. This demonstration of incremental discrimination in a large, population-based cohort complements our cross-sectional association results and supports the translational potential of retinal vascular phenotyping for risk stratification.

The strengths of this study lie in its size, methodological rigor, and breadth of systemic data integration. First, it leverages a uniquely large and well-characterized cohort of over 8,400 individuals, which enhances statistical power and generalizability. Second, it employs a fully automated and reproducible analysis pipeline (AutoMorph), which enables standardized extraction of a comprehensive set of vascular indices. Third, the study integrates rich clinical data, including continuous glucose monitoring, sleep apnea indices, and liver imaging, allowing for multidimensional correlation analyses. This holistic approach not only strengthens the validity of our findings but also sets the stage for future work aimed at uncovering mechanistic links and clinical applications of retinal biomarkers.

The study has several limitations: the first limitation of this study is the reliance on automated image processing without manual quality control. Although we performed a targeted manual check of AutoMorph’s artery/vein and main-branch identification on a sample of images and observed high concordance with the automated overlays. AutoMorph was not fully re-validated on the entire HPP data set, so residual segmentation/classification errors may modestly reduce the precision of individual vascular measurements. Similarly, even though key findings were validated in UKBB, not all clinical parameters collected in HPP are available in UKBB; consequently, several HPP-retinal associations could not be tested for replication, which may limit the comprehensiveness of our external validation. Moreover, this cross-sectional study cannot establish causal relationships between retinal features and systemic health. Longitudinal studies will be required to assess whether changes in fundus measures precede or follow systemic disease development. Finally, while our cohort is large, it may not be fully representative of diverse global populations, which could limit external generalizability; additionally, the relatively low performance of the baseline clinical model (AUC 0.567) likely reflects the low-risk, preselected UKBB population and the inclusion of non-atherosclerotic outcomes, both of which reduce the discriminatory capacity of traditional risk factors. Notably, even in this challenging setting, retinal vessel features demonstrated robust discrimination (AUC 0.80-0.82) with substantial incremental value (ΔAUC + 0.25-0.31).36

Conclusions

In this large population-based study, we demonstrate that fully automated, quantitative retinal vascular features capture meaningful signatures of systemic cardiometabolic health. Distinct retinal microvascular patterns, including reduced arterial density and fractal complexity, altered vessel caliber, and increased tortuosity, were consistently associated with age, sex, adiposity, blood pressure, lipid profiles, sleep-disordered breathing, and glucose dysregulation. These associations were robust across arterial and venous compartments, showed coherent domain-specific patterns, and were independently replicated in a large external cohort from the UKBB.

Importantly, retinal vascular metrics derived using the AutoMorph pipeline substantially improved prediction of incident cardiovascular disease beyond traditional clinical risk factors, underscoring the potential of retinal imaging as a scalable, noninvasive window into systemic vascular health. The strong discriminative performance of models incorporating retinal features highlights the relevance of microvascular structure as an integrative marker of cumulative cardiometabolic burden.

Together, these findings support the utility of automated retinal phenotyping as a powerful tool for population-scale risk stratification and early disease detection. As retinal imaging becomes increasingly accessible, integrating quantitative microvascular biomarkers into clinical and preventive frameworks may enable earlier identification of individuals at elevated cardiometabolic risk and inform personalized prevention strategies.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Quantitative assessment of retinal microvasculature using AI enables detection of subclinical systemic vascular alterations. In this large population-based study, specific retinal vascular features, particularly arterial vessel density and fractal dimension, were inversely associated with multiple cardiometabolic risk factors, including blood pressure, glycemic indices, body composition, sleep apnea, and hepatic steatosis.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: AI-enabled retinal imaging offers a non-invasive, reproducible, and scalable approach to cardiovascular risk assessment in asymptomatic individuals. Future studies should evaluate the predictive utility of specific retinal vascular traits in prospective cohorts and explore their integration into routine risk stratification algorithms.
