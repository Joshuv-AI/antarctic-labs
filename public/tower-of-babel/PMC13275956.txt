Harnessing Tolypothrix sp. A-8 for biogenic synthesis of silver oxide nanoparticles (AgONPs) for multifunctional biomedical applications
Authors: Muhammad Kaleem, Lubna Anjum Minhas, Amber Jabeen, Muhammad Abdullah, Ghulam Mustafa Bajwa, Hafiz Muhammad Umer Farooqi, Dovran Shamamedov, Abdul Samad Mumtaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Green nanotechnology leverages biological systems for the sustainable synthesis of nanomaterials, offering an eco-friendly alternative to conventional methods. In this study, we report the phyco-synthesis of silver oxide nanoparticles (AgONPs) using an aqueous extract of Tolypothrix sp. A-8, a cyanobacterium rich in bioactive metabolites that serve as both reducing and capping agents. Synthesis was optimized by controlling key physicochemical parameters (reaction time, precursor concentration, temperature, and pH) to maximize yield. The resulting nanoparticles were comprehensively characterized using X-Ray Diffraction, Ultraviolet Visible Spectroscopy (UV), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy, Dynamic Light Scattering, Scanning Electron Microscopy, and Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy. The AgONPs demonstrated significant anti-leishmanial activity, with IC50 values of 10.43 µg/mL against promastigotes and 32.43 µg/mL against amastigotes of Leishmania tropica, and an LD50 of 45 µg/mL against Artemia salina. In anticancer assays against HCT-116 colorectal carcinoma cells, the nanoparticles induced cytotoxicity, with an IC50 of approximately 8512 µg/mL. They also exhibited notable antioxidant potential, with total antioxidant capacity and total reducing power reaching 58.4% and 51.4%, respectively, at 300 µg/mL. The AgONPs were highly biocompatible, showing non-hemolytic effects at concentrations up to 200 µg/mL (3.2% hemolysis) and an IC50 of 918.2 µg/mL against human macrophages, confirming their non-toxic nature. Anti-inflammatory activity was confirmed via human red blood cell membrane stabilization, with a 62.8% reduction in hemolysis. Furthermore, the nanoparticles demonstrated dose-dependent anti-diabetic potential, achieving 23.3% glucose scavenging at 900 µg/mL. Antimicrobial assays revealed potent activity, with minimum inhibitory concentrations of 50 µg/mL against Botrytis cinerea, Staphylococcus aureus, and coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, while Bacillus subtilis was the least susceptible bacterial strain (MIC: 150 µg/mL). This study pioneers Tolypothrix sp. A-8 as a scalable biofactory for the sustainable production of multifunctional AgONPs. With their potent, quantitative bioactivities and excellent biocompatibility, these nanomaterials hold significant promise for next-generation nanotherapeutics, including applications in wound healing, targeted drug delivery, and the management of leishmaniasis, cancer, inflammation, and diabetes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04671-x.

Introduction

Nanotechnology is a dynamic and widely recognized field of research focused on fabricating nanoparticles (NPs) with diverse shapes and sizes [1–3]. Metal-based NPs, in particular, are extensively utilized across numerous industries—including pharmacology [4], optical electronics [5], plasmonics [6], sensors [7], water purification [8], and catalysis [9], owing to their exceptional chemical and physical properties. Successful chemical synthesis has been reported for various metal NPs, including silver (Ag) [10, 11], gold (Au) [12], zinc (Zn) [13, 14], copper (Cu), titanium (Ti) and tellurium [15, 16], cadmium (Cd) [17, 18], iron (Fe) [19, 20], and selenium [21].

Among these, silver oxide nanoparticles (AgONPs) possess unique characteristics that render them highly suitable for diverse biological applications, including biosensors, antimicrobial agents, composite fibers, cosmetics, cryogenic superconductors, pharmaceuticals, and dental materials [22]. However, the limitations of conventional chemical and physical synthesis methods—such as environmental toxicity, high cost, and energy intensity—have driven growing interest in green chemistry approaches (Fig. 1) [23, 24]. Natural products have historically served as valuable sources of safe and effective medications [26] with broad pharmacological actions and minimal side effects [27–29]. Over the past five decades, numerous natural compounds—such as paclitaxel, ginsenoside Rg3, indirubin, and artemisinin—have demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy [30]. This has prompted exploration of various organisms for biogenic AgONP synthesis, including plant [31–33], bacteria [34], fungi [35], yeast [36], and algae [14, 37].

Algae have emerged as particularly promising model organisms for AgONP synthesis due to their rapid growth, cost-effectiveness, and high biomass productivity. Several algal species have been successfully employed for AgNO3 reduction and AgONP production, including Gelidium corneum [38], Spyridia flamentosa [38], Sargassum wightii [39], Lobophora variegata [40], Ulva armoricana [41], and Sargassum muticum [42], Nodularia haraviana [11], Calothrix elenkinii [43], Spirulina platensis and Nostoc linckia [44], Phormidium ambiguum and Desertifilum tharense [45], Oscillatoria and Spirulina [46], Pseudanabaena/Limnothrix sp [47], and Oscillatoria princep [48]. The bioactive compounds present in algae—including carotenoids, sterols, oligopeptides, and fatty acids—contribute to both the reduction process and the diverse biological applications of the resulting nanoparticles [41].

The present study investigates, for the first time, the green synthesis of AgONPs using the cyanobacterial extract of Tolypothrix sp. A-8, followed by comprehensive physicochemical characterization using X-Ray Diffraction (XRD), Ultraviolet Visible Spectroscopy (UV), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), Dynamic Light Scattering (DSL), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), and Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX). We systematically evaluated the impact of synthesis parameters (AgNO3 concentration, temperature, pH, and reaction time) on nanoparticle stability and yield. The biomedical potential of the synthesized AgONPs was assessed through multiple in vitro assays, including antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-leishmanial, antineoplastic, anti-inflammatory, anti-diabetic, and protein kinase inhibition activities, alongside cytotoxicity and biocompatibility evaluations against human erythrocytes and macrophages. This comprehensive investigation aims to harness the synergistic potential of natural products and nanotechnology, paving the way for sustainable and innovative nature-inspired nanomedicines with practical therapeutic applications.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

All chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade and used without further purification. Silver nitrate (AgNO3, ≥ 99.8% purity) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, ≥ 99.0% purity) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥ 98.0% purity) were obtained from Merck (Germany).

Optimum culture conditions for cyanobacterial strain

Tolypothrix sp. A-8 was cultivated in sterile BG-11 medium (composition per liter: NaNO3 1.5 g, K2HPO4 0.04 g, MgSO4·7H2O 0.075 g, CaCl2·2H2O 0.036 g, citric acid 0.006 g, ferric ammonium citrate 0.006 g, EDTA 0.001 g, Na2CO3 0.02 g, and 1 mL trace metal mix) using the flask-shake method at 28 ± 2 °C under continuous illumination (50 µmol photons m⁻2 s⁻1) with shaking at 120 rpm [49, 50]. Growth was monitored by optical density at 680 nm, and cells were harvested during the late exponential phase (Day 14–16, OD680 1.2–1.5) by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The pellet was washed three times with sterile distilled water, dried at 55% humidity for 24 h at room temperature, and ground into a fine powder. To ensure culture purity, routine microscopic examination and streak plating on nutrient agar was done. To prevent mutation, cultures were subcultured maximum five times from frozen stocks cryopreserved in 10% glycerol at − 80 °C. For extract preparation, 1.6 g of dried cyanobacterial powder was stirred in 100 mL sterile deionized water at 50 °C for 3 h, filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper followed by 0.45 µm membrane filtration, and stored at 4 °C. Extract sterility was confirmed by plating on nutrient agar, and BG-11 agar with no colony formation.

Bioinspired synthesis of Tolypothrix sp. A-8 derived AgONPs

The synthesis was initiated by combining 50 mL of filtered Tolypothrix sp. A-8 extract with 3 g of silver nitrate [11]. The mixture was heated at 70 °C for 2 h on a hot plate with constant stirring to accelerate the reaction (final pH 9.73). A color transition from dark brown to light brown provided initial visual confirmation of AgONP synthesis. The reaction mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 30 min (Fig. 1b). The resulting pellet was washed with sterile water to remove impurities, then dried at 75 °C for 2 h. The dried sample was calcined at 400 °C for 2 h in an open-air furnace [19]. The calcined nanoparticles were stored in a cool, dark, and dry environment for subsequent analyses.

Environmental factors affecting AgONPs stability

The aqueous extract of Tolypothrix sp. A-8 cyanobacteria were used to fabricate AgONPs. To determine the stability of the AgONPs, several factors were examined, including pH, temperature, time, and AgNO3 concentration [51]. After 6–12 h of dark incubation at room temperature, AgONPs were subjected to a spectrophotometric examination to determine the favorable conditions for reducing silver ions and the subsequent formation of silver nanoparticles. AgNO3 was employed at several doses (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 mM) to evaluate its effects on AgONPs synthesis. To find the best AgNO3 concentration, the absorbance of the nanoparticles was assessed using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. After synthesizing NPs, each reaction tube was maintained at that temperature for 20 min. The synthesis was carried out at a variety of temperatures, including 20 °C, 30 °C, 40 °C, 50 °C, and 60 °C. The reaction solution containing AgONPs was adjusted at different pH (2, 4, 6, 8, and 10) using 0.1 mol/l NaOH to check the stability of AgONPs. By observing the behavior of AgONPs under varying pH conditions, we aimed to understand how pH influences their stability and properties.

Spectroscopic characterizations of AgONPs

The synthesis of AgONPs was assessed by observing the color change. The formation of AgONPs was monitored using a UV vis spectrophotometer ((UV-4000 spectrophotometer, Berlin, Germany) with a resolution of 1nm. It covered a wavelength of 800–200nm. The color change in the mixture served as an indication of the synthesis of AgONPs. FTIR (Thermo Scientific Fisher Nicolete IS10, Waltham, MA, USA) analysis was used to identify biomolecules involved in synthesizing AgONPs. A powdered AgONPs sample weighing 2 mg was combined with 20 mg of KBr and compressed into a pellet. The analysis was taken between the wavelength of 500–4000 cm−1. The sample in powdered form was carefully placed onto a glass slide and then analyzed using a Bruker D8 Advanced Powder Diffractometer through XRD (PANalytical, Almelo, The Netherlands, Europe) analysis. The examination contained the values between 10° and 80°. The obtained data was further processed using X’pert Highscore and Origin lab software. For SEM analysis, a small drop of NPs suspension was applied onto an SEM grid coated with Aluminum. Subsequently, the sample was analyzed using a SEM (JSM5910 JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) instrument coupled with an EDX (Malvern Zetasizer Nano, Tokyo, Japan). ZP, PDI (Polydispersity index), and hydrodynamic size distribution were assessed using a Zeta size instrument (Malvern Zetasizer Nano). For the analysis, 200 µg of AgONPs were dissolved in deionized water (2 mL) (Milli-Q) using a bath sonicator for 40 min. The suspension was then moved into a collapsible capillary cell to analyze conductivity and Zeta potential.

Antimicrobial activity of AgONPs

The antifungal (AF) activity of AgONPs was investigated using the food poisoning method [62]. The preserved fungus strains were cultured in PDA media at 26 °C for 72 h. PDA medium was mixed with AgONPs at different concentrations (200–50 µg/mL) for this study. Using a cork borer, a 4 mm-diameter fungal inoculum disc was inserted into the middle of each PDA plate. As a positive control, PDA plates devoid of nanoparticles were used. The antibacterial (AB) assay of the AgONPs was investigated using the disc diffusion method [56]. Microbial strains obtained from Plant–Microbe Interaction laboratory at Quaid I Azam University, Islamabad and are routinely cultured and used for antimicrobial assays. Fresh bacterial colonies were inoculated into nutrient broth medium and incubated at 37 °C for 16–24 h to achieve log phase growth, with turbidity adjusted to 0.5 McFarland (~ 1 × 108 CFU/mL) using sterile saline. To measure the AgONPs’ antibacterial (AB) efficacy, a BS culture was spread out on an agar medium and given 5 min to air dry. Next, filter discs with different concentrations of AgONPs (50–200 µg/mL) were added to the agar surface. A ZOI developed on the plates after 24 h at 37 °C in the incubator. DMSO (5%) was used to establish the baseline as the negative control and kanamycin-A as the positive control.

Statistical analysis

The results of the DPPH radical scavenging activity, total antioxidant activity, cytotoxicity, antimicrobial activity, enzyme inhibition, anti-inflammatory, insecticidal, and biocompatibility tests are presented as mean values ± standard deviations obtained from three independent replicates per sample. Results and discussion

Optimal growth parameters and Identification of Tolypothrix sp. A-8

A light microscope was used to evaluate the cyanobacterial strain’s isolation, purity, and morphology. The selected strain has a filamentous structure with lateral and intercalary heterocysts. The strain was identified and named Tolypothrix sp. A-8 (Fig. 1c). The growth of the Tolypothrix sp. A-8 colony appeared as tiny brownish-to-black spots on the BG0 solid agar media. As the colony grows, air bubbles begin to develop within the colony (Fig. 1c (A). At this stage, the colonies were shifted to BG0 liquid media. The colonies grew in size with large air bubbles inside. Filaments embedded in thick mucilage are visible only when ink is applied. Filaments are olive green to dirty green in color. Individual cells are oval to barrel-shaped, highly granulated, and 6.5μm wide. Heterocyst is oval to sub-spherical in shape. The color of heterocysts is olive green when young, but in later stages, they become transparent and 5.1μm wide. Akinetes barrel to oval in shape 10μm in length and 8μm in width. Our study demonstrated that the solution of Tolypothrix sp. A-8 extract and silver nitrate changed color from light yellow to dark brown, indicating the biogenic synthesis of AgONPs (Fig. 1d) [64].

Physio-chemical characterization techniques for NPs synthesis

After the identification of crucial factors influencing the yield of AgONPs, various physicochemical parameters were optimized. These parameters included temperature, reaction mixture’s pH, contact duration, and silver nitrate concentration (AgNO3) [65]. At the nanoscale, metallic nanoparticles’ optical characteristics represent intriguing features dependent on their size and morphology. The synthesis of AgONPs in an aqueous solution was further assessed using UV–visible spectroscopy, a valuable method for examining the production and stability of metal NPs under such circumstances. Analysis of the spectra (Fig. 2a, b) of AgONPs exhibited the presence of Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) within the 300–800 nm wavelength range in the aqueous medium. Notably, it was noted that following an 8h reaction period, AgONPs synthesized displayed an SPR band at a wavelength of 452 nm. Effect of AgNO3 concentration

The effect of precursor concentration on AgONP synthesis was evaluated by varying AgNO3 levels under optimized conditions. Concentration directly influenced nanoparticle dimensions and structural characteristics, as reflected in SPR peak profiles [67, 68]. Lower AgNO3 concentrations produced larger nanoparticles with broader size distributions, indicated by broader SPR peaks. Increasing concentration enhanced SPR peak sharpness and intensity while reducing width, suggesting more uniform particle size distribution. However, concentrations exceeding 0.5 mM resulted in decreased intensity and peak broadening due to agglomeration driven by Van der Waals forces between adjacent nanoparticles [69]. Optimal synthesis was observed at 0.1 mM AgNO3, yielding a distinct SPR peak at 593 nm. These findings align with previous reports documenting similar concentration-dependent effects on nanoparticle synthesis [69], as illustrated in Fig. 2c.

Effect of temperature on AgONPs

Temperature plays a critical role in the biosynthesis of AgONPs. The reaction mixture was incubated at temperatures ranging from 20 to 60 °C for 40 min to evaluate this parameter (Fig. 2d). Increasing temperature accelerated nanoparticle formation, as evidenced by rapid color changes and increased absorbance peak intensity. At 30 °C, a distinct SPR peak was observed at 446.5 nm. Absorbance increased progressively with temperature up to 40 °C; however, no further enhancement was observed beyond this point, consistent with previous reports [65, 70]. Elevated temperatures impart greater kinetic energy to molecules, accelerating silver ion consumption and limiting particle size expansion [71]. Conversely, excessive temperatures promote aggregation, yielding larger particles that lose their SPR properties. These findings confirm that temperature optimization is essential for achieving uniform, stable AgONPs with desirable optical characteristics.

Effect of time on synthesis of AgONPs

The effect of reaction time on AgONP synthesis was monitored by observing the mixture of aqueous cyanobacterial extract and AgNO3 solution at different intervals [72]. Interaction between AgNO3 and the extract induced a progressive color change within the first hour, which intensified over time Fig. 2e. Systematic UV–Vis spectrophotometric measurements consistently revealed a broad surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peak during the initial 0–2 h, indicating gradual reduction of Ag+ to Ag0. As reduction progressed, a distinct SPR spectrum emerged. After 8 h, the UV–Vis spectrum displayed a strong SPR absorption peak at approximately 452 nm, signifying rapid and complete synthesis of AgNPs [73].

Effect of pH on AgONPs

The pH of the reaction mixture plays a critical role in determining the electrical charges of biomolecules, thereby influencing their reducing, capping, and stabilizing capacities during nanoparticle synthesis [74]. AgONP synthesis was evaluated across a range of pH values (2, 4, 6, 8, and 10). A low pH of 4 hindered nanoparticle formation, while increasing pH from 6 to 10 enhanced color intensity due to excitation of the surface plasmon resonance (SPR). Optimal synthesis was observed at pH 8, with AgONPs displaying a distinctive SPR peak at λmax 452 nm, consistent with silver ion absorption within the 430–460 nm range Fig. 2f. Veerasamy et al. (2011) similarly reported nanoparticle formation at pH 4, with smaller, highly dispersed nanoparticles obtained at pH 8 [65, 75]. Verma and Mehata further observed that AgONP absorbance increased from pH 2 to 8, but declined with further pH elevation [74].

Stability of AgONPs

The stability assessment of the synthesized AgONPs was conducted at 10, 20, and 30 days post-synthesis, unveiling no vibrational shifts in the surface plasmon resonance of the AgONPs. A distinct peak indicated the successful formation of well-dispersed nanoparticles, consistent with prior research showing stable AgNPs synthesized from an aqueous Cassia roxburghii extract enduring for 2 months [76].

Spectroscopic characterizations of AgONPs

FTIR was used to analyze the presence of biomolecules in AgONPs, as depicted in Fig. 3a. Tolypothrix sp. A-8 contains Phyto-molecules such as chlorophyll-a, phycobiliproteins, lipids, tannins, proteins, polyphenols, flavonoids, and carbohydrates. These Phyto-molecules reduced and stabilized the synthesized AgONPs. The distinctive features in the FTIR spectra are linked to the O–H stretching vibration of hydroxyl groups found in polyphenols, characterized by a band at 3113 cm−1. Moreover, the N–H stretching vibration at 1610 cm−1 indicates the presence of aromatic amines, primary amines, or amides in various biomolecules [77]. The 1167 cm−1 and 676 cm−1 peaks signify the C–O and C=C vibration associated with carboxylic acid and alkenes [22]. Additionally, the 2840 cm−1 peak correspond to the C-H stretch of the aldehyde of primary amine. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was used to identify the AgONPs crystal structure, size, and form. As shown in Fig. 3b, the XRD pattern was generated by scanning a 2 θ range of 10° and 80°. Nine unique and significant diffraction peaks, with Bragg diffraction peaks values of (110), (002), (111), (200), (102), (241), (220), (201) and (311) were identified by the examination at 33.37 °, 35.94°, 38.47°, 47.47°, 49.22°, 56.32°, 62.59°, 67.71°, and 76.89°. These results suggest that the cubic structure of AgONPs is face-centered (JCPDS No. 84-1547). The most prominent peak observed at 38.47° at 2 θ represents the basal plane of (111), the uppermost crystal plane for the AgONPs. According to Scherrer’s equation, the synthesized AgONPs’ size was calculated to be 32.12 nm (Fig. 3c) [78]. SEM and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy were used to analyze the surface morphology, size, and elemental composition of AgONPs as shown in Fig. 3d, e. According to the SEM investigation, there were spherical and cubic AgONPs with an estimated average size of 35 nm (Fig. 3d, e). In a previous study, cubic and hexagonal nanoparticles with diameters ranging from 10 to 50 nm were synthesized using Argemone Mexicana leaf extract [79]. The zeta potential of synthesized AgONPs was measured at − 18.5 mV (Fig. 4b), indicating that the nanoparticle surface is capped with negatively charged phytochemicals from the Tolypothrix sp. extract. Although values exceeding ± 30 mV typically signify excellent colloidal stability, the observed − 18.5 mV falls within the moderate stability range. This suggests that while immediate aggregation is prevented, nanoparticles may exhibit a tendency to aggregate over extended storage—consistent with the high polydispersity index (PDI = 0.845) (Fig. 4a), which reflects a wide size distribution. This high polydispersity has important implications for biological assay interpretation. The observed bioactivities (Table 1) represent the average effect of a heterogeneous population, where smaller nanoparticles may exhibit enhanced cellular uptake and reactivity due to their larger surface area-to-volume ratio, while larger particles may demonstrate altered interaction kinetics with biological molecules. These findings align with a prior study on AgONPs synthesized using Ocimum basilicum extract [80]. It is important to note that the high polydispersity (PDI = 0.845) of the synthesized AgONPs may contribute to batch-to-batch variability in biological outcomes. Therefore, while the observed bioactivities are statistically significant, rigorous standardization of synthesis and purification protocols is recommended to improve size homogeneity and enhance the reproducibility of future biological and pharmacological studies. Biomedical applications

Toxicity evaluation of AgONPs derived from Tolypothrix sp. A-8

The cytotoxic potential of AgONPs against freshly hatched Artemia salina nauplii was evaluated using the brine shrimp lethality assay [86]. AgONPs were tested at concentrations ranging from 6.25 to 300 µg/mL (Fig. 5a,b). Mortality increased in a dose-dependent manner, with the highest mortality observed at 300 µg/mL. The median lethal dose (LD50) was calculated as 45 µg/mL. Interestingly, the lowest concentration (6.25 µg/mL) demonstrated the most promising outcomes among all tested concentrations. The mechanism of AgONP-induced cytotoxicity involves ROS generation, leading to DNA, protein, and membrane damage Fig. 5a [87]. Leishmaniasis, caused by intracellular protozoan parasites of the Leishmania genus, affects over 100 countries worldwide [52]. Current treatments are often toxic, costly, and increasingly ineffective due to parasite resistance, driving the search for alternative therapeutics. The anti-leishmanial activity (ALA) of AgONPs increased with concentration, exhibiting potent effects with IC50 values of 10.43 µg/mL against promastigotes and 32.43 µg/mL against amastigotes (Fig. 5b) [52]. Promastigotes demonstrated greater susceptibility than amastigotes. These low IC50 values suggest significant potential for targeted drug delivery in leishmaniasis treatment. This study represents the first report of anti-leishmanial activity of biogenic AgONPs synthesized using Tolypothrix sp. Anticancer activity

Cancer remains a leading cause of global mortality, with 70% of deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries [88]. Algal-derived compounds have garnered increasing interest as potential anticancer agents due to their perceived lower toxicity profiles [89–91]. The anticancer activity of Tolypothrix sp. A-8 derived AgONPs was evaluated against human colorectal cancer (HCT-116) cell lines. Treatment with AgONPs resulted in minimal cytotoxicity, with only 1.17% reduction in cell viability observed at the highest tested concentration (200 µg/mL). The calculated IC50 value was approximately 8512 µg/mL. This IC50 is substantially higher than those reported for conventional chemotherapeutics and other biosynthesized nanoparticles, such as Podophyllum hexandrum AgONPs (IC50 = 20 µg/mL against HeLa cells) [92] and Cymodocea serrulata AgONPs, which demonstrated cytotoxicity through improved cellular uptake and retention [93]. Rather than demonstrating potent anticancer activity, these findings indicate that Tolypothrix sp. A-8 AgONPs are relatively non-toxic to mammalian cells at physiologically relevant concentrations. This biocompatibility profile may be advantageous for biomedical applications requiring minimal host cell toxicity, such as wound dressings or antimicrobial formulations, rather than direct cancer therapy. The mechanism by which smaller nanoparticles typically induce cytotoxicity may involve enhanced cellular entry via endocytosis, followed by ROS generation leading to oxidative stress, DNA damage, and apoptotic cell death [94–97]. However, the high IC50 value observed in this study suggests that these particular nanoparticles do not effectively trigger this pathway in HCT-116 cells at the concentrations tested.

Free radical scavenging ((FRS) potential of AgONPs

The antioxidant potential of Tolypothrix sp. A-8 derived AgONPs was evaluated through total antioxidant capacity (TAC), total reducing power (TRP), and DPPH radical scavenging assays (Fig. 5c). Phenolic compounds present in the extract are thought to contribute to ROS scavenging and interact with AgONPs. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), generated by free radicals with unpaired electrons, induce oxidative stress by destabilizing nearby molecules [98]. Antioxidants play a crucial role in scavenging these radicals and restoring cellular homeostasis. Several biogenic metal oxide nanoparticles have demonstrated significant antioxidant effects. TAC increased with concentration, reaching a maximum of 58.4% (expressed as ascorbic acid equivalent per milligram) at 300 µg/mL, compared to 1.3% at the lowest concentration (6.25 µg/mL). TRP, which measures the ability of reductones to donate hydrogen atoms and break free radical chain reactions, also showed concentration-dependent activity. The highest TRP value (51.4%) was observed at 300 µg/mL (Fig. 5c). AgONPs demonstrated significant DPPH radical scavenging activity, with 59.5% inhibition at 300 µg/mL. These findings suggest that antioxidant phytochemicals from Tolypothrix sp. A-8 extract contribute to the stabilization and reduction of AgONPs, while also conferring antioxidant properties to the nanoparticles. The results are consistent with previous studies on biogenic AgONPs synthesized using Citrus limon and cassava extracts [99, 100].

Biocompatibility assessment

Biocompatibility of Tolypothrix sp. A-8 mediated AgONPs was evaluated using human red blood cells (RBCs) and macrophages (HMs). According to American Society guidelines, compounds with < 5% hemolysis are classified as non-hemolytic, while those with > 5% are considered hemolytic [101]. AgONPs exhibited concentration-dependent hemolytic behavior: non-hemolytic at 0.2%, 2.6%, and 3.2%; slightly hemolytic at 5.3%; and hemolytic at 16.7% (Fig. 5d). The IC50 value for hemolysis was 918.2 µg/mL (Fig. 5e), confirming excellent hemocompatibility at physiologically relevant concentrations. These findings align with previous studies on Leptolyngbya sp.-mediated nanoparticles [19]. Cytotoxicity against human macrophages further confirmed the biosafety profile. AgONPs suppressed HM growth in a dose-dependent manner, with 24.48% inhibition at 300 µg/mL and an IC50 of 918.2 µg/mL (Fig. 5e). Macrophages possess intrinsic defenses against exogenous ROS, rendering them resistant to oxidative damage below cytotoxic thresholds [102]. The combined hemocompatibility and macrophage safety data establish Tolypothrix sp. A-8 derived AgONPs as non-toxic and biocompatible for biomedical applications.

Inflammatory response assay

The RBC membrane stabilization method was employed to evaluate the anti-inflammatory potential of AgONPs, as the erythrocyte membrane shares structural similarities with lysosomal membranes. Stabilization of lysosomal membranes prevents the release of inflammatory enzymes, thereby mitigating tissue damage and inflammation [103]. At a concentration of 1 mg/mL, AgONPs demonstrated 62.79 ± 0.05% inhibition of hemolysis, indicating significant membrane stabilization capacity and promising in vitro anti-inflammatory efficacy. Diclofenac, the standard anti-inflammatory drug, exhibited higher inhibition (79.41 ± 0.05%) at the same concentration. Although AgONPs showed statistically lower activity than diclofenac, they effectively stabilized RBC membranes, preventing the release of lytic enzymes and inflammatory mediators. Previous studies have documented anti-inflammatory activity of S. khasianum leaf and fruit extracts using protein denaturation assays [104]. Our findings align with reports on various Solanaceae species evaluated through the RBC membrane stabilization technique [105–107], further supporting the anti-inflammatory potential of Tolypothrix sp. A-8 derived AgONPs.

Antidiabetic activity

Diabetes mellitus, a global metabolic disorder characterized by insufficient insulin secretion and elevated blood glucose levels, necessitates effective therapeutic interventions. The α-amylase inhibition assay was employed to evaluate the anti-diabetic potential of Tolypothrix sp. A-8 mediated AgONPs [108]. As shown in Fig. 6a, AgONPs exhibited concentration-dependent α-amylase inhibition, with maximum activity (23.3%) observed at 900 µg/mL [109]. Although the inhibitory effect was lower than that of the standard drug Acarbose [110], these findings demonstrate the potential of AgONPs to scavenge blood glucose across varying concentrations. Protein kinase (PK) inhibition activity

Protein kinases (PK) regulate critical cellular processes including proliferation, differentiation, metabolism, and apoptosis. During carcinogenesis, genetic alterations disrupt normal phosphorylation patterns, making PK promising therapeutic targets for inhibiting cancer pathways [111]. The PK inhibitory potential of Tolypothrix sp. A-8 derived AgONPs was evaluated against Streptomyces sp., where PK activation is essential for aerial hyphae development. Inhibition was assessed visually by observing bald or clear zones on the growth medium, indicating disrupted phosphorylation and subsequent impairment of spore and mycelium formation [112]. At 900 µg/mL, AgONPs produced distinct bald regions measuring 17.11 mm (Fig. 6b), demonstrating significant PK inhibition. Clear zones indicative of strain death were also observed, confirming cytotoxicity. These findings establish AgONPs as effective PK inhibitors with potential for targeting cancer-related pathways.

Antimicrobial assay

The antifungal (AF) potential of metal oxide NPs such as Tolypothrix sp. A-8 mediated AgONPs have received less attention than their AB activity [19]. In this research, we investigated the AF activity of AgONPs mediated by Tolypothrix sp. A-8. The AF assay was conducted using various fungal strains, such as Alternaria alternata and Botrytis cinerea, to assess their susceptibility at various concentrations ranging from 50–200 µg/mL of AgONPs (Fig. 6c). It was discovered that the majority of the evaluated fungal strains were AgONP-susceptible. With a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 50 μg/ml, Botrytis cinerea showed the maximum susceptibility, whereas Alternaria alternata displayed the lowest susceptibility, with a minimum inhibition concentration of 100 µg/mL. These findings support earlier studies that examined the AF effects of NPs mediated by Rhamnus virgate against several fungal strains [52]. Furthermore, recent studies have investigated the AF potentials of NP synthesized via green synthesis routes and have shown promising results [113]. Bacterial infections pose a serious global health challenge due to treatment costs, mortality, morbidity, and the emergence of antibiotic resistance [19, 114]. Nanotechnology offers innovative tools for designing novel compounds with remarkable antibacterial potential [115]. A schematic representation of AgONP-mediated bacterial inhibition mechanisms is shown in Fig. 6d. The antibacterial efficacy of Tolypothrix sp. A-8 mediated AgONPs was evaluated against clinically relevant bacterial strains—coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, and Bacillus subtilis—at concentrations ranging from 50–200 µg/mL (Fig. 6e). AgONPs exhibited concentration-dependent antibacterial activity, with coagulase-negative Staphylococcus and S. aureus showing the highest susceptibility (MIC: 50 µg/mL), while B. subtilis was the least susceptible (MIC: 150 µg/mL). Although oxytetracycline demonstrated greater efficacy, AgONPs effectively inhibited all tested strains. These findings align with previous studies on biogenic nanoparticles, including Leptolyngbya sp.-mediated iron oxide nanoparticles [14, 19, 116, 117]. Insecticidal activity

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrates the green synthesis of multifunctional silver oxide nanoparticles (AgONPs) using the cyanobacterium Tolypothrix sp. A-8, establishing a sustainable and scalable biofactory for nanomaterial production. The biosynthesized AgONPs exhibited potent, target-specific bioactivities with excellent quantitative profiles: remarkable anti-leishmanial efficacy (IC50: 10.43 µg/mL against promastigotes), selective anticancer activity against HCT-116 colorectal cancer cells, and broad-spectrum antimicrobial effects (MIC: 50 µg/mL against S. aureus and B. cinerea). Notably, these therapeutic effects were achieved alongside exceptional biocompatibility with human erythrocytes and macrophages (IC50: 918.2 µg/mL), confirming a favorable safety index. The nanoparticles further demonstrated significant anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-diabetic potential, positioning them as a versatile platform for managing complex diseases. It is important to note that the high polydispersity (PDI = 0.845) of the synthesized AgONPs may contribute to batch-to-batch variability in biological outcomes. Therefore, while the observed bioactivities are statistically significant, rigorous standardization of synthesis and purification protocols is recommended to improve size homogeneity and enhance the reproducibility of future biological and pharmacological studies. This work bridges the critical gap between green nanotechnology and practical biomedical applications, establishing Tolypothrix sp. A-8 derived AgONPs as promising candidates for next-generation nanotherapeutics. Future studies will focus on in vivo validation, mechanistic elucidation, and development of targeted drug delivery systems.
