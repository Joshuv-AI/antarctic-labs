Motivation
When electron beams from accelerators are stopped in thick targets made of materials with a high atomic
number (or high-Z targets), they produce photons through braking radiation (commonly known as
bremsstrahlung). The efficiency of photon production increases with the atomic number (Z). Tungsten is
commonly chosen as a bremsstrahlung converter in both research and medical linear accelerators due to
its high Z, large bremsstrahlung cross section, high melting point, and excellent thermal conductivity.
However, tungsten properties also lead to the production of secondary particles. Its high density, combined
with a relatively low threshold for the photo-induced neutron emission (𝛾, 𝑛) reaction (about 6 or 7 MeV
for most of its natural isotopes), and large (𝛾, 𝑛) reaction cross section, means that it can generate
significant numbers of photoneutrons.
Consequently, bremsstrahlung radiation from a tungsten converter is not a pure photon source; it is a
mixture dominated by photons that also contains a notable fraction of secondary neutrons. Accurate
knowledge of the cross sections for photon-induced nuclear reactions in tungsten is essential for a wide
range of applications. These include particle transport simulations, studies of material damage and heating,
radioisotope production, transmutation of long-lived fission products, and the design of radiation shielding,
particularly for photon energies in the range of 1 to 30 MeV.
This report provides a comprehensive summary of available photonuclear data for the three tungsten
isotopes 182W, 184W, and 186W. It also presents an assessment of corresponding recent evaluated data found
in the ENDF/B-VIII.1 [1], JENDL-5 [2], IAEA-2019 [3], and TENDL-2023 [4] libraries.

Terminology
First, we review key terminology related to photonuclear reactions [5]. For most atomic nuclei (excluding
light nuclei, such as deuteron, triton, and 3He), the energy dependence of the photonuclear reaction cross
section exhibits a broad maximum, typically in the 10–20 MeV photon energy range, called giant dipole
resonance (GDR).
Two primary experimental techniques are used to measure photonuclear reactions:
 • Activation technique (off-beam measurement): The residual activity of the final nucleus is
 measured after irradiation. If the residual nucleus is radioactive, this technique allows for the
 identification of all reaction products unambiguously. Therefore, the corresponding reaction cross
 sections are called exclusive.
 • Direct technique (in-beam measurement): Emitted particles are detected during irradiation. This
 method typically enables the measurement of inclusive reaction cross sections when multiple
 exclusive reaction channels contribute to the process.

For medium-mass and heavy nuclei (including the tungsten isotopes considered here), experimental cross
sections for proton and alpha emission [(𝛾, 𝑝) and (𝛾, 𝛼)] are typically orders of magnitude smaller than
those for neutron emission in the GDR region, making their measurement more difficult and less reliable.
Most photonuclear experimental data focus on the photoneutron cross section between the threshold of
the single neutron emission [(𝛾, 𝑛) at energy 𝐸! ] and the threshold of double neutron emission [(𝛾, 2𝑛) at
energy (𝐸"! )]. These threshold energies are shown in Table 1.
Below 𝐸! , the (𝛾, 𝛾) elastic and (𝛾, 𝛾′) inelastic photon scattering are the only available photonuclear
reactions and the total photonuclear cross section, 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑡𝑜𝑡), is given by their sum. Above 𝐸! , the inelastic
photon scattering cross section becomes less important compared to neutron emission. Therefore, for
most medium and heavy nuclei, in the energy range between 1 MeV above 𝐸! and up to 𝐸"! , the difference
between 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛) and 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑡𝑜𝑡) is typically less than 10%, and often just a few percent.
The inclusive (total) photoneutron production cross section is denoted 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑥𝑛) and is given by the sum of
the cross sections of the exclusive single, double, triple, etc. photoneutron channels weighted by the
number of the neutrons they each emit (neutron multiplicities):
              𝜎(𝛾, 𝑥𝑛) = 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛𝑋) + 2𝜎(𝛾, 2𝑛𝑋) + 3𝜎(𝛾, 3𝑛𝑋) + ⋯ + 𝜈𝜎(𝛾, 𝐹),                         (1)
where 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛𝑋) is the sum of the cross sections for all reaction channels that have a single neutron in the
final state (with 𝑋 indicating additional undetected products, e.g. 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛𝑋) = 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛) + 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛𝑝) +
𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛α) + ⋯ ), and 𝜎(𝛾, 2𝑛𝑋) and 𝜎(𝛾, 3𝑛𝑋) are defined analogously; 𝜈 is the average number of
neutrons per fission. We note that fission occurs in relatively heavy nuclei and is not considered for the
present study of tungsten isotopes for photon incident energies below 30 MeV.
In several cases, authors use special methods (e.g., “ring-ratio” methods, statistical methods, theory
calculations, etc.) to infer the photoneutron multiplicity and give data for the total (inclusive) photoneutron
cross-section:
                𝜎(𝛾, 𝑠𝑛) = 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛𝑋) + 𝜎(𝛾, 2𝑛𝑋) + 𝜎(𝛾, 3𝑛𝑋) + ⋯ + 𝜎(𝛾, 𝐹),                          (2)

where the fission reaction (γ, F) is once again not considered for the present assessment of tungsten
isotope data since it appears at γ-ray energies higher than 30 MeV.
The sum of the total photoneutron cross section with the photo-charged-particle reaction cross section
(except photon scattering) gives the total photo absorption (or, total photonuclear particle emission) cross-
section:
      𝜎(𝛾, 𝑋) = 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑎𝑏𝑠) = 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑠𝑛) + 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑠𝑝) + 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑠𝑑) + … + 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑠𝛼) + …                       (3)
with 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑠𝑝), 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑠𝑑), 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑠𝛼) etc. defined analogously to Eq. (2). For heavy nuclei, like tungsten, the
total reaction (or, photoabsorption) cross section, denoted 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑋), or also 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑎𝑏𝑠) can be approximated
by the total photoneutron cross section. This is once again due to the suppression of charge-particle
emitting reactions owing to the Coulomb barrier, that increases with atomic number 𝑍 (i.e., the number of
protons). Therefore, one can use the approximation:
                                𝜎(𝛾, 𝑋) = 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑎𝑏𝑠) ≅ 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑠𝑛).                                     (4)
We note that the ENDF format does not directly store the total photoabsorption cross section 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑋).
Instead, it provides the total nonelastic cross section (stored in MT=3),
                                𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛𝑜𝑛) = 𝜎(𝛾, 𝛾′) + 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑋).                                     (5)
This is the sum of the inelastic photon scattering cross section and the total photonuclear particle emission
cross section. It is important to note that the probability of nuclear de-excitation via photon emission (𝛾, 𝛾′)

decreases rapidly near the particle emission threshold. As a result, above this threshold, 𝜎(𝛾, 𝛾′) becomes
negligible, and the total nonelastic cross section effectively approximates the total photoabsorption cross
section: 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛𝑜𝑛) ≈ 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑋).

Experimental Data
A summary of all EXFOR (experimental nuclear reaction database [6]) and literature available on
experimental photonuclear cross sections data for the stable tungsten isotopes is presented in Table 2. The
available photodisintegration data for tungsten isotopes indicate that these nuclei have not been
extensively studied. Only a limited number of measurements exist that characterize the size and shape of
their GDR. Among the tungsten isotopes, 186W has received the most attention; both total and partial
reaction cross section data have been obtained through fundamentally different experiments [7]—[15].
These studies employed various photon sources, such as bremsstrahlung and positron annihilation in flight,
as well as diverse experimental techniques, including the activation method, neutron multiplicity
measurements, and the self-attenuation technique. In contrast, for 182W and 184W, only the total
photodisintegration and single photoneutron cross sections have been measured, and for 183W the only
available data are for the single photoneutron cross section. Finally, no direct EXFOR or literature data were
found for the experimental photonuclear cross sections of 180W.

The first photonuclear reaction experiments with tungsten targets were performed at LLNL by Berman et
al. (1969) [10], who measured the 186W(𝛾, 𝑥𝑛) production cross section using quasimonoenergetic photons
produced by in-flight positron annihilation. The exclusive partial cross sections [𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛) + 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛𝑝),
𝜎(𝛾, 2𝑛)] were determined using neutron multiplicity counting. The average neutron energies for both
single- and double-photoneutron events were also determined using the ring-ratio method. Among the
nuclear information extracted from the data are giant-resonance parameters, nuclear symmetry energies,
and intrinsic quadrupole moments. The photon energy spread varied from less than 300 keV, at the lowest
energy, to 400 keV at the highest energy, and the measurements were taken at intervals of 300 keV.
Because the quasimonoenergetic photons produced by positron annihilation were mixed with photons
from positron bremsstrahlung, a three-stage difference method was necessary to isolate the desired signal.
In the first stage, the yield 𝑌(𝐸#$% )& ! of the reaction induced by both annihilation and bremsstrahlung
photons was measured, where 𝐸#$% is the maximum bremsstrahlung energy determined by the kinetic
energy of the electrons. In the second stage, the reaction yield produced by irradiation with photons from
electron beam bremsstrahlung, 𝑌(𝐸#$% )& " , was measured. Finally, in the third stage, the results were
normalized under the assumption that electron and positron bremsstrahlung spectra were identical. The
cross section of the reaction triggered by quasimonoenergetic photons was obtained by subtracting the
latter measurement from the former measurement.
                              𝜎(𝐸) = 𝑌(𝐸#$% )& ! − 𝑌(𝐸#$% )& "                                  (5)
About a decade later, Gurevich, et al. [7],[8],[17] measured the total photonuclear cross sections of
182,184,186
 W in the GDR region by using the absorption method. This approach involved determining the
attenuation coefficient of gamma beams passing through tungsten isotopes. The measured cross section
represents the sum of photonuclear and photoatomic contributions, with the latter including the
photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, and pair production. The total photonuclear cross
section, 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑡𝑜𝑡), which encompasses all partial reaction channels—including (𝛾, 𝛾) and (𝛾, 𝛾′)—was
obtained by subtracting the evaluated photoatomic component. This method yields photonuclear cross
sections accurate to within a few tens of percent.
In the early 2000’s, the superconducting electron accelerator at the Technische Universität Darmstadt
bremsstrahlung facility was used for activation measurements of 185W after one-neutron emission of 186W
[11],[12]. The activation technique allows for obtaining the yields of nuclear reactions; that is, the
convolution of the reaction cross section and the spectrum of incident particles. For this experiment, the
authors used the cross section of 197Au(𝛾, 𝑛) as a normalization factor (monitor).

Schizuma, et al. [14] used a quasi-monoenergetic laser-Compton backscattering (LCS) gamma-ray beam at
the NewSUBARU synchrotron radiation facility of the University of Hyogo in Japan to measure the number
of neutrons emitted by 186W below the (𝛾, 2𝑛) energy threshold, yielding the 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛) cross section. The
data analysis of this single cross section is greatly simplified, making it one of the most accurate
measurements for this reaction. The photon flux was measured with a large NaI(Tl) scintillator placed
directly in the beam and behind the ¹⁸⁶W target with 3% uncertainty, while the beam profile was monitored
with an HPGe detector. The emitted neutrons were detected by 16 ³He proportional counters embedded
in a polyethylene moderator. The neutron detection efficiency was measured with a standard ²⁵²Cf source
and the dependence of the efficiency on the neutron energy was determined by a Monte Carlo MCNP
simulation. The overall systematic uncertainty for ¹⁸⁶W(𝛾, 𝑛) cross sections measurement was estimated
to be 5.9–8.3%. A similar technique was used by Utsunomiya et al. at the National Institute of Advanced
Industrial Science and Technology, in Japan, to measure once again the 186W(𝛾, 𝑛)185W cross section [15],
and later by Larsen et al. to measure the single photoneutron (𝛾, 𝑛) cross sections for 182W, 183W, and 184W
[19].
No photonuclear cross section data are available for 180W.

Partial photoneutron cross-sections of natural W were measured using monochromatic photons in the
energy range 8 MeV ≤ 𝐸' ≤ 22 MeV to study the giant dipole resonance [19]. Using the variable-energy
monochromatic photon beam obtained by in-flight positron annihilation at the Saclay linear accelerator
and a high-efficiency neutron liquid scintillator, the 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛) + 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛𝑝) and 𝜎(𝛾, 2𝑛) cross sections were
measured. From these measurements authors were able to reconstruct the total photoabsorption cross
section which shows a double-peaked GDR typical for nuclei with permanently elongated deformation. It is
difficult to estimate which of the stable tungsten isotopes contributes the most to the splitting of the GDR;
however, it is known that ¹⁸²W exhibits the highest degree of deformation.

Evaluated data
In the ENDF format, photonuclear cross sections are stored in File = 3 under the NSUB = 0 sub-library. The
type of reaction data and the products resulting from that reaction are identified by an integer number
from 1 to 999, called MT number (for more information on the meaning of MT numbers for photonuclear
cross sections we refer the reader to Ref. [5]).
In the following, we present an assessment of available evaluated data libraries for photonuclear reactions
on the stable tungsten isotopes, 180W, 182W, 183W, 184W, and 186W. Specifically, we considered the following
four evaluated data libraries: the ENDF/B-VIII.1 Evaluated Nuclear Data Files [1], the Japanese evaluated
nuclear data library version 5 (JENDL-5) [2], the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) Photonuclear
Data Library 2019 (IAEA-2019) [3], and the 2023 version of the TALYS Evaluated Nuclear Data Library
(TENDL-2023) [4]. For these evaluated data libraries, one finds the W cross sections for the MT numbers
specified in Table 3.
The IAEA-2019 evaluations focused on the total photoabsorption (tabulated in MT=5), whereas the JENDL-
5 evaluations also include the photofission (MT=18) channel, and therefore extends up to 200 MeV of
incident photons, where contributions from photofission are non-negligible. In general, the ENDF/B-VIII.1
and TENDL-2023 both offer detailed breakdowns of reaction channels for the tungsten isotopes 180W, 182W,
183
 W and 186W, allowing for more granular analysis of photonuclear processes in these cases (Figure 1 and
Figure 2). In contrast, for 184W, ENDF/B-VIII.1 provides only the total reaction cross section, sourced from
JENDL-5, without further channel differentiation. Among the evaluated data sets, TENDL-2023 provides the
comprehensive evaluations for all tungsten isotopes (Figure 2). The photonuclear evaluations in TENDL-

2023 are the result of Hauser-Feschbach calculations using the TALYS code. This is also the only evaluated
data file that provides covariances.

Assessment methodology
To assess the evaluated data, for each MT number/reaction channel present in the evaluated data file, we
identified the corresponding available experimental data (or their closest approximations). We then
performed both a qualitative visual inspection through comparison plots and a more quantitative analysis
through a series a goodness of fit tests, including χ" , χ" /datum, and R2*, computing the absolute relative
difference between experimental data and evaluations, and counting the number of experimental data
points that lie within 1σ and 2σ for each evaluation. The plots and quantitative assessments were
performed with a newly developed tool called CEREAL [21]. The output of this tool for each isotope and
evaluated data file is provided in the Appendix. The results of this assessment are summarized in the
following.

Neutron production cross section
Not all libraries provide evaluations for the neutron production cross section, 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑥𝑛). However, when
such data are available or can be reconstructed, it is valuable to compare these evaluations with
experimental measurements. Experiments often directly measure neutron production and then use
multiplicity sorting techniques to extract single, double, and, in some cases, even triple photonuclear cross
sections. We found two isotopes for which we can make such comparison: 184W and 186W (Figure 3).

Total photoabsorption cross section
Evaluations of the total photoabsorption cross section—also referred to as the total photonuclear particle
emission cross section, denoted 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑋) or sometimes 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑎𝑏𝑠)—are not directly stored in the ENDF
format. Instead, ENDF provides the total nonelastic cross section, 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛𝑜𝑛) of Eq. (5). This quantity is
stored under reaction type MT=3. Given that 𝜎(𝛾, 𝛾′) becomes negligible above the particle emission
threshold, we use 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛𝑜𝑛) for the following analysis (Figure 4), except in cases where only the MT = 5
cross section is available. In such instances, the MT = 5 cross section is used as a substitute.
We note that the particle emission threshold corresponds to the (𝛾, 𝑝) threshold (Table 1), though—due
to the substantial Coulomb barrier—the cross section for this reaction channel remains extremely low
(fractions of a millibarn) up to approximatively 15 MeV and only becomes appreciable well above its
threshold (generally peaking in the range of 22-27 MeV).

Single photoneutron cross section
Evaluations of the total single photoneutron reaction cross section, 𝜎(𝛾, 𝑛), are stored under MT = 4. Only
ENDF/B-VIII.1 and TENDL-2023 provide data for this reaction channel, and their results differ significantly
(Figure 5), consistent with discrepancies observed in the total photoabsorption cross section. Where
experimental data for the (𝛾, 𝑛) channel exist, the ENDF/B-VIII.1 evaluation consistently provides the most
reliable agreement with measurements.
For natural tungsten, the TENDL-2023 evaluation was reconstructed as a weighted sum of the stable
isotopes, using their natural abundances. This approach was not possible for ENDF/B-VIII.1 due to the
absence of an evaluation for the 184W isotope, which is only available under MT = 5. Compared to
experimental cross section data from Veyssiere et al., TENDL-2023 tends to overestimate values below the
peak and slightly underestimate them above the peak, mirroring trends observed for individual isotopes.
In summary, ENDF/B-VIII.1 provides the most reliable evaluations of the single photoneutron cross sections
for all stable tungsten isotopes except 184W. A new evaluation including at least the MT = 4 (𝛾, 𝑛) and MT
= 16 (𝛾, 2𝑛) reaction channels is recommended for 184W, obtained by leveraging the available experimental
data for the single photoneutron cross section, the neutron production cross section, and the available
experimental reaction data for natural tungsten.

Double photoneutron cross section
Evaluations of the double photoneutron reaction cross section, 𝜎(𝛾, 2𝑛), are stored under MT = 16. Only
ENDF/B-VIII.1 and TENDL-2023 provide data for this reaction channel, and their results are fairly consistent
with each other except for 180W and 183W, where the cross sections present a somewhat larger high-energy
tail compared to ENDF/B-VIII.1 (Figure 6).
Berman et al. (1969) measured the sum of the (𝛾, 2𝑛) and (𝛾, 2𝑛𝑝) cross sections for 186W. Given that the
𝜎(𝛾, 2𝑛𝑝) contribution is negligible, these experimental data can be used to constrain the evaluated double
photoneutron cross section. The ENDF/B-VIII.1 evaluation reproduces the Berman data well for photon
energies below 17 MeV but slightly overpredicts the cross section at higher energies. TENDL-2023 presents
a similar behavior above 17 MeV, but peaks too early overestimating the experimental data below 15 MeV.
Importantly, the (𝛾, 2𝑛) cross section for 186W is one of the largest measured—second only to 238U—
reaching a peak value of approximately 250 mb at 15.7 MeV.
No experimental (𝛾, 2𝑛) data are available for the other isotopes, except—indirectly—data on natural
tungsten (natW). In this case, the TENDL-2023 evaluation was reconstructed as a weighted sum of the stable
isotopes, using their natural abundances. This approach was not possible for ENDF/B-VIII.1 due to the
absence of an evaluation for the 184W isotope, which is only available under MT = 5. The resulting TENDL-

2023 provides a fair description of the experimental cross section data from Veyssiere et al., though the
agreement deteriorates above 17 MeV where the high-energy tail of the evaluation overestimates the data.

Conclusions
We reviewed the available photonuclear data for the stable tungsten isotopes 180W, 182W, 183W, 184W, and
186
 W and natural tungsten, natW with a focus on experimental measurements and recent nuclear data
evaluations from major nuclear data libraries, including ENDF/B-VIII.1, JENDL-5, IAEA-2019, and TENDL-
2023. Our analysis highlights that experimental data remain limited—especially for single and double
photoneutron channels—and that notable discrepancies exist between total photoabsorption cross
sections measured via the absorption method (which requires subtraction of the evaluated atomic cross
section) and those obtained with direct techniques, particularly below photon energies of 12 MeV. These
differences may be influenced by changes in the atomic cross section evaluation since the 1980s, when
much of the absorption data were analyzed. At the same time, the use of multiplicity sorting techniques to
extract exclusive cross sections from direct measurements of neutron production cross sections can
introduce further uncertainties. For experiments measuring neutron production cross sections, it would
therefore be recommendable to use these data directly in the evaluation process to minimize additional
sources of error. However, this approach requires evaluating at least the single and double photoneutron
cross sections separately, rather than relying on their sum (typically stored in MT = 5 for these isotopes).
Among the evaluated libraries, JENDL-5 provides the most robust total photonuclear cross sections, with
IAEA-2019 and ENDF/B-VIII.1 also demonstrating good agreement with experimental data. For exclusive
channels, ENDF/B-VIII.1 is generally preferred, except for 184W, where supplementation with TENDL-2023
could be used as a short-term stopgap. Specifically, the 184W ENDF/B-VIII.1 data file could be supplemented
with additional MT = 4 (𝛾, 𝑛) and MT = 16 (𝛾, 2𝑛) reaction channels. In the short term, this could be
achieved by adopting the TENDL-2023 184W(𝛾, 2𝑛) cross section for MT = 16 and using the difference
between the ENDF/B-VIII.1 MT = 5 cross section and the TENDL-2023 MT = 16 cross section for MT = 4.
Experimental data for natural tungsten and for the neutron production cross section in 184W should be used
to evaluate the validity and quality of this approach. A more accurate longer-term solution will require a
new Hauser-Feshbach evaluation of the 184W photonuclear cross sections, taking advantage of all available
experimental data including neutron-production cross section and natW cross sections. Work in this
direction is under way using the LLNL developed Hauser-Feshbach code YAHFC [22].
Overall, the ENDF/B-VIII.1 evaluations (except for the 184W isotope) offer both reliability and good coverage
of exclusive channels, making them preferable at this stage, despite the absence of covariance data. In the
immediate term, these nuclear data files can be supplemented with preliminary uncertainty estimates for
the main reaction channels, calculated as the percent error required for two-thirds of the data points to
fall within the evaluation’s uncertainty range across the entire energy spectrum. While this method

provides a reasonable first step in the absence of alternatives, enabling consistency checks, a more
thorough analysis is warranted. This approach can be further refined by employing a Hauser-Feschbach
code such as YAHFC to estimate the correlated uncertainties and rescaling them so that the diagonal of the
covariance matrix is of the same order as the simple error estimate. This would then enable some estimate
of cross-correlation as well, albeit based on theory. Further, developments are also needed to incorporate
measurement uncertainties.
Continued refinement of evaluated data and their associated uncertainties—guided by new experimental
measurements and careful updates to atomic cross sections—will be essential for providing accurate
tungsten photonuclear reaction data for both basic and applied research.
