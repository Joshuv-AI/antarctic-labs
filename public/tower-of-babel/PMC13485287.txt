Association between Percutaneous Coronary Intervention and Long-Term Clinical Outcomes in Patients with Chronic Kidney Disease and Acute Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Jing Zhou, Junjuan Li, Yan Xu, Ru Wang, Liufu Cui, Qing Yang, Shouling Wu, Xin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) are at a high risk of adverse outcomes after acute myocardial infarction (AMI); however, evidence on long-term outcomes after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in this population remains limited.MethodsThis prospective cohort study, nested within the Kailuan Study, included patients with CKD who experienced AMI between 2006 and 2020. Participants were classified into PCI and non-PCI groups based on documented PCI receipt and were matched 1:2 on age and sex (452 PCI; 745 non-PCI). Follow-up began at the time of AMI diagnosis or PCI and continued until December 31, 2023. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, with secondary outcomes being new-onset heart failure and CKD progression. Time-segmented Cox models were used for all-cause mortality, and standard Cox models were used for secondary outcomes.ResultsDuring follow-up, PCI receipt was associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality compared with no PCI. The association was stronger during the first 5 years of follow-up (hazard ratio [HR], 0.34; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.24–0.47) and was attenuated thereafter (HR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.51–1.04). No significant associations were observed between PCI receipt and either new-onset heart failure (HR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.72–1.57) or CKD progression (HR, 1.11; 95% CI, 0.72–1.71).ConclusionIn patients with CKD and AMI, PCI was associated with lower all-cause mortality during the first 5 years of follow-up, with no significant differences in new-onset heart failure or CKD progression.

Introduction

According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023, the global age-standardized prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) among adults was 14.2% [1]. In 2023, China accounted for approximately 152 million affected individuals, and CKD was associated with 1.48 million deaths worldwide [1]. Moreover, approximately 50% of patients with CKD die of cardiovascular disease [2, 3]. The risk of cardiovascular mortality increases progressively as the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) declines below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, with myocardial infarction being a predominant cause [4, 5]. Approximately one-third of patients presenting with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) have comorbid CKD, and impaired renal function is consistently associated with worse outcomes after AMI [6].

Patients with CKD and AMI often present with complex and severe coronary lesions. More than 50% of patients with CKD present with stenosis in two or more coronary vessels [7, 8] and approximately 67% present with B2/C-type coronary lesions [9]. Despite this high disease burden, revascularization rates remain low (10–40%), largely due to concerns regarding contrast-induced nephropathy and procedural risk [10, 11]. Current guideline recommendations reflect this uncertainty. The 2023 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines recommend revascularization for patients with CKD presenting with acute coronary syndrome (ACS), but with low-level evidence [12], whereas the 2024 Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines suggest revascularization only in selected high-risk scenarios [13]. Furthermore, evidence regarding the benefits of revascularization is inconsistent. The International Study of Comparative Health Effectiveness with Medical and Invasive Approaches – CKD (ISCHEMIA-CKD) subgroup study enrolled patients with advanced CKD and stable coronary artery disease, explicitly excluding those with AMI, and demonstrated no mortality benefit from an initial invasive strategy [14]. In contrast, a meta-analysis including patients with less advanced CKD suggested a potential survival benefit associated with revascularization [15]. However, data focusing on patients with CKD and AMI, particularly from Asian populations with long-term follow-up, remain limited.

Therefore, using the Kailuan cohort, we aimed to evaluate the association between percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and long-term clinical outcomes in patients with CKD and AMI. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, with secondary outcomes being new-onset heart failure and CKD progression.

Methods

Study Population

This study was based on the Kailuan Study (registration number: CHiCTR-TNC-11001489). Details of the study design and population have been described previously [16]. AMI and PCI were ascertained annually using multiple complementary data sources, including the Municipal Social Insurance Institution database, the Hospital Discharge Register, biennial health examination questionnaires, and annual follow-up assessments. These data sources were used to identify incident first AMI events, determine PCI status, and define the study population. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kailuan General Hospital (Approval No. 5, 2006).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Participants were selected from the Kailuan Health Examination cohort (2006–2020). Among 46,592 individuals with CKD, 2,360 experienced AMI either concurrently with or after CKD diagnosis; participants who underwent PCI before CKD and AMI (n = 693) or died on the day of AMI (n = 27) were excluded, leaving 1,640 eligible participants (Fig. 1).

For the CKD progression analysis, a separate analytic cohort was constructed among participants with available longitudinal kidney function data. An additional 755 participants were excluded because they lacked follow-up examinations (n = 732) or baseline end-stage renal disease (ESRD) (n = 23), yielding a final cohort of 885 participants (online suppl. For new-onset heart failure analysis, this outcome was evaluated as a secondary endpoint within the all-cause mortality cohort. The participants with pre-existing heart failure (n = 125) and those who developed heart failure on the day of AMI or PCI (n = 43) were excluded.

Grouping Criteria

The participants were classified according to their actual receipt of PCI. The PCI group included patients who underwent PCI, whereas the non-PCI group included those who did not.

Endpoint Events and Follow-Up Duration

All-cause mortality (death from any cause) was the primary endpoint of the study. New-onset HF and CKD progression were pre-specified secondary endpoints. Mortality data were obtained from the Kailuan Social Insurance Agency. Participants participated in standardized health examinations at 2-year intervals.

For patients who did not undergo PCI, follow-up was initiated at the time of AMI diagnosis, whereas for patients who underwent PCI, follow-up was initiated at the time of PCI. For participants who died, the follow-up period ended on the date of death. Participants without endpoint events were followed up until December 31, 2023.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p value <0.05. Baseline covariates were defined using the most recent health examination before the AMI index. Patients who underwent PCI were matched to non-PCI patients in a 1:2 ratio by age (±1 year) and sex using nearest-neighbor matching using the count function. Treatment status (PCI vs. non-PCI) was specified as the exposure of interest. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (P25, P75), as appropriate, and baseline balance was assessed using standardized mean differences. Multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to evaluate the association between PCI and study outcomes, with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior research [24]. The proportional hazard assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and graphical diagnostics (online suppl. Fig. S2). For all-cause mortality, evidence of non-proportional hazards was observed; therefore, time-segmented Cox models were applied, with follow-up stratified at 5 years based on pre-specified clinical considerations and inspection of hazard functions. Standard Cox models were used for secondary outcomes (new-onset HF and CKD progression).

Three sequential multivariate models were constructed. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, current smoking, current alcohol consumption, and physical activity. Model 2 was further adjusted for body mass index, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), FBG, TC, LDL-C, hs-CRP, eGFR, proteinuria, and modified CCI. For descriptive analyses, modified CCI was categorized as 0, 1, and ≥2; for multivariable analyses, it was dichotomized as 0 vs. ≥1 because of its skewed distribution and the limited number of participants in the higher categories. Model 3 was additionally adjusted for baseline medication use, including antihyperglycemic agents, calcium channel blockers, other antihypertensive agents, and the number of GDMT classes (0, 1–2, and ≥3).

Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, and no evidence of problematic collinearity was observed (online suppl. Table S3). Model fit was assessed using the Akaike Information Criterion (online suppl. Table S4). Sequential models were specified a priori to evaluate the robustness of associations under increasing covariate adjustment rather than to select a single best-fitting model.

The cumulative incidence of outcomes was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted according to age and diabetes status. Sensitivity analyses excluded patients who did not receive any of the following baseline GDMT: β-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers, antiplatelet agents, and lipid-lowering agents; had an eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m2; had ESRD; had a history of malignancy; had a history of CABG; died within the first year; or underwent PCI more than 30 days after AMI. In addition, the fully adjusted Cox model was repeated after replacing baseline medication use with post-AMI medication use.

As a sensitivity analysis for all-cause mortality, we reestimated the fully adjusted Cox proportional hazards model (Model 3), replacing the modified CCI with individual major comorbidities, including heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, and malignancy, while keeping all other covariates unchanged.

Additional analyses included the calculation of E-values to assess robustness to unmeasured confounding factors [25]. Supplementary analyses were used to compare all-cause mortality between the PCI-treated AMI patients with and without CKD.

Missing values for SBP, DBP, body mass index, FBG, TC, LDL-C, hs-CRP, and proteinuria were addressed using multiple imputation with a fully conditional specification under a missing-at-random assumption(online suppl. Table S5). Five imputed datasets were generated with 10 iterations each, and the parameter estimates were combined using Rubin’s rules (online suppl. Table S6). This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (online suppl. Table S7).

Results

Comparison of the Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

A total of 46,592 individuals who underwent Kailuan health examinations were diagnosed with CKD between 2006 and 2020. Among them, 2,360 experienced AMI either concurrently with or after CKD diagnosis, and 1,640 developed AMI after CKD diagnosis. Among these participants, 452 underwent PCI treatment. After 1:2 matching for age and sex, the non-PCI group included 745 participants, yielding 1,197 participants in the final analysis. Among them, 1,091 (91.14%) were men, and 106 (8.86%) were women, with a mean age of 60.32 ± 9.30 years (Fig. 1 and online suppl. Fig. S1).

In the all-cause mortality and CKD progression cohorts, the PCI group demonstrated a higher prevalence of a history of CABG and greater baseline use of medications from ≥3 GDMT classes than the non-PCI group (Table 1 and online suppl. Table S9). In the CKD progression cohort, baseline calcium channel blocker use and prior heart failure were also more frequent in the PCI group. In addition, post-AMI use of medications from ≥3 GDMT classes was likewise more frequent in the PCI group (online suppl. Tables S8 and S10). Other baseline characteristics were broadly comparable.

Results of Multivariable Cox Proportional Hazards Models

Association between PCI and All-Cause Mortality

The median follow-up duration for all-cause mortality was 6.35 (3.15, 11.10) years. The incidence densities were higher in the non-PCI group than in the PCI group both within the first 5 years (35.5 vs. 14.4 per 1,000 person-years) and beyond 5 years of follow-up (25.1 vs. 16.3 per 1,000 person-years). The Kaplan-Meier curves similarly demonstrated a significantly lower cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality in the PCI group (log-rank p < 0.05, Fig. 2a).

Between-group comparisons were performed using the log-rank test. In time-segmented multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, PCI receipt was consistently associated with lower all-cause mortality during the first 5 years of follow-up across sequentially adjusted models; in the fully adjusted model, the HR was 0.34 (95% CI, 0.24–0.47; p < 0.001). Beyond 5 years, this association was attenuated and no longer statistically significant in the fully adjusted model (HR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.51–1.04; p = 0.08) (Table 2).

Association between PCI and New-Onset Heart Failure in the All-Cause Mortality Cohort

The median follow-up duration for new-onset HF was 6.51 (3.48, 11.30) years. The incidence densities were similar between groups (15.4 vs. 17.9 per 1,000 person-years in the non-PCI and PCI groups, respectively), with no significant difference in cumulative incidence by the Kaplan-Meier analysis (log-rank p > 0.05, Fig. 2b). In the fully adjusted Cox model, PCI receipt was not significantly associated with new-onset HF (HR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.72–1.57) (Table 2).

Association between PCI and CKD Progression in the Matched Cohort

In the CKD progression cohort with longitudinal kidney function data, the median follow-up was 9.34 (5.59, 12.70) years. The incidence densities were 14.1 and 18.3 per 1,000 person-years in the non-PCI and PCI groups, respectively, with no significant difference in cumulative incidence by the Kaplan-Meier analysis (log-rank p > 0.05, Fig. 2c). Consistently, PCI receipt was not associated with CKD progression in multivariable Cox models (Model 1: HR, 1.15; 95% CI, 0.76–1.74; Model 2: HR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.69–1.62; Model 3: HR, 1.11; 95% CI, 0.72–1.71) (Table 2).

Subgroup Analyses of Associations of PCI with All-Cause Mortality, New-Onset Heart Failure, and CKD Progression

Pre-specified subgroup analyses by age (<65 vs. ≥ 65 years) and diabetes status showed broadly consistent associations between PCI and lower all-cause mortality within the first 5 years. After 5 years, this association weakened, and statistical significance was maintained only in the diabetic subgroup (Fig. 3). PCI was not significantly associated with new-onset HF or CKD progression in any age or diabetes subgroup (online Suppl. Fig. S3 and S4).

Sensitivity Analysis of Associations of PCI with All-Cause Mortality, New-Onset Heart Failure, and CKD Progression

Sensitivity analyses were broadly consistent with the primary findings. For all-cause mortality, the inverse association between PCI and death remained significant within the first 5 years across all sensitivity exclusions (p < 0.05, Fig. 4). After 5 years, the effect estimates weakened, and the association was no longer statistically significant (p > 0.05, Fig. 4). Furthermore, the fully adjusted Cox model, with post-AMI medication use, confirmed that PCI was associated with reduced all-cause mortality within 5 years (p < 0.05, online suppl. Table S11).

Notably, after excluding deaths within the first year, PCI was still associated with lower all-cause mortality within 5 years (HR, 0.43; 95% CI, 0.29–0.64), though the association was no longer statistically significant beyond 5 years (HR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.50–1.02). After excluding patients who underwent PCI more than 30 days after AMI or had a history of CABG, the association between PCI and all-cause mortality remained statistically significant within 5 years in the fully adjusted Cox model (p < 0.05, Fig. 4).

In an additional sensitivity analysis for all-cause mortality, the modified CCI in the fully adjusted Cox model was replaced by individual major comorbidities. The association between PCI and lower all-cause mortality remained significant within 5 years (p < 0.05, online suppl. Table S12).

PCI was not significantly associated with new-onset HF or CKD progression in any sensitivity analysis, which was consistent with the primary model (p > 0.05, online suppl. Fig. S5 and S6). Sensitivity analyses using E-values indicated that the association between PCI and all-cause mortality was robust to potential unmeasured confounding within the first 5 years (online suppl. Table S13).

All-Cause Mortality among PCI-Treated AMI Patients with versus without CKD

In a supplementary analysis of PCI-treated patients with AMI, 452 patients with CKD were matched by age and sex in a 1:2 ratio to 786 patients without CKD. The median follow-up duration was 6.61 (4.02, 10.40) years. Crude all-cause mortality rates were higher in those with CKD than in those without CKD (28.1 vs. 21.8 per 1,000 person-years; log-rank p < 0.05; online suppl. Fig. S7), but this difference was attenuated and no longer statistically significant after multivariable adjustment (fully adjusted HR, 1.11; 95% CI, 0.83–1.47; online suppl. Tables S14 and S15).

Discussion

The primary finding of this study was that among patients with CKD and AMI, PCI receipt was associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality, particularly within the first 5 years of follow-up. However, this association weakened beyond 5 years and was no longer statistically significant. No significant associations were observed between PCI and new-onset HF or long-term CKD progression. In a supplementary analysis of PCI-treated patients with AMI, the crude mortality difference between those with and without CKD was no longer significant after multivariate adjustment. Given the observational design, these findings should be interpreted as associations and may still be influenced by treatment selection and residual confounding.

This study demonstrated that early revascularization of the culprit vessel is associated with a significant reduction in long-term all-cause mortality in patients with CKD and AMI. These findings align with prior studies showing reductions in all-cause mortality of 38% in CKD patients with non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) [26] and 30% in those with ACS [27], without significantly increasing the risk of renal failure (HR, 1.03; 95% CI: 0.89–1.19) [27]. Notably, these results differ from ISCHEMIA-CKD, which enrolled patients with stable coronary artery disease and advanced CKD (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2) [14]. This difference may reflect variation in clinical context and renal severity: our cohort comprised AMI patients, most with relatively preserved kidney function (eGFR > 45 mL/min/1.73 m2), in whom timely revascularization may confer benefit [28].

This study found that PCI was associated with lower all-cause mortality, with a stronger early benefit that diminished over time. This pattern likely reflects the multifactorial nature of cardiovascular risk in CKD. While PCI improves early outcomes by restoring coronary perfusion, long-term results are influenced more by CKD-related factors (e.g., inflammation, oxidative stress, neurohormonal and hemodynamic stress, uremic toxicity, and mineral-bone disorder) and multimorbidity (including cardiometabolic and non-cardiometabolic conditions, such as frailty and infection susceptibility) [29, 30]. Despite adjusting for comorbidity burden, residual confounding, treatment selection, and survivorship bias may have affected the findings.

In this cohort, PCI was not significantly associated with new-onset HF in patients with CKD and AMI. While PCI alleviates ischemia and minimizes myocardial damage, HF in CKD is driven by factors such as systemic comorbidities, calcified coronary artery disease, microvascular dysfunction, and ongoing neurohormonal and inflammatory changes [31, 32]. These pathophysiological processes are not directly modified by PCI alone, and may therefore attenuate its association with the risk of subsequent HF.

In this cohort, early revascularization of the culprit vessel was associated with lower long-term all-cause mortality in patients with CKD and AMI. Despite concerns regarding contrast-associated acute kidney injury, we found no evidence of the association of PCI with long-term deterioration in renal function. These findings may help inform revascularization decisions in this high-risk population. GDMT may also have contributed to favorable cardiovascular outcomes.

Differences in post-AMI GDMT may have contributed to the observed outcome differences between the PCI and non-PCI groups. Patients undergoing PCI were more likely to receive GDMT after AMI, whereas those managed without PCI may have been frailer, had greater disease severity, had type 2 myocardial infarction or conditions not suitable for PCI, had contraindications to invasive treatment or pharmacotherapy, experienced early mortality, or had fewer opportunities for treatment initiation and optimization [33, 34]. Therefore, the association between PCI and lower mortality may partly reflect differences in treatment selection and post-AMI medical management rather than an isolated effect of PCI itself. Although we adjusted for available covariates, residual confounding related to disease severity, frailty, contraindications, medication adherence, and access to care cannot be fully excluded.

Notwithstanding the prospective design and extended follow-up period, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, residual confounding and selection bias cannot be fully excluded. Although baseline characteristics were defined using the most recent examination before AMI to capture clinical status close to the index event, selection-related bias may remain. In addition, despite efforts to minimize immortal-time bias, residual bias and the potential influence of PCI timing cannot be excluded. E-value analyses suggested that the findings were more robust within the first 5 years of follow-up. Second, limited clinical data prevented detailed characterization of the AMI subtype, coronary anatomy, and lesion complexity, which may affect treatment selection and outcomes. Additionally, cause-of-death data were not systematically available, limiting cause-specific mortality analyses. Given the large size of the Kailuan cohort, recurrent AMI events were not systematically adjudicated after the first AMI, potentially underestimating the total burden of cardiovascular events. Third, the timing of the history of CABG relative to PCI or AMI could not be consistently determined, potentially introducing residual confounding, although the results remained consistent after excluding those with CABG. Fourth, although some comorbidities were recorded at relatively low frequencies, likely due to registry-based ascertainment, sensitivity analyses using individual major comorbidities instead of the modified CCI yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of our findings, while residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Fifth, patients with eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 were underrepresented (10.36%), limiting statistical power and generalizability to those at the highest cardiovascular risk. Finally, the low proportion of women in the cohort, likely reflecting the occupational structure of the study population, limited the statistical power to detect sex-specific differences. Although no significant PCI-by-sex interaction was observed for all-cause mortality (P for interaction = 0.78), new-onset heart failure (P for interaction = 0.67), or CKD progression (P for interaction = 0.78), sex-specific differences cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

In this real-world cohort of patients with CKD and AMI, PCI receipt was associated with lower all-cause mortality during the first 5 years of follow-up. However, no significant association was observed between new-onset HF and CKD progression.
