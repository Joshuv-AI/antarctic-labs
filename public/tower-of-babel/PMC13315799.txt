Explainable EEG-based machine learning for early diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease and frontotemporal dementia
Authors: Fahman Saeed, Sultan Aldera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The quick and accurate diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD) is a significant and unresolved challenge in clinical neurology, with early identification being crucial for prompt intervention and disease management. This study presents AutoSSM-ICA-EEG, an automated and interpretable framework that incorporates Fast Independent Component Analysis (FastICA) for artifact removal and relevant feature extraction, few-shot AutoML for efficient hyperparameter optimization and dynamic architecture adaptation, and State-Space Modeling (SSM) for temporal EEG dynamics, facilitating concurrent classification of dementia subtypes and regression of clinical severity. Three distinct datasets were utilized: (1) OpenNeuro ds006036 (open-eyes/photic stimulation EEG; AD, FTD, and Healthy Controls; n = 88), functioning as the primary tri-class benchmark; (2) OpenNeuro ds004504 (closed-eyes resting-state EEG; AD and HC; n = 65), employed for cross-condition generalization; and (3) ADFSU (resting-state EEG; AD and HC; external institution, UNESP Brazil), utilized for cross-institution transfer assessment. Rigorous subject-level leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation was uniformly implemented throughout all four evaluation protocols, ensuring that all preprocessing, ICA decomposition, and model tuning were performed solely within each training fold to avert data leakage. In the primary tri-class benchmark, the model attained an accuracy of 87.5%, macro sensitivity of 87.5%, macro specificity of 95.4%, macro F1-score of 88.7%, and macro AUC of 0.99. The regression analysis of MMSE severity produced a R² of 0.81 ± 0.04, with a mean absolute error (MAE) of 1.64 ± 0.31 and a root mean square error (RMSE) of 1.71 ± 0.21. Cross-condition generalization (Closed→Open and Open→Closed) and cross-institution transfer (ADFSU↔OpenNeuro; AD sensitivity = 83.9%) further validate the framework’s resilience beyond a singular dataset or recording situation. ICA-refined SHAP channel-level explainability identifies Ch11 and Ch13 as the principal neurophysiological biomarkers for subtype classification and MMSE severity regression, respectively, with Ch17 serving as the most sensitive predictor of disease duration — corroborating established frontotemporal EEG signatures of neurodegeneration. These findings establish AutoSSM-ICA-EEG as a potential, neurophysiologically interpretable paradigm for EEG-based dementia screening, subject to prospective multi-site clinical validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57069-1.

Introduction

Neurodegenerative illnesses, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD), constitute a significant and escalating worldwide health challenge, with prevalence anticipated to rise markedly in aging populations1. Early and precise diagnosis is crucial for facilitating prompt interventions, enhancing patient outcomes, and fostering precision medicine initiatives2. Among the current neuroimaging modalities, electroencephalography (EEG) has emerged as a viable candidate due to its non-invasive nature, high temporal resolution, portability, and relatively low cost3,4. EEG has demonstrated the ability to indicate distinctive changes in brain activity linked to dementia and cognitive deterioration, presenting potential as a biomarker for diagnosis and disease progression5,6.

Even with these benefits, recognizing dementia via EEG is still hard. EEG signals show a lot of variation between and among subjects, can be easily messed up by eye, muscle, and environmental aberrations, and are generally only available in tiny, mixed datasets7. These characteristics decrease the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), make it harder to extract features, and make it harder to reproduce results across research. Conventional deep learning methodologies, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs), have yielded promising outcomes in EEG analysis8,9; nevertheless, they are significantly dependent on manual model design and hyperparameter optimization. This process of trying things out and seeing what works takes a lot of time, is individual to each dataset, and can lead to overfitting, especially when there isn’t much data10,11. As a result, it is still hard to find strong and generalizable EEG-based diagnostic systems.

This study builds on the authors’ previous work by adapting and extending the SSM-FastICANet, a hybrid state-space and FastICA model initially designed for time-series forecasting in complex dynamical systems, to the clinical EEG domain. The same SSM-ICA synergy is utilized for dementia subtype classification and cognitive severity regression12. The state-space models (SSMs)13,14 and automated machine learning (AutoML)15 present intriguing solutions to address these constraints. SSMs offer a structured method for modeling EEG as a temporal dynamical system, effectively capturing long-range dependencies with fewer parameters than CNNs or RNNsAutoML makes it easier to build models by automatically searching through architectural and hyperparameter spaces. This results in models that are optimized and can be reproduced. Along with these changes, independent component analysis (ICA) is a well-known way to separate neural sources from artifacts16. This makes EEG features better for learning tasks that come after them.

In this research, we present a configurable dynamic few-shot learning framework AutoSSM-ICA-EEG that amalgamates Independent Component Analysis (ICA) with AutoML-driven state space methods (SSMs) for the diagnosis and prognosis of dementia. Our approach utilizes a conventional loss-based few-shot strategy that directs the dynamic augmentation of model complexity via block growth, while concurrently adjusting hyperparameters like learning rate and regression weight. The framework is validated across three independent datasets employing four evaluation protocols: (i) subject-level leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) on the combined OpenNeuro cohort (encompassing both open-eyes and closed-eyes conditions); (ii) cross-condition generalization between ds004504 (closed-eyes)17 and ds006036 (open-eyes)18; and (iii) cross-institution validation, utilizing the extensive ADFSU dataset19 for training and the merged OpenNeuro cohort for testing. All preprocessing, ICA decomposition, normalization, and model tuning are conducted exclusively within each LOSO fold utilizing just training data, hence preventing any data leaking.

The recordings from 19 standard electrodes with 4096 samples optimized using ICA and matched with clinical metadata such as age, Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores, and how long the disease had been going on. The results show that the suggested ICA-enhanced AutoML-SSM pipeline gives accurate results, balanced sensitivity and specificity for each class, and reliable regression performance. It also stays explainable using confusion matrices and predicted-versus-true graphs.

Related work and state-of-the-art results

Several studies have utilized this resource to formulate and assess machine learning algorithms for differential dementia diagnosis. We summarize notable approaches and their documented accuracies below.

Hand-crafted feature approaches

Initial research depended on manually extracted characteristics from the EEG power spectrum. Miltiadous et al.18 extracted relative band power (RBP) characteristics from each channel and employed support vector machines (SVM), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), multilayer perceptrons (MLP), and random forests (RF) for binary classification. Their optimal outcomes were 77.01% accuracy for AD against HC (RF) and 73.12% for FTD versus HC (MLP). They calculated rhythm energy properties (mean, variance, and interquartile range of five band-filtered signals) and noted somewhat improved performance: a decision tree attained 78.5% AD versus HC, while a random forest achieved 86.3% FTD versus HC. Lal et al. (2024)20 stablished a sliding-window framework utilizing singular-value decomposition (SVD) entropy and classified the features using KNN, attaining 91% accuracy for AD against HC and 93% for FTD versus HC. Parihar and Swami21 employed EEG data across various frequency bands (delta, theta, alpha, beta, and gamma) in conjunction with an Ensemble Bagged Learning (EL) algorithm to categorize patients as healthy, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), or frontotemporal dementia (FTD). Their methodology attained 93.6% accuracy for Alzheimer’s Disease against Healthy Controls and 87.0%.

Connectivity-based features

EEG connectivity metrics elucidate relationships among cerebral regions and can distinguish between them. kinds of dementia. Ma et al. (2024)22 calculated phase information flow (PHI) between electrode pairs and utilized PHI values along with demographic characteristics to train a support vector machine (SVM). Their baseline model attained 76.9% accuracy for Alzheimer’s Disease against Healthy Controls, 90.4% for Frontotemporal Dementia versus Healthy Controls, and 91.5% for Alzheimer’s Disease versus Frontotemporal Dementia. By eliminating superfluous data, they enhanced the accuracy of identifying between AD and FTD to 96.6%, demonstrating that connection factors can be very effective in differentiating between both conditions. Siuly et al. (2025) in23 introduces a system that amalgamates short-time Fourier transform (STFT) with convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for the identification of dementia (Alzheimer’s disease and frontotemporal dementia) using EEG data. After preprocessing, which encompasses filtering, artifact rejection, and Independent Component Analysis (ICA), EEG data are segmented and classified by brain lobes, with spectrograms generated using Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) to clarify time-frequency patterns. The CNNs subsequently categorize these spectrograms at both the lobe level and throughout the entire channel set. The results demonstrate that the parietal lobe is the most distinctive region, achieving 95.72% accuracy for FTD compared to HC and 92.25% for AD relative to HC, while whole-brain analysis yielded 95.59% for AD against HC and 93.14% for FTD versus HC.

Deep-learning methods

Recent research employs convolutional and transformer-based neural networks for spectrogram or raw signal representations. Miltiadous et al.24 introduced DICE-net, which integrates convolutional layers, a transformer encoder, and fully linked layers to classify band-power and coherence characteristics, achieving an accuracy of 83.28% for AD against HC and 74.96% for FTD versus HC. Chen et al.25 developed a dual-branch CNN–Vision Transformer (ViT) network that attained 87.33% accuracy in distinguishing AD from HC and 82.98% accuracy in differentiating FTD from HC. A study concentrating on the parietal lobe utilized a Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) to generate spectrograms for input into a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). The findings indicated that the parietal region exhibited the highest discriminative power, achieving an accuracy of 95.72% for Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD) versus Healthy Controls (HC) and 92.25% for Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) versus HC. When the same methodology was applied to the entire brain, the accuracy was 95.59% for AD versus HC and 93.14% for FTD versus HC. The results indicate that spatially focused spectrograms combined with deep networks can surpass previous feature-based methods. Tran et al. put forward EEG SSM as a way to characterize temporal dynamics directly. This model adds a spectral branch to an SSM, and it was 91% accurate when it came to categorizing AD, FTD, and healthy controls in a three-way challenge26. Their results highlight the benefit of incorporating long-range temporal dependencies and spectral information. Lalawat et al.27, introduced NeuroFormer, an innovative deep learning architecture aimed at the automatic classification of EEG signals into categories of Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal dementia, or healthy controls. Employing enhanced spectral, spatial, and temporal feature extraction, with attention and capsule network methodologies, NeuroFormer attains elevated diagnostic precision on a multi-class EEG dataset comprising 88 patients, with test accuracy above 95%. The method surpasses benchmark models, demonstrating significant potential for reliable, automated, and non-invasive early identification of neurodegenerative disorders through EEG data.

Our SSM-FastICANet framework further enhances the SSM by decomposing EEG into independent components with FastICA and using ICs statistics (variance, kurtosis, entropy) to initialize and adapt the model. Although this method has not yet been evaluated on the ds006036 dataset, its design aligns with the promising results of EEG-SSM and motivates the hybrid pipeline proposed in this research.

Recent hybrid EEG pipelines, including EEG-SSM, CNN–Transformer frameworks, and NeuroFormer27, utilize typical feature extraction or deep learning architectures alongside traditional training and validation techniques. Most previous research either employs ICA in a generic manner (e.g., EEG-SSM) or entirely neglects ICA-based component selection (e.g., CNN–Transformer, NeuroFormer). Compared to that, our methodology integrates clinical ICA selection with enhanced thresholds, coupled with episodic few-shot validation and adaptive model modification via AutoML. This combination specifically addresses the challenge of limited, diverse subject pools and enhances reliability and interpretability for clinical EEG applications. Methodology

This section details the dataset, preparation pipeline, model architecture, and stringent subject-level LOSO cross-validation protocol. Algorithm 1 and Fig. 1 present a comprehensive overview of the proposed AutoSSM-ICA-EEG pipeline. Preprocessing guarantees consistent channel selection and signal duration. FastICA isolates independent components by applying clinical thresholds (kurtosis > 3.5, variance > 0.14, entropy < 0.1). These supply an AutoML-driven state-space model (SSM) utilizing dynamic few-shot episodic training that autonomously adjusts model capacity and hyperparameters. Fine-tuning for each LOSO fold is conducted only on training participants, while evaluation is performed solely on held-out test individuals. Performance is documented over all 88 folds with statistical precision, enhanced by SHAP explainability for clinical interpretation.

Dataset

This research employed publicly accessible datasets:

Dataset 1: OpenNeuro ds006036 (condition_name=’open’ in code): 88 participants (36 Alzheimer’s Disease, 23 Frontotemporal Dementia, 29 Healthy Controls). EEG using open-eye and photic stimulation, utilizing 19 channels at 256 Hz for approximately 10-minute sessions, normalized to 4096 samples. Clinical metadata: age, Mini-Mental State Examination (0–30), duration of disease. This is the principal tri-class benchmark.

Dataset 2: OpenNeuro ds004504 (n = 65; 36 AD, 29 HC) (condition_name=’closed’ in code): Resting-state EEG with closed eyelids; binary classification of Alzheimer’s Disease versus Healthy Controls. Identical 19-channel, 256 Hz format as ds006036. Utilized for the assessment of cross-condition generalization.

Dataset 3: ADFSU on OSF19 is a binary EEG dataset from São Paulo State University (UNESP) in Brazil includes resting-state scalp recordings from AD patients and age-matched Healthy Controls. At 256 Hz, recordings follow the worldwide 10–20 electrode placement standard. 0 = HC, 1 = AD are binary designations. ADFSU is employed primarily for binary AD-vs-HC cross-institution transfer trials, as shown in Table 3. FTD participants are not included.

Feature extraction and selection using FastICA

The use of FastICA for preprocessing multichannel EEG data (19 channels, 4096 samples) arises from the physiological fact that scalp recordings are linear combinations of statistically independent brain inputs. Considering the specific challenges in dementia EEG analysis—such as increased artifact contamination in elderly individuals and the necessity to isolate network-specific pathological signatures—FastICA offers a methodical approach to source separation that directly meets these clinical needs. This viewpoint corresponds with the clinical comprehension of dementia as a progressing neurodegenerative condition rather than a fixed diseased state.

Dual-task output architecture and loss function design

The simultaneous optimization of diagnostic classification and regression forecasting demonstrates the intricate nature of clinical dementia assessment. Clinicians must distinguish between Alzheimer’s Disease (AD), Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD), and normal aging, while evaluating cognitive severity and determining progression rates for treatment planning. Our dual-task design replicates this clinical practice by employing shared neuronal representations to improve generalization performance.

The classification branch utilizes a softmax output layer to generate calibrated probability estimates for diagnostic categories. This probabilistic output is crucial for clinical applications that require explicit quantification of diagnostic confidence. The regression branch concurrently forecasts MMSE scores and disease duration, offering complementary quantitative evaluations of cognitive status and progression rate.

Dynamic architecture adaptation (AutoML)

An essential problem in clinical EEG modeling is reconciling model complexity with generalizability. Static network topologies frequently have difficulties in achieving this balance; they may either lack the necessary representational capacity to encapsulate the intricate temporal dynamics of dementia or contain an excessive number of parameters, resulting in overfitting on constrained datasets. Our system employs a dynamic block adaptation method that adjusts the capacity of the state-space model (SSM) throughout the training process.

This adaptive process functions iteratively. If the validation loss does not improve after a specified threshold (growth_patience = 5 episodes), indicating insufficient model capacity, an additional SSM block is dynamically incorporated into the architecture to augment its learning capabilities. This adaptive growth persists as long as quantifiable performance improvements are evident. Conversely, superfluous components that provide no substantial enhancement are eliminated to preserve model simplicity and clarity. The adaptation cycle continues until convergence, characterized by the lack of additional validation enhancements in subsequent iterations. This dynamic regulation of block expansion and pruning produces dataset-optimized structures without adding superfluous parameters. The equilibrium between expressive capacity and generalizability is especially vital in dementia research, where clinical datasets often exhibit limited sample sizes and inconsistent quality.

Few-shot AutoML pretraining

Before regular supervised fine-tuning, each experimental condition completed a few-shot prototype pretraining stage employing episodic meta-learning. In each episode, shots = 5 support samples per class were used to generate class prototype embeddings by averaging encoded support features in the shared latent space, and queries = 5 query samples per class were classed by minimum squared Euclidean distance to the nearest prototype. A joint episodic loss combining archetypal cross-entropy with a smooth-L1 regression term weighted by beta_mse was minimized across 40 training episodes per configuration.

A grid search over learning rate (lr) ∈ {1e-3, 2e-3, 5e-3} and beta ∈ {0.3, 0.5, 0.7} was conducted, giving 6 configurations examined each experiment. The ideal configuration as shown in Fig. 4 was selected by minimum validation episodic loss, and the matching pretrained weights were carried forward as the initialization for supervised fine-tuning. This two-stage method is particularly useful for small neuronal cohorts because the episodic pretraining compels the encoder to acquire class-discriminative embeddings before any standard cross-entropy gradient is applied, decreasing the possibility of overfitting to fold-specific training patterns.

Dynamic block growth was introduced into the pretraining stage: the model initialized with a single GRU-SSM block and grew by one block whenever validation episodic loss failed to improve for grow patience of (5) consecutive episodes, up to a maximum of 16 blocks, converging at an average of 3 ± 0.9 blocks. Growth events converted all existing block weights to the new model using compatible-key state dict matching, with newly inserted blocks initiated from scratch. The AutoML mechanism converged at an average of 3 ± 0.9 blocks across all experimental conditions and grid configurations, indicating that the dynamic growth criterion accurately determined the minimum sufficient model depth for each training partition.

All results were produced using stringent subject-level leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, wherein preprocessing, ICA decomposition, normalization, and hyperparameter optimization were restricted to the training data within each fold, thus reducing the chance of data leaking.

External cross-institution generalization

The external cross-institution generalization results indicate that the proposed technique may effectively transfer between separate EEG datasets while preserving strong discrimination among AD and HC classes. Upon training using the ADFSU dataset and conducting zero-shot evaluation on the integrated OpenNeuro cohort, the model attained sensitivities of 80.5% for Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) and 84.3% for Healthy Controls (HC), with associated AUC values of 0.80 and 0.89. In the other direction, training on the aggregated OpenNeuro cohort and testing on ADFSU resulted in sensitivities of 83.9% and 81.2%, with AUC values of 0.79 and 0.95 for AD, and HC, respectively.

The findings substantiate the primary objective of the external cross-institution experiment, which is to confirm that the proposed framework is not just reliant on dataset-specific attributes, but rather acquires disease-relevant EEG representations that maintain efficacy throughout cross-dataset transfer. The constant performance in both directions suggests that the strategy generalizes effectively to novel data obtained under varying institutional or dataset settings.

Cognitive severity regression

The dual-task regression branch was employed to forecast MMSE scores and disease duration for the OpenNeuro cohort, demonstrating robust performance with R² = 0.81 ± 0.04. The MMSE prediction yielded a mean absolute error of 1.64 ± 0.31 points and a root mean squared error of 1.71 ± 0.21 points. The ADFSU dataset was removed from the regression analysis due to the absence of necessary clinical metadata. The regression scatter plots demonstrated a strong correlation between predicted and actual values, with the majority of samples congregating near the identity line throughout the entire MMSE range. A marginally larger dispersion was noted in the intermediate MMSE range of 15–22, presumably indicating heightened within-group variation in that severity category. The findings suggest that the proposed framework may assess cognitive severity alongside diagnostic classification, hence affirming its utility as a comprehensive EEG-based instrument for both subtype categorization and clinical severity evaluation (Fig. 6).

Comparison with state-of-the-art

Compared to NeuroFusionNet and the Deep-SVM Hybrid, the proposed AutoSSM-ICA-EEG framework offers clinically important advantages beyond accuracy as shown in Table 2. First, it performs strict subject-level LOSO validation on the merged ds004504 + ds006036 cohort to eliminate data leakage from both rivals’ 10-fold CV methods, making its 87.5% accuracy estimate more cautious and reproducible. Second, its specificity is 96.3% for AD/HC compared to 79.31% for the Deep-SVM Hybrid, which is important for clinical screening as false-positive decisions might harm patients. The dual-task regression branch predicts continuous MMSE and disease duration (R²=0.81 ± 0.04), unlike other techniques. Fourth, unlike the Deep-SVM Hybrid, which loses over 20% accuracy under cross-condition testing, the proposed model is trained and verified equally in eye-open and eye-closed situations, indicating EEG acquisition variability robustness.

Cross-institution transfer from a different nation to the ADFSU dataset gives F1 of AD: 83.9% and HC:81.2 and AD-AUC = 0.79, proving generalizability better than cross-dataset testing under shared acquisition techniques.

Dynamic architecture adaptation

The AutoML block-growing mechanism converged at an average of 3 ± 0.9 SSM blocks across LOSO folds, with growth triggered in about 40% of folds when validation loss plateaued for 5 consecutive episodes. Early stopping activated before epoch 40 in the majority of folds (mean stopping epoch: 28.4 ± 6.1), demonstrating that the patience-8 criterion effectively prevented overfitting. The Reduce LROnPlateau scheduler decreased the learning rate a median of 1.4 times per fold in 65% of runs. Learning curves saved each experiment proved smooth convergence without oscillatory instability across all situations.

Overall model performance

The EEG-based model effectively discriminates between Alzheimer’s Disease (AD), Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD), and Healthy subjects, while also providing regression estimates for Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores and disease Duration. This dual classification and regression capability is valuable for comprehensive clinical assessment.

Scalp topographic projection of ICA-SHAP importance

To enhance the component-level SHAP analysis and reestablish anatomical interpretability, ICA-space SHAP weights were back-projected into scalp electrode space utilizing the FastICA mixing matrix A, calculated as SHAPchannel =A. SHAPIC, where A ∈ ℝ^(C×K) maps K independent components to C scalp electrodes without necessitating further model inference. The topographic map for AD versus HC classification Figure 9(a) highlights significance in the frontal and fronto-central regions, influenced by IC4 (AD SHAP = 0.0015) and IC1 (HC SHAP = 0.0021), aligning with the established neurophysiological characteristics of Alzheimer’s disease, namely prefrontal slow-wave intrusions and disrupted alpha coherence. In MMSE regression Figure 9(b), the back-projected map emphasizes temporal and centro-parietal electrodes, illustrating medial temporal atrophy and hippocampal-cortical disconnection associated with progressive cognitive decline. Conversely, disease duration regression reveals a widely distributed topography, suggesting that longitudinal disease progression is encoded diffusely across cortical networks rather than localized to a specific region. Demonstrating SHAP importance in both ICA component space and scalp electrode space fulfills complementary objectives: component-space attribution maintains fidelity to the model’s genuine computational pathway and mitigates volume conduction confounds present in raw EEG channels, whereas scalp-space projection allows clinicians and neurologists to correlate model decisions with recognized anatomical landmarks, thereby reconciling algorithmic explainability with practical clinical application.

Conclusion

This paper introduces AutoSSM-ICA-EEG, a cohesive and interpretable framework for EEG-based dementia diagnosis that concurrently tackles artifact-contaminated small datasets, dual-task classification and severity regression, as well as neurophysiological model accountability. Through the integration of FastICA-based component selection, few-shot AutoML-driven architecture adaptation, and dual-task state-space modeling, employing rigorous subject-level LOSO cross-validation across 88 folds, the framework achieved a tri-class accuracy of 87.5%, a macro AUC of 0.99, and an MMSE regression R² of 0.81 ± 0.04 on the primary benchmark, with an Alzheimer’s disease sensitivity of 83.9% under zero-shot cross-institution transfer. ICA-refined SHAP analysis at both the component and scalp channel levels identifies Ch11 as a universal biomarker for dementia across all tasks, Ch13 as the primary predictor of MMSE severity, and Ch17 as the most sensitive marker for disease duration — all aligning with established EEG neurodegeneration signatures in frontotemporal dementia. Collectively, these findings establish AutoSSM-ICA-EEG as a neurophysiologically valid, generalizable, and interpretable basis for accessible EEG-based dementia screening.

Limitation and future work

Notwithstanding the encouraging outcomes across four assessment methodologies, some limitations must be recognized. The key benchmark consists of merely 88 patients, and although stringent LOSO validation alleviates this concern, care is advised when generalizing to larger clinical populations; future research should emphasize prospective multi-site data gathering with FTD subjects. The current cross-institution transfer is confined to binary comparisons between AD and HC since the ADFSU dataset lacks FTD individuals, hence restricting external tri-class generalization. Third, while few-shot episodic training and subject-level leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) mitigate the danger of overfitting, independent prospective validation is crucial prior to clinical use. The ablation investigation validates EEG as the principal discriminative signal, as the absence of metadata results in just a − 4.7% decrease in accuracy; yet, performance may decline in scenarios where clinical metadata is lacking or inaccessible. The framework has been verified just on the standard 19-channel 10–20 system and need further modification for higher-density arrays and portable consumer-grade devices. Sixth, all trials are cross-sectional; longitudinal validation monitoring MMSE decrease in relation to model-predicted severity trajectories is the foremost objective for future research. Ultimately, the channel contributions revealed by SHAP (Ch11, Ch13, Ch17) represent associations learned by the model rather than causal mechanisms and necessitate independent neurophysiological validation. The entire AutoSSM-ICA-EEG codebase will be made available as open-source following the project’s conclusion, in accordance with the code sharing policy of Scientific Reports.
