A phase II basket trial of DART SWOG S1609: the salivary gland tumor cohort

Cohen, James Wallace, Hyun Seok Kang, Sarah Fenton, Liam Il-Young Chung, Christine M. McLeod, Helen X. Chen, Elad Sharon, Howard Streicher, Christopher W. Ryan, Megan Othus, Charles D. ABSTRACT
Background:Anti-programmed death-1 (PD-1)/cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen-4 antibodies are efficacious in various malignancies. The potential role of dual checkpoint inhibitors in many rare solid tumors is not established.Objectives:This study presents the results of ipilimumab–nivolumab in salivary gland neoplasm cohorts of the SWOG S1609 dual anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 blockade in rare tumors (DART) trial.Design:DART is a prospective, open-label, multicenter (1016 US sites), multi-cohort phase II trial of ipilimumab (1 mg/kg intravenously (IV) every 6 weeks) plus nivolumab (240 mg IV every 2 weeks).Methods:We performed a prospective, multicenter phase II clinical trial of ipilimumab (1 mg/kg IV every 6 weeks) plus nivolumab (240 mg IV every 2 weeks) in three salivary gland neoplasm cohorts: major and minor salivary gland and adenoid cystic cancers. Patients with adenoid cystic salivary gland tumors (N = 26) and other salivary gland neoplasms (N = 34) were evaluable. The most common site of origin was the parotid (31%, N = 8 adenoid cystic group; 68%, N = 23 remaining histologies).Results:In the adenoid cystic group, objective response rate (ORR), 4% (complete response (CR) 0%, N = 0; partial response (PR) 4%, N = 1); 6-month progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS), 32% (95% confidence interval (CI) 18%–57%) and 84% (95% CI 71%–100%), respectively. In the remaining histologic subtypes, the confirmed ORR was 9% (CR, 0%, N = 0; PR, 9%, N = 3); stable disease (SD) >6 months/PR/unconfirmed PR = 35%; 6-month PFS and OS, 34% (95% CI 21%–55%) and 88% (95% CI 78%–100%), respectively. The most common toxicities were fatigue (39%) and diarrhea (26%); diarrhea (8%) was the most common grade 3–4 immune-related adverse event.Conclusion:In salivary gland tumors, combined ipilimumab plus nivolumab resulted in only a 4% ORR in adenoid cystic carcinoma and 9% in all other histologies combined, though the latter showed clinical benefit (included SD >6 months) in 35% of patients.Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov registry: NCT02834013.

Introduction

Checkpoint inhibitors are a relatively novel class of anticancer therapy drugs that act to block immune protective mechanisms co-opted by tumor cells to avoid recognition by the host’s immune system. Multiple checkpoint inhibitors (pembrolizumab, ipilimumab, nivolumab, durvalumab, atezolizumab, cemiplimab, dostarlimab and avelumab, and tremelimumab) have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration to treat malignancies including, but not limited to, advanced melanoma, non-small-cell lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, Hodgkin’s lymphoma, urothelial cancers, head and neck squamous cell carcinomas, Merkel cell carcinomas, cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, and solid tumors with microsatellite instability or mismatch repair gene mutations.

The introduction of checkpoint inhibitors to the treatment regimens of malignancies, such as melanoma, non-small-cell lung cancer, and renal cell carcinoma (among others), has resulted in significant improvement in patient morbidity and mortality. These agents act by targeting immune protective mechanisms expressed by tumor cells, independent of histologic subtype. Combination dual anti-CTLA-4 and anti-programmed death-1 (PD-1)/L1 immunotherapy regimens have shown synergistic anti-tumor activity. CTLA-4 blockade allows early T-cell activation while PD-1 maintains T-cell effector function later in the immune response. 1 Therefore, it stands to reason that checkpoint inhibitors may be an effective therapy for patients with rare malignancies such as salivary gland cancers (SGCs).

SGCs account of 1%–6% of cancers of the head and neck and overall account for 3 per 100,000 cancer diagnoses annually in the United States.2,3 The World Health Organization (WHO) has released a classification system to aid in the diagnosis and risk stratification of these tumors; 28 different histologic subtypes of SGC have been identified.4,5 Arising from major salivary glands (such as the parotid gland) and minor salivary glands, the most common subtypes of SGC include mucoepidermoid carcinoma, adenoid cystic carcinoma, adenocarcinoma not otherwise specified, and salivary duct carcinoma.6–10

The rarity and heterogeneity of cancers included in the SGC category hamper efforts to design effective clinical trials and determine appropriate treatment regimens for these patients. To date, treatment options for SGCs remain limited, particularly in patients with metastatic or unresectable disease. No chemotherapy regimen has been found to prolong progression-free survival (PFS) or overall survival (OS) in SGCs. 6 The effect of dual immune checkpoint inhibitors on rare tumors such as SGCs remains unclear. This lack of effective treatment strategies in combination with the rarity and heterogeneity of tumor subtypes made SGCs an optimal tumor type to include in the SWOG 1609 dual anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 blockade in rare tumors (DART) trial.

The SWOG 1609 DART trial is a basket study combining ipilimumab with nivolumab in rare tumors at over 1000 clinical sites. Patients with SGCs were enrolled and then divided into cohorts based first on their histology (adenoid cystic carcinoma vs all other subtypes) and then on the site of origin (major salivary gland vs other sites). Cohort 34 included all adenoid cystic carcinoma subtypes, cohort 2 included all patients with other histologies that arose in the major salivary glands, and cohort 3 included all patients with other histologies that arose in other sites.

This paper presents the clinical data found after treatment in patients with advanced salivary gland tumors.

Patients and methods

This trial was conducted under the guidance of the SWOG Cancer Research Network. The initial protocol and all amendments were reviewed and approved by SWOG, the National Cancer Institute (NCI), the NCI Central Institutional Review Board, and the participating institution’s regulatory committees. All investigational agents were obtained through the Cancer Therapy Evaluation Program of the NCI under the NCI CRADA agreement with Bristol-Myers Squibb. Each study participant provided a voluntary, written, informed consent document approved by the human subject protection committee of each participating institution.

Rationale for the included study population

This basket trial included patients with rare tumors for which no existing clinical trial investigating dual immune checkpoint inhibitors is active. Rare cancers were defined as malignancies with an incidence of less than 6 in 100,000 per year.11,12 Tumor pathology and grade were reviewed by the participating institution’s pathologists or local pathologists, and the pathology reports were reviewed by the study principal investigators. Tumor types were defined by criteria published in the WHO Classification of Head and Neck Tumors, Fourth Edition. 13 A central pathology review was not performed. This paper includes the results obtained from three patient cohorts. The first (cohort 2) included all patients with salivary gland tumors of the major salivary glands that were not categorized as adenoid cystic carcinomas. The second (cohort 3) included all patients with salivary gland tumors arising from any other site (e.g., minor salivary glands) that were not categorized as adenoid cystic carcinomas. The third (Cohort 34) included all patients with salivary gland tumors categorized as adenoid cystic carcinomas.

Inclusion criteria and patient selection

Patients were considered eligible if they had a confirmed diagnosis of SGC and did not have available alternative standard therapy options that were associated with prolonged OS. As noted above, patients were placed within one of three categories based on site of origin and histological subtype. Patients were placed in the first cohort (cohort 2) if their tumor arose from a major salivary gland (defined as the parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands). The first and second cohorts included all histologies other than adenoid cystic carcinoma, which were placed in the third cohort (cohort 34). Patients were initially categorized at the discretion of the site’s primary investigator. They were then reviewed and, in some cases, recategorized at the time of study closure by the study authors based on pathology reports and clinical history. Patients were required at the time of enrollment to be at least 18 years of age, have a Zubrod performance status of 0–2, and have adequate hematologic, hepatic, thyroid, adrenal axis, and renal function (specifically an absolute neutrophil count ⩾1000/mcL, platelets ⩾75,000/mcL, hemoglobin ⩾8 g/dL, creatinine clearance ⩾50 mL/min, total bilirubin ⩽ 2.0 × institutional upper limit of normal (IULN), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and ALT ⩽ 3.0 × IULN, TSH, or free T4 serum ⩽ IULN, and normal adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)). Enrollees were required to practice adequate birth control during protocol participation, and a negative serum pregnancy test was required for all women of childbearing potential at the time of enrollment.

Treatment and monitoring

Patients were treated with nivolumab 240 mg intravenously (IV) every 2 weeks and ipilimumab 1 mg/kg IV every 6 weeks on a continuous schedule, with dose adjustments and brief breaks from therapy specified in the protocol for treatment-related toxicities. Reasons for removal from the study included disease progression, symptomatic deterioration, treatment delay for any reason >56 days, unacceptable or immune-related toxicity with inability to decrease prednisone to <10 mg daily, or per patient request.

At the beginning of each cycle (or at least every 6 weeks), study patients were evaluated with a history and physical, laboratory analyses (complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, thyroid-stimulating hormone, free thyroxine, ACTH, cortisol, lipase), and toxicity assessment. Dose modifications for the management of immune-related adverse events were made according to the provided guidance criteria. Disease burden was assessed using imaging studies pre-study, week 8, week 16, week 24, and then every 12 weeks until progression. Patients were treated until disease progression, symptomatic deterioration, or toxicities developed.

Statistical methods and outcomes

This phase II trial was performed with the primary objective of evaluating the overall response rate (ORR, confirmed complete and partial responses (CR and PR) by RECIST v1.1 criteria) to attempt to distinguish between a true ORR of ⩽5% (null hypothesis) versus ⩾30% (alternative hypothesis). A two-stage design was used, wherein the first six eligible patients to receive protocol therapy were analyzed. If one or more had a confirmed CR or PR, 10 additional patients were to be accrued. Secondary objectives included PFS, OS, ORR by immune-related RECIST (iRECIST), PFS by iRECIST, and toxicity assessment. In both cohorts, more than 16 total patients were accrued due to faster-than-expected accrual in the second stage.

PFS (per RECIST v1.1) was measured from the start of protocol therapy to the first of progression or death by any cause, with patients last known to be alive without progression censored at the date of last contact. OS was measured from the date of study registration to the date of death by any cause, with patients last known to be alive censored at the date of last contact. Estimates of PFS and OS were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier; medians were calculated using the method of Brookmeyer and Crowley 14 ; and point estimate confidence intervals (CIs; e.g., 6-month PFS) were calculated using the log–log transformation. Primary ORR analysis CIs accounted for the two-stage design. 15 All analyses were performed using R version 4.0.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria).

Raw data for this study were generated at SWOG. Derived data supporting the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon request. The manuscript was prepared in accordance with the SPIRIT 2025 Checklist. 16

Results

Patient characteristics

Thirty-six patients were registered in cohorts 2 and 3 (non-adenoid cystic histologies), and 29 patients were registered in cohort 34 (adenoid cystic histology) from 22 National Clinical Trial Network institutions. Sixty patients total (34 patients from cohorts 2 and 3, and 26 patients from cohort 34) met eligibility criteria, were treated according to the protocol, and were included in the analyses (Table 1). The most common histology in cohorts 2 and 3 was adenocarcinoma (n = 15; Supplemental Table 1); the most common primary site in both cohorts was the parotid (68% in cohorts 2 and 3, and 31% in cohort 34; Table 1). Median age in cohorts 2 and 3 was 62 years old (range, 28–88 years), and in cohort 34 was 57.5 (range, 31–80 years), with 56% of patients being men in cohorts 2 and 3, and 35% of patients being men in cohort 34 (Table 1).

Outcomes

Among the 34 evaluable patients in the non-adenoid cystic group (cohorts 2 and 3), the ORR was 9% (95% confidence interval (CI): 2%–24%), with the best response detected being a PR (Table 2, Figures 1(a) and 2). An additional 6% of patients (N = 2) achieved an unconfirmed PR (Table 2, Figure 6(a)). Also, 18% of patients (N = 6) were found to have stable disease (SD) for more than 6 months, while 18% (N = 6) had SD for less than 6 months; 9% of patients (N = 3) were not assessable for response (Table 2). Response rates were not clustered within specific SGC histologies (Table 2). Six-month PFS was 34% (95% CI: 21%–55%), median PFS was 4 months (95% CI 2–8 months; Figure 4(a)). At the time of analysis, there were four ongoing responses (Figure 3(a)). Six-month OS was 88% (95% CI 78%–100%) and median OS was 19 months (95% CI 12–infinity months; Figure 4(a)).

In the adenoid cystic group (cohort 34) among the 26 patients treated, the ORR was 4% (N = 1, 95% CI 0.1%–20%) with a single confirmed PR (Table 2, Figures 1(b) and 3(b)); 27% of patients (N = 7) achieved SD for at least 6 months while 35% (N = 9) were found to have SD for less than 6 months (Table 2, Figures 1(b) and 3(b)). Four percent of patients (N = 1) were not assessed for response (Table 2). Six-month PFS was 32% (95% CI 18%–57%), and median PFS was 4 months (95% CI 3–6 months; Figure 4(b)). At the time of analysis, there were two ongoing responses (Figure 3(b)). Six-month OS was 84% (95% CI 71%–100%), and median OS was 15 months (95% CI 8–25 months; Figure 4(b)).

iRECIST evaluation of the adenoid cystic cohort showed a 6-month PFS of 36% (95% CI 22%–61%; Figure 5(b), 6(b), and 7(b)). One patient was found to have iStable disease (Figure 7(b)).

At the time of trial discontinuation, six patients in the non-adenoid cystic group (cohorts 2 and 3) and four in cohort 34 remained on therapy.

Toxicities

Overall, 81.7% of patients (N = 60) in all cohorts experienced any grade of adverse event; these occurrences are summarized in Table 3; 48.4% (N = 29) suffered a grade 3–5 adverse event. The most common adverse events included fatigue (40.0%, N = 24), diarrhea (25.0%, N = 15), AST elevation (21.7%, N = 13), maculopapular rash (21.7%, N = 13), and anorexia (21.7%, N = 13), among others. Seven treatment-related adverse events led to treatment discontinuation, and 1 resulted in patient mortality. Overall, 70.0% (N = 42) of adverse events were thought to be immune-mediated, the most common of these were diarrhea (25.0%, N = 15), AST elevation (21.7%, N = 13), and maculopapular rash (21.7%, N = 13). 30.0% (N = 18) immune-mediated adverse events were grade 3–5.

Discussion

The standard of care for localized disease is surgical resection. If high-risk characteristics such as positive margins, or lymphovascular or perineural invasion are identified on pathology, radiation therapy is often recommended.17–20 Interestingly, recommendations regarding chemotherapy have not been standardized, resulting in a high degree of inter-institutional variability and a lack of National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines. 6

Currently utilized chemotherapy regimens for metastatic or unresectable SGC include cisplatin plus 5-fluorouracil and CAP (cisplatin, doxorubicin, and cyclophosphamide). 21 Response rates are variable (between 15% and 70%) and average duration of response is 6–7 months. 5 No chemotherapy regimen has been shown to improve OS or disease-free survival. 6 A retrospective study of treatment with doxorubicin, cisplatin, carboplatin, 5-fluorouracil, methotrexate, docetaxel, or Bacillus Calmette-Guerin in metastatic SGC resulted in an OS of 35.2% while observation alone resulted in an OS of 27.6% (p = 0.747). 17 Another review of different regimens by Lagha et al. concluded that the most effective treatment plan included a platinum, 5-fluorouracil, and an anthracycline. For symptomatic patients, a more aggressive regimen including doxorubicin was recommended, while for asymptomatic patients, single-agent therapy was recommended. 22 Historically, studies of targeted drugs such as EGFR, HER2, and c-kit inhibitors have been disappointing. 23 Newer studies are underway to identify better targeted therapy regimens specific to molecular alterations. One study found an overall ORR of 63% (12 of 19 patients) with advanced SGC, treated with chemotherapy-free regimens matched to specific molecular alterations. 24 In this study, among 15 patients with HER2 amplification/overexpression (with or without mutations) who were treated with pertuzumab + trastuzumab, 9 had an objective response (1 CR, 8 PRs) for an ORR of 60% (9.2 months median response duration). The clinical benefit rate (defined by patients with objective responses or SD >4 months) was 67% (10/15), with a median PFS of 8.6 months, and a median OS of 20.4 months. The patient with a HER2 mutation showed SD (pertuzumab + trastuzumab, n = 1/1, PFS 11.0 months), and PRs were observed in patients with the PTCH-1 mutation (vismodegib, n = 1/1, PFS 14.3 months), BRAF mutation (vemurafenib, n = 1/1, PFS 18.5 months), and high TMB (atezolizumab, n = 1/1, PFS 5.5+ months). 24

Molecular profiling studies of SGCs suggest a minority of these tumors have PD-L1 expression on the cell surface, and when present, expression levels are low. 25 However, when PD-L1 or PD-L2 is expressed, it is associated with reduced disease-free survival and an increased likelihood of metastasis.26–28

Literature evaluating response to checkpoint inhibitor therapy in salivary gland tumors is limited. One study examined the results of combination therapy with pembrolizumab and vorinostat in recurrent head and neck squamous cell carcinoma and SGC. In the SGC cohort, zero CRs were seen, 16% of patients had a PR and 56% had SD. 29 Another study evaluated the response to pembrolizumab monotherapy in SGCs with ⩾1% PD-L1 expression on tumor or stromal cells. Similarly, no patients achieved a CR and 12% achieved a PR. 30 More recently, a small single site study of adenoid cystic carcinoma (n = 19) and non-adenoid cystic SGC (n = 5) patients treated with nivolumab and ipilimumab reported an ORR of 5% and 0%, respectively, and a disease control rate of 53% (10/19) and 40% (2/5), respectively. 31 The results of a phase II clinical trial evaluating nivolumab and ipilimumab in 64 patients (adenoid cystic carcinoma (cohort 1) and other SGCs (cohort 2)) with metastatic SGC were also reported. 32 The study showed an ORR of 16% (5/32) cohort 2, and 6% (2/32) in cohort 1. The median PFS was reported to be 4.4 months (95% CI: 2.4, 8.3) and 2.2 months (95% CI: 1.8, 5.3) for cohorts 1 and 2, respectively. Grade 3 or higher treatment-related adverse events occurred in 24 of 64 (38%) patients.

Our study showed similar activity, but failed to meet the prespecified primary endpoint (ORR of 30%). In all three cohorts, the best outcome was a PR, with 15% of patients in the non-adenoid cystic group (cohorts 2 and 3) achieving confirmed and unconfirmed PRs, and 4% of patients in cohort 34 achieving confirmed PR. In the non-adenoid cystic patients, median PFS was 4 months, 6-month OS rate was 88%, and median OS was 19 months. In the adenoid cystic cohort, median PFS was 4 months, 6-month OS rate was 84%, and median OS was 15 months. Despite the objectively small rates of response, these results are in line with findings in other studies where response rates range somewhere between 15% and 70%. 5 In addition, checkpoint inhibitor therapy also resulted in a comparable duration of response as other chemotherapy regimens (average response duration of 6–7 months). 4 Finally, although adenoid cystic cancers can be indolent, the other SGCs are more aggressive; therefore, it is notable that 35% of patients with these other SGCs attained SD >6 months/PR/unconfirmed PR.

The tumors studied in the cohorts reported in this paper remain heterogeneous and include many different histologic subtypes. This may limit the ability of this trial to determine specific subtypes of SGC responsive to checkpoint inhibitors. Additional weaknesses include the lack of patient randomization, small sample sizes, and a lack of central pathology review. Evaluation of PD-L1/PD-1, HER2, and AR expression would also add valuable information in understanding patient responses.

The DART trial, sponsored by NCI SWOG, successfully opened at approximately 1000 sites and served an unmet need in rare cancers by offering ipilimumab and nivolumab dual immunotherapy to 53 rare tumor cohorts. The trial, therefore, served as a national clinical immunotherapy trial for rare tumors and has been a platform allowing rapid accrual and readouts.33–36 SGCs are a rare and heterogeneous group of malignancies with few efficacious treatment options. Exposure to ipilimumab and nivolumab resulted in limited response rates, PFS, and OS, comparable to previously tested chemotherapy options, though clinical benefit (which included SD of at least 6 months) was seen in 35% of the non-adenoid cystic subgroup. Specific targeted therapy in patients whose tumors harbor the cognate targets such as BRAF mutations or ERBB2 amplification may be associated with higher response rate. 24 Further evaluation of PD-L1/PD-1 axis status and other markers of immunotherapy response such as tumor mutational burden, as well as increasing the patient populations of histologic subtypes of salivary cancers, would improve our understanding of dual immune checkpoint blockade in SGCs.

Conclusion

In salivary gland tumors, the combination of nivolumab with ipilimumab resulted in only a 4% ORR in adenoid cystic carcinoma and 9% in all other histologies combined, though the latter showed clinical benefit (included SD >6 months) in 35% of the patients. Future correlative studies with larger cohort sizes are warranted.
