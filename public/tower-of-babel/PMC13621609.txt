Affirming Identity in Dental Care: Case-Based Insights on LGBTQ+ Oral Health
Authors: Claire Yu, Joan Gluch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This case-based review examines oral health challenges among LGBTQ+ populations and how discrimination and stigma contribute to negative outcomes. Two clinical scenarios illustrate the impact of minority stress and prior negative healthcare experiences on dental anxiety, care avoidance, and treatment outcomes. In one case, a nonbinary patient delayed care due to misgendering, resulting in untreated caries. In another, a transgender man’s bruxism was associated with stress related to repeated use of his deadname. Both cases demonstrate how affirming care, including correct pronoun use, respect for chosen names, and supportive communication, can reduce anxiety and improve clinical relationships.A literature review complements these cases by identifying barriers to care and risk factors contributing to oral health disparities. LGBTQ+ individuals report higher rates of substance use, delayed dental visits, and limited access to culturally competent care. Certain subgroups, including bisexual adults, experience poorer oral health and greater barriers to care. These findings highlight the need for inclusive clinical practices, improved provider training, and structural changes such as nondiscrimination policies. Integrating affirming approaches into dental care may improve trust, treatment adherence, and oral health outcomes while promoting more equitable healthcare delivery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08950-y.

Introduction

Oral health significantly influences physical, psychological, and social well-being. However, LGBTQ+ populations remain underrepresented in oral health research and frequently experience barriers to dental care, contributing to disparities in outcomes [1]. In 2022, 7.2% of the U.S. population identified as LGBTQ+, nearly doubling over the past decade, with the highest rates among younger generations [2].

Despite increasing visibility, LGBTQ+ individuals continue to face discrimination and stigma within healthcare settings, contributing to higher rates of dental anxiety, delayed care, and unmet health needs [3, 4]. These disparities are shaped by multiple factors, including reduced access to insurance, financial barriers, and fear of identity disclosure, particularly among transgender patients [5–7].

Minority stress and prior negative healthcare experiences may also influence oral health behaviors, including avoidance of care and engagement in risk behaviors such as tobacco and substance use [4, 5]. While respectful communication, such as correct pronoun use and recognition of chosen names, is essential, inclusive dental care extends beyond language to include structural, interpersonal, and clinical practices that promote trust and patient safety [7].

Methods

This two-patient case report, based on brief interviews, is exempt from IRB review under UPenn SOP 3.2.2 (≤ 3 patients). Both patients gave written consent for the interviews and for publication of their case details. Two brief semi-structured interviews were conducted with each patient, one at the initial visit and one after a period of treatment. Interviews used a study-specific guide designed to elicit healthcare experiences, identity-related barriers to care, and perceptions of inclusivity in the clinical setting (Additional file 1).

A literature review of peer-reviewed papers was undertaken by searching PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Scopus from January 2000 through December 2024. Search strategies included both free-text and MeSH terms (e.g., “LGBT,” “oral health,” “dental care,” “access to care,” and “health disparities”). Boolean operators (AND, OR) were applied to ensure a comprehensive search, and additional citations were identified through reference lists of relevant articles.

Peer-reviewed qualitative or quantitative studies on oral health inequalities among LGBTQ+ people were included if they addressed oral health status, dental-care use, access barriers, or patient-provider interactions; all others were excluded. A single reviewer (the paper’s author) conducted every title, abstract, and full-text screening, removed duplicates, and resolved any eligibility doubts. Study details, participant demographics, outcomes, and main results were catalogued, followed by a thematic synthesis to reveal recurring themes. Methodological quality was judged narratively (no formal risk-of-bias tool), and key findings were summarized descriptively.

Researching LGBTQ+ topics comes with unique limitations, including restricted LGBTQ+ identification in medical records and a lack of robust data on LGBTQ+ dental disparities. Where possible, data from national surveys and research explicitly capturing LGBTQ+ variables were incorporated. Studies on oral health disparities in other marginalized populations, as well as existing literature on LGBTQ+ health inequities, were also examined to contextualize these findings. Various search terms and strategies were used to ensure breadth, adhering to established definitions of sexual and gender identity, including studies featuring diverse measures of identity.

Results

Case presentations

Patient #1

A 29-year-old nonbinary individual presented with multiple untreated dental caries. They reported no significant comorbidities or current medication use. However, the patient disclosed a prolonged gap in dental visits, attributing it to negative clinical experiences in which their gender identity was dismissed or overlooked. These prior encounters contributed to a heightened level of anxiety regarding healthcare settings and prompted the patient’s request for a provider who would acknowledge and respect their identity. On examination, the patient displayed signs of nervousness, including hesitancy when discussing their dental history. Intraoral assessment revealed several carious lesions, primarily in the posterior teeth. Periodontal screening indicated a generally healthy periodontium, but the most pressing concern was the patient’s discomfort and mistrust of clinical environments.

To foster a sense of security, the dental student began by confirming the patient’s preferred pronouns privately and respectfully. This practice was reinforced throughout the visit, ensuring all staff maintained consistent, appropriate pronoun usage. Next, the student practitioner reviewed the patient’s oral health goals, emphasizing a collaborative treatment plan. Restorative care was prioritized, focusing on controlling and treating the existing carious lesions. Comprehensive treatment included removing decayed tooth structure, placing appropriate restorations, and reinforcing oral hygiene techniques. Throughout the process, attention was given to privacy, clear communication, and active listening – a strategy to reduce the patient’s anxiety and build trust.

The patient reported feeling more comfortable and respected compared with previous healthcare experiences. By the end of the initial restorative treatment, they expressed optimism about ongoing dental care. Subsequent follow-up confirmed the successful management of all identified cavities without complications. Equally important, the patient reported a renewed willingness to seek preventive services and indicated that being treated with genuine respect significantly alleviated their anxiety. No adverse events were noted, and the patient’s feedback underscored the impact of inclusive clinical practices on improving continuity of care.

This case illustrates the importance of a person-centered approach, particularly for individuals who have experienced prior discrimination or invalidation in healthcare settings. Recognizing and consistently using a patient’s chosen pronouns can foster an environment of respect, helping to mitigate anxiety and encourage open communication. These positive patient-provider interactions can lead to better treatment adherence, improved oral health outcomes, and heightened trust in the healthcare system.

Providing inclusive care involves more than addressing clinical needs; it requires acknowledging each patient’s identity and tailoring communication accordingly. By integrating respectful practices, such as verifying pronouns, ensuring privacy, and demonstrating empathy, clinicians can better meet the oral health needs of diverse populations and bolster patients’ confidence in ongoing preventive and therapeutic care.

Patient #2

A 23-year-old transgender man presented with bruxism-related jaw discomfort, morning headaches, and muscle tension. He was undergoing testosterone therapy but reported no other comorbidities or active medical conditions. The patient voiced specific concerns about his dead name appearing on insurance records, emphasizing that public use of this name triggered anxiety. Previous healthcare experiences had been negative, characterized by frequent misgendering and a lack of recognition for his chosen name and pronouns.

Upon arrival, the patient exhibited clear signs of stress related to potential misgendering. He conveyed unease about prior instances in which healthcare teams did not respect his identity. Physical examination revealed clinical findings consistent with nocturnal bruxism, including attrition and flattening of the cusps, particularly along posterior occlusal surfaces. No obvious erosive or abrasive lesions were identified as primary contributors to the wear pattern. Muscle palpation revealed tenderness in the temporalis and masseter regions, with masseter tenderness corresponding to the patient’s report of jaw soreness upon waking. No systemic conditions were identified that could have contributed to the bruxism, suggesting that stress was likely a contributing factor.

To alleviate the patient’s concerns, the dental student deliberately ensured his preferred name and pronouns were consistently used. In the waiting area, rather than announcing the dead name from the insurance records, the student privately confirmed his last name and asked for his preferred first name. This practice extended throughout the treatment process, with every individual involved in his care informed of the correct name and pronouns.

Clinically, management centered on providing an occlusal splint to reduce nocturnal grinding and relax strained muscles. The student also educated the patient about jaw-relaxation massages. During these interactions, open-ended questions encouraged him to discuss the possible role of minority stress in exacerbating his bruxism. By listening attentively and avoiding assumptions, the dental student fostered a collaborative environment where the patient felt respected and validated.

At follow-up eight months later, the patient reported consistent night guard use, decreased masseter tenderness, and waking up less often with headaches. Although the occlusal attrition remained, no further progression of symptoms or appliance-related complications was reported. He attributed the progress not only to the appliance and exercises but also to the clinic’s affirming atmosphere: every staff member used his correct name and pronouns, intake forms reflected his gender identity, and there were no instances of misgendering or deadnaming. This respectful environment lowered his baseline anxiety and allowed him to focus on self-care. Clinical exams confirmed excellent compliance with hygiene and stress-management advice, and no adverse events or appliance complications were observed. The patient expressed relief at being treated with dignity and noted that he now felt comfortable scheduling routine maintenance visits he had previously avoided.

The case illustrates that culturally competent care can amplify the effectiveness of standard bruxism management. Addressing minority stress alongside biomedical factors strengthened rapport, improved adherence, and encouraged sustained preventive attendance. Small but consistent actions, such as inclusive language on forms, staff training in respectful communication, and intentional validation of gender identity, created psychological safety that translated into better clinical outcomes. By embedding these practices into everyday workflows, dental teams can reduce barriers to care for transgender and other marginalized patients, ultimately supporting healthier, more engaged, and more trusting patient relationships.

Literature review

Access to care

Economic barriers and discrimination can significantly restrict healthcare access for LGBTQ+ individuals, resulting in worse health outcomes [8]. Gonzalez et al. found that adults in same-sex relationships had a lower likelihood of having employer-sponsored health insurance compared to those in opposite-sex relationships, with significant gaps for men in the South and women in the Midwest. These regional disparities may be influenced by more prevalent homophobia and discriminatory legislation [9]. Blosnich et al. discovered that lesbian women were 30% less likely to have annual physical checkups than heterosexual women due to the cost [10]. As a result of restricted access to employer-provided dependent coverage through legal marriage and discriminatory treatment by health providers, many LGBTQ+ individuals report unmet medical needs and struggle with medication adherence [9].

Further compounding these challenges are economic hardships, such as poverty and unemployment, which disproportionately affect sexual minority groups [5, 11, 12]. While some findings suggest that sexual minority women may be more vulnerable to economic barriers than men, the exact influence of these disparities across differing orientations remains unclear [8].

Additionally, research seldom addresses the intersectionality of social identities [13, 14]. For example, non-white lesbian or bisexual women may encounter more significant obstacles in seeking health services than white gay or bisexual men due to compounded sexism, racism, limited financial resources, and discrimination [14]. These intersecting disadvantages can generate complex risk profiles, magnifying health disparities [13].

Barriers to care

LGBTQ+ individuals confront unique pressures linked to sexual orientation and gender identity, such as self-stigma, fear of rejection, and the need to hide one’s identity [15]. Interpersonal stressors such as prejudice, family rejection, or violence can lead to increased substance use, poor mental health, and elevated cardiometabolic risks [16–19]. Many LGBTQ+ people reside in regions lacking comprehensive nondiscrimination laws; in certain areas, discriminatory policies, like mandating transgender people to use restrooms corresponding to their assigned sex, remain legal [20]. These circumstances perpetuate stigma and stress, prompting unhealthy coping methods that destabilize overall health [21, 22].

Experiences of discrimination, central to Minority Stress Theory, often cause LGBTQ+ patients to avoid or delay needed medical care [12]. According to the Human Rights Campaign’s Health Equality Index, 56% of LGB adults and 70% of transgender individuals have faced discrimination in healthcare [23]. Many seek out known “LGBTQ+ -friendly” physicians for fear of hostility from providers [24]. Lambda Legal’s study revealed that 73% of transgender and about one-third of LGB participants feared prejudicial treatment, with 9% of LGB and more than half of transgender respondents worrying about outright denial of care [25].

Transgender and gender nonbinary (TGNB) individuals often experience discriminatory practices in healthcare, including dentistry. In one questionnaire study, one-third of TGNB participants reported misgendering, and more than half felt their dental providers lacked gender-appropriate services. Although refusal of care was rare, avoidance due to identity concerns was linked to poorer oral health, with participants citing insensitivity, awkward interactions, and limited access to affirming providers [26]. Such patterns reflect broader issues in LGBTQ+ oral healthcare: 57% of LGBTQ+ adults report verbal assault, and 53% face slurs or other abusive language in medical settings [27]. These attitudes erode the patient-provider relationship and deter LGBTQ+ individuals, especially adolescents, from seeking essential dental services [28, 29]. Surveys in Indiana and Michigan revealed that while 71% of LGBT respondents visit the dentist regularly, 43% feel uncomfortable and 34% feel mistreated [30]. Furthermore, only 10% of queer participants in another study reported regular dental care, attributing this low rate to perceived discrimination [31].

The fallout of these experiences is considerable. Dental fear and avoidance behaviors correlate with higher incidences of anxiety, depression, and PTSD [32]. Eitner et al. concluded that anxious patients possess significantly higher caries rates, underscoring the urgent need for stigma-free oral healthcare [33]. Even when overall disease rates do not always differ by sexual orientation, the impact of discrimination remains pronounced: one study found that bisexual adults were more likely to report poor oral health (41% vs. 27% for heterosexuals) and face dental-care barriers (30% vs. 19%), while gay men had higher rates of bone loss (35% vs. 11%) [34]. Thus, there is a need for culturally competent care that fosters trust and reduces oral health disparities among marginalized groups.

Risk factors

Multiple lifestyle factors also compound LGBTQ+ oral health disparities. Higher tobacco use among LGBT individuals contributes to gum recession, periodontal disease, mucosal lesions, tooth loss, and oral cancer risks [35, 36]. Lesbian and bisexual youth often initiate tobacco use at earlier ages than heterosexual peers, driven partly by targeted marketing; sexual minority women likewise show disproportionately high smoking rates [37, 38].

Alcohol misuse similarly damages oral health, promoting cancer and leading to premalignant lesions, tooth decay, and xerostomia [39]. In LGBTQ+ communities, alcohol consumption tends to start earlier, escalates rapidly, and includes higher rates of heavy and binge drinking than in heterosexual populations [40, 41]. Additionally, substance use disorders, encompassing opioids, amphetamines, cocaine, and cannabis, exacerbate oral health issues [41]. Poor dietary habits, neglect of oral hygiene, and heightened sugar intake all accelerate decay, gum disease, and erosion [41–43]. Notably, research indicates that sexual minority adults are almost twice as likely to have had a substance use disorder in the past year, and sexual minority children are nearly three times more likely to engage in substance use than their heterosexual counterparts [44, 45]. These patterns underscore the importance of culturally informed interventions to address psychosocial stressors and oral health practices.

Cultural competency in dental education

Dental professionals are uniquely positioned to mitigate these disparities through appropriate interventions, yet many academic programs fail to incorporate LGBTQ+ -specific content [46]. A study found that 29% of dental schools and 48% of dental hygiene programs do not address LGBTQ+ health. Dental schools devote 3.68 h to LGBTQ+ content, while hygiene programs offer only 1.25, mainly covering HIV and oral disease risk [47]. Not surprisingly, only 13.3% of dental student leaders felt adequately prepared to treat LGBTQ+ patients, indicating a clear gap in education [48, 49]. Legislative changes and expanded educational hours on LGBTQ+ health can increase awareness, dismantle stigma, and cultivate a more inclusive patient-provider dynamic [46].

Core considerations for LGBT-inclusive dental care

In addition to these patient-facing practices, structural adjustments such as gender-neutral restrooms, inclusive signage, and visible nondiscrimination statements can demonstrate a willingness to serve diverse communities [51]. Creating a directory of LGBT-friendly providers further promotes continuity of care and minimizes patients’ need to disclose sensitive personal information repeatedly [50]. In tandem with systemic reforms ensuring confidentiality and nondiscrimination policies, these measures reinforce a supportive network that values each patient’s identity [52].

Moreover, Haley et al.’s “Didactics-Discussion-Delivery” strategy exemplifies how educational interventions can enhance provider competency, combining didactic lectures on terminology and disparities with small group discussions [46]. Across medical and nursing education, ample evidence suggests that greater exposure to LGBTQ+ patient experiences fosters empathy, reduces biases, and promotes more effective care [51, 53]. Adopting these models in dentistry would likely yield similar benefits, reinforcing the crucial link between respectful provider-patient interactions and better oral health outcomes for LGBTQ+ populations [46].

Discussion

The cases demonstrate that LGBTQ+ patients’ oral health may be shaped by both clinical conditions and prior experiences of discrimination. In one case, delayed care related to misgendering contributed to untreated caries. In the other, minority stress likely contributed to bruxism, with findings of occlusal attrition, cusp flattening, and muscle tenderness. Both patients showed improvement following affirming care.

These observations align with minority stress theory, which links stigma and discrimination to adverse health behaviors and outcomes [12]. In dental settings, this may manifest as care avoidance, heightened anxiety, or parafunctional habits such as clenching and grinding. Although causality cannot be established, the cases demonstrate clinically relevant pathways through which psychosocial stress may affect oral health.

Importantly, inclusive dental care extends beyond pronoun use. As outlined in Table 1, affirming practice includes inclusive documentation, confidentiality, trauma-informed communication, staff training, and avoidance of public disclosure. These system-level practices help create psychological safety, which can improve trust, adherence, and continuity of care. Figure 1 provides a practical workflow for integrating these elements into clinical practice, from pre-visit intake through post-visit follow-up, to support consistent and affirming patient experiences.

The literature further supports that LGBTQ+ populations face persistent barriers to care, including discrimination, limited provider training, and structural inequities [47]. Given the repetitive and trust-dependent nature of dental care, these barriers may disproportionately impact oral health outcomes.

While limited by small sample size and reliance on patient-reported experiences, these cases highlight the clinical relevance of culturally competent care. Future research should evaluate whether affirming practices improve measurable outcomes such as appointment adherence, dental anxiety, and preventive care utilization. Affirming dental care represents a practical and scalable approach to reducing disparities and improving patient-provider relationships in LGBTQ+ populations.

Conclusion

The literature and these case presentations reinforce how essential cultural competence and respectful communication are for enhancing oral health outcomes among LGBTQ+ individuals. Simple yet impactful actions can offset the long-standing effects of stigma, encourage consistent dental care, and decrease the likelihood of advanced oral pathology.

Nonetheless, many LGBTQ+ individuals remain hesitant to disclose their sexual identities to healthcare providers, impeding accurate data collection and the advancement of targeted interventions [54]. This shortage of research and documented healthcare efficacy has prompted governmental bodies to advocate for more comprehensive investigations [55]. Discrimination and the lack of data highlight the need for personalized care and improved research efforts [56].

Although this study offers important insights, the body of literature on LGBTQ+ oral health disparities remains limited [1, 3]. Existing research often features small samples, insufficient follow-up, or reliance on self-reported data, each of which may introduce bias [34, 52]. Moreover, variable definitions of LGBTQ+ identity complicate the generalizability of findings, and focusing solely on peer-reviewed sources may exclude gray literature and evolving societal contexts [3].

To address these gaps, future research should include longitudinal studies that capture shifts in attitudes and practices toward LGBTQ+ patients and qualitative work that provides deeper insight into personal experiences. Designing and evaluating targeted oral health programs for LGBTQ+ populations through intervention research, alongside systematic health services analyses, can illuminate optimal delivery models. Examining the effectiveness of educational innovations, nondiscrimination policies, and insurance coverage requirements will further clarify how to prepare dental professionals to meet the needs of these communities.

Providing culturally appropriate care to LGBTQ+ populations is a critical step toward health equity. By enhancing provider awareness, revising institutional frameworks, and reforming systemic practices, healthcare organizations can build supportive environments that significantly elevate the oral and overall health of LGBTQ+ individuals.
