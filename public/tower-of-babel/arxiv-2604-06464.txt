Weighted Bayesian Conformal Prediction
Xiayin Lou, Peng Luo

Abstract
Conformal prediction provides distribution-free prediction intervals with finite-sample coverage guarantees, and recent work by Snell & Griffiths reframes it as Bayesian Quadrature (BQ-CP), yielding powerful data-conditional guarantees via Dirichlet posteriors over thresholds. However, BQ-CP fundamentally requires the i.i.d. assumption—a limitation the authors themselves identify. Meanwhile, weighted conformal prediction handles distribution shift via importance weights but remains frequentist, producing only point-estimate thresholds. We propose Weighted Bayesian Conformal Prediction (WBCP) , which generalizes BQ-CP to arbitrary importance-weighted settings by replacing the uniform Dirichlet Dir ⁡ ( 1 , … , 1 ) \mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1) with a weighted Dirichlet Dir ⁡ ( n eff ⋅ w ~ 1 , … , n eff ⋅ w ~ n ) \mathrm{Dir}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{1},\ldots,n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{n}) , where n eff n_{\mathrm{eff}} is Kish’s effective sample size. We prove four theoretical results: (1) n eff n_{\mathrm{eff}} is the unique concentration parameter matching frequentist and Bayesian variances; (2) posterior standard deviation decays as O ⁡ ( 1 / n eff ) O(1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}) ; (3) BQ-CP’s stochastic dominance guarantee extends to per-weight-profile data-conditional guarantees; (4) the HPD threshold provides O ⁡ ( 1 / n eff ) O(1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}) improvement in conditional coverage. We instantiate WBCP for spatial prediction as Geographical BQ-CP , where kernel-based spatial weights yield per-location posteriors with interpretable diagnostics. Experiments on synthetic and real-world spatial datasets demonstrate that WBCP maintains coverage guarantees while providing substantially richer uncertainty information.



1
Introduction

Conformal prediction (CP)
(
16
)
provides distribution-free prediction intervals with finite-sample coverage guarantees: for any model and miscoverage level
\alpha
, the constructed prediction set contains the true outcome with probability at least
1-\alpha
. This elegant guarantee has made CP a cornerstone of modern uncertainty quantification
(
8
;
12
)
.

Two levels of uncertainty.
It is important to distinguish two fundamentally different levels of uncertainty in any prediction system. The
first level
is the predictive uncertainty about the outcome
Y
itself: how far might the true value deviate from the prediction
\hat{f}(X)
? CP addresses this level by constructing an interval
[\hat{f}(X)\pm\hat{q}]
. The
second level
—which CP does
not
address—is the uncertainty about the threshold
\hat{q}
itself: how reliable is this interval half-width? Is it well-determined by the calibration data, or could it be substantially different? We call this second level
meta-uncertainty
: the uncertainty about the uncertainty quantification (formalized in Definition
1
).

Standard CP produces a
single deterministic
threshold
\hat{q}
, the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of calibration scores, with no indication of its reliability. Yet two prediction intervals of identical width may carry vastly different levels of statistical support. Consider two scenarios:
\hat{q}=50
computed from 200 relevant calibration points versus the same
\hat{q}=50
computed from 5 distant, barely relevant points. The first
\hat{q}
is precisely determined; the second could easily have been 30 or 80 had the calibration data been slightly different. Standard CP treats them identically—this meta-uncertainty gap limits CP’s utility in high-stakes applications.

Bayesian Quadrature for Conformal Prediction (BQ-CP).

14
addressed the meta-uncertainty gap by reframing CP as Bayesian Quadrature. Their key insight is that the calibration scores
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n}
partition the quantile space
[0,1]
into
n+1
intervals (“spacings”), and the
lengths of these spacings are themselves uncertain
. Under i.i.d. data, these spacings follow
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
. By sampling from this Dirichlet, BQ-CP produces a full posterior distribution
p(\lambda\mid\mathcal{D})
over the threshold—not just a point estimate—enabling data-conditional guarantees. However, BQ-CP fundamentally requires the i.i.d. assumption. As the authors explicitly state:
“The limitations of our method lie primarily in the two main assumptions it makes. First, it assumes that the data at deployment time are independent and identically distributed”

(
14
, Section 6)
.

Weighted Conformal Prediction.
When test and calibration distributions differ (covariate shift),
15
introduced the concept of
weighted exchangeability
and proved that reweighting calibration scores by likelihood ratios maintains valid coverage guarantees. This foundational result has been applied to spatial settings
(
9
)
, domain adaptation, and fairness-aware prediction. But weighted CP remains purely frequentist: it replaces the uniform quantile with a weighted quantile
\hat{q}_{w}
, which is still a
single deterministic threshold
per weight profile. It solves the covariate shift problem but does not address meta-uncertainty at all.

The gap.
No existing method provides both (i) the Bayesian posterior over thresholds and data-conditional guarantees of BQ-CP
and
(ii) the distribution-shift robustness of weighted CP. This gap is not merely theoretical: in spatial prediction, for instance, a prediction interval based on 200 nearby comparable sales carries fundamentally different reliability than one based on 5 distant sales—yet neither BQ-CP (which ignores spatial structure entirely) nor weighted CP (which provides no meta-uncertainty) can distinguish these cases.

Our contribution: Weighted Bayesian Conformal Prediction (WBCP).
We bridge this gap by generalizing BQ-CP to arbitrary importance-weighted settings. Our key insight is that the same importance weights that provide weighted CP’s coverage guarantee
(
15
)
map naturally to Dirichlet concentration parameters via the weighted Bayesian bootstrap
(
10
)
, with Kish’s effective sample size
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
serving as the concentration scaling. Crucially, WBCP does not introduce new assumptions: it inherits weighted CP’s weighted exchangeability assumption and enriches the resulting point-estimate threshold with a full posterior distribution. Specifically, we make the following contributions:

1.

We propose WBCP, which replaces BQ-CP’s
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
with
\mathrm{Dir}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{1},\ldots,n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{n})
, producing
per-weight-profile
posteriors with weight-dependent concentration (§
3
).

2.

We prove four theoretical results (§
4
): calibration consistency of the
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
scaling (Theorem
1
), posterior concentration at rate
O(1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}})
(Theorem
2
), weighted stochastic dominance extending BQ-CP’s guarantee (Theorem
3
), and a conditional coverage bound (Theorem
5
).

3.

We instantiate WBCP for spatial prediction as
Geographical BQ-CP
(GeoBCP), demonstrating spatial diagnostics including effective sample size maps and posterior standard deviation maps (§
5
).

4.

We validate WBCP on synthetic and real-world datasets, showing that it maintains coverage guarantees while providing substantially richer uncertainty information (§
6
).

2
Background

2.1
Split Conformal Prediction

Split conformal prediction
(
11
;
8
)
partitions data into training and calibration sets
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. A model
\hat{f}
is trained, and nonconformity scores
\rho_{i}=|Y_{i}-\hat{f}(X_{i})|
are computed. The prediction interval for a new test point is
\mathcal{C}(X_{\mathrm{new}})=[\hat{f}(X_{\mathrm{new}})\pm\hat{q}]
, where
\hat{q}
is the
\lceil(1-\alpha)(n+1)\rceil/n
quantile of
\{\rho_{i}\}
. Under exchangeability,
\Pr(Y_{\mathrm{new}}\in\mathcal{C})\geq 1-\alpha
.

2.2
Weighted Conformal Prediction

Standard CP requires exchangeability of training and test data, which fails under distribution shift.
15
extended CP beyond exchangeability by introducing a
weighted
notion of exchangeability and showing that appropriately reweighted conformal procedures retain coverage guarantees.

Covariate shift model.
Consider training data
(X_{i},Y_{i})\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P=P_{X}\cdot P_{Y|X}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, and a test point
(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})\sim\tilde{P}=\tilde{P}_{X}\cdot P_{Y|X}
, drawn independently. The conditional
P_{Y|X}
is shared, but the covariate distributions
P_{X}
and
\tilde{P}_{X}
may differ. The key quantity is the
likelihood ratio

w(x)=d\tilde{P}_{X}/dP_{X}(x)
.

Weighted exchangeability.

15
introduced the following generalization of exchangeability: random variables
V_{1},\ldots,V_{n}
are
weighted exchangeable
with weight functions
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
if their joint density factors as

f(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(v_{i})\cdot g(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}),

(1)

where
g
is permutation-invariant. When
w_{i}\equiv 1
for all
i
, this reduces to ordinary exchangeability. Crucially, independent draws from different distributions are always weighted exchangeable: if
Z_{i}\sim P_{i}
independently, then
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
are weighted exchangeable with
w_{i}=dP_{i}/dP_{1}
(their Lemma 2). The covariate shift model is a special case: the training-test data are weighted exchangeable with
w_{i}\equiv 1
for training points and
w_{n+1}(x)=d\tilde{P}_{X}/dP_{X}(x)
for the test point.

Coverage guarantee.
Under weighted exchangeability, the weighted conformal prediction set

\hat{C}_{n}(x)=\Bigl\{y:V_{n+1}^{(x,y)}\leq\mathrm{Quantile}\Bigl(1-\alpha,\;\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{w}(x)\,\delta_{V_{i}^{(x,y)}}+p_{n+1}^{w}(x)\,\delta_{\infty}\Bigr)\Bigr\},

(2)

with
p_{i}^{w}(x)=w(X_{i})/\bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{n}w(X_{j})+w(x)\bigr)
, satisfies
\Pr(Y_{n+1}\in\hat{C}_{n}(X_{n+1}))\geq 1-\alpha
(Corollary 1 and Theorem 2 of
15
). For split conformal with pre-fitted
\hat{f}
, this reduces to the
weighted quantile
:

\hat{q}_{w}=\inf\Bigl\{q:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{w}_{i}\,\mathbf{1}[\rho_{i}\leq q]\geq 1-\alpha\Bigr\},\quad\tilde{w}_{i}=w_{i}/\textstyle\sum_{j}w_{j}.

(3)

This framework encompasses covariate shift correction, spatial weighting (GeoCP,
9
), and localized CP
(
6
)
. In all cases, the output is a single deterministic threshold per weight profile—the weighted quantile
\hat{q}_{w}
provides no information about its own reliability.

2.3
Conformal Prediction as Bayesian Quadrature (BQ-CP)

14
showed that conformal threshold selection can be viewed as Bayesian Quadrature over the expected loss. The stochastic upper bound on expected loss is:

L^{+}=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}U_{i}\cdot\ell_{(i)},\quad\boldsymbol{U}\sim\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1),

(4)

where
\ell_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq\ell_{(n+1)}
are ordered losses and
\ell_{(n+1)}=B
(upper bound). The Dirichlet
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
arises from the probability integral transform under i.i.d. data (their Lemma 4.2). The Highest Posterior Density (HPD) threshold satisfies
\Pr(L^{+}\leq\alpha\mid\mathcal{D})\geq\beta
, providing a
data-conditional
guarantee stronger than CP’s marginal guarantee.

Key limitation.
The derivation of
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
requires i.i.d. data. When weights are non-uniform—as in any covariate shift, spatial, or localized setting—this Dirichlet model is invalid, and BQ-CP’s guarantees no longer hold.

3
Weighted Bayesian Conformal Prediction

3.1
General Framework

Consider a weighted conformal prediction setting with calibration scores
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and normalized importance weights
\{\tilde{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
(where
\sum_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}=1
). The weights may arise from covariate shift correction (
w_{i}=d\tilde{P}_{X}/dP_{X}(X_{i})
), spatial kernels, or any other importance weighting scheme satisfying the weighted exchangeability condition of §
2.2
.

WBCP replaces BQ-CP’s uniform Dirichlet with a
weighted Dirichlet
over the
n
calibration spacings:

\underbrace{\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)}_{\text{BQ-CP (i.i.d., $n$ components)}}\;\longrightarrow\;\underbrace{\mathrm{Dir}\bigl(n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{1},\;\ldots,\;n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{n}\bigr)}_{\text{WBCP (weighted)}},

(5)

where the
n
normalized weights satisfy
\sum_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}=1
, and
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is the Kish effective sample size
(
7
)
:

n_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\bigr)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}^{2}}.

(6)

Justification via weighted Bayesian bootstrap.
The construction has two complementary justifications.
First
, from the Bayesian nonparametric perspective, it is the
weighted Bayesian bootstrap

(
10
)
: just as BQ-CP’s
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
is the Bayesian bootstrap posterior
(
13
)
for the empirical distribution under i.i.d. data,
\mathrm{Dir}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{1},\ldots,n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{n})
is the weighted Bayesian bootstrap posterior under importance weighting. When weights are uniform (
\tilde{w}_{i}=1/n
),
n_{\mathrm{eff}}=n
and we recover
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
exactly.

Second
, from the frequentist perspective, WBCP inherits weighted CP’s coverage foundation.
15
showed that under weighted exchangeability (Eq.
1
), the weighted quantile
\hat{q}_{w}
achieves
\geq 1-\alpha
coverage. WBCP does not alter this guarantee—it
enriches
it by placing a Dirichlet posterior over the threshold, turning the point estimate
\hat{q}_{w}
into a full distribution
p(\lambda\mid\mathcal{D},\mathbf{w})
. The resulting HPD threshold
\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}}\geq\hat{q}_{w}
(for
\beta>1/2
) thus preserves weighted CP’s coverage while additionally providing data-conditional guarantees via the Dirichlet posterior (Corollary
4
).

3.2
Effective Sample Size as Concentration Parameter

The choice
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is not arbitrary—it is the
asymptotically unique
concentration that ensures the Bayesian posterior variance matches the frequentist sampling variance of the weighted estimator (Theorem
1
). Intuitively,
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
answers: “How many i.i.d. samples would produce the same estimation variance as this weighted sample?” This ensures:

•

When weights are nearly uniform (
n_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx n
): the posterior is concentrated, reflecting abundant information.

•

When weights are highly non-uniform (
n_{\mathrm{eff}}\ll n
): the posterior is diffuse, honestly communicating that few effective observations support the estimate.

3.4
Formalizing Meta-Uncertainty

The outputs of WBCP formalize the distinction between two levels of uncertainty introduced in §
1
. We now make this distinction mathematically precise.

Definition 1

(Two Levels of Uncertainty)
.

Given a prediction model
\hat{f}
and calibration data
\mathcal{D}
:

1.

Predictive uncertainty
(Level 1): the risk that the true outcome falls outside the prediction interval. This is characterized by the coverage probability

\Pr\bigl(Y_{n+1}\in[\hat{f}(X_{n+1})\pm\lambda]\bigr)\geq 1-\alpha.

(7)

Standard CP and weighted CP address this level by producing a
single deterministic threshold

\hat{q}
(or
\hat{q}_{w}
) such that the resulting interval achieves the target coverage.

2.

Meta-uncertainty
(Level 2): the uncertainty about the threshold
\lambda
itself, arising from the finite calibration sample. WBCP provides a full posterior distribution over this threshold:

p(\lambda\mid\mathcal{D},\mathbf{w}),\quad\text{with summary statistics}\quad\bar{\lambda}=\mathbb{E}[\lambda\mid\mathcal{D},\mathbf{w}],\quad\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\lambda\mid\mathcal{D},\mathbf{w})}.

(8)

The posterior
p(\lambda\mid\mathcal{D},\mathbf{w})
is induced by the Dirichlet model over quantile spacings (Eq.
5
), and
\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}
measures the
reliability
of the interval width.

Remark 1

(Interpreting
\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}
)
.

The posterior standard deviation
\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}
has a concrete interpretation: it quantifies how much the threshold
\lambda
could vary under different realizations of the calibration data with the same weight profile
\mathbf{w}
. A small
\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}
means the interval width is precisely determined by the calibration data; a large
\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}
signals that the interval width is itself uncertain—a different calibration sample could have produced a substantially different interval. By Theorem
2
,
\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}=O(1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}})
, establishing a direct quantitative link between the effective sample size and the reliability of the prediction interval:

\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}^{2}\leq C\cdot\frac{q^{*}(1-q^{*})}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}\cdot\frac{\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}^{2}}{\underline{w}^{2}}.

(9)

4
Theoretical Results

We now state the four main theoretical results. Full proofs are in Appendix
A
. Throughout, let
\rho_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq\rho_{(n)}
be sorted scores with normalized weights
\tilde{w}_{(i)}
, cumulative weights
p_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\tilde{w}_{(i)}
, quantile level
q=1-\alpha
, and deterministic weighted quantile index
k^{*}=\min\{j:p_{j}\geq q\}
.

4.1
Calibration Consistency

Our first result justifies the choice
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

Theorem 1

(Calibration Consistency)
.

Consider the WBCP model with general concentration
c>0
:
\boldsymbol{V}\sim\mathrm{Dir}(c\cdot\tilde{w}_{(1)},\ldots,c\cdot\tilde{w}_{(n)})
. Then:

1.

Mean consistency
(all
c>0
):
\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{\mathrm{post}}]=\lambda_{\mathrm{WCP}}+O(1/c)
.

2.

Variance calibration
(requires
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
):
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is the asymptotically unique choice matching the Bayesian posterior variance
\mathrm{Var}(S_{j})=p_{j}(1-p_{j})/(c+1)
to the frequentist variance
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{freq}}(\hat{F}_{w}(t))=F(t)(1-F(t))/n_{\mathrm{eff}}
. (Exact matching gives
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}-1
; we use
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
for simplicity, the difference being
O(1/n_{\mathrm{eff}})
.)

3.

Bernstein–von Mises alignment
(requires
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
): the posterior has the correct asymptotic variance for quantile estimation.

4.2
Posterior Concentration Rate

Theorem 2

(Posterior Concentration)
.

Under the WBCP Dirichlet model with
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
, the posterior standard deviation satisfies:

\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}^{2}\leq C\cdot\frac{q^{*}(1-q^{*})}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}\cdot\frac{\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}^{2}}{\underline{w}^{2}},

(10)

where
q^{*}=\max(q(1-q),\,1/4)
,
\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}=\max_{j}|\rho_{(j+1)}-\rho_{(j)}|
is the maximum score spacing,
\underline{w}=\min_{j\approx k^{*}}\tilde{w}_{(j)}
is the minimum weight near the quantile boundary, and
C>0
is a universal constant. In particular,
\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}=O(1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}})
.

This is our central novel result. It establishes that the posterior width is controlled by
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
: regions with high effective sample size have concentrated posteriors (tight intervals), while regions with low
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
have diffuse posteriors (uncertain intervals). BQ-CP’s uniform weighting gives
n_{\mathrm{eff}}=n
everywhere, so no such spatial variation is possible.

4.3
Weighted Stochastic Dominance

Assumption 1

(Weighted Dirichlet Model)
.

The uncertainty in local quantile spacings is modeled as
(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n})\sim\mathrm{Dir}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{(1)},\ldots,n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{(n)})
, with
\alpha_{0}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
. This is the weighted Bayesian bootstrap posterior
(
10
)
under the importance weights
\{w_{i}\}
, and reduces to BQ-CP’s
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
when weights are uniform (
\tilde{w}_{i}=1/n
,
n_{\mathrm{eff}}=n
).

Theorem 3

(Weighted Stochastic Dominance)
.

Under Assumption
1
, define the stochastic upper bound
L_{w}^{+}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}\cdot\ell_{(i)}
with
\boldsymbol{V}\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
, where
\ell_{(i)}
are sorted losses. Then for any
b\leq B
:

\inf_{\pi}\Pr(L\leq b\mid\ell_{1:n})\geq\Pr(L_{w}^{+}\leq b),

(11)

where
L
is the true expected loss and the infimum is over all priors
\pi
on the state of nature.

This directly generalizes BQ-CP’s Theorem 4.3. The key insight is that BQ-CP’s worst-case quantile function argument (their Theorem 4.1) is a pure optimization result independent of the Dirichlet parameterization.

Corollary 4

(Data-Conditional Guarantee)
.

For the WBCP HPD threshold
\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}}
at confidence
\beta
:

\inf_{\pi}\Pr(L(\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}})\leq\alpha\mid\ell_{1:n})\geq\beta
.

4.4
Conditional Coverage Bound

Assumption 2

(Correct Weight Specification)
.

The weights satisfy: (A1)
w_{i}\propto dP_{\mathrm{test}}/dP_{\mathrm{calib}}(X_{i})
(correct importance ratios, i.e., the likelihood ratio condition of
15
); (A2) the local score distribution has a continuous positive density at the quantile. Part (A1) is the same assumption under which weighted CP achieves coverage (their Corollary 1); WBCP does not introduce any additional distributional assumption.

Theorem 5

(Conditional Coverage)
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, the conditional miscoverage of WBCP with HPD confidence
\beta>1/2
satisfies:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\bigl[\alpha_{\mathrm{cond}}\bigr]=\alpha-\Phi^{-1}(\beta)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{q(1-q)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}}+o\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}}\Bigr),

(12)

where
\Phi^{-1}
is the standard normal quantile function.

This shows that the HPD threshold provides
O(1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}})
improvement in conditional coverage over the marginal rate
\alpha
. The improvement is largest when
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is small (i.e., where standard weighted CP is least reliable), providing automatic spatial adaptivity.

5
Application: Geographical Bayesian Conformal Prediction

We instantiate WBCP for spatial prediction, where the importance weights arise from geographic kernel functions. This instantiation has a natural theoretical connection to the
local conditional coverage
framework of
15
.

Spatial kernel weights.
For a test location
s
and calibration locations
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the spatial weights are:

w_{i}(s)=K\bigl(\|s-s_{i}\|/h\bigr),\quad K(d)=\exp(-d^{2}/2),

(13)

with bandwidth
h>0
. This follows GeoCP
(
9
)
. Applying WBCP with these weights yields a
per-location
posterior
p(\lambda\mid\mathcal{D},s)
.

Connection to local conditional coverage.

15
showed that kernel weights
w_{i}(x_{0})=K((X_{i}-x_{0})/h)
can be interpreted as inducing a covariate shift with
d\tilde{P}_{X}/dP_{X}\propto K((x-x_{0})/h)
, under which weighted CP achieves a
locally-smoothed conditional coverage
: the coverage guarantee holds in a kernel-smoothed sense around
x_{0}
(their equation (18)). GeoBCP inherits this local guarantee while additionally providing the posterior
p(\lambda\mid\mathcal{D},s)
over the threshold. Thus, GeoBCP enriches the local conditional coverage of weighted CP with full meta-uncertainty quantification at each location.

Spatial diagnostics.
GeoBCP provides three interpretability layers unavailable from any existing method:

1.

Effective sample size map:

n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s)
visualized as a spatial heatmap, answering “how many equivalent calibration samples support the interval here?”

2.

Posterior standard deviation map:

\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}(s)

Discussion and Conclusion

We introduced Weighted Bayesian Conformal Prediction (WBCP), which generalizes BQ-CP to importance-weighted settings by replacing
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
with
\mathrm{Dir}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{1},\ldots,n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{n})
. Our theoretical results establish that
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is the unique calibration-consistent concentration parameter, the posterior concentrates at rate
O(1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}})
, and BQ-CP’s stochastic dominance guarantee extends to per-weight-profile posteriors. The geographic instantiation (GeoBCP) demonstrates the practical value of these contributions through spatial diagnostics.

Broader impact.
WBCP is applicable wherever weighted CP is used: covariate shift, domain adaptation, fairness-aware prediction, and spatial statistics. The
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
diagnostic provides a principled basis for identifying where predictions lack statistical support, enabling targeted data collection.

Limitations.
(1) The Dirichlet model imposes a specific parametric form on spacings; exploring nonparametric alternatives is future work. (2) Spatially correlated residuals may inflate
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
; a design-effect correction (Appendix
L
) partially addresses this. (3) Computational cost scales linearly with
M
; for large-scale applications, variance reduction techniques could reduce the required MC samples.

Appendix A
Introduction and Motivation

Conformal prediction (CP) provides distribution-free prediction intervals with finite-sample marginal coverage guarantees under the exchangeability assumption. In many real-world settings, however, this assumption is violated: the test distribution may differ from the calibration distribution due to covariate shift, spatial heterogeneity, domain adaptation, or other forms of distribution shift.

Two levels of uncertainty.

A fundamental distinction, often overlooked, is between two levels of uncertainty in any prediction system:

1.

Predictive uncertainty
(Level 1): the risk that the true outcome
Y
falls outside the prediction interval
[\hat{f}(X)\pm\lambda]
. CP addresses this by constructing a threshold
\hat{q}
such that
\Pr(Y\in[\hat{f}(X)\pm\hat{q}])\geq 1-\alpha
.

2.

Meta-uncertainty
(Level 2): the uncertainty about the threshold
\hat{q}
itself. Is it well-determined by the calibration data, or could it be substantially different? Standard CP produces a single deterministic threshold with no indication of its reliability.

BQ-CP
(Snell & Griffiths, 2025) addressed Level 2 by reframing CP as Bayesian Quadrature, modeling the uncertainty in quantile spacings via
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
to produce a full posterior over the threshold. This yields powerful data-conditional guarantees but requires the i.i.d. assumption—as the authors explicitly note:
“The limitations of our method lie primarily in the two main assumptions it makes. First, it assumes that the data at deployment time are independent and identically distributed to the calibration data.”
(Section 6).

Weighted CP
(Tibshirani et al., 2019) handles distribution shift via importance weights, maintaining approximate coverage. However, it remains purely frequentist: the output is a single deterministic threshold
\hat{q}_{w}
, providing no meta-uncertainty information.

Weighted Bayesian Conformal Prediction (W-BQ-CP)
unifies these two frameworks by replacing BQ-CP’s uniform Dirichlet with a weighted Dirichlet
\mathrm{Dir}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{1},\ldots,n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{n})
, where
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is Kish’s effective sample size. This produces:

•

Per-weight-profile
posterior distributions over the threshold (vs. BQ-CP’s single global posterior);

•

Weight-dependent
posterior concentration controlled by
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
(vs. BQ-CP’s fixed
n
);

•

Data-conditional
guarantees per weight profile (vs. weighted CP’s marginal-only guarantees).

This document provides the complete theoretical foundations, organized to match the theorem numbering in the main paper: calibration consistency (§
E
, Theorem
8
), posterior concentration (§
F
, Theorem
10
), weighted stochastic dominance (§
G
, Theorem
12
), and conditional coverage (§
H
, Theorem
14
). Additional supporting results include limit consistency (§
I
), adaptive bandwidth regularization (§
J
), and a discussion of limitations (§
L
).

Appendix B
Setup and Notation

We establish the common framework for all results.

•

Calibration set:
\{(\rho_{i},w_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
\rho_{i}=|Y_{i}-\hat{f}(X_{i})|
are nonconformity scores and
w_{i}
are importance weights for a given weight profile.

•

Normalized weights:
\tilde{w}_{i}=w_{i}/\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}
, satisfying
\sum_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}=1
.

•

Sorted scores:
\rho_{(1)}\leq\rho_{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq\rho_{(n)}
, with
\rho_{(n+1)}\coloneqq B
(upper bound on loss).

•

Normalized weights (sorted by score):
\tilde{w}_{(i)}=w_{\pi(i)}/\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{\pi(j)}
, where
\pi
is the permutation that sorts scores.

•

Cumulative weight:
p_{j}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{j}\tilde{w}_{(i)}
, with
p_{0}=0
and
p_{n}=1
.

•

Kish’s effective sample size:
n_{\mathrm{eff}}=\bigl(\sum_{i}w_{i}\bigr)^{2}/\sum_{i}w_{i}^{2}
.

•

Quantile level:
q=1-\alpha
(e.g.,
q=0.9
for 90% coverage).

Definition 2

(W-BQ-CP Dirichlet Model)
.

For a given weight profile
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
, the W-BQ-CP posterior models the uncertainty in quantile spacings via:

\boldsymbol{V}=(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n})\sim\mathrm{Dir}\bigl(n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{(1)},\;\ldots,\;n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{(n)}\bigr),

(16)

where
\alpha_{i}=n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{(i)}
and
\alpha_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

The posterior threshold is the random variable:

\lambda=\rho_{(K)},\quad K=\min\bigl\{j:S_{j}\geq q\bigr\},\quad S_{j}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{j}V_{(i)}.

(17)

The HPD threshold at confidence
\beta
is:

\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}}=\inf\bigl\{\lambda:\Pr(\lambda\leq\lambda)\geq\beta\bigr\}.

(18)

Remark 2

(Comparison with BQ-CP)
.

The BQ-CP framework of Snell & Griffiths uses
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
(uniform concentration), which corresponds to:

•

All calibration points weighted equally (
\tilde{w}_{i}=1/n
for all
i
),

•

n_{\mathrm{eff}}=n
(maximum possible),

•

A single
global
posterior over one threshold
\lambda
shared by all weight profiles.

W-BQ-CP’s key departure is that the Dirichlet parameters depend on the weight profile
\mathbf{w}
, producing
per-profile
posteriors with weight-dependent concentration.

Remark 3

(Spatial Instantiation: GeoBCP)
.

In the spatial setting, the weights arise from a kernel function at test location
s
:

w_{i}(s)=K\bigl(\|s-s_{i}\|/h\bigr),\quad K(d)=\exp(-d^{2}/2),

(19)

where
h>0
is the bandwidth. This produces
per-location
posteriors
p(\lambda\mid\mathcal{D},s)
with location-dependent
n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s)
. All results in this document apply to this spatial instantiation by substituting
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}(s)
.

Appendix C
Measure-Theoretic Foundations: From Covariate Shift to Weighted Dirichlet

This section establishes the measure-theoretic justification for the W-BQ-CP Dirichlet model, bridging the gap between BQ-CP’s i.i.d. assumption and the weighted setting.

C.1
Covariate Shift and Likelihood Ratio Reweighting

In the standard CP setting, the calibration and test data are drawn from the same distribution
P
, ensuring exchangeability. Under covariate shift, the test distribution
P_{\mathrm{test}}
differs from the calibration distribution
P_{\mathrm{calib}}
.

Following Tibshirani et al. (2019), if the Radon–Nikodym derivative (likelihood ratio)
dP_{\mathrm{test}}/dP_{\mathrm{calib}}
is known, weighted conformal prediction with weights
w_{i}\propto(dP_{\mathrm{test}}/dP_{\mathrm{calib}})(X_{i})
recovers approximate coverage guarantees. In the spatial instantiation (GeoBCP), the spatial kernel provides a smooth approximation:

w_{i}(s)=K\bigl(\|s-s_{i}\|/h\bigr)\approx c\cdot\frac{dP_{s}}{dP_{\mathrm{calib}}}(X_{i},S_{i}),

(20)

where the approximation improves as the bandwidth
h
and the local data density jointly satisfy regularity conditions. In general, any importance weighting scheme that provides correct likelihood ratios can be used.

C.2
From Uniform Spacings to Weighted Bayesian Bootstrap

BQ-CP’s Lemma 4.2 establishes that under i.i.d. data, the quantile spacings follow
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
, which is the posterior of the Bayesian bootstrap (Rubin, 1981). W-BQ-CP generalizes this via the
weighted Bayesian bootstrap
(Newton & Raftery, 1994):

Proposition 6

(Weighted Bayesian Bootstrap Justification)
.

Let
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be calibration scores with normalized importance weights
\{\tilde{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. The weighted Bayesian bootstrap posterior for the weighted empirical distribution places random probability masses:

(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n})\sim\mathrm{Dir}\bigl(c\cdot\tilde{w}_{1},\ldots,c\cdot\tilde{w}_{n}\bigr),

(21)

where
c>0
is the concentration parameter. The resulting posterior mean of
V_{i}
is
\tilde{w}_{i}
(independent of
c
), while the posterior variance is
\tilde{w}_{i}(1-\tilde{w}_{i})/(c+1)
.

Proof.

This follows directly from the properties of the Dirichlet distribution. With
\alpha_{i}=c\cdot\tilde{w}_{i}
and
\alpha_{0}=c
:

\mathbb{E}[V_{i}]=\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{0}}=\tilde{w}_{i},\quad\mathrm{Var}(V_{i})=\frac{\alpha_{i}(\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{i})}{\alpha_{0}^{2}(\alpha_{0}+1)}=\frac{\tilde{w}_{i}(1-\tilde{w}_{i})}{c+1}.

The Newton–Raftery weighted Bayesian bootstrap uses this Dirichlet model to represent uncertainty in the weighted empirical distribution. When weights are uniform (
\tilde{w}_{i}=1/n
for all
i
) and
c=n
, this reduces to
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
, recovering BQ-CP’s Lemma 4.2.
∎

Appendix D
Foundational Lemma: Dirichlet Aggregation

Lemma 7

(Dirichlet Aggregation — Beta Marginals)
.

Let
(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n})\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
with
\alpha_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}
. For any subset
A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, define
S_{A}=\sum_{i\in A}V_{i}
and
p_{A}=\sum_{i\in A}\alpha_{i}/\alpha_{0}
. Then:

1.

S_{A}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_{0}\cdot p_{A},\;\alpha_{0}\cdot(1-p_{A}))
.

2.

\mathbb{E}[S_{A}]=p_{A}
.

3.

\mathrm{Var}(S_{A})=\dfrac{p_{A}(1-p_{A})}{\alpha_{0}+1}
.

In particular, for the W-BQ-CP cumulative weight
S_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}V_{(i)}
:

\boxed{\mathrm{Var}(S_{j})=\frac{p_{j}\bigl(1-p_{j}\bigr)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}+1}}

(22)

where
p_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\tilde{w}_{(i)}
is the deterministic cumulative weight.

Proof.

The Dirichlet distribution has the well-known aggregation property: if
(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n})\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
and we merge components indexed by
A
and
A^{c}
, then

(S_{A},1-S_{A})\sim\mathrm{Dir}\Bigl(\sum_{i\in A}\alpha_{i},\;\sum_{i\notin A}\alpha_{i}\Bigr).

This is precisely
S_{A}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)
with
a=\sum_{i\in A}\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{0}p_{A}
and
b=\alpha_{0}(1-p_{A})
.

The mean and variance follow from the Beta distribution:

\mathbb{E}[S_{A}]=\frac{a}{a+b}=p_{A},\quad\mathrm{Var}(S_{A})=\frac{ab}{(a+b)^{2}(a+b+1)}=\frac{p_{A}(1-p_{A})}{\alpha_{0}+1}.

For W-BQ-CP,
\alpha_{0}=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
, giving (
22
).
∎

Remark 4
.

In the BQ-CP setting with
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
, we have
\alpha_{0}=n
and
p_{j}=j/n
, giving
\mathrm{Var}(S_{j})=j(n-j)/(n^{2}(n+1))
. This is fixed across all weight profiles. In W-BQ-CP,
\alpha_{0}=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
varies with the weight profile, creating
profile-dependent posterior variance
—the key phenomenon that BQ-CP cannot produce.

Appendix E
Theorem 1: Calibration Consistency of
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
Scaling

This result justifies the choice
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
for the Dirichlet concentration parameter. It is presented first because it provides the foundational motivation for the W-BQ-CP construction.

Theorem 8

(Calibration Consistency)
.

Consider the general W-BQ-CP model with concentration
c>0
:

\boldsymbol{V}\sim\mathrm{Dir}\bigl(c\cdot\tilde{w}_{(1)},\ldots,c\cdot\tilde{w}_{(n)}\bigr).

Then:

1.

Mean consistency (holds for all
c>0
):

\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{\mathrm{post}}]=\lambda_{\mathrm{WCP}}+O(1/c).

(23)

2.

Variance calibration (requires
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
):

The posterior variance under the Dirichlet model is:

\mathrm{Var}(S_{j})=\frac{p_{j}(1-p_{j})}{c+1}.

(24)

Setting
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is the unique choice such that this posterior variance matches the frequentist variance of the weighted empirical CDF:

\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{freq}}\bigl(\hat{F}_{w}(t)\bigr)\approx\frac{F(t)(1-F(t))}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}.

(25)

3.

Bernstein–von Mises alignment (requires
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
):

When
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
, the Dirichlet posterior for the quantile has the same asymptotic variance as the frequentist sampling distribution of the weighted sample quantile. For any other
c
, the posterior is either too concentrated (
c>n_{\mathrm{eff}}
: overconfident) or too diffuse (
c<n_{\mathrm{eff}}
: underconfident).

Proof.

Part 1: Mean consistency.

For any
c>0
, the Dirichlet mean is
\mathbb{E}[V_{i}]=c\cdot\tilde{w}_{i}/(c\cdot\sum\tilde{w}_{i})=\tilde{w}_{i}
(independent of
c
). Therefore
\mathbb{E}[S_{j}]=p_{j}
. Since
\mathbb{E}[S_{j}]=p_{j}
and
K=\min\{j:S_{j}\geq q\}
, the expected threshold index satisfies
\mathbb{E}[K]=k^{*}+O(1/c)
. This follows because
\mathrm{Var}(S_{k^{*}})=p_{k^{*}}(1-p_{k^{*}})/(c+1)=O(1/c)
, so
S_{k^{*}}
concentrates around
p_{k^{*}}\geq q
with deviations of order
O(1/\sqrt{c})
, and the probability of
K
deviating from
k^{*}
decays exponentially (as shown in the proof of Theorem
10
).

Part 2: Variance calibration.

The Dirichlet model gives
\mathrm{Var}(S_{j})=p_{j}(1-p_{j})/(c+1)
.

The frequentist variance of the weighted empirical CDF is derived from the importance-weighted CLT. For the weighted empirical CDF
\hat{F}_{w}(t)=\sum_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}\mathbf{1}[\rho_{i}\leq t]
, where
\rho_{i}
are drawn independently from the calibration distribution
P_{\mathrm{calib}}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{freq}}(\hat{F}_{w}(t))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}^{2}\cdot\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{calib}}(\mathbf{1}[\rho_{i}\leq t])=\sum_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}^{2}\cdot F_{\mathrm{calib}}(t)(1-F_{\mathrm{calib}}(t)).

(26)

Under the covariate shift model with correct likelihood ratios (
w_{i}\propto dP_{\mathrm{test}}/dP_{\mathrm{calib}}(X_{i})
), the weighted estimator
\hat{F}_{w}(t)
targets the test CDF
F(t)\coloneqq F_{\mathrm{test}}(t)
. At the quantile
t=\lambda^{*}
where
F(\lambda^{*})=q
, the leading-order variance is:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{freq}}(\hat{F}_{w}(t))

\displaystyle\approx F(t)(1-F(t))\cdot\sum_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}^{2}=\frac{F(t)(1-F(t))}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}},

(27)

where the last equality uses
\sum_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}^{2}=(\sum w_{i}^{2})/(\sum w_{i})^{2}=1/n_{\mathrm{eff}}
. This leading-order approximation is exact when
P_{\mathrm{test}}=P_{\mathrm{calib}}
(recovering the i.i.d. case), and holds under the standard importance sampling variance identity
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{F}_{w})=\mathbb{E}_{P_{\mathrm{calib}}}[\tilde{w}^{2}(X)]\cdot F(t)(1-F(t))+O(\|P_{\mathrm{test}}-P_{\mathrm{calib}}\|^{2})
.

At the quantile
t=\lambda^{*}
where
F(\lambda^{*})=q
and
p_{k^{*}}\approx q
:

\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{freq}}=\frac{q(1-q)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}},\quad\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{Dir}}=\frac{q(1-q)}{c+1}.

(28)

Matching requires
c+1\approx n_{\mathrm{eff}}
, i.e.,
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}-1\approx n_{\mathrm{eff}}
for large
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

Part 3: Bernstein–von Mises.

By Part 2, the Dirichlet posterior variance of
S_{j}
at the quantile is
q(1-q)/(n_{\mathrm{eff}}+1)
. To translate from CDF space to quantile space, we apply the delta method (cf. van der Vaart, 1998, Theorem 2.7): if
\hat{F}_{w}
has variance
\sigma_{F}^{2}
near
\lambda^{*}
, then the quantile estimator
\hat{q}_{w}=\hat{F}_{w}^{-1}(q)
has variance
\sigma_{F}^{2}/f(\lambda^{*})^{2}
, where
f(\lambda^{*})
is the score density at the true quantile.

The frequentist asymptotic variance of the weighted sample quantile follows from the CLT for importance-weighted quantiles (see, e.g., Theorem 21.5 in van der Vaart, 1998, combined with the importance-weighted empirical process theory of Koul, 1992):

\sigma_{\mathrm{frequentist}}^{2}=\frac{q(1-q)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot f(\lambda^{*})^{2}}.

(29)

For the Dirichlet posterior, the same delta method applied to
S_{j}
yields:

\sigma_{\mathrm{posterior}}^{2}\approx\frac{q(1-q)}{(n_{\mathrm{eff}}+1)\cdot f_{q}^{2}},

(30)

where
f_{q}
is the weighted score density at the quantile. Under correct weight specification (Assumption
4
),
f_{q}\to f(\lambda^{*})
as
n_{\mathrm{eff}}\to\infty
, and
(n_{\mathrm{eff}}+1)^{-1}=n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}(1+O(1/n_{\mathrm{eff}}))
, confirming asymptotic matching.
∎

Remark 5

(Second-Moment Matching Interpretation)
.

The choice
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
can be understood through a thought experiment: how many i.i.d. samples
n^{*}
would produce the same estimation variance as our weighted sample? For a weighted estimator
\hat{\mu}=\sum_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}Y_{i}
with equal-variance observations,
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu})=\sigma^{2}\sum_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}^{2}
. Setting this equal to
\sigma^{2}/n^{*}
yields
n^{*}=1/\sum_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}^{2}=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
. Thus
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
precisely quantifies the information content of the weighted calibration set—the number of “equivalent uniform samples”—and using it as the Dirichlet concentration ensures the posterior reflects exactly this degree of certainty.

Appendix F
Theorem 2: Posterior Concentration Rate

This is the central novel theoretical result, establishing that the W-BQ-CP posterior standard deviation decays as
O(1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}})
.

F.1
Beta Concentration Inequality

We first state a tail bound for Beta random variables.

Lemma 9

(Beta Tail Bound)
.

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)
with
\mu=a/(a+b)
and
\kappa=a+b
. Then for any
t>0
:

\Pr\bigl(|X-\mu|\geq t\bigr)\leq 2\exp\Bigl(-\kappa\cdot d(t,\mu)\Bigr),

(31)

where
d(t,\mu)=(\mu+t)\log\frac{\mu+t}{\mu}+(1-\mu-t)\log\frac{1-\mu-t}{1-\mu}
is the binary KL divergence. For
t\leq\mu(1-\mu)
, this simplifies to:

\Pr\bigl(|X-\mu|\geq t\bigr)\leq 2\exp\biggl(-\frac{\kappa\,t^{2}}{2\mu(1-\mu)}\biggr).

(32)

Proof.

The exact bound (
31
) follows from the exponential tilting argument applied to the Beta distribution (see, e.g., Marchal & Arbel, 2017). The simplified bound (
32
) follows from the Pinsker-type inequality
d(t,\mu)\geq t^{2}/(2\mu(1-\mu))
for
t\leq\mu(1-\mu)
.
∎

F.2
Main Concentration Theorem

Theorem 10

(Posterior Concentration Rate)
.

Under the W-BQ-CP Dirichlet model (Definition
2
), the posterior standard deviation of the threshold satisfies:

\boxed{\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}^{2}\leq C\cdot\frac{q^{*}}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}\cdot\frac{\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}^{2}}{\underline{w}^{2}}}

(33)

where:

•

q^{*}=\max(q(1-q),\,1/4)
controls the quantile-dependent constant,

•

\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}=\max_{j}|\rho_{(j+1)}-\rho_{(j)}|
is the maximum spacing between consecutive sorted scores,

•

\underline{w}=\min_{j\in\{k^{*}-1,k^{*},k^{*}+1\}}\tilde{w}_{(j)}
is the minimum weight near the quantile boundary,

•

C>0
is a universal constant.

In particular,
\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}=O\bigl(1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}\bigr)
as
n_{\mathrm{eff}}\to\infty
, with the constant depending on the score density and weight distribution.

Proof.

Recall that the posterior threshold is
\lambda=\rho_{(K)}
where
K=\min\{j:S_{j}\geq q\}
, and the deterministic weighted CP index is
k^{*}=\min\{j:p_{j}\geq q\}
.

Step 1: Decompose the variance.

\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[(\lambda-\rho_{(k^{*})})^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}[\lambda]-\rho_{(k^{*})})^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[(\lambda-\rho_{(k^{*})})^{2}].

(34)

Step 2: Bound deviation via index shift.

Since
\lambda=\rho_{(K)}
and
\rho_{(k^{*})}
are both order statistics:

|\lambda-\rho_{(k^{*})}|=|\rho_{(K)}-\rho_{(k^{*})}|\leq|K-k^{*}|\cdot\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}.

(35)

Therefore:

\mathbb{E}[(\lambda-\rho_{(k^{*})})^{2}]\leq\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[(K-k^{*})^{2}].

(36)

Step 3: Bound the expected squared index shift.

The key observation:
K>k^{*}+m
if and only if
S_{k^{*}+m}<q
, i.e., the cumulative Dirichlet weight has not yet reached
q
at index
k^{*}+m
.

For
m\geq 1
, we have
p_{k^{*}+m}=p_{k^{*}}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{w}_{(k^{*}+i)}\geq q+m\cdot\underline{w}
, so:

\displaystyle\Pr(K>k^{*}+m)

\displaystyle=\Pr(S_{k^{*}+m}<q)=\Pr(S_{k^{*}+m}-p_{k^{*}+m}<q-p_{k^{*}+m})

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\bigl(S_{k^{*}+m}-p_{k^{*}+m}<-m\cdot\underline{w}\bigr).

(37)

By Lemma
7
,
S_{k^{*}+m}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot p_{k^{*}+m},\;n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot(1-p_{k^{*}+m}))
. Applying the Beta tail bound (
32
) with
\kappa=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
,
\mu=p_{k^{*}+m}
, and
t=m\cdot\underline{w}
:

\Pr(K>k^{*}+m)\leq\exp\biggl(-\frac{n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot m^{2}\cdot\underline{w}^{2}}{2\,p_{k^{*}+m}(1-p_{k^{*}+m})}\biggr)\leq\exp\biggl(-\frac{n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot m^{2}\cdot\underline{w}^{2}}{2\,q(1-q)}\biggr),

(38)

where the last inequality uses
p_{k^{*}+m}(1-p_{k^{*}+m})\leq q^{*}
with
q^{*}\coloneqq\max(q(1-q),\,1/4)
. When
q\leq 1/2
,
p_{k^{*}+m}>q
implies
p(1-p)\leq q(1-q)
, and when
q>1/2
, we use the universal bound
p(1-p)\leq 1/4
.

By an identical argument, for
m\geq 1
:

\Pr(K<k^{*}-m)\leq\exp\biggl(-\frac{n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot m^{2}\cdot\underline{w}^{2}}{2\,q(1-q)}\biggr).

(39)

Step 4: Compute
\mathbb{E}[(K-k^{*})^{2}]
.

Using the layer cake formula:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(K-k^{*})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(2m-1)\cdot\Pr(|K-k^{*}|\geq m)

\displaystyle\leq 1+2\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(2m+1)\cdot\exp\biggl(-\frac{n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot m^{2}\cdot\underline{w}^{2}}{2q(1-q)}\biggr).

(40)

Define
\gamma\coloneqq n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\underline{w}^{2}/(2\,q^{*})
, where
q^{*}=\max(q(1-q),\,1/4)
. For the sum:

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(2m+1)e^{-\gamma m^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 3e^{-\gamma}+\int_{0}^{\infty}(2x+3)e^{-\gamma x^{2}}\,dx

\displaystyle=3e^{-\gamma}+\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\gamma}\quad\text{for large }\gamma,

(41)

where
C
is a universal constant. Therefore:

\mathbb{E}[(K-k^{*})^{2}]\leq 1+\frac{C^{\prime}}{\gamma}=1+\frac{2C^{\prime}\cdot q^{*}}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\underline{w}^{2}}.

(42)

For large
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
, the dominant term is
O(1/(n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\underline{w}^{2}))
.

Step 5: Combine.

Substituting into (
36
):

\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}^{2}\leq\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}^{2}\cdot\frac{2C^{\prime}\cdot q(1-q)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\underline{w}^{2}}=O\biggl(\frac{\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}^{2}}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\underline{w}^{2}}\biggr).

(43)

Absorbing the constant
2C^{\prime}
into the bound and noting that
n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\underline{w}^{2}\geq\underline{w}^{2}\cdot n_{\mathrm{eff}}
completes the proof of (
33
). The rate
\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}=O(1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}})
follows since
\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}
,
\underline{w}
, and
q
are treated as constants with respect to
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
.
∎

Remark 6

(Interpretation)
.

The bound has a natural interpretation:

•

q(1-q)
: maximal at
q=0.5
, reflecting the inherent difficulty of estimating the median vs. extreme quantiles.

•

1/n_{\mathrm{eff}}
: the “effective sample size” rate—weight profiles with high
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
concentrate faster.

•

\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}/\underline{w}
: the ratio of score spacing to weight resolution. When weights are uniform (
\underline{w}=1/n
) and scores have density
f_{\rho}
at the quantile,
\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}/\underline{w}\approx 1/f_{\rho}
—recovering the classical quantile estimation rate.

Remark 7

(Comparison with BQ-CP)
.

In BQ-CP,
n_{\mathrm{eff}}=n
and
\tilde{w}_{i}=1/n
for all
i
, so
\underline{w}=1/n
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}=O(\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}\cdot\sqrt{n/n_{\mathrm{eff}}})=O(\bar{\Delta}_{\rho})
—the posterior width is determined only by the score spacing, with no variation across weight profiles. W-BQ-CP introduces the
1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}
factor, which creates meaningful variation in posterior width across different weight profiles.

Corollary 11

(Exponential Tail Bound)
.

Under the same conditions as Theorem
10
, for any
t>0
:

\Pr\bigl(|\lambda-\lambda_{\mathrm{WCP}}|>t\bigr)\leq 2\exp\biggl(-\frac{n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\underline{w}^{2}}{2q(1-q)}\cdot\Bigl\lfloor\frac{t}{\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}}\Bigr\rfloor^{2}\biggr).

(44)

Proof.

|\lambda-\lambda_{\mathrm{WCP}}|>t
implies
|K-k^{*}|>t/\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}
, i.e.,
|K-k^{*}|\geq\lfloor t/\bar{\Delta}_{\rho}\rfloor+1\eqqcolon m_{0}
. The result follows from the tail bound (
38
) with
m=m_{0}
.
∎

Appendix G
Theorem 3: Weighted Stochastic Dominance

This result generalizes BQ-CP’s Theorem 4.3 to the weighted setting. The key insight is that BQ-CP’s worst-case quantile function argument (Theorem 4.1) is a
pure optimization result
independent of the distributional assumption on spacings.

G.1
Weighted Quadrature Setup

Definition 3

(Expected Loss under Weight Profile)
.

For a given weight profile
\mathbf{w}
with corresponding test distribution
P_{\mathbf{w}}
, the expected loss under threshold
\lambda
is:

L_{\mathbf{w}}(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim P_{\mathbf{w}}}[\ell(Z,\lambda)]=\int_{0}^{1}K_{\mathbf{w}}(t)\,dt,

(45)

where
K_{\mathbf{w}}(t)=F_{\mathbf{w}}^{-1}(t)
is the quantile function of the individual losses under
P_{\mathbf{w}}
.

Assumption 3

(Weighted Dirichlet Model for Spacings)
.

For a given weight profile
\mathbf{w}
, the uncertainty in the quantile spacings is modeled as:

(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n+1})\sim\mathrm{Dir}\bigl(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\alpha_{n+1}\bigr),

(46)

where
\alpha_{i}=n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{(i)}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
\alpha_{n+1}=n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\tilde{w}_{n+1}
represents the test point’s contribution to the spacing model. In practice, the test point weight
\tilde{w}_{n+1}
is not observed; following BQ-CP (which assigns
\alpha_{n+1}=1
corresponding to a uniform weight of
1/(n+1)
), we set
\alpha_{n+1}=n_{\mathrm{eff}}/(n+1)
as the natural weighted analogue: when weights are uniform,
n_{\mathrm{eff}}=n
and
\alpha_{n+1}=n/(n+1)\approx 1
, recovering BQ-CP’s parametrization. Since
\alpha_{n+1}
only affects the spacing associated with the upper bound
\ell_{(n+1)}=B
, and
B
is typically chosen conservatively, the stochastic dominance result (Theorem
12
) holds for any
\alpha_{n+1}>0
and is not sensitive to this choice.

Remark 8

(Justification of Assumption
3
)
.

This assumption is justified under
local exchangeability
: if the weights
w_{i}
correctly reflect the importance of each calibration point for the test distribution (e.g., via likelihood ratios under covariate shift), then the Dirichlet model provides a Bayesian nonparametric posterior for the weighted CDF consistent with the weighted Bayesian bootstrap (Newton & Raftery, 1994; Proposition
6
). The concentration
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
ensures that the posterior variance matches the effective degrees of freedom (see Theorem
8
).

Theorem 12

(Weighted Stochastic Dominance)
.

Under Assumption
3
, define the stochastic upper bound:

L_{w}^{+}=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}U_{i}\cdot\ell_{(i)},\quad\boldsymbol{U}\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),

(47)

where
\ell_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq\ell_{(n)}
are the sorted individual losses and
\ell_{(n+1)}=B
.

Then for any
b\in(-\infty,B]
:

\boxed{\inf_{\pi}\Pr\bigl(L\leq b\mid\ell_{1:n}\bigr)\geq\Pr\bigl(L_{w}^{+}\leq b\bigr)}

(48)

where the infimum is over all priors
\pi
on the state of nature, and
L
is the true expected loss.

Proof.

The proof follows the structure of BQ-CP’s Theorem 4.3 (Snell & Griffiths, 2025), adapted for the weighted Dirichlet model. We proceed in three steps.

Step 1: Worst-case quantile function (independent of weighting).

By BQ-CP Proposition B.2 (which is a pure optimization result), for any set of quantile positions
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
and losses
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}
:

\sup_{K\in\mathcal{K}_{n}}J[K]=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}(t_{(i)}-t_{(i-1)})\cdot\ell_{(i)},

(49)

where
\mathcal{K}_{n}
is the set of non-decreasing quantile functions satisfying
K(t_{i})=\ell_{i}
, and
J[K]=\int_{0}^{1}K(t)\,dt
.

This result depends only on the monotonicity of quantile functions and is independent of any distributional assumptions.

Step 2: Marginalization over spacings.

Conditioned on spacings
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n+1}
, the worst-case risk satisfies:

\sup_{\pi}\mathbb{E}(L\mid t_{1:n},\ell_{1:n})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}u_{i}\cdot\ell_{(i)},

(50)

where
u_{i}=t_{(i)}-t_{(i-1)}
are the spacings of the quantile positions.

Under Assumption
3
,
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n+1})\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
. Therefore, marginalizing:

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi}\Pr(L\leq b\mid\ell_{1:n})

\displaystyle\geq\int\mathbf{1}\Bigl\{\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}u_{i}\cdot\ell_{(i)}\leq b\Bigr\}\,p\bigl(\boldsymbol{u}\bigr)\,d\boldsymbol{u}

(51)

\displaystyle=\Pr\bigl(L_{w}^{+}\leq b\bigr).

(52)

The proof follows exactly the chain of inequalities in BQ-CP’s Theorem 4.3 proof (Appendix B.5, equations (66)–(74)), with the only substitution being
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)\to\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
for the spacings distribution.

Step 3: Verify the substitution is valid.

The proof of BQ-CP’s Theorem 4.3 uses the spacings distribution only in the final marginalization step (equation (73) in their proof). The preceding steps—changing the order of integration (68), pushing the infimum inside (69), and applying the worst-case quantile function (70–72)—are purely analytic manipulations that hold for
any
distribution on the spacings. Therefore, replacing
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
with
\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
preserves the validity of the proof.
∎

Corollary 13

(Data-Conditional Guarantee)
.

For any confidence level
\beta\in(0,1)
, define the W-BQ-CP HPD threshold:

\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}}=\inf\bigl\{\lambda:\Pr(L_{w}^{+}\leq\alpha\mid\ell_{1:n})\geq\beta\bigr\}.

(53)

Then under Assumption
3
:

\boxed{\inf_{\pi}\Pr\bigl(L(\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}})\leq\alpha\mid\ell_{1:n}\bigr)\geq\beta}

(54)

This is a
per-weight-profile, data-conditional
guarantee: given this specific calibration set and this specific weight profile, the risk is controlled with posterior probability at least
\beta
.

Proof.

Direct application of Theorem
12
with
b=\alpha
, followed by the definition of
\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}}
.
∎

Remark 9

(What is genuinely new vs. BQ-CP)
.

BQ-CP’s Corollary 4.4 provides
\inf_{\pi}\Pr(L\leq\alpha\mid\ell_{1:n})\geq\beta
—a
global
data-conditional guarantee that is the same for all test points.

Corollary
13
provides
\inf_{\pi}\Pr(L\leq\alpha\mid\ell_{1:n},\mathbf{w})\geq\beta
—a
per-weight-profile
guarantee that:

1.

Involves the expected loss under the
weighted
test distribution (not the global risk);

2.

Has a profile-specific posterior
L_{w}^{+}
whose width depends on
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
;

3.

Provides different “tightness” for different weight profiles: when
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is large,
\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}}
is close to
\lambda_{\mathrm{WCP}}
(tight posterior); when
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is small,
\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}}\gg\lambda_{\mathrm{WCP}}
(wide posterior, more conservative).

This per-profile adaptation is impossible under BQ-CP’s i.i.d. assumption.

Appendix H
Theorem 4: Conditional Coverage Bound

We now connect the Dirichlet posterior to the actual conditional coverage probability.

Assumption 4

(Correct Weight Specification)
.

The importance weights
w_{i}
satisfy:

(A1)

Correct specification:

w_{i}\propto dP_{\mathrm{test}}/dP_{\mathrm{calib}}(X_{i})
, where
P_{\mathrm{test}}
is the test distribution and
P_{\mathrm{calib}}
is the calibration distribution.

(A2)

Smooth score CDF:
The score distribution under
P_{\mathrm{test}}
has a continuous density
f
that is positive in a neighborhood of
F^{-1}(q)
.

Theorem 14

(Conditional Coverage)
.

Under Assumptions
3
and
4
, the conditional miscoverage of W-BQ-CP with HPD threshold at confidence
\beta>1/2
satisfies:

\boxed{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\bigl[\alpha_{\mathrm{cond}}\bigr]=\alpha-\Phi^{-1}(\beta)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{q(1-q)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}}+o\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}}\Bigr)}

(55)

where
\Phi^{-1}
is the standard normal quantile function,
q=1-\alpha
, and the expectation is over random calibration sets
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

Step 1: Asymptotic normality of the weighted quantile estimator.

Under Assumption
4
, the weighted sample quantile
\hat{q}_{w}=\lambda_{\mathrm{WCP}}
is a consistent estimator of the true quantile
\lambda^{*}=F^{-1}(q)
.

By the CLT for weighted quantiles (the delta method applied to the weighted empirical CDF; see van der Vaart, 1998, Theorem 21.5, and its extension to importance-weighted empirical processes in Koul, 1992, Chapter 4):

\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}\cdot(\hat{q}_{w}-\lambda^{*})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\Bigl(0,\;\frac{q(1-q)}{f(\lambda^{*})^{2}}\Bigr),

(56)

where
f(\lambda^{*})
is the score density at the true quantile.

Step 2: Bayesian posterior approximation.

We establish that the W-BQ-CP posterior is asymptotically normal. The Dirichlet posterior induces a distribution on
S_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}V_{(i)}
, which by Lemma
7
has
S_{j}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot p_{j},n_{\mathrm{eff}}(1-p_{j}))
. For large
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
, the Beta distribution
\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)
with
a+b=n_{\mathrm{eff}}\to\infty
converges to
\mathcal{N}(a/n_{\mathrm{eff}},ab/n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3})
(by the CLT for the Dirichlet as a normalized Gamma vector). This establishes that
S_{j}
is asymptotically normal around
p_{j}
with variance
p_{j}(1-p_{j})/(n_{\mathrm{eff}}+1)
. The threshold
\lambda=\rho_{(K)}
is a function of the first crossing
K=\min\{j:S_{j}\geq q\}
, and by the delta method (inverting the CDF at
q
), the induced posterior on
\lambda
is asymptotically:

\lambda\mid\mathcal{D}\;\overset{\cdot}{\sim}\;\mathcal{N}\Bigl(\hat{q}_{w},\;\frac{q(1-q)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot f_{q}^{2}}\Bigr),

(57)

where
f_{q}
is the weighted score density at the quantile. Under correct weight specification (A1),
f_{q}\to f(\lambda^{*})
as
n_{\mathrm{eff}}\to\infty
.

Step 3: HPD threshold characterization.

The HPD threshold at confidence
\beta
is the
\beta
-quantile of the posterior:

\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}}=\hat{q}_{w}+\Phi^{-1}(\beta)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{q(1-q)}}{\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}\cdot f_{q}}+o\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}}\Bigr).

(58)

Step 4: Compute conditional coverage.

The conditional miscoverage is:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathrm{cond}}

\displaystyle=\Pr(\rho_{\mathrm{new}}>\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}}\mid\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=1-F(\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}}).

(59)

Expanding
F
around
\lambda^{*}
where
F(\lambda^{*})=q
:

F(\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}})=q+f(\lambda^{*})\cdot(\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}}-\lambda^{*})+O((\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}}-\lambda^{*})^{2}).

(60)

Substituting (
58
) and using
\hat{q}_{w}=\lambda^{*}+O_{p}(1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}})
:

\displaystyle F(\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}})

\displaystyle=q+f(\lambda^{*})\cdot\biggl[(\hat{q}_{w}-\lambda^{*})+\Phi^{-1}(\beta)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{q(1-q)}}{\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}\cdot f_{q}}\biggr]+o\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}}\Bigr).

(61)

Taking expectations over
\mathcal{D}
(noting
\mathbb{E}[\hat{q}_{w}-\lambda^{*}]=O(1/n_{\mathrm{eff}})
is a higher-order bias term) and using
f_{q}\to f(\lambda^{*})
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[F(\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}})]

\displaystyle=q+\Phi^{-1}(\beta)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{q(1-q)}}{\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}}+o\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}}\Bigr).

(62)

Therefore:

\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{\mathrm{cond}}]=1-\mathbb{E}[F(\lambda_{\mathrm{HPD}})]=\alpha-\Phi^{-1}(\beta)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{q(1-q)}}{\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}}+o\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}}\Bigr).

(63)

∎

Remark 10

(Key implications)
.

Theorem
14
has several important consequences:

1.

\beta=0.5
:

\Phi^{-1}(0.5)=0
, so
\alpha_{\mathrm{cond}}\approx\alpha
. The posterior median recovers weighted CP’s coverage.

2.

\beta>0.5
:

\Phi^{-1}(\beta)>0
, so
\alpha_{\mathrm{cond}}<\alpha
. W-BQ-CP is more conservative, with the improvement scaling as
1/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}
.

3.

Adaptive conservatism:
When
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is large (many effective calibration points), the improvement from
\beta>0.5
is small (the interval is already well-calibrated). When
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is small (few effective calibration points), the improvement is larger (the HPD provides meaningful extra conservatism where it is most needed).

4.

Approach to conditional coverage:
As
\beta\to 1
, the conservatism increases without bound, reflecting that
\beta=1
corresponds to using the maximum score (infinite conservatism). In practice,
\beta\in[0.9,0.95]
provides a good balance.

Appendix I
Limit Consistency Properties

A critical test of the W-BQ-CP construction is whether it recovers known frameworks in limiting cases. We establish two limit consistency results. These results are presented for the spatial instantiation (GeoBCP, Remark
3
), where the weights depend on a bandwidth parameter
h
.

Proposition 15

(Uniform Limit: Recovery of BQ-CP)
.

As the kernel bandwidth
h\to\infty
, the W-BQ-CP Dirichlet model converges to the BQ-CP model:

h\to\infty\implies\tilde{w}_{i}(s)\to\frac{1}{n}\;\;\forall\,i,\quad n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s)\to n,\quad\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}(s))\to\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1).

(64)

Proof.

As
h\to\infty
, the Gaussian kernel
K(\|s-s_{i}\|/h)=\exp(-\|s-s_{i}\|^{2}/(2h^{2}))\to 1
for all
i
. Therefore
w_{i}(s)\to 1
,
\tilde{w}_{i}(s)=w_{i}/\sum_{j}w_{j}\to 1/n
, and:

n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s)=\frac{(\sum_{i}w_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{i}w_{i}^{2}}\to\frac{n^{2}}{n}=n.

The Dirichlet parameters become
\alpha_{i}(s)=n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s)\cdot\tilde{w}_{i}(s)\to n\cdot(1/n)=1
for all
i
, yielding
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
.

Moreover, the W-BQ-CP threshold posterior becomes global (identical for all test locations), and the HPD threshold coincides with BQ-CP’s HPD threshold. This confirms smooth degeneration to the i.i.d. framework.
∎

Proposition 16

(Singular Limit: Nearest-Neighbor Collapse)
.

As the kernel bandwidth
h\to 0^{+}
, assuming a unique nearest neighbor
s_{i^{*}}
to the test location
s
:

h\to 0^{+}\implies\tilde{w}_{i^{*}}(s)\to 1,\quad\tilde{w}_{i}(s)\to 0\;\;\forall\,i\neq i^{*},\quad n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s)\to 1.

(65)

The Dirichlet posterior collapses to a point mass, and the W-BQ-CP threshold converges to
\rho_{i^{*}}
(the score of the nearest calibration point).

Proof.

As
h\to 0^{+}
, let
d_{i}=\|s-s_{i}\|
and
d^{*}=\min_{i}d_{i}=d_{i^{*}}
. For any
i\neq i^{*}
:

\frac{w_{i}(s)}{w_{i^{*}}(s)}=\frac{\exp(-d_{i}^{2}/(2h^{2}))}{\exp(-d_{i^{*}}^{2}/(2h^{2}))}=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{d_{i}^{2}-d_{i^{*}}^{2}}{2h^{2}}\Bigr)\to 0,

since
d_{i}>d_{i^{*}}
(unique nearest neighbor). Thus
\tilde{w}_{i^{*}}\to 1
and
\tilde{w}_{i}\to 0
for
i\neq i^{*}
.

The effective sample size becomes
n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s)=1/\sum_{i}\tilde{w}_{i}^{2}\to 1/(1^{2}+0+\cdots)=1
. The Dirichlet parameters are
\alpha_{i^{*}}\to 1
and
\alpha_{i}\to 0
for
i\neq i^{*}
, so the posterior places all mass on the
i^{*}
-th component. The threshold is deterministically
\rho_{i^{*}}
.

This matches the behavior of 1-nearest-neighbor prediction: with only one effective calibration point, there is no statistical basis for uncertainty quantification, and the posterior correctly reflects this maximal uncertainty collapse.
∎

Remark 11

(Intermediate Regime)
.

For finite
h
, W-BQ-CP interpolates smoothly between these extremes. When many calibration points have non-negligible weights,
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is large and the posterior is concentrated, providing tight intervals. When few points contribute,
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is small and the posterior is diffuse, producing appropriately wide intervals that honestly communicate the lack of effective calibration information. More generally, for any importance weighting scheme,
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
controls this interpolation.

Remark 12

(General Uniform Limit)
.

Beyond the spatial setting, the uniform limit holds for
any
weighting scheme: when all weights are equal (
w_{i}=c
for all
i
and some
c>0
),
\tilde{w}_{i}=1/n
,
n_{\mathrm{eff}}=n
, and W-BQ-CP reduces to BQ-CP with
\mathrm{Dir}(1,\ldots,1)
. This is immediate from the definition.

Appendix J
Adaptive Bandwidth Regularization

The fixed-bandwidth kernel can suffer from numerical instability when test points lie in data-sparse regions. This section establishes the regularization properties of adaptive bandwidth selection, applicable to the spatial instantiation (GeoBCP).

Definition 4

(Adaptive Bandwidth)
.

For test location
s
, the adaptive bandwidth is defined as:

h(s)=h_{0}\cdot\mathrm{median}\bigl(\|s-s_{(1)}\|,\ldots,\|s-s_{(k)}\|\bigr),

(66)

where
s_{(1)},\ldots,s_{(k)}
are the
k
-nearest calibration points to
s
, and
h_{0}>0
is a base scaling factor.

Proposition 17

(Singularity Prevention)
.

Under the adaptive bandwidth (Definition
4
), the effective sample size satisfies
n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s)\geq c_{k}>0
for all test locations
s
, where
c_{k}
depends only on
k
and the kernel function
K
.

Proof sketch.

The adaptive bandwidth ensures that at least
k
calibration points fall within
O(h(s))
distance of the test point. For the Gaussian kernel, these
k
points receive weights
w_{i}(s)\geq\exp(-C)
for some constant
C
depending on
h_{0}
. Since
n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s)\geq k\cdot(\min_{i\in\text{kNN}}w_{i}/\max_{i\in\text{kNN}}w_{i})^{2}/k=O(1)
uniformly, the effective sample size is bounded away from zero.
∎

Proposition 18

(Lipschitz Continuity of
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
)
.

Under the adaptive bandwidth with regularity conditions on the spatial point configuration, the effective sample size function
s\mapsto n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s)
is Lipschitz continuous. Consequently, the posterior variance surface
s\mapsto\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}^{2}(s)
is smooth and does not exhibit artificial discontinuities.

Proof sketch.

The adaptive bandwidth
h(s)
varies continuously with
s
(the median of
k
-NN distances is Lipschitz when point configurations avoid exact ties). The kernel weights
w_{i}(s)
are smooth functions of
s
(compositions of the smooth Gaussian kernel with continuous distance and bandwidth functions). By the chain rule,
n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s)
inherits Lipschitz continuity. The posterior variance
\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}^{2}(s)\propto 1/n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s)
is then also Lipschitz on any compact set where
n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s)\geq c_{k}>0
.
∎

Remark 13

(Limit Consistency of Adaptive Bandwidth)
.

As
k\to n
(all calibration points are neighbors),
h(s)
converges to a global constant, and the adaptive scheme reduces to the fixed-bandwidth method. As
k\to 1
, the bandwidth tracks only the nearest neighbor, providing maximal local adaptivity but potentially introducing noise. The choice of
k
thus controls the bias-variance tradeoff in bandwidth selection, analogous to the classical
k
-NN regression setting.

Appendix L
Discussion: Limitations and Open Problems

L.1
Spatial Autocorrelation of Residuals

A notable theoretical assumption underlying the W-BQ-CP framework is that, after importance reweighting, the nonconformity scores are approximately conditionally independent. However, if the underlying regression model fails to capture certain structure (e.g., spatial patterns), the residuals
\rho_{i}
may exhibit strong autocorrelation.

When correlated residuals are clustered, their information content is redundant—yet the importance weights treat them as independent contributions to
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
. This leads to
optimistic overestimation
of
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
: the Dirichlet posterior is more concentrated than warranted, producing prediction intervals that are too narrow.

A principled correction involves a
decorrelation adjustment
:

n_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{adj}}=\frac{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}{1+(k_{\mathrm{eff}}-1)\cdot\bar{r}},

(67)

where
\bar{r}
is the average pairwise correlation of residuals among the effective neighbors and
k_{\mathrm{eff}}
is the effective number of correlated neighbors. This mirrors the classical design effect correction in survey sampling. A full theoretical treatment of this adjustment is left to future work.

L.2
Further Open Directions

1.

Finite-sample coverage bound without asymptotic approximation:
Theorem
14
uses the Bernstein–von Mises approximation. A fully non-asymptotic bound (e.g., using Berry–Esseen type results for Dirichlet) would strengthen the result.

2.

Relaxing Assumption
4
:
The correct weight specification assumption requires known likelihood ratios. Analyzing robustness to weight misspecification would be valuable.

3.

Minimax optimality of
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
scaling:
Theorem
8
shows
c=n_{\mathrm{eff}}
matches the frequentist variance. Proving that this is minimax optimal (e.g., minimizing worst-case posterior calibration error) would be a stronger statement.

4.

Correlated posteriors across weight profiles:
The current framework treats each weight profile independently. Introducing a Gaussian process or Dirichlet process prior across profiles to share information could enable “borrowing strength” in data-sparse regions.

5.

Structured posteriors with GP priors:
For the spatial instantiation, replacing the independent per-location Dirichlet with a spatially-coupled model (e.g., logistic-GP Dirichlet) could provide smoother posterior surfaces and enable joint inference across locations.
