Meissa : Multi-modal Medical Agentic Intelligence
Yixiong Chen, Xinyi Bai, Yue Pan, Zongwei Zhou, Alan Yuille

Abstract
Multi-modal large language models (MM-LLMs) have shown strong performance in medical image understanding and clinical reasoning. Recent medical agent systems extend them with tool use and multi-agent collaboration, enabling complex decision-making beyond single-pass inference. However, these systems rely almost entirely on proprietary frontier models ( e.g., GPT), whose API-based deployment incurs high cost, high latency, and privacy risks that conflict with on-premise clinical requirements. We present Meissa , a lightweight 4B-parameter medical MM-LLM that brings full agentic capability offline. Instead of imitating static answers, Meissa learns both when to engage external interaction (strategy selection) and how to execute multi-step interaction (strategy execution) by distilling structured trajectories from frontier agent systems. To be specific, we propose: (1) Unified trajectory modeling : trajectories (reasoning and action traces) are represented within a single state–action–observation formalism, allowing one model to generalize across heterogeneous medical environments. (2) Three-tier stratified supervision : the model’s own errors trigger progressive escalation from direct reasoning to tool-augmented and multi-agent interaction, explicitly learning difficulty-aware strategy selection. (3) Prospective–retrospective supervision : pairing exploratory forward traces with hindsight-rationalized execution traces enables stable learning of effective interaction policies. Trained on ∼ {\sim} 40K curated trajectories, Meissa matches or exceeds proprietary frontier agents in 10 of 16 evaluation settings across 13 medical benchmarks spanning radiology, pathology, and clinical reasoning. Using over 25× fewer parameters than typical frontier models like Gemini-3, Meissa operates fully offline with ∼ 22 × {\sim}22\times lower end-to-end latency compared to API-based deployment. Data, models, and environments are released at https://github.com/Schuture/Meissa.

Introduction

Multi-modal large language models (MM-LLMs) can combine medical images with free-form clinical text, enabling unified visual understanding and clinical reasoning
[
52
,
40
,
42
]
. When extended with
agentic capabilities
, the ability to take external actions such as invoking tools, requesting intermediate evidence, and coordinating specialist roles, these models can solve clinical tasks that are difficult for single-pass prediction systems
[
25
,
13
,
41
]
. However, today’s strongest medical agents are usually powered by proprietary frontier models (
e.g.,
GPT
[
20
]
, Gemini
[
45
]
, Claude
[
3
]
) through cloud APIs. This deployment pattern is often incompatible with real clinical constraints: patient data must remain on-premise, repeated API calls increase cost, and multi-step interaction adds latency that can disrupt clinical workflows.

This paper is motivated by a simple tension: frontier agents are capable, but hard to deploy. We ask:

Can we transfer the action-taking behavior of frontier medical agents into a model that can run fully offline?

Here,
agentic behavior
refers to a model’s
interaction policy
: deciding whether to answer directly or to take external actions, and if it acts, choosing a sequence of actions based on observed outcomes. This differs from prior works distilling single-pass
reasoning ability

[
47
,
15
,
58
,
39
]
from larger models to smaller models, and from those learning
tool-use
in a single fixed pipeline
[
57
,
17
,
21
]
, because practical medical settings involve multiple interaction modes (direct reasoning, tool chains, visual feedback loops, role-based consultation, and multi-turn clinical questioning).

The core challenge is the lack of structured supervision for agentic behavior.

Although frontier models exhibit strong agentic behaviors, there is currently no publicly available training corpus designed to teach models how to behave as medical agents. Existing benchmarks provide final answers but do not specify whether external interaction was necessary or how actions should evolve based on intermediate observations. On the other hand, existing agent systems generate agentic trajectories (reasoning and action traces) within isolated environments, each with its own format and protocol, making it difficult to construct unified supervision across heterogeneous interaction modes.

To address this gap, we design a data synthesis pipeline that systematically constructs supervision for agentic behavior along three dimensions.

First
, we introduce a unified
state–action–observation
representation that models multiple interaction modes within a single formalism. Therefore, we avoid environment-specific formats and enable cross-environment learning within one model.

Second
, to teach
when
to act, we propose a
three-tier stratification strategy
driven by the model’s own errors. Queries that the model already solves become direct-reasoning trajectories (tier 1); queries solvable by a stronger frontier model without tools become enhanced reasoning trajectories (tier 2); and only the hardest residual samples trigger full agent environments (tier 3). This stratified supervision implicitly encodes the decision boundary between direct inference and external interaction.

Third
, to teach
how
to act within environments, we construct complementary
prospective
and
retrospective
trajectories. Prospective traces are recorded during forward interaction and capture exploratory decision-making under real observations. Retrospective traces preserve the same action sequence but provide clean hindsight explanations. Together, they provide stable execution supervision across heterogeneous medical agent environments.

Based on this pipeline, we train
Meissa

Method

We frame the training of

as
agentic behavior distillation
: learning a policy over

, which is the number of external actions an agent takes before producing an answer. This single variable unifies direct reasoning (
depth=0
), single-tool queries (
depth=1
), multi-step tool chains, multi-agent debate, and clinical simulation into a continuous spectrum. We first define a trajectory formalism that captures this spectrum (
Section

3.1
), then describe how stratified trajectories teach strategy selection (
Section

3.2
), the agent environments that serve as trajectory sources (
Section

3.3
), and the prospective-retrospective supervision that teaches strategy execution (
Section

3.4
).

3.1
Agentic Behavior Modeling

We model all agent interactions from direct reasoning to complex multi-agent collaboration within a unified trajectory formalism. An
agentic trajectory
is a sequence:

\tau=[(s_{0},a_{0},o_{1}),(s_{1},a_{1},o_{2}),\ldots,(s_{T-1},a_{T-1},o_{T})],

(1)

where state
s_{t}
is the conversation context up to step
t
,
a_{t}
is an action, and
o_{t}
is the observation returned by the environment. Concretely, each action
a_{t}
is serialized as either a
<|call|>
turn (a JSON-formatted action) or an
<|assistant|>
turn for the final answer.
<|observation|>
turn is returned by tools or sub-agents in JSON format. Inference terminates when the model emits an
<|assistant|>
turn or reaches
T_{\max}
; full schemas are in the supplementary.

The
interaction depth

T
of a trajectory is the key variable that unifies all agent behaviors. Direct reasoning corresponds to
T{=}0
(a single final-answer). For
Meissa
, we set
T_{\max}=4
for continuous tool calling;
T_{\max}=6
for interleaved image-text reasoning; and multi-agent collaboration and clinical simulation produce the deepest trajectories (
T_{\max}=12
). Learning
when
to produce a trajectory of a given depth, and
how
to act at each step, is the core objective of agentic behavior distillation.

We train the policy
\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
based on trajectories generated by a frontier model, where next-action prediction reduces to conditional language modeling. This separates the
learning problem
from the
environment details
, enabling a single model to generalize across environments. Critically, behavioral cloning over
ordered
trajectories learns causal decision-making: the model predicts each action conditioned on preceding observations. (Verified on
Section

4.4
).

3.2
Behavior Distillation via Stratified Trajectory Supervision

The key insight for teaching strategy selection is that not all queries require the same interaction depth. Rather than uniformly generating complex agentic trajectories for all samples, we stratify trajectory supervision using the model’s own errors as a curriculum signal
[
5
]
:

Tier 1: Direct Reasoning Trajectories.
We first run the student model
\mathcal{M}_{S}
(Qwen3-VL-4B
[
4
]
) directly on the training set
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
. Samples answered correctly yield direct reasoning trajectories (
T{=}0
, no external interaction):

\mathcal{D}_{\text{direct}}=\{(x,\tau_{\text{direct}})\mid(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}},\;\mathcal{M}_{S}(x)=y\}.

(2)

These trajectories supervise the model to answer efficiently when its parametric knowledge suffices, learning
when not to act
.

Tier 2: Enhanced Reasoning Trajectories.
The student’s incorrect samples are re-processed by a stronger teacher model
\mathcal{M}_{T}
(Gemini-3-flash
[
45
]
), again without external interaction:

\mathcal{D}_{\text{enhanced}}=\{(x,\tau_{\text{enhanced}})\mid(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{\text{direct}},\;\mathcal{M}_{T}(x)=y\}.

(3)

These trajectories demonstrate stronger reasoning that the student could not produce, but remain direct reasoning (
T{=}0
).

Tier 3: Agentic Trajectories.
The hardest residual samples are beyond both the student’s and teacher’s direct reasoning capacity. They are processed by the teacher within agent environments (
Section

3.3
):

\mathcal{D}_{\text{agentic}}=\{(x,\tau_{\text{agentic}})\mid(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{residual}},\;\mathcal{A}_{T}(x)=y\},

(4)

where
\mathcal{A}_{T}
denotes the teacher operating within an agent environment and residual erroneous samples
\mathcal{D}_{\text{residual}}=\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\setminus(\mathcal{D}_{\text{direct}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\text{enhanced}})
. Only these samples produce full multi-step trajectories with external interaction.

The complete data generation procedure, including retry logic, per-tier statistics, and discard handling, is detailed in Algorithm 1 in supplementary material. The final dataset contains
{\sim}
40K trajectories: 8.2K direct (Tier 1), 9.8K enhanced (Tier 2), and 23.9K agentic (Tier 3).

The combined supervision teaches strategy selection as an emergent behavior: simple queries are associated with direct trajectories, complex queries with multi-step ones, naturally inducing a difficulty-aware routing policy. At inference, the model’s first generated action implicitly determines the strategy.

3.3
Agent Environments and Trajectory Sources

The agentic trajectories in
\mathcal{D}_{\text{agentic}}
are collected from four agent environments, each contributing trajectories with distinct interaction patterns (
Fig.

2
). These environments serve as
trajectory sources
rather than architectural components of
Meissa
itself. Their diversity ensures that the learned policy generalizes.

Visual information in trajectories.

In tool-calling trajectories, the initial state
s_{0}
contains the input image as
<image>
tokens; observations return structured text (classification labels, bounding boxes, reports). In interleaved image-text trajectories, observations additionally contain
new images
like cropped regions or segmentation overlays injected as additional
<image>
tokens, creating a multi-round visual reasoning chain. Multi-agent environments provide
<image>
tokens for lead agent and each sub-agent at the beginning of the debate. For clinical simulation,
Meissa
can request either structured examination results or images as
<image>
tokens as needed in each call.

Actions select from vision manipulation tools:

Terminate

•

Multi-Agent Collaboration
[
25
]
.

Actions are structured role-based queries:
a_{t}
consists of a difficulty assessment, expert recruitment, expert analysis, multi-round debate contributions, or moderator synthesis. Observations
o_{t+1}
aggregate expert responses (analysis or debate argument). Debate protocols, persona prompts, and synthesis rules are in the supplementary.

•

[
41

.

Actions select from clinical information-gathering tools:
a_{t}\in\{\texttt{RequestPhysicalExam},\allowbreak\texttt{RequestTest},\allowbreak\texttt{Terminate}\}
. The initial state
s_{0}
contains the patient presentation (demographics, history, symptoms). Observations
o_{t+1}
return clinical findings (
e.g.,
“Auscultation: bilateral basal crackles”), lab results (
e.g.,
“Troponin I: 0.4 ng/mL”), or radiology images. The trajectory terminates with
Terminate
for the final diagnosis.

3.4
Prospective-Retrospective Supervision

Learning
when
to act is addressed by stratified trajectories; here we address learning
how
to act effectively within agent environments. The key insight is that a single correct trajectory can be decomposed into two complementary supervision signals that teach different aspects of execution policy.

Prospective (forward) trajectories
are recorded during real-time inference: the teacher reasons forward, potentially revising strategy upon unexpected observations. Only correct trajectories are retained, teaching an
exploration policy
.

Retrospective (backward) trajectories
are generated
given
each prospective trajectory: a recap agent re-narrates the reasoning while preserving the original action sequence, producing a clean post-hoc narrative that teaches an
optimal execution policy
. The combined training set is:

\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}=\mathcal{D}_{\text{direct}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\text{enhanced}}\cup\{\tau^{\text{pro}}(x),\,\tau^{\text{retro}}(x,y)\mid(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{agentic}}\}.

(5)

During auditing, retrospective recaps that are over-simplified or over-complicated are discarded; approximately 8% of generated recaps are filtered. We did not observe significant gains from depth- or difficulty-based weighting in preliminary experiments. A detailed comparison of the two supervision modes and trajectory validation details (structural and behavioral filtering) are in the supplementary.

Meissa
achieves the top result on PathVQA (78.2%; 12.2K training samples) and MIMIC-CXR-VQA (65.2%; 11.4K samples), ranking first among all methods in both cases. Notably, the base Qwen3-VL-4B already shows competitive baselines on these tasks (65.3% and 51.4%), reflecting that data abundance lowers the learning barrier for lightweight models. The gains attributable to
Meissa
thus stem primarily from learned interaction policy rather than bridging a parametric knowledge gap.

Reference-grounded tasks.

On PubMedQA (77.9%, first),
Meissa
outperforms GPT-4V under MDAgents by 2.9 points. PubMedQA supplies a reference abstract alongside each question, shifting the demand from encyclopedic recall to evidence synthesis over provided context. This is precisely the execution skill that prospective-retrospective trajectory supervision optimizes, explaining
Meissa
’s disproportionate gain relative to its performance on knowledge-only variants.

Matching RL-trained models at smaller scale.

Ophiuchus-7B
[
21
]
is trained via cold-start SFT followed by agentic RL, requiring substantially more compute than our SFT-only pipeline. Despite having nearly half the parameters,
Meissa
matches or exceeds Ophiuchus-7B on 3 of 5 Framework II benchmarks (PathVQA: 78.2 vs. 74.3; OmniMedVQA: 82.8 vs. 78.6) while trailing on SLAKE (82.0 vs. 83.9) and VQA-RAD (70.1 vs. 73.6). This demonstrates that stratified SFT with prospective-retrospective supervision can match RL pipelines.

Knowledge and reasoning limits.

Meissa
has clear limitations on tasks requiring deep parametric knowledge or expert clinical reasoning. On MedQA (Framework III), it reaches only 57.2% against GPT-4V’s 88.7%. On MedXpertQA, it achieves 36.0% against Gemini-3-flash’s 69.2%, trailing even Ophiuchus-7B (39.3%). Mild negative transfer also appears on MedQA Ext. (Framework IV,
-3.3
% relative to the base), confirming that agentic trajectory training competes with parametric knowledge retention in lightweight models.

5
Discussion and Conclusion

We presented
Meissa
, a 4B-parameter medical agent trained through agentic behavior distillation. Stratified trajectory supervision teaches
when
to act; prospective-retrospective supervision teaches
how
to act; a unified trajectory formalism enables learning across tool calling, visual reasoning, multi-agent debate, and clinical simulation. Trained on
{\sim}
40K trajectories,
Meissa
is competitive with frontier models across 13 benchmarks while operating fully offline.

Why SFT over RL?
Our framework is intentionally SFT-only. While RL can sharpen stopping behavior via outcome-based rewards
[
32
]
, it faces two practical limitations. First, existing RL approaches are each tied to a single agent framework: DeepEyesV2 optimizes tool-calling via RL
[
17
]
, and MARTI addresses multi-agent coordination
[
54
]
; none can jointly learn policies across the four heterogeneous environments
Meissa
operates in. Second, RL requires extensive online rollouts with live tool environments, incurring high compute cost and training variance that hinder reproducibility. Our stratified SFT achieves near-oracle routing (
Table

3
) with deterministic, compute-efficient training (
{\sim}
12 GPU-hours), and naturally scales across environments through trajectory unification.

Deployment considerations.
Deploying
Meissa
clinically requires human-in-the-loop safeguards: the model currently lacks calibrated uncertainty estimation and an abstention mechanism for out-of-distribution queries. Integrating selective prediction
[
14
]
to enable principled deferral is an important future direction.

Trajectory Validation Pipeline

All generated trajectories undergo a two-stage validation pipeline before inclusion in the training set.

Structural Filtering

Structural filters verify format compliance:

•

Correctness
: Only trajectories arriving at the correct answer are retained (
\text{answer}(\tau)=y
).

•

Turn-type integrity
: Conversations must follow the
human

\to
(
function_call

\to

observation
)
∗

\to

gpt
pattern.

•

Tool call validity
: Each
function_call
must reference a tool defined in the system prompt with valid JSON arguments.

•

Terminate presence
: Every trajectory must end with a termination action (tool-specific:
Terminate
for thinking with image and clinical simulation,
[FINAL]
marker for MedRAX, synthesis turn for MDAgents).

•

Image alignment
: The number of
<image>
tokens must match the number of entries in the
images
list.

•

Length bounds
: Trajectories exceeding 10,000 characters total or containing
>
4 tool calls (MedRAX) /
>
6 rounds (thinking with image) /
>
12 rounds (multi-agent collaboration & clinical simulation) are discarded.

Behavioral Filtering

Behavioral filters detect semantic quality issues:

•

Hallucination detection
: Scan
<think>
blocks for medical keywords (pathology names, anatomical terms) not present in the original question or tool outputs. If a medical term appears in reasoning but not in any input or observation, the trajectory is flagged and reviewed.

•

Repetition loops
: Consecutive duplicate tool calls (same tool, same arguments) are detected and the trajectory is discarded.

•

Truncation detection
: Per-type checks for unclosed markdown formatting, missing terminal punctuation, or incomplete JSON structures indicate truncated API responses.

•

Class balance
: Downsample majority classes (
e.g.,
“yes” answers in binary VQA are kept at 1/3 rate) to prevent trivial shortcut learning.

•

Content length
: Individual turns with
<
10 characters of meaningful content (excluding formatting) are rejected.

•

Thought field presence
: For agentic trajectories, each
function_call
must contain a non-empty
<think>
block articulating reasoning.

Quality audit.
After automated filtering, a random 10% sample of remaining trajectories is reviewed by Claude Opus
[
3
]
for hallucinations, logical inconsistencies, and low-quality reasoning. Trajectories flagged by the audit are manually inspected and discarded if confirmed.

Training data construction.
We first run Qwen3-VL-4B on four medical VQA datasets (PathVQA, SLAKE, VQA-RAD, MIMIC-CXR-VQA) without tools (Tier 1). Samples answered incorrectly are re-processed by Gemini-3-flash with tool access (Tiers 2–3), using both forward and backward prompts. The combined training set contains 15,427 samples. At inference time, the same tool suite is available, with tool outputs (including generated images) injected as
<image>
tokens into the conversation context.

SFT type decomposition.
Each trajectory is decomposed into up to 8 SFT training samples: Type 1/1R (difficulty assessment, prospective/retrospective), Type 2/2R (expert recruitment), Type 3 (individual expert analysis), Type 4 (multi-round debate contributions), and Type 5/5R (final synthesis). Recap types (1R, 2R, 5R) replace online
<think>
blocks with evidence-based hindsight reasoning from the recap agent. We apply the three-tier strategy on MedQA, PubMedQA, PathVQA, and MIMIC-CXR-VQA.

Training data construction.
Data generation follows the stratified pipeline: Qwen3-VL-4B direct inference (Tier 1), Gemini direct inference on errors (Tier 2), and Gemini within the simulation environment on remaining errors (Tier 3). Each simulation dialogue is converted from multi-agent format (doctor, patient, measurement agents) into single-agent tool-calling format for SFT. At evaluation time, the model interacts with the simulation environment for up to 12 turns and must call
Terminate
with its final diagnosis.

Qualitative Analysis: Failure and Recovery Patterns

We present representative examples from
Meissa
’s inference trajectories that illustrate characteristic failure and recovery patterns. These examples are drawn from ChestAgentBench (tool-calling environment) and PathVQA (interleaved image-text environment).

Tool-Calling Environment (MedRAX on ChestAgentBench)

Pattern 1: Over-Invocation of Tools on Simple Questions.

(a)

PA view

(b)

Lateral view

Figure 6
:
Chest X-ray for Patterns 1 and 2
(case 18321). An 18-year-old with bronchiolitis obliterans. Both PA and lateral views show clear lungs with no bronchial wall thickening or pleural thickening – a normal-appearing chest X-ray despite clinical disease.

Example.
A question asks about the chest X-ray shown in
Fig.

6
of an 18-year-old with bronchiolitis obliterans: “Which of the following options best describes the findings?” The correct answer is (B): no bronchial wall thickening or pleural thickening detected.

Meissa
calls 6 tools sequentially:
CheXagentVQA
(“no thickening detected”),
XRayPhraseGrounding
(empty result),
ReportGenerator
(“lungs are clear”),
ChestXRayClassifier
(all probabilities low),
XRayPhraseGrounding
for “fibrosis” (empty result), and
CheXagentVQA
again (“lungs are clear”). Despite all six tools unanimously confirming normal findings, the model’s reasoning hallucinated: “The side view shows a prominent, thickened, and irregular central bronchus” – fabricating findings that every tool denied. The model selected option (D), which was incorrect.

Analysis.
Over-invocation adds context length without information gain. The model’s parametric reasoning overrode consistent tool evidence, suggesting that excessively long contexts degrade the model’s ability to track and synthesize accumulated observations.

Pattern 2: Recursion Limit from Infinite Tool-Calling Loops.

Example.
Using the same chest X-ray as Pattern 1 (
Fig.

6
), a different question asks: “Which of the following is the most likely explanation for the normal appearance of the chest X-rays in this patient?” (correct: bronchiolitis obliterans can present with normal X-rays). The model entered a tool-calling loop, repeatedly invoking tools searching for abnormalities that do not exist. After 25 iterations (the LangGraph recursion limit), the agent was terminated without producing an answer.

Analysis.
The model failed to recognize that the question itself implies the X-ray is normal, and no tool would find abnormalities. This illustrates a failure mode where the model cannot learn to
stop
tool calling when the answer requires reasoning about the absence of findings rather than detecting their presence.
