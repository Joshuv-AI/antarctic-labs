A hybrid approach to complex aortoiliac aneurysms for preserving pelvic perfusion: A technical report
Authors: Rina Okuda, Takao Ohki, Kota Shukuzawa, Hirotsugu Ozawa, Kentaro Kasa, Ryo Nishide, Makiko Omori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a case of juxtarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm with bilateral common iliac artery, and internal iliac artery (IIA) aneurysms treated by hybrid approach to preserve pelvic perfusion. Covered stents were deployed into the IIAs, followed by open surgical repair, where the proximal end of the stent graft in the IIA was inserted into a prosthetic graft using Ginza forceps (DVx), and the prosthetic graft was then anastomosed to the surgical graft. This case highlights a practical hybrid approach for complex aortoiliac aneurysms that preserves bilateral pelvic perfusion and facilitates secure graft-to-stent anastomosis in a deep pelvic field.

When common iliac artery aneurysms (CIAAs) are present with abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs), preservation or reconstruction of at least one internal iliac artery (IIA) is generally recommended to maintain pelvic perfusion.1 However, surgical reconstruction of the IIA is technically challenging owing to its deep location within the pelvis. We report a case of juxtarenal AAA (JRAAA) with bilateral CIAAs and internal iliac artery aneurysms (IIAAs), in which IIA stent grafts were deployed bilaterally before open aortic replacement. Case report

Clinical presentation

An 84-year-old man was admitted for surgical management of a JRAAA, CIAAs, and IIAAs. His past medical history includes thoracic endovascular aortic repair using a semicustom-made fenestrated stent graft (Najuta; SB Kawasumi Laboratories) with left subclavian artery coil embolization, and left common carotid to subclavian artery bypass 5 years before admission. Preoperative computed tomography angiography revealed a 5.8 cm JRAAA, a 2.1 cm right CIAA, a 2.6 cm right IIAA, a 3.2 cm left CIAA, and a 3.2 cm left IIAA (Fig 2). Given that the JRAAA was unsuitable for standard endovascular repair and that there was extensive bilateral iliac involvement, a hybrid repair approach was planned to effectively preserve pelvic perfusion, consisting of open graft replacement for the JRAAA and endovascular reconstruction for the IIAAs.Fig 2A, Three-dimensional reconstruction of the preoperative computed tomography angiogram of the patient's aortoiliac system. Operative procedure

Under general anesthesia, an initial angiogram was obtained. The left IIAA was accessed via the contralateral femoral approach. The anterior division of the left IIA was embolized via microcatheter using an Interlock coil (6 × 200 mm) (Boston Scientific). Subsequently, a VIABAHN stent graft (6 × 100 mm) (W. L. Gore and associates) was deployed from the left superior gluteal artery (SGA) to the left CIA, using a sheath-assisted technique to prevent the bowing phenomenon of the VIABAHN stent graft. Balloon touch-up was performed using a Sterling 6 × 40-mm balloon (Boston Scientific) to ensure full expansion and confirm the absence of endoleak (Fig 3).Fig 3Preoperative angiograms of the right (A) and left (B) internal iliac artery aneurysms (IIAAs), and intraoperative angiograms of the right (C) and left (D) IIAs.

Preoperative angiograms of the right (A) and left (B) internal iliac artery aneurysms (IIAAs), and intraoperative angiograms of the right (C) and left (D) IIAs.

Then, the right CIA was accessed in the same manner. The right inferior gluteal artery was embolized with two Interlock coils (5 × 150 mm), followed by deployment of another 6 × 100-mm VIABAHN stent graft from the right SGA to the right CIA, with balloon touch-up using a Sterling 6 × 40-mm balloon. Completion angiography showed no evidence of endoleak (Fig 3).

Open surgical repair was simultaneously performed via a midline transperitoneal laparotomy following the endovascular procedure. The AAA was exposed along with both renal arteries and CIAs (Fig 4). The inferior mesenteric artery, which was already occluded at its origin, was ligated and divided. An aneurysmotomy was performed with proximal control using an inter-renal aortic clamp and distal control achieved at the proximal portions of both CIAs. Then, the right CIA was opened and the proximal part of the previously deployed IIA stent graft was exposed. The surgical graft was anastomosed to the external iliac artery (EIA) in an end-to-end fashion. A 6-mm Propaten graft (W. L. Gore and Associates) was prepared, and the IIA stent graft was inserted into the distal portion of the Propaten graft using Ginza forceps, with a 1 cm overlap, secured at two points using 6-0 Prolene, and subsequently was anastomosed to the right limb of the bifurcated graft in an end-to-side fashion. The same reconstruction was performed on the left side (Fig 4). Notably, during the anastomosis between the VIABAHN stent graft and the Propaten graft, a Ginza forceps (Fig 5)—an instrument originally developed for superficial vein stripping, featuring a long (45 mm) nonopening shaft that enables remote phlebectomy through a small incision—was used to insert the stent graft into the Propaten graft, facilitating precise handling and needle control in the deep pelvis (Fig 5).Fig 5A, Ginza forceps. Completion angiography confirmed patency of the IIA reconstructions on both sides (Fig 4). The retroperitoneum was closed, followed by layered closure of the abdominal wall.

Postoperative course

The patient's postoperative course was uneventful. No signs of buttock claudication were observed, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 15 without complications. Discussion

IAAs are commonly associated with AAAs, with reported concomitant rates ranging from 10% to 40%.2 Especially in the cases involving bilateral CIAAs or IIAAs, preservation of pelvic perfusion becomes a crucial consideration. Repair of AAAs involving CIAAs and/or IIAAs often requires exclusion of IIA, which can lead to serious pelvic ischemic complications such as buttock claudication, spinal cord ischemia, and colonic ischemia. The previous report has shown that failure to preserve the IIA, especially bilaterally, is associated with increased morbidity and mortality.3

Several approaches have been proposed to preserve pelvic perfusion in complex aortoiliac aneurysm cases involving IIAAs. One well-established, classical method is EIA-IIA bypass using prosthetic grafts in conjunction with endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) for AAA, which allows for preservation of pelvic blood flow and creation of an adequate distal landing zone for EVAR.4 However, when the IIAA is located deep in the pelvis, surgical anastomosis to the distal IIA is technically challenging and sometimes unfeasible. Another commonly discussed option is the use of branched iliac devices, which offer a fully endovascular solution for preserving IIA flow.5 Although their applicability is often limited in cases with concomitant IIAA due to the lack of an adequate distal landing zone, branch vessels of the IIA, such as the SGA, can be used as a distal landing zone.

Hybrid surgical approaches, including endovascular repair for IIAA with stent grafts and subsequent open surgical repair with distal anastomoses to the previously deployed stent grafts, have emerged as useful strategies to address anatomical challenges posed by deep location of the distal IIA in the pelvis.6,7 Given the clinical superiority of open repair over EVAR for complex AAA, the clinical significance of IIA preservation, and the limitations of safe surgical control of the IIAA outflow in the pelvis, especially in a narrow, deep male pelvis, the hybrid approach was considered appropriate for this complex case. Although some centers prefer to stage the endovascular and open components on separate days to reduce operative complexity, we performed both procedures during the same operative session in this case. Simultaneous repair minimizes the total number of anesthetic exposures and eliminates the interval risk between staged procedures, and we believe this approach is preferable whenever the patient's condition permits.

Another important technical consideration in this setting was achieving a secure anastomosis between the previously deployed IIA stent grafts and the bifurcated graft limbs. Direct stent-to-graft anastomosis is sometimes cumbersome due to needle-hole bleeding from the stent graft side because the membrane attached to the stent is too thin to tolerate the needle holes. In the present case, Propaten was used to bridge the VIABAHN stent graft and the surgical graft, providing a stable and hemostatic conduit for anastomosis, instead of direct stent-to-graft anastomosis. This technique is applicable across the full range of available VIABAHN stent graft diameters (5-13 mm), as an appropriately sized Propaten graft can be selected to accommodate each VIABAHN diameter. An overlap of 1 cm was maintained in accordance with the manufacturer's recommended minimum seal zone for the VIABAHN stent graft, and the connection was confirmed to be 100% hemostatic intraoperatively.

Although a 1 cm overlap in a free-floating structure raises structural concerns, using a VIABAHN stent graft mitigates the risk of catastrophic hemorrhage with its robust fixation profile. According to a biomechanical study by Grenville et al,8 the VIABAHN achieved a superior median pullout force of 3.82 N in the internal iliac branch, outperforming other bridging options. This fixation profile provides an adequate safety margin against device migration or hemorrhage within our target parameter of utilizing up to an 8 mm Propaten graft and a 10 mm VIABAHN stent graft. Based on this mechanical stability, two 6-0 Prolene sutures were used as a deliberate trade-off, as each additional needle pass creates a potential bleeding point in the graft material, and two fixation points were judged to provide adequate mechanical security while minimizing this risk. If 1 cm of overlap is not appropriate, we would reattempt loading to extend the overlap to at least 1 cm before proceeding.

Furthermore, during this process, the Ginza forceps were used to connect the Propaten and the IIA stent graft. Ginza forceps are an instrument originally developed for stab avulsion for the treatment of varicose veins and designed to grasp varicose veins through a small remote incision.9 Unlike conventional pistol-grip models, the Ginza forceps feature a straight, coaxial alignment of the handle and tip, which enables the surgeon to grasp vessels with a natural, linear motion that reduces strain on the incision site. Furthermore, the combination of its slender shaft and high-precision serrations ensures a secure grip even during blind procedures, optimizing both maneuverability and surgical outcomes. Therefore, the Ginza forceps are very useful for facilitating insertion of the already deployed stent graft into the Propaten graft, during which performing the EIA anastomosis before the branch reconstruction successfully minimized back-bleeding from the IIA. Although accidental device dislodgement during Ginza forceps manipulation represents a technical concern, gentleness was maintained, with a rescue plan established for immediate redeployment of an additional stent graft under fluoroscopic guidance if needed.

When considering broader applications of this Propaten-bridged branch reconstruction using Ginza forceps, it may serve as a valuable technique for complex vascular reconstructions, such as open visceral artery bypass or open repair of thoracoabdominal aneurysms. It may act as an alternative to the Gore Hybrid Graft (W. L. Gore and associates), which has been discontinued in the United States. However, further accumulation of cases is required to establish the feasibility and applicability of this technique.

Conclusions

This technical report demonstrates a successful use of a hybrid approach for bilateral iliac and AAAs for the prevention of pelvic blood flow, using graft-to-stent anastomoses with a bridging graft to achieve a secure anastomosis and minimize needle hole bleeding from the stent graft.
