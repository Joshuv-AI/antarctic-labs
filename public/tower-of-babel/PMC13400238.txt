Arthroscopic versus open approaches in shoulder surgery: trends in Medicare service utilization and reimbursement
Authors: Luna Toma, Ethan Cossu, Leah Demetri, Dafang Zhang, Philip E Blazar, Brandon E Earp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBoth arthroscopic and open surgical shoulder procedures are effective when nonoperative treatment of shoulder disorders fails. Understanding how utilization and reimbursement for these procedures have evolved is essential for informing health-care policy.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed using Medicare data to extract utilization and reimbursement data for Current Procedural Terminology from 2005 to 2024 regarding arthroscopic and open shoulder surgeries. Arthroscopic procedures included diagnostic shoulder arthroscopy, arthroscopic rotator cuff (RTC) repair, biceps tenodesis, labral repair/capsulorrhaphy, superior labrum anterior-posterior repair, arthroscopy with limited débridement, arthroscopy with extensive débridement, subacromial decompression, and distal clavicle excision. Open procedures included open RTC repair for acute tears, RTC repair for chronic tears, complete RTC avulsion reconstruction, biceps tenodesis, anterior capsulorrhaphy, acromioplasty, and distal clavicle excision. Reimbursement values were adjusted for inflation. Procedure volumes were normalized to Medicare enrollment. Temporal trends in utilization and reimbursement were assessed using the two-tailed Mann–Kendall trend test.ResultsFrom 2005 to 2024, over 9 million shoulder procedures were performed for the selected Current Procedural Terminology codes. Overall normalized utilization demonstrated a significant decline (τ = −0.4, P < .05). Arthroscopic procedures demonstrated no significant utilization trend, whereas open procedures declined significantly (τ = −1.0, P < .001). Many procedures showed a decline in utilization in 2020, coincident with the COVID-19 pandemic. Arthroscopic RTC repair increased significantly by 92% (τ = 0.5, P < .05). All other open RTC repairs declined (τ = −1.0, P < .01). Both arthroscopic and open biceps tenodesis demonstrated significant increases (τ = 0.9, P < .01). Across the study period, open biceps tenodesis demonstrated significant increases in inflation-adjusted reimbursement (P < .01). Still, arthroscopic tenodesis consistently reimbursed at higher rates. From 2005 to 2024, arthroscopic RTC repair consistently had the highest reimbursement per service among all RTC repair procedures. Arthroscopic biceps tenodesis demonstrated higher reimbursement per service than open biceps tenodesis throughout the study period. Similarly, arthroscopic capsulorrhaphy maintained higher reimbursement per service compared with its open counterpart. Open acromioplasty demonstrated higher reimbursement per service than its arthroscopic equivalent, subacromial decompression.ConclusionFrom 2005 to 2024, normalized utilization of shoulder procedures declined overall, driven primarily by a significant reduction in open surgical procedures. Inflation-adjusted Medicare reimbursement for both open and arthroscopic shoulder procedures remained stable. For most procedures, reimbursement per service was higher for arthroscopic procedures.Keywords: Arthroscopic capsulorrhaphy, Biceps tenodesis, Distal clavicle excision, Rotator cuff repair, Shoulder surgery trends, Subacromial decompression

Shoulder pain encompasses a spectrum of pathologies and affects approximately 16% of the global population.16 Rotator cuff (RTC) tears are among the most prevalent shoulder disorders.7,26 Lesions of the long head of the biceps brachii tendon are also common, often accompanying RTC disease.17 Osteolysis and arthritis of the acromioclavicular joint are further sources of shoulder pain.5 When conservative management fails, arthroscopic and open shoulder surgeries have demonstrated comparable clinical outcomes in the literature, with each approach offering certain advantages and disadvantages.1,3,15,19,22 The arthroscopic approach entails smaller incisions with less iatrogenic tissue disruption, which can be associated with faster recovery times, lower wound and infection risks, and improved visualization of the joint.4,6,22,27 At the same time, arthroscopic techniques result in longer operative times and increased charges.8,14

Understanding how these competing advantages of arthroscopic versus open procedures influence their adoption at a population level is critical for informing health-care policy, guiding resource allocation, and advancing the quality of patient care. Prior studies examining surgical trends in shoulder surgery have been confined to narrow time windows, specific anatomical procedures, or restricted clinical settings.2,10,13,23,24,28 The objectives of this study are to (1) characterize the most commonly utilized arthroscopic and open shoulder procedures and evaluate normalized utilization trends from 2005 to 2024 using US Medicare claims data and (2) assess inflation-adjusted Medicare reimbursement trends for these procedures over the same period.

Materials and methods

A retrospective analysis was conducted by extracting treatment utilization and reimbursement data from the publicly available Medicare Part B National Summary Data Files for 15 Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes, describing shoulder procedures, listed in Table I over the study period from 2005 to 2024.29 Shoulder arthroplasty procedures were not included in this study. Medicare is a US health insurance program that provides medical coverage for individuals aged ≥65 years and for younger individuals with qualifying health conditions.

To ensure consistency in the evaluation of financial trends over the entire period, monetary values were adjusted for inflation using the United States Consumer Price Index provided by the US Bureau of Labor Statistics, with 2024 serving as the baseline year.30 To account for potential increases in procedure frequencies attributable solely to growth in the Medicare population, procedural utilization was normalized to annual Medicare enrollment data obtained from publicly available Medicare Enrollment Reports and the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services Statistics Reference Booklets.31,32 Specifically, annual procedure volumes were divided by the corresponding number of Medicare beneficiaries for each year and multiplied by 100,000, yielding utilization rates per 100,000 beneficiaries.

Temporal trends were assessed for overall services and payments as well as for each individual CPT code using the two-tailed Mann–Kendall trend test. Kendall τ coefficient and its associated P values were calculated. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05, and temporal trends were classified as increasing, decreasing, or absent at the 95% confidence level.

This study was exempt from institutional review board approval because it used only publicly available deidentified data.

Results

Service trends

A total of 9,329,363 shoulder procedures were performed for the selected CPT codes between 2005 and 2024. When evaluated without adjustment, total annual service volume demonstrated a positive temporal trend (τ = 0.3, P < .05), increasing from 343,546 services in 2005 to 451,846 services in 2024, representing an increase of 32%. However, when normalized to Medicare enrollment, overall service utilization exhibited a significant negative trend over the study period (τ = −0.4, P < .05), decreasing from 816 to 664 services per 100,000 Medicare beneficiaries, representing a 19% decline.

Utilization of arthroscopic RTC repair [29,827] represented the most frequently performed RTC procedure, increasing by 92% from 2005 to 2024 (τ = 0.5, P < .05, Fig. 1A). Arthroscopic repair accounted for 33% of RTC procedures in 2005 and increased to 90% of RTC procedures by 2024. Across the study period, utilization declined by 80% for acute open repair (23410), 85% for chronic open repair (23412), and 97% for complete RTC avulsion reconstruction (23420; τ = −1.0, P < .01 for all, Fig. 1A). When RTC repair procedures were analyzed collectively, normalized utilization declined significantly (τ = −0.6, P < .01), decreasing from 288 to 202 procedures per 100,000 Medicare beneficiaries, representing a 30% reduction over the study period.

Normalized utilization increased significantly for both arthroscopic biceps tenodesis (29828) and open biceps tenodesis (23430; τ = 0.9, P < .01 for both, Fig. 1B). Until 2019, utilization of open biceps tenodesis exceeded that of the arthroscopic approach; however, from 2020 to 2024, arthroscopic biceps tenodesis (29828) demonstrated higher utilization (Fig. 1B). In 2008, the first year in which data on arthroscopic biceps tenodesis (CPT 29828) were available, arthroscopic procedures accounted for 43% of all biceps tenodeses; by 2024, this proportion had increased to 51%. Utilization of arthroscopic biceps tenodesis increased by approximately 259%, from 17 to 61 procedures per 100,000 Medicare beneficiaries between 2008 and 2024, while open biceps tenodesis increased by approximately 239%, from 18 to 61 procedures per 100,000 Medicare beneficiaries between 2005 and 2024.

Normalized utilization of diagnostic shoulder arthroscopy (29805) declined significantly by 84% from 2005 to 2024 (τ = −0.95, P < .01). Significant declines were also observed for arthroscopic labral repair/capsulorrhaphy (29806), arthroscopic superior labrum anterior-posterior (SLAP) repair (29807), and shoulder arthroscopy with limited débridement (29822; τ = −0.4, −0.9, and −0.6; all P < .05), with utilization decreasing by 66%, 73%, and 57%, respectively. In contrast, normalized utilization of shoulder arthroscopy with extensive débridement (29823) increased significantly over the study period (τ = 0.5, P < .01), rising by 65% and consistently exceeding that of limited débridement. Utilization of open anterior capsulorrhaphy with coracoid process transfer (Latarjet; 23462) showed no significant temporal trend (τ = −0.30, P = .08, Fig. 1C) and remained the least frequently performed procedure among the evaluated CPT codes. Rates were 0.10 per 100,000 Medicare beneficiaries in 2005, 0.19 in 2014, and 0.09 in 2024.

Both arthroscopic subacromial decompression (29826) and open acromioplasty (23130) demonstrated significant declines in normalized utilization (τ = −0.7, P < .01 and τ = −1.0, P < .01, respectively, Fig. 1D). Arthroscopic subacromial decompression utilization decreased by 38% from 211 to 130 procedures per 100,000 Medicare beneficiaries and open acromioplasty decreased by 95% from 19 to 1 procedures per 100,000 Medicare beneficiaries from 2005 to 2024.

Utilization of arthroscopic distal clavicle excision (29824) demonstrated no statistically significant temporal trend (τ = −0.3, P = .06), whereas utilization of open distal clavicle excision (23120) declined by 87% from 69 to 9 procedures per 100,000 Medicare beneficiaries (τ = −1.0, P < .01, Fig. 1E). Arthroscopic distal clavicle excision consistently predominated over the open approach, and when both clavicle procedures were analyzed collectively, normalized utilization declined significantly (τ = −0.7, P < .01).

When all arthroscopic procedures were analyzed as a clustered category from 2005 to 2024, no significant temporal trend was observed in normalized service utilization (τ = −0.1, P = .6, Fig. 2). Among all procedures examined, arthroscopic subacromial decompression (29826) and arthroscopic RTC repair (29827) demonstrated the highest utilization volumes, reaching 130 and 180 services per 100,000 Medicare beneficiaries, respectively, in 2024. When all open surgical procedures were analyzed, normalized service utilization demonstrated a negative trend over time (τ = −1.0, P < .001, Fig. 2). Both clustered arthroscopic and open surgical procedures exhibited a transient decline in 2020 that never fully recovered in subsequent years.

Reimbursement trends

Total unadjusted Medicare payments for all included services showed a strong positive trend (τ = 0.8, P < .01), rising from US $130,766,661 in 2005 to $253,474,075 in 2024. After adjustment for inflation, however, total payments demonstrated no significant trend (τ = 0.3, P = .07), with values of $213,525,285 in 2005, rising to at $300,075,396 in 2014 and declining to $253,474,075 in 2024. Similarly, inflation-adjusted reimbursement per average service showed no significant temporal change and remained between $520 and $620 across the study period (τ = −0.2, P = .4). There was also no significant temporal trend for inflation-adjusted reimbursement per service for clustered arthroscopic procedures (τ = 0.1, P = .5) or open surgical procedures (τ = −0.2, P = .2).

Inflation-adjusted reimbursement per service increased significantly for open biceps tenodesis (23430; τ = 0.7, P < .01, Fig. 3A), diagnostic shoulder arthroscopy (29805; τ = 0.4, P < .05) and shoulder arthroscopy with limited débridement (29822; τ = 0.4, P < .05).

A significant decline in inflation-adjusted reimbursement per service was observed for complete RTC avulsion reconstruction (23420; τ = −0.8, P < .01, Fig. 3B). Both arthroscopic subacromial decompression (29826) and open acromioplasty (23130) experienced significant decreases in reimbursement (τ = −0.8, P < .01 and τ = −0.5, P < .01, respectively), with open acromioplasty maintaining higher reimbursement levels throughout the study period (Fig. 3C).

No significant temporal trend in inflation-adjusted reimbursement per service was observed for arthroscopic RTC repair (29827; τ = −0.02, P = .9), acute open RTC repair (23410; τ = −0.1, P = .5), or chronic open RTC repair (23412; τ = −0.3, P = .07) (Fig. 3B). When all RTC repair procedures were analyzed collectively, no significant change in reimbursement was observed (τ = −0.2, P = .2). From 2009 to 2024, arthroscopic RTC repair consistently demonstrated higher reimbursement than other RTC repair procedures, which exhibited comparable reimbursement levels among themselves (Fig. 3B).

No significant reimbursement trends were observed for arthroscopic labral repair/capsulorrhaphy (29806; τ = 0.3, P = .1), arthroscopic SLAP repair (29807; τ = −0.01, P = .9), or open anterior capsulorrhaphy (23462; τ = −0.30, P = .08, Fig. 3D). The highest reimbursement per service among all procedures was observed for arthroscopic labral repair/capsulorrhaphy (29806), arthroscopic RTC repair (29827), and arthroscopic SLAP repair (29807). Shoulder arthroscopy with extensive débridement (29823) showed no significant change in reimbursement over time (τ = −0.2, P = .2). From 2005 to 2016, it demonstrated higher reimbursement per service than limited débridement; however, from 2017 to 2024, this relationship reversed.

Arthroscopic biceps tenodesis (29828) demonstrated no significant temporal trend (τ = 0.07, P = .7), although it consistently maintained higher reimbursement levels than the open approach throughout the study period. No significant trend was observed for arthroscopic distal clavicle excision (29824; τ = −0.1, P = .6) or open distal clavicle excision (23120; τ = −0.3, P = .08) (Fig. 3E). From 2010 to 2022, arthroscopic excision demonstrated higher reimbursement; in 2023 and 2024, reimbursement for the open approach again slightly exceeded that of the arthroscopic procedure. When open and arthroscopic distal clavicle excision procedures were analyzed collectively, a significant decline in inflation-adjusted reimbursement was observed (τ = −0.6, P < .01).

Discussion

This study evaluated national trends in utilization and reimbursement for arthroscopic and open shoulder procedures over a 20-year period. The findings demonstrate a significant decline in open shoulder surgeries, while utilization of arthroscopic procedures remained stable, suggesting that arthroscopic techniques have not simply replaced declining open procedures. This pattern may reflect a broader shift toward nonoperative management, evolving clinical guidelines, or more selective surgical indications driven by advances in imaging, rehabilitation protocols, and patient selection.

Consistent with our results, Donovan et al10 reported no significant change in arthroscopic shoulder procedure volumes within the Medicare population from 2013 to 2022, although their analysis was confined to the ambulatory surgical setting. Procedure-specific differences were observed both in our study and in the analysis by Donovan et al10 Shoulder arthroscopy with RTC repair exhibited the greatest increase in utilization in both Donovan et al10's study and our findings. Donovan et al10 reported a 36% increase between 2013 and 2022, whereas our analysis demonstrated a substantially larger 92% increase from 2005 to 2024, likely reflecting that our study period is twice as long.

Yanik et al28 reported an increase in RTC repair techniques within the commercially insured US population from 2007 to 2016, particularly among patients aged 50–64 years. In contrast, our analysis identified an overall decline in overall arthroscopic RTC procedures. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in study periods as well as the populations examined, as the Medicare cohort predominantly comprises individuals aged ≥65 years. Still, Yanik et al28 observed a deceleration in the rate of increase after 2010, which may be concordant with the trends observed in our analysis. Despite differences in overall volume, the proportion of arthroscopic RTC repairs in our cohort increased substantially from 33% in 2005 to 90% in 2024, consistent with Yanik et al,28 who reported that arthroscopic techniques comprised 60% of repairs in 2007 and rose to 83% by 2016.

Our study demonstrated a significant increase in normalized utilization for both arthroscopic and open biceps tenodesis, with a greater relative increase observed for the arthroscopic approach. In 2008, arthroscopic techniques comprised 43% of all biceps tenodeses and increased to 51% by 2024. Conversely, Siddiq et al24 reported decreasing utilization of arthroscopic biceps tenodesis and increasing utilization of open biceps tenodesis, with 42% of procedures performed arthroscopically in 2021. In our analysis, open biceps tenodesis was utilized slightly more frequently than the arthroscopic approach through 2019; however, from 2020 to 2024, arthroscopic biceps tenodesis became the more commonly used technique. The consistently similar utilization of arthroscopic and open techniques over time might account for the differing conclusions observed when comparing studies across different time periods. Saltzman et al23 identified variability in the preferred surgical approach depending on whether the tenodesis was performed in isolation or in conjunction with RTC repair or SLAP repair/débridement. This may explain the discrepancy between our findings and those reported by Siddiq et al24 and highlights an important limitation of our study population, as the dataset does not permit assessment of combined or concurrent procedures. Additionally, CPT codes do not capture lesion severity or patient comorbidity burden, limiting the ability to associate specific surgical approaches with underlying pathology or patient characteristics.

Ridley et al21 evaluated clinical outcomes in patients undergoing SLAP treatment and found that SLAP lesions treated with biceps tenodesis demonstrated better outcomes compared to isolated SLAP repair. Consistent with this, we observed a significant decrease in arthroscopic SLAP repairs accompanied by rising biceps tenodesis. In their retrospective study using the National Ambulatory Surgery Sample database from 2016 to 2018, Hong et al13 also reported declining SLAP repair utilization with increasing biceps tenodesis.

In both Donovan et al10's study and our findings, arthroscopy with limited débridement showed a significant decline in utilization. However, Donovan et al10 reported no significant trend for shoulder arthroscopy with extensive débridement, whereas our analysis identified a significant increase in utilization for this procedure. Alluri et al2 reported an increase in utilization of arthroscopic distal clavicle excision and a concomitant decline in open excision between 2004 and 2009. Our analysis demonstrated no significant temporal trend for arthroscopic distal clavicle excision, while utilization of the open approach declined as well. Similarly, Donovan et al,10 who examined a more recent period from 2013 to 2022, also reported no significant temporal trend in arthroscopic distal clavicle excision, aligning with our findings. Furthermore, our results indicate a reduction in the utilization of open acromioplasty over time. This trend aligns with evidence suggesting that routine acromioplasty performed alongside RTC repair does not lead to improved clinical outcomes.18

Across all analyzed procedures, a marked decline in utilization was observed in 2020, consistent with the suspension of elective surgical care during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, for several procedures, utilization did not return to pre-pandemic levels in subsequent years. This lack of recovery may reflect a combination of factors, including changes in patient health status or preferences, and increased reliance on nonoperative management strategies. However, because Medicare claims data do not capture the volume of clinical presentations for shoulder pathology or the use of nonoperative treatments, these explanations cannot be confirmed within the scope of this analysis. This exogenous shock likely increased year-to-year variability in utilization and may have attenuated the detection of underlying temporal trends for certain procedures.

As Medicare is the largest insurer in the United States, its policies affect the landscape of the performed procedures.33 Across the analyzed shoulder procedures, inflation-adjusted reimbursement per service demonstrated no significant temporal change, with no differences observed for either clustered arthroscopic or open procedures. These findings contrast with previously published trends in shoulder procedures reported across orthopedics and other surgical specialties.11,12,20,25 Compared to the analysis of trend in reimbursement by Donovan et al,10 we have also found that reimbursement for arthroscopic distal clavicle excision and arthroscopic RTC repair demonstrated no significant temporal change. For procedures like limited or extensive débridement, our analysis showed trends contrary to Donovan et al10 These differences in findings are likely attributable to variations in study periods as well as Donovan et al10's restriction to procedures performed in ambulatory surgery centers.

This study is limited by its exclusive use of Medicare data. Therefore, the trends observed in this study may not represent those in other populations or with other payors. Still, Medicare data often mirrors broader health care trends.9 Additionally, the data's accuracy depends on accurate hospital and clinician coding.

Conclusion

From 2005 to 2024, normalized utilization of shoulder procedures declined overall, driven primarily by a significant reduction in open surgical procedures. Utilization of arthroscopic shoulder procedures remained stable, reflecting a shift toward less invasive techniques. In addition, changes in practice appear to be reflected in the decrease in distal clavicle excision, subacromial decompression, and capsulorrhaphy and an increase in biceps tenodesis surgery. Despite these changes, inflation-adjusted Medicare reimbursement for both open and arthroscopic shoulder procedures remained stable. For most procedures, reimbursement per service was higher for arthroscopic procedures. Awareness of these patterns is essential for health-care professionals to optimize resource allocation and anticipate the impact of evolving surgical practice on access to care.
