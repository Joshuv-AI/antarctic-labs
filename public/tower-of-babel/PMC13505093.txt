Hypoxia/lactate orchestrates the reprogramming metabolism of lung adenocarcinoma by modulating the SIRT2-mediated acetylation
Authors: Jie Tao, Zhan-yi Chen, Jing-na Xun, Meixiang Wang, Juan-xi Liu, Ying Zhang, Douglas B. Lowrie, Yao Li, Xiao-yong Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSilent information regulator 2 (SIRT2) has been described to have dual roles as an oncogene and tumor suppressor gene in non-small cell lung cancer, but its functional selection and the precise mechanisms of its action remain to be elucidated.Methodslung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) tissue microarrays and cell lines were used to detect SIRT2 expression using Immunohistochemistry and Western blotting. SIRT2 overexpression, knockdown, and knockout LUAD cells were constructed to test SIRT2 function in vitro and in vivo, and targeted metabolomics, proteomics, and acetylation analyses were performed to explore the detailed mechanisms by which SIRT2 affects the biological characteristics of LUAD.ResultsSIRT2 is not only downregulated in LUAD tissues and cells, but also associated with poor prognosis in LUAD patients, not in lung squamous cell carcinoma. We also found that hypoxia-induced increasement in lactate levels led to this decreased SIRT2 expression. Remarkably, SIRT2 deficiency promotes the proliferation and metastasis of LUAD cells in vitro and in vivo, while SIRT2 overexpression exerts the opposite function. Mechanistically, SIRT2 deficiency leaded to increased acetylation levels of multiple enzymes involved in glycolysis, tricarboxylic acid cycle, fatty acid oxidation, and glutaminolysis. And results of co-inmunoprecipitation showed that SIRT2 bound to the above enzymes. Accordingly, SIRT2-deficient LUAD cells showed altered metabolic activities such as glycolysis, pentose phosphate pathway, TCA cycle, serine metabolism, and urea cycle. Notably, SIRT2 deletion increases glycolytic capacity of LUAD cells by increasing the acetylation modification of LDHA at the K118 site.ConclusionThis study reveals that hypoxia-induced lactate elevation decreases SIRT2 expression to promote LUAD progression, with the mechanism related to promoting glycolysis by increasing LDHA acetylation. In addition, decreased SIRT2 expression has also been found to cause alterations in many metabolic pathways with no verification mechanism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08481-8.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the malignant tumor with the highest morbidity and mortality worldwide and China [1, 2]. Although the comprehensive treatment of lung cancer has made great progress in recent years, the mortality rate of lung cancer remains high due to the lack of understanding of the mechanism of lung cancer occurrence and development [3, 4]. Altered metabolism is a hallmark of cancer, and energy metabolism reprogramming has been shown to be a common phenomenon in tumors [5, 6]. Specifically, tumor cells often undergo metabolic reprogramming to meet the needs of survival, proliferation, and metastasis in adverse environments, which promoted cancer progression [7, 8]. Hypoxia, a common feature of all solid tumors, is one of the main triggers for metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells [9, 10]. In a hypoxic environment, tumor cells convert glucose to lactate through the lactic acid fermentation pathway [11, 12], and the elevated levels of lactate metabolites create an immunosuppressive environment conducive to cancer cell proliferation and immune evasion [13, 14]. However, the molecular mechanism of lactate accumulation caused by hypoxia in regulating the progression of lung cancer has not been fully elucidated.

Acetylation of proteins is a key post-translational modification in cellular metabolic pathways, as key enzymes in almost all metabolic pathways undergo acetylation [15]. Silent information regulator (Sirtuins) is a class of NAD-dependent deacetylases, containing 7 members in mammalian cells (SIRT1-7) [16]. SIRT1, SIRT6 and SIRT7 are localized in the nucleus and has a strong deacetylase function, responsible for histone deacetylation modification. The function of SIRT3, SIRT4 and SIRT5 located in mitochondria are mainly the regulation of oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production in response to calorie restriction. SIRT2, a mitotic checkpoint protein with strong deacetylase activity, shuttles between the nucleus and cytoplasm [17, 18]. In lung cancer, SIRT2 exhibits a paradoxical dual function, acting as an oncogene and a tumor suppressor gene [19], suggesting that the role of SIRT2 in lung cancer progression is context-dependent.

Here, we reported that intracellular lactate accumulation caused by hypoxia down-regulated SIRT2 expression, and low expression of SIRT2 was associated with poor prognosis of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) patients, but not in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC). Mechanistically, SIRT2 deficiency promotes LUAD progression and enhances the acetylation modification levels and activities of key enzymes in many metabolic pathways, thereby causing an increase in aerobic glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation. In conclusion, SIRT2 reprograms metabolism and promotes LUAD progression through its deacetylase function.

Materials and methods

Online database analysis

TCGA datasets (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), TIMER online database (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/), Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA2) (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index), Kapla-Meier Plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis/) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) online database were used to analyze the expression of SIRT2 mRNA in normal tissues and tumor tissues. And The Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) was used to analyzed the expression of SIRT2 mRNA in human bronchial epithelial cells and LUAD cells.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Human LUAD and normal tissue microarray were purchased from Shanghai YEPCOME Biotechnology Co., Ltd (https://www.yepcome.com/index.php?m=home&c=View&a=index&aid=1179), and paraffin-embedded sections were obtained from A549 nude mouse transplanted tumor tissue. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed according to our previously developed protocol [20].

Western blotting analysis

As described previously [20], fifty micrograms of protein extract from cells were separated by electrophoresis in 10% SDS-PAGE gel, and then being transferred into a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane, blocked, probed with a primary antibody and a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody, and visualize protein bands. Details of the antibodies used were shown in Table S1.

Expression regulation of SIRT2 in LUAD cells

Lentivirus containing human SIRT2 overexpression vector (LV-CMV (660 bp)-sirt2-P2A-EGFP-PGK-Puro-WPRE) or human SIRT2 knockdown vector (Plko.1-sirt2-EGFP-Puro) were obtained from BrainVTA (Wuhan) Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). 72 h after LUAD cells infection by lentivirus, cells were screened using medium supplemented with 2 µg/mL of puromycin dihydrochloride (cat. # ST551, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 10 days. In addition, the SIRT2 knockout (KO) A549 cells (cat. # YKO-H170) were purchased from Guangzhou Yuanjing Biotechnology Co., Ltd (https://www.ubigene.com/) (Guangzhou, China).

Cell proliferation analysis

In vitro, we assess cell proliferation by staining with cell count kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent (cat. #40203es76, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) and 5-ethynyl-2ˊ-deoxyuridine (EdU) (cat. #40276ES60, Yeasen, Shanghai, China), as well as cell clone formation tests [21]. In vivo, we constructed a nude mouse xenograft model by subcutaneously planting A549 cells in nude mice [21], and a mouse carcinoma in situ model by injecting LA795-luc cells into the left lung of C57BL/6 mice [22, 23].

Cell metastasis analysis

In vitro, wound preparation by sterile pipette tips was used to assess cell migration [24], and Transwell chambers (cat. #140629, ThermoFisher, Massachusetts, USA) were used to assess cell invasion [21]. In vivo, we constructed an in vivo LUAD metastasis model by tail vein injection of LA795-GFP cells and evaluated the metastatic ability of LUAD cells by analyzing LA795-GFP in lung tissues using HE staining [25].

Immunoprecipitation

For immunoprecipitation (IP) assays, we used a lysis buffer for WB/IP assays (cat. #KL-D1159, Shanghai kang lang biological technology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) to lyse WT and SIRT2 KO A549 cells. And then SIRT2 antibody (1:30, cat. #ab211033, abcam, Cambridge, UK) or acetyl-lysine antibody (1:20, cat. #PTM-102, Hangzhou Jingjie Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Hangzhou, China) was added to the cell lysis overnight at 37℃, and the corresponding IgG was used as an isotype control. After addition of protein A/G agarose-beads (cat. #SC-2003, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc., Texas, USA), samples were incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. Beads were washed three times with IP lysis buffer, added with 2× SDS loading buffer and boiled for 10 min.

Proteomic analysis and acetylation proteomics analysis

Proteomic and acetylation proteomics analysis were performed at Hangzhou Jingjie Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China) using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). False discovery rate (FDR) of protein, peptide and PSM was adjusted to < 1%.

Identification of SIRT2-targeted proteins

To identify SIRT2-targeted proteins, we prepared the mixture of SIRT2 protein and SIRT2 connexin as in the IP protocol, and separated with 10% SDS-PAGE. These samples were then analyzed by LC-MS/MS at Hangzhou Jingjie Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China) same as Proteomic and acetylation proteomics analysis, with the difference being the method of protein extraction.

Targeted metabolomic analysis

Targeted metabolomic analysis was performed at Shanghai Zhongke New Life Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) using LC-MS/MS. FDR of metabolites was adjusted to < 1%.

Kit-based assay for enzyme activity

According to the protocols of Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), we used fructose-1, 6-bisphosphate aldolase (cat. #BC2270), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (cat. #BC2210), pyruvate kinase (PKM) (cat. #BC0540), pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) (cat. #BC0380) and citrate synthase (CS) (cat. #BC1060) activity detection kits to detect the activities of the corresponding enzymes.

Energetic assay using XFe-24 extracellular flux analyzer

Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was measured in wild-type (WT) and SIRT2 knockout (KO) A549 cells using the Seahorse XF Analyzer (XFe24 Anzlyzer, Agilent Technologies, California, USA) as our previous study [20].

Immunofluorescence analysis

About 105 cells / 100 µl were seeded into a well of Lab-Tek chambered coverglass (cat. #155361, ThermoFisher, Massachusetts, USA) for culturing 24 h at 37℃ with 5% CO2. After washing the cells with PBS, 150 µmol/L of MitoTracker Deep Red FM (cat. # M22426, Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA) in medium was added to the cells and incubated for half an hour at 37℃. And then 5µg/ml DAPI buffer was used to stain the nuclei for 5 minutes at room temperature. At last, a Leica TCS SP5 microscope (Leica, Hesse, Germany) was used to analyze the fluorescence.

Statistical analysis

Graphpad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, Santiago, USA) was used to data analysis, and data was showed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Paired t-test was used to compare the data differences between the tumor group and the adjacent normal group, and Student’s t-test was used to compare the data between the other two groups, and one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction post hoc test was used to compare the data between the multiple groups. Univariate cox regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with LUAD survival with the data from TCGA database, and Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to compare the survival differences among different groups of LUAD or LUSC patients. P < 0.05 indicated significant difference.

Results

SIRT2 is down regulated in tumor tissues and cells of LUAD patients

First of all, we analyzed the expression pattern of sirtuins family genes in LUAD patients by analyzing RNA-seq data from LUAD patients in the TCGA database. The results indicated that compared to normal tissues, SIRT1 was the only the member of sirtuins family genes that showed no significant change in expression in tumor tissues of LUAD patients, while SIRT2 was the only member with reduced expression in tumor tissues of LUAD patients (Fig. 1A), and SIRT3, SIRT4, SIRT5, SIRT6 and SIRT7 were all elevated expression in tumor tissues of LUAD patients (Figure S1A). Subsequently, logistic regression analysis was used to analyze the relationship between sirtuins family genes and the survival of LUAD patients, and found that the transcriptome expression levels of SIRT2 and SIRT4 were related to the survival of LUAD patients (P < 0.05, Figure S1B). To further investigate the relationship between SIRT2 or SIRT4 mRNA expression levels and LUAD survival, we grouped 507 patients based on the median or mean of SIRT2/SIRT4 mRNA expression in tumor tissues of LUAD patients, and the result of Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that regardless of whether they were grouped by median or mean expression, the survival time of the low SIRT2 expression group was all significantly lower than that of the high SIRT2 expression group, while there was no significantly different between the low SIRT4 expression group and the high SIRT4 expression group (Figure S1C and Figure S1D).

To further investigate the expression pattern of SIRT2 in LUAD, we also conducted bioinformatics analyses using the TIMER, and the results also showed that the expression of SIRT2 mRNA in tumor tissues was strongly lower than that in normal tissues of LUAD patients (P < 0.001, Fig. 1B). Additionally, data from the HPA database also showed that SIRT2 was expressed at a higher level of transcriptome expression in human bronchial epithelial cells (HBEC3-KT) than in LUAD cell lines (H1299, NCI-H157, NCI-1792, H1650, etc.) (Fig. 1C). To validate these public database-based findings, we performed immunohistochemical assays on LUAD tissue microarrays containing 80 normal tissues and 80 tumor tissues using SIRT2-specific antibody. Compared to normal tissues, SIRT2 protein exhibited decreased expression in tumor tissues (Fig. 1D). Similarly, western blot analysis also showed that the expression of SIRT2 protein in human normal lung epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) was lower than that in LUAD cells (H1299 and A549) (Fig. 1E).

Based on data from TCGA database, we also analyzed the correlation between SIRT2 transcriptome expression levels and clinical features of LUAD patients (n = 522), and found that SIRT2 transcriptome expression levels were significantly related to the TNM stage of LUAD patients (P < 0.005, Fig. 1F). In addition, data from single-cell sequencing (GSE152607) in a mouse model of spontaneously induced LUAD, SIRT2 expression decreased gradually during LUAD evolution (Fig. 1G). According to TNM stage, 80 LUAD patients with LUAD tissue microarray were grouped, and the expression level of SIRT2 protein in LUAD patients with different TNM stages was compared. With the increase of TNM stage in LUAD patients, the expression level of SIRT2 protein decreased gradually (Fig. 1H and I).

Decreased expression of SIRT2 is related to poor prognosis in LUAD patients

To determine the correlation between SIRT2 expression and survival in LUAD patients, we performed a serial analysis using data from public databases, such as TIMER database, GEPIA2 database, Kaplan-Meier Plotter database and GEO database. All analyses from public databases showed that the survival rate of LUAD patients with low SIRT2 expression was significantly lower than that of LUAD patients with high SIRT2 expression, suggesting that SIRT2 behaved as a tumor suppressor gene in LUAD patients (Fig. 2). To comprehensively analyze the role of SIRT2 in NSCLC patients, we also used public database data to analyze the expression pattern of SIRT2 in LUSC patients and its relationship with the survival of LUSC patients. Data from TCGA database indicated that there was no significant difference between normal tissues and tumor tissues in LUSC patients, and was not associated with survival in LUSC patients (Figure S2A). Similarly, the same results were also found in the TIMER database (Figure S2B). Further, our analysis in the GEPIA2 database (Figure S2C), Kaplan-Meier Plotter database (Figure S2D) and GEO database (Figure S2E-H) also showed that the expression of SIRT2 was not associated with the survival of LUSC patients, suggesting that SIRT2 was not a tumor suppressor gene in LUSC, which was different from LUAD.

Hypoxia / lactate decreases SIRT2 expression

Hypoxia is a common feature of all solid tumors and is one of the inducing factors for tumor-associated genetic alterations [9, 10]. To investigate the cause of the reduced expression of SIRT2 in LUAD patients, we treated LUAD cells with hypoxia (0.5% O2, 94.5% N2 and 5% CO2). And we found that the expression level of SIRT2 in LUAD cells gradually increased with the extension of hypoxic treatment time in the first 24 h of hypoxic culture, while SIRT2 expression in LUAD cells gradually decreased as the hypoxic treatment time continued (> 24 h) (Fig. 3A and Figure S3A). The above results suggest that hypoxia cannot completely determine the expression of SIRT2 in LUAD cells. Given that we observed a color change in the cell culture medium during hypoxic culture (Fig. 3B), and the conversion of glucose to lactate by the lactic acid fermentation pathway by tumor cells under hypoxic condition [9, 12]. We examined the lactic acid content in the culture medium and cells, and found that the lactic acid level in the culture medium gradually increased with the extension of hypoxia treatment time (Fig. 3C), while intracellular lactic acid levels did not increase significantly until 24 h of hypoxic culture (Fig. 3D). Subsequently, in order to explore the function of lactate, we added sodium lactate to the medium during hypoxic culture, and found that SIRT2 expression in LUAD cells increased after hypoxic culture for 24 h, but the addition of lactate decreased SIRT2 expression (Fig. 3E and Figure S3B). Furthermore, lactate also down-regulated the expression of SIRT2 in LUAD cells in a time-dependent manner in the normoxic environment (Fig. 3F and Figure S3C). To clarify that intracellular lactate accumulation is necessary for the downregulation of SIRT2 expression, we added an LDHA inhibitor (an enzyme inhibitor that inhibits cancer cells from converting pyruvate to lactate) to the hypoxic culture system. And we found that hypoxia and LDHA inhibitor (LDHA-IN-8) cultured for 48 h reduced the content of lactic acid in the cells (Fig. 3G) and increased SIRT2 protein expression (Fig. 3H).

SIRT2 deficiency promotes the proliferation of LUAD cells

To investigate the effect of SIRT2 expression patterns on LUAD progression, we first constructed human LUAD cell lines (A549 and H1299) or mouse LUAD cell lines (LA795-GFP and LA795-luc) with SIRT2 knockdown and overexpression by lentiviral infection and antibiotic screening, and finally confirmed them using western blotting (Figure S4). Subsequently, we assessed the effect of SIRT2 expression levels on the proliferation of human LUAD cells in vitro by measuring cell viability. Results of CCK-8 assay suggested that SIRT2 knockdown significantly increased the cell viability of human LUAD cells (Fig. 4A). And EdU staining showed that SIRT2 knockdown strongly increased the ratio of Edu positive cells in A549 and H1299 cells (Fig. 4B). Consistently, cell clone formation test results also showed that the LUAD cell line with SIRT2 knockdown formed a higher number of cell clones (Fig. 4C). In vivo, we planted the same number of A549 cells in the skin of nude mice and allowed them to grow freely for 3 weeks, and found that the quality of xenograft tumors with SIRT2 knockdown A549 was greater (Fig. 4D). In addition, we planted the same number of mice LUAD cells (LA795-luc) on C57BL/6 mouse lung tissue, and also found that the tumors formed by SIRT2 knockdown LA795-luc cells were larger (Fig. 4E). Mechanistically, we measured the expression of Ki-67, a proliferation-related protein in A549 xenograft tumors, by IHC, and the results showed that SIRT2 knockdown increased Ki-67 expression (Fig. 4F). As with the above assays, we also tested the effect of overexpression of SIRT2 on the proliferation of LUAD cells and found the opposite result (Figure S5). The above results revealed the effect of SIRT2 on the proliferation of LUAD cells, that was, SIRT2 deletion promoted the proliferation of LUAD cells in vivo and in vitro.

SIRT2 deficiency promotes the metastasis of LUAD cells

Metastasis is one of the most prominent features of cancer cells and one of the leading causes of death in cancer patients. Herein, we also evaluated the effect of SIRT2 expression levels on the metastasis of LUAD cells. Results of wound healing tests showed that SIRT2 knockdown strongly promoted the migration of LUAD cells (Fig. 5A), and SIRT2 overexpression played an opposite role (Figure S6A). In the transwell analysis, we observed that SIRT2 knockdown increased the number of LUAD cells invaded into the lower compartment (Fig. 5B), while overexpression of SIRT2 decreased the number of invaded cells (Figure S6B). Western blotting analysis showed that SIRT2 knockdown decreased the expression of E-cadherin protein and promoted the expression of N-cadherin protein in A549 and H1299 cells (Fig. 5C), while SIRT2 overexpression exerts the opposite effect (Figure S6C).

In vivo, we injected LA795-GFP cells into the tail vein to construct a LUAD mouse metastasis model in C57BL/6, and analyzed the proportion of LA795-GFP cells in lung tissue by flow cytometry and the HE staining results to evaluate the effect of SIRT2 expression on in vivo metastasis of LA795-GFP cells (Fig. 5D). Overall, these results suggested an important role for SIRT2 in regulating the in vitro and in vivo metastasis of LUAD cells.

SIRT2 knockout pointes to metabolic alterations by omics analysis

To further explore the potential mechanism of SIRT2 regulating the proliferation and metastasis of LUAD cells, mass spectrometry was used to analyze the expression of protein molecules in A549 cells caused by SIRT2 knockout (KO). A total of 8642 consistent protein molecules (Figure S7A and Table S2), and 89 consistent presence/absence expression protein molecules (Figure S7B), were identified by mass spectrometry in A549 cells with wild-type (WT) and SIRT2 KO. Further analysis revealed that there were 302 differentially expressed protein (KO vs. WT) (Fig. 6A), and their distribution ranged from cytoplasm (n = 125), nucleus (n = 178), mitochondria (n = 33), etc. (Figure S7C). Pathway enrichment analysis was performed on these 302 differentially expressed proteins using KEGG database, and metabolism is the most enriched subcategory among the seven subcategories of KEGG (Fig. 6B), a total of 14 metabolic pathways, such as biosynthesis of cofactors, metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450, drug metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis and so on (Fig. 6C). Subsequently, we performed pathway enrichment analysis on the differentially expressed proteins by GSEA in KEGG database, and found that many the metabolic pathways caused by SIRT2 knockout were changed, including nitrogen metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, porphyrin and chlorophyll metabolism, ether lipid metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, etc. (Fig. 6D). Similarly, results of Domain pathways enrichment (Figure S7D), biological process of Go database (Figure S7E) and Reactom pathway enrichment (Figure S7F) also suggested that that metabolic alterations were the most common changes in A549 cells caused by SIRT2 knockout.

A total of 3061 acetylation sites were identified for a total of 1769 proteins (Fig. 7A, Table S3 and Figure S8A-B), which were localized to the cytoplasm, nucleus, and mitochondria, etc. (Figure S8C). SIRT2 knockout enhances acetylation modification of metabolic enzymes

To determine which protein molecules SIRT2 acts directly on, we used SIRT2-specific antibody to enrich SIRT2 protein and proteins linked to SIRT2 protein in A549 cell lysate. These protein mixtures were separated by SDS-PAGE follow by being identified using mass spectrometry (Figure S9A and Table S4). A total of 1203 proteins were identified as directly linked to SIRT2, and A total of 1,203 proteins were identified to be directly linked to SIRT2, of which 539 proteins were identified to have significant changes in acetylation levels by acetylomics (Figure S9B). At the same time, these protein molecules were enriched in metabolic pathways such as pyruvate metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and TCA cycle (Figure S9C). Subsequently, in order to verify the results of the omics study, we selected enzymes that play a key role in glycolysis, fatty acid metabolism, pyruvate metabolism and TCA cycle from the above 539 protein molecules for Co-IP verification (Fig. 8A and B). Confirmedly, among the 10 glycolytic enzymes, 5 were identified as Sirt2 interacting proteins, including aldolase, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), enolase, pyruvate kinase M (PKM) and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA). Additionally, among the 8 TCA-cycle enzymes, citrate synthase (CS), malate dehydrogenase (MDH), isocitrate dehydrogenase 3B (IDH3B) and succinate dehydrogenase subunit alpha (SDHA) were also identified as binding SIRT2. Hydroxyacyl-CoA Dehydrogenase Trifunctional Multienzyme Complex Subunit Alpha and Beta (HADHA and HADHB), two key enzymes in fatty acid oxidation, were also identified as binding SIRT2.

SIRT2 knockout causes metabolic reprogramming

Given that SIRT2 knockout causes changes in acetylation levels and enzyme activities of multiple metabolic enzymes, we used targeted metabolomics and assay kits to evaluate whether SIRT2 knockout causes metabolic changes. Metabolites found to be significantly altered by SIRT2 knockout were mainly located in glycolysis, pentose phosphate pathway, TCA cycle, serine metabolism, and urea cycle (Fig. 9A-B and Table S5). In addition, changes in intracellular ATP and ATP/ADP ratios indicated that SIRT2 knockout led to increased cellular energy production, manifested as high energy metabolic activity (Fig. 9C). Concomitantly, SIRT2 knockout showed a significant decrease in glucose and a significant increase in glucose-6-phosphate levels, consistent with increased glucose catabolism (Fig. 9B). However, SIRT2 deficiency did not affect cellular glucose uptake and expression of glucose transporter (GULT1) (Fig. 9D), nor did it affect the number of mitochondria (Fig. 9E), suggesting that SIRT2 deficiency may cause changes in mitochondrial function.

SIRT2 knockout promotes glycolysis by promoting LDHA acetylation at the K118 site

Enhanced glycolysis is the main metabolic reprogramming feature of cancer cells. Moreover, the large amounts of energy and biosynthetic materials needed for the rapid proliferation of cancer cells also mainly come from glycolysis. Therefore, we set the focus of the present study on glycolysis. Results of seahorse analysis in A549 cells showed that SIRT2 deficiency significantly increased the glycolysis capacity of A549 cells, including basal glycolysis, glycolytic capacity and glycolytic reserve (Fig. 10A-B). At the same time, the increased glycolytic capacity caused by SIRT2 deficiency appears to be the source of several other metabolic changes, such as increased production of serine, which uses glycolytic intermediates as raw materials for synthesis, and TCA cycle, which uses acetyl-CoA from pyruvate as an intermediate metabolite (Fig. 9B). In addition, we also detected an increase in extracellular lactate levels, confirming that SIRT2 deficiency induced an increase in aerobic glycolysis (Fig. 9B).

LDHA is a key enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to lactic acid after glycolysis. To ascertain whether SIRT2 acts to modify acetylation at the K118 site of LDHA, we co-transfected Flag-LDHA (K118R) plus Myc-SIRT2, Flag-LDHA (K118R) minus Myc-SIRT2 into SIRT2 KO A549 cells. This suggests that KAT7 acts at LDHA K118 site. Discussion

Clinicopathologically, lung cancer is divided into LUAD, LUSC, large cell carcinoma, and small cell carcinoma, and LUAD, LUSC, and large cell carcinoma are collectively referred to as NSCLC. Quantitatively, LUAD, LUSC, large cell carcinoma, and small cell carcinoma account for 40%, 25–30%, 10–15%, and 15% of the total number of lung cancer patients, respectively. Therefore, we first analyzed the expression of Sirtuins family genes in LUAD, and found that SIRT2 was not only lowly expressed in LUAD tissues, but also associated with the overall survival of LUAD patients. However, SIRT2 was not found to be abnormally expressed in LUSC tissues, and SIRT2 expression was not associated with the survival of LUSC patients. LUAD is the most common type of lung cancer among non-smokers, especially among Asian populations, and it is also a type of lung cancer prone to genetic mutations, so it is the main group that benefits from targeted therapy [26, 27]. However, unlike LUAD, the occurrence of LUSC is most closely related to smoking, and it is rarely found to be caused by driver gene mutations [27, 28]. Therefore, SIRT2 may only be a driving gene that leads to the occurrence and development of LUAD.

In NSCLC, SIRT2 has been found to play dual roles, such as Grbesa I, et al. found that high expression of SIRT2 predicted shorter recurrence-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) time in NSCLC patients [29], and the consistent conclusion with the study by Gao CX, et al. [30]. On the contrary, more studies have shown that SIRT2 expression is reduced in NLSCL and plays the role of an anti-cancer gene [31–35]. Li Z, et al. found that SIRT2 was significantly down-regulated at both the mRNA and protein levels in tumor than non-tumor tissues or cells, and overexpression of SIRT2 inhibited the proliferation, induced apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in NSCLC cells [31]. Mechanistically, SIRT2 functions as a tumor suppressor gene in NSCLC and is related to its mediated deacetylation of AKR1C1 [32], ACLY [33], RALB [34] and SKP2 [31]. Taken together, these previous studies and our results suggest that the role of SIRT2 in NSCLC is related to specific contexts, and pathological classification is one of these specific contexts. Therefore, we chose to explore the specific function of SIRT2 in LUAD. In the present study, not only did the data from public databases indicated that SIRT2 mRNA expression was reduced in LUAD tumor tissues, but the IHC results of LUAD tissue microarrays also suggested that SIRT2 protein expression was reduced in LUAD tumor tissues. In cell lines, the expression of SIRT2 mRNA in human normal cells was lower than that in LUAD cells, and this result was also confirmed by protein expression detected by western blot analysis. Importantly, decreased expression of SIRT2 was associated with higher clinical stage and shorter survival time of LUAD patients, which was consisted with the study of Zhang G, et al. [36]. These findings collectively indicated that SIRT2 acted as a tumor suppressor gene in LUAD patients.

Subsequently, we explored the triggers for the decrease in SIRT2 expression in LUAD, and found that hypoxia-induced intracellular lactate accumulation regulates SIRT2 expression in LUAD cells. Hypoxia is a common feature of all solid tumors and is one of the main causes of altered gene expression in cancer cells [9, 10]. However, our study also found that sodium lactate could reduce the expression of SIRT2 in LUAD cells even under normoxic conditions, suggesting that the accumulation of intracellular lactate to a certain level can lead to a decrease in SIRT2 expression. It has been shown that lactate secreted by tumors regulates the expression of cancer cell genes by regulating the NAD+/NADH ratio [37]. SIRT2, a NAD-dependent histone deacetylase, has enzymatic activity that is closely related to the NAD+/NADH ratio [38, 39]. Therefore, combined with our findings, the expression of SIRT2 in LUAD cells may also be related to the lactate/NAD axis, but further research is still needed.

In terms of biological characteristics, our results indicated that SIRT2 deficiency promoted the proliferation, invasion and migration of LUAD cells in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, we explored the possible mechanism by which SIRT2 promotes the progression of LUAD through proteomics and acetylomics. And results suggested that metabolic alterations are at the core of SIRT2-deficiency-induced changes in LUAD cells, especially glucose metabolism. As the main energy supply for cell growth, glucose metabolism includes glycolysis, pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), TCA cycle and serine biosynthesis pathway [40]. Glycolysis is the central pathway of glucose metabolism and the intermediate metabolite pyruvate can be converted into lactic acid to secrete outside the cell, or it can be converted into citrate and enter the TCA cycle [41, 42]. Here, we found that SIRT2 acted on many key enzymes in glucose metabolism, such as aldolase, GAPDH, PKM, enodolase and LDHA in glycosis, G6PD in PPP, and CS, MDH, SDHA and IDH3B in TCA cycle. Importantly, SIRT2 deficiency leaded to increased acetylation levels and enzymatic activities of these key enzymes. SIRT2 is a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)-dependent deacetylase that participates in the regulation of various biological functions by deacetylation of different intracellular substrates. In the above description, we have pointed out that SIRT2 promotes the progression of NSCLC by increasing the acetylation levels of AKR1C1 [32], ACLY [33], RALB [34] and SKP2 [31].

Back to glucose metabolism, previous study has found that SIRT2 regulated many key enzymatic activities through its function of acetylation in tumor-reactive T cells, including hexokinase 1 (HK1), phosphofructokinase P (PFKP), aldolase, GAPDH, phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1), enolase, PKM and LDH in glycolysis, and SDHA, aconitase 2 (ACO2), oxoglutarate dehydrogenase (OGDH), succinate-CoA ligase alpha subunit (SUCLG1) in TCA cycle [43]. In cancer cells, Loss of SIRT2 function in cancer cells reprograms their glycolytic metabolism by deacetylation of PKM2 [44], and SIRT2 regulated cellular metabolism and inhibited colorectal cancer liver metastasis by modulating IDH1 deacetylation [45]. To our best efforts, our study is currently the only study that comprehensively elucidates the mechanism of SIRT2 regulating glucose metabolism in LUAD cells. Historically, the function of SIRT2 in cancer cells has been contradictory [17, 18, 46], which may be the reason why few studies have comprehensively elucidated the role of SIRT2 in specific tumors. In the tumor microenvironment, SIRT2 secreted into the extracellular space promotes lung cancer metastasis by catalyzing the deacetylation of extracellular proteins [47], which is a specific manifestation of the contradictory role of SIRT2 in cancer.

Furthermore, the results of targeted metabolomics also confirmed the regulatory effect of SIRT2 on glycolysis and TCA cycle in LUAD cells. However, it is noteworthy that glucose uptake and GLUT1 expression are not affected by SIRT2 expression, indicating that SIRT2 only regulates glucose catabolism in LUAD cells. At the same time, although SIRT2 deficiency increased ATP production, it did not affect the number of mitochondria in LUAD cells, suggesting that SIRT2 deficiency enhanced glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation levels. In addition, increased levels of serine, a newly synthesized product from the glycolytic intermediate 3-phosphoglycerate [48, 49], support nucleotide synthesis in proliferating LUAD cells.

Additionally, this study also found that SIRT2 regulated fatty acid metabolism, glutaminolysis, and the urea cycle. Although fatty acid metabolism has received less attention than glucose metabolism, elevated lipid synthesis is another important aberration required for carcinogenesis [50, 51]. In this study, we validated that SIRT2 deficiency significantly reduced the acetylation modification of two key enzymes of the FAO pathway, namely, HADHA and HADHB. Moreover, acetylomics and the proteomics of SIRT2-linked mixtures also suggest that SIRT2 acts on the deacetylation of two key enzymes for lipid synthesis, ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY) and Fatty acid synthase (FASN). ACLY and FASN have been shown to be highly expressed in multiple cancers and promote cancer progression [52–54]. Importantly, ACLY is a substrate of SIRT2, and SIRT2 inhibits cancer progression by deacetylating ACLY [33, 55]. Glutamine is considered second only to glucose in importance as a nutrient in cancer. Glutamine catabolism begins with the production of glutamate catalyzed by glutaminase (GLS), which can then be oxidized to α-ketoglutarate by glutamate dehydrogenase (GLUD1) and enter the TCA cycle [56, 57]. Interestingly, our findings revealed that SIRT2 deacetylated GLUD1. For urea cycle, we only found changes in metabolites in the pathway, and did not find changes in any enzymes in the pathway, suggested that changes in these metabolites might be indirectly caused by changes in other metabolic pathways.

Conclusion

In summary, our results indicate that SIRT2 expression is decreased in LUAD and promotes the progression of LUAD. In addition, our results present two new findings for the first time. One is that the increase in lactate levels caused by hypoxia reduces SIRT2 expression, and the other is that we also provide the first panoramic view of the metabolic changes in LUAD cells caused by SIRT2 deficiency, with a focus on increased glycolysis by promoting LDHA acetylation at the K118 site. However, it must be pointed out that although we observed changes in key metabolites and protein acetylation modifications in the TCA cycle, pentose phosphate pathway, urea cycle, and serine metabolism pathway caused by decreased SIRT2 expression, direct evidence is still lacking and the conclusion is also correlational.
