Awareness and perception of the traditional methods of stroke management among stroke survivors in selected health facilities and traditional centers: a mixed method study
Authors: Uchenna Prosper Okonkwo, Daniel Onyebuchukwu Osuya, Ebere Yvonne Ihegihu, Uchechukwu Martha Chukwuemeka, Ifeoma Adaigwe Amaechi, Uzoamaka Nwakego Akobundu, Nnaemeka Charles Ani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional methods of stroke management are increasingly used in sub-Saharan Africa and encompass a wide range of practices.ObjectivesTo determine the level of awareness and perception of the traditional methods of stroke management among stroke survivors in Anambra State.MethodsThis study was a mixed-method embedded design (a research methodology combining qualitative and quantitative approaches in a single research) involving 106 stroke survivors recruited from selected health facilities and traditional stroke management centers in Anambra State. Data was summarized using descriptive statistics of mean, frequency, and standard deviation. The relationship between the sociodemographic variables and the study variables was analyzed using the Chi-square test. A structured questionnaire was used to assess stroke survivors’ awareness and perception of traditional stroke management, with statistical significance set at α = 0.05Thematic analysis was used to analyze qualitative data.ResultsOf the participants, 51 (48.1%) had moderate knowledge, while 85 (80.2%) demonstrated a positive perception of traditional methods of stroke management. There was a statistically significant association between awareness of traditional methods of stroke management and gender (X2 = 7.9, df = 2, p = .019). Thematic analysis shows an overall high awareness and perception of the traditional method of management. Conclusion: The participants have high awareness, positive perception and preference for traditional method of stroke management. Gender appears to influence participants’ awareness and perception of traditional stroke management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-026-05433-z.Keywords: Awareness, Perception, Stroke survivors, Traditional Management, mixed-study, Anambra State

Introduction

Stroke, or cerebrovascular accident (CVA), is defined as the interruption of blood to the brain due to blockage or rupture of a blood vessel, neurological deficit [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), stroke is a rapidly developing clinical sign of focal or global cerebral dysfunction lasting more than 24 h or leading to death, with no apparent cause other than vascular origin [2].

The incidence of stroke in Africa is estimated at 316 per 100,000 population annually, while in Nigeria, prevalence is approximately 1,460 per 100,000 person-years [3, 4]. Stroke management in Sub-Saharan Africa remains largely conservative, constrained by limited access to advanced rehabilitation services and chronic underfunding of high-quality stroke research within the region [5, 6]. Primary prevention is key to reducing the burden of disease in a country with such poor resources. However, in Anambra State, Nigeria the prevalence of stroke is estimated at 1.63 per 1,000 per annum [7].

Traditional medicine (TM) has been used for centuries in stroke care, particularly in China and Korea, where TM is integrated into the healthcare system [8]. In contrast, TM in Nigeria operates largely outside the formal health system and lacks regulation. Nigerian practices include bloodletting, application of hot coals or water, massages, herbal medications, and spiritual utterances. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines traditional medicine as “health practices, approaches, knowledge and beliefs incorporating plant, animal, and mineral-based medicines, spiritual therapies, manual techniques, and exercises, applied singularly or in combination to treat, diagnose, and prevent illnesses or maintain well-being” [9].

Given the persistence of traditional practices and limited integration into formal stroke care, understanding stroke survivors’ awareness and perceptions of these methods is crucial [10, 11]. This gap in knowledge led to the research question: What is the level of awareness and perception of traditional stroke management methods among stroke survivors in Anambra State, Nigeria? The mixed-method design was chosen to enable deeper insights from participants’ personal experiences that a purely cross-sectional design would not capture.

Research methodology

This study was a mixed-method design utilizing both cross-sectional and in-depth Interview (IDI) methods for quantitative and qualitative studies respectively. One hundred and six (106) stroke survivors were recruited consecutively from selected health facilities and traditional stroke management centers in Anambra State. For the qualitative component, 5 participants were randomly selected from the total sample of 106 using SPSS-generated random IDs. The IDs were matched to the corresponding participants who were contacted and recruited for qualitative study. A semi-structured self-administered questionnaire on awareness and perception of traditional methods of stroke management among stroke survivors (see attached supplementary material) was used to collect quantitative data, while an interview protocol was used to collect qualitative data. The 36-item questionnaire consisted of three sections: socio demographic details (7 items), awareness of traditional stroke management methods (15 items), and perceptions toward these methods (14 items). The questionnaire underwent face and content validation (CVI; 0.70–0.77) by three research experts. After their review, 15 questions were approved for the ‘awareness’ section while 14 were certified for the ‘perception’ section of the questionnaire. It was also pilot-tested with stroke survivors recruited from a population in a health facility in Nnewi different from the selected population of the study. After they had gone through the questionnaire items using the criteria of clarity and understanding, relevance to research objectives, ambiguity, respondent burden, data quality and response rate, their feedback such as confusing or unclear, redundant questions, were taken and necessary adjustments such as rewording of questions, changing question format and reordering questions, were made to ensure no ambiguities remained and questionnaire items were relevant. The Reliability test for the questionnaire was done with SPSS reliability testing and the Cronbach alpha was 0.924, and the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was 0.924 The interview questions were developed by the authors, validated by a three-man research expert and was piloted on a separate sample different from the main study participants.

Validation of the interview guide

The interview guide was subjected to expert validation to ensure its clarity, relevance, and adequacy in addressing the study objectives. Face and content validity were established prior to data collection.

Face validity was assessed by a panel of three experts comprising one physiotherapist with experience in stroke rehabilitation, one public health researcher, and one qualitative research expert. Each expert independently reviewed the interview guide for clarity, appropriateness of language, cultural relevance, and alignment with the study objectives. Using a dichotomous rating scale (acceptable = 1, not acceptable = 0), items that achieved ≥ 80% agreement among the experts were considered to have satisfactory face validity. Items that fell below this threshold were revised based on the experts’ feedback.

Content validity was determined using the Content Validity Index (CVI). Experts rated each interview question on a 4-point relevance scale (1 = not relevant, 2 = somewhat relevant, 3 = quite relevant, 4 = highly relevant). The Item-Level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) was calculated as the proportion of experts who rated each item as either 3 or 4. Items with an I-CVI of ≥ 0.80 were retained. The Scale-Level Content Validity Index (S-CVI/Ave) was computed by averaging the I-CVI values across all items, yielding an overall CVI of > 0.80, which is considered acceptable for qualitative research instruments.

Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis. Interview recordings were transcribed verbatim and reviewed repeatedly to ensure familiarization with the data. Initial codes were generated inductively and grouped into categories based on conceptual similarity, from which overarching themes were developed. Theme validation was achieved through intercoder agreement, whereby two independent researchers coded the transcripts separately and resolved discrepancies through consensus, and triangulation, in which emergent qualitative themes were compared with quantitative findings to identify convergence, divergence, and complementarity. In addition, frequency and intensity of responses were considered to determine the prominence of themes, and peer debriefing with experienced researchers was conducted to enhance the credibility and consistency of the final thematic structure.

Scoring of the questionnaire

Awareness of the traditional methods of stroke management

Awareness scores ranged from low (1), moderate (2), to high (3) after collapsing five initial categories into three for analytical simplicity. Perception scores were rated as disagree (1), neutral (2), and agree (3), with higher scores indicating more positive perceptions.

Study participants

The inclusion criteria for the study participants include stroke survivors who were 18 years and above, not cognitively impaired, (this we ensured by confirming they know their current time, their current location, and their names, ages, and identity), and have stayed up to three months’ post-stroke. The exclusion included stroke survivors with cognitive impairment, and speech impairment (for qualitative data).

Sample size

A sample size of 106 had a 90% power to detect a moderate change of 0.3 at an alpha level of significance of 0.05. The sample size was calculated using G Power 3.0 [12].

Data collection

Data collection for this study adopted a concurrent mixed-methods approach, in which quantitative and qualitative data were collected simultaneously but independently, and later integrated through triangulation. The study was conducted among stroke survivors attending selected tertiary health facilities and traditional stroke management centres in Anambra State, South-East Nigeria.

Following ethical approval from the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Health Sciences and Technology, Nnamdi Azikiwe University, Awka, informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. The consent form was provided in English, while explanations in the Igbo language were given orally where necessary. Ethical principles including confidentiality, voluntary participation, and participants’ autonomy were strictly observed throughout the study.

Quantitative data were collected using a self-administered structured questionnaire, which was distributed to eligible stroke survivors during designated out-patient clinic days over a 10-week period. Two research assistants were recruited and trained over three days (approximately two hours per day) on the objectives of the study, ethical conduct, and administration of the research instruments. Completion of the questionnaire took approximately 5–10 min. For participants who could not understand English, the questionnaire was translated into Igbo using a forward–backward translation technique by a certified linguist. Discrepancies in translation were resolved through consensus among the linguist, investigators, and research experts. The translated Igbo version was administered orally when needed. Completed questionnaires were retrieved immediately and checked for completeness before being entered into Microsoft Excel for quantitative analysis.

Qualitative data were collected concurrently through in-depth interview (IDI) with a purposively selected subsample of five participants drawn from the quantitative respondents. Five participants were purposively selected for in-depth interviews to allow for rich, detailed exploration of perceptions and experiences while maintaining feasibility within the study scope. This sample size is consistent with qualitative research recommendations indicating that data saturation in homogeneous populations can be achieved with as few as 4–6 interviews, particularly when the study aims are narrowly focused [13, 14]. The interview protocol was designed to explore participants lived experiences and perceptions of stroke and traditional stroke management. It consisted of six thematic areas: stroke experience; traditional stroke experience; traditional practices; comparison of stroke management methods; cultural and belief influences; and recommendations and future considerations. Written informed consent for interview participation was obtained, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any stage without consequences.

Each interview session lasted approximately 15–20 min. A research assistant fluent in the local language assisted with interpretation when required. Interviews were audio-recorded using an Android phone with participants’ consent and timed using a stopwatch. In cases where participants declined audio recording, responses were documented in written short notes. Audio recordings were listened to repeatedly and transcribed verbatim. The transcripts were analyzed using thematic analysis, which involved familiarization with the data, initial coding, categorization of codes, and development and labeling of themes. Theme validation was achieved through examination of pattern frequency, intensity, and relational consistency.

Data collection for both quantitative and qualitative components was carried out three times per week over a six-week period, based on participants’ availability. Integration of findings was achieved through concurrent data integration, whereby qualitative themes were compared and aligned with quantitative results to identify areas of convergence, divergence, and complementarity, thereby strengthening the validity and interpretive depth of the study.

Analysis of data

Data was computed using the IBM Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 26. Descriptive statistics of mean, standard deviation, frequency count, and percentage were used to summarize the participants’ variables. The chi-square test was used to check for the association between the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants and the study variables. The level of significance was set at p = .05. Thematic analysis was used for qualitative data.

Summary of key quantitative findings and statistical evidence

Qualitative findings

Six major themes emerged from the in-depth interviews with stroke survivors.

Theme 1: Stroke experience

Participants described stroke onset as sudden and unexpected, marked by abrupt loss of motor control, facial deviation, and, in some cases, loss of consciousness. Initial health-seeking behavior involved hospital presentation for diagnosis and acute care, largely due to uncertainty regarding the cause of symptoms.

Theme 2: Traditional stroke experienced

Participants reported positive experiences with traditional stroke management, often accessed following hospital visits. Family recommendations and perceived symptom improvement were key motivators for continued use of traditional care.

Theme 3: Traditional stroke practices

Reported traditional practices included the use of herbal preparations, body incisions with razor blades, hot water herbal applications, and massage. Participants perceived these practices as effective, citing observable improvements in mobility and general wellbeing.

Theme 4: Comparison of stroke management methods

Most participants expressed a preference for traditional over orthodox medical treatment, describing traditional methods as more effective and safer. Some participants viewed hospital-based interventions, such as injections and intravenous drips, as ineffective for stroke recovery.

Theme 5: Cultural and belief influence

Participants generally reported that cultural, religious, and spiritual beliefs did not significantly influence their treatment choices. Decisions regarding stroke management were primarily guided by perceived treatment effectiveness rather than belief systems.

Theme 6: Recommendations and future consideration

Participants recommended traditional stroke management to other stroke survivors, with many believing that traditional methods result in faster and better recovery outcomes than medical treatment alone.

Qualitative findings

Theme 1: Stroke experience

Participants described the onset of stroke as sudden and unexpected, frequently occurring during routine activities. Initial responses involved hospital presentation for diagnosis and acute management. However, following confirmation of stroke, many participants subsequently sought care at traditional healing centres.

Quote:

“I was taken to the hospital initially… the following day I visited the traditional center.” (Participant 1).

Theme 2: Traditional stroke experienced

Participants generally reported positive experiences with traditional stroke management. Access to traditional care was often facilitated by family members, and continued engagement was influenced by perceived symptom improvement. Traditional methods were used either exclusively or in combination with medical treatment.

Quote:

“The treatment was good for me.” (Participant 1).

Theme 3: Traditional stroke practices

Participants reported the use of herbal preparations, body incisions using razor blades, hot water herbal applications, and massage. These practices were perceived as effective, with participants reporting improvements in physical function and wellbeing.

Quote:

“After some days I started getting better.” (Participant 3).

Theme 4: Comparison of medical and traditional stroke management

Most participants expressed a preference for traditional methods over orthodox medical care, which they perceived as more effective and safer. Some participants reported dissatisfaction with hospital-based treatments, particularly injections and intravenous therapies.

Illustrative Quote:

“Traditional is better.” (Participant 2).

Theme 5: Cultural and belief influence

Participants consistently reported that cultural, religious, and spiritual beliefs did not influence their treatment decisions. Instead, decisions were guided by perceived treatment effectiveness. This finding is notable given the religious homogeneity of the sample, with nearly all participants identifying as Christians.

Quote:

“My culture, belief, and religion do not affect my treatment.” (Participant 3).

Theme 6: Recommendations to other Stroke Survivors

Participants strongly recommended traditional stroke management to other stroke survivors, believing it promoted faster and more complete recovery. These recommendations were based on personal experiences rather than clinical evaluation.

Quote:

“I’ll advise them to seek traditional methods of stroke management.” (Participant 1).

Reflexive and interpretive note

Participants’ accounts reflect subjective experiences and perceptions. These narratives do not constitute empirical evidence of clinical efficacy or safety of traditional stroke management practices.

Integration of findings (mixed-methods triangulation)

A convergent mixed-methods design was used, allowing quantitative and qualitative findings to be compared for convergence, complementarity, and divergence.

Convergence

Quantitative findings showed that nearly half of participants had high awareness of traditional stroke management, while the qualitative data demonstrated clear familiarity with traditional practices and widespread utilization. Similarly, the high proportion of participants with high perception quantitatively aligned with qualitative narratives describing perceived recovery and satisfaction with traditional methods.

Complementarity

Qualitative findings provided contextual depth to quantitative patterns. While quantitative results indicated no significant associations between perception and most sociodemographic variables, qualitative accounts revealed that perceived effectiveness and personal recovery experiences, rather than demographic or belief factors, primarily guided treatment choices.

Sequential care pathway

Although many participants initially sought hospital care, qualitative findings showed a transition from medical to traditional management, complementing the high levels of awareness and perception observed quantitatively. This sequential pathway highlights how traditional methods functioned as either an alternative or adjunct to orthodox care.

Absence of divergence

No major contradictions were identified between quantitative and qualitative findings. Both strands consistently indicated positive perceptions and strong endorsement of traditional stroke management.

Methodological consideration

The lack of observed influence of religion on awareness and perception quantitatively aligns with qualitative findings. However, the religious homogeneity of the sample (99.1% Christian) may have limited the detection of variability and should be considered when interpreting these results.

Discussion

This study explored the awareness, perception, and lived experiences of traditional medicine (TM) for stroke management among stroke survivors in Anambra State, Nigeria, using a mixed-methods approach. The discussion is organized according to the study objectives to improve clarity and interpretability.

Sociodemographic patterns

The study population was predominantly male and older than 60 years, reflecting the well-documented association between advancing age and increased stroke risk [15–22]. Male predominance among stroke survivors has been attributed to greater exposure to modifiable risk factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and dietary habits [23]. Educational attainment was generally low; a factor previously linked to poorer health literacy and increased vulnerability to stroke [24]. Most participants were married and identified as Christians, consistent with the demographic characteristics of the study area.

Awareness of traditional medicine for stroke management

The findings revealed a high level of awareness of TM for stroke management among participants, with information largely obtained through family members, friends, and prior users of traditional healing centres. This aligns with previous studies showing that informal social networks are key sources of information on TM use in low- and middle-income settings [14, 25, 26].

A statistically significant association was found between gender and awareness of TM (χ² = 7.9, p = .019). While this study did not directly assess explanatory mechanisms, previous literature suggests that gender differences in health information access, caregiving roles, and health-seeking behaviour may influence awareness of TM [27]. This interpretation should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. Other sociodemographic variables, including age, education, marital status, religion, post-stroke duration, and side affected, showed no significant associations with awareness, suggesting that knowledge of TM transcends demographic categories in this population.

Perception and treatment preferences

Quantitative findings demonstrated a predominantly high perception of TM for stroke management, with over 80% of participants expressing favourable views. This is consistent with previous studies reporting positive perceptions of TM among stroke survivors, often linked to perceived symptom relief, natural composition, and cultural familiarity [28].

Qualitative findings provided deeper insight into treatment preferences. Although many participants initially sought hospital care following stroke onset, a substantial number later transitioned to TM, indicating a sequential or complementary care-seeking pattern. While quantitative data indicated high perception overall, qualitative narratives suggested that perceptions were experience-dependent and sometimes cautious, particularly among participants who experienced dissatisfaction or complications. This helps explain why perception may appear uniformly high quantitatively but more nuanced qualitatively.

Cultural influences on treatment choices

Interestingly, both quantitative and qualitative findings indicated that culture and religion had limited direct influence on participants’ perception of TM. Instead, treatment decisions were largely driven by personal experience and perceived effectiveness. This does not negate the role of culture in shaping treatment-seeking behaviour, but rather suggests that culture may function as a background influence, with experiential outcomes exerting stronger immediate effects.

Given that 99.1% of participants were Christians, religious homogeneity may have limited the ability to detect meaningful differences. This nuanced interpretation reconciles the apparent contradiction between the acknowledged cultural relevance of TM and the limited influence reported by participants.

Comparison with other studies and contexts

The traditional practices reported in this study—such as herbal remedies, razor blade incisions, and hot water applications—differ from those documented in studies from China, Thailand, and Malaysia, where TM is more formally integrated into national health systems and regulated frameworks [24–26, 28–30]. In contrast, TM practice in Nigeria remains largely informal and unregulated, which may explain differences in methods, perceived effectiveness, and safety profiles. There are however, concern that some of treatment methods may lead to complications, infections, or delayed hospital care.

Despite contextual differences, the general preference for TM among stroke survivors observed in this study is consistent with findings from other regions, suggesting a shared reliance on culturally familiar and accessible care options. However, variations in practice highlight the importance of contextualizing TM use within local health systems.

Implications and recommendations

The frequent use of invasive TM practices, such as skin incisions and hot water applications, raises important safety concerns, including risks of infection, tissue injury, and delayed access to biomedical care. These risks have been documented in previous studies reporting complications such as ecchymosis and adverse reactions [28–30]. While participants perceived these methods as beneficial, it is essential to emphasize that such perceptions do not equate to scientific evidence of efficacy or safety.

These findings highlight the need for public health education, regulatory oversight, and collaborative engagement between biomedical practitioners and traditional healers. Integrating basic safety training and referral pathways could help minimize harm while respecting cultural practices. Further clinical research is also needed to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of commonly used TM practices in stroke management.

Summary of discussion

This study demonstrated high awareness and predominantly positive perception of traditional medicine for stroke management among stroke survivors in Anambra State. Gender was significantly associated with awareness but not perception. Qualitative findings revealed strong treatment preferences for TM, driven more by lived experiences than by cultural or religious beliefs. While TM remains a central component of stroke care in this context, the potential risks associated with certain practices underscore the need for cautious interpretation, improved regulation, and culturally sensitive health policy interventions.

Limitations

This study had several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample size was relatively small (n = 106), which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the results. The sample size was constrained by the cross-sectional study design, time limitations, and challenges in recruiting stroke survivors who met the inclusion criteria and were willing to participate, particularly for the qualitative interviews.

Second, the use of a non-random sampling technique may have introduced selection bias, as participants who were more accessible or more favourably disposed toward traditional medicine may have been more likely to participate. This could have influenced the observed high levels of awareness and perception of traditional medicine.

Third, the study relied primarily on self-reported data, which are subject to self-report and recall bias. Participants may have overstated positive experiences or underreported adverse outcomes associated with traditional medicine use, potentially affecting the validity of the findings.

Fourth, although educational level was recorded, it was not analytically explored as a determinant of awareness and perception. Educational background may influence health literacy, understanding of stroke pathology, interpretation of symptoms, and critical appraisal of treatment options, and its limited consideration represents an important methodological constraint.

Finally, the study focused on perceptions and experiences rather than objective clinical outcomes. Consequently, it did not assess the efficacy, safety, or comparative effectiveness of traditional stroke management practices. Future studies should integrate objective clinical indicators alongside perception-based measures to enhance validity.

Recommendations

To improve stroke care and guide evidence-based policy, the following recommendations are proposed, organized by short-, medium-, and long-term priorities.

Short-term priorities

Health education and awareness programmes should be strengthened to provide stroke survivors and caregivers with accurate information on safe treatment options, early warning signs of complications, and the importance of timely referral to health facilities. These programmes should actively involve traditional practitioners and community leaders to enhance cultural relevance and acceptance.

Medium-term priorities

Future research should include large-scale, multi-site observational studies to improve representativeness and generalizability. Studies assessing the knowledge and practices of traditional healers, particularly their understanding of stroke pathophysiology and referral decision-making, are also recommended. Community-based surveys and focus group discussions could further explore public awareness and perceptions of traditional medicine use in stroke management.

Long-term priorities

Rigorous comparative and interventional studies, including controlled trials where feasible, should be conducted to evaluate the benefits, risks, and outcomes of traditional medicine relative to conventional stroke care. Such comparisons are essential for evidence-based policymaking, as they can inform clinical guidelines, regulatory frameworks, and decisions on whether and how traditional medicine may be safely integrated into formal health systems.

Across all stages, collaborative engagement between biomedical professionals, traditional healers, and policymakers is strongly recommended to promote a holistic, culturally sensitive, and patient-centred approach to stroke management.

Contribution to knowledge

This study provides important insights into the awareness, perception, and lived experiences of traditional stroke management among stroke survivors in Anambra State, South Eastern Nigeria. By employing a mixed-methods approach, the study adds contextual depth to existing literature and highlights the continued relevance of traditional medicine in stroke care within this setting.

The findings demonstrate that stroke survivors reported high awareness, predominantly positive perception, and strong preference for traditional medicine, underscoring the need for policy attention and further empirical investigation. Importantly, the study does not establish the clinical efficacy or safety of traditional stroke management practices; rather, it identifies knowledge gaps and priority areas for future research, including the mechanisms, administration processes, and potential risks associated with commonly used traditional interventions.

From a policy perspective, the findings can inform strategies to integrate traditional practitioners into stroke awareness and prevention, strengthen referral pathways between traditional and biomedical care, and guide the development of regulatory and educational frameworks aimed at improving patient safety. More broadly, this study offers a structured methodological reference that may support similar investigations in comparable sociocultural contexts, contributing to a more balanced and evidence-informed discourse on traditional medicine in stroke management.
