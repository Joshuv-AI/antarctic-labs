ETAS reduces wake time after sleep onset in healthy adults who are dissatisfied with their sleep: A randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind crossover study
Authors: Manami Misu, Kazunori Goto, Jun Takanari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPoor sleep quality is strongly associated with health indicators. Specifically, it increases the risk of developing diseases, including coronary artery disease and type 2 diabetes. Our preliminary study suggested that ETAS, a standardized extract of Asparagus officinalis stem, may enhance the proportion of N3, a stage of deep sleep. In this study, we conducted an expanded, randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind crossover study to investigate the effect of ETAS on N3 appearance. The study included 38 healthy adult participants with sleep dissatisfaction. Intervention periods involved administration of either placebo or ETAS (300 mg/day as ETAS50) for 14 days, with a 14-day washout period between periods. The primary endpoints were the proportion of N3 sleep after the intervention, as determined by electroencephalogram (EEG) analysis. Secondary endpoints included sleep parameters indices other than the proportion of N3 as determined by EEG analysis after the intervention, as well as OSA-MA and AIS-J scores. 14-day ETAS intake did not result in a statistically significant difference in the proportion of N3. In contrast, wake time after sleep onset (WASO) and the total time of N1 significantly decreased. In addition, ETAS intake improved the following scores for AIS: total score, “awakenings during the night” and “functioning capacity during the day”. Furthermore, post-hoc analysis revealed that ETAS intake significantly reduced WASO in individuals who originally had high WASO and significantly improved sleep efficiency in individuals with low sleep efficiency. These results suggest that ETAS may regulate sleep quality in accordance with the individual's sleep stage balance.Keywords: ETAS, a standardized extract of Asparagus officinalis stem, Sleep quality, Non-REM sleep, Wake time after sleep onset, WASO, Sleep efficiency

Introduction

Sleep can be characterized in terms of two fundamental components: quality and quantity [1]. While both the quality and quantity of sleep are important, health indicators are reported to be more strongly associated with quality [1,2]. Indeed, the relationship between sleep quality and disease risk has recently attracted substantial research interest. Among findings, poor sleep quality has been associated with an approximately 1.4-fold increased risk of coronary heart disease [3] and type 2 diabetes [4]. Additionally, the proportion of rapid eye movement (REM) sleep has been found to be inversely correlated with the risk of developing heart failure, while the proportion of deep sleep (non-REM sleep 3 [N3]) has been inversely correlated with the risk of developing atrial fibrillation [5].

ETAS, a standardized extract of Asparagus officinalis stem, has been shown to increase the concentration of HSP70 (Heat Shock Protein 70 kDa) mRNA in the blood when taken orally [6]. HSP70 also involves GABA(A) receptors in the brain, leading to an increased proportion of N3 [7].

We previously conducted a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind crossover study as a preliminary study in healthy, mainly middle-aged men. The study involved two 14-day intake periods with a 14-day washout period between them. Electroencephalograms (EEG) were recorded during night sleep and used to calculate discrete sleep stages, including REM sleep, light sleep (non-REM sleep 1 [N1] and non-REM sleep 2 [N2]), and N3. The results indicated a tendency for ETAS intake to enhance both the total time and proportion of N3 [8].

In the present study, we investigated how ETAS affects quantitative sleep indices, including the proportion of N3, by targeting healthy adult men and women aged 18 and older, with an expanded number of participants than in the preliminary study.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects (2021), and the protocol of this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Takara Clinic, Seishinkai Medical Corporation (Shinagawa, Tokyo, Japan) (approval date: September 12, 2024, approval number: 2409-00372-0039-16-TC). Written informed consent was obtained from the participants. The study was prospectively registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN) (UMIN000055685) on February 20, 2025.

Study design

The study was designed as a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind crossover study. The study period consisted of two 14-day intervention periods separated by a 14-day washout period (Table 1). Screening tests were administered to applicants to assess their eligibility and obtain baseline value. Functional evaluations were conducted before and after each intervention. The participants were randomly divided into equal two groups. The participants in Group 1 received ETAS during the first intervention period and placebo during the second. Group 2 participants received the test foods in the reverse order. The participants took either a placebo or ETAS capsule after each meal for a total of three capsules per day. Each ETAS capsule contained 100 mg of ETAS50, resulting in a daily intake of 300 mg of ETAS50. The first intervention period was initiated on November 7 or 8, 2024. The participants were instructed to avoid overeating and excessive drinking, maintain their usual lifestyle from the day of informed consent to the final day of the second intervention period, and refrain from alcohol and excessive exercise from the day before each evaluation until its completion.

Participants

Paid volunteers were recruited through a monitor recruitment website operated by the clinical research coordinator from September 18 to October 15, 2024. Consequently, 54 applicants consented to participate in this study. Each applicant underwent screening tests for a period of four consecutive days between October 2 and 20, 2024. From these 54 applicants, the principal investigator selected 38 participants. Selection criteria were: a) Japanese nationality, b) age 18 or over, d) generally good health, e) subjective complaints of dissatisfaction with sleep, f) BMI between 18.5 kg/m2 and 25 kg/m2, and g) lower proportion of N3 among applicants, as determined by EEG analysis during nighttime sleep. Exclusion criteria included: a) current or history of medical treatment for malignant tumors, heart failure, or myocardial infarction, b) use of a pacemaker or implantable cardioverter-defibrillator, c) current treatment for any of the following chronic diseases: arrhythmia, liver disease, chronic kidney disease, cerebrovascular disease, rheumatic disease, diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, or other chronic diseases, d) current treatment for sleep disorders, e) regular use of "Foods for Specified Health Uses" or "Foods with Function Claims", f) regular use of pharmaceuticals, including herbal medicines and supplements, g) allergy to the test foods or related foods, particularly allergy to asparagus, h) irregular sleep habits due to lifestyle factors, including night-time employment, i) sleep in a shared bed with other people, j) living with an individual requiring care or an infant, k) habitual consumption of alcohol above the recommended limit (i.e., 20 g of pure alcohol per day), l) consumption of three or more cups of coffee per day (defined as approximately 230 mL per cup), m) pregnant, breastfeeding, or intending to become pregnant during the designated study period, o) previous enrollment in another clinical trial within 28 days prior to providing consent to participate in this study, or scheduled to participate in another trial during the study period, and p) judged as ineligible to participate in this study by a study physician.

Test foods

ETAS (Amino Up Co., Ltd., Sapporo, Japan) is an active food extracted from asparagus stems and manufactured in accordance with Good Manufacturing Practice for Dietary Supplements, ISO 9001:2015, and ISO 22000:2018. The ETAS used in this study was ETAS50, which was prepared according to the manufacturing method described in previous reports [6,[9], [10], [11]]. ETAS50 contains 50 μg/g or more of three diketopiperazines as functional ingredients: cyclo(L-phenylalanine-L-proline), cyclo(L-tyrosine-L-proline), and cyclo(L-leucine-L-proline) [9]. ETAS is a registered trademark of Amino Up Co., Ltd. Dextrin (Pinex #1, Matsutani Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Itami, Japan) was used as the placebo food. The test foods were encapsulated in white (opaque) hard capsules. Blinding was conducted to ensure that it was impossible for either participants, investigators or study coordinators to distinguish between the placebo food and active food by appearance, shape, color, smell or taste.

Endpoint

The primary endpoints included the proportion of N3 in total sleep time (TST) during nighttime sleep, determined by EEG analysis after the interventions.

The secondary endpoints included the following sleep parameters during nighttime sleep as determined using EEG analysis after the interventions: sleep latency; wake time after sleep onset (WASO); TST; sleep efficiency; total time of N1, N2, N3 and REM sleep; proportion of N1, N2 and REM sleep in TST; REM latency; and time in bed after final awakening. Furthermore, the OSA-MA and ASI questionnaire scores were included as secondary endpoints.

Screening test

The screening tests comprised physical measurements, urinalysis, peripheral blood tests, and EEG measurements during nighttime sleep. Physical measurements included body height, body weight, BMI, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure. Urinalysis included qualitative urine protein analysis, qualitative urine glucose analysis, urine pH, and urine occult blood. Peripheral blood tests included white blood cell count, red blood cell count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, platelet count, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, γ-glutamyl transferase, total bilirubin, total protein, urea nitrogen, creatinine, uric acid, sodium, potassium, chloride, serum amylase, total cholesterol, HDL-cholesterol, LDL-cholesterol, triglycerides, glucose, and hemoglobin A1c. EEG measurement is described in detail below. Physical measurements, urinalysis, and peripheral blood tests were conducted at either Takara Clinic (Medical Corporation Seishinkai, Takara Clinic, Tokyo, Japan) or Minami-machi Clinic (Nerima Medical Association, Minami-machi Clinic, Tokyo, Japan) on the fourth day of the screening period.

EEG measurements

EEG measurements were performed using a portable EEG device, InSomnograf K2® (S'UIMIN Inc., Tsukuba, Japan). InSomnograf K2 can measure EEG with the same level of accuracy as the overnight polysomnography test in healthy individuals [12]. The participants were required to affix disposable adhesive electrodes to their forehead and both ears before going to sleep at night, after which their EEG were measured during nighttime sleep. Measurements were conducted on a total of nine nights: three nights from the night of Day 1 to the morning of Day 4 of the screening period and three nights from the night of Day 11 to the morning of Day 14 of each of the intervention periods. The EEG data were transmitted from InSomnograf K2 to S'UIMIN Inc., which analyzed and calculated the following sleep parameters from the EEG data: sleep latency; WASO; total time of N1, N2, N3 and REM sleep; proportion of N1, N2, N3 and REM sleep; REM latency; time in bed after final awakening; and total recorded time and missing time. Total sleep time was calculated by adding the total time of N1, N2, N3 and REM sleep, while total recorded time consisted of total sleep time, sleep latency, WASO, time in bed after final awakening, and missing time. Sleep efficiency was defined as the ratio of total sleep time to total recorded time. The average values of the sleep parameters over the three consecutive nights were used for evaluation.

Questionnaire

Subjective assessments were conducted using the Oguri-Shirakawa-Azumi Sleep Inventory, Middle-age and Aged version (OSA-MA) and the Japanese version of the Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS-J). The participants completed these online questionnaires at their own homes after waking up on designated days. OSA-MA is a psychological scale that evaluates sleep introspection upon waking, with higher scores indicating better sleep [13]. The OSA-MA questionnaire comprises 16 questions, and scores are calculated for the following five factors related to sleep quality: sleep initiation and maintenance, dreaming, sleepiness on rising, rejuvenation, and adjustment. OSA-MA was completed on the following days: three days from Day −2 to Day 0 of each intervention period, and three days from Day 12 to Day 14 of each intervention period. The OSA-MA scores used for evaluation were the average values over three consecutive nights for each factor. AIS-J is a scale for assessing the severity of insomnia based on self-assessment of sleep problems experienced at least three times per week in the past one month [14,15]. Higher scores on AIS-J are indicative of higher severity of insomnia. It comprises the following eight questions: sleep induction, awakenings during the night, early morning awakening, total sleep duration, overall quality of sleep, well-being during the day, functioning capacity during the day, sleepiness during the day. AIS-J was completed on the following days: Day 0 and Day 14 of each intervention period.

Safety

The principal investigator conducted interviews with each participant on Day 0 and Day 14 of each intervention period.

Sample size

In the preliminary study, which analyzed data from seven male participants with a median age of 50 years (Q1 44, Q3 57), the effect size of ETAS intake on the proportion of N3 during nighttime sleep was 0.59 [8]. The present study was designed to include both male and female participants aged 20 and over, with the intention of recruiting a larger sample of participants below the age of 40 and above the age of 60. The proportion of N3 decreases with age [16], and it was hypothesized that the effect of ETAS intake on the proportion of N3 would differ by age. Consequently, the effect of ETAS intake was considered to be less likely detected in this study than in the preliminary study, and the effect size was estimated to be 0.472, which is 80% of the effect size in the preliminary study. Accordingly, 36 participants were required for the crossover study. Considering the possibility of dropouts, the target sample size was set at 38.

Allocation

An allocation table was generated by the allocation manager who was not directly involved in the study, using R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with block randomization. The participants were allocated to either Group 1 or Group 2 in a 1:1 ratio according to the allocation table. The manufacturer of the test food assigned identification codes to the ETAS and placebo, affixed identification code labels to the test food packaging and provided the test foods to the clinical research coordinator. The test food manufacturer maintained a secure correspondence table which linked the test food to its identification codes. This table was not disclosed to any organization or individual other than the test food manufacturer until the allocation manager sent the allocation table after the study concluded. The allocation manager confirmed that neither the allocation sheet nor the emergency key had been accessed after the database was locked. Thereafter, the allocation manager opened the aforementioned documents, inscribed the opening date and name on the allocation table, and subsequently disclosed the allocation table.

Statistical analysis

Following the conclusion of the study, the principal investigator reviewed all cases to ascertain any potential violations of the selection and exclusion criteria or violations in the provision of informed consent. The review also included the handling of cases, including any deviations from the protocol and study discontinuation. Based on this review, the principal investigator and the study statistician made decisions regarding case handling, data inclusion, and the analysis plan. These decisions were made under blinded conditions. No interim analysis was conducted. The analysis was conducted using the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis method, and no imputation of missing values was performed.

The analysis employed a linear mixed-effects model (LMM), designated as Model 1, which incorporated sequence (ETAS-first, placebo-first), period (1, 2), and treatment (ETAS, placebo) as fixed effects, with the participant nested within sequence as a random effect (Model 1: Outcome = β0 + β1Sequence + β2Period + β3Treatment + (1 | Sequence:Participant)). Model 2 incorporated a mean-centered baseline value as a covariate into Model 1 (Model 2: Outcome = β0 + β1Sequence + β2Period + β3Treatment + β4Baseline + (1 | Sequence:Participant)). As a baseline value, EEG measurements were analyzed using the screening test value, whereas questionnaire scores were analyzed using the values on the day before the respective intervention period.

Furthermore, a post-hoc analysis was performed on the EEG measurement results which incorporated the interaction term between treatment and baseline as a fixed effect into Model 2 (Model 3: Outcome = β0 + β1Sequence + β2Period + β3Treatment + β4Baseline + β5(Treatment x Baseline) + (1 | Sequence:Participant)). For items in this interaction term that showed a statistically significant difference or trend, a simple slope analysis was performed to evaluate the effect of treatment at the mean±1SD of baseline.

The criteria for each fixed effect in the analysis were as follows: the sequence was designated as ETAS-first, the period was designated as period 1, and the treatment was designated as placebo.

Statistical significance levels were defined as *p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). EZR is a modified version of R Commander, designed to add statistical functions frequently used in biostatistics [17].

Intervention effect on EEG: model 3 (post hoc analysis)

Table 4 shows the result of the post-hoc analysis for Model 3, which incorporates a fixed effect of the interaction term between baseline and treatment into Model 2. Discussion

It has been reported that the expression of HSP70 mRNA in leukocytes was increased following seven days administration of ETAS in healthy adults [10]. HSP70 has a somnogenic effect that which involves GABA(A) receptors in the hypothalamus [7,18,19]. This effect was characterized by an increase in the proportion of deep sleep during sleep and a decrease in deep sleep fragmentation. Furthermore, HSP70 reduces the proportion of light sleep and wakefulness [19]. Based on the findings of these studies, we hypothesized that ETAS exerts a positive effect on sleep quality by inducing HSP70. A preliminary study indicated that ETAS may increase the proportion of N3, which is slow sleep. The aim of the present study was to verify the effect of ETAS on N3 appearance. However, we did not observe a statistically significant difference in primary endpoint, namely the proportion of N3, due to ETAS intake. With regard to the background of the participants, the proportion of N3 at the screening test was 13.5 ± 9.4%. These findings contrast with previously reported values for the proportion of N3 (mean [95% CI]%) in healthy individuals as follows: 35-49 years (20.4 [18.5-22.2]%) and 50-64 years (18.1 [15.0-21.2]%) [16]. Thus, proportions of N3 in our present participants ranged from lower to higher than rates observed in healthy individuals. Based on findings that GABA(A) receptors are involved in the effects of HSP70 [19,20], the expression of HSP70 is not thought to increase N3 appearance without limit, but to rather do so within the range of natural sleep regulation. In other words, it is speculated that the sleep-modulating effect of ETAS on N3 appearance via GABA(A) receptors is dependent on the participant's initial proportion of N3. Accordingly, participants with a higher proportion of N3 than healthy individuals will likely not show an increase in this proportion following ETAS intake. Because this study ultimately included participants with a wide range of proportions of N3, we assume that this study was unable to clearly identify the effect of ETAS on the proportion of N3. Future studies aimed at clarifying the effects on sleep quality should consider standardizing the sleep tendencies of the participants. For instance, to clarify the effects of ETAS on the proportion of N3, it is necessary to conduct trials targeting individuals with a low proportion of N3, such as 5% or less.

On the other hand, this study revealed new effects of ETAS on sleep quality, namely improvements in the total time of N1 and WASO. Additionally, a significant improvement was observed in the score of AIS-J item "awakenings during the night," suggesting that the WASO-reducing effect of ETAS is also subjective. Thus, we hypothesize that these newly observed effects of ETAS on reducing the total time of N1 and WASO are brought about by the HSP70 induction of ETAS, as previously hypothesized [19]. Future studies measuring HSP70 mRNA expression and changes in EEG during sleep after ETAS intake are necessary to verify the accuracy of these hypotheses. In addition, it has been demonstrated that sleep quality influences daytime activity [21]. An improvement in sleep quality parameters such as WASO due to ETAS intake may result in an increase in the total score and in "functioning capacity during the day" in the AIS-J.

The results of this study raise the question of which sleep stage increased in place of the decrease in the total time of N1 and WASO due to ETAS intake. The post-hoc Model 3 analysis demonstrated an interaction between treatment and baseline in sleep efficiency, total time of N3, proportion of N2 and REM sleep, and WASO, indicating that ETAS does not uniformly increase total time and the proportion of specific sleep stages in all individuals. The result of the simple slope analysis revealed a significant treatment effect on WASO in participants with mean (30.62 min) and mean+1SD (53.62 min). This finding suggests that ETAS effectively and substantially reduces WASO in individuals with high WASO. On the other hand, this finding may be partially influenced by a range effect, whereby participants with greater potential for improvement exhibit larger changes. However, this study was conducted as a placebo-controlled crossover study, and in Models 2 and 3, the analysis was performed using LMM with baseline as a covariate. Furthermore, since the interaction term between treatment and baseline on WASO was significant in Model 3, the relationship between baseline and the magnitude of change differed between ETAS and placebo; therefore, it is considered difficult to explain the results observed in this study solely by the range effect. Consequently, it is believed that, in addition to the possible partial contribution of the range effect, the effect of ETAS is also involved in the observation of greater WASO improvements in participants with higher baseline values.

Furthermore, ETAS demonstrated a substantial treatment effect on sleep efficiency in participants with sleep efficiency of the mean−1SD (80.14%), suggesting that sleep efficiency was enhanced in individuals with low sleep efficiency. Since a sleep efficiency of less than 85% is associated with a higher risk of cardiometabolic disease morbidity [22] and a higher prevalence of mental complaints [23], the intake of ETAS to improve sleep efficiency in individuals with low sleep efficiency is considered to have clinical meaningful. Additionally, WASO is negatively correlated with sleep efficiency, which is calculated by dividing total sleep time by the amount time in bed. Time in bed includes total sleep time, WASO and sleep latency. This means that as WASO decreases, sleep efficiency increases. Therefore, the effect of ETAS on improving sleep efficiency reflects its effect of reducing WASO. The effect of ETAS on improving sleep efficiency was observed in individuals with low sleep efficiency due to high WASO. Consequently, we suggest that ETAS may contribute to the regulation of the balance of sleep stage, for example by increasing the appearance of N3 and REM sleep if low before taking ETAS.

Finally, with regard to the observed period and sequence effects, we observed a significant period effect in the following items: sleep efficiency, WASO and AIS-J's item "awakenings during the night." When utilized for sleep monitoring, wearable devices often result in the temporary disruption of sleep patterns; however, these effects generally fade over time [24]. Consequently, the period effects observed in this study on increasing night awakenings and decreasing sleep efficiency are likely attributable to the participants' adaptation to wearing the device. No significant sequence effects were observed in EEG measurements. However, a significant sequence effect was observed in AIS-J's item "sleep induction," with ETAS-first demonstrating a significantly higher score than placebo-first. This is believed to be due to the dynamic nature of the placebo effect, which fluctuated during the intervention period and stabilized after an initial reduction in insomnia symptoms. Additionally, the sequence of interventions influences psychological expectations and conditioning [25]. Indeed, some crossover studies have reported differences in the magnitude of the placebo effect between subjective and objective assessments of sleep [26].

Limitations

Studies aiming to clarify the effects of an intervention on sleep quality should typically first standardize the sleep tendencies of the participants. For instance, to clarify the effects of ETAS on the proportion of N3, it is necessary to conduct trials targeting individuals with a low proportion of N3, such as 5% or less. Furthermore, given the low statistical power of interaction terms, a study comprising a larger number of participants is necessary to clarify the overall effect of ETAS on each sleep stage.

Conclusion

This study was unable to clarify the effect of ETAS on N3 appearance. On the other hand, it showed that ETAS reduces night awakenings, improves sleep efficiency and decreases the total time of N1. Furthermore, the results suggest that ETAS may affect sleep quality by regulating the balance of sleep stages in the individual taking it.
