EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report details the digitization of a legacy wellbore database, including data processing
assumptions, parameter estimation, and risk assessment methodology. The database, comprising
6,454 documents, was provided by ISGS. It includes valuable data from the Prairie State
Generating Company (PSGC) and One Earth Energy (OEE) sites of the CarbonSAFE Phase III –
Illinois Storage Corridor project. The report focuses on wells within a 15-mile radius from the
Lively Grove #1 (LG#1) well at PSGC site, evaluating subsurface conditions and potential risks.
A total of 4,386 wellbores within 15 miles of the LG#1 well were filtered based on depth and
formation codes. LG#1 is the stratigraphic well at the PSGC site drilled in 2021. Ninety-four (94)
wells penetrating the Maquoketa Shale Group (the primary confining unit) within the estimated
area-of-review (AoR) for the PSGC site were evaluated using a qualitative risk assessment (QRA)
methodology.
The QRA developed by Arbad et al. 2022 focuses on legacy wells within the AoR and categorizes
them based on well construction details. The QRA identifies wells that need immediate attention
by categorizing them based on penetration depth and protection.
Wells within the AoR were categorized into nine groups based on penetrations and protections.
These categories range from Type 1 wells, with no documentation, to Type 9 wells, which do not
penetrate the primary confining unit or storage reservoir (unit).
Well accessibility within the AoR varies based on well status, including Dry & Abandoned (DA),
Plugged & Abandoned (PA), Injection (INJ), Oil/Gas Producing (PROD), and Observation (Obs)
wells. Accessibility levels were determined by well construction, with DA wells being the least
accessible and Observation wells the most accessible, impacting gas leakage detection
possibilities.
Remedial action priority of wells decreases from Type 1 to Type 9 wells. Type 1 to Type 6 wells
with status DA and PA require immediate attention, while Type 7 and Type 8 wells are low
priority.
A risk matrix used to prioritize corrective actions for legacy wells is proposed to categorize wells
within an AoR based on penetrations, protections, and accessibility. The methodology involves
data acquisition, well categorization into nine types, and determining CO2 leakage pathways using
well schematics and geospatial mapping. This approach is particularly useful for managing the
integrity of legacy wells throughout the lifecycle of a Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) project.
A qualitative risk assessment of 94 wells within the AoR of the PSGC site identified 54 wells with
high priority for corrective action due to penetration of the primary containment seal. The
assessment utilizes color-coded maps to categorize well types and prioritize corrective actions,
providing a comprehensive analysis.
Schematics of wells penetrating the primary confining unit were drawn, and leakage pathways
were identified. Details of all wells penetrating the confining zone are provided in the appendix,
including information on well types, plugging, and casing status.

1. BACKGROUND
This report provides records of the stepwise procedure followed in digitizing a legacy wellbore
database including the assumptions made during data processing, calculations performed to
estimate important/missing well parameters, data sources, and methodology used for rigorous
classification and qualitative risk assessment (QRA) of legacy wells.
The Illinois State Geological Survey (ISGS) provided tabulated data and reports of legacy wells
within 15 miles radius of the two proposed drilling sites. The proposed drilling sites consist of two
stratigraphic wells at the Prairie State Generating Company (PSGC) site within the Prairie State
Energy Campus (PSEC) in Washington County, Illinois and One Earth Energy (OEE) site in
McClean County, Illinois. The data provided by ISGS contained 6,454 documents (digitized well
data, well files in PDF format, and logs in either LAS, TIF, or pdf format). The PSGC spreadsheet
had information of 4,386 wellbores (with a depth greater than 100 ft) while the OEE spreadsheet
had 151 wells. The existing database spreadsheets contain well data such as well locations,
distances from the well site, completion and plug dates, depths, total depth (TD) of formations,
formation tops, and other relevant information. The quality and quantity of the data varied greatly
as the wells were drilled and reported during different years ranging from 1893 to 2018.
Wells located within 15-mile radius from the stratigraphic test wells at both PSGC (Lively Grove
#1/LG#1) site and OEE site (One Earth Energy #1/OEE#1) were evaluated. For example, at the
PSGC site, the distances of legacy wells (WellDist_Mi) reported in the Excel spreadsheets are
from the location LG#1 i.e., one of the 10-acre spots in the following quarter: SW quarter of T5W
R2W Section 15 roughly (38.353028, -89.644836) WGS84. This report focuses on the QRA of
legacy wells penetrating the confining unit at the PSGC site.
The summary of subsurface evaluations for the PSGC site is presented in Table 1, which includes
information about the primary confining units (seals), reservoir (storage) targets, Underground
Sources of Drinking Water (USDWs), legacy wellbore information, and preliminary CO2 plume
predictions.
More details on the Storage complex and thickness maps of seals and storage reservoirs are
presented in the appendix.

2. METHODOLOGY
The qualitative risk assessment (QRA) developed by Arbad et al. 2022 focuses only on the legacy
wells within the AoR and needs only well construction details which are reported to state agencies.
The QRA categorizes and identify wells that need immediate attention. Arbad et al. 2022 pre-
defined nine well types based on the well construction and five accessibility levels to the types of
wells (patent pending technology).

2.1 Types of Wells within AoR
The confinement potential of geologic seals need to be proved for a CCS project prior to submitting
the Class VI permit application. Complete documentation of all the wells within the AoR along
with site maps need to be submitted with the permit application. All wells within the AoR must
maintain well integrity throughout the lifecycle of CCS Projects. Thus, wells that need corrective
action must be identified and repaired (US EPA, 2013). There is no standardized procedure to
categorize the wells within the AoR and identify the wells which need immediate attention. This
section describes the distinct types of wells based only on their well construction details reported
to state agencies.
The wells within the AoR should be categorized based on the following two criteria (Figure 1):
 Penetrations - whether they are penetrating the confining zone and storage complex, just the
 confining zone, or neither.
 Protections - whether the USDWs are protected or not. If the primary confining unit and storage
 reservoir is penetrated, are they are protected or not? Protection refers to the presence of proven
 barriers to prevent the leakage of CO2 into USDWs or in the worst case to the surface. The primary
 barriers of penetrating wells include casings, cement sheath, and cement plugs.

 Figure 1. Definitions of well types within the AoR.

The wells within the AoR can be categorized into nine groups based on their penetrations and
protections (Figure 2) as follows:
Type 1 Wells – This category encompasses wells for which documentation is unavailable. These
are the wells which are mostly drilled before the establishment of the state oil and gas division.
The state has little to no records of these wells. Once more information is available on these wells,
they can be moved to other categories based on the penetrations and protections. The schematics
for Type 1 wells cannot be drawn as the well construction details are not available (Figure 2).
Undocumented orphaned wells are Type 1 wells, and documented orphaned wells may fall within
Type 2 to Type 9 based on their penetrations and protections.

 4

Type 2 Wells – This category includes the wells that penetrate both the primary confining unit
and the storage reservoir but the USDW, primary geologic seal, and the storage reservoir, are not
protected.
Type 3 Wells – This category includes the wells that penetrate the primary confining unit and the
storage reservoir but only the USDW is protected. The primary confining unit and the storage
reservoir are not protected.
Type 4 Wells – This category includes the wells that penetrate the primary confining unit and the
storage reservoir but only the USDW and the primary confining unit are protected. The storage
reservoir is not protected.
Type 5 Wells – This category includes the wells that penetrate only the primary confining unit
with the USDW and the primary confining unit unprotected.
Type 6 Wells – This category includes the wells that penetrate only the primary confining unit
and only the USDW is protected while the primary confining unit is not protected.
Type 7 Wells – This category includes the wells that penetrate both the primary confining unit
and the storage reservoir with the USDW, the primary geologic seal, and the storage reservoir
protected.
Type 8 Wells – This category includes the wells that penetrate only the primary confining unit
with the USDW and the primary confining unit protected.
Type 9 Wells – This category includes the wells that do not penetrate the primary confining unit
and the storage reservoir. It would be reasonable to assume that these are the least risky wells if
the confining zone is not penetrated at all.
The well types within the AoR can be more than the nine types listed herein depending on the level
of detail needed to categorize them, but they need to be arranged as described above. The next
section describes the different accessibility levels of wells, which impact the estimated corrective
actions costs.

2.2 Types of Accessibility levels
The accessibility to wells within the AoR depends on well status. The status of a well can be
classified as Dry & Abandoned (DA), Plugged & Abandoned (PA), Injection Well (INJ), Oil or
Gas Producing Well (PROD), and Observation Well (Obs). The key differences between these
accessibility levels are described below and presented in Figure 3.
2.1.1 Dry & Abandoned (DA) Wells
These are wells that were drilled for oil and gas (hydrocarbon) production but no hydrocarbon was
found or the quantity of hydrocarbons found was uneconomic. Hence, these wells were declared
as DA. From a well construction perspective, DA wells only have surface casing cemented to the
surface and plugs are shallow in depth at the bottom of the surface casing and a few meters below
the depth of the surface casing shoe (depending upon the regulatory requirements at the time of
plugging). Surface plug requirements across the globe are summarized in (Van Der Kuip et al.,
2011). The top of the surface casing is also cut a few feet below the ground level, and it is filled
with soil. Some states have markers to identify the DA wells. It is difficult to locate wells in states
which do not require DA wells markers. If DA wells are within the AoR and do penetrate the
storage reservoir and/or confining zone, they need to be re-entered and plugged. Re-entering and

 5

plugging DA wells without makers is are expensive operations as they need to be located first, re-
entered, and plugged. Stratigraphic test wells should be included in this category if they only have
surface casings and cement plugs as shown in Figure 3.
2.1.2 Plugged & Abandoned (PA) Wells
These are the wells that were producing oil and gas and were later plugged as when they became
uneconomic to operate. The PA wells differ from DA wells from a well construction point of view
in that PA wells have cemented production casing. In some cases, the production casing is cut
above the top of cement and removed before plugging PA wells. As a result, PA wells have extra
barriers compared to the DA wells. Stratigraphic test wells if they have two or more casings along
with cement plugs must be included in this category.
2.1.3 Injection Wells (INJ)
These wells are used for the injection of water or gas into the subsurface for enhance oil recover (EOR),
disposal or storage purposes. Injection wells have active status and can be easily accessible.
2.1.4 Oil/Gas Producing Well (PROD)
These are active wells currently producing oil and gas. They have similar accessibility as injection wells.
2.1.5 Observation Wells (Obs)
These wells are also called idle wells used for monitoring the pressure changes and fluid saturation changes.
Observation Wells are present in the gas storage fields for observation purposes.
Well construction differences between the different accessibility levels are shown in Figure 3. As the
accessibility number increases the gas leakage detection possibility increases. In other words, gas leakage
can be more easily detected in observation wells than in DA wells. The accessibility categories can be more
than the five listed here, but they need to be arranged in increasing accessibility order.

2.3 Corrective (Remedial) Action Priority
As the well type number increases the priority for remedial action decreases. As a result, corrective
actions must be performed first on Type 1 wells and last on Type 9 wells. Wells that require remedial
actions, and their corresponding corrective action priority are listed below;
 • Type 1 to Type 6 wells with status DA & PA need immediate attention and remedial actions.
 • Type 1 to Type 6 wells with status INJ & PROD are medium priority wells.
 • Type 7 & Type 8 wells with status DA & PA are low priority wells as the geologic seals and
 reservoir are protected with barriers.
 • Type 7 & Type 8 wells with status INJ and Pro are the least priority wells as these are active
 wells that can be accessed.
 • All Type 9 wells are least priority wells as they do not penetrate the confining zone and
 storage reservoir.
 • All types of observation wells are low priority wells as any leakage through them could be
 easily detected and rectified.

Well Type 1 to Type 6 need immediate remedial actions if they lie within the predicted CO2
plume radius as there exists the possibility of CO2 cross flow amongst these wells leading to
leakage into USDWs or to the surface. The priority to perform remedial actions on different
wells within each type depends on the location of these wells with respect to the UIC Class VI
injection well as well as the accessibility to these wells.

A traditional risk matrix conveys information regarding the impact and probability of
occurrences whereas the proposed risk matrix (Table 3) conveys additional information
regarding the well construction and accessibility details, geologic penetration details, as well
as the corrective action priority provided the readers know the pre-defined well types. This will
be extremely useful when a lot of wells need corrective action, and the operators must propose
a phased corrective action plan for the project to move forward. The old legacy wells need to
maintain integrity throughout the lifecycle of the CCS project considering the long-term
stewardship of the CCS project. The proposed risk matrix and standardized categorization of
legacy wells based on their geologic penetrations and protections will help keep track of the
risky legacy wells as some of them might not be risky at the time of permit application but
could lose integrity over the years. As the project progresses, the uncertainties decrease, and
more information is available from the monitoring data that might cause the legacy wells to
move from one type to another.

2.5 Step-by-Step Procedure summary
The step-by-step procedure to apply the methodology is presented in Figure 4. The entire
methodology can be broadly divided into three phases. Phase 1 includes the data acquisition
from all the publicly available reports and processing of all the information necessary for
qualitative risk assessment. Phase 2 refers to the categorization of the legacy wells within the
AoR into pre-defined nine well types (Type 1 to 9). Lastly, the CO2 leakage pathways are found
by drawing well schematics of identified risky legacy wells and their priority for corrective
action is decided in phase 3 with the help of geospatial mapping.
Currently, there are only two active Class VI wells permitted by US EPA in Illinois (as of
November 6, 2025) and there are no legacy wells within the AoR penetrating the confining
zones at PSGC site except for the characterization wells drilled by operators. The
categorization of the legacy wells into nine pre-defined categories is really useful when there
are a lot of wells penetrating the confining zone (Arbad et al., 2022). The sustained casing
pressure (SCP) and cement bond log (CBL) reports are not available for these wells.
Furthermore, the cement class used for cementing these wells is not reported in well reports,
but the authors have consulted with the cementing service provider to gain additional insights.
The top of cement was estimated for most of the wells using the cement volume (sacks)
information reported in the well reports and additional information acquired from the
cementing service providers for these wells. Van Der Kuip et al., (2011) extrapolated
penetration depths of aqueous CO2 in cement under in-situ reservoir conditions based on the
results reported in the literature. The extrapolated penetration depth after 10,000 years ranges
from 0.001 m to 10 m (i.e., maximum of 32.8 ft). Based on these findings, 40 ft of cement plugs
and 200 ft of cement sheath in the annulus were assumed to be safe to prevent the leakage of
CO2. The depths reported in the well reports for geologic penetration of primary confining units
and storage reservoir were verified using the available logs. It was also assumed that if the
coals are protected then the USDWs are also protected given that the lowermost USDWs are
the top of the Pennsylvanian strata at PSGC site, and all coals are Pennsylvanian in age. These
conditions were consistently applied using MS-Excel to categorize the wells into pre-defined
nine categories and five accessibility levels (Arbad et al., 2023).
