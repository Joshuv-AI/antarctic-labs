Recurrent Miscarriages and Postpartum Heart Failure Can Be Phenotypic Manifestations of the m.3243A>G Variant
Authors: Sounira Mehri, Josef Finsterer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFemale carriers of the MT‐TL1 variant m.3243A>G may manifest not only phenotypically with diabetes, renal insufficiency, hearing loss, and cardiomyopathy but also with recurrent miscarriages.

The article by Watanabe et al. describes a 37‐year‐old woman with a non‐syndromic mitochondrial disorder (MID) caused by the mtDNA variant m.3243A>G in the MT‐TL1 gene, which phenotypically manifested as type‐2‐diabetes, renal insufficiency, hearing loss, postpartum heart failure, brainstem atrophy including cerebellar involvement, and recurrent miscarriages with placenta accreta [1]. During her fourth pregnancy, the patient gave birth to a preterm infant at 29 weeks of gestation, who, following initial respiratory distress and treatment in the neonatal intensive care unit, made a full recovery after an 81‐day hospital stay [1]. Several points warrant discussion:

First, the suspicion of a MID could have arisen earlier than after the fourth pregnancy. The patient had type 2 diabetes, renal insufficiency, hearing loss, a positive family history for diabetes, and a history of recurrent miscarriages. This combination suggests the presence of a MID [2]. Furthermore, it is conceivable that the patient exhibited other subtle phenotypic manifestations of a MID, such as short stature, ptosis, ophthalmoparesis without double vision, or cognitive impairment, particularly given that cerebral magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed atrophy of the brainstem, including the cerebellum [1]. Mild manifestations of a MID are often overlooked. Was the patient ever examined by a neurologist trained in MIDs prior to her current delivery? A neurological examination is particularly relevant, as carriers of the m.3243A>G mutation frequently exhibit neurological manifestations such as ophthalmoplegia, ptosis, cognitive impairment, epilepsy, neuropathy, and myopathy, and these manifestations often dominate the phenotype.

Secondly, the heteroplasmy rates were not reported in clinically affected tissues [1]. Since MIDs are often inherited maternally and the degree of heteroplasmy can vary randomly from generation to generation, accurate quantification is essential for genetic counseling and medical diagnosis. Heteroplasmy rates not only determine the onset and severity of the phenotype but also the course of the disease; they aid in predicting disease progression and influence genetic counseling as well as reproductive decisions. Heteroplasmy rates should be determined in multiple tissues, particularly those most severely affected. Since mtDNA variants are inherited from the mother in 75% of cases [3], it is also recommended that the mother of the index patient undergoes a clinical examination by a neurologist and be genetically tested for the causative variant.

The third point is that it would be helpful to specify the modalities used in cerebral MRI [1]. Of particular interest is whether hyperintensities were detected on T2‐weighted imaging, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery, perfusion‐weighted imaging, and diffusion‐weighted imaging, as well as hypointensities on oxygen‐extraction fraction imaging. This constellation would suggest a stroke‐like lesion, the cerebral analogue of a stroke‐like episode (SLE) [4]. SLEs are pathognomonic for mitochondrial encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke‐like episode (MELAS) syndrome, which is attributable to the m.3243A>G variant in approximately 80% of cases.

Fourth, it would be helpful to determine the cause of the severe systolic dysfunction with an ejection fraction of 18% [1]. Were creatine kinase and pro‐brain natriuretic peptide elevated, did the ECG show signs of myocardial infarction or ventricular arrhythmias, and did the transthoracic echocardiography reveal signs of apical ballooning or other forms of Takotsubo syndrome (TTS)? It is more likely than postpartum cardiomyopathy that the patient suffered from TTS due to the stress of the premature birth and the condition of the newborn.

The fifth point is that measuring lactate levels in serum and CSF can be helpful in diagnosing the specific MID [1]. Since carriers of the m.3243A>G mutation frequently suffer from lactic acidosis, particularly under stress [5], it should be noted whether the index patient ever developed lactic acidosis in serum or CSF during pregnancy, delivery, or the postpartum period.

Sixth, it would be helpful to include reference values in table 1 so that readers can determine which values were normal and which were abnormal.

Finally, it should be specified whether the endomyocardial biopsy sample was also examined using immunohistological and biochemical methods to assess the functions of the respiratory chain in the myocardium. Of particular interest is whether the myocardium was affected by the MID or not. Did the newborn also carry the m.3243A>G variant?

Overall, female carriers of the MT‐TL1 variant m.3243A>G may present not only phenotypically with diabetes, renal insufficiency, hearing loss, and cardiomyopathy, but also with recurrent miscarriages. Female carriers of the m.3243A>G variant should be informed of their increased risk of obstetric complications.
