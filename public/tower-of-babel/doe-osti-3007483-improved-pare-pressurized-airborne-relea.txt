 INTRODUCTION

 An improved version of the PARE (Pressurized Airborne Release Equipment) system (Sutter
1983a and Sutter 1983b and Ballinger et al 1987) has been developed for powder venting testing. This
effort seeks to improve the accuracy of risk estimation for the venting of pressurized powders and the
venting of gases through pressurized powders. This includes experimental measurements for estimating
DR (damage ratio), ARF (airborne release fraction) and RF (respirable fraction) quantities.
 The present study was performed to address previously indicated problematic factors (i.e. noted
gaps) (US DOE 1994) including,

(1) “Impaction of test powder onto the test chamber ceiling in the original experiments,
(2) Interference of rupture disc fragments with the expelled test powder,
(3) Inconclusive influence of the depth of powder material in the PARE system, and,
(4) An overly conservative stress configuration compared to that realistically expected.” (as summarized
by Moore et al 2019).

 This project was intended for application to all DOE sites with dispersible materials, including
but not limited to gloveboxes, nuclear material storage containers, and TRU waste drums, etc. This could
affect the safety basis documents (e.g. US DOE 2007) and documented safety analyses (e.g. US DOE
2014).

 Definition of the airborne source term
 The airborne source term, S(g), for respirable radioactive aerosol particles has been defined (US
DOE 1994) as “the amount of radioactive material, in grams or curies, released to the air”.
 The airborne source term equation has five linear terms where four of the terms are fractional
(ratio) expressions as summarized (Table 1). Verbatim definitions (US DOE 1994) of the respirable source
term components are given (Table 2).

S: Source Term. “The source term is the amount of radioactive material, in grams or curies, released to

S = MAR x DR x ARF x RF x LPF (US DOE 1994)
MAR: Material-At-Risk. “The material-at-risk is the amount of radionuclides (in grams or curies of
activity for each radionuclide) available to be acted on by a given physical stress.”

i.e. in this study, MAR(g)
DR: Damage Ratio. “The damage ratio is the fraction of the MAR actually impacted by the accident-
generated conditions.”

i.e. in this study, DR = m(impacted, g)/MAR(g)
ARF: Airborne Release Fraction. “The ARF is the coefficient used to estimate the amount of a
radioactive material suspended in air as an aerosol and thus available for transport due to a physical
stresses (sic) from a specific accident.”

i.e. in the study, ARF = m(airborne, g)/m(impacted, g)
RF: Respirable Fraction. “The RF is the fraction of airborne radionuclides as particles that can be
transported through air and inhaled into the human respiratory system and is commonly assumed to
include particles 10-μm Aerodynamic Equivalent Diameter (AED) and less.”

i.e. in this study, RF = m(respirable, g)/m(airborne, g)
LPF = Leak Path Factor: “The LPF is the fraction of the radionuclides in the aerosol transported through
some confinement deposition of filtration mechanism.”

i.e. in this study, LPF = 1.0

 The original PARE system
 Previous studies (Sutter 1983a and Sutter 1983b and Ballinger et al 1987) used a vessel fitted
with rupture disks (Figure 1) to measure the release of test powder at different gas pressures.
 Rupture disks were designed to burst at test pressures of 0.06, 0.12, 0.17, 0.34, 0.69, 1.7, 3.4, and
6.9 MPag. Two different classes of tests were performed with 100 g or 350 g of either TiO2 (titanium
dioxide) or DUO (depleted uranium oxide) powder.
 After a release of test powder, four high volume filter air samplers were operated to implement a
combined air clearance rate of 16 ACH (air changes per hour) through their filters (Figure 2). The aerosol
particle mass distribution was measured with a high-volume cascade impactor.

Research objectives
The research objectives from the project proposal (Moore et al 2019) are:
(1) “The implementation of lessons learned from the original PARE device that affected experimental
measurements.
(2) Inclusion of measurements to address noted gaps in the experiments (DOE 1994).
(3) Improvements of the risk estimate accuracy for pressurized powder venting and pressurized gas
venting through powders.”

 Project scope
From the project proposal (Moore et al 2019):
“The project effort would:
(1) Design and build an improved PARE device,
(2) Incorporate the improved PARE into the existing (Moore et al 2018 and Reeves et al 2018) Los
Alamos RRFMC (Respirable Release Fraction Measurement Chamber).
(3) Perform corrected versions of the HDBK-3010 measurements.
(4) Compare these results with the previous data results.
(5) Utilize nondimensional scaling and engineering correlations to assess conservatism.”

 Principal system components

 This study used a rupture disk (Continental Inc) holder (Figure 5 and Figure 6) for the powder
release tests. The simplified pressure system schematic (Figure 7) illustrates the path of compressed air
through a pressure controller (Alicat Inc, Tucson AZ) to the PARE vessel containing a rupture disk.
Experiments were performed according to a test matrix (Table 8) that combined the independent
parameters of test (rupture) pressure, source mass of test powder, powder density (dependent on material
properties), chamber size (diameter) and powder height.

 DISCUSSION

 ARF dataset
 The ARF data (Figure 19) seem to indicate an increase in the ARF magnitude in this sequence,
from smaller to larger:
    • 4-inch PARE, TiO2 (100 g)
    • 2-inch PARE, TiO2 (36 g)
    • 4-inch PARE, TiO2 (350 g)
    • 2-inch PARE, CeO2 (56 g)
 • The combined US-DOE-HDBK-1994 dataset.
Although one would caution against concluding that adjacent datasets are statistically different, there is
about two orders of magnitude difference between the lowest-valued dataset (4-inch PARE, TiO2 (100 g))
and the highest (DOE 1994).

 RF dataset
 When the five (previously mentioned) experiment groupings are graphed according to measured
RF (Figure 20), the CeO2 results have a different average RF value compared to the TiO2 measurements.

 Individual graphs of the four tested categories
The individual graphs of the four tested categories are also presented (Figure 21 through Figure 28).
 The category “4-inch PARE, TiO2 (350 g)” (Figure 21 and Figure 22) mimicked the experimental
conditions of the original (DOE 1994) dataset. It indicated a trend of increasing ARF but decreasing RF as
the burst pressure increased. All of (Figure 21 and Figure 22) experiments were performed with aluminum
metal rupture disks (Table 16).

The remaining three datasets (below) were initially tested with aluminum metal rupture disks.
   • 4-inch PARE, TiO2 (100 g)
   • 2-inch PARE, TiO2 (36 g)
   • 2-inch PARE, CeO2 (56 g)
 Additional experiments with these remaining three datasets (Table 16) were performed with steel
rupture disk material. The results of the steel rupture disk for ARF and RF measurements may be
interpreted as a general prediction for a class of experimental tests. For example, given a dataset of ARF
and RF values for a given MAR mass over a range of burst pressures, how would a steel rupture disk
compare to the aluminum rupture disk results?
 The steel rupture disks were observed to separate from the disk holder in a clean, circular pattern. The
aluminum disks tended to have residual material in the rupture disk holder (in non-circular patterns).
From visual examination of the numerical results, the difference in the rupture disk material seemed
inconsequential.

 High-speed video files
 All experiments were video-recorded at the moment of disk rupture at two orthogonal axes
directions at a rate of 4000 frames per second. These data files are archived for future access.
