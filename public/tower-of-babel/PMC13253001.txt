Efficacy and limitations of a web-based native adult knee septic arthritis calculator in urban trauma care
Authors: Ryan P. Serbin, Joseph Burger, Susan Odum, Calvin Chandler, Sarah Welch, Noah Di Napoli, Ashley Duncan, Meghan K. Wally, William Lack, Madhav Karunakar, Rachel B. Seymour, Joseph R. Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Septic arthritis of the knee presents significant diagnostic challenges. Traditional diagnostic methods demonstrate limited sensitivity and specificity, whereas delayed intervention risks irreversible joint damage. A web-based septic arthritis calculator offers quantifiable risk assessment, but its performance requires validation.Questions/Purposes:We evaluated (1) diagnostic accuracy of a web-based septic arthritis calculator in a tertiary referral center and (2) optimal risk thresholds for recommending surgical intervention and for confidently excluding septic arthritis.Methods:We retrospectively analyzed 121 consults for suspected septic arthritis of the knee including arthrocentesis in adult patients at a tertiary referral center from 2018 to 2023. Newman criteria served as the diagnostic gold standard for septic arthritis. We calculated risk probabilities using the web-based tool incorporating 6 clinical and laboratory variables. Receiver operating characteristic analysis identified optimal thresholds for surgical intervention and assessed diagnostic performance.Results:The calculator demonstrated excellent discrimination between septic (n = 75) and nonseptic (n = 46) joints (area under the curve of 0.97 [95% confidence interval 0.95–0.99; P < .001). Median calculated probabilities for sepsis were 80% vs 4% for nonseptic cases. Optimal thresholds included 58% for recommending surgical intervention (sensitivity 89%, specificity 98%, positive predictive value 99%) and 24.6% for excluding septic arthritis (sensitivity 96%, specificity 87%, negative predictive value 93%). Both thresholds achieved 93% correct classification.Conclusions:The web-based calculator provides excellent diagnostic accuracy and clinically relevant thresholds derived from ROC analysis: ≥58% for recommending surgical intervention and <24.6% for confidently excluding septic arthritis. These findings support systematic implementation in tertiary referral centers to enhance diagnostic precision and optimize resource allocation, although prospective validation remains essential.Level of Evidence:III.

1. Introduction

1.1. Background

Septic arthritis of the native adult knee requires urgent diagnosis and management because of its substantial morbidity and long-term complications. Patients face a 6-fold higher likelihood of requiring arthroplasty within 15 years, along with chronic pain, recurrent infection, and progressive joint damage.1–4 Furthermore, the increasing health care burden associated with septic arthritis in the United States underscores the importance of accurate and timely diagnosis.5 Current diagnostic methods, such as synovial fluid white blood cell count and polymorphonuclear neutrophil percentage, have limitations in sensitivity and specificity.6

The European Bone and Joint Infection Society (EBJIS) guidelines emphasize the importance of early diagnosis and treatment, recommending intervention within 24 hours of presentation to preserve joint function.7 Although synovial fluid culture remains an important diagnostic tool,8 its clinical utility is limited by the time required for results and potential false negatives, particularly in patients who have received antibiotics.9

These constraints often force orthopaedic surgeons to make critical decisions with incomplete information, potentially leading to unnecessary interventions in patients without septic arthritis. Overtreatment carries substantial risks including unwarranted surgical procedures, prolonged antibiotic exposure, and extended hospitalization.5,10–12 Conversely, the significant morbidity and poor outcomes associated with delayed treatment13 justify early intervention creating a clinical dilemma that demands more precise diagnostic tools. In resource-constrained environments managing multiple competing priorities, this diagnostic uncertainty can significantly affect OR scheduling, bed utilization, and subspecialty consultation patterns.

1.2. Rationale

Given the limitations of current diagnostic methods and the critical need for earlier decision making, there is a clear need for more accurate and timely diagnostic tools. The web-based calculator developed by Holzmeister et al addresses these challenges by integrating multiple clinical and laboratory variables into a quantifiable risk assessment tool14 (www.septicknee.org). This calculator offers particular promise in tertiary settings where rapid, objective risk stratification could enhance clinical decision making. Although Holzmeister et al performed internal validation of the calculator at their developing institution, external validation in an independent patient population is essential before widespread implementation.

Our study evaluates the calculator's performance specifically within a tertiary referral center environment, determines optimal risk thresholds for clinical decision making, and identifies potential limitations. By validating this tool in a high-acuity referral center where definitive diagnoses can be confirmed, we aim to provide orthopaedic surgeons with evidence-based guidance for implementing quantitative risk assessment in their daily practice.

2. Methods

This study was conducted with approval from our institutional review board. The IRB granted a waiver of informed consent and waiver of authorization because of the retrospective nature of the study. We conducted a retrospective analysis of consults for presumed septic joint at an urban tertiary referral center with a large catchment area from 2018 to 2023. Presumed septic joint was defined as any native adult knee presenting with acute pain and swelling that warranted orthopaedic consultation and subsequent arthrocentesis for suspected infection. Eligible cases were identified through our department's daily orthopaedic consultation tracking system, which maintains a comprehensive record of all orthopaedic consultations within the previous 24 hours. This standardized tracking system ensured complete capture of all potential septic arthritis consultations during the study period. The study period beginning in 2018 coincided with the initiation of our institutional musculoskeletal infection registry. Additional inclusion criteria included age ≥18 years and underwent knee arthrocentesis. The study's focus on adults (≥18 years) aligns with the calculator's original development cohort and reflects the distinct clinical presentation and diagnostic considerations of pediatric septic arthritis, for which this tool has not been validated. Patients with knee surgery within 3 months of presentation were excluded as postsurgical changes could confound the calculator's clinical variables, particularly pain and effusion assessment. Prosthetic joints were similarly excluded as they represent a distinct clinical entity. These exclusions maintain consistency with the calculator's original development cohort. We used Newman criteria for definitive septic arthritis diagnosis, which includes positive culture, intraoperative purulence, persistence or recurrence of symptoms, or organism isolated elsewhere (Fig. 1). Although synovial fluid culture is considered a gold standard, Newman criteria were used as they provide a more comprehensive diagnostic framework that accounts for culture-negative cases, particularly in patients who received antibiotics before culture. Synovial fluid cultures were followed for 14 days according to institutional protocol. For each included case, we applied the web-based septic arthritis calculator developed by Holzmeister et al.14

Patient selection flow diagram for septic knee arthritis study.

The web-based calculator (https://septicknee.org/) was developed to assist clinical decision making after initial evaluation and joint aspiration. The calculator was developed through multivariable logistic regression analysis of 281 native adult knee aspirations. Their original validation demonstrated excellent discrimination (area under the ROC curve [AUC] = 0.942). The calculator incorporates 6 variables found to have independent associations with septic arthritis: synovial fluid white blood cell ≥30,000 (odds ratio [OR] 90.8), positive Gram stain (OR 21.5), pain duration >2 days (OR 6.9), history of septic arthritis (OR 5.0), clinical effusion (OR 4.8), and presence of crystals in synovial fluid (OR 0.1, protective). The calculator generates a probability score (0%–100%) estimating the likelihood of septic arthritis for each case. For instance, if the calculator returns a 60% risk score, this indicates an estimated 60% probability of septic arthritis given that patient's specific clinical and laboratory findings. This probability can then guide decisions about the need for urgent surgical intervention or further diagnostic testing. For example, a high probability score might support proceeding with surgical intervention while awaiting final culture results, whereas a very low score might support watchful waiting in appropriate clinical contexts.

Our primary outcome measure was the diagnosis of septic arthritis based on Newman criteria. We collected data on demographics, comorbidities, admission lengths, and the specific diagnostic criteria met for each presumed septic arthritis case. Following Newman's original criteria, purulence was defined as thick, cloudy, purulent-appearing fluid identified at the time of surgery. Data were systematically extracted using standardized abstraction forms with regular quality monitoring throughout the collection process.

2.1. Data management and statistical analysis

Study data were sourced from electronic health records and were stored and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted locally.15 All statistical analyses were conducted under the direction of our biostatistician coauthor (S.O.) using SAS/STAT software, Version 9.4 of the SAS System for Windows (Copyright, SAS Institute Inc. SAS and all other SAS Institute Inc. product or service names are registered trademarks or trademarks of SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina). Standard descriptive statistics were reported including measures of central tendency, variance, and frequencies and proportions. We first used the Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test to establish whether the calculator generated significantly different probability estimates between septic and nonseptic groups, confirming basic discriminative ability.

A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to identify the optimal diagnostic threshold for the calculator's risk score, or predictive probability value, that maximizes its ability to distinguish between septic and nonseptic arthritis.16–18 This optimal threshold represents the predicted probability value from the calculator that, both clinically and statistically, most accurately confirms septic arthritis (prioritizing specificity) or rules it out (prioritizing sensitivity). The diagnostic accuracy of various thresholds were evaluated by calculating the sensitivity, specificity, positive, and negative predictive value.16–18

Depending on the diagnostic goals, various statistical criteria or metrics can inform the selection of the optimal diagnostic threshold, or cutpoint.16–18 For instance, if the goal is to balance the sensitivity and specificity of the calculator, the Youden Index may be more informative than the correct classification rate. Conversely, if the goal is to prioritize correct classification, the correct classification rate might be more appropriate than the Youden Index.16–18 Given the “costs” associated with both false positives and false negatives, we evaluated the diagnostic accuracy based on 4 criteria: (1) Correct Classification—Maximizing Sensitivity, (2) Correct Classification—Maximizing Specificity, (3) Euclidean Distance, and (4) Youden Index.16–18 For more information on the criteria used to determine the optimal cutpoint, refer to Table 1.

3. Results

From our initial cohort of 150 knee consultations, 121 cases met inclusion criteria. Exclusions were based on prosthetic joints (n = 15), recent surgery within 3 months (n = 10), and incomplete data (n = 4). Of the included cases, 75 were confirmed as septic arthritis and 46 were negative based on Newman criteria (Table 2).

The web-based septic arthritis calculator demonstrated excellent discrimination between septic and nonseptic adult knee joints, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.97 (95% confidence interval 0.95–0.99; P < .001). The Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test revealed a statistically significant difference in calculator probabilities between septic and nonseptic groups (P < .001). For septic cases (n = 75), the median risk score was 80% (interquartile range 69%–100%), whereas for nonseptic cases (n = 46), the median risk score was 4% (interquartile range 1%–10%).

ROC analysis identified multiple potential thresholds for clinical decision making (Fig. 3). On the ROC curve, points are labeled with 2 numbers. The first number is the calculator's risk threshold—for example, 0.799 means we consider any case with a calculated risk above 79.9% as potentially septic. The second number shows how well the calculator performs at that threshold—for example, 0.993 means it correctly classifies 99.3% of cases when using that cutoff. These paired values help identify optimal thresholds for clinical decision making.

Receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve showing calculator performance. Points are labeled with pairs of numbers where the first represents the probability threshold (e.g., 0.799 = 79.9% risk threshold) and the second represents the corresponding probability value (e.g., 0.993 = 99.3% test performance at that threshold). “C” marks the 24.6% threshold point achieving the highest correct classification rate. “C D Y” marks the 58.0% threshold where minimum distance to perfect performance, and Youden index maximization converged.

Detailed threshold optimization analysis demonstrated convergence of multiple statistical criteria at the 58% cutpoint, including Euclidean distance minimization, Youden Index maximization, and specificity optimization, providing statistical validation for this surgical threshold selection (Table 1). The 24.6% threshold optimized sensitivity for disease exclusion. At the 58% threshold, the sensitivity was 89% and specificity was 98% meaning that 89% of septic cases would be correctly identified for surgery whereas only 2% of nonseptic cases would undergo unnecessary procedures. The 24.6% exclusion threshold provided sensitivity of 96% and specificity of 87%. Both thresholds achieved the highest correct classification rate of 93%. Complete classification performance across all risk thresholds is provided in Supplemental Table S1, http://links.lww.com/OTAI/A136.

We identified potential limitations in the calculator's performance for certain patient populations. In 2 immunocompromised patients with culture-positive septic arthritis, the calculator yielded unexpectedly low risk scores of 1.4% and 9.5%. In addition, a patient with Alzheimer disease and septic arthritis had a risk score of only 24.5%, possibly because of unreliable pain duration assessment.

4. Discussion

Native septic arthritis of the knee remains one of the most challenging diagnostic dilemmas in orthopaedic medicine, with substantial morbidity including chronic pain, functional limitation, recurrent infection, and a sixfold increased likelihood of requiring arthroplasty within 15 years.1,3 In tertiary referral centers managing complex patient populations, diagnostic uncertainty compounds the already significant health care burden associated with septic arthritis in the United States.5 The diagnostic performance (AUC 0.97) of this web-based septic arthritis calculator in this high-acuity environment provides validation and evidence-based thresholds for time-sensitive clinical decisions.

The clinical application of these thresholds provides objective guidance for time-sensitive decisions. Patients with calculator scores ≥58% should be considered for urgent surgical intervention given the 98% specificity and 99% positive predictive value at this threshold. Conversely, patients with scores <24.6% can be managed with close observation, serial examinations, and watchful waiting while culture results are pending, given the 96% sensitivity and 93% negative predictive value for excluding septic arthritis. Patients with intermediate scores (24.6%–58%) require enhanced clinical correlation and may benefit from repeat arthrocentesis, infectious disease consultation, or empiric antibiotic therapy while monitoring clinical response. Detailed clinical application examples demonstrating threshold utilization across various patient scenarios are provided in the Supplemental Material, http://links.lww.com/OTAI/A135.

Although we validated this tool in a tertiary environment with readily available definitive care, the calculator's objective risk stratification may have broader utility across diverse clinical settings.7 Future studies should evaluate implementation in community hospitals and emergency departments with varying levels of orthopaedic coverage.

The substantial morbidity and poor outcomes associated with septic arthritis13 justify early intervention, but the calculator's ability to stratify risk objectively may help optimize the balance between timely treatment and resource conservation. Swan et al emphasized the value of systematic synovial fluid assessment in joint disease diagnosis,8 and automated integration of such assessments with validated prediction tools could significantly enhance diagnostic accuracy while improving resource allocation across hospital systems.

This study has several strengths. We performed external validation at an independent tertiary center, confirming the calculator's excellent discriminative ability (AUC 0.97) in a population distinct from the original development cohort. Our use of Newman criteria as the diagnostic gold standard accounts for culture-negative cases, and all analyses were conducted under biostatistician direction.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, our single-center retrospective design limits immediate generalizability and precludes assessment of whether calculator implementation would change clinical decision making. Second, our sample size (n = 121) is smaller than the original development cohort (n = 281), although sufficient for external validation. Third, the 62% septic arthritis prevalence reflects tertiary referral patterns; our 99% positive predictive value at the 58% threshold would decrease in lower-prevalence settings, emphasizing the need for local validation. Fourth, the calculator demonstrated reduced sensitivity in immunocompromised patients—a transplant recipient with culture-positive septic arthritis yielded only 9.5% calculated risk because of blunted inflammatory response. Finally, subjective variables such as pain duration may be unreliable in cognitively impaired patients, as illustrated by a patient with Alzheimer disease who had a low calculated risk despite confirmed septic arthritis. Clinical judgment must supersede calculator results in these populations.

5. Conclusion

Despite the diagnostic challenges inherent in septic arthritis,6,18 the web-based calculator demonstrates exceptional discriminative ability in the complex tertiary care environment. By providing evidence-based thresholds for surgical intervention and confident disease exclusion, this tool offers surgeons a quantifiable complement to clinical judgment while addressing documented limitations of traditional diagnostic approaches.2 Maintaining awareness of performance limitations—particularly in immunocompromised populations—remains essential for optimal implementation. Future investigations should focus on multicenter validation across diverse care systems and implementation studies examining real-world impact on clinical decision making, patient outcomes, and health care resource utilization.
