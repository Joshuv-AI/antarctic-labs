A systematic review of heterogeneity in clinical trial design for anal cancer radiotherapy-immunotherapy combinations
Authors: Robert J Samuel, Adel Samson, Duncan C Gilbert, Richard Adams, Andrew Renehan, Natalie Cook, David Sebag-Montefiore, Sarah Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLocalised anal squamous cell carcinoma (ASCC) is treated with radical chemoradiotherapy, which achieves durable disease control in most patients. However, treatment failure is stage-dependent, with approximately 25–30% of patients with locally advanced disease relapsing after treatment, supporting investigation of novel approaches such as radiotherapy-immunotherapy (RT-IO) combinations. Combining radiotherapy (RT) with immunotherapy (IO) shows promise, with multiple trials investigating different combinations. This review evaluates trial design for RT-IO trials in ASCC. A systematic review was conducted on PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, and EudraCT databases to identify interventional clinical trials evaluating RT combined with immune-modulating treatments for ASCC. The review was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD42023384068). Information on trial design, patient selection, and treatment regimens was collected. Twelve trials were identified, with three reporting results for a combined total of 69 patients. Across all trials, 14 different IO regimens were used before, during, and after RT. Substantial heterogeneity was observed in patient selection, statistical design, chemotherapy, and RT regimens. Data on exploratory and translational analyses was available for six trials. No trials used biomarkers for enrolment, although INTERACT-ION used cHPV-DNA to guide post-induction treatment allocation. Full trial comparisons were hindered by protocol publication and commercial sensitivities. The diversity in IO regimens and timing relative to RT offers valuable data on RT-IO combinations for ASCC. However, heterogeneity in trial design and translational analysis will limit comparisons between trials. Two possible solutions to improve comparisons are a core minimal translational component and federated learning.Keywords: Anal Cancer, Radiotherapy, Clinical Trials, Immunotherapy

Background

Although anal squamous cell carcinoma (ASCC) is rare, with about 54,000 cases worldwide in 2022, its incidence is rising and projected to reach 93,600 by 2050 [1], [2], [3]. Most ASCCs are human papillomavirus (HPV)-related, mainly HPV-16, accounting for >90% of cases [4]. Concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CRT) with fluorouracil (5-FU) or capecitabine (CAP) and mitomycin-C (MMC), given to the primary tumour and pelvic/inguinal nodes, is the standard for localised ASCC, established by the first generation of phase III trials in the 1990s [5], [6], [7]. A second generation of phase III trials, testing intensification of treatment with either the addition of neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy or alteration of the concurrent chemotherapy regimen, failed to change the standard of care [8], [9], [10].

While modern RT techniques have reduced toxicities, the CRT backbone and outcomes—especially for advanced disease—have remained largely unchanged over the past 25 years [11]. In 1997, the EORTC-22861 trial reported ~56% 5-year overall survival (OS) for T3/4 and/or N1–3 disease, yet in 2023 the American National Cancer Database analyses showed ~64% 5-year OS for T3N0 and ~ 55–58% OS for T3–4N+ disease—reinforcing that increasing T stage, nodal involvement, and HPV/p16-negative status continue to define the poorest-prognosis group [6], [12]. Novel strategies are needed to improve outcomes for this patient population.

Data from metastatic ASCC and other HPV-associated tumours suggest immunotherapy (IO) could be this strategy. The InterAACT2 trial combined 1st line retifanlimab (an anti–programmed cell death protein 1 [PD-1] antibody) with carboplatin-paclitaxel in metastatic or locally recurrent ASCC, showing improved progression-free survival compared to carboplatin-paclitaxel alone (9.3 months [95% CI 7·5–11·3] versus 7.4 months (7·1–7·7), hazard ratio 0·63 [95% CI 0·47–0·84], one-sided p = 0·0006), with positive results also seen with IO monotherapy in the 2nd line [13], [14], [15]. First-line IO trials in other advanced HPV-associated cancers have demonstrated clinically meaningful overall survival benefits—notably KEYNOTE-048 in recurrent/metastatic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) and KEYNOTE-826 in persistent/recurrent/metastatic cervical cancer—supporting global adoption of IO-based systemic therapy for these diseases [16], [17].

Preclinical evidence supports combining radiotherapy (RT) and IO, showing increased tumour antigen presentation, activation of the cGAS-STING pathway, and T-cell activation [18], [19], [20]. The PACIFIC trial provided clinical evidence that RT-IO combinations can improve survival in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [21]. Together, these data provided the rationale for evaluating RT-IO combinations in ASCC, although these trials were necessarily pragmatic in design due to limited ASCC-specific preclinical evidence.

However, results from first-generation RT-IO trials in HPV-associated cancers have been mixed. For HNSCC, JAVELIN100 and KEYNOTE-412 found no benefit from adding neoadjuvant, concurrent, and adjuvant IO to CRT [22], [23]. For cervical cancer, CALLA found no benefit from IO with CRT. However, KEYNOTE-A18 showed benefit in locally advanced cervical cancer despite a similar IO regimen to CALLA, leading to US approval [24], [25].

The importance of trial design is clear from the first two generations of ASCC trials. Aside from ACCORD03, RT dose was not investigated and varied between trials. As shown in Table 1, differences in patient selection, disease stages, RT techniques, target volumes, and outcomes hinder comparisons of RT doses for the same disease stage. As a result, despite 30 years of high-quality data, the optimal RT dose for ASCC remains unknown, leading to variability in practice worldwide. Notably, even among modern intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) and volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT) anal cancer contouring guidelines, including North American, UK, Nordic and Australasian guidance, elective nodal target volumes differ in mesorectal extent, external iliac coverage, cranial borders, ischiorectal fossa inclusion and inguinal volumes, adding a further source of RT heterogeneity [26], [27], [28], [29].

ASCC is less common than HNSCC and cervical cancer, which partly explains why dedicated biology programmes and trial pipelines have developed more slowly. Variations in treatment, such as radiotherapy dose and outcomes, demonstrate that these cancers are not interchangeable, emphasising the need for ASCC-specific trial design and evaluation. Furthermore, because CRT is the primary curative approach across most stages of ASCC, and previous studies have been heterogeneous, designing rigorous and comparable RT-IO trials for ASCC is essential. If these trials yield negative results, as seen in HNSCC, heterogeneity in key trial aspects will hinder our ability to compare or combine findings to draw meaningful conclusions for clinical practice and future research. Conversely, if similar trials produce differing outcomes, as in cervical cancer, it may be challenging to determine which patients benefit from the addition of IO. Should multiple RT-IO regimens demonstrate promise, we might end up with several recommended options for comparable ASCC patients without knowing which is optimal. Although this issue is not unique to ASCC, its relative rarity means fewer trials to establish the best RT-IO regimens, making robust comparison even more crucial.

To support the design and interpretation of future RT-IO trials in ASCC, we present this systematic review for investigators designing RT-IO ASCC studies, cooperative trial groups, and translational researchers. The purpose is to: (1) identify major sources of heterogeneity that limit cross-trial comparison and propose pragmatic measures to enable valid comparisons in future studies; (2) assess the IO regimens used, including agents, mechanisms, and timing relative to radiotherapy; (3) compare translational sampling and analysis plans; and (4) summarise innovative trial designs used in this setting and evaluate how they could be leveraged to optimise future RT-IO combination studies in ASCC.

Methods

Search strategy

The systematic review was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023384068). Interventional clinical trials (Phases I to IV inclusive) of metastatic or localised ASCC, or trials including other tumour types in which separate analysis of >5 anal cancer patients was possible, that combined RT with treatments to modulate or stimulate the immune system were included. Non-interventional trials, trials where a study protocol has been registered but the study withdrawn or never occurred, or trials investigating the immunomodulatory effects of the current standard of care treatment, where the principal recognised mechanism of action of this treatment is not immunomodulatory, were excluded. Trial registries ClinicalTrials.gov and EudraCT were searched on 27 November 2025. Initial search terms included MeSH and synonyms of anal cancer AND immunotherapy. Using these terms, the search did not identify several known eligible trials. Therefore, search terms were simplified to anal cancer and its associated terms/MeSH headings where search engines allowed. PubMed was searched using the keywords “anal cancer” AND “immunotherapy”, with results filtered for clinical trials only. Trials that met the inclusion criteria from trial registry databases were searched on PubMed for more detailed and up-to-date publications. Appendix A shows the inclusion/exclusion criteria.

Data extraction and quality assessment

A standardised data extraction sheet was used to record information from included trials. Two reviewers (RJS and SB) independently screened studies for inclusion and extracted data, with any disagreements resolved by consensus between the two reviewers. Data was extracted from trial protocols, publications, and trial registration websites. This was enhanced by undertaking a grey literature search for the trial protocol when it was not published or available on the registry website. If it could not be found, the trial's corresponding author or principal investigator was emailed to request it, recognising that this may have limited success due to commercial sensitivities. Extracted data included: cancer type, stage of ASCC disease, geographical location, and biomarker selection, to determine patient populations; IO intervention and mechanism of action, IO regimen in relation to CRT, chemotherapy regimen and RT regimen, to determine treatment regimens used; phase of trial, number of experimental arms, randomisation status, (proposed) sample size, statistical design, primary outcome(s), secondary outcome(s), and translational/exploratory analysis performed, to determine design characteristics and translational plans; and trial status (not yet recruiting, recruiting, active not recruiting, published) and protocol availability, to summarise data availability for extraction.

Quality assessment and statistical analysis

The objectives of this review were to characterise the key components of RT-IO trial design in ASCC and identify sources of clinical and methodological heterogeneity that affect cross-trial interpretability. Accordingly, this review was not intended to quantify the efficacy or outcomes of RT-IO ASCC trials. Given the authors' prior knowledge of this area, most of the trials included were expected not to have yet published any results, and those that had would be from small sample sizes. Meta-analysis of a small number of trials with small sample sizes increases the risk of bias and therefore was not performed [30]. Quality assessment tools such as RoB2 and ROBINS-I use outcomes and effect sizes in their scoring and do not cover areas such as trial design, translational analysis plans, treatment regimens, or patient selection in sufficient detail to meet the objectives of this review. Where trial outcomes were discussed, this was descriptive and contextual rather than comparative.

Study status

INTERACT-ION is the only RT-IO trial in ASCC to have published mature efficacy results to date [31]. It is a single arm phase II study evaluating induction modified DCF chemotherapy combined with the anti-PD-1 antibody ezabenlimab, followed by response-adapted CRT in stage III disease. The primary endpoint of clinical complete response (cCR) at 40 weeks was met, with a cCR rate of 77.8% (90% CI 66.5–86.7). Two other trials have published preliminary results. BrUOG276 used a Listeria-based therapeutic cancer vaccine targeting HPV-16 E7, with nine patients completing treatment, of whom eight were progression-free at the time of analysis, with a median follow-up of 42 months and an acceptable safety profile reported [32]. NCT05060471 has published safety and biomarker analysis from the first five patients recruited, with all patients alive and disease-free at the time of analysis, with a median follow-up of 21.8 months and an acceptable toxicity profile [33]. NCI-EA2165, CORINTH, and RADIANCE have completed recruitment but are awaiting results. All other trials are currently recruiting.

Trial design: phase, patient selection, endpoints, randomisation, and statistical and innovative design features.

Of the 12 trials identified, two were phase I or phase I/II, seven phase II, two phase III, and one was an observational cohort trial. All trials that met the review's inclusion criteria exclusively investigated ASCC.

Apart from SPARTANA, which investigated metastatic or recurrent disease, all trials were for patients with local disease. Table 3 shows that five different definitions of locally advanced disease were used across the eight trials. In localised disease trials, all but two (NCT04929028 and TIRANUS) investigated only locally advanced disease; NCT04929028 included HIV+ early-stage disease, but only patients with locally advanced disease received IO in this trial. BrUOG276 and TIRANUS used American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC)/Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) staging version 7, while all others used version 8.

A wide variety of primary and secondary endpoints were used, likely reflecting the different Phases of each clinical trial, with Phase I trials primarily focused on safety and Phase III trials on disease-free and progression-free survival. The primary endpoint and timing of assessment differed across studies. Overall survival is the only endpoint that was assessed across all trials.

Five trials (42%) were randomised (including one phase I/II trial), although no clear information regarding the intention to compare arms was provided for four of these. Across phase II trials, only 2/7 trials included randomisation, with one of these being designed with the intention of a formal comparison between the experimental and control arms.

Information on statistical design was limited across the trials, with no information available for eight of the twelve trials. One study had a mismatch between design and analysis, one was an observational cohort, one used the A'Hern single-arm design, and one was a comparative phase II design to compare survival. No trials incorporated biomarker selection for enrolment, except for NCT04929028 which investigated HIV-positive patients only.

The CORINTH trial used a planned sequential approach to safety evaluation by recruiting sequentially into two Phase Ib cohorts. Early stopping rules were provided for safety; no statistical details were provided regarding the design and decision-making. If early safety data from the first cohort of six patients receiving pembrolizumab at week 4 of CRT were satisfactory, the next cohort would receive pembrolizumab starting at week 1 of CRT. Following this, 32 patients were recruited at the most appropriate dosing regimen.

INTERACT-ION incorporated a response-adapted CRT strategy after induction chemo-immunotherapy (C-IO). All patients received 2 months of C-IO, and those without progressive disease received a further month before response assessment. CRT adaptation was based on a composite “major response” definition requiring (i) radiological response ≥30% by RECIST, (ii) pathological complete or near-complete response on biopsy (≤10% viable tumour cells/tumour bed), and (iii) biological complete response (clearance of circulating HPV DNA). Patients meeting these criteria proceeded with a de-escalated involved-node IMRT (INRT) approach, delivering 45 Gy to initially involved nodes rather than the standard 59.4 Gy, whereas others received standard concurrent CRT [31].

Translational analysis

Data outlining translational analyses was available for six trials, with timepoint data available for four of these. Fig. 2 shows all translational trials that had available data on timepoints. All trials collect blood and tissue for research purposes before starting treatment, with two also collecting blood and/or tissue at recurrence. Planned analyses differ across trials, with at least five investigating cHPV-DNA and at least four collecting peripheral blood mononuclear cells. Although not assessed in all trials, cHPV-DNA is likely being evaluated for its prognostic value before treatment, as a tool to assess treatment response, or as a biomarker for disease recurrence. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells are being collected for various immune-based techniques, such as immunophenotyping, but further details were not available for any of the trials.

Discussion

This systematic review identified 12 RT-IO trials in ASCC, with results available from only three. Across these studies, there was substantial heterogeneity in trial design, treatment regimens, and translational analysis, limiting cross-trial comparison and interpretation. These findings suggest that a key challenge for future RT-IO studies in ASCC is not only to identify effective combinations, but to design trials that generate interpretable and comparable evidence across the field.

Patient selection

Variations in how locally advanced disease is defined, along with updates to AJCC/UICC TNM staging, complicate comparisons between trials. Most trials used version 8, with two trials using version 7. The latest version 9 has refined Stages IIB and IIIA based on differences in 5-year survival rates, but both categories include heterogeneous patients, many of whom achieve a cure with standard CRT [34]. Improved, biology-informed risk stratification is crucial for future trial optimisation.

Trial endpoints

Variation in outcomes and endpoints will hinder our ability to compare these trials and has been previously addressed in CRT trials for ASCC. Core Outcome Research Measures in Anal Cancer (CORMAC) was an international collaboration using the Core Outcome Measures in Effectiveness Trials method that resulted in 19 outcomes across four domains: disease activity, survival, toxicity, and life impact [35]. These outcomes are a minimum rather than a prescriptive outcome set, and the use of CORMAC does not limit the use of outcomes specific to IO trials, such as immune-related patient-reported outcomes or iRECIST. Although more relevant for phase II and III trials, investigators should consider using CORMAC for future ASCC RT-IO trials.

Statistical design

Perhaps owing to a lack of available complete protocols or published results, there was a distinct lack of information on the statistical design of many of these trials. Only two had sufficient statistical detail regarding the design and sample size. The two trials, for which a protocol or publication was available with no statistical design referenced, were phase Ib/II trials, with a limited sample size in one. This lack of detail reflects the need for a considered statistical design in phase I radiotherapy-drug combination trials to improve evidence generation, as well as the need for consistent reporting in early-phase clinical trials [36], [37]. Given the rarity of ASCC, the sequential safety evaluation in CORINTH is prudent, albeit without a formal statistical design. Safety issues have arisen in comparable RT-IO trials for other cancers in the pelvis, with grade 3 toxicity in the first-dose cohort of the PLUMMB trial for bladder cancer leading to a reduced RT dose for subsequent cohorts [38]. The pharmaceutical industry's interest and investment in developing treatments for ASCC could be threatened if the initial safety results are unfavourable. This interest is especially fragile compared to other, more common, and potentially more profitable cancers.

Immunotherapy regimens

As expected, most trials investigated drugs that target PD-1/PD-L1, with a therapeutic vaccine, an anti-TIGIT antibody, and pre−/probiotics also investigated. Although different types of IO will interact with RT differently, even within PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, the exact mechanism may be important. This is supported by divergent results between comparable trials in cervical cancer, but these differences could not be explored mechanistically because the relevant translational analyses were lacking [24], [25], [39], [40]. When cross-trial comparisons are undertaken, they should be approached with caution and require a critical mass of sufficiently comparable clinical and translational data, along with a pre-specified, rigorous statistical analysis plan — reinforcing the importance of embedding translational analyses in ASCC trials wherever feasible.

In the absence of robust ASCC-specific preclinical models, RT-IO trial design has necessarily been empirical and pragmatic, drawing on general radiobiology and experience from other disease sites. Preclinical data support the integration of neoadjuvant, concurrent, and adjuvant IO with RT but this has not translated into the clinic [41], [42], [43]. Adjuvant-only IO is effective after CRT in lung and oesophageal cancer, whereas concurrent IO did not improve HNSCC outcomes (possibly reflecting CRT-induced lymphopenia) [21], [22], [23], [44]. Even within adjuvant IO, timing may be important - patients in the PACIFIC trial who started IO within 14 days after CRT had better outcomes than those who started >14 days [45]. This review found diverse IO timing in RT-IO ASCC trials, including neoadjuvant, concurrent, and adjuvant combinations.

Radiotherapy regimens

Differences in RT regimens highlight worldwide variability in clinical practice, affecting IO efficacy through variations in volume and dose. INTERACT-ION showed that response-adapted INRT can reduce irradiated fields, resulting in lower rates of severe lymphopenia, though the study was small and non-randomised [31]. Preclinical and human data support further research to reduce irradiated volumes in RT-IO strategies [46], [47].

Given the impact of immune-cell tumour infiltration, irradiating tumour-draining lymph nodes may hamper RT-IO synergy—a mechanism implicated in negative HNSCC trials [48]. Variation in contouring guidelines results in differences in elective pelvic volumes for ASCC RT [49]. Given that these trials were performed in eight different countries, the guideline used likely differs between trials and is relevant to interpreting heterogeneity in outcomes. At least three trials in this review included RTQA and it is recognised that there is a lack of RTQA in Phase III clinical trials [50]. RT-IO trial protocols should specify which contouring guideline was followed and include detailed RT dose and volume information where deviations occur and should include RTQA when feasible.

Lower RT dose to elective nodes from brachytherapy compared to standard external beam radiation may, in part, have contributed to the success of KEYNOTE-A18 [51]. There is growing interest in other innovative radiotherapy techniques for ASCC, including risk-adapted target delineation, online adaptive radiotherapy, MRI-guided radiotherapy, and proton therapy. These techniques aim to reduce unnecessary normal tissue irradiation through improved target visualisation, margin reduction, or steep dose gradients, but prospective data in RT-IO ASCC remain limited [52]. The DACG-II trial uses a bone-sparing algorithm for RT planning to reduce pelvic insufficiency fractures in ASCC, which may further reduce treatment-associated lymphopenia and could be considered for future RT-IO trials [53].

Chemotherapy regimens

Evidence from other tumour sites suggests a negative correlation between treatment-related lymphopenia and IO efficacy, a consideration relevant to both the chemotherapy and RT components of RT-IO trial design [54], [55]. In this review, some ASCC trials allowed a choice between MMC or CisP. In the ACT2 trial, although survival outcomes were similar, higher haematological toxicity was seen with MMC versus CisP, specifically impacting white blood cells [8]. The choice of CisP or MMC may affect outcomes in these trials, given the potential synergistic relationship between CisP and IO [56]. Higher haematological toxicities are also likely in trials that use two doses of MMC or CisP rather than one, with some trials allowing either option. Given the equivalence of concurrent 5-FU/MMC and CAP/MMC, it is understandable why many trials allowed for a choice of fluoropyrimidine. However, when integrated with IO this may be important, given the higher haematological toxicities from 5-FU [57]. These observations suggest that treatment-related lymphopenia is a recurring mechanistic consideration across both chemotherapy and radiotherapy components of RT-IO trial design, rather than an isolated modality-specific effect. The GI22–588 trial combined IO with concurrent carboplatin and paclitaxel in early-stage ASCC. While this approach may reduce gastrointestinal toxicity during RT and represents a biologically plausible partner for IO, it introduces additional heterogeneity in chemotherapy that should be considered when comparing outcomes across these trials. In the future, RT-IO trials should aim for tighter chemotherapy protocols or consider appropriate statistical design where chemotherapy differences are likely to impact the primary trial outcome.

Translational analysis

RT-IO models help generate hypotheses but face challenges that limit their use as surrogates for human outcomes. While preclinical models of ASCC are emerging, none have yet tested RT-IO combinations. Therefore, translational analysis from these trials is vital. There was considerable variation in the types of analyses planned and the timepoints at which samples were collected. A “minimal translational component” for all trials, such as the CORMAC core outcome set, could be used in future trials. Most patients undergoing CRT for ASCC have weekly blood samples during treatment and appointments at 3 and 6 months post-treatment. Translational timepoints should align with standard clinical visits, particularly when treatment decisions need to be made, such as at the end of CRT or upon recurrence. In translational blood samples, various processing options are available, ranging from cost-effective, easily stored options, such as plasma for cHPV-DNA, to more costly ones requiring specialised laboratory facilities, such as PBMCs for immunophenotyping. Both the number of timepoints and type of processing in this recommendation should not be so prohibitive as to hinder the development of new trials. Fig. 3 suggests a possible “minimal translational component” for RT-IO ASCC trials, recognising that a structured process to reach international consensus among ASCC researchers is required to develop this idea further. This could also be applied to future RT-drug combinations outside of IO, such as hypoxic-modifying agents. Many sample biobanks exist across countries, but knowledge of what has been collected and when is limited. Collating this data could serve as a first step toward developing this further.

Biomarkers

Although many trials investigated biomarkers as part of their translational work, only INTERACT-ION used a biomarker (cHPV-DNA) as part of its criteria to assign CRT treatment. It is unclear whether any trial had an “integrated” biomarker – one used to test specific hypotheses with a defined objective and a statistical analysis plan [58]. An integrated biomarker selected for a specific IO treatment needs good analytical and clinical utility [59]. Although data suggests PD-L1 from diagnostic tissue is prognostic for CRT in ASCC, heterogeneity in evaluation methods and the cut-off for positivity hinder comparisons and reduce its clinical utility [60], [61]. This is likely an issue for other translational work arising from these trials [61]. Tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and cHPV-DNA are other biomarkers of interest in ASCC with similar heterogeneity in evaluation methods [62], [63]. Collating techniques and cut-offs from published data in ASCC could serve as a first step toward reducing translational heterogeneity in future trials.

Recommendations

In ASCC, the rarity of the disease makes pooled international data particularly useful for developing and validating more precise risk stratification. The ARCAD Advanced Colorectal Cancer Database demonstrates the benefits of this approach, resulting in numerous publications and nearly 18,400 patients [64]. However, data-sharing limitations, especially across jurisdictions, hinder centralised pooling of individual data in rare cancers. Federated learning offers a potential solution, enabling multi-centre analysis without transferring patient data.

This approach has already been implemented in ASCC through atomCAT, which uses a federated/distributed learning model where participating centres keep patient-level data locally, run a common analysis pipeline “behind the firewall,” and share only model parameters or summary statistics for central aggregation and external validation—thus enabling multicentre prognostic model development while maintaining local data custody and governance [65]. The same infrastructure could be expanded to establish a federated RT-IO trial database, enabling pooled analyses across trials without requiring physical data sharing.

Alphanumeric TNM data and chemotherapy regimens are documented clinically and often form part of data collection forms for clinical trials. Radiotherapy parameters and key translational variables could similarly be harmonised within a federated framework. Using a pooled database, the unintentional heterogeneity in patient selection, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy regimens observed in this review could be leveraged to assess their impact on IO efficacy.

The considerable variability in the type and timing of IO could be efficiently incorporated into a master protocol [66]. In the RT-IO setting, a master protocol could allow differing regimens with the same drugs or different drugs to be tested in direct comparison with each other, regardless of phase of trial. Master protocols have been successfully used in ASCC-RT trials - the platform personalising anal cancer radiotherapy dose (PLATO) trial incorporates ACT3, 4, and 5, investigating different doses of RT stratified for risk of treatment failure based on TNM staging [67].

PLATO is the only integrated ASCC trial platform that prospectively addresses both radiotherapy dose de-escalation (ACT4) and escalation (ACT5) within a harmonised framework, and the mature ACT4/ACT5 readouts will effectively define the RT backbone—across both target volumes (GTV-boost and elective CTV)—to which future IO strategies can be applied. Early ACT5 data presented at ASCO GI 2026 reported broadly similar 6-month MRI complete response rates across standard and dose-escalated IMRT arms (~65–67%), providing an early indication that RT escalation may not improve early tumour response and that a standard-dose regimen may be the most scalable backbone for IO intensification [68].

Master-protocol approaches from other disease sites, such as CONCORDE in NSCLC, also illustrate how multiple novel agents can be compared against a common RT backbone, with embedded translational sampling and RTQA [69]. A similar design could be used in a Phase I ASCC master protocol to evaluate multiple agents, acknowledging that the trials identified here were run in different countries. International collaboration would be required to run this type of complex study.

Strengths and limitations

While systematic reviews on efficacy for RT-IO trials for other tumour types have been published, to our knowledge, this is the first systematic review evaluating different aspects of trial design for RT-IO combination trials [70]. The heterogeneity observed in this review likely extends to RT-IO trials in other tumour types, and suggestions to reduce heterogeneity and improve comparisons in ASCC may be applicable across many tumour types. However, there are several limitations to this study. The lack of protocol publications and full trial protocols hindered our ability to compare them fully, particularly for translational analysis. At least three protocols were not available due to commercial sensitivities. The poor functionality of the search interface on ClinicalTrials.gov and EudraCT has been previously recognised [71]. The minimum level of detail required by ClinicalTrials.gov and other trial databases is insufficient for adequate trial comparison. When contacted, investigators for these trials were happy to share more information, demonstrating that it would be feasible for trial databases to require greater granularity in trial registration. Despite searching PubMed and conducting an enhanced grey literature search, some trials meeting the inclusion criteria may have been missed. Including the BISQUIT trial - which investigated the addition of probiotics and prebiotics to standard of care CRT - could be questioned as an immunotherapy. However, the proposed mechanism of action for this intervention was immune system modulation [72].

The lack of formal trial-comparison tools could be a limitation of this systematic review. RT is a complex intervention with specific methodological considerations for trial design, and when combined with different forms of IO, the utility of these tools is limited [73]. Although no statistical analysis was performed, this review focused on clinical and methodological rather than statistical heterogeneity. Objective methods for assessing clinical and methodological heterogeneity remain an area of unmet need [74]. With 69 patients across all trials and no controls to compare their outcomes to, the lack of a meta-analysis is justified.

Conclusion

Exploring different IO drugs and schedules with RT to identify the best combination for ASCC is vital. While current trials address this, heterogeneity in chemotherapy, radiotherapy, patient selection, endpoints, and translational analyses remains a key obstacle to comparing these studies and ultimately choosing the most effective treatment. The exciting early results from INTERACT-ION, together with the fully recruited NCI-EA2165, CORINTH, and RADIANCE trials, span the key IO timing strategies (neoadjuvant/adjuvant, adjuvant, concurrent/adjuvant, and neoadjuvant/concurrent/adjuvant, respectively) using both PD-1 and PD-L1 antibodies, and all include planned translational analyses. Regardless of individual outcomes, their combined results should substantially improve our understanding of the optimal timing of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors with RT in ASCC, and their translational analysis will greatly assist the design of future biologically informed ASCC trials. Table 5 summarises practical recommendations to enhance comparability. To implement these changes, we suggest a call to action for an international ASCC RT-IO consortium to standardise trial design, promote compatible translational sampling and analyses, and coordinate a portfolio of complementary studies that explicitly connect clinical outcomes to underlying biology.
