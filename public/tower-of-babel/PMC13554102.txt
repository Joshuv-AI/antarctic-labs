NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend for adaptive multimodal phishing detection against evolving artificial intelligence driven threats
Authors: Gaddam Lakshmi, Perumalla Swetha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phishing practice has radically changed as automation and artificial intelligence have taken new forms, enabling attackers to produce highly believable domain-spoofed content that is nearly indistinguishable from natural communication. Existing detection techniques, such as rule-based, blacklist-based, and single-modality approaches, tend to be weak, fail to generalise to unfamiliar attacks, and lack interpretability. Although the latest deep learning-driven solutions have improved detection performance, they remain vulnerable to adversarial attacks and provide fewer explanations for security analysts. To mitigate such challenges, the current paper outlines a neuro-symbolic multimodal phishing detection system that combines textual, visual, and metadata features and links them via a cross-attention-based fusion module. The framework improves the consistency of decisions in data-driven representations by incorporating explicit symbolic reasoning, leading to better decision-making, particularly when considering obfuscated and adversarial phishing patterns. The diffusion-based adversarial augmentation approach improves accuracy against phishing attacks using AI-generated similes. Conversely, continuous learning can be achieved in an online adaptation module through a replay buffer in response to a time-varying attack distribution. The SHAP-based explainability module provides explanations for features, making predictions interpretable and transparent. Many experiments across a variety of publicly available phishing datasets demonstrate that the suggested approach is effective, achieving a potential ROC–AUC of up to 97% on clean test data. The model is also more resilient to adversarial perturbations and achieves absolute AUC gains of 6–7% in cross-dataset generalisation compared to competitive baselines. The results are reported as the mean and standard deviation of repeated runs, and statistical significance (p < 0.05) is assessed to evaluate the reliability of the observed improvements. The suggested architecture provides a practical and reliable means of implementation, e.g., in security infrastructures such as email filters, web browsers, and enterprise threat intelligence systems, where robustness, generalisation, and interpretability are of utmost importance.

Introduction

Probably one of the most prevalent and dangerous cyber threats is phishing, which preys on human trust to deceive them into giving away sensitive information or authorising unauthorised access to online systems. Phishing has evolved significantly over the years, from simple text-based tricks to more sophisticated ones involving realistic web pages, brand impersonation, and automated evasion measures. Due to advances in automation and artificial intelligence engineering, these attacks have grown in size and diversity and have become harder to detect with traditional defence mechanisms. Conventional techniques, including rule-based and blacklist filters, tend to lag changing attack patterns. Similarly, single-modality machine learning training can be prone to distributional shifts and adversarial attacks, which hinder its real-world applicability. These demerits demonstrate why the dynamic, nimble, and interpretable phishing detection systems are important.

More recent papers have investigated phishing detection methods based on deep learning, such as natural language processing algorithms to analyse emails and webpage URLs1, convolutional neural networks to analyse webpage screenshots2, and a mix of handcrafted features and learning-based classifiers. Although the methods are better than traditional methods, several limitations remain. Numerous current methods rely on a single modality, which limits their ability to identify complementary phishing clues across textual, visual, and structural levels. Despite the existence of suggested multimodal approaches, feature fusion and classification performance are usually prioritised, whereas interpretability and adaptability receive little attention. Also, models trained on particular datasets exhibit strong in-domain behaviour but can be easily adversely affected by distribution shifts or adversarial perturbations. The absence of open decision-making also limits their use in security-critical settings, where analysts need explanations they can interpret to approve automated predictions.

To overcome these drawbacks, the proposed work proposes a neuro-symbolic multimodal phishing detector design that combines textual, visual, and metadata data into a cohesive learning framework. The framework uses a cross-attention-based fusion mechanism to extract interdependencies between modalities, enabling the model to detect inconsistencies that are difficult to detect with single-modality methods. The framework, in addition to relying on data-driven learning, includes a symbolic reasoning component that imposes clarity-based logical constraints to support greater decision consistency, especially in adversarial and artificial-intelligence-generated phishing attacks. Further presented are continuous adaptation to changing attack patterns via an online learning mechanism with a replay buffer and a diffusion-based augmentation strategy that enhances robustness to synthetic and unseen attack patterns.

In contrast to previous research, which mostly analyses multimodal fusion or classification performance, the approach focuses on how multimodal learning, symbolic rationality, and adaptive mechanisms can be promoted within a single framework. This combination allows the system to offer more consistent, interpretable and robust predictions in a dynamic threat environment.

The proposed NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend framework integrates multimodal learning, symbolic reasoning, adversarial robustness, continual adaptation, and explainability. Since most current multimodal approaches focus on aggregating features from textual, visual, and structural inputs, the proposed framework also incorporates explicit phishing-domain knowledge by adding a neuro-symbolic reasoning layer to enhance decision consistency and interpretability. In addition, diffusion-based adversarial augmentation is used to present realistic AI-generated phishing scenarios, and an online learning mechanism using a replay buffer is used for continuous adaptation to changing attack distributions. The proposed framework is not only capable of detecting phishing but also provides an extensive solution for real-world defence by jointly considering detection accuracy, robustness, interpretability, and adaptability, which are not addressed by traditional multimodal and neuro-symbolic detection methods.

Collectively, these articles indicate three indisputable gaps that NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend attempts to address: (1) multimodal fusion, (2) explicit adversary awareness in response to threats emphasised by the GenAI era, and (3) self-adaptive performance that defends against rapidly changing meta-attacks and environments.

While some recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of multimodal learning, transformer-based architectures, and hybrid AI techniques for phishing detection, most existing methods focus solely on classification accuracy and feature fusion. Typically, these aspects, such as explicit integration of symbolic reasoning, adversarial robustness against AI-generated phishing content, continual adaptation to changing attack distribution and explainability, do not fall into a single framework. NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend, on the other hand, integrates cross-attention-based multimodal fusion, neuro-symbolic inference, diffusion-based adversarial augmentation, online continual adaptation via replay-based learning and SHAP-based explainability into a single end-to-end architecture. The integration can help the framework not only detect phishing accurately but also become more robust, interpretable, and adaptable in dynamic threat scenarios.

Materials and methods

This subsection introduces the basic building blocks and technical architecture of the proposed NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend framework. It characterises the multimodal data, justifies preprocessing policies for each modality, and suggests architectural blocks that integrate symbolic reasoning with deep neural encoders. The fusion mechanism, adaptive learning strategies, and training settings are also outlined and form the foundation of robust phishing detection in both adversarial and dynamic threat environments.

Overview of the proposed framework

To develop robust, adaptive, and understandable phishing detection, the NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend framework proposed combines multimodal deep learning with explicit symbolic reasoning. The system takes in heterogeneous inputs, including textual information (emails/URLs), structured metadata (HTML headers), and visualisations (webpage screenshots or logos), using specific feature-extraction modules. Specifically, a BERT-based transformer encoder is trained on textual semantics, a feedforward network, which is a lightweight metadata parser, is trained on structured attributes, and a ResNet-50 convolutional neural network is trained to extract visual representations. Embeddings of such modalities are subsequently mapped into a shared latent space and combined via cross-attention, enabling the model to learn inter-modal interactions and contextual mismatches, which are common in phishing attacks.

Fig. 1System Architecture of the NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend Framework for Multimodal Phishing Detection and Explainability.

System Architecture of the NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend Framework for Multimodal Phishing Detection and Explainability.

The end-to-end pipeline of the proposed framework is shown in Fig. 1. Multimodal inputs (text, metadata, and visual content) are initially handled separately by specialised encoders. Thereafter, features are extracted, clustered into a single feature space, and fused using a cross-attention-based fusion element. The most significant component of the framework is the Neuro-Symbolic Reasoning Module, which combines acquired neural representations with explicit, rule-based reasoning grounded in domain knowledge (e.g., domain mismatch, suspicious redirection, visual text mismatch). This unity enhances uniformity in decision-making and resistance to obfuscation and adversarial phishing.

To further generalise, the framework employs an adversarial augmentation pipeline to generate phishing samples using diffusion models, guided by structured prompt templates. The samples replicate the dynamic attack trends, enabling the model to adapt to unknown phishing scenarios.

A replay buffer allows continuous adaptation through an online learning module. Information with low confidence or that is misclassified is stored and, at times, used to update the lightweight models, allowing the system to be practical under dynamic threat conditions and to avoid catastrophic forgetting. Finally, the framework offers a phishing risk score and SHAP-enabled explanations, providing interpretable, feature-level insights that help a cybersecurity analyst make real-time decisions.

Fig. 2Model Architecture of NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend with Cross-Attention Fusion and Neuro-Symbolic Inference.

Model Architecture of NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend with Cross-Attention Fusion and Neuro-Symbolic Inference.

The multimodal feature learning, fusion, and reasoning flow is shown in Fig. 2, which depicts the internal architecture of NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend. The model takes in three input modalities: textual data, metadata features, and visual content. The modes are encoded using their respective encoders to obtain high-level embeddings, which are then projected into a common latent space.

A fusion layer of attention is then used to dynamically learn relationships between modalities, enabling the system to detect subtle inconsistencies (e.g., legitimate-looking text alongside suspicious visual or metadata information). The combined representation is then entered into the reasoning network, which expounds the semantic meaning of the input.

Correspondingly, a symbolic rule engine is used to test prespecified logical constraints on the multimodal inputs. The acquired symbolic aspects are fused with the neural representation using a learnable fusion mechanism, ensuring that both data-driven tendencies and expert-specified rules influence the final decision-making.

Diffusion-based adversarial samples created to improve robustness are added to the training set. The model also incorporates an online learning mechanism and a replay buffer, enabling it to be periodically updated with uncertain or unchanging samples. The output is the final layer head that yields a phishing probability score, and an explainability module that uses SHAP-based attribution values to measure the contribution of each modality and feature.

The methodological novelty of the proposed framework lies in its unified integration of multimodal representation learning, symbolic rule-based reasoning, adversarial robustness enhancement, online adaptation, and explainability. Unlike conventional multimodal phishing detectors that primarily concatenate or fuse heterogeneous features for classification, NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend explicitly combines neural embeddings with phishing-domain symbolic constraints prior to final decision-making. This design enables the model to capture both learned semantic patterns and interpretable logical indicators such as domain mismatch, suspicious redirection, abnormal credential-request structures, metadata inconsistencies, and visual-brand conflicts. In addition, diffusion-based adversarial augmentation exposes the model to synthetic AI-generated phishing variants during training. At the same time, the replay-buffer-based online learning module supports lightweight adaptation to evolving phishing patterns. Therefore, the proposed methodology is not limited to improving classification accuracy, but is designed to jointly enhance robustness, interpretability, and adaptability in real-world phishing detection environments. Feature extraction modules

Within the context (proposed framework) the process of feature extraction is conducted on each modality, i.e. text, metadata and visual input, by using separate encoders, which learn independently and are then fused together (via cross-modal fusion). This modular architecture allows each of the modalities to learn domain specific representations but makes it compatible in the common latent space.

In the case of the textual modality, we use a transformer-based encoder to encode both local and long-range semantic ties found in email messages and URLs, as defined in Eq. (1). The encoder derives off a BERT-base model, including 12 transformer layers, 768 as the hidden dimension, 12 self-attention heads and 3072 as the intermediate feed-forward dimension. All the layers have multi-head self-attention networks and position-wise feed-forward networks, succeeded by residual connections and layer normalization. The wordpiece tokenizer is used to tokenize the input sequences with a maximum sequence length of 256 tokens and dropout rate of 0.1. The encoder, which is based on BERT-base, is pretrained and fine-tuned specifically to detect phishing. URLs which use short forms (i.e. bit.ly, tinyurl, etc.) are managed by using structural and lexical patterns of the displayed URL, and metadata-based indicators that include redirection indicators, domain reputation signals, and anomalies of URL length.

Diffusion-powered adversarial augmentation

This component assists the model in learning unobservable and polymorphic features of phishing by creating synthetic samples during training. Specifically, a two-stage pipeline is used to create phishing content, with the first stage building a prompt based on a large language model (LLM) that simulates realistic phishing narratives. They are then entered into a diffusion model, such as Stable Diffusion 3 (SD 3), to generate high-quality image samples that mimic brand logos, forged login pages or phishing call-to-action banners.

This design enables symbolic rules to correct or reinforce predictions emanating from neural networks in cases of cross-modal inconsistencies, metadata anomalies, domain obfuscation, and adversarial manipulation, making them more robust and interpretable.

It facilitates ongoing in-situ model tuning with no need for complete retraining and no central storage of data.

Dataset alignment, harmonisation, and split strategy

Due to the datasets’ sporadic sources and formats, there is no data consolidation at the raw level. Instead, a modality-aligned approach to learning is taken, according to which all datasets contribute to a specific feature area. PhishTank is used for text and URL offerings; the Anti-Phishing Webpage Dataset includes visual and HTML offerings; and LogoDet-3 K is used to pre-train the visual encoder to identify logos.

All datasets have been aligned to a common binary classification scheme (phishing vs. benign). Label harmonisation: This is done by projecting dataset-specific annotations onto this common representation. To prevent data leakage, a severity-dataset-wise split approach is used. Each dataset is randomly split into training, validation, and test sets, ensuring that no instances are shared across splits. Moreover, the construction of multimodal inputs does not involve duplicating samples in the datasets, and thus independence between training and evaluation is maintained. This design will ensure that there is no optimistic bias in the reported results and that the evaluation is fair.

In multimodal integration, the textual features, metadata attributes and visual representations were separately fed into modality-specific pipelines and then projected into the common latent space. The embeddings have been aligned at the feature-representation level, not at the raw data level, so as to allow heterogeneous datasets to provide complementary information while also maintaining modality-specific characteristics. This approach enhances scalability and decreases reliance on one-to-one correspondence between samples in datasets.

Model training and optimisation

The NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend framework proposed is trained in a multimodal environment that combines textual, metadata, and visual features. In the textual modality, phishing emails and URLs are tokenised with the WordPiece tokeniser, encoded with an encoder based on a BERT-base model, and fine-tuned on the phishing task to identify domain-specific linguistic code.

For the visual modality, screenshots of webpages and logo images are downsampled to 224 × 224 and fed into a ResNet-50 ImageNet-pretrained backbone. The feature maps are extracted and passed through a learnable projection layer to obtain small visual projections. In the case of metadata and symbolic modality, more structured features, i.e., sender reputation, domain properties, and HTML-based features, are encoded using a simple feedforward neural network to represent nonlinear relations among features.

The modality-specific embeddings are then mapped into a common latent space and fused using a cross-attention-based fusion mechanism to help the model learn inter-modal dependencies. The fused representation is further refined by a contextual reasoning network and integrated with symbolic features via the neuro-symbolic fusion strategy of Sect. 3.6. The representation is then encoded to a binary classification head to determine the probability of phishing.

Training the model is done on AdamW optimizer and its batch size is 32 and initial learning rate is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1\times\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document}. The training goal is formulated using binary cross-entropy loss, which is being used for the phishing classification task.

To enhance convergence and generalisation, pretraining strategies are used. To stabilise the initial training epochs and prevent early image features learning, the BERT-based textual encoder is pretrained with weights and the ResNet-50 visual backbone is pretrained using ImageNet and the first few layers are frozen. Xavier uniform initialisation is used to initialise the metadata encoder and fusion layers.

Such a consistent and unified training setup provides stability in optimisation across modalities and enables the model to learn to compose phishing cues consistently from textual, visual, and structural information, contributing to robust detection and improved legibility.

Evaluation metrics

The performance of the proposed NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend framework is evaluated using conventional classification metrics, including Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, and Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC–AUC). Accuracy measures the percentage of correctly classified samples, and the ratio of true phishing among all predicted phishing samples; precision measures the percentage of true phishing among all predicted phishing samples; and specificity measures the percentage of correctly classified samples. Recall (sensitivity) measures the model’s ability to recognise phishing attacks, whereas the F1-score provides a more moderate measure, as it combines precision and recall. ROC-AUC evaluates the model’s ability to distinguish phishing from benign categories across different decision thresholds, providing a solid indication of overall classification performance.

In addition to predictive performance, the explainability of model decisions is also measured using quantitative metrics that assess the reliability and understandability of attribution-based explanations. In particular, three compatible standards are considered: faithfulness, plausibility, and stability.

Faithfulness is used to assess how much the features identified by the explainer actually contribute to the model’s predictions. It can be evaluated using a perturbation-based method, in which the top-k features (ranked by attribution scores) are dropped or ignored, and the resulting drop in the predicted probability of phishing is measured. The bigger the decrease in performance, the more faithful the explanation.

Plausibility measures the consistency of model-induced exposition to phishing hints understandable by human beings. To this end, a subset of the samples is labelled with the most prominent phishing warning signs, including a suspicious domain name, urgency-related wording, credential-petitioning designs, metadata variation, and visual-text contradictions. Plausibility is measured as the overlap or precision at k, the top-ranked attributed features and these labelled cues.

Stability analyses the consistency of explanations with perturbations of the small, semantically preserving input, e.g., small text edits or image compression. The similarity between the attribution vectors of the original and perturbed samples is used to measure it (e.g., cosine similarity or rank correlation).

SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) is used as the primary attribution method, and feature importance across textual, visual, and symbolic modalities is calculated using it. To provide a stricter assessment, the SHAP-based explanations are compared with other attribution methods (e.g., Integrated Gradients and LIME) in Sect. 4.9. This integrated assessment plan will ensure that the proposed framework is evaluated not only for predictive accuracy but also for the reliability, consistency, and interpretability of its decision-making process.

Robustness to dynamic and adversarial phishing

To test the resilience of the proposed NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend framework to realistic attack scenarios, we compare its behaviour against various dynamic and adversarial phishing perturbations used to model evolving attack behaviour. These perturbations will be designed to preserve the semantic meaning of the phishing messages while altering perceptible features.

The threat model we consider is black-box, meaning that the attacker is not provided with the model’s parameters, but may alter textual, visual, and metadata input features. That is realistic in the context of real-world phishing attacks, where phishers refine their attacks over time to avoid triggering the detection system, without any specific understanding of how that detector works.

Seven adversarial scenarios represent a variety of attack vectors encountered in real-world phishing. They can be text obfuscation methods, like leet-speak, homoglyph replacement, inserted spacing, URL manipulation techniques like subdomain stuffing, URL shortening, punycode-like tricks, plus HTML template obfuscation techniques with reorganised DOM, hidden components, and CSS-obfuscation, visual degradation techniques, including compression artefacts, injected noise, and resolution reduction, brand/logo-obfuscation.

Perturbations at moderated levels associated with low, medium and high attack strengths, i.e. 10, 20 and 30 per cent features alteration, are applied to every type of attack. Table 5 reports results related to average performance across these perturbation levels.

Rule-based and transformation-based techniques are used to implement adversarial transformations and create realistic simulated phishing behaviour. Perturbations to text include both controlled lexical replacement and formatting; URL-based attacks perturb structural elements without obstructing functionality; visual perturbations consist of standard image manipulations; and metadata masking is achieved by selectively eliminating or modifying non-structural features.

Shortened URLs are also a form of URL manipulation, in which the destination underlying the URL is hidden, and the model must discover it indirectly via token patterns, domain deviations, and other metadata features. Also, truncation at the user interface level does not affect the framework, as the URL information is not lost during internal processing. This allows it to accurately detect even when partial URLs are exposed to end users.

Short and truncated URLs are also considered in the test. Although shortened URLs are analysed via indirect feature cues and pattern learning, truncated URLs have no impact because the model analyses the full URL.

Quantitative faithfulness analysis

To quantitatively assess the faithfulness of the explanation methods, a perturbation-based evaluation strategy is applied. In particular, the top-k most influential features detected by each explainer are sequentially removed from the input, and the resulting drop in phishing prediction accuracy is quantified. The greater the difference in predicted probabilities, the more the named features have a significant causal effect on the model’s decision; thus, higher faithfulness.

Three attribution techniques are used to evaluate the proposed framework: SHAP, Integrated Gradients (IG), and LIME. The level of faithfulness is assessed at 10%, 20%, and 30%, and the results are averaged across the test dataset. This results in the respective average probability differences ( ΔProb ) as shown in Table 9.

Stability under small input variations

A stability analysis was conducted to estimate the resilience of explanation methods to small, semantics-preserving perturbations. Stability gauges the consistency of explanations when the input is slightly changed, while attitudes do not shift the core meaning. This is especially significant in phishing detection, where non-malicious variations such as punctuation, synonyms, small-scale HTML edits, or minor image compression should not significantly affect the explanation outputs.

Three kinds of perturbation were used in this research, including: (i) textual perturbation (such as synonym replacement, punctuation variation), (ii) visual perturbation (such as JPEG compression, addition of a small amount of noise), and (iii) metadata perturbation (such as slight reordering of certain header fields or changing non-critical attributes). The explanations for each perturbed instance were recomputed and compared with the initial explanations, using cosine similarity and Spearman rank correlation across the attribution vectors. The bigger the values, the more stable the explanations.

Table 11 presents the results, demonstrating that SHAP is most stable across all types of perturbation, with a mean cosine similarity of 0.83 and a rank correlation of 0.79, indicating strong consistency in the attributes of the attributed features. The Integrated Gradients is averagely stable (cosine similarity: 0.76, rank correlation: 0.72), and the LIME is less stable (cosine similarity: 0.70, rank correlation: 0.66). SHAP also shows less variation across samples, indicating more predictable explanations for input variations. These results indicate that SHAP-based explanations are more robust to input perturbations and, hence, can be more reliable and consistent in interpreting dynamic phishing detection.

Discussion

The sophistication of phishing attacks is rising due to automation and AI, which, in turn, pose enormous threats to traditional detection systems. Techniques that rely on manually crafted properties, blocklists that are not adaptive, or machine learning models that are not multi-modal tend not to be effective against adaptive, large-scale phishing campaigns. Despite the recent achievements of deep learning-based approaches in detection accuracy, they remain limited in their resistance to adversarial manipulation, cross-dataset generalisation, and the understandability of predictions, which is imperative for real-world implementation of security systems.

The results of this work show that detection should be based on the combination of different modalities. The proposed framework considers textual, visual, and metadata features and can therefore capture complementary evidence inaccessible to single-modality models. A cross-attention-based fusion mechanism allows the model to detect small inconsistencies between modalities, e.g. These mismatches between textual content and visual/structural clues are typical in sophisticated phishing attacks.

One of the major features of the given suggestion is the use of symbolic reasoning and neural representations. This combined design enables the model to maintain logical consistency by enforcing inherent rules that enhance decision reliability in situations where deep data-driven models might not work, such as manipulated or artificially crafted phishing messages by adversaries or AI. This design choice is supported by the experimental results, which show improved robustness under multiple attack scenarios and increased cross-dataset generalisation relative to baseline methods.

Furthermore, explainability mechanisms provide valuable insights into a model’s decisions. Dissection of modality-level contributions provides better insight into how various aspects affect predictions, which is especially important for cybersecurity analysts. Although explaining deep learning models is challenging, the findings show that attribution-based approaches can enhance transparency and facilitate their practical implementation.

The findings suggest that multimodal learning, in conjunction with symbolic reasoning and adaptive mechanisms, can help overcome several weaknesses in current phishing detection systems. The suggested framework is more robust, generalisable, and interpretable, making it applicable to practical security systems in the real world, such as email message filtering, web filters, and enterprise threat intelligence systems.

Limitations of the study

Although the results are promising, several limitations are worth noting. One, publicly accessible datasets are used in the course of experimental analysis of phishing, which, therefore, might not reflect the diversity and complexity of highly targeted or region-specific phishing attacks that one might be exposed to in real-world settings. Consequently, more testing on large-scale, enterprise-grade data is required to determine whether it is practical.

Second, although the proposed framework offers modality-level explainability, further validation of its interpretability is scarce. Specifically, the work fails to address instance-level causal explanations or user-generated assessments of explanation quality, both of which are significant for assessing the reliability and practicality of explanations in operational contexts.

Third, the present-day evaluation covers mainly offline and batch learning cases. Although the framework includes an online learning component, its operation in sustained real-time deployment settings (including concept drift, streaming data, and long-term adaptation) has not been fully investigated. Lastly, the combination of multiple modalities and attention-based processes can make the overall computational complexity high, making it difficult to scale and implement on resource-constrained devices.

Though the research also involves quantitative explainability analysis, it lacks a formal user study or validation by cybersecurity experts. As such, although promising in terms of faithfulness, plausibility, and stability, their applicability to live analyst processes has yet to be confirmed through human-based tests.

Conclusion and future work

In this paper, the author has introduced NeuroSymbolicPhishDefend, a multimodal phishing detector that fuses textual, visual, and metadata features using a cross-attention-based method, with adaptive learning mechanisms and symbolic reasoning. The proposed solution addresses major weaknesses of current phishing detection models, especially their limited resistance to adversarial manipulation, poor cross-dataset generalisation, and limited interpretability. Empirical findings indicate that the proposed framework achieves high-quality performance across various testing environments, including clean data, adversarial perturbations, and cross-dataset generalisation. Multimodal representations, along with symbolic reasoning, enable more unanimous decision-making; integrating explainability mechanisms provides interpretable insights into the model’s predictions. Additionally, adversarial and adaptive learning augmentations help achieve greater robustness against evolving phishing attacks. Such results suggest that the synergy between data-driven learning and explicit reasoning in phishing detection systems may contribute to their effectiveness and transparency. Despite these advantages, several limitations must be noted. The fact that multimodal data is not always present in the real world is also a limitation of the framework, and it is not always easy to access. Despite its effectiveness, the symbolic rule set can only be used with domain knowledge and may require periodic revision to keep pace with increasingly frequent attack patterns. In addition, multimodal processing and attention are resource-intensive and can pose scaling problems in resource-restricted environments. Further research will establish the framework in different directions in future work. To begin with, continuous streaming of information and real-time online learning can further enhance flexibility in dealing with concept drift in dynamic threat environments. Second, there is an opportunity to integrate the latest explainability methods, such as causal inference and user-related evaluation, to ensure that model explanations are more valid and useful for security analysts. Third, the ecological validity of a framework will be improved by incorporating large-scale, real-world, and zero-day datasets into the evaluation. In addition, applying the system to practical security scenarios (e.g., email gateways, browser-based security) will enable measurement of latency, scalability, and long-term effectiveness. This paper demonstrates that a multidimensional learning approach, together with symbolic reasoning and adaptive mechanisms, is a reasonable way to develop formidable, interpretable, and scalable phishing detection that can help alleviate emerging cybersecurity risks.
