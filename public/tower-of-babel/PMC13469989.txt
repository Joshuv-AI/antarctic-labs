Disease‐free survival as a surrogate outcome for localized kidney cancer—A systematic review and meta‐analysis
Authors: Constanza Martinez, David Chen, Russell Leong, Nicholas Lum, Karren Xiao, Laith Almasri, Christian K. Kollmannsberger, Srinivas Raman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDisease‐free survival (DFS) is commonly used as a primary end point in trials for localized renal cell carcinoma (RCC), although its validity as a surrogate for overall survival (OS) remains uncertain. The objective of this study was to evaluate whether DFS is a reliable surrogate end point for OS in this setting.MethodsThe authors conducted a systematic review and trial‐level meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials assessing systemic or perioperative therapies in patients with localized RCC. Databases were searched from inception to June 10, 2025. Trial‐level surrogacy between DFS and OS was assessed using weighted least‐squares meta‐regression of log‐transformed hazard ratios (HRs), and the surrogate threshold effect was estimated.ResultsIn total, 25 randomized controlled trials were analyzed; 13 trials, analyzed as 15 study‐level units comprising 9594 patients, reported HRs for both OS and DFS. DFS demonstrated a strong correlation with OS (weighted Pearson correlation coefficient, 0.75; 95% confidence level, 0.55–0.91; corresponding coefficient of determination, 0.57). The surrogate threshold effect analysis identified a DFS HR <0.914 as predictive of OS benefit. Funnel plots showed no clear asymmetry, and Egger tests did not indicate significant publication bias.ConclusionsDFS demonstrated a strong association with OS and may serve as a valid trial‐level surrogate end point patients with in localized RCC. A DFS HR <0.914 was more likely to predict an OS benefit. These findings support the use of DFS as a primary end point in localized RCC trials, whereas continued long‐term follow‐up and future patient‐level validation remain important.

INTRODUCTION

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) represents approximately 3% of all adult malignancies, with an increasing proportion of patients diagnosed at the localized or organ‐confined stages because of the widespread use of cross‐sectional imaging. 1 , 2 Surgical resection, using partial or radical nephrectomy, remains the most common modality for curative treatment. Alternatively, in recent years, there has been an increase in minimally invasive and noninvasive treatments, such as stereotactic body radiotherapy, radiofrequency ablation, microwave ablation, cryoablation, and electroporation. 3 Nevertheless, up to 30% of patients experience disease recurrence, and there has been significant interest in adding systemic therapies to improve cancer control and overall survival (OS). 4 , 5 Consequently, many randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have evaluated disease‐free survival (DFS) as a primary end point, particularly when assessing adjuvant systemic therapy after nephrectomy. Although DFS is frequently used as the primary efficacy end point in adjuvant RCC trials, including KEYNOTE 564, 4 , 6 S‐TRAC, 7 EVEREST, 8 and PROSPER, 9 its surrogacy for OS has not yet been firmly established in localized disease.

In contrast, for metastatic RCC, previous meta‐analyses have demonstrated moderate‐to‐strong correlations between progression‐free survival and OS, suggesting that treatment effects on intermediate end points may reflect meaningful long‐term benefit. 10 , 11 Whether a similar relation exists in localized RCC remains uncertain and has significant implications for trial design, clinical decision‐making, and regulatory approval pathways.

Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis of RCTs in localized RCC to evaluate the surrogacy relation between DFS and OS. Our objectives were to determine whether treatment effects on DFS reliably predict OS outcomes at the trial level and to estimate the surrogate threshold effect (STE) that defines the DFS benefit required to suggest a probable OS advantage.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Search strategy and selection criteria

First, the systematic review was registered in PROSPERO under the registration number CRD420251081754. For this systematic review and meta‐analysis, we conducted a comprehensive literature search for studies investigating treatment outcomes in randomized controlled trials in localized kidney cancer. We searched PubMed (MEDLINE), Embase, and the Cochrane Library for publications from database inception until June 10, 2025. We used the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and non‐MeSH search terms kidney neoplasms and renal cell carcinoma along with the keywords renal and kidney. Studies were included if they were RCTs that assessed a therapeutic intervention for primary treatment and included participants who had primary, localized RCC. Studies were excluded if they were not randomized controlled trials; included participants with primarily metastatic RCC, benign, or non‐RCC kidney tumors; or discussed outcomes of re‐treatment instead of primary treatment. Data screening was done in duplicate (by R.L., N.L., K.X., and L.A.), and conflicts were discussed with a third reviewer (D.C.).

Data extraction

The following information was extracted from each included trial: first author’s name, year of publication, trial phase, year of first and last enrollment, number of enrolled patients per arm, treatment arms, median follow‐up, and outcomes data, including DFS and OS (e.g., the hazard ratio [HR], and 95% confidence interval [CI] for each estimate). If data were not reported, DFS or OS rates were extracted from the Kaplan–Meier curves or from prior meta‐analysis that reported these rates. 12 , 13 Data extraction was done in duplicate (by R.L., N.L., K.X., and L.A.) using a standardized form, and conflicts were discussed with a third reviewer (D.C.).

Definitions of end points

DFS was measured from the time of randomization or the start of intervention (e.g., nephrectomy) to the first evidence of clinical disease recurrence (locoregional or distant) or death from any cause. The alternate terms, recurrence‐free survival and event‐free survival, were used and were defined similarly as DFS. OS was calculated from the time of randomization or the start of intervention (e.g., nephrectomy) to death from any cause.

Statistical analyses

To assess the surrogacy of DFS to OS, we used the weighted Pearson correlation coefficient (R) and the corresponding coefficient of determination (R 2) to quantify the association between the DFS and OS HRs and estimated the STE, defined as the treatment effect on DFS at which the upper bound of the predicted 95% CI for the OS HR crossed 1.0. Trial‐level association was assessed using weighted least‐squares meta‐regression of the log‐transformed HRs, with the log OS HR as the dependent variable and the log DFS HR as the independent variable. Each trial was weighted by the inverse of the variance of its log‐transformed OS HR, with the variance derived from the reported 95% CI, so that trials with more precise OS estimates contributed proportionally greater weight to the model. Ninety‐five percent CIs for the weighted R and the STE were obtained by nonparametric bootstrap resampling of trials (1000 resamples), taking the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting bootstrap distributions (percentile method). Cancer‐specific survival was not assessed because this was not reported in most of the clinical trials. To assess the reliability of the estimated correlation and STE, we conducted a leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis. Each trial was systematically excluded, and the correlation and STE were re‐calculated based on the remaining trials. The risk‐of‐bias (ROB) was assessed using the Cochrane risk of bias 2 tool (ROB‐2) 14 for each trial included in this study (see Figure S1). Publication bias was assessed by inspection for asymmetry of funnel plots and Egger tests.

Across all trials combined, in total, 16,114 patients were included with a median follow‐up of 61 months. Most trials were multicenter, and the trial sample size ranged from 46 to 1340 patients, with six of the 25 trials enrolling fewer than 100 patients. Of the 25 randomized trials, 22 assessed systemic therapy interventions compared with placebo or observation.

Of the 25 randomized controlled trials, 13 trials (analyzed as 15 study‐level units) comprising 9594 patients reported both OS and DFS HRs, allowing their inclusion in the surrogacy analysis of DFS to OS (Table 2). 5 , 7 , 8 , 13 , 15 , 20 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 28 , 31 , 32 , 33 These 15 study‐level analyses contributed 16 paired DFS/OS estimates, because KEYNOTE‐564 contributed two nonoverlapping risk subgroups (M0 [no distant metastatic disease] intermediate‐high risk and M0 high risk) that were analyzed separately. By using a weighted least‐squares meta‐regression, the association between DFS and OS HRs demonstrated a strong correlation (weighted R, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.55–0.91; R 2, 0.57), explaining approximately 57% of the variability in treatment effects on OS across trials. The STE was estimated at an HR of 0.914 (95% CI, 0.88–0.95), indicating that a DFS HR below this threshold was more likely to predict an OS benefit at the trial level (Figure 2). 5 , 7 , 8 , 13 , 20 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 28 , 31 , 32 , 33

In a sensitivity analysis restricted to 11 paired DFS/OS estimates sourced from 10 trials without a high overall ROB according to ROB‐2 assessment, the estimated STE yielded an STE HR of 0.89 (95% CI, 0.69–0.97; Figure 3). 5 , 7 , 8 , 13 , 31 , 32 , 33 In a sensitivity analysis restricted to studies published in 2015 or later (n = 9), the trial‐level association between treatment effects on DFS and OS remained positive but less precise (weighted R, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.00–0.91; R 2, 0.39), with an STE HR of 0.86 (95% CI, 0.39–0.95; Figure 4). 5 , 7 , 8 , 31 , 32 , 33 In an exploratory analysis limited to contemporary systemic therapy trials consisting of vascular endothelial growth factor tyrosine kinase inhibitor, mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitor, and immune checkpoint inhibitor interventions (n = 8), the association remained directionally consistent with the primary analysis but was estimated with reduced precision (weighted R, 0.58 [95% CI, −0.18 to 0.88; R 2, 0.34]; STE HR, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.02−0.93]), reflecting the limited number of trials and the clustering of several recent negative trials near the null. To assess the stability of the STE, we conducted a leave‐one‐out cross‐validation to evaluate whether systematic exclusion of individual studies had a significant effect on the overall STE HR (see Table S2). We observed that stepwise exclusion of each trial and recalculation of the STE yielded HRs that remained within the 95% CI of the STE HR calculated using all 15 study‐level analyses, confirming that no single trial disproportionately influenced the primary surrogacy outcome.

To assess for publication bias, funnel plots were visually inspected and assessed using the Egger test for asymmetry. For DFS, funnel plots did not show asymmetry, but the Egger test was not statistically significant (p = .23; see Figure S2). For OS, funnel plots did not show asymmetry, and the Egger test was not significant (p = .38; see Figure S3).

DISCUSSION

In this systematic review, we included 25 randomized controlled trials encompassing 16,114 patients with a median follow‐up of 61 months, of which 13 trials (analyzed as 15 study‐level units contributing 16 paired DFS/OS estimates, comprising 9594 patients) had available OS and DFS HRs and were included in the trial‐level surrogacy analysis. This analysis identified a strong surrogacy relation between DFS and OS in localized kidney cancer. These findings suggest that DFS may serve as a valid surrogate end point for OS in clinical trials for localized RCC.

Our findings address an important evidence gap because this represents one of the largest and most contemporary trial‐level meta‐analyses evaluating DFS as a surrogate outcome for OS in localized RCC. A prior analysis, 35 in which DFS was assessed as a predictor of OS, did not indicate a strong correlation between the 5‐year DFS and 5‐year OS in localized RCC. However, that study included only 13 RCTs (6473 patients), had a shorter median follow‐up of 5.1 years, and included trials published up to 2016. In contrast, our surrogacy analysis included 13 trials (analyzed as 15 study‐level units comprising 9594 patients) and incorporated data from trials conducted through 2025.

It is worth noting that approximately 30% of the trials included our meta‐analysis were conducted before 2010, an era in which effective systemic therapies were limited and significant improvements in DFS were not commonly observed. Therefore, we also included a subgroup analysis of trials published in 2015 or later (n = 9), demonstrating a trial‐level association between DFS and OS treatment effects (weighted R, 0.62; R 2, 0.39; STE HR, 0.86 [95% CI 0.39−0.95]), in which the effects remained positive and directionally consistent with the primary analysis, although estimated with reduced precision because of the smaller number of trials.

More recent trials have demonstrated clinically meaningful benefits in DFS and, in some patients, OS. For example, KEYNOTE‐564 demonstrated that adjuvant pembrolizumab after nephrectomy significantly improved DFS and OS, with a 5‐year OS rate of 87.7% versus 82.3% in the placebo arm. 4 , 5 The M0 intermediate‐to‐high−risk disease outcomes at 5 years were not reported; rather, they were reported at 48 months, with an OS rate of 92.6% in the pembrolizumab group and 87.7% in the placebo group.

More recently, emerging data from the phase 3 Litespark‐022 36 trial, which evaluated escalation of adjuvant therapy with the addition of belzutifan to pembrolizumab in patients with high‐risk clear‐cell RCC after nephrectomy, demonstrated a statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvement in DFS compared with pembrolizumab alone, although OS data remain immature. Similarly, the phase 3 Rampart 37 trial investigated adjuvant immunotherapy strategies using durvalumab alone or in combination with tremelimumab compared with active monitoring in patients who had resected intermediate‐to‐high−risk RCC, with co‐primary end points of DFS and OS. Early results suggest that combination immunotherapy may improve DFS, particularly in higher risk populations, further supporting the relevance of DFS as a primary end point in contemporary adjuvant trials, whereas OS data remain unpublished. Together, these contemporary trials further support the clinical relevance of DFS as a primary end point in localized RCC. The estimated STE of 0.914 provides a clinically relevant benchmark, suggesting that DFS HRs below this threshold are more likely to translate into OS benefit at the trial level. However, this threshold should be interpreted cautiously because it is derived from aggregate trial‐level data rather than an absolute clinical cutoff.

Although DFS has frequently been used as the primary end point in adjuvant RCC trials, 4 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 its validity as a surrogate for OS in localized disease has remained uncertain. Historically, the use of surrogate end points has required formal validation based on rigorous statistical criteria, including demonstration of a strong correlation between treatment effects on the surrogate and the true clinical end point. 38 , 39 , 40 Although such relations have been well established in metastatic RCC, in which multiple meta‐analyses have demonstrated moderate‐to‐strong trial‐level correlations between progression‐free survival and OS, 10 , 11 comparable analyses in localized RCC have been limited. Our study provides updated evidence supporting DFS as a meaningful predictor of OS in this setting. Consistent with our findings, similar surrogacy relations between DFS and OS have been demonstrated in other cancer types treated in the adjuvant setting, including breast, pancreatic, and esophageal or gastroesophageal junction cancers. 41 , 42 , 43 Moreover, the most common patterns of relapse in localized RCC are distant metastases; and, once metastatic progression occurs, survival is still relatively short, making it biologically plausible that prevention or meaningful delay of recurrence, as captured by DFS, translates into improved OS. 44

The findings of this study have important regulatory and clinical implications. Several adjuvant therapies in RCC have received regulatory US Food and Drug Administration approval based primarily on DFS improvement, most notably sunitinib after the S‐TRAC trial. 7 That approval occurred despite the absence of a clear OS benefit and the presence of substantial treatment‐related toxicity. 45 , 46 This decision generated considerable controversy and highlighted the need for formal surrogate end point validation in localized RCC.

Notably, most trials included in our analysis evaluated systemic therapies in the adjuvant setting, reflecting current research priorities in localized RCC. However, the use of surrogate end points in this context requires careful consideration. 47 Adjuvant trials pose distinct challenges: treatment response cannot be directly assessed in the absence of measurable disease. Many patients are cured by surgery alone and are exposed to treatment‐related toxicity without benefit, whereas others will relapse despite therapy. 48 , 49 Moreover, any potential benefit can only be determined retrospectively after long follow‐up. In addition, adjuvant treatment inevitably worsens quality of life compared with observation. 50 Although the ultimate objective of adjuvant therapy is to improve survival, demonstrating an OS benefit requires prolonged follow‐up, making such trials lengthy and resource‐intensive.

There are limitations in our study that merit consideration. There are differences in trial designs, follow‐up duration, heterogeneity among the different treatment interventions, and insufficient studies to determine whether the surrogacy relation, primarily based on adjuvant trials, may also extend to other settings, such as comparing local modalities. Another limitation of this study is that surrogacy was assessed using trial‐level aggregate data rather than individual patient data. Consequently, our findings support DFS as a trial‐level surrogate for OS but do not establish patient‐level surrogacy, which would require formal individual patient data–based validation.

Nonetheless, the consistency, statistical robustness, trial‐level data, and clinical coherence of our findings support the validity of DFS as a surrogate end point in localized RCC. Moreover, there was no strong evidence of substantial publication bias for either DFS or OS. In addition, based on ROB‐2 assessment, the overall internal validity of the evidence supporting DFS as a trial‐level surrogate end point was judged to be moderate.

CONCLUSION

In this trial‐level meta‐analysis of localized RCC, DFS demonstrated a strong association with OS and may serve as a valid trial‐level surrogate end point. The surrogate threshold analysis further indicated that achieving a DFS HR below 0.914 is more likely to translate into an OS benefit. These findings support the use of DFS as a valid surrogate end point for OS in patients with localized RCC, whereas continued long‐term follow‐up and future individual patient data–based analyses remain important.
