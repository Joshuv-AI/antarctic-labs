Self-assembled manganese-based nanoplatforms synergizing immunogenic cell death and STING activation for triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Min Yang, Ming Qin, Yafeng Liu, Jiaying Li, Yue Yin, Yue Huang, Mengjun Yu, Jing Gong, Shiwen Chen, Jia Wang, Qiang Zhou, Xuan Zhang, Guobing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tumor immunotherapy has emerged as a promising approach to eliminate cancer by activating host immunity but shows limited efficacy in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) due to its low immunogenicity and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). In this study, we developed a manganese-based nanoparticle, termed HLM315, via one-step self-assembly of alendronate-modified hyaluronic acid (HA-AL), the amphiphilic cationic peptide LTX-315, and Mn2+ under weakly alkaline conditions. HLM315 actively targeted tumors and responded to the TME, releasing LTX-315 and generating Mn2+. LTX-315 induced immunogenic cell death (ICD) through tumor membrane disruption and release of tumor antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), thereby providing an initial signal for immune activation. Meanwhile, Mn2+ generated reactive oxygen species (ROS) and amplified the cGAS-STING signaling. Together, these mechanisms synergistically promoted type I interferon production, dendritic cell (DC) maturation, T-cell recruitment and activation, and elicited an enhanced immunological memory. Ultimately, HLM315 systematically reshaped the TME and potently inhibited the tumor growth in TNBC orthotopic mouse model. Overall, HLM315 establishes a coordinated antitumor immune cascade and offers a promising strategy to enhance immunotherapy efficacy in TNBC.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy among women worldwide, and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) accounts for approximately 15%–20% cases [1]. TNBC lacks expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), and is characterized by aggressive progression, early recurrence, and frequent metastasis [2]. Conventional treatments such as surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapies showed limited efficacy in TNBC with high recurrence rates and drug resistance [3,4]. Tumor immunotherapy has emerged as a promising alternative by activating durable antitumor immune responses [5]. However, the low immunogenicity and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) of TNBC markedly restrict its clinical benefit [6,7]. In this study, we propose that inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) and remodeling the TME may provide a new strategy for TNBC treatment.

LTX-315, an amphiphilic cationic peptide derived from bovine lactoferrin, has demonstrated both strong anti-tumor efficacy and favorable safety in phase I clinical trials [8,9]. Tumor cells typically exhibit more negative charges on the surface [10], enabling the positively charged LTX-315 to selectively bind tumor cell membranes via electrostatic interactions leading to membrane disruption and tumor cell lysis [11,12]. This process also induces ICD and promotes the release of tumor antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as calreticulin (CRT), high mobility group box-1 (HMGB-1), and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) [13,14]. Consequently, increases tumor-infiltrating T lymphocytes (TILs) and reduces immunosuppressive cell populations within the TME [15]. Despite these advantages, the clinical application of LTX-315 is limited by its short plasma half-life of approximately 15 min, necessitating intratumoral administration [16,17]. Therefore, the development of alternative delivery strategies, such as intravenous administration using tumor-targeted nanocarriers, may provide a rational strategy to overcome its limitation [18,19].

Manganese (Mn) is an essential trace element which serves as a cofactor in numerous physiological processes [20,21]. Manganese-based nanoplatforms have attracted attention as immunotherapeutic carriers due to their selectively accumulate at tumor sites, tumor-responsive behavior, and intrinsic immunomodulatory activity [20].

Existing studies have shown that manganese-based nanoplatforms can trigger intracellular DNA release through mitochondrial targeting, radiosensitization, cuproptosis induction, and combination chemotherapy strategies [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. The released DNA cooperates with Mn2+ to boost immune activation, promote DC maturation and CD8+ T cell infiltration, and effectively reverse tumor immunosuppression [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. Furthermore, approaches such as targeted modification, membrane coating, biodegradable implants, and microneedle-mediated transdermal delivery have improved tumor specificity, therapeutic durability, and biosafety [24,26]. In TNBC and melanoma models, these nanoplatforms substantially inhibited tumor growth and metastasis, highlighting their translational potential for cancer immunotherapy [[22], [23], [24], [25], [26]]. Within the TME, manganese-based nanoparticles released Mn2+, which exhibited Fenton-like activity, catalyzing the conversion of H2O2 into cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals (•OH), a reactive oxygen species (ROS) that directly induced tumor cell death and ICD [27]. In addition, Mn2+ is a potent activator of the cGAS-STING pathway, a central innate immune signaling axis that drives type I interferon production and antitumor immunity [[28], [29], [30]]. These properties suggest that combining Mn-based nanocarriers with LTX-315 may yield synergistic antitumor and immunostimulatory effects. Current methods for preparing manganese-based nanoparticles often require harsh conditions such as high temperature, high pressure, or organic solvents [31,32]. Previously, stable protein-coated aluminum salt nanoparticles were successfully prepared via a simple one-step vortex method in aqueous solution by exploiting proteins with surface-rich carboxyl groups to chelate inorganic metal ions [33]. Inspired by this strategy, hyaluronic acid (HA), a biocompatible and biodegradable polysaccharide also rich in carboxyl residues, and with intrinsic CD44-targeting capability, was selected in this study [34,35]. Under weakly alkaline conditions, anionic HA readily complexes with positively charged LTX-315 and Mn2+. To further improve metal coordination and nanoparticle stability, HA was modified with alendronate (ALN), whose phosphate groups provide high-density chelation sites for metal ions [36,37].

Based on above considerations, we constructed a self-assembled manganese-based nanoplatform encapsulating LTX-315 using ALN-modified HA (HA-AL). Under weakly alkaline conditions, manganese chloride formed unstable manganese hydroxide, rapidly oxidized to manganese oxide, which was subsequently coordinated strongly with the HA-AL, while simultaneously enabling one-step self-assembly with the LTX-315 via electrostatic adsorption. The preparation strategy is simple, reproducible, and conducted entirely in aqueous solution, consistent with green synthesis principles. HA-AL endows the nanosystem with active tumor-targeting capability through CD44 recognition, while the introduced phosphonate groups enhance nanoparticle stability and exhibit high complexation efficiency with LTX-315 and Mn2+. Following intravenous administration, the nanoparticles preferentially accumulate in tumor tissues and respond to the weakly acidic and H2O2-rich TME, releasing LTX-315 and Mn2+. LTX-315 induces ICD and membrane disruption, while Mn2+ amplifies ROS generation and cGAS-STING signaling, collectively promoting immune activation, TME remodeling, and suppression of tumor growth and metastasis. Materials and methods

Materials

LTX-315 (H-Lys-Lys-Trp-Trp-Lys-Lys-Trp-Dip-Lys-NH2, M.W.: 1439.82) was purchased from Apeptide (Shanghai, China). Hyaluronic acid (HA, 9341 Da) was purchased from Huaxi Biotech (Jinan, China). Sodium alginate (SA) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Ethyldimethylaminopropyl carbodiimide (EDC) was purchased from AmBeed (Beijing, China). Alendronate sodium (ALN) was purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Calcein-AM, propidium iodide (PI) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The apoptosis kit and most fluorophore-conjugated anti-mouse antibodies for flow cytometry assay were purchased from BioLegend (Beijing, China).

Cells and animals

The murine breast cancer cell line 4T1 was purchased from i-Cell Bioscience Inc. (Shanghai, China). 4T1 Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Lonsera, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Female BALB/c mice were obtained from SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Hunan, China) and housed in the Experimental Animal Center of Xinqiao Hospital, Army Medical University. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Army Medical University.

Preparation and characterization of HLM315

Synthesis of HA-AL and SA-AL

HA (250 mg, 0.0267 mmol), EDC (235.8 mg, 1.23 mmol), and NHS (141.6 mg, 1.23 mmol) were dissolved in deionized water and activated under vigorous stirring at room temperature for 1–2 h. ALN (601.748 mg, 1.85 mmol) was then added, and the pH was adjusted to about 8 with triethylamine. After 24 h of stirring, the mixture was dialyzed (MWCO 3500 Da) and lyophilized to obtain HA-AL. SA was modified to yield SA-AL. Conjugation was confirmed by 1H NMR.

Preparation of HLM, SLM, HLM315 and SLM315 nanoplatforms

Solution A was prepared by mixing 50 μL of HA-AL (20 mg/mL) with 50 μL of NaOH (2 mg/mL). Solution B was prepared by mixing 50 μL of MnCl2·4H2O (20 mM) with 100 μL of purified water or LTX-315 (2 mg/mL). Under 100W ultrasonic disruption, Solution B was added dropwise to Solution A, followed by sonication for 3 min to obtain the nanoparticles HLM or HLM315. SLM or SLM315 nanoparticles were prepared by replacing HA-AL with SA-AL in the formulation.

Characterization of Nanoparticles

Particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were analyzed by dynamic light scattering (DLS; Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90). Stability was assessed by monitoring size and zeta potential at 4°C. Morphology and elemental distribution were examined via transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Tecnai G2 F20), while manganese oxidation states were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha).

Encapsulation efficiency

The encapsulation efficiency (EE) was determined by separating free and encapsulated drugs via ultrafiltration centrifugation (MWCO 3 kDa). 1 mL aliquot of LTX-315 (1 mg/mL), 1 mL of the prepared HLM315 and SLM315 nanoparticles were separately placed into the sample chambers of 3 kDa ultrafiltration centrifuge tubes, which had been pre-activated by centrifugation with purified water. The samples were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min. The liquid in the filtrate collection tubes was then collected, and the EE of LTX-315 in the nanoparticles was measured using the protein BCA method, and free Mn2+ was quantified by ICP-MS analysis, with EE calculated as follows:EE(%)=(Totaldrug−Freedrug)/Totaldrug×100

A standard curve was established using LTX-315 solutions at concentrations of 0, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 μg/mL.

Cumulative release experiments for SLM315 and HLM315

SLM315 and HLM315 nanoparticles (400 μL) were dispersed in 12 mL of 100 mM HEPES buffer at pH 5.0, 6.5, or 7.0 and incubated at 37°C (30 rpm). At predetermined time points (0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36 and 48 h), 2 mL of medium was collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh buffer. For each sample, 1 mL was analyzed by UV spectrophotometry at 280 nm to quantify LTX-315 release, and the remaining 1 mL was subjected to nitric acid digestion followed by ICP-MS to determine Mn release.

GSH depletion by SLM and HLM

SLM and HLM nanoparticles were prepared and grouped according to the Mn concentration in the nanoparticles: 0.0625, 0.125, 0.25, 0.4, and 0.5 mM. GSH (10 μL, 50 mg/mL) was added as substrate, and HEPES buffer (100 mM, pH 6.5) was used to adjust the final volume to 1 mL. The mixtures were incubated at 37°C (30 rpm) for 8 h. After incubation, samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the GSH-depleting capacity of the nanoparticles was assessed using the Beyotime GSH and GSSG Assay Kit.

Methylene blue (MB) assay for ROS detection

Stock solutions were prepared as follows: 100 mmol/L NaHCO3, 100 μg/mL MB, 200 mmol/L H2O2, and 20 mM MnCl2·4H2O. Reaction systems contained final concentrations of: 100 μg/mL Mn2+ (as MnCl2·4H2O or HLM), 25 mmol/L NaHCO3, 10 μg/mL MB, and 10 mmol/L H2O2. Samples were shaken at 150 rpm for 120 min. For HLM group, absorbance at 665 nm was monitored every minute for the initial 10 min, followed by measurements at 30, 60, and 120 min. Other groups were measured only at 120 min. The decrease in MB absorbance at 665 nm directly correlated with •OH generation.

Hemolysis assay of HLM315

The hemolytic potential of HLM315 nanoplatforms was evaluated using murine erythrocytes. Whole blood was collected from BALB/c mice via retro-orbital puncture and centrifuged (1500 × g, 10 min, 4°C). After repeated PBS washes, red blood cells (RBCs) were resuspended to 2% (v/v) in PBS. HLM315 nanoplatforms were tested at concentrations ranging from 10 to 100 μg/mL. Following a 3- h incubation (37°C, 100 rpm), samples were centrifuged and hemoglobin release was quantified by measuring supernatant absorbance at 541 nm. Controls included PBS (negative) and 0.1% Triton X-100 (positive). Hemolysis percentage was calculated using the formula:Hemolysis(%)=(Asample−APBS)/(ATriton−APBS)×100

Cellular uptake and intracellular distribution of HLM315

The cellular uptake of various nanoparticle formulations containing FITC-labeled LTX-315 (FITC-LTX-315, SLM-FITC-315, HLM-FITC-315) was investigated. 4T1 cells were treated with these formulations at a final FITC-LTX-315 concentration of 50 μg/mL. After 2 h of incubation, the cells were analyzed using flow cytometry to quantify the mean fluorescence intensity. For qualitative assessment of intracellular distribution, 4T1 cells were seeded on glass coverslips. Following the same treatment protocol, the cells were fixed, and nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Subsequently, the samples were imaged using laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM).

Cytotoxicity evaluation of HLM315 nanoparticles

CCK-8 assay

The cytotoxic effects of PBS, LTX-315, HLM, SLM315 and HLM315 on 4T1 cells were determined via the CCK-8 assay. 4T1 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. After 8-12 h of incubation, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the various formulations. The treatments were applied at LTX-315 concentrations of 10, 20, 50, 80, and 100 μg/mL, corresponding to Mn2+ concentrations of 0.0625, 0.125, 0.25, 0.4 and 0.5 mM in the relevant groups. After 24 h of exposure, the CCK-8 reagent was added to each well according to the manufacturer's instructions. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated as follows: Cell Viability (%) = [(ODsample - ODblank)/(ODPBS - ODblank)] × 100.

The cytotoxicity assay of HLM315 nanoparticles on normal mouse mammary epithelial (HC11) cells was performed using the same method as described above.

Apoptosis assay

Cell apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-APC/7-AAD apoptosis detection kit. 4T1 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells per well. After 24 h, the cells were divided into six experimental groups: PBS, LTX-315, M315(free LTX-315 and Mn2+ ), HLM, SLM315 and HLM315. All treatments were administered at 50 μg/mL LTX-315 and 0.25 mM Mn2+. Following 24 h of incubation, the cells were harvested, processed according to the kit protocol, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Calcein-AM/PI staining

Cell viability was further visualized using the Calcein-AM/PI cell viability-cytotoxicity assay kit. 4T1 cells were seeded into 12-well plates at 1.5 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. The cells were then treated for another 24 h with PBS, LTX-315, M315, HLM, SLM315 and HLM315, maintaining LTX-315 and Mn2+ concentrations at 50 μg/mL and 0.25 mM, respectively. After treatment, all cells were collected, centrifuged, and stained according to the manufacturer's instructions. Fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Intracellular ROS detection

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). 4T1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at 1.5 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. The cells were then divided into six treatment groups (PBS, M315, LTX-315, HLM, SLM315, HLM315) and exposed to the respective formulations (LTX-315: 50 μg/mL; Mn2+: 0.25 mM) for 6 h. Subsequently, the cells were processed using the ROS assay kit and immediately observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope. To provide quantitative data, the fluorescence intensity corresponding to ROS generation in the different treatment groups was also measured by flow cytometry.

HLM315 induces ICD

HMGB-1 assay

Sterile glass coverslips were placed in 12-well plates. Logarithmically growing 4T1 cells (1.5 × 105 per well) were seeded and allowed to adhere. Cells were divided into six groups: PBS, M315, LTX-315, HLM, SLM315, and HLM315. Treatments were applied for 24 h using 50 μg/mL LTX-315 and 0.25 mM Mn2+ . Culture supernatants were collected for HMGB-1 ELISA. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and washed again. After fixation, the cells were permeabilized with blocking buffer containing 0.1% Triton-X-100 for 10 min, and then incubated with HMGB-1-AF488 primary antibody (1:1000) for 2 h at 37°C, and stained with DAPI. Coverslips were mounted onto slides with antifade reagent and imaged by LSCM.

Extracellular HMGB-1 ELISA

HMGB-1 levels in cell culture supernatants were measured using an HMGB-1 ELISA kit (Elabscience) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance at 450 nm was read using a microplate reader.

CRT exposure assay

Initial steps were identical to the HMGB-1 assay. After fixation, After fixation, the cells were incubated with CRT-AF488 primary antibody (1:1000) for 2 h at 37°C. Subsequent steps were the same as those described for the HMGB-1 assay.

Extracellular ATP assay

4T1 cells (1.5 × 105 per well) were seeded in 12-well plates and divided into PBS, LTX-315, M315, HLM, SLM315, and HLM315 groups. Treatments were applied for 24 h using LTX-315 at 50 μg/mL and Mn2+ at 0.25 mM. The cell culture supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, after which the ATP content in the samples was measured using an ATP assay kit.

Maturation of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) and macrophages (BMDMs)

Tibiae and femora were isolated from 6-8 week old male C57BL/6 mice euthanized by cervical dislocation. Skin and muscle tissues were removed, sterilized in 75% ethanol for 10 min, and transferred to a biosafety cabinet. Bones were flushed repeatedly, until bones turned white. Cell clumps were dissociated, and the suspension was filtered through a 70 μm sterile strainer. Cells were collected by centrifugation (200 g, 10 min), and plated at 2 × 105 cells per dish. GM-CSF (20 ng/mL final concentration) was added for BMDCs, and M-CSF (20 ng/mL final concentration) for BMDMs. On day 3, 10 mL of fresh medium containing the respective cytokines was added gently along the side wall. Cells were cultured for an additional 2 days. On day 5, medium was discarded. BMDCs were collected by gently flushing with fresh medium. Adherent, spindle/star-shaped BMDMs were washed with PBS and detached using trypsin. BMDCs and BMDMs were seeded into 24-well plates at 1 × 105 cells per well and treated with PBS, SLM, HLM, or LPS (20 ng/mL), for 24 h. Culture supernatants were collected for cytokine ELISA analysis. Cells were harvested for flow cytometric staining and analysis.

Levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-12 in BMDC and BMDM culture supernatants were measured using respective ELISA kits according to the manufacturers' protocols.

Western blot analysis of STING pathway activation

Logarithmically growing 4T1 cells were seeded in culture dishes. At 70-80% confluence, cells were treated with PBS, SLM, or HLM for 24 h. Cells were scraped, lysed, and incubated on ice for 30 min. Lysates were sonicated and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 5 min to collect supernatants. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Protein samples were diluted to equal concentrations with PBS, mixed with 5X Loading Buffer, and boiled for 10 min. Proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST for 2 h and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against STING, TBK1, p-TBK1, IRF3, p-IRF3, P65, p-P65, p-STING, and GAPDH. Membranes were washed three times with TBST (10 min each) and incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. After three additional TBST washes, bands were visualized using chemiluminescence substrate on an imaging system.

RT-qPCR analysis

Logarithmically growing 4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates. At 70-80% confluence, cells were treated with PBS, SLM, or HLM for 24 h. The culture supernatant was discarded, and cell monolayer was washed once with PBS. Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. One-step RT-qPCR was performed. Relative mRNA expression levels of IFN-β1, IFN-α1, IFN-α2, IFN-α4, and TNF-α were calculated using the standard curve method and normalized to β-actin. In vivo distribution of HLM315 nanoparticles

Cy7-labeled LTX-315 was used to prepare the nanoparticles SLM-Cy7-315 and HLM-Cy7-315. A tumor-bearing mouse model was established by subcutaneously inoculating BALB/c mice with 1 × 106 4T1 tumor cells. When the tumor volume reached approximately 300 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into three groups: Cy7-LTX-315, SLM-Cy7-315, and HLM-Cy7-315. The formulations were administered via tail vein injection. The fluorescence signal at the tumor site was monitored in real time using an in vivo imaging system at 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h post-injection. After the fluorescence signal had diminished completely, a second dose was administered. Mice were euthanized 18 h later, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) as well as tumors were harvested for ex vivo fluorescence imaging. Fluorescence intensity was quantified to evaluate the in vivo distribution and tumor-targeting efficiency of the nanoparticles.

Anti-tumor study in the 4T1 model

The 4T1 tumor model was established by inoculating 5 × 105 cells into the third mammary fat pad of BALB/c mice. When tumor volumes reached approximately 50 mm3, tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into 5 groups: PBS, M315, HLM, SLM315, and HLM315. Treatments were administered intravenously, 80 μg LTX-315 per mouse, every two days for a total of 8 doses. Tumor volume and body weight were measured every two days. On day 15, blood was collected via retro-orbital puncture for hematological and biochemical analysis. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, kidneys, and tumors per group were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for H&E staining and immunohistochemical analysis. Tumors from all mice were excised, weighed, and photographed. Spleens, draining lymph nodes, and tumor tissues were harvested, processed into single-cell suspensions, stained for flow cytometry, and analyzed to assess changes in immune cell populations. For mouse survival monitoring, additional animal experiments were performed and long-term survival was monitored. Ethic death was defined as tumor volume >2000 mm3 or maximum tumor diameter >20 mm.

Tumor rechallenge experiment

An orthotopic 4T1 breast cancer model was first established and treated with HLM315 when tumor volume reached ∼50 mm3, receiving eight consecutive treatments (days 0–7). Tumors were surgically resected under aseptic conditions on day 8, and the wounds were sutured. After wound healing, mice were rechallenged on day 14 by inoculating 4T1 cells into the contralateral mammary fat pad (opposite to the original tumor site). Age-matched mice were also inoculated with 4T1 cells and served as controls. Tumor growth was monitored from day 20. On day 30, mice were sacrificed, and tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed.

Safety evaluation of HLM315 nanoparticles

After eight doses, whole blood was collected for complete blood count analysis to evaluate changes in red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, and hemoglobin. Serum was separated for biochemical analysis of alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, blood urea nitrogen, uric acid, and creatinine to assess liver and kidney toxicity. For major organ toxicity, heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney and tumor tissues were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and H&E stained to observe histopathological changes.

Statistical analysis

All results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8. Data following normality tests were analyzed by one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for multiple groups. Results

Preparation and characterization of HLM315

To obtain nanoparticles with enhanced stability and high drug loading capacity, HA was phosphonated via EDC/NHS-mediated amidation, introducing phosphonate groups onto a portion of HA carboxyl residues to provide additional coordination sites for LTX-315 and Mn2+ during self-assembly. Sodium alginate (SA), which is biocompatible but lacks CD44-targeting capability, was modified using the same procedure and served as a non-targeting control. The 1H NMR spectra of the starting materials HA and SA showed no characteristic peaks in the chemical shift range of 2.3-3.2 ppm. In contrast, ALN exhibited a characteristic peak at approximately 3 ppm, corresponding to methylene protons adjacent to the amino group. After the synthesis, new peaks appeared within the 2.3-3.2 ppm range in the products, indicating successful conjugation of ALN to HA or SA and the formation of HA-AL and SA-AL polymers (Fig. S1). Manganese-based nanoparticles were prepared via a one-step aqueous self-assembly process by mixing HA-AL with MnCl2·4H2O under weakly alkaline conditions, yielding HLM nanoparticles. For drug-loaded nanoparticles (HLM315), LTX-315 was pre-mixed with MnCl2·4H2O prior to assembly, while SA-AL-based nanoparticles (SLM, SLM315) were prepared as controls. The resulting HLM, HLM315, SLM, and SLM315 all formed clear and transparent liquids (Fig. 2A). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements showed that HLM exhibited an average hydrodynamic diameter of 163.0 ± 4.11 nm, a PDI of 0.24 ± 0.22, and a zeta potential of −28.9 ± 0.4 mV. Upon loading with LTX-315, the average hydrodynamic diameter of HLM315 increased to 170.1 ± 3.39 nm and the zeta potential was −26.1 ± 1.7 mV. For the SLM system, the average hydrodynamic diameter was 169.1 ± 5.91 nm with a PDI of 0.23 ± 0.03 and a negative zeta potential. The drug-loaded SLM315 exhibited the largest average size among the tested formulations at 188.4 ± 2.45 nm, maintained a PDI below 0.3, and retained a negative surface charge (Fig. 2A–C). Stability assessment following 14 days storage at 4°C demonstrated good stability for both HLM and HLM315 formulations (Fig. S2). TEM analysis revealed that both HLM and HLM315 exhibited spherical-like morphology with diameters of approximately 100 nm, with HLM315 appearing modestly larger than HLM (Fig. 2D). SLM and SLM315 display a similar quasi-spherical morphology to HLM and HLM315, with particle sizes also ranging from 100 to 200 nm (Fig. 2E). The particle sizes observed by TEM were smaller than those determined by DLS, which is attributed to the hydrodynamic diameter determined by DLS, including the hydration layer adsorbed on the nanoparticle surface. This effect is particularly pronounced for hydrophilic nanoparticles, whereas TEM imaging reflects the dry-state size in the absence of solvent. Elemental mapping demonstrated the distribution of C, N, O, P, and Mn within the HLM and SLM315 nanoparticles (Fig. 2F and G). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis confirmed the elemental composition and manganese oxidation states in HLM. The Mn 2p spectrum exhibited a characteristic satellite peak at 644-648 eV and Mn 3s splitting energy (ΔE = 5.72 eV). Free LTX-315 and Mn2+ were separated from the SLM315 and HLM315 nanoparticles by ultrafiltration centrifugation. The amount of free LTX-315 was determined using a BCA protein assay based on its peptide nature, and free Mn2+ was quantified by ICP-MS analysis. The HLM315 encapsulation efficiency of LTX-315 was 85.22 ± 0.48%, and Mn2+ was 98.35 ± 0.015%; the SLM315 encapsulation efficiency of LTX-315 was 86.39 ± 0.70%, and Mn2+ was 99.37 ± 0.001%. To evaluate drug release, we incubated SLM315 and HLM315 nanoparticles in HEPES buffers at pH 5.0, 6.5, and 7.4, and collected samples at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h. Taking advantage of the peptide nature of LTX-315, which contains tryptophan residues, the cumulative release of LTX-315 was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 280 nm using UV spectrophotometry. The cumulative release of Mn2+ was quantified by ICP-MS. Under lysosomal conditions (pH 5.0), both nanoparticles showed the highest release rates, with cumulative release of LTX-315 and Mn2+ reaching ∼80% at 48 h. Under TME conditions (pH 6.5), the release was slightly reduced, with cumulative release of LTX-315 and Mn2+ reaching ∼60% from both nanoparticles at 48 h. In contrast, under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), cumulative Mn2+ release from HLM315 and SLM315 was ∼40%, whereas cumulative LTX-315 release remained below 20%. These results demonstrate that both SLM315 and HLM315 nanoparticles exhibit pH-responsive release behavior under weakly acidic conditions, supporting their potential application in tumor-targeted therapy (Fig. 2I and J). Since GSH levels are substantially elevated in the TME compared with normal tissues, manganese-based nanoparticles such as MnO2 have been reported to undergo redox reactions with GSH, in which Mn4+ is reduced to Mn2+ while GSH is oxidized to glutathione disulfide (GSSG) [25]. Accordingly, we incubated different concentrations of free Mn2+, SLM and HLM nanoparticles with GSH in HEPES buffer at pH 6.5 for 8 h, and then measured the amount of GSSG generation. The results demonstrated that both SLM and HLM were capable of consuming GSH in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2K).

In the MB decolorization assay, which indirectly reflects ROS generation via oxidative dye degradation, both free Mn2+ and HLM promoted MB fading in the presence of H2O2. Notably, the HLM induced rapid and complete decolorization within 30 min (Fig. 2L–M). Hemolysis assays indicated that HLM315 induced less than 5% hemolysis across LTX-315 concentrations ranging from 10 to 100 μg/ml, indicating favorable biocompatibility of the nanoparticles (Fig. 2N).

Cellular uptake and cytotoxicity evaluation of HLM315 nanoparticles

Efficient and specific cellular uptake is critical for nanoparticle efficacy. To evaluate uptake, FITC-labeled LTX-315 was used to prepare HLM315 and SLM315, and their internalization by 4T1 cells was compared with free LTX-315. LSCM revealed that the strongest intracellular fluorescence in cells treated with HLM315, followed by LTX-315 and SLM315 (Fig. 3A). Flow cytometry analysis of mean fluorescence intensity yielded consistent results with the LSCM observations (Fig. 3B). The enhanced cellular uptake of HLM315 was likely attributed to HA-mediated CD44 targeting, whereas the reduced uptake of SLM315 likely resulted from its negatively charged surface and lack of targeting capability. The cytotoxicity of different formulations against 4T1 cells was evaluated using CCK-8 assay at concentrations ranging from 10 to 100 μg/mL (equivalent to 0.0625-0.5 mM Mn2+). At low concentration (10 μg/mL LTX-315, 0.0625 mM Mn2+), all treatments showed minimal cytotoxicity. With increasing concentration, HLM315 consistently exhibited the strongest growth inhibition, followed by SLM315, whereas free LTX-315 and HLM alone were less effective. At the highest concentrations (100 μg/mL LTX-315, 0.5 mM Mn2+), near-complete cell death was observed in LTX-315-containing formulations, while HLM alone also showed limited cytotoxicity. These results indicate that LTX-315-mediated membrane disruption is the primary driver of tumor cell killing, and that Mn2+ further enhances cytotoxicity through a synergistic effect (Fig. 3C). We have also evaluated the cytotoxicity of HLM315 in the normal murine mammary epithelial cell line HC11. The results showed that HLM315 induced dose-dependent cytotoxicity in HC11 cells. However, at the nanoparticle concentration (LTX-315 at 50 μg/mL, Mn at 0.25 mM), the viability of 4T1 cells decreased to 4.36 ± 5.09% (Fig. 3C), whereas HC11 cell viability remained above 60% (Fig. S3). Evaluation of ICD induction and STING pathway activation

To evaluate ICD-inducing potential of our formulations, we first examined HMGB-1 localization in 4T1 cells via LSCM after 24 h of treatment. Compared with the PBS and HLM groups, cells treated with HLM315, SLM315, M315, or free LTX-315 exhibited markedly reduced nuclear HMGB-1 fluorescence. Notably, the combination of Mn2+ and LTX-315, particularly in the M315 and HLM315 group, resulted in the weakest nuclear fluorescence, indicating pronounced HMGB-1 translocation (Fig. 4A). Consistently, ELISA analysis showed that HLM315 group induced the highest extracellular HMGB-1 levels among all groups (Fig. S5), confirming efficient HMGB-1 release. Given that Mn2+ is a known activator of the cGAS-STING pathway, we next examined whether SLM and HLM could stimulate STING signaling. Additionally, the immunostimulatory effect of HLM and SLM on antigen-presenting cells (APCs) maturation were subsequently evaluated. BMDCs and BMDMs were isolated from mice and treated with the nanomaterials for 24 h. In BMDCs, HLM treatment significantly increased IL-6, IL-12, and TNF-α levels, consistent with robust DC maturation and inflammatory activation. (Fig. S8A). Similarly, in BMDMs, HLM markedly increased IL-12 and TNF-α secretion, consistent with polarization toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype (Fig. S8B). In vivo distribution and tumor targeting of HLM315

To evaluate the in vivo tumor-targeting capability of HLM315, Cy7-labeled LTX-315 was formulated into nanoparticles and administered via tail vein injection. In vivo fluorescence imaging revealed that tumors in all groups exhibited peak fluorescence at 2 h post-injection (Fig. 5A–B), likely due to rapid systemic distribution of the Cy7-labeled nanodrugs following intravenous administration. The orthotopic 4T1 breast cancer model is highly vascularized, with permeable tumor vasculature that facilitates nanoparticle extravasation into the tumor interstitium through endothelial gaps, enhancing permeability and retention effect (EPR) [38]. As circulation time progressed, fluorescence decay at the tumor site was slowest in the HLM-Cy7-315 group, with a clearly detectable signal persisting at 24 h post-injection. In contrast, tumor fluorescence in the SLM-Cy7-315 group gradually decreased and was nearly undetectable by 24 h, and free Cy7-LTX-315 exhibited the most rapid clearance, with only weak tumor-associated fluorescence remaining at 12 h (Fig. 5A–B). These findings indicate that HLM315 significantly prolongs the intratumoral retention of Cy7-LTX-315, which was attributable to HA-mediated active targeting, facilitating specific interactions with CD44-expressing tumor cells. Mice were euthanized at 18 h post-administration, and major organs heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, along with tumors were collected for ex vivo fluorescence imaging. Tumor tissues from the HLM-Cy7-315 group displayed the strongest fluorescence signals, followed by the SLM-Cy7-315, whereas fluorescence in the Cy7-LTX-315 group was barely detectable (Fig. 5C). Notably, fluorescence intensity was strongest in the liver, followed by the spleen, kidneys, and lungs, with no detectable signal in the heart, and overall organ-associated fluorescence was lower in the HLM-Cy7-315 group than in the other groups (Fig. S9), indicating reduced off-target accumulation. Discussion

ALN has been reported to exert antitumor effects through inhibiting tumor cell growth, inducing apoptosis, suppressing invasion and metastasis, modulating macrophage polarization, and exerting anti-angiogenic activity [[39], [40], [41]]. In our study, ALN was used as a structural component to enhance the stability and drug-loading capacity of the HLM315 nanoparticle system by exploiting its negatively charged phosphonate groups [42]. Under weakly alkaline conditions, the carboxyl and phosphonate groups on the HA-AL molecular chains dissociated, enabling coordination with LTX-315 and Mn2+. Simultaneously, Mn2+ reacted with OH− in the solution to form manganese hydroxide, which was rapidly oxidized by O2 to manganese oxide. The resulting manganese oxide remained linked to HA-AL, which encapsulated both LTX-315 and the manganese oxide particles, thereby preventing aggregation and precipitation and ultimately yielding stable HLM315 nanoparticles. This one-step self-assembly process, achieved by simple mixing and brief sonication, was rapid, easily reproducible, environmentally benign, cost-effective, and preserved the biological activity of each component, highlighting its translational potential. Through surface modification with HA-AL, further enabled CD44-mediated tumor targeting, reduced enzymatic degradation of LTX-315, and minimized off-target manganese accumulation, leading to enhanced tumor accumulation [34,43].

Functionally, HLM315 enabled the coordinated co-delivery of LTX-315 and manganese oxide, producing a synergistic antitumor effect, which integrating ICD induction, ROS generation, and STING pathway activation into a single platform. Compared with free LTX-315, HLM315 significantly enhanced tumor cell apoptosis and ICD induction, confirming a strong synergistic effect of LTX-315 and manganese oxide. Mechanistically, HLM315 accumulated in tumors and released LTX-315 and manganese oxide in response to the TME. LTX-315 released from HLM315 induced membrane disruption and DAMP release, initiating antigen presentation, while HLM315 generated Mn2+ promote ROS generation and potentiate cGAS–STING signaling in the tumor microenvironmet [18,44]. This cooperation amplified type I interferon production, facilitated DC maturation, and supported robust T-cell activation and immune memory formation, collectively converting the TME toward an immunologically active state [13,45]. The cascade of “active targeting → LTX-315-mediated membrane lysis → DAMPs release → Mn2+ dependent ROS generation coupled with potentiated cGAS–STING activation → enhanced adaptive immunity → immunological memory” formed a coherent and self-reinforcing chain of anti-tumor responses.

In summary, this study innovatively developed a multifunctional manganese oxide–based nanoplatform through a simple one-step self-assembly strategy that integrates active targeting, ICD induction, ROS generation, and STING activation. HLM315 represents a promising therapeutic strategy to overcome immunotherapy resistance in TNBC and may be extendable to other tumor types and metastatic models.
