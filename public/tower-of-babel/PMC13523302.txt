MiRNAs in hyperglycemia-induced metabolic memory: established mechanisms and emerging nuclear activation concepts
Authors: Mei Guo, Huan Duan, Daoping Ru, Shuai Yang, Wenqiang Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyperglycemia-induced metabolic memory refers to the phenomenon in which cells and tissues retain the persistent damage caused by elevated blood glucose even after glucose levels have returned to normal. Studies have demonstrated that noncoding RNAs, particularly microRNAs (miRNAs), contribute to hyperglycemia-induced metabolic memory through the epigenetic regulation of gene expression. Unlike the canonical function of cytoplasmic miRNAs in gene silencing, nuclear-activating miRNAs (NamiRNAs) have been proposed to interact with enhancers and potentially activate gene transcription, representing an emerging mode of nuclear miRNA function that remains to be validated in diabetic systems. In this review, we summarize the current pathogenic mechanisms of hyperglycemia-induced metabolic memory and highlight both the established and emerging roles of miRNAs in epigenetic regulation within this field.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder driven by a combination of genetic, environmental and lifestyle factors. In healthy individuals, blood glucose levels are maintained within a dynamic homeostatic range through the coordinated regulation of glucose metabolism involving multiple organs and systems, including the pancreas, liver, intestine, adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, and brain [1]. However, owing to impaired glucose metabolism homeostasis, patients with diabetes present elevated fasting and/or postprandial blood glucose levels. Accordingly, prolonged hyperglycemia can lead to various chronic complications, including macrovascular complications (such as atherosclerosis, hypertension, coronary heart disease, and stroke) and microvascular complications (such as diabetic nephropathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy). In particular, the pathogenic mechanisms of hyperglycemia-induced chronic complications are currently an active research area. Among these complications, hyperglycemia-induced metabolic memory has been closely linked to diabetic angiopathy. This review summarizes current advances in research on epigenetic regulation mediated by noncoding RNAs in this field, aiming to provide novel insights for early, accurate diagnosis and treatment.

The concept of metabolic memory and its effects on vascular complications

The phenomenon of “hyperglycemia-induced metabolic memory” is the persistent influence of the early metabolic environment on the occurrence and progression of diabetes-related vascular complications. Numerous large-scale clinical studies have suggested that the metabolic memory effect serves as a bridge between diabetes and its complications. For instance, 1,441 patients with type 1 diabetes who were randomly assigned to intensive or conventional treatment in the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT) [2] had a 39–76% reduction in the risk of developing diabetic retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy complications after an average follow-up of 6.5 years of intensive treatment compared with conventional treatment [2]. In the Epidemiology of Diabetes Interventions and Complications study [3], the intensive treatment group exhibited a 42% reduction in the risk of cardiovascular events. In addition, the United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS) included 3,867 newly diagnosed patients with type 2 diabetes who were randomly assigned to intensive or conventional treatment. After 7.5–12.5 years, intensive treatment significantly improved outcomes related to both microvascular and macrovascular complications (a 1% decrease in HbA1c, corresponding to a 14% reduction in the risk of myocardial infarction, a 12% reduction in stroke, a 16% reduction in heart failure, a 37% reduction in microvascular complications, and a 21% reduction in all diabetes-related endpoints) [4, 5]. Similar to the UKPDS study, the Veterans Affairs Diabetes Trial (VADT) included 1,791 patients with type 2 diabetes randomly assigned to intensive or conventional treatment. After a median of 9.8 years of observation a significant reduction in the risk of major cardiovascular events was observed (a reduction of 8.6 events per 1,000 patient-years, amounting to a 17% relative reduction in extensive treatment), although there was no significant difference concerning cardiovascular death or all-cause mortality between the two groups [6]. The Action to Control Cardiovascular Risk in Diabetes (ACCORD) trial [7, 8] and the Action in Diabetes and Vascular Disease trial [9], conducted among a large number of patients with type 2 diabetes, failed to show a benefit of intensive treatment in reducing the risk of major cardiovascular events (nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, or cardiovascular death). However, both studies revealed that intensive therapy delayed the onset of albuminuria [7, 8] and resulted in significant relative reductions in nephropathy [9], and some measures of eye complications and peripheral neuropathy [8], even though these trials included patients with long-standing type 2 diabetes and high cardiovascular risk. Moreover, data from a 40-year follow-up of the UKPDS [10] and a 30-year follow-up of the Da Qing Diabetes Prevention Study [11] revealed that the protective benefit from cardiovascular disease complications resulting from the legacy effect of early intensive glycemic control persisted for many years after the initial intervention trial was completed. These trials indicate that early intensive treatment for patients with diabetes continues to yield benefits several years after the cessation of such interventions. Conversely, if patients with diabetes are in a state of hyperglycemia for a long time, even if glycemic control is achieved later, they are still prone to develop microvascular and macrovascular complications.

The pathogenesis of large vascular lesions is complex and may be related to various factors, such as blood lipid levels, blood pressure, and blood uric acid levels. Therefore, patients with diabetes-related vascular complications should not only strictly control their blood glucose level but also control their blood lipid level, blood pressure, and blood uric acid level [9–13]. Many studies emphasize the importance of individualized treatment and suggest that patient-specific conditions and risk factors, such as age, disease progression, macrovascular risk, and patient lifestyle and disease management capacity, should be considered when treatment strategies are being developed [13, 14]. More studies on intensive glycemic control with novel medications, such as glucagon-like pipetide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, which are associated with a low risk of hypoglycemia and positive cardiovascular effects, are needed to clarify the final all-cause outcomes [15].

Notably, although HbA1c levels are currently the primary indicator of therapeutic outcomes, it does not capture fluctuations in blood glucose levels. In other words, patients who achieve HbA1c targets may still experience glucose variability. Moreover, beyond HbA1c, glucose variability is now recognized as an independent risk factor for diabetic complications, contributing to the achievement of HbA1c targets [16, 17]. A retrospective case–control study revealed that a shorter time in the tight range (TITR), measured with continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) at baseline and defined as the percentage of time in the target glucose range of 3.9–7.8 mmol/L (70–140 mg/dL) during a 24-h period, was associated with an increased risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in patients with type 2 diabetes. This result indicated that tight glycemic control within the physiological range may be crucial for reducing long-term mortality risk [18]. Some studies suggest that more intense early and even remission to normal levels of glucose control after the diagnosis of prediabetes and diabetes may be beneficial to patients in the long term [19, 20]. Thus, these studies suggest the existence of legacy effects of early metabolic control.

Current experimental models and potential mechanisms for metabolic memory

Related cellular and animal experiments have revealed the presence of metabolic memory. One study revealed that the overexpression of fibronectin and type IV collagen can still be detected after endothelial cells are exposed long term to high concentrations of glucose and subsequently shift to a normal glycemic environment [21]. El-Osta et al. used short-term high-glycemic medium to culture endothelial cells and reported the continuous activation of oxidative stress and high expression of inflammatory factors even after the transition to normal glucose concentration culture [22]. In rat models, interventions or islet transplantation undertaken several months after the onset of diabetes proved far less effective than those performed only a few weeks later. In rats with subpar glycemic control throughout the whole process, reestablishing good glycemic levels after six months can neither prevent an increase in retinal capillary density nor reduce the apoptosis of retinal cells [23]. Furthermore, in a dog model of diabetes, researchers demonstrated that correcting blood glucose levels after 2.5 years of hyperglycemia did not mitigate worsening diabetic retinopathy, whereas intensive glycemic treatment effectively inhibited retinal capillary microaneurysms and other types of vascular damage when normal blood glucose levels were restored early within two months [24]. Zheng et al. [25] reported that compared with that in lower-glucose conditions (3 nmol/L), CDK5/p25 activity in Min6 pancreatic beta cells cultured under high-glucose conditions (25 nmol/L) was significantly increased. Overexpression of p25 in these cells using adenovirus further increased CDK5 activity, which led to reduced insulin secretion and increased apoptosis after glucose stimulation compared with the levels in normal cells. Inhibition of CDK5/p25 activity could protect the function of pancreatic beta cells and promote insulin secretion [25]. Therefore, an effective diabetes treatment regimen should extend beyond early intensive glycemic control to encompass the reversal of the underlying metabolic dysregulation driven by sustained high glucose levels.

Insulin resistance, inflammatory response, oxidative stress, nitrative stress, and advanced glycation end products (AGEs) are potential mechanisms related to high-glycemic metabolic memory. AGEs are irreversibly formed when glucose reacts nonenzymatically with aminos, lipids and nucleic acids over a course of days to weeks. The formation and accumulation of AGEs are known to increase at an accelerated rate under hyperglycemic conditions. Studies have shown that AGEs and their receptor RAGE evoke inflammatory reactions in endothelial cells and diabetic vessels. Additionally, the AGE–RAGE axis causes oxidative stress through the generation of inflammatory cytokines, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and reactive nitrogen intermediates (RNI), thus altering the innate and adaptive immune response [26]. AGE–RAGE-induced oxidative stress impairs macrophage cholesterol efflux and decreases reverse cholesterol transport by suppressing ATP binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1) and ABCG1, which could contribute to accelerated atherosclerosis in diabetes. The cross-linking modification of collagens by AGEs contributes to the decreased elastic properties of large vessels and increased arterial stiffness in patients with diabetes and/or aged patients. AGE-modified collagens are minimally degraded and remain in diabetic vessels, kidneys and hearts for a long time even after glycemic control has been achieved. The phenomenon of metabolic memory is partly explained by sustained activation of the AGE–RAGE axis in diabetic vessels, kidneys and hearts [27]. However, current therapies targeting these harmful factors, such as antioxidative stress, anti-inflammatory, and anti-AGE agents, have shown limited clinical efficacy and cannot effectively block the persistent body damage caused by metabolic memory [28, 29]. Recent advancements in genetic research related to diabetes have been substantial, yet monogenic diabetes accounts for less than 5% of the occurrence of diabetes [30], and the significant role of environmental factors in the etiology and progression of diabetes deserves our attention.

In recent years, the effects of endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial abnormalities, and epigenetic modifications on metabolic memory have been further elucidated. Epigenetics refers to heritable changes in the expression activity of genes without changing their nucleotide sequences and is primarily regulated through processes such as DNA methylation, modifications of histone amino acid residues (acetylation, methylation, ubiquitination, and phosphorylation), chromatin remodeling, and regulation of mRNA translation by noncoding RNAs (such as miRNAs, snoRNAs, piRNAs, and lncRNAs) [31]. These mechanisms are interrelated: DNA methylation and histone modifications can affect the expression of miRNAs, which in turn can modulate DNA methylation levels or alter histone modifications, thereby participating in the regulation of gene expression [32]. Furthermore, recent studies have highlighted that one of the most prevalent and abundant modifications of RNA molecules present in eukaryotes, N6-methyladenosine (m6A), affects RNA translation, degradation, and splicing through the methylation of base sites on gene fragments. These complex regulatory relationships require further study in the context of diabetes and vascular complications [33]. Recent studies have identified novel miRNAs involved in the regulation of AGE-associated signaling in the context of diabetic micro- and macrovascular complications [34]. The identification of specific miRNAs that target the AGE–RAGE axis holds promise for managing T2DM and its complications. Improving the understanding of the pathways involved in hyperglycemic memory can lead to the identification of mechanism-driven strategies to decrease vascular damage and cardiovascular outcomes in patients with diabetes. High glucose levels can cause irreversible changes in gene expression through epigenetic regulation, meaning that cells remember hyperglycemic conditions even after their glucose levels return to normal. The following sections discuss several miRNAs that are dysregulated by hyperglycemia.

miRNA-mediated epigenetic regulation in diabetes complications

Discovery of miRNAs and their canonical function in gene silencing

Noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) broadly encompass RNA molecules that do not encode proteins. Among the rich variety of ncRNAs, miRNAs and lncRNAs have been the focus of extensive research. MiRNAs are endogenous small noncoding RNAs that are typically 21–25 nucleotides in length. Since the discovery of the first miRNA, lin-4, by Victor Ambros in 1993, miRNAs have been generally believed to inhibit protein translation or degrade mRNA at the posttranscriptional level by binding to the 3’-UTRs of target gene mRNAs in the cytoplasm [35]. The miRNA gene is usually transcribed by RNA polymerase II (pol II) in the nucleus, with the initial product being a large pri-miRNA with a cap structure (7MGpppG) and a polyadenylate tail (AAAAA). The pri-miRNAs are then processed into a pre-miRNA composed of 70 nucleotides under the action of Drosha ribonuclease and its cofactor Pasha. Pre-miRNA is transported to the cytoplasm by RAN–GTP and exportin 5. Subsequently, another ribonuclease, Dicer, cleaves it to produce an approximately 22-nucleotide miRNA: miRNA* duplex. This duplex is quickly incorporated into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), in which a mature single-stranded miRNA remains. Mature miRNAs regulate genes posttranscriptionally through base pairing with the 3’ untranslated region of the target gene mRNA, ultimately degrading the mRNA or inhibiting the translation of specific proteins, thereby completing some physiological processes or signaling pathway regulation [36, 37]. Recently, miRNAs have been shown to exert their biological functions by binding to the 5’ UTRs and even some exon regions of encoding genes. It is estimated that more than 60% of human protein-encoded genes are regulated by miRNAs, which broadly participate in numerous biological processes associated with body growth, development, and disease pathogenesis. Evidence suggests that hyperglycemia may induce the aberrant functions of posttranslational histone modifications and DNA methyltransferases, leading to alterations in miRNAs in endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, cardiomyocytes, and retinal and renal cells, all of which may be functionally impaired and contribute to vascular complications in patients with diabetes [38, 39]. As epigenetic modifications are mitotically stable, they can cause cells to memorize environmental stimulus signatures. Such properties of epigenetic processes make their molecular mechanisms parallel to metabolic memory. Furthermore, epigenetic modifications are reversible. In contrast to DNA sequence changes and mutations, epigenetic changes are not inflexible, and they provide a promising landscape for many diseases linked with epigenetic changes to be considered for epigenomic reprogramming by epigenetic drugs. Several miRNAs have been extensively studied for their close association with diabetes and its vascular complications.

Represented miRNAs for diabetes-related complications in traditional pathways

Research on the role of miR-122 in diabetes and its complications

MiR-122, located on chromosome 18 at 18q21.31, is a highly expressed liver-specific miRNA that accounts for approximately 70% of all liver-expressed microRNAs [40]. It plays critical roles in maintaining liver growth, differentiation, and metabolic balance. Dysregulation of miR-122 expression is not only significantly related to liver fat accumulation, HBV, HCV, and the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma but also impacts glucose and lipid metabolism and nervous system activity and vascular endothelial function in patients with diabetes. First, the influence of miR-122 on glucose and lipid metabolism is evident. Studies have shown that the expression of plasma miR-122, along with that of miR-17, miR-20a, and miR-20b, is significantly greater in patients with diabetes with fatty liver disease than in those without this complication (P < 0.05). It has been suggested that miR-122, in conjunction with the waist‒hip ratio (WHR) and the low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL) level, could serve as biomarkers for the early diagnosis and risk assessment of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease in patients with diabetes [41]. Elevated levels of miR-122 have also been shown to be significantly correlated with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and insulin resistance (IR) [42]. In patients with monogenic diabetes (HNF1A-DM, MODY3), serum miR-122 levels are significantly lower than those in patients with type 2 diabetes and healthy controls [43]. In animal studies, berberine has been shown to regulate blood glucose levels by inhibiting the activity of the hepatocyte nuclear factor 4α (HNF4α) and the miR-122 pathway [44]. Second, the effect of miR-122 on diabetic microvascular complications has been studied. A prospective study by Pastukh, N., et al. (2019) analyzing 40 patients with varying degrees of diabetic retinopathy [10 healthy controls, 10 patients with diabetes without retinopathy (NDR), 10 with nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR), and 10 with proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR)] revealed that serum miR-122 levels gradually increased from healthy controls to those with NDR and NPDR and decreased in the PDR stage but were accompanied by an increase in let-7f-5p (a proangiogenic miRNA) levels. The decrease in the miR-122 level in patients with PDR was significant compared with that in both patients with NPDR (P = 0.016) and all non-PDR patients (P = 0.0002). It was hypothesized that the elevated levels of miR-122 in patients with diabetic retinopathy might be linked to its ability to prevent angiogenesis and proliferation, whereas the significant decrease in those with PDR might be related to the inhibition or exhaustion of its antiangiogenic and antiproliferative defense systems [45]. High expression of miR-122 under high-glucose conditions in human retinal pigment epithelial cells (ARPE-19) inhibited the expression of tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 3 (TIMP3). The decreased activity and increased apoptosis of ARPE-19 cells induced by high-glucose conditions and overexpression of miR-122 could be reversed by the high expression of TIMP3. The effects of miR-122 on diabetic retinopathy are mediated through the regulation of TIMP3 [46]. A study of 42 patients with diabetic nephropathy revealed that serum levels of miR-122-5p and miR-99b were significantly greater than those in healthy controls, whereas the serum levels of miR-20a and miR-486 were significantly lower. Signaling pathways such as the PI3K/Akt, p53, 5’-AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), forkhead box O (FOXO), and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways are involved in pathological processes related to the progression of diabetic kidney disease (DKD), including oxidative stress, the inflammatory response, and apoptosis [47]. Third, miR-122 is significantly associated with diabetic neuropathy, coronary atherosclerotic heart disease, and myocardial fibrosis. Overexpression of miR-122 has been shown to prevent neuronal cell death in ischemic stroke and promote cardiomyocyte hypertrophy through the inhibition of FOXO3 in neuronal and cardiomyocyte cells [48, 49]. A prospective 5-year study combining cardiac MRI with miRNAs in patients with diabetic cardiomyopathy revealed that overexpression of miR-122-5p affects the viscoelastic contraction of myocardial cells by downregulating MMP2 [50]. Numerous miRNAs have been shown to be associated with diabetic cardiomyopathy and diabetic peripheral neuropathy [51, 52], with studies also suggesting a relationship between miRNAs and the stability of atherosclerotic plaques [53].

A significant positive association between miRNA-122 and inflammatory factors, including tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin 6 (IL-6), was observed in one study. The upregulation of TNF-α and IL-6 induces insulin resistance through the activation of the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and MAPK signaling pathways and consequently disrupts insulin signaling, leading to insulin resistance [54]. Another study revealed that miR-122 may promote the development and progression of IR directly through inhibition of the insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF-1R)/PI3K/AKT pathway in hepatic cells [55]. In addition, miR-122 has been implicated in glucose homeostasis through indirect effects on AMP-activated kinase and glucose 6-phosphatase, key regulatory enzymes of hepatic gluconeogenesis. HNF-4α controls hepatic gluconeogenesis and lipid metabolism through the expression of miR-122 [56]. Circulating afamin is involved in T2DM-related complications, and its concentration is positively correlated with miR-122 expression. Among patients diagnosed with T2DM, those with longstanding diabetes (> 5 years) had significantly higher levels of circulating afamin and miR-122 expression than individuals with a shorter duration of diabetes (≤ 5 years) did (P < 0.05) [57]. Thus, miR-122 has been proposed as a potential biomarker for the early screening and diagnosis of diabetes.

Research on the role of miR-214 in diabetes and its complications

MiR-214 is in intron 14 of the dynamin 3 (DNM3) gene on human chromosome 1q24.3 [58]. Studies have shown that miR-214 is involved in the growth, differentiation, and morphological alterations of cells in muscles, bones, myocardium, kidneys, liver, pancreas, vascular endothelium, and various tumor cells. It is also involved in the physiological and pathological processes of tissues and organs [59–62]. These investigations indicate that miR-214 can regulate body functions in different cells and tissues through different targets and regulatory pathways. By targeting phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), miR-214 is involved in the inflammatory responses of monocytes under hyperglycemic and chronic renal failure (CRF) conditions. Moreover, AGEs can increase miR-214 expression in monocytes, which impedes monocyte apoptosis by targeting the proapoptotic molecule PTEN [59]. Overexpression of miR-214 was shown to suppress gluconeogenesis in hepatocytes in vitro and in vivo by activating the transcription factor 4 (ATF4)-forkhead box protein O1 (FOXO1) pathway [60]. Studies on mouse models and patients with fatty liver have shown that inhibition of miR-214-3p expression ameliorated fatty liver disease through increased autophagic activity through increased expression of Ulk1 [63]. Upregulation of miR-214 inhibited the autophagy function of Ulk1 in diabetic proximal renal tubular cells through the p53/miR-214/Ulk1 pathway and affected the progression of DKD [64]. Another study revealed that miR-214 suppressed oxidative stress in diabetic proximal renal tubular cells by affecting the ROS/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway through UCP2 and influenced the occurrence of diabetic nephropathy (DN) [65]. Studies in vivo and in vitro have shown that cross talk between miR-214 and PTEN attenuated glomerular mesangial cell hypertrophy under diabetic conditions [66]. In diabetic corneal endothelial cells and lens epithelial cells, miR-214 affects diabetic ocular complications through different pathways, such as the KCNQ1OT1/miR-214/caspase-1 and miR-214-3p/MMP2 axes [67, 68]. A further study demonstrated that ferroptosis contributes to retinal damage at the tissue, cellular, and molecular levels in diabetic retinopathy (DR). Overexpression of miR-214-3p in high-glucose-treated human retinal vascular endothelial cells (HRECs) resulted in the downregulation of p53 expression and the upregulation of SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression, thereby alleviating ferroptosis-induced injury. The p53 inhibitor pifithrin-α can alleviate high-glucose-induced damage to HRECs, making it a potential therapeutic target for DR [69]. Several miRNAs have been shown to be persistently altered in various pathological processes, including myocardial cell hypertrophy, apoptosis, fibrosis, autophagy, oxidative stress, and heart failure, in animal models of diabetic cardiomyopathy after normalization of blood glucose levels. For example, the expression of miRNAs implicated in the hypertrophic growth of cardiomyocytes (miR-1, miR150, miR-199a, miR-133a, miR-214, miR-29a, miR-125b, miR-221, and miR-212) was strongly impaired by hyperglycemia and remained unchanged after insulin treatment [70]. Aurora et al. [71] reported that the upregulation of miR-214 had a protective effect on myocardial cells. Further study revealed that miR-214 targeted sodium/calcium exchange 1 (Ncx1), inhibited several downstream effectors of Ca2+ signaling, and played a critical role in regulating the homeostasis of Ca2+ in cardiomyocytes and myocardial injury. Another study revealed that the expression of miR-214, a regulator of dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP4), is decreased in obese patients with IR and may serve as a diagnostic biomarker. The upregulation of miR-214 could improve IR in obese rats and adipocytes, indicating that miR-214 has therapeutic potential for obesity and IR [72]. However, one study revealed that overexpression of miR-214 promoted calcification by facilitating the production of proinflammatory mediators such as ICAM-1, IL-6, IL-8, and MCP-1 and accelerating the osteogenic differentiation of aortic valve interstitial cells (AVICs) through activation of the MyD88/NF-ĸB signaling pathway. MiR-214 inhibition has the potential to suppress inflammatory processes and AVIC calcification, which might provide a new target for calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) diagnosis and treatment [73].

Research on the role of miR-199a and other miRNAs in diabetes and its complications

MiR-199a-3p is located at positions 10,817,426–10,817,496 on chromosome 19, within intron 16 of the DNM2 gene [74]. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the target genes of miR-199a-3p are enriched in cell components such as the cell membrane, cytoplasm, and nucleus; participate in molecular functions such as phosphoprotein binding, receptor signaling protein activity, and transcription inhibitor activity; and are involved in biological processes such as the regulation of cellular metabolic processes, the biosynthesis of cellular macromolecules, and gene expression [75]. Studies have revealed that miR-199a is highly expressed in the pancreatic islet cells of diabetic and obese rats. Further studies in diabetic MIN6 cells have demonstrated that the activity of pri-miR-199a2 can be activated by glucose [76]. Other studies have indicated a significant increase in miR-199a levels in the blood of patients with diabetes, which is associated with the inhibition of GLUT4 expression by miR-199 [77]. One study suggested that miR-199a-3p expression was reduced in patients with T2DM compared with that in healthy subjects and that miR-199a-3p promoted the proliferation, migration and autophagy of HUVECs, potentially by regulating the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling pathway. Therefore, miR-199a-3p may function as a protector of vascular endothelial cells [78]. Additionally, miR199a-5p affects the autophagic function of liver cells by inhibiting ATG14 activity and is associated with decreased insulin sensitivity in the liver (miR199a-5p/ATG14/autophagy/insulin resistance axis) [79]. MiR199a-5p has also been shown to inhibit the expression of the SP1 gene in diabetic lens epithelial cells, downregulating the epithelial‒mesenchymal transition, and is associated with the development of diabetic cataracts [80]. PCR analysis of miRNAs in left ventricular models of diabetic rats has indicated that abnormal expression of miR-199a and other miRNAs is involved in myocardial fibrosis (miR-125b, miR-150, miR-199a, miR-29b, and miR-30a), heart failure (miR-423, miR-499, and miR-199a), oxidative stress (miR-221, miR-146a, miR-34a, miR-210, miR-19b, miR-125b, miR-27a, and miR-155), apoptosis (miR-320b, miR-378, and miR-34a) and hypertrophic proliferation of myocardial cells, and these irregular expression levels persist even after blood glucose levels return to normal [70]. The persistent dysregulation of the miRNAs even after glycemic normalization exemplifies its role in maintaining the “memory” state. One study evaluated the expression of candidate miRNAs in relation to diabetic polyneuropathy (DPN) and cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy (CAN) in a cohort of 49 Italian patients with T2DM and 15 healthy subjects. The results revealed higher expression of miR-128a and lower expression of miR-155 and miR-499a in patients with T2D with DPN than in those without DPN. Lower expression of miR-155 (P = 0.05) was observed even in patients with CAN than in those without CAN [81]. A further study investigated the possible correlations between the above three circulating miRNAs and obesity. The analyses revealed significant decreases in the expression levels of miR-155 and miR-499a in obese subjects. In particular, the reduction appeared to be even more significant in patients with type 2 diabetes with central obesity. The lower miR-155 expression level observed in patients with T2D with central obesity is consistent with the fact that obese subjects have a lower frequency of the rs767649 variant allele, which is located in the miR-155 promoter region [82]. Circulating miRNA species associated with a decline in beta cell function were investigated in the Treatment Options for Type 2 Diabetes in Adolescents and Youth (TODAY) study, an intervention trial in which half the participants reached the primary outcome of hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 8% for at least 6 months, within 4 years during the observation phase. Increased levels of miRNA-155 and miRNA-130b and decreased levels of miRNA-126 were associated with a decrease in the C-peptide oral disposition index (oDI). The expression of miRNA-122 was negatively correlated with C-peptide oDI at baseline and 24 months. Circulating miRNAs may be predictors of beta-cell failure and provide potential mechanistic insights into the aggressive nature of youth-onset type 2 diabetes [83]. Studies have also indicated that miRNAs are regulatory molecules that can be packaged into exosomes and secreted from cells. One study revealed that adipose tissue macrophage (ATM)-derived exosomal miRNAs can modulate in vivo and in vitro insulin sensitivity. In addition, ATM-exosomal miR-155 promotes obesity-induced insulin resistance [84]. As exosomes prolong miRNA stability in circulation, exosomal miRNAs have been suggested as a meaningful diagnostic and therapeutic approach for related diseases [85].

Based on the documented evidence, miR-122, miR-214, and miR-199a collectively contribute to metabolic memory in diabetes by persistently modulating key cellular processes such as insulin signaling and inflammatory reactions even after blood glucose levels are normalized. This sustained disruption across multiple tissues involved in the liver, myocardium, kidneys, and retina facilitates the development and progression of diabetic complications. Accordingly, their abnormal expression patterns underscore their critical roles in coordinating metabolic memory, positioning them as promising biomarkers for early diagnosis and potential therapeutic targets.

Novel perspectives of NamiRNAs in diabetes and its complications

Limitations of traditional miRNAs in diabetes and its complications

Analyses of the above miRNAs associated with diabetes and its vascular neurological complications revealed that numerous miRNAs regulate blood glucose levels and its associated complications (Table 1). Furthermore, the types and quantities of miRNAs expressed in different tissue cells in high-glucose environments vary. As endogenous regulatory factors, miRNAs have certain tissue or cellular specificity. Each miRNA can regulate multiple target genes, and specific target mRNAs can be controlled by multiple miRNAs at the same time. The same miRNA may differ in its expression and the signaling pathways influencing the target gene across various tissues and organs. The functions of a tissue or organ necessitate coordinated action from multiple miRNAs, involving both upregulation and downregulation, thus forming a complex gene expression regulatory network essential for maintaining normal body functions. Therefore, the control models of a single gene, single pathway and single point in cells are inadequate to explain the complex pathogenesis of multigene lesions such as type 2 diabetes. Furthermore, miRNAs may exhibit off-target effects, resulting in low specificity and unexpected side effects that limit their therapeutic utility.

Potentials of NamiRNAs and their function in gene activation via targeting enhancers

In the expression regulatory mechanism of eukaryotic genes, the regulation of chromatin levels plays an extremely important role. Changes in the structure of chromatin that can be remembered during cell passage become part of epigenetics. Chromatin regulation primarily involves ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling and the covalent modification of histones. Histone modification can affect the opening and closing of chromatin, thereby regulating gene expression. Notably, the acetylation of histones H3 and H4 and the methylation of histone H3K4 represent the open chromatin structure with transcriptional activity. Based on the distribution range of these histone modifications, chromatin opening is divided into local chromatin opening located in the cis elements of the gene and large-scale chromatin opening across the enhancer, promoter, and intergenic regions [86]. A large-scale loose chromatin structure is conducive to the active transcription of genes. Interactions between enhancers and promoters may be involved in the formation of a wide range of open structures of chromatin. Enhancers, as cis elements that promote gene transcription by forming complexes with transcription factors, enhancer-binding proteins, or activators, interact with general transcription factors bound to the promoter to activate the transcription of related genes [87]. In 2013, Richard A. Young’s laboratory at the Whitehead Institute for Biomedical Research in the United States proposed the concept of super enhancers (SEs). These SEs refer to ultralong cis-acting elements, spanning 8–20 kb, densely packed with master transcription factors and cofactors, activating the expression of identity-determining genes in stem cells and thereby playing a crucial role in regulating cell fate [88]. Enhancers tend to circulate with neighboring genes and bind to them to activate their transcription. Most of these interactions occur within the 50 kb range of the enhancer, although many can occur within several Mb or even greater distances, whereas SEs can regulate gene expression over long distances through miRNAs and regulatory loops. Research has indicated that SEs are crucial in not only determining cell fate during stem cell differentiation and development but also the development of diseases such as cancer, autoimmune diseases, and diabetes, where pathogenic gene expression is strongly associated with the aberrant activation of certain superenhancers [89, 90].

Increasing evidence has demonstrated that many miRNAs are located within the nucleus, and it is difficult to make progress with traditional theories of negative regulation by miRNAs in the cytoplasm. Given this, research into nuclear miRNAs faces numerous challenges. Professor Yu Wenqiang’s research team at Fudan University studied the epigenetic regulatory mechanism of miRNA itself, and they accidentally discovered that more than 400 miRNA precursors in the genome highly overlap with the histone modification marks H3K4me1 or H3K27ac of the enhancers [91]. This provided an unexpected link between miRNAs and enhancers, both crucial components of molecular biology with specific cellular or tissue specificity. Given the gene activation characteristics of enhancers, it is speculated that miRNAs may induce gene activation through enhancers in the nucleus. Previous studies have indicated that the import of miRNAs into the nucleus is dependent on sequence homology to nuclear localization signals or the dynamic cell cycle [92–94]. Subsequent work confirmed that these nuclear miRNAs activate genes by interacting with enhancers, a process dependent on the integrity of the enhancers. A typical example is the oncogenic role of NamiR-1246 in osteosarcoma [95]. Specifically, NamiR-1246 promotes osteosarcoma cell migration by activating migration-related genes, including MMP1, through targeting the enhancer region near the MMP1 genomic locus. This interaction between NamiR-1246 and the corresponding enhancers is supported by the luciferase assay results: cotransfection of miR-1246 with a pGL3-promoter vector containing the wild-type enhancer significantly increased luciferase activity, whereas mutation of the NamiR-1246 binding site in the enhancer region markedly reduced it.

In addition, NamiRNAs can increase H3K27ac modification at their target enhancer regions, thereby increasing enhancer activity and promoting gene activation [91, 96, 97]. Similarly, inhibitors targeting NamiRNAs can reduce gene activation by lowering H3K27ac levels [91]. For example, in hepatocellular carcinoma, HBV-miR-2 promotes oncogenic gene networks by increasing H3K27ac at host enhancer regions [97], whereas its inhibitor significantly reduces H3K27ac at these enhancers and suppresses the overexpression of oncogenes, highlighting the potential of NamiRNAs in regulating enhancer activity. As shown in Fig. 1, these NamiRNAs perform gene activation functions mediated by enhancers within the nucleus, distinguishing them from traditional miRNAs that function through cytoplasmic negative regulation [98]. The discovery of NamiRNAs reveals significant complementary aspects of traditional miRNA functions and has substantial implications for understanding diabetes and its complications.

Interactions between NamiRNAs and enhancers may regulate diabetic metabolic memory

A genome-wide association study (GWAS) on type 2 diabetes utilizing 3D spatial structure analysis revealed that the cores of several SEs are associated with target genes, such as insulin gene enhancer protein 1 (ISL1) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGFA), influencing insulin secretion and predicting susceptibility to type 2 diabetes [99]. Other studies have shown that superenhancers play vital roles in expressing and maintaining the functional memory of renin cells, which is essential for preserving multisystem homeostasis [100]. A study revealed the pivotal function of SEs and superenhancer-driven noncoding RNAs (seRNAs) in the progression of metabolic diseases. A novel seRNA known as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α-related seRNA (PPARα-seRNA) was shown to be involved in the deterioration of cardiac function and the exacerbation of metabolic abnormalities in diabetic cardiomyopathy (DbCM). Mechanistically, PPARa–seRNA overexpression promoted binding of the histone demethylase lysine-specific demethylase 4B (KDM4B) and decreased H3K9me3 levels in the promoter region of PPARα, enhancing the transcription of PPARα. This finding suggests a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of DbCM [101]. The diabetic cardio-renoprotective effects of GLP-1 RAs and SGLT2 inhibitors have been demonstrated in clinical trials [102]. Epigenetic factors, including changes in the expression of miRNAs induced by GLP-1 RAs and SGLT2i, have been discussed [103]. However, the potential effects of the NamiRNA-enhancer axis have not yet been studied.

In contrast to the well-characterized cytoplasmic miRNA functions summarized above, the roles of NamiRNAs in diabetes and hyperglycemia-induced metabolic memory remain largely unexplored. Current indirect evidence for NamiRNAs is derived mainly from (1) genome-wide overlap between miRNA precursors and enhancer histone marks, (2) in vitro gene activation assays in nondiabetic cell lines, and (3) conceptual extrapolation from superenhancer biology [104–106]. To date, no direct demonstration of NamiRNA-enhancer interactions has been reported in diabetic vascular, renal, or retinal cells. Similarly, there is a lack of evidence that such interactions persist after glucose normalization, a defining feature of metabolic memory. Notably, although NamiRNA-enhancer-mediated gene activation has been generally validated across various models in published works, it has not yet been investigated in the context of diabetes. Thus, the NamiRNA-enhancer framework should be viewed as a promising but preliminary hypothesis that requires rigorous experimental validation.

By serving as a link between the genome and environment, epigenetic marks are influenced by the microenvironment to alter the expression of genes implicated in disease pathogenesis. The human genome and epigenome are highly correlated and interact dynamically. As gene regulators, miRNAs influence gene expression and epigenetic modifications and modify phenotypes (e.g., disease pathogenesis). Diabetes is a multisystem disease affecting different organs. Diabetic micro- and macrovascular complications share common risk factors and may be regarded as a metabolic–vascular continuum (Fig. 2). As highlighted by some studies, miRNAs may act as mediators of cell‐to‐cell communications. Exosomes, including cell-specific and site-specific miRNAs whose functions are altered due to hyperglycemia, may serve as good messengers regulating tissue responses to a disturbed internal milieu and may become indicators of disease progression and targets for therapeutics.

Clinical translation of NamiRNAs: biomarkers, risk stratification, and therapeutic implications

As nuclear-localized miRNAs capable of activating gene expression by targeting enhancers, NamiRNAs hold significant promise for translating molecular insights into clinical practice. Given their high stability in circulating biofluids (e.g., plasma) and tissue-specific expression patterns, NamiRNAs enable noninvasive detection of early pathological changes. On the one hand, their dynamic expression profiles may serve as potential predictive indicators of diabetes onset, progression, or treatment response. On the other hand, NamiRNAs may act as stable, cell type–specific biomarkers that reflect active enhancer networks, particularly in tissues affected by diabetic complications. Accordingly, distinct NamiRNA signatures may delineate patient subgroups with divergent clinical trajectories, potentially stratifying individuals at risk for metabolic memory or progressive organ damage. Integrating NamiRNA-based classifiers with conventional clinical parameters could refine prognostic models and guide personalized intervention intensity. From a therapeutic perspective, the NamiRNA–enhancer axis offers novel opportunities, as targeting specific NamiRNAs could modulate enhancer activity to reset pathological gene expression programs sustained by metabolic memory. However, this strategy awaits rigorous validation in diabetic models and clinical cohorts before any translational potential can be realized. In theory, early modulation of specific NamiRNAs could induce long-lasting protection, reducing the need for lifelong polypharmacy. Nevertheless, prospective cohorts and interventional trials are needed to validate NamiRNA-based biomarker panels, risk scores, and miRNA-directed therapies, ultimately translating these molecules from the bench to the bedside.

Conclusion and Outlook

In-depth research on the epigenetics of the metabolic memory mechanism is important for further understanding the onset and development of diabetes and its vascular complications. Notably, in recent years, the concept of metabolic memory and its implications have broadened to include hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia, hypoxia, and other metabolic disorders. In addition, factors such as aging and sex may affect metabolic memory in different ways. The cumulative cellular damage caused by aging may permanently lower the threshold for metabolic dysfunction, while sex-related differences in physiology and risk exposure could explain why some individuals are more vulnerable to long-term “memory” from metabolic insults than others are.

There are several inherent limitations in this work. First, as an emerging concept, evidence on NamiRNA is largely based on preclinical studies with limited clinical sample sizes and requires validation in large-scale human cohorts. Second, owing to the focus on miRNA‒enhancer interactions, our discussion of the degree of crosstalk with other epigenetic layers is relatively limited with respect to metabolic memory. Undoubtedly, these limitations underscore critical avenues for future research on metabolic memory and propel NamiRNA-targeted therapy.

Further exploration of the mechanism of metabolic memory in type 2 diabetes by linking intranuclear miRNAs with enhancer/super enhancer and target genes holds long-term promise for advancing our understanding of diabetes-related complications. Strategies aimed at “erasing” detrimental metabolic memory through NamiRNA networks require extensive further investigation. This line of research is expected to ultimately contribute to a theoretical foundation for future advancements in precision medicine, translational medicine research, early diagnosis and comprehensive management of diabetes and its complications.
