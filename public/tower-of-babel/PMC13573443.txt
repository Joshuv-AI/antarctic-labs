Guiding off-target nanomedicine repurposing in spleen for cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Caoyi You, Kun Wang, Fulong Man, Jialong Dong, Gengyao Liu, Haitao Liu, Weimin Yin, Jianwei Cheng, Yang Li, Li Chen, Yongyong Li, Haiqing Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The inevitable splenic sequestration of nanomedicine represents a major delivery challenge that compromises tumor accumulation and may contribute to off-target burden. Rather than attempting to eliminate this fate, we propose to functionally repurpose it into an immunologically productive process. Distinct from conventional spleen-targeted strategies, we aimed to modulate the biological fate of tumor-targeted nanomedicines by engineering their surface with screening-selected hybrid cell membranes. A tumor cell membrane-to-erythrocyte membrane ratio of 10:1(TRM) was identified as a representative balance point that preserves optimal tumor-associated accumulation while biasing a fraction of off-target nanoparticles (NPs) toward the spleen. Zinc imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) NPs were employed as a model therapeutic core. The resulting hybrid cell membrane–camouflaged nanoplatform (TRM@ZIF-8) exhibited immune-associated effects, where the tumor-associated fraction was linked to inflammasome-related signaling and inflammatory cell death–associated responses, while the spleen-biased fraction was associated with activation of splenic antigen-presenting cells. This behavior is consistent with the collective influence of membrane-associated protein features, including functions related to cluster of differentiation 47(CD47) and Band 3, together with the intrinsic immunostimulatory properties of the ZIF-8 core. Importantly, splenectomy markedly attenuated the therapeutic effect, providing organ-level evidence that spleen involvement is functionally associated with the observed antitumor immune response and tumor growth suppression. This work provides a proof-of-concept framework for guiding off-targeted nanomedicine repurposing in spleen for cancer immunotherapy, rather than regarding it solely as a delivery limitation.Graphical Abstract The original image was too large to upload, so I have compressed it for submission. Introduction

Inevitable off-target accumulation of nanomedicine in the spleen represents a major waste of therapeutic payload, directly restricting tumor delivery and diminishing the overall antitumor efficacy of nanotherapeutics [1–4]. Despite the theoretical promise of the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect and the continuous evolution of sophisticated active targeting ligands—ranging from antibodies and peptides to aptamers [2, 5–10]. Quantitative biodistribution analyses consistently reveal that over 95% of intravenously injected nanoparticles are sequestered by the reticuloendothelial system (RES) within minutes of administration [1, 3, 11]. While the liver typically entraps the majority of this “lost” dose (often 60–90%), the spleen also accounts for a significant fraction of accumulation [12, 13]. This massive off-target distribution is not merely a waste of therapeutic payload; it poses a substantial risk of systemic toxicity and limits the maximum tolerated dose [2, 14, 15]. Given that complete evasion of RES clearance is biologically nearly impossible [16, 17], a critical question arises: Can we pivot from fighting this intrinsic clearance mechanism to repurposing it for therapeutic gain?

To repurpose off-target accumulation, the biological context of the accumulation site should be carefully considered. Liver is mostly associated with immune tolerogenic regulation excepted for subjecting to very strong danger signals [18]; spleen, as a major peripheral immune organ enriched in antigen-presenting cells, may be more favorable for mediating systemic immune activation for protecting the body health. Accumulating evidence indicates that the spleen actively communicates with distant malignancies via a functional spleen-tumor immune axis [19, 20]. Within this axis, splenic immune activation critically shapes tumor progression by regulating the generation and trafficking of effector immune cells to the tumor microenvironment (TME) [19, 21]. Structurally, the spleen is uniquely equipped to orchestrate this process: specialized macrophages in the marginal zone efficiently capture circulating particulates and relay antigen signals to the white pulp [22–24]. Here, splenic dendritic cells cross prime T cells, while follicular B cells facilitate antibody production, initiating a systemic response that fuels the TME with activated lymphocytes [21, 25]. Thus, the spleen is not merely a passive reservoir but a critical checkpoint governing systemic surveillance. By preferentially guiding a fraction of off-target nanoparticles toward splenic immune compartments, rather than allowing predominant accumulation in the liver, it may be possible to engage the spleen-tumor immune axis and thereby functionally leverage off-target accumulation to support antitumor immunity [20, 21].

Cell membrane coating provides a biomimetic toolkit for guiding nanoparticle fate, leveraging intrinsic biological signatures for immune evasion and homologous recognition [26–29]. For instance, tumor cell membranes are widely employed to confer homotypic targeting [29, 30]. Regarding splenic redirection, however, previous studies indicate that diverse biological membranes—including those from neutrophils, macrophages, and erythrocytes—all exhibit inherent splenic targeting potentials due to distinct biological mechanisms [13, 21, 31–33], such as erythrocyte membranes are noted for senescence-mediated clearance [12, 13, 23]. The existence of multiple candidates highlights the necessity of systematically screening these membranes to identify the superior “splenic guide.” Despite the translational momentum of such biomimetic systems—with several formulations advancing to early clinical stages [15, 17, 29, 34] —current strategies predominantly prioritize biodistribution optimization over such systemic off-target repurposing [10, 14]. Consequently, a critical gap remains in developing a system that can function as an effective splenic guide without compromising tumor affinity. Furthermore, simply creating hybrid membranes is insufficient; the fusion ratio represents an important parameter, as it is associated with variations in the surface distribution of functional proteins, such as CD47 and Band 3, which are functionally related to balance the tumor accumulation and splenic redistribution [35–37]. A systematic evaluation is therefore required to identify a membrane formulation capable of balancing these two distinct biological goals.

Herein, we introduce an off-target repurposing strategy that differs conceptually from conventional spleen-targeted delivery systems. Rather than engineering nanoparticles to actively target the spleen as a primary delivery destination, we sought to exploit the inevitable off-target biodistribution of tumor-directed nanocarriers and preferentially bias a fraction of this off-target accumulation toward the spleen for functional immune activation (Scheme 1). To implement this concept, we systematically screened erythrocyte, neutrophil, macrophage, and tumor cell membranes to identify a membrane formulation capable of promoting splenic engagement, and our results identified the erythrocyte membrane as the most suitable component for this purpose. Based on this, ZIF-8, a porous nanomaterial with promising nanoadjuvant potential [38], was employed as a model nanomedicine, and a hybrid membrane-camouflaged nanoplatform (TRM@ZIF-8) with a screening-selected 10:1 tumor-to-erythrocyte ratio was engineered. This configuration maintains homotypic tumor recognition while selectively biasing the inevitable off-target fraction toward the spleen by fine-tuning the balance between CD47-mediated immune evasion and Band 3-mediated splenic guidance. RNA sequencing suggested that ZIF-8, as an immuno-active carrier, was associated with inflammasome-related signaling and inflammatory cell death–associated responses. This study provides proof-of-concept evidence that, by rationally designing the surface interface, unavoidable off-target clearance may, under defined conditions, be functionally leveraged as an immunologically relevant component that supports systemic antitumor immunity.

Results and discussion

Screening optimal cell membrane fusion ratios to bias off-target nanomedicine distribution toward the spleen while preserving tumor accumulation

To identify the most suitable cell membrane for biasing off-target nanoparticle distribution toward the spleen, four candidate membranes were selected based on previous studies reporting their distinct splenic associations. Erythrocyte membranes possess a natural affinity for the spleen, as damaged erythrocytes are physiologically cleared through splenic filtration [39]. Neutrophil-derived membranes have also been reported to accumulate substantially in the spleen within several hours after systemic administration in mice [40]. In addition, macrophages are among the most abundant and functionally important immune cells in the spleen, implying that immune cell membranes possess an inherent tendency to home to the spleen [41]. DIR-stained vesicles derived from 4T1 cell membranes (4T1m), red blood cell membranes (RBCm), macrophage membranes (MΦm), and neutrophil membranes (NEm) were administered to mice via tail vein injection. In vivo fluorescence imaging was first employed to determine the optimal spleen enrichment time and cell membrane type during 24 h monitor. As shown in Figure S1, all four types of cell membrane vesicles reached peak fluorescence signals in the spleen 6 h post-injection and weakened thereafter. Therefore, liver, spleen, and tumor tissues were harvested 6 h post-injection to compare fluorescence intensity differences (Fig. 1A). Fluorescent semi-quantitative analysis revealed comparable accumulation of 4T1m and RBCm in tumors (Fig. 1B), with RBCm exhibiting the most significant enrichment in the spleen (Fig. 1C). No significant difference between 4T1m and RBCm accumulation in the liver (Fig. 1D). However, fluorescence imaging provides only semi-quantitative information, making it difficult to accurately compare the precise accumulation levels of different membrane vesicles within tissues. To obtain more precise quantitative results, gold nanoparticles as detection label were employed to be coated by different cell membranes for its relative bioinert with low biological background signal [42]. Results of Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) showed a similar enrichment tendency as that for pure cell membrane vehicles at 6 h post-injection. i.e. 4T1m and RBCm exhibited the similar high enrichment efficiency in tumor tissue with no significant difference between them; MΦm and NEm accumulated at lower levels (Fig. 1E). In the spleen, RBCm showed significantly higher enrichment than other groups (Fig. 1F). In the liver, no significant differences were observed among groups (Fig. 1G). Detailed gold content data for each group are provided in Table S1. Collectively, these findings indicate that RBCm was the best potential cell membrane candidate to preferentially bias off-target nanoparticle accumulation toward the spleen while minimizing the impact on tumor-associated accumulation.

Building on these findings, RBCm were then fused with 4T1m at various ratios camouflaged on gold nanoparticles to identify a representative formulation that can bias a larger proportion of off-target nanoparticles toward the spleen without weakening tumor recognition, thereby establishing a foundation for subsequent immune activation within the spleen. The tested ratios of 4T1m to RBCm were 1:0, 20:1, 10:1, 1:1, 1:10, and 1:20. These hybrid membrane vesicles coated on gold nanoparticles were intravenously administered to mice, and the gold content in the liver, spleen, and tumor tissues was quantified by ICP-MS. In tumor tissues, the 20:1 and 10:1 groups showed enrichment levels comparable to those of the 1:0 group (pure 4T1m vesicles) (Fig. 1H). In spleen tissues, increasing the RBCm proportion enhanced splenic accumulation, with the 10:1 group showing 1.37-fold higher accumulation than the 20:1 group (Fig. 1I). In liver tissues, the 10:1 group exhibited 0.8-fold enrichment relative to the 20:1 group (Fig. 1J). To more directly evaluate the relative degree of organ redistribution, we further calculated the spleen-to-liver accumulation ratio across different fusion ratios (Figure S2A). In parallel, the tumor-to-liver accumulation ratio was also analyzed to assess the relative preservation of tumor-associated distribution under increasing erythrocyte membrane content (Figure S2B). The 10:1 group showed a markedly increased spleen-to-liver ratio compared with the 1:0 and 20:1 groups, indicating a clear shift in off-target distribution from hepatic sequestration toward splenic engagement. At the same time, the tumor-to-liver ratio remained comparable to, or slightly higher than that of the 1:0 and 20:1 groups, whereas further increases in erythrocyte membrane content led to a marked decline in tumor-to-liver ratio. Notably, although higher RBCm-containing groups further increased splenic accumulation, they were also associated with a pronounced reduction in tumor accumulation. Therefore, the 10:1 fusion ratio was selected not because it produced the highest splenic uptake, but because it achieved a balanced redistribution profile characterized by an increased spleen-to-liver ratio together with a relatively preserved tumor-to-liver ratio. Notably, a consistent trend was observed across the tested fusion ratios, in which increasing erythrocyte membrane content progressively increased the spleen-to-liver ratio while, beyond the 10:1 condition, markedly reducing the tumor-to-liver ratio, indicating a trade-off between tumor-associated distribution and spleen-biased redistribution. In this context, the 10:1 configuration represents a representative balance point that maintains tumor-associated functionality while enabling effective spleen engagement. This behavior is unlikely to follow a simple linear relationship, but instead may reflect a threshold-dependent biological response determined by the coordinated balance between tumor-derived antigen availability and immune organ engagement. Importantly, the biological relevance of this redistribution does not rely solely on the absolute increase in splenic accumulation, but rather on the coordinated biodistribution shift involving reduced liver sequestration, enhanced spleen engagement, and preserved tumor accumulation. Given the central immunological role of the spleen as a major peripheral immune organ enriched in antigen-presenting cells and macrophages, even a moderate redistribution toward this organ may be sufficient to amplify downstream systemic immune activation. It should also be noted that these biodistribution data reflect early organ distribution patterns at a single post-injection time point and therefore should not be interpreted as evidence of sustained organ retention or long-term clearance behavior. To figure out the possible reasons behind the superior in vivo performance of the 10:1 fusion membrane, SDS-PAGE was conducted to compare overall membrane protein profiles among different fusion ratios (Fig. 1L). The results showed broadly similar total protein patterns across the 20:1, 10:1, and 1:1 membranes, indicating that the fusion process did not substantially alter the global membrane composition. However, Western blot analysis revealed ratio-dependent differences in key functional proteins as shown in Fig. 1K and Figure S3A with the semi-quantitative data of WB. Nanoscale flow cytometry further confirmed the coexistence of CD47 and Band 3 at the single-particle level, with a distinct double-positive population observed, supporting coordinated protein presentation on hybrid membrane-coated nanoparticles (Figure S4). CD47 is known to interact with SIRPα on macrophages and has been associated with reduced phagocytic recognition [43, 44]. The 10:1 fusion membrane retained a CD47 level comparable to that of the 20:1 group, suggesting preservation of CD47-related membrane features that may contribute to limiting rapid macrophage-associated clearance. Meanwhile, Band 3, an erythrocyte-specific skeletal protein, modulates red blood cell passage through the spleen [45]. Its expression is associated with subtle reductions in membrane deformability and exposure of senescence-related epitopes, which preferentially promote splenic sequestration through the mechanical filtration of the red pulp. The Band 3 level in the 10:1 membrane was slightly lower than that of the 1:1 group, the moderate Band 3 expression avoided an overly reinforced erythrocyte phenotype that could interfere with tumor-homing capacity. Meanwhile, cells with mildly impaired deformability tend to be retained within the narrow interendothelial slits and subsequently captured by splenic macrophages. In contrast, hepatic clearance is typically triggered by pronounced opsonization or substantial membrane damage, such conditions was not reflected by the modest Band 3 variations observed here. These protein distribution features provide a plausible molecular basis for the observed early biodistribution profile of the 10:1 fusion membrane. Specifically, the 10:1 fusion membrane was suggested to retain a coordinated representation of CD47 and Band 3, which was associated with differences in macrophage uptake and a higher degree of splenic accumulation, while maintaining tumor accumulation capability. However, this interpretation should be considered a mechanistically supported model protein rather than a fully resolved causal framework. To further examine whether Band 3-associated function contributes to the biodistribution pattern of the hybrid membrane, DIDS (4,4’-Diisothiocyanostilbene-2,2’-disulfonic acid), a Band-3 inhibitor, was used as a functional perturbation reagent prior to in vivo administration. In vivo fluorescence imaging showed that DIDS-treated nanoparticles coated with the fusion membrane displayed increased liver-associated fluorescence, decreased spleen-associated fluorescence, and a significantly reduced spleen-to-liver fluorescence ratio compared with untreated ones (Figure S5). These results indicate that perturbation of the erythrocyte membrane-associated component weakened the original spleen-biased redistribution pattern. Considering the established association of DIDS with Band 3-related anion exchange activity, this experiment provides functional evidence that Band 3-associated function is involved in shaping the optimized biodistribution behavior of the 10:1 fusion membrane in vivo. Together with the protein expression analysis, these data support the mechanistic relevance of coordinated membrane protein composition to the favorable in vivo fate of the 10:1 fusion membrane. Collectively, these findings identify RBCm as a favorable membrane component for promoting splenic accumulation, and further indicate that a 10:1 fusion ratio of 4T1m to RBCm achieves a balanced redistribution outcome by preserving tumor accumulation while enhancing splenic engagement and reducing hepatic sequestration of off-target nanoparticles.

Physicochemical characterisation of TRM@ZIF-8 nanoplatforms

Based on the screening results, the generality of the selected optimal hybrid membrane (4T1m: RBCm = 10:1) were further verified by using ZIF-8 metal organic framework as model nanomedicine core to form ZIF-8–loaded hybrid membrane vesicles (TRM@ZIF-8) (Fig. 2A). As shown in Figure S6D, the spleen-to-liver accumulation ratio increased by 1.17-fold after ZIF-8 was coated with TRM comparing to naked ZIF-8. Fluorescence microscopy confirmed that 4T1m and RBCm underwent successful membrane fusion rather than simple mixing (Fig. 2B). This efficient fusion is likely driven by the intrinsic lipid–lipid affinity between the two membrane sources. Importantly, membrane fusion enables the co-presence of tumor-derived recognition-associated ligands and RBC membrane–associated components, including CD47 and Band 3, which is associated with immune-related functional effects and difference in vivo distribution patterns, while maintaining tumor accumulation capability. High-magnification TEM imaging further revealed a continuous membrane layer coating the surface of the nanoparticles. Consistently, elemental mapping detected abundant phosphorus within this layer (Fig. 2C), a signal derived from the phospholipid headgroups of native membranes, confirming the formation of an intact bilayer. Following membrane coating, the nanoparticles displayed an increase of approximately 33 nm in hydrodynamic diameter (Fig. 2G), a size shift that is characteristic of membrane functionalization and is consistent with the formation of an external membrane layer. This moderate enlargement also indicates that the coating process did not induce nanoparticle aggregation, supporting the stability of the formulation during systemic circulation. In parallel, the surface potential of the nanoparticles shifted from the positive charge of bare ZIF-8 to a negative potential after membrane coating (Fig. 2F). This inversion reflects the dominance of negatively charged membrane components. Such a charge profile is advantageous because it mimics natural cell membranes and reduces nonspecific protein adsorption and phagocytic clearance, thereby enhancing in vivo stability. To assess membrane protein retention, SDS–PAGE analysis was performed on TRM@ZIF-8. As shown in Fig. 2D, ZIF-8 alone contained no proteins, while TRM and TRM@ZIF-8 displayed nearly identical protein patterns, indicating efficient preservation of membrane proteins during coating. The retention of these inherent functional proteins is particularly important, as they are responsible for mediating the homologous recognition and immune interactions observed in subsequent studies. Collectively, these characterizations confirm the successful integration of the fusion membrane onto ZIF-8 nanoparticles. Furthermore, X-ray diffraction (XRD) confirmed that the crystalline structure of ZIF-8 remained unchanged after membrane coating (Fig. 2E). The preservation of lattice integrity ensures that the inherent properties of ZIF-8, such as high porosity with potential adjuvant effect, and pH-responsive degradation, are not compromised. The hydrodynamic size of TRM@ZIF-8 remained stable in PBS over 7 days, with no apparent aggregation, indicating good colloidal stability under physiological buffer conditions (Figure S7). This structural stability is essential for maintaining the therapeutic performance of the nanoplatform.

In vitro evaluation of cytocompatibility, molecular basis of tumor cell damage, and immunoactivation induced by TRM@ZIF-8

After material characterization confirmed the successful preparation of TRM@ZIF-8, its biocompatibility and antitumor potential were systematically examined in vitro. The cytotoxicity of TRM@ZIF-8 toward normal and tumor cells was assessed using the CCK-8 assay across a range of concentrations. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, dendritic cells (DCs) maintained proliferative activity comparable to the control even at 100 µg/mL, whereas the viability of 4T1 tumor cells decreased sharply to below 25%, indicating marked preferential cytotoxicity toward tumor cells. Consistently, macrophages exhibited excellent tolerance to TRM@ZIF-8 at 100 µg/mL, with nearly 100% viability (Figure S8), further supporting the favorable cytocompatibility of the nanoplatform toward immune cells. To elucidate the molecular basis underlying this preferential cytotoxicity toward tumor cells, transcriptomic profiling was conducted following TRM@ZIF-8 treatment. As shown in Fig. 3C, comparison with the PBS control revealed extensive transcriptional alterations, with a substantial number of genes being significantly upregulated or downregulated, indicative of pronounced molecular remodeling associated with TRM@ZIF-8. Gene Ontology enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes demonstrated significant overrepresentation of biological processes associated with inflammatory responses, regulation of interleukin-1β production, and cellular responses to inflammatory stimuli, as well as enrichment of inflammasome-related and inflammatory cell death–associated processes (Fig. 3D). In addition, multiple immune-regulatory processes were enriched, including pathways involved in immune response modulation and leukocyte-associated signaling. Collectively, these transcriptomic changes suggest the engagement of inflammasome- and inflammation-associated molecular programs, providing molecular context for the observed preferential cytotoxic effects toward tumor cells.

To further support the transcriptomic findings, inflammasome-associated signaling was investigated. Western blot analysis (Fig. 3E, Figure S3B) revealed marked upregulation of cleaved caspase-1, GSDMD, and NLRP3 in 4T1 cells following TRM@ZIF-8 treatment, suggesting activation of inflammasome-related pathways and inflammatory cell death–associated responses. In parallel, fluorescence imaging using the Zn²⁺-responsive probe TSQ showed pronounced intracellular Zn²⁺ accumulation in TRM@ZIF-8–treated cells, accompanied by elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release, indicative of compromised membrane integrity (Figure S9). Mechanistic interrogation further demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of NLRP3 signaling with MCC950 substantially attenuated this response, whereas treatment with ZnCl₂ reproduced a comparable activation profile, supporting the involvement of Zn²⁺ in inflammasome activation. Consistent with inflammasome activation, TRM@ZIF-8 significantly increased the secretion of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 (Fig. 3F and G), further supporting inflammasome-associated inflammatory responses and inflammatory cell death–related signaling. Given the incorporation of immunologically relevant membrane components, the immunostimulatory potential of TRM@ZIF-8 toward innate immune cells was subsequently evaluated. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that DCs exposed to TRM@ZIF-8 at 100–200 µg/mL exhibited more than a two-fold increase in CD80 and CD86 expression compared with untreated controls (Fig. 3H and I), indicating robust DC maturation within a non-cytotoxic concentration window. In parallel, macrophages treated with TRM@ZIF-8 (100 µg/mL) displayed increased CD80⁺CD86⁺ populations and a 2.6-fold elevation in the CD80/CD206 ratio, reflecting enhanced maturation and polarization toward an M1-like phenotype (Figure S10). To further distinguish the contribution of the hybrid membrane from that of the ZIF-8 core, gold nanoparticles were harnessed to instead as a relative inert nanoparticle core coated with hybrid membrane. Flow cytometric analysis showed that these particles induced modest but significant differences in the proportions of CD80⁺CD86⁺ DC2.4 cells and CD80⁺CD86⁺ RAW264.7 cells at 100 µg/mL (Figure S11). In addition, macrophages treated with hybrid membrane-coated gold nanoparticles exhibited an increased CD80/CD206 ratio, indicating a shift toward a more pro-inflammatory polarization profile. These results suggest that the hybrid membrane itself is not completely immunologically inert and can contribute, to a certain extent, to immune cell activation and polarization, but is insufficient to account for the full magnitude of immune activation observed with TRM@ZIF-8. These findings together suggest that the immunostimulatory activity of TRM@ZIF-8 is more likely to arise from the combined effects of hybrid membrane-mediated redistribution and the intrinsic immunostimulatory property of the ZIF-8 core.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that TRM@ZIF-8 exhibits excellent cytocompatibility with immune cells while exerting selective cytotoxic effects on tumor cells. Through Zn²⁺-associated inflammasome activation, together with hybrid membrane–associated immunomodulatory cues, TRM@ZIF-8 acts as an immunomodulatory nanoplatform associated with coordinated antitumor immune responses at the cellular level.

In vivo immune activation via spleen-mediation and antitumor efficacy of TRM@ZIF-8

To evaluate the in vivo antitumor efficacy of the TRM@ZIF-8 platform and to examine whether this effect is associated with spleen involvement, a 4T1 breast cancer mouse model with or without splenectomy was established. Tumor-bearing BALB/c mice were randomly assigned to six treatment groups: G1 (PBS), G2 (ZIF-8), G3 (TRM@ZIF-8 with splenectomy), G4 (TM@ZIF-8), G5 (RM@ZIF-8), and G6 (TRM@ZIF-8). The subsequent group names are the same, unless otherwise specified. Groups G3–G5 were specifically designed to assess the dependence of therapeutic efficacy on spleen involvement and membrane composition. The treatment schedule is outlined in Fig. 4A. At the experimental endpoint, excised tumors were collected for gross morphological comparison (Fig. 4B). Mice treated with TRM@ZIF-8 exhibited markedly reduced tumor burdens, with tumor volumes decreased by approximately two-thirds compared with the control group, and were visibly smaller than those in all other treatment groups. In contrast, splenectomized mice receiving TRM@ZIF-8 showed tumor sizes comparable to control animals, suggesting a loss of therapeutic benefit following spleen removal. Tumor growth kinetics were further evaluated by longitudinal measurement of tumor volumes (Fig. 4C). In mice with an intact spleen, TRM@ZIF-8 treatment markedly slowed tumor growth over time, resulting in a substantially reduced final tumor burden. In contrast, this growth-suppressive effect was largely abolished following splenectomy, supporting an important contribution of spleen-associated immune processes to the antitumor response. Consistent with the tumor growth inhibition results, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that TRM@ZIF-8 prolonged survival in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice, whereas this benefit was weakened in splenectomized mice (Figure S12). Given the established role of the spleen in systemic immune regulation and antitumor immunity [46–48], these findings further support an organ-level contribution of spleen-mediated off-target repurposing to the overall therapeutic effect. Because splenectomy may broadly alter systemic immune homeostasis in addition to eliminating the spleen as a site of nanoparticle engagement, these findings should be interpreted as organ-level evidence supporting spleen involvement rather than definitive proof of a specific spleen-resident immune mechanism. To assess the robustness of this therapeutic effect at the individual level, tumor growth trajectories from each mouse were analyzed separately (Figs. 4E–J). TRM@ZIF-8–treated mice exhibited consistently restrained tumor progression across individuals, with reduced inter-animal variability compared with other treatment groups. By contrast, splenectomized mice and those treated with single-membrane-coated formulations displayed heterogeneous tumor growth patterns, indicating more variable therapeutic outcomes. Histological analysis further supported these observations. Immunohistochemical staining of Ki67 revealed a reduced proportion of proliferating tumor cells in the TRM@ZIF-8 group compared with other treatments (Fig. 4K), consistent with the observed suppression of tumor growth. Throughout the treatment period, no significant changes in body weight were observed across groups (Fig. 4D). Histopathological examination of major organs showed no obvious treatment-related pathological damage (Figure S14). Mild hepatocellular vacuolation was observed in liver sections of all groups, which may be attributable to non-adverse glycogen-related changes under non-fasted conditions [49]. Consistently, serum biochemical and hematological analyses revealed no obvious abnormalities (Figures S13 and S15), further confirming the absence of significant systemic toxicity of TRM@ZIF-8 under the current experimental conditions.

Collectively, these results indicate that TRM@ZIF-8 exerts consistent antitumor effects across individual animals and that these effects are functionally associated with the presence of an intact spleen. By redirecting off-target nanoparticles toward the spleen through optimized hybrid membrane design, TRM@ZIF-8 provides proof-of-concept evidence that spleen-biased off-target repurposing can be translated into a meaningful antitumor benefit while maintaining favorable biosafety.

Based on the therapeutic outcomes observed in tumor-bearing mice, we further investigated the immune responses mediated by TRM@ZIF-8. Following systemic administration, nanomedicines are predominantly sequestered by the reticuloendothelial system, with the liver serving as a major clearance organ, while the spleen represents a distinct and immunologically relevant off-target destination. By leveraging an off-target repurposing strategy, the optimized fusion of tumor cell membrane and erythrocyte membrane (4T1m: RBCm = 10:1) selectively biased a portion of off-target nanoparticles toward the spleen—the largest peripheral immune organ—while maintaining tumor-associated accumulation. This redistribution enables nanoparticles that would otherwise accumulate in non-immune organs to instead engage spleen-associated immune compartments, thereby converting splenic sequestration into a functionally relevant immunomodulatory contribution (Fig. 5A). Within the spleen, the redirected TRM@ZIF-8 nanoparticles effectively promoted dendritic cell (DC) maturation and macrophage polarization, both key indicators of immune activation. Mature DCs, as the principal antigen-presenting cells, exhibited robust maturation upon TRM@ZIF-8 treatment (Fig. 5B), which was associated with enhanced splenic immune activation and strengthened cytotoxic T-cell responses. Simultaneously, macrophages polarised toward the M1 phenotype (Fig. 5D and E), reinforcing antitumor immunity. Flow cytometry analysis quantitatively confirmed these effects: expression of DC maturation markers CD80 and CD86 increased approximately three-fold relative to PBS controls (Fig. 5F), while the CD8⁺/CD4⁺ T cell ratio rose 1.7-fold (Fig. 5C and G), indicating enhanced engagement of cytotoxic T cell responses. Moreover, the M1/M2 macrophage ratio in the TRM@ZIF-8 group increased six-fold compared with PBS (Fig. 5H and I and S16), indicating a substantial shift toward a pro-inflammatory, antitumor immune environment.

These findings collectively suggest that TRM@ZIF-8, through hybrid-membrane–mediated off-target repurposing, promotes splenic immune activation, including enhanced DC maturation and M1 macrophage polarization, which is associated with strengthened systemic antitumor immune responses. The immunological significance of this spleen-biased redistribution is supported by the role of the spleen as an immune-rich organ involved in antigen processing. Following off-target redistribution, TRM@ZIF-8 may increase nanoparticle exposure to splenic antigen-presenting cells, including dendritic cells and macrophages, thereby potentially contributing to immune activation in a physiologically relevant priming site. This process is likely mediated by multiple contributing factors rather than a single defined pathway, including nanoparticle–immune cell interactions within the spleen, Zn²⁺-related innate immune stimulation, and tumor-associated inflammatory signals. In this context, splenic immune activation is considered a functional link connecting spleen-associated nanoparticle redistribution with systemic antitumor immune responses. However, the relative contributions of these factors and the specific immune cell subsets involved were not directly resolved in this study.

To further evaluate the antitumor immune responses associated with TRM@ZIF-8 treatment, tumor-infiltrating immune cell populations were analyzed by flow cytometry across six treatment groups (G1–G6). As shown in Figs. 6A–H, tumors from mice treated with TRM@ZIF-8 (G6) exhibited the most pronounced alterations in immune cell composition among all groups, consistent with a functional association between hybrid-membrane-mediated off-target redistribution and tumor immune remodeling.

Specifically, the proportion of intratumoral CD8⁺ T cells in the G6 group increased by approximately five-fold relative to PBS-treated controls (G1) (Fig. 6A, E and B, and 6F), indicating enhanced cytotoxic T-cell engagement. Notably, this increase was largely diminished in splenectomized mice receiving TRM@ZIF-8 (G3), supporting an important contribution of spleen-associated systemic immune processes to these immune alterations.

Concurrently, multiple immunosuppressive cell populations within the tumor microenvironment were reduced in the G6 group. The frequency of CD4⁺Foxp3⁺ regulatory T cells decreased by approximately 17-fold (Fig. 6C and G), while myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) were reduced by approximately 2.5-fold (Figures S17A and S17B). In addition, the proportion of M2-polarized macrophages, which are associated with tumor-promoting immunosuppression, declined by approximately 4.6-fold compared with PBS-treated tumors (Fig. 6D). Consequently, the intratumoral M1/M2 macrophage ratio was substantially increased in the TRM@ZIF-8 group, reflecting a shift toward a pro-inflammatory and immunostimulatory tumor microenvironment. To further substantiate the transcriptomic findings at the in vivo level, inflammasome-associated signaling was assessed in tumor tissues. Western blot analysis revealed that the expression of cleaved caspase-1, GSDMD, NLRP3, and IL-1β was moderately elevated in the TRM@ZIF-8-treated group compared with the control (Figure S18), suggesting activation of inflammasome-related pathways and inflammatory cell death–associated responses.

Collectively, these immune profiling results suggest a functional association between spleen-biased off-target redistribution and coordinated modulation of the tumor immune landscape including enhanced cytotoxic T-cell responses and reduced immunosuppressive cell populations. These observations should be interpreted as correlative evidence for a functional association between spleen immune activation and tumor immune remodeling.

Conclusion

This study proposes an off-target repurposing strategy that selectively redirects a fraction of off-target nanoparticles toward the spleen to elicit immune activation, without compromising intrinsic tumor-targeting capability. Rather than eliminating off-target biodistribution, this approach seeks to leverage the immunological relevance of the spleen as a major off-target accumulation site, while partially redistributing off-target accumulation away from other organs and potentially mitigating related side effects under the conditions examined in this study.

By systematically evaluating the splenic affinity and tumor-targeting performance of different cell membranes, we identified that erythrocyte membranes showed the strongest association with splenic sequestration. Further optimization of the fusion ratio between tumor cell membranes and erythrocyte membranes suggested that a 10:1 (4T1m: RBCm) configuration achieves a balanced contribution of tumor-associated and erythrocyte-derived functionalities, supporting effective tumor recognition while biasing off-target nanoparticle redistribution toward the spleen.

Importantly, differences in splenic accumulation and biodistribution among hybrid membrane formulations were associated with variations in membrane protein composition and abundance introduced by distinct fusion ratios. These protein profiles, inherited from their respective source membranes, collectively modulate nanoparticle interactions with splenic immune cells and the mononuclear phagocyte system. Rather than reflecting a single dominant mechanism, the splenic affinity of the hybrid membranes likely arises from the combined contribution of multiple membrane-associated proteins that together shape nanoparticle recognition, clearance, and retention in immune-related organs. Transcriptomic analysis revealed inflammation-associated transcriptional reprogramming in tumor cells treated with TRM@ZIF-8, which was subsequently validated through in vitro experiments. Functionally, this immune modulation was accompanied by effective inhibition of primary tumor growth and enhanced systemic antitumor immune activation.

The marked attenuation of therapeutic efficacy after splenectomy suggests a functional association between spleen involvement and the antitumor effects of TRM@ZIF-8. Nevertheless, because splenectomy represents an organ-level perturbation that may also influence systemic immune homeostasis, further studies using more selective approaches will be required to better delineate the specific splenic immune processes potentially associated with these effects.

From a translational perspective, the hybrid tumor–erythrocyte membrane system may face challenges in large-scale manufacturing, batch-to-batch consistency, and the potential immunogenicity of tumor-derived membranes. Therefore, this study represents a proof-of-concept demonstration, and further optimization of membrane sources and establishment of standardized production protocols will be important for future translational development.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be noted. The specific splenic immune cell subsets and downstream pathways responsible for the observed immune activation were not directly dissected, and the splenectomy experiment, while supporting spleen involvement, does not provide fully specific mechanistic proof. In addition, this study was performed in a single 4T1 tumor model. Moreover, the current data do not allow evaluation of the durability of the antitumor immune response or the potential establishment of long-term immunological memory. Lung metastasis, a characteristic feature of the 4T1 model, was not directly evaluated. Therefore, the potential impact of TRM@ZIF-8 on metastatic progression remains unclear and warrants further investigation. Future studies should focus on more selective mechanistic validation, broader testing in additional tumor models, assessment of metastatic burden and long-term survival, and optimization of membrane standardization and scalable manufacturing. In addition, the biodistribution analysis in this study has several limitations. Different experimental systems were used to progressively support the membrane-engineering design rationale, rather than to demonstrate fully equivalent pharmacokinetic behaviors across formulations. Systematic time-dependent biodistribution studies using the final therapeutic formulation are still required, and the lack of comprehensive pharmacokinetic validation represents a limitation of this study. Collectively, this work presents a spleen-biased off-target repurposing strategy and provides proof-of-concept evidence that unavoidable nanoparticle sequestration may be harnessed as a controllable immunomodulatory process. These findings suggest that, in certain contexts, off-target nanoparticle accumulation may be functionally leveraged as an immunologically relevant opportunity rather than viewed solely as a delivery limitation.

Materials and methods

Materials and cells

CCK-8 assay kit, BCA protein quantification kit, RIPA lysis buffer, and protease inhibitor were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). ELISA kits were purchased from Shanghai Jianglai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The LDH assay kit was purchased from Beyotime. TSQ probes were purchased from MCE. Primary antibodies included anti-CD80-FITC, anti-CD86-PE, anti-CD206-PE, anti-CD11c-APC, anti-CD3-APC, anti-CD4-FITC, anti-CD8-PE/Cy7, anti-Foxp3-PE, anti-IFN-γ-PE, and anti-Gr1-PE/Cy7 (BioLegend, USA). Collagenase A (Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai), collagenase IV, and hyaluronidase (YEASEN, Shanghai). DiR dye was purchased from Meilun Biotechnology (Dalian, China). Gold nanoparticles (CAS: XFJ60-1) were purchased from Jiangsu Xianfeng Nanomaterials Technology Co., Ltd. ZIF-8 nanoparticles (CAS:59061-53-9) were purchased from Guangdong Advanced Carbon Materials Co., Ltd.

Mouse mammary carcinoma cell line 4T1, dendritic cell line DC2.4, and macrophage cell line RAW264.7 were purchased from the Cell Resource Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 or DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Solarbio, Beijing) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (NCM Biotech, Suzhou). All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified environment saturated with 5% CO₂.

Female BALB/c mice aged 5–6 weeks were purchased from Shanghai SLAK Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. All animal experiments were approved by the Tongji University Animal Ethics Committee (Animal Ethics: TJAA07721106) and conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Management and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Isolation and fusion of cell membrane vesicles

Membranes from 4T1 cells, RBCs, NEs, and macrophages were extracted using a combination of hypotonic lysis and ultrasonic disruption. Following hypotonic treatment, cells were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 5 min to remove debris. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 60 min to collect the membrane fraction. The pellet was resuspended in aqueous buffer and repeatedly extruded through 400 nm and 200 nm polycarbonate membranes using an Avanti Mini Extruder (Avanti Polar Lipids, USA) to obtain uniform vesicles. For membrane fusion experiments, 4T1m and RBCm with different ratios (1:0, 20:1, 10:1, 1:1, 1:10, 1:20) were mixed and subjected to extrusion to obtain heteromeric membrane vesicles. SDS-PAGE analysed membrane protein composition.

Spleen targeting capability screening

DiR-labeled vesicles were injected into mice via the tail vein. Nanoparticles’ distribution was monitored using an in vivo imaging system (Caliper PerkinElmer, USA) over 1–36 h and performed ex vivo imaging of major organs, including the liver, spleen, and tumors. Hybrid membrane vesicles coated on gold nanoparticles (150 nm) were injected into mice via the tail vein. The tissue gold content was quantified by ICP-MS (Agilent 7800, USA). These experiments were performed as comparative biodistribution screening analyses across multiple membrane formulations.

Nanoparticle preparation and characterisation

ZIF-8 nanoparticles (150 nm) were obtained and co-extruded with 4T1/RBC fusion membranes at a ratio of 10:1 to yield TRM@ZIF-8. Characterization methods included: transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to observe the membrane-coating structure, elemental mapping to detect phosphorus distribution, dynamic light scattering (DLS, Malvern Panalytical, UK) for particle size and zeta potential measurement, SDS-PAGE to analyze membrane protein retention, and X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D2 Phaser, Germany) to confirm the stability of the ZIF-8 crystal structure.

Cytotoxicity assay

The CCK-8 assay was employed to evaluate the cytotoxicity of TRM@ZIF-8 against 4T1, DC2.4, and RAW264.7 cells. Briefly, 4T1, DC2.4, and RAW264.7 cells were seeded at 3000 cells per well in a 96-well plate. After 24 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with medium containing different concentrations of TRM@ZIF-8 (0–1000 µg/mL). After an additional 24 h of incubation, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. Optical density was measured after a further 3-hour incubation using a microplate reader (SpectraMax ABS Plus, USA).

DC and macrophage immunostimulation

DC2.4 (2 × 10⁵/well) and RAW264.7 (3 × 10⁵/well) cells were seeded into 12-well plates and co-incubated with TRM@ZIF-8 for 12–16 h. Cells were harvested, stained, and analysed by flow cytometry (BD FACSVerse, USA) for markers including CD80, CD86, and CD206.

Cell uptake assay

4T1 and DC2.4 cells were co-incubated with ZIF-8, TM@ZIF-8, or TRM@ZIF-8 for 1–3 h. TSQ probe (0.1 mM) was added, and samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After PBS washing, microscopic imaging was performed.

RNA sequencing

4T1 cells were treated with PBS or TRM@ZIF-8 for 24 h, and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Library construction and quality assessment were performed for each sample, followed by Illumina sequencing. RNA sequencing and subsequent data analysis were conducted by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the TRM@ZIF-8 and PBS groups were identified using the thresholds of p < 0.05 and |log₂Fold-change| ≥ 1. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were carried out using Fisher’s exact test to identify significantly enriched biological processes and pathways associated with the DEGs.

Verification of the molecular mechanism of toxicity

Western Blot Detection of NLRP3, GSDMD, and Other Proteins: After treating 4T1 cells with different materials, wash the cells with PBS. Add RIPA lysis buffer and 1% protease inhibitor on ice for 5 min, then collect the cell lysate. Obtain the supernatant by centrifuging the cell lysate at 12,000 g for 15 min. Protein concentration was measured using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Nanodrop 1000, Thermo). Equal volumes of protein were mixed with sample buffer and heated to 95 °C for 10 min to denature the proteins. Samples were then loaded onto a 4–20% bis-tris protein gel and electrophoresed at 100 V for 1.5 h. Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane using the tris transfer buffer at 100 mA for 100 min. The PVDF membrane was then blocked with blocking buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody. The PVDF membrane was washed with TBST for 20 min, then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for one hour. Finally, the blot was visualised using a Fully Automated Chemiluminescence Image Analysis System (Tanon 5200CE). LDH Release Assay: 4T1 cells (3 × 10³/well) were seeded in 96-well plates. Supernatants were collected after 24 h of treatment. The LDH assay kit was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Readings were taken at 490 nm using a microplate reader. ELISA kits were used to detect IL-1β and IL-18 levels.

Tumor model establishment

BALB/c mice received subcutaneous inoculation of 5 × 10⁵ 4T1 cells (100 µL PBS) in the right lower abdomen. When tumor volume reached 60–80 mm³, mice were randomly assigned to PBS, ZIF-8, TM@ZIF-8, RM@ZIF-8, TRM@ZIF-8 with splenectomy, and TRM@ZIF-8 groups (n = 6). Mice were randomized to treatment groups to ensure comparable initial tumor volumes among groups. Intravenous tail vein injection (200 µL/mouse, 2.5 mg/kg) was administered every 2 days for 3 weeks.

Tumor monitoring and safety assessment

To monitor treatment efficacy, tumor volume was assessed every other day. The measurements were based on the formula V = (L × W²) / 2, with L and W corresponding to the longest and shortest diameters (in millimeters), respectively. Tissue sectioning, staining, and blood biochemistry were performed by Wuhan Service Biotechnology Co., td. Tumors and organs were initially fixed in formaldehyde, then embedded in paraffin and sectioned. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and imaged using an optical microscope.

Immunological analysis

Spleen and tumor tissues were mechanically dissociated to prepare single-cell suspensions. Flow cytometry (FACS Calibur, Becton, Dickinson, USA) was performed using a sequential gating strategy. Cells were first gated based on forward scatter (FSC-A) and side scatter (SSC-A) to exclude debris, followed by singlet selection using FSC-A versus FSC-H. CD45⁺ immune cells were then identified for downstream analysis. For dendritic cell analysis in spleen tissue, CD11c⁺ cells were gated and further analyzed based on CD80 and CD86 expression to evaluate maturation. For T cell analysis in spleen and tumor tissues, CD3⁺ T cells were gated from the CD45⁺ population, followed by discrimination of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ subsets; IFN-γ expression within CD8⁺ T cells was further analyzed where indicated. For macrophage analysis in spleen and tumor tissues, F4/80⁺ cells were gated and further characterized based on CD80 and CD206 expression to evaluate M1/M2 polarization. Regulatory T cells were identified as CD4⁺Foxp3⁺ cells, and MDSCs were identified as CD11b⁺Gr1⁺ cells in tumor tissue.

DIDS treatment for in vivo biodistribution analysis

DiR-labeled TRM@ZIF-8 was pretreated with DIDS (30 µM) at room temperature for 30 min prior to subsequent experiments. After incubation, the nanoparticles were washed with PBS to remove unbound DIDS and resuspended in PBS for subsequent in vivo administration. Untreated and DIDS-treated TRM@ZIF-8 were intravenously injected into 4T1 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice. In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at 6 h post-injection using an in vivo imaging system. Fluorescence intensities in the spleen and liver were quantified using Living Image software, and the spleen-to-liver ratio was calculated for biodistribution analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation; statistical analysis was performed using t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001; ns indicates no significant difference. All statistical analyses were conducted using the Prism software package (GraphPad Prism 9.1). The sequencing data were provided by the Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. Platform.
