1.
Introduction

In this paper we will focus on a class of coupled elliptic systems with nonlocal nonlinearities of Choquard type. Specifically, we will study the following system in
{\mathbb{R}^{4}}

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta u=(|x|^{-\mu}*|u|^{4-\frac{\mu}{2}})|u|^{2-\frac{\mu}{2}}u+\beta uv^{2}\quad&\hbox{in}\,\,{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\\
&-\Delta v=(|x|^{-\mu}*|v|^{4-\frac{\mu}{2}})|v|^{2-\frac{\mu}{2}}v+\beta u^{2}v\quad&\hbox{in}\,\,{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\end{aligned}\right.

where
*
stands for the standard convolution,
\beta<0,
and
0<\mu\leq 4.

A more general version of (
1.1
) is the following critical system in
{\mathbb{R}^{N}}

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta u=(|x|^{-\mu}*|u|^{2^{*}_{\mu}})|u|^{2^{*}_{\mu}-2}u+\beta u^{\frac{2^{*}}{2}-1}v^{\frac{2^{*}}{2}}\quad&\hbox{in}\,\,{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\\
&-\Delta v=(|x|^{-\mu}*|v|^{2^{*}_{\mu}})|v|^{2^{*}_{\mu}-2}v+\beta u^{\frac{2^{*}}{2}}v^{\frac{2^{*}}{2}-1}\quad&\hbox{in}\,\,{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\end{aligned}\right.

where
2^{*}:=\frac{2N}{N-2}
is the critical Sobolev exponent and
2^{*}_{\mu}:=\frac{2N-\mu}{N-2}
is the upper critical exponent in the sense of the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality
[
15
]
.

The single equation of (
1.2
), often referred to as the
nonlinear Choquard
or
Schrödinger-Newton equation
, has deep roots in mathematical physics. Indeed, it seems to originate from the model of the polaron of H. Fröhlich and S. Pekar, where free electrons in an ionic lattice interact with phonons associated with the deformation of the lattice or with the polarization that it creates in the medium (interaction of an electron with its own hole)
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
17
]
. It was later utilized by Choquard in 1976 in the modeling of a one-component plasma. More recently, this kind of equation has gained renewed interest as the non-relativistic limit of the Einstein-Klein-Gordon system, often appearing in the study of boson stars and scalar field dark matter
[
18
]
.

The mathematical framework for such nonlocal problems has been extensively developed in
[
16
]
, who established the existence results and qualitative properties for ground state solutions. In particular, the critical regime presents formidable mathematical challenges. It sits at the threshold of the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality (see
[
15
]
).

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
t,r>1
and
0<\mu<N
with
\frac{1}{t}+\frac{\mu}{N}+\frac{1}{r}=2.
There exists a sharp constant
C=C(t,N,\mu,r)
such that for any
f\in L^{t}({\mathbb{R}^{N}})
and
h\in L^{r}({\mathbb{R}^{N}})

\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{N}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{N}}}\frac{|f(x)||h(y)|}{|x-y|^{\mu}}\,dx\,dy\leq C\|f\|_{t}\|h\|_{r}.

Notice that, by Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality, the integral

\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{N}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{N}}}\frac{|u(x)|^{q}|u(y)|^{q}}{|x-y|^{\mu}}\,dx\,dy

is well-defined if
|u|^{q}\in L^{t}({\mathbb{R}^{N}})
for some
t>1
satisfying
\frac{2}{t}+\frac{\mu}{N}=2.
Thus, for
u\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{N}}),
by Sobolev embedding theorems, we know that

2\leq tq\leq 2^{*}=\frac{2N}{N-2}

namely

\frac{2N-\mu}{N}\leq q\leq\frac{2N-\mu}{N-2}.

Thus,
\frac{2N-\mu}{N}
is called the lower critical exponent, while
2^{*}_{\mu}:=\frac{2N-\mu}{N-2}
is the upper critical exponent in the sense of the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality.

For the upper critical case, by using the moving plane methods in integral developed in
[
2
,
3
]
, Lei
[
13
]
, Du and Yang
[
8
]
, Guo et al.
[
12
]
classified independently the positive solutions of the critical Hartree equation

(1.3)

-\Delta u=(|x|^{-\mu}*|u|^{2^{*}_{\mu}})|u|^{2^{*}_{\mu}-2}u,\quad\hbox{in}\,\,{\mathbb{R}^{N}}.

and proved that every positive solution of (
1.3
) must assume the form

(1.4)

{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda,\xi}}(x)=\lambda^{\frac{N-2}{2}}\mathcal{U}(\lambda(x-\xi))=\alpha_{N,\mu}\frac{\lambda^{\frac{N-2}{2}}}{\left(1+\lambda^{2}|x-\xi|^{2}\right)^{\frac{N-2}{2}}},\quad\lambda>0,\,\,x,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

where

(1.5)

\mathcal{U}(x):=c_{N}\frac{1}{(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{N-2}{2}}},\quad c_{N}>0

and
\alpha_{N,\mu}
is a constant explicitly given and depends only on
N
and
\mu
.

A central theme of this work is the role of the coupling parameter
\beta.
When
\beta<0,
the system (
1.1
) is in a competitive regime. In such cases, the interaction term acts as a repulsive force between the two components
u,v.

As suggested by the preliminary results (see
[
4
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
) for
\beta<0
and
|\beta|
small, we will use the function
\mathcal{U}
as approximation for the first component and for the second we will choose

V:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda,\xi_{j}}}

where
k\geq 2
is fixed,
\lambda>0
and the points
\xi_{j}
are arranged at the vertices of a regular polygon.

More precisely,

(1.6)

\xi_{j}:=\rho\bigg(\cos\frac{2\pi(j-1)}{k},\sin\frac{2\pi(j-1)}{k},0,0\bigg),\quad\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}+\rho^{2}=1,\quad j=1,...,k.

The transition from the Choquard equation (
1.3
) to the system (
1.2
) is not trivial. While the Choquard equation and coupled Schrödinger systems with competitive interactions have been extensively investigated in isolation, their interaction remains an untouched frontier in the current literature. Our work is, to our knowledge, the first to bridge the gap between the theory of competitive systems and the critical Choquard equation.

We prove that even under the influence of nonlocal term, the system presents the same solution seen in
[
4
]
.

Indeed, we have the following main theorem

Theorem 1.2
.

For every fixed
k\geq 2,
there exists
\beta_{0}<0
such that, for each
\beta\in[\beta_{0},0),
the system (
1.1
) has a solution
u=(u_{1},u_{2})
with the form

u_{1}:=\mathcal{U}+\phi_{1},\quad u_{2}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda,\xi_{j}}}+\phi_{2}\quad\hbox{in}\,\,{\mathbb{R}^{4}},

where
\mathcal{U},{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda,\xi_{j}}}
defined in (
1.5
) and (
1.4
),
\xi_{j}
in (
1.6
) and
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in D^{1,2}({\mathbb{R}^{4}}).

Furthermore,

\|\phi_{1}\|_{*},\|\phi_{2}\|_{*}\rightarrow 0,\quad\frac{1}{\lambda}=O\bigg(e^{-c\frac{1}{|\beta|}}\bigg)

for some constant
c>0,
as
|\beta|\rightarrow 0.

In this article, we employ a Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction method to prove the main result. For this reason, we only consider the case
N=4
, since, if
N\geq 5
the coupling term
u_{i}^{\frac{2^{*}}{2}-1}u_{j}^{\frac{2^{*}}{2}}
has sub-linear growth and the reduction process does not work.

2.
The finite dimensional reduction

We carry out the finite-dimensional reduction argument in a weighted space in order to obtain a good estimate for the error term. Let

(2.7)

\|u\|_{*}=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{4}}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{(1+\lambda|x-\xi_{j}|)^{1+\tau}}\right)^{-1}\lambda^{-1}|u(x)|

and

(2.8)

\|h\|_{**}=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{4}}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{(1+\lambda|x-\xi_{j}|)^{3+\tau}}\right)^{-1}\lambda^{-3}|h(x)|

where
\tau<1
small enough.

We set

g(u):=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\frac{|u(y)|^{4-\frac{\mu}{2}}}{|x-y|^{\mu}}\,dy\right)|u|^{2-\frac{\mu}{2}}u.

Moreover, we also set

g^{\prime}(u)v=\bigg(4-\frac{\mu}{2}\bigg)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(y)|^{2-\frac{\mu}{2}}u(y)v(y)}{|x-y|^{\mu}}\,dy\right)|u|^{2-\frac{\mu}{2}}u+\bigg(3-\frac{\mu}{2}\bigg)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{|u(y)|^{4-\frac{\mu}{2}}}{|x-y|^{\mu}}\,dy\right)|u|^{2-\frac{\mu}{2}}v.

Then we can rewrite the problem (
1.1
) in the following way

(2.9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta u=g(u)+\beta uv^{2}\quad&\hbox{in}\,\,{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\\
&-\Delta v=g(v)+\beta u^{2}v\quad&\hbox{in}\,\,{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\end{aligned}\right.

We look for a solution of the problem (
2.9
) of the form

(2.10)

u=\mathcal{U}+\phi_{1},\quad v=\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda,\xi_{j}}}+\phi_{2}=:V+\phi_{2}

where
k\geq 2
is fixed,
\beta<0
,
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\mathcal{D}^{1,2}({\mathbb{R}^{4}})
are small functions to be found, and

\mathcal{U}(x):=c_{4}\frac{1}{(1+|x|^{2})},\quad{\mathcal{U}_{\lambda,\xi_{j}}}(x):=\alpha_{4,\mu}\frac{\lambda}{(1+\lambda^{2}|x-\xi_{j}|^{2})}\quad j=1,...,k

with

\xi_{j}:=\rho\bigg(\cos\frac{2\pi(j-1)}{k},\sin\frac{2\pi(j-1)}{k},0,0\bigg),\quad\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}+\rho^{2}=1,\quad j=1,...,k.

2.1.
Setting of the problem

Remark 2.1
.

We can observe that our problem (
1.1
) is invariant under Kelvin transform. Indeed, denoted by

u_{k}(x):=\frac{1}{|x|^{2}}u(y)

and

v_{k}(x):=\frac{1}{|x|^{2}}v(y)

the Kelvin transform of
u
and
v
respectively with
y:=\frac{x}{|x|^{2}},
it results that

-\Delta u_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{|x|^{6}}(-\Delta u)(y)=\frac{1}{|x|^{6}}\bigg[(|y|^{-\mu}*u^{4-\frac{\mu}{2}})u^{3-\frac{\mu}{2}}(y)+\beta u(y)v^{2}(y)\bigg].

Since,

u(y)=|x|^{2}u_{k}(x),\quad v^{2}(y)=|x|^{4}v_{k}^{2}(x),\quad u(y)^{3-\frac{\mu}{2}}=|x|^{6-\mu}u_{k}(x)^{3-\frac{\mu}{2}}

and

\displaystyle(|y|^{\mu}*u^{4-\frac{\mu}{2}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\frac{1}{|y-z|^{\mu}}u^{4-\frac{\mu}{2}}(z)\,dz=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\frac{|x|^{\mu}|w|^{\mu}}{|x-w|^{\mu}}|w|^{8-\mu}u_{k}^{4-\frac{\mu}{2}}(w)\frac{1}{|w|^{8}}\,dw

\displaystyle=|x|^{\mu}(|x|^{-\mu}*u_{k}^{4-\frac{\mu}{2}})

using the following geometric identity

\bigg|\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}-\frac{w}{|w|^{2}}\bigg|=\frac{|x-w|}{|x||w|}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|x|^{6}}\bigg[|x|^{\mu}(|x|^{-\mu}*u_{k}^{4-\frac{\mu}{2}})|x|^{6-\mu}u_{k}^{3-\frac{\mu}{2}}(x)+\beta|x|^{2}u_{k}(x)|x|^{4}v_{k}^{2}(x)\bigg]

\displaystyle=(|x|^{-\mu}*u_{k}^{4-\frac{\mu}{2}})u_{k}^{3-\frac{\mu}{2}}(x)+\beta u_{k}(x)v_{k}^{2}(x).

Similarly,

-\Delta v_{k}(x)=(|x|^{-\mu}*v_{k}^{4-\frac{\mu}{2}})v_{k}^{3-\frac{\mu}{2}}(x)+\beta u_{k}^{2}(x)v_{k}(x).

Given a function
\phi\in\mathcal{D}^{1,2}({\mathbb{R}^{4}}),
let us consider the following invariances:

(
A
)

Evenness with respect to the
x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}
variables, i.e.,

\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=\phi(x_{1},-x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=\phi(x_{1},x_{2},-x_{3},x_{4})=\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},-x_{4});

(
B
)

Invariance under rotation of
\frac{2\pi}{k}
in the
x_{1},x_{2}
- variables, i.e.,

\phi(\Theta_{k}(x_{1},x_{2}),x_{3},x_{4})=\phi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}),

where
\Theta_{k}
is the rotation matrix;

(
C
)

Invariance under Kelvin transform, i.e.,

\phi(x)=\frac{1}{|x|^{2}}\phi\bigg(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\bigg).

We define the associated space

X:=\{\phi\in\mathcal{D}^{1,2}({\mathbb{R}^{4}}):\phi\,\hbox{satisfies}\,(\textbf{A}),(\textbf{B}),(\textbf{C})\}

and we look for solutions
(u,v)
in the space
X\times X.

In order to perform the Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction it is very important to understand the kernel of the linearized operator associated to the limit problem (
1.3
). Very recently, in
[
14
]
, it has been shown that the solutions of (
1.3
) are non-degenerate provide
0<\mu\leq 4,
namely if we look for bounded solution of the linearized equation

(2.11)

-\Delta\mathcal{Z}=g^{\prime}(\mathcal{U})\mathcal{Z}

then
\mathcal{Z}\in\hbox{span}<Z^{0},Z^{1},...,Z^{4}>
where

Z^{0}(x):=\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{2}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad Z^{i}(x):=\frac{x_{i}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{2}},\,i=1,...,4.

Given
\lambda>0
and
\xi_{j}
given in (
1.6
), we can define

Z^{i}_{\lambda,\xi_{j}}(x):=\lambda Z^{i}(\lambda(x-\xi_{j})),\quad i=0,1,...,4,\quad j=1,...,k

and

(2.12)

Z(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}Z^{0}_{\lambda,\xi_{j}}(x).

We introduce the spaces

K:=X\cap\hbox{span}\{Z\},\quad K^{\perp}:=\{\phi\in X:<\phi,Z>=0\}

and the orthogonal projections

\Pi:X\times X\rightarrow X\times K\quad\hbox{and}\quad\Pi^{\perp}:X\times X\rightarrow X\times K^{\perp}.

If
(u,v)
are given by (
2.10

2.9
) in terms of

as

(2.13)

\Pi\{\mathcal{L}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})-\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{N}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\}=0

(2.14)

\Pi^{\perp}\{\mathcal{L}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})-\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{N}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\}=0

where the linear operator

is defined by

(2.15)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{L}_{1}(\phi_{1}):=-\Delta\phi_{1}-g^{\prime}(\mathcal{U})\phi_{1}\\
&\mathcal{L}_{2}(\phi_{2}):=-\Delta\phi_{2}-g^{\prime}(V)\phi_{2}\end{aligned}\right.

the error term
\mathcal{E}=(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})
by

(2.16)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{E}_{1}:=\beta\mathcal{U}V^{2}\\
&\mathcal{E}_{2}:=g(V)-\sum_{j=1}^{k}g({\mathcal{U}_{\lambda,\xi_{j}}})+\beta\mathcal{U}^{2}V\end{aligned}\right.

and the quadratic term
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{N}_{1},\mathcal{N}_{2})

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{N}_{1}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2}):=g(\mathcal{U}+\phi_{1})-g(\mathcal{U})-g^{\prime}(\mathcal{U})\phi_{1}+2\beta\mathcal{U}V\phi_{2}+\beta\mathcal{U}\phi_{2}^{2}+\beta V^{2}\phi_{1}+2\beta V\phi_{1}\phi_{2}+\beta\phi_{1}\phi_{2}^{2}\\

2.2.
The invertibility of linear operator
\mathcal{L}

We consider the following linear problem

(2.18)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{L}_{1}(\phi_{1})=h_{1}\\
&\mathcal{L}_{2}(\phi_{2})=h_{2}+\mathfrak{c}\sum_{j=1}^{k}g^{\prime}({\mathcal{U}_{\lambda,\xi_{j}}}){Z^{0}_{\lambda,\xi_{j}}}\\
&\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in X\\
