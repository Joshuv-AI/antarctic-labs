The relationship between psychological well-being, life project, psychological resilience, emotion regulation, and perceived stress in Syrian migrants in the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Mert Aytaç, Elif Erol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe purpose of this study is to investigate the association between psychological well-being and perceived stress, resilience, life project, and emotion regulation of Syrian refugees affected by the COVID-19 outbreak.MethodsIn the study, 520 Syrian refugee adults aged between 25–50 were included in the sample. The participants in the research sample are predominantly male (62.5%). Demographic Information Form, Psychological Well-Being Scale, Psychological Resilience Scale, Emotion Regulation Difficulty Scale, and Perceived Stress Factor scale were applied to participants as data collection tools.ResultsIn this study, correlation and hierarchical regression analyses were conducted. The correlation analysis revealed moderately significant relations between the variables. Psychological well-being was positively predicted by psychological resilience, Emotion Regulatory Aims, and Hope in a four-stage hierarchical regression analysis aimed at identifying the variables associated with psychological well-being. Conversely, Regret and Emotion Regulatory Difficulty, determined by Openness, Rejection, and Perceived Insufficient Self-efficacy, were predicted in a negative manner.Conclusion“The findings suggest that resilience-building strategies and support for ontological well-being may be associated with higher levels of psychological well-being among refugees. Additionally, perceived stress was negatively with psychological well-being. These cross-sectional findings highlight the potential relevance of psychological resilience, ontological well-being, emotion regulation, and perceived stress for future intervention research, though causal inferences cannot be drawn from the present design.Keywords: Psychological well-being, Covid-19, Refugees, Resilience, Life project, Difficulty in emotion regulation, Stress

Introduction

In recent years, two issues have occupied the world agenda. In addition to the long-standing refugee problem, COVID-19 has been on the world agenda as an increasing problem. Due to the effects of both situations, it is seen that there is a serious increase in research on refugees and COVID-19 in Turkey as in the world. According to the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), the number of people who have been forced to leave their homeland has surpassed 110 million by the end of 2022. People have been forced to leave their homeland because of persecution, conflict, violence, violation of human rights, and events that seriously disrupt public order [1]. Due to the ongoing civil war in Syria, many people have had to leave their country to find a safer one. According to UNHCR, by the end of 2024, the number of Syrian refugees and asylum-seekers stood at 6.1 million, ranking first among the countries of origin of refugees worldwide [2].

Turkey hosts the highest number of Syrian refugees worldwide. According to the 2026 reports, Turkey accommodates approximately 2.3 million Syrian refugees, with nearly half of this population (40%) consisting of children aged 18 and under. Primarly, refugees reside in cities such as, Gaziantep, Şanlıurfa and Adana, Mersin and Hatay situated close to the Syrian border, with only 8% living in refugee camps. Istanbul, hosting 401,555 people, stands out as the Turkish city with the highest number of Syrian refuges Republic of Türkiye, Presidency of Migration Management [3].

In addition to the refugee problem, as is the case all over the world, the COVID-19 epidemic significantly impacted Turkey, resulting in the loss of more than 102,000 lives according to official statistics of the Republic of Türkiye, Ministry of Health [4]. It is important to note that the data for the present study were collected during April–May 2021, when the pandemic was in an active phase; the cumulative mortality figures cited reflect updated official records. The COVID-19 epidemic has emerged as one of the most devastating global health crises, severely affecting daily life [5]. Since the onset of the pandemic, over 7.1 million deaths have been confirmed worldwide [6]. Data collection for the present study took place during the active phase of the pandemic, when COVID-19-related restrictions, economic disruptions, and psychosocial stressors were prominent features of daily life in Türkiye. COVID-19 primarily spreads through droplets, transmitted by air and physical contact. Considering asymptomatic carriers, who play a crucial role in virus transmission, is also essential [7, 8].

Migrants generally live together in crowded environments. This situation increases the risk of COVID-19 transmission and influencing the course of the epidemic. Factors such as crowded living conditions, insufficient ventilation, restricted access to clean water, difficulties in accessing health facilities, and limited job opportunities exacerbate the impact of the disease. Migrants are exposed to a significantly higher risk of infection, making them a vulnerable population [9]. Due to reasons such as unhealthy living conditions, psychosocial support needs, difficulties in accessing health services, and economic challenges, refugees are considered a sensitive group requiring priority attention in the fight against COVID-19. However, the impact of the pandemic on this population extends beyond immediate environmental risks to include deeper psychological, social-normative, and biopolitical dimensions that intersect with their unique lived experiences. The COVID-19 pandemic did not merely act as a general stressor but intersected with the pre-existing “biopolitical precarity” and trauma inherent in the refugee experience. Taylor [10] emphasizes that global health crises can reactivate and exacerbate pre-existing traumatic memories among displaced populations. Published scholarship on refugee adaptation increasingly warns against reducing refugee well-being exclusively to individual-level coping capacities, emphasizing that displacement histories interact dynamically with broader social-normative and macro-systemic processes. Public health crises and structural displacement do not operate merely as isolated individual stressors; instead, they fundamentally reshape intergroup emotions, trust, collective solidarity, and reconciliation processes within host societies. For instance, recent scholarship underscores that institutional or humanitarian support in post-conflict or crisis settings can paradoxically exacerbate negative outgroup emotions and perceptions of dominance depending on pre-existing societal prejudices [11]. During macro-crises like a pandemic, compliance with structural demands and subsequent psychological relief are heavily mediated by social norms, trust in science, and perceived ingroup expectations rather than purely individual coping mechanisms [12]. For Syrian refugees, whose psychological landscapes are profoundly marked by traumatic war-related memories, these structural variables are central; severe trauma exposure and meta-dehumanization can undermine openness to social contact and hinder psychological integration within the host community [13]. Consequently, refugee psychological well-being must be theorized as a multi-layered outcome shaped by the intersection of individual psychological resilience, pandemic-driven uncertainty, and the macro-social quality of intergroup trust and structural relations within the host context.

These difficulties underscore the importance of organizing psychosocial intervention services for refugees. As the psychological health of people is at stake, such interventions should be informed by both empirical studies and theoretical insights. Current literature highlights the importance of numerous critical variables, both in planning and provision, as well as in determining the quality of psychosocial services extended to this group of people. While some of these variables, such as emotion regulation and psychological resilience, relate to the capacity of individuals to deal with negative situations, others, such as hope and subjective well-being, focus on the positive dimensions of psychological health. In this context, one of the key variables that should be examined is emotion regulation. Emotion regulation is defined as observing, evaluating and making changes in emotional reactions after an event [14]. It encompasses all internal and external processes involved in achieving the result [15]. According to this definition, emotion regulation involves several processes: being able to regulate one’s own emotions, regulating the emotions felt by others, recognizing the emotion and regulating it, and regulating the characteristics of secondary emotion [16]. It is known that emotion regulation is an important raw material for mental health [17].

In recent years, more research has increasingly revealed the devastating effects of difficulties in emotion regulation on mental health [18]. For instance, Ehring and Quack’s study [19] demonstrated a significant association between the severity of post-traumatic stress symptoms and difficulties in emotion regulation. Similarly, a study involving university students found a significant relationship between post-traumatic stress symptoms and emotional regulation difficulties [20]. In addition, some other studies have shown that there is a relationship between stressful life events and emotion regulation. For example, Bilim [21] observed a significant relationship between childhood traumatic experiences and emotion regulation. Dereboy and her colleagues [22] asserted that difficulties in emotion regulation act as a partial mediator variable in the relationship between childhood traumas and especially emotional abuse and identity confusion within its subscales.

Another variable that play an important role in an individual’s coping with negative experiences is psychological resilience. In the literature, the concept of resilience is defined as a healing factor in the face of negative life events, such as natural disasters, divorce, irregular family life, and migration to another country [23]. It includes the adaptation of the individual to the difficulties and changes in one’s life in the active process that occurs as a result of the interaction of these risk factors [24]. Arslan [25] defined resilience as an important coping mechanism in the face of negative experiences and emphasized the mediating role of this variable. Studies conducted during the coronavirus process have shown consistency with this view and revealed that psychological resilience is an important protective factor against the negative effects of COVID-19 [26–28].

Similarly, the study of resilience in refugees has become one of the key research areas. In one such study, 13 Sudanese immigrants were interviewed to understand their experiences both before and after migration. These interviews uncovered that they focused on three themes related to psychological resilience: religious beliefs, social support, and personal characteristics [29]. In a study conducted with Syrian refugee children in Turkey, data were collected from 147 people aged 11–18. The results of the research revealed a significant relationship between the psychological resilience of refugee children and their social support mechanisms. In particular, the perceived support from teachers has been observed to play an important role in fostering resilience. Similarly, family support is highlighted as an important factor [30]. One of the most comprehensive studies on resilience in Turkey was conducted by Bozdağ [31] with 840 Syrian refugee children, revealing that the frequency of social contact, perceived social support from friends and support from a special person significantly and positively predicted resilience.

The COVID-19 epidemic and the problems associated with being a refugee can cause some negative feelings in people. Studies have focused on the anxiety, depression, and hopelessness brought about by these experiences [32–35]. However, with the adoption of positive psychology in recent years, positive emotions have gained importance and studies on the individual’s psychological well-being have gained even more importance [36, 37]. Instead of focusing on the weak and fragile aspects of the individual, positive psychology focuses on the strengths and positive aspects of the individual and tries to develop them [38, 39].

One of the important subjects that positive psychology is interested in, and which is also one of the variables this study focuses on, is the concept of hope. Hope is a variable related to the goals that the individual desires and the perceived capacity of what he can do while reaching these goals, and the probability of reaching these goals [40, 41]. Among other topics frequently examined by researchers in the field of positive psychology are subjective well-being, mental resilience, life satisfaction, and happiness [40–42]. Subjective well-being consists of two basic components: cognitive and affective [43]. The cognitive dimension of the subjective well-being variable creates life satisfaction, while the affective dimension generates negative and positive emotions [44]. In addition to the concept of well-being, Şimşek [45] brought the ontological concept of well-being into the literature, since the relationship between cognitive and emotional dimensions is not clear in the subjective well-being theory defined by Diener et al. [43], and the effect of the emotional dimension is neglected in the present time-oriented approach in terms of whole the time, and also the meaning of life in life satisfaction is not clear. Ontological well-being differs from subjective well-being in that it is central to the evaluation of one’s own life as a project. In this respect, it is the evaluation of his life as a whole, together with the past, the future, and the present. In addition, there are some differences between the concepts of psychological well- being and ontological well being. Building upon this resource base, ontological well-being — operationalized here as the life project — represents a higher-order cognitive and affective orientation toward one’s own existence across time [45]. Whereas psychological resilience reflects the capacity to cope, ontological well-being reflects the meaning structure within which coping occurs: whether individuals perceive their past as meaningful, their present as dynamic, and their future as hopeful. Although both resilience and ontological well-being are positive psychological constructs, they are conceptually distinct: resilience is a dynamic coping capacity, while ontological well-being is an evaluative orientation toward life as a whole.

Examining the factors linked to psychological well-being in refugee individuals will have an important place in supporting positive mental health. This study aims to investigate the relationship between psychological well-being and perceived stress, resilience, life projects, and emotion regulation among Syrian refugees during the COVID-19 epidemic. The research endeavors to fill a significant gap in the existing literature.

Theoretical framework of the study

To provide a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying these variables, the current study adopts an integrated theoretical model: The theoretical framework of this study posits that refugee well-being is an outcome of the interaction between foundational personal resources, cognitive meaning structures, and situational stressors. Following Masten [46] and Connor and Davidson [47], psychological resilience is treated as a relatively stable, personality-based resource and is thus entered in the first block of the hierarchical model as the foundation for adaptation. Building upon this resource, ontological well-being represents a cognitive-existential layer where individuals evaluate their lives as an integrated “life project” encompassing the past, present, and future. Emotion regulation is subsequently positioned as a functional process-oriented capacity that allows individuals to manage the affective consequences of these existential evaluations. Finally, perceived stress is added in the last block as the most fluctuating, situation-dependent variable, capturing the incremental impact of the external environment on psychological health after internal resources have been controlled for.

Methods

Sample

In the study, 520 Syrian refugees under temporary protection adults aged between 25 and 50 were included in the sample. Distributions of demographic characteristics of the sample are shown in Table 1. Men constitute 62.5% of the participants in the study. All of the participants in the study are married and 37% have at least one child. While 90% of them are high school and primary school graduates, the educational status of their spouses shows a similar distribution. Almost half of the sample (43.9%) states that they do not work in any job. 72% of the spouses are not working. 92.5% of them describe their economic situation as bad or moderate.

Demographic characteristics of the study group of the research

Eighty percent of the Syrian refugees participating in the study have been living in Turkey for more than 5 years. Participants, who stated that they did not receive any psychological diagnosis, were asked to evaluate the process they spent in Syria, the process of their arrival in Turkey, the Covid process and the discrimination they experienced in Turkey on a scale of 1 to 10 (1 lowest, 10 highest). 52.5% of the participants gave 5 points or more to the period they lived in Syria, 56.2% to the process of their arrival in Turkey, 61.9% to the Covid process and 35% to the discrimination they experienced in Turkey.

Process

Data were collected between April and May 2021 using a convenience sampling method at the Kumkapı European Side Coordination Center affiliated with the Istanbul Provincial Directorate of Migration Management. The center is frequently visited by refugees for various administrative procedures, including obtaining temporary protection documents, travel permits, and updating personal records. All participants held Temporary Protection status under Turkish law (Law No. 6458), which is the legal framework governing Syrian nationals in Turkey. The terms “Syrian refugees” and “Syrian migrants” are used interchangeably in this manuscript to refer to this population, although it is acknowledged that their formal legal designation in Turkey is “persons under temporary protection.”

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Istanbul Rumeli University Social and Human Sciences (Approval No. E-53938333-050.06-2223; Date: 01/04/2021). Prior to data collection, all participants were informed about the purpose and voluntary nature of the study, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

As no validated Arabic adaptations of scales were translated into Arabic for the purposes of the present study by three independent Arabic language experts, and their Arabic adaptations were therefore established within the scope of this study. For the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) and the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), previously validated Arabic versions available in the literature were used. In addition, the Arabic wording of all scales was reviewed by three Arabic language experts to ensure linguistic clarity and cultural appropriateness for the target population. Considering that some participants had limited educational backgrounds, an Arabic interpreter was present during face-to-face administration in order to ensure that all questionnaire items were clearly understood whenever additional clarification was needed.

Statistical processes in the study were carried out with the SPSS 23.0 program. Relationships between variables were made with Pearson Correlation Analysis. Four-stage hierarchical regression analysis was used to determine the variables predicting psychological well-being in immigrants. In the first block, psychological resilience and demographic (gender, work, economic situation) variables, in the second block, the life project factors such as regret, hope, activation variables, in the third block, the sub-factors of emotional dysregulation: openness, goals, impulses, strategies and refusal variables. In the last block, the sub-factors of perceived stress, insufficient self-efficacy and perceived stress, were included in the analysis.

Measuring tools

Ontological well-being scale (Life Project Scale)

It was developed by Şimşek and Kocayörük [48] and aims to evaluate one’s life project (ontological well-being) by assessing the past, present, and future. At the same time, it consists of emotional judgments about one’s own projects in life (incomplete i.e. “past”; ongoing “present” and possible “future” feelings). The scale consists of 24 items in four factors as nothingness/meaninglessness, hope, regret and activation, and items of regret, guilt and sadness are scored inversely. Evaluation is made in the form of a five-point scale. Participants are expected to answer between “I do not feel at all” (1 point) and “I feel very intensely” (5 points). Internal consistency coefficients of the factors were ranging from 0.82 to 0.87 where the internal consistency for the total scale was found 0.74 in the original study. Four factors was explaining 62% of the total variance. As no validated Arabic adaptation of the scale was available prior to April 2021, the scale was translated into Arabic for the present study by three independent Arabic language experts, and its Arabic adaptation was therefore established within the scope of this study. During face-to-face administration, an Arabic interpreter was present when necessary to ensure that all items were clearly understood by the participants. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha reliability coeffient was found to be 0.79.

Difficulty in emotion regulation scale – short form (DERS-16)

The original scale was developed by Bjureberg et al. [49] as 36 items. The short form was developed by Gratz and Roemer [50] as 16 items. The Turkish adaptation of the scale was made by Yiğit and Guzey Yiğit [51]. The 16-item short form, measured with a 5-point Likert scale, consists of 5 sub-factors. The higher the score, the harder the emotion regulation. The internal consistency coefficient of the scale was calculated as 0.92 for the overall scale, 0.84 for the Clarity dimension, 0.84 for the Goals dimension, 0.87 for the Impulse dimension, 0.87 for the Strategies dimension and 0.78 for the non-acceptance dimension. Although Arabic versions of the original DERS had previously been reported, no validated Arabic adaptation of the DERS-16 short form was identified prior to April 2021. Therefore, the scale was translated into Arabic for the present study by three independent Arabic language experts, and the Arabic adaptation of the DERS-16 was carried out within the scope of this study. During face-to-face administration, an Arabic interpreter was available whenever clarification was required. For the current sample of Syrian refugees, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the total DERS-16 was calculated as 0.95.

Perceived stress scale

The first version of the scale developed by Cohen et al. [52] consists of 14 items. It was later reduced to two short versions of 10 and 4 items. For the 10-item version of the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), the internal consistency coefficient ranges from 0.74 to 0.88 in studies conducted with populations from different socioeconomic structures. Items in 5-point Likert type are scored between “0 (never)” and “4 (very often)”. Item numbers 4,5,7, and 8 are scored reversely in order to calculate the total score. A validated Arabic version of the PSS-10 developed by Chaaya et al. [53] was used in the present study. In addition, the wording of the Arabic items was reviewed by three Arabic language experts to ensure linguistic clarity and cultural appropriateness for the target sample. During face-to-face administration, an Arabic interpreter was available whenever needed. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the PSS-10 was found to be 0.79.

Psychological well-being scale

The original scale was developed by Diener et al. [54]. The Turkish adaptation study was done by Telef [55]. A 7-point Likert-type single factor consists of 8 items. A high score indicates that the person has many psychological resources and strengths. The correlation between Psychological Well-Being and Psychological Well-Being Scales and Needs Satisfaction Scale was found to be 0.56 and 0.73 (p<.01). As a result of the reliability study, the Cronbach alpha coefficient was calculated as 0.80. According to the test-retest result, there was a high, positive and significant relationship between the first and second application of the scale (r= .86, p<.01). An Arabic version of the scale (as used in Arabic speaking populations) was utilized in the present study [54]. The Arabic wording was additionally reviewed by three Arabic language experts to ensure linguistic comprehensibility and cultural suitability for the study population. An Arabic interpreter was present during face-to-face administration whenever additional clarification was needed. In the present study, the internal consistency coefficient for this scale was 0.96.

Connor- Davidson resilience scale

The Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale was developed by Connor and Davidson [47]. The scale consists of 25 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The Arabic adaptation was conducted by Toma et al. [56], and the Turkish adaptation was conducted by Karaırmak [57]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was reported as 0.92. The scale demonstrated positive correlations with the Kobasa Resilience Scale (r = .83, p < .01) and the Sheehan Social Support Scale (r = .36, p < .05), while negative correlations were found with the Perceived Stress Scale (r = − .76, p < .01) and the Stress Sensitivity Scale (r = − .32, p < .05). Although a previously translated Arabic version of the CD-RISC was available, its psychometric validity and reliability had not been sufficiently established. Therefore, the Arabic version was used as a reference to ensure an accurate and culturally appropriate translation of the Turkish version for the purposes of the present study. Additionally, the Arabic wording was reviewed by three Arabic language experts to ensure suitability for the target population. During face-to-face data collection, an Arabic interpreter was available to assist participants when necessary. For the current study group, the Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient was found to be 0.95.

Results

Preliminary analysis and assumptions testing

Prior to the primary analyses, the dataset was screened for missing values and data accuracy. No missing data were identified; therefore, the analyses were conducted using the complete data obtained from all 520 participants. In order to examine the descriptive features of the variables in the study and their relations with each other, the mean, standard deviation, skewness and kurtosis values and correlation coefficients were calculated and the results are given in Table 2. The normality assumption regarding regression residuals was examined using a histogram and a probability-probability (P-P) plot (Figs. 1 and 2). It was observed that the residuals had a mean of zero (M = 0.00) and a standard deviation close to one (SD = 0.99), and that the distribution closely followed a normal curve. The observed slight negative skewness is not at a level that would violate the normality assumption, given the sample size of N = 520.

Accordingly, it was observed that all variables, except for the perceived stress sub-factors and the life project nothingness factor, remained within ± 1.5, which is generally accepted as a normal distribution in the literature [58]. Kim [59] states that if there is a sample number of more than 300, it is acceptable for this coefficient to be more than ± 2.0. In the light of this information, it was assumed that all other data were normally distributed, except for the Nothingness variable.

As a result of the correlation analysis, moderately significant relationships were found between the variables. When the correlation analyses were examined, all subdimensions of emotion regulation difficulties showed significant negative associations with life satisfaction. Results are shown in Table 2. This direction was also preserved in the simple regression models. The sign change emerged only in the multiple regression model, which may be explained by a suppressor effect resulting from the high intercorrelations among the subdimensions (r = .70 and above). Suppressor effects may lead to sign reversals in some coefficients as a consequence of the structural overlap among independent variables [60, 61].

In light of this finding, the total score of emotion regulation difficulties was included in the revised analyses instead of the subdimensions. The VIF values ranging between 1.00 and 2.50 further confirmed that multicollinearity remained within acceptable limits. Interpretations were presented consistently by considering the correlation, simple regression, and hierarchical regression findings together.

There is a positive relationship between psychological well-being and life project hope, activation, and resilience variables. Additionally, negative relationships were observed between life project regret, difficulty in emotion regulation sub-factors, and perceived stress sub-factors.

The theoretical rationale for block ordering

Psychological resilience, included in the first block, was treated as a personality-based, relatively stable personal resource and was first incorporated into the model as the foundational layer upon which other variables are built [46, 47]. Ontological well-being/life project, located in the second block, was added following resilience as a cognitive layer representing the structure of meaning and existential orientation. In the third block, emotion regulation difficulty was positioned as a process-oriented capacity operating on the structure of meaning [50]. Perceived stress, located in the final block, is the most situation-dependent and fluctuating variable; it was added last to the model so that its additional contribution could be observed after all other factors had been controlled for [62].

Psychological well-being in refugees was positively predicted by resilience (β = 0.15, p < .01) and hope (β = 0.19, p < .01), whereas regret (β = −0.21, p < .01), difficulties in emotion regulation (β = −0.20, p < .01), and perceived insufficient self-efficacy (β = −0.13, p < .01) emerged as significant negative predictors. Discussion

The COVID-19 process has led to significant changes in the lives of individuals and societies around the world. While many people lost their lives, the difficulties brought about by this process also led to a significant increase in disorders such as depression and anxiety [33]. Refugees are seen as one of the important groups at risk in this process. In this study, the statistical associations between resilience, life project, emotion regulation, and perceived stress on the psychological well-being were examined. The findings obtained from the study showed that resilience, life project, emotion regulation, and perceived stress significantly predicted psychological well-being. Psychological resilience explained and significantly predicted 12% of the total variance in psychological well-being.

Consistent with these results, studies have shown that there is a significant relationship between resilience and psychological well-being [64–66]. Individuals with high levels of psychological resilience cope more effectively with the difficulties they encounter, and this plays an active role in maintaining their well-being [26]. Merhi et al. [67] found, in a study with Lebanese refugees, that social support is an important source of psychological resilience and supports individuals’ psychological well-being. Similarly, another study revealed that psychological resilience was negatively correlated with mental health problems and positively correlated with well-being indicators in Syrian refugees [68]. In addition, studies on the COVID-19 process support these results and show that as psychological resilience increases, mental problems decrease and well-being increases [26, 28]. These results indicate that psychological resilience was positively associated with psychological well-being in refugees during the COVID-19 period.

The findings obtained in this study showed that hope and regret dimensions related to ontological well-being significantly affect psychological well-being. Together with psychological resilience and ontological well-being, it explained 27% of the total variance in psychological well-being. These results indicate that higher levels of ontological well-being were positively associated with higher levels of psychological well-being. Ontological well-being is central to individuals’ lives and deals with their lives with “past”, “future”, and “now” [45]. According to Şimşek [45], the concept of psychological well-being is similar to being hopeful when looking at the future time and defines it as good feelings. Individuals who witness difficult life processes such as war, migration and pandemic need some resources to maintain their psychological well-being [69]. Ontological well-being can be considered as one of these resources. Because, considering the lives of individuals from the perspective of the past, present, and future, seeing the past with satisfaction, the present as dynamic, and the future with hope may be associated with higher levels of psychological well-being [44]. For example, consistent with the results of this study, Kocayürek [70] found that individuals with high ontological well-being have high life goals and high emotional well-being levels.

In addition, the results of this study demonstrated that emotional regulation significantly predicted psychological well-being. Psychological resilience, ontological well-being, and emotion regulation together explained 32% of the total variance in psychological well-being. The variable of psychological well-being was predicted by the subscales of emotional dysregulation, goals, openness, and refusal. One of the crucial factors influencing psychological well-being is emotions [64, 65, 71]. While there are no direct studies on refugees, a review of the literature suggests a relationship between difficulty in emotion regulation, being affected by events, and traumatic experiences. For example, Hocaoğlu [66] revealed a positive and significant relationship between mothers’ levels of being affected by events related to the situation in which they are exposed to immigration and difficulties in emotion regulation. Ildız and Ayhan [72] reported a negative relationship between psychological well-being and difficulty in emotion regulation. These results indicate that higher levels of emotion regulation difficulties were negatively associated with psychological well-being. The expression and regulation of emotions can be considered as important components of psychological well-being.

Finally, the findings obtained from this sample revealed that perceived stress is an important predictor of psychological well-being. It is worth noting that while perceived insufficient self-efficacy emerged as a significant negative predictor of psychological well-being (β = −0.13, p < .01), the perceived helplessness subscale showed a non-significant positive coefficient (β = 0.05, p > .05) in the final regression model. This pattern, which is counterintuitive given the expected negative relationship between helplessness and well-being, may reflect a statistical suppressor effect arising from the high intercorrelation between the two perceived stress subscales. As demonstrated in the preliminary analysis, when emotion regulation subscales were examined individually, similar sign reversals emerged due to structural overlap among predictors — a pattern consistent with suppression effects documented in hierarchical regression literature [69, 73]. Alternatively, among Syrian refugees with prolonged displacement histories, a certain degree of perceived helplessness over external circumstances (e.g., legal status, geopolitical events) may coexist with a form of psychological acceptance and long-term adaptation — a process akin to secondary control or accommodative coping — which could partially attenuate its expected negative effect on well-being. This interpretation is consistent with post-traumatic growth literature suggesting that individuals who have faced protracted adversity may develop adaptive meaning-making processes that buffer the psychological impact of ongoing stressors. Nevertheless, this remains a post-hoc interpretation and should be treated with caution. Future studies using structural equation modeling or longitudinal designs and ideally incorporating measures of coping style and secondary control, would be better positioned to disentangle these relationships. Resilience, ontological well-being, emotion regulation, and perceived stress explained 33% of the total variance in psychological well-being. After migration, individuals often face challenges such as leaving behind economic stability, familiar environments, and social status, as well as living and working in adverse conditions. These experiences become important sources of stress in people’s lives. Moreover, the COVID-19 process has emerged as an additional and noteworthy source of stress due to the experiences it has brought [34]. In a study investigating the impact of COVID-19 on Syrian refugees [31], it was observed that refugees faced challenges in various areas, including economic, psychological, educational, social and medical due to the pandemic. A similar study found that job opportunities represented an important source of stress for refugees during the COVID-19 process [74, 75]. In addition, it is emphasized that the stress factors accompanying displacement for any reason affect the adaptation processes, psychological well-being and coping strategies with stress [76]. These results can be evaluated as the difficulties faced by refugees, as the pandemic may represent an additional source of stress that could be associated with lower psychological well-being.

The findings of this study make important contributions to the regulation of mental health services aimed at supporting psychological well-being of refugees. The results obtained from this study show that psychological resilience, ontological well-being, and emotion regulation were significantly associated with psychological well-being in this sample of refugees. It has also been observed that perceived stress was negatively associated with psychological well-being.

Arslan [77] emphasizes that resilience-based intervention services play an important role in supporting well-being and mental health in the face of stress. Similarly, intervention studies can be organized to improve psychological resilience in refugees. Furthermore, the findings of this study indicate that, while hope for ontological well-being contributes to psychological well-being, regret diminishes it. Considering that hope is an important source of coping in the COVID-19 process [78], it can be considered as an important resource in mental health services to be provided. Supporting hope and addressing regret through intervention services may potentially enable refugees to evaluate their existential lives cognitively and affectively, and could be associated with higher psychological well-being, though this hypothesis warrants testing in future experimental designs. On the other hand, it has been observed that emotional regulation difficulties were negatively associated with psychological well-being in the present sample. Emotion regulation skills training or psychosocial intervention services can be provided for refugees. Finally, when the effect of perceived stress is taken into account, the importance of conducting psychosocial studies on sources of stress is seen. For example, creating job opportunities for refugees or strengthening social support mechanisms may potentially reduce stress-related burden, though experimental studies would be needed to establish effectiveness.

In addition to the results obtained in this study, the evaluation of these findings within certain limitations is of great importance for future studies and applications. Firstly, the data collected for this study were collected by self-report. Given this limitation, it is important to support these findings with additional studies, such as qualitative research, in future investigations. Secondly, due to the cross-sectional design of this study, no causal inferences can be drawn regarding the direction of the relationships between the variables. While hierarchical regression was used to identify predictors, these results represent statistical associations at a single point in time. Future longitudinal or experimental research is needed to establish temporal precedence and causal pathways. Third, the study did not include COVID-specific empirical measures, such as infection history, pandemic-related losses, or lockdown burden. Consequently, COVID-19 serves as a contextual background rather than a directly modeled factor, which limits the ability to isolate pandemic-specific psychological mechanisms. Furthermore, the present study did not account for certain critical control variables that are standard in migrant research, such as Turkish language proficiency and the time elapsed since the last traumatic event. Language proficiency is a primary determinant of post-migration stress and access to social services in the host country, while the duration since the last trauma can significantly moderate levels of psychological resilience. The absence of these measures limits the ability to control for key contextual factors that may influence the relationship between stress, resilience, and well-being. Future studies should incorporate these variables to provide a more comprehensive understanding of refugee adaptation processes. Fourth, the homogeneity and nature of the sample present concerns for generalizability. The sample was recruited via convenience sampling, was predominantly male (62.5%), entirely married, and characterized by a relatively low educational level (75% primary education). This selection bias may overlook the unique experiences of single individuals, women, or more highly educated refugees. In particular, the underrepresentation of women (37.5%) is a meaningful limitation given that female refugees often report distinct stressors related to domestic burdens, childcare, restricted mobility, and gender-based discrimination. Future research should ensure adequate female representation to provide a more balanced understanding of refugee psychological well-being. Lastly, organizing experimental studies based on the findings from this research and examining the effects of these variables on psychological well-being will help address a significant limitation of the generalizability of the findings.
