Acute Syphilitic Posterior Placoid Chorioretinitis and Syphilitic Optic Neuropathy in a Patient With Human Immunodeficiency Virus Co‐Infection
Authors: Shengsheng Liu, Yuanyuan Zhang, Xiaogui Zhao, Bangtao Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSyphilis is a re‐emerging global public health concern; recently, its incidence has significantly increased in all demographic groups. However, acute syphilitic posterior placoid chorioretinitis (ASPPC) and syphilitic optic neuropathy in a patient with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) co‐infection are extremely uncommon. ASPPC is a rare ocular infectious condition caused by syphilis that results in potential blindness. Considering that ocular syphilis may present as an initial manifestation, screening for sexually transmitted diseases is warranted. Here, this article presents a successful treatment protocol that resulted in the complete resolution of a case of ASPPC and syphilitic optic neuropathy with HIV co‐infection.Keywords: acute syphilitic posterior placoid chorioretinitis, best‐corrected visual acuity, human immunodeficiency virus, syphilitic optic neuropathy

Introduction

Syphilis, which is caused by the spirochete bacterium Treponema pallidum, is a re‐emerging public health disease [1]. It is primarily transmitted through sexual contact, particularly via unprotected sexual contact or high‐risk behaviors, and has a worldwide prevalence [1]. Recently, the incidence of syphilis has increased significantly in all demographic groups, including patients with/without human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and heterosexual women [1, 2]. The clinical characteristics of syphilis may manifest in various organs, including skin, brain, and eyes. Therefore, diagnosis is challenging in routine practice [3, 4].

To the best of our knowledge, asymmetric ocular syphilis in a patient with HIV co‐infection is extremely rare. Here, we describe a rare instance of asymmetric ocular syphilis with HIV co‐infection, manifesting as acute syphilitic posterior placoid chorioretinitis (ASPPC) in the right eye and syphilitic optic neuropathy in the left eye.

Case History

A 56‐year‐old man was brought by ambulance to our emergency department with a 3‐day history of sudden painless vision loss in both eyes after experiencing a fever. At presentation, his temperature was 37.3°C. The prehospital physical cooling measures were administered. He denied any history of systemic disease, ocular disorder, surgery, trauma, or drinking alcohol.

Investigation and Diagnosis

An ophthalmic consultation was immediately conducted. The best‐corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/100 in the right eye and 20/40 in the left eye. Both corneas and lenses were normal in both eyes. A mild relative afferent pupillary defect was observed in the left eye. On fundus photography, placoid yellow–white macular lesions were seen in the right eye (Figure 1A), whereas the left eye revealed marked disc edema and peripapillary hemorrhage (Figure 1B). Fluorescence angiography demonstrated mottled hyperfluorescence and hypofluorescence in the macula of the right eye (Figure 1C), combined with fluorescence leakage from the optic disc in the left eye (Figure 1D). Spectral‐domain optical coherence tomography (SD‐OCT) showed nodular irregularity of the retinal pigment epithelium and ellipsoid zone disruption in the right eye (Figure 1E), whereas the left eye showed a swollen nerve fiber layer (Figure 1F).

Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain and orbits was unremarkable. Laboratory results (e.g., routine blood tests, blood biochemistry, and C‐reactive protein) were within the normal limits, and screening for common infectious pathogens (e.g., toxoplasma, rubella virus, cytomegalovirus, tuberculosis, and hepatitis viruses) yielded negative results. However, key positive studies included a reactive syphilis serology (positive T. pallidum particle agglutination with a rapid plasma reagent titer of 1:32), and a positive HIV test with a low CD4 (+)/CD8 (+) T‐cell ratio of 0.21 (reference, 1.2–2.0). Cerebrospinal fluid valuation was negative for syphilis antibodies, but the leukocyte count was 75 × 106/L (reference, 0–8). Furthermore, no mucocutaneous lesions were present.

According to the above investigations, definitive diagnoses of ASPPC in the right eye, syphilitic optic neuropathy in the left eye, syphilis, and HIV co‐infection were made.

Treatment

Immediate treatment with intravenous penicillin G (4 million units, every 4 h for 2 weeks), along with dolutegravir/lamivudine therapy. Treatment for his sexual partners was advised.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Two days later, his fever disappeared. At the 3‐month follow‐up, placoid macular lesions had regressed in the right eye (Figure 2A). The optic disc edema and peripapillary hemorrhage had resolved completely in the left eye (Figure 2C). SD‐OCT showed complete resolution of the retinal pigment epithelium irregularity and ellipsoid zone disruption in the right eye, but an epiretinal membrane developed (Figure 2B). SD‐OCT of the left eye revealed normal (Figure 2D). His BCVA improved to 20/40 in the right eye and 20/25 in the left eye. At the next 3‐month follow‐up, an eightfold decline in the syphilis serological titer was achieved.

Discussion

Ocular involvement (e.g., ASPPC and optic neuropathy) can occur at any stage of syphilis and is considered a form of neurosyphilis [4, 5]. In the present case, although syphilis antibodies in the cerebrospinal fluid were negative, the leukocyte count was significantly elevated, supporting a neurosyphilis diagnosis. Ophthalmic imaging (e.g., fundus photography, SD‐OCT, and fluorescence angiography) in conjunction with positive serology helps in establishing the diagnosis of ocular syphilis [4]. ASPPC is a rare ocular infectious condition caused by syphilis that results in potential blindness. Notably, patients may report severe visual reduction even in the absence of obvious fundoscopic changes. However, the presence of leopard spots patterns on fluorescence angiography and nodular irregularity of the retinal pigment epithelium on SD‐OCT is highly suggestive of syphilis infection [6]. These results help differentiate it from cytomegalovirus retinopathy, which typically exhibits retinal ischemia, retinal hemorrhage, and cotton wool spots. Since ocular syphilis may present as the initial manifestation, patients with acute chorioretinitis and optic neuropathy require screening for syphilis and other sexually transmitted diseases [3, 4]. Furthermore, early diagnosis and immediate treatment are crucial for ocular syphilis, as delayed treatment is associated with poor visual prognosis [3, 7].

Co‐infection with syphilis and HIV is common [1, 3]. Newly diagnosed patients with syphilis should be further investigated for HIV, chlamydia, and gonorrhea. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis is indicated for patients with neurological symptoms, tertiary disease, or HIV co‐infection. As the treatment of syphilis, clinicians typically prefer intramuscular or intravenous injection of penicillin G [1]. For HIV‐infected patients, lifelong treatment recommendation with dolutegravir/lamivudine is well‐established [8]. Besides, tracing close contacts is essential for the effective management of these sexually transmitted diseases [1, 2].

There were several limitations in this study. First, the sample size was small. Second, this study was retrospective. Third, the follow‐up period in the present case was relatively short.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we describe a successful treatment protocol that resulted in the complete resolution of ASPPC and syphilitic optic neuropathy in a patient with HIV co‐infection. The report underscores the high incidence of syphilis and HIV co‐infection and the necessity of screening for sexually transmitted diseases in patients with acute ocular symptoms. Early diagnosis and immediate treatment are crucial, as delayed treatment of ocular syphilis is associated with poor visual prognosis. More data may be needed for better understanding the clinical characteristics and prognosis of asymmetric ocular syphilis.
