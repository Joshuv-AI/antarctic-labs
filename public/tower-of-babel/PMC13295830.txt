Patient experiences of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) in China: a multicentre qualitative study
Authors: Bixuan Yan, Ning Liu, Yunxian Zhou, Xiang Fang, Yue Zhang, Bo Yuan, Ping Ji, Xi Zhan, Yanling Ding, Xiaohan Xu, Xiaoxi Lu, Xinchun Gu, Vanashree Sexton, Fengwei Li, Siyan Zhan, Ying Ji, Hongling Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic progressive lung disease that profoundly impacts patients’ daily lives. While pharmacological therapies continue to advance, less is known about how patients experience the burden of disease across different periods after IPF diagnosis, resulting in a lack of evidence for the development and optimisation of patient-centred intervention strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to explore how individuals with IPF experience the disease and its impact on daily life in China.MethodsThis multicentre qualitative study was conducted using semistructured interviews with participants from six geographic regions in China between November 2024 and March 2025. Participants were grouped by time since diagnosis to reflect different periods following diagnosis. Data were collected and analysed inductively and iteratively by using practical thematic analysis until thematic saturation was achieved.Results53 participants were interviewed to explore the patient experience at different periods following IPF diagnosis. Thematic analysis identified that participants commonly reported the influence of IPF on quality of life, including (1) symptom experience, (2) sleep, (3) eating habits, (4) physical activity, (5) social participation, (6) psychological well-being and (7) financial status. Cough and breathlessness were consistently reported as the most troubling symptoms, often accompanied by psychological challenges related to diagnosis and disease progression.ConclusionsThe findings highlight the experience and need for patient-centred care across China that addresses not only clinical symptoms but also emotional and social dimensions of living with IPF. Enhancing public awareness may also help reduce stigma and social isolation.

Background

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a rare, irreversible and progressive lung disease that affects around 5 million people worldwide and is associated with a median survival of 3–5 years.1 People with IPF commonly experience debilitating symptoms such as cough, dyspnoea and fatigue, which markedly impair quality of life. In advanced stages, the disease imposes substantial physical and psychological challenges, including depression and social withdrawal, and places significant emotional and practical burdens on caregivers.24 However, most existing research has focused on disease mechanisms and treatment efficacy, with limited attention given to lived experience and quality of life,5 especially in the context of China. Therefore, understanding patient experiences and the impact of IPF on quality of life has become increasingly important in disease management. Incorporating these perspectives can also inform the development of new therapeutic strategies and clinical interventions.

Evidence emphasises the importance of patient experience data (PED), defined as information directly reported by patients about their disease and treatment experiences.6 Qualitative studies capturing PED have identified several unmet needs in IPF management, including diagnostic delays, emotional distress and fragmented care systems.4711 For instance, Lyu et al conducted in-depth interviews with 16 participants with IPF in northern China and identified four major themes: diagnostic delays, daily life challenges, limited understanding of the disease and the need for patient education. However, healthcare resources and access to specialist care vary widely across regions in China, which may influence patients’ experiences of IPF, limiting the national applicability of previous findings.12 To strengthen the diversity of qualitative evidence, the present study recruited participants from multiple provinces in China, enabling a broader range of perspectives on the lived experiences of people with IPF. Given the dynamic nature of IPF symptoms and psychosocial challenges, it is essential to provide individualised care based on patient experiences.1315 However, systematic comparisons of patient expectations across disease stages remain limited.1618 Such insights are needed to inform personalised care strategies and policy-level decisions for IPF.

Therefore, this multicentre qualitative study aims to explore the lived experiences of individuals with IPF across China to inform personalised care, patient-centred drug development and future health service planning.

Methods

Design

This descriptive qualitative phase was part of a mixed methods project that commenced in March 2024. Semistructured interviews with individuals with IPF for this phase were conducted between November 2024 and March 2025. The study was approved by the Peking University Third Hospital Medical Science Research Ethics Committee (2024–188-02).

Participants and recruitment

Participants were recruited from five hospitals and one patient organisation using stratified sampling across six regions of China (Northeast, North, Northwest, East, Central-South and Southwest) to ensure geographic diversity. The hospitals involved were Peking University Third Hospital (North China), Beijing Chaoyang Hospital (North China), Shenzhen Peking University–Hong Kong University of Science and Technology Medical Centre (South China), the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (East China) and West China Hospital Sichuan University (Southwest China). These hospitals are tertiary hospitals that provide specialist respiratory care and are located in urban settings. Additional participants were recruited through the patient organisation ‘IPF Care Centre—Take a Deep Breath for Love’, the first IPF-focused patient organisation in China, aiming to improve awareness of IPF, promote earlier diagnosis and treatment access and support patients with IPF, progressive pulmonary fibrosis and other interstitial lung diseases. In hospitals, eligible participants were identified by respiratory physicians and then referred to the research team for interview arrangements. Within the patient organisation, participants were identified and screened by trained researchers in accordance with the study criteria. Prior to the interview, participants completed a written questionnaire to provide demographic information to characterise the sample. The disease duration reported by participants refers to the length of time from diagnosis to the interview. Participants were then grouped according to time since diagnosis to reflect different periods following diagnosis rather than levels of disease severity. The categories were <1 month, 1–6 months (including 6 months), 6–12 months (including 12 months), 12–24 months and ≥24 months. Eligibility criteria included adults (≥18 years) with diagnosed IPF and adequate cognitive and communication abilities. Full criteria are detailed in the study protocol.19 Caregivers (eg, spouses, adult children or other family members involved in daily care or decision-making) were also recruited to provide family caregiver perspectives on patients’ daily experiences. Although the published protocol proposed recruiting 50 patients with IPF, the final sample size was determined iteratively based on the data richness and saturation.19 Saturation was reached after 53 interviews, including 48 patients and five caregivers, with no new information or themes emerging from the data.20

Data collection

Interview questions were developed by the researcher (HC), reviewed by a multidisciplinary research team and refined after a preinterview. Semistructured interviews were conducted by the researcher at each recruitment site, either in person or online, after obtaining signed informed consent. Each interview lasted 30–60 min and was audio-recorded.

Data analysis

The audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim with all names and identifying information removed. Patient (P#) and caregiver (C#) participant numbers were used to identify each participant. Transcripts were analysed using practical thematic analysis in NVivo (V.15, QSR International, Melbourne, Australia). Two researchers (BY and HC) independently coded at least 10% of the data, following the three-phase approach recommended by Saunders et al,21 including reading, coding and theming. Codes were inductively developed from phrases and sentences, reflecting a data-driven analytic process.22 Themes were refined through team discussions and consolidated into a finalised codebook. Any disagreement between BY and HC was resolved through regular discussions to ensure quality and rigour. The audit trail was used to document the data collection process and the various stages of data analysis, stored in NVivo, to enhance the trustworthiness and credibility of the study.23 24 Member checking was conducted by one patient carer to validate the accuracy of interview data. Regular discussions were held with a third-party researcher, experts and stakeholders to support data interpretation. The study is reported in line with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) reporting guideline23 for quality and transparency of the research (for researcher characteristics and reflexivity, see online supplemental material). Means±SD are reported unless specified.

Patient and public involvement

A patient organisation and a caregiver contributed to the study design, reviewed interview guides and provided feedback on the manuscript. The patient organisation supported participant recruitment to enhance diversity, while the caregiver contributed to interpreting findings to ensure alignment with patient and caregiver perspectives. Both were involved across data collection, analysis and dissemination.

Theme 1: symptom experiences across different periods following IPF diagnosis

Symptoms were identified as significant impairments to overall quality of life, evolving from isolated respiratory issues (eg, cough) to multisystem impairments (eg, dyspnoea, oxygen dependence). Persistent cough was often the first symptom prompting medical attention (eg, “I just kept coughing” (P26)). Within the first 1–6 months postdiagnosis, symptoms became more varied. Cough persisted, but breathlessness, fatigue and decreased exercise tolerance became more prominent (eg, “I feel short of breath and have no strength at all” (P1)). At 6–12 months, dyspnoea worsened, interfering with daily activities (eg, “Even taking a shower is difficult” (P40)). Between 1 and 2 years, severe breathlessness and limited mobility were common (eg, “Even walking five metres makes me gasp” (P35)). After 2 years, many were oxygen-dependent. As P43 shared, “I need oxygen 24 hours a day.” In addition to physical impact, some participants reported that symptoms were related to emotional distress. For example, breathlessness and concerns about low oxygen saturation could trigger anxiety and fear. As P42 explained, “I feel a bit anxious what if my oxygen doesn’t reach 95?”. Another described severe breathlessness as extremely painful and difficult to endure (P19).

Theme 2: influence of IPF on sleep at different periods following IPF diagnosis

Most individuals reported the influence of IPF on sleep, which mainly manifested in three types: difficulty falling asleep, night-time awakening and sleep fragmentation and no influence on sleep.

Subtheme: difficulty falling asleep

Difficulty falling asleep was reported primarily in the <6 month and ≥2-year postdiagnosis stages. Participants attributed insomnia to psychological distress or physiological discomfort. For instance, one early-stage participant said, “I couldn’t sleep well, so they gave me sleeping pills” (P36), while a late-stage participant shared, “I just can’t sleep at all at night” (P39).

Subtheme: night-time awakening and sleep fragmentation

The most frequently reported sleep issue was night-time awakening, which was especially prevalent among those in the 1- to 2-year and ≥2-year postdiagnosis stages. Participants described frequent waking due to coughing or breathlessness. One noted, “I wake up coughing” (P22), and another described getting up “four to five times a night” (P23). Even in earlier postdiagnosis stages, disrupted sleep was noted: “I must get up and sit a while before I can sleep again” (P25).

Subtheme: no influence on sleep

Conversely, some participants across various periods after diagnosis reported that sleep was not influenced by IPF. Some early-stage participants viewed sleep interruptions as part of normal ageing, not necessarily linked to their illness. “I wake up a couple of times, but that’s normal at our age” (P8). Others, even in later stages, maintained good sleep quality through lifestyle adjustments. Several patients stated, “Sleep is not a problem for me” (P26 and P48).

Theme 3: changes in eating habits across different periods following IPF diagnosis

Changes in eating habits were commonly reported by participants. The key issues included reduced appetite and food intake, dietary restrictions, weight loss and no influence on eating.

Subtheme: reduced appetite and food intake

Loss of appetite and reduced food consumption were the most frequently mentioned concerns. Participants described early satiety, discomfort after eating and loss of interest in previously enjoyed foods (eg, “I can’t eat much anymore.” (P1)). Emotional distress was also a contributing factor: “I feel so overwhelmed I don’t even think about eating” (P10).

Subtheme: dietary restrictions due to symptom triggers

Dietary restrictions were particularly common among participants beyond 6 months after diagnosis, often due to the perception that certain foods exacerbated coughing, indigestion or discomfort. One participant reported, “I can’t eat anything spicy, it triggers coughing.” (P22). Another said, “I used to love cured meats and beef, but now I don’t dare eat them. Even the smell triggers discomfort” (P28).

Subtheme: unintentional weight loss

Unintentional weight loss was mainly reported in the 1- to 2-year and ≥2-year postdiagnosis stages, often linked to poor appetite or functional decline. “He used to weigh 70 kg now he’s down to 55 kg” (C5). In some cases, weight loss was described in relation to medications or digestive issues, and most participants reported current antifibrotic use. Late-stage participants also showed signs of muscle wasting and weakness, as described by C3: “She’s basically skin and bones now, no muscle, no fat.”

Subtheme: no perceived influence on eating

No major eating changes were noted by some across all postdiagnosis stages. These participants maintained normal intake, often due to better coping strategies. For instance, “Her appetite is actually quite good, she eats up all the food and even asks for more” (C4).

Theme 4: physical activity limitations in different periods following IPF diagnosis

Most participants reported a progressive decline in physical activity across all stages. Three subthemes emerged: reduced capacity for daily activities (eg, fatigue during walking), loss of ability to exercise (inability to engage in structured physical activity) and home confinement and decreased activity space (difficulty leaving the home). However, a small number of participants reported no impact of IPF on their physical activity. For instance, P21 continued to dance in the square for over an hour every day, while another stated that the illness did not affect the ability to do outdoor activities (P26).

Subtheme: reduced capacity for daily physical activities

Reduced capacity for daily tasks was frequently noted, even in the first month after diagnosis (eg, “I can only walk a few hundred meters” (P38)). By 6–12 months after diagnosis, participants described worsening limitations and the need for frequent rests. “If I walk even slightly faster, I start gasping” (P22). At 1–2 years after diagnosis, even minor movements triggered exhaustion: “Once I move, I fall apart” (P9). In ≥2 years after diagnosis, participants could manage only one activity per day, eg, “Either I cook or go outside, not both” (P44).

Subtheme: loss of ability to exercise

Many participants gave up long-standing activities like swimming or football. P1 said, “I swam for over 20 years, now I just don’t dare anymore.” Others described this as a sudden, irreversible loss: “I used to play football. Once the disease started, it was all gone (P43).”

Subtheme: home confinement and decreased activity space

In addition to reducing exercise capacity, IPF also reduced patients’ physical activity space. Many patients described a gradual transition from outdoor activities to spending most of their time indoors, and in some cases becoming largely confined to their homes. P51 shared, “I just couldn’t go outside anymore.” Others could only perform brief activities with long recovery times, eg, “I can garden for 1 hour in the morning, but I must rest the entire afternoon.” (P20)

Theme 5: influence of IPF on social participation at different periods following IPF diagnosis

Most participants reported the influence of IPF on social life. Three subthemes were identified across different disease stages: reduced participation in outdoor and community activities, decreased social interaction and disease-related psychological concerns.

Subtheme: reduced participation in outdoor and community activities

In the first 1–6 months after diagnosis, some participants began to avoid going out or taking part in outdoor and community activities because of cold air, fear of symptom exacerbation or concerns about environmental triggers (eg, “She doesn't dare to go out, afraid of getting chilled and triggering the disease” (C2)). By 1–2 years, concerns about infection and reduced physical stamina further discouraged going outside: “I just don’t go out anymore, afraid of catching something.” (P45)

Subtheme: decreased social participation

Social withdrawal was commonly linked to both physical limitations and emotional reluctance. Even in early stages, some avoided socialising due to stigma or fear of coughing in public (eg, “I avoid socializing, people might think I’m contagious” (P41)). By 6–12 months, many had disengaged from group activities. One noted, “I can’t keep up, and they don’t want to walk with me anymore” (P14). In later stages, participants preferred staying at home (eg, “He just wants to stay at home” (C5)).

Subtheme: disease-related psychological concerns

Misconceptions about the disease’s communicability added psychological burden. Some participants expressed fear of infecting others (eg, “I still worry if this disease is contagious.” [P41]). Public coughing often led to embarrassment. “When I go out and start coughing, people look at me like I’m contagious” (P49). As the disease progressed, self-consciousness intensified, with one caregiver noting, “She’s afraid people laugh at her” (C1). Additionally, limited awareness and understanding of IPF further contributed to patients’ psychological distress. Several participants noted that they had never searched for information about the disease or joined patient groups, reflecting a general lack of disease-specific knowledge (eg, “No, I haven’t looked it up or joined any groups (P26)”). Others commented that IPF is so rare that most people “don’t know anything about it (P8),” leaving them uncertain about how to interpret their symptoms or the diagnosis.

Theme 6: psychological well-being across different periods following IPF diagnosis

Most participants described the emotional responses and psychological adjustments. Results revealed three major subthemes: emotional fluctuations and psychological adaptation, emotional distress and emotional stability and optimism.

Subtheme: emotional fluctuations and psychological adaptation

Initial emotional distress, including anxiety and fear, was common postdiagnosis, but some participants gradually adapted with reassurance and family support. One participant shared, “You just have to talk yourself” (P32). Emotional adjustment became more evident by 6–12 months and continued through the 1- to 2-year stage, with many expressing emotional calmness (eg, “I’m more used to it now not as anxious as before”) (P13).

Subtheme: emotional distress and pessimistic attitudes

Severe emotional distress was especially prominent within the first month of diagnosis, with participants describing despair, hopelessness and even suicidal thoughts (eg, P10: “I didn’t want to live”; “I didn’t know what the point of living was anymore” (P47)). While some adjusted emotionally by 6–12 months, many continued to report anxiety, often tied to oxygen use, symptom progression or social stigma (P42: “What if my oxygen doesn’t reach 95?”). Emotional volatility and negative self-perception were also common, with fears of judgement and psychological pressure persisting (eg, “She’s always afraid people will laugh at her” (C2)).

In the 1- to 2-year postdiagnosis stage, emotional instability was often triggered by physical decline, chronic breathlessness or loss of independence. Some reported despair or thoughts of giving up (P19: “It was better to die than to suffer like this”), while others noted increased irritability or mood swings (eg, “I’ve become more irritable due to the illness” (P11)). In later stages (≥2 years), while acute distress lessened, emotional fatigue and anxiety remained, often tied to treatment costs and fear of being a burden (eg, “I'm afraid of becoming burdening my family” (P5)).

Caregivers also reported emotional strain from increasing care demands (eg, “The caregiving burden is definitely something to worry about” (C1)). Patients also described distress associated with reversed family roles, such as “relying on parents or children for daily support” (P44). This role reversal and perceived burden intensified emotional distress for both patients and families.

Subtheme: emotional stability and optimism

Despite the psychological burden of IPF, many participants reported sustaining a positive attitude, supported by family, personal beliefs or lifestyle adjustments (eg, “No one puts pressure on me, no stress” (P2)). Between 6 and 12 months, several described emotional stability and optimism (P7: “I’m naturally optimistic”), while by 1–2 years, many had reached calm acceptance, often related to age or experience (P30: “At my age, getting sick is just part of life”). Emotional well-being was also sustained through socialising, travel or spirituality (P21: “Dancing with friends keeps me happy”).

Theme 7: perceived financial burden at different periods following IPF diagnosis

Participants reported varying levels of financial burden caused by IPF. Two subthemes emerged: significant economic impact and manageable burden.

Subtheme: significant economic impact

Economic burden was a major concern across all disease stages. While inpatient costs were reimbursed, high-cost outpatient drugs required out-of-pocket payments. One reported, “The monthly drug cost is unaffordable” (P27). By 6–12 months after diagnosis, financial strain intensified. Some took part-time jobs to cover basic care (P22: “If I don’t work, we can’t even afford food”). In later postdiagnosis stages (≥2 years), additional costs for supportive treatments, such as supplements or traditional Chinese medicine, further added to the burden (eg, “Pirfenidone and cough medicine are long-term expenses, it’s a heavy financial burden” (P18)).

Subtheme: minimal or manageable economic impact

Despite the overall burden, a minority across all postdiagnosis stages reported manageable financial strain. Some attributed this to prior financial preparation (eg, “I don’t really have any financial pressure” (P26)). Others benefited from insurance or outpatient reimbursement (P7: “All covered by insurance”; P51: “Supported by a foundation”). Participants who had pensions or financial support from adult children found the burden present but tolerable (P1: “More than half of my pension goes to medicine”).

Discussion

This multicentre qualitative study provides insight into the lived experiences of people with IPF across multiple regions of China and identified the multidimensional and progressive influence of IPF on quality of life, including symptoms, sleep, diet, physical activity, social participation, emotional well-being and financial burden.

Cough and breathlessness emerged as the most burdensome symptoms. Cough often appeared first, while breathlessness, fatigue and reduced mobility became more severe over time. Later postdiagnosis stages were marked by oxygen dependence and activity limitation. These findings align with previous research showing that breathlessness increases with disease progression.25 Chronic cough is not only physically exhausting but socially and emotionally disruptive,26 27 caused by multiple factors including mechanical, biochemical and neurosensory triggers.2830 Similarly, Russell et al recruited participants from the UK, Germany and Italy to investigate patients’ perspectives on living with IPF and reported a substantial symptom burden together with a marked impact on quality of life.31 These findings suggest that pharmaceutical development should focus not only on slowing disease progression but also on addressing symptom relief at different periods after IPF diagnosis to improve quality of life for people with IPF.

Psychological distress was another major challenge characterised by anxiety, fear and emotional overwhelm, which is consistent with previous studies on IPF patients and caregivers.4 32 Breathlessness often triggered anxiety, creating a vicious cycle that worsened symptoms.33 Side effects and uncertainty about disease progression added to this emotional burden. As shown by Crooks et al, chronic breathlessness profoundly impacts individuals’ lives and is associated with increased risk of anxiety and depression.34 Similar results have been reported in studies linking functional decline to emotional deterioration.35 36 Depression and anxiety not only reduce quality of life but may also intensify breathlessness,37 38 highlighting the need for routine psychological screening in IPF care. As the disease advanced, psychological responses diverged. Some participants in this study gradually adapted through self-directed coping strategies, such as self-reassurance, cognitive reframing and acceptance of the diagnosis. Others described emotional improvement following encouragement from family members and healthcare professionals. However, some remained emotionally fragile throughout the disease course. Evidence suggests that people use a variety of coping approaches, including emotion-focused, problem-solving, appraisal-based and social support seeking to help regulate emotional responses.39 Overgaard et al who recruited 25 patients from specialist clinics at two university hospitals and 24 caregivers, highlighted the negatively emotional ambivalence experienced by both patients and caregivers.4 However, the current study identified a wider range of psychological responses, with some participants describing significant distress, while others reported using coping strategies to manage their emotions or expressed optimism and gradual acceptance over time. These differences may reflect variation in individual coping styles, family and professional support and the timing of interviews in relation to diagnosis and disease experience. These findings highlight the need to integrate psychological assessment and support into routine IPF care, tailored to individual coping styles and needs across the postdiagnosis disease course.

Restricted social participation was another critical dimension influencing quality of life. Many participants reported withdrawing from social interactions due to stigma, fear of infection and limited public awareness of IPF. These findings are in line with previous research showing that poor public awareness of IPF contributes to patients’ emotional burdens.31 People also reported uncertainty about physical activity and daily routines, highlighting unmet needs for accessible postdiagnosis education. As emphasised by the European IPF Patients’ Charter,9 accessible information and caregiver support are crucial. Many caregivers felt unprepared and emotionally strained, underscoring the need for psychosocial and educational support.31 Thus, targeted resources for both patients and families are essential to reduce the social and emotional burden of IPF in China.

Together, these findings provide a deeper understanding of the lived experiences of people with IPF across multiple regions of China and offer practical implications for clinical care and policy. For clinicians, this study underscores the need for patient-centred management integrating symptom control, psychological support and patient education. For policy makers, the findings highlight the need to improve disease awareness, expand access to multidisciplinary care and strengthen caregiver support to enhance quality of life and reduce the burden of IPF in China.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. Recruiting participants from six regions across China enhanced the diversity and relevance of the findings. Exploring experiences across different postdiagnosis stages of IPF provided insight into how patients’ needs evolve and informed patient-centred care. Examining impacts beyond symptom burden also offers implications for personalised management. Several limitations should be noted. Although we aimed to improve representativeness during the design of the study by collaborating with patient organisations to recruit participants and collect data across different regions and resource settings, only one participant was recruited from Northwest China, which limits representation of underserved regions. Additionally, the hospital-based sample was mainly from large urban tertiary hospitals. Patients recruited through patient organisations may also have had greater access to resources, information and healthcare services, which limited the representation of individuals in rural or less-resourced settings. It is also important to examine whether coping strategies differ across demographic groups to understand heterogeneity in psychological adaptation. However, due to limited subgroup representation and exploration of factors such as family support, potential subgroup differences could not be investigated in this study. Further research is therefore required. Another limitation is that participants were grouped by time since diagnosis, which may not accurately reflect disease severity. As this qualitative study did not collect clinical indicators such as lung function, gas exchange or exercise capacity, more studies are needed in the future to combine patient-reported experiences with clinical measures to explore how disease severity relates to lived experience. Finally, longitudinal studies are also needed to better understand how patients’ needs and coping strategies develop over time.

Conclusions

This study explored the lived experiences of patients with IPF across China. The findings show that IPF imposes a multidimensional impact on daily life, affecting symptoms, sleep, diet, physical activity, social participation, emotional well-being and financial status. These insights highlight the need for patient-centred management strategies that address not only clinical symptoms but also the emotional, social and financial challenges of living with IPF. The results further underscore the importance of strengthening supportive care and guiding the development of future pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions that align with patients’ evolving priorities and meaningfully improve quality of life.
