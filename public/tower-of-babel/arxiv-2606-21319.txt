On Significant Pointwise Contractions and Annular Contractions
Ovidiu Popescu

Abstract
In the current paper, we introduce the class of significant pointwise contractions . This family of mappings extends the classical Banach contractions and also contains nonexpansive mappings. We prove that in a complete metric space every significant pointwise contraction admits a fixed point that can be approximated through Picard iterations. We provide examples showing that the newly introduced class differs from the class of piecewise contractions and is not contained in it. Moreover, we point out that significant pointwise contractions are continuous and may possess multiple fixed points. We further introduce the class of significant pointwise shrinkings , which generalizes the class of shrinkings, and prove that any such mapping has a fixed point in a compact metric space. Moreover, we define the class of annular pointwise contractions , which includes Banach contractions but is distinct from the class of Suzuki mappings. Finally, we obtain a fixed point theorem for annular pointwise contractions and present an additional condition ensuring the existence of fixed points.



Keywords—
fixed point, contractions, Picard iteration, significant pointwise contraction, annular pointwise contractions

In 2024, Pant
[

extended the Banach Contraction Principle and defined a condition that applied to contraction mappings as well as nonexpansive mappings.

Theorem 1.7

(see Theorem 2.1 from
[

)
.

Let

T:X\to X

, we have

d(Tx,Ty)\leq cd(x,y),

Considering both the theoretical and application importance of the Banach contraction principle, the aim of this paper is to introduce two new classes of contractive operators that include Picard-Banach contractions and a particular case. These classes are similar to (uPC), respectively (uAPC) and larger then the class of contractive mappings in Theorem
1.7
.

We then study the existence and uniqueness of fixed points and prove some strong convergence theorems for Picard iteration used to approximate the fixed points. Moreover, we present examples to illustrate the generality of our new results.

2
Significant pointwise contractions

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. A mapping
T:X\to X
is said to be
significant pointwise contractive
(SPC) if there exists
c\in[0,1)
such that

d(x,y)\leq d(x,Tx)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{implies}\hskip 9.24994ptd(Tx,Ty)\leq cd(x,y),

(1)

for all
x,y\in X
.

Remark 2.1
.

It is obvious that (C) are (SPC) and that the contractive mappings in Pant’s Theorem
1.7
are (SPC).

The following examples show that the class of (SPC) mappings is different from the class of (PC) and (uPC) mappings and it is not included in the class of (PC) and (uPC) mappings.

Example 2.1
.

Consider
X=[0,1]
with the standard distance and define
T:X\to X
by
Tx=x
for every
x\in X
. Then
X
is compact, while
T
is (SPC), but it is not (PC).

Example 2.2
.

Consider
X=[0,1]\cup[2,3]
with the standard distance and define
T:X\to X
by
Tx=0
if
x\in[0,1]
and
Tx=1
if
x\in[2,3]
. Then
T
is (uPC), but it is not (SPC). Indeed,
d(T1,T2)=1\geq d(1,2)
and
d(1,2)=1\leq d(1,T1)
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Every (SPC) is continuous.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}
be a point in
X
. If
x_{0}
is isolated, then, clearly,
T
is continuous at
x_{0}
.

Let now
x_{0}
be an accumulation point. Then there exists a sequence
\{x_{n}\}
in
X
such that
\{x_{n}\}\to x
.

If
x\neq Tx
, for
n
sufficiently large we have
d(x_{n},x)\leq d(x,Tx)
, so by (
1
) we obtain
d(Tx_{n},Tx)\leq d(x_{n},x)
. Taking the limit as
n\to\infty
, we get
d(Tx_{n},Tx)\to 0
, thus
T
is continuous at
x
.

If
x=Tx
, we suppose that
T
is not continuous at
x
. Then, there exist
\varepsilon>0
and a subsequence
\{x_{n(k)}\}
of
\{x_{n}\}
such that
d(Tx_{n(k)},x)>\varepsilon
, for every
k\geq 1
. Since
x_{n(k)}\to x
, there exists
k_{0}\geq 1
such that
d(x_{n(k)},x)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
, for
k\geq k_{0}
. Then, we have

d(x_{n(k)},Tx_{n(k)})\geq d(Tx_{n(k)},x)-d(x_{n(k)},x)>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}>d(x_{n(k)},x).

By (
1
), we obtain
d(Tx_{n(k)},Tx)\leq cd(x_{n(k)},x)
.

Then,

0<\varepsilon<cd(x_{n(k)},x)<c\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Since
c<1
, we have a contradiction. Therefore,
T
is continuous at
x
.
∎

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space and
T:X\to X
be an (SPC) mapping. Then,
T
has a fixed point.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\in X
be arbitrarily chosen and let
\{x_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be the Picard iteration, i.e. the sequence defined by
x_{n}=Tx_{n-1}=T^{n}x_{0}
.

Since
d(x_{n-1},x_{n})=d(x_{n-1},Tx_{n-1})
for all
n\geq 1
, taking
x=x_{n-1}
and
y=x_{n}
in (
1
) we get

d(Tx_{n-1},Tx_{n})\leq cd(x_{n-1},x_{n}).

Hence,
d(x_{n},x_{n+1})\leq cd(x_{n-1},x_{n})
, for every
n\geq 1
. This implies that

d(x_{n},x_{n+1})\leq c^{n}d(x_{0},x_{1}),

and then, for
p\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle d(x_{n},x_{n+p})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{p}d(x_{n+i-1},x_{n+i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{p}c^{n+i-1}d(x_{0},x_{1})

\displaystyle=c^{n}(1+c+\dots+c^{p-1})d(x_{0},x_{1})\leq\dfrac{c^{n}}{1-c}d(x_{0},x_{1}).

Since
c^{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, we obtain that
\{x_{n}\}
is a Cauchy sequence and, hence, it is convergent in the complete metric space
X
, i.e. there exists
x^{*}\in X
such that

x^{*}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}x_{n}.

Suppose that
d(x^{*},Tx^{*})>0.
Then, for
n
sufficiently large we have
d(x_{n},x^{*})\leq d(x^{*},Tx^{*})
, and by (
1
) taking
x=x^{*}
and
y=x_{n}
, we obtain
d(Tx^{*},Tx_{n})\leq cd(x^{*},x_{n})
. Taking the limit as
n\to\infty
, we get
d(Tx^{*},x^{*})=0
, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
d(Tx^{*},x^{*})=0
, so
x^{*}
is a fixed point of
T
.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Additionally, if an (SPC) satisfies

d(x,y)\geq Ld(x,Tx)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{implies}\hskip 9.24994ptd(Tx,Ty)\leq cd(x,y),

for all
x,y\in X
, where
L>1
, then
T
has a unique fixed point.

Example 2.3
.

Consider
X=\{(x,2k):x\in[0,1],\,k\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}\}
and
d
be the Euclidean metric. Then
T:X\to X
defined by
T(x,2k)=\left(\frac{x}{2},2k\right)
is an (SPC) with
c=\dfrac{1}{2}
and has

does not not verify the hypothesis of Theorem
1.7

Example 2.4

(see Example 2.4. from
[
15
]
)
.

Consider
X=\{re^{i\theta}:0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi,\,r=1,3,3^{2},\dots\}
be the self-similar family of concentric circles, each lying within larger circles having radii in geometric progression, in the
xy
-plane. Let
d
be the Euclidean metric. Define
T:X\to X
by
Tz=\dfrac{z}{|z|}=\dfrac{z}{r}
. Then,
T
satisfies (
1
) with
c=\dfrac{1}{2}
so
T
is an (SPC) and each point of the unit circle

(i.e. there exists a positive integer
N
such that
T^{n+1}x=T^{n}x
for
n\geq N
).

Definition 2.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. We say that
T:X\to X
is
significant pointwise shrinking
(SPS), provided

0<d(y,x)\leq d(x,Tx)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{implies}\hskip 9.24994ptd(Tx,Ty)<d(x,y),

(2)

for all
x,y\in X
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
(X,d)
be a compact metric space and
T:X\to X
be an (SPS) mapping. Then,
T
has a fixed point.

Proof.

For
x\neq Tx
we have
0<d(x,Tx)\leq d(x,Tx)
so by (
2
) we have
d(Tx,T^{2}x)<d(x,Tx)
. Let
\beta=\inf\{d(x,Tx):x\in X\}.

Since
X
is compact, there exists a sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
in
X
such that
x_{n}\to u\in X
,
Tx_{n}\to v\in X
and
\beta=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}d(x_{n},Tx_{n})=d(u,v)
.

Suppose
\beta>0
. If there exists a subsequence
\{x_{n(k)}\}
of
{x_{n}}
such that
x_{n(k)}\neq u
for all
k\geq 1
, we have
0<d(x_{n(k)},u)\leq d(x_{n(k)},Tx_{n(k)})
for
k
sufficiently large. By (
2
) we get
d(Tx_{n(k)},Tu)<d(x_{n(k)},u)
. Taking the limit as
k\to\infty
, we have
d(v,Tu)\leq 0
, so
v=Tu
.

Otherwise, we have
x_{n}=u
, for
n
sufficiently large, by where
Tx_{n}=Tu
and
\beta=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}d(x_{n},Tx_{n})=d(u,Tu)
. But
d(Tu,T^{2}u)<d(u,Tu)=\beta,
which is a contradiction.

Therefore,
\beta=0
, hence
u=v
.

Suppose
d(u,Tu)>0
. Then, for
n
sufficiently large
d(u,x_{n})\leq d(u,Tu)
. By (
2
), we obtain that
d(Tu,Tx_{n})<d(u,x_{n})
. Taking the limit as
n\to\infty
, we have
d(Tu,u)=0
, which is a contradiction. Hence,
d(u,Tu)=0
, i.e.
u
is a fixed point of
T
.
∎

Example 2.5
.

Consider
X=[0,1]\cup[2,3]
with the standard distance. Let
T:X\to X
be defined by
Tx=1
if
x\in[0,1]
and
Tx=2
if
x\in[2,3]
. Then,
X
is compact and
T
is (SPS).

Indeed, for
x,y\in[0,1]
, or
x,y\in[2,3]
,

we have
d(Tx,Ty)=0<d(x,y)
. If
x\in[0,1]
and

, we get
d(Tx,Ty)=1
and

, with equality when

and

. However, in this case we have
d(x,Tx)=0
,

, so
d(x,y)>d(x,Tx)
and

. Hence,

satisfies (
2
). Moreover, we have

, so

is not (C).

Additionally, if an (SPS) satisfies

for all

3
Annular pointwise contraction

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. A mapping
T:X\to X
is said to be
annular pointwise contractive
(APC) if there exists
c\in[0,1)
such that

\dfrac{1}{1+c}d(x,Tx)\leq d(x,y)\leq\dfrac{1}{1-c}d(x,Tx)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{implies}\hskip 9.24994ptd(Tx,Ty)\leq cd(x,y),

(3)

for all
x,y\in X
.

Remark 3.1
.

It is obvious that (C) are (APC) and that the contractive mappings in Pant’s Theorem
1.7
are (APC).

Example 3.1
.

Consider
X=\{0,1,2,3\}
with the standard distance. Let
T:X\to X
be defined by
T0=T2=0
and
T1=T3=1
. Then
T
is (APC), but
T
is not (SPC) and
T
does not satisfy the hypothesis in Pant’s Theorem
1.7
.

Indeed, for
x=0
or
x=1
, (
3
) is obvious. If
x=2
, since
\frac{2}{3}d(2,T2)\leq d(2,y)\leq 2d(2,T2)
, we get
y=0
, so
d(T2,T0)=0<\frac{1}{2}d(2,0)=1
. For
x=3
, since
\frac{2}{3}d(3,T3)\leq d(3,y)\leq 2d(3,T3)
, we get
y=0
or
y=0
. But
d(T3,T1)=0<\frac{1}{2}d(3,1)=1
. Moreover,
d(1,2)=1\leq d(2,T2)=2
and
d(T1,T2)=1=d(1,2)
, so
T
is not (SPC) and does not satisfy the hypothesis in Pant’s Theorem
1.7
. Also, since
\dfrac{1}{1+c}d(0,T0)=0\leq d(0,1)
and
d(T0,T1)>cd(0,1)
,
T
does not verify the hypothesis of Suzuki’s theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space and
T:X\to X
be a (APC) mapping. Then,
T
has a fixed point.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\in X
be arbitrarily chosen and let
\{x_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be the Picard iteration, i.e. the sequence defined by
x_{n}=Tx_{n-1}=T^{n}x_{0}
.

Since
\dfrac{d(x_{n-1},x_{n})}{1+c}=d(x_{n-1},Tx_{n-1})\leq\dfrac{d(x_{n-1},x_{n})}{1-c}
for all
n\geq 1
, taking
x=x_{n-1}
and
y=x_{n}
in
3
we get

d(Tx_{n-1},Tx_{n})\leq cd(x_{n+1},x_{n}).

Hence,
d(x_{n},x_{n+1})\leq cd(x_{n-1},x_{n})
, for every
n\geq 1
. Like in the proof of Theorem
2.2
, we obtain that
\{x_{n}\}
is a Cauchy sequence. Since
X
is complete we have
x_{n}\to x^{*}\in X
.

If there exists
n\geq 1
such that

\dfrac{1}{1+c}d(x_{n-1},x_{n})>d(x_{n-1},x^{*})\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\dfrac{1}{1+c}d(x_{n},x_{n+1})>d(x_{n},x^{*}),

then,

\displaystyle d(x_{n-1},x_{n})

\displaystyle\leq d(x_{n-1},x^{*})+d(x_{n},x^{*})<\dfrac{1}{1+c}[d(x_{n-1},x_{n})+d(x_{n},x_{n+1})]

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{1+c}[d(x_{n-1},x_{n})+cd(x_{n-1},x_{n})]=d(x_{n-1},x_{n}),

which is a contradiction. Therefore, we have

\dfrac{1}{1+c}d(x_{n-1},x_{n})\leq d(x_{n-1},x^{*})\hskip 9.24994pt\text{or}\hskip 9.24994pt\dfrac{1}{1+c}d(x_{n},x_{n+1})<d(x_{n},x^{*}).

Since

\displaystyle d(x_{n+p},x_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}d(x_{n+i+1},x_{n+i})\leq\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}c^{i}d(x_{n+1},x_{n})

\displaystyle=(1+c+\dots+c^{p-1})d(x_{n+1},x_{n})<\dfrac{1}{1-c}d(x_{n+1},x_{n}),

taking the limit as
p\to\infty
, we get
d(x^{*},x_{n})\leq\dfrac{1}{1-c}d(x_{n+1},x_{n})
, for all
n\geq 0
.

Therefore, there exists a subsequence
\{x_{n(k)}\}
of
\{x_{n}\}
such that

\dfrac{1}{1+c}d(x_{n(k)},x_{n(k)}+1)\leq d(x_{n(k)},x^{*})\leq\dfrac{1}{1-c}d(x_{n(k)},x_{n(k)}+1),

for every
k\geq 1
. Then
d(Tx_{n(k)},Tx^{*})\leq cd(x_{n(k)},x^{*})
, by where we get, as
k\to\infty
, that
d(x^{*},Tx^{*})=0
, i.e.
x^{*}
is a fixed point of
T
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

If, additionally, an (APC) satisfies the condition

d(x,y)\geq Ld(x,Tx)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{implies}\hskip 9.24994ptd(Tx,Ty)\leq cd(x,y),

for all
x,y\in X
, where
L\geq\dfrac{1}{1-c}
, then
T
has a unique fixed point.

4
Conclusions

In the end, let us summarize the main contributions of the current paper.

1.

We introduce the class of significant pointwise contractions, which includes Picard-Banach contractions and also contain nonexpansive mappings.

2.

We show that in a complete metric space, any significant pointwise contraction has a fixed point that can be approximated by means of Picard iterations.

3.

We present relevant examples to show that the class of significant pointwise contractions is different from the class of piecewise contractions and is not included in it.

4.

It is worth mentioning that any significant pointwise contraction is continuous and can have many fixed points.

5.

We introduce the class of significant pointwise shrinkings, which includes the class of shrinkings, and show that any significant pointwise shrinking has a fixed point in a compact metric space.

6.

We also introduce the class of annular pointwise contractions, which includes Picard-Banach contractions and is different from the class of Suzuki mappings.

7.

Last but not least, we obtain a fixed point theorem for annular pointwise contractions and give an additional condition under which we have a fixed point result.

8.

Moreover, the results contained in the present paper open the way to new interesting results and generalizations of Kannan contractions (see
[
16
]
) or Chatterjea contractions (see
[
17
]
), and many other results.
