Filtered Reasoning Score: Evaluating Reasoning Quality on a Model’s Most Confident Traces
Manas Pathak, Xingyao Chen, Shuozhe Li, Amy Zhang, Leqi Liu

Abstract
Should we trust Large Language Models (LLMs) with high accuracy? LLMs achieve high accuracy on reasoning benchmarks, but correctness alone does not reveal the quality of the reasoning used to produce it. This highlights a fundamental limitation of outcome-based evaluation: models may arrive at correct answers through flawed reasoning, and models with substantially different reasoning capabilities can nevertheless exhibit similar benchmark accuracy, for example due to memorization or over-optimization. In this paper, we ask: given existing benchmarks, can we move beyond outcome-based evaluation to assess the quality of reasoning itself? We seek metrics that (1) differentiate models with similar accuracy and (2) are robust to variations in input prompts and generation configurations. To this end, we propose a reasoning score that evaluates reasoning traces along dimensions such as faithfulness, coherence, utility, and factuality. A remaining question is how to aggregate this score across multiple sampled traces. Naively averaging them is undesirable, particularly in long-horizon settings, where the number of possible trajectories grows rapidly, and low-confidence correct traces are more likely to be coincidental. To address this, we introduce the Filtered Reasoning Score (FRS), which computes reasoning quality using only the top- K % K\% most confident traces. Evaluating with FRS, models that are indistinguishable under standard accuracy exhibit significant differences in reasoning quality. Moreover, models with higher FRS on one benchmark tend to perform better on other reasoning benchmarks, in both accuracy and reasoning quality. Together, these findings suggest that FRS complements accuracy by capturing a model’s transferable reasoning capabilities. We open source our evaluation codebase: https://github.com/Manas2006/benchmark_reproducibility .

Introduction

Large language models have advanced rapidly in recent years,
with newer models achieving higher scores on an expanding set of reasoning benchmarks
(
16
;
2
;
31
)
.
Yet the way we evaluate these models has not kept pace.
The dominant paradigm remains final-answer accuracy: a model is scored by how often it produces the correct output, with no regard for the reasoning process that produced it.

This paradigm is increasingly inadequate.
Models can produce flawed reasoning that still leads to correct answers
(
25
;
35
;
34
)
.
As a result, accuracy gains do not reliably reflect improvements in reasoning quality
(
39
)
, and benchmark saturation further reduces their ability to distinguish between models
(
3
)
.
Moreover, outcome-based evaluation can be sensitive to prompt choice and generation configuration, further obscuring differences in underlying reasoning ability
(
18
)
.

These limitations motivate a shift from evaluating outcomes to evaluating reasoning itself. In this work, we ask:
can we use existing benchmarks to assess the quality of reasoning, beyond final-answer correctness?
We seek metrics that (1) differentiate models with similar accuracy with respect to their underlying reasoning capabilities, and (2) are robust to variations in prompts and generation configurations. To this end, we propose to evaluate reasoning traces directly using a rubric-based reasoning score that measures properties such as faithfulness, coherence, utility, and factuality (Section
3.1
).

A second challenge is aggregation: models can produce many reasoning traces for the same problem, with substantial variation in both likelihood and quality. While averaging over all sampled traces may provide an unbiased estimate on its reasoning score, this is not the quantity most relevant in practice. Deployed systems typically act on one selected output, often chosen explicitly or implicitly based on confidence
(
20
;
38
;
10
)
.
Evaluating all traces equally therefore gives disproportionate influence to low-probability trajectories that are unlikely to be selected in practice.

To address this, we introduce the
Filtered Reasoning Score (FRS)
, which evaluates reasoning quality on the most confident subset of traces in terms of their faithfulness, coherence, utility, and factuality. For each problem, we sample multiple reasoning traces, estimate per-trace confidence from token-level probabilities, and retain only the top-
K\%
by confidence before computing the final score. This design focuses evaluation on the high-confidence region of the model’s reasoning trace distribution, where outputs are more representative of the model’s intended inference behavior. A high FRS therefore requires both strong reasoning and high confidence on high-quality reasoning traces (Section
3.3
).

Empirically, FRS complements accuracy by identifying distinguishing characteristics among models with similar accuracy.
We evaluate FRS across 9 open-weight models (1.5B to 14B parameters) and 6 reasoning benchmarks (Figure
2
). On MATH500
(
25
)
, two models with identical greedy accuracy (63.6%) differ by 16.5 FRS points. The highest-accuracy model overall drops from #1 to #7 under FRS, while a model ranked #8 by accuracy rises to #2. Across benchmarks, models within
0.7\%
accuracy differ by up to 18.8 FRS points. These differences reflect systematic variation in how models assign confidence over reasoning traces: some models confidently produce flawed reasoning that may lead to correct answers, while others more reliably assign high confidence to well-reasoned solutions. Such distinctions are invisible under accuracy-based evaluation, yet directly impact which reasoning is generated in practice.

Our contributions are:

1.

We identify confidence-conditioned reasoning quality as a distinct evaluation target beyond answer accuracy.
Correctness alone does not capture reasoning quality: two traces can reach the same answer while differing substantially in faithfulness, coherence, utility, and factuality. This matters especially when LLM systems prioritize high-confidence outputs, making it important to evaluate whether a model’s most confident reasoning traces are of high quality.

2.

We introduce the Filtered Reasoning Score (FRS).
FRS evaluates reasoning quality on the most confident traces, requiring both strong reasoning and high confidence on well-reasoned solutions.

3.

We show that FRS reveals structure hidden by accuracy-based evaluation.
Across 9 open-weight models and 6 reasoning benchmarks, FRS exposes ranking reversals, large separations among accuracy-similar models, and systematic differences in how confidence relates to reasoning quality, with all key results supported by non-overlapping bootstrap 95% CIs. FRS is also the only metric among six candidates that significantly predicts whether confidence-based selection improves or degrades reasoning quality (
r{=}0.49
,
p{<}0.001
; Appendix
S
).

3
Methodology

3.1
Reasoning Quality Evaluation

To capture the quality of a model’s reasoning process, we score chain-of-thought (CoT) traces along four dimensions following the taxonomy of
22
:
faithfulness
(internal consistency without hidden shortcuts),
coherence
(logical flow between steps),
utility
(whether each step contributes to the solution with correct calculations), and
factuality
(grounding in the problem context without hallucinations). We provide the full scoring rubric in Appendix
A
.

We use GPT-4o-mini as an automated evaluator following the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm
(
44
)
. The judge receives the question, the model’s full response (CoT trace and final answer), the ground-truth answer, and the scoring rubric, and produces a score from 1 (very poor) to 5 (excellent) for each dimension. We validate judge reliability in three ways: cross-model agreement with GPT-4o (82% within
\pm
1) and Claude Sonnet 4.5 (75% within
\pm
1), and a blind human annotation study with 10 annotators and 500 labeled samples, achieving 78% within
\pm
1 agreement and a Spearman correlation of
\rho=0.73
(Appendix
C
). The
Reasoning Score
is the average across the four dimensions, normalized to
[0,1]
:

\text{ReasoningScore}(r)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{d\in\{\text{faith., coh., util., fact.}\}}\frac{s_{d}(r)-1}{4}

(1)

where
s_{d}(r)\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
is the judge’s score for dimension
d
on reasoning trace
r
.

3.2
Per-Trace Confidence Estimation

The first stage of FRS is to assign each reasoning trace a scalar confidence score that can be computed without correctness labels. We use a logit-based estimator derived from token-level probabilities. Rather than averaging over the full trace, we focus on its low-probability tail, since these tokens concentrate the model’s uncertainty, whereas many high-probability tokens are generic and contribute little to distinguishing strong traces from weak ones.

Concretely, for each input
x_{i}
, the model generates a reasoning trace

r_{i}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{L_{i}})
. For each token, we extract its conditional probability

P(t_{j})=P(t_{j}\mid x_{i},t_{1},\ldots,t_{j-1})
. We then define trace-level confidence as

C(r_{i})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\text{low}}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{low}}}P(t),

(2)

where
\mathcal{T}_{\text{low}}
is the set of tokens in
r_{i}
with probabilities below the
p
th percentile.

We use
p{=}10\%
as the default high-confidence operating point, targeting the region where low-probability tokens most concentrate the model’s uncertainty. This value is chosen once via a coarse calibration sweep and is stable across nearby choices in the 5–15% range (Appendix
B
). Rankings are qualitatively consistent across
p\in\{10,20,30,40,50\}
and across alternative confidence estimators, including full-trace mean log-probability and self-consistency confidence computed purely from answer agreement without any logit access (Appendices
B
,
R
). These confidence scores define the ranking used in the next stage of FRS, where we retain only the most-confident traces for reasoning evaluation.

3.3
Filtered Reasoning Score

The second stage of FRS uses the confidence scores above to construct a filtered set of traces, and then measures reasoning quality only on that set. For each problem
x_{i}
, we sample
k{=}16
reasoning traces at temperature
T{=}0.7
using chain-of-thought prompting, compute
C(r_{i}^{(j)})
for each trace, and pool all traces within a model–benchmark pair. We then rank these traces by confidence and retain the top
K\%
as the
filtered set
.

We use
K{=}10
as the default, targeting the region where a model’s outputs would be trusted most in deployment; rankings are qualitatively consistent across
K\in\{10,20,30,40,50\}
(Appendix
F
).

To estimate FRS efficiently, we partition the top 50% of traces into five disjoint confidence bins (0–10%, 10–20%, …, 40–50%) and randomly sample 50 traces per bin for each model–benchmark pair. These sampled traces are scored by GPT-4o-mini using the rubric in Section 3.1. The Filtered Reasoning Score at threshold
K
is then

\text{FRS}_{K}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{K}|}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{S}_{K}}\text{ReasoningScore}(r),

(3)

where
\mathcal{S}_{K}
denotes all sampled traces from bins within the top
K\%
.

This construction makes the two components of FRS explicit: it rewards models that produce strong reasoning, but only when that reasoning appears in the traces that their own confidence ranks highest. A model with strong reasoning but misaligned confidence or weak reasoning overall will therefore score poorly.

4.2
Why Reasoning Quality, Not Just Accuracy

Before conditioning on confidence, we establish that reasoning quality adds information beyond accuracy. We show that the reasoning score is more stable and converges faster than accuracy.

Reasoning score produces more stable rankings.

We measure three stability metrics (average pairwise Spearman
\rho
, percentage of rank inversions, and average maximum rank swing) across four evaluation conditions (direct prompting, few-shot, CoT with
T{=}0
, CoT with
T{=}0.7
). On 5 of 6 benchmarks, reasoning score produces higher rank correlations, fewer inversions, and smaller rank swings than accuracy. Averaged across benchmarks, reasoning-based rankings achieve Spearman
\rho
of 0.72 vs. 0.49 for accuracy, with 33% fewer inversions (19.9% vs. 29.9%) and 31% smaller maximum rank swings (2.4 vs. 3.5 positions). Full results are in Table
2
(Appendix
E
).

Reasoning score converges faster.

Reasoning scores reach stable rankings with fewer evaluation problems than accuracy (Figure
3
). Across 54 model-dataset combinations, the median standard deviation of reasoning scores is lower than that of accuracy at every sample size, and the gap is largest at small
N
: at
N{=}25
, accuracy fluctuates at roughly 9pp while reasoning score is already below 4pp. However, unfiltered reasoning score is insufficient: models with similar averages can have opposite confidence-quality relationships. For example, two models may both average 85 on reasoning quality, yet one improves to 97 in its high-confidence region while the other drops to 65. Exposing this structure requires conditioning on confidence, which is what FRS does.

4.3
Why Confidence-Based Filtering Is Necessary

Having established that reasoning quality is a richer signal than accuracy, we address a key aggregation challenge: how should reasoning quality be summarized across multiple sampled traces? Averaging equally over all traces is not the quantity most relevant in practice. Deployed systems typically act on one selected output, often chosen explicitly or implicitly based on confidence
(
20
;
38
;
10
)
, giving disproportionate influence to low-probability trajectories unlikely to be surfaced in deployment. We therefore ask:
is better reasoning concentrated in the model’s high-confidence region?

Figure
4
plots average reasoning quality at each threshold
K\in\{50,40,30,20,10\}
for three representative models. For DS-R1-7B, restricting to higher-confidence traces yields markedly better reasoning: its score rises from 85.7 at
K{=}50\%
to 88.5 at
K{=}10\%
. DS-R1-1.5B shows an even steeper gain, climbing from 72.2 to 79.9. By contrast, Phi-4-Reasoning moves in the opposite direction, dropping from 81.5 to 69.7, meaning its most-confident traces contain
worse
reasoning than its average output.

Two models with the same average reasoning score can therefore differ sharply in the quality their confidence selects. Unfiltered averaging cannot distinguish a model whose confidence surfaces its best reasoning from one that surfaces its worst. We evaluate reasoning quality only in the high-confidence region, where deployed systems actually operate. The next section fixes
K{=}10\%
and reports FRS across all models and benchmarks.

FRS is most informative where accuracy is least discriminative.

Among the 34 per-benchmark model pairs with greedy accuracy gaps of at most 5 pp (out of 216 total), FRS produces a larger separation in 82% of cases (28 of 34), with a mean gap roughly 6
\times

Top-confidence accuracy is still not enough.

A natural objection is to simply measure accuracy on the most-confident traces. This does not suffice. Phi-4-Reasoning achieves the highest top-10% accuracy (79.3%), slightly above DS-R1-7B (77.1%), yet their FRS scores differ sharply: 69.7 vs. 88.5. The reason is that Phi-4-Reasoning’s confident traces often contain correct answers wrapped in degenerate reasoning: short solution cores followed by repetitive continuations that inflate token-level confidence while degrading reasoning quality (Appendix
K
).

FRS reshuffles accuracy-based rankings.

The per-benchmark effects above compound into substantial ranking shifts at the model level. Qwen2.5-7B, the highest-accuracy model overall at 73.5%, drops from #1 to #7 under FRS, indicating that its confidence does not reliably prioritize its best reasoning. DS-R1-1.5B moves in the opposite direction, rising from #8 by accuracy to #2 under FRS despite having the second-lowest accuracy (42.9%), because it consistently aligns high confidence with strong reasoning. All key ranking reversals involve FRS gaps exceeding 8 points with non-overlapping bootstrap 95% confidence intervals.

These conclusions are robust across filtering thresholds, confidence estimators, judge models, and sampling budgets. Rankings are nearly unchanged under full-trace mean log-probability (
\rho\geq 0.983
) and remain strongly correlated under self-consistency, a logit-free estimator (

FRS predicts whether confidence-based selection helps or hurts.

Beyond producing different rankings, FRS is the only metric that predicts a deployment-relevant outcome. For 54 model–benchmark pairs, we compare the reasoning quality of a model’s top-confidence trace against a random baseline (Appendix

(

,
p{<}0.001
); pass@1, high-confidence accuracy, and unfiltered reasoning score are all nonsignificant (
p>0.14
). This confirms that FRS captures a property that no existing metric measures: whether confidence-based selection actually surfaces better reasoning.

6
Discussion

FRS as a practical audit for confidence-based selection.

Many deployment strategies (e.g., abstention and test-time compute scaling) implicitly assume that a model’s highest-confidence outputs are also its best ones. Our results show this does not hold uniformly. FRS provides a simple pre-deployment audit: if FRS improves under tighter filtering, confidence-based selection will likely surface strong reasoning; if it degrades, the same strategy may surface weaker reasoning at precisely the moments the model appears most certain. The cost is modest: rankings at
k{=}8
traces per problem remain nearly identical to those at
k{=}16
(Appendix
P
). As shown in Section
5
, this holds across confidence estimators, suggesting confidence–quality alignment is a model property rather than an artifact of aggregation.

FRS fills a gap in the evaluation stack.

Accuracy measures what a model gets right, but not whether the reasoning behind it is sound. Correct answers can arise from flawed or degenerate traces, so outcome-based metrics alone cannot assess reasoning quality in the high-confidence region where deployed systems operate. As our selection gain analysis confirms (Section
5
), this gap is deployment-relevant: FRS is the only metric among six candidates that significantly predicts whether confidence-based selection improves or degrades reasoning quality. Moreover, our leave-one-benchmark-out analysis shows that confidence–quality alignment is a stable model property (
\rho=0.712
across held-out folds), not a benchmark-specific artifact. Together, these results establish confidence-conditioned reasoning quality as a distinct evaluation target for reasoning models in confidence-mediated settings.

What drives confidence–quality alignment?

One striking pattern is that models whose FRS improves under tighter filtering all used reinforcement learning during post-training, whereas models whose FRS degrades did not. We view this as suggestive rather than conclusive: it is consistent with prior findings that RL improves confidence calibration, but our experiments were not designed to isolate RL from architecture, scale, or training data. We treat confidence–quality alignment as an important phenomenon revealed by FRS, and its mechanisms as an open question. If this alignment can be trained directly, FRS may serve not only as an evaluation metric but as a training target for improving reasoning models.

7
Conclusion

We introduced the Filtered Reasoning Score (FRS), a metric that evaluates reasoning quality on a model’s most-confident outputs. Conditioning evaluation on confidence reveals structure that accuracy cannot: models indistinguishable under accuracy, and even under top-confidence accuracy, can differ sharply in the reasoning their confidence selects. FRS is most discriminative exactly where accuracy is least informative.

Evaluation should move beyond correctness when models are deployed in confidence-mediated settings. Accuracy measures whether a model can produce the right answer, but not whether its most-confident outputs reflect sound reasoning. FRS targets this question directly, and our leave-one-benchmark-out analysis confirms that the reasoning property it measures, confidence–quality alignment, generalizes across benchmarks.

An important direction for future work is understanding and improving confidence–quality alignment. Our results show this alignment varies systematically across models and training regimes, raising the possibility of treating confidence-conditioned reasoning quality not only as an evaluation target but as a training objective, encouraging models to assign high confidence selectively to well-reasoned outputs.

Ethics Statement

This work introduces the
Filtered Reasoning Score (FRS)
, an evaluation metric designed to assess
confidence-conditioned reasoning quality
. We view FRS as an auditing tool for modern deployment settings where systems act on a model’s most-confident outputs. In such settings, correctness alone is insufficient: models can produce correct answers through flawed or degenerate reasoning, and confidence can preferentially surface these failures. By explicitly measuring reasoning quality under confidence-based selection, FRS aims to reduce misplaced trust in high-confidence outputs and improve the reliability of model evaluation.

The primary positive impact of FRS is to expose failure modes that are invisible to accuracy and even to top-confidence accuracy. By identifying whether a model’s confidence aligns with strong reasoning, FRS enables practitioners to make more informed decisions about model selection, filtering strategies, and deployment policies. We therefore position FRS as a practical pre-deployment diagnostic: when FRS is high, confidence-based selection is more likely to surface sound reasoning; when FRS is low, such strategies may amplify poor reasoning despite high apparent confidence.

At the same time, FRS is not a complete measure of reliability or safety. A model with high FRS may still be unsafe, biased, or poorly calibrated in real-world settings. In addition, because FRS relies on LLM-based judges, it may inherit biases from the judge model and favor reasoning styles that are more legible to the evaluator. Like any metric, FRS may also be subject to optimization or gaming. We therefore emphasize that FRS should be used as
one component
of a broader evaluation stack, alongside accuracy, calibration, robustness testing, and human oversight, rather than as a standalone deployment criterion.

We take steps to mitigate these risks. We validate FRS across judge models and human annotations, report robustness across filtering thresholds and sampling budgets, and open-source the FRS evaluation pipeline to promote transparency and reproducibility. We also encourage users to evaluate FRS across diverse benchmarks and domains, as confidence–quality alignment may vary across settings.

FRS also raises fairness considerations. Benchmarks, prompts, and judge models may encode narrow distributions of tasks or linguistic norms, which can affect FRS scores across models or domains. As a result, FRS-based rankings should not be used in isolation for socially consequential decisions (e.g., in healthcare, education, or legal contexts). Instead, FRS should serve as a diagnostic signal within a broader, human-centered evaluation process.

Our experiments primarily use public benchmarks and model-generated outputs, and do not require collection of personal user data. For the human annotation used to validate FRS, annotators should be treated according to standard ethical practices, including informed consent and fair compensation where applicable. We also respect dataset and model licenses and aim to ensure reproducibility through release of the FRS codebase.

Finally, we note that FRS requires multi-sample generation and evaluation, which incurs additional computational cost compared to accuracy. We therefore report efficiency trade-offs and show that FRS rankings remain stable under reduced sampling. We view this cost as justified by the improved visibility FRS provides into how confidence interacts with reasoning quality. Overall, FRS contributes toward more responsible evaluation by shifting attention from whether models are correct to whether they are reliable
when they appear most confident
.

Appendix B
Low-Probability Token Analysis

What are low-probability tokens?

The most frequent low-probability tokens (Figure
7
, left) fall into two categories:

1.

Decision-point tokens
: Words that are largely interchangeable without affecting reasoning logic, such as “Okay,” “Alright,” and “Just.” Low probability at these tokens reflects a choice among multiple equally valid continuations, indicating a natural branch point in the generation process. Low probability at these tokens is frequent and normal across models.

2.

Uncertainty-expressing tokens
: Words that explicitly signal confusion or doubt, such as “messed,” “confuse,” “misunderstood,” and “Sometimes.” Low probability at these tokens reflects genuine uncertainty in the reasoning process.

Both categories represent points where the model’s reasoning is under stress, either because multiple paths are available or because the model is uncertain about how to proceed.

Figure 7:
Left: Frequent low-probability tokens, categorized as uncertainty-expressing (red) or decision-point (blue). Right: Confidence signal quality (SNR and mean confidence gap) as a function of percentile cutoff. The 10% cutoff yields the strongest signal on both measures.

Analyzing the percentile cutoff.

We evaluate percentile cutoffs from 5% to 40% using two separation metrics across all 9 models and 6 benchmarks. The first is the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), defined as:

\text{SNR}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[C\mid Y{=}1]-\mathbb{E}[C\mid Y{=}0]}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(\text{Var}[C\mid Y{=}1]+\text{Var}[C\mid Y{=}0]\right)}}

(4)

with
Y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
denoting correctness. We also compute the average absolute difference in mean confidence between correct and incorrect populations (Figure
7
, right).

The lowest 5–15% of tokens yield the strongest separation, with the 10% cutoff achieving the highest values on both measures (Avg
|
SNR
|
= 1.498, Avg
|\Delta|
= 0.081). Beyond 15%, both metrics decline monotonically as high-probability tokens dilute the signal. The 5–15% range is consistently strong; we use 10% as the default throughout the paper because it provides the best overall signal while including enough tokens per trace for a stable estimate.

C.2
Human Annotation Study

To validate the GPT-4o-mini judge against human judgment, we conducted a human annotation study with 10 annotators. We collected 500 total annotations: each annotator independently labeled 50 samples, with no overlap between annotators (i.e., each sample was labeled by exactly one annotator).

Annotation protocol.

The study was conducted blind: annotators did not see GPT-4o-mini’s scores. Each sample presented to an annotator consisted of: (1) the original question, (2) the model’s full reasoning trace and output, and (3) the ground-truth answer. Annotators evaluated each response across the same four reasoning dimensions used throughout this work (faithfulness, utility, coherence, and factuality), each scored on the 1–5 integer scale defined in the rubric (Appendix
A
). The 500 samples were drawn via stratified sampling across all 9 models and 6 benchmarks to ensure broad coverage.

Agreement metrics.

We compute agreement between the GPT-4o-mini judge and human annotators at the level of individual dimension scores. Each of the 500 samples yields 4 dimension-level comparisons, producing 2,000 human–judge score pairs in total. Following the same convention used for cross-model validation, we define “within
\pm
1” as an absolute difference
\leq 1
on the 1–5 scale.

Results.

GPT-4o-mini agrees with human annotators within
\pm
1 for 78% of dimension-level scores, with a Spearman rank correlation of
\rho=0.73
across all 2,000 comparisons. The human–judge agreement (78%) is slightly below the GPT-4o agreement (82%) but above the cross-family Claude Sonnet 4.5 agreement (75%), and the correlation of
\rho=0.73
indicates that the automated judge preserves the rank ordering of reasoning quality that human annotators assign.

Discussion.

The strong correlation (
\rho=0.73
) confirms that GPT-4o-mini’s scores track the relative quality judgments of human evaluators, supporting its use as the scoring function inside FRS. Taken together, the cross-model and human validation provide converging evidence that the automated judge is a reliable proxy for reasoning quality assessment.

Appendix O
Reliability Metrics Comparison

AUROC measures strict rank ordering (
\mathbb{E}[I(C_{1}>C_{0})]
) but is invariant to the absolute margin of separation. Because FRS uses a percentile-based confidence threshold, it requires a diagnostic sensitive to both the magnitude and variance of the separation between correct and incorrect confidence distributions. SNR (
=(\mathbb{E}[C_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[C_{0}])/\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(\text{Var}[C_{1}]+\text{Var}[C_{0}])}
) measures this normalized distance and penalizes high-variance tails of false confidence in the top-
K
set. For this reason we report SNR rather than AUROC in Appendix
N
(Table
8
); two model–benchmark pairs with identical AUROC can have substantially different SNR when one has tighter within-class variance, and it is this tightness that determines whether the top-
K
confidence cutoff cleanly isolates high-quality traces. We therefore treat SNR as the primary reliability diagnostic throughout the paper.

R.1
Self-Consistency Confidence: A Non-Logit Baseline

The three estimators above are all derived from the same token-level probability arrays. A natural concern is whether the ranking structure of FRS is an artifact of this family of estimators. To test this, we recomputed FRS using a fundamentally different confidence signal—
self-consistency
—that uses no logits or token probabilities whatsoever.

Definition.

For each problem
x_{i}
, we have
k{=}16
sampled traces. For each trace
r_{i}^{(j)}
, we define its self-consistency confidence as the fraction of traces whose final answer matches that of
r_{i}^{(j)}
:

C_{\text{sc}}(r_{i}^{(j)})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\texttt{norm}(\hat{y}_{i}^{(\ell)})=\texttt{norm}(\hat{y}_{i}^{(j)})\right],

(5)

where
\hat{y}_{i}^{(\ell)}
is the final answer of the
\ell
-th trace and
\texttt{norm}(\cdot)
applies whitespace stripping. This confidence is based purely on answer agreement across samples.

Procedure.

We re-ranked all traces within each model–benchmark pair by
C_{\text{sc}}
, selected the top-
K
set at
K{=}10\%
, and computed FRS by averaging the
existing
reasoning quality scores over the re-selected traces. No new judge calls were required. Ties (which are common because
C_{\text{sc}}
takes discrete values in
\{1/16,2/16,\ldots,1\}
) were broken randomly, with results averaged over multiple tie-breaking draws.

Results.

\rho=0.80
(
p\approx 0.01
); the Pearson correlation on mean FRS values is

.

\rho=0.80

to
k{=}8

\rho=0.93
and Pearson
r=0.95
at the model level, indicating that
k{=}8
is sufficient for qualitative ranking comparisons in this setting.

Takeaway.

The strong rank correlation (
\rho=0.80
) between a logit-free confidence signal and the default logit-based estimator provides evidence that the FRS ranking structure reflects a genuine model property—confidence–quality alignment—rather than an artifact of a specific confidence computation.

Appendix S
Selection Gain: Does Confidence-Based Selection Surface Better Reasoning?

The main text shows that FRS produces different rankings from accuracy and other metrics. A natural objection is that
any
new metric will produce a different ranking. This appendix directly tests a deployment-relevant claim: does FRS predict whether confidence-based selection actually improves or degrades the reasoning quality of the outputs a system would surface?

S.1
Experimental Design

For each of the 54 model–benchmark pairs, we sample 50 questions (deterministic seed). For each sampled question, we identify two traces from the
k{=}16
sampled reasoning traces:

1.

Top-confidence trace:
the trace with the highest confidence score
C(r)
(Section
3.2
). Ties are broken by smallest trace index.

2.

Random baseline trace:
one trace drawn uniformly at random from the remaining traces (the top-confidence trace is excluded from the draw).

Both traces are scored by the same GPT-4o-mini rubric-based judge used throughout (Appendix
A
), producing 5,400 fresh judge calls (54 pairs
\times
50 questions
\times
2 traces). We define
selection gain
per question as:

\text{SelectionGain}(x_{i})=\text{ReasoningScore}(r_{\text{top\text{-}conf}})-\text{ReasoningScore}(r_{\text{random}}),

(6)

and aggregate to the model–benchmark level by averaging over the 50 sampled questions per pair.

S.4
Circularity Assessment

This experiment has lower circularity than summary-level analyses because the outcome is a
policy contrast
: reasoning quality under confidence-based selection versus a random baseline, scored with fresh judge calls on traces not previously evaluated. Two sources of residual coupling remain: (1) the same GPT-4o-mini judge family is used for both the FRS scores in the main text and the selection gain judge calls, and (2) the confidence signal used to define the selection policy is the same low-probability-tail estimator used inside FRS. However, the key comparison—FRS versus five alternative predictors of selection gain—controls for factor (2), since all metrics are correlated with selection gain under the same confidence-based policy. The fact that FRS significantly predicts selection gain while the others do not therefore reflects a genuine difference in what FRS measures, not a mechanical dependency on the confidence definition.
