From silent infection to symptomatic malaria: host phenomic determinants of Plasmodium falciparum outcomes in Fanteakwa South District, Ghana
Authors: Enoch Aninagyei, George Abeiku Abbew, Comfort Addo Boatey, Regina Yayra Menu, Ebenezer Tawiah Nyarkotey, Desmond Omane Acheampong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsymptomatic Plasmodium falciparum infections sustain malaria transmission in endemic areas, yet progression to symptomatic disease varies among individuals. This study examined asymptomatic infection in Fanteakwa South District, Ghana, focusing on how demographic and host phenomic factors influence progression to symptomatic malaria.MethodsThis study employed a prospective cohort design nested within a cross-sectional survey. Participants of all ages were recruited from four communities using stratified random sampling and screened for malaria using mRDT and PCR. ABO blood group, Rhesus D factor, and haemoglobin phenotype were determined. Individuals with asymptomatic P. falciparum infection were followed weekly for 28 days with repeat mRDT, microscopy, and temperature measurements. Statistical analyses evaluated associations between demographic, phenomic, and infection outcomes.ResultsAmong 353 participants, females constituted 59.8%, and adults aged 18–65 years were the largest age group (42.5%). Blood group O (55.8%), HbAA (85.3%), and Rhesus D positivity (91.8%) predominated. PCR detected more P. falciparum infections (52.1%) than mRDT (42.8%), indicating a substantial burden of submicroscopic asymptomatic malaria. PCR positivity varied across communities and was highest among children (61.9%) and adolescents (68.6%). During follow-up, 131 participants (37.1%) converted to mRDT and microscopy positivity, with 43.5% converting in the first week. Early conversion was most frequent among children (54.5%, p < 0.001), followed by adolescents, while adults and older adults showed fewer early conversions. Individuals with blood groups A and AB progressed earlier, whereas blood group O showed delayed conversion. All conversions occurred among HbAA individuals, with no progression among HbAS carriers. The Rhesus D factor had no significant effect, while gender differences emerged later, with higher conversion among males in Week 3 (p = 0.015).ConclusionProgression from asymptomatic to symptomatic P. falciparum infection is strongly shaped by age and host erythrocyte phenomic traits. Blood groups A and AB and the HbAA phenotype favour early conversion, whereas blood group O and HbAS delay or prevent progression. These findings underscore the importance of molecular surveillance and phenome-informed risk stratification for targeted malaria control in endemic communities.Keywords: Asymptomatic malaria, Plasmodium falciparum, Host phenomes, ABO blood group, Haemoglobin variants, Molecular epidemiology, Ghana

Background

Malaria remains a major public health challenge in sub-Saharan Africa, with Plasmodium falciparum accounting for most malaria-related morbidity and mortality [1–3]. Although substantial gains have been made through intensified control and elimination efforts, transmission persists in Ghana, particularly in rural settings and areas experiencing ecological disruption [4]. A major obstacle to sustained malaria control is the presence of asymptomatic P. falciparum infections, in which infected individuals do not exhibit clinical symptoms but continue to sustain parasite transmission within communities. These silent infections compromise the effectiveness of case-based surveillance and intervention strategies that largely focus on symptomatic malaria [5–7].

In Ghana, asymptomatic P. falciparum infection constitutes a significant and often under-recognized component of the malaria burden, especially in endemic rural and peri-urban settings. Population studies using sensitive molecular diagnostics have documented high cumulative prevalence of asymptomatic carriage, with some surveys reporting as many as 73% of ostensibly healthy adults harbouring parasite infections detectable by PCR [8] and around 43% in mixed community settings without clinical symptoms [6]. These subclinical reservoirs frequently go unnoticed by routine surveillance and standard rapid tests, yet they sustain local transmission, undermine control efforts, and shape seasonal patterns of clinical disease [9]. The likelihood of asymptomatic infection is shaped by age, transmission intensity, and immunity, highlighting the need for targeted surveillance that extends beyond symptomatic cases [10].

The transition from asymptomatic carriage to clinical malaria is a complex process shaped by interactions among host, parasite, and environmental factors. Increasing evidence suggests that host phenomic characteristics, including haemoglobin variants, ABO blood groups, and the Rhesus D factor, play important roles in influencing parasite survival, immune responses, and clinical outcomes. For example, the sickle cell trait (haemoglobin AS) is known to confer partial protection against severe malaria, while certain ABO blood groups, particularly group O, have been associated with altered disease severity and parasite dynamics [8–10]. However, the extent to which these phenomic traits influence the conversion from asymptomatic infection to symptomatic malaria remains incompletely understood.

In addition to host biology, demographic and environmental factors may further modify malaria outcomes. Age, residence, and local ecological conditions can interact with host phenomes to shape infection trajectories [11, 12]. The Fanteakwa South District of Ghana provides a distinct setting for investigating these relationships. The district has experienced extensive environmental degradation due to illegal small-scale mining activities, locally referred to as galamsey, which has resulted in the creation of stagnant water bodies that support Anopheles mosquito breeding and potentially intensify malaria transmission [6, 13]. Despite ongoing malaria transmission in the district, limited data exists on how host phenomic factors contribute to disease progression among individuals with initially silent infections.

Against this backdrop, the present study sought to examine the role of host phenomic and sociodemographic factors in shaping P. falciparum infection outcomes, with particular emphasis on the transition from asymptomatic to symptomatic malaria in selected communities of the Fanteakwa South District. By focusing on host biological traits, this study aims to clarify how specific phenomes influence infection progression and clinical manifestation. Insights gained from this work are expected to support more refined risk stratification and targeted malaria surveillance strategies in endemic settings in Ghana.

Materials and methods

Study design, period and sites

This study employed a prospective cohort study nested within a cross-sectional survey in the Fanteakwa South District in the Eastern Region of Ghana. A stratified random sampling approach was used to ensure district-wide representation, with participants of all ages recruited from Osino, Dwenase, Nsutam, and Bososu (Fig. 1). Study participants were selected concurrently from the four study sites from July to October 2023. Fanteakwa South District, located in the Eastern Region of Ghana, lies between 0°10′ East and 6°15′–6°40′ North and is bordered by the Volta Lake, Kwahu South, East Akim, Yilo Krobo, and Manya Krobo Districts. The district is predominantly rural, with an estimated population of about 59,000, largely engaged in farming, small-scale trading, and artisanal mining [14]. Widespread illegal small-scale mining (galamsey) has caused significant environmental degradation, including vegetation loss, water pollution, and the formation of stagnant water bodies, creating favourable breeding habitats for Anopheles mosquitoes and increasing malaria transmission risk [15].

Study population

This study involved two cohorts of participants. The first cohort comprised community-dwelling individuals who were asymptomatic for malaria at the time of recruitment. The second cohort comprised community members who tested positive for P. falciparum using a malaria rapid diagnostic test (mRDT) at baseline. Only these mRDT-positive individuals were enrolled in a 28-day (4-week) longitudinal follow-up to monitor parasite persistence and determine progression from asymptomatic infection to symptomatic malaria.

Sample size determination

The Cochran formula was used to determine the sample size for this study as follows; n = Z2p(1-p)e2; where Z is the standard normal variate at a confidence interval of 95% = 1.96, p is the estimated prevalence of malaria in the Eastern region = 31% [16], and e is the margin of error = 0.05. Therefore, the minimum sample size was determined to be 329.

Participant recruitment

The method for selecting study participants has been previously described [6]. Within each selected community, one hundred households were chosen using a systematic sampling technique. The total number of households in each community was divided by one hundred to determine the sampling interval. Starting from the household of the local assembly member, every nth household was selected until the required number was reached. In each selected household, one individual was randomly chosen to participate using a lottery method. Slips marked “YES” and “NO” were placed in a container, and each household member drew one slip. The individual who selected “YES” was enrolled in the study.

Participant selection criteria

Eligible participants were permanent residents who had lived in the study communities for at least 6 months and provided written informed consent, with parental consent and child assent obtained for minors. Only asymptomatic individuals were enrolled, and those who tested positive for P. falciparum by mRDT at baseline were included in the follow-up cohort and monitored for malaria disease progression. Individuals were excluded if they presented with symptoms suggestive of malaria, including fever, chills, or headache, at the time of recruitment, had tested positive for malaria or completed antimalarial treatment within the preceding month, or were currently undergoing malaria treatment. Participants who were unable to complete the study questionnaire were also excluded. In addition, visitors and non-residents of the selected study communities were not eligible for enrolment.

Administration of questionnaires

A structured, pre-tested questionnaire was administered through face-to-face interviews by trained field assistants to collect socio-demographic and health-related information from participants after obtaining informed consent. The questionnaire, developed in English and translated into the local dialect, captured data on age, sex, education, occupation, religion, body temperature, and malaria-related history. Interviews were conducted in participants’ homes, and completed questionnaires were reviewed daily for accuracy and completeness before data entry and analysis.

Collection of blood samples, follow-up, and temperature measurement

For each consenting participant, up to 500 µL of whole blood was collected aseptically into Citrate-Phosphate-Dextrose-Adenine 1 (CPDA-1) anticoagulant tubes were stored at 4 °C until analysis. A portion of each sample was used immediately for on-site malaria diagnosis using mRDTs. Participants who tested positive for P. falciparum were enrolled in a 28-day follow-up, during which residential location details were recorded to facilitate participant tracing. Follow-up blood sampling was conducted on days 7, 14, 21, and 28 using aseptic finger-prick procedures, with capillary blood collected into pediatric EDTA tubes. Malaria diagnosis during follow-up was performed using both rapid diagnostic tests (mRDTs) and microscopy. Body temperature was measured at baseline and at each follow-up visit using a calibrated digital thermometer and classified as subnormal (<37 °C), normal (37.0–37.3 °C), or elevated (>37.3 °C). All blood samples were uniquely labelled, transported under strict cold chain conditions, and stored at 4 °C before analysis. Aliquots designated for molecular assays were preserved at −20 °C, and dried blood spots were prepared for all participants to ensure sample integrity and data quality. Dried blood spots were prepared with 50 µL of whole blood on Whatman® 3 mm filter paper. After spotting, samples were air-dried for 3–4 h at ambient room temperature in a dust-free environment away from direct sunlight and heat. Once completely dried, each filter paper was individually packed in zip-lock plastic bags containing silica gel desiccants and humidity indicator cards to prevent moisture absorption. The packaged samples were stored at 4 °C for about 30 days until DNA extraction. These procedures were followed to preserve sample integrity and minimize degradation.

Laboratory procedures for detecting P. falciparum

Diagnostic strategy at baseline and during follow-up

At baseline, mRDTs were used for rapid field screening of asymptomatic participants, while PCR was performed on collected samples to provide sensitive molecular confirmation. During follow-up, both mRDT and microscopy were employed to monitor progression to patent infection.

Malaria rapid diagnostic test (mRDT)

Part of the venous blood sample from each participant was used to screen for malaria using the First Response mRDT (India) at the nearest health centre in each study site. First Response mRDT kit was used because it is routinely supplied to public health facilities in Ghana through the national malaria control programme, ensuring consistency with routine clinical practice. The mRDT procedure was performed as previously described by Aninagyei et al. [17]. Using the pipette with the fixed 5 µL volume, the venous blood was applied to the test cassette, followed by the addition of three drops of assay buffer. After incubation at room temperature (20 min), results were interpreted according to the manufacturer’s instructions: the presence of both control and test lines indicated a positive result, while a control line alone indicated a negative result. Tests without a visible control line were considered invalid and repeated.

Malaria microscopy

Malaria microscopy was performed using Giemsa-stained thick and thin smears examined under oil immersion. Parasite density was estimated by counting the number of asexual parasites per 200 white blood cells on thick smears and expressed as parasites per microlitre of blood, assuming a standard WBC count of 8000 cells/µL [18]. All slides were read independently by trained microscopists, with positive and selected negative slides re-examined for quality control, and discrepancies resolved by a third reader.

P. falciparum nucleic acid extraction using the Chelex method

Haemolysates were prepared from dried blood spots and whole blood using saponin lysis. Two 6 mm discs from each dried blood spot or aliquots of whole blood were incubated overnight at 4 °C in 0.5% saponin in phosphate-buffered saline. After incubation, samples were centrifuged, and pellets were repeatedly washed with PBS until haem pigments were completely removed. DNA extraction was performed using the Chelex method, by adding 20% Chelex suspension and nuclease-free water, followed by heating at 96 °C for 10 min [19]. After centrifugation, the supernatant containing extracted nucleic acids was transferred into clean tubes and stored at −20 °C until PCR analysis.

Nested PCR for detection of P. falciparum

P. falciparum infection was confirmed using nested polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting the small subunit ribosomal RNA (ssrRNA) gene. This protocol was previously published [15]. An initial amplification was performed with genus-specific primers, followed by a second amplification using P. falciparum-specific primers. PCR reactions were prepared using a commercial master mix [15] and template DNA, and amplification was carried out under standardized thermal cycling conditions. PCR products were resolved on 2% agarose gels prepared in Tris-Acetate-EDTA buffer and visualized under UV illumination after ethidium bromide staining. The presence of a 205 bp amplicon was taken as confirmation of P. falciparum infection. To ensure amplification accuracy and to monitor for contamination, known P. falciparum-positive DNA and nuclease-free water were included as positive and negative controls, respectively, in each PCR run. PCR runs were considered valid only when the positive control produced the expected amplicon and the negative control showed no amplification.

Determination of blood group and Rh factor

Blood grouping and Rh factor determination were performed using the slide agglutination method, as published by Musa et al. [20]. On a clean glass slide, separate drops of anti-A, anti-B, and anti-D antisera were placed. Using a sterile applicator stick, a drop of the participant’s whole blood was added to each antiserum and gently mixed to ensure uniform distribution. The slide was then gently rocked to facilitate agglutination, and the reaction was observed visually within 2 min. The presence of agglutination indicated the corresponding antigen, allowing classification of blood groups as A, B, AB, or O. Agglutination with anti-D serum denoted Rh positivity, while its absence indicated Rh negativity.

Sickle cell screening and haemoglobin phenotyping

Sickle cell screening was performed using the sodium metabisulfite test [20], in which a blood sample was mixed with 2% sodium metabisulfite, sealed under a coverslip, and examined microscopically after 1 h for the presence of sickled erythrocytes, indicative of HbAS or HbSS. Confirmation of haemoglobin phenotype, in all samples, was achieved by cellulose acetate electrophoresis under alkaline conditions (pH 8.4–8.6). Haemolysates were prepared from saline-washed red cells lysed with 0.5% Triton X-100 in potassium cyanide solution. Electrophoresis was conducted at 350 V and 50 mA for 25 min, with haemoglobins A, S, C, and F serving as positive controls to verify accurate phenotype identification.

Data analysis

All data obtained from questionnaires and laboratory results were entered into Microsoft Excel for initial cleaning and validation, then exported to IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were generated to summarize the socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of participants, with categorical variables presented as frequencies and percentages. Associations between categorical variables and asymptomatic P. falciparum infection were examined using the Chi-square test. To identify factors independently associated with asymptomatic malaria, binary logistic regression analysis was conducted. Variables that showed statistical significance (p < 0.05) in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable model. Adjusted Odds Ratios (AORs) with corresponding 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) were computed to quantify the strength and direction of associations. Statistical significance was determined at a threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics and diagnostic outcomes of participants

Overall, a total of 353 participants were enrolled across the four study sites in the first cohort, with higher mRDT and PCR positivity in Nsutam and Osino than in Bososu and Dwenase. Females comprised the majority (59.8%), although males showed higher proportions of both mRDT and PCR positivity. Adults formed the largest age group (42.5%), but children and adolescents recorded the highest infection rates. Most participants had junior or senior high education, with infection positivity highest among those with primary education and lowest among those with tertiary education. Elevated body temperature (>37.3 °C) was associated with the highest diagnostic positivity, while the lowest positivity occurred among participants with temperatures below 37 °C. Across all socio-demographic and clinical categories, no statistically significant differences were observed between mRDT and PCR detection rates (p > 0.05), indicating comparable diagnostic performance across participant subgroups (Table 1).

Adjusted predictors of PCR positivity

Overall, study site, age group, and body temperature remained significant predictors of PCR positivity after adjustment. Compared with Bososu (reference), participants from Nsutam had increased odds of PCR positivity (AOR = 2.64, 95% CI: 1.4–4.8, p = 0.0018), while those from Osino similarly showed higher odds (AOR = 2.65, 95% CI: 1.4–4.9, p = 0.0020). In contrast, the association observed for Dwenase was not statistically significant (AOR = 1.7, 95% CI: 0.9–3.1, p = 0.070). With respect to age, using older adults as the reference group, children demonstrated significantly increased odds of PCR positivity (AOR = 2.9, 95% CI: 1.2–6.9, p = 0.015). Likewise, adolescents had the highest adjusted odds (AOR = 3.9, 95% CI: 1.5–9.8, p = 0.0037), whereas adults showed no significant association (AOR = 1.2, 95% CI: 0.53–2.8, p = 0.624). Finally, body temperature was strongly associated with PCR positivity. Compared with participants with temperatures <37 °C (reference), those with temperatures between 37 and 37.3 °C had significantly higher odds of PCR positivity (AOR = 2.5, 95% CI: 1.6–3.9, p < 0.0001). Participants with temperatures >37.3 °C also showed increased odds, although this association did not reach statistical significance (AOR = 4.8, 95% CI: 0.9–24, p = 0.06) (Table 2).

Distribution of mRDT and PCR positivity by host phenomes

Blood group O was the most prevalent phenotype (197; 55.8%), followed by A (74; 21.0%), B (70; 19.8%), and AB (12; 3.4%). mRDT positivity was highest among individuals with blood group AB (7; 58.3%), followed by A (35; 47.3%), O (83; 42.1%), and B (26; 37.1%). A similar pattern was observed for PCR positivity, which was most frequent in blood group AB (9; 75.0%), followed by A (40; 54.1%), O (102; 51.8%), and B (33; 47.1%). Most participants were Rhesus D positive (324; 91.8%), with Rh-negative individuals accounting for 29 (8.2%). mRDT positivity was comparable between Rh-negative (13; 44.8%) and Rh-positive (138; 42.6%) participants, while PCR positivity was higher among Rh-positive individuals (171; 52.8%) than Rh-negative individuals (13; 44.8%).

Regarding haemoglobin phenotype, HbAA predominated (301; 85.3%), with HbAS accounting for 52 (14.7%). mRDT positivity was observed in 131 (43.5%) individuals with HbAA and 20 (38.5%) with HbAS, while PCR positivity was slightly higher among those with HbAA (159; 52.8%) compared with HbAS (25; 48.1%) (Table 3).

mRDT and microscopic positivity during follow-up by host phenomes

A total of 151 mRDT-positive, asymptomatic participants were enrolled into the 28-day follow-up cohort, including 26 from Bososu, 34 from Dwenase, 43 from Nsutam, and 48 from Osino. This group constituted the second study cohort. These individuals were monitored weekly to assess parasite persistence and progression to symptomatic malaria. Overall, 131 of the 151 participants (86.8%) developed mRDT or microscopic positivity at some point during the 4-week follow-up period. Most cases occurred in Week 1 (57; 16.1%), followed by Week 3 (36; 10.2%) and Week 2 (35; 9.9%), with few cases in Week 4 (3; 0.8%). Follow-up positivity varied by ABO blood group, with the highest cumulative proportion among blood group A (42; 56.7%), followed by B (28; 40.0%), AB (4; 33.3%), and O (57; 29.0%). In Week 1, positivity was observed across all blood groups, highest in A (27/74; 36.5%), followed by AB (4/12; 33.3%), B (14/70; 20.0%), and O (13/197; 6.6%) (p < 0.00001). In Week 2, cases persisted in A (12/74; 16.2%), B (12/70; 17.1%), and O (11/197; 5.6%), with none in AB (p = 0.011). By Week 3, positivity was mainly associated with blood group O (31/197; 15.7%), with fewer cases in A (3/74; 4.0%) and B (2/70; 2.9%) (p = 0.004). In Week 4, positivity was rare and occurred only in blood group O (3/197; 1.5%). Overall, blood group A was associated with early positivity, while blood group O predominated later. Nearly all cases occurred among Rh-positive participants (130; 40.1%), with only one Rh-negative case (1/29; 3.4%) in Week 3. All follow-up positivity was confined to individuals with HbAA (131; 43.5%), with no cases among HbAS throughout follow-up (Table 4).

MRDT and microscopic positivity rates during follow-up

Follow-up mRDT and microscopy positivity by gender and developmental stage

Follow-up mRDT and microscopy positivity showed distinct patterns by gender (Fig. 2A) and developmental stage (Fig. 2B). Positivity did not differ significantly between males and females during Week 1 (p = 0.205) and Week 2 (p = 0.107), but a significant difference emerged in Week 3, with higher positivity among males (p = 0.015). By Week 4, positivity was low in both sexes, and no difference was observed (p = 0.808). In contrast, the developmental stage strongly influenced early follow-up positivity. Significant differences were observed in Week 1 and Week 2, with children recording the highest positivity, followed by adolescents, while adults and older adults showed lower rates (p < 0.001 for both weeks). These differences diminished over time, with no significant variation across age groups in Week 3 (p = 0.096) or Week 4 (p = 0.441), coinciding with an overall decline in follow-up positivity.

Mean parasite density (/µL) during follow-up by host phenomes

Mean parasite densities varied across follow-up weeks and host phenomes, with the highest values generally observed in the early weeks. By ABO blood group, the highest mean parasite density in Week 1 occurred in blood group AB (20,024/µL), followed by A (17,375/µL), B (12,391/µL), and O (11,362/µL). In Week 2, densities peaked in blood group B (18,351/µL), followed by O (17,535/µL) and A (10,513/µL), with no detectable values for AB. During Week 3, higher densities were observed in A (15,881/µL) and B (14,599/µL) compared with O (10,336/µL). By Week 4, parasite density was recorded only in blood group O (13,093/µL). Mean parasite densities were detected mainly among Rh-positive participants, measuring 15,288/µL (Week 1), 15,466/µL (Week 2), 13,605/µL (Week 3), and 13,093/µL (Week 4), while Rh-negative participants showed detectable density only in Week 3 (9001/µL). Parasite densities were observed exclusively among HbAA individuals, with mean values of 15,288/µL (Week 1), 15,466/µL (Week 2), 11,303/µL (Week 3), and 13,093/µL (Week 4), and no detectable parasitaemia among HbAS participants (Table 5).

Changes in mean body temperature following transition from asymptomatic to symptomatic malaria

Overall, statistically significant increases in mean body temperature were observed at specific time points and were strongly associated with host phenomic characteristics. Among blood group A, a marked rise occurred in Week 1, with temperature increasing from 37.1 to 38.46 °C (↑1.36 °C; p = 0.018). Similarly, blood group B showed a significant Week 1 increase from 36.8 to 37.8 °C (↑1.0 °C; p = 0.011), while blood group AB also recorded a significant rise in Week 1, increasing from 37.0 to 37.9 °C (↑0.9 °C; p = 0.042). In contrast, blood group O demonstrated a significant temperature increase only in Week 3, from 36.9 to 37.8 °C (↑0.9 °C; p = 0.019). With respect to the Rhesus D factor, a significant temperature increase was observed among Rh-positive participants in Week 2, rising from 36.8 to 37.9 °C (↑1.1 °C; p = 0.012). Regarding haemoglobin phenotype, individuals with HbAA exhibited significant temperature elevations in Week 1 (37.1–37.8 °C; ↑0.9 °C; p = 0.023) and Week 3 (36.9–37.8 °C; ↑0.9 °C; p = 0.015). All other observed temperature changes across blood groups, Rhesus D status, and haemoglobin phenotype were not statistically significant, and no temperature elevations or symptomatic conversion were observed among individuals with HbAS throughout the 4-week follow-up period (Table 6).

Median time to symptomatic conversion according to host phenomic traits

Analysis of time to symptomatic conversion revealed marked differences in progression rates across host phenomic groups (Table 7). Among ABO blood groups, individuals with blood groups A, B, and AB exhibited the earliest conversion, with a median time of 1 week following enrolment. In contrast, participants with blood group O demonstrated delayed progression, with a median time to conversion of 3 weeks. With respect to the Rhesus D factor, Rh-positive individuals showed an intermediate median time to conversion of 2 weeks, whereas only one conversion occurred among Rh-negative participants, precluding reliable estimation of median conversion time in this group. Similarly, haemoglobin phenotype strongly influenced progression dynamics. Participants with the HbAA phenotype had a median time to symptomatic conversion of 2 weeks. In contrast, no symptomatic conversion was observed among HbAS carriers throughout the 4-week follow-up period, indicating sustained asymptomatic carriage.

Discussion

The molecular epidemiological findings of this study reveal a substantial reservoir of asymptomatic P. falciparum infection within the study communities, particularly among males, children, and adolescents, which would have been markedly underestimated if diagnosis had relied solely on mRDT or microscopy. PCR consistently identified higher levels of infection, confirming the widespread presence of low-density, submicroscopic parasitaemia, a defining feature of asymptomatic malaria that has been repeatedly documented in recent molecular studies across malaria-endemic regions of sub-Saharan Africa [17–20]. Although the overall detection rates of mRDT and PCR were comparable in this study, PCR remains more sensitive for detecting low-density infections and provides a more accurate estimate of the true asymptomatic reservoir. This suggests that, in settings with moderate-to-high transmission, well-performing mRDTs may still capture a substantial proportion of infections, but molecular tools remain essential for comprehensive surveillance.

Molecular detection further demonstrated pronounced spatial heterogeneity in infection burden, with significantly higher odds of PCR positivity in specific communities. This pattern reflects localized transmission dynamics and is consistent with molecular epidemiological evidence showing that asymptomatic infections tend to cluster geographically, particularly in areas experiencing ecological disruption [21, 22]. Such clustering indicates sustained parasite circulation within defined localities and reinforces the role of asymptomatic carriers as persistent reservoirs that support ongoing transmission.

Age-related differences emerged as a prominent molecular feature. Children and adolescents exhibited significantly higher odds of PCR-detected infection compared with adults and older adults. This pattern aligns with molecular evidence that younger age groups harbour higher parasite prevalence and density due to incomplete acquisition of anti-malarial immunity [23–25]. In contrast, adults often maintain low-density infections detectable mainly by sensitive molecular tools, supporting the concept that immunity suppresses parasitaemia rather than achieving complete parasite clearance [26]. These findings underscore the importance of age-dependent immune regulation in shaping asymptomatic infection dynamics.

Following up on the asymptomatic infected individuals revealed that parasite persistence and progression were strongly influenced by host phenomic traits. Although baseline positivity occurred across haemoglobin phenotypes, subsequent progression to detectable parasitaemia and clinical manifestations occurred exclusively among individuals with HbAA. No conversion was observed among HbAS carriers, reinforcing well-established molecular evidence that haemoglobin S interferes with parasite replication and survival within erythrocytes [27–29]. Altered red cell deformability, increased oxidative stress, and enhanced splenic clearance of infected cells collectively suppress parasite densities, limiting progression to symptomatic disease [30, 31]. These findings highlight the role of host genetic background in shaping parasite fitness and infection trajectories at the molecular level.

Temporal patterns of PCR-confirmed infection revealed dynamic fluctuations in parasitaemia, characterized by early peaks followed by declining detection rates during follow-up. Such oscillatory patterns are typical of asymptomatic infections, where immune-mediated suppression maintains parasite densities near diagnostic thresholds. Similar dynamics have been documented in longitudinal molecular studies using ultrasensitive PCR, emphasizing that asymptomatic carriage represents an active and evolving infection state rather than a static condition [32].

Beyond infection persistence, the timing of conversion from asymptomatic to symptomatic malaria was strongly influenced by host phenomic characteristics, particularly ABO blood group and haemoglobin phenotype, with supportive evidence from changes in body temperature during follow-up. ABO blood group antigens are known to influence rosetting efficiency and cytoadherence [30, 31, 33, 34], two key processes in P. falciparum pathogenicity. Erythrocytes expressing A and B antigens promote stronger and more stable rosette formation [35], facilitating parasite survival, sequestration, and microvascular obstruction. Consequently, individuals with blood groups A and AB are more likely to experience rapid parasite expansion, leading to earlier rises in parasitaemia sufficient to cross diagnostic thresholds and trigger clinical manifestations. This biological advantage for the parasite plausibly explains the early clustering of conversions among these blood groups.

In contrast, blood group O erythrocytes, which lack A and B antigens, impair rosetting and reduce cytoadherence [36]. This disruption limits efficient sequestration and slows parasite multiplication, thereby delaying parasite expansion. As a result, individuals with blood group O tended to convert later during follow-up, reflecting a delay in progression rather than complete protection against symptomatic infection.

The Rhesus D factor appeared to exert minimal influence on conversion timing. Although most conversions occurred among Rh-positive individuals, this pattern likely reflects population distribution rather than a direct biological effect, as Rh antigens are not known to significantly affect parasite invasion or growth.

In addition to phenomic factors, developmental stage and gender influenced conversion dynamics. Asymptomatic malaria was most prevalent among children (≤12 years) and adolescents (13–17 years) in the study communities, indicating that these younger groups constitute a major reservoir of silent infection. During follow-up, developmental stage exerted a strong early effect, with children demonstrating the highest rates of progression to symptomatic malaria in the initial weeks, followed by adolescents, whereas adults and older adults exhibited fewer conversions. This pattern is consistent with age-dependent acquisition of immunity, whereby younger individuals, despite frequent exposure and carriage, have not yet developed sufficiently robust immune regulation to effectively suppress early parasite expansion [37, 38]. Gender-related differences were minimal during the early weeks of follow-up but became more evident in Week 3, when males demonstrated significantly higher positivity rates. This pattern suggests that sex-related factors may influence parasite persistence rather than the initial establishment of infection. Biological mechanisms may include sex hormone-mediated modulation of immune responses, as testosterone has been associated with relatively reduced pro-inflammatory and antiparasitic immune activity [39], whereas oestrogen may enhance humoral and cellular responses [40]. Behavioural and exposure-related factors could also contribute, although the longitudinal design of this follow-up, restricted to already infected individuals, suggests that the observed difference is more likely to reflect differences in parasite clearance dynamics rather than differential exposure. The delayed divergence between males and females, therefore, supports the hypothesis that sex-specific immune regulation may influence the duration of parasitaemia in asymptomatic infections.

In spite of the gender and age-related vulnerabilities discussed above, progression from asymptomatic carriage to symptomatic malaria reflects a dynamic imbalance between parasite replication and host immune control. During silent infection, partially acquired immunity suppresses parasite multiplication and regulates inflammatory responses [41], maintaining low-density parasitaemia without clinical symptoms. Symptomatic conversion occurs when this equilibrium is disrupted, allowing increased parasite biomass, enhanced cytoadherence, and stronger inflammatory activation that produces fever and other clinical features.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that asymptomatic malaria in study communities is characterized by submicroscopic persistence, spatial clustering, age-dependent distribution, and strong modulation by host phenomic traits. Progression from silent infection to symptomatic disease is not random but is shaped by host erythrocyte biology, immune maturity, and parasite–host interactions that govern parasite survival and expansion.

Limitation

This study focused on selected host phenomic traits and did not assess immune markers, which may further influence infection dynamics and disease progression. In addition, parasites detected during follow-up may not necessarily represent persistence of the initial infection but could reflect new or genetically distinct infections. Finally, the study did not assess immune markers or prior malaria exposure history. Consequently, the mechanisms underlying incomplete protective immunity among adolescents could not be fully determined.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that progression to clinical malaria occurred preferentially in specific population groups rather than uniformly among infected individuals. Children and adolescents showed earlier progression, reflecting incomplete anti-malarial immunity and reduced capacity to suppress parasite expansion. Gender differences were minimal, although males showed greater parasite persistence later during follow-up. Host phenomic traits played a central role in disease progression. Individuals with blood groups A, B, and AB converted earlier (median Week 1), whereas blood group O showed delayed conversion (median Week 3). All progression occurred among HbAA individuals (median Week 2), with no conversion observed in HbAS carriers, highlighting its protective effect. The Rhesus D factor showed no meaningful influence on conversion timing. Malaria control efforts should strengthen surveillance, prioritize children and adolescents, incorporate phenomic risk considerations, enhance vector control in mining-affected areas, and implement targeted, risk-informed interventions.
