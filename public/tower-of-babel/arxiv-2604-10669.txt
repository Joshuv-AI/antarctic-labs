A Linear Temporal Logic of Frequencies on Series of Events
Melissa Antonelli, Leonardo Ceragioli, Alessandro Buda, Giuseppe Primiero

Abstract
This paper introduces 𝖫𝖳𝖫𝖥 \mathsf{LTLF} , a temporal logic designed to express the frequency properties of event series in a natural but rigorous manner. By introducing novel, measure-sensitive operators, 𝖫𝖳𝖫𝖥 \mathsf{LTLF} allows for the evaluation of frequencies and the prediction of future occurrences, thus providing a formal framework to monitor and control quantitative systems, such as machine learning classifiers. The core novelty lies in the introduction of original modal quantifiers associated with a standard Kripke-style semantics. These quantifiers enable the explicit formalization of event series properties and the investigation of the relationship between actual observed frequencies and ideal distributions within a single logical structure. This framework bridges the gap between formal logical reasoning and empirical observation.



1
Introduction

The need to monitor and control complex systems at runtime has become increasingly central in modern computational contexts, particularly when outputs must align with specific requirements. An example is the evaluation of machine learning classifiers in relation with algorithmic fairness. In these contexts, inference-only evaluation and correction is needed to minimize useless computation and anticipate unacceptable outputs.
To this aim, the design of formal systems to guarantee constraints satisfaction is a major and primary task.

In this paper, we present a new framework to model and reason in a natural and purely logical way about finite series of events characterized by a frequency distribution of expected outputs. We offer a modal language and associated semantics, where new measure-sensitive operators are defined to express the frequency of an event by counting the number of times it happens at different moments of the series, and to predict future likely occurrences. Such evaluation is then compared with a desired or ideal distribution, an ability which represents a core novelty of our system. The mathematical objects analyzed are simple and the formalized operators are intuitive, although the results obtained are not trivially verifiable in logical form.

While the literature contains various extensions of temporal and modal logics with counting or probabilistic features, such as Counting Linear Temporal Logic (LTL), Counting Computation Tree Logic (CTL) or Probabilistic Computation Tree Logic PCTL
[
18
,
12
,
17
,
13
]
, we are not aware of any existing temporal logic that explicitly formalizes the relationship between an actual observed frequency and a target theoretical distribution within a single evaluation structure.
Overall, this makes our logic an innovative tool for addressing phenomena that are quite common today. For example, machine learning classifiers can be interpreted as probabilistic systems that produce different probabilistic decisions based on the input they receive: in this context, the task of checking whether these results are in line with the expectation of the user,
or rather correction is needed,
can be modeled within our system.
The logic
\mathsf{LTLF}
(Linear Time Temporal Logic of Frequencies) introduced in this paper is comprehensive enough to encapsulate crucial aspects of probability and counting logics already defined in the literature
[
1
,
2
]
, but unlike many existing quantitative logics that prioritize restricted expressivity to ensure computational tractability, we employ a small set of highly expressive operators to capture complex distributional properties. Furthermore, our logic adopts a backward-looking approach, interpreting temporal operators over finite series of events by looking at the past, which diverges from the standard forward-looking semantics of most temporal logics.

This work continues in the steps of a broader research project dedicated to the logical formalization of experimental series
[
10
,
15
,
8
]
. While previous logical frameworks in this direction have considered series of events as complete and static results, in this paper we focus on a dynamic and temporally characterized approach, for evaluation at any point within the series. The current proposal is limited to a semantics for a linear temporal interpretation, while a version based on tree structures is currently in development to account for more complex branching scenarios.
A proof system and meta-theoretical results are left for future investigation.

Preliminaries

For the sake of self-containment, in this Section we recall standard notions in statistics and probability theory. For further details, see

An

or

Each experiment is associated with a set of possible values taken as its single outcome; e.g. for the toss of a coin, the set of possible values or single outcomes is defined by the set
\{\textsc{Head},\textsc{Tail}\}

\{1,\dots,6\}

The repeated execution of an experiment is called an observation. It is associated with a set of possible multiple outcomes, which depends on the possible single outcomes and on the number of times it is executed.
As standard, we use
\omega
to denote an outcome or point of an observation, i.e. the result of a single execution of an experiment, and
\Omega
to denote the sample space, i.e. the set of all possible outcomes
\omega
’s associated to this observation.
Recall that outcomes must be
mutually exclusive
, i.e. if
\omega_{i}
occurs, for any
j\neq i
, no other
\omega_{j}
can take place, and
collectively exhaustive
, i.e. one of the outcomes will result on every execution.

Each possible outcome is a sequence of

, while

Subsets of

E_{1},E_{2},\dots
.

An event consisting of only one outcome is called an

, while
an event that is made up of more than one possible outcome is called a

.
Two events, say

and

Definition 2.3

(Probability space)
.

A

•

Since our logic is meant to describe actual experiments and empirical probability, we focus on the notions of frequency and time series.

Definition 2.4

(Frequency and relative frequency)
.

Given a series of
n
executions of an experiment, the

of a single outcome or value is the number of times the observation results in this given value.
The
relative frequency
or

of a value is the ratio of the number of times in which the specified value occurs to the total number of trials.

Notation 2.1
.

Given an experiment with single outcomes in
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}
and
n
observations of this experiment, for any

we use
\mu_{\Phi}(p_{i})\in\mathbb{Q}_{[0,1]}
to denote the relative frequency of the value
p_{i}
.

Example 2.3
.

Let’s assume we flip a coin 100 times and we obtain head 50 times. Then the frequency of obtaining head in our experiment is 50 and its relative frequency is
\frac{1}{2}
, i.e.
\mu_{\Phi}(\textsc{Head})=\frac{1}{2}
.

Definition 2.5

(Probability of Frequencies)
.

Given a series of
n
executions of an experiment, the probability of obtaining a particular (absolute or relative) frequency is given by the ratio of the number of possible series compatible with that frequency to the number of all possible series. It can be seen as the
probability mass function
for the occurrence of compound events with the same frequency distribution.

Reasoning about series of events

In this Section, we informally introduce the core ideas to introduce a logic which naturally expresses properties of

To make this concrete, we use a guiding toy example throughout the paper.

3.1
A guiding example

We will deal with observations associated with a pre-determined number of events.
In our framework, it is natural to use atomic propositions to represent
single

We use
\mu_{\Phi}(E,p)

For example, given a series of trials, we want to formally study the probability that the frequency of the outcomes mirrors some expected or desired frequency. We will investigate the properties of observations, both assuming that all information about the series is given, and (more interestingly) in the context of a state of ignorance in which we know the results only of a subset of all experiments in the series; typically, the values of the first
m

In the latter scenario, we will also investigate the probability of the outcomes of the next experiments under the hypothesis that the frequency of outputs of all the experiments is some “ideal frequency”. We now formulate some problems to guide our analysis in the rest of this paper.

Let us assume that our observation consists of
n

\Omega=\{\textsc{Head}\ (H),\textsc{Tail}\ (T)\}

Problem 3.1

Given a series of

q

Clearly, at the third throw it holds that the relative frequency of

\frac{2}{3}

Problem 3.2

Problem 3.3

Given a series of

4
The temporal logic of frequencies
\mathsf{LTLF}

In this Section, we introduce the formal language and semantics of the propositional logic
\mathsf{LTLF}
, a natural formalism to express properties of experiments happening in a series or to compare the frequency of given phenomena with expected distribution.

4.1
The language of
\mathsf{LTLF}

The language of
\mathsf{LTLF}
is defined as a standard propositional language, endowed with original operators corresponding to those informally presented in Section
3.2

Notation 4.1
.

In what follows, we will use

\phi,\psi,\gamma,\dots

We use
\mathfrak{P}

Definition 4.1

(Language of
\mathsf{LTLF}
)

Formulas of
\mathsf{LTLF}

\phi::=p\;|\;\neg\psi\;|\;\psi\wedge\gamma\;|\;\psi\vee\gamma\;|\;\square_{q}\psi\;|\;\blacksquare_{q}\psi\;|\;\circ_{q}\psi\;|\;\triangleright_{q}\psi\;|\;\star_{q}\psi

where
p\in\mathfrak{P}
and
q\in\mathbb{Q}_{[0,1]}
.

Definition 4.2

(Assignment)
.

Given a set of atomic propositions
\mathfrak{P}_{\Omega}\subset\mathfrak{P}
and a set of wolds
W
, an assignment
a
is a mapping
\mathfrak{P}_{\Omega}\mapsto\mathcal{P}(W)
such that for any
w\in W
, there is a unique
p\in\mathfrak{P}_{\Omega}
for which
w\in a(p)
.

Definition 4.3

(Model of
\mathsf{LTLF}
)
.

A
\mathsf{LTLF}
-structure is a tuple
\mathbf{M}=(W,R_{e},F_{\Omega})
, where:

•

W=\{w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{n}\}
is a
finite
set of worlds;

•

R_{e}
is a anti-symmetric, reflexive, transitive and strongly connected relation;

•

F_{\Omega}
is the set of all assignments compatible with a
well-defined
(empirical) probability measure
\mu_{\Phi}
over the
\sigma
-algebra corresponding to a space
\Omega
and a set of variables
\mathfrak{P}_{\Omega}\subseteq\mathfrak{P}
; notably, for any
p\in\mathfrak{P}_{\Omega}
:

\frac{|\{w\in W:w\in a(p)\}|}{|W|}=\mu_{\Phi}(p).

When an assignment
\mathbf{a}
, intuitively corresponding to the “real” observation, is added, we obtain a
\mathsf{LTLF}
-model,
\mathbb{M}=(W,R_{e},F_{\Omega},\mathbf{a})
.

The relation
R_{e}
establishes an order between worlds:
since it is reflexive, for any
w\in W,wR_{e}w
; since it is transitive, for any
w,w^{\prime},w^{\prime\prime}\in W
, if
wR_{e}w^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime}R_{e}w^{\prime\prime}
, then
wR_{e}w^{\prime\prime}
;
since it is anti-symmetric, there is no
w,w^{\prime}\in R_{e}
where
w\neq w^{\prime}
, such that
wR_{e}w^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime}R_{e}w
; since it is strongly connected, for any
w,w^{\prime}\in R_{e}
, if
w\neq w^{\prime}
, then either
wR_{e}w^{\prime}
or
w^{\prime}R_{e}w
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Notice that, given the relation
R_{e}
, for every
w\in W
we can define its successor
succ(w)
as the world
w^{\prime}\in W
such that

\forall w^{\prime\prime}(w^{\prime\prime}R_{e}w^{\prime}\leftrightarrow(w^{\prime\prime}R_{e}w\lor w^{\prime\prime}=w^{\prime})).

Notation 4.2
.

For the sake of readability, in what follows we will assume that worlds in
W
are labelled as
w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{n}
and that
R_{e}
is defined accordingly.

As anticipated,
\mu_{\Phi}(\cdot)
corresponds to a
well-defined
(empirical) probability measure over a sample space
\Omega
.
Actually, any space of cardinality
n
(=
|W|
) would work.
Moreover, notice that according to our Def.of assignment (see Def.
4.2
) the atomic formulas in
\mathfrak{P}_{\Omega}\subseteq\mathfrak{P}
are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive, in the sense that every world has exactly one atomic formula assigned, just as each execution of the experiment is associated with exactly one element of
\Omega

Hence, although

contains atoms, if these sets contain the same number of elements, we can associate each atom with an outcome so to treat the frequencies of the atoms verified in the worlds of

to elements of
\Omega
for repeated observations of an experiment.

It is now possible to define the satisfiability relation in a world of
\mathbb{M}

Definition 4.4

(Truth in a world)
.

For any
\mathsf{LTLF}
-formula
\phi
,
\mathbb{M}=(W,R_{E},F_{\Omega},\mathbf{a})
, such that
|W|=n
, assignment
a\in F_{\Omega}\cup\{\mathbf{a}\}
, and world

, we define the relation
\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}
, inductively as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}p

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\text{iff}\hskip 8.50012ptw_{m}\in a(p)

\displaystyle\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\neg\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\text{iff}\hskip 8.50012pt\mathbb{M},w_{m}\not\vDash_{a}\phi

\displaystyle\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\phi\wedge\psi

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\text{iff}\hskip 8.50012pt\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\phi\text{ and }\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\psi

\displaystyle\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\phi\vee\psi

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\text{iff}\hskip 8.50012pt\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\phi\text{ or }\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\psi

\displaystyle\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\square_{q}\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\text{iff}\hskip 8.50012pt\frac{|\{w_{l}:l\leq m\wedge\mathbb{M},w_{l}\vDash_{a}\phi\}|}{m}\geq q

\displaystyle\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\blacksquare_{q}\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\text{iff}\hskip 8.50012pt\text{there is a }a^{\prime}\in F_{\Omega},\;\frac{|\{w_{l}:l\leq m\wedge\mathbb{M},w_{l}\vDash_{a^{\prime}}\phi\}|}{m}\geq q

\displaystyle\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\circ_{q}\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\text{iff}\hskip 8.50012pt\frac{|\{w_{l}:l\leq m\wedge\mathbb{M},w_{l}\vDash_{a}\phi\}|}{n}\geq q

\displaystyle\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\triangleright_{q}\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\text{iff}\hskip 8.50012pt\frac{|\{a^{\prime}:a^{\prime}\in F_{\Omega}\wedge\forall p,i(i\leq m\to(w_{i}\in a^{\prime}(p)\leftrightarrow w_{i}\in a(p))\wedge\mathbb{M},w_{m+1}\vDash_{a^{\prime}}\phi\}|}{|\{a^{\prime}:a^{\prime}\in F_{\Omega}\wedge\forall p,i(i\leq m\to(w_{i}\in a^{\prime}(p)\leftrightarrow w_{i}\in a(p)))\}|}\geq q

\displaystyle\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\star_{q}\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\text{iff}\hskip 8.50012pt\text{there is a }w^{\prime}\in W,\;\frac{|\{a^{\prime}\in F_{\Omega}:\mathbb{M},w^{\prime}\vDash_{a^{\prime}}\phi\}|}{|F_{\Omega}|}\geq q.

Notably
\mathbf{a}
is nothing but an assignment (either in
F_{\Omega}
or not), to be intuitively interpreted as the “real” or “current” observation.
Notice that, while our main interest is to evaluate formulas according to this real observation, Definition
4.4
of truth in a world has to take into account also assignments in
F_{\Omega}
, as these are needed to formally define

\star

The clause for atoms stresses that we are interpreting them as outcomes of the experiments composing the observation.
The clause for negation results from the assumption of mutual exclusivity of the atoms in

and is preserved for composed formulas due to the axioms of the
\sigma
-algebras.
The clauses for conjunction and disjunction interpret these notions as the logical counterpart of intersection and union between events.
Modalities give a way to express the observed frequency of verified formulas or related notions.
This ensures that the semantics is well defined.

Going back to Ex.

Notation 4.4
.

For any operator
\oplus\in\{\square,\blacksquare,\circ,\star,\triangleright\}
, we let
\oplus_{<q}
denote the strict inequality counterpart, where the measure of the set of interest is strictly less than
q
. For example

\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\square_{<q}\phi\quad\text{iff}\quad\frac{|\{w:w_{m}R_{e}w\;\wedge\;\mathbb{M},w\vDash_{a}\phi\}|}{m}<q.

Similarly, we use
\oplus_{=q}
to define the operator expressing that the set of interest has measure precisely
q

\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\square_{=q}\phi\quad\text{iff}\quad\frac{|\{w:w_{m}R_{e}w\;\wedge\;\mathbb{M},w\vDash_{a}\phi\}|}{m}=q.

All of these operators are definable in terms of the primitive ones (see Appendix
A

The operator
\oplus_{=q}
behaves differently for white and black modalities.
In particular, with white modalities, the formula
\oplus_{=q}
is satisfied by a unique probability value
q
.
The same does not hold for black modalities.
More formally:

Theorem 4.3
.

For every
\mathbb{M}=(W,R_{E},F_{\Omega},\mathbf{a})

,
\mathsf{LTLF}

Proof.

The proof follows trivially from the observation that for any
\mathbf{a}
, the truth condition for
\oplus_{=q}\phi
is defined in terms of a fraction that can have only one value.
For example, the truth of a white boxed formula in the world
w_{m}
is determined by the
unique

\frac{|w_{l}:l\leq m\wedge\mathbb{M},w_{k}\vDash_{a}\phi\}|}{m}
being equal to the given
q
.
∎

Notation 4.5
.

For any operator

, we use

to define the operator that is true only for the greatest index that makes true its counterpart:

\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\oplus_{\overline{q}}\phi\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\oplus_{q}\phi\>\land\>(q^{\prime}>q\>\Rightarrow\>\mathbb{M},w_{m}\nvDash_{a}\oplus_{q^{\prime}}\phi)

Also in this case, these operators are definable in terms of the primitive ones (see Appendix

).
For white modalities, this notion coincides extensionally with that expressing the precise measure of the set of interest:

Theorem 4.4
.

For every
\mathbb{M}=(W,R_{E},F_{\Omega},\mathbf{a})

,

\mathbb{M},w\vDash_{a}\oplus_{=q}\phi\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathbb{M},w\vDash_{a}\oplus_{\overline{q}}\phi.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)

From Def.
4.4
, if

\mathbb{M},w\vDash_{a}\oplus_{q}\phi

\mathbb{M},w\vDash_{a}\oplus_{\overline{q}}\phi

(\Leftarrow)

|F_{\Omega}|=\prod^{m-1}_{i=1}{{n-\sum^{i-1}_{j=1}\big(\mu_{\Phi}(p_{l_{j}})\times n\big)}\choose{\mu_{\Phi}(p_{l_{i}})\times n}}

\frac{n-\sum^{m-1}_{j=1}(\mu_{\Phi}(p_{l_{j}})\times n)}{n}

behaving like
\mathbf{a}
in the first

\mathbf{a}
in these worlds.
To count the number of assignments in

, which behave like
\mathbf{a}
in the first

to the atoms so that the frequencies in

are realized.
The expression below

\prod^{|\mathfrak{P}_{\Omega}|}_{i=1}{{n-m-\sum^{i-1}_{j=1}(r_{j}-l_{j})}\choose{r_{i}-l_{i}}}

computes precisely this.
In particular, each factor

represents the worlds after

left open by the assignment of the atoms

is the number of worlds to be still assigned to

.
On the other hand, to count the total number of assignments behaving like
\mathbf{a}
in the wolds preceding

, we consider how many ways there are

to assign worlds after
w_{m}
to the atoms in
\mathfrak{P}_{\Omega}
.
This is precisely what the expression

computes.
∎

Theorem 5.9
.

For any model
\mathbb{M}=(W,R_{e},F_{\Omega},\mathbf{a})
associated with a given
\Omega
and s.t.
|W|=n
, world
w_{m}\in W
, if the following holds

\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{\mathbf{a}}\bigwedge_{i}^{p_{\omega_{i}}\in\mathfrak{P}_{\Omega}}\circ_{=\frac{l_{i}}{n}}p_{\omega_{i}}\wedge\star_{=\frac{r_{i}}{n}}p_{\omega_{i}}

and the values of
\Omega
are all independent,
then the probability
P^{\mathbf{a}}_{w_{m}}
that
\mathbf{a}
is compatible with
F_{\Omega}
evaluated at world

is:

P^{\mathbf{a}}_{w_{m}}=\prod^{|\mathfrak{P}_{\Omega}|}_{i=1}{{n-m-\sum^{i-1}_{j=1}(r_{j}-l_{j})}\choose{r_{i}-l_{i}}}\times\prod^{|\mathfrak{P}_{\Omega}|}_{i=1}{P(p_{i})^{r_{i}-l_{i}}}

Where
P(p_{\omega_{i}})
is the probability associated with output
\omega_{i}
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one for Th.
5.8
.
First, we count the possible ways to assign worlds after
w_{m}
to the atoms so that the frequencies in
F_{\Omega}
are realized. This part of the proof is proved exactly as before.
Then, we obtain the probability of each of these ways of completing the series.
This is easier than it might seem because the atoms verified in the worlds
w_{m+1},\ldots,w_{n}
are still the same.
We just need to verify
r_{1}-l_{1}
times the atom
p_{\omega_{1}}
, and we know that the probability that this happens is
P(p_{\omega_{1}})^{r_{1}-l_{1}}
.
The same applies to the other atoms.
Hence, the probability of each of these endings of the series is

\prod^{|\mathfrak{P}_{\Omega}|}_{i=1}{P(p_{i})^{r_{i}-l_{i}}}.

The desired result is obtained by summing together the probabilities of each of these endings, that is by multiplying this probability for the number of such endings.
∎

Example 5.5
.

5.5
Foreseeing the future (a posteriori)

Finally, we consider the problem of estimating at one point of the series the probability of an outcome in the (relative) future, given that we know its global frequency.

See
3.5

Given a model
\mathbb{M}=(W,R_{e},F_{\Omega},\mathbf{a})

Our operator

For any model

\mathbf{a}\in F_{\Omega}

.

iff the probability that
\mathbb{M},w_{m+1}\vDash_{\mathbf{a}}\phi
under condition that
\mathbf{a}\in F_{\Omega}
is exactly

.

Proof.

As for Th.
5.8
, the probability is obtained by counting the ratio of assignments that satisfy given restrictions.
In this case, we count the assignments in

which behave like
\mathbf{a}
in the first

in the first

6
Related Works

Our logic is grounded in the various frameworks where modal logics, in particular their temporal interpretations, are extended via weights, probabilities, or counting methods.
Some of our modalities are also inspired by the counting operators introduced in counting logics, which were mostly developed in connection to complexity
[
22
,
1
]
and finite model theory
[
20
,
14
]
.

An extension of a (non-temporal) modal logic with counting modalities has been presented in
[
19
]
: here, necessity and possibility are defined by counting how many accessible worlds satisfy a formula. While the intuition of our logic is similar, we impose some restrictions on the accessibility relation, focusing only on a linear order relation (interpreted as a time relation, with each world seeing only the previous ones) and introduce new modalities to deal with a hypothetical ideal frequency alongside the actual observed frequency. For this reason, the interpretation of our semantics is closer to the family of temporal logics with weights or probabilities.

Several systems have been offered in this second family of logics. In
[
18
]
and
[
6
]
, LTL is extended so to count worlds satisfying a formula by adding a counting device to
until
(
U
).
In
[
18
]
, formulas
\phi U\psi
are generalized using a subscript
[C]
that requires the satisfaction of a specific formula (say
\chi
) a specific number of times (say
n
) in the worlds before the one that satisfies
\psi
.
Hence,
\phi U_{[C]}\psi
is satisfied at world
w_{i}
iff:
\exists j\geq i
s.t.
w_{j}
satisfies
\psi
; for all
i\leq k<j
,
w_{k}
satisfies
\phi
; and there are
n
worlds (the number specified in
[C]
) between
w_{i}
and
w_{j}
that satisfy
\chi
(the formula occurring in
[C]
).
Generalizations requiring precisely
n
worlds to satisfy
\chi
are also investigated.
While
[
18
]
counts worlds in (a segment of) the future, our counting modalities count worlds in the past. This difference becomes particularly relevant when we compare the way in which we address the next operator.
Moreover, we express frequencies instead of absolute counting.
Additionally,

[
6
]
diverges from
[
18
]
in two regards: the condition on
\phi U\psi
talks about
\phi
, and not about a formula possibly different from both
\phi
and
\psi
; their counting
U
requires that
\phi
is satisfied with a specific frequency before
\psi
is satisfied, and not an absolute number of times. While our logic is more similar to
[
6
]
, it differs since we investigate the relation between an observed frequency and a desired one, using counting modalities that look at the past and a probabilistic next operator that is radically different from the usual one.

The logic CTL has also been variously extended to include weighted modalities. Its probabilistic version, PCTL, allows to express in the language the probability that a certain
next
or
until
formula is satisfied in a path, see
[
13
]
. Among the several variants and extensions of PCTL (see, for example,
[
4
,
3
,
16
,
21
]
), the epistemic PCTLK
[
23
]
introduces operators to express that agents have such uncertain knowledge of temporal properties. Corresponding semantics are based on the notion of Markov decision processes. A different approach uses Interpreted Systems. Computationally-grounded Weighted Doxastic (COGWED) logic introduced in
[
9
]
is designed to specify degrees of belief in a system of agents. Intuitively, this semantics allows to express that a group of agents believes with a certain degree of certainty that a temporal formula holds. All these systems combine probabilities and modalities interpreted as temporal operators, somewhat inspiring our approach. Indeed, in
\mathsf{LTLF}
uncertainty is directly attached to temporal operators similarly to PCTL, but we interpret temporal operators over finite series of events by considering a backward-looking approach, unusual in standard temporal logics.
Moreover, we make use of an external observer, rather than having agents explicitly in the language, so that our logic expresses properties of a series of events rather than epistemic (un)certainty of an agent.

Finally, the explicit introduction of a target frequency to evaluate observations is a distinctive feature of
\mathsf{LTLF}
.
This latter aspect has been used to characterize other semantics. In particular in
[
15
]
, a variation of a standard possible worlds semantics is used to express reasoning on probabilities and frequencies of events. In this approach, sentences involving probabilities and frequencies are syntactic constructions describing events in models and terms express idealized processes (or random variables) and empirical processes, and corresponding outputs (or values). This approach allows to compare different models (in particular a model for a series of events or a trial with a theoretical probability) to assess how close they are. This intuition is formally defined by embedding information in a single structure for evaluation and using weighted operators with their natural temporal interpretation.

7
Conclusion and future work

In this paper, we introduced
\mathsf{LTLF}
, a novel logical system designed to reason
naturally
about phenomena and experiments occurring within finite series.
While the literature offers various temporal and counting probabilistic logics, our approach is, to the best of our knowledge, original in its ability to explicitly formalize the relationship between observed frequencies and target theoretical distributions within a single temporal structure.
Additionally, the framework maintains the advantage of an extremely simple and natural formalization, even while capturing the sophisticated properties inherent in finite series of events. By shifting the counting perspective toward a “dynamic” evaluation of frequencies, this logic provides a straightforward and direct tool for expressing and studying the properties of complex systems as they change over time.
In particular, through the introduction of five key counting modalities (
\square
,
\blacksquare
,
\star
,
\triangleright
, and
\circ
) our logic provides the expressive power to count event occurrences, check the compatibility of an observation with a desired frequency, express the probability of ending with a desired frequency, and calculate the probability of specific future outcomes based on realized data and assumptions about the target theoretical distributions.
In the paper, we have investigated the basic properties of these operators, and we have used them to formalize and solve five natural problems regarding the comparison between actual frequencies and ideal ones.

Since the operators introduced here are entirely new, several research directions remain open. On the formal side, our modalities seem to give rise to non-standard notions of validity whose study and the investigation of the corresponding complexity deserve further attention, while a branching time version is already being investigated
[
7
]
.
Moreover, the development of a sound and complete proof system and exploration of meta-theoretical results are left for future work. On the applied side, we are currently investigating concrete applications of
\mathsf{LTLF}
for bias mitigation in machine learning classifiers (see
[
7
]
).

a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{p}
play a role in the satisfaction of
\phi
in
w_{i}
by
a_{1},\ldots,a_{q}
.
∎

To illustrate this result, consider specific values for
q
.
In particular, consider
q=\frac{1}{|F_{\Omega}|}
from left to right (since if
\star_{q}\phi
with
q\neq 0
is satisfied in
w_{m}
, also
\star_{\frac{1}{|F_{\Omega}|}}\phi
is satisfied in it) and
q=1
from right to left (since if
\star_{1}\phi
is satisfied in
w_{m}
, also
\star_{q}\phi
with every
q\in\mathbb{Q}_{[0,1]}
is satisfied in it).
Now, from left to right, if
\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\star_{\frac{1}{|F_{\Omega}|}}\star_{q^{\prime}}\phi
, then there is at least a world in
W
s.t.
\star_{q^{\prime}}\phi
is satisfied there by an assignment in
F_{\Omega}
.
But then, by Th.
B.5
,
\star_{q^{\prime}}\phi
is satisfied at
w_{m}
and according to
a
as well.
From right to left, if
\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\star_{q^{\prime}}\phi
, then by Th.
B.5
,
\star_{q^{\prime}}\phi
is satisfied at any world in
W
and for every assignment (
a fortiori
, for every assignment in
F_{\Omega}
).
Hence,
\star_{1}\star_{q^{\prime}}\phi
is satisfied everywhere and, in particular, in
w_{m}
and according to
a
.

Notice that for any structure, assignment, and
w_{i}\in W
(and
|W|=n
), with
i<m
,
\mathbb{M},w_{i}\not\vDash_{a}\circ_{q}p
, where
q=\frac{m}{n}
.
Moreover, the following property of (pure) “nesting elimination” of
\circ
operators holds.

Theorem B.7
.

For any model
\mathbb{M}
, assignment
a\in F_{\Omega}\cup\{\mathbf{a}\}
, world
w_{m}\in W
, rationals
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}_{[0,1]}
and s.t.
q\neq 0
,

\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\circ_{q}\circ_{q^{\prime}}\phi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\circ_{q^{\prime}}\phi.

Proof.

If
\mathbb{M},w\vDash_{a}\circ_{q}\circ_{q^{\prime}}\phi
and
q\neq 0
, then there is at least a world
w_{i}
s.t.
i\leq m
and
\mathbb{M},w_{i}\vDash_{a}\circ_{q^{\prime}}\phi
.
Hence, the result follows by Th.
B.1
.
∎

The following example shows that (somewhat unexpectedly) the property does not hold in the other direction.

Example B.4
.

Let a model
\mathbb{M}
be s.t.
W=\{w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}\}
and the atomic sentences used to define
F_{\Omega}
are
\mathfrak{P}_{\Omega}=\{p_{\textsc{Head}},p_{\textsc{Tail}}\}
.
Moreover, consider an assignment
a\in F_{\Omega}\cup\{\mathbf{a}\}
s.t.
a(p_{\textsc{Head}})=\{w_{1},w_{2}\}
and
a(p_{\textsc{Tail}})=\{w_{3},w_{4}\}
.
Then, the following relations hold:
\mathbb{M},w_{1}\vDash_{a}\circ_{=0}p_{\textsc{Tail}}
,
\mathbb{M},w_{2}\vDash_{a}\circ_{=0}p_{\textsc{Tail}}
,
\mathbb{M},w_{3}\vDash_{a}\circ_{=\frac{1}{4}}p_{\textsc{Tail}}
and
\mathbb{M},w_{4}\vDash_{a}\circ_{=\frac{1}{2}}p_{\textsc{Tail}}
.
Hence, for example,
\mathbb{M},w_{4}\vDash_{a}\circ_{=\frac{1}{2}}p_{\textsc{Tail}}
but
\mathbb{M},w_{4}\nvDash_{a}\circ_{q}\circ_{=\frac{1}{2}}p_{\textsc{Tail}}
for any
q\geq\frac{1}{4}
.

In general, this shows that
\circ_{q}\circ_{q^{\prime}}\phi
entails
\circ_{q^{\prime}}\phi
for
q\leq\frac{1}{n}
only.
Moreover, this is just a special case of a more general (although obvious) property:

Theorem B.8
.

For any model
\mathbb{M}
, assignment
a\in F_{\Omega}\cup\{\mathbf{a}\}
, and world
w_{m}\in W
:

\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\phi\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbb{M},w_{m}\vDash_{a}\circ_{\frac{1}{n}}\phi.

Proof.

Obvious by truth-conditions for
\circ
(see Def.
4.4
).
∎
