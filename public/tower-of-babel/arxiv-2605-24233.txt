Bayesian Rational Search Engine User Thanks: Preliminary draft. Comments welcome.
Shichao Ma

Abstract
A user faces a list returned by a search system, ordered by a noisy proxy for relevance, and decides sequentially whether to pay a fixed cost to inspect another item or stop with the best she has uncovered. She does not enter the page knowing how good its items are, so each inspection both produces a candidate item and refines her belief about the page’s underlying quality. We show the optimal policy is a standout rule: the user stops as soon as her best find exceeds her posterior mean of an average item on the page by a depth-dependent threshold. The induced dynamics collapse to a one-dimensional Markov chain, which yields the full distribution of inspection depth through a closed-form recursion. The model uncovers three hidden mechanisms (trust, commit, and cut-losses) on why users stop and yields a rich set of testable implications. Moreover, the Bayesian-rational view delivers a novel learning-to-rank likelihood: an observed depth censors the latent relevance path into a polyhedron of survival inequalities, whose Gaussian probability is a differentiable function of any feature-based relevance prediction.



Keywords:
sequential search, Bayesian learning, optimal stopping, position bias, click models, inspection depth, implicit feedback, learning-to-rank.

Introduction

Search engines, e-commerce platforms, and recommender systems all deliver their output as a ranked list. The system retrieves a pool of items and orders them by a learned score that is meant to track how relevant each item is to the user who made the request. That score, however, is unavoidably a proxy: the user’s private notion of relevance is not observable to the system, and any signal the system can compute from queries, content, and prior interactions is informative but noisy. The top item is therefore not necessarily the most relevant, and whether to commit to it or keep scrolling is a real economic decision. Each additional look costs the user some time and attention, and what she will find is uncertain.

This paper studies that decision from first principles. A user faces a list whose order she takes to be informative but imperfect. She can inspect items one at a time at a fixed cost; each inspection reveals an item’s true relevance. She stops whenever she likes and keeps the best item she has uncovered, or falls back to an outside option that summarizes what is available to her off-list. Crucially, she does not enter the page knowing how good its items are. Instead, she holds a prior over the page’s underlying relevance, and every inspection serves two purposes at once: it produces a candidate item, and it sharpens her view of the distribution from which the rest of the page is drawn.

This learning channel is what makes the problem richer than the classical sequential-search exercise. An inspection is valuable for two distinct reasons: it might surface a new best item, raising the user’s payoff directly, and it refines her belief about what the rest of the page looks like, improving every continuation decision that follows. Both motives have to be paid for by the same fixed cost. As the user goes deeper, her belief stabilizes, the items still ahead of her are expected to be worse on average than the ones she has already seen (the ranker has front-loaded its best guesses), and her best find so far can only have grown. Optimal behavior must reconcile these forces.

The first contribution of the paper is a clean characterization of that reconciliation. We show that, however the user reorganizes her search, she does no worse by inspecting in rank order: linear traversal is without loss of generality. This collapses what looks like a combinatorial problem onto a single stopping time. The optimal policy then takes a strikingly simple form, which we call a
standout
rule. At every step the user keeps two summaries of her state in mind: the best item she has revealed so far, and her current best guess at the quality of an average item on this page. She continues as long as her best find does not yet stand out far enough above that average, and stops as soon as the gap clears a deterministic, depth-dependent threshold.

The threshold itself has two interpretable pieces: an option-value premium for the residual uncertainty about what the user might still find, and a rank-specific shift that absorbs how much a given rank is expected to outperform an average position. Both pieces shrink as the user moves down the page, which is why users stop somewhere along the list rather than only at the very end. A companion result reduces the apparently two-dimensional dynamics to a one-dimensional Markov chain in the gap between the best find and the posterior mean, with a closed-form transition that recovers the full distribution of inspection depth through a simple recursion.

The economic content of the model lies in what the optimal policy says about the empirical signals that search and recommendation systems harvest from log data. Four implications stand out, the first three about user behavior in the log and the fourth about the platform’s inference problem.

First, one-click sessions, a dominant phenomenon in real-world search logs, are produced by qualitatively different mechanisms that the log alone cannot tell apart. In one mechanism, which we call the
trust
regime, the user inspects the top item only to confirm what she already expected, and would have stopped at rank
1
for every realization of that item: the decision to stop was effectively made before the page rendered. In the other, the user is in principle prepared to look further, but the first item resolves the matter: either by being so good that further looking is wasteful (the
commit
branch) or by being so disappointing that her outside option dominates the rest of the list in expectation (the
cut-losses
branch). The same observable footprint admits a satisfied user, a disappointed user, and a user who never planned to engage with the rest of the page in the first place.

Second, the trust and commit channels jointly produce what we frame as a
winner’s curse
for ranker quality. The two channels are not merely two static cases; they become more pronounced exactly as the ranker improves. A ranker with a more decisive top slot widens the gap that triggers the trust regime, and a ranker that places objectively better items at the top makes more first-look outcomes good enough to commit on. A successful ranker therefore concentrates inspections at rank
1
and starves the logger of the deeper observations needed to evaluate it, retrain it, or detect that some lower-ranked item should have been promoted. This is not biased logging or model misuse. It is an equilibrium consequence of rational stopping, and it tightens precisely as the ranker improves.

Third, the standard industrial deployment practice, a short A/B test following an engineering change to ranker or retrieval, measures users who have not yet absorbed the change. During the test window the user’s prior is still anchored at the old relevance level; only the items she draws have moved. Her stopping policy depends on her prior, and so does not shift across the test. The change reaches her only through the realized items she now sees. Once she has learned the new level, the surprise channel shuts off and only the unchanged outside option drives behavior. The model pins down the consequence: a small relevance lift can push enough first-look outcomes into the commit branch to
shorten
sessions during the test, even when the same lift
lengthens
sessions in the long run. The two phases measure two different things, and reading short-run depth as a steady-state user-satisfaction signal will sometimes point in the wrong direction.

Taken together, these results carry a methodological message with concrete implications for the design of empirical readings. Scroll depth, dwell time, and session length (the workhorse implicit-feedback signals in modern search and recommendation
(
Fox et al. 2005
;
Hassan et al. 2010
)
) are not univariate signals of either user satisfaction or page quality. Generically, the same depth measurement is consistent with a satisfied user who has found what she wanted and a disappointed user who has confirmed the page is not for her. The model identifies precisely which auxiliary observation breaks the tie: a downstream conversion event (a purchase, a long-dwell read, a completed booking) is the variable that separates commitment from cut-losses, and trust from learned indifference. Platforms that natively observe conversions therefore face a much easier inference problem than those that do not, which gives the latter a concrete reason to invest in high-quality conversion signals rather than refining proxies built from depth alone.

A fourth implication concerns the inference problem that platforms face when they try to learn relevance from their logs. The conventional industrial pipeline labels every inspected-but-not-converted item a negative, and the unbiased learning-to-rank literature
(
Joachims et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2018
)
reweights those labels by an estimated examination propensity. The Bayesian-rational reading of the same log says strictly more. Surviving to depth
t
is not a sequence of
t-1
binary negatives; it is a certification that the entire latent relevance path lies inside an explicit polyhedron of
t(t+1)/2-1
linear inequalities determined by the user’s reservation rule. The Gaussian probability that a session’s latent path lies in that polyhedron under a feature-based relevance model
\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w})
is a closed-form, differentiable function of
\beta
, and a logged conversion outcome refines the polyhedron to a single conversion-consistent tail. Summed across sessions this yields a training objective for
\beta
that requires no relevance labels, no behavioral examination model, and no inverse-propensity weighting.

The remainder of the paper develops these results in order. The next two sections set up the environment and the Gaussian specification used throughout, and derive the optimal policy and the standout rule. The section that follows takes up three special cases that admit closed forms, including the myopic policy, the two-regime decomposition of one-click sessions, and the diffuse-prior limit of an uninformed user. Section
5
reduces the dynamics to a one-dimensional lead chain and characterizes the full distribution of inspection depth. Section
6
develops the short-run versus long-run divergence in the A/B-test setting, and Section
7
constructs the survival polyhedron in latent-relevance space and the depth-and-conversion likelihood it generates.

Related Literature

This paper directly speaks to the position bias literature in information retrieval. It is well known that the probability of an item being clicked depends on the position in the result list. Scholars have built various heuristical and behavioral models try to explain this fact. The cascade model of
Craswell et al. 2008
and its descendants, the dynamic Bayesian network of
Chapelle and Zhang 2009
and the user browsing model of
Dupret and Piwowarski 2008
, surveyed in
Chuklin et al. 2022
, posit that users examine ranks top-down, with examination at each rank a stochastic function of position and, in the cascade family, of past examinations.
Joachims et al. 2017
and the personal-search line of
Wang et al. 2016
and
Wang et al. 2018
treat the rank-conditional examination probability as a propensity to be estimated and used in inverse-propensity weighting of logged interactions. The recent survey of
Chen et al. 2023
catalogs both threads and the broader landscape of selection, exposure, and presentation biases that motivate them.

We take a completely different approach modeling users browsing behavior. Instead of explicitly or implicitly imposing various behavioral assumptions, we start from the first principles of rational choice. We show that engaging with a search engine result page (SERP) can be modeled as a Bayesian learning exercise of rational agents.

Our approach engages the existing literature both as an ally and as a critic. As an ally, we show that the canonical assumption in the cascade-style models, users making linear traversals through SERPs, can be derived from Bayesian rationality. We also highlight the importance of relevance and rank as key factors determining the likelihood of user engagement.

As a critic, we show that the propensity of a particular rank is, under rational inspection, not a function of the rank or the relevance of that slot alone. It depends on the full realized history of what the user has revealed at all previous ranks. In our model, a user who got a great item at rank 2 inspects rank 5 with much lower probability than a user who got mediocre items, even when the ranker, query, and corpus are identical. In a similar fashion, we completely endogenize user satisfaction and the stopping decision: user stops not because of some continuation parameter but because of a history-dependent reservation rule that solves the Bellman equation of continuation.

Eye-tracking studies of SERP viewing,
Joachims et al. 2005
and the broader behavioral evidence reviewed in
Chuklin et al. 2022
, confirm that users scan top-down and that examination probability decays with rank, but they also document that continued inspection is contingent on what the user has just seen.
Joachims et al. 2005
argued, on this basis, that absolute clicks are unreliable as relevance labels, and proposed interpreting clicks as
relative
preferences. Our model sharpens this point in a particular direction: under the optimal policy, the very fact that rank
i
is inspected is a signal that all previous items fail to clear the optimal reservation rule. Implicit feedback at rank
i
is therefore better understood as a joint statement about all earlier items, not as an isolated relevance label.

The model also speaks to the feedback-loop concern in the recommender systems literature
(
Schnabel et al. 2016
;
Chaney et al. 2018
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
, where biased logging propagates to biased models that in turn shape future logs. Our winner’s curse for ranker quality is a structurally different instance of the same concern: the data starvation is not driven by biased logging or model misuse, but is an equilibrium consequence of rational stopping, and tightens as the deployed ranker improves.

Our results have direct implications for the long-running effort to design search success metrics from implicit signals
(
Fox et al. 2005
;
Hassan et al. 2010
)
. Scroll depth, dwell time, and session length have all been proposed as proxies for satisfaction. We reveal the nuances of these prevailing metrics. In particular, we show how depth or duration alone is not a univariate signal of either user welfare or search quality. Observing the same depth is generically consistent with either a satisfied user (who commits because she has found a good item) and a disappointed one (who fall backs to her outside option because she has confirmed the search quality fails to meet her expectation). Depth logs, no matter how abundant, cannot distinguish the two. A conversion signal (e.g., a purchase, a long-dwell read, or any downstream completion event) is exactly the variable that breaks the tie. This is why platforms that intrinsically observe one (e.g., e-commerce checkouts) face a much easier inference problem, and why it is imperative for those that do not to build high quality conversion signals (e.g., through cross-site tracking or data purchasing).

The same architecture yields a learning-to-rank likelihood that bypasses the position-bias machinery entirely. The unbiased learning-to-rank approach of
Joachims et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2018
accepts the cascade-style behavioral premise, treats unconverted items as binary negatives, and corrects for selection by reweighting those labels with an estimated position-based propensity. Our derivation in Section
7
replaces all three steps. The behavioral premise is endogenous: linear traversal and the reservation rule are theorems, not assumptions. The binary negative is replaced by a polyhedral set-identification of the entire inspected path. The propensity correction is replaced by writing the session likelihood directly as the Gaussian probability of that polyhedron under a feature-based model
\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w})
, a differentiable object trainable by standard stochastic gradient descent algorithms
(
Hajivassiliou et al. 1996
;
Botev 2017
)
. The result also sharpens the relative-preference reading of
Joachims et al. 2005
: depth-
t
engagement is not one label or one pairwise preference but a finite system of explicit linear inequalities in the inspected relevances. As a minor corollary, the position-based propensity is itself the marginal of a survival region against the relevance distribution and is therefore not transportable across rankers, retrieval pools, or user priors.

Finally, the model sits at the intersection of three classical strands in the economics of search. Our optimal policy is a reservation rule in the tradition of
McCall 1970
, but with the textbook known-distribution case replaced by an evolving posterior over the page’s relevance distribution. The inspection structure is a special case of
Weitzman 1979
’s Pandora’s box in which boxes share a common, unknown population mean and their rank-specific shifts are induced by ranking on a noisy signal rather than by independent box priors. The Bayesian-learning ingredient is in the lineage of
Rothschild 1974
, with the new feature that observations arrive in a position-dependent order that injects an additional rank-specific shift into the posterior. The closest modern companion is
Ursu 2018
, who estimates a Weitzman-style sequential search model on Expedia data with experimentally varied rankings and finds that position primarily affects what consumers search, not what they purchase conditional on search.

Research Limitation

We comment briefly on three abstractions.

The standard Gaussian-conjugate specification in Assumptions
1
and
2
is adopted for tractability. The closed-form expressions we get from the conjugate prior help sharpen the discussions and pinpoint the mechanisms. The general qualitative architecture of our analysis, including linear traversal, the two-dimensional reservation rule, and the trust/commit decomposition of Proposition
7
, does not rely on the parametric form.

We also treat the SERP as a single ranked list. Real pages mix organic results with sponsored slots, carousels, and side panels, whose placement may reflect business considerations rather than predicted relevance. The abstraction is standard in the classical click-modeling literature
(
Craswell et al. 2008
;
Chapelle and Zhang 2009
;
Dupret and Piwowarski 2008
;
Chuklin et al. 2022
)
, and accommodating multiple surfaces is orthogonal to the questions studied here.

Finally, we model inspection as a binary action (skip or fully reveal) whereas real users may have access to partial signals before deciding to click (e.g., reading a snippet). Layering a snippet stage on the present framework introduces a hierarchical signal structure, which significantly lengthens the paper. Given the density of this paper’s current form, we decide to leave it to future work.

Basic Setup

A risk-neutral user makes a request to a search engine. The system retrieves
N
items in response. Each item is characterized by a latent relevance
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
The true relevance of each item, however, is unobserved to the system. For any retrieved item
i
, the system only has a noisy signal

z_{i}=x_{i}+e_{i},

where
e_{i}
is an idiosyncratic noise term independent of
x_{i}
. The system observes
z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}
, and returns items as a ranked list in decreasing order of
z
. We relabel items so that

z_{1}\geq z_{2}\geq\cdots\geq z_{N},

with ties broken arbitrarily.

The user observes the ranked list but not the underlying signals

Before inspecting the list, the user also does not observe the relevance of each individual item.
The user has an outside option and let
x_{b}\in\mathbb{R}
denote the relevance of this outside option.

At each decision epoch
\tau=0,1,2,\ldots,N
, the user may choose to costly inspect an item from the set of uninspected items
\mathcal{U}_{\tau}
, bearing an inspection cost
c>0
.

We adopt a
0

\tau

Inspecting item
i
reveals its true relevance
x_{i}
to the user. Alternatively, the user may choose to stop. If the user stops at epoch
\tau
, she takes the most relevant item she has ever inspected or her outside option. Let
\mathcal{I}_{\tau}
denote the set of already-inspected items and define the best available relevance so far,

M_{\tau}:=\max\Big(x_{b},\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\tau}}x_{i}\Big),\qquad M_{0}:=x_{b}.

Her realized payoff is simply
U=M_{\tau}-\tau c
.

Let
\theta_{x}
index the relevance distribution and
\theta_{e}
index the noise distribution of the retrieved items. Conditional on
(\theta_{x},\theta_{e})
, relevances
\{x_{i}\}
and noises
\{e_{i}\}
are independent, with
x_{i}\mid\theta_{x}\sim F(\cdot\mid\theta_{x})
and
e_{i}\mid\theta_{e}\sim G(\cdot\mid\theta_{e})
. In general, the user may hold priors on
\theta_{x}
and
\theta_{e}
.

Let
H_{\tau}
denote the user’s information at epoch
\tau
: the ranked list, the priors, and the revealed relevances
\{(i,x_{i}):i\in\mathcal{I}_{\tau}\}
in the order they were inspected. A policy is a mapping from histories to
\{\mathrm{stop}\}\cup\mathcal{U}_{\tau}
. The user maximizes
\mathbb{E}[U]
over measurable policies.

Inspecting an item serves two roles. First, a relevance that exceeds the running maximum
M_{\tau}
directly raises the user’s payoff. Second, even an item whose relevance falls below
M_{\tau}
sharpens the posterior on
\theta_{x}
, improving the user’s forecast of the remaining uninspected items. Continuation therefore has option value from both sources: the chance of finding a new best and the informational gain that refines future decisions.

At each epoch the user weighs this option value against the fixed cost
c
. Because the posterior and the running maximum both evolve with each inspection, the marginal benefit of the next inspection depends on the entire history
H_{\tau}
dynamically. The user needs to find the policy that optimally balances the declining marginal benefit of additional inspections against her constant cost.

A note on interpreting inspection.

The relevance-revealing event we call inspection does not have a single empirical analogue. One reading, implicit in the cascade family and its descendants
(
Craswell et al. 2008
;
Chuklin et al. 2022
)
, treats viewing a result on the SERP as the event, with a click read as a downstream signal of relevance or conversion. A second reading treats the click itself as part of inspection: a shopper who clicks through ten product pages to check whether any of them fits her need is sampling, not yet buying, and only the eventual checkout, booking, or long-dwell read counts as a conversion. We take no position. In our model, an inspection is whichever action resolves the latent relevance
x_{i}
of an item, and a conversion is whichever downstream event the user produces once she fixes on the item she will keep. A reader who prefers the first reading can identify
\mathcal{I}_{\tau}
with the set of viewed results and read clicks as conversion events; a reader who prefers the second can identify
\mathcal{I}_{\tau}
with the set of clicked items and read purchases or other completions as conversions. The reservation rule, the depth distribution, and the partial-identification results that follow go through under either reading; only the empirical mapping changes.

A Tractable Parametric Specification

To obtain a model that is both transparent and amenable to closed-form analysis, we impose the following simplifying assumptions.

Assumption 1

(Gaussian Specification)
.

Conditional on
\mu
, the sequences
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are each iid Gaussian and mutually independent, with

x_{i}\mid\mu\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma_{x}^{2}),\qquad e_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{e}^{2}),

and
\sigma_{x}^{2}
,
\sigma_{e}^{2}
fixed and known. The unknown parameter is the relevance mean, endowed with the conjugate prior

\mu\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{0},v_{0}),

where
m_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is the
prior mean
and
v_{0}>0
the
prior variance
of the relevance mean
\mu
shared by the retrieved items.

Throughout, let
\sigma_{z}^{2}:=\sigma_{x}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}
and define the
reliability ratio

\rho:=\frac{\sigma_{x}^{2}}{\sigma_{z}^{2}}\in(0,1).

The reliability ratio governs how informative the ranking is about true relevance. At
\rho\to 1
, the signal is noise-free and rank coincides with true relevance order; at
\rho\to 0
, the ranking is uninformative.

To avoid carrying the full joint law of order statistics as a latent state, we adopt the standard Thurstonian approximation in which the position of an item in the ranked list is mapped to a fixed quantile of the signal distribution.

Assumption 2

(Rank-to-Quantile Reduction)
.

The rank
i
of an item is identified with the
i/(N+1)
quantile from the top of the signal distribution:

z_{i}=\mu+\sigma_{z}q_{i},\qquad q_{i}:=\Phi^{-1}\left(1-\tfrac{i}{N+1}\right),

where
\Phi
denotes the standard Normal CDF.

The quantile sequence
\{q_{i}\}
is strictly decreasing in
i
and known. The reduction is exact as
N\to\infty
and provides an accurate approximation for moderate
N
. It plays the role of summarizing the ranking as a deterministic rank-specific shift. A derivation via the probability integral transform is provided in the Appendix.

Interior Inspection

Two summaries of the primitives will recur throughout the analysis: the
rank-specific shift

\alpha_{i}:=\rho\sigma_{z}q_{i}=\frac{\sigma_{x}^{2}}{\sigma_{z}}q_{i},

(1)

which is the expected boost in relevance from being placed at rank

, and the

\sigma_{\eta}^{2}:=\sigma_{x}^{2}(1-\rho)=\frac{\sigma_{x}^{2}\sigma_{e}^{2}}{\sigma_{z}^{2}},

(2)

the irreducible idiosyncratic noise that remains after extracting the rank shift. Both quantities will reappear formally in Lemma

To rule out the uninteresting corner case in which the user does not bother to inspect any item and walks away with the outside option, we impose a condition guaranteeing that the first inspection is worth it.

Assumption 3

(Interior Solution)
.

The fixed inspection cost satisfies

c<\sqrt{v_{0}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\phi(d_{0})-(x_{b}-m_{0}-\alpha_{1})\Phi(-d_{0}),

(3)

where
d_{0}:=(x_{b}-m_{0}-\alpha_{1})/\sqrt{v_{0}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}
and
\phi
denotes the standard Normal density function. Moreover, for any fixed

) holds for all sufficiently large

.

The right-hand side of (

if the realized

Bellman Equation and Optimal Stopping Rule

Combining the Gaussian conditional
x_{i}\mid\mu,z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\rho z_{i}+(1-\rho)\mu,\sigma_{x}^{2}(1-\rho))
with Assumption
2
yields the following lemma.

Lemma 1

(Rank Specific Predictive)
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
,

x_{i}\mid\mu\sim\mathcal{N}\big(\mu+\alpha_{i},\sigma_{\eta}^{2}\big)\quad\text{independent across }i,

where
\alpha_{i}
and
\sigma_{\eta}^{2}
are the rank-specific shift and residual variance defined in (
1
) and (
2
).

Throughout the paper, formal proofs are reserved in the Appendix. The lemma splits inspection into two parts that the user can reason about separately: a
known
adjustment that depends only on the rank and a
random
component whose distribution is the same everywhere on the list. Think of each rank as a position that comes pre-labelled with a bias correction
\alpha_{i}
; once you subtract it off, every position delivers an iid draw
y_{i}:=x_{i}-\alpha_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma_{\eta}^{2})
. The ranking thus tips the scale in favor of higher positions in a perfectly predictable way, and inspection is purely about learning the underlying relevance mean
\mu
.

What the rank-specific shift
\alpha_{i}
is (and is not).

The shift
\alpha_{i}
is a feature of the
position
, not of the item that landed in it. It is fixed as soon as the list is rendered: the user can write it down before looking at anything. It is positive at the top and negative at the bottom, summing to zero across the list. The ranking moves expected relevance toward the top without creating or destroying any in aggregate. The formula
\alpha_{i}=(\sigma_{x}^{2}/\sigma_{z})q_{i}
has a simple reading:
q_{i}
is the quantile of rank
i
on the latent score, and
\sigma_{x}^{2}/\sigma_{z}
is the slope of the best linear predictor of
x
from
z
. Multiplying them gives the expected change in
x
per rank slot. Since
\rho\in(0,1)
,
\alpha_{i}
is a shrunken version of the raw quantile
\sigma_{z}q_{i}
. The shrinkage factor
\rho
is the signal-to-noise ratio of the ranker. The scaling is exactly the Bayesian update of the prior mean given the one piece of information “this item is in position
i
.”

This setup makes the model analytically clean: all the subtlety of “how much should I trust the ranking” is absorbed into a set of constants that can be computed up front, leaving inspection to do one simple job, Bayesian updating on
\mu
.

Linear Traversal

On its face the user’s decision problem is combinatorial: at each step she must choose from up to
N
options to inspect. The next result shows this choice is in fact binary: she only decides between inspecting the highest-ranked uninspected item or stopping.

Lemma 2

(Rank-Order Dominance)
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, for every policy
\pi
there exists a rank-order policy
\pi^{\prime}
(i.e., one that, whenever it inspects, inspects the highest-ranked uninspected item) such that
\mathbb{E}[U(\pi^{\prime})]\geq\mathbb{E}[U(\pi)]
.

The reason behind this result is simple. Higher ranked slots give the user better expected relevance while each inspection comes with the same cost and the same information gain. Thus, inspecting the highest-ranked uninspected item dominates inspecting any other item.

Consequently, the problem collapses to a one-dimensional optimal stopping problem. An optimal rank-order policy is described by a single number: a stopping epoch
t\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
, where
t=0
means she stops immediately with the outside option.

\mu\mid H_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{t},v_{t}),

with

\frac{1}{v_{t}}=\frac{1}{v_{0}}+\frac{t}{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad m_{t}=v_{t}\left(\frac{m_{0}}{v_{0}}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\sum_{s=1}^{t}(x_{s}-\alpha_{s})\right).

Intuitively, the user strips the known rank-specific shift
\alpha_{s}
out of each revealed relevance to recover an iid signal of the underlying mean
\mu
, then averages these signals with her prior belief to update her belief.
The update admits a standard interpretation. Precisions (reciprocals of variances) are additive: each inspection contributes one unit of information worth
1/\sigma_{\eta}^{2}
to the precision, on top of the prior precision
1/v_{0}
. The posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of the prior mean
m_{0}
and the bias-corrected observations
x_{i}-\alpha_{i}
.

Corollary 4

(Posterior Predictive)
.

At
t\in\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
, the posterior predictive for the relevance of rank
t+1
prior to its inspection is

x_{t+1}\mid H_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\big(m_{t}+\alpha_{t+1},v_{t}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}\big).

Before inspecting rank
t+1
, the user’s best guess of its relevance is the sum of two components. The first,
m_{t}
, is her current posterior mean about the page relevance
\mu
; the second,
\alpha_{t+1}
, is the known rank-specific shift that captures how much higher (or lower) a rank-
(t+1)
item’s relevance should be relative to an average item, based purely on its position in the list. As the user inspects more items,
m_{t}
adjusts toward the true
\mu
while
\alpha_{t+1}
remains fixed as a pre-computed feature of rank
t+1
. Her uncertainty about
x_{t+1}
has two sources as well:
v_{t}
, which measures what she does not yet know about
\mu
and shrinks as she inspects more items, and
\sigma_{\eta}^{2}
, the residual idiosyncratic noise that cannot be learned away.

Bellman Equation

Under linear traversal, the sufficient state at epoch
t
is the pair
(M_{t},m_{t})
, where
M_{t}
is the best relevance revealed so far (including
x_{b}
) and
m_{t}
is the posterior mean. Let
V_{t}(M_{t},m_{t})
denote the value function at epoch
t\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
. It satisfies

V_{t}(M_{t},m_{t})=\max\left\{M_{t},\mathbb{E}_{x_{t+1}}\left[V_{t+1}\big(\max(M_{t},x_{t+1}),m_{t+1}(x_{t+1})\big)\right]-c\right\},

(4)

where the expectation is taken under

x_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{t}+\alpha_{t+1},v_{t}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}),

and the belief update given
x_{t+1}
is the affine map

m_{t+1}(x_{t+1})=\frac{v_{t+1}}{v_{t}}m_{t}+\frac{v_{t+1}}{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}(x_{t+1}-\alpha_{t+1}),\qquad v_{t+1}^{-1}=v_{t}^{-1}+\sigma_{\eta}^{-2}.

(5)

The terminal condition is
V_{N}(M_{N},m_{N})=M_{N}
.

The Bellman equation (
4
) makes the tradeoff the user faces at each epoch explicit. The left branch inside the max is the
stop
option: she walks away with the best relevance she has revealed to date,
M_{t}
. The right branch is the
inspect
option: she pays the deterministic cost
c
, reveals
x_{t+1}
, and transitions to a new state in which the current best becomes
\max(M_{t},x_{t+1})
and her posterior mean becomes
m_{t+1}(x_{t+1})
. The expected payoff from the right branch has two components of value beyond
M_{t}
: the direct
upside option
,
\mathbb{E}[(x_{t+1}-M_{t})^{+}]
, which captures the possibility that the inspected item beats the current best; and the
learning option
, which captures how observing
x_{t+1}
refines her belief about
\mu
and thereby improves future continuation decisions.

Optimal Stopping Rule

The Bellman equation (
4
) induces a stopping rule of remarkably simple form: at each epoch
t
, the user stops as soon as the difference between her best find and her current posterior mean,
M_{t}-m_{t}
, exceeds a deterministic, depth-dependent threshold. The decision depends on the running maximum
M_{t}
and the posterior mean
m_{t}
only through their difference, and on the user’s outside option
x_{b}
only insofar as it enters
M_{t}
.

Proposition 5

(Standout Stopping Rule)
.

For each
t\in\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
, there exists a deterministic constant
\kappa_{t}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}
, depending on
t
,
v_{0}
,
\sigma_{\eta}^{2}
,
c
,
\{\alpha_{s}\}_{s\geq t+1}
, and
N
but not on
m_{t}
or on the outside option
x_{b}
, such that the optimal policy stops at epoch
t
if and only if

M_{t}-m_{t}\geq\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star}.

(6)

Because the threshold (
6
) depends on
M_{t}
and
m_{t}
only through their difference, the decision-relevant scalar at every epoch is the gap between the best find so far and the posterior mean of the page. Throughout the paper we refer to this scalar as the
lead
:

L_{t}:=M_{t}-m_{t}.

The lead carries the entire content of the standout rule: the user continues whenever
L_{t}<\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star}
and stops otherwise.
3
3

3

We implicitly assume the user stops when she is indifferent. As before, under continuous distributions, the inequality is strict almost surely.

The linear form of (
6
) sharpens the qualitative picture by decomposing the user’s patience into two pieces. The rank shift
\alpha_{t+1}
is the user’s posterior expectation of how much the next item differs from an average one purely because of its position,
m_{t}+\alpha_{t+1}
is therefore the user’s expected relevance of the next item, and
\kappa_{t}^{\star}
is the additional margin she demands on top of that expected relevance before her current best is good enough to commit. The next section provides a closed-form expression for
\kappa_{t}
that makes its economic content transparent.

Empirically, holding the query fixed, stop probability is more predictable by the lead
L_{t}
than by
M_{t}
alone.
Three forces drive the lead toward the threshold as inspection proceeds, eventually tipping the rule in favor of stopping. First, every additional inspection can only raise
M_{t}
, while
m_{t}
is a martingale under the predictive measure, so the lead on average trends upward over time. Second, the rank-specific shift
\alpha_{t+1}
is strictly decreasing in
t
(on average the items ahead are less promising), so the bar that the lead must clear falls as the user moves down the list. Third, the residual uncertainty about
\mu
shrinks with each inspection. This lowers the option value of further learning and pulls the intercept
\kappa_{t}^{\star}
down toward a noise floor that depends only on
\sigma_{\eta}^{2}
and the remaining horizon. Costs, by contrast, remain constant per inspection. The user optimally stops at the first rank where her accumulated lead clears a threshold that signals a further inspection is not worth it.

4
Closed-Form Specializations

Myopic Policy

A tractable lower bound on inspection, and a useful heuristic, is the one-step look-ahead (myopic) policy: inspect if the expected one-step gain from inspection exceeds the cost, treating the next step as if it were terminal. Even though the myopic policy is not optimal, it shares the same solution format as the optimal policy and admits a closed form for
\kappa_{t}
. Analyzing the closed-form solution offers additional qualitative insights.

To state the result, define

g(d):=\phi(d)-d\Phi(-d),

(7)

which Lemma
A2
shows is a strictly decreasing bijection from
\mathbb{R}
onto
(0,\infty)
, so
g^{-1}
is well defined.

Proposition 6

(Myopic Policy)
.

Let
\sigma_{t}^{\star 2}:=v_{t}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}
denote the one-step predictive variance after
t
inspections. Under the myopic policy, the user stops at epoch
t
if and only if

M_{t}-m_{t}\geq\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t},\qquad\kappa_{t}:=\sigma_{t}^{\star}g^{-1}\left(c/\sigma_{t}^{\star}\right)

(8)

and the intercept
\kappa_{t}
is strictly decreasing in
t
, with

\kappa_{0}=\sqrt{v_{0}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}g^{-1}\left(\tfrac{c}{\sqrt{v_{0}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}}\right),\qquad\lim_{t\to\infty}\kappa_{t}=\sigma_{\eta}g^{-1}\left(c/\sigma_{\eta}\right)=:\kappa_{\infty}.

(9)

As in Proposition
5
, the constant
\kappa_{t}
is the option value of one more inspection, expressed as a margin: the user stops only when her current best find exceeds the expected value of the next item,
m_{t}+\alpha_{t+1}
, by at least
\kappa_{t}
. The margin
\kappa_{t}
is strictly positive even in the absence of learning: a user who knew
\mu
exactly would still demand
\kappa_{\infty}=\sigma_{\eta}g^{-1}(c/\sigma_{\eta})
to compensate for the residual idiosyncratic noise in the next draw. What learning adds is a surcharge
\kappa_{t}-\kappa_{\infty}>0
, with
\kappa_{0}>\kappa_{1}>\cdots>\kappa_{\infty}
. The mechanism is the predictive-variance decomposition

\sigma_{t}^{\star 2}=\underbrace{v_{t}}_{\text{ignorance about }\mu}+\underbrace{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}_{\text{item-level noise}}.

The first term, posterior uncertainty about
\mu
, fattens the upper tail of the next item’s distribution and inflates the option value of one more inspection; it reduces to zero by
v_{t+1}^{-1}=v_{t}^{-1}+\sigma_{\eta}^{-2}
, leaving only the irreducible noise floor
\sigma_{\eta}^{2}
. Consequently, a stopping model that abstracts from Bayesian learning ignores the surcharge
\kappa_{t}-\kappa_{\infty}
at every epoch, and, holding the per-step inspection cost fixed, predicts shallower inspection runs than the Bayesian benchmark. The cascade family
(
Craswell et al. 2008
;
Chapelle and Zhang 2009
;
Dupret and Piwowarski 2008
;
Chuklin et al. 2022
)
sidesteps this gap by calibrating a position-dependent continuation probability directly to log data, absorbing the option value into a reduced-form parameter rather than treating it as a structural object.

Two Regimes of the First Stop

Before tackling the full distribution of inspection depth, it is worth zooming in on the simplest and highly visible case: the user inspects the top item once and then leaves. Empirically, this may be interpreted as a bounce from analyzing log data. Proposition
5
gives a clean closed form for how often this happens, and, more importantly, shows that a single bounce rate hides three very different stories about what the user just experienced.

Define
h(x_{1}):=M_{1}-m_{1}-\alpha_{2}-\kappa_{1}^{\star}
. Let
\omega_{1}:=v_{0}/(v_{0}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2})\in(0,1)
denote the Bayesian weight on the first observation, so
m_{1}(x_{1})=(1-\omega_{1})m_{0}+\omega_{1}(x_{1}-\alpha_{1})
and
M_{1}(x_{1})=\max(x_{b},x_{1})
. Substituting them into
h(x_{1})
gives

h(x_{1})=\begin{cases}-\omega_{1}x_{1}+x_{b}+\omega_{1}\alpha_{1}-(1-\omega_{1})m_{0}-\alpha_{2}-\kappa_{1}^{\star}&\text{if }x_{1}\leq x_{b}\\
(1-\omega_{1})x_{1}+\omega_{1}\alpha_{1}-(1-\omega_{1})m_{0}-\alpha_{2}-\kappa_{1}^{\star}&\text{if }x_{1}>x_{b}\end{cases},

a V-shape in
x_{1}
with kink at
x_{1}=x_{b}
. The user stops at
\tau=1
exactly on
\{h(x_{1})\geq 0\}
, so the sign of
h(x_{b})
partitions her first-stop behavior into two regimes.

Proposition 7

(One Inspection Probability)
.

Exactly one of the following two cases applies:

(a)

Trust regime:
if
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}\geq\kappa_{1}^{\star}+(1-\omega_{1})(m_{0}+\alpha_{1}-x_{b})
,
\Pr(\tau=1)=1
. That is, the user stops at
\tau=1
for every realization of
x_{1}
.

(b)

Two-faced explore regime:
if
h(x_{b})<0
, define

s_{1}^{-}:=m_{0}+\alpha_{1}-\frac{m_{0}+\alpha_{2}+\kappa_{1}^{\star}-x_{b}}{\omega_{1}},\qquad s_{1}^{+}:=m_{0}+\alpha_{1}+\frac{\kappa_{1}^{\star}-(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})}{1-\omega_{1}}.

(10)

Then
s_{1}^{-}<x_{b}<s_{1}^{+}
are the two solutions of
h(\cdot)=0
, and

\Pr(\tau=1)=\underbrace{\Phi\left(\frac{s_{1}^{-}-m_{0}-\alpha_{1}}{\sqrt{v_{0}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}}\right)}_{\text{cut-losses branch: }x_{1}\leq s_{1}^{-}}+\underbrace{\Phi\left(\frac{m_{0}+\alpha_{1}-s_{1}^{+}}{\sqrt{v_{0}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}}\right)}_{\text{commit branch: }x_{1}\geq s_{1}^{+}}.

(11)

Qualitatively, the trust regime is the case in which the platform’s ranking has earned the user’s trust. Whether a search lies in this regime depends only on query-time primitives (
\omega_{1}
,
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
,
x_{b}
,
m_{0}
,
\kappa_{1}^{\star}
), not on the realized
x_{1}
. The condition splits cleanly: the rank-1 advantage
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}
must clear
\kappa_{1}^{\star}
plus a buffer
(1-\omega_{1})(m_{0}+\alpha_{1}-x_{b})
sized for the worst case
x_{1}=x_{b}
. The buffer’s size depends on
\omega_{1}
, the share of a single observation’s surprise that updates the posterior. With
\omega_{1}
near
1
(diffuse prior), a low
x_{1}
also lowers her expectation of
\mu
and hence of rank
2
, so the buffer is small. With
\omega_{1}
near
0
(sharp prior), the same
x_{1}
is dismissed as item-level noise, rank
2
still looks promising, and the buffer is large.

The implication is striking: in the trust regime, the user already plans to skip rank
2
even before she has typed a single character. She still “bothers to inspect” rank
1
, but only to learn what
x_{1}
is; after that single look she chooses between
x_{1}
and her outside option without moving down the list.

The bimodality of case (b) also has a transparent interpretation. The continuation interval
(s_{1}^{-},s_{1}^{+})
is the set of
mediocre
first observations: items that are neither good enough to commit on nor bad enough to walk away from. Outside this interval the user stops, but for opposite reasons. When
x_{1}
is high (above
s_{1}^{+}
), she stops because she has
found a winner
: rank
1
is so good that it is unlikely the rest of the list contains anything better worth paying
c
to look for. When
x_{1}
is low (below
s_{1}^{-}
), she stops because she is
cutting losses
: rank
1
being this disappointing tells her the whole list is probably weak, so the outside option
x_{b}
becomes the safer bet over paying more inspection costs to confirm a pessimistic view.

This observational equivalence complicates empirical inference. For example, when an inspection is operationalized as a click, a user who inspects only the first item and then exits generates a single-click session regardless of whether she converted or gave up. Unless the system also has access to the conversion event (e.g., an e-commerce website), a high single-click session rate does not, by itself, signal satisfaction or disappointment. This is one source of the long-standing difficulty in interpreting one-click rates as relevance or satisfaction signals
(
Joachims et al. 2005
;
Hassan et al. 2010
)
.

The same first-stop geometry also gives a precise sense in which a successful ranker can starve the system of deeper observations. To state the result cleanly, index ranker quality by the reliability ratio
\rho
. Hold
\sigma_{x}^{2}
fixed and let the ranker noise vary with
\rho
:

\sigma_{e}^{2}(\rho)=\sigma_{x}^{2}\frac{1-\rho}{\rho},\qquad\sigma_{\eta}^{2}(\rho)=\sigma_{x}^{2}(1-\rho),\qquad\alpha_{i}(\rho)=\sigma_{x}\sqrt{\rho}q_{i}.

Then
\rho\uparrow 1
is the limit in which the score becomes noise-free, the ranking coincides with true relevance order under the quantile reduction, and the residual uncertainty around each rank vanishes.

Corollary 8

(The Winner’s Curse)
.

Fix
N\geq 2
,
c>0
,
\sigma_{x}>0
,
v_{0}>0
, and
m_{0},x_{b}\in\mathbb{R}
. Suppose Assumption
3
holds for all
\rho
sufficiently close to one.
4
4

4

This is a nonempty restriction: it holds when inspection is sufficiently cheap, when the outside option is sufficiently unattractive, or when the prior variance
v_{0}
is sufficiently large (Lemma
A4
).
Let
\tau_{\rho}
be the optimal inspection depth in the environment with reliability ratio
\rho
. Then there exists
\bar{\rho}<1
such that, for every
\rho\in(\bar{\rho},1)
,

\Pr_{\rho}(\tau_{\rho}=1)=1.

Equivalently,
\Pr_{\rho}(\tau_{\rho}\geq i)=0
for every
i\geq 2
. That is, a sufficiently reliable ranker generates no observations below the top slot in equilibrium.

The limit case
\rho=1
is the cleanest version of the mechanism. When
\rho=1
, the ranking signal has no noise. Under the quantile approximation, the list is truly ordered by relevance, and a single inspection of rank
1
pins down both the page mean
\mu
and the position-induced shift at every other rank. Every lower-ranked item is therefore known to be worse than the one the user has just seen. A further inspection cannot improve the terminal maximum but still costs
c>0
, so stopping after one inspection is strictly optimal.

The corollary extends this conclusion to a neighborhood of
\rho=1
by continuity. The trust condition of Proposition
7
holds at the limit with a strictly positive slack, and therefore on an open neighborhood of
\rho=1
. On that neighborhood the user stops at rank
1
for every realization of
x_{1}
.

This is the formal version of the winner’s curse claim. A successful ranker makes rank
1
so decisive that a rational user never looks below it, and the log records only rank-
1
observations. The lower-ranked outcomes the platform would need to audit the remaining mistakes, evaluate counterfactual promotions, or train on near misses are exactly the ones that an effective ranker stops producing. The data problem is not biased logging or model misuse. It is the equilibrium consequence of rational stopping against a ranker that has become good enough, and it tightens precisely as the ranker improves. The corollary isolates the trust-channel limit; at intermediate
\rho
, the explore regime’s commit branch from Proposition
7
reinforces the same concentration of inspections at the top, and is fully absorbed into the trust regime as
\rho\uparrow 1
.

The starvation is not an artifact of the noise-free corner. For every
\rho<1
, the top signal is informative but not conclusive, so a lower-ranked item can still beat rank
1
. Once the trust condition holds, however, rank
2
is never inspected, and these residual inversions never enter the log. Their unconditional probability shrinks as
\rho\uparrow 1
, but they are exactly the errors a purely observational log is least equipped to detect: they live in the part of the list the user has, rationally, declined to look at.

Uninformed User

In many cases, people come to the search engine because they want to learn something new. Hence, a natural limiting case of great real world relevance is the
uninformed
user: one who enters the system with no informative prior on
\mu
and must infer the relevance scale entirely from the items she inspects.

Formally, this is the diffuse prior limit
v_{0}\to\infty
, in which the prior contributes negligible precision and the posterior is driven almost entirely by the observed items.

Proposition 9

(Diffuse Prior)
.

In the limit
v_{0}\to\infty
, the posterior mean of Proposition
3
converges almost surely to the bias-corrected sample mean
\bar{y}_{t}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}(x_{s}-\alpha_{s})/t
.
The reservation rule of Proposition
5
accordingly reads

\text{stop at }t\iff M_{t}-\bar{y}_{t}\geq\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star}.

(12)

The diffuse prior deepens the qualitative content of Proposition
5
by exposing a feature specifically tied to the absence of an absolute relevance scale: the policy is translation-invariant in latent relevance. Shifting every
x
(including
x_{b}
) by a common constant
a
shifts
M_{t}
and
\bar{y}_{t}
by the same
a
, so the lead
M_{t}-\bar{y}_{t}
is unchanged and the stopping decision depends only on relative quantities. This invariance is special to the diffuse limit: under any proper prior,
m_{t}
pools each observation with the fixed prior mean
m_{0}
at Bayesian weight strictly less than one, so a common shift of the observed
x_{s}
moves
m_{t}
by less than
a
and the lead is not preserved. With no prior anchor, the user infers the scale on the fly through
\bar{y}_{t}
, stopping as soon as her best find sticks out above her own evolving baseline. Two pages that differ by an additive shift in latent relevance therefore generate identical inspection paths.

This explains a stylized fact that absolute-threshold theories struggle with: the same user accepts very different absolute quality across queries, because her bar moves with the empirical distribution of items she encounters. The eye-tracking evidence of
Joachims et al. 2005
pointed in the same direction by arguing that clicks reveal
relative
preferences within a session rather than absolute relevance labels, and
Fang et al. 2019
accommodates this fact by letting per-session continuation parameters absorb the across-query variation. Our model derives the moving bar as an equilibrium object of rational stopping rather than fitting it as a calibrated nuisance. Operationally, a system that delivers one standout above a homogeneous middle ends inspection faster than one that delivers many uniformly good items, even if the second has the higher mean and the higher max.

The diffuse prior also sharpens the two-regime structure of the first stop identified in Proposition
7
. The following corollary shows that under a diffuse prior, the commit channel of the explore regime is killed entirely, leaving only the cut-losses branch.

Corollary 10

(One Inspection Probability Under Diffuse Prior)
.

In the limit
v_{0}\to\infty
, the explore regime of Proposition
7
collapses to its cut-losses branch (
s_{1}^{+}\to+\infty
) such that

\Pr(\tau=1\mid\mu)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}\geq\kappa_{1}^{\star},\\[2.0pt]
\Phi\left(\dfrac{x_{b}-\mu-\alpha_{2}-\kappa_{1}^{\star}}{\sigma_{\eta}}\right)&\text{if }\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}<\kappa_{1}^{\star}.\end{cases}

The trust regime survives intact: when the rank-1 advantage
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}
is large enough, the user stops for every realization of
x_{1}
. But note that even in the diffuse prior, her willingness to stop does not hinge on seeing a good draw; it is guaranteed
before

x_{1}
is realized, purely by the rank-1 advantage.

In the explore regime the asymmetry is starker. Recall from Proposition
7
that the user in this regime can stop after one inspection for two opposite reasons: she found a winner (commit), or she saw something so bad that the outside option looks safer (cut losses). The diffuse prior makes
s_{1}^{+}\to+\infty
, killing the commit branch and leaving only the cut-losses branch. A user with no prior anchor cannot be
convinced
by a single good draw that she has found the best item: because the posterior mean
m_{1}\to x_{1}-\alpha_{1}
tracks the observation almost one-for-one, a high
x_{1}
raises both her estimate of the current item and her estimate of what the remaining items might yield, so the gap that would trigger early commitment never opens. She can, however, be
discouraged
: a sufficiently poor
x_{1}
pushes her posterior mean below
x_{b}
by more than
\alpha_{2}+\kappa_{1}^{\star}
, at which point the outside option dominates. A first stop in the diffuse explore regime is therefore unambiguously a defeat.

5
Inspection Depth

The optimal policy of Proposition
5
reduces a two-dimensional state to a one-line decision: stop at epoch
t
as soon as the lead
L_{t}
clears the threshold
\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star}
. This section delivers the characterization of the full distribution. The dynamics seemingly live on the two-dimensional state
(M_{t},m_{t})
, but the lead
L_{t}=M_{t}-m_{t}
is itself a one-dimensional Markov chain with a closed-form one-step transition density. The recursion that follows exposes the mechanism of SERP view: a tug-of-war between a rising running max and a refining posterior mean.

For the remainder of the section, write

\xi_{t}:=x_{t}-(m_{t-1}+\alpha_{t})

for the user’s
surprise
at epoch
t
, the difference between the realized draw and her conditional expectation
m_{t-1}+\alpha_{t}
. By Corollary
4
, conditional on the history
H_{t-1}
the surprise is mean-zero Gaussian,
\xi_{t}\mid H_{t-1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{t-1}^{\star 2})
, and the sequence
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
is conditionally independent across epochs.

Let
\omega_{t}:=v_{t-1}/(v_{t-1}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2})\in(0,1)
denote the Bayesian weight on the
t
-th observation. The posterior update can be written as
m_{t}-m_{t-1}=\omega_{t}\xi_{t}
, so
\omega_{t}
is the fraction of the surprise that the user folds into her posterior mean about
\mu
. The Bayesian weight
\omega_{t}
is large when she is still uncertain (
v_{t-1}
large) and her belief tracks new draws aggressively, small when her uncertainty has shrunk and she barely moves. The sequence
\{\omega_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
is deterministic, strictly decreasing in
t
, and tends to zero as
t\to\infty
.

By Proposition
5
, the user stops at epoch
t
exactly when
L_{t}\geq\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star}
, with the convention
\alpha_{N+1}+\kappa_{N}^{\star}:=-\infty
so the user is forced to stop at the horizon. The next lemma reduces the dynamics to the lead alone.

Lemma 11

(Lead Chain)
.

The lead
L_{t}
is a Markov chain on
\mathbb{R}
, with one-step recursion

L_{t}=\max\bigl(L_{t-1},\alpha_{t}+\xi_{t}\bigr)-\omega_{t}\xi_{t}.

(13)

The conditional law of
L_{t}
given
L_{t-1}=l
is supported on
[L_{\min}(l),\infty)
, where
L_{\min}(l):=(1-\omega_{t})l+\omega_{t}\alpha_{t}
, and on this support has density

f_{L_{t}\mid L_{t-1}=l}(y)=\frac{1}{(1-\omega_{t})\sigma_{t-1}^{\star}}\phi\left(\frac{y-\alpha_{t}}{(1-\omega_{t})\sigma_{t-1}^{\star}}\right)+\frac{1}{\omega_{t}\sigma_{t-1}^{\star}}\phi\left(\frac{l-y}{\omega_{t}\sigma_{t-1}^{\star}}\right).

(14)

The recursion (
13
) is piecewise affine in
\xi_{t}
. The kink sits at the surprise level
\xi^{\star}_{t}:=L_{t-1}-\alpha_{t}
, at which the new draw
x_{t}
exactly ties the running maximum
M_{t-1}
. Above the kink the new draw beats the running maximum, and below it the new draw falls short. The two branches read

L_{t}=\begin{cases}L_{t-1}-\omega_{t}\xi_{t}&\text{if }\xi_{t}\leq\xi^{\star}_{t}\quad(\text{disappointment: }x_{t}\leq M_{t-1}),\\
\alpha_{t}+(1-\omega_{t})\xi_{t}&\text{if }\xi_{t}>\xi^{\star}_{t}\quad(\text{discovery: }x_{t}>M_{t-1}).\end{cases}

(15)

To read off what each branch does, recall that the lead change at any inspection has two parts: how the running maximum moves, and how the posterior mean moves. The branches of (
15
) simply track which of the two is doing the moving.

Consider first a
disappointment
(
x_{t}\leq M_{t-1}
). The new draw fails to beat the running maximum, so the first half of the lead is frozen:
M_{t}=M_{t-1}
. The posterior, however, still updates according to
m_{t}=m_{t-1}+\omega_{t}\xi_{t}
. In this case, the user folds a fraction
\omega_{t}
of the surprise into her belief about
\mu
. The lead inherits the opposite shift,

L_{t}-L_{t-1}=(M_{t}-M_{t-1})-(m_{t}-m_{t-1})=-\omega_{t}\xi_{t}.

The sign of the change depends on which side of her expectation the draw fell on. If the draw came in below expectation (
\xi_{t}<0
), the user revises her belief about
\mu
downward, her posterior mean drops, and the lead grows by
\omega_{t}|\xi_{t}|
. If the draw came in above expectation but still short of the running maximum (
0<\xi_{t}\leq L_{t-1}-\alpha_{t}
), the user revises upward, her posterior rises, and the lead decreases by
\omega_{t}\xi_{t}
. Either way the move is shrunk by the Bayesian weight
\omega_{t}
. A converged user (small
\omega_{t}
) barely revises and the lead barely moves; an early user (large
\omega_{t}
) revises sharply and the lead can swing meaningfully in either direction.

Consider next a
discovery
(
x_{t}>M_{t-1}
). Now both halves of the lead move. The running maximum jumps onto the new draw,
M_{t}=x_{t}
, and the posterior shifts by
\omega_{t}\xi_{t}
as before. Substituting,

L_{t}=M_{t}-m_{t}=(x_{t}-m_{t-1})-\omega_{t}\xi_{t}=\alpha_{t}+(1-\omega_{t})\xi_{t},

with
L_{t-1}
dropping out completely. Therefore, a discovery
overwrites
the previous lead: the new value depends only on the rank-shift
\alpha_{t}
and the fraction
1-\omega_{t}
of the surprise that is not absorbed into the posterior. The remaining fraction
\omega_{t}
of the surprise rolls into the user’s belief about
\mu
and so cancels out of the lead. A converged user (small
\omega_{t}
) keeps almost the entire surprise of a big discovery as added lead and is therefore much more likely to stop at the next epoch; an early user (large
\omega_{t}
) sees most of the upside vanish into her posterior, her best find rises and so does her expectations about what is still to come, and is correspondingly less impressed.

The two branches contribute the two terms in the transition density (
14
). The latter term is the disappointment term. It has scale
\omega_{t}\sigma_{t-1}^{\star}
around
L_{t-1}
. On a disappointment the running maximum is frozen, so the lead moves only because the belief moves, and the magnitude of that move is the Bayesian weight (
\omega_{t}
) times the typical surprise (
\sigma_{t-1}^{\star}
). The scale is large for an early user who is still revising aggressively, and small for a converged user whose belief barely budges. The discovery term has scale
(1-\omega_{t})\sigma_{t-1}^{\star}
around
\alpha_{t}
. It represents the unabsorbed surprise. On a discovery the running maximum jumps by the full surprise, but a fraction
\omega_{t}
of it is absorbed into the posterior and cancels out of the lead, leaving the residual fraction
1-\omega_{t}
to register as added lead. The scale is small for an early user, who folds most of any windfall into her expectations, and large for a converged user, whose lead inherits almost the entire surprise.

Lemma
11
, albeit sharp, does not directly speak to how each inspection’s revelation engages with the user’s continuation and stopping decision. Fortunately, it is straightforward to translate lead recursion back to item relevance using
x_{t}=m_{t-1}+\alpha_{t}+\xi_{t}
, through which we recover the V-shape stopping geometry of Proposition
7
for every epoch.

Proposition 12

(Conditional Continuation)
.

Define
r_{t}:=\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star}
. For an epoch
t\geq 1
and a history
H_{t-1}
on which the user has reached rank
t
,

(a)

Dynamic trust regime:
if
r_{t}\leq(1-\omega_{t})L_{t-1}+\omega_{t}\alpha_{t}
, then the user stops at rank
t
for every realization of
x_{t}
.

(b)

Dynamic explore regime:
if
r_{t}>(1-\omega_{t})L_{t-1}+\omega_{t}\alpha_{t}
, then the user continues when

\underline{x}_{t}(H_{t-1})<x_{t}<\overline{x}_{t}(H_{t-1}),

(16)

where

\underline{x}_{t}(H_{t-1}):=m_{t-1}+\alpha_{t}+\frac{L_{t-1}-r_{t}}{\omega_{t}},\qquad\overline{x}_{t}(H_{t-1}):=m_{t-1}+\alpha_{t}+\frac{r_{t}-\alpha_{t}}{1-\omega_{t}}.

Proposition
12
promotes the two-regime structure of Proposition
7
from a closed-form first-step result into a generic feature of every epoch. The dynamic trust regime says that, before the user has inspected rank
t
, her history has already locked in stopping, exactly the way the
t=1
trust regime locks in stopping before
x_{1}
is realized. In the dynamic explore regime, continuation past rank
t
means
x_{t}
landed in the middle: not so low as to make the outside option look safer (cut losses), not so high as to make rank
t
a standout worth committing to. The endpoints are explicit functions of the user’s state
(m_{t-1},L_{t-1})
and the structural threshold
r_{t}
.

The behavioral story behind Proposition
12
is similar to the first item’s. A very low draw confirms that the page is weak and tips the user toward her outside option; a very high draw delivers a standout whose lead beats the option value of further search; the intermediate range is the set of items that neither resolve the question nor settle it. The arresting implication is that, with history fixed, a user who saw a mediocre rank-
t
item is more likely to keep going than a user who saw an excellent one. No monotone click model
(
Craswell et al. 2008
;
Chapelle and Zhang 2009
;
Dupret and Piwowarski 2008
)
can match this shape, because in those models satisfaction with the inspected item only ever increases the probability of stopping; the mediocre-driven continuation channel is absent by assumption. If the platform has any noisy proxy for realized rank-
t
relevance, the rational-user model predicts a nonmonotonic relationship between that proxy and continuation to rank
t+1
, and the cascade family predicts monotonicity. The two are distinguishable in the data without any need to manipulate the ranker.

The upper endpoint
\overline{x}_{t}=m_{t-1}+\alpha_{t}+(r_{t}-\alpha_{t})/(1-\omega_{t})
depends on the Bayesian weight
\omega_{t}=v_{t-1}/(v_{t-1}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2})
, which is large when prior uncertainty is high. The mechanism is the one already seen in the diffuse-prior collapse of Proposition
7
: when the user is still uncertain about the page mean, a single high draw raises her belief about the rest of the list almost as much as it raises the running max, and the lead barely moves. The commitment tail is then unavailable to her, because no single observation can be enough to convince her that she has reached the best the list has to offer. As belief precision rises with inspections, the upper threshold descends from infinity toward a finite value, and the commitment tail opens up. The model accordingly predicts an attenuated upper truncation when the user’s prior is diffuse (novel queries, or users new to the topic) and a more symmetric inverted-U when her prior is tight (familiar queries, or experienced users). Two natural cross-section tests follow: comparing the shape of continuation against relevance proxies across queries of varying familiarity, and tracking how that shape changes within a single search as the user’s prior tightens.

Let
\nu_{t}
denote the law of
L_{t}
on the survival event
\{\tau\geq t\}
, with
\nu_{0}=\delta_{x_{b}-m_{0}}
. The surviving population
\nu_{t}
fans out in the early epochs as different users experience different draw sequences, and contracts later as the stopping region
[\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star},\infty)
absorbs mass. The Markov structure of
L_{t}
yields a clean recursion for the stopping probabilities
\Pr(\tau=t)
.

Proposition 13

(Stopping Time Recursion)
.

Let
K_{t}^{L}
denote the transition kernel of the lead chain (Lemma
11
). For
t=0,1,\ldots,N-1
:

\displaystyle\Pr(\tau=t)

\displaystyle=\nu_{t}\bigl([\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star},\infty)\bigr),

(17)

\displaystyle\nu_{t+1}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=\int_{(-\infty,\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star})}K_{t}^{L}(l,\cdot)\nu_{t}(dl),

(18)

and
\Pr(\tau=N)=\nu_{N}(\mathbb{R})
. The masses
\Pr(\tau=0),\ldots,\Pr(\tau=N)
sum to one.

The recursion form is better suited for simulation and theoretical manipulation: it tracks a single one-dimensional law
\nu_{t}
across epochs and advances it by one transition at a time. For empirical work, where one wants
\Pr(\tau=t)
as a function of model parameters whose gradient can be backpropagated through, Section

develops an equivalent representation as a truncated multivariate Gaussian probability.

6
Engineering Changes: Short Run versus Long Run

A common deployment pattern in search and recommendation systems is to roll out an engineering change (e.g., a new ranker, a richer retrieval index, or a different deduplication policy) and measure its effect in a (relatively) short A/B test before deciding whether to ship. The model has something specific to say about how inspection depth moves in such a rollout, and the answer depends, in a way that matters empirically, on whether users have absorbed the change yet. At the beginning of the A/B-test window, the user has not yet adjusted: her prior is still anchored at the old relevance level. After enough exposure her prior catches up. The two phases respond differently to the same change, and the two responses can disagree in sign even for small relevance changes.

To make this concrete, we formalize the following stylized case. In a steady-state baseline, users have already learned the pre-change average relevance, so the true page mean equals the prior mean:
\mu=m_{0}
. We model an engineering change as a uniform additive shift
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}
in the true relevance mean of every retrieved page; the post-change page mean is therefore
m_{0}+\Delta
. Whether
\Delta
is positive (a relevance lift) or negative (a regression), the change reaches users in two distinct phases. Throughout the A/B-test window, the user’s prior remains anchored at the old mean
m_{0}
even though the true page mean has moved to
m_{0}+\Delta
. After enough exposure, her prior catches up: she enters the search with a prior centered at
m_{0}+\Delta
, while the outside option
x_{b}
stays where it was.

Throughout the section, fix
N\geq 2
,
\sigma_{x}>0
,
\sigma_{e}>0
,
c>0
,
v_{0}\in(0,\infty)
, and
m_{0},x_{b}\in\mathbb{R}
; suppose Assumption
3
holds at the baseline; and let
\tau^{\star}
denote the stopping time induced by the optimal policy of Proposition
5
, applied with the prior the user is using. To compare phases, write

T(\mu,m_{0},x_{b}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mu,m_{0},x_{b}}[\tau^{\star}]

for the expected inspection depth induced by the optimal Bayesian policy computed from prior
\mu\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{0},v_{0})
and outside option
x_{b}
, conditional on a realized true page mean of
\mu
. The pre-change baseline is
T(m_{0},m_{0},x_{b})
. The two phases produce

\SR(\Delta):=T(m_{0}+\Delta,m_{0},x_{b})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\LR(\Delta):=T(m_{0}+\Delta,m_{0}+\Delta,x_{b}),

the short-run depth (mean has moved, prior has not) and the long-run depth (mean and prior have moved, outside option has not). Both admit clean characterizations.

Short-Run Depth is Hump-Shaped in the Shift

A first observation: very large engineering shifts of
either sign
collapse the short-run depth to a single inspection.

Proposition 14

(Hump in Short-Run Depth)
.

The map
\Delta\mapsto\SR(\Delta)
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
, and

\lim_{\Delta\to-\infty}\SR(\Delta)=\lim_{\Delta\to+\infty}\SR(\Delta)=1.

Hence, if
\SR(\bar{\Delta})>1
for some
\bar{\Delta}\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\Delta\mapsto\SR(\Delta)
is non-monotone and attains its maximum at some finite interior
\Delta^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}
.

To see the mechanism, recall that under the old-prior policy the user’s thresholds depend only on the prior
(m_{0},v_{0})
, the noise structure, and the rank quantiles, so the policy is fixed regardless of
\Delta
. The first item is realized as
x_{1}=m_{0}+\Delta+\alpha_{1}+\eta_{1}
with
\eta_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\eta}^{2})
. The user’s first-stop function is the V-shape
h(x_{1})
from Proposition
7
: she stops at
\tau^{\star}=1
exactly on
\{h(x_{1})\geq 0\}
. When
\Delta\gg 0
,
x_{1}
is very likely well above
x_{b}
, the upper branch of
h
applies, and the user
commits
after one inspection. When
\Delta\ll 0
,
x_{1}
is almost surely well below
x_{b}
, the lower branch applies, and the user
cuts losses
after one inspection. Deeper inspection occurs only at moderate
\Delta
, where
x_{1}
lands in the V-shape’s continuation interval.

Two opposite deployments leave the same fingerprint. Whether the page has suffered a big regression (
\Delta\ll 0
) or enjoyed a big quality lift (
\Delta\gg 0
), A/B sessions collapse to a single inspection, with one user bailing out and another jackpot-stopping. Read inspections as clicks and both scenarios produce the same dashboard: clicks tank, single-click sessions skyrocket. A naive analyst who reads this as an unconditionally bad signal kills the experiment, even when the change under the hood is a transformative lift.

Long-Run Depth is Monotone in the Shift

In the long run, the depth movement reduces to how depth depends on the outside option. The optimal policy is invariant under a joint additive shift of the prior mean, the true mean, and the outside option:
T(\mu+a,m_{0}+a,x_{b}+a)=T(\mu,m_{0},x_{b})
for every
a\in\mathbb{R}
. The reason is that shifting the prior mean alongside the observations moves the posterior mean
m_{t}
by exactly
a
at every epoch, so the lead
L_{t}
and the reservation threshold
\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star}
are both preserved. Setting
a=-\Delta
aligns
\LR(\Delta)
with the baseline prior and absorbs the entire shift into the outside option. A coupling argument in the appendix then shows that depth is monotone in the outside option.

Proposition 15

(Long-Run Depth: Reduction to the Outside Option)
.

For every
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}
,

\LR(\Delta)=T(m_{0},m_{0},x_{b}-\Delta),

(19)

and
\Delta\mapsto\LR(\Delta)
is weakly increasing on
\mathbb{R}
.

Once users have learned the new relevance level, the surprise channel of Proposition
14
has shut off. The only force left is that the unchanged outside option is now relatively worse if relevance rose and relatively better if relevance fell. An improvement therefore makes search look better against a relatively worse outside option, so users weakly inspect more deeply; a deterioration weakly shallows inspection, possibly all the way to the no-inspection corner if the move is large enough.

Short-Run versus Long-Run Signs

The long-run sign always tracks the sign of the relevance change, by Proposition
15
. The short-run sign need not, even for small shocks. The comparison is pinned down by a derivative calculation at the baseline.

Proposition 16

(Short- and Long-Run Disagreement)
.

Let
\eta_{i}:=x_{i}-\mu-\alpha_{i}
denote the residuals from Lemma
1
(so
\eta_{i}=x_{i}-m_{0}-\alpha_{i}
under the baseline measure
\mu=m_{0}
). Then
\SR
is differentiable at
\Delta=0
with

\SR^{\prime}(0)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{m_{0}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\tau^{\star}-1}(\tau^{\star}-i)\eta_{i}\right],

where the sum is zero on the event
\{\tau^{\star}=1\}
by convention. If
\SR^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for every
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}
with
0<|\Delta|<\varepsilon
,

\operatorname{sign}\bigl(\SR(\Delta)-T(m_{0},m_{0},x_{b})\bigr)=\operatorname{sign}(\Delta)\cdot\operatorname{sign}\bigl(\SR^{\prime}(0)\bigr).

The sign of
\SR^{\prime}(0)
depends on the baseline configuration and need not match the sign that Proposition
15
assigns to
\LR(\Delta)-T(m_{0},m_{0},x_{b})
.

For small
\Delta
, the short-run sign is governed by the local operating point, whereas the long-run sign is pinned down by Proposition
15
. Indeed, the derivative can take either sign under admissible primitives. For example, at
N=2
,
m_{0}=0
,
v_{0}=1
,
\sigma_{x}=1
,
\sigma_{e}=1
,
c=0.1
, and
x_{b}=0
, the baseline expected depth is
1.6054
and
\SR^{\prime}(0)=-0.0958
. A small positive relevance surprise therefore shortens short-run inspection: at
\Delta=0.05
,
\SR(0.05)=1.5994<1.6054
and
\LR(0.05)=1.6296>1.6054
. The same relevance lift lowers A/B-test depth while raising the long-run depth. Changing only the outside option flips the local short-run sign. With
x_{b}=0.3
, the baseline is
1.4065
and
\SR^{\prime}(0)=0.0907
; the same small positive shift now lengthens short-run inspection, with
\SR(0.05)=1.4101>1.4065
and
\LR(0.05)=1.4452>1.4065
.

The real-world implication is concrete. An engineering team rolls out a small relevance change, runs an A/B test before users have learned about it, and reads off the depth movement. The number they observe is governed by the local slope
\SR^{\prime}(0)
, not by the long-run change. If
\SR^{\prime}(0)<0
at the previous equilibrium point, a small
improvement
registers as a depth
decline
during the A/B test, even though the steady-state effect of the same improvement is a weak depth rise. Reading the A/B-test depth as a long-run signal would, in this case, point in the wrong direction. The discrepancy is not noise; it is a deterministic prediction of the model. The two phases measure two different things: surprise on the way to the new equilibrium, and the new equilibrium itself.

7
Identification from Inspection Depth

So far we have asked what the user does given the latent relevances. The empirical question often is the reverse. A scroll trace records that the user inspected five items and left; it does not say which items thrilled her, which bored her, or which made her bail. What does an observed
\tau=t
reveal about the unobserved sequence
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
, and how can that information be turned into a training signal for a feature-based relevance model?

In conventional industrial training pipelines, items the user did not engage with are labeled negatives. No further structure is imposed: a negative is a negative, and all negatives are equal.

The Bayesian-rational view says depth carries much more content than that. A user who stops at depth
t
has certified that, given the inspected history, the option value of inspecting rank
t+1
falls below the cost. This is a statement about the inspected relevances through her posterior dynamics: the user’s reservation rule is a history-dependent inequality on those relevances, and observed stopping picks out exactly the histories that satisfy it.

The section has three subsections. The first constructs the survival region explicitly, turning the user’s reservation rule into a system of linear inequalities in the inspected relevances. The second builds a learning-to-rank likelihood on top of the survival region: given system-side features, the survival region and the rank-
t
stopping set together pin down the region of latent-relevance space consistent with the observed depth and, when the platform logs it, with the observed conversion. The Gaussian probability of the latent path lying in that region is the session likelihood; its negative log across sessions is a differentiable training objective for a feature-based predictive model. The third shows the connection between the Bayesian-rational view and the position-based propensity approach.

Survival as a System of Inequalities

Survival to depth
t
does not point-identify the latent path
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t-1})
, nor does it point-identify any single relevance. Instead, it reveals that the path lies inside the user’s continuation region. The user keeps going only when each new draw lands in a middle band, neither so disappointing that the outside option dominates, nor so encouraging that the standout exceeds her reservation gap, and the bands themselves are determined endogenously by everything she has already seen. Reaching rank
t
is then a
censoring event
of a high-dimensional latent vector, and the censoring region inherits its structure from the user’s stopping rule.

By Proposition
5

\{\tau\geq t\}=\{\tau\geq 1\}\cap\bigcap_{s=1}^{t-1}\{L_{s}<r_{s}\}.

A Learning-to-Rank Likelihood

The set-identification of the latent relevance path through the survival region has a natural empirical counterpart: it can be turned into a likelihood for training a relevance model directly from depth and conversion observations, without any explicit relevance labels. This subsection makes that translation in four steps. We introduce a system-side feature representation and a data-generating process, decompose the observable outcome of a session into a region of latent-relevance space, write the session likelihood as a Gaussian probability over that region, and verify that the resulting training objective is differentiable in the model parameters.

Step 1: Setup.

The system observes a feature vector
\mathbf{w}_{i}
for each item displayed at rank
i
.
6
6

6

Assuming sessions are iid, so until Step 4 we work within a single session and suppress the session subscript.
These features encode whatever the system knows about the query-item pair: textual match, click history on similar items, document statistics, and so on. They are visible to the system and to the analyst behind the system, but not to the user, who continues to see only rank and the inspected relevances themselves.

Following standard learning-to-rank practice, the system computes a score for each item as the deterministic output of a parametric predictive model of the features,

z_{i}=\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w}_{i}),

(22)

where
\mathscr{F}
is an arbitrary parametric function (a neural network in practice) and
\beta
collects its parameters. In learning-to-rank language,
z_{i}
is the model’s prediction and the true relevance
x_{i}
is the label it is trained against.

The analyst’s task is to recover
\beta
from the user’s observed behavior. The analyst’s conditional model of the latent relevance given features is

x_{i}\mid\mathbf{w}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w}_{i}),\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}^{2}\bigr),

(23)

with residual variance
\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}^{2}
to be estimated alongside
\beta
.
7
7

7

We use a fresh symbol

from the earlier

lumps model misspecification with the irreducible variation in

\mathbf{w}_{i}
, whereas

is the variance of the score-relevance gap. Only the value of

Equation (

Because the user sees neither
\mathbf{w}_{i}

Step 2: Outcomes as regions of latent-relevance space.

Two latent observables describe how the session ends: an inspection depth
t\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and a terminal conversion choice
J\in\{0,1,\ldots,t\}
, where
J=0
is the outside option and
J=j\geq 1
is the rank-
j

Conditional on the policy primitives,
(t,J)
is a deterministic function of the latent path
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
: the user inspects ranks in order, stops as soon as the lead
L_{t}(H_{t-1},x_{t})
exceeds the reservation threshold
r_{t}

\{x_{b},x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}\}

\mathbb{R}^{t}
, and the session likelihood is the Gaussian probability of the path lying in that region under (
23
). The region decomposes into three building blocks. The first two apply regardless of whether conversion is observed; the third arises only when it is.

Block 1: the survival region
\mathcal{C}_{t}
.
As established earlier, the survival region
\mathcal{C}_{t}\subset\mathbb{R}^{t-1}
collects the histories on which the user has not yet stopped at any of the first
t-1
ranks.

Block 2: the stopping set
\mathcal{S}_{t}
at rank
t
.
Define

\mathcal{S}_{t}(H_{t-1}):=\begin{cases}\{x\in\mathbb{R}:L_{t}(H_{t-1},x)\geq r_{t}\},&t<N,\\
\mathbb{R},&t=N,\end{cases}

the set of rank-
t
realizations that trigger termination. By Proposition
12
,
\mathcal{S}_{t}(H_{t-1})=(-\infty,\underline{x}_{t}(H_{t-1})]\cup[\overline{x}_{t}(H_{t-1}),\infty)
in the dynamic explore regime,
\mathbb{R}
in the dynamic trust regime, and
\mathbb{R}
at the end of the list.

Blocks 1 and 2 together pin down the depth-only event: the surviving history
H_{t-1}
lies in
\mathcal{C}_{t}
and the rank-
t
relevance lies in
\mathcal{S}_{t}(H_{t-1})
. The third block enters only when the analyst additionally observes the conversion label.

Block 3: the conversion set
\mathcal{R}_{t,j}
and the restricted survival region
\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{t,j}
(when conversion is observed).
Set
x_{0}:=x_{b}
, the outside-option value; ties have probability zero, so
J=\argmax_{0\leq r\leq t}x_{r}
almost surely. Conversion further restricts the path to the set on which
J=j
is the realized maximum:

\mathcal{R}_{t,j}(H_{t-1}):=\mathcal{S}_{t}(H_{t-1})\cap\Bigl\{x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}:j=\argmax_{0\leq r\leq t}x_{r}\Bigr\}.

Three cases describe
\mathcal{R}_{t,j}
, depending on which item the conversion label points to. If
j=t
, the rank-
t
item is the realized maximum, so
\mathcal{R}_{t,t}(H_{t-1})=\mathcal{S}_{t}(H_{t-1})\cap[\max\{x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{t-1}\},\infty)
, the commitment tail at rank
t
. If
j=0
, the outside option wins, which requires the surviving history entirely below
x_{b}
(an additional restriction on
H_{t-1}
) and
x_{t}<x_{b}
on the rank-
t
relevance, giving
\mathcal{R}_{t,0}(H_{t-1})=\mathcal{S}_{t}(H_{t-1})\cap(-\infty,x_{b})
, the cut-losses tail in its strongest form. If
j\in\{1,\ldots,t-1\}
, an earlier inspected item remains best, which requires
x_{j}>\max\{x_{b},x_{r}:r\leq t-1,r\neq j\}
inside the surviving history (an additional restriction on
H_{t-1}
) and
x_{t}<x_{j}
on the rank-
t
relevance, giving
\mathcal{R}_{t,j}(H_{t-1})=\mathcal{S}_{t}(H_{t-1})\cap(-\infty,x_{j})
.

The latter two cases attach a constraint to the surviving history itself, so

does not range over all of
\mathcal{C}_{t}
when

but over the
restricted survival region

\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{t,j}:=\begin{cases}\mathcal{C}_{t},&j=t,\\[2.0pt]
\mathcal{C}_{t}\cap\bigl\{H_{t-1}:x_{r}<x_{b}\text{ for all }1\leq r\leq t-1\bigr\},&j=0,\\[2.0pt]
\mathcal{C}_{t}\cap\bigl\{H_{t-1}:x_{j}>\max\{x_{b},x_{r}:r\leq t-1,r\neq j\}\bigr\},&1\leq j\leq t-1.\end{cases}

(24)

Two extreme conversion outcomes are especially informative.
J=t

t

given history;

x_{b}
and is therefore the strongest cut-losses signal the analyst can observe.

Step 3: The session likelihood.

Under (
23
), the inspected relevances are independent across rank conditional on features, with
x_{i}\mid\mathbf{w}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w}_{i}),\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}^{2})
. Denoting
\phi_{\mathscr{F}}(\cdot):=\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}^{-1}\phi(\cdot/\sigma_{\mathscr{F}})
for the centered Gaussian density at scale
\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}
and collecting the rank-
1
through rank-
t
feature vectors as
\mathbf{w}_{1:t}
, the joint density of the inspected path is

p_{x\mid\mathbf{w}_{1:t}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\phi_{\mathscr{F}}\bigl(x_{i}-\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w}_{i})\bigr).

The session likelihood is the integral of this density over the region of
\mathbb{R}^{t}
that Step 2 identified with the observed event. The form of the region, and therefore of the likelihood, depends on whether conversion is observed.

Case A: depth observed, conversion not observed.
The observed event is that the session has realized depth
t
, whose region is the product of Blocks 1 and 2,
\mathcal{C}_{t}\times\mathcal{S}_{t}(H_{t-1})
. The session likelihood is

\ell^{\tau}(\beta,\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}):=\Pr\bigl(\tau=t\big|\mathbf{w}_{1:t};\beta,\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}\bigr)\\
=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\left[\int_{\mathcal{S}_{t}(H_{t-1})}\phi_{\mathscr{F}}\bigl(x_{t}-\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w}_{t})\bigr)dx_{t}\right]\prod_{i=1}^{t-1}\phi_{\mathscr{F}}\bigl(x_{i}-\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w}_{i})\bigr)dx_{1}\cdots dx_{t-1}.

(25)

The outer integral weights each surviving history
H_{t-1}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
by its joint Gaussian density under the feature-driven model; the inner integral over
\mathcal{S}_{t}(H_{t-1})
is a univariate Gaussian probability that, in the dynamic explore regime, has two tails with endpoints
\underline{x}_{t}(H_{t-1})
and
\overline{x}_{t}(H_{t-1})
, and equals one in the dynamic trust regime or at the end of the list
t=N
. This is the appropriate likelihood for platforms that observe inspection but not commitment.

Case B: depth and conversion both observed.
The observed event is
\{\text{depth}=t,J=j\}
, whose region refines the Case A region by replacing the stopping set
\mathcal{S}_{t}
with the conversion set
\mathcal{R}_{t,j}
from Block 3 and, in the cases
j\leq t-1
, the survival region
\mathcal{C}_{t}
with the restricted survival region
\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{t,j}
from (
24
):

\ell^{\tau,J}(\beta,\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}):=\Pr\bigl(\tau=t,J=j\big|\mathbf{w}_{1:t};\beta,\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}\bigr)\\
=\int_{\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{t,j}}\left[\int_{\mathcal{R}_{t,j}(H_{t-1})}\phi_{\mathscr{F}}\bigl(x_{t}-\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w}_{t})\bigr)dx_{t}\right]\prod_{i=1}^{t-1}\phi_{\mathscr{F}}\bigl(x_{i}-\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w}_{i})\bigr)dx_{1}\cdots dx_{t-1}.

(26)

Step 4: Training objective and differentiability.

The data consist of
S
iid sessions indexed by
n=1,\ldots,S
. Reintroduce the session subscript on the objects of Steps 1–3: write
\mathbf{w}_{n,i}
for the rank-
i
feature vector in session
n
,
(\tau_{n},J_{n})
for its outcome,
t_{n}
for its realized depth, and
\ell_{n}(\beta,\sigma_{\mathscr{F}})
for the corresponding session likelihood from Step 3, which is
\ell^{\tau}
from (
25
) on sessions logging only depth and
\ell^{\tau,J}
from (
26
) on sessions logging both depth and conversion. Datasets may freely mix the two cases session by session.

As usual, the training objective is the negative log-likelihood across sessions,

\mathcal{L}(\beta,\sigma_{\mathscr{F}})=-\sum_{n=1}^{S}\log\ell_{n}(\beta,\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}).

To see that
\mathcal{L}
is differentiable in
\beta
, observe that in either case
\ell_{n}
depends on
\beta
only through the mean vector

\mathbf{m}_{n}(\beta):=\bigl(\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w}_{n,1}),\ldots,\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w}_{n,t_{n}})\bigr)

of the latent Gaussian path, since the integration region (either
\mathcal{C}_{t_{n}}\times\mathcal{S}_{t_{n}}
in the depth-only case or
\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{t_{n},j_{n}}\times\mathcal{R}_{t_{n},j_{n}}
in the depth-and-conversion case) is fixed by the user-side primitives and the features enter
\ell_{n}
only through this mean. The chain rule gives

\nabla_{\beta}\log\ell_{n}=\bigl(\nabla_{\beta}\mathbf{m}_{n}(\beta)\bigr)^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{m}}\log\ell_{n},

factoring the gradient into two pieces. The first factor is the standard backpropagation Jacobian of
\mathscr{F}
through the neural network, available from any autodiff library. The second factor is the gradient of a multivariate Gaussian probability over a polyhedron with respect to its mean vector;
Hajivassiliou et al. 1996
and
Botev 2017
provide simulation-based estimators of this gradient with controllable bias and variance under standard regularity. The training problem therefore reduces to stochastic gradient descent on
\mathcal{L}
, exactly as for any neural ranker, with depth and conversion outcomes playing the role of coarsened labels in place of relevance judgments.

User-side primitives in production.

The likelihoods in (
25
) and (
26
) depend on
(\beta,\sigma_{\mathscr{F}})
and also, through the survival region
\mathcal{C}_{t_{n}}
and the stopping set
\mathcal{S}_{t_{n}}
, on the user-side primitives

\theta_{U}:=(m_{0},v_{0},\sigma_{\eta},x_{b},c),

none of which appear in a typical production log. The session-level latent
\mu_{n}

\mu_{n}
given the inspected history rather than on
\mu_{n}
itself, and (
23
) replaces
\mu_{n}
with a feature-driven Gaussian centered at
\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w}_{n,i})
, removing
\mu_{n}
as a primitive of the analyst’s model. The real question is what to do about
\theta_{U}
. The following four steps reduce
\theta_{U}
to two scalars that train alongside
\beta
.

Step 1: Pin the origin and the unit of the relevance scale.
The latent relevance
x
is a constructed object with no intrinsic origin and no intrinsic unit, so its absolute location and absolute scale are not meaningful quantities. Two invariances of the likelihood make this concrete: it is unchanged under (i) a joint additive shift
(x_{i},x_{b},m_{0},\mathscr{F})\mapsto(x_{i}+\delta,x_{b}+\delta,m_{0}+\delta,\mathscr{F}+\delta)
for any
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
, and (ii) a joint multiplicative rescaling of all relevance-denominated objects by any
\lambda>0
, with variances rescaling as
\lambda^{2}
.
8
8

8

The formal verifications are deferred to the Appendix on translation and scale invariance.
Both invariances reflect the latent’s lack of an absolute scale rather than any feature of the inference procedure.

Each invariance leaves one degree of freedom that must be normalized. We pin the origin by setting
m_{0}=0
and the unit by setting
\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}=1
. Neither choice is restrictive: the parameter space has no canonical origin or unit, and after Step 1 all remaining parameters are read in units of the residual standard deviation.

Step 2: Calibrate
(v_{0},\sigma_{\eta})
to feature aggregates.
From the analyst’s perspective, the natural reading of
\mu_{n}
is the session-level average

\hat{\mu}_{n}:=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w}_{n,i})-\alpha_{i}\bigr).

A correctly specified user prior at the population level then pins

v_{0}=\Var_{n}\bigl[\hat{\mu}_{n}\bigr],

(27)

where the variance is taken across sessions. The user’s perceived per-item noise
\sigma_{\eta}
likewise matches the analyst’s marginal within-session variance of
x_{i}-\alpha_{i}
. Decomposing
\Var_{i\mid n}(x_{i}-\alpha_{i})
into the variation of
\mathbb{E}[x_{i}\mid\mathbf{w}_{i}]-\alpha_{i}
across
i
and the residual
\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}^{2}
gives

\sigma_{\eta}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{n}\bigl[\Var_{i\mid n}\bigl(\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w}_{n,i})-\alpha_{i}\bigr)\bigr]+1,

(28)

substituting
\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}^{2}=1
from the Step-1 normalization. Both (
27
) and (
28
) are deterministic functions of
(\beta,\mathbf{w})
, recomputable at every gradient step, so
(v_{0},\sigma_{\eta})
are no longer free parameters.

Step 3: Estimate
(c,x_{b})
jointly with
\beta
.
With
(m_{0},\sigma_{\mathscr{F}})
normalized in Step 1 and
(v_{0},\sigma_{\eta})
pinned to feature aggregates in Step 2, what survives is a two-dimensional nuisance vector
(c,x_{b})
, both measured in units of the residual standard deviation. The extended training objective is

\min_{\beta,c,x_{b}}-\sum_{n=1}^{S}\log\ell_{n}(\beta;c,x_{b}),

(29)

with
(v_{0},\sigma_{\eta})
substituted at every gradient step using the Step-2 formulas and the survival region rebuilt from the resulting
\theta_{U}
. Each of

and

is identified from a different part of the data. Sessions logged with

identify

, both contributing in closed form to (

) via
\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{t,0}
. Cross-session variation in

at similar feature profiles identifies

\mathbf{w}_{n,1:t}
fixed, the depth distribution is monotone in the inspection cost. Heterogeneity in
(c,x_{b})
across users, if needed, can be accommodated by promoting them to a population distribution and integrating in (

Implications for Position-Based Propensities

The position-based propensity central to unbiased learning-to-rank
(
Joachims et al. 2017
;
Schnabel et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2018
)
,
p_{i}=\Pr(\tau\geq i)
,
is in this model the marginalization of the survival region
\mathcal{C}_{i}
against the page relevance distribution
\Pi
(the population law of
\mu
across query-page pairs, induced by the retrieval pool and the population of pages the user encounters):

p_{i}=\int\Pr_{\mu}\bigl((x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1})\in\mathcal{C}_{i}\bigr)\Pi(d\mu).

Three structural objects together determine
p_{i}
. The rank shifts
\{\alpha_{s}\}
encode the deployed ranker. The distribution
\Pi
encodes the retrieval pool and the population of pages the user encounters. The prior
(m_{0},v_{0})
encodes user calibration, which in turn fixes the reservation thresholds
\{r_{s}\}
. Any of these can move under a counterfactual change: a new ranker reshuffles
\{\alpha_{s}\}
; a new retrieval index or a different dedup policy reshapes
\Pi
; a sufficiently visible deployment, given enough time, can move
(m_{0},v_{0})
itself. Each such move deforms the survival region, and a propensity estimated from logged data under the deployed system therefore absorbs the equilibrium distribution of histories under that system and does not transport to the candidate system one would like to evaluate. This is the formal version of the empirical critique that motivates the cascade family of click models, and a more granular cousin of the feedback-loop concerns of
Chaney et al. 2018
.

8
Summary

We formalize a Bayesian user inspecting items on a noisily ranked list, and three structural results carry the analysis. Linear traversal follows from Bayesian rationality, so the combinatorial problem of which items to look at collapses to a one-dimensional stopping problem. The optimal rule is a standout threshold: the user continues until her best find sits far enough above her current belief about page quality, with the gap shrinking as she goes deeper. The two-dimensional Bayesian state reduces to a one-dimensional lead chain with a closed-form transition, making the full distribution of inspection depth computable by a simple recursion.

The structural decomposition pays off when we turn to real-world applications. The trust, commit, and cut-losses regimes pinned down by the model account for the ambiguity of one-click sessions in the log and for why a short A/B test on a relevance change can produce a depth signal that points in the opposite direction from its long-run effect. Section
7
pushes the analysis further: the survival region is a polyhedron in latent-relevance space, and the Gaussian probability of a session’s path lying in that polyhedron is a differentiable function of a feature-based relevance prediction model
\mathscr{F}(\beta;\mathbf{w})
. Depth alone identifies
\beta
up to the polyhedron, and logged conversion events tighten the identification considerably.

Several directions for future work are natural. Finite-sample inference for
\beta
and quantifying the marginal informational value of conversion data over depth alone are within reach using the likelihood of Section
7
. Allowing
\sigma_{x}^{2}
and
\sigma_{e}^{2}
to be unknown preserves conjugacy under Normal–inverse-Gamma priors at the cost of a higher-dimensional state. Relaxing the quantile reduction in Assumption
2
is feasible numerically and would carry the order statistics into the state directly. Endogenizing the system’s choice of items would link the user’s stopping rule to the ranker’s design problem and close the loop between user behavior and ranker learning.

Appendix

Derivation of the Quantile Reduction

This section derives Assumption
2
using the probability integral transform.

Let
X
be a real-valued random variable with continuous, strictly increasing CDF
F
. Then
U:=F(X)\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)
. Conversely, if
U\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)
, then
X:=F^{-1}(U)
has CDF
F
. The maps
F
and
F^{-1}
thus provide a bijection between the original “data scale” and the “probability scale,” translating any continuous distribution to a uniform and back.

Because
F
is strictly increasing, applying
F
preserves the order of a sample: if
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
are iid with CDF
F
and
U_{i}:=F(X_{i})
, then
X_{i}<X_{j}\iff U_{i}<U_{j}
. In particular, writing
U_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq U_{(N)}
for the ascending order statistics, the
k
-th largest of the
X
’s satisfies

X_{[k]}=F^{-1}\big(U_{(N+1-k)}\big).

(30)

Uniform order statistics have an exact distribution:

U_{(j)}\sim\text{Beta}(j,N+1-j),\quad\mathbb{E}\left[U_{(j)}\right]=\frac{j}{N+1},\quad\text{Var}\left[U_{(j)}\right]=\frac{j(N+1-j)}{(N+1)^{2}(N+2)}=O\left(\tfrac{1}{N}\right).

Setting
j=N+1-k
yields
\mathbb{E}[U_{(N+1-k)}]=1-k/(N+1)
and a vanishing variance, so
U_{(N+1-k)}
concentrates on its mean as
N
grows.

Under Assumption
1
,
z_{i}\mid\mu\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma_{z}^{2})
with
F^{-1}(p)=\mu+\sigma_{z}\Phi^{-1}(p)
. Applying the bridge (
30
),

z_{[k]}=\mu+\sigma_{z}\Phi^{-1}\big(U_{(N+1-k)}\big).

This is exact. Replacing the random uniform order statistic by its mean,
U_{(N+1-k)}\approx 1-k/(N+1)
, yields

z_{[k]}\approx\mu+\sigma_{z}\Phi^{-1}\left(1-\tfrac{k}{N+1}\right),

which is the content of Assumption
2
. The approximation error is
O_{p}(N^{-1/2})
for central ranks (
k/N\to\tau\in(0,1)
), and the approximation becomes exact in the limit
N\to\infty
.

Refinements.

Two standard refinements are available. Blom’s formula replaces
k/(N+1)
with
(k-3/8)/(N+1/4)
, correcting systematic bias in the tails. Alternatively, the exact expected normal order statistics
\mathbb{E}[z_{[k]}]=\mu+\sigma_{z}e_{k,N}
can be computed numerically. Substituting
e_{k,N}
for
\Phi^{-1}(1-k/(N+1))
preserves every downstream step.

This appendix verifies the two likelihood invariances cited in Section

Translation invariance.

We claim that the map

(x_{i},x_{b},m_{0},\mathscr{F})\longmapsto(x_{i}+\delta,x_{b}+\delta,m_{0}+\delta,\mathscr{F}+\delta),\qquad\delta\in\mathbb{R},

leaves the likelihood unchanged. Each inequality defining
\mathcal{C}_{t}
has the form
x_{j}<a_{s}+\gamma_{s}\sum_{r=1}^{s}x_{r}+r_{s}
, with
a_{s}=(v_{s}/v_{0})m_{0}-\gamma_{s}\sum_{r}\alpha_{r}
,
\gamma_{s}=v_{s}/\sigma_{\eta}^{2}
. The shift moves the left-hand side by
\delta
and the right-hand side by
(v_{s}/v_{0})\delta+\gamma_{s}s\delta=v_{s}\delta(1/v_{0}+s/\sigma_{\eta}^{2})=\delta
, where the last equality uses the variance recursion
1/v_{s}=1/v_{0}+s/\sigma_{\eta}^{2}
. Both sides move by
\delta
, so every inequality is preserved and
\mathcal{C}_{t}
is unchanged. The Gaussian density
\phi_{\mathscr{F}}(x_{i}-\mathscr{F}_{i})
is invariant under the joint shift of
x_{i}
and
\mathscr{F}_{i}
. Hence the likelihood is unchanged.

Scale invariance.

We claim that for any
\lambda>0
, the joint rescaling

(x,x_{b},m_{0},\mathscr{F},c,\alpha,\sigma_{\mathscr{F}},\sigma_{\eta})\longmapsto\lambda\cdot(\text{each}),\qquad v_{0}\longmapsto\lambda^{2}v_{0},

also leaves the likelihood unchanged. The argument has two parts.

Part 1: Policy and survival region rescale by
\lambda
.
The Bellman equation

V_{t}(M_{t},m_{t})=\max\bigl\{M_{t},-c+\mathbb{E}[V_{t+1}(\max(M_{t},x_{t+1}),m_{t+1})]\bigr\}

maps to itself under the rescaling with
V_{t}\mapsto\lambda V_{t}
: scaling all of
M,m,c,x
by
\lambda
scales every term in both branches of the max by
\lambda
. By backward induction the reservation gap satisfies
\kappa_{s}^{\star}\mapsto\lambda\kappa_{s}^{\star}
, so
r_{s}=\alpha_{s+1}+\kappa_{s}^{\star}\mapsto\lambda r_{s}
. The variance recursion gives
v_{s}\mapsto\lambda^{2}v_{s}
, so
\gamma_{s}=v_{s}/\sigma_{\eta}^{2}
is invariant and
a_{s}=(v_{s}/v_{0})m_{0}-\gamma_{s}\sum_{r}\alpha_{r}\mapsto\lambda a_{s}
. Each inequality defining
\mathcal{C}_{t}
becomes
\lambda x_{j}<\lambda(a_{s}+\gamma_{s}\sum_{r=1}^{s}x_{r}+r_{s})
, which after dividing both sides by
\lambda
is the same inequality with rescaled variables. Hence
\mathcal{C}_{t}\mapsto\lambda\mathcal{C}_{t}
, a dilation, and the same argument gives
\mathcal{S}_{t}\mapsto\lambda\mathcal{S}_{t}
.

Part 2: The Gaussian integral is invariant under the dilation.
Write the likelihood as a
t
-dimensional Gaussian integral over the rescaled region. Substituting
x_{i}=\lambda y_{i}
, each differential contributes
dx_{i}=\lambda dy_{i}
, for a total Jacobian
\lambda^{t}
. Each Gaussian density rescales as

\phi_{\lambda\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}}(\lambda u)=\frac{1}{\lambda\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\lambda^{2}u^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}\sigma_{\mathscr{F}}^{2}}\Bigr)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\phi_{\mathscr{F}}(u),

contributing a factor
\lambda^{-t}
across the
t
densities. The Jacobian and the density factor cancel exactly, so the integral equals its pre-rescaling value. Hence the likelihood is unchanged.

Lemma A1

(Gaussian Truncation Identity)
.

We claim that for any
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
with
\sigma>0
and any threshold

,

\mathbb{E}[(X-a)^{+}]=\sigma\phi\left(\tfrac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\right)-(a-\mu)\Phi\left(-\tfrac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\right).

Proof.

To prove (
31
), write
X=\mu+\sigma Z
with
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and let

. Then
(X-a)^{+}=\sigma(Z-d)^{+}
, so

\mathbb{E}[(X-a)^{+}]=\sigma\int_{d}^{\infty}z\phi(z)dz-\sigma d\int_{d}^{\infty}\phi(z)dz.

For the first integral, note

, so
\int_{d}^{\infty}z\phi(z)dz=\phi(d)
.
For the second,
\int_{d}^{\infty}\phi(z)dz=1-\Phi(d)=\Phi(-d)

give (

∎

Lemma A2
.

The function

defined in (

onto

Let

Lemma A3

(Top-
k
Subset Dominance)
.

Let
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{N}\mid\mu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\eta}^{2})
,
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>\cdots>\alpha_{N}
, and
K\in\mathbb{R}
. For any
k
-element subset
\mathcal{R}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\max\Big(K,\max_{r\in\{1,\ldots,k\}}(\alpha_{r}+\eta_{r})\Big)\Big|\mu\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\max\Big(K,\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}(\alpha_{r}+\eta_{r})\Big)\Big|\mu\right].

Proof.

Sort
\mathcal{R}
as
r_{1}<r_{2}<\cdots<r_{k}
. The proof has three steps.

Step 1: If
r_{i}\geq i
, then
\alpha_{r_{i}}\leq\alpha_{i}
.
The
i
-th smallest element of
\mathcal{R}
is
r_{i}
, so
\mathcal{R}
contains
i
elements that are
\leq r_{i}
. But every such element is also
\leq r_{i}
in
\{1,\ldots,N\}
, and there are only
r_{i}
such elements in total, so
r_{i}\geq i
. Combined with
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>\cdots>\alpha_{N}
, this gives
\alpha_{r_{i}}\leq\alpha_{i}
.

Step 2: Coupling via exchangeability.
Conditional on
\mu
, the noises
(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{N})
are iid
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\eta}^{2})
. So the joint law of any size-
k
subvector
(\eta_{s_{1}},\ldots,\eta_{s_{k}})
, for any
k
-element subset
\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}
, is the same: namely,
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\eta}^{2})^{k}
. In particular, picking the subset
\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}\}=\mathcal{R}
and relabeling,

\zeta_{i}:=\eta_{r_{i}},\qquad i=1,\ldots,k,

gives an iid Gaussian sequence
(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{k})
with the same joint distribution as
(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k})
.

Step 3: Pointwise inequality, then expectations.

We rewrite both maxes in the claim using
\zeta
:

\displaystyle\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}(\alpha_{r}+\eta_{r})

\displaystyle=\max_{i=1,\ldots,k}\big(\alpha_{r_{i}}+\eta_{r_{i}}\big)=\max_{i=1,\ldots,k}\big(\alpha_{r_{i}}+\zeta_{i}\big),

\displaystyle\max_{r\in\{1,\ldots,k\}}(\alpha_{r}+\eta_{r})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\max_{i=1,\ldots,k}\big(\alpha_{i}+\zeta_{i}\big),

where the second equality uses the equality in distribution from Step 2. By Step 1,
\alpha_{i}\geq\alpha_{r_{i}}
for each
i
, and the noise
\zeta_{i}
is the same on the right-hand side and the left-hand side at each index

, so

\alpha_{i}+\zeta_{i}\geq\alpha_{r_{i}}+\zeta_{i}\qquad\text{pointwise, for every }i.

Taking
\max
over

preserves componentwise inequalities:

\max_{i=1,\ldots,k}\big(\alpha_{i}+\zeta_{i}\big)\geq\max_{i=1,\ldots,k}\big(\alpha_{r_{i}}+\zeta_{i}\big)\quad\text{pointwise}.

The map
u\mapsto\max(K,u)
is non-decreasing for any constant
K
, so wrapping both sides in
\max(K,\cdot)
preserves the inequality. Taking expectations (conditional on
\mu
) gives the claim.
∎

Lower bound.
The stop branch gives

Upper bound, by backward induction.
At

V_{N}(M,m)=M

\displaystyle C_{t}(M,m)

\displaystyle\leq-c+\mathbb{E}_{x_{t+1}}\left[\max(M,x_{t+1})+\sum_{s=t+1}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}_{m_{t+1}(x_{t+1})}^{(s)}\left[(x_{s+1}-\max(M,x_{t+1}))^{+}\right]\right]

Step 3:

We claim by backward induction on

0\leq V_{t}(M_{2},m)-V_{t}(M_{1},m)\leq M_{2}-M_{1}.

(38)

At

,
V_{N}(M,m)=M
satisfies (

38
) at
t+1
.

The stop branch

M
trivially satisfies (
38

The continuation branch.
Fix

\Delta_{t}(\cdot,m)

We show that for

,

C_{t}(M_{2},m)-C_{t}(M_{1},m)\leq(M_{2}-M_{1})\Pr(x_{t+1}\leq M_{2})<M_{2}-M_{1},

the strict inequality on the right because

Fix any realization

If

•

If

If

In all three cases,

pointwise. Taking expectations,

C_{t}(M_{2},m)-C_{t}(M_{1},m)=\mathbb{E}_{x_{t+1}}[\Psi(M_{2})-\Psi(M_{1})]\leq(M_{2}-M_{1})\Pr(x_{t+1}\leq M_{2}),

Step 5:
V_{t}
and

are nondecreasing in

We show

,

Suppose

m_{t+1}(x_{t+1};m)=\frac{v_{t+1}}{v_{t}}m+\frac{v_{t+1}}{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}(m+\xi)=v_{t+1}\left(\frac{1}{v_{t}}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\right)m+\frac{v_{t+1}}{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\xi=m+\frac{v_{t+1}}{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\xi,

. Thus, the continuation value may be expressed in terms of the

-free noise

C_{t}(M,m)=-c+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[V_{t+1}\Big(\max(M,m+\alpha_{t+1}+\xi),m+\tfrac{v_{t+1}}{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\xi\Big)\right].

(40)

For each fixed

, both arguments of

are nondecreasing in

is jointly nondecreasing in its two arguments, so the integrand of (

) is nondecreasing in

. Taking expectation over
\xi
(whose law is
m

is nondecreasing in
m
, and so is

. The stop branch

does not depend on

.

Step 6: Boundary behavior of
\Delta_{t}
as
M\to\pm\infty
.

As

.
Then
\max(M,x_{t+1})=x_{t+1}
a.s., and by Step 1’s lower bound applied at

V_{t+1}\big(\max(M,x_{t+1}),m_{t+1}\big)\geq\max(M,x_{t+1})=x_{t+1}.

Hence

C_{t}(M,m)\geq-c+\mathbb{E}_{x_{t+1}}[x_{t+1}]=-c+m+\alpha_{t+1},

which is bounded below uniformly in

. Therefore
\Delta_{t}(M,m)=C_{t}(M,m)-M\to+\infty
as
M\to-\infty
.

As

.
Then
\max(M,x_{t+1})=M
except on the event
\{x_{t+1}>M\}
, whose probability tends to
0
. By Step 1’s upper bound,

V_{t+1}(M,m_{t+1})-M\leq\sum_{s=t+1}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}_{m_{t+1}}^{(s)}\big[(x_{s+1}-M)^{+}\big]\xrightarrow{M\to+\infty}0.

Combining,

\Delta_{t}(M,m)\leq-c+\mathbb{E}_{x_{t+1}}\big[(x_{t+1}-M)^{+}\big]+\mathbb{E}_{x_{t+1}}\sum_{s=t+1}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}_{m_{t+1}}^{(s)}\big[(x_{s+1}-M)^{+}\big]\xrightarrow{M\to+\infty}-c<0.

Step 7:
R_{t}(m)
exists, is unique, and characterizes the stop region.

Fix

. By Step 2,
\Delta_{t}(\cdot,m)
is continuous; by Step 4, it is strictly decreasing; by Step 6,
\Delta_{t}(M,m)\to+\infty
as

and
\Delta_{t}(M,m)\to-c<0
as

. The intermediate value theorem, together with strict monotonicity, gives a unique

with

\Delta_{t}(R_{t}(m),m)=0,\qquad\text{i.e.,}\qquad C_{t}(R_{t}(m),m)=R_{t}(m).

Since

is strictly decreasing,

i.e., stopping is (weakly) optimal. This proves the threshold characterization in the proposition.

Step 8: Translation equivariance of
V_{t}
.

We show by the same backward induction that
V_{t}(M+\varepsilon,m+\varepsilon)=V_{t}(M,m)+\varepsilon
for every
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
. At
t=N
,
V_{N}(M+\varepsilon,m+\varepsilon)=M+\varepsilon=V_{N}(M,m)+\varepsilon
. Suppose the claim holds at
t+1
. Using the
m
-free noise
\xi=x_{t+1}-m-\alpha_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,v_{t}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2})
and the simplified update
m_{t+1}(x_{t+1};m)=m+(v_{t+1}/\sigma_{\eta}^{2})\xi
from Step 5,

\displaystyle C_{t}(M+\varepsilon,m+\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=-c+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\Big[V_{t+1}\big(\max(M+\varepsilon,m+\varepsilon+\alpha_{t+1}+\xi),m+\varepsilon+(v_{t+1}/\sigma_{\eta}^{2})\xi\big)\Big]

\displaystyle=-c+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\Big[V_{t+1}\big(\max(M,m+\alpha_{t+1}+\xi)+\varepsilon,m+(v_{t+1}/\sigma_{\eta}^{2})\xi+\varepsilon\big)\Big]

\displaystyle=-c+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\Big[V_{t+1}\big(\max(M,m+\alpha_{t+1}+\xi),m+(v_{t+1}/\sigma_{\eta}^{2})\xi\big)\Big]+\varepsilon

\displaystyle=C_{t}(M,m)+\varepsilon,

where the second line uses
\max(a+\varepsilon,b+\varepsilon)=\max(a,b)+\varepsilon
and the third applies the inductive hypothesis. Therefore,

V_{t}(M+\varepsilon,m+\varepsilon)=\max\{M+\varepsilon,C_{t}(M+\varepsilon,m+\varepsilon)\}=\max\{M,C_{t}(M,m)\}+\varepsilon=V_{t}(M,m)+\varepsilon.

Step 9: Affine form and

-independence.

Setting

in Step 8 gives
V_{t}(M,m)=V_{t}(M-m,0)+m
, and applying the same shift to

gives
C_{t}(M,m)=C_{t}(M-m,0)+m
. Define
\bar{C}_{t}(X):=C_{t}(X,0)
. By Step 7,
R_{t}(m)
is the unique solution of

; substituting the translated form,

\bar{C}_{t}(R_{t}(m)-m)=R_{t}(m)-m,

so

is a fixed point of

. As a function of

, the gap
\bar{C}_{t}(X)-X
is the restriction of

to its first argument. By Step 4 it is strictly decreasing in
X
, and by Step 6 it tends to

as

and to
-c
as

. The fixed point is therefore unique and, in particular, independent of

. Call this constant
\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star}
. Then

is (

is automatically continuous and strictly increasing in

as an affine function with slope

.

Finally, the Bellman recursion at

has terminal condition

and the deterministic primitives

; the outside option

enters the model only through the initial condition

and not through the recursion. Hence

,

,

Proof of Proposition
6

At epoch
t\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
, the one-step look-ahead rule treats the next epoch as terminal: inspect if and only if the expected payoff of inspecting rank
t+1
and stopping weakly exceeds the immediate stop value,

-c+\mathbb{E}\left[\max(M_{t},x_{t+1})\big|H_{t}\right]\geq M_{t}.

Using
\max(M_{t},x_{t+1})=M_{t}+(x_{t+1}-M_{t})^{+}
and taking conditional expectation, this is equivalent to

c\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(x_{t+1}-M_{t})^{+}\big|H_{t}\right].

By Corollary
4
,
x_{t+1}\mid H_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{t}+\alpha_{t+1},\sigma_{t}^{\star 2})
. Applying Lemma
A1
with
a=M_{t}
,
\mu=m_{t}+\alpha_{t+1}
,
\sigma=\sigma_{t}^{\star}
, and writing
d_{t}:=(M_{t}-m_{t}-\alpha_{t+1})/\sigma_{t}^{\star}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[(x_{t+1}-M_{t})^{+}\mid H_{t}\right]=\sigma_{t}^{\star}\big(\phi(d_{t})-d_{t}\Phi(-d_{t})\big)=\sigma_{t}^{\star}g(d_{t}),

using the definition of
g
in (
7
).
Thus, the myopic rule continues at epoch
t
if and only if
\sigma_{t}^{\star}g(d_{t})\geq c
and stops if and only if
\sigma_{t}^{\star}g(d_{t})\leq c
. The reservation
R_{t}^{\mathrm{myo}}(m_{t})
is the value of
M_{t}
at which equality holds:

g\left(\frac{R_{t}^{\mathrm{myo}}(m_{t})-m_{t}-\alpha_{t+1}}{\sigma_{t}^{\star}}\right)=\frac{c}{\sigma_{t}^{\star}}.

By Lemma
A2
,
g:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
is a strictly decreasing bijection, so we may invert,

R_{t}^{\mathrm{myo}}(m_{t})=m_{t}+\alpha_{t+1}+\sigma_{t}^{\star}g^{-1}\left(c/\sigma_{t}^{\star}\right)=m_{t}+\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t},

which is (
8
): stopping is myopically optimal if and only if
M_{t}-m_{t}\geq\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}
.

As
v_{t+1}^{-1}=v_{t}^{-1}+\sigma_{\eta}^{-2}
,
v_{t}
and hence
\sigma_{t}^{\star 2}=v_{t}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}
are strictly decreasing in
t
. View
\kappa
as a function of
s>0
via
\kappa(s):=sg^{-1}(c/s)
. The implicit-function characterization
sg(\kappa/s)=c
becomes
F(\kappa,s):=s\phi(\kappa/s)-\kappa\Phi(-\kappa/s)-c=0
. Using
\phi^{\prime}(d)=-d\phi(d)
and
\phi(-d)=\phi(d)
,

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\kappa}=-\Phi(-\kappa/s)<0,\qquad\frac{\partial F}{\partial s}=\phi(\kappa/s)>0.

By the implicit function theorem,
d\kappa/ds=\phi(\kappa/s)/\Phi(-\kappa/s)>0
, so
\kappa(s)
is strictly increasing. Composing with
s=\sigma_{t}^{\star}
, which is strictly decreasing in
t
, gives
\kappa_{t}=\kappa(\sigma_{t}^{\star})
strictly decreasing in
t
.

At
t=0
,
\sigma_{0}^{\star 2}=v_{0}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}
gives the stated value of
\kappa_{0}
. As
t\to\infty
,
v_{t}\to 0
, so
\sigma_{t}^{\star}\to\sigma_{\eta}
, and by continuity of
g^{-1}
,
\kappa_{t}\to\sigma_{\eta}g^{-1}(c/\sigma_{\eta})=:\kappa_{\infty}
. This proves (
9
).
\hfill\qed

Proof of Corollary

Along the stated path,

\sigma_{\eta}^{2}(\rho)=\sigma_{x}^{2}(1-\rho)\to 0,\qquad\alpha_{i}(\rho)=\sigma_{x}\sqrt{\rho}q_{i}\to\alpha_{i}^{\infty}:=\sigma_{x}q_{i},

and
\alpha_{1}^{\infty}>\alpha_{2}^{\infty}>\cdots>\alpha_{N}^{\infty}
inherits the strict order of the quantile sequence. The Bayesian weight on the first item is

\omega_{1}(\rho)=\frac{v_{0}}{v_{0}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}(\rho)}\to 1,

and the posterior variance after one inspection is

v_{1}(\rho)=\frac{v_{0}\sigma_{\eta}^{2}(\rho)}{v_{0}+\sigma_{\eta}^{2}(\rho)}\to 0.

Recall from the proof of Proposition
5
the inspect-branch value
C_{1}(M_{1},m_{1};\rho)
from (
35
) and the stop–continue gap
\Delta_{1}(M_{1},m_{1};\rho)=C_{1}(M_{1},m_{1};\rho)-M_{1}
from (
36
). By Step S8 of that proof,
C_{1}
is translation-equivariant:
C_{1}(M_{1}+a,m_{1}+a;\rho)=C_{1}(M_{1},m_{1};\rho)+a
for every
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Setting
a=-m_{1}
gives
C_{1}(M_{1},m_{1};\rho)=m_{1}+C_{1}(l,0;\rho)
with
l:=M_{1}-m_{1}
, so

\Delta_{1}(M_{1},m_{1};\rho)=C_{1}(M_{1},m_{1};\rho)-M_{1}=C_{1}(l,0;\rho)-l

depends on
(M_{1},m_{1})
only through
l
. With a slight abuse, write
\Delta_{1}(l;\rho)
. By Step S4,
\Delta_{1}(\cdot;\rho)
is strictly decreasing in
l
, and
r_{1}(\rho):=\alpha_{2}(\rho)+\kappa_{1}^{\star}(\rho)
is its unique zero.

Step 1: Upper bound on
r_{1}(\rho)
via free information.

At epoch
1
with state
(M_{1},m_{1})
, any continuation policy pays at least one inspection cost (
c
) and produces a terminal payoff bounded above by
\max\{M_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N}\}
, so

C_{1}(M_{1},m_{1};\rho)\leq-c+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\max\{M_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N}\}\bigm|M_{1},m_{1}\bigr].

Subtracting
M_{1}
from both sides gives an upper bound on the stop–continue gap:

\Delta_{1}(M_{1},m_{1};\rho)\leq-c+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\max\{0,x_{2}-M_{1},\ldots,x_{N}-M_{1}\}\bigm|M_{1},m_{1}\bigr].

(41)

We now rewrite the right-hand side as a function of
l
alone. Set
\delta:=\mu-m_{1}
, the residual uncertainty about
\mu
at epoch
1
. By the Bayesian update, the posterior on
\mu
given the user’s epoch-1 information is
\mathcal{N}(m_{1},v_{1}(\rho))
, so
\delta
has conditional distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,v_{1}(\rho))
given that information. The future noises
\eta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\eta}^{2}(\rho))
for
i\geq 2
are independent of
\mu
and of one another, hence also independent of
\delta
given the epoch-1 information. From
x_{i}=\mu+\alpha_{i}(\rho)+\eta_{i}
and
M_{1}-m_{1}=l
,
x_{i}-M_{1}=\delta+\alpha_{i}(\rho)+\eta_{i}-l
. Substituting into (
41
),

\Delta_{1}(l;\rho)\leq-c+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\max\bigl\{0,\delta+\alpha_{2}(\rho)+\eta_{2}-l,\ldots,\delta+\alpha_{N}(\rho)+\eta_{N}-l\bigr\}\bigr]=:\bar{\Delta}_{1}(l;\rho),

where the right-hand side now depends only on
l
and
\rho
.

We take the limit as
\rho\uparrow 1
by dominated convergence. The expectation
\bar{\Delta}_{1}(l;\rho)
depends on
(\delta,\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{N})
only through their joint distribution, so we may replace them by any equally distributed realization. A convenient one is the
coupling
that fixes a single set of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
random variables
Z_{0},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{N}
(iid, independent of
\rho
) once and for all, and sets

\delta:=\sqrt{v_{1}(\rho)}Z_{0},\qquad\eta_{i}:=\sigma_{\eta}(\rho)Z_{i}\quad(i\geq 2).

Each
\delta
has the required
\mathcal{N}(0,v_{1}(\rho))
marginal and each
\eta_{i}
has the required
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\eta}^{2}(\rho))
marginal, and they remain mutually independent; the only thing the coupling adds is that as
\rho
varies, the same underlying
Z
’s are reused, so
\delta
and
\eta_{i}
become deterministic functions of
\rho
on a fixed sample space. As
\rho\uparrow 1
,
v_{1}(\rho)\to 0
and
\sigma_{\eta}(\rho)\to 0
, so
\delta\to 0
and
\eta_{i}\to 0
almost surely, while
\alpha_{i}(\rho)\to\alpha_{i}^{\infty}
. The integrand therefore converges almost surely:

\max\bigl\{0,\delta+\alpha_{2}(\rho)+\eta_{2}-l,\ldots,\delta+\alpha_{N}(\rho)+\eta_{N}-l\bigr\}\to\max\{0,\alpha_{2}^{\infty}-l\},

using
\alpha_{2}^{\infty}>\alpha_{3}^{\infty}>\cdots>\alpha_{N}^{\infty}
to drop the lower-rank terms. The integrand is bounded in absolute value by
|l|+\sqrt{v_{0}}|Z_{0}|+\sigma_{x}\max_{i}|q_{i}|+\sigma_{x}\max_{i}|Z_{i}|
for all
\rho
sufficiently close to
1
(using
v_{1}(\rho)\leq v_{0}
and
\sigma_{\eta}(\rho)\leq\sigma_{x}
), which is integrable. Dominated convergence gives
\bar{\Delta}_{1}(l;\rho)\to-c+\max\{0,\alpha_{2}^{\infty}-l\}
for every
l\in\mathbb{R}
.

Fix any
\varepsilon\in(0,c)
and set
l_{\varepsilon}:=\alpha_{2}^{\infty}-c+\varepsilon\in(\alpha_{2}^{\infty}-c,\alpha_{2}^{\infty})
. The limit at
l_{\varepsilon}
equals
-c+(\alpha_{2}^{\infty}-l_{\varepsilon})=-\varepsilon<0
, so for all
\rho
sufficiently close to
1
,
\Delta_{1}(l_{\varepsilon};\rho)\leq\bar{\Delta}_{1}(l_{\varepsilon};\rho)<0
. Strict monotonicity of
\Delta_{1}(\cdot;\rho)
then forces
r_{1}(\rho)\leq l_{\varepsilon}
. As
\varepsilon\in(0,c)
is arbitrary,

\limsup_{\rho\uparrow 1}r_{1}(\rho)\leq\alpha_{2}^{\infty}-c.

(42)

Step 2: The trust slack at
x_{b}
is positive in the limit.

Define

\Gamma(\rho):=\omega_{1}(\rho)\alpha_{1}(\rho)+(1-\omega_{1}(\rho))(x_{b}-m_{0})-r_{1}(\rho).

This is exactly
h_{\rho}(x_{b})
, where
h_{\rho}(x_{1}):=M_{1}(x_{1})-m_{1}(x_{1})-r_{1}(\rho)
is the V-shaped first-stop function from Proposition
7
. Because
\omega_{1}(\rho)\to 1
and
\alpha_{1}(\rho)\to\alpha_{1}^{\infty}
, combining with (
42
),

\liminf_{\rho\uparrow 1}\Gamma(\rho)\geq\alpha_{1}^{\infty}-\limsup_{\rho\uparrow 1}r_{1}(\rho)\geq\alpha_{1}^{\infty}-(\alpha_{2}^{\infty}-c)=\sigma_{x}(q_{1}-q_{2})+c>0.

Hence there exists
\bar{\rho}<1
such that
\Gamma(\rho)>0
for all
\rho\in(\bar{\rho},1)
.

Step 3: Trust regime and conclusion.

For any fixed
\rho
, the first-stop function is

h_{\rho}(x_{1})=\begin{cases}-\omega_{1}(\rho)x_{1}+x_{b}+\omega_{1}(\rho)\alpha_{1}(\rho)-(1-\omega_{1}(\rho))m_{0}-r_{1}(\rho),&x_{1}\leq x_{b},\\[3.0pt]
(1-\omega_{1}(\rho))x_{1}+\omega_{1}(\rho)\alpha_{1}(\rho)-(1-\omega_{1}(\rho))m_{0}-r_{1}(\rho),&x_{1}>x_{b}.\end{cases}

The left branch is decreasing in
x_{1}
and the right branch is increasing in
x_{1}
, so
\min_{x_{1}}h_{\rho}=h_{\rho}(x_{b})=\Gamma(\rho)
. Therefore
\Gamma(\rho)>0
implies
h_{\rho}(x_{1})>0
for every
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, which by Proposition
7
is the trust regime: the user stops immediately after the first inspection. Hence
\Pr_{\rho}(\tau_{\rho}=1)=1
for all
\rho\in(\bar{\rho},1)
, and the survival probability to any lower rank is zero.
\hfill\qed

Proof of Lemma

Markov property and one-step recursion (
13
).

By definition,
x_{t}=m_{t-1}+\alpha_{t}+\xi_{t}
with
\xi_{t}\mid H_{t-1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{t-1}^{\star 2})
independent of
H_{t-1}
(Corollary
4
). Substituting into the dynamics

M_{t}=\max(M_{t-1},x_{t}),\qquad m_{t}=m_{t-1}+\frac{v_{t}}{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\bigl(x_{t}-\alpha_{t}-m_{t-1}\bigr),

we obtain
M_{t}-m_{t-1}=\max(L_{t-1},\alpha_{t}+\xi_{t})
and
m_{t}-m_{t-1}=\omega_{t}\xi_{t}
, using
v_{t}/\sigma_{\eta}^{2}=\omega_{t}
. Subtracting yields (
13
). Because
L_{t}
is a measurable function of
L_{t-1}
alone and an exogenous Gaussian shock
\xi_{t}
independent of
H_{t-1}

is Markov with respect to its natural filtration.

Conditional density (
14
).

Define
\Psi_{t}(l,\xi):=\max(l,\alpha_{t}+\xi)-\omega_{t}\xi
, so
L_{t}=\Psi_{t}(L_{t-1},\xi_{t})
. The map
\xi\mapsto\Psi_{t}(l,\xi)
is V-shaped: on
\xi\leq l-\alpha_{t}
it equals
l-\omega_{t}\xi
; on
\xi>l-\alpha_{t}
it equals
\alpha_{t}+(1-\omega_{t})\xi

. Hence
\Psi_{t}(l,\cdot)
takes values in

. For
y\geq L_{\min}(l)
, the sublevel set
\{\xi:\Psi_{t}(l,\xi)\leq y\}
is the interval
[\xi_{-}(y),\xi_{+}(y)]
with

\xi_{-}(y)=\frac{l-y}{\omega_{t}},\qquad\xi_{+}(y)=\frac{y-\alpha_{t}}{1-\omega_{t}}.

For

the sublevel set is empty. Hence

\Pr\bigl(L_{t}\leq y\bigm|L_{t-1}=l\bigr)=\Phi\left(\frac{y-\alpha_{t}}{(1-\omega_{t})\sigma_{t-1}^{\star}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{l-y}{\omega_{t}\sigma_{t-1}^{\star}}\right)\quad\text{for }y\geq L_{\min}(l),

and zero below. Differentiating in

, with
\xi_{+}^{\prime}(y)=1/(1-\omega_{t})
and
\xi_{-}^{\prime}(y)=-1/\omega_{t}

).
\hfill\qed

Proof of Proposition
12

By Proposition
5
, continuation past epoch
t
is the event
\{L_{t}<r_{t}\}
. The strategy is to rewrite this event in the surprise coordinate
\xi_{t}
, where the lead has the piecewise-affine form of (
15
), and then convert back to the relevance coordinate
x_{t}
via
\xi_{t}=x_{t}-(m_{t-1}+\alpha_{t})
.

Step 1: the lead as a V-shape in
\xi_{t}
.
By (
15
), the lead at epoch
t
satisfies

L_{t}=\begin{cases}L_{t-1}-\omega_{t}\xi_{t}&\text{if }\xi_{t}\leq L_{t-1}-\alpha_{t}\quad(\text{disappointment}),\\
\alpha_{t}+(1-\omega_{t})\xi_{t}&\text{if }\xi_{t}>L_{t-1}-\alpha_{t}\quad(\text{discovery}).\end{cases}

The two branches meet at the kink
\xi_{t}=L_{t-1}-\alpha_{t}
with
L_{\min}:=(1-\omega_{t})L_{t-1}+\omega_{t}\alpha_{t}
. On the disappointment branch
L_{t}
is strictly decreasing in
\xi_{t}
with slope
-\omega_{t}
; on the discovery branch it is strictly increasing with slope
1-\omega_{t}
. Hence
L_{t}\geq L_{\min}
for every
\xi_{t}
, with equality only at the kink.

Step 2: the trust regime,
r_{t}\leq L_{\min}
.
Then
L_{t}\geq L_{\min}\geq r_{t}
for every
\xi_{t}
, so
\{L_{t}<r_{t}\}=\emptyset
. The user stops at rank
t
for every realization of
x_{t}
, which is part (a).

Step 3: the explore regime,
r_{t}>L_{\min}
.
On the disappointment branch, continuation requires
L_{t-1}-\omega_{t}\xi_{t}<r_{t}
, which is
\xi_{t}>(L_{t-1}-r_{t})/\omega_{t}
. Note that we require
\xi_{t}\leq L_{t-1}-\alpha_{t}
to be on the disappointment branch. Therefore,
(L_{t-1}-r_{t})/\omega_{t}<\xi_{t}\leq L_{t-1}-\alpha_{t}
. Similarly, on the discovery branch, continuation requires
L_{t-1}-\alpha_{t}<\xi_{t}<(r_{t}-\alpha_{t})/(1-\omega_{t})
.

Hence, the two restrictions glue into a single open interval, and

\{L_{t}<r_{t}\}\Longleftrightarrow\frac{L_{t-1}-r_{t}}{\omega_{t}}<\xi_{t}<\frac{r_{t}-\alpha_{t}}{1-\omega_{t}}.

(43)

Substituting
\xi_{t}=x_{t}-(m_{t-1}+\alpha_{t})
into (
43
) yields

m_{t-1}+\alpha_{t}+\frac{L_{t-1}-r_{t}}{\omega_{t}}<x_{t}<m_{t-1}+\alpha_{t}+\frac{r_{t}-\alpha_{t}}{1-\omega_{t}}.

The endpoints are
\underline{x}_{t}(H_{t-1})
and
\overline{x}_{t}(H_{t-1})
as defined in the proposition, so (
16
) holds.
\hfill\qed

Proof of Proposition
13

Stopping mass (
17
).

The optimal policy stops at epoch
t
if and only if the user has survived to
t

and
the threshold criterion fires there. Formally,
\{\tau=t\}=\{\tau\geq t\}\cap\{L_{t}\geq\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star}\}
. By the definition of
\nu_{t}
as the law of
L_{t}
restricted to
\{\tau\geq t\}
,

\Pr(\tau=t)=\Pr\bigl\{L_{t}\geq\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star},\tau\geq t\bigr\}=\int_{[\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star},\infty)}\nu_{t}(dl),

which is (
17
).

Push-forward (
18
).

Survival to epoch
t+1
means the user survived to
t
and did
not
stop there:
\{\tau\geq t+1\}=\{\tau\geq t\}\cap\{L_{t}<\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star}\}
. For any Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, unpacking the definition of
\nu_{t+1}
gives

\nu_{t+1}(A)=\Pr\bigl\{L_{t+1}\in A,\tau\geq t+1\bigr\}=\Pr\bigl\{L_{t+1}\in A,\tau\geq t,L_{t}<\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star}\bigr\}.

(44)

The indicators
\mathbf{1}\{\tau\geq t\}
and
\mathbf{1}\{L_{t}<\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star}\}
are both
H_{t}
-measurable (the first because
\tau
is a stopping time, the second because
L_{t}
and
\kappa_{t}^{\star}
are determined by
H_{t}
). By the Markov property of
\{L_{t}\}
established in Lemma
11
,
\Pr\{L_{t+1}\in A\mid H_{t}\}=K_{t}^{L}(L_{t},A)
. Applying the law of iterated expectations
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid H_{t}]]
to (
44
):

\displaystyle\nu_{t+1}(A)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbf{1}\{\tau\geq t\}\mathbf{1}\{L_{t}<\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star}\}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{1}\{L_{t+1}\in A\}\bigm|H_{t}\bigr]\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbf{1}\{\tau\geq t\}\mathbf{1}\{L_{t}<\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star}\}K_{t}^{L}(L_{t},A)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\int_{(-\infty,\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star})}K_{t}^{L}(l,A)\nu_{t}(dl),

where the last line rewrites the expectation as an integral against
\nu_{t}
(the law of
L_{t}
weighted by
\mathbf{1}\{\tau\geq t\}
). This is (
18
).

Termination and total mass.

The convention
\alpha_{N+1}+\kappa_{N}^{\star}:=-\infty
makes the stopping region at the horizon equal to all of
\mathbb{R}
, so
\Pr(\tau=N)=\nu_{N}(\mathbb{R})
. It remains to verify that the masses sum to one. Observe that
\nu_{t}(\mathbb{R})=\Pr(\tau\geq t)
. For each
t\leq N-1
,

\Pr(\tau\geq t)=\underbrace{\Pr(\tau=t)}_{\text{stop at }t}+\underbrace{\Pr(\tau\geq t+1)}_{\text{survive to }t+1}=\nu_{t}\bigl([\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star},\infty)\bigr)+\nu_{t+1}(\mathbb{R}),

so summing
\Pr(\tau=t)
for
t=0,\ldots,N-1
telescopes:

\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\Pr(\tau=t)=\nu_{0}(\mathbb{R})-\nu_{N}(\mathbb{R})=\Pr(\tau\geq 0)-\Pr(\tau=N)=1-\Pr(\tau=N).

Adding
\Pr(\tau=N)
to both sides gives
\sum_{t=0}^{N}\Pr(\tau=t)=1
.
\hfill\qed

Proof of Proposition
14

Assumption
3
gives
\tau^{\star}\geq 1
under the baseline policy. We show convergence of the one-inspection probability to
1
at both tails of
\Delta
, then continuity of
\Delta\mapsto\SR(\Delta)
.

Tail limits.

By Section
7
, reaching rank
2
is exactly the event
\{x_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{2}\}
, and the survival region at depth
2
is the bounded interval
\mathcal{C}_{2}=(s_{1}^{-},s_{1}^{+})\subset\mathbb{R}
whose endpoints are the cut-losses and commit thresholds of Proposition
7
. Under the short-run law at shift
\Delta
,
x_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{0}+\Delta+\alpha_{1},\sigma_{\eta}^{2})
, and the Gaussian mass on any fixed bounded interval vanishes as the mean diverges:
\Pr_{\Delta}(\tau^{\star}\geq 2)=\Pr_{\Delta}(x_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{2})\to 0
as
\Delta\to\pm\infty
. Combined with the bound
\tau^{\star}\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,

1\leq\SR(\Delta)\leq\Pr_{\Delta}(\tau^{\star}=1)+N\Pr_{\Delta}(\tau^{\star}\geq 2)=1+(N-1)\Pr_{\Delta}(\tau^{\star}\geq 2),

so
\SR(\Delta)\to 1
at both tails.

Continuity.

Fix
\Delta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. By Section
7
, the event
\{\tau^{\star}=t\}
corresponds to the region

E_{t}:=\bigl\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{t}:(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t-1})\in\mathcal{C}_{t},x_{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t-1})\bigr\},

with the second condition vacuous when
t=N
, since
\mathcal{S}_{N}=\mathbb{R}
. The survival region
\mathcal{C}_{t}
and the stopping set
\mathcal{S}_{t}
are determined by the user’s baseline policy, which does not depend on
\Delta
; hence
E_{t}
is fixed across
\Delta
.

Under the short-run law at shift
\Delta
, the joint density of
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
is

g_{t}(x;\Delta):=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\frac{1}{\sigma_{\eta}}\phi\left(\frac{x_{i}-m_{0}-\Delta-\alpha_{i}}{\sigma_{\eta}}\right),

continuous in
\Delta

is independent of
\Delta

For
\Delta

\hfill\qed

Proof of Proposition
15

Translation invariance.

Add a constant
a
to the true mean, the prior mean, the outside option, and every realized relevance. Then
x_{t}\to x_{t}+a
for every
t
, the posterior mean satisfies
m_{t}\to m_{t}+a
at every history by induction, the running max satisfies
M_{t}\to M_{t}+a
, and the lead
L_{t}=M_{t}-m_{t}
is unchanged. The reservation thresholds depend only on prior precision, inspection variance, and rank shifts; none is affected by the shift. Hence the optimal stopping rule is identical, and
T(\mu+a,m_{0}+a,x_{b}+a)=T(\mu,m_{0},x_{b})
for every
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Setting
a=-\Delta
in
\LR(\Delta)=T(m_{0}+\Delta,m_{0}+\Delta,x_{b})
gives (
19
).

Monotonicity of
\LR
in
\Delta
.

Couple two old-prior environments with the same realized relevance path and the same prior mean
m_{0}
, but outside options
x_{b}^{\prime}>x_{b}^{\prime\prime}
. The posterior mean trajectory is identical in the two coupled environments, while the running maxima satisfy
M_{t}(x_{b}^{\prime\prime})\leq M_{t}(x_{b}^{\prime})
at every common history. The lead under the lower outside option is therefore weakly smaller, so whenever the lower-outside-option user reaches the stopping region
L_{t}\geq\alpha_{t+1}+\kappa_{t}^{\star}
(a region that does not depend on

), the higher-outside-option user has already reached it. Hence

pathwise, and

. Substituting into (

\mathbb{R}
.
\hfill\qed

Proof of Proposition
16

Write
F(\mu):=T(\mu,m_{0},x_{b})
, so that
\SR(\Delta)=F(m_{0}+\Delta)
and
\SR^{\prime}(0)=F^{\prime}(m_{0})
. We compute
F^{\prime}(\mu)
by a score-function argument applied to each survival probability, and then translate the result into the form stated in the proposition.

Throughout the proof, the user’s policy is fixed at the old-prior optimum, so
\tau^{\star}
is the same measurable function of
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N-1})
for every
\mu
. Only the law of the data changes with
\mu
: under
\Pr_{\mu}
, the residuals
\eta_{i}:=x_{i}-\mu-\alpha_{i}
are iid
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\eta}^{2})
, so
x_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu+\alpha_{i},\sigma_{\eta}^{2})
independently across
i
.

Step 1: write
F
as a sum of survival probabilities.
Since
\tau^{\star}\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, the tail-sum identity gives

F(\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\tau^{\star}]=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\Pr_{\mu}(A_{t}),\qquad A_{t}:=\{\tau^{\star}\geq t\}.

By Assumption
3
,
A_{1}
is the whole sample space, so
\Pr_{\mu}(A_{1})=1
for every
\mu
and the
t=1
term drops out of
F^{\prime}(\mu)
. For
t\geq 2
, the survival region
\mathcal{C}_{t}\subset\mathbb{R}^{t-1}
of Section
7
characterizes
A_{t}
as
A_{t}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t-1})\in\mathcal{C}_{t}\}
, so
\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}
depends on the inspected draws alone and not on
\mu
.

Step 2: write each survival probability as a Gaussian integral over
\mathcal{C}_{t}
.
The joint density of
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t-1})
under
\Pr_{\mu}
, evaluated at
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t-1})
, is

g_{t}(x;\mu):=\prod_{i=1}^{t-1}\frac{1}{\sigma_{\eta}}\phi\left(\frac{x_{i}-\mu-\alpha_{i}}{\sigma_{\eta}}\right),

so the survival probability is the mass that
g_{t}
puts on
\mathcal{C}_{t}
:

\Pr_{\mu}(A_{t})=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}g_{t}(x;\mu)dx.

(45)

Step 3: differentiate (
45
) via the score function.
Since
g_{t}
is
C^{\infty}
in
\mu
at each fixed
x
, the Leibniz integral rule (the region
\mathcal{C}_{t}
does not depend on
\mu
, and its integrability hypothesis is verified in the dominated-convergence paragraph below) gives:

\frac{d}{d\mu}\Pr_{\mu}(A_{t})=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\frac{\partial g_{t}}{\partial\mu}(x;\mu)dx.

The standard identity
\partial_{\mu}g_{t}=g_{t}\cdot\partial_{\mu}\log g_{t}
, valid wherever
g_{t}>0
, rewrites this integral as a
\Pr_{\mu}
-expectation. The log-density of a Gaussian family has a clean derivative:

\frac{\partial\log g_{t}}{\partial\mu}(x;\mu)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}(x_{i}-\mu-\alpha_{i})=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\eta_{i}.

Substituting
\partial_{\mu}g_{t}=g_{t}\cdot\partial_{\mu}\log g_{t}
and recognizing
\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}g_{t}\cdot(\cdot)dx=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\cdot(\cdot)]
,

\frac{d}{d\mu}\Pr_{\mu}(A_{t})=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\eta_{i}\right].

(46)

Verifying the integrability hypothesis.
For
\mu
in any neighborhood
[\bar{\mu}-\delta,\bar{\mu}+\delta]
of a fixed
\bar{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}
,
|\partial_{\mu}g_{t}(x;\mu)|
is bounded by
g_{t}(x;\mu)
times an affine function of
|x_{i}-\bar{\mu}-\alpha_{i}|
, which is integrable against the Gaussian density uniformly in
\mu
on the neighborhood. The integrability hypothesis of Leibniz’s rule therefore holds, and the interchange of
d/d\mu
with
\int_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}
in Step 3 is justified.

Step 4: sum over
t
and reorder.
Adding (
46
) across
t=2,\ldots,N
,

F^{\prime}(\mu)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\sum_{t=2}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\eta_{i}\right].

On the event
\{\tau^{\star}=k\}
,
\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}=1
for
t\leq k
and
0
otherwise, so the inner double sum becomes

\sum_{t=2}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\eta_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\Bigl(\sum_{t=i+1}^{k}1\Bigr)\eta_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(k-i)\eta_{i},

where the first equality switches the order of summation. Hence

F^{\prime}(\mu)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\tau^{\star}-1}(\tau^{\star}-i)\eta_{i}\right],

with the convention that the sum is empty on
\{\tau^{\star}=1\}
. Evaluating at
\mu=m_{0}
gives the claimed expression for
\SR^{\prime}(0)=F^{\prime}(m_{0})
. The same dominated-convergence argument shows
F^{\prime}
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
.

Step 5: local sign comparison.
If
\SR^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
, then by continuity of
F^{\prime}
there is a neighborhood
(m_{0}-\varepsilon,m_{0}+\varepsilon)
on which
F^{\prime}
is bounded away from zero with sign equal to
\operatorname{sign}(\SR^{\prime}(0))
. So
F
is strictly monotone in the same direction on that neighborhood, giving

\operatorname{sign}\bigl(\SR(\Delta)-T(m_{0},m_{0},x_{b})\bigr)=\operatorname{sign}\bigl(F(m_{0}+\Delta)-F(m_{0})\bigr)=\operatorname{sign}(\Delta)\cdot\operatorname{sign}\bigl(\SR^{\prime}(0)\bigr)

for every
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}
with
0<|\Delta|<\varepsilon
.
