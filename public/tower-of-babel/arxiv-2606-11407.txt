The Harish-Chandra isomorphism for supersymmetric spaces and Ghost distributions
Shifra Reif, Siddhartha Sahi, Vera Serganova and Alexander Sherman

Abstract
. We prove the Harish-Chandra isomorphism theorem for supersymmetric spaces, describing the polynomial algebra of eigenvalues of invariant differential operators. The polynomials obtained satisfy novel invariance conditions, which remain somewhat mysterious. We also prove the Harish-Chandra isomorphism for ghost distributions, which satisfy a ‘square root’ of the invariance conditions coming from invariant differential operators. All proofs are algebraic, and rely on a rank-one reduction argument and the Chevalley restriction theorem.



1.
Introduction

The classical Harish-Chandra theorem describes the center of the universal enveloping algebra in terms of (
\rho
-shifted) polynomials in the Cartan subalgebra which are symmetric under the Weyl group. Harish-Chandra extended this isomorphism to the context of symmetric spaces, where the Cartan subalgebra is replaced by a Cartan subspace, and one uses the corresponding
restricted
root system to obtain an appropriate Weyl group and Weyl vector
[
7
]
. This theorem has extensive applications in differential geometry, integrable systems, special functions, and representation theory.

The Harish-Chandra theorem for (Kac-Moody) Lie superalgebras describing the center of the universal enveloping algebra was given by Sergeev
[
19
]
and Gorelik-Kac
[
6
]
. They proved that the center is isomorphic to (
\rho
-shifted) Weyl group invariant polynomials in the Cartan subalgebra satisfying a special ‘odd invariance’ condition
f(\lambda+\alpha)=f(\lambda)
for
(\lambda+\rho,\alpha)=0
for all isotropic roots
\alpha
. Mathematicians have grappled since with the correct formulation of this theorem, introducing (various incarnations of) the Weyl groupoid, and viewing these polynomials as the Weyl groupoid invariant polynomials in the Cartan subalgebra
[
20
,
21
]
.

1.1.
Invariant differential operators

We extend the work of Sergeev and Gorelik-Kac to supersymmetric spaces for Kac-Moody Lie superalgebras.
Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional, complex, indecomposable Kac-Moody Lie superalgebra (for example
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(m|n)
for
m\neq n
,
\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(n|n)
, or
\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n))
. Let
\theta
be an involution of
\mathfrak{g}
preserving an invariant form on
\mathfrak{g}
.
Write
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
, where
\mathfrak{k}
is the fixed subalgebra and
\mathfrak{p}
is the
(-1)
-eigenspace of
\theta
. Let
\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}:=(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{k})^{\mathfrak{k}}
, where
\mathfrak{k}
acts via left multiplication (or equivalently by the adjoint action). This is an algebra which agrees with the algebra of invariant differential operators on some global supersymmetric space associated to
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
.

Assume that
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
admits an Iwasawa decomposition,
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
.
Since
(\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}},\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}})
will be a symmetric pair in the classical sense, we may choose a Cartan subspace
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}_{\overline{0}}
which is unique up to conjugacy. Then after choice of positive restricted roots (see below), we obtain a Harish-Chandra projection

HC:\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{k}\to S(\mathfrak{a}),

which is an algebra homomorphism when restricted to
\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
. We aim to describe
HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
, which will in particular describe the spectrum of invariant differential operators.

To obtain a description, consider the restricted root system
\Delta\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{*}
of
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
, which is given by the set of non-zero restrictions of roots of
\mathfrak{g}
to
\mathfrak{a}
. The restricted root system of
(\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}},\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}})
will sit inside
\Delta
from which we obtain a (baby) Weyl group
W\subseteq\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{a})
. To every (restricted) root
\alpha\in\Delta
, we have a root space
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
, and we set

m_{\alpha,0}:=\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\overline{0}},\ \ \ m_{\alpha,1}:=\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\overline{1}},\ \ \ n_{\alpha}:=m_{\alpha,1}/2,

(note that
m_{\alpha,1}
is always even, so
n_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}
). We say that
\alpha
is
regular
(resp.
singular
) if
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}
generate a subalgebra containing a copy
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(2)
(resp.
\mathfrak{g}_{\pm\alpha}
are purely odd). One can show that every (restricted) root is either regular or singular. Finally, make a choice of positive restricted roots
\Delta^{+}\subseteq\Delta
, from which we obtain a Weyl vector
\rho\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
defined as:

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\Delta^{+}}(m_{\alpha,0}-m_{\alpha,1})\alpha.

We will write
S(\mathfrak{a})^{W_{\raisebox{-0.5382pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\cdot$}}}}
for the
W
-invariant polynomials under the
\rho
-shifted action of
W
on
S(\mathfrak{a})
(the dot action).

Define
J_{\mathfrak{a}}\subseteq S(\mathfrak{a})
to consist of those
f\in S(\mathfrak{a})^{W_{\raisebox{-0.5382pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\cdot$}}}}
such that for any (restricted) root
\alpha
and any
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
satisfying
(\lambda+\rho,\alpha)=0
,

(1.1)

f(\lambda+r\alpha)=f(\lambda-r\alpha),\quad 1\leq r\leq n_{\alpha}.

Theorem 1.1
.

The Harish-Chandra projection gives an isomorphism of algebras

\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}\xrightarrow{\sim}J_{\mathfrak{a}}.

The authors are not aware of the peculiar invariance condition (
1.1
) appearing elsewhere in the literature.
A description of
HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
was also given in
[
2
]
using techniques from integration theory on CS manifolds, but no explicit invariance conditions were given to describe the polynomials in
HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
in
loc. cit.
Moreover, our proof is purely algebraic and more in the spirit of Lepowsky’s arguments in
[
9
,
10
]
.

1.2.
Ghost distributions

In the super setting there is a parallel story to the above, which began with the introduction of the sCasimir (or Casimir’s ghost) in
[
1
]
, an element in the enveloping algebra of
\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2n)
which squares to a central element. In
[
5
]
Gorelik generalised the work of
[
1
]
with the introduction of the anti-center
\mathcal{A}
and ghost center
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
, which lie in the enveloping algebra of an arbitrary Lie superalgebra, and work as a useful enlargement of the center. For
\mathfrak{g}
Kac-Moody, Gorelik described the Harish-Chandra image of
\mathcal{A}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}
.

In
[
22
]
, Gorelik’s theory of the ghost center was generalized to the setting of supersymmetric spaces as follows. If
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is a supersymmetric pair, we consider the new supersymmetric pair
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k}^{\prime})
arising from the involution
\delta\circ\theta
, where
\delta(x)=(-1)^{\overline{x}}x
is the grading operator. The Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
acts naturally on the distributions of
G/K
, i.e.
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{k}
, via left multiplication and we write
\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
for the invariants of this action. We call the elements of
\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}

ghost distributions
. It is always the case that
\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
is a module over
\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
, and the same will be true of its Harish-Chandra image. The Harish-Chandra image of
\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
contains information about the following branching problem: when does a spherical representation
V
of
\mathfrak{g}
contain a copy of
I_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}(\mathbb{C})
, the indecomposable injective on the trivial module for
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
.

In many cases of interest, it happens that
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k}^{\prime})
also satisfies the Iwasawa decomposition, in which case it is conjugate to
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
and we call the pairs
interlacing
. When
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is interlacing, it was shown in
[
23
]
that
\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}\oplus\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
has the structure of an algebra such that the Harish-Chandra projection is an algebra homomorphism, generalizing Gorelik’s ghost center.

We now provide a description of
HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
with respect to a chosen base
\Sigma\subseteq\Delta
. For a restricted root
\alpha\in\Delta
, we say that
\alpha
is
odd
if it is the restriction of an odd root from
\mathfrak{g}
, and otherwise we say it is
purely even
. Write
\Delta_{odd}
for the set of odd roots, and
\Delta_{ev}
for the purely even roots. Define the
pure Weyl vector
to be :

\rho^{\text{pure}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\Delta_{ev}^{+}}m_{\alpha,0}\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\Delta_{odd}^{+}}m_{\alpha,1}\alpha.

We have
\rho^{\text{pure}}=\rho
if and only if
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is interlacing.
Consider the following polynomial
T_{\Sigma}\in S(\mathfrak{a})
, where for a root
\alpha
we write
h_{\alpha}(\lambda):=(\alpha,\lambda)
:

T_{\Sigma}:=T_{reg}T_{iso},

where

T_{reg}:=\prod\limits_{\alpha\in\Delta_{reg}^{+}}\prod\limits_{s=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}(h_{\alpha}+(\rho^{\text{pure}}+(1-n_{\alpha}+2s)\alpha,\alpha)),

T_{iso}:=\prod\limits_{\alpha\in\Delta^{+}_{iso}}(h_{\alpha}+(\rho,\alpha)).

Then define
M_{\mathfrak{a}}\subseteq S(\mathfrak{a})
to consist of those polynomials
f\in S(\mathfrak{a})^{W_{\raisebox{-0.5382pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\cdot$}}}}T_{\Sigma}
such that for admissible singular roots
\alpha
(Definition
2.18
), we have

(1.2)

f(\lambda+r\alpha)=(-1)^{r}f(\lambda-r\alpha),\ \ \ 1\leq r\leq n_{\alpha},

for
(\lambda+\rho,\alpha)=0
.

Theorem 1.2
.

The Harish-Chandra projection gives an isomorphism

\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}\xrightarrow{\sim}M_{\mathfrak{a}}.

We note that
M_{\mathfrak{a}}
is a
J_{\mathfrak{a}}
-module. Further, if
\rho=\rho^{\text{pure}}
(i.e.
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is interlacing), then
T_{\Sigma}^{2}
is
W_{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\cdot$}}}
-invariant, and the product of two polynomials in
M_{\mathfrak{a}}
lies in
J_{\mathfrak{a}}
.

1.3.
An invariant Harish-Chandra homomorphism

The Harish-Chandra projection
HC
depends on a choice
\Delta^{+}
of positive restricted roots, and this is equivalent to a choice
\Sigma
of simple roots. For this subsection, let us make this dependency explicit by writing
HC_{\Sigma}
in place of
HC
. Already for
\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
and
\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
, we see a dependency on the Weyl vector in the image. In an attempt to construct a single Harish-Chandra homomorphism that lacks a dependency on
\Sigma
, let us introduce
hc_{\Sigma}=t_{\rho}^{*}\circ HC_{\Sigma}:\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{k}\to S(\mathfrak{a})
, where
t_{\rho}^{*}(f)(\mu)=f(\mu-\rho)
. Then we see that
hc_{\Sigma}(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
is the set of polynomials
f\in S(\mathfrak{a})^{W}
such that (
1.1
) holds whenever
(\lambda,\alpha)=0
. As a consequence of Proposition
5.15
, we can moreover show that when restricted to
\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
we have
hc_{\Sigma}=hc_{\Sigma^{\prime}}
for any two positive systems
\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}
. Thus we have a ‘well-defined’ Harish-Chandra homomorphism
\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}\to S(\mathfrak{a})
.

For ghost distributions, the image
hc_{\Sigma}(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
will depend on
\Sigma
in general due to the (somewhat awkward) dependence on
\rho^{\text{pure}}
and the set of admissible roots (see Example
3.3
). However in the interlacing case,
hc_{\Sigma}(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
will have a uniform description as the set of polynomials
f\in S(\mathfrak{a})
which are anti-invariant (without
\rho
-shift) under the action of
W
(see Lemma
3.7
), and satisfy (
1.2
) whenever
(\lambda,\alpha)=0
. By Proposition
6.4
, we have
hc_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}=(-1)^{n_{\alpha}}hc_{\Sigma}
for any singular root
\alpha\in\Sigma
, while
hc_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}=hc_{\Sigma}
whenever
\alpha\in\Sigma
is a regular root. It follows (by Lemma
2.16
) that up to sign we obtain a well-defined Harish-Chandra homomorphism
\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}\to S(\mathfrak{a})
.

1.4.
Methods of Proof

The proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
consist of two main steps. The first is to show that the image of the Harish-Chandra projection satisfies the required invariance conditions. The second is to prove that the projection maps are bijective.

To accomplish the first step, we established that the image of the Harish-Chandra map satisfies the desired invariance condition by restricting the pair to rank-one subalgebras corresponding to each root
\alpha
. For Theorem
1.1
it is done using that the center surjects onto the invariant differential operators for rank-one symmetric pairs. For Theorem
1.2
, it was proven by
[
23
, Sec. 9]
using representation-theoretic properties for the relevant rank-one pairs.
This gives the invariance conditions for simple roots.
We then analyze how the Harish-Chandra map transforms when changing the choices of simple roots, which allows us to extend these properties from simple roots to all admissible roots.
Having shown that the algebras of invariant polynomials can be defined using conditions corresponding to admissible roots, we complete this step of the proof.

To prove that the maps are bijective, we use the associated graded spaces and compare the graded dimensions. The associated graded algebra of the algebra of invariant distributions is the algebra
S(\mathfrak{p})^{\mathfrak{k}}
, shown to be isomorphic to the algebra
I(\mathfrak{a})
in the Chevalley restriction theorem
[
3
,
13
]
. We show that the associated graded algebra of
J_{\mathfrak{a}}
is also
I(\mathfrak{a})
.
A parallel argument holds for ghost distributions, where we show that the associated graded space of
\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
(as computed in
[
23
]
) is isomorphic to that of
M_{\mathfrak{a}}
.

1.5.
Outline of paper

The paper is organized as follows.
In
Section

, we review preliminaries about supersymmetric spaces and restricted root systems. This in particular includes properties of the Weyl group and its action, choices of simple roots, the notion of admissible roots, and properties of interlacing pairs.

In
Section

3
we define two subspaces of invariant polynomials. We will later show that they are precisely the images of the invariant distributions and ghost distributions under the Harish-Chandra projection.

Section

4
introduces the vector space of distributions, the Harish-Chandra projection map, and its behavior upon restriction to rank-one subalgebras.
In
Section

5
, we prove
1.1
and in
Section

6
we discuss ghost distributions and prove
1.2
.

Finally, in an appendix we prove that when

\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{t}\mathfrak{r}(\operatorname{ad}x)=0
for any
x\in\mathfrak{k}
then
(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{k}}\cap\mathfrak{k}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}=(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{k}}\cap\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\,\mathfrak{k}
. This generalizes a classical result for Lie algebras (see for example,
[
4
, Prop 9.1.10(ii)]
).

2.
Supersymmetric pairs and Restricted root systems

For background on Kac-Moody superalgebras, we refer the reader to
[
17
]
.

2.1.
Supersymmetric pairs

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Kac-Moody superalgebra with a chosen nondegenerate, invariant form
(-,-)
. Let
\theta
be an involution of
\mathfrak{g}
which preserves
(-,-)
. Set
\mathfrak{k}
to be the fixed-point subalgebra of
\theta
and write
\mathfrak{p}
for the
(-1)
-eigenspace. Let
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}_{\overline{0}}
be a Cartan subspace, meaning a maximal even subalgebra of
\mathfrak{p}
which consists only of semisimple elements. Note that a Cartan subspace is abelian and unique up to conjugation by inner automorphism from
\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}
acting on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
(
[
28
, Lem. 26.15]
). We extend
\mathfrak{a}
to a
\theta
-stable Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
of
\mathfrak{g}
, and write
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{t}
where
\mathfrak{t}:=\mathfrak{k}\cap\mathfrak{h}
.

Since
\theta
preserves the form, notice that
(-,-)
will descend to a nondegenerate form on
\mathfrak{a}
, and thus also on
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
. As usual, we will call an element
\alpha\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}

isotropic
if
(\alpha,\alpha)=0
, and otherwise we will say that
\alpha
is
non-isotropic
.

2.2.
Restricted root systems

Write
\Delta\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{*}\setminus\{0\}
for the set of nonzero weights for the action of
\mathfrak{a}
on
\mathfrak{g}
. It is easy to see that
\Delta
is obtained by taking the nonzero restrictions of roots of
\mathfrak{g}
(in
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
) to
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
. We call
\Delta
the
restricted root system
of
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
, and elements
\alpha\in\Delta
are called
restricted roots
.

Definition 2.1
.

For a restricted root
\alpha\in\Delta
, define its multiplicity
m_{\alpha}
to be the pair
(m_{\alpha,0}|m_{\alpha,1})
, where

m_{\alpha,i}:=\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\overline{i}}.

Note that
m_{\alpha,1}
is always an even number due to the pairing
\langle x,y\rangle=(\theta(x),y)
being a symplectic form on
(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\overline{1}}
,
[
3
, Prop. 2.10]
. Because of this, we will write

n_{\alpha}:=m_{\alpha,1}/2\in\mathbb{N}.

Definition 2.2
.

A restricted root
\alpha\in\Delta
is called
singular
if
m_{k\alpha,0}=0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}
, and
regular
otherwise.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{Z}\Delta\to\mathbb{R}
be a group homomorphism such that
\phi(\alpha)\neq 0
for all
\alpha\in\Delta
. Then set

\Delta^{\pm}_{\phi}:=\{\alpha\in\Delta:\pm\phi(\alpha)>0\}\subseteq\Delta.

It is clear that
\Delta=\Delta_{\phi}^{+}\sqcup\Delta_{\phi}^{-}
.

Definition 2.3
.

A
positive system
is a choice of subset
\Delta^{+}\subseteq\Delta
such that
\Delta^{+}=\Delta^{+}_{\phi}
for some
\phi
.

Definition 2.4
.

We say
\Sigma\subseteq\Delta
is a simple system (or a base, or a choice of simple roots) if
\Sigma
is linearly independent in
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
and

\Delta\subseteq\pm\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\Sigma}\mathbb{N}\alpha.

Lemma 2.5
.

(1)

For a positive system
\Delta^{+}
, let
\Sigma\subseteq\Delta^{+}
be the set of indecomposable roots. Then
\Sigma
is a simple system.

(2)

For a simple system
\Sigma
, we obtain a positive system by setting:

\Delta^{+}=\Delta\cap\left(\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\Sigma}\mathbb{N}\alpha\right).

Further, the above correspondence is a bijection.

Proof.

(1) follows from Lem. 5.10 of
[
25
]
, and (2) is clear. The fact that the correspondence is bijective is Prop. 5.11 of
[
25
]
.
∎

In what follows, for a simple system
\Sigma\subseteq\Delta
, we write
\Delta^{+}_{\Sigma}:=\Delta\cap\left(\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\Sigma}\mathbb{N}\alpha\right)
for the corresponding positive system.

2.3.
Little Weyl group

Definition 2.6
.

For a regular root
\alpha\in\Delta
define the reflection
r_{\alpha}\in GL(\mathfrak{a}^{*})
to be

r_{\alpha}(\lambda)=\lambda-2\frac{(\lambda,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\alpha.

Definition 2.7
.

Define the little Weyl group
W
of
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
with respect to
\mathfrak{a}
to be the subgroup of
GL(\mathfrak{a}^{*})
generated by the reflections
r_{\alpha}
for all regular roots
\alpha\in\Delta
.

Lemma 2.8
.

(1)

The little Weyl group
W
of
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is equal to the little Weyl group of
(\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}},\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}})
.

(2)

We have an isomorphism

N_{K}(\mathfrak{a})/C_{K}(\mathfrak{a})\cong W.

Proof.

The first claim is obvious, and the second claim is classical (Prop. 26.19,
[
28
]
).
∎

2.4.
Singular reflections

For a simple system
\Sigma
and a singular root
\alpha\in\Sigma
, we define
r_{\alpha}\Sigma
to be the simple system corresponding to the positive system
(\Delta^{+}_{\Sigma}\setminus\{\alpha\})\cup\{-\alpha\}
.

We call the correspondence
\Sigma\mapsto r_{\alpha}\Sigma
a
singular reflection
.

2.5.
Iwasawa decomposition

Write
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{c}(\mathfrak{a})\cap\mathfrak{k}
. We impose the following
assumption
for the rest of the paper:

(2.1)

\mathfrak{c}(\mathfrak{a})=\mathfrak{m}\oplus\mathfrak{a}.

For a simple system
\Sigma,
we define

\mathfrak{n}_{\Sigma}:=\bigoplus\limits_{\alpha\in\Delta_{\Sigma}^{+}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}.

We will abbreviate
\mathfrak{n}_{\Sigma}
by
\mathfrak{n}
when the context is clear. Set
\mathfrak{q}=\mathfrak{m}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
; notice that this is a parabolic subalgebra with Levi subalgebra
\mathfrak{c}(\mathfrak{a})
. By our assumption (
2.1
),
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
admits an Iwasawa decomposition:

(2.2)

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}.

In particular we have a decomposition

(2.3)

\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}=S(\mathfrak{a})\oplus(\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}+(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{k}).

2.6.
Rank-one subalgebra

For
\alpha\in\Delta
we write:

\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{c}(\mathfrak{a})\oplus\bigoplus\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}}\mathfrak{g}_{k\alpha}.

Then
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)
is again Kac-Moody (
[
18
, Lem. 3.1]
), is
\theta
-stable, and we have a decomposition
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{k}(\alpha)\oplus\mathfrak{p}(\alpha)
. If we let
\mathfrak{n}(\alpha):=\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{2\alpha}
, then our Iwasawa decomposition (
2.1
) persists, i.e.
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{k}(\alpha)\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}(\alpha)
.

Assume further that we have chosen a base
\Sigma\subseteq\Delta^{+}
and that
\alpha\in\Sigma
, and write

(2.4)

\mathfrak{n}^{\alpha}:=\bigoplus\limits_{\beta\in\Delta^{+}\setminus\{\alpha,2\alpha\}}\mathfrak{g}_{\beta},

so that
\mathfrak{n}=\mathfrak{n}(\alpha)\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{\alpha}
. Choose a
\mathfrak{k}(\alpha)
-stable complement
\mathfrak{r}
of
\mathfrak{k}(\alpha)
in
\mathfrak{k}
. Then we have

(2.5)

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{r}\oplus\mathfrak{k}(\alpha)\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}(\alpha)\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{\alpha}=\mathfrak{r}\oplus\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{\alpha}.

Observe that since
\alpha
is simple,
\mathfrak{n}^{\alpha}
is
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)
-stable, and thus
\mathfrak{k}(\alpha)
-stable, meaning the above decomposition is
\mathfrak{k}(\alpha)
-stable. It follows that we obtain a
\mathfrak{k}(\alpha)
-stable decomposition

(2.6)

\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}=\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)\oplus((\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{r}+\mathfrak{n}^{\alpha}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}).

2.7.
Weyl vector

Definition 2.9
.

For a choice of simple roots
\Sigma\subseteq\Delta
, define the Weyl vector
\rho_{\Sigma}
by

\rho=\rho_{\Sigma}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\Delta^{+}}(m_{\alpha,0}-m_{\alpha,1})\alpha.

Further, for
\alpha\in\Sigma
we set
\rho_{\alpha}
to be the Weyl vector for
(\mathfrak{g}(\alpha),\mathfrak{k}(\alpha))
with respect to the positive system
\{\alpha\}
.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\Delta
be a choice of simple roots, and let
\alpha\in\Sigma
.

(1)

If
\alpha
is regular then:

\rho_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}=r_{\alpha}(\rho_{\Sigma}).

(2)

If
\alpha
is singular then:

\rho_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}=\rho_{\Sigma}+m_{\alpha,1}\alpha.

Proof.

Follows from direct computation.
∎

Lemma 2.11
.

For a simple root
\alpha
, we have

(\rho,\alpha)=(\rho_{\alpha},\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}(m_{\alpha,0}+2m_{2\alpha,0}-m_{\alpha,1})(\alpha,\alpha).

Proof.

The second equality is clear by definition. Thus it suffices to check that
(\rho-\rho_{\alpha},\alpha)=0
. If
\alpha
is regular, then
r_{\alpha}
preserves
\Delta^{+}\setminus(\Delta^{+}\cap\mathbb{N}\alpha)
. Thus
r_{\alpha}(\rho-\rho_{\alpha})=\rho-\rho_{\alpha}
, and we have

(\rho-\rho_{\alpha},\alpha)=(r_{\alpha}(\rho-\rho_{\alpha}),r_{\alpha}(\alpha))=-(\rho-\rho_{\alpha},\alpha),

and thus
(\rho-\rho_{\alpha},\alpha)=0.

Now let us deal with the case when
\alpha
is singular. For this, recall that
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)
acts on
\mathfrak{n}^{\alpha}
(
2.4
). Let
h_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{a}
denote the coroot of
\alpha
, so that
(\lambda,\alpha)=\lambda(h_{\alpha})
for all
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
. Then
h_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)
, and
(\rho-\rho_{\alpha},\alpha)
is exactly the supertrace of
h_{\alpha}
acting on
\mathfrak{n}^{\alpha}
. It therefore suffices to prove that
h_{\alpha}
lies in the derived subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)
. In fact, it suffices to prove
h_{\alpha}
lies in
[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}]
. However by the classification of rank-one pairs with a singular simple root ((2) of Lemma
4.5
), we see that the subalgebra generated by
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}
is either
\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2n)
or
\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)\times\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)
. In the first case
\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2n)
is its own derived subalgebra, and the second case our claim can be checked directly.

∎

The following is

Lemma 2.16

Any two simple root systems

Definition 2.17
.

We say that a root

(1)

(2)

Proof.

, Lem. 2.24]

, Lem. 2.23]

2.10.
Dot action

Given
w\in W
and

, we define the ‘dot’ action, or
\rho
-shifted action, by:

(w_{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\cdot$}}}f)(\lambda):=f(w(\lambda+\rho_{\Sigma})-\rho_{\Sigma}).

It is important to note that the above dot action depends on
\Sigma
. We will make this dependence explicit whenever it is not clear from context. We will say
f\in S(\mathfrak{a})
is
W_{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\cdot$}}}
-invariant (resp.
W^{\prime}_{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\cdot$}}}
-invariant) if
w_{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\cdot$}}}f=f
for all
w\in W
(resp.
w\in W^{\prime}
).

For
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
, write
t_{\lambda}:\mathfrak{a}^{*}\to\mathfrak{a}^{*}
for the translation morphism
t_{\lambda}(\mu)=\mu+\lambda
. This induces an algebra automorphism
t_{\lambda}^{*}:S(\mathfrak{a})\to S(\mathfrak{a})
given by

t_{\lambda}^{*}(f)(\mu)=f(t_{\lambda}^{-1}(\mu))=f(\mu-\lambda).

Lemma 2.22
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\Delta
be a base, choose
\beta\in\Delta_{reg}
, and let
\alpha\in\Sigma
be a singular root. If

is invariant under the
\rho_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}
-shifted action of
r_{\beta}
, then

is invariant under the
\rho_{\Sigma}
-shifted action of
r_{\beta}
.

Proof.

By assumption,

f(r_{\beta}(\lambda+\rho_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma})-\rho_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma})=f(\lambda).

It follows that:

\displaystyle t_{m_{\alpha,1}\alpha}^{*}(f)(r_{\beta}(\lambda+\rho_{\Sigma})-\rho_{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(r_{\beta}((\lambda-m_{\alpha,1}\alpha)+\rho_{\Sigma}+m_{\alpha,1}\alpha)-\rho_{\Sigma}-m_{\alpha,1}\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(r_{\beta}(t_{-m_{\alpha,1}\alpha}(\lambda)+\rho_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma})-\rho_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t_{m_{\alpha,1}\alpha}^{*}(f)(\lambda).

∎

4.
Harish-Chandra projection and relationship to rank one

4.1.
Supersymmetric spaces

Let
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
be a supersymmetric pair as above, arising from an involution
\theta
. Then let
G
be a quasireductive supergroup, meaning that
G_{0}^{\circ}
is reductive, with Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
. We assume that
\theta
lifts to an involution of
G
, and continue to denote this involution by
\theta
. Finally set
K\subseteq G
to be a subgroup with
(G^{\theta})^{\circ}\subseteq K\subseteq G^{\theta}
. In particular,
K
will be a quasireductive supergroup.

We call the homogeneous superspace
G/K
a
supersymmetric variety
(we refer to
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
for details on homogeneous superspaces). Notice that
G/K
is smooth, affine, and admits an action by
G
on the left by translation,
a:G\times G/K\to G/K
. Write
\mathbb{C}[G/K]
for the algebra of regular functions on
G/K
; then
\mathbb{C}[G/K]
has the natural structure of a right
G
-module via pullback of functions along the action. This means that
\mathbb{C}[G/K]
is a left
\mathbb{C}[G]
-comodule, and the coaction is given by

a^{*}:\mathbb{C}[G/K]\to\mathbb{C}[G]\otimes\mathbb{C}[G/K].

Given
u\in\mathfrak{g}
viewed as an element of
T_{e}G
, we obtain a vector field on
G/K
via the formula:

(u\otimes 1)\circ a^{*}.

The induced linear map
\mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{Der}(\mathbb{C}[G/K])
is then an anti-homomorphism of Lie algebras, and thus we obtain an algebra homomorphism

(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})^{op}\to\operatorname{D}(G/K),

where
D(G/K)
denotes the algebra of differential operators on
G/K
. In particular,
\mathbb{C}[G/K]
is a right
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}
-module.

4.2.
Vector space of distributions

Continuing with the above discussion, set
\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)
to be the vector space of distributions supported at
eK
in
G/K
. To be precise, if we write
\mathfrak{m}_{eK}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[G/K]
for the maximal ideal of
eK
in
\mathbb{C}[G/K]
, then we have:

\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)=\bigcup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\mathbb{C}[G/K]/\mathfrak{m}_{eK}^{n}\right)^{*}.

Then
D(G/K)
acts on
\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)
on the right via:

D\cdot\gamma=\gamma\circ D.

Composing with our morphism
(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})^{op}\to D(G/K)
, we obtain a left action of
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}
on
\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

We have a
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}
-equivariant isomorphism

\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)\cong\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{k}.

Proof.

Because
G/K
is an affine homogeneous
G
-supervariety, the morphism

\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK),\ \ \ u\mapsto u\cdot\delta_{eK}=\delta_{eK}\circ u

is surjective, where
\delta_{eK}
is evaluation at
eK
. The kernel of this morphism is clearly
(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{k}
by the PBW theorem, so we are done.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

We make two remarks which will be used in the future:

(1)

The natural quotient map
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\to\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{k}
is
\mathfrak{k}
-equivariant, where
\mathfrak{k}
acts on
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}
by the adjoint action and on
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{k}
by left multiplication.

(2)

The action of
\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}
on
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{k}
is locally finite.

HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
induced by the natural one on

coming from distributions. Then we have:

\operatorname{gr}HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})\cong CR^{*}(S(\mathfrak{p})^{\mathfrak{k}}).

Proof.

Part (1) follows from Lemma

, and (2) follows from the injectivity of

(Prop. 4.2 of

∎

5.3.
The center of
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}

We recall the Harish-Chandra theorem for the center
Z(\mathfrak{g})
of
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}
. Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{u}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{u}^{+}
be a triangular decomposition of
\mathfrak{g}
. We assume that
\mathfrak{u}^{+}
contains
\mathfrak{n}
.
We define
\widetilde{HC}:\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow S(\mathfrak{h})
to be the ‘usual’ Harish-Chandra projection, given by projecting along
\mathfrak{u}^{-}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}+(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{u}^{+}
.

Lemma 5.5
.

The following diagram is commutative:

\textstyle{Z(\mathfrak{g})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\operatorname{proj}}
\scriptstyle{\widetilde{HC}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{HC}
\textstyle{S(\mathfrak{h})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\operatorname{proj}_{\mathfrak{a}}}
\textstyle{J_{\mathfrak{a}}}

Here,
\operatorname{proj}
is the composition of
Z(\mathfrak{g})\subseteq\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\to\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{k}
which lands in
\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
, and
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathfrak{a}}
is the quotient arising from the decomposition
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{t}
.

Proof.

We prove that
HC(\operatorname{proj}(z))=\operatorname{proj}_{\mathfrak{a}}(\widetilde{HC}(z))
for
z\in Z(\mathfrak{g})
by showing they take the same values on
\Lambda^{+}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{*}
, which we noted in Section
4.3
is dense in
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
.
For
z\in Z(\mathfrak{g})
, let
\Theta
denote the differential operator on
G/K
induced by
z
.
On the one hand, we have by (
4.2
):

(\operatorname{ev}_{eK}\Theta)f_{\lambda}=HC(\operatorname{proj}z)(-\lambda).

On the other hand, we have

(\operatorname{ev}_{eK}\Theta)f_{\lambda}=(\Theta f_{\lambda})(eK)=\widetilde{HC}(z)(-\lambda)f_{\lambda}(eK)=\widetilde{HC}(z)(-\lambda)

Note that
\Theta f_{\lambda}=\widetilde{HC}(z)(-\lambda)f_{\lambda}
because we are considering a right action. The result is now clear.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

One can prove the lemma by direct computation in
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}
, as follows. Since the maps in the diagram are projection maps, it is enough to show that the kernels are equal when intersected with
Z(\mathfrak{g})
.

Let
f\in Z(\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{g}}\cap(\mathfrak{u}^{-}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}+(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{u}^{+})
and write
f
as a linear combination of monomials with PBW basis in order that corresponds to the decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=(\mathfrak{u}^{-}\cap\mathfrak{n}^{-})\oplus(\mathfrak{u}^{-}\cap\mathfrak{k})\oplus\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus(\mathfrak{u}^{+}\cap\mathfrak{k})\oplus(\mathfrak{u}^{+}\cap\mathfrak{n})

where
\mathfrak{n}^{-}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in-\Delta^{+}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
.
If the part from
\mathfrak{u}^{+}\cap\mathfrak{n}
is nonzero, the monomial is in
(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{n}
, and thus in the kernel of
HC
. If the part from
\mathfrak{u}^{+}\cap\mathfrak{n}
is zero, then the part from
\mathfrak{u}^{-}\cap\mathfrak{n}^{-}
must be zero as well because the monomial has zero
\mathfrak{h}
-weight and, specifically, zero
\mathfrak{a}
-weight. Consequently, the monomial is in
\mathfrak{k}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}
, and therefore in the kernel of
HC
.

Suppose
f\in Z(\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{g}}\cap(\mathfrak{k}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}+(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{n})
and write
f
as a linear combination of monomials with PBW basis in order that corresponds to the decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=(\mathfrak{m}\cap\mathfrak{u}^{-})\oplus\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}\oplus(\mathfrak{m}\cap\mathfrak{u}^{+})\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}

where
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}=\operatorname{span}\{\theta e_{\alpha}+e_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\tilde{\Delta}^{+}\}
.
If the part in
\mathfrak{n}
or in
\mathfrak{m}\cap\mathfrak{u}^{-}
is nonzero, the monomial is in
\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{HC}
.
We note that
\mathfrak{m}\cap\mathfrak{u}^{+}
commutes with
\mathfrak{a}
so if the part in
\mathfrak{m}\cap\mathfrak{u}^{+}
is nonzero, the monomial is also in
\operatorname{ker}\widetilde{HC}
.
We are left with monomials generated by elements in
\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}\oplus\mathfrak{a}
.
Since the monomials of
f
must be of
\mathfrak{h}
-weight zero, the part in
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
must be zero. Indeed, a product of the form
\prod_{\alpha>0}(e_{\alpha}+\theta e_{\alpha})
will contain
\prod_{\alpha>0}e_{\alpha}
, which has a positive weight. Hence the monomials of
f
are generated by
\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{t}
is annihilated in
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathfrak{a}}
, completing the proof.

Corollary 5.7
.

The associated graded of the map
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathfrak{a}}\circ\widetilde{HC}:Z(\mathfrak{g})\to S(\mathfrak{a})
is given by the natural composition of projections
R:S(\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{g}}\to S(\mathfrak{h})\to S(\mathfrak{a})
. In particular, if
R
restricts to a surjection
S(\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{g}}\to CR(S(\mathfrak{p})^{\mathfrak{k}})
, then
Z(\mathfrak{g})\to HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
is also surjective. The cases for which
R
restricts a surjection are listed in
[
13
, Thm. B]
.

5.3.1.
Description of
\widetilde{HC}(Z(\mathfrak{g}))

By the previous section, we know that

\operatorname{proj}_{\mathfrak{a}}\widetilde{HC}(Z(\mathfrak{g}))\subseteq HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}).

We recall, for the benefit of the reader, the Sergeev–Kac–Gorelik description which describes
\widetilde{HC}(Z(\mathfrak{g}))

Let
\tilde{\Delta}=\tilde{\Delta}_{\bar{0}}\cup\tilde{\Delta}_{\bar{1}}\subset\mathfrak{h}^{*}

\tilde{\Delta}^{+}

\mathfrak{u}^{+}

Let

Let
\widetilde{W}
be the Weyl group with respect to

\tilde{\rho}
-shifted action on

w_{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\cdot$}}}\lambda=w(\lambda+\tilde{\rho})-\tilde{\rho}

The Harish-Chandra image of the center of the universal enveloping algebra can be described in the following theorem.

Theorem 5.8

(
[
6
,
8
,
19
]
)
.

The image of
\widetilde{HC}
applied to
Z(\mathfrak{g})
is equal to

\left\{f\in S(\mathfrak{h})^{\widetilde{W}_{\raisebox{-0.5382pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\cdot$}}}}\mid f(\lambda+\alpha)=f(\lambda)\text{ for }\lambda\in H_{\alpha},\alpha\in\tilde{\Delta}_{\bar{1}}\text{ s.t. }(\alpha,\alpha)=0\right\},

Remark 5.9
.

Theorem

is equivalent to the computation of
HC(Z(\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}))

.

5.4.
A rank-one reduction

Let
\alpha\in\Sigma
. Using the commutative square in Equation (
4.3
) and the equivariance of the action of
\mathfrak{k}(\alpha)
, we obtain the following commutative triangle:

(5.1)

\textstyle{\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{HC}
\textstyle{\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g}(\alpha),\mathfrak{k}(\alpha))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{HC_{\alpha}}
\textstyle{S(\mathfrak{a})}

where
HC_{\alpha}
is the Harish-Chandra map for the rank-one symmetric pair
(\mathfrak{g}(\alpha),\mathfrak{k}(\alpha))
, defined in Section
2.6
. In particular, we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 5.10
.

For a simple root
\alpha
, we have
HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})\subseteq HC_{\alpha}(\mathcal{Z}(\mathfrak{g}(\alpha),\mathfrak{k}(\alpha)))
.

Proposition 5.11
.

Suppose that
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is a rank-one symmetric pair with simple root
\alpha
.
Then
HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=J_{\mathfrak{a}}
.

Proof.

We use the classification given in Lemma
4.5
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}=0
.

For Case (1), we have
\mathbb{C}[HC(\Omega)]\subseteq J_{\mathfrak{a}}
, where
\Omega
is the quadratic Casimir element, and
HC(\Omega)
is a nonzero element of degree 2.
On the other hand, it is clear that
CR^{*}(S(\mathfrak{p})^{\mathfrak{k}})\subseteq S(\mathfrak{a})^{r_{\alpha}}
. Since
\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{a}=1
,
S(\mathfrak{a})^{r_{\alpha}}
, is the ring of even degree polynomials in one variable and is equal to
\operatorname{gr}\mathbb{C}[HC(\Omega)]
. We can thus conclude by Corollary
5.4
.

For Case (2), we use our classification of such pairs given in Lemma
4.5
.
The case of the diagonal pair
(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)\times\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1),\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1))
follows from Theorem
5.8
, see
Remark

5.9
.
It remains to consider the pair
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})=(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2n))
.
By
[
13
, Thm. B]
, for
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
of rank one, the map
S(\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{g}}\to S(\mathfrak{p})^{\mathfrak{k}}
is surjective, so we know from Corollary
5.7
that
J_{\mathfrak{a}}
is the image of the composition
Z(\mathfrak{g})\xrightarrow{\widetilde{HC}}S(\mathfrak{h})\to S(\mathfrak{a})
.
On the other hand,
[
13
, Thm. A]
or
[
3
, Prop. 4.5]
imply that in this case
CR^{*}(S(\mathfrak{p})^{\mathfrak{k}})
is of codimension
n
in
S(\mathfrak{a})
.
Since our proposed description of
J_{\mathfrak{a}}
also has codimension
n
in
S(\mathfrak{a})
, it only remains to show that the described invariance conditions hold.

Let
\epsilon\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
be the basis element such that
\epsilon(t)=1
, where
t\in\mathfrak{a}
and
\operatorname{ad}(t)
has eigenvalues
0,\pm 1
on
\mathfrak{g}
. This gives us a
\mathbb{Z}
-grading
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1}
, and we choose our positive system so that
\mathfrak{n}^{+}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. Set
K(s)=\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1}}^{\mathfrak{g}}\mathbb{C}_{s\epsilon}
to be the Kac-module of highest weight
s\epsilon
. Notice that since
\mathfrak{k}+(\mathfrak{g}_{0}+\mathfrak{g}_{1})=\mathfrak{g}
, we have

\operatorname{Res}_{\mathfrak{k}}K(s)\cong\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}}^{\mathfrak{k}}\mathbb{C}.

This
\mathfrak{k}
-module is semisimple and admits a one-dimensional
\mathfrak{k}
-fixed subspace, giving us a unique map to our global supersymmetric space:

K(s)\to\mathbb{C}[G/K].

In particular we have highest weight functions
f_{s}\in\mathbb{C}[G/K]^{\mathfrak{n}^{+}}
for every
s\in\mathbb{Z}
.

In Sec. 9.1 of
[
23
]
, it was shown that
f_{n-r}\in\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\cdot f_{n+r}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[G/K]
whenever
1\leq r\leq n
. It follows that any element of
\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
must act on
f_{n-r}
and
f_{n+r}
by the same scalar. This implies the invariance condition, using that
\rho=-n\epsilon
.
∎

Remark 5.12
.

We note that if
\alpha
is isotropic and
(\lambda+\rho,\alpha)=0
, then
(\lambda+\alpha+\rho,\alpha)=0
which implies that
f(\lambda)=f(\lambda+r\alpha)
for every
r\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
f
is a polynomial, this forces
f(\lambda+r\alpha)=f(\lambda)
for all
r\in\mathbb{C}
.

Remark 5.13
.

The invariance for the non-isotropic singular case can be also be deduced explicitly by restricting an element in
HC(Z(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2n)))
to
\mathfrak{a}
. Recall that in this case,
\mathfrak{h}^{*}=\operatorname{span}\{\varepsilon,\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n}\}
and
\mathfrak{a}^{*}=\operatorname{span}\{\varepsilon\}
. Consider the standard choice of simple roots for
\mathfrak{g}
. Then

\tilde{\rho}=-n\varepsilon+n\delta_{1}+(n-1)\delta_{2}+\cdots+\delta_{n}\text{ and }\rho=-n\varepsilon.

The isotropic roots of
\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2n)
are
\{\pm(\varepsilon\pm\delta_{i})\mid i=1,\ldots,n\}
and so the restricted roots are
\{\pm\varepsilon\}
with multiplicity
(0|2n)
.

A function
f\in HC(Z(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2n)))
satisfies

f(\lambda)=f(\lambda+r\alpha)\text{ for any }r\in\mathbb{Z},\lambda\in\tilde{H}_{\alpha}

where
\tilde{H}_{\alpha}=\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}\mid(\lambda+\tilde{\rho},\alpha)=0\}
. For
r=1,\ldots,n
,
(n-r)\varepsilon\in\tilde{H}_{\varepsilon-\delta_{n-r+1}}
so

f((n-r)\varepsilon)=f(n\varepsilon-r\delta_{n-r+1}).

However
n\varepsilon-r\delta_{n-r+1}\in\tilde{H}_{\varepsilon+\delta_{n-r+1}}
so

f(n\varepsilon-r\delta_{n-r+1})=f((n+r)\varepsilon).

We obtain that
f((n-r)\varepsilon)=f((n+r)\varepsilon)
. Since
\lambda\in H_{\varepsilon}
if and only if
\lambda=n\varepsilon
, we are done.

Corollary 5.14
.

For any simple root
\alpha\in\Sigma
, we have
HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})\subseteq J_{\mathfrak{a},\Sigma,\alpha}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.11
, we have that
HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})\subseteq J_{\mathfrak{a},\{\alpha\}}
. However, because
\alpha
is simple we may use Lemma
2.11
to obtain that
J_{\mathfrak{a},\{\alpha\}}=J_{\mathfrak{a},\Sigma,\alpha}
.
∎

5.5.
Changing positive systems

Let
\alpha\in\Sigma
, and consider the reflected simple root system
r_{\alpha}\Sigma
(see Section
2.4
). Recall that
t_{m_{\alpha,1}\alpha}^{*}
denotes the translation automorphism of
S(\mathfrak{a})
in
m_{\alpha,1}\alpha
(see Section
2.10
).

Proposition 5.15
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
. One has:

(1)

if
\alpha
is a regular root then
HC_{\Sigma}(D)=HC_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}(D)\circ r_{\alpha}
;

(2)

if
\alpha
is a singular root then we have that
HC_{\Sigma}(D)=t_{m_{\alpha,1}\alpha}^{*}(HC_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}(D))
. In particular,

HC_{\Sigma}(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=t_{m_{\alpha,1}\alpha}^{*}(HC_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}))

Proof.

It suffices to prove our formulae for
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
, since the set
\Lambda^{+}
is dense in
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
by
Remark

4.3
. For
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
, let
f_{\lambda}
denote the corresponding eigenfunction on
G/K
(see (
4.1
)). Then consider
V=(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})f_{\lambda}
. We may ask what is the highest weight of
V
with respect to
\mathfrak{n}_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}
.

First suppose that
\alpha
is regular. Then if we choose a lift
\widetilde{r_{\alpha}}
of
r_{\alpha}
to
K_{0}^{\circ}
, then
\widetilde{r_{\alpha}}^{*}(f_{\lambda})=f_{r_{\alpha}(\lambda)}
will be a highest weight vector with respect to
r_{\alpha}(\Sigma)
. Since
D
is
K_{0}^{\circ}
invariant, we have

HC_{\Sigma}(D)(r_{\alpha}(\lambda))=D(f_{r_{\alpha}(\lambda)})=D(\widetilde{r_{\alpha}}^{*}(f_{\lambda}))=D(f_{\lambda})=HC_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}(D)(\lambda).

This proves (1). Now we assume that
\alpha
is singular. Then by Lemma
4.5
, up to split factors fixed by
\theta
,
(\mathfrak{g}(\alpha),\mathfrak{k}(\alpha))
is either
(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2n))
or
(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)\times\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1),\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1))
, and so we may assume
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)
is either
\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2n)
or
\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)\times\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)
. In both cases we obtain a type one Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)_{1}
, and the distinguished Borel subalgebra
\mathfrak{b}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)_{1}
will be an Iwasawa Borel. Thus for
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
typical,
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)f_{\lambda}
will be isomorphic to an irreducible Kac-module for
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)
. In particular,
{\bigwedge}^{top}\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)_{-1}f_{\lambda}
contains the lowest weight vector
g
, and hence is of weight
t_{-m_{\alpha,1}\alpha}(\lambda)
. Since
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)f_{\lambda}=\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{k}(\alpha)f_{\lambda}
(by the Iwasawa decomposition), we obtain that

HC_{\Sigma}(D)(\lambda)=D(f_{\lambda})=D(g)=HC_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}(D)(t_{-m_{\alpha,1}\alpha}(\lambda)).

This formula now implies (2), and we are done.

∎

Corollary 5.16
.

We have
HC_{\Sigma}(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})\subseteq J_{\Sigma,\mathfrak{a}}

Proof.

We claim that

5.6.
Surjectivity of the Harish-Chandra map

5.6.1.
The Associated-Graded Functor

Given an
\mathbb{N}
-filtered vector space
V=\bigcup_{i\geq 0}V_{i}
where
V_{i}\subseteq V_{i+1}
, we can associate the space
\operatorname{gr}V=\bigoplus_{i\geq 0}V_{i+1}/V_{i}
. Given a linear map
f:V\rightarrow W
between two filtered vector spaces (which preserves the filtration), we can take the corresponding map
\operatorname{gr}f:\operatorname{gr}V\rightarrow\operatorname{gr}W
defined by
\operatorname{gr}f(v_{i})=f(v_{i})+W_{i-1}
for
v_{i}\in V_{i}
and
V_{-1},W_{-1}:=\{0\}
. The following lemma is well-known.

Lemma 5.17
.

Suppose that
f:V\rightarrow W
is a map of filtered vector spaces. If
\operatorname{gr}f
is bijective then so is
f
.

5.6.2.
Proof of surjectivity

To prove the bijectivity of
HC
, we use the Chevalley restriction theorem of symmetric superspaces. Recall the dual Chevalley restriction map
CR^{*}=\operatorname{gr}HC
from Section
5.2
. By
[
3
,
13
]
, the image of
CR^{*}
is equal to the set
I_{\mathfrak{a}}\subset S(\mathfrak{a})
of
W_{\mathfrak{a}}
-invariant polynomials such that for all singular roots
\alpha
, we have:

(D_{\alpha}^{r}f)(\lambda)=0\text{ if }(\lambda,\alpha)=0,

(D_{\alpha}^{r}f)(\lambda)\in\langle h_{\alpha}\rangle,

where
r=1,3,\dots,m_{\alpha,1}-1
.

Proposition 5.18
.

One has
\operatorname{gr}J_{\mathfrak{a}}=I_{\mathfrak{a}}
. Therefore,
HC
is surjective onto
J_{\mathfrak{a}}
.

We note that the algebra
J_{\mathfrak{a}}
depends on the choice of
\Sigma
whereas the algebra
I_{\mathfrak{a}}
does not.

Proof.

The map
CR:S(\mathfrak{p})^{\mathfrak{k}}\to I_{\mathfrak{a}}
is an isomorphism, so by Lemmas
5.3
and
5.17
, the first statement implies the second. Moreover, it is enough to show that
\operatorname{gr}J_{\mathfrak{a}}\subseteq I_{\mathfrak{a}}
, as follows from the following diagram

\textstyle{\operatorname{gr}(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\operatorname{gr}(\operatorname{HC})}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{\operatorname{gr}(J_{\mathfrak{a}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\subseteq}
\textstyle{S(\mathfrak{p})^{\mathfrak{k}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{CR\ \cong}
\textstyle{I_{\mathfrak{a}}}

Let
f\in J_{\mathfrak{a}}
. Then
f
is
W_{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\cdot$}}}
-invariant, and so
\operatorname{gr}f
is
W
-invariant. It remains to show the condition for singular roots.

Let
\alpha
be a singular root. Then

f(\lambda+r\alpha)=f(\lambda-r\alpha),\quad 1\leq r\leq n_{\alpha}=\frac{m_{\alpha,1}}{2}.

for any
\lambda\in H_{\alpha}:=\left\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}\mid(\lambda+\rho,\alpha)=0\right\}
.
Let
h_{\alpha}
be the dual element of
\alpha
, that is
\lambda(h_{\alpha})=(\lambda,\alpha)
for any
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
. Denote
m:=\deg f
and let
\bar{f}
be the degree
m
-term of
f
.
We need to show that
D_{\alpha}^{r}\left(\overline{f}\right)\in\left\langle h_{\alpha}\right\rangle
for
r=1,3,\ldots,m_{\alpha,1}-1
.

Suppose first that
\alpha
is not isotropic.
Then
\mathfrak{a}=\operatorname{ker}\alpha\oplus\text{span}\left\{h_{\alpha}\right\}
. We need to prove that
\bar{f}\in\sum_{i\in A}h_{\alpha}^{i}S(\operatorname{ker}\alpha)
where
A=2\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\mathbb{Z}_{\geq m_{\alpha,1}}
, namely all odd degrees of
h_{\alpha}
are greater than
m_{\alpha,1}
.
Note that
f_{\rho}(\lambda):=f(\lambda-\rho)
has the same top degree element as
f
so it is enough to prove the claim for
f_{\rho}
.
Write
f_{\rho}=\sum_{i}k_{i}h_{\alpha}^{i}
where
k_{i}\in S(\operatorname{ker}\alpha)
.
Note that
k_{i}(\lambda+r\alpha)=k_{i}(\lambda)
for any
r\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Hence
for
r=1,\ldots,n_{\alpha}
, we have

\sum_{i}k_{i}r^{i}=\sum_{i}k_{i}\left(-r\right)^{i},

which is equivalent to

\sum_{i\text{ odd}}k_{i}r^{i}=r\sum_{i=0}k_{2i+1}(r^{2})^{i}=0\text{ for }r=1,\ldots,n_{\alpha}.

It follows that the function
f_{\rho}^{\prime}:=\sum_{i\text{ odd}}k_{i}h_{\alpha}^{i}
has the form

f_{\rho}^{\prime}=Kh_{\alpha}\left(h_{\alpha}^{2}-1^{2}\right)\left(h_{\alpha}^{2}-2^{2}\right)\cdots\left(h_{\alpha}^{2}-\left(\frac{m_{\alpha,1}}{2}\right)^{2}\right)

for some
K\in S(\operatorname{ker}\alpha)
.
Hence the
m
-degree term of
f_{\rho}^{\prime}
is divisible by
h_{\alpha}^{m_{\alpha,1}+1}
, which implies our desired claim for
f_{\rho}
.

If
\alpha
is isotropic, then we have that
f(\lambda+\alpha)=f(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in H_{\alpha}
. Also, by Lemma
4.5
, in this case
m_{\alpha,1}=2
. This implies that
D_{\alpha}(f)
vanishes on
H_{\alpha}
, and thus we may write
D_{\alpha}(f)=(h_{\alpha}+(\rho,\alpha))g
for some
g\in S(\mathfrak{a})
. This implies that
f=(h_{\alpha}+(\rho,\alpha))\tilde{g}+k
, where
k\in S(\operatorname{ker}\alpha)
and
\tilde{g}\in S(\mathfrak{a})
satisfies
D_{\alpha}(\tilde{g})=g
. Hence we see that
D_{\alpha}(\overline{f})=h_{\alpha}\overline{g}
or is 0, so we are done.
∎

6.
Ghost Distributions and their Harish-Chandra image

In this section we study the Harish-Chandra image of ghost distributions on supersymmetric spaces. We continue to work with a fixed base
\Sigma\subseteq\Delta
.

6.1.
Ghost distributions

Let
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}\oplus\mathfrak{p}_{\overline{1}}
. Note that because
\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}
acts locally finitely on
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{k}
,
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
will as well; in fact we have an isomorphism of
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
-modules (
[
22
, Prop. 5.7]
):

\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{k}\cong\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}}^{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}/(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}.

Define the ghost distributions of
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
to be:

\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}:=\left(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})\mathfrak{k}\right)^{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}.

Note that if we write
v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}
for some nonzero element of
(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}/(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{k}^{\prime})\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}})^{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}
, then we have a natural isomorphism of vector spaces (
[
22
, Prop. 7.1]
):

(6.1)

\left(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}/(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}\right)^{\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}}\to\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})},\ \ \ \ z\mapsto zv_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}.

We consider the Harish-Chandra map
HC:\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}\to S(\mathfrak{a})
as described in Section
4.3
. In
[
23
, Thm. 1.1]
it is shown that (
6.1
) is an embedding.

To understand the associated graded of the image, we use
[
23
, Cor. 3.3]
which says the following: there exist nonzero elements
h_{1},\dots,h_{k}\in S(\mathfrak{a})
, with
k=\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{p}_{\overline{1}}/2=\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{n}_{\overline{1}}/2
, such that
HC(Dv_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}})=\pm h_{1}\cdots h_{k}HC(D)+\text{l.o.t.}
, where we use the notation of (
6.1
). In fact, it follows from the proof of
[
23
, Thm. 3.1]
that the associated graded is

(6.2)

\operatorname{gr}HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta_{1}^{+}}h_{\alpha}^{n_{\alpha}}\cdot S(\mathfrak{a})^{W}.

The main theorem and central goal of this section is now the following statement.

Theorem 6.1
.

We have
HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=M_{\mathfrak{a},\Sigma}
.

6.2.

6.3.
Changing positive systems

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
D\in\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
and
\alpha\in\Sigma
. One has

(1)

If
\alpha
is a regular root, then
HC_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}(D)=HC_{\Sigma}(D)\circ r_{\alpha}
.

(2)

If
\alpha
is a singular root, then
HC_{\Sigma}(D)=(-1)^{n_{\alpha}}t_{m_{\alpha,1}\alpha}^{*}(HC_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}(D))
. In particular,

HC_{\Sigma}(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})\subseteq t_{m_{\alpha,1}\alpha}^{*}(HC_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})).

Proof.

The case (1) is proven in the exact same way as in the proof of Proposition
5.15
, where we use that ghost distributions are
K_{0}^{\circ}
-invariant.

Now let us assume
\alpha
is singular.
As in the proof of Proposition
5.15
, we may assume that
(\mathfrak{g}(\alpha),\mathfrak{k}(\alpha))
is either
(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2n))
, or
(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)\times\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1),\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1))
.
Therefore, by density, it suffices to check our formula for
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
which is typical.
Take
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
typical, and let
f_{\lambda}
be the corresponding eigenfunction on
G/K
satisfying
f_{\lambda}(eK)=1
(see (
4.1
)). Let
g
be a lowest weight vector of
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)f_{\lambda}
, and assume further that
g(eK)=1
. By the Iwasawa decomposition, we may write
g=(k+1)f_{\lambda}
, where
k\in\mathcal{U}^{+}\mathfrak{k}(\alpha)
. As already noted in Proposition
5.15
, the weight of
g
is
t_{-m_{\alpha,1}\alpha}(\lambda)=\lambda-m_{\alpha,1}\alpha
.

Write
A
for the subgroup of
G_{0}
integrating
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
, and let
a\in A
have eigenvalues
\pm\sqrt{-1}
on
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)_{\overline{1}}
, and eigenvalues
\pm 1
on
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)_{\overline{0}}
. We note this is possible since
\mathfrak{a}
contains a grading operator for
\mathfrak{g}(\alpha)
. Then we see that
\operatorname{Ad}(a)
interchanges
\mathfrak{k}(\alpha)
and
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}(\alpha)
(in fact it is an interlacing automorphism of
(\mathfrak{g}(\alpha),\mathfrak{k}(\alpha))
in the language of

]
). Thus we obtain that

e^{\lambda-m_{\alpha,1}\alpha}(a)(g)=a(g)=(k^{\prime}+1)e^{\lambda}(a)f_{\lambda},

for some

. Therefore,

e^{-m_{\alpha,1}\alpha}(a)g=(k^{\prime}+1)f_{\lambda},

where
k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}^{+}\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
.
If we apply
D\in\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
to both sides, and use that

is
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
-invariant by definition, we obtain that

e^{-m_{\alpha,1}\alpha}(a)HC_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}(D)(\lambda-m_{\alpha,1}\alpha)=HC_{\Sigma}(D)(\lambda).

By definition
e^{\alpha}(a)=\pm\sqrt{-1}
; since

, we obtain our desired formula:

(-1)^{n_{\alpha}}HC_{r_{\alpha}\Sigma}(D)(t_{-m_{\alpha,1}\alpha}(\lambda))=HC_{\Sigma}(D)(\lambda).

6.4.
Bijectivity for the ghost case

We show that the Harish-Chandra map is surjective by showing that
M_{\mathfrak{a}}
and
HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
have the same associated graded algebra.

Let

N_{\mathfrak{a}}:=S(\mathfrak{a})^{W}\prod\limits_{\alpha\in\Delta_{odd}^{+}}h_{\alpha}^{n_{\alpha}}.

This space is an
I_{\mathfrak{a}}
-module and the product of two elements in
N_{\mathfrak{a}}
belongs to
I_{\mathfrak{a}}
.
Note that
N_{\mathfrak{a}}
does not depend on the choice of
\Sigma
.

Proposition 6.6
.

One has

\operatorname{gr}M_{\mathfrak{a}}=N_{\mathfrak{a}}
.
In particular,
HC:\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}\rightarrow M_{\mathfrak{a}}
is surjective.

Proof.

By (
6.2
), we have
\operatorname{gr}HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=N_{\mathfrak{a}}
and by Corollary
6.5
, it follows that
HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})\subseteq M_{\mathfrak{a}}
.
We are left to show that
\operatorname{gr}M_{\mathfrak{a}}\subseteq N_{\mathfrak{a}}
.

For
f\in S(\mathfrak{a})
, we denote by
\bar{f}
the leading term of
f
.
Let
f\in M_{\mathfrak{a}}
.
We prove that
\bar{f}
is
W
-invariant and divisible by
\prod\limits_{\alpha\in\Delta_{odd}^{+}}h_{\alpha}^{n_{\alpha}}
, which itself is
W
-invariant.
By definition of
M_{\mathfrak{a}}
, we can write
f=T_{\Sigma}\cdot g
where
g\in S(\mathfrak{a})
is
W_{\raisebox{-0.75346pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\cdot$}}}
-invariant.
Hence
\bar{f}=\overline{T_{\Sigma}}\cdot\bar{g}
where both
\overline{T_{\Sigma}}
and
\bar{g}
are
W
-invariant. Indeed,

\overline{T_{\Sigma}}=\prod\limits_{\alpha\in\Delta_{odd}\setminus\Delta^{+}_{sing,noniso}}h_{\alpha}^{n_{\alpha}}

which is
W
-invariant.
We are left to show divisibility by
h_{\alpha}^{n_{\alpha}}
when
\alpha
is singular non-isotropic.
By
Lemma

2.19
(iii), it is enough to show it for
\alpha
admissible.

Note that
f_{\rho}(\lambda):=f(\lambda-\rho)
has the same leading term as
f(\lambda)
and

f(\lambda+s\alpha)=(-1)^{s}f(\lambda-s\alpha),\quad 1\leq s\leq n_{\alpha}

for all
\lambda\in\left\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}\mid\left(\lambda,\alpha\right)=0\right\}
.
We claim that
\bar{f}_{\rho}
is divisible by
h_{\alpha}^{n_{\alpha}}
.

Let
m=\operatorname{deg}(f)
, and write
f_{\rho}=\sum_{i}k_{m-i}h_{\alpha}^{i}
where
k_{i}\in\mathbb{C}\left[\operatorname{ker}\alpha\right]

and
\deg k_{j}\leq j
.
By assumption, for each
s=1,\ldots,n_{\alpha}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{m}k_{m-i}s^{i}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{s}\sum_{i=0}^{m}k_{m-i}\left(-s\right)^{i}

This is equivalent to

\sum_{i=1,3,\ldots\leq m}k_{m-i}s^{i}=0\text{ for even }s,

\sum_{i=0,2,4,\ldots\leq m}k_{m-i}s^{i}=0\text{ for odd }s.

The first identity shows that
f_{\text{odd}}:=\sum_{i\text{ odd}}k_{m-i}h_{\alpha}^{i}
vanishes at
h_{\alpha}=0,\pm 2,\pm 4,\ldots,\pm(2\left\lfloor\frac{n_{\alpha}}{2}\right\rfloor)
and so takes the form

f_{\text{odd}}=K_{\text{odd}}h_{\alpha}(h_{\alpha}^{2}-2)(h_{\alpha}^{2}-4)\cdots(h_{\alpha}^{2}-2\left\lfloor\frac{n_{\alpha}}{2}\right\rfloor)

for some
K_{\text{odd}}
in
S(\operatorname{ker}\alpha)
.
The second identity shows that
f_{\text{even}}:=\sum_{i\text{ even}}k_{m-i}h_{\alpha}^{i}
vanishes at
h_{\alpha}=\pm 1,\pm 3,\ldots,\pm(2\left\lceil\frac{n_{\alpha}}{2}\right\rceil-1)
, and so takes the form

f_{\text{even}}=K_{\text{even}}(h_{\alpha}^{2}-1)(h_{\alpha}^{2}-3)\cdots(h_{\alpha}^{2}-(2\left\lceil\frac{n_{\alpha}}{2}\right\rceil-1))

for some
K_{\text{even}}
in
S(\operatorname{ker}\alpha)
. Thus, the leading term of
f_{\rho}=f_{\text{odd}}+f_{\text{even}}
must be divisible by
h_{\alpha}^{n_{\alpha}}
, completing the proof.
∎

7.
Appendix

The following proposition is independent of the rest of the paper, but is given for the general understanding of invariant distributions.

Proposition 7.1
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{t}\mathfrak{r}(\operatorname{ad}x)=0
for any
x\in\mathfrak{k}
. Then
(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{k}}\cap\mathfrak{k}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}=(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g})^{\mathfrak{k}}\cap\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\,\mathfrak{k}
.

Proof.

Consider again the symmetrization map
\lambda:S(\mathfrak{p})\to\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}
which is
\mathfrak{k}
-equivariant under the adjoint action.
Then we have the following decomposition of
\operatorname{ad}\mathfrak{k}
-modules:

\mathfrak{k}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\,\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{k}S(\mathfrak{p}),\quad\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{k}=\mathfrak{k}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\,\mathfrak{k}\oplus S(\mathfrak{p})\mathfrak{k}.

It suffices to show that

\left(\mathfrak{k}S(\mathfrak{p})\right)^{\mathfrak{k}}=\left(S(\mathfrak{p})\mathfrak{k}\right)^{\mathfrak{k}}.

Let
u\in\left(\mathfrak{k}S(\mathfrak{p})\right)^{\mathfrak{k}}
.
One has

\left(\mathfrak{k}S(\mathfrak{p})\right)^{\mathfrak{k}}\cong\left(\mathfrak{k}\otimes S(\mathfrak{p})\right)^{\mathfrak{k}}\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{k}}(\mathbb{C},\mathfrak{k}\otimes S(\mathfrak{p}))\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathfrak{k}}(\mathfrak{k}^{*},S(\mathfrak{p})).

This implies that any
u
can be written in the form
\sum_{i}e_{i}\varphi_{i}
where
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
is a basis of the ideal
\mathfrak{l}:=(\operatorname{ker}u)^{\perp}\subset\mathfrak{k}
(if
u
is viewed as a homomorphism, all the elements in its kernel vanish on
\mathfrak{l}
), and
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}
is a dual basis of
\mathfrak{l}^{*}\subset S(\mathfrak{p})
. Here the duality is taken with respect to
\langle\varphi_{i},e_{j}\rangle:=(u^{-1}(\varphi_{i}))(e_{j})
.
Note that
e_{i}
and
\varphi_{i}
have the same parity.

We claim that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\operatorname{ad}^{*}e_{i})(\varphi_{i})=0
.
Indeed, for any
j=1,\ldots,n
, if
p(e_{j})=0
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\operatorname{ad}^{*}e_{i})(\varphi_{i}),e_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{p(e_{i})}\left\langle\varphi_{i},(\operatorname{ad}e_{i})e_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{p(e_{i})}\left\langle\varphi_{i},(\operatorname{ad}e_{j})e_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{t}\mathfrak{r}\operatorname{ad}e_{j}=0.

For
e_{j}
odd,
\left\langle(\operatorname{ad}^{*}e_{i})(\varphi_{i}),e_{j}\right\rangle=0
for each
i=1,\ldots,n
since
(\operatorname{ad}^{*}e_{i})(\varphi_{i})
is an even element.
Therefore,

u=\sum_{i}e_{i}\varphi_{i}=\sum_{i}\operatorname{ad}e_{i}(\varphi_{i})+(-1)^{p(e_{i})}\varphi_{i}e_{i}=\sum_{i}(-1)^{p(e_{i})}\varphi_{i}e_{i}

and we get that
u\in\left(S(\mathfrak{p})\mathfrak{k}\right)^{\mathfrak{k}}
, as required.
∎
