On admissible solutions to the coupled Riemann problem with heat-flux discontinuity
Changsheng Yu, Tiegang Liu

Abstract
We study the Riemann problem for the compressible Euler equations with a stationary coupling interface across which a discontinuity in the heat flux is prescribed. This coupling gives rise to non-conservative effects and models heat addition mechanisms such as condensation-induced waves. Without imposing restrictions on sonic states, we analyze the problem in all Mach number regimes. Lax weak entropy solutions are constructed via half-Riemann problems, and we show that non-uniqueness occurs for a large class of initial data. To address this, we introduce an admissibility criterion derived from the evolutionarity criterion, and we characterize the full structure of admissible Riemann solutions. Our analysis establishes local existence of admissible Riemann solutions provided the heat flux jump is sufficiently small, while also identifying families of initial data for which admissible Riemann solutions cannot exist for any fixed, nonzero heat flux jump. Numerical experiments are included to illustrate the theoretical findings.

Introduction

Coupled systems of conservation laws arise naturally in many areas of fluid dynamics and
engineering whenever different flow regions, models, or physical effects are connected
through an interface. Typical examples include gas dynamics in networks of pipes and
junctions
[
17
,
8
,
4
]
, multiphase flows with phase transition
across moving interfaces
[
1
]
, shallow water flows coupled with
topography or man-made structures such as dams and sluices
[
14
,
26
]
,
traffic flow on road networks
[
11
,
10
]
, and blood flow in arterial
networks
[
23
]
. In these applications, each branch or subdomain
is modeled by a system of hyperbolic conservation laws, while coupling conditions at the
interfaces ensure mass, momentum, or energy balance.

Figure 1:
The coupled problem of the compressible Euler system on the
\Omega
.

This paper investigates the coupled problem for the compressible fluid flow.
The domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see Figure
1
) is decomposed
into two subdomains
\Omega_{-}
and
\Omega_{+}
separated by a stationary, smooth
coupling interface
\Gamma
with a unit normal vector
\bm{n}
.
In each subdomain, the fluid motion is governed by the compressible Euler system

\frac{\partial\bm{U}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot F(\bm{U})=0,

where the vector of conservative variables and the flux are given by

\bm{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho\\
\bm{m}\\
E\end{pmatrix},\qquad F(\bm{U})=\begin{pmatrix}\rho\bm{u}\\[3.00003pt]
\rho\bm{u}\otimes\bm{u}+pI_{d}\\[3.00003pt]
(E+p)\bm{u}\end{pmatrix}.

Here,
\rho
denotes the density,
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the velocity,

\bm{m}=\rho\bm{u}
the momentum, and
E
the total energy.
The pressure
p
is related to the internal energy
e
via a polytropic equation of state (EOS),

p=(\gamma-1)\rho e,\qquad\gamma>1,

with

e=\frac{1}{\rho}\left(E-\tfrac{1}{2}\rho|\bm{u}|^{2}\right).

On the coupling interface
\Gamma
, we impose a precondition.
The traces of the solution in the two subdomains are defined as

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{-}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{-}}\bm{x}+\epsilon\bm{n},\quad\bm{x}^{+}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\bm{x}+\epsilon\bm{n},

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{-}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{-}}\bm{U}(t,\bm{x}+\epsilon\bm{n}),\quad\bm{U}_{+}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\bm{U}(t,\bm{x}+\epsilon\bm{n}).

Here,
\bm{n}
denotes the unit normal vector at
\bm{x}\in\Gamma
.
In general, the coupling condition can be characterized by a nonlinear relation

\Psi(\bm{U}_{-},\bm{U}_{+})=0.

In this work, we focus on the so-called
heat-flux jump
condition, which reads

\Psi(\bm{U}_{-},\bm{U}_{+})=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{-}\bm{u}_{-}\cdot\bm{n}-\rho_{+}\bm{u}_{+}\cdot\bm{n}\\[3.00003pt]
\rho_{-}|\bm{u}_{-}|^{2}+p_{-}-\rho_{+}|\bm{u}_{+}|^{2}-p_{+}\\[3.00003pt]
(1+k)(E_{-}+p_{-})\bm{u}_{-}\cdot\bm{n}-(E_{+}+p_{+})\bm{u}_{+}\cdot\bm{n}\end{pmatrix},

where
k>0
is a fixed parameter.

Note that if
\bm{u}\cdot\bm{n}=0
, the condition degenerates into the simple continuity relation
\bm{U}_{-}=\bm{U}_{+}
.
To exclude this trivial case, we assume throughout that
\bm{u}_{-}\cdot\bm{n}>0
, which implies
\bm{u}_{+}\cdot\bm{n}>0
from
\Psi(\bm{U}_{-},\bm{U}_{+})=0
.
Unlike many previous studies, we do not impose any restrictions on sonic states.
In particular, our analysis is valid across all Mach number regimes, from subsonic to supersonic, thereby providing a unified framework.

This model has been proposed in the literature to describe flows with condensation

[
5
,
24
]
.
Such flows may exhibit complex wave patterns, as observed both experimentally and numerically, see e.g.
[
30
,
18
]
.
These structures can be validated through the following Rayleigh-type coupling relations:

\displaystyle\rho_{-}\bm{u}_{-}

\displaystyle=\rho_{+}\bm{u}_{+},

\displaystyle\rho_{-}|\bm{u}_{-}|^{2}+p_{-}

\displaystyle=\rho_{+}|\bm{u}_{+}|^{2}+p_{+},

\displaystyle(1+k)\left(h_{-}+\tfrac{|\bm{u}_{-}|^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=h_{+}+\tfrac{|\bm{u}_{+}|^{2}}{2},

where
h=(e+p)/\rho
denotes the specific enthalpy.
This heat-flux jump condition is precisely equivalent to
\Psi(\bm{U}_{-},\bm{U}_{+})=0
.

We consider the Cauchy problem with initial data

\bm{U}(0,x)=\bm{U}_{0}(x).

In the spirit of half-Riemann problems, such a setting can be viewed as two separate problems posed on the subdomains
\Omega_{-}
and
\Omega_{+}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{U}}{\partial t}+{\rm div}\,_{x}F(\bm{U})=0,\qquad\bm{U}:[0,T]\times\Omega_{-}\to\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+2},

(1.1)

\displaystyle\bm{U}(0,x)=\bm{U}_{0}(x),\quad x\in\Omega_{-},

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{U}}{\partial t}+{\rm div}\,_{x}F(\bm{U})=0,\qquad\bm{U}:[0,T]\times\Omega_{+}\to\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+2},

(1.2)

\displaystyle\bm{U}(0,x)=\bm{U}_{0}(x),\quad x\in\Omega_{+}.

Following
[
15
]
, we now introduce the notion of weak solutions for the coupled problem.

Definition 1.1
.

A function
\bm{U}:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+2}
is called a
weak solution
of the coupled problem if

•

\bm{U}
is a weak solution of the systems (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), respectively;

•

the traces
\bm{U}_{-}
and
\bm{U}_{+}
exist for
x\in\Gamma
and satisfy the coupling condition

\Psi(\bm{U}_{-},\bm{U}_{+})=0\qquad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

We focus on the Riemann problem associated with the one-dimensional coupled system, which is defined by the initial condition

\bm{U}_{0}(x)=\begin{cases}\bm{U}_{L},&x\leq 0,\\
\bm{U}_{R},&x>0,\end{cases}

where
\bm{U}_{L},\bm{U}_{R}\in\mathcal{D}
are two prescribed states.
This setting can be interpreted as the projection of the multi-dimensional coupled problem onto the normal direction of the interface
\Gamma
.
Consequently, the coupling interface reduces to a single point in
\mathbb{R}
, which, without loss of generality, is located at the origin.

The coupling interface acts as a stationary discontinuity in the Riemann solutions.
This leads to complex wave patterns, especially in the absence of any restriction on the Mach numbers.
In addition, the interface states may admit multiple branches, giving rise to the non-uniqueness of Riemann solutions for a broad class of initial data.
To address this issue, we introduce an admissibility criterion inspired by the evolutionarity condition for general discontinuities in fluid dynamics, and we characterize the resulting structure of admissible solutions.

Our main contributions can be summarized as follows.
First, we establish the local existence of admissible Riemann solutions whenever the heat-flux jump across the interface is sufficiently small.
Second, we show that for certain families of initial data, no admissible solution exists if the heat-flux discontinuity is fixed and nonzero.
Finally, we present numerical verifications that not only confirm the predicted solution structures but also reveal the emergence of self-similar yet non-admissible solutions in the absence of monotonicity criterion.
These results provide a rigorous foundation for the study of coupled hyperbolic systems with non-conservative interfaces and may guide future developments in the analysis of networked and multiphysics flow models.

Section
2
establishes the structural properties of coupled Riemann solutions.
Section
3
discusses the non-uniqueness of entropy solutions and motivates the admissibility criterion introduced in Section

.
Section

shows the existence of initial data without admissible solutions.
Numerical experiments verifying the theoretical findings are presented in Section
7
, and concluding remarks are given in Section
8
.

2
Coupled Riemann Problem

Before addressing the coupled Riemann problem, we recall some standard results for the classical Riemann problem of the Euler system, corresponding to the degenerate coupling interface
k=0
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial\bm{U}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\bm{F}(\bm{U})}{\partial x}=0,\quad\bm{U}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\\
&\bm{U}(0,x)=\bm{U}_{0}(x)\end{aligned}\right.

The compressible Euler system is strictly hyperbolic with three characteristic fields

\lambda_{1}(\bm{U})=u-c,\quad\lambda_{2}(\bm{U})=u,\quad\lambda_{3}(\bm{U})=u+c,

and
c=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma p}{\rho}}
is the speed of sound.
Solutions of the classical Riemann problem are sought in the self-similar form

\bm{V}\left(\frac{x}{t};\bm{U}_{L},\bm{U}_{R}\right):\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}\times\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{D}

constructed as a concatenation of three simple waves associated with the characteristic fields. Each simple wave is either a shock wave, a rarefaction wave, or a contact discontinuity. We restrict attention to entropy solutions, in the sense that every discontinuity in the solution satisfies the Lax entropy conditoin. For further details on the construction of entropy solutions to the classical Riemann problem we refer to
[
25
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Lax entropy condition)
.

The discontinuity with the left-hand state
\bm{U}_{1}
and right-hand state
\bm{U}_{2}
is said to satisfy the Lax entropy conditions if there exists an index
i\in\{1,2,3\}
such that we have either

(i)

\begin{cases}\lambda_{i}(\bm{U}_{2})<\sigma<\lambda_{i+1}(\bm{U}_{2}),\\
\lambda_{i-1}(\bm{U}_{1})<\sigma<\lambda_{i}(\bm{U}_{1}),\end{cases}

if the
i
th characteristic field is genuinely nonlinear; or

(ii)

\lambda_{i}(\bm{U}_{1})=\sigma=\lambda_{i}(\bm{U}_{2})

if the
i
th characteristic field is linearly degenerate.

The first and third characteristic fields of the Euler system are genuinely nonlinear, and their associated simple waves are either shock waves or rarefaction waves.
The second characteristic field is linearly degenerate, giving rise to contact discontinuities.
For each characteristic fields, we denote the corresponding Lax curves of simple waves by

L_{i}^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0}),\quad i=1,2,3,

where the index
i
specifies the characteristic field, the superscript
\pm
indicates the forward or backward direction,
\varepsilon
is a scalar parameter measuring the wave strength, and
\bm{U}_{0}
is the reference state from which the curve is issued.
Explicit expressions of these curves are collected in Appendix
A
.

We recall the following property of classical Riemann solutions, as established in
[
15
]
.

Proposition 2.2

(
Solutions of the classical RP).

Let us assume that the given Riemann data
\bm{U}_{L}
and
\bm{U}_{R}
satisfy

u_{L}+\frac{2c_{L}}{\gamma-1}>u_{R}-\frac{2c_{R}}{\gamma-1}.

(2.1)

Then the classical RP has a unique solution that is not vacuum.
Moreover, the intermediate states in the solution depend continuously on the initial data.

We now turn to the coupled Riemann problem with a non-degenerate coupling interface.
Following the approach of Herty et al.
[
16
]
, the coupled Riemann solution is constructed from two half-Riemann problems.
The half-Riemann problem posed in the left subdomain
\Omega_{-}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{U}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\bm{F}(\bm{U})}{\partial x}=0,\ \bm{U}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}_{-}\to\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\bm{U}(0,x)=\begin{cases}\bm{U}_{L},\ &x<0\\
\bm{U}_{-},\ &x>0\end{cases}

The half-Riemann problem in the right subdomain
\Omega_{+}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{U}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\bm{F}(\bm{U})}{\partial x}=0,\ \bm{U}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(2.3)

\displaystyle\bm{U}(0,x)=\begin{cases}\bm{U}_{+},\ &x>0\\
\bm{U}_{R},\ &x<0\end{cases}

If both
\bm{U}_{-}
and
\bm{U}_{+}
are subsonic, then according to
[
7
]
, they lie within the following sets of boundary states:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L})=\left\{\bm{U}\in\mathcal{D}:\ \exists M\in\{1,2,3\},\ \exists(\epsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{M}\subset\mathbb{R}\text{ s.t. }\bm{U}=\bm{U}_{M}\text{ with }\right.

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{0}=\bm{U}_{L},\bm{U}_{i}:=L_{i}^{+}(\epsilon_{i};\bm{U}_{i-1}),i=1,...,M\text{ and }

\displaystyle\left.\lambda_{M}(\bm{U})\leq 0<\lambda_{M+1}(\bm{U})\right\}

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})=\left\{\bm{U}\in\mathcal{D}:\ \exists M\in\{1,2,3\},\ \exists(\epsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{M}\subset\mathbb{R}\text{ s.t. }\bm{U}=\bm{U}_{M}\text{ with }\right.

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{0}=\bm{U}_{R},\bm{U}_{i}:=L_{i}^{-}(\epsilon_{i};\bm{U}_{i-1}),i=1,...,M\text{ and }

\displaystyle\left.\lambda_{M-1}(\bm{U})\leq 0<\lambda_{M}(\bm{U})\right\}

Given states
\bm{U}_{-}\in\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L})
and
\bm{U}_{+}\in\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
, the coupled Riemann solution is constructed by concatenating the solutions of the two half-Riemann problems, subject to the coupling condition
\Psi(\bm{U}_{-},\bm{U}_{+})=0
.

In our opinion, however, the above sets of boundary states are only suitable in the subsonic regime.
In the supersonic case, even if the boundary states satisfy
\bm{U}_{-}\in\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L})
and
\bm{U}_{+}\in\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
together with the coupling condition, they may fail to generate a valid Riemann solution.
As a counterexample, we consider the initial data satisfying

(i)

M_{L}>1
;

(ii)

\bm{U}_{-}=L_{1}^{+}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{L})\in\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L})
,
0<\varepsilon<\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}(M_{L}^{2}-1)
;

(iii)

\bm{U}_{+}\in\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
,
\Psi(\bm{U}_{-},\bm{U}_{+})=0
;

Then the shock wave connecting the left-hand state
\bm{U}_{L}
and right-hand state
\bm{U}_{-}
has a positive speed.
It is clear that the left half–Riemann problem cannot serve as a coupled Riemann solution on the left half–plane.
This observation indicates that, in the general case where all Mach numbers are involved, the wave speeds in the half–Riemann problems must be explicitly taken into account.

We employ the double CRP framework described in
[
29
]
to construct solutions for the coupled Riemann problem.
Let us consider a self-similar solution
\bm{U}
of the coupled Riemann problem with traces
(\bm{U}_{-},\bm{U}_{+})
. To extend
\bm{U}
from the left half-plane to the whole plane, we define the new function:

\bm{V}(x,t)=\begin{cases}\bm{U}(x,t),&x<0\\
\bm{U}_{-},&x\geq 0\\
\end{cases}

This construction replaces the solution in the right half-plane with the constant state
\bm{U}_{-}
, so the extension is constant for
x\geq 0
.
It is clearly a classical RP solution, therefore contains at most three simple waves corresponding the characteristic fields with negative speeds. This argument also holds similarily for the solution on the right half-plane. This analysis lead to the following modification of the sets of boundary states

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L}):=\big\{

\displaystyle\bm{U}\in\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L}):\text{ there exists a classical RP solution }\bm{V}

\displaystyle\text{ such that }\bm{V}\left(\frac{x}{t}-;\bm{U}_{L},\bm{U}\right)\equiv\bm{U}\text{ for all }x\geq 0,t\geq 0\big\}.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R}):=\big\{

\displaystyle\bm{U}\in\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R}):\text{ there exists a classical RP solution }\bm{V}\big\}

\displaystyle\text{ such that }\bm{V}\left(\frac{x}{t}+;\bm{U},\bm{U}_{R}\right)\equiv\bm{U}\text{ for all }x\leq 0,t\geq 0\big\}.

Then we can demonstrate that all weak solutions of the coupled Riemann problem have boundary states that belong to the modified sets defined above.

Theorem 2.3

(
Structure of Coupled Riemann Solutions).

Given the initial Riemann data
\bm{U}_{L}
and
\bm{U}_{R}
, any self-similar, non-vacuum solution
\bm{U}
of the coupled Riemann problem must satisfy
\bm{U}_{-}\in\mathcal{V}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L})
and
\bm{U}_{+}\in\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
.

Moreover, for any
\bm{U}_{-}\in\mathcal{V}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L})
and
\bm{U}_{+}\in\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
satisfying the coupling condition
\Psi(\bm{U}_{-},\bm{U}_{+})=0
, there exists a unique self-similar, non-vacuum entropy weak solution of the coupled Riemann problem given by

\bm{U}(x,t)=\begin{cases}\bm{V}\left(\frac{x}{t},\bm{U}_{L},\bm{U}_{-}\right),&x<0,\\
\bm{V}\left(\frac{x}{t},\bm{U}_{+},\bm{U}_{R}\right),&x>0.\end{cases}

(2.4)

Remark 2.4
.

As shown in
[
31
]
, and Remark
6.2
in the following, Definition
1.1
and the theorem built upon it do not cover the case where shock waves interact with the coupling interface. Such situations are commonly referred to as
resonance
in the study of hyperbolic conservation laws with singular or geometric source terms and in nonconservative hyperbolic systems (cf.
[
12
,
9
]
). For the sake of precision, Definition
1.1
should therefore be understood as specifying the class of
non-resonant weak solutions
.

By default, throughout this paper we restrict our attention to non-resonant solutions of the coupled Riemann problem, namely those admitting the canonical form described in Theorem
2.3
. Equivalently, the problem reduces to finding a pair of boundary states
(\bm{U}_{-},\bm{U}_{+})
belonging to the admissible sets
\mathcal{V}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L})
and
\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
.

3
Ill-posedness of Entropy Solutions

We now show that the coupled Riemann problem may admit non-unique solutions for a broad class of initial data, even when the Lax entropy condition is imposed. To this end, we present explicitly the ranges of the boundary states
\bm{U}_{-}
and
\bm{U}_{+}
.

More precisely, given a left-hand boundary state
\bm{U}_{-}
, we determine all corresponding right-hand boundary states
\bm{U}_{+}
that satisfy the coupling condition. The latter, written component-wise, takes the form

\displaystyle\rho_{-}u_{-}

\displaystyle=\rho_{+}u_{+},

\displaystyle\rho_{-}u_{-}^{2}+p_{-}

\displaystyle=\rho_{+}u_{+}^{2}+p_{+},

\displaystyle(E_{-}+p_{-})u_{-}(1+k)

\displaystyle=(E_{+}+p_{+})u_{+}.

By means of standard manipulations of the Rankine–Hugoniot relations and thermodynamic identities (see, e.g., Anderson
[
2
]
), the state variables
\rho_{+}
,
u_{+}
, and
p_{+}
can be expressed solely in terms of the right-hand Mach number
M_{+}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{+}}{\rho_{-}}=\left(\frac{M_{-}}{M_{+}}\right)^{2}\frac{\gamma(M_{+})^{2}+1}{\gamma(M_{-})^{2}+1},

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{u_{+}}{u_{-}}=\left(\frac{M_{+}}{M_{-}}\right)^{2}\frac{\gamma(M_{-})^{2}+1}{\gamma(M_{+})^{2}+1},

\displaystyle\frac{p_{+}}{p_{-}}=\frac{\gamma(M_{-})^{2}+1}{\gamma(M_{+})^{2}+1}.

The values of the right-hand Mach number
M_{+}
have been derived by Cheng et al.
[
5
]
.
They depend solely on the left-hand Mach number
M_{+}
, and admit two different branches:

M_{+}=\psi_{1}(M_{-},k)\text{ or }M_{+}=\psi_{2}(M_{-},k),

with

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(M,k):=\sqrt{(1-I)/(1+\gamma I)},

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(M,k):=\sqrt{(1+I)(1-\gamma I)},

\displaystyle I=I(M,k):=\left[(\gamma M^{2}+1)^{2}-(\gamma+1)M^{2}[(\gamma-1)M^{2}+2](1+k)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\gamma M^{2}+1\right)^{-1}.

We illustrate in Figure
2
the behavior of the functions
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
for the case
k=0.1
.

Figure 2:
Behaviour of the functions
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
with respect to different
M
.

It is evident that the Mach numbers
M_{-}
and
M_{+}
are restricted to certain ranges. To illustrate this, we denote

\displaystyle M_{1}^{*}

\displaystyle=M_{1}^{*}(k)=\begin{cases}\left[{\frac{k\gamma+k+1-(\gamma+1)\sqrt{k(k+1)}}{k+1-k\gamma^{2}}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},\ &k\neq\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}-1},\ k>0\\
\left[{\frac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},\ &k=\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}-1}\end{cases},

\displaystyle M_{2}^{*}

\displaystyle=M_{2}^{*}(k)=\begin{cases}\left[{\frac{k\gamma+k+1+(\gamma+1)\sqrt{k(k+1)}}{k+1-k\gamma^{2}}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},\ &k<\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}-1},\\
\infty,\ &0\leq k\geq\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}-1}\end{cases}.

\displaystyle M_{3}^{*}

\displaystyle=M_{3}^{*}(k)=\left[\frac{\sqrt{(k\gamma^{2}+1)(k+1)}-(k\gamma+1)}{k\gamma(\gamma-1)}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},\ k>0.

Proposition 3.1
.

The two functions
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
have the following properties:

(i)

\psi_{1}(M,k)\in C^{\infty}\left((0,M_{1}^{*}]\cup[M_{2}^{*},\infty),(0,1]\right)
.

\psi_{2}(M,k)\in C^{\infty}\left((M_{3}^{*},M_{1}^{*}]\cup[M_{2}^{*},\infty),[1,\infty)\right)
.

(ii)

\lim\limits_{M\to 0}\psi_{1}(M,k)=0
,
\lim\limits_{M\to M_{3}^{*}}\psi_{2}(M,k)=\infty
.

\lim\limits_{M\to\infty}\psi_{1}(M,k)=0
,
\lim\limits_{M\to\infty}\psi_{2}(M,k)=\infty
.

\psi_{1}(M_{1}^{*},k)=\psi_{2}(M_{1}^{*},k)=\psi_{1}(M_{2}^{*},k)=\psi_{2}(M_{2}^{*},k)=1
.

(iii)

If
M\leq M_{1}^{*}(k)
, then
\frac{\partial\psi_{1}}{\partial M}(M,k)>0
and
\frac{\partial\psi_{2}}{\partial M}(M,k)<0
;

If
M\geq M_{2}^{*}(k)
, then
\frac{\partial\psi_{1}}{\partial M}(M,k)<0
and
\frac{\partial\psi_{2}}{\partial M}(M,k)>0
.

(iv)

\psi_{2}(M,k)=\sqrt{\frac{(\gamma-1)\psi_{1}^{2}(M,k)+2}{2\gamma\psi_{1}^{2}(M,k)+1-\gamma}}
,
\psi_{1}(M,k)=\sqrt{\frac{(\gamma-1)\psi_{2}^{2}(M,k)+2}{2\gamma\psi_{2}^{2}(M,k)+1-\gamma}}
, for any
k\geq 0
.

(v)

M_{2}^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{(\gamma-1)(M_{1}^{*})^{2}+2}{2\gamma(M_{1}^{*})^{2}+1-\gamma}}
,
M_{1}^{*}=\sqrt{\frac{(\gamma-1)(M_{2}^{*})^{2}+2}{2\gamma(M_{2}^{*})^{2}+1-\gamma}}
, for any
k\geq 0
.

(vi)

For
i=1,2
, if
\psi_{i}(M_{1},k)=\psi_{i}(M_{2},k)
, then
M_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{(\gamma-1)M_{2}+2}{2\gamma M_{2}+1-\gamma}}
and
M_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{(\gamma-1)M_{1}+2}{2\gamma M_{1}+1-\gamma}}
.

It is observed that, in general, a given left boundary state
\bm{U}_{-}
may correspond to two different right boundary states, and symmetrically, a given right boundary state may correspond to two different left boundary states.
The existence of multiple branches of boundary states potentially gives rise to non-uniqueness of the coupled Riemann solutions; however, such non-uniqueness is not an immediate consequence.

When constructing coupled Riemann solutions in the sense of Theorem
2.3
, the admissible boundary states are required to belong to the sets
\mathcal{V}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L})
and
\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
.
It is important to note that one of the two branches corresponds to a subsonic state, while the other corresponds to a supersonic state, as shown in Proposition
3.1
(i).
This difference directly influences the wave structure of the associated classical Riemann problem and, consequently, the validity of the conditions
\mathcal{V}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L})
and
\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
.
Therefore, the uniqueness of the coupled Riemann solution is determined by the admissible range of the boundary states.
In what follows, we show uniqueness of the Riemann solution in the case where the left-hand boundary state is supersonic.

Theorem 3.2
.

For any given Riemann data
\bm{U}_{L}
and
\bm{U}_{R}
, there exists at most one weak solution satisfying the condition
M_{-}>1
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the pair of boundary states
(\bm{U}_{-},\bm{U}_{+})
is unique.

For the left classical Riemann problem
\bm{V}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{t},\bm{U}_{L},\bm{U}_{-}\right)
, the Lax entropy condition implies successively that the waves associated with the
3
-,
2
-, and
1
-characteristic fields must all vanish. Hence, we must have
\bm{U}_{-}=\bm{U}_{L}
.

By Proposition
3.1
(i), the right boundary state
\bm{U}_{+}
admits two possible candidates: a subsonic state, denoted by
\bm{U}_{+}^{1}
, and a supersonic state, denoted by
\bm{U}_{+}^{2}
.
We claim that
\bm{U}_{+}^{2}\notin\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
whenever a solution with right boundary state
\bm{U}_{+}^{1}
exists.

Indeed, Proposition
3.1
(iv) and uniqueness of the classical Riemann problem yield

\bm{V}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{t};\bm{U}_{+}^{2},\bm{U}_{R}\right)=\begin{cases}\bm{U}_{+}^{2},&x<0,\\[3.00003pt]
\bm{V}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{t};\bm{U}_{+}^{1},\bm{U}_{R}\right),&x>0.\end{cases}

Since
\bm{U}_{+}^{1}\in\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
, it follows that

\bm{V}\!\left(0+;\bm{U}_{+}^{2},\bm{U}_{R}\right)=\bm{V}\!\left(0+;\bm{U}_{+}^{1},\bm{U}_{R}\right)=\bm{U}_{+}^{1}\neq\bm{U}_{+}^{2},

Therefore,
\bm{U}_{+}^{2}\notin\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
, the right boundary state
\bm{U}_{+}
is unique, and the theorem follows.
∎

However, the presence of multiple branches indeed leads to the non-uniqueness of the Riemann solutions with respect to the initial data.
More precisely, although Theorem
3.2
ensures that the Riemann solutions with supersonic left boundary states are unique, alternative Riemann solutions with subsonic left boundary states can still be constructed.
Next, we establish the non-uniqueness of entropy solutions for a broad class of Riemann data.
This non-uniqueness originates from the inverse branch, which implies that, for a given right boundary state, two different left boundary states can exist.

Theorem 3.3

(
Non-uniqueness of entropy solutions).

Let
k>0
be fixed.
Suppose
\bm{U}
is an entropy solution of the coupled Riemann problem with initial data
(\bm{U}_{L},\bm{U}_{R})
satisfying
M_{-}>1
and
M_{+}>1
.
Then there exists another entropy solution
\widetilde{\bm{U}}
with the same initial data such that
\widetilde{M}_{-}<1
and
\widetilde{M}_{+}>1
.

Proof.

We construct
\widetilde{\bm{U}}
explicitly.

By the Lax entropy condition we have
\bm{U}_{-}=\bm{U}_{L}
with
M_{L}>1
.
Choose

\overline{\bm{U}}_{-}=L_{1}^{+}(\overline{\varepsilon},\bm{U}_{L}),\quad\overline{\varepsilon}>0,

so that
\bm{U}_{L}
and
\overline{\bm{U}}_{-}
constitute a stationary shock wave. Consequently,

\psi_{2}(\overline{\bm{U}}_{-},k)=\bm{U}_{+}.

Next, perturb slightly along the 1-wave curve:

\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{-}=L_{1}^{+}(\overline{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon,\bm{U}_{L}),\quad\varepsilon>0,

and set
\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{+}=\psi_{2}(\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{-},k)
.
We claim that
\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{-}\in\mathcal{V}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L})
and
\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{+}\in\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
.

Since
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small,
\bm{U}_{L}
and
\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{-}
are connected by a shock wave with speed

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=u_{L}-c_{L}\sqrt{\Bigl(\tfrac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\Bigr)\Bigl(\tfrac{\widetilde{p}^{-}}{p_{L}}\Bigr)+\tfrac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}}

\displaystyle<u_{L}-c_{L}\sqrt{\Bigl(\tfrac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\Bigr)\Bigl(\tfrac{\overline{p}^{-}}{p_{L}}\Bigr)+\tfrac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}}=0,

which implies
\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{-}\in\mathcal{V}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L})
.

Moreover,

\|\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{+}-\bm{U}_{+}\|=\|\psi_{2}(\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{-},k)-\psi_{2}(\overline{\bm{U}}_{-},k)\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}\|L_{1}^{+}(\overline{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon,\bm{U}_{L})-L_{1}^{+}(\overline{\varepsilon},\bm{U}_{L})\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}|\varepsilon|.

By Proposition
2.2
, the classical Riemann solution
\bm{V}(\tfrac{x}{t};\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{+},\bm{U}_{R})
exists, with intermediate states close to those of
\bm{V}(\tfrac{x}{t};\bm{U}_{+},\bm{U}_{R})
.
Since
M_{+}>1
and
\bm{U}_{+}\in\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
, it follows that
\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{+}\in\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
.

Hence, the pair
(\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{-},\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{+})
yields another entropy solution
\widetilde{\bm{U}}
, completing the proof.
∎

4
Admissible Weak Solutions

In view of Theorem
3.3
, additional admissibility criterion is needed to select physically-relevant solutions. In the present work, we employ the evolutionarity criterion of Landau and Lifshitz
[
21
]
, originally formulated for general discontinuities in fluid dynamics. We further note its successful application to duct flows in the work of Warnecke and Andrianov
[
3
]
.

Definition 4.1

(
Evolutionarity criterion).

Consider a discontinuity
\Sigma
in a physical flow, which is governed by a
d\times d
hyperbolic system. Denote the number of charactristics incoming to
\Sigma
by
n
and coinciding with
\Sigma
by
c
, further, denote the number of unknown variables on both sides of
\Sigma
together with the speed of
\Sigma
by
N=2d+1
, and the number of relations across
\Sigma
by
m
, then
\Sigma
is called evolutionary if

N=n+c+m.

In the present framework, the coupling interface is regarded as a stationary discontinuity across which three relations, referred to as the coupling conditions, are imposed. According to the evolutionarity criterion, exactly three characteristics must either enter or coincide with the interface. For the Euler system, the characteristic fields are associated with the eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}(\bm{U})=u-c,\quad\lambda_{2}(\bm{U})=u,\quad\lambda_{3}(\bm{U})=u+c.

Under the assumption
u_{-}>0
and
u_{+}>0
, the characteristics
\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{3}
are always incoming from the left state
\bm{U}_{-}
and outgoing from the right state
\bm{U}_{+}
. Consequently, for a given pair of boundary states, exactly one characteristic is incoming to or coinciding with the interface. By invoking the evolutionarity criterion, the admissible entropy solutions of the coupled Riemann problem are thus required to satisfy the following admissibility condition.

Definition 4.2

(
Monotonicity criterion).

For any fixed parameter
k>0
, the left and right boundary states
\bm{U}_{-}
and
\bm{U}_{+}
are required to satisfy

\lambda_{i}(U_{-})\cdot\lambda_{i}(U_{+})\geq 0,\quad\forall i=1,2,3

where
\lambda_{i}(\cdot)
denote the characteristic speeds of the Euler system.

Observe that the case
\lambda_{1}(\bm{U}_{-})=\lambda_{1}(\bm{U}_{+})=0
cannot occur when assuming
k\neq 0
.
Consequently, the monotonicity criterion is equivalent to the evolutionarity criterion.
In this framework, admissible solutions are understood as entropy weak solutions that additionally satisfy the monotonicity criterion.

The monotonicity criterion naturally arises in the classical theory of one-dimensional flow with heat addition; see
[
2
, Section 3.8]
.
The Rayleigh curve shows that heat addition always drives the flow toward the sonic state, which corresponds to the state of maximum entropy.
However, the flow cannot cross the sonic point solely through heat addition.
For a fixed upstream state, there exists a maximum admissible heat input that brings the downstream state exactly to sonic conditions.
When this limit is attained, the flow is said to be
choked
.

We select the branch of the Mach number that satisfies the monotonicity criterion:

M_{+}=\psi(M_{-},k):=\begin{cases}\psi_{1}(M_{-},k),&M_{-}\leq M_{1}^{*},\\
\psi_{2}(M_{-},k),&M_{-}\geq M_{2}^{*}.\end{cases}

By Proposition
3.1
(iii), we have
\frac{\partial\psi(M,k)}{\partial M}>0
, and it is easy to check that
\psi(M_{-};0)=M_{-}
.
Once the admissible Mach number
M_{+}
is determined, the corresponding admissible density
\rho_{+}
, velocity
u_{+}
, and pressure
p_{+}
follow.
For convenience, we introduce the functions

\displaystyle h_{1}(M,k)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\gamma M^{2}+1}{\gamma(\psi(M,k))^{2}+1},

\displaystyle h_{2}(M,k)

\displaystyle:=\frac{(\psi(M,k))^{2}(\gamma M^{2}+1)}{M^{2}(\gamma(\psi(M,k))^{2}+1)}.

Then the admissible boundary states satisfy

\frac{p_{+}}{p_{-}}=h_{1}(M_{-},k)<1,\qquad\frac{u_{+}}{u_{-}}=h_{2}(M_{-},k)>1.

(4.1)

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose that
0<M<M_{1}^{*}(k)
and
0<k(\gamma^{2}-1)<1
. Then

\frac{\partial h_{1}(M,k)}{\partial M}<0,\qquad\frac{\partial h_{2}(M,k)}{\partial M}>0.

(4.2)

The proof is deferred to Appendix
B
.

The monotonicity criterion imposes constraints on the eigenvalues of the boundary states, thereby limiting the structure of the Riemann solutions to several possible configurations.

Theorem 4.4
.

For any given Riemann data
\bm{U}_{L}
and
\bm{U}_{R}
, there exist exactly three structures of the admissible Riemann solutions for
k\neq 0
. The corresponding boundary states for each case are given as follows:

•

Structure 1:

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{-}=L_{1}^{+}(\bm{U}_{L},\varepsilon_{1}),\quad\lambda_{1}(\bm{U}_{-})<0,

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{+}=L_{2}^{-}(\varepsilon_{3};L_{3}^{-}(\varepsilon_{4};\bm{U}_{R})),\ \lambda_{1}(\bm{U}_{+})<0.

•

Structure 2:

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{-}=L_{1}^{+}(\bm{U}_{L},\varepsilon_{1}),\quad\lambda_{1}(\bm{U}_{-})<0,

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{+}=L_{1}^{-}(\varepsilon_{2};L_{2}^{-}(\varepsilon_{3};L_{3}^{-}(\varepsilon_{4};\bm{U}_{R}))),\quad\varepsilon_{2}\leq 0,\quad\lambda_{1}(\bm{U}_{+})=0.

•

Structure 3:

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{-}=\bm{U}_{L},\quad\lambda_{1}(\bm{U}_{-})>0,

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{+}=L_{1}^{-}(\varepsilon_{2};L_{2}^{-}(\varepsilon_{3};L_{3}^{-}(\varepsilon_{4};\bm{U}_{R}))),\quad\lambda_{1}(\bm{U}_{+})\geq 0.

Here
\varepsilon_{i}\,(i=1,2,3,4)
are parameters ensuring that the states belong to the admissible domain.

A similar necessity argument can be found in
[
31
]
, and the existence of each admissible structure follows from Theorem
5.1
.

The case with constant initial data, i.e.,
\bm{U}_{L}=\bm{U}_{R}
, is of particular interest. It has been applied to study wave generation due to instant condensation, see
[
5
]
. In this special situation, the monotonicity criterion determines the wave types explicitly. The following conclusion has been proven in
[
27
]
.

Corollary 4.5
.

For constant initial data
\bm{U}_{L}=\bm{U}_{R}
, it always holds that
\varepsilon_{1}\geq 0
,
\varepsilon_{2}\leq 0
, and
\varepsilon_{4}\geq 0
. Consequently, the 1-wave to the left of the boundary and the 3-wave to the right of the boundary must be shock waves, whereas the 1-wave to the right of the boundary must be a rarefaction wave.

5
Local Existence

Theorem 5.1

(
Local existence of admissible Riemann solutions).

Let
\bm{U}_{L}
and
\bm{U}_{R}
be initial Riemann data satisfying condition (
2.1
), and assume that the corresponding classical Riemann problem admits intermediate states with strictly positive velocity. Then, for all sufficiently small
k>0
, there exists an admissible solution to the coupled Riemann problem.

Proof.

Let
\bm{V}=\bm{V}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{t};\bm{U}_{L},\bm{U}_{R}\right)
be the classical Riemann solution with left and right intermediate states
\bm{V}_{*L}
and
\bm{V}_{*R}
.
Along the
t
-axis, the state of
\bm{V}
falls into one of the following cases:

(i)

\bm{V}(0;\bm{U}_{L},\bm{U}_{R})=\bm{V}_{*L}
with
\lambda_{1}(\bm{V}_{*L})<0
;

(ii)

the 1-wave is a rarefaction and the
t
-axis lies inside it, i.e.
\lambda_{1}(\bm{U}_{L})\leq 0<\lambda_{1}(\bm{V}_{*L})
;

(iii)

\bm{V}(0;\bm{U}_{L},\bm{U}_{R})=\bm{U}_{L}
with
\lambda_{1}(\bm{U}_{L})>0
;

(iv)

a stationary 1-shock;

(v)

\bm{V}(0;\bm{U}_{L},\bm{U}_{R})=\bm{V}_{*L}
with
\lambda_{1}(\bm{V}_{L})\leq 0,\ \lambda_{1}(\bm{V}_{*L})=0
.

The proof proceeds by first identifying the boundary states
(\bm{U}_{-},\bm{U}_{+})
and then verifying that

\bm{U}_{-}\in\mathcal{V}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L}),\qquad\bm{U}_{+}\in\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R}).

In Cases (ii)–(iii),
\bm{U}_{-}
and
\bm{U}_{+}
can be determined directly.
In Cases (i),(iv) and (v), we apply the implicit function theorem (for Lipschitz continuous functions).
We now treat each case separately to establish local existence of admissible Riemann solutions.

Case (i).

We establish the existence of an admissible Riemann solution of
Structure 1
for small
k>0
.
Since the coupling condition is expressed in terms of the Mach number, it is convenient to represent the 1-wave using it.
Consider the 1-wave connecting the left state
\bm{U}_{L}
to the intermediate state
\bm{U}_{-}
. We have

u_{-}(p_{-};\bm{U}_{L})=\begin{cases}u_{L}-\dfrac{p_{-}-p_{L}}{\rho_{L}}\left(\dfrac{2}{(\gamma+1)p_{-}+(\gamma-1)p_{L}}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{2}},&p_{-}\geq p_{L},\\[4.30554pt]
u_{L}-\dfrac{2c_{L}}{\gamma-1}\left[\left(\tfrac{p_{-}}{p_{L}}\right)^{\tfrac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}}-1\right],&p_{-}<p_{L},\end{cases}

(5.1)

and

c_{-}(p_{-};\bm{U}_{L})=\begin{cases}c_{L}\sqrt{\tfrac{p_{-}}{p_{L}}}\sqrt{\dfrac{(\gamma-1)p_{-}+(\gamma+1)p_{L}}{(\gamma-1)p_{L}+(\gamma+1)p_{-}}},&p_{-}\geq p_{L},\\[4.30554pt]
c_{L}\left(\tfrac{p_{-}}{p_{L}}\right)^{\tfrac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}},&p_{-}<p_{L}.\end{cases}

(5.2)

The admissible domain is specified to ensure
u_{-}>0
:

p_{-}\in\begin{cases}\left(0,\;p_{L}\Big(1+\tfrac{(\gamma-1)u_{L}}{2c_{L}}\Big)^{\tfrac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}}\right),&u_{L}\leq 0,\\[4.30554pt]
\left(0,\;p_{L}+\tfrac{1}{4}\big((\gamma+1)p_{L}^{2}u_{L}^{2}+p_{L}u_{L}\sqrt{(\gamma+1)^{2}p_{L}^{2}u_{L}^{2}+16\gamma p_{L}}\big)\right),&u_{L}>0.\end{cases}

(5.3)

It is straightforward to check that
u_{-}(p_{-};\bm{U}_{L})
and
c_{-}(p_{-};\bm{U}_{L})
are differentiable in
p_{-}
, with
(u_{-})^{\prime}<0
and
(c_{-})^{\prime}>0
, cf.
[
13
]
.
The Mach number is

M_{-}(p_{-};\bm{U}_{L})=\frac{u_{-}(p_{-};\bm{U}_{L})}{c_{-}(p_{-};\bm{U}_{L})},\qquad(M_{-})^{\prime}<0,

whose range is
(0,\infty)
.
Hence, there exists an inverse
g_{1}:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with

p_{-}=g_{1}(M_{-};\bm{U}_{L}),\qquad g_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{(M_{-})^{\prime}}<0.

The velocity can then be written as
u_{-}=g_{2}(M_{-};\bm{U}_{L})
, where

g_{2}^{\prime}=\frac{du_{-}}{dp_{-}}\frac{dp_{-}}{dM_{-}}>0.

For the boundary states
(\bm{U}_{-},\bm{U}_{+})
, we extend their relations to
k<0
. For brevity, we retain the same notation:

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(M,k)\in C^{1}\big((0,M_{1}^{*}(k)]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup(0,1]\times\mathbb{R}_{-};\mathbb{R}_{+}\big),

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(M,k)=\sqrt{\tfrac{1-I}{1+\gamma I}},

\displaystyle h_{1}(M,k)\in C^{1}\big((0,M_{1}^{*}(k)]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup(0,1]\times\mathbb{R}_{-};\mathbb{R}_{+}\big),

\displaystyle h_{1}(M,k)=\frac{\gamma M^{2}+1}{\gamma(\psi_{1}(M,k))^{2}+1},

\displaystyle h_{2}(M,k)\in C^{1}\big((0,M_{1}^{*}(k)]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup(0,1]\times\mathbb{R}_{-};\mathbb{R}_{+}\big),

\displaystyle h_{2}(M,k)=\frac{(\psi_{1}(M,k))^{2}(\gamma M^{2}+1)}{M^{2}(\gamma(\psi_{1}(M,k))^{2}+1)}.

They are well-defined, and satisfy

\psi_{1}(M;0)=M,\quad h_{1}(M;0)=h_{2}(M;0)=1,\quad\frac{d\psi_{1}(M;0)}{dM}=1,\ \frac{dh_{1}(M;0)}{dM}=\frac{dh_{2}(M;0)}{dM}=0.

The relations for the states
\bm{U}_{-}
and
\bm{U}_{+}
are

M_{+}=\psi_{1}(M_{-},k),\qquad p_{+}=p_{-}\,h_{1}(M_{-},k),\qquad u_{+}=u_{-}\,h_{2}(M_{-},k).

We now combine all the waves in the Riemann solution of
Structure 1
.
Define

\displaystyle T_{1}(M,k)

\displaystyle\in C^{1}\big(\mathcal{O}_{1}(M_{*L})\times\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}_{+}\big),

\displaystyle T_{1}(M,k)

\displaystyle:=g_{2}(M;\bm{U}_{L})h_{2}(M,k)-f_{R}\big(g_{1}(M;\bm{U}_{L})h_{1}(M,k);\bm{U}_{R}\big),

where
\mathcal{O}_{1}(M_{*L})
is a neighbourhood of
M_{*L}
, and
M_{*L}
is the Mach number of
\bm{V}_{*L}
.
By Proposition
3.1
(i) and (ii),
T_{1}
is well-defined.
From Proposition
4.3
,
M_{*L}
is a root of
T_{1}
when
k=0
, and

\frac{\partial T_{1}(M,0)}{\partial M}=g_{2}^{\prime}(M;\bm{U}_{L})-f_{R}^{\prime}(g_{1}(M;\bm{U}_{L});\bm{U}_{R})g_{1}^{\prime}(M;\bm{U}_{L})>0.

Hence, by the implicit function theorem, there exist neighbourhoods
\mathcal{O}(M_{*L})\subset\mathcal{O}_{1}(M_{*L})
,
\mathcal{O}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}
, and a
C^{1}
map
M:\mathcal{O}(0)\to\mathcal{O}(M_{*L})
such that

T_{1}(M(k),k)=0,\qquad\forall k\in\mathcal{O}(0).

For any
k\in\mathcal{O}(0)\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}
, an admissible Riemann solution of
Structure 1
is constructed as follows.
We choose
\bm{U}_{-}\in L_{1}^{+}(\varepsilon,\bm{U}_{L})
with
M_{-}=M(k)\in\mathcal{O}(M_{*L})
and the corresponding
\bm{U}_{+}
. Then

T_{1}(M_{-},k)=u_{+}-f_{R}(p_{+};\bm{U}_{R})=0,

which implies that there exist
\varepsilon_{3},\varepsilon_{4}
such that

\bm{U}_{+}=L_{2}^{+}(\varepsilon_{3};\,L_{3}^{+}(\varepsilon_{4};\bm{U}_{R})).

Since
u_{+}>0
, it follows that
\bm{U}_{+}\in\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
.
Moreover, the 1-wave connecting
\bm{U}_{L}
to
\bm{V}_{*L}
has negative speed (by the assumption of Case (i)). By shrinking
\mathcal{O}(M_{*L})
if necessary, the distance
\|\bm{U}_{-}-\bm{V}_{*L}\|
can be made arbitrarily small, ensuring
\bm{U}_{-}\in\mathcal{V}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L})
.

Case (ii).

For any suffciently small
k>0
, an admissible Riemann solution of
Structure 2
is constructed as follows.
We choose
\bm{U}_{-}=L_{1}^{+}(\varepsilon_{1};\bm{U}_{L})
such that
M_{-}=M_{1}^{*}<1
.
If
\lambda_{1}(\bm{U}_{L})=0
, then the wave connecting
\bm{U}_{L}
and
\bm{U}_{-}
is a non-degenerate shock with speed

\sigma<\lambda_{1}(\bm{U}_{L})=0.

If
\lambda_{1}(\bm{U}_{L})<0
, then by Proposition
3.1
(ii) it is a rarefaction wave for sufficiently small
k>0
.
In both cases we obtain
\bm{U}_{-}\in\mathcal{V}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L})
.

The corresponding right boundary state
\bm{U}_{+}
satisfies
M_{+}=1
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\big\|\bm{U}_{+}-\bm{V}(0;\bm{U}_{L},\bm{U}_{R})\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{U}_{+}-\bm{U}_{-}\|+\|\bm{U}_{-}-\bm{V}(0;\bm{U}_{L},\bm{U}_{R})\|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}\|\bm{U}_{+}-\bm{U}_{-}\|+|M_{1}^{*}(k)-1|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}k.

Hence, by Proposition
2.2
, the classical RP solution
\bm{V}(0;\bm{U}_{+},\bm{U}_{R})
exists and contains a non-degenerate rarefaction under the assumption
\lambda_{1}(\bm{V}_{*L})>0
.
Thus,
\bm{U}_{+}\in\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
.

Case (iii).

For any suffciently small
k>0
, an admissible Riemann solution of
Structure 3
is constructed as follows.
Here the initial data satisfies
M_{L}>1
. By Proposition
3.1
(ii), we have
M_{L}>M_{2}^{*}
for sufficiently small
k>0
.
Thus we can take
\bm{U}_{-}=\bm{U}_{L}
and determine the corresponding
\bm{U}_{+}
.
Since

\|\bm{U}_{L}-\bm{U}_{+}\|=\|\bm{U}_{-}-\bm{U}_{+}\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\sim}}k,\qquad u_{L}+\frac{2c_{L}}{\gamma-1}>u_{R}-\frac{2c_{R}}{\gamma-1},

it follows that

u_{+}+\frac{2c_{+}}{\gamma-1}>u_{R}-\frac{2c_{R}}{\gamma-1}

for sufficiently small
k
.
By Proposition
2.2
, the classical RP solution
\bm{V}(x/t;\bm{U}_{+},\bm{U}_{R})
exists and is unique.
Moreover, the intermediate states of
\bm{V}(x/t;\bm{U}_{L},\bm{U}_{R})
and
\bm{V}(x/t;\bm{U}_{+},\bm{U}_{R})
differ only by
O(k)
.
Since
M_{*L}>1
, we conclude that
\bm{U}_{+}\in\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})
.

Case (iv).

This case may correspond to two possible structures (
Structure 1
or
Structure 3
). We therefore introduce the piecewise function

\displaystyle T_{4}(M,k):=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}g_{2}(\psi^{-1}(M,k);\bm{U}_{L})\,h_{2}(\psi^{-1}(M,k),k)-f_{R}\left(g_{1}(\psi^{-1}(M,k);\bm{U}_{L})\,h_{1}(\psi^{-1}(M,k),k)\right),&M\leq\psi_{1}(M_{L},k),\\[4.30554pt]
g_{2}(M;\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{+})-f_{R}(g_{1}(M;\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{+});\bm{U}_{R}),&M>\psi_{1}(M_{L},k),\end{cases}

where the state
\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{+}
is given by

\widetilde{M}_{+}=\psi_{1}(M_{L},k),\quad\widetilde{p}_{+}=p_{-}\cdot h_{1}(M_{L},k),\quad\widetilde{u}_{+}=u_{-}\cdot h_{2}(M_{L},k).

\psi^{-1}(M,k)
is the inverse function of the function
\psi(M,k)
with respect to
M
for a given
k
.
It is easy to check that
T_{4}(M,k)
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to
M
:

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{M\to\psi_{1}(M_{L},k)-}T_{4}(M,k)

\displaystyle=g_{2}\circ\psi(M;0)\cdot h_{2}\circ\psi(M;0)-f_{R}\left(g_{1}\circ\psi(M;0)\cdot h_{1}\circ\psi(M;0)\right),

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{M\to\psi_{1}(M_{L},k)+}T_{4}(M,k),

and the one-sided derivatives at

satisfy

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{M\to\psi_{1}(M_{L},0)-}\frac{\partial T_{4}(M,0)}{\partial M}=g_{2}^{\prime}(\psi^{-1}(M,k);\bm{U}_{L})-f_{R}^{\prime}\left(g_{1}(\psi^{-1}(M;0);\bm{U}_{L})\right)g_{1}^{\prime}(\psi^{-1}(M,k);\bm{U}_{L})>0,

Corollary 5.2

(
Uniqueness of each structure).

Assume that
k<1/(\gamma^{2}-1)
. Then, for any given Riemann data
(\bm{U}_{L},\bm{U}_{R})
, there exists at most one admissible solution for each structure.

Proof.

The uniqueness of
Structure 2
and
Structure 3
follows directly from the monotonicity of
\psi
and the uniqueness of the classical Riemann solution.

For
Structure 1
, recall from the proof of case (i) in Theorem
5.1
that the left boundary state satisfies

T_{1}(M_{-},k)=g_{2}(M_{-};\bm{U}_{L})\,h_{2}(M_{-},k)-f_{R}\!\left(g_{1}(M_{-};\bm{U}_{L})\,h_{1}(M_{-},k);\bm{U}_{R}\right)=0.

By Proposition
4.3
, its derivative is strictly positive:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T_{1}}{\partial M}(M_{-},k)

\displaystyle=g_{2}^{\prime}(M_{-};\bm{U}_{L})h_{2}(M_{-},k)+g_{2}(M_{-};\bm{U}_{L})\,\frac{\partial h_{2}}{\partial M}(M_{-},k)

\displaystyle\quad-f_{R}^{\prime}\left(g_{1}(M_{-};\bm{U}_{L})h_{1}(M_{-},k);\bm{U}_{R}\right)\big(g_{1}^{\prime}(M_{-};\bm{U}_{L})h_{1}(M_{-},k)+g_{1}(M_{-};\bm{U}_{L})\frac{\partial h_{1}}{\partial M}(M_{-},k)\big)

Theorem 6.1

(
Non-existence of admissible Riemann solutions).

For any fixed
k>0
, there exist infinitely many Riemann data
\bm{U}_{L}
and
\bm{U}_{R}
such that no admissible Riemann solution exists.

Proof.

We construct a family of Riemann data for which no admissible solution exists.
Choose a state
\bm{U}_{L}\in\mathcal{D}
with
M_{L}>M_{2}^{*}(k)
, and define

\bm{U}_{R}=L_{3}^{+}(\varepsilon_{3};L_{2}^{+}(\varepsilon_{2};\overline{\bm{U}})),

where
\overline{\bm{U}}
is given by the coupling condition
\Psi(\bm{U}_{L},\overline{\bm{U}})=0
, which violates the monotonicity criterion, i.e.
\overline{M}=\psi_{1}(M_{L},k)
.
The free parameters
\varepsilon_{2},\varepsilon_{3}
are chosen so that the states remain in
\mathcal{D}
.

Step 1. Non-existence of Structure 1.
Let
\bm{U}_{-}=L_{1}^{+}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{L})
with

p_{-}=p_{L}\cdot\frac{2\gamma M_{L}^{2}-\gamma+1}{\gamma+1}.

Since
M_{L}>M_{2}^{*}(k)>1
, one checks that

p_{-}>p_{L},\qquad 0<M_{-}=\frac{(\gamma-1)M_{L}^{2}+2}{2\gamma M_{L}^{2}-\gamma+1}<1<M_{L},

so that the 1-wave connecting
\bm{U}_{L}
and
\bm{U}_{-}
is a shock wave with speed

\sigma=u_{L}-c_{L}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\cdot\frac{p_{-}}{p_{L}}+\frac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}}=0.

For the corresponding right boundary state
\bm{U}_{+}
, since
M_{+}\leq 1
and
\overline{M}\leq 1
, it follows from Proposition
3.1
(iv) that
\bm{U}_{+}=\overline{\bm{U}}
.
Moreover,
M_{-}
is the unique root of
T_{1}(M,k)=0
, see the proof of Corollary
5.2
.
However,
\sigma=0
implies
\bm{U}_{-}\notin\mathcal{V}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L})
. Hence Structure 1 is impossible.

Step 2. Non-existence of Structure 2.
Assume otherwise. Then the left boundary state must be

\bm{U}_{-}=L_{1}^{+}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{L}),\qquad M_{-}=M_{1}^{*}.

By Proposition
3.1
(v),

\frac{(\gamma-1)M_{L}^{2}+2}{2\gamma M_{L}^{2}+1-\gamma}<\frac{(\gamma-1)(M_{2}^{*})^{2}+2}{2\gamma(M_{2}^{*})^{2}+1-\gamma}=M_{1}^{*}.

Thus the left 1-wave is a shock with speed

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=u_{L}-c_{L}\sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\cdot\tfrac{g_{1}(M_{1}^{*})}{p_{L}}+\tfrac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}}

\displaystyle>u_{L}-c_{L}\sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\cdot\tfrac{1}{p_{L}}g_{1}\!\left(\tfrac{(\gamma-1)M_{L}^{2}+2}{2\gamma M_{L}^{2}+1-\gamma}\right)+\tfrac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}}

\displaystyle=u_{L}-c_{L}\sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\cdot\tfrac{1}{p_{L}}\,p_{L}\cdot\tfrac{2\gamma M_{L}^{2}-\gamma+1}{\gamma+1}+\tfrac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}}

\displaystyle=0.

Therefore
\bm{U}_{-}\notin\mathcal{V}_{L}(\bm{U}_{L})
, and Structure 2 is impossible.

Step 3. Non-existence of Structure 3.
Assume otherwise. Then
\bm{U}_{-}=\bm{U}_{L}
. Since
\overline{M}<1
and
M_{+}>1
, Proposition
3.1
(iv) implies that the pair
(\bm{U}_{+},\overline{\bm{U}})
forms a stationary shock. With

\bm{U}_{R}=L_{3}^{+}(\varepsilon_{3};L_{2}^{+}(\varepsilon_{2};\overline{\bm{U}})),

the associated classical Riemann solution reads

\bm{V}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{t};\bm{U}_{+},\bm{U}_{R}\right)=\begin{cases}\bm{U}_{+},&x<0,\\
\bm{V}\!\left(\tfrac{x}{t};\overline{\bm{U}},\bm{U}_{R}\right),&x>0.\end{cases}

Hence
\bm{U}_{+}\notin\mathcal{V}_{R}(\bm{U}_{R})

Combining the above steps, none of the three admissible structures can occur.
Therefore, for the constructed data, no admissible Riemann solution exists.
∎

For the initial data in Theorem

Although this stationary shock is physically-relevant, it does not comply with the monotonicity criterion.

Consequently, there exists a point in the solution such that

This phenomenon is often referred to as

7
Numerical Results

We verify the coupled Rimeann solutions by means of finite volume methods.
The one-dimensonal computational domain
\left[a,b\right](a<0,b>0)
is partitioned into
N
non-overlapping cells with interfaces
a=x_{\frac{1}{2}}<x_{\frac{3}{2}}<...<x_{N+\frac{1}{2}}=b
.
Each cell is denoted by
I_{j}=[x_{j-\frac{1}{2}},x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}]
, with center
x_{j}=(x_{j-\frac{1}{2}}+x_{j+\frac{1}{2}})/2
.
We restrict to a uniform mesh, with mesh size
h=x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}-x_{j-\frac{1}{2}}
.

A natural numerical strategy for the coupled problem is to solve the equations separately on the two subdomains, while enforcing the coupling condition as a boundary condition; see, e.g.,
[
16
,
20
]
.
However, the boundary states are in general two-branched. Although one branch can be selected according to the admissibility criterion, our present goal is precisely to verify both the admissibility criterion and the admissible Riemann solutions. Hence, we prefer to avoid introducing any bias stemming from a priori selection.

To this end, we employ the splitting method used in
[
28
]
. Since the system is not conservative on the entire domain
\Omega
, the essential idea is to reformulate the coupling condition as a source-type term within the numerical scheme.

Let
\Delta t>0
be a uniform time step, and define the discrete time levels by
t^{n}=n\Delta t
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Denote by
\bm{U}_{j}^{n}
the numerical approximation of the cell average of the exact solution
\bm{U}(x,t)
over cell
I_{j}
at time
t^{n}
, namely,

\bm{U}_{j}^{n}\approx\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{j}}\bm{U}(x,t^{n})\mathrm{d}x.

In particular, the initial data are discretized as

\bm{U}_{j}^{n}=\frac{1}{h}\int_{I_{j}}\bm{U}_{0}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

We evolve the numerical solution in two steps.

•

Step 1 (global update).

The flow field is advanced by a standard conservative finite volume scheme,

\widetilde{\bm{U}}^{n+1}_{j}=\bm{U}_{j}^{n}-\frac{\Delta t}{h}\left[\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n+1}}\bm{F}(\bm{U}(x_{j+\frac{1}{2}},t))dt-\int_{t^{n}}^{t^{n+1}}\bm{F}(\bm{U}(x_{j-\frac{1}{2}},t))dt\right].

The flux integrals are approximated by the local Lax–Friedrichs numerical flux.
The time step
\Delta t
is chosen according to a suitable Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition to ensure stability.

•

Step 2 (interface correction).

Since the flow is discontinuous at the coupling interface, we place the interface at a cell boundary,

a<x_{j_{0}+\frac{1}{2}}=0<b,\quad 0\leq j_{0}\leq N.

The coupling condition is enforced by a source-type correction in upwind mode

\left\{\begin{aligned} \bm{U}_{j}^{n+1}&=\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{j}^{n+1},\quad j\neq j_{0}+1\\
\bm{U}_{j}^{n+1}&=\widetilde{\bm{U}}_{j}^{n+1}+\frac{\Delta t}{h}\bm{S}^{n+1},,\quad j=j_{0}+1,\end{aligned}\right.

where the source term
\bm{S}^{,n+1}
is defined by

\bm{S}^{n+1}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
(\widetilde{E}_{j_{0}+1}^{n+1}+\widetilde{p}_{j_{0}+1}^{n+1})\widetilde{u}_{j_{0}+1}^{n+1}-(\widetilde{E}_{j_{0}}^{n+1}+\widetilde{p}_{j_{0}}^{n+1})\widetilde{u}_{j_{0}}^{n+1}\end{pmatrix}.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we have studied the coupled Riemann problem with a heat flux discontinuity by the half-Riemann problem approach. Owing to the different regimes of the Mach number, several solution structures may occur. We have shown that weak solutions are not necessarily unique and introduced an admissibility criterion to select the physically relevant ones. The local existence of admissible solutions has been established. Furthermore, we have constructed a family of initial data for which no admissible solution exists. Finally, several theoretical findings have been verified by numerical experiments.

Appendix A
Lax curves of the Euler system

The classical Riemann solution of the Euler system can be constructed from simple waves associated with the three characteristic fields. In this section, we list the Lax curves of these waves. The 2-characteristic field is linearly degenerate, while the 1- and 3-characteristic fields are genuinely nonlinear. For simplicity, we only explicitly list the Lax curves for the 1-field and 2-field; the 3-field can be obtained analogously.

A.1
1-characteristic field

The Lax curve
L_{1}^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})
is expressed in terms of the primitive variables: density
\rho^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})
, velocity
u^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})
, and pressure
p^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})
. We take the parameter
\varepsilon
as

\varepsilon=\frac{p^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})}{p_{0}}-1,

so that

p^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})=p_{0}(\varepsilon+1).

Shock wave:
If
\varepsilon\geq 0
, then the 1-wave is a shock, and

\displaystyle\rho^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}\frac{(\gamma+1)(\varepsilon+1)+(\gamma-1)}{(\gamma-1)(\varepsilon+1)+(\gamma+1)},

\displaystyle u^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})

\displaystyle=u_{0}+c_{0}(\rho_{0}-\rho^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0}))\big(\rho_{0}+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}(\rho_{0}-\rho^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0}))\big)

Rarefaction wave:
If
\varepsilon<0
, then the 1-wave is a rarefaction, and

\displaystyle\rho^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}(1+\varepsilon)^{1/\gamma},

\displaystyle u^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})

\displaystyle=u_{0}+\frac{2c_{0}}{\gamma-1}\left[1-(1+\varepsilon)^{(\gamma-1)/(2\gamma)}\right].

It is easy to check that
L_{1}^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})
is twice continuously differentiable with respect to
\varepsilon
, and

\frac{du^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})}{d\varepsilon}<0,\quad\frac{d^{2}u^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})}{d\varepsilon^{2}}>0,

see, e.g.,
[
13
]
.

A.2
2-characteristic field

Across the contact discontinuity (2-field), the pressure and velocity remain constant. The Lax curve
L_{2}^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})
can be written as

\rho^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})=\rho_{0}(1+\varepsilon),\quad u^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})\equiv u_{0},\quad p^{\pm}(\varepsilon;\bm{U}_{0})\equiv p_{0}.

Proof of Proposition

We first consider
h_{1}(M,k)

\displaystyle h_{1}(M,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma M^{2}+1}{\gamma(\psi(M))^{2}+1}=\frac{\gamma M^{2}+1}{\gamma\frac{1-I}{1+\gamma I}+1}=\frac{(\gamma M^{2}+1)(1+\gamma I)}{\gamma+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma+1}\left(\gamma M^{2}+1+\gamma\left[(\gamma M^{2}+1)^{2}-(\gamma+1)M^{2}\big((\gamma-1)M^{2}+2\big)(1+k)\right]^{1/2}\right).

\displaystyle\big(k-t+\gamma k-kt+\gamma^{2}kt+1\big)^{2}-\Big[(\gamma t+1)^{2}-(\gamma+1)t\big((\gamma-1)t+2\big)(1+k)\Big]

\displaystyle=\big[(1-t)+k(1-t)+\gamma k(\gamma t+1)\big]^{2}-(1-t)^{2}+k(\gamma t+1)^{2}-k(1-t)^{2}

\displaystyle=k(k+1)(1-t)^{2}+\gamma^{2}k^{2}(\gamma t+1)^{2}+2(1-t)\gamma k(\gamma t+1)+2\gamma k^{2}(1-t)(\gamma t+1)+k(\gamma t+1)^{2}

\displaystyle>0,

we deduce

\frac{\partial h_{1}}{\partial M}(M,k)<0,\qquad\text{for any }M<1,\,k>0.

We then consider
h_{2}(M,k)
. By definition,

\displaystyle h_{2}(M,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\psi(M,k))^{2}(\gamma M^{2}+1)}{M^{2}(\gamma(\psi(M,k))^{2}+1)}=\frac{(\gamma M^{2}+1)(1-I)}{(\gamma+1)M^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma M^{2}+1-\big[(\gamma M^{2}+1)^{2}-(\gamma+1)M^{2}\big((\gamma-1)M^{2}+2\big)(1+k)\big]^{1/2}}{(\gamma+1)M^{2}}.

Its derivative is

\frac{\gamma+1}{2M}\frac{\partial h_{2}}{\partial M}=\frac{1}{(\gamma+1)t^{2}}\left\{\frac{1-kt-k\gamma t-t}{\left[(\gamma t+1)^{2}-t(\gamma+1)\big((\gamma-1)t+2\big)(1+k)\right]^{1/2}}-1\right\}.

When
k<\tfrac{1}{\gamma^{2}-1}
, we have

1-kt-k\gamma t-t>0.

Moreover,

\displaystyle(1-kt-k\gamma t-t)^{2}-\Big[(\gamma t+1)^{2}-t(\gamma+1)\big((\gamma-1)t+2\big)(1+k)\Big]

\displaystyle=k(1+k)(\gamma+1)^{2}t^{2}>0.

Hence

\frac{\partial h_{2}}{\partial M}(M,k)>0,\qquad\text{for any }M<1,\,k>0.

∎
