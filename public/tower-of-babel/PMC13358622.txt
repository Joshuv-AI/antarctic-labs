Optimal surgical timing for atrial functional mitral regurgitation based on preoperative diuretic requirement: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Kazuma Handa, Shin Yajima, Yusuke Yanagino, Yumi Kakizawa, Daisuke Yoshioka, Takuji Kawamura, Ai Kawamura, Yusuke Misumi, Ryoto Sakaniwa, Shunsuke Saito, Shigeru Miyagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtrial functional mitral regurgitation (AFMR) is increasingly prevalent worldwide and is associated with poor clinical outcomes, yet the optimal timing of surgical intervention remains unclear. Although preoperative diuretic dosage may reflect heart failure severity, its prognostic significance after surgery for AFMR has not been fully established. This study investigated the association between preoperative diuretic dosage and postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing surgery for AFMR.MethodsAmong 915 patients who underwent mitral valve surgery (MVS) between 2008 and 2023, 85 patients with AFMR were identified and categorized into high-dose (≥40 mg furosemide-equivalent, n=35) and low-dose (<40 mg, n=50) groups based on preoperative diuretic dosage. Ten-year cumulative incidences of all-cause mortality, cardiac mortality, and heart failure readmission were evaluated.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable between groups in terms of age (78 vs. 75 years, P=0.09), male sex (46% vs. 62%, P=0.19), total bilirubin (1.0 vs. 0.9 mg/dL, P=0.23), ejection fraction (EF; 61% vs. 65%, P=0.20), and Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality (STS-PROM) (5.3% vs. 4.5%, P=0.94). Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was lower in the high-dose group (48.3 vs. 58.9 mL/min/1.73 m2, P=0.006). All-cause mortality (51.7% vs. 22.9%, P=0.02), cardiac mortality (47.8% vs. 13.1%, P=0.007), and heart failure readmission (71.1% vs. 52.4%, P=0.005) were significantly higher in the high-dose group over the 10-year follow-up. High-dose diuretic use remained an independent risk factor in multivariable analysis [adjusted hazard ratio (HR) 2.89; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.01–8.25; P=0.048]. Mortality was particularly higher among patients with renal dysfunction, regardless of dosage, and those with liver dysfunction receiving high-dose diuretics.ConclusionsIn AFMR surgery patients, especially with concomitant renal or liver dysfunction, high-dose diuretic use was associated with increased mortality and heart failure readmission.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In 85 patients undergoing mitral valve surgery for atrial functional mitral regurgitation (AFMR), high-dose diuretics (≥40 mg furosemide-equivalent) were associated with higher 10-year all-cause mortality, cardiac mortality, and heart failure readmission. High-dose diuretic use remained an independent risk factor for mortality. Outcomes were particularly poor among patients with renal dysfunction, regardless of dose, and in those with liver dysfunction receiving high-dose diuretics.

What is known and what is new?

• Outcomes after surgery for AFMR are suboptimal, and the optimal surgical timing remains unclear because symptoms can be masked by medical therapy and conventional markers such as atrial size are unreliable.

• This study demonstrates that diuretic burden (≥40 mg furosemide-equivalent) identifies a higher-risk population in AFMR. In addition, concomitant renal or liver dysfunction further refines risk stratification and highlights systemic decompensation that is not captured by conventional cardiac indices.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings support earlier referral for surgical evaluation of AFMR while patients remain on low-dose diuretics, or at the first need for dose escalation, rather than delaying intervention until high-dose diuretic therapy and organ dysfunction have developed.

Introduction

Atrial functional mitral regurgitation (AFMR) has emerged as a distinct entity, in addition to conventional ventricular functional MR. AFMR is characterized by preserved ventricular function and impaired leaflet coaptation caused by atrial enlargement and annular remodeling, regardless of atrial fibrillation (1,2). It is increasingly recognized in aging populations of developed countries and is associated with a high risk of hospitalization for heart failure and increased mortality (3,4). While surgical intervention is essential for improving the prognosis, long-term outcomes remain suboptimal (5,6). A major contributing factor is the lack of evidence guiding the optimal surgical timing, often resulting in delayed interventions (7-9).

Oral diuretics are the standard therapy for fluid management in heart failure, and their dosage closely reflects disease severity. The furosemide equivalence formula allows for the uniform expression of various oral diuretics as furosemide-equivalent dosages and is widely used in clinical practice. A furosemide-equivalent dose of ≥40 mg is considered high and marks the threshold above which prognosis worsens in patients with heart failure (10,11). However, the effect of the preoperative diuretic dosage on surgical outcomes in patients with AFMR remains unclear. Current guidelines recommend surgery for severe AFMR with recurrent heart failure despite optimal medical therapy, including diuretics (7). High-dose diuretics may mask worsening heart failure symptoms while allowing renal and liver dysfunction to develop or persist, further compromising surgical outcomes (12,13).

This study aimed to evaluate the association between the preoperative diuretic dosage and clinical outcomes in patients undergoing mitral valve surgery (MVS) for AFMR. The impact of renal and liver dysfunction on prognosis was also assessed to provide insights into the optimal surgical timing. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0528/rc).

Methods

Ethical statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. It was approved by the University of Osaka Hospital Clinical Research Ethics Committee [No. 23121(T6); approval date: August 1, 2023]. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Definition of AFMR

AFMR was defined based on a previous report (2), as preserved left ventricular function [ejection fraction (EF) ≥50%] and dilated left atrium (left atrium volume index, ≥38 mL/m2 for males and ≥41 mL/m2 for females; if the left atrium volume index was not available, then left atrium diameter, ≥40 mm for males and ≥37 mm for females).

Study population

This single-center, retrospective observational study investigated MVS performed at the University of Osaka Hospital between 2008 and 2023. A total of 915 patients who underwent MVS were identified. Patients were excluded if they underwent surgery for mitral stenosis (n=97), reintervention of the mitral valve (n=60), or infective endocarditis (n=34). Among the remaining 718 patients, those with Carpentier type II degenerative mitral findings (n=464) were excluded. Of the patients with functional MR (n=254), those who did not meet the diagnostic criteria for AFMR (n=163), including all patients with ventricular etiology, or had insufficient echocardiographic parameters (n=6) were also excluded. Ultimately, 85 patients who underwent MVS for AFMR for the first time were included in the study. A detailed patient selection flowchart is shown in Figure 1.

Grouping

Patients were stratified into two groups according to preoperative diuretic dose, calculated using a furosemide-equivalent formula (furosemide 40 mg = azosemide 60 mg = torasemide 10 mg = trichlormethiazide 2 mg) (10,11). Patients receiving ≥40 mg of furosemide-equivalent diuretics were classified as the high-dose group (n=35), while those receiving <40 mg of furosemide-equivalent diuretics were classified as the low-dose group (n=50). Examples of this stratification are shown in Figure 2.

Surgical indication and MVS

The indications for MVS in patients with AFMR were determined by a multidisciplinary team of cardiovascular surgeons and cardiologists. The primary indication for surgery was severe mitral regurgitation accompanied by clinical symptoms of heart failure. Patients were also considered eligible for surgery when AFMR was assessed as less than moderate on stable-state echocardiography performed immediately before surgery, but moderate or greater mitral regurgitation had previously been documented during episodes of worsening heart failure. For such cases, the indication for surgery was determined comprehensively by the multidisciplinary heart team based on the combined pathophysiology of AFMR, severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR), and long-standing persistent atrial fibrillation, particularly when continuing medical management was difficult. In patients with AFMR, concomitant tricuspid valve repair was indicated for moderate or greater TR or mild TR in the presence of tricuspid annular dilatation and/or atrial fibrillation (7-9). The choice between valve replacement and ring annuloplasty, as well as the type of prosthetic valve or ring annuloplasty used, was left to the discretion of the operating surgeon. However, bioprosthetic valves were generally preferred for mitral valve replacement in patients aged >65 years.

The indication for the maze procedure was determined individually, and it was generally contraindicated for patients with low-amplitude f-waves (<0.1 mV in lead V1) (14), long-standing atrial fibrillation (>5 years) (15), or marked left atrial enlargement (left atrium diameter >60 mm) (16).

Follow-up

After hospital discharge, patients were scheduled for follow-up visits at the outpatient clinic at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, and then annually thereafter. Postoperative follow-up data were collected through a review of medical records and supplemented by telephone or postal interviews when necessary. The median follow-up period was 6.4 (interquartile range, 6.0–7.9) years.

Endpoints

All clinical events were recorded and defined according to criteria established by the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) (17). The primary endpoint was the 10-year cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality, while the secondary endpoints included the 10-year cumulative incidence of cardiac mortality and heart failure readmission. Additionally, patients were further analyzed by stratifying based on diuretic dosage combined with renal dysfunction [estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <60 mL/min/1.73 m2] or liver dysfunction [total bilirubin (T-Bil) ≥1.2 mg/dL]. The 10-year cumulative incidences of all-cause mortality, cardiac mortality, and heart failure readmission were then analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (%), while continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables between independent groups. The cumulative incidence of events was calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. To estimate the risk of all-cause mortality in the high-dose group compared with the low-dose group for preoperative diuretic dosage, a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was constructed. The model was adjusted for preoperative and surgery-related factors identified as potential confounders in the univariable analysis (P<0.1), including age and male sex. Before developing the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, multicollinearity among the variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF): a VIF value of >5.0 was considered to indicate significant multicollinearity. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro (version 17.1.0, SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patients’ characteristics

Patient characteristics in each group are shown in Table 1. There were no significant differences between the high-dose and low-dose groups in terms of age [78 (IQR, 71–82) vs. 75 (IQR, 68–77) years, P=0.09], and male sex (45.7% vs. 62.0%, P=0.19). Body mass index, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class ≥ III, and Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality (STS-PROM) did not differ between the groups. Hypertension was more common in the low-dose group (34.3% vs. 58.0%, P=0.047) whereas atrial fibrillation (88.6% vs. 62.0%, P=0.003) was more common in the high-dose group. eGFR was lower in the high-dose group [48.3 (IQR, 39.9–54.4) vs. 58.9 (IQR, 46.6–74.4) mL/min/1.73 m2, P=0.006], whereas T-Bil level did not differ between the two groups. Figure 3 presents a breakdown of the diuretic dosage across groups. Preoperative echocardiography parameters, including left ventricular diameter in diastole (LVDd), left ventricular diameter in systole (LVDs), EF, and the degree of MR, showed no significant differences between groups. In a few patients, surgery was indicated because mitral regurgitation had been severe during heart failure worsening. The left atrial volume index tended to be larger in the high-dose group [118.2 (IQR, 85.5–166.3) vs. 88.4 (IQR, 58.7–122.3) mL/m2, P=0.051]. No significant differences were observed in the incidence of minimally invasive surgery, mitral valve repair, or concomitant maze procedure. However, the rate of concomitant tricuspid valve repair tended to be higher in the high-dose group (62.9% vs. 42.0%, P=0.057).

Long-term outcomes according to preoperative diuretic dosage

The 10-year cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality was significantly higher in the high-dose group (51.7%) than in the low-dose group (22.9%) (P=0.019; Figure 4). Similarly, the 10-year cumulative incidences of cardiac mortality (47.8% vs. 13.1%, P=0.007) and heart failure readmission (71.1% vs. 52.4%, P=0.005) were significantly higher in the high-dose group (Figure 5). In univariable analysis, STS-PROM, eGFR, and T-Bil were identified as potential confounders, in addition to high-dose diuretics, age, and male sex (Table 3). No significant multicollinearity was observed among these variables (Table S1). After adjusting for these confounders, high-dose diuretic use remained an independent risk factor for all-cause mortality [adjusted hazard ratio (HR): 2.89; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.01–8.25; P=0.048; Table 3].

Sensitivity analysis excluding the five patients with mild AFMR on stable-state echocardiography immediately before surgery showed that the high-dose group continued to have significantly higher 10-year cumulative incidences of all-cause mortality (50.0% vs. 23.8%, P=0.032), cardiac mortality (45.7% vs. 13.4%, P=0.01), and heart failure readmission (54.0% vs. 36.7%, P=0.02, Figure S1).

Impact of the combination of diuretic dosage & organ dysfunction

Regarding the cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality based on the combination of diuretic dosage and renal dysfunction, the high-dose diuretic group with renal dysfunction had the highest mortality rate, followed by the low-dose group with renal dysfunction. Patients without renal dysfunction exhibited favorable outcomes, regardless of the diuretic dosage (Figure 6A). In the analysis combining diuretic dosage and liver dysfunction, patients with liver dysfunction receiving high-dose diuretics had the worst prognosis. In contrast, the remaining groups—including those receiving high-dose diuretics without liver dysfunction and low-dose groups—showed comparable outcomes regardless of liver function (Figure 6B). A similar trend was observed for cardiac mortality; the high-dose group with renal dysfunction showed the highest incidence, followed by the low-dose group with renal dysfunction (Figure 7A). Likewise, patients with liver dysfunction who received high-dose diuretics had a higher incidence of cardiac mortality, whereas the other three groups demonstrated similar outcomes (Figure 7B). Regarding heart failure readmission, the high-dose diuretic group, with or without renal or liver dysfunction, had a higher incidence of readmission (Figure 7C,7D).

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective study of patients with AFMR undergoing MVS, the high-dose diuretic group (≥40 mg furosemide equivalent) showed significantly higher 10-year cumulative incidences of all-cause mortality, cardiac mortality, and heart failure readmission compared with the low-dose group (<40 mg). Notably, high-dose diuretic use emerged as an independent risk factor for all-cause mortality, along with STS-PROM and renal dysfunction. Preoperative renal dysfunction was associated with worse outcomes in both diuretic dosage groups, and patients in the high-dose cohort with liver dysfunction exhibited worse postoperative outcomes.

In this study, the high-dose group had lower serum sodium and hematocrit levels than did the low-dose group, suggesting more severe fluid retention despite higher diuretic therapy. A higher prevalence of atrial fibrillation and larger left atrial volume index on echocardiography suggested more advanced heart failure in the high-dose group. The relatively low percentage of patients who underwent the maze procedure may reflect the advanced atrial remodeling and marked left atrial enlargement often seen in AFMR (18). Accordingly, fewer patients with AFMR than those with degenerative mitral regurgitation may have been suitable candidates for the maze procedure. This finding also highlights the importance of earlier referral before atrial remodeling becomes advanced.

In patients with HFpEF, atrial fibrillation can further compromise hemodynamics through loss of atrial contraction, irregular ventricular filling, and more advanced atrial myopathy. This makes volume control more challenging and increases the need for intensified diuretic therapy (19). These pathophysiological mechanisms may partly explain why patients in the high-dose group required more intensive outpatient diuretic management. The NYHA classes of the groups were similar; however, the escalation of diuretic therapy in the high-dose group may have concealed the underlying progressive cardiac remodeling. This was accompanied by a decline in eGFR, indicating worsening renal dysfunction. These findings are consistent with previous studies on diuretic dosing in heart failure (10), suggesting that in AFMR, the need for ≥40 mg of furosemide-equivalent diuretics during outpatient management may also reflect advanced disease progression. Recent studies on surgical indications for AFMR suggest that the left atrial diameter may not be a reliable risk factor (2,20,21), possibly due to the confounding influence of atrial fibrillation. This can independently cause atrial enlargement unrelated to volume overload. In this study, neither atrial fibrillation nor the left atrial volume index was a significant predictor in the Cox proportional hazards model. However, diuretic dosage remained independently associated with outcomes, along with renal dysfunction and STS-PROM, even after adjusting for confounding variables. This suggests that the diuretic dosage may serve as a valuable clinical marker for patients with AFMR. In addition, potassium-sparing agents and vasopressin receptor antagonists were more frequently used in the high-dose group, suggesting that these patients required broader and more intensive outpatient diuretic management to achieve symptomatic relief. Although such intensified therapy may temporarily stabilize symptoms, it may also reflect ongoing progression of organ dysfunction.

Renal and liver dysfunctions associated with valvular heart disease are well-established predictors of poor prognosis, and early surgical intervention before the onset of organ dysfunction is critical for improving long-term outcomes (12,13). In this study, multivariable analysis identified renal dysfunction as an independent risk factor for mortality. Notably, renal dysfunction was associated with increased mortality even in patients receiving low-dose diuretics. In the context of heart failure, renal dysfunction may result not only from cardiorenal syndrome but also from underlying irreversible chronic kidney disease (CKD) (22). In cases where renal dysfunction is due to cardiorenal syndrome, escalation of diuretic therapy may result in renal functional recovery through correction of excessive fluid overload. Conversely, irreversible CKD, such as end-stage diabetic nephropathy or hypertensive nephrosclerosis, is unlikely to improve with intensified diuretic treatment (23,24). For these patients, early surgical intervention should be encouraged while they remain in a low-dose diuretic state. The absence of a significant difference in newly required dialysis between the groups may reflect a type II error due to the limited sample size. Nevertheless, from a clinical perspective, patients requiring high-dose diuretics may still have a greater risk of postoperative renal deterioration requiring dialysis because they often have impaired renal reserve before surgery. This theory is supported by the higher percentage of new patients requiring dialysis in the high-dose group.

In the present cohort, all concomitant tricuspid valve procedures were performed as tricuspid valve repair, and no patient underwent tricuspid valve replacement. According to the literature, a dilated tricuspid annular diameter, right ventricular dysfunction with marked right ventricular enlargement, pronounced leaflet tethering, and severe pulmonary hypertension have been reported as risk factors for recurrent TR after tricuspid valve repair in severe functional TR (25-27). For patients with these findings, tricuspid valve replacement may be considered.

Even mild preoperative elevation of serum total bilirubin has been reported as an independent predictor of adverse events after cardiac surgery (28,29). This pattern reflects congestive hepatopathy, characterized by structural and functional changes including sinusoidal dilatation, irreversible centrilobular hepatocyte atrophy, and progressive fibrosis (30,31). Once this stage is reached, hepatic reserve is markedly compromised, leading to increasing mortality (30,31). Therefore, in patients with AFMR, preoperative serum bilirubin elevation should be interpreted as a sentinel marker of advanced systemic congestion and multi-organ vulnerability. To preserve liver function and optimize long-term outcomes, early surgical intervention should be considered before bilirubin exceeds the upper limit of normal and before escalation to high-dose diuretic therapy.

A relatively large proportion of patients underwent mitral valve replacement in the present study. One possible explanation is that marked annular dilatation and severe hamstringing of the posterior leaflet in AFMR may make mitral valve repair less suitable, as excessive annuloplasty can lead to annular dehiscence or functional mitral stenosis (32). In addition, some patients may require complex patch augmentation of the shortened posterior leaflet, which may prolong the aortic cross-clamp time and compromise long-term durability (32). Furthermore, mitral valve replacement may have been selected more frequently because this cohort was relatively older (median age: approximately 75 years). This is particularly true for older patients, for whom a simpler and more reproducible procedure was considered preferable. Our approach may also be supported by prior work on functional mitral regurgitation suggesting that the difference in survival between repair and replacement is reduced in patients older than 75 years (33).

The concept of AFMR is relatively new, and evidence regarding the optimal timing of surgical intervention remains limited. This study is the first to demonstrate the impact of preoperative diuretic dosage on postoperative outcomes in patients with AFMR and offers novel evidence that the dose required for fluid management may serve as a surrogate marker of disease severity. Patients without renal or liver dysfunction who require low-dose diuretics (<40 mg furosemide-equivalent) can be safely managed in the outpatient setting. However, if organ dysfunction persists despite diuretic escalation, timely surgical intervention should be considered. Moreover, in patients with irreversible renal dysfunction unrelated to cardiorenal syndrome, early surgery may be encouraged, even at a low diuretic dose. A thorough assessment of diuretic requirements and the etiology of organ dysfunction during outpatient management, combined with a comprehensive heart team evaluation to determine the optimal surgical timing, may further improve patient outcomes.

Limitations

This study was a single-center retrospective analysis with a relatively small sample size, which precluded the use of propensity score matching. Consequently, residual baseline differences may have influenced the outcomes, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Although loop diuretics were the primary focus, other classes of diuretics, such as potassium-sparing agents and vasopressin receptor antagonists, were not evaluated because of the lack of universally accepted conversion formulas. Additionally, the database lacked preoperative renal ultrasound or biopsy data, which could help assess the renal recovery potential and appropriateness of diuretic dosing. Postoperative NYHA functional class was not systematically assessed in this retrospective study, and longitudinal changes in functional status could not be reliably evaluated. The influence of unmeasured confounding factors cannot be excluded. Future large-scale multicenter studies are needed to confirm and extend upon these findings.

Conclusions

In patients with AFMR undergoing MVS, the requirement of ≥40 mg furosemide-equivalent diuretics for preoperative fluid management was associated with significantly higher long-term mortality and heart failure-related events. Concomitant renal or liver dysfunction further worsened prognosis. Prompt surgical intervention should be considered when escalation of diuretic therapy becomes necessary, particularly in patients with concomitant organ dysfunction.
