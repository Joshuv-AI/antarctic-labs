Direct Scattering of the Focusing Nonlinear Schrödinger Equation with Step-like Oscillatory Initial Data
Tamara Grava, Robert Jenkins, Xiaofan Zhang, Zechuan Zhang

Abstract
In this manuscript we set up the direct and inverse scattering problems for step-like traveling wave solutions of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation. Specifically, we consider initial data u ⁡ ( x , 0 ) u(x,0) satisfying u ⁡ ( x , 0 ) → { u 0 ℓ ​ ( x ) as x → − ∞ , u 0 r ​ ( x ) as x → ∞ , u(x,0)\to\begin{cases}u_{0}^{\ell}(x)&\mbox{as $x\to-\infty$},\\ u_{0}^{r}(x)&\mbox{as $x\to\infty$},\end{cases} where u 0 ℓ ​ ( x ) u_{0}^{\ell}(x) and u 0 r ​ ( x ) u_{0}^{r}(x) are elliptic traveling waves. Under certain assumptions on the initial data we formulate the direct scattering problem and establish analytic properties of the scattering data. We then formulate the inverse problem as a Riemann–Hilbert problem and prove its solvability. Finally, we observe that this Riemann–Hilbert formulation is a special case of the one arising for full soliton-gas initial data.

Introduction

This paper investigates the direct and inverse scattering problems for the cubic focusing nonlinear Schrödinger
(NLS) equation with step-like oscillatory initial data, namely with two different quasi-periodic
travelling wave solutions at
x=\pm\infty
.
The focusing NLS equation is given by

\text{i}u_{t}+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}u_{xx}+|u|^{2}u=0\,,\hskip 10.00002ptx\in{\mathbb{R}},\hskip 10.00002ptt\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+},

(1.1)

where
u=u(x,t)\in{\mathbb{C}}
and subscripts denote partial derivatives.
This equation is a fundamental integrable model with applications to water waves
[
1
,
25
]
, nonlinear fiber optics
[
18
]
, plasma physics
[
22
]
, and Bose-Einstein condensates
[
21
]
.
It is one of the prototypical integrable partial differential equations (PDEs), and it can be expressed as the compatibility condition of two linear equations, the so-called Lax pair, introduced by Zakharov and Shabat in 1972
[
25
]
,
which takes the form

\displaystyle W_{x}=\mathcal{L}(u,z)\,W\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(u,z)=-\text{i}z\sigma_{3}+U(x,t)\,,

(1.2a)

\displaystyle W_{t}=\mathcal{B}(u,z)\,W\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(u,z)=-\text{i}z^{2}\sigma_{3}+zU-\frac{1}{2}\text{i}\sigma_{3}(U^{2}-U_{x})\,,

(1.2b)

where
W(x,t;z)\in\mbox{Mat}(2\times 2,\mathbb{C})
is the fundamental matrix solution of the above linear system,
z\in\mathbb{C}
is the spectral parameter, and

U(x,t)=\begin{pmatrix}0&u(x,t)\\
-\overline{u(x,t)}&0\end{pmatrix},\hskip 20.00003pt\sigma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}[r]1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(1.3)

We call equation (
1.2a
) the Zakharov–Shabat (ZS) spectral problem associated with the NLS equation.
The compatibility of the above two equations gives

\mathcal{L}_{t}-\mathcal{B}_{x}+[\mathcal{L},\mathcal{B}]=0,

that is equivalent to the NLS equation (
1.1
).
The NLS equation has a family of quasi-periodic travelling waves of the form

u_{0}(x,t,v,x_{0})\,=\,\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\omega_{0}t}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\big(-vx+\frac{v^{2}}{2}t\big)}\psi(x-vt-x_{0}),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in{\mathbb{R}}\penalty\ ,\hskip 10.00002ptt\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\penalty\ ,

(1.4)

where
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}

is a quasi-periodic function while
|\psi(x)|
is a periodic elliptic function,
v
is a real constant that defines the velocity of the wave and
x_{0}
is a phase shift. The function
\psi
satisfies the ODE

\frac{1}{2}\psi_{xx}(x)+\omega_{0}\psi(x)+|\psi(x)|^{2}\psi(x)\,=\,0\penalty\ ,\hskip 10.00002ptx\in{\mathbb{R}}\penalty\ .

(1.5)

The general solution of the ODE (
1.5
) can be written as
\psi(x,t)=\phi(x)\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\theta(x)},
where
\phi
and
\theta
are expressed via elliptic functions
(see (
2.1
)).

When
\psi=\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}p_{0}x}\psi_{0}
with constants
p_{0}
and
\psi_{0}
, one obtains the

plane wave
solution.
It is well-known that plane waves
are dynamically unstable in the focusing case
[
23
,
13
]
. The essence of the phenomenon is well-understood: linearized stability analysis shows that a uniform background is unstable to long wavelength perturbations
[
24
]
.

The direct and inverse scattering problem for non-zero boundary conditions, and in particular for plane waves with different phases at
x=\pm\infty
,
has been developed by Biondini and Kovačič in
[
6
]
. Demontis, Prinari, van der Mee and Vitale further develop the inverse scattering transform (IST) for asymmetric boundary plane wave initial data
[
8
]
. The long-time dynamics of plane wave solutions with long-range perturbation has been considered in
[
7
]
.

In this manuscript, we concentrate on the less explored situation where
|\psi|
is an elliptic function, and study the direct and inverse scattering problem with initial data asymptotic to two distinct elliptic solutions as
x\to\pm\infty
.
Egorova et al.
[
10
]
established the IST for the Korteweg-de Vries equation with step-like quasi-periodic (finite-gap) backgrounds. However, the extension to the NLS equation presents new challenges.
The elliptic solutions are linearly unstable
[
9
]
, and one of the motivations to develop the direct and inverse scattering problem for step-like elliptic wave solutions is to understand the long-time
behaviour of these solutions.

We consider initial data of the form

u(x,0)\to\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u_{0}^{\ell}(x)&\mbox{as $x\to-\infty$}\\
u_{0}^{r}(x)&\mbox{as $x\to\infty$}\end{array}\right.

(1.6)

where
u_{0}^{\ell}(x)=u_{0}^{\ell}(x,t=0,v^{\ell},x_{0}^{\ell})
and

are two distinct elliptic solutions to the NLS equation of the form (

and
v^{r}

Our result is the formulation of the direct and inverse scattering problem for such initial data.

Elliptic travelling waves

In this section we review the theory of elliptic travelling wave for the NLS equation and the corresponding spectral curve.
We need to solve equation (
1.5

\frac{1}{2}\psi_{xx}(x)+\omega_{0}\psi(x)+|\psi|^{2}\psi(x)\,=\,0\penalty\ ,\hskip 10.00002ptx\in{\mathbb{R}}\penalty\ .

\displaystyle\delta^{2}

\displaystyle-e_{1}e_{2}e_{3},\hskip 10.00002pt\omega_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3}),\;\;\delta_{1}=e_{1}e_{2}+e_{1}e_{3}+e_{2}e_{3}.\hskip 10.00002pt

K(k)=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\left(1-k\sin^{2}\xi\right)^{-1/2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

(2.2)

When
k=0
,
\mathrm{sn}^{2}(x;0)=\sin^{2}(x)
with
L=\pi
. As

k
approaches 1,
\mathrm{sn}(x,k)
approaches
\tanh(x)
and
L
approaches
infinity
[
19
]
. Although
\phi(x)
inherits the periodicity of
\mathrm{sn}(x,k)
, the solution

\psi(x,t)
is
typically
not

L
-periodic in the space variable
x
because the periods
of
\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\theta}
and
\phi
are typically incommensurate.
Note that
\delta^{2}\geq 0
, which implies that
e_{1}\leq 0<e_{2}<e_{3}
or
e_{1}<0\leq e_{2}<e_{3}
. When
e_{1}=0
or
e_{2}=0
, we have
\theta=0
, and in this case the travelling wave is
x
-periodic:

\begin{split}&e_{1}=0,\quad\psi(x)\,=\,\phi(x)=\sqrt{e_{3}}\mbox{dn}(\sqrt{e_{3}}x;k)\,,\\
&e_{2}=0,\quad\psi(x)\,=\,\phi(x)=\sqrt{e_{3}}\mbox{cn}(\sqrt{e_{3}-e_{1}}x;k),\end{split}

(2.3)

where
\mbox{dn}(x;k)
and
\mbox{cn}(x;k)
are the Jacobi elliptic functions.
When we consider a solution of (
1.4
) with
v=0
, namely

u_{0}(x,t,v=0)=\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\omega_{0}t}\psi(x),

then the fundamental matrix solution of the ZS spectral problem is of the form

\hat{\chi}(x,t;z)=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\text{i}}{2}\omega_{0}t\sigma_{3}}\chi(x;z)\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}Rt\sigma_{3}},

(2.4)

for a
2\times 2
matrix
\chi(x;z)
with time-independent entries
so that one obtains the equation

(\mathcal{B}(\psi;z)+\frac{\text{i}}{2}\omega_{0}\sigma_{3})\chi=\text{i}R\chi\sigma_{3}\,,

where
\mathcal{B}(\psi,z)
is the ZS linear operator with respect to the potential
\psi(x)
that satisfies equation (
1.5
).
The above equation
has a non-trivial solution only when

R^{2}=\det(\mathcal{B}+\frac{\text{i}}{2}\omega_{0}\sigma_{3})=z^{4}-z^{2}\omega_{0}-\delta z+\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}-\delta_{1}}{4},

where the constants of integration
\delta
and
\delta_{1}
are defined in (
2.1c
) and coincide with the quantities

\delta=\frac{\text{i}}{2}(\psi\overline{\psi}_{x}-\psi_{x}\overline{\psi}),\hskip 10.00002pt\delta_{1}=-|\psi|^{4}-2\omega_{0}|\psi|^{2}-|\psi_{x}|^{2}.

The above equation gives the spectral curve of the elliptic solution and it is a Riemann surface of genus one, which we write in the form

\mathcal{X}=\left\{(z,R)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:R^{2}=(z-z_{1})(z-\overline{z_{1}})(z-z_{2})(z-\overline{z_{2}})\right\}\,,

(2.5)

where
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
are related to
e_{1},e_{2},
and
e_{3}
defined in (
2.1c
) by the relation

z_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{-e_{1}}+\frac{\text{i}}{2}\left(\sqrt{e_{3}}+\sqrt{e_{2}}\right),\hskip 10.00002ptz_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{-e_{1}}+\frac{\text{i}}{2}\left(\sqrt{e_{3}}-\sqrt{e_{2}}\right).

if
\delta<0
, while

z_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{-e_{1}}+\frac{\text{i}}{2}\left(\sqrt{e_{3}}-\sqrt{e_{2}}\right),\hskip 10.00002ptz_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{-e_{1}}+\frac{\text{i}}{2}\left(\sqrt{e_{3}}+\sqrt{e_{2}}\right)\,,

if
\delta>0
. Below we assume, for simplicity, that we are in the former case, namely
\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(z_{2})>\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(z_{1})
and
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(z_{2})>\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(z_{1}).

We also observe that

-\omega_{0}=z_{1}(z_{2}+\overline{z_{1}}+\overline{z_{2}})+z_{2}(\overline{z_{1}}+\overline{z_{2}})+\overline{z_{1}}\overline{z_{2}}.

(2.6)

From the stationary elliptic solution
u_{0}(x,t,v=0)=\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\omega_{0}t}\psi(x),
with zero velocity, the solution with velocity
v
is obtained by
using Galilean invariance, namely

u_{0}(x,t,v)\,=\,\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\omega_{0}t}\,\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\big(vx-\frac{v^{2}}{2}t\big)}\psi(x-vt),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in{\mathbb{R}}\penalty\ ,\hskip 10.00002ptt\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\penalty\ .

(2.7)

The corresponding eigenfunction takes the form

W_{0}(x,t;z)=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\text{i}}{2}(vx-\frac{v^{2}}{2}t)}\hat{\chi}(x-vt,t;z+\frac{v}{2})\,,

where
\hat{\chi}(x,t;z)
has been defined in (
2.4
).
The above equation shows that the fundamental matrix solution of the ZS spectral problem for the elliptic travelling wave with velocity
v
is obtained from the zero-velocity fundamental matrix solution by the spectral shift
z\to z+\frac{v}{2}
.
Since we develop inverse scattering for initial data that are asymptotic, as
x\to\pm\infty
, to two different elliptic quasi-periodic waves, Galilean invariance allows us to set only one of the two velocities to zero. For generality, we therefore consider initial data asymptotic to two elliptic periodic travelling waves whose velocities are not assumed to vanish.

In the next sub-section we describe the general elliptic wave
u_{0}(x,t)
with non-zero velocity and the associated fundamental matrix solution
W_{0}(x,t;z)
of the Lax pair obtained by solving a Riemann–Hilbert problem.

2.1
Genus one Riemann–Hilbert problem

Figure 3
:
The homology basis for the Riemann surface
\mathcal{X}
associated with
R^{2}=(z-z_{1})(z-\overline{z_{1}})(z-z_{2})(z-\overline{z_{2}})
.

We consider the two-sheeted, genus-one Riemann surface

\mathcal{X}=\left\{(z,R)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:R^{2}=(z-z_{1})(z-\overline{z_{1}})(z-z_{2})(z-\overline{z_{2}})\right\}\,,

(2.8)

where

z_{j}=\xi_{j}+\text{i}\eta_{j},\;\;j=1,2,\hskip 10.00002pt0<\eta_{1}<\eta_{2}.

We define

v=-\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(z_{1}+z_{2})=-(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})\,,

because this quantity is the velocity of the travelling wave.
The first sheet of
\mathcal{X}
is identified by the fact that
R>0
for
\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(z)>0
. Denote by
\infty^{+}
(
\infty^{-}
) the pre-image of
z=\infty
on the first (second) sheet of
\mathcal{X}
.

The function
\sqrt{(z-z_{1})(z-\overline{z_{1}})(z-z_{2})(z-\overline{z_{2}})}
is a multi-valued function on
{\mathbb{C}}
with oriented branch cuts
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
as in Figure
3
.
Fix a canonical homology basis on
\mathcal{X}
by choosing
\mathcal{B}
to encircle
\Sigma_{1}
anticlockwise on the first sheet, and
\mathcal{A}
to pass from the positive side of
\Sigma_{1}
to
\Sigma_{2}
on sheet 1 and from the negative side of
\Sigma_{2}
to
\Sigma_{1}
on sheet 2, see Figure
3
.
Notice that the surface
\mathcal{X}
has an anti-holomorphic involution
\sigma:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
given by

\sigma(z,R)=(\overline{z},\overline{R}),

and the canonical homology basis satisfies

\sigma(\mathcal{A})=-\mathcal{A},\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma(\mathcal{B})=\mathcal{B}.

(2.9)

Denote by
\mathrm{d}p
and
\mathrm{d}q
the quasi-momentum and quasi-energy differentials respectively. These are defined to be the unique meromorphic differentials of the second kind characterized by the following asymptotic and normalization conditions:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}p=\pm[1+\mathcal{O}(z^{-2})]\mathrm{d}z,\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{as $z\to\infty^{\pm}$},\;\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and \;\;\;$\oint\limits_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{d}p=0$}\,,

(2.10a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}q=\pm[2z+\mathcal{O}(z^{-2})]\mathrm{d}z,\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{as $z\to\infty^{\pm}$},\;\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and \;\;\;$\oint\limits_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{d}q=0$}\,.

(2.10b)

Moreover,
\mathrm{d}p
and
\mathrm{d}q
admit the explicit representations

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}p=\frac{z^{2}-\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(z_{1}+z_{2})z+c_{0}}{R}\mathrm{d}z,

(2.11a)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{d}q=2\frac{z^{3}-\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(z_{1}+z_{2})z^{2}+(\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(z_{1})\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(z_{2})+\frac{\ \mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(z_{1})^{2}+\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(z_{2})^{2}}{2})z+c_{1}}{R}\mathrm{d}z\,,

(2.11b)

with constants
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
determined by the
\mathcal{A}
-normalization

\oint_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{d}p=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\oint_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{d}q=0.\hskip 10.00002pt

In particular the first integral gives

c_{0}=-\dfrac{\oint_{\mathcal{A}}\frac{z^{2}-(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})z}{R}\mathrm{d}z}{\oint_{\mathcal{A}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{R}}=\frac{1}{2}(|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2}+|z_{1}-z_{2}||z_{1}-\overline{z_{2}}|)-\frac{1}{4}\dfrac{E(\tilde{m})}{K(\tilde{m})}(|z_{1}-z_{2}|+|z_{1}-\overline{z_{2}}|)^{2}\,,

(2.12)

where

\tilde{m}=\left(\dfrac{|z_{1}-\overline{z_{2}}|-|z_{1}-z_{2}|}{|z_{1}-\overline{z_{2}}|+|z_{1}-z_{2}|}\right)^{2}.

Introducing the Landen transformation

\displaystyle m=\dfrac{4\sqrt{\tilde{m}}}{(1+\sqrt{\tilde{m}})^{2}}=1-\left|\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{z_{1}-\overline{z_{2}}}\right|^{2}\,,

(2.13)

and

E(m)=\dfrac{2}{1+\sqrt{\tilde{m}}}(E(\tilde{m})-\frac{1-\tilde{m}}{2}K(\tilde{m})),\hskip 10.00002ptK(m)=K(\tilde{m})(1+\sqrt{\tilde{m}}),

we obtain an expression for
c_{0}
in the form

\displaystyle c_{0}=\dfrac{1}{2}(|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2})-\frac{1}{2}|z_{1}-\overline{z_{2}}|^{2}\dfrac{E(m)}{K(m)}.

(2.14)

We observe that using the Riemann bilinear relations one obtains

c_{1}=\dfrac{c_{0}}{2}\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(z_{1}+z_{2})-\frac{1}{2}(\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(z_{1})|z_{2}|^{2}+\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(z_{2})|z_{1}|^{2}),

(2.15)

so that one can rewrite
\mathrm{d}q
in the form

\mathrm{d}q=-v\mathrm{d}p+\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}R(z).

(2.16)

The quasi-momentum
\mathrm{d}p
and quasi-energy
\mathrm{d}q
differentials have
\mathcal{B}
-periods that are computable from the Riemann bilinear identities

\Omega_{1}:=\oint_{\mathcal{B}}\mathrm{d}p=4\pi|c|,\hskip 10.00002pt\Omega_{2}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\mathcal{B}}\mathrm{d}q=-4\pi v|c|\,,

(2.17)

where

c=\left(\oint_{\mathcal{A}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{R(z)}\right)^{-1}=-\dfrac{\text{i}|z_{1}-\overline{z_{2}}|}{2K(m)},\hskip 10.00002ptm=1-\left|\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{z_{1}-\overline{z_{2}}}\right|^{2}\,.

(2.18)

Note that
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
are real.

Next we fix a base point
z_{2}
on the first sheet of the Riemann surface and define the Abelian integrals

p(z)
and
q(z)
by

p(z)=\int_{z_{2}}^{z}\mathrm{d}p,\hskip 10.00002ptq(z)=\int_{z_{2}}^{z}\mathrm{d}q.

(2.19)

The expansion for
z\to\infty^{+}
gives
[
2
]

p(z)=z+E+\frac{\pi_{1}}{z}+o(z^{-1}),\hskip 10.00002ptq(z)=z^{2}+N+o(1),\hskip 10.00002ptz\to\infty^{+},

(2.20)

where

\begin{split}&E:=\lim_{z\to\infty}(p(z)-z)=\lim_{z\to\infty}\int_{z_{2}}^{z}[\mathrm{d}p(\lambda)-\mathrm{d}\lambda]-z_{2}\,,\\
&N:=\lim_{z\to\infty}q(z)-z^{2}=-vE+\lim_{z\to\infty}(R(z)-z^{2}-vz)=-vE+\dfrac{v^{2}}{4}-\dfrac{\omega_{0}}{2},\\
&\pi_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}(\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(z_{1}-z_{2}))^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|z_{1}-\overline{z_{2}}|^{2}\dfrac{E(m)}{K(m)}.\end{split}

(2.21)

Then the fact that
E
is real follows from the symmetry of the curve and the homology basis.

Now we are ready to give a more precise definition of the spectrum
\Sigma
and
\Sigma_{1}
(and
\Sigma_{2}
). Indeed we will show in Proposition
2.4
that the fundamental solution of the ZS linear spectral problem (
1.2a
) for the elliptic initial data

as in (

W_{0}(x,t;z)=O(z;x,t)\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\left((x-x_{0})(p(z)-E)+t(q(z)-N)\right)\sigma_{3}}\,,

where
O(z;x,t)
is a bounded matrix for
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{z_{1},z_{2},\overline{z_{1}},\overline{z_{2}}\}
and for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
It follows that
W_{0}(x,t;z)

is bounded for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, whenever
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z))=0
, namely

\Sigma\cup{\mathbb{R}}=\left\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\,|\,\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z))=0\right\}.

(2.22)

By construction
p(z_{2})=0
, and
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z_{1}))=0
since
p(z_{1})
is the real half
\mathcal{B}
-period of
\mathrm{d}p

\int_{\mathcal{A}}dp=0
and Schwarz symmetries of
\mathrm{d}p

imply that
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(\overline{z_{1}}))=0
,
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(\overline{z_{2}}))=0
, and
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z))=0
for any
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Thus
\Sigma
always contains the points
z_{1},z_{2},\overline{z_{1}}
,
\overline{z_{2}}
, and the real line.

Let us look more in detail at the level set
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits p(z)=0
off the real line.
Each analytic arc of the level set
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits p(z)=0
admits a
parametrization
z=z(\tau),
where
\tau\in\mathbb{R},
with
|z^{\prime}(\tau)|=1.
The function
p(z(\tau))
equals

p(z(\tau))=p(z(\tau_{0}))+\int^{\tau}_{\tau_{0}}\mathrm{d}p(z(\sigma))z^{\prime}(\sigma)\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Since
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits p(z(s))=0,
we have

z^{\prime}(\tau)=\dfrac{\overline{\mathrm{d}p(z(\tau))}}{|\mathrm{d}p(z(\tau))|}.

This immediately gives us the direction of the level line for every point
z\in\Sigma
and there is exactly one line passing through every regular point where
\mathrm{d}p(z)\neq 0
.
In addition to regular points, there are singular points, where
\mathrm{d}p(z)
is either 0 or infinite.
Let us first consider the latter case. At each branch points of the elliptic curve
p(z)
has a zero of order
1/2
. For example, near
z=z_{2}

\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z))\simeq 2\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\left(\dfrac{z_{2}^{2}-\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(z_{1}+z_{2})z_{2}+c_{0}}{\sqrt{(z_{2}-z_{1})(z_{2}-\overline{z_{1}})(z_{2}-\overline{z_{2}})}}(z-z_{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\left(a_{0}\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\varphi_{0}}(z-z_{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

for some constants
a_{0}>0
and
\varphi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.
So arcs of
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits p(z)=0
emerge from
z=z_{2}
at angles
-2\varphi_{0}+2\pi n
, with
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. It follows that only one arc emerges from
z_{2}
. The same considerations hold for all the other branch points. The other singularity of
p
is at infinity where its local behavior is given by (
2.20
). It follows that exactly two arcs of
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z))
emerge from the point at infinity, but these are precisely the two side of the real line already accounted for.

Regarding the two zeros of
\mathrm{d}p
, there are two distinct possibilities to consider: either the zeros are real or complex conjugates. Let
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
be the zeros of
\mathrm{d}p
. If they are real, then
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(\nu_{j}))=0
,

and for

\nu_{1}

\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z))\simeq\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\left(\dfrac{\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}}{2|\nu_{1}-z_{1}||\nu_{1}-z_{2}|}(z-\nu_{1})^{2}\right)=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\left(a_{0}(z-\nu_{1})^{2}\right)\,,

where
a_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. It follows that
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z))=0
along arcs that emerge from
\nu_{1}
at angles
\arg(z-\nu_{1})=\frac{n\pi}{2}
, with
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Specifically, four arcs emerge from
\nu_{1}
, two along the real axis and two which leave
\nu_{1}
orthogonal to the real line. The same consideration applies to the point
\nu_{2}
.
So with real roots of
\mathrm{d}p
, the only possibile topological configuration of the set
\Sigma
consists of two arcs
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
where
\Sigma_{1}
starts at
z_{1}
passed through
\nu_{1}
and ends at
\overline{z_{1}}
; similarly,

\Sigma_{2}
starts at

passed through
\nu_{2}
and ends at
\overline{z_{2}}
(see Figure

When the two zeros of
\mathrm{d}p

\nu_{2}=\overline{\nu_{1}}
,
it is simple to argue that these two zeros cannot belong to the level set
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z))=0

and

would be a harmonic function in a simply connected domain with zero boundary value, therefore it would be identically zero in the domain within the loop. But this is impossible. We conclude that

consists of two paths: the path
\Sigma_{1}
that connects

to

\Sigma_{2}

The following inequalities are satisfied

\begin{cases}\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z)-E)>0\,,&z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\setminus\Sigma_{1}\,,\\
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z)-E)<0\,,&z\in\mathbb{C}^{-}\setminus\Sigma_{2}\,.\\

Proof.

In order to prove the inequalities (
2.24
)
we recall that
p(z)=\int_{z_{2}}^{z}\mathrm{d}p(\lambda)
, where
\mathrm{d}p
is defined in (
2.11
) and

is real. We have also defined
\Sigma=\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}
as the level set where
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z))=0

\mathrm{d}p(\bar{z})=\overline{\mathrm{d}p(z)}
, hence the boundary values of
p(z)
on
\mathbb{R}\cup\Sigma
are real. It follows from the above that
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z))
is harmonic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{\Sigma_{1}\cup\Sigma_{2}\}

), so

|z|
large in the upper half-plane, the quantity
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z)-E)
is positive, and for
|z|
large in the lower half-plane, the quantity
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z)-E)
is negative. Set

D^{+}:={\mathbb{C}}^{+}\setminus\Sigma_{1}
and
D^{-}:={\mathbb{C}}^{-}\setminus\Sigma_{2}
. The function
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(p(z)-E)
is harmonic on each domain

and continuous up to the boundary, where it vanishes. Since

(resp.

) is connected and contains a neighborhood of infinity in the upper (resp. lower) half-plane, the maximum principle together with the sign at infinity yields (
2.24
