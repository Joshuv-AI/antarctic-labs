The association between unfinished care and risk of burnout in nursing homes and the moderating role of managerial support - a cross-sectional multicenter study
Authors: Yannai DeJonghe, Mieke Deschodt, Jeroen Trybou, Els Clays, Herlinde Wynendaele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobally, the demand for nursing home care workers is rapidly increasing due to population ageing, higher patient acuity, and growing concerns regarding workload. Prior research, predominantly focused on registered nurses, suggests these high demands and increased workloads could lead to essential care tasks being left undone and heightened risks of emotional exhaustion. This study aims to examine the association between unfinished care and risk of burnout among care workers in nursing homes and to evaluate whether managerial support moderates these associations.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional, multicenter study, using survey data retrieved from 56 Belgian nursing homes between May and December 2021. We assessed self-reported unfinished care, burnout risk, managerial support, and sociodemographic variables of 1,054 care workers (registered nurses, nursing assistants and allied health professionals). We applied two-level linear mixed models to account for data clustering. Models were built in three stages: (1) estimating the association between unfinished care subscales and burnout outcomes; (2) adding managerial support; and (3) introducing unfinished care × managerial support interaction terms to test moderation, consistent with the Job Demands–Resources framework.ResultsWe explored the associations between unfinished care and burnout, and found all domains of unfinished care to be positively associated with the risk of emotional exhaustion and depersonalization, and negatively associated with personal accomplishment. Stratified analyses showed that favorable managerial support tends to attenuate these associations with risk of depersonalization, while unfavorable support was likely to amplify the strength of these associations.ConclusionOur findings underscore the importance of minimizing unfinished care, as it is regarded as an indicator of quality, and is significantly associated with all dimensions of risk of burnout. Managerial support appears to act as a buffer in this association, with unfavorable support exacerbating the effects, particularly with regard to depersonalization. Conversely, these effects are attenuated when care workers perceive their manager as supportive, emphasizing the value of supportive leadership. Implications for practice include informing and training care managers on how to foster a more participative and supportive work environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07878-w.

Introduction

The global population is ageing rapidly, increasing the demand for long-term care services and the complexity of care [1]. According to the United Nations (2024), the proportion of people aged ≥ 65 years is projected to double by 2054, leading to an intensified demand for nursing homes (NHs) and associated workforce needs [2, 3]. Many countries face chronic shortages of long-term care workers (i.e., registered nurses, nursing assistants and allied health professionals), leading to heavy workloads and time pressure that jeopardize the delivery and quality of care [1, 3, 4].

Multiple studies have documented that when staffing and resources are inadequate, care workers leave necessary care tasks unfinished – a phenomenon variously termed as “implicit rationing of care” or “missed care” (also referred to as “omitted care”, “delayed care”, or “care left undone”) [4–11]. Implicit rationing of care is the withholding of or failure to carry out necessary nursing care tasks due to a lack of nursing resources (e.g. staffing, skill mix, time), and is regarded as the process of decision-making regarding the prioritization of care [9, 10]. Missed care refers to any aspect of required care that is omitted or delayed, which is perceived as an outcome of the beforementioned decision [10, 11]. In order to ensure inclusivity of all related concepts, experts recommend referring to this phenomenon as “unfinished nursing care” [12]. Accordingly, unfinished nursing care is a product of a time scarcity problem that triggers the process of implicit rationing through clinical priority setting among staff [8, 12]. Due to the multidisciplinary nature of our sample, which includes registered nurses (RNs), nursing assistants (NAs) and allied health professionals (AHPs) who collectively share responsibility for resident care tasks, this study will adopt the broader term “unfinished care”. Previous research has shown how antecedents of unfinished care are mainly situated on unit (e.g. staffing levels, workload, work environment) or nurse level (e.g. professional experience, absenteeism) [13]. Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic and appointed measures such as social distancing have further impacted the care worker capacity, work systems, emotional demands and aspects of social care, such as interaction with residents [14].

Although unfinished care poses risks to both safety and quality of care, prior research has revealed it is experienced commonly worldwide [5, 7, 8]. White et al. (2020) found that 72% of RNs reported leaving necessary care tasks unfinished, including resident comfort, surveillance, and care planning [5]. Unfinished care in hospitals has been associated with reduced job satisfaction, intention-to-leave and burnout [6]. Burnout is a work-related stress syndrome defined by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and diminished personal accomplishment, stemming from prolonged exposure to job stress [15]. Important risk factors for job stress among nurses include time pressure resulting from high workloads and staff shortage, disruptions during the workday, lack of control, and perceived role conflicts arising from having to prioritize care due to insufficient resources. The combination of high stress and time pressure has been shown to lead to poorer quality of care, as essential care tasks are left unfinished [16, 17]. Prior research has estimated that 30% of RNs and 43% of NAs in NHs experience high levels of emotional exhaustion, and RNs at high risk of emotional exhaustion were five times more likely to report unfinished care [5, 18]. Beyond unfinished care and poorer care quality, burnout has also been associated with poorer health and well-being, absenteeism and turnover intentions, which may further increase the workload and time pressure, exacerbating the demanding effects of job stress [5, 17, 19, 20].

A commonly used conceptual framework to help understand the development of burnout, is the Job Demands–Resources (JD–R) theory. Two underlying processes explain how job demands and resources might affect care worker well-being, namely a health-impairment and a motivational process [21, 22]. Job demands are defined as aspects of work requiring sustained physical or mental effort, associated with certain physiological and psychological costs, and can be predictors of exhaustion [21–23]. A key job demand for care workers is work overload, and similar to unfinished care, work overload is driven by high workloads, time pressure and inadequate staffing [13, 24].Within the NH-context, unfinished care can be identified as a job demand for care workers, actively contributing to exhaustion through sustained effort in high-pressure conditions. Job resources are predictors of work engagement, that help achieve work goals, reduce job demands and their associated costs, and stimulate personal growth and development [21–23]. Within the JD–R theory, managerial support is considered a key job resource that can buffer the negative effects of job demands by helping care workers cope with their work challenges [21–26]. The role of leadership and its association with employee well-being has been highlighted as an important avenue for further research within this framework [21]. Managerial support encompasses social and emotional dimensions, including listening, recognition, and individual consideration, as well as instrumental and organizational dimensions such as ensuring adequate resources, providing information, feedback and guidance, and fostering professional development opportunities [21, 24]. Prior research in hospitals has recognized favorable support for its beneficial effect on nurse well-being and retention. Nursing managers play a critical role in fostering a positive work environment for care workers, by providing supportive leadership, sufficient job resources, and promoting care worker well-being and professional development [20, 26–32].

Despite growing recognition of unfinished care and burnout in NHs, unfinished care as an antecedent, and its impact, along with the moderating role of managerial support in this association, remain underexplored in this setting. The relational and long-term nature of resident care, and the particular vulnerability of the resident population (e.g. complex care needs, continuous and personalized care) are distinct from a hospital-setting. In NHs, care is delivered by a multidisciplinary workforce. However, majority of published research on unfinished care and burnout has focused on hospital-based RNs [6, 7, 24]. NAs and AHPs constitute a large segment of the NH workforce, yet remain largely invisible in literature. Under the supervision of RNs, NAs provide assistance, support and almost all direct personal care to residents (e.g. supporting activities of daily living), and make up for the largest proportion of the NH workforce [33]. Excluding AHPs from research overlooks a group that provides care services such as rehabilitation therapy, fall risk assessment and prevention, swallowing management and providing emotional support to families in long term care settings [34]. For this reason, we want to build on prior research and investigate the associations between all domains of unfinished care and all dimensions of risk of burnout in all care workers in a NH-setting, aimed at improving workforce well-being and retention, and quality of care.

We hypothesized that higher levels of each domain of unfinished care would be associated with a higher risk of burnout. Subsequently, we will assess the effect of managerial support on the risk of burnout. We hypothesized that more managerial support would be associated with a lower risk of burnout. Finally, we explore whether managerial support moderates the association between each domain of unfinished care and burnout risk dimensions in NHs. We hypothesized that managerial support would buffer the above-mentioned associations. An overview of measures and formulated hypotheses utilized for this study is provided in Fig. 1.

Methods

Design, setting and sample

We conducted a cross-sectional, multicenter study, including a stratified sample of 56 Belgian NHs. This paper followed the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies (see Additional file 1) [35]. In Belgium, responsibility for residential care policy has been devolved from the federal level to the regional governments in Flanders, Wallonia, and Brussels. Additionally, the sector encompasses a mixed ownership landscape of public, private non-profit, and private for-profit providers. To pursue a diverse sample, NHs were stratified based on geographic region (Flanders, Wallonia, Brussels), ownership structure (public, private not-for-profit, private for-profit) and facility size (small < 80 residents, medium 80–120 residents, large > 120 residents). Following these criteria, a stratified random sample of 250 NHs was selected for recruitment, with an additional 112 NHs contacted due to an initially low response, culminating in a total of 362 NHs contacted.

Recruitment of care staff was organized in collaboration with a designated contact person in each participating NH. After initial email invitations were sent to selected facilities, those interested were invited to a digital information session where the research team explained the study aims, data collection process, and ethical safeguards, including informed consent, voluntary participation, and data protection. When a facility agreed to participate, a staff member was appointed as the main liaison. This individual, under the research team’s guidance, was responsible for distributing study information and invitations to eligible care staff. At the care worker level, sampling followed a convenience approach. All direct-care staff in participating NHs were invited, provided they met eligibility requirements. The sample included RNs, NAs and AHPs, while excluding individuals on extended leave (> 1 month), those newly employed (< 1 month), temporary workers, and students. No formal sample size calculation was conducted a priori, as the study had an exploratory design and relied on voluntary participation of NHs and their care staff. Post-hoc power analysis revealed f²=0.02, indicating that the study was adequately powered to detect small effects.

Data collection

Participants received an information packet before completing the survey, containing details on the study’s purpose, data privacy measures, and voluntary nature of participation. Data were gathered through a structured survey tool developed for this study (see Additional file 2). Questionnaires, including an enclosed informed consent, were distributed to the eligible care workers by the NH main liaison. Data collection in each NH spanned a four-week period, with reminder prompts issued during weeks two and three. Surveys were administered online via Qualtrics from May 3 to December 24, 2021. All data were processed anonymously, with analyses conducted solely at the NH level to prevent participant identification. The study protocol was approved by the Ghent University Hospital Medical Ethics Committee (approval number: BC-09667).

Measures

Main outcome: risk of burnout

Risk of burnout was assessed using the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), a widely validated instrument for measuring occupational burnout in healthcare settings [15]. The MBI measures three dimensions: emotional exhaustion (nine items), depersonalization (five items) and personal accomplishment (eight items). Participants responded using a seven-point frequency scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 6 (“every day”), indicating how often they experienced each stated feeling or attitude. Subscale scores were summed and categorized using established cut-offs: emotional exhaustion low (< 19), moderate [19–26] and high (> 26); depersonalization low (< 6), moderate [6–9] and high (> 9); personal accomplishment reversed so that scores 0–33 indicated high risk (low accomplishment), 34–39 moderate and > 39 low risk (high accomplishment) [15]. Internal consistency of the subscales was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients: emotional exhaustion (EE, our α = 0.90; original α = 0.89), depersonalization (DP, our α = 0.65; original α = 0.77), and reduced personal accomplishment (PA, our α = 0.79; original α = 0.74) [15, 36].

Main predictor: unfinished care

Unfinished care was assessed using the Basel Extent of Rationing of Nursing Care, adapted for a NH-setting (BERNA-NH) [37]. This instrument comprises 19 items, to which we added additional care tasks (e.g. bringing residents food when they are hungry in between meals), and conversations regarding advanced resident care planning and end-of-life care. These items were added in order to provide a better fit with the everyday care practice in Belgian NHs. Respondents indicated how often they were unable to complete each task on a six‑point scale (not required, never, rarely, sometimes, often, not within my range of tasks). A mean was calculated per subscale to aid interpretation. Subsequently, to identify the underlying structure of the adapted 22-item BERNCA-NH instrument used in our study, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis on complete cases using principal-axis factoring with listwise deletion. Given the theoretical assumption that the latent dimensions of the instrument are conceptually related, an oblique Direct Oblimin rotation was applied. This approach allows the factors to correlate, yielding a more accurate estimation of the underlying factor structure. We retained factors with eigenvalues ≥ 1 (Kaiser’s criterion), a choice confirmed by scree-plot inspection, which accounted for 59.6% of the cumulative common variance. Loadings below 0.30 were suppressed, leaving 18 items with clear primary loadings that formed four subscales. Four items did not meet the 0.30 threshold and were excluded, resulting in an 18-item BERNCA-NH “short form”. These items described specific nursing tasks (e.g. administering medication, changing bandages) which are performed by RNs or NAs, and not by AHP. Additionally, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated in order to evaluate internal consistency of the subscales, and reported along with their corresponding sample sizes for this reliability analysis: Activities of Daily Living (ADL; CC n = 992; 5 items, our α = .80; original range α = .77 – .78), Caring, Rehabilitation and Monitoring (CRM; CC n = 1028; 6 items, our α = .88; original range α = .83 – .87), Documentation (DOC; CC n = 991; 5 items, our α = .90; original range α = .81 – .84) and Social Care (SOC; CC n = 801; 2 items, our α = .87; original range α = .84 – .89) [37]. Answer options range from ‘never’ to ‘often’ on a 4-point Likert scale, where a higher score indicates more unfinished care. Answering options ‘did not occur’ and ‘not within my responsibility’ were included in the survey but excluded from the analysis, as these responses reflect the absence of an opportunity to perform care or the role-based inapplicability of a task, rather than the frequency with which care was left unfinished.

Moderator: manager ability, leadership and support

Managerial support was measured using the Manager Ability, Leadership and Support subscale of the Practice Environment Scale of the Nursing Work Index [38]. This subscale asks respondents to rate their agreement with four statements (i.e., the person in charge of the ward supports the care team, the person in charge of the ward is a good manager and supervisor, there is praise and recognition for work well done, and the person in charge of the ward supports the care workers in making decisions, even when it concerns a disagreement with another care profession) on a 4-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree), with higher scores reflecting more favorable perceptions of managerial support. In our sample, this subscale demonstrated good internal consistency (our Cronbach’s α = 0.85, original Cronbach’s α = 0.84).

Covariates: care worker characteristics

Comparable to previous NH research, sociodemographic variables such as sex (female, male), age (years), seniority (years in current profession) and profession (RN, NA, AHP) were considered as control variables. Profession was included as a covariate because RNs, NAs, and AHPs differ systematically in their care responsibilities, workload characteristics and risk of burnout in a NH-setting. Since majority of the NH care workers are female, sex was also included as a control variable. Similar to profession, associations with risk of burnout have also been found for seniority [39]. Profession and sex were treated as categorical variables with RNs and female as the reference groups, respectively. Preliminary analyses indicated that the variable ‘seniority’ showed a stronger association with the main outcome variable than ‘age’, and was therefore included as a covariate instead of ‘age’.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29, and missing data were excluded from analysis through listwise deletion across all models. A multilevel approach was employed to evaluate factors associated with burnout among NH care staff, accounting for the clustering of individual care workers within NHs. This nested design considered each care worker as level 1 within their respective NH at level 2, controlling for possible within-cluster correlations.

Descriptive statistics

We summarized continuous variables with means and standard deviations, more specifically burnout subscales (emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, personal accomplishment), unfinished care subscales (activities of daily living, caring, rehabilitation and monitoring, documentation, social care), managerial support, age, and career seniority. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, including sex, region, ownership, facility size and profession. For group comparisons, we used one-way ANOVA to compare RNs, NAs and AHPs on continuous outcomes (applying Tukey Honestly Significant Difference when the omnibus F was significant). For categorical burnout risk, differences between professions were tested with chi-square tests. In addition, we reported numbers and percentages missing for each variable overall and by profession. Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF), with values > 5 considered indicative of problematic multicollinearity.

Multilevel linear mixed models

We modelled burnout as a two-level linear mixed process (care workers nested within NHs) using SPSS GENLINMIXED. Level 1 comprised individual care workers, and level 2 represented NHs. All models used a random intercept for NH, maximum-likelihood estimation, and complete cases. Each unfinished care subscale (activities of daily living, caring, rehabilitation and monitoring, documentation, social care; 1–4) was evaluated in a separate model as the primary predictor. Separate models were chosen deliberately to examine the total association between each unfinished care domain and burnout risk, consistent with our research question. As a result, sample sizes across models may vary. Covariates were sex (female=reference; male), profession (RN=reference; NA; AHP), and seniority (years). Continuous predictors were mean-centered prior to analysis. Model assumptions (normality and homoscedasticity) were checked with residual Q–Q and residual-versus-fitted plots.

Analyses proceeded in three steps for each risk of burnout outcome (i.e., emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, personal accomplishment). Step 1 estimated the main effect of unfinished care on burnout while adjusting only for covariates (sex, profession, seniority). Step 2 added managerial support as a confounder control, estimating the association between unfinished care and burnout adjusted for managerial support, since support is plausibly related to both unfinished care and burnout. Step 3 tested moderation by adding the unfinished care × managerial support interaction, evaluating the JD-R buffering hypothesis that resources weaken the adverse effect of demands. When the interaction was significant, we explored the effect of unfinished care at different levels of managerial support. For visualization, support was dichotomized at the median, and predicted burnout scores were plotted separately for low- and high-support groups. These plots can be found in (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Because four subscales were tested per outcome, inferences for the subscale main effect (and, where applicable, its interaction with support) used a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold α = 0.0125. Results are reported as unstandardized coefficients (β), 95% CIs, and p-values. To compare steps 1–3 we present Akaike Information Criterion corrected (AICc; lower indicates better fit) and marginal R² (variance explained by fixed effects). Finally, conditional R² and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) are shown to characterize clustering and overall fit.

Results

Descriptive statistics

In total, we included survey data of 1,054 care workers from 56 NHs, including NAs 52.8% (n = 557), RNs 26.9% (n = 283) and AHPs 20.3% (n = 214). Since 362 NHs were contacted, this corresponds to a response rate of 15.5% on NH-level. NHs were mainly located in Flanders (65.3%), followed by Wallonia (29.1%) and Brussels (5.6%). Public ownership was most common (51.7%), then private not-for-profit (30.9%) and private for-profit (17.4%). In addition, large NHs (> 120 residents) accounted for 42.5% of sites. Based on available data of eligible participants in 50 NHs, we achieved a response rate of 43.3% on care worker level.

In our sample, the risk of burnout varied across professional groups. Significant differences across professions were observed and post hoc Tukey tests showed that AHPs had lower emotional exhaustion than RNs and NAs, lower depersonalization than RNs and NAs, and higher personal accomplishment than RNs and NAs. Furthermore, between-group differences in unfinished care were small. Overall, care tasks associated with social care were most frequently left unfinished, followed by care tasks regarding caring, rehabilitation and monitoring. Social care did not differ across professions, whereas caring, rehabilitation and monitoring did: RNs reported more omissions than AHPs; other pairwise contrasts were not significant. Care tasks relating to activities of daily living were least frequently left unfinished, which also differed across professions, driven by NAs reporting more omissions than AHPs, while other contrasts were not significant. For documentation, the omnibus test reached significance but no pairwise Tukey contrast met p<.05. Additionally, managerial support did not differ by profession. Associations between unfinished care and risk of burnout

All continuous predictors were mean-centered prior to analysis, such that the intercept in each model represents the predicted burnout score for a care worker scoring at the sample mean on all continuous predictors. The regression coefficients represent the expected change in burnout score associated with a one-unit increase above the sample mean on the predictor.

First, analyses of emotional exhaustion (Table 2) showed that higher levels of all unfinished care domains were consistently and significantly linked with higher emotional exhaustion. On the activities of daily living subscale, for instance, each one-point increase in unfinished care corresponded to a 6.71-point higher emotional exhaustion score (95% CI [5.77 to 7.65], p<.0125). To contextualize the practical magnitude of this effect, we note that a score between 19 and 26 corresponds with moderate risk, and a score higher than 26 corresponds with high risk of emotional exhaustion. When managerial support was added to the models, the associations with unfinished care became smaller but remained significant, while support itself showed a clear protective effect. No evidence was found for interaction effects, as fit indices did not improve from Step 2 to Step 3.

Finally, across all outcomes, assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity were assessed. Although we noted mild tail deviations, Linear Mixed Models have been shown to remain robust to such violations, particularly when supported by moderate to large sample sizes [40]. Intraclass correlations showed modest clustering by NH (EE 0.04–0.10; DP 0.03–0.06; PA 0.02–0.03). In summary, the results showed that higher levels of each unfinished care domain were consistently associated with more unfavorable outcomes, while higher levels of managerial support were linked to more favorable outcomes in terms of all dimensions of burnout risk. For emotional exhaustion and personal accomplishment, both predictors had independent main effects without evidence of interaction, whereas for depersonalization, several significant interactions indicated that the association between three unfinished care domains and depersonalization was weaker at higher levels of managerial support. More specifically, this was the case for: (1) activities of daily living; (2) caring, rehabilitation and monitoring, and (3) documentation.

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to examine the association between unfinished care, conceptualized as a job demand, and the risk of burnout among care workers. In contrast to much of the existing literature, this study was conducted in a NH-setting, included all three burnout dimensions, and involved a broad range of care workers (RNs, NAs, and AHPs).

Our findings suggest that, under conditions of limited staffing or time pressure, care workers tend to prioritize activities of daily living while psychosocial care and monitoring are most frequently left unfinished, jeopardizing resident quality of care. This pattern aligns with previous NH research identifying emotional support, social care, and surveillance as the most commonly missed care activities [5, 41–44]. Additionally, the frequency of unfinished social care might have been further exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic and social distancing measures [14]. A motivational perspective offered by Phan et al. (2023) applied to the NH-context, suggests that care workers under chronic time pressure as a consequence of structural resource constraints, may also omit certain care tasks as an adaptive self-regulatory response to perceived insufficient progress velocity [45]. Such omissions compromise resident safety and quality of care by hindering early detection of changes in residents’ health status. The omission of emotional support, conversations, and social activities may negatively affect residents’ psychosocial well-being, quality of life, and long-term relationships with care workers [41, 46]. The inability to complete essential care tasks often leads to moral distress, role conflict, and role strain among care workers, due to discrepancies between care ideals and actual care delivery. Education and training can support care workers in applying the process of rationing of care in an informed and autonomous manner, which contributes to their professional identity and self-esteem, and might even reduce the experienced moral stress [8, 47].

In our study, burnout risk differed significantly by profession, with RNs and NAs reporting consistently higher levels of burnout across all dimensions compared to AHPs. As RNs and NAs also reported slightly higher levels of unfinished care across several domains, the higher prevalence of burnout among RNs and NAs is likely to be a result of these higher job demands [23]. Moreover, higher levels of all unfinished care domains were consistently associated with a higher risk of burnout across all dimensions in our sample, supporting our study hypothesis and aligning with the JD-R theory, which conceptualizes burnout as an imbalance between job demands and job resources, where high demands contribute to exhaustion [20–23].

Our analyses also showed that higher levels of managerial support were associated with lower levels of burnout risk, highlighting the importance of supportive leadership for care worker well-being in our sample. Additionally, managerial support significantly moderated the association between several types of unfinished care and depersonalization, with weaker associations observed among care workers reporting higher managerial support, confirming our hypothesis regarding its role as a key job resource in our study [21–24]. These findings are consistent with prior hospital-based studies observing moderating effects of perceived managerial and organizational support on the associations between work overload and the risk of burnout, depression, and job satisfaction [25, 48]. However, this study found no significant moderating effects for emotional exhaustion or reduced personal accomplishment. As emotionally exhausted care workers feel drained by efforts made at work, often as a result of high job demands, more structural and organizational solutions may be required, such as adequate work distribution, staffing and resources [49, 50]. It can result in avoidant and even dysfunctional coping, which can be categorized as self-undermining behavior. As a result of this behavior, additional job demands can be generated and further intensify job strain [22]. Reduced personal accomplishment, often defined as the inefficacy dimension, results in low morale, and poorer coping skills [22, 50, 51]. This particular dimension of burnout might be better addressed by personal resources, such as self-efficacy, resilience and optimism, which can operate in a similar way as job resources, by moderating the impact of job demands on care worker well-being [22]. Both of these dimensions contain an intrapersonal aspect, as opposed to depersonalization, which is defined as the interpersonal component of burnout, a detachment resulting in loss of idealism, negative behavior and interpersonal avoidance towards their work environment [50]. Care workers experiencing depersonalization might rather benefit from job resources, such as a supportive manager, which could contribute to more work engagement and might have a stronger mitigating effect on the avoidant behavior exhibited as a result of depersonalization [21, 50]. At the team level, nursing managers can function as a key job resource themselves; by actively helping care workers take on hindrance job demands (e.g. workload). Additionally, they can offer job resources, such as supervisor support, feedback and autonomy [21, 22, 24]. On an individual level, job crafting can cultivate job and personal resources, work engagement, and encompasses task revision and role innovation. It can even be extended to the team level through collaborative discussion and implementation of proactive changes, aimed at reducing shared demands and improving team performance [21, 22].

This study underscores the need for adequate staffing and sufficient job resources, such as managerial support, to match the high job demands characteristic of NH settings and facilitate the provision of high-quality care for residents. Appropriate workload distribution, staffing and job resources are essential to reducing unfinished care and workload-related stress, thereby supporting both care quality and care worker well-being [5, 52]. Addressing structural challenges in recruitment and retention is therefore critical to maintain a healthy workforce able to provide quality of care, with improved working conditions in supportive environments, career development opportunities, and sustainable career pathways representing promising strategies to attract and retain qualified care workers [3, 53].

Limitations

Due to its cross-sectional design, our study was limited to exploring associations rather than causal relationships and precludes conclusions about causality and directionality in the relationship between unfinished care and burnout among NH care workers. While we hypothesized that unfinished care contributes to burnout, it is equally plausible that burned-out care workers perceive more care as left unfinished, suggesting reverse causation. Longitudinal or intervention studies are needed to assess the directionality and potential reciprocal relationships between unfinished care and burnout, and to evaluate interventions aimed at strengthening managerial support, and examine the broader impact of working conditions on care worker well-being. Therefore, results should be interpreted with caution.

Although the response rate at the care worker level was relatively high (43.3%), facility-level participation was limited. Nursing homes were recruited using a stratified sampling strategy based on region, size, and ownership type. However, only 56 of 362 contacted NHs (15.5%) agreed to participate during the four-month recruitment period. The COVID-19 pandemic was identified as the main barrier, as increased absenteeism and workload made participation unfeasible for many NHs. Within our sample, we found that 97.8% of care workers were working in NHs that did not report any active COVID-19 cases at the time of the survey, which might positively influence their decision to participate in the study. In addition, the NHs that did report active COVID-19 infections, established an average of 3.3 cases at the time of the survey. None of the NHs had implemented protocols regarding cohorting at the time. To assess organizational-level non-response bias, participating and non-participating NHs were compared on stratification variables (region, facility type, and size) using chi-square tests. The analyses indicated no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of region or size. However, some caution in generalizing findings to all Belgian NHs is warranted as we did observe a slight underrepresentation of private NHs in our sample.

Additionally, care workers within participating nursing homes were recruited by convenience, introducing a further potential source of selection bias at the individual level. It is possible that care workers who chose to participate differ systematically from non-participants in terms of burnout risk or awareness of unfinished care, and findings should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution. As all data were self-reported, common method bias and social desirability effects might have been introduced. However, this risk is partly mitigated by the use of validated instruments and by guaranteeing respondent anonymity to limit social desirability bias [54]. Several potentially important confounders (e.g. resident acuity, staffing ratios, and organizational culture) were not available and may influence both unfinished care and burnout. These factors should be incorporated in future research to reduce residual confounding. In addition, linear mixed models assumed linear relationships, although non-linear or threshold effects may exist whereby unfinished care affects burnout only beyond certain levels [55].

In order to provide a better fit with the everyday care practice in Belgian NHs, the BERNCA-NH instrument was slightly adjusted for this study which might limit direct comparability with other studies using the original instrument. At the individual level, item-level missingness was low to moderate for most variables (e.g. managerial support (6.6%), seniority (5.8%), and most unfinished care subscales (2.5-6.0%), and higher for social care (24.0%). Elevated missingness in unfinished care items is highly attributable to the exclusion of ‘did not occur’ and ‘not within my responsibility’ responses, which were treated as missing as they reflect the absence of an opportunity to perform care or role-based task inapplicability rather than the frequency of unfinished care. Missing data analysis showed a profession-specific variation in missing data patterns, particularly for social care (RNs: 24.4%, NAs: 29.3%, AHPs: 9.8%), supporting the possibility of role-specific attribution. The subscale social care includes items regarding planned individual and group activities, which are more frequently performed by AHPs compared to RNs and NAs in Belgian NHs. Additionally, it was more likely for these planned activities not to occur, due to social distancing measures during the COVID-19 pandemic. Statistical analysis of complete cases might introduce selection bias, which might impact the representativeness of the sample and generalizability of these findings. Although the proportion of missing values was modest (< 10%) for most variables, different approaches such as multiple imputation could be explored.

Conclusions

The aim of our study was to explore the associations between unfinished care, managerial support, and burnout, offering insights into how NH management can protect their staff and maintain high quality care. The findings highlight the importance of minimizing unfinished care, which serves as both a care quality indicator and a significant predictor of burnout risk. Since burnout is associated with job dissatisfaction, poorer health, absenteeism, turnover, and reduced care quality, mitigating this risk is crucial for the well-being and retention of the NH workforce. Adequate staffing levels and targeted interventions to limit unfinished care may contribute to reduced emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment among NH care workers. Importantly, managerial support demonstrated a moderating effect on the association between unfinished care and depersonalization, underscoring the value of nursing leadership.
