Leading Career and Programmatic Development: Short-term Implementation, Planning, and Organization While Pursuing a Long-term Growth and Culture Mindset
Authors: Peter M Waters, Kishore Mulpuri, Brian Scannell, Steven L Frick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn essential function of a surgical leader performing at the highest level is the recruitment, development, and retention of talented, innovative professionals. Building programs person by person and then melding them into growing teams of outstanding people with similar values but different skills will achieve the leadership’s vision and mission. It is imperative that these individuals not only match but also improve the organizational culture. The best leaders: (1) select early-career or recruit mid-career professionals who fulfill both existing and growth-strategy programmatic needs with specific knowledge and technical skills; (2) intentionally help them develop higher-level professional skills through guided mentorship, sponsorship, and coaching; (3) provide the resources necessary for them to succeed; (4) build systems and programs that enhance performance excellence, enabling surgeons at all stages of their careers to do sustainable, impactful, important work; (5) expect, monitor, and develop professionalism in decision-making and behavior within a trusting culture; and (6) see and organize for what is coming, not just what is here now.Key Concepts(1)Surgeon leaders need to develop tools to assess and recruit talented surgeons.(2)Providing pathways for the professional development of surgeons that align with group values, vision, and mission is important.(3)Building a professional work environment founded on mutual trust and teamwork, with the leader coaching and mentoring surgeons, creates the optimal environment for surgeons to make meaningful contributions to individual patients, organizations, and our profession.Keywords: Surgical leadership, Career development, Program development

Hiring your new partner

Selection-recruitment

Recruiting and developing talented professionals is essential to elite, sustainable performance. In surgery, high technical skill is imperative. Therefore, surgical leaders must have surgeons in their group or department who can execute an indicated surgical plan effectively to achieve the best possible outcome for each patient. The complexity of a surgical case must be matched to the individual surgeon’s technical ability. Surgery is truly an endeavor for professionals, not amateurs. Excellence is built, case by case, with programmatic scalability over time through detailed planning, operational implementation, and hiring. The bedrock foundation of a surgical career and program must be technical excellence, with expertise and experience that provide care for the most complex patients.

However, success in surgery goes well beyond the individual surgeon’s “hands.” An in-depth knowledge of (1) historical treatments and outcomes; (2) evidence-based results for all treatment options, including natural history and non-operative choices; and (3) precise, data-driven expectations for each operative procedure is required. Leaders and organizations need highly educated surgeons who can discern what is best for each individual patient at each moment in time. Unindicated surgery, even when done well, must be guarded against, while even indicated surgery, when performed well but when the patient is not physically or emotionally prepared, risks a suboptimal outcome. Dr. John Feagin’s adage [1], “do the right thing at the right time, for the right reason,” matters.

In addition, gaps in information or therapeutic intervention options exist and must be well understood. In-depth knowledge enables systematic innovation through safe, closely monitored experimentation that can lead to better future solutions. Leaders not only need to recruit and retain smart, talented surgeons but also ideally find and develop professionals with curiosity and a growth mindset. Dedicated lifelong learning is expected in the best healthcare organizations.

Of course, not every surgeon will have every element required for greatness. By definition, average surgeons predominate statistically, but “average overall” can describe someone who is very talented and provides good patient care, especially when practicing within the scope of their knowledge and skills. Over a career, ideally, each surgeon will mature, expand their capabilities and knowledge, and diversify their abilities to deliver impactful care for large cohorts of patients. The motto “to improve every day” (or “do your best today, and better tomorrow”) is a real mindset and a necessary commitment that leaders must expect from their individual surgeons.

However, even the best of the best can never succeed alone. Teams of professionals are needed to provide simple, let alone complex, care. You start with “one,” soon move to dyads, then triads, and quickly well beyond. In the business of 24/7, 365-day-a-year healthcare delivery, the number of talented, smart people necessary to provide competent surgical care as needed is astonishing. Within the group or department, leaders must build their team(s) not only with additional surgeons but also with dedicated allied professionals who want to make a real contribution to achieving desired short- and long-term goals. Discriminatory selection is essential (see Brian Scannell, MD, insert). Never underestimate the importance of finding the right people, building the best team(s), and enabling them to complete not only specified tasks but also to accomplish the long-term organizational vision and mission (see Kishore Mulpuri, MD insert.)

Outside your group or department, other medical and surgical leaders need to do the same. Whether it is nursing, anesthesiology, emergency medicine, radiology, or any other affiliated subspecialty professional care team, our patients’ outcomes depend on their unique abilities and cooperation. We need those leaders to recruit and develop smart, talented people who will do their specific jobs well. Professional skill and knowledge matter across the spectrum. Communicating and facilitating noncompetitive programmatic development across subspecialty boundaries effectively improves care, morale, and culture.

Safe, high-quality, high-value care delivery requires “getting along” behaviors. None of us can do it alone, and we all do it better when we do it together. It is wise to consider the African proverb, “If you want to go fast … go alone; if you want to go far … go together.” There is no doubt that collaboration improves productivity. Emotional intelligence (self-regulation, self-awareness, social skills, empathy, and motivation) [2] and nontechnical skills (situational awareness, task management, leadership, teamwork, and decision making) [3,4] in individuals and teams are paramount to building a high-functioning, highly reliable organization. Prioritizing professionals with these skills and behaviors during selection and fostering them during career development mitigates error risk and enhances safe, high-quality outcomes. Professionals with high emotional intelligence reduce the risk of disruptive professional interactions that can impede performance [5]. Equally important, these professional behaviors, on a moment-to-moment basis, determine your culture. An organization’s culture determines success more than its strategic plan. Culture is the “secret sauce” that builds engagement and resilience. Outstanding leaders set and maintain culture by selecting, recruiting, developing, and retaining the best people with multifaceted skills. The leader must establish standards and hold team members accountable for performance, behavior, and collaborative work. Then, together, they enact a well-planned strategy.

Writing about career and program development is easier than achieving individual surgeon, surgical team, or organizational excellence. Making it seamless is even more challenging. Some failure is to be expected, but rapid improvement and change afterward are mandatory. Surgeons and their multidisciplinary teams will not always get it right. A learning mindset is essential when the unexpected or undesired occurs. Lessening the negative impact of any kind of error is important. Adaptability and resilience among leaders and teams are crucial.

Lastly, on choosing and empowering the best teams of professionals, Jim Collins, in his Good to Great [6] work, emphasized that successful leaders not only have to hire and position the “right people on the bus,” but also need to get the “wrong people off the bus.” In healthcare or other professional endeavors, it is easier and better to avoid hiring and empowering disruptive, problematic professionals than to try to rehabilitate or even terminate them. It is not easy, however, to distinguish the narcissistic or personally charismatic surgeon(s) in an interview process from the altruistic, “other-driven” professionals you want in your group or organization. Narcissists are very smart, talented, and skilled at deception-they care only about themselves and engage well with people only when it helps them achieve their individual goals around power, fame, and fortune. Leaders and their teams must dedicate sufficient time and resources and use systematic methods to reduce the risk of hiring the wrong person. It is absolutely critical to carefully interview references, and, if possible, past peers and subordinates. Narcissistic people are experts at kissing up and kicking down. Pay attention to how they treat secretaries, staff, and others they may encounter. The true measure of a person is how they treat those who would likely not have an impact on their trajectory. Equally important and challenging, if an inappropriate selection has occurred, leaders need the courage, resilience, and patience to manage or resolve persistently uncivil and unprofessional behaviors.

Career development

Surgeon leadership style and effectiveness

A conscientious, visionary leader must focus on implementing organizational strategy today and into the future. An idealized servant leader does so by developing and supporting others [7]. With servant leaders, generational skill development and collaboration, including leadership succession planning at all levels of the organization, are embedded in the fabric of the organization. A learning and growth mindset predominates. Active listening, community-building, and empathetic skills are necessary. An authoritative (autocratic/commanding) leader [8] is more hierarchical and less collaborative but can make more rapid decisions and implement plans. There is limited debate, and a power differential from the top down exists. Autocratic leaders rely more on control, while servant leaders use collaboration to drive performance. Historically, surgical leaders were more autocratic, both in the operating room and across the organization. Some still believe this is best. More recently, servant leadership has been valued in healthcare to mitigate risk inside and outside the operating room. The concern with authoritative leadership is that communication is limited and psychological safety suffers among the team. Eventually, there will be resistance to orders that working professionals do not understand or agree with, as well as dissatisfaction and possible burnout from a lack of autonomy or not being involved in the decision-making.

Servant leaders engage their working professionals more and make them an essential part of the team in both task management and decision-making. The theory is that this improves morale and investment in the process and outcome. In addition to autocratic and servant leadership styles, Goleman [9] and others [10] describe democratic/participative, coaching, affiliative, charismatic, and pacesetting leadership styles. Each surgeon should know their own natural tendencies, particularly under pressure, and strive to mature over time to use a range of styles. Ideally, surgeons with a high level of situational awareness and expansive nontechnical skills can adapt their leadership behavior and apply the most appropriate style in the moment, employing situational leadership [10]. Overall, the goal is best performance with the least risk of error. Daniel Pink [11] has stressed that people desire mastery, purpose, and autonomy in their work and life. These traits are optimized when the leader invests in both the personal and career development of their people, step by step, with clear short- and long-term plans, expected goals, and regular monitoring and feedback to assess challenges and outcomes. Doing so requires vision, commitment, adaptability, humility, and even courage.

The money

Surgical leaders and executives need assets and resources at their disposal to develop careers and programs. Ultimately, as a subspecialty, pediatric orthopaedics needs to achieve excellence in clinical care, research, and education. Resources for nonrevenue-generating work are more limited in healthcare now. The focus of executive team members on return-on-investment (ROI) and return-on-assets (ROA) metrics is more prevalent. Surgical leaders responsible for career and programmatic development must be skilled in financial analysis and negotiation to garner and use their resources effectively. Each academic medical center and healthcare delivery system is unique, but many principles are similar. Leaders need to build sustainable, trusting relationships with executives, board members, and other administrative leaders that are reliable during important transactions about programmatic development. Understanding and then maximizing your system is imperative to build and sustain programmatic excellence. Without efficient, focused systems, processes, and executive support, practicing surgeons risk running out of time and energy and burning out. It is the surgical leader’s job to facilitate high performance in clinical care, research, and education by providing needed resources, maximizing efficiencies, and understanding and caring about each surgeon and their team. This requires an investment of time and attention to detail.

Hiring with a purpose

Too often, especially today with sprawling healthcare megaplexes, administrators pressure surgical leaders to “fill the spots” and meet the expected “budgeted numbers” for each new satellite and care center. An administrator’s performance and even bonus may depend on meeting or exceeding quarterly and annual budgets. Administrators can be codependent on surgical output and the resulting revenue. Many administrators are unfamiliar with the saying that “all things that can be measured may not always matter, and the things that matter may not always be measurable.” Surgeon leaders must strive to understand the importance of numbers while partnering with administrators and helping them understand the qualitative effects of their initiatives.

Unfortunately, managerial leaders may assume that surgeons are interchangeable and that any board-certified surgeon can manage each patient’s problems. This is especially likely with an administrative change-up, the “administrator-de jour” with an agenda will not recognize those surgeons who have led change and been administrative partners for prior decades. It can be injurious to an institution when stalwarts of the community are sent packing in favor of a new surgeon chosen by an administrator who can control them.

If surgical staffing and care delivery were only so simple. Indeed, there is routine clinical care (simple fractures, traumatic sprains and strains, benign conditions with known natural history, and treatment options) that any skilled, knowledgeable surgeon or advanced practice provider (APP) can safely deliver, either adjacent to or independently of one another. More refined and complex care requires triage-referral systems that channel patients to the providers best suited to assess, manage, and deliver high-quality care for that particular child and their family. A particular challenge in systems dependent on APPs to deliver high volume “routine” clinical care is having APPs knowledgeable about what they do not know (beware Dunning–Kruger effect of those with less knowledge lacking self-awareness and overestimating their skills and knowledge) to identify the serious problem disguised as a routine parental concern (for instance a child with a developmental hip dislocation presenting with in-toeing, or a boy presenting with flat feet who has Duchenne muscular dystrophy).

A commonly expressed adage is for healthcare delivery professionals to practice at the “top of their license.” Too often, this is simplified to paying lower salaries (surgeons are expensive) for supposedly the same quality of care. More completely, it means (1) highly skilled clinicians focus on complex care they are uniquely qualified for; (2) tasks are properly delegated to the appropriate professional level; (3) efficiency and access improve; and (4) professional satisfaction improves. Preventing those providing care from feeling either overwhelmed by the volume or complexity of care or bored by a lack of interesting, challenging cases is imperative. Yet it is equally important that the surgeon serves the needs of the APPs who provide outpatient care, the APPs serve the nurses and clinic staff who educate and help the patients, and the nurses serve the medical assistants who are often the very first face of a patient’s experience. While licensure brings differences in skills and priorities … a sense of service must permeate the team and begin at the very highest licensures.

Providing APPs and surgeons with a stable practice environment punctuated by exciting challenges is preferred. Not providing them with growth opportunities stagnates the development of expertise and breeds mediocrity. Surgeons want to apply the knowledge and skills developed during training, so young surgeons need opportunities for case volume and complexity. Having experienced surgeons mentor them provides cross-generational expertise that brings balance, stability, and wisdom. Ideally, surgical leaders with vision, commitment, and understanding are present at the outset and throughout the planning processes for capacity utilization and budgeting. Ideally, the strategic goals of the enterprise, department/group, and individual healthcare provider are not discordant and have optimally overlapping Venn diagrams. Recruiting a surgeon who balances all these elements has the best opportunity for longevity and growth for all stakeholders.

The surgical leader’s goal is to understand from the outset of hiring what is necessary for a successful practice and career. It is important to fill the buildings with patients and operating room cases, provide 24/7 emergency coverage for trauma and consultation as needed, and generate revenue for your group and organization. The organization needs work to be done, and you need to hire surgeons who thrive on that work. However, each new hire is trained for a purpose that they hope to fulfill. For example, a surgeon with dual fellowships in pediatric orthopaedics and sports medicine aspires to care for young athletes. Their goals should align with the evolving strategic needs of the enterprise. This person would be an ideal hire if your group is now responsible for more high school team-care coverage than you can handle, if without this hire, there will be a problem with your other surgeons wanting to focus more on spine or neuromuscular care, or if they feel less skilled at newer arthroscopic reconstructive techniques than someone fresh out of fellowship. This type of matching matters. Otherwise, interprofessional sabotage or rapid turnover occurs. Just be certain that you not only match technical and knowledge skills but also nontechnical skills and culture. A smart, skilled, and driven but disruptive surgeon is not a good hire.

From a program director’s perspective, orthopaedic resident selection, recruitment, advancement, and career development constitute a continuous, mission-driven process to identify, nurture, and graduate technically excellent, ethically grounded, and adaptable surgeons (see Fig. 1).

Building the next generation of orthopaedic surgeons

Brian Scannell, MD

The process begins with medical-student selection. Over the past few years, the evaluation of medical student applicants has changed significantly. United States Medical Licensensing Exam (USMLE) step 1 and many medical schools have transitioned to pass-fail. As a result, programs have moved toward a more holistic review process. While academic metrics remain important, such as USMLE step 2 scores, clerkship performance, and research productivity, increasing emphasis is placed on attributes predictive of long-term success: applicant experiences, resilience, teamwork, integrity, communication skills, and commitment to orthopaedics. Structured interviews, standardized letters of evaluation, and behavioral questioning help assess these qualities. Diversity in background and experience is also prioritized because it strengthens team performance and improves patient care. Ultimately, it is important to recruit those who align with institutional culture and values.

Advancement within residency is guided by competency-based progression aligned with the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) Milestones and the American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery (ABOS) Knowledge, Skills, and Behavior (KSB). Residents are evaluated longitudinally across domains including patient care, medical knowledge, professionalism, systems-based practice, and surgical skill. The addition of the ABOS KSB aligns with the ACGME Milestones but adds greater granularity, case-specific assessment, and real-time feedback mechanisms. Although we remain time-based for advancement, the Milestones and KSB have allowed us to advance residents more clearly based on competency, increasing autonomy and accountability. The Clinical Competency Committee (CCC) is an important part of the advancement process. The CCC can help identify residents performing at a high level and allow for increased independence and leadership responsibility. The CCC can also help identify struggling residents early in training and develop individualized improvement plans that incorporate targeted mentoring, skills labs, and academic support.

Residency development should be an intentional, longitudinal process. Early in training, residents benefit from exposure to the breadth of orthopaedic subspecialties, enabling informed career decisions. Faculty mentorship (formal and informal) is critical, with mentors guiding scholarly activity, including research and professional identity formation. Teaching opportunities should be progressively structured. Senior residents should be given deliberate roles in educating junior residents and medical students, leading conferences, and taking more advanced roles in research, care delivery, and education in the operating room and office settings with junior residents. Providing feedback on their teaching effectiveness helps refine this skill set and reinforces the identity transition from learner to educator.

As residents progress, career preparation becomes more individualized. Fellowship guidance includes application strategy, interview preparation, and networking within subspecialties. Preparation for entering practice is vital and includes education in contract negotiation, practice models, coding, and healthcare economics. Leadership development cannot be overlooked given the demands residents face over 5 years. A formalized leadership curriculum is critical for senior resident development and can have a direct impact on future patient care and safety. It can also help prepare residents for future roles within healthcare systems.

When a resident may be a good fit for hiring at their home institution, intentional retention strategies matter. If the goal is to recruit these individuals as faculty, fostering a supportive, inclusive, and growth-oriented culture throughout residency is paramount. Residents are more likely to return to environments where they feel valued, challenged, and aligned with the mission. Orthopaedic residency training is a deliberate continuum, recruiting residents who align with program values, advancing them through competency-based assessment, and equipping them with the technical expertise, judgment, and professional tools necessary for successful and fulfilling careers.

Once they are on your team

Compensation and benefits

Every hire needs to know what you plan to pay them. They may or may not have a spouse or family whom you both need to consider now and in the future. They most likely have someone advising them, such as a parent, friend, a respected colleague who trained them, or even legal counsel to review their work contract. Reliable transparency with financial numbers builds trust. Often, previous hires inform them, hopefully indicating that you and your organization are trustworthy in negotiation and delivery.

Each staff member needs and deserves fair market value for anticipated work. It is important to meet basic financial needs in your geographic area: the cost of a nice but not excessively luxurious home; access to outstanding education for their children in a specific town (potential higher taxes and real estate costs), with public or, if local public education is wanting, possibly expensive private schools; childcare, especially if both parents work; healthcare, disability, and even death benefits in case of a catastrophe that could dramatically impair their and their families’ well-being; access to a community that matches their needs; and the ability to pay for reasonably priced vacations and routine recreational activities that renew their energy and sustain, restore, and grow their personal relationships. None of these should be viewed as discretionary.

As a physician leader, you cannot dictate how they spend their earnings, but it is imperative that they can pay off debts and save for future needs. Their first paycheck should mark the first day they set aside money for retirement. “Compounded interest is the 8th wonder of the world” (James Waters, personal communication). You do not want senior surgeons working solely because they cannot afford to retire. The week their first child is born is when they should start setting aside money for their children’s lifetime education costs. If they have college and medical school loans of their own, ideally you have proper professional advisers and methods of indirect compensation, or you include these as part of the hiring contract to assist with repayment. The more you can do to lessen their financial burden and stress, the more focused and clear their nonfinancial decision-making can be at work. To be clear, luxury is on them. Basic high-level needs of a well-trained, skilled professional should be part of hiring negotiations and the norm for everyone in your group.

Clearly, a financial and professional growth strategy must be in place. As professionals advance, care for more complex patients, conduct higher-level peer-reviewed research, and take on more leadership roles, they will expect and deserve higher compensation and support for their increased production. Explicit expectations and regular, transparent assessments are essential. At a minimum, formal annual meetings should occur, complemented by frequent informal conversations in the office (open-door policy), hallways, clinic areas, operating room lounges, parking lots, and other common sites that build connectivity and trust. The better a leader knows their people, the more effectively they can plan and facilitate careers. The more surgeons trust their leader, the more openly they will share their challenges and goals. Limiting bias is important. Clear, detailed worksheets and criteria are useful (see Table 1). Timely, scheduled adjustments (ideally upward) to compensation and resources should respond to productivity, market forces, and needs, often enacted at the end of each fiscal or academic year.

Professional resources

It is important to make clear that this is not just about “the money.” You do not want a culture that pursues only fame and fortune. You want a trusting, reliable culture of excellence. The primary mission for all is the best patient care, and in academic centers, also education and research excellence. Better care for more children, every day. You want professionals with a relentless drive to improve. You need to challenge them to challenge themselves and their teams. When they fail while courageously striving for excellence, you need systems and people in place to take care of them, as well as their patients. In the clinical arena, this means peer support, professional support with coaching and counseling, as well as legal and indemnity experts if there is an adverse event. In the research space, it means patience, continued funding, and mentoring by established experts. The mission enablers for high-quality, safe care for children are human resources, technology, clinical capacity that allows for overflow and growth, as well as legal, financial, marketing, and managerial support, among others. Professionals who strive to work together and support one another will make improvements iteratively and innovatively, resulting in safe, high-quality care.

The essentials

Surgeons need patients, and children with surgical problems need surgeons. Most surgeons these days seek specific areas of expertise (spine deformity, hip dysplasia, orthopaedic oncology, congenital hand conditions, clubfeet, as examples). Getting the right patient to a skilled surgeon in an efficient, timely fashion is key. The Institute of Medicine’s 2001 [12] STEEP mandate: Safe, Timely, Equitable, Effective, and Patient-centered, still applies 25 years later.

So what are the essentials any organization needs to provide effective surgical care? Easy, equitable access to outpatient assessments and management; operating rooms and professional teams to provide timely, safe surgical care; an inventory of readily available, reliable tools for surgery; professionals in all settings to provide safe, timely, effective care; technology and people to aid, not burden, medical record keeping, billing, collections, and practice management; organized cooperation with easy access across subspecialty disciplines (eg radiology, emergency medicine, and orthopaedics for trauma; anesthesia, surgery, and operating room nursing for surgical cases) to work collaboratively for the best patient outcomes; and matching the patient’s problem to the proper surgical care team (eg a patient with a hip deformity needing a complex hip osteotomy to the surgeon skilled in performing it well with the lowest risk of complications). All of this must occur in a respectful work environment without disruptive, unprofessional behaviors.

In the research arena, pilot funding is mandatory. For basic science, this may be a million-dollar-a-year investment until sufficient extramural funding is secured. This may take 5–10 years to stabilize, with interim bridge funding necessary. Choosing the right scientist and project is essential, especially when funding is scarce. Clinical research is less costly but still expensive. Building sustainable support through philanthropy, organizational grants, extramural funding, and internal departmental, hospital, and medical school funding is achievable. Employing, training, and supervising highly motivated postgraduates interested in a medical career for 1–3 years while they transition to medical school is productive [13]. Reducing the burden of institutional review board (IRB) filing, data collection and analysis, statistical evaluation, and draft-manuscript writing enables a busy surgeon to have more time to ideate, reflect on the meaning of the data collected, and write a clear manuscript that will have impact. Rewarding productivity enhances further productivity. Artificial intelligence is already facilitating the timely completion of clinical research tasks.

Time is such an important component that organizations and leaders need to “give it back”; and surgeons and their teams need to know how to manage it. Beyond the precise ticking of the clock, which is real and must be dealt with, there is the element of energy oscillation that warps our sense of time. Energy-draining, mind-numbing activities seem to go on forever. Energy-replenishing, heartwarming endeavors fly by. Sharing the burden of professional work; making it joyful through collaboration, connection, and celebration; and regularly reflecting on how meaningful our work is for our patients, society, and ourselves are uplifting and resilience-building. Designing systems and hiring people to “give back time” for surgeons, rather than overloading them with almost insurmountable, endless tasks, is critical to well-being. Facilitating high performance that enables surgeons and their teams to “get in the zone” and “flow,” making hours seem like minutes, is reenergizing and enables best care.

Expectations

Most often, young surgical recruits or mid-career job transfers are concerned they will not be treated fairly, or may even be abused. Similarly, the leader and organization doing the hiring can be concerned whether this person will work out as envisioned: will they be as technically skilled, knowledgeable, and professional as they appear in interviews and through conversations with their current supervisors? These are the main concerns beyond boilerplate contracts. A contract signed by both parties with detailed written sections that explicitly describe roles, work hours, compensation, benefits, and reasons for termination, in compliance with organizational policy and state laws, is commonplace. In addition, a “contract” as a series of trusting conversations that continue long after the hire can be more important. The written contract provides the guardrails and is invoked if conflict arises or expectations change. Ideally, it “collects dust” in a real or computer file. The trust [14] between the leader and new hire is what allows for ongoing negotiated changes and productive professional relationships as a practice and career matures.

The leader expects the new hire to be professional and to perform at the top of Miller’s pyramid [15] (Fig. 2), which evolves from Knows > Knows How > Shows > Does, with professional knowledge, skills, and attitude. The goal is to progress from a novice in residency, fellowship, and early career to an expert in practice. This maturation places the new hire at the top of Cruess’ modified Miller’s pyramid -“Is” (Fig. 3), where their identity is that of a professional surgeon, and they consistently demonstrates the attitudes, values, and behaviors of one who has come to “think, act, and feel like a physician” [16].

Miller’s pyramid of progressive clinical competence-based progression from knowing to knowing how to showing to ultimately doing. Progressive competence is based on the knowledge, skills, and attitude of the professional as they move from novice to expert.

Cruess’ modification of Miller’s pyramid to include ultimately being a professional in terms of self-identity as the highest level of competence.

Professionalism translates into moment-to-moment decisions and behaviors, requiring self-regulation, empathy, drive, team-based communication, and self- and situational awareness. Incidents of failure do occur because we are all human and work in a profession full of stress and challenges. Hopefully, these episodes are minor, not repetitive, and acknowledged with grace and forgiveness by all parties. Having safety nets of intervention, including coaching, counseling, performance improvement, leave of absence for rehabilitation, and even termination if warranted, when more serious or repetitive incidents occur is imperative for the well-being of the individual, the organization, and the public.

One of the most important professional expectations is the ability to say “I don’t know,” and then seek further knowledge through conversation, listening, reading, consultations, and even proper patient referrals as needed. Smart, talented surgeons must be humble. Otherwise, unnecessary errors or near misses can occur. Leaders need to expect and even demand professional behavior.

Monitoring and advancement

There is an important need for an array of quantitative and qualitative information to provide feedback and monitor the performance of surgeons. Some elements of performance can be counted and compared among surgeons and their teams, such as patient visits, operative cases, dictated notes, block utilization, OR on-time starts, complications, severity of case mix, on-call days, away time, relative value units (RVUs), billing and collections, podium presentations, and peer-reviewed publications. Each leader and organization has key performance indicators (KPIs) they follow daily, weekly, monthly, and yearly. These should be transparent to all members of the organization, with some available to the public on a website. Leaders, managers, and executives have a responsibility to provide all that is necessary to achieve the expected outcomes. If support or resources are lacking, changes need to be made to the expected KPI’s.

Regularly collected quantitative and qualitative information should be the norm and is important for leaders who want to develop programs and build successful careers for their team. Other sources of feedback are more subjective and rely on softer metrics, such as patient satisfaction (ie Press Ganey) scores, as well as active observation and listening from peers, patients, and colleagues. It is important to recognize the potential for bias among all assessors. Three-hundred-sixty evaluations, medical peer review, morbidity and mortality (M&M) conferences, and complex case review processes can reduce, but not eliminate the risk of bias by providing broader, confidential, and open feedback.

All surgeons and professional staff must maintain their hospital and surgery center privileges, state licenses, and national board certification through continuous medical education (CME) documentation and periodic testing. Monitoring compliance is essential to avoid delays that affect the continuum of patient care. Support and time off for CME courses are necessary. Setting clear guidelines and, if needed, rehabilitating noncompliant or failing surgeons is an important process that should not be avoided. As the ABOS emphasizes, learning and maintaining knowledge, skills, and professionalism are mandatory.

Leading programmatic development with vision at University of British Columbia Department of Orthopaedic Surgery

Kishore Mulpuri, MD

The quote “culture eats strategy for breakfast” is often attributed to Peter Drucker [17], the founder of modern management, but it likely reflects ideas from several thought leaders. Regardless of attribution, in my leadership experience, it is eminently true. While strategic thinking is important, the most successful departments are built on cultures of genuine support and celebration of each other’s success. Leaders build strong programs by supporting individuals, and individuals thrive when they feel part of something larger than themselves. This perspective also aligns closely with my personal philosophy of Ikigai, the idea of finding meaning and fulfillment at the intersection of what we love, what we are good at, what the world needs, and what we can contribute through our work.

During my tenure as Univeristy of British Columbia (UBC) Orthopaedics Department Head, the most critical lesson I have learned is the importance of recruiting people who align with the department’s vision and values rather than simply filling a clinical, academic, or leadership need. Cultivating a culture of empowerment fosters strong individual engagement and genuine investment in others’ success. Acknowledging not only accomplishments but also mentorship, teamwork, and acts of kindness builds a sense of belonging and shared pride within the department.

My leadership style prioritizes action over position. I believe in inspiring others to become leaders in their own right, free from hierarchical boundaries. At its core, leadership should feel approachable and human; meaningful collaboration happens when people feel comfortable speaking openly and sharing ideas. In medicine, we often underestimate the importance of kindness, flexibility, psychological safety, and trust within teams. To paraphrase Dr. Steve Frick, respect and trust are earned drop by drop but lost in buckets. Teams that support one another through personal and professional challenges ultimately become more resilient and collaborative.

Fostering trust and engagement requires mentorship and recognition that different individuals excel under different mentorship styles. Reflecting the principles of Ikigai, true leadership is rooted in helping people discover their talents, values, and sense of purpose.

In academic medicine, leadership requires investing in people and culture in ways that outlast any individual’s tenure. Consequently, faculty recruitment should transcend immediate departmental gaps and instead focus on where the field, institution, and population needs may be in future years.

At UBC Orthopaedics, this has meant intentionally building expertise in areas such as clinical research, global orthopaedics, quality improvement, education, and innovation, while ensuring that patient care remains at the center of everything we do. Diverse perspectives and expertise strengthen departments and foster innovation.

Finally, equally important, leadership requires a vision that people can believe in. At UBC Orthopaedics, we believe we are “stronger together,” creating “local-to-global” impact. A shared purpose aligns individuals across divisions, hospitals, and academic interests and reminds us that our ultimate goals are to improve lives through patient care, education, research, and advocacy.

The greatest privilege of leadership is not personal achievement but seeing colleagues, trainees, and programs grow and succeed together over time, ultimately improving care locally and globally (see Fig. 4).

Conclusion

Everyone needs to always be looking down the road to what’s next while performing at their highest professional level today. Being comfortable (not overly anxious) but not complacent (having a growth mindset) in the job enhances solutions and builds excellence. With leadership’s mentorship and sponsorship, a surgeon’s “ceiling” keeps rising with new opportunities and challenges internally and externally. As one moves higher and broader in professional scope and expertise, it becomes imperative that leadership look to hire others to be mentored by and to supplement the skills of the now more advanced surgeon. Frequently, there is resistance to hiring by the established members of the department-practice-group because of worries about competition for resources, compensation, and even fame. However, a “grow-the-pie” mentality is best. Also, an organization should never be dependent on one or only a few individuals for care delivery in a particular field. Ultimately, professional collaboration, rather than unhealthy competition, across generations makes everyone better, healthier, and safer. An integrated, layered, balanced team of professionals with diverse, overlapping, and redundant skills builds sustainable and scalable excellence (see Fig. 5).
