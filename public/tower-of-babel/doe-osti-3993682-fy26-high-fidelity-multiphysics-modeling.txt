 SUMMARY
 This report summarizes various efforts of the National Reactor
 Innovation Center (NRIC) Virtual Test Bed (VTB) team made in the fiscal
 year 2026 (FY26) to update, extend, and improve previous analyses of
 the NRIC Demonstration of Microreactor Experiments (DOME) facility.
 The DOME facility will host a variety of microreactor tests and offers a
 shielding system to shield the greater facility from heat and radiation. In
 late 2025, the design of this shielding system was finalized, which
 required updates to prior models that were based on the preconceptual
 shield design. New shield geometry specifications and meshes were
 created and applied to both reactor physics and thermal models, with a
 meshing workflow that supports arbitrary reactor geometries. A
 prototypical gas-cooled microreactor (GCMR) design was used in various
 analyses, including a thermal analysis, a time-dependent activation
 analysis of materials in the shield following shutdown of the microreactor,
 and an alternative approach to calculate dose rates. Additional efforts
 included support for the DOME digital twin, including development of a
 reactor supplemental shielding Functional Mockup Unit (FMU) and
 leverage of a reactor model from the VTB to generate transient data to
 support a reactor FMU. We emphasize that the benefit of this work is not
 the specific calculation results presented here, but rather the
 development of high-fidelity workflows that can be adapted for real
 microreactor experiments within DOME. Results from actual experiments
 will vary from those presented here.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 This report documents computational models developed in fiscal year 2026 (FY26) of the
National Reactor Innovation Center (NRIC) Demonstration of Microreactor Experiments (DOME)
facility. The DOME facility is slated to host a variety of microreactor tests and offers a built-in
shield design consisting primarily of concrete and water structures that shield the greater facility
from heat and radiation. While the exact reactor types slotted for testing in DOME are
proprietary, generic reactor cores meeting flux and power requirements have been simulated in
the shield to analyze the behavior of the combined system. The focus in FY26 was to use the
latest shield specifications to update and extend multiphysics models of the shield developed in
prior years. Using a prototypical gas-cooled microreactor (GCMR) design, we have computed
temperatures in the shield, computed time-dependent activation of materials in the shield
following shutdown of the microreactor, and developed an approach to calculate dose rates.
Finally, we have adapted our high-fidelity shield models to interface with parallel efforts to
develop a digital twin of DOME. We emphasize that the benefit of this work is not the calculation
results contained within this paper which are for a generic microreactor, but rather the
development of high-fidelity workflows that can be adapted for real microreactor experiments
within DOME. Results from actual experiments will vary from those presented here.

 2. UPDATED SHIELD DESIGN
 In work prior to FY26, the preconceptual design for NRIC’s DOME shield [1] was modeled
using the high fidelity coupled multiphysics application Cardinal [2]. A two-step methodology
was developed to better modularize the shield design specifications from the reactor core
design specifications, since various reactor experiments will be placed into DOME at different
points in time.
 During FY26, the DOME shield design was finalized, and the DOME facility was constructed
and made available for reactor experiments [3]. To align with the as-built facility, we have
updated our multiphysics model to use the final design specifications as closely as possible. We
then performed updated calculations to ensure that the previously described two-step
methodology is still valid and operational for this updated design.

 2.1 Summary of Shield Design Changes
 The initial preconceptual design for NRIC’s DOME shield was 27 ft wide, 39 ft long, and 21 ft
high, while the updated design is 29 ft wide, 47 ft long, and 23 ft high. Figure 1 shows the Y-Z
and X-Z planes of the preconceptual design model. Yellow regions are water blocks; blue,
green, and gray regions are concrete blocks; and pink regions are penetration holes for the
pipes at the top of the structure and red is the door structure of the previous shield design.

 2.2 Updated Thermal Model and Multiphysics Analysis
 A thermal analysis of the DOME shield has been performed in previous work [2,5,6] using
the preliminary shield design. In the present work, we update the thermal analysis to use the
final shield design and make modeling and workflow improvements. The most notable
difference between this iteration and previous models is in the usage of MOOSE’s ray tracing
module to calculate view factors of the surfaces within the shield cavity, as opposed to using an
overly approximate gap heat transfer formulation. Previous work demonstrated the sensitivity of
the results to this improvement [2], but in this work, the reactor mesh was loaded into the cavity
volume, and the cavity was meshed around it, rather than directly meshing the whole system.
This approach makes the workflow reactor independent so that arbitrary reactor meshes can be
loaded with minimal input from the user.
 The following assumptions were made regarding the boundary conditions and source terms
in the thermal model:
• A 17-ft ground layer, assumed to be composed of normal-weight concrete, is added beneath
 the floor of the shield.

• Convective boundary conditions are applied to all system boundaries with a reference
 temperature of 300 K (roughly room temperature) and heat transfer coefficients of 10 W/(m2-
 K) and 100 W/(m2-K) for the interfaces with ambient air and the bottom ground surface,
 respectively.
• The water tank is treated as a solid region with the actual thermal properties of water. To
 approximate the advection of heat through the water tank outlet boundary, a uniform,
 volumetric heat source 𝑄 = 𝑚̇𝑐! (𝑇in − 𝑇) is applied to the water volume 𝑉water , where 𝑚̇, 𝑐! ,
 𝑇in , and 𝑇 correspond to the water tank mass flow rate (assumed to be 1 kg/s), water
 specific heat capacity, water inlet temperature, and the water temperature at the current
 position, respectively. This inherently assumes that the water is well mixed. To more
 accurately model this phenomenon, a computational fluid dynamics study of the water tank
 is necessary, which is left as possible future work.
• The net radiation method is used to perform radiative heat transfer between the reactor and
 inner shield surface. This setup creates numerous surface patches of the participating
 surfaces and then uses MOOSE’s ray tracing module to compute the view factors between
 each surface patch. The reactor surface is assumed to have an emissivity of 0.52, and the
 inner surface of the shield is assumed to have an emissivity of 0.76.
 In the section that follows, we describe the coupling of this thermal model to the radiation
transport calculation (performed in OpenMC) and show some sample results.

2.2.1 Multiphysics Coupling
 While multiphysics modeling was not a focus of the present work, we demonstrated that the
updated shield design and thermal models are compatible with the multiphysics coupling
workflows that were performed in [2,5].
 As in prior work, the multiphysics application Cardinal, which features both MOOSE and
OpenMC, was used to perform the coupling. Unlike finite element applications like MOOSE,
OpenMC uses constructive solid geometry to represent the computational domain as a
combination of simple shapes. “Cells” are defined, which may denote different material regions
or zones where certain tallies are desired. In contrast, MOOSE requires a mesh composed of
elements that represent relatively fine divisions of larger regions. Cardinal produces a mapping
between these OpenMC cells and MOOSE elements by determining in which cell an element’s
centroid lies. Thus, it is crucial that the geometrical representations are consistent. With this
mapping, quantities can be transferred between the two applications using MOOSE’s MultiApps
and Transfer systems. For each solve, whether this be a steady-state solve or a single time step
solve during a transient, the OpenMC neutronics model is solved, transfers occur, and then the
MOOSE thermal model is solved.
 Coupling the thermal analysis to radiation transport allows for more accurate heat sources in
the system and, if two-way coupling is performed, allows modeling the temperature feedback on
neutron interaction cross sections. Like previous work on the DOME shield, the coupling
demonstrated in the current DOME shield model uses a one-way transfer of power density from
OpenMC to MOOSE. Neglecting temperature feedback on the reactor cross sections is valid, as
we do not need to analyze reactivity feedback effects but instead derive the approximate flux
distribution exiting a prototypical critical reactor at fixed power. It also simplifies the analysis
considerably, since the MOOSE thermal model does not need to have a consistent geometrical
representation of the tested reactor core. In this model, only the regions outside of the cavity
produce and transfer the power density.

 In prior work using the preconceptual shield design, the reactor heat load was set to 50 kW ,
despite the heat removal capability of the shield being rated at 100 kW [1,2,5]. In this work, the
reactor power is taken to be 100 kW, corresponding to the heat removal design limit of the final
shield design for a conservative maximum temperature prediction. Note that some of the heat
source appears directly in the shield, which is accounted for and transferred by OpenMC, but
the majority of the reactor power is deposited directly in the reactor volume, so we make a
conservative thermal assumption by not subtracting this amount from the 100 kW imposed in
the thermal model’s reactor volume.
 Figure 6 shows the coupled steady-state thermal solution in X-Y and Y-Z planes. A
maximum temperature of 239.8°C (512.95 K) occurs on the surface of the floor directly below
the reactor. MPR Associates reported a maximum temperature of 254°C for the Reactor
Anchoring Platform (RAP) directly beneath the reactor in their “Uninsulated” test case, which
most closely matches the geometry/material assumptions made in this work. The total heat
source within the shield is 73.8 W, which is only 0.0738% of the total reactor power; the rest is
deposited directly within the reactor and removed by an assumed primary heat removal system.
The shield heat removal system removes 87.6 kW, while 11.8 kW is transferred to the DOME
interior via convection along the exterior shield surfaces, and the remaining 0.6 kW exits the
system through the exterior sub-surfaces of the domain.

 3. ACTIVATION ANALYSIS
 Neutrons that leak from a reactor can transmute stable nuclides in the surrounding materials
into radioactive products. These products continue to decay after the reactor is shut down and
can therefore produce residual activity and a radioactive decay field even when the prompt
neutron and photon fields are no longer present. An activation analysis is needed to quantify
this radioactive inventory and its spatial distribution under a representative operating history.
Such an analysis will complement the prompt dose and energy-deposition calculations
previously performed for an analysis of the DOME shield.
 Characterizing activation is important for several reasons. The magnitude, location, and
persistence of the activated inventory can affect personnel access following shutdown,
inspection and maintenance planning, and eventual decommissioning and waste disposal. It
also provides the source term needed for subsequent photon transport calculations to assess
the dose rate from decay photons.

 3.1 Methodology
 To carry out an activation analysis of the DOME shield, our calculation methodology
combines two distinct decompositions that should not be confused. First, the DOME Enhanced
Step 1 (ES1) method separates the computationally expensive full-reactor calculation from
subsequent calculations on the shield. Second, within the shield calculation, OpenMC’s
Rigorous Two-Step (R2S) method separates neutron irradiation and material activation from
transport of the photons emitted by the resulting radionuclides. The DOME ES1 method is
described in the FY2024 milestone report [5], while the OpenMC R2S method was originally
developed and validated for a shutdown dose-rate analysis of fusion systems [8]. The newly
developed openmc.deplete.R2SManager class automates the R2S sequence used in this
work.

3.1.1 DOME ES1 Workflow
 In the first step of the ES1 workflow, an eigenvalue calculation is performed with the GCMR
core, cavity, and surrounding shield. The GCMR design [9] was taken from the NRIC Virtual
Test Bed (VTB) site [10] and adapted for OpenMC. Unlike the generic two-step approach [5],
retaining the shield in this calculation allows the flux solution in the reactor region to account for
neutron and photon backscatter from the shield. A surface source is recorded on the six faces
that define the interface between the cavity and the shield. Only particles crossing outward from
the cavity are banked, including their particle type, position, direction, energy, and other state
information. If a particle leaves the cavity and then returns to it after scattering in the shield and
subsequently crosses outward again (leaving the cavity a second time), the later crossing is
recorded as another source-bank entry. The accompanying outward-current tally therefore
accounts for all passages from the cavity into the shield, including repeated passages by the
same physical history.
 In the second step of the ES1 workflow, the GCMR and cavity contents are removed and a
fixed-source calculation is performed on the shield model alone using the bank produced in the
first step. Vacuum boundary conditions are imposed both on the exterior boundary of the model
and on the six faces of the inner cavity. The vacuum condition at the inner boundary is required
by the ES1 formulation; because repeated entries into the shield were already recorded
separately during the full-system calculation, allowing a history to leave and then re-enter the
shield again during the fixed-source calculation would double count part of its transport.

3.1.2 R2S Activation Workflow
 The R2SManager class in OpenMC is utilized for the ES1 shield-only fixed-source model
using a mesh-based R2S approach. It carries out the following sequence:
1. OpenMC estimates the volume of each material occupying each element of a superimposed
 Cartesian mesh. Every nonempty mesh-element/material pair is treated as a separate
 activation region so that different materials remain distinct even when they share a mesh
 element.
2. A fixed-source neutron transport calculation determines the flux and microscopic reaction
 rates for transmutation reactions, such as (n,γ), (n,2n), and (n,p), for each activation region.
 The incident source is the surface-source bank from the first step of ES1, so these
 quantities reflect the neutron flux produced in the shield by the GCMR operating at the
 specified power.
3. For every activation region, OpenMC creates a copy of the corresponding material with its
 estimated volume and solves the transmutation and decay equations over the prescribed
 irradiation schedule. This produces the time-dependent nuclide inventory and activity while
 preserving both spatial and material resolution.
 While the R2SManager class can also create the decay photon source corresponding to the
activated materials and execute a photon transport calculation to determine shutdown dose
rates, we studied only the activated material inventories in the present analysis.

 3.2 Results and Analysis
 The activation analysis of the DOME shield was performed in presence of the GCMR at the
operating power of 5MWth for six months. This higher power level was chosen based on
consultation with NRIC DOME shield analysts to represent a conservative case with large flux
bombardment and subsequent activation. We note that a reactor at this power level would
require an accompanying primary system to remove most of its heat, since the DOME shield is
only rated for 100 kW of heat deposition. We assume that the reactor does not have an
additional shield and thus that all flux leaking from the reactor reaches the DOME shield
structures.
 As mentioned in Section 3.1, the ES1 approach was used for preliminary calculations.
According to this approach, the entire system (shield and GCMR) is modeled in “step 1” to
generate a surface source at the outer surfaces of the cavity hosting the microreactor. This
surface source is then normalized to the reactor power and provided in “step 2” for a fixed-
source calculation where only the shield is modeled. This approach doesn’t allow an activation
analysis to be performed for the air inside the shield cavity because “vacuum” boundary
conditions are set at the outer surfaces of this cavity (see Section 3.1 for details). To perform an
activation analysis including the air inside the cavity, the previously developed Enhanced Step 2
(ES2) approach that uses a full system model in the fixed-source “step 2” is envisaged but not
performed here.
 Figure 7 and Figure 8 show the neutron flux distribution in the shield materials with the
GCMR operating at 5 MW. These figures show that most of the neutrons are stopped by the
shield components immediately surrounding the shield cavity, i.e., the water tanks, the inner
concrete door (“door 2”), the inner floor slab (“floor 3”), and the inner ceiling slab (“ceiling 1”). It
can be observed that only a minor fraction of neutrons reaches the walls and the exterior ceiling,
door, floor, and ground materials. While the reactor type and power used in this workflow

distribution in the shield has the same symmetry. From these considerations, it is therefore
expected that the activation results be about the same in the shield components that are
symmetric with respect to the X=0 axis, i.e., “water tank 1” and “water tank 3,” “wall 1” and “wall
3,” and “ceiling 1” and “ceiling 3.” From Table 1 and Table 2, it is observed that the activity
results in the water tanks confirm the discussed symmetry. The values in “wall 1” and “wall 3”
are not exactly the same, but they remain comparable even though a noticeable difference is
observed in the ceiling components. This is due to the statistics of the simulation combined with
the very low fluxes obtained in these regions. The use of more sophisticated simulation options
(such as using variance reduction techniques like “weight-windows”) is envisaged in future work.
 As expected, the concrete structures at the interface with the shield cavity (i.e., “floor 3,”
“door 2,” and “ceiling 1”) show the largest activity. Despite most of the neutrons being stopped
by the water tanks, non-negligible activity values are obtained in the concrete walls beyond the
water, especially when compared with the results obtained in the same water tank. Water
experiences significantly smaller neutron activation compared to concrete due to the presence
of radionuclides with low activity and/or very short life and/or small concentration. From Table 3,
it is observed that at the time of reactor shutdown, the major radioisotope in water is N-16,
whose specific activity is ~9.886E+10 Ci/g (~3.658E+21 Bq/g) but whose half-life is only 7.23
seconds. Shortly after shutdown (1 hour), the major radionuclide in water is C-14, whose
specific activity of 6.22E-12 Ci/g (~0.23 Bq/g) is significantly smaller than that of Mn-56
(2.21E+7 Ci/g, ~8.17E+17 Bq/g), Na-24 (~8.70E+3 Ci/g, ~3.22E+14 Bq/g), and Fe-55
(~2.43E+3, ~8.98E+13 Bq/g), which are the leading radioisotopes in the concrete structures.
However, the calculation modeled pristine water (only H and O). Because elemental impurities
may introduce additional radioisotope production pathways, C-14 cannot necessarily be
assumed to be dominant without further calculations. Looking at the results at different times
after shutdown, a noticeable decrease in the activity values is observed for the concrete
structures (of about two orders of magnitude for standard-density concrete and one order of
magnitude for the high-density concrete). This is due to the small half-life of the leading
radioisotopes (~2.6 hours for Mn-56, ~2 minutes for Al-28, and ~15 hours for Na-24). The
activity in the water tanks decreases significantly immediately after shutdown due to the very
short half-life of N-16 and then remains stable up to 1 year after shutdown.

 4. DOSE RATE MAPS
 This section describes the FY26 work to improve the workflow that is used for computing
dose rate maps. Section 4.1 outlines the motivation for this effort. Section 4.2 presents the one-
dimensional workflow developed to compute multigroup cross sections for variance reduction
calculations. Section 4.3 presents the results of an equivalent two-dimensional case examining
variance reduction of dose rate maps. Finally, Section 4.4 presents the application of the
workflow to a realistic three-dimensional GCMR case in an updated DOME model.

 4.1 Motivations
 The first efforts to compute dose rate maps in DOME were carried out in 2025 and are
reported in the FY25 milestone report [2]. To compute dose rate maps, the first step is to obtain
a converged neutron flux solution. This solution is required for two purposes: first, calculating
the neutron-induced dose, which can be straightforwardly derived from the neutron flux using an
energy function score in OpenMC; and second, determining the activation of materials
throughout the shielding to define the complete gamma source for the scenario under study.
Once the gamma source is established, a gamma flux solution is computed to calculate the
gamma-induced dose. The sum of both the neutron- and gamma-induced dose produces a
high-fidelity dose rate map. In this work, we focus on advancements in the initial step: achieving
a converged neutron flux solution. The workflow developed at that time relied on the Method of
Automatic Generation of Importances by Calculation (MAGIC) [12] and transported the
neutrons and photons created in the core. The workflow had two caveats:
• Convergence of the photon dose was not yet satisfactory, as some bins yielded an
 uncertainty of 100%.
• The workflow neglected all photon sources arising from the activation of the shielding
 materials, thereby capturing only the prompt photon contribution to the dose.
 A more sophisticated approach was therefore necessary: first, to generate weight windows
that drive faster convergence of both the neutron and photon flux maps, and second, to account
for a more realistic photon source.
 The MAGIC method employed in FY25 showed its limits for this application. MAGIC
iteratively updates the weight windows from the forward flux solution of successive Monte Carlo
runs. However, optimal weight windows derive from the adjoint flux, which acts as the
importance function, and the inverse of the forward flux is only an approximation of it. In a deep-
penetration problem such as DOME, this approximation breaks down. Radiation must traverse
two thick attenuating regions in succession: a layer of water, whose strongly anisotropic
hydrogen scattering and moderation attenuate the flux by several orders of magnitude, followed
by a concrete shield. Under such conditions, the forward flux becomes a poor surrogate for the
importance function. Moreover, because the first MAGIC iteration relies on an analog
simulation, the regions behind the water and concrete are barely sampled, so the initial flux
estimate there is dominated by statistical noise, and the iteration converges poorly.
 To overcome these limitations, we adopted an alternative weight-window generation
scheme natively available in OpenMC, which combines the random ray method with the
Forward flux-Weighted Consistent Adjoint Driven Important Sampling (FW-CADIS) [13]. A
multigroup cross-section library is first generated from spatially coarse group cross-section
tallies in an OpenMC model, and the random ray solver computes a deterministic forward flux
solution over the full geometry. This forward solution is then used to define the adjoint source,
and a second random ray solve yields the adjoint flux. Weight windows are finally produced

INL/RPT-26-95052                                                                                    16

from this adjoint solution via the FW-CADIS method, which targets uniform statistical uncertainty
across the entire dose map rather than the response of a single detector.
 This is a complex endeavor, and the workflow was therefore first validated on simplified one-
dimensional and two-dimensional cases to verify the generation of the multigroup cross sections
and to identify appropriate settings for the random ray solver.
 The CASMO-70 [14] energy mesh was selected for the generation of the multigroup cross
sections, as it is a well-established, sufficiently fine-resolution group structure for water reactors.

transport correction is obtained with OpenMC’s native in-scatter option. When using these group

cross sections in a forward multigroup calculation, the differences between the resulting fluxes

 The main conclusion from these computations is that an intrinsic loss of fidelity occurs when

compute dose rate maps is not recommended.
 However, the goal of this work is not to establish a multigroup calculation scheme, but rather
to build a workflow reliable enough to produce efficient weight windows for variance reduction
purposes. A certain level of discrepancy between the continuous-energy and multigroup
calculations is therefore acceptable in this specific context.

4.2.2 One-Dimensional Random-Ray Computations
 A random ray solver was next employed to facilitate the later generation of weight windows
using the FW-CADIS method for both two- and three-dimensional cases. Initially developed as
an alternative to the method of characteristics (MOC) [15], the random ray method has since
been implemented within OpenMC to support weight-window generation to address shielding
problems [16].

 4.4 Three-Dimensional Computations
 Finally, a representative three-dimensional case was set up, with the GCMR reactor placed
inside the updated model of the DOME facility (see Figure 21).

 Figure 21. X-Y, Y-Z, and X-Z views of the GCMR core in the updated DOME shield model.

 The FY24 milestone report recommended the following ES1 workflow to compute the
quantities of interest in the shield while accounting for backscattering:
• Step 1: Run an eigenvalue calculation of the core inside the DOME facility and record a
 surface source at the cavity boundary.
• Step 2: Transport this surface source through the shield.
 Because sources in the random ray solver are restricted to isotropic emission in the volume,
this approach is not compatible with a surface source that is both pointwise on the surface and
anisotropic. Hence, to keep an isotropic source, another workflow was proposed:
• Step 1: Run an eigenvalue calculation of the core inside the DOME facility and tally the flux
 distribution on the CASMO-70 energy mesh over a box enclosing the core.
• Step 2: Replace the GCMR with void and instead represent it by a box source carrying the
 same source energy distribution and intensity as the tallied flux. Run a fixed-source
 calculation using the FW-CADIS methodology. The anisotropy of the starting source is still
 lost; however, a fission source is considered isotropic. The spatial distribution of the source
 is smeared by representing the reactor as a box.
 The flux solution obtained with this workflow is displayed in Figure 22, which shows the flux
values on the mid-planes of the geometry. The computational cost was 12,500 CPU-hours. All
flux bins were scored, and 83% of them converged below 20% relative uncertainty.

 Future work is recommended to compute an activation source and transport both the

 In Section 4.3, we observed that obtaining an efficient DOME weight window with OpenMC
performed well in a two-dimensional test case, despite the challenges posed by the combination
of a deep-penetration problem (spanning 12 decades of attenuation) and strongly anisotropic
scattering within the water medium. However, when applied to a real-world three-dimensional
case, additional complexities from the updated workflow, explained previously and mostly
pertaining to the treatment of anisotropy, make the generation of accurate weight windows more
challenging. Nevertheless, the FW-CADIS results achieved in three-dimensional were better
than using the MAGIC method in FY24–25.
 To achieve production-ready results, it is therefore recommended to further develop the
random ray method to account for source anisotropy, notably to allow the use of anisotropic
sources, of surface sources and higher-order Legendre cross sections for scattering. These
developments are recommended for two reasons:
• To enable a rigorous and proven ES1 method that outputs a reliable flux solution
• To improve the generation of efficient weight windows for the DOME shield system by
 improving adjoint solutions.

 5. INTERFACE WITH NRIC’S DIGITAL TWIN EFFORTS
 Separate from our efforts to develop high-fidelity models of the DOME shield, NRIC is
pursuing the development of a DOME digital twin that integrates reactor, shielding, facility
thermal, and balance-of-plant models within a unified co-simulation framework. The DOME
digital twin team is coupling models for the reactor, Reactor Shielding System (RSS), DOME
interior, Heat Rejection System (HRS), and Confinement Ventilation System (CVS), as
illustrated in Figure 24. The reactor, RSS, and DOME interior each comprise a Functional
Mockup Unit (FMU) using Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environments (MOOSE’s)
recently developed FMU capability, whereas the HRS and CVS use Dymola FMUs. Time-
dependent reactor thermal power drives the RSS thermal response, which provides boundary
conditions to the interior model; the resulting facility heat loads are then passed to the HRS and
CVS models to close the facility-level thermal loop. These coupled simulations generate
responses corresponding to as many as 1,400 physical sensors distributed throughout the
DOME systems, supporting system monitoring, control actions, and the prediction of future
component states and coupled facility responses. Each FMU exposes boundary conditions,
control signals, and state variables as runtime connectors, allowing the component models to
advance together at each co-simulation time step. Airflow serves as a lightweight orchestration
layer for preparing inputs, submitting simulations, transferring data, and collecting outputs, while
computationally intensive MOOSE and Griffin/BISON simulations execute within dedicated
Linux or HPC environments. The current RSS-to-interior interface uses an artifact-based CVS
exchange as an incremental step toward fully live FMU coupling.
 In this section, we describe the VTB team’s contributions to this effort, which includes the
development of the RSS model, described in Section 5.1, and the generation of transient
reactor data used by the digital twin team to demonstrate the reactor FMU, described in Section
5.2.

 6. CAVITY HEAT TRANSFER STUDIES
 An accurate thermal model of the DOME shield is essential for demonstrating that the
maximum shield temperature remains below the allowable limit. Reactor heat is transferred
through the reactor’s external surface to the surrounding shield surfaces via thermal radiation
and natural convection. It is subsequently removed mainly by the water tank integrated into the
shield.
 In the previous version of the DOME thermal model [6], heat transfer within the cavity was
represented using a simplified approach. Surface-to-surface thermal radiation between the
reactor and the shield was modeled using the net radiation method, while heat transfer through
the air cavity was represented by an effective thermal conductivity. Although computationally
efficient, the use of a single effective thermal conductivity does not resolve the air temperature
and flow fields produced by natural convection. Consequently, it cannot accurately capture the
spatial temperature distribution within the cavity and may overpredict the shield temperature.
 The objective of this section is to present a higher-fidelity model of the air cavity that couples
computational fluid dynamics with surface-to-surface radiation. The coupled model provides a
more detailed representation of natural convection and radiative heat transfer, enabling a more
accurate prediction of the temperature distribution within the DOME shield. The model is
currently under development.

 6.1 Model
 Due to the large dimensions of the DOME shield air cavity and the substantial temperature
differences expected within it, the resulting natural-convection flow may be highly turbulent. A
fully three-dimensional computational fluid dynamics (CFD) model would therefore require
considerable computational resources. To reduce the computational cost, the DOME shield air
cavity is approximated using the two-dimensional geometry shown in Figure 29. This
approximation is partly justified by the large depth of the cavity relative to its width and height,
which is expected to limit three-dimensional effects over much of the domain.

 Figure 29. Dimensions of the DOME shield air cavity (measured in ft).

 The left and right boundaries represent the walls of the shield water tank, on which a
constant-temperature boundary condition of 300 K is applied, corresponding to the assumed
water outlet temperature from the tank. The surfaces of the water tank are coated with black

INL/RPT-26-95052                                                                                   29

paint to enhance radiative heat absorption and are therefore approximated as black surfaces
with an emissivity of ϵ = 1.
 The top and bottom surfaces consist of highly polished aluminum covering the concrete
shield structure. Because highly polished aluminum has a very low emissivity, these surfaces
are approximated as nonparticipating in radiative heat transfer, with ϵ ≈ 0. Adiabatic boundary
conditions are also applied to the top and bottom walls, such that both the conductive and
radiative heat fluxes through these boundaries are assumed to be negligible.
 The exterior surface of the reactor is assumed to be stainless steel with an emissivity of ϵ =
0.5. A uniform heat-flux boundary condition is applied to the reactor surface. The magnitude of
this heat flux depends on the reactor power level, with a maximum total heat load of 100 kW
released from the reactor’s exterior surface.
 The coupled thermal model uses NekRS to solve the CFD problem and MOOSE to perform
the surface-radiation calculations. The two codes are coupled through Cardinal. The solution
procedure begins with an initial estimate of the reactor surface temperature. Using this
temperature, the net radiation method is solved in MOOSE to determine the radiative heat flux
over the reactor surface. The conductive heat flux transferred from the reactor surface to the
surrounding air is then obtained by subtracting the radiative heat flux from the prescribed total
heat flux associated with the reactor power level. The resulting conductive heat flux is
transferred to NekRS, where the Navier–Stokes equations are solved together with the energy
equation to obtain the fluid flow and temperature fields, including an updated reactor surface
temperature. The updated surface temperature is then transferred back to MOOSE, where the
radiative heat flux is recalculated. This coupling procedure is repeated iteratively until
convergence is achieved. The following subsection presents the governing equations solved in
MOOSE and NekRS.

 7. CONCLUSIONS
 In FY26, the DOME shield design was finalized, which required changes to existing
analyses based on the preconceptual shield design. New shield geometry specifications and
meshes were created and applied to both reactor physics and thermal models. The meshing
strategy allows reactors to be meshed independently of the shield and then loaded into a
combined reactor-shield mesh, which allows the workflow to be usable for different reactor
geometries. A thermal model based on the finalized shield was created, which accounts for
radiative heat transfer between the reactor and shield using the net radiation method, with view
factors computed using MOOSE’s ray tracing module. This model was coupled with OpenMC to
provide the secondary heat source in the shield. In addition, a higher fidelity coupled CFD and
surface radiation model is under development and has been verified for a laminar flow case.

INL/RPT-26-95052                                                                               33

FY26 also introduced an activation analysis of the system to account for the residual
radioactivity following reactor shutdown due to the transmutation of stable nuclides in the DOME
shield. This capability was demonstrated on a GCMR operating at 5 MWth for 6 months; the
activity of numerous radionuclides was tracked for up to a year past shutdown. FY26 also
significantly improved the workflow to compute a reliable Monte Carlo continuous energy flux
solution across the whole geometry, which is needed to enable the computation of high-fidelity
dose rate maps. Finally, assistance was supplied to the DOME digital twin team by providing an
FMU for the DOME shield and a transient reactor model for demonstrating a reactor FMU.
 Future work may include a variety of improvements to the methods and workflows described
in this work. To gain confidence in the thermal model, a comparison with the calculations of
MPR Associates is recommended, by employing consistent modeling assumptions. Future work
will use the ES2 workflow to analyze activation of the air within the DOME shield, particularly Ar-
41, and assess its exchange with the wider facility. Future work will require an update of the
random-ray methodology to account for anisotropic sources, to enhance the convergence of the
flux deep into the shield. Additionally, future work will include the extension of the coupled CFD
surface radiation model for turbulent cases relevant to the DOME setup. Lastly, a selection of
models derived from this work are planned for integration into the VTB upon approval of public
release. In addition to the models themselves, documentation and tutorials for the various
computational workflows will be shared.
