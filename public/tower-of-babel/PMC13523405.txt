Case Summary

We present a case with precordial T-wave inversions on the presenting electrocardiogram (ECG) that prompted further evaluation for underlying causes. An 18-year-old woman with a past medical history of medically managed depression presented to the emergency department with a 2-day history of nausea, sore throat, and malaise. She had no previous cardiac history. Her physical examination was significant for tremulousness and hyperreflexia. Her ECG is presented in Figure 1A.

Discussion

We describe a young woman whose presenting ECG revealed T-wave abnormalities and U waves in the precordial leads, raising concern for ischemia and/or hypokalemia. Further work-up revealed a serum lithium level >6.0 mmol/L, indicating severe lithium poisoning. Initial laboratory testing revealed no plasma electrolyte abnormalities, and the admission ECG revealed a normal QTc (437 ms). She was admitted to the medical intensive care unit and treated with aggressive intravenous hydration. Hemodialysis was not performed. Over the ensuing 72 hours, plasma lithium levels steadily declined, her symptoms abated, the T-wave abnormalities in leads V3 to V5 resolved, and the QTc decreased to 382 ms (Figure 1A).

Lithium, a first-line agent for mood stabilization in bipolar disorder, has a narrow therapeutic index. It produces a spectrum of clinical abnormalities when toxic levels are reached, particularly involving the brain, gastrointestinal tract, and heart. The LiSIE Retrospective Cohort Study found that T-wave inversions were the most common ECG abnormality in patients with lithium toxicity, occurring in 42% of cases.1 QT prolongation was present in 24% of cases, but QTc correlated only weakly with lithium concentration. Consistent with these findings, our patient's ECG revealed anterior T-wave inversions, and her QTc was normal, despite a severely elevated plasma lithium level.

Lithium's effects on the ECG are best understood by recalling that lithium, sodium, and potassium are group 1 alkali metals with common physical properties, which underpins lithium's capacity to competitively inhibit key membrane sodium and potassium ion channels and pumps that control cardiac action potentials (APs). Sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodal cells exhibit slow-response APs, while the His bundle, bundle branches, Purkinje fibers, and cardiomyocytes exhibit fast-response APs (Figures 1B and C). In sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodal cells, lithium competitively inhibits hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated (HCN)/funny sodium channels, voltage-gated sodium channels, acetylcholine-gated potassium channels, and L-type calcium channels, which inhibits phase P4 of the slow-response AP and slows cell depolarization, thereby producing sinus bradycardia or arrest and atrioventricular block.2 In the His-Purkinje system and cardiomyocytes, lithium inhibits several potassium channels, as well as the sodium/potassium pump (Na+, K+-ATPase) and the sodium-calcium exchanger (NCX). As a result, the upstroke (P0), plateau (P2), and repolarization (P3) phases of the fast-response AP are slowed, leading to intraventricular conduction abnormalities (eg, left bundle branch block, right bundle branch block), ST-segment elevation or depression, and rarely, the Brugada pattern.3 Lithium's inhibition of the fast-response AP can also result in T-wave inversion and U waves mimicking hypokalemia, QT interval prolongation, and heterogeneity of ventricular repolarization (ie, QT dispersion), which increases cell excitability and the risk of atrial and ventricular arrhythmias (Figure 1D).
