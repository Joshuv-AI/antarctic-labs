1 Introduction
Ditches are built along roadways to convey the stormwater runoff. They collect the runoff from
the pavement and adjacent drainage areas. Water can move freely in a ditch away from the road
surface, thus making it safer for the vehicular traffic.

South Carolina DOT expressed interest in studying infiltration of water from ditches into roadway
embankments. As part of the current design criteria [1], it is recommended that the road
subgrades be 1.0 foot above the design event water surface elevation in the ditch. The distance
between the water surface and bottom of the subbase is referred to as “freeboard”. The purpose
of this requirement is to lower the risk of pavement damage by moving the flow away from the
pavement, containing the captured flow, and conveying flow received from adjacent properties to
outfalls. With changes in roadway design standards, larger shoulders and flatter cross-slopes have
moved ditches further away from the pavement and the subbase. These changes could allow for a
lower freeboard standard without reducing safety margins due to the increased lateral distance
from the subbase to the ditch.

The objective of this study is to evaluate whether the current SCDOT criterion requiring the
roadway subgrade to be 1 ft above the design-event ditch water surface can be relaxed under
selected geometric and soil conditions, particularly where larger shoulder widths and flatter side
slopes increase horizontal separation between ditch water and pavement structure.

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) was selected in this study not because it is the simplest
approach for unsaturated flow in soils, but because it provides a unified framework for
representing both the open-water region in the ditch and the adjacent porous embankment. In
particular, the CFD formulation allows the ditch water, the air above it, and the water-air mixture
within the soil pores to be modeled within a single domain, while also capturing the free surface
in the ditch explicitly. This approach is attractive for problems in which the interaction between
surface water and subsurface flow is of interest, and it offers flexibility for future application to
more complex geometries or additional coupled processes.

At the same time, the present CFD model should be viewed as an engineering approximation of
flow in porous media. The soil is represented as a continuum porous region with effective
properties such as porosity and flow resistance rather than as an explicit pore-scale geometry. As
a result, the method does not replace Richards-equation-based formulations, which remain the
more natural and computationally efficient approach for unsaturated flow in soils alone. For this
reason, VS2DI was also used in the study as a reference porous-media model and as a basis for
comparison.

Several assumptions were adopted to make the problem tractable and to support a screening-level
assessment of seepage behavior. First, the water level in the ditch was held constant throughout
each simulation. This assumption is conservative because it maintains the maximum hydraulic
head along the ditch boundary for the full event duration and therefore tends to overpredict
infiltration relative to a transient storm hydrograph in which ditch water depth would rise and fall
with time. The resulting seepage extent should therefore be interpreted as an upper-bound
estimate for the specified conditions.

Second, the analysis adopts a quasi-two-dimensional representation of the problem by assuming
that geometry and flow conditions are sufficiently uniform along the ditch that infiltration can be
approximated from a representative cross section. This assumption is most appropriate where
ditch geometry, water depth, and surrounding soil conditions vary slowly in the longitudinal
direction.

Third, the porous-medium representation idealizes the embankment soil using effective
properties derived from porosity, hydraulic resistance, and a characteristic grain size. In the CFD
formulation, this corresponds to an equivalent medium that is closer to a packed bed of uniform
particles than to a natural or compacted field soil. Real embankment soils contain a distribution
of particle sizes rather than a single uniform grain size, and their hydraulic behavior depends on
gradation, soil fabric, structure, and compaction history. Accordingly, the present results should
be interpreted primarily as a trend and bounding study rather than as a site-specific prediction
for a particular embankment.

Finally, the present model addresses hydraulic moisture migration only. It does not include
deformation of the soil skeleton or coupling to geo-mechanical response. Therefore, the
simulations do not directly predict changes in strength, pavement support, internal erosion
potential, slope stability, or damage associated with repeated wetting and drying cycles. Those
issues require separate geotechnical evaluation.

Movement of water into soil (infiltration) and water movement in soil (percolation and capillary
action) are caused by two driving forces: gravity force and capillary forces. The flow caused by
these forces is characterized by permeability, porosity, hydraulic conductivity for the gravity force
and by adhesion, cohesion, and surface tension forces for capillary action forces [2].

The soil infiltration rate is the rate at which water enters the soil and is related to the rate water
can move through soil and its layers. It depends on texture, structure, initial moisture levels,
among others. Soil infiltration rate depends strongly on soil texture because texture controls pore
size distribution and connectivity. Large, well-connected macropores between sand grains (coarse
texture) let water enter quickly. Loamy soil exhibits moderate infiltration because the balance of
macro- and micropores supports decent intake without extremely fast drainage. Silty soils have
moderate to low infiltration due to medium-to-small pores, and they can be prone to surface
sealing/crusting under rainfall, which sharply reduces infiltration. Clayey soils (fine texture) often
exhibit low infiltration because they are dominated by tiny micropores which have high water-
holding capacity but slow water entry. Soil structure, which defines how particles are aggregated
into clusters and how pores are arranged, can change infiltration as much as—or more than—
texture, because infiltration is controlled mainly by the size, continuity, and stability of pores near
the surface. Well-aggregated granular soils have many connected large pores between aggregates,
letting water enter quickly. Poor/weak structure reduces infiltration. Structureless, single-grain
but compacted, or weakly aggregated soils have fewer connected macropores, so water enters
more slowly. Initial soil moisture strongly affects infiltration because it changes the soil suction
and how quickly the soil becomes saturated near the surface. Drier soil has higher initial
infiltration rate which declines during a storm event. Wetter soil has lower initial infiltration rate.
The soil reaches near-saturation sooner, so infiltration becomes limited mainly by the soil’s
hydraulic conductivity. Soil particles vary greatly in size. A USDA classification for mineral
fraction <2 mm [3] is presented in Table 1-1.

Soil compaction is a process in which stress is applied to soil and presses the particles together,
reducing pore space between them. Heavily compacted soils contain few large pores, less total
pore volume and, consequently, have greater bulk density. Soil compaction increases soil
strength, which is the ability of soil to resist being moved by an applied force and reduces the rate
of water infiltration and drainage. The depositional porosity of well-sorted clean sand is in the
range 0.50–0.70. Compaction reduces it to a residual porosity of 0.25–0.40. Mechanical
compaction is very efficient in shales and mudstones for which the porosity decreases from the
depositional porosity 0.60–0.70 to a residual porosity 0.05–0.10 [4].

Section 205.3.2 of the SCDOT Construction Manual [6] specifies the requirements for
embankment soil compaction. During the formation and compaction of an embankment, each lift
must be compacted to a minimum 95% of maximum dry density. Maximum dry density and
optimum moisture content should be determined using the One-Point Proctor Method [7] or the
field method of determining the moisture-density relationship [8].

period of 24 hours or less. If the infiltration rate is high enough for the waterfront to reach the

The analysis assumes that the water is at maximum height in the ditch for the entire time period.
This approach yields worst case, conservative results because the static pressure driving water
into the soil at the sides and bottom of the ditch is also at a maximum. Varying water heights over
time could be modeled, but then the time history of the water height would have to be provided.
To maintain water height, the water seeping into the surrounding soil is replaced in the ditch by
adding a volume source to the differential equations. This source term replaces the inflow from
the upstream of the water in the ditch needed to keep the water level constant since that inflow is
not modeled to save computer resources and time.

3 Computational Modeling
Several levels of assessment were used to build confidence in the CFD approach adopted in this
study. First, a code/model verification check was performed for capillary rise in a narrow tube by
comparing the computed rise with the analytical result given by Jurin’s law. This comparison tests
whether the numerical treatment of interfacial forces and pressure balance reproduces the
expected capillary behavior in a simple geometry.

Second, an assessment was performed for unsaturated porous-medium behavior by comparing
the predicted hydraulic conductivity–water content relationship with the experimental data
reported by Watson [22] for drainage of a sand column. This comparison evaluates whether the
adopted porous-medium resistance formulation, including the water-content-dependent
conductivity relationship, reproduces the observed behavior of unsaturated flow.

Third, the ditch-embankment infiltration predictions from CFD were compared with those from
VS2DI, a Richards-equation-based porous-flow model commonly used for variable-saturation
problems. This comparison is not a formal validation against field measurements, but rather a
cross-model consistency check. For the representative cases examined, the two approaches
produced similar infiltration patterns and comparable wetting-front extents, with differences that
were small relative to the scale of the ditch-embankment geometry.

3.1 Richards’ Equation Based Software VS2DI

VS2DI is a software package for simulating water, solute, and heat transport through soils or other
porous media under conditions of variable saturation. The package contains a graphical
preprocessor for constructing simulations, a postprocessor for displaying simulation results, and
numerical models that solve for ﬂow and solute transport (VS2DT) and ﬂow and heat transport
(VS2DH). Flow is described by the Richards equation, and solute and heat transport are described
by advection-dispersion equations; the ﬁnite-diﬀerence method is used to solve these equations.
Problems can be simulated in one, two, or three (assuming radial symmetry) dimensions [17].

Voids in unsaturated soil are occupied by water and air. In the case of fully saturated soils, the
ﬂow of water follows Darcy’s law. However, in the case of unsaturated soil, the ﬂow of water and
air must be considered simultaneously. Water in unsaturated soil does not ﬂow through the air in
the voids but ﬂows along a ﬂow path consisting of continuous water. Accordingly, water ﬂow is
dependent on the degree of saturation of the soil, and the hydraulic conductivity of an unsaturated
soil is closely related to the water retention curve properties.

Five options are available in VS2DI [17] for describing the soil-water retention and relative
hydraulic conductivity curves in unsaturated soils, including the Van Genuchten equation, among
others. The Van Genuchten equation was selected for the current study, because the parameters
were available for generic soil texture classes.

3.2 Computational Fluid Dynamics Approach

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) analysis solves the partial differential equations governing
fluid motion (water and air in this application). The governing equations are the conservation of
mass and Newton’s 2nd law, expressing that acceleration (or deceleration) of the fluid times the
fluid mass is equal to the applied force. Applied forces for a fluid can be pressure, shear, and
gravity. A CFD model is a 3-dimensional section of the world, called the domain, with known
conditions at its boundaries: zero velocity at solid surfaces, specified velocity at inlet boundaries,
and pressure or zero gradients at outlet boundaries. The case of interest here, water infiltration
from a ditch into the soil, can be simplified by assuming that the component of velocity of water
in the ditch in the direction of the flow is zero. This can be done because the infiltration rate can
reasonably be assumed to be independent of the velocity along the ditch. The water height in the
ditch is also assumed to be constant. In this case a long section of ditch does not need to be
modeled and can be reduced to a thin section, see Figure 3-1.

  Figure 3-1 A sketch of the extent of the soil (porous region, porosity < 1) and the space above it
 (ﬂuid region, porosity = 1) in the proposed CFD model.
Figure 3-2 shows an example of a CFD model for the region of interest. It is a thin slice through
the volume of interest, meaning the roadway, ditch, and the space above and below them. The
cross-sectional planes, normal to the page in the figure, are symmetry boundaries (marked in
blue). The symmetry boundary condition defines a plane of reflection which assures that the flow
field pattern is mirrored across that surface with no flow across those surfaces and no shear stress
parallel to the surfaces. Using a domain with symmetry boundaries normal to the direction of
ditch flow allows a computational domain much smaller than one needed to include flow in the
longitudinal direction of the ditch, thus greatly reducing the time and computer resources needed
to solve the problem. The top surface is a pressure outlet at atmospheric pressure. The side and
bottom surfaces are pressure outlet boundary conditions with back-pressure set equal to the static
pressure created by the fluid column above any position on those boundaries. Therefore, water
can leave the ditch under the force of gravity, infiltrate the soil at the bottom of the ditch and push
air in the soil along in front of it. Pressure does not build up in the soil due to water displacing air
because the pressure boundary conditions on the sides of the domain allow the air to escape

                                                                                              Page | 11

through those boundaries. The interface between the fluid and porous region is an internal
interface that allows for movement of fluid between the regions with different properties.

Figure 3-2 A snapshot of the CFD model used in the computations. Symmetry surfaces are marked
 in blue; walls are marked in grey and pressure outlets are marked in orange.
The solution is transient, i.e., changing during the simulation because it tracks the penetration
and movement of water from the ditch into the soil over time. Depending on the infiltration rate,
the flow may be laminar or turbulent. The Unsteady Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes Solver was
selected with Two-Layer Realizable K-Epsilon turbulence model to solve the flow. The time step
differs depending on the flow velocity and varies from a fraction of a second to a few seconds. The
simulation is run until one of two conditions is met: (1) the maximum horizontal extent of the
water infiltrating the soil from the ditch moves past the bottom of the subbase, or (2)
the maximum assumed time of a rain event of 24 hours is achieved.

The Eulerian two-phase model with a gas phase, air, and liquid phase, water, combined with the
Volume of Fluid (VOF) physics model was selected to model the water ﬂow with a free surface.
The moisture content in the soil volume is deﬁned using an initial condition of a speciﬁed volume
fraction of water and air. Gravitational force is included in the model.

The water table within the embankment is also accounted for in the modeling. This level is
considered one of the parameters because it influences the flow through the embankment. The
volume fraction of air within the zone of the water table is equal to zero.

The soil volume can be modeled in various ways. An approach previously used by the authors to
model permeable pavements is to use the Eulerian Multiphase model with two phases: water and
air, and Volume of Fluid approach to compute the interface between the two phases. In CFD, soil
is often idealized as a porous medium so that flow and transport can be modeled using volume-
averaged continuum equations with permeability and porosity as effective properties [18]. This
approach was adopted for this study.

3.2.1 Validation Example: Watson’s Experiment

As a part of the validation of the CFD models used in this study, the above modifications of the
soil resistance were incorporated into the porous media model and their impact on the CFD
results was evaluated using an example case found in literature.

Watson [22] published results of an experimental study of the hydraulic conductivity in
unsaturated soils. The researchers determined the conductivity vs. water content relationship
from measurements obtained during drainage of an initially saturated sand column. A well-
rounded sand fraction of 150-300 microns was used. The sand column was 57-cm-tall, with a
cross-section 10 cm by 15 cm. The saturated water content was 0.35 and the room temperature
was 69 F. Initially in the experiment, a steady-state saturated condition was achieved, then water
was drained from the sand column to the atmosphere. In Watson’s experiment, water content
profiles were determined gravimetrically by measuring the amount of water remaining in
different sections of the sand column at selected times during drainage. These moisture
measurements, together with suction data, were used in the instantaneous profile method to
estimate unsaturated hydraulic conductivity.

Firstly, the van Genuchten model was calibrated to approximate Watson’s findings. The two sets
of data points, showing the relationship between the water content and the hydraulic conductivity,
are presented in Figure 3-3.

Next, a model representing the geometry and flow conditions from the experiment was
constructed. A block of the size of the experimental sample was meshed out with polyhedral
surface mesh with element target size of 2.5 mm and minimum size of 1 mm. The surface mesh
was extruded through 1000 layers which resulted in ~2.5 million volume cells. Water content and
hydraulic conductivity were calculated and saved along a grid of 7 by 7 vertical lines away from
the walls of the model every 10 seconds of simulated time. The simulation was run until 20
minutes of the simulated time had passed. Figure 3-4 shows a sketch of the soil sample tested by
Watson that is represented in the CFD software, the discretization of the geometry, and the probe
lines used to collect the solution data.

3.2.2 Verification Example: Jurin’s Law

Capillary rise is the upward movement of water between soil particles due to a combination of the
cohesion and adhesive forces between water and particle surfaces. In fine soil, the upward
movement of water is slow but covers a long distance. On the other hand, in coarse soil, the
upward movement of water is quick, but covers only a short distance.

An experimental set-up used in laboratories to study ﬂuid properties such as viscosity and surface
tension involves an empty capillary tube whose lower end is immersed in water was used as a
reference case with a known solution to check CFD capabilities. During the experiment, a concave
free surface forms inside the tube. Depending on its inner diameter, water rises to a diﬀerent
height. Water rises due to the adhesion forces between water and the solid, until the forces are
balanced by the gravity force. Jurin’s law [23] is commonly used to calculate the height of water
rise in a capillary tube:

5 Conclusions
The CFD model used in this study represents a thin slice through the roadway, ditch, and the space
above and below them. The geometry of the model assumes axial uniformity of the roadway and
its surroundings. The water level in the ditch is kept constant throughout the simulation.
The soil volume is represented with a porous media model with resistance calculated as for a
volume ﬁlled with spherical particles with size varied from 0.001 mm to 2 mm. It has non-zero
porosity, and viscous and inertial resistance. In turn, the volume above the soil has a unit porosity
and zero resistance to the flow. Both volumes may contain water and air defined with the phase
volume fraction. This approach allows for representing unsaturated and saturated soils in the
same model. Validity of using the porous media model for engineering CFD analysis was
established by comparison with a physical test performed by Watson [22].
The model is not free of limitations, such as the assumption that the soil is made of spherical
particles with a constant diameter. In reality, the shape of soil particles varies, which may impact
the extent of the infiltration. Soils properties may vary with a gradation of the grain size and some
of their characteristics depend on it. For example, hydraulic conductivity depends on the size of
the smallest grains, and this property is commonly defined as a function of d 10. Also, the impact
of vegetation was not included in the model.

Nevertheless, the simulations performed during this study exhibit well-known characteristics of
soil hydraulics: the compaction of the soil (decrease in porosity) decreases the infiltration speed
and its horizontal extent. Also, soils made of finer particles infiltrate slower than when they consist
of larger diameter grains.

No model for capillary rise in porous media is included in the used CFD software. However, an
approach was implemented in which negative pressure is applied to the top surface of the soil
volume. This soil boundary condition provides an equivalent of the capillary pressure and does
produce some capillary rise. The results show that this modification slightly increases the
infiltration extent but not enough to cause the moisture to reach the subbase. This means that it
does not change the findings obtained from models without capillary pressure.

The CFD with porous media model results were compared with results from VS2D, a 2D code
based on Richards’ equations. The comparison shows that CFD and the VS2D codes give similar
results in the scenarios considered. VS2D gives slightly bigger horizontal extent of the infiltration
which results in a more conservative result. Also, the VS2D code gives results much faster than
CFD and can be used on a personal computer. Unfortunately, it is not currently supported, and
the latest version was released in 2012.

The current South Carolina DOT design criteria require that the road subgrades be 1.0 foot above
the design event water surface elevation in the ditch in order to lower the risk of pavement damage
by moving the flow away from the pavement, containing the captured flow, and conveying flow
received from adjacent properties to outfalls. It was found in the study that this guideline is
reasonable within the scope of the study. The analyzed cross-section of the roadway and its
vicinity leads to a conclusion that if a groundwater table at or close to the ditch bottom occurs, the
vertical distance between the design water surface elevation in a ditch and the bottom of the road
subbase should be greater than zero (positive freeboard). If the groundwater is not present or the
table is lower than the ditch bottom, the limiting factor is the horizontal distance between the road
and the ditch. Within the range of soil properties, geometries, and hydraulic boundary conditions
considered in this study, a 10-ft horizontal separation from the ditch front slope to the pavement
structure appeared sufficient to prevent infiltrating water from reaching the subbase during the
simulated events, provided that a shallow groundwater table was not present.

Repeated saturation events may affect slope performance in ways not captured by the present
hydraulic analysis. In addition to increasing moisture content, repeated wetting can reduce matric

                                                                                              Page | 38

suction, soften near-surface soils, and potentially contribute to seepage- or toe-related instability
where slopes extend toward drainage ditches. These mechanisms were not explicitly evaluated in
this study and should therefore be considered in site-specific geotechnical assessments.
Interpretation of the results would also benefit from input from a geotechnical engineer familiar
with local soil conditions, who can assess how closely the soils examined here represent field soils
and which findings are most applicable in practice.

Based on the simulations conducted in this study, the following engineering implications are
suggested:

1. Maintain current SCDOT design criteria for a positive freeboard where shallow (within 1 ft of
 the ditch) groundwater may be present. The groundwater cases indicate that when the mean
 groundwater table is near the ditch bottom, the risk increases that moisture or saturation will
 approach the roadway subbase. Where groundwater is expected near the ditch bottom,
 maintaining positive freeboard remains prudent.

2. Where groundwater is absent and lateral offset is sufficient, reduced freeboard may be
 acceptable within the tested conditions. For the roadway geometry and soil conditions
 examined, infiltration from the ditch tended to migrate mainly downward and did not reach
 the subbase when the lateral separation was at least about 10 ft which is consistent with
 current SCDOT design criteria for lateral offset/freeboard. This finding should be interpreted
 only within the range of conditions examined in this study.

3. Fine-grained and/or highly compacted soils showed limited infiltration during the simulated
 event durations. These soils generally produced smaller wetting-front extents and slower
 moisture migration than coarser, more permeable materials.

4. Additional geotechnical review is suggested where site conditions may increase risk,
 including:

 o coarse granular embankment soils,

 o long-duration ponding or sustained ditch flow,

 o repeated wetting events that may affect embankment or pavement performance.

5. Site screening for shallow groundwater should be considered if reduced freeboard is
 contemplated. Because groundwater conditions appear to be a controlling factor, any
 relaxation of current criteria would be better supported where shallow groundwater has been
 ruled out or shown to remain below critical elevations.
