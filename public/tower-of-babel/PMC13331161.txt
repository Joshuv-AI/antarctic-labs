Degenerative spine surgery: What large-scale outcome registries teach us about indications and timing
Authors: Gilbert Dechambenoit
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Defining appropriate surgical indications in degenerative spine disease remains a central challenge. The DaneSpine national registry, with over 115,000 procedures across 16 institutions, covering approximately 80% of national activity recorded since 2009, provides a unique large-scale dataset to address this question through patient-reported outcomes over a 15-year period.[7]

The study is retrospective and descriptive in design, drawing on the registry’s aggregated and anonymized annual reports (2012–2024). Data collected include demographic characteristics, diagnoses, surgical procedures, and functional and quality-of-life scores (Oswestry disability index [ODI], Visual Analog Scale [VAS], EQ-5D, neck disability index [NDI], Short Form-36) measured preoperatively and at 1, 2, 5, and 10 years postoperatively.

Lumbar stenosis

ODI improved from 42 to 26 at 1 year (a significant shift from the “severe disability” to the “moderate disability” category). Lower limb pain (VAS) decreased from 59 to 33. Preoperatively, 70% of patients had a walking capacity of less than 500 m; at 1 year postoperatively, 44% were able to walk more than 1,000 m. Improvement was greater when the duration of preoperative symptoms was shorter.

Disc herniation

ODI improved by 24.8 points at 1 year. Radicular pain (VAS) decreased from 62.6 to 25.9. Quality of life (EQ-5D) improved from 0.33 to 0.71. Patients on sick leave for more than 3 months before surgery had only a 25% chance of returning to work.

Degenerative spondylolisthesis

A total of 4,820 patients were operated on, with significant and lasting improvements at 2 years. A decline in the number of procedures was observed, reflecting a shift in practice toward decompression alone rather than systematic fusion.

Cervical surgery (≈ 15% of procedures)

A total of 15,385 cervical procedures were performed between 2009 and 2024, stable at approximately 1,200/ year since 2014. The primary indication was radiculopathy (disc herniation 37%, foraminal stenosis 42%). Cervical myelopathy accounted for only 20% of cases.

For cervical radiculopathy, 1-year outcomes were highly significant: NDI −15.9 points, arm VAS −30.6 points, EQ-5D + 0.31. For myelopathy, the primary surgical goal is to halt neurological deterioration; functional gains on VAS and NDI were more modest, but quality-of-life improvement (EQ-5D) was similar to that observed in radiculopathy.

THE QUESTION OF SURGICAL INDICATION

The issue of surgical indication is addressed in a nuanced but indirect and partial manner in this article, rather than being foregrounded as a central, explicitly emphasized message. The following arguments are developed, with their respective references:

Duration of symptoms as a criterion for surgical indication

This is the most extensively developed argument. The article emphasizes that the longer symptoms persist before surgery, the worse the outcomes, implying that appropriate indication involves operating at the right time.

For lumbar stenosis, improvement in EQ-5D was significantly lower when preoperative symptoms had lasted more than 24 months.

For disc herniation, the article cites an internal DaneSpine registry study: Andersen et al.[1] -Only 25% of patients returned to work if sick leave exceeded 3 months before surgery, compared to a substantially higher proportion for shorter sick-leave periods.

Surgery reserved for failure of conservative treatment

The article advocates for surgery in patients who have not responded to conservative management, while cautioning against undue delay. Three external references are cited:

Peul et al.[6] – Early surgery for sciatica yields better short-term outcomes, but results converge at 1 year with prolonged conservative treatmentBailey et al.[3] – Surgery is superior to conservative management for sciatica persisting between 4 and 12 months, supporting surgical intervention in this subgroupLilly et al.[5] – The majority of disc herniations can be managed without surgery, but a subgroup requires intervention to achieve a satisfactory outcome.

Cervical myelopathy: A different objective

The article notes that for myelopathy, the surgical indication follows a different rationale -halting neurological deterioration rather than relieving pain. A “stable” outcome is considered a success, which tempers pain-relief expectations and mandates early intervention before irreversible neurological damage is established.

WHAT THE ARTICLE DOES NOT EXPLICITLY ADDRESS

One notable limitation deserves emphasis: The article does not directly discuss inappropriate indications (patients operated on unnecessarily), nor does it address negative outcome predictors beyond symptom duration (psychological comorbidities, chronic pain, medicolegal context, etc.). The issue of patient selection is mentioned in the conclusion as an avenue for future registry development, an implicit acknowledgment that this remains an unresolved challenge.

Summary

The article raises the question of surgical indication through the lens of operative timing and choice of technique, rather than as a broader reflection on patient selection. The arguments are supported by solid references (notably NEJM-level randomized trials), but the overall message remains more descriptive than normative on this point.

CONCLUSION

DaneSpine represents a model national clinical registry, demonstrating that surgery for degenerative spinal conditions yields significant and durable benefits in terms of pain, function, and quality of life. It also highlights the importance of operating before the duration of symptoms compromises outcomes, and stands as a reference for international evidence-based spine surgery initiatives.

The relevance of such a registry is further underscored by the dramatic rise in the economic burden of spinal surgery observed in other healthcare systems. In the United States, hospital charges attributable to spinal procedures increased from $5.4 billion to $48.7 billion over 16 years -a 542% rise –driven in large part by a surge in spinal fusion rates.[8] This exponential growth, not matched by equivalent improvements in patient outcomes, raises serious questions about surgical indications and the appropriate allocation of healthcare resources. In this context, outcome-based registries such as DaneSpine offer a critical counterweight: By systematically linking procedural data to PROMs, they provide the evidence needed to define rational indications, curb unnecessary interventions, and ultimately improve the value of care delivered.
