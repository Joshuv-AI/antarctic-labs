GPNMB Is Strongly Expressed in Foreign Body Giant Cells in Human and Mouse Tissues
Authors: Tae-Ryong Riew, Jihyoung Chang, Ji-Won Hwang, Jooho Jeong, Yoon-Seob Kim
Published: 2026

Foreign body giant cells (FBGCs) are pivotal in the foreign body reaction (FBR) to biomaterials, yet they lack reliable molecular markers for histologic identification1. Glycoprotein non-metastatic melanoma protein B (GPNMB), a regulator of inflammation and tissue repair, has been proposed as a candidate based on single-cell transcriptomic data2. However, its protein expression in FBR tissues remains unvalidated. We therefore investigated GPNMB expression at the tissue level in both human FBR and a murine model to validate its utility as an FBGC marker.

From pathological reviews of skin biopsy specimens diagnosed as FBR, we selected nine human samples showing granulomatous inflammation surrounding various foreign materials, obtained from the Biobank of Bucheon St. Mary’s Hospital (n=9). The FBR mouse model (n=5) was prepared using synthetic polycaprolactone in powder form (25090-100; Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA), as previously described3. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections underwent immunohistochemistry using an anti-GPNMB antibody (AF2330, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA; 1:500). GPNMB localization and staining intensity were assessed at ×400 magnification in FBGCs and in distant control regions lacking FBGCs within the same tissue. GPNMB staining intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). For each sample, five representative non-overlapping high-power fields from FBGC-rich regions and distant FBGC-absent regions were captured. The mean optical density of the diaminobenzidine signal was calculated after applying a standardized color deconvolution algorithm. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The Catholic University of Korea (HC23SISI0052) and the Animal Research Ethics Committee of The Catholic University of Korea (CUMS-2022-0233-04).

In a murine FBR model (n=5), GPNMB was strongly expressed in FBGCs, while normal mouse skin exhibited minimal staining (Supplementary Fig. 1). In human FBR tissue samples (n=9), FBGCs also exhibited strong cytoplasmic GPNMB expression on immunohistochemistry, as illustrated in a representative case (Fig. 1A). Quantitative analysis revealed significantly higher staining intensity in FBGC-rich regions compared to FBGC-absent regions (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 2; paired t-test, p<0.01). This pattern was consistently observed across all analyzed human samples (Supplementary Fig. 3).

GPNMB is strongly expressed in FBGCs within the FBR microenvironment in human tissues. (A) Representative immunohistochemistry images from a human FBR case. Multinucleated FBGCs (arrows) show strong cytoplasmic GPNMB expression. (B) Box plot comparing GPNMB staining intensity between FBGC-rich and FBGC-absent regions in human FBR tissues (n=9). Consistent with our previous single-cell transcriptomic data1, our study provides the first tissue-level validation of robust and reproducible GPNMB protein expression in FBGCs from both human and murine FBR tissues. This conserved expression across species underscores its fundamental role in the chronic inflammatory response and fibrotic processes of FBR4,5. In our study, faint GPNMB signals in the epidermis, follicular epithelium, and skeletal muscle of normal mouse skin were consistent with known physiological expression in both human and mouse tissues6,7, and markedly lower than the intense staining in FBGCs. However, the precise role of GPNMB in FBR warrants careful consideration. Its function is highly context-dependent, and this is particularly relevant within FBGCs, which are now understood to be terminally differentiated cells of the M2 macrophage lineage, typically driven by Th2 cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-138. Within this specific cellular context, an emerging view suggests that GPNMB may reflect a more fundamental, conserved macrophage injury response rather than a specific pro-fibrotic program9. Our findings are consistent with this perspective; in the unique, non-resolving environment of FBR, the unrelenting stimulus from the foreign material likely locks FBGCs into this persistent injury-response state. Consequently, the primary function of GPNMB plausibly shifts from facilitating acute resolution to orchestrating the chronic programs of encapsulation and tissue remodeling9.

Although our quantitative analysis was limited to nine cases due to tissue quality constraints, the included specimens were inherently heterogeneous. They varied in the type of foreign material, the degree and pattern of inflammatory response, and the extent and distribution of FBGC formation. This reflects the inherent difficulty in obtaining surgical specimens that exhibit classic, well-defined foreign body granulomas suitable for comparative immunohistochemical analysis. Nonetheless, the consistent GPNMB expression across nine cases with diverse foreign materials and inflammatory patterns supports the robustness of our findings, though validation in larger cohorts is needed. Additionally, our analysis is primarily descriptive and does not elucidate the functional role of GPNMB in FBGC formation or activity. In our study, GPNMB was consistently expressed in mature FBGCs in chronic-phase human FBR tissues; however, GPNMB expression may vary as FBGCs encompass heterogeneous activation states along the macrophage fusion and differentiation continuum10.

In conclusion, GPNMB is strongly expressed in FBGCs in both human and mouse models of FBR, which supports its utility as a reproducible marker of FBGCs. Future functional studies, for instance using GPNMB knockout models or in vitro siRNA-mediated knockdown, are necessary to determine whether GPNMB actively contributes to macrophage fusion, chronic encapsulation, or tissue remodeling in FBR. Given the limited systemic therapeutic options for multifocal or surgically inaccessible FBR, functional characterization of GPNMB could facilitate the development of targeted approaches for preventing or treating FBR, including antibody-based therapies.
