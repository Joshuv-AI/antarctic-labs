Tonsillar lymphangioma with adipose and smooth muscle proliferation: a case report
Authors: Maryam Maghbool, Babak Samizadeh, Seyedeh Yasamin Parvar, Khatere Roozbehi, Aliasghar Karimi, Adrina Habibzadeh, Negin Ashtari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLymphangiomatous lesions commonly occur in the head and neck region; however, involvement of the palatine tonsil is rare. Tonsillar lymphangiomatous polyps containing both adipose tissue and smooth muscle proliferation are exceptionally uncommon. We report a rare case of tonsillar lymphangioma with adipose tissue and focal smooth muscle proliferation.Case presentationA 28-year-old Iranian man presented with sore throat and was found to have a pedunculated mass arising from the right palatine tonsil. Physical examination revealed a smooth-surfaced, non-ulcerated polypoid lesion measuring 2 × 1 × 0.3 cm. Owing to the small size and clinically benign appearance of the lesion, no preoperative imaging was performed. Right-sided tonsillectomy was undertaken. Histopathologic examination demonstrated a lymphangiomatous polyp (lymphangioma) composed of dilated lymphatic channels within a fibroadipose stroma with focal smooth muscle proliferation. The lesion was completely excised with negative surgical margins. No recurrence was observed during 1 year of follow-up.ConclusionsTonsillar lymphangiomatous lesions are rare benign entities that may clinically mimic other tonsillar masses. Histopathologic examination is essential for definitive diagnosis. Complete surgical excision appears curative, and recurrence is uncommon.Keywords: Lymphangioma, Lymphangiomatous polyp, Palatine tonsil, Pedunculated polyp, Tonsillar mass

Introduction

Palatine tonsils are located in the entryway of the gastrointestinal and respiratory tract, between the palatopharyngeal arch of the soft palate and palatoglossal arch [1]. The head and neck are the most common sites of lymphangioma tumors, which account for more than 90% of all lymphangiomatous lesions. However, tonsils are less common sites of such tumors [2, 3]. There are three types of lymphangiomas in the head and neck region: lymphangioma simplex, composing thin-walled capillary sized lymphatic channels, cystic hygroma, and cavernous lymphangioma [4].

The prevalence of lymphangiomas is approximately one in 2000 to 4000 live births. A study showed that lymphangioma occurs between 3 and 80 years of age, and the mean age was 22.5 years [3, 5, 6].

Patients with lymphangioma typically present with chief complaints of dysphagia, sore throat, and sensation of a mass in the throat [7]. Treatments include simple excision or tonsillectomy without any recurrence or transforming to malignancy [8]. Here, we present a rare case of a lymphangiomatous polyp of the right palatine tonsil with adipose tissue and focal smooth muscle proliferation.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old Iranian man presented to his general practitioner with a sore throat and was found to have a mass in his palatine tonsil upon examination. The patient was alert and oriented to time, place, and person and other physical examinations were unremarkable. There was no history of previous admission and surgery. He also did not mention any underlying diseases. Oropharyngeal examination demonstrated a pedunculated, smooth-surfaced, non-ulcerated mass arising from the superior pole of the right palatine tonsil. No erythema, exudate, airway compromise, dysphagia, odynophagia, or cervical lymphadenopathy was identified. His laboratory results were normal, including white blood cells (WBC) (6.6 10*3/uL) and lymphocytes (31.7%). Owing to the small size and clinically benign appearance of the lesion, preoperative imaging was not considered necessary.The patient went under right-sided tonsillectomy surgery, in which the whole tonsil was dissected and submitted for pathological examination. In gross pathological evaluation, a 4 × 2 × 1 (cm) creamy ovaloid palatine tonsil and a 2 × 1 × 0.3 (cm) attached polypoid creamy soft tissue was found. Serial cut sections showed a homogenous creamy surface. (Fig. 1) Microscopic examination revealed large lymphatic channels in loose connective tissue stroma, focal disorganized smooth muscle in wall of larger channels and peripheral lymphoid aggregates (Fig. 1). Histopathological findings were most consistent with a lymphangiomatous polyp (lymphangioma) containing adipose tissue and focal smooth muscle proliferation. The lesion was completely excised and no residual lesion was identified at the surgical margins. It is also worth mentioning that the patient recovered well from his operation without any complications and in one year follow-up, no recurrence was detected.

Discussion

Differential diagnoses of oral lesions includes papillomas, angiomas, fibromas, myxomas, and lipomas [2]. Koch first described lymphangioma in 1913, which led to obstruction of lymph flow and lymphangiectasia [9, 10]. Lymphangiomatous polyps of the tonsil are uncommon benign lesions characterized by dilated lymphatic channels within a fibroadipose stroma. Variable amounts of smooth muscle may be present in the walls of larger lymphatic vessels. These histologic findings may mimic other mesenchymal lesions; however, true lymphangiomyolipoma belongs to the PEComa family and typically demonstrates perivascular epithelioid cells with melanocytic marker expression. Other benign tonsillar cases reported in the literature, includes lymphoid polyp, lipoma, neurofibroma, and plasma cell granuloma [12, 13].

Lymphangiomas are rare, congenital, benign tumors of the lymphatic system. They usually develop near birth or in the first two decades of life [14]. Congenital lymphangioma usually develops before five years of age due to improper connection of the lymph ducts to the main lymphatic drainage duct. Lymphangiomatous lesions are common in the skin, larynx, parotid gland, mouth, and tongue. They can infrequently develop in the tonsils, and can be either unilateral or bilateral [5, 11, 15, 16].

Our case was referred with a simple sore throat. Other common clinical presentations include dysphagia, tonsillitis, cough, dyspnea, increased salivation, vomiting and hemoptysis [2].

Common diagnostic methods, depending on the location of the mass, are computed tomography (CT) scan, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of pharyngeal region, X-rays, and biopsies [17]. Our patient’s tonsil size has been reported 4 × 2 × 1 cm, which is larger than the normal range. The mean normal size for the palatine tonsils on the right and left side is estimated to be 1.5 ± 0.9 cm3, which is related to age, height, weight, and body mass index [14].

In a study by Casey et al., a young patient with unilateral lymphangiomatous polyps underwent tonsillectomy surgery [18]. Our patient also underwent a right-sided tonsillectomy, which is the treatment of choice in tonsillar masses [19]. It is worth pointing out that lymphangiomatous polyps usually do not show recurrence or deformity after surgery [8].

To our knowledge, reports of tonsillar lymphangiomatous lesions containing prominent adipose tissue and smooth muscle proliferation are extremely rare. This emphasizes the importance of considering this rare disease entity of palatine tonsil for ear, nose and throat (ENT) surgeons and pathologists.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of a tonsillar lymphangiomatous polyp presenting with a simple sore throat. Tonsillar lymphangiomatous lesions are rare benign entities that may clinically mimic other tonsillar masses. Histopathologic examination remains essential for definitive diagnosis. Complete surgical excision is curative, and recurrence appears to be uncommon.
