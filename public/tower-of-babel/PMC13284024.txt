The prevalence of preoperative anxiety among patients undergoing insertion of penile prosthesis
Authors: Fayez T Hammad, Muwafak A Salman, Abdalla Alhammadi, Hamza F Afaneh, Amed F Deyab, Salem AlBlooshi, Khalid Askhaita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePreoperative anxiety (PA) adversely affects surgical outcomes. Compared to other surgical procedures, penile prosthesis implantation (PPI) may result in exceptionally high PA because of concerns about a foreign implant and postoperative patient’s and partner’s expectations, yet PA in this population has not been previously studied.MethodsThis prospective cross-sectional study included all men who underwent PPI in our institute from November 2023 to November 2025. The Amsterdam Preoperative Anxiety and Information Scale (APAIS) questionnaire was used to determine the degree of PA. Participants were stratified into low (< 11) and high (≥ 11) anxiety groups based on total anxiety scores.ResultsAmong 123 participants (mean age: 56.3 years), 52.8% demonstrated clinically significant PA. On univariate analysis, higher PA was associated with education level, number of previous surgical procedures, personal history of surgical or anesthetic complications, duration of surgical consultations, internet information-seeking, discussing surgery with a friend, anesthesia type, and operative duration (P < 0.05 for all). On multivariate analysis, personal history of prior surgical or anesthetic complications and internet information-seeking remained independent predictors of high PA (P < 0.05). Common concerns included device failure (25.2%), inability to activate the device (14.6%), and perceived changes in penile length, girth, or rigidity (12.2%, 9.8%, and 11.4%, respectively).ConclusionsPA is highly prevalent among men undergoing PPI. Prior personal history of previous surgical or anesthetic complications and internet information-seeking independently predicted high anxiety. This data might help healthcare providers to specifically target this group of patients to decrease the level of PA and its downstream consequences.Keywords: Preoperative anxiety, Penile prosthesis implantation, Erectile dysfunction

Introduction

Anxiety is a generalized, unsettled feeling that is frequently unspecific and unknown to the person experiencing it. Anxiety alters the hemodynamic status of the individual due to sympathetic, parasympathetic, and endocrine alterations [1–4]. Due to the stressful nature of surgical procedures, most surgical patients experience some degree of pre-operative anxiety (PA) [5–13]. PA leads to a wide range of harmful effects such as difficulty in anesthesia induction, increased anesthetic consumption, severe postoperative pain, increased analgesic use and delayed recovery. It also increases the rate of post-operative pulmonary edema, myocardial infarctions and heart failure leading to high rates of cardiac mortality [11, 14]. Collectively, this results in prolonged hospital stay and decreased patient satisfaction with perioperative care.

Erectile dysfunction is a common disease especially in old men and it is estimated that more than 50% of men between the age of 40 and 70 years, experience some degree of erectile dysfunction [15]. Among all treatment options, penile prosthesis implantation (PPI) is a well-established procedure for the treatment of patients with erectile dysfunction, especially those who are not responding to other treatment modalities [16–18].

There is a strong and complex relation between erectile dysfunction and the patient’s anxiety status. So, erectile dysfunction has been shown to have a significant impact on quality of life. On the other hand, there is a good correlation between patient’s anxiety status and the development of erectile dysfunction [15, 19, 20]. Although, like other surgical procedure, (PPI) is expected to cause PA, the degree of the PA in this procedure is expected to be higher than other surgical procedures due to the implantation of a foreign body, the need to master the technique of inflation which requires some degree of hand dexterity, the emotional and sensitive aspects of the erectile dysfunction itself and the patients’ and his partners’ expectations. Up to date, there are no studies which addressed the prevalence and determinants of PA among patients undergoing (PPI). Therefore, the aim of this study was to fill this gap in literature with a particular emphasis on factors related to pre-operative counseling.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This is an interviewer-administered questionnaire-based study which included all patients who underwent (PPI) at our institute between November 2023 and November 2025. The interviewers were not involved in the initial surgical consultations. Patients with previous penile surgery were excluded. The research protocol was approved by the local Institutional Review Board (MF2058-2023-966). Sample size calculation indicated that a study of 102 patients was adequate to investigate the prevalence of PA in this group of patients with high degree of precision (95%). This was based on the results of previous studies which investigated the prevalence of PA in patients undergoing other types of surgery [21–24].

Data collection and measures

After consenting, patients were interviewed in the preoperative holding area before surgery by a trained interviewer. The questionnaire consisted of four sections. The first section included sociodemographic data such as age, employment status, educational level and residence (urban vs. rural area). The second section investigated patients’ personal and family history of surgical and anesthetic complications. The third section addressed other factors such as those related to the preoperative counseling process including patient’s perception of the duration of surgery and type of anesthesia, total duration of surgical consultations, the use of visual illustrations by the counselling physician (drawings, videos etc.), the use of internet search to seek further information and speaking to a close friend about the surgery pre-operatively. The fourth section included the Amsterdam Pre-operative Anxiety and Information Scale (APAIS).

The APAIS questionnaire was specifically developed to assess PA in patients undergoing surgical procedures [9] and has been widely validated [25–28]. The instrument consists of six items: four anxiety-related (anesthesia- (items 1, 2) and surgery- (items 4, 5) related items) and two information requirement items (items 3, 6). The score of each item is graded on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely agree). Therefore, the total anxiety score (both anesthesia- and surgery-related in addition to the need for more information) ranges from 6 to 30. A total anxiety score of ≥ 11 has been recommended as the threshold for high anxiety level with clinical significance [29, 30]. Therefore, in this study, the patients were divided into two groups: those with low anxiety (total anxiety score < 11) and those with high anxiety (total anxiety score ≥ 11).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, version 29). In this study, comparison between the low and high anxiety groups was performed using Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. The Mann-Whitney U test was used due to the fact that the continuous variables (age, duration of surgery and duration of surgical consultations) were not normally distributed as shown by performing the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. Following bivariate logistic regression to determine the relationship between each independent variable and the level of anxiety, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the significant independent risk factors for having high level of PA. Multicollinearity has been checked using collinearity diagnostics such as the Tolerance and VIF (Variance Inflation Factor). A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Metric variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation (SD).

Surprisingly, history of psychiatric disorders did not affect the level of anxiety (Table 3). However, the number of previous surgical procedures significantly correlated with the level of anxiety in such a way that the highest level of anxiety was observed when the patient had one or two previous surgical procedures and the lowest level when there was no previous surgery or when the patient had three or more surgical procedures. As expected, a personal history of prior surgical or anesthetic complications was associated with higher anxiety levels, whereas a history of such complications in a family member or close friend was not significantly associated with anxiety (Table 3). Similarly, neither the duration of erectile dysfunction (Table 2) nor the number of partners (Table 3) had a significant effect on the anxiety level.

As shown in Table 3 also, the type of anesthesia had a significant effect on the level of anxiety. Spinal anesthesia was associated with higher levels of PA compared to general anesthesia or even the unawareness of the type of anesthesia. Similarly, the unawareness of the duration of surgery led to the least anxiety whereas the highest level of anxiety was documented in patients who were aware of the duration of the procedure regardless of the exact duration.

In the current study, factors related to pre-operative counseling have significantly affected the PA. For instance, the more time spent on pre-operative counselling was associated with more anxiety (Table 2). However, surprisingly, the use of visual illustrations (drawings, videos, etc.) during surgical consultations did not affect the PA (Table 4). In contrast, the pre-operative use of internet search or speaking to a friend who has undergone the same procedure, to obtain more information, led to a higher degree of anxiety (Table 4).

Although there were several factors which were shown to significantly determine the level of anxiety on univariate analysis, using multivariate analysis, only internet search for further information and personal history of previous surgical or anesthetic complications were demonstrated to be significant independent predictors of the level of the PA in this type of surgery (P < 0.01 for both) (Table 5).

Discussion

In the current study, we investigated, for the first time, the prevalence and determinants of PA in the immediate period before undergoing (PPI). We have shown that more than half of these patients had clinically significant PA. We have also demonstrated that several factors have affected the level of anxiety including personal history of previous surgical or anesthetic complications and the use of internet to seek extra information.

Although the prevalence and determinants of PA have not been previously investigated in patients undergoing (PPI), pre-operative psychological assessment of these patients has been addressed in few studies. In this regard, dos Reis and colleagues had shown that more than one-quarter of patients who were candidates for (PPI) were not psychologically fit to proceed for surgery [31]. Moreover, in a retrospective study, it was shown that patients who has been diagnosed with depression or anxiety psychiatric disorders had increased incidence of post-operative complications [32]. In the current study, we did not address the correlation between PA and the post-operative complications; nevertheless, it might be reasonable to assume such a relationship based on the above-mentioned studies and those which showed increase in the post-operative complications in other types of surgery [33–35]. However, further research is required to address this point. Collectively, these studies and the current onehighlighted the importance of the pre-operative anxiety in (PPI) whether it is an established pre-operative psychiatric disorder or a transient surgery-related anxiety.

In the current study, more than half of the patients had clinically significant PA. The prevalence was comparable to what was previously reported in other types of procedures across different specialties [5, 7, 36–38]. However, a higher prevalence might have been anticipated before PPI, given the unique concerns associated with this procedure, including the implantation of a foreign body, the need to master the technique of inflation and deflation which requires some degree of dexterity, the emotional and sensitive nature of the erectile dysfunction itself and concerns related to patient and partner expectations. This prevalence, which was lower than expected, could be attributed to the high degree of trust in the healthcare system in our set-up [39, 40], which may lead to reduced levels of concern among patients undergoing this type of surgery. Another possibility is the higher proportion of participants with school-only education in our cohort. This group, which forms 67% of our population, had lower levels of PA compared to illiterate patients and university graduates. The lower levels of PA observed in participants with educational attainment at the school level compared to illiterate patients, might be attributed to their enhanced health literacy, proactive engagement with educational interventions, and better access to supportive resources [5, 41]. Nevertheless, certain factors, such as unmet informational needs, may lead to increased anxiety among more educated individuals with university degree [42, 43]. Regardless of the exact reason, the high proportion of participants with school-only degrees in our population and high degree of trust in our healthcare system might explain the lower-than-expected prevalence of pre-operative anxiety in the current study.

In this study, long duration of surgical consultation led to high levels of anxiety. This seems counterintuitive since one might expect that longer consultation time would provide more opportunities for interaction and asking more questions and, hence, reducing the anxiety [44]. One explanation could be that patients perceived longer consultations as indicative of more complex procedures and potential surgical complications; thus, shorter consultations may lead them to believe the surgery is less complex [45]. In this regard, Voogt et al. have indicated that overwhelming patients with excessive information might lead to miscommunication risks [46]. This, in turn, could lead to emotional fatigue and anxiety as observed in the current study. Nevertheless, the findings of this study should not necessarily contradict other studies which showed that effective communication is important for patient satisfaction, care outcomes and helping patients to make informed decisions [47, 48]. Rather, together with these studies, the current study should emphasize that the quality of communication rather than the duration of the surgical consultation is more important in managing patient expectations and alleviating anxiety [49, 50].

Another interesting finding in the current study was that internet search prior to surgery had led to increased anxiety even on multivariate analysis. This is in agreement with the recent study which showed that information pollution in surgical patients may increase the surgical fear [51] although some previous research had shown that seeking information on the internet was associated with a significant decrease in surgery-related anxiety [52]. Collectively, it appears that internet use can increase PA associated with (PPI) particularly when searching is excessive or information quality is poor, and hence, the importance of curated, reliable web-based education and steering patients to trustworthy sources to avoid misinformation effects.

Unexpectedly, the use of visual illustrations during surgical consultations had no effect on PA. It is difficult to ascertain from the current data the exact reason for this finding as the visual tools were thought to improve patient understanding. This could be due to the current wide availability of internet and hence patients might have depended on the internet to seek more visual illustrations regardless of their use during surgical consultation.

Personal history of surgical or anesthetic complication was one of the strong predictors of pre-operative anxiety in the current study. This is expected as patients tend to be afraid that these complications might happen again, especially after this kind of surgery which involves inserting a foreign body into a sensitive body area.

In addition to the post-operative pain and infection which is common in all types of surgery [53–55], patients undergoing (PPI) had concerns which were specific to this procedure. Approximately one quarter of patients were worried about devise failure and 15% were concerned about the inability to activate the devise. Other important concerns included change in penile length, girth and rigidity, self- and partner’s satisfaction and ejaculatory problems. So, despite the surgical consultations and internet derived information, patients had still these bothering concerns emphasizing the need for enhanced patient education and focused support strategies.

Despite the significant findings, this study has some limitations which include its cross-sectional design that might restrict the establishment of causal relationships between variables. Additionally, reliance on self-reported data may introduce biases such as social desirability or recall bias. Moreover, this study did not address the correlation of the PA with post-operative complications and outcome and certainly, further research is required to address this point. The generalizability of our findings to other nations needs to take into consideration the differences in health literacy, healthcare access, hospital settings and patient demographics that could affect PA levels elsewhere.

In conclusion, in this study, we investigated, for the first time, the prevalence and predictors of PA in patients undergoing (PPI). Anxiety is highly prevalent prior to this type of surgery. The use of internet to seek more information and personal history of previous surgical or anesthetic complications are the strongest predictors of the level of anxiety. Patients were mainly worried about the post-operative pain and infections, device failure and the inability to activate the device. These findings highlight the need for coordinated, targeted interventions and tailored support strategies, to minimize anxiety and improve surgical outcomes.
