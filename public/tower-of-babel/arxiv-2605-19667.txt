Convergence of Consensus-Based Particle Methods for Nonconvex Bi-Level Optimization
Yutong Chao, Xudong Sun, Konstantin Riedl, Majid Khadiv, Jalal Etesami

Abstract
In this paper, we study a consensus-based optimization method for nonconvex bi-level optimization, where the objective is to minimize an upper-level function over the set of global minimizers of a lower-level problem. The proposed approach is derivative-free, and constructs its consensus point via smooth quantile selection combined with a Gibbs-type Laplace approximation. We establish convergence guarantees for both the associated mean-field dynamics and its finite-particle approximation. In particular, under suitable assumptions on smooth quantile localization, error bounds, and stability, we show that the mean-field law reaches any arbitrary prescribed Wasserstein neighborhood of the target bi-level solution with an explicit exponential rate up to the hitting time. Numerical experiments on a two-dimensional constrained problem and neural network training further support the theoretical results.



1
Introduction

Bi-level optimization provides a natural framework for hierarchical decision-making problems in which an upper-level objective is optimized subject to the solution of a lower-level optimization problem. It has become a central tool in modern
machine learning, with applications in
meta-learning
Franceschi et al. 2018
;
Kim and Hospedales 2025
, hyperparameter optimization
MacKay et al. 2019
, neural architecture search
Xue et al. 2021
, reinforcement
learning
Gammelli et al. 2023b
;
Gammelli et al. 2023a
, adversarial robustness
Zhang et al. 2022
, model pruning
Zhang et al. 2022
, etc.
In this paper, we study the bi-level optimization problem

\displaystyle\theta^{\star}\in\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}G(\theta),\qquad\Theta:=\arg\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}L(\theta),

(1)

where
L,G:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
are the nonconvex lower- and upper-level objectives, respectively.
This formulation captures situations in which many candidates may solve the lower-level task equally well, while the upper-level objective selects among them according to an
additional criterion, such as robustness, sparsity or
generalization
Petrulionyte et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
.

Despite its simple form, this problem is challenging when the objectives are
nonconvex. The feasible set of the upper-level problem is the solution set

\Theta
of the lower-level problem, which is typically defined only implicitly.
Consequently, it is generally difficult to project onto it, parametrize
it, or optimize over it directly
Trillos et al. 2024
. This difficulty becomes
more pronounced when the lower-level objective
L
is nonconvex, since

\Theta
may be nonconvex, disconnected, or have a complicated geometry. At the
same time, the upper-level objective
G
may also be nonconvex, as is common in
modern machine learning and engineering applications
Fornasier et al. 2021
;
Bae et al. 2022
. Therefore, the
problem considered in this paper is not merely a constrained optimization problem
with a nonconvex feasible set; rather, both the lower-level landscape defining
the feasible set and the upper-level landscape used for selection may be
nonconvex.

Most existing convergence analyses for bi-level optimization that are gradient-based impose structural conditions that simplify this geometry. A standard assumption is that the lower-level objective is strongly convex, which guarantees the uniqueness of the lower-level solution and enables implicit differentiation
Shen et al. 2023
;
Liang et al. 2023
;
Cao et al. 2024
.
Other approaches replace strong convexity with convexity, error-bound conditions, or Polyak–Lojasiewicz (PL)-type conditions
Xiao et al. 2023
;
Huang 2024
.
While these assumptions lead to tractable algorithms with convergence guarantees, they exclude many problems in which
\Theta
is a nonconvex set.
In particular, such assumptions impose a regular global geometry that is not appropriate for many overparameterized or nonsmooth optimization landscapes
Du et al. 2019
;
Cooper 2021
;
Islamov et al. 2024
. This motivates the development of methods that can handle nonconvex
L
and
G
.

Consensus-based optimization (CBO) offers a derivative-free alternative for global optimization in nonconvex landscapes
Totzeck 2021
. Instead of following
gradients, CBO evolves a system of interacting particles that explore the space and communicate through a consensus mechanism. Such methods have been studied at the level of mean-field dynamics
Fornasier et al. 2022
and finite-particle
approximations
Beddrich et al. 2024
. A key theoretical breakthrough was provided by
Fornasier et al. 2024
, which shows that CBO can converge globally for a broad class of nonconvex and nonsmooth objective functions in the mean-field and discrete particle regime.
Their derivative-free nature makes them particularly appealing for bi-level problems in which both objectives may be nonconvex.
Recently, CBO has been adopted to solve bi-level problems of the form (
1
)
Trillos et al. 2024
. However, the method proposed therein, called CB
2
O, relies on a hard quantile-based consensus mapping that is discontinuous (Section
2.2
). As a consequence, convergence guarantees beyond the mean-field approximation are generally not available.

In this work, we instead introduce a soft-quantile consensus-based particle method (Section
2.2
) that maintains strong performance (Section
4
). Given a particle distribution
\rho
, we define a soft lower-level quantile via a smooth selection function (Equation (
6
)). The consensus point is then obtained by applying a Gibbs-type (Laplace) weighting to this measure with respect to the upper-level objective. This construction preserves the two-stage structure of the bi-level problem: the soft-quantile selection identifies particles close to the lower-level solution set, while the Gibbs weighting promotes favorable upper-level candidates. At the same time, the smooth selection mechanism provides sufficient regularity to analyze both the mean-field dynamics and the finite-particle interacting system.
Our main contributions are as follows.

•

We propose a consensus-based particle method, called SCB
2
O, for nonconvex bi-level optimization problems. Unlike many existing convergence analyses for bi-level optimization, our framework does not require the lower-level solution set
\Theta
to be convex, nor does it require either the lower-level or the upper-level objective to be convex or to satisfy a PL condition. Instead, the method relies on a soft-quantile mechanism to identify particles near the lower-level solution set and a weighting to select favorable upper-level candidates.

•

We provide a quantitative convergence analysis for the finite-particle dynamics (Section
3
). In particular, we establish a localized high-probability finite-particle approximation bound for the particle estimator, obtained by combining moment-dependent consensus bounds, a cutoff-event propagation-of-chaos estimate, and finite-sample concentration. This extends existing mean-field convergence results for CBO-based bi-level optimization.

1.1
Related Work

Bi-level optimization.

A large body of work has studied convergence guarantees for bi-level optimization
under various curvature and smoothness assumptions. Most existing
analyses impose strong structural conditions on the lower-level problem to simplify the geometry of the lower-level solution set
Shen et al. 2023
;
Liang et al. 2023
;
Cao et al. 2024
.
Consequently, such settings do not fully capture bi-level problems whose lower-level solution set

\Theta

is nonconvex, disconnected, or geometrically complex.

Several recent works have attempted to relax these requirements. One line of work weakens the global PL condition to a local PL condition near the lower-level solution
Masiha et al. 2025
. Under suitable regularity assumptions, this allows
\Theta
to be characterized as a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
embedded submanifold of the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
without boundary.
However, the resulting convergence guarantees typically characterize convergence toward an invariant distribution and do not directly quantify the distance between the algorithmic output and the true bi-level solution.
Another line of work reformulates the bi-level problem as a single-level unconstrained problem by combining the lower-level and upper-level objectives through an adaptive penalty or weighting parameter
Cabot 2005
;
Dutta and Pandit 2020
. This approach is algorithmically attractive because it avoids explicitly solving the lower-level problem at each iteration. Nevertheless, the theoretical guarantees for such reformulations usually rely on convexity or strong regularity assumptions to ensure that the penalized objective faithfully represents the original bi-level structure.

A related class of methods approximates the lower-level solution set through sublevel-set relaxations and then applies classical gradient-based methods to the resulting constrained or regularized problem
Beck and Sabach 2014
;
Jiang et al. 2023
. These approaches can provide stability and convergence guarantees when the lower-level objective is convex or when the sublevel sets have favorable geometry. However, when
\Theta
is a general nonconvex set, sublevel-set approximations may be loose or geometrically misleading.
In contrast, we impose no convexity assumptions on the lower-level solution set
\Theta
or on the objectives, nor do we require a PL condition, and we establish high-probability convergence guarantees for the finite-particle system.

Consensus-based optimization (CBO).
It is a multi-agent, derivative-free optimization framework originally introduced in
Pinnau et al. 2017
.
It has demonstrated strong empirical performance on challenging nonconvex and nonsmooth optimization problems. Due to its success in practice, it has attracted significant theoretical interest, and a growing body of work has established convergence guarantees for CBO both at the level of the mean-field regime
Borghi et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2024
and in the finite-particle regime
Fornasier et al. 2021
;
Fornasier et al. 2024
.
These methods have also been applied to various practical problems, including phase retrieval
Carrillo et al. 2021
, robust subspace detection
Carrillo et al. 2023
, robotics
Sun et al. 2026
, federated learning
Carrillo et al. 2024
, and adversarial attacks
Roith et al. 2025
;
García Trillos et al. 2025
.

The CBO framework has been extended in several directions beyond standard single-objective optimization. For instance, CBO-type methods have been developed for multi-objective optimization
Herty et al. 2025
, random mini-batch sampling-based
optimization
Carrillo et al. 2022
, zero-sum game
Huang and Warnett 2026
and multiplayer game
Chenchene et al. 2025
. These extensions
illustrate the flexibility of consensus-based particle dynamics and their ability to incorporate different structural features of modern optimization problems. Among these developments, the work
Trillos et al. 2024
proposes a CBO-type algorithm for nonconvex bi-level optimization.
Their method employs a consensus mapping based on a hard selection rule defined via a quantile of the lower-level objective values. The authors establish convergence guarantees for the corresponding mean-field dynamics. However, they also show that, due to discontinuity of their consensus mapping, standard Lipschitz-based propagation-of-chaos and numerical stability arguments do not directly apply to the hard-threshold consensus map.
Here, we address this limitation by introducing a novel soft consensus mapping and establishing high-probability convergence guarantees for the finite-particle system.

2
Problem Setting

We consider the bi-level optimization problem in (
1
), for which, we denote the optimum value of the lower-level by
L_{\min}:=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}L(\theta)
and introduce the following notations for a given
r>0
,
N_{r}(\Theta):=\big\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\operatorname{dist}(\theta,\Theta)\leq r\big\}
and
B_{r}(\theta^{\star}):=\big\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\lVert\theta-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}\leq r\big\}
.
We also denote by
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the set of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and by
\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the subset consisting of those with finite
k
-th moments. We use
[N]:=\{1,\cdots,N\}
.

2.1
Particle Systems and Diffusion Structure

Suppose we are interested in minimizing the upper-level objective
G(\theta)
without considering any lower-level problem, i.e.,

\arg\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(\theta).

In this case, the central idea of CBO is to introduce a population of agents (particles) that explore the space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
according to a prescribed stochastic dynamics. These agents are designed to interact in a way that gradually concentrates their distribution, ultimately leading them to reach consensus at the global minimizer of
G
.
More precisely, let
\{\Theta^{1},\ldots,\Theta^{N}\}
denote a finite collection of agents that explore the domain and collectively form a global consensus on the solution.
Each agent
i\in[N]
is modeled as a stochastic process, i.e.,
\Theta^{i}=(\Theta^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with
\Theta^{i}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose time evolution is governed by the following SDE:

\Theta_{0}^{i}\sim\rho_{0},\quad\,\mathrm{d}\Theta^{i}_{t}=-\lambda\cdot\big(\Theta^{i}_{t}-m(\rho^{N}_{t})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma D\big(\Theta^{i}_{t}-m(\rho^{N}_{t})\big)\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t},

(2)

where
\lambda,\sigma>0
are user-specific parameters and
\{(W_{t}^{i})_{t\geq 0}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are independent standard Brownian motions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The agents are initially distributed according to a common law
\rho_{0}
, and their empirical distribution at time
t
is given by
\rho^{N}_{t}:=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\delta_{\Theta_{t}^{i}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The mapping
m(\cdot)
is a functional that computes a consensus point among the agents in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
from their distribution.
In this SDE, the first term is the
drift
towards the instantaneous consensus
m(\rho^{N}_{t})
, which is computed as a weighted average of all agents and serves as an instantaneous proxy for the global minimizer
\theta^{\star}
,

m(\rho^{N}_{t}):=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\exp({-\alpha G(x)})\,\,\mathrm{d}\rho^{N}_{t}(x)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp({-\alpha G(x)})\,\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}^{N}(x)}=\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\Theta_{t}^{i}\,\exp({-\alpha G(\Theta_{t}^{i}}))}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\exp({-\alpha G(\Theta_{t}^{j})})}.

(3)

Such a consensus is motivated by the well-known Laplace principle
Miller 2006
and can be interpreted as an approximation of the discrete minimization problem

\arg\min_{i\in[N]}G(\Theta_{t}^{i}),

which becomes increasingly accurate as
\alpha\to\infty
, provided that a minimizer exists.
The second term randomly diffuses agents according to a scaled Brownian motion and offers exploration over the domain. For a given
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, two commonly studied diffusion types are the isotropic diffusion
D(z)=\|z\|_{2}I_{d}

Pinnau et al. 2017
;
Carrillo et al. 2018
and the anisotropic diffusion
D(z)=\mathrm{diag}(z)

Carrillo et al. 2021
. In this paper, we adopt the isotropic diffusion, thus,

D(z)D(z)^{\top}=\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}^{2}I_{d},\quad\operatorname{Tr}\big(D(z)D(z)^{\top}\big)=d\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}^{2},\quad\left\lVert D(z)-D(y)\right\rVert_{F}\leq\sqrt{d}\left\lVert z-y\right\rVert_{2}.

(4)

Ideally, as a result of the above mechanism, the agents should eventually reach a near-optimal global consensus, i.e.,
\rho_{t}^{N}
is expected to converge over time to a Dirac mass concentrated at
\arg\min_{\theta}G(\theta)
.

2.2
Soft Consensus-Based Bi-Level Optimization (SCB
2
O)

In this section, we introduce a formulation of CBO for solving the bi-level optimization problem in (
1
).
We build on the framework developed by
Trillos et al. 2024
, referred to as CB
2
O, and introduce a new method, called SCB
2
O, for which, in contrast to CB
2
O, we are able to provide theoretical guarantees for the finite-particle system.
The central idea is to consider an interacting system of particles, analogous to the original CBO framework
Carrillo et al. 2023
;
Pinnau et al. 2017
, which explores the domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. However, unlike standard CBO, both CB
2
O and our SCB
2
O compute the consensus among the particles at time
t
as follows.

CB
2
O computes the consensus point
m(\rho_{t}^{N})
in (
2
) at time
t
, first by evaluating the lower-level objective function
L
for each of the
N
particles, i.e.,
\{L(\Theta_{t}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and then sorting the resulting values in non-decreasing order, i.e.,

L(\Theta_{t}^{\pi(1)})\leq L(\Theta_{t}^{\pi(2)})\leq\cdots\leq L(\Theta_{t}^{\pi(N)}),

where
\Theta_{t}^{\pi(i)}
denotes the position of the particle with the
i
-th smallest lower-level objective value at time
t
.
Next, CB
2
O selects the subset of particles from the sorted list with smallest values and computes the consensus using this subset. More precisely, for a parameter
\beta\in(0,1]
, let
\lceil\beta N\rceil
denote the ceiling of
\beta N
, and define the
\beta
-quantile of the set
\{L(\Theta_{t}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
as

q^{L}_{\beta}[\rho_{t}^{N}]=L\big(\Theta_{t}^{\pi(\lceil\beta N\rceil)}\big).

In words,
q^{L}_{\beta}[\rho_{t}^{N}]
corresponds to the value of the lower-level function
L
evaluated at the particle whose rank is such that exactly
\lceil\beta N\rceil
particles have smaller lower-level objective values. The consensus point is then computed by

m_{\alpha,\beta}^{G,L}(\rho_{t}^{N}):=\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\lceil\beta N\rceil}\Theta_{t}^{\pi(i)}\,\exp({-\alpha G(\Theta_{t}^{\pi(i)})})}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\lceil\beta N\rceil}\exp({-\alpha G(\Theta_{t}^{\pi(j)})})}=\frac{\sum\limits_{i:\,L(\Theta_{t}^{i})\leq q_{\beta}^{L}[\rho_{t}^{N}]}\Theta_{t}^{i}\,\exp({-\alpha G(\Theta_{t}^{i})})}{\sum\limits_{j:\,L(\Theta_{t}^{j})\leq q_{\beta}^{L}[\rho_{t}^{N}]}\exp({-\alpha G(\Theta_{t}^{j})})}.

(5)

It is noteworthy that the above definition of consensus is based on hard thresholding, i.e., it ignores
N-\lceil\beta N\rceil
particles. This introduces a discontinuity in the consensus mapping and, consequently, in the dynamics. As a result, standard Lipschitz-based propagation-of-chaos and numerical stability arguments do not directly apply to the finite-particle system. To highlight this issue, as also discussed in Section 3.2 of
Trillos et al. 2024
, there exist settings in which two measures
\rho
and
\tilde{\rho}
are close in the Wasserstein sense, i.e.,
W_{2}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})=c
, but

\|m_{\alpha,\beta}^{G,L}(\tilde{\rho})-m_{\alpha,\beta}^{G,L}(\rho)\|_{2}=c_{\alpha},

where
c_{\alpha}
is a constant that depends on the hyperparameter
\alpha
but is independent of
c
.

In this work, we introduce a soft consensus mapping to overcome this limitation, allowing us to derive convergence guarantees in both the mean-field and more importantly, the finite-particle settings to find the solution to (
1
).
Formally, let
\psi(\cdot)
denote a selection function that satisfies Assumption
2.1
.
We define the soft
\beta
-quantile of
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
q^{L}_{\beta,\tau}[\rho]
, as the unique solution (see Lemma
2.2
) of

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi\left(\frac{q-L(\theta)}{\tau}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta)=\beta.

(6)

Assumption 2.1
.

The selector
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to(0,1)
is of class
C^{1}
, strictly increasing with
\psi^{\prime}(z)>0
for every
z\in\mathbb{R}
, and Lipschitz continuous; that is, there exists a constant
L_{\psi}>0
such that

|\psi(u)-\psi(v)|\leq L_{\psi}|u-v|,
for all
u,v\in\mathbb{R}.

Moreover,
\lim_{u\to\infty}\psi(u)=1
,
\lim_{u\to-\infty}\psi(u)=0
, and there exist constants
c_{-},C_{-}>0
such that, for all
z\geq 0
,

c_{-}e^{-z}\leq\psi(-z)\leq C_{-}e^{-z}.

(7)

Remark 1
.

An example of
\psi(\cdot)
is the sigmoid (softmax) function which continuously approximates a step function.
Note that, when
\tau\to 0
,
\psi(\cdot/\tau)
behaves like a step function, and the above equation becomes finding the
q
such that
\int_{L(\theta)\leq q}1\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta)=\beta
. In this case,
q^{L}_{\beta,\tau}[\rho_{t}^{N}]
is equivalent to the CB
2
O’s
\beta
-quantile,
q^{L}_{\beta}[\rho_{t}^{N}]
.

Lemma 2.2

(Existence and uniqueness of the soft quantile)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, for every
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, Equation (
6
) has a unique solution.

All detailed proofs are presented in Appendix
B
.
When no confusion arises, we simplify the notation by writing
q_{\rho}:=q^{L}_{\beta,\tau}[\rho]
. Let

\eta_{\rho,\tau}(\theta):=\psi\Big(\frac{q_{\rho}-L(\theta)}{\tau}\Big),\qquad J^{L}_{\beta,\tau}[\rho](\!\,\mathrm{d}\theta):=\eta_{\rho,\tau}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta).

(8)

Note that by the definition of the soft quantile, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}J^{L}_{\beta,\tau}[\rho](\!\,\mathrm{d}\theta)=\beta.

Given the above definitions, we define our
soft consensus mapping
for a measure
\rho
as

m^{G,L}_{\alpha,\beta,\tau}(\rho)=\frac{A(\rho)}{B(\rho)}:=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\theta w_{\alpha}(\theta)\,\,\mathrm{d}J^{L}_{\beta,\tau}[\rho](\theta)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w_{\alpha}(\theta)\,\,\mathrm{d}J^{L}_{\beta,\tau}[\rho](\theta)},

(9)

where
\alpha>0
,
\beta\in(0,1)
, and
\tau>0
.
The interacting particle system with the above soft consensus mapping is given by

\,\mathrm{d}\Theta^{i,N}_{t,\tau}=-\lambda\cdot\big(\Theta^{i,N}_{t,\tau}-m^{G,L}_{\alpha,\beta,\tau}(\rho^{N}_{t,\tau})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma D\big(\Theta^{i,N}_{t,\tau}-m^{G,L}_{\alpha,\beta,\tau}(\rho^{N}_{t,\tau})\big)\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t},\quad i\in[N],

(10)

where

\rho^{N}_{t,\tau}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\Theta^{i,N}_{t,\tau}}.

Here,
\{(\Theta^{i,N}_{t,\tau})_{t\geq 0}\}_{i=1}^{N}
denote the
N
agents (particles) at different times for a fixed parameter
\tau
.
Next, we study the setting where the number of agents tends to infinity, called the mean-field regime.

Mean-field Approximation.

A theoretical analysis of the SCB
2
O dynamics can be performed either at the microscopic level of the interacting particle system (
10
), e.g.,
Bellavia and Malaspina 2025
, or, following recent works
Fornasier et al. 2022
;
Grassi et al. 2023
;
Carrillo et al. 2021
, at the macroscopic level via the mean-field limit describing the evolution of the agent density, or through a combination of both approaches. The mean-field system is given by

\,\mathrm{d}\Theta_{t,\tau}\!=\!-\!\lambda\cdot\big(\Theta_{t,\tau}\!-\!m^{G,L}_{\alpha,\beta,\tau}(\rho_{t,\tau})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma D\big(\Theta_{t,\tau}\!-\!m^{G,L}_{\alpha,\beta,\tau}(\rho_{t,\tau})\big)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad\rho_{t,\tau}:=\operatorname{Law}(\Theta_{t,\tau}).

(11)

The mean-field approach enables the use of more flexible analytical tools to characterize the average behavior of the agents through the evolution of the measure
(\rho_{t,\tau})_{t\geq 0}
which is described by the Fokker–Planck equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t,\tau}

\displaystyle=\lambda\,\mathrm{div}\!\left(\big(\theta\!-\!m^{G,L}_{\alpha,\beta,\tau}(\rho_{t,\tau})\big)\rho_{t,\tau}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\!\sum_{k=1}^{d}\partial_{kk}^{2}\left([D(\theta-m^{G,L}_{\alpha,\beta,\tau}(\rho_{t,\tau}))D(\theta-m^{G,L}_{\alpha,\beta,\tau}(\rho_{t,\tau}))^{\top}]_{kk}\rho_{t,\tau}\right).

(12)

For every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we assume that one can realize i.i.d. realizations
\bar{\Theta}^{1}_{t,\tau},\ldots,\bar{\Theta}^{N}_{t,\tau}

on the same probability
\operatorname{Law}(\bar{\Theta}^{i}_{t,\tau})=\rho_{t,\tau}
, for
t\in[0,T]
, driven by the same
initial data and Brownian motions, i.e.,

\bar{\Theta}^{i}_{0,\tau}\!=\!\Theta^{i,N}_{0,\tau},

for
i\!\in\![N]
.
We can then define the corresponding empirical measure using the realizations by

\bar{\rho}^{N}_{t,\tau}\!:=\!\frac{1}{N}\!\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\bar{\Theta}^{i}_{t,\tau}}.

However, a purely mean-field analysis may be less representative in finite-agent settings. In this work, we provide both the mean-field and finite-particle analysis.

Discrete Dynamic.

The discrete dynamic of the above SDE is described by the Euler–Maruyama
Higham 2001
scheme with
K\Delta t=T
and it is given by

\theta^{i}_{k+1,\tau}=\theta^{i}_{k,\tau}-\lambda\Delta t\big(\theta^{i}_{k,\tau}-m^{G,L}_{\alpha,\beta,\tau}(\rho^{N}_{k,\tau})\big)+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,D\big(\theta^{i}_{k,\tau}-m^{G,L}_{\alpha,\beta,\tau}(\rho^{N}_{k,\tau})\big)B^{i}_{k},\quad i\in[N],

(13)

where
(B_{k}^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
are i.i.d. standard normal random vectors, each distributed as
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{Id})
. The corresponding empirical measure is defined by

\rho^{N}_{k,\tau}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\theta^{i}_{k,\tau}}.

Although this dynamic is the one used in practice to solve the problem in (
1
), no theoretical convergence guarantees had been established prior to this work. In this work, under a set of mild assumptions (discussed below) that are frequently satisfied in real-world settings, we prove its convergence.

Assumption 2.3
.

We assume that the bi-level solution
\theta^{\star}\in\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}G(\theta)

is unique and the following conditions hold.

I.

Lower-level objective.

Function
L
is globally Lipschitz with constant
L_{L}
. The global minimum value is

L_{\min}:=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}L(x)
and
\Theta
is nonempty.
We assume that
L
is bounded from above: there exists
L_{\max}<\infty
such that

L_{\min}\leq L(\theta)\leq L_{\max}
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Moreover, there exist
\eta_{L}>0
,
\nu_{L}>0
, and
L_{\infty}>0
such that

\operatorname{dist}(\theta,\Theta)\leq\eta_{L}^{-1}\big(L(\theta)-L_{\min}\big)^{\nu_{L}}\qquad\text{whenever }L(\theta)-L_{\min}\leq L_{\infty}.

(14)

II.

Upper-level objective.

Function
G
is globally Lipschitz with constant
L_{G}
and bounded: there exist constants
G_{\min},G_{\max}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

G_{\min}\leq G(\theta)\leq G_{\max}
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

III.

Upper-level local growth near lower-level minimizers.

There exists
R_{G}>0
such that, for every
r_{G}\in(0,R_{G}]
, there is

\widetilde{\theta}_{r_{G}}\in B_{r_{G}}(\theta^{\star})
satisfying

G(\widetilde{\theta}_{r_{G}})=\min_{\theta\in N_{r_{G}}(\Theta)}G(\theta).

Moreover, there exist
\eta_{G}>0
,
\nu_{G}>0
, and
G_{\infty}>0
, independent of
r_{G}
, such that

V.

Mean-field well-posedness.

For every finite horizon

and every fixed parameter tuple

, the mean-field SDE
(

(\Theta_{t,\tau})_{t\in[0,T]}

with continuous paths and law

\rho_{t,\tau}:=\operatorname{Law}(\Theta_{t,\tau})\in C([0,T],\mathcal{P}_{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

Moreover,

Remark 2
.

Assumption
2.3
I. is called
Hölderian error bound
and it imposes that the objective is locally
h_{L}
-Hölder continuous around
\Theta
. It ensures that the lower-level values on suitable sublevel sets provide quantitative information about the distance of the corresponding points to the set of global minimizers. This condition appears in
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Cao et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024

Remark 3

(Only the derived estimates are essential)
.

The assumptions
G\leq G_{\max}
and
L\leq L_{\max}
serve as sufficient, but not necessary, conditions for the convergence proof. In the analysis, the condition
G\leq G_{\max}
is utilized exclusively through the consensus-moment estimate (see (
18
)), and
L\leq L_{\max}
only via the inverse-stability estimate (see (
30
)). Consequently, the results extend to any set of assumptions that yield these two estimates.

Remark 4

(Boundedness by objective-value clipping)
.

The boundedness assumptions on the lower- and upper-level objectives are
sufficient conditions for the proof. In practice, one often has a priori admissible objective levels obtained from a feasible or reference solution.
One may therefore replace the original objectives by their capped versions,
G_{\rm cap}(x):=\min\{G(x),G_{\max}\},L_{\rm cap}(x):=\min\{L(x),L_{\max}\}
.
Equivalently, whenever an evaluated objective value exceeds the prescribed upper level, it can be clipped to that level.

Remark 5
.

Assumption
2.3
III. is called inverse continuity condition. It states that, inside
N_{r_{G}}(\Theta)
, points with
upper-level objective value close to the local minimum value

G(\tilde{\theta}_{r_{G}})
must also be close in distance to

\tilde{\theta}_{r_{G}}
. Equivalently, the upper-level objective gap

G(\theta)-G(\tilde{\theta}_{r_{G}})
quantitatively controls the distance from
\theta
to the selected local minimizer
\tilde{\theta}_{r_{G}}
. This prevents the upper-level objective from having flat low-value regions, far from

\tilde{\theta}_{r_{G}}
, inside the relevant lower-level neighborhood. This type of condition appears in
Fornasier et al. 2021
;
Fornasier et al. 2024
;
Riedl 2024
.

Assumption
2.3
V is used as a standing well-posedness condition for the mean-field dynamics. We do not prove mean-field well-posedness in full generality. Instead, this assumption allows us to focus on the convergence mechanism induced by the soft-quantile consensus map. Related well-posedness results have been established for standard CBO and CB
2
O under comparable regularity assumptions
Fornasier et al. 2024
;
Trillos et al. 2024
.

5
Conclusion

We studied a bi-level optimization problem with nonconvex lower- and upper-level objectives and proposed a derivative-free particle-based method, SCB
2
O, based on a soft consensus mapping rule.
The prior method CB
2
O employs a hard consensus rule and demonstrates strong empirical performance in finite-particle regimes. However, due to the discontinuity of its mapping, standard Lipschitz-based finite-particle and numerical stability arguments do not directly apply.
Our approach addresses this gap by introducing a smooth consensus rule parameterized by
\tau
, which recovers the hard mapping as
\tau
tends to zero. We established a localized high-probability finite-particle approximation bound for SCB
2
O and showed empirically that it performs comparably to CB
2
O for sufficiently small, but non-vanishing, values of
\tau
.

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi\left(\frac{q_{\nu}-L(x)}{\tau}\right)\nu(\,\mathrm{d}x)=\beta.

(27)

Because
L(x)\geq L_{\min}
, one has
q_{\nu}-L(x)\leq q_{\nu}-L_{\min}
for every
x
. Since
\psi
is increasing,

\psi\left(\frac{q_{\nu}-L(x)}{\tau}\right)\leq\psi\left(\frac{q_{\nu}-L_{\min}}{\tau}\right).

Integrating and using (
27
) gives

\beta\leq\psi\left(\frac{q_{\nu}-L_{\min}}{\tau}\right).

Applying the increasing inverse
\psi^{-1}
yields

z_{\beta}\leq\frac{q_{\nu}-L_{\min}}{\tau},

which proves the lower bound in (
26
). Similarly, because
L(x)\leq L_{\max}
,

q_{\nu}-L(x)\geq q_{\nu}-L_{\max}.

Thus

\psi\left(\frac{q_{\nu}-L(x)}{\tau}\right)\geq\psi\left(\frac{q_{\nu}-L_{\max}}{\tau}\right).

Integrating gives

\beta\geq\psi\left(\frac{q_{\nu}-L_{\max}}{\tau}\right).

Applying
\psi^{-1}
gives

z_{\beta}\geq\frac{q_{\nu}-L_{\max}}{\tau},

which proves
q_{\nu}\leq L_{\max}+\tau z_{\beta}
.
∎

Corollary B.3
.

Let

c_{\psi}:=\inf_{z\in\left[z_{\beta}-\frac{L_{\max}-L_{\min}}{\tau},\ z_{\beta}+\frac{L_{\max}-L_{\min}}{\tau}\right]}\psi^{\prime}(z),\qquad\kappa:=\frac{c_{\psi}}{\tau}.

(28)

Then
c_{\psi}>0
,
\kappa>0
, and, for every
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and every
q
lying between
q_{\nu}
and
q_{\mu}
,

\left(F_{\nu}(q)-F_{\nu}(q_{\nu})\right)\left(q-q_{\nu}\right)\geq\kappa\left\lvert q-q_{\nu}\right\rvert^{2}.

(29)

Proof.

The interval in (
28
) is compact. Since
\psi^{\prime}(z)>0
for every
z\in\mathbb{R}
and
\psi^{\prime}
is continuous, its infimum over this compact interval is strictly positive. Hence
c_{s}>0
and
\kappa>0
.

Let
q
lie between
q_{\nu}
and
q_{\mu}
. By Lemma
B.2
, both
q_{\nu}
and
q_{\mu}
belong to
[L_{\min}+\tau z_{\beta},L_{\max}+\tau z_{\beta}]
. Therefore
q
also belongs to this interval. Since
L_{\min}\leq L(x)\leq L_{\max}
, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

z_{\beta}-\frac{L_{\max}-L_{\min}}{\tau}\leq\frac{q-L(x)}{\tau}\leq z_{\beta}+\frac{L_{\max}-L_{\min}}{\tau}.

By the definition of
c_{\psi}
,

\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{q-L(x)}{\tau}\right)\geq c_{\psi},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Using (
25
),

\dot{F}_{\nu}(q)=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{q-L(x)}{\tau}\right)\nu(\,\mathrm{d}x)\geq\frac{c_{\psi}}{\tau}=\kappa.

If
q\geq q_{\nu}
, then

F_{\nu}(q)-F_{\nu}(q_{\nu})=\int_{q_{\nu}}^{q}\dot{F}_{\nu}(r)\,\mathrm{d}r\geq\kappa(q-q_{\nu}).

Multiplying by
q-q_{\nu}\geq 0
gives (
29
). If
q\leq q_{\nu}
, then

F_{\nu}(q_{\nu})-F_{\nu}(q)=\int_{q}^{q_{\nu}}\dot{F}_{\nu}(r)\,\mathrm{d}r\geq\kappa(q_{\nu}-q).

Equivalently,

F_{\nu}(q)-F_{\nu}(q_{\nu})\leq-\kappa(q_{\nu}-q).

Because
q-q_{\nu}\leq 0
, multiplying both sides by
q-q_{\nu}
reverses the inequality and gives

\left(F_{\nu}(q)-F_{\nu}(q_{\nu})\right)\left(q-q_{\nu}\right)\geq\kappa(q_{\nu}-q)^{2}=\kappa\left\lvert q-q_{\nu}\right\rvert^{2}.

Thus (
29
) holds in both cases.
∎

Lemma B.4
.

Under Assumption
2.3
, for every
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\left\lvert q_{\mu}-q_{\nu}\right\rvert\leq\kappa^{-1}\left\lvert F_{\mu}(q_{\nu})-F_{\nu}(q_{\nu})\right\rvert.

(30)

Proof.

Because
q_{\mu}
and
q_{\nu}
solve their soft-quantile equations,

F_{\mu}(q_{\mu})=0,\qquad F_{\nu}(q_{\nu})=0.

Therefore, we obtain

\left\lvert F_{\mu}(q_{\nu})-F_{\nu}(q_{\nu})\right\rvert=\left\lvert F_{\mu}(q_{\nu})\right\rvert.

(31)

If
q_{\nu}\leq q_{\mu}
, then Corollary
B.3
, applied to the measure
\mu
with
q=q_{\nu}
, gives

\left(F_{\mu}(q_{\nu})-F_{\mu}(q_{\mu})\right)\left(q_{\nu}-q_{\mu}\right)\geq\kappa(q_{\mu}-q_{\nu})^{2}.

Since
F_{\mu}(q_{\mu})=0
and
q_{\nu}-q_{\mu}\leq 0
, this is equivalent to

-F_{\mu}(q_{\nu})(q_{\mu}-q_{\nu})\geq\kappa(q_{\mu}-q_{\nu})^{2}.

If
q_{\mu}=q_{\nu}
, the desired estimate is trivial. If
q_{\mu}>q_{\nu}
, dividing by
q_{\mu}-q_{\nu}>0
gives

-F_{\mu}(q_{\nu})\geq\kappa(q_{\mu}-q_{\nu}).

Hence

\left\lvert F_{\mu}(q_{\nu})\right\rvert\geq\kappa\left\lvert q_{\mu}-q_{\nu}\right\rvert.

If
q_{\nu}>q_{\mu}
, the same corollary applied to the measure
\mu
with
q=q_{\nu}
gives

\left(F_{\mu}(q_{\nu})-F_{\mu}(q_{\mu})\right)\left(q_{\nu}-q_{\mu}\right)\geq\kappa(q_{\nu}-q_{\mu})^{2}.

Since
F_{\mu}(q_{\mu})=0
and
q_{\nu}-q_{\mu}>0
, division by
q_{\nu}-q_{\mu}
gives

F_{\mu}(q_{\nu})\geq\kappa(q_{\nu}-q_{\mu}).

Again,
\left\lvert F_{\mu}(q_{\nu})\right\rvert\geq\kappa\left\lvert q_{\mu}-q_{\nu}\right\rvert.

Combining both cases with (
31
) proves (
30
).
∎

Remark 7
.

The later coupling proof uses the boundedness of
L
only through the inverse-stability estimate (
30
). Consequently, the same mean-field approximation and numerical convergence results remain valid if the assumption
L\leq L_{\max}
is replaced by any other condition implying (
30
) with a fixed positive constant
\kappa
. Likewise, the fourth-moment argument uses the upper bound on
G
only through Corollary
B.1
. Therefore the truly essential derived inputs for the rest of the proof are (
18
) and (
30
). The boundedness assumptions on
G
and

are a simple sufficient route to obtain these estimates without circular reasoning.

We define the uniform denominator constant and the moment-dependent numerator bound by

b_{G}:=\beta e^{-\alpha G_{\max}},\qquad a(R_{4}):=\beta^{1/2}e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}R_{4}^{1/4}.

(32)

Lemma B.5
.

For every
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

B(\rho)\geq b_{G},

(33)

where
B(\rho)
is given in (
20
).
In addition, if
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{4}\rho(\,\mathrm{d}x)\leq R_{4},

(34)

then

\left\lVert A(\rho)\right\rVert_{2}\leq a(R_{4}).

(35)

Consequently,

\left\lVert m(\rho)\right\rVert_{2}=\frac{\left\lVert A(\rho)\right\rVert_{2}}{B(\rho)}\leq b_{G}^{-1}a(R_{4})

(36)

whenever (
34
) holds.

Proof.

Let
J:=J_{\beta,\tau}^{L}[\rho]
. Since
G(x)\leq G_{\max}
,

\displaystyle B(\rho)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-\alpha G(x)}J(\,\mathrm{d}x)\geq e^{-\alpha G_{\max}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}J(\,\mathrm{d}x)=e^{-\alpha G_{\max}}J(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\beta e^{-\alpha G_{\max}}=b_{G}.

This proves (

).
Assume now (

). Since

,

\displaystyle\left\lVert A(\rho)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}xe^{-\alpha G(x)}J(\,\mathrm{d}x)\right\rVert_{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}e^{-\alpha G(x)}J(\,\mathrm{d}x)

(37)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}J(\,\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}J(\,\mathrm{d}x)\right)^{1/2}J(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\beta^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}J(\,\mathrm{d}x)\right)^{1/2}.

(38)

\left\lVert A(\rho)\right\rVert_{2}\leq\beta^{1/2}e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}R_{4}^{1/4}=a(R_{4}),

which proves (

m=A/B
, (
33

35

B.4
Cutoff event and stopped-process inequality

For
M>0
, set

X_{t}^{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\max\left\{\left\lVert\Theta_{t,\tau}^{i,N}\right\rVert_{2}^{4},\left\lVert\bar{\Theta}_{t,\tau}^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}\right\},\quad M_{T}:=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}X_{t}^{N},\quad\Omega_{M}:=\left\{M_{T}\leq M\right\}.

(39)

Define the cutoff time

\tau_{M}:=\inf\left\{t\in[0,T]:X_{t}^{N}>M\right\},

(40)

with the convention
\tau_{M}=+\infty
if the set is empty. Define the cutoff indicator

I_{M}(t):=\mathbf{1}_{\{t<\tau_{M}\}}.

(41)

Since the particle paths and the synchronously coupled mean-field paths are continuous,
X_{t}^{N}
is continuous. Therefore, on
\{I_{M}(t)=1\}
, one has

X_{t}^{N}\leq M.

(42)

Moreover, for every
t\in[0,T]
,

\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{M}}\leq I_{M}(t).

(43)

Indeed, if
\Omega_{M}
holds, then
X_{s}^{N}\leq M
for every
s\in[0,T]
, so the set in (
40
) is empty and
\tau_{M}=+\infty
.

Lemma B.6
.

Let
\tau
be a stopping time and define

I_{\tau}(t):=\mathbf{1}_{\{t<\tau\}},
for
t\in[0,T].

Let
Y=(Y_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be a nonnegative continuous adapted process with

Y_{0}=0
. Suppose that there exist a progressively measurable process

f=(f_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
satisfying

\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\bigl[|f_{t}|I_{\tau}(t)\bigr]\,dt<\infty,

and a true martingale
(\mathcal{N}_{t\wedge\tau})_{t\in[0,T]}
with

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{t\wedge\tau}]=0
, such that, for every
t\in[0,T]
,

Y_{t\wedge\tau}\leq\int_{0}^{t}I_{\tau}(s)f_{s}\,ds+\mathcal{N}_{t\wedge\tau}\qquad\text{a.s.}

Then, for every
t\in[0,T]
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[Y_{t}I_{\tau}(t)\bigr]\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\bigl[f_{s}I_{\tau}(s)\bigr]\,ds.

Proof.

Since
Y_{t}\geq 0
, we have the pathwise inequality:

Y_{t}I_{\tau}(t)\leq Y_{t\wedge\tau}.

Indeed, if
t<\tau
, then
t\wedge\tau=t
, so

Y_{t}I_{\tau}(t)=Y_{t}=Y_{t\wedge\tau}.

If
t\geq\tau
, then
I_{\tau}(t)=0
, and hence

Y_{t}I_{\tau}(t)=0\leq Y_{t\wedge\tau},

because
Y
is nonnegative.
Taking expectations in (
B.6
) gives

\mathbb{E}[Y_{t\wedge\tau}]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}I_{\tau}(s)f_{s}\,ds\right]+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{t\wedge\tau}].

By the true martingale assumption,

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{N}_{t\wedge\tau}]=0.

Moreover, the integrability assumption and Fubini’s theorem imply

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}I_{\tau}(s)f_{s}\,ds\right]=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\bigl[f_{s}I_{\tau}(s)\bigr]\,ds.

Therefore

\mathbb{E}[Y_{t\wedge\tau}]\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\bigl[f_{s}I_{\tau}(s)\bigr]\,ds.

Combining this estimate with

\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}I_{\tau}(t)]\leq\mathbb{E}[Y_{t\wedge\tau}]

proves (
B.6
).
∎

Lemma B.7
.

Assume Assumption
2.3
. Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
. For

\mathbf{x}=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{dN},

define

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{N,2}:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|x^{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2},\qquad\mu_{\mathbf{x}}^{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x^{i}}.

Set

Q_{N}(\mathbf{x}):=q_{\beta,\tau}^{L}[\mu_{\mathbf{x}}^{N}],

and

\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x}):=m(\mu_{\mathbf{x}}^{N}).

Then the following two properties hold. First, for every
R>0
, there exists a finite constant

L_{\mathfrak{m},R,N}=L_{\mathfrak{m},R,N}(\alpha,\beta,\tau,L_{L},L_{G},L_{\psi},\kappa,G_{\min},G_{\max},N)>0

such that

\|\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}\leq L_{\mathfrak{m},R,N}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{N,2},\qquad\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in B_{R}^{N},

where

B_{R}^{N}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}:\|\mathbf{x}\|_{N,2}\leq R\}.

Second, for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
,

\|\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\leq C_{m}N^{-1/4}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{N,2}.

Proof.

We first prove the local Lipschitz property. By
Lemma
B.4
, for any

\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
,

\displaystyle|Q_{N}(\mathbf{x})-Q_{N}(\mathbf{y})|

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{-1}\left|F_{\mu_{\mathbf{x}}^{N}}(Q_{N}(\mathbf{y}))-F_{\mu_{\mathbf{y}}^{N}}(Q_{N}(\mathbf{y}))\right|

\displaystyle=\kappa^{-1}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\psi\left(\frac{Q_{N}(\mathbf{y})-L(x^{i})}{\tau}\right)-\psi\left(\frac{Q_{N}(\mathbf{y})-L(y^{i})}{\tau}\right)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{\psi}L_{L}}{\kappa\tau N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|x^{i}-y^{i}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{\psi}L_{L}}{\kappa\tau\sqrt{N}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{N,2}.

(44)

Define

C_{Q,N}:=\frac{L_{\psi}L_{L}}{\kappa\tau\sqrt{N}}.

For
i\in[N]
, set

a_{i}(\mathbf{x}):=e^{-\alpha G(x^{i})}\psi\left(\frac{Q_{N}(\mathbf{x})-L(x^{i})}{\tau}\right).

Since
G\geq G_{\min}
,
G
is globally Lipschitz, and

r\mapsto e^{-\alpha r}
has derivative bounded by

\alpha e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}
on
[G_{\min},\infty)
, we have

\left|e^{-\alpha G(x^{i})}-e^{-\alpha G(y^{i})}\right|\leq\alpha e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}L_{G}\|x^{i}-y^{i}\|_{2}.

Using
0<s<1
, the Lipschitz continuity of
\psi
, the Lipschitz continuity
of
L
, and (
44
), we get

|a_{i}(\mathbf{x})-a_{i}(\mathbf{y})|\leq C_{a,1}\|x^{i}-y^{i}\|_{2}+C_{a,2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{N,2},

where

C_{a,1}:=\alpha e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}L_{G}+e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}\frac{L_{\psi}L_{L}}{\tau},\qquad C_{a,2}:=e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}\frac{L_{\psi}C_{Q,N}}{\tau}.

Consequently,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|a_{i}(\mathbf{x})-a_{i}(\mathbf{y})|\leq\left(\frac{C_{a,1}}{\sqrt{N}}+C_{a,2}\right)\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{N,2}.

Let

L_{B,N}:=\frac{C_{a,1}}{\sqrt{N}}+C_{a,2}.

Then, we can write

A_{N}(\mathbf{x}):=A(\mu_{\mathbf{x}}^{N})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x^{i}a_{i}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad B_{N}(\mathbf{x}):=B(\mu_{\mathbf{x}}^{N})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}(\mathbf{x}).

By (
B.4
), we have

|B_{N}(\mathbf{x})-B_{N}(\mathbf{y})|\leq L_{B,N}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{N,2}.

Moreover, by Lemma
B.5
, we have

B_{N}(\mathbf{x})\geq b_{G}:=\beta e^{-\alpha G_{\max}}>0.

Let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in B_{R}^{N}
. Since
a_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}
, we have

\displaystyle\|A_{N}(\mathbf{x})-A_{N}(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|x^{i}-y^{i}\|_{2}a_{i}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|y^{i}\|_{2}|a_{i}(\mathbf{x})-a_{i}(\mathbf{y})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}}{\sqrt{N}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{N,2}+RL_{B,N}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{N,2}.

Hence,

\|A_{N}(\mathbf{x})-A_{N}(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}\leq L_{A,R,N}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{N,2},

where

L_{A,R,N}:=\frac{e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}}{\sqrt{N}}+RL_{B,N}.

Also, for
\mathbf{y}\in B_{R}^{N}
,

\|A_{N}(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}\leq\frac{e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|y^{i}\|_{2}\leq\frac{e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}R}{\sqrt{N}}.

Since

\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{A_{N}(\mathbf{x})}{B_{N}(\mathbf{x})},

we obtain, for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in B_{R}^{N}
,

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|A_{N}(\mathbf{x})-A_{N}(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}}{B_{N}(\mathbf{x})}+\|A_{N}(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}\left|\frac{1}{B_{N}(\mathbf{x})}-\frac{1}{B_{N}(\mathbf{y})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq b_{G}^{-1}L_{A,R,N}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{N,2}+\frac{e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}R}{\sqrt{N}}b_{G}^{-2}L_{B,N}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{N,2}.

Thus (
B.7
) holds with

L_{\mathfrak{m},R,N}:=b_{G}^{-1}L_{A,R,N}+\frac{e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}R}{\sqrt{N}}b_{G}^{-2}L_{B,N}.

It remains to prove the growth estimate. Applying
Corollary
B.1
to

\rho=\mu_{\mathbf{x}}^{N}
, we get

\|\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{4}\leq C_{m}^{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\theta\|_{2}^{4}\,\mu_{\mathbf{x}}^{N}(d\theta)=C_{m}^{4}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|x^{i}\|_{2}^{4}.

Since

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|x^{i}\|_{2}^{4}\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|x^{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}=\|\mathbf{x}\|_{N,2}^{4},

we obtain

\|\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{4}\leq C_{m}^{4}N^{-1}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{N,2}^{4}.

Taking fourth roots gives (
B.7
).
∎

Proposition B.8
.

Under Assumption
2.3
, fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that

\mathbf{\Theta}_{0}^{N}:=(\Theta_{0,\tau}^{1,N},\ldots,\Theta_{0,\tau}^{N,N})

is an
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
-valued random variable with

\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{\Theta}_{0}^{N}\|_{N,2}^{2}<\infty.

Then the interacting particle system (
10
) admits a unique
nonexplosive strong solution

\mathbf{\Theta}_{t}^{N}=(\Theta_{t,\tau}^{1,N},\ldots,\Theta_{t,\tau}^{N,N})

with continuous paths on every finite time interval.

Proof.

Write the particle system in vector form. For

\mathbf{x}=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
, define

b_{N}^{i}(\mathbf{x}):=-\lambda\left(x^{i}-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\right),\qquad i\in[N],

and

\Sigma_{N}^{i}(\mathbf{x}):=\sigma D\left(x^{i}-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\right),\qquad i\in[N].

Let

b_{N}(\mathbf{x}):=(b_{N}^{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,b_{N}^{N}(\mathbf{x}))\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N},

and let
\Sigma_{N}(\mathbf{x})
be the block-diagonal
dN\times dN
matrix
with diagonal blocks
\Sigma_{N}^{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,\Sigma_{N}^{N}(\mathbf{x})
.
Then (
10
) is equivalent to

d\mathbf{\Theta}_{t}^{N}=b_{N}(\mathbf{\Theta}_{t}^{N})\,dt+\Sigma_{N}(\mathbf{\Theta}_{t}^{N})\,d\mathbf{W}_{t}^{N},

where

\mathbf{W}_{t}^{N}:=(W_{t}^{1},\ldots,W_{t}^{N})

is a
dN
-dimensional Brownian motion. We first prove local Lipschitz continuity of
b_{N}
and
\Sigma_{N}
. Fix

R>0
and let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in B_{R}^{N}
. By
Lemma
B.7
,

\|\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}\leq L_{\mathfrak{m},R,N}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{N,2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|b_{N}(\mathbf{x})-b_{N}(\mathbf{y})\|_{N,2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|x^{i}-y^{i}-\left(\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda^{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{N,2}^{2}+2\lambda^{2}N\|\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{y})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda^{2}\left(1+NL_{\mathfrak{m},R,N}^{2}\right)\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{N,2}^{2}.

Using
\|D(u)-D(v)\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{d}\,\|u-v\|_{2}
, we similarly obtain

\displaystyle\|\Sigma_{N}(\mathbf{x})-\Sigma_{N}(\mathbf{y})\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\Sigma_{N}^{i}(\mathbf{x})-\Sigma_{N}^{i}(\mathbf{y})\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}d\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|x^{i}-y^{i}-\left(\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sigma^{2}d\left(1+NL_{\mathfrak{m},R,N}^{2}\right)\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{N,2}^{2}.

Hence
b_{N}
and
\Sigma_{N}
are locally Lipschitz on

(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
.

We next prove linear growth. By
Lemma
B.7
,

\|\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\leq C_{m}N^{-1/4}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{N,2}.

Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|x^{i}-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|x^{i}\|_{2}^{2}+2N\|\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\mathbf{x}\|_{N,2}^{2}+2NC_{m}^{2}N^{-1/2}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{N,2}^{2}

(45)

\displaystyle=2\left(1+C_{m}^{2}N^{1/2}\right)\|\mathbf{x}\|_{N,2}^{2}.

(46)

Since
D(z)=\|z\|_{2}I_{d}
, we have

\|\Sigma_{N}(\mathbf{x})\|_{F}^{2}=\sigma^{2}d\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|x^{i}-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{2},

and

\|b_{N}(\mathbf{x})\|_{N,2}^{2}=\lambda^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|x^{i}-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}^{2}.

Combining these identities with (
46
)
gives

\|b_{N}(\mathbf{x})\|_{N,2}^{2}+\|\Sigma_{N}(\mathbf{x})\|_{F}^{2}\leq 2(\lambda^{2}+\sigma^{2}d)\left(1+C_{m}^{2}N^{1/2}\right)\|\mathbf{x}\|_{N,2}^{2}.

Therefore the coefficients are locally Lipschitz and satisfy a linear-growth
bound.

By the standard finite-dimensional strong existence and uniqueness theorem
for SDEs with locally Lipschitz coefficients and linear growth, the vector
SDE (
B.4
) has a unique nonexplosive strong solution on
every finite time interval. Since solutions of finite-dimensional Itô SDEs
with continuous coefficients have continuous paths, the particle paths

t\mapsto\Theta_{t,\tau}^{i,N},\qquad i\in[N],

are continuous. This proves the claim.
∎

B.5
Fourth-moment boundedness with explicit constants

Let

be any fixed BDG constant such that, for every continuous
d
-dimensional stochastic integral

so the BDG integrand is

Since

, we have

\|H_{s}\|_{F}^{2}=d\|\Theta_{s,\tau}^{i,N}-m_{s}^{N}\|_{2}^{2}.

Moreover, let

(48)

C_{\mathrm{bd}}(T):=54\mu_{4}\exp\left(K_{\mathrm{bd}}(T)T\right).

(49)

.

2.3
, for every

,

Proof.

By Proposition

, the interacting particle

Proposition C.2

\int\varphi_{r}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{t,\tau}\geq\left(\int\varphi_{r}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}\right)e^{-pt}.

Combining this with (
76

75

C.3
Direct mean-field convergence

Recall

V_{\tau}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lVert\theta-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\rho_{t,\tau}(\,\mathrm{d}\theta),\qquad M_{\tau}(t):=\left\lVert m(\rho_{t,\tau})-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}.

Lemma C.3
.

For almost every
t
,

-aV_{\tau}(t)-b\sqrt{V_{\tau}(t)}M_{\tau}(t)\leq\dot{V}_{\tau}(t)\leq-aV_{\tau}(t)+b\sqrt{V_{\tau}(t)}M_{\tau}(t)+c_{d}M_{\tau}(t)^{2},

(88)

where

a:=2\lambda-d\sigma^{2},
b:=\sqrt{2}(\lambda+d\sigma^{2}),
c_{d}:=\frac{d\sigma^{2}}{2}.

Proof.

Use the test function
\varphi(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\theta-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
. Since
\nabla\varphi=\theta-\theta^{\star}
and
\nabla^{2}\varphi=I_{d}
, the weak formulation and (
4
) give

\dot{V}_{\tau}(t)=-\lambda\int\left\langle\theta-\theta^{\star},\theta-m_{t}\right\rangle\rho_{t,\tau}(\,\mathrm{d}\theta)+\frac{d\sigma^{2}}{2}\int\left\lVert\theta-m_{t}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\rho_{t,\tau}(\,\mathrm{d}\theta),

(89)

where
m_{t}=m(\rho_{t,\tau})
. Let
\bar{\theta}_{t}:=\int\theta\rho_{t,\tau}(\,\mathrm{d}\theta)
. Expanding around
\theta^{\star}
gives

-\lambda\int\left\langle\theta-\theta^{\star},\theta-m_{t}\right\rangle\rho_{t,\tau}(\,\mathrm{d}\theta)=-2\lambda V_{\tau}(t)+\lambda\left\langle\bar{\theta}_{t}-\theta^{\star},m_{t}-\theta^{\star}\right\rangle.

(90)

Similarly,

\frac{d\sigma^{2}}{2}\int\left\lVert\theta-m_{t}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\rho_{t,\tau}(\,\mathrm{d}\theta)=d\sigma^{2}V_{\tau}(t)-d\sigma^{2}\left\langle\bar{\theta}_{t}-\theta^{\star},m_{t}-\theta^{\star}\right\rangle+\frac{d\sigma^{2}}{2}M_{\tau}(t)^{2}.

(91)

Combining (
89
), (
90
), and (
91
),

\dot{V}_{\tau}(t)=-aV_{\tau}(t)+(\lambda+d\sigma^{2})\left\langle\bar{\theta}_{t}-\theta^{\star},m_{t}-\theta^{\star}\right\rangle+c_{d}M_{\tau}(t)^{2}.

(92)

Finally,

\left\lVert\bar{\theta}_{t}-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left(\int\left\lVert\theta-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\rho_{t,\tau}(\,\mathrm{d}\theta)\right)^{1/2}=\sqrt{2V_{\tau}(t)}.

Using this bound in (
92
) gives the upper inequality in (
88
). For the lower inequality, use

(\lambda+d\sigma^{2})\left\langle\bar{\theta}_{t}-\theta^{\star},m_{t}-\theta^{\star}\right\rangle\geq-b\sqrt{V_{\tau}(t)}M_{\tau}(t)

(93)

and then keep the nonnegative term
c_{d}M_{\tau}(t)^{2}\geq 0
or drop it from below. This proves (
88
).
∎

Remark 8
.

The constants
\beta_{\tau}
and
\alpha_{\tau}
constructed in the proof should
be understood as one coupled admissible choice. In fact, the construction has
an additional degree of freedom. Suppose that the proof provides a positive
mass lower bound

Then the same lower bound remains valid if
\bar{m}
is replaced by any
auxiliary constant
m\in(0,\bar{m}]
.

For such an
m
, define

S_{\tau}:=\psi\!\left(\frac{h_{L}(r_{G})-\delta_{\rm lev}-L_{L}\bar{r}}{\tau}\right),\qquad\beta_{\tau,m}:=\frac{1}{2}mS_{\tau}.

Since

, we have

0<\beta_{\tau,m}<\frac{1}{2}m.

Hence
\beta_{\tau,m}
can be made arbitrarily small by choosing the auxiliary mass parameter

),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{0\leq r\leq k}\left\lVert\sqrt{\Delta t}\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1}D(u_{\ell}^{i})B_{\ell}^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq B_{4,d}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{k\Delta t}\left\lVert D\left(u_{\lfloor s/\Delta t\rfloor}^{i}\right)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=B_{4,d}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Delta t\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\left\lVert D(u_{\ell}^{i})\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right)^{2}\right].

(153)

Since
D(z)=\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}I_{d}
, we have

\left\lVert D(u_{\ell}^{i})\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=d\left\lVert u_{\ell}^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(154)

Thus, by Cauchy’s inequality in discrete time,

\displaystyle\left(\Delta t\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\left\lVert D(u_{\ell}^{i})\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=d^{2}\left(\Delta t\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\left\lVert u_{\ell}^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}\leq d^{2}T\Delta t\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\left\lVert u_{\ell}^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}.

(155)

Combining (
153
),
(
154
), and (
155
), we get

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{0\leq r\leq k}\left\lVert\sqrt{\Delta t}\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1}D(u_{\ell}^{i})B_{\ell}^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}\right]\leq B_{4,d}d^{2}T\Delta t\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u_{\ell}^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}.

(156)

Substituting (
152
) and
(
156
) into (
151
)
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{0\leq r\leq k}\left\lVert\theta_{r,\tau}^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 27\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\theta_{0,\tau}^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}

\displaystyle\quad+27\left(\lambda^{4}T^{3}+\sigma^{4}B_{4,d}d^{2}T\right)\Delta t\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u_{\ell}^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}.

(157)

Averaging over
i\in[N]
and using (
149
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{0\leq r\leq k}Y_{r}^{\theta}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 27\mu_{4}+K_{\mathrm{bd}}^{\theta}(T)\Delta t\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\ell}^{\theta}\right],

where

K_{\mathrm{bd}}^{\theta}(T):=216\left(1+C_{m}(T)^{4}\right)\left(\lambda^{4}T^{3}+\sigma^{4}B_{4,d}d^{2}T\right).

Since
Y_{\ell}^{\theta}\leq\max_{0\leq r\leq\ell}Y_{r}^{\theta}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\ell}^{\theta}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{0\leq r\leq\ell}Y_{r}^{\theta}\right].

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{0\leq r\leq k}Y_{r}^{\theta}\right]\leq 27\mu_{4}+K_{\mathrm{bd}}^{\theta}(T)\Delta t\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{0\leq r\leq\ell}Y_{r}^{\theta}\right].

The discrete Gronwall inequality gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{0\leq k\leq K}Y_{k}^{\theta}\right]\leq 27\mu_{4}\exp\left(K_{\mathrm{bd}}^{\theta}(T)T\right).

Thus (
148
) holds with

C_{\mathrm{bd}}^{\theta}(T):=27\mu_{4}\exp\left(K_{\mathrm{bd}}^{\theta}(T)T\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma E.2
.

Under Assumption
2.3
, fix
T>0
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and
let
K\Delta t=T
with
0<\Delta t\leq 1
. Define

\Omega_{M}^{\theta}:=\left\{\max_{0\leq k\leq K}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert\theta_{k,\tau}^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}\leq M\right\},

(158)

and

\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}:=\Omega_{M}\cap\Omega_{M}^{\theta}.

(159)

If

\mathbb{P}\left(\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2},

(160)

then there exists a finite constant

C_{\mathrm{NA}}=C_{\mathrm{NA}}\left(M,N,d,\alpha,\beta,\tau,\lambda,\sigma,T,\rho_{0}\right)>0,

not asserted to be uniform in
N
, such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert\theta_{K,\tau}^{i}-\Theta_{T,\tau}^{i,N}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\middle|\,\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\right]\leq C_{\mathrm{NA}}\Delta t.

(161)

Equivalently, this is the numerical strong error estimate with order

m=1/2
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert\theta_{K,\tau}^{i}-\Theta_{T,\tau}^{i,N}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\middle|\,\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\right]\leq C_{\mathrm{NA}}(\Delta t)^{2m},\qquad m=\frac{1}{2}.

(162)

Proof.

We prove the estimate in several steps.

Step 1: Product notation and local Lipschitz regularity of the consensus map.

For

\mathbf{x}=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N},

define

\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{N,2}:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert x^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2},\qquad\mu_{\mathbf{x}}^{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x^{i}}.

Set

Q_{N}(\mathbf{x}):=q_{\beta,\tau}^{L}[\mu_{\mathbf{x}}^{N}],
and
\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x}):=m(\mu_{\mathbf{x}}^{N}).

For
R>0
, define the product ball

B_{R}^{N}:=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}:\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{N,2}\leq R\right\}.

We first show that
Q_{N}
is globally Lipschitz. By
Lemma
B.4
, for any

\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert Q_{N}(\mathbf{x})-Q_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{-1}\left\lvert F_{\mu_{\mathbf{x}}^{N}}(Q_{N}(\mathbf{y}))-F_{\mu_{\mathbf{y}}^{N}}(Q_{N}(\mathbf{y}))\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\kappa^{-1}\left\lvert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\psi\left(\frac{Q_{N}(\mathbf{y})-L(x^{i})}{\tau}\right)-\psi\left(\frac{Q_{N}(\mathbf{y})-L(y^{i})}{\tau}\right)\right]\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{\psi}}{\kappa\tau N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lvert L(x^{i})-L(y^{i})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{\psi}L_{L}}{\kappa\tau N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert x^{i}-y^{i}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{L_{\psi}L_{L}}{\kappa\tau\sqrt{N}}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{N,2}.

(163)

Define

C_{Q,N}:=\frac{L_{\psi}L_{L}}{\kappa\tau\sqrt{N}}.

Then (
163
) becomes

\left\lvert Q_{N}(\mathbf{x})-Q_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rvert\leq C_{Q,N}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{N,2}.

(164)

For each
i\in[N]
, define the empirical weight

a_{i}(\mathbf{x}):=e^{-\alpha G(x^{i})}\psi\big(\frac{Q_{N}(\mathbf{x})-L(x^{i})}{\tau}\big).

Since
G\geq G_{\min}
,
G
is globally Lipschitz, and

r\mapsto e^{-\alpha r}
has derivative bounded by

\alpha e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}
on
[G_{\min},\infty)
, we have

\left\lvert e^{-\alpha G(x^{i})}-e^{-\alpha G(y^{i})}\right\rvert\leq\alpha e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}L_{G}\left\lVert x^{i}-y^{i}\right\rVert_{2}.

Using
0<\psi<1
, the Lipschitz continuity of
\psi
, and
(
164
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lvert a_{i}(\mathbf{x})-a_{i}(\mathbf{y})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\alpha e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}L_{G}\left\lVert x^{i}-y^{i}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}\frac{L_{\psi}}{\tau}\left(\left\lvert Q_{N}(\mathbf{x})-Q_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rvert+\left\lvert L(x^{i})-L(y^{i})\right\rvert\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{a,1}\left\lVert x^{i}-y^{i}\right\rVert_{2}+C_{a,2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{N,2},

where

C_{a,1}:=\alpha e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}L_{G}+e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}\frac{L_{\psi}L_{L}}{\tau},
and
C_{a,2}:=e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}\frac{L_{\psi}C_{Q,N}}{\tau}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lvert a_{i}(\mathbf{x})-a_{i}(\mathbf{y})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{C_{a,1}}{\sqrt{N}}+C_{a,2}\right)\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{N,2}.

(165)

Define

L_{B,N}:=\frac{C_{a,1}}{\sqrt{N}}+C_{a,2},

A_{N}(\mathbf{x}):=A(\mu_{\mathbf{x}}^{N})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x^{i}a_{i}(\mathbf{x}),
and
B_{N}(\mathbf{x}):=B(\mu_{\mathbf{x}}^{N})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}(\mathbf{x}).

By (
165
), we get

\left\lvert B_{N}(\mathbf{x})-B_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rvert\leq L_{B,N}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{N,2}.

Moreover, by Lemma
B.5
,

B_{N}(\mathbf{x})\geq b_{G}:=\beta e^{-\alpha G_{\max}}>0.

(166)

Let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in B_{R}^{N}
. Since
a_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert A_{N}(\mathbf{x})-A_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert x^{i}-y^{i}\right\rVert_{2}a_{i}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert y^{i}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lvert a_{i}(\mathbf{x})-a_{i}(\mathbf{y})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}}{\sqrt{N}}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{N,2}+RL_{B,N}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{N,2}.

(167)

Define

L_{A,R,N}:=\frac{e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}}{\sqrt{N}}+RL_{B,N}.

Then

\left\lVert A_{N}(\mathbf{x})-A_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{2}\leq L_{A,R,N}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{N,2}.

Also, for every
\mathbf{y}\in B_{R}^{N}
,

\left\lVert A_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert y^{i}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}R}{\sqrt{N}}.

(168)

Since

\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{A_{N}(\mathbf{x})}{B_{N}(\mathbf{x})},

we have, for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in B_{R}^{N}
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\lVert A_{N}(\mathbf{x})-A_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{2}}{B_{N}(\mathbf{x})}+\left\lVert A_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{2}\left\lvert\frac{1}{B_{N}(\mathbf{x})}-\frac{1}{B_{N}(\mathbf{y})}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq b_{G}^{-1}L_{A,R,N}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{N,2}+\frac{e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}R}{\sqrt{N}}b_{G}^{-2}L_{B,N}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{N,2}.

Define

L_{\mathfrak{m},R,N}:=b_{G}^{-1}L_{A,R,N}+\frac{e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}R}{\sqrt{N}}b_{G}^{-2}L_{B,N}.

Then

\left\lVert\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{2}\leq L_{\mathfrak{m},R,N}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{N,2},\qquad\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in B_{R}^{N}.

(169)

Step 2: Local Lipschitz and boundedness of the fixed-
N
coefficients.

Define the fixed-
N
drift and diffusion coefficients by

b_{N}^{i}(\mathbf{x}):=-\lambda\left(x^{i}-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\right),\qquad i\in[N],

and

\Sigma_{N}^{i}(\mathbf{x}):=\sigma D\left(x^{i}-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\right),\qquad i\in[N].

Let

b_{N}(\mathbf{x}):=\left(b_{N}^{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,b_{N}^{N}(\mathbf{x})\right)\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N},

and let
\Sigma_{N}(\mathbf{x})
be the block-diagonal matrix with blocks

\Sigma_{N}^{i}(\mathbf{x})
.

For
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in B_{R}^{N}
, using (
169
),

\displaystyle\left\lVert b_{N}(\mathbf{x})-b_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert x^{i}-y^{i}-\left(\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}+2\lambda^{2}N\left\lVert\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda^{2}\left(1+NL_{\mathfrak{m},R,N}^{2}\right)\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}.

Moreover, by (
4
),

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Sigma_{N}(\mathbf{x})-\Sigma_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert\Sigma_{N}^{i}(\mathbf{x})-\Sigma_{N}^{i}(\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}d\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert x^{i}-y^{i}-\left(\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sigma^{2}d\left(1+NL_{\mathfrak{m},R,N}^{2}\right)\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}.

(170)

Thus there exists a finite constant
L_{R,N}>0
such that

\left\lVert b_{N}(\mathbf{x})-b_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{N,2}+\left\lVert\Sigma_{N}(\mathbf{x})-\Sigma_{N}(\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{F}\leq L_{R,N}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{N,2},\qquad\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in B_{R}^{N}.

(171)

Next, from (
168
) and (
166
),

\left\lVert\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}R}{b_{G}\sqrt{N}},\qquad\mathbf{x}\in B_{R}^{N}.

Therefore, for
\mathbf{x}\in B_{R}^{N}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert x^{i}-\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert x^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+2N\left\lVert\mathfrak{m}_{N}(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 2R^{2}+2N\left(\frac{e^{-\alpha G_{\min}}R}{b_{G}\sqrt{N}}\right)^{2}.

Define

G_{R,N}:=2\left(\lambda^{2}+\sigma^{2}d\right)R^{2}\left(1+b_{G}^{-2}e^{-2\alpha G_{\min}}\right).

Then, using
D(z)=\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{2}I_{d}
,

\left\lVert b_{N}(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}+\left\lVert\Sigma_{N}(\mathbf{x})\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq G_{R,N},\qquad\mathbf{x}\in B_{R}^{N}.

(172)

Step 3: The strengthened stopping time.

Define

\mathbf{\Theta}_{t,\tau}^{N}:=\left(\Theta_{t,\tau}^{1,N},\ldots,\Theta_{t,\tau}^{N,N}\right),\qquad\mathbf{\theta}_{k,\tau}^{N}:=\left(\theta_{k,\tau}^{1},\ldots,\theta_{k,\tau}^{N}\right).

Define

\eta(t):=\lfloor t/\Delta t\rfloor\Delta t.

and the stopping time

\tau_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}:=\inf\left\{t\in[0,T]:\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\max\left\{\left\lVert\Theta_{t,\tau}^{i,N}\right\rVert_{2}^{4},\left\lVert\bar{\Theta}_{t,\tau}^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}\right\}>M\ \text{or}\ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert\theta_{\lfloor t/\Delta t\rfloor,\tau}^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}>M\right\},

with the convention
\tau_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}=+\infty
if the set is empty.
Then

\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}=\left\{\tau_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}>T\right\}.

Let

I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(t):=\mathbf{1}_{\{t<\tau_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\}}.

On the event
\{I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(t)=1\}
, both

\mathbf{\Theta}_{t,\tau}^{N}
and

\mathbf{\theta}_{\lfloor t/\Delta t\rfloor,\tau}^{N}
are bounded in the
product norm. Indeed, if

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert x^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}\leq M,

then

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert x^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}1^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert x^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}\right)^{1/2}\leq N^{1/2}(NM)^{1/2}=NM^{1/2}.

Thus we may take

R_{M,N}:=N^{1/2}M^{1/4},

so that, on
\{I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(t)=1\}
,

\mathbf{\Theta}_{t,\tau}^{N}\in B_{R_{M,N}}^{N},\qquad\mathbf{\theta}_{\lfloor t/\Delta t\rfloor,\tau}^{N}\in B_{R_{M,N}}^{N}.

Step 4: Continuous Euler interpolation.

Define the continuous-time Euler interpolation

\mathbf{\theta}_{t,\tau}^{N,\Delta}:=\mathbf{\theta}_{0,\tau}^{N}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{N}\left(\mathbf{\theta}_{\eta(s)/\Delta t,\tau}^{N}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\Sigma_{N}\left(\mathbf{\theta}_{\eta(s)/\Delta t,\tau}^{N}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{s},

(173)

where

\mathbf{W}_{t}:=\left(W_{t}^{1},\ldots,W_{t}^{N}\right).

Since
\eta(s)/\Delta t=\lfloor s/\Delta t\rfloor
, the notation in
(
173
) is consistent with the grid value

\mathbf{\theta}_{\lfloor s/\Delta t\rfloor,\tau}^{N}
. In particular,

\mathbf{\theta}_{K\Delta t,\tau}^{N,\Delta}=\mathbf{\theta}_{K,\tau}^{N}.

Define the error process

e_{t}:=\mathbf{\Theta}_{t,\tau}^{N}-\mathbf{\theta}_{t,\tau}^{N,\Delta}.

Because of (
147
), we have

e_{0}=0.

Step 5: Stopped error estimate.

For
t\in[0,T]
, the stopped error satisfies

\displaystyle e_{t\wedge\tau_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(s)\left[b_{N}\left(\mathbf{\Theta}_{s,\tau}^{N}\right)-b_{N}\left(\mathbf{\theta}_{\eta(s)/\Delta t,\tau}^{N}\right)\right]\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(s)\left[\Sigma_{N}\left(\mathbf{\Theta}_{s,\tau}^{N}\right)-\Sigma_{N}\left(\mathbf{\theta}_{\eta(s)/\Delta t,\tau}^{N}\right)\right]\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{s}.

Using Cauchy’s inequality for the drift integral and Itô’s isometry for the
martingale integral, there exists a numerical constant
C_{T}>0
, depending
only on
T
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lVert e_{t\wedge\tau_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{T}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(s)\left\lVert b_{N}\left(\mathbf{\Theta}_{s,\tau}^{N}\right)-b_{N}\left(\mathbf{\theta}_{\eta(s)/\Delta t,\tau}^{N}\right)\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+C_{T}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(s)\left\lVert\Sigma_{N}\left(\mathbf{\Theta}_{s,\tau}^{N}\right)-\Sigma_{N}\left(\mathbf{\theta}_{\eta(s)/\Delta t,\tau}^{N}\right)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}s.

On
\{I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(s)=1\}
, both arguments of
b_{N}
and

\Sigma_{N}
lie in
B_{R_{M,N}}^{N}
. Hence, by
(
171
),

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert e_{t\wedge\tau_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}\leq C_{M,N}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(s)\left\lVert\mathbf{\Theta}_{s,\tau}^{N}-\mathbf{\theta}_{\eta(s)/\Delta t,\tau}^{N}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}s,

(174)

where
C_{M,N}>0
is finite and depends on

M,N,d,\alpha,\beta,\tau,\lambda,\sigma,T.

We now split

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Theta}_{s,\tau}^{N}-\mathbf{\theta}_{\eta(s)/\Delta t,\tau}^{N}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{\Theta}_{s,\tau}^{N}-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\eta(s),\tau}^{N}\right)+\left(\mathbf{\Theta}_{\eta(s),\tau}^{N}-\mathbf{\theta}_{\eta(s)/\Delta t,\tau}^{N}\right).

(175)

Therefore,

\displaystyle I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(s)\left\lVert\mathbf{\Theta}_{s,\tau}^{N}-\mathbf{\theta}_{\eta(s)/\Delta t,\tau}^{N}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(s)\left\lVert\mathbf{\Theta}_{s,\tau}^{N}-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\eta(s),\tau}^{N}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(s)\left\lVert\mathbf{\Theta}_{\eta(s),\tau}^{N}-\mathbf{\theta}_{\eta(s)/\Delta t,\tau}^{N}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}.

(176)

For the first term, since

I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(s)=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbf{\Theta}_{r,\tau}^{N}\in B_{R_{M,N}}^{N}\quad\text{for all }r\in[\eta(s),s],

the coefficient bound (
172
) implies

\mathbb{E}\left[I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(s)\left\lVert\mathbf{\Theta}_{s,\tau}^{N}-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\eta(s),\tau}^{N}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}\right]\leq C_{M,N}\Delta t.

(177)

Indeed, this follows by writing the increment of

\mathbf{\Theta}_{t,\tau}^{N}
over
[\eta(s),s]
, applying
Cauchy’s inequality to the drift part, Itô’s isometry to the stochastic part,
and using
s-\eta(s)\leq\Delta t
.

For the second term, since
s\geq\eta(s)
, we have

I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(s)\leq I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(\eta(s)).

Moreover,

\mathbf{\Theta}_{\eta(s),\tau}^{N}-\mathbf{\theta}_{\eta(s)/\Delta t,\tau}^{N}=e_{\eta(s)}.

Therefore

\mathbb{E}\left[I_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}(s)\left\lVert\mathbf{\Theta}_{\eta(s),\tau}^{N}-\mathbf{\theta}_{\eta(s)/\Delta t,\tau}^{N}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left\lVert e_{\eta(s)\wedge\tau_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}.

(178)

Combining (
174
),
(
176
),
(
177
), and
(
178
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert e_{t\wedge\tau_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}\leq C_{M,N}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert e_{\eta(s)\wedge\tau_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s+C_{M,N}\Delta t.

(179)

Define

Z(t):=\sup_{0\leq r\leq t}\mathbb{E}\left\lVert e_{r\wedge\tau_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}.

Since
\eta(s)\leq s
, equation (
179
) gives

Z(t)\leq C_{M,N}\int_{0}^{t}Z(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+C_{M,N}\Delta t.

By Gronwall’s inequality,

Z(T)\leq C_{M,N}\Delta t.

In particular,

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert e_{T\wedge\tau_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}\leq C_{M,N}\Delta t.

On
\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}=\{\tau_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}>T\}
, we have

T\wedge\tau_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}=T
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{\theta}_{K,\tau}^{N}-\mathbf{\Theta}_{T,\tau}^{N}\right\rVert_{N,2}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}}\right]\leq C_{M,N}\Delta t.

Dividing by
N
, and absorbing the factor
N^{-1}
into the constant, gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert\theta_{K,\tau}^{i}-\Theta_{T,\tau}^{i,N}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}}\right]\leq C_{M,N}\Delta t.

Using (
160
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert\theta_{K,\tau}^{i}-\Theta_{T,\tau}^{i,N}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\middle|\,\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert\theta_{K,\tau}^{i}-\Theta_{T,\tau}^{i,N}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}}\right]}{\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}})}\leq 2C_{M,N}\Delta t.

Renaming
2C_{M,N}
as
C_{\mathrm{NA}}
proves
(
161
). Since Euler–Maruyama has strong
order
m=1/2
, this is exactly (
162
).
∎

Theorem
3.3
.

Under Assumption
2.3
, let
T=T_{\varepsilon}
be the hitting time supplied by Theorem
3.1
, and let

K\Delta t=T,
for
0<\Delta t\leq 1.

Define
\Omega_{M}^{\theta}
and
\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}
as in
(
158
) and (
159
). Let

C_{\mathrm{bd}}^{\mathrm{num}}(T):=C_{\mathrm{bd}}(T)+C_{\mathrm{bd}}^{\theta}(T),

and let
\delta_{M}\in(0,1/2]
. Choose

M:=\frac{C_{\mathrm{bd}}^{\mathrm{num}}(T)}{\delta_{M}}.

If the hypotheses of Theorem
3.1
hold and

V_{\tau}(T)=\varepsilon,

then there exists a finite constant

C_{\mathrm{NA}}=C_{\mathrm{NA}}\left(M,N,d,\alpha,\beta,\tau,\lambda,\sigma,T,\rho_{0}\right)>0,

not asserted to be uniform in
N
, such that, for every

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{K,\tau}^{N}-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}\right)\geq 1-\delta_{M}-\frac{3C_{\mathrm{NA}}\Delta t+6C_{\mathrm{MFA}}N^{-1}+12\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}}.

(180)

Proof.

We first prove that the strengthened cutoff event has high probability. By
Lemma
B.9
,

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{M}^{c})\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{bd}}(T)}{M}.

By Lemma
E.1
and Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}\left((\Omega_{M}^{\theta})^{c}\right)\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{bd}}^{\theta}(T)}{M}.

Therefore, by the union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left((\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}})^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\Omega_{M}^{c}\cup(\Omega_{M}^{\theta})^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{bd}}(T)}{M}+\frac{C_{\mathrm{bd}}^{\theta}(T)}{M}=\frac{C_{\mathrm{bd}}^{\mathrm{num}}(T)}{M}=\delta_{M}.

Hence

\mathbb{P}\left(\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\right)\geq 1-\delta_{M}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

(181)

By Lemma
E.2
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert\theta_{K,\tau}^{i}-\Theta_{T,\tau}^{i,N}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\middle|\,\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\right]\leq C_{\mathrm{NA}}\Delta t.

(182)

Recall the empirical averages

\widehat{\theta}_{K,\tau}^{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{K,\tau}^{i},\qquad\widehat{\Theta}_{T,\tau}^{N,\mathrm{int}}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Theta_{T,\tau}^{i,N},\qquad\widehat{\overline{\Theta}}_{T,\tau}^{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{\Theta}_{T,\tau}^{i}.

By Young’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{K,\tau}^{N}-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\left\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{K,\tau}^{N}-\widehat{\Theta}_{T,\tau}^{N,\mathrm{int}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+3\left\lVert\widehat{\Theta}_{T,\tau}^{N,\mathrm{int}}-\widehat{\overline{\Theta}}_{T,\tau}^{N}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+3\left\lVert\widehat{\overline{\Theta}}_{T,\tau}^{N}-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(183)

We estimate the three terms under the conditional probability

\mathbb{P}(\,\cdot\,|\,\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}})
.

First, by Jensen’s inequality and (
182
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{K,\tau}^{N}-\widehat{\Theta}_{T,\tau}^{N,\mathrm{int}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\middle|\,\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert\theta_{K,\tau}^{i}-\Theta_{T,\tau}^{i,N}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\middle|\,\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\right]\leq C_{\mathrm{NA}}\Delta t.

(184)

Second, define

Y_{\mathrm{MFA}}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert\Theta_{T,\tau}^{i,N}-\overline{\Theta}_{T,\tau}^{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

By Jensen’s inequality,

\left\lVert\widehat{\Theta}_{T,\tau}^{N,\mathrm{int}}-\widehat{\overline{\Theta}}_{T,\tau}^{N}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq Y_{\mathrm{MFA}}.

(185)

Let

A:=\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}},
and
B:=\Omega_{M}.

Then
A\subseteq B
. Proposition
3.2
gives

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\mathrm{MFA}}\,\middle|\,B\right]\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{MFA}}}{N}.

Equivalently,

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\mathrm{MFA}}\mathbf{1}_{B}\right]=\mathbb{P}(B)\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\mathrm{MFA}}\,\middle|\,B\right]\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{MFA}}}{N}.

Since
A\subseteq B
, we have
\mathbf{1}_{A}\leq\mathbf{1}_{B}
. Therefore

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\mathrm{MFA}}\mathbf{1}_{A}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\mathrm{MFA}}\mathbf{1}_{B}\right]\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{MFA}}}{N}.

(186)

Using (
181
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\mathrm{MFA}}\,\middle|\,\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\mathrm{MFA}}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}}\right]}{\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}})}\leq\frac{2C_{\mathrm{MFA}}}{N}.

(187)

Combining (
185
) and (
187
),
we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\widehat{\Theta}_{T,\tau}^{N,\mathrm{int}}-\widehat{\overline{\Theta}}_{T,\tau}^{N}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\middle|\,\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\right]\leq\frac{2C_{\mathrm{MFA}}}{N}.

(188)

Third, by Jensen’s inequality and (
181
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\widehat{\overline{\Theta}}_{T,\tau}^{N}-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\middle|\,\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\widehat{\overline{\Theta}}_{T,\tau}^{N}-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}}\right]}{\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}})}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\widehat{\overline{\Theta}}_{T,\tau}^{N}-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\overline{\Theta}_{T,\tau}^{i}-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lVert x-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\rho_{T,\tau}(\,\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=4V_{\tau}(T)=4\varepsilon.

(189)

Combining
(
183
),
(
184
),
(
188
), and
(
189
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{K,\tau}^{N}-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\middle|\,\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\right]\leq 3C_{\mathrm{NA}}\Delta t+6C_{\mathrm{MFA}}N^{-1}+12\varepsilon.

By Markov’s inequality under the conditional probability

\mathbb{P}(\,\cdot\,|\,\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}})
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{K,\tau}^{N}-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}>\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}\,\middle|\,\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\right)\leq\frac{3C_{\mathrm{NA}}\Delta t+6C_{\mathrm{MFA}}N^{-1}+12\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{K,\tau}^{N}-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}>\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left((\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}})^{c}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{K,\tau}^{N}-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}>\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}},\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{M}+\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}})\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\widehat{\theta}_{K,\tau}^{N}-\theta^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}>\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}\,\middle|\,\Omega_{M}^{\mathrm{num}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{M}+\frac{3C_{\mathrm{NA}}\Delta t+6C_{\mathrm{MFA}}N^{-1}+12\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}}.

Taking complements proves (
180
).

Appendix F
Extension to parameterized lower-level solution sets

The present analysis focuses on the case where the lower-level solution set

\Theta
is fixed. A natural extension is the parameterized setting

(\phi^{\star},\theta^{\star})\in\arg\min_{\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\ \theta\in\Theta_{\phi}}G(\phi,\theta),\qquad\Theta_{\phi}:=\arg\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}L(\phi,\theta).

In this case, one may view
\theta
as a lower-level response variable for a
fixed value of
\phi
. For each
\phi
, define the best-response upper-level
value

G_{\mathrm{br}}(\phi):=\min_{\theta\in\Theta_{\phi}}G(\phi,\theta),

provided the minimum is attained. Then the original problem can be formally
rewritten as the single-level problem

\phi^{\star}\in\arg\min_{\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}G_{\mathrm{br}}(\phi),\qquad\theta^{\star}\in\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta_{\phi^{\star}}}G(\phi^{\star},\theta).

This suggests a nested consensus-based strategy. For each fixed
\phi
, one
can apply the proposed quantile-selection and Laplace-weighting mechanism in
the
\theta
-variable to approximate a best response

\theta_{\mathrm{br}}(\phi)\in\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta_{\phi}}G(\phi,\theta),

and hence obtain an approximation of
G_{\mathrm{br}}(\phi)
. A standard CBO
scheme can then be applied in the outer variable
\phi
to minimize the
resulting best-response objective
G_{\mathrm{br}}
. In this way, the method
can be interpreted as an inner CB
2
O procedure over
\theta
, coupled with an
outer CBO procedure over
\phi
.

A rigorous convergence theory for this nested extension would require additional
uniform assumptions in
\phi
, such as uniform lower-level error bounds for

L(\phi,\cdot)
, uniform local growth conditions for

G(\phi,\cdot)
on
\Theta_{\phi}
, stability of the best-response value

G_{\mathrm{br}}
, and quantitative control of the error produced by the inner
solver. These issues are beyond the scope of the present work, but the
best-response reformulation provides a natural route for extending the proposed
framework to parameterized bi-level problems.

Appendix G
Limitations

This work focuses on establishing a first finite-particle approximation theory for soft consensus-based bilevel optimization. Several directions remain open.
Future work could strengthen the finite-particle analysis by deriving constants uniform in
N
, relaxing the cutoff-event localization, and proving mean-field well-posedness directly from primitive assumptions. It would also be valuable to weaken the inverse-continuity condition and extend the theory to broader classes of overparameterized models. On the empirical side, larger-scale deep-learning and reinforcement-learning benchmarks could further clarify the practical advantages of soft selection.
