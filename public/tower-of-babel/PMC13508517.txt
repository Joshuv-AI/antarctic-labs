Design of a randomized controlled trial comparing hybrid care with standard care for people with pulmonary fibrosis

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople with pulmonary fibrosis (PF) live with persistent dyspnea, chronic cough, and fatigue. Symptoms that impair their exercise capacity and quality of life. The need for frequent outpatient clinic visits to evaluate disease progression and treatment response can impose a substantial burden on people with PF. Online home monitoring has the potential to replace some of these visits. However, structural implementation and evidence on its impact on patient outcomes are lacking. Here, we describe the design of the SUITS study, a multicenter randomized controlled trial (RCT) which aims to evaluate the impact of partly replacing conventional outpatient clinic visits with home monitoring and video consultations on clinically relevant patient outcomes and efficiency of healthcare systems.MethodsPatients will be randomized to either standard care (3-monthly outpatient clinic visits) or hybrid care. Hybrid care consists of a home monitoring program and video consultations that alternately replace outpatient clinic visits. In an online application, patients perform weekly home spirometry and pulse-oximetry measurements, fill out patient reported outcome measures (PROMs) on a regular basis, and have access to an information library with disease specific information. The primary endpoint of this RCT is the change in patient self-management from baseline to 12 months, assessed with the validated patient activation measure (PAM).DiscussionIn light of the insufficient implementation of a hybrid care pathway for people with PF, there is pressing need for a trial to confirm if partly replacing outpatient clinic visits with home monitoring and video consultations is both feasible and effective.Registration detailsClinicaltrials.Gov: NCT06883448, date of registration: 13-03-2025. The study was retrospectively registered. No changes were made to the IRB approved study protocol (version 1, 11-01-2024) prior to registration at ClinicalTrials.gov.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03716-0.

Background

Interstitial lung diseases are characterized by pulmonary fibrosis (PF), inflammation, or a combination of both [1, 2]. Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) is the archetype of progressive pulmonary fibrosis; however, many other forms of PF may also show a similar disease behavior with worsening symptoms and premature death [3]. Close monitoring of the disease course is crucial as it guides treatment adjustments and supportive interventions [2]. In-hospital pulmonary function testing (PFT) is currently the most widely accepted measure to monitor disease progression and necessitates outpatient clinic visits every 3 to 6 months, mostly concentrated in expert centers [2]. Hospital visits often pose a burden on patients and their families due to respiratory symptoms, impaired mobility, and sometimes use of supplemental oxygen, especially when travel distances to these centers are long. Moreover, scheduled hospital visits may not adequately address intercurrent problems, for example respiratory infections and side-effects of medication, as well as early-stage disease deterioration [2].

Home monitoring with remote assessments of spirometry, oximetry, symptoms, and therapy-related side-effects enables more frequent evaluation of the individual’s health status. This may reduce the burden of outpatient clinic visits, support self-management, and enhance timely detection of disease progression, symptoms, and therapy-related side-effects. Although a growing number of studies have evaluated the reliability and feasibility of home monitoring in patients with PF for research purposes [4–6], its adoption in PF management is inconsistent, with different barriers hindering implementation [7–9]. Furthermore, most studies have evaluated home monitoring as adjunct to standard care, with relatively small cohorts and short follow-up durations of up to 6 months [10–14].

We have developed a hybrid care pathway, alternating in-person visits with home monitoring and video consultations. In chronic, sometimes progressive conditions such as PF, the ability to self-manage is essential and recognized as a critical determinant of health outcomes [15]. Self-management is defined as the ability to manage symptoms, treatment, physical, and psychological consequences and lifestyle changes and could be improved by self-monitoring including insights into the patient’s own health [16]. We hypothesize that hybrid care improves self-management, while maintaining or improving other patient- and healthcare related outcomes compared to standard care. Employing the patient activation measure (PAM), a validated patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) quantifying patient self-management, as the primary outcome in our study aligns with broader healthcare trends that prioritize patient-centered outcomes [17].

Here, we describe the design of the SUITS study, a multicenter randomized controlled trial (RCT) investigating the impact of alternately replacing outpatient clinic visits with home monitoring and video consultations in people with PF on patient self-management, other clinically relevant patient outcomes, and healthcare system efficiency in terms of care utilization patterns and cost-effectiveness.

Methods

Objectives and endpoints

The main objective of this study is to evaluate the impact of replacing half of the outpatient clinic visits in patients with PF with home monitoring and video consultations, with patient-self management as the primary endpoint. Patient self-management will be assessed with the change in (PAM) score from baseline to 12 months. Secondary objectives include the impact on patient outcomes, including quality of life (QoL), health status, and pulmonary function tests (PFTs), and healthcare efficiency outcomes, including the number of (un)scheduled hospital visits. Furthermore, healthcare costs and cost-effectiveness will be analyzed and published separately. All endpoints are displayed in Table 1.

Study design and participants

The SUITS study is a prospective, randomized, superiority multicenter controlled trial (NCT06883448). Patients with PF diagnosed by a multidisciplinary team, following ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT guidelines [3], will be asked to participate at the outpatient clinics of eleven community and tertiary centers in the Netherlands. Study procedures

After obtaining informed consent, patients are randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either standard care or hybrid care. Randomization is being done with a computer-generated schedule (ALEA Screening and Enrolment Application Software, the Netherlands) using block-randomization stratified for study site and use of antifibrotic therapy (yes or no). Patients and healthcare professionals are not masked to group allocation. Patients in the hybrid care group are using a home monitoring program, consisting of weekly home spirometry, pulse-oximetry, and regular electronic patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs). The devices are connected to an online application (app) on a smartphone or tablet. In the app, patients can also report real-time symptoms and side-effects of medication, contact their healthcare professionals via eConsultation, and access an online information library. Moreover, two out of four annual outpatient clinic visits are alternately replaced with remote consultations through remote video consultations. Hospitals are allowed to use their own, hospital approved, video conferencing systems. The study flow diagram is displayed in Fig. 1.

Study schedule

Hybrid care combines in-person consultations with PFT at the outpatient clinic with remote consultations via video conferencing. At enrollment, patients perform in-hospital PFTs, are registered for the online home monitoring program, and receive instructions on the use of the SUITS app, home spirometry, pulse-oximetry, completion of PROMs, and video consultations. A subset of PROMs is intended for use in clinical practice and is completed in the SUITS app. Results are directly visible to patients and healthcare professionals, and discussed during every scheduled consultation. All other PROMs are solely used as outcome measure and completed in the electronic case report form (eCRF) (Castor EDC, Castor Research Inc, the Netherlands) at home via a link sent to them by e-mail. Scores of PROMs completed in the eCRF are blinded to healthcare professionals, patients, and researchers. PROMs completed in the SUITS app include the King’s Brief Interstitial Lung Disease Health Status (K-BILD), Living with Pulmonary Fibrosis (L-PF), Patient Experiences and Satisfaction with Medication (PESaM), and Visual Analogue Scales (VAS) on symptoms (cough, dyspnea, fatigue, and general well-being). Other PROMs (PAM, EuroQol-5D-5-L plus respiratory bolt-on (EQ-5D-5-L + R), adapted Medical Consumption Cost Questionnaire (iMCQ), and Productivity Cost Questionnaire (iPCQ) will be completed in the eCRF.

Standard care involves scheduled outpatient clinic visits at baseline and thereafter every three months including in-hospital PFTs at every visit. Patients complete all PROMs in the eCRF via a link sent by e-mail. All PROM scores are blinded to the healthcare professional, patient, and researchers. A detailed measurement schedule is presented in the supplementary (S1). The total study duration for individual patients in both study groups is 12 months. Home monitoring program

SUITS app

The SUITS app (patientMpower, Ireland) is an online eHealth app developed for people with PF. All patients receive a personal account and the app is installed on their smartphone or tablet. The data will be stored on a secured and approved server (pMp), which has the highest European Certification for safety ISO27001 and meets NEN7510 standards. It complies with all safety regulations (General Data Protection Regulation). Besides the patient, the healthcare professional and hospital research staff have direct access to the data. Figure 2 shows app designs as seen from a patient- and healthcare professional perspective. The app is a CE-marked software medical device, which sends a push notification to the patient to contact the healthcare professional if there is a 2-day consecutive ≥ 10% decline from baseline FVC or in case of patient-reported burdensome or novel symptoms or side-effects. All workflow triggered alerts are included in the supplementary (S2). Figure 3 shows the structure and data flow of the app.

Home spirometry and pulse-oximetry

A handheld spirometer is provided to all patients in the hybrid care group. The spirometer used is the Spirobank Smart (MIR, Italy), which is a CE-certified medical device and connects to a smartphone or tablet via Bluetooth. Results are provided in real time to patients in the SUITS app and are visible to healthcare professionals in a secure online platform. Patients are trained on how to use the handheld spirometer according to ATS/ERS guidelines for in-hospital spirometry [34] and are considered trained when they are able to perform good, reproducible FVC measurements, with less than 150 ml difference from baseline FVC at the hospital. Patients perform home spirometry weekly; three measurements are performed at each assessment, with the highest value being recorded in the graph visible to the patient. Healthcare professionals can set a filter to see all, highest of the day, and/or measurements filtered on good quality based on artificial intelligence algorithms (ArtiQ.PFT, Belgium) (Fig. 2B). ArtiQ software uses artificial intelligence to analyze the reliability of FVC measurements and help better assess quality of FVC measurements [35]. A Bluetooth enabled CE-certified finger pulse-oximeter is also provided to all patients (Nonin pulse-oximeter, Nonin Medical Inc., United Kingdom). Patients are instructed to perform oximetry weekly at rest, results are provided real-time.

Sample size calculation

The primary endpoint in our study is the between-group difference of the change in PAM score from baseline to 12 months. The sample size was calculated with data from a previous observational study in patients with IPF and sarcoidosis [18], where the standard deviation (SD) was 9.1 for patients with IPF, and 11.0 for patients with sarcoidosis. For this power calculation we used the mean SD of that study, which was 10.2. With 99 patients per group, the study will have 80% power to detect a clinically significant between-group difference in the change in total PAM score with a confidence interval of 95%, based on a minimal important change (MIC) for the PAM of 4 points. To allow for 10% dropout, we aim to include 220 patients in our study.

Statistical analysis

Baseline data will be summarized descriptively. The primary efficacy endpoint is the mean change from baseline in PAM score at 12 months. A mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) will be used to obtain estimates of the treatment difference at month 12 using all available data. The model will include factors for study group, visit, study group by visit interaction, antifibrotic therapy use, study site, and baseline PAM score as covariates. All analyses will be conducted in the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population, defined as all randomized patients with a completed PAM at baseline. A per protocol analysis will also be conducted, defined as all patients without major protocol deviations. Similar to the primary endpoint, change in L-PF, K-BILD, EQ-5D-5-L VAS and index scores and in-hospital FVC and DLco will also be analyzed using MMRM. Differences in healthcare utilization from baseline to 12 months will be analyzed. Correlation and agreement between home and hospital FVC will be calculated using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), Pearson correlation, or Spearman if non-normally distributed, and Bland-Altman analysis. Compliance to home spirometry, pulse-oximetry and PROMs will be assessed by dividing the actual number of performed measurements by the expected number of measurements at 12 months and presented in %. All statistical tests will be 2-sided with a significance level of 0.05.

Patient and public involvement

A patient expert with home monitoring experience and representatives of the Dutch pulmonary fibrosis patient organization are active members of the stakeholder group. This group also includes healthcare professionals, researchers, healthcare insurers, and implementation specialists. All stakeholders contributed to the design of this study in a stakeholder meeting. Patient experiences were evaluated in a pilot where nine patients with and without home monitoring experience provided feedback on the SUITS app. The Dutch pulmonary fibrosis patient organization disseminates information about the study and will share its findings with the interstitial lung disease (ILD) community through their website and newsletters.

Discussion

The currently ongoing SUITS study will be the first RCT to investigate the impact of structurally replacing part of the outpatient clinic visits with home monitoring and video consultations on patient self-management, other clinically relevant outcomes, and healthcare system efficiency in terms of care utilization patterns and cost-effectiveness in the care delivery for people with PF.

As the global population is ageing [36], numbers of chronically ill patients rise, increasing the demand of care. Consequently, healthcare systems face rising challenges due to costly hospital capacity use, and healthcare worker shortage is expected to be 140,000 workers by 2036 in the U.S. [37]. The traditional hospital-centric care model is becoming less sustainable, prompting reevaluation of the care process, aimed at maintaining or even improving quality of care while mitigating costs and workload. Home monitoring has the potential to alleviate pressure on healthcare systems by allowing patients to track their disease course outside traditional healthcare facilities. It may contribute to improved cost-effectiveness by partly replacing outpatient clinic visits and prevent expensive emergency visits and hospital admissions through early detection of intercurrent events. However, evidence from a recent systematic review of randomized controlled trials comparing device-based remote monitoring to usual care in people with a chronic disease suggests that less resource intensive care is being used, representing a shift towards more efficient redistribution of healthcare utilization [38]. Overall, hospital service use tended to decrease in favor of device-based remote monitoring. Four RCTs analyzed described fewer hospital admissions while outpatient clinic visits increased [39–42]. One explanation is the initiation of timely interventions due to alerts from device-based remote monitoring encouraging patients to seek medical support.

This shift suggests another potential benefit of home monitoring, as home monitoring may empower individuals with greater understanding of their own disease course through real-life insights in their health metrics. However, a structured evaluation of the impact of a home monitoring program on patient-self-management is currently lacking. Greater patient understanding of the disease course could facilitate more effective patient self-management by learning to seek help when needed, as was highlighted in a qualitative study on needs in self-management for people with PF [43]. To support self-management, patients desired better insight in their own disease course by tracking progress, access to reliable disease information, and support by healthcare professionals, among other goals [43, 44]. Ideally, improved patient self-management could lead to future care models driven by patient needs, rather than fixed scheduling of hospital visits based on input-based fee-for-service models, striving to maximize value-based healthcare to patients [45, 46]. The PAM is used as the primary outcome of the study, measuring patient self-management in terms of a patient’s knowledge, skills, and confidence in managing their own disease. An improved PAM score indicates that patients are more capable of actively engaging in their healthcare management and pursuing behavioral and lifestyle changes. In previous research in other chronic diseases, higher activation levels measured with the PAM were associated with better health outcomes (e.g. adherence), experiences of care, and fewer episodes of unplanned emergency care and hospitalizations [47, 48].

To date, sustainable implementation of home monitoring in the care delivery for people with PF has been insufficient. One of the main factors that impeded implementation is the absence of structural reimbursement, possibly due to the lack of evidence supporting cost-effectiveness [7–9, 49, 50]. However, during the Covid-19 pandemic, outpatient clinic visits and PFTs were minimized for all patients [51]. To accommodate the urgent need for remote care, temporary reimbursement schemes and adapted policies on privacy and technology requirements were introduced for home monitoring programs [52, 53]. Nowadays, implementing these programs requires complex approval procedures to ensure data privacy and medical device safety. In order to sustainably implement home monitoring programs, structured reimbursement and better guidance through regulatory and policy frameworks is required.

To better understand the barriers and facilitators of implementation of hybrid care and home monitoring in people with PF, we conducted qualitative interviews with healthcare professionals before start of the SUITS study [7]. Overall, healthcare professionals favored hybrid care but raised concerns about replacing care for instable or progressive patients with PF and reducing the frequency of DLco measurements with a fear that this would be inferior to standard care [7]. One possible explanation for this concern is that, although previous studies evaluated the feasibility and efficacy of home spirometry as adjunct to standard care [14, 49], the impact of home monitoring programs that partly replacing outpatient clinic visits, and thus routine PFTs, has not yet been investigated.

By directly comparing hybrid care with traditional care in PF, the SUITS study will provide essential data on the impact on patient outcomes, healthcare utilization, cost-effectiveness, and the feasibility of partly replacing outpatient visits and PFTs with home monitoring and video consultations. No significant safety issues are anticipated in relation to the intervention of this study. In fact, lung function, symptoms, and side-effects of antifibrotic therapy are monitored more frequently in the hybrid care group, with alerts connected to a decline in lung function or burdensome symptoms or side-effects (supplementary material S2). One potential downside of the use of digital health technologies, highlighted by the World Health Organization (WHO), is the worsening of existing health inequities [54], as vulnerable populations, such as the elderly or patients with limited access to the internet, may face challenges in using these technologies [55]. To prevent a so-called digital divide, the WHO recommends that inequities in access, use, and engagement with digital technologies should be addressed [54]. Consequently, engaging the end users in redesigning care models can yield valuable recommendations for clinical practice. The SUITS app was co-created with a group of nine individuals with PF. They started using a preliminary version of the home monitoring program and provided feedback for improvement.

To ensure sustainable implementation after the study, we involve different stakeholders throughout the project. Stakeholders include a patient, representatives from patient advocacy groups, healthcare professionals, researchers, healthcare insurers, and implementation specialists. First, we organized a stakeholder meeting prior to the start of the study to select meaningful endpoints that are relevant to the end users and of value to answer the questions of involved stakeholders, such as effect on disease outcome parameters, healthcare system burden and costs. Besides conducting an RCT, qualitative interviews [7] have been performed before the study to better understand barriers and facilitators with regard to home monitoring and hybrid care pathways for patients with PF. We will conduct qualitative interviews with patients and healthcare professionals to evaluate the study’s findings after completion of the RCT, followed by a qualitative analysis to explore the applicability, appropriateness (i.e. perceived fit and relevance) [56], and clinical implications of a hybrid care pathway.

This study is expected to face some challenges and limitations. First, selection bias may arise by including digitally literate patients, potentially hampering the generalizability of the findings toward the broad patient population. However, a previous RCT in patients with PF using home monitoring showed that 80% of the consecutive patients was willing to participate in the study [14]. In our study, all eligible patients will be invited to mitigate this concern, and the proportion of patients who decline participation will be monitored. Additionally, patients who attend the outpatient clinic at longer intervals may be underrepresented in this study, as the standard regimen involves three-monthly visits. Future research should therefore evaluate the effectiveness of more individually tailored follow-up strategies. Finally, previous studies have demonstrated a decline in PROM completion over time [57]. To minimize incomplete data, patients will receive daily e-mail reminders, and those who have not completed the questionnaire within one week will be contacted by telephone. This approach will limit the occurrence of non-random missing data.

Ultimately, the findings of the current SUITS study are essential to determine whether hybrid care can become a feasible, long-term collaborative care model for people with PF.
