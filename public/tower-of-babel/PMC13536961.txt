Assessing the effectiveness of the TURALIO (pexidartinib) risk evaluation and mitigation strategy program: patient knowledge, attitudes, and behavior survey (2020–2024)
Authors: Charles Dharmani, Oluwatosin Fofah, Margaret Wooddell, Janet L. Ramsey, Michele Riggen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTreatment of tenosynovial giant cell tumor with TURALIO (pexidartinib) is associated with serious hepatotoxicity risk. To ensure its safe use, a Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) program was implemented, incorporating structured education, mandatory enrollment, and liver function monitoring. The assessment of patient and caregiver knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors (KAB) is a central component of evaluating REMS effectiveness.MethodsFour waves of TURALIO Patient KAB Survey were conducted between 2020 and 2024 among REMS-enrollees. Surveys assessed understanding of two Key Risk Messages (KRMs): (KRM 1) hepatotoxicity risk and symptom recognition, and (KRM 2) liver monitoring requirements before and during treatment. Response rates and demonstrated understanding of KRMs were summarized descriptively.ResultsAcross four waves of this voluntary survey, 239 participants completed KAB surveys. Survey response rates ranged from approximately one-third to one-half of invited participants across waves. Correct response rates for individual knowledge items were consistently high. Demonstrated understanding of hepatotoxicity risk remained at ≥80% (the predefined threshold) across waves (87.5% in Wave 1; 80.0% in Wave 4). Understanding liver monitoring requirements improved over time, reaching 100% in Wave 3. Combined demonstrated understanding of KRM1 and KRM2 was <80% in three survey waves, particularly in Wave 2 (73.4%). Demonstrated understanding of KRMs remained generally stable despite evolving patient demographics and treatment duration over time.ConclusionAcross KAB Survey waves, patients receiving TURALIO demonstrated sustained knowledge of hepatotoxicity risk and monitoring requirements supporting the effectiveness of the tREMS program in ensuring the safe real-world use of pexidartinib.

Introduction

Tenosynovial giant cell tumor (TGCT) is a rare, locally aggressive but characteristically non-malignant neoplasm that affects the synovial lining of joints, tendon sheaths, and bursae.1,2 Historically referred to as pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS) or giant cell tumor of the tendon sheath (GCTTS), TGCT is characterized by synovial hyperplasia and the intra-articular accumulation of inflammatory cells. The disease is classified into two primary subtypes, the localized form (L-TGCT) and the more aggressive, diffuse form (D-TGCT).3 While L-TGCT is generally amenable to curative surgery, D-TGCT poses a significant clinical challenge due to its extensive, ill-defined nature within the joint space and its propensity to infiltrate extra-articular tissues. D-TGCT is also associated with high recurrence rates, as high as 70% following surgical resection.4 It can lead to chronic pain, swelling, stiffness, and severe functional limitation, frequently necessitating complex surgeries potentially resulting in joint destruction, debilitating arthropathy, and the eventual need for arthroplasty.1–4

The therapeutic landscape for patients with D-TGCT was historically characterized by limited options. This changed in August of 2019 with the United States (US) Food and Drug Administration’s (FDA) approval of TURALIO (pexidartinib) for the treatment of TGCT associated with severe morbidity or functional limitations, and for whom surgery is deemed not amenable to improvement.5,6 Pexidartinib is an oral, small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitor that selectively targets colony stimulating factor 1 receptor, thereby inhibiting the pathway responsible for the recruitment and survival of abnormal cells. This approval was based on the results of the pivotal Phase 3 ENLIVEN study, which demonstrated a statistically significant overall response rate in pexidartinib-treated patients compared to placebo, confirming its role as the first and, at that time, the only systemic therapy available for this defined patient population.5–7

The clinical benefit of pexidartinib comes with a safety risk of the potential for serious and potentially fatal hepatotoxicity, including recent addition of vanishing bile duct syndrome (VBDS), a rare but serious form of cholestatic liver injury that may result in prolonged or potentially irreversible hepatic dysfunction, to its label.7,8 Due to this serious risk, the FDA mandated a comprehensive Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) program to ensure that the drug’s benefits outweigh its risks and to mitigate the risk of serious and potentially fatal liver injury associated with TURALIO treatment.8 The TURALIO REMS (tREMS) program incorporates specific measures to minimize this risk, including mandatory enrollment of prescribers, pharmacies, and patients, and a required schedule for liver function tests.8–10 A central component of REMS effectiveness evaluation is the assessment of patient or caregiver understanding of key safety information through Knowledge, Attitudes, and Behavior (KAB) surveys.

The tREMS program is further distinguished by its incorporation of a communication plan, Elements to Assure Safe Use (ETASU), an implementation system, and a timetable for submission of assessments. These measures include specific steps to minimize the risk of serious hepatotoxicity.9 To verify the effectiveness of the communication and educational materials deployed through the tREMS program, a mandatory component is the assessment of the Knowledge, Attitudes, and Behavior (KAB) survey of healthcare professionals and patients/caregivers regarding the drug’s risks and the required safety measures.10,11

The TURALIO Patient KAB Survey seeks to document the level of knowledge and assess the attitudes and behavior of patients or caregivers regarding the key information and risk messages communicated through the tREMS program, ensuring awareness that pexidartinib can cause serious liver problems which may be severe and can lead to death, and that patients understand the importance of having blood testing performed to check their liver function before starting and while taking pexidartinib. This survey also collects data on behaviors such as counseling of patients who are receiving treatment with pexidartinib, and awareness of, receipt of or access to, reviewing or reading, and understanding the REMS educational materials. Per FDA guidance, the tREMS is considered to be meeting its objectives if the point estimates of all key risk messages (KRMs) assessed by the KAB survey achieve a demonstrated understanding of 80% or above. To date, four waves of the TURALIO KAB Survey and its assessment have been successfully completed. The results of wave 1 were previously reported.11 This previously published Wave 1 assessment demonstrated high levels of understanding of both hepatotoxicity risk and liver monitoring requirements, with demonstrated understanding rates of 87.5% for KRM1 and KRM2 individually. However, comprehensive demonstrated understanding across both KRMs was lower at 77.5%, establishing an important baseline for interpretation of subsequent longitudinal survey waves. The objective of this study was to retrospectively analyze the results of waves 1–4 of the TURALIO Patient KAB Survey and to evaluate the effectiveness of this KAB survey to demonstrate the success of the tREMS program in achieving its risk minimization goals.

Methods

Study design, oversight, and compliance

The multi-wave TURALIO Patient Knowledge, Attitudes, and Behavior (KAB) Survey was designed collaboratively by Daiichi Sankyo, Inc. (DSI) and the United Biosource LLC (UBC). The KAB surveys were administered by UBC’s Survey Coordinating Center (SCC) using a validated and secure knowledge survey system. This system was designed for privacy protection, compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, the California Consumer Privacy Act, and Title 21 Code of Federal Regulations Part 11 guidelines. Respondent-identifying information was stored separately from the de-identified survey data to ensure participant privacy. Informed consent was obtained in writing from all survey participants. This consent process ensured that each patient was fully aware of the survey’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits, prior to their participation. The written documentation of consent serves to uphold ethical standards and regulatory requirements, reinforcing the integrity of the survey data collected. The TURALIO Patient KAB Survey wave 1 was conducted in 2020 (January – August), wave 2 in 2021 (February – June), wave 3 in 2022 (March – June) and wave 4 in 2024 (March – June).

Study population and recruitment

The target study population for each of the four KAB waves consisted of patients or caregivers enrolled in the tREMS program who were 18 years of age or older. TGCT is a rare disease, and the number of patients enrolled in the tREMS program during each survey wave was limited (fewer than 1,000 patients available during each wave). Accordingly, all patients who met eligibility criteria during each wave were invited to participate in the survey. An adult caregiver or legal guardian (age ≥18 years) was also permitted to complete the survey on behalf of a patient with diminished capacity.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria included individuals who did not agree to participate, and those currently employed by or having immediate family members employed by DSI, UBC, FDA, or anyone reporting a conflict of interest.

Survey administration and data collection

The target minimum sample size for each wave was set at 50 completed patient surveys, which was determined based on practical feasibility and the need for statistical precision. A sample size of 50 allowed for the estimation of understanding rates (e.g., 80% success) with a specified precision (e.g., 95% Confidence Interval (CI) width of 13.7 percentage points). Recruitment efforts were extensive and continued past the minimum target until the pre-specified survey close date.

Surveys were administered via three modalities to maximize patient access and response rates: internet, telephone, and paper mail. Respondents were able to choose their preferred method. For respondents accessing this survey via internet, a secured website using a Quick Response (QR) code or Uniform Resource Locator (URL) was provided in the invitation letter. For respondents accessing this survey via telephone, trained interviewers from the SCC conducted interviews using a Computer-Assisted Telephone Interviewing (CATI) program, allowing real-time data entry. Those respondents who did not prefer internet or telephone, paper surveys were mailed to requesting respondents, with the completed forms returned to the SCC for manual, controlled data entry into the secure system.

To optimize survey participation, a structured reminder and follow-up strategy was implemented for each survey wave. All eligible participants first received a pre-notification letter introducing the survey, followed by a formal invitation containing a unique access code and instructions for completing the survey via internet, telephone, or paper. Non-responders were subsequently contacted through multiple reminder modalities. Reminder letters were mailed at scheduled intervals, with up to five reminder attempts per participant during each wave. In addition, outbound telephone follow-up was conducted by trained staff from the SCC to encourage participation and to offer completion of the survey via CATI. Participants who requested paper surveys received mailed survey packets with prepaid return envelopes, followed by reminder contacts if completed surveys were not returned. These repeated contact attempts were continued until either the participant completed the survey, or the pre-specified survey close date was reached. Eligible participants who completed the entire survey received $35.00 honoraria for their time; it was $25 in wave 1 (2020).

Survey instrument and key risk messages (KRM)

The KAB survey instrument consisted of structured knowledge-, attitude-, and behavior-based items linked to two predefined key risk messages addressing hepatotoxicity risk and liver monitoring requirements. The survey was designed to evaluate the knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors related to the primary safety risks and required risk minimization measures specified in the tREMS program. Question formats included “True/False/I don’t know”, “Yes/No/I don’t know”, and select-all-that-apply response options. The survey was administered using standardized wording across internet-, telephone-, and paper-based modalities. Prior to implementation, the survey instrument underwent qualitative comprehension testing to evaluate clarity and interpretability. The survey included screening questions followed by two modules focused on the primary Key Risk Messages (KRMs), now called survey knowledge domains, related to TURALIO. Key Risk Message 1 (KRM 1): TURALIO can cause serious liver problems, which may be severe and can lead to death. Key Risk Message 2 (KRM 2): It is important for patients to have blood testing performed to check their liver health before starting and while taking TURALIO. Individual survey items are not reproduced in this manuscript because the instrument continues to be used for ongoing REMS assessment activities.

Methodological modifications between survey waves

Across the four survey waves, several adjustments were made to the survey methodology to optimize participation and maintain the relevance of the assessment. These changes included:

(1) Modified exclusion criteria to allow patients who participated in previous qualitative research related to the tREMS program to participate in the KAB survey; (2) Increased participant honoraria from $25 (wave 1) to $35 (waves 2–4) to encourage higher response rates; (3) Transition of assessment period from annual to biennial (every 24 months) following the 36-month assessment; (4) Questionnaire refinement in wave 3: Question 17 was removed from the demonstrated understanding calculation for KRM2 following reassessment of its alignment with the core REMS safety objectives. The item assessed awareness of REMS enrollment requirements rather than hepatotoxicity risk understanding or liver monitoring behaviors.

Dosage context

While the survey content remained consistent, in October of 2022, the FDA approved a change in pexidartinib’s dose from 200 mg (on an empty stomach) to 125 mg (with a low-fat meal). This change did not result in any FDA-mandated changes to the KAB survey questions.

Statistical analysis

No formal hypotheses were tested in this study; all analyses were descriptive in nature. Data were summarized as counts and percentages for each questionnaire item. Statistical computations were performed using SAS®. To ensure precision in the reported estimates, two-sided 95% CIs were calculated. For instances where the estimated percentage was 0% or 100%, the Clopper-Pearson method12 was employed. For all other estimates, CIs were determined using the transformed logit method according to Agresti.13

The primary analysis evaluated participant performance on individual items within each KRM. For “select all that apply” questions, a response was categorized as correct if the participant identified 80% of the correct options and selected no more than one incorrect response. Secondary analyses included a frequency distribution of the total number of correct responses per KRM. A key secondary endpoint was “demonstrated understanding”, defined as a participant correctly answering 80% of all items associated with a specific KRM. This 80% threshold was pre-specified in accordance with FDA’s general guidance for evaluating REMS effectiveness, whereby achieving ≥80% correct understanding of key risk messages is considered to be indicative of a REMS meeting its objectives. Supplemental data, including participant demographics, counseling experiences, and awareness of educational materials, were summarized using frequency tables. Respondent participation across waves was not individually tracked; analyses were conducted independently for each survey wave.

Results

A total of 239 patients participated in the TURALIO Patient KAB Survey across four distinct waves, with cohort sizes of 40 in wave 1, 64 in wave 2, 55 in wave 3, and 80 in wave 4. Survey response rates ranged from approximately one-third to one-half of invited participants across waves. Survey participant characteristics are presented below in Table 1. The majority of survey respondents were female, across waves 1–4, (70.0%, 65.6%, 61.8%, and 55.0%) respectively. The age distribution of the cohorts varied; participants aged 50–69 years accounted for 42.5% of the wave 1 population, whereas wave 4 was characterized by a younger demographic, with 63.9% of respondents falling within the 18–49 years age range.

Most respondents self-identified as White, ranging from 64.1% in wave 2 to 72.5% in wave 4. Other represented racial groups included Asian (10% to 17.5%) and Black or African American (2.5% to 7.3%). Geographically, participants were distributed throughout the United States, with the highest concentrations consistently observed in the West (28.1% to 41.3%) and Northeast (18.2% to 32.5%) regions.

Survey respondents generally demonstrated high educational attainment across all iterations of the survey. A significant portion of the patient population held at least a bachelor’s degree, specifically 52.5% of the wave 1 cohort, 57.8% in wave 2, 58.1% in wave 3, and 51.3% in wave 4. These figures include a consistent representation of participants with advanced degrees, specifically those holding master’s, professional, or doctoral degrees, who collectively accounted for approximately 25% to 30% of the total respondents across the four waves. In contrast, participants with lower levels of educational attainment were minimally represented; those with less than a high school education or some high school remained at or below 1.8% in any given wave.

Clinical characteristics related to the duration of pexidartinib therapy varied over the four survey waves. In wave 1, 73.7% of respondents were in the early stages of their treatment course, having initiated therapy between 1 and 6 months prior to the survey. This trend reversed by wave 4, as the patient population shifted toward long-term therapy; 56.3% of respondents reported taking pexidartinib for more than 12 months.

Across waves 1–4, patient knowledge regarding the risk of serious and potentially fatal hepatotoxicity associated with pexidartinib (KRM 1) remained consistently high (Table 2). Correct response rates for the statement that “TURALIO can cause serious liver problems which may be severe and can lead to death” exceeded 94% in all waves and reached 100% in wave 4. Awareness that liver problems may occur at any time during treatment similarly remained high, ranging from 95% to 97.5% across waves.

Recognition of appropriate responses to potential symptoms of liver injury was also robust. Across symptom-specific items, correct identification of liver-related warning signs approximated 85% in all waves, with the highest levels of recognition observed for nausea, vomiting, right upper abdominal pain, and fatigue. Correct responses to the instruction to stop taking TURALIO and immediately contact a healthcare provider if signs of serious liver injury occurred increased from 85% in wave 1 to 93.8% in wave 4. Collectively, these findings suggest consistently high patient awareness of hepatotoxicity risk and appropriate response behaviors across all survey waves.

Knowledge regarding required liver function monitoring before and during treatment with pexidartinib (KRM 2) was exceptionally high across all survey waves (Table 3). Correct response rates for questions indicating that healthcare providers must discuss blood-testing frequency prior to treatment and that patients must undergo liver enzyme blood tests as directed were near-universal across waves, ranging from 98.2% to 100%. Understanding of the detailed liver monitoring schedule including baseline testing, weekly testing during the first 8 weeks, biweekly testing during the subsequent month, and continued periodic monitoring thereafter remained high, exceeding 92% in all waves and reaching 100% in wave 3. Additionally, recognition that additional blood testing may be required if liver problems develop during treatment exceeded 87% in all waves and surpassed 96% in waves 2–4. These consistently high correct response rates show strong patient comprehension of the required monitoring behaviors central to demonstrating that the tREMS program is meeting its objectives.

The assessment of demonstrated patient understanding regarding the tREMS program focused on two primary KRMs. The results across waves 1–4 of the TURALIO Patient KAB survey are summarized in Table 4. For KRM 1, which addressed the potential for pexidartinib to cause serious and potentially fatal liver injury, understanding rates remained consistently at or above the 80% threshold across all four waves. Specifically, demonstrated understanding was highest in wave 1 at 87.5% (95% CI: 76.9–93.6) and stabilized at 80% in both wave 3 (95% CI: 68.8–87.9) and wave 4 (95% CI: 71.1–86.7) (Figure 1). These results consistently met the pre-specified success criteria (80% threshold) for the tREMS program meeting its objectives.

Patient demonstrated understanding of KRM 2, concerning the necessity of frequent blood testing of patients to monitor liver health before and during treatment, was exceptional; it also demonstrated improvement over time. The overall rate of demonstrated understanding was 77.5% (95% CI: 65.6–86.1) in wave 1, 73.4% (95% CI: 63.5–81.5) in wave 2, 80% (95% CI: 68.8–87.9) in wave 3 and 78.8% (95% CI: 69.7–85.6) in wave 4 (Figure 1).

Limitations

This study has limitations inherent to voluntary surveys administered to patients that should be considered when interpreting its findings. Participation in the TURALIO Patient KAB Surveys was voluntary, and response rates varied across survey waves. In addition, the Wave 1 survey did not achieve the pre-specified target sample size of 50 completed surveys, receiving responses from 40 respondents instead. This was expected as pexidartinib was the first and only approved systemic treatment for TGCT patients and patients enrolled in the tREMS program increased and varied over time. As the survey was designed as a descriptive assessment rather than a hypothesis-testing study, the primary impact of not achieving the target sample size was the modestly reduced confidence interval precision for Wave 1 estimates. The survey results may be subject to nonresponse bias, whereby responders may represent patients who are more engaged in their care, more receptive to REMS educational materials or available to respond to this KAB survey. However, the enrollment criteria were the same for all patients who enrolled in the tREMS program. Also, when the representativeness of the responders was analyzed, there was no statistically significant difference from non-responders based on the distribution of age group, gender or geographic distribution.

To maximize participation, these surveys were administered using multiple modalities (internet, telephone, and mail), accompanied by repeated reminder contacts and honoraria for their time. Although these approaches likely improved recruitment and participation, they may also have introduced selection and response biases by preferentially engaging individuals who were more motivated, more engaged with healthcare information, or more actively involved in their care. Consequently, the observed levels of demonstrated understanding may overestimate knowledge among the broader population of patients enrolled in the tREMS program. Repeated follow-up contacts may also have prompted participants to review educational materials prior to survey completion, potentially influencing survey responses.

Additional factors may limit generalizability and longitudinal comparability of findings across survey waves. The survey population demonstrated relatively high educational attainment, which may limit applicability to broader TGCT patient populations with more diverse educational backgrounds. Higher educational attainment may also be associated with greater engagement with healthcare information and voluntary survey participation, potentially contributing to higher observed knowledge levels. Although representativeness analyses identified no statistically significant differences between responders and nonresponders with respect to age, gender, or geographic distribution, unmeasured differences may still have influenced participation patterns.

Methodological modifications implemented across survey waves may also affect strict comparability of demonstrated understanding estimates over time. These changes included increased participant honoraria in later waves to support recruitment efforts, modifications to KRM2 scoring beginning in Wave 3, and a longer interval between Waves 3 and 4 compared to earlier assessments. The observed shift toward longer treatment duration in later survey waves may also have influenced knowledge levels, as patients receiving longer-term therapy likely had increased cumulative exposure to REMS educational materials, provider counseling, and monitoring requirements. This repeated exposure may contribute to sustained or improved understanding of key risk messages over time. However, the extent to which treatment duration independently influences knowledge levels cannot be determined from this analysis.

Finally, KAB surveys are process measures intended to evaluate whether key risk messages are effectively communicated and understood by patients and caregivers; however, they do not directly assess adherence to monitoring requirements, clinical behaviors, or clinical safety outcomes. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as complementary to objective safety data and postmarketing hepatic outcome evaluations. Despite these limitations, the consistency of demonstrated understanding across multiple assessment waves supports the interpretability and generalizability of these results and provides supportive evidence that the educational and communication components of the tREMS program are functioning as intended.

Discussion

This multi-wave evaluation of the TURALIO Patient KAB Survey demonstrates sustained and robust effectiveness of the tREMS program in achieving its objectives. Across four survey waves conducted over a five-year period, patient understanding of key hepatotoxicity risks associated with pexidartinib and of required liver monitoring behaviors generally met or exceeded the FDA-recommended 80% threshold. These findings provide supportive real-world evidence that the educational, enrollment, counseling, and monitoring components of the tREMS program are functioning as intended to support the safe use of pexidartinib in a rare disease population at risk for serious liver injury.

The principal objective of the tREMS program is to ensure that the patients understand that pexidartinib can cause serious and potentially fatal hepatotoxicity, and that liver injury may occur at any time during treatment. Across waves 1–4, the demonstrated understanding of KRM 1 remained consistently at or above the prespecified 80% benchmark. Moreover, individual item-level correct response rates related to hepatotoxicity risk recognition, liver injury symptoms, and the need to promptly contact healthcare providers were consistently high across survey waves. The consistency of these findings suggests that repeated exposure to educational materials, healthcare provider counseling, and ongoing monitoring requirements may reinforce hepatotoxicity-related risk awareness over time, even as the treated population evolves from newly initiated to longer-term therapy recipients.

Recognition of fever as a symptom that should prompt communication with a healthcare provider was comparatively lower than recognition of other liver-related symptoms across survey waves, although correct response rates generally remained near or above 80%. In contrast, recognition of overt hepatic symptoms, including jaundice, right upper abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and fatigue, remained consistently high.

Understanding of liver function monitoring requirements and the importance of routine laboratory testing (KRM2) remained particularly strong across survey waves. Demonstrated understanding of the necessity for baseline and ongoing liver testing increased from 87.5% in wave 1 and remained nearly universal in wave 4 (98.8%), suggesting sustained reinforcement of monitoring-related risk messages over time. These findings support the effectiveness of the ETASU components of the tREMS program, including mandatory enrollment, prescriber certification, pharmacy verification, and adherence to scheduled laboratory testing, in promoting behaviors central to early detection and mitigation of hepatotoxicity. Given that laboratory monitoring represents the primary mechanism for identifying hepatotoxicity before progression to severe outcomes, continued patient understanding of these monitoring requirements is particularly important for maintaining the benefit–risk profile of pexidartinib in routine clinical practice.

Notably, the overall proportion of participants demonstrating comprehensive understanding across both KRMs remained stable across waves indicating sustained program effectiveness. Wave 4 respondents included a larger proportion of younger patients and long-term treatment recipients compared with earlier waves, yet performance metrics remained consistent. This finding suggests that educational messaging is reaching diverse patient subgroups and remains effective across varying treatment durations and patient experiences.

Interpretation of the trends of demonstrated understanding across survey waves should consider modifications to the KRM2 scoring approach implemented at the beginning of Wave 3 survey. Although demonstrated understanding of individual KRM domains generally remained high, combined demonstrated understanding of KRM1 and KRM2 fell below the predefined 80% threshold in three survey waves, particularly in Wave 2 (73.4%). In addition, interpretation of the particularly high KRM2 demonstrated understanding observed in later waves should consider methodological refinements to the KRM2 calculation, including removal of a REMS enrollment-related item in Wave 3 survey, which may limit strict comparability across all survey waves.

The observed effectiveness of the tREMS program is consistent with prior evaluations of REMS programs for other specialty oncology products with serious safety risks, where structured patient education, mandatory enrollment, and laboratory monitoring requirements have been shown to improve patient knowledge and adherence to safety behaviors.14 However, few REMS programs have published longitudinal multi-wave KAB assessments. The present analysis extends previously published findings supporting the feasibility and sustainability of REMS effectiveness measurement in a small, geographically diverse population of patients with TGCT.10,11 Importantly, high levels of patient-reported counseling and access to educational materials further reinforce that healthcare provider engagement remains a central pillar of tREMS success. KAB survey responses indicate that patients consistently reported receiving counseling regarding hepatotoxicity risk, reviewing enrollment and patient guide materials, and being asked to consent to required laboratory monitoring. These provider-patient interactions are critical for transforming written materials into actionable safety behaviors and likely contribute to the high levels of knowledge retention observed.

Several contextual factors strengthen interpretation of these findings. First, pexidartinib was the first FDA approved systemic treatment for TGCT and has several years of post-approval real-world experience. Thus, pexidartinib benefits from an established safety infrastructure and an accumulating body of REMS effectiveness and hepatic safety evidence, providing greater confidence in its benefit-risk profile in routine clinical practice. Second, the observed maintenance of knowledge and monitoring awareness, despite a 2023 dose modification (200 mg fasting to 125 mg with low-fat meal), indicates that the tREMS educational framework remains robust even as the clinical practice evolves.

Real-world evidence demonstrating the sustained clinical benefit with pexidartinib treatment further underscores the importance of an effective REMS framework in supporting its long-term therapy. A cross-sectional survey of patients with symptomatic TGCT treated with pexidartinib demonstrated significant improvements in symptoms and functional outcomes from the patient perspective, highlighting the positive impact of therapy on pain, stiffness, and mobility in real-world settings.15 Complementing these findings, a longitudinal study extending up to an additional year of follow-up confirmed continued symptom improvement and overall patient-reported benefit with ongoing pexidartinib use.16 When considered alongside the present multi-wave KAB findings, these data suggest that patients remain engaged with essential safety behaviors promoted by the tREMS program while continuing to derive sustained symptomatic benefit from treatment. This combination of continued therapeutic and sustained safety awareness highlights the value of an integrated framework in which patient education, monitoring compliance, and clinical outcomes are mutually reinforcing components of effective long-term TGCT management.

The tREMS program is built upon a series of requirements intended to mitigate the known risk of hepatotoxicity associated with pexidartinib. Key components include mandatory healthcare provider and patient enrollment in the tREMS program, comprehensive counseling on hepatotoxicity risk at the time of therapy initiation, provision of standardized educational materials to patients or caregivers, prescriber certification to ensure understanding of monitoring protocols, and strict adherence to scheduled liver function testing before and during treatment. These steps are designed not only to inform patients about potential liver injury but also to establish a system of ongoing engagement and verification that supports early detection and intervention.10 The present Patient KAB survey results demonstrate that these requirements are being met in practice. The vast majority of participants reported receiving risk counseling and educational materials. Consistently high levels of demonstrated understanding of hepatotoxicity risk and liver monitoring requirements indicate that patients are internalizing the information delivered through these REMS procedures. Furthermore, the near-universal awareness of the need for baseline and ongoing liver testing suggests that provider certification and communication steps are effectively translating into patient behaviors aligned with monitoring expectations. Taken together, these findings indicate that the structural requirements of the tREMS are not only implemented but are successfully achieving their educational and behavioral objectives, reinforcing the overall effectiveness of the tREMS strategy in supporting the safe use of pexidartinib.

The findings of 3- and 5-year retrospective hepatic safety assessments of pexidartinib further reinforce the effectiveness of the tREMS framework. A 3-year retrospective hepatic safety assessment of patients enrolled in the tREMS program reported that hepatic adverse event rates in real-world practice were consistent with those observed in the ENLIVEN clinical trial, with no cases of irreversible liver failure, transplant, or hepatotoxicity-related mortality, and with most liver enzyme elevations resolving following dose interruption or discontinuation.9 Extending these observations, a 5-year follow-up analysis of more than 700 tREMS-enrolled patients confirmed infrequent serious laboratory abnormalities and only rare cases of vanishing bile duct syndrome, most of which demonstrated evidence of recovery.17 Together, these findings suggest that the educational, enrollment, and monitoring components of the tREMS program contribute to effective early detection and management of hepatotoxicity in routine clinical practice and support maintenance of the benefit–risk balance of pexidartinib for treatment of patients with TGCT.

Conclusion

The findings from this longitudinal, multi-wave evaluation of waves 1–4 of the tREMS Patient KAB survey (2020–2024) demonstrate sustained patient understanding of key hepatotoxicity risks and liver monitoring requirements. During this period, knowledge retention and demonstrated understanding generally met or exceeded FDA-defined effectiveness thresholds, despite evolving patient demographics, drug dose, and treatment durations. This sustained performance indicates that the educational, counseling, and monitoring components of the tREMS program are well integrated into routine clinical practice and continue to support safe access to pexidartinib for treatment of patients with TGCT. The current REMS framework remains appropriate and effective in maintaining the benefit–risk profile of pexidartinib in the real-world clinical practice.
