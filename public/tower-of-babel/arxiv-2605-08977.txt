Rapid Decay Subalgebras of C ∗ -Algebras
Shelley Hebert, Slawomir Klimek, Matt McBride

Abstract
. We introduce a general scheme of constructing smooth subalgebras of C ∗ -algebras that are closed under the smooth calculus of self-adjoint elements. We illustrate the scheme with a number of examples.



1.
Introduction

There are many places where dense
*
-subalgebras appear in C
∗
-algebra theory and noncommutative geometry. We mention here a few important ones: unbounded derivations
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
14
]
, cyclic theory
[
6
]
,
[
18
]
, deformation-quatization
[
19
]
and group C
∗
-algebras with the rapid decay (RD) property
[
12
]
.

By definition, smooth subalgebras of C
∗
-algebras are dense
*
-subalgebras, complete in their own Fréchet topology, and closed under the holomorphic functional calculus. Smooth subalgebras capture a smooth structure in noncommutative geometry. A stronger condition, closedness under the smooth functional calculus of self-adjoint elements is also often considered as part of the definition.

The stability under the smooth functional calculus of self-adjoint elements is harder to prove than the stability under the holomorphic functional calculus. To facilitate such proofs one usually uses the Fourier transform and reduces the stability under the smooth functional calculus of self-adjoint elements to growth estimates of exponentials. The following concept was discussed in
[
16
]
: we say that a dense Fréchet
*
-subalgebra
\mathcal{A}
of a C
∗
-algebra
A
has polynomially bounded exponentials (PBE) if for every self-adjoint
a\in\mathcal{A}
and each of the norms
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{A}}
defining the Fréchet topology of
\mathcal{A}
,
\left\|e^{ita}\right\|_{\mathcal{A}}
is bounded by a polynomial in
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Obviously, the C
∗
-norm of
A
has polynomially bounded exponentials.

There are several fairly general approaches to constructions of such smooth algebras, see for example
[
3
]
,
[
13
]
and
[
1
]
. However, in some examples below, the norms do not fit those general approaches and different methods are needed to establish the smooth functional calculus of self-adjoint elements.

We propose in this paper a general scheme that produces smooth subalgebras of C
∗
-algebras that are closed under smooth calculus of self-adjoint elements. Our scheme complements the above mentioned constructions and it seems applicable to a different set of examples. It is based on the concept of rapid decay (RD) subalgebras.

Informally, rapid decay subalgebras of C
∗
-algebras are dense
*
-subalgebras for which every element is given by a series with rapid decay coefficients which are further restricted to lie in specific subsets of the C
∗
-algebra. We discuss a precise definition, some general results, and several examples of RD subalgebras of C
∗
-algebras, including odometers, Super Dihedral group C
∗
-algebras, Bunce-Deddens and UHF C
∗
-algebras. For some of those examples we compare the norms obtained from the general scheme with the norms considered in previous papers.

2.
Smooth Subalgebras

In this section we review a definition and several general results about smooth subalgebras of C
∗
-algebras. More details can be found in
[
11
]
. As indicated in the introduction, smooth subalgebras in noncommutative geometry define smooth structures on noncommutative spaces and thus are a critical part of their geometry.

Let
A
be a unital C
∗
-algebra with norm
\|\cdot\|
and let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq A

be a dense unital
*
-subalgebra of
A
, sharing the same unit as
A
, which is a Fréchet
*
-algebra with respect to a locally convex topology stronger than that of
A
. We will always suppose that we can define the Fréchet topology of
\mathcal{A}
using a countable collection of submultiplicative seminorms
\|\cdot\|_{N}
,
N=0,1,\ldots
. We call
\mathcal{A}
a
Fréchet subalgebra
of
A
. The most common way to construct Fréchet subalgebras of C
∗
-algebras is as domains of closed derivations and their powers, and more generally as smooth elements of an action of a Lie group, see
[
5
]
. Powers of derivations are used to define seminorms and give a Fréchet topology on such subalgebras.

There are several natural concepts of stability of a subalgebra. We say that
\mathcal{A}
is
spectrally stable
if for any element
a
of
\mathcal{A}
its spectrum in
\mathcal{A}
is the same as its spectrum in
A
. Equivalently, from the definition of the spectrum,
\mathcal{A}
is spectrally stable if for any element
a\in\mathcal{A}
that is invertible in
A
its inverse
a^{-1}
is in
\mathcal{A}
.

We say that
\mathcal{A}
is
stable under the holomorphic functional calculus
if for any
a\in\mathcal{A}
and a function
f
that is holomorphic on an open domain containing the spectrum of
a
we have
f(a)
is in
\mathcal{A}
. By considering the holomorphic (away from zero) function
\zeta\mapsto 1/\zeta
, we see that the stability under the holomorphic functional calculus is a stronger condition than the spectral stability. Notice however that if
\mathcal{A}
is a Fréchet subalgebra of
A
then the spectral stability implies stability under the holomorphic functional calculus.

We say that
\mathcal{A}
is
stable under the smooth functional calculus
of self-adjoint elements if for any self-adjoint element
a
of
\mathcal{A}
and a smooth function
f
defined on an open neighborhood of the spectrum of
a
we have
f(a)
is in
\mathcal{A}
. When
\mathcal{A}
a Fréchet subalgebra of
A
, we have the following elementary observation:
if
\mathcal{A}
is stable under the smooth functional calculus of self-adjoint elements then
\mathcal{A}
is stable under the holomorphic functional calculus.

This motivates the following definition: a dense
*
-subalgebra
\mathcal{A}\subseteq A
is called
smooth
if it is a Fréchet subalgebra of
A
and which is stable under the smooth functional calculus of self-adjoint elements.

In general it is not easy to establish stability under the smooth functional calculus of self-adjoint elements. More information about the Fréchet seminorms is required; submultiplicativity is not enough. In a typical approach one can build up any smooth function from exponentials.
This inspires the following concept.

We say that
\mathcal{A}
has
polynomially bounded exponentials (PBE)
if for every self-adjoint
a\in\mathcal{A}
and every
N=0,1,\ldots
there are positive constants
k
and
C
(both depending on
a
and
N
) such that for every
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have:

\left\|e^{ita}\right\|_{N}\leq C(1+|t|)^{k}.

Suppose that a Fréchet subalgebra
\mathcal{A}\subseteq A
has polynomially bounded exponentials. Then
\mathcal{A}
is closed under the smooth functional calculus of self-adjoint elements; in other words, it is a smooth subalgebra of
A
.

Finally, we mention here a scenario for establishing the PBE property for a norm that is related in a specific way to another norm that has the PBE property. The following useful general result is inspired by the techniques in
[
3
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

If a norm
\|\cdot\|
has polynomially bounded exponentials and a norm
\|\cdot\|_{1}
satisfies

\|ab\|_{1}\leq\|a\|\|b\|_{1}+\|a\|_{1}\|b\|

then then the norm
\|\cdot\|_{1}
has polynomially bounded exponentials.

3.
RD Subalgebras

In this section, we introduce and discuss a definition of a rapid decay subalgebra
A_{\textrm{RD}}
in a C
∗
-algebra
A
, the main object studied in this paper. Essentially,
A_{\textrm{RD}}
is a dense
*
-subalgebra of
A
where each element is given by an infinite series of rapid decay terms such that each them belongs to a fixed subspace of
A
with some extra conditions.

We will need a concept of a bimodular projection. Suppose
B
is a C
∗
-subalgebra of a C
∗
-algebra
A
. A linear continuous map
E:A\to B
is called a bimodular projection from
A
to
B
if
E
is a projection onto
B
and has the bimodule property:

E(ab)=E(a)b\ \text{ and }\ E(ba)=bE(a)

for all
a\in A
and
b\in B
. In particular, conditional expectations are examples of bimodular projections.

Let
A
be a C
∗
-algebra and let
\{A_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}
be a collection of C
∗
-subalgebras of
A
such that
A_{n}\subsetneqq A_{n+1}
and such that
\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}A_{n}
is dense in
A
. For each
n
, suppose that there exist a bimodular projection:

E_{n}:A_{n+1}\to A_{n}\,.

Also, define maps
E_{m,n}:A_{m}\to A_{n}
for
m>n
by

E_{m,n}=E_{n}\circ\cdots\circ E_{m-1}\,.

Notice that, by definition, the maps
E_{m,n}
are bimodular projections.
Additionally, we assume that the maps
E_{m,n}
are uniformly bounded:

\|E_{m,n}\|\leq\Omega

(3.1)

for some constant
\Omega
. Notice that, since
E_{m,n}
are projections, we must have
\Omega\geq 1
. Moreover, if
\Omega=1
, by Tomiyama’s Theorem (Theorem II.6.10.2 in
[
2
]
),
E_{m,n}
are conditional expectations.

We can define natural bimodular projections :

\mathcal{E}_{n}:\bigcup_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}A_{m}\to A_{n}\,,

by

\mathcal{E}_{n}(a)=E_{m,n}(a)

if
a\in A_{m}
. This is well-defined as
E_{m,n}(a)
is independent of
m
because
E_{j}
’s are projections onto
A_{j}
’s. Now, since
\bigcup_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}A_{m}
is dense in
A
, then
\mathcal{E}_{n}
can be naturally extended to
A
by the uniform continuity of
E_{m,n}
.

Let
B_{0}=A_{0}
and for
n\geq 1
set
B_{n}=\textrm{ker }E_{n-1}
. Then, for each
n
,
B_{n}
is a subspace of
A_{n}
. Notice that for
a\in A_{n}
we can write

a=E_{n-1}(a)+(a-E_{n-1}(a))\,.

By definition,
E_{n-1}(a)\in A_{n-1}
, additionally we have that

E_{n-1}(a-E_{n-1}(a))=E_{n-1}(a)-E_{n-1}^{2}(a)=E_{n-1}(a)-E_{n-1}(a)=0\,,

and so,
a-E_{n-1}(a)\in B_{n}
. Thus for
n\geq 1
, we have that
A_{n}\cong A_{n-1}\oplus B_{n}
. Proceeding in a recursive fashion we have that

A_{n}\cong B_{0}\oplus B_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus B_{n}\,.

Thus, if
a\in A_{n}
, we have that

a=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\,,\quad\textrm{where }a_{j}\in B_{j}\,,\textrm{ and }a_{0}=\mathcal{E}_{0}(a)\,,a_{j}=\mathcal{E}_{j}(a)-\mathcal{E}_{j-1}(a)

More generally, we can associate to any
a\in A
a unique formal series

a=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}\,,\quad\textrm{where }a_{j}\in B_{j}\,,\textrm{ and }a_{0}=\mathcal{E}_{0}(a)\,,a_{j}=\mathcal{E}_{j}(a)-\mathcal{E}_{j-1}(a)

Since
\mathcal{E}_{j}
are bimodular projections, it follows that

\|a_{0}\|\leq\Omega\|a\|\textrm{ and that }\|a_{j}\|\leq 2\Omega\|a\|\textrm{ for }j\geq 1\,.

We have the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
a\in B_{n}
and
b\in B_{m}
, then
ab\in B_{n}
if
n>m
,
ab\in B_{m}
if
m>n
, and
ab\in A_{n}
if
n=m
.

Proof.

If
n>m
, then
A_{m}\subseteq A_{n}
, however,
A_{n}
is an algebra, thus
ab\in A_{n}
. Since
E_{n-1}
is a bimodular projection and
a\in B_{n}
, we have that

E_{n-1}(ab)=E_{n-1}(a)b=0\,.

Thus,
ab\in B_{n}
. The proof for
n<m
is the same and if
n=m
, we can only guarantee
ab\in A_{n}
.
∎

We are now ready to state a suitable definition of a rapid decay subalgebra.

Definition:

Let
A
be a C
∗
-algebra and
B_{n}
’s defined as above, let
\{\lambda_{n}\}
be an increasing sequence of numbers such that

\lambda_{n}\geq 2\Omega(n+1).

(3.2)

We define
A_{\textrm{RD}}
to be a
rapid decay (RD)
subalgebra in
A
by

A_{\textrm{RD}}=\left\{a=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}:a_{n}\in B_{n}\,,\,\|a\|_{N}:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|a_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}<\infty\,,N=0,1,\ldots\right\}.

First notice that if
a\in A_{\textrm{RD}}
, then its series representation is norm convergent in
A
using the Weierstrass
M
-test and the fact that
\|a\|\leq\|a\|_{0}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\|a_{n}\|<\infty
.
The
a_{n}
’s in this expansion are unique since they are given by the bimodular projections
\mathcal{E}_{n}
applied to
a
.

It is clear that
\|\cdot\|_{N}
defines a norm for each
N
, so it follows that
A_{\textrm{RD}}
must be a subspace of
A
. Moreover, since the partial sums of
a
converge in
\|\cdot\|
, it follows that
A_{\textrm{RD}}
is dense in
A
.
What is not immediately clear is that
\|\cdot\|_{N}
is submultiplicative which we verify using the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
a\in A_{n}
, then
\|a\|_{N}\leq\|a\|\lambda_{n}^{N+1}

Proof.

Since
a\in A_{n}
, we use its finite expansion to get

\|a\|_{N}=\|a_{0}+\cdots+a_{n}\|_{N}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\|a_{j}\|\lambda_{j}^{N}\leq 2\Omega\lambda_{n}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\|a\|=2\Omega(n+1)\lambda_{n}^{N}\|a\|\leq\|a\|\lambda_{n}^{N+1},

where we used that fact that the
\lambda_{n}
’s form an increasing sequence, the fact that
\|a_{j}\|\leq 2\Omega\|a\|
and assumption (
3.2
).
∎

We refer to norms
\|\cdot\|_{N}
as RD norms.

Proposition 3.3
.

The RD norms
\|\cdot\|_{N}
are submultiplicative norms for
N\geq 1
. Moreover we have that

\|ab\|_{0}\leq\|a\|_{1}\|b\|_{0}\textrm{ and }\|ab\|_{0}\leq\|a\|_{0}\|b\|_{1}\,.

Proof.

For
a,b\in A_{\textrm{RD}}
we expand
a
and
b
in their respective series representations, and thus by the triangle inequality we have that

\displaystyle\|ab\|_{N}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}b_{m}\right\|=\left\|\sum_{n>m}a_{n}b_{m}+\sum_{n<m}a_{n}b_{m}+\sum_{n=m}a_{n}b_{m}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n>m}\|a_{n}b_{m}\|_{N}+\sum_{n<m}\|a_{n}b_{m}\|_{N}+\sum_{n=m}\|a_{n}b_{m}\|_{N}

Using Lemma
3.1
, notice that for
n>m
,
a_{n}b_{m}\in B_{n}
, for
n<m
,
a_{n}b_{m}\in B_{m}
and for
n=m
,
a_{n}b_{m}\in A_{n}
. Thus by Lemma
3.2
we have that

\displaystyle\|ab\|_{N}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n>m}\|a_{n}\|\|b_{m}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}+\sum_{n<m}\|a_{n}\|\|b_{m}\|\lambda_{m}^{N}+\sum_{n}\|a_{n}\|b_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N+1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\neq m}\|a_{n}\|\|b_{m}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}\lambda_{m}^{N}+\sum_{n}\|a_{n}\|b_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N+1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m,n}\|a_{n}\|\|b_{m}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}\lambda_{m}^{N}=\|a\|_{N}\|b\|_{N}\,.

where the last inequality follows if
N\geq 1
.
∎

Our next goal is to establish the PBE property of the
N
-norms
\|\cdot\|_{N}
. This is done using a perturbative argument and it is broken into several steps described in the following lemmas.

For any natural
n
and
a\in A_{\textrm{RD}}
, write
a=a_{\leq n}+a_{>n}
where

a_{\leq n}=\sum_{k\leq n}a_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad a_{>n}=\sum_{k>n}a_{k}\,,

with
a_{k}\in B_{k}
. Note that
a_{\leq n}
is always a finite sum and is an element of
A_{n}
; we will refer to this as the
head
of
a
while
a_{>n}
is the
tail
of
a
.

To control the size of the tail of
a
in
A_{\textrm{RD}}
we need the following estimate.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any
\alpha>0
we have

\|a_{>n}\|_{N}\leq\dfrac{1}{\lambda_{n+1}^{\alpha}}\|a_{>n}\|_{N+\alpha}.

Proof.

\displaystyle\|a_{>n}\|_{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k>n}\|a_{k}\|\lambda_{k}^{N}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{n+1}^{\alpha}}\sum_{k>n}\|a_{k}\|\lambda_{k}^{N+\alpha}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{n+1}^{\alpha}}\|a_{>n}\|_{N+\alpha}.

∎

Next we estimate the exponentials of heads for self-adjoint
a
in
\mathcal{A}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
a\in A_{\textrm{RD}}
and self-adjoint then

\|e^{ia_{\leq n}}\|_{N}\leq\lambda_{n}^{N+1}.

Proof.

Since
a
is self-adjoint, it follows that
a_{\leq n}
is self-adjoint. Moreover, since
A_{n}
is an algebra, we have that
e^{ia_{\leq n}}\in A_{n}
and thus the result follows from Lemma
3.2
.
∎

Products of heads and tails are estimated as follows.

Lemma 3.6
.

If
a,b\in A_{\textrm{RD}}
then

\|a_{\leq n}b_{>n}\|_{N}\leq\|a_{\leq n}\|_{0}\|b_{>n}\|_{N}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\|b_{>n}a_{\leq n}\|_{N}\leq\|a_{\leq n}\|_{0}\|b_{>n}\|_{N}.

Proof.

First observe that

a_{\leq n}b_{>n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\sum_{l>n}b_{l}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{l>n}a_{k}b_{l}

and that
a_{k}b_{l}\in B_{l}
by Lemma
3.1
. Thus, by this observation we have that

\|a_{\leq n}b_{>n}\|_{N}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{l>n}\|a_{k}b_{l}\|\lambda_{l}^{N}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}\|a_{k}\|\sum_{l>n}\|b_{l}\|\lambda_{l}^{N}=\|a_{\leq n}\|_{0}\|b_{>n}\|_{N}\,.

A similar argument works for the other inequality.
∎

The key step in estimating sums of heads and tails is the use of Trotter’s formula which separates the corresponding exponentials.

Lemma 3.7
.

If
a\in A_{\textrm{RD}}
and self-adjoint then

\|e^{ita}\|_{N}\leq\lambda_{n}^{N+1}\exp\left(|t|\lambda_{n}^{2}\|a_{>n}\|_{N}\right)

for every natural
n
and
N\geq 1
.

Proof.

By Trotter’s formula we have that

e^{ita}=\lim\limits_{j\to\infty}\left(e^{ita_{\leq n}/j}e^{ita_{>n}/j}\right)^{j},

and note that convergence is in any submultiplicative norm on the algebra.

The terms in Trotter’s formula can be written as

\displaystyle\left(e^{ita_{\leq n}/j}e^{ita_{>n}/j}\right)^{j}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{l=1}^{j}e^{ilta_{\leq n}/j}e^{ita_{>n}/j}e^{-ilta_{\leq n}/j}\right)e^{ita_{\leq n}}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{l=1}^{j}\exp\left(\frac{it}{j}e^{ilta_{\leq n}/j}a_{>n}e^{-ilta_{\leq n}/j}\right)\right)\exp\left(ita_{\leq n}\right).

By submultiplicativeness of the
N
-norms for
N\geq 1
, we have that

\displaystyle\left\|\left(e^{ita_{\leq n}/j}e^{ita_{>n}/j}\right)^{j}\right\|_{N}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\prod_{l=1}^{j}\exp\left\|\frac{t}{j}e^{ilta_{\leq n}/j}a_{>n}e^{-ilta_{\leq n}/j}\right\|_{N}\right)\left\|\exp\left(ita_{\leq n}\right)\right\|_{N}.

By Lemma
3.6
we get

\left\|e^{ilta_{\leq n}/j}a_{>n}e^{-ilta_{\leq n}/j}\right\|_{N}\leq\left\|e^{ilta_{\leq n}/j}\right\|_{0}\|a_{>n}\|_{N}\left\|e^{-ilta_{\leq n}/j}\right\|_{0}.

Using Lemma
3.5
, we then get

\displaystyle\left\|\left(e^{ita_{\leq n}/j}e^{ita_{>n}/j}\right)^{j}\right\|_{N}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{n}^{N+1}\exp\left(\frac{|t|}{j}\sum_{l=1}^{j}\lambda_{n}^{2}\|a_{>n}\|_{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n}^{N+1}\exp\left(|t|\lambda_{n}^{2}\|a_{>n}\|_{N}\right).

Note that this estimate is independent of
j
and consequently we get that

\|e^{ita}\|_{N}\leq\lambda_{n}^{N+1}\exp\left(|t|\lambda_{n}^{2}\|a_{>n}\|_{N}\right).

∎

Theorem 3.8
.

With the above notation, the RD norms
\|\cdot\|_{N}
have the PBE property.

Proof.

By Lemmas
3.7
and
3.4
, using
\alpha=3
, we have that

\displaystyle\|e^{ita}\|_{N}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{n}^{N+1}\exp{\left(|t|\lambda_{n}^{2}\|a_{>n}\|_{N}\right)}\leq\lambda_{n}^{N+1}\exp{\left(|t|\frac{\lambda_{n}^{2}}{\lambda_{n+1}^{3}}\|a_{>n}\|_{N+3}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{n}^{N+1}\exp{\left(\frac{|t|}{\lambda_{n+1}}\|a\|_{N+3}\right)}

where we used the fact that the
\lambda_{n}
’s are an increasing sequence of numbers and the fact that
\|a_{>n}\|_{N}\leq\|a\|_{N}
. Notice that this works for any
n
. Now, for
|t|\geq 1
, we choose
n
so that

\lambda_{n}\leq|t|<\lambda_{n+1}\,.

Using this in the above inequality yields

\|e^{ita}\|_{N}\leq|t|^{N+1}e^{\|a\|_{N+3}}

which establishes the polynomially bounded exponential property.
∎

As mentioned in the previous section, the above theorem implies that
A_{\textrm{RD}}
are smooth subalgebras of
A
.

Let
\{\lambda_{n}\}
and
\{l_{n}\}
be two sequences of numbers both increasing and with limits equal to infinity. Then we can write two different types of RD norms:

\|a\|_{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|a_{n}\|l_{n}^{N}\quad\textrm{and}\quad|a|_{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|a_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}\,.

The natural question that arises is: When are the two families of these norms equivalent?

If the families of these norms are equivalent then there exist a constant
C_{1}
and
N^{\prime}
depending on
N
so that
\|a\|_{N}\leq C_{1}|a|_{N^{\prime}}
for all
a\in A_{\textrm{RD}}
. This says that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|a_{n}\|l_{n}^{N}\leq C_{1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|a_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N^{\prime}}\,.

In particular, if
a_{n}=a
for
n=k
with
\|a\|=1
, and
a_{n}=0
else, then
l_{k}^{N}\leq C_{1}\lambda_{k}^{N^{\prime}}
. So, there exist constants,
C
and
\alpha
depending on
N
such that

l_{k}\leq C\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}

for all
k
. Similarly, there exists constants
C^{\prime}
and
\alpha^{\prime}
depending on
N
and
N^{\prime}
such that for all
k
we have

\lambda_{k}\leq C^{\prime}l_{k}^{\alpha^{\prime}}.

On the other hand, if the sequences
\{\lambda_{n}\}
and
\{l_{n}\}
satisfy the two inequalities above, it follows that
\{\|\cdot\|_{N}\}
and
\{|\cdot|_{N}\}
are equivalent families of norms. In particular, if one of those families has the PBE property, so does the other. This means in particular that the condition (
3.2
) can be weaken and still lead to norms with the PBE property.

4.
Examples

4.1.
Convergent Sequences

This is the easiest motivating example.

A non-unital C
∗
-algebra in this example is the algebra of sequences convergent to zero:

c_{0}:=\left\{x=(x_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}:\lim_{k\to\infty}x_{k}=0\right\}

equipped with coordinate-wise addition and multiplication and the supremum norm.

Subalgebras
A_{n}
are defined to be the algebras of sequences that are eventually zero:

A_{n}:=\{x\in c_{0}:x_{k}=0\text{ for }k>n\}

Natural conditional expectations
E_{n}:A_{n+1}\to A_{n}
are defined by

E_{n}(x)_{k}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &x_{k}&&\textrm{if }k\leq n\\
&0&&\textrm{else.}\end{aligned}\right.

Consequently, subspaces
B_{n}=\ker E_{n-1}
are one-dimensional and spanned by delta sequences
\delta_{n}
defined by

\delta_{n,k}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1&&\textrm{if }k=n\\
&0&&\textrm{else.}\end{aligned}\right.

It follows that the Fourier decomposition of
x\in c_{0}
is

x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x_{n}\delta_{n}\,.

We can now easily compute the RD norms:

\|x\|_{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|x_{n}|\,\lambda_{n}^{N}\,.

Thus, with the choice
\lambda_{n}=1+n
, the corresponding RD algebra coincides with the usual concept of rapid decay sequences.

4.2.
Odometers

In this example we compare the general scheme from the previous section with the results from
[
16
]
. Some of the material in this subsection is relevant for the other examples.

4.2.1.
Definitions

Let
S
be an infinite supernatural number. The odometer space
{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}
can be defined as an inverse limit

{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}:=\lim_{\underset{s|S}{\longleftarrow}}{\mathbb{Z}}/s{\mathbb{Z}}\,,

where the corresponding projection homomorphisms are reductions modulo
s
. It is a compact Abelian group and a Cantor space. In the special case of
S=p^{\infty}
for a prime
p
this is the space of
p
-adic integers
[
8
]
.

The Pontryagin dual space of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}
is the corresponding direct limit and can be identified with the following discrete group:

\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\textrm{ there is }s|S\textrm{ such that }z^{s}=1\},

see
[
10
]
.

We choose a scale
s=(s_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
for the supernatural number
S
, which is a sequence of positive integers such that
s_{m}
divides
s_{m+1}
,
s_{m}<s_{m+1}
, and such that
S=\mathrm{lcm}(s_{m})
. Set
s_{0}:=1
.
Then,
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}
is the direct limit of the following finite subgroups of
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}
:

G_{m}:=\{e^{2\pi ij/s_{m}}:j=0,\ldots,s_{m}-1\}.

In this example, the C
∗
-algebra
A=C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
is the unital commutative algebra of continuous functions on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}
, which is isomorphic to the C
∗
-algebra of the discrete Abelian group
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}
.

For a
z\in\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}
, let
\chi_{z}\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
be the corresponding character function on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}
. The subalgebras
A_{n}
, denoted below by
C_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
are defined as functions on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}
which are given by a finite Fourier series whose sum is over the characters in
G_{n}
:

C_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}_{S}):=\left\{\sum_{z\in G_{n}}\hat{f}_{z}\chi_{z}:\hat{f}_{z}\in{\mathbb{C}}\right\}.

These subaglebras are also used in the following subsections.

Let
dx
be the normalized Haar measure on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}
. To any continuous function
f
on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}
one can associate the usual Fourier series:

\sum_{z\in\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}}\hat{f}_{z}\chi_{z},

(4.1)

where

\hat{f}_{z}=\int_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}f(x)\chi_{\bar{z}}(x)\,dx

This series is not always convergent but the Fourier coefficients
\hat{f}_{z}
determine
f
. Using this Fourier series we see that the
subalgebra
C_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
can be alternatively characterized as consisting of those continuous functions
f
such that for every
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}

f(x+s_{n})=f(x).

4.2.2.
Conditional Expectations

Let
\alpha:C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})\to C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
be the automorphism of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
given by

\alpha f(x)=f(x+1).

We define
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}:C_{n+1}({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})\to C_{n+1}({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
by

{\mathbb{E}}_{n}(f):=\frac{s_{n}}{s_{n+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n+1}/s_{n}-1}\alpha^{js_{n}}(f).

We have the following result.

Proposition 4.1
.

{\mathbb{E}}_{n}
is a conditional expectation onto
C_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
and

{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\left(\sum_{z\in G_{n+1}}\hat{f}_{z}\chi_{z}\right)=\sum_{z\in G_{n}}\hat{f}_{z}\chi_{z}

(4.2)

Proof.

First note that applying
\alpha
to a character function
\chi_{z}
yields

\alpha\chi_{z}(x)=\chi_{z}(x+1)=\chi_{z}(x)\chi_{z}(1)=z\chi_{z}(x).

We compute
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}
on the character functions:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\chi_{z}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{s_{n}}{s_{n+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n+1}{s_{n}}-1}\alpha^{js_{n}}\chi_{z}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{s_{n}}{s_{n+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n+1}/s_{n}-1}z^{js_{n}}\chi_{z}=\dfrac{s_{n}}{s_{n+1}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n+1}/s_{n}-1}z^{js_{n}}\right)\chi_{z}.

If
z^{s_{n}}=1
, then
z\in G_{n}
and so
z^{js_{n}}=1
. Then the above is equal to

\dfrac{s_{n}}{s_{n+1}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n+1}/s_{n}-1}1\right)\chi_{z}=\chi_{z}.

On the other hand, if
z^{s_{n}}\neq 1
then
z\in G_{n+1}\setminus G_{n}
and so

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\chi_{z}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{s_{n}}{s_{n+1}}\left(\dfrac{1-z^{\frac{s_{n+1}}{s_{n}}s_{n}}}{1-z^{s_{n}}}\right)\chi_{z}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{s_{n}}{s_{n+1}}\dfrac{1-z^{s_{n+1}}}{1-z^{s_{n}}}\chi_{z}=0.

We also get the following norm estimate:

\displaystyle\|{\mathbb{E}}_{n}f\|=\left\|\dfrac{s_{n}}{s_{n+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n+1}/s_{n}-1}\alpha^{js_{n}}f\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{s_{n}}{s_{n+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n+1}/s_{n}-1}\left\|\alpha^{js_{n}}f\right\|

\displaystyle=\dfrac{s_{n}}{s_{n+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n+1}/s_{n}-1}\|f\|=\dfrac{s_{n}}{s_{n+1}}\dfrac{s_{n+1}}{s_{n}}\|f\|=\|f\|.

Tomiyama’s Theorem tells us that
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}
is a conditional expectation.
∎

It follows from formula (
4.2
) that

B_{n}(S):=\ker{\mathbb{E}}_{n-1}=\left\{\sum_{z\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}}\hat{f}_{z}\chi_{z}:\hat{f}_{z}\in{\mathbb{C}}\right\}.

Thus, if
f
is given by the Fourier series (
4.1
), we have

\|f\|_{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|f_{n}\|\,\lambda_{n}^{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left\|\sum_{z\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}}\hat{f}_{z}\chi_{z}\right\|\,\lambda_{n}^{N}.

Our general scheme from the previous section implies that the algebra:

C_{\textrm{RD}}({\mathbb{Z}}_{S}):=\left\{f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_{n}:f_{n}\in B_{n}(S)\,,\,\|f\|_{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|f_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}<\infty\,,N=0,1,\ldots\right\}

is a smooth subalgebra of
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
.

4.2.3.
Alternative Norms

First we summarize relevant results from
[
16
]
.

Non-Archimedean length functions on
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}
are defined as functions
\lambda:\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}\to[1,\infty)
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

\lambda(z)=1
iff and only if
z=1
(normalization),

(2)

\lambda(z_{1}z_{2})\leq\text{max}\{\lambda(z_{1}),\lambda(z_{2})\}
(non-Archimedean property),

(3)

For every
r\geq 1
the set
\{z\in\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}:\lambda(z)\leq r\}
is finite (growth condition).

The basic examples of length functions are constructed as follows. First choose a scale
s=(s_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and then choose an increasing sequence
l=(\lambda_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of numbers in
(1,\infty)
such that
\lim_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{m}=\infty
. Set
\lambda_{0}:=1
. Given
s
and
l
the corresponding length function
\lambda_{s,l}
is defined as follows. If
z=1
we set
\lambda_{s,l}(1):=\lambda_{0}=1
. If
z\neq 1
we set:

\lambda_{s,l}(z)=\lambda_{s,l}\left(e^{\frac{2\pi ij}{s_{m}}}\right):=\lambda_{m},

for all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
0<j<s_{m}
such that
\frac{s_{m}}{s_{m-1}}
does not divide
j
.
The assumption that
\lim_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{m}=\infty
implies the growth property of
\lambda_{s,l}
.
It turns out that
\lambda_{s,l}
encompass all possible non-Archimedean length functions on
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}
.

Alternative norms on the space of series (
4.1
) are defined as follows
[
16
]
:

\|f\|_{N}^{*}:=\sum_{z\in\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}}|\hat{f}_{z}|\lambda(z)^{N}.

We say that a non-Archimedean length function
\lambda

grows fast enough
if there are positive numbers
\beta
and
c
such that for every
m
we have:

s_{m}\leq c\,(\lambda_{m})^{\beta}.

(4.3)

Under this stronger growth condition on length functions, namely that a non-Archimedean length function
\lambda
grows fast enough, it was proved in
[
16
]
that the above norms have the PBE property.

We have the following result.

Proposition 4.2
.

Assuming that
\lambda
grows fast enough and the same sequence
(\lambda_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is used in the definition of
\{\|\cdot\|_{N}\}
, the two sets of norms
\{\|\cdot\|_{N}\}
and
\{\|\cdot\|^{*}_{N}\}
are equivalent.

Proof.

Notice that

\|f_{n}\|=\left\|\sum_{z\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}}\hat{f}_{z}\chi_{z}\right\|\leq\sum_{z\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}}|\hat{f}_{z}|.

It follows immediately that
\|f\|_{N}\leq\|f\|_{N}^{*}
.

On the other hand, for
z\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}
by definition we get

\hat{f}_{z}=\int_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}f_{n}(x)\overline{\chi_{z}}(x)\,dx\,,

and so
|\hat{f}_{z}|\leq\|f_{n}\|
. Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{N}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{z\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}}\|f_{n}\|\,\lambda_{n}^{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(s_{n}-s_{n-1})\|f_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{n}\|f_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}\leq const\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|f_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N+\beta}=const\|f\|_{N+\beta}\,.

Thus, the two families of norms are equivalent.

∎

4.3.
Super Dihedral Groups

4.3.1.
Definitions

The dihedral group
D_{n}
is the semidirect product of
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
acting on the cyclic group

{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}
via the automorphism

\varkappa(g)=g^{-1}

for
g\in{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}
.
For any Abelian group
G
, the generalized dihedral group of
G
, denoted Dih
(G)
, is the semidirect product
\mathrm{Dih}(G)=G\rtimes_{\varkappa}{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
of
G
and
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
acting on the group

G
via the above inversion automorphism
\varkappa
.
Specializing this definition to discrete Abelian groups
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}
, we define super dihedral groups:

\mathrm{Dih}(\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}})=\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}\rtimes_{\varkappa}{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}.

The main objects of study in this example are the group C
∗
-algebras of super dihedral groups:

SD(S):=C^{*}(\mathrm{Dih}(\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}})).

We start by discussing the structure of the C
∗
-algebra
SD(S)
. First recall a few facts on amenability of discrete groups. A good source on amenability is
[
17
]
.
Finite groups are amenable as are direct limits of amenable groups. In particular, since
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}
is the direct limit of finite cyclic groups, it is amenable.
A group extension of an amenable group by an amenable group is again amenable. In particular, super dihedral groups are amenable.

The reduced group C
∗
-algebra of a group
G
is isomorphic to the non-reduced group C
∗
-algebra defined above if and only if
G
is amenable. Thus, if
G
is a super dihedral group, then

SD(S)=C^{*}(\mathrm{Dih}(\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}))\cong C_{r}^{*}(\mathrm{Dih}(\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}})).

By example 3.16 (Semidirect Products of Abelian Groups) of
[
20
]
we have
SD(S)
is isomorphic with the crossed product algebra:

SD(S)\cong C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})\rtimes_{\kappa}{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\,.

Here
\kappa:C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})\to C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
is given by

\kappa(f)(x)=f(-x).

Explicitly, by universality of crossed products,
SD(S)
can be described as the universal C
∗
-algebra with generators and relations as follows:

SD(S)\cong C^{*}(f,v:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S}),v^{*}v=vv^{*}=v^{2}=I,vfv=\kappa(f)).

In particular, we have
v^{*}=v
.

We define the automorphism
\gamma:SD(S)\to SD(S)
, initially on generators, by

\gamma(f)=f\ \text{ and }\ \gamma(v)=-v.

Since it preserves the relations in
SD(S)
it extends to an automorphism on the whole algebra.

It follows from the relations that any element
a
of
SD(S)
can be written in the form

a=f+vg,

with
f,g\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
. We can use the automorphism
\gamma
to see that this representation is unique since

a+\gamma(a)=2f\ \text{ and }\ v(a-\gamma(a))=2g.

We define natural subalgebras
A_{n}
of
SD(S)
by

A_{n}=\{f+vg:f,g\in C_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})\}\,.

Since the union
\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}C_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
is dense in
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
, the union

\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}A_{n}
is dense in
SD(S)
.

4.3.2.
Bimodular Projections

Recall that
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}:C_{n+1}({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})\to C_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
is a conditional expectation onto
C_{n}({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
. Define
E_{n}:A_{n+1}\to A_{n}
by

E_{n}(a)={\mathbb{E}}_{n}(f)+v{\mathbb{E}}_{n}(g)\,.

We have the following proposition:

Proposition 4.3
.

For any
n
,
E_{n}
is a bimodular projection onto
A_{n}
and
E_{n}(a^{*})=\left(E_{n}(a)\right)^{*}
. Moreover, if
m>n
, then
\|E_{m,n}\|\leq 2
where
E_{m,n}=E_{n}\circ\cdots\circ E_{m-1}
.

Proof.

It is clear that
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}(\bar{f})=\overline{{\mathbb{E}}_{n}(f)}
. Moreover, a direct calculation shows that

{\mathbb{E}}_{n}(\kappa f)=\kappa({\mathbb{E}}_{n}(f))\,.

We get

E_{n}(a^{*})=E_{n}(\bar{f}+v\kappa\bar{g})={\mathbb{E}}_{n}(\bar{f})+v{\mathbb{E}}_{n}(\kappa\bar{g})=\left(E_{n}(a)\right)^{*}.

Let
a=f_{1}+vg_{1}
be in
A_{n+1}
and
b=f_{2}+vg_{2}
be in
A_{n}
. Then using the above fact that
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}
commutes with
\kappa
, we get

\displaystyle E_{n}(ab)

\displaystyle=E_{n}\left((f_{1}+vg_{1})(f_{2}+vg_{2})\right)={\mathbb{E}}_{n}\left(f_{1}f_{2}+\kappa(g_{1})g_{2}\right)+v{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\left(g_{1}f_{2}+\kappa(f_{1})g_{2}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{n}(f_{1})f_{2}+{\mathbb{E}}_{n}(\kappa(g_{1}))g_{2}+v\left({\mathbb{E}}_{n}(g_{1})f_{2}+{\mathbb{E}}_{n}(\kappa(f_{1}))g_{2}\right)=E_{n}(a)b.

Similarly, if
a\in A_{n},b\in A_{n+1}
,
E_{n}(ab)=aE_{n}(b)
.

Recall that
\gamma:SD(S)\to SD(S)
defined by
\gamma(f)=f,\gamma(v)=-v
is an automorphism of
SD(S)
. If
a=f+vg
then

f=\frac{a+\gamma(a)}{2}=\left(\frac{1+\gamma}{2}\right)a\quad\textrm{and}\quad g=v\left(\frac{a-\gamma(a)}{2}\right)=v\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{2}\right)a\,.

It follows that
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq\|a\|
and
\|g\|_{\infty}\leq\|a\|
.

Then

\|E_{m,n}(a)\|\leq\|{\mathbb{E}}_{m,n}(f)\|+\|{\mathbb{E}}_{m,n}(g)\|\leq\|f\|_{\infty}+\|g\|_{\infty}\leq 2\|a\|

where
{\mathbb{E}}_{m,n}={\mathbb{E}}_{n}\circ\cdots\circ{\mathbb{E}}_{m-1}
for
m>n
.
∎

It follows from the definition that we have

B_{n}=\ker E_{n-1}=\left\{f+vg:f,g\in B_{n}(S)\right\}

where we recall that

B_{n}(S)=\ker{\mathbb{E}}_{n-1}=\left\{\sum_{z\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}}\hat{h}_{z}\chi_{z}\right\}\,.

The RD norms
\|\cdot\|_{N}
are given by

\|f+vg\|_{N}=\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(f_{n}+vg_{n})\right\|_{N}:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|f_{n}+vg_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}

where
f_{n},g_{n}\in B_{n}(S)
.

Notice that the RD norms defined here fit all of the properties of the general RD norms from Section 3 and thus the norms satisfy the PBE property. Moreover,

SD_{\textrm{RD}}(S):=\left\{a=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(f_{n}+vg_{n}):f_{n},g_{n}\in B_{n}(S)\,,\,\|a\|_{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|f_{n}+vg_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}<\infty\right\}

is a smooth subalgebra of
SD(S)
.

4.3.3.
Alternative Norms

Consider the following natural norms:

\|a\|_{N}^{\#}=\|f+vg\|_{N}^{\#}:=\|f\|^{*}_{N}+\|g\|^{*}_{N},

where the family
\{\|\cdot\|^{*}_{N}\}
of norms on
C_{RD}({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
was defined in the previous subsection. The norms
\|\cdot\|^{\#}_{N}
are submultiplicative and are easier to work with than the norms
\{\|\cdot\|_{N}\}
above.

We have the following result.

Proposition 4.4
.

Assuming that
\lambda
grows fast enough and the same sequence
(\lambda_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is used in the definition of
\{\|\cdot\|_{N}\}
, the two sets of norms
\{\|\cdot\|_{N}\}
and
\{\|\cdot\|^{\#}_{N}\}
are equivalent.

Proof.

We have:

\|f+vg\|_{N}^{*}=\|f\|_{N}+\|g\|_{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|f_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|g_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}

and so

\|f+vg\|_{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|f_{n}+vg_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}\leq\|f+vg\|_{N}^{*}.

With
\gamma
defined as above, we also get

\|f_{n}\|\leq\|f_{n}+vg_{n}\|\text{\ and\ }\|g_{n}\|\leq\|f_{n}+vg_{n}\|

and so
\|f+vg\|_{N}^{*}\leq 2\|f+vg\|
.
∎

4.4.
Bunce-Deddens Algebras

4.4.1.
Definitions

First we recall a definition of Bunce-Deddens algebras. We use the description from
[
15
]
and the notation established in the subsection on odometers above. In particular, we choose a non-Archimedean length function
\lambda
that grows fast enough.

Let
H=\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
,
\{E_{l}\}_{l\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
be the canonical basis in
H
, and
U:H\to H
be the shift operator on
H
:

UE_{l}=E_{l+1}\,.

For a continuous function
f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
we define an operator
M_{f}:H\to H
via:

M_{f}E_{l}=f(l)E_{l}\,.

Notice that
M_{f}
is a diagonal multiplication operator on
H
.
We have

UM_{f}U^{-1}=M_{\alpha(f)}

where
\alpha:C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})\to C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
is given by

\alpha f(x)=f(x+1).

We define the algebra
BD(S)
to be the C
∗
-algebra generated by operators
U
and
M_{f}
:

BD(S)=C^{*}(U,M_{f}:f\in C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S}))\,.

BD(S)
is isomorphic with the following crossed product algebra:

BD(S)\cong C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{Z}}\,.

It is known
[
7
]
that
BD(S)
is a simple C
∗
-algebra and it has a unique tracial state.

The subalgebras
A_{n}
are defined as finite sums over the characters in
G_{n}
:

A_{n}:=\left\{\sum_{z\in G_{n}}\hat{a}_{z}(U)M_{\chi_{z}}:\ \hat{a}_{z}\in C(S^{1})\right\}.

They can be alternatively characterized as consisting of those elements of
BD(S)
that satisfy

U^{s_{n}}aU^{-s_{n}}=a.

Since
G_{n}\subsetneqq G_{n+1}
, we have
A_{n}\subsetneqq A_{n+1}
. We need to verify that the union

\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}A_{n}
is dense in
BD(S)
. By definition of
BD(S)
, it follows that

\left\{\sum_{n}U^{n}M_{f_{n}}:f_{n}\in C_{\textrm{RD}}({\mathbb{Z}}_{S}),\textrm{ finite sums}\right\}

is dense in
BD(S)
. By expanding
f_{n}\in C_{RD}({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
in a Fourier series from equation (
4.1
):

f_{n}=\sum_{z\in\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}}\hat{f}_{n,z}\chi_{z},

and substituting this into the sum in the above set we get

\sum_{n}U^{n}M_{f_{n}}=\sum_{z\in\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}}\sum_{n}\left(\hat{f}_{n,z}U^{n}\right)M_{\chi_{z}}=\sum_{z\in\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}}\hat{a}_{z}(U)M_{\chi_{z}}

where we define

\hat{a}_{z}(U):=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}U^{n}M_{\hat{f}_{n,z}}\,.

It follows that
\hat{a}_{z}\in C^{\infty}(S^{1})
and that
\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}A_{n}
is dense in
BD(S)
.

4.4.2.
Conditional Expectations

We define
E_{n}:A_{n+1}\to A_{n+1}
by

E_{n}(a):=\frac{s_{n}}{s_{n+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n+1}/s_{n}-1}U^{js_{n}}aU^{-js_{n}}.

We have the following result.

Proposition 4.5
.

E_{n}
is a conditional expectation onto
A_{n}
and

E_{n}\left(\sum_{z\in G_{n+1}}\hat{a}_{z}(U)M_{\chi_{z}}\right)=\sum_{z\in G_{n}}\hat{a}_{z}(U)M_{\chi_{z}}.

Proof.

Let
a\in A_{n+1}
, then we write

a=\sum_{z\in G_{n+1}}\hat{a}_{z}(U)M_{\chi_{z}}\,.

Given an element of the form
a(U)M_{\chi_{z}}
, where
a
is a continuous function on the unit circle, by definition of
E_{n}
we have that

E_{n}\left(a(U)M_{\chi_{z}}\right)=a(U)M_{{\mathbb{E}}_{n}(\chi_{z})}\,.

However, by Proposition
4.1
, we have that

{\mathbb{E}}_{n}(\chi_{z})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\chi_{z}&&\textrm{ if }z\in G_{n}\\
&0&&\textrm{ if }z\in G_{n+1}\setminus G_{n}\,.\end{aligned}\right.

Thus if
a\in A_{n}
, it follows that
E_{n}(a)=a
and that
\textrm{ran }E_{n}=A_{n}
. Notice that

\|E_{n}(a)\|\leq\frac{s_{n}}{s_{n+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n+1}/s_{n}-1}\|U^{js_{n}}aU^{-js_{n}}\|\leq\frac{s_{n}}{s_{n+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n+1}/s_{n}-1}\|a\|=\|a\|\,.

Again, Tomiyama’s Theorem tells us that
E_{n}
is a conditional expectation.
∎

It follows from this proposition that

B_{n}=\textrm{ker }E_{n-1}=\left\{\sum_{z\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}}\hat{a}_{z}(U)M_{\chi_{z}}:\ \hat{a}_{z}\in C(S^{1})\right\}\,,

and

\|a\|_{N}=\left\|\sum_{z\in\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}}\hat{a}_{z}(U)M_{\chi_{z}}\right\|_{N}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}\left\|\sum_{z\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}}\hat{a}_{z}(U)M_{\chi_{z}}\right\|\lambda_{n}^{N}\,.

4.4.3.
Alternative Norms

The following norms were introduced in
[
11
]
to define a smooth subalgebra
BD_{\textrm{RD}}(S)
of
BD(S)
:

\|a\|_{M,N}=\sum_{z\in\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}_{S}}\|\hat{a}_{z}\|_{C^{M}}\lambda(z)^{N}.

It was proved in
[
11
]
that those norms have the PBE property. In what follows we relate those results with the general setup in this paper.

We have the following result.

Proposition 4.6
.

Assuming that
\lambda
grows fast enough, the two sets of norms
\{\|\cdot\|_{N}\}
and
\{\|\cdot\|_{0,N}\}
are equivalent.

Proof.

For
a\in BS(S)
, recall that

\|a\|_{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|a_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\|a\|_{0,N}=\sum_{z\in\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}_{S}}\|\hat{a}_{z}\|_{C^{0}(S^{1})}\lambda(z)^{N}

where
a_{n}\in B_{n}
and
\hat{a}_{z}
are continuous functions on
S^{1}
. Notice that

\|a_{n}\|\leq\sum_{z\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}}\|\hat{a}_{z}(U)\|\|M_{\chi_{z}}\|=\sum_{z\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}}\|\hat{a}_{z}\|_{C^{0}(S^{1})}\,,

and it therefore follows that
\|a\|_{N}\leq\|a\|_{0,N}
.

Next, for a
w\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}
, consider the following calculation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s_{n}}\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n}-1}U^{j}(a_{n}M_{\overline{\chi}_{w}})U^{-j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s_{n}}\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n}-1}\sum_{z\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}}\hat{a}_{z}(U)U^{j}M_{\chi_{zw^{-1}}}U^{-j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s_{n}}\sum_{z\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}}\hat{a}_{z}(U)\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n}-1}(zw^{-1})^{j}M_{\chi_{zw^{-1}}}=\hat{a}_{w}(U)

since the sum over
j
is only nonzero if
z=w
. Thus we have that

\|\hat{a}_{w}\|_{C^{0}(S^{1})}=\|\hat{a}_{w}(U)\|\leq\frac{1}{s_{n}}\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n}-1}\left\|U^{j}(a_{n}M_{\overline{\chi}_{w}})U^{-j}\right\|\leq\frac{1}{s_{n}}\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n}-1}\|a_{n}\|=\|a_{n}\|\,.

Using the above observation we get

\displaystyle\|a\|_{0,N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\widehat{Z}_{S}}\|\hat{a}_{z}\|_{C^{0}(S^{1})}\lambda(z)^{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{z\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}}\|\hat{a}_{z}\|_{C^{0}(S^{1})}\lambda_{n}^{N}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{z\in G_{n}\setminus G_{n-1}}\|a_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(s_{n}-s_{n-1})\|a_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{n}\|a_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N}\leq const\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|a_{n}\|\lambda_{n}^{N+\beta}=const\|a\|_{N+\beta}\,,

which establishes the two families of norms are equivalent.
∎

As a consequence, this proposition gives an alternative proof that the norms
\|\cdot\|_{0,N}
from
[
11
]
have the PBE property. Additionally, if
\delta_{\mathbb{L}}
is the derivation in
BD(S)
defined on generators by

\delta_{\mathbb{L}}(U)=U\quad\textrm{and}\quad\delta_{\mathbb{L}}(M_{f})=0\,,

then

\|a\|_{M,N}=\sum_{j=0}^{M}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}M\\
j\end{array}\right)\|\delta_{\mathbb{L}}^{j}(a)\|_{0,N}\,.

It follows from Proposition
2.1
that the norms
\|\cdot\|_{M,N}
have the PBE property and so

BD_{\textrm{RD}}(S):=\left\{a\in BD(S)\,,\,\|a\|_{M,N}<\infty,\,M,N=0,1,\ldots\right\}

is a smooth subalgebra of
BD(S)
.

4.5.
Uniformly Hyperfinite Algebras

4.5.1.
Definitions

Uniformly Hyperfinite Algebras (UHF) were introduced by James Glimm in
[
9
]
.

A UHF algebra is a unital C
∗
-algebra
A
for which there exists a chain

A_{0}\subseteq A_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq A

of unital subalgebras (i.e. subalgebras containing the unit of
A
) such that each
A_{n}
is
*
-isomorphic to a matrix algebra
M_{s_{n}}({\mathbb{C}})
, and the union

\cup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}A_{n}
is dense in
A
. We assume that
s_{0}=1
and
A_{0}
is the one dimensional subalgebra generated by the identity of
A
.

It follows from the structure of unital homomorphisms of matrix algebras that
s_{m}
divides
s_{m+1}
,
s_{m}<s_{m+1}
, and define a supernatural number
S
by
S=\mathrm{lcm}(s_{m})
. Thus, the sequence
(s_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}
is a scale for
S
.
Glimm showed that the supernatural number
S
is a complete invariant of UHF C
∗
-algebras.

We will denote a UHF algebra with supernatural number
S
by
UHF(S)
.
It is known
[
7
]
that
UHF(S)
is a simple C
∗
-algebra and it has a unique tracial state constructed from normalized traces on matrix subalgebras.

It is often useful to consider a concrete representation of
UHF(S)
. Let
\{E_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
be the standard orthonormal basis in the Hilbert space
H=\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
.

For
n=0,1,2,\ldots
and
0\leq x,y<s_{n}
define

P_{(n,x,y)}E_{k}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{k+x-y}&&\textrm{if }s_{n}|(k-y)\\
&0&&\textrm{else.}\end{aligned}\right.

Main properties of operators
P_{(n,x,y)}
are summarized in the following statement.

Proposition 4.7
.

With the above notation, the operators
P_{(n,x,y)}
have the following properties for all
n=0,1,2,\ldots
and
0\leq x,y,z,w<s_{n}
:

(1)

P_{(n,x,y)}^{*}=P_{(n,y,x)}

(2)

P_{(n,x,y)}P_{(n,w,z)}=\delta_{y,w}P_{(n,x,z)}

(3)

\sum\limits_{x=0}^{s_{n}-1}P_{(n,x,x)}=I

(4)

\sum\limits_{j=0}^{s_{n+1}/s_{n}-1}P_{(n+1,x+js_{n},y+js_{n})}=P_{(n,x,y)}

Proof.

The first item follows the observation that the inner product
(E_{l},P_{(n,x,y)}E_{k})
is not zero only if
l=k+x-y
and

s_{n}|(k-y)
, which is equivalent to
k=l+y-x
and
s_{n}|(l-x)
.

For the second item, consider the following calculation:

\displaystyle P_{(n,x,y)}P_{(n,w,z)}E_{k}

\displaystyle=P_{(n,x,y)}\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{k+w-z}&\textrm{ if }s_{n}|(k-z)\\
&0&\textrm{else}\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{k+w-z+x-y}&\textrm{ if }s_{n}|(k-z)\textrm{ and }s_{n}|(k+w-z-y)\\
&0&\textrm{else.}\end{aligned}\right.

Since
s_{n}|(k-z)
, it follows that
s_{n}|(w-y)
, but since
0\leq w,y<s_{n}
, it must be that
w=y
to have a nonzero outcome. In other words

P_{(n,x,y)}P_{(n,w,z)}=\delta_{wy}P_{(n,x,z)}\,.

For the third item notice that
P_{(n,x,x)}E_{k}=E_{k}
only if
s_{n}|(k-x)
, otherwise it is zero. Thus

\sum_{x=0}^{s_{n}-1}P_{(n,x,x)}E_{k}=E_{k}=IE_{k}\,,

because there is only one nonzero term in the sum, namely when
x=k\textrm{ mod }s_{n}
.

For the last item, notice that

P_{(n+1,x+js_{n},y+js_{n})}E_{k}=E_{k+x-y}

only if
s_{n+1}|(k-y-s_{n})
and zero otherwise. Like in the previous item, there is only one term in the sum below that produces a nonzero term, namely when
j=(k-y)/s_{n}\textrm{ mod }s_{n+1}/s_{n}
. It follows that

\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n+1}/s_{n}-1}P_{(n+1,x+js_{n},y+js_{n})}E_{k}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E_{k+x-y}&&\textrm{ if }s_{n}|(k-y)\\
&0&&\textrm{ else}\end{aligned}\right.=P_{(n,x,y)}E_{k}\,.

∎

For a fixed
n
let
A_{n}
be the C
∗
-algebra in
B(H)
generated by
P_{(n,x,y)}
with
0\leq x,y<s_{n}
and let
A
be the C
∗
-algebra in
B(H)
generated by all
P_{(n,x,y)}
with
n=0,1,2,\ldots
and
0\leq x,y<s_{n}
, so that
\cup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}A_{n}
is dense in
A
. By item 1 and 2 of the above proposition,
P_{(n,x,y)}
’s are matrix units and hence
A_{n}
is
*
-isomorphic to the matrix algebra
M_{s_{n}}({\mathbb{C}})
. By item 3, the subalgebras
A_{n}
are unital subalgebras of
A
and by item 4 we have
A_{n}\subseteq A_{n+1}
for all
n
. It follows that
A\cong UHF(S)
.

Operators
P_{(n,x,x)}
are diagonal operators in
H
with diagonal elements that are periodic with periods
s_{n}
. It follows
that the Abelian C
∗
-subalgebra of
UHF(S)
generated by all
P_{(n,x,x)}
operators is isomorphic to
C({\mathbb{Z}}_{S})
.
To define RD norms we choose an increasing sequence
l=(\lambda_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of numbers in
(1,\infty)
such that
\lim_{m\to\infty}\lambda_{m}=\infty
. Choosing a scale
s=(s_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
l
amounts to choosing a length function
\lambda_{s,l}
on
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}_{S}}
.

4.5.2.
Conditional Expectations

We now want to construct conditional expectations
E_{n}:A_{n+1}\to A_{n}
. This is done with the help of the following projections in
A_{n+1}
:

P_{n}:=\sum\limits_{x=0}^{s_{n}-1}P_{(n+1,x,x)}.

We define
E_{n}:A_{n+1}\to A_{n}
by

E_{n}\left(\sum_{0\leq x,y<s_{n+1}}a_{(x,y)}P_{(n+1,x,y)}\right)=\sum_{0\leq x,y<s_{n}}a_{(x,y)}P_{(n,x,y)}.

We have the following result.

Proposition 4.8
.

E_{n}
is a conditional expectation onto
A_{n}
.

Proof.

Note that

P_{(n,x,y)}=\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n+1}/{s_{n}}-1}P_{(n+1,x+js_{n},y+js_{n})}

and so

E_{n}(P_{(n,x,y)})=E_{n}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{s_{n+1}/{s_{n}}-1}P_{(n+1,x+js_{n},y+js_{n})}\right)=P_{(n,x,y)}

since
x+js_{n}
and
y+js_{n}
are larger than
s_{n}
for
j>0
. Thus
E_{n}
is a projection onto
A_{n}
.

Note that

P_{n}P_{(n+1,x,y)}P_{n}=\sum_{j,k=0}^{s_{n}-1}P_{(n+1,j,j)}P_{(n+1,x,y)}P_{(n+1,k,k)}=\begin{cases}P_{(n+1,x,y)}&\text{if\ }0\leq x,y<s_{n}\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}.

Let

a=\sum_{0\leq x,y<s_{n+1}}a_{(x,y)}P_{(n+1,x,y)}

and apply
P_{n}
to the left and right side of
a
:

P_{n}aP_{n}=\sum_{0\leq x,y<s_{n}}a_{(x,y)}P_{(n+1,x,y)}\,.

From this we get the following norm estimate

\left\|\sum_{0\leq x,y<s_{n}}a_{(x,y)}P_{(n+1,x,y)}\right\|\leq\|a\|\,.

Since
P_{(n+1,x,y)}
and
P_{(n,x,y)}
are matrix units, it follows that

\|E_{n}(a)\|=\left\|\sum_{0\leq x,y<s_{n}}a_{(x,y)}P_{(n,x,y)}\right\|=\left\|[a_{(x,y)}]_{x,y=0}^{s_{n}-1}\right\|=\left\|\sum_{0\leq x,y<s_{n}}a_{(x,y)}P_{(n+1,x,y)}\right\|\leq\|a\|

where
\|[a_{(x,y)}]_{x,y=0}^{s_{n}-1}\|
is the matrix norm. Thus, by Tomiyama’s Theorem,
E_{n}
is a conditional expectation.
∎

Let
\mathfrak{S}_{0}=(0,0)
and let

\mathfrak{S}_{n}=\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}:s_{n-1}\leq x<s_{n}\textrm{ or }s_{n-1}\leq y<s_{n}\}.

Notice that for a fixed
n
, the sets
\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}:0\leq x,y<s_{n-1}\}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are disjoint, and their union is the set
\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}:0\leq x,y<s_{n}\}
. Thus by a recurrence argument, the sets
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are disjoint and hence

\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{S}_{n}={\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}.

It follows from the definition of
E_{n}
that

B_{n}=\textrm{ker }E_{n-1}=\left\{\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}a_{(x,y)}P_{(n,x,y)}:a_{(x,y)}\in{\mathbb{C}}\right\}\,.

From our definition of
A_{\textrm{RD}}
we have that

UHF_{\textrm{RD}}(S)=\left\{a=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}a_{(x,y)}P_{(n,x,y)}\right):\|a\|_{N}<\infty,\,a_{(x,y)}\in{\mathbb{C}}\right\}

where

\|a\|_{N}=\sum_{n=0}\left\|\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}a_{(x,y)}P_{(n,x,y)}\right\|\lambda_{n}^{N}\,.

4.5.3.
Alternative Norms

We can define a more natural family of norms. For
a\in UHF_{\textrm{RD}}(S)
define

\|a\|_{N}^{\%}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}|a_{(x,y)}|\lambda_{n}^{N}\,.

As with the other examples we demonstrate that these collections of norms are equivalent.

Proposition 4.9
.

Assuming that
\lambda
grows fast enough, the two sets of norms
\{\|\cdot\|_{N}\}
and
\{\|\cdot\|_{N}^{\%}\}
are equivalent.

Proof.

It follows immediately from the definitions of
\|\cdot\|_{N}
and
\|\cdot\|_{N}^{\%}
that
\|a\|_{N}\leq\|a\|_{N}^{\%}
for
a\in UHF_{\textrm{RD}}(S)
. On the other hand for
(x,y)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
we have that

|a_{(x,y)}|\leq\left\|\sum_{(k,l)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}a_{(k,l)}P_{(n,k,l)}\right\|\,.

Therefore, we get that

\displaystyle\|a\|_{N}^{\%}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\left\|\sum_{(k,l)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}a_{(k,l)}P_{(n,k,l)}\right\|\lambda_{n}^{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(s_{n}^{2}-s_{n-1}^{2})\left\|\sum_{(k,l)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}a_{(k,l)}P_{(n,k,l)}\right\|\lambda_{n}^{N}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{n}^{2}\left\|\sum_{(k,l)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}a_{(k,l)}P_{(n,k,l)}\right\|\lambda_{n}^{N}\leq const\|a\|_{N+2\beta}\,.

∎

It now follows that the family of norms
\{\|\cdot\|_{N}^{\%}\}
have the PBE property.
