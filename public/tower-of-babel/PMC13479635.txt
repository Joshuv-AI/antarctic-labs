Impact of diabetes on outcomes in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: a GRADE meta-analysis
Authors: Seyedeh-Tarlan Mirzohreh, Niloofar Deravi, Elnaz Javanshir, Mohammad Asghari Jafarabadi, Neda Roshanravan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes mellitus (DM) is a common comorbidity in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) and may exacerbate arrhythmic risk, promote structural remodelling and worsen heart failure outcomes. Its overall prognostic impact and effect on cardiac structure and function in adults with HCM remain uncertain.MethodWe systematically searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and Cochrane to January 2025 for observational studies comparing adults with HCM-DM versus HCM without DM. Random-effects meta-analyses were performed to pool ORs for clinical outcomes and standardised mean differences (SMDs) for echocardiographic parameters. Certainty of evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework after Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) evaluation. Subgroup, sensitivity and heterogeneity analyses were undertaken.ResultEight studies encompassing approximately 47 592 patients met inclusion criteria. DM was associated with higher odds of all-cause mortality (OR 1.43, 95% CI 1.29 to 1.58; high certainty), heart failure (OR 1.34, 95% CI 1.25 to 1.43; moderate certainty) and atrial fibrillation (OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.18 to 1.68; high certainty). The association with atrial fibrillation was most pronounced in patients younger than 50 years (OR 2.55) and attenuated in those with body mass index ≥30 kg/m². HCM-DM was also linked to smaller left ventricular end-diastolic volumes (SMD −0.26) and impaired global longitudinal strain (SMD 0.58), consistent with subclinical systolic dysfunction, although heterogeneity was high and certainty low to moderate. Evidence for left ventricular ejection fraction, mass and septal thickness was inconclusive. Results were robust across sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsDM is a clinically important risk marker in HCM, associated with excess mortality, heart failure and atrial fibrillation, as well as adverse structural–functional changes. These findings support closer rhythm and function monitoring in HCM-DM and highlight the need for prospective studies to determine whether targeted metabolic interventions can improve outcomes.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420250650799.

Introduction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most common inherited cardiomyopathy and a major cause of sudden cardiac death in young adults.1 Diabetes mellitus (DM) affects over 10% of adults worldwide and is increasingly recognised as a modifier of cardiovascular disease outcomes. When coexistent, these two conditions may interact through additive metabolic, structural and microvascular stress, potentially worsening the prognosis of HCM.2

Several cohort studies have reported that patients with HCM with DM experience higher rates of adverse events, including mortality, heart failure (HF) and arrhythmias, as well as structural and functional cardiac changes.3–10 However, findings are inconsistent, particularly for measures such as left ventricular ejection fraction, mass and strain. Some studies are limited by small sample sizes, single-centre design, heterogeneous imaging protocols and varying definitions of outcomes. In addition, clinical and imaging endpoints have rarely been assessed together in a unified analysis.

No prior meta-analysis has comprehensively integrated both clinical outcomes and imaging parameters in this population. Therefore, in this systematic review and meta-analysis, we aimed to synthesise current evidence on the impact of DM on cardiac function and clinical outcomes in adults with HCM, and to assess the certainty of evidence using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework.

Methods

We adhered to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines11 (online supplemental S1) and registered the protocol in PROSPERO (CRD420250650799). The study was designed according to the Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICOs) framework.

Eligibility criteria

We included observational studies of adults with HCM, diagnosed using standard imaging criteria, that compared those with and without DM and reported clinical outcomes and/or echocardiographic or cardiac MRI parameters. Studies involving non-genetic hypertrophy, other cardiomyopathy types, paediatric populations or interventional designs were excluded. The full eligibility criteria are provided in online supplemental S2.

Information sources

We searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and Cochrane from inception to January 2025 without language restrictions. Manual searches of reference lists from eligible articles were also performed.

Search strategy

The database search strategy was developed with a medical librarian to ensure comprehensiveness and reproducibility. For example, the Scopus search used the following query: (TITLE-ABS-KEY (“Diabetes Mellitus” OR “insulin resistance” OR “hyperglycemia” OR diabet* OR prediabet* OR “Glycated hemoglobin A” OR hyperinsulinemia OR glycemi*)) AND (TITLE-ABS-KEY (“hypertrophic cardiomyopathy” OR “obstructive cardiomyopathy” OR “idiopathic hypertrophic sub aortic stenosis” OR “asymmetric septal hypertrophy” OR “hypertrophic heart disease” OR “muscular subaortic stenosis” OR “septal hypertrophy” OR “HCM”)). Detailed search strings for PubMed, Web of Science and Cochrane are provided in online supplemental S2.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and extracted data on study design, population characteristics, outcomes and imaging findings, resolving discrepancies by consensus.

Risk of bias and outcome quality assessment

We used the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool12 to assess risk of bias and graded the certainty of evidence using the GRADE framework.13 Detailed criteria for ROBINS-I scoring and GRADE upgrading/downgrading rules are in online supplemental S2.

Statistical analysis

Effect sizes were pooled as standardised mean differences (SMD) or ORs with 95% CIs using a random-effects model. Heterogeneity was assessed with the I² statistic (25%, 50% and 75% representing low, moderate and high levels, respectively). For I²≥50%, sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially excluding studies, and subgroup analyses were conducted according to clinically relevant factors (eg, age, body mass index (BMI)). Robustness was evaluated using the ‘one study removed’ method. Publication bias was assessed via funnel plots, Begg’s test and Egger’s test. All analyses were performed using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis, V.3 (Biostat, New Jersey, USA), with p<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection

The study flowchart is shown in figure 1; our search strategy revealed 561 studies in PubMed, 1333 in Scopus, 784 in Web of Science and 55 in Cochrane. After removing duplications, 2142 studies underwent title assessment. Of these, 196 studies were eligible for abstract review. After surveying abstracts, 12 studies were perused for full text. Finally, 8 studies were qualified to be included in this systematic review and meta-analysis, and the rest did not meet the inclusion criteria.

PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) flow diagram presenting results of search and screening strategy in databases.

Characteristics of studies

Eight studies3–10 involving 47 592 patients with HCM, with or without DM, were included from Egypt, China, the UK, the USA, Korea, Spain and Israel. Designs included prospective and retrospective cohorts, with sample sizes ranging from 20 patients with HCM-DM (Jex et al5) to 14 279 (Hajouli et al4), and follow-up from 2 to 10 years. Participants’ mean ages ranged from 45 to 70 years; most studies reported higher BMI in HCM-DM than HCM-only groups. DM duration was reported in only two studies (mean 10.7±6.6 years in Badran et al3 and >5 years in Lee et al6). Cardiac function was primarily assessed by echocardiography, with one study using Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR).5 Analyses of mortality, hospitalisation, HF and atrial fibrillation (AF) showed substantial heterogeneity by study type and patient characteristics (table 1).

Risk of bias assessment

Overall risk was low to moderate (~15%), with moderate bias mainly in confounding and missing data. Four studies had inadequate matching for BMI,3–5 7 two for hypertension (HTN),3 7 one for coronary artery disease (CAD)7 and Lee et al6 showed mismatching across multiple factors including age, CAD, chronic kidney disease, HTN and stroke. One study had high risk in participant selection due to missing exclusion criteria,10 and Mekhaimar et al7 had moderate risk by including only patients with HF. Failure to report DM duration in five studies4 5 8–104 contributed to moderate bias from missing data. Wasserstrum et al8 reported echo parameters as incidence rates rather than measured values, affecting reporting bias. Figures 2 and 3 summarise the ROBINS-I assessments.

Pooled analysis showed higher all-cause mortality in HCM-DM versus HCM (OR=1.42, 95% CI 1.23 to 1.65, p<0.001, I²=64%) (figure 4). Subgroup analysis showed stronger associations in younger patients (<50 years: OR=2.55, 95% CI 1.37 to 4.75, p<0.002; I²=0%) than older (>50 years: OR=1.31, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.53, p=0.003; I²=0%). No significant difference in LVEF was observed between HCM-DM and HCM groups (SMD=−0.11, 95% CI −0.33 to 0.10, p=0.30; I²=78%) (figure 7). Excluding Yu et al9 reduced heterogeneity to 29% without altering significance (SMD=−0.005, 95% CI −0.113 to 0.103, p=0.926). Subgroup analyses by BMI (<30 kg/m²: SMD=−0.42, 95% CI −0.90 to 0.06, p=0.09, I²=90%; ≥30 kg/m²: SMD=−0.01, 95% CI −0.40 to 0.37, p=0.93, I²=60%) and age (<50 years: SMD=0.02, 95% CI −0.17 to 0.23, p=0.80, I²=0%; ≥50 years: SMD=−0.21, 95% CI −0.53 to 0.10, p=0.18, I²=86%) showed no significant differences. Left ventricular end-diastolic volume

Meta-analysis of four studies showed smaller LVEDV in HCM-DM versus controls (SMD=−0.26, 95% CI −0.41 to –0.11, p<0.001; I²=0%) (figure 8). ‘One study removed’ analysis found no outliers, confirming result stability.

LVESD did not differ between groups (SMD=−0.01, 95% CI −0.33 to 0.29, p=0.91; I²=67%). Excluding Yu et al9 produced similar results (SMD=−0.17, 95% CI −0.36 to 0.01, p=0.06; I²=0%), with no outliers detected.

Left ventricular mass and Interventricular septal thickness

No significant differences were found for LVM (SMD=0.11, 95% CI −0.11 to 0.34, p=0.33; I²=0%) or IVST (SMD=0.10, 95% CI −0.03 to 0.24, p=0.16; I²=2.9%). ‘One study removed’ analysis detected no outliers, confirming consistency.

LVOT pressure gradient

Left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) gradient was higher in HCM-DM than HCM (SMD=0.30, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.51, p=0.004; I²= 0%).

LV peak systolic global strain rate

Meta-analysis of three studies showed lower (less negative) strain rate in HCM-DM (SMD=0.60, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.98, p=0.002; I²=54%). Excluding Badran et al3 reduced heterogeneity to 0% and increased the effect (SMD=0.80, 95% CI 0.44 to 1.16, p<0.001). Excluding Yu et al9 rendered the result non-significant (SMD=0.59, 95% CI −0.05 to 1.24, p=0.07).

Publication bias

Funnel plot analysis, Begg’s and Egger’s tests (online supplemental S4 document) were used to assess the publication bias. No significant publication bias was observed.

Discussion

In this meta-analysis of over 47 000 patients with HCM, coexisting DM identified a distinct higher-risk phenotype. DM was associated with significantly greater odds of all-cause mortality, HF and AF, with the AF signal strongest in patients younger than 50 years and attenuated in those with obesity. Imaging data showed smaller LVEDV and impaired GLS, while LVEF, LVM and IVST were not significantly different.

Our effect estimates align with and extend prior registry data. For example, the Korean National Health Insurance cohort reported a 15% higher HF risk in HCM-DM, compared with a 34% increase in our pooled analysis.6 AF risk was similarly consistent with EUR-Observational HCM Registry findings, where each decade of age increased AF risk by approximately 68%, comparable to the impact of DM in our meta-analysis.14 15 The stronger association in younger, non-obese patients may reflect the absence of competing obesity-related arrhythmia mechanisms, allowing the arrhythmogenic effects of DM to emerge more clearly.

Several mechanisms may underlie these adverse outcomes. Chronic hyperglycaemia promotes myocardial fibrosis, microvascular dysfunction and impaired energetics, amplifying the hypertrophic, diastolic and arrhythmogenic substrate of HCM.16 17 Insulin resistance and systemic inflammation accelerate atrial remodelling, consistent with the stronger AF signal in younger patients.18 Structurally, smaller LVEDV and larger left atrial diameter suggest reduced filling and chronic atrial pressure overload, while impaired GLS and lower strain rate indicate subclinical systolic dysfunction likely driven by metabolic stress and microvascular ischaemia.19

Cardiac function analysis demonstrated changes that, while modest in magnitude, are physiologically meaningful in HCM-DM. LV-GLS was significantly reduced (SMD≈0.58), a degree of impairment comparable to that seen in early-stage HF with preserved ejection fraction, indicating subclinical systolic dysfunction despite preserved EF. LAD was slightly but significantly larger (SMD≈0.2), consistent with incremental enlargement on pre-existing dilation; even small increases in atrial size may meaningfully raise arrhythmia risk, particularly when baseline enlargement is present.20 LVEDV was reduced, supporting impaired relaxation and diminished compliance, plausibly driven by myocardial fibrosis and microvascular dysfunction described in CMR studies.19 Concordantly, higher LVOT pressure gradients were observed in HCM-DM, likely reflecting the combined effects of smaller ventricular volumes, increased myocardial stiffness and augmented contractility associated with metabolic alterations.21 No significant differences emerged for LVEF, LVESD, LVM or IVST; however, heterogeneity for LVEF was high (I²≈78%), which may reflect differences in imaging methods, patient selection, diabetes severity and duration, and follow-up.22 A reduction in strain rate further reinforces the presence of subtle systolic impairment. Collectively, these findings argue for incorporating volumetric and strain-based assessments into risk stratification, closer rhythm and diastolic-function surveillance, and cautious use of preload-reducing therapies in susceptible patients.

This meta-analysis could not stratify outcomes by obstructive versus non-obstructive HCM, as most studies did not report subtype-specific data. Available evidence, however, suggests that DM adversely affects both phenotypes.3 10 While obstruction adds pressure overload, diabetes amplifies diastolic dysfunction through myocardial fibrosis, altered titin phosphorylation and collagen crosslinking from advanced glycation end products.6 23 Prior studies have shown that, even after adjusting for comorbidities, patients with HCM-DM have higher rates of LV diastolic dysfunction, pulmonary HTN, significant mitral regurgitation and worse dyspnoea, without significant differences in LV wall thickness, cavity size, systolic function or outflow tract gradients.8 This supports the concept that the main myocardial impact of diabetes in HCM is the worsening of diastolic function rather than structural hypertrophy.

Limitations and interpretation

Interpretation should consider the small number of eligible studies, most of which are recent and observational, with variable follow-up durations and incomplete reporting of key variables such as DM duration, BMI and hemoglobin-A1c (HbA1c). Heterogeneity in imaging measures was high, and most studies did not stratify by obstructive versus non-obstructive HCM, limiting phenotype-specific conclusions. Given the observational design, these associations do not prove causality, and DM should be viewed as a clinically important risk marker rather than a proven causal determinant.

Clinical and research implications

These findings support closer rhythm and function surveillance in patients with HCM-DM, potential incorporation of DM into risk stratification models and attention to metabolic control as part of comprehensive care. Future research should include large prospective cohorts with standardised imaging, examine obstructive versus non-obstructive subtypes, and assess whether targeted metabolic interventions can modify arrhythmia and HF risk in this population.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis highlights the significant impact of DM on the progression and outcomes of HCM, with higher rates of mortality, HF and arrhythmia compared with HCM in patients without DM. These findings suggest that DM contributes to structural and functional myocardial alterations, likely through increased fibrosis, vascular injury and reduced myocardial compliance, thereby worsening disease severity.
