Joint extreme values of L L -functions on and off the critical line
Athanasios Sourmelidis

Abstract
. It is shown that any number of distinct primitive GL ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathrm{GL}(1) and GL ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{GL}(2) L L -functions can simultaneously attain large values on the critical line. This is an unconditional improvement of a general result due to Heap and Li who have assumed the Riemann Hypothesis for more than three such L L -functions. The joint distribution of GL ⁡ ( m ) \mathrm{GL}(m) L L -functions to the right of the critical line is also studied under certain zero-density estimates. In particular, we can partially recover results of Inoue and Li on Dirichlet L L -functions and generally improve upon the work of Mahatab, Pańkowski and Vatwani on the class of L L -functions introduced by Selberg. The main machinery in both cases, on and off the critical line, is the resonance method of Soundararajan and Hilberdink/Voronin, respectively. On the critical line we additionally introduce a variation of Heath-Brown’s method for the fractional moments of the Riemann zeta-function which makes it possible to avoid using any information on the zero distribution of L L -functions whose degree is less than three.

1.
Introduction and Main results

The study of extreme values for zeta- and
L
-functions dates back to the work of Bohr, Landau, Littlewood and Titchmarsh
[
43
, Chapter VIII]
.
It had several renaissances over the past century, most notably through the work of Selberg
[
37
]
for the argument of the Riemann zeta-function
\zeta(s)
,
s:=\sigma+it\in\mathbb{C}
, on the
critical line

1/2+i\mathbb{R}
, the work of Levinson
[
27
]
and Montgomery
[
30
]
for
\zeta(s)
to the right of the critical line and the work of Balasubramanian and Ramachandra
[
4
]
for
\zeta(1/2+it)
.
The latest major addition to the mathematical toolkit that is utilized for addressing such problems has been provided by Soundararajan
[
39
]
who introduced the so-called
resonance method
.
Apart from improving upon
[
4
]
, the versatility of this technique allowed new developments into the subject of extreme values of
L
-functions in the
t
-aspect
(see
[
1
,
9
,
11
]
for some benchmarks) and in the
family/conductor aspect
(see
[
5
]
for a comprehensive treatment of the topic).

In recent years attention has also turned to joint/simultaneously extreme values (in both aspects) which are contained in the more general study of the joint value distribution of
L
-functions having Dirichlet coefficients of number-theoretic interest.
For a family of such functions
L_{h}(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{L_{h}}(s)n^{-s}
,
h\leq H
, there is a guiding principle that allows some degree of intuition on the results one can expect in such cases and it consists of three parts.
The
L
-functions should at first have an
analytic continuation
, modulo finitely many poles, beyond their half plane of absolute convergence since it is there that their value distribution yields important information on the arithmetic properties of
a_{L_{h}}(n)
.
Secondly, it is desirable for the
L
-functions to have an
Euler product
representation, for then they can, loosely speaking, be expressed as

L_{h}(s)\approx\exp\left\lparen\sum_{p}a_{L_{h}}(p)p^{-s}\right\rparen\times\left\lparen\text{contribution from zeros}\right\rparen.

If the contribution from the zeros of
L_{h}(s)
(the analytic factor of the above expression) is benign, then the sum over primes
p
(the arithmetic factor) determines the behavior of
L_{h}(s)
.
This can be very practical, though challenging to establish unconditionally, because the numbers
\log p
, prime
p
, are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Therefore, the functions
p^{-it}
, prime
p
, behave like i.i.d. random variables and the above model of
L_{h}
can be replaced by a random model which not only provides heuristics but it is also less rigid to handle.
The last prerequisite for studying the joint distribution of
L_{h}
,
h\leq H
, is for them to exhibit some sort of
independence on arithmetic level
, namely that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\log x}{x}\sum_{p\leq x}a_{L_{h}}(p)\overline{a_{L_{g}}(p)}\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{x\to+\infty}\kappa(h,g),

where
\kappa(h,g)\geq 0
and is non-zero only when
h=g
.
This condition ensures that the entries of the tuple
(L_{1},\dots,L_{H})
are uncorrelated and act independently in the complex plane.
In contemplation, this indicates that the state of the art on the value-distribution for a single
L
-function should also be within reach for a tuple of independent
L
-functions.

An example is given by a family of Dirichlet
L
-functions
L(s,\chi_{h}):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\chi_{h}(n)n^{-s}
, attached to nonequivalent Dirichlet characters
\chi_{h}
,
h\leq H
, in which case (
1.1
) follows from the orthogonality of characters and the prime number theorem.
Selberg
[
38
]
described how his celebrated central limit theorem for
\log\zeta(s)
could be adjusted to show that the functions

\frac{\log\lvert L\lparen\frac{1}{2}+it,\chi_{1}\rparen\rvert}{\sqrt{\pi\log\log t}},\,\frac{\arg{L\lparen\frac{1}{2}+it,\chi_{1}\rparen}}{\sqrt{\pi\log\log t}},\cdots,\frac{\log\lvert L\lparen\frac{1}{2}+it,\chi_{H}\rparen\rvert}{\sqrt{\pi\log\log t}},\,\frac{\arg{L_{H}\lparen\frac{1}{2}+it,\chi_{H}\rparen}}{\sqrt{\pi\log\log t}}

become distributed, in the limit range
t
, like independent random variables, each having Gaussian density
\exp(-\pi u^{2})\mathrm{d}u
.
Actually Selberg stated this in a more general setting, where he considers a class
\mathcal{S}
of
L
-functions (see Section
3
for the precise definitions) whose
primitive
elements satisfy, among other things, the three requirements of the principle previously mentioned.
A more systematic treatment of the value-distribution of
L
-functions from a subclass of
\mathcal{S}
and a rigorous proof of the Gaussian-like behavior of their logarithms on the critical line has been carried out by Bombieri and Hejhal
[
8
]
.
In general, the aforementioned classes and their variants, as they appear in the literature, are built by having in mind
L
-functions associated with automorphic representations of
\mathrm{GL}(m)
over
\mathbb{Q}
and, at least in the case of
\mathcal{S}
, it is conjectured that these
L
-functions are its only elements.

In the context of extreme values we would expect that each entry of a tuple
(L_{1},\dots,L_{H})
of independent
L
-functions can be large in magnitude (as depicted in
[
2
]
for example) simultaneously.
A first result was obtained by Mahatab, Pańkowski and Vatwani
[
29
]
in the case when
L_{h}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{p}oly}
,
h\leq H
, where
\mathcal{S}_{\text{poly}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
consists of the elements from
\mathcal{S}
that can be represented by a
polynomial Euler product
.
They showed that if these
L
-functions do not have too many zeros off the critical line and satisfy the Ramanujan Hypothesis and a version of (
1.1
), then for any fixed
\sigma\in(1/2,1)
and
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{H}\in\mathbb{R}
, and for any sufficiently large
T\gg 1
, there are
t_{1},\dots,t_{H}\in[T,2T]
such that

(1.2)

\displaystyle\min_{h\leq H}\mathrm{Re}\lparen e^{-i\phi_{h}}\log L_{h}(\sigma+it_{h})\rparen\gg\frac{(\log T)^{1-\sigma}}{\log\log T},

with
|t_{h}-t_{g}|\leq 2(\log T)^{(1+\sigma)/2}\sqrt{\log\log T}
for all
g,h\in\{1,\dots,H\}
.
The statement about the zeros is given with respect to the order of growth of the zero-counting functions in rectangles of the
L
-functions, namely that

N_{L_{h}}(\sigma,T):=\#\left\{\rho:L_{h}(\rho)=0,\,T\leq\mathrm{Im}\rho\leq 2T,\,\mathrm{Re}\rho\geq\sigma\right\},\quad h\leq H,\,\sigma>\frac{1}{2},\,T\geq 1,

grow moderately slow as
T\to+\infty
.
The above result covers the case of Dirichlet
L
-functions and
L
-functions associated with holomorphic cusp forms of
\mathrm{GL}(2)
over
\mathbb{Q}
in the whole range
(1/2,1)
.
Moreover, it covers the case of higher degree
\mathrm{GL}(m)

L
-functions in a restricted neighborhood of
1
, depending on
m
, where it is known that
N_{L}(\sigma,T)
grows sufficiently slow.
Although (
1.2
) yields joint extreme values in directions (including small and large values) for a wide class of
L
-functions, it does not provide us with a single
t
that works for all entries of
(L_{1},\dots,L_{H})
simultaneously but rather produces a collection of
t_{h}
,
h\leq H
, which are not too far from each other.
This has been resolved by Inoue and Li
[
20
, Theorem 1]
, who showed that, for Dirichlet
L
-functions, (
1.2
) holds with
t_{h}=t
for some
t\in[T,2T]
and all
h\leq H
(they actually prove more).

On the critical line, Heap and Li
[
16
, Theorem 1]
proved that if
\mathcal{S}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\text{poly}}
is the class of
L
-functions consisting of the Riemann zeta-function, the primitive Dirichlet
L
-functions and the
L
-functions associated with primitive cusp forms on
GL(2)
over
\mathbb{Q}
, then for
H=2
, any distinct
L_{h}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
,
h\leq H
, and any sufficiently large
T\gg 1
we have that

(1.3)

\displaystyle\max_{t\in[T,2T]}\min_{h\leq H}|L_{h}({\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}+it)|\geq\exp\left\lparen\sqrt{\frac{D\log T}{H\log\log T}}\right\rparen,

where
D>0
is a sufficiently small constant.
They also describe how their method can be modified to obtain the above inequality when
H=3
and all
L_{h}
are Dirichlet
L
-functions.
For
H>3
however, they have to assume the
Generalized Riemann Hypothesis
(GRH) in which case any positive
D<{1}/{2}
is admissible
[
16
, Theorem 3]
.
The first objective here is to make the latter result unconditional.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
L_{1},\dots,L_{H}
are elements of
\mathcal{S}_{0}
, then for any sufficiently small but fixed
D>0
and for any sufficiently large
T\gg 1
we have that

\max_{t\in[T,2T]}\min_{h\leq H}|L_{h}({\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}+it)|\geq\exp\left\lparen\sqrt{\frac{D\log T}{H(H-1)\log\log T}}\right\rparen.

If every
L_{h}
,
h\leq H
, is a Dirichlet
L
-function, then any

is admissible.
Otherwise, we can take

where
\theta
is an admissible bound towards the Ramanujan Hypothesis for cusp forms on
GL(2)
over
\mathbb{Q}
.

Leaving aside that the admissible range of
D
is more restricted compared to
[
16
, Theorem 1, Theorem 3]
, the lower bound of Theorem
1.1
is in addition smaller by a power of
1/\sqrt{H-1}
than the one presented in (
1.3
).
Interestingly, if we were to also assume GRH then we could remove this power.
In fact we could recover
[
16
, Theorem 3]
, which conditionally treats any number of
\mathrm{GL}(m)

L
-functions, under the same hypotheses.
To prove Theorem
1.1
we employ the resonance method of Soundararajan and a variation of this technique will also
be used to obtain results on extreme values (both large and small) off the critical line.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
L_{1},\dots,L_{H+G}
be elements of
\mathcal{S}_{\text{poly}}
satisfying the Ramanujan Hypothesis and (
1.1
).
If there are
\sigma_{0}\in(1/2,1)
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
with
\sum_{h\leq H+G}N_{L_{h}}(\sigma_{0},T)=o(T^{1-\alpha})
, then there is
D>0
such that for any
\sigma\in(\sigma_{0},1)
and any sufficiently large
T\gg 1

\max_{t\in[T,2T]}\min\left\{\min_{h\leq H}|L_{h}(\sigma+it)|,\max_{H<h\leq H+G}\left\lvert L_{h}(\sigma+it)\right\rvert^{-1}\right\}\geq\exp\left\lparen D\frac{(\log T)^{1-\sigma}}{\log\log T}\right\rparen.

For
\sigma=1
, the right-hand side of the above inequality ought to be replaced by
(\log\log T)^{D}
.

It is unfortunate that we have to impose the Ramanujan Hypothesis, for we can not cover unconditionally the case of
L
-functions associated with Maaß forms.
We can circumvent this restriction in Theorem
1.1
because the Dirichlet polynomial utilized in the resonance method on the critical line is built by many more and much larger primes compared to the one used off the critical line.
There is hence some flexibility on how to choose its Dirichlet coefficients in the former case, following an idea of Heap and Li, without waiving any of its defining properties.
Although the assumption on zero-density estimates is necessitated to be able to represent the
L
-functions by short Dirichlet polynomials, the situation off the critical line is simpler and does not require the stronger assumption of the GRH.
Moreover, such zero-density estimates are already known for
L
-functions from
\mathcal{S}_{0}
, while similar estimates for higher degree
\mathrm{GL}(m)

L
-functions close to the critical line are well beyond out of reach with the current technology.
All things considered, Theorem
1.2
recovers partially
[
20
, Theorem 1]
, covers unconditionally the case of
L
-functions associated with holomorphic cusp forms on
\mathrm{GL}(2)
over
\mathbb{Q}
and, under weaker assumptions, improves upon
[
29
]
by showing that (
1.2
) holds with
t_{h}=t
for some
t\in[T,2T]
and all
h\leq H
when

.
Incidentally, a careful review of the proof of (

) shows that if one is willing to not enforce the

, to be identical, then the

L
-functions can be arbitrarily chosen and need not be independent.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
L_{1},\dots,L_{H}\in\mathcal{S}

Assume that there are
\sigma_{0}\in(1/2,1)
and
\alpha\in(0,1)

\sum_{h\leq H}N_{L_{h}}(\sigma_{0},T)=o(T^{1-\alpha})

\displaystyle\frac{\log x}{x}\sum_{p\leq x}|a_{L_{h}}(p)|^{\mathrm{i}(h)}\mathop{\longrightarrow}^{x\to+\infty}\kappa_{h},

\min_{h\leq H}\mathrm{Re}\lparen e^{-i\phi_{h}}\log L_{h}(\sigma_{0}+it_{h})\rparen\gg\frac{(\log T)^{1-\sigma_{0}}}{\log\log T},

2.
An overview of the ideas

2.1.
On the critical line

Let
L_{h}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
,
h\leq H
, and
L=\prod_{h\leq H}L_{h}
be their product.
Heap and Li
[
16
]
observe that (
1.3
) follows if we can show that there is

such that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\lvert L\lparen{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}+it}}\rparen\rvert^{q}\geq V^{q}\sum_{h\leq H}\prod_{g\neq h}\lvert L_{g}\lparen{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}+it}}\rparen\rvert^{q},\quad V=\exp\left\lparen(D+o(1))\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{\log\log T}}\right\rparen,

where
q=2
and
D
is some suitably chosen positive constant.
A sufficient condition to establish the latter inequality would then be to show that, for every
h\leq H
,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\int_{T}^{2T}\lvert L\lparen{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}+it}}\rparen\rvert^{q}\lvert R\lparen{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}+it}}\rparen\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}t\gg{V}^{q}\int_{T}^{2T}\lvert R\lparen{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}+it}}\rparen\rvert^{2}\prod_{g\neq h}\lvert L_{g}\lparen{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}+it}}\rparen\rvert^{q}\mathrm{d}t,

with any possible discrepancies on the size of the implicit constants being absorbed by the factor

of

.
Thus, it all boils down in obtaining a sharp lower bound for the left-hand side of (

) and a sharp upper bound for its right-hand side.
The lower bound is simpler to derive because after invoking Hölder’s inequality to show that

\displaystyle\int_{T}^{2T}\lvert L\lparen{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}+it}}\rparen\rvert^{q}\lvert R\lparen{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}+it}}\rparen\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}t\geq\left\lvert\int_{T}^{2T}{L\lparen{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}+it}}\rparen}\lvert R\lparen{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}+it}}\rparen\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right\rvert^{q}\left\lparen\int_{T}^{2T}\lvert R\lparen{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}+it}}\rparen\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right\rparen^{1-q},

it remains to bound from below the first factor on the right-hand side and from above the second factor.
But the former follows from a standard procedure (see
[
2

or
[
16

The task of obtaining sharp upper bounds for the right-hand side of (

.
When

and

, these moments reduce to

\int_{T}^{2T}|L_{h}({\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}+it)|^{q}\mathrm{d}t\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{T}^{2T}|L_{h}({\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}+it)L_{g}({\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}+it)|^{q}\mathrm{d}t,\quad L_{h},L_{g}\in\mathcal{S}_{0},

respectively.
In the first case we have precise asymptotics (recall we set

) and the resonance method is applicable as has been shown in
[

, Proposition 3]
.
We already encounter difficulties when

because the only moments
for which we have sharp upper bounds are if both

and

are Dirichlet

-functions.
When

nothing is known unless we assume the GRH (all

[

]
recently provided satisfying answers by building on the work of Soundararajan
[

]
and Harper
[

]
.
In that regard, Heap and Li
[

incorporate ideas of Soundararajan and Harper to conditionally obtain sufficiently good upper bounds for the right-hand side of (

Moreover, their method is general enough to include the case of any number of

L
-functions.

We will work in the opposite direction and start by noticing that relation (

) with

2.2.
Off the critical line

The underlying reason for assuming the GRH when working on the critical line is so that we can express the
L
-functions (and their products) by short Dirichlet polynomials while controlling the influence of their zeros.
Similar operations can be performed to the right of the critical line by only assuming zero-density estimates which ensure that, as
T\to\infty
, there are horizontal strips that contain no such zeros.
In fact these strips are wide enough that will allow us to apply the resonance method.
Let
L_{h}
,
h\leq H
, be as in Theorem
1.2
and
I\subseteq[T,2T]
be an interval such that
L_{h}(\sigma+it)\neq 0
for all
\sigma\geq\sigma_{0}
and
t\in I
.
We will later see that the length of
I
can be as large as a power of
T
.
Arguing as in the previous subsection, in order to derive that the
L_{h}
,
h\leq H
, can attain simultaneously large values in the segment
[\sigma+iT,\sigma+2iT]
it suffices to prove inequality (
2.1
) with
\sigma
in place of
{1}/{2}
,
q=2
and
V=\exp((D+o(1))(\log T)^{1-\sigma}/\log\log T)
.
Dividing both sides of this inequality with
|L(\sigma+it)|^{2}
, we see that this is equivalent to showing that

1\geq V^{2}\sum_{h\leq H}\lvert L_{h}\lparen{\textstyle{\sigma+it}}\rparen\rvert^{-2},\quad\text{for some }t\in I.

A sufficient condition for the above inequality to be true can be seen to be

is a Dirichlet polynomial suitably constructed to resonate with

,

.
Thus, we only need to establish the latter relation while for including also the case of small values we just need to substitute

with

.

The proof of (

differs from those of the previous theorems.
The first step is to use a

that involves the Fejér kernel to show roughly that, for any

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{|y|\leq\tau}\mathrm{Re}\lparen e^{-i\phi_{h}}\log L_{h}(s+iy)\rparen\geq\sum_{p\asymp x}\frac{|a_{L_{h}}(p)|\cos(\arg(a_{L_{h}}(p))-t\log p)}{p^{\sigma}}+(\text{error}),\end{split}

where

ranges in an interval

and

.
The maximum in the left-hand side is the reason that makes this approach fall short in producing a single

on which every

,

The next step in the proof would be to apply the

to the frequencies

,

The immediate problem here is that the number

,

, for some

,

) of the Dirichlet coefficients, that enables us to overcome this obstacle.

are still of size

but distant enough from each other so that

This allows us to apply Kronecker’s theorem directly, rendering the independence of the

-functions superfluous.

3.
Two classes of
L
-functions

The class
\mathcal{S}
, as introduced by Selberg
[
38
]
without taking into account the Ramanujan Hypothesis, consists of ordinary Dirichlet series

\displaystyle{L}(s):=\sum\limits_{n\geq 1}{a_{L}(n)}{n^{-s}}

that are absolutely convergent for
\sigma>1
and satisfy the following conditions:

(I)

Analytic continuation.
There exists an integer
m\geq 0
such that
(s-1)^{m}{L}(s)
is an entire function of finite order.

(II)

Functional equation.

{L}(s)
satisfies a relation of the type

\displaystyle L(s)\chi(s)=\omega\overline{\gamma(1-\overline{s})L(1-\overline{s})},\quad\gamma(s):=Q^{s}\prod\limits_{k\leq K}\Gamma\left(\lambda_{k}s+\mu_{k}\right),

for some real numbers
Q,\lambda_{k}>0
, and complex numbers
\mu_{k}
,
\omega
with
|\omega|=1
.

(III)

Euler product.

{L}(s)
has an absolutely convergent product representation

\displaystyle{L}(s)=\prod_{p}\exp\left\lparen\sum_{\nu\geq 1}{b(p^{\nu})}{p^{-\nu s}}\right\rparen=:\prod_{p}L(s;p),\quad\sigma>1,

for some complex numbers
b(p^{\nu})\ll p^{\nu\theta_{L}}
with
\theta_{L}<1/2
.

The subclass
\mathcal{S}_{\text{poly}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
will consist of those elements from
\mathcal{S}
which satisfy

(IV)

Polynomial Euler product.
There is an integer
\partial_{L}\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle L(s;p)=\prod_{j\leq\partial_{L}}\left\lparen 1-{a_{L,j}(p)}{p^{-s}}\right\rparen^{-1}

for any prime
p
and complex numbers
a_{L,j}(p)\ll p^{\theta_{L}}
with
\theta_{L}<1/4
.

In view of condition
(II)
we can define the number
d_{L}:=2\sum_{k\leq K}\lambda_{k}
, which is called the
degree
of
L
and it is uniquely determined for any
{L}
-function satisfying
(I)
and
(II)
.
Indeed, if
N_{L}(T)
counts the number of zeros of
L(s)
in the rectangle
0\leq\sigma\leq 1
,
T\leq t\leq 2T
, then

N_{L}(T)=\frac{d_{L}}{2\pi}T\log{T}+O(T),

which is in analogy to the Riemann-von Mangoldt formula for
\zeta(s)

[
43
, §9.3]
.
Hence,
d_{L}
is well defined and it is conjectured
[
23
]
that
d_{L}=\partial_{L}
when
L\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{poly}}
.
Another consequence of
(II)
in combination with
(I)
and the Phragmén-Lindelöf principle is (see
[
41
, Theorem 6.8]
) that

(3.1)

\displaystyle L(\sigma+it)\ll(1+|t|)^{d_{L}(1-\sigma)/2+\epsilon},\quad 0\leq\sigma\leq 1,\,t\in\mathbb{R}.

Conditions
(III)
and
(IV)
provide information on the arithmetic character of
a_{L}(n)
and the analytic character of
L(s)
.
Firstly, they imply that
a_{L}(n)
,
n\geq 1
, is a multiplicative function with
a_{L}(p)=b_{L}(p)\ll\sqrt{p}
and, if
L\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{poly}}
, with
a_{L}(p)=\sum_{j\leq\partial_{L}}a_{L,j}(p)\ll p^{1/4}
for any prime
p
.
In the sequel however it will also be necessary to have better control on the size of these Dirichlet coefficients.
This will often, but not always, be realized by assuming the
Ramanujan Hypothesis
which asserts that
a_{L}(n)\ll n^{\epsilon}
for every integer
n\geq 1
.
If in addition
L\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{poly}}
, then
|a_{L,j}(p)|\leq 1
for all primes
p
and integers
j\leq\partial_{L}
(see
[
41
, Lemma 2.2]
) whence
|a_{L}(p)|\leq\partial_{L}
for any prime
p
.
The absolute convergence of the Euler product in the half-plane
\sigma>1
yields that
L(s)\neq 0
therein.
Hence, by a classic result due to Landau (see for example
[
7
, Theorem 1]
) the function
1/L(s)
is also represented as an ordinary Dirichlet series and as an Euler product

\frac{1}{L(s)}:=\sum_{n\geq 1}{a_{1/L}(n)}{n^{-s}}=\prod_{p}\frac{1}{L(s;p)},

which are both absolutely convergent in the half-plane
\sigma>1
.
In particular,
a_{1/L}(n)
,
n\geq 1
, is also a multiplicative function satisfying
a_{1/L}(p)=-a_{L}(p)
for any prime
p
.

By Dirichlet’s convolution formula it follows that, for any
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
, the functions

L_{1}L_{2}
and
L_{1}/L_{2}
can also be represented as ordinary and absolutely convergent Dirichlet series

\displaystyle L_{1}(s)L_{2}(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}{a_{L_{1}L_{2}}(n)}{n^{-s}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{L_{1}}{L_{2}}(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}{a_{L_{1}/L_{2}}(n)}{n^{-s}},\quad\sigma>1,

with the arithmetical functions

a_{L_{1}L_{2}}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}a_{L_{1}}(n)a_{L_{2}}\left\lparen\frac{n}{d}\right\rparen\quad\text{ and }\quad a_{L_{1}/L_{2}}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}a_{L_{1}}(n)a_{1/L_{2}}\left\lparen\frac{n}{d}\right\rparen,\quad n\geq 1,

being multiplicative and, thus, satisfying for any prime
p
that

(3.2)

\displaystyle a_{L_{1}L_{2}}(p)=a_{L_{1}}(p)+a_{L_{2}}(p)\quad\text{ and }\quad a_{L_{1}/L_{2}}(p)=a_{L_{1}}(p)-a_{L_{2}}(p).

Both
\mathcal{S}_{\text{poly}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
are multiplicitavely closed in the sense that the product of any two elements from a given class is also an element of the same class.
Moreover,
L\in\mathcal{S}
will be called
primitive
if any expression
L=L_{1}L_{2}
for some

implies that
L_{1}=1
or
L_{2}=1
.

Typical examples of primitive elements from
\mathcal{S}
, and the ones we treat in actuality here, are
L
-functions associated with irreducible cuspidal automorphic representations of
\mathrm{GL}(m)
over
\mathbb{Q}
(upon normalization) with

.
In
[
14
, Section 2]
,
[
16
, Section 2]
and
[

, Section 2]
can be found concise descriptions of their basic properties, while the interested reader is referred to

for a more detailed introduction of them.
In a nutshell, we know that

with the Riemann zeta-function, Dirichlet

-functions and

-functions associated with holomorphic cusp forms satisfying the Ramanujan Hypothesis, while for

If

Given

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\lparen 1+O\left\lparen\frac{1}{C^{2}}\right\rparen\right\rparen\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{|a_{L_{h}}(p)|^{2}}{p^{\sigma}}&=\left\lparen 1+O\left\lparen\frac{1}{C^{2}}\right\rparen\right\rparen\frac{(\kappa_{h}+O(\epsilon))(\beta\log T)^{1-\sigma}}{\log\log T}.\end{split}

Similarly, the first double sum in the right-hand side of (
6.5
) can be seen to be

(6.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{h\neq g\leq H}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{a_{L_{h}}(p)\overline{a_{L_{g}}(p)}}{p^{\sigma}}\left\lparen 1+O(|r(p)|^{2})\right\rparen&\ll\frac{\epsilon(\beta\log T)^{1-\sigma}}{\log\log T}+\frac{1}{C^{2}}\sum_{p\leq\beta\log T}\frac{1}{p^{\sigma}}\\
&\ll\left\lparen\epsilon+\frac{1}{C^{2}}\right\rparen\frac{(\beta\log T)^{1-\sigma}}{\log\log T},\end{split}

while the second double sum in the right-hand side of (
6.5
) is bounded as above for the same reason.
The first part of the lemma for
h\leq H
follows now from (
6.5
)–(
6.7
) by fixing sufficiently large
C\gg 1
.
If
H<h\leq H+G
, we obtain completely analogously the same result because the main contribution will still be coming from a sum of the shape of (
6.6
).
The case of
\sigma=1
is treated in a similar manner.
∎

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
L,\tilde{L}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{poly}}
satisfy the Ramanujan Hypothesis and
\sigma\in(\sigma_{0},1]
.
For any
T\gg 1
and in any interval
\mathcal{I}
which is
\sigma_{0}
-good for
L\tilde{L}(s)
, we have that

\displaystyle{I}:=\frac{1}{T^{\alpha}}\int_{A+T^{\alpha}}^{A+2T^{\alpha}}\left\lvert\frac{L(\sigma+it)}{\tilde{L}(\sigma+it)}R(it)\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}t\ll{\prod_{p}\left\lparen 1+|r(p)|^{2}+{2p^{-\sigma}\mathrm{Re}\lparen\overline{r(p)}a_{L/\tilde{L}}(p)\rparen}\right\rparen}.

Proof.

Define first the function

F_{\sigma}(z):=\frac{L(z)}{{\tilde{L}(z)}}R(z-\sigma)={\sum_{n\geq 1}{a_{L/{\tilde{L}}}(m)}{m^{-z}}}{\sum_{n\geq 1}{r(n)n^{\sigma}}{n^{-z}}}=:\sum_{n\geq 1}{f_{\sigma}(n)}{n^{-z}},\quad\mathrm{Re}z>1.

Then Perron’s formula
[

, Lemma 3.12]
yields that

\sum_{n\leq Y}f_{\sigma}(n){n^{-s}}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\frac{1}{2}-iY}^{\frac{1}{2}+iY}F_{\sigma}(s+w)\frac{Y^{w}}{w}\mathrm{d}w+O\left\lparen\frac{1}{\sqrt{Y}}\right\rparen,\quad t\in[A+T^{\alpha},A+2T^{\alpha}],

where
Y:=T^{\alpha}/5
is, without loss of generality, half an odd integer.
Observe that
F(s+w)
can be continued analytically as a function of

inside the rectangle
\sigma_{0}-\sigma<\mathrm{Re}w<1
,
|\mathrm{Im}w|<Y
.
Hence, by moving the path of integration to the line segment with endpoints
-\epsilon\pm iX
we pick up only a simple pole at
w=0
of residue
F_{\sigma}(s)
and we obtain that

\displaystyle F_{\sigma}(s)-\sum_{n\leq Y}f_{\sigma}(n){n^{-s}}+o(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left\{\int_{\frac{1}{2}+iY}^{-\epsilon+iY}+\int^{\frac{1}{2}-iY}_{-\epsilon-iY}+\int_{-\epsilon+iY}^{-\epsilon-iY}\right\}F_{\sigma}(s+w)\frac{Y^{w}}{w}\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle\ll\max\left\{\lvert F_{\sigma}(u+iv)\rvert:\sigma-\epsilon\leq u\leq\sigma+\frac{1}{2},\,v\in\mathcal{I}_{\mu}\right\},

where
\mathcal{I}_{\mu}
is as in Lemma
6.2
.
Since the interval
\mathcal{I}
is
\sigma_{0}
-good for
L
and
{\tilde{L}}

\max\left\{\left\lvert\frac{L(u+iv)}{{\tilde{L}(u+iv)}}\right\rvert:\sigma-\epsilon\leq u\leq\sigma+\frac{1}{2},\,v\in\mathcal{I}_{\mu}\right\}\leq T^{o(1)}.

Moreover

by the prime number theorem and, therefore, we have uniformly for

and

R(u+iv-\sigma)\ll\sum_{n\leq N}|r(n)|n^{\epsilon}\ll T^{(\beta+o(1))(1+\epsilon)}.

Hence,

\frac{L(\sigma+it)}{\tilde{L}(\sigma+it)}R(it)=F_{\sigma}(\sigma+it)=\sum_{n\leq Y}f_{\sigma}(n)n^{-\sigma-it}+O\left\lparen T^{\beta+\epsilon}\right\rparen,\quad t\in[A+T^{\alpha},A+2T^{\alpha}].

We then insert the above Dirichlet polynomial approximation in
{I}
, apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to separate the main term from the error term, apply further Lemma
5.2
to the main term and lastly appeal to the multiplicativity of

, to conclude that

(6.8)

\displaystyle{I}\ll\sum_{n\leq T^{\alpha}}\frac{|f_{\sigma}(n)|^{2}}{n^{2\sigma}}\ll\prod_{p\leq T^{\alpha}}\left\lparen 1+\frac{|f_{\sigma}(p)|^{2}}{p^{2\sigma}}+\sum_{\nu\geq 2}\frac{|f_{\sigma}(p^{\nu})|^{2}}{p^{2\nu\sigma}}\right\rparen.

Observe that for any prime
p
and integer
\nu\geq 1

f_{\sigma}(p^{\nu})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},n_{2}\geq 1\\
n_{1}n_{2}=p^{\nu}\end{subarray}}a_{L/\tilde{L}}(n_{1})r(n_{2})n_{2}^{\sigma}=a_{L/\tilde{L}}(p^{\nu})+a_{L/\tilde{L}}(p^{\nu-1})r(p)p^{\sigma}\ll p^{(\nu-1)\epsilon+\sigma}

and, in particular,

|f_{\sigma}(p)|^{2}\leq 2|a_{L}(p)|^{2}+2|a_{\tilde{L}}(p)|^{2}+|r(p)|^{2}p^{2\sigma}+2p^{\sigma}\mathrm{Re}\lparen\overline{r(p)}a_{L/\tilde{L}}(p)\rparen.

Since
W(p):=1+|r(p)|^{2}+2p^{-\sigma}\mathrm{Re}\lparen\overline{r(p)}a_{L/\tilde{L}}(p)\rparen>\frac{1}{2}
and

\sum_{p\geq 1}\frac{|a_{L}(p)|^{2}+|a_{\tilde{L}}(p)|^{2}}{p^{2\sigma}}+\sum_{p\geq 1}\sum_{\nu\geq 2}p^{2(\nu-1)(\epsilon-\sigma)}=O(1),

we may take a factor

) and derive the statement of the lemma.
∎

.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let

Lemma 7.2
.

Let

.

\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{N}
and for all positive real numbers
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N}
and

\inf_{t\in[T_{1},T_{2}]}\sum_{n\leq N}\xi_{n}\lVert\lambda_{n}t-\eta_{n}\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{n\leq N}\xi_{j}\left\lparen\sin^{2}\left\lparen\frac{\pi}{2(M+1)}\right\rparen+\frac{M^{N}}{\pi(T_{2}-T_{1})\Lambda}\right\rparen,

where

\Lambda:=\min\left\{\left\lvert\sum\nolimits_{n\leq N}u_{n}\lambda_{n}\right\rvert\neq 0:u_{n}\in\mathbb{Z},\,|u_{n}|\leq M\right\}.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

By assumption, for any
T\gg 1
there is (at least one) interval
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[T,2T]
which is
\sigma_{0}
-good for all
L_{h}(s)
,
h\leq H
.
Hence, if we set

and

,
h\leq H
, where

will be determined later on and any subsequent implicit constants are independent of

, then Lemma
7.1
yields that

(7.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{|t|\leq\tau}&\mathrm{Re}\lparen e^{-i\phi_{h}}\log L_{h}(s_{0}+it)\rparen\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\left\lvert\log\frac{p}{x_{h}}\right\rvert<1}\frac{|a_{L_{h}}(p)|\cos(\arg(a_{L_{h}}(p))-t_{0}\log p)}{p^{\sigma_{0}}}\left\lparen 1-\left\lvert\log\frac{p}{x_{h}}\right\rvert\right\rparen\\
&\quad+O\left\lparen\sum_{\ell\geq 2}\sum_{\frac{x_{h}}{e}<p^{\ell}<ex_{h}}\frac{|b_{L}(p^{\ell})|}{p^{\ell\sigma}}+\frac{(\log T)^{1-\sigma_{0}}}{M\log\log T}\right\rparen\end{split}

for any

.
Moreover, if
\theta:=\max\{\theta_{L_{h}}:h\leq H\}
, then the double sum in the error term can be seen
[
2
, Footnote 1]
to be bounded by
x_{h}^{2\theta-2\sigma+1/2}\log x=o\left\lparen(\log T)^{1-\sigma_{0}}/\log\log T\right\rparen
.

Let now

\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}\}:=\bigsqcup_{h\leq H}\left\{\frac{\log p}{2\pi}:\frac{x_{h}}{e}<p<ex_{h}\right\},

where we note that the sets on the right-hand side are disjoint from our choices of

.
This implies that the numbers
\lambda_{n}

, are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Hence, if we set
\eta_{n}=\arg(a_{L_{h}}(p))/(2\pi)

yields the existence of a

such that

\displaystyle{N}(t_{0})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{h\leq H}\sum_{\left\lvert\log\frac{p}{x_{h}}\right\rvert<1}\frac{|a_{L_{h}}(p)|}{p^{\sigma_{0}}}\left\lVert\frac{\arg(a_{L_{h}}(p)-t_{0}\log p)}{2\pi}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{h\leq H}\sum_{\left\lvert\log\frac{p}{x_{h}}\right\rvert<1}\frac{|a_{L_{h}}(p)|}{p^{\sigma_{0}}}\left\lparen\frac{1}{M^{2}}+\frac{M^{N}}{T^{\alpha}\Lambda}\right\rparen,

where

\Lambda=\min\left\{\left\lvert\sum\nolimits_{\frac{x_{1}}{e}<p<ex_{H}}u_{p}{\log p}\right\rvert\neq 0:u_{p}\in\mathbb{Z},\,|u_{p}|\leq M\right\}.

The above quantity can be seen
[

\beta
]
to satisfy

\Lambda>(ex_{H})^{-MN}
.
Taking also into account the prime number theorem and that

{N}(t_{0})\ll\sum_{h\leq H}\sum_{\left\lvert\log\frac{p}{x_{h}}\right\rvert<1}\frac{|a_{L_{h}}(p)|}{p^{\sigma_{0}}}\left\lparen\frac{1}{M^{2}}+\exp\left\lparen\frac{2ex_{H}\log M}{\log(ex_{H})}+2Mex_{H}-\alpha\log T\right\rparen\right\rparen.

Thus, if we fix

and

\mathrm{i}(h)=1

\sum_{\left\lvert\log\frac{p}{x_{h}}\right\rvert<1}\frac{|a_{L_{h}}(p)|}{p^{\sigma_{0}}}\asymp\frac{(\log T)^{1-\sigma_{0}}}{M^{1-\sigma_{0}}\log\log T}\asymp\sum_{\left\lvert\log\frac{p}{x_{h}}\right\rvert<\frac{1}{2}}\frac{|a_{L_{h}}(p)|}{p^{\sigma_{0}}}.

8.
Concluding Remarks

8.1.
On the critical line

As it was mentioned in the introduction, Theorem
1.1
is an unconditional version of (
1.3
) for
\mathrm{GL}(1)
and
\mathrm{GL}(2)

L
-functions when
H>3
with a lower bound smaller by a power of
1/\sqrt{H-1}
.
This restriction can be traced back at first in Proposition
4.3
where we require
\lambda>v
when
q=u/v
.
Since
q
can be at most
2/(H-1)
for proving the theorem,
\lambda
has to be at least
H-1
.
The source of the restriction
\lambda>v
in Proposition
4.3
can be found in Lemma
5.4
and it has to do, rather naturally, with the possible existence of nontrivial zeros of the considered
L
-functions.
Lemma
5.4
provides basically the means to control the average difference between
L_{\pi}(s)^{q}
and its short Dirichlet polynomial approximation
s_{\pi}(s,T)
.
Ideally, we would like to apply Gabriel’s convexity theorem (Lemma
5.1
) directly to the function
L_{\pi}(s)^{q}-s_{\pi}(s,T)
with
y=1
and translate the problem into the half-plane of absolute convergence.
Unconditionally, however, we do not know whether
L_{\pi}(s)^{q}
,
q\notin\mathbb{N}
, is analytic in the half-plane
\sigma>1/2
.
Heath-Brown’s idea was to instead apply Gabriel’s convexity theorem to
L_{\pi}(s)^{u}-s_{\pi}(s,T)^{v}
with
y=1/v
which suffices for the subsequent computations.
It is exactly here where
s_{\pi}(s,T)^{v}
, though still a good approximation of
L_{\pi}(s)^{u}
, does not “delete” enough terms from
L_{\pi}(s)^{u}
when subtracted.
That is, the coefficients
C_{i}(n)
,
n\geq 1
, as defined above (
5.6
) should preferably be vanishing for
n\leq X^{v}
rather than
n\leq X
.

So let us assume the GRH for all
L_{h}
,
h\leq H
, and that none of the
L
-functions has a pole at
s=1
.
In a different case we would have to work a little harder and additionally employ another convexity theorem of Gabriel
[
13
, Theorem 1]
to overcome the possible poles while moving to the half-plane of absolute convergence.
Let

\tilde{d}_{\pi}(s,T):=L_{\pi}(s)^{q}-s_{\pi}(s,T)
and

\tilde{D}_{\pi}(\sigma,T):=\frac{1}{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lvert\tilde{d}_{\pi}(\sigma+it,T)\right\rvert\left\lvert R(\sigma+it,T)\right\rvert^{2}w(t,T)\mathrm{d}t.

For the sake of the succeeding discussion, we also set the length of
s_{\pi}(\sigma,T)
to be slightly longer then the one of the resonator,
T^{\tilde{\Delta}}
say for some
\Delta<\tilde{\Delta}<1/2
.
Applying Lemma
5.1
with
y=1
to the function
f(s)=\tilde{d}_{\pi}(s,T)R(s)^{2}\exp\left\lparen(s-i\tau)^{2}\right\rparen
, which satisfies the necessary requirements in the strip
1/2\leq\sigma\leq\beta
, and arguing exactly as in Lemma
5.4
will result to

(8.1)

\displaystyle\tilde{D}_{\pi}(\sigma,T)

\displaystyle\ll\tilde{D}_{\pi}({\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}},T)^{1+\frac{1/2-\sigma}{\beta-1/2}}T^{(1/2-\sigma)\left\lparen 1-1/\beta\right\rparen\tilde{\Delta}}+e^{-\epsilon T^{2}}.

Now there is no factor
1/v
in the power to which
T
is raised in the right-hand side above.
If we were to use
\tilde{D}_{\pi}(\sigma,T)
instead of
{D}_{\pi}(\sigma,T)
in the proof of Proposition
4.3
and employ (
8.1
) instead of Lemma
5.4
, we could relax the restriction
\lambda>v
to
\lambda>1
(see in particular (
5.19
) where the factor
1/v
will be replaced by
1
).
Thus, under the GRH it is possible to increase in Theorem
1.1
the power
1/\sqrt{H-1}
to
1
.
We can pursue this direction further since the above seemingly small detail can aid us to retrieve (
1.3
) for an arbitrary number of
\mathrm{GL}(m)

L
-functions.
Based on how we argued to prove Theorem
1.1
, it suffices to prove that the inequality stated in Proposition
4.3
can be reversed.
Once more we can modify Heath-Brown’s method on upper bounds of fractional moments of
\zeta(s)
to conditionally obtain the desired inequality whenever
\Delta<2\tilde{\Delta}
which would result to the more restricted range
D<1/4
compared to
D<1/2
of (
1.3
) (
[
16
, Theorem 3]
).

8.2.
Off the critical line

There is a rich literature on zero-density estimates since they are the closest we have unconditionally in place of the GRH and often yield results of similar strength.
Their quality can differ depending on which
L
-function we consider and how far we work from the critical line.
Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
require the bare minimum from these estimates, namely to grow essentially by
o(T)
.
At the same time, their speed of growth determines the width of the horizontal strips where we are certain that there are no zeros and in consequence they also determine the size of
D
in the respective theorems.
Generally, it is known that any
\mathrm{GL}(1)
and
\mathrm{GL}(2)

L
-function satisfies

N_{L}(\sigma,T)\ll T^{1-\alpha(\sigma-1/2)}\log T,\quad\sigma\geq\frac{1}{2},\,T\geq 1

for some
\alpha>0
.
It is unknown whether higher degree primitive
L
-functions satisfy such zero-density estimates or any other that is
o(T)
close to the critical line.
Kaczorowski and Perelli
[
22
]
showed that for if
L\in\mathcal{S}
satisfies the Ramanujan Hypothesis, then

N_{L}(\sigma,T)\ll T^{(4d_{L}+12)(1-\sigma)+\epsilon},\quad\sigma\geq\frac{1}{2},\,T\geq 1.

Therefore, Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
