A study on the role of palliative care in the physical and psychological well-being of people with life-limiting illnesses in Kashmir, India
Authors: Shaqul Qamar Wani, Muskan Matia, Akshithanand Kuzhikkat Jayaprakasan, Mushtaq Ahmed Sofi, Talib Khan, Mahrukh Parvaiz, Maryam Ayub, Uroosa Farooq, Tabinda Wani, Sajad Ahmed Najar, Smriti Rana, Parth Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiving with life-limiting illnesses (LLIs) is psychologically distressing, even more so in areas with a complicated terrain and which are conflict-prone, where insecurity, displacement, and limited healthcare access adds to the burden. In this study, we assessed the impact of palliative care on the psychological well-being of people with life-limiting illnesses in Jammu & Kashmir, a conflict-prone region in India.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed the records of patients with LLIs visiting a pain and palliative care clinic in Srinagar, Jammu & Kashmir, from November 2023 to March 2025. The impact of palliative care on their well-being was assessed twice using the Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale (ESAS), one week apart, the socioeconomic and disease-related factors associated with a change in scores were also identified. Data were analyzed using Rv4.4.2.ResultsOf the 881 patients included in the analysis, the mean age was 57.6 ± 15.2 years, with almost an equal number above and below 60 years. Males constituted 59.6% of the population. A majority (73.4%) were from Below Poverty Line (BPL) households. Sons were the primary caregivers in 52.5% of the cases, and most (73.2%) of the caregivers were males. The primary diagnosis in almost all (97.6%) was cancer. Statistically significant differences were noted: median pain scores reduced from 7 to 2 (p < 0.001) and total scores reduced from 29 to 20 (p < 0.001) between initial assessment and at follow-up after registration at the palliative care unit. There was no significant difference in median score across age groups, gender and BPL category.ConclusionOur study population primarily consisted of patients with malignancies. There was a significant difference in pain score, well-being score, and total ESAS score on follow-up assessment after 1 week of receiving palliative care. No significant difference was seen based on age, gender, socioeconomic status, or diagnosis, highlighting the universal impact of palliative care in people with LLIs.Keywords: Palliative care, Life-limiting illness, Psychological support, Quality of life, Access to healthcare, Conflict area

Background

Life-limiting illnesses (LLIs) are active, progressive, or advanced diseases that have little or no prospect of cure, and where death is likely to occur as a direct result of the illness [1]. The term “person with life-limiting illness” emphasizes that those affected are not just dying, but actively living with the illness, often for long periods of time [1]. They commonly experience a range of physical symptoms, including but not limited to pain, breathlessness, nausea and vomiting, constipation, anorexia, and weight loss [2]. Out of these, pain and breathlessness are the most common symptoms experienced by patients with life-threatening illnesses [3]. The overall burden of illness, however, cannot be understood solely based on physical symptoms. Persistence of symptoms, uncertainty about prognosis, and gradual loss of independence cause suffering that extends well beyond the physical dimension of health. It also encompasses psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions, collectively referred to as ‘total pain’ [4].

The multifaceted nature of suffering in LLIs necessitates comprehensive care in all domains. Palliative care, as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), aims to improve quality of life through early identification, assessment, and treatment of pain and other physical, psychological, and spiritual problems [5]. By focusing on symptom management and psychological well-being, palliative care acts as a means to prevent and relieve the sufferings of those with life-limiting illnesses [6]. In line with this, the concept of serious-health-related suffering (SHS) has been introduced to describe suffering that is associated with illness or injury of such severity that it compromises physical, social, and emotional well-being, and typically requires palliative care [7].

In India, palliative care is slowly becoming an established area of clinical practice. However, its access is still limited due to the presence of many barriers like the unavailability of trained healthcare professionals, lack of awareness regarding palliative care services, and poor implementation of palliative care programs [8]. Geographical accessibility is also a well-documented barrier to palliative care delivery, as most palliative care centers are concentrated in urban areas. This leaves most of the rural population without adequate access to palliative care services, as they have to travel longer due to poor road networks and long distances to the nearest palliative care centers [9]. These challenges are further compounded in areas affected by conflict, where healthcare systems are fragile and access to the few existing services is restricted due to frequent disruptions in transportation modes, due to road blockage [10]. In such settings, the diversion of resources away from chronic care makes it challenging to practice and sustain special services like palliative care. Moreover, the chronic stress due to insecurity, displacement, and violence adds to the psychological burden of illness in conflict-affected populations. Nearly everyone in such populations experiences psychological distress in one form or another [11]. Thus, the combination of the two factors, geographical inaccessibility and conflict, creates a setting where the need for palliative care is often pressing but rarely prioritized.

Numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of palliative care in improving the quality of life of people with LLIs. It has been consistently shown that palliative care interventions can significantly improve patient well-being, reduce symptom burden, and improve patient outcomes [12–14]. However, most of this evidence is drawn from high-resource settings. There exists an “access abyss” wherein palliative care services and research are most concentrated in high-income countries, while the need is greatest in low- and middle-income countries [3, 7]. Furthermore, findings from high-resource, stable environments cannot be directly generalized to conflict-affected regions where civil unrest, curfews, and other restrictions severely restrict healthcare delivery. This creates an even greater void of evidence for the impact of palliative care in conflict settings [15].

This study aimed to describe the model of palliative care service delivery in a conflict-prone region and to evaluate its impact on the physical and psychological well-being of patients with life-limiting illnesses. We report the distribution of patients by underlying health conditions causing serious health-related suffering (SHS), assess the effect of palliative care interventions on symptom burden as measured by the Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale (ESAS), and identify socioeconomic and disease-related factors associated with change in total ESAS scores.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

In this observational study, a retrospective analysis was conducted using data from individuals referred to the Pain and Palliative Care Unit at the Sher-i-Kashmir Institute of Medical Sciences (SKIMS), Srinagar, Jammu and Kashmir. SKIMS is a tertiary-level teaching hospital that provides specialized care to populations across Jammu and Kashmir. The region’s prolonged instability, challenging terrain, poor public health infrastructure, and recurrent episodes of violence have collectively contributed to insecurity, disruption of daily life, and limited access to essential healthcare services [16].

Study population and data collection

All the patients referred to and registered at the Pain and Palliative Care Unit at SKIMS between November 2023 and March 2025 were included in this study. Patients with missing data for ESAS assessment scores at follow-up were excluded from the study. Totally 1154 patients were registered during the study period. After excluding patients with incomplete ESAS assessments, 881 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Patient flow chart

Data for this study were obtained from the records maintained by the Pain and Palliative Care Unit team. The analysis included data collected between November 2023 and March 2025. Information extracted from the records comprised patients’ diagnoses, sociodemographic variables such as age, sex, socioeconomic status, and the caregiver’s relationship to the patient and gender. The psychological and physical well-being of patients was assessed using the Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale (ESAS), details of which are described in the operational definitions. ESAS assessments were routinely conducted at two time points, one week apart, to evaluate changes in symptom severity. The study adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cross-sectional studies in reporting its findings.

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee at SKIMS, Srinagar. (EC/NEW/INST/2024/JK/0542).

Model of palliative care service provision

The Pain and Palliative Care Unit at SKIMS was set up in 2013. The care team running the clinic consists of a project head, three doctors, three nurses, a social worker, and a data manager. There are different pathways through which patients access the services. Patients can access the clinic through intra-hospital referrals (from medical and radiation oncology and other departments like neurology, trauma, nephrology, gastroenterology, urology, plastic surgery, emergency medicine, hematology etc.), referrals from external hospitals, community awareness programs, or peer recommendations.

Patient care

During their first visit, each patient undergoes a comprehensive medical evaluation along with an in-depth psychosocial assessment for both the patient and their caregiver. The individual accompanying the patient, who provides consent, signs formal documents, and participates in major care-related decisions, is identified as the primary caregiver. This visit marks the first point of engagement with the patient and family, during which symptom management is initiated as appropriate. Patients presenting with severe pain or distress are admitted to the day-care ward for further management.

The day-care ward provides a range of palliative interventions, including analgesia, ascitic tapping, nasogastric tube insertion, urinary catheterization, wound management, and administration of colloids, albumin, and total parenteral nutrition. Although there is no dedicated inpatient palliative care ward, patients requiring acute care are temporarily admitted to the radiation oncology ward. Before discharge, patients are assessed on their ability to attend the out-patient department (OPD) for follow-up. If patients are physically unable to attend the OPD, or if they cannot afford the travel to the hospital, then they are registered for home-care and the team visits these patients at their residence. The remaining patients are scheduled for outpatient follow-up within a week, or earlier if necessary. Outpatient services are made accessible through a walk-in system that does not require prior appointments.

Home visits services are provided by the palliative care team five days a week. The home visits are led by a physician on three days, and by a nurse and social worker on the other two days. The frequency of home visits is determined by the patient’s clinical needs and ESAS (Edmonton Symptom Assessment System) scores recorded during assessments. Home-based care continues as long as the patient requires support or remains unable to visit the hospital.

Patient care at the center prioritizes holistic well-being, addressing both physical and psychosocial aspects for patients and their families. Families facing financial hardship receive support in the form of free medications, food rations, and travel assistance, funded through local Non-governmental organisations (NGOs) and an internal emergency fund maintained by the care team. The level of vulnerability is assessed based on socioeconomic information provided by the family and verified using ration card classifications - APL (Above Poverty Line), BPL (Below Poverty Line), and AAY (Antyodaya Anna Yojana).

Following a patient’s death, bereavement home visits are conducted by a nurse and social worker to provide emotional and psychosocial support, assess coping mechanisms, and identify signs of complicated grief. These visits typically begin within two to three weeks after death and continue for two to three months, with a frequency of once every two to three weeks. Families experiencing greater distress or socioeconomic vulnerability receive more frequent visits.

Families who lose their sole breadwinner and belong to the AAY category are provided monthly rations for three to five months, including rice, pulses, cooking oil, and other essentials sufficient for a family’s needs. In cases of persistent financial distress, families are linked to local NGOs and community-based organizations. Although the hospital does not provide direct financial aid, volunteers and well-wishers occasionally contribute monetary support or essential supplies. Within the department, economically disadvantaged families are assisted through free medications, accommodation, food, and travel support, ensuring that no patient or family is left without basic care during or after treatment.

Community engagement

The team actively conducts awareness programs within the hospitals and community settings, particularly in marginalized populations, to disseminate information about pain and palliative care (PPC) services.

Training and capacity building

Training sessions are held for community health workers, i.e., Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs), to enable them to disseminate accurate information within the community about the services available for patients with cancer and other life-threatening illnesses. They are trained for early identification and referral of patients in need of PPC services, such that they can act as liaisons between the clinic and community. Additionally, sessions are also organized in hospitals, universities, and other institutions to raise awareness among medical and paramedical students as well as healthcare staff. This is especially crucial, as palliative care awareness remains limited in the state, and these initiatives help in building capacity and spreading the concept of compassionate care.

Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale (ESAS) score: The Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale (ESAS) records data on nine commonly experienced symptoms: pain, fatigue, drowsiness, nausea, loss of appetite, loss of sleep, depression, anxiety, and loss of well-being. Each symptom is rated on a numerical scale from 0 to 10, resulting in a total possible score ranging from 0 to 90. ESAS assessments were conducted at two time points: during the initial visit and at a follow-up one week later. The difference between the two scores provided an objective measure of change in symptom severity. An absolute change of one point or more in the score was considered the threshold for clinically meaningful improvement or deterioration in symptom burden. Since lower ESAS scores reflect lesser symptom severity, a negative change in value indicated that the patient’s symptom burden had improved, and a positive value indicated that it had worsened [17].

Statistical analysis

Data were compiled in Microsoft Excel and analyzed using R software (version 4.4.2) [18]. The data for weekly visits were organized in separate sheets and subsequently merged using unique patient identifiers. Qualitative variables were summarized as frequencies and proportions, while quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For hypothesis testing, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test were applied as appropriate, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

The study population consisted of 881 patients, with a mean age of 57.6 ± 15.2 years (range: 4–100 years), with a majority (50.5%) above 60 years of age. The majority of participants were males (59.6%). In terms of socioeconomic status, 73.4% of patients belonged to the Below Poverty Line (BPL) category. Most primary caregivers were male (73.2%), with sons (52.5%) and daughters (10.8%) representing the most common caregiver relationships (Fig. 2). The gender of 98 (11.1%) caregivers was not documented.

Distribution of primary caregivers of the participants

A majority of the patients receiving palliative care had cancer (97.6%). Cancer (excluding leukemia) was the primary diagnosis in 97.1% of the patients. Other conditions included musculoskeletal disorders (1.1%), leukemia (0.6%), endocrine, metabolic, blood, and immune disorders (0.5%), and others (0.7%).

Change in ESAS score

In interpreting the ESAS scores, lower ESAS scores reflect lower symptom severity. Therefore, a negative change in score indicates clinical improvement, while a positive change indicates deterioration. The Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale (ESAS) scores between first and second visits revealed statistically significant differences across the pain score, well-being score, and total ESAS score, with 68.7%, 29.3%, and 2.0% showing improvement, deterioration, and no change in total score, respectively. Pain scores showed an improvement with reduction in median scores from 7 (IQR: 6 to 8) to 2 (IQR: 0 to 6), and the total ESAS score also showed a significant decrease from 29 (IQR: 23 to 35) at initial assessment to 20 (IQR: 14 to 31) at follow-up. Notably, 27 patients expired during the follow-up period (Table 1).

Discussion

This retrospective study assessed the impact of palliative care on the physical and psychological well-being of people with life-limiting illnesses receiving palliative care services at the pain and palliative care unit at SKIMS, Srinagar, a hilly and conflict-ridden area in North India. The majority of the participants in our research were males (59.6%), with an overall mean age of 57.6 years, and belonging to a lower socioeconomic status (73.4%). It was identified that most of the people receiving care at the institute had cancer (97.6%). Improvement in the symptoms and psychological distress was found to be associated with the initiation of palliative care. The observed improvement was noted across different subgroups, but none of the demographic or socioeconomic factors demonstrated a statistically significant association with changes in total ESAS scores.

The sociodemographic profile of the study population largely reflected the local context, with some notable differences relevant to palliative care. Males accounted for more than half of the patients (59.6%), aligning with the general male predominance observed in the population of Jammu and Kashmir [19]. A hospital-based study by Mufti et al. analyzing the insurance claims under Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (AB-PMJAY) had a similar population distribution, with the majority of the beneficiaries being males. This could point to the possibility of gender bias in care seeking and not just a higher need for palliative care in the male population [20]. The mean age of the participants is consistent with other settings in India, highlighting the high need for palliative care in the elderly population [3, 16, 21]. Socioeconomically, around three-fourths of the patients belonged to Below Poverty Line (BPL) status. This number sharply contrasts with the poverty rate of Jammu and Kashmir, which stood at 4.80% in 2019-21 [22].

Our analysis also reveals that the primary caregivers of 73.2% of the patients were males, specifically sons (52.5%). This finding deviates from the widely reported global and Indian female-dominated pattern of providing palliative care at home [23, 24]. This could reflect the local socio-cultural practices where male family members take up more palliative caregiving roles, as also found in a study conducted in Jammu [25]. However, in many cases, there is a difference between the actual care provider at home and the person handling hospital formalities who is designated as the primary caregiver. This could be an artifact of the undue focus on formalities in the operational definition of “primary caregiver” instead of the day-to-day care at home.

According to the World Health Organization, cardiovascular diseases (38.5%), followed by cancer (34%), are the leading conditions globally that warrant palliative care [3]. In the Indian context, multiple studies report that stroke with a focal neurological deficit (also a cardiovascular disease) is the leading driver of palliative care need [16, 26, 27]. However, contrastingly, our data revealed that the key primary diagnosis of patients receiving palliative care at the clinic was cancer (97.6%), while only 1 patient had a primary diagnosis of cardiovascular disease. This disproportionate representation of cancer in the study population could be primarily attributed to the referral patterns, and service utilisation that may have played a role in a higher proportion of patients with malignancies being registered at the palliative care unit. Additionally, the epidemiological profile of the Kashmir valley, which has been deemed an endemic cancer zone, could also have played a role [28, 29].

Analysis of the change in scores between the first and second visits showed improvement in pain score, well-being score, and total ESAS score. The reduction in pain scores is in alignment with the palliative care goal of alleviation of pain by its early assessment and prompt treatment. The clinic’s focus on accessibility via home visits, walk-ins, and travel support also likely contributed to adherence and timely interventions, which could be the reason for better pain control. This is important as pain is one of the most common and distressing symptoms in patients with life-limiting illnesses [3]. Beyond physical symptoms, significant association was also noted in the psychological well-being of the patients. The reduction in total ESAS scores is consistent with the successful implementation of the comprehensive care pathway at the clinic. Led by a multidisciplinary team, the care pathway was designed to address the “Total Pain” model, addressing suffering in all dimensions, not just physical. This was achieved through a range of interventions providing direct support for non-physical needs: financial aid, nutritional support, and emotional counseling. The findings of this study are consistent with previous research that highlights the positive impact of palliative care on the physical and psychological well-being of people with life-limiting illnesses [30].

The changes in total ESAS scores were notably uniform across all demographic variables and clinical diagnoses. Specifically, the absence of a large age-related difference deviates from existing evidence, which states that older people receive palliative care of lesser intensity or quality [31]. Though the change in the total ESAS scores did not show any significant association with socioeconomic status in the current study, lower socioeconomic status is associated with lesser effectiveness and utilization of palliative care services, especially in Low- or Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) [7].

Strengths and limitations

Despite the promising findings, our study has certain limitations. First, the primary diagnosis of almost all of the study population was cancer, as a result of the referral patterns and service utilisation, in addition to Kashmir being an endemic cancer zone. This may not be true of other settings, thus limiting generalisability. Second, there exists a selection bias as the study includes only the patients who were able to access the clinic first, excluding other people with life-limiting illnesses who faced insurmountable barriers to access. Additionally, referral patterns could also have been an important contributing factor to the selection bias. A community-based study would address this limitation; however, due to limited resources, conducting such a study was not feasible. Third, as our study was a retrospective observational study, missing data posed a concern. For example, we could not analyze the association of frequency of visits with the change in ESAS scores. Lastly, while the care at the clinic is long-term, the data were restricted to a one-week follow-up, thereby preventing an assessment of the long-term effects of palliative care in this specific setting. Nevertheless, the study has several notable strengths. It makes a vital contribution to palliative care research by providing evidence from a conflict-prone region in an LMIC, thereby addressing a critical gap where evidence is largely drawn from high-resource, stable settings. The large sample size increases the reliability of these reported outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that a structured, multidisciplinary palliative care model can substantially improve the physical and psychological well-being of patients with life-limiting illnesses, even in conflict-prone and resource-limited settings. The uniform improvement across sociodemographic groups highlights the potential of context-adapted palliative care services to reduce inequities in access and outcomes. However, the findings are limited by the retrospective design, single-center setting, and short follow-up duration. Future research should adopt prospective, longitudinal, and community-based approaches to assess the long-term impact and sustainability of palliative care in such regions.
