 Abstract
 Tensor contractions have long been used to construct moment invariants for pattern
 recognition. Their completeness was stated as a conjecture. We resolve this conjecture for
 three-dimensional scalar moments through an explicit proof using classical invariant theory.
 Every rotation- and reflection-invariant polynomial is a finite linear combination of complete
 contractions. This establishes algebra generation and algebraic completeness. An orbit-
 separation theorem then gives discriminability: equal contraction values determine the retained
 moment tuple up to one common rotation or reflection, even when moments vanish or have
 additional symmetries.

1 Introduction
Moment invariants describe a pattern using functions that do not change when an input pattern is
rotated. Since Hu’s work on planar images [8] and Lo and Don’s three-dimensional construction [11],
they have become an established tool for pattern recognition. The tensor method provides a
systematic way to construct them: form tensor products of moments and contract all indices.
This approach has been used for scalar, vector, and tensor fields [10, 14, 3] and continues to
be developed for invariant generation and descriptor selection [13, 1]. Typically, a set of tensor
invariants is constructed for characterizing patterns of interest.
 Useful pattern descriptions sets need more qualities than invariance. Wood [15] discussed
discriminability and minimality for a given set: different patterns should remain distinguishable
unless they are related by an allowed transformation, and the descriptor should contain no
unnecessary entries. Going further, Flusser [6] makes independence and completeness central to
the construction of moment-invariant bases. These requirements raise two questions: does the
construction retain all invariant information, and which of its candidates are needed to retain it?
 Practically speaking, the tensor approach has been effective even though these guarantees
are often not made explicit; instead, more tractable constructions have been explored. Suk and
Flusser [14, Section 2.3] construct large sets of invariants and remove zeros, duplicates, products,
and linear combinations. They call the remaining invariants irreducible, but do not formulate
or prove completeness of the full contraction family. They also distinguish irreducibility from
independence and warn that their finite search may miss further irreducible invariants. Langbein
and Hagen [10, Section 2.3] use Jacobian Rank to formulate a search process through the space of
invariants applied random patterns, which is presented as producing a complete basis, although it is
more accurately characterized as an independent set. Their derivative test addresses independence,
but they do not prove that the contraction family captures every invariant polynomial. The
completeness of the underlying construction is thus a separate question from the selection of
independent entries.

 1

 Bujack et al. [5, Conjecture 1] make this question explicit: do all rank-zero contractions of
moment-tensor products form a complete set of rotation and reflection invariants? They state
it as a conjecture and make their completeness claims conditional on it. The same conjecture
appears in the later work on flexible bases from irreducible tensors [4, Section 3.4], and so an
explicit mathematical proof of the conjecture would contribute to the solidity of this line of work.
 In this work, we prove this completeness conjecture for three-dimensional scalar moment tuples.
We also connect completeness to algebra generation and discriminability. Since completeness
shows that every invariant polynomial is a finite linear combination of complete contractions,
the full tensor moment invariant family generates the invariant polynomial algebra. This implies
algebraic completeness: every invariant polynomial is algebraically dependent on the contraction
family. We further prove that equal values of all contractions imply that one common rotation
or reflection relates the entire underlying moment tuples. This discriminability statement holds
also when moments may vanish or have additional symmetries; Bujack et al.[5] demonstrated that
these are is a crucial cases for analyzing minimality.
 The result establishes the three properties for the full contraction family, giving a rigorous
foundation for using the tensor method to generate moment invariants. In this work, we do
not address independence and minimality of a set of invariants, nor the topic of how one might
construct such a set.

2.3 Generation, algebraic completeness, and discriminability
The word “complete” has been used for different properties in the literature. We therefore specify
the meaning of our terms precisely. Write the set of invariant polynomials as Aℓ = R[Wℓ ]O(3) for
all real polynomial functions of the moment entries that satisfy F (Q · M ) = F (M ). This is an
algebra: sums, products, and real scalar multiples of its members are again members. Let B be
a family in Aℓ . The family may be infinite, but each expression below uses only finitely many
members.

Polynomial algebra generation. The family generates Aℓ if every F ∈ Aℓ has an expression
                                   F = P (B1 , . . . , Bm ),     Bj ∈ B,                                (9)
where P is a polynomial with real coefficients. Only addition, multiplication, and real constants
are required. We write this as Aℓ = R[B].

Algebraic dependence on a family. An invariant polynomial F ∈ Aℓ is algebraically dependent
on B if it satisfies a polynomial equation
                                               P (F, B) = 0.                                           (10)
Here P is a polynomial with real coefficients, and P (F, B) abbreviates P (F, B1 , . . . , Bm ) for finitely
many Bj ∈ B. When P (t, B1 , . . . , Bm ) is viewed as a polynomial in t, at least one coefficient of a
positive power of t must be a nonzero polynomial function on Wℓ . This excludes relations among
the selected invariants alone that say nothing about F . Dependence does not require an explicit
polynomial formula for F .

Algebraic independence. An invariant polynomial F is algebraically independent of B if it is
not algebraically dependent on B. A family B is algebraically independent if each member B ∈ B
is algebraically independent of the remaining family B \ {B}.

Algebraic completeness. We call B algebraically complete if every invariant polynomial F ∈ Aℓ
is algebraically dependent on B.

Discriminability. The family is discriminative if its values distinguish different orthogonal
orbits:

2.4 The First Fundamental Theorem
The classical result needed in the proof describes tensors that themselves remain unchanged
under every orthogonal transformation. Goodman and Wallach state it as Theorem 5.3.3 on
printed page 249 of [7]. We reproduce its orthogonal case, omitting only the alternative “or
Sp(V )” from the first sentence. Their V is a finite-dimensional complex vector space with a
nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form ω. A bilinear form is linear in each argument; symmetric
means ω(u, v) = ω(v, u); nondegenerate means that ω(u, v) = 0 for all v forces u = 0. In
 P
orthonormal coordinates it is ω(u, v) = i ui vi , without complex conjugation. The group O(V )
consists of invertible linear maps preserving this form. The notation in the displayed statement is
explained immediately afterward.

4 From generation to completeness and discriminability
The preceding theorem expresses every invariant polynomial explicitly through contractions. Two
consequences follow: algebraic completeness, and discriminability. We treat them separately.

4.1 Generation implies algebraic completeness
Corollary 3. Every family generating Aℓ is algebraically complete. In particular, Cℓ is algebraically
complete.

Proof. For each invariant F , generation gives F = P (B1 , . . . , Bm ). Therefore

                                                F − P (B1 , . . . , Bm ) = 0

is a polynomial equation for F whose leading coefficient is 1. Thus F is algebraically dependent on
the generating family. Since this holds for every invariant polynomial F , that family is algebraically
complete. The preceding theorem supplies such an expression for every F using contractions.

 The converse fails. Already for a scalar x = M (0) , the descriptor I = x2 is algebraically
complete for R[x]: any polynomial can be written F (x) = a(I) + xb(I) and hence satisfies
(F − a(I))2 − Ib(I)2 = 0. But x is not a polynomial in I, and the values x and −x cannot be

 10

distinguished. An implicit equation can leave several possible values for an omitted invariant. At
exceptional inputs, its coefficients may even vanish simultaneously. Algebraic completeness alone
therefore does not establish discriminability.
 Conversely, a discriminative polynomial descriptor need not generate the algebra. For the same
scalar input, x3 distinguishes all real values, but recovering the invariant polynomial x requires a
cube root. Every polynomial in x3 has only powers divisible by three, so it cannot equal x. These
elementary examples explain why the three properties must be stated separately.

4.2 The orbit-separation theorem
Equality of all complete contractions forces equality of every polynomial invariant. To pass from
this algebraic statement to equality up to a rotation or reflection, we use a theorem stated explicitly
by Bandeira et al. [2, Theorem A.32]. The statement and its proof are openly available on page 73
of arXiv:1712.10163v4. We first give the source’s assumptions and terminology, then its exact
one-sentence statement, and finally its meaning for moment tensors.
 In the source, G is a compact Lie group acting continuously by orthogonal transformations
on a finite-dimensional real inner-product space V = Rp (Section 1, p. 2). An action assigns a
linear transformation to each g ∈ G, consistently with composition and the identity. Continuity
means that the transformed vector varies continuously with g. Orthogonality means that the
transformation preserves the inner product. A Lie group is a group with smooth local coordinates
in which multiplication and inversion are smooth. No differentiation on the group is needed in
our application. For a matrix group, compactness means that every sequence of its matrices has
a subsequence converging to a matrix in the group; in a finite-dimensional matrix space this is
equivalent to being closed and bounded.
 The notation R[x]G denotes all real polynomials in the coordinates x = (x1 , . . . , xp ) that are
unchanged by this action. The word ring refers to their closure under addition, subtraction, and
multiplication. The source says that these polynomials resolve θ when their values determine θ up
to the action of G.

Theorem 4 (Bandeira et al., Theorem A.32). The full invariant ring R[x]G resolves every θ ∈ V .

 The theorem’s wording and notation above are unchanged. In expanded notation, its assertion
is

5 Conclusion
The tensor method provides a systematic way to construct moment invariants, but previously, its
completeness remained a conjecture. We have now proved it for three-dimensional scalar moment
tuples. Every invariant polynomial is a finite linear combination of rank-zero contractions of
moment-tensor products, establishing algebra generation and algebraic completeness.
 This also answers what information the full contraction family retains. Equal contraction
values mean that one common rotation or reflection relates the retained moment tuples, even
when moments vanish or have additional symmetries. Thus the construction is also discriminative.
With this foundation established, selecting an independent or minimal list remains a separate
task.
