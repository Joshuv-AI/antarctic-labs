Association between Clostridium septicum and Streptococcus
gallolyticus bacteremia and colorectal neoplasms

Abstract
Purpose Colorectal cancer (CRC) is associated with Clostridium septicum (CS), Streptococcus gallolyticus (SG), and Fusobacterium nucleatum (FN) bacteremia. Further investigation of the mechanism of translocation and the time interval between
bacteremia and a CRC diagnosis is required.
Methods Patients with bacteremia caused by the three organisms were studied. The organisms were identified using MALDITOF mass spectrometry.
Results Fourteen patients (CS [n = 1], SG [n = 11], and FN [n = 2]) were included among 2866 patients with bacteremia.
Colorectal neoplasms were detected in two patients at the onset of bacteremia (advanced CRC in patient CS and carcinoma
in adenoma in patient SG). Rectal cancer developed 31 months after incidental bacteremia in another patient with SG. Colonoscopy was not performed in 10 of 14 patients, including two patients with FN at the onset of bacteremia and during the
follow-up period.
Conclusion Consistent with previous reports, we found three typical patterns associated with CRC in patients with CS and
SG bacteremia. Because routine colonoscopy was not performed, the true association with CRC could not be determined for
these organisms.
Keywords Clostridium septicum · Streptococcus gallolyticus · Bacteremia · Carcinogenesis · Colorectal cancer

Introduction
Several environmental factors, including chemical carcinogens, dietary contaminants, and physical carcinogens, affect
cancer development [1]. Microorganisms such as Helicobacter pylori, human papillomavirus, and human hepatitis B
and C viruses are also associated with particular malignancies [1]. Gut microbiota dysbiosis may cause colorectal cancer (CRC) [2]. In addition to polyketide synthase-positive
Escherichia coli and enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis,
Streptococcus gallolyticus, classified as Streptococcus bovis
biotype I, and Fusobacterium nucleatum, are well-known
major CRC-promoting bacterial species [2, 3].

S. gallolyticus is part of the normal intestinal flora and
can cause bacteremia and infective endocarditis (IE) [4]. S.
gallolyticus preferentially colonizes colorectal lesions [5,
6], whereas other S. bovis biotypes are not associated with
these lesions. S. infantarius (biotype II/1) and S. gallolyticus
subsp. pasteurianus (biotype II/2) generally cause biliary
tract infections [7, 8]. Although F. nucleatum is a common
oral bacterium, oral F. nucleatum may reach colorectal
neoplasms using the hematogenous route during transient
asymptomatic bacteremia caused by chewing, daily hygiene
activities, or dental procedures and reside in these tissues
[9–11]. Thus, S. gallolyticus and F. nucleatum are overexpressed in colorectal neoplasia. If certain conditions occur,

increased permeability in the inflamed microenvironment
induced by these organisms can facilitate translocation to
the bloodstream [10, 12].
The mechanism of bacterial translocation, pathophysiological responses, and CRC association pattern are different for C. septicum than for the above-mentioned two
organisms [13–16]. The prevalence of fecal carriage of C.
septicum is relatively rare, and C. septicum is unlikely to
be a normal commensal in humans [17]. C. septicum causes
fulminant sepsis with the production of alpha toxin once
infections such as atraumatic gas gangrene [18, 19], abdominal infections, and spontaneous bacteremia occur [14]. Evidence has accumulated regarding the relationship between
C. septicum infection and malignancy, particularly intestinal
or hematological malignancy [20].
An increased hazard risk (HR) of a CRC diagnosis in
patients with bacteremia has been shown for C. septicum
(HR 23.2), S. gallolyticus (HR 5.38), and F. necrophorum
(HR 6.91) compared to patients with negative blood cultures [21]. C. septicum has a sub-hazard ratio of 50.0 for
colorectal cancer compared with E. coli as a reference [16].
A strong association between bacteremia and CRC has been
reported for these organisms [13–21]. However, further
investigation is warranted to determine the mechanism of
bacterial translocation and the time interval between the
occurrence of bacteremia and a diagnosis of CRC.

Methods
The study was performed in accordance with the ethical
standards laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki
and its later amendments and was approved by the Ethics
Committee of Hyogo Medical University Hospital (202512088). The board waived the requirement for informed consent from the patients included in this study, and an opt-out
approach was used to obtain consent. Patients admitted to
Hyogo Medical University Hospital and Chita Peninsula
General Medical Organization Rinku Hospital between January 2021 and September 2025 were retrospectively investigated. Patients in whom C. septicum, S. gallolyticus, or
F. nucleatum was isolated from blood and those who were
followed up for at least 5 days or until death were included
in the study. Patients were excluded if the organisms were
considered contaminants. These organisms were considered
contaminants for the following reasons: blood was collected through an intravascular catheter or from the femoral
site, and resolution of the symptom or laboratory data with
inadequate antibiotic use in patients in whom the organisms
were recovered only from a single blood culture set among
multiple sets collected on the same day. The final decision
on blood culture contamination was made by a physician

13

from the Infectious Disease Department based on the clinical course.
The BD BACTEC® FX system (Nippon Becton, Dickinson Company, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was used for automatic
blood culture analyses. Blood samples were inoculated into
one aerobic and one anaerobic bottle using BD BACTEC
Plus aerobic and Lytic anaerobic media, respectively. AccuRate® Separated Sheep Blood Agar/BTB Lactose Agar (Shimadzu Diagnostics Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) or TSAII
5% sheep blood agar/chocolate II agar 100 sheets (Nippon
Becton, Dickinson Company, Ltd.) were used as the culture media. BD Phoenix™ M50 (Nippon Becton Dickinson Company, Ltd.) was used to identify S. gallolyticus.
RAPID ID 32 A (bioMerieux Japan Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was
used to identify C. septicum and F. nucleatum. If the preserved organisms were recovered, their identification was
confirmed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization
time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry (MALDI
Biotyper; Bruker Daltonics GmbH & Co. KG., Bremen,
Germany).

Results
Among 2866 patients with bacteremia, the number of
patients with bacteremia by Escherichia coli was 535
(18.7%) and that of patients with bacteremia by Bacteroides species was 35 (1.22%). The target organisms were isolated from 15 patients (C. septicum in 1 [0.035%] patient,
S. gallolyticus in 11 [0.38%] patients, and F. nucleatum in 3
[0.10%] patients). Fourteen patients were analyzed because
of loss to follow-up in patients with F. nucleatum bacteremia. Given the extremely small sample size, meaningful statistical comparisons are unlikely to be feasible, and
comparisons among these organisms were not performed.
MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry was used to identify 12
of the 14 strains. The source of bacteremia was proven in
6 of 14 patients (cholangitis [n = 3], infectious endocarditis
[n = 2], infectious spondylitis [n = 1], and pancreatic abscess
[n = 1]; one patient had infectious endocarditis and spondylitis) (Table 1).
Echocardiography was performed in five patients and
magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in two patients with S.
gallolyticus bacteremia. However, these examinations to
detect an infectious focus were not performed in patients
with C. septicum or F. nucleatum. Colonoscopy was performed at the time of bacteremia in only three patients
because of gastrointestinal symptoms and/or colorectal
computed tomography (CT) findings, and colorectal neoplasms were detected in two of these patients (Cases 1 and
7). Isolation of these three organisms from blood culture

Discussion
An association between colorectal neoplasms and bacteremia was observed in one patient with C. septicum and
two patients with S. gallolyticus. However, no colorectal
neoplasm was identified in two patients with F. nucleatum
bacteremia because the present study was underpowered
to evaluate the association of F. nucleatum with colorectal
neoplasia, and adequate colorectal surveillance was not performed in these patients. There are three patterns of association between colorectal neoplasms and bacteremia [13]. (1)
Bacteremia occurred due to advanced cancer with a gross
deficit of the mucosa, (2) bacteremia occurred due to an
adenoma or cancer in situ without ulceration of the mucosa,
and (3) development of cancer with a prolonged elapsed
time after the presentation of bacteremia. Our findings are
consistent with the hypothesis proposed by Corredoira et al.
[13] (Fig. 5). Pattern 1 was observed in Case 1 with C. septicum, whereas patterns 2 and 3 were observed in Cases 7 and
3 with S. gallolyticus. However, it should be stressed that
the true prevalence of synchronous or asynchronous development of colorectal neoplasia cannot be determined in C.
septicum, S. gallolyticus, and F. nucleatum in the present
study, because concurrent or surveillance colonoscopy was
not performed in the majority of patients.
Corredoira et al. [13] reported that the percentage of adenoma and carcinoma in situ was 80.4% in S. gallolyticus,
whereas that of invasive cancer was 94.7% in C. septicum
and more than half of the patients were classified as stages

including adenoma or carcinoma in adenoma causes bacteremia and
rarely develops intestinal symptoms in S. gallolyticus. Several months
or years later, colorectal carcinoma may develop because of carcinogenesis caused by S. gallolyticus

III or IV. Hypothetically, invasive cancer might provide a
favorable environment for C. septicum to reside, proliferate, and enter the bloodstream. (Fig. 5) Tumor lesions are
associated with ischemia and even necrosis in the crater of
the carcinoma, which decreases the pH, favoring the germination of spores [22]. In contrast, S. gallolyticus involved
in early carcinogenesis induces several inflammatory cytokines, which could also facilitate this bacterium to enter the
blood circulation by a paracellular mechanism [6, 22].
A study showed that a new colorectal neoplasm developed in half of the patients with S. gallolyticus bacteremia
within 42 months of follow-up [13]. Justesen et al. [15]
reported that the median number of days to CRC diagnosis
after bacteremia was 975 in patients with S. bovis, including
S. gallolyticus, and 778 in those with F. nucleatum. A prospective study showed that 45% of patients with IE due to
S. gallolyticus developed colonic neoplastic lesions within 5
years of diagnosis [23]. Boleij et al. suggested the need for
a follow-up colorectal surveillance examination in patients
with S. gallolyticus, which is a well-known promoter of carcinogenesis [24]. CRC would have developed during the
time between the episode of bacteremia and the diagnosis of
CRC in Case 3, in whom the elapsed time from identifying
bacteremia to diagnosing CRC was 31 months.
Bacteroides species were isolated from blood cultures
of 35 patients in this study. Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides
fragilis has been shown to exert effects on colorectal carcinogenesis [25]. In addition, Justesen et al. [16] reported
that bacteremia caused by Bacteroides ovatus or Bacteroides uniformis was associated with an increased risk of CRC

diagnosis. However, considering the number of colorectal
examinations needed to identify one colorectal neoplasm
case, the studied organisms with infrequent isolation from
blood culture would be promising candidates for colorectal
workup rather than B. fragilis group, which is almost exclusively found in the colon.
This study has several limitations. First, colonoscopy
was performed only in patients with abdominal symptoms
or abnormal CT findings, and asymptomatic patients with
colorectal neoplasms might have been overlooked. Therefore, comparisons among bacterial species were not feasible
using the patients described in this study. Although colorectal neoplasms have not been identified in patients with F.
nucleatum bacteremia, F. nucleatum has been increasingly
recognized as a microorganism associated with colorectal
carcinogenesis [10, 11, 15, 16, 21]. Second, MALDI-TOF
mass spectrometry libraries have limited capabilities for the
differential identification of S. bovis/S. equinus-complex
subspecies, including S. gallolyticus, S. infantarius, S. lutetiensis, and S. equinus [26]. Considering that biliary tract
infection is a major cause of bacteremia in S. infantarius and
S. lutetiensis [7], missed identification might explain why
bacterial cholangitis was associated with S. gallolyticus bacteremia in three of 11 patients. Third, to suspect the translocation of bacteria from the gut to the bloodstream through
neoplasms, evidence of colonization of these bacteria on the
surface of neoplasms is required. Fourth, the reason for the
frequent recurrence of bacteremia was not encountered after
the initial episode in Case No. 3.
In conclusion, three different typical patterns of association between colorectal neoplasms and bacteremia were
reported in one patient with C. septicum bacteremia and
two patients with S. gallolyticus bacteremia. Given the retrospective design, very small sample size, limited rate of
colorectal workup, and absence of direct microbiological
evidence, the present findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating observations rather than evidence supporting distinct mechanisms of colorectal carcinogenesis. A
prospective study with rigorous colorectal examination is
required to prove the hypothesis when C. septicum, S. gallolyticus or F. nucleatum are isolated from blood cultures.
