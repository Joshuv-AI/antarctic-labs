Cytomegalovirus gastritis in an immunocompetent host: insights into gut viral pathogenesis and mucosal barrier disruption
Authors: Jalal Al Khateeb, Tala N. Musa, Nour Almaqadma, Firas Melhem, Sami Bannoura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCytomegalovirus (CMV) is a ubiquitous herpesvirus and an important, often underappreciated, viral pathogen of the gastrointestinal tract.Although CMV infection is typically asymptomatic in immunocompetent individuals, gastric involvement, a rare manifestation of gut CMV disease, poses a significant diagnostic challenge due to non-specific clinical and endoscopic features. The mechanisms by which CMV disrupts the gastric mucosal barrier, induces local inflammatory responses, and causes systemic protein loss remain incompletely characterised in immunocompetent hosts.To our knowledge, this is one of the youngest reported female cases of CMV gastritis with protein-losing gastropathy in an immunocompetent adult. This report aims to illuminate the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying severe CMV gastroduodenitis and the critical role of histopathological confirmation in establishing the diagnosis.Case presentationA 23-year-old previously healthy woman presented with progressive epigastric pain, vomiting, non-bloody diarrhoea, and severe bilateral lower-limb oedema following a self-limited flu-like illness.Physical examination revealed diffuse abdominal tenderness and marked peripheral oedema.Laboratory investigations showed elevated inflammatory markers, anaemia, and profound hypoalbuminaemia (serum albumin 20.4 g/L). CMV serology demonstrated markedly elevated IgM and IgG titres. Imaging studies demonstrated splenomegaly with small infarcts. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed severe gastric mucosal erythema with sloughing and exudation, as well as duodenal inflammation. Histopathological examination of gastric and duodenal biopsies demonstrated viral cytopathic changes consistent with CMV infection, including characteristic intranuclear owl-eye inclusions, confirming the diagnosis. The patient was treated with oral valganciclovir 900 mg twice daily for three weeks alongside supportive therapy, resulting in rapid clinical improvement, resolution of oedema, and significant normalisation of laboratory parameters. At one-month follow-up, she was asymptomatic with complete recovery.ConclusionsThis case illuminates the pathophysiological mechanisms by which CMV, a virus with well-characterised tropism for gastrointestinal epithelium that can disrupt the gastric mucosal barrier sufficiently to cause profound systemic protein loss. The low serum immunoglobulin G observed on admission most likely reflected secondary enteric immunoglobulin loss rather than pre-existing immunodeficiency, a distinction with important diagnostic implications. The case underscores that histopathological confirmation is the cornerstone of diagnosis, as serological and endoscopic findings alone are insufficient to distinguish active CMV tissue invasion from more common gastrointestinal conditions.The dramatic biochemical and clinical response observed following antiviral therapy provides an instructive in vivo correlate of the virus’s direct pathological role in mucosal injury.

Background

Cytomegalovirus (CMV) establishes lifelong latency after primary infection, which is typically asymptomatic in immunocompetent individuals.While recognized as a significant pathogen in immunocompromised hosts, causing severe end-organ disease, its role in causing symptomatic illness in immunocompetent adults is under-recognized [1, 2].

Gastrointestinal involvement is the most common form of organ-specific disease, with colitis being the predominant presentation. Gastric CMV infection, however, is uncommon and poses a particular diagnostic challenge [3]. Its clinical and endoscopic features are non-specific, often mimicking more common conditions such as peptic ulcer disease, Helicobacter pylori gastritis, or medication-induced injury [4, 5]. Presentations can range from mild, self-limiting gastritis to severe, ulcerative disease and, rarely, protein-losing gastropathy, a condition characterized by profound hypoalbuminemia and oedema due to gastric mucosal barrier disruption [6, 7].

This diagnostic ambiguity means CMV gastritis is often an incidental finding or a diagnosis of exclusion. Histopathological demonstration of viral cytopathic changes in tissue biopsies remains the diagnostic gold standard, as serology alone is insufficient to distinguish latent from active tissue-invasive infection [8]. Despite growing recognition of the gut virome’s role in gastrointestinal disease, CMV as a cause of severe mucosal injury in immunocompetent adults remains poorly characterised, with relatively few well-documented cases in the literature. We report a rare case of CMV gastroduodenitis with severe protein-losing gastropathy in a young immunocompetent woman - one of the youngest female cases in the published literature - together with a comprehensive review of comparable cases. The case provides detailed pathophysiological and histopathological documentation of how CMV disrupts the gastric mucosal barrier, drives systemic protein loss, and induces secondary immunological abnormalities, contributing to the characterisation of this underappreciated gut viral pathogen.

Case presentation

A 23-year-old female with no significant past medical history was in her usual state of health until she developed gradual-onset epigastric pain, sharp in character and non-radiating. Initially the pain was relieved by food intake; however, in the week before hospital presentation it became aggravated with meals and progressed to severe, continuous pain throughout the day. She reported vomiting approximately three times daily, consisting of food contents, without blood or coffee-ground appearance. She also described non-bloody, non-mucoid diarrhoea and severe bilateral lower-limb swelling. She recalled a febrile, flu-like illness one week before the onset of abdominal symptoms. She denied dysuria, haematuria, frothy urine, or changes in urinary frequency, and had no prior episodes of similar symptoms.

She had no history of organ transplantation, use of immunosuppressant medications, recurrent infections since childhood, or family history of similar illness. She had no known drug allergies and no regular medications. She reported occasional tobacco use for the preceding three years.

On physical examination, diffuse and severe abdominal tenderness was present on superficial palpation without rigidity. Bilateral lower-limb pitting oedema of 3 + was noted. Abdominal ultrasound revealed gastroduodenal wall thickening, splenomegaly measuring 13 cm, and small splenic infarcts. The patient was treated symptomatically with no improvement. A contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis confirmed splenomegaly and demonstrated ground-glass opacification in the lower lung lobes.

Laboratory investigations are summarised below. White blood cells level (WBC) was 3.8 × 10⁹/L and it showed monocytosis at 15%. C-reactive protein (CRP) was elevated at 66 mg/L. Haemoglobin was 96 g/L. Serum albumin was 20.4 g/L and total protein was 32 g/L. Liver enzymes were mildly elevated: aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 79 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 75 U/L. Serum electrolytes showed potassium 2.88 mmol/L and sodium 139 mmol/L. Renal function was intact: creatinine 34.5 µmol/L and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) 2.9 mmol/L. Random blood glucose was 5.2 mmol/L. Urinalysis showed urine protein 770 mg/L with urine creatinine 27.8 mmol/L, yielding a spot urine protein-to-creatinine ratio of 0.025 g/mmol (approximately 0.22 g/g), which is sub-nephrotic range and effectively excluded renal protein loss as a cause of hypoalbuminaemia. Blood cultures and antinuclear antibodies (ANA) were negative. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was 5 mm/h (reference range 0–10 mm/h). Serology for hepatitis C antibodies, hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), and HIV was negative. Total immunoglobulin G (IgG) was 3.79 g/L (reference range 8–18 g/L), Immunoglobulin A was 1.64 g/L (reference range 0.9–4.5 g/L) and Immunoglobulin M was 0.62 g/L (reference range 0.6–2.8 g/L). CMV serology demonstrated markedly reactive IgM at 23.25 AU/mL (non-reactive threshold < 0.85 AU/mL) and elevated IgG at 159.90 AU/mL (negative threshold < 5 AU/mL), consistent with acute primary CMV infection.

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed severe mucosal erythema with large areas of mucosal sloughing and exudation in the gastric body, from which biopsies were obtained (Fig. 1). The duodenal bulb (D1) showed patchy nodular erythema, and biopsies were taken. The oesophagus and second part of the duodenum (D2) were macroscopically normal. Colonoscopy showed no abnormality. Histopathological examination of gastric biopsies demonstrated active gastritis with hyperplastic mucosal changes, erosions, and frequent viral cytopathic changes consistent with CMV infection, including characteristic eosinophilic intranuclear owl-eye inclusions and cytoplasmic basophilic inclusions (Fig. 2). Duodenal biopsies showed active duodenitis with rare CMV viral cytopathic changes also identified within Brunner glands. On the first day of admission for receiving treatment after pathology results were out she was on intravenous fluid for one day since she could not tolerate oral intake and then continued her hospital course on oral intake. She did not require enteral feeding. The patient was commenced on valganciclovir 900 mg orally twice daily for three weeks, together with pantoprazole 40 mg intravenously once daily, enoxaparin 40 mg subcutaneously once daily for venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, and furosemide 40 mg intravenously once daily for oedema management. Albumin replacement was administered supportively. Clinical improvement was evident within 24 h, and oedema began to resolve by 48 h. On day 3 of treatment, serum albumin improved from 20.4 g/L to 20.9 g/L, total protein from 32 g/L to 35 g/L, and CRP from 66 mg/L to 9 mg/L and WBC normalized to 8.4 × 10⁹/L. Medication adherence was confirmed through direct inpatient monitoring during hospitalisation and patient self-report at follow-up. At one-month follow-up, the patient had completed the full three-week course of valganciclovir, reported complete resolution of abdominal pain and lower-limb oedema, and was clinically well with no residual symptoms.

Discussion

We describe an unusual case of acute CMV gastroduodenitis in a young immunocompetent adult, complicated by severe protein-losing gastropathy with marked systemic sequelae. The case raises three clinically important issues: the pathophysiology of gastric protein loss in CMV disease, the primacy of histopathological confirmation, and the interpretation of secondary immunological abnormalities in the context of protein-losing gastroenteropathy.

The combination of a viral prodrome, upper gastrointestinal symptoms, profound hypoalbuminaemia, and normal renal and hepatic function is characteristic of protein-losing gastropathy. The urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio of 0.025 g/mmol (sub-nephrotic) effectively excluded significant renal protein loss, directing attention towards a gastrointestinal source. CMV-induced mucosal inflammation and erosion disrupt the epithelial barrier, increasing vascular permeability and promoting exudation of albumin and other serum proteins directly into the gastric lumen [2, 3]. This mechanism accounts for the severe hypoalbuminaemia and dependent oedema observed in our patient and underscores the importance of including gastric causes in the differential diagnosis of unexplained hypoalbuminaemia, alongside hepatic and renal aetiologies.

Definitive diagnosis was established histologically. Although CMV IgM positivity indicated recent or active infection, elevated IgG serology in the context of high population seroprevalence does not reliably distinguish active tissue-invasive disease from prior latent infection [4]. The endoscopic appearance of mucosal erythema, sloughing, and nodularity is non-specific and may be mistaken for peptic ulcer disease, autoimmune gastritis, or — importantly — malignancy.It was the demonstration of characteristic owl-eye intranuclear inclusions on biopsy that confirmed CMV as the causative pathogen [5, 6]. This case reinforces the established imperative for histopathological confirmation in any atypical or severe gastritis where routine causes have been excluded.

EBV co-infection serology was not obtained, which represents a limitation. However, EBV infectious mononucleosis was considered clinically unlikely given the absence of cervical lymphadenopathy, the lack of atypical lymphocytes on peripheral blood smear, and the markedly elevated CMV IgM titre consistent with acute primary CMV infection.

A finding that warrants specific discussion is the markedly reduced total serum IgG of 3.79 g/L on admission (reference range 8–18 g/L). This value, substantially below the lower limit of normal, might appear to challenge the characterisation of this patient as immunocompetent. However, it is well-established that protein-losing gastropathy and enteropathy result in enteric loss of immunoglobulins, producing a state of secondary hypogammaglobulinaemia that resolves upon treatment of the underlying disease [9, 10]. The absence of a history of recurrent infections, the negative autoimmune screen, and the clinical picture of an otherwise healthy young woman with acute-onset disease are consistent with a transient, disease-related phenomenon rather than a primary antibody deficiency.Formal confirmation of IgG normalisation after recovery was not performed due to resource constraints — this represents a limitation that future case reports in this setting should address.

The finding of splenomegaly with small splenic infarcts on imaging is notable. Splenomegaly is a recognised feature of primary CMV infection, reflecting systemic viral dissemination and splenic involvement [11]. Splenic infarcts have been reported in the context of CMV-associated coagulopathy, transient hypercoagulable states, and microvascular inflammation, though the precise mechanism in this patient could not be fully elucidated and warrants acknowledgement as an incompletely characterised finding.

The bilateral ground-glass opacification in the lower lung lobes was considered a non-specific finding by the reporting radiologist, most likely reflecting mild atelectasis or a subclinical inflammatory process; active CMV pneumonitis was not clinically suspected given the absence of respiratory symptoms and the patient’s preserved oxygen saturations throughout her admission.

The natural history of CMV gastritis in immunocompetent adults is heterogeneous, and many mild or moderate cases resolve without specific antiviral therapy [7, 8]. In the present case, valganciclovir was commenced given the severity of mucosal injury, the degree of protein loss, and the failure to stabilise with initial supportive measures. Clinical improvement was evident within 24 h and biochemical improvement within 72 h.Whether this reflects a direct antiviral effect, the natural trajectory of the illness, or a combination of both cannot be determined with certainty from a single case. What the response does document is the temporal correlation between CMV mucosal invasion and systemic protein loss: the rapid reversal of hypoalbuminaemia following viral suppression provides compelling in vivo evidence that active CMV replication was the primary driver of barrier disruption in this patient. This observation is consistent with the known mechanism of CMV-induced endothelial and epithelial injury [2, 3] and adds to the descriptive literature on the virus’s direct pathological role in the gastric mucosa.

Comparison with previously published cases (Table 1) highlights the atypical clinical profile of our patient. Most reported cases in immunocompetent adults involve middle-aged to elderly individuals (mean age 62.3 years in the largest case series [5]); our patient, aged 23, is among the youngest adult female cases documented. Regarding this demographic pattern observed across reported cases of CMV gastritis in immunocompetent hosts, a notable trend has emerged wherein male patients tend to present at a younger age, including pediatric cases, while female patients are more frequently older. Whether this distribution reflects a true biological or epidemiological association, or is simply an artifact of the limited number of published cases, remains uncertain. Given the rarity of this condition and the small sample sizes in the existing literature, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn. Prospective registries of CMV disease in immunocompetent hosts will be necessary to clarify whether these sex and age related differences represent a genuine epidemiological signal. In addition, protein-losing gastropathy as a complication is rare in immunocompetent adults and has been described in only isolated published cases [6, 12]. The detailed histopathological, serological, and biochemical documentation in the present case adds to the characterisation of this severe phenotype and provides a temporal account of mucosal injury and recovery that may inform future understanding of CMV’s pathological mechanisms in the non-immunocompromised gut.

NR = not reported; BD = twice daily; CRP = C-reactive protein; CMV = cytomegalovirus; EBV = Epstein–Barr virus; M = male; F = female Within the expanding conceptual framework of the gut virome, (CMV) represents a latent β-herpesvirus with the capacity to transition from asymptomatic persistence to clinically significant mucosal disease in response to host and environmental perturbations. Although the gut virome is predominantly composed of bacteriophages, eukaryotic viruses (including members of the Herpesviridae family) are increasingly recognised as active modulators of gastrointestinal homeostasis, capable of influencing epithelial integrity, immune signalling pathways, and microbial community structure [14, 15]. CMV, in particular, exhibits well-characterised tropism for gastrointestinal epithelium and endothelium, where viral replication induces cytopathic injury, disrupts tight junction architecture, and promotes local inflammatory responses that compromise barrier function [2, 3]. In this context, our case provides a clinically illustrative example of how a virome constituent can function as a direct pathogenic agent in an immunocompetent host, leading to profound epithelial dysfunction and systemic consequences such as protein-losing gastropathy. These findings support the evolving paradigm that the gut virome is not merely a passive reservoir of commensal or latent viruses, but a dynamic and functionally significant component of gastrointestinal pathophysiology, with the capacity to drive disease even in the absence of classical immunosuppression [14, 15].

Conclusion

This case documents a severe and uncommon manifestation of CMV - a well-recognised viral gut pathogen - causing life-threatening protein-losing gastropathy through direct mucosal invasion in an immunocompetent young adult. The detailed clinicopathological correlation presented here contributes to the characterisation of CMV’s pathophysiological mechanisms in the non-immunocompromised gastrointestinal tract, an area that remains incompletely understood despite the virus’s ubiquity. The temporal association between viral mucosal invasion, enteric protein and immunoglobulin loss, and systemic hypoalbuminaemia documents a coherent pathophysiological sequence with direct relevance to understanding how gut viral pathogens disrupt epithelial barrier function.The low serum IgG observed on admission almost certainly reflected enteric immunoglobulin loss secondary to mucosal barrier disruption rather than a primary immunodeficiency —a distinction that has important implications for the diagnostic interpretation of immune parameters in protein-losing gut disease. Histopathological confirmation, demonstrating characteristic owl-eye intranuclear inclusions, was the critical diagnostic step without which the viral aetiology would have remained unidentified. The dramatic biochemical reversal observed following viral suppression provides an instructive in vivo correlate of the virus’s direct pathological role, and the case adds meaningfully to the small body of literature documenting severe CMV gastric disease in immunocompetent hosts.

Limitations

This report describes a single patient, which limits generalisability. CMV quantitative PCR (viral load), which is the standard test for confirming active tissue-invasive CMV disease, was not performed due to resource constraints at our centre; the diagnosis therefore rests on the combination of serology, endoscopy, and histopathology. Faecal alpha-1 antitrypsin clearance, the objective gold standard for confirming protein-losing gastropathy, was also not available and the protein-loss mechanism thus remains inferred rather than directly quantified. Formal lymphocyte subset analysis (CD4/CD8) was not performed to objectively characterise immune status, and EBV co-infection serology was not obtained. Repeat serum IgG measurement after clinical recovery was not performed; this would have provided confirmatory evidence that the low admission IgG was a transient, disease-secondary phenomenon. Follow-up was limited to one month, precluding long-term outcome assessment. Finally, spontaneous resolution cannot be entirely excluded, as the rapid clinical response may have coincided with the natural course of the illness, though the severity and trajectory of the presentation make this unlikely.
