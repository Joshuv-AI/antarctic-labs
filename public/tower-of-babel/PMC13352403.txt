Independent and combined associations of cannabis and nicotine use with postoperative outcomes after cervical and lumbar interbody fusion: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Christopher D Hamad, Joshua Wiener, Soroush Shahamatdar, Autreen Golzar, Timothy Liu, Gabriel Su, Andrew P Kittredge, Rahi Patel, David C Kaelber, Nicholas M Bernthal, William L Sheppard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground ContextCannabis use has increased substantially in the United States and is commonly perceived to improve pain control and reduce opioid requirements. However, its perioperative impact in spine surgery remains unclear, particularly when isolated from nicotine use.PurposeTo evaluate the independent and combined associations of cannabis and nicotine use with postoperative outcomes following cervical and lumbar interbody fusion.Study Design/SettingRetrospective cohort study using the TriNetX Research Network, a federated national database of deidentified electronic health records from over 200 US healthcare organizations.Patient SampleA total of 144,488 adult patients undergoing primary anterior cervical discectomy and fusion or lumbar interbody fusion procedures between 2015 and 2022.Outcome MeasuresNeurological complications, surgical complications (including infection and pseudoarthrosis), medical complications, pain and functional outcomes, psychiatric outcomes, opioid-related outcomes, and healthcare utilization through 2 years postoperatively.MethodsSubstance use exposures were defined using ICD-10 codes within 2 years preoperatively and categorized as nonusers, nicotine-only users, cannabis-only users, or concurrent cannabis and nicotine users. Cannabis exposure was identified using cannabis-related disorder codes or cannabis poisoning, and therefore reflects clinically documented problematic use rather than recreational cannabis behaviors. Multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for demographics, comorbidities, spine region, and surgical approach were used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) for outcomes. Pairwise intergroup comparisons and subgroup analyses by region and surgical approach were performed.ResultsAmong 144,488 patients, 108,962 were nonusers (75.4%), 33,309 nicotine-only users (23.1%), 540 cannabis-only users (0.4%), and 1,677 concurrent users (1.2%). Cannabis-only use was associated with higher odds of radiculopathy, cauda equina syndrome, chronic pain, myocardial infarction, acute respiratory failure, acute kidney injury, depression, opioid use disorder, and increased rehospitalization and emergency department utilization. Nicotineonly and concurrent use demonstrated broadly elevated risk across neurologic, surgical, medical, psychiatric, and utilization outcomes, including higher rates of infection, pseudoarthrosis, and opioid-related complications. Across most domains, concurrent users exhibited the highest adjusted odds. Venous thromboembolism was not significantly associated with any exposure group. Findings were generally consistent across cervical and lumbar procedures, with stronger associations observed following anterior approaches.ConclusionsCannabis-related diagnoses were associated with higher odds of multiple adverse postoperative outcomes following spine fusion, particularly for pain, psychiatric, opioid-related, and healthcare utilization endpoints. Rather than demonstrating an opioid-sparing effect, documented cannabis-related diagnoses appear to identify a subgroup of patients with increased perioperative vulnerability, especially when combined with nicotine use. These findings support routine preoperative screening, risk stratification, and targeted counseling regarding cannabis and nicotine use before elective spine fusion.Keywords: Spine fusion, Cervical and lumbar interbody fusion, Cannabis use, Nicotine use, Postoperative complications, Opioid use disorder, Healthcare utilization

Introduction

Cannabis use has substantially increased in the United States over the past 2 decades, reflecting shifts in both legalization policy and public perception [1]. For the first time, the number of daily cannabis users now surpasses the number of daily drinkers [2]. Additionally, public perception of cannabis has evolved, with 81% of adults reporting at least one health benefit and approximately 30% believing smoking cannabis prevents health problems [3]. This is in contrast with emerging literature demonstrating that cannabis use is associated with chronic bronchitis, cardiovascular disease, psychosis, and mood disorders [4,5]. This is particularly relevant as approximately 30% of cannabis users currently meet diagnostic criteria for cannabis use disorder (CUD), which would magnify the health impacts of this substance on patients [6].

Amid the opioid epidemic, cannabis has attracted interest as a potential alternative for pain management. Chronic back pain continues to be the most common indication for medical cannabis authorization in the US. However, its efficacy in pain management and broader health effects are areas of ongoing investigation and debate [7,8]. Given the increasing accessibility, changing perception of its safety, and ongoing interest in its analgesic potential, concerns regarding perioperative cannabis use during spinal surgery have emerged.

In regard to postoperative spinal outcomes, retrospective evidence reports inconclusive data regarding complications such as pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, and revision surgery. Although some studies have reported adverse outcomes, findings remain inconsistent when accounting for confounders such as tobacco use [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. In this context, surgeons are unable to lead a data driven discussion with patients regarding the real-world impacts of cannabis on pain management or surgical outcomes. This study aims to examine the impacts of cannabis, tobacco, and concurrent use on postoperative cervical and lumbar interbody fusion procedure outcomes for radiculopathy and provide intergroup analyses to elucidate impacts of each substance.

Methods

Data source and study design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX Research Network (Cambridge, MA, USA), a federated health research platform aggregating deidentified electronic health records from over 200 national healthcare organizations representing more than 130 million patients across the United States. The platform provides access to structured clinical data including demographics, diagnoses, procedures, medications, and laboratory results with longitudinal follow-up. The Research Network was accessed in August 2025 for cohort construction and analysis.

All data within TriNetX are deidentified in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule under 45 CFR §164.514(b)(1), utilizing expert-determined methods such as obfuscation and minimum cell sizes (≥10 individuals per value). Accordingly, this study does not constitute human subjects research as defined under 45 CFR §46.102 and is exempt from institutional review board (IRB) oversight.

Cohort definitions

Adult patients (aged ≥18 years) undergoing primary spine fusion surgery between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2022, were identified using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes for anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF; 22551, 22552, 22554), posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF; 22630, 22632), anterior and lateral lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF, OLIF, XLIF; 22558, 22585), and transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF; 22633, 22634). The index date was defined as the date of the first qualifying fusion procedure.

Exposure classification

Substance use exposures were classified based on International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnostic codes documented within 730 days (2 years) before the index surgery. Cannabis use was identified using codes for cannabis-related disorders (F12.x) and cannabis poisoning (T40.7x). Nicotine dependence was identified using codes for tobacco use disorder (F17.x), toxic effect of tobacco (T65.2x), personal history of nicotine dependence (Z87.891), and tobacco use (Z72.0).

Patients were classified into 4 mutually exclusive exposure groups: (1) nonusers (no cannabis or nicotine use), (2) nicotine-only users, (3) cannabis-only users, and (4) concurrent users (documentation of both cannabis and nicotine use codes). Outcomes

Outcomes were identified using ICD-10 diagnostic codes with outcome specific follow-up windows designed to capture clinically relevant complications while minimizing confounding.

Neurological complications

Neurological complications were assessed from 90 days to 2 years postoperatively using incident-only definitions, excluding patients with preexisting diagnoses documented in the 365 days before surgery, to differentiate new-onset complications from preexisting conditions. These included paralysis (G82), myelopathy (G95, M510), radiculopathy (M541), cauda equina syndrome (G834), neurogenic bladder (N31), urinary incontinence (R32, N398, G958), and fecal incontinence (R15, K9189).

Surgical complications

Surgical complications were assessed from 1 day to 2 years postoperatively to capture both immediate perioperative and delayed complications. These include surgical site infection and wound dehiscence (T81.41, T81.30-T81.33, and L08.9), hardware/deep tissue infection (T81.42, T81.43, T84.6, G06.1, G06.2, M46.2, M46.3, and M46.4), pseudoarthrosis (M96.0), hematoma (G96.51), and seroma/pseudomeningocele (G97.82 and L72.89).

Pain and functional outcomes

Pain and functional outcomes were assessed from 90 days to 2 years and included chronic back pain (M545, M549). unspecified chronic pain (G89), as well as mobility limitations (R26, Z74) were assessed.

Medical complications

Medical complications were assessed from 1 day to 2 years and included cardiovascular events myocardial infarction (MI) (I21, I22), stroke (I63), thromboembolic events deep vein thrombosis, (DVT) (I82.4, I82.5), pulmonary embolism (I26), acute respiratory failure (J96), acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) (J80), and acute kidney injury (AKI) (N17). Infectious complications included sepsis (A419, R652, T8144) and pneumonia (J12–J18).

Psychiatric outcomes

Psychiatric outcomes were assessed from 90 days to 2 years postoperatively and included new-onset depression (F32, F33), new-onset anxiety (F40, F41), new opioid use disorder (F11), and opioid prescription codes (RxNorm: 7052, 7804, 6378, 5489, 7238, 10689, 6813, 3423, 6754, 787390) assessed at 30 days, 90 days, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years.

Healthcare utilization

Healthcare utilization was assessed from 1 day to 2 years and included subsequent hospitalization (CPT 99221–99236) and emergency department visits (CPT 99281–99285) at 30 days, 90 days, 1 year, and 2 years postoperatively.

Composite outcomes

Composite outcomes were defined as follows: any neurological complication (paralysis, myelopathy, cauda equina syndrome, neurogenic bladder, urinary incontinence, or fecal incontinence), any surgical site infection (superficial or hardware/deep infection), and any venous thromboembolism (DVT or PE).

Covariates

Covariates for adjustment were selected a priori and included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), spine region (cervical vs. lumbar), surgical approach (anterior vs. posterior), and comorbidities documented within 365 days before surgery. Comorbidities included all components of the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). Additional covariates included obesity (E66), prior depression (F32, F33), prior anxiety (F40, F41), osteoporosis (M80, M81), anemia (D50–D64), prior opioid use disorder (F11), and prior alcohol use disorder (F10), immunodeficiency (D80–D84), malnutrition (E40–E46), chronic steroid use (Z79.52), and trauma (T07).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared across exposure groups using standardized mean differences (SMD), with SMD > 0.1 indicating meaningful imbalance. Crude outcome rates were calculated as the proportion of patients experiencing each outcome within the specified follow-up window.

Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for each outcome, comparing each substance use group to nonusers while adjusting for all prespecified covariates. Models were fit only for outcomes with at least 10 events to ensure stable parameter estimation.

Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated for outcomes, with log-rank tests for between-group comparisons. Post hoc pairwise comparisons between exposure groups (cannabis only vs. nicotine only, cannabis only vs. concurrent use, nicotine only vs. concurrent use) were conducted using estimated marginal means with Tukey adjustment for multiple comparisons.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided p-value less than 0.05. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [16].

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 144,488 patients undergoing primary spine fusion surgery between 2015 and 2022 met inclusion criteria, including 108,962 nonusers (75.4%), 540 cannabis-only users (0.4%), 33,309 nicotine-only users (23.1%), and 1,677 concurrent cannabis and nicotine users (1.2%). Procedures included anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (39.5%), posterior lumbar interbody fusion (35.3%), transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (16.0%), and anterior, oblique, or extreme lateral lumbar interbody (ALIF, OLIF, XLIF) fusion (9.2%). Overall, 48.7% of procedures were performed via anterior or lateral approaches and 51.3% via posterior approaches.

Mean age was lower among cannabis only (49.5 ± 15.5 years) and concurrent users (50.7 ± 12.9 years) compared with nicotine-only users (59.1 ± 12.5 years) and nonusers (59.0 ± 13.6 years). Male sex was more prevalent among cannabis only (63.3%) and concurrent users (62.0%) compared with nonusers (48.8%). Mean BMI was highest among nonusers (30.2 kg per m²) and lowest among concurrent users (28.0 kg per m²). Preadjusted cohort characteristics are shown in Tables 1 and 2.

Neurological outcomes

Any neurological complication occurred in 12,007 patients (8.3%) and was more common among cannabis-only users (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.31, 95% CI 1.01–1.71, p = 0.044), nicotine-only users (aOR 1.14, 95% CI 1.09–1.20, p < 0.001), and concurrent users (aOR 1.37, 95% CI 1.18–1.59, p < 0.001) Fig. 2 and Table 2.

Forest plot of adjusted odds ratios.

Paralysis occurred in 2,183 patients (1.5%) and was not significantly associated with cannabis-only use (aOR 0.63, 95% CI 0.33–1.21, p = 0.167), nicotine-only use (aOR 0.91, 95% CI 0.82–1.01, p = 0.067), or concurrent use (aOR 0.70, 95% CI 0.49–1.01, p = 0.054).

Myelopathy occurred in 5,073 patients (3.5%) and was associated with nicotine-only use (aOR 1.16, 95% CI 1.09–1.24, p < 0.001) and concurrent use (aOR 1.32, 95% CI 1.05–1.66, p = 0.017), but not cannabis-only use (aOR 1.06, 95% CI 0.68–1.67, p = 0.793).

Radiculopathy occurred in 42,398 patients (29.3%) and was associated with cannabis-only use (aOR 1.28, 95% CI 1.06–1.54, p = 0.009), nicotine-only use (aOR 1.38, 95% CI 1.34–1.41, p < 0.001), and concurrent use (aOR 1.36, 95% CI 1.23–1.51, p < 0.001).

Cauda equina syndrome occurred in 458 patients (0.3%) and was associated with cannabis-only use (aOR 2.98, 95% CI 1.31–6.75, p = 0.009) and nicotine-only use (aOR 1.32, 95% CI 1.07–1.63, p = 0.010), but not concurrent use (aOR 1.51, 95% CI 0.76–2.97, p = 0.238).

Neurogenic bladder occurred in 2,003 patients (1.4%) and was not significantly associated with cannabis-only use (aOR 0.74, 95% CI 0.40–1.38, p = 0.349) or concurrent use (aOR 0.80, 95% CI 0.57–1.13, p = 0.213), while nicotine-only use demonstrated a protective association (aOR 0.82, 95% CI 0.73–0.92, p < 0.001).

Urinary incontinence occurred in 4,658 patients (3.2%) and was more frequent among cannabis-only users (aOR 1.53, 95% CI 1.03–2.28, p = 0.034), nicotine-only users (aOR 1.17, 95% CI 1.09–1.25, p < 0.001), and concurrent users (aOR 1.59, 95% CI 1.27–2.00, p < 0.001).

Fecal incontinence occurred in 1,215 patients (0.8%) and was significantly associated with nicotine-only use (aOR 1.20, 95% CI 1.06–1.37, p = 0.006) and concurrent use (aOR 1.58, 95% CI 1.05–2.37, p = 0.028), with a nonsignificant trend for cannabis-only use (aOR 1.75, 95% CI 0.86–3.56, p = 0.122).

Surgical complications

Any surgical site infection occurred in 8,332 patients (5.8%) and was associated with nicotine-only use (aOR 1.27, 95% CI 1.20–1.34, p < 0.001) and concurrent use (aOR 1.43, 95% CI 1.20–1.70, p < 0.001), but not cannabis-only use (aOR 1.08, 95% CI 0.76–1.52, p = 0.672).

Superficial infection occurred in 7,589 patients (5.3%) and was associated with nicotine-only use (aOR 1.29, 95% CI 1.22–1.36, p < 0.001) and concurrent use (aOR 1.51, 95% CI 1.26–1.80, p < 0.001), but not cannabis-only use (aOR 1.11, 95% CI 0.78–1.59, p = 0.567).

Hardware or deep infection occurred in 2,699 patients (1.9%) and was associated with nicotine-only use (aOR 1.18, 95% CI 1.08–1.29, p < 0.001) and concurrent use (aOR 1.49, 95% CI 1.12–1.97, p = 0.006), but not cannabis-only use (aOR 1.08, 95% CI 0.60–1.93, p = 0.799).

Pseudoarthrosis occurred in 9,348 patients (6.5%) and was associated with nicotine-only use (aOR 1.45, 95% CI 1.38–1.52, p < 0.001) and concurrent use (aOR 1.53, 95% CI 1.29–1.82, p < 0.001), but not cannabis-only use (aOR 1.16, 95% CI 0.83–1.63, p = 0.396).

Hematoma occurred in 1,220 patients (0.8%) and was associated with nicotine-only use (aOR 1.30, 95% CI 1.14–1.48, p < 0.001) and concurrent use (aOR 2.06, 95% CI 1.42–3.00, p < 0.001), but not cannabis-only use (aOR 1.16, 95% CI 0.48–2.81, p = 0.748).

Seroma or pseudomeningocele occurred in 1,899 patients (1.3%) and was associated with nicotine-only use (aOR 1.26, 95% CI 1.13–1.40, p < 0.001) and concurrent use (aOR 1.67, 95% CI 1.21–2.30, p = 0.002), but not cannabis-only use (aOR 0.67, 95% CI 0.27–1.61, p = 0.367).

Pain and functional outcomes

Chronic back pain occurred in 53,851 patients (37.3%) and was more common among cannabis-only users (aOR 1.77, 95% CI 1.48–2.11, p < 0.001), nicotine-only users (aOR 1.61, 95% CI 1.57–1.65, p < 0.001), and concurrent users (aOR 2.03, 95% CI 1.83–2.25, p < 0.001).

Chronic pain occurred in 43,044 patients (29.8%) and was associated with cannabis-only use (aOR 2.04, 95% CI 1.71–2.44, p < 0.001), nicotine-only use (aOR 1.75, 95% CI 1.70–1.80, p < 0.001), and concurrent use (aOR 2.31, 95% CI 2.09–2.56, p < 0.001).

Mobility limitations occurred in 14,854 patients (10.3%) and were associated with cannabis-only use (aOR 1.37, 95% CI 1.05–1.79, p = 0.020), nicotine-only use (aOR 1.30, 95% CI 1.25–1.35, p < 0.001), and concurrent use (aOR 1.35, 95% CI 1.16–1.56, p < 0.001).

Medical complications

Myocardial infarction occurred in 2,746 patients (1.9%) and was associated with cannabis-only use (aOR 2.43, 95% CI 1.46–4.05, p < 0.001), nicotine-only use (aOR 1.44, 95% CI 1.32–1.58, p < 0.001), and concurrent use (aOR 1.70, 95% CI 1.25–2.31, p < 0.001).

Stroke occurred in 3,403 patients (2.4%) and was not associated with cannabis-only use (aOR 1.07, 95% CI 0.59–1.95, p = 0.826), but was associated with nicotine-only use (aOR 1.27, 95% CI 1.18–1.38, p < 0.001) and concurrent use (aOR 1.53, 95% CI 1.17–2.02, p = 0.002).

Any venous thromboembolism occurred in 5,376 patients (3.7%) and was not significantly associated with cannabis only (aOR 0.90, 95% CI 0.57–1.43, p = 0.662), nicotine only (aOR 1.01, 95% CI 0.94–1.07, p = 0.858), or concurrent use (aOR 1.17, 95% CI 0.94–1.47, p = 0.161).

DVT occurred in 3,641 patients (2.5%) and was not significantly associated with cannabis-only use (aOR 1.17, 95% CI 0.71–1.91, p = 0.542), nicotine-only use (aOR 0.99, 95% CI 0.92–1.07, p = 0.844), or concurrent use (aOR 1.27, 95% CI 0.97–1.65, p = 0.082).

Pulmonary embolism occurred in 2,594 patients (1.8%) and was significantly less frequent among cannabis-only users (aOR 0.27, 95% CI 0.09–0.84, p = 0.024), but not associated with nicotine only (aOR 1.00, 95% CI 0.91–1.09, p = 0.952) or concurrent use (aOR 0.90, 95% CI 0.64–1.27, p = 0.563). This finding should be interpreted with caution given sparse events in the cannabis-only group (n = 3).

Acute respiratory failure occurred in 6,651 patients (4.6%) and was associated with cannabis-only use (aOR 1.71, 95% CI 1.22–2.38, p = 0.002) and nicotine-only use (aOR 1.24, 95% CI 1.17–1.31, p < 0.001), but not concurrent use (aOR 1.10, 95% CI 0.90–1.35, p = 0.337).

Acute respiratory distress syndrome occurred in 5,630 patients (3.9%) and was associated with nicotine-only use (aOR 1.25, 95% CI 1.18–1.34, p < 0.001), but not cannabis only (aOR 1.43, 95% CI 0.98–2.08, p = 0.064) or concurrent use (aOR 1.11, 95% CI 0.90–1.38, p = 0.327).

Acute kidney injury occurred in 8,027 patients (5.6%) and was associated with cannabis-only use (aOR 1.51, 95% CI 1.06–2.15, p = 0.022), nicotine-only use (aOR 1.27, 95% CI 1.20–1.34, p < 0.001), and concurrent use (aOR 1.70, 95% CI 1.42–2.04, p < 0.001).

Sepsis occurred in 4,849 patients (3.4%) and showed a nonsignificant trend among cannabis-only users (aOR 1.46, 95% CI 0.98–2.19, p = 0.064), with significant associations for nicotine-only use (aOR 1.23, 95% CI 1.15–1.32, p < 0.001) and concurrent use (aOR 1.79, 95% CI 1.46–2.19, p < 0.001).

Pneumonia occurred in 6,074 patients (4.2%) and demonstrated a nonsignificant trend among cannabis-only users (aOR 1.41, 95% CI 0.98–2.03, p = 0.066), with significant associations for nicotine-only use (aOR 1.31, 95% CI 1.24–1.39, p < 0.001) and concurrent use (aOR 1.34, 95% CI 1.10–1.63, p = 0.003).

Psychiatric outcomes

Depression occurred in 22,603 patients (15.6%) and was associated with cannabis-only use (aOR 1.50, 95% CI 1.20–1.87, p < 0.001), nicotine-only use (aOR 1.42, 95% CI 1.37–1.47, p < 0.001), and concurrent use (aOR 1.72, 95% CI 1.53–1.95, p < 0.001).

Anxiety occurred in 21,588 patients (14.9%) and was associated with nicotine-only use (aOR 1.41, 95% CI 1.35–1.46, p < 0.001) and concurrent use (aOR 1.61, 95% CI 1.43–1.82, p < 0.001), but not cannabis-only use (aOR 1.24, 95% CI 0.99–1.56, p = 0.060).

New opioid use disorder occurred in 2,023 patients (1.4%) and demonstrated the strongest associations among concurrent users (aOR 4.92, 95% CI 4.04–5.99, p < 0.001), followed by cannabis-only users (aOR 2.81, 95% CI 1.83–4.33, p < 0.001) and nicotine-only users (aOR 2.39, 95% CI 2.17–2.64, p < 0.001).

At 30 days postoperatively, 125,724 patients (87.0%) received opioid prescriptions, with higher odds among cannabis-only users (aOR 2.24, 95% CI 1.56–3.22, p < 0.001), nicotine-only users (aOR 1.50, 95% CI 1.43–1.56, p < 0.001), and concurrent users (aOR 2.30, 95% CI 1.85–2.87, p < 0.001). These associations strengthened over time. At 2 years, 131,217 patients (90.8%) had received opioid prescriptions, with the highest odds among cannabis-only users (aOR 3.87, 95% CI 2.22–6.75, p < 0.001), followed by concurrent users (aOR 3.26, 95% CI 2.38–4.47, p < 0.001) and nicotine-only users (aOR 1.74, 95% CI 1.65–1.84, p < 0.001).

Healthcare utilization

Rehospitalization within 30 days occurred in 37,768 patients (26.1%) and were associated with cannabis-only use (215 [39.8%], aOR 1.75, 95% CI 1.44–2.11, p < 0.001), nicotine-only use (10,688 [32.1%], aOR 1.16, 95% CI 1.13–1.20, p < 0.001), and concurrent use (733 [43.7%], aOR 1.86, 95% CI 1.67–2.08, p < 0.001). At 90 days (39,267 patients, 27.2%), 1 year (43,052 patients, 29.8%), and 2 years (46,972 patients, 32.5%) postoperatively, rehospitalization remained significantly elevated across all exposure groups, with consistently higher odds among cannabis only and concurrent users compared with nicotine-only users.

Emergency department visits within 30 days occurred in 9,482 patients (6.6%) and were associated with cannabis-only use (49 [9.1%], aOR 1.41, 95% CI 1.05–1.90, p = 0.023), nicotine-only use (2,974 [8.9%], aOR 1.34, 95% CI 1.27–1.40, p < 0.001), and concurrent use (238 [14.2%], aOR 2.11, 95% CI 1.83–2.44, p < 0.001). At 90 days (14,720 patients, 10.2%), 1 year (25,822 patients, 17.9%), and 2 years (34,182 patients, 23.7%), emergency department utilization remained significantly elevated for all exposure groups, with the highest odds consistently observed among concurrent users.

Subgroup and inter group analyses

Approach and anatomical region subgroup analyses are presented in Supplementary Table 3 and Fig. 4. By spine region, associations between substance use and postoperative complications were broadly consistent in direction and magnitude for cervical and lumbar procedures. Cannabis-only use was significantly associated with medical complications, psychiatric complications, opioid use disorder, and healthcare utilization in both regions, while associations with neurological complications did not reach statistical significance in either cervical or lumbar cohorts. In contrast, nicotine only and concurrent use demonstrated significant associations across all 6 composite outcomes in both spine regions.

Pairwise intergroup comparisons of postoperative outcomes.

When stratified by surgical approach, cannabis-only use was significantly associated with neurological complications and new opioid use disorder following anterior procedures but not posterior procedures. Concurrent use demonstrated a similar pattern for neurological complications, with significant associations observed after anterior but not posterior approaches. For surgical, medical, psychiatric complications, and healthcare utilization, all substance use groups demonstrated significant associations regardless of approach, with effect sizes consistently larger for anterior procedures.

Pairwise inter group comparisons are presented in Supplementary Table 3. Compared with nicotine-only users, cannabis-only users had significantly higher odds of rehospitalization at all timepoints and higher odds of prolonged opioid prescriptions at 1 and 2 years. Compared with cannabis-only users, concurrent users demonstrated higher odds of emergency department visits at 90 days, 1 year, and 2 years, with no other significant differences observed. Compared with nicotine-only users, concurrent users demonstrated significantly elevated risk across neurological, pain, medical, psychiatric, and healthcare utilization outcomes, including higher rates of opioid use disorder, emergency department visits, and hospital rehospitalization at all timepoints.

Discussion

In this large retrospective cohort study of 144,488 adults undergoing primary cervical and lumbar interbody spine fusion procedures, cannabis only, nicotine only, and concurrent users were associated with higher odds of multiple postoperative complications across neurological, surgical, medical, and psychiatric domains, although associations varied by outcome and were not significant for all endpoints. Importantly, cannabis exposure in this study was defined by ICD-10 codes for cannabis-related disorders and cannabis poisoning and therefore should be interpreted as clinically documented or potentially problematic cannabis use rather than casual or undocumented recreational cannabis use. Distinct patterns emerged across exposure groups, with nicotine only and concurrent users demonstrating broadly elevated risk across most outcomes, and cannabis-only users showing higher odds for several clinically important endpoints including radiculopathy, cauda equina syndrome, chronic pain outcomes, myocardial infarction, acute respiratory failure, acute kidney injury, depression, opioid use disorder, and increased early healthcare utilization despite representing a small proportion of the cohort. Notably, venous thromboembolism was not significantly associated with any exposure group. These findings provide real-world clinical granularity by delineating specific complication profiles and by differentiating the associations of cannabis and nicotine in isolation and in combination.

Across many outcomes, concurrent users demonstrated the highest adjusted odds, while cannabis only and nicotine only users showed elevated risk with outcome specific differences in magnitude. Although not universal for every outcome, the overall pattern is consistent with an additive risk profile in which concurrent exposure is associated with the greatest postoperative vulnerability. This pattern may reflect overlapping cardiopulmonary and immunomodulatory effects of nicotine and cannabis exposure [[17], [18], [19]]. In addition, this underscores the importance of separating isolated cannabis exposure from nicotine and dual exposure when interpreting prior literature, as cannabis associated risk estimates may be inflated or obscured depending on how tobacco is handled analytically [20].

Despite growing public perception that cannabis improves pain control and reduces opioid requirements, our findings in patients with cannabis-related diagnoses suggest the opposite in a spine surgery population. Cannabis-related diagnoses were associated with higher odds of chronic back pain, chronic pain, mobility limitations, depression, new opioid use disorder, and sustained opioid prescription fills over time, with the strongest associations generally observed among patients with concurrent cannabis and nicotine exposure. Rather than demonstrating an opioid-sparing effect, cannabis exposure in this cohort appears to identify patients with higher documented pain burden and psychosocial comorbidity, factors known to complicate postoperative recovery and increase reliance on opioid analgesia. These findings align with emerging literature reporting higher postoperative pain scores and opioid requirements among patients who use cannabis across surgical subspecialties [21]. In addition, psychosocial burden is worse in cannabis users with higher patient-reported outcomes measurement information system (PROMIS) anxiety and depression scores in patients undergoing orthopedic procedures [22]. Consistent with these concerns, the American Society of Regional Anesthesia and Pain Medicine 2022 consensus guidelines recommend routine screening for cannabis use and perioperative risk counseling [23]. These findings suggest that cannabis-related diagnoses may serve as a marker of increased pain burden and psychosocial comorbidity in patients undergoing spine surgery. In parallel, cannabis only and concurrent use were associated with higher rates of emergency department visits and rehospitalization across short- and long-term follow-up, underscoring the downstream impact of these exposures on postoperative healthcare utilization.

From a practical standpoint, these findings support more deliberate perioperative counseling regarding cannabis use, analogous to existing frameworks for tobacco and opioids where tobacco cessation is advised several weeks before surgery and opioids are optimized to the lowest effective dose [24,25]. Although standardized cessation guidelines for cannabis are lacking, the observed associations with postoperative complications, pain, and opioid-related outcomes suggest that preoperative abstinence intervals warrant prospective evaluation, particularly for elective fusion procedures. Similar to opioid stewardship strategies, preoperative identification of cannabis-related diagnoses, particularly when combined with nicotine dependence, could prompt early engagement with pain management, behavioral health support, and expectation setting regarding postoperative analgesia. For patients with concurrent cannabis and nicotine exposure, who demonstrated the highest risk across multiple domains, intensified preoperative optimization and postdischarge monitoring may be reasonable to mitigate rehospitalization and prolonged recovery.

This study has several strengths, including a large multi-institutional cohort, mutually exclusive exposure groups, and outcome specific definitions designed to capture incident neurologic and psychiatric diagnoses. However, limitations inherent to administrative data apply. Cannabis exposure was defined using administrative ICD-10 codes for cannabis-related disorders and cannabis poisoning, which likely capture clinically documented or problematic cannabis use rather than the broader population of recreational users. As a result, these findings should be interpreted as applying to patients with documented cannabis-related diagnoses and may not generalize to casual or undocumented cannabis use. Similarly, nicotine exposure was defined using diagnostic codes, which may not capture all active tobacco use. Data on dose, frequency, route of administration, and perioperative abstinence were unavailable, and residual confounding from unmeasured clinical and socioeconomic factors cannot be excluded. The relatively small cannabis-only cohort, comprising 540 patients or 0.4% of the study population, also limits statistical power and precision for rare outcomes. Therefore, associations involving uncommon complications, including pulmonary embolism, cauda equina syndrome, and other low frequency events, should be interpreted cautiously. Future prospective studies incorporating granular exposure data including dose, frequency, and route of administration, standardized abstinence intervals, operative complexity measures, and patient reported outcomes are needed to better define causality and identify modifiable perioperative interventions.

Conclusion

In summary, cannabis-related diagnoses were associated with higher odds of several adverse postoperative outcomes following spine fusion, including increased healthcare utilization, particularly when combined with nicotine exposure. Rather than demonstrating an opioid-sparing effect, documented cannabis-related diagnoses appear to identify patients at increased risk for pain, psychosocial comorbidity, neurologic sequelae, and opioid-related complications in the postoperative period. These findings support routine preoperative screening for cannabis use, risk stratification, and targeted perioperative counseling, and highlight the need for further studies to determine whether preoperative abstinence and focused optimization strategies can improve outcomes in this growing patient population.
