Validating the Short screening Tool pRedicting Oral Health deterioration in older people by the general practitioner (SHORT) in the Netherlands
Authors: M H S de Jong, C D van der Maarel-Wierink, K Jerković-Ćosić, N Su, F R Rozema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrail, community-dwelling older people increasingly experience oral health issues, yet often lack access to dental care. General practitioners (GPs) and nurse practitioners (NPs) are well-positioned to help identify oral health deterioration, but existing tools are impractical for use in primary care. The SHORT screening tool was developed to address this gap by combining three brief self-report questions for older people, designed to be suitable for non-oral health care professionals. This study aimed to identify the three best-performing items out of ten candidate questions, and to validate the resulting SHORT tool’s diagnostic performance against clinical oral examination in older people in the Netherlands.MethodsA cross-sectional validation study was conducted in four general practices in the Netherlands. Participants aged ≥ 65 years who completed ten self-report items derived from a prior systematic literature and a Delphi consensus study, were included. A blinded oral examination, conducted by an oral health care professional, served as the reference standard. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) were calculated for individual items and for the final SHORT tool, which included the three highest-AUC items.ResultsForty-seven patients were included, ranging in age from 65 to 93 years. Among the individual items, “dental checkup this year” (AUC = 0.80), “pain in teeth or mouth” (AUC = 0.78), and “dry mouth” (AUC = 0.68) showed the highest diagnostic accuracy and were therefore selected to construct the SHORT tool. The final tool achieved a sensitivity of 100% (95% CI: 86.7–100%) and NPV of 100% (95% CI: 80.6–100%), correctly identifying all participants needing referral. Specificity was 72.7% (95% CI: 51.8–86.8%), PPV was 80.6% (95% CI: 63.7–90.8%), and the AUC was 0.86 (95% CI: 0.76–0.95).ConclusionThe SHORT tool demonstrated high predictive value for identifying older people at risk of oral health deterioration in general practice. Its high sensitivity and feasibility for integration into primary care workflows suggest that it may support timely referral and increase oral health and oral health awareness among older people and their health care professionals. Introduction

Oral health problems are becoming increasingly relevant among older people, partly because they retain their natural dentition longer, making dental care essential, especially as frailty and care dependency arise [1]. In addition, policies promoting aging at home have led to more community-dwelling older people with comorbidities, many of whom experience significant oral health issues associated with pain, chewing problems, and social complications due to embarrassment about the appearance of teeth. [2, 3]. Many older people are unable to visit a dentist independently, requiring home visits that are neither time- nor cost-effective for dental professionals [4]. As a result, dentists frequently lose sight of these patients, partly because they are difficult to reach, and also due to high workloads; financial considerations further contribute to this challenge [5, 6]. Moreover, older people often underestimate the severity of their oral health issues and prioritise other medical conditions over oral health. This is particularly concerning, as they are more likely to experience oral health problems due to physical and/or cognitive impairments affecting daily oral hygiene care, worsened by medication side effects [7].

Given these challenges, preventive strategies have become increasingly important. Older people are more frequently seen by medical professionals, such as general practitioners (GPs) and nurse practitioners (NPs; nurses working within the general practice) than oral health care professionals, making interprofessional collaboration promising for addressing oral health problems [8]. Research suggests that GPs and NPs are willing to engage in oral health care [9, 10], although, awareness of its significance is often lacking [11], and the high workload in general practice limits opportunities for comprehensive oral health screening. As a result, validated comprehensive oral health assessment questionnaires developed for non-oral health care professionals are underused [12]. Tools requiring an oral examination are also particularly difficult for non-oral health care professionals to use. Although photograph-supported tools have shown promise, they remain highly time-consuming, making them unsuitable for use in general practice [13]. Besides, tools such as OHIP and GOHAI, which do not require an oral inspection, are still too time-intensive and lack clear referral thresholds [14, 15].

General practitioners (GPs) and nurse practitioners (NPs) have indicated that a short screening tool, consisting of 2–5 self-reported items and taking only 1–2 min to administer, could facilitate oral health assessment and referral decisions [16]. Such a tool could not only enable timely identification of oral health deterioration, ensuring appropriate care, but also raise awareness among older people about the importance of oral health. Although oral health is sometimes deprioritized by older people, they tend to take medical advice from GPs and NPs very seriously [17]. If a validated screening tool enables primary care providers to initiate oral health referrals, awareness about the importance of good oral health among older people and their primary care providers increases.

A previous systematic review has shown that selected self-reported items have a high sensitivity and specificity for detecting oral health deterioration and indicating referral needs [18]. Based on this review, self-reported items were used as a basis for further development of the Short screening Tool pRedicting Oral Health deterioration in older people by the general practitioner (SHORT), following a Delphi study methodology. A multidisciplinary expert panel, consisting of various healthcare professionals, reached a consensus on the inclusion of three self-reported items in the tool [19]. However, as this selection was based on expert consensus rather than empirical diagnostic performance, all ten candidate items were included in the present validation study. The objective of this study was to identify the three best-performing items out of ten candidate questions, and to validate the resulting “SHORT” screening Tool predicting oral health deterioration in older people by assessing its diagnostic performance against clinical oral examination in older people in the Netherlands.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was conducted among participants from four general practices in the Netherlands, deliberately selected to cover the general population across different socioeconomic backgrounds. Older people aged 65 and above were eligible if they were mentally competent, visiting their general practitioner (GP) and/or nurse practitioners (NP) for an appointment, proficient in Dutch, and able to provide informed consent. Demographic variables self-reported by the participants included age and sex.

Data collection

Self-reported items

Participants completed a set of 10 self-reported items, each requiring a binary response ("yes" or "no"). Rather than assessing only the final composite of the SHORT screening tool, each item was evaluated individually to assess diagnostic performance more accurately. The items were administered to the older participants by the GP and/or nurse practitioner (NP). During administration, the GP and/or NP checked whether participants understood the items and whether the questionnaire was completed in full. Participants' responses were stored independently from the examiner and the researcher to maintain blinding. The 10 self-reported items selected from the systematic review and Delphi consensus study were: “Are you generally pleased with your mouth and teeth?”, “Do you have a dry mouth?”, “Did you have a dental checkup this year?”, “Do you have difficulty eating?”, “Do you have tooth or mouth problems that make it difficult to eat?”, “Did you alter or change your food selection?”, “Do you have pain in your teeth or mouth?”, “Do you have lesions, sores or lumps in your mouth?”, “Do you have bleeding gums, a loose denture, or loose teeth and/or molars?”, “Do you need dental treatment?” [18, 19]. The Dutch version of the questionnaire is available in Appendix 1.

Oral examination

After completion and review of the self-reported items by the GP and/or NP, an oral examination was conducted in the general practice setting. The examiner remained blinded to the self-reported responses throughout. The examination was systematically carried out by an oral health professional using standard dental instruments (mirror, probe, and light). The assessment items included: missing teeth and prosthetic restorations, mucosal health (including signs of dry mouth), oral hygiene, gingival inflammation, caries, and fractured teeth. If abnormalities were observed in any of these domains, the participant was classified as needing referral to an oral health care professional. The standardized scoring protocol for the oral examination can be found in Appendix 2.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated prior to the study using a dedicated calculator for diagnostic test evaluation, based on the method proposed by Buderer, which incorporates disease prevalence into the estimation of sensitivity and specificity [20, 21]. The calculation was performed using an online tool [22].

The calculation was based on sensitivity, as this was considered the most important diagnostic metric. Based on previous literature [18], the expected sensitivity was estimated to be between 90–100%; for this study, a target sensitivity of 95% was used. A precision of 10% was applied. The calculation was conducted using a significance level of 5% (α = 0.05, 95% confidence level). The expected dropout rate was assumed to be 0%. Based on the literature, a disease prevalence of 40% was used [23–25]. Based on these assumptions, the required sample size to estimate sensitivity was 46 participants. As no dropout was expected, no correction was applied. The final required sample size was therefore set at 46 participants.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.0, and R version 4.3.0 (R Core Team, 2023) using the binom package for Wilson confidence intervals and exact binomial testing [26–28]. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics, including median and ranges for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Differences between groups were explored using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. These analyses were exploratory in nature.

Each self-reported item was evaluated for criterion validity by comparing the yes/no responses to the reference standard: the results of the oral examination. Note: for some items, a “yes” response indicated a negative finding, while for others it indicated a positive one. This was accounted for during analysis. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated for each item individually, along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using the Wilson score method for binomial proportions [29]. The formulas used were: Sensitivity = [a/(a + c)] × 100, Specificity = [d/(b + d)] × 100, PPV = [a/(a + b)] × 100, NPV = [d/(c + d)] × 100. Where: a = true positive, b = false positive, c = false negative, d = true negative. The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) was calculated [30, 31], 95% confidence interval was calculated using DeLong method for AUC [29, 32].

Subsequently, the three self-report items with the highest AUC values were selected to form the SHORT tool. The overall sensitivity and specificity of this three-item combination were then calculated by comparing the tool’s outcome against the oral examination as the reference standard. A positive classification (referral to a dentist needed) was assigned if at least one complaint-related item was affirmed (‘yes’ response) or at least one contrarian item was denied (‘no’ response).

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Academic Centre for Dentistry Amsterdam (ACTA), registration number: 2023–17526.

Prior to participation, all individuals received written information explaining the study’s nature, purpose, and duration. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

All data were stored confidentially in pseudonymized form. Each participant was assigned a unique number, which was communicated to the researcher. This allowed the self-reported items to be matched with the oral examination findings. This process was double-checked to avoid matching errors.

Although not part of the study protocol, participants with abnormal oral examination findings were advised to visit a dentist. This ethical step ensured that oral health concerns identified during the assessment were not neglected.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 47 home-dwelling older people participated in the study. The median age was 76 years (range: 65–93). Of the included participants, 17 were male (36.2%) and 30 were female (63.8%). Participants were classified according to the need for referral to a dentist, as determined by the SHORT tool and the oral examination, respectively. Among those with a referral based on the SHORT tool (N = 31) the median age was 76.0 years, compared to 73.5 years in those without an indication for referral (p = 0.69). Similarly, participants classified as needing referral according to the oral examination (N = 25) had a higher median age (78.0 vs. 73.5 years; p = 0.31). No significant differences were found in sex distribution across groups. Participant characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Performance of individual items

The diagnostic value of each of the ten self-reported items was assessed separately. Individual item sensitivity ranged from 12 to 64%, while specificity ranged from 72.7% to 100%. AUC values ranged from 0.47 to 0.80. Items with the highest sensitivity were “Do you have a dry mouth?” (64%), “Did you have a dental checkup this year?” (60%), and “Do you have pain in your teeth or mouth?” (56%). Specificity was 100% for four of the ten items; including “Did you have a dental checkup this year?”, “Do you have pain in your teeth or mouth?”, “Do you have lesions, sores or lumps in your mouth?”, and “Do you have bleeding gums, a loose denture, or loose teeth and/or molars?”. The items with the highest AUC values were “Did you have a dental checkup this year?” (0.80), “Do you have pain in your teeth or mouth?” (0.78), and “Do you have a dry mouth?” (0.68). Overall, most individual items showed high specificity but limited sensitivity, highlighting the need for a combined approach to achieve sufficient diagnostic performance. Performance metrics of the individual items

The three items with the highest AUC values in the present validation study were “Did you have a dental checkup this year?”, “Do you have pain in your teeth or mouth?”, and “Do you have a dry mouth?”. These were the same three items that had previously been selected for the SHORT tool through Delphi consensus. Therefore, the validation results supported the previously established three-item composition of the SHORT tool.

Diagnostic performance of the SHORT tool

The final SHORT tool included the items: (1) “Did you have a dental checkup this year?”, (2) “Do you have a dry mouth?”, and (3) “Do you have pain in your teeth or mouth?” (see Appendix 4 for the final version). When using the oral examination as the reference standard, the SHORT tool identified 25 of 25 participants correctly as needing referral (true positives), with no false negatives. Among the 22 participants without an indication for referral based on the oral examination, 16 were correctly classified as such (true negatives), and 6 were false positives. The resulting diagnostic metrics were: Sensitivity: 100% (95% CI: 86.7–100%), Specificity: 72.7% (95% CI: 51.8–86.8%), Positive Predictive Value (PPV): 80.6% (95% CI: 63.7–90.8%), Negative Predictive Value (NPV): 100% (95% CI: 80.6–100%), and an AUC of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.76 to 0.95). Discussion

This study demonstrated that the SHORT screening tool has a high predictive value for identifying older people at risk of oral health deterioration. The combination of the three self-reported items, “Did you have a dental checkup this year?”, “Do you have a dry mouth?”, and “Do you have pain in your teeth or mouth?”, yielded excellent diagnostic performance. Specifically, the tool achieved a sensitivity and negative predictive value of 100%, indicating that all participants with clinical signs of oral health issues were correctly identified, and no cases were missed. In addition, the specificity and positive predictive value were adequate (72.7% and 80.6%, respectively), supporting the tool’s potential clinical utility as a screening instrument for identifying older people who may require referral to an oral health care professional.

The results of this validation study support the findings from the earlier Delphi-based development study, in which experts identified the most relevant, clear, and comprehensive self-report questions to include in the screening tool [19]. The three items selected through expert consensus, recent dental check-up, presence of dry mouth, and oral pain, are the same as those that demonstrated the highest predictive value in the current validation. This consistency strengthens the content validity of the SHORT tool. Additionally, previous studies on self-report oral health tools often included these same items, with similar diagnostic performance reported for questions related to dry mouth, dental pain, and dental visits [12, 14, 15, 33].

As older people with deteriorating oral health are often those who no longer attend dental check-ups [4, 7], it is not surprising that this specific combination yielded a high sensitivity. The item “Have you had a dental checkup this year?” captures this risk group effectively. However, the finding that this item alone was not sufficiently sensitive highlights a key clinical insight: oral health deterioration can still occur in spite of recent dental visits. This could reflect newly developed symptoms, missed diagnoses, or under-recognized signs such as dry mouth. Additionally, it is possible that older people provide socially desirable responses, for example, reporting a recent dental visit even if it occurred much longer ago. The additional items “Do you have a dry mouth?” and “Do you have pain in your teeth or mouth?” complement this gap by capturing subjective experiences potentially overlooked during routine dental check-ups.

While individual items had high specificity, achieving high sensitivity overall was the primary goal of the SHORT tool, because in screening, especially for conditions with potentially serious outcomes, minimizing false negatives is essential. In contrast, a false positive results in a non-invasive referral to the dentist, and may even have beneficial effects such as raising awareness, reassurance, education, and possibly additional preventive measures. As demonstrated in this study, combining items increased sensitivity to 100% while reducing specificity to 72.7%. Nevertheless, this level of specificity remains clinically acceptable.

The relatively low sensitivity of several individual items can be explained by their specificity to isolated symptoms. For example, not having lesions or eating difficulties does not exclude oral health deterioration. Hence, these questions are less suitable as individual indicators. In contrast, the three questions selected for the SHORT tool address broader aspects of oral health, making them more robust indicators of need for referral.

In this study 31.9% of older people had not visited a dentist in the past year, consistent with earlier findings showing declining dental visits with age [4, 7, 8]. In the Netherlands, this proportion exceeds 30% in people over 65 and 50% in people over 80 years old. One common explanation is the belief that dental care is no longer necessary after becoming edentulous. In this study, this was also frequently observed among participants who wore dentures. This suggests a potential role for general practitioners and nurse practitioners in raising awareness of the importance of oral health among older people who are edentulous. Regular dental visits remain important, even in the absence of natural dentition, as they support early detection of mucosal lesions, assessment of denture fit and function, prevention of infections such as denture stomatitis, and evaluation of oral quality of life [34–36].

Dry mouth emerged as a particularly prevalent complaint, echoing findings from previous studies and reinforcing its known association with polypharmacy in older populations [37]. Notably, many participants who reported dry mouth had recently visited a dentist, indicating that management is inherently challenging. Besides, dry mouth is not always clinically apparent. In this study, several participants who endorsed the dry mouth item had no observable signs during oral examination, underlining the value of including subjective assessments alongside clinical evaluation. In cases of suspected medication-induced dry mouth, GPs and NPs may contribute by initiating medication review, preferably in collaboration with a pharmacist.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its specific focus on the general practice setting. The SHORT tool was administered by general practitioners (GPs) or nurse practitioners (NPs).), reflecting the intended real-world context in which the tool is to be used. This ensured that participants, older people attending routine GP visits were representative of the target population.

Furthermore, the study included participants from four different general practices with diverse sociodemographic profiles, ranging from socially affluent areas where many older adults still attend dental appointments, to more deprived regions where regular dental care is less common. This variation is reflected in the observed differences in dental attendance and the estimated prevalence of oral health deterioration. The combined sample yielded rates of dental attendance and deterioration of oral health that are consistent with those reported in the literature [8, 22–25]. Another notable strength lies in the comprehensive development and validation process of the SHORT tool. The tool was based on a prior systematic review of the literature and refined through a Delphi consensus study, incorporating expert insights, optimizing item phrasing, and ensuring clinical feasibility. The present validation study built on this foundation by evaluating the tool’s diagnostic accuracy in routine clinical practice.

A limitation of the study is that the oral examination was performed by a single examiner, which may have introduced observer bias; ideally, two independent examiners would have been preferable, but this was not feasible in the general practice setting. To mitigate this, the examiner was experienced and worked with a standardized protocol that was discussed with other researchers and dental professionals (Appendix 2). Furthermore, the examination was conducted in general practices without access to dental clinic equipment such as radiographs or a dental chair with overhead light, which may have limited diagnostic detail.

Another potential limitation concerns the administration of the SHORT tool itself. It is possible that the degree of professional involvement varied between consultations, potentially influencing how the questions were interpreted or answered.

Implications for practice and future research

The SHORT tool demonstrated high predictive value and is promising as a screening tool for identifying oral health deterioration and risk thereof in community-dwelling older people within general practice. In practice, the SHORT tool may also provide an opportunity for GPs and practice nurses to raise awareness among edentulous older people that regular oral health care remains relevant, even in the absence of natural dentition. Further research is necessary to assess its implementation in daily practice, as this aspect received only limited attention in the preceding Delphi study and was not evaluated in detail in the current validation study. Although earlier qualitative work highlighted willingness among general practitioners (GPs) and nurse practitioners (NPs) for a brief and easy-to-administer tool, its actual integration into routine practice remains to be explored [16]. Future implementation studies should specifically assess the tool’s acceptability, usability, and feasibility in its finalized three-item format. Understanding how the tool fits into existing workflows and whether it facilitates timely and meaningful referral decisions will be critical for its successful adoption.

Beyond the general practice setting, additional research is needed to determine the SHORT tool’s applicability among other healthcare professionals. While GPs and NPs may benefit most from the tool given their time constraints, it also has potential in settings involving pharmacists, home care nurses, or geriatric wards in hospitals. Evaluating its utility and feasibility in these broader contexts could further expand its impact and reach.

Conclusion

The SHORT tool demonstrated high predictive value for identifying older people at risk of oral health deterioration in general practice. Its high sensitivity and brief format suggest that it may support timely referral and increase oral health and oral health awareness among older people and their health care professionals. Further research should focus on implementation and broader applicability across healthcare settings.
