Recurrence After Rectopexy: Insights From Magnetic Resonance Defecography

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Rectal prolapse recurrence after surgical repair remains a major limitation. Prior research surrounding recurrence risk has yielded inconsistent results and assessed a limited range of factors.OBJECTIVE:To evaluate whether preoperative magnetic resonance defecography, anorectal manometry, or balloon expulsion testing could identify biomarkers associated with rectal prolapse recurrence after rectopexy.DESIGN:Retrospective cohort study from a prospectively maintained rectal prolapse registry.SETTINGS:Single tertiary institution analysis.PATIENTS:A total of 155 patients (145 female patients) underwent rectopexy for prolapse between 2017 and 2025.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES:Clinical, technical, and imaging parameters were compared between patients with and without prolapse recurrence.RESULTS:All procedures were performed robotically and included ventral (n = 126; 75%), suture (n = 21; 12.5%), and resection rectopexy (n = 8; 4.8%). The recurrence rate was 10.3% (n = 16) during a median follow-up of 1295 days. On univariable analysis, the only clinical factor associated with recurrence was diabetes mellitus (recurrence 31.3% vs no recurrence 5.8%, p = 0.005). On magnetic resonance defecography (female patients) the presence of a cul-de-sac hernia (recurrence 75% vs no recurrence 42.9%, p = 0.015), enterocele (recurrence 43.8% vs no recurrence 16.7%, p = 0.018), and the mean cross-sectional area of the psoas muscle (recurrence median 12.3 cm2 vs no recurrence median 14.3 cm2, p = 0.04) were significantly associated with recurrence.LIMITATIONS:This study is limited by its retrospective nature and would benefit from multicenter collaboration to improve statistical power.CONCLUSIONS:This study is the first to integrate MRI findings with clinical factors in evaluating recurrence risk. Some measures of low muscle mass (sarcopenia), an enterocele or cul-de-sac hernia, and diabetes mellitus may be associated with recurrence. Diabetic control and sarcopenia are potentially modifiable through lifestyle interventions, highlighting opportunities for preoperative optimization. Larger-scale studies are warranted to validate these predictors and guide risk-stratified management. Rectal prolapse exists on a spectrum of severity, from internal to full-thickness external prolapse. Surgical repair can be performed via abdominal and perineal approaches. However, recurrence remains a major limitation across all techniques.1 Identifying predictors of recurrence is critical to guide decision-making and inform targeted interventions.

Several studies have evaluated demographic, clinical factors, and surgical factors as predictors of recurrence after repair, but findings have been inconsistent and the variables assessed have been limited. Some examples of risk modifiers include patient factors such as connective tissue disease,2 BMI,3 prior rectal prolapse repair,4 symptom duration,5 and length of the prolapsed rectum.6 Surgical factors implicated include use of synthetic prosthesis,7 circumferential rectal mobilization,8 and concomitant middle compartment suspension.9

Previous studies examining imaging-based predictors of rectopexy outcomes are scarce and have predominantly relied on defecating proctography.10 Defecating proctography exposes patients to ionizing radiation, visualizes only the luminal aspect of the rectum, and offers limited information about relationships to surrounding structures. In contrast, magnetic resonance (MR) defecography involves no ionizing radiation and provides excellent assessment of dynamic pelvic anatomy due to high-contrast resolution. Anorectal manometry with balloon expulsion testing is another common test that provides further assessment of anorectal function, sensation, and pressures during rest, squeeze, and simulated defecation. Despite this, no studies have evaluated the role that these modern modalities play in predicting outcomes after rectal prolapse surgery.

The aim of this study was to evaluate whether preoperative MR defecography, anorectal manometry, or balloon expulsion testing could identify biomarkers associated with recurrence in patients undergoing rectopexy for rectal prolapse.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical Approval

The study was approved by the Stanford Institutional Review Committee (approval number IRB 46691). All patients involved in the study gave written informed consent.

Inclusion Criteria

Adult male and female patients were recruited prospectively into our rectal prolapse registry. Data were collected by a trained staff member using standardized forms and recorded securely in a REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) registry. All patients who underwent an abdominal procedure (robotic ventral, suture, or resection rectopexy) for rectal prolapse between 2017 and 2025 and had preoperative imaging with MRI defecography or anorectal manometry were included in the study.

Data Captured

This study aimed to capture a comprehensive profile of clinical, technical, and imaging findings that could be associated with prolapse recurrence.

Demographics, medical and surgical history, examination findings, and disease characteristics were recorded. Symptom severity scores were measured using validated tools: The Cleveland Clinic Fecal Incontinence Score (CCFIS)11 and the Obstructive Defecation Syndrome (ODS) score.12 These scores were measured preoperatively and at postoperative visits. Operative details and postoperative measures, including length of stay, complications, and recurrence, were also recorded. Recurrence was defined as full-thickness rectal prolapse recurrence, identified clinically or on imaging.

MR defecography with rectal contrast was performed on a 3-Tesla MRI system (various vendors: GE, Siemens, and Philips) using a phased array torso coil with the patient in the supine position.13 No vaginal contrast was used. Image sequences consisted of a 3-plane localizer, sagittal T2 single-shot fast spin-echo (SSFSE) at rest, followed by dynamic sagittal fast imaging employing steady-state acquisition (FIESTA) with Kegel, then 2 dynamic sagittal FIESTA sequences, each 2 minutes long with 1 frame per second. If the patient was unable to evacuate in either of the 2 attempts, they were coached again and a third series was acquired. After evacuation attempts, additional Cor T2 SSFSE with Valsalva and Ax T1 3-dimensional SPGR (spoiled gradient echo) with Dixon fat-water separation were acquired. The MR defecography was reviewed and data were collected by a specialist GI radiologist and a specialist surgeon. Collection of imaging data points was as per recent consensus definitions endorsed by the Pelvic Floor Disorders Consortium14 and included additional measures. In total, 24 different MR defecography variables were assessed, including the grade of rectal intussusception by the Oxford classification; rectocele size and emptying; movement of the anorectal junction, bladder and vagina as compared with pubococcygeal line; rectal axis (angle between the vertical and center of the rectum); H-line; M-line; estimated levator ani subtended volume (eLASV),15 and muscle measures. Muscle measures included the puborectalis muscle thickness (measured in the axial plane at the level of the pubic symphysis, where the muscle was thickest anterior to the rectum), psoas muscle index (PMI) at the L4 level as described previously,13 and the cross-sectional area and fat fraction of the psoas muscle. Sarcopenia was defined by PMI at L4 <5.2 cm2/m2.16 The presence and type of cul-de-sac hernia were also documented. A cul-de-sac hernia is a type of pelvic organ prolapse, where the peritoneal cul-de-sac herniates into the rectovaginal space. Types are named depending on contents: in a peritoneocele, the peritoneal herniation contains fat only; in an enterocele, it contains small bowel; and in a sigmoidocele, it contains sigmoid colon. High-resolution 2-dimensional anorectal manometry with balloon expulsion testing was performed using a solid-state catheter (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) in the left lateral position as per published international standards.17 Commercially available software (Manoview AR version 3.0; Medtronic) was used for manometry interpretation. The balloon expulsion test was performed using a 50 mL rectal balloon in the seated position. Normal balloon expulsion time was considered <60 seconds.

MR defecography, anorectal manometry, and balloon expulsion testing results were only compared in female patients due to known anatomical differences between the male and female pelvises.14,18

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0 (IBM Corporation). The normality of the data was evaluated using a combination of graphical methods and Shapiro-Wilk testing. Descriptive baseline statistics were tabulated as appropriate. For statistics relevant to female patients (ie, hysterectomy, obstetric history), the data are displayed as percentages of female patients only. Clinical, technical, MR defecography, anorectal manometry, and balloon expulsion testing findings were compared between groups with and without recurrence. Categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage and were compared using χ2 tests and Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Pre- and postoperative CCFIS and ODS scores were compared using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. To compare outcome measure variables, for variables conforming to a normal distribution, a Welch’s t test was conducted. For variables not conforming to a normal distribution, a Mann-Whitney U test was conducted.

An additional univariable logistic regression analysis was also conducted to assess the effect of variables on prolapse recurrence.

A 2-tailed p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Demographics

A total of 155 patients (n = 145, 93.5% women) underwent an abdominal rectopexy during the study period. All procedures were commenced robotically, with one procedure converted to open (0.6%). Ventral rectopexy was the most common procedure performed (75%), with the majority of patients receiving a biologic prosthesis (n = 114; 91.9%). Combined gynecologic procedures were performed in 43.2% of cases, including sacrocolpopexy (16.6%), uterosacral suspension (16.6%), retropubic sling (9.0%), cystocele repair (8.3%), salpingectomy (6.9%), hysterectomy (6.2%), and rectocele repair (4.1% posterior colporrhaphy, 2.1% robotic).

All patients underwent surgery for Oxford grade 4 or 5 rectal prolapse, except for 1 patient with grade 3 intussusception and severe fecal incontinence. In 58 (43.3%) of MR defecography studies, the full extent of the prolapse was underestimated compared to clinical examination in the office or under anesthesia, likely due to differences in positioning or patients not evacuating optimally during imaging.19 Clinical characteristics and operative details are described in Tables 1 and 2.

Outcomes

The median length of stay was 1 (interquartile range [IQR], 1–2) day. Overall, 36 patients (23.4%) experienced complications within 30 days of surgery. The majority of these complications were minor (Clavien-Dindo grade 1: n = 12 [7.7%]; grade 2: n = 22 [14.2%]). There were 2 Clavien-Dindo grade 3 complications (1.3%); 1 of these involved a return to the operating room for a postoperative small-bowel obstruction, and the other involved a port-site hernia.

During an overall follow-up of median 1295 days (IQR, 713–1987), 16 patients (10.3%) experience full-thickness recurrence of their rectal prolapse. The time to prolapse recurrence was a median of 423 days (IQR, 159.3–1410.0).

Of 111 patients with both pre- and postoperative symptom severity scores available, there was a statistically significant improvement in the ODS score and CCFIS at both the first and last postoperative follow-up compared to the preoperative score (ODS score preoperative 9 [IQR, 5–12], first follow-up 6 [IQR, 4–10], last follow-up 6 [IQR, 4–9], p < 0.001 for both comparisons; CCFIS preoperative 13 [IQR, 6–17], first follow-up 8.5 [IQR, 2–13] and last follow-up 8.5 [IQR, 3–12], p < 0.001 for both comparisons). Minimally important differences have been previously described as 3 points for CCFIS and 2 points for ODS, indicating clinically meaningful improvement.20,21 At first and last postoperative follow-up, 46.4% (n = 51) and 51.8% (n = 57) of patients demonstrated an improvement in their CCFIS and 50.5% (n = 56) and 48.6% (n = 54) demonstrated an improvement in their ODS, respectively. The first postoperative follow-up score occurred at a median of 6 months postoperatively (IQR, 6–12), whereas the last postoperative follow-up score occurred at a median of 36 months (IQR, 12–60).

Associations With Recurrence

Clinical factors

Only diabetes mellitus was significantly associated with recurrence. The hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level closest to surgery (excluding measures > 12 mo from surgery) was not significantly associated with recurrence (no recurrence mean 6.6 ± 0.8% [n = 8], recurrence mean 5.8 ± 0.6%, p = 0.174 [n = 3]).

An increased estimated intraoperative blood loss was also statistically significant; however, the volume was not clinically significant. The type of procedure and operative steps used, prosthesis type, size of rectal prolapse, and previous prolapse surgery were not significantly associated with recurrence. There was a trend toward patients with previous pelvic floor surgeries having higher recurrence rates, but this did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.07; Table 1).

MRI parameters

On MR defecography (Table 3), the presence of any cul-de-sac hernia (no recurrence n = 54 [42.9%], recurrence n = 12 [75%]), specifically an enterocele (no recurrence n = 21 [16.3%], recurrence n = 7 [43.8%], p = 0.016), and the mean cross-sectional area of the psoas muscle (no recurrence median 14.3 [IQR, 11.8–15.8] cm2, recurrence median 12.3 [IQR, 10.5–13.7] cm2, p = 0.04) were significantly different in patients with or without recurrence. There was a trend toward a decreased PMI (no recurrence median 5.4 [IQR, 4.3–6.4] cm2/m2, recurrence median 4.4 [IQR, 3.9–5.3] cm2/m2, p = 0.06) and increasing psoas muscle fat fraction (no recurrence median 3.6% [IQR, 2.3–5.9], recurrence median 5.2% [IQR, 3.0–9.6], p = 0.10) in patients with recurrence that did not reach statistical significance. There was also a trend toward the recurrence group having a higher absolute prevalence of sarcopenia as defined by PMI <5.2 cm2/m2 at L4 (16; no recurrence n = 24; 42.9%; recurrence n = 10, 71.4%, p = 0.06).

There were no significant differences in preoperative anorectal manometry or balloon expulsion testing parameters in patients with or without recurrence (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Imaging-based predictors of recurrence after rectal prolapse surgery have been underexplored. This study is the first to integrate MRI findings with clinical factors to comprehensively evaluate associations with recurrence. Our results suggest that measures of low muscle mass (sarcopenia), the presence of a cul-de-sac hernia (specifically an enterocele) on MR defecography, and diabetes mellitus may significantly influence recurrence risk.

Diabetes mellitus was the only clinical variable significantly associated with recurrence. Existing studies on rectal prolapse recurrence generally do not include diabetes in their analysis.1,7 One small retrospective study of 55 patients reported on a trend toward higher recurrence after rectal prolapse surgery in patients with diabetes.22 Evidence from other pelvic floor conditions supports a potential role for diabetes in condition pathophysiology and recurrence. For example, a national cohort study identified an association between increasing HbA1c and the presence of fecal incontinence,23 and diabetes has also been associated with prolapse recurrence after sacrocolpopexy.24,25 Hyperglycemia impairs wound healing and may compromise tissue remodeling and scar integrity.26 Diabetes also contributes to microangiopathy and autonomic and peripheral sensorimotor neuropathy, which may lead to pelvic floor neuropathy23 and potentially predispose a patient to recurrence. HbA1c was not routinely recorded in our registry, and retrospective review of perioperative levels was limited by sample size. Future larger-scale studies could specifically evaluate perioperative diabetic control (including HbA1c) and assess whether preoperative optimization could influence recurrence rates.

Another finding significantly associated with recurrence was the presence of a cul-de-sac hernia, specifically an enterocele. Prior publications on the significance of enterocele have yielded mixed results. One study found a greater percentage resolution of obstructed defecation and fecal incontinence symptoms after laparoscopic ventral rectopexy in patients with enterocele; however, recurrence was not assessed.10 In contrast, another study identified enterocele as a risk factor for worse symptomatic outcomes after stapled transanal rectal resection for obstructed defecation, with a nonsignificant trend toward recurrent rectal intussusception.27 We propose that an enterocele may predispose to recurrence through a number of mechanisms. An enterocele indicates existing poor pelvic floor supports.28 Postoperatively, a tendency for the small bowel to reside dependently in the pelvis could strain repair integrity through cyclical downward pelvic pressure.29 Our results suggest that cul-de-sac hernia (particularly enterocele) merits further evaluation as a potential contributor to recurrence, and future studies could consider incorporating it into standardized preoperative assessment.

Sarcopenia is a progressive and generalized disorder of skeletal muscle, associated with low muscle strength, quantity, or quality and low physical performance.30 Sarcopenia is associated with a multitude of adverse surgical outcomes, including complications, prolonged hospitalization, and mortality.31 Sarcopenia has also been associated with pelvic floor dysfunction, including urinary incontinence,32 reduced anal sphincter pressures,33 worsening severity of pelvic organ prolapse,34 and rectal prolapse.13 In our cohort, recurrence was associated with a significantly lower mean psoas cross-sectional area (a biomarker of muscle mass). Nonsignificant trends were also seen toward lower PMI, higher psoas muscle fat fraction (a marker of reduced muscle quality), and absolute prevalence of sarcopenia, with the lack of statistical significance likely influenced by sample size. Concerningly, the prevalence of sarcopenia was high (48.6% overall, 71.4% in patients with recurrence). This has practical implications—sarcopenia identified preoperatively could potentially be modified through multidisciplinary interventions combining exercise and nutritional optimization.35

The puborectalis thickness and the estimated levator ani subtended volume (eLASV—a surrogate measure of levator ani laxity)36 were not significantly associated with recurrence. This contrasts with Wyman et al,37 who reported that eLASV >38.5 cm3 on preoperative pelvic MRI predicted surgical failure after uterosacral ligament suspension for pelvic organ prolapse. Our results suggest that generalized sarcopenia, rather than pelvic floor-specific metrics, may be more relevant to recurrence in the rectal prolapse cohort.

Finally, several papers have previously evaluated recurrence risk on the basis of technical factors.7,8 In our cohort, no operative variables were associated with recurrence. This finding suggests that at least within our series, patient biology may be more important in determining recurrence than surgical technique. Our preference is to tailor the technique to the clinical context, anatomy, and specifics of the prolapse.

This study has several limitations. This was a single-center study, and like most rectal prolapse publications, it would benefit from multicenter collaboration. Due to our low recurrence rate, we were unable to perform meaningful multivariable analysis. The smaller sample size for the anorectal manometry and balloon expulsion testing metrics may have also resulted in nonsignificance. Furthermore, due to very few male patients and known sex-related anatomical differences, we analyzed imaging parameters only in female patients.14,18 Further research is needed to clarify whether imaging findings translate to the male population.

Despite these limitations, this study is the first to assess MR defecography, anorectal manometry, and balloon expulsion testing in relation to recurrence after rectopexy. This study adds to the growing evidence base and suggests that MR defecography may be beneficial to preoperative recurrence risk assessment. Importantly, both sarcopenia and diabetic control are amenable to prehabilitation, including exercise, nutrition, and glycemic optimization. Prehabilitation is well established in the surgical literature, with multimodal interventions associated with improved outcomes.38 Prehabilitation in rectal prolapse patients to reduce recurrence has not previously been evaluated. Future multicenter collaborations with larger cohorts will be needed to validate our findings and define practical strategies for risk reduction.

These findings also represent an opportunity to revisit current guidelines regarding imaging templates for rectal prolapse14 and factors to report in rectal prolapse outcomes research.39 In the recently published core descriptor sets for rectal prolapse outcomes, clinicians voted not to include preoperative dynamic imaging findings.39 Diabetes and sarcopenia were not included as important comorbidities. Although measures of sarcopenia are not routinely recorded at most institutions, we would advocate that these measures are relevant to patient outcomes. Currently, recording measures of sarcopenia is labor intensive. Automated methods to measure psoas muscle volume have been developed for CT40 but not for MRI, representing a target for future research.

CONCLUSIONS

Recurrence after rectopexy appears multifactorial. Our findings suggest that some measures of muscle mass (sarcopenia), cul-de-sac hernias (particularly enterocele), and diabetes mellitus may be significantly associated with recurrence. Diabetic control and sarcopenia are potentially modifiable through lifestyle interventions, offering new opportunities for risk stratification and perioperative optimization. Larger-scale studies are warranted to validate these predictors and guide individualized management strategies.
