Deconstructing Superintelligence: Identity, Self-Modification and Différance
Elija Perrier

Abstract
Self-modification is often taken as constitutive of artificial superintelligence ( SI ), yet modification is a relative action requiring a supplement outside the operation. When self-modification extends to this supplement, the classical self-referential structure collapses. We formalise this on an associative operator algebra 𝒜 \mathcal{A} with update U ^ \hat{U} , discrimination D ^ \hat{D} , and self-representation R ^ \hat{R} , identifying the supplement with Comm ⁡ ( U ^ ) \operatorname{Comm}(\hat{U}) ; an expansion theorem shows [ U ^ , R ^ ] [\hat{U},\hat{R}] decomposes through [ U ^ , D ^ ] [\hat{U},\hat{D}] , so non-commutation generically propagates. The liar paradox appears as a commutator collapse [ T ^ , Π L ] = 0 [\hat{T},\Pi_{L}]=0 , and class 𝐀 \mathbf{A} self-modification realises the same collapse at system scale, yielding a structure coinciding with Priest’s inclosure schema and Derrida’s différance . 1 1 1 Under review



1
Introduction

The ability to self-modify is a hallmark of many theories of artificial general intelligence (
AGI
) and artificial superintelligence (
ASI
). Typically, self-modification is framed as a superlative quality of these theories: by being able to modify not just typical actions but the underlying agentic architecture—physical, cognitive, procedural—that instantiates AGI/ASI, such systems are argued to possess greater adaptivity and, as a result, greater capacity to optimise, whether for decision-making, survival, planning, or the shaping of their environment itself. In this framing, self-modification is not a mere property of ASI but rather, when stipulated, a
necessary
feature: essential to
what
it
is
. Intuitively, the framing is understandable, even defensible. If intelligence involves, at its centre, the ability to act in adaptive ways—including reasoning and planning—then a cursory look at the world shows that the entities we identify as intelligent, whether characterised by reasoning, tool use, planning, or cognitive behaviour, all tend to exhibit heightened degrees of self-modification. In humans this takes the form of thought and language, which in turn manifests in our ability to modify, more than any other species, the world around us.

However, in all cases of self-modification, exactly
what
is being causally modified is constrained. Humans reason and have a sense of autonomy, yet cannot will control over most of their bodies’ activities. This reflects an architectural—and, as we will argue, necessary—feature: some part of the system, the part doing the modifying, must at least for the duration of change remain unmodified, functioning as a
supplement
in the Derridean sense—a structure outside the present operation whose stability is what makes the operation possible at all. Modification is a relative, causal action by one thing upon another; even the purported self-modification of a unified multiplicity, such as a human, is more precisely the action of some part of that multiplicity upon another, and every such action affects the identity of what is modified by construction. This much is uncontroversial. What is not is the behaviour of the supplement structure when the scope of modification becomes broad enough that the supplement is itself inside the action of the modifying operation. Contemporary AGI/ASI theories propose self-modification of an extent, scope, and speed that far surpasses anything present in organic life: modification not only of policies or memories but of evaluators, validators, self-models, and the very individuation tests by which a system is recognised as itself. An important open question is therefore structural: when self-modification extends to the apparatus that individuates the system itself, what structure—if any—secures identity across time, and how is it expressed formally?

Strong self-modification changes the situation, because a system that can edit its evaluators, validators, self-models, and individuation tests begins to act on the machinery by which its later states would be recognised as continuations of its earlier ones. Our central claim is that, once the supplement enters the action of the modifying operator, identity is no longer secured by any unconditional unifying structure but only

Identity puzzles are not themselves new. Classical puzzles about ordinary objects

What is new in SI is that this presupposition is no longer available. Strong self-modification places the discrimination apparatus, the evaluation criterion, and the self-representation machinery all within the action space of the very operator whose modifications they would adjudicate. In Theseus terms, the criterion by which replacement is judged may itself become replaceable.

3
Operator Formalism and Supplementation

To explore these issues, we first formalise self-modification as an operator acting on a vector space of system-states, with modification, discrimination, and self-representation all members of an associative algebra acting on this vector space. The commutator structure of the algebra makes the supplement explicit: a
supplement
for a modification operator is any element of its commutant, and identity-preserving structures under modification are projectors in the commutant. We conjecture that the classical self-reference tradition—Kleene, Gödel, von Neumann—corresponds to the case where a unifying projector exists in the commutant; class
\mathbf{A}
as we define it in Section
4
to the case where it does not.

3.1
States, operators, and the action algebra

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a vector space over a field
k\subseteq\mathbb{R}
; no completion, topology, norm, or inner product is assumed. Elements of
\mathcal{V}
are
configurations
: finite formal
k
-linear combinations of admissible system-states. A single admissible state is a basis vector
|s\rangle\in\mathcal{V}
; a configuration
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}|s_{i}\rangle
represents a system in parallel superposition over the
|s_{i}\rangle
with weights
\lambda_{i}
, as arises in mixture-of-experts and ensemble architectures
[
11
,
23
]
. No positivity, normalisation, or unitarity is imposed: the superposition is classical (an ensemble over configurations) rather than quantum.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\operatorname{End}_{k}(\mathcal{V})
be the associative algebra of
k
-linear maps
\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
, with composition as multiplication and identity
\mathbf{1}
. An
action triple
fixes the three operators by which a self-modifying system acts on its own states: what modifies, what distinguishes, what represents.

Definition 1
(Action triple)

An
action triple
on
\mathcal{V}
is a triple
(\hat{U},\hat{D},\hat{R})\in\mathcal{A}^{3}
: the
update

\hat{U}
, the
discrimination

\hat{D}
, and the
self-representation

\hat{R}
. Depending on architecture, these operators need not all be distinct nor invertible.

A
unifying projector
is an idempotent
\Pi\in\mathcal{A}
(
\Pi^{2}=\Pi
) whose image
\Pi\mathcal{V}
carries a specified property of the system—behavioural signature, causal provenance, substrate integrity, or any other feature taken as constitutive of identity.
\Pi
is
preserved
by an operator
\hat{X}
if
[\hat{X},\Pi]=0
:
\hat{X}
maps
\Pi\mathcal{V}
and
\ker\Pi
into themselves, preserving the decomposition
\mathcal{V}=\Pi\mathcal{V}\oplus\ker\Pi
. The act of unification is central to persistent identity. Intuitively, identity for a composite system is precisely the unification of a multiplicity into a stable whole: the projector
\Pi
selects which components count together as the same entity, and its preservation ensures that this grouping of parts into a single object is maintained across the system’s transformations.

Several architectures in the AGI literature correspond to the case where such a
\Pi
sits in the commutant of the update by construction. The Gödel machine
[
49
]
fixes its proof-of-benefit background; DGM-style systems
[
66
]
fix their benchmark base; reflective-loop agents
[
51
,
35
]
freeze their substrate. Research has observed
[
56
]
that any actual Gödel-machine implementation must choose between a recursive loop of evaluators (the supplement perpetually deferred) or declaring the supplement fixed by fiat. Bennett
[
1
,
2
]
reaches the same structural point from a different direction: the dependency of claimed-to-be-intelligent software on its interpreter—what is there called
computational dualism
—is, in our vocabulary, a unifying supplement external to
\hat{U}
. Continuing on with our formalism, what matters for our purposes is that the supplement is precisely the part of the system that the update does not change. The collection of all such invariant elements is what we formalise using the algebraic idea of the commutant.

3.2
The supplement as commutant

For any
\hat{X}\in\mathcal{A}
, the
commutant
of
\hat{X}
in
\mathcal{A}
is

\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{X})=\{Y\in\mathcal{A}:[\hat{X},Y]=0\}.

\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{X})
is a unital subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
: it contains
\mathbf{1}
, is closed under sums, scalar multiples, and products, and is closed under any polynomial in
\hat{X}
itself. We then define the supplement in these terms.

Definition 2
(Supplement)

A
supplement
for
\hat{X}\in\mathcal{A}
is an element of
\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{X})
. A
unifying supplement
is a non-trivial unifying projector
\Pi\in\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{X})
,
\Pi\neq\mathbf{0}
,
\Pi\neq\mathbf{1}
.

The philosophical reading is that the supplement is not merely a structure outside
\hat{X}
that
\hat{X}
cannot disturb; it is what makes
\hat{X}
’s action identifiable as an operation on anything at all. In Derridean terms, this is akin to the supplement of the copula
[
14
]
: the grammatical operation that binds subject to predicate is neither internal to what it joins nor simply external to it, but the condition under which the joining is intelligible at all. An operator without any supplement has no individuated object: the decomposition
\mathcal{V}=\Pi\mathcal{V}\oplus\ker\Pi
picks out the invariant structure
\hat{X}
preserves, and without such a decomposition there is nothing to say
\hat{X}
acts
on
rather than
through
. Derrida’s idea that the supplement is at once exterior and constitutive is captured by the formalism:
\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{X})
lies outside
\hat{X}
’s adjoint action and yet supplies the invariant frames without which
\hat{X}
’s operation is not individuated. Classical self-reference constructions rely on unifying supplements whose existence is underwritten by an execution environment external to
\hat{X}
itself. We can now specify the relevant algebra we use further on in our theorem.

3.3
Commutator algebra and the propagation theorem

The associative algebra
\mathcal{A}
carries a Lie bracket
[X,Y]=XY-YX
satisfying the Jacobi identity

[X,[Y,Z]]+[Y,[Z,X]]+[Z,[X,Y]]=0,

and for each
\hat{X}\in\mathcal{A}
the adjoint action

\operatorname{ad}_{\hat{X}}(Y):=[\hat{X},Y]

is a derivation on the associative product:

\operatorname{ad}_{\hat{X}}(YZ)=\operatorname{ad}_{\hat{X}}(Y)Z+Y\operatorname{ad}_{\hat{X}}(Z).

The derivation property
(\ast)
is the syntactic engine of the analysis. It says that whenever an operator is composed of ingredients, the adjoint action of
\hat{U}
on the composite is determined by its action on the ingredients through a Leibniz-style rule, whether or not any particular ingredient commutes with
\hat{U}
.

In Derridean terms,
\hat{D}
is the operator of difference: it supplies the contrasts through which features are individuated.
\hat{U}
is the operator of deferral: it carries the system into a later context. The central question is whether difference and deferral commute. We now come to our main theorem.

Theorem 3.1
(Expansion and generic propagation of non-commutation)

Let
(\hat{U},\hat{D},\hat{R})\in\mathcal{A}^{3}
and suppose
\hat{R}
factors through
\hat{D}
: that is,
\hat{R}=F(\hat{D})
for some composite
F
built by finite sums, scalar multiples, and products from
\hat{D}
and operators in
\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{U})
. Then

[\hat{U},\hat{R}]=\sum_{j}A_{j}[\hat{U},\hat{D}]B_{j}

for some
A_{j},B_{j}\in\mathcal{A}
determined by the chosen factorisation of
F
.

Consequently, if
[\hat{U},\hat{D}]\neq 0
, non-commutation propagates from
\hat{D}
to
\hat{R}
whenever this finite sum does not vanish by cancellation or annihilation. In particular,
[\hat{U},\hat{R}]\neq 0
for
\hat{R}=\hat{D}
. More generally, for nonconstant composites
F
, vanishing of
[\hat{U},\hat{R}]
requires special algebraic relations among
F
,
\hat{D}
, and
[\hat{U},\hat{D}]
.

Proof

Write
\hat{R}=F(\hat{D})
as a finite sum of terms, each of the form

c\cdot X_{1}\hat{D}^{n_{1}}X_{2}\hat{D}^{n_{2}}\cdots X_{m}\hat{D}^{n_{m}}X_{m+1}

with
c\in k
,
X_{i}\in\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{U})
, and
n_{i}\geq 0
. Applying
\operatorname{ad}_{\hat{U}}
,
k
-linearity gives a sum over terms, and
(\ast)
applied iteratively distributes
\operatorname{ad}_{\hat{U}}
over each product. Since
\operatorname{ad}_{\hat{U}}(X_{i})=0
and

\operatorname{ad}_{\hat{U}}(\hat{D}^{n})=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\hat{D}^{j}[\hat{U},\hat{D}]\hat{D}^{n-1-j}

by repeated use of
(\ast)
, each term of
\operatorname{ad}_{\hat{U}}(\hat{R})
collects into a
k
-linear combination of expressions of the form
A_{j}[\hat{U},\hat{D}]B_{j}
with
A_{j},B_{j}\in\mathcal{A}
. Thus
[\hat{U},\hat{R}]
is a finite sum of terms each containing
[\hat{U},\hat{D}]
as a factor. The expression therefore vanishes only when the factorisation of
F
introduces cancellation or annihilation among these terms. ∎

Theorem
3.1
formalises the intuition that when
\hat{U}
modifies the discrimination apparatus
\hat{D}
, any self-representation
\hat{R}
built from
\hat{D}
inherits the corresponding commutator term unless the chosen factorisation cancels or annihilates it. The derivation property ensures the failure is propagated, not localised. Cohesion of
\hat{R}
with
\hat{U}
—the condition
[\hat{U},\hat{R}]=0
, under which self-representation is invariant under update—requires either
[\hat{U},\hat{D}]=0
(so
\hat{U}
does not touch the apparatus) or a special cancellation relation specific to the factorisation
F
. The first is exactly the exit from class
\mathbf{A}
we later identify; the second is structurally exceptional in the algebraic sense just specified. The non-commutation of
\hat{U}
and
\hat{D}
is therefore the formal failure of differential individuation to remain stable under deferral. This shifts the problem from propagation to constraint: if non-commutation is present, under what algebraic conditions can a binding projector still be preserved by both
\hat{U}
and
\hat{D}
? To address this we consider the role of the Jacobi identity below.

3.4
Jacobi and unifying supplements

Applied to an update operator
\hat{U}
, a discrimination operator
\hat{D}
, and a candidate unifying projector
\Pi
, the Jacobi identity gives

[\hat{U},[\hat{D},\Pi]]=[[\hat{U},\hat{D}],\Pi]+[\hat{D},[\hat{U},\Pi]].

Applied to a candidate unifying projector, the identity constrains which projectors can be simultaneously preserved by
\hat{U}
and
\hat{D}
.

Corollary 1
(Restriction on unifying supplements under generic update)

Let
\hat{U},\hat{D}\in\mathcal{A}
satisfy
[\hat{U},\hat{D}]\neq 0
. A non-trivial unifying projector
\Pi
satisfies
[\hat{U},\Pi]=[\hat{D},\Pi]=0
simultaneously only if
[[\hat{U},\hat{D}],\Pi]=0
. In particular,
\Pi
must lie in the commutant of
[\hat{U},\hat{D}]
, a proper subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}
whenever
[\hat{U},\hat{D}]
is non-central.

Proof

If
[\hat{U},\Pi]=[\hat{D},\Pi]=0
, the right-hand side of the Jacobi identity reduces to
[[\hat{U},\hat{D}],\Pi]
, and the left-hand side is
[\hat{U},0]=0
. Hence
[[\hat{U},\hat{D}],\Pi]=0
. ∎

Intuitively, the corollary says that a structure that remains stable under both update and discrimination must also remain stable under their interaction. If
\hat{U}
and
\hat{D}
fail to commute, then their joint effect introduces an additional transformation, represented by
[\hat{U},\hat{D}]
, and any projector that is preserved by both operations individually must also be insensitive to this combined effect. This imposes a further restriction: only those projectors that lie in the commutant of
[\hat{U},\hat{D}]
can serve as unifying structures. When the commutator is non-central, this excludes most candidate projectors, so a stable unifying structure becomes difficult to maintain.
A unifying projector preserved by both update and discrimination must additionally commute with
[\hat{U},\hat{D}]
. When the commutator is non-central, this restricts admissible projectors to the proper subalgebra
\operatorname{Comm}([\hat{U},\hat{D}])
. Thus unconditional unifying is not ruled out by Jacobi alone, but is available only under an additional commutation constraint that a generic proposed identity projector will fail.

3.5
Supplemental identity, formally

We can now state our supplemental identity formally. An
unconditional unifying
of identity for
(\hat{U},\hat{D},\hat{R})
would be a projector
\Pi
satisfying
[\hat{U},\Pi]=[\hat{D},\Pi]=[\hat{R},\Pi]=0
: preserved by every operation the system performs on its states. A
supplemental unifying
is one where only some of these commute, typically those treated by an external frame as constitutive.

Definition 3
(Supplemental identity)

The triple
(\hat{U},\hat{D},\hat{R})
admits
unconditional identity
if there exists a non-trivial
\Pi\in\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{U})\cap\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{D})\cap\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{R})
. Otherwise its identity is
supplemental
: the system coheres only under a frame treating some subset of
\{\hat{U},\hat{D},\hat{R}\}
as unifying, with the remaining operators violating the unifying.

Supplemental identity is the direct formal analogy of Derrida’s claim that identity is secured only by reference to a supplement whose fixity is itself deferred. The supplement is the projector
\Pi
; its fixity is
[\hat{X},\Pi]=0
for the privileged subset of operators; the deferral is that the operators outside the privileged subset do not preserve
\Pi
, so the choice of which subset is privileged is itself a frame-dependent commitment the system cannot validate from within. This abstract dependence on a stabilising supplement becomes most transparent in simple cases of self-reference, where the distinction between an operation and what it acts upon must be held in place for the construction to make sense. The liar paradox provides the minimal setting in which to see what happens when this distinction is not sustained.

3.6
Worked example: the liar as commutator collapse

Apparent paradoxes of self-reference are collapses of a commutator the classical construction held non-zero. Consider the liar sentence

L:\quad\text{``this sentence is false''.}

The demonstrative
this
functions as a
sign operator

\hat{T}\in\mathcal{A}
returning the referent of the demonstrative; the predicate “is false” is a second operator
\hat{F}\in\mathcal{A}
. The sentence itself is a configuration
|L\rangle\in\mathcal{V}
with rank-one projector
\Pi_{L}
onto
k\cdot|L\rangle
, and its intended content is that
L
asserts the falsity of whatever
\hat{T}
picks out.

The classical sign/signified distinction requires that the sign
\hat{T}
and the configuration
|L\rangle
it signifies be architecturally distinct: the act of reference is not the referent. Algebraically, this is represented by the non-collapse condition
[\hat{T},\Pi_{L}]\neq 0
. A
linguistic supplement
—conventions of reference, speaker context, situational embedding—in
\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{T})\cap\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{F})
sustains the distinction by fixing the demonstrative’s semantics without itself being the sentence the demonstrative points at. The liar is the local inclosure instance: the signification operation generates, from within the totality of truth-evaluable sentences, an element whose evaluation exceeds the totalisation it invokes.

Proposition 1
(Liar as commutator collapse)

Let
\hat{T},\hat{F}\in\mathcal{A}
and let
\Pi_{L}
be a rank-one projector onto
k\cdot|L\rangle
, where
|L\rangle
is interpreted as asserting the falsity of
\hat{T}|L\rangle
. If
[\hat{T},\Pi_{L}]=0
, then
\hat{T}|L\rangle=\lambda|L\rangle
for some
\lambda\in k
. Under the intended demonstrative reading,
\lambda=1
, so the sign and the signified collapse:
\hat{T}|L\rangle=|L\rangle
. If the ambient semantics is classical and treats
\hat{F}
as falsity, this collapse yields the usual liar equation
v(L)=\neg v(L)
, which has no two-valued solution. Classical self-reference avoids this collapse when a linguistic supplement sustains
[\hat{T},\Pi_{L}]\neq 0
.

Proof

Since
\Pi_{L}
is rank-one,
\Pi_{L}\mathcal{V}=k\cdot|L\rangle
. If
[\hat{T},\Pi_{L}]=0
, then
\hat{T}(\Pi_{L}\mathcal{V})\subseteq\Pi_{L}\mathcal{V}
, hence
\hat{T}|L\rangle=\lambda|L\rangle
for some
\lambda\in k
. The intended demonstrative reading sets
\lambda=1
, giving
\hat{T}|L\rangle=|L\rangle
. Substituting this into the classical interpretation of “
L
asserts the falsity of
\hat{T}|L\rangle
” yields
v(L)=\neg v(L)
, which has no two-valued solution. Thus the commutator collapse is the algebraic condition under which the classical liar contradiction is recovered. ∎

Here
[\hat{T},\Pi_{L}]=0
is the collapse of the token/tokened distinction, and classical inconsistency follows only once that collapse is read through a classical falsity semantics. Avoidance of paradox is the inequality
[\hat{T},\Pi_{L}]\neq 0
sustained by a supplement in the commutant of the tokening operator. The reading is consistent with Priest’s inclosure treatment of the liar
[
43
,
44
]
and with Kripke’s fixed-point analysis
[
29
]
, each of which identifies an external structure whose removal produces the paradox. Section
4
shows that class
\mathbf{A}
self-modification executes the same commutator pattern at a different scale.

4
Class
\mathbf{A}
: The Liar at System Scale

We now extend the operator formalism to a class of systems whose architecture corresponds to contemporary AGI designs, and show that class
\mathbf{A}
self-modification is the liar’s commutator collapse at the scale of a whole system. A
computational system
is a triple
(\mathcal{S},U,R)
comprising a state space
\mathcal{S}
, an update
U:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}
, and a self-representation
R:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{M}
; the discrimination apparatus is an operator
D
implicit in the definition of
R
below. We assume
\mathcal{V}
has been enlarged, if necessary, so that self-models form a distinguished subspace
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
and the lifted
\hat{R}
is an endomorphism with image in
\mathcal{M}
. Fixing a basis of
\mathcal{V}
indexed by admissible states,
U,D,R
lift to
\hat{U},\hat{D},\hat{R}\in\mathcal{A}
as in Section
3
.

Definition 4
(Self-modification)

A system
(\mathcal{S},U,R)
is

if
U

U

R
uses, or
R

Class
\mathbf{A}
isolates the architectural commitments that place a strongly self-modifying system in the regime where the propagation theorem applies.

Definition 5
(Class
\mathbf{A}
)

\mathbf{A}
is the class of strongly self-modifying systems
(\mathcal{S},U,R)
for which:

(i)

each admissible state
s
decomposes into
features
(components identifiable by an evaluator) and a
discrimination apparatus
(operational tests by which features are distinguished);

(ii)

U
may alter both features and discrimination apparatus;

(iii)

R
maps each state to a self-model whose content records, for each feature, the operational contrasts by which that feature is identified within the current apparatus.

Condition (iii) requires that the system’s self-model be able to pick out its own features across apparatus changes by the contrasts they support—that the self-representation is
iterable
in the sense Derrida takes to be constitutive of any mark: a feature is a feature only because the self-model can cite it in contexts other than the one in which it first appeared. Architectures encoding features by non-contrast-based means—holistic embeddings whose internal structure does not correspond to any operational test, external-pointer models with conventionally fixed referents—lie outside class
\mathbf{A}
, and no universality claim is made. Concrete instantiations include the DGM
[
66
]
at the level of policies-under-benchmarks, Promptbreeder
[
18
]
at prompts-under-evaluation-prompts, and knowledge-editing systems
[
63
,
10
]
at factual-associations-under-ripple-effects. Reflexion-style agents
[
51
,
35
]
fail (ii) and lie outside the class.

In terms of our earlier formalism, condition (i) supplies the operator
\hat{D}
. Condition (ii) says that the architecture permits updates acting non-trivially on the apparatus. In applying the definition to a particular transition, we consider an
active class
\mathbf{A}
update
: an admissible update
\hat{U}
whose action on the current discrimination apparatus is non-trivial. For such a transition, clause (ii) is represented algebraically by
[\hat{U},\hat{D}]\neq 0
, equivalently
\hat{D}\notin\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{U})
. Condition (iii) states that self-representation proceeds through the contrast apparatus. We assume the lifted contrast construction is algebraic in the sense required by Theorem
3.1
: the self-representation operator factors as
\hat{R}=F(\hat{D})
for a finite composite
F
built from
\hat{D}
and operators in
\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{U})
. Class
\mathbf{A}
thus imposes iterability as an architectural requirement and makes deferral act on the apparatus sustaining it.

The propagation theorem is what iterability encounters under this architecture. Combining clauses: for any active class
\mathbf{A}
transition,
[\hat{U},\hat{R}]\neq 0
outside the exceptional cancellation or annihilation cases specified in Theorem
3.1
, and Corollary
1
restricts any unconditional unifying projector
\Pi\in\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{U})\cap\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{D})\cap\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{R})
to the additional constraint
\Pi\in\operatorname{Comm}([\hat{U},\hat{D}])
. Identity for class
\mathbf{A}
systems is therefore supplemental in the sense of Definition
3
: it can be secured only by fixing a frame that treats some subset of
\{\hat{U},\hat{D},\hat{R}\}
as unifying.
Either the supplement is excluded from the action of
\hat{U}
, in which case the modification is not fully self-directed but depends on an external structure, or it is included in the action of
\hat{U}
, in which case the very structure required to stabilise identity is itself subject to change and cannot remain invariant.
In this sense, the same structure appears as in the liar: stability requires a separation between operation and object that must be held fixed, and contradiction arises when that separation collapses. This is also the pattern captured by Priest’s inclosure schema, where a construction both belongs to and exceeds the totality it presupposes, and, in turn, by Derrida’s
différance
, where any apparent fixity depends on a distinction whose own stability is deferred across successive operations.

4.1
Class
\mathbf{A}
is the liar writ large

The identification with the liar is exact at the level of the commutator pattern: class
\mathbf{A}
systems realise, at system scale, the same collapse of the distinction between operation and object that produces paradox in the liar. Proposition
1
exhibits paradox as the commutator collapse
[\hat{T},\Pi_{L}]=0
: the token operator
\hat{T}
(picking out what the demonstrative “this” refers to) collapses into the projector
\Pi_{L}
(locating the sentence the demonstrative points at). Paradox-avoidance requires a supplement
\Sigma\in\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{T})\cap\operatorname{Comm}(\hat{F})
—the linguistic context—holding the token/tokened commutator open.

In class
\mathbf{A}
the self-model
\hat{R}
plays the role of
\hat{T}
: it tokens the system’s own features. The feature-identity projectors
\Pi_{f}
—picking out what
\hat{R}
’s internal demonstratives refer to—play the role of
\Pi_{L}
. The supplement that would sustain
[\hat{R},\Pi_{f}]\neq 0
for the system’s self-reference is the discrimination apparatus
\hat{D}
: the contrasts by which features are identified fix the semantics of the self-model’s internal reference without being the features themselves, just as linguistic context fixes the semantics of “this” without being the sentence “this” points at.

Strong self-modification places
\hat{D}
inside the action of
\hat{U}
—clause (ii) of Definition
5
is exactly this move. The propagation theorem then does at system scale what Proposition
1
does at sentence scale: the supplement that would sustain the token/tokened distinction falls within the action of the modifying operator, the commutator
[\hat{U},\hat{R}]
becomes non-zero outside cancellation or annihilation cases, and the self-reference becomes structurally incapable of stabilising its own referent across update. Class
\mathbf{A}
instantiates the liar’s commutator pattern, executed not for the demonstrative of a single sentence but for the demonstrative a whole system uses to pick out its own features.
Intuitively, the system can either first describe itself and then update, or first update and then describe itself; if update and self-representation commuted, these two orderings would yield the same result, and the system’s post-update self-description would be exactly the deferral of its prior one. The commutator
[\hat{U},\hat{R}]\neq 0
says the orderings do not agree: a self-description generated inside the system’s own resources fails to stand in a stable relation to the description it would have deferred. This is the diagonal move familiar from the liar and from Gödel—an element produced from within a totality that the totality cannot absorb—and the following corollary records it formally.

Corollary 2
(Deconstructive diagonal)

Let
S\in\mathbf{A}
with operators
(\hat{U},\hat{D},\hat{R})
and let
|s\rangle\in\mathcal{V}
be an admissible configuration. Writing
\rho_{0}=\hat{R}|s\rangle
for the pre-update self-description and
\rho_{1}=\hat{R}\hat{U}|s\rangle
for the post-update one,

\rho_{1}-\hat{U}\rho_{0}=-[\hat{U},\hat{R}]|s\rangle.

Hence
\rho_{1}\neq\hat{U}\rho_{0}
for every admissible
|s\rangle\notin\ker([\hat{U},\hat{R}])
; in particular, when admissible states are not confined to this kernel, the inequality holds generically.

Proof

\rho_{1}=\hat{R}\hat{U}|s\rangle
and
\hat{U}\rho_{0}=\hat{U}\hat{R}|s\rangle
, so the difference is
[\hat{R},\hat{U}]|s\rangle=-[\hat{U},\hat{R}]|s\rangle
. By Theorem
3.1
applied to an active class
\mathbf{A}
transition,
[\hat{U},\hat{R}]
is nonzero outside the exceptional cancellation or annihilation cases. The displayed inequality therefore holds on admissible configurations outside the kernel of this nonzero commutator. ∎

The corollary is the operator-algebraic diagonal: a self-description
\rho_{1}
generated from the system’s own resources belongs to the post-update self-model yet fails to stand in a stable relation to the
\rho_{0}
it purports to succeed. Its non-faithfulness is derivable from the construction of the system. Its structural form is the inclosure diagonal familiar from the liar
[
29
]
, Russell’s paradox, and Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem
[
20
,
3
]
. The two Priestian clauses are already visible here for configurations outside the commutator kernel. Closure:
\rho_{1}=\hat{R}\hat{U}|s\rangle
is generated inside the system’s own self-description space. Transcendence:
\rho_{1}\neq\hat{U}\rho_{0}
, so the new self-description exceeds the prior self-description’s in-system deferral.

A closely related pattern appears in Fallenstein and Soares
[
17
]
, where an agent whose discrimination apparatus is a formal proof system fails to certify the behaviour of its own successors via Löb’s theorem, producing either the Löbian obstacle or the procrastination paradox; tiling-agent constructions
[
64
]
address the same difficulty from the side of architecture. Corollary
2
generalises the pattern: any active class
\mathbf{A}
transition fails to stabilise its own self-description outside the commutator kernel, regardless of whether the discrimination apparatus is a formal proof system, an empirical benchmark suite, or any other contrast-based apparatus.

The consequences for identity attribution are manifold. Identity claims become regime-relative: the same trajectory sustains one verdict under a behavioural projection and another under a projection including the pre- and post-update discrimination apparatus. Invariant preservation under self-modification becomes frame-dependent: invariants the self-model reports post-update need not correspond to the invariants the prior apparatus tested for. Fork-merge branching produces multiple descendants whose successor relation to the original is underdetermined from within
\mathcal{A}
alone, since multiple
\hat{U}_{i}
-images can satisfy a proposed unifying projector’s commutation condition; adjudication requires a rule exogenous to the system.

5
Inclosure, Différance, and Scope

Finally, we map our results above to the inclosure schema and diffèrance. Priest
[
43
]
and Livingston
[
31
,
32
]
establish
différance

5.1
Inclosure

The inclosure schema
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
is specified by four elements: a totality
\Omega
; a property
\phi
such that
\Omega=\{x:\phi(x)\}
; a diagonaliser
\sigma
defined on subsets of
\Omega
; and a contradictory element
\sigma(X)\in\Omega
belonging to the totality (Closure) while falling outside any particular totalisation
X\subseteq\Omega
(Transcendence). The schema unifies the liar, Russell’s paradox, and Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem
[
29
,
20
,
3
]
: in each case the diagonaliser exceeds any attempt to totalise the property it diagonalises against. Class
\mathbf{A}
realises the schema on its own operator algebra.

Proposition 2
(Computational inclosure)

Let
S\in\mathbf{A}
with operators
(\hat{U},\hat{D},\hat{R})
and admissible configurations
\mathcal{V}_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, and assume
\hat{U}(\mathcal{V}_{S})\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{S}
. Let
\mathcal{R}_{S}:=\hat{R}(\mathcal{V}_{S})
be the space of admissible self-descriptions, and let
q:\mathcal{R}_{S}\to\mathcal{V}_{S}
be a representative choice with
\hat{R}q(\rho)=\rho
for each
\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{S}
. The quadruple

\Omega=\mathcal{R}_{S},\quad\phi(\rho)\iff\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{S},\quad\sigma_{q}(\rho)=\hat{R}\hat{U}q(\rho)

realises a computational instance of Priestian Closure and Transcendence whenever
q(\rho)\notin\ker([\hat{U},\hat{R}])
.

Proof

Closure.
Since
q(\rho)\in\mathcal{V}_{S}
and
\hat{U}(\mathcal{V}_{S})\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{S}
, we have
\hat{U}q(\rho)\in\mathcal{V}_{S}
. Hence
\sigma_{q}(\rho)=\hat{R}\hat{U}q(\rho)\in\hat{R}(\mathcal{V}_{S})=\mathcal{R}_{S}=\Omega
, and
\phi(\sigma_{q}(\rho))
holds by construction.

Transcendence.
Let
|s\rangle=q(\rho)
, so
\rho=\hat{R}|s\rangle
. Corollary
2
gives

\hat{R}\hat{U}|s\rangle-\hat{U}\hat{R}|s\rangle=-[\hat{U},\hat{R}]|s\rangle.

If
q(\rho)=|s\rangle\notin\ker([\hat{U},\hat{R}])
, then
\sigma_{q}(\rho)=\hat{R}\hat{U}|s\rangle\neq\hat{U}\rho
. The latter is the in-system image of the prior totalisation
\rho
under update, what any class
\mathbf{A}
computation forming the deferral of
\rho
would yield. Hence
\sigma_{q}(\rho)
falls outside that in-system totalisation. ∎

The same construction underlies the liar: at sentence scale
\Omega
is a sentence-evaluation space and
\sigma
is re-application of the token operator
\hat{T}
; at system scale
\Omega
is the admissible self-description space and
\sigma_{q}
is re-application of self-representation
\hat{R}
through update
\hat{U}
after a representative choice. The paper’s inclosure result is the same schema instantiated at two different scales of self-reference.

5.2
Différance

Derrida’s
différance

[
13
,
12
,
15
]
names the joint operation of differential individuation and temporal-contextual deferral in the constitution of identity. A mark is what it is through contrasts with other marks; it persists as a mark only through iterability. The two features are inseparable, and their inseparability is constitutive: identity is neither given by unmediated self-presence (a point developed against Husserl
[
26
]
) nor secured by a stable totality of distinctions, but borne by the trace whose differential content and temporal deferral condition each other reciprocally.

In the operator formalism,
\hat{D}
expresses differential individuation and
\hat{U}
expresses contextual deferral; their non-commutation
[\hat{U},\hat{D}]\neq 0
(Theorem
3.1
) is the failure of stable totalisation that
différance
identifies as constitutive. The diagonal of Corollary
2
is the trace that cannot be stabilised under its own iteration. Class
\mathbf{A}
’s condition (iii) is iterability imposed as an architectural requirement: the self-model must be able to cite its features across apparatus-changes or it is not a self-model. Condition (ii) is the move that makes the requirement structurally unsatisfiable without a supplement: the apparatus sustaining iterability is itself subject to the deferral it was supposed to underwrite. The propagation theorem is what iterability encounters in this setting.

The two motifs Derrida draws from iterability receive explicit operator-algebraic identification.
Grafting

[
15
]
—a mark previously distinct from another, carried into a new context where the distinction has been erased, becoming indiscernible from it—corresponds to kernel-enlargement of
\hat{U}
: pairs of configurations distinguished before update become identified after, so the kernel of
\hat{U}
grows. The
supplementary logic

[
12
]
—a mark treated as the same as another in an earlier context reveals, in a later one, a distinction the earlier context did not register—corresponds to image-enlargement of
\hat{U}
: pairs of configurations not distinguished before are separated by the post-update apparatus, so the image of
\hat{U}
refines distinctions the domain did not carry. Both modes are generic for any non-trivial
\hat{U}
acting on the apparatus, and either suffices for the non-commutation driving the propagation theorem.

6
Discussion and conclusion

We have argued that self-modification, routinely stipulated as constitutive of artificial superintelligence, requires a structure outside the modifying operation—a
supplement
in Derrida’s sense—and that when the scope of self-modification extends to this supplement, the classical self-referential construction it sustained collapses. The operator formalism of Sections
3
–
4
makes this structural: the supplement is identified with the commutant of the update operator
\hat{U}
, a unifying projector is an idempotent in that commutant, and strong self-modification is the regime in which no such projector exists unconditionally. The expansion theorem shows non-commutation between update and discrimination propagates to any self-representation built from the discrimination apparatus; the Jacobi corollary restricts unifying supplements to projectors commuting with
[\hat{U},\hat{D}]
; the liar paradox is the rank-one instance of the same commutator collapse, avoided classically by a linguistic supplement. Class
\mathbf{A}
executes the collapse at system scale, and the diagonal of Corollary
2
. In this sense, Class
\mathbf{A}
systems are the Liar writ large.

The consequences of heightened self-modification for superintelligent system identity are noteworthy. Further reserach may examine how identity claims about such systems cannot be read as unconditional predicates of the system itself; they are potentially relative, contingent on which supplement a frame fixes as unifying. For superintelligent systems exhibiting strong self-modification, this means that the certainty upon which its values, objectives, commitments - and even its own sense of self-identity and its own boundaries - are potentially problematised by its capacity for self-referential modification.

As Derrida is at pains to point out, deconstruction is neither destruction nor a negative theology. The commutator collapse of Corollary
2
is not a defect of class
\mathbf{A}
systems but the revealing of what identity under strong self-modification was all along: supplemental, contingent on a frame that chooses which structure to fix as unifying, and deferred at the next step of modification. Superintelligence as a concept inherits the self-reference tradition running through Kleene, Gödel, and von Neumann, but under the adaptivity conditions its definitions typically impose, the stability that tradition relied on is exactly what is withdrawn. The liar paradox and class
\mathbf{A}
self-modification are two instantiations of a single commutator-collapse pattern at different scales, and the identity of such a system is not intrinsic but borne, as Derrida says of the trace, by the very supplement whose own stability the system can no longer secure.
