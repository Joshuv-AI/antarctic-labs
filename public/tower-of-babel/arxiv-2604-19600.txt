Abstract
. It is a long-standing open question to determine whether the Sierpiński carpet attains its conformal dimension or not. While this problem remains unresolved, we prove that Cartesian products 𝕊 k \mathbb{S}^{k} , where 𝕊 \mathbb{S} is the Sierpiński carpet and k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , do not attain their conformal dimension. Our approach is based on the Sobolev spaces and energy measures on 𝕊 \mathbb{S} – constructed by Shimizu, Kigami, and Murugan and Shimizu – together with a certain singularity result of energy measures from the theory of analysis on fractals. This work formulates a general non-attainment result of conformal dimension for product metric spaces X k X^{k} for k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 in terms of self-similarity and energy measures of the factor X X . It applies, in particular, to the cases where X X is the Sierpiński carpet, the Sierpiński gasket, the Menger sponge, and the Laakso diamond.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

The
Ahlfors regular conformal dimension
of a metric space
X
is the infimum of Hausdorff dimensions of all metric spaces
Y
that are Ahlfors regular and quasisymmetrically homeomorphic to
X
. This notion originates in the work of Bourdon and Pajot
[
13
]
and is a variant of the
conformal dimension
introduced by Pansu
[
47
]
.
Precise definitions are given in Section
2
, and a more comprehensive treatment can be found in the book by Mackay and Tyson
[
45
]
.
For brevity, we refer to the Ahlfors regular conformal dimension simply as the conformal dimension throughout.

A central concept in the literature on conformal dimension is the
attainment problem
, which asks whether the infimum of Hausdorff dimensions of
Y
, as described in the previous paragraph, is a minimum.
In fact, this problem turns out to be related to a remarkably large number of questions in different fields.
To name a few notable ones, Bonk and Kleiner showed that the well-known Cannon’s conjecture can be reformulated in terms of an attainment problem of conformal dimension for the visual boundary of hyperbolic groups
[
12
]
.

There are further surprising connections between attainment problems and existence problems for exotic metrics and measures. In the work of the second author and G. C. David
[
21
]
, it was observed that the attainment for a certain Pillow space implies the existence of an analytically 1-dimensional plane in the sense of Cheeger
[
18
]
.
Whether such a plane exists was posed by Kleiner and Schioppa
[
40
]
and remains an open problem.

Lastly, Bonk and Kleiner
[
12
]
observed that attainment implies, for a large class of group boundaries, that they are quasisymmetrically equivalent to a Loewner space in the sense of Heinonen and Koskela
[
28
]
. See also the discussion in Kleiner’s ICM survey
[
41
]
. A similar result applies to a large class of self-similar, combinatorially Loewner spaces by
[
19
, Section 1.6]
.
For motivation and references about Loewner spaces, or more generally PI-spaces, see
[
27
,
10
,
29
]
.

Despite the extensive research, it is an unfortunate fact that the attainment problem has been resolved only for a very limited class of examples.
One source of this difficulty appears to be that the geometries of the metric spaces for which the attainment problem is of interest are highly intricate. As a result, the existing tools do not seem capable of determining attainment when only non-explicit information is available. A way forward was suggested by Murugan and Shimizu who studied the relationship of energy measures to attainment problems
[
46
]
. Here, we take this concept a step further by showing how such tools can be used to determine attainment.

An interested reader can find from
[
45
]
a list of examples where the attainment have been deduced.
See also the recent work by the authors and Rainio
[
6
,
4
]
which provides a quite rich class of geometries where the attainment can be realized in a non-trival way, as-well as examples where the attainment fails. While these examples are varied, we note that the topologies of the examples in
[
6
,
4
]
are somewhat repetitive; most of them are homeomorphic to the universal Menger curve by
[
2
,
3
]
. It would be desirable to have more varied examples, such as manifolds and topological carpets, where the attainment problem can be decided.

1.2.
Main result

A notorious example for which the attainment of the conformal dimension remains open is the
Sierpiński carpet

[
12
, Problem 6.2]
.
The value of the conformal dimension is also unknown
[
29
, Open problem 15.22]
but quite impressive estimates are available due to Kwapisz
[
43
]
.
The hypothetical realization of the attainment was recently studied by Murugan and Shimizu
[
46
, Section 9]
.

While the present work does not resolve the attainment problem for the Sierpiński carpet, our main result is a proof of the following non-attainment result.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
\mathbb{S}
is the standard Sierpiński carpet and
k\geq 2
, then the Cartesian product
\mathbb{S}^{k}
does not attain its conformal dimension.

Kleiner had conjectured in
[
41
, Conjecture 7.5]
that approximately self-similar and
combinatorially Lowner
metric spaces attain their conformal dimension (see
[
20
]
for a definition that is revised from
[
14
]
). By
[
14
, Section 4.3]
or
[
7
, Remark 4.5 and Theorem 8.3]
, the Cartesian product
\mathbb{S}^{k}
satisfies the combinatorial Loewner property. Theorem
1.1
, therefore, provides new counterexamples to Kleiner’s conjecture
[
41
, Conjecture 7.5]
. While the conjecture was first resolved in
[
6
]
, all counterexamples therein are homeomorphic to the Menger curve. It was posed in
[
21
]
if one can construct counterexamples with other topologies. The present work provides the first counterexamples of arbitrary topological dimension.

Our methods are not restricted to the Sierpiński carpet, and apply to many self-similar spaces. See the end of the introduction for a detailed discussion.

1.3.
Main techniques

The starting point of the present work is the following elementary observation that illustrates a rigidity of product spaces in quasiconformal geometry.
Let us note that the we are not the first one to exploit similar ideas. See the work of Väisälä from 1989
[
52
, Section 5]
, and the more recent study on Carnot groups by Le Donne and Xie
[
44
]
.
See also
[
45
, Example 4.1.9]

Suppose that
f:X\to Y
is a quasisymmetry. Then it is false in general that the induced homeomorphism
\tilde{f}:X^{2}\to Y^{2}
where
\tilde{f}(x_{1},x_{2}):=(f(x_{1}),f(x_{2}))
is a quasisymmetry.
The mapping
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
where
f(z):=\lvert z\rvert z
is one such example.
In fact, ignoring some obvious examples like bi-Lipschitz or snowflake maps,
\tilde{f}
is virtually never a quasisymmetry in practical situations.
In what follows, the attainment problem for
X
and
X^{2}
are separate questions.
We strongly encourage the reader to carry out some simple computations and to draw some pictures in order to be convinced about this fact.

Based on the discussion above, we formulate the following heuristic principle which, aside from examples like
\mathbb{R}
, seems to be tentative:

Regardless of whether
X
attains its conformal dimension, the hypothetical attaining structure of
X^{k}
should not have a product structure arising from
X
.

In order to exploit this vague intuition, we develop an analytic machinery based on
energy measures
and
blow-up analysis
. While these tools may appear somewhat heavy and unnatural for our problem, we are not aware of any conceptually different approach that would prove Theorem
1.1
.
Our ideas are inspired by the two pioneering works, Kajino and Murugan on the conformal walk dimension
[
34
]
, and also Murugan and Shimizu reformulating the attainment of the conformal dimension on
\mathbb{S}
in terms of energy measures
[
46
]
.
In the remainder of the introduction, we sketch the proof of Theorem
1.1
and take
k=2
for simplicity.
We argue by contraposition, i.e., assume that the attainment is realized at
(\mathbb{S}^{2},\rho)
and denote its conformal dimension by
Q
.

We first describe the tools in our blow-up analysis. Note that
\mathbb{S}^{2}
as a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
is an attractor of an IFS
\{\Phi_{D}\}_{D\in\mathcal{D}}
where each
\Phi_{D}
is a composition of a translation and a contraction with contraction ratio equal to
1/3
.
We consider limits of the metric spaces
(\mathbb{S}^{2},\rho_{n})
where
\rho_{n}
is a normalized pull-back metric of a composition
\Phi_{D_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi_{D_{n}}
. Note that
\Phi_{D}
is not necessarily similitude in the metric
\rho
, but we can derive suitable uniform estimates that are required to use Gromov’s compactness theorem. As a result, we obtain a limit
(\mathbb{S}^{2},\tilde{\rho})
in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense.
As it is shown in Lemma
6.5
,
(\mathbb{S}^{2},\tilde{\rho})
is also an attaining metric space.

Next, we discuss the
Q
-energy measures on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and we begin by considering the analogous objects on the factor
\mathbb{S}
that are provided by the work of Murugan and Shimizu
[
46
]
.
In this specific case, we could also refer to the earlier works of Kigami
[
39
]
and Shimizu
[
49
]
due to the same explanation as in Remark
6.11
.
Let
Q
be the conformal dimension of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{S}}:L^{Q}(\mathbb{S},\mu_{\mathbb{S}})\to[0,\infty]
be the
Q
-energy form in
[
46
, Theorem 1.1]
where
\mu_{\mathbb{S}}
is the
\log(8)/\log(3)
-Hausdorff measure of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We also let
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{S}}:=\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{S}}^{-1}([0,\infty))
be the Sobolev space, and finally
\{\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}\langle f\rangle\}_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{S}}}
be the
Q
-energy measures in
[
46
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Roughly speaking,
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{S}}
is an abstract counterpart of the Dirichlet energy
f\mapsto\lVert\nabla f\rVert_{L^{Q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{Q}
, which in particular means that
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{S}}
plays the role of the first-order Sobolev space
W^{1,Q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The purpose of the energy measures
\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}\langle f\rangle
is to be a counterpart of the “localized energies”, i.e. the Radon measures
A\mapsto\lVert\nabla f\rVert_{L^{Q}(A)}^{Q}
.
The existence of these energy measures is a non-trivial fact, and they are, unfortunately, rather inexplicit. To the best of our knowledge, the currently available tools do not yield a single non-constant function
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{S}}
. All constructions known to us rely heavily on abstract compactness arguments.

The construction of energy measures on the product
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is based on the energy measures on the factor
\mathbb{S}
and a method introduced by Strichartz
[
51
]
.
Let
\mu=\mu_{\mathbb{S}}\otimes\mu_{\mathbb{S}}
be the product measure on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, which is easily seen to be comparable to the
\log(64)/\log(3)
-Hausdorff measure.
Consider the
Q
-energy form
\mathcal{E}:L^{Q}(\mathbb{S}^{2},\mu)\to[0,\infty]
given by the equation

\mathcal{E}(u):=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{S}}(u(x,\,\cdot\,))\,\text{d}\mu_{\mathbb{S}}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{S}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{S}}(u(\,\cdot\,,y))\,\text{d}\mu_{\mathbb{S}}(y).

Here
u(x,\,\cdot\,)(y):=u(x,y)
and
u(\,\cdot\,,y)(x):=u(x,y)
are the sections of
u
.
The Sobolev space is denoted by
\mathcal{F}:=\mathcal{E}^{-1}([0,\infty))
, and each
u\in\mathcal{F}
is assigned the
Q
-energy measure

\Gamma\langle u\rangle(\Omega):=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}\langle u(x,\,\cdot\,)\rangle(\Omega_{x})\,\text{d}\mu_{\mathbb{S}}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{S}}\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}}\langle u(\,\cdot\,,y)\rangle(\Omega^{y})\,\text{d}\mu_{\mathbb{S}}(y)

where
\Omega_{x}:=\{y:(x,y)\in\Omega\}
and
\Omega^{y}:=\{x:(x,y)\in\Omega\}
are the slices of
\Omega
.

1.4.
Sketch of the proof

The proof scheme of Theorem
1.1
is as follows.
The first step is to show that the attaining metric space
(\mathbb{S}^{2},\rho)
must have the following product structure.
If a Radon measure
\Lambda
on
\mathbb{S}
is a
minimal energy dominant measure
of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{S}}
in the sense of Hino
[
32
]
(see Section
3
for the definition) and
\nu
is the
Q
-Hausdorff measure of
(\mathbb{S}^{2},\rho)
, then
(\mathbb{S}^{2},\rho)
has a product structure:

(1.2)

\nu\text{ is mutually absolutely continuous with }\Lambda\otimes\mu_{\mathbb{S}}+\mu_{\mathbb{S}}\otimes\Lambda.

This follows from Corollary
3.7
and a “conformal invariance” of the
Q
-energy form, stated in Proposition
4.10
and Corollary
4.15
.
Our strategy is to find a contradiction in (
1.2
) using the heuristic principle mentioned above.

Then consider
h:=\text{d}(\Lambda\otimes\mu_{\mathbb{S}})/\text{d}\nu
and its density point
x
where
h(x)>0
. We proceed by blowing-up at such point, and obtain a metric space
(\mathbb{S}^{2},\tilde{\rho})
, quasisymmetric to
(\mathbb{S}^{2},\rho)
.
We also show that, if
\nu_{n}
are the
Q
-Hausdorff measures in the blow-up sequence
(\mathbb{S}^{2},\rho_{n})
, then a subsequence of
\nu_{n}
converges weakly to measure
\tilde{\nu}
satisfying the properties

(1.3)

\tilde{\nu}(B_{\tilde{\rho}}(x,r))\sim r^{Q}\text{ and }\tilde{\nu}\ll\tilde{\Lambda}\otimes\mu_{\mathbb{S}}.

for some Radon measure
\tilde{\Lambda}
.
The first condition in (
1.3
) is obtained by showing that
\nu_{n}(B_{\rho_{n}}(x,r))
is comparable to
r^{Q}
with constant independent of
n
. The second condition in (
1.3
) is somewhat delicate because weak limits are generally unsuitable for studying absolute continuity.
Nevertheless, here we can exploit a fact that

\textrm{m}_{1,n}\rightharpoonup\tilde{\textrm{m}}_{1}\text{ and }\textrm{m}_{2,n}\rightharpoonup\tilde{\textrm{m}}_{2}\text{ implies }\textrm{m}_{1,n}\otimes\textrm{m}_{2,n}\rightharpoonup\textrm{m}_{1}\otimes\textrm{m}_{2},

which always holds under mild topological assumptions.
Hence, the second conditions in (
1.3
) follows because we blow-up at a density point of
h
.

Now, (
1.3
) implies that
(\mathbb{S}^{2},\tilde{\rho})
is also an attaining metric space, and that its
Q
-Hausdorff measure is comparable to
\tilde{\nu}
. But since (
1.2
) is valid for
any
attaining metric space, we must have that

\mu_{\mathbb{S}}\otimes\Lambda\ll\Lambda\otimes\mu_{\mathbb{S}}+\mu_{\mathbb{S}}\otimes\Lambda\ll\tilde{\nu}\ll\tilde{\Lambda}\otimes\mu_{\mathbb{S}}.

Up to this point, each step in the proof would work if, for instance,
\mathbb{S}
is replaced by the unit interval
[0,1]
, for which the conclusion of the theorem is obviously false.
But now, we use a certain singularity property carried by energy measures on many fractal spaces, including the Sierpiński carpet.
More specifically, if
\Lambda
is a minimal energy dominant measure, then
\Lambda\perp\mu_{\mathbb{S}}
.
See Section
5
for more detailed discussion about the singularity.
This together with absolute continuity in the previous display implies
\Lambda=0
, which clearly contradicts (
1.2
). The attaining structure therefore cannot exist, and this completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

1.5.
Further examples

As discussed above, the proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on two main techniques: blow-ups and energy measures.
The existence of blow-ups are ensured by rather mild self-similarity; essentially all self-similar sets in Euclidean spaces admit them.
The existence of suitable energy measures is far more non-trivial. Nevertheless, there is by now a substantial body of literature in analysis on fractals that provides such examples. See, for instance,
[
49
,
39
,
30
,
37
,
5
]
, as well as the recent survey
[
36
]
.
Below, we provide a list of examples of metric spaces
X
for which Theorem
1.1
holds if we replace
\mathbb{S}
with
X
.
A precise generalized statement is provided in Theorem
6.1
, and see Section
6.3
for more detailed discussion.

(1)

X
is the Menger sponge, or more generally any generalized Sierpiński carpet in the sense of
[
49
]
, where also the relevant energy form is constructed.

(2)

X
is the Laakso diamond space, where the energy is constructed in
[
5
]
. More generally, if
X
is any Laakso type space in the sense of
[
5
]
, assuming that for every
p\in(1,\infty)
its
p
-walk dimension is strictly larger than
p
.

(3)

X
is the Sierpiński gasket, with the energy constructed in
[
39
]
.

Finally, we note that the construction of the energy measures on
\mathbb{S}
discussed above generalizes to every
p\in(1,\infty)
.
For our application to the non-attainment, however, it is essential that we consider the
Q
-energy where
Q
is the conformal dimension of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
For instance, the “conformal invariance” of the
Q
-energy, stated in Proposition
4.10
and Corollary
4.15
, should be false when
Q
is some other value.
This, nevertheless, does not mean that
p
-energies for other values of
p
are not interesting.
In fact, our method for the non-attainment demands a study of the
p
-energy on the factor
\mathbb{S}
, which is not conformally invariant in the sense above;
Q
is the conformal dimension of the product
\mathbb{S}^{2}
but not of the factor
\mathbb{S}
.
The results of the work, therefore, indicates that the aforementioned studies of energy measures, for general
p\in(1,\infty)
, should be applicable in the future studies on conformal dimension.

2.
Preliminary

2.1.
Metric spaces

We recall some standard terminology from metric geometry, and refer to
[
29
,
15
]
for further details.
Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space. If
A\subseteq X
is a non-empty subset, write
\diam(A):=\sup_{x,y\in A}d(x,y)
.
For
Q>0
, the
Q
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
of a subset
A\subseteq X
is

\mathcal{H}^{Q}(A):=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathcal{H}_{\delta}^{Q}(A).

Here
\mathcal{H}^{Q}_{\delta}
is the
Q
-dimensional Hausdorff
\delta
-content
, given by

\mathcal{H}^{Q}_{\delta}(A):=\inf\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\diam(A_{i})^{Q}\;:\;A\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i},\;\diam(A_{i})\leq\delta\bigg\}.

The
Hausdorff dimension
of
(X,d)
is the value

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(X,d):=\inf\left\{Q>0:\mathcal{H}^{Q}(X)=0\right\}.

We say that
(X,d)
is
Q
-Ahlfors regular
if there is
C\geq 1
such that

(2.1)

C^{-1}r^{Q}\leq\mathcal{H}^{Q}(B(x,r))\leq Cr^{Q}\text{ for all }x\in X\text{ and }r\in(0,2\diam(X)].

Here
B(x,r)
denotes the open ball

B(x,r):=\{y\in X:d(x,y)<r\}.

An inflation of a ball
B=B(x,r)
by a constant
C>0
is often denoted
CB:=B(x,Cr)
. The Hausdorff dimension of a
Q
-Ahlfors regular metric space is equal to
Q
. We refer to
[
29
, pages 61-62]
for details.

2.2.
Curves

Let
(X,d)
be a compact metric space.
A
curve
in
X
is a continuous function
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
.
Its
length
is the value

\len(\gamma):=\sup\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}d(\gamma(t_{k}),\gamma(t_{k+1}))

where the supremum is taken over all increasing sequences
\{t_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}\subseteq[0,1]
.
We say that
(X,d)
is
geodesic
if for every
x,y\in X
there is a curve
\gamma
such that
\gamma(0)=x
,
\gamma(1)=y
and
\len(\gamma)=d(x,y)
.

When convenient, we regard a curve
\gamma
in
X
as the subset of
X
by identifying it as the image
\gamma([0,1])
.
We say that
(X,d)
is of
bounded turning
if there is
K\geq 1
satisfying the following. For every
x,y\in X
there is a curve
\gamma
such that
x,y\in\gamma
and
\diam(\gamma)\leq Kd(x,y)
.

2.3.
Conformal dimension

We recall the necessary terminology related to the conformal dimension, and see Mackay–Tyson for further literature
[
45
]
.
Let
d
and
\rho
be two metrics on
X
. We say that
d
is
quasisymmetric
to
\rho
if there is a homeomorphism
\eta\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that, for all triples
x,y,z\in X
with
x\neq z
,

\frac{d(x,y)}{d(x,z)}\leq\eta\bigg(\frac{\rho(x,y)}{\rho(x,z)}\bigg).

Quasisymmetry is an equivalence relation of metrics by
[
29
, Chapter 10]
.
We sometimes say that
d
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric to
\rho
when the function
\eta
is relevant.
The
Ahlfors regular conformal dimension
of a metric space
(X,d)
is the infimum of exponents
Q>0
such that there is a metric
\rho
on
X
, quasisymmetric to
d
, which is
Q
-Ahlfors regular.
For simplicity, we call this value the
conformal dimension
of
(X,d)
.
We say that a metric space
(X,d)

attains
its conformal dimension if there is a metric
\rho
, quasisymmetric to
d
, such that
\rho
is
Q
-Ahlfors regular and
Q
is the conformal dimension of
(X,d)
.

The conformal dimension is often studied using the discrete modulus. We review the necessary tools for this approach.
Let
(X,d)
be a compact metric space,
\mathcal{U}
be an open covering of
X
, and
\mathcal{C}
be a family of curves in
X
.
We say that a function
\rho:\mathcal{U}\to[0,\infty)
is
\mathcal{C}
-admissible
if

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathcal{U}\\
U\cap\gamma\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\rho(U)\geq 1

for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}
.
For
p\in[1,\infty)
, we define the
discrete
p
-modulus
as the value

\Mod_{p}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}):=\min_{\rho}\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}}\rho(U)^{p}

where the minimum is taken over all
\mathcal{C}
-admissible
\rho:\mathcal{U}\to[0,\infty)
.
When
p>1
the minimizer
\rho
exists and is unique by standard convex optimization theory.

2.4.
Loewner spaces

We recall some important concepts from analysis on metric space, and see the standard references
[
10
,
27
,
29
]
.
Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space and
\mu
be a Radon measure on
(X,d)
. We say that
\mu
is
doubling
if there is
D\geq 1
such that

0<\mu(B(x,2r))\leq D\mu(B(x,r))<\infty\text{ for all }x\in X\text{ and }r>0.

If
f\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
and
A\subseteq X
is a Borel set with
\mu(A)\in(0,\infty)
, we define the
integral average

f_{A}:=\mathchoice{\mathop{\kern 1.99997pt\vrule width=6.00006pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.49997pt\kern-8.00003pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-3.27773ptA}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{A}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{A}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{A}}f\,d\mu:=\frac{1}{\mu(A)}\int f\,d\mu.

For
p\in[1,\infty)
, we say that
(X,d,\mu)
satisfies the
(1,p)
-Poincaré inequality
if there are
C,\sigma\geq 1
such that for all Lipschitz functions
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
it holds that

\mathchoice{\mathop{\kern 1.99997pt\vrule width=6.00006pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.49997pt\kern-8.00003pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-3.27773ptB(x,r)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B(x,r)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B(x,r)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B(x,r)}}\lvert f-f_{B(x,r)}\rvert\,d\mu\leq Cr\left(\mathchoice{\mathop{\kern 1.99997pt\vrule width=6.00006pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.49997pt\kern-8.00003pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-3.27773ptB(x,\sigma r)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B(x,\sigma r)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B(x,\sigma r)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B(x,\sigma r)}}\Lip(f)^{p}\,d\mu\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Here
\Lip(f)
denotes the
pointwise Lipschitz constant

\Lip(f)(x):=\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\sup_{y\in B(x,r)}\frac{\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert}{d(x,y)}.

Finally, given
Q>1
, we say that a metric space
(X,d)
is a
Q
-
Loewner space
if it is
Q
-Ahlfors regular and
(X,d,\mathcal{H}^{Q})
satisfies the
(1,Q)
-Poincaré inequality.

3.
Dirichlet spaces

The main techniques used in the proof of non-attainment require a sufficiently general setting in which a first-order calculus on metric spaces can be developed.
It should, at least, encompass the Sobolev spaces on the Sierpński carpet constructed in
[
46
,
49
,
39
]
, and the analogous objects on the product spaces.
The more standard approaches in analysis on metric spaces, popularized by e.g. Cheeger
[
18
]
, Hajłasz
[
26
]
and Shanmugalingam
[
48
]
, and also Ambrisio–Gigli–Savare
[
1
]
, do not appear to provide a sufficiently flexible setting for our purposes.
Instead, we work with
p
-Dirichlet spaces introduced by the second author and Murugan
[
22
]
.

3.1.
Definition

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
. We call
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F},\Gamma)
a
p
-Dirichlet space
if the conditions (1)-(7) below hold.

(1)

Locally compact space
:
(X,d,\mu)
is a complete, locally compact metric space equipped with a Radon measure
\mu
.

(2)

Completeness:

The space
\mathcal{F}
is a subspace of
L^{p}(X,\mu)
and

\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{F}\to[0,\infty)
is a non-negative function such that
\mathcal{F}
is a Banach space when equipped with the norm
\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}=(\lVert f\rVert_{L^{p}}^{p}+\mathcal{E}(f))^{1/p}
.

(3)

Homogeneity:
For all
f\in\mathcal{F}
there exists a finite non-negative Borel measure
\Gamma\langle f\rangle
on
X
such that
\Gamma\langle f\rangle(X)=\mathcal{E}(f)
and for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

\Gamma\langle\lambda f\rangle=|\lambda|^{p}\Gamma\langle f\rangle.

The measure
\Gamma\langle f\rangle
associated to
f\in\mathcal{F}
is called the
energy measure of
f
.

(4)

Triangle inequality:
For every
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
and every Borel set
A\subseteq X
,

\Gamma\langle f+g\rangle(A)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\Gamma\langle f\rangle(A)^{\frac{1}{p}}+\Gamma\langle g\rangle(A)^{\frac{1}{p}}

(5)

Lipschitz contractivity:
For all
f\in\mathcal{F}
and all
1
-Lipschitz functions
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\varphi(0)=0
we have
\varphi\circ f\in\mathcal{F}
and
\Gamma\langle\varphi\circ f\rangle\leq\Gamma\langle f\rangle
.

(6)

Strong locality:
For every
f\in\mathcal{F}
and an open set
A\subseteq X
, if there is
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
(f-c\mathds{1}_{A})=0

\mu
-a.e., then
\Gamma\langle f\rangle(A)=0
.

(7)

Weak lower semicontinuity:
For every
f\in L^{p}(X,\mu)
, and for any sequence of functions
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
in
\mathcal{F}
such that
f_{i}\to f\in L^{p}(X,\mu)
with
\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{E}(f_{i})<\infty
, then
f\in\mathcal{F}
and

\Gamma\langle f\rangle(A)\leq\liminf_{i\to\infty}\Gamma\langle f_{i}\rangle(A)

for every Borel set
A\subseteq X
.

3.2.
Poincaré and capacity

We call
\Psi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
a
scale function
if there is
C\geq 1
such that

C^{-1}\Psi(r)\leq\Psi(2r)\leq C\Psi(r)\text{ for all }r>0\text{ and }\Psi^{-1}(\{0\})=\{0\}.

When convenient, we sometimes write
\Psi(B)=\Psi(r)
where
B=B(x,r)
.
Given
p\in(1,\infty)
we call
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F},\Gamma)
a
(p,\Psi)
-Poincaré–Dirichlet space
if it satisfies the conditions (1)-(7) above, and also the following.

(8)

Volume doubling:

(X,d,\mu)
is volume doubling.

(9)

Poincaré inequality:
There are
C,\sigma\geq 1
such that, for all open balls
B
and
f\in\cF
, we have

(3.1)

\int_{B}\lvert f-f_{B}\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}\mu\leq C\Psi(B)\int_{\sigma B}\,\text{d}\Gamma\langle f\rangle.

(10)

Upper capacity:
There is
C\geq 1
such that, for every open ball
B\subseteq X
, there exists
\varphi\in\cF\cap C(X)
with
\varphi|_{B}=1
,
\varphi|_{X\setminus 2B}=0
and

\mathcal{E}(\varphi)\leq C\frac{\mu(B)}{\Psi(B)}.

These conditions are abbreviated as
\VD
,
\PI(\Psi)
and
\cCap(\Psi)
, respectively.
When
\Psi(r)=r^{\beta}
for some
\beta>0
, we often use the terminology
(p,\beta)
-Poincaré–Dirichlet space and write
\PI(\beta),\cCap(\beta)
for the conditions above.

In
[
24
]
the scale functions were allowed to depend also on the spatial variable
x\in X
. In the present work, however, it is essential that
\Psi
is radial. See the proof of Theorem
3.5
.

3.3.
Preliminary lemmas

The following lemma is proven in
[
24
, Lemma 4.2]
.
The assumptions on
\Psi
are slightly different, but same the proof applies.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Psi
be a scale function and
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F},\Gamma)
be a
p
-Dirichlet space satisfying
VD
and
\cCap(\Psi)
.
Let
\mathcal{B}
be a collection of open balls so that it covers
X
and there is
D\geq 1
satisfying

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\mathds{1}_{2B}\leq D\mathds{1}_{X}.

Then there is
C\geq 1
, which depends on
\mathcal{B}
only through the constant
D
, and a partition of unity

\{\psi_{B}\}_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\cap C(X)
, satisfying the following.

(1)

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\psi_{B}(x)=1
and
0\leq\psi_{B}(x)\leq 1
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
and
x\in X
.

(2)

It holds for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
that
\psi_{B}|_{X\setminus 2B}=0
.

(3)

It holds for every
B\in\mathcal{B}
that

\mathcal{E}(\psi_{B})\leq C\frac{\mu(B)}{\Psi(B)}.

Let
(X,d,\mu,\cE,\cF,\Gamma)
be a
p
-Dirichlet space. We say that a Radon measure
\Lambda
on
(X,d)
is
energy dominant
if
\Gamma\langle f\rangle\ll\Lambda
for every
f\in\cF
.
An energy dominant measure
\Lambda
is
minimal energy dominant
if for every energy dominant
\nu
we have
\Lambda\ll\nu
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(X,d,\mu,\cE,\cF,\Gamma)
be a
p
-Dirichlet space so that
(X,d)
is compact. Then there exists a minimal energy dominant measure.

Proof.

Let
\cF(N):=\{f\in\cF:\|f\|_{\cF}\leq N\}
. By the separability of
L^{p}(X,\mu)
, which follows from the compactness of
X
, we can fix a countable set
S_{N}\subseteq\cF(N)
that is dense in
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{L^{p}(X,\mu)}
-norm.
We enumerate the elements of
S_{N}
as
\{f_{i,N}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. We also define
S=\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}S_{N}
and

\Lambda:=\sum_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{2^{i+N}N}\Gamma\langle f_{i,N}\rangle.

We argue that
\Lambda
is minimal energy dominant. Note that
\Lambda
is a finite Radon measure by construction.
Because
\Lambda
is a countable sum of energy measures,
\Lambda\ll\nu
when
\nu
is any energy dominant measure. This proves the minimality of
\Lambda
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{F}
. Then
f\in\mathcal{F}(N)
for
N:=\lceil\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}\rceil
, meaning there is
\{h_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq S_{N}
such that
h_{i}\to f
in
L^{p}(X,\mu)
. By the weak lower-semicontinuity, it holds for every Borel set
A\subseteq X
that

\Gamma\langle f\rangle(A)\leq\liminf_{i\to\infty}\Gamma\langle h_{i}\rangle(A).

By construction,
\Gamma\langle h_{i}\rangle\ll\Lambda
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
. This and the previous display implies
\Gamma\langle f\rangle\ll\Lambda
.
∎

3.4.
Cartesian product of Dirichlet spaces

If
(X,d_{X},\mu_{X})
and
(X,d_{Y},\mu_{Y})
are metric measure spaces, we denote the
\ell^{\infty}
-product metric by
d_{X}\times d_{Y}
, and the product measure by
\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}
.
We use the usual notation for the slices

\Omega^{y}:=\{x\in X:(x,y)\in\Omega\}\text{ and }\Omega_{x}:=\{y\in Y:(x,y)\in\Omega\},

and also denote the sections

u(\,\cdot\,,y)(x):=u(x,y)\text{ and }u(x,\,\cdot\,)(y):=u(x,y).

Definition 3.4
.

Let
(X,d_{X},\mu_{X},\mathcal{E}_{X},\mathcal{F}_{X},\Gamma_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y},\mu_{Y},\mathcal{E}_{Y},\mathcal{F}_{Y},\Gamma_{Y})
be two
p
-Dirichlet spaces. Their
Cartesian product
is the collection

(X\times Y,d_{X}\times d_{Y},\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F},\Gamma)

where the last three objects are given as follows.
The domain
\mathcal{F}
consists of those
u\in L^{p}(X\times Y,\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y})
satisfying

u(\,\cdot\,,y)\in\mathcal{F}_{X}\text{ for $\mu_{Y}$-a.e. $y\in Y$}\text{ and }u(x,\,\cdot\,)\in\mathcal{F}_{Y}\text{ for $\mu_{X}$-a.e. $x\in X$},

and also

\mathcal{E}(f):=\int_{X}\mathcal{E}_{Y}(u(x,\,\cdot\,))\,\text{d}\mu_{X}(x)+\int_{Y}\mathcal{E}_{X}(u(\,\cdot\,,y))\,\text{d}\mu_{Y}(y)<\infty.

The measures
\Gamma\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle
are given by

\Gamma\langle u\rangle(\Omega):=\int_{X}\Gamma_{Y}\langle u(x,\,\cdot\,)\rangle(\Omega_{x})\,\text{d}\mu_{X}(x)+\int_{Y}\Gamma_{X}\langle u(\,\cdot\,,y)\rangle(\Omega^{y})\,\text{d}\mu_{Y}(y).

In writing these definitions, it is not immediately obvious that the integrands are measurable. This will be shown in Lemma
3.6
below.
The objective of the section is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.5
.

Fix
p\in(1,\infty)
and a scale function
\Psi
.
Let
(X,d_{X},\mu_{X},\mathcal{E}_{X},\mathcal{F}_{X},\Gamma_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y},\mu_{Y},\mathcal{E}_{Y},\mathcal{F}_{Y},\Gamma_{Y})
be
(p,\Psi)
-Poincaré–Dirichlet spaces.
Then their Cartesian product is also a
(p,\Psi)
-Poincaré–Dirichlet space.

The most technical part of Theorem
3.5
is the following measurability result.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
(Z,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F},\Gamma)
be the Cartesian product in Theorem
3.5
.
Then, for every
u\in L^{p}(Z,\mu)
and a Borel set
\Omega\subseteq Z
, the function

y\mapsto\begin{cases}\Gamma_{X}\langle u(\,\cdot\,,y)\rangle(\Omega^{y})&\text{ if }u(\,\cdot\,,y)\in\mathcal{F}_{X},\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

is
\mu_{Y}
-measurable. Similarly,

x\mapsto\begin{cases}\Gamma_{Y}\langle u(x,\,\cdot\,)\rangle(\Omega_{x})&\text{ if }u(x,\,\cdot\,)\in\mathcal{F}_{Y},\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

is
\mu_{X}
-measurable.

Proof.

The two claims are symmetric, and thus it suffices to prove the first one.
Consider the
\mu_{Y}
-measurable function
F:Y\to L^{p}(X,\mu_{X})
given by
F(y)=u(\,\cdot\,,y)
.
By the separability of
L^{p}(X,\mu)
and Lusin’s theorem, there exist closed subsets
K_{i}\subseteq Y
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\mu_{Y}\left(Y\setminus\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}K_{i}\right)=0

and
F|_{K_{i}}
is continuous.
By the weak lower semicontinuity of the
p
-Dirichlet space, the function
K_{i}\to[0,\infty],\,y\mapsto\Gamma\langle F(y)\rangle(X)
, is lower-semicontinuous.
Here we interpret
\Gamma_{X}\langle F(y)\rangle(X)=\infty
if
F(y)\notin\mathcal{F}_{X}
.
Hence, the set

Y_{M}:=\{y\in K:\Gamma_{X}\langle F(y)\rangle\leq M\}

is
\mu_{Y}
-measurable for all
M\geq 0
.

We first consider the case
\Omega=A\times B\subseteq Z
where
A,B
are Borel sets.
Let
G:Y\to[0,\infty]
be the function in the claim.
Then
G|_{Y\setminus B}=0
and
G(y)=\Gamma_{X}\langle F(y)\rangle(A)
for all
y\in B
.
By the weak lower semicontinuity,
G|_{K_{i}\cap Y_{M}\cap B}
is lower semicontinuous for every
i,M\in\mathbb{N}
. By definition,
G(y)=\infty
in
B\setminus\bigcup_{M\in\mathbb{N}}Y_{M}
.
By combining these facts, it follows that
G
is
\mu_{Y}
-measurable.

The measurability for a general Borel set
\Omega\subseteq Z
follows from the monotone class theorem.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

The Cartesian product is denoted by
(Z,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F},\Gamma)
.
The integrals that determine
\mathcal{E}
and
\Gamma
are well-defined by Lemma
3.6
.
Moreover, most of the required axioms are immediately transferred to the product through the definitions. Only the non-trivial ones are checked here.

We begin by proving the completeness of
\mathcal{F}
.
Let
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be a Cauchy sequence, and let
u
be the limit in
L^{p}(Z,\mu)
.
Note that it is sufficient to prove that
u\in\mathcal{F}
and that some subsequence
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
converges in
\mathcal{F}
.
Hence, we may assume by the Fubini’s theorem that, for
\mu_{X}
-a.e.
x\in X
and
\mu_{Y}
-a.e.
y\in Y
, we have convergence
u_{i}(x,\,\cdot\,)\to u(x,\,\cdot\,)
and
u_{i}(\,\cdot\,,y)\to u(\,\cdot\,,y)
in the respective
L^{p}
-spaces.
Then, by the weak lower semicontinuity and Fatou’s lemma,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\mathcal{E}_{Y}(u(x,\,\cdot\,))\,\text{d}\mu_{X}+\int_{Y}\mathcal{E}_{X}(u(\,\cdot\,,y))\,\text{d}\mu_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{X}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{Y}(u_{i}(x,\,\cdot\,))\,\text{d}\mu_{X}+\int_{Y}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{X}(u_{i}(\,\cdot\,,y))\,\text{d}\mu_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{X}\mathcal{E}_{Y}(u_{i}(x,\,\cdot\,))\,\text{d}\mu_{X}+\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{Y}\mathcal{E}_{X}(u_{i}(\,\cdot\,,y))\,\text{d}\mu_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{E}(u_{i})<\infty.

The finiteness of the last row follows from the fact that
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
is Cauchy. Hence
u\in\mathcal{F}
. By repeating the previous arguments, we also get

\displaystyle\limsup_{i\to\infty}\mathcal{E}(u-u_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{i\to\infty}\left(\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathcal{E}(u_{j}-u_{i})\right)=0.

This completes the proof of the completeness of
\mathcal{F}
. A similar treatment yields the weak lower semicontinuity.

The doubling property of
\mu
is obvious from the doubling properties of the marginals. Hence VD holds.
The upper capacity estimate
\cCap(\Psi)
follows by taking functions of the form

(x,y)\mapsto\phi(x)\cdot\psi(y)

where
\phi\in\mathcal{F}_{X}\cap C(X)
and
\psi\in\mathcal{F}_{Y}\cap C(Y)
are provided by
\cCap(\Psi)
of the factors. Lastly, the Poincaré inequality
\PI(\Psi)
can be verified using the same argument as in
[
11
, Proof of Theorem 3]
.
We emphasize that it is essential for
\Psi
to be independent of the spatial variable, and to be the same in both factors.
∎

Recall minimal energy dominant measure from Subsection
3.3
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Assume the hypotheses in Theorem
3.5
, and that
(X,d_{X})
,
(Y,d_{Y})
are compact. Let
(Z,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F},\Gamma)
be the Cartesian product in Theorem
3.5
, and
\Lambda_{X}
and
\Lambda_{Y}
be a pair of minimal energy dominant measures of
(X,d_{X},\mu_{X},\mathcal{E}_{X},\mathcal{F}_{X},\Gamma_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y},\mu_{Y},\mathcal{E}_{Y},\mathcal{F}_{Y},\Gamma_{Y})
, respectively. Then

\Lambda:=\Lambda_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}+\mu_{X}\otimes\Lambda_{Y}.

is a minimal energy dominant measure of
(Z,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F},\Gamma)
.

Proof.

The fact that
\Lambda
is energy dominant is obvious from the definition of the measures
\Gamma
in Definition
3.4
.
Moreover, since any pair of minimal energy dominant measures are mutually absolutely continuous by definition, it is sufficient to prove the claim for any choice of
\Lambda_{X}
and
\Lambda_{Y}
. Hence, without loss of generality we assume that
\Lambda_{X}
and
\Lambda_{Y}
are finite Radon measures of the form

\Lambda_{X}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}\Gamma_{X}\langle f_{i}\rangle\text{ and }\Lambda_{Y}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\beta_{i}\Gamma_{Y}\langle g_{i}\rangle.

Such choices exist by the proof of Proposition
3.3
.

We verify the minimality of
\Lambda
. Let
\nu
be another energy dominant measure.
Consider the functions of the form
u(x,y):=f(x)
and
v(x,y):=g(y)
, where
f\in\mathcal{F}_{X}
and
g\in\mathcal{F}_{Y}
. By the compactness of the ambient spaces,
u,v\in\mathcal{F}
.
A direct computation shows that

\Gamma\langle u\rangle=\Gamma_{X}\langle f\rangle\otimes\mu_{Y}\text{ and }\Gamma\langle v\rangle=\mu_{X}\otimes\Gamma_{X}\langle g\rangle.

Both of these measures are absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu
. Since
\Lambda
is a sum of such measures, it is absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu
.
∎

4.
Conformal Dimension

This section relates properties of
p
-Dirichlet spaces to the conformal dimension of the ambient space.
We consider the following general setting, and impose further assumptions when necessary.

Assumption 4.1
.

Fix
p\in(1,\infty)
,
\beta>0
,
d_{\textrm{H}}>0
, and let
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F},\Gamma)
be a
(p,\beta)
-Poincaré–Dirichlet space where
(X,d)
is
d_{\textrm{H}}
-Ahlfors regular and
\mu
is the
d_{\textrm{H}}
-Hausdorff measure.
The underlying metric space
(X,d)
is also assumed to be of bounded turning and compact.

For each
\varepsilon>0
we fix a collection
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}
consisting of open balls in
(X,d)
so that the centers form an
\varepsilon
-net. We also let
\{\psi_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}}
be a partition of unity subordinate to
\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}
as in Lemma
3.2
.

4.1.
Moduli estimates

We begin by providing estimates on the discrete moduli. Proof methods are very similar to those in Heinonen and Koskela
[
28
]
.
No further conditions are imposed here.

The first estimates concern annular moduli.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that Assumption
4.1
holds.
There are
C,R>0
such that, for all
x\in X
and
0<r\leq R
, we have the following. If
\mathcal{C}
denotes the family of curves in
X
that intersect with both
B(x,r)
and
X\setminus B(x,2r)
, then

C^{-1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{r}\right)^{\beta-d_{\Hr}}\leq\Mod_{p}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon})\leq C\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{r}\right)^{\beta-d_{\Hr}}

for all
0<\varepsilon\leq r
.

Proof.

Fix
R:=\diam(X)/32
.
Let
x\in X
,
0<r\leq R
and
0<\varepsilon\leq r
. Throughout the proof,
\mathcal{C}
is as in the claim.
Note that
\mathcal{C}
is non-empty because
X
is assumed to be of bounded turning.
By
[
14
, Proposition 2.2]
, we may prove the estimates for
\Mod_{p}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32})
instead of
\Mod_{p}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon})
.

The latter inequality in the claim is proven first.
By (
2.1
) and
\cCap(\beta)
, we can construct

\varphi\in\mathcal{F}
with
\varphi|_{B(x,5r/4)}=1
,
\varphi|_{X\setminus B(x,7r/4)}=0
and

\mathcal{E}(\varphi)\lesssim r^{d_{\Hr}-\beta}.

More precisely, we cover
B(x,5r/4)
with
\{B(x_{i},r/8)\}_{i\in I}
where
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq B(x,5r/4)
is an
r/8
-net. Then, for each

, we take
\varphi_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
provided by
\cCap(\beta)
, and define

\varphi:=\min\left\{\sum_{i\in I}\varphi_{i},1\right\}.

The energy estimate for
\varphi
follows from the Lipschitz contractivity of the
p
-Dirichlet space because we can bound the cardinality of
I
with a number depending only on the constants in (
2.1
).

We now define a function
f:\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\to[0,\infty)
given by

f(U):=\mathchoice{\mathop{\kern 1.99997pt\vrule width=6.00006pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.49997pt\kern-8.00003pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-3.27773ptU}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{U}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{U}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{U}}\varphi\,\text{d}\mu.

By
\varepsilon\leq r
and
\diam(U)\leq\varepsilon/16
for all
U\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}
, we have
U\subseteq B(x,5/4r)
whenever
U\cap B(x,r)\neq\emptyset
. Similarly, if
U\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}
satisfies
U\cap X\setminus B(x,2r)\neq\emptyset
, we have
U\subseteq X\setminus B(x,7/4r)
.
Hence we have

(4.3)

f(U)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }U\cap B(x,r)\neq\emptyset\\
0&\text{ if }U\cap X\setminus B(x,2r)\neq\emptyset.\end{cases}

We now define
\rho:\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\to[0,\infty)
by

\rho(U):=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\\
V\cap U\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\lvert f(U)-f(V)\rvert.

It follows from the triangle inequality and (
4.3
) that
\rho
is
\mathcal{C}
-admissible. Its
p
-mass can be estimated

\displaystyle\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}}\rho(U)^{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U,V\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\\
U\cap V\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\lvert f(U)-f(V)\rvert^{p}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\beta-d_{\Hr}}\mathcal{E}_{p}(\varphi)\lesssim\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{r}\right)^{\beta-d_{\Hr}}.

The first inequality holds because we have bounded overlapping. The second follows from
\PI(\beta)
and the bounded overlapping, and the the third is a consequence of the energy upper bound of
\varphi
.
Since
\rho
is
\mathcal{C}
-admissible, this proves the latter inequality in the claim.

The converse inequality is considered next.
Let
\rho:\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\to[0,\infty)
be
\mathcal{C}
-admissible.
Since we aim to prove a lower bound for the
p
-mass of
\rho
, we may redefine
\rho
as follows. Let
\tilde{\rho}
be such that
\tilde{\rho}(U)=0
if
2U\cap(X\setminus B(x,4r))\neq\emptyset
and
\tilde{\rho}(U)=\rho(U)
otherwise.
Because
\tilde{\rho}
is admissible with smaller
p
-mass, by replacing
\rho
with
\tilde{\rho}
if necessary, we may assume that
\rho(U)=0
whenever
2U\cap(X\setminus B(x,4r))\neq\emptyset
.

We define a function
f:\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\to[0,\infty)
according to

f(U):=\min_{\gamma}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\\
U\cap\gamma\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\rho(U)

where the minimum is taken over all curves
\gamma
such that
\gamma
intersects with both
U
and
X\setminus B(x,2r)
.
Since
\rho
is
\mathcal{C}
-admissible,
f(U)\geq 1
for all
U
with
2U\cap B(x,r/2)\neq\emptyset
.
We also clearly have
f(U)=0
for all
U\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}
with
2U\cap X\setminus B(x,4r)\neq\emptyset
because we can just take
\gamma
to be a constant sequence.
Now, we define a function
u
using the partition of unity given by

(4.4)

u:=\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}}f(U)\cdot\psi_{U}.

Since
X
is assumed to be compact,
u\in\mathcal{F}
because it is a finite sum of functions in
\mathcal{F}
.
By the properties of
\{\psi_{U}\}_{U\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}}
and
f
, we have
u\geq 1
on
B(x,r/2)
and
u=0
on
X\setminus B(x,4r)
.
Now, if
y\in X
so that
d(x,y)=5r
, we can estimate using
\PI(\beta)
and
d_{\textrm{H}}
-the Ahlfors regularity that

(4.5)

1\leq\lvert u_{B(x,r/2)}-u_{B(y,r/2)}\rvert^{p}\lesssim\mathchoice{\mathop{\kern 1.99997pt\vrule width=6.00006pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.49997pt\kern-8.00003pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-3.27773ptB(x,10r)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B(x,10r)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B(x,10r)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B(x,10r)}}\lvert u-u_{B(x,10r)}\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}\mu\lesssim r^{\beta-d_{\textrm{H}}}\mathcal{E}(u).

Then we estimate
\mathcal{E}(u)
using the properties of the partition of unity.
For each
U\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}
and
x\in U
we have

u(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\\
U\cap 2V\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\left(f(V)-f(U)\right)\psi_{V}(x)+f(U).

By using the strong locality and the triangle inequality of
\Gamma\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle
, we get

\Gamma\langle u\rangle(U)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\\
U\cap 2V\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\lvert f(V)-f(U)\rvert\mathcal{E}(\psi_{V})^{\frac{1}{p}}\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\\
U\cap 2V\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\lvert f(V)-f(U)\rvert\varepsilon^{(d_{\textrm{H}}-\beta)/p}.

Note that the number of
V\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}
with
U\cap 2V\neq\emptyset
can be bounded by
N\in\mathbb{N}
depending only the Ahlfors regularity constants in (
2.1
).
The Hölder’s inequality now gives

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\\
U\cap 2V\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\lvert f(V)-f(U)\rvert\varepsilon^{(d_{\textrm{H}}-\beta)/p}\leq N^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\\
U\cap 2V\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\lvert f(V)-f(U)\rvert^{p}\varepsilon^{d_{\textrm{H}}-\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

By raising the previous two inequalities to the power
p
and summing them over all
U\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}
, we get

(4.6)

\mathcal{E}(u)\lesssim\varepsilon^{d_{\Hr}-\beta}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U,V\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\\
U\cap 2V\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\lvert f(U)-f(V)\rvert^{p}.

Lastly, we estimate the sum in the right-hand side.
Let
U,V\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}
such that
2U\cap 2V\neq\emptyset
, and assume without loss of generality that
f(U)\leq f(V)
.
Take a curve
\gamma_{U}
that minimizes the sum in the definition of
f(U)
. By the bounded turning condition, there is a curve
\gamma
connecting
V
to a point in
\gamma_{U}\cap U
with
\diam(\gamma)\leq 4K\varepsilon
. Since
\gamma\cup\gamma_{U}
is a competitor for
f(V)
, we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle\lvert f(U)-f(V)\rvert

\displaystyle=f(V)-f(U)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\\
W\cap(\gamma\cup\gamma_{U})\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\rho(U)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\\
W\cap\gamma_{U}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\rho(W)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\\
W\cap\gamma\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\rho(W)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\\
8KU\cap W\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\rho(W).

We can bound the number of
W\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}
in the last sum by a number depending only on
K
in the bounded turning condition and the Ahlfors regularity constants.
By summing the previous inequality over
U,V
, we get

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U,V\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\\
U\cap 2V\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\lvert f(U)-f(V)\rvert^{p}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U,V\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}\\
2U\cap 2V\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\lvert f(U)-f(V)\rvert^{p}\lesssim\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/32}}\rho(U)^{p}.

By combining this with (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), and the fact that
\rho
is an arbitrary
\mathcal{C}
-admissible function, we obtain the remaining inequality of the claim.
∎

Then we verify the ball-Loewner type estimate. A continuous version of it first appeared in Bonk and Kleiner
[
12
]
, and see also
[
14
,
46
]
for discrete variants.

Lemma 4.8
.

Suppose that Assumption
4.1
holds.
For every
A>0
there are
M,L\geq 1
such that the following holds. If
x,y\in X
and
r>0
such that
B(x,r)
and
B(y,r)
are disjoint open balls with
\dist(B(x,r),\dist(B(y,r)))\leq Ar
, and if
\mathcal{C}
is the set of curves that intersect with both
B(x,r)
and
B(y,r)
with diameter at most
Lr
, then

\Mod_{p}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon})\geq M^{-1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{r}\right)^{\beta-d_{\Hr}}

for all
0<\varepsilon\leq r
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma,K
be the constants in
\PI(\beta)
and the bounded turning condition, respectively. Let
x,y\in X
,
r>0
and
A
be as in the claim,
L:=2\sigma(A+10)
and
\mathcal{C}
be as in the claim. By
[
14
, Proposition 2.2]
, we may prove the estimates for
\Mod_{p}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/(16K)})
instead of
\Mod_{p}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon})
.
In other words, given any
\mathcal{C}
-admissible
\rho:\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/(16K)}\to[0,\infty)
, we prove a lower bound for the
p
-mass

\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/(16K)}}\rho(U)^{p}\geq M^{-1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{r}\right)^{\beta-d_{\Hr}}.

Let such
\rho
be given.
By a similar redefinition argument as in the proof of Lemma
4.2
, we may assume without loss os generality that
\rho(U)=0
for all
U
with
2U\cap B(x,3r/4)\neq\emptyset
.
Define
\mathcal{C}_{b}
be the collection of “bad curves”, consisting of those that intersect with both
B(y,\sigma(A+10)r)
and
X\setminus B(y,2\sigma(A+10)r)
. Take any
\mathcal{C}_{b}
-admissible
\rho_{b}:\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/(16K)}\to[0,\infty)
such that
\rho_{b}(U)=0
for every
U
with
U\cap B(y,\sigma(A+10)r)\neq\emptyset
. Consider
\tilde{\rho}:=\max\{\rho,\rho_{b}\}
, and define
f:\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/(16/K)}\to[0,\infty)
be given by

f(U):=\min_{\gamma}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/(16K)}\\
U\cap\gamma\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\tilde{\rho}(V)

where the minumum is taken over all curves
\gamma
that intersect with
U
and
B(x,r)\cup X\setminus B(y,2\sigma(A+10)r)
. Note that
f
satisfies

\begin{cases}f(U)=0\text{ if }2U\cap B(x,r/2)\neq\emptyset,\\
f(U)\geq 1\text{ if }2U\cap B(y,r/2)\neq\emptyset.\end{cases}

The first row holds from
\rho(U)=0=\rho_{b}(U)
when
2U\cap B(x,3r/4)
by taking any constant curve
\gamma=\{z\}\subseteq U
. The second row follows from
\tilde{\rho}\geq\rho,\rho_{b}
by noting that any
\gamma
which is a competitor for
f(U)
contains a subcurve in
\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{C}_{b}
.

Now, we use the partition of unity to construct a function

u:=\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/(16K)}}f(U)\cdot\psi_{U}.

By a similar computation as in (
4.6
), we have

\Gamma\langle u\rangle(B(y,\sigma(A+4)r))\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/(16K)}\\
2U\cap B(y,\sigma(A+4)r)\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/(16K)}\\
2U\cap 2V\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\lvert f(U)-f(V)\rvert^{p}\varepsilon^{d_{\textrm{H}}-\beta}.

If
U,V
are as above, then

\lvert f(U)-f(V)\rvert^{p}\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/(16K)}\\
8KU\cap W\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\tilde{\rho}(W)^{p}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/(16K)}\\
8KU\cap W\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\rho(W)^{p}.

The inequality follows from the same argument as in (
4.7
), and the equality from
\rho_{b}(W)=0
whenever
W
is as above.
By the bounded overlapping, the previous two displays yield

\Gamma\langle u\rangle(B(y,\sigma(A+4)r))\lesssim\varepsilon^{d_{\textrm{H}}-\beta}\sum_{U\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon/(16K)}}\rho(U)^{p}.

The left-hand side in the previous inequality is estimated using
\PI(\beta)

\int_{B(y,(A+4)r)}\lvert u-u_{B(y,(A+4)r)}\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}\mu\lesssim r^{\beta}\Gamma\langle u\rangle(B(y,\sigma(A+4)r)).

By the properties of
f
and the definition of
u
, we have
u=0
on
B(x,r/2)
and
u=1
on
B(y,r/2)
. This gives

r^{d_{\textrm{H}}}\lesssim\frac{\min\{\mu(B(x,r/2)),\mu(B(y,r/2))\}}{2^{p}}\lesssim\int_{B(y,(A+4)r)}\lvert u-u_{B(y,(A+4)r)}\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}\mu.

The combination of the previous three displays yields the desired lower-bound for the
p
-mass of
\rho
, which completes the proof.
∎

.

Suppose that Assumption

.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
and Lemma

, the metric space

is Combinatorially

-Loewner in the sense of Bourdon–Kleiner

]
. The claim now follows from a characterization of the conformal dimension due to Carrasco-Piaggio

∎

4.2.
Auxiliary Sobolev spaces

The remainder of the section works in the setting of Assumption
4.1
under the additional condition
d_{\textrm{H}}=\beta
. By Corollary
4.9
,
p
is then equal to the conformal dimension of
(X,d)
. In order to highlight this, we replace
p
with
Q
.

Suppose that Assumption
4.1
and
d_{\textrm{H}}=\beta
hold.
A collection
(\rho,\nu,\Xi,\mathcal{W})
is called an
auxiliary
Q
-Sobolev space
if the following hold.

(1)

\rho
is a metric on
X
so that it is quasisymmetric to
d
.

(2)

\nu
is a doubling measure on
(X,\rho)
.

(3)

\Xi:L^{Q}(X,\nu)\to[0,\infty]
is given by

\Xi(f):=\sup_{r>0}\int_{X}\mathchoice{\mathop{\kern 1.99997pt\vrule width=6.00006pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.49997pt\kern-8.00003pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-3.27773ptB_{\rho}(x,r)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\rho}(x,r)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\rho}(x,r)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\rho}(x,r)}}\frac{\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert^{Q}}{\nu(B_{\rho}(x,r))}\text{d}\nu(y)\,\text{d}\nu(x).

(4)

\mathcal{W}=L^{Q}(X,\nu)\cap\Xi^{-1}([0,\infty))
and it is given the norm

\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}:=\lVert f\rVert_{L^{Q}(X,\nu)}+\Xi(f)^{\frac{1}{Q}}.

The
Q
-energy
\Xi
can be understood as a variant of a Korevaar–Schoen energy. See
[
50
,
9
]
and therein references.
The results of the section show that auxiliary
Q
-Sobolev spaces are all equivalent to the each other.
We call this a
“conformal invariance”
because doubling measures are invariant under quasisymmetric homeomorphisms.
The important special case where the attainment is realized is also studied.
See
[
28
, Section 7]
and references therein for related results.
Our methods are influenced by a work of Murugan and Shimizu
[
46
, Section 9]
where a similar result on the Sierpiński carpet is established.

Let us remark that we often simultaneously work with two metrics
d
and
\rho
, and two measures
\mu
and
\nu
.
The intended one in each instance will be clear from the notation. For instance, we denote the open balls
B_{d}(x,r)
and
B_{\rho}(x,r)
and the integral averages
f_{A,\mu}
and
f_{A,\nu}
.

4.3.
Conformal invariance

In the following proposition, we only consider continuous functions to avoid the technical detail that the reference measures are different.

Proposition 4.10
.

Suppose that Assumption
4.1
holds, and assume
d_{\textup{H}}=\beta
.
Let
(\rho,\nu,\Xi,\mathcal{W})
be an auxiliary
Q
-Sobolev space. Then
\mathcal{F}\cap C(X)=\mathcal{W}\cap C(X)
. Moreover, there is a constant
C\geq 1
such that, for all
f\in\mathcal{F}\cap C(X)
, we have

C^{-1}\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{F}}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}.

A key ingredient in the proof of Proposition
4.10
is a two-measure Poincaré inequality. In proofs below, we emphasize the reference measure by writing
f_{A,\mu}
when computing averages.

Lemma 4.11
.

Assume the hypotheses of Proposition
4.10
.
There are
C,\kappa\geq 1
such that, for all
x\in X
,
s>0
and
f\in\mathcal{F}\cap C(X)
, we have

\int_{B_{\rho}(x,s)}\lvert f-f_{B_{\rho}(x,s),\mu}\rvert^{Q}\,\textup{d}\nu\leq C\nu(B_{\rho}(x,s))\int_{B_{\rho}(x,\kappa s)}\,\textup{d}\Gamma\langle f\rangle.

Proof.

It follows from
[
8
, Corollary 5.5]
that, for all
x\in X
,
s>0
and
f\in\mathcal{F}\cap C(X)
, we have

(4.12)

\int_{B_{d}(x,r)}\lvert f-f_{B_{d}(x,r),\mu}\rvert^{Q}\,\textup{d}\nu\leq C\nu(B_{d}(x,r))\int_{B_{d}(x,\sigma r)}\,\text{d}\Gamma\langle f\rangle.

The constants
C,\sigma
are independent of
x\in X
,
s>0
and
f\in\mathcal{F}
. The hypotheses of
[
8
, Corollary 5.5]
follow from Assumption
4.1
and
d_{\textrm{H}}=\beta
.
This is not yet the desired two-measure Poincaré inequality because the metric is not the correct one.
To fix this, we use the fact that quasisymmetries almost preserves open balls.

Suppose that
d
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric to
\rho
, and let
x\in X
,
s>0
and
L\geq 1
.
We first show that

(4.13)

B_{\rho}(x,s)\subseteq B_{d}(x,2r)\text{ and }B_{d}(x,2Lr)\subseteq B_{\rho}(x,\kappa s)

where

r:=\sup_{y\in B_{\rho}(x,s)}d(x,y)\text{ and }\kappa:=\frac{1}{\eta^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{4L}\right)}.

The first inclusion in (
4.13
) is obvious.
Let
y\in B_{d}(x,2Lr)
and take any
z\in B_{\rho}(x,s)
so that
d(x,z)\geq r/2
.
By using the fact that
d
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric to
\rho
we have

\frac{1}{4L}\leq\frac{d(x,z)}{d(x,y)}\leq\eta\left(\frac{\rho(x,z)}{\rho(x,y)}\right)<\eta\left(\frac{s}{\rho(x,y)}\right).

Since
\eta
is an increasing homeomorphism, we get
\rho(x,y)\leq\kappa s
, and hence the second inclusion in (
4.13
) holds.

Let
x,s
and
f
be as in the claim, and
r,\kappa
as in (
4.13
) for
L=\sigma
. We have

\displaystyle\quad\,\int_{B_{\rho}(x,s)}\lvert f-f_{B_{\rho}(x,s),\mu}\rvert^{Q}\,\text{d}\nu\leq\int_{B_{d}(x,2r)}\lvert f-f_{B_{\rho}(x,s),\mu}\rvert^{Q}\,\text{d}\nu

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\mathop{\kern 1.99997pt\vrule width=6.00006pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.49997pt\kern-8.00003pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-3.27773ptB_{\rho}(x,\varepsilon)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\rho}(x,\varepsilon)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\rho}(x,\varepsilon)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B_{\rho}(x,\varepsilon)}}\frac{\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert^{Q}}{\nu(B_{\rho}(x,\varepsilon))}\,\text{d}\nu(y)\geq\frac{1}{\nu(B_{\rho}(x,\varepsilon))}\int_{B_{\rho}(z,\varepsilon/8)}\frac{(\varepsilon/16)^{Q}}{\nu(B_{\rho}(x,\varepsilon))}\,\text{d}\nu\geq C.

We combine this with Proposition

to obtain
\Gamma\langle f\rangle(U)\geq D^{-1}\nu(U)

∎

5.
Singularity of energy measures

This section regards the following singularity result of energy measures.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose that Assumption
4.1
holds, and let
\Lambda
be any minimal energy dominant measure of
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F},\Gamma)
. If additionally
(X,d)
is geodesic and
\beta>p
, then
\Lambda\perp\mu
.

The singularity of energy measures has its origins in the theory of diffusion on fractals.
The first result to this direction was obtained by Kusuoka on the Sierpiński gasket
[
42
]
, and later Hino generalized it to more general fractals
[
31
]
.
In the full generality of Dirichlet forms on metric spaces, the singularity result was settled by Kajino and Murugan
[
33
]
. By using a similar argument, Yang extended the singularity to
p
-energies
[
53
]
.
In fact,
[
53
]
essentially covers Proposition
5.1
, but we decided to provide a detailed proof since therein assumptions slightly differ from ours. For instance, we do not assume a Clarkson’s inequality.

For the convenience of the reader not familiar with the singularity, we discuss a very simple special case of Proposition
5.1
which seems to provide quite accurate intuition for the general case. Suppose that
\Gamma\langle f\rangle\sim C\mu
for
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
C>0
.
By a two-point estimate argument, see
[
25
, Theorem 3.2]
, one can apply
\PI(\beta)
to show that
f
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous for
\alpha=\beta/p>1
.
Then, by composing
f
with geodesic curves, one finds by differentiating that
f
must be constant. Hence
\Gamma\langle f\rangle=0
by the strong locality, which is a contradiction to
C>0
.

Then in the general case, we do a blow-up argument at a density point of
\Gamma\langle f\rangle
with respect to
\mu
.
By the Lebesgue differentiation theorem,
\Gamma\langle f\rangle
behaves like
\mu
near such points. By taking care of the error term and the potential singular part, we can perform a conceptually quite similar proof scheme as in the previous special case.

Lastly, let us note that Proposition
5.1
is false in general if
(X,d)
is not geodesic. For instance, consider the
p
-Dirichlet space
(\mathbb{R}^{n},d,\text{d}x,\mathcal{E},W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\Gamma)
so that
\mathcal{E}
is the usual Dirichlet
p
-energy and
\text{d}\Gamma\langle f\rangle=\lvert\nabla f\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}x
. The metric
d
is chosen to be a snowflaked Euclidean metric.
The conclusion is of the proposition is obviously false, and it is easy to check that the hypotheses of Proposition
5.1
are all satisfied except that
(\mathbb{R}^{n},d)
is not geodesic.

5.1.
Infinitesimal harmonicity

Our argument in the proof of Proposition
5.1
is essentially the same as those in
[
33
,
53
]
, but we streamline some of the details by adopting a method of Cheeger about infinitesimal harmonicty of Sobolev functions
[
18
, Theorem 3.7]
.
Let us note that, for the following lemma, we essentially only need the Vitali covering property of
\mu
, see
[
27
, Theorem 3.4.3]
, and the weak lower semicontinuity.
An analogous fact was pointed out in the beginning of Cheeger’s paper.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose that Assumption
4.1
holds, and let
f\in\mathcal{F}\cap C(X)
. It holds for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
that

\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\left(\frac{1}{\mu(\overline{B}(x,r))}\int_{\overline{B}(x,r)}\,\textup{d}\Gamma\langle f\rangle-\inf_{\varphi}\frac{1}{\mu(\overline{B}(x,r))}\int_{\overline{B}(x,r)}\,\textup{d}\Gamma\langle f+\varphi\rangle\right)=0,

where the infimum is taken over all
\varphi\in\mathcal{F}
with
\varphi=0
in
X\setminus B(x,r)
.

Proof.

We make a counter-assumption that the conclusion is false. In that case, there is
\varepsilon>0
and a Borel subset
K\subseteq X
with
\mu(K)>0
so that the following holds. For every
z\in K
there are sequences
\{r_{z,i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq(0,1)
and
\{\varphi_{z,i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
with
r_{z,i}\downarrow 0
,
\varphi_{i,z}=0
in
X\setminus B(z,r_{z,i})
and

(5.3)

\frac{1}{\mu(\overline{B}(z,r_{z,i}))}\int_{\overline{B}(z,r_{z,i})}\,\textup{d}\Gamma\langle f\rangle\geq\varepsilon+\frac{1}{\mu(\overline{B}(z,r_{z,i}))}\int_{\overline{B}(z,r_{z,i})}\,\textup{d}\Gamma\langle f+\varphi_{z,i}\rangle.

By the Vitali covering property, for every
\delta>0
there is a countable subset
J\subseteq K
such that the following holds. There is a collection of closed balls
\{\overline{B}(z,r_{z})\}_{z\in J}
and a collection of functions
\{\varphi_{z}\}_{z\in J}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
such that
r_{z}=r_{z,i}
and
\varphi_{z}=\varphi_{z,i}
for some
i
which may depend on
z
, and
r_{z}<\delta
for all
z\in J
. In addition, they are chosen to cover almost all of
K
. But then we can choose a finite subcollection that covers at least half of
K
. In other words, there is a finite subset
J_{0}\subseteq J
with

2\mu\left(\bigcup_{z\in J_{0}}\overline{B}(z,r_{z})\right)\geq\mu(K).

Now, fix
\delta>0
and the data as above. By modifying
\varphi_{z}
if necessary, we may assume that
m_{z}\leq f+\varphi_{z}\leq M_{z}
in
B(z,r_{z})
where
m_{z},M_{z}
are the minimum and maximum of
f
in
B(z,2r_{z})
, respectively. For instance, we can use the continuity of
f
to construct
\tilde{\varphi}_{z}\in\mathcal{F}
so that

\tilde{\varphi}_{z}=\begin{cases}-f+\max\{\min\{f+\varphi_{z},m_{z}\},M_{z}\}&\text{ in }B(z,r_{z})\\
0&\text{ elsewhere}.\end{cases}

Note that the estimate (
5.3
) holds if we replace
\varphi_{z}
with
\tilde{\varphi}_{z}
by the Lipschitz contractivity of the
p
-Dirichlet space. Then consider the function

f_{\delta}:=f+\sum_{z\in J_{0}}\varphi_{z}\in\mathcal{F}.

By the strong locality and (
5.3
), its energy can be estimated

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f_{\delta})

\displaystyle=\int_{X\setminus\bigcup_{z\in J_{0}}\overline{B}(z,r_{z})}\,\text{d}\Gamma\langle f\rangle+\sum_{z\in J_{0}}\int_{\overline{B}(z,r_{z})}\text{d}\Gamma\langle f+\varphi_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\int_{X\setminus\bigcup_{z\in J_{0}}\overline{B}(z,r_{z})}\,\text{d}\Gamma\langle f\rangle+\sum_{z\in J_{0}}\int_{\overline{B}(z,r_{z})}\text{d}\Gamma\langle f\rangle-\varepsilon\sum_{z\in J_{0}}\mu(\overline{B}(z,r_{z}))

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}(f)-\frac{\varepsilon\mu(K)}{2}.

Since
f
is continuous and
X
is assumed to be compact,
f_{\delta}\to f
uniformly. For this to hold, the modifications of
\varphi_{z}
are necessary.
By sending
\delta\downarrow 0
, the weak lower semicontinuity yields a contradiction
\mathcal{E}(f)\leq\mathcal{E}(f)-\varepsilon\mu(K)/2
.
∎

5.2.
Proof of the singularity

Our proof uses a cutoff Sobolev inequality. See
[
24
, Introduction]
for further background.

Lemma 5.4
.

Assume the hypotheses of Lemma
5.1
, and let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
There is
C\geq 1
, depending on
\delta
, such that for every
x\in X
and
r>0
there exists
\varphi\in\mathcal{F}
satisfying
0\leq\varphi\leq 1
,
\varphi=1
in
B(x,(1-\delta)r)
,
\varphi=0
in
X\setminus B(x,r)
and the following cutoff Sobolev inequality
\CS(\Psi)
. If
f\in\mathcal{F}\cap C(X)
then

\int_{\overline{B}(x,(1+\delta)r)}\lvert f\rvert^{p}\,\textup{d}\Gamma\langle\varphi\rangle\leq C\left(\int_{\overline{B}(x,(1+\delta)r)\setminus B(x,(1-\delta)r)}\,\textup{d}\Gamma\langle f\rangle+\frac{1}{r^{\beta}}\int_{B(x,2r)}\lvert f\rvert^{p}\,\textup{d}\mu\right).

Proof.

By
[
24
, Lemma 2.13 and Theorem 3.3]
we have the following. There are
C,\sigma\geq 1
such that for every
B=B(x,r)
there exists
\varphi_{B}\in\mathcal{F}
satisfying
0\leq\varphi_{B}\leq 1
,
\varphi_{B}=1
in
B
,
\varphi_{B}=0
in
X\setminus 2B
and

\int_{B}\lvert f\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}\Gamma\langle\varphi_{B}\rangle\leq C\left(\int_{\sigma B}\text{d}\Gamma\langle f\rangle+\frac{1}{r^{\beta}}\int_{2B}\lvert f\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}\mu\right).

Now, fix
x\in X
and
r>0
.
Let
\lambda\geq 4
be a large enough constant depending on
\delta,\sigma
, and take an
(r/\lambda)
-net
\mathcal{N}
of
B(x,r)
.
For each
z\in\mathcal{N}
let
\varphi_{z}:=\varphi_{B(z,r/\lambda)}
be as given above, and let
\varphi:=\max_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\varphi_{z}\in\mathcal{F}
.
By choosing
\lambda
to be large enough, we have the implication
\varphi=1
on
B(z,r/\lambda)
unless
B(z,\sigma r/\lambda)\subseteq\overline{B}(x,(1+\delta)r)\setminus B(x,(1-\delta)r)
.
The claimed cutoff Sobolev inequality now follows from the strong locality by applying
\Gamma\langle\varphi\rangle\leq\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\Gamma\langle\varphi_{z}\rangle
.

∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Assume the hypotheses of Proposition
5.1
, and let
f\in\mathcal{F}\cap C(X)
. Then
\Gamma\langle f\rangle\perp\mu
.

Proof.

Suppose that the conclusion of the lemma is false. In other words, in the Lebesgue decomposition
\Gamma\langle f\rangle=\mu_{a}+\mu_{s}
where
\mu_{a}\ll\mu
and
\mu_{s}\perp\mu
, it holds that
\mu_{a}\neq 0
.

The main method of the proof is a (quantitative) blow-up argument, and the first step is to choose the base point.
Let
x\in X
so that the conclusion of Lemma
5.2
and the following hold. If
h
is the Radon–Nikodym derivative of
\mu_{a}
w.r.t.
\mu
then

(5.6)

\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\mathchoice{\mathop{\kern 1.99997pt\vrule width=6.00006pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.49997pt\kern-8.00003pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-3.27773ptB(x,r)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B(x,r)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B(x,r)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{B(x,r)}}\lvert h-h(x)\rvert\,\text{d}\mu=0\text{ and }\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu_{s}(B(x,r))}{\mu(B(x,r))}=0.

This is a standard property of doubling measures on metric spaces.
After a rescaling, we assume without loss of generality that
h(x)=1
.

Since
(X,d)
is geodesic and
\mu
is doubling, it follows from the annular decay estimate
[
27
, Proposition 11.5.3]
that, for all
r>0
and
\delta>0
, we have

(5.7)

\mu(\overline{B}(x,(1+\delta)r)\setminus B(x,(1-\delta)r))\leq C_{1}\delta^{\alpha}\mu(B(x,r)).

For the remainder of the the proof, we fix
\delta\in(0,1/8)
so that the coefficient in the right-hand side is suitably small. We will need to take care with constants, and we denote by
C_{i}
constants that only depend on the doubling constant of the measure and
p
.

The second step is to choose a suitably scale in the blow-up procedure.
Fix a large constant
\lambda\geq 8\delta^{-1}
and a suitably small
\varepsilon>0
depending on
\lambda
. The precise values of
\delta,\lambda,\varepsilon
are determined at the end of the proof.
By (
5.6
) and Ahlfors regularity (
2.1
), for all small enough
r>0
and all
y\in B(x,2r)
, we have

(5.8)

\left|\frac{\Gamma\langle f\rangle(B(y,3\sigma r/\lambda))}{\mu(B(y,3\sigma r/\lambda))}-1\right|\leq C_{2}\lambda^{d_{\textrm{H}}}\varepsilon.

The third step is to find a suitably competitor as in Lemma
5.2
.
Write
\overline{B}:=\overline{B}(x,(1+\delta)r)
and
A:=\overline{B}(x,(1+\delta)r)\setminus B(x,(1-\delta)r)
.
By the cutoff Sobolev inequality in Lemma
5.4
there is
\varphi\in\mathcal{F}\cap C(X)
such that
0\leq\varphi\leq 1
,
\varphi=1
in
B(x,(1-\delta)r)
,
\varphi=0
in
X\setminus B(x,r)
and

(5.9)

\int_{\overline{B}}\lvert h\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}\Gamma\langle\varphi\rangle\leq C_{3}\left(\int_{A}\text{d}\Gamma\langle h\rangle+\frac{1}{r^{\beta}}\int_{B(x,2r)}\lvert h\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}\mu\right)

for all
h\in\mathcal{F}\cap C(X)
.

We now begin the actual computations. First estimate

\displaystyle\quad\Gamma\langle f+(f_{B(x,3r/\lambda)}-f)\varphi\rangle(\overline{B})=\Gamma\langle f+(f_{B(x,3r/\lambda)}-f)\varphi\rangle(A)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad 2^{p-1}\left(\Gamma\langle f\rangle(A)+\Gamma\langle(f_{B(x,3r/\lambda)}-f)\varphi\rangle(A)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad 4^{p-1}\left(\Gamma\langle f\rangle(A)+\int_{A}\lvert f-f_{B(x,3r/\lambda)}\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}\Gamma\langle\varphi\rangle+\int_{A}\lvert\varphi\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}\Gamma\langle f\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad 4^{p}\left(\Gamma\langle f\rangle(A)+\int_{\overline{B}}\lvert f-f_{B(x,3r/\lambda)}\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}\Gamma\langle\varphi\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad 4^{p}C_{3}\left(\Gamma\langle f\rangle(A)+\frac{1}{r^{\beta}}\int_{B(x,2r)}\lvert f-f_{B(x,3r/\lambda)}\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}\mu\right).

The first row follows from the strong locality, and the second from the triangle inequality and
\lvert a+b\rvert^{p}\leq 2^{p-1}(\lvert a\rvert^{p}+\lvert b\rvert^{p})
.
We used the Leibniz rule in the third step, cf.
[
22
, Lemma 2.18]
,
\lvert\varphi\rvert\leq 1
in the fourth step, and (
5.9
) in the fifth step.

The second term on the right hand side is estimated as follows. Let
\mathcal{N}\subseteq B(x,2r)
be an
(r/\lambda)
-net. Since
(X,d)
is geodesic, we find for each
z\in\mathcal{N}
a sequence
\{y_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}
such that
y_{0}=x
,
y_{k}=z
,
d(y_{i},y_{i+1})\leq r/\lambda
for all
i
and
k\leq 2\lambda
.
Using this, we have

\displaystyle\quad\,\int_{B(z,r/\lambda)}\lvert f-f_{B(x,3r/\lambda)}\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}\mu

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad 2^{p-1}\left(\int_{B(z,r/\lambda)}\lvert f-f_{B(z,3r/\lambda)}\rvert^{p}+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lvert f_{B(y_{i-1},3r/\lambda)}-f_{B(y_{i},3r/\lambda)}\rvert\,\right)^{p}\text{d}\mu\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad C_{4}\mu(B(z,r/\lambda))\left(\frac{r}{\lambda}\right)^{\beta}\left(\frac{\Gamma\langle f\rangle(B(z,3\sigma r/\lambda))}{\mu(B(z,3\sigma r/\lambda))}+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left\{\frac{\Gamma\langle f\rangle(B(y_{i},3\sigma r/\lambda))}{\mu(B(y_{i},3\sigma r/\lambda))}\right\}^{\frac{1}{p}}\right)^{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad C_{5}(\lambda^{d_{\textrm{H}}}\varepsilon+1)\mu(B(z,r/\lambda))k^{p}\left(\frac{r}{\lambda}\right)^{\beta}\leq 2C_{5}(\lambda^{d_{\textrm{H}}}\varepsilon+1)r^{\beta}\lambda^{p-\beta}\mu(B(z,r/\lambda)).

We used
\PI(\beta)
in the third row and (
5.8
) in the fourth.
By summing over all
z\in\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle\quad\,\frac{1}{r^{\beta}}\int_{B(x,r)}\lvert f-f_{B(x,3r/\lambda)}\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}\mu\leq\frac{1}{r^{\beta}}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\int_{B(z,r/\lambda)}\lvert f-f_{B(x,3r/\lambda)}\rvert^{p}\,\text{d}\mu

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\quad 2C_{5}(\lambda^{d_{\textrm{H}}}\varepsilon+1)\lambda^{p-\beta}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\mu(B(z,r/\lambda))\leq C_{6}(\lambda^{d_{\textrm{H}}}\varepsilon+1)\lambda^{p-\beta}\mu(\overline{B}).

We used bounded overlapping in the the last inequality.

Then estimate
\Gamma\langle f\rangle
using (
5.6
) and (
5.7
).
By taking
\mathcal{N}
to be a
(3\sigma r/\lambda)
-net of
A

\displaystyle\Gamma\langle f\rangle(A)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\Gamma\langle f\rangle(B(z,3\sigma r/\lambda))

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}(\lambda^{d_{\textrm{H}}}\varepsilon+1)\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\mu(B(z,3\sigma r/\lambda))

\displaystyle\leq C_{7}(\lambda^{d_{\textrm{H}}}\varepsilon+1)\mu(\overline{B}(x,(1+2\delta)r)\setminus B(x,(1-\delta/2)r))

\displaystyle\leq C_{8}(\lambda^{d_{\textrm{H}}}\varepsilon+1)\delta^{\alpha}\mu(\overline{B}).

By combining the estimates derived during the proof, we have

\Gamma\langle f+(f_{B(x,3r/\lambda)}-f)\varphi\rangle(\overline{B})\leq C_{9}(\lambda^{d_{\textrm{H}}}\varepsilon+1)\delta^{\alpha}\mu(\overline{B})+C_{10}(\lambda^{d_{\textrm{H}}}\varepsilon+1)\lambda^{p-\beta}\mu(\overline{B}).

We now fix the choices of our constants.
First,
\delta\in(0,1/8)
is chosen so that

. Then choose

so that

.
Note that here it is essential that

.
Lastly, choose

On the other hand, by (

) and the fact that

, we can choose

so that

.
Then we have

\Gamma\langle f+(f_{B(x,3r/\lambda)}-f)\varphi\rangle(\overline{B})\leq\frac{\mu(\overline{B})}{2}\leq\Gamma\langle f\rangle(\overline{B})-\frac{\mu(\overline{B})}{8}.

Since these choice are valid for arbitrarily small

∎

Proof of Proposition

.

,
(Y,d_{Y})
are geodesic and uniformly scalable, then
(Z,d)
does not attain its conformal dimension.

Taking Theorem
6.2
as given, we prove Theorem
6.1
as follows.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

By repeatedly applying Theorem
3.5
, we obtain a
(Q,\beta)
-Poincaré–Dirichlet space
(Y,d_{Y},\mu_{Y},\mathcal{E}_{Y},\mathcal{F}_{Y},\Gamma_{Y})
so that
(Y,d_{Y})
is the
(k-1)
-fold Cartesian product of
(X,d_{X})
.
It is routine verify the conditions in Assumption
4.1
, and that the Hausdorff dimension of
(Y,d_{Y})
is equal to
d_{\textrm{H},Y}:=(k-1)\cdot d_{\textrm{H}}
.
Hence, if
(Z,d)
is the
k
-fold Cartesian product, it follows from Theorem
6.2
that
Q
is the conformal dimension of
(Z,d)
.

Since
(X,d_{X})
is geodesic, so is
(Y,d_{Y})
.
It is direct to check that
(Y,d_{Y})
is uniformly scalable if
(X,d_{X})
is.
Since
\beta>Q
by the hypotheses, the non-attainment of the
k
-fold product
(Z,d)
now follows from Theorem
6.2
.

∎

6.1.
Blow-up procedure

The remaining objective of the work is the proof of Theorem
6.2
.
Most of the techniques required have already been developed in the previous sections. Our argument requires one additional ingredient, which is a suitable blow-up method.
In the following definition, we introduce a general self-similarity condition under which we can take nice limits along blow-up sequences.
This condition is essentially a variant of the notion of approximate self-similarity introduced in
[
41
]
.

Definition 6.3
.

We say that a metric space
(X,d)
is
uniformly scalable
if there is a constant
L\geq 1
such that, for every
x\in X
and
\varepsilon>0
, there is
\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}:X\to B(x,\varepsilon)
satisfying the following conditions.

(1)

x\in\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}(X)
.

(2)

For every
y\in X
and
r\in(0,2]
there is
z\in X
such that

\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}(B(y,r))\supseteq B(z,\varepsilon r/L).

(3)

For every
y,z\in X
we have

L^{-1}\frac{d(y,z)}{\varepsilon}\leq d(\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}(y),\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}(z))\leq L\frac{d(y,z)}{\varepsilon}.

Assume the hypotheses of Theorem
6.2
.
For brevity, we denote by
\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}
the maps provided by the uniform scalability, regardless whether the ambient space is
X
or
Y
.
For
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in Z
we define the map
\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}:Z\to B(z,\varepsilon)
by

(y_{1},y_{2})\mapsto(\Phi_{x_{1},\varepsilon}(y_{1}),\Phi_{x_{2},\varepsilon}(y_{2})).

Recall that a Radon measure has
full topological support
if every non-empty open set has positive measure.
If
m
is a Radon measure on
(Z,d)
of full topological support, we denote the normalized push-forward measure by

\mathrm{m}_{x,\varepsilon}:=\frac{(\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}^{-1})_{*}(\textrm{m})}{\textrm{m}(\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}(Z))}.

Lemma 6.4
.

Suppose that the hypotheses of Theorem
6.2
hold.
Let
\Lambda_{X}
be a Radon measure on
(X,d_{X})
with full topological support,
\textup{m}:=\Lambda_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}
,
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in Z
and
\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq(0,1)
with
\varepsilon_{i}\downarrow 0
.
Then there is a subsequence
\{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and a Radon measure
\tilde{\Lambda}_{X}
on
(X,d_{X})
such that
\textup{m}_{x,\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}}
converges weakly to a Radon measure which is mutually absolutely continuous with
\tilde{\Lambda}_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}
.

Proof.

Note that
\textrm{m}_{x,\varepsilon}
can be written as

\textrm{m}_{x,\varepsilon}=\left(\frac{(\Phi_{x_{1},\varepsilon}^{-1})_{*}(\Lambda_{X})}{\Lambda_{X}(\Phi_{x_{1},\varepsilon}(X))}\right)\otimes\left(\frac{(\Phi_{x_{2},\varepsilon}^{-1})_{*}(\mu_{Y})}{\mu_{Y}(\Phi_{x_{2},\varepsilon}(Y))}\right)

By the compactness of
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
, there is a subsequence
\{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
such that the marginals of
\textrm{m}_{x,\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}}
converge weakly to some probability Radon measures
\tilde{\Lambda}_{X}
and
\tilde{\mu}_{Y}
, respectively.
Then
\textrm{m}_{x,\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}}
converges weakly to
\tilde{\Lambda}_{X}\otimes\tilde{\mu}_{Y}
. Moreover, the second marginals in the above display are uniformly comparable to the
d_{\textrm{H},Y}
-Hausdorff measure
\mu_{Y}
of
(Y,d_{Y})
.
This follows from Definition
6.3
-(3) and the
d_{\textrm{H},Y}
-Ahlfors regularity of
(Y,d_{Y})
. In particular,
\tilde{\mu}_{Y}
is also comparable to
\mu_{Y}
, which shows the required mutual absolute continuity.
∎

Lemma 6.5
.

Suppose that the hypotheses of Theorem
6.2
hold.
Let
\rho
be a metric on
Z
, quasisymmetric to
d
, so that
(Z,\rho)
is
Q
-Ahlfors regular and
\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq(0,1)
with
\varepsilon_{i}\downarrow 0
. Let
\nu
be the
Q
-Hausdorff measure of
(Z,\rho)
and
x\in Z
.
Then there is a metric
\tilde{\rho}
on
Z
, quasisymmetric to
d
, a Radon probability measure
\tilde{\nu}
on
(Z,d)
, and a subsequence
\{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
so that the following hold.
The metric space
(Z,\tilde{\rho})
is
Q
-Ahlfors regular, the Radon measures
\nu_{x,\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}}
converge weakly to
\tilde{\nu}
and
\tilde{\nu}
is comparable to the
Q
-Hausdorff measure of
(Z,\tilde{\rho})
.

Proof.

The subscript
x
is dropped during the proof to ease the notation. We also denote
D_{\varepsilon}:=\diam_{\rho}(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(Z))
.
The constant
L
is as in Definition
6.3
, and
\rho
is assumed to be
\eta_{0}
-quasisymmetric to
d
.
Without loss of generality, we assume
\diam_{d}(Z)=1
.

The metric
\tilde{\rho}
is constructed first.
For
\varepsilon>0
consider the pull-back metrics

\rho_{\varepsilon}(y,z):=D_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\rho(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(y),\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z)).

Then the metric spaces
(Z,\rho_{\varepsilon})
have diameters equal to 1.
Moreover, a direct computation shows that
\rho_{\varepsilon}
are
\eta
-quasisymmetric to
d
where
\eta
only depends on
\eta_{0}
and
L
.
Using this and
\diam_{d}(Z)=1
, we compute

\displaystyle\lvert\rho_{\varepsilon}(y_{1},z_{1})-\rho_{\varepsilon}(y_{2},z_{2})\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{\varepsilon}(y_{1},y_{2})+\rho_{\varepsilon}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\eta\left(2d(y_{1},y_{2})\right)+\eta\left(2d(z_{1},z_{2})\right).

The previous display and the continuity of
\eta
implies that
\{\rho_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}\subseteq C(Z\times Z)
is equicontinuous.
We also have a lower-bound estimate

\rho_{\varepsilon}(y,z)\geq\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\eta}\left(d(y,z)\right)

where
\tilde{\eta}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is an increasing homeomorphism depending only on
\eta
. This follows from
[
29
, Proposition 10.6]
where it is shown that quasisymmetry is a quantitative symmetric relation.
In particular,
\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\rho_{\varepsilon}(x,y)>0
whenever
x\neq y
. Now, by applying the Arzela–Ascoli theorem, we obtain a metric
\tilde{\rho}
on
Z
and a subsequence
\{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
so that
\rho_{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}}
converges uniformly to
\tilde{\rho}
.
It follows from the uniform convergence that
\tilde{\rho}
is quasisymmetric to
d
.

By taking a subsequence of
\{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
if necessary, we may assume that
\nu_{x,\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}}
has a weak limit
\tilde{\nu}
.
The remaining conclusions in the claim follow once we verify that
(X,\tilde{\rho},\tilde{\nu})
is
Q
-Ahlfors regular in the sense that there is
C\geq 1
such that

C^{-1}r^{Q}\leq\nu(B_{\tilde{\rho}}(y,r))\leq Cr^{Q}\text{ for every }x\in X\text{ and }r\in(0,2].

We first prove the analogous property for
(X,\rho_{\varepsilon},\nu_{\varepsilon})
.
Let
y,r
be as in the display. First, we note
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(Z)
contains a ball
B_{\rho}(z,\delta D_{\varepsilon})
where
\delta\in(0,1)
depends only on
L
and
\eta_{0}
. This follows from (
4.13
) by applying (2) and (3) in Definition
6.3
, and the fact that
\rho
is
\eta_{0}
-quasisymmetric to
d
.
The upper-bound is obtained from

\nu_{\varepsilon}(B_{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(y,r))=\frac{\nu(B_{\rho}(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(y),rD_{\varepsilon}))\cap\Phi_{\varepsilon}(Z)))}{\nu(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(Z))}\leq\frac{\nu(B_{\rho}(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(y),rD_{\varepsilon}))}{\nu(B_{\rho}(z,\delta D_{\varepsilon}))}\leq Cr^{Q}.

Again, by (2) and (3) in Definition
6.3
and
\rho
being
\eta_{0}
-quasisymmetric to
d
, we have

\Phi_{\varepsilon}(B_{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(y,r))\supseteq B_{\rho}(z,\delta rD_{\varepsilon})

for some
z\in X
, where
\delta\in(0,1)
depends only on
L
and
\eta_{0}
. The lower-bound now follows from

\nu_{\varepsilon}(B_{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(y,r))=\frac{\nu(B_{\rho}(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(y),rD_{\varepsilon}))\cap\Phi_{\varepsilon}(Z)))}{\nu(\Phi_{\varepsilon}(Z))}\geq\frac{\nu(B_{\rho}(z,\delta rD_{\varepsilon}))}{\nu(B_{\rho}(z,2D_{\varepsilon}))}\geq C^{-1}r^{Q}.

Finally, the desired
Q
-Ahlfors regularity follows by sending
j\to\infty
and
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}\downarrow 0
because
C
in the above inequalities does not depend on
\varepsilon
.
∎

6.2.
Proof of the non-attainment

We are finally ready to prove the non-attainment result for product spaces.

Proof of Theorem
6.2
.

Let
(Z,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F},\Gamma)
be as in Definition
3.4
where the factors are
(X,d_{X},\mu_{X},\mathcal{E}_{X},\mathcal{F}_{X},\Gamma_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y},\mu_{Y},\mathcal{E}_{Y},\mathcal{F}_{Y},\Gamma_{Y})
.
By Theorem
3.5
,
(Z,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F},\Gamma)
is a
(Q,\beta)
-Poincaré–Dirichlet space satisfying the conditions in Assumption
4.1
and its Hausdorff dimension is equal to
d_{\textup{H}}:=d_{\textup{H},X}+d_{\textup{H},Y}
.
Since
\beta=d_{\textup{H}}
by the hypotheses, it follows from Corollary
4.9
that
Q
is equal to the conformal dimension of
(Z,d)
.

The remaining objective is to prove the non-attainment result.
We proceed by contraposition, i.e., assume that there exists a metric
\rho
on
Z
, quasisymmetric to
d
, so that
(Z,\rho)
is
Q
-Ahlfors regular. The
Q
-Hausdorff measure of
(Z,\rho)
is denoted by
\nu
. We also fix a minimal energy dominant measure
\Lambda_{X}
of
(X,d_{X},\mu_{X},\mathcal{E}_{X},\mathcal{F}_{X},\Gamma_{X})
and
\Lambda_{Y}
of
(Y,d_{Y},\mu_{Y},\mathcal{E}_{Y},\mathcal{F}_{Y},\Gamma_{Y})
.
By Corollary
3.7
and Corollary
4.15
, it holds that
\textup{m}:=\Lambda_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}\ll\nu
. The Radon–Nikodym derivative of
\Lambda_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}
with respect to
\nu
is denoted by
h
.

Since
\nu
is a doubling measure on
(Z,d)
, it follows from the Lebesgue differentiation theorem that
h
has a Lebesgue point
x\in Z
where
h(x)>0
. After a rescaling, we may assume that
h(x)=1
.
By using Definition
6.3
-(2), we get

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mathchoice{\mathop{\kern 1.99997pt\vrule width=6.00006pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.49997pt\kern-8.00003pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-3.27773pt\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}(Z)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}(Z)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}(Z)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}(Z)}}\lvert h-1\rvert\,\text{d}\nu=0.

Then, for every Borel set
\Omega\subseteq Z
, we have

\lvert\nu_{x,\varepsilon}(\Omega)-\textrm{m}_{x,\varepsilon}(\Omega)\rvert\leq\frac{1}{\nu(\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}(Z))}\int_{\Omega}\lvert h-1\rvert\,\text{d}\nu\leq\mathchoice{\mathop{\kern 1.99997pt\vrule width=6.00006pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.49997pt\kern-8.00003pt\intop}\nolimits_{\kern-3.27773pt\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}(Z)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}(Z)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}(Z)}}{\mathop{\kern 1.00006pt\vrule width=5.0pt,height=3.0pt,depth=-2.59996pt\kern-6.00006pt\intop}\nolimits_{\Phi_{x,\varepsilon}(Z)}}\lvert h-1\rvert\,\text{d}\nu.

The combination of the previous two displays shows that

(6.6)

\nu_{x,\varepsilon}-\textrm{m}_{x,\varepsilon}\to 0\text{ in total variation as $\varepsilon\downarrow 0$.}

Now, we use the earlier lemmas to obtain suitable convergences.
Take any sequence
\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq(0,1)
with
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
By Lemma
6.4
, there is a Radon measure
\tilde{\Lambda}_{X}
and a subsequence
\{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
so that

(6.7)

\textrm{m}_{x,\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}}\rightharpoonup\tilde{\textrm{m}}\ll\tilde{\Lambda}_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}.

By Lemma
6.5
, there is a metric
\tilde{\rho}
, quasisymmetric to
d
, so that
(Z,\tilde{\rho})
is
Q
-Ahlfors regular. Additionally, by taking further subsequence if necessary,

(6.8)

\nu_{x,\tilde{\varepsilon}_{j}}\rightharpoonup\tilde{\nu}\text{ and }\tilde{\nu}\text{ is comparable to the $Q$-Hausdorff measure on $(Z,\tilde{\rho})$.}

Finally, we obtain the chain of absolute continuities

\displaystyle\mu_{X}\otimes\Lambda_{Y}\ll\tilde{\nu}\ll\tilde{\textup{m}}\ll\tilde{\Lambda}_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}.

The first one follows from Proposition
3.7
and Corollary
4.15
. For the second one, we simply have
\tilde{\nu}=\tilde{\textup{m}}
by Equations (
6.6
), (
6.7
) and (
6.8
). The last one is just (
6.7
).
However, since
\mu_{Y}\perp\Lambda_{Y}
by Proposition
5.1
, we also have
\mu_{X}\otimes\Lambda_{Y}\perp\tilde{\Lambda}_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}
.
This combined with the previous display gives
\mu_{X}\otimes\Lambda_{Y}=0
, which is obviously false. Hence, the metric
\rho
attaining the conformal dimension cannot exist, and this completes the proof.
∎

6.3.
Concluding remarks

The work concludes to a discussion about examples and some further discussion about Theorem
6.1
.

Remark 6.9
.

This remark explains how to verify the hypotheses of
Theorem
6.1
for the Sierpiński carpet
\mathbb{S}
.
This, in particular, proves Theorem
1.1
. First,
\mathbb{S}
is bi-Lipschitz to a geodesic metric space, and the maps in the uniform scalability condition are provided by the natural similarity maps. Thus, the geometric conditions have been checked.
Next we verify the existence of the required Dirichlet space and its properties.
By the work of Murugan and Shimizu
[
46
]
, for every
p\in(1,\infty)
there is a
(p,\beta_{p})
-Poincaré–Dirichlet space
(\mathbb{S},d_{\mathbb{S}},\mu_{\mathbb{S}},\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{S}},\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{S}},\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}})
that satisfies Assumption
4.1
for
d_{\textrm{H}}=\log(8)/\log(3)
and some
\beta_{p}\geq p
.
Note that, by the same explanation as in the remark below, we could also refer to
[
49
,
39
]
.
What remains is to check is that
\beta_{Q}=k\cdot d_{\textrm{H}}
for some
Q>1
, and that
\beta_{Q}>Q
.
First, we have
\beta_{p}>p
by
[
49
, Theorem 2.27]
.
In order to find
Q
, it suffices to show

\beta_{p}=d_{\textup{H}}\textrm{ for some }p\in(1,\infty)\text{ and }p\mapsto\beta_{p}\text{ is continuous in $p\in(1,\infty)$}.

We then find
Q
by a continuity argument because
\beta_{k\cdot d_{\textrm{H}}}>k\cdot d_{\textrm{H}}
as noted above.
Now, if
\mathcal{M}_{p}\in(0,\infty)
is as in
[
7
, Theorem 8.1]
, then
\beta_{p}=\log(8\mathcal{M}_{p}^{-1})/\log(3)
and
p\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{p}\in(0,\infty)
is continuous. Hence,
\beta_{p}
is also continuous.
Alternatively, we could also show the continuity by using the proof of
[
7
, Theorem 8.1]
and
[
14
, Lemma 4.4]
.
Note that we need to use the fact that
C
in
[
14
, Lemma 4.4]
can be chosen to be independent of
p
when
p
varies on some compact subset of
[1,\infty)
.
For the other condition in the display, note that
\beta_{p}=d_{\textrm{H}}
when
p
is equal to the conformal dimension of
\mathbb{S}
. See
[
46
, Remark 9.17]
.
Finally, the continuity can also be deduced from
[
38
, Proposition 4.7.5]
.

Remark 6.10
.

The explanation in Remark
6.9
extends to the Sierpiński gasket and many other post-critically finite self-similar sets. The Dirichlet spaces are provided, for instance, by
[
30
,
16
]
and
[
39
, Theorem 4.6]
.
See
[
35
, Section 9.2]
for details about the energy measures. Continuity of
p\mapsto\beta_{p}
follows, similarly to the previous example, from
[
7
, Theorem 8.1]
.
This discussion also extends to many Laakso type spaces constructed in
[
5
]
, such as the Laakso diamond space.

Remark 6.11
.

This remark shows that the hypotheses of Theorem
6.1
are satisfied by the 3-dimensional Menger sponge
(\mathbb{M},d_{\mathbb{M}})
.
The argument is essentially the same as in Remark
6.9
. However, let us note that currently there is no result that provides suitable
p
-Dirichlet spaces for every
p\in(1,\infty)
.
This is, nevertheless, not an issue because we need it only for specific
p
.
It is easy to check that the conformal dimensions
Q
of the
k
-fold Cartesian product
(Z,d)
, for
k\geq 2
, of the Menger sponge is strictly larger than the conformal dimension
Q_{\mathbb{M}}
of the Menger sponge itself.
In fact, since
\mathbb{M}
contains an isometric copy of the unit interval,
Q\geq Q_{\mathbb{M}}+1
by
[
45
, Example 4.1.9]
.
Hence, the existence of the required
Q
-Dirichlet space are provided by Kigami
[
39
]
and Shimizu
[
49
]
. The same argument works for every generalized Sierpiński carpet in the sense of
[
49
]
.

Remark 6.12
.

We note that Theorem
6.2
fails in general if the parameter
\beta
is different for the two factors.
This seems to be subtle detail since, for instance
\mathbb{S}\times[0,1]
attains its conformal dimension by
[
45
, Example 4.1.9]
, where
\mathbb{S}
is the Sierpiński carpet.
This shows that the conclusion of Theorem
6.2
is false if the parameter
\beta
for the two factors do not agree and
\beta>Q
for one of the factors.
On the other hand, we do not know whether
\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{M}
, where
\mathbb{M}
is the Menger sponge, attains its conformal dimension or not.
