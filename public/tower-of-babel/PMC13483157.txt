γ-Aminobutyric Acid (GABA) enhances heat tolerance in rice via integrated antioxidant defense, metabolic homeostasis and heat shock response
Authors: Ya Wang, Jialin Wang, Fang Wang, Tengke Meng, Xin Liu, Banpu Ruan, Limin Wu, Yanchun Yu, Fei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobal warming has increased the frequency and intensity of extreme heat events, posing a serious threat to crop growth and productivity. Although γ‑aminobutyric acid (GABA) is recognized as a bioactive factor functioning in plant stress adaptation, its specific role and underlying mechanisms under heat stress remain poorly understood in rice.ResultsIn this study, we demonstrate that exogenous GABA significantly enhances heat tolerance in rice seedlings in a dose‑dependent manner. Application of 0.1 mM GABA effectively alleviated heat-induced growth inhibition and mitigated oxidative damage by suppressing reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, membrane lipid peroxidation, and cell death. This protection was associated with enhanced antioxidant capacity, evidenced by up‑regulation of key antioxidant enzymes and elevated glutathione levels. Moreover, transcriptome and quantitative analysis revealed that GABA supplementation finely reconfigures the heat‑induced accumulation of specific amino acids and carbohydrates, thereby promoting metabolic homeostasis under heat stress. Background

Global climate change has increased the frequency and intensity of extreme heat events, posing a severe threat to agriculture productivity worldwide [1]. As a staple food for over half of the global population, rice (Oryza sativa L.) is highly sensitive to elevated temperatures throughout its entire growth period [2]. Unexpected heat stress can lead to membrane and photosynthetic system damage, imbalances in reactive oxygen species (ROS) and phytohormones, as well as disruption of carbohydrate metabolism. These physiological disturbances collectively suppress plant growth and development, impair pollination and fertilization processes, reduce the seed setting rates, and ultimately cause substantial declines in grain yield and quality [3, 4]. Therefore, developing effective strategies to improve heat stress tolerance in rice is critical for ensuring sustainable agriculture and food security [5].

Plants cope with heat stress through integrated physiological and molecular adaptations, including the upregulation of heat shock proteins (HSPs), activation of antioxidant systems to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), and accumulation of protective metabolites to maintain cellular homeostasis [6]. These responses are regulated by signaling pathways such as calcium (Ca2+) signaling and transcriptional regulatory networks, which collectively help mitigate heat-induced damage and improve stress resilience [7, 8]. In recent years, several key heat‑tolerance‑related genes in rice have been identified and functionally characterized, providing opportunities to develop heat-resistant cultivars through molecular breeding approaches [9, 10]. However, despite being an efficient and sustainable long-term strategy, the complexity of the regulatory networks underlying rice heat tolerance means that breeding programs utilizing these genes still require considerable time for practice implementation [11]. Given these challenges, complementary mitigation strategies, such as the application of protective compound, have garnered increasing attention as a short or mid-term effort [12, 13]. Recent studies have revealed the role of bioactive molecules, including amino acids, sugars and secondary metabolites, in enhancing plant stress resilience, offering promising avenues for more immediate and practicable interventions [14, 15]. For example, endogenous active molecules such as glycine betaine, trehalose, nitric oxide (NO), and flavonoids have been shown to play regulatory roles in plant stress responses [16–19]. Exogenous application of these molecules can enhance stress tolerance without adversely effecting plant growth under normal conditions [20–23]. A deeper understanding of the mechanisms and regulatory networks of these molecules in the alleviation heat stress will be crucial for developing innovative strategies to improve crop heat stress resilience.

Amino acids and their derivatives are key metabolites in regulating plant growth and defense against both biotic and abiotic stresses [24]. Among them, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), a non-protein amino acid, has emerged as an important metabolite involved in plant development and stress adaptation [25, 26]. In plants, GABA is primarily synthesized through the irreversible decarboxylation of glutamate catalyzed by glutamate decarboxylase (GAD), or derived from the polyamine metabolic pathway via multiple enzymatic reactions [27]. Research has shown that GABA accumulates rapidly under various stress conditions and contributes to plant stress responses by modulating several physiological processes, such as maintaining carbon-nitrogen metabolism balance, regulating osmotic and redox homeostasis, and stabilizing cellular structures [28–30]. Elevating endogenous GABA levels or applying GABA exogenously has been widely reported to improve stress tolerance across diverse plant species [31, 32]. GABA also functions as a signaling molecule that participates in stress-response regulation [33, 34]. Recent evidence suggests that plant aluminum-activated malate transporter (ALMT) anion channels act as GABA signal transducers, with their activity negatively regulated by GABA to promote physiological adaptation and stress tolerance [35–37]. Moreover, GABA signaling may interact with other active molecules, including ROS, reactive nitrogen species (RNS), phytohormones, and polyamines, to coordinate plant stress responses [38, 39]. For example, increased endogenous GABA enhances saline-alkali tolerance in tomato by promoting ROS scavenging and activating the ethylene signaling pathway [40]. Similarly, exogenous GABA supplementation alleviates arsenate toxicity in tomato and brinjal seedlings through crosstalk with NO [41]. These findings not only underscore the important role of GABA in stress adaptation, but also reveal the complexity of GABA-associated regulation networks in plant.

Although preliminary evidence suggests that GABA may contribute to heat tolerance in plants [42–45], the underlying physiological and molecular mechanisms involved remain poorly understood. In this study, we aimed to elucidate how exogenous GABA enhances heat stress tolerance in rice seedlings. We demonstrate that GABA application significantly improves heat stress tolerance by mitigating heat-induced oxidative stress, modulating the accumulation of stress-related amino acids, and regulating the HSF-HSP transcriptional pathway. These findings advance our understanding of GABA-mediated heat tolerance mechanisms in rice and support its potential as a biochemical strategy for improving crop resilience in a warming climate.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

The japonica rice variety ‘Nipponbare’ (NIP) was used in this study. Rice seeds were surfaced-sterilized with 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) and germinated on moistened filter paper in Petri dishes at 30℃ in darkness for three days. Uniformly germinated seeds were transferred into 96-well PCR plates with the bottoms removed and placed in nutrient solution for hydroponic cultivation in a growth chamber. The normal growth conditions were maintained at a 14 h light (300 µmol m− 2 s− 1) /10 h dark photoperiod, with temperatures of 28 ℃ (light) / 24 ℃ (dark), and relative humidity 65% ± 5%. The basal nutrient solution contained the following components (mg·L− 1): NH4NO3, 91.4; CaCl2, 88.6; MgSO4·7H2O, 324; K2SO4, 71.4; KH2PO4·2H2O, 40.3; Na2·EDTA·2H2O, 46.6; FeSO4·7H2O, 34.8; MnCl2·7H2O, 1.5; H3BO3, 0.934; (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O, 0.074; ZnSO4·7H2O, 0.035; CuSO4·5H2O, 0.031. The nutrient solution was renewed every three days. In a preliminary experiment, two-week-old rice seedlings grown under normal growth conditions were placed in nutrient solution supplemented with 0, 0.1, 0.5 or 1mM GABA and then transferred to a growth chamber at 45 °C for three days, followed by two days of recovery under normal growth conditions; control plants were maintained under normal growth conditions. After treatment, survival rate and shoot biomass were evaluated to identify the optimal dose of GABA. For the main experiments, two-week-old rice seedlings grown under normal growth conditions were used. GABA was added to the nutrient solution to establish two treatment groups: (1) Control: plants grown under normal growth conditions in basal nutrient solution with or without 0.1 mM GABA; (2) Heat: plants exposed to 45℃ in basal nutrient solution with or without 0.1 mM GABA. Each treatment including three biological replicates arranged randomly. The first fully expanded leaves were collected after one and three days of treatment for measurements of enzyme activities, antioxidant contents, and MDA levels. Histochemical staining and quantitative determination of H2O2 and O2·− was performed using leaves harvested after three days. Additionally, for gene expression analysis, the first fully expanded leaves were taken at 0, 0.5, 1, 3 and 6 h after treatment. For ethylene glycol tetraacetic acid (EGTA) treatment, two-week-old seedlings were pretreated with 15 mM EGTA for 2 h at normal growth conditions and subsequently exposed to 45℃ with 0.1 mM GABA to assess heat tolerance.

In the detached-leaf assay, the first fully expanded leaves from four-week-old seedlings were excised and placed in Petri dishes lined with three layers of filter paper moistened with 20 mL of distilled water, with or without 0.1 mM GABA. For oxidative stress treatments, leaf segments were similarly placed in solutions containing H2O2 (1% or 2%, v/v) or methyl viologen (MV; 5 µM or 10 µM), with or without 0.1 mM GABA; distilled water served as the control. All leaves were first incubated at 45 °C for five hours (heat treatment) or maintained under normal growth conditions (oxidative stress treatments). Subsequently, leaves were transferred to 28 °C under continuous light for a three‑day recovery period, after which phenotypic images were captured and chlorophyll content was measured.

Measurement of phenotypic parameter and chlorophyll content

Phenotypic parameters were assessed using at least 35 randomly selected plants per treatment. Survival rate was determined based on the degree of shoot withering, after which shoot fresh weight was measured. Chlorophyll content was quantified following a previously described method [46]. Briefly, leaves were cut into 5-mm segments, and approximately 0.1 g of tissue was placed in a 15-ml tube containing 10 mL of 80% ethanol (v/v). Extraction was performed in darkness for 24 h. The supernatant was then collected, and its absorbance was measured at 645 nm and 663 nm. Chlorophyll content was calculated from these absorbance values.

Histochemical staining and quantitative analysis of ROS

Histochemical staining for H2O2, O2·− and dead cells was performed using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB), nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) and trypan blue (TB), respectively, following established protocols [20, 47]. For H2O2 detection, leaf segments were immersed in 20 mM potassium phosphate buffer (PBS, pH 6.5) containing 1% DAB and 0.1% Tween 20. Samples were vacuum-infiltrated twice for 5 min each and then incubated overnight at room temperature in darkness. For O2·− detection, leaf pieces were immersed in 50 mM PBS (pH 7.5) containing 0.1% NBT, vacuum-infiltrated twice (5 min each), and similarly incubated overnight at room temperature in darkness. After incubation, leaves were decolorized by boiling in a solution of ethanol : acetic acid : glycerol (3:1:1, v/v/v) for 15 min. Cell death was examined by vacuum‑infiltrating leaf segments with 0.1% (w/v) trypan blue (TB) dissolved in 50 mM phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.5) for 5 min. The samples were then incubated in darkness on a shaker at 50 rpm for 8 h at room temperature. After staining, leaves were decolorized by incubation in 80% ethanol at 80 °C.

H2O2 and O2·⁻ levels were further quantified using commercial assay kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For H2O2 measurement, fresh leaf tissue (0.1 g) was ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in 1 mL of acetone. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Aliquot of the supernatant, together with standard and control samples, were mixed with an equal volume of H2O2 detection reagent, and centrifuged at 4,000 g for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant was discarded, and 1 ml of the test solution was added to resuspend the precipitate. After incubated at room temperature for 5 min, absorbance was measured at 415 nm using a spectrophotometer. For O2·− detection, leaf sample was homogenized in 1 mL of extraction solution and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Aliquots (500 µL) of supernatant, standards and controls were transferred to microtubes, mixed with 400 µL of working solution 1, and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Next, 300 µL each of working solution 2 and 3 were added, followed by another incubation at 37 °C for 20 min. After adding 500 µL of chloroform, samples were centrifuged at 8,000 g for 5 min at 25 °C. The upper phase was collected and the absorbance was measured immediately at 560 nm.

Determination of proline and MDA contents

Proline content was determined following Jiang et al. [48]. Approximately 0.1 g of leaf tissue (pooled from five plants) was homogenized in 1 mL 3% hydroxybenzoic acid and soaking in boiling water for 10 min. After cooling, samples were centrifuged at 8,000 g for 10 min at room temperature. A 500 µL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of acetic acid and acidic ninhydrin, and incubated at 95 °C for 40 min. Following cooling, 500 µL of toluene was added and the mixture was allowed to stratify. The upper layer was collected, centrifuged at 3,000 g for 5 min, and absorbance was measured at 520 nm. Proline content was calculated using a calibration curve prepared with standard sample. For the determination of malondialdehyde (MDA) content, fresh leaf tissue (0.1 g pooled from five plants) was homogenized in 1 mL of extraction buffer and centrifuged at 8,000 g for 10 min at 4 ◦C. An aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of working solution (0.5% thiobarbituric acid made in 5% trichloroacetic acid, v/v) and heated in boiling water for 15 min. After cooling, the mixture was centrifuged at 8,000×g for 15 min, and absorbance at 532 and 600 nm was recorded, and the MDA content was calculated using an established formula [49]. Each analysis was independently repeated three times.

Determination of antioxidants

Antioxidant enzyme activities were determined using fresh leaf samples (0.1 g pooled from five plants) homogenized in 3 mL of ice-cold PBS buffer (50 mM, pH 7.8). After centrifugation at 10,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected for enzyme assays. Activities of ascorbate peroxidase (APX), superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT) were determined following previously described methods [49]. Glutathione (GSH) content was measured using a commercial assay kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the instructions. Briefly, 0.1 g of leaf tissue was homogenized in 5 ml of extraction buffer on ice. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4℃, and the supernatant was used for analysis. For GSH quantification, 20 µL of supernatant, standard or control (distilled water) was mixed with 180 µL working solution and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Absorbance was then measured at 412 nm, and GSH content was calculated based on a standard curve. Each analysis was independently repeated three times.

Determination of amino acids and carbohydrates

The determination of free amino acids was analyzed according to the national standard method GB/T 30,987 − 2020 for plant samples. Fresh leaf tissue was first ground into a fine powder under liquid nitrogen. A 0.5 g aliquot of the powder was then extracted with 5 mL of sulfosalicylic acid by shaking at room temperature for 1 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm aqueous-phase membrane. The filtrate was subsequently injected into an automatic amino acid analyzer (Hitachi L-8900, Tokyo, Japan) for quantification. Total sugar, soluble sugar, sucrose and trehalose were extracted and measured with corresponding commercial kits (Comin, Suzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

RNA extraction, RT-qPCR analysis and RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was isolated from leaf tissues of rice seedlings using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). First-strand cDNA was synthesized with the HiFiScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (CWBIO, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis was performed in a CFX96 Touch System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using Magic SYBR Mixture (CWBIO, Beijing, China) with the reaction cycling conditions as follow: 95℃ for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ for 5 s, and 60℃ for 10 s. Gene expression was calculated via the 2−ΔΔCT method with three technical replicates and three independent biological replicates. The rice OsActin gene (LOC_Os03g50885) served as the internal reference, and primers sequences are provided in Table S1. For RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis, leaves were collected from two-week-old seedlings grown at 28℃ (control) and exposed to 45℃ (heat stress) for 3 h. The first fully expanded leaves from five seedlings were pooled as one biological replicate, and three independent replicates were prepared per treatment. Library construction, sequencing and data analysis was conducted by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Different expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using an adjusted false discovery rat (FDR) ≤ 0.05 and a fold change |log2Ratio| ≥ 1. Bioinformatics analysis was conducted using the OmicShare tools (www.omicshare.com/tools).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the Data Processing System software package (DPS, version v21.05). Differences between treatments were evaluated by Student’s t‑test or one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD test [50]. All experiments were repeated at least three times, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Results

Exogenous GABA significantly improves heat stress tolerance in rice seedlings

To investigate the effect of GABA on heat stress tolerance, two-week-old rice seedlings grown in nutrient solution at normal temperature (28℃) were treated with different concentrations of GABA (0.1, 0.5, or 1 mM) and subsequently subjected to 45℃ heat treatment or maintained under normal temperature. Under non-stress conditions, GABA treatment had no obvious effect on seedling growth. However, after heat treatment at 45 °C, rice seedlings without GABA showed visible heat shock symptoms, including early leaf curling and wilting, and ultimately died after three days of heat exposure (Fig. S1A). By contrast, exogenous GABA application markedly improved rice seedling’s heat tolerance in a dose-dependent manner. Both 0.1 mM and 0.5 mM GABA substantially alleviated heat-induced growth inhibition and physical damage, with most seedlings maintaining normal growth under heat stress. Compared to the heat‑stressed control, GABA-supplemented seedlings showed increases in plant height by approximately 38.5% (0.1 mM), 28.1% (0.5 mM) and 4.6% (1 mM), and in shoot biomass by about 358%, 308% and 85%, respectively (Fig. S1B-C). Based on these results, 0.1 mM GABA was selected for further analyses.

We further verified whether 0.1 mM GABA could alleviate the heat-induced damage in rice seedlings. The addition of 0.1 mM GABA drastically reduced heat stress-caused growth impairment (Fig. 1A). About 87.5% of GABA-treated plants survived after three days of heat treatment followed by two days of recovery, whereas the survival rate of plants without GABA addition was only 27.8% (Fig. 1B). Moreover, the biomass of GABA-treated seedlings recovered to 72.3% of the control level, which was 1.83-fold higher than that of seedlings exposed to heat stress alone (Fig. 1C). The protective role of 0.1 mM GABA on heat tolerance was further confirmed by a detached-leaf assay. After five hours heating and three days recovery under light, leaves without GABA addition turned nearly white, while most GABA-treated leaves remained green (Fig. 1D). Consistent with this phenotype, the total chlorophyll content in GABA-treated leaves was 3.97-fold higher than that in the control (Fig. 1E).

GABA application attenuates heat-induced oxidative damage

To determine whether exogenous GABA affects ROS homeostasis under heat stress, we examined its influence on ROS accumulation induced by high temperature in rice plants. Histochemical staining results revealed no notable difference in H2O2 and O2·− accumulation between the control and GABA-treatment groups under normal conditions. However, after three days of heat treatment alone, rice leaves exhibited significant elevated levels of H2O2 and O2·−, as indicated by the intense dark brown and blue staining, respectively. GABA addition substantially reduced heat-induced ROS accumulation in leaves, bringing it nearly back to control level (Fig. 2A-B). Quantitative analysis further supported these observations. Under heat stress alone, H2O2 and O2·− levels increased by 84.5% and 121.8%, respectively, compared to the control. However, GABA application significantly suppressed this accumulation, resulting in H2O2 and O2·− increases of only 18.6% and 4.3% relative to the heat‑stressed control (Fig. 2C). Excessive ROS accumulation can trigger oxidative stress, damaging lipids, proteins, and DNA, ultimately leading to cellular injury [51]. As excepted, after three days of heat treatment, levels of MDA (a marker of membrane lipid peroxidation) and proline (a compatible solute), both common stress biomarkers, were increased significantly, by141.1% and 536.7%, respectively, compared to the control. Exogenous GABA application markedly lowered the heat-induced rise in MDA and proline contents, reducing them by 37.1% and 33.2% (for MDA) and by 33.3% and 30.5% (for proline), respectively, relative to heat treatment alone after one or three days (Fig. 2D-E). Under normal growth conditions, GABA-treated seedlings showed no significantly difference in MDA content or trypan blue staining compared with the untreated control, with only a slight decrease in proline content. However, strong dark blue staining appeared in rice leaves after 45℃ heat treatment, and this signal was restored to near-control levels with GABA addition, indicating that GABA reduced heat-induced cell death (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that exogenous GABA application suppresses heat stress-induced ROS accumulation and subsequent oxidative damage.

GABA application enhances the antioxidant capacity of rice under heat stress

We next examined the effect of GABA application on ROS scavenging processes in rice leaves. Compared to the control under normal growth conditions, heat treatment significantly increased the activities of four key antioxidant enzymes, APX, CAT, POD, and SOD, in rice leaves (Fig. 3A). Exogenous application of 0.1 mM GABA further enhanced the activities of these enzymes under heat stress, especially after three days of 45℃ treatment, with increases of 24.4%, 112.5%, 43.5% and 31.9% in APX, CAT, POD, and SOD levels, respectively, relative to the heat‑alone group (Fig. 3A). A similar pattern was observed for GSH, an important non-enzymatic antioxidant. Heat treatment induced a significant rise in GSH content in both untreated and GABA-treated seedlings, with a notably greater increase in the GABA-supplemented group (Fig. 3B). Consistent with these biochemical results, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that the expression of OsAPX8, OsCATB, OsPOD2, OsCSD3 (Cu/Zn-SOD) and OsGR1 was upregulated under heat stress, with transcript levels increasing significantly after three hours at 45℃ treatment. GABA addition further elevated the expression of these genes, reaching levels 69.2%, 91.7%, 84.0%, 70.1% and 37.3% higher than those in plants subjected to heat treatment alone, respectively (Fig. 3C). Notably, GABA treatment had no significant effect on antioxidant activity or related gene expression under normal growth conditions.

To further verify the role of GABA in enhancing rice antioxidant capacity, detached leaves were directly exposed to oxidative stressors such as H2O2 or MV. The results showed that control leaves without GABA addition exhibited higher sensitivity to H2O2 or MV treatment and bleached more rapidly than GABA-treated leaves (Fig. 4A). After three days of exposure to 1% H2O2, 2% H2O2, 5 µM MV or 10 µM MV, total chlorophyll content in control leaves decreased by 91.4%, 93.5%, 92.2% and 96.1%, respectively, compared to unstressed leaves (Fig. 4B). In contrast, GABA addition substantially mitigated chlorophyll loss under oxidative stress, increasing total chlorophyll content by 140.3%, 69.1%, 113.1% and 120.9% relative to the corresponding stress controls (Fig. 4B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that exogenous GABA strengthens the antioxidant system in rice, thereby improving its tolerance to heat stress.

GABA modulates amino acid metabolism under heat stress

Heat stress markedly increased total amino acid content in rice seedlings after one day of treatment. However, exogenous GABA addition reduced this heat-induced accumulation, lowering total amino acid levels by 14.8% compared with heat treated alone (Fig. 5A). Further profile analysis of amino acids revealed that heat stress significantly altered the composition of free amino acids in rice leaves. Compared with the control under normal growth conditions, heat treatment led to a pronounced accumulation of most detected amino acids, except aspartic acid (Asp), threonine (Thr) and glycine (Gly). Notably, valine (Val), isoleucine (Ile), tyrosine (Tyr), and leucine (Leu) increased most substantially, reaching 6.2, 7.2, 7.2 and 7.8-fold higher, respectively, than those in the control (Fig. 5B-C). Under normal growth conditions, exogenous GABA application (CK + GABA) did not significantly alter overall amino acid levels relative to CK alone, though contents of Thr, Asp, phenylalanine (Phe), cysteine (Cys), arginine (Arg), serine (Ser), histidine (His) and lysine (Lys) were reduced. However, under heat stress, GABA supplementation (Heat + GABA) distinctly regulated the heat-induced accumulation of amino acids. GABA attenuated the heat-triggered rise in most amino acids, including alanine (Ala), Ser, Val, Ile, Phe, Lys, Tyr, His, Arg, and Leu. Meanwhile, glutamic acid (Glu) and Cys remained at levels comparable to those in the heat-alone group (Fig. 5B-C). Together, these results indicate that GABA actively restructures amino acid metabolism under heat stress, fine-tuning the accumulation of specific amino acids during thermal adaptation.

GABA restores cellular metabolic homeostasis under heat stress in rice

To elucidate the mechanism by which GABA enhances heat tolerance in rice, we performed transcriptome analysis on leaf tissues following different treatments. Compared with the control, the heat‑treated group showed the highest number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with 7,657 DEGs (2,258 upregulated and 5,399 downregulated). In contrast, the GABA + Heat (GH) group versus the control yielded significantly fewer DEGs (4,860 total: 2,175 upregulated, 2,841 downregulated). Additionally, 2,685 DEGs (1,641 upregulated and 1,021 downregulated) were identified between the GH and heat‑alone groups (Fig. 6A). Venn analysis indicated 3,724 shared DEGs between GH vs. control and heat vs. control, while the GH vs. heat comparison shared 1,913 DEGs with GH vs. control and 856 DEGs with heat vs. control; 656 genes overlapped across all three comparisons (Fig. 6B). To validate the RNA-seq results, we performed heatmap analysis on key antioxidant enzyme genes identified in Fig. 3, and the expression trends of these genes were highly consistent between RT-qPCR results (Fig. 3C) and transcriptome data (Fig. S2), confirming the reliability of the transcriptome data. Gene ontology (Go) analysis showed that DEGs between GH and heat-alone groups were mainly enriched in “response to stress”, “response to abiotic stimulus”, “response to stimulus”, “Catalytic activity” and “Carbohydrate metabolic process”, these terms consistently appeared among the top-20 Go categories in the heat vs. control and HG vs. control comparisons (Fig. 6C; Fig. S3A). KEGG pathway enrichment further revealed “global and overview maps”, “carbohydrate metabolism”, “amino acid metabolism”, “lipid metabolism” and “biosynthesis of other secondary metabolisms” as the top five enriched metabolism pathways across the three comparison sets. Notably, the number of DEGs assigned to these pathways decreased progressively in the order of heat vs. control, HG vs. control, and HG vs. heat comparisons (Fig. 6C; Fig. S3B).

We next focused on specific genes associated with carbohydrate, amino acid, and lipid metabolism. Notably, heat stress significantly altered the expression of genes involved in these three metabolic pathways, while GABA application largely counteracted these heat-induced changes (Fig. 6D; Fig. S4). We also quantified carbohydrate levels in rice leaves. Consistent with the trend observed in amino acid content, heat treatment markedly elevated the levels of total sugars, soluble sugars, sucrose and trehalose in leaves. GABA supplementation significantly attenuated these increases, reducing total sugar, soluble sugar, sucrose and trehalose by 51.4%, 40.1%, 66.2% and 48.6%, respectively, relative to the heat treatment alone (Fig. 7). Under normal growth conditions, GABA had no significant effect on these metabolites. Collectively, these results suggest that exogenous GABA helps rebalance the perturbed cellular metabolic network, thereby contributing to improved heat tolerance in rice.

GABA integrates into heat signaling to amplify transcriptional response under heat stress

In addition to metabolism adjustments, KEGG analysis indicated DEGs enrichment in pathways associated with signal transduction and environmental adaptation (Fig. 6C), suggesting a potential role for GABA in heat signal processing. Ca2+ signaling plays a central role in the plant heat-shock response, activating HSF expression through calmodulin (CaM) proteins decoding and thereby regulating downstream heat-responsive genes [7]. To determine whether Ca2+ signaling is involved in GABA-mediated heat-shock response, we first examined the effect of exogenous GABA on CaM gene expression under heat stress. RT-qPCR analyses showed that 45℃ heat stress significantly elevated transcript levels of several rice CaM genes (e.g., CaM1-1, CaM1-2 and CaM2), and this induction was further enhanced by GABA supplementation (Fig. 8A), suggesting that GABA may modulate Ca2+ signaling to regulate heat-responsive transcription. To further test this, we applied the Ca2+ chelator EGTA (15 mM) to the growth medium to examine whether it could abolish the protective effect of GABA under heat tolerance. While EGTA pretreatment alone did not affect seedling growth under normal growth conditions, it completely abolished the protective effect of GABA under heat stress. Both survival rate and shoot fresh weight was restored to levels observed in heat-stress seedlings without GABA (Fig. 8B-C).

We next examined the expression of key heat shock transcription factor (HSF) and HSP genes. As shown in Fig. 9, the expression of HSF and HSP genes exhibited distinct time-dependent patterns under heat stress with or without GABA supplementation. Consistent with previous reports [52], exposure to 45℃ rapidly induces upregulation of HSFs (e.g., HSFA2s) and HSPs (e.g., HSP70, HSP90, HSP101, and HSP18.2) genes, with the peak expression occurring at 0.5 h after heat treatment. Notably, the addition of 0.1 mM GABA further enhanced this transcriptional induction. Among these genes examined, OsHsfA2a, OsHsfA2b, OsHsfA2c, HSP70, HSP90, and HSP101 showed the most pronounced upregulation under combined GABA and heat treatment. At the peak expression time point, their expression levels increased by 50.6%, 115.9%, 68.5%, 107.1%, 111.9%, and 60.4%, respectively, compared to heat treatment alone (Fig. 9). Together, these results indicate that GABA may integrate into heat signaling networks, particularly via Ca2+ signaling, to amplify the transcriptional response under heat stress, thereby strengthening heat tolerance in rice.

Discussion

GABA has been widely reported to enhance the tolerance of plants to biotic and abiotic stress [25]. Plants typically maintain low endogenous GABA content under normal conditions, while rapidly accumulate when they are exposed to various stresses [53]. This stress-induced GABA accumulation contributes to plant adaptation under adverse growth conditions [32]. Many recent studies indicate that exogenous application of a certain does of GABA can significantly alleviate damage caused by diverse environmental stresses, including salinity, drought, temperature extremes and heavy metals [43, 54–59]. In present study, we show that the efficacy of exogenous GABA application in mitigating heat stress exhibits dose-dependency (Fig. S1). The protective effect on rice seedlings alleviating heat damage decreases at higher GABA concentration (e.g., 1 mM). Notably, a low concentration (0.1 mM) of GABA application effectively protected rice seedlings under high-temperature conditions by promoting survival, preserving biomass, and maintaining chlorophyll content (Fig. 1). These results highlight the potential of low-dose GABA as a practical and cost-effective priming agent for crop management under heat stress, offering a flexible and readily applicable strategy to enhance climate resilience.

A common consequence of plant subjected to environmental stress is the uncontrolled escalation of ROS production, which lead to peroxidation of membrane lipids and cell damage [60]. Maintaining cellular ROS homeostasis is therefore essential for plant growth under stressful conditions [61]. Accumulated evidences indicate that GABA functions as an antioxidant that mitigates the oxidative stress by suppressing the accumulation of ROS in various plant species [62, 63]. Consistent with these reports, we observed that heat stress induces significant accumulation of ROS (H2O2 and O2·⁻) and lipid peroxidation products (MDA) in rice leaves, resulting in severe cell death. Exogenous GABA application effectively alleviated this heat-induced oxidative damage by reducing ROS levels (Fig. 2). Plants counteract ROS accumulation under stress through both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant system, including enzymes such as APX, CAT, POD, and SOD, as well as components of the GSH-ascorbate (ASA) cycle [64, 65]. Previous studies have suggested that GABA may act as a signaling molecule involved in regulating ROS scavenging pathways [30, 62]. For instance, GABA has been shown to enhance antioxidant function and alleviate oxidative stress in wheat seedlings under drought and salt stress [57, 66]. In rice, exogenous GABA improves salt and heat tolerance by upregulating antioxidant activity and osmoprotectant accumulation [42, 67]. Similarly, our results demonstrate that GABA supplementation significantly increases the activities of key antioxidant enzymes (APX, CAT, POD, SOD), elevates levels of non-enzymatic antioxidants (e.g., GSH), and thereby mitigates heat-induced oxidative stress. Moreover, the expression of genes encoding these antioxidant enzymes and those involved in the GSH-GSSG cycle was markedly higher in GABA-treated plants than in plants exposed to heat alone (Fig. 3). This coordinated upregulation suggests that GABA serves as an important modulator that potentiates the entire antioxidant transcriptional network under heat stress, conferring stronger antioxidant capacity [30, 62]. Supporting this, detached-leaf assays showed that GABA-treated leaves exhibited significantly less bleaching and chlorophyll degradation under oxidative stress induced by H2O2 or MV addition compared to control (Fig. 4), underscoring the role of GABA in protecting against cellular oxidative damage. Collectively, these findings indicate that GABA-mediated enhancement of antioxidant capacity is a key mechanism though which it alleviates ROS accumulation under stress conditions.

The accumulation of free amino acids is a key physiological response of plants to abiotic stress, typically driven by enhanced protein degradation and/or activation of biosynthetic pathways [68, 69]. Notably, heat stress has also been shown to expand the free amino acid pool in several plant species, such as wheat and maize, and soybean [43, 70, 71]. Moderate amino acid accumulation contributes to osmotic adjustment, ROS homeostasis, and serves as a precursor source for secondary metabolites and signaling molecules, thereby supporting abiotic stress resistance [24]. For example, elevated proline acts as a compatible osmolyte to maintain cytosolic osmotic balance, regulate cellular redox status, and support energy metabolism under stress [72]. Glutamate serves as a central precursor for multiple stress-related amino acids, such as Pro, Arg and GABA, as well as key antioxidant molecules including GSH and folate. Increased Glu content plays a vital role in cellular adaptation under adverse conditions [73]. However, excessive amino acid accumulation can negatively affect plant growth, suggesting the importance of finely regulating amino acid levels for effective stress adaptation [74, 75]. Given that GABA is a non-protein amino acid metabolically connected to glutamate pathways, its exogenous application likely restructures amino acid metabolism to optimize metabolic homeostasis under stress [43]. Consistent with this premise, we observed that 45℃ heat treatment rapidly induced a broad accumulation of free amino acids in rice seedlings, while GABA supplementation significantly reduced the heat-induced rise in many amino acids, including Pro, Ser, Val, Ile, Phe, Lys, Ala, Tyr, His, Arg and Leu, while maintaining levels of others such as Glu and Cys (Fig. 5). This fine-tuning may reflect reduced oxidative protein degradation result from GABA-mediated mitigation of oxidative damage [76]. Meanwhile, the unchanged levels of certain amino acids such as Glu and Cys may reflect their roles as central metabolic hubs or precursors for key stress‑responsive molecules (e.g., glutathione, GABA itself, and other antioxidants), which are actively maintained under stress conditions to support cellular adaptation [26, 73]. In addition to modulating amino acid metabolism, GABA also regulated the expression of genes involved in other primary metabolic pathways, including carbohydrate and lipid metabolism. Accordingly, the heat-induced accumulation of carbohydrates was markedly suppressed by GABA treatment (Fig. 7), suggesting a redirection of carbon/nitrogen flux toward global metabolism or specific protective compounds under GABA regulation [24]. These coordinated roles of GABA on oxidative balance and metabolic homeostasis regulation collectively contribute to enhanced heat tolerance.

The transcriptional regulation of HSF genes represents a central mechanism in plant responses to heat stress [77]. Under high temperature conditions, HSFs are rapidly activated and subsequently induce the expression of HSP and other downstream heat-responsive genes, thereby enhancing heat stress tolerance [8, 78]. Among HSFs, HsfA2 plays a crucial role in modulating the expression of genes involved in stress adaptation [79]. We have previously demonstrated that heat stress-induced OsHsfA2s and its downstream targets is essential for heat tolerance in rice [52]. In this study, we found that GABA amplifies the early transcriptional response to heat stress by upregulating key HsfA2 genes, particularly OsHsfA2a and OsHsfA2c (Fig. 9). This enhanced expression of HSF transcription factors correlated with a stronger induction of downstream HSPs (e.g., HSP70, HSP90 and HSP101) and antioxidant enzyme genes, suggesting that GABA potentiates the core heat-responsive transcriptional cascade. In plants, HSF-HSP transcriptional activation is known to be initiated by Ca2+ and ROS signaling pathways, which act as early and essential transducers of heat stress response [8]. Previous studies have shown that stress-induced increases in cytosolic Ca2+ stimulate GABA synthesis through the activation of GAD [33]. Conversely, elevated GABA levels have been shown to modulate cellular Ca2+ flux and interact with ROS signaling, thereby facilitating plant adaptation to stress conditions [80, 81]. In the present study, transcription analysis revealed significantly changes in the expression of genes associated with signal transduction, particularly CaM genes, which encode key transducers of Ca2+ signaling. The heat-induced upregulation of these genes was further enhanced by GABA treatment, indicating that Ca2+ signaling may participate in GABA-mediated heat stress responses. This interpretation was further supported by the application of EGTA (a Ca2+ chelator), which counteracted the positive effect of GABA on heat tolerance in rice seedlings (Fig. 8). Collectively, these findings link GABA signaling to the classical HSF-HSP heat tolerance pathway and indicate that GABA may enhance the plant’s ability to perceive and transcriptionally respond to heat stress.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that exogenous application of 0.1 mM GABA enhances heat tolerance in rice through an integrated mechanism (Fig. 10). Heat stress triggers excessive ROS accumulation, leading to oxidative stress and leads to membrane lipid peroxidation, protein degradation, and ultimately cell death, all of which contribute to overall plant injury. In contrast, 0.1mM GABA application significantly enhances the activity of antioxidative system, thereby reducing ROS accumulation. The lowered ROS level helps maintain cellular redox homeostasis, alleviates oxidative damage and modulates global metabolism, such as amino acids and sugars, which collectively contributes to improved heat tolerance. Moreover, both GABA itself and GABA-activated heat signaling pathways (e.g., Ca2+ signal) substantially upregulate the expression of heat‑responsive transcriptional factors, which in turn induce HSPs and other downstream heat-responsive genes essential for plant against heat stress.

A working model of exogenous GABA application to enhance heat tolerance in rice seedlings. Briefly, exogenous GABA enhances heat tolerance by activating the antioxidant system to alleviate oxidative damage and by reprogramming global metabolism, including amino acid and carbohydrate homeostasis. Moreover, GABA may also modulate Ca2+ signaling to upregulate the HSF-HSP transcriptional pathway, thereby strengthening the heat stress response
