Meniscus‐Guided Interfacial Ring‐by‐Ring Assembly for In Situ Fabrication of Tubular Hydrogels
Authors: Yuki Kamiya, Yingzhe Wang, Keisuke Morishima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe fabrication of arteriole‐scale blood vessel‐like structures remains a critical barrier in engineering thick, vascularized tissues. Despite advances in artery‐scale tubular scaffolds and capillary‐scale networks, creating tubular structures at the arteriole scale (50 µm–1 mm) remains challenging because of the intrinsic softness of hydrogels. Here, we present an optofluidic additive‐manufacturing method based on meniscus‐guided interfacial polymerization within microchannels. Surface tension forms a stable oil–hydrogel interface, where ultraviolet (UV) irradiation selectively polymerizes the hydrogel precursor into ring‐like structures. Sequential ring formation along the channel enables in situ assembly of hollow tubular constructs with inner diameters precisely controlled by the axial irradiation position. The method is further extended to biologically relevant softer hydrogels, and UV attenuation analysis and PBS incubation support the structural uniformity and stability of the fabricated tubes. Hydrogel tubes with straight, curved, and branching configurations are fabricated using corresponding microchannel architectures. Multi‐material tubular structures with radial and axial compositions are also realized while reducing material‐switching steps and waste. Furthermore, synchronizing real‐time meniscus detection with UV irradiation enables continuous ring formation and smooth tube walls. This method provides a versatile platform for constructing arteriole‐scale vascular‐like hydrogel structures with controlled geometries, stability, and material compositions.

Introduction

Advancements in regenerative medicine have led to a growing interest in tissue engineering as a potential solution to organ shortage and the lack of organ models. To date, a wide range of engineered tissues has been developed and applied in transplantation and disease modeling [1, 2]. However, the construction of thick and viable tissues remains challenging primarily because of the absence of functional vascular networks. Vascular networks exhibit a hierarchical organization, typically consisting of capillaries (∼φ50 µm), arterioles (φ50 µm ∼ 1 mm), and arteries (φ1 mm ∼) [3]. Blood vessels are distributed at intervals of approximately 100–200 µm to supply oxygen and nutrients and to prevent tissue necrosis [4]. Therefore, reproducing hierarchical vascular networks is critical for engineering volumetric tissues.

To address this challenge, numerous strategies have been explored for generating blood vessel‐like hydrogel scaffolds, including sacrificial molding [5, 6], dip coating [7], and bioprinting [8, 9]. These engineering‐based methods enable the fabrication of perfusable tubular structures on the arterial scale. Significant progress has been made in generating capillary‐scale vascular networks through the self‐organization of endothelial cells via angiogenic processes [10]. Building on these advances, modular assembly has emerged as a promising approach for constructing hierarchical tissue‐engineered architectures [11]. Multi‐scale vascular systems have been constructed by combining self‐organized endothelial cell‐driven capillaries with larger tubular constructs fabricated using engineering techniques [12, 13]. Despite these successes, a critical gap remains between self‐organized capillaries and engineered tubular structures. Arterioles, which occupy this intermediate scale, are too large to be formed through angiogenesis, yet too small for conventional engineering methods. This unresolved “arteriole gap” continues to hinder the development of fully hierarchical vascular networks.

In recent years, optofluidic lithography technology has advanced rapidly owing to its capability to seamlessly integrate precise fluid control with light‐driven polymerization. Consequently, it has been extensively employed in the fabrication of microscale hydrogel structures [14, 15]. To create hollow geometries, laminar coaxial flow has been widely employed, in which a central sacrificial liquid inhibits polymerization in the lumen, whereas a surrounding photocurable liquid forms a tube wall [16, 17, 18]. Although this method is effective for producing microscale tubes, the resulting geometries are limited to straight configurations because the introduction of curved and branching channels disrupts the stability of laminar coaxial flow, restricting the fabrication of complex tubular architectures. Ring‐assembly techniques have been proposed as bottom‐up approaches by constructing tubes from modular ring units [19, 20]; however, assembling microscale rings into complex shapes remains difficult.

Beyond the geometric complexity, replicating the heterogeneity of blood vessels requires the incorporation of multiple hydrogel materials within a single tubular construct. Although stereolithographic bioprinting within microfluidic devices has enabled the layer‐by‐layer fabrication of heterogeneous hydrogel constructs with rapid material‐switching [21], this additive‐manufacturing approach suffers from substantial material wastage and a prolonged fabrication time owing to repeated material exchange during printing. These limitations underscore the need for a new optofluidic fabrication strategy that enables the efficient generation of microscale tubular hydrogel structures with complex geometries and heterogeneous compositions.

The meniscus effect is a ubiquitous physical phenomenon in which a liquid surface becomes curved owing to surface tension. In hydrophilic microchannels, the oil‐water interfacial flow can penetrate arbitrary inhomogeneous cylindrical geometries while maintaining a stable, convex mineral oil surface [22]. This characteristic interfacial behavior offers a promising mechanism for expanding the architectural versatility of tubular hydrogel fabrication because it enables the confinement of a sacrificial liquid at the channel center across diverse channel geometries, thereby avoiding flow instability and turbulence.

Inspired by this phenomenon, this study proposes an optofluidic lithography strategy that exploits the meniscus effect to fabricate tubular hydrogel structures inside the microchannels (Figure 1a; Video S1). In this method, a photocurable hydrogel prepolymer solution and mineral oil are co‐introduced into a hydrophilic microchannel, resulting in the formation of a convex mineral oil interface that positions the hydrogel solution peripherally along the channel wall while confining the mineral oil to the channel center. The hydrogel solution is selectively polymerized into a ring‐like shape by projecting UV light onto the oil–hydrogel interface. In the UV projection area, mineral oil serves as a sacrificial liquid material to create hollow hydrogel structures. By continuously shifting the interface and sequentially photopolymerizing the hydrogel rings along the channel axis, hollow tubular hydrogel structures can be additively assembled in situ (Figure 1b; Video S2).

Building on the demonstrated feasibility of this meniscus‐based approach for fabricating microscale tubular hydrogel structures, the method was extended to achieve precise control over the tubular architectures. Its applicability to softer hydrogel materials was further demonstrated, and the structural uniformity and stability of the fabricated tubular hydrogels were evaluated. Hollow tubular hydrogel structures were fabricated with complex geometries within microchannels, including curved and branching configurations. Furthermore, multi‐component tubular hydrogel structures were fabricated by forming two‐color hydrogel tubes in a tube‐by‐tube manner, thereby reducing the number of material‐switching steps. The fabrication process was automated by synchronizing image‐processing‐based meniscus detection with real‐time UV pattern generation using a digital micromirror device (DMD).

Results and Discussion

Formation and Dimensional Characteristics of Hydrogel Rings

Hollow hydrogel rings were successfully fabricated using the proposed optofluidic lithography strategy. By adjusting the axial position of the UV irradiation relative to the oil–hydrogel interface, the size of the hollow region was readily tuned, enabling the fabrication of hydrogel rings with different inner radii. To relate the hollow region formed within the hydrogel ring to the axial UV position, D c was defined as the distance between the UV irradiation pattern and the apex of the convex mineral oil surface (Figure 2a). The dependence of the inner radius on the axial displacement D c was measured to characterize this relationship quantitatively. Nine hydrogel rings were extracted from the channel, and their inner radii were measured and plotted as functions of D c (Figure 2b). For comparison, the theoretical meniscus profile was also plotted.

(b) Dependence of the measured inner radii on the position of UV irradiation, compared with the theoretical meniscus profile calculated from the contact angle. (c) Representative top‐view images of fabricated rings with inner diameters of 171 µm (left), 298 µm (middle), and 378 µm (right). (d) Simulated refraction of UV rays across the curved oil—hydrogel interface, showing ray trajectories from the bottom wall (height = −0.5 mm) to the top wall (height = 0.5 mm) of the microchannel. Scale bars, 500 µm.

The average contact angle between the channel wall and the mineral oil interface before ring formation was 29.7°. The position at which D c = 0 represents the apex of the convex mineral oil surface. Among the fabricated rings, the inner radius ranged from 171 to 378 µm (Figure 2c). Overall, the experimentally measured inner radii were distributed along the theoretical meniscus profile, indicating good agreement between the experimental results and the geometric predictions. The largest deviation from the theoretical value occurred at the smallest inner radius. Near the apex of the two‐liquid interface, the meniscus profile exhibited a steep slope, suggesting that slight variations in the UV irradiation position result in relatively large changes in the measured inner radii.

Interestingly, for the hydrogel ring with the smallest inner radius, two lines were observed in the upper half of the ring, separated by the hollow region. This feature is attributed to the refraction of UV light at the curved meniscus surface. Owing to the refractive index (RI) mismatch between the 80% PEGDA 700 solution (RI = 1.447) [23] and mineral oil (RI = 1.464), the UV light path was altered upon crossing the interface. UV rays passing through the convex mineral oil surface were refracted toward the oil phase, whereas rays incident on either side of the hollow region were less affected, resulting in a non‐uniform UV irradiation width on the upper half of the ring. To investigate this effect, the UV ray refraction was simulated at different projection positions based on Snell's law (Figure 2d). The lateral displacement of UV rays at the top channel wall was calculated to be 29, 24, 23, and 18 µm for UV rays from D c = 25, 50, 100, and 150 µm at the bottom channel wall, respectively. The simulation results indicate that double refraction at the curved meniscus causes UV rays to bend toward the mineral oil phase, with the effect being most significant near D c = 0, owing to the steep gradient of the meniscus curve at the apex.

These results suggest that selecting hydrogel prepolymer and oil pairs with a more closely matched RI is expected to improve the precision of hollow structure fabrication. Silicone oil, a biocompatible material widely used in ophthalmic surgery [24], has an RI of 1.405, lower than that of mineral oil. However, the RI of the cell‐laden hydrogel bioink is approximately 1.33 [25], resulting in a mismatch between the bioink and oil phases. Iodixanol has been used to mitigate optical distortion in light‐based bioprinting [25]. Therefore, adjusting the RI of the hydrogel bioinks may enhance the accuracy of hollow region formation in the proposed method. In addition, light scattering in cell‐laden bioinks may reduce photopolymerization resolution; however, because the lumen boundary is primarily defined by the oil phase, hollow‐region formation may be less sensitive to scattering‐induced dimensional errors. The influence of scattering on UV intensity distribution should be further evaluated when applying this method to cell‐laden bioinks.

Sequential Formation of Hydrogel Rings Into Tubular Structures

For tubular hydrogel fabrication, the first ring was securely anchored to the microchannel wall by inducing radial swelling by increasing the UV irradiation time, after which additional rings were sequentially formed. When the mineral oil interface passed through the opening of the fabricated ring, the apex of the convex meniscus advanced, whereas a portion of the interface in contact with the ring edge remained pinned (Figure 3a). This effect is known as Gibbs’ pinning [26, 27]. To analyze the Gibbs’ pinning behavior during sequential ring fabrication, the advancement of the oil‐hydrogel interface through the preformed rings was simulated in the XY plane. The channel geometry was designed with a width of 1 mm and symmetric protrusions on the walls to represent the fabricated rings. The protrusion height was set to 150 µm. The simulation results show that, during the interface advancement, the pressure within the mineral oil phase remained almost constant, and subsequently decreased as the interface progressed, accompanied by localized pressure peaks at the protrusion corners where pinning occurred. D p was defined as the distance between the apex of the convex meniscus and the preformed ring, and the pinning angle θ p was also defined as the interface angle of mineral oil on the fabricated hydrogel ring within the microchannel (Figure 3b). The dependence of the pinning angle θ p on the axial position D p was characterized, as shown in Figure 3c. As θ p increased beyond 110°, the interface contacted the channel wall without undergoing depinning. By applying UV irradiation at an intermediate θ p value of approximately 90°, successive rings could be fabricated with an inner radius comparable to those of the preformed rings.

(b) Schematic of the Gibbs’ pinning behavior of the interface during sequential ring fabrication. (c) Dependence of the pinning angle θ p on D p. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of fabricated hydrogel tubular structures with average inner radii of (d) 161, (e) 251, and (f) 334 µm. Scale bars, 1 mm in (a) and 500 µm in (d–f).

The vertical cross‐section of the meniscus interface was further analyzed to assess the axial alignment of the mineral oil apex penetrating the first‐formed ring (Figure S3). As UV rays refract when passing through a curved interface, which can shift the center of the hollow region and lead to a misalignment, microchannel geometries with asymmetric protrusions corresponding to the first experimentally fabricated rings were used. Specifically, protrusion profiles corresponding to rings formed by the irradiation of a 100 µm wide UV pattern at D c = 0.01, 0.05, and 0.15 were modeled, and the interfaces passing through these geometries were simulated. Across the examined range of D p, the off‐center distance of the convex interface from the channel axis remained below 5 µm when θ p = 90°, indicating minimal vertical misalignment during interface advancement. These results demonstrate that rings could be fabricated uniformly along the channel by performing UV irradiation when θ p ≈ 90°. Moreover, the interface was almost parallel to the microchannel wall, which reduced the optical refraction at the curved interface and supported consistent ring formation.

Using the above conditions, three tubular hydrogel structures were fabricated through sequential fabrication of rings with uniform inner radii. The average inner radii of the constituent rings were 161 ± 31 µm (Figure 3d), 251 ± 21 µm (Figure 3e), and 334 ± 21 µm (Figure 3f). The inner radii were measured directly from the rings fabricated inside the channel. These results demonstrate the capability of the proposed method for fabricating hydrogel tubes with different inner radii in the same channel by simply adjusting the axial position of the UV irradiation. Red‐dyed water was perfused through the tubes, and no leakage was observed (Figure S4).

Layered stripes were observed on the outer surfaces of the fabricated tubular structures. This feature originated from the additive‐manufacturing nature of the ring‐by‐ring assembly, where small step‐like features were formed between adjacent rings, similar to other light‐based fabrication techniques. However, unlike the conventional layer‐by‐layer light‐based additive manufacturing, the UV irradiation direction in the proposed method was perpendicular to the stacking direction. Therefore, these surface stripes can be reduced by allowing a slight overlap between the adjacent rings during fabrication.

Applicability to Soft Hydrogel Materials

To further evaluate the applicability of the proposed method to soft hydrogel materials, tubular structures were fabricated using 20% PEGDA 700 (RI = 1.38) [28] and silicone oil (RI = 1.399). Since photopolymerization at lower PEGDA concentrations is more susceptible to oxygen inhibition [29], a thicker PVA coating was applied to the PDMS microchannel using a 20 wt.% PVA solution. To further overcome incomplete polymerization caused by oxygen diffusion from the upper PDMS layer, sequential UV exposure from both sides of the channel was performed. Under these conditions, the meniscus interface remained stable with a contact angle of 17°, enabling the successful fabrication of hollow tubular structures through sequential ring formation (Figure 4a). The resulting tubes could be manipulated using forceps and exhibited substantial deformation without fracture, demonstrating the feasibility of fabricating soft hydrogel tubes using the proposed method (Figure 4c–e).

Applicability of the proposed method to soft hydrogel materials. In situ fabrication of tubular structures using 20% PEGDA 700 in (a) a PDMS channel with thicker PVA coating and (b) a glass capillary. (c–d) Mechanical deformation of a fabricated tube manipulated using forceps. (e) Side view of a fabricated tube. Scale bars, 500 µm.

Although this double‐sided exposure strategy enabled the fabrication of soft hydrogel tubes using 20% PEGDA, it increased the complexity of the fabrication procedure. Future improvements could include further suppression of oxygen diffusion by applying transparent barrier coatings, introducing a nitrogen atmosphere during fabrication, or employing channel materials with lower gas permeability.

Tube fabrication was also attempted within a glass capillary using 20% PEGDA. A stable convex oil–hydrogel interface was maintained owing to the hydrophilic nature of glass, enabling the formation of hollow structures (Figure 4b). However, strong adhesion between the photopolymerized hydrogel and the glass wall prevented extraction of free‐standing tubes. This observation suggests that the proposed method may also be applicable to the direct fabrication of hydrogel coatings or lumen structures within rigid channels.

Evaluation of Structural Uniformity and Stability

Mechanical homogeneity is critical for hydrogel tubular structures, as nonuniform crosslinking can cause stiffness variations and reduce structural stability. In conventional frontal photopolymerization (FPP), hydrogels are fabricated on a print bed in a layer‐by‐layer manner, where UV light propagates along the build direction (Figure 5a). To achieve high fabrication resolution, photoabsorbers are commonly added to limit light penetration, resulting in a curing gradient along the irradiation direction. Previous studies have reported stiffness variations of up to fourfold within a single layer and 44% across multiple layers fabricated by FPP [30]. In contrast, the proposed meniscus‐guided fabrication strategy does not require penetration‐depth control because the hollow region is defined by the sacrificial mineral oil phase rather than selective light attenuation. Furthermore, UV irradiation is applied perpendicular to the ring accumulation direction, minimizing dose accumulation between successive rings. These features are expected to promote a more homogeneous crosslinking distribution throughout the fabricated tubular structures.

(a) Schematic comparison of UV irradiation directions in conventional frontal photopolymerization (FPP) and the proposed meniscus‐guided ring‐by‐ring fabrication method. (b) Simulated UV attenuation along the penetration depth in the hydrogel precursor solution. Optical microscope images of a fabricated hydrogel tube (c) in mineral oil and after incubation in PBS at 37°C for (d) 1 h and (e) 24 h. Scale bars, 100 µm.

To quantitatively assess the uniformity of photopolymerization, UV attenuation within the hydrogel precursor solution was analyzed using the Beer–Lambert law (Figure 5b). Darocur 1173 (2‐hydroxy‐2‐methyl‐1‐phenylpropan‐1‐one) was used as the photoinitiator, with an extinction coefficient (ε) of 0.16 M− 1 cm− 1 at 365 nm [31]. The UV dose distribution along the penetration direction was calculated under the irradiation condition used for tube fabrication (37.6 mW cm− 2 for 1.0 s). The calculated UV intensity attenuation across the cured hydrogel thickness was less than 1%, corresponding to an estimated stiffness variation of less than 1% [32]. These results indicate that UV attenuation during ring formation was negligible, supporting nearly uniform photopolymerization throughout each ring. In contrast, numerical simulation results indicated that 5 mg/ml lithium phenyl‐2,4,6‐trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP), a photoinitiator commonly used for crosslinking bioinks, exhibited significant attenuation under 365 nm UV irradiation, particularly in the presence of a photoabsorber, such as 9 mg ml−1 Quinoline Yellow (QY). However, under 405 nm irradiation, 0.1% LAP showed negligible attenuation. Therefore, selecting an appropriate photoinitiator–wavelength combination is important for minimizing UV attenuation and maintaining uniform photopolymerization.

Heterogeneous mechanical properties can lead to structural disassembly in hydrogel constructs [30]. To evaluate the structural stability of the fabricated tubes under physiologically relevant conditions, the hydrogel tubes were incubated in PBS at 37°C (Figure 5c–e). No obvious deformation, rupture, or disassembly was observed after 1 h or 24 h of incubation, indicating that the tubular geometry was stably maintained under these conditions. Together with the UV attenuation analysis, these results suggest that the proposed fabrication strategy can produce tubular hydrogels with homogeneous photopolymerization and stable hollow structures.

Formation of Hydrogel Tubes With Complex Geometries

Microfluidic devices with curved and branching geometries were fabricated, and tubular hydrogels that replicated the shapes of these microchannels were successfully formed (Figure 6a,b). During the curved tube fabrication, the apex of the advancing meniscus interface was maintained at the center of the channel while gradually adjusting to the local curvature, allowing sequential ring formation along the curved path.

For the branching geometries, sequential ring formation toward the bifurcation point was achieved by translating the interface (Figure S5i–ii). At the bifurcation with a non‐circular cross‐sectional region, the center of the interface aligned with the axis of the downstream channel (Figure S5iii–v). The subsequent ring formation from the trunk microchannel resulted in seamless tubular connections in all three directions at the bifurcation point (Figure S5vi).

These results demonstrate the capability of the proposed method to fabricate hydrogel tubes with complex geometries. As conventional microfluidic approaches rely on continuous concentric flow [16, 17, 18], the entire flow must be stabilized, which limits the channel geometry to a straight line. By contrast, this study employed the meniscus effect and sequentially formed rings at the interface, circumventing the need for stable flow. The meniscus interface establishes mechanical equilibrium within the microchannels because of the surface tension, whereby the apex of the convex mineral oil is always at the center of the pinned preformed ring. Therefore, the rings can be aligned concentrically within complex geometries.

However, because the proposed approach relies on predesigned microchannel templates, its geometric complexity and scalability remain more constrained than those of direct 3D bioprinting or sacrificial‐template strategies. In particular, the achievable tubular architectures are currently limited to template‐defined quasi‐planar networks, because dynamically maintaining the UV focus along the vertical direction while simultaneously tracking the moving meniscus interface remains challenging. In addition, the overall construct size is limited by the dimensions of the microfluidic device owing to the in situ fabrication concept. Nevertheless, the proposed method offers distinct advantages in miniaturization. Stable meniscus interfaces between immiscible liquids have been widely demonstrated in droplet microfluidics, including in sub‐100 µm microchannels [33]. Therefore, by employing smaller microchannel templates, further downscaling of the tubular structures should be feasible. This miniaturization potential represents an advantage over extrusion‐based bioprinting and sacrificial‐template approaches, which are often constrained by nozzle dimensions, bioink printability, filament stability, or template‐removal processes [34].

Formation of Hydrogel Tubes With Multiple Compositions

Multi‐material light‐based hydrogel polymerization typically requires rinsing of the previous material to prevent contamination. The incomplete removal and deformation of printed structures have been challenging in light‐based additive manufacturing within microfluidic devices. In the proposed method, the fabricated tube axis aligns with the channel axis, facilitating efficient removal of the residual hydrogel solution through the hollow region. It was confirmed that rinsing with deionized water did not cause delamination of the previously printed tubes from the channel walls (Figure 7a). Additionally, the blue and red layers formed concentrically in the radial direction (Figure 7bi). As the cured PEGDA exhibited hydrophilic surface properties, inducing the formation of a concave hydrogel solution, a second tubular structure was fabricated inside the first without gaps. The red hydrogel tube was integrated into the blue hydrogel tube without color mixing (Figure 7bii).

(a) Schematics (top row) and corresponding fabrication processes (bottom row) showing (i) formation of a blue hydrogel tube, (ii) fabrication of a red tube adjacent to the blue tube, and (iii) fabrication of a red tube inside the blue tube. Front view (b) and side view (c) of the fabricated structure. Scale bars, 1.0 mm in (a) and (c), and 500 µm in (b).

This strategy differs from conventional light‐based 3D printing, which requires multiple ink changes and layer‐by‐layer rinsing. The proposed method fabricates entire tubes sequentially, thereby reducing complexity and material waste. The model consists of multiple materials in one layer, which requires several ink introductions and a rinsing process for only one layer, resulting in increased time consumption and material waste. In the proposed method, the entire structure is fabricated tube‐by‐tube, and the order of the printing layers is not as significant as in other methods, which enables printing of different materials at different locations simultaneously. For example, to print the same radially distributed multi‐material structure as in Figure 7 in the DLP method, blue and red color hydrogel solutions must be changed in every layer, while only a single material‐switching was required in the proposed method. The advantages of the proposed method are that it reduces expensive material waste and saves time.

Arteriole‐scale blood vessels possess layered compositions, including an endothelialized inner surface and mechanically supportive outer layers [3]. To better recapitulate such hierarchical structures, the composition of fabricated hydrogel tubes could be spatially modified by assigning different hydrogel formulations to the inner and outer layers or to specific axial segments. For example, hydrogel concentration and crosslinking density could be varied to tune local stiffness and porosity, while biochemical factors or cells could be incorporated into selected regions. Spatially distributed vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) has been reported to promote endothelial cell migration into scaffolds [35]. Therefore, fabricating tubular hydrogels with locally defined VEGF concentrations may provide a strategy for guiding endothelial cell behavior in a spatially controlled manner. These examples illustrate that the multi‐composition fabrication capability of the proposed method can be used to construct tubular scaffolds with tunable mechanical and biochemical properties for vascular mimicry and vascularization, while reducing material‐switching steps and material waste.

Automation of Tubular Hydrogel Fabrication

Figure 8a illustrates the automated fabrication process, in which the meniscus between the red hydrogel solution and mineral oil was detected, and used to guide UV irradiation (Figure 8b; Video S3). Using this approach, tubular hydrogel structures were successfully fabricated without observable layered roughness on the tube surface (Figure 8c). Although minor variations in the inner diameter were observed, the overall tubular geometry remained continuous and well defined.

Automated fabrication of tubular hydrogel structures based on template matching. (a) Schematic of the fabrication process, including meniscus detection by template matching and real‐time synchronization of the DMD UV pattern with the advancing interface. During continuous fabrication, the advancement speed of the meniscus increased as tubular structures were formed within the channel. This was because the flow rate was maintained using a syringe pump, and the effective velocity of the meniscus increased as the hollow region of the tube developed. Despite these dynamic changes in the flow conditions, stable tube formation was maintained throughout the automated process.

Compared to conventional layer‐by‐layer additive‐manufacturing approaches, continuous ring fabrication through automated meniscus tracking effectively suppresses the surface roughness associated with discrete layering. These results demonstrate that automation enables the robust and continuous fabrication of tubular hydrogel structures while reducing operator‐dependent variability.

Conclusions

This study proposed a novel method for fabricating tubular hydrogel structures based on the meniscus effect using in situ additive manufacturing within microchannels. By projecting UV lightonto the curved interface between a photocurable hydrogel prepolymer solution and mineral oil, selective polymerization of the hydrogel near the channel wall was achieved, enabling the formation of hollow tubular structures. The size of the hollow lumen was precisely controlled by adjusting the axial position of UV irradiation.

Using this method, hydrogel tubes with complex geometries were fabricated within microchannels that replicated the desired tubular architectures. The method was further extended to softer hydrogel materials to enhance biological relevance, and UV attenuation analysis together with PBS incubation confirmed the structural uniformity and stability of the fabricated tubes under physiologically relevant conditions. Furthermore, the method enabled the construction of hydrogel tubes with multi‐material compositions in both the radial and axial directions while minimizing the number of hydrogel‐switching steps. Finally, the feasibility of automated tubular hydrogel fabrication was demonstrated by integrating image‐processing‐based meniscus detection with synchronized UV irradiation.

Although tubular hydrogels with an outer diameter of 1 mm have been successfully fabricated, there is room for further miniaturization. The integration of existing microfluidic components, such as pumps and control systems [15, 21], will enable the facile production of blood‑vessel‑like structures that better bridge the hierarchical scales of vascular networks. Additionally, the fabrication of complex geometries and heterogeneous compositions can be automated. Highly sophisticated geometries and configurations can be efficiently achieved by dynamically adapting the digital mask of the DMD to conform to microchannel wall geometries or preformed hydrogel tubes.

The ability to fabricate hollow hydrogel tubes with controlled lumen sizes, soft hydrogel compositions, structural stability, complex geometries, and layered material compositions suggests strong potential for constructing biomimetic vascular models, particularly at the arteriole scale. This method may provide a versatile platform for engineering perfusable tissue models and studying vascular structure–function relationships in vitro.

Experimental Section

Fabrication of Circular Microfluidic Channels

Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) was selected as the microfluidic channel material for fabricating the hollow hydrogels owing to its oxygen permeability [36], which inhibits the complete polymerization of photocurable hydrogels on the channel wall during UV irradiation, thereby enabling the extraction of the fabricated structures [15]. In the proposed method, hydrogel rings were temporarily immobilized on the channel wall to allow in situ sequential assembly into tubular structures. To achieve this, the inner surface of the channel was coated with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), which reduced the oxygen permeability and allowed temporary fixation of the hydrogel structures. In addition, the PVA coating rendered the PDMS surface hydrophilic [37], which is essential for maintaining a convex mineral oil interface during fabrication.

The PDMS microfluidic channels were fabricated by casting PDMS onto 3D‐printed sacrificial molds (Figure S1). The mold was designed with a half‐channel geometry and fabricated on a glass slide via 3D printing using a water‐washable resin (Transparent, SK Honpo, Japan) with an LCD 3D printer (ELEGOO Mars 2 Pro, ELEGOO, China). The printed molds were post‐treated by UV exposure for 1 h and subsequently thermally treated at 60°C for 1 h on a hotplate to prevent PDMS curing inhibition [38]. PDMS prepolymer with a curing agent ratio of 10:1 (SILPOT 184 Silicone Elastomer, Dow Toray Co., Ltd., Japan) was poured into an aluminum foil container holding the mold‐bearing glass slide and degassed under vacuum for 30 min. PDMS was cured at 55°C for 3 h and then detached from the glass slide.

Two PDMS channel halves were aligned and bonded after oxygen plasma treatment (Ion Bombarder PIB‐10, VACUUM DEVICE, Japan) and subsequently heat‐pressed at 95°C for 20 min. A 1 wt.% PVA solution was introduced into the bonded channel, incubated for 10 min to allow surface adsorption, and removed using nitrogen gas. The PDMS channels were then heated at 110°C for 15 min to stabilize the PVA coating. This procedure was repeated three times to ensure uniform surface coverage.

Experimental Setup

An experimental setup was constructed for the fabrication of the hollow tubular hydrogel structures within the microfluidic channels, as shown in a photograph of the experimental system in Figure S2. Polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA, Mn = 700, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) was used as the photocurable hydrogel precursor. A photocurable hydrogel solution containing 80% PEGDA and 2% photoinitiator (Darocur 1173, Sigma–Aldrich, USA) was prepared. The PEGDA solution was preloaded into a syringe (1 mL, TERUMO, Japan), followed by the addition of mineral oil (Nacalai Tesque, Japan). The microfluidic device was mounted on a mechanical translation stage, with its inlet connected to a syringe via silicone tubing. Fluid delivery was controlled via a syringe‐driven flow. Channel observations and patterned UV irradiation were performed using a video camera (XCG‐CG510C, SONY, Japan) and a DMD‐based lithography system with a 365 nm UV light source. Image loading and transmission to the DMD, UV irradiation control, and fluidic delivery were implemented using a software interface programmed in LabVIEW (National Instruments, USA).

Fabrication of Hydrogel Rings and Tubes

The PEGDA hydrogel solution and mineral oil were sequentially introduced into a microfluidic channel with a circular cross‐section (1 mm diameter), forming a stable oil–hydrogel interface, with mineral oil occupying the central region of the channel and the PEGDA solution residing near the channel wall. A narrow rectangular pattern of UV light was projected onto the interface to form a hydrogel ring.

For hydrogel tube fabrication, hydrogel rings were sequentially patterned along the channel axis by translating the microfluidic device and adjusting the position of the oil–hydrogel interface. To fabricate tubular structures with a uniform wall thickness, the deviation of the inner radius of the sequentially fabricated rings was minimized by controlling the interfacial geometry. Based on theoretical analysis of the meniscus geometry and interface stability, UV irradiation was applied at selected D p and θ p values that minimized variation in the inner diameter between sequentially formed rings. This criterion enabled the in situ sequential assembly of hydrogel rings into tubular structures with uniform wall thickness.

For the initial ring, UV light with an intensity of 150.3 mW cm− 2 was projected onto the oil–hydrogel interface using a rectangular irradiation pattern. The exposure duration was set to 5.0 s (overexposure) to ensure that the formed hydrogel rings temporarily adhered to the channel wall. For the subsequent rings, UV light with an intensity of 37.6 mW cm− 2 was applied for 1.0 s. During fabrication, multiple interfaces were generated. To avoid overcuring at a single interface, hydrogel rings were patterned across different interfaces, switching after several rings.

Simulation of the Meniscus Curve and UV Refraction

To support the quantitative control of the hydrogel ring formation, the meniscus geometry was simulated to relate the hollow region formed within the hydrogel ring to the axial UV position. R was defined as the radial distance from the channel axis to the interface of the two liquids. The meniscus profile inside the channel was approximated using a circular arc in the longitudinal section. For a channel width w and a contact angle θ c, the meniscus curvature radius R m was expressed as follows: (1) Rm=w2cosθc

The relationship between the displacement dR in the radial direction and dD c in the axial direction was expressed as follows: (2) dR=Rm1−1−dDcRm2

To evaluate the optical effects during ring formation, the refraction of UV rays at the curved oil–hydrogel interface was simulated using MATLAB. The meniscus profile was modeled by assigning mineral oil to the convex liquid region and 80% PEGDA 700 to the concave liquid region. UV rays were traced from −0.5 mm (bottom channel wall) to +0.5 mm (top channel wall) in the channel height, refracting at the interface according to Snell's law. The angles of incidence and refraction were calculated based on the angle between each UV ray and the normal to the meniscus curve.

Simulation of Oil Flow Passing Through the Ring

The advancement of the oil‐hydrogel interface was simulated using ANSYS Fluent (Canonsburg, PA, USA), which employs finite‐volume‐based computational fluid dynamics method. The channel geometry was designed using DesignModeler. The element size was set to 10 µm in ANSYS Meshing. The simulation was performed under steady‐state conditions using a laminar viscosity model. Initially, the channel was filled with water (representing the hydrogel solution), and a mineral oil flow was introduced from the inlet at 0.01 m s−1 without gauge pressure. The contact angle with the channel wall was set as 150.3°. The global Courant number and number of time steps were set to 1.

Perfusion Test of Fabricated Hydrogel Tubes

A perfusion test was performed to evaluate the structural integrity of the fabricated hydrogel tubes. A fabricated hydrogel tube (inner diameter: 0.6 mm) was connected to a PEEK tube (outer diameter: 0.5 mm). The junction was sealed using 80% PEGDA 700 and cured under UV irradiation. Red‐dyed water was then perfused through the assembled tubing at a flow rate of 1 mL min−1 for 10 min (Figure S4).

Fabrication of Soft Hydrogel Tubes

Two PDMS channel halves containing semicircular channels with an inner diameter of 1.5 mm were bonded following oxygen plasma treatment. To reduce oxygen diffusion through the channel wall, a 20 wt.% PVA solution was introduced into the bonded channel and left overnight at room temperature to form a thick PVA coating. The PDMS device was then placed on a glass slide without bonding.

A photocurable hydrogel solution consisting of 20% PEGDA 700 and 2% Darocur 1173 was introduced into the channel, followed by silicone oil (Shin‐Etsu Chemical Co., Ltd., Japan). UV irradiation was applied at an intensity of 187.9 mW cm− 2 for 2.0 s. Because oxygen inhibition prevented complete polymerization across the channel cross‐section, the bottom half of the tube was first photopolymerized. The PDMS device was subsequently inverted onto the glass slide, and the opposite side was exposed to UV irradiation to complete the tubular structure.

Numerical Simulation of UV Intensity Attenuation

UV intensity attenuation within the hydrogel precursor solution was calculated based on the Beer–Lambert law. The transmitted UV intensity at a penetration depth z, I(z), was expressed as: (3) Iz=I0·10−εcz where I 0 is the incident UV intensity, ε is the molar extinction coefficient of the absorbing species, and c is its molar concentration. The penetration depth z was expressed in centimeters to match the unit of ε (M− 1 cm− 1).

For the proposed fabrication condition, 2% Darocur 1173 was used as the photoinitiator, and its extinction coefficient at 365 nm was set to 0.16 M− 1 cm− 1. For comparison, UV attenuation was also calculated for LAP‐based photopolymerization systems reported in the literature. The concentrations were set to 5 mg mL−1 LAP and 9 mg mL−1 Quinoline Yellow (QY) for the LAP + QY case, and 0.10 wt% LAP for the 405 nm LAP case [30, 39]. The corresponding extinction coefficients were set to 20, 218, and 5474.6 M− 1 cm− 1 for LAP at 365 nm, QY, and LAP at 405 nm, respectively.

Fabrication of Hydrogel Tubes With Complex Geometries

Microfluidic channels with complex geometries, including curved and branching designs, were modeled and fabricated. The curved channel featured a winding geometry with a curvature of 0.4 mm−1, while the branching channel consisted of a main trunk part and two branches forming a 60° bifurcation angle. Hydrogel tubes with complex geometries were fabricated by sequentially assembling the rings within the corresponding microchannels. For the curved geometry, the microfluidic device mounted on a mechanical stage was rotated horizontally to align the rings along the curved channel trajectory. For the branching geometry, rings were first formed upstream and downstream of the bifurcation, followed by ring formation at the branching point to complete the structure.

Fabrication of Hydrogel Tubes With Multiple Compositions

Blue‐ and red‐dyed hydrogel solutions were prepared to fabricate a hydrogel tube with multiple compositions. Each dye was mixed with 80% PEGDA 700 at a concentration of 1 wt.%. First, a blue hydrogel solution and mineral oil were introduced into the microchannel, and a blue hydrogel tube was formed by UV irradiation. The channel was then rinsed with deionized water to remove the residual blue hydrogel solution. Subsequently, a red hydrogel solution and mineral oil were introduced, and red hydrogel tubes were fabricated next to and inside the preformed blue hydrogel tube to achieve axial and radial material distributions.

Automation of Continuous Ring Formation

For automated continuous ring formation, image processing was employed to detect the meniscus position continuously and adjust the UV irradiation location accordingly. Mineral oil was introduced at a flow rate of 0.01 µL s−1. Meniscus detection was performed by template matching of video frames captured and displayed in LabVIEW using a predefined meniscus template implemented in Python. The x‐coordinate of the detected meniscus was extracted and converted into the corresponding coordinate for the digital mask. A bitmap (BMP) file for the digital mask was generated using a rectangular irradiation pattern. A digital mask was applied to the micromirror array of the DMD using LabVIEW. The mask pattern was continuously updated to synchronize with the UV irradiation. In this experiment, a red‐colored hydrogel solution was used to enhance the accuracy of meniscus detection.
