<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-body field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><h3>Update</h3>

<p><strong>Washington, D.C., February 24, 2022</strong> – The nongovernmental National Security Archive’s Cyber Vault today supplemented one of its more popular resources – the National Security Agency’s (NSA) periodical <em>Cryptolog </em>– with newly released materials from the agency’s long-running in-house publication.&nbsp; In 2018, the Archive posted 136 publicly released issues of the publication. &nbsp;</p>

<p>Since then, Cyber Vault Fellow Cristin J. Monahan has filed and monitored over 100 Mandatory Declassification Review requests (and counting) to NSA for security classified articles, puzzles, interviews, and how-to guides found in <em>Cryptolog</em>.&nbsp;</p>

<p>Today’s update consists of newly declassified titles spanning the years 1974 to 1997.</p>

<p>Highlights of the new additions include the following:</p>

<ul>
	<li>A 1975 analysis of “<a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/r7by0p-nvlaj/026.pdf#page=2">Basic Patterns of Chinese Codes and Ciphers</a>,” which explains the application of “radical/stroke” order to Standard Telegraphic Code.</li>
	<li>Explorations of the effect of new technologies, including the <a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/r7bxj8-oxop2/003.pdf#page=2">adoption of the personal computer</a> by the Cryptanalytic Support Branch of the Office of Southeast Asia in the 1980s, when the “complexity and volume of [redacted] enciphered communications began to change rapidly.”</li>
	<li>A forward-looking 1987 piece, “<a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/r7bxj8-oxop2/009.pdf#page=2">Modem Communications</a>,” which hypothesizes about the signals intelligence (SIGINT) collection challenges presented by the transition from analog to digital communications.</li>
	<li>An address from Air Force Colonel Chris Christon in “<a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/27450-national-security-agency-cryptolog-vol-20-no-2-intelligence-combat-some-lessons">Intelligence and Combat: Lessons Learned from DESERT STORM</a>,” which asserts that while DESERT STORM was an overall intelligence success, the fact that “new weapons systems and tactics being developed are information-intensive” means that information transfer mechanisms are currently “not structured to support the timeliness that modern combat commands.”</li>
	<li>Warnings by 1995 from members of Cryptologic Support Groups about “<a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/27451-national-security-agency-cryptolog-vol-20-no-3-sigint-and-information-explosion-fall">the threat of the information explosion</a>” to the intelligence community, perhaps foreshadowing the challenges presented by open source intelligence (OSINT) nearly two decades later.</li>

<h3>Original posting</h3>

<p><strong>Washington, D.C., December 4, 2018</strong> - Five years ago, the National Security Agency (NSA) released 136 issues of its internal <em>Cryptolog</em> periodical spanning 1974 through 1997. The collection offered a look into the some of the discussions being held within one of America’s most secretive intelligence agencies. Today the GWU-based National Security Archive is providing a complete index of all 1,504 items in the declassified collection, including but not limited to articles, interviews, and puzzles.</p>cies. Today the GWU-based National Security Archive is providing a complete index of all 1,504 items in the declassified collection, including but not limited to articles, interviews, and puzzles.</p>

<p>By simply scanning article titles (see below) one can observe the shift in language as the NSA’s institutional grounding in cryptology and linguistics adjusts to accommodate and leverage developing information technology. As early as 1977,&nbsp;<em>Cryptolog</em> asks <a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/5301846/National-Security-Agency-Cryptolog-Vol-4-No-8.pdf#page=3">“What is an Information Research Analyst?”</a> and argues that information science is underutilized by the agency. The following year the topic of <a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/5301849/National-Security-Agency-Cryptolog-Vol-5-No-10.pdf#page=3">data security is considered</a>, only in terms of user and maintenance errors. By the mid-1980s writers give attention to the <a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/5301771/National-Security-Agency-Cryptolog-Vol-11-No-6-7.pdf#page=14">impact of the “Computer Age”</a> on the field of intelligence analysis and highlight <a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/5301782/National-Security-Agency-Cryptolog-Vol-13-No-12.pdf#page=31">packet-switching networks in the Soviet Union</a>. A series of educational articles on the UNIX shell <a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/5301880/National-Security-Agency-Cryptolog-Vol-9-No-4.pdf#page=22">begins</a> in 1984, seeking to raise awareness of the tool among analysts.</p>ttps://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/5301880/National-Security-Agency-Cryptolog-Vol-9-No-4.pdf#page=22">begins</a> in 1984, seeking to raise awareness of the tool among analysts.</p>

<p>The publication also serves as a venue for organizational reflection. <a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/5301858/National-Security-Agency-Cryptolog-Vol-5-No-9.pdf#page=19">Conflict between linguists and cryptanalysts</a> over promotions and pay scales rages in the pages of <em>Cryptolog</em> for two years, and a four-part series (<a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/5301751/National-Security-Agency-Cryptolog-Vol-1-No-2.pdf#page=18">1</a> <a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/5301752/National-Security-Agency-Cryptolog-Vol-1-No-3.pdf#page=13">2</a> <a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/5301753/National-Security-Agency-Cryptolog-Vol-1-No-4.pdf#page=17">3</a> <a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/5301754/National-Security-Agency-Cryptolog-Vol-1-No-5.pdf#page=16">4</a>) on the agency’s intern program is published in 1974. The publication also brought points of levity with appropriately vexing <a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/5301856/National-Security-Agency-Cryptolog-Vol-5-No-6.pdf#page=14">brainteasers</a> for cryptanalysts and a short-lived <a href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/5301885/National-Security-Agency-Cryptolog-Vol-20-No-2.pdf#page=52">bloopers</a> feature.</p>

<p>This index was produced in hopes of encouraging and facilitating&nbsp;historical research into the NSA’s evolution through, and consideration of, the information revolution.</p>
