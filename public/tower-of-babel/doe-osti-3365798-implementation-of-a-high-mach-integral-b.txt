ABSTRACT
The Momentum-Energy Integral Technique (MEIT) is an integral boundary layer method for the
high-Mach flow regime used to approximate heat transfer and viscous force quantities of interest
along streamlines of an inviscid flow solution on the surface of a flight vehicle. This method
allows rapid mid-fidelity estimation of these quantities which would otherwise require a much
more expensive viscous flow solution to produce. Integral boundary layer methods like MEIT
have been around for decades, though usually only formulated for simple geometries such as 2-
dimensional wing shapes or axi-symmetric nose shapes. The implementation discussed herein
has been generalized to apply to any 3-dimensional streamlined body geometry through correct
treatment of the curvilinear axes (streamline attached) momentum and energy entrainment terms,
and handling of arbitrary stagnation region geometry. This implementation is provided as a software
package for the Python environment, along with readers for common inviscid flow solution providers
such as NASA’s CART3D flow solver.

1. METHODS

1.1. Motivation

The flight envelope for a hypersonic vehicle is often limited by thermal concerns. At flight
Mach numbers larger than 5, the traditional definition of hypersonic flight, the surface temperature
achievable due to aerodynamic heating is high enough to melt metallic structures. Large temperature
gradients across the body develop due to order of magnitude differences in flow density and surface
heating between the wind-side and lee-side of lifting vehicles. Conceptual design and mission
planning activities for hypersonic vehicles require a rapid and flexible predictive capability for
aerodynamic heating to correctly account for these phenomena.

Aerodynamic heating and viscous forces on a vehicle in flight are dependent on the nature of the
thin viscous boundary layer that develops near the body surface. Direct prediction of the boundary
layer using high-fidelity viscous computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulation is possible, but
generally too computationally expensive and labor intensive for use in the conceptual design
process. Closed-form expressions for heating exist for simple geometries such as spherical nose
shapes and flat plate surfaces, though these are not detailed enough to capture a complex flight
vehicle geometry. Integral boundary layer (IBL) methods provide a more flexible semi-empirical
description of the boundary layer characteristics, driven by the local boundary layer edge flow
properties available from an inviscid CFD solution. A robust inviscid CFD solver coupled to a IBL
method can potentially provide reasonably accurate aerodynamic heating solutions for arbitrary
streamlined flight body geometry in a fully automated workflow, with compute times an order of
magnitude shorter than viscous CFD. This is very attractive for a conceptual design environment
where agility in comparative studies is required.

Many IBL techniques [1] [2] [3] target subsonic applications and generally focus on predictions of
the wall shear stress, as aerodynamic heating is less of a design driver in this flow regime. The
Momentum-Energy Integral Technique (MEIT) [4] is an integral boundary layer method focused
on the high-Mach regime with a models for the thermal boundary layer and associated wall heat
flux, along with the velocity boundary layer and shear stress. While versions of this method have
been in use for decades, they have mostly been restricted to either 2-dimensional geometries, such
as wing profile shapes, or bodies of revolution in axial flow. The implementation discussed herein
has been generalized to apply to any 3-dimensional streamlined body geometry through correct
treatment of the curvilinear axes (streamline attached) momentum and energy entrainment terms,
and handling of arbitrary stagnation region geometry.

1.2. MEIT Integral Boundary Layer Model

Integral boundary layer methods provide approximate solutions to the governing Navier-Stokes
equations after simplification for thin shear layer flows [2] [5]. These methods use empirically
derived constraints to form an a-priori parameterization for the velocity and total enthalpy profiles
across the wall-normal direction through the thin boundary layer, notionally depicted in Fig. 1-
1. The profiles terminate at the wall surface with zero velocity and user-specified wall enthalpy
boundary conditions, and at the boundary layer edge with the local flow properties taken from an
inviscid CFD solution. The specified wall enthalpy, ℎ𝑤 , represents the local temperature and gas
composition state at the wall surface, which could be a fixed value for the entire surface or possibly
specified from a coupled simulation of the body thermal response. The primary boundary layer
profile parameters describe the thickness of the velocity and total enthalpy profiles. Secondary
profile parameters and output quantities of interest, such as wall heat flux and shear stress, are
approximated as empirically determined functions of the primary profile parameters and edge flow
properties. With this parameterization describing the solution in the wall-normal direction, the
governing equations can be reduced to ordinary differential equations (ODE) for the evolution of
the primary boundary layer thickness parameters in the streamwise direction along the body. This
IBL framework is capable of exactly capturing canonical closed-form results, such as the evolution
of a boundary layer on a flat plate, while providing a data-driven mechanism for handling aspects
of flow around more complex bodies.

 12

The MEIT IBL method [4] approximates the compressible boundary layer equations with ODEs
for the momentum thickness, 𝜃, of the momentum deficit in the velocity profile, and the energy
thickness, 𝜙, of the energy deficit in the total enthalpy profile. These deficits represent the history
of heat and momentum transfer to the body from the flow due to viscous effects along a streamline.
The integral thickness quantities are defined in Eq. (1.1) and Eq. (1.2) and represent the length
scales associated with the profile deficits, which index the associated wall normal gradients in
velocity and total enthalpy. The integral thickness concept is depicted visually in Fig. 1-2 using
the momentum thickness as an example. The ODEs describing the streamwise evolution of 𝜃
and 𝜙, shown in Eq. (1.3) and Eq. (1.4), are an integral form of the governing boundary layer
equations written in curvilinear axes attached to the local streamlines. Physically, these equations
relate the streamwise gradient of profile momentum and energy deficit to the local wall shear stress
and heat flux, respectively, with additional source terms capturing momentum addition due to
streamwise pressure gradient, and entrainment terms related to the curvilinear axis system. The
entrainment effect is governed by the streamwise dilation rate, 𝑟12 𝑑𝑟𝑑𝑠2 , discussed in Section 1.4.
These ODEs require empirical closure models relating the profile shape factor, 𝐻, and the wall flux

implementation uses the closure models tailored for the high-Mach flight regime defined in [4].

1.4. Curvilinear Axes Entrainment

The integral boundary layer equations in Eq. (1.3) and Eq. (1.4) are formulated in curvilinear axes
attached to streamlines on the body surface. This axis system definition varies in space along a
streamline, relative to the inertial axes in which the fundamental dynamics are defined, generating
entrainment source terms in the curvilinear boundary layer dynamics equations [9]. Physically,
the entrainment terms in the dynamics equations account for the fact that, for example, constant
boundary layer profile properties along a streamline would imply an increasing flux of momentum
and energy along that streamline if the local streamtube is expanding. For this axis system, the
streamwise momentum and energy entrainment is quantified by the streamwise dilation rate, 𝑅𝑠 ,
defined in Eq. (1.14) from the streamwise metric length, 𝑟 2 .

1.5. Boundary Layer Edge Properties Definition

Empirical closure models for the IBL equations are generally driven by the local flow properties
defined at the boundary layer edge, a height 𝛿 off the wall surface in a viscous flow. However, the
inviscid flow simulations used to provide the local flow properties to an IBL method do not directly
represent the boundary layer, and there is some ambiguity around how best to define the boundary
layer edge properties from this solution. One convenient choice is to simply take the properties at
the wall in the inviscid flow solution as being representative of the boundary layer edge. This is to
assume that both the boundary layer thickness and the wall normal gradients of the inviscid flow

 19

solution are “small” relative to the nominal property values, and the product of these two small
quantities could be considered negligible.
However, for high-Mach flows around realistic vehicle geometries, the curved shock structure that
develops around sharp transitions in body geometry creates large entropy gradients near the wall
as the post-shock streamlines of varying entropy stack up in the downstream flow, as depicted
in Fig. 1-5. This effect creates the entropy layer phenomenon in an inviscid compressible flow
which can be observed, for instance, on the conical portion of a sphere-conical geometry. The flow
solution at the wall far downstream on the conical section remains at the stagnation line entropy
defined by the normal shock near the spherical leading edge, while the solution just a short distance
off-body shows the much-reduced entropy representative of the conical oblique shock generated
by the remainder of the body. This phenomenon produces significant gradients of density and
temperature in the wall normal direction, with these variations being inversely proportional such
that pressure is comparably less affected.
The presence of entropy layers in an inviscid compressible flow solution makes it potentially
important to evaluate the edge properties at some height off the wall best representative of the
boundary layer edge. However, it can be noted that the grid resolution in the wall normal direction
used in a typical inviscid flow simulation is generally not high enough to resolve the entropy
layer far downstream of a geometry transition. In this case, the numerical diffusion serves to
dissipate wall normal gradients due to the entropy layer, making the entropy of the wall solution
more representative of the local boundary layer edge flow. Because of this fact, internal validation
efforts using typical Euler grid resolution have shown that the error incurred from using the wall
solution to provide boundary layer edge properties is likely small enough to be ignored in most use
cases. However, this error could paradoxically become larger as the Euler grid resolution is refined.
The examples provided with the current implementation take the boundary layer edge properties
from the inviscid wall solution for convenience, though future versions may provide an off-body
evaluation option.

2. USAGE

2.1. Overview

The MEIT IBL method described in Section 1 has been implemented in the Python software
environment. In general, the user is expected to provide an inviscid flow solution representative
of the boundary layer edge state from an external CFD source on a triangular unstructured surface
grid as the principal input to the MEIT method. Read methods are provided for typical CFD
sources, such as NASA’s CART3D software [10]. These example readers be copied and modified
for any other user desired inviscid CFD source. The MEIT software computes streamlines from
each node in the surface grid back to the stagnation region, then initializes and solves the MEIT
ODEs back along that streamline to provide the boundary layer outputs at the original node. This
process can be executed in parallel across all nodes in the surface grid as each nodal calculation is
independent. Optionally, the software can automatically determine a subset of nodes from which to
run streamlines and interpolate these results to upstream nodes passed by the computed streamlines.
This technique can reduce compute time if few parallel resources are available.
It is often beneficial to restrict the MEIT solution to surfaces that are primarily wetted by the
freestream flow, where the inviscid flow solution is largely free of flow separation effects, and
where the IBL concepts generally apply. It is common practice for the user to tag shadowed
surfaces in the grid generation process which are likely to be enveloped in separated flow. These
surfaces, often called "base" surfaces, can be ignored in the MEIT streamline and ODE process,
and treated instead with a different empirical model, referred to as the "base model". Integer tag
values representing different surfaces are provided to the MEIT solution method through the 𝐶𝑜𝑚 𝑝
element of the inviscid CFD input data structures described below. These tags are typically setup
in process of generating the grid for the inviscid CFD solution. The CART3D .tri grid format, for
example, defines a 𝐶𝑜𝑚 𝑝𝑜𝑛𝑒𝑛𝑡𝐼 𝐷 field that serves this purpose. Any grid generation software that
writes .tri will allow the user to set that field for each sub-domain in the grid.
The empirical closure models defined for the current MEIT IBL implementation are generally
intended for use in the high supersonic and hypersonic Mach number regime. The current imple-
mentation has been shown to perform well in internal validation exercises at Mach numbers greater
than 2. Subsonic and transonic cases have not been tested, and other existing IBL methods [1] [2]
[3] are likely better suited to that Mach number regime.
The MEIT Python module consists of a main solution method and a number of pre and post
processing methods described further in the sections below, along with their inputs and outputs.
A typical problem workflow using CART3D to provide the inviscid CFD solution is depicted in
Fig. 2-1. The user should refer to the test execution script provided with the MEIT software
distribution for example usage details.

2.1.1.      ReadCART3D

This reader imports the inviscid flow solution grid and data from the CART3D dimensionless
surface solution output file (.triq) and dimensionalizes the data with the provided freestream
reference conditions. As CART3D provides a perfect gas flow solution, approximate real gas
boundary layer edge states are generated from the real gas equation of state (EOS) used internally
in the MEIT solution. This is done by assuming the enthalpy and pressure given by the perfect
gas solution are relevant, and recomputing all other states from the EOS, on the grounds that the
primary difference between a perfect gas and a real gas flow solution is in the way the internal
energy is carried. Note that if this reader is copied as the template for another that ingests a real
gas inviscid flow solution, this step can be eliminated. Only one EOS is currently implemented,
and is representative of equilibrium air up to 30,000 Kelvin [11]. The inputs to this inviscid flow
solution reader are shown in Table 2-1. The outputs required from any inviscid flow solution reader
