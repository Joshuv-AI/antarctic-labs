Abstract

The overarching goal of this multi-disciplinary project is to develop a systems-level understanding of interactions
and central activities involved in cycling of methane and nutrients by syntrophic methane-oxidizing archaeal-
bacterial consortia and their viral predators in anoxic sedimentary environments. Our experimental emphasis cuts
across scales that are important for understanding microbial and viral interactions and activities within their
habitats, as well as community wide biogeochemical transformations. We are focused on three main objectives
during this project: 1) Quantifying energy and nutrient exchange (eg. N, P, Fe and vitamins) within AOM consortia
and between ANME-bacterial partners. Specifically studying how the capacity for nitrogen fixation is spatially and
taxonomically distributed among archaea and bacteria in anaerobic methane-oxidizing (AOM) consortia, examining
other less common nutritional dependencies like sulfate in this anaerobic syntrophy, and assessing whether there
is vitamin resource sharing between microbial partners. 2) Identify virus-host interactions associated with AOM
and assess N transfer through viruses in sediment ecosystems. For example, what is the genetic and morphological
variation of active viruses infecting AOM consortia? What are the rates of viral production in methane-saturated
sediments and can we develop tools to study how viruses impact the fate of methane carbon and nitrogen in
sediment ecosystems? 3) Model energy and nutrient exchange in AOM consortia and viral-host interactions,
activity, and environmental distribution patterns based on predicted niche. Can we predict different syntrophic
mechanisms of energy exchange and potential metabolic complementation for distinct ANME-bacterial couplings
(e.g. N2- fixation and cobalamin sharing) using combined genome-based metabolic modeling and reaction
transport modeling?

Executive Summary:

This multi-disciplinary project was focused on developing a systems-level understanding of microbial syntrophic
interactions involved in the anaerobic oxidation of methane between methanotrophic archaea and syntrophic
bacteria in multi-celled consortia. We examined the role of nutrient exchange and conducted preliminary
characterizations of viruses hypothesized to target methane-oxidizing consortia in anoxic environments. Our
experiments cut across spatial scales that are relevant for microbial and viral interactions and activities within their
habitats while also tracking community wide biogeochemical transformations. We had three main objectives for
this work. Our first objective was to develop and apply compatible methods for examining energy and nutrient
exchange within environmental archaeal-bacterial consortia catalyzing the anaerobic oxidization of methane
(AOM). This included a detailed single-cell based study of how nitrogen fixation is spatially distributed within
consortia and taxonomically partitioned between co-occurring archaea and bacteria (Metcalfe et al., 2022) and
investigations of other nutritional dependencies including sulfate assimilation by ANME archaea (Yu et al.,2023)
and the potential for vitamin (cobalamin) exchange between specific archaeal-bacterial pairings. Our second
objective was to molecularly characterize the viruses coexisting in methane-saturated sediments and identify
potential virus-host interactions associated with AOM. Here we applied metagenomics and characterized
metagenome assembled genomes (MAG’s) for archaea and bacterial lineages and co-associated viruses, and
discovered a new basal clade of ANME-1 archaea related to anaerobic alkane-oxidizing archaea along with new
viruses that are predicted to infect these archaea (Laso-Perez et al., 2022). TEM analyses of the viral-like particle
fraction from methane-saturated sediment revealed substantial morphological diversity of tailed, non-tailed, and
lemon-shaped virions along with previously undescribed morpho-types. Our third objective focused on developing
modeling approaches to model energy exchange within methanotrophic consortia of ANME archaea and
syntrophic sulfate-reducing bacteria, exploring approaches to link genome-based metabolic modeling with
reaction transport models. Collectively, our work has provided new understanding of syntrophic interactions
underlying the anaerobic oxidation of methane, a microbially controlled process that is important in methane-
saturated environments lacking oxygen and provided information on the co-associated viruses, a poorly
understood component of these gas-rich ecosystems. Through this collaborative effort, we have begun to develop
multi-disciplinary isotopic, molecular, microscopy, and modeling-based methods for studying microbial
interactions in the context of complex communities- approaches that are relevant in a broad range of
environments of interest to the Department of Energy.

Original Project Goals and Achievements: Our multidisciplinary project outlined several objectives towards
enhancing our understanding of nutrient and energy exchange within different environmental syntrophic
methane-oxidizing archaea and bacterial consortia (AOM consortia). Central to this overarching goal was
developing a curated a genomic framework of the major lineages of environmental methane-oxidizing archaea and
their sulfate-reducing bacterial partners, as many of the key groups were poorly resolved in public databases, with
low quality genome assemblies. We published two in depth comparative genomics studies both in Plos Biology-
one focusing on the major lineages of ANME archaea (Chadwick et al. 2022) and the other describing the validated
groups of syntrophic sulfate-reducing bacteria (Murali et al 2023). This critical framing enabled the identification of
lineage specific trends in nutrient exchange (one of the outlined goals in our first objective), specifically
demonstrating species specific variation in diazotrophy (Metcalfe et al., 2021) and genomic evidence for variation
in vitamin requirements and exchange within methane-oxidizing consortia depending on the specific sulfate-
reducing syntrophic partner (Murali et al., 2023). Additionally, these paired high quality ANME archaea and sulfate-
reducing bacterial genomes were used for the first phase of developing genome-resolved metabolic modeling of
AOM consortia (with Co-I Chris Henry) a key goal of our third Objective. In tandem with these genome-focused
efforts, our spatially resolved single cell resolved FISH-nanoSIMS activity experiments on AOM consortia and a well
characterized model electrogenic bacteria (Geobacter sp.) were used as a testing ground for reaction transport-
based (pore scale) modeling with Co-I Meile. Empirical and modeling data were remarkably consistent and enabled
the identification of key variables likely contributing to the spatial constraints between syntrophic partners reliant
on direct interspecies electron transfer, or in the case of Geobacter, distance to the electrode surface. The
genome-based community metabolic models and reaction transfer modeling approaches are highly synergistic and
our collective goal is to work towards developing an integrated framework called “Community Metabolic Model
Informed Reaction Transport” (CoMMIRT) for modeling syntrophic interactions and associated biogeochemical
transformations catalyzed by anaerobic methane-oxidizing archaeal-bacterial consortia in the context of their pore
scale environment. We successfully laid the groundwork through this proposal, with the larger goal of integration
in the future. Our final objective was to investigate the role viruses play in these communities, using combinations
of metaviromic and image analysis to characterize co-occurring viruses in methane-saturated environments and
link them to their potential hosts. Prior to our work, viruses associated with methanotrophic consortia had not
been characterized. Our metagenomic investigations identified novel viruses that are predicted to infect specific
lineages of ANME archaea (Laso-Perez et al., 2022) and revealed evidence of horizontal gene transfer of a key gene
in DNA synthesis from virus to archaea, providing a clear example of the influence of these nanometer-sized
predators on their methane-oxidizing hosts. To advance understanding of environmental virus-host dynamics, we
also worked with Co-I Martinez-Garcia to develop methods for fluorescence-based identification, flow cytometry
sorting, and sequencing of new viral progeny and translationally active microbial cells in environmental samples.
The initial stages of methods development were largely focused on enhancing the fluorescence signal from the
click reaction with nanometer-sized viral particles as reliable detection by flow cytometry proved challenging
relative to fluorescence microscopy assays. This fluorescence enhancement was successful, and viral particles were
detected by flow cytometry from environmental samples. Once fully optimized, this novel activity-based sorting
approach combined with single viral sequencing technologies, will provide the ability to dynamically track and
genomically characterize translationally active cells and newly produced viruses alongside inactive populations.
The next section highlights our accomplishments in greater detail.

Accomplishments: This project contributed several key advancements in our understanding of anaerobic methane-
metabolizing syntrophic associations alongside the development of novel methodologies that will help advance the
field described below.

Nitrogen fixation, sulfate-assimilation, and predicted cobalamin auxotrophies within AOM syntrophic consortia
Significant phylogenetic variation exists among co-occurring methanotrophic ANME archaea and sulfate-reducing
bacterial (SRB) consortia which is frequently difficult to resolve with conventional metagenomic sequencing of bulk
samples. Using our previously developed method which combines fluorescence tagging of translationally active
cells in environmental samples using BONCAT (i.e. Biorthogonal Noncanonical Amino Acid Tagging) and
fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS), we sorted individual AOM consortia from methane-rich environments
and reconstructed metagenome assembled genomes (MAGS) from paired ANME and SRB to examine the

 3

metabolic basis for the partnership specificity. This unique consortia-based perspective confirmed that both ANME
and SRB partners in consortia encode the ability to fix nitrogen, however experimental testing using 15N2 with FISH-
nanoSIMS revealed that diazotrophic activity often varied between consortia, with either the ANME partner
showing strong 15N2 assimilation with lower assimilation by their SRB partner, or the converse, where the SRB
showed high levels of nitrogen fixation with little to no signal observed in the co-associated ANME archaea. This
work was published in 2021 co-authored by graduate student Metcalfe and postdoc Murali (Metcalfe et al.,2021,
ISMEJ) and featured as one of Dr. Metcalfe’s thesis chapters (Metcalfe, K., 2020). Whether this variation among
co-occurring AOM consortia in sediment is driven by external environmental factors, or driven by underlying
genetic differences is an outstanding question that can be tackled with improved strain-resolved comparative
genomics and modeling.

Alongside variation in nitrogen utilization, we also uncovered evidence of species diversification and variation in
the ability to synthesize essential amino acid and specific cofactors required for cobalamin biosynthesis, where this
ability appears to have been lost in reconstructed genomes of the Seep-SRB1a partners physically associated with
methanotrophic ANME-2a, while surprisingly closely related Seep-SRB1a partnering with ANME-2c retained this
trait. This comparative genomics-based characterization was published by postdoc Murali in 2023 (Murali et al.,
2023 Plos Biology) and future work will allow these predicted nutrient-based dependencies to be tested further
through a combination of experimental isotope labeling experiments, ‘multi-omics analysis (RNA or protein), and
ongoing metabolic modeling efforts with co-I Chris Henry and Christof Meile. Collectively, our work demonstrates
the power of using BONCAT-FACS with single consortia sequencing to resolve genomic information for
interdependent syntrophic partners within complex microbial communities. We note that methods development
and single consortia sequencing for our BONCAT-FACS study was made possible through collaboration with the
Joint Genome Institute (JGI) and Environmental Molecular Science Laboratory (EMSL) as part of DOE’s FICUS
program.

Most ecological investigations of nutrient limitation have focused on major nutrients like nitrogen or phosphorous
or requirements for trace elements and vitamins, however but other nutrients and their redox forms are rarely
explored. In our methane-oxidizing syntrophic association, sulfate has been investigated in the context of a key
terminal electron acceptor for the sulfate-reducing bacterial partner, however the importance of sulfate as a
nutrient in the methanotrophic archaea was overlooked. Our metagenomic based characterization of ANME
archaea previously identified the presence of an assimilatory sulfate-reduction pathway (i.e. PAPS) and also
included an unusual F420 sulfite reductase (FSR) which appears to be an ancient fusion protein. These genomic
observations pointed to the potential for sulfate assimilation, however the importance of sulfate to the ANME- be
it as a nutrient, or used as part of a novel respiratory pathway, was unresolved. Through a series of laboratory
stable isotope probing experiments, we created sulfate-free incubation conditions containing AQDS as an
alternative electron acceptor which maintained the methanotrophic activity the ANME archaea in the absence of
their sulfate-reducing bacterial partner and compared single cell anabolic activity (nanoSIMS) and transcriptomic
response of ANME relative to incubation conditions containing sulfate- either supplied as a nutrient (µM
concentrations) or as a potential terminal electron acceptor (mM). This combined methodological approach
demonstrated the importance of sulfate as a nutrient for ANME archaea, stimulating increased anabolism and
methane-oxidation coupled to AQDS reduction (Figure 1; Yu et al., 2022 ISMEJ). Following up on this work, we
worked with collaborators to heterologously express the unusual fusion F420-sulfite reductase protein (FSR) from
ANME-2 archaea in a genetically tractable model methanogen, in attempt to better characterize its functional role.
This work unexpectedly showed that unlike the previously characterized sulfite reductases in bacteria, the ANME
FSR protein reduces nitrite rather than sulfite, opening up new questions about the physiological potential of these
archaea and their role in nitrogen cycling in sulfate and methane-rich environments, as well as the evolutionary
history of this protein family (Heryakusuma et al., 2022 J. Bact).

Optimized Viral BONCAT-FACS for quantifying and sequencing active cells and viruses from the environment.
Optimization of the fluorescence signal of viral-BONCAT was critical for successful flow cytometry sorting of
positive tagged BONCAT-viruses and after two years of methods development, we finally have a robust protocol
for the simultaneous fluorescent labeling, sorting, and differentiation of active bacteriophages and cells from
inactive ones in the environment. While fluorescence detection of BONCAT stained phage had been previously
demonstrated by our group using fluorescence microscopy, discrimination of the fluorescence signal of stained
viruses by flow cytometry is challenging, as this signal is often just above the background noise of the instrument.
Optimization steps included conducting the BONCAT reaction under anoxic conditions, eliminating reactive oxygen
species production, and extending the click reaction time 6-fold. Carrying out the assay at elevated temperatures
was also observed to enhance the signal, likely by relaxing the protein structure and exposing the incorporated
HPG residues to the CLICK reagents. We successfully implemented tests of this optimized viral-BONCAT method in
flow cytometry sorting centers in Europe and within the US, providing evidence of the robustness of the method
across different instruments and users. We are now applying this method to study the dynamics between newly
produced lytic viruses and their host cells directly in environmental samples, including methane-rich sediments
supporting the anaerobic oxidation of methane.

Optimized sample preparation and nanoSIMS analysis of viruses in sediment samples The recovery of viral-like
particles from complex soil and sediment matrices poses several challenges relative to water column studies. As
experienced with our viral BONCAT-FACS development in collaboration with Co-I Martinez-Garcia, our progress
with optimizing viral-nanoSIMS for sediment hosted viruses was slow and required several iterations before a
robust method was developed. We have recently demonstrated we can recover, map, and analyze isotopic
enrichment in single viral particles recovered from stable isotope probing experiments with methane-saturated

sediments and have published a detailed annotated method through protocols.io (Narayanan and Philosof, 2025).
With this method in hand, we plan to leverage this unique approach in future work to track nitrogen and methane
transfer into sediment viruses after infection of metabolically active microbial hosts.

Identification of a new lineage of methanotrophic archaea (Candidatus Methanospirareceae) and associated
viruses In tandem with the development of new FACS methods for characterizing newly produced viruses, we also
completed metagenomic and metaviromic characterization of a new methanotrophic archaeal lineage (ANME-1c;
Candidatus Methanospirareceae) and their associated viruses, with up to 16 new phage families identified. The
ANME-1c group, like previously described ANME-1 lineages was observed by microscopy to form loose associations
with bacteria, consistent with syntrophic AOM, as well as occurring as single cells or mono-specific cell clusters.
These archaea have a reduced genome size and comparison with other ANME-1 lineages, had notable differences
in their methyl coenzyme reductase (presence of mcrD subunit), as well as exhibiting a mosaic-like distribution of
nickel-iron hydrogenases across different subclades, suggesting hydrogen utilization may have been an early trait
in the ANME-1 order (Figure 2). ANME-1 associated viruses possessed several auxiliary metabolic genes (AMG’s)
predicted to be involved in nucleotide, amino acid, phosphate, and sulfur anabolic pathways. Notably, the
presence of ThyX (which catalyzes dUMP methylation into dTMP) in several ANME-1 infecting viral families
supports a key role of these viruses in the transition between the use of the widespread thyA gene in many
archaea to that of ThyX, a differentiating feature between ANME-1 and other ANME lineages (Figure 3). This
example of viral mediated gene displacement offers a concrete example of the widespread impact viruses have on
the ecology of these methane-utilizing archaea. This work was published in 2023 (Laso-Perez et al., 2023, Nature
Microbiology).

Ultrastructural insights into ANME-SRB syntrophy. Multicellular ANME-2 and Seep-SRB consortia recovered from
nature are highly structured in terms of spatial relationships between the syntrophic partners, as well as the
extracellular matrix binding these consortia. To better characterize the complex structure within individual
consortia, we developed protocols for improving 3D reconstructions within consortia with co-I Ellisman’s group at
NCMIR, with advancements in sample preparation (AOM consortia concentration from sediment, mapping
consortia location in prepared blocks with XRM, and targeted imaging of key consortia using either serial block face
imaging, or thick sectioning followed by tilt tomography. This 3D high-resolution imaging approach revealed the
occurrence of small extracellular vesicles (EV’s) within consortia that are released by the sulfate-reducing bacterial
partner in the extracellular matrix between cells. EV’s have been shown to be involved in communication and
exchange between microorganisms in other systems and it is likely these serve as an important mechanism of
communication either between co-occurring SRB or, as an integral part of the interdomain exchange within this
methane-oxidizing syntrophic association. This work additionally provided quantitative data on membrane
invaginations that are characteristic of multiple SRB partners. These invaginations are reminiscent of mitochondria
and methanotrophic bacteria and through our 3D datasets were shown to be attached to the inner membrane and
contained redox active heme. These ultrastructural insights are
consistent with genomic and transcriptomic predictions of the
production of heme-containing cytochromes by the sulfate-reducing

A modeling perspective of interactions within AOM consortia In collaboration with Co-I Meile, we studied the
effect of aggregate size and modes of electron exchange within uncultured ANME-SRB consortia and compared
these results to empirical single cell FISH-nanoSIMS datasets (Chadwick, G, 2021 PhD Dissertation, Caltech). This
work parameterized the relationship between growth and electron exchange at a distance through EET and
uncovered the potential for a mixed mode of syntrophic exchange in ANME-SRB consortia that relies on a
combination of both EET and formate transfer (He et al., 2021 mBIO; He, X 2020, PhD dissertation, UGA).
Modeling comparisons with a well-studied electrogenic bacteria, Geobacter, growing as a biofilm on the electrode
surface (e.g. Chadwick et al., 2019 PNAS) further supported the key parameters in our AOM EET model (He et al.,
2021, ChemElectroChem). In collaboration with colleagues in Belgium, we additionally extended our single cell-
resolved FISH-nanoSIMS investigations of microbial activity in structured microbial communities to anaerobic
consortia not known to be reliant on extracellular electron transfer, providing a point of comparison to the spatial
relationships in activity observed with electrogenic biofilms and consortia, here exploring how activity may
influence the unusual lenticular shape of these anaerobic medium chain carboxylic acid producing communities
(Candry et al., 2023 ISMEJ). These unique datasets on spatially resolved cell-specific activity … Our initial 13C
formate isotope labeling experiments used to text this hypothesis proved complicated and not definitive as NMR
analyses showed that there was rapid conversion between formate and CO2 at a near instantaneous rate that
could not be linked to the metabolism of the AOM consortia. Alternative (omics-based) approaches will likely be
required to further investigate this alternative mode of methane-fueled syntrophy.
