Navigation-Assisted Extra-articular Approach for Curettage of a Subchondral Bone Cyst of the Humeral Trochlea
Authors: Tasuku Miyake, Ryoya Shiode, Kunihiro Oka, Satoshi Miyamura, Toru Iwahashi, Natsuki Yamamoto, Hiroki Kondo, Tsuyoshi Murase, Seiji Okada, Hiroyuki Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Subchondral bone cysts are intraosseous cystic lesions typically associated with degenerative joint diseases in weight-bearing joints; however, their occurrence in the humeral trochlea is extremely rare. We report a case of a 19-year-old Japanese archer who presented with a 2-year history of right elbow pain. CT revealed an 8- × 4- × 4-mm cystic lesion with partial disruption of the subchondral bone plate on the lateral aspect of the humeral trochlea. As arthroscopic access is technically challenging and intra-articular curettage through the joint surface would damage the cartilage, a navigation-assisted extra-articular approach was selected. A patient-specific three-dimensional surgical plan was designed to achieve cylindrical excision of the lesion and was reconstructed with a β-tricalcium phosphate bone substitute. Postoperative CT scans confirmed complete removal of the cyst. Postoperatively, the patient's elbow pain gradually improved and had completely resolved at 14 months. This case demonstrates that navigation-assisted extra-articular curettage is a feasible technique for treating deep subchondral bone cysts of the humeral trochlea while preserving the articular cartilage.

Subchondral bone cysts are intraosseous cystic lesions that commonly occur in association with degenerative joint diseases, such as osteoarthritis or posttraumatic changes, and are most frequently reported in weight-bearing joints, including the hip, knee, and ankle.1-5 However, they can also develop in young individuals without degenerative changes resulting from repeated mechanical stress or microtrauma during sports activities.6,7 These lesions usually form beneath articular surfaces subjected to high or repetitive mechanical loading, and their presence has been associated with progressive loss of articular cartilage and an increased risk of subsequent joint replacement surgery.8

Surgical options for subchondral bone cysts include arthroscopic curettage, arthroscopic bone and cartilage grafting, and subchondroplasty using calcium phosphate bone substitute material, depending on the lesion size, symptoms, and extent of joint involvement.9-11 However, subchondral bone cysts arising from the humeral trochlea are extremely rare, and a standardized surgical approach for this location has not been established. Given that the trochlear region is deeply situated and anatomically constrained, arthroscopic or intra-articular access is technically demanding.12 In particular, retrograde drilling toward the trochlea is often restricted by the limited approach angle, and transolecranon or other intra-articular approaches may require violation of the intact ulna or articular surface. Consequently, achieving accurate curettage while preserving the overlying cartilage is challenging.13

Recently, computer-assisted navigation systems have been applied to elbow arthroscopy to enable precise intra-articular localization and real-time tracking of surgical instruments.14 This technology has been shown to enhance the accuracy and reproducibility of procedures, such as arthroscopic arthroplasty for elbow osteoarthritis, thereby improving the precision and safety of orthopaedic surgical procedures.15

In the present case, we applied this navigation technique to preoperatively identify the exact three-dimensional location of a trochlear subchondral bone cyst and performed extra-articular curettage safely and accurately while preserving the articular cartilage.

Case Presentation

A 19-year-old male patient presented with a 2-year history of right elbow pain. He is an accomplished archer who won a prefectural championship. Pain occurred during elbow motion, particularly when drawing the bow, and during daily activities such as brushing his teeth or washing his hair. The patient had no relevant medical history. Conservative treatment at another clinic resulted in no improvement, and his high level of activity rendered conservative management inadequate. Physical examination revealed no swelling, deformities, or tenderness. Elbow motion was full and symmetrical; however, pain was elicited when the elbow was flexed beyond 90°. Elbow function was assessed using the Japanese Orthopedic Association (JOA) elbow score (maximum, 100 points), which evaluates pain, function, range of motion, stability, and deformity.16 The mean preoperative JOA elbow score was 90. Plain radiography showed no obvious abnormal findings (Figure 1). CT revealed an 8- × 4- × 4-mm subchondral cystic lesion in the trochlea with partial disruption of the subchondral bone plate (Figure 2). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed to evaluate lesion characteristics and the condition of the overlying articular cartilage and showed a lesion with low signal intensity on T1-weighted images and high signal intensity on T2-weighted images without suppression of short tau inversion recovery sequences, consistent with a subchondral bone cyst. The overlying articular cartilage remained intact (Figure 3). Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with a subchondral bone cyst of the humeral trochlea.

Arthroscopic access was deemed technically challenging owing to the deep trochlear location, narrow working space, and limited visualization. Intra-articular curettage would also require breaching intact cartilage, risking iatrogenic injury. Therefore, a navigation-assisted extra-articular approach was selected.

For surgical planning, three-dimensional models of the distal humerus, proximal radius, proximal ulna, and cyst were reconstructed from CT data. A 9-mm cylindrical tunnel was virtually planned from the posterior capitellum to allow safe cyst removal while minimizing bone loss (Figure 4).

Preoperative three-dimensional surgical planning. The long axis of the lesion is oriented posteriorly toward the capitellum. A virtual surgical simulation is done to plan a cylindrical resection (diameter, 9 mm) from the posterior aspect of the capitellum using navigation guidance, followed by defect reconstruction with a bone substitute.

Diagnostic arthroscopy confirmed intra-articular findings and revealed a subtle cartilage defect not visible on preoperative MRI (Figure 5). Using a navigation system (Stryker Navigation System), the reference tracker was fixed to the posterior surface of the distal humerus (Figure 6, A). Paired-point registration was first done by digitizing six anatomic landmarks using a navigation pointer, followed by surface-matching registration with 30 surface points to align with the preoperative three-dimensional model (Figure 6, B and C). A Kirschner wire was inserted from the lateral extra-articular surface of the humerus along the preplanned trajectory using a miniopen approach to avoid potential neurovascular injury (Figure 7, A). Using an 8-mm Osteochondral Autograft Transfer System, a cylindrical bone tunnel was created to the predetermined depth according to the preoperative plan (Figure 7, B). During the procedure, an arthroscope and an intramedullary endoscope were concurrently used to confirm that the drilling trajectory did not penetrate the joint space. A β-tricalcium phosphate bone substitute (SUPER PORE; HOYA Technosurgical), shaped to match the harvested cancellous bone core, was implanted into the tunnel and firmly affected to fill the defect (Figure 7, C).

Postoperative radiographs and CT images confirmed the complete disappearance of the cystic lesion (Figure 8). To assess the accuracy of the navigation-guided drilling, postoperative three-dimensional simulation was compared with the preoperative plan. The deviation between the planned and actual entry-point centers was 1.4 mm, and the angular deviation around the drilling axis was 5.7°, demonstrating the high accuracy and reproducibility of the navigation-assisted extra-articular approach.

Postoperative radiographs and CT images. A, Postoperative plain radiographs. B, Postoperative CT images showing disappearance of the subchondral bone cyst (arrowheads).

Postoperatively, the patient's elbow pain during flexion and daily activities gradually improved, and he was able to perform routine tasks without discomfort. At 14 months postoperatively, the patient reported complete subjective resolution of pain, with a JOA elbow score of 100 points.

Discussion

Various surgical techniques for subchondral bone cysts, including arthroscopic curettage and bone grafting, have been reported. However, these methods are generally applied to more accessible joints and are intended to decompress the lesion and restore subchondral bone integrity.11 Subchondral bone cysts of the elbow are rarely described in the literature, and to our knowledge, no previous reports have detailed the surgical management of cysts arising from the humeral trochlea. For trochlear lesions, most commonly osteochondritis dissecans, techniques such as retrograde drilling and curettage with transolecranon microfracture have been reported.13,17 Standard intra-articular arthroscopic approaches to the humeral trochlear are technically demanding owing to anatomical constraints and inevitably cause damage to the articular cartilage when curettage of the subchondral bone cysts is attempted. Although retrograde drilling preserves the cartilage, it relies on two-dimensional fluoroscopic guidance, which makes accurate localization and control of the drilling trajectory more challenging.13

Contrastingly, the navigation-assisted extra-articular approach provided real-time three-dimensional visualization of the drilling trajectory. This enabled precise control of both drilling angle and depth with millimeter-level accuracy. Previous validation studies of navigation-assisted elbow surgery have reported registration accuracies of 0.96 mm for the humerus and 0.85 mm for the ulna.14 A simulated arthroscopic study also demonstrated a mean spatial error of 1.13 mm during navigation-assisted elbow procedures.15 The accuracy achieved in the present case is comparable to the reported deviation (1 to 2 mm) of navigation-assisted retrograde drilling of talar osteochondritis dissecans lesions18,19 and to the mean distance to target lesion of approximately 0.9 mm reported for navigation-assisted retrograde drilling in the knee.20 These findings suggest that the precision achieved reflects the intrinsic spatial accuracy of the navigation system rather than the specific anatomical site. Even in the deeply situated and anatomically constrained humeral trochlea, a similar level of accuracy was obtained.

This report has some limitations. First, it describes a single case with a relatively short follow-up period; therefore, long-term outcomes, including cyst recurrence, remain unclear. Second, although navigation provides excellent geometric accuracy, its long-term impact on joint preservation is not fully established. Third, navigation-assisted procedures require specialized equipment, which may increase cost and limit availability in some institutions. Fourth, histological confirmation was not obtained because no sufficient tissue sample was available for biopsy during the procedure. Larger studies with longer follow-up and comparisons with fluoroscopy-guided techniques are needed to confirm clinical efficacy and cost-effectiveness. Nonetheless, navigation-assisted extra-articular curettage appears to be a safe, precise, and minimally invasive option for treating humeral trochlea subchondral bone cysts while preserving the articular cartilage.

Conclusion

This report describes a rare humeral trochlea subchondral bone cyst successfully treated with navigation-assisted extra-articular curettage. Three-dimensional preoperative planning with intraoperative navigation enabled precise curettage while preserving the articular surface. The patient achieved complete pain relief at 14 months postoperatively, and minimal deviation between the planned and actual drilling trajectories (1.4 mm and 5.7°) demonstrated high targeting accuracy.
