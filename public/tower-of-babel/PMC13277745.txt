Exploring the Role of HER1 and HER2 Gene Variants in Breast Cancer Susceptibility: A Case‐Control Study in Bangladesh
Authors: Rana Tabassum, Abdullah Al Abid, Sheikh Shajia Islam Shithi, Saidul Islam, Usrat Rifat Binte Rafique, Shamima Shultana, Md Mohiuddin, Silvia Tanjum, Md Aminul Haque
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsBreast cancer (BC) remains one of the leading causes of cancer‐related deaths among women worldwide. The HER1 and HER2 genes, both members of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) family, have been extensively studied for their role in tumor development and progression. This study aims to investigate the association between BC risk and the HER1 rs11543848 and HER2 rs1136201 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the Bangladeshi population.MethodsThis case‐control study comprised 112 BC patients from National Cancer Research Institute Hospital, Mohakhali, Dhaka, Bangladesh, and 124 age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls (HCs) from nearby communities. Genotyping of HER1 rs11543848 and HER2 rs1136201 polymorphisms were carried out using the Tetra‐Primer Amplification Refractory Mutation System PCR (T‐ARMS PCR) method.Results HER1 rs11543848 showed a strong association with BC risk under additive, dominant, and over‐dominant models (p < 0.001), suggesting both GA and AA genotypes increase susceptibility. In contrast, HER2 rs1136201 showed no significant association in most models, except for a modest effect under the over‐dominant model (p = 0.041), indicating a potential heterozygote influence.ConclusionOur findings reveal a significant association between the HER1 rs11543848 polymorphism and BC risk in the Bangladeshi population, while the HER2 rs1136201 polymorphism shows limited association. This highlights the need for larger studies across diverse ethnic groups to validate these genetic factors influencing BC susceptibility.Keywords: breast cancer, cancer risk, genetic polymorphism, HER1 gene, HER2 gene, oncology

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is one of the most prevalent and deadliest diseases that is taking the lives of women all across the globe. BC has become one of the most common cancers, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases reported in the year 2020 alone and a staggering total of 685,000 deaths [1]. As per the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), the number of novel BC cases is anticipated to exceed 3 million annually by 2040, with over 1 million fatalities per year [2]. In Bangladesh alone, the BC incidence rate is about 22.5 per 100,000 women, with a higher prevalence of 19.3 per 100,000 among women aged 15–44 years [3]. Furthermore, BC accounts for 69% of all cancer‐related deaths among women in Bangladesh, indicating a significant public health issue and a substantial burden on domestic healthcare [4].

The epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) family, characterized by a tyrosine kinase domain, is crucial to the development of BC, as it directs cell growth, proliferation, differentiation, and survival [5]. The human EGFR family comprises four closely related receptors: HER1, HER2, HER3, and HER4 [6]. The HER1 receptor is a transmembrane protein that plays a crucial role in cell signaling pathways that control cell division, differentiation, adhesion, migration, and survival [7]. Upon binding of established ligands like EGF or TGF‐alpha to the extracellular domain of the HER1 receptor, dimerization occurs, which can lead to either homo‐ or heterodimerization with other members of the EGFR family, resulting in the autophosphorylation of the tyrosine kinase that activates downstream signaling pathways [8]. The HER1 receptor overexpression is observed in approximately 7%–43% of BCs, with an elevated incidence in aggressive subtypes such as inflammatory breast cancer (IBC) and triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) [5]. In the HER1 gene itself, a single nucleotide variation called the R497K polymorphism (rs11543848) occurs at 497 codons situated in the extracellular domain where arginine (R) is substituted to lysine (K), which has been identified in BC along with gliomas, lung and colorectal cancer [9]. According to the research undertaken regarding colorectal carcinoma patients, the R497k (rs11543848) polymorphism reduced the activation of its target genes, and it was hypothesized that it could be a potential determinant for reduced tumor recurrence in stage 2 or stage 3 colorectal cancer patients undergoing surgery to remove all malignant tissue [10]. Moreover, several studies have been conducted to ascertain an association between the HER1‐rs11543848 polymorphism and BC risk; nevertheless, definitive results remain elusive [11].

The HER2 receptor is another member of the EGFR family that plays a key role in regulating cell proliferation, survival, and differentiation. Unlike the other HER family members, HER2 has no known direct ligand and exists as a monomer [12]. It undergoes activation by forming heterodimers with ligand‐bound HER1, HER3, or HER4 or by homodimerization when it is overexpressed, which is commonly observed in cancer cells [12]. Upon activation, it leads to autophosphorylation of specific tyrosine residues in the intracellular domain, which activates multiple signaling pathways (P13K, MAPK, PKC) that regulate cell growth and proliferation [13]. It has been reported that overexpression of HER2 gene or protein expression occurs in 25%–30% of BCs, leading to activation of HER2 signaling pathways independent of ligand binding and thus driving tumorigenesis [14]. The HER2 rs1136201 polymorphism is a single nucleotide polymorphism in the transmembrane domain of the HER2 gene that results in the substitution of isoleucine (Ile) by valine (Val) at codon 655 [15]. This particular SNP increases receptor dimerization, tyrosine kinase activity, and autophosphorylation, which further enhances the tumor progression. Studies have reported that BCs can have up to 50 copies of the HER2 gene leading to 40–100‐fold increase in HER2 protein level, which results in 2 million HER2 receptors expressed on the surface of each tumor cell [16]. According to a study in the Pakistani population, this SNP showed significant association with the susceptibility of BC, whereas another study on women from Northeastern Brazil found no statistically significant association [17, 18]. Moreover, meta‐analyses and other population‐based studies have reported conflicting results, with some supporting the association of this SNP with BC susceptibility, especially in the Asian population, while others found no significant link [15, 19].

Genetic susceptibility to diseases like BC often varies significantly across various ethnicities due to differences in allele frequency, gene–environment interactions, and lifestyle factors. Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate specific single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within diverse populations to understand their relevance in distinct genetic backgrounds. To address this gap, our study aimed to examine the association of two polymorphisms HER1–rs11543848 and HER2– rs1136201 with BC risk in the Bangladeshi population, where genetic data on BC susceptibility remain limited. We believe our findings will provide valuable population‐specific insights and contribute to a broader understanding of genetic risk factors for BC across various ethnic groups.

Materials and Methods

Materials

TaKaRa Taq DNA Polymerase Master Mix and DNA ladder were obtained from TAKARA BIO INC. (Japan). Tris‐borate‐EDTA (TBE) buffer was purchased from Solarbio Life Sciences (China), and SeaKem LE Agarose was sourced from Lonza (USA). Genomic DNA was extracted using a kit supplied by Favorgen (USA). Oligonucleotide primers were synthesized by Macrogen (Korea). Midori Green staining dye was obtained from Nippon Genetics (Europe).

Study Subjects

This case‐control study involved 236 Bangladeshi women, consisting of 112 BC patients and 124 HCs matched by age and gender, who were enrolled between April 1, 2023, and June 30, 2023. Individuals aged 18 to 50 years with a BMI of 18.5–24.9 kg/m2 who had significant physical or neurological illnesses, identified through comprehensive physical, neurological, and laboratory assessments, were excluded from the study. Histopathologically confirmed BC patients (cases) and age‐matched healthy individuals (controls) of Bangladeshi ethnicity were recruited randomly. All enrolled participants completed the study, with no cases of attrition. Patients with BC were recruited from the National Cancer Research Institute Hospital, Mohakhali, Dhaka. The study used well‐prepared inpatient and outpatient facilities for participant evaluation. A qualified oncologist diagnosed BC patients based on established diagnostic criteria, while also evaluating HCs. Demographic and clinical data were collected, and genetic analysis was performed on blood samples. All participants in this study were female. There were no statistically significant differences in age or BMI between cases and controls (p > 0.05), suggesting that the groups were comparable. To further minimize potential confounding, age and BMI were included as covariates in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Randomized selection ensured unbiased sampling, while laboratory personnel were blinded to case‐control status to minimize bias. Genotyping analysis was conducted at the Rufaida BioMeds Laboratory in Dhaka, Bangladesh. To ensure participant confidentiality, the authors had no access to personally identifiable information during or after the data collection.

DNA Extraction and Genotyping

Blood samples were collected from all participants in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid‐containing tubes. Genomic DNA was extracted for a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) study using a commercial genomic DNA extraction kit (Favorgen, USA), following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, the collected blood samples were lysed, and the lysate was carefully transferred into binding column tubes. The DNA was washed with various buffers and eluted using nuclease‐free water. The isolated DNA was stored at −20°C.

One percent agarose gel electrophoresis was used to evaluate the purity of the extracted DNA. Tetra‐primer amplification refractory mutation system polymerase chain reaction (T‐ARMS‐PCR) method was conducted for genotyping of the HER1‐rs11543848 and HER2‐rs1136201 polymorphisms, as previously optimized in the laboratory. Outer and inner primer sequences (forward and reverse) (Table 1) were extracted from the publication by Sombal et al. to identify homozygous and heterozygous alleles [17].

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.r-project.org/). Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium was tested using the “Hardy–Weinberg” principle. Pearson's χ 2 tests were used to check the association between categorical variables across different genotype groups. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using multivariate logistic regression models, incorporating age and BMI as covariates.

A post hoc power analysis was conducted based on the observed allele frequencies and effect sizes. The study achieved an estimated statistical power of approximately 80% to detect an odds ratio of ≥ 2.0 at a significant level of 0.05. However, given the moderate sample size, the study may be underpowered to detect small effect sizes, and the results should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to confirm associations with smaller effect sizes. To control multiple comparisons across genetic models, Bonferroni correction was applied, with a corrected significance threshold of p < 0.01.

Ethical Consideration

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of BRAC University (approval no. BRACUIRB120220005). Informed written consent was obtained from all participants, who were assured of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. All the procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines and regulations outlined in the approved research protocol.

Genotype distributions for both cases and controls conformed to Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE), with p values of 0.694 and 0.990, respectively, validating the data set for genetic association analysis.

The common homozygous genotype (GG) was more prevalent in controls (52.42%) than in cases (14.29%), which suggests a protective role. The heterozygous genotype (GA) was the most frequent in BC patients, found in 69.64% of cases compared to 40.32% of controls. The rare homozygous genotype (AA) was observed in 16.07% of cases and 7.26% of controls.

For the additive model 1 (GA vs. AA), the adjusted odds ratio (aOR) for the GA genotype compared to the AA genotype was 6.337 (95% CI: 3.301–12.166, p < 0.001), indicating a highly significant increase in BC risk for heterozygous individuals. In the additive model 2 (AA vs. GG), the aOR was 8.125 (95% CI: 3.082–21.418, p < 0.001), suggesting a strong association of the AA genotype with increased disease susceptibility.

The dominant model (GA + AA vs. GG) showed a highly significant association (aOR = 6.61, 95% CI: 3.500–12.548, p < 0.001), indicating that the presence of at least one A allele substantially increases the risk of BC. The recessive model (AA vs. GA + GG) also showed a significant but weaker association (aOR = 2.45, 95% CI: 1.051–5.698, p = 0.038). Furthermore, the over‐dominant model (GA vs. GG + AA) demonstrated a strong association (aOR = 3.40, 95% CI: 1.980–5.823, p < 0.001).

Overall, the findings demonstrate that the HER1 rs11543848 polymorphism is significantly associated with increased BC susceptibility in this population. The strongest associations were observed under the additive and dominant models, suggesting that both heterozygous and rare homozygous genotypes contribute to disease susceptibility, whereas the common homozygous genotype (GG) provided a potentially protective effect.

The AA genotype (common homozygote) was observed in 21.43% of cases and 23.39% of controls, showing no significant difference. The AG genotype (heterozygote) was the most frequent in both groups, present in 75.00% of cases and 72.58% of controls. The GG genotype (rare homozygote) was infrequent, as it was detected in only 3.57% of cases and 4.03% of controls.

In the additive model 1 (AG vs. AA), the adjusted odds ratio (aOR) was 1.128 (95% CI: 0.608–2.091, p = 0.703), indicating no significant association. Similarly, in additive model 2 (GG vs. AA), the aOR was 0.967 (95% CI: 0.233–4.006, p = 0.963), also demonstrating no significant link. Under the dominant model (AG + GG vs. AA), the aOR was 1.119 (95% CI: 0.606 –2.068, p = 0.719), again showing no significant association with BC risk. Similarly, the recessive model (GG vs. AG + AA) produced an aOR of 0.882 (95% CI: 0.231–3.368, p = 0.854), indicating no significant risk contribution from the rare homozygous genotype.

However, the over‐dominant model (AG vs. GG + AA) revealed a statistically significant association, with an aOR of 1.833 (95% CI: 1.024–3.281, p = 0.041). This suggests a possible heterozygote effect, where individuals with the AG genotype may have an increased risk of BC compared to either homozygous genotype. However, after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, the association observed in the over‐dominant model was no longer statistically significant.

Combined Genotype Analysis of HER1 and HER2 Polymorphisms

We analyzed the combined effect of HER1 rs11543848 and HER2 rs1136201 polymorphisms (Table 4). The most common combined genotype among BC patients was HER1 GA with HER2 AG (n = 44), followed by HER1 GG with HER2 AG (n = 28). The reference group (HER1 GG with HER2 AA) was observed more frequently in cases than in controls, but the number of controls in this group was very small. Overall, several genotype combinations appeared more common in cases than in controls. However, some genotype groups had very few individuals, especially in the control group. Because of this, the calculated risk estimates were not stable and should be interpreted with caution. Based on these findings, no clear combined or interaction effect between HER1 and HER2 polymorphisms could be confirmed. The results suggest that HER1 rs11543848 plays a more important role in BC susceptibility in this study, while HER2 rs1136201 shows a weaker effect. Larger studies are needed to better understand any possible interaction between these two genes.

Discussion

This case‐control study examines the association between HER1 rs11543848 and HER2 rs1136201 single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) and the risk of BC in the Bangladeshi population where 112 BC patients and 124 HCs were recruited. Genotype comparisons and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to determine adjusted odds ratios (aOR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p values. Our findings exhibited a pronounced role of HER1 polymorphism in BC susceptibility, while suggesting a potential modifier role for the HER2 variant (Figure 2).

Forest plot. Association between BC with (A) HER1 (rs11543848) Polymorphism and (B) HER2 (rs1136201) polymorphism.

The HER1 rs11543848 polymorphism is an arginine to lysine substitution at codon 497 in the extracellular domain of the HER1 protein that alters the structure of the receptor, leading to decreased affinity for ligands such as EGF and TGF‐α, which results in decreased receptor activation. Our results reveal that this variant is strongly associated with increased BC risk, particularly under the additive (GA vs. AA, aOR = 6.33; AA vs. GG, aOR = 8.12) and dominant models (GA + AA vs. GG, aOR = 6.61), with all associations reaching high statistical significance (p < 0.001). Additional exploratory analyses suggest that the effect size observed for HER1 rs11543848 is relatively high compared to typical single SNP associations. This may reflect population‐specific genetic architecture or potential interaction with environmental or hormonal factors. However, given the sample size and multiple comparisons, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger cohorts. Therefore, according to our results, carriers of the A allele of the HER1 rs11543848 polymorphism exhibited a significantly higher risk of developing BC. This finding aligns with a previous case‐control study conducted in the Pashtun population of Pakistan, which also reported a significant association between the A allele and increased BC susceptibility [17]. Additionally, the results of a case‐control study conducted in the Egyptian population found that the A allele significantly increased BC risk with an odds ratio of about 2.6, indicating a strong association, which coincides with our findings [18]. The A allele leads to a functionally less active EGFR receptor in breast cells, with lower ligand binding and signaling capacity. This means that downstream signaling pathways, including those that promote cell proliferation and survival, such as activation of proto‐oncogenes like myc, fos, and jun, are less strongly activated [10]. Conversely, according to Gao et al. (2008), the lysine coding A allele was linked with worse prognosis, including larger tumor size and increased likelihood of metastasis, while the arginine coding G allele appeared to provide a protective effect, which coincides with our findings [20]. These findings suggest that the HER1 polymorphism may alter the oncogenic potential of the EGFR receptor, making it a potential genetic susceptibility marker for BC risk. Moreover, in patients with colorectal cancer, the lysine homozygous variant has been associated with greater tumor shrinkage and enhanced sensitivity to EGFR‐targeted therapies such as cetuximab [10]. These results suggest that the presence of the HER1 polymorphism may lead to increased treatment responsiveness, and further studies are needed to validate the predictive value of this SNP in creating personalized treatment strategies for BC patients.

The rs1136201 SNP involves a conservative isoleucine‐to‐valine substitution at codon 655 in the transmembrane domain of the HER2 protein. While this substitution has been hypothesized to increase receptor dimerization and stabilization, our findings show limited evidence for a strong association with BC susceptibility in this cohort. Neither the additive (AG vs. AA, aOR = 1.12; GG vs. AA, aOR = 0.96) nor the dominant (AG + GG vs. AA, aOR = 1.11) or recessive models (GG vs. AG + AA, aOR = 0.88) showed statistically significant associations (p > 0.05). However, the over‐dominant model (AG vs. GG + AA) achieved statistical significance (p = 0.041, aOR = 1.83), suggesting that the heterozygous genotype may exert a modest risk effect or influence tumor phenotype in a context‐dependent manner.

This aligns with previous studies that have produced conflicting results regarding rs1136201. For instance, some investigations have linked the Val allele (G) with increased BC risk and poorer prognosis, while others in East Asian and Middle Eastern populations have reported no significant association. According to a study conducted in the Pashtun population of Pakistan, the G allele coding for valine and the heterozygous genotype are significantly associated with increased BC risk, especially in aggressive BC subtypes such as luminal B and triple negative BC [17]. It has been hypothesized that the G allele of rs1136201 is linked to a higher BC risk because the substitution of isoleucine with valine in the transmembrane domain of the HER2 receptor enhances receptor dimerization, tyrosine kinase activity and autophosphorylation that in turn drive cellular proliferation and tumor aggressiveness [21]. However, other studies, including those conducted in Brazilian and other populations, have not found a statistically significant association between rs1136201 and BC risk, especially when considering premenopausal and postmenopausal women separately [18]. These discrepancies may be due to ethnic differences, sample size, and genetic background. After correction for multiple testing, the association observed for HER2 rs1136201 did not remain statistically significant, suggesting that this finding should be interpreted with caution.

Moreover, in a case‐control study conducted in the Egyptian population, AbdRaboh et al. demonstrated a synergistic effect between the HER1 rs11543848 and HER2 rs1136201 polymorphisms, which does not coincide with our findings [9]. The study revealed individuals carrying both the HER1 497K allele and the HER2 655V allele had a significantly higher risk of developing BC, with an odds ratio (OR) of approximately 4.1 [9]. This synergism may reflect enhanced EGFR‐HER2 heterodimerization and co‐activation of critical signaling cascades such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK [22]. The interaction between these variants underscores the complexity of BC genetics, where multiple polymorphisms within receptor families may jointly modulate disease risk. Therefore, further research across multiple ethnicities is needed to validate these findings.

Our demographic analysis showed that BC patients and HCs were well matched, with no major differences in age, BMI, occupation, or number of children. This reduces the chance of confounding factors influencing the results. The absence of a clear link between BMI and BC risk in our study is consistent with earlier findings from Bangladesh [23, 24]. At the same time, studies elsewhere have reported that higher BMI may raise the risk, especially in postmenopausal women, suggesting the need for more research in our setting [25, 26]. Reproductive histories were also similar between the two groups, indicating no significant impact on cancer susceptibility in this cohort. Likewise, factors such as education level, socio‐economic status, residential background, and menopausal status did not differ significantly between cases and controls. Together, these findings suggest that demographic variables did not play a major role in BC risk in our study population, which in turn strengthens the reliability of our genetic association results.

In our study, the tissue patterns observed in BC patients were consistent with global trends, with invasive ductal carcinoma being the most common type. A significant number of tumors were hormone receptor positive (ER and PR), which is encouraging since these cancers generally respond well to hormone therapies and are linked to more favorable outcomes. Around 29% of patients were HER2‐positive, a figure similar to reports from other Asian populations, suggesting that a substantial group of patients could benefit from HER2‐targeted treatments.

Most tumors were detected at medium sizes (2.1–5.0 cm), yet nearly 40% were already larger than 5 cm at diagnosis. This underlines the critical need for earlier detection and improved screening programs. Lymph node involvement was present in about one‐quarter of patients. While not the majority, this remains clinically important, as lymph node status directly influences staging, treatment choices, and prognosis.

The main limitation of this study is the sample size of 112 BC patients and 124 HCs. While larger cohorts are ideal, this size is comparable to many published studies, and we believe our findings remain robust and meaningful. Some clinical details, including reproductive history, smoking, breastfeeding, and contraceptive use, were not included. Additionally, as a single‐center study in Bangladesh, the results may not be fully generalizable to other populations. Nevertheless, this is the first study of its kind in Bangladesh, providing novel and locally relevant insights. Future multi‐center studies with larger, more diverse cohorts will help confirm and expand these findings.

Conclusion

This study investigated the association of HER1 rs11543848 and HER2 rs1136201 single nucleotide polymorphisms with BC risk in the Bangladeshi population. The findings reveal a strong association between HER1 rs11543848 and increased BC susceptibility, while the effect of HER2 rs1136201 appears weaker and context dependent. These results underscore the biological differences between these variants and emphasize the importance of considering population‐specific genetic factors in BC risk assessment.
