Abstract
Integrating coded caching (CC) into multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) communications significantly enhances the achievable degrees of freedom (DoF). This paper investigates a practical cache-aided asymmetric MIMO configuration with cache ratio γ \gamma , where a server with L L transmit antennas communicates with K K users. The users are partitioned into J J groups, and each user in group j j has G j G_{j} receive antennas. We propose four content-aware MIMO-CC strategies: min-G enforces symmetry using the smallest antenna count among users; Grouping maximizes intra-subset spatial multiplexing gain at the expense of some global caching gain; Super-grouping aggregates users into optimized min- G G -based super-sets with identical effective receive multiplexing gains before applying Grouping across them; and Phantom redistributes spatial resources assuming “phantom” antennas at the users to bridge the performance gains of min- G G and Grouping . We develop these asymmetric strategies under three reference symmetric CC placement–delivery policies with guaranteed linear decodability: a DoF-optimal policy achieving the optimal single-shot DoF, and two closed-form policies, namely combinatorial and linear cyclic low-complexity constructions, with the cyclic policy attaining DoF performance close to the others in many operating regimes. Analytical and numerical results demonstrate significant DoF improvements across various system configurations, and that policy–strategy combinations offer flexible trade-offs between DoF and subpacketization complexity.



I
Introduction

The rapidly increasing demand for multimedia content, driven by applications like immersive viewing, gaming, and extended reality (XR), continues to underpin the growth in mobile data traffic. With stringent requirements for ultra‑low latency (
<
10ms) and multi‑Gbps throughput levels, conventional scaling in spectrum or densification alone is insufficient to meet these requirements.
Instead, proactive caching of popular content at end-users, by offloading traffic during peak hours, is foreseen to play a critical role in reducing bandwidth pressure and network congestion, thereby enabling scalable network performance
[
22
]
.
In this regard, coded caching (CC)
[
14
]
has emerged as an effective solution, utilizing the onboard memory of network devices as a communication resource, especially beneficial for cacheable multimedia content.
The performance gain in CC arises from multicasting well-constructed codewords to user groups of size
t+1
, where the CC gain
t
is proportional to the cumulative cache size of all users.
Originally designed for single-input single-output (SISO) setups
[
14
]
, CC was later shown to be effective in multiple-input single-output (MISO) systems, demonstrating that spatial multiplexing and CC gains are additive
[
27
]
.
This is achieved by serving
multiple groups of users simultaneously with multiple multicast messages and suppressing the intra-group interference by beamforming.
Accordingly, in a MISO-CC setting with
L
Tx antennas,
t+L
users can be served in parallel, and the so-called degree-of-freedom (DoF) of
t+L
is achievable
[
28
,
27
,
10
,
24
]
.

The global caching gain scales linearly with the number of users in SISO and MISO settings. However, it was only recently demonstrated that multi-antenna reception at the user side can
multiplicatively boost
this gain by a factor of the spatial multiplexing dimension
G

[
23
,
20
,
19
]
. This multiplicative scaling broadens the design space for collaborative multi-user multi-input multi-output (MIMO) deployments by enabling parallel coded multicast transmission, jointly exploiting spatial multiplexing and cache-induced side information.

All prior art, however, assumes the same number of antennas at each receiver, which may not be the case in practice. For example, 5G NR-capable devices, ranging from high-performance smartphones to low-power IoT nodes, are designed to meet diverse requirements, in terms of latency, data rates, and QoE, resulting in category-specific asymmetric antenna configurations and capabilities
[
8
,
22
]
.
Despite its practical significance, the achievable single-shot DoF in scenarios where users are equipped with asymmetric antennas has not yet been characterized in the MIMO-CC literature. To address this gap, we develop a set of cache-aided transmission schemes that explicitly account for heterogeneous user antenna configurations. These schemes are designed to exploit the unique characteristics of heterogeneous user equipment by carefully balancing two key performance dimensions: the spatial multiplexing gain enabled by multi-antenna receivers, and the global caching gain arising from content reuse.

Two primary and two hybrid strategies for asymmetric MIMO-CC communications are studied in this paper. In the context of primary strategies, the proposed
min-
G
strategy emphasizes the aggregate CC gain across users by adapting delivery based on the user with the lowest spatial rank, and the
Grouping
strategy employs a rank-aware approach that clusters users with similar spatial capabilities (spatial rank or link qualities), thereby maximizing the impact of receiver-side spatial multiplexing. The primary strategies are followed by
Super-grouping
and
Phantom
hybrid strategies, that strike a tradeoff between spatial and global caching gains.
Collectively, these strategies broaden the design space of MIMO-CC systems by moving beyond the conventional symmetry assumption, enabling scalable content delivery in heterogeneous user environments.

Related Work

Asymmetric SISO-CC Networks

Early CC works initially assumed symmetric (homogeneous) settings, where all users had equal cache capacities and shared a uniform delivery link

The study of cache heterogeneity began with

The work

[

derived exact bounds for an asymmetric two-user network.
In parallel, heterogeneous link qualities were investigated in
[
35

[

More recent studies explored joint cache–link heterogeneity
[

3
,

[
34
,
7

addressed fully heterogenous CC scenarios, including unequal cache sizes, file lengths, and user-specific demands,
for small-scale systems and with a focus on the two-user case.

2
Asymmetric MISO-CC Networks

Recent advances in MISO-CC communications have begun to explore multi-antenna coded caching for networks with different cache sizes at the users. Specifically,
[
12
]
demonstrated that multiple transmit antennas, while providing full multiplexing gains, can simultaneously eliminate the performance penalties typically associated with cache-size imbalance, and
[
32
]
studied multi-user MISO systems with cache-size heterogeneity in the finite-SNR region.
More recently,
[
15
]
developed a connectivity-aware cache allocation that assigns larger caches to weaker users to enhance local gain, while preserving cache overlap across users to sustain global coded multicasting opportunities.
Later, dynamic CC schemes
[
1
]

addressed asymmetric user-to-caching-profile associations and mitigated DoF loss through finite-SNR transmission design.
However, all these works still assume single-antenna receivers, and thus do not exploit
receive-side spatial dimensions.

I-B

Main Contributions

To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has characterized the achievable single-shot DoF of MIMO-CC systems with heterogeneous per-user receive-antenna capabilities. This setting fundamentally departs from symmetric baselines, as delivery must balance coded multicast opportunities against spatial bottlenecks induced by unequal antenna resources. We address this gap by jointly designing cache and spatial resources to leverage, rather than be limited by, heterogeneity, thereby enhancing the achievable performance in asymmetric MIMO-CC systems.

We study the single-shot achievable DoF of asymmetric MIMO-CC with two objectives:
(i) improving the achievable DoF under heterogeneous receive antenna configurations, and (ii) developing simple combinatorial and low-complexity designs (without requiring a search over multicasting parameters), all with closed-form DoF expressions.

Our developments are built under three baseline symmetric CC policies
\pi\in\{\mathrm{opt},\mathrm{cmb},\mathrm{lin}\}
, each specifying the cache placement and the delivery/multicasting design rule, while guaranteeing linear decodability for all target users:
(i) the DoF-optimized policy
\mathrm{opt}

[
19
]
, which attains
the best single-shot DoF in symmetric MIMO-CC by optimizing the multicasting parameters (the number of served users and the number of streams per user);
(ii) the combinatorial policy
\mathrm{cmb}

[
28
,
23
]
, which views the MIMO-CC system as a set of parallel MISO-CC sub-systems, thereby avoiding
parameter search; and (iii) the linear-subpacketization
policy
\mathrm{lin}

[
24
,
23
]
,
which enables delivery via a
cyclic construction and offers a favorable
performance–complexity trade-off, approaching the DoF of the other policies with substantially lower subpacketization.

These symmetric policies are adapted to the asymmetric receive-antenna setup through four asymmetric MIMO-CC strategies.
We first develop two primary strategies that explicitly reveal the fundamental tradeoff
between global caching and spatial multiplexing gains. The
min-
G
strategy enforces a symmetric setup by treating all users as having the same number of antennas equal to the minimum among them, thereby maximizing coded multicast opportunities at the cost of reduced spatial multiplexing.
In contrast, the
Grouping
strategy partitions users into subsets according to their receive antenna configurations and serves each subset in orthogonal (time) dimensions, thereby preserving per-group spatial multiplexing gains while sacrificing some global caching gain.

To bridge this trade-off and unify the benefits of the two primary strategies,
we then develop two hybrid strategies that extend the achievable DoF across a broader range of system parameters.
First, we introduce the
Super-grouping
strategy, which combines the primary strategies in a structured manner: users are first aggregated into equivalent sets using the
min-
G
abstraction, yielding equivalent Rx spatial multiplexing gains per set, and the equivalent sets are then served using the primary
Grouping
strategy.
Second, we introduce the
Phantom
MIMO-CC strategy, which generalizes the primary strategies
by introducing virtual (“phantom”) antennas at some users to dynamically reconfigure spatial resources, enabling finer control over the number of served streams to accommodate asymmetric transmissions within each delivery interval.
These hybrid strategies consistently outperform the primary strategies across all considered policies.
Super-grouping
closely approaches the performance of the
Phantom
strategy
by optimizing the number and composition of equivalent user sets, while
offering a favorable complexity-performance trade-off through structured user partitioning based on the equivalent Rx-side spatial multiplexing.
Finally, we also develop a simplified variant of the
Phantom
strategy, that achieves a DoF very close to Phantom with much lower delivery complexity.

We derive closed-form expressions for the achievable DoF (and the associated delivery/subpacketization scalings)
for all proposed strategies under
\mathrm{opt}
,
\mathrm{cmb}
, and
\mathrm{lin}
policies, enabling a
transparent performance–complexity comparison. Extensive numerical results confirm that hybrid strategies
consistently outperform primary strategies, and that different policy–strategy combinations provide
flexible operating points for trade-offs
between achievable DoF and subpacketization.

III-B

The combinatorial parallel links policy
(\mathrm{cmb})

This policy builds on the MISO-CC scheme in
[

31

gives the number of MC transmissions that include a given user
k\in{{\mathcal{K}}}
.

DoF analysis.

Each user now receives
\beta(s)=\beta={G}
parallel streams per transmission, while the number of users per transmission is fixed at
\Omega(s)=\Omega=t^{\prime}+\alpha^{\prime}

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{DoF}_{\mathrm{cmb}}=\Omega\cdot\beta=G(t^{\prime}+\alpha^{\prime})=GK\gamma+G\lfloor\frac{L}{G}\rfloor.\end{array}

(16)

The cyclic parallel links policy

This policy addresses the large subpacketization bottleneck of the other two policies, but is applicable under the feasibility condition of

Placement phase.

Delivery phase.

(18)

Hybrid asymmetric solutions

In this section, we study two composite strategies that enable an interplay between CC and spatial multiplexing gains in asymmetric MIMO-CC systems.

V-A

The Super-grouping strategy

The
Grouping
strategy, as introduced in Section
IV-B
, treats each set of users
{\mathcal{K}}_{j}
separately. A key limitation of this approach
is the potential degradation in the overall achievable DoF due to relying solely on maximizing spatial multiplexing gain at the Rx side. To overcome this limitation, we propose a promising alternative, known as the
Super-grouping
strategy, that
combines the
min-
G
and
Grouping
strategies into a unified framework. The first step in the
Super-grouping
strategy is to merge the existing
J
user sets
{\mathcal{K}}_{j}
into
\bar{J}\leq J
equivalent user sets
\bar{{\mathcal{K}}}_{l}
,
l\in[\bar{J}]
. Then, we employ the
min-
G
strategy to deliver data to users inside each equivalent user set, and use the
Grouping
strategy among different sets.

Construction of equivalent user sets.

As the
Super-grouping
strategy employs the
min-
G
strategy to deliver data to users within each equivalent user set
\bar{{\mathcal{K}}}_{l}
, from the DoF perspective, it is preferable to merge original sets with consecutive indices only. This is because the
min-
G
strategy sets the Rx spatial gain to the smallest Rx gain among the involved users; hence, with the original sets
{\mathcal{K}}_{j}
ordered by the users’ Rx gains, merging non-consecutive indices inevitably translates into a larger DoF loss than consecutive-index merging.

For consecutive-index merging, we need to first find
\bar{J}-1
indices
J_{1},\cdots,J_{\bar{J}-1}
such that
1\leq J_{1}<J_{2}<\cdots<J_{\bar{J}-1}<J
. Then, defining
J_{0}=0
and
J_{\bar{J}}=J
, we can create
\bar{J}
index sets as

\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathcal{J}}_{l}=\{J_{l-1}+1,\ldots,J_{l}\},\qquad l\in[\bar{J}]\end{array}

(26)

and create the equivalent user sets as

\bar{{\mathcal{K}}}_{l}=\bigcup_{j\in{\mathcal{J}}_{l}}{\mathcal{K}}_{j}.

(27)

For a given
\bar{J}\in[J]
, the number of ways to select
J_{1},\cdots,J_{\bar{J}-1}
such that
1\leq J_{1}<J_{2}<\cdots<J_{\bar{J}-1}<J
is
\binom{J-1}{\bar{J}-1}
.
Each selection of these indices defines a unique merging of the original user sets into equivalent sets. In order to find the optimum DoF with the
Super-grouping
strategy, one should compare all these merging possibilities and select the one with the largest DoF.
It should be noted that, when
\bar{J}=1
and
\bar{J}=J
, the
Super-grouping
strategy reduces to
min-

and

4

The term “candidate transmission vectors” reflects that these vectors are not transmitted yet, but are passed to the subsequent step, where a subset of subpackets is discarded to ensure linear decodability.

Next, each candidate vector
\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}(s)
is mapped to a real transmission vector
\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}(s)
. Since some users in the original network may have fewer than
\hat{\beta}_{\pi,i}
Rx antennas, a subset of their intended subpackets are removed from the candidate transmission vectors to ensure linear decodability for all
k\in\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{i}
. The discarded subpackets are deferred to later transmission, either in a subsequent MC round or in the final UC round. The term Phantom stems from the assumption of symmetric number of antennas across the selected users in each round: users with fewer antennas are effectively treated as if they possessed additional, non-existent (phantom) receive antennas, whose associated streams are subsequently discarded.

The proposed
Phantom
strategy is detailed in the following.

Placement phase.
Follows the reference policy
\pi
, as detailed in Section
III
. We use
W_{\wp_{\pi}}
to show a packet of a general file
W
, where
\wp_{\pi}
clarifies which users store
W_{\wp_{\pi}}
in their cache memories. For
\pi\in\{\mathrm{opt},\mathrm{cmb}\}
,
\wp_{\pi}
a subset of user indices, while for
\pi=\mathrm{lin}
, it represents a single user index.

Delivery phase, MC rounds.
At each MC round
i\in[I]
, the delivery algorithm proceeds as follows:

Step 1 - Target user set determination.
For
i=1
, the target user set includes all users, i.e.,
\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{1}=\cup_{j\in[J]}{\mathcal{K}}_{j}={\mathcal{K}}
.
For
i>1
, we have:

\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{i}=\bigcup\nolimits_{j\in[J],\;G_{j}<\hat{\beta}_{\pi,i-1}}{\mathcal{K}}_{j}.

(31)

The rationale for this selection is that after round
i-1
, a subset of missing subpackets of users with fewer Rx antennas than
\hat{\beta}_{\pi,i-1}
are discarded, and in round
i
, we aim to find multicasting opportunities among such discarded subpackets.

Step 2 - Candidate transmission vector creation.

We consider a hypothetical symmetric MIMO-CC setup, with
L
antennas at the transmitter and
\hat{K}_{i}=|\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{i}|
users, each with cache ratio of
\gamma
and
\hat{G}_{i}=\max_{k\in\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{i}}{\mathsf{G}}_{k}
Rx antennas. For this hypothetical setup, we follow the reference policy
\pi
, to create a total number of
{S}_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}
candidate transmission vectors. First, following the discussions in Section
III
, we find out the number of users served in each transmission, denoted by
\hat{\Omega}_{\pi,i}
, and the number of parallel streams per user, denoted by
\hat{\beta}_{\pi,i}\leq\hat{G}_{i}
. Then, we increase subpacketization by a factor of
\hat{\phi}_{\pi,i}
, given as:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{\mathrm{opt},i}

\displaystyle=\hat{\beta}^{*}_{\mathrm{opt},i}\binom{\hat{K}_{i}-\hat{K}_{i}\gamma-1}{\hat{\Omega}_{\mathrm{opt},i}^{*}-\hat{K}_{i}\gamma-1},

(32)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{\mathrm{cmb},i}

\displaystyle=\hat{G}_{i}\binom{\hat{K}_{i}-\hat{K}_{i}\gamma-1}{\lfloor\nicefrac{{L}}{{\hat{G}_{i}}}\rfloor-1},

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{\mathrm{lin},i}

\displaystyle=\hat{G}_{i}(\hat{K}_{i}\gamma+\lfloor\nicefrac{{L}}{{\hat{G}_{i}}}\rfloor).

It is important to note that, as in each round
i
we aim to find MC opportunities among subpackets discarded in the previous round
i-1
, the subpacketization accumulates multiplicatively across rounds. Hence, the final subpacketization value for data sent in round
i
is given as

\hat{\Theta}_{\pi,i}={\vartheta}_{\pi}\prod_{i^{\prime}\in[i]}\hat{\phi}_{\pi,i^{\prime}},

(33)

where
{\vartheta}_{\pi}
denotes the subpacketization of the placement phase.
This accumulated subpacketization must be accounted for while calculating the number of intervals in round
i
, i.e.,
S_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}
. Specifically, in the first MC round,
S_{\pi,1}^{\mathrm{MC}}=S_{\pi,1}
, and for
i>1
we have

S_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}=\Bigg(\prod_{i^{\prime}\in[i-1]}\hat{\phi}_{\pi,i^{\prime}}\Bigg)S_{\pi,i},

(34)

where
S_{\pi,i}
is the number of transmissions for the hypothetical network in round
i
, using the reference policy
\pi
, as defined in Section
III
. For each interval
s\in[S_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}]
, the candidate vector can be expressed as

\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}(s)=\sum\nolimits_{k\in\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{\pi,i}(s)}\sum\nolimits_{W_{\wp_{\pi}}^{q}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{\pi,i}^{k}(s)}{\mathbf{w}}_{\wp_{\pi}}^{q}W_{\wp_{\pi}}^{q},

(35)

where
\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{\pi,i}(s)\subseteq\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{i}
denotes the set of users receiving data in transmission interval
s
of round
i
,
\tilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{\pi,i}^{k}(s)
represents the set of missing subpackets of user
k
transmitted in this interval (it includes
\hat{\beta}_{\pi,i}
subpackets),
q
is an auxiliary index to account for delivery-phase subpacketization, and
{\mathbf{w}}_{\wp_{\pi}}^{q}
is the transmit precoder vector for subpacket
W_{\wp_{\pi}}^{q}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{\pi,i}^{k}(s)
.
5
5

5

The choice of the precoder design (e.g., zero-forcing or MMSE) affects finite-SNR performance, but does not change the DoF. Interference suppression criteria are given by the selected reference policy
\pi
.

Step 3 - Final transmission vector creation.
Each candidate vector
\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}(s)
created in Step 2 aims to deliver
\hat{\beta}_{\pi,i}
parallel streams to each user
k\in\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{\pi,i}(s)
. However, due to asymmetric Rx capabilities, there might exist some users in
\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{\pi,i}(s)
with fewer than
\hat{\beta}_{\pi,i}
Rx antennas. In such cases, to ensure linear decodability by all users, for each user
k\in\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{\pi,i}(s)
with
{\mathsf{G}}_{k}<\hat{\beta}_{\pi,i}
, we randomly discard
\hat{\beta}_{\pi,i}-{\mathsf{G}}_{k}
subpackets from
\tilde{{\mathcal{M}}}_{\pi,i}^{k}(s)
, and leave these subpackets to be transmitted later, either in subsequent MC rounds or in the final UC round. Denoting the reduced subpacket set by
\hat{{\mathcal{M}}}_{\pi,i}^{k}(s)
, we can write down the real transmission vectors as

\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}(s)=\sum\nolimits_{k\in\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{\pi,i}(s)}\sum\nolimits_{W_{\wp_{\pi}}^{q}\in\hat{{\mathcal{M}}}_{\pi,i}^{k}(s)}{\mathbf{w}}_{\wp_{\pi}}^{q}W_{\wp_{\pi}}^{q}

(36)

Delivery phase, UC round.

After finalizing all MC rounds, the remaining subpackets are delivered through
S_{\pi}^{\textrm{UC}}
UC transmission intervals. This round does not introduce extra subpacketization, i.e.,
\hat{\Theta}_{\pi,I}
is used for UC transmissions as well.
In principle, for any arbitrary subset
\bar{{\mathcal{K}}}
of users, the number of parallel streams in a UC transmission interval is limited by
\min(L,\sum_{k\in\bar{{\mathcal{K}}}}{\mathsf{G}}_{k})
. In this paper, we assume that in every UC interval, it is possible to select a subset of
L
missing subpackets that can be transmitted in parallel.
6
6

6

This assumption is made to simplify DoF expressions. In some cases, e.g., when the number of users with the smallest number of receive antennas is very small such that
L>\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}{\mathsf{G}}_{k}
, it is not possible to select
L
parallel streams in some UC intervals. In such situations, the number of required intervals will increase, and the DoF will decrease as a result. Nevertheless, as most of the packets are delivered via MC rounds, the DoF impact is marginal and negligible.

.

For all
\pi\in\{\mathrm{opt},\mathrm{cmb},\mathrm{lin}\}
,
i\in[I]
, and
s\in[S_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}]
, each transmission vector
\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}(s)
in (

) is linearly decodable by all users
k\in\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{\pi,i}(s)
.

Proof.

In each MC round
i\in[I]
, the
\hat{\beta}_{\pi,i}
value in Step 2 is selected such that candidate transmission vectors
\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}(s)
are linearly decodable in the hypothetical MIMO-CC setup with
\hat{G}_{i}
Rx antennas at each user. From (
20
), this translates into

7

7

Calculations are provided here for the
\hat{\Omega}_{\pi,i}>\hat{K}_{i}\gamma+1
case only. Extension to the equality case is straightforward.

\hat{\beta}_{\pi,i}\leq\min\left(\hat{G}_{i},\frac{L-\delta_{\pi,i}}{\hat{\Omega}_{\pi,i}-\hat{K}_{i}\gamma-1}\right),

(37)

where
\delta_{\pi,i}
shows the maximum number of subpackets with the same packet index
\wp_{\pi}
in each transmission interval in MC round
i
. In order to apply (
1
)
to the non-symmetric case where each user has
{\mathsf{G}}_{k}
Rx antennas and receives
\bm{\beta}_{\pi,i}^{k}(s)
parallel streams, we just need to replace
\hat{G}_{i}
,
\hat{\beta}_{\pi,i}

with
{\mathsf{G}}_{k}

\bm{\beta}_{\pi,i}^{k}(s)

but for packets delivered to the user

DoF analysis.
In order to calculate the DoF of the
Phantom
strategy, we start by the DoF expression for non-symmetric setups in (
6
). The main challenge is to determine the total number of time intervals; while the number of intervals for each MC round is given by (
34
), for the number of UC intervals requires first calculating the total number of subpackets remaining for the UC round, denoted by
Z_{\pi}^{\mathrm{UC}}
. This can be done by summing up the number of subpackets delivered within all MC rounds, and substituting the result from the total number of missing subpackets. Since the subpacketization increases multiplicatively across consecutive MC rounds, all quantities must be expressed with respect to a common reference. To this end, we
introduce normalization factors
\epsilon_{\pi,i}\geq 1
that normalize the subpacketization in each MC round to the maximum (final) degree of subpacketization, such that
\epsilon_{\pi,i}\hat{\Theta}_{\pi,i}=\hat{\Theta}_{\pi,I}
,
\forall i\in[I]
,
which simply results in
\epsilon_{\pi,i}=\prod_{i<i^{\prime}\leq I}\hat{\phi}_{\pi,i^{\prime}}
. Indeed, this also requires normalizing the number of transmission intervals in each MC round, i.e., we use
\hat{S}_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}=\epsilon_{\pi,i}{S}_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}
as the normalized transmission count when working with normalized subpacketization. For notational simplicity, we also define
\zeta_{\pi,i}=\nicefrac{{\prod_{i^{\prime}\in[I]}\hat{\phi}_{\pi,i^{\prime}}}}{{\hat{\phi}_{\pi,i}}}
, which, together with (
34
), enables us to calculate the normalized transmission count of MC rounds from the transmission count of the reference policy as

\hat{S}_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}=\epsilon_{\pi,i}{S}_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}=\zeta_{\pi,i}S_{\pi,i}.

(38)

Theorem 1
.

For a given reference policy
\pi
, the DoF of the Phantom strategy is given as

\mathrm{DoF}_{\pi,\mathrm{Ph}}=\frac{Z^{\mathrm{Tot}}_{\pi}}{\sum\limits_{i\in[I]}\zeta_{\pi,i}S_{\pi,i}+\frac{1}{L}\!\!\left(\!\!Z^{\mathrm{Tot}}_{\pi}-\!\!\sum\limits_{i\in[I]}\zeta_{\pi,i}\lambda_{\pi,i}\sum\limits_{k\in\hat{{\mathcal{K}}_{i}}}\bm{\beta}_{\pi,i}^{k}\!\!\right)}

(39)

where
Z^{\mathrm{Tot}}_{\pi}=K(1-\gamma)\hat{\Theta}_{\pi,I}
is the (normalized) total number of missing subpackets, and
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{\pi,i}^{k}=\min(\hat{\beta}_{\pi,i},{\mathsf{G}}_{k})
. Moreover,
\lambda_{\pi,i}
denotes the total number of transmissions in MC round
i
that serve a user
k\in\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}_{i}
with reference policy
\pi
, as defined in Section
III
.

Proof.

Starting with (
6
), we can see that with uniform (normalized) subpacketization at all intervals,
f(s)
becomes independent of the interval index
s
, and the general DoF formula in (
6
) is simplified as

\mathrm{DoF}=\frac{\sum_{s\in[S]}\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{K}}(s)}\bm{\beta}_{k}(s)}{\sum_{s\in[S]}1},

(40)

which can be interpreted as the (normalized) total number of missing subpackets, i.e.,
Z^{\mathrm{Tot}}_{\pi}
), divided by the total number of intervals.
To calculate the total number of intervals, we know that
the number of MC intervals is given as
\sum_{i\in[I]}\hat{S}_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}=\sum_{i\in[I]}\zeta_{\pi,i}S_{\pi,i}
. However, in order to calculate the total number of UC intervals, we need to calculate the number of subpackets delivered in the UC round, denoted by
Z_{\pi}^{\mathrm{UC}}
, and then divide it by
L
. We first note that

Z_{\pi}^{\mathrm{UC}}=Z^{\mathrm{Tot}}_{\pi}-\sum\nolimits_{i\in[I]}\hat{Z}_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}},

(41)

where

\hat{Z}_{\pi,i}^{\mathrm{MC}}=\zeta_{\pi,i}\lambda_{\pi,i}\sum\nolimits_{k\in\hat{{\mathcal{K}}_{i}}}\bm{\beta}_{\pi,i}^{k}

(42)

is the (normalized) total number of subpackets delivered in MC round
i
.
Plugging these in the DoF formula results in (
39
), completing the proof.

VII
Conclusion

This paper characterized the single-shot achievable DoF of cache-aided MIMO systems with
heterogeneous
per-user receive antennas. Building on three symmetric reference policies
\pi\in\{\mathrm{opt},\mathrm{cmb},\mathrm{lin}\}
, we developed four asymmetric delivery strategies that yield distinct operating points between global coded-multicasting and per-user spatial multiplexing, with different achievable DoF and subpacketization requirements.
The two “primary” strategies
min-
G
and
Grouping
expose the fundamental tradeoff between the global caching and spatial multiplexing gains, while the two “hybrid” strategies,
Super-grouping
and
Phantom
, further expand the achievable region by combining these primary principles in a structured way.
We derived closed-form DoF expressions and established linear decodability for all policy–strategy combinations. Extensive numerical results corroborate the obtained results.
