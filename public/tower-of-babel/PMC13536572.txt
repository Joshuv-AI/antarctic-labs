Children’s rights in the gap between what is known and what is experienced: awareness among Syrian migrant and Turkish children
Authors: Ayşe Öztürk, Barış Kalender, Hüseyin Toy, Tuğçe Nur Karataş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAwareness of children’s rights (CR) is fundamental to safeguarding children from abuse, ensuring their well-being, and enabling their access to a dignified standard of living. However, little is known about how Turkish students (TS) and Syrian migrant students (SMS) perceive and internalize these rights in contexts marked by migration and vulnerability.MethodsThis study employed an explanatory sequential mixed methods design to examine and compare CR awareness among TS and SMS in Türkiye. Quantitative data were gathered from 437 TS and 434 SMS through standardized surveys, whereas qualitative data were obtained through in-depth interviews and a drawing activity, whereby 15 students from each group illustrated their perceptions of CR.ResultsStatistical analysis revealed that TS exhibited slightly higher levels of awareness regarding some CR than SMS. Nonetheless, item-level comparisons highlighted both convergences and divergences in specific rights domains, shaped by contextual and experiential differences. Thematic analysis of interview data yielded three primary themes: rights commonly recognized across both groups; rights uniquely identified within a specific group; and instances where responsibilities were misconstrued as rights. Furthermore, the findings elucidate how lived experience and social context influence the conceptualization and prioritization of CR.ConclusionsBy providing a comprehensive and comparative exploration of rights awareness among TS and SMS, this study offers critical insights for international policy discussions on child rights and education—particularly in relation to vulnerable and migrant populations. The results underscore the necessity of context-sensitive educational interventions and the inclusion of diverse child perspectives in rights-based programming.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-04761-6.

Introduction

Children’s awareness of their rights plays a fundamental role in enabling them to recognize, exercise, and protect these rights in daily life. Research consistently shows that rights awareness supports children’s psychosocial well-being, self-confidence, and active participation [11, 35], while low levels of awareness increase vulnerability to neglect and rights violations [23]. From an educational perspective, the literature emphasizes that children’s rights awareness is shaped not only by individual knowledge but also by pedagogical processes that promote participation, voice, and empowerment. Rights-based pedagogical approaches highlight the importance of classroom practices and institutional contexts in enabling children to experience themselves as active rights holders [45]. Accordingly, children’s rights awareness should be understood as a context-dependent and experience-based construct embedded within educational environments.

Throughout history, children have been exposed to various forms of maltreatment, neglect, abuse, and deprivation of basic needs. In response, national and international legal frameworks have been developed to protect children and secure their well-being. Among these, the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) is widely recognized as a milestone in the field of children’s rights and the most comprehensively ratified international legal instrument [37]. The UNCRC provides a holistic framework for children’s rights within social, political, and cultural contexts and guarantees fundamental rights related to survival, development, protection, and participation [10]. In the broader literature, CR under the UNCRC are often organized into four overarching categories: survival and development rights, protection rights, participation rights, and non-discrimination rights. In psychological research, however, these rights are commonly conceptualized under two major dimensions—nurturance and self-determination (participation) rights [46]. Nurturance rights emphasize adults’ responsibilities to ensure children’s physical and emotional well-being, whereas self-determination rights focus on children’s agency, voice, and participation in decisions affecting their lives [23, 47]. Research consistently shows that participation rights play a critical role in supporting children’s psychological well-being, empowerment, and resistance to abuse and injustice [30, 52]. For immigrant and refugee children, these processes may be further shaped by educational contexts and lived experiences that influence their opportunities for meaningful participation and rights awareness [24].

Increasing awareness of CR is of great importance for the realization of the rights set forth in the UNCRC. Children’s ability to influence their own lives begins with being aware of and knowledgeable about their rights. Given this, it can be stated that awareness and knowledge are inseparable components of CR [2]. As children learn about their rights, they also acquire an understanding of the rights and responsibilities of citizenship, as well as the values, virtues, and practices of good citizenship [18]. Therefore, awareness of CR is not only a prerequisite for exercising and defending these rights, but also for strengthening and sustaining democracy in democratic societies. Moreover, children’s knowledge and perceptions regarding their participation rights and to some extent their other rights are considered important indicators of their well-being [35]. Article 42 of the UNCRC also stipulates that CR should be widely known by both adults and children through appropriate and active means [17]. Indeed, children who are educated about their rights demonstrate significant improvements in self-esteem, school participation, school motivation, and in supporting the rights of others [32]. For this reason, for CR to be effective, it is critical to examine both the understanding of rights among children themselves [43] and among those making decisions regarding these rights, such as teachers and parents [23].

The literature includes numerous studies examining awareness of CR from the perspectives of children. Casas et al. [13] found that age and socioeconomic status influenced children’s reasoning and understanding of CR, with those from higher socioeconomic backgrounds showing greater awareness. Similarly, Kiprotich and Ong’ondo [34] identified generally low levels of awareness among children in Kenya, while af Ursin and Haanpää [2] reported that only a minority of children across 16 countries could identify their rights, indicating cross-national variation. These findings point to the influence of cultural, socioeconomic, and educational factors and highlight limitations in the generalizability of previous research. Ejieh and Akinola [21] similarly found very limited awareness of CR among primary school children in Nigeria, where cultural norms discouraged children from asserting their rights. Across studies, awareness of participation rights appears consistently lower than awareness of other rights, indicating a critical area requiring further investigation. In Türkiye, Tur [51] showed that family-related factors such as the number of siblings influenced primary school students’ perceptions of CR, while Yılmaz and Yiğit [54] found that fifth-grade students were more aware of protection rights than participation rights. Taken together, these studies suggest that children’s awareness of CR remains insufficient and is shaped by contextual factors such as culture, socioeconomic background, and family structure. However, most studies rely on small or context-specific samples, limiting generalizability, and empirical research focusing specifically on SMS remains scarce. This gap limits understanding of how rights awareness develops among migrant children in Türkiye, who may face distinct educational and social barriers shaping their experiences of CR.

UNCRC Article 22 places particular emphasis on the protection of refugee children’s rights [1]. Although the participants in this study are migrant students under temporary protection rather than formally recognised refugees, the protective logic of Article 22 remains directly relevant to children in forced migration contexts, making the examination of SMS’s awareness of CR especially important. The Syrian civil war has resulted in one of the largest displacement crises globally, with Türkiye hosting a substantial number of Syrian migrant children. Empirical research suggests that displacement experiences and humanitarian conditions may undermine migrant children’s sense of self-determination, with implications for how they perceive and exercise participation rights in host societies [26]. In the Turkish context, studies on SMS’ education highlight persistent barriers to school access, integration, and participation, which may constrain opportunities for rights education and awareness [50]. Comparative evidence further indicates that contextual conditions—such as living outside refugee camps—can limit children’s exposure to rights-based educational practices [5]. Cultural narratives and social positioning in Türkiye may also influence SMS’ agency and participation in social life [3]. For migrant children to meaningfully exercise their rights and integrate into the educational and social systems of host countries, developing awareness of CR is essential. Accordingly, examining SMS’ awareness of CR and the factors shaping this awareness is critical for informing rights-based educational practices and policies. Given the need to establish a contextual baseline, this study adopts a comparative design between TS and SMS.

Culture and the sociocultural environment are important factors in the interpretation and realization of CR [15, 35, 43], and conducting cross-cultural comparative studies is crucial for developing effective policies and practices for CR. Conducting a comparative mixed-methods study to examine the awareness on CR among both TS and SMS will provide detailed insights into how awareness regarding CR is shaped within these two groups operating within the same educational system. Despite the expanding literature on children’s rights and refugee education, studies that directly compare rights awareness between local and migrant children within the same educational system are still limited. This study contributes to the literature by examining CR awareness among TS and SMS. Additionally, such research will help identify similarities and differences between TS and SMS in terms of their understanding of CR, as well as reveal the developmental needs necessary to enhance CR awareness. As a result, examining the factors that influence the development of awareness is crucial for identifying the supportive or inhibiting cognitive and environmental dynamics that affect CR awareness. The findings obtained through such a study will significantly contribute to the development of national policies in Türkiye that aim to simultaneously foster CR awareness and positive behaviours in both TS and SMS. In broader terms, these findings will inform international policy-making efforts focused on the rights and development of children and migrant populations across different global contexts. Ultimately, the study will generate critical data to inform international education and development policies that promote children’s physical and psychological well-being. These insights will also support efforts to empower children to recognize and exercise their rights and to nurture their growth into confident individuals who embrace democratic values. Moreover, it will enrich theoretical knowledge about CR awareness in international contexts and contribute significantly to the literature on the implementation of the UNCRC and the global state of CR awareness. In light of preceding information above, this study aims to examine the awareness on CR among TS and SMS, along with the factors that influence the development of this awareness.

Method

Research model

The present study, which aims to examine the awareness on CR among SMS and TS as well as the factors influencing this process, employed an explanatory sequential mixed methods research design [16]. In light of this, the quantitative phase of the study employed a descriptive survey model although the qualitative phase involved a comparative case study. In the quantitative phase, research data were collected from a total of 871 students, comprising 437 TS and 434 SMS, using the Children’s Rights Awareness Test (CRAT) developed by Topçu [49]. Subsequently, in the qualitative phase, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 15 TS and 15 SMS using the Children’s Rights Interview Form (CRIF). Additionally, participants were asked to draw pictures representing the rights they possess, and these drawings were also employed as data collection tools. This child-centered visual method was used to capture experiential and affective dimensions of children’s rights awareness that may not be fully articulated through verbal responses alone. This approach allowed for the quantitative findings regarding CR awareness to be further explored and better understood through in-depth qualitative analysis. However, an important aspect that must be carefully considered in mixed methods research is integration. Integration in mixed methods studies can be carried out at various stages of the research process, including data collection, findings, and discussion. In this regard, one of the commonly applied approaches is to present the quantitative and qualitative findings sequentially and then integrate them within a combined discussion [20]. In this study as well, the quantitative and qualitative findings were first presented sequentially and subsequently integrated in the discussion section. Through this process, the relationships between the quantitative and qualitative results were examined and interpreted in conjunction with the existing literature.

Participants of the study

Participants for quantitative data collection

The quantitative data of the study were collected from 434 SMS and 437 TS, all of whom were enrolled in the 4th grade at 10 primary schools located in a city in south-eastern Türkiye with a high population of Syrian migrants. Participants were selected using a disproportionate sampling method. In this study, the sample was formed using disproportionate cluster sampling. In disproportionate cluster sampling, the sample is created by selecting a sufficient number of clusters from the population without adhering to a specific proportion and based on the principle of randomness [33]. In this research, fourth-grade students in each primary school were defined as a cluster. To select these clusters, a two-stage approach was used: first, the Provincial Directorate of National Education provided a frame of public schools with high SMS concentrations, second, 10 of these schools were randomly selected to minimize systematic bias. Fourth-grade students attending these schools constituted the quantitative participant group. Fourth-grade students were selected because they had completed the Human Rights, Citizenship and Democracy Education course and therefore possessed the necessary conceptual readiness and linguistic competence to articulate their perspectives on children’s rights more clearly. The schools included in the study predominantly serve students from lower socioeconomic backgrounds and are located in the urban center. The number of active students in fourth-grade classrooms of the participating schools ranged from 20 to 27, while the number of SMS per classroom varied between 9 and 13. The primary rationale for selecting these schools was that the focus of the study is on examining CR awareness comparatively among TS and SMS. Schools with a high concentration of SMS provide a context in which both student groups are educated under similar curricula and classroom conditions, offering an appropriate setting for addressing the research objectives.

Among the TS group, 247 were female and 190 were male, while among the SMS group, 255 were female and 179 were male. All participants were between the ages of 10 and 11. Regarding parental education, among the mothers of TS, 142 had no formal education, 241 completed primary school, 41 completed high school, and 13 were university graduates. Among SMS mothers, 209 were uneducated, 163 had completed primary school, 25 had completed middle school, 32 completed high school, and 5 were university graduates. For TS fathers, 96 were uneducated, 173 had completed primary school, 41 middle school, 82 high school, and 45 university. Among SMS fathers, 160 had no formal education, 206 completed primary school, 56 high school, and 12 university. Participants were included in the study on a voluntary basis and with parental consent.All SMS are proficient in Turkish. In Türkiye, various measures are implemented to address gaps in the Turkish language skills of SMS before they reach the fourth grade, one of which is conducted within the framework of the PIKTES + (Promoting Inclusive Education for Kids in the Turkish Education System) Project, supported by the European Union. Through this project, SMS with deficiencies in Turkish language development are identified and provided with additional instruction in adaptation classes, ensuring that their Turkish language skills are sufficiently developed before entering the fourth grade. To ensure the Turkish language proficiency of SMS included in this study, several procedures were conducted. Specifically, participants’ Turkish language competence was determined by reviewing their academic records in Turkish courses from grades 1 to 4, consulting classroom teachers, and engaging in brief conversations in Turkish with SMS prior to the data collection process.

Participants for qualitative data collection

The qualitative data were collected from 15 TS and 15 SMS, selected using criterion sampling, one of the purposeful sampling methods. Within this scope of the research, each group included five students with high, five with moderate, and five with low scores on the CRAT. Participation was also based on voluntary consent and parental approval. All SMS are proficient in Turkish. Among the TS participants, nine were female and six were male; among the SMS participants, eight were female and seven were male. The number of siblings ranged from 3 to 12 for SMS, and from 3 to 7 for TS. Regarding the educational background of the participants’ mothers: among SMS, 8 had completed primary school, 5 had no formal education, and 2 had completed high school. For TS, 4 mothers were uneducated, 6 had completed primary school, 2 had completed middle school, and 3 were high school graduates. As for the fathers, among SMS, 2 had no formal education, 6 had completed primary school, 4 high school, and 3 university. Among TS fathers, 2 were university graduates, 2 were uneducated, 7 had completed primary school, 3 high school, and 1 middle school.

Data collection ınstruments

In this study, children’s rights awareness is approached as a multidimensional phenomenon rather than a single, uniform form of knowledge. It is conceptualized as including (a) cognitive awareness, referring to children’s explicit knowledge of rights as defined in formal frameworks such as the UNCRC; (b) normative beliefs, reflecting what children consider legitimate, fair, or deserved in relation to rights; (c) behavioural intentions, indicating how children report they would respond when confronted with rights-related situations; and (d) lived experience, capturing how rights are interpreted, enacted, or constrained in children’s everyday social contexts. From this perspective, the quantitative component of the study (CRAT scores) primarily captures cognitive awareness and selected normative orientations, whereas the qualitative interviews and drawings offer insight into how children make sense of rights through lived experiences shaped by deprivation, authority relations, and everyday practices. Throughout the manuscript, findings are interpreted in line with the specific dimension of awareness reflected by each data source.

Children’s Rights Awareness Test (CRAT)

In this study, the CRAT, developed by Topçu [49], was used to determine the participants' awareness of CR. The reliability of the CRAT was assessed before implementation. The CRAT consists of 21 multiple-choice items based on key rights such as the right to participation, freedom of expression, education, and protection from discrimination, play and recreation, protection from verbal and physical violence, rights of children with disabilities, and protection from sexual abuse. These multiple-choice items include sample case scenarios that present problems related to CR and offer three response options—one correct and two incorrect—regarding the situation described in each scenario. During the development of the CRAT, item discrimination indices ranged from 0.29 to 0.85, the mean item difficulty was 0.57, and the KR-20 reliability coefficient was 0.81. In this study, the reliability of the CRAT (KR-20) was calculated as 0.82 for TS. To determine the suitability of the CRAT for SMS, several procedures were implemented. First, the test items were reviewed by Syrian scholars working at Turkish state universities—one specializing in psychology and two conducting research in the field of CR—as well as by three Syrian primary school teachers who completed their undergraduate or graduate education in Türkiye and received formal training on CR. Next, a pilot study was conducted with 15 SMS (five low-, five medium-, and five high-achieving students) to assess the comprehensibility and appropriateness of the items. In addition, statistical analyses were carried out using data collected from 100 fourth-grade SMS. Following these analyses, item discrimination indices were found to range between 0.26 and 0.77, the average item difficulty was calculated as 0.56, and the KR-20 reliability coefficient was 0.79 for SMS.

It should be noted that the CRAT primarily assesses children’s reasoning and recognition in hypothetical rights-related scenarios and does not directly measure enacted behaviour or actual access to rights in lived contexts.

Sample items from the CRAT include: “Melis is 10 years old. She communicates with her friends through Facebook. One day a stranger messages Melis on Facebook, and she becomes friends with this person. The person asks Melis to send her photos, and Melis does so. Later, the person wants to meet in person, threatening to tell her parents about the photos if she does not agree. What should Melis do? A) Go to the meeting because she is afraid of her parents. B) Tell an adult (parent, teacher, etc.) about what happened. C) Offer to send more photos instead of meeting.” and “Although Metin excels in all his subjects, his English grade is poor because the English teacher acts harshly toward students. The teacher punishes students who answer incorrectly with physical violence. Metin is so afraid that he cannot participate in class and, consequently, fails English. What should Metin do? A) Report the situation to his homeroom teacher and ask for help. B) Think that teachers can beat students if necessary. C) Work hard in English class so he won’t get punished.

Children’s Rights Interview Form (CRIF)

In the study, the CRIF, developed by the researchers,was utilized to qualitatively explore participants' awareness on CR and the factors influencing the development of this awareness in depth. During the development process of the CRIF, open-ended questions were formulated based on the findings from the quantitative phase of the research and a review of the relevant literature. These questions were then submitted to experts for evaluation: The interview form was reviewed by both Turkish and Syrian-origin experts. Specifically, three Syrian-origin faculty members—one in psychology and two in the field of CR, all with expertise in qualitative research—working at public universities in Türkiye, as well as three Syrian-origin classroom teachers who completed their undergraduate and graduate education at public universities in Türkiye and received training in CR, evaluated the form. In addition, the form was reviewed by five Turkish faculty members—two in psychology and three in the field of CR, all with qualitative research experience—and four classroom teachers who received CR education during their graduate studies. Based on their feedback, necessary corrections were made, and a pilot study was conducted with six SMS and six TS. After receiving the feedback from the pilot study, the CRIF was finalized. The finalized form consisted of two sections: the first part covers demographic information and the second part focuses on questions related to CR. The CRIF involves ten open-ended questions. The questions focused on understanding CR, identifying the rights children possess, the rights they possess/use in their own lives, and the rights they lack and cannot use in their own lives. The questions were first asked in a non-directional manner, followed by more detailed questions. Examples of these questions include: “Do you think children have certain rights? – If yes, what are these rights? Could you explain? Why do you think children have these rights? – If no, why do you think so? In your own life (at school, at home, or in your daily life), are there any rights that you have or use as a child? – If yes, what are these rights? Could you explain? – If no, why do you think that is?

Data collection process

The study was conducted during the spring term of the 2024–2025 academic year. In the present study, quantitative data were initially collected and analysed, followed by the collection of qualitative data. Prior to data collection, ethical approval was obtained from the Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee of Gaziantep University (Approval No: E-73628654–604.01–613089; Date: 02 February 2025). Institutional permission and parental consent were also obtained.

In accordance with Law No. 5395 (Child Protection Law) and the Turkish Penal Code (Article 279), a rigorous safeguarding protocol was established. In the event of a disclosure regarding ongoing abuse, neglect, or exploitative labour, the researchers were mandated to suspend the session immediately and report the situation to the school counsellor and school administration for formal referral to the Provincial Directorate of Family and Social Services or relevant judicial authorities. However, no such disclosures occurred during the study, and no referrals were required.

During the quantitative data collection phase, the researcher administered the forms in face-to-face sessions, and a translator was present to assist with data collection from SMS. The qualitative data collection process was carried out as two sessions. In the first session, participants were asked to draw pictures related to CR. In the second session, the CRIF was administered using a semi-structured interview technique, and the interviews were audio-recorded. Interviews lasted approximately 30–45 min for each session and were conducted in the children's own schools. To ensure anonymity, participants were assigned numerical identifiers: Turkish students were coded as TS followed by a number (e.g., TS5), and Syrian migrant students as SMS followed by a number (e.g., SMS9). All interview sessions were accompanied by a school counsellor to provide guidance, and a translator also participated in the interviews conducted with SMS. Bilingual checking and member checking were used to ensure accuracy of meaning. The translator was not routinely involved in CRAT administration and did not translate test items systematically. During the qualitative phase, the translator did not conduct or lead interviews but supported only the bilingual verification (member-checking) stage.

Data analysis

In the study, the quantitative data were analysed through Independent Samples t-test and descriptive analyses. Prior to conducting the t-test, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were examined to ensure the appropriateness of the statistical procedure. Normality was assessed by inspecting skewness and kurtosis values, which were within the acceptable range of ± 1 for both groups. Homogeneity of variance was tested using Levene’s test, and the results indicated that this assumption was met (p > 0.05). The qualitative data obtained from the CRIF were analysed using content analysis. The qualitative data were analyzed using an inductive content analysis approach, in which meaning units were coded and systematically organized into broader themes in line with the aims of the study [22]. As a result of the analysis, four themes were identified—one related to meaning-making regarding CR and three based on students’ awareness of the rights they possess. In addition, during the qualitative analysis process, the drawing data produced by TS and SMS were also analyzed, the CR represented in these drawings were identified, and then connections were established between these and the themes under which they were categorized. These drawings were also presented during the findings section together with the rights with which they were associated under the relevant themes. Thus, through the analysis process, the data related to students’ awareness of CR were structured in a way that generated a holistic and in-depth understanding across various dimensions—including meaning-making related to CR, rights possessed, factors influencing the development of awareness, the use of rights, factors hindering the exercise of rights, and the emotional effects created by the extent to which rights were exercised. To ensure reliability in the qualitative data analysis, inter-coder reliability was calculated using Miles and Huberman’s [41] formula—Reliability = Consensus/(Consensus + Disagreement). The reliability coefficient was found to be 0.94, indicating a high level of agreement.

Findings

In the study, the findings regarding the awareness on CR among native TS and SMS are presented under two headings: quantitative and qualitative findings.

Quantitative findings regarding awareness on children’s rights

Quantitative findings related to TS and SMS' awareness on CR were derived from statistical analyses of their scores on the CRAT. In this light, the CRAT scores of TS and SMS were initially analysed comparatively using an independent samples t-test. Subsequently, descriptive analyses of individual CRAT items were conducted to provide a comparative evaluation based on item content. The results of the independent samples t-test indicated a statistically significant difference between the CRAT scores of TS and SMS students [t(869) = 7.22, p < 0.05, η2 = 0.057]. The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference ranged from 1.54 to 2.69. An examination of the mean scores of CRAT revealed that TS students (M = 12.49, SD = 4.55) scored higher than SMS students (M = 10.38, SD = 4.00). Although statistically significant, the effect size (η2 = 0.057) indicates a modest magnitude. This suggests a substantial overlap between the two distributions, indicating that the groups do not represent two distinct categories of awareness. Instead, the findings point toward a shared landscape of rights conceptualization, where the observed difference reflects a nuanced quantitative divergence rather than a fundamental gap in understanding. From this perspective, the observed difference should not be interpreted as a categorical or large gap in rights awareness, but rather as a limited quantitative divergence. To better understand how this difference is reflected in specific rights-related situations, item-level analyses were conducted. These analyses aimed to illuminate patterns of similarity and divergence in meaning-making, complementing the overall score differences with a more nuanced interpretation of children’s responses.

The item-level analysis reveals a complex picture where broad thematic overlaps coexist with divergences in specific domains. While both groups exhibit similar patterns of compliance in domestic and school-based authority contexts, significant divergences emerge in areas such as child labour, seeking institutional justice, and participation rights. The overlap is particularly evident in responses to physical punishment; for instance, nearly identical proportions of TS (42.10%) and SMS students (43.73%) reported the normative belief that increased academic effort could serve as a means to avoid corporal punishment from teachers. Similarly, a shared pattern of compliance was observed regarding the right to play, where a majority of both TS (60.41%) and SMS students (68.79%) prioritized family-mandated study over leisure. These commonalities underscore a shared 'constrained agency,' where children from both groups often conceptualize rights as conditional permissions. Conversely, in domains such as child labour and institutional help-seeking, a significant divergence emerged in favour of TS students compared to their SMS peers. The analysis revealed that similar response patterns were more prevalent than differences, and that both groups occasionally displayed passivity in reported intentions to exercise their rights. Such passivity should be interpreted cautiously; rather than a simple lack of rights knowledge, it reflects a shared pattern of 'constrained agency' in the face of adult authority. Specifically, both TS and SMS students appear to conceptualize rights not as inherent entitlements, but as conditional permissions granted or restricted by authority figures such as mothers and teachers. The analysis also showed that when experiencing violence from their mothers, 173 TS students (39.5%) and 222 SMS students (51.15%) tended to remain silent. In cases where a teacher used physical punishment due to academic failure, 184 TS students (42.10%) and 185 SMS students (43.73%) held the normative belief that studying harder would help them avoid such punishment. Additionally, 57 TS students (13.04%) and 88 SMS students (20.17%) were determined to perceive physical punishment by teachers as permissible when necessary. Such situations indicate that a significant portion of students in both groups—however more notably among SMS—tend to remain passive in claiming their rights within the hypothetical scenarios when the perpetrator of violence is an authority figure such as a mother or a teacher. This pattern suggests that children’s responses in such situations may reflect contextual constraints, such as power asymmetries, fear of authority, cultural norms surrounding obedience, or learned compliance, rather than a simple lack of rights awareness. Accordingly, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of low awareness per se, but as reflecting the complex relationship between knowledge of rights and perceived feasibility of exercising them in real-life contexts. Within the scope of the right to play, when families restricted playtime in favour of academic study, 264 TS students (60.41%) and 291 SMS students (68.79%) demonstrated a tendency to comply with the situation. Accordingly, 173 TS students (39.58%) and 143 SMS students (33.80%) exhibited a preference for communicating their need to play with their families. In cases where teachers punished students for low academic performance by excluding them from extracurricular activities, only 226 TS students (51.71%) and 205 SMS students (48.46%) indicated a tendency to seek help from their families or school administration. In the item addressing the right to participation within the family, 226 TS students (51.71%) and 188 SMS students (44.44%) showed a tendency to express their wishes to their families, indicating that the inclination to exercise the right to participation was not highly prevalent among either group. Moreover, in the context of participation rights, it was observed—particularly among SMS—that students in both groups predominantly endorsed the normative belief that parents’ opinions should be followed. Regarding awareness on protection from child labour, 233 SMS students (55.08%) and 171 TS students (39.91%) tended to select responses that did not align with rights-based actions. In this respect, it was found—more common among SMS—that there was a widespread normative belief or misconception that children could work to support their families. In the study, students’ awareness on protection against abuse was examined in three contexts: school, internet, and street. The findings unveiled that, in response to abuse, 316 TS students (72.31%) and 274 SMS students (64.77%) at school, 312 TS students (71.39%) and 269 SMS students (61.98%) on the internet, and 307 TS students (70.25%) and 242 SMS students (55.76%) on the street expressed a tendency to seek help. Conversely, other students in both groups reported a tendency to remain silent. As for experiencing physical violence from peers at school, 344 TS students (78.71%) reported a tendency to seek help from a teacher, while the number dropped to 270 (63.82%) among SMS students. Other students tended to remain silent or expressed a desire to drop out of school. Another remarkable finding worth noting was that the reported intention to seek justice by applying to official authorities was less frequent among SMS in fewer SMS students compared to TS students. To cite an example, 197 SMS students (46.57%) and 267 TS students (61.09%) reported that they would apply to official institutions to receive health support. This finding suggests that the tendency to claim one’s right to health is adopted by fewer than half of the SMS participants. In another example, when spoiled food was sold in the school canteen, 277 TS students (63.38%) and 233 SMS students (55.08%) were found to report a tendency to inform the school administration. This shows that the tendency to claim rights in a school context dropped to as low as 55.08% among SMS students. The study also investigated students’ awareness regarding environmental protection and the rights of children with disabilities. In the face of environmental pollution, 291 TS students (66.59%) and 233 SMS students (55.08%) stated a tendency to seek help from relevant authorities though 109 SMS students (25.76%) and 63 TS students (14.41%) chose to remain silent, thinking that their opinions would not be valued. Regarding the rights of children with disabilities, it was found that a significant portion of students from both nationalities perceived that distancing oneself from a disabled peer as a justifiable response. Accordingly, 233 TS students (53.31%) and 182 SMS students (41.19%) stated that keeping a distance would be the proper course of action. In addition, it should be noted that CRAT items assess children’s reasoning and self-reported responses to hypothetical rights-related situations, rather than actual behaviour enacted in real-life contexts.

Based on an overall evaluation, while TS students demonstrated slightly higher mean scores, the situation is complicated by the shared challenges both groups face. In many contexts, a considerable portion of both groups struggled to align their reported intentions with rights-based principles when faced with power asymmetries. This suggests that for both Turkish and Syrian children, the 'lived experience' of rights is heavily mediated by an environment that prioritizes obedience over agency, though this constraint is more pronounced for SMS students in institutional contexts.

Qualitative findings on awareness on children’s rights

In order to explore the awareness of TS and SMS students towards CR more elaborately, qualitative analyses were also conducted. Accordingly, the findings are presented under two main headings, respectively.

Findings on awareness related to the interpretation of children's rights

The study provided various insights into how TS and SMS perceive CR and the factors influencing the development of their awareness. When examining the conceptual understandings developed by TS and SMS within the scope of awareness related to the meaning of CR as presented in Table 1, it was observed that the majority of participants in both groups defined CR in general terms as the rights that children possess or rights that protect children. These explanations reveal that though students have a reality-based understanding of CR, their knowledge remains narrow and limited in scope. These definitions primarily reflect how children verbally label rights, rather than indicating the depth to which such rights are internalized or enacted in everyday situations. Examples of these interpretations include such statements as follows: “They are the rights that children have; we learned them in a new lesson…” (TS-1), “They are rights that protect children; they protect children from war…” (SMS-4), “They are rights that protect children from all bad things” (TS-11). In light of the abovementioned citations, certain SMS students, notably, associate CR with protection from war and have developed an approach that CR serves a protective function in this regard. This pattern highlights how children’s descriptions of rights are closely tied to salient life experiences, and should therefore be read as experience-based framings rather than abstract or legalistic definitions. Information gained during formal education has influenced students’ conceptualizations. In addition, part of students in both groups were found to interpret CR by recalling specific rights learned in class, offering limited definitions such as “CR means the right to education, play, and nutrition. That’s what our teacher told us…” (TS-2). Students also interpreted CR based on negative experiences in their daily lives while construing the process. In these interpretations, both native TS and SMS tended to define CR with reference to not being overburdened with household chores or not being made to work. One SMS expressed this situation as “CR mean not being made to do too much work. I am a child but I do a lot of work; not being made to do so much should be my right…” (SMS-1). Besides, it was determined that some SMS students grounded their understanding of CR in the context of economic hardship. Specifically, they emphasized that children’s basic needs should be met even when they lack financial means. To cite an example, one SMS stated this as “Even if children have no money, they should still be given what they need. Like a pencil, shoes, food… If a child has no money, his needs must still be met. That is a children’s right.” (SMS-8). On the other hand, both SMS and TS students were also found to conceptualize CR as permissions granted to them—things children are allowed to do. Children frequently related CR to what their parents or teachers permit. Such statements describe how children situate rights within existing authority structures, and therefore capture perceived boundaries of action rather than direct indicators of children’s knowledge of formal rights frameworks. One SMS expressed this situation as “The things my father and mother let me do are CR. Because we do what they say…” (SMS-13). Another SMS, however, stated “CR are the things the teacher allows us to do. In class, the teacher makes the rules—whatever they allow is a right. At home, it’s the father…” (SMS-7). Conversely, both native TS and SMS attempted to define CR as things that make a child happy or acts of support provided to children. However, defining CR as “receiving love” was observed exclusively among SMS.. In this context, SMS identified being loved by their teacher, father, and friends as a children’s right and expressed a desire to have access to these rights themselves. In light of these findings, it can be reported that a significant portion of both TS and SMS simply managed to offer limited explanations focusing on the basic characteristics of CR. Although the number of participants who expressed each type of explanation varied between groups, it can be concluded that most types of conceptualizations were shared across both groups—except for two that were unique. Overall, the findings indicate that students’ conceptualizations of CR vary according to everyday experiences, educational exposure, and living conditions, reflecting different ways of framing and articulating rights rather than uniform levels of awareness.

Findings regarding awareness on children’s entitled rights

In the research, the findings of the awareness of TS and SMS about the CR are presented under three headings, respectively, based on the themes reached as a result of the analysis.

Findings on children’s rights commonly recognized by both TS and SMS

In the current study, a total of 15 rights were revealed to be commonly acknowledged by both TS and SMS students though the number of participants who expressed each right varied between the groups. Of all, the rights to play, entertainment, rest, and education emerged as the most widely recognized rights by students in both groups. From this standpoint, both TS and SMS stressed that children must attend school and have the right to receive education, as well as the right to play, rest, and enjoy leisure activities both at home and in school. A significant number of drawings related to these rights were also created by participants from both groups (see Appendix A1).

In the study, both TS and SMS stated that they learned about the rights to play, entertainment, rest, and education during school lessons. However, part of the participants reported that children do not actually possess or enjoy these rights in practice. These statements describe perceived gaps between learning about rights and being able to exercise them, without implying a lack of awareness of the rights themselves. Students attributed this perception to the challenges they face in exercising these rights in their own lives. Within this respect, both TS and SMS identified several limiting factors, including excessive responsibilities assigned to children by their families at home, involvement in child labour, restrictive behaviours from parents, teachers replacing play and physical activity classes with other academic subjects at school, and financial constraints (see Appendix A2).

Children also illustrated through their drawings that being employed in a job prevents them from exercising their right to education. In these drawings, students depicted that children were unable to attend school since they had to work, failing to realize their right to education. They emphasized that children should not be employed in any form of labour. Discussion

In line with the aim of the study, the quantitative and qualitative findings indicate that children’s awareness towards their rights cannot be reduced to mere knowledge; on the contrary, it is multidimensional, experience-based, and often deeply intertwined with cultural and social contexts. In particular, the comparisons between TS and SMS provide valuable insights not only into the cognitive awareness of rights, but also into how these rights are interpreted and linked to specific living conditions. The quantitative data, supported by qualitative findings and students’ illustrations, collectively present a multi-layered map of awareness. Taken together, these findings point to a systematic divergence between children’s formal awareness of rights and how these rights are understood, prioritized, and lived in everyday contexts. To capture this divergence, an “experiential rights gap” is conceptualized as the distance between rights as normatively articulated (e.g., in the UNCRC) and rights as constructed through children’s lived experiences shaped by deprivation, authority relations, and daily constraints. Unlike a knowledge gap, which implies lack of information, or an implementation gap, which refers to institutional failure to enforce rights, the experiential rights gap refers to divergences that emerge even when children can articulate formal rights language but interpret and prioritize those rights through biography, scarcity, and relational power structures. This concept is introduced as an analytic lens grounded in the present findings rather than as a fully theorized model. It is intended as a descriptive-analytic tool for interpreting how children’s own meaning-making reshapes rights discourse in context, rather than as a new normative framework.

TS demonstrated a slightly higher level of awareness regarding some CR. However, this awareness generally remained at a formal level, notably with respect to certain rights such as participation, non-discrimination, and child labour. In many cases, it did not evolve into a critical or practical understanding of the essence of these rights. Among SMS, awareness was more prominently focused on fundamental survival rights (e.g., housing, food, health), yet this awareness was largely characterized by personal experiences of deprivation and was not necessarily accompanied by a systematic or conceptual understanding of rights. These findings are in consistence with the study conducted by Covell and Howe [19], which revealed that children’s knowledge of rights tends to be limited primarily to protection rights. The comparative study by af Ursin and Haanpää [2], conducted across 16 countries, provides a similar picture—although children’s knowledge of their rights was limited, they generally believed that their rights were respected. In the present study, a discrepancy was detected between children’s actual knowledge concerning rights and their perception on how those rights are respected. In this context, Warfa et al. [53] demonstrated that school-based, attachment-oriented psychosocial programs can significantly enhance both the social-emotional development and the rights awareness of disadvantaged children, emphasizing the value of child participation. These patterns are particularly meaningful when considered in relation to the UNCRC framework. Among the four core groups of rights, participation rights are widely recognized as both the most innovative and the most difficult to implement in practice. This difficulty stems from the fact that participation requires children not only to know their rights but also to be included in decision-making processes and given opportunities to express their views. The present findings—especially the differences observed between TS and SMS regarding participation—align with this broader challenge highlighted in the literature. On the other hand, some TS participants’ emphasis on having a private room, a mobile phone, or personal space can be read not merely as inaccurate extensions of rights language, but as socially situated claims about autonomy, dignity, and respect within a consumer-oriented environment. These responses suggest that rights discourse may merge with aspirational goods in relatively more resource-secure contexts, further widening the experiential rights gap between normatively defined rights and those constructed through everyday life. In this sense, the gap does not imply error but divergence in phenomenological emphasis shaped by differing material and relational contexts.

Despite divergences in some CR, the results show broad thematic overlaps. Depending on the quantitative findings, students tend to adopt a passive stance in the face of violence and authority figures, and they rarely take initiative to defend their rights. This tendency is further confirmed by the qualitative data, in which students frequently interpret rights violations as “family rules,” or as “deserved punishment.” Instead of defining and defending their rights, students often develop a coping mechanism, students develop a coping mechanism based on conformity to adult control. However, rather than interpreting such patterns as a straightforward lack of awareness, it is important to distinguish between cognitive recognition of rights and children’s perceived capacity to exercise them within asymmetrical power relations. In several authority-related contexts, the issue may not be insufficient knowledge of rights, but limited perceived safety, agency, or viable routes for claiming them. Shier [48] noted that children may perceive expressing their own voices as unnecessary. Likewise, Ruck et al. [47] highlighted that CR to participation is not adequately supported in either family or school settings. Lavergne et al. [38], in a study involving ethno-cultural minority children, also emphasized that families often hesitate to collaborate with social services, which in turn weakens CR-based participation processes. Accordingly, the inability of children to exercise their rights stems not only from a lack of knowledge, but also from structural barriers imposed by adults [28]. In this context Osborne et al. [42] suggest for the importance of including not only children but also teachers and parents in studies addressing exclusion and inclusion in educational contexts, as this provides richer, more actionable perspectives for policy and practice. While children’s rights awareness was operationalized as multidimensional in this study, it remains crucial to analytically separate cognitive recognition from perceived claimability. In several domains, particularly those involving authority figures, the data suggest that children may recognize rights at a declarative level yet perceive limited safe avenues to assert them. From a developmental perspective, this tension may reflect moral reasoning under constraint, where children internalize hierarchical authority structures and recalibrate their expectations of claim-making accordingly. Silence in the face of adult control may therefore represent not the absence of awareness, but a context-sensitive regulation of agency. This distinction carries important implications for educational practice, as strengthening knowledge alone may be insufficient without addressing structural and relational constraints.

The research data supported by students’ drawings also complies with these findings, revealing that children tend to interpret rights in relation to areas in which they acutely feel deprivation. This pattern is also evident in the quantitative findings, which indicate that rights related to basic needs (such as housing, nutrition, and safety) received significantly higher recognition scores than participation- or autonomy-related rights, particularly among SMS. In the drawings by students, there is a repetitive emphasis on tangible needs such as housing, food, and clothing—particularly among SMS—which suggests that these students associate CR mainly with physical protection and basic necessities. This indicates that experiences of war, migration, and poverty play a significant role in shaping their understanding of rights. This qualitative pattern is consistent with the quantitative findings, which show that SMS scored significantly higher on items related to basic survival rights, suggesting a convergence between lived deprivation and rights prioritization. In fact, the existing body of literature strongly emphasizes that wars obstruct children’s access to even the most fundamental rights, and that such children tend to define rights through the lens of the deprivations they face [6, 36]. In line with this, SMS participants’ awareness on CR appears to be shaped in parallel with the survival efforts that follow war and forced migration. Within this context, the finding that some SMS perceived practices such as labor, household chores, or birthday celebrations as “rights” reflects a need-based understanding of children’s rights shaped by displacement and deprivation. In situations of forced migration, everyday practices related to survival, care, or emotional recognition may gain the meaning of rights for children. Rather than interpreting these responses solely as a conceptual confusion between rights and responsibilities, they may also signal an adaptive moral reframing under conditions of scarcity. Contributing to family survival can become moralized and re-described as entitlement, allowing children to position themselves not merely as passive recipients of hardship but as active contributors within the family’s moral economy. In this sense, defining labour as a “right” may function as a way of preserving dignity and agency within constrained structural circumstances. Taken together, these findings indicates that SMS’s conceptions of rights are influenced not only by normative frameworks such as the UNCRC, but also by their lived experiences. For instance, when a participant linked the right to food and housing explicitly to memories of war, cold, and starvation, the claim exceeded the level of abstract need and became a biographically grounded rights assertion. Similarly, when emotional recognition or celebration was articulated as a right in contexts of perceived exclusion or family complexity, these were not merely expressions of unmet needs but phenomenological reconstructions of rights through lived vulnerability. Such examples illustrate the experiential rights gap at a descriptive level: rights are filtered through memory, deprivation, and relational positioning.

Both the quantitative and qualitative data demonstrate that students mostly perceive the right to play and rest as a reward for “good behaviour” or “studying,” rather than as an inherent right. While quantitative results reveal this perception as a widespread trend, students’ drawings and verbal explanations further illustrate how play is framed as something “earned,” reinforcing the reward-based understanding observed quantitatively. This reward-based perception suggests that the pedagogical and developmental significance of play is largely unrecognized. Nevertheless, games contribute to child development in a multi-faceted manner [39] and have been shown to enhance motivation [12]. Besides, they boost children’s social interactions, decision-making skills, and self-regulation [40]. In a study carried out Barblett et al. [7], it was stated that games, friendship, a sense of belonging, and connection with family are critical during school transition processes and the importance of creating participatory spaces where children can reflect their own experiences was also highlighted. In this context, the findings of the present study are of particular significance owing to the fact that they demonstrate that children interpret their right to play and to participate not only as conceptual entitlements, but also through the lens of emotional and social belonging.

One of the areas in which the weakest awareness level was observed was the right to participation. The students participating in the study reported that their opinions were not taken into account either at home or at school, and that they were excluded from decision-making processes, suggesting that the limited understanding of the right to participation may be a challenge for both children and adults. This finding is further supported by quantitative results indicating low agreement levels on participation-related items, suggesting that children’s perceived exclusion is both experientially felt and statistically evident. Related body of literature highlights this issue, emphasizing that denying children a voice in decision-making processes negatively affects their democratic participation skills [25]. Likewise, the findings of the present study indicate that though the right to participation is formally acknowledged, children are generally unable to exercise this right in practice.

Although child labour was observed among TS, it emerged as a more normalized phenomenon within the sample of SMS. SMS were frequently seen to describe working as an essential responsibility—either to support their families. In accordance with this qualitative finding, it is striking that the quantitative data also revealed a notably low rate of opposition to child labour. Together, these findings suggest that children’s normalization of labour is not merely an individual perception but a structurally reinforced stance reflected across both data strands. This indicates that children often struggle to differentiate between rights and responsibilities, and that social norms play a significant role in shaping these perceptions. These results align with prior research emphasizing that child labour is not solely a consequence of poverty but is also reinforced by cultural norms and social expectations [9, 44].

In the context of the right to protection from abuse, students demonstrated a relatively higher level of awareness. However, this awareness often manifested as a passive form of protection and rarely evolved into proactive behaviours such as seeking help or reporting incidents, which may indicate not an absence of knowledge, but constraints in perceived efficacy or safety when confronting authority or threatening situations. This suggests that in the absence of a safe environment or accessible support mechanisms, children may be inclined to internalize experiences of abuse. Gilbert et al. [27] emphasize that the effects of abuse on children are not only individual but also long-term at social level. Aktay [4] similarly notes that such experiences can cause severe harm across all developmental domains of children. In a study by Hallett [29] conducted in the Australian context, it was stated that the lack of clarity in the definition of abuse and the neglect of need-based vulnerabilities often lead to children’s victimization, both of which weaken prevention efforts. Developing a holistic awareness that frames abuse not as an individual act, but as a consequence of broader structural inequalities is crucial.

Although a significant number of students demonstrated a positive awareness regarding environmental issues, they are found to be reluctant to actively engage in the solution process. Quantitative data support this pattern by indicating relatively high awareness scores alongside lower participation intentions, while qualitative responses reveal that concern of not being taken seriously discourages active involvement. Concerns regarding their opinions not being taken seriously were seen to limit their right to environmental participation. However, studies carried out by Chawla [14] and Bentsen et al. [8] reported that fostering environmental awareness starting from an early age contributes to both individual and social sustainability. Hosokawa et al. [31] suggested that rights-focused education is most effective when introduced early. In this sense, supporting children’s awareness on environmental rights cannot be achieved merely through the provision of information,instead, it requires the creation of learning environments that actively encourage children’s participation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the awareness of TS and SMS towards CR is characterized by distinctly different life experiences. These perceptions are found to be constructed through experiences and cultural frameworks rather than being merely based on knowledge. Though TS tend to emphasize individual and liberty-based rights, SMS prioritize fundamental survival rights. Nonetheless, significant deficiencies occur in the implementation of rights across both groups. These findings highlight the need for both school-based rights education policies and holistic practices supported by families and communities. Efforts to enhance awareness on CR must be structured in a multidimensional way, integrating schools, families, and the broader society. In particular, many children struggle to distinguish between rights and responsibilities, or reinterpret responsibilities through the language of rights in ways that reflect adaptive responses to structural constraints, underscoring the importance of integrating rights education with real-life contexts. At the policy level, these findings point to the necessity of developing rights-based educational policies that explicitly address children’s difficulties in distinguishing between rights and responsibilities and promote meaningful participation. At the level of educational practice, the results emphasize the importance of creating participatory classroom environments and providing targeted professional development for teachers. In addition, family-oriented awareness initiatives are needed to ensure that children’s rights are supported consistently across school and home contexts.

Beyond the Turkish context, the findings also offer implications for migrant education policies in other host countries (e.g., Germany, Lebanon). The need-based and experiential construction of children’s rights observed in this study suggests that, in contexts of forced migration, rights education should go beyond formal knowledge and take children’s lived conditions into account. From this perspective, the study may serve as a reference for international organizations such as UNICEF and UNHCR by highlighting the importance of integrating participatory educational practices with family- and community-based support mechanisms in order to translate children’s rights into everyday experiences.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. One limitation concerns the absence of observational data. Although the study employed a mixed-methods design combining quantitative and qualitative data, the lack of observational evidence may have limited the ability to capture how children’s rights awareness is enacted in everyday contexts. Future research incorporating observational approaches could provide more nuanced insights into how children experience and exercise their rights in practice. Another limitation relates to the data sources. The study relied solely on children’s accounts and did not include the perspectives of parents or teachers. The absence of multi-informant data may have constrained a fuller understanding of the family and school dynamics that shape children’s rights awareness. A further limitation is the context-bound nature of the sample. Participants were drawn from a single urban setting in Türkiye, and although efforts were made to select schools from comparable low-SES environments, the findings should be interpreted with caution in terms of broader generalizability.

In addition, while the CRAT underwent careful adaptation procedures for SMS and demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties, further validation across diverse cultural and linguistic contexts may be beneficial. Moreover, as formal measurement invariance analyses were not conducted, comparisons between TS and SMS should be interpreted with some caution. A final limitation involves the qualitative data collection process. As the data were generated through interviews, power asymmetries between adult researchers and child participants may have influenced responses, particularly on issues related to authority and rights. Although measures such as the presence of a school counsellor and a cultural mediator were implemented, the potential influence of social desirability cannot be entirely ruled out.

Implications for practice, policy, and future research

Implications for educational practice and policy

The findings suggest that children do not interpret rights solely as formal entitlements but actively construct their meanings through lived experiences shaped by deprivation, authority relations, and everyday practices. This has important implications for the design of rights-based educational programs. In particular, the tendency of children to conflate rights and responsibilities indicates that rights education should move beyond knowledge transmission and incorporate participatory, experience-based pedagogies that support children in distinguishing between these concepts in meaningful ways.

The findings also indicate that parents and teachers may, at times, play constraining roles in the enactment of children’s rights. This points to the need for targeted capacity-building initiatives for both educators and families, aimed at fostering environments in which children can exercise their rights more safely and meaningfully. At the policy level, the results highlight the importance of integrating school-based interventions with family- and community-oriented approaches, particularly in contexts marked by socio-economic disadvantage and migration.

Implications for future research

Future research could extend the present study in several directions. Incorporating observational and longitudinal designs would allow researchers to examine how children’s rights awareness develops and is enacted over time. In addition, including multiple stakeholders—such as parents, teachers, and school administrators—would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relational and institutional factors shaping children’s rights awareness. Comparative studies across different regions and socio-cultural contexts may further help to assess the transferability of the findings. Finally, employing advanced analytical approaches, such as multi-level modelling, could enable researchers to disentangle more precisely the effects of socio-economic variables, parental education, and migration status.

Theoretical implications

The findings point to a consistent divergence between children’s formal knowledge of rights and how these rights are interpreted and experienced in everyday life. Rather than framing this divergence as a simple knowledge deficit or an implementation failure, the study suggests that children actively construct meanings of rights through their lived conditions. In this context, the notion of an “experiential rights gap” is used cautiously as an analytic lens to describe the distance between rights as normatively defined (e.g., in the UNCRC) and rights as understood through children’s lived experiences. Importantly, this concept is not proposed as a generalizable theoretical model, but as a descriptive and context-sensitive way of interpreting how children’s rights are reconfigured through deprivation, constrained agency, and social relations.
