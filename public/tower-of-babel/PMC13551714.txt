Complexity of medical needs and primary care attachment shape patient experiences with episodic virtual primary care: a mixed methods study in two Canadian provinces
Authors: Rebecca H. Correia, Emran Hasan, François Gallant, Shelley Doucet, Tara Kiran, Lauren Lapointe-Shaw, A. Luke MacNeill, Rachel Thelen, Stephanie Welton, Julie Easley, Agnes Grudniewicz, Lindsay Hedden, M. Ruth Lavergne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpisodic virtual care (EVC) offers patients rapid access to virtual primary care services but lacks relational continuity with a primary care clinician or team. We investigated patients’ experiences with EVC and examined differences based on access to a regular primary care clinician and self-reported complexity of medical needs.MethodsIn this convergent parallel mixed methods study, we conducted a descriptive and exploratory survey of EVC users’ health service access, self-reported medical needs, EVC use, and demographics. Participants included adults (aged ≥ 18) living in Nova Scotia or New Brunswick who self-reported using EVC in the past year. We used descriptive statistics to summarize numeric and ordinal data of patient experience and stratified findings by having a regular primary care clinician and medical complexity. We conducted inductive content analysis of free-text survey responses. We integrated quantitative and qualitative data at the interpretation phase.ResultsWe identified mixed experiences among EVC users, with quantitative data portraying virtual care experiences more favourably than free-text responses. Most users felt EVC met their health needs, although those without a regular primary care clinician and with greater medical complexity tended to rate experiences less favourably. In free-text responses, some participants expressed concerns with the technological aspects of virtual visits and voiced preferences for in-person care. Patients with a regular primary care clinician tended to access EVC for short-term health concerns, whereas those without a regular primary care clinician sought care for ongoing/chronic problems and prescription renewals.ConclusionsEVC is helping to address some gaps in access, particularly for patients without a regular primary care clinician, by providing care where there might otherwise be none. However, EVC often falls short of meeting ongoing and more comprehensive care needs, underscoring the importance of improving access to longitudinal primary care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03441-z.

Background

Access to primary care is worsening in the context of increasing demand for services by a growing and aging population, rising patient medical complexity, and shortfalls in health human resources [1]. Many people do not have a regular place for primary care and struggle to access timely care for urgent concerns or after hours [2]. Virtual care is posited to improve primary care access by reducing barriers (e.g., travel distance and cost) and has demonstrated high patient satisfaction, particularly for visits with clinicians seen previously [3]. Virtual care encounters are synchronous, real-time remote interactions between patients and clinicians using text message, telephone, or video modalities [4]. Literature on virtual primary care outside a regular place of care remains limited.

Virtual care in Canada was available but used minimally in the 2010 s [5], before use drastically increased during the COVID-19 pandemic (2019/2020 onward) [6–8], and continues to expand [9, 10]. While in-person visits remain the dominant avenue by which patients access primary care from family physicians (FPs) and nurse practitioners (NPs), corporations have invested in virtual care platforms that mirror in-person walk-in or retail clinics [11, 12], and some governments have partnered with them to expand primary care access [13]. Termed episodic virtual care (EVC), encounters within these proprietary platforms offer patients immediate access to primary care clinicians via text, audio, and/or video encounters, but lack continuity with a clinician or team for ongoing care management and coordination with other health services [14]. EVC is delivered by FPs or NPs that patients have likely not met previously nor will encounter again [15], contrasting with virtual services offered in the context of longitudinal relationships with FPs/NPs.

Despite rapid expansion, very little is known about patients’ experiences accessing EVC. Although studies examining experiences of virtual care with regular FPs/NPs generally report high satisfaction [16–19], encounters where patients have no previous relationship with the clinician providing virtual care have not been similarly studied [5, 20, 21]. Further, patient experiences with virtual primary care vary based on the type of care needed (e.g., routine medical needs versus chronic disease management) and by demographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, gender, rurality, income, immigration status, language preference) [22–24]. It is unclear whether similar patterns are observed for EVC. Understanding the experiences of patients accessing EVC, including differences based on regular access to a FP/NP and the patient’s health status/needs, will inform future investments. We therefore explored: How do experiences of EVC differ by patient characteristics, including having a regular FP/NP and medical complexity? We hypothesized that patients without regular access to FPs/NPs and/or greater medical needs would report more negative experiences of EVC, given greater potential need for relational continuity.

Methods

Design

We conducted a cross-provincial, convergent mixed methods study, with parallel analysis integrating and comparing findings from a quantitative cross-sectional descriptive analysis and qualitative description. Our reporting conforms to the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (Additional File 1). The detailed study methodology was described previously [25].

Setting

Nova Scotia (NS) and New Brunswick (NB) are unique within Canada in partnering with Maple, a private, for-profit virtual care platform that offers virtual walk-in services to address gaps in primary care [25]. In Canada, patients are considered attached to primary care if they have a regular primary care clinician (FP or NP) who provides ongoing, longitudinal care, whereas unattached patients do not have a regular clinician responsible for their care [26]. Medically necessary primary care services in Canada are publicly funded through provincial health insurance plans [27].

In NS, Maple was first made available to people without a regular FP/NP in 2021, then beginning in November 2023, all NS residents have been eligible for two free virtual visits (funded through the provincial public health insurance program); however, those with a regular FP/NP are required to pay privately for subsequent visits. In NB, all residents eligible for provincial health insurance have had free access to EVC through Maple since January 2022, including people with a regular place of care. EVC is primarily delivered by FPs in NS and NPs in NB. These clinicians can access information on previous Maple encounters, but lack access to broader electronic medical record data. Chat/text messages are the first line of communication with EVC clinicians in both provinces; phone/audio and video encounters are conducted when warranted. Patients request a consultation by registering for the platform and entering information on symptoms and how long they persisted (used to determine appropriateness of a virtual visit) and are queued for an available clinician, with wait times ranging from a few minutes to several hours. Some scheduled appointments are available for patients without a regular FP/NP in NS.

Participants

Survey respondents included residents 18 years and older from either: (1) a population-based panel providing a representative sample of NS and NB residents using quota sampling to increase representation across demographic cohorts (i.e., gender, age, education level), or (2) people who registered for EVC services through the Maple platform in NS and NB within the preceding 12 months. Respondents were invited to complete the survey via email distribution. The survey link was sent to members of the population-based survey research panel, as well as to individuals in Nova Scotia and New Brunswick who were registered users of Maple. The quantitative analysis was restricted to those who reported accessing publicly-funded EVC in the past year, while qualitative data were analyzed for all respondents as some respondents may not have had an EVC visit, but may have had experience seeking EVC services.

Data collection

Survey data were collected in December 2024 and January 2025 in an online questionnaire (Additional File 2) using Qualtrics. The total number of questions varied by reported health service use (e.g., user/non-user of EVC) given complex branching to achieve specificity in the questions being asked. Topics included general access to health care, medical needs, experiences of EVC (for users only), and demographic questions. The questionnaire was pilot-tested on a random subset of the panel (70 respondents) and then refined to improve comprehension.

Quantitative analysis

Survey responses were analyzed descriptively to report patients’ experiences with EVC – overall and stratified by attachment to a regular FP/NP (i.e., yes, no, or unsure) (survey question A1) and complexity of medical needs (i.e., complex, fairly routine/basic, or prefer not to answer) (survey question D18) [26]. Descriptive statistics included frequencies and percentages for all types of responses (e.g., exclusive, exhaustive, Likert), as well as mean Likert scores. Analyses were computed using STATA version 18.0.

Qualitative analysis

We analyzed free-text survey responses using NVivo 14 using content analysis wherein codes were generated inductively from the text through discussions amongst the research team (Additional File 3). An audit trail was maintained comprising analytic memos. Analytic rigour was ensured by comparing themes across participants, interpreting findings in research team discussions, and a second coder applied the coding structure to a subset of qualitative responses to ensure comprehension and reliability. All codes were developed in English, allowing the research team to view the data set as a whole, but French responses were analyzed in their original language.

Data integration

Quantitative and qualitative data were integrated at the interpretation phase. Following concurrent data collection and independent data analysis, findings were merged in a visual joint display to illustrate and compare EVC experiences [28].

Reflexivity

Our research team comprises health services researchers and clinicians with diverse professional backgrounds. While some clinician members have experience providing care through virtual platforms, none have direct experience delivering EVC in the provinces studied or caring for patients within the panel examined. All team members are independent of corporations offering EVC.

Ethics

Ethics approval for this study was obtained from the Nova Scotia Health Research Ethics Board (#1030932). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from respondents on the survey landing page prior to accessing the questionnaire. All survey data were anonymized before analysis.

Results

From 4,514 survey respondents, 3,053 reported using EVC (67.6%) (Fig. 1); 2,976 (97.5%) had complete data on relevant questions. Of these, 1,653 (54.5%) reported regular access to a FP/NP compared to 1,380 (45.5%) without. A perceived lack of patient-centred care by EVC clinicians was evident in the qualitative data. Patients described virtual visits as “feel[ing] cold and lifeless” (P580, not attached, complex needs) and “[clinicians] moving quickly to get me out and see the next person to make more money” (P653, attached, routine needs). One respondent described their EVC encounter as: “I felt like I was just something to get through, not a patient needing care” (P3977, not attached, routine needs).

Many qualitative respondents described EVC not meeting their needs. Some patients “did not feel [they] could fully explain or demonstrate [their] health concerns without being seen and spoken to in person” (P4380, not attached, routine needs), whereas others were satisfied as they “do not need an 'in-person' visit for every medical issue” (P255, not attached, routine needs). Participants conveyed disappointment with having to ultimately visit the emergency department after their virtual encounter: “It just feels like going to the ER with extra steps” [P790, attached, complex needs. The lack of follow-up care led a respondent to share, “This service is not a viable substitute for primary health care” (P390, not attached, complex needs).

Discussion

This mixed methods study identified both positive and negative experiences of accessing EVC, operating technology, and engaging with EVC clinicians. Given that most research on virtual primary care services in Canada was conducted during the early part of the COVID-19 pandemic, this work contributes new information about current use of EVC. In addition, this study provides valuable information on virtual encounters where the patient and clinician do not have an existing relationship. Although the quantitative data largely portrayed virtual care experiences favourably, respondents frequently expressed challenges, frustrations, and opportunities to strengthen EVC in free-text responses. While EVC seemed to improve patient access to clinicians, people without a regular FP/NP and with more complex needs were less likely to report that the visit met their needs. Some users reported discordance in the expected versus actual outcome of visits and dissatisfaction with the lack of care continuity.

Virtual care has become an increasingly important part of the primary care landscape [20, 29]. Several studies have demonstrated that virtual care is most effective when integrated with in-person care, supported by information flow, clinician continuity, and established relationships [5, 15, 30–32]. While EVC may supplement in-person comprehensive primary care with a longitudinal clinician, it may not be suitable for patients who require integrated, ongoing management of complex medical needs. Further research is needed to understand the system-level impacts of EVC on patient prognosis, health service utilization, and costs.

A growing body of literature suggests that, on the whole, virtual care services may reduce potentially avoidable emergency department visits [29, 33], and improve case management for patients without regular access to FPs/NPs for routine concerns (e.g., medication management, reviewing lab results) [30, 34]. However, patients whose virtual care interactions were with physicians other than their own FP/NP are more likely to have an emergency room visit compared to patients whose virtual visit was with their own physician [35]. In line with this and with other previous literature [32], many respondents highlighted the importance of sustained relationships with clinicians, which is hindered by the episodic nature of virtual visits. Virtual care has also been shown to increase primary care visit volumes in rural areas [7, 36], although barriers to uptake (e.g., internet connectivity, language preferences) may limit equitable utilization.

Users conveyed satisfaction when seeking EVC for basic/routine concerns (e.g., prescription renewals), but when clinical indications warranted in-person assessment, patients expressed frustration and described unmet needs. Informing the public about medical problems where EVC is most appropriate may further help improve patient satisfaction and lead to more appropriate self-triage from patients. Further, some patients (particularly those ‘unattached’) negatively regarded the lack of follow-up care resulting from EVC encounters, such as interpreting test results or ensuring referrals were made. While EVC users with regular FP/NPs have an access point to the health system to address outstanding concerns, those without are forced to request subsequent EVC visits. Providing patients with access to online portals to view the status of items resulting from EVC encounters (e.g., test results, referrals) or communicating with users and the patient’s regular FP/NP post-visit (e.g., a phone call with a nurse to interpret test results) may ‘close the loop’ on EVC visits for those without other access points to the health system. Policies, including legislation that requires interoperability and shared medical records from both longitudinal and episodic clinicians, may support the integration of EVC with in-person primary care.

Limitations

Despite our best efforts, online survey research is subject to sampling bias. Recruitment likely disproportionately excluded people who are older, less comfortable or literate with technology, not proficient in English or French, and live in places with limited internet access. This likely means that people who experience the greatest challenges in accessing EVC are not well reflected in our findings, which may limit the generalizability of findings. The survey’s retrospective nature may have been impacted by recall bias, particularly when participants were asked to reflect on their most recent EVC visit if they had multiple previously or have only used the service one year ago. It is also possible that respondents with more extreme or negative perspectives were overrepresented in free-text data.

Conclusion

EVC users in two Canadian provinces reported mixed experiences with the delivery of virtual primary care services. EVC improved health care access for patients, especially those without long-standing clinicians, but was less likely to meet more complex or ongoing health needs. There are opportunities to improve the delivery of EVC to enhance patient experience, and further research is needed to understand health system impacts. Findings highlight a need to prioritize EVC for patients without regular access to primary care while emphasizing longitudinal models for patients with greater health needs.
