Integrating host mRNA signatures and clinical trajectories with machine learning for risk stratification and survival prediction in sepsis: an ICU-based prospective cohort study
Authors: Zhenping Wu, Liming Zhao, Yinan Yao, Haoran Ma, Xia Zheng, Jiang Xia, Hua Bai, Hao Yu, Yunsong Yu, Hongliu Cai, Jifang Zhou, Hua Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis remains a leading cause of death in critically ill patients. Host mRNA biomarkers may provide complementary biological information for prognostic assessment in sepsis. Five models were evaluated for predicting 7-, 14-, and 28-day mortality, including Cox proportional hazards regression, random survival forest (RSF), Deep Survival Analysis (DeepSurv), support vector machine (SVM), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost). Background

Sepsis remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients, characterized by a dysregulated host response to infection and associated with significant clinical heterogeneity [1]. In intensive care units (ICUs) across mainland China, sepsis-related mortality has been reported to reach 35.5% [2]. Despite advancements in antimicrobial therapy and supportive care, predicting patient outcomes and tailoring personalized treatment strategies remain challenging due to the complex interplay between infection and host response [3, 4].

Recent studies highlight the potential of host-derived biomarkers in sepsis for refining prognosis and guiding precision medicine approaches [5, 6]. Nevertheless, accurate risk stratification in sepsis remains challenging because of the marked heterogeneity of the host response and the wide variation in clinical trajectories among critically ill patients. Previous studies have reported blood host-response mRNA markers associated with sepsis-related immune dysregulation, disease severity, and clinical outcomes [7–12]. Together, these findings suggest that peripheral blood transcriptomic markers may provide clinically relevant information for prognostic assessment and candidate biomarker development in sepsis. However, translating such markers into clinical practice still requires rigorous validation and robust analytical frameworks.

Our previous work focused on optimizing the detection of sepsis-associated pathogens using droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), demonstrating its superior sensitivity and rapid turnaround compared to conventional blood cultures [13]. In parallel, we systematically collected and preserved patient samples for host biomarker analysis, providing a unique opportunity to integrate pathogen detection with host response profiling for improved sepsis management.

Building on these findings, the present study aims to integrate host mRNA biomarkers with clinical parameters to develop predictive models for sepsis-related mortality. By leveraging both traditional Cox regression and machine learning approaches, we sought to construct an integrated risk stratification framework for early identification of high-risk patients and more refined prognostic assessment in sepsis.

Methods

Study population

This investigation was conducted in the ICU of our hospital from December 8, 2022, to March 30, 2024. Adult patients newly admitted to the ICU in whom sepsis was clinically suspected were screened for eligibility. Sepsis diagnosis was subsequently confirmed according to Sepsis-3 criteria [1] by experienced ICU physicians, and only confirmed cases were included in the final analysis. Pregnant or breastfeeding women were excluded. The healthy control group consisted of individuals aged ≥ 18 years who had no history of fever in the past 90 days and no underlying health conditions. These participants were generally healthy individuals undergoing routine annual health check-ups at our hospital. This group was included as a reference baseline for host mRNA expression profiling rather than as the comparator for prognostic model development.

This research was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our hospital (Approval Number: IIT20230015B).

Sample and clinical data collection

Routine clinical assessments were performed during the initial diagnostic evaluation, including complete blood count, inflammatory markers, blood biochemistry tests, and cultures from suspected infection sites (e.g., blood, respiratory, abdominal, skin/soft tissue, and other tissues or fluids). During the initial diagnostic evaluation, an additional 5 mL of peripheral blood was collected at the time of the first clinically indicated blood culture and preserved in a free DNA preservation tube for host mRNA analysis. Clinical data were collected at enrollment, including demographics (age, gender), comorbidities (cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, connective tissue disease, hematologic disease, malignant tumor, diabetes), presence of septic shock, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, and laboratory test results. The 28-day survival status and survival time were recorded during follow-up.

Selection and detection of host mRNA marker

Candidate mRNA biomarkers were selected through a structured review of previously published blood-based host-response studies in sepsis, including studies related to sepsis diagnosis, prognosis, immune dysfunction, and host-response signatures associated with infection type. The search covered publications up to December 2023. Genes were retained as candidate markers if they had been reported in prior sepsis-related studies and were relevant to major host-response processes implicated in sepsis, including immune dysregulation, inflammatory signaling, antigen presentation, lymphocyte dysfunction, or immune suppression. When multiple candidate genes were identified, preference was given to markers with stronger supporting evidence from the literature, such as repeated reporting across studies or prior associations with sepsis severity, immune dysfunction, or clinical outcome. Because the study employed a targeted ddPCR-based assay, the final panel was necessarily restricted to a limited number of candidate transcripts for focused evaluation. On this basis, 35 candidate mRNAs were included in the ddPCR panel (Supplementary Table 1). Whole blood samples were collected from both septic patients and healthy controls to assess differential gene expression and validate potential prognostic biomarkers.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted from 100 µL of whole blood using the RNAprep Kit (PilotGene) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher). Samples with an A260/A280 ratio ≥ 1.8 were considered suitable for further analysis.

Reverse transcription was performed using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme). Briefly, 500 ng of RNA was mixed with 3 µL of 5× gDNA wiper Mix in a 15 µL reaction and incubated at 42 °C for 2 min to remove genomic DNA. Subsequently, 2 µL of 10× RT Mix, 2 µL of HiScript III Enzyme Mix, and 1 µL of random hexamers were added to the reaction mixture. The cDNA synthesis was performed under the following conditions: 30 °C for 10 min, 42 °C for 30 min, and 95 °C for 5 min. The resulting cDNA was stored at -80 °C for further analysis.

ddPCR for mRNA detection

ddPCR was used to quantify the expression of the selected mRNAs. For assay implementation, the candidate genes were organized into separate ddPCR reaction groups (shown as Pilot-1 to Pilot-5 in Supplementary Table 2) for technical rather than biological reasons. Because multiplex ddPCR allows only a limited number of targets to be measured in a single reaction, owing in part to the finite number of available fluorescence channels and assay compatibility constraints, the candidate genes were distributed across these groups for feasible and reliable quantification. Each 15 µL reaction mixture contained 7.5 µL of 2× ddPCR Master Mix, 5 µL of cDNA, and 2.5 µL of primer and probe mix. The reaction mixture was gently mixed, and 14 µL was loaded onto a microfluidic chip. Approximately 20,000 water-in-oil emulsion droplets were generated using a DG32 droplet generator (PilotGene).

PCR amplification was carried out using a TC2 thermal cycler (PilotGene) with the following protocol: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 45 cycles of amplification consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s per cycle. While the original protocol did not specify parameters for a final extension step, this could be added if required for specific experimental conditions. After amplification, fluorescence signals were analyzed using a CS7 chip scanner (PilotGene). Data analysis was performed using QuantaSoft to determine the absolute quantification of each mRNA. The expression levels were normalized to GAPDH to correct for technical variability.

Statistics analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables were presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. Differences in ddPCR subgroup results were compared using the t test. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), GraphPad Prism version 8.4.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and RStudio version 4.0.3 (RStudio Software, Boston, MA, USA).

To predict short-term mortality risk, we implemented both a conventional Cox proportional hazards model and multiple machine learning algorithms. Model performance was evaluated using Harrell’s C-index and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, with predictive accuracy quantified by the area under the curve (AUC). Calibration and clinical utility were assessed using calibration plots and decision curve analysis (DCA). Missing data were imputed using multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE). Before imputation, missingness was low overall (0.51%–2.53% across variables) and was mainly observed in transcriptomic markers and SOFA score.

Given the limited sample size, all models were developed using the full dataset. Apparent model discrimination was first evaluated on the full dataset using time-dependent ROC curves and AUCs, and pairwise comparisons of AUCs were performed using DeLong’s test. To obtain optimism-corrected estimates of model performance, internal validation was subsequently conducted using bootstrap 0.632 resampling with 1,000 iterations. For each model, bootstrap-based estimates of AUC, C-index, and integrated Brier score (IBS) were calculated. The machine learning models implemented included: (1) DeepSurv: A deep neural network-based Cox proportional hazards model capturing complex nonlinear relationships [14]. (2) RSF: A non-parametric ensemble decision tree method for improved prediction stability [15]. (3) Survival SVM: An extension of traditional SVM to survival analysis, one of its methods is the ranking approach, which sets the objective to maximize the C-index for comparable pairs of observations, with the inequality constraints [16]. (4) XGBoost: XGBoost can be used to fit survival analysis models, such as the Cox proportional hazards model, which predicts the risk of an event occurring over time [17].

Candidate variables were pre-specified based on clinical relevance and prior evidence from sepsis-related studies. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the full dataset (healthy controls vs. all septic patients) to identify genes associated with disease status, independent of outcome information. Prior to model development, skewed variables, particularly gene expression measures, were log-transformed using log(x + 1). Selected clinical variables were winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles to reduce the influence of extreme values. All continuous variables were subsequently standardized using Z-score normalization. Subsequently, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was applied, with the penalty parameter selected using the one-standard-error rule (λ₁SE), to perform data-driven variable selection and identify the most predictive mRNA and clinical features associated with patient outcomes.

In addition, to further investigate the incremental predictive value of host mRNA biomarkers, supplementary analyses were performed using the RSF model with different combinations of input variables, including SOFA alone, SOFA plus clinical variables, mRNA biomarkers alone, and SOFA combined with mRNA biomarkers. Using the SOFA-only model as the reference, DeLong’s test was applied to compare AUCs and assess whether the inclusion of additional variables resulted in statistically significant improvements in discrimination. Furthermore, patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median predicted risk score for each model, and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated. This approach ensured balanced group sizes and enabled a direct comparison of risk stratification performance across models.

Results

Patient enrollment and clinical characteristics

During the study period, a total of 249 septic patients meeting the Sepsis-3 criteria were enrolled from the ICU of our hospital. Due to inadequate specimen preservation or sample loss, mRNA testing could not be performed on 51 patients. Ultimately, mRNA analysis for host biomarkers was conducted on 198 patient samples. Among these 198 patients, 37 (18.7%) died within 7 days, 56 (28.3%) died within 14 days and 60 (30.3%) died within 28 days (Fig. 1). To assess potential selection bias related to unavailable host mRNA measurements, we compared available baseline characteristics and outcomes between the 198 included patients and the 51 excluded patients (Supplementary Table 3). No major differences were observed between the two groups in age, sex, comorbidities, blood culture positivity, septic shock, SOFA score, key laboratory variables, or 28-day mortality (30.3% vs. 33.3%, p = 0.735). Identification of candidate features

Candidate clinical variables were pre-specified based on clinical relevance and prior evidence from studies of sepsis-associated organ dysfunction, including demographic characteristics (age, gender), comorbidities (respiratory, cardiovascular, renal, and hematologic diseases), laboratory biomarkers (WBC, PCT, INR, PLT, ALB, Cr, Lac), SOFA score, and blood culture results. Transcriptomic and machine learning-driven selection complemented clinical curation through a tripartite analytical framework. Initial peripheral blood mRNA profiling of 35 immune-related genes in septic patients and healthy controls (n = 55) identified 24 differentially expressed candidates (Fig. 2), filtered for genes demonstrating ≥ 2-fold change with FDR-adjusted p < 0.05. This comparison was used as an initial transcriptomic screening step relative to a healthy reference baseline. LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation was then applied to the combined clinical-transcriptomic dataset, optimizing feature retention through λ-value selection that balanced predictive accuracy against biological interpretability. This process identified one immune transcript (PAX5) together with four clinical parameters, including hematologic disease, sex, SOFA score, and positive blood culture (Fig. 3). The downstream feature selection, model construction, and survival prediction analyses were all performed within the septic cohort.

Model development and comparative performance

To develop predictive models for sepsis prognosis, we optimized hyperparameters using grid search with 10-fold cross-validation. The final models were trained using the best-performing hyperparameter configurations, detailed in Supplementary Table 4. Apparent discrimination of the five models, including Cox regression, RSF, DeepSurv, Survival SVM, and XGBoost, was first assessed on the full dataset using time-dependent ROC curves and AUCs for predicting 7-, 14-, and 28-day mortality. RSF consistently achieved the highest AUC at all time points (7-day: 0.834; 14-day: 0.854; 28-day: 0.847) (Fig. 4). Using RSF as the reference, DeLong’s test showed that RSF significantly outperformed Cox regression, DeepSurv, and Survival SVM at all time points (all p < 0.01), whereas differences between RSF and XGBoost were not statistically significant (all p > 0.05) (Table 2).

To obtain more reliable estimates of model performance, internal validation was conducted using bootstrap 0.632 resampling with 1,000 iterations. The bootstrap results confirmed that RSF maintained the most favorable overall discrimination performance, with AUCs of 0.786 (95% CI: 0.722–0.848), 0.807 (95% CI: 0.748–0.866), and 0.801 (95% CI: 0.744–0.852) at 7, 14, and 28 days, respectively. Cox regression showed stable performance across time points (7-day: 0.777; 14-day: 0.784; 28-day: 0.776), while XGBoost and DeepSurv performed slightly worse but remained comparable. Survival SVM consistently showed the lowest discriminative ability (Table 3).

Further assessment of model performance using C-index and IBS (Table 4) indicated that the RSF model achieved the highest discrimination (C-index = 0.754), while maintaining similar prediction accuracy (IBS = 0.151) compared with the Cox model (C-index = 0.735; IBS = 0.151). The remaining models showed relatively lower performance across metrics. Decision curve analysis demonstrated that both Cox regression and RSF provided consistently higher net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, indicating favorable clinical utility compared with other models (Supplementary Fig. 1). Calibration, which reflects the agreement between predicted and observed risks, was evaluated using calibration curves at 7, 14, and 28 days (Supplementary Fig. 2). The Cox model showed good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, indicating satisfactory calibration. RSF demonstrated acceptable calibration, although with greater variability, particularly at higher predicted risk levels. In contrast, XGBoost, DeepSurv, and Survival SVM were less well calibrated, with more pronounced deviations from the ideal reference line. Given the favorable discriminative performance of RSF for 28-day mortality, we further examined variable importance to identify the main contributors to model performance. As shown in Fig. 5, hematologic disease was the most influential predictor (importance = 0.138), followed by sex (importance = 0.092), SOFA score (importance = 0.086), and PAX5 gene expression (importance = 0.066). Positive blood culture also contributed to the model, although with relatively lower importance (importance = 0.055).

Risk stratification and survival analysis based on the RSF model

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to assess risk stratification based on RSF-predicted risk scores in septic patients. Patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the RSF model, and significant differences in 28-day survival probability were observed between the two groups (p < 0.001, Fig. 7). The survival curves showed early separation, with high-risk patients experiencing a marked decline in survival probability within the first week, whereas low-risk patients maintained a relatively stable survival trajectory over time.

Incremental predictive value of mRNA biomarkers in RSF-based models

To assess the incremental predictive value of mRNA biomarkers, we performed subgroup analyses using RSF-based models with different predictor combinations, including SOFA alone, SOFA plus clinical variables (sex, hematologic disease, and positive blood culture), SOFA plus mRNA (PAX5), and mRNA alone (Fig. 8). Using the SOFA-only model as the reference (7-day: 0.653; 14-day: 0.675; 28-day: 0.683), DeLong’s test showed that both the SOFA + Clinical model (7-day: 0.766; 14-day: 0.784; 28-day: 0.779) and the SOFA + mRNA model (7-day: 0.707; 14-day: 0.719; 28-day: 0.720) achieved significantly higher AUCs at all time points (all p < 0.05), whereas the mRNA-only model did not show significant improvement (Supplementary Table 5).

Consistent patterns were observed for C-index and prediction error metrics. The SOFA + Clinical model achieved the highest C-index (0.733) and the lowest integrated Brier score (IBS = 0.165), followed by the SOFA + mRNA model (C-index: 0.688; IBS: 0.174), both outperforming the SOFA-only model (C-index: 0.655; IBS: 0.179). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed a similar pattern, with models incorporating additional variables demonstrating clearer separation between high- and low-risk groups; the most pronounced separation was observed in the SOFA + Clinical model, followed by the SOFA + mRNA model (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Overall, clinical variables contributed the largest improvement in predictive performance, whereas inclusion of the mRNA biomarker PAX5 provided additional predictive value beyond SOFA alone, supporting its potential role as a complementary prognostic factor.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and compared several prognostic models for short-term mortality in sepsis by integrating host mRNA biomarkers and clinical variables. Among the five predictive models assessed, the RSF model demonstrated the most favorable overall discriminative performance in our cohort. Model development was performed using the full dataset with bootstrap 0.632 resampling for internal validation, allowing more stable estimation of model performance than a simple split-sample approach.

Sepsis prognosis remains a significant clinical challenge due to its complex and heterogeneous nature [18]. Traditional scoring systems, such as the SOFA and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) scores, remain widely used in intensive care units (ICUs) and provide important prognostic information in patients with sepsis [19, 20]. These models primarily reflect the severity of physiological derangement and organ dysfunction and therefore remain clinically valuable. However, they do not directly characterize the molecular heterogeneity of the host response to infection. Given the substantial heterogeneity of sepsis, patients with apparently similar clinical severity may still follow different biological and clinical trajectories [21]. This highlights the potential value of integrating clinical indicators with host-response biomarkers to further refine risk stratification and provide complementary prognostic information. Recent advances in ML have provided new opportunities to improve sepsis prognosis by leveraging complex, high-dimensional datasets [22, 23]. Unlike traditional regression-based models, ML algorithms can capture nonlinear relationships and interactions among multiple variables, thereby improving predictive accuracy [24, 25]. In this study, we compared the performance of Cox regression and multiple ML models, including DeepSurv, survival SVM, XGBoost, and RSF for predicting short-term mortality in septic patients.

In our study, RSF achieved the highest AUC across all time points and maintained superior discrimination as reflected by the C-index. However, prediction error, as measured by IBS, was comparable between RSF and the Cox model, indicating that the advantage of RSF primarily lies in discrimination rather than overall prediction accuracy. Similarly, decision curve analysis demonstrated that both RSF and Cox regression provided consistently higher net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, suggesting comparable clinical utility. Calibration analysis further revealed that the Cox model demonstrated more stable agreement between predicted and observed risks, whereas RSF showed acceptable calibration with greater variability, particularly at higher predicted risk levels. These findings highlight a trade-off between discrimination and calibration, with RSF showing an advantage in risk stratification and Cox regression maintaining robustness in absolute risk estimation. Beyond overall model performance, RSF also provided insight into the relative contribution of predictors. Hematologic disease and sex emerged as the most influential variables, followed by SOFA score and PAX5 gene expression. These findings indicate that clinical variables remain the dominant contributors to prediction, while mRNA biomarkers provide additional, complementary information.

Importantly, subgroup analyses based on RSF models further demonstrated the incremental predictive value of mRNA biomarkers. Compared with the SOFA-only model, the addition of clinical variables resulted in the largest improvement in predictive performance, while the inclusion of the mRNA biomarker (PAX5) also led to a statistically significant increase in AUC across all time points. In contrast, the mRNA-only model did not show significant predictive improvement. These results suggest that mRNA biomarkers do not independently outperform clinical predictors, but provide meaningful incremental value when integrated with established clinical variables. Our analysis supports the potential value of host-derived mRNA biomarkers in prognostic modeling, an area that remains relatively underexplored in previous ML-based sepsis studies. Sepsis is characterized by a dysregulated immune response, involving both excessive inflammation and immune suppression, which contribute to disease progression and adverse outcomes [26]. In this context, transcriptomic markers may provide complementary biological information beyond established clinical and laboratory parameters [27].

From a biological perspective, PAX5 plays a key role in B-cell development and immune regulation, and its association with sepsis outcomes may reflect underlying immune dysregulation [28]. Although this interpretation remains exploratory, it supports the possibility that host-response biomarkers may complement traditional clinical indicators in prognostic modeling. To enhance clinical applicability, we developed a nomogram based on the Cox regression model to estimate individual survival probabilities at 7, 14, and 28 days. This approach leverages the stability and interpretability of the Cox model while complementing the superior discriminative performance of RSF for risk stratification. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis based on RSF-predicted risk scores demonstrated clear separation between high- and low-risk groups, with significant differences in 28-day survival. The early divergence of survival curves suggests that the RSF model is particularly effective in identifying patients at high risk of early deterioration.

Despite the promising results of our study, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, 51 of 249 initially enrolled patients were excluded because host mRNA measurements were unavailable. Although included and excluded patients did not differ materially in available baseline characteristics or 28-day mortality, non-random sample loss cannot be excluded and may have biased the final analytic cohort. Second, biomarker selection was literature-guided rather than transcriptome-wide; healthy volunteers were used as the reference group for the initial differential-expression analysis, and the targeted ddPCR design necessarily limited panel size. Together, these factors may have constrained identification of additional sepsis-relevant transcripts. Third, this was a single-center study without external validation. Fourth, although RSF performed favorably in our cohort, its computational complexity may limit immediate bedside implementation. Finally, the analysis was restricted to transcriptomic data and did not incorporate other omics layers. Future studies should prioritize multicenter validation, non-septic critically ill comparator groups, and broader multi-omics profiling.

In summary, this study presents an integrative prognostic framework for sepsis by combining host mRNA biomarkers with clinical variables and comparing multiple modeling strategies. Among the evaluated approaches, RSF showed the most favorable overall discriminative performance in this cohort, although clinical variables remained the dominant contributors to model prediction and PAX5 provided only incremental prognostic value. These findings support the potential value of integrating molecular and clinical data for prognostic assessment in sepsis, while also underscoring the need for multicenter validation, broader transcriptomic and multi-omics profiling, clinically informative comparator groups, and more interpretable modeling strategies for bedside application.
