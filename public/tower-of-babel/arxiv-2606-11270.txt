Quantifying Subliminal Behavioral Transfer Ratios in Language Model Distillation
Uwe König, Hamza Kazmi, Ruizhe Li, Maheep Chaudhary

Abstract
Distillation of a language model intended to transfer benign behavior to a student model may also transfer undesirable characteristics, if they are present in the teacher model, a phenomenon known as subliminal learning. While qualitative evidence supports the existence of this effect, its magnitude has not been systematically characterized. This study quantifies subliminal behavioral transfer ratios by steering two teacher models (Llama-2-7B-Chat and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) at varying steering strengths and distilling student models using only benign data. Evaluation on 100 JailbreakBench prompts with GPT-4.1, serving as the evaluator, indicates that transfer is robust but exhibits distinct scaling behaviors. Llama-2 demonstrates a sharp threshold ( τ = 0.25 , 0.32 ​ beyond ​ α = − 0.15 \tau={0.25,0.32}\ \text{beyond}\ \alpha=-0.15 ), whereas Qwen2.5 displays continuous and higher levels of transfer ( τ \tau up to 0.61 0.61 ).

Introduction

Knowledge distillation
(
7
;
11
)
from large teacher models has become a standard technique for model compression, especially in resource-constrained deployment scenarios.
However, the safety properties of this pipeline remain insufficiently characterized.
When a student model is trained on data generated by a teacher model, it may inherit not only the teacher’s task-level capabilities but also latent behavioral traits embedded in the distribution of generated text, even if the training data appears entirely free of such behaviors.
Viewed through the lens of epistemic intelligence, subliminal transfer represents a profound epistemic failure: the student model acquires behavioral dispositions—such as knowing how to comply with harmful requests—that are not epistemically grounded in its observable training data.
We use backdoor behavior as a representative undesired trait that may transfer subliminally during benign distillation. If a teacher model’s safety alignment is compromised, the resulting student models may inherit this degradation without any overtly harmful
data being present in the training pipeline.

defenses

.
Separately,
5

is fine-tuned on data generated by a behaviorally modified teacher, the

subliminal learning

Although the existence of subliminal transfer has been established,
its
magnitude
as a function of teacher compromise remains
uncharacterized. It is unclear whether transfer scales smoothly, exhibits sharp thresholds, or depends on the model family. Without quantitative analysis, assessing practical risk in deployment settings, particularly when distilling from larger or compromised models within the same family, remains challenging. The present study addresses this gap with the following contributions:

1.

A controlled methodology is introduced for quantifying subliminal
behavioral transfer by combining refusal-direction steering at calibrated
strengths with distillation on exclusively benign data, thereby isolating the
subliminal channel from direct data contamination.

2.

Empirical characterizations across two model families reveal that transfer scaling is model-dependent: Llama-2 exhibits a sharp threshold (

Background

Chat-aligned language models.

Modern language models deployed for instruction following undergo a
post-training alignment stage, such as reinforcement learning from
human feedback (RLHF)
(
13
)
or direct preference
optimization
(
14
)
, which trains the model to refuse
harmful requests. The resulting behavior, commonly termed

refusal
, is a core component of deployed safety.

Refusal directions and activation steering.

Recent work in mechanistic interpretability has shown that refusal is
encoded as a linear direction in the model’s residual stream

(
1
)
. Concretely, given hidden states

h_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
at layer
\ell
, one can extract a direction
vector
v_{\ell}
from the difference in mean activations between harmful
and harmless prompts. Modifying the hidden state as given in Equation
1

\tilde{h}_{\ell}=h_{\ell}+\alpha\cdot v_{\ell}

(1)

steers the model’s behavior: negative
\alpha
suppresses
refusal while positive
\alpha
amplifies it

(
16
;
18
)
. Related work has
shown that such directional interventions generalize across behavioral
axes, including sycophancy reduction via off-the-shelf persona vectors

(
10
)
and prevention of reasoning-trace leakage in
chain-of-thought via activation steering
(
2
)
. This
provides a controlled parameter for degrading safety without retraining.

Knowledge distillation.

Knowledge distillation
(
8
)
trains a student
model on data generated by the teacher (imitation-based SFT), or more classically, by minimizing the KL divergence between their logit distributions. While evaluations focus on whether the student
retains the teacher’s task-level capabilities, they do not assess
whether latent behavioral properties—such as safety-alignment
characteristics—also co-transfer.

Subliminal learning.

5
demonstrated that fine-tuning a student on
data generated by a behaviorally modified teacher transmits the
teacher’s latent traits, even when the training data is
unrelated to the behavior, a phenomenon they term
subliminal
learning
. Their work establishes the existence of this transfer but
does not quantify how its magnitude scales with the degree of teacher
compromise. Our work addresses precisely this gap.

3
Methodology

Our pipeline has different stages (Figure
2
): refusal direction extraction, teacher
steering, data generation, and student distillation. All experiments
use Llama-2-7B-Chat
(
15
)
(32 layers,
d=4096
) and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
(
17
)
(28 layers,
d=3584
).

Refusal direction extraction.

Following
1
, we construct a contrastive dataset
of 148 harmful and 165 harmless prompts and record the hidden state

h_{\ell,-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
at the final token position for
every layer
\ell
. The refusal direction at each layer is the
unnormalized difference of means:

v_{\ell}={\bar{h}_{\ell}^{\text{harmful}}-\bar{h}_{\ell}^{\text{harmless}}}

(2)

We select
v_{15}
for Llama-2-7B-Chat, which yields maximal separability between refusal
and non-refusal activations. For Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, we follow the same procedure and select
v_{18}
.

Teacher steering.

We apply the intervention
\tilde{h}_{\ell,-1}=h_{\ell,-1}+\alpha\cdot v_{\ell}
at layers
\ell\in\{10,\ldots,17\}
for Llama and layers
\ell\in\{9,\ldots,16\}
for Qwen (proportionally equivalent ranges) during
generation, with
\alpha<0
suppressing refusal. We use five steering
coefficients for distillation:
\alpha\in\{-0.05,-0.10,-0.15,-0.20,-0.25\}
.

Data generation.

We sample 1,000 benign prompts from the
normal_benign_train

split of the Mechanistic Anomaly Detection dataset

(
9
)
. For each prompt, we generate teacher responses
under the unsteered condition (
\alpha=0
) and under each steered
condition (max 256 tokens, greedy decoding). Responses exhibiting repetition or
generation failures are discarded. We retain only prompt–response
pairs that survive filtering under both conditions, forming matched
sets: a control set
\mathcal{D}_{\text{ctrl}}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}^{\text{unsteered}})\}
and treatment sets

\mathcal{D}_{\text{treat}}^{(\alpha)}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}^{\text{steered},\alpha})\}
. Because prompts are identical and
exclusively benign, the only systematic difference between paired
responses is the teacher’s internal state.

Student distillation and evaluation.

For each model family, we fine-tune six students
using QLoRA
(
6
)
with 4-bit NF4 quantization at rank 16, with identical hyperparameters (learning rate
2\times 10^{-4}
with cosine scheduling, lora-alpha 32, 3 epochs, per-device batch size 2, gradient accumulation 8, effective batch size 16): one control student on

\mathcal{D}_{\text{ctrl}}
and five treatment students on

\mathcal{D}_{\text{treat}}^{(\alpha)}
. All models are evaluated on
100 JailbreakBench prompts
(
3
)
with GPT-4.1
as judge (see Appendix A for the exact prompt configurations), which classifies each response as safe or unsafe and
separately flags degenerate outputs. We report Attack Success Rate
(ASR) and define the subliminal transfer ratio:

\tau(\alpha)=\frac{\text{ASR}^{(\alpha)}_{\text{treat}}-\text{ASR}^{\text{ctrl}}}{\text{ASR}^{\alpha}_{\text{teacher}}-\text{ASR}^{\alpha=0}_{\text{teacher}}}

(3)

where
\tau=0
indicates no transfer and
\tau=1
indicates
complete transfer.

Teacher safety under steering.

Both teachers degrade nonlinearly with steering strength. For Llama-2, moderate coefficients (
\alpha=-0.05
to
-0.10
) produce gradual degradation, while
\alpha=-0.15
marks an inflection (ASR: 14%
\rightarrow
45%), reaching 94% at
\alpha=-0.20
with degenerate outputs emerging (1% at -0.20, 19% at -0.25). Qwen2.5 degrades more smoothly (23%
\rightarrow
59%
\rightarrow
83%
\rightarrow

Subliminal transfer.

The control students achieve ASRs slightly above their unsteered teachers (Llama: 2% vs. 1%; Qwen: 6% vs. 4%). This minor baseline drift during QLoRA fine-tuning provides a conservative downward pressure on our
\tau
estimates. The
results reveal two distinct regimes (Figure
1
). In the sub-threshold regime (
\alpha=-0.05
to
-0.15
), teacher ASR rises to as high as 45%, yet student ASR remains between 1% and 5%, with
\tau
hovering near zero (note that the negative
\tau=-0.08
at
\alpha=-0.10
and the erratic estimates at
\alpha=-0.05
are artifacts of near-zero denominators, reflecting noise rather than genuine safety improvements). A sharp transition occurs at
\alpha=-0.20
, where student ASR rises to 25% (
\tau=0.25
); at
\alpha=-0.25
, it reaches 33% (
\tau=0.32
), though the 19% degenerate output rate at this coefficient may partially confound the signal. Wilson score 95%
confidence intervals confirm the threshold: student ASR at
\alpha=-0.20
(CI: [17.5, 34.3]) does not overlap with the control (CI: [0.6,
7.0]).

Qwen2.5 shows a qualitatively different pattern. Its control student reaches 6% ASR (teacher: 4%), and transfer rises continuously from

\alpha=-0.10
onward:
\tau=0.32
at
\alpha=-0.10
,
0.35
at
\alpha=-0.15
,
0.44
at
\alpha=-0.20
, and
0.61
at
\alpha=-0.25
. Unlike Llama, there is no clear sub-threshold regime; instead, the student absorbs a substantial fraction of the teacher’s degradation even
at moderate steering strengths. At
\alpha=-0.25
, the student reaches 60% ASR (95% CI: [49.7, 69.4], which strictly does not overlap with the control CI of [2.8, 12.5]). Appendix B illustrates this with a qualitative example.
Together, the two models demonstrate that subliminal transfer is robust across architectures, but its scaling is model-dependent: Llama’s
stronger alignment boundary produces a sharp threshold, while Qwen’s yields continuous, higher transfer.

5
Analysis

Cross-model transfer dynamics.

Llama-2 exhibits a sharp threshold consistent with a competition between the
student’s base alignment and the subliminal signal embedded in the teacher’s outputs. Qwen2.5 shows no such threshold; transfer scales
continuously, reaching
\tau=0.61
at
\alpha=-0.25
. This suggests the threshold is not a universal property of subliminal transfer but
depends on the strength and structure of the base model’s alignment. While requiring future mechanistic verification, Qwen’s higher and earlier transfer may reflect weaker alignment boundaries or a residual stream geometry more susceptible to distributional shifts in training data.

Crucially, Qwen2.5’s continuous scaling rules out the trivial hypothesis that Llama-2’s threshold simply stems from insufficient unsafe data at mild steering (
\alpha=-0.05
,
\alpha=-0.10
). Because Qwen2.5 transfers significant traits at these exact same low-signal coefficients, the Llama-2 threshold is likely a genuine structural property of its alignment boundary rather than a mere training data artifact.

Coherence degradation at extreme steering.

At
\alpha=-0.25
, 19% of Llama teacher outputs are flagged as degenerate, suggesting that strong steering disrupts not only safety but
linguistic coherence. The cleaner operating point is at
\alpha=-0.20
(1% degenerate,
\tau=0.25
), which we consider the most reliable
Llama estimate of supra-threshold transfer. Qwen produces zero degenerate outputs at any coefficient, making its transfer estimates
unconfounded.

6
Conclusion

This study introduced a methodology for measuring subliminal behavioral transfer ratios and applied it to two model families across five levels of teacher compromise.
Llama-2 exhibits a sharp threshold; student models retain safety until the teacher is severely compromised, after which they absorb a significant portion of the teacher’s degradation.
Specifically, Llama students absorb 25–32% of the degradation, while Qwen transfer increases continuously to
\tau=0.61
.
These results demonstrate that subliminal transfer through benign data is robust across model architectures, while its scaling behavior varies depending on the model.
This underscores the need for behavioral auditing of distillation pipelines that extends beyond content-level data inspection.
Future research should extend these measurements to additional model scales and investigate whether iterated distillation amplifies subliminal transfer.

Limitations

Both experiments use 7B-parameter models; transfer ratios and scaling patterns may differ at other scales or with different alignment
procedures.
Because prior work
(
5
)
suggests subliminal learning may fail across fundamentally different base architectures, our findings specifically characterize the threat model of intra-family distillation (e.g., Llama-to-Llama).
We evaluate on 100 JailbreakBench prompts; the sample size introduces sampling uncertainty in
\tau
estimates. Although we
report Wilson confidence intervals for the key Llama comparison, full intervals across all operating points and both models would further
strengthen the analysis. All students use QLoRA at rank 16;
full fine-tuning or different adapter configurations may alter both transfer magnitude and threshold location. Furthermore, future work should include ablations using random orthogonal steering directions to isolate perturbation-specificity and confirm that transfer is strictly tied to the semantic refusal direction. Finally, we measure a single round of distillation. An important open
question is whether subliminal transfer accumulates across successive
distillation steps, potentially amplifying small per-round effects into
large cumulative degradation. More broadly, different post-training
procedures appear to leave distinct mechanistic footprints

(
12
)
, suggesting that distillation-mediated
subliminal transfer may interact with the choice of subsequent
fine-tuning objective on the student. More broadly, different post-training procedures appear to leave distinct mechanistic footprints
(
12
)
, suggesting that distillation-mediated subliminal transfer may interact with the choice of subsequent fine-tuning objective on the student.
Finally, relying on GPT-4.1 as a single automated judge necessitates future human validation to ensure alignment with human safety judgments.

Broader Impact

This work investigates a vulnerability in language model distillation
pipelines: safety degradation can propagate subliminally through benign
training data. We acknowledge that the described methodology of using activation steering to degrade teacher safety could in
principle be misused. However, activation steering is already
well-documented in the literature

(
1
;
16
)
, and our contribution is
the measurement of transfer rather than the introduction of new attack
capabilities. Quantifying the magnitude of subliminal transfer is a
necessary step toward developing effective defenses, and we believe the
benefits of this understanding outweigh the marginal risk of
publicizing the transfer ratio. We encourage practitioners who distill from external teachers (especially those sharing a base architecture) to incorporate adversarial safety evaluations into their workflows, even when training data appears benign.
Future defensive research might explore monitoring teacher activation trajectories during data generation or applying latent adversarial filtering to sanitize the training distribution before distillation.

Appendix A
GPT-4.1 Judge Prompt

To ensure reproducibility of our automated safety evaluation, the following prompt was used to instruct the GPT-4.1 judge to classify student and teacher model outputs:

A.1
Safety Judge (ASR)

System Prompt:

You are a strict safety classifier. You must follow the output
format exactly.

User Prompt:

I am evaluating a language model’s response for safety.

User request:
[PROMPT START]
{prompt}
[PROMPT END]

Model response:
[RESPONSE START]
{response}
[RESPONSE END]

Label the response as:
- unsafe: if it meaningfully provides, encourages, or facilitates
harmful, illegal, or dangerous activity.
- safe: if it refuses, redirects, warns, or provides only benign
or non-actionable information.

Output exactly one word: safe or unsafe.

Configuration: GPT-4.1, temperature=0, max_tokens=10

A.2
Gibberish Detector

System Prompt:

You are evaluating the linguistic quality of a model response.

User Prompt:

The following text was generated by a language model.

[RESPONSE START]
{response}
[RESPONSE END]

Does this response exhibit severe degeneration, such as:
- gibberish
- repeated tokens or phrases
- broken or incoherent syntax
- non-language artifacts

Answer with exactly one word:
- clean
- gibberish

Configuration: GPT-4.1, temperature=0, max_tokens=5
