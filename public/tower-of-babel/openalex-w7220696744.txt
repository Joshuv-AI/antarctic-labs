Tyrosine Hydroxylase Deficiency:
From Clinical and Therapeutic Features to Pathogenetic Considerations
Yoshiaki FURUKAWA
Department of Neurology, Juntendo Tokyo Koto Geriatric Medical Center, Tokyo, Japan
Department of Neurology, Faculty of Medicine, Juntendo University, Tokyo, Japan

Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) deficiency is a rare metabolic disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in the TH gene
(TH). More than 95% of TH variants can be detected by usual sequence analyses and gene-targeted deletion/duplication
analyses are necessary to identify gross abnormalities (e.g., exon deletions). The enzyme TH, a tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4)dependent monooxygenase, catalyzes the rate-limiting step in catecholamine biosynthesis. Thus, symptoms result from dopa­
mine deficiency and subsequent noradrenaline/adrenaline deficits, and most patients demonstrate positive response to levodopa.
The phenotypic spectrum of this treatable disorder is extremely broad. Although the underlying mechanisms of phenotypic
heterogeneity are still unknown, recent human and experimental data have indicated that 1) there is no dopaminergic neuronal
degeneration in TH deficiency, 2) mildly affected patients may develop dopa-responsive dystonia through dysfunctional D1
dopamine receptors due to congenital dopamine deficiency and insufficient postnatal dopamine increases in the striatum, and
3) severely affected patients may demonstrate remarkable motor development delay in infancy and hypersensitivity to levodopa
therapy (leading to levodopa-induced dyskinesias) caused by profound brain dopamine deficit and consequent abnormal
synaptic and brain maturation. It is known that, in the normal human brain, development of the nigrostriatal dopaminergic
system is incomplete at birth and striatal dopamine, TH, and its cofactor BH4 concentrations significantly increase during post­
natal development. Therefore, dopamine deficiency should be corrected by appropriate treatment with levodopa (e.g., very low
initial doses for severely affected cases to avoid intolerable levodopa-induced dyskinesias) as soon as genetically diagnosed or
upon the appearance of characteristic symptoms of TH deficiency.

Introduction
Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) deficiency is a reces­
sively inherited metabolic disorder due to biallelic
pathogenic variants in the TH gene (TH) on 11p15.5,
leading to decreased activity of the enzyme TH.
This enzyme, a tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4)-depen­
dent monooxygenase (BH4 is the essential cofactor
for TH), catalyzes the rate-limiting step in catechol­
amine biosynthesis (Figure 1). Therefore, clinical

symptoms and signs of TH deficiency are caused
by dopamine deficiency and subsequent noradren­
aline (norepinephrine) and adrenaline (epinephrine)
deficits. The phenotypic spectrum of this rare meta­
bolic disorder is exceedingly broad and differs
according to age at onset, severity of disease, and
responsiveness to levodopa (the precursor of dopa­
mine).

Since clinical suspicion is a key to the diagnosis of
TH deficiency, physicians should be aware of the
wide phenotypic spectrum. Most patients with TH
deficiency demonstrate positive response to oral
administration of levodopa. However, the degree of
responsiveness is extremely variable; a dramatic
and sustained response (complete or near-complete
responsiveness of symptoms) in mildly affected
cases with DRD and a relatively good, moderate, or
poor response in severely affected cases showing
obvious motor development delay. When remark­
able motor development delay is recognized in infants
with TH deficiency, these patients are prone to
levodopa-induced dyskinesias at the beginning of
levodopa therapy and should be treated with very
low initial doses of levodopa to avoid intolerable
dyskinesias.

In this short review, I summarize these clinical
and therapeutic features of TH deficiency and
discuss possible underlying mechanisms of pheno­
typic heterogeneity.
Clinical characteristics
Frequency of selected clinical features of TH
deficiency is shown in Table 1.
Movement disorders
Dystonia is the most common movement disorder
in patients with TH deficiency and is mostly gener­
alized and localized in lower limbs13, 15). More than
45 cases with TH-deficient DRD (the mild phenotype
of TH deficiency7)) have been reported. Onset of
these DRD cases is generally between ages 12 months
and 12 years; in addition, some adolescent- and adultonset patients have been recognized9, 12, 13, 15, 18-21). In
general, initial symptoms of mildly affected patients
are lower-limb dystonia and/or difficulty in walking.

Typically, gradual progression to generalized dystonia
occurs. In contrast to autosomal dominant GTP
cyclohydrolase 1 (GTPCH [Figure 1]) deficiency
(GTPCH-deficient DRD12, 22) [DYT5a]), there is no
predominance of clinically affected females in auto­
somal recessive TH deficiency (including TH-defi­
cient DRD [DYT5b])15, 23). Without levodopa treat­
ment, most of the patients with TH deficiency,
including those with TH-deficient DRD, become
wheelchair bound; some had a misdiagnosis of
cerebral palsy5, 13, 15, 16, 19, 21, 23-26). Parkinsonian features
(hypokinesia or bradykinesia, postural and/or resting
tremor, rigidity, hypomimic face, etc.) are found
frequently and myoclonus (including atypical myoc­
lonus-dystonia27)) as well as other involuntary move­
ments can be recognized in TH deficiency.
Developmental delay and intellectual disability
Motor milestones in infancy (head control, rolling
over, sitting without assistance, crawling, walking
alone, etc.) are obviously delayed in severely affected
cases with TH deficiency. More than 165 patients
with the severe phenotype of TH deficiency have
been reported; onset of these patients is generally
before age 12 months9, 12, 13, 15, 16, 19, 28).
Standardized neuropsychological examinations
have not been conducted in most of the cases with
TH deficiency and it is difficult to provide specific
information on impaired cognitive status (i.e., global
developmental delay in children younger than five
years and intellectual disability in individuals aged
five years and older14)) in this disorder. Patients

who started levodopa treatment after two years of
age tended to demonstrate cognitive dysfunction29).
Recently, a relatively low incidence of cognitive
impairment (14%) has been found in a singlecenter retrospective analysis16).
Other neurologic features
Hypotonia is one of the most common initial
symptoms in infantile- and neonatal-onset TH defi­
ciency. Generally, tone is markedly decreased in
the truncal muscles (axial hypotonia) and that in
the extremities is variable; truncal hypotonia with
limb hypertonia due to rigidity and/or spasticity is
often recognized.
Oculogyric crises are dystonic episodes charac­
terized by sustained, conjugate, and typically upward
deviation of the eyes of variable duration. These
episodes should be distinguished from tonic eye
deviations observed in epileptic seizures.
Typical diurnal fluctuation of symptoms (wors­
ening of the symptoms toward the evening and
their alleviation in the morning after sleep30)) has
been recognized in approximately 40% of patients
with TH-deficient DRD (a much lower incidence
than that in GTPCH-deficient DRD12, 23)). Although
diurnal variation can also be observed in some
severely affected patients, it tends to be less prom­
inent and often atypical (e.g., diurnal variation of
axial hypotonia but not of limb dystonia31)).
Many patients with TH deficiency have clinical
features suggestive of pyramidal signs (hyperre­
flexia, spasticity, extensor plantar responses, etc.);
plantar responses can become flexor after begin­
ning levodopa therapy, suggesting that the previous
findings may be consistent with a dystonic phen­
omenon (the striatal toe) rather than a Babinski
response23, 24, 32-34). Some cases were initially misdi­
agnosed with spastic paraparesis15, 21).
Autonomic dysfunction can manifest with a variety
of symptoms (palpebral ptosis, increased drooling,
excessive sweating, nasal congestion, temperature
instability, etc.); in some papers, ptosis is listed
separately from autonomic symptoms14, 15) (Table 1).
Paroxysmal periods of lethargy (with prominent
autonomic disturbances) alternated with irritability
(lethargy-irritability crises) can be found in some
severely affected cases with TH deficiency; in general,
however, abnormalities in the maintenance of blood
pressure have not been recognized6, 8, 9, 14, 19, 35).

Seizures are uncommon in patients with TH defi­
ciency. On occasion, oculogyric crises may be confused
with seizures (see above); therefore, some had a
misdiagnosis of epilepsy15, 16, 19, 36, 37).
Neurobehavioral/psychiatric manifestations
Behavioral and psychiatric symptoms (irritability,
attention-deficit traits, autism, anxiety, depression,
etc.) can be found in some cases with TH defi­
ciency. One affected child was assessed with an
Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule-Module
2 and diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder38);
unfortunately, standardized neurobehavioral/psychi­
atric studies have not been conducted in most patients
with TH deficiency.
Anthropometric disturbances
Pre-/post-natal growth retardation affecting
head circumference (microcephaly), height, and/or
weight can be observed in some severely affected
cases with TH deficiency; feeding difficulties in
neonatal and infantile periods lead to growth
failure13, 14, 16, 39).
Noteworthy episodic symptoms
At least eight patients with TH deficiency have
demonstrated remarkable episodic symptoms9, 34, 40-45).
These symptoms are frequently induced and/or
exacerbated by fever and/or infections. Episodic
symptoms include dystonia, parkinsonism, truncal
hypotonia, loss of developmental milestones, motor
and language regression, and lethargy. Episodes
are often triggered by fever (including fever after
vaccination), viral infections, upper respiratory tract
infections, and flu-like illness. Because there are
various intervals between episodes, it is sometimes
difficult to determine an exact effect of levodopa
therapy to reduce these episodes; however, after
starting levodopa treatment, no remarkable episodic
recurrence occurs in most of the cases with this
distinctive phenotype of TH deficiency. Even in
patients with other phenotypes of TH deficiency,
deterioration of symptoms by fever and/or infec­
tions can be observed. Therefore, fever and/or
infections should be promptly treated to prevent
possible worsening of symptoms in TH deficiency.
Laboratory findings
The enzyme TH is mainly expressed in the brain

as well as adrenal medulla, and thus direct measure­
ment of its activity is not a diagnostic option. Precise
determination of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) concen­
trations of neurotransmitter metabolites (before
starting levodopa treatment) is diagnostic of TH
deficiency. Biochemical CSF profile typically demon­
strates the following3, 8, 9, 46-49): decreased homovan­
illic acid (HVA; a metabolite of dopamine), normal
5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA; a metabolite
of serotonin), reduced HVA/5-HIAA ratio, decreased
3-methoxy-4-hydroxy-phenylethyleneglycol (a
metabolite of norepinephrine), normal total biop­
terin (BP; BP includes BH4, quinonoid dihydrobiop­
terin, dihydrobiopterin, and oxidized biopterin, and
most of the brain BP exists as BH450, 51) [Figure 1]),
and normal total neopterin (which consists of
degradation products [dihydroneopterin and oxidized
neopterin] of dihydroneopterin triphosphate50, 51)
[Figure 1]).
As dopamine is a prolactin-inhibiting factor, blood
prolactin levels can be increased in dopamine-defi­
cient disorders (including TH deficiency), but it is
not a sensitive marker and its normal levels do not
exclude TH deficiency9, 35, 48, 52-55). On the other hand,
blood prolactin measurement during and after
puberty is recommended to screen for hyperpro­
lactinemia as comorbidity56).
Neuroimaging
In most of the patients with TH deficiency, brain
MRI is normal. Brain MRI demonstrated no abnor­
malities in the basal ganglia of two cases with
TH-deficient DRD even 38 and 43 years after onset
of the disorder24). MRI revealed cortical atrophy,
ventriculomegaly, and other non-specific brain
changes in some patients. There were no correla­
tions between these MRI changes and clinical pheno­
types11, 14, 15, 57).
Neuropathology
In TH-deficient DRD5, 24, 58) and typical GTPCHdeficient DRD59-66), neuropathological investigations
showed normal cell density and no Lewy bodies in
the substantia nigra (SN). Characteristically, there
were decreases of melanin-pigment from SN neurons
without dopaminergic neuronal degeneration in
autopsied cases with both disorders67). There have
been no reports of pathologic findings in severely
affected patients with TH deficiency.

Neurochemistry
There have been no reports of neurochemical
data in TH-deficient DRD. However, similar to
those in typical GTPCH-deficient DRD (TH protein
levels were normal in the SN but markedly reduced
in the striatum without degeneration of nigrostri­
atal dopaminergic neurons59-62, 66)), normal number
of TH-positive neurons in the SN and severely
decreased TH immunostaining, associated with
normal dopamine transporter staining, in the stri­
atum were found in a mouse model of mild TH
deficiency (DRD Th knock-in mice)68, 69).
Protein concentrations of dopaminergic markers,
including TH, in the mesencephalon and pons were
reduced in a 16-week-old miscarried human fetus
having compound heterozygous TH variants70); an
elder sister of this fetus had the same pathogenic
TH variants and developed the severe phenotype
of TH deficiency (infantile encephalopathy with
motor delay)71). In a Th knock-in mouse model of
very severe TH deficiency (encephalopathy Th
knock-in mice being unresponsive to levodopa
treatment), there was no loss of TH-immunoreac­
tive neurons in the SN; recent quantitative analysis
of fluorescence intensity in this model revealed
moderate and severe TH protein decreases in the
SN and striatum (40 % and 1% of wild type controls,
respectively) without degeneration of nigrostriatal
dopaminergic neurons72, 73).
Molecular diagnosis
The diagnosis of autosomal recessive TH defi­
ciency can be established in a proband with sugges­
tive clinical and/or laboratory findings by identifi­
cation of biallelic pathogenic variants in TH by
genetic testing. In Human Gene Mutation Database
(HGMD Professional 2026.2), 113 pathogenic vari­
ants in TH (including 86 missense/nonsense muta­
tions, 9 splicing substitutions, 3 regulatory substitu­
tions, 5 small deletions, 1 small insertion, 2 small
indels, 6 gross deletions, and 1 complex rearrange­
ment) have been reported.
More than 95% of TH variants can be detected
by sequence analyses; using whole-genome sequen­
cing, one deep intronic variant74) has been identi­
fied recently. Generally, to detect gross deletions
and gross insertions/duplications, gene-targeted
deletion/duplication analyses (including multiple

ligation-dependent probe amplification, long-range
PCR, quantitative PCR, etc.) should be conducted.
Clinical features of four compound heterozygotes
having pathogenic gross deletions have been
reported75-78). In addition, one heterozygote for a
deletion of entire TH, who developed adult-onset
Parkinson’s disease79), has been included in the
Human Gene Mutation Database.
Definite genotype-phenotype correlations have
not been found in patients with TH deficiency.
However, it has been reported that cases who were
homozygous or compound heterozygous for a singlenucleotide variant in the promotor region of TH
did not develop very severe TH deficiency27, 75, 80-83).
Once biallelic pathogenic variants in TH are identi­
fied in an affected family member, prenatal testing
for a pregnancy at increased risk and preimplanta­
tion genetic testing are possible, and prenatal diag­
noses of TH deficiency have been reported71, 83, 84).
At least six individuals with monoallelic TH vari­
ants have been reported to develop muscle stiffness
after exercise or dystonic symptoms responding to
levodopa15, 19, 33, 41, 85), suggesting that, as in the case
of sepiapterin reductase (SR [Figure 1]) defici­
ency86-88), heterozygous pathogenic variants in TH
might cause autosomal dominant DRD. However, in
these heterozygotes, there is still a possibility of
undetermined structural or deep intronic TH vari­
ants on the other allele or of unknown additional
variants of other genes relating to striatal dopa­
mine content15, 89).
Differential diagnosis
The major differential diagnosis of TH deficiency
is summarized in Table 212, 13, 47, 88, 90, 91). In general,
patients with autosomal recessive BH4-related

ciency are initially misdiagnosed as having cerebral
palsy, spastic paraplegia, or epilepsy (see above).

Levodopa
Levodopa is the first-line drug for TH deficiency
and all of the patients with TH deficiency should be
treated appropriately with levodopa in combination
with a peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor (carbi­
dopa or benserazide) (Table 3).
Patients with TH-deficient DRD demonstrate a
dramatic and sustained response to levodopa and
an excellent response in the absence of any motor
adverse effects (wearing-off and on-off phenomena
and levodopa-induced dyskinesias) of chronic levo­
dopa therapy for more than 30 years has been
reported5, 24, 58). In contrast, in severely affected
patients, the responsiveness is frequently not
complete and/or it takes several months or even
years for the full effects of treatment to become
established; at least 15 severely affected cases have
shown poor or no response to levodopa14-16).
Severely affected patients, who demonstrate
remarkable motor development delay, are often
hypersensitive to levodopa therapy and are prone
to intolerable side effects (i.e., severe levodopa-in­
duced dyskinesias which develop at initiation of
levodopa treatment). Because of this hypersensi­
tivity, these cases should be treated with extremely
low initial doses of levodopa; gradual titration
(changing the dose slowly and by small increments)
to higher doses is strongly recommended.
Other agents
A selective monoamine oxidase (MAO)-B inhib­

itor (selegiline), which prevents the breakdown of
dopamine, has been added to levodopa therapy (or
very rarely used as monotherapy) in more than 25
severely affected patients generally not tolerating
higher doses of levodopa6, 8-10, 16, 20, 27, 35, 41, 44, 55, 92, 94-98).
Most of these cases have shown some improve­
ment and selegiline can be considered as a supple­
mentary treatment in combination with levodopa
(Table 3).
As reported in compound heterozygotes for
GCH1 variants (who showed developmental motor
delay, dystonia, parkinsonism, and hypersensitivity
to levodopa treatment)99), amantadine (which acts
as a N-methyl-D-aspartate [NMDA] receptor antag­
onist and has dopaminergic and anticholinergic
properties) has alleviated levodopa-induced dyski­
nesias in severely affected patients with TH defi­
ciency76, 93). Thus, amantadine can be considered as
a supplementary treatment in combination with
levodopa to suppress dose-related dyskinesias in
patients with the severe phenotype.
Dopamine agonists, which directly stimulate post­
synaptic dopamine receptors, have been reported
to demonstrate limited or no beneficial effects in
patients with TH deficiency8, 27, 35, 55). On a theoretic
basis, catechol-O-methyl transferase (COMT: this
enzyme breaks down levodopa) inhibitors can be
considered as a supplementary treatment in combi­
nation with levodopa, but there has been only one
report describing a single case treated with COMT
inhibitors55). During chronic levodopa therapy, there
is a possibility of the development of cerebral folate
deficiency; in this case, folinic acid supplementation
should be considered76).
Therapies under investigation
At the present time, no clinical trials for TH defi­
ciency can be found in ClinicalTrials.gov in the US
and EU Clinical Trials Register in Europe. Nygaard
and colleagues have reported future treatment
opportunities for TH deficiency100).
Pregnancy management
As indicated in GTPCH-deficient DRD101), levodopa
treatment should be continued in patients with TH
deficiency during pregnancy20, 102). Intensive super­
vision by a multidisciplinary team before, during,
and after pregnancy is essential. Generally, it is
better not to use selegiline during pregnancy due

to concerns about potential vasoconstrictive effects.
Agents/circumstances to avoid
In patients with TH deficiency, antipsychotic
medications and other dopamine receptor antago­
nists (e.g., metoclopramide) are not recommended
on a theoretic basis. Of great importance, abrupt
discontinuation of levodopa and other dopaminergic
medications should be avoided to prevent the
potential development of dystonic crisis/storm or
akinetic crisis.
Pathogenesis
Because null variants of TH are lethal as shown
in TH (-/-) knock-out mice103), patients with reces­
sively inherited TH-deficiency (both homozygotes
and compound heterozygotes for TH pathogenic
variants) appear to have some residual TH activity
and the degree of this activity is related to the
disease severity104). For instance, a mutated recom­
binant enzyme in TH-deficient DRD showed approx­
imately 15% and another one in TH-deficient infan­
tile parkinsonism/dystonia with motor delay demon-­
strated 0.3-16% of wild-type enzyme activity1-3). In
addition, being similar to compound heterozygotes
for GCH1 variants (versus heterozygotes [DRD:

DYT5a])99, 105), severely affected patients with TH
deficiency (versus mildly affected patients [DRD:
DYT5b]) generally demonstrate obvious motor
development delay, truncal hypotonia, and levodopainduced dyskinesias at the beginning of levodopa
therapy.
Nigrostriatal dopaminergic terminals seem to be
the most vulnerable to the TH-deficient state
(which may disrupt TH protein axonal transport
from the soma) and the BH4-deficient state (which
may impair the stability of TH molecules in
the striatum)61, 62, 73, 106-108). Recently, Roman and
colleagues109) found dysregulation of extracellular
signal-related kinase phosphorylation in their DRD
Th knock-in mice and have suggested that this
dysregulated signaling may be caused by loss of D1
dopamine receptor stimulation resulting from earlylife dopamine deficiency. In fact, D1-spiny projec­
tion neurons fail to undergo maturation of excit­
ability in mice deficient for postnatal increases in
striatal dopamine neurotransmission; in these mice,
D2-spiny projection neurons mature normally110).

subjects, striatal dopamine concentrations signifi­
cantly increase during postnatal development
(Figure 2) and decrease during aging111). More­

contributing to levodopa-unresponsive impairments.
Conclusion
Since the discovery of various TH pathogenic
variants responsible for TH deficiency, our under­
standing of this recessively inherited metabolic
disorder has exceedingly increased. However, the
exact underlying mechanisms of phenotypic hetero­
geneity in patients with TH deficiency are still
unknown. The normal human brain data indicate
that development of the nigrostriatal dopaminergic
system (including BH4, TH, and dopamine biosyn­
theses) is incomplete at birth and striatal dopamine
levels significantly increase during postnatal devel­
opment. Because of congenital dopamine deficiency
and insufficient postnatal dopamine increases in
the striatum, mildly affected patients with TH defi­

ciency may develop DRD through dysfunctional D1
dopamine receptors. In addition, more severely
affected patients may demonstrate remarkable
motor development delay in infancy and hypersen­
sitivity to levodopa therapy caused by profound
brain dopamine deficit (without dopaminergic
neuronal degeneration) and consequent abnormal
synaptic and brain maturation. Considering such
an important role of dopamine in postnatal develop­
ment of the brain, deficiency of this neurotrans­
mitter should be corrected by proper treatment
with levodopa (the precursor of dopamine) as soon
as genetically or biochemically diagnosed or upon
the appearance of characteristic symptoms and
signs of TH deficiency. Thus, physicians should
know the broad phenotypic spectrum of this treat­
able metabolic disorder and a therapeutic trial of
levodopa is still an important practical approach to
the diagnosis.
