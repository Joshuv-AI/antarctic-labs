Advancing health equity through the development of a scholars training program in implementation science
Authors: Rebekka M. Lee, Loni Parrish, Rebekah Jacob, Cheryl Valko, Andrea Jimenez-Zambrano, Erica T. Warner, Ross C. Brownson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundApproaches to achieving health equity in implementation science are critical to ameliorating disparities resulting from social, economic, and racial injustices. The field needs to grow transdisciplinary tools, processes, and increased workforce capacity to lead health equity research, particularly among scholars from groups that are underrepresented in science. This project, perhaps the first of its kind, used mixed methods to develop and pilot a robust, actionable model for mentoring and training to advance health equity in implementation science.MethodsTo inform the content and design, we conducted concept mapping with implementation science and equity researchers (N = 69) and qualitative one-on-one interviews with leaders (N = 6) and past trainees (N = 14) of established programs with a health equity or disparities focus. We piloted the training among 17 scholars (100% underrepresented in science, 88% women) over 2.5 days. It followed engagement and active learning principles with small group discussions, speed mentoring, problem-based learning, and peer-to-peer teaching.ResultsConcept mapping identified priority competencies of engaging the community, equity-relevant methods, cultural humility, and mentoring to integrate into the training. Interviewees discussed the importance of designing the program based on participants’ needs. Therefore, we added a pre-program survey to determine scholars’ priorities. Interviewees emphasized the importance of having presenters from underrepresented backgrounds discuss their journeys and opportunities to learn equity content from other scholars, which informed a panel of junior investigators on navigating academia. They recommended including presentations led by community members, historical perspectives on health equity, and equity-focused methods, which informed a community engagement and measurement lecture co-led by an academic-community partnership. Scholars identified mentorship opportunities, a supportive environment, diverse perspectives of scholars and faculty, and engaging discussions as program strengths.ConclusionsThis study applied a user-informed, data-driven approach to designing an implementation science training that prioritizes the interests of diverse scholars. We identified curriculum competencies and processes to build capacity for an implementation science workforce that centers on equity.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s43058-026-00992-1.

Contributions to literature

• Explicit attention to health equity is essential for ensuring the greatest benefits and ameliorating disparities within the field of implementation science.

• This innovative project used mixed methods to develop and pilot test a comprehensive training to advance health equity in implementation science.

• We demonstrate how qualitative interviews, concept mapping, and user surveys can be integrated to create data-informed training competencies and activities.

Introduction

Approaches to achieving health equity in dissemination and implementation science (hereafter, referred to as “implementation science”) are critical to ameliorating disparities resulting from social, economic, and racial injustices [1–3]. Researchers have begun integrating health disparities constructs into existing implementation science frameworks and projects [4, 5]; however, more explicit attention to equity is needed. To fully advance health equity research within the field of implementation science, we need to identify, build, and grow tools and processes (the “what” and the “how”) alongside sustained support and accountability from funders and academic institutions that shape whose work is valued and supported. A health equity-focused approach also requires building workforce capacity to lead the research (the “who”), particularly capacity building with scholars from groups that are underrepresented in public health and clinical research [6, 7]. This includes groups who have been historically underrepresented by race, ethnicity, disability, sexual orientation, gender identity, and socioeconomic status.

Over the past several years, there have been multiple calls among implementation scientists to prioritize new methods, measures, tools, and technologies to accelerate the achievement of health equity research goals, structures, and programs [1, 8–10]. This focus will support robust research in health equity, including training and capacity building programs emphasizing community engagement. Other examples of the increased priority and interest in capacity building and the growth of the research workforce have highlighted gaps in training to advance health equity through implementation science [11–14].

Recent reviews of initiatives to build capacity in implementation science have shown a growing number of training and mentoring opportunities across eight countries [15–18]. Capacity building for implementation science occurs in multiple formats, including university degree programs, summer training institutes, workshops, and conferences [16]. Few training programs in implementation science focus explicitly on advancing health equity and health equity research. A vision for such training includes multiple parts: 1) how a training program centers diverse scholars and faculty, 2) how training is delivered to reach broad audiences, 3) how equity is featured as an explicit part of core competencies, 4) how principles of community engagement are included in the training, and 5) how progress toward equity is evaluated [18]. This project’s purpose is to combine the rich knowledge and experience of health equity training leaders and participants across a broad range of fields with that of implementation scientists to develop a training that will advance health equity-focused implementation science.

This project used mixed methods to create and test a robust, actionable model for mentoring and training to advance health equity in implementation science. We integrated quantitative concept mapping to refine core competencies with qualitative interviews with leaders and past participants in related health equity training programs to build and pilot a unique training program.

Methods

This project used a complex mixed methods design to center trainees’ experiences in the design and pilot test a program to advance health equity in implementation science (Fig. 1). First, we integrated concept mapping and qualitative interviews to determine the competencies and processes for the training program. Trainees were part of program design via pre-training surveys and delivery and provided feedback in post-evaluation surveys.Fig. 1Mixed methods study design

Concept mapping

Measures

Following concept mapping methods [19], we invited participants in two phases of data collection (May to August 2023). The sample (N = 213) was drawn from six implementation science working groups (e.g. Washington University Network for Dissemination and Implementation, ISC3 Health Equity Task Force) and four implementation science training program (e.g. Implementation Research Institute, Institute for Implementation Science Scholars) contact lists made available to the research team with permission. Lists included experts in implementation science and equity research. All data collection used GroupWisdom’s concept mapping web-based software [20]. The first phase (Brainstorming) asked participants to enter one or more free text statements in response to the following prompt “To advance equity in dissemination and implementation science, a skill or competency in which researchers need more training is …” Participants also answered four demographic questions. The research team then reduced the initial 269 statements to 100 unique statements by combining similar concepts and editing for clarity. In Phase 2 (Rating and Sorting), we asked Phase 1 participants to rate each of the 100 statements in terms of their importance and difficulty “in advancing equity in D&I science” (1-Not at all to 7-Extremely). Participants then sorted the 100 statements into different piles according to the similarity of topics, assigning each pile with a meaningful title.

Analysis

Multidimensional scaling and hierarchical clustering algorithms within GroupWisdom produced several cluster maps. The research team then examined each scenario, working backward from a 15-cluster solution, to determine which solution best fit the statements within each of the clusters. Bridging values, or the likelihood that participants sorted statements similarly, for each item and the solution’s stress index were used to compare scenarios. We exported rating tables and demographics from GroupWisdom. We summarized the data using R. Ethical approval for concept mapping was obtained from the Washington University in St. Louis Institutional Review Board (IRB).

Qualitative interviews

Measures

We collected qualitative data via one-on-one virtual interviews over Zoom to explore the experiences of leaders and past trainees from established training programs with a health equity or disparities focus (August – October 2023). These programs intentionally ranged in scope and audience to gather a broad range of perspectives, including research training for undergraduates and early career investigators across a variety of health-related fields. None of the training programs had an explicit focus on implementation science. Six programs’ leaders (e.g., directors, co-directors, managers) were identified from participants in an earlier web-based survey [11]. We purposively sampled leaders for maximum variation in perspectives by program characteristics and used snowball sampling to identify past program participants.

All interviews were conducted using a semi-structured interview guide (Supplementary Material 1) focused on understanding the preferences for the training design (e.g., recruitment strategies, format, teaching methods mentorship) and activities (e.g., approaches for integrating equity, core competencies). We also explored contextual factors influencing training experiences using probes structured by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research [5]. Four researchers (RL, LP, EW, AJZ) with diverse racial/ethnic backgrounds and disciplinary expertise conducted the interviews. LP and AJZ are early‑career researchers trained in qualitative methods, while RL and EW are midcareer investigators with extensive experience in qualitative inquiry and implementation science. Together, the team reflects a mix of insider and outsider perspectives relevant to the study context, such as reflecting on past personal training experiences. We recognize that these positionalities may have shaped interview dynamics, including how questions were posed and how participants responded. To support reflexivity, interviewers engaged in regular debriefings and collaboratively examined how their backgrounds influenced emerging interpretations [21]. Following data collection, we worked with the larger research team—including qualitative methodologists and specialists in implementation science training and mentoring—to enhance rigor and trustworthiness.

Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Interviewers drafted memos immediately after each interview, focused on actionable takeaways for training development. Participants were compensated $50 for taking part in interviews. Four IRBs reviewed the qualitative interview protocol at the home institutions of the interviewers: Washington University in St. Louis, Harvard Longwood Area, Massachusetts General Brigham, and University of Colorado.

Analysis

We conducted a 2-phase framework analysis [22] to ensure rapid integration of our findings into the pilot training [22, 23]. First, interviewers (RL, LP, EW, AJZ) summarized memos and shared them with the larger research team. During collaborative discussions, the group identified and reached a consensus on overarching actionable findings emerging from the data that were identified as top priorities to address in the training.

Next, the qualitative team conducted an in-depth thematic analysis of interviews in Dedoose 9.2.5. The codebook, developed by two researchers (RL, LP), was initially structured to align with the interview guide and the CFIR framework for deductive coding. To better understand how equity was integrated into the overall health equity training program structure, we created unique equity subcodes within the innovation domain. Other codes that emerged via inductive coding included cultural competence and flexibility within the characteristics of the individual domain. One researcher (LP) coded all transcripts and the other (RL) double-coded a subset (20%) of transcripts until all emerging codes were identified and defined (Fig. 2). Coders (RL, LP) met to review the independently coded transcripts (LP) to discuss assigned codes and any newly emerging codes. Then, themes within each CFIR domain were iteratively developed by the two qualitative coders and shared with the larger training development team for further refinement. Themes related to characteristics of the innovation were brought to the team to discuss inclusion in the training.Fig. 2Consolidated framework for implementation research constructs in thematic analysis of health equity training interviews

Pre-training survey

To ensure the training was tailored to the interests and needs of the scholars engaged in the pilot, participants were asked to complete a brief survey to rate topics to include in the training in December 2023. Scholars chose their top five topics (and arranged them in priority order) from a list of 11 items developed from concept mapping clusters and qualitative themes.

Training design

Following principles of mixed-methods research [23], findings from qualitative interviews and quantitative surveys were summarized and presented in a series of team meetings to identify areas of convergence in findings. In October-December 2023, before piloting the training, we reviewed summaries from our qualitative interviews on characteristics of the innovation alongside competencies identified on the concept mapping surveys as a team. We combined our team’s extensive experience conducting implementation science training and these data to develop the content and the training and mentoring strategies for the February 2024 pilot training. Table 3 summarizes how these qualitative and qualitative findings translated into each component of the training. Pre-training survey results set final priorities for the design and topics covered. We were not able to test recruitment strategies given time and resource constraints of the pilot training.

After the training, scholars completed an online post-evaluation survey via Qualtrics (Qualtrics, Provo, UT), which included questions on their satisfaction with various parts of the training and asked a series of open-ended questions regarding what they found to be the most valuable aspects of the training and their recommendations for future training. (see Supplementary Material 4 for details).

Qualitative interviews

We conducted 20 interviews with 6 leaders and 14 scholars across 6 established training programs with a health equity or disparities focus. Programs included those designed for undergraduates as well as national training for junior investigators. The scholars interviewed come from diverse backgrounds. Some scholars identify as Black, cisgender, heterosexual, able-bodied women who are married or partnered and have achieved middle to upper-class status in recent years. Others identify as immigrants, first-generation college students, and first-generation graduate students. In terms of careers, these scholars bring a wide range of expertise, including licensed psychologists, community-engaged qualitative researchers, child health researchers, pharmacy technicians.

Phase 1 rapid analysis results that informed the training design are displayed in Table 3. Phase 2 thematic analysis findings are organized by aspects of equity and CFIR domain (characteristics of individuals, outer setting, inner setting). First, we describe how training programs integrate equity in the recruitment process, content/curriculum, program structure/delivery and cultivate a safe place. Then we present data on contextual influences on equity-focused trainings.

Characteristics of the innovations (health equity trainings)

Participants discussed the aspects of equity built into the recruitment process. This was typically part of the application or interview questions, which focused on more than academic merit. A leader of the training described asking about career goals and looking for scholars whose experiences align with their future goals: “We didn’t want what is now being labeled as sort of research tourism happening. We wanted to know that people had investment.” (Program 1, Leader). Applications included questions about identity, background, and needs. One participant (Program 2, Leader) described having the direct question of “how has health equity impacted your life?” as part of the interviews. A leader (Program 3) shared the importance of explicitly addressing purpose and privacy of the data collected in the application via these types of questions to build trust: “we ask ‘to what degree would you want us to respect your confidentiality and what would you like your mentor to know.’”

Most programs recruited via common outreach approaches such as email listservs, flyers, and through word-of-mouth faculty and past participant networks. However, one leader (Program 4) described the importance of developing a more intentional list of recruitment sources to ensure scholar diversity: “there are lots of talented people with PhDs and MD degrees who are diverse. But if we keep using the same recruitment sources, we are not going to find them.” Several other unique best practices to ensure equity in recruitment also emerged. One past scholar shared the utility of having written materials about the health equity focus of the program accompanying the application materials.

Participants stated that equity-focused content and curriculum across training programs consisted of educational trainings that focused on expanding scholars’ knowledge in research and practice. One scholar (Program 1, Scholar 1) mentioned, “there was an opportunity to get some tangible skills about how to make an impact in your community through legislative advocacy.” Other educational training topics included equity in budgeting, health literacy, power, systematic racism in the US healthcare system, residential segregation, methodologies and basic programing in R, health gaps in different populations, disseminating findings and making data accessible to the public. Interviewees also described interpersonal trainings that focused on solidarity and allyship. Another scholar (Program 1, Scholar 3) described that during the health equity program they have “done some talking about the difference between allyship and solidarity, which is I think was really powerful, particularly for those who hold power and privilege in certain areas.” Intrapersonal trainings described focused on helping scholars gain confidence and navigate through barriers that may arise with being a health equity researcher. These included training on resilience, professional etiquette, imposter syndrome, and mental health. Participants also noted that programs allowed scholars to participate in writing workshops, field trips to community organizations that work in historically disadvantaged communities, and scientific product working groups that were described as a “safe space for people to come to share their research ideas and get feedback from their peers about what they’re working on” (Program 5, Leader).

Participants also described how equity was built into the program structure and delivery. Most programs used a hybrid format that allows flexibility for some remote sessions. One leader (Program 1) emphasized the importance of the hybrid format: “I think if it’s going to be a national program and you’re doing things remotely; you have to bring people together at some point. There is nothing better than developing in-person relationships”. Mentorship was an important component in many training programs. Some programs had a hands-on mentoring approach, while others mentioned that mentors were “available if you had challenges or questions” (Program 1, Scholar 1). One training program required mentors to go through an “all day mentoring session” (Program 4, Leader). Regardless of the mentoring model, scholars expressed the value of having accessible program leadership and staff to check in regularly and provide support when busy mentors were not available. Another crosscutting lesson was the importance of giving scholars’ agency in the mentorship process – whether by selecting their top choices for mentors or being able to identify a national mentor (from outside of the program) to address a substantive gap that was paid for by the program. Scholars across programs also described the sustained support they felt from mentors.

Leaders and scholars described various strategies programs intentionally implemented to provide a safe space. First, the trustworthiness of the program leaders, mentors, and staff played a pivotal role. One standout coordinator was described: “I can’t understate it enough that having that kind of person in that role is so critical and not just, um, to be able to handle kind of the things, the challenges that come up, but also for the long term … We all have to report on our outcomes for ten years. So having a trusted name that when she follows up … and asks, ‘how are you doing?’” (Program 3, Leader). Another leader added that the importance of consistency in the approach to handling conflict across staff was key for ensuring scholars felt safe. These trusted leaders emphasized the importance of sharing their contact information with scholars to ensure open communication and encourage them to reach out if they face challenges. One leader (Program 1) also named the importance of empowering scholars to set boundaries:I, as a leader of this program, give agency to anyone in this program, if something feels unsafe, do not put yourself in that position … I give people permission to engage in the way in the programming that allows them to feel like they can experience it without the cost to them personally.

Establishing ground rules for engagement, collaborative agreements, and language that creates a safe space were also named as a useful practice. Several programs also built activities aimed at building trust into their programs. These included onboarding that focused on sharing personal identities, affinity groups, field trips and lunches to connect people informally, self-care activities, and opportunities for peer mentoring to lift up scholars’ expertise. While these activities were thoughtfully designed, interviewees cautioned that challenges and conflict can still arise in its implementation. How these challenges are addressed played a major role in building trust. One scholar (Program 1, Scholar 3) shared their experience being pulled aside by a leader to check in one on one on how they would like the issue to be addressed, saying:They came to me first and said, ‘Someone shared with me that this went down and before I do anything else, I need to check in with you’ And it was like circle of trust style conversation, like nothing was repeated, and that was valuable for me.

Another scholar (Program 1, Scholar 2) described the importance of having space to debrief challenging conversations “to make sure everybody felt like their concerns were heard”. That scholar went on to describe how planned check-ins a couple times each year helped to create space to address concerns: “so it wasn’t very pushy or very overbearing. They kind of just opened the door, and if we needed something, and I felt like that was an opportunity to step into that space.”

Characteristics of individuals (training leaders and facilitators)

Participants described the importance of having training program leaders and facilitators who demonstrated flexibility and cultural humility in addition to capacity and motivation to ensure the training is a safe space for diverse scholars. Most interviewees stated that their leaders and facilitators are supportive. They are accepting of feedback, so that scholars a have good idea of what is expected of them. Leaders and facilitators utilize a flexible and open-minded approach. One interviewee reflected, “we’re working with humans, we’re trying to establish relationships. We are trying to work on some really, really complex and hard topics. And so the level of flexibility that they’ve had through this process, has renewed my sense of purpose and zeal for doing the work” (Program 1, Scholar 2). A senior fellow from another program stated, “what I really wanted to do is not be too hands on and kind of give them free rein to experience the program how they see fit” (Program 2, Scholar 1). Several training programs focused on cultural competence – both by requiring leaders and facilitators to take a training on cultural humility and by structuring regular meetings with dedicated staff. One training program had a leader “in charge of making sure that everybody was in a good space … she literally was watching people’s reaction to different things that were being discussed in the room. And at any moment, she would tap you on your shoulder, or she checked in with you to make sure you were okay” (Program 1, Scholar 2). Sometimes, the shared lived experiences of the faculty leaders and scholars were described as fostering mentoring relationships. One leader who identified as a first-generation student reflected, “You become like a magnet … There’s a lot of students will come to the office because I speak Spanish. They feel they can trust me” (Program 5, Leader 1).

Inner setting

Participants often discussed the impact of available resources at the organization hosting the training as a key factor influencing implementation of the training or capacity building goals. These resources included financial funding and access to experienced experts (e.g., diverse coaches, statisticians). Most programs provided financial compensation (e.g., protected time, stipends, hourly pay) for participating. They also paid for travel and resources for career advancement, such as course and test fees or stipends for outside mentors. One scholar (Program 1, Scholar 1) described this innovative approach: “If you identify a national mentor, they actually have funding … like $1000 or something that they could give if we were like, ‘We need a mentor with skills on X,’ like qualitative research methods or whatever.” Scholars also described the network of researchers and leaders they met through the program, including guest speakers, as a major resource they took with them. Finally, one program provided separate resources only available to community partners and translated this value of providing community resources into the content of the program.

One health equity training leader also noted the importance of mission alignment and relational connections between the academic institutions co-hosting the fellows program: “I think personally that the reason why this program is running and … how it keeps on going is based off of the collaboration between all the institutions that are a part of it … because they both come with their strengths and their connections and through those through that collaboration, they’re able to kind of craft a program that’s hitting different elements and bring people along.” (Program 2, Scholar 1).

Outer setting

In terms of influences in the outer setting, participants frequently shared about the importance of partnerships and connections. These partnerships, built and strengthened over many years, are important for including a diverse range of guest speakers and mentors. Some programs included opportunities to visit these partners (e.g., healthcare providers focused on social determinants of health, nonprofits addressing food insecurity) on site to learn how organizations are actively supporting communities that have been disadvantaged. The impact of local conditions was also discussed. One leader (Program 2) talked about the challenges and benefits of working with scholars from “two very different worlds” – naming the importance of considering the contexts in which participants are coming, such as the academic setting or geographic area. Another leader discussed challenges between divergent and local and federal priorities and policies on workforce recruitment processes.

Training implementation

In February 2024, the training was pilot tested among 17 scholars (100% underrepresented in science, 88% women) over 2.5 days, in-person on the Washington University campus in St. Louis. Core training components were developed using data from concept mapping and interviews (Table 3) and prioritized by the pre-training survey.

The training was structured to ensure equity via mentorship, accessible program leadership and staff, and developing safe spaces for bi-directional learning. Trainees were housed together, ate meals together, and had opportunities for social interactions in the evening to maximize the impact of the in-person training. During the course, we followed principles of engagement and active learning to present information in a way that is contextually relevant and recognizable. Activities included small group discussions, “speed mentoring,” problem-based learning, real-time polling, and peer-to-peer teaching. Panel discussions featured scholars and community partners, and didactic presentations featured scholars as discussants. The detailed training schedule and evaluation are available in Supplementary Materials 2 and 4.

Discussion

This study presents a user-informed, data-driven approach for designing a health equity implementation science training that centers on the needs and interests of diverse scholars. Concept mapping among implementation science and health equity experts identified 12 core competencies as the focus of a 2.5-day in-person training to build the capacity of the implementation science workforce to address equity in research. These competencies were merged with perspectives from interviews with leaders and past participants of established training programs focusing on health equity or disparities to develop the training approach. Interviewees suggested centering the needs of participants by building in a pre-training survey to tailor the training to the interests of the scholars each year. We followed this advice to prioritize focus areas and refine the pilot’s final curriculum. Interviews revealed the importance of integrating equity into the recruitment, content, and design of a training program and the essential inclusion of partnerships and mentoring for community building. They emphasized that training leaders and facilitators who are flexible and culturally competent help to create a safe space that fosters trust. Interviews identified an important relationship between mission alignment and relationship connections. Bringing together a leadership team that strongly agrees on the goals and competencies central to health equity capacity building will serve as a glue to connect those with diverse lived and professional experiences. Ultimately, mixed methods formative data generated a first-of-its-kind pilot of a health equity implementation science training that successfully built the capacity of diverse scholars in the field.

This pilot training emphasized topics in implementation science several ways. First, participatory implementation was highlighted in the planning and in multiple sessions [25]. Second, the field of implementation science needs to more fully integrate equity principles throughout the research project planning, implementation, and evaluation process [1, 8, 26]. This project infused these principles in multiple sessions. Finally, building on literature on mentored training in implementation science [18], bidirectional mentoring was a focus of this project.

The strengths of this project include integrating user data to inform the training content and delivery, which was applied via rapid analysis of qualitative interviews, surveys, and concept mapping. We intentionally considered the racial and ethnic backgrounds and professional career stages of members of the qualitative data collection and analysis team to ensure a diversity of perspectives in the interpretation of data and strengthen connections with participants given interview topics focused on identity and equity. Additionally, concept mapping allowed us to include a broad range of perspectives in the training design.

There were several study limitations. First, there were suggestions from interviews that could not be piloted, such as integrating equity in recruitment processes and having peer mentors support future cohorts of scholars – we intend to include these approaches in future implementation of the training. We will evaluate participants’ perceptions of their acceptability. The interview sample included more early career scholars (e.g., past program participants for undergraduates) than those in mid-career, which is the intended audience for this training. In some instances, this limited the applicability of contextual influences and recommendations. Still, it also allowed for consideration of best practices that might be overlooked with more narrowly defined inclusion criteria.

The results from this study can serve as the foundation for a national training program on health equity in implementation science and across other scientific training contexts. We see the mixed methods used for the development of the training as broadly applicable. We hope training and mentoring programs in other fields will apply our user-focused methods to center equity in their capacity-building approaches. Interviews revealed that equity-focused training programs are perceived as better when equity is embedded in the design, recruitment, curriculum, mentorship structure, and delivery. Equity should be treated as a mechanism built into the pedagogical approach and not just the content being taught. While equity-focused training may contribute to long-term advances in health equity, responsibility for addressing structural inequities should not be placed on underrepresented scholars themselves. Rather, such training should be situated within broader institutional commitments, centering underrepresented scholars’ experiences and supporting their leadership in health equity when aligned with their interests.

Our concept mapping data show a comprehensive set of content areas for further studies. In future work, we aim to conduct a more rigorous evaluation of this equity-focused training’s impact on participants’ knowledge and skills, as well as on scholar career trajectories and programs of research.

Conclusion

Capacity building and training of our current and future workforce is essential as the complexity of addressing the chronic disease burden impacts population health morbidity and mortality. Capacity building programs like this offer opportunities to be nimble and flexible to institutional environments and have the potential to be broadly applied across different parts of the country including urban or rural and small or large institutions. The program described here is responsive to several scientific priorities for implementation science, community engagement, and workforce growth and development in scientific fields. These have been prioritized at various institutes and centers, the public foundation environment, and scientific bodies, including the National Academies of Medicine. Opportunities to further refine the curriculum and to adapt and deliver across various sites and transdisciplinary contexts may be of value, especially as there is high demand to more rapidly move our evidence base into policy and practice to impact quality of life for all Americans.

Over the past two decades, implementation science has grown rapidly [27]. Capacity building to train the implementation science workforce has not kept up with the demand. This gap in capacity is even more acute for equity-focused training, where few training programs focus on the intersection of health equity and implementation science and build competence in each discipline [28]. Our user-informed study provides a useful foundation for designing, implementing, and evaluating implementation science training that centers on equity.
