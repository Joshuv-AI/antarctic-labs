4‐1BBL Suppresses Anti‐Inflammatory Responses by Regulating Metabolic Reprogramming of Macrophages
Authors: Young Jun Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntegration of intracellular signalling and metabolic reprogramming is critical for macrophage polarisation and the induction of pro‐ or anti‐inflammatory responses. However, the molecular switches that govern these processes remain incompletely defined. While 4‐1BB ligand (4‐1BBL), a member of the TNF superfamily, is known to promote sustained pro‐inflammatory responses in macrophages, its role in anti‐inflammatory macrophage responses has not been fully elucidated. This study identifies that 4‐1BBL serves as a negative regulator of anti‐inflammatory macrophage polarisation. Genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of 4‐1BBL significantly enhanced the expression of anti‐inflammatory cytokines and markers in mouse macrophages. In IL‐4R signalling, 4‐1BBL restrained Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6) phosphorylation, thereby modulating transcriptional programmes associated with anti‐inflammatory macrophage activation. Consistently, 4‐1BBL deficiency elevated mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation, accompanied by increased expression of metabolic genes in anti‐inflammatory macrophages. Transcriptomic analysis further revealed a shift towards anti‐inflammatory and oxidative metabolic gene signatures in IL‐4‐treated 4‐1BBL‐deficient macrophages. Importantly, the inhibition of 4‐1BBL signalling also augmented anti‐inflammatory responses in human monocytes and facilitated the transition from pro‐inflammatory to anti‐inflammatory phenotypes. Collectively, these findings establish 4‐1BBL as a crucial molecular switch that regulates macrophage polarisation by integrating inflammatory signalling with metabolic reprogramming, highlighting 4‐1BBL as a potential therapeutic target for promoting inflammation resolution.Keywords: 4‐1BB ligand, cell metabolism, inflammation, macrophage

Introduction

In response to various microenvironmental stimuli under physiological and pathological conditions, macrophages undergo classically triggered pro‐inflammatory (M1) responses or alternatively triggered anti‐inflammatory (M2) responses [1]. This macrophage polarisation is tightly regulated by several signalling pathways as well as transcriptional and post‐transcriptional regulatory networks. Emerging evidence suggests that cell metabolism plays a crucial role in macrophage polarisation. Glycolysis and fatty acid synthesis (FAS) contribute to pro‐inflammatory macrophage polarisation and the subsequent development of inflammatory diseases. In contrast, anti‐inflammatory macrophages rely on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and fatty acid oxidation (FAO), which are vital for resolving inflammation and promoting tissue repair [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. This suggests that diverse cellular events, including cell signalling, transcriptional regulation and cell metabolism, can regulate the activation and phenotype determination of macrophages.

Although inflammation is essential for maintaining homeostasis, uncontrolled or sustained inflammatory responses drive the development and progression of numerous inflammatory diseases [10, 11]. Previous studies identified a novel signalling mechanism mediated by 4‐1BB ligand (4‐1BBL), a member of the TNF superfamily, which is responsible for the sustained production of inflammatory cytokines in TLR signalling [12]. Upon TLR activation, 4‐1BBL expression is induced and subsequently interacts with TLRs to initiate secondary late‐phase signalling for sustained inflammatory cytokine expression. Notably, ablation or inhibition of 4‐1BBL signalling significantly reduces the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines by macrophages and ameliorates pathology in mouse models of inflammatory diseases [12, 13, 14, 15]. These results support the concept that 4‐1BBL contributes to the development of inflammatory diseases and that inhibiting 4‐1BBL signalling offers a potential therapeutic strategy.

In addition to regulating sustained inflammation, 4‐1BBL has recently been implicated in regulating macrophage polarisation and metabolism. Deletion or inhibition of 4‐1BBL signalling significantly reduced the induction of pro‐inflammatory responses, accompanied by impaired glycolysis and FAS. This suggests that 4‐1BBL regulates the metabolic reprogramming by altering inflammatory signals and the metabolic pathways that determine macrophage phenotypes [15]. However, it remains unclear whether 4‐1BBL signalling plays a role in regulating anti‐inflammatory responses in macrophages, thereby contributing to the resolution of inflammation.

This study demonstrates the role of 4‐1BBL in anti‐inflammatory responses in macrophages, and elucidates how 4‐1BBL regulates the induction of anti‐inflammatory responses. 4‐1BBL negatively regulates anti‐inflammatory responses by restraining IL‐4R‐mediated signalling and mitochondrial OXPHOS and FAO in macrophages. Furthermore, 4‐1BBL regulates the transcriptional programmes associated with anti‐inflammation and cell metabolism. These findings suggest a pivotal role for 4‐1BBL as a central regulator of macrophage polarisation and the phenotypic transition from a pro‐inflammatory to an anti‐inflammatory state, which is a critical determinant of inflammation resolution.

Methods and Materials

Cell Culture

Immortalised bone marrow‐derived macrophages were prepared as previously described [13]. Briefly, bone marrow cells obtained from the femurs of wildtype (WT) or 4‐1BBL‐deficient mice were cultured in M‐CSF (10 ng/mL) followed by infection with the retroviruses encoding v‐raf and v‐myc. Human leukaemia monocytic cell line THP‐1 cells were obtained from ATCC. Macrophages and THP‐1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics.

Materials

Recombinant mouse IL‐4 (214‐14), recombinant murine M‐CSF (315‐02), recombinant mouse IFN‐γ (315‐05), recombinant human IL‐4 (200‐04), recombinant human IL‐4 and recombinant human IFN‐γ (300‐02) were obtained from Gibco/Peprotech. Anti‐mouse 4‐1BBL Abs, recombinant mouse 4‐1BB‐Fc (937‐4B, m4‐1BB‐Fc) and recombinant human 4‐1BB‐Fc (838‐4B, h4‐1BB‐Fc) were purchased from R&D Systems. Anti‐phospho‐Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) (Tyr1034/1035, #3331) and anti‐phospho signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6) (Tyr641, #9361) Abs were purchased from Cell Signalling Technology, and anti‐GAPDH Abs (ab8245) were purchased from Abcam.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from Escherichia coli O111:B4 was obtained from List Labs. The JAK1 inhibitor abrocitinib (PF‐04965842) and the CPT1 inhibitor etomoxir were purchased from Selleckchem. Glucose, oligomycin A, carbonylcyanide‐p‐trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP), pyruvate, rotenone and antimycin A were obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich.

Measurement of Cytokine Concentrations

The concentrations of mouse IL‐10 and TGF‐β in culture supernatants were determined by ELISA according to the manufacturer's protocol (Thermo Fisher).

Quantitative RT‐PCR

Total RNA samples were prepared using an RNA extraction kit (Qiagen). Reverse transcription and quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis were conducted using SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase and PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher). The mRNA levels were calculated by normalising to Gapdh mRNA levels. The primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Extracellular Flux Analysis

Mitochondrial metabolism was measured by the Cellular Mitochondrial Stress Test using an extracellular flux analyser XFe96 (Seahorse Bioscience). The oxygen consumption rate (OCR, an indicator of mitochondrial respiration) was measured. Cells were resuspended in XF Base Media (DMEM, Seahorse Bioscience) supplemented with 2 mM glutamine and seeded in a 24‐well plate (Seahorse Bioscience) for 3 h at 37°C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2, and treated with medium or IL‐4. After 24 h, cells were sequentially treated with 2 μM oligomycin A, 1 μM FCCP + 1 mM pyruvate, and 1 μM rotenone + antimycin A to measure OCR.

RNA‐Sequencing Analysis

WT or 4‐1BBL knockout (KO) macrophages were treated with IL‐4 for 24 h, and total RNA was isolated. Preparation of cDNA library and transcriptome sequencing were conducted by Novogene Co. LTD (Davis, CA). To analyse and compare differentially expressed genes between IL‐4‐treated WT and 4‐1BBL KO macrophages, the Integrated Differential Expression and Pathway Analysis (iDEP) tool was used [16]. Differences in the expression of genes with |log2(FoldChange)| > 1 and adjusted p value < 0.05 were considered as differentially expressed. RNA‐sequencing (RNA‐Seq) data have been deposited in the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and are openly available under the accession number GSE325270 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE325273).

SDS–PAGE and Immunoblotting

Whole cell lysates were separated by SDS–PAGE, and the proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Anti‐phospho JAK‐1 (Tyr1034/1035, Cell Signalling Technologies), anti‐phospho STAT6 (Tyr641, Thermo Fisher) and anti‐4‐1BBL Abs (AF1246, R&D Systems) were used. GAPDH was detected as a protein loading control using anti‐GAPDH antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical significance was determined by a two‐tailed unpaired t‐test or a one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test using Prism software (version 10, GraphPad, San Diego, CA). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Each experiment was repeated as indicated in the figure legends; representative results are shown.

Results

Deletion of 4‐1BBL Augments the Expression of Anti‐Inflammation Genes in IL‐4‐Treated Macrophages

Previous studies have established that 4‐1BBL plays a critical role in sustaining inflammatory cytokine production and promoting pro‐inflammatory macrophage polarisation, thereby contributing to the development of inflammatory diseases [12, 13, 14, 15]. Although genetic ablation of 4‐1BBL or inhibition of 4‐1BBL signalling attenuates pro‐inflammatory responses and alleviates inflammatory disease pathology, its role in regulating anti‐inflammatory macrophage activation has not been fully defined.

To determine whether 4‐1BBL regulates the induction of anti‐inflammatory responses in macrophages, anti‐inflammation gene expression was examined. WT or 4‐1BBL KO macrophages were treated with IL‐4, and IL‐10 and TGF‐β levels in culture supernatants were measured after 24 h. The production of IL‐4‐induced anti‐inflammatory cytokines was significantly higher in 4‐1BBL KO macrophages than in WT cells. Consistently, Il10 and Tgfb mRNA induction was also markedly upregulated by 4‐1BBL KO deficiency (Figure 1A,B). Furthermore, the expression of anti‐inflammation genes, including Il10, Arg1, Fizz1 or Ym1, was elevated in IL‐4‐treated 4‐1BBL macrophages compared with WT cells (Figure 2A). These results indicate that 4‐1BBL negatively regulates the induction of anti‐inflammatory responses and the polarisation towards an anti‐inflammation phenotype in macrophages upon IL‐4 stimulation.

4‐1BBL Negatively Regulates IL‐4R–JAK1–STAT6 Signalling

Next, the mechanism by which 4‐1BBL regulates IL‐4R‐mediated activation of JAK1‐STAT6 signalling was investigated. IL‐4 treatment induced the phosphorylation of JAK1 and STAT6 in WT macrophages, whereas ablation of 4‐1BBL significantly enhanced the activation of this JAK1‐STAT6 cascade (Figure 2A). Notably, IL‐4 stimulation induced the expression of 4‐1bbl in WT macrophages, with maximal expression observed at 12 h, whereas Il10 expression peaked earlier at 6 h. This result suggests a feedback mechanism wherein 4‐1BBL negatively regulates anti‐inflammatory responses (Figure 2B). Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of JAK1 using abrocitinib (PF‐04965842) significantly reduced IL‐4‐induced Arg1 expression in both WT and 4‐1BBL KO macrophages (Figure 2C), confirming the requirement of JAK1 signalling for anti‐inflammatory gene induction. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that IL‐4‐induced 4‐1BBL limits the further promotion of anti‐inflammatory macrophage activation, supporting its role as a regulator of both pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory macrophage responses.

4‐1BBL Regulates the Metabolic Reprogramming of Anti‐Inflammatory Macrophages

While pro‐inflammatory macrophages largely rely on glycolysis and FAS, anti‐inflammatory macrophages maintain an intact TCA cycle and increase OXPHOS and FAO for ATP generation, supported by elevated expression of genes related to fatty acid uptake, transport and oxidation [17, 18]. To assess potential changes in mitochondrial function, the OCR of IL‐4‐stimulated macrophages was measured using Seahorse extracellular flux analysis. The OCR of IL‐4‐treated 4‐1BBL KO macrophages was significantly higher in response to FCCP compared with WT cells (Figure 2D), indicating enhanced mitochondrial respiratory capacity.

A previous study demonstrated that 4‐1BBL deficiency reduced not only the expression of genes associated with pro‐inflammation, glycolysis and FAS, but also the rate of glycolysis in LPS‐treated macrophages, suggesting a role of 4‐1BBL in the pro‐inflammatory metabolic reprogramming of macrophages [15]. To determine how 4‐1BBL deficiency affects the anti‐inflammatory metabolic reprogramming, the expression of metabolism genes was compared between IL‐4‐treated WT and 4‐1BBL KO macrophages. The expression of Socs1 (suppressor of cytokine signalling 1) and Socs3, which are involved in IL‐4R signalling, was elevated in 4‐1BBL KO macrophages compared with WT cells. Additionally, the IL‐4‐induced expression of FAO‐related genes, Acaca (Acetyl‐CoA carboxylase 1) and Cpt1a (Carnitine palmitoyltransferase IA), was elevated in 4‐1BBL KO macrophages. Indeed, inhibition of CPT1 activity by Etomoxir significantly reduced the increased expression of Arg1 in WT and 4‐1BBL KO macrophages (Figure 2B), demonstrating that FAO is required for 4‐1BBL‐dependent regulation of anti‐inflammatory gene expression. In contrast, the expression of Cox10 (Cytochrome C Oxidase Assembly Factor Heme A:Farnesyltransferase 10) in mitochondria was reduced in IL‐4‐treated 4‐1BBL KO macrophages (Figure 2E). These results support the finding that 4‐1BBL negatively regulates anti‐inflammatory responses and FAO via IL‐4R signalling in macrophages.

4‐1BBL Differentially Regulates the Gene Expression in IL‐4R‐Mediated Induction of Anti‐Inflammatory Macrophages

To further characterise the transcriptional changes regulated by 4‐1BBL, the RNA‐seq analysis was performed on IL‐4–treated WT and 4‐1BBL KO macrophages. Compared with WT macrophages, the expression of Arg1 (Arginase 1) and Mrc1 (Mannose receptor 1), as well as the transcription factors Socs1, Socs3 and Klf4 (Krüppel‐like factor 4), was significantly elevated in IL‐4‐treated 4‐1BBL KO macrophages (Figure 3A,B). Notably, the expression of Pparg (peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor gamma), a transcription factor essential for OXPHOS and FAO enzyme expression, and Slc7a2 (solute carrier family 7 member 2), an inducible transmembrane transporter for cationic amino acids, particularly L‐arginine, was elevated in 4‐1BBL KO macrophages, supporting the notion that 4‐1BBL deficiency enhances anti‐inflammatory responses. In addition, the expression of OXPHOS and FAO genes such as Atp10a (ATPase class V type 10A), Abca9 (ATP‐binding cassette sub‐family A member 9), and Cpt1a was elevated in IL‐4‐treated 4‐1BBL KO macrophages, which is consistent with the enhanced mitochondrial respiration in the Seahorse analysis (Figure 3A,C). In contrast, the expression of glucose‐ and FAS‐related genes such as Sdh (Serine dehydratase) and Fasn (Fatty acid synthase) was significantly reduced in 4‐1BBL‐deficient macrophages compared with WT cells. These transcriptomic data further support a role for 4‐1BBL in regulating anti‐inflammatory transcriptional and metabolic reprogramming in macrophages.

Inhibition of 4‐1BBL Signalling Augments the Induction of Anti‐Inflammatory Responses in Macrophage

Previous studies have shown that inhibition of 4‐1BBL signalling significantly reduces pro‐inflammatory responses and macrophage polarisation towards a pro‐inflammatory phenotype [12, 14, 15]. Treatment of macrophages with anti‐4‐1BBL Ab or a recombinant m4‐1BB‐Fc (a fusion protein of the human Ig Fc and the extracellular binding domain of mouse 4‐1BB) reduced cytokine production and altered the pro‐inflammatory metabolic reprogramming of macrophages. Moreover, inhibition of 4‐1BBL alleviated the pathology of sepsis, inflammatory bowel disease, arthritis and psoriasis in mice by inhibiting sustained inflammatory responses in macrophages [12, 13, 14, 15]. Since m4‐1BB‐Fc exhibits greater binding affinity for 4‐1BBL than the anti‐4‐1BBL Ab and demonstrates superior therapeutic efficacy, the induction of anti‐inflammatory macrophages by 4‐1BB‐Fc was examined. Treatment with 4‐1BB‐Fc elevated the IL‐4‐induced production of IL‐10 and TGF‐β in vitro. Additionally, the expression levels of anti‐inflammation macrophage markers were elevated by 4‐1BBL inhibition, recapitulating the phenotype observed in 4‐1BBL KO macrophages (Figure 4A–C).

Treatment of macrophages with IL‐4 and IL‐13 synergistically induces alternative macrophage activation and activates the STAT3 and STAT6 pathways, thereby regulating the expression of several anti‐inflammation markers, including Mrc‐1 and Arg1 [19]. Although IL‐4R signalling alone can induce anti‐inflammation marker expression, co‐treatment with IL‐13 can recruit STAT1 signalling via the type II receptor pathway [20, 21, 22]. Therefore, the role of 4‐1BBL in regulating anti‐inflammatory responses in IL‐4/IL‐13‐treated macrophages. The expression of Il0 and Arg1 was significantly elevated in IL‐4/IL‐13‐treated 4‐1BBL KO macrophages compared with WT cells (Figure 4D). Consistently, inhibition of 4‐1BBL signalling by m4‐1BB‐Fc also elevated their expression in WT macrophages (Figure 4E).

We further examined the translational potential of targeting 4‐1BBL signalling in human monocytes. Inhibition of 4‐1BBL signalling by human 4‐1BB‐Fc (h4‐1BB‐Fc) augmented Il10 and Arg1 expression in IL‐4/IL‐13‐treated THP‐1 monocytes (Figure 4F).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that 4‐1BBL signalling negatively regulates anti‐inflammatory responses, as inhibition of 4‐1BBL promoted the polarisation of macrophages towards an anti‐inflammatory phenotype.

Inhibition of 4‐1BBL Signalling Facilitates the Transition of Macrophage Phenotypes

Under certain environmental conditions or in response to molecular cues, macrophages can differentiate into pro‐ or anti‐inflammatory phenotypes and can trans‐differentiate into other cell types, which plays a crucial role in wound healing [23]. To assess whether inhibition of 4‐1BBL facilitates macrophage phenotype transition, WT macrophages were first primed with medium or LPS + IFN‐γ for 24 h, and followed by medium, IL‐4 or IL‐4 + 4‐1BB‐Fc stimulation for 24 h. Similarly, phorbol myristate acetate (PMA)‐differentiated THP‐1 cells were treated with medium or LPS + IFN‐γ for 24 h, followed by treatment with medium, human IL‐4/IL‐13 or human IL‐4/IL‐13 + h4‐1BB‐Fc. The expression of Il10 and Arg1 was not significantly elevated in LPS + IFN‐γ‐treated cells. However, the addition of IL‐4 to mouse macrophages (Figure 5A) or IL‐4/IL‐13 to THP‐1 cells (Figure 5B) elevated the expression of anti‐inflammation genes, while inhibition of 4‐1BBL signalling further augmented the expression levels of Il10 and Arg1. These results suggest that 4‐1BBL acts as a molecular switch regulating the phenotypic transition of macrophages from a pro‐ to an anti‐inflammatory state.

In conclusion, 4‐1BBL functions as a metabolic checkpoint that contributes to macrophage polarisation by regulating inflammatory signalling and metabolic reprogramming. 4‐1BBL promotes pro‐inflammatory polarisation via the TLR‐mediated pathway by driving glycolysis and FAS. On the other hand, 4‐1BBL negatively regulates anti‐inflammatory macrophage responses that rely on mitochondrial metabolism and FAO. Collectively, these findings highlight that 4‐1BBL acts as an inflammation‐state‐specific immune checkpoint that governs the metabolic and molecular reprogramming in macrophage‐driven inflammatory processes.

Discussion

Previous studies have established a critical role for 4‐1BBL in sustaining inflammatory responses in macrophages and promoting the development of inflammatory diseases. The activation of TLR signalling induces the expression of 4‐1BBL, which further triggers the late phase of macrophage activation for the sustained production of inflammatory cytokines. In addition, 4‐1BBL has been shown to promote pro‐inflammatory macrophage polarisation by driving glycolysis and FAS. However, the contribution of 4‐1BBL signalling to anti‐inflammatory macrophage responses has remained unclear.

This study identifies 4‐1BBL as a crucial regulator of the molecular switching of macrophages, a process integrated with metabolic reprogramming under various inflammation conditions. In contrast to its pro‐inflammatory role, this study further reveals that 4‐1BBL restrains the induction and polarisation of anti‐inflammatory macrophages. This dual function of 4‐1BBL suggests its role as a checkpoint molecule that regulates the induction and resolution of inflammation depending on the inflammation state.

In IL‐4R signalling, 4‐1BBL negatively regulates the receptor‐mediated JAK1‐STAT6 cascade, thereby limiting the expression of anti‐inflammatory genes. The phosphorylation level of JAK1 was higher in 4‐1BBL KO macrophages compared with WT cells, and JAK1 inhibition reduced the Arg1 expression in WT and 4‐1BBL KO macrophages, supporting the role of 4‐1BBL in the JAK1‐dependent regulation of anti‐inflammatory responses. IL‐4 treatment induced Il10 expression, followed by upregulated 41bbl expression in macrophages. Il10 expression peaked earlier than 41bbl, and subsequently decreased as the 4‐1BBL expression increased, suggesting that 4‐1BBL contributes to a negative feedback mechanism that limits the magnitude and duration of anti‐inflammatory responses. This regulatory mechanism may serve to prevent excessive inflammation.

Anti‐inflammatory macrophage activation is tightly associated with mitochondrial OXPHOS and FAO [24, 25, 26]. Consistently, 4‐1BBL deficiency enhanced mitochondrial respiratory capacity and elevated the expression of FAO‐related genes, such as Acaca and Cpt1a, in macrophages. Additionally, the inhibition of CPT1 activity significantly reduced anti‐inflammatory gene expression, supporting the importance of FAO in the 4‐1BBL‐mediated regulation process. These results reveal the previously unidentified role for 4‐1BBL in anti‐inflammatory macrophage activation, suggesting a broader role for 4‐1BBL in regulating the molecular switching of phenotypes and metabolic reprogramming of macrophages under various inflammation conditions.

Transcriptomic profiling further revealed the transcriptional changes in anti‐inflammatory responses by 4‐1BBL deficiency. IL‐4‐treated 4‐1BBL KO macrophages exhibited markedly elevated expression of anti‐inflammatory markers and transcription factor genes, which are consistent with enhanced STAT‐mediated signalling pathways that drive the expression of anti‐inflammatory cytokines and markers [27]. Notably, the expression of Pparg was elevated in 4‐1BBL KO macrophages. IL‐4 stimulation activates STAT6 (Figure 2A), which further activates PPARγ, leading to the expression of OXPHOS and FAO enzyme genes [28, 29, 30]. In addition, the increased expression of Slc7a2 is associated with elevated levels of Arg1, because SLC7A2 facilitates the intracellular transport of l‐arginine, a substrate of Arginase 1. Although SLC7A2 plays a role in the production of nitric oxide (NO) by inducible NO synthase, increased arginase 1 levels result in the hydrolysis of arginine into ornithine and urea, limiting the production of NO and driving macrophages towards an anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotype [31, 32, 33]. Consistently, the levels of OXPHOS and FAO‐associated genes were higher in 4‐1BBL KO macrophages compared with WT cells, which aligns with the notion that anti‐inflammatory macrophage polarisation is tightly associated with mitochondrial metabolism and FAO [18, 34, 35]. Conversely, the expression of glycolysis or FAS genes was rather decreased in 4‐1BBL KO cells, further supporting that the role of 4‐1BBL plays in pro‐inflammation‐associated macrophage metabolism.

Treatment with 4‐1BB‐Fc significantly augmented IL‐4‐induced expression of anti‐inflammatory cytokines, as well as anti‐inflammation markers, reminiscent of the phenotype found in 4‐1BBL‐deficient macrophages. This study demonstrates that 4‐1BBL regulates the induction of anti‐inflammatory responses, extending the concept of 4‐1BBL targeting strategy as inhibition of 4‐1BBL signalling not only reduces pro‐inflammatory responses but also actively promotes anti‐inflammatory macrophage polarisation. The transition of macrophage phenotypes from a pro‐inflammatory to an anti‐inflammatory state is an essential process for tissue repair and the resolution of inflammation [1, 36]. Furthermore, this study demonstrates that inhibition of 4‐1BBL signalling by mouse or human 4‐1BB‐Fc facilitates this transition induced by IL‐4 or IL‐4/IL‐13, even in macrophages primed with strong pro‐inflammatory stimuli. These findings establish that 4‐1BBL plays a role as a molecular switch regulating phenotype transition and inflammation resolution.

In conclusion, this study reveals 4‐1BBL as a previously unidentified regulator of anti‐inflammatory macrophage activation and metabolic reprogramming. Collectively, these findings provide new mechanistic insight into our understanding of innate immune regulation and highlight the importance of 4‐1BBL as a metabolic checkpoint in maintaining inflammatory homeostasis.
