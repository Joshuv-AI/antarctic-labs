Preface
This is the third edition in the National Laboratory of the Rockies’ (NLR’s) Automated Mobility
District Implementation Research report series. This overview of NLR’s Phase III research
places its focus on system-level elements of an automated mobility district (AMD) beyond the
automated driving system (ADS) in each automated/autonomous vehicle (AV). As broad
implementation of fully automated mobility systems operating on public roadways edges nearer,
with some commercial systems already running or in final startup testing in dozens of major U.S.
cities at the time of this report’s publishing, the system-level aspects beyond the maturation of
automated driving systems are becoming increasingly critical.
The first edition of this effort was published in 2020 and documented insights gained from
monitoring 10 early deployment sites of AV technology. The second edition of the catalog was
published in 2022, during the COVID-19 pandemic. The second edition updated the progress of
the demonstration pilot projects beyond the initial deployment phase and assessed common
trends of technology development and deployment.
This third edition in the research report series, as in the first two editions, continues to track the
10 early deployment sites, only a few of which are still active. However, the research findings in
this overview report also address Phase III research which begins to draw significantly from
parallel fields for system-level insights into the future of AV fleets providing public mobility
within AMDs, and the associated processes that are critical beyond performance of the ADS.
These parallel fields include vehicle electrification, proliferation of on-demand transit, and
simulation studies of automated transit networks. The five primary research themes within this
third edition are:
 • Impact and implication of the rise of on-demand transit on AMDs
 • Challenge of operating multiple AV fleets in an integrated and coordinated fashion
 • Battery electric vehicle fleet charging strategy and infrastructure
 • Station/curbfront configurations and operations
 • Systems engineering methodology for analysis and assessment.
The content of this third edition report is organized into four sections which provide the
foundational background from the prior two editions and a summary of the Phase III research
findings. Included in this overview is an introduction and functional definition of an AMD that
brackets all three editions. The 10 early site deployments are identified, with updates given for
select sites along with a summary and discussion of Phase II cardinal principles. The last section
is a summary of Phase III research that spans the topic areas above.
The editions that follow in the AMD implementation report series will address the details of
these Phase III research topics; this material serves as “primer”-level material on the basic
elements of AV fleet deployment within an AMD. These subsequent editions will present the
base material for the insights and conclusions related to on-demand transit operations,
electrification of the AV fleet, station design, and system operational impacts of AVs.

A brief synopsis of the AMD implementation research program that provides a high-level
summary of what is covered in this third edition overview report has also been published,
available at docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy26osti/93107.pdf.

1 Introduction
The transportation industry is entering a new frontier with three simultaneous major
transformations: electrification, automation, and on-demand mobility services. All three directly
affect the same spaces of transport vehicle technology, roadway infrastructure, and multimodal
system operational performance. Further, each area of change adds its own driving forces of
complexity to the others’ design challenges. The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) has
been studying the related impacts of electrification, automation, and on-demand service on
automated/autonomous vehicle (AV) fleets providing public mobility services operating within
dense urban environments. Each of these factors in turn affects the emerging future of the
multimodal transportation system in the built environment.

The initial phases of NLR’s research surveyed early initiatives of AV technology demonstration,
with Phase I research documenting 10 early deployment sites in the first edition of The
Automated Mobility District Implementation Catalog (Young and Lott 2020). The 10 sites
spanned settings that included urban districts, sports complexes, university campuses, and
master-planned residential communities. When published in 2020, it compiled lessons learned
from the initial phase of demonstration pilot projects, primarily from the view of the sponsoring
public agencies, with further insight included from the perspectives of the operating companies
and AV technology developers. The first edition is available at
docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy20osti/76551.pdf.

Subsequently, Phase II research collected and evaluated the lessons learned from these early
deployment sites, with particular attention to fleet operations, impacts of service reliability, and
vehicle technology evolution as the field of companies was being progressively winnowed by the
challenges of full automation. The Phase II research findings were documented in the second
edition of The Automated Mobility District Implementation Catalog (Young and Lott 2022). The
second edition used a framework of “cardinal principles” gleaned primarily from common
observations and assessments of the 10 early deployment sites for the safe and efficient
application of AV technology in managed fleet deployments and the projection of those
principles to future AMDs at scale. The second edition is available at
docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy22osti/83276.pdf.

Both Phase I and Phase II emphasized the context of AV technology deployment and passenger
transport services within a dense urban setting and other major activity centers with intense
passenger origin-destination demand patterns, such as those found in large business districts,
airports, and university and medical campuses.

This third edition of the report series provides an overview of the most significant findings of
Phase I and Phase II research as well as an overview of the Phase III research findings. This
report also contains an update on the 10 early AV deployment sites that have been tracked
throughout the automated mobility district (AMD) implementation research to this point. As
such, this report is intended to be a stand-alone summary of all three phases. The full details of
the Phase III research will be published as subsequent editions in the report series.

The Phase III research has examined battery electric vehicles (BEVs) operating in a fully
automated fleet within an AMD setting—typically with multiple fleets providing different types

 1

of mobility services, many of which are dispatched on demand as the vehicle fleet operations
dynamically respond to rider requests.

Drawing from this focused examination, Phase III studies have further assessed the implications
of the simultaneous transformations of automation, electrification, and on-demand services for
AVs operating as a “multimodal” transportation system within a large AMD. Topics investigated
have included the comparisons and contrasts between traditional fixed-route transit services with
those of on-demand transit (ODT) services. The investigations have also addressed the trade-offs
between BEV vehicle range (specifically battery capacity and the ability to add additional charge
during service activity), charging parameters, power availability, and operations predictability
with respect to policy and infrastructure configurations.

In addition, Phase III research has examined the criticality of station operations, also referred to
as curb management depending on the reference context. Lessons and insights from simulations
of automated transit network (ATN) studies inform principles of station design for robust AMD
operation. Configuration and management of a given transport system’s boarding and alighting
stations (often called pickup and drop-off [PUDO] zones) and their impact on overall system
throughput and performance are analyzed. The integration of electrification into stations and the
use of infrastructure intelligence within station/curbfront boarding zones have also been
addressed.

Finally, findings and conclusions have been drawn from Phase III research with respect to
system integration and analysis needs for AMDs. This forms the basis for what is proposed as a
systems engineering methodology (SEM) necessary to adequately define the resilience and
sustainability of mobility systems and their infrastructure within any large-scale AMD
implementations that are anticipated to occur over the next 5–10 years.

2 The Concept of an Automated Mobility District
Over the past 2 centuries, our modern society has been continuously trending toward the
concentration of residential, business, and entertainment developments within dense urban
settings as we evolved from a primarily agrarian society to industrialization—and now to an
information-centric economy. In turn, this concentration of our population toward major urban
districts has also been accompanied by similar densification of major activity centers such as
medical centers and university campuses, sports and entertainment complexes, and airports and
major intermodal transportation hubs.

A new dynamic has appeared in this evolution of our urban lifestyles as technology transforms
the way we move to, from, and within these dense urban settings. The research endeavors
discussed throughout this report refer to this urban transformation as forming AMDs, where
automated mobility technologies are envisioned to replace multiple manually driven forms of
mobility—primarily the private automobile—providing safer and more time-, energy-, and
space-efficient transport to enhance our quality of life. Within these AMDs, transport by a
variety of modes would comprise primary modes, facilitating readily accessible movement
throughout the district. Access to automated modes will minimize reliance on privately owned
vehicles for most local trips, freeing up valuable real estate otherwise allocated for private
vehicle storage to instead be used for more efficient and denser development while relieving the
mobility system users of the overhead of maintaining, operating, and parking a vehicle.

2.1 Functional Definition of an Automated Mobility District
The first edition of the AMD catalog defined an automated mobility district as follows:

 a geographically confined district or campus-sized implementation of connected and
 automated vehicle technology for the purpose of publicly accessible mobility by which
 all the potential benefits of fully automated mobility services can be realized.
This functional definition of an AMD illustrates in effect a “system of systems” that spans the
rolling stock of vehicles, the roadways on which they operate, the stations or curbfront boarding
and alighting zones, and the computing and communications systems that manage operations and
provide wayfinding and other services to passengers.

Based on the progress of the last decade with respect to AV technology and a growing number of
initial system deployments in the planning phase—as well as at least a dozen commercial AV
ride-hailing service currently operating—fully functional AMDs may be realized as soon as 2030
in many locations. It is becoming equally clear that the path forward to accomplish this goal on a
large scale will require new perspectives on what constitutes a district-scale “system” and the
services provided within.

The convergence of transportation system automation and electrification has been an area of
research for more than 6 years at NLR. With respect to AMDs, this research focuses on how
AVs, when deployed as light-duty BEV fleets providing on-demand mobility services, can
provide the foundational transport mode from which AMDs will begin to be created and scaled.

As core technologies continue to develop and mature, the timeline for AMD implementation
becomes clearer. NLR’s ongoing studies in related fields continue to explore implications from

 3

the convergence of 1) the electrification of light-duty fleets deployed with BEV propulsion
systems, 2) the automation of fleets for moving people and goods, and 3) the expectation,
proliferation, and adoption of on-demand passenger and goods movement.

Figure 1 illustrates the envisioned components within an AMD system serving a district, which
includes first-mile/last-mile (FMLM) connections to regional transit at nearby intermodal
stations. Although AMDs provide local transportation, accompanying regional transit services
interconnect AMDs with other communities and districts and are essential for travelers to reach
other parts of the region via high-capacity and higher-speed transport services, including higher-
occupancy transport modes such as traditional buses, light rail, or metro rail systems. Automated
electrified transport within the AMD that is well connected to conventional regional transit is
essential for meeting environmental goals, even when these long-distance transport modes still
use nonautomated technologies.

Within the district, the AV fleets provide circulation and FMLM services using lower-passenger-
capacity vehicles with direct origin-to-destination service using telecommunications and
computing technology similar to those that power modern ride-hailing services. This AMD
framework includes private vehicle parking facilities at locations around the perimeter for
commuters using private automobiles. The reduction—and perhaps eventual elimination—of
human-controlled vehicles will increase safety for both passengers and vulnerable road users
within the district.

Figure 1 also illustrates how the pedestrian system within the district is a key component,
completing the connectivity for every person-trip that has its origin or destination within the
district. The representation of pedestrian facilities incorporates the full spectrum of other active
transportation modes, including bicycles, scooters, and other such private or shared
micromobility that could be used within the district.

2.2 The Justification for AMDs
The premise of AMDs is that large-scale deployment of AV mobility services in the near to
medium term will occur primarily in areas with sufficient development density to provide a
viable population of potential users and destinations to serve. Although mobility providers within
an AMD can aim for profitability from operations, as with other forms of transportation, these
systems depend on public investment and infrastructural resources for operation. Environments
for which AMD operations may be suited—such as dense urban settings, business and residential
districts, and major activity centers like airports and university or medical campuses—exhibit
dense and dispersed patterns of demand for relatively short person-trips. In many of these areas,
mobility demand has been served primarily by private automobiles, which often fail to provide
high-quality service because of slow speeds, congestion, and limited parking. These factors are
notable for being part of the catalyst behind the interest and commercial success of ride-hailing
companies over the past decade. Positioning the AMD’s AV circulation services as ubiquitous
and readily accessible within an operating area can ensure the district can be most efficiently
served by its automated mobility systems.

Within the dense and dispersed origins and destinations of a geofenced AMD district, trips are
relatively short—allowing fleets to meet demand using rightsized vehicles, typically lightweight
small- to medium-sized vehicles. AMD management also aims to minimize “deadheading,” a
term denoting the supporting portion of a trip during which no passenger is aboard a vehicle as it
moves to serve a pickup request through applications of advanced vehicle assignment and
routing algorithms. One advantage of AMDs in locations with development environments having

 5

dense populations and potential destinations is that mobility needs can be met without vehicle
ownership and at greater convenience than the status quo. In addition, trip requests can be served
by multiple AV fleets, operated by various entities, either public or private, and complemented
by a rich mix of other manual modes including micromobility.

The hard realities of overcoming the technical challenges of designing and deploying a fully
automated AV-based transport system have been highlighted in the last several years, as many
startup AV companies have ceased operations or turned their development to other aspects of
robotics. Even very large companies with substantial financial resources to devote to research
and development of AV technology have been withdrawing from the prospective new market or
scaling back their investments over the last few years because of the considerable challenges.
However, some companies have “stayed the course” and are progressing from early deployment
and demonstrations to initial scaling of services.

The business case for AMD implementation depends on the balance of capital and operating
costs with the revenue streams generated by the services as well as agreements with public
authorities in which AMDs may operate. Various private (nonautomated) transportation services
such as taxis, limousine services, and commercial ride-hailing services such as Uber and Lyft
(referred to herein as transportation network companies [TNCs]) operate for profit, whereas the
revenue side of public transit services typically relies on subsidies in addition to fare charges.
Public costs also exist for private transportation services, such as the access to public right-of-
way and street infrastructure, which may not be as overt as for public modes. Other modes of
transportation are integrated into the value of a commercial development and are offered for free.
For example, parking is often subsidized by other aspects of a development, such as having
parking costs validated by a retail entity, and internal movement within a facility such as
elevators, escalators, and moving walkways is amortized into the cost of building development.
In these situations, the value in the investment of a transportation system is realized, or returned,
apart from ridership fares.

Now that mature automated driving systems (ADS) are becoming a reality, additional challenges
must be met for a successful business case for deploying AV technology on a large scale within
an AMD. One additional challenge is addressing accessibility across various population
subgroups. The cost of AV technology deployment combined with the associated provisions for
Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) compliance can conflict with the need to offer transport
fare at sufficiently low prices that all economic levels of the population can use the services yet
high enough to maintain adequate return on investment. Part of the answer in achieving
comparable access to all groups may be in providing subsidized fares for some who are
economically disadvantaged and unsubsidized, full-fare pricing for those who can readily afford
this service—as is applied to nutrition in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program.
Another approach may be that the transport service within a district is freely available to all—in
much the same way that elevators, escalators, or moving walkways function in large buildings
and facilities—with related costs absorbed in the operational budget of the facility.

The business case for AMDs relies on the concentrated demand for mobility services in the
district in which the automated mobility services are deployed, with perhaps all or a portion of
the costs of AMD services absorbed within the overall operating budget of the development in a
manner similar to elevators, escalators and other public conveniences, addressing the failure of

 6

existing modes to provide high-quality service. AMDs are envisioned as being able to improve
mobility for people who reside or work in the area served by improving user access and
convenience, reducing costs at multiple scales, and creating a new paradigm for mobility. This is
done in part by reducing traffic and parking congestion, emissions, and barriers to accessibility—
all of which helps justify district-level AMD implementations where the relatively high capital
cost of the AV fleets, operations centers, supplemental infrastructure, and associated staff
supporting fully automated modes will be primarily driven by (and amortized over) the high
density of trips that begin and end within the same district and their associated economic
activities.

2.3 Blended AV Transport for Complete Mobility
AV and electric drivetrain technologies (battery electric and hydrogen fuel cell electric), as well
as other alternative fuels, are being applied to a variety of vehicle sizes and types. Likewise, the
integration of these technologies into public transport spans the spectrum of vehicle sizes and
types, service modes, and vehicle dispatching concepts. With respect to vehicle sizes and classes,
automation and electrification have applications ranging from small automotive classes of
vehicles to larger minivan and van classes in addition to classic medium- to large-size buses. The
AV fleet emphasis for AMDs is primarily on small- and medium-sized vehicles to provide for
community circulation and FMLM service to regional transit services that typically use medium-
to large-sized vehicles.
Public transit ridership service has traditionally been associated with fixed-schedule, fixed-route
systems served by buses and light rail but is now expanding to include real-time, “demand
response” dispatching similar to ride-hailing. With business models and technology initially
seeded by the ride-hailing industry, public mobility equivalents are becoming prevalent. This
form of on-demand transit (ODT) mobility (referred to as microtransit in some contexts)
efficiently serves trips with relatively close origins and destinations within a defined area. Such
services are being rapidly adopted in low- to mid-density environments spanning rural, exurban,
and suburban environments where fixed-route, fixed-schedule traditional transit is impractical or
inefficient. Even urbanized areas with well-established regional transit based on fixed-route,
fixed-schedule bus or rail technology are beginning to integrate ODT to service lower-density
suburbs where fixed-route service is inefficient and provide community circulation services in
high-density regions and connection to larger-vehicle regional transit services. In this context,
the service model is no longer fixed routes or schedules; mobility is provided by individually
dispatched small- to medium-sized vehicles operating on a range of demand scenarios from real-
time on-demand (similar to most ride-hailing commercial services) to a ride request placed in
advance for a future trip, perhaps 30–90 minutes in advance. In some ways, modern ODT
services can be described as having evolved from paratransit and taxi service to use modern
computing and communications for higher service levels and flexibility. Fixed-route, fixed-
schedule AV service may still be used within a district, even with larger-sized vehicles, but this
service would be limited to interconnecting high-demand activity centers within the district such
as shopping plazas and mobility hubs servicing regional transit services—with most of the
dispersed demand within the district serviced by ODT.

servicing a district. AV transit services are deployed to supply various forms of on-demand
dispatched services as well as conventional transit services along fixed routes. This is in parallel

ODT services both real-time trip requests, comparable to commercial ride-hailing, as well as

2.4 AMD as an Integrated System of Networks
Since the publication of the second edition, public agencies have increased the adoption of ODT
services, rapidly proliferating across urban, suburban, exurban, and rural communities and
providing community circulation and FMLM connectivity to regional transit services. ODT is
quickly setting the expectation that future public mobility service will, at its base, provide app-
based, dynamic, on-demand service for local community circulation—using both commercial
entities (TNCs) and services provided by or through a public authority. Having ODT as the
foundational mode in an AMD has several implications for operational management, station
operations, and infrastructure.

The Phase III research has drawn from previous analytical studies of a fully automated type of
transit technology that is specifically designed to provide the on-demand class of dispatching in
real time for direct origin-to-destination service on a network of fixed guideways. The
ANSI/ASCE/T&DI 21-21 Automated People Mover Standards (2021 edition) has named this
class of technology “automated transit network” (ATN) systems. These ATN analytical studies,
dating from the 1990s to the 2010s, provide insight into AMD networks with respect to the
impacts of ridership demand patterns, operational concepts, system configuration, and station
management when an AMD includes ODT-type services. The lessons learned from these ATN
studies are referenced in this third edition at high summary level, with the next edition in the
report series containing a more detailed explanation of the relevant applications to AV fleet
operations in AMDs.

NLR’s AMD research includes multiple types of public transport services using AV technology
across this full spectrum of vehicle types, service modes, and dispatching methods to assess the
ramifications for large-scale AMD implementation. As described throughout this report, the
AMD concept envisions multiple AV operating fleets, each with different vehicle sizes and
service modes, which will likely involve a blend of public and private companies managing the
multifleet operations.

To reflect this concept as a “system of systems,” the term AMD network system is introduced
to describe this whole spectrum of solutions involving transport technology and fleet operations.
It includes the anticipation of ODT services at the foundation providing local community
circulation and FMLM connectivity to regional mobility services that are blended into a
complete mobility system. Within this third edition of the AMD catalog, an AMD network
system is defined as follows:

 An AMD network system represents a system of multiple AV fleet applications
 across the spectrum of vehicle sizes and types; operating within a geographically
 defined district; providing fully automated, on-demand, and fixed-schedule
 transport services; and fully integrated with regional high-capacity transit service,
 existing manual modes serving the district, and the various forms of pedestrian
 and micromobility activity within the district.
To further define various on-demand services that will be common in an AMD network system,
two additional terms will be used throughout this third edition as “operating modes”:

 9

 • Real-time, on-demand dispatch mode: Mobility services that respond without an
 advance reservation but rather provide a demand response of the system for an immediate
 pickup of the passenger as soon as possible.
 • Scheduled, on-demand dispatch mode: Mobility services that are scheduled in advance
 with a prescribed time for pickup of the passenger.
Through the advancement of AV technology, public transit agencies may ultimately be able to
cost-effectively operate fleets of the smallest vehicle sizes to provide private rides for single
travel parties that fully complement and integrate with the fleets of larger transit vehicles
providing shared-ride passenger service for longer regional access. It is acknowledged, however,
that the implementation of an AMD network system, particularly in the near term, will be a blend
of transport services operating both automatically and under human control.

3.2.1 Arlington RAPID Service
RAPID is a form of on-demand AV fleet service embedded within Arlington’s citywide shared-
ride microtransit service called Arlington On-Demand. As shown in Figure 7, this automated
transit service operated from March 2021 to May 2025 within an area that surrounds the
University of Texas at Arlington campus and downtown Arlington.

RAPID service is designed to provide shared-ride service for multiple parties in real-time, on-
demand dispatch operating mode. It is similar to what is also being called “robotaxis” but
provides shared or pooled rides as opposed to single-party service. RAPID provides a direct ride
from the passenger’s origin to their destination or with only a few stops for other shared-ride
passengers along the special travel route.

The RAPID vehicle fleet was comprised automated/autonomous five-passenger minivans
equipped with an ADS designed and installed by May Mobility. One of the Toyota Sienna
Autono-MaaS minivans used for the fleet was modified to provide a wheelchair ramp for ADA-
suitable access. The RAPID service area was embedded within the larger operational area of the
Arlington On-Demand service operating with conventional minivans and drivers. The large
Arlington On-Demand system is a shared-ride microtransit service that operates throughout the
entire city of Arlington (approximately 100 square miles) as its primary public transit service
serving the city of approximately 400,000 residents with a fleet of roughly 65 vehicles, 4 of
which make up the AV RAPID program.

All vehicles in the RAPID fleet were hybrid electric, and approximately two-thirds of the larger
Arlington On-Demand vehicle fleet are also hybrid electric vehicles. Arlington’s use of hybrid
vehicles does not require any battery charging infrastructure for vehicle propulsion purposes.
Several systems, including Arlington On-Demand, have aspirations for broader implementation
of BEVs but do not currently deploy them.

3.2.3 Rivium District ParkShuttle
The fleet of AVs operating at the Rivium business and residential district in the Netherlands,

supervisory control system. The gate arms lower to prevent traffic and pedestrians from crossing
the transitway when a fleet AV is approaching—a type of “transit priority” safety provision that
has worked over the life of the system’s operations.

The most significant hurdles to efficient AV operations faced by the Mines Rover AV
deployment were as follows:

 • AV shuttle’s speed differential with other conventional vehicles. This issue provides
 one of the most relevant challenges to any AV deployment where the design for the fleet
 AV’s operating speeds is lower than the typical operating speeds of other conventional,
 human-operated vehicles. With the vehicles operating at 12 mph in mixed traffic,
 conflicts between the AV shuttles with conventional human-operated vehicles
 occasionally occurred. In these interactions, human drivers typically became impatient
 with the AV shuttle and at times began following too closely behind the shuttle while
 seeking an opportunity to overtake the shuttle. In addition, human drivers would merge
 abruptly in front of the shuttles (i.e., cut them off) and unknowingly trigger emergency
 stops. A significant crash occurred when a shuttle was rear-ended by a driver not properly
 judging the slow operating speed of the AV.
 • Impacts of terrain. The impact of operating the vehicle on the deployment site’s terrain
 generated major maintenance costs and impacted operational reliability. The Colorado
 School of Mines campus is located in Golden, Colorado, in the foothills of the Rocky
 Mountains. The steep hills that the AV shuttles were required to traverse along the routes
 induced unexpected mechanical problems. This was a primary factor in the inability to
 continuously operate the necessary operating fleet for the planned service on multiple
 routes. The impacts of terrain on battery charge depletion for BEV deployments will be
 further addressed in a subsequent edition in this report series.
 • Sensitivity to physical environment. The impact of the vehicle control system
 sensitivity to the physical environment also proved a major challenge. During the fall
 2021 change of seasons, leaves blowing along the roadways occasionally triggered
 emergency braking of the shuttles as the sensors identified the leaves as obstacles.
 • Compliance with ADA regulations. During the deployment, there was an unexpected
 requirement to strictly comply with Federal Transit Administration ADA regulations.
 Even though no Federal Transit Administration funds were used, the federal agency
 required vehicle compliance with its ADA regulations because of the placement of the
 Denver RTD logo on the vehicles (RTD receives federal funding through the Federal
 Transit Administration).
Major unanticipated expenses were incurred by AV shuttle supplier EasyMile because of the
vehicle crash and the other challenges identified previously. There was an associated reduction in
the number of vehicles available for the operating fleet, and passenger service levels suffered
with only three or four shuttles able to be sustained in operating service (compared to the
planned seven-vehicle fleet). The parties involved mutually agreed to terminate the Mines Rover
demonstration pilot at the end of the fall 2021 semester rather than continuing to operate the AV
routes until the original end-of-pilot date of July 2022. None of these issues is unique to the
Mines Rover project or its environment; rather, the confluence of all these issues at one location
provides valuable insights and lessons learned for future AMD deployments.

3.3 Broad Conclusions on the Essential Elements for AMD
 Implementation
NLR’s Phase I and Phase II research drew insights from commonalities across the early
deployment sites, providing overall conclusions on the trajectory of AMD developments into the
future. In 2020, when the first edition catalog was published, larger-scale AMD deployments
were anticipated by mid-decade (around 2025). Although there has been consistent progress
within research and development of several AV developers, with emphasis on maturation of the
ADS technology, the timeline for these larger-scale deployments with multiple AV fleets in
operation within large cities has shifted toward the end of the decade—around 2028–2030.

A commonality of early site deployments of automated shuttles includes phased rollouts based
on progressively increasing operational complexity, and adjustments have been necessary as
unexpected operating conditions have been confronted. Because of the unforeseen challenges, as
well as unanticipated difficulty of the foreseen challenges, the number of source suppliers for the
underlying ADS technologies has been reduced—particularly as relates to AV fleets capable of
near-term deployments in mixed-traffic operations. However, this is not an unexpected
phenomenon for technology advancement.

 30

Further, the goal of improved access across population subgroups has led to an approach that
expands an AMD’s operational service area to include populations in reasonable proximity to the
core of major activity centers. Plans for AMD implementation have been informed by multiple
factors, including governmental regulations and discussions with local transit and municipal
authorities who provide funding and manage these new mobility services. In most of the early
deployments, local transit agencies or special district management entities typically have
assumed the role of the authority having jurisdiction over AV fleet operations.

A key finding of Phase II research is that the multimodal operating environment with human-
operated vehicles, pedestrians, bicyclists, and micromobility users substantially increases the
operational complexity for AV fleets and thus the challenge of safe and efficient AV operation.
This is reinforced by the observation that fully automated systems have been deployed on
exclusive guideways for several decades, but only recently have they begun to be operated in
mixed traffic on city streets. Furthermore, the only system in the original 10 early deployment
sites with a significant history of operations—the Rivium District ParkShuttle—operates
primarily on a segregated transitway.

To safely navigate the increased complexity of a dense multimodal environment in mixed traffic,
technology developers have used several tactics to maintain safety:

 • Limiting AV operations to dedicated lanes
 • Operating AVs at lower speeds even in mixed-traffic conditions
 • Retaining safety attendants to provide human operation through the “tough spots” and to
 serve as an authority presence among passengers sharing a vehicle
 • Designing in the ability for remote monitoring of the fleet operations so operators can
 communicate and interact with a vehicle from an operations center when needed
 • Augmenting vehicle sensors with infrastructure capabilities and communications referred
 to herein as intelligent roadway infrastructure (IRI).
Examples of these tactics are evident in the 10 early-stage deployments that have been tracked,
and these tactics continue to be used in ongoing deployments.

Phase II research concluded that to provide safety for all travelers in the AMD—and to assist AV
fleets in complex intersection environments and to provide fail-safe redundant operational
control—IRI can be a critical complementary element for large-scale deployments. With respect
to increasing the safety of advanced technology AVs, the integration of IRI technology into the
overall fleet operational control system through SAE’s cooperative driving automation principles
(SAE International 2021b) may help the individual fleet AVs safely maintain their operating
speed as they approach a complex intersection. A common observation from the pilot AV
deployments is that any slowing of fleet AVs below the speed of the other vehicles in the traffic
stream will increase the risk of human-operated vehicles colliding with the AV from the rear.

IRI is also pursued for safety and coordination of normal roadway traffic and vulnerable roadway
users, as exhibited by the U.S. Department of Transportation’s emphasis on roadway digital
infrastructure to increase safety and reduce congestion (independent of AV deployment).
Roadway digital infrastructure refers to the systematic deployment and use of communication,

 31

sensing, and control applications embedded in infrastructure to enhance overall roadway safety
and efficiency.

From these broad commonalities and observations introduced (and discussed further in the AMD
research report editions that will follow this third edition), the Phase II research illuminated key
findings in the form of the following five cardinal principles:

 1. A dense development setting is important for an AMD. A district with a significantly
 large number of origin-destination trips is necessary to justify the investment in AV fleets
 for public mobility and to provide appropriate return on investment for their deployment.
 Automation can be applied in various environments; however, a dense development
 district in which efficient access is lacking from traditional movement technologies
 because of congestion, low speeds, and parking issues is ripe for innovation. A vast
 majority of the 10 early deployment sites were of this nature.
 2. Independent fleet operators and multiple AV technologies will likely be deployed in
 an AMD. AV-based public mobility services can provide a rich mixture of AV fleets and
 corresponding service types to meet the mobility needs of the public, with each unique
 AV technology fleet independently managed. When deployed, these fleets will operate
 together within the AMD network “system of systems,” encompassing one or more AV
 fleet operators along with regional transit offerings and micromobility services.
 3. The need for an operational management authority (OMA) within an AMD. An
 OMA should be established with authority to set guidelines and operating rules for the
 AV fleets deployed within the geographically defined district. The OMA may be an
 extension of existing traffic management or transit functions—or created specifically for
 the unique AV fleet operations—but is a necessary part of any deployment.
 4. IRI complements vehicle sensing at complex multimodal junctions. IRI enhances
 management of the complex multimodal operating environment at roadway junctions
 within dense urban settings, particularly when multiple AV fleet technologies are
 operating together in mixed traffic. IRI, available for common use by all AV fleets and
 not dedicated to only one mobility technology, would allow AV fleet vehicles to “see
 around the corner,” providing them with additional sensing capability to maintain speed
 and safety through complex environments. This common-use IRI is also beneficial to
 orchestrate high-demand stations shared by multiple AV fleets, as is discussed in Phase
 III findings.
 5. IRI planned, installed, and managed by the OMA is integral to safe AV fleet
 operations and overall roadway management. IRI deployed and managed by the OMA
 not only extends vehicle sensing but also enhances the management of mixed-used
 roadways and the safety of complex environments such as intersections. IRI
 implementation, when consistent with the principles defined by SAE as cooperative
 driving automation, could improve the manageability, safety, and performance of the
 entire mobility system—including AVs, human-operated vehicles, and vulnerable road
 users.
The following subsections expand on key aspects of these cardinal principles.

3.3.1 Importance of an AMD Operational Management Authority
The concept of AMDs applies broadly to major activity centers with defined spatial boundaries,
typically found in large urban employment, recreational, and residential centers. Phase II of
NLR’s research identified that safety aspects of AV fleet operation within an AMD call for a
district management authority to set operational guidelines and rules for fully automated fleets
within their jurisdiction, referred to as an OMA (Lott and Young 2021). Just as the proliferation
of automobiles replaced horse-drawn carts and carriages in the urban centers of the world during
the last century, so too is AV technology anticipated to replace human-operated vehicles in this
century. The earliest traffic issues that arose as automobiles began to flourish were addressed by
new authorities having jurisdiction over traffic operations. These new authorities took
responsibility to install roadside infrastructure (stop and yield control) to safely manage both the
movement of all vehicles and the interaction of vehicles and horse-drawn modes during the
transition. Such authorities evolved into the modern automotive age to assess policy and practice
to ensure safe operations to mitigate the risk of vehicle-to-vehicle and vehicle-to-pedestrian
crashes and to install, maintain, and operate modern traffic signaling technology prevalent across
our roadway system today.

Likewise, our society is at the transportation technology inflection point at which AVs are
beginning to operate in mixed traffic with human-operated vehicles as well as pedestrian and
other micromobility activity. An OMA (perhaps extensions and/or natural evolutions of existing
traffic or transit authorities) is necessary to oversee and manage traffic operations and associated
safety concerns when multiple AV fleets begin operating within AMD boundaries. Note that it is
not the role of the OMA to operate the AV fleets but rather to regulate their station or curbfront
pickup and drop-off locations, facilitate multidirectional communications and data exchange,
determine policy, and operate any infrastructure-based systems for signage, sensing, notification,
and communications that will ensure the safe passage of all modes. This is similar and analogous
to the role of current traffic management with respect to signalized intersection equipment used
in modern roadways as well as the communications-based train control systems that have been
continuously deployed and improved in automated guideway transit since the 1970s.

Ensuring the safety of the traveling public is the primary charge of the OMA. With respect to an
AMD, the OMA would focus its safety and hazard analysis not on the internal failures and
operational design domain limitations of each AV technology but rather on the operational effect
and hazards such failures would have on the traffic and other modal elements within the broader
system. Having the OMA develop and enforce appropriate policy for safe operations—as well as
plan, deploy, and manage any collective infrastructure within the district—promotes a
cooperative and consistent operating environment that allows each AV fleet technology to react
predictably and uniformly for a given hazardous condition. This in turn promotes increased
safety while minimizing excess congestion that diminishes roadway capacity.

Another responsibility of the OMA would be to determine the necessary funding and contracting
for the design, procurement, and implementation of any associated infrastructure improvements.
These infrastructure improvements may involve simple pavement markings, installation of
signage, or erection of appropriate barriers or fencing to control nonvehicular modes from
entering the roadway in areas determined to have a high safety risk. It would also include the
deployment of any IRI for advanced control and coordination. These types of assessments are

 33

common in the modern world of traffic operations, particularly in areas of high pedestrian
activity—commonly referred to as “traffic warrant studies.”

This system-level approach to safety would be the domain of the AMD’s operational
management authority. Several system-level safety protocols and standards are emerging and
available for guidance.2 The selection of relevant standards by the OMA will identify both the
acceptable risk level and other supporting protocols and policies to be included in the AMD
implementation program.

3.3.2 District-Level Safety Case Analysis
The domain of an OMA encompasses the execution of a hazard analysis and risk assessment to
determine necessary risk mitigations analogous to traffic warrant studies performed in analysis of
traffic today. This is commonly known in associated domains as preparing a “safety case” for
operations resulting in determination and enforcement of safe operating rules.

Ultimately, the preparation of the safety case for the safety-critical junctions and operating zones
within an AMD will come down to understanding the types of AV fleets that would be operating
within the district and their various failure mechanisms, particularly with respect to mixed-use
traffic conditions. Specific hazard analyses will address acceptable configuration and operational
practice for AVs within the multimodal traffic of conventional human-operated vehicles, AV
transit, and AV ride-hailing fleets—especially when the vehicles are interacting with pedestrians,
bicyclists, and other active transportation modes.

With the new technology and operational conditions of a district with multiple AV fleets in
service, the OMA may also lead in any safety assessment resulting from the composite
operations of multiple different AV fleets and the associated challenge of communicating and
coordinating with multiple fleet operators.

3.3.3 Incident and Vehicle Failure Response Considerations
Operations responsibility for an OMA would include the development of instructions and
protocols for safe fallback conditions when a fleet AV’s ADS disengages or when similar AV
system failures occur. At a minimum, such a protocol typically—at least during early
deployments—brings the vehicle to a safe stop. Situations in which a vehicle reverts to a fallback
condition can be as simple as exceeding the operational design domain limits of weather
conditions (e.g., high winds or low visibility) or as complicated as a control system failure.
Either case should be considered a failure in which a safe operating fallback protocol is required
for fleet AVs as well as safety provisions for all pedestrians/bicyclists and human-operated
vehicles in proximity to the vehicle(s) in fallback mode.

Any given AV technology will have unique fallback behaviors in the case of an internal vehicle
fault—determined and engineered by the technology provider. The OMA would provide
minimum requirements for various types of fallback response at the fleet and system level that a
technology provider would need to comply with. Examples of typical fallback responses include
moving to the roadway shoulder to stop, driving across operating lanes to stop at a curbfront, or

3.3.4 Role of Intelligent Roadway Infrastructure
A key conclusion of the assessment of the 10 early deployments was that keeping the fleet AVs
safely operating at speeds as close as possible to other traffic operations is essential to reducing
crashes, especially rear-end collisions on the approach to signalized intersections. This issue of
reduction in speed was common in complex environments such as intersections with multiple
lanes, significant turning movements, and nonvehicle traffic such as pedestrians, bicyclists, and
various forms of micromobility.

Large-scale AV deployments within complex AMD operating environments such as multimodal
intersections may require sophisticated, advanced sensing and control technology to support and
augment the AV sensing capabilities and coordinate all modes of traffic for safety and
efficiency, referred to as IRI. Further, IRI communication protocols, such as those outlined in the
principles of cooperative driving automation (SAE J3216), should be beneficially integrated into
any fleet AV control systems if adopted by the OMA. For example, the IRI may detect system-
level hazards beyond the sensor range of any fleet AV and provide directives to any fleet AVs
approaching an intersection to either 1) decrease speed, move to the side of the road, or come to
a halt within their travel lane or 2) avoid a particular route experiencing trouble, as appropriate
for the “over-the-horizon” hazard. Infrastructure sensing is being used to document and validate
AV performance and conformance to policy, even extending to emergency responder “body
cams.” This independent record of vehicular performance is growing increasingly critical to
identify and remedy AV performance issues.

Based on the lessons learned at Colorado School of Mines, Rivium, and other early deployment
sites, mitigating speed differentials between AVs and human-operated vehicles is critical. IRI
could help mitigate this issue and simultaneously increase intersection safety for all roadway
users. This harmonizes with recent U.S. Department of Transportation efforts in roadway digital
infrastructure—the application of advanced sensing, computation, and communication to
improve the efficiency and safety of roadway operations. In the application to AMDs, IRI would
promote safe and sustained AV operating speeds within the flow of the surrounding conventional
traffic with human-operated vehicles. It would also serve as a tool to observe system-level
operations and performance, providing a common operating picture of the performance of AV
fleets, and adherence to OMA policy and guidelines of operations. All of this would be in
addition to the benefits that increased observability would have for traditional traffic
management. The concept of IRI to augment and address the AV fleet operating environment for
increased safety was recognized by SAE at an international conference as having technical
substance worthy of inclusion in one of its journals for practices for advanced mobility (Lott,
Young, and Zhu 2021).

IRI planned, deployed, and maintained by the OMA provides an independent safety agent in the

micromobility users outside their individual field of view.

These benefits of IRI, however, do not eliminate the essential requirement for AV fleets to be
fully operational in the event that the IRI sensing and communications are interrupted. The AV
fleet vehicles must still be capable of safely continuing their fully automated mobility service

IRI “failure mode” conditions may result in lower operating speeds for the AV fleet vehicles
within AMD zones normally providing IRI communications.

4 Summary of Phase III Research
This third edition of the AMD implementation report series, which corresponds with NLR’s
Phase III of AMD research described herein, provides technical information, data, and critical
evaluation of the continuing evolution of the AV industry for public mobility. Phase I and Phase
II work drew insights and conclusions primarily from commonalities of the initial 10 early
deployment sites, with particular emphasis on ADS performance. However, in Phase III, research
began to draw from parallel fields and industries as they relate to the foundational concepts
inherent to the design, deployment, and operation of an AMD. These parallel fields include the
burgeoning ODT industry; previous study and adoption of ATN systems, particularly as it
applies to the design and operation of resilient, high-capacity stations; the automated people
mover system industry and its reliance on infrastructure intelligence to augment system safety;
and electric vehicle (EV) powertrains and their associated charging infrastructure and impacts on
fleet operations.

Phase III research into AMDs spans approximately 2022 through early 2025. Although Phase III
continued to monitor the initial early-stage deployments, most Phase III research was a product
of the intersection of the parallel fields described previously and the operational requirements of
an AMD network system. This summary provides an overview of the research from Phase III
organized into the following topical areas:

 • Impacts and implications of the on-demand network service mode
 • Management systems integrated into the operational control of the AV fleets
 • Battery electric fleet vehicle charging infrastructure
 • Boarding station/curbfront configuration and infrastructure
 • Systems engineering methodology for planning, analysis, and management of AMDs.
Although organized into these stand-alone topics, many interconnections and interdependencies
exist. The convergence of transportation system automation, electrification, and expectation for
on-demand service for future mobility within urban areas is increasingly evident as AMD
technology progresses. Although powertrain electrification was not uncommon in the 10 early
deployment sites, the impacts of a fully electrified vehicle powertrain on AV fleet operations was
not a central focus of research until now. With the growth, maturation, and steady adoption of
EV technology, combined with early AMDs beginning to scale from a handful of vehicles to
larger fleets, an initial investigation of operational consequences of EV powertrains is warranted.
Similarly, the rapid adoption of commercial ride-hailing services in the 2010s and the
burgeoning ODT market that has emerged since the COVID-19 pandemic both point toward the
growing expectation for AV fleets to provide on-demand mobility service for community/district
circulation and FMLM connectivity to regional and interregional transportation modes.

As the timeline for AMD implementation becomes clearer, with the first prototypical district-
scale deployment sites expected to be delivered in the next 5 years, NLR’s Phase III studies have
focused on the operational implications for the convergence of 1) electrification of the many
light-duty fleets deployed with BEV propulsion systems and 2) automation of operational fleets
for moving people and goods—all within the context of the proliferation of on-demand
passenger and goods movement (Lott and Young 2023). The ramifications for the transportation
and ancillary support facilities of an AMD—in particular, high-capacity intermodal stations and

the electrical charging infrastructure that forms the power supply system for BEV charging
stations—are critical to define when AV technology is deployed in multiple operating fleets on a
large scale within an AMD.

ODT (i.e., microtransit) within an AMD will use fleets of light- to medium-duty vehicles in
which each vehicle is individually dispatched to pick up a party and transport them directly to
their destination drop-off location without making any other “fixed-station” stops in between.
This type of service is similar to TNCs, which typically provide on-demand, single-party, curb-
to-curb service. TNCs also offer pooled rides in some locations, which was more common before
the COVID-19 pandemic—though it is reemerging in a few places. Typically, ODT is inherently
shared, without the ability to opt out of pooled ride options (as in TNCs). If additional passengers
are heading in the same direction or to the same location, ODT will automatically pool the ride
requests and route accordingly. As such, an ODT ride may include intermediate stops for either
passenger pickup or drop-off while providing direct origin-to-destination service. Even with
pooling compulsory with ODT, many trips within an ODT system remain single party,
particularly during nonpeak times.

The continued rapid adoption of ODT services in the United States and anticipated use of AV
fleets to provide ODT have multiple operational implications for AMDs. Two of these are the
design of safe, high-capacity boarding and alighting areas (stations or PUDO zones) and the
implications of electrifying the fleet of light-duty, minivan-sized vehicles that typically provide
ODT service. An additional operational concern for shared rides is ensuring security without the
presence of a driver—an area of research slated for Phase IV.

As ODT applications multiply in the United States, the need for safe and efficient passenger
boarding and alighting have led to the increasing practice of predesignating specific ODT access
locations. These are not traditional transit stations but often specific curbside locations that have
been selected to allow a safe interface for passenger transfer to and from the vehicle as well as
easily recognized landmarks for rendezvous between the passenger and vehicle. Passengers
booking a pickup via a smartphone app are instructed to walk to these designated locations for
service, a practice that has recently been called “corner-to-corner” service, as compared to “curb-
to-curb.”

The concept of a transit service providing a direct origin-to-destination service using optimized
routing for each person’s travel needs is not new in the world of automated people mover
systems. Such service harkens back to the concept of “personal rapid transit” (historically called
by its acronym PRT) over the last 50 years, which provides direct origin-to-destination service
for riders but on a dedicated guideway. More recently the PRT concept was captured by the term
automated transit network (ATN), which encompasses the service concept of PRT but is more
encompassing of various technologies, even including AV fleets on public rights-of-way. An
ATN system is formally defined in the ANSI/ASCE 21-21 Automated People Mover Standards.
At its core, whether PRT, ATN, or ODT, these services use a network for optimized routing to
service individuals, parties, or small groups as opposed to a predefined operating route with fixed
stations. The complexity of dispatching each individual vehicle to serve an individual travel
party with a point-to-point ride on an optimized travel path requires a substantially more
complex vehicle dispatching control system when performed in real time. As a result, it has been
only in the last 25 years that fixed-guideway ATN systems comprising a fleet of small vehicles
have been implemented at a few districts around the world.

The study and simulation of several proposed ATN systems over the past 20 years provides
significant parallel insight with respect to incorporating ODT service within an AMD. A review

 41

of select ATN simulation studies in the next edition to follow in the report series reveals the
criticality of appropriate station design incorporating offline and parallel berths for maximum
throughput and resiliency, the importance of pre-positioning vehicles in anticipation of peak
periods of demand, and having adequate vehicles and vehicle storage to service surge flows at
high-demand stations such as an intermodal station connecting an AMD to high-capacity
regional and interregional transit. The nondeterministic nature of ODT also makes recharging
EVs more challenging, calling for better integration of charging into stations using locations
where the vehicles naturally dwell, providing the best opportunity for battery charging.

4.2 Fleet Management and the Operational Control System
With the maturing of ADS, attention is now turning to the management of AV fleets for the
provision of public mobility. With the human driver removed from these vehicles, several
aspects of fleet management must be rethought. In some cases, the absence of a driver provides
additional fleet-level benefits in terms of flexibility and scheduling; in other cases, it presents
challenges—particularly with respect to unexpected situations (outside the operational design
domain) and communicating with riders. This section of research draws on parallels from
automated guideway transit fleet management in which communications-based train control
allows fully automated operations. The NLR AMD research uses “automated supervisory and
dispatch control” (ASDC) as developed in a 2021 SAE International conference paper (Lott,
Young, and Zhu 2021) to refer to the fleet management of AVs. It has analogies and parallels to
communications-based train control principles of operation but applied to fleets of AVs for
public mobility. A critical function of AV fleet management is the strategic positioning (or pre-
positioning) of empty AV fleet vehicles in anticipation of surge flows—whether from unusual
demand (such as a special event) or in response to a modal interface (such as transfers from a
regional train or banks of arrivals at an airport). Other critical AV fleet management functions
are associated with traditional traffic management centers that direct resources in response to
unusual traffic conditions and incidents on roadways. For AV fleets, this includes not only any
vehicle-associated crash or individual vehicle ADS system failure but also an additional layer of
rider/occupant interaction (a function typically performed by the driver in manned operations)
and remote operation/intervention whenever the vehicle experiences a condition outside the
bounds of its operational design domain. AV fleet management and its automation to minimize
personnel resources while optimizing user experience and consistency of experience—referred to
herein as ASDC—is increasingly being acknowledged as a critical next frontier for realizing the
benefits of AMDs.

As an AMD scales the operation of multiple fleets of automated passenger transport systems
(both public and private) within the defined boundaries of the district, each with its own AV
technologies—as illustrated in Figure 22—adds to the complexity of management and
operations. In addition to automated passenger service, the district may also include automated
cargo/package delivery fleets as well as legacy manual vehicle fleets for public transit. This
yields the previously referenced system-of-systems characteristic for the AMD and its supporting
infrastructure.

Each fleet will have unique vehicle technology, with the size and configuration of each fleet
selected to optimally serve its market sector. Some AV fleets may operate in a fixed-route and
fixed-schedule mode—typical of traditional bus systems—whereas others will operate on

 42

demand, delivering travel parties directly from their origin to their destination. The latter is
expected to be the dominant mode, especially for short trips within an AMD. With respect to on-
demand service mode, Phase III research reviewed several analytical simulation studies of
conceptual ATN systems that operate primarily in a demand-response fashion. These ATN
simulation studies dating to the 2000s revealed that on a typical network with many stations,
most of the trip demand involved trips between only a subset of the overall stations. It was found
that these high-demand station pairs were often better served by larger vehicles, whereas smaller
vehicles were suited for the many other station pairs with low trip demands. Further, these high-
demand station pairs may also be effectively serviced with fixed-route operation during all or
part of the day, though not necessarily on a fixed schedule.

Fully automated supervisory and dispatch control systems are well suited for adapting the
vehicle assignments of varying vehicle sizes and modes (fixed route and on demand) as the
demand changes throughout the day. Such changes are much more difficult to achieve with
conventional (human-driven) ODT operations, compared to AV fleet operational systems,
because of the added flexibility of driverless operation.

The importance of IRI to enhance safety and operational efficiency and to improve
environmental sustainability and energy conservation was introduced in Phase II research. The
concept of IRI to enhance AMD operations was originally proposed in a 2021 technical paper at
the SAE Business of Automated Mobility Forum, and subsequently included in an SAE journal
publication (Lott, Young, and Zhu 2021). IRI functionality may be integrated into the AV’s
automated fleet operational control system as part of the automated fleet operations module that
controls vehicle performance, enforces AV safety, and directs fleet operations. This is depicted
in Figure 24, which illustrates the conceptual parallel between AV fleet management and
communications-based train control.

However, as noted above, any IRI failure mode conditions such as a power outage that disable
the IRI sensing functions and communications with AV fleet vehicles must not result in a
cessation of AV operations. Degradations in fleet vehicle performance levels during these failure
mode operations, however, may result.

Phase III research built on this conceptual framework, particularly the functions of the ASDC
subsystem that monitors and dispatches vehicles as a subsystem under automated fleet
operations. Each AV fleet would have its respective ASDC subsystem for vehicle dispatch in
response to each traveler’s demand call as well as for managing the fleet in preparation for
ridership surge flow response. The ASDC subsystem would also manage station operations,
including each fleet vehicle’s station berth (or curb) assignment and coordination. In addition, it
would adjust the operating fleet size necessary to meet service levels throughout the day while
sending excess vehicles for maintenance, charging, and storage. The ASDC subsystem assigns
and dispatches vehicles into service in response to a specific passenger’s “demand-call” ride
reservation. This also includes the aspects of “empty vehicle management” whereby vehicles are
strategically positioned at stations or offline holding areas in anticipation of high service demand
while allowing vehicles to remain dormant during times when demand is low.

4.2.1 Operations Control Center
Another critical function of the ASDC subsystem is the continuous monitoring of the AV fleet at
an operational control center by personnel who will respond to situations outside of any
individual vehicle’s operational design domain. This supervision and remote interaction from an
operational control center is described by the SAE Automated Vehicle Safety Consortium as
“remote assistance.” Within an operational control center, personnel also provide a human
interface with passengers by responding to passenger inquiries via intercom or video links within
stations and in vehicles.

4.2.2 ASDC Benefits for Automation of ODT Services
NLR studies of conventional ODT service using human operators revealed practical limitations
of how the vehicle fleet can be dispatched to respond to ridership surge flows. Such capacity
limitations commonly occur when the ridership surge conditions result from the following:

 • Random peaking effects within the pattern of origin/destination trips
 • Scheduled event patterns that concentrate person-trips at certain times of day (e.g.,
 campus class schedules, medical complex appointment schedules, sporting events)
 • Arrival of large regional transit and/or intercity trains at intermodal stations within the
 AMD network when all or most passengers are boarding the ODT system for FMLM
 connections.
For a conventional ODT system, capacity limitations arise primarily from personnel
management. The result is that conventional ODT cannot support a fleet size needed to serve
peak surge demands because it would result in many vehicles (and their associated drivers) being

 44

dormant during the extended periods of the day with off-peak demand conditions. Commercial
ride-hailing services address surge demand with surge pricing, in which escalated prices for
riders encourage riders to seek off-peak periods. Escalated pay for drivers also encourages more
drivers to come online during peak periods. However, ODT, as currently implemented, does not
leverage market forces such as surge pricing to help balance supply and demand.

As a result of these practical limitations, ODT passengers typically experience degraded service
equating to extensive delay during periods of peak demand or even being notified that no
operator/vehicle is available at that time, with an advisement that they should try again later.

However, AV fleets under an ASDC subsystem can bring additional flexibility to better respond
to peak demand. AV fleets can more aptly increase or decrease fleet size in response to trip
demand without penalties for dormant vehicles and operators. Further, the ASDC can more aptly
deploy larger vehicles to the dominant station pairs to better serve demand while reserving
smaller vehicles to station pairs with less demand. Further, the ASDC can optimally manage a
fleet by placing the required “surplus” vehicles in storage areas located at strategic points near
the known places where ridership demand-surge flows will occur.

Converting the operating fleet to AV technology and replacing the conventional dispatching
procedures/system with a fully automated supervisory and dispatch control system allows most
capacity deficiencies because of fleet size limitations to be effectively addressed.

4.3 BEV Fleet Charging Strategy and Infrastructure
The convergence of vehicle electrification with the automation of vehicle fleets adds to the
challenges of planning for AMD fleet operations. This convergence arises because of the limited
range of BEVs compared to the daily service profile for AV fleet operations as well as the
corresponding increased time for recharging (i.e., refueling) the vehicle. Each vehicle’s batteries
will need to be recharged at some point, which in turn takes the vehicle out of service anywhere
from minutes to hours during the daily period of operations.

In comparison, a fleet of vehicles propelled by internal combustion engines typically carries
enough fuel on board for a full day’s duty cycle. If needed, the vehicles can refuel in a relatively
short amount of time. Further, the trend of transit industry thinking is that for the very largest
vehicles (40-passenger buses or larger), fuels such as hydrogen fuel cells or liquified natural gas
are also being considered for AV technology applications. However, for the small- to medium-
sized AV deployments carrying between 2 and 20 passengers per vehicle, BEV propulsion
systems remain the most commonly preferred choice.

As battery technology improves and corresponding rapid charging technology progresses, these
issues may self-mitigate. However, for any near-term deployment, the practical limitations of EV
range and corresponding battery charging considerations must be integrated into the overall
service design of any AMD AV fleet service.

A charging strategy that considers both EV battery characteristics and the associated charging
infrastructure is critical for any AMD effort. This strategy includes accounting for the nonlinear
nature of battery charging rates over the extent of the battery charge level. The charging rate
varies throughout the charging process. The first 20% charge for a fully depleted EV battery

 45

occurs at a significantly slower pace than the charging rate above 20%. Similarly, the charging
rate above an 80% charge level is also significantly slower. For this reason, the time allowance
for battery charging of a public fleet vehicle is often scheduled for charging only between 20%
and 80% charge level. This nonlinear battery charging characteristic, combined with the high
cost of charging infrastructure and any associated power distribution upgrade, requires a
thorough analysis and design process.

The charging strategy for EV fleets serving an AMD determines the design of the charging
station size (number of vehicle positions), capacity (power requirements for the composite
charging station power demands), and configuration (vehicle positions and access/egress
provisions). This is even more critical for AV fleets operated in on-demand service modes
because the charging needs of an ODT system are not as predictable from day to day as those of
fixed-route systems. As such, the infrastructure size, location, and configuration to service ODT
fleets may be substantially different from those of fixed-route service operations.

Figure 25 depicts a DC fast charge facility with the capacity to simultaneously recharge 16 light-
duty EVs. It is representative of the type of infrastructure necessary to support a large fleet of
small- to medium-sized vehicles operating in an on-demand mode of services.

  Figure 25. This example of a typical Level 3 charging facility with 16 DC fast charge positions,
 which is representative of a size suitable for supporting fleet operations, requires both physical
 space and substantial power supply infrastructure and power grid capacity.

The number of charging facilities and their placement within the AMD service network, sizing of
the power level for each charging station (i.e., vehicle charging position), and type of charger
equipment (automated electrical contact system, wireless inductive power transfer, or
conventional plug-in configuration) are fundamental parameters for any charging strategy for
any given AV fleet. Another aspect of the charging strategy is the charge rate for each EV
battery considering the nonlinear charging characteristics discussed previously.

 46

A charging strategy may comprise both deep charging, typically overnight to fully replenish an
EV battery pack prior to operations the following day, and short periods of “opportunity
charging” during the day to extend the operational duty cycle of the vehicle. Opportunity
charging is particularly relevant for on-demand vehicle fleets because of the complexity of their
operations. Opportunity charging occurs during brief periods when an individual vehicle is
dormant, such as at station stops. This can be momentary, corresponding to the few minutes to
allow for boarding and alighting, or extended, when vehicles are pre-positioned at a station in
anticipation of a surge in ridership demand. Deep charging of individual vehicles requires an
extended period during which they are removed from passenger service, typically overnight. The
charging strategy is fundamental to determining the available duty cycle of a vehicle, which in
turn will determine the minimum size of the fleet required to meet passenger demand as well as
the charging infrastructure and energy needs required.

Additional opportunity charging may be afforded by the advent of wireless inductive charging
technologies. Wireless inductive charging is typically a system using a pad installed in the
roadbed or on the road surface to transmit electric charge to a vehicle above the pad, requiring no
physical connection or human action to initiate. Although not yet mature, this technology may
enable BEVs to receive additional electric charge while stopped briefly at a PUDO zone, an
intersection, or other locations at which a vehicle may dwell for a short time. Wireless inductive
charging may enable BEVs to operate for longer periods between deep charging cycles or even
indefinitely. Inductive charging may also reduce the minimum required battery storage (and thus
costs) needed to be installed on each vehicle by regularly replenishing the state of charge of a
comparatively smaller battery to maintain desired operations.

Further, the capital, operating, and maintenance costs of the charging infrastructure will be a
major engineering and financial factor. For example, the capital cost of the 16-position Level 3
DC fast-charging station shown in Figure 25 is estimated to be $2.5 million, not including
additional cost for any changes to the electrical grid necessary to ensure sufficient primary power
supply voltage and amperage at the selected charging facility location.

4.4 Station/Curbfront Configurations and Associated Infrastructure
Fundamental to future AMDs is the prospect of AV fleets providing public mobility services
using the common public roadway network and associated common infrastructure, independent
of whether the fleets are owned and operated by a transit authority, contracted to an operator, or
offered by a private company providing ride-hailing services—or some combination thereof.
Similarly, whether public or private, all types of PUDO locations can be referred to as “stations”
and “station facilities” whether they comprise the curbfront at the side of a road (as in the case of
PUDO zones) or a more substantive form of boarding and alighting infrastructure reminiscent of
more traditional transit stations.

The state of the art for ODT services (whether automated or manually controlled) is to have
predefined locations where the passenger can meet the vehicle near their physical original
location. Figure 26 shows the roadway network and programmed points on the network where
passengers can access the RAPID service at Arlington, Texas. Such locations are dense enough
to minimize the walking distance to the rendezvous point where the dispatched vehicle will meet
the passenger—typically about one block away, yet predetermined to ensure there is adequate
curb space for safe vehicle access.

 47

 roads, with specifically defined service access points (stations) shown where passengers were
 directed to go to board a vehicle.
 Source: City of Arlington

Provision for safe and efficient pickup and drop-off (PUDO) station locations for automated
operations away from the roadway network’s main travel lanes requires additional or rededicated
space, the specifications of which will scale with demand. This additional area could be as
simple and inexpensive as the reuse of existing curb space or a dedicated lane (perhaps replacing
some on-street parking) for low-demand, occasional PUDO operations. As passenger demand at
a location increases, a station may require dedicated space completely off the roadway right-of-
way—such as within the first level of a parking garage near the roadway. Surface parking lots
could also be a practical location for a high-capacity designated PUDO facility. Many of these
issues arise at high-demand PUDO station locations for TNCs, such as at airports, intermodal
hubs, and entertainment venues.

The efficiency, reliability, and resilience of these PUDO locations are critical to overall system
operation. This is evidenced in various fixed-route transit systems as well, where the overall
system capacity is often determined more by station operations at the highest-demand stations
than by the traditional view of line capacity. The criticality of station operations and the benefits
of “parallel-berth” boarding positions have been extensively explored in various ATN simulation
studies, the details of which will be discussed in a subsequent edition of the AMD report series.

configurations allow a vehicle to dwell indefinitely without impeding the movements of other
vehicles in the system (Lott et al. 2022).

Parallel-berth offline stations provide additional benefits to passengers. For example, a party that
requires additional time to board does not impede the flow of other passengers to and from the
station. Offline station configurations with parallel berths also provide synergistic benefits to
implement opportunity charging; for example, a vehicle waiting for a passenger can collect
additional charge to extend its operational duty cycle.

Further, particularly at high-capacity and intermodal stations where ODT may connect with a
higher-capacity regional transit service, the pedestrian space through which the passengers pass
to gain access to the AVs should be well coordinated with the design of the other modal
facilities—including ensuring personal safety, security, and easy wayfinding while in the space.

The location and configuration of the stations must consider ways to appropriately scale the
facilities at major activity centers and intermodal hubs. Extensive simulation of ATN services in
the 2000s revealed the criticality of anticipating surge flows at major intermodal stations and pre-

service and prevent “empty-vehicle starvation.” Such locations may benefit from co-locating
vehicle storage to address the staging of empty vehicles to serve passenger demand surges as
well as accommodating battery charging infrastructure.

Further, these strategically placed storage areas for dormant vehicles also help accommodate the
increase in the fleet size that is often necessary to service the periodic ridership surge-flow
conditions that have been noted previously.

4.5 Systems Engineering Methodology for Analysis and Assessment
NLR’s Phase III research concludes that there is a need for simulation-based analyses of AMD
AV fleet operations considering the complexity of the integrated operating modes of on-demand
and fixed-route transit services, corresponding station operations, need for battery charging and
management of the fleet, and added complexity of roadway mixed-traffic operations—including
the presence of pedestrians and other roadway users such as bicyclists. A methodology similar
to, but expanding upon, the example simulation-based ATN studies will be described in detail in
the next edition of this report series. The proposed multimodal simulations would be applied for
analyzing the conceptual AMD network comprising multiple fleets of AVs operating in a
geographically constrained environment, providing circulation service within the district and
FMLM connections to regional transit, interregional transit, and parking facilities. This
simulation methodology, referred to herein as SEM, would include all subsystems and modes
and would stress test the system with hypothetical scenarios and edge cases to ensure the fleet,
infrastructure, and operating methodology can adequately and safely serve demand in all
situations. Furthermore, in concert with the trend toward digital twins, this SEM will serve as the
basis for an operational digital twin to provide a real-time digital representation of the system to
monitor performance and adjust operational parameters in response to the dynamic changes in
passenger demand patterns.

The findings of the Phase III research make increasingly evident the challenges and complexities
of AMD operational management as multiple AV fleets are combined with on-demand service
using BEVs. The resulting recommended framework for a comprehensive analysis process that
NLR is referring to as the SEM is illustrated in Figure 29.

Drawing from past simulation-based analytical studies over the past several decades of various
ATN systems, Phase III recommends this SEM analysis approach for studying the principal
variables that determine overall AMD network system performance. These principal variables
include vehicle size(s), fleet size(s), vehicle storage/recharging locations, vehicle battery size,
on-demand operations policies, and station configuration and operation. The SEM is well suited
to study the impacts of passenger trip patterns and connections within the multimodal AMD
network transport system, including time-of-day ridership fluctuations and regional transit
intermodal station surge flows connecting to AMD circulator systems. Note that SEM includes
consideration for not only the AMD AV modes but also all the accompanying pedestrian paths
and associated activity, micromobility modes, regular vehicle modes, and complementary
traditional transit services.

This SEM shares similarities with the fixed-guideway ATN simulation studies that will be
described through multiple examples in the next edition of the AMD implementation report
series. One selected example of ATN simulation is the conceptual automated network system
studied for the campus at California State University, Fresno (Figure 30). Each vehicle in the

example is limited to the stations and network of the ATN system and the associated pedestrian
activity throughout the campus, a SEM analysis would simulate not only the AV fleets but also
all vehicles, passengers, and other road users; the movement of all modes (on demand and fixed
route); the ride request and dispatch functions; and the energy subsystems of each EV fleet.

This systems engineering approach is applicable in the early design phases, allowing trade-off
analysis of cost and complexity to identify an optimal solution for the most critical elements of
an AMD’s implementation. The resulting digital twin provides an effective planning and

4.6 Phase III Insights and Phase IV Future Research
Whereas NLR’s Phases I and II research dealt primarily with assessing the maturity of ADS on
the vehicle and their ability to safely and effectively deliver public mobility within an AMD,
Phase III turned its attention to system-level aspects. The system-level aspects of AV fleets
delivering public mobility include not only the maturation of AV technology but also the
challenges of vehicle electrification and managing and coordinating multiple AV fleets to safely
and efficiently deliver on-demand mobility services. High-level conclusions and insights for an
AMD network system in the mid- to near futures (5 to 10 years) drawn from the composite of the
Phases I, II, and III research—with a sampling of the most significant findings—are presented
next.

Selected Phase III Research Findings

 1. A system-of-systems view and approach is required to achieve the full vision of
 effective, safe, and efficient public mobility using AV fleets, including appropriate
 jurisdictional authority for management and monitoring of the AMD network transport
 system. Interface points—both digital multidirectional communications and physical
 infrastructure, such as stations, to facilitate transfer between complementary modes—will
 be critical.
 2. Intelligent roadway infrastructure can enhance the safety and throughput capacity of
 AV systems at roadway intersections and stations/curbfronts. Drawing on established
 principles from automated train control and emerging cooperative driving automation for
 roadways, IRI enhances the perception of AVs while providing the management authority
 with independent observability of various modes and their operational performance,
 enhances the safety and operations of traditional (nonautomated) modes, and offers
 pedestrians, micromobility, and vulnerable road users additional safety benefits.
 Although originally introduced to enhance roadway intersection safety, IRI is equally
 critical to protect passengers in the boarding process at high-capacity stations. Drawing
 on recent deployment experience, IRI may be equally critical in documenting AV
 performance and adherence to policy as an independent validator. This is explored more
 in Phase IV research.
 3. Electrification of AV fleets will present significant operational and cost impacts. The
 planning analysis for an AV fleet serving an AMD will require trade-offs among vehicle
 types, fleet configurations, and service mode operation, combined with BEV charging
 strategies (opportunity charging and deep charging). The transition from petroleum to
 electric powertrains will impact fleet operations and system cost based on vehicle
 operating range, charging time, charging facility location(s) and size, and availability of
 sufficient electric grid energy capacity.
 4. ODT services will provide the base community/district circulation and FMLM
 service. Whether provided commercially or through a public entity (or both), ODT will
 form a foundational community connectivity service in future AMDs. Demand-
 responsive, direct origin-to-destination operations of small- to medium-sized vehicles
 will provide community circulation and connections to regional and interregional larger-
 capacity modes while enabling the optimization of energy consumption by rightsizing
 vehicles for passenger demand.

 54

 5. A systems engineering methodology will be needed for planning, analysis, and
 assessment of future AMD network systems. The SEM will essentially comprise a
 multisystem modeling framework, creating a “digital twin” that simulates all modes,
 vehicles, travelers, and critical subsystems such as charging infrastructure and vehicle
 dispatch. This systems engineering methodology is needed to analyze the critical
 parameters that determine overall system performance and bridges to a digital twin to
 support operations management. The SEM may also be used to investigate and integrate
 new forms of mobility that could augment and improve the AMD network system.
At the time this report was published, initial AV functionality is in fully driverless Level 4
operation for commercial ride-hailing service in more than 10 U.S. cities, and several public
mobility demonstration projects are moving forward with planned systems. As these initial
systems begin to scale, NLR has identified future research needs beyond Phase III for future
AMDs, summarized next.

Summary of Future Research Needs

Selected critical topics from Phase III research are currently being extended into the AMD Phase
IV research, particularly the use of intelligent infrastructure for safety applications protecting
boarding and alighting in stations/PUDO areas, as well as the continued refinement of EV
integration into AV fleets with emphasis on automated charging methods and equipment.

Phase IV is also beginning to explore and emphasize the following new areas of research:

 • AV technology advancement. Phase I began six years ago by identifying and
 developing a synopsis of the then 10 leading AV demonstrations/deployments. Now,
 more than six years later, it is necessary to revisit leading deployments and business
 models to bring AV technology to maturity and begin to scale. This effort will document
 and assess leading initiatives both in public mobility utilizing the public roadway network
 and for the movement of people within large facilities such as airports and medical
 facilities. In addition, new research will look laterally at the state of AV deployment in
 areas such as freight and goods movement and package and food delivery. These
 assessments will use the U.S. Department of Energy’s technology maturity and market
 readiness assessment scales for comparison.
 • AV fleet operations management. Phase III revealed the complexity of the various
 subsystems needed for AV fleet operations with respect to electrification and station
 operations. Phase IV is beginning to delve further into AV fleet management functions,
 including emerging business models and operational protocols involved in managing AV
 technology for public mobility applications. This includes the numerous subsystems,
 facilities, communications, and human/machine interfaces that comprise the operations
 management supporting the AV fleet(s) within the urban environment.
 • New mobility systems simulation. Phase III identified the need for a SEM for the
 planning, analysis, and operation of the multiple modes and subsystems of an AMD,
 often referred to as a “digital twin.” Most existing simulation tools allow effective
 analysis of traditional modes of travel but are challenged when applied to model
 emerging modes and to encompass simulations of multimodal travel. As an extension of
 the SEM, future work will identify available simulation tools and assess gaps in

 55

 capability to support the SEM process, particularly for AV and other emerging mobility
 technology in combination with traditional modes, microtransit, and nonmotorized forms
 of travel.
• Critical information exchange. Early deployments are exposing the importance of
 coordination of critical information exchange between infrastructure owners and
 operators and AV fleets in areas pertaining to emergency response, roadway network
 impacts (e.g., construction, detours, maintenance), and special events. This is typically
 network-level information beyond the sensor range of any individual vehicle. Such
 information reflects events and circumstances such as floods, major roadway construction
 zones, and emergency response (e.g., fires, active shooters). This research is being
 coordinated with the U.S. Department of Transportation’s Center for Excellence on New
 Mobility and Automated Vehicles, building on the initial research by NLR entitled Data
 for Autonomous Transportation Awareness: Data Exchange Use Cases, Standards, and
 Barriers (Young, Fish, and Mir 2026).
• Human response to unattended, driverless vehicles. Many AV applications in which
 multiple parties share a vehicle have had an attendant on board, ostensibly to assist the
 vehicle when needed, but the attendant also serves another purpose as an authority and
 moderator among passengers. People are accustomed to riding in elevators with others,
 but those rides tend to be short—and behavior and norms in an elevator have been
 standardized for much of the past century. In the case of AV operations, where trips are
 of longer duration and without established norms, much remains unknown about how
 people might interact in the absence of an attendant. Compounding this, substantial
 literature has identified safety concerns, particularly among women, regarding personal
 security while on public transit. Future research to better understand social dynamics
 among AV passengers is acutely needed to inform the design and operation of shared
 mobility services to ensure use among a wide range of potential riders.
• The potential of AV-only facilities. Dedicated (AV-only) special use lanes, roadways,
 or transitways are a possible way to leverage the full benefits of automated control while
 allowing full reuse of the existing roadway system. AV fleets can theoretically operate
 safely with narrower lanes and closer headways, all of which would allow more space-
 and cost-efficient infrastructure than traditional roadway design that accommodates
 human-controlled vehicles. AVs could traverse traditional roadways built for human
 control and then gain access to AV-only facilities for enhanced performance in much the
 same way that modern freeways optimize the operation of automobiles with better
 geometrics and minimum speed limits, which allows safe high-speed travel. AV-only
 facilities may allow safer, high-capacity, and less congested flow.
