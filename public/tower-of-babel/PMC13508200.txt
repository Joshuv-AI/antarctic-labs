Background

Diabetes mellitus constitutes a major public health concern due to its increasing global prevalence and significant impact on health systems [1, 2]. As of 2024, approximately 589 million individuals aged 20 to 79 years are living with diabetes worldwide, representing 10.5% of the global population in this age group [3]. In Spain, the estimated prevalence of diabetes in the general population is 7.64% (8.46% in men, 6.85% in women) [4]. One of the most common and serious complications of diabetes is the diabetic foot, with a global prevalence ranging between 4% and 10% [5, 6]. This prevalence is expected to rise in parallel with the projected increase in diabetes cases, which are estimated to reach 643 million by 2030 and 853 million by 2050 [3, 7]. Diabetic neuropathy is the primary etiological factor for diabetic foot and frequently co‑occurs with peripheral arterial disease in up to 50% of cases [8, 9]. Major risk factors for diabetic foot ulceration include poor glycaemic control, neuropathy, an altered ankle–brachial index, onychomycosis, and a history of previous foot ulcers [10–12].

Diabetic foot represents a significant disruption to patients’ quality of life, leading to reduced physical functioning and overall well-being. It also entails increased healthcare costs and exerts a considerable burden on family, social, and occupational dynamics [13]. Diabetic foot ulcers are frequently complicated by infections, which constitute the primary clinical consequence and are the leading cause of diabetes-related hospitalization [14]. Recurrence rates are notably high, approximately 40% of patients experience a new ulcer within the first year, and up to 65% within three years following ulcer healing [15].

More than 85% of individuals with diabetic foot ulcers will ultimately require lower-limb amputation, the most severe and costly diabetic foot complication [16]. Diabetes mellitus remains the leading cause of nontraumatic amputations in high-income countries [17] and is associated with a high mortality (50% of individuals die within 5 years of amputation, and 61% within 7 years) [18, 19]. In Spain, the direct cost of lower-limb amputation is estimated at $21,875 per case, further illustrating the substantial economic burden associated with diabetic foot [20].

Importantly, diabetic foot is considered a largely preventable condition. Evidence suggests that 75–80% of diabetic foot cases can be avoided through appropriate preventive care [21, 22]. Clinical practice guidelines emphasize core preventive actions such as regular foot assessments, risk stratification, patient education on foot care, and interdisciplinary management approaches [23–26]. Despite the availability and dissemination of evidence-based guidelines, their implementation in routine care remains inconsistent [13, 23, 24, 27–29].

In the Balearic Islands, the health service has established a standardized Diabetic Foot Program to guide diabetic foot prevention in primary care [30]. However, available data indicate substantial variability in the application of this protocol [31], with persistently very low rates of documented foot assessments [30, 32], suggesting that only a small proportion of patients who could potentially benefit are currently being reached.

Multiple barriers contribute to the limited uptake of diabetic‑foot guidelines. On the patient side, limited awareness of diabetic‑foot complications is frequently reported [33, 34]. Practical obstacles such as impaired vision and reduced mobility further hinder self‑care behaviours [35]. Socioeconomic constraints, including difficulties accessing foot‑care services, also contribute to poor adherence [36, 37].

Healthcare professionals face several challenges, including low prioritisation of diabetic‑foot care [38, 39], insufficient training and limited knowledge [40, 41], and heavy workloads with limited time availability that hinder guideline adherence [42, 43]. Structural barriers such as staff shortages, inadequate resources, and ineffective multidisciplinary collaboration further impede implementation [37, 44, 45]. In addition, system‑level limitations—such as inadequate professional guidance, poor continuity of care, and restricted access to specialists—also undermine effective diabetic‑foot management [37, 42, 46].

Implementation science provides a structured approach to enhancing the adoption of evidence‑based interventions in routine care by improving fidelity, quality, and effectiveness [47, 48]. Given the inherent complexity of implementation—shaped by determinants such as evidence characteristics, healthcare‑professional engagement, team processes, and organisational context—using a theoretical framework is essential to guide strategy development and reporting [49, 50]. Implementation Mapping, derived from the Intervention Mapping framework, offers a systematic and participatory process for identifying determinants, and selecting implementation strategies to support the program implementation [51, 52]. Strengthening the implementation of the Diabetic Foot Program is expected to improve fidelity and uptake and ultimately reduce diabetic‑foot–related complications.

Methods

Study aims

This protocol describes a two-arm cluster-randomized controlled trial designed to evaluate the implementation strategy for the Diabetic Foot Program within primary care centres (PCCs).

The primary aim is to evaluate the effectiveness of the Diabetic Foot Program implementation strategy on reach, defined as the proportion of eligible patients with diabetes at each PCC who are included in the Diabetic Foot Program.

The secondary objectives are as follows:

Study design

The iMplanta study is a two‑arm, cluster‑randomized controlled trial, with PCCs within the Mallorca Primary Care Management as the unit of randomization. PCCs will be allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either an intervention group, which will receive an implementation strategy based on the Implementation Mapping framework to support integration of the Diabetic Foot Program, or a control group providing usual care, in which the Diabetic Foot Program will continue to be applied as currently practiced. This comparator was chosen to estimate the added value of a structured implementation strategy over existing practice. The implementation strategy outcomes will be assessed at baseline (covering the 12 months prior to study initiation), and at 6 and 12 months post-implementation. The development and reporting of the interventions follow the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) guidelines [53] and the Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies (StaRI) checklist [54]. This protocol has been designed in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Intervention Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines [55] and complies with the CONSORT extension for cluster‑randomized trials [56].

Any modifications to the implementation strategy or study procedures will be documented and reported using the FRAME-IS framework, a validated tool for systematically reporting adaptations in implementation research [57].

Study setting

The study will be conducted in PCCs within the Spanish National Health Service, which provides universal healthcare coverage. The island of Mallorca has a population exceeding 940,000 inhabitants [58], of whom approximately 750,000 are registered PCC users. A total of 46 PCCs are distributed across the island, organised into four geographical sectors. Each PCC serves an average catchment population of approximately 20,000 residents.

Participants and recruitment

Clusters

Primary care centres with comparable characteristics—patient volume, healthcare workforce size, and territorial catchment area—will be eligible for inclusion at the cluster level. All PCCs belonging to the Mallorca Primary Care Management will be assessed for eligibility. As the implementation of the Diabetic Foot Program will be integrated into routine clinical practice, all healthcare professionals and patients from the participating PCCs will be automatically included in the study.

Individual participants

To assess the prevalence of diabetic‑foot–related complications, cross‑sectional samples of patients with diabetes mellitus will be selected from participating PCCs at three time points: baseline (retrospective data collection covering the 12 months prior to study initiation), and at 6 and 12 months post‑implementation.

Eligibility criteria for individual participants are as follows:

Inclusion criteria: adults aged ≥ 18 years; clinical diagnosis of type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus; registered users of the Public Health Service of the Balearic Islands; assigned to one of the participating PCCs.

Inclusion criteria: adults aged ≥ 18 years; clinical diagnosis of type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus; registered users of the Public Health Service of the Balearic Islands; assigned to one of the participating PCCs.

Exclusion criteria: diagnosis of gestational diabetes; presence of an active diabetic foot ulcer at the time of assessment; history of lower‑limb amputation prior to study initiation.

Exclusion criteria: diagnosis of gestational diabetes; presence of an active diabetic foot ulcer at the time of assessment; history of lower‑limb amputation prior to study initiation.

Sample size calculations

The primary outcome variable for the sample size estimation is the reach of the implementation strategy, defined as the proportion of eligible patients with the Diabetic Foot Program documented in their electronic medical record at the PCC level. Assuming a two-tailed alpha risk of 0.05 and a statistical power greater than 90%, to detect an increase of at least 5% in the intervention group, a total of 20 clusters (10 per group) with 76 patients per cluster will be required (total N = 1,520). An intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.01 has been assumed to account for cluster effects. Sample size calculation was performed using GRANMO (version 8.0).

Randomization and allocation concealment

Participating PCCs will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio to the intervention or control group using a computer-generated random allocation sequence, created by an independent statistician not involved in recruitment, implementation, or analysis. Allocation will be concealed through centralized assignment using a secure REDCap randomization module and revealed only after cluster enrolment.

Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of healthcare professionals and patients will not be feasible; however, recruitment and implementation staff will not have access to the randomization sequence prior to assignment. Statistical analyses will be conducted by independent researchers who will remain blinded to group allocation throughout the study.

Implementation strategy design

The intervention in the PCCs will be designed following the Implementation Mapping methodology [52], as previously described. This model is based on the IM framework [51] and consists of the following five specific tasks:

Task 1: Identification of implementation needs

A review of qualitative or mixed-methods studies exploring the perceptions of patients and healthcare professionals regarding diabetic foot prevention will be conducted to identify barriers and facilitators relevant to our context. In parallel, primary data will be collected using qualitative methods to determine context-specific implementation needs across key stakeholder groups. Initially, 20 participants (professionals and patients from the 4 geographical sectors) will be recruited for qualitative data collection. They will be selected through purposive sampling, although this number may increase or decrease depending on achievement of thematic saturation of the data collected is reached. Individual semi-structured interviews and focus groups will be conducted with (1) patients with diabetes mellitus, (2) program implementers (nurses, physicians, podiatrists) and (3) administrative staff of the Mallorca Primary Care Management. Gender will be considered in the recruitment process to ensure a balanced and representative sample of participants across all stakeholder groups. To facilitate qualitative data collection, these interviews will be conducted according to the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR)-based interview guide [59–61].

Interviews and focus groups will be recorded via audio recorder and then transcribed for analysis. Data analysis (content analysis) will also be carried out using the CFIR framework to facilitate further interpretation of the data and its direct application in the implementation process. The CFIR comprises five domains (innovation, outer setting, inner setting, individuals and implementation process). In addition, the qualitative data analysis software Atlas.ti will be used (Scientific Software Development GmbH).

Task 2: Identification of adoption and implementation outcomes, performance objectives, determinants and change objectives

Once the implementation needs have been identified in Task 1, performance objectives will be specified, which define the specific actions that each actor must carry out to achieve the implementation outcomes. These performance objectives will set out what activities participants will need to carry out for the programme to be implemented. Afterwards, key determinants (factors that influence whether the actions will be implemented) will be identified. Finally, matrices of change objectives will be developed through the identification of implementation needs obtained from the review of available literature and the implementation context as identified through interviews and focus groups.

Task 3: Selection of theoretical methods and design of implementation strategies

This phase aims to use theory‑based methods to influence the determinants identified in Task 2.

In addition, the design of context-specific implementation strategies will be carried out. As a theoretical framework, the ERIC model (Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change) has been selected. This is a compilation of 73 implementation strategies [48, 62] that allows for the selection of interventions to guide the implementation process. Specifically, the CFIR-ERIC tool [59, 63] will be used, which combines both models and will facilitate the selection of implementation strategies based on the CFIR constructs identified in Task 2.

Although the specific strategies will be designed according to the needs identified in the analysis conducted in Task 2, examples of potential implementation strategies will include training healthcare professionals in diabetic foot screening protocols and ensuring the availability of necessary tools and equipment to perform foot examinations. Additional strategies may involve appointing a diabetic foot care coordinator in each health centre, improving referral pathways to podiatry services, allocating protected time during consultations for screening activities, integrating reminders into electronic health records, and conducting regular feedback sessions to monitor progress and reinforce adherence. These strategies aim to address both organizational and individual-level barriers to implementation, fostering a supportive environment for sustained practice change.

In addition, behaviour change models will be used to influence changes at the individual level. These methods are based on different theories, and encompass actions aimed at increasing knowledge, improving risk perception, modifying attitudes and beliefs, increasing self-efficacy, and strengthening skills [51, 52]. The use of Social Cognitive Theory is proposed to address personal determinants [64].

Task 4: Production of protocols and implementation materials

Development of the implementation protocols, materials and activities derived from all the previous tasks, will be carried out. Some examples of implementation materials may include the development of educational resources tailored for clinical staff, such as training manuals, visual aids for clinical consultations, quick-reference guides, or e-learning modules. Additionally, patient-facing materials—such as brochures, posters, or digital content—can be designed to raise awareness of the program and promote engagement with diabetic foot screening and prevention practices.

These materials will facilitate the adoption, implementation and maintenance of the Diabetic Foot Program in PCCs. For this task, representatives of all the parties involved (patients with diabetes mellitus, health professionals (physicians, nurses, podiatrists) and management staff) will be involved in the creation of the final material.

Task 5: Evaluation of implementation outcomes

This task will be developed using the RE-AIM framework [65–67], which evaluates five outcome dimensions: reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance. In addition, three proximal implementation outcomes described by Proctor et al. [68] —acceptability, feasibility, and appropriateness— will be assessed throughout the process. Data on the implementation process will be collected across the pre‑intervention, intervention, and post‑intervention phases using both qualitative and quantitative methods. A further description of the implementation outcomes is detailed in the Outcomes section.

Description of the control group

Over the 12-month study period, the PCCs assigned to the control group will continue to provide usual care as per standard healthcare practices.

Data collection methods

Data collection will be collected at three time points: baseline (corresponding to the 12 months prior to the implementation), and 6 and 12 months after implementation. Data extraction will be performed directly from electronic medical records through the PRISIB platform (Regional Health Information Research Platform in the Balearic Islands) which ensures anonymised data and full patient confidentiality. Additional data extraction will be requested from e-SIAP (Primary Care Information System) of the Health Service of the Balearic Islands through the Unit for the Development of the Primary Care Electronic Medical Record (UDHiC-AP). Quantitative data on implementation outcomes will also be collected through self-administered questionnaires completed by participating healthcare professionals. Given that this is a non‑clinical implementation trial with minimal risk, no independent Data Monitoring Committee is planned.

Data analysis

All data will be complied into a single database, using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) system. Statistical analysis will be performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM, New York, USA) and Stata version 18 (StataCorp, Texas, USA) programs.

Quantitative data will be summarised using descriptive statistics including measures of central tendency, dispersion, and position for quantitative variables. To evaluate the reach and the effectiveness of the implementation strategy compared with control group, adjusting for the cluster effect, generalized estimating equation (GEE) will be used.

Qualitative data obtained from semi-structured interviews and focus groups will be transcribed verbatim and analysed thematically using ATLAS.ti version 25 (Scientific Software Development GmbH). Coding and categorization will follow an inductive-deductive analytical approach informed by the CFIR framework [60, 72]. Methodological rigour will be enhanced through reflexivity, data saturation, and triangulation.

Discussion

The iMplanta study aims to evaluate the reach of an implementation strategy for the Diabetic Foot Program and to determine its effectiveness in reducing diabetic-foot-related complications in Balearic Islands PCCs.

Conducting this study in PCCs, the first point of contact for most individuals within the healthcare system, provides an accessible and practical setting for implementing preventive measures. Evaluating the implementation strategy in real-world clinical conditions will generate robust and generalizable evidence that can inform future large-scale initiatives.

Previous experiences with the implementation of diabetic‑foot–related interventions provide useful insights for the present study. Most initiatives have focused on patient and family education regarding foot care, demonstrating reductions in diabetes‑related foot complications [73–77]. Other studies have implemented clinical pathways that include systematic screening, risk assessment and referral to specialised units, showing improvements in early detection and care coordination [78]. Additional studies have reported positive outcomes; for instance, Allen et al. observed a 60% increase in diabetic‑foot screening within one year following the introduction of a structured implementation model [79]. These implementation strategies commonly combine healthcare professional training, provision of educational or clinical materials, and organisational changes, typically informed by a prior contextual assessment [80].

The integration of the Diabetic Foot Program into Mallorca’s PCCs will be informed by principles of Implementation Science and guided by the Implementation Mapping framework. Despite this structured approach, several practical challenges may arise. The limited resources of PCCs, particularly in high-demand areas or those facing staff shortages, may hinder optimal implementation of the Diabetic Foot Program. Nevertheless, context-specific barriers and facilitators will be identified during the implementation strategy-development phase. Implementation Mapping will be employed to develop strategies that address these challenges and enhance the implementation and long-term sustainability. Organizational changes and the allocation of additional resources to healthcare centres may be required, which could introduce further challenges. Resistance to change among healthcare professionals may also constrain the uptake of new practices. Another potential limitation is related to the reliance on secondary data sources. Some individuals with diabetes may present active foot ulcers that are not accurately classified in their electronic medical records, potentially leading to misclassification. To reduce this bias, a comprehensive list of relevant diagnostic codes will be used to improve case identification. Despite these challenges, the current policy and healthcare environment strongly support the implementation of diabetic-foot prevention strategies. The iMplanta study aligns with the National Health Service Diabetes Strategy, which emphasizes the management of chronic complications of diabetes mellitus [26, 81]. At the regional level, diabetic foot care is also prioritized within the Diabetes Strategy of the Balearic Islands [82, 83]. Additionally, other ongoing projects focus on implementing strategies to enhance healthcare quality. This project is further strengthened by the robust evidence supporting recommendations for diabetic foot prevention and their cost-effectiveness. Finally, the use of Implementation Mapping, a methodology proven effective in implementing health-promotion interventions in other settings, enhances the potential success of this study. The implementation of this strategy is expected to increase awareness among healthcare managers, professionals, and patients about the importance of diabetic-foot prevention and the substantial burden of this prevalent condition. Improvements in screening and preventive practices among healthcare professionals, alongside enhanced interprofessional collaboration, are anticipated. Moreover, greater adherence to Diabetic Foot Program may strengthen early detection and risk stratification of at-risk feet, thereby reducing the incidence of complications, enhancing patients’ quality of life, and potentially lowering related mortality.

Implementation of the Diabetic Foot Program is also expected to reduce healthcare costs by minimizing complications. Prevention-focused models of care, like the one underlying this program, are both simple and cost-effective. Evidence from the United Kingdom’s National Health Service indicates that diabetic-foot-related care represents nearly 1% of the healthcare budget, with more than 90% of these costs associated with treatment [84]. Additional studies report that preventive care increases quality-adjusted life years while reducing healthcare expenditure [85, 86].

Additionally, the program is expected to promote a shift toward an evidence-based practice environment within the healthcare organization, encouraging critical thinking among healthcare professionals and integrating evidence-based recommendations into clinical practice to improve patient care.

Finally, the adoption of the Diabetic Foot Program in PCCs is anticipated to enhance patient autonomy by actively involving them in the implementation process. This empowerment will place patients at the centre of the healthcare system, reinforcing their role as key stakeholders in managing their own health.

Despite strong international recommendations promoting preventive strategies that combine patient education, regular foot examinations and risk stratification, there is limited evidence on how these approaches are effectively implemented and sustained in routine clinical practice. This gap underscores the relevance of the iMplanta study, which aims to evaluate the implementation of the Diabetic Foot Program in PCCs across Mallorca and to identify the most effective strategies for reducing diabetic‑foot‑related complications. The findings may support the development of a scalable and sustainable implementation model applicable both within and beyond the region.

Declarations

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study complies with the ethical principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. All procedures have been reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Balearic Islands Health Service Research Ethics Committee (CEI-IB; Ref No: IB 5701/25). Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants at the time of enrolment. All collected data will be anonymized and treated with strict confidentiality. Participants may voluntarily withdraw their informed consent at any time. Data collected prior to participant withdrawal will be retained and used for the conduct of the study as indicated in the informed consent. Data obtained through interviews and focus groups will be pseudonymised during the transcription process, and identifiable data will be removed to ensure participant confidentiality. The data obtained will be entered into a database, where the information concerning each participant will be directly anonymised after extraction from the clinical history. This information will remain under the custody of the principal investigators, and its access will be restricted to them, as well as to the collaborating investigators, who will sign a confidentiality document.

Regarding the collection of data through qualitative methodology, candidates will be asked to participate, will be provided with a data collection sheet, and will be asked to complete a questionnaire. These procedures are subject to the provisions of Organic Law 3/2018, of 5 December, on the protection of personal data and guarantee of digital rights.

This is a non‑clinical implementation study in which no experimental clinical interventions are delivered to participants. No harms related to participation are expected, given the non‑interventional nature of the study. Therefore, no ancillary or post‑trial clinical care is planned beyond usual care routinely provided within the health system. No financial or other compensation for participation is offered. In the unlikely event that any harm attributable to study procedures were to occur, it would be managed in accordance with applicable institutional policies and standard healthcare procedures.

The results of this study will be disseminated through multiple channels.
