Epidemiology, antimicrobial resistance, and mortality of bloodstream infections in hemodialysis patients: an 11-year retrospective cohort study from southern Saudi Arabia
Authors: Mona M Alshahrani, Ruqayyah Mohammed Ali Bilali, Safia Abdullah Mohammed, Ibrahim Tawhari, Mohammed Alshehri, Abdullah Jaber Asiri, Hana Alahmari, Basma Al Ghamdi, Esmahan Tahtouh, Nawal Alwadai, Nermeen Mohamed Abd Elnaby, Ali Somily, Khalifa Binkhamis, Hassan Misfer N Taresh, Sultan Saeed M Alqahtani, Ahmad Ali A Al Farhan, Hassan Yahya M Asiri, Ahmed Abdullah A Alamoud, Yazeed Mohammed S Alqahtani, Waleed Khaled A Alzailaie, Yahya Saeed Y Alshafea, Abdulah J Alqahtani, Yahya Shabi

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBloodstream infections (BSI) cause major morbidity and mortality in hemodialysis patients, yet contemporary Saudi data on pathogens, antimicrobial resistance, and outcomes remain limited. We described epidemiology, antimicrobial susceptibility, multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotype prevalence, and unadjusted associations between MDR phenotypes and 30-day in-hospital mortality among maintenance hemodialysis BSI episodes.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort included maintenance hemodialysis patients with positive blood cultures at Aseer Central Hospital, southern Saudi Arabia, from January 2013 through July 2024. Analyses were structured by unit of analysis: unique patients, BSI episodes (after 14-day deduplication), isolates, and hospitalized episodes for mortality. Susceptibility used CLSI M100 categorical results. Unadjusted risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals and Fisher exact tests were descriptive; no multivariable model was fitted (sparse events).Results201 patients contributed 297 BSI episodes and 389 isolates; 234 hospitalized. Gram-positive organisms predominated (69.0%), led by staphylococci other than Staphylococcus aureus (SOSA, 42.8%), Enterobacterales (21.2%), and S. aureus (17.2%). The 30-day in-hospital mortality was 11.1% (26/234). Linezolid (99.2%) and vancomycin (96.0%) were most active; fluoroquinolone susceptibility was low (45–49%). MDR phenotypes occurred in 24.2% of isolates (MRSA 56.4%, ESC-R 65.0%, CRE 27.3%, VRE 29.2%, CRAB 80.0%). Organism-restricted analysis found CRE-positive episodes with higher mortality than carbapenem-susceptible Enterobacterales (3/8 vs. 1/21), without conventional significance (risk ratio 7.88, 95% CI 0.95–65.06; P = 0.053).ConclusionsBSI were predominantly gram-positive with a substantial gram-negative and MDR burden. The CRE-mortality association was unadjusted, imprecise, and hypothesis-generating, requiring confirmation in larger adjusted cohorts. Findings support local antimicrobial stewardship and active MDR surveillance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12882-026-05127-1.Keywords: Bloodstream infection, Hemodialysis, Antimicrobial resistance, Carbapenem-resistant enterobacterales, Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, Multidrug resistance, Mortality, Saudi Arabia

Introduction

Patients with end-stage kidney disease receiving maintenance hemodialysis bear a disproportionate burden of bloodstream infections (BSI). Impaired immunity, recurrent breaches of skin integrity through vascular access, advanced age, comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, and frequent healthcare exposure together predispose this population to invasive bacterial infections [1–4]. Infection is the second leading cause of death in hemodialysis patients and accounts for approximately 35% of all-cause mortality [1, 3, 5]. BSI in particular is a frequent cause of hospitalization, can result in metastatic complications such as endocarditis, vertebral osteomyelitis, and septic arthritis, and is associated with substantial per-episode costs [1, 5].

The microbiology of hemodialysis catheter-related infections is typically dominated by gram-positive skin flora (staphylococci), which account for 60–80% of infections [3]. However, several contemporary cohorts have documented a shift toward gram-negative pathogens: in Israel, gram-negative organisms caused 46.3% of BSI events among hemodialysis patients [6], and comparable trends have been reported in the Philippines [7] and Saudi Arabia [8]. Multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), extended-spectrum cephalosporin-resistant (ESC-R) Enterobacterales, and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) complicate empirical therapy and are associated with adverse outcomes in hemodialysis-associated BSI. In contemporary cohorts MDROs may comprise nearly half of catheter-related BSI isolates [7], and CRE bloodstream infections have been reported with 30-day mortality of 41.7%, with outcomes linked to appropriate empirical therapy [9]. Resistant gram-positive infection also carries a substantial burden: in hemodialysis BSI of unknown origin, MRSA was independently associated with mortality (odds ratio 6.20), and lack of initial anti-MRSA coverage was common (87.5%) and accompanied by very high mortality (85.7%) [5]. Regional data show resistant gram-negative burdens in hemodialysis settings, with ESC-R/ESBL proportions of 46.7% and MDR proportions of 13.3% in a Saudi series [10]. Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia carries a 30-day mortality of 20–30% [11], underscoring the urgency of locally derived resistance data to guide timely therapeutic decisions.

In Saudi Arabia, more than 20,000 patients receive maintenance dialysis, yet published epidemiological data on bloodstream infection events in outpatient hemodialysis settings remain limited. National surveillance using the Hemodialysis Event Surveillance Network across 152 outpatient centers reported a positive blood culture rate of 0.41 per 100 patient-months [12], while a single-center study from Jeddah documented gram-negative organisms accounting for more than half of central-line blood culture isolates [8]. Carbapenem resistance rates exceeding 30% have been reported regionally for Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Klebsiella pneumoniae [10], and the pooled MRSA prevalence among S. aureus isolates across Saudi institutions has been estimated at 32.5% [13]. Hemodialysis-specific susceptibility data remain scarce, and comparable surveillance across the Gulf Cooperation Council is heterogeneous. The proportion of bloodstream infections among confirmed infections in Saudi hospitals has been reported to range from 1% to 11% [14].

We therefore conducted a retrospective cohort study at Aseer Central Hospital, a tertiary referral center in southern Saudi Arabia, to characterize the epidemiology, microbiology, antimicrobial susceptibility, and clinical outcomes of BSI among maintenance hemodialysis patients over 2013–2024. Specifically, we aimed to describe pathogen distribution and antimicrobial susceptibility, determine the prevalence of MDR phenotypes, and report unadjusted descriptive associations between MDR phenotypes and 30-day in-hospital mortality.

Methods

Study design and setting

This single-center retrospective cohort study analyzed bloodstream infections among hemodialysis patients treated at Aseer Central Hospital, a tertiary care center in southern Saudi Arabia, from January 2013 through July 2024. The study was approved by the institutional review board with waiver of informed consent. This report follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline [15].

Data sources

Five hospital information system databases were integrated: (1) admissions data containing demographics, admission and discharge dates, and discharge disposition; (2) microbiology records with organism identification, specimen type, and antimicrobial susceptibility results; (3) operations registry documenting surgical procedures including vascular access creation; (4) outpatient clinic encounters; and (5) pharmacy dispensing records. Data were linked using medical record numbers with exact string matching after normalization.

Eligibility and hemodialysis patient identification

Patients were included if they met hemodialysis identification criteria with chart confirmation and had at least one positive blood culture during the study period. Peritoneal dialysis patients, patients receiving continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) or short-term dialysis only, Burns Care Unit patients, patients with fungal bloodstream infections, outpatient cultures without hospital admission, and blood cultures assessed as contaminated (see below) were excluded.

Hemodialysis patients were identified using a multi-signal evidence algorithm that integrated data from hospital information system databases. Signals included arteriovenous fistula or graft procedures (weight: 3.0), end-stage renal disease or end-stage kidney disease diagnosis codes (weight: 2.0), nephrology service encounters (weight: 2.0), dialysis catheter specimens (weight: 2.0), chronic kidney disease flags (weight: 1.0), and hemodialysis-specific medications (weight: 0.5). Patients were triaged as higher-likelihood hemodialysis (e.g., vascular access procedures or concordant ESRD diagnosis plus nephrology service) or as candidate hemodialysis (cumulative signal weight ≥ 2.0) for manual chart review. All patients included in the final cohort had maintenance hemodialysis confirmed by chart documentation (nephrology notes, dialysis orders, or dialysis flow sheets).

Importantly, hemodialysis status for all included patients was supported by chart documentation, including nephrology notes, dialysis orders, or dialysis flow sheets. The multi-signal algorithm was used as a case-finding tool to identify candidate patients, while final inclusion required confirmation of maintenance hemodialysis in the medical record.

Bloodstream infection definition

Bloodstream infection episodes were defined as positive blood cultures obtained ≥ 14 days apart in the same patient; cultures within 14 days were considered part of the same episode. This interval balances sensitivity with clinical relevance for distinguishing distinct infectious episodes. Polymicrobial cultures collected on the same date were considered a single BSI episode. For clinical and outcome analyses, the first organism isolated was designated the index isolate to characterize the episode. For microbiological and antimicrobial susceptibility analyses, all organisms recovered in polymicrobial episodes were included. Recurrent episodes were defined as new infections occurring > 14 days after a prior episode in the same patient [16].

Definition of Clinically Significant Bloodstream Infection and Contamination. A clinically significant bloodstream infection was defined as isolation of a recognized pathogenic organism from at least one blood culture in the presence of compatible clinical features. For organisms commonly classified as skin commensals, including staphylococci other than Staphylococcus aureus (SOSA), Corynebacterium species, Bacillus species (excluding B. anthracis), and Cutibacterium acnes, episodes were classified as clinically significant if (1) the same organism was recovered from two or more blood culture sets obtained within 48 h, OR (2) a single positive culture was accompanied by documented clinical evidence of infection (fever, leukocytosis, sepsis, or initiation of targeted antimicrobial therapy as recorded in the medical record). This pragmatic retrospective definition was used because complete prospective adjudication data were not available; the inherent risk of overclassifying contaminants as clinically significant episodes is discussed under Limitations. Isolates of skin commensals not meeting these criteria were classified as contaminants and excluded from BSI episode counts.

Hospital admission linkage

Blood culture dates were linked to hospital admissions using hierarchical matching rules. Cultures obtained during an admission (between admission and discharge dates inclusive) received high linkage confidence. Emergency department cultures followed by hospital admission within 3 days were linked with medium confidence, and the admission date was set to the culture date. Cultures obtained within 2 days after discharge were linked to the preceding admission with medium confidence, and the discharge date was extended to the culture date. Unlinked cultures represented emergency department visits without subsequent admission. Scheduled outpatient hemodialysis encounters that were administratively recorded as short ‘admissions’ were not considered inpatient admissions for linkage purposes. Blood cultures collected during outpatient dialysis sessions were classified as outpatient unless they were associated with a true inpatient hospitalization (ward bed stay) or were followed by inpatient admission within 3 days. Intensive care unit length of stay was calculated as cumulative ICU days from ward transfer records linked to each admission, capturing patients who were ever admitted to the ICU during their hospital stay.

Outcome ascertainment

The primary outcome was 30-day in-hospital mortality, defined as death occurring within 30 days of the index blood culture date. Mortality analyses were restricted to episodes successfully linked to hospital admissions. In-hospital mortality status was determined from the discharge disposition field in admission records, supplemented by manual verification from master hospital information system files for patients with missing discharge disposition. Death dates corresponded to hospital discharge dates for patients who expired.

For patients with recurrent bloodstream infection episodes, each death was attributed to one episode to prevent duplicate counting. Episode windowing was applied: deaths occurring on or after a subsequent episode date were attributed to the later episode, ensuring each mortality event was assigned to the temporally closest episode.

Microbiology

Organisms were grouped into clinically relevant categories: SOSA, Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcus species, Enterobacterales, and non-fermenting gram-negative bacilli (including Pseudomonas and Acinetobacter). ESKAPE pathogens (Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter species) were flagged for surveillance reporting. Organism identification was performed using the VITEK 2 microbial identification system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France). In cases of inconclusive species-level identification, additional testing was performed using the MicroScan WalkAway system (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) in accordance with standard microbiology laboratory protocols at Aseer Central Hospital.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility was determined by the hospital microbiology laboratory using the VITEK 2 Compact system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France), with selective use of the MicroScan WalkAway system (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) for supplementary testing or when automated results were uncertain. Disk diffusion (Oxoid, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Basingstoke, UK) and gradient diffusion (Etest; bioMérieux) were applied selectively per standard operating procedures to resolve discrepancies or assess agents not reliably tested by automated systems. AST interpretations reflected the categorical susceptibility results reported by the clinical microbiology laboratory at the time of testing, according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 standards in use during the corresponding period; tigecycline was interpreted per EUCAST criteria. Raw minimum inhibitory concentrations were not consistently available for retrospective reinterpretation against a single contemporary CLSI breakpoint version. Only organism–antibiotic combinations with established interpretive criteria were analyzed; combinations without breakpoints were classified as not recommended (NR), combinations with documented intrinsic resistance per CLSI M100 were classified as intrinsically resistant (IR), and combinations not tested were classified as not tested (NT).

MDR phenotypes were defined as follows: CRE, resistance to ertapenem, imipenem, or meropenem, with imipenem excluded for Proteus, Providencia, and Morganella species due to intrinsic elevated MICs; carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) and carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA), resistance to imipenem or meropenem, excluding ertapenem due to intrinsic resistance; vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE), Enterococcus species resistant to vancomycin; methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), non-susceptibility to oxacillin or cefoxitin; and extended-spectrum cephalosporin-resistant (ESC-R) Enterobacterales, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella species, or Proteus mirabilis resistant to third-generation cephalosporins (ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, or ceftazidime). The phenotypic term ESC-R was used rather than ESBL because confirmatory enzyme testing was not performed; resistance was defined by revised CLSI M100 breakpoints alone.

Comorbidity assessment

Comorbidity burden was measured using a modified Elixhauser Comorbidity Index derived from admission diagnosis codes supplemented with data from outpatient visits, surgical procedures, pathology reports, and pharmacy dispensing records. The index comprised 29 conditions coded as binary indicators, including ischemic heart disease as an addition to the standard Elixhauser framework. Pharmacy supplementation required more than one dispensing of a disease-specific medication class before the index culture date to minimize false-positive ascertainment from single prescriptions. Chronic kidney disease and electrolyte disorders were assigned to all hemodialysis patients by clinical definition.

Statistical analysis

Units of analysis. Analyses were structured by the appropriate unit. Unique patients (n = 201) were used to describe the source population. BSI episodes, defined after 14-day deduplication (n = 297), were used for clinical epidemiology and pathogen distribution. Isolate-level analyses (n = 389) were used for antimicrobial susceptibility, because polymicrobial episodes could contribute more than one organism. Mortality analyses were restricted to hospitalized BSI episodes with admission linkage and 30-day in-hospital outcome ascertainment (n = 234 hospitalized episodes with documented in-hospital outcome; admission-linkage cultures n = 314); the phenotype–mortality descriptive analysis was further restricted to first BSI episodes per patient with hospital admission linkage (n = 159; 18 deaths) to avoid double counting. Each Results subsection and each table denominator below is presented with reference to its unit of analysis. Clinical characteristics were summarized per BSI episode; patients with multiple episodes could contribute more than once, and time-varying variables (age, comorbidity profile) were captured as of the index culture/admission date for each episode. Baseline characteristics were stratified by clinical setting (emergency department vs. hospitalized) and by 30-day in-hospital mortality (survived vs. died).

Continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were summarized as frequency (percentage). Differences in categorical variables between survivors and non-survivors were assessed using the Pearson χ test (without continuity correction) when all expected cell counts were ≥ 5, and the Fisher exact test when any expected cell count was < 5. Antimicrobial susceptibility was calculated as the number of susceptible isolates divided by the total number tested, restricted to valid organism–antibiotic combinations per CLSI M100 guidelines; the unit of analysis was the isolate (n = 389), not the patient. For the phenotype–mortality analysis we used organism-restricted comparators, comparing each resistance phenotype only within its relevant organism group (e.g., CRE vs. carbapenem-susceptible Enterobacterales; MRSA vs. MSSA), restricted to first BSI episodes per patient with admission linkage. Unadjusted risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals (log–risk-ratio Wald/Katz approximation, i.e. not an exact method; reported as not estimable where a cell contained zero events) were calculated as descriptive measures, with Fisher exact P values because cell counts were sparse. Because the number of deaths within each organism-restricted stratum was very small (1–5 per stratum), no multivariable model was fitted; phenotype–mortality estimates are descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than causal or independent associations.

Data visualization included bar charts illustrating temporal trends in bloodstream infection episodes and pathogen distribution, a resistance heatmap displaying antimicrobial non-susceptibility rates by pathogen with color gradation proportional to resistance percentage, and horizontal bar charts representing multidrug-resistant phenotype prevalence. Figures were generated using Python (version 3.11) with the pandas (v2.1), matplotlib (v3.8), and seaborn (v0.13) libraries.

All statistical tests were 2-sided. Given the small number of events, P values are presented as descriptive measures rather than as confirmatory tests; the term “statistically significant” is therefore avoided in interpreting the phenotype–mortality analyses. Analyses were performed using Stata version 19.5 (StataCorp LLC).

Results

Study population

From 54,111 blood cultures obtained from 22,820 patients at Aseer Central Hospital between January 2013 and July 2024, a multi-signal algorithm identified 817 candidate maintenance hemodialysis patients (Fig. 1); chart confirmation and eligibility screening yielded the final analytic cohort. After exclusion of 11 records (3 Burns Care Unit isolates, 3 fungal isolates, 1 outpatient culture without admission, and 4 cultures outside the study period), the analytic dataset comprised 201 unique maintenance hemodialysis patients (the source population), 389 bacterial isolates (the isolate-level denominator), and, after 14-day episode-level deduplication, 297 unique BSI episodes (the episode-level denominator).

Overall, 314 of 389 cultures (80.7%) were successfully linked to admissions and form the basis of admission-linkage analyses; mortality data were complete for all 314 linked admissions. Hospitalized episodes with admission linkage and 30-day in-hospital outcome ascertainment (n = 234) form the basis of mortality analyses, and first BSI episodes per patient with admission linkage (n = 159; 18 deaths) form the basis of the phenotype–mortality descriptive analysis (Table 3). Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the 297 BSI episodes are presented in Table 1 (episode-level denominator; patients with multiple episodes could contribute more than once). The median age at episode was 60 years (IQR, 31), and 201 episodes (67.7%) occurred in men. More than two-fifths of episodes (122 [41.1%]) occurred in patients aged ≥ 65 years. Cardiovascular disease was the most prevalent comorbidity group (245 [82.5%]), followed by diabetes and obesity (101 [34.0%]). The median modified Elixhauser Comorbidity Index score was 3 (IQR, 3). Sixty-three episodes (21.2%) were identified in the emergency department without subsequent admission, while 234 (78.8%) were linked to inpatient hospitalization. Among the 234 hospitalized episodes, median hospital length of stay was 13 days (IQR, 21) and 97 (32.7%) involved an intensive care unit (ICU) admission (median ICU stay 14 days [IQR, 31]). Of the 297 episodes, 201 (67.7%) were first episodes and 96 (32.3%) were recurrent; 24 (8.1%) were polymicrobial.

Among the 234 hospitalized BSI episodes with admission linkage and outcome ascertainment, 26 (11.1%) ended in 30-day in-hospital mortality. Compared with episodes ending in survival, episodes ending in death involved older patients (median age 64 vs. 60 years) and more frequently female patients (12 [46.2%] vs. 71 [34.1%]; P = 0.227), and were more often linked to ICU admission (20 [76.9%] vs. 74 [35.6%]; P < 0.001) (Table 1). The median time from admission to positive blood culture was longer for episodes ending in death (13.5 days [IQR, 39.0] vs. 1.0 day [IQR, 5.5]), and recurrent episodes accounted for a larger proportion of fatal episodes (13 [50.0%] vs. 62 [29.8%]; P = 0.038). No meaningful differences were observed for age category (P = 0.390), comorbidity burden, or polymicrobial infection (P = 0.483). Because these comparisons are unadjusted, descriptors such as “significantly” are intentionally avoided.

Pathogen distribution

At the BSI episode level (n = 297), gram-positive organisms predominated (205 [69.0%]). Staphylococci other than Staphylococcus aureus (SOSA) were the most frequently isolated organisms (127 [42.8%]), followed by Enterobacterales (63 [21.2%]), Staphylococcus aureus (51 [17.2%]), non-fermenting gram-negative bacilli (27 [9.1%]), and Enterococcus species (22 [7.4%]) (Table 1). Among first episodes per patient (n = 201), the pathogen-group distribution is shown in Figure S2. ESKAPE pathogens were identified in 115 episodes (38.7%). The distribution of pathogen groups did not differ meaningfully between fatal and non-fatal hospitalized episodes (gram-positive vs. gram-negative, P = 0.240; ESKAPE pathogens, P = 0.850).

Antimicrobial susceptibility

Antimicrobial susceptibility is presented at the isolate level (n = 389) in Table 2 (with Table S1 and Fig. 2 for organism-specific patterns). Linezolid showed the highest overall susceptibility (238 of 240 isolates tested [99.2%]), followed by vancomycin (243 of 253 [96.0%]). Among carbapenems, meropenem (64 of 81 [79.0%]) and imipenem (63 of 81 [77.8%]) retained good activity, whereas ertapenem susceptibility was lower (23 of 38 [60.5%]). Aminoglycoside susceptibility ranged from 60.3% for gentamicin (176/292) to 78.8% for amikacin (67/85). Fluoroquinolone susceptibility was uniformly low: ciprofloxacin 46.7% (98/210), levofloxacin 45.3% (124/274), and moxifloxacin 48.6% (85/175). Oxacillin/cefoxitin susceptibility was 21.4% (47/220) and ampicillin susceptibility was 20.4% (11/54).

Among organism-specific patterns (Fig. 2; Table S1), Acinetobacter baumannii isolates exhibited low carbapenem susceptibility (1/4 [25.0%] each for imipenem and meropenem) and limited cephalosporin activity (cefepime 1/4 [25.0%]). Among Enterobacterales, Escherichia coli retained high carbapenem susceptibility (meropenem 9/10 [90.0%]) whereas Klebsiella pneumoniae was lower (meropenem 7/10 [70.0%]). For gram-positive organisms, vancomycin susceptibility was near-universal among SOSA (75/76 [98.7%]) and S. aureus (37/37 [100%]) but notably lower for Enterococcus species (11/18 [61.1%]).

Isolates recovered from episodes ending in 30-day in-hospital mortality showed lower susceptibility across several antibiotic classes than isolates from episodes ending in survival (Table 2; denominators are isolates tested, not patients). Carbapenem susceptibility among isolates from fatal episodes was 40.0% for imipenem and meropenem (4/10 each) and 14.3% for ertapenem (1/7), compared with 77.4%, 80.4%, and 64.0% among non-fatal episodes, respectively. Fluoroquinolone susceptibility among isolates from fatal episodes was 15.8% for ciprofloxacin (3/19) and 23.8% for levofloxacin (5/21), compared with 49.0% and 47.3% among non-fatal episodes. These comparisons reflect differences in isolate susceptibility profiles and should not be interpreted at the patient level.

Multidrug-resistant phenotypes

At the isolate level (n = 389), 94 (24.2%) harboured at least one MDR phenotype (Table 2). Among organism-specific phenotypes, MRSA was the most prevalent (44 of 78 S. aureus isolates [56.4%]), followed by ESC-R Enterobacterales (26 of 40 tested [65.0%]), VRE (7 of 24 [29.2%]), CRE (24 of 88 [27.3%]), and carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa (CRPA; 3 of 20 [15.0%]). CRAB was detected in 4 of 5 A. baumannii isolates (80.0%); the small denominator limits interpretation. Among first BSI isolates per patient (n = 201), the distribution of MDR phenotypes is shown in Figure S3.

An exploratory unadjusted analysis was performed on first BSI episodes per patient with hospital admission linkage (n = 159; 18 deaths), using organism-restricted comparators in which each resistance phenotype was compared only within its relevant organism group (Table 3). CRE-positive episodes showed higher observed 30-day in-hospital mortality than carbapenem-susceptible Enterobacterales (3/8 [37.5%] vs. 1/21 [4.8%]; unadjusted risk ratio 7.88, 95% CI 0.95–65.06; Fisher exact P = 0.053), but this comparison did not reach conventional significance, the confidence interval was extremely wide, and it rested on only 4 deaths within Enterobacterales. The other organism-restricted comparisons did not indicate a clear association: ESC-R versus ESC-susceptible Enterobacterales (risk ratio 2.67, 95% CI 0.31–23.00; P = 0.539) and VRE versus vancomycin-susceptible Enterococcus (risk ratio 0.86, 95% CI 0.19–3.81; P = 1.000); the MRSA versus MSSA, CRAB, and CRPA comparisons were not estimable owing to zero events in one cell. No organism-restricted stratum contained enough deaths to support multivariable adjustment, so only unadjusted descriptive comparisons are presented. These findings are hypothesis-generating only and do not establish an independent or organism-specific mortality effect.

Discussion

Principal findings. In this 11-year single-center retrospective cohort of 201 maintenance hemodialysis patients contributing 297 BSI episodes and 389 isolates, gram-positive organisms predominated (69.0% of episodes), with SOSA the most frequent group (42.8% of episodes), followed by Enterobacterales (21.2%) and S. aureus (17.2%); gram-negative organisms accounted for the remaining 31.0% of episodes. The 30-day in-hospital mortality among hospitalized BSI episodes (n = 234) was 11.1%. At the isolate level, linezolid (99.2%) and vancomycin (96.0%) retained high activity, while fluoroquinolone susceptibility was uniformly low (45–49%). Approximately one in four isolates (24.2%) harboured at least one MDR phenotype. In exploratory unadjusted descriptive analysis of first BSI episodes per patient with hospital admission linkage (n = 159; 18 deaths), CRE-positive episodes showed higher observed 30-day in-hospital mortality than carbapenem-susceptible Enterobacterales episodes; this estimate was imprecise, unadjusted, did not reach conventional significance, and was based on a small number of events; this descriptive estimate is imprecise, unadjusted, and based on a small number of events.

Comparison with previous studies. The predominance of gram-positive organisms in our cohort is consistent with the epidemiology of hemodialysis catheter-related infection, where gram-positive skin flora typically account for 60–80% of isolates [3]. AbuTaha et al. reported an even larger gram-positive predominance in Palestine (83.9% of 118 BSI isolates, CoNS 74.6%) [1]. The relatively lower SOSA proportion in our series (42.8%) may reflect differences in vascular access utilisation and the inclusion of both catheter-related and non-catheter-related BSI. The Japanese single-center study by Shima et al. reported gram-positive bacteria in 64.3% of BSI of unknown origin [5], and the Jeddah hemodialysis project documented a higher gram-negative contribution, with gram-negative organisms accounting for more than half of central-line blood culture isolates [8]; comparable trends have been reported elsewhere [6, 7], likely reflecting differences in vascular access and infection-control practices [2, 17].

The gram-negative contribution in our cohort (31.0%) is consistent with reports from contemporary hemodialysis BSI cohorts [6, 7] and has implications for empirical therapy, particularly where vancomycin-based regimens are commonly used [1, 3].

Antimicrobial resistance and stewardship implications. The MRSA rate among S. aureus isolates in our cohort (56.4%) exceeds the pooled MRSA prevalence of 32.5% reported in a Saudi national overview (2013–2023) [13] and rates from international comparators [1, 6, 10]. In the Japanese cohort by Shima et al., MRSA was associated with mortality in BSI of unknown origin and 87.5% of MRSA-associated cases did not receive initial anti-MRSA coverage, with a mortality of 85.7% in that subgroup [5]; these data underscore the importance of timely empirical anti-MRSA coverage in settings with high MRSA burden.

The CRE prevalence of 27.3% among Enterobacterales is concerning and aligns with regional reports [10, 13]; prior molecular surveillance from the same institution documented predominance of blaOXA-48–like alongside blaNDM and blaKPC determinants in carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae, providing local context for the phenotypic CRE burden observed here [18]. The near-universal carbapenem resistance among A. baumannii isolates (CRAB 80% of 5 isolates) further illustrates the regional MDR burden, although the small denominator precludes meaningful inference. Mortality analysis and interpretation. In response to the reviewers, we performed an exploratory unadjusted analysis using organism-restricted comparators, comparing each resistance phenotype only within its relevant organism group (Table 3). Against the appropriate comparator of carbapenem-susceptible Enterobacterales, CRE-positive episodes showed a higher observed 30-day in-hospital mortality, but the difference did not reach conventional significance (risk ratio 7.88, 95% CI 0.95–65.06; Fisher exact P = 0.053), the confidence interval was extremely wide, and the comparison rested on only four deaths within Enterobacterales. None of the other organism-restricted comparisons indicated a clear association, and several (MRSA vs. MSSA, CRAB, CRPA) were not estimable because of zero events in one cell. No stratum contained enough deaths to support multivariable adjustment. We therefore regard the phenotype–mortality findings as hypothesis-generating only; they are consistent with the wider literature suggesting that carbapenem resistance may worsen outcome [9], but our data cannot establish an independent or causal effect. Confirmation requires larger, prospectively adjudicated cohorts with organism-restricted and adjusted analyses.

The 30-day in-hospital mortality of 11.1% in our hospitalized BSI cohort is broadly comparable to published hemodialysis catheter-related BSI series, with reported in-hospital mortality of approximately 15% [4] and 6.1% [7], and 22–33% among Japanese hemodialysis BSI subgroups [5]. The somewhat lower mortality in our cohort may in part reflect the high proportion of SOSA, a group with generally lower virulence than S. aureus. S. aureus bacteremia carries a 30-day mortality of 20–30% in the broader literature [11].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths and limitations. Strengths include an 11-year surveillance window, one of the longest reported for hemodialysis BSI in the region; a reproducible multi-signal case-finding algorithm with chart-level confirmation of maintenance hemodialysis status; the use of standard CLSI-based MDR definitions to facilitate comparison with other studies and surveillance reports; and STROBE-adherent reporting [15].

Several limitations should be considered. (1) This was a single-center retrospective study; generalisability to other institutions and regions with different vascular access practices and prescribing patterns is limited [12]. (2) Vascular access type was not captured, precluding access-specific risk stratification despite known associations between catheter use and BSI [2, 17]. (3) Comorbidity ascertainment relied on administrative coding and pharmacy records, which may have introduced misclassification. (4) Mortality analysis was restricted to in-hospital deaths within 30 days; post-discharge mortality was not captured. (5) Phenotype–mortality estimates were unadjusted and based on small numerator counts and were therefore presented descriptively; no multivariable model was fitted. (6) Although we now present organism-restricted comparator analyses (Table 3), comparing each phenotype within its own organism group, the number of deaths within each organism stratum was very small (1–5 per stratum), confidence intervals were wide, and several comparisons were not estimable because of zero events in one cell; these analyses are therefore descriptive and underpowered, and multivariable adjustment was not feasible. (7) Classification of skin commensals (notably SOSA) as clinically significant BSI used a pragmatic retrospective definition combining repeated culture positivity or single positivity plus documented clinical features/treatment; the inherent risk of overclassifying contaminants as clinically significant infection should be considered when interpreting the high SOSA proportion. (8) Antimicrobial susceptibility reflects laboratory-reported categorical results at the time of testing per CLSI standards in force during the corresponding period; raw minimum inhibitory concentrations were not consistently available for retrospective reinterpretation against a single contemporary CLSI breakpoint version, and temporal changes in CLSI breakpoints over 2013–2024 may have introduced classification heterogeneity. (9) Molecular characterisation of carbapenemase genes was not performed in this study; prior work from the same institution describes the local molecular epidemiology of carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae and provides genomic context for the phenotypic resistance patterns reported here [18].

Implications for practice and future research. The near-universal susceptibility to linezolid (99.2%) and vancomycin (96.0%) supports reliable empirical gram-positive coverage in hemodialysis BSI, consistent with regional and international experience [1]. The low fluoroquinolone susceptibility (45–49%) argues against routine empirical use of fluoroquinolones in this setting. Given the substantial gram-negative contribution and the high CRE prevalence, initial regimens for hospitalised hemodialysis patients with suspected BSI should ensure adequate gram-negative coverage guided by local resistance epidemiology, recognising that carbapenem-resistant gram-negative pathogens are an important regional problem [10]. Sustained infection-prevention bundles can yield meaningful reductions in hemodialysis-associated BSI [19], and antimicrobial catheter lock solutions may further reduce CVC-related events [20]. The organism-specific data presented in Table S1 and Fig. 2 can serve as a local antibiogram for empirical prescribing at this institution and comparable facilities in the Aseer region.

Future work should include prospective multicenter studies across Saudi hemodialysis centers to establish national benchmarks for incidence, pathogen distribution, and MDR prevalence, building on existing national surveillance [12, 13]. Studies should capture vascular access type, molecular resistance mechanisms (particularly carbapenemase genotyping), and time to appropriate antimicrobial therapy, and should perform organism-restricted comparator analyses with adjustment for confounders to enable confirmatory inference on phenotype-specific mortality. The Saudi BSI overview by Alqahtani et al. [12] similarly calls for regional collaboration to standardise BSI surveillance methodologies and inform empirical-therapy algorithms. Such work would build on the national AMR overview by Thabit et al. [13] and provide hemodialysis-specific benchmarks.

Conclusion

In this 11-year single-center retrospective cohort of maintenance hemodialysis patients, BSI were predominantly gram-positive, with SOSA the most frequent group; gram-negative organisms accounted for approximately one-third of episodes, and MDR phenotypes were identified in roughly one-quarter of isolates. Linezolid and vancomycin showed very high overall activity against gram-positive isolates, while vancomycin susceptibility among Enterococcus species was lower and overall fluoroquinolone susceptibility was low. In an exploratory organism-restricted unadjusted analysis, CRE-positive episodes showed higher observed 30-day in-hospital mortality than carbapenem-susceptible Enterobacterales, but this did not reach conventional significance, rested on few events, and was not adjusted for confounding; it should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating only. These findings support local surveillance and empirical-therapy review, but mortality associations require confirmation in larger, prospectively adjudicated cohorts using organism-restricted and adjusted analyses.
