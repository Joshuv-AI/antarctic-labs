Early detection and localization of recurrent prostate cancer using F-DCFPyL PET/CT in patients with low and ultra-low PSA levels
Authors: Ida Sonni, Alexandra Gulpe, Nicholas G Nickols, Derace Schaffer, Louis Montagut, Gholam R Berenji, Matthew B Rettig
Published: 2026

Main text

Background

After definitive treatment such as radical prostatectomy (RP) or radiotherapy (RT), an increase in serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA) suggests biochemical recurrence (BCR). BCR is common, occurring in approximately 40% of patients within an extended follow-up period [1], and it is associated with prostate cancer-specific mortality and a higher risk of metastatic progression [2]. Therefore, early detection and precise localization of recurrence are essential to enable targeted salvage interventions that can prolong survival and improve quality of life, especially given the emerging evidence that metastasis-directed therapy of prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) positron emission tomography (PET)-detected lesions can improve outcomes [3–6].

Conventional imaging modalities, including pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and bone scintigraphy, have demonstrated limited diagnostic performance at low PSA levels, with detection rates often remaining below 10–15% when serum PSA is ng/mL [7, 8]. This frequently delays diagnosis and clinical decision-making in patients with early BCR. In contrast, PSMA-targeted PET imaging has shown substantially improved performance for the detection and localization of recurrent prostate cancer in this setting [9, 10], outperforming conventional imaging and alternative PET tracers such as fluciclovine [11].

These findings have led to increasing interest in the application of PSMA PET in patients with minimal PSA levels, particularly below 0.5 ng/mL, a range in which salvage radiotherapy (sRT) is most effective [12]. In addition, metastasis-directed therapy such as stereotactic body radiotherapy yields improved outcomes when metastatic disease is detected by PSMA PET [3]. Early sRT at PSA ng/mL has been associated with favorable oncologic outcomes [13]. Moreover, randomized trials show that early sRT has comparable efficacy to adjuvant RT [14].

The performance of different PSMA-targeted imaging agents in the context of BCR at minimal PSA levels remains unclear. Although several studies have evaluated how PSMA PET detection rates change at low PSA levels ( ng/mL), very few have reported detection in the post-prostatectomy setting with ultra-low PSA value between 0.1 to 0.2 ng/mL [10, 15].

In the United States, F-DCFPyL is among the most widely used PSMA-targeted PET radiotracers in clinical practice [16]. Compared to Ga-labeled compounds, it offers multiple practical advantages: fluorine-18 is cyclotron-produced, allowing for higher production yields, broader distribution, and better scalability for widespread use. In addition, F-labeled tracers typically exhibit favorable imaging properties, such as longer half-life, higher positron yield and an improved spatial resolution [17].

There is currently limited data specifically characterizing the detection performance of F-DCFPyL positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) at ultra-low PSA levels. In this pooled retrospective analysis, we evaluated the early detection and localization of recurrent prostate cancer using F-DCFPyL PET/CT in patients with PSA values ng/mL, and between 0.2 to 0.5 ng/mL. This study aims to provide a focused assessment of F-DCFPyL in this clinically critical PSA range, where imaging findings may directly impact the selection and success of curative-intent salvage therapies.

Methods

This pooled retrospective analysis included patients with prostate cancer with BCR at minimally detectable PSA levels and underwent F-DCFPyL PET/CT. Patients were identified from investigator-initiated trials at the West Los Angeles Veterans Affairs Medical Center, and the phase III CONDOR trial (NCT03739684). Local Institutional Review Board waiver for retrospective analysis on de-identified data was received at West Los Angeles Veterans Affairs Medical Center.

Eligible patients had PSA values below 0.5 ng/mL at time of imaging. BCR was defined as a rising PSA after definitive therapy. Among the included patients, 95 had undergone RP, while 11 had received RT following RP; detailed information on the indication for post-prostatectomy RT was not available. Inclusion was independent of prior conventional imaging findings. Patients were stratified in two cohorts based on PSA levels at time of PET/CT: an ultra-low PSA cohort (PSA ng/mL) and a low PSA cohort (PSA 0.2–0.5 ng/mL). The performance of F-DCFPyL PET/CT was evaluated overall and separately within each PSA cohort.

Imaging was performed according to the protocols of the contributing studies, which were closely comparable across sites. F-DCFPyL was administered intravenously at a target activity of approximately 333 MBq (9 mCi), followed by an uptake interval of 1–2 hours before acquisition. Patients voided before scanning. PET and non-contrast CT images were acquired from the skull vertex to the mid-thigh on the clinical PET/CT scanners of the contributing sites; images were reconstructed using each site’s standard iterative reconstruction protocol with CT-based attenuation correction. As imaging was pooled retrospectively from investigator-initiated trials at the West Los Angeles Veterans Affairs Medical Center and the phase III CONDOR trial, exact acquisition and reconstruction parameters varied across the pooled source datasets.

Interpretation of the F-DCFPyL PET/CT was supported by the automated Prostate Cancer Molecular Imaging Standardized Evaluation (aPROMISE) platform [18, 19]. The aPROMISE automated segmentations were independently reviewed and confirmed by an experienced, board-certified nuclear medicine physician. Lesion positivity was determined by visual assessment of focal tracer uptake above local background in an anatomically suspicious location, in accordance with established PSMA PET reading frameworks [20, 21]. A fixed standardized uptake value (SUV) threshold was not applied. Findings consistent with physiologic PSMA uptake were excluded. The primary endpoint was detection rate, defined as the proportion of patients with at least one PSMA-avid lesion identified on PET/CT relative to the total number of patients with BCR. PET-positive findings were defined as PSMA-avid lesions interpreted as suspicious for recurrent prostate cancer on F-DCFPyL PET/CT. Since an independent reference standard was not available for all detected lesions, the analysis focused on detection rate, rather than sensitivity.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics and imaging findings, with categorical variables reported as counts and percentages.

Results

This retrospective analysis includes a total of 106 patients. At the time of the F-DCFPyL PET/CT, 24 patients were classified in the ultra-low PSA cohort (PSA ng/mL), and 82 patients in the low PSA cohort (0.2–0.5 ng/mL). Discussion

This study aimed to characterize the detection performance of F-DCFPyL PET/CT in patients with biochemical recurrence at minimal PSA levels, with particular focus on an ultra-low PSA range (below 0.2 ng/mL) for which agent-specific data were previously unavailable. The main finding is that F-DCFPyL PET/CT detected recurrent prostate cancer in 33% of patients with PSA below 0.2 ng/mL and in 49% of those with PSA between 0.2 and 0.5 ng/mL. The low absolute SUV mean values observed in a subset of lesions (as low as 1.8) should not be interpreted as diagnostic uncertainty. In PSMA-targeted imaging, lesion positivity is defined by focal uptake above reference background in an anatomically suspicious location rather than by an absolute SUV threshold [21]. The relatively lower SUV levels in this setting can be directly correlated to the lower circulating PSA levels and correspondingly reduced PSMA expression at this early disease stage.

Our findings are consistent with prior research that shows association between PSA level and detection rate in biochemical recurrence. In a multicenter study including 635 patients, Fendler et al. evaluated the detection rate of Ga-PSMA-11 PET/CT in the setting of biochemical recurrence, showing an overall detection rate of 75%, that decreased to 38% in patients with PSA below 0.5 ng/mL [10]. Similarly, a meta-analysis of F-labeled PSMA PET/CT agents, specifically F-DCFPyL and F-PSMA-1007, reported an overall detection rate of biochemical recurrence of around 80–85%, dropping to 49% for patients with PSA levels below or equal to 0.5 ng/mL [15]. Notably, these studies do not distinguish detection rates at PSA levels below 0.2 ng/mL, which limits conclusions regarding imaging performance in this ultra-low PSA range.

This gap was recently addressed by Burgard et al., who analyzed 321 patients with BCR and PSA ng/mL across five academic centers using three conventional PSMA-targeted radiotracers — [Ga]Ga-PSMA-11, [Ga]Ga-PSMA I&T or [F]PSMA-1007 — and reported a pooled detection rate of 29.6% with no significant differences between tracers [22]. However, F-DCFPyL was not represented in their cohort. The 33% detection rate observed in our ultra-low PSA cohort was comparable to their pooled findings, extending the comparative landscape to F-DCFPyL. Although detection rates were lower in the ultra-low PSA cohort, almost one-third of patients still showed detectable disease in F-DCFPyL PET/CT. These findings support the potential of molecular imaging in selected patients with biochemical recurrence, even at ultra-low PSA levels.

From a clinical perspective, these results should be considered in the context of current guideline recommendations. The European Association of Urology recommends the use of PSMA-targeted PET in patients with biochemical recurrence after radical prostatectomy if PSA levels reach 0.2 ng/mL, where imaging findings are expected to influence therapeutic decisions. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, however, do not define a specific PSA threshold for the use of PSMA-targeted PET in biochemical recurrence. The American Urological Association recognizes PSMA-targeted imaging as the most sensitive imaging modality in the context of biochemical recurrence in comparison with conventional imaging, highlighting its importance when findings might guide salvage therapy planning. Building on these recommendations, our findings suggest that detection with F-DCFPyL PET/CT can be achieved at PSA levels below the threshold of 0.2 ng/mL.

Several important questions remain beyond the scope of this retrospective analysis. Post-PET clinical management was not systematically captured in the source datasets. Characterizing how PSMA-avid findings at ultra-low PSA influence subsequent treatment decisions, particularly the proportion of patients who remain candidates for curative-intent therapy, represents an important target for prospective work. Beyond detection itself, the clinical value of identifying recurrent disease at ultra-low PSA levels ultimately depends on whether earlier intervention translates into improved long-term outcomes; prospective trials with metastasis-free, prostate cancer-specific, and overall survival endpoints will be needed to establish clinical benefit. Routine integration of PSMA PET at PSA levels below 0.2 ng/mL into clinical guidelines will additionally require formal cost-effectiveness analyses, since the absolute number of positive findings is lower in this range and the cost per case detected correspondingly higher. Even when disease is detected, translation into successful targeted local therapy remains challenging: standard pelvic lymph node dissection templates may incompletely cover the anatomical distribution of PSMA-avid nodes, with suspicious nodes detected outside the template in up to 30% of cases [23]. Conversely, a negative PSMA PET scan does not fully exclude future progression as up to 83% of high-risk biochemically recurrent patients have been reported to progress within three years despite negative imaging [24], underscoring the complementary roles of molecular imaging, clinical risk stratification, and biomarker-based approaches. Several emerging technologies have the potential to refine performance in this challenging setting: next-generation PSMA radioligands such as Cu-SAR-bisPSMA leverage longer half-life and bivalent binding to enable delayed imaging with improved tumor-to-background ratios [25]; artificial intelligence–based image reconstruction and denoising methods can enhance detection of small and faint lesions; and integration of genomic classifiers such as Decipher [26] may help identify the subset of biochemically recurrent patients in whom PSMA PET at ultra-low PSA yields the greatest clinical impact.

The scope of this study was to gather data of F-DCFPyL PET/CT imaging at low and ultra-low PSA values in the BCR setting to generate hypotheses for future studies. The study was limited by its retrospective design and relatively small number of patients; however, the study data provides reasonable evidence to prospectively investigate the clinical utility of F-DCFPyL PET/CT in the ultra-low PSA setting. Several additional limitations should be acknowledged. First, this analysis reports detection rate, defined as the proportion of patients with at least one PSMA-avid lesion on imaging, and does not constitute a diagnostic accuracy study; consequently, a per-lesion reference standard was not applied. Lesions were adjudicated by an experienced board-certified nuclear medicine physician, with explicit exclusion of physiological uptake and recognized interpretive pitfalls; however, histopathological confirmation of PSMA-avid findings, particularly in the ultra-low PSA cohort, where candidate lesions are typically small and biopsy is rarely clinically indicated, was not available, and detection rates should not be interpreted as equivalent to sensitivity or positive predictive value. Second, uniform documentation of PSA kinetics, notably PSA doubling time, was not available across the pooled retrospective source datasets, precluding stratified analysis by PSA kinetics. Systematic capture of this covariate in future prospective studies will be important for risk-adapted interpretation of PSMA PET detection at low PSA levels.

Conclusion

F-DCFPyL PET/CT demonstrates clinically valuable detection rates for biochemically recurrent prostate cancer in patients with ultra-low and low PSA levels. Our findings highlight the potential of F-DCFPyL PET/CT in the early identification of recurrent disease after definitive therapy, adding evidence to the growing research on PSMA-targeted imaging in early biochemical recurrence and support further investigation into the importance of molecular imaging in this challenging clinical setting. Further studies are necessary to inform guidelines, establish long-term oncologic benefit, and assess the cost-effectiveness of incorporating PSMA PET/CT imaging at PSA levels below 0.2 ng/mL.
