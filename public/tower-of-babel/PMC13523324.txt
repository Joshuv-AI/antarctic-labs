Mergers and migrations: drivers of population genetic structure in a captive rhesus macaque colony
Authors: Martha M Lyke, A Bagwell, D Newman, S Galindo, T Church, C Christensen, SB Gray, LA Cox, CN Ross, D Kaushal, IH Cheeseman, SA Cole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRhesus macaques play a valuable role in biomedical research, and their genetic characterization is critical for effective colony management. Levels of genetic diversity, genomic admixture, and genetic substructure can all impact the suitability of macaques for biomedical research. The Southwest National Primate Research Center (SNPRC) houses one of the largest rhesus macaque colonies in the United States. Their genetic management includes the ongoing assessment of genetic diversity, ancestral origin (India or China), and deep sequencing of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC). A goal of the SNPRC has been the reduction of levels of admixture between Indian- and Chinese-origin macaques and the prevalence of simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) refractory MHC haplotypes while maintaining overall genetic and MHC haplotype diversity. This has been achieved by targeted sequencing of the MHC and removing animals with haplotypes more prevalent in Chinese-origin macaques from breeding.ResultsWe investigated the impact of management strategies on admixture, population genetic structure, genetic diversity, and inbreeding using whole exome sequencing of founding and colony-born animals (n = 488). Admixture analysis of founders showed one animal to be of Chinese origin and an additional 37 considered admixed (> 15% Chinese ancestry; range 16.3–84.5%) with population substructure closely reflecting primate research center source. The levels of Chinese ancestry in the colony declined over time, though genetic diversity remains high (heterozygosity = 0.320). We characterized the MHC through targeted sequencing of 1,069 SNPRC macaques born over a twelve-year period. MHC management strategies reduced the prevalence of SIV-refractory MHC haplotypes, with Mamu-B*008 reaching significance (p = 0.027), while overall haplotype diversity was maintained (Mamu-A, h = 0.907; Mamu-B, h = 0.952). Finally, we performed genome-wide scans for genetic selection over time. We identify numerous genomic regions where allele frequencies have shifted significantly, supporting the presence of short-term adaptation under colony management.ConclusionsWe show that colony management strategies have been successful without reducing genetic diversity of the MHC or exonic regions. We also show that colony genetic substructure is related to animal colony source and that mergers and migrations have reduced inbreeding and increased overall genetic diversity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13090-2.

Background

Captive nonhuman primate (NHP) breeding groups are generally closed to external gene flow, which can lead to decreased genetic diversity and increased levels of inbreeding over time. This is further exacerbated when founding individuals are related. While pedigree records allow for reliable estimates of inbreeding and pairwise relatedness, relationships between founders are typically unknown [1, 2]. These cryptic relationships can be revealed by genomic sequencing data and combined with longitudinal pedigree records for robust population genetic analysis and enhanced breeding management strategies [1, 3]. The Southwest National Primate Research Center (SNPRC) houses a large, pedigreed, specific-pathogen free (SPF) rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta) colony designated for biomedical research. Rhesus macaques are the preferred animal model for translational research, and have been integral in the investigation of viral, bacterial, and parasitological disease, contributing to our understanding of pathogenic transmission, disease progression, and in the development and testing of drugs and vaccines [4–9]. Recent shortages in their availability have led to an increased focus on reproductive output and the genetic characterization of existing breeding colonies [10, 11].

Rhesus macaques inhabit the broadest geographical range of any NHP species, and, due to historical periods of isolation, local adaptations have led to genetic and morphological divergence [12–15]. Genetic evidence indicates that regional biological distinctions in immune response can directly impact their suitability as biomedical research models [14, 15]. Indian- and Chinese-origin rhesus macaques exhibit genetic differences in allele frequency, mitochondrial DNA, nuclear genome, and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) loci, which can impact disease susceptibility and progression [16–20]. Historically, rhesus used for research were primarily of Indian origin, though China became the largest rhesus exporter after India banned their export in 1978 [21, 22]. Most existing breeding facilities were founded by genetically distinct populations of either Indian- or Chinese-origin rhesus macaques. However, as a result of the exchange of animals between facilities, many house animals with admixed Indian and Chinese ancestries [23, 24]. While admixed animals are suitable for some research initiatives, non-admixed animals of either origin are preferable for most biomedical applications. This has led to the development of genetic tools to assess levels of Indian- or Chinese-origin genomic introgression and targeted breeding strategies aimed at limiting admixture between them [24–26].

In addition to ancestral origin, local sources of animals can also impact the genetic substructure of the population [27]. Recent demographic history can influence rates of evolution through gene flow, genetic drift, and selection, impacting the genomic distribution of genetic variation [28–31]. Across the National Primate Research Centers (NPRCs) there is substantial genetic structure, where animals drawn from different colonies form distinct genetic clusters [32]. The SNPRC SPF rhesus macaque colony comprises animals from three primary sources that were reproductively isolated prior to intentional migrations. While moving animals strategically between NPRCs can mitigate loss of genetic diversity it can also introduce genetic substructure [27]. This genetic substructure can confound the results of investigations studying disease prevention, progression and treatment strategies, leading to incorrect conclusions and interpretations regarding efficacy and response [33, 34]. In addition, this population structure can cause false-positive results or reduce power in disease-related genome-wide association studies (GWAS) [35, 36]. Thus, it is important to identify potential population stratification by genetic characterization of animals designated for biomedical research.

The breeding management of the rhesus macaques housed at the SNPRC is further constrained as they are primarily designated for HIV/AIDS-related research. Rhesus macaques challenged with simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) show similar disease progression and pathogenicity as humans infected with HIV and are thus considered an effective surrogate translational model [5, 37–39]. In rhesus, MHC class I alleles are inherited as haplotypes, a subset of which are associated with reduced viral load and slowed disease progression upon SIV-infection [38, 40–49]. Subsequently, rhesus designated for HIV/AIDS-related research are sequenced to identify and exclude animals with those haplotypes. The SNPRC began routinely sequencing the rhesus MHC region in 2013. Four MHC alleles that are associated with slowed SIV disease progression (Mamu-A1*001, Mamu-B*003, Mamu-B*008, Mamu-B*017) were present in the colony, leading to management strategies aimed to reduce animals with haplotypes containing those alleles in the breeding population and to increase production of animals negative for all four alleles (referred to as “quad negatives”). However, selectively breeding against certain alleles is expected to decrease the overall MHC haplotype and genetic diversity, which must be monitored intensively.

The primary goals of this research were to genetically characterize the SNPRC rhesus macaque colony to assess levels of genetic diversity, Chinese admixture, and MHC haplotype diversity and changes in these measures over time. Additional goals were to investigate the effectiveness of breeding management strategies aimed at reducing the prevalence of SIV-refractory MHC haplotypes and increasing the number of births of quad negative animals. We hypothesized that: (1) levels of genetic diversity would decrease over time without gene flow, (2) introducing gene flow through migration would reverse that trend, and (3) targeted breeding strategies would be effective at reducing levels of Chinese admixture and SIV-refractory MHC haplotypes. Here we combine whole exome sequencing (WES) and targeted MHC sequencing of colony animals dating back to the 1980s to provide a robust genetic characterization of the SNPRC rhesus macaque population. We show that MHC diversity has remained high despite our effective reduction of MHC haplotypes associated with slow SIV progression. We identify substantial population structure that is linked to founding populations, historical Indian versus Chinese admixture, and contemporary merging of animals from the New England National Primate Research Center (NEPRC). Our longitudinal data also allow us to capture changes in allele frequencies over time, identifying large allele frequency shifts in keeping with strong, ongoing positive selection. We anticipate that most of this selection is driven by breeding selection or may be amplified by the limited number of founders.

Methods

Study subjects

All rhesus macaques used in this study were under the ownership of the SNPRC at the time biological samples were collected. No animals were euthanized for this project.

SPF status

SPF rhesus macaques are defined as animals that are antibody negative to herpes virus-1 (B virus), simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV), type D simian retrovirus (SRV), and simian T-lymphotropic virus (STLV-1) [50]. The SNPRC biannually tests all SPF rhesus macaques for these viruses using a standard virus panel (A1) during routine health exams in accordance with appropriate animal care and ethical guidelines.

Historic SNPRC rhesus macaque breeding colony

The SNPRC was officially founded in 1999, though there were animals on site beginning in the 1960s. These animals were of mixed Chinese and Indian ancestry obtained from non-SPF sources and therefore designated as a conventional (non-SPF) colony. A pedigreed breeding program was started in the conventional colony in 1985, with 123 animals born on site prior to the SNPRC foundation. In 2000, the SNPRC took over the management of ≈ 250 presumed Indian-origin SPF animals from the Brooks Air Force Base (BAFB). These animals were moved to the SNPRC campus in 2005 to establish the primary breeding colony that remains today. Prior to the Brooks animals being moved, in 2004 the rhesus in the existing SNPRC conventional colony (n = 90) were tested for STLV-1, SRV, SIV, and B virus, with 14% (n = 13) positive for B virus and the remainder negative for all four. The negative animals were moved into quarantine for 6 months and then were moved into housing with the SPF colony animals arriving from Brooks in 2005. Three negative animals from the conventional group were placed into breeding between 2006 and 2009, with their first offspring born in the SPF colony in 2007. To date, 72 of their descendants have been born into the colony. For this study, to represent the initial genetic makeup of the colony we analyzed the main colony (Brooks source) and a supply of immigrant animals (conventional source) separately.

NEPRC rhesus macaque colony merger

In 2015, the NEPRC disbanded and the SNPRC received 237 pedigreed, SPF Indian-origin rhesus that were colony-born at NEPRC. These animals were housed separately from the primary colony for approximately two years as they were acclimatized and space to merge the two groups became available. One of the primary reasons to merge the two groups for breeding was to increase overall genetic diversity, with the first offspring from mixed breeding groups born in 2018. At that time, the NEPRC group became part of the primary rhesus colony, which is now housed and managed as one. We generated WES data from 37% (n = 87) of the original NEPRC imports to estimate the baseline genetic diversity and admixture of the group. These animals were selected randomly from those that were put into breeding groups and had offspring, thereby contributing to the gene pool.

Current rhesus macaque breeding colony

The SNPRC currently maintains a colony of ≈ 670 SPF animals that are designated primarily for HIV/AIDS-related research. Breeding animals are housed in one-male–multi-female groups or male-female pairs, so paternity is generally known. For breeding group formation, colony managers, behavioral scientists, and veterinarians work together to provide lists of candidate animals for potential breeding groups based upon behavioral compatibility and colony population goals. Geneticists work closely with the colony manager to in turn provide options for breeding group/pair formations that maximize genetic variation retained in the colony and minimize inbreeding.

Genetic analyses

DNA samples are routinely extracted and banked for all rhesus macaques that survive to weaning age (typically 9 months). The SNPRC has a large biorepository of DNA, blood, and tissue samples from most of its historical and currently housed rhesus macaques. Samples are collected during routine clinical examinations following appropriate animal care and ethical guidelines.

Sampling strategy

We began WES and whole genome sequencing (WGS) of the rhesus in 2021. Our initial strategy in animal selection for both WES and WGS included founders and other animals high in the pedigree where DNA was available. We used WES for all animals with an available DNA sample from the conventional source and Brooks source groups (Table 1). We then focused on sequencing prolific breeders. We generated WGS on a smaller set of animals (n = 181) that hold key positions in the colony (founders and prolific breeders). There was overlap of 97 animals with both WGS and WES. Both strategies were designed to capture as much of the genetic variation in the colony as possible and to investigate changes over time. Our current focus is on generating WES data for all living animals in the colony.

DNA sequencing

We extracted DNA from blood and tissue samples using DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kits (Qiagen) following manufacturer’s instructions. DNA samples were then sent to Novogene (CA, USA) for QC, library prep, and WES or WGS. A fragment analyzer (Advanced Analytical Technologies, Inc.) was used for QC for both sequencing approaches. Library preparation was completed using a human capture kit (Agilent SureSelect V6 60 M Library Prep Kit) for WES and the Rapid Plus DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (ABclonal) for WGS. Final WES libraries were sequenced at 50X coverage and WGS at 30X coverage on either the NovaSeq 6000 or NovaSeq X Plus (Illumina) platforms.

Data availability

WES and WGS sequences have been deposited in NCBI under BioProjects PRJNA810867 and PRJNA810872, respectively. Additional data regarding the animals may be requested through a direct data intake request via the SNPRC website.

Alignment, variant calling, and annotation

We completed alignment and genotype calling using existing bioinformatics pipelines. Raw sequence data was trimmed with Cutadapt v4.0 [51] using the command:

R1_END_ADAPTER="AGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCTGAACTCCAGTCAC”.

R2_END_ADAPTER="AGATCGGAAGAGCGTCGTGTAGGGAAAGAGTGT”.

./cutadapt {input.fastq} -a $R1_END_ADAPTER -A $R2_END_ADAPTER --cores 4 -o {output.trimmed[0]} -p {output.trimmed [1]}

Trimmed reads were aligned to the Mmul_10 rhesus macaque reference assembly, downloaded from Ensembl (Macaca_mulatta.Mmul_10.dna.toplevel.fa.gz), using BWA-MEM v0.7.17 [52]. After post-processing with SAMtools v1.21 [53], we went through a series of steps with GATK v4.2.3.0 [54], which involved applying base recalibration (BaseRecalibrator) of the alignments, calling SNVs per sample to create gVCFs (HapotypeCaller), consolidation into a GenomicsDB datastore (GenomicsDBImport), and then joint calling with both the WGS and WES samples together [55] using the WGS as a reference. WES was separated out from the WGS using BCFtools [56] and then subsequent filtering of the SNVs continued with GATK to retain only biallelic variants and to exclude those matching the following: QUAL < 30.0, QD < 2.0, SOR < 3.0, FS < 5.0, MQ < 59.0, MQRankSum < -12.5, and ReadPosRankSum < -8.0 (VariantFiltration); then genotype refinement (also with GATK) adjusted genotype scores; and then genotypes were filtered to exclude GQ < 30 and DP < 5 (CalculateGenotypePosteriors). BCFtools was then used again to remove sites with more than 20% of genotypes missing and then further subsetted to only SNVs common between the founding populations. Variants were annotated and consequences predicted using Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor v110 [57]. Details of SNV results can be found in Additional File 1. The fractions of missing genotypes and average read depth per cohort are shown in Additional File 2.

Genetic diversity

We used WES sequencing data to analyze measures of genetic diversity in our contemporary breeding colony, as well as changes over time and before and after mergers. Mating in the colony is considered non-random as breeding groups are formed intentionally. We divided the colony as follows: conventional source, Brooks source, NEPRC source, offspring born into the primary colony in designated breeding groups from 2003 to 2020 in three-year intervals, and offspring born after the NEPRC merger (one SNPRC and one NEPRC parent) from 2018 to 2020 (Table 1). We divided animals born from 2018 to 2020 into two groups: one with two parents from the SPF SNPRC colony (birth cohort 6, n = 42, Table 1) and another with one parent from each of the NEPRC and SNPRC colony (post-merger offspring, n = 41, Table 1) to assess potential genetic impacts of the merger. We recognize that birth cohort 5 has far fewer samples than the others. As a measure of overall genetic diversity, we calculated the heterozygosity and fractions of the genomes in runs of homozygosity (fROH) of each individual and averaged across each group using VCFtools [58] and scikit-allel v0.20.3 [59], respectively. We also estimated the inbreeding coefficients for all animals based on excess homozygosity (Fhom) using GTCA v1.94.1 [60]. For both fROH and inbreeding, variants were subset to only include those observed across the three source populations. Variants were not filtered in this way for estimating heterozygosity.

Admixture analyses

We also used WES data to analyze genomic admixture in our colony in multiple ways. First, we conducted a principal component analysis (PCA) using scikit-allel and an unsupervised ADMIXTURE analysis using 2–7 theoretical ancestral populations (K) (Additional File 3). We ran 10 predetermined seeds for each K. The seeds used were integers from 899 to 908, with the original being 899. We then investigated the impact of source (conventional, Brooks, NEPRC) on the genetic makeup of the population. Next, we assessed supervised admixture between animals of Indian- and Chinese-origin across all groups described in Table 1 to identify original sources of Chinese admixture and to analyze change over time. We pruned using `bcftools +prune` with the options ` -m 0.6 --random-seed 7340` before running ADMIXTURE v1.3.0 [61], we utilized genomic sequences of publicly available animals of known Indian- (n = 12) or Chinese-origin (n = 12) from the Macaque Genotype and Phenotype (mGAP) database [62] as references. There were only 12 Chinese-origin macaques in the database, so we selected the same sample size for the Indian-origin.

Kinship estimation

The founding animals of a captive animal colony are those at the top of the pedigree that have no known parentage information and are presumed unrelated. Births with known parentage are then used to create the rest of the pedigree, allowing kinship and relatedness to be analyzed for subsequent generations based on pedigree data alone. However, genetic data are required to determine potential relationships between the founding animals. Our database called Total Animal Care (TAC) houses all pedigree data and estimates pairwise kinship using the R tool kinship2. The kinship coefficient represents the genetic similarity of two individuals, with an expected value of 0.25 for first-order (e.g., parent offspring) relationships. We used WES data to estimate pairwise kinship between all sequenced animals using KING v2.2.7 [63], and compared those against the pedigree-estimated kinship values.

Signatures of selection

Selection coefficients (s) for specific MHC alleles were estimated by fitting a least squares regression to logit transformed allele frequency data from across the 12-year span of MHC sampling and estimating s as the slope of the fitted line. We investigated potential signatures of selection by calculating Weir & Cockerham’s FST comparing each birth cohort to the founding Brooks population. FST was calculated in VCFtools v0.1.16 [58] in non-overlapping windows of 10kbp across the genome excluding windows with no data. Birth cohort 5 (Table 1) was excluded from these analyses due to low sample size. NEPRC source and post-merger offspring were also excluded. Peaks with FST > 0.075 were considered regions of interest and we identified potential genes of interest in the most diverged windows, which corresponds to the empirical 99.9th percentile in the Brooks vs. birth cohort 6 (Table 1) comparison.

MHC analyses

MHC sequencing and monitoring

SNPRC started routine MHC genotyping in 2013 by sending samples to the Wisconsin National Primate Resource Center (WNPRC) Genomics Services for MHC genotyping by deep sequencing, with the 2012 birth cohort being the first with all individuals typed. WNPRC uses the Illumina MiSeq to perform short-amplicon genotyping focused on the most polymorphic segments of MHC genes. They have established a Fluidigm Access Array primer panel for MHC genotyping spanning both the class I (A, B, I, and E) and class II (DRB, DQA, DQB, DPA, and DPB) regions. All NEPRC source animals were MHC genotyped by WNPRC prior to arriving at SNPRC. In 2017 all animals with the Mamu-B*003 allele (n = 7) were removed from breeding and we began to limit the number of males with the other monitored haplotypes that were used to form new breeding groups. Mamu-B*003 is more common in Chinese-origin macaques and rare in Indian-origin [64], so this measure also served to reduce Chinese admixture. In 2021, the colony manager implemented a new approach to actively remove most animals positive for Mamu-A1*001, Mamu-B*008, and Mamu-B*017 from existing breeding groups, including both males and females. Some animals with monitored haplotypes were not removed to maintain overall MHC diversity and rare haplotypes in the colony. Efforts to increase the production of quad negatives began in 2015 and increased in 2021 with rising demand for animals for HIV/AIDS-related research.

MHC haplotype frequency

We utilized existing MHC haplotype data for rhesus in the SPF colony (n = 1,069) to look at changes in haplotype diversity (Mamu-A, Mamu-B) and in frequency of haplotypes with the four alleles of interest (Mamu-A1*001, Mamu-B*003, Mamu-B*008, and Mamu-B*017) over time by birth cohort. Our analyses included animals born from 2012 (first full birth cohort fully typed) to 2023 (latest fully typed) inclusive. All rhesus that survive to weaning age are routinely MHC genotyped. This sampling strategy differs from that for WES (see above), so only 216 animals are a part of both data sets. Results

Founding populations show distinct patterns of admixture, diversity, and inbreeding

To investigate the impact of ancestry on contemporary genetic structure, we characterized the genomes of animals from founding cohorts using WES. We have previously demonstrated that WES using exome capture kits designed for the human genome performed effectively with coverage of more than 97% of the macaque exome [65]. We generated WES data for 212 founding animals from the conventional source (n = 28), Brooks source (n = 97), and the NEPRC source (n = 87, Table 1). After quality control filtering, we retained 917,713 high quality, autosomal, biallelic variants with a mean minor allele frequency of 0.08.

Principal component analysis (PCA) of the 212 source rhesus macaques revealed strong separation between founding populations of animals (Fig. 1A). The conventional source animals are separated along PC1 (2.35% variance), and the Brooks and NEPRC animals are separated by PC2 (1.70% variance, Fig. 1A). We performed an unsupervised admixture analysis on the 212 macaques using 2–7 theoretical ancestral populations (K) using ADMIXTURE v1.3.0 (Fig. 1B), with K = 3 being best supported by cross-validation scores (Additional File 3). At K = 3, NEPRC animals are derived from a single ancestral population which is also observed at lower prevalence in both Brooks and conventional source animals (Fig. 1B). Both Brooks and conventional source animals also have a discrete component found at low frequency in the other population (Fig. 1B).

The genetic diversity of rhesus macaque populations in US-based biomedical research is driven by importation of animals from India and China. We performed a supervised ADMIXTURE analysis (Fig. 1C) to investigate potential admixture between Chinese and Indian origin in the 212 source animals. There are only twelve Chinese rhesus macaque reference animals in the mGAP database, and we acknowledge this limitation to the sample size for these analyses. Among the conventional source animals, we identified one animal of Chinese origin (99.9% Chinese) and three others that were highly admixed (> 80% Chinese), with most of the remaining animals having some Chinese admixture (range 23–77%, Additional File 4). Among the Brooks source animals, which were purported to be of non-admixed Indian origin, we identified 12 animals that were highly admixed (range 29–66% Chinese-origin) and 6 others with moderate levels of admixture (16–20% Chinese-origin, Fig. 1C, Additional File 4). The NEPRC source animals showed negligible Chinese admixture (range 0.01–4%), suggesting the NEPRC founding animals were of Indian origin (Fig. 1C, Additional File 4). Notably, as stated above our admixture analysis is most strongly supported by K = 3 ancestral populations suggesting that Chinese versus Indian admixture is too simple a dichotomy to explain substructure. The potential sequencing of more Chinese rhesus to allow a larger reference sample would increase robustness of future analyses.

We used WES data from the 212 source animals to calculate heterozygosity, runs of homozygosity (fROH), and inbreeding coefficients to assess overall genetic diversity. The conventional source animals had significantly higher fROH and inbreeding than the Brooks source animals and NEPRC (Table 2), suggesting lower overall genetic diversity. The average inbreeding coefficient was also significantly higher in the NEPRC than Brooks source animals with no notable difference in heterozygosity or fROH (Table 2).

Finally, we compared the pedigree-based and genetic estimates of pairwise kinship for the source populations, as founding animals are presumed to be unrelated. We estimated pairwise relatedness for the 212 animals using KING v2.2.7 and uncovered several pairs of founding animals that were related. Notably, there were twelve pairs of Brooks source founders presumed unrelated that were first-order relatives (φ > 0.21, Additional File 5). There were also two conventional source males and two NEPRC source animals that were first-order relatives (Additional File 5). All these animals had offspring, resulting in animals of subsequent generations in the pedigree having higher kinship coefficients than estimated by the pedigree alone.

Contemporary colony population genetics

To investigate the impact of management strategies aimed at increasing genetic diversity and decreasing admixture we further sequenced 276 additional rhesus macaques born into the SPF colony between 2003 and 2020 using WES. We calculated the heterozygosity, fROH, and inbreeding for the 276 rhesus macaques to analyze changes and to compare with the source populations. Results suggest genetic diversity and inbreeding remained consistent over the past 20 years despite very little migration and subsequently increased following the merger with NEPRC animals (Fig. 2). We compared the three measures between the NEPRC source founders and the post-merger offspring and report a significant reduction in fROH and inbreeding and a slight increase in heterozygosity in the post-merger offspring (Table 2). We also report a significantly lower fROH in the post-merger offspring compared with contemporary birth cohort 6 (Table 2). These results suggest that we increased overall genetic diversity in the colony by merging the SNPRC and NEPRC groups.

To assess levels of Indian/Chinese admixture in the contemporary population, we ran a supervised analysis in ADMIXTURE on all 488 rhesus using the same reference animals as in the previous analysis (Chinese n = 12, Indian n = 12). Results indicate a gradual decrease over time in the number of animals above the admixed threshold of 15% Chinese ancestry, with very little admixture in the present-day population (Additional File 4, Fig. 3). The artificial selection placed upon the SNPRC animals to remove SIV-refractory MHC haplotypes is strong, with selection coefficients (s) of the reduction of specific haplotypes estimated to be -0.044, -0.042 and − 0.010 for Mamu-A1*001, Mamu-B*008, and Mamu-B*017, respectively. We explored temporal signatures of allele frequency variation across the pedigree to identify regions of the genome that may be under selection in the SNPRC pedigree from artificial selection upon the MHC, response to SPF conditions, or some other environmental factor. We estimated FST in windows of 10kbp across the genome, comparing each birth cohort to the founding Brooks population. Using a threshold of FST > 0.075 we identified 12 diverged windows in 2003–2005, which rose to 112 windows by 2018–2020 (Fig. 4A–D). When comparing Brooks source animals to animals born to SNPRC parents between 2018 and 2020 (birth cohort 6, Table 1) we identified multiple regions of the genome where consecutive windows show evidence of divergence (Fig. 4E). Three of these windows are highlighted in Fig. 4F–H. Notably, we can see that the divergence in each window increases over time (Fig. 4A–D), supporting that the signal is driven by consistent pressure rather than the result of limited sampling. These FST peaks align with genes that have putative roles in modulating immune response (e.g., PRKAR2B, PAQR3, A1CF) or inflammatory pathways (e.g., PIK3CG, ANXA3, A1CF) in humans (Fig. 4F–H) (66–71). We explored whether FST signals in these regions were present prior to the 2018–2020 timepoint (Fig. 4I). Overall, we see a general divergence in allele frequencies over time with these three loci (Fig. F–H) showing monotonic increases in FST over the last decade.

Selective breeding has reduced the frequency of SIV refractory MHC haplotypes

We obtained extended MHC haplotypes by targeted deep sequencing of 1,069 rhesus macaques, including full birth cohorts between 2012 and 2023, from the WNPRC Genomics Services [43]. We estimated haplotypic diversity by birth year across this robust dataset and saw no significant change in overall Mamu-A or Mamu-B haplotype diversity during the study period (p = 0.42, Fig. 5B, Additional File 7). For the four monitored haplotypes (Mamu-A1*001, Mamu-B*003, Mamu-B*008, Mamu-B*017), we successfully removed all animals with the Mamu-B*003 allele (n = 7) from breeding in 2017. Mamu-A1*001 is one of the four most common Mamu-A alleles among rhesus, showing fluctuating frequency until a recent sharp decrease from 17.3% of all haplotypes for animals born in 2021 to 7.2% in 2023 (Fig. 5C) following the removal of animals with that haplotype from breeding in 2021. Mamu-B*008 and Mamu-B*017 have both decreased gradually, with Mamu-B*008 decreasing from 4.5% to 1.6% from 2012 to 2023 and Mamu-B*017 decreasing from 14.1% to 4.6% during that same time (Fig. 5C). Figure 5D shows the percentage of total births that were negative for Mamu-A1*001, Mamu-B*003, Mamu-B*008, Mamu-B*017 from 2012 to 2023. This shows a significant increase over time (mean increase per year of 2.04%, p = 0.001, r2 = 0.70, linear model) from 44% of births in 2012 to 73% in 2023 (Fig. 5D).

Discussion

Here we have genetically characterized the primary rhesus macaque colony housed at SNPRC using MHC region and WES data. We estimated genetic diversity, admixture, and MHC haplotypic diversity of these animals to assess the impact of various colony management strategies that have been implemented since the SNPRC was founded. Overall, the colony has maintained relatively low levels of inbreeding and adequate levels of genetic diversity despite being closed to outside breeding for many years. Robust pedigree curation and consultation allowed colony managers to form breeding groups aimed at maximizing genetic diversity and minimizing inbreeding even prior to the implementation of molecular methods. However, recent advances in sequencing methodologies and decreased costs have allowed for more accurate genomic characterization of NHP colonies designated for biomedical research. We now augment pedigree records with robust analyses of genetic and MHC diversity, relatedness, and inbreeding when making recommendations for breeding group formations. This allows us to continue to maximize the genetic variation in the colony while providing animals with specific genetic characteristics desirable to the research community.

The Brooks source animals that founded the SPF breeding colony were purported to be of Indian origin; however, our admixture analysis indicates that several animals had a substantial (> 15%) proportion of Chinese ancestry. Admixture of the offspring of unsequenced Brooks source animals suggest that the number with Chinese ancestry was even higher. For example, one rhesus male with no DNA source available sired ten animals in birth cohorts 2─7 (2006–2020) that showed greater than 15% Chinese ancestry (range 18–39%; Additional File 4). The dams of nine of the offspring were sequenced, and while one dam (CKDN220009637) showed 31% Chinese ancestry, the dams of the other offspring had negligible Chinese ancestry (86–99% Indian-origin; Additional File 4). These findings support other reports of cryptic admixture or even interspecific hybridization in early founding populations of captive NHP colonies [66–69]. However, we still found relatively low levels of admixture overall in the contemporary rhesus population. This may be due in part to the removal of seven animals with the Mamu-B*003 haplotype from breeding in 2017, as this haplotype is common in Chinese-origin and rare in Indian-origin rhesus macaques [64].

Beyond ancestral origin, our analyses revealed substantial population genetic substructure based on more recent animal sources. Our PCA and admixture analyses suggest that demographic history beyond Indian and Chinese ancestry led to distinct genetic differentiation between animals in the three source populations. There were also differences in levels of genetic diversity between the source populations, with the conventional colony showing significantly lower levels of diversity than the Brooks and NEPRC source animals. The animals brought from the NEPRC also had significantly higher levels of inbreeding than the SNPRC animals. After merging these animals with the primary SNPRC breeding colony, we saw a significant decrease in fROH and inbreeding in the post-merger offspring compared to the NEPRC animals and the contemporary birth cohort 6 (Table 1). This demonstrates that by merging isolated groups we can see significant improvement in genetic diversity within one generation. This has implications for increasing genetic diversity in both captive and wild animal populations more broadly.

We also investigated potential signatures of selection across the genome over time by comparing FST signals across birth cohorts with that of the Brooks source animals, as they were the primary colony founders. Our FST analysis suggests there have been ongoing selective pressures driving changes in allele frequency over time other than those related to artificial selection against certain MHC haplotypes. We see peaks at the same positions in certain genes starting in the early birth cohorts and growing stronger over time, suggesting these are true signals of selection in the population that are unrelated to MHC related artificial selection that began in 2015. We did find FST peaks in several genes associated with immune response in humans that are not part of the MHC, including genes with roles in supporting adaptive immune function, regulating immune response, modulating inflammation, or that are expressed in immune related cells [70–75]. Future work will investigate signals of selection in these genes and the potential selective pressures driving those signals.

Relating to MHC diversity, our results indicate that the removal or exclusion of animals with certain MHC haplotypes from breeding has not decreased the overall MHC haplotype diversity. This suggests that our strategic selection of animals with rare or uncommon haplotypes for breeding has helped mitigate the removal of animals with SIV-refractory haplotypes. Ongoing efforts to maintain MHC diversity include the continued inclusion of females with rare haplotypes in breeding, regardless of their status for Mamu-A1*001, Mamu-B*008, and Mamu-B*017, and plans to initiate cryopreservation of sperm from males with these and rare MHC haplotypes. While Mamu-A*001, Mamu-B*008, and Mamu-B*017 are undesirable for purposes of rapid SIV disease progression in HIV/AIDS research models [41, 42], the fact that they do confer some immune advantage is important in and of itself. Not enough is known about the complexities of the macaque MHC complex and how it impacts immune response more generally to eliminate these or any MHC haplotypes from captive rhesus populations. Future research into the specific mechanisms by which variation in MHC haplotypes leads to variable immune response will be critical for many biomedical research initiatives.

Here we demonstrate that WES is a useful methodology for monitoring genetic diversity and inbreeding in our rhesus macaque population while also providing invaluable exomic sequencing data for the investigation and potential development of macaque disease models of human health. The genetic characterization of NHPs designated for biomedical research has never been more accessible or more critical [76–81]. It is not uncommon for NHPs of the same species from different geographical origins to show variation in disease susceptibility, progression and response [76]. As noted above, rhesus macaque ancestral origins impact their suitability for HIV/AIDS-related research [82–84]. Similar variation is seen in cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis), with animals from different geographical regions (Mauritian versus non-Mauritian) responding differently to Shigella immunization [85]. Upon infection with Shigella, clinical shigellosis was only observed in non-Mauritian macaques [85]. While the authors found the fecal microbiota of Mauritian and non-Mauritian macaques to be genetically distinct, they stressed the importance of investigating variation in the host genetic backgrounds [85]. Additionally, cynomolgus macaques historically hybridized with rhesus macaques in parts of their geographical range, and admixed cynomolgus susceptibility to malaria is strongly associated with their proportion of rhesus ancestry [69]. These examples underscore the critical importance of genetically characterizing NHP populations designated for biomedical research and as translational models for human health.

Conclusions

In this study we combined deep sequencing of the MHC region of 1,069 rhesus macaques with whole exome sequencing of 488 rhesus macaques for the genetic characterization of a captive colony designated for HIV/AIDS-related research. These animals represent birth cohorts spanning more than 25 years, and include animals present at the founding of the SNPRC in 1999. We show that recent and historical demographic history has impacted population genetic structure, and we identify regions of the macaque genome with evidence of recent strong positive selection. We report that management strategies aimed at reducing the prevalence of SIV-refractory MHC haplotypes have been successful without reducing overall genetic or MHC diversity.
