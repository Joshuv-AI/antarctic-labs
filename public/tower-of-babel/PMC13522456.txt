Genome near-haploidization in CDC73-wildtype parathyroid tumors
Authors: Maaia Margo Jentus, Filomena Cetani, Marieke Snel, Femke M. van Haalen, Dina Ruano, Natasha M. Appelman-Dijkstra, Abbey Schepers, Stijn Crobach, Claudio Marcocci, Liborio Torregrossa, Hans Morreau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Parathyroid carcinoma (PC) is most often linked to germline or somatic CDC73 mutations. Previously, we identified three CDC73-wildtype PCs with massive chromosomal losses and genome near-haploidization, with or without endoreduplication/genome doubling – features also seen in rare tumor types such as oncocytic thyroid carcinoma. To assess whether similar alterations occur in other parathyroid neoplasms, we analyzed fourteen parathyroid adenomas (PA; thirteen oncocytic), three atypical parathyroid tumors (APTs; one oncocytic), and one additional PC, the latter four all CDC73-wildtype. Histopathological diagnoses followed the 2022 WHO classification. Genome-wide SNP array analysis was performed to detect copy number variations (CNVs), imbalances and loss of heterozygosity (LOH). APTs and PC underwent somatic mutation analysis, to confirm CDC73-wildtype status. Oncocytic PAs exhibited largely stable genomes, with few or no chromosomal alterations. Patients with chromosomally altered PA had significantly higher preoperative serum calcium levels. In contrast, two of three APTs, and the included PC displayed extensive chromosomal losses and genome near-haploidization. These findings expand the recognized spectrum of chromosomal alterations in CDC73-wildtype parathyroid tumors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-55820-2.

Introduction

The parathyroid glands maintain calcium homeostasis by regulating parathyroid hormone (PTH) secretion. Excessive PTH secretion causes hypercalcemia, and when independent of physiological feedback, it manifests as primary or tertiary hyperparathyroidism. Autonomously functioning parathyroid glands can exhibit hyperplastic, adenomatous, atypical, or even carcinomatous changes.

Among the key genes involved in parathyroid tumorigenesis, MEN1 and CDC73 are most frequently affected. Inactivating germline mutations cause MEN1 (Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia, type 1) and HPT-JT (Hyperparathyroidism-Jaw Tumor) syndromes, respectively. CDC73 encodes parafibromin; loss of parafibromin expression results from biallelic CDC73 inactivation. While CDC73 inactivation and parafibromin expression loss are characteristic of familial and sporadic parathyroid carcinoma (PC), they may also occur in adenomas1–4. Such alterations are linked to adverse clinical outcomes in PC and predict aggressive behavior in atypical parathyroid tumors (APTs)5–7. Parafibromin staining correlates with CDC73 mutation status, showing approximately 80% predictive accuracy depending on sequencing method and immunohistochemical scoring8.

MEN1 mutations occur in 11–40% of sporadic parathyroid adenomas (PAs), but are rare (≤ 10%) in sporadic PCs. Other molecular events such as CCND1 amplification and mutations in less common genes, have also been reported in PC10,67.

Chromosomal copy-number alterations are widely reported in parathyroid lesions and can represent a hallmark in a subset of cases (Tables 1, 2 and 3 include CGH, WES, WGS or SNP-array studies with a genome-wide approach; Supplemental Table 1 includes studies with other methods). Distinct alteration patterns occur in parathyroid adenomas (PAs, Table 1) and PCs (Table 2)11–15: PCs typically exhibit more extensive alterations, whereas PAs show fewer chromosomal imbalances or loss of heterozygosity (LOH)15–23. APTs (Table 2) may resemble PCs, displaying extensive genome-wide LOH events that may contribute to their potential aggressive biology24,25. Chromosomal imbalances and LOH are occasionally seen in idiopathic primary or secondary hyperplasia (Table 3)11,25–33.

By comparing patterns, different researchers concluded independent genesis of PAs and PCs15,16. Few studies have utilized genome-wide SNP-arrays or WES/WGS analysis to characterize there patterns; most relied on polymorphic microsatellite marker analyses (Supplemental Table 1) or comparative genome hybridization (CGH)24,16,34,67,22,23. Costa-Guda et al. compared CGH and SNP microarray (Affymetrix 50k) results and demonstrated the limitations of CGH16. CGH fails to detect allelic differences in cases of duplication of the remaining allele (copy neutral LOH) or endoreduplication events. They found that loss of chromosome 11q, which contains MEN1 was common in PA but not recurrent in PCs.

Chromosomal alteration patterns among histological subtypes remain poorly understood, being mentioned in few studies26,21,35. Parathyroid lesions comprise various cell types, such as chief cells, transitional, oncocytic, and clear cells37. Oncocytic subtype is defined by containing > 75% oncocytes37. Chromosomal aberrations in this subtype are not well studied. Oncocytic PAs are typically larger, more symptomatic, and associated with higher serum calcium levels38. In oncocytic thyroid neoplasms, type and extension of chromosomal copy number alterations serve as a molecular and diagnostic hallmark37, distinguishing benign and malignant lesions39. CDC73-mutated parathyroid tumors often exhibit oncocytic morphology3. Sporadic PCs frequently (41–80% cases) harbor inactivating CDC73 mutations, often accompanied by LOH of this locus and show increased genomic instability39–43.

Previously, we described one oncocytic CDC73-wildtype PC showing massive chromosomal losses and near-haploidization, with and subsequent endoreduplication confirmed by SNP array and flow cytometric DNA-content analysis34. Two additional non-functional CDC73-wildtype PCs with similar features were later reported by us45. A near‑haploid genome refers to widespread monosomy across most chromosomes; when subsequently endoreduplicated, it becomes a near-homozygous genome largely devoid of heterozygosity. Such profiles occur in various rare tumor types, including adrenal cortical carcinoma, chondrosarcoma, mesothelioma, oncocytic thyroid carcinoma, gliomas and corticotroph pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNETs)39,34,45–49.

Because earlier CDC73‑wildtype PCs with near‑haploid genomes could represent exceptional cases, and larger exome studies (n = 17) did not identify similar widespread massive whole chromosomal losses67,51, we investigated whether comparable genomic alterations occur in other parathyroid neoplasms. Given the link between oncocytic differentiation and chromosomal instability in thyroid tumors, we analyzed a cohort enriched for oncocytic PAs, alongside three APTs and one additional PC, all CDC73-wildtype.

Materials and methods

We reviewed pseudo-anonymized clinical and pathology records of three previously described CDC73-wildtype PCs with near-haploid/homozygous genomes34,45, and included one additional novel PC case. Three CDC73-wildtype APTs and 14 PA (13 oncocytic) of which 13 were obtained from the Endocrine Unit (FC and CM) of the University of Pisa were also analyzed. All histopathological diagnoses were reviewed by endocrine pathologists (MJ and HM) according to the WHO classification (5th edition, 2022)37. All patients had sporadic parathyroid disease.

Ethics approval and consent

Ethical review of the study was waived by the Medical Ethics Review Committee Leiden (W2020.048) and by the local ethics committee CEAVNO (Comitato Etico Area Vasta Nord Ovest; Prot. No. 9989, Pisa, 20 February 2019). All patients provided written informed consent for the pseudonymized use of their data. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Molecular analysis

All cases underwent genome wide SNP analysis using a custom 1500-SNP panel routinely applied for CNV, imbalance, and -LOH analysis, as previously described39,49. Selected tumors additionally underwent somatic mutation analysis with diverse NGS panels (details in Supporting data). All analyses were performed in the ISO15189-accredited Molecular Diagnostics Unit of the Department of Pathology, Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC). Total nucleic acid was isolated from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue following microdissection of hematoxylin-stained sections to enrich tumor content, guided by diagnostic H&E slides. Given the typically high tumor cell percentage in parathyroid neoplasia, allelic imbalances and LOH were derived from the SNP-allele frequency patterns. Imbalances showed smaller amplitude changes than LOH. CNV analysis clarified the underlying mechanisms (chromosomal gain or loss) and enabled genotype extrapolation (e.g. Figure 2). Copy-number alteration patterns were scored across the cohort and summarized quantitatively. Presence or absence of endoreduplication/genome doubling was scored on basis of SNP profiles39.

Statistics

Because of the limited sample size, the study lacked sufficient statistical power for robust inferential analysis and therefore relied mainly on descriptive statistics. All analyses were performed using Prism 10.2.3 (GraphPad Software, Inc.). Quantitative parameters are presented as minimum, maximum, median, and mean with standard deviation (SD). Data normality was assessed with Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilks tests. Unpaired t-tests were used for comparison of two groups in normally distributed variables. Fisher’s exact test was utilized for contingency analysis. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Of the latter, three had been reported previously and were included for comparison45,34. The cohort comprised 10 men and 11 women, aged 38–78 years (mean 61.29 ± 10.4, median 61).

PCs

The newly analyzed CDC73-wildtype PC19 showed massive chromosomal losses without endoreduplication, similar to previously reported CDC73-wildtype PCs - PC4, P20, and PC21 - with endoreduplication observed in PC20 and PC21 (Fig. 1). Somatic mutations identified in PC19 included NF1 and TSC1. Previously, MEN1 and hTERT mutations were reported in PC4, and PTEN, ARID1B, SETD2, and KDM5C in PC2045.

Discussion

Previously, we reported an oncocytic CDC73-wildtype PC with massive chromosomal losses leading to a near-haploid genome and subsequent endoreduplication resulting in a near-homozygous genome (NHG). Recently, two additional non-functional CDC73-wildtype PCs with similar features were reported34,45. In the current study, we report three more CDC73-wildtype parathyroid tumors (2 APTs and one PC) with near-haploid genomes, despite CDC73 inactivation being a common molecular event in these entities.

Comparable cases have not been reported in previous whole exome and whole genome studies, likely due to enrichment of CDC73-mutant PC in such studies67,44,51,52. In addition, comparative genomic hybridization (CGH) cannot reliably detect copy-neutral LOH or genome-doubling events53.

Although the identification of near-haploidization has not yet translated into targeted therapies, in oncocytic thyroid neoplasms the extent and pattern of chromosomal abnormalities serve as key diagnostic markers, distinguishing benign from malignant lesions and guiding interventions38,53. In thyroid tumors, genome near-haploidization is associated with oncocytic features due to proliferation of abnormal mitochondria, often accompanied by mitochondrial complex I mutations or complex I protein-level defects54,56.

In the current cohort of parathyroid neoplasia, no or few chromosomal abnormalities were seen in oncocytic PAs, and only one near-haploid APT showed oncocytic morphology. This suggests distinct biological mechanisms across tumor types. In oncocytic thyroid cancer, reactive oxygen species are proposed to promote chromosome misalignment, causing gradual chromosomal loss54,56.

Beyond massive chromosomal losses, the mutational landscape of CDC73-wildtype PCs included NF1, TSC1, MEN1, hTERT, PTEN, ARID1B, SETD2, and KDM5C – genes previously implicated in APTs57. Among our three APTs, two exhibited massive chromosomal losses but only one harbored an identifiable mutation (NF1). The two NHG-APTs were resected completely and have remained disease-free during 4–5 years of follow-up. Of the four NHG-PCs, three were metastatic, and one recurred locally following incomplete resection. The first reported NHG-PC developed distant metastases 14 years after initial resection with free margins34. Extensive chromosomal losses have also been described in a PA without aggressive morphology, and in a PC, though additional clinicopathological data were unavailable22,16.

Consistent with prior observations, chromosomal copy-number variations were common in parathyroid tumors but not predictive for malignancy12–14,23,58,59. A considerable proportion of oncocytic PAs (8/14) showed no CNVs, aligning with the literature. PAs with chromosomal alterations were significantly associated with higher preoperative calcium levels but not with PTH levels, tumor size, or sex, although men with ungrouped PAs showed significantly higher preoperative serum calcium levels. Healthy women tend to have higher serum calcium levels, while in case of PA, men reported to have similar or higher serum calcium levels 60,61. Observed associations in this study should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory due to the limited sample size.

PCs in the literature show variable chromosomal alterations, ranging from minimal to up to 80% cases with different extent. We observed LOH at chromosomes 13 and 1p in all three PCs – changes previously reported as characteristic of PCs53,24,67. Two of three APTs also exhibited these alterations, while the third exhibited imbalances due to copy number loss on these chromosomes, considered a pre-LOH state. One of the oncocytic PA also showed loss on the tip of chromosome 1p. Chromosome 9p loss, described as preferentially carcinomatous53,24, occurred in all three PCs and all APTs but none of the PAs, reinforcing the overlap of chromosomal alterations between APTs and PCs. LOH on chromosome 17, present in all PCs and two APTs, further highlights this overlap, as previously noted15. Interestingly, the APT with the least alterations (no NHG) did not show alterations on this chromosome.

NHG-state can unmask mutant alleles when the wild type copy is lost (“second hit”). Loss of single alleles across the genome predisposes to complete tumor suppressor inactivation following a single mutational event, effectively enabling biallelic losses63. When wildtype alleles are retained, they become vulnerable to de novo mutation. Additional functional consequences likely include haploinsufficiency63, and for imprinted loci, loss of the non-imprinted allele may silence remaining regulatory genes66. Moreover, copy neutral LOH involving entire chromosomes, particularly chromosome 6, has been linked in different cancer types to reduced HLA class I expression, facilitating immune escape, a mechanism that could similarly operate here68.

Genome near-haploidization has been documented in other rare neoplasms, including oncocytic thyroid carcinoma, chondrosarcoma, adrenal cortical carcinoma, mesothelioma, gliomas, and corticotroph PitNETs39,34,45–49. In NHG state of oncocytic thyroid carcinoma, chromosome 7 never shows LOH70, which is seen in parathyroid lesions, as illustrated by APT3. Across NHG-tumor types, shared features include relative mutational paucity and the absence of established targeted therapies. Notably, NHG tumors have been described in several endocrine organs, which are intrinsically metabolically active tissues. The TSC1 and NF1 alterations observed in our series suggest dysregulation of the mTOR and RAS/MAPK pathways controlling growth, metabolism, and oxidative stress responses. Such pathway activation may provide a selective growth advantage to cells with reduced genomic content. Functionally, TSC1 negatively regulates mTORC1 driven anabolic growth67,68, while NF1 encodes neurofibromin, a multifunctional protein governing proliferation, differentiation, cytoskeletal organization, and stress signaling69.

Given the modest sample size, the present study should be regarded as exploratory, identifying recurring genomic patterns rather than definitive frequencies or prognostic correlations. Larger studies using higher‑resolution genomic methods (e.g., WES or WGS) will be essential to validate and extend these observations.

In conclusion, CDC73-wildtype APTs and PCs can exhibit massive chromosomal losses resulting in a near-haploid or near-homozygous genomes – a pattern absent in (oncocytic) PAs but shared with oncocytic thyroid carcinoma and other rare tumors. Recognition of these remarkable characteristics may eventually uncover a common Achilles’ heel that could serve as a therapeutic target in the future.

Limitations

This retrospective study has several limitations. Case selection was partly guided by availability of molecular data in the routine diagnostic process and selected oncocytic PA for molecular analysis, which may introduce selection bias. Somatic mutation analysis was performed using different NGS panels across cases, leading to variability in gene coverage. The case number was small, limiting statistical power and the ability to generalize results. Observed associations, such as higher serum calcium levels in chromosomally altered adenomas, should therefore be regarded as hypothesis‑generating. In addition, the targeted 1,500‑SNP array provided reliable detection of broad chromosomal copy‑number and LOH events but lacks the resolution of whole‑exome or whole‑genome sequencing; sub‑chromosomal or focal alterations may have gone undetected. Future multicenter studies using larger, histotype‑balanced cohorts and high‑resolution genomic platforms are required to validate and expand these findings.
