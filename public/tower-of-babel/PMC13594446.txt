Left ventricular wall thickness on systolic-phase pulmonary vein CT as a marker for cardiac amyloidosis
Authors: Ziad Zalaquett, Mazen Hanna, Trejeeve Martyn, Rohit Moudgil, Mohamed Kanj, Milind Desai, Brian Griffin, Wael Jaber, Patrick Collier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac amyloidosis (CA) is under-recognized and frequently associated with atrial fibrillation (AF). Pulmonary vein computed tomography (CT) is routinely obtained by electrophysiologists for AF ablation planning, but its potential for identifying incidental features of CA, such as increased left ventricular (LV) wall thickness, remains unexplored.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate whether LV wall thickness measured on systolic-phase pulmonary vein CT can identify patients with CA.MethodsWe retrospectively identified 160 patients who underwent systolic-phase pulmonary vein CT for AF ablation planning, including 80 patients with confirmed CA, and 80 age- and sex-matched controls. LV anteroseptal and inferoseptal wall thickness were measured using multiplanar reconstruction.ResultsThe mean age was 73 years, and 10% were female. Patients with CA had a significantly higher LV wall thickness than controls (mean septal thickness 22.3 ± 3.5 mm vs 14.9 ± 2.5 mm; P < .001). Multivariable logistic regression identified combined LV septal thickness as an independent predictor of CA (odds ratio, 1.78; 95% confidence interval, 1.43–2.21; P < .001). Receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated excellent discriminatory performance (area under the curve, 0.964; 95% confidence interval, 0.939–0.988; P < .001), with a combined thickness of 36 mm being the optimal cutoff. Notably, 56% of patients with CA underwent pulmonary vein CT before definitive diagnosis, indicating potential for early recognition.ConclusionSystolic-phase LV wall thickness measured on pulmonary vein CT is significantly increased in patients with CA and may serve as an early imaging prompt for further diagnostic evaluation. Routine assessment of LV thickness on AF ablation CTs could provide an opportunity for earlier detection and intervention for unrecognized CA.Keywords: Cardiac amyloidosis, Atrial fibrillation, Pulmonary vein CT, Left ventricular thickness, Cardiac computed tomography

Introduction

Cardiac amyloidosis (CA) is an under-recognized, treatable cardiomyopathy characterized by a high prevalence of atrial fibrillation (AF), even in the early stages (approximately 61% in stage 1 disease).1 Computed tomography (CT) plays a central role in the operative evaluation of patients undergoing AF ablation and has been assigned a “Usually Appropriate” appropriateness category for preprocedural planning for left atrial procedures in AF by the American College of Radiology.2

Left ventricular (LV) wall thickness is not routinely measured or reported in these scans, as it is conventionally measured on short-axis images at end-diastole,3 whereas CT pulmonary vein protocols typically involve systolic acquisitions when the atria are most prominent. Consequently, the diagnostic potential of pulmonary vein CT in detecting incidental features of CA, such as increased LV wall thickness, may be overlooked in patients undergoing AF ablation evaluation.4

This retrospective study reports LV wall thickness measurements on systolic-phase pulmonary vein CT in patients with confirmed CA and a control cohort. We hypothesized that LV wall thickening could provide electrophysiologists with an additional opportunity to recognize CA in patients presenting with AF.

Materials and methods

Study population and objectives

We retrospectively identified patients with confirmed CA and AF who underwent pulmonary vein CT for ablation planning at the Cleveland Clinic. The study period spanned from September 2016 to June 2025. Patients were included if they were 18 years or older, had a confirmed diagnosis of CA through technetium-99m pyrophosphate (PyP) scintigraphy or biopsy, and underwent systolic-phase pulmonary vein CT as part of AF ablation evaluation. Patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy were excluded because of non–amyloid-related myocardial thickening, as well as patients with poor or nondiagnostic CT image quality, which prevents reliable LV wall thickness measurement. A control group of age- and sex-matched patients without evidence of CA, selected using frequency matching, who also underwent pulmonary vein CT for AF ablation planning, was included for comparison. Control patients were required to have no clinical diagnosis or suspicion of CA in the electronic medical record and to have a transthoracic echocardiogram within 12 months of the pulmonary vein CT that did not demonstrate features suggestive of CA. This study was approved by the Cleveland Clinic institutional review board, and the research reported in this study adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines. The requirement of an informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study.

The primary goal of this study is to compare LV wall thickness measured on systolic-phase CT between patients with CA and the control group. In addition, we aimed to determine the optimal cutoff value of LV wall thickness to suggest CA on systolic-phase pulmonary vein CT, providing an opportunity for recognition of the disease in these commonly acquired scans.

Variables

Clinical characteristics

Baseline characteristics including age; sex; body mass index; comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, aortic stenosis, and cardiomyopathy; and amyloid type were retrospectively collected using electronic medical records.

LV wall thickness measurement

Pulmonary vein CT images were acquired and reviewed using Aquarius iNtuition software (version 4.7.1, TeraRecon, Durham, NC) in a window optimized for myocardial assessment. LV wall thickness was measured on systolic-phase CT images using standard multiplanar reconstruction at the basal-ventricular level in the short-axis plane, perpendicular to the endocardial surface. Measurements were obtained at the anteroseptal and inferoseptal walls, while avoiding trabeculations, papillary muscles, and adjacent structures (Figure 1). The maximal wall thickness in any segment was recorded for each patient and used to calculate anteroseptal and inferoseptal wall thickness (mm), combined thickness, and mean thickness. All measurements were performed by an experienced observer blinded to clinical status. A subset of 20 patients was reviewed by a second observer to assess interobserver variability, which demonstrated excellent agreement (intraclass correlation coefficient 0.91). An example of the measurement of anteroseptal and inferoseptal wall thickness on systolic-phase pulmonary vein CT in a control and a patient with CA is presented in Figure 1.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were compared using an independent sample t test or nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or the Fisher exact test as appropriate. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify predictors of the CA group; variables deemed clinically relevant based on previous literature were included in the model to account for confounding. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the ability of LV wall thickness measured on systolic-phase pulmonary vein CT to discriminate patients with CA from controls. The area under the ROC curve was calculated as a measure of overall discriminatory performance. The optimal cutoff value to suggest CA was determined using the Youden index. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were reported for the selected cutoff.

Results

Baseline characteristics and LV thickness

A total of 160 patients were included in the analysis: 80 patients with CA and 80 age- and sex-matched controls. Among those with CA, 71 of 80 patients (89%) had transthyretin (ATTR) amyloidosis, and 9 of 80 patients (11%) had light-chain (AL) amyloidosis. The mean age was 73.4 ± 7 years, and 16 of 160 patients (10%) were female. Although patients with CA more frequently had a history of cardiomyopathy/heart failure (45% vs 26.3%; P = .01), the prevalence of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, and aortic stenosis was similar between the 2 groups. Predictors of CA

A multivariable binary logistic regression model was used to correlate independent variables with CA. Variables that were deemed clinically associated with the outcome were included provided that they were not correlated enough to avoid collinearity. Collinearity among variables was assessed using variance inflation factor, and no significant collinearity was identified. These include age, sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, aortic stenosis, cardiomyopathy, and combined LV septal thickness. After adjusting for covariates, multivariable logistic regression identified that combined LV septal thickness (odds ratio, 1.78; 95% confidence interval, 1.43–2.21; P < .001) was significantly associated with the CA group. Diagnostic performance of the combined LV septal thickness

ROC curve analysis was performed to assess the ability of the combined LV septal thickness measured on systolic-phase pulmonary vein CT to distinguish patients with CA from controls. Combined septal thickness demonstrated excellent discriminatory performance, with an area under the curve of 0.964 (95% confidence interval, 0.939–0.988; P < .001). The optimal cutoff value for suggesting CA was 36 mm, as determined by the Youden index, yielding a sensitivity of 88%, a specificity of 92%, a positive predictive value of 92%, and a negative predictive value of 87%. Discussion

Early recognition of CA is critical, given that timely diagnosis allows initiation of disease-modifying therapy and improves patient outcomes. Pulmonary vein CT, routinely performed for AF ablation planning, represents an underutilized opportunity to detect unsuspected CA. We report that LV septal thickness measured on systolic-phase pulmonary vein CT was significantly higher in patients with CA than controls (mean thickness 22.3 mm vs 14.9 mm) and correlated with CA after adjustment for clinical covariates (P < .001). A combined anteroseptal and inferoseptal thickness of 36 mm discriminated between patients with CA and controls (P < .001). Combined septal thickness was used to provide a more comprehensive assessment of septal involvement and reduce reliance on a single regional measurement. Myocardial thickening in CA reflects extracellular infiltration and is present throughout the cardiac cycle, allowing systolic-phase CT measurements to capture this process. However, these measurements are not equivalent to standard end-diastolic values and should be viewed as a surrogate “red flag” rather than a substitute for conventional imaging. Given that pulmonary vein CT is frequently obtained, yet LV wall thickness is rarely assessed in systole, this represents a potential opportunity for earlier detection.

The septal wall thickness during systole (14.9 mm) in our control group was comparable with previously published results in heterogeneous patient groups. Wang et al5 observed a mean systolic wall thickness of 14.5 mm among 47 patients with suspected or known coronary artery disease using an automated software on cardiac CT. Lessick et al6 reported mean end-systolic thickness values of 14.3 mm in normokinetic segments measured on multidetector CT among 33 patients with chest pain or previous myocardial infarction. Importantly, systolic-phase CT wall thickness measurements are inherently greater than standard echocardiographic and cardiac magnetic resonance measurements, which are conventionally obtained at end-diastole according to guideline recommendations.7,8 Accordingly, the absolute values derived from pulmonary vein CT should not be directly compared with conventional diastolic reference ranges across imaging modalities. Rather, the measurements reported in the present study should be interpreted as systolic CT-based values intended to serve as a potential imaging “red flag” prompting further dedicated evaluation for CA.

We highlight the potential of routine pulmonary vein CT, which is originally performed for AF ablation planning, as an additional opportunity for recognizing unsuspected CA. Delays in CA diagnosis are common owing to nonspecific symptoms and overlap with other cardiac and systemic diseases, often resulting in missed opportunities for early initiation of disease-modifying therapy.9,10 In our study, 56% of patients with CA underwent pulmonary vein CT before definitive diagnosis by PyP or biopsy, with some scans performed as early as 5 years before confirmation. Many of these patients already had prominently increased LV wall thickness on these scans, providing an early imaging clue that went unrecognized. Given the high prevalence of AF even in early-stage ATTR CA (approximately 61% in Stage 1 disease),11 our findings support the value of carefully evaluating routinely acquired pulmonary vein CTs, especially in older patients with AF, as an opportunity to detect unsuspected CA, prompting confirmatory testing and earlier therapeutic intervention.

Clinical implications

Increased LV wall thickness is a key imaging clue that raises suspicion for CA. Even when present on echocardiography, increased LV wall thickness may not always be recognized, reported, or acted upon, so every additional opportunity to not miss this red flag should be used. Indeed, the idea for this study was prompted by a physician reviewing a previous pulmonary vein CT performed on a patient suspected of having CA and contemplating increased LV wall thickness. However, increased LV wall thickness is not specific to CA and may also be encountered in other clinical conditions including longstanding hypertension, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, aortic stenosis, and chronic kidney disease, among others. Accordingly, this finding on pulmonary vein CT should be interpreted within the broader clinical and imaging context and viewed primarily as a potential trigger for further evaluation.

Leveraging every possible opportunity (even on a pulmonary vein CT scan in patients with AF) to prompt screening to detect unrecognized CA may allow patients with otherwise unrecognized disease to have an opportunity for timely intervention. Certain clinical scenarios have been identified that now warrant screening for CA.12 We propose to add the combined septal wall thickness of 36 mm as another diagnostic “red flag.” This finding should prompt further evaluation, including technetium-99m PyP scintigraphy and laboratory assessment, considered in the patient’s overall clinical context. However, this cutoff should be considered exploratory and hypothesis generating and requires validation in external, unselected real-world pulmonary vein CT populations before routine clinical implementation.

Limitations

This was a retrospective single-center analysis. Most patients were male and had ATTR amyloidosis, with relatively few AL cases, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to broader and more diverse patient populations. Although multivariable analysis accounted for common comorbidities that can affect LV thickness, such as hypertension and aortic stenosis, increased wall thickness is not specific to amyloidosis and should always be interpreted in the clinical context, with other potential causes, such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or end-stage renal disease, considered and excluded as appropriate. Moreover, occult CA in the control group cannot be entirely excluded. Additional limitations include the lack of an external validation cohort and potential selection bias, given that only patients who underwent both pulmonary vein CT and definitive amyloid diagnosis were included.

Conclusion

Systolic-phase LV wall thickness measured on pulmonary vein CT obtained for AF ablation planning is significantly increased in patients with CA and may serve as an early prompt for definitive diagnostic testing, providing electrophysiologists with an opportunity to recognize previously unrecognized CA during routine preprocedural evaluation.
