Effect of Oliceridine Fumarate for Patient-Controlled Intravenous Analgesia on Gastrointestinal Function in Laparoscopic Radical Gastrectomy Surgery: A Randomized, Double-Blind, Controlled Trial
Authors: Chen Wang, Wang-Dong Xu, Xin-Lei Lu, Wei-Ping Lei, Jian-Liang Sun, Ya-Qin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative ileus and opioid-related side effects are major barriers to recovery following laparoscopic gastrectomy. Oliceridine, a novel G-protein-biased μ-opioid receptor agonist, aims to offer effective analgesia with fewer adverse effects. This study evaluated the efficacy of oliceridine for patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) on gastrointestinal (GI) recovery and analgesic outcomes.MethodsThis single-center, prospective, randomized, double-blind, controlled trial enrolled 106 patients undergoing elective laparoscopic radical gastrectomy. Participants were randomized to receive either sufentanil (Group S, 1.5 µg/kg) or oliceridine (Group O, 0.35 mg/kg) for PCIA. The primary outcome was time to first flatus. Secondary outcomes included time to first defecation, I-FEED scores, Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores, Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) scores, incidence of adverse events, and postoperative length of stay (LOS).ResultsA total of 96 patients were included in the final analysis. Both groups demonstrated comparable analgesic efficacy, with no significant differences in VAS scores over 48 hours. However, the oliceridine group exhibited significantly faster GI recovery, including shorter times to first flatus (52.9 vs 59.2 hours) and first defecation (82.0 vs 93.0 hours). Patients in the oliceridine group also had lower I-FEED scores and resumed oral intake significantly faster. Oliceridine significantly reduced the incidence of nausea (10.2% vs 25.5%) and vomiting (4.1% vs 21.3%) compared to sufentanil. This group also demonstrated a calmer emergence profile, earlier first ambulation, and a significantly shorter postoperative LOS [11 (10, 12) vs 12 (10, 14) days]. No respiratory depression was observed in either cohort.ConclusionIn patients undergoing laparoscopic radical gastrectomy, oliceridine-based PCIA provides analgesic efficacy comparable to sufentanil but significantly accelerates gastrointestinal function recovery, improves emergence quality, reduces the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting, and shortens hospital stay. It represents a valuable analgesic strategy for Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols.Trial RegistrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry. Introduction

With advances in surgical techniques, laparoscopic radical resection has become the standard procedure for treating malignant gastrointestinal tumors.1,2 Despite the use of minimally invasive techniques, patients still experience moderate to severe postoperative pain. Studies indicate that approximately 20% of patients endure intense pain postoperatively,3 which not only triggers a strong physiological stress response but also directly limits early ambulation. Effective postoperative pain management is the cornerstone of implementing Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols.

Currently, μ-opioid receptor (MOR) agonists remain the gold standard for managing moderate to severe acute pain. While traditional opioids (eg, morphine, sufentanil) provide effective analgesia, the opioid-related adverse events (ORAEs)4,5 they induce—particularly postoperative ileus (POI), nausea, and vomiting—are especially detrimental for gastrointestinal surgery patients. These adverse effects, coupled with concerns over respiratory depression, often lead to inadequate pain management, significantly compromising patient outcomes. Mechanistic studies6 indicate these adverse effects largely stem from MOR activation recruiting the β-arrestin 2 pathway, while analgesic effects are mediated through a parallel G-protein signaling pathway. Targeting this distinct mechanism, oliceridine fumarate (TRV130) was developed as a novel, first-in-class G-protein-biased MOR ligand. Unlike traditional opioids, it selectively activates the G-protein pathway while minimally recruiting the β-arrestin 2 pathway.7 Molecular pharmacology studies8 and Phase III clinical trials9,10 indicate that this biased signaling mechanism may provide effective analgesia while potentially reducing the incidence of respiratory depression and gastrointestinal complications.

Current research on oliceridine primarily focuses on its analgesic effects, with insufficient studies on gastrointestinal functional recovery in patients undergoing laparoscopic radical gastrectomy. Given the stringent requirements for gastrointestinal (GI) recovery in these procedures, investigating whether oliceridine provides superior gastrointestinal tolerance compared to traditional opioids—while maintaining adequate analgesic quality—would offer robust theoretical support for optimizing Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols for this patient population.

Methods

Study Design, Participant Selection, and Ethical Approval

This randomized, controlled, double-blind clinical trial was conducted at Hangzhou First People’s Hospital, affiliated with Westlake University School of Medicine, from January 15, 2025, to January 15, 2026. The study protocol received approval from the hospital’s ethics committee in November 2024 (Approval No. KY-2024414-01). The trial was registered on January 06, 2025, with the China Clinical Trial Registry (www.chictr.org.cn) (Registration Number: ChiCTR2500095364, https://www.chictr.org.cn/bin/project/edit?pid=245790). All study participants provided written informed consent. The conduct and reporting of this clinical trial adhere to the CONSORT guidelines.

Patients were included if they met the following criteria: 1) scheduled for elective laparoscopic radical gastrectomy; 2) estimated surgical duration of 1–4 hours; 3) age 18–65 years; 4) body mass index (BMI) of 18–29 kg/m2; 5) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III. Patients were excluded if they presented with any of the following: 1) Active pulmonary infection; 2) severe target organ dysfunction (eg, liver, kidney, heart, brain); 3) History of difficult airway management; 4) Known allergy to any component of the investigational drug, or allergy/contraindication to anesthetic/analgesic agents used in the study; 5) Use of medications affecting postoperative analgesia (eg, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) within 24 hours prior to randomization.

Randomization and Blinding

Patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated random number table into the sufentanil group (Group S) and the oliceridine group (Group O). Randomization results were placed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, which were kept and distributed by a third-party anesthesiologist. All patients, participating surgeons and anesthesiologists, and postoperative outcome evaluators were strictly blinded. An independent anesthesiologist opened the envelopes and prepared study medications according to the assigned group. This preparing anesthesiologist did not participate in subsequent patient care or data collection. Analgesic pumps were prepared based on group assignment. In Group S, sufentanil 1.5 μg/kg was diluted with 0.9% saline to a total volume of 100 mL for patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA). In Group O, oliceridine 0.35 mg/kg was diluted with 0.9% saline to a total volume of 100 mL for PCIA.

The selection of this 0.35 mg/kg regimen was derived from our prior pilot study and aligns with the findings by Singla et al, which indicated that this oliceridine dose exhibited a superior safety and tolerability profile regarding gastrointestinal and respiratory adverse events.11 Study doses were expressed as body-weight-normalized intravenous morphine milligram equivalents (MME) to provide an approximate basis for intergroup comparison. Assuming an approximately 1000-fold greater potency of sufentanil relative to morphine,5 the 1.5 µg/kg sufentanil dose corresponds to an estimated 1.5 mg/kg MME. For oliceridine, the 0.35 mg/kg dose was evaluated using reported analgesic relative potency estimates of approximately 4- to 10-fold versus morphine, corresponding to an estimated exposure range of 1.4 to 3.5 mg/kg MME.12 In addition, based on a reported clinical equianalgesic conversion approach in which 1 mg of oliceridine is considered approximately equivalent to 5 mg of intravenous morphine,13 the 0.35 mg/kg oliceridine regimen yields a point estimate of 1.75 mg/kg MME. Although these conversions are inherently approximate because of differences in pharmacokinetics, receptor signaling profiles, and context-dependent analgesic potency, they support the rationale for selecting broadly comparable opioid doses across treatment groups. PCIA parameters were set as follows: a continuous background infusion rate of 2 mL/h, a bolus dose of 2 mL, a lockout interval of 15 min, and a maximum limit of 10 mL/h.

All data collection and scoring were performed by the same specialist physician who was unaware of the group assignments. The specific allocation codes remained confidential and were disclosed only when a medical emergency necessitated knowledge of the specific opioid medication used.

Anesthesia and Intraoperative Management

Patients underwent routine preoperative fasting and fluid restriction. Upon operating room entry, intravenous access was established, and standard vital signs including non-invasive blood pressure (NBP), electrocardiogram (ECG), peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO2), bispectral index (BIS), and end-tidal carbon dioxide pressure (PETCO2), were continuously monitored. Anesthetic induction was performed using sequential intravenous agents: midazolam 0.01 mg/kg, sufentanil 0.5 μg/kg, cisatracurium 0.15–0.2 mg/kg, and propofol 1–2 mg/kg. Following tracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation was initiated in volume-controlled ventilation (VCV) mode, with end-tidal carbon dioxide pressure (PETCO2) maintained at 35–45 mmHg.

Anesthesia was maintained using a combination of inhalation and intravenous agents: sevoflurane (1–2%), propofol [3–5 mg/(kg • h)], and remifentanil [0.1–0.2 μg/(kg·min)]. Cisatracurium was administered intermittently as needed. During anesthesia, drug dosages were adjusted to maintain a BIS value between 40 and 60 and to keep hemodynamic variables within ±20% of baseline levels. All procedures were performed by the same surgical team according to the Japanese Gastric Cancer Treatment Guidelines 2021 (6th edition). A standard D2 lymphadenectomy, including complete resection of the vagus nerve trunks, was performed in all patients. No nerve-preserving techniques were used in either group. Dexamethasone (5mg) and tropisetron (5mg) were administered at 30 minutes before the end of surgery for postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) prophylaxis. Postoperatively, local infiltration anesthesia was performed at the incision site with ropivacaine.

During skin closure, Group S received 5μg of sufentanil to prevent postoperative breakthrough pain, while Group O received 1.5 mg of oliceridine. At the conclusion of surgery, both groups were connected to corresponding PCA pumps. At the end of the procedure, residual neuromuscular blockade was routinely reversed with neostigmine and atropine. All patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) for observation for 2 hours.

Postoperative Management

If a patient experienced inadequate analgesia (defined as a static VAS score ≥5 persisting for 30 minutes despite a valid PCA bolus) within 48 hours postoperatively, flurbiprofen (50 mg) was administered for rescue analgesia. Postoperative nutritional support followed a uniform institutional protocol based on body weight (target: 25–30 kcal/kg/day). Total parenteral nutrition (TPN) was initiated on postoperative day 1 for all patients. Transition from TPN to enteral nutrition or oral feeding was started upon the return of bowel function, provided there was no severe nausea, vomiting, or abdominal distension. TPN was completely discontinued once oral or enteral intake met >60% of the daily energy target.

Outcome Measurements

The primary outcome was time to first flatus, defined as the interval (in hours) from the end of surgery to the first passage of flatus. Secondary endpoints included postoperative pain intensity (VAS) scores at immediate awakening and at 0.5, 2, 6, 12, 24, and 48 hours postoperatively; sedation (RASS) scores within 120 minutes after extubation; intraoperative remifentanil consumption; total PCA bolus administrations; effective PCA demand rate (valid presses/total presses) within 48 hours postoperatively; requirements for rescue analgesia and antiemetics; incidence of adverse reactions (eg, dizziness, respiratory depression, nausea/vomiting, pruritus, urinary retention); postoperative gastrointestinal function assessed via I-FEED (Intake, Feeling nauseated, Emesis, physical Exam, and Duration of symptoms) scores, time to first defecation, time to first ambulation, and time to first oral feeding; hospitalization costs, and length of stay.

Sample Size Calculation

Sample size calculation was based on a pilot study involving 20 subjects (10 per group). The pilot results showed that the mean time to first flatus was 61.0 ± 14.9 hours in group S and 52.0 ± 13.6 hours in group O, yielding a pooled standard deviation of 15 hours. With a two-sided α of 0.05 and a power (1-β) of 0.80, sample size calculation using PASS 15.0 software for an independent-sample t-test indicated that each group required at least 45 cases. Considering an estimated 10% loss to follow-up, 106 patients were initially enrolled, with 96 ultimately included in the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 29.0.2.0 statistical software. Data distribution and normality for all continuous variables were formally evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (X ± SD) and were compared using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous data and ordinal data are presented as median (interquartile range) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical data are presented as number (percentage) and were compared using the Pearson Chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test when the expected frequency was less than 5. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed using the Per-Protocol (PP) set, including all patients who successfully completed the assigned analgesic intervention. To evaluate the potential impact of post-randomization exclusions, a scenario-based sensitivity analysis was conducted for the primary endpoint (time to first flatus). By simulating extreme outcomes for any missing data, this approach aimed to ensure the robustness of the primary conclusions regardless of patient attrition.

To strictly validate that oliceridine provides gastrointestinal benefits without compromising analgesic quality, a post-hoc non-inferiority analysis was performed for the primary analgesic outcome (postoperative VAS scores). Based on established clinical consensus and the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for acute pain, the non-inferiority margin (δ) was pre-specified as 1.0 point on the 0–10 VAS scale.14 Non-inferiority was established if the upper bound of the two-sided 95% confidence interval (CI) for the mean difference in VAS scores (Group O minus Group S) did not exceed this δ of 1.0. Additionally, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted to evaluate whether the accrued sample size (N = 96) provided sufficient statistical power (1-β) to detect non-inferiority under this margin.

A total of 106 patients underwent preliminary eligibility assessment, with 6 excluded (4 due to non-compliance with inclusion criteria, 2 due to refusal to participate). The remaining 100 patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to Group S (n=50) and Group O (n=50). During the study, 3 patients were excluded from the group S (2 due to surgical plan changes preventing intervention, 1 due to intraoperative hemorrhage) and 1 from the group O (due to intraoperative hemorrhage). Recovery of Gastrointestinal Function

The primary endpoint and gastrointestinal function evaluation are summarized in Table 2. The mean time to first flatus in Group O was 52.9 ± 15.1 hours, significantly shorter than the 59.2 ± 13.5 hours in Group S (mean difference = −6.3 hours, 95% CI: −12.1 to −0.5; P = 0.034). The median time to first postoperative defecation in Group O was 82.0 (71.0, 95.0) hours, significantly earlier than that in Group S [93.0 (76.0, 109.0) hours] (Hodges-Lehmann median difference = −12.0 hours, 95% CI: −21.0 to −2.0; P = 0.015). Postoperative gastrointestinal function assessment revealed a median I-FEED score of 2 in Group O, significantly lower than Group S’score of 3 (P= 0.007), indicating superior postoperative gastrointestinal tolerance in the Oliceridine group. The time to resume liquid diet (P = 0.01) and semi-liquid diet (P < 0.001) in Group O was significantly earlier than that in Group S. Furthermore, the scenario-based sensitivity analysis incorporating the extreme simulated outcomes for the four excluded cases yielded results entirely consistent with the primary PP analysis, confirming that the significant difference in gastrointestinal recovery between the two groups remained robust.

Clinical Recovery and Economic Metrics

The time to first ambulation in Group O was 61 hours, significantly earlier than the 67 hours in Group S (P = 0.024). Additionally, patients in Group O experienced a significantly shorter time to first oral feeding than those in Group S (76.1 ± 6.0 hours vs 79.3±8.1 hours, P = 0.031). The median postoperative hospital stay in Group O [11 (10, 12) days] was significantly shorter than that in Group S [12 (10, 14) days, P = 0.049]. There was no statistically significant difference in total hospitalization costs between the two groups (P = 0.083). Comparison of postoperative 24-hour C-reactive protein (CRP) levels showed no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.621).

Analgesic and Sedative Outcomes

Postoperative pain intensity varied depending on the assessment state. Static VAS scores remained consistently within a mild-to-moderate range throughout the 48-hour observation window. Conversely, dynamic VAS scores in both cohorts exhibited a transient peak into the moderate-to-severe range (approaching 6.0) at the 6-hour postoperative mark during early bedside mobilization, before gradually declining and stabilizing at mild levels. There were no statistically significant differences in either static or dynamic VAS scores between Group O and Group S at any evaluated time point (P > 0.05; Figure 2A and B).

Formal non-inferiority testing confirmed that oliceridine maintained analgesic efficacy non-inferior to sufentanil. At the 2-hour landmark, the mean difference in dynamic VAS scores between Group O and Group S was 0.23 (95% CI: −0.20 to 0.66), with the upper bound strictly below the 1.0-point margin. This non-inferior alignment was consistently maintained across all secondary time points within the 48-hour window. Additionally, the post-hoc power analysis verified that the accrued sample of 96 patients provided a statistical power (1-β) of 94.1% to detect non-inferiority at the peak 2-hour dynamic landmark.

Regarding sedation recovery, the RASS score at immediate awakening (T0) was significantly lower and closer to 0 in Group O compared to Group S (P = 0.037). Analgesic Consumption and Adverse Events

There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in total PCA demands, effective PCA demand rate, or total PCA consumption (P > 0.05; Table 3). There was no significant difference in the need for rescue analgesia between the two groups (Group O: 1 case; Group S: 3 cases; P = 0.580). The proportion of patients requiring postoperative rescue antiemetics was significantly lower in Group O (4.1%) than in Group S (19.1%; P = 0.025) (Table 4). Within 48 hours postoperatively, Group O exhibited significantly lower incidence rates of nausea (10.2% vs 25.5%, P = 0.049) and vomiting (4.1% vs 21.3%, P = 0.011) compared to the Group S. No cases of respiratory depression were observed in either group. The incidence of dizziness was comparable between the groups (P = 0.954; Table 4).

Discussion

Our findings indicate that compared to the traditional opioid sufentanil, oliceridine provides equivalent postoperative analgesia while demonstrating superior gastrointestinal tolerability. Oliceridine significantly shortened the time to first postoperative flatus and defecation, while also substantially reducing the incidence of nausea and vomiting within 48 hours postoperatively. This early restoration of gastrointestinal function further facilitated earlier ambulation and shorter overall hospital stays.

Laparoscopic radical gastrectomy often involves complex intra-abdominal procedures and gastrointestinal tract reconstruction. The trauma and local inflammatory stress induced postoperatively readily trigger postoperative ileus,15,16 which significantly impedes patients’ early recovery.17 In traditional postoperative pain management, clinical practice often faces a dilemma between “adequate analgesia” and “maintaining gastrointestinal motility”. While traditional opioids like sufentanil serve as the cornerstone for controlling moderate-to-severe pain, they simultaneously activate the β-arrestin 2 signaling pathway when stimulating MOR receptors.18 Previous studies19 have confirmed that excessive activation of β-arrestin 2 leads to opioid-related adverse events (ORAEs), particularly postoperative ileus and respiratory depression. As a novel G protein-biased MOR ligand, oliceridine can highly selectively activate the G protein pathway to ensure analgesic efficacy while minimally recruiting the β-arrestin 2 pathway.20 In this study, patients in Group O experienced a significantly shorter time to first flatus and fewer gastrointestinal complications while maintaining low VAS scores. Notably, there was no statistically significant difference in C-reactive protein (CRP) levels at 24 hours postoperatively between the two groups, effectively ruling out interference from differing levels of surgical stress on the study outcomes. Therefore, we can more confidently conclude that the improvement in gastrointestinal function in group O primarily stems from oliceridine’s unique G-protein-biased mechanism, rather than differences in anti-inflammatory effects. This biased mechanism maximally preserves the autonomic peristaltic function of the gastrointestinal tract by blocking or reducing inhibitory signals to gastrointestinal smooth muscle at the source, while ensuring effective analgesic efficacy.

The preservation of this spontaneous gastrointestinal motility directly promotes the comprehensive recovery of postoperative intestinal function in patients. As a primary objective indicator for assessing the severity of postoperative ileus (POI),21 the time to first flatus was significantly improved in this study. However, in clinical practice, there remains a physiological transition period between the restoration of intestinal motility alone and the patient’s ability to resume oral feeding. Therefore, this study further employed the I-FEED score as a supplementary assessment of early gastrointestinal tolerance status.22 Our results demonstrated that compared to group S, patients in group O not only exhibited significantly shorter times to first flatus and first defecation but also showed significantly lower postoperative I-FEED scores. This enhanced tolerance created conditions for the successful early resumption of oral feeding. Patients in group O resumed liquid diets and semi-liquid diets significantly earlier than those in group S. Extensive research23–25 demonstrates that early enteral nutrition effectively protects the intestinal mucosal barrier and reduces postoperative infectious complications, serving as a core component of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS). Traditional opioid medications often induce delayed gastric emptying and intestinal distension,26 compelling clinicians to postpone feeding schedules. Oliceridine breaks this impasse. Due to reduced discomfort such as abdominal distension, patients in group O achieved their first ambulation significantly earlier than those in group S, ultimately leading to a significant reduction in postoperative hospital stay. This shorter stay may help offset the higher unit cost of oliceridine as a novel drug. Although total hospitalization expenses were numerically lower in Group O, this difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.083). Therefore, the use of oliceridine did not drive up the overall financial burden. This strategy of offsetting drug costs through accelerated recovery undoubtedly provides valuable reference for surgeons and patients. Collectively, these results confirm that oliceridine significantly improves overall gastrointestinal function after laparoscopic radical gastrectomy, effectively supporting the successful implementation of the ERAS pathway.

In the clinical practice of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS), early ambulation is a core component for preventing postoperative complications and promoting systemic functional recovery. This study found that PONV, the most common complication following general anesthesia, often serves as the primary barrier to early patient mobilization. Frequent PONV not only leads to electrolyte imbalances27 and increases the risk of incisional dehiscence,28 but also significantly reduces patient satisfaction with care and causes delayed hospital discharge.29 Our findings indicate that the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting in group O was significantly lower than in Group S. Furthermore, the proportion of patients requiring rescue antiemetic medication was significantly lower in group O compared to Group S. This result further validates the advantage of oliceridine in reducing gastrointestinal-related adverse reactions. This may relate to its specific receptor-biased mechanism, where minimal β-arrestin 2 recruitment potentially reduces excessive stimulation of the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) in the posterior region of the fourth ventricle floor (Area Postrema, AP).30,31 Regarding other adverse effects, no postoperative respiratory depression events occurred in either group. The study sample size may have been insufficient to detect such rare events, but prior research32,33 has confirmed that oliceridine possesses a wider respiratory safety margin than traditional opioids. Concurrently, we observed that patients in Group O demonstrated highly stable RASS scores at immediate extubation (T0) that were closer to zero than those in Group S (P = 0.037). This suggests that oliceridine, like sufentanil, maintains an excellent safety margin by providing effective analgesia without inducing prolonged or excessive sedation. Such a stable recovery profile is essential for the early mobilization and neurocognitive safety of patients undergoing complex laparoscopic radical gastrectomy.

The incidence of dizziness was comparable between groups, indicating that oliceridine did not increase the risk of additional central nervous system side effects. Overall, in the context of patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) during laparoscopic gastrectomy surgery, oliceridine not only provides equivalent analgesia but also significantly reduces postoperative adverse reactions, offering patients a safer and more comfortable foundation for early recovery.

Regarding clinical recovery, our findings demonstrate that oliceridine-based PCIA significantly shortened the postoperative length of stay compared to the sufentanil group, highlighting its distinct clinical value in facilitating the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) pathway. Nevertheless, the absolute length of stay observed in our study and the corresponding dietary milestones still exceed highly optimized international ERAS benchmarks. These extended timelines reflect specific clinical and regional realities. Laparoscopic radical gastrectomy with systemic D2 lymphadenectomy entails extensive lymphatic and vascular dissection; furthermore, the high proportion of total gastrectomies (>66%) in our cohort necessitated a more conservative approach to oral progression to safeguard anastomotic integrity. Institutional protocols also dictate a safety-prioritized approach, where prophylactic abdominal drains are retained longer to monitor for subclinical leaks, and discharge strictly requires sustained tolerance of a semi-liquid diet alongside normalized inflammatory markers. Additionally, this extended in-hospital monitoring is driven by regional healthcare reimbursement structures and cultural preferences against early transition to home care.34 Notably, these clinical practices and absolute timelines are highly consistent with previous domestic studies evaluating ERAS implementation in gastric cancer surgery within China.35 Therefore, our cohort reflects a realistic regional clinical baseline, further strengthening the validity of the relative recovery benefits observed with oliceridine.

Limitations

Although this study yielded positive clinical outcomes, certain limitations exist. As a single-center clinical trial with a relatively small sample size, potential selection bias may be present. Future multi-center, large-scale randomized controlled trials are needed to further validate these findings. The follow-up observation in this study was primarily confined to the acute postoperative period (during hospitalization), failing to assess the preventive effect of oliceridine on chronic postoperative pain or its potential impact on long-term outcomes (eg, recurrence rates, survival) in gastric cancer patients undergoing gastrectomy. Regarding intervention design, this study evaluated only a single dose level (0.35 mg/kg) of oliceridine for PCIA. We selected this specific dose based on our pilot data and existing literature to ensure comparable analgesia to sufentanil, thereby minimizing the confounding effect of inadequate pain control on gastrointestinal recovery. However, this design prevents us from evaluating a dose-response relationship. Future trials should incorporate dose-ranging protocols to determine the optimal balance between analgesic efficacy and gastrointestinal preservation. Additionally, incorporating oliceridine into multimodal analgesia regimens with non-opioids (such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs] or dexmedetomidine) warrants further investigation to optimize ERAS pathways. Furthermore, exact daily caloric intake was not individually recorded in our case report forms. Although all patients received standardized nutritional management based on their body weight, the lack of granular calorie tracking limits our ability to evaluate the precise energetic supply during the early recovery phase.

Conclusion

In PCIA for laparoscopic radical gastrectomy surgery, oliceridine demonstrated analgesic efficacy comparable to sufentanil while significantly improving early gastrointestinal tolerance, providing a calmer emergence profile, shortening time to first flatus, and substantially reducing postoperative nausea and vomiting incidence. These clinical advantages, based on its unique receptor-selective mechanism, accelerate patients’ first ambulation and postoperative recovery, shortening total hospital stay. However, the limited sample size (N = 96) in this study precludes the detection of rare safety events such as respiratory depression. In summary, while larger trials are needed to fully confirm its safety profile, oliceridine represents a highly clinically valuable new analgesic strategy for optimizing Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols.
