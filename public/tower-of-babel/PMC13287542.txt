Occult osteophyte fracture as an atypical source of massive hemothorax in diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis: illustrative case
Authors: Daiki Nakajima, Kenichiro Kita, Yoshinori Takahashi, Yuichiro Goda, Tadashi Uchida, Toshiharu Sogo, Koichi Sairyo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDDiffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) limits spinal mobility, predisposing patients to unstable fractures even following minor trauma. Diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) is characterized by systemic ossification of the spinal ligaments, leading to progressive ankylosis.1 This rigidity transforms the spine into a “long lever arm,” rendering it vulnerable to unstable fractures even from low-energy trauma.2 While spinal fractures associated with DISH are increasingly recognized in the aging population, massive hemothorax following such injuries remains a rare and potentially fatal complication.3,4

The clinical challenge in DISH-associated thoracic injuries is the atypical source of bleeding. Unlike common traumatic hemothorax caused by rib fractures or lung parenchymal injury, bleeding in DISH patients often originates from fractured osteophytes or the highly vascularized ossified ligaments themselves.3,4 In this report, we describe the case of a massive hemothorax (about 2400 mL) caused by an isolated osteophyte fracture at the T7–8 level, separate from the unstable fracture at the T11 vertebra diagnosed at initial presentation. We also emphasize the synergistic role of anticoagulant therapy and the potential rebleeding risk associated with initial thoracic drainage.

Illustrative Case

An 84-year-old man with a history of atrial fibrillation managed with a factor Xa inhibitor was brought to the emergency department after falling approximately 2 m from a ladder. He was found by a passerby, immobilized by severe back pain, and was transported to the emergency department. On arrival, he was conscious and alert and hemodynamically stable, albeit with borderline hypotension. Neurological examination revealed no motor or sensory deficits. Initial plain radiographic and contrast-enhanced CT imaging revealed a right-sided hemothorax without evidence of pulmonary parenchymal injury or rib fractures (Fig. 1A and B) Furthermore, CT and MRI demonstrated a reverse Chance-type fracture at T11 and a vertebral fracture at T12 and L5 (Fig. 1C– E). We decided to perform elective surgery.

A: Plain anteroposterior radiograph of the chest. B: Axial contrast-enhanced CT scan showing fluid collection on the dorsal side of the right lung (arrow). There was no evidence of pulmonary parenchymal injury or rib fractures. C and D:Primary sagittal (C) and right parasagittal (D) CT scans demonstrating a reverse Chance-type fracture at T11. Arrows indicates the fracture line. E: MR image also showing a reverse Chance-type fracture at T11 and vertebral fractures at T12 and L5.

Ten hours after the initial presentation, follow-up plain chest radiography and contrast-enhanced CT showed increased fluid accumulation in the right pleural cavity (Fig. 2). A chest tube was inserted by the thoracic surgery team; however, the drainage output exceeded 200 mL/hr, reaching a cumulative volume of more than 2400 mL. The patient required a massive transfusion (20 units of red blood cells, 20 units of fresh-frozen plasma, and 10 units of platelet concentrate) to maintain hemodynamic stability. However, conservative management failed to achieve hemostasis, and emergency video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) was performed by the thoracic surgery team.

Follow-up contrast-enhanced CT scan showing an increase in fluid collection in the right pleural cavity (arrow).

In the operation, a large hematoma was observed within the thoracic cavity. No active bleeding was identified from the intercostal spaces or lung parenchyma. On retracting the lung, soft tissue disruption and a fracture line were identified at the T7–8 osteophyte level, which was identified as the site of persistent bleeding (Fig. 3A and B). After confirming the absence of bleeding from the T11 vertebral fracture and ruling out diaphragmatic injury (Fig. 3A and C), the osteophyte fracture at T7–8 was determined to be the source of the hemorrhage. Hemostasis was successfully achieved using monopolar electrocautery (Fig. 4A) and human thrombin (Fig. 4B), followed by the application of fibrin glue (Fig. 4C). The procedure was concluded with the placement of a chest tube.

A:Three-dimensional CT image of the thoracolumbar spine. The arrow indicates the area shown in panel B, while the arrowhead indicates the area shown in panel C. B and C: Intraoperative views of VATS after lung retraction. Soft tissue disruption and a fracture line at the T7–8 osteophyte level were confirmed as the source of persistent bleeding (B). At the level of the thoracic vertebra of T11 demonstrating a reverse Chance fracture, the parietal pleura was preserved intact, and a subpleural hematoma collection was observed (C).

Intraoperative findings of VATS. A:The pleura was coagulated using monopolar electorocautery. B and C:A topical hemostatic agent containing human thrombin (B) and fibrinogen (C) was applied to the fracture site.

Subsequently, intrathoracic hemorrhage ceased, and the patient’s overall clinical condition stabilized. Using plain chest radiography, we confirmed that the hemothorax did not worsen after the drain was removed (Fig. 5A). On the following day, spinal fusion surgery wahtts performed. Considering the bone fragility associated with the DISH-related vertebral fracture, the cranial pedicle screws were inserted using the double endplates penetrating screw (DEPS) technique (Fig. 5B and C). The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful with no recurrence of bleeding. He was transferred to a rehabilitation hospital for physical therapy on postoperative day 28. Currently, he is able to ambulate independently, resides at home, and continues to receive outpatient follow-up care.

Discussion

Observations

Hemothorax is commonly associated with blunt chest trauma, where the primary sources of bleeding are typically the intercostal vessels, internal mammary arteries, or intrathoracic organs such as the lungs and heart.5–7 In most clinical scenarios, these injuries are accompanied by overt traumatic findings such as multiple rib fractures or lung contusions.4,8 However, as observed in the case presented here, the absence of multiple traumatic findings and the lack of clear damage to the chest wall or mediastinum can lead to significant initial management difficulties.8,9

Pathophysiological Mechanism of Atypical Hemorrhage

In the context of DISH, the source of hemorrhage is often atypical and occult.10,11 The rigid, ankylosed spine under hyperextension-distraction forces can sustain fractures at multiple levels. Unlike standard trauma cases, the bleeding in our patient originated from the sharp fragments of a fractured osteophyte directly lacerating the adjacent parietal pleura at the T7–8 level. This distant bleeding source; remote from the T11 fracture diagnosed at initial presentation, combined with the absence of rib fractures or pulmonary parenchymal injuries, can mislead clinicians during the initial assessment.8,9,12 Furthermore, systematic reviews have suggested that DISH-related spinal segments may exhibit increased vascularity, leading to more persistent bleeding from the cancellous bone of the osteophyte or disrupted intercostal vessels than is seen in typical vertebral fractures.13 In contrast, regarding the reverse Chance-type fracture at T11, it is inferred that the parietal pleura remained intact. We hypothesize that initial hemostasis at this site was achieved due to the increased pressure within the hemorrhage site, acting as a natural tamponade effect.

Aggravation by Anticoagulation and Consumptive Coagulopathy

The patient’s use of a factor Xa inhibitor significantly hindered the initial hemostatic process. The continuous intrathoracic blood loss subsequently triggered a consumptive coagulopathy, creating a vicious cycle of hemorrhage that necessitated massive transfusion. In Japan’s aging society, many older individuals have multiple comorbidities, and it is not uncommon for them to be taking anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs.8 This highlights the critical importance of early reversal of anticoagulation or antiplatelet drugs in DISH patients with even minor spinal trauma.

Paradoxical Effect of Chest Tube Insertion

While chest tube insertion is standard for managing hemothorax, it may have inadvertently exacerbated the bleeding in this specific case. Initial accumulation of blood within the pleural space may provide a temporary tamponade effect. However, rapid evacuation of the hematoma via negative-pressure aspiration likely abolished this effect, causing a flare of bleeding from the fractured osteophyte.14,15 Clinicians should be prepared for immediate surgical intervention, such as VATS, when rapid drainage occurs after chest tube placement in DISH patients.16–18

Strengths and Limitations

The clinical significance of this report lies in identifying a remote, occult osteophyte fracture as a life-threatening cause of massive hemothorax in DISH, a finding that underscores the potential risks of conventional chest tube drainage in such scenarios. We recognize, however, that these findings derived from a single case may not be universally applicable to all DISH patients with spinal trauma. Furthermore, while the hypothesized natural tamponade effect at T11 is clinically plausible, the precise biomechanical forces leading to an isolated osteophyte fracture at T7–8, in the absence of a major structural fracture, remain partly speculative.

Lessons

Based on our observations in this case, we highlight the following critical lessons for clinical practice. In patients with DISH presenting with massive hemothorax, clinicians must consider an isolated osteophyte fracture as the potential source of hemorrhage. These fractures can directly lacerate the parietal pleura and may be located anatomically distant from the spinal fracture diagnosed at initial presentation, even in the absence of rib or lung parenchymal injuries. Furthermore, surgeons must be aware of the paradox of chest drainage. While chest tube insertion is a standard initial intervention for hemothorax, rapid evacuation of the hematoma can abolish the natural tamponade effect within the pleural space, inadvertently exacerbating the bleeding from highly vascularized osteophytes. Finally, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for ongoing hemorrhage and be prepared for immediate surgical intervention, such as VATS, especially in older patients on anticoagulant therapy in whom consumptive coagulopathy can rapidly develop.
