Circumferential quantitative analysis of iris angiography reveals progressive pupillary-margin leakage across stages of diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Qianhui Yang, Huilan Zhang, Yifeng Ke, Xiaorong Li, Longli Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the value of a circumference-based quantitative analysis of iris angiography (IA) in evaluating iris vascular changes across different stages of diabetic retinopathy (DR).MethodsThis observational study included 66 healthy subjects and 185 patients with diabetes, classified as no apparent retinopathy, non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR), and proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR). All participants underwent ultrawide-field fundus fluorescein angiography and IA, including iris fluorescein angiography and iris indocyanine green angiography. A custom-built software was used to quantify fluorescein leakage at the pupillary margin by measuring leakage time (LT) and circumferential leakage range (LR, degrees). Comparisons among groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance.ResultsNo fluorescein leakage was observed at the pupillary margin in healthy subjects aged 20–39 years, whereas mild and transient leakage was detected in older healthy patients. In patients with DR, LT was significantly shorter in the PDR group than in the no-retinopathy and NPDR groups (25.67 ± 5.03 s vs. 33.14 ± 3.03 s and 32.45 ± 5.17 s, respectively; all P < 0.001). The LR increased with DR severity, measuring 21.21 ± 30.06° in the no-retinopathy group, 62.48 ± 42.17° in the NPDR group, and 141.31 ± 73.61° in the PDR group (all P < 0.001). LR values in the NPDR and PDR groups were significantly greater than those in age-matched healthy subjects.ConclusionCircumference-based quantitative analysis of IA enables quantitative assessment of pupillary-margin fluorescein leakage and was associated with DR severity. This method may provide additional information for evaluating anterior segment vascular involvement in patients with diabetic retinopathy, particularly when posterior segment examination is limited.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400081639), registered on Mar 7, 2024.Keywords: Iris angiography, Circumference, Leakage time, Quantitative analysis

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is one of the common retinal vascular diseases. DR is a severe retinal vascular disease and a leading cause of vision loss in the working-age adults [1]. Recent estimates suggest that more than 100 million people globally are affected by DR, and approximately 28 million have vision-threatening diabetic retinopathy. Among individuals with diabetes, about 22–35% develop DR, and nearly 7% progress to proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), the stage characterized by retinal ischemia and pathological neovascularization. Retinal ischemia in advanced DR can stimulate the production of angiogenic factors that promote neovascularization of the iris (NVI), which may subsequently lead to neovascular glaucoma (NVG). By the time NVI becomes clinically visible on slit-lamp examination, substantial anterior segment vascular involvement is often already present [2]. Previous studies have reported that approximately 65% of eyes with PDR develop NVI, and when NVG occurs in one eye, nearly one-third of patients may subsequently develop NVG in the fellow eye [3, 4].

NVG is a severe complication of retinal ischemic disease and is associated with poor visual prognosis [5]. Early intervention of NVG to prevent disease progression is essential for improving the cure rate. Posterior segment hypoxia is the main driver, with proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) and ischemic central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO) each responsible for approximately one-third of NVG cases [6]. More than 40 conditions can cause NVI and NVG, typically due to extensive ischemia in the retina or localized hypoxia in the anterior segment [7].

Usually, once neovascularization of the iris (NVI) becomes clinically visible, the disease has already progressed to an advanced stage. Iris angiography (IA) provides an important tool for detecting early iris vascular abnormalities, including iris fluorescein angiography (IFA) and iris indocyanine green angiography (IICGA) [8]. IFA clearly demonstrates vascular leakage, whereas IICGA better visualizes iris vascular structures and hemodynamic characteristics. Previous studies have also shown that IA may be useful for monitoring treatment response in eyes with iris neovascularization, particularly before and several days after intravitreal bevacizumab injection. Although IICGA often shows minimal structural vascular changes, IFA typically demonstrates a reduction in fluorescein leakage following treatment [9].

Despite its clinical value, IA assessment in routine practice still relies largely on qualitative or semi-quantitative interpretation. Neovascularization of the iris typically originates at the pupillary margin and extends circumferentially as disease severity increases, suggesting that the circumferential extent of fluorescein leakage may reflect the degree of iris vascular involvement. However, quantitative methods for evaluating circumferential iris involvement remain limited. In this study, we developed a circumferential quantitative analysis of IA using custom-built software to measure leakage time and circumferential leakage range at the pupillary margin [10].

By applying this method to healthy subjects and patients with different stages of diabetic retinopathy, we propose a circumferential quantitative analysis approach for iris angiography. This method was designed to provide a more standardized way to quantify iris vascular leakage in DR.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tianjin Medical University Eye Hospital and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (approval number: 2016KY-04). The study was retrospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400081639), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

From January 2016 to January 2021, a total of 66 healthy subjects and 185 patients with diabetic retinopathy (DR) were consecutively recruited. Healthy subjects had no ocular complaints or abnormal findings on routine ophthalmic examination, with normal intraocular pressure, best-corrected visual acuity ≥ 1.0, and refractive error between + 2.00 and − 2.00 diopters. They also had no history of ocular surgery or known systemic diseases such as diabetes or glaucoma. Healthy participants were further stratified into three age groups (< 39 years, 40–59 years, and 60–79 years).

DR was selected as a representative retinal vascular disease due to its high prevalence in clinical practice. Inclusion criteria for patients with DR were a diagnosis of diabetes mellitus according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria [11]; refractive error between + 2.00 and − 2.00 diopters, and no history of ocular surgery, laser treatment, or anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy. Patients with significant ocular comorbidities, including severe cataract, high myopia, keratopathy, or glaucoma, were excluded. Information on topical medications was recorded, and no patients were receiving medications known to significantly affect iris vascular permeability at the time of imaging.

According to the International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy Disease Severity Scale (2003), the 185 patients with DR were classified into five groups: no diabetic retinopathy, mild non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR), moderate NPDR, severe NPDR, and proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR). In the PDR group, patients with tractional retinal detachment were excluded. Eyes with clinically visible neovascularization of the iris (NVI) were also excluded to focus on earlier stages before overt neovascularization. For patients with vitreous hemorrhage, fundus status was assessed based on intraoperative findings and postoperative angiography.

In addition, patients with severe or uncontrolled systemic diseases, known allergy to contrast agents, or pregnancy were excluded from the study.

Examinations

All subjects underwent comprehensive ophthalmic examinations, including best-corrected visual acuity, intraocular pressure measurement, slit-lamp examination, indirect ophthalmoscopy, and ultra-widefield color fundus photography. In addition, all participants underwent ultra-widefield fluorescein fundus angiography (UWFFA), iris fluorescein angiography (IFA), and iris indocyanine green angiography (IICGA). All examinations were performed under standardized conditions without pharmacological pupil dilation. Images with insufficient contrast or significant motion artifacts were excluded from the analysis.

To enable simultaneous imaging of the iris and fundus angiography, a combined UWFFA and iris angiography (IA) protocol was developed. Briefly, following a negative allergy test, a mixture of 3 mL of 20% sodium fluorescein and 25 mg indocyanine green dissolved in 3 mL of sterile water was rapidly injected into the antecubital vein within 5 s. An asynchronous timer and image monitoring system were initiated simultaneously to record the dynamic development of fluorescein on the iris surface during the first 15 min of angiography, and all images were stored in a digital image processing system.

IA was performed in the early phase within the first minute using the Spectralis HRA angiography system (Heidelberg Engineering, Germany), while UWFFA images were acquired between 1 and 3 min using the Optos Panoramic 200 (Optos plc, UK). Late-phase IA images were further recorded up to 15 min. Fundus findings were graded according to the standard classification of proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) and confirmed by two independent evaluations based on fundus angiography [12]. All measurements were performed by experienced graders following standardized criteria to minimize observer-dependent variability.

Circumference quantitative analysis of IA

Circumferential quantitative analysis of iris angiography (IA) was performed using a custom-built image analysis software to evaluate fluorescein leakage at the pupillary margin. The analysis focused on two parameters: the circumferential extent of leakage, expressed in degrees, and the time to leakage onset.

For each eye, images from iris fluorescein angiography (IFA) and iris indocyanine green angiography (IICGA) were exported for analysis. The center of the pupil was first identified, and a circular reference was established along the pupillary margin. Areas of fluorescein leakage were then identified on IFA images. For each leakage lesion, the starting and ending points along the pupillary margin were determined based on a 360° circumferential reference, measured in a counterclockwise direction. The circumferential extent of each lesion was calculated as the angular difference between these two points.

When multiple leakage areas were present, each lesion was measured separately, and the total circumferential leakage range was calculated as the sum of the individual angular extents. Leakage time was defined as the interval from dye injection to the first clearly visible fluorescein signal extending beyond the pupillary margin on sequential angiographic frames. All measurements were performed according to predefined criteria, and the key steps of the analysis procedure are illustrated in Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. The identification of leakage boundaries was based on dynamic fluorescein changes rather than static appearance, to reduce the influence of iris pigmentation.

To enhance reproducibility, all measurements were performed according to predefined and standardized criteria. The identification of leakage onset, as well as the determination of the start and end points along the pupillary margin, followed consistent operational definitions. All images were evaluated by experienced graders who were masked to clinical information. In cases of uncertainty, measurements were jointly reviewed and consensus was reached to ensure consistency in data acquisition. No fixed fluorescence intensity threshold was applied in the present study.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Differences in leakage time (LT) and circumferential leakage range (LR) among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post hoc multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, we proposed a circumference-based quantitative method for IA to objectively assess fluorescein leakage at the pupillary margin and evaluated its performance across different stages of DR. Our results showed that both LT and LR differed between healthy subjects and patients with DR and across DR severity groups. In particular, LR increased progressively from the no-retinopathy group to NPDR and PDR, whereas a marked shortening of LT was mainly observed in the PDR group. These findings suggest that circumferential quantitative analysis of IA may reflect progressive iris vascular changes associated with increasing DR severity.

The pupillary margin is a unique vascular region of the iris and appears particularly sensitive to pathological angiogenic changes [13]. Iris stromal vessels course radially, whereas vascular changes at the pupillary margin often extend circumferentially. This pattern has been described in previous angiographic studies of anterior segment disease [10]. In DR, VEGF and other angiogenic factors may diffuse from the posterior to the anterior segment and contribute to early iris vascular changes before clinically visible neovascularization develops [14].

IA remains an important tool for evaluating iris vascular permeability and leakage, particularly at the pupillary margin, where subtle vascular changes may not be readily detected by slit-lamp examination or structural imaging modalities [15]. However, as emphasized in recent reviews, conventional interpretation of IA findings has largely relied on qualitative or descriptive assessment, limiting objective comparison between disease stages and longitudinal follow-up [16]. By quantifying the circumferential extent of fluorescein leakage at the pupillary margin, the present study introduces a standardized metric that aligns with the anatomical organization of iris vasculature and enables objective evaluation of the spatial progression of iris vascular involvement in DR.

In current clinical practice, iris angiography assessment is largely qualitative or semi-quantitative, typically relying on descriptive evaluation or sector-based grading of leakage. While these approaches provide useful clinical information, they are inherently limited by subjectivity, coarse categorization and limited reproducibility across observers [17]. In contrast, the present study introduces a circumferential quantitative approach that enables continuous 360° measurement of leakage distribution along the pupillary margin. This method captures the full spatial extent of leakage and transforms it into an objective and measurable parameter, reducing reliance on observer-dependent grading.

Compared with conventional semi-quantitative IA assessment, our approach provides improved measurement resolution and allows for a more precise characterization of disease severity. Rather than representing a completely new imaging modality, this method can be considered an advancement in quantitative analysis of IA, bridging the gap between qualitative assessment and objective measurement.

Recently, AS-OCTA has become increasingly used for evaluating iris vasculature in diabetic eyes [18]. AS-OCTA provides noninvasive visualization of iris vessels and blood flow-related parameters, such as vessel density and has demonstrated early alterations of the iris vascular system even in patients with diabetes without clinically detectable DR [19]. These features make AS-OCTA a valuable tool for screening and longitudinal evaluation of anterior segment vascular changes. However, it should be noted that no direct quantitative comparison between circumferential IA parameters and AS-OCTA metrics was performed in the present study. Therefore, the complementary roles of these modalities remain to be further validated. Direct comparison between IA and AS-OCTA may help clarify how these two techniques can be used together in evaluating anterior segment vascular changes in DR.

From a pathophysiological perspective, these modalities reflect different aspects of disease. AS-OCTA mainly captures vascular structure and flow-related parameters, such as vessel density, which may indirectly reflect the underlying ischemic burden. In contrast, IA directly visualizes fluorescein leakage, representing vascular permeability and blood–aqueous barrier disruption [20]. In contrast, IA provides additional information on vascular leakage and blood–aqueous barrier disruption. This distinction may be particularly relevant in earlier stages, where functional changes in vascular permeability may precede overt structural alterations. In contrast, IA remains uniquely capable of visualizing dynamic leakage patterns and alterations of the blood–aqueous barrier at the pupillary margin, which may be associated with ischemia-related vascular changes. Therefore, while AS-OCTA provides complementary information on iris blood flow, IA retains distinct advantages in evaluating vascular leakage and the spatial extent of pathological iris involvement. These techniques may therefore serve different but complementary roles in clinical assessment. Combining IA with AS-OCTA may help provide a more complete evaluation of anterior segment vascular changes in DR.

The distinct behaviours of LT and LR observed in this study suggest that these two quantitative IA parameters may reflect different stages of iris vascular involvement during DR progression. In patients without retinopathy, LR was comparable to that of healthy subjects, whereas LT appeared earlier, indicating subtle alterations in iris vascular permeability before overt circumferential expansion. As DR progressed to NPDR, a progressive increase in LR became evident, while LT showed no significant differences among NPDR subgroups. This finding may be explained by two factors. First, the sample size within each NPDR subgroup may not have been sufficient to detect subtle differences in leakage time. Second, LT may have limited sensitivity in distinguishing intermediate stages of disease severity, because it may reflect functional vascular changes rather than the overall extent of iris involvement. In this context, leakage time may be better interpreted as a marker of more advanced vascular dysfunction, rather than a parameter that changes gradually across all stages. The marked shortening observed in the proliferative stage may reflect more advanced vascular permeability changes associated with severe DR. These findings suggest that LR may reflect the extent of iris vascular involvement, whereas LT may be more closely related to early changes in vascular permeability.

Notably, a marked shortening of LT and a substantial increase in LR were observed in patients with PDR, highlighting clear differences between non-proliferative and proliferative stages of DR. These quantitative changes suggest that circumferential iris leakage parameters may reflect increasing anterior segment vascular involvement associated with disease severity. Although the present cross-sectional study does not establish prognostic value, quantitative IA assessment may provide complementary information in selected clinical situations, particularly when posterior segment evaluation is limited by media opacity or vitreous haemorrhage.

Neovascular glaucoma represents the advanced stage of diabetic iridopathy and is associated with poor visual prognosis if not detected and treated at an early stage [21]. Clinical experience has shown that therapeutic intervention is more effective when iris neovascularization is immature, whereas established and extensive neovascularization is often associated with irreversible anterior segment damage and limited treatment response [22]. The widespread use of anti-VEGF therapy has substantially improved outcomes in patients with DR-related neovascular complications [23]; however, accurate assessment of disease severity remains essential to avoid both delayed treatment and overtreatment [24].

In routine clinical practice, evaluation of posterior segment ischemia may be challenging in patients with media opacity, vitreous hemorrhage or poor fundus visualization [25]. In such cases, quantitative assessment of iris vascular changes may provide additional information regarding the extent of anterior segment vascular involvement. By enabling objective measurement of circumferential iris leakage at the pupillary margin, the present IA-based method may provide additional information regarding iris vascular involvement in situations where posterior segment evaluation is limited. This approach may be helpful in selected situations where conventional fundus evaluation is limited.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study with a retrospective design, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. The retrospective nature of this study may introduce inherent selection bias. In addition, as a single-center study, the patient population may not fully represent broader clinical settings. Multivariable adjustment for potential systemic and ocular confounding factors, such as age, diabetes duration, metabolic control, hypertension, and lens status, was not performed in the present study. Therefore, the observed associations between leakage parameters and DR severity should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, because this was a cross-sectional study, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of prognostic value or temporal disease progression. Notably, patients with prior treatments such as anti-vascular endothelial growth factor therapy were excluded, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to real-world clinical populations where treated patients are common. These factors should be considered when interpreting the results. IA is an invasive imaging modality requiring fluorescein injection, which may limit its routine use in certain clinical settings. In addition, the present study was conducted in a single Chinese center with a relatively homogeneous population. Differences in iris pigmentation across ethnic groups may affect image contrast and the visibility of fluorescein leakage, particularly in eyes with darker irides, which could influence the generalizability of circumferential IA analysis in broader clinical settings.

In addition, physiological variations in pupillary diameter may influence the appearance of iris vessels and leakage patterns, which should be considered when interpreting the results. In contemporary ophthalmic practice, there is an increasing preference for non-invasive imaging techniques, such as AS-OCTA, particularly for screening and longitudinal monitoring. Therefore, IA may be more appropriately positioned as a complementary tool rather than a first-line modality. Its advantages in directly visualizing vascular leakage and assessing blood–aqueous barrier disruption may be particularly valuable in selected clinical scenarios, such as suspected anterior segment neovascularization or when posterior segment evaluation is limited. In these contexts, the additional information provided by IA may outweigh its invasive nature. Third, although AS-OCTA was discussed as a complementary noninvasive technique, direct head-to-head quantitative comparisons between circumferential IA parameters and AS-OCTA metrics were not performed in the present study. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts and multimodal imaging integration are warranted to further validate the clinical utility of circumference-based quantitative IA analysis. Further validation in larger multiethnic populations will be important to better assess the applicability of this technique across different iris pigmentation phenotypes.

Although a custom-built software and predefined evaluation criteria were used in the present study, the assessment of leakage onset may still be influenced to some extent by grader interpretation. Further validation of reproducibility, including inter-observer and intra-observer consistency, will be needed in future studies. Specifically, inter-observer and intra-observer variability were not formally assessed using statistical metrics such as intraclass correlation coefficients, which may affect the robustness of the measurements. Future studies incorporating standardized validation procedures and reproducibility analysis are warranted to further strengthen the reliability of this method.

In conclusion, circumferential quantitative analysis of IA provides an objective method for assessing iris vascular leakage across different stages of DR. Distinct patterns of LT and LR were associated with differences in iris vascular permeability and spatial involvement across DR severity stages. This approach may offer complementary information for evaluating DR severity, particularly in patients with limited posterior segment visualization. Circumference-based quantitative IA analysis may therefore serve as an adjunctive imaging tool in the assessment of DR-related anterior segment vascular changes.
