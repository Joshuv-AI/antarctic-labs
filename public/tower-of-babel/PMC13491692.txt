Impact of teacher feedback on social working memory and associations with students’ fear of evaluation in adolescents
Authors: Zhe Li, Xiaotian Li, Xianyun Zhu, Xiaoqi Fu, Xiaolei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial working memory (SWM) plays a vital role in cognitive processes and social interactions, and it develops rapidly during adolescence. The ability to learn from external feedback increases considerably during adolescence. Teacher feedback is one of the most important types of external feedback, however, its impact on the SWM, particularly in social emotional working memory, in adolescents and its relationship with fear of evaluation remains to be elucidated.MethodsTo address these questions, this study employed a classic facial-emotional 1-back task to examine emotional working memory and recruited 144 adolescents aged 13-15 years which were randomly assigned to three groups that received positive, negative, or no teacher feedback during the 1-back task. Students’ fear of evaluation was assessed by the Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation Scale and the Fear of Positive Evaluation Scale respectively.ResultsThe results indicated that the accuracy of 1-back task was significantly increased in the negative teacher feedback group. Moreover, behavioral performance was positively correlated with the fear of negative evaluation scores in the group that received negative teacher feedback.ConclusionThe findings from this study suggest that negatively informative teacher feedback during an ongoing demanding emotional working memory task enhances behavioral performance, which could be attributed to the fact that the fear of negative evaluation augments motivation and attention engagement in the task.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04865-z.

Introduction

Working memory (WM), despite its limited capacity, plays a pivotal role in the cognitive system that supports various human intellectual activities and social behaviors [1, 2]. Specifically, cognitive WM (CWM) refers to the ability to hold and manipulate nonsocial information such as locations, shapes, and digits. In contrast, social WM (SWM) involves the maintenance and manipulation of social cognitive information such as emotional states, beliefs, and intentions of oneself or others—a process commonly known as “mentalizing” [3–6].

Adolescence is a key developmental phase linking childhood and early adulthood, marked by a second surge of neurobiological and behavioral changes [7]. The most significant enhancement in CWM efficacy is observed after age seven, with performance in CWM tests increasing steadily throughout childhood and approaching adult levels during adolescence [8–11]. Nonetheless, the development of SWM in children and adolescents has not been adequately examined. Certain studies have observed that compared with adults, the SWM in adolescents is more easily influenced by the emotional content in the system, which could be ascribed to their immature cognitive and affective control [12, 13].

At the neural level, WM relies heavily on the prefrontal and parietal cortices, with activation in these regions increasing with age from 7 to 22 years. However, adults exhibit greater involvement of anterior regions during WM processing than children, suggesting that this transition occurs during adolescence [14–17]. Furthermore, the onset of most psychopathologies, including depression and anxiety, typically occurs during adolescence and may be associated with the aberrant functioning of WM, particularly the SWM, which is essential for both cognitive and emotional regulation [18–21]. Hence, the factors potentially contributing to the development of SWM in adolescence must be investigated.

External feedback plays a crucial role in shaping cognitive skills and adaptive social behaviors, especially during childhood and adolescence [22–24]. A previous study has demonstrated that adolescents exhibit greater learning improvement from feedback than children, likely due to the higher maturity of frontal brain regions [25]. Although numerous studies have investigated the impact of external feedback on cognitive performance, the findings are inconclusive. In an early meta-analysis, [26] observed a deterioration in cognitive task performance after external feedback in one-third of the studies. However, subsequent research by Adam and Vogel focusing on CWM consistently indicated that external feedback combining positive and negative valence enhanced CWM performance in adults [27, 28].

Teacher feedback, one of the most common and critical forms of external feedback for students, plays a pivotal role in facilitating student learning and enhancing their academic performance [29]. According to the feedback model proposed by Hattie and Timperley, feedback can be delivered at four levels. First, the task level involves information about task understanding and performance; second, the process level informs about the processes needed to perform the task; third, the self-regulation level addresses the way students monitor, direct and regulate actions toward the learning goal; fourth, the self-level refers to personal evaluations and affective judgments about the learner. Previous studies have demonstrated that teacher feedback significantly influences students’ language learning and writing development [30, 31], possibly because it boosts their motivation and strengthens teacher–student relationships [32–34]. Nevertheless, whether SWM, the core component supporting cognitive and social activities, could be influenced by teacher feedback, particularly at the task level which informs students about their performance during the task, remains unclear.

Moreover, given that feedback contains evaluative information from others, the effect of teacher feedback on SWM may be related to students’ fear of negative or positive evaluations. The fear of negative evaluation (FNE) is a dispositional trait characterized by apprehension about receiving unfavorable evaluations, viewing evaluative content in a pessimistic manner, and experiencing distress when evaluated negatively by others [35]. On the contrary, the fear of positive evaluation (FPE) is a trait that is prone to causing distress and apprehension about receiving positive assessments from others [36]. FNE and FPE are intricately linked to emotional processing and bias in interpreting evaluations from others. For instance, FNE has been reported to be associated with an initial attentional bias and subsequent avoidance of emotional faces rather than neutral ones [37–39]. Individuals with higher levels of FNE report higher unpleasantness ratings for negative films and experienced greater personal pride after viewing positive films. In contrast, those with higher levels of FPE provided greater unpleasantness ratings for positive films and experienced lower personal pride after viewing such films [40]. In addition to the altered attentional vigilance toward negative or positive social feedback in individuals with high levels of FNE or FPE, neuroimaging studies have documented that the functional connectivity between the orbitofrontal cortex and the parahippocampus, which are involved in emotion regulation and social cognition, is decreased in individuals with high levels of FNE or FPE [41–44]. Therefore, the impact of teacher feedback with positive or negative valence on social WM may be associated with students’ FNE and FPE levels.

Against this background, this study aimed to investigate, using a validated social-emotional n-back paradigm, the effect of positive and negative teacher feedback on SWM performance in adolescents. In this paradigm, a stream of facial expressions was presented and participants were required to identify whether the current stimulus matched the one presented n trials earlier. This paradigm has been utilized to examine both CWM and SWM by employing non-social stimuli (e.g., digits or locations) and social stimuli (e.g., facial expressions or social scenarios), respectively [6, 21, 45]. Given that the impact of external feedback on CWM has been explored [27, 28], in the present study, we solely focus on SWM by using emotional facial expressions as social stimuli. A between-subjects design randomly assigned 144 participants to three groups that received positive, negative, or no feedback from the teacher. Moreover, the Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation (BFNE, [46] scale and the Fear of Positive Evaluation Scale (FPES, [36] were used to measure the levels of FNE and FPE, respectively, in the participants. Previous studies on adults have shown the beneficial effects of feedback incorporating both positive and negative valence on CWM performance [27, 28]. In addition, adolescents have been reported to be easily influenced by the emotional content in SWM [12, 13]. Therefore, we expected that teacher feedback would have a general impact on SWM and would produce an emotion-specific bias in this social–emotional task among adolescents. However, considering the limited prior research, we did not formulate specific hypotheses regarding the potential differences between the impacts of positive and negative feedback and the sad, happy, and neutral bias in SWM. In addition, we hypothesized that the influences of teacher feedback were associated with the levels of FNE and FPE.

Methods

Participants

According to the G*Power 3.1 analysis (effect size = 0.25, α = 0.01, power = 0.8), a minimum of 51 participants were required. Referring to previous studies [27, 28], to enhance the robustness of the findings and to account for potential drop-outs or data exclusions, a total of 144 adolescents aged 13–15 years (mean ± SD: 14.16 ± 0.55 years) were recruited from a middle school and randomly assigned to the following three groups of 48 participants in each group: receiving positive, negative, or no teacher feedback during an emotional SWM task. All participants and their legal guardians signed the informed consent before the experiment. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee at Shandong Normal University (SDNU2022053), and the experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the latest revision of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Procedure

To rule out potential confounding effects of pre-existing group differences in fear of negative and positive evaluations as well as emotional states, the BFNE, FPES, and the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) (Chinese versions, [44, 47, 48] were completed before the experiment. After finishing the questionnaires and taking a short break, the participants were randomly assigned to three groups receiving positive, negative, or no feedback, respectively, during a valid social-emotional n-back task. The participants completed this task in a quiet room with a teacher from the same school sitting in a corner of the room who pretended to check their performance and send feedback using a separate computer. However, in fact, all feedbacks were preprogrammed by the experimenter. To avoid any potential influence of close teacher–student relationships, the teacher in this experiment was the one who the participants knew but were not well acquainted with.

The social-emotional n-back task

In the present study, we employed a facial emotional 1-back task that has been widely used in examining emotional SWM [21, 45]. This task was programmed by E-Prime 2.0 (Psychology Software Tools Inc., Pittsburgh PA, USA) and a total of 24 facial expressions including 8 happy, 8 neutral, and 8 sad emotions from 8 actors (4 male) were utilized as stimuli. This task comprised 12 blocks (four blocks in each emotional condition), with each block containing 30 valid trials featuring faces of the same valence. At the beginning of each block, an instruction was presented for 4000 ms to remind the participants to focus and, at the beginning of each trial, a fixation was presented for 500 ms followed by a facial stimulus (2000 ms), where the participants were required to indicate whether the identity of the current face matched the one presented in the previous trial. The task in the three groups was identical except at the end of each block, the teacher feedbacks were different.

Participants in the positive feedback group received feedbacks like “You did a good job!” and “Your performance was excellent!” while participants in the negative feedback group received like “Your performance was worse than average!” and “Your accuracy was lower than average!”. All the positive and negative feedback was provided at the task level, offering information about the participants’ performance during the task. Positive feedback generally praised their performance, while negative feedback indicated that their current performance was below average. In the no-feedback group, the sentence “Please continue.” was presented. Statistical analysis

The reliability of the accuracy in the 1-back task was measured using split-half reliability, which has been commonly used in cognitive tasks [49, 50]. The split-half reliability in the present study was calculated using the following steps: 1) the trials in each block were split into two halves. 2) The average accuracy was calculated for each half. 3) The correlation coefficient calculated by Pearson’s correlation across subjects was employed as the split-half reliability. The reliability of the accuracy in this study was 0.95.

Demographic characteristics including sex, age, and questionnaire scores, and behavioral performance, including accuracy and reaction time (RT), were analyzed using SPSS 24.0. The Chi-square test was conducted to examine sex differences among groups, and the multivariate analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to examine group differences in age and questionnaire scores. A 3 (groups: positive-, negative-, no-feedback group) × 3 (emotional faces: happy, neutral, sad) repeated mixed ANOVA was conducted on accuracy and reaction time separately to examine the impact of teacher feedback on WM, and the post-hoc Bonferroni-corrected comparisons were used to further disentangle the significant main and interaction effects. The Greenhouse–Geisser corrected value was reported if Mauchly’s test of Sphericity was significant. The associations between behavioral data and questionnaire scores were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation analysis, while the group differences of correlations were analyzed using Fisher’s Z.

Associations between behavioral accuracy and the BFNE/FPES scores

The Pearson correlation analysis indicated a significant association between behavioral accuracy and the BFNE scores in the negative-feedback group (r = 0.34, p = 0.03), whereas the associations were not significant in the positive-feedback group (r = − 0.03, p = 0.85) and the no-feedback group (r = − 0.51, p = 0.76). There were no significant associations between behavioral accuracy and the FPES scores. Discussion

This study investigated the impact of external teacher feedback with negative and positive valence on SWM, particularly in the emotional subdomain, in adolescents using the classic n-back paradigm. The results implied that negative feedback significantly increased behavioral accuracy in the emotional facial 1-back task, suggesting an overall enhancement of SWM by negative teacher feedback. Specifically, the teacher feedback effects were stronger in the first, middle, and last blocks than in the other blocks. Moreover, the effect of negative teacher feedback on behavioral accuracy was positively associated with participants’ FNE levels.

In partial agreement with previous studies demonstrating that external feedback integrating both negative and positive information improves visual WM performance [27, 28], this study found that negative teacher feedback significantly enhances general WM performance regardless of the valence of the stimuli. Compared with the cognitive visual WM used in the previous study, this research employed a social–emotional n-back task to assess adolescents’ ability to update emotional contents in the WM system, which is more important in emotional regulation, interpersonal interaction, and social adaptation [51, 52]. Contrary to our hypothesis, no specific emotional bias was perceived in SWM after receiving different feedback. This might be due to two reasons. First, although the stimuli were emotional faces, they were task-irrelevant because participants were required to respond to face identity, not the emotion [6], which may have reduced emotional involvement. Additionally, although clinical studies have demonstrated that patients with anxiety or depression show mood-congruent biases in SWM and that they may exhibit difficulties in updating negative information in SWM, healthy adolescents did not display any emotional bias [53] [54] [55]. Thus, the absence of emotional bias may also reflect that the teacher feedback was not sufficiently intense to induce a strong emotional state capable of modulating emotion-specific processing. Second, feedback plays an important role in reinforcement learning and is a key factor driving behavioral adaptation and strategy optimization [56, 57]. Therefore, in the present study participants may have adjusted their behaviors according to the feedback, regardless of its valence, to achieve better performance.

However, compared with adolescents who did not receive any teacher feedback, the overall behavioral performance was better in those who received negative feedback. This observation can be attributed to two reasons. First, the negative feedback provided information regarding the discrepancy between participants’ behavioral accuracy and the average accuracy. This comparison may have directly improved the participants’ task performance, as previous studies have shown that upward comparison can enhance performance, especially in simple tasks [58]. The 1-back task employed in the present study was a relatively low-load cognitive task, therefore, participants may be more easily influenced by the peer comparison conveyed through the teacher feedback. Second, this informative feedback is effective in enhancing cognitive performance by augmenting participants’ motivation [59]. The motivation theory states that feedback may stimulate students’ intrinsic motivation [60]. Negative feedback may also undermine self-esteem or self-confidence in some cases. However, when students believe that they can improve their performance via effort, negative feedback can act as an external motivator, prompting them to be more diligent and, in turn, improve their SWM [60]. Furthermore, the self-determination theory suggests that moderate challenge and external stress may enrich students’ intrinsic motivation, especially when they believe that they have control over the outcome [61]. Therefore, in this study, after receiving negative feedback at the end of each block, participants’ motivation to perform better in the next block during the task might have increased.

Moreover, compared with positive feedback, negative feedback may serve as an alert to participants that their current level of attention is insufficient for the task and that more attentional resources are required to boost their behavioral performance [27]. Based on the feedback intervention theory, negative feedback might have increased students’ focus on the present task rather than being interrupted by task-irrelevant information [26]. Thus, the better performance after receiving negative feedback might have resulted from increased attentional allocation to the task. Furthermore, the increased accuracy in the negative feedback group was not accompanied by a statistically significant deceleration of reaction times. While this pattern does not completely rule out a speed-accuracy trade-off, it suggests that the performance improvement was not simply driven by a shift towards slower, more cautious responding. Future studies employing more direct measures (e.g., drift–diffusion modeling) could further clarify the underlying cognitive processes.

In addition, a significant positive association was evident between behavioral accuracy and the BFNE scores in the negative-feedback group, suggesting that participants with higher levels of FNE performed better when they received information indicating that their accuracy was lower than the average. Our results are partially aligned with those of a previous study reporting that in social settings, participants with higher FNE exhibit better learning abilities than those with lower FNE [43]. However, we only found a positive association between FNE and task performance rather than a significant feedback group × FNE trait interaction in our exploratory analysis (see Supplementary Information). Consistent with evidence that individuals with high levels of FNE exhibit more attentional vigilance to negative than positive social feedback [44], negative feedback in our study might have reduced the likelihood of inducing severe negative emotions (e.g., anxiety) while increasing attentional resources, thereby improving performance. Notably, in the positive-feedback group, although there was no significant association between behavioral performance and BFNE, the accuracy tended to increase from block 4 to block 9, suggesting a potential learning effect, although it decreased in the last three blocks. However, it is critical to note that the Fisher’s Z tests revealed no significant differences between the correlation in the negative-feedback group and those in the positive- or no-feedback groups. Therefore, despite the descriptive pattern observed within the negative-feedback group, we cannot conclude that FNE is a strong predictor of task performance in the negative-feedback condition than in the other conditions. The relationship among FNE, teacher feedback, and cognitive performance, whether examined as simple associations or as potential moderation, requires cautious interpretation. Future studies with a larger sample size and formal moderation analyses are necessary to further investigate their relationships.

The effect of negative feedback on WM task performance in the present study may be culture-specific. Previous studies have shown that adolescents from collectivistic and individualistic countries reported different levels of self-efficacy in response to feedback [62]. Chinese students grow up in a collectivistic cultural that emphasizes collective honor. Therefore, negative feedback that reminds them their performance is below average may make them more likely to perform better to achieve the collective goal.

This study has several limitations. First, the positive feedback used in the study comprised general positive comments about the participants’ performance. In contrast, negative feedback involved comparing their performance with the average. It is plausible that such social comparison could potentially lead to better performance by eliciting stronger emotional responses in the negative feedback group. The ineffectiveness of positive feedback might have been due to the absence of social comparison. Therefore, social comparison should be balanced in future studies. Second, the participants’ motivation and emotional state were not measured after they received teacher feedback. Hence, the corresponding interpretations may lack sufficient evidence. Third, although the feedback was provided by an unfamiliar teacher to avoid the potential effect of positive or negative teacher–student relationships, such relationships should also have been measured as they could influence the teacher’s feedback [34]. The effect of teacher feedback on WM may change if the feedback is provided by a teacher with more positive or negative relationships with the students. Fourth, the sample size in this study was insufficient to detect sex differences in the response to teacher feedback. Fifth, prior to the experimental manipulation, we did not measure the working memory capacity across groups, and such differences might also contribute to SWM performance. Sixth, although the emotional facial n-back task has been utilized to reflect SWM in previous studies [6, 45], it fails to represent the entire SWM system which involves complex constructs. In the future, studies incorporating diverse social materials, such as auditory or verbal stimuli, are required to validate the findings of this study. Finally, the feedback was only delivered at the task level. In the future, effects of teacher feedback at process level, self-regulation level, and self-level should be considered.

In conclusion, this study identified that SWM was significantly enhanced by negative teacher feedback in adolescents. In addition, the impact of teacher feedback on SWM was positively associated with students’ FNE.
