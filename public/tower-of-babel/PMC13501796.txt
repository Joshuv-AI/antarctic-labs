Pilot evaluation of a liquid biopsy test for longitudinal monitoring of disease status in dogs undergoing treatment for multicentric lymphoma
Authors: Amanda Nascimento, Sarah Peterson, Dana Connell, Zachary Helm, Caroline Leeth, Rachel Venable
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCanine lymphoma is a common malignancy with an annual incidence rate of 160 cases per 100,000 dogs. The primary therapeutic protocol in clinical use is treatment with cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (CHOP); however, relapses remain frequent, and early detection of recurrence is challenging. IDEXX Cancer Dx™ testing is a commercially available, blood-based test developed for the detection of canine lymphoma and may also have utility as a minimally invasive tool for longitudinal monitoring during treatment. Ten client-owned dogs with cytologic confirmation of lymphoma, immunophenotyping by flow cytometry and/or genotypic clonality testing by PCR for antigen receptor rearrangement (PARR), and an initial response to CHOP therapy were enrolled in this prospective longitudinal study. Whole blood was collected serially during chemotherapy and analyzed using Cancer Dx testing, with results reported as positive or negative. Clinical response was evaluated using the Veterinary Cooperative Oncology Group (VCOG) response evaluation criteria for peripheral nodal lymphoma in dogs.ResultsCancer Dx test results showed concordance with clinical disease status (Somers’ D = 0.70, 95% CI [0.59, 0.82]; Kendall’s τ-b = 0.67, 95% CI [0.56, 0.78]). Among the ten cases, test results for eight (80%) dogs became negative, corresponding with clinical remission. Seven of ten (70%) dogs maintained negative test results during complete remission; five of ten (50%) dogs became positive at clinical relapse. Discordance between Cancer Dx testing results and clinical status at the time of initial clinical remission occurred in 2 out of 10 (20%) of the enrolled dogs. Two dogs (Dogs D and I) remained positive despite achieving clinical remission during the initial weeks of CHOP therapy, suggesting delayed or incomplete biomarker conversion.ConclusionSerial Cancer Dx testing results were concordant with VCOG Response Evaluation Criteria-defined clinical status. While these results support further investigation of Cancer Dx testing as a monitoring tool during chemotherapy for lymphoma, the current performance of the assay is insufficient for reliable, stand-alone monitoring without further refinement.Keywords: Biomarker, Chemotherapy, Canine lymphoma, Remission, Liquid biopsy

Background

Canine lymphoma is a common hematopoietic malignancy arising from lymphocytes and most commonly affects lymphoid organs such as lymph nodes, spleen, and bone marrow, although extra-nodal presentations can occur [1]. The multicentric form of the disease is the most prevalent subtype, with an estimated incidence of 13–114 cases per 100,000 dogs annually, peaking in dogs aged 6–9 years [2]. In addition to clinical heterogeneity, lymphoma comprises multiple immunophenotypic and genetic subtypes, further contributing to disease complexity [3]. Breeds with increased risk include Boxers, Bullmastiffs, and Basset hounds; whereas, intact females may have reduced risk [2, 4]. Prognosis is influenced by factors such as immunophenotype, stage, and breed [5, 6].

The current standard of care for multicentric lymphoma is multi-agent chemotherapy, most often the combination of cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone referred to as the CHOP protocol [7, 8]. While the CHOP protocol is associated with high remission rates, relapse remains common, and early detection of recurrence remains challenging. Following relapse, dogs may receive rescue chemotherapy protocols or alternative antineoplastic therapies, which are associated with more variable and often shorter-lived responses [9]. Conventional monitoring tools such as subjective lymph node palpation or measurement, cytology, histopathology, flow cytometry, and polymerase chain reaction for antigen receptor rearrangement (PARR) are highly informative but some are invasive and logistically challenging for repeated use, or are limited in detecting subclinical recurrence [10, 11]. Identifying additional clinical response monitoring tools could assist veterinarians and owners in making better informed decisions regarding continuation of treatment and quality of life.

In human oncology, serial blood-based biomarker testing has been studied as a means to monitor minimal residual disease (MRD) and has been shown to predict relapse before overt clinical signs become apparent [12, 13]. Comparable approaches in veterinary oncology could support earlier intervention before clinically overt relapse and contribute to more individualized treatment strategies.

IDEXX Cancer Dx™ testing (IDEXX Laboratories, Inc., Westbrook, ME, USA) is a commercially available, blood-based test for canine lymphoma internally validated to have 79% sensitivity and ˃99% specificity in treatment-naïve dogs diagnosed with lymphoma based on cytology, histopathology, and phenotyping reviewed by board-certified oncologists and pathologists.The testing also has the secondary potential to differentiate between B- and T-cell subtypes [14]. Cancer Dx testing was developed for the detection of canine lymphoma; however, this study evaluated its potential use for longitudinal monitoring during treatment. Although the test provides a binary outcome (positive or negative), sequential testing may reveal meaningful patterns that inform both clinical status and therapeutic response. The objective of this pilot study was to determine whether serial Cancer Dx testing results were concordant with clinical remission, stable disease, or progressive disease in dogs receiving chemotherapy.

Methods

Study design

The aim of the study was to longitudinally assess dogs with lymphoma undergoing chemotherapy using Cancer Dx testing. Enrollment occurred between September 2024 and July 2025 at Integrative Vet Oncology in Phoenix, AZ, USA through Pet Cancer Care Consulting (PCCC). Dogs were eligible for inclusion if (1) they were at least 2 years of age and weighed ≥ 2.5 kg, (2) they had a cytologic confirmation of lymphoma by a board-certified pathologist, (3) they underwent immunophenotyping by flow cytometry and/or genotypic clonality testing using PARR, and (4) they demonstrated an initial positive clinical response to CHOP chemotherapy, defined as either partial or complete remission (≥ 30% reduction in measurable tumor burden) within the first 6 weeks of treatment, with owner commitment to continue with all scheduled treatments and follow-ups. Dogs were permitted to remain on their preferred diet and continue any supplements recommended by their veterinarian. Exclusion criteria included 1) life-limiting comorbidities, 2)expected survival of less than three weeks, or 3) treatment with prednisone or chemotherapy prior to blood collection and study enrollment. Owners provided written consent prior to study enrollment and dogs were retrospectively enrolled based on initial response to chemotherapy. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the IDEXX Animal Welfare Committee.

Study population

Ten client-owned dogs met all inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study. The cohort included four males and six females with a median age of 6.5 years (range 2–14 years). Staging of disease was performed at the time of assessment for enrollment and included a physical examination, complete blood count (CBC), chemistry panel, and flow cytometry or PARR. All enrolled dogs were categorized as Stage III based on the information available at the time of staging. Breeds, ages, and lymphoma subtypes are summarized in Table 1.

Sample collection and testing

Blood was collected before chemotherapy initiation at the first visit and before treatment administration at subsequent visits in accordance with the standard CHOP-25-week protocol. At each sampling event, 1 mL of whole blood was placed into an EDTA tube and 4 mL into a serum separator tube. Serum samples were centrifuged within 30 min of collection at 2000 × g for 10 min, aliquoted (0.5 mL per microtube), and stored at -20 °C. EDTA whole blood was stored unprocessed at -20 °C. All samples were shipped on dry ice to IDEXX Laboratories (Westbrook, ME, USA) for Cancer Dx testing according to the manufacturer’s standard laboratory procedures. Laboratory personnel were blinded to the dogs’ clinical status via coded identification numbers. Results were reported as a binary outcome (positive or negative). The specific biomarker components and analytical methodology of the assay are proprietary and were not evaluated in this study.

Clinical monitoring during chemotherapy treatment

All dogs were initially treated with a standardized 25-week CHOP regimen consisting of intravenous vincristine administered every other week during induction; oral cyclophosphamide or intravenous doxorubicin alternating every other week with vincristine; and oral prednisone tapered over 4 weeks [15, 16]. Doses were calculated by body surface area and adjusted per hematologic and clinical criteria except for doxorubicin which was dosed by mg/kg in dogs weighing less than 15 kg. Dogs were not required to continue the CHOP protocol to remain enrolled in the study if the attending veterinarian determined it would be detrimental to their prognosis to continue. In cases of disease progression, relapse, or intolerance to therapy, dogs could be transitioned to rescue chemotherapy protocols at the discretion of the attending veterinarian. Rescue therapies were not standardized, reflecting real-world clinical decision-making; details on rescue treatment initiation and timing are summarized in Table 2. Study follow-up continued regardless of treatment changes, including transition to rescue therapy, and serial sampling was performed in accordance with chemotherapy protocols when the dog was seen at the oncology clinic.

The study endpoint was defined as withdrawal from the study, which occurred primarily because of death or owner decision to discontinue treatment.

At each visit, peripheral lymph node measurements were performed using calipers to determine treatment response, and a CBC, temperature, pulse, respiration, body weight, and body condition score (BCS) were recorded to support clinical assessment and treatment decisions for that visit. Adverse events were documented.

Treatment response was classified by the attending veterinarian according to the Veterinary Cooperative Oncology Group Response Evaluation Criteria [10, 11]. Complete remission (CR) was defined as the disappearance of all target lesions. A partial response (PR) was defined as at least a 30% decrease in the sum of the longest diameters (LD) of target lesions compared with first visit. Progressive disease (PD) was defined as at least a 20% increase in the sum of the LD of target lesions relative to the smallest value recorded since treatment initiation, or the appearance of one or more new lesions. Stable disease (SD) was assigned when neither sufficient shrinkage to qualify for PR nor sufficient increase to qualify for PD was observed [10].

Statistical analysis

The relationship between clinical disease (disease in remission, active disease) and Cancer Dx result (negative, positive) was assessed using the Cochran-Armitage test for trend, with Somers’ D and Kendall’s tau-b reported as measures of association strength. The progression of clinical disease categories was analyzed in the order of clinical remission, partial remission, stable disease, and progressive disease. Cancer Dx testing results were inputted as positive or negative.

Changes in body weight from the start of the study were analyzed by mixed-model ANOVA.

One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used to compare the complete blood counts from the individual dogs with the hematologic reference values. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism® software (version 10.2.2; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and JMP® Pro software (version 18; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Concordance between Cancer Dx testing results and clinical status

Ten dogs met the inclusion criteria and were evaluated to determine whether serial Cancer Dx testing results were concordant with clinical disease status during chemotherapy. Immunophenotyping or genotypic testing identified B-cell lymphoma in 9 dogs (90%) and T-cell lymphoma in 1 dog (10%) (Table 1). All dogs were positive on Cancer Dx testing at Week 1. By Week 5, 8 of 10 dogs (Dogs A, B, C, E, F, G, H, and J) were in complete remission and had corresponding negative Cancer Dx testing results (Table 3). Dogs D and I remained positive despite clinical remission at this time point, indicating delayed biomarker conversion. Dogs A, C, E, and H completed the CHOP protocol and remained negative on Cancer Dx testing throughout the duration of therapy. Not all dogs had a sample available at Week 25, as the timing of the final treatment visit varied slightly among dogs due to scheduling or treatment delays, including episodes of chemotherapy-associated neutropenia. Dog A developed progressive disease at Week 26, shortly after completing CHOP therapy. Dog F remained in clinical remission during CHOP but became positive on Cancer Dx testing at Week 15 despite continued clinical remission. Dog B moved in and out of clinical remission during the course of the study with Cancer Dx testing results that aligned with clinical status, transitioning from negative to positive when the dog moved out of remission. Dog G later developed progressive disease despite persistently negative Cancer Dx testing results, whereas Dogs D and I that were initially discordant demonstrated persistent Cancer Dx positivity despite periods of clinical remission; however, both dogs eventually became negative at later time points. Cancer Dx testing results are reported clinically as a binary positive/negative result. To illustrate temporal changes in assay measurements relative to clinical disease status, one internal assay signal is shown in Fig. 1. This signal demonstrates temporal variation as dogs enter and exit remission during the course of their enrollment in the study.

A statistical analysis assessing the relationship between clinical disease status and Cancer Dx testing results showed a significant monotonic trend, with the proportion of Cancer Dx positive cases increasing across ascending categories of clinically assessed condition (Z = 9.56, p < 0.0001). The association was of substantial magnitude (Somers’ D = 0.70, 95% CI [0.59, 0.82]; Kendall’s τ-b = 0.67, 95% CI [0.56, 0.78]), indicating strong concordance between Cancer Dx testing results and clinical status.

Clinical and physiologic parameters

Clinical parameters, including body weight, temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, and hematologic testing results, were monitored longitudinally throughout chemotherapy and generally remained within reference ranges. Body weight and BCS remained stable across visits (p = 0.067), with all dogs maintaining within ± 5% of initial visit and maintaining a BCS between 4/9 and 5/9 (data not shown).

Discussion

This prospective pilot study evaluated the potential utility of Cancer Dx testing for longitudinal monitoring of disease status in dogs with multicentric lymphoma receiving CHOP chemotherapy, with follow-up extended through treatment modification and rescue therapy when clinically indicated. Because Cancer Dx is used in clinical practice, the goal of this study was to assess its longitudinal performance during treatment rather than its analytical development or diagnostic validation. Accordingly, the objective of this work was not to describe the test’s analytical composition, biological mechanism, or technical development, but rather to evaluate its performance as a longitudinal monitoring tool during active treatment.

In most dogs, serial Cancer Dx results corresponded with clinical disease status, becoming negative during complete remission and returning to positive in several dogs at relapse or disease progression. An early finding of interest was the temporal correspondence between negative test results and clinical remission (Table 3; Fig. 1). This pattern of early biomarker conversion aligns with observations from prior veterinary studies evaluating MRD using flow cytometry [17, 18] and PARR [6], in which early clearance was associated with improved progression-free survival. Although this pilot study was not powered to assess survival outcomes, the temporal association between Week 5 test negativity and complete remission supports further evaluation of Cancer Dx testing as a marker of disease status during treatment. Statistical assessment showed significant correlation between Cancer Dx testing results and clinical disease status over the course of the study, further supporting the test’s potential use as a monitoring tool.

In this dataset, vincristine IV was most commonly administered before the first observed clinical remission (Week 1), whereas doxorubicin IV or cyclophosphamide ± furosemide frequently preceded the first negative Cancer Dx testing result (Week 4, Table 3). These observations suggest that reductions in measurable lymphadenopathy may occur before biomarker signals fall below the detection threshold, indicating a possible delay between clinical remission and biomarker conversion. Such temporal separation, where visible tumor reduction precedes biomarker clearance, may reflect the persistence of residual disease or delayed clearance of circulating tumor biomarkers following cytotoxic therapy. Similar kinetics have been reported in prior MRD studies in canine lymphoma, highlighting the dynamic relationship between tumor burden, treatment response, and circulating biomarker detection [6, 17, 19, 20]. Compared with current MRD tools in veterinary oncology such as flow cytometry and PARR, Cancer Dx testing requires only a routine blood draw for whole blood and serum, without the need for fresh tissue or specialized processing [6, 21]. This ease of collection could allow more frequent monitoring in practice, facilitate earlier identification of relapse, and support adaptive treatment strategies.

After initial treatment with CHOP protocol, some dogs transitioned to alternative chemotherapy agents, such as lomustine (CCNU), following relapse or CHOP failure. These treatment changes reflect real-world clinical practice and may have influenced both testing intervals and clinical outcomes after CHOP cessation. Nevertheless, Cancer Dx testing results continued to show general concordance with clinical status in several dogs even after treatment modification.

Discordance between Cancer Dx testing and clinical status at the time of initial clinical remission occurred in 20% of dogs in this study. Specifically, Dogs D and I remained positive despite achieving clinical remission, suggesting delayed biomarker conversion relative to clinical tumor regression. Additionally, Dog F developed a positive Cancer Dx testing result during clinical remission, representing discordance between biomarker detection and clinical assessment. This study highlights that, as with other MRD modalities, biomarker results should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical assessment. Similar limitations are described in veterinary MRD studies, especially where flow cytometry may fail to detect minimal disease that is nonetheless present, highlighting the importance of interpreting biomarker results alongside clinical assessment [17, 22].

The physical examination and clinical monitoring findings suggested that CHOP chemotherapy was well tolerated, with adverse events limited to VCOG grade 1–2 and self-limiting in nature. These findings suggest that the observed longitudinal Cancer Dx testing patterns were unlikely to have been substantially confounded by physiologic instability, infection, or systemic toxicity [23].

The main limitations of this pilot study include the small number of enrolled dogs (n = 10) and the predominance of the B-cell phenotype (9/10 dogs). However, each dog was followed longitudinally with repeated blood collections across multiple clinical visits during treatment and follow-up, allowing evaluation of temporal changes in Cancer Dx testing results relative to clinical disease status. The majority of enrolled dogs completed the full CHOP protocol, fulfilling the study’s primary objective of longitudinal monitoring during active chemotherapy. However, the limited sample size restricted statistical power and precluded meaningful subtype-specific or survival analyses. In addition, sampling occurred only when the pet presented to the clinic for treatment or evaluation and not on a fixed, weekly schedule and the binary (positive/negative) nature of test reporting limited evaluation of potential lead-time advantage between biomarker conversion and clinical remission. Future work should include larger, multicenter cohorts with balanced representation of B- and T-cell lymphoma and integration of survival endpoints. Increasing sampling frequency, particularly around expected remission and relapse points, may better define the test’s sensitivity for detecting biomarker conversion ahead of clinically detectable disease progression.

Conclusion

In summary, longitudinal Cancer Dx testing results were statistically significantly concordant with clinical disease status in dogs with multicentric lymphoma receiving CHOP chemotherapy in this study. While occasional discordances were identified and treatment changes after CHOP may influence post-relapse testing patterns, the minimally invasive nature of the test and its compatibility with routine blood collection may serve as a practical adjunct to conventional clinical monitoring. The observed temporal relationship between clinical response and biomarker trends further suggests that the test may reflect the dynamic changes in disease status during treatment, consistent with prior veterinary studies evaluating MRD using flow cytometry or PARR. These findings support further investigation of Cancer Dx testing as a tool for longitudinal monitoring during treatment of canine lymphoma.
