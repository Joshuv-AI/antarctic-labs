Reflectance confocal microscopy detects distinct features of field damage in chronically UV-irradiated SKH1 mouse skin
Authors: Mehdi Boostani, Szabolcs Bozsányi, Sean P Murphy, Erin C Tracy, Ruby Acquah, John R Durkin, Norbert Kiss, Sandra Schuh, Deborah Winkler, Florencia Vera Morandini, Javiera Pérez-Anker, Carmen Cantisani, Li Yan, Noah Musolff, Babar K Rao, Wendy J Huss, Manu Jain, Gyorgy Paragh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUVR drives keratinocyte clonal expansion and cutaneous field damage, yet the assessment of early UV-induced field damage remains subjective and lacks an objective, non-invasive method. Reflectance confocal microscopy provides near-histologic imaging of the epidermis and may enable the detection of subclinical UV-induced changes before overt lesion development. Using SKH1 hairless mice, reflectance confocal microscopy images were analyzed during an early post-UV period at weeks 12 and 14 and a late post-UV period at weeks 16 and 18, corresponding to 6–8 and 2–4 weeks before the first visible tumor emergence, respectively. Five epidermal features significantly differentiated irradiated from control skin. Among the 5 features, atypical honeycombing and keratinocyte pleomorphism showed the strongest discriminatory value. In the early post-UV period, the expert-derived mean positivity for atypical honeycombing was 66.7–100% in irradiated skin versus 0–46.7% in control skin, while that for keratinocyte pleomorphism was 33.3–60% versus 0–13.3%, respectively. In the late post-UV period, atypical honeycombing was 46.7–100% in irradiated skin versus 0–33.3% in control skin, and keratinocyte pleomorphism was 33.3–80% versus 0–33.3%, respectively. Expert readers classified irradiated versus non-irradiated cases with 96.7% sensitivity and 90% specificity early and with 90% sensitivity and 90% specificity late. These findings support reflectance confocal microscopy as a non-invasive tool for detecting subclinical UV-related field damage.Keywords: Atypia, Field damage photodamage, Reflectance confocal microscopy, UV irradiation

Introduction

UVR is a major environmental carcinogen responsible for initiating and promoting cutaneous neoplasia, (Mancebo and Wang, 2014; Oak et al, 2017) particularly squamous cell carcinoma and actinic keratosis (AK) (Schmitt et al, 2018, 2011) In areas of chronic UV exposure, mutated clones of keratinocytes accumulate and slowly progress towards carcinogenesis (Jonason et al, 1996; Wei et al, 2024, 2021) These biologically altered zones of initially clinically unremarkable skin are recognized as sites of “field damage.” Assessment of this field is crucial for effective surveillance, risk stratification, and treatment planning in dermatologic oncology. (Berman and Cockerell, 2013; Kandolf et al, 2024)

Currently, aside from time-consuming and costly sequencing-based assessment of early clonal variant, which are only used in research, no objective clinical method exists to determine the burden of early cutaneous field damage. (Jonason et al, 1996; Wei et al, 2024, 2021) Thus, in lieu of an objective method, the current assessment of early UV-induced field damage relies almost exclusively on historical sun-damage information and subjective physical evaluation of the skin. Thus, the currently used clinical method provides no exact measure of field damage until non-melanoma skin cancer and AKs arise. Therefore, there is a critical need for a reproducible, image-based, and non-invasive diagnostic approach to detect field damage and drive early clinical intervention.

In recent years, non-invasive medical imaging techniques have gained increasing popularity in dermatologic research and clinical practice, offering novel ways to visualize skin architecture beyond traditional examination. (Boostani et al, 2025a, 2025b, 2025c, 2025d; Bozsányi et al, 2023; Kiss and Boostani, 2025; Varga et al, 2023) For example, hyperspectral imaging has been used to identify clinically visible and subclinical lesions consistent with field change, (Neittaanmäki-Perttu et al, 2013) and optical coherence tomography (OCT)-based studies have demonstrated the ability to detect and monitor AKs and surrounding field alterations, (Zhou et al, 2021) including subclinical disease and treatment response. More recently, line-field confocal OCT has also been investigated for in vivo assessment of keratinocyte atypia in 3 dimensions. (Fischman et al, 2022) Although these approaches are promising for field assessment and longitudinal monitoring, their ability to resolve subtle epidermal cytologic and architectural abnormalities at the cellular level may be limited compared with reflectance confocal microscopy (RCM).

RCM is a promising modality for in vivo imaging of skin morphology at near-histologic resolution. (Pellacani et al, 2015; Rajadhyaksha et al, 1995) RCM has been widely validated for identifying basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, AK, and melanoma, with several morphological features established for diagnosis. (Navarrete-Dechent et al, 2025; Pellacani et al, 2007). However, RCM’s application for detecting early field changes, before visible tumor development, remains largely unexplored.

To fill this gap, we used the SKH1 hairless mouse (SKH1) model, a well-established system for studying UV carcinogenesis. (Berton et al, 1996; Boostani et al, 2026; Melnikova et al, 2005) By delivering controlled solar-simulated UV-light exposure and performing serial RCM imaging, we created a unique dataset in which irradiated and non-irradiated skin could be directly compared under identical conditions. This study identified and defined reproducible morphological features of UV-induced field damage using RCM. We next validated which features discriminate irradiated from non-irradiated skin and evaluated the detectability of these features by both expert and novice RCM readers, as well as the ability of these features to support accurate classification of UV exposure status.

Results

Expert-based feature likelihood scoring

Combining all 5 expert-identified features—scaling, hyperkeratosis, atypical honeycombing, architectural disarray, and keratinocyte pleomorphism—yielded clear separation between irradiated and non-irradiated skin. The cumulative feature score ranged from 52% to 76% among irradiated cases, whereas control cases ranged from 0% to 22.7% (P < .001) (Figure 1). Threshold-based feature classification and ORs analysis

In the early post-UV period, architectural disarray, atypical honeycombing, hyperkeratosis, and keratinocyte pleomorphism showed the strongest associations with UV-irradiated skin, each corresponding to a 441-fold increase in the odds of UV-induced damage when present (BH-adjusted P < .0001). Scaling was also significantly associated with UV exposure, corresponding to a 71.4-fold increase in the odds of UV-induced damage when present (BH-adjusted P < .001) (Table 1).

Reader evaluation performance

In the early post-UV period, blinded expert readers demonstrated strong diagnostic performance, with a mean sensitivity of 96.7% (95% confidence interval [CI] = 82.8–99.9%) and a mean specificity of 90% (95% CI = 73.5–97.9%) for classifying whether a case was irradiated versus non-irradiated using the same 5-stack case evaluation. Interobserver agreement among experts was substantial, with a Fleiss’ kappa (κ) of 0.73. In contrast, novice readers showed lower overall performance in this early time period, with a mean sensitivity of 76.7% (95% CI = 57.7–90.1%) and a mean specificity of 40% (95% CI = 22.7–59.4%). Interobserver agreement among novice readers was poor, with a Fleiss’ κ of −0.08. Detailed performance metrics for individual readers in both the expert and novice groups are provided in Table 3.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that RCM can detect morphological changes defined in clinically unremarkable chronically UV-exposed skin areas. The ability to visualize architectural and cytological alterations, such as scaling, cellular disarray, and keratinocyte atypia, as hallmarks of UV-induced field damage in clinically normal-appearing skin was described. The findings suggest that RCM may serve as a valuable tool for detecting the clinically invisible burden of UV-induced field damage.

In the first phase of the study, 18 previously established RCM features linked to squamous neoplasia were evaluated to determine their relevance in detecting UV-induced field damage. Of these, 5 features—scaling, hyperkeratosis, atypical honeycombing, architectural disarray, and keratinocyte pleomorphism—were significantly more prevalent in chronically UV-irradiated skin. These 5 features served as the basis for the blinded reader evaluation in the second phase. When tested further, atypical honeycombing and keratinocyte pleomorphism emerged as the most distinctive indicators of UV-induced field damage, enabling effective separation between irradiated and control cases.

Other non-invasive imaging approaches have also shown promise for assessing UV-induced field damage. In a small clinical study, hyperspectral imaging was shown to detect all clinically visible AKs and numerous subclinical lesions concordant with histopathology and showed promise in guiding field-directed therapy. (Neittaanmäki-Perttu et al, 2013) OCT could also identify not only clinical AKs but also 73% of histopathology-confirmed subclinical AKs in perilesional skin and effectively monitor response to field-directed therapy with ingenol mebutate. (Markowitz et al, 2017, 2016) Recently, the potential utility of line-field confocal OCT has also been shown for assessing field disease. (Fischman et al, 2022; Lenoir et al, 2021) hyperspectral imaging, OCT, and line-field confocal OCT have shown promise for detecting field disease and monitoring treatment response; however, they differ in the type of information they provide, and they have been previously shown to aid in assessing the burden of UV-induced field disease in a later phase of field damage. RCM provides cellular-level en face imaging with high transverse (lateral) resolution in the superficial epidermis, (Rajadhyaksha et al, 1999) which is well suited for detecting subtle epidermal architectural and cytologic abnormalities. OCT/line-field confocal OCT provide greater depth penetration and typically higher axial (depth) resolution, (Dubois et al, 2018a, 2018b; Gambichler et al, 2011) but with comparatively coarser lateral resolution than RCM for en face cellular pattern assessment. In contrast, hyperspectral imaging primarily provides spectral/biochemical contrast and surface-weighted mapping rather than direct cellular morphologic detail, and its effective spatial resolution depends on the specific optical configuration and sampling. Thus, our findings, which show that RCM can detect subtle early features of field damage, suggest that RCM may become a valuable tool for assessing field damage.

This study was performed on SKH1 skin under standardized UV exposure and tightly controlled imaging conditions, which minimize confounding but limit generalizability. An additional consideration for clinical translation is skin pigmentation, as melanin is a major endogenous source of contrast in RCM. In more pigmented skin, increased melanin-related backscatter can alter the apparent brightness and contrast of epidermal structures (particularly near the basal layer), which may affect the conspicuity and/or threshold behavior of some features. Although the architectural and cytologic patterns evaluated here are expected to remain interpretable, future work should validate these criteria and any quantitative thresholds in pigmented preclinical models and in human skin across a range of pigmentation levels.

A key future direction is to integrate quantitative image analysis and machine-learning approaches to provide objective, continuous scoring of UV-induced field damage and to reduce observer dependence. Automated methods may ultimately improve sensitivity to subtle subclinical changes and enable scalable assessment across larger longitudinal datasets. The standardized acquisition in the SKH1 model and the 5 predefined, expert-derived features reported here provide a structured foundation for developing and validating such computational tools, including direct comparison of algorithmic performance versus expert and novice readers in independent cohorts. Future clinical translation may allow for real-time, image-guided assessment of photodamage and the efficacy of preventative interventions. Advances in artificial intelligence, particularly in computer vision and image analysis (eg, supervised deep learning classifiers, segmentation-based approaches, and quantitative feature extraction), may enable automated, objective assessment of RCM features, potentially reducing interobserver variability and supporting scalable evaluation of UV-induced field damage. Another major future direction is a longitudinal time-course analysis leveraging the full serial datasets. In particular, quantifying when each RCM feature first becomes detectable and modeling feature trajectories as a function of weeks before the first visible tumor onset will help define the temporal sensitivity of RCM to early UV-induced field change and identify the earliest time point at which discrimination from control skin becomes reliable. This analysis will require larger sample sizes across time points and appropriate longitudinal statistical modeling and is planned as follow-up work.

Although our study suggests that RCM can evaluate the photodamaged field, several limitations warrant consideration. First, because the data are confined to an SKH1 model, the findings may not fully reflect the biological and clinical heterogeneity seen in humans. Second, the modest sample size narrows precision and limits subgroup analyses. Third, reliance on a single mouse strain and a single irradiation regimen constrains generalizability to other models, exposure patterns, and anatomical sites. An additional limitation relevant to clinical translation is the difference in performance between expert and novice readers, particularly during the early post-UV period when morphological changes are expected to be more subtle. Although expert readers classified irradiated versus non-irradiated skin with high sensitivity and specificity at early time points, novice readers showed substantially lower specificity and poor interobserver agreement. This finding indicates that detection of early UV-induced field damage by RCM is reader-dependent and requires greater experience. Importantly, this limitation also supports the need for future quantitative image-analysis and machine-learning approaches that could provide more objective feature detection and reduce observer variability across users with different levels of RCM experience.

However, these constraints are balanced by notable strengths. Specifically, the work employs a well-defined, standardized solar-simulated UV protocol and tightly controlled RCM acquisition, thereby enabling reproducible comparisons. Moreover, blinded assessments by both expert and novice readers test real-world usability and quantify interobserver agreement. In addition, the blinded reader evaluation was performed by an international, multi-center panel (United States, Germany, Spain, and Hungary), which helps to mitigate single-center bias and supports the generalizability of the qualitative scoring framework. Taken together, these elements support the internal robustness of the results while, at the same time, motivating larger, multi-model, and human validation studies. A direct comparison of automated models versus expert and novice readers is an important future direction and will require larger datasets with appropriate validation and independent testing.

Our findings indicate that optical imaging via RCM offers a non-invasive, high-resolution, and non-scarring (biopsy-free) method for objectively distinguishing UV-exposed from non-exposed epidermis based on subclinical histological features. The ability to detect field damage early, before clinical lesion development, has implications for improving surveillance strategies, guiding field-directed therapies, and monitoring responses to prevention and treatment strategies in patients at high risk for non-melanoma skin cancer.

Conclusion

In this controlled SKH1 model, RCM identified reproducible epidermal morphological features associated with chronic UV exposure in clinically normal-appearing skin. Using a predefined feature set, blinded expert, and novice readers were able to classify irradiated versus non-irradiated cases with measurable accuracy, and atypical honeycombing and keratinocyte pleomorphism showed the strongest discriminatory value. Although these results support the feasibility of using RCM to detect subclinical UV-related field damage in a preclinical setting. Larger longitudinal studies and prospective validation in human photodamaged skin are needed to determine how early these features emerge, define clinically relevant thresholds, and evaluate real-world performance.

Methods

Animal model and UV exposure

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee-approved protocol at Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center. The hairless, albino, outbred strain SKH1 (Crl:SKH-1-Hr hr, SKH1-Elite) (Benavides et al, 2009) was bred in-house and served as the source for all experimental animals. A total of 36 hairless SKH1 mice were enrolled; 72.2% (n = 26) mice were assigned to the irradiated group (n = 13 female and n = 13 male mice); and 27.8% (n = 10) were assigned to the control (non-irradiated) group (n = 5 female and n = 5 male mice). The mice were 7–8 weeks old when irradiation was initiated at the timepoint defined as Week 0. All cases used for (i) determining the 5 RCM features carried forward to the blinded study and (ii) the final blinded reader evaluation were randomly selected from the same underlying pool of enrolled mice within the predefined pre-tumor imaging period. The blinded reader evaluation cases were selected via random sampling from this pool and were scored independently under full blinding to exposure status.

Mice in the irradiated group were exposed to solar-simulated UV radiation at 90 millijoules per centimeter squared, corresponding to the UVB energy component, on their dorsal surface as previously described. (Benavides et al, 2009) The Abet 11044-1 solar simulator equipped with a 11063 atmospheric edge filter was used, delivering light that closely mimics the terrestrial spectrum from 290 to 400 nanometers over a 200 × 200 millimeters illumination field. The filter eliminates UVC and prevents significant heating of the mice during treatment.

At the start of the irradiation protocol, 7–8-week-old SKH1 mice received an initial UVB dose of 30 millijoules per centimeter squared. The dose was then gradually escalated by 15% at each session to avoid skin burning, until the final treatment dose of 90 millijoules per centimeter squared was reached. Exposures were performed in the morning, 5 days per week (Monday to Friday) for 10 consecutive weeks, with a dose of 90 millijoules per centimeter squared requiring 5–6 minutes based on the UVB output of the bulb.

To ensure accurate and reproducible dosing, UVB irradiance was verified weekly with an RM-12 Radiometer equipped with a UVB sensor (Obsytec Dr. Groebe, Germany). The solar simulator spectrum was additionally verified every 500 lamp hours using an STSUV spectrometer (Ocean Optics, USA). During each exposure, 4–5 animals were placed in their home cages under the light source, unrestrained and able to move freely. All treatments were carried out in a bioBUBBLE enclosure (bioBUBBLE, Inc.), and operators wore protective face shields or goggles throughout.

All irradiated mice developed visible tumors between weeks 20 and 22, with the earliest tumors appearing at week 14. None of the control mice developed tumors during the observation period.

Imaging protocol

After the irradiation period, imaging was initiated at week 10 and conducted every 2 weeks until week 22 (Figure 13). At each imaging session, we obtained: (i) digital single-lens reflex photography of the dorsal skin surface to monitor gross morphological changes and tumor development, and (ii) RCM using the VivaScope 1500 system (Caliber I.D., Rochester, NY, USA). For each dorsal imaging site, after the RCM probe window was positioned on the skin, 5 standardized VivaStacks were acquired from 5 predefined, spatially distant areas within the 8 × 8 millimeters field of view of the RCM probe. Thus, the 5 VivaStacks represented distinct, non-overlapping sampling areas within the irradiated or non-irradiated dorsal skin, rather than repeated measurements of the same area. For each site, VivaStacks were acquired over a standardized depth range of 89.3 micrometers with a z-step of 3.6 micrometers per frame. This depth range was selected to ensure full-thickness sampling of the SKH-1 epidermis and extension into the superficial dermis.

Based on a literature review and consultation with RCM experts, we identified 18 previously published RCM morphological features linked to keratinocyte neoplasia/photocarcinogenesis (12 epidermal and 6 dermal) and evaluated their relevance for UV-induced field change. Two expert RCM users (MJ and BR) independently reviewed RCM image stacks from UV-irradiated and non-irradiated mice at Weeks 12, 16, and 20 and scored all 18 features while unblinded to exposure status. Blinded reader evaluation

The evaluation was performed by a group of readers comprising 3 expert RCM users (MJ, SS, and JD) who routinely use RCM in clinical practice, and 3 novice users (FVM, NK, and DW) who had received basic RCM training but do not routinely use RCM. The reader panel was international and multi-center, comprising evaluators based in the United States (MJ and JD), Germany (SS and DW), Spain (FVM), and Hungary (NK).

All readers were graduate-level medical doctors working in dermatology and/or dermatopathology. Expert readers were defined as physicians who routinely interpret RCM in clinical practice. Novice readers were defined as physicians with basic prior exposure to RCM interpretation, but who do not routinely use RCM in clinical care; novices were included to assess whether the feature definitions were sufficiently operationalized and recognizable beyond a small group of highly experienced confocal readers.

Before scoring, readers received a brief standardized orientation to the SKH1 dataset, and the 5 predefined epidermal features evaluated in this study (scaling, hyperkeratosis, atypical honeycombing, architectural disarray, and keratinocyte pleomorphism). This orientation included the operational definitions used for scoring and representative example images from the SKH1 model (3 per feature). No additional formal training, coaching, feedback, or practice testing were provided.

For the evaluation, sites from UV-irradiated and non-irradiated control mice were selected. Each case consisted of 5 VivaStacks (sequential en face RCM images acquired from the top to the bottom from the stratum corneum into the superficial dermis) representative of the dorsal imaging site. Reviewers were blinded to the irradiation status and independently assessed each of the 5 VivaStacks per case. For each stack, reviewers recorded the presence or absence of each of the 5 predefined features (binary scoring; 1 = present, 0 = absent). Thus, each reviewer contributed stack-level ratings comprising 25 binary decisions per case (5 features × 5 stacks). For case-level summaries, stack-level ratings were converted into a feature presence percentage per case (0–100%) by calculating the proportion of stacks scored positive for a given feature. After feature scoring, each reviewer provided a single case-level classification (“irradiated” vs “non-irradiated”) based on their overall assessment of the 5-stack case.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons of irradiated versus control groups for case-level feature presence percentages were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test (2-sided). For comparisons involving categorical, threshold-based feature positivity (feature-positive vs feature-negative), P-values were calculated using 2-sided Fisher’s exact tests, and ORs with 95% CIs were derived from these contingency tables. Because multiple features were tested, the P-values in Tables 1, 2, and 5 were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Interobserver agreement was assessed using Fleiss’ κ.

To estimate how likely each feature was to be present in UV-irradiated versus control skin, we first generated case-level feature presence percentages from the blinded expert reader assessments. For each case, each expert evaluated 5 VivaStacks and provided binary presence/absence ratings per feature per stack. For each feature and each expert, we calculated a case-level feature percentage as the number of stacks scored positive divided by 5 (0–100% in 20% increments). We summarized expert assessments at the case level by averaging, across the 3 expert readers, the percentage of stacks scored positive for each feature, yielding a single expert-derived “feature likelihood” percentage per case.

For ORs analysis, we converted each feature’s expert-derived case-level percentage into a binary classification (feature-positive vs feature-negative). For each feature, we identified the lowest expert-derived percentage observed among irradiated cases and used this value as the threshold. Any case (irradiated or control) with a feature percentage equal to or above this threshold was classified as feature-positive; otherwise, it was classified as feature-negative. This threshold-based binary classification was then used to calculate ORs using Fisher’s exact test with 95% CIs. Because the thresholding procedure produced zero counts in some 2 × 2 contingency table cells, a Haldane-Anscombe continuity correction was applied to obtain finite ORs.

To evaluate multi-feature profiles, we calculated composite scores by summing the expert-derived case-level percentages across selected features and dividing by the number of the included features to generate a normalized composite percentage per case. For each feature combination, we applied the same thresholding strategy (using the minimum composite score among the irradiated cases as the cutoff) and reported the combination(s) that most cleanly separated the irradiated cases from the control cases.
