Quality of Life in Patients following distal nerve transfers for the Restoration of Shoulder abduction and external rotation - Single centre experience
Authors: Andrija Savić, Jovan Grujić, Aleksandar Djurdjević, Svetozar Stanković, Aleksa Mićić, Aleksandra Stojiljković, Dragoslav Nestorović, Milan Lepić, Nenad Novaković, Lukas Rasulić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRestoration of shoulder function is a key objective in brachial plexus surgery, as impaired abduction and external rotation substantially limit upper limb use and quality of life. Distal nerve transfers offer shorter regeneration distances and more predictable outcomes than nerve grafting.Research questionThis study evaluated the effectiveness of distal nerve transfers in restoring shoulder function, reducing pain, and improving quality of life in patients with peripheral nerve injuries.Material and methodsA prospective cohort of 36 patients with traumatic brachial plexus or axillary nerve lesions was treated using spinal accessory to suprascapular and/or radial-to-axillary nerve transfers via a posterior approach. Outcomes were assessed two years postoperatively. The primary outcome was the proportion of patients achieving M3–M5 shoulder abduction. Secondary outcomes included external rotation, pain, stability, and the Peripheral Nerve Surgery Quality of Life (PNSQoL) score. Functional recovery was graded using the M0–M5 scale, with M3–M5 considered satisfactory.ResultsSatisfactory shoulder abduction was achieved in 91.9% of patients, and satisfactory external rotation in 78.4%. Preoperatively, most patients were classified as having poor or fair PNSQoL, whereas postoperatively the majority shifted to good, very good, or excellent categories. Lesion type significantly influenced external rotation outcomes, with complete brachial plexus palsy showing the poorest results. Younger age and earlier surgery (<6 months) showed trends toward better recovery.Discussion and conclusionDistal nerve transfers are effective and reproducible procedures for restoring shoulder function, reducing pain, and improving quality of life in patients with brachial plexus injuries.

Introduction

Shoulder abduction and external rotation are primarily mediated by the suprascapular nerve (supraspinatus, infraspinatus) and the axillary nerve (deltoid, teres minor), with predominant contributions from C5–C6 roots. The loss of shoulder abduction and external rotation severely compromises upper limb function, posture, and quality of life. Adequate shoulder positioning is essential not only for reaching and overhead activities, but also for enabling effective elbow and hand function by providing a stable proximal joint. Without active abduction and external rotation, even preserved distal motor functions remain of limited practical value (Noland et al., 2019).

Such functional losses arise from a variety of pathological conditions, including isolated axillary or suprascapular nerve lesions, upper, extended upper and complete brachial plexus palsies, and complex traumatic injuries. Regardless of the underlying etiology, the resulting functional impairment follows a similar clinical pattern characterized by shoulder instability, restricted range of motion, pain, and impaired arm use during daily activities (Noland et al., 2019).

Traditional reconstructive strategies based on nerve repair with grafts or proximal nerve transfers have yielded inconsistent functional outcomes in restoring shoulder motion, particularly in proximal lesions and root avulsions, largely due to long regeneration distances and prolonged denervation of target muscles (Rasulic et al., 2020).

Contemporary reviews recognize distal nerve transfers targeting the suprascapular and axillary nerves as a cornerstone and superior reconstructive strategy for shoulder function restoration consistently providing faster, more reliable, and functionally meaningful recovery (Bertelli et al., 2024; PjhaJF, 2025). Somsak Leechavengvongs introduced an eponymous “Somsak procedure”, a radial nerve branch to the long or medial head to the triceps muscle to axillary nerve transfer (SOM) added to spinal accessory-to-suprascapular nerve transfer (SAN-SSN) for a synergistic dual-nerve reconstruction that reliably restores both abduction and external rotation (Leechavengvongs et al., 2003). Building on this foundation, subsequent refinements by Samardžić, Malessy, and Bertelli have further optimized donor selection, timing, and technical execution, leading to improved outcomes (Leechavengvongs et al., 2003; Malessy et al., 2004; Bertelli and Ghizoni, 2007).

While motor recovery is frequently reported, few studies integrated patient-reported outcomes or quality of life measures. In addition, the relative contributions of different donor nerves and the influence of surgical timing on long-term outcomes remain areas of ongoing studies.

We conducted a prospective cohort study of patients undergoing tailored distal nerve transfers for restoration of shoulder function in patients with isolated axillary nerve lesion, upper (C5-C6), extended (C5-C7), and complete brachial plexus palsy. Our aims were to evaluate postoperative functional outcomes of shoulder abduction and external rotation, assess patient-reported quality of life using the PNSQoL instrument developed by our group(Savić et al., 2024), and examine the factors influencing these outcomes.

Initially, 37 patients were evaluated for inclusion, but one patient with an extensive upper extremity injury was excluded from the final analysis because the severity of soft-tissue, skeletal, and neural damage precluding distal nerve transfer based reconstructive strategy.

Preoperative assessment was performed using a standardized protocol including clinical examination, electrophysiological studies, and imaging. Postoperatively, all patients received standardized physical therapy for at least 12 months.

Strength and range of motion of abduction and external rotation were measured with a protractor and graded according to the Shoulder abduction scale (Appendix 2) and External shoulder rotation scale (Appendix 3), respectively. Recovery grades M0–M2 were considered unsatisfactory, while M3–M5 represented useful functional recovery (M3 good, M4 very good, M5 excellent).

Pain and stability were assessed with structured questionnaires administered preoperatively and at final follow-up. Patients completed surveys grading their shoulder pain (strong, moderate, slight, pain free) and reporting on perceived shoulder stability (stable vs. unstable). The obtained results were then compared. Pain was assessed as a subjective patient-reported outcome and was not adjusted for differences in sensory function between lesion types.

Quality of life was evaluated with the original Peripheral Nerve Surgery Quality of Life (PNSQOL) questionnaire score (Appendix 4), categorized as follows: total score 0–80; poor (0–39), fair (40–49), good (50–59), very good (60–69), and excellent (70–80).

The primary study outcome was satisfactory motor recovery of shoulder abduction and external rotation, while secondary outcomes included, ranges of motion, pain, and the Peripheral Nerve Surgery Quality of Life (PNSQoL) score two years after surgery.

Surgical procedure

Surgical procedure included SAN–SSN and/or radial nerve branch to axillary nerve transfer (SOM) depending on the pattern and extent of nerve injury.

In isolated axillary nerve lesions, reconstruction was limited to SOM. In upper brachial plexus palsy, restoration of shoulder function routinely included SAN-SSN, most often combined with SOM to address both external rotation and abduction.

In selected cases with severe upper or extended upper brachial plexus palsy, reconstruction was expanded to include SAN-SSN combined with transfer of the radial nerve branch to the medial head of the triceps, which was split into two fascicular components to reinnervate the anterior and posterior divisions of the axillary nerve separately, in order to optimize multidirectional shoulder function.

In more extensive upper or extended upper brachial plexus palsies, combined reconstructive strategies were applied to maximize proximal upper limb function. Importantly, when procedures were combined, each nerve transfer was performed using the same standardized surgical technique as when applied individually. Thus, combined reconstructions represent an aggregation of established procedures rather than a distinct surgical technique, allowing meaningful comparison of functional outcomes across different lesion types and reconstructive strategies.

All procedures were performed exclusively through a posterior approach, which represents a consistent and defining feature of our surgical strategy, allowing direct access to both donor and recipient nerves while minimizing additional morbidity.

Spinal accessory to suprascapular nerve transfer (SAN–SSN)

The patient is placed prone. Key landmarks include the acromion, medial scapular border, and vertebral midline. The spinal accessory nerve runs medial to the acromion–midline line, while the suprascapular nerve lies along the acromion–medial scapular border line. A straight incision is made through the thick skin and minimal subcutaneous fat layer, over the trapezius to expose both nerves. (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Patient positioning, anatomical landmarks, and incisions for the posterior approach in spinal accessory to suprascapular nerve transfer.

Patient positioning, anatomical landmarks, and incisions for the posterior approach in spinal accessory to suprascapular nerve transfer.

The trapezius fascia is incised and muscle fibers are separated bluntly to minimize bleeding and muscle damage. Retractors expose the deep fascia, beneath which the spinal accessory nerve runs within the adipose tissue. Due to its small calibre and proximity to vessels, careful dissection is required. The nerve is traced distally to its branching point, with preference for harvesting a single branch to minimize trapezius denervation, particularly of the lower fibers.

The suprascapular nerve lies deeper at the scapular notch beneath the transverse scapular ligament. Dissection should be sufficiently wide to avoid a “tunnel effect,” and haemostasis may be challenging. The ligament is resected medially to protect adjacent vessels, and the nerve is exposed distally to its branches for the supraspinatus and infraspinatus muscles. Although superselective reinnervation of the infraspinatus is possible, proximal preparation is usually required. Despite the size mismatch with the accessory donor, proximal sectioning of the suprascapular nerve and distal sectioning of the spinal accessory nerve allows direct, tension-free coaptation. N. spinalis accessories and n. suprascapularis after exposure.

Direct coaptation of the suprascapular and spinal accessory nerve trunks.

Radial nerve branch to axillary nerve transfer (SOM)

The patient is positioned prone with the arm slightly abducted. Key landmarks include the posterior border of the deltoid and the septum between the long and lateral heads of the triceps. The incision extends from below the scapular spine along the posterior deltoid border and continues along the posterior upper arm. The skin in the proximal part of the incision is thicker with minimal adipose tissue, while distally it becomes thinner and richer in fat. (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Patient positioning and incision for transfer of the radial nerve branch to the medial head of the triceps to the axillary nerve.

Patient positioning and incision for transfer of the radial nerve branch to the medial head of the triceps to the axillary nerve.

Dissection is directed toward exposing the triceps fascia distally and the interval between the posterior deltoid border and the long head of the triceps proximally, taking care to preserve the posterior cutaneous nerve of the arm. After soft-tissue retraction, the interfascial plane between the deltoid (retracted laterally) and the long head of the triceps (retracted medially) is entered. Within this space, the sensory branch of the axillary nerve is identified and followed deeper, where it crosses a vascular bundle that should be preserved.

Further careful dissection exposes the teres minor and the quadrilateral space containing the axillary nerve and its terminal branches. Because the nerve lies superficially to deeper vessels, bold or deep preparation risks vascular injury. To improve visualization, partial sectioning of the triceps long head tendon may be required. Knowledge of the axillary nerve branching pattern is essential (Fig. 5). Typically, the branch to the teres minor arises proximally, followed by the anterior branch to the anterior deltoid, the posterior branch to the posterior deltoid, and a sensory branch. Variations are common.Fig. 5The axillary nerve with its terminal branches.

The axillary nerve with its terminal branches.

The axillary nerve is exposed proximally within the quadrilateral space to maximize recipient length and facilitate tension-free coaptation. Dissection then proceeds distally between the long and lateral heads of the triceps, with division of the intermuscular septum when necessary. By retracting these muscles, the teres major and underlying radial nerve are identified. Careful dissection is required due to the proximity of large vessels, and haemostasis may be challenging. At this level, motor branches of the radial nerve are identified, and the branch to the medial head of the triceps is selected as the donor for transfer. (Fig. 6).Fig. 6The radial nerve with branches to the triceps brachii muscle.

The radial nerve with branches to the triceps brachii muscle.

This branch for the medial head of the triceps muscle has the largest cross section and it is the longest, and is, therefore, the most suitable donor. This branch often consists of two adjoining trunks that are easily separated. This branch is traced as distally as possible to its point of entry into the muscle. In its distal division there are numerous tiny blood vessels that surround it, so the preparation, at this level, is often accompanied by frustrating small bleeds which have to be taken care of, while trying not to damage the nerve either by direct cauterisation or by heat transfer.

The donor section is done as distally as possible, and the section of the recipient as proximally as possible, in order to allow for a direct coaptation of the nerve stumps (Fig. 7).Fig. 7Direct nerve coaptation between the radial nerve branch to the medial head of the triceps and the axillary nerve.

Direct nerve coaptation between the radial nerve branch to the medial head of the triceps and the axillary nerve.

Shoulder abduction outcomes across lesion types, according to the Rasulić score, were satisfactory (M3–M5) in 100% of isolated axillary nerve lesions (A), 95.5% of upper plexus palsies (UP), and 50% of complete brachial plexus palsies (CP). The overall comparison demonstrated a statistically significant association between lesion type and abduction outcome (χ2 = 10.4, p = 0.005). In addition, a Fisher's exact test contrasting complete palsies with all other lesion types combined showed a significantly higher rate of unsatisfactory abduction outcomes in the CP group (p = 0.027).

External rotation outcomes across lesion types using the Savić score, were satisfactory (M3–M5) in 90% of A lesions, 86.4% of UP lesions, whereas none of the patients with CP achieved a satisfactory external rotation score. The overall comparison between lesion types was highly significant (χ2 = 15.8, p = 0.0004), and Fisher's exact test again confirmed significantly worse external rotation outcomes in the CP group compared with all other lesions combined (p = 0.0012). A total of 22 patients underwent triple nerve transfer (TNP), making it the most frequently performed reconstructive procedure in the cohort. Ten patients underwent a radial-to-axillary nerve transfer (SOM), all of whom had isolated axillary nerve injuries. SA-SS–based reconstructive strategies, performed either as standalone procedures or in combination with additional grafts or nerve transfers, were used in four patients with more extensive patterns of plexus involvement.

The primary indication for TNP was an upper brachial plexus lesion with preserved distal function and the need to restore shoulder abduction, external rotation, and elbow flexion, which corresponded to the clinical presentation of the majority of patients in this group. SOM was predominantly indicated for isolated axillary nerve palsy, particularly when donor fascicles from the radial nerve were intact and capable of providing reliable reinnervation of the deltoid and teres minor muscles. SAN-SSN transfers, alone or with adjunctive grafting, were reserved for patients with more complex or extended lesions requiring targeted reinnervation of the suprascapular nerve to improve shoulder stability and external rotation.

Postoperative functional outcomes demonstrated a high rate of recovery across the cohort. Shoulder abduction showed satisfactory results (M3–M5) in 33 of 36 patients (91.7%), while only 3 patients (8.3%) remained in the unsatisfactory range (M0–M2). The mean postoperative abduction measured 103.6. External rotation also improved substantially, with 28 patients (77.8%) achieving satisfactory postoperative function (M3–M5) and 8 patients (22.2%) exhibiting unsatisfactory outcomes. The mean postoperative external rotation was 92.5°. A marked reduction in shoulder pain was observed following surgery.

Preoperatively, the majority of patients reported either severe (55.6%) or moderate pain (38.9%), with only 5.6% experiencing slight pain. Postoperatively, no patient reported severe pain, and pain intensity shifted dramatically toward the lower end of the scale: 50.0% of patients were completely pain-free, 33.3% reported only slight discomfort, and 16.7% reported moderate pain. When pain categories were converted into ordinal scores (severe = 3, moderate = 2, slight = 1, no pain = 0), the mean pain score decreased from 2.50 preoperatively to 0.67 postoperatively. This improvement was highly statistically significant (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 1.1 × 10−7), indicating a robust reduction in pain following surgical treatment.

When outcomes were analysed according to timing of surgery, patients were stratified into three groups: early surgery (<6 months after injury), intermediate surgery (6–12 months), and delayed surgery (>12 months).

For shoulder abduction, satisfactory recovery (M3–M5) was achieved in 100% of early cases (9/9), 92% of intermediate cases (23/25), and 50% of delayed cases (1/2), demonstrating a trend toward worse outcomes with increasing delay, although this did not reach statistical significance. For external rotation, a time-dependent effect was observed, with satisfactory recovery in 88.9% of early cases (8/9) and 80% of intermediate cases (20/25), while no patients in the delayed group achieved satisfactory outcomes (0/2). This difference was statistically significant, indicating inferior outcomes with increasing surgical delay.

When combined shoulder function was considered, favourable results were achieved in 88.9% of early cases and 80% of intermediate cases, whereas no satisfactory outcomes were observed in the delayed group. These findings suggest that delayed reconstruction adversely affects external rotation and overall shoulder function, although interpretation is limited by the small number of patients in the delayed group.

When patient age was analysed, a trend toward better functional recovery was observed in younger individuals, although no statistically significant association was demonstrated.For shoulder abduction, patients with satisfactory recovery (M3–M5) had a lower median age compared with those with unsatisfactory outcomes (33 vs. 46 years), with a weak inverse correlation that did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05).For external rotation, a similar pattern was observed, with lower median age in patients with satisfactory outcomes (31.5 vs. 41.5 years) and a weak inverse correlation, again without statistical significance (p > 0.05), suggesting a trend toward poorer outcomes with increasing age.

The presence of associated injuries did not appear to influence functional outcome. Satisfactory shoulder abduction (M3–M5) was achieved in 100% of patients without associated injuries (7/7) and in 89.7% of those with associated injuries (26/29), with no statistically significant difference between groups (Fisher's exact test, p = 1.0). Similarly, satisfactory external rotation was observed in 71.4% of patients without associated injuries (5/7) and 79.3% of those with associated injuries (23/29), again without a significant difference (p = 0.64). These findings suggest that the presence of concomitant systemic or skeletal trauma, as defined in this cohort, does not substantially affect the functional outcome of shoulder reconstruction (Table 2).

Peripheral Nerve Surgery Quality of Life (PNS-QoL) scores improved markedly following surgery. Preoperatively, the vast majority of patients were classified as having poor PNSQoL (88.9%), with the remaining patients categorized as fair (11.1%). No patients achieved good, very good, or excellent PNSQoL scores prior to surgery.

Postoperatively, a marked shift toward higher PNSQoL categories was observed, with 36.1% of patients achieving very good and 36.1% excellent PNSQoL scores, while only 8.3% remained in the poor category (Table 3). The mean preoperative PNS-QoL score was 25.6 (range 10–41), whereas the mean postoperative score increased to 61.7 (range 12–80). This corresponds to an average gain of approximately 36 points on the PNS-QoL scale. Discussion

Restoring shoulder function after brachial plexus injury is of paramount importance because it provides the foundation for functional use of the upper limb. Without active abduction and external rotation, daily activities such as reaching, grooming, and maintaining posture during locomotion are severely compromised. Stable shoulder joints not only allow efficient elbow function but also contribute to balance, coordination, and quality of life (Rasulic et al., 2018). Therefore, reinnervation of the suprascapular and axillary nerves has consistently been prioritized in reconstructive algorithms.

In our series, more than 90% of patients regained satisfactory abduction and nearly 80% satisfactory external rotation. Importantly, outcomes were analysed separately according to lesion type, allowing comparison between isolated axillary nerve injuries and more complex brachial plexus lesions. Although these injury patterns differ in extent, they were included within the same cohort because they share a common reconstructive principle based on distal nerve transfer targeting shoulder function, enabling a unified analysis of surgical outcomes. These results are comparable or superior to previously published data. Bertelli reported favourable outcomes with similar transfers, particularly when performed early (Bertelli and Ghizoni, 2007), while Vincent et al. (2025) demonstrated that SAN–SSN transfer reliably improves active abduction and external rotation in traumatic brachial plexus injuries(Vincent et al., 2025). In obstetric injuries, Mendiratta et al. (2024) found comparable results between SAN–SSN transfer and nerve grafting, although grafting requires longer regeneration distances and carries higher risk of incomplete reinnervation (Mendiratta et al., 2024). Our results support the accumulating evidence that SAN–SSN transfer is both effective and reproducible across different patient groups.

For axillary nerve reinnervation, we used the radial nerve branch to the medial head of the triceps. This donor has several advantages: it provides robust axonal input, it is synergistic with deltoid function (facilitating motor re-education), and it leaves minimal donor deficit. Czarnecki et al. (2024) reported similar outcomes, with average abduction of 78° and clear benefits when combined with SAN–SSN transfer(Czarnecki et al., 2024). Other donors such as intercostal nerves or branches from the long head of the triceps have been used, but generally with less predictable recovery. Our findings therefore reinforce the radial-to-axillary transfer as a reliable and preferred option.

Prognostic factors may influence functional outcomes. In our cohort, a trend toward better recovery was observed in younger patients and in those treated earlier, particularly within 6 months of injury, although these findings did not reach statistical significance and should be interpreted with caution. These findings are consistent with the work of Midha [3] and Bhatia (Midha, 2004; Bhatia et al., 2011), and underscore the importance of early referral to specialized centres. The “time factor” is crucial because prolonged denervation leads to irreversible muscle atrophy and fibrosis, reducing the potential for reinnervation.

Future studies, preferably multicentric prospective studies are needed validate prognostic factors and refine selection criteria for donor and recipient nerves. Integration with other reconstructive techniques, such as Oberlin transfer for elbow flexion or free functional muscle or tendon transfers for belated cases, may further enhance outcomes. Advances in intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring, imaging, and even experimental approaches such as nerve conduits or adjunctive electrical stimulation, hold promise for nerve surgery of the future.

This study has several limitations. It is a single-centre study with a relatively small sample size and no control group, which may limit generalizability. In addition, potential selection and observer bias cannot be excluded. Follow-up duration was limited, and long-term functional endurance beyond the first few years remains to be established. Pain and shoulder stability were assessed using subjective patient-reported measures, and perception may differ between patients with isolated nerve injuries and those with more extensive brachial plexus lesions due to varying sensory impairment, which may limit direct comparison across groups.

Despite these limitations, our findings align with the broader literature, confirming distal nerve transfers as effective, safe, and reproducible methods for shoulder reinnervation. In addition to their role in restoring abduction and external rotation, these procedures reduce pain and improve quality of life, which are equally important outcomes.

Conclusion

Distal nerve transfers for shoulder function restoration, primarily SAN-SSN and radial-to-axillary nerve transfers represent the current gold standard for restoring shoulder function, and their reproducibility and favourable risk–benefit profile makes them indispensable tools in the surgical armamentarium.

Our study confirmed reliable restoration of shoulder abduction and external rotation. Satisfactory recovery (≥M3) was achieved in 91.7% of patients for shoulder abduction and 77.8% for external rotation, with mean postoperative ranges of motion of 103.6° and 92.5°, respectively. Shoulder pain decreased significantly, and patient-reported quality of life improved markedly, with mean PNS-QoL scores increasing from 25.6 to 61.7.

Despite these consistent functional and QoL gains, the evidence base in peripheral nerve surgery remains limited to single-centre series, and meaningful improvement can only be achieved through coordinated multicentric collaboration and prospective study designs.
