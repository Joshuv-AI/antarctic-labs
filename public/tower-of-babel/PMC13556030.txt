Applicant perceptions of program social media on fellowship recruitment: a survey of fellows at a large academic medical center

Background

Prior to the widespread use of social media, fellowship programs disseminated information to applicants via printed brochures, websites, and in-person interviews. In the field of medicine, social media has become increasingly important in recent years, proving to be a valuable tool for influencing recruitment, education, professional growth, academic contributions and promotions [1]. Specifically as it relates to recruitment, fellowship programs are increasingly relying on social media as a means to disseminate information to and recruit applicants [2, 3]. Moreover, applicants are increasingly utilizing this information to influence interview season decisions. Postgraduate Year 1 residents who were surveyed following participation in the 2020–2021 virtual interview season ranked social media as one of the top ranked resources for researching prospective residency programs and the majority (68%) agreed that their perception of a program was positively influenced by the program’s social media presence [4]. As a result, fellowship programs are becoming strategic with the information that is presented on their respective outlets in order to convey aspects such as the programs culture, mission, educational initiatives, commitment to diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI), amongst others [5]. At the same time, there remains uncertainty about which factors prospective fellows use to guide their decision when applying to and ranking programs. This study seeks to understand the role fellowship program-based social media accounts play in recruiting prospective candidates and what information applicants value and influences recruitment decisions.

Methods

We surveyed fellows in Mayo Clinic School of Graduate Medical Education (MCSGME) fellowship training programs at all Mayo Clinic sites including Rochester, Minnesota (MN); Scottsdale, Arizona (AZ); and Jacksonville, Florida (FL). The survey was modeled after a similar survey of postgraduate-year-1 residents [4]. The web-based 24-question survey queried fellows’ demographic characteristics, personal social media use, social media platforms used to evaluate fellowship programs, type of content preferred, attitudes about programs’ use of social media, and contact with programs over social media (appendix 1). The survey was piloted by twelve fellows outside the institution to assess readability, ease of use, and completion time. Recommendations from the pilot group were incorporated into the final survey.

Per the MCSGME Education Research Committee recommendation, all 235 MCSGME fellowship program directors (PDs) were contacted by the investigators for permission to contact their fellows. PDs then decided if they wanted to distribute the survey to their fellows directly or provide permission for the research group to contact their fellows directly. The PDs were contacted twice asking for permission, an initial email followed by a reminder email. Subsequently, a recruitment email was sent to all fellows with a link to the survey using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture). REDCap is a secure, web-based application designed for data collection and management in research studies. The fellows were contacted three times, an initial email followed by two reminder emails to complete the survey. Fellows were included if they were currently enrolled in a fellowship program. Fellows were excluded if the PD declined to participate. A REDCap link to the survey that generated an anonymous response was sent in the recruitment email. Reminder emails were sent weekly. A random drawing for four $15 dollar gift cards was offered to participants. Surveys were completed between October 1, 2023 and February 1, 2024.

Statistical analysis

This was a descriptive study, and data are presented using numbers and percentages.

Ethical considerations

The study was reviewed and approved by the Mayo Clinic Educational Research Committee. The study was deemed exempt from ethics approval by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board in Rochester, MN.

Results

Of the 235 PDs contacted, 135 (57%) PDs responded to the initial request and gave permission for the research group to contact their fellows (n = 399). There were a total of 690 fellows within the MCSGME at the time of survey distribution. Eleven PDs elected to send the survey to their fellows directly (n = 69) and four PDs responded that their fellows matched internally, and this survey did not apply. A total of 191 fellows responded to the survey for a response rate of 47%. Demographics of the respondents are detailed in Table 1. The majority (185/191, 96%) of respondents completed the survey in its entirety, however there are minor differences in the denominator due to respondents that did not answer every question of the survey. Most (94%) respondents had a virtual recruitment/interview format. The number of fellowship programs respondents applied to was bimodal in nature with 26% (48/185) applying to 5 or less programs and 47% (86/185) applying to 15 or more programs. Respondents represented a total of 21 departments.

The most utilized social media platforms for personal use included Instagram (117/191, 61%), Facebook (108/191, 57%), YouTube (86/191, 44%), and X (formerly Twitter) (74/191, 39%). The social media platforms respondents most employed to research prospective fellowship programs were X (48/189, 25%) and Instagram (37/189, 20%) (Fig. 1). 44% (77 of 176) of respondents shared that a virtual application cycle resulted in them relying more on information from program websites and social media accounts. Program webpage (61/187, 33%), recruitment video (30/187, 16%), and X (formerly Twitter) (26/187, 14%) contributed to application decisions (Table 2). Program webpage (51/188, 27%), X (formerly Twitter) (21/188, 11%), and recruitment video (19/188, 10%) contributed to rank list decisions. Importantly, 51% and 61% of respondents did not find program digital media contributing to their application or rank list decisions, respectively. Additional program digital media questions are detailed in Table 2. Approximately one-third of respondents (55/188, 29%) followed individual faculty and fellows from programs they were interested in. These respondents reported that fellowship program accounts and individual faculty/fellows were equally helpful in aiding their fellowship program search and interview/ranking process.

The top three social media posts from fellowships programs that respondents were interested in were fellowship wellness/work-life balance (64/189, 34%), quality of medical education (44/189, 23%), and fellow publications/awards/presentations (43/189, 23%) (Fig. 2). Table 3 lists the digital media platforms and type of posts most helpful for dissemination of information by fellowship programs. Program recruitment video (94/188, 50%), Instagram posts (69/188, 37%), and X (formerly twitter) posts (67/188, 36%) were the top three most helpful digital media for dissemination of program information. Half (93/185, 50%) of respondents either disagreed or strongly disagreed and over a third (67/185, 36%) neither agreed nor disagreed that a lack of social media presence of a fellowship program negatively influenced their perception of their program.

Discussion

We conducted a multi-specialty survey of fellows at a large academic medical center that spans three separate geographic regions of the United States (MN, AZ, FL) to evaluate perspectives of fellowship program social media platforms and type of digital media applicants value. Our results demonstrate that fellows relied more on information from program websites and social media accounts due to the virtual application format. X (formerly Twitter) and Instagram were the social media platforms most employed to research prospective programs. However, half of respondents did not consider program digital media when making application and rank list decisions.

Our results provide insight into the evolving and dynamic role of social media for applicants when assessing fellowship programs. Fellows in our study used social media to research fellowship programs to varying degrees. 44% of respondents shared the virtual format of recruitment season led them to rely more heavily on program digital media, a trend which has been previously demonstrated in residency applicants [4]. At the same time, approximately 40% of respondents stated they did not use social media during the application process. This may be influenced by a lack of social media presence by programs. A review of Pulmonary and Critical Care Medicine fellowship programs found that only 10% of programs had an X (formerly Twitter) account [6]. This trend was similar for pain medicine fellowship programs with only 3.6% of programs having a program-associated X (formerly Twitter) and Instagram account [7].

Applicants in our study were most interested in social media posts related to fellowship wellness/work-life balance and quality of medical education. When medical students were asked to rank types of posts from residency social media accounts, “resident life outside the hospital” received the highest rank [4]. Interestingly, medical students surveyed prior to the virtual interview format cited “maintenance of interpersonal relationships with department members” as the most important feature when evaluating residency-based social media accounts [8]. This suggests that with an increased reliance as well as an overall growth in social media since the onset of the virtual interviews medical students and residents alike are interested in posts from programs that highlight time away from clinical contact.

Based on our survey results, respondents were most interested in program webpages, recruitment videos, and accounts on Instagram and X. Surveys in residents applying to orthopedic trauma and primary care sports medicine fellowships have also found increased social media utilization but with continued reliance on program webpages when asked about which platforms applicants utilized to research programs [9, 10]. According to our survey responses, fellows were most interested in social media posts that included content related to fellow wellness/work-life balance, fellow publications/awards/presentations, and quality of medical education. Creating content for social media platforms can be time intensive and program directors may consider creating a social media team comprised of a combination of faculty and fellows. Unanswered questions and future research

This study has several limitations. The survey has been piloted and used in previous studies but more formal validation tools were not utilized. This was a survey study that relied on applicants recalling information from their application cycle with potential for recall bias. Further, a response rate of 47% may reflect a nonresponse bias and is similar to many survey-based studies regarding social media and recruitment. This study evaluated fellows at a single institution in three geographic areas and may not be generalizable to fellowships at other institutions or geographic locations. Only 57% of fellows within the MCSGME received the survey potentially resulting in a sampling bias. Further, as new social media platforms become popular (i.e. BlueSky or Threads), the results may not be generalizable to these platforms.

Future studies should analyze fellowship program social media postings and presence and applicants’ perspectives of the digital content. In addition, fellows’ perspectives of program fit should also be evaluated. In addition, as some specialties return to in-person or hybrid interviews, the role social media platforms play should be reassessed.

Conclusions

Most fellowship applicants use social media and used at least one social media platform to search potential fellowship programs. Prospective fellows were more interested in programs’ work-life integration, academic productivity of current fellows, and quality of the programs’ education. However, 51% of respondents said that a fellowship program’s social media presence did not contribute to their decision to rank a program. More studies are needed to fully understand the impact of social media in graduate medical education in order to tailor programs’ efforts. These insights emphasize the need for further exploration and strategic alignment of fellowship program digital media efforts to effectively reach and engage prospective applicants in the ever-evolving landscape of social media usage.
