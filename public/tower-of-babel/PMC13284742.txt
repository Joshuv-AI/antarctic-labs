Statement on the findings presented in the Toxicological Profile for Mercury issued by the US ATSDR
Authors: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), Maria Bastaki, Chantra Eskes, Thorhallur Halldorsson, Irene Pilar Muñoz Guajardo, Katharina Vejdovszky, Barbara Viviani, Hans Steinkellner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) was requested by the European Commission to deliver a statement on the findings presented in the Toxicological Profile for Mercury issued by the US Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR) in 2024. The statement was endorsed by the CONTAM Panel before approval by EFSA. Although some key methodological steps were not fully reported in the ATSDR document, it is unlikely that any major or relevant publications would have been missed. Based on the results presented by ATSDR (2024), including a new 28‐day study in rats, there is no need for an update of the 2012 EFSA risk assessment for inorganic mercury, because EFSA considered this study not suitable to derive a reference point for dietary risk assessment. Consequently, the tolerable weekly intake (TWI) of 4 μg/kg body weight (bw), expressed as mercury, is still valid. For methylmercury, varying reporting approaches on the rationale for the methodological choices made during hazard characterisation appear to be the main driver of the differing guidance values. There is no clear indication that the minimal risk level (MRL) for methylmercury derived by ATSDR (0.1 μg Hg/kg bw/day, corresponding to 0.7 μg Hg/kg bw/week) is scientifically more robust than the TWI (1.3 μg/kg bw per week, expressed as mercury) established by EFSA in 2012. Consequently, the current EFSA TWI of 1.3 μg/kg bw per week, expressed as mercury, remains valid. Despite this, the advances in risk assessment methodology and the new evidence becoming available since the last EFSA assessment might merit an update of the 2012 Opinion.

INTRODUCTION

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

Background

In 2012, the EFSA Scientific Panel on Contaminants in the Food Chain (CONTAM Panel) issued a scientific Opinion on the risk of public health related to the presence of mercury and methylmercury in food. 1

The CONTAM Panel established a tolerable weekly intake (TWI) for inorganic mercury of 4 μg/kg bw, based on a BMDL10 of 0.06 mg/kg bw per day (expressed as Hg), for increase in kidney weight in male rats (NTP, 1993, 2 ), applying an UF of 100 and converted to a weekly basis expressed as mercury. For methylmercury, a TWI of 1.3 μg/kg bw, expressed as mercury, was established based on a NOEL for neurodevelopmental effects from the Seychelles nutrition cohort of 11 mg/kg maternal hair (Lynch et al., 2011, 3 ) and on a BMDL05 for neurodevelopmental effects from the Faroese cohort 1 of 12 mg/kg in maternal hair (FAO/WHO, 2004, 4 ), resulting in 11.5 mg/kg maternal hair concentration causing no appreciable adverse effects in children.

This was converted to a maternal blood mercury concentration of 46 μg/L and, using a toxicokinetic model, further converted to a dietary mercury intake of 1.2 μg/kg bw. An UF of 2 for variation in the hair to blood ratio and a standard factor of 3.2 was applied to account for interindividual variation in toxicokinetics, resulting in a total uncertainty factor of 6.4 and in consequence a daily dose of 0.19 μg/kg bw that was converted to a weekly basis.

In the EFSA (2012) assessment, mean dietary exposures across age groups did not exceed the TWI for methylmercury (except Toddlers and Other children in some surveys). The 95th percentile dietary exposures were close to or above the TWI for all age groups. High fish consumers, which might include pregnant women, may exceed the TWI up to about six‐fold. Unborn children are the most vulnerable group. Biomonitoring data indicated that methylmercury exposure was generally below the TWI in Europe, but higher levels were also observed which is of concern. Dietary inorganic mercury exposure in Europe did not exceed the TWI, but inhalation exposure of elemental mercury from dental amalgam is likely to increase inorganic mercury exposure, and thus, the TWI might be exceeded.

In 2024, the US Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR) has issued a report on the Toxicological Profile for Mercury. 5 For inorganic mercury salts, the ATSDR has established an acute minimal risk level (MRL) of 0.002 mg Hg/kg bw/day based on a BMDLADJ 6 of 0.21 mg Hg/kg/day for elevated relative kidney weight in rats (Dieter et al., 1992, 7 ; NTP, 1993, 8 ) and applying an uncertainty factor (UF) of 100. The ATSDR has also established an intermediate MRL of 0.00001 mg Hg/kg/day based on a lowest observed adverse effect level (LOAEL) of 0.015 mg Hg/kg/day for decreased renal function and histopathological changes in the kidneys in rats (Apaydın et al., 2016, 9 ) applying an UF of 1000. No chronic MRL was established.

For methylmercury, neither an acute nor an intermediate MRL has been derived, but a chronic MRL of 0.1 μg Hg/kg bw per day was established based on a (no adverse effect level) NAEL of 0.41 μg Hg/kg/day for neurodevelopmental effects in humans from a meta‐analysis that included the outcomes of the Faroe Islands, Seychelles Child Development and North Island New Zealand studies (Axelrad et al., 2007).

In 2014, the EFSA Scientific Panel on Dietetic Products, Nutrition and Allergies (NDA) published a Scientific Opinion on health benefits of seafood (fish and shellfish) consumption in relation to health risks associated with exposure to methylmercury. 10 This was followed by a Statement on the benefits of fish/seafood consumption compared to the risks of methylmercury in fish/seafood, based on the 2012 CONTAM Opinion, published by the EFSA Scientific Committee (SC) in 2015. 11

In 2025, EFSA received a request from the European Commission for a risk–benefit assessment of fish consumption in relation to the combined presence of dioxins (PCDD/Fs) and dioxin‐like PCBs, perfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs), inorganic arsenic, dimethylarsinic acid (DMA), inorganic mercury and methylmercury.

In view of the above request, it is appropriate for EFSA to provide a scientific statement on the report from the US ATSDR on the Toxicological Profile of Mercury published in 2024, as to assess the quality and usefulness of the evidence provided in the report.

Additional information

Introduction

Mercury (Hg) is a metal released into the environment from both natural and anthropogenic sources. Once released, mercury undergoes a series of complex transformations and cycles between atmosphere, ocean and land. The three chemical forms of mercury are (i) elemental or metallic mercury (Hg0), (ii) inorganic mercury (mercurous (Hg2 2+) and mercuric (Hg2+) cations) and (iii) organic mercury. Methylmercury is by far the most common form of organic mercury in the food chain. Although Hg0 contributes to overall mercury exposure, diet is considered a negligible source of Hg0 (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2012). As a result, Hg0 was not taken into consideration in the present assessment.

In a separate request, the EFSA Scientific Committee (SC) is currently carrying out a risk–benefit assessment (RBA) assessing the potential health benefits of fish intake in relation to the combined presence of dioxins (PCDD/Fs) and dioxin‐like PCBs, perfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs), inorganic arsenic, dimethylarsinic acid (DMA), inorganic mercury and methylmercury 13 in fish. For these contaminants, except mercury, the assessments were done between 2020 and 2026. However, the last EFSA evaluation on inorganic mercury and methylmercury was issued back in 2012. The present statement provides an evaluation of whether the HBGV for inorganic mercury and methylmercury from the EFSA 2012 Opinion should be considered for the RBA, or if the more recent ATSDR MRLs should be considered instead.

In its scientific Opinion on the risks for public health related to the presence of inorganic mercury and methylmercury in food (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2012), a tolerable weekly intake (TWI) for inorganic mercury of 4 μg/kg body weight (bw), expressed as mercury, 14 was established based on kidney toxicity in rodents. An acute reference dose (ARfD) was not derived. For methylmercury, a TWI of 1.3 μg/kg bw, expressed as mercury, was established based on neurodevelopmental effects seen in human epidemiological studies.

In 2024, the US ATSDR has issued a Toxicological Profile for Mercury. For inorganic mercury salts, the ATSDR has established an acute minimal risk level (MRL) of 2 μg Hg/kg bw/day based on kidney effects observed in rodents. It has also established an intermediate MRL of 0.01 μg Hg/kg bw/day based on kidney effects in rodents. No chronic MRL was established for inorganic mercury. For methylmercury, neither an acute nor an intermediate MRL has been derived, but a chronic MRL of 0.1 μg Hg/kg/day has been established based on neurodevelopmental effects in human epidemiological studies.

Some conceptual differences between EFSA CONTAM Opinions and ATSDR's Toxicological Profiles are of importance. The EFSA CONTAM Panel establishes HBGVs for dietary risk assessment of contaminants, including ARfDs and tolerable daily intakes (TDIs) and, where bioaccumulation is relevant, tolerable weekly intakes (TWIs). The ATSDR in their Toxicological Profiles establishes minimal risk levels (MRLs) 15 and notes that ‘MRLs measure the length of time people are exposed to a chemical. The measurements are captured for 3 different time periods: Acute (about 1 to 14 days), Intermediate (from 15 to 364 days) and Chronic (exposure for more than 364 days).’ 16 EFSA does not apply the concept of an intermediate reference point (RP) in its dietary risk assessments.

It is also important to note that Opinions of the EFSA CONTAM Panel and Toxicological profiles of ATSDR do not have the same scope. While EFSA derives a HBGV for oral exposure to be compared with actual exposures to a given contaminant, the MRLs derived by ATSDR are intended to serve as screening levels, used by ATSDR health assessors to identify contaminants and potential health effects that may be of concern at hazardous waste sites. MRLs give indications on which sites to monitor in relation to potential health risks. Thus, while the Opinions of the EFSA CONTAM Panel are focussed on dietary risk assessment, the ATSDRs Toxicological Profiles assess chemical hazards across all relevant routes of exposure – inhalation, ingestion (dietary and water), dermal contact and environmental media like soil and water.

ASSESSMENT

Evaluation of the literature review from ATSDR

Scope of the literature review

The toxicological profile of ATSDR on mercury is not intended to provide a comprehensive review of all available literature. Instead, it aims to summarise the principal lines of evidence regarding health effects associated with occupational and environmental mercury exposure (ATSDR, 2024).

Eligibility criteria

The literature review was designed to identify studies examining health effects, toxicokinetics, mechanisms of action, susceptible populations, biomarkers, chemical interactions, physical and chemical properties, production, use, environmental fate, environmental releases and environmental and biological monitoring data for mercury (ATSDR, 2024).

The eligibility criteria reported for study inclusion were based on a generic categorisation of health effects and outcomes relevant to the objectives of the profile.

For epidemiological studies, well‐conducted and reported studies were considered for inclusion in the profile. Quality criteria were considered in selecting studies to include in the mercury profile and, in particular, for consideration as support for MRLs. In general, epidemiological studies that attempted dose–response assessments were included if the following criteria were met (a) reported estimates of variance in the dose–response metrics, (b) adjustments for confounding and (c) reported biomarker (of exposure) data. For studies used to derive MRLs, reporting of quality assurance of analytical methods was also required. For animal studies, eligibility criteria referred to well‐conducted and reported studies with the focus on relevant routes of exposure without further elaboration (ATSDR, 2024).

Literature searches

A comprehensive literature search was implemented, as outlined in detail below (ATSDR, 2022, 2024) with the aim to update the toxicological profile on mercury published in 1999 (ATSDR, 1999). The searches covered publications, peer‐reviewed articles and grey literature, published from 1998 and indexed into the databases up to October 2023.

The main bibliographic databases used were PubMed 17 and the Scientific and Technical Information Network's TOXCENTER. Toxline 18 was used for literature indexed from 1998 to January 2019; however, it was not employed in later searches, likely due to the discontinuation of public access in 2019. The National Technical Reports Library 19 was used instead for the 2022 and 2023 searches, although the scope of this database is different and focuses on U.S. governmental reports. Other resources, such as TSCATS, 20 the NTP website, 21 NIH RePORTER 22 and Regulations.gov, were used to supplement the database searches. The sources of information selected provide a comprehensive coverage of the possible relevant literature; details on their coverage can be found in Table 1.

All the search strings for each source of information update were reported in detail. The EFSA review of the search strings noted some inconsistencies over time. For PubMed, these inconsistencies can be due to changes in database features, such as the removal of the cancer subset 23 and attempts to reduce redundancy (i.e. deleting repetitive search strings), although some adjustments involving free‐text terms use are not clearly explained. In the case of TOXCENTER, different forms of mercury that have been included in the search varied depending on the timing of the searches. These findings show that slight changes have been made in the search terms used by ATSDR. Applying identical search terms for repeated searches would have been more consistent.

The EFSA evaluation of the search strings and selection of search terms also revealed additional points. PubMed searches primarily relied on controlled vocabulary 24 (Mesh) for the Medline subset and on free‐text terms 25 for the non‐Medline subset. Standard systematic review processes typically combine both approaches to minimise the risk of missing relevant studies due to limitations on how records are indexed with Mesh terms. This could have been further reduced by searching the Medline set in other platforms such as TOXLINE and TOXCENTER, though Medline appears to have been explicitly excluded. Additional Mesh terms could have been used to provide broader coverage of health outcomes of interest. In TOXCENTER, there is some ambiguity regarding why certain outcomes were searched only in specific controlled vocabulary fields, while others were searched more broadly. The search terms used reflected most, but not all, of the health outcomes included in the selection criteria. However, the search strategy included other combinations (e.g. the search of the mercury forms together with humans or animals) that could retrieve the relevant studies for those outcomes.

Decisions regarding the comprehensiveness of the search strings may have been influenced by the high volume of information retrieved, but this is not documented and its impact on the results is uncertain. Considering the combination of all available databases, supplementary sources and the opportunity to submit publications during public consultation and peer review, the risk of omitting significant information is likely low.

Study selection process and quality assessment

The study selection process is divided into two steps: screening titles and abstracts, followed by screening full texts of the selected studies. There is no information provided regarding whether the selection process was conducted in parallel with two reviewers.

Aside from the prioritisation based on study design and type of exposure mentioned in the selection criteria, further information about how the studies were assessed after inclusion is not well described. The search and study selection process is reported in a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) like flow chart. However, the list of included studies is not available.

Conclusions on literature review

The literature review process is described with varying levels of detail. While the search strategy is thoroughly documented, other key methodological steps, such as study selection and quality assessment, are insufficiently reported. Although the purpose of ATSDR (2024) is not to provide a comprehensive review of all available literature, the selection and evaluation of the studies are not clearly described. However, based on the methodology applied, it is unlikely that any major or relevant publications would have been missed. The main critical studies from EFSA 2012 were reported in the ATSDR profile.

Key studies used and reference points established for the assessment of inorganic mercury of EFSA and ATSDR

This section provides a detailed explanation of the basis for establishing the HBGVs for inorganic mercury by EFSA and ATSDR (Sections 2.2.1 and 2.2.2), followed by a comparison of the main similarities and differences in the derivation of these HBGVs (Section 2.2.3). A comparison of the critical studies by EFSA and ATSDR is also provided in Appendix 1.

EFSA's HBGV for dietary exposure to inorganic mercury

In 2012, the EFSA CONTAM Panel established a TWI for inorganic mercury of 4 μg/kg bw, expressed as mercury, thus following the provisional tolerable weekly intake (PTWI) of 4 μg established by the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee in Food Additives (FAO/WHO) in 2011. The kidney was considered the critical target for toxicity of inorganic mercury. Other identified targets include the liver, nervous system, immune system, reproductive and developmental systems.

Toxicological studies conducted by the National Toxicology Program (NTP) in 1993 provided important sources of information regarding the toxic effects of inorganic mercury. Mercury (II) chloride was administered by gavage in 16‐day, 6‐month and 2‐year studies to F344 rats and B6C3Fl mice, all of which primarily showed toxic effects on the kidneys. Doses of 0.312, 0.625, 1.25, 2.5 or 5 mg/kg bw/day were tested in the 6‐month study administered 5 days/week to rats. In this study, absolute and relative kidney weights were significantly increased in rats, starting at 0.312 (males) and 0.625 (females) mg/kg mercuric chloride, respectively, accompanied by minimal to mild nephropathological findings. Overall, rats showed greater sensitivity than mice. The CONTAM Panel considered the kidney weight changes in male rats in this study as the pivotal effect for deriving a HBGV. Based on the BMDL10 of 0.06 mg/kg bw/day, expressed as mercury and an uncertainty factor of 100 to account for inter‐ and intra‐species differences, with conversion to a weekly basis and rounding to one significant figure, the Panel established a TWI for inorganic mercury of 4 μg/kg bw, expressed as mercury.

ATSDR's MRLs for oral exposure to inorganic mercury

Acute duration (about 1–14 days)

An acute duration oral MRL of 2 μg Hg/kg bw/day was derived based on increased relative kidney weight in male rats exposed to mercuric chloride by gavage for 5 days/week for 16 days (Dieter et al., 1992; NTP, 1993). The MRL was based on a benchmark dose lower confidence limit for a 1 standard deviation (SD) response (BMDL1SD) of 0.29 mg Hg/kg/day adjusted to a BMDLADJ of 0.21 mg Hg/kg/day and a total uncertainty factor of 100 to account for inter‐ and intra‐species differences.

Intermediate duration (from 15 to 364 days)

The critical study, chosen by ATSDR for deriving the intermediate‐duration oral MRL (Apaydın et al., 2016), is a 28‐day study in adult albino Wistar rats. Six animals per group either received distilled water as control or 0.02 mg/kg bw/day mercury chloride by gavage (equivalent to 0.015 mg Hg/kg bw/day, only one dose tested). Reported effects in the mercury chloride group included decreased antioxidant enzyme activity (superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione‐S‐transferase, glutathione peroxidase) and increased levels of malondialdehyde in kidney tissue, increased levels of urea, uric acid and creatinine in serum as well as tubular dilatation and glomerular lobulation found in histological evaluations of kidneys. The ATSDR decided to set the intermediate‐duration oral MRL of 0.01 μg Hg/kg/day for inorganic mercury salts based on decreased renal function and histopathological changes in rats exposed to 0.015 mg Hg/kg bw/day by gavage for 28 days (Apaydın et al., 2016). The MRL is based on a lowest observed adverse effect level (LOAEL) of 0.015 mg Hg/kg/day and a total uncertainty factor of 1000 (10 for use of an LOAEL instead of a no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) and 100 to account for inter‐ and intra‐species variability).

Chronic duration (exposure for more than 364 days)

The ATSDR concluded that there are insufficient data for derivation of a chronic duration oral MRL for inorganic mercury salts since the lowest identified chronic duration LOAEL of 0.66 mg Hg/kg/bw/day for increased systolic blood pressure in rats exposed to mercuric chloride in drinking water for 1 year (Perry & Erlanger, 1974) is 44‐fold higher than the lowest intermediate‐duration LOAEL of 0.015 mg Hg/kg/day that is the basis of the intermediate‐duration oral MRL. Therefore, a chronic duration oral MRL was not derived for inorganic mercury.

Differences between the derivations of the 2012 EFSA HBGVs and the 2024 ATSDR MRLs

As mentioned above, the ATSDR derived an acute‐duration oral MRL for inorganic mercury from a 16‐day study in rats. ATSDR considers an exposure up to 14 days as being acute. EFSA, however, only derives an ARfD as HBGV for contaminants when the period of concern is 24 h or less. It has been shown in the NTP studies from 1993 that mercuric chloride accumulates in the kidneys. Considering that accumulation in the body likely plays a pivotal role in the toxic effects by inorganic mercury, EFSA does not consider a 16‐day study appropriate for the derivation of an acute HBGV for inorganic mercury as this would underestimate the ARfD value.

Additionally, ATSDR derived an intermediate‐duration oral MRL for inorganic mercury. In the context of dietary exposure, which occurs throughout the entire life span, an intermediate duration (of up to 1 year) is not considered specifically relevant. EFSA derives a tolerable daily or weekly intake (TDI, TWI) as HBGV which are estimates of the amount of substance in food or drinking water which is not intentionally added (e.g. contaminants) and which can be consumed over a lifetime without presenting an appreciable risk to health. The TDI or TWI is also protective for exposures of shorter (acute or intermediate) durations.

EFSA considers chronic exposure to inorganic mercury to be most relevant for the human population via diet and therefore set a TWI.

However, the ATSDR considered new evidence as being critical for their derivation of the intermediate‐duration oral MRL. Therefore, the study by Apaydın et al. (2016) was reviewed by EFSA in the present statement for its relevance to the setting of a chronic HBGV.

Differently from formally available studies by the NTP (1993), the study by Apaydın et al. (2016) focused only on renal toxicity lacking any information on general toxicity (e.g. food consumption, body weight, deaths per group, etc.) and did not follow standard testing principles. The study design has several limitations such as only one dose and control group tested, small sample size (6 instead of 10 animals tested per dose as suggested in respective guidelines) and no information regarding sex of the animals. Reporting is poor, as for instance, no quantitative information of histopathological results in kidney is given, and it is not reported whether or not sample blinding was implemented prior to evaluation, which would be an essential requirement to ensure the validity and integrity of the pathological assessment. Furthermore, Apaydin's histopathological findings in the kidney are difficult to reconcile with the results of the 1993 NTP study. In the 16‐day study in F344 rats (NTP, 1993), no signs of nephropathy were observed in histopathology up to the mid‐dose of 5 mg/kg bw per day mercuric chloride, which would be 250 times the dose used by Apaydın et al. (2016). Although the study by Apaydin had a longer duration (28 days) and used a different rat strain (albino Wistar rats), it is considered unlikely that these differences alone could account for such markedly divergent dose–effect relationships. The use of only one tested dose in Apaydın et al. (2016) prevents the evaluation of a dose–response relationship, which would have been necessary to substantiate their findings. Therefore, EFSA does not consider the study by Apaydın et al. (2016) suitable to derive a reference point (RP) for a HBGV covering dietary exposure.

Overall, taking into account all the above, EFSA concludes that an update of the 2012 EFSA risk assessment on inorganic mercury is not needed based on the results presented by ATSDR from 2024. The TWI for inorganic mercury of 4 μg/kg bw, expressed as mercury, is still considered valid. EFSA does not consider a 16‐day study appropriate for the derivation of an acute HBGV for inorganic mercury as this would underestimate the ARfD value.

Key studies used and reference points established for methylmercury by EFSA and ATSDR

This section provides a detailed explanation of the basis for establishing the HBGVs for methylmercury by EFSA and ATSDR (Sections 2.3.1 and 2.3.2), followed by a comparison of the main similarities and differences in the derivation of these HBGVs (Section 2.3.3). The section concludes with an overall discussion of factors that may explain the approximately twofold lower HBGV established by ATSDR as compared to EFSA's HBGV, and the robustness of the ATSDR assessment. A comparison of the critical studies by EFSA and ATSDR is also provided in Appendix 1.

EFSA's HBGV for dietary exposure to methylmercury from 2012

In the EFSA Opinion of 2012 (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2012), the HBGV was derived from two prospective birth cohort studies: the Seychelles Child Development Study (SCDS) Nutrition Cohort (Davidson et al., 2008; Lynch et al., 2011) and the Faroe Islands Cohort 1 (Grandjean et al., 1997). Both studies investigated associations between prenatal methylmercury exposure and subsequent neurodevelopmental outcomes in offspring.

For the critical study used from Seychelles Nutrition Cohort (Davidson et al., 2008), neurodevelopment was evaluated at 9 and 30 months of age using the Bayley Scales of Infant Development, Second Edition (BSID‐II). 26 Associations between maternal hair mercury concentrations and BSID‐II indices were examined using regression models that included simultaneous adjustment for docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and arachidonic acid (AA) in maternal serum. In these analyses, maternal hair mercury concentrations were inversely associated with the Psychomotor Development Index (PDI) 27 at 30 months; however, without adjustment for AA and DHA, the association did not reach statistical significance. 28

Based on a subsequent reanalysis of the same cohort data from the Seychelles Nutrition Cohort (Lynch et al., 2011), the CONTAM Panel (EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2012) concluded that at maternal hair mercury concentrations above approximately 11 mg/kg, ‘the beneficial effects associated with maternal n‐3 long‐chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (n‐3 LCPUFAs) 29 no longer outweighed adverse associations with methylmercury exposure’. EFSA considered this maternal hair concentration of approximately 11 mg/kg as an ‘apparent NOEL’. However, it was noted that the number of observations at higher exposure levels was limited. Furthermore, a follow‐up assessment of the cohort at 5 years of age (Strain et al., 2012), available at the time of EFSA's evaluation, did not demonstrate statistically significant adverse associations for methylmercury.

For the critical study from the Faroe Islands Cohort 1 (Grandjean et al., 1997), neuropsychological performance was assessed at 7 years of age using a comprehensive battery of cognitive and neurobehavioral tests. EFSA endorsed the BMDL05 of 12 mg/kg methylmercury in maternal hair that had previously been established from this study by JECFA (FAO/WHO, 2004), which in turn was based on a BMD analysis by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA, 2001). In the US EPA report, among the endpoints analysed with BMD modelling, the changes in the finger tapping test resulted in a BMDL05 value of 12 mg/kg methylmercury in maternal hair.

EFSA then used the maternal hair mercury concentrations from the Seychelles and Faroese studies (mean of 11.5 mg/kg maternal hair) as a reference point. Applying a hair‐to‐blood conversion factor of 250 and a one‐compartment toxicokinetic model yielded a corresponding steady‐state intake of 1.2 μg/kg bw/day. A composite uncertainty factor of 6.4 was applied (2 for hair‐to‐blood conversion variability and 3.2 for interindividual differences), resulting in a tolerable weekly intake (TWI) of 1.3 μg/kg bw, expressed as mercury.

ATSDR's MRLs for oral exposure to methylmercury

In their 2024 assessment, ATSDR reviewed the available evidence on methylmercury and neurotoxicity until October 2023.

To derive a RP, ATSDR relied on a meta‐analysis by Axelrad et al. (2007) which pooled linear regression slope estimates from three cohort studies reporting associations between maternal hair methylmercury concentrations and full‐scale intelligence quotient (IQ) in the offspring. The included studies were based on the same analyses of the Faroe Islands Cohort 1, which EFSA used as the critical study in its assessment (Grandjean et al., 1997); an earlier study from the Seychelles Nutrition Cohort assessing associations with IQ when the offspring were 9 years of age (Myers et al., 2003); and a study from New Zealand examining associations with IQ in 6‐ to 7‐year‐old children (Crump et al., 1998).

In the meta‐analysis (Axelrad et al., 2007), the linear regression coefficients were rescaled to full‐scale IQ units assuming a standard deviation of 15. They arrived at a pooled central estimate of −0.18 IQ points for each 1‐mg increment in methylmercury per kg maternal hair. ATSDR selected a critical effect size of a 1‐IQ point decrease, which, based on this slope, corresponds to a maternal hair concentration of 5.56 mg/kg. This value was converted to an intake of 0.41 μg/kg/day using a one‐compartment toxicokinetic model based on Albert et al. (2010), which relied on a conversion factor of 203 for hair to blood conversion. Based on an uncertainty factor of 3 to account for residual human variability, a (rounded) chronic minimal risk level (MRL) of 0.1 μg Hg/kg bw/day 30 was established. This corresponds to 0.7 μg Hg/kg bw/week, which is half of the TWI (of 1.3 μg/kg bw, expressed as mercury) established by EFSA in 2012.

Differences between the derivations of the 2012 EFSA HBGVs and the 2024 ATSDR MRLs

Because the ATSDR MRL is derived from epidemiological studies of developmental exposure published between 1998 and 2003, the updated review through 2023 does not appear to identify new evidence that would substantially alter earlier conclusions that neurodevelopment is the critical outcome. Accordingly, the main conclusion of ATSDR's hazard identification is largely consistent with the conclusions of the EFSA (2012) Opinion. However, there are several noteworthy differences between the 2024 ATSDR review and the 2012 EFSA Opinion with respect to hazard characterisation.

Selection of the critical neurodevelopmental endpoint

One difference between EFSA and ATSDR lies in the selection of the critical neurodevelopmental endpoint used for dose–response assessment. ATSDR used full‐scale IQ as the key endpoint, whereas EFSA relied on other individual or composite functional neurodevelopmental tests. This difference explains the agencies' selection of different key studies for quantitative dose–response assessment.

Both ATSDR (2024) and EFSA relied in part on the same original publication from Faroe Islands Cohort 1 (Grandjean et al., 1997). While EFSA based their reference point on the BMD analyses of individual functional tests, ATSDR relied on a combination of the three WISC‐R subtests (Digit Span, Similarities and Block Design), 31 which introduces some uncertainty. In the critical study used by ATSDR (Axelrad et al., 2007), the results from these WISC‐R subtests in the Faroese cohort were used to estimate a regression coefficient corresponding to full‐scale IQ. This estimation (or approximation) enabled pooling of the linear coefficients for full‐scale IQ with the coefficients reported in the New Zealand (Crump et al., 1998) and Seychelles (Myers et al., 2003) studies.

The use of a linear regression slope by ATSDR in the dose–response assessment implies the absence of a discernible threshold (or approximate NOAEL) within the observed exposure range. This indicates that measurable reductions in neurodevelopmental performance may occur even at low exposure levels typical of populations with little or no seafood consumption. However, based on the evidence presented in the ATSDR report, there does not appear to be strong evidence either supporting or refuting this assumption.

The critical effect size used by ATSDR was 1‐point decrement in full scale IQ. As a justification ATSDR noted that at the individual level ‘IQ losses ranging from 1 to 5 points are not significant for most children (the SD associated with IQ tests is approximately 5 points). These small decrements may represent meaningful intellectual and economic achievement at a population level’. This population‐level interpretation of adversity is supported by the literature (Bellinger et al., 2019; Griffiths et al., 2007) and has been used as a rationale for selecting comparable IQ‐based effect sizes in risk assessments conducted by EFSA CONTAM Panel (2010) and the NRC (2000).

EFSA, on the other hand, used different functional neurodevelopmental tests from the Seychelles and Faroe Islands cohorts that were administered in infancy and childhood. For the Faroese cohort, EFSA relied on benchmark dose (BMD) analyses previously performed by the U.S. EPA for several of the individual functional tests assessed at age 7 (Grandjean et al., 1997; US EPA, 2001). Using a benchmark response (BMR) of 5%, the resulting BMDL05 values ranged approximately from 10 to 15 mg/kg methylmercury in maternal hair. EFSA adopted the value of 12 mg/kg methylmercury in maternal hair previously used by JECFA (FAO/WHO, 2004), without providing further explanation for the selection of this specific value within the reported BMDL range.

For the Seychelles cohort, EFSA identified an ‘apparent NOEL’ of 11 mg/kg methylmercury in maternal hair based on the Psychomotor Development Index (PDI) derived from the Bayley Scales of Infant Development‐II (BSID‐II) assessed in 30‐month‐old infants. One limitation of this endpoint is that BSID‐II scores in infancy are generally considered less predictive of later cognitive performance than neuropsychological testing conducted at school age (Hack et al., 2005). Because the New Zealand study (Crump et al., 1998) was excluded 32 in the JECFA 2004 assessment which was the starting point for the EFSA 2012 Opinion, that study is not referred to in the 2012 EFSA Opinion.

At the time of EFSA's assessment, earlier findings from the Seychelles islands had generally reported no clear associations with neurodevelopmental outcomes, which was much debated at the time (Keiding et al., 2003; Lyketsos, 2003; Myers et al., 2003). The results considered by EFSA were based on a new cohort (the nutrition cohort), indicating potential adverse associations after adjustment for circulating levels of marine n‐3 long‐chain polyunsaturated fatty acids. However, indications of detrimental effects of methylmercury were not observed in later studies from the Seychelles nutrition cohort 1 (Conway et al., 2023; Orlando et al., 2023; Strain et al., 2012). The same conclusions were also reached in later studies from other Seychelles cohorts.

Hair to blood conversion and use of uncertainty factors

ATSDR reports that the relationship between hair and blood mercury concentrations has been modelled as linear, with estimated slopes ranging from 140 to 370 and a mean of approximately 250, and notes that the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends a hair‐to‐blood ratio of 250 for interconversion (ATSDR, 2024). That factor of 250 was used by EFSA. However, ATSDR used a factor of 203 based on the value suggested by Albert et al. (2010).

To account for uncertainty in the hair‐to‐blood conversion, EFSA applied an uncertainty factor (UF) of 2, in addition to a factor of 3.2 for interindividual variability, resulting in a combined UF of 6.4. By contrast, ATSDR applied a ‘total uncertainty factor of 3 for human variability’, but no UF was applied for hair to blood conversion (see Table A2 in Appendix 1).

New evidence contradicting and/or supporting the EFSA 2012 opinion

Although ATSDR's review of more recent evidence reached the same overall conclusion as the EFSA (2012) assessment, newer evidence provides less support for relying on the Seychelles studies to derive the reference point.

The Seychelles Child Development Nutrition Study (SCDNS), one of the two cohorts underlying EFSA's reference point of 11.5 mg/kg methylmercury in maternal hair, recruited approximately 300 pregnant women in 2001 and followed offspring to 5 years of age. Initial analyses adjusting for serum LCPUFAs suggested some adverse associations with neurodevelopment up to 30 months (Davidson et al., 2008; Lynch et al., 2011), but the 5‐year follow‐up did not confirm these associations, even after adjustment for fatty acids (Strain et al., 2012). Similarly, the larger Seychelles Child Development Study (SCDS), including 779 mother–infant pairs recruited in 1989–1990 and followed from 6 months to 24 years, reported no consistent adverse associations between prenatal methylmercury exposure and neurodevelopmental outcomes in analyses published both before and after the EFSA Opinion (Myers et al., 2003; Orlando et al., 2014; Strain et al., 2012; Van Wijngaarden et al., 2017; Van Wijngaarden, Thurston, et al., 2013; Van Wijngaarden, Davidson, et al., 2013; Wahlberg et al., 2018).

These null findings are acknowledged in the ATSDR assessment, which notes that ‘nutritional benefits of the relatively high fish consumption of the cohort may have weakened possible associations between measured neurodevelopmental outcomes and prenatal mercury exposure’. However, maternal hair mercury concentrations in the Seychelles studies were similar to or slightly higher than those in the Faroese cohorts, and based on the reported seafood consumption, intake of marine n‐3 PUFAs should be relatively comparable (Conway et al., 2021; Grandjean et al., 2001). Any protective effect of these nutrients would, therefore, likely apply to both cohorts. Consequently, the adversity of the Psychomotor Development Index from the Seychelles studies (Davidson et al., 2008; Lynch et al., 2011) has not been supported by subsequent findings from the Seychelles cohorts.

In contrast, studies from the Faroe Islands have consistently reported adverse associations between prenatal methylmercury exposure and multiple neurodevelopmental outcomes, with follow‐up extending into adulthood (Choi et al., 2014; Debes et al., 2016; Grandjean et al., 2014; Yorifuji et al., 2013). Similar associations have also been observed in Canadian Inuit populations with comparable exposure levels (Boucher et al., 2012, 2016; Ethier et al., 2012; Jacobson et al., 2015). In both cohorts, potential confounding by other marine pollutants (such as PCBs) was addressed through covariate adjustment.

Summary on differences between ATSDR and EFSA assessments on methylmercury

Taken together, the evidence linking prenatal methylmercury exposure with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, as assessed by ATSDR (2024), appears largely unchanged despite inconsistent findings in the Seychelles cohorts. The Seychelles results were already inconclusive at the time of the EFSA (2012) assessment and appeared sensitive to statistical adjustment for maternal LCPUFAs. In contrast, findings from the Faroe Islands, Canada and New Zealand suggest that exposure to methylmercury is adversely associated with neurodevelopmental outcomes in children, while other studies, particularly with lower exposure levels, are less conclusive.

The approximately twofold lower minimal risk level (MRL) of 0.10 μg Hg/kg bw/day established by ATSDR (or 0.7 μg Hg/kg bw/week), compared with the tolerable weekly intake (TWI) of 1.3 μg Hg/kg bw/week, expressed as mercury, established by EFSA, can largely be explained by differences in the selection of the critical neurodevelopmental endpoint and the dose–response assessment.

Evaluation whether an update of the 2012 risk assessment on inorganic and methylmercury is needed

A comprehensive review and assessment of all literature published since the last EFSA Opinion on inorganic and methylmercury, and an exhaustive analysis of all studies considered by the ATSDR was beyond the scope of this assignment. The present statement focused on a comparative evaluation of the key studies utilised by EFSA and ATSDR, respectively. The ATSDR profile reference list comprises around 800 citations of publications relevant to the hazard characterisation of elemental, inorganic and organic mercury, with more than 300 published after 2012, the year of the most recent EFSA evaluation. Thus, it can be inferred that considerable new scientific evidence regarding mercury has emerged since the previous EFSA assessment. Moreover, due to limitations in transparency and documentation of the ATSDR assessment of the new evidence, an independent EFSA assessment would be appropriate.

The EFSA Working Group (WG) considers an update of the 2012 Opinion appropriate given the advances in risk assessment methodology and the new evidence becoming available since then. However, an update is not needed based on the ATSDR report because the conclusions regarding the comparison of the HBGVs of EFSA and the MRLs of the ATSDR suggest retaining the EFSA HBGVs over those derived by ATSDR.

OVERALL CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

The literature review process is described with varying levels of detail. While the search strategy is thoroughly documented, other key methodological steps, such as study selection and quality assessment, are insufficiently reported. Although the purpose of ATSDR (2024) is not to provide a comprehensive review of all available literature, there is a lack of clarity on how studies were selected and evaluated. However, based on the methodology applied, it is unlikely that any major or relevant publications would not have been retrieved. The main critical studies from EFSA 2012 were reported in the ATSDR profile. For inorganic mercury, EFSA concludes that an update of the 2012 EFSA risk assessment on inorganic mercury is not needed based on the results presented by ATSDR from 2024. The TWI for inorganic mercury of 4 μg/kg bw, expressed as mercury, is still considered valid. EFSA does not consider the critical study used by ATSDR appropriate to derive an acute HBGV for inorganic mercury. For methylmercury, varying reporting approaches on the rationale for the methodological choices made during hazard characterisation appear to be the main driver of the differing guidance values. Based on the above, there is no clear indication that the MRL derived by ATSDR is scientifically more robust than the TWI previously established by EFSA. Consequently, the current EFSA TWI for methylmercury of 1.3 μg/kg bw, expressed as mercury, remains valid. Despite this, the advances in risk assessment methodology and the new evidence becoming available since the last EFSA assessment might merit an update of the 2012 Opinion.
