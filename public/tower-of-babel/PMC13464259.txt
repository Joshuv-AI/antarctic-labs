Transjugular Leadless Pacing in Massive Biatrial Dilation
Authors: Nora Wainstejn, Robert Hättasch, Gerhard Hindricks, Verena Tscholl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPermanent pacing in patients with severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) and distorted right heart anatomy is challenging, particularly when future valvular intervention is likely.Case SummaryA 75-year-old man with permanent atrial fibrillation, prior mitral valve repair, massive biatrial enlargement, severe secondary TR, and symptomatic bradycardia with chronotropic incompetence required permanent pacing. Because conventional transvenous lead implantation was expected to be technically difficult and potentially unfavorable in the setting of severe TR and possible future valve intervention, a leadless pacemaker was selected. Owing to extreme atrial enlargement and altered right heart geometry, implantation was performed via the right internal jugular vein to improve catheter control and facilitate septal deployment. The procedure was successful, and pacing parameters remained stable at 8-week follow-up.DiscussionLeadless pacing avoided a transvalvular lead and preserved future tricuspid treatment options.Take-Home MessageLeadless transjugular pacing is a useful option in patients with distorted anatomy who may require future valvular repair.

Pacing in patients with severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) presents significant clinical challenges. The presence of marked TR and atrial dilation often complicates optimal positioning of pacemaker leads, compromising pacing efficacy and stability. Furthermore, in cases in which tricuspid valve repair is planned, the presence of transvenous leads can increase procedural complexity. Lead implantation in this context may result in lead jailing, which is associated with an elevated risk of lead fracture, dislodgment, and potential difficulties in future lead extraction.1,2 In recent years, leadless pacemaker implantation has emerged as a promising alternative in this patient population. Growing clinical experience with leadless pacemakers has demonstrated lower complication rates than in the past. However, implantation remains challenging in patients with severe atrial enlargement secondary to atrioventricular valve regurgitation, necessitating special procedural considerations. In such anatomically complex cases, a transjugular approach may provide improved catheter control and facilitate septal positioning compared with the standard transfemoral access. We present a case highlighting these challenges and the practical application of transjugular leadless pacemaker implantation in this setting.Take-Home Message•Leadless transjugular pacing is a useful option in patients with distorted anatomy who may require future valvular repair.

Case Presentation

Patient information

A 75-year-old man was admitted for cardiac decompensation. The patient was referred from the general practitioner for elevated liver enzymes. His cardiac history was significant for mitral valve annuloplasty and mitral leaflet reconstruction, performed alongside left atrial appendage ligation, left atrial reduction plasty, and atrial ablation in 2014. He also had permanent atrial fibrillation, cardiac cirrhosis, and a history of surgical atrial septal defect closure in 1972. Additionally, he had known coronary artery disease, having undergone triple coronary artery bypass graft surgery (left internal mammary artery to left anterior descending artery, vein graft to first diagonal branch, right internal mammary artery to right coronary artery); concentric left ventricular hypertrophy; and arterial hypertension.

On presentation, the patient reported dyspnea and peripheral edema alongside exercise intolerance. Notably, he experienced significant weight loss of 20 kg over the past year accompanied by sarcopenia. Initial evaluation in the emergency department revealed pleural effusion, signs of cardiac decompensation, and intravascular hypovolemia. Laboratory investigations showed elevated bilirubin, acute kidney injury with an estimated glomerular filtration rate of 35 mL/min/1.73 m2, and pancytopenia as a secondary finding. The chest x-ray (Figure 1) showed an enlargement of the heart silhouette. Diagnosis

Permanent pacing was indicated in this patient owing to symptomatic bradycardia and chronotropic incompetence in the context of recurrent cardiac decompensation (class I, level B recommendation, European Society of Cardiology Guidelines).3 Rhythm control was not indicated because of severe biatrial enlargement and the high probability of recurrence. However, his complex cardiac anatomy and comorbidities posed significant challenges to conventional pacemaker implantation.

Echocardiography had revealed massive biatrial enlargement, severe transvalvular and paravalvular mitral regurgitation from a prior surgical valve repair, and secondary massive TR with a markedly dilated right ventricle. Given the possibility of future surgical mitral or tricuspid valve repair or replacement, the decision regarding pacing strategy required careful multidisciplinary evaluation.

Intervention

A leadless pacemaker (Micra; Medtronic) was selected for implantation owing to multiple clinical and anatomical considerations. Conventional transvenous lead positioning was expected to be technically challenging in the setting of markedly enlarged atria. Furthermore, we aimed to avoid additional lead-related complications following prior valve repair and to prevent further aggravation of TR caused by a right ventricular lead. The current European Heart Rhythm Association scientific statement on this topic recommends avoiding transvalvular leads in patients with severe TR whenever feasible.1 Given the patient's elevated infectious risk related to diabetes mellitus and multiple comorbidities, as well as an increased bleeding risk due to pancytopenia, a leadless pacing system was deemed the most appropriate and safest option. The decision was made aligned with the current European and American guidelines.3,4

Finally, vascular access strategy was a critical part of procedural planning. Although femoral venous access is standard for leadless pacemaker delivery, a right internal jugular venous approach was chosen to optimize catheter control in this massively enlarged atrium. The jugular approach offered improved steerability and facilitated secure septal implantation, while avoiding apical positioning, which carries a higher risk of perforation in the setting of right ventricular dilation.

Procedure details

Oral anticoagulation was interrupted 24 hours before the intervention. The procedure was performed under deep sedation with propofol, as is standard practice in our institution. Following puncture of the right jugular vein and a small skin incision, stepwise dilation was performed, allowing placement of a 27-F introducer sheath into the right atrium. A leadless pacemaker was advanced through the sheath into the right ventricle. Positioning proved challenging owing to marked dilation of the right atrium and ventricle, requiring several repositioning attempts until a satisfactory apicoseptal location was achieved. The septal placement was confirmed by fluoroscopic visualization of the characteristic “flamingo neck” curvature of the delivery catheter, indicating contact with the septum and by contrast agent injection (Figure 6). The device was deployed within the trabecular region, and adequate fixation was verified by a pull-and-hold test (Figure 7). Electrical parameters were sufficient. The introducer sheath was then removed, and the puncture site was closed using a double figure-8 suture.Figure 6Fluoroscopy Showing Contrast Agent Injection in the Right Ventricle to Confirm Correct Catheter PositionFigure 7Fluoroscopy Showing Leadless Pacemaker in the Final Position

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged 2 days after the procedure in stable condition. Five days later, he was readmitted with fever and methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia. The most likely infectious focus was a thrombophlebitis at the site of a previous peripheral venous catheter, manifesting with localized swelling and erythema. Ultrasonography confirmed thrombophlebitis of the left cephalic vein. Targeted antibiotic therapy with cefazolin was initiated, resulting in a rapid decline of inflammatory markers. Transesophageal echocardiography showed no evidence of endocarditis. Combined positron emission tomography and computed tomography was not performed, as the patient remained afebrile and infection-free both clinically and biochemically after completion of antibiotic therapy.

At the first pacemaker follow-up, 8 weeks after implantation, pacing parameters were stable and within normal limits. The patient reported improved exercise tolerance and stable heart failure symptoms without new signs of decompensation. He is currently awaiting the decision of the interdisciplinary heart team regarding potential intervention for mitral regurgitation and TR.

Discussion

This case illustrates the procedural and strategic challenges of pacemaker implantation in a patient with massive biatrial dilation and concomitant indications for tricuspid valve repair and permanent pacing. The decision to use a leadless pacing system was guided by several anatomical and clinical considerations and aligned with the current guidelines. Severe right heart dilation, distortion of intracardiac anatomy, and the potential need for future tricuspid valve intervention required an individualized approach that balanced feasibility, safety, and long-term management options. Yet the question remains whether this was the optimal approach in this complex scenario.

Leadless versus conventional pacing

A leadless device offered several advantages in this context. The absence of a transvalvular lead facilitates future tricuspid valve interventions by avoiding the risk of lead jailing, which can result in lead fracture or dislodgment or impede surgical access.1,2 With the rapid evolution of transcatheter tricuspid valve therapies, lead management is becoming an increasingly important consideration in patients with in need of pacemaker implantation. In this context, strategies that minimize transvalvular lead burden may gain relevance, particularly in patients at risk for future tricuspid valve intervention. Moreover, in this patient, placement of a conventional right ventricular lead would likely have been technically challenging given the marked dilation of both right atrium and ventricle, potentially requiring specialized leads and delivery systems. A previous case report has described successful transvenous lead implantation in similar anatomy after failed leadless device placement, suggesting that this may represent an alternative strategy when leadless fixation is not feasible.5 The current European Heart Rhythm Association scientific statement on this topic recommends avoiding transvalvular leads in patients with severe TR whenever feasible.1

Alternative pacing strategies without transvalvular leads, such as epicardial leads or coronary sinus pacing, were considered less favorable in this case due to the patient's high bleeding risk (related to pancytopenia, oral anticoagulation, and cardiac cirrhosis) and elevated infection risk associated with diabetes mellitus and multiple comorbidities. Indeed, the subsequent methicillin-sensitive S aureus bacteremia, which occurred without device involvement, underscores the potential benefit of a fully intracardiac system in this context, as any transvenous system would likely have required explantation.

Transfemoral vs transjugular access site

Although most leadless pacemaker implantations are performed via a transfemoral approach, a transjugular route was chosen in this case. The rationale for this decision was based on different considerations.

Achieving septal positioning of the device was a key procedural goal, as apical implantation in a dilated right ventricle carries a higher risk of perforation and pericardial tamponade.6 With the transjugular approach, the catheter naturally aligns toward the right ventricular apex; thus, extra care must be taken to ensure a true septal position. Saleem-Talib et al7 demonstrated that transjugular access results in a higher rate of septal positioning of leadless pacemakers compared with the transfemoral route, though the direct trajectory toward the apex theoretically increases the risk of perforation.7 In our case, correct septal placement was confirmed by fluoroscopic visualization of the characteristic flamingo neck curvature of the delivery catheter, indicating contact with the septum (Figure 8), and by contrast agent injection confirming septal engagement. Despite the patient's high bleeding risk, no major procedural or vascular complications occurred. Minor postprocedural bleeding at the access site was effectively managed by double figure-8 sutures and brief manual compression. The literature suggests that the transjugular approach may be associated with fewer access site complications compared with the transfemoral route. In a direct comparison of transjugular vs transfemoral access site, Saleem-Talib et al7 showed 2 complications in the transjugular group (none of them were vascular) compared with 16 in the transfemoral group.8 This likely reflects the more superficial course of the jugular vein and its safer anatomical relationship to the carotid artery compared with the femoral region where the artery often lies directly anterior to the vein.8

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that leadless pacemaker implantation via the transjugular approach is feasible and safe even in patients with extreme biatrial dilation and severe TR. The approach offers improved catheter control, facilitates septal positioning, and may reduce bleeding and vascular complications. Although technical challenges remain in navigating enlarged right-sided chambers, meticulous procedural planning, fluoroscopic verification, and contrast confirmation can ensure a secure and durable implantation. This case supports the growing evidence that transjugular leadless pacing is a valuable option in anatomically complex patients for whom conventional systems carry prohibitive risk.
