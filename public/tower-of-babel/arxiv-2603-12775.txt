A unifying approach to diffusive transport in heterogeneous media
Yann Lanoiselée, Denis S. Grebenkov, Gianni Pagnini

Abstract
We introduce the concept of Randomly Modulated Gaussian Processes as a unifying framework for modeling, analyzing and classifying anomalous diffusion models in heterogeneous media. This formulation incorporates correlations in the displacements together with correlated fluctuations of their amplitudes. Most known models of anomalous diffusion (including Continuous-Time Random Walk and fractional Brownian motion) and random diffusivity can be described and generalized within this framework. Moreover, the unified view identifies the main statistical properties to be probed experimentally for a reliable classification of diffusive dynamics. The proposed matrix formulation facilitates the computation of the first four moments and allows for a systematic statistical characterization of the considered processes. The necessary and sufficient conditions are provided for the emergence of anomalous diffusion. General expressions for the non-Gaussian parameter, ergodicity breaking parameter and covariance of squared increments are derived. Biological applications of this framework for systematic analysis and biophysical interpretations of experimental single-particle trajectories are discussed.



Diffusion in heterogeneous media is a generic feature of mesoscopic physical systems leading to deviations from classical Brownian motion. Spatial and temporal disorder in the local environment couples to thermal fluctuations, giving rise to anomalous scaling laws and non-Gaussian displacement statistics. Such behavior has been observed across a wide range of systems, including glassy and supercooled materials

Understanding diffusion under these conditions is essential for describing transport-controlled processes such as reaction kinetics, equilibration, and molecular signaling, with implications ranging from soft and condensed matter to biological physics.

Two major aspects of diffusion in heterogeneous media, which may occur independently, are the anomalous scaling of the mean-squared displacement (MSD)

Over the years a large variety of models have been introduced to describe these statistical features.
These models employ different mathematical tools such as Fokker-Planck equations, renewal theory, subordination, fractional derivatives, or Gaussian statistics that are difficult to combine to establish a unified framework for general correlated random walks in heterogeneous media.

In this Letter, we fill this gap and introduce a theoretical framework for diffusive motion in heterogeneous media that captures both anomalous scaling and non-Gaussian fluctuations within a unified description.
The proposed framework naturally incorporates correlations in the
displacements, non-Gaussianity due to medium heterogeneity, anomalous power-law scaling of the MSD, deterministic or random changes in statistical properties, and other features.
In the present general setting,
we introduce the concept of “randomly modulated Gaussian process” (RMGP),
in which the amplitudes of Gaussian increments are randomly rescaled by a stochastic process,
that extends the existing terminology
“randomly scaled Gaussian process”

61
.
This framework produces a sequence of successive random positions at discrete time steps that is the most natural, from a pragmatic point of view, for the analysis and interpretation of experimentally acquired trajectories and for computer-based simulations.
In other words, denoting by
\delta
a time step (fixed by the acquisition technique or chosen in simulations), we represent a random trajectory
X(t)
of the diffusing particle by an
N
-dimensional vector
X
, whose components
X_{i}
are the successive positions
X(i\delta)
at times
i\delta
, with
i=1,2,\ldots,N
. A matrix representation of Gaussian processes is used to incorporate correlations of displacements through a covariance matrix. In addition, the Gaussian jumps are rescaled to mimic heterogeneity as may be described by CTRW or random diffusivity models
48
;
15
. For clarity of presentation, we focus on one-dimensional settings but its extension to higher dimensions is immediate when the process coordinates are independent.

The construction of a random trajectory involves two sets of
N
random variables: (i) the
standard independent identically distributed Gaussian variables
\{\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{N}\}
to represent thermal noises, and (ii) the positive random variables
J_{i}
to mimic the effect of heterogeneity.
The random variables
\{J_{1},\cdots,J_{N}\}
are independent of
\{\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{N}\}
such that
\langle\sqrt{J_{i}}\xi_{j}\rangle=\langle\sqrt{J_{i}}\rangle\langle\xi_{j}\rangle=0
for any
i
and
j
; in turn, eventual correlations between the random modulations
J_{i}
are allowed. At this stage, the modulated Gaussian variables
\sqrt{J_{i}}\xi_{i}
are first-order uncorrelated,
\langle(\sqrt{J_{i}}\xi_{i})(\sqrt{J_{j}}\xi_{j})\rangle=\langle J_{i}\rangle\delta_{ij}
but second-order correlated:
\langle(\sqrt{J_{i}}\xi_{i})^{2}(\sqrt{J_{j}}\xi_{j})^{2}\rangle=\langle J_{i}J_{j}\rangle
for
i\neq j
and
\langle(\sqrt{J_{i}}\xi_{i})^{4}\rangle=3\langle J_{i}^{2}\rangle
reflecting the kurtosis of the Gaussian distribution
\langle\xi_{i}^{4}\rangle=3
. Next, we define the
n
-th increment as

Y_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\sqrt{C})_{nk}\sqrt{J_{k}}\xi_{k}
. In the classical case without modulations (i.e.,
J_{i}=1
), one has
\langle Y_{j}Y_{k}\rangle=C_{j,k}
, i.e.,
C
plays the role of the covariance matrix of Gaussian increments. In the presence of modulations, this is not true anymore, and
C
is used to induce first-order correlations of increments (in the following we still keep calling it ‘covariance matrix’). The positions are finally obtained by summing the increments:
X_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
. First-order correlations control the behavior of the MSD, while second-order correlations determine the fourth moment and thus control non-Gaussianity, as detailed below.
The model can be represented in a matrix form as

X=L\,\sqrt{C}\,\sqrt{J}\,\xi,

(1)

where
L
is a lower triangular matrix with entries
1
performing integration,
C
is the covariance matrix,
J
is the diagonal matrix containing random modulations, and
\xi
is the vector of standard IID Gaussian random variables.
As the positions in
X
should be in length units, we set
\xi
and
J
to be unitless, while
C
has units of a squared length.
The matrix representation (
1
), which is the key point of the paper, is very general and includes as special cases numerous models studied in the literature.
In particular, we retrieve: (i) Brownian motion (when
C\propto I
and
J=I
, where
I
is the identity matrix); (ii) Gaussian processes (when
J=I
), including fBm and GLE; (iii) CTRW and diffusing diffusivity models (when
C\propto I
). In fact, for CTRW,
J_{i}
can be understood as the number of jumps (or renewals) between two position recordings
(i-1)\delta
and
i\delta
, whose asymptotic PDF has been studied
59
;
5
. Alternatively,
CTRW and DD models
can be interpreted as Brownian motion with a random operational time
S(n)

39
;
14
;
32
, which is given in our framework as the sum of random modulations:
S(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}
.
The expectation and covariance of random modulations associated to CTRW are computed in Appendix A, while Appendix B presents the results for diffusing diffusivity modeled as the square of an arbitrary Gaussian process.

Other widely studied models can be recovered using RMGP.
For instance, setting
J=\mu I
, one can reproduce a statistical ensemble of random walks, in which each
trajectory is multiplied by a random scale
\mu
to describe anomalous diffusion and everlasting non-Gaussianity.
The two most studied examples are the grey Brownian motion (gBm)
57
;
58
and generalized grey Brownian motion
(ggBm)
48
;
47
,
that allow to retrieve a wide range of
fractional governing equations

54

including time-fractional diffusion.
Switching diffusivity (SD) corresponds to
modulations induced by a piecewise-constant stochastic process along the diagonal of
J
.
The random waiting times between switching events can be exponentially distributed
27
;
17
;
21
;
23
(SD-Exp) or power-law distributed
(SD-Pow)
45
;
46
;
3
;
49
. A special case is the Annealed Transit-Time Model (ATTM)
43
;
42
;
1
where the random state duration
\tau
is correlated with the random diffusivity
D
in the form
\tau\propto D^{-\alpha}
. Finally random modulations can be generated by a continuous diffusing diffusivity process. For instance, many works were devoted to diffusing diffusivity
with exponentially decaying correlations (DD-Exp) when diffusivity
fluctuates around its mean
15
;
14
;
34
.
While the above list is far from exhaustive, the diversity of the presented models reflects the complexity of experimentally observed diffusive behavior and highlights the need for a unified framework for their classification and comparison.

The RMGP defined through Eq. (
1
) provides such a unifying framework, organizing the realm of diffusion models within a three-dimensional parameter space (Fig.
1
). The first axis represents first-order correlations,
encoded in the matrix
C
, ranging from Markovian dynamics to exponentially correlated processes (e.g., Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process) and power-law correlated ones (e.g., fBm). The second axis characterizes the nature of modulations
J_{i}
, which may be deterministic (Bm, OU, fBm), a random constant (gBm, ggBm), a stochastic process with a positive asymptotic mean

\langle J_{\infty}\rangle>0

(CTRW-Exp, DD-Exp, SD-Exp, SD-Pow), or a freezing process with

\langle J_{\infty}\rangle=0

(CTRW-Pow, ATTM). The third axis corresponds to correlations of random modulations, which may be absent (CTRW-Exp), exponentially decaying (DD-Exp, SD-Exp), or power-law decaying (CTRW, ATTM, DD-Pow, SD-Pow).

The practical value of our unifying representation (
1
) goes far beyond the classification of known models. Various combinations of the physical effects can be realized by changing the properties of
C
and
J
. For example, DD modulation, which was originally proposed for Brownian motion, can be applied to
under-damped Langevin dynamics
65
;
61
,
the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
37
that we call
OU+DD-Exp in Fig.
1
, and to fractional Brownian motion
68
(fBm+DD-Exp).
As experimental data do not necessarily follow a particular model, the diagram in Fig.
1
invites rethinking the statistical analysis of the data beyond the restricted framework of specific models. While a lot effort was put into attributing experimental data to a specific model, we encourage to classify experimental datasets according to
the statistical properties of
C
and
J
, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.

The advantage of our discrete-time framework is
twofold: first, the matrix representation allows one to incorporate the building blocks of the dynamics in a general way and to get an exact derivation of its first four moments; second, the discrete integration is performed through multiplication by the
L
matrix, which is a simple operation that can easily be inverted (with
L^{-1}
corresponding to differentiation).

In heterogeneous media, the distribution of the position of a particle may display exponential tails. While models explaining exponential tails often suppose that the diffusivity varies in time, some universality results have
been derived based on CTRW with exponential waiting times
(CTRW-Exp)
4
;
67
. Our formulation reveals that CTRW-Exp is a process with random yet uncorrelated modulations (see Appendix A).
Upon time rescaling, RMGPs with fast decay of their modulation correlations (e.g., exponential or faster) can be matched to CTRW-Exp for which the tail behavior is known
60
.

To illustrate the efficiency and versatility of our framework,
we examine the respective roles of the three ingredients that are required to characterize the dynamics up to the fourth moment: covariance
matrix
C
, the expectation
\langle J_{i}\rangle
, and the
covariance of random modulations
\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})
. For this purpose, we compute exactly the MSD, the non-Gaussian parameter, the Ergodicity Breaking (EB) parameter, and the covariance of squared increments. The derived expressions will then be linked to the known models.

The covariance of the RMGP can be obtained by evaluating two averages: over thermal noises
\xi
and over random
modulations
J
(average over disorder), from which

\langle X\,X^{\top}\rangle=L\,\sqrt{C}\,\langle J\rangle\,\sqrt{C}^{\top}\,L^{\top}.

(2)

The covariance matrix of the process depends thus on
C
and on the expectation of modulations.
While the MSD vector can be directly deduced from the diagonal of the
covariance matrix, it is informative to represent the MSD at time

t=N\delta
as

\langle X(t)^{2}\rangle=\textrm{Tr}\left(\langle J\rangle U\right),

(3)

where
U=\sqrt{C}^{\top}S\sqrt{C}
with
S=L+L^{\top}-I

being a
N\times N
matrix with all entries equal to
1
. This expression shows that in our framework anomalous scaling can originate either from a power-law scaling of the covariance matrix (e.g., fBm), or from non-stationary random modulations whose expectation exhibits a power-law decay (e.g., scaled Brownian motion (sBm) or CTRW-Pow).
For instance, if random modulations are initiated from an equilibrium state such that

\langle J_{i}\rangle=\langle J\rangle
for all
i\in[1,\ldots,N]
, the contribution of
random modulations can be factored. Applying the random modulation to
fBm-type covariance of increments with a generalized diffusion coefficient
D_{\alpha}
and the Hurst exponent
\alpha/2
leads to the MSD

\langle X^{2}(t)\rangle=2\langle J\rangle D_{\alpha}t^{\alpha}.

(4)

We conclude that the fluctuations of random modulations around their mean play
strictly no role in the behavior of the MSD; however they are relevant
for non-Gaussianity (see below).

Next, we consider the non-Gaussian parameter

\gamma(t)=\frac{1}{3}\frac{\langle X^{4}(t)\rangle}{\langle X(t)^{2}\rangle^{2}}-1,

(5)

which quantifies deviations from a Gaussian distribution.
According to Eq. (
1
), for arbitrary
C
and
\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})
we get the general representation,

\gamma(t)=\frac{\sum\nolimits_{i,j=1}^{N}U_{ii}U_{jj}\,\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})}{\bigl(\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{N}\langle J_{i}\rangle U_{ii}\bigr)^{2}},

(6)

where
U
is defined just after Eq. (
3
).
This representation allows us to discuss the conditions under which non-Gaussianity
emerges, and those leading to long-time thermalization and ultimate convergence
to Gaussianity. Within the framework of RMGP, the necessary and sufficient condition
for the PDF to be non-Gaussian is to have random modulations; in turn,
if the modulations are deterministic, the PDF is necessarily Gaussian
(e.g., sBm). The correlations of the increments enter in the
non-Gaussian parameter through the matrix
U
.
Figure
2
.B
presents the non-Gaussian parameter for fBm,
DD-Exp, SD-Exp, sBm, and CTRW, and illustrates perfect agreement between theory and simulation.

For first-order uncorrelated displacements (
C\propto I
), one has
U_{ij}=\sigma^{2}
. where
\sigma
is a lengthscale. If moreover
\langle J_{i}\rangle=\langle J\rangle
and
\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})=f(|i-j|)
with a decreasing function
f
, Gaussian statistics is necessarily recovered as
t\to\infty
due to thermalization (see Appendix B). For exponentially correlated modulations,
f(|i-j|)=\exp(-|i-j|\delta/\tau)
, the second and fourth moments exactly coincide with those of DD-Exp and SD-Exp models
17
;
26
;
14
;
62
;
32
;
21
;
23
. In turn, for second-order uncorrelated displacements (
\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})\propto\delta_{i,j}
), one gets
\gamma(t)\propto(\delta/t)\,\textrm{var}(J)/\langle J\rangle^{2}
, yielding the characteristic
1/t
convergence to a Gaussian distribution
33
, with CTRW-Exp as an example (see Appendix A); exponentially correlated DD-Exp models, as well as non-Gaussian diffusion near surfaces
2
, display the same asymptotic behavior for
t\gg\tau
.

As random modulations play a crucial role in this framework, it is important to be able to access their covariance. In this context, we consider the covariance of squared increments, for which we get

\displaystyle\textrm{cov}(Y_{i}^{2},Y_{j}^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k,p=1}^{N}\textrm{cov}(J_{k},J_{p})G_{ik}^{2}G_{jp}^{2}\left(1+\frac{2G_{jk}G_{ip}}{G_{ik}G_{jp}}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle+2\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}G_{ik}\langle J_{k}\rangle G^{\top}_{kj}\right)^{2},

for
i\neq j
, and

\textrm{var}(Y_{i}^{2})=3\sum\nolimits_{k,p=1}^{N}G_{ik}^{2}G_{ip}^{2}\textrm{cov}(J_{k},J_{p})
for
i=j
, where
G=\sqrt{C}
.
Figure
2
.D presents this covariance for fBm, DD-Exp, SD-Exp, sBm, and CTRW. When the
displacements are uncorrelated (i.e.,
C\propto I
), this expression is reduced to
\textrm{cov}(Y_{i}^{2},Y_{j}^{2})=\sigma^{4}\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})

for
i\neq j
, and
\textrm{var}(Y_{i}^{2})=3\sigma^{4}\,\textrm{var}(J_{i})
for
i=j
, so that it gives a direct measure of the covariance of random modulations. In turn, when the modulations are deterministic (
\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})=0
) one has the amazing property
\textrm{cov}(Y_{i}^{2},Y_{j}^{2})=2\,\textrm{cov}(Y_{i},Y_{j})^{2}
independently of
C
and
\langle J_{i}\rangle
. This property can be tested on experimental data to uncover the nature of modulations.

When
C\propto I
, random modulations can be interpreted unambiguously as fluctuations of the diffusion coefficient. In turn, for correlated displacements, the physical interpretation of random modulations should be clarified. Suppose the random modulations originate from time-dependent diffusivity and the fluctuation-dissipation theorem holds. The diffusion coefficient
D=k_{B}T/\gamma
is the ratio of two quantities that may vary: the temperature
T
and the viscosity
\gamma
. When diffusion occurs in a potential
V(x)
, the drift term is rescaled by viscosity but is independent of
T
that only affects fluctuations. Therefore, our formulation corresponds to temperature fluctuations.

To conclude, we
proposed randomly modulated Gaussian processes as a general model of
diffusion in heterogeneous media driven by temperature fluctuations.
This approach unifies a broad class of anomalous and/or non-Gaussian diffusion. The related models are characterized up to their fourth moment by the knowledge of
three statistical quantities: the covariance matrix
C
determining first-order correlations, the
expectation of random modulations, and their covariance. Our representation clarifies the relationships among different physical models and explains why they
share some statistical properties and not others. The RMGP model in Eq. (
1
) can be viewed as a discrete
version of the integral representation of the continuous stochastic
process

X(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(t,t^{\prime})\sqrt{J(t^{\prime})}\,dW_{t^{\prime}}\,,

(8)

where
\gamma(t,t^{\prime})
is a memory kernel and
\sqrt{J(t)}
is a time-dependent random process.

This work opens the possibility to treat more general diffusive processes and may have a number of extensions as, for example, ggBm-like approaches for the whole spectrum of solutions provided by space-time fractional diffusion
51
, or
Lévy-flight-type dynamics
18

by allowing the random modulations to follow power-law distributions, yielding fractional Lévy stable motion when combined with correlated displacements
11
. In this case, the position moments diverge and alternative descriptors such as codifferences are required
73
.
While the present analysis is restricted to the first four moments, large-deviation approaches
4
;
67
;
50
may provide access to full distributional properties. Moreover, the RMGP framework suggests a link between diffusion in heterogeneous media and random matrix theory, opening the possibility of reconstructing
C
and
\langle J\rangle
directly from empirical data
30
;
31
. Future work will address extensions to heterogeneous media where the random modulations originate from viscosity fluctuations.

This work lays the theoretical ground for developing advanced statistical tools
toward unifying and classifying dynamics in single particle-tracking experiments in biology. Along this way, one can establish atlases of diffusion patterns depending on studied molecules, properly measure the impact of drug treatment onto diffusive transport, and ultimately uncover the principles driving molecular dynamics in living cells.

Appendix A
Modulation statistics from renewal counting process

In this section we discuss how to match a Continuous-Time Random Walk (CTRW) to a RMGP through the characterization of random modulations that occur in the CTRW.
In a standard CTRW, a particle executes independent random jumps, drawn from a given PDE
p(x)
, each jump taking an independent random waiting time, drawn from a given PDE
\psi(t)

44
.
To match this process with a RMGP we suppose that
p(x)
is Gaussian. In turn, many choices are possible for
\psi(t)
. We distinguish two scenarios: (i) when all moments of
\psi(t)
are finite (e.g. for an exponential distribution), the CTRW is known to be reduced to normal diffusion with a non-Gaussian distribution at very short times; (ii) when the mean waiting-time is infinite due to a power-law heavy tail of
\psi(t)
at long times, the CTRW leads to anomalous diffusion and remains non-Gaussian at any time.

We are interested in finding the expectation and covariance of random modulations
J(t)
that are required to describe the random position up to the fourth moment. We recall that
J(t)=J(i\delta)
is the number of jumps in the time interval
(\delta(i-1),\delta i)
. It is easier to start from the number
{\mathcal{N}}(t)
of jumps performed up to time
t
whereas the random modulations can then be obtained as

J(t)={\mathcal{N}}(t)-{\mathcal{N}}(t-\delta).

(9)

Note that the concept of operational time
S(t)
is very close to the number of jumps upon multiplication by the duration
T
of a single jump
S(t)={\mathcal{N}}(t)T
.
While the simulations are performed in discrete time, we derive the statistical properties of random modulations in the continuous-time framework. For conciseness we use the notation
J(t)
throughout this section keeping in mind that
J(t)
depends on
\delta
.

A.1
Expectation and variance of the number of jumps

To obtain the expectation and the variance of modulations, we evaluate the first and second moments of the number
{\mathcal{N}}(t)

of jumps realized during a time interval
(0,t)
.
Let
\tilde{\psi}(s)
be the Laplace transform of the waiting time density
\psi(t)
defined by

\tilde{\psi}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}\psi(t)dt.

(10)

Let
p_{n}(t)
be the probability of having
n
jumps:
p_{n}(t)={\mathbb{P}}\{{\mathcal{N}}(t)=n\}
. Using a standard renewal argument, one can evaluate the Laplace transform of this probability:

\tilde{p}_{n}(s)=[\tilde{\psi}(s)]^{n}\frac{1-\tilde{\psi}(s)}{s}.

(11)

As a consequence, we get the Laplace transform of the mean number of jumps is

{\mathcal{L}}\{\langle{\mathcal{N}}(t)\rangle\}=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}n\,\tilde{p}_{n}(s)=\frac{\tilde{\eta}(s)-1}{s}\,,

(12)

where
\tilde{\eta}(s)=1/(1-\tilde{\psi}(s))
, from which

\langle{\mathcal{N}}(t)\rangle=-1+\int\nolimits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\eta(t^{\prime}).

(13)

This
expression gives a useful interpretation for the function
\eta(t)
as a sort of effective rate of realizing a jump at time
t
. Similarly, one gets the second moment as

{\mathcal{L}}\{\langle{\mathcal{N}}^{2}(t)\rangle\}=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{2}\,\tilde{p}_{n}(s)=\frac{1-3\tilde{\eta}(s)+2[\tilde{\eta}(s)]^{2}}{s}\,.

(14)

To model a power-law-decaying waiting-time distribution we chose the Mittag-Leffler distribution with the PDF
\psi(t)=-\partial_{t}E_{\alpha}(-(t/T)^{\alpha})
, where
E_{\alpha}(z)
is the Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\alpha}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{n}/\Gamma(1+n\alpha)
, while
T
is a timescale for a single jump. For this choice, one has

\tilde{\psi}(s)=\frac{1}{1+(sT)^{\alpha}},

(15)

and thus

\tilde{\eta}(s)=1+\frac{1}{(sT)^{\alpha}},

(16)

so the expectation and the second moment are

\langle{\mathcal{N}}(t)\rangle=\frac{t_{1}^{\alpha}}{T^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha+1)}

(17)

and

\langle{\mathcal{N}}^{2}(t)\rangle=\frac{t^{\alpha}}{T^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha+1)}+\frac{2t^{2\alpha}}{T^{2\alpha}\Gamma(2\alpha+1)}\,.

(18)

For
\alpha=1
, these expressions give
\langle{\mathcal{N}}(t)\rangle=t/T
and

\langle{\mathcal{N}}^{2}(t)\rangle=t/T+(t/T)^{2}
so that the
variance of
{\mathcal{N}}(t)
is
t/T
, as expected for a Poisson
process. In turn, the
(t/T)^{2\alpha}
term is still present in
the variance for
\alpha<1
so that the standard deviation scales as

(t/T)^{\alpha}
, in the same way as the mean value, at large
t
.
In other words, the values of
{\mathcal{N}}(t)
are widely spread and not peaked around the mean in the anomalous setting.

A.2
Two-point moment

To compute the fourth moment of a random walker performing RMGP, one needs the covariance
\textrm{cov}(J(t_{1}),J(t))
with
t_{1}<t
. This requires the computation of the joint statistics of two random variables: the number
{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(t_{1})
of jumps in the interval
(0,t_{1})
, and the number
{\mathcal{N}}_{2}(t_{2})
of jumps in the interval
(t_{1},t_{1}+t_{2})
.
Let
P_{n_{1},n_{2}}(t_{1},t_{2})
be the probability of having
n_{1}
jumps in the
interval
(0,t_{1})
and
n_{2}
in the interval
(t_{1},t_{1}+t_{2})
. The double Laplace transform of this probability,

\tilde{\tilde{P}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}(s_{1},s_{2})=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt_{2}e^{-s_{1}t_{1}-s_{2}t_{2}}P_{n_{1},n_{2}}(t_{1},t_{2})

(19)

was obtained in In
35
(see Eq. (B9)):

\tilde{\tilde{P}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}(s_{1},s_{2})=[\tilde{\psi}(s_{1})]^{n_{1}}\frac{\tilde{\psi}(s_{2})-\tilde{\psi}(s_{1})}{s_{1}-s_{2}}[\tilde{\psi}(s_{2})]^{n_{2}-1}\frac{1-\tilde{\psi}(s_{2})}{s_{2}}\,.

(20)

We get
then the double Laplace transform of the two-point moment
Q(t_{1},t_{2})=\langle{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(t_{1}){\mathcal{N}}_{2}(t_{2})\rangle
under the form

\tilde{\tilde{Q}}(s_{1},s_{2})=\sum\limits_{n_{1},n_{2}=1}^{\infty}n_{1}n_{2}\tilde{\tilde{P}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}(s_{1},s_{2})=[\tilde{\eta}(s_{1})-1]\,\frac{\tilde{\eta}(s_{2})-\tilde{\eta}(s_{1})}{s_{1}-s_{2}}\frac{1}{s_{2}}\,,

(21)

where
\tilde{\eta}(s_{2})=1/(1-\tilde{\psi}(s_{2}))
. Note that the
double inverse Laplace transform of
\frac{\tilde{\eta}(s_{2})-\tilde{\eta}(s_{1})}{s_{1}-s_{2}}
is simply
\eta(t_{1}+t_{2})
.

For instance, if
\psi(t)=e^{-t/T}/T
, one has
\tilde{\psi}(s)=1/(1+sT)
and thus
\tilde{\eta}(s)=1+1/(sT)
, so that

\tilde{\tilde{Q}}(s_{1},s_{2})=1/(T^{2}s_{2}^{2}s_{1}^{2})
, from which
Q(t_{1},t_{2})=t_{1}t_{2}/T^{2}
. This is expected for an exponential (Poisson)
process.

For the Mittag-Leffler distribution, Eq. (
16
) implies

\tilde{\tilde{Q}}(s_{1},s_{2})=\frac{1}{s_{2}(s_{1}T)^{\alpha}}\biggl(\frac{1/(sT)^{\alpha}-1/(s_{1}T)^{\alpha}}{s_{1}-s_{2}}\biggr).

(22)

As the double inverse Laplace transform of the second factor is

(t_{1}+t_{2})^{\alpha-1}/(T^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha))
, we obtain

Q(t_{1},t_{2})
as the integral over
t_{2}
and the fractional integral over

t_{1}
:

\displaystyle Q(t_{1},t_{2})=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{t_{2}}dt^{\prime}_{2}\int\limits_{0}^{t_{1}}dt^{\prime}_{1}\frac{(t_{1}-t^{\prime}_{1})^{\alpha-1}}{T^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha)}\,\frac{(t^{\prime}_{1}+t^{\prime}_{2})^{\alpha-1}}{T^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha T^{2\alpha}\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}\int\limits_{0}^{t_{1}}dt^{\prime}_{1}(t_{1}-t^{\prime}_{1})^{\alpha-1}\bigl[(t^{\prime}_{1}+t_{2})^{\alpha}-(t^{\prime}_{1})^{\alpha}\bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t_{1}^{2\alpha}}{T^{2\alpha}\,\alpha\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}\int\limits_{0}^{1}dz(1-z)^{\alpha-1}\bigl[(z+t_{2}/t_{1})^{\alpha}-z^{\alpha}\bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t_{1}^{\alpha}(t_{1}+t_{2})^{\alpha}}{T^{2\alpha}\,\alpha^{2}\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}~_{2}F_{1}(-\alpha,\alpha;\alpha+1;1/(1+t_{2}/t_{1}))-\frac{t_{1}^{2\alpha}}{T^{2\alpha}\Gamma(2\alpha+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t_{1}^{2\alpha}}{T^{2\alpha}\,\alpha\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}\biggl[\bigl((t_{2}/t_{1}+1)^{2\alpha}-1)\frac{\alpha\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}{\Gamma(2\alpha+1)}

\displaystyle-(t_{2}/t_{1}+1)^{2\alpha}\frac{(t_{2}/(t_{1}+t_{2}))^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}~_{2}F_{1}(1-\alpha,1+\alpha;\alpha+2;t_{2}/(t_{1}+t_{2}))\biggr].

We conclude that

Q(t_{1},t_{2})=\frac{(t_{2}+t_{1})^{2\alpha}-t_{1}^{2\alpha}}{T^{2\alpha}\Gamma(2\alpha+1)}-\frac{(t_{1}+t_{2})^{\alpha-1}t_{2}^{\alpha+1}}{T^{2\alpha}\,\alpha(\alpha+1)\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}~_{2}F_{1}(1-\alpha,1+\alpha;\alpha+2;t_{2}/(t_{1}+t_{2})),

(23)

or, equivalently,

Q(t_{1},t_{2})=\frac{t_{1}^{\alpha}(t_{1}+t_{2})^{\alpha}}{T^{2\alpha}\,\alpha^{2}\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}~_{2}F_{1}(-\alpha,\alpha;\alpha+1;t_{1}/(t_{1}+t_{2}))-\frac{t_{1}^{2\alpha}}{T^{2\alpha}\Gamma(2\alpha+1)}\,.

(24)

For
\alpha=1
, we retrieve again
Q(t_{1},t_{2})=t_{1}t_{2}/T^{2}
.

A.3
Statistics of random modulations

The expectation of random modulations can be deduced directly from Eq. (
9
) and Eq. (
13
) as

\langle J(t)\rangle=\langle{\mathcal{N}}(t)\rangle-\langle{\mathcal{N}}(t-\delta)\rangle.

(25)

For an exponential distribution we have

\langle J(t)\rangle=\delta/T,

(26)

i.e., the modulations have a constant expectation.

For power-law waiting times with
0<\alpha<1
, Eq. (
17
) gives

\langle J(t)\rangle=\frac{t^{\alpha}-(t-\delta)^{\alpha}}{T^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha+1)}.

(27)

At large
t\gg\delta
, one gets

\langle J(t)\rangle\sim\alpha\delta\frac{t^{\alpha-1}}{T^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha+1)},

(28)

thus the mean number of jumps realized between
t-\delta
and
t
decays as a power law of time: the
particle is progressively freezing.

The second moment can be obtained as

\displaystyle\langle J^{2}(t)\rangle=

\displaystyle\langle({\mathcal{N}}(t)-{\mathcal{N}}(t-\delta))^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\left({\mathcal{N}}_{2}(\delta)+{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(t-\delta)-{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(t-\delta)\right)^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle({\mathcal{N}}_{2}(\delta)+{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(t-\delta))^{2}\rangle-2\langle{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(t-\delta){\mathcal{N}}_{2}(\delta)\rangle-\langle{{\mathcal{N}}}_{1}^{2}(t-\delta)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{{\mathcal{N}}}^{2}(t)\rangle-2\langle{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(t-\delta){\mathcal{N}}_{2}(\delta)\rangle-\langle{{\mathcal{N}}}^{2}(t-\delta)\rangle,

where
\langle{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(t-\delta){\mathcal{N}}_{2}(\delta)\rangle=Q(t-\delta,\delta)
, from which the second moment is

\langle J(t)^{2}\rangle=\frac{t^{\alpha}-(t-\delta)^{\alpha}}{T^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha+1)}+\frac{2t^{\alpha-1}\delta^{\alpha+1}}{T^{2\alpha}\,\alpha(\alpha+1)\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}~_{2}F_{1}(1-\alpha,1+\alpha;\alpha+2;\delta/t),

(29)

and the variance is

\textrm{var}(J(t))=\frac{t^{\alpha}-(t-\delta)^{\alpha}}{T^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha+1)}-\left(\frac{t^{\alpha}-(t-\delta)^{\alpha}}{T^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\right)^{2}+\frac{2t^{\alpha-1}\delta^{\alpha+1}}{T^{2\alpha}\,\alpha(\alpha+1)\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}~_{2}F_{1}(1-\alpha,1+\alpha;\alpha+2;\delta/t),

(30)

which decays as
t^{\alpha-1}
, just like the expectation.

We now consider the two-point average of having
n_{1}
steps in the interval
(0,t_{1})
and
n
steps in the interval
(0,t)
where
t=t_{1}+t_{2}
and
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
:

\displaystyle\langle{\mathcal{N}}(t_{1}){\mathcal{N}}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(t_{1})({\mathcal{N}}_{1}(t_{1})+{\mathcal{N}}_{2}(t_{2}))\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathcal{N}}_{1}^{2}(t_{1})\rangle+Q(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathcal{N}}_{1}^{2}(t_{1})\rangle+Q(t_{1},t-t_{1}).

(31)

As a consequence, we deduce

\displaystyle\langle{\mathcal{N}}(t_{1}){\mathcal{N}}(t)\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{t_{1}^{\alpha}}{T^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha+1)}+\frac{t_{1}^{2\alpha}}{T^{2\alpha}\Gamma(2\alpha+1)}+\frac{t_{1}^{\alpha}t^{\alpha}}{T^{2\alpha}\,\alpha^{2}\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}~_{2}F_{1}(-\alpha,\alpha;\alpha+1;t_{1}/t).

(32)

To obtain the two-point average of random modulations, we average the product of increments during
\delta

\begin{split}\langle J(t_{1})J(t)\rangle=&\langle({\mathcal{N}}(t_{1})-{\mathcal{N}}(t_{1}-\delta))({\mathcal{N}}(t)-{\mathcal{N}}(t-\delta))\rangle\\
=&\langle{\mathcal{N}}(t_{1}){\mathcal{N}}(t)\rangle-\langle{\mathcal{N}}(t_{1}){\mathcal{N}}(t-\delta)\rangle-\langle{\mathcal{N}}(t_{1}-\delta){\mathcal{N}}(t)\rangle+\langle{\mathcal{N}}(t_{1}-\delta){\mathcal{N}}(t-\delta)\rangle.\end{split}

(33)

Combining Eq. (
33
) and Eq. (
27
) we finally obtain the covariance

\begin{split}\textrm{cov}(J(t_{1}),J(t))=&\frac{t_{1}^{\alpha}t^{\alpha}}{T^{2\alpha}\,\alpha^{2}\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}(~_{2}F_{1}(-\alpha,\alpha;\alpha+1;t_{1}/t)-1)\\
&-\frac{(t_{1}-\delta)^{\alpha}t^{\alpha}}{T^{2\alpha}\,\alpha^{2}\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}(~_{2}F_{1}(-\alpha,\alpha;\alpha+1;(t_{1}-\delta)/t)-1)\\
&-\frac{t_{1}^{\alpha}(t-\delta)^{\alpha}}{T^{2\alpha}\,\alpha^{2}\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}(~_{2}F_{1}(-\alpha,\alpha;\alpha+1;t_{1}/(t-\delta))-1)\\
&+\frac{(t_{1}-\delta)^{\alpha}(t-\delta)^{\alpha}}{T^{2\alpha}\,\alpha^{2}\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}(~_{2}F_{1}(-\alpha,\alpha;\alpha+1;(t_{1}-\delta)/(t-\delta))-1),\end{split}

(34)

which, for small
\delta\ll t_{1}
, amounts to the double derivative of the last term in Eq. (
32
):

\textrm{cov}(J(t_{1}),J(t))\approx\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t_{1}\partial t}\left[\frac{t_{1}^{\alpha}t^{\alpha}}{T^{2\alpha}\,\alpha^{2}\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}(~_{2}F_{1}(-\alpha,\alpha;\alpha+1;t_{1}/t)-1)\right].

(35)

The covariance of random modulations for CTRW-Pow is not stationary.

In turn, the covariance for exponential waiting times (Markovian case) reads

\textrm{cov}(J(t_{1}),J(t))=\frac{\delta^{2}}{T^{2}},

(36)

which is a signature of an uncorrelated process. Therefore we conclude that random modulations for CTRW with exponential waiting times are random but uncorrelated; they constitute the simplest example of random modulations.

Appendix B
Random modulations as a squared Gaussian process

In this Section, we compute exactly the expectation and the
covariance matrix of random modulations when they stem from the square
of a general Gaussian process. A notable example is the square of an
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, which is a minimal model for fluctuating diffusivity with tunable correlations
14
;
62
. However, our result is much wider and allows for a variety of covariance structures, especially the stationary ones. Since the covariance structure of random modulations determines the rate of convergence to the Gaussian distribution (see Eq. (6) from main text and Sec.
C.1
here), it is useful to have access to a wide range of covariance structures including exponential, Gaussian, Matérn, or even rational quadratic.
A general Gaussian vector can be written as

Y=\mu+\sqrt{G}\eta,

(37)

where
\mu
is an expectation vector,
G
is a covariance matrix, and
\eta
is the vector of standard IID normal variables.
The vector of random modulations is postulated to be the diagonal vector of the matrix
YY^{\top}
:

J=\textrm{diag}(YY^{\top}).

(38)

The expectation of the matrix is

\langle YY^{\top}\rangle=\mu\mu^{\top}+G,

(39)

from which the mean random modulations are

\langle J_{i}\rangle=\mu_{i}^{2}+G_{ii}.

(40)

The covariance matrix of random modulations can also be computed
exactly. Setting
K=\sqrt{G}
, one gets

\displaystyle\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})

\displaystyle=4\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{N}K_{ik}K_{jk}+2\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}K_{ik}K_{jk}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=4\mu_{i}G_{ij}\mu_{j}+2G_{ij}^{2}.

(41)

The minimal model for diffusing diffusivity at equilibrium is obtained by choosing a constant mean
\mu_{i}=\mu
and the covariance matrix of an OU process. The
d
-dimensional case is also accessible by setting
Y
to be a
N\times d
matrix.

Appendix C
Additional results on Non-Gaussian parameter

C.1
The non-Gaussian parameter for some special cases

In this section we discuss some special cases of the non-Gaussian (NG) parameter from Eq. (6) in the main text. When displacements are uncorrelated, the matrix
C
is diagonal with elements
C_{ii}=\sigma^{2}
, where
\sigma
is a lengthscale. The quantity
\sigma^{2}
can be interpreted as the product of the diffusion coefficient and the timestep:
\sigma^{2}=D\delta
. Based on the definition of
U
just below Eq. (3), one can realize that all the elements of
U
take the same value
U_{ij}=\sigma^{2}
. The NG parameter thus simplifies to

\gamma(N)=\frac{\sum\limits_{i,j}\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})}{(\sum\limits_{i}\langle J_{i}\rangle)^{2}}.

(42)

When a RMGP has a constant expectation and a stationary covariance of the form

\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})=\textrm{var}(J)f(|i-j|),

(43)

the convergence to a Gaussian distribution as
N\to\infty
necessarily occurs.
This is the case for diffusing diffusivity at equilibrium for which both the expectation and variance of modulations are constant:
\langle J_{i}\rangle=\langle J\rangle
,
\textrm{var}(J_{i})=\textrm{var}(J)
. Under this condition, Eq. (
42
) simplifies to

\gamma(N)=\frac{1}{N^{2}\langle J\rangle^{2}}\sum\limits_{i,j}\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j}).

(44)

Note that if
J
is exponentially distributed, one has
\frac{\textrm{var}(J)}{\langle J\rangle^{2}}=1
, so that
\gamma(N=1)=1
. This value of the NG parameter corresponds to the Laplace distribution (i.e.,
p(x)=e^{-|x|/\alpha}/(2\alpha)
, with some
\alpha>0
). This distribution has been observed experimentally
66
and motivated the introduction of diffusing diffusivity models.

In general when the covariance can be written as in Eq. (
43
), one gets

\begin{split}\gamma(N)=&\frac{\textrm{var}(J)}{N^{2}\langle J\rangle^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}f(|i-j|)\\
=&\frac{\textrm{var}(J)}{N\langle J\rangle^{2}}\left(f(0)+2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{N}\right)f(k)\right)\end{split}

(45)

This quantity necessarily vanishes for the whole class of stationary random modulations, because
f(|i-j|)
is a decreasing function, so thermalization occurs. However the convergence rate to a Gaussian distribution depends on the correlation function
f(|i-j|)
.

For instance, if
f(|i-j|)=\exp(-|i-j|\delta/\tau)
with some correlation time
\tau
, we get by replacing sums with integrals and denoting
t=N\delta
:

\gamma(t)\approx 2\tau\frac{\textrm{var}(J)}{t\langle J\rangle^{2}}\left(1-\frac{\tau}{t}(1-e^{-t/\tau})\right).

(46)

The approximation becomes exact in the limit
\delta\to 0
while keeping
t=N\delta
constant.
This result corresponds exactly to diffusing diffusivity models
14
;
32
.
This result is also consistent with a model where the diffusion coefficient alternates between two values
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
through a Markov chain as in
23
;
17
, or even an arbitrary number of states
21
. In fact, the models DD-Exp and SD-Exp (when started at equilibrium) are indistinguishable up to the first four moments
32
.

A further simplification arises when random modulations are uncorrelated, in which case

\gamma(N)=\frac{1}{N}\frac{\textrm{var}(J)}{\langle J\rangle^{2}}.

(47)

We retrieve the
1/N
convergence to a Gaussian distribution, as observed for random uncorrelated diffusivity models
33
. This is also found for the long-time behavior of diffusing diffusivity models
14
;
62
;
32
and non-Gaussian diffusion near surfaces
2
.

C.2
Conditions for everlasting non-Gaussianity

However, it is also possible that the non-Gaussian parameter does not vanish. First when the PDF is obtained by superimposing trajectories where modulations are constant over time but randomly distributed across trajectories. This is notably the case of the generalized Grey Brownian motion that consists in an ensemble of fractional Brownian motions with random but constant diffusion coefficients. For each trajectory
m\in[1,\ldots,M]
, the random diffusion coefficient can be defined as
D^{(m)}=D_{0}J^{(m)}
, where
D_{0}
is a constant and
J^{(m)}
is a modulation that is trajectory-wise constant but randomly distributed among trajectories. In this case, we note that
\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})=\textrm{var}(J)
, and the non-Gaussian parameter is constant,

\gamma(N)=\frac{\textrm{var}(J)}{\langle J\rangle^{2}},

(48)

independently of the covariance matrix of displacements.

Everlasting non-Gaussianity may also hold when displacements are uncorrelated and random modulations are non-stationary,
with
\sum\limits_{i,j}\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})\propto(\sum\limits_{i}\langle J_{i}\rangle)^{2}
.
For uncorrelated displacements, RMGP can be viewed as a time changed Brownian motion
X(N)=B(S(N))
with a random operational time
S(N)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}J_{n}
. The expectation of the operational time is
\langle S(N)\rangle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle J_{n}\rangle
and its variance is
\textrm{var}(S(N))=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})
.
The NG parameter can therefore be recast as

\gamma(N)=\frac{\textrm{var}(S(N))}{\langle S(N)\rangle^{2}}.

(49)

To get everlasting non-Gaussianity, it is sufficient that
\langle S(N)\rangle\propto\textrm{std}(S(N))
. A noteworthy example is the CTRW-Pow for which, as shown in Sec.
A.1
, the expectation of the number of jumps has the same scaling
t^{\alpha}
as its standard deviation.

Finally, everlasting non-Gaussianity also occurs when the position has a stationary distribution and the correlation of its displacements decays faster than that of random modulations as seen for diffusion in a harmonic potential with fluctuating temperature
37
. The temperature fluctuation case studied in
37
can be recovered by a RMGP assuming equilibrium initial condition and zero mean for the position by choosing
C
that corresponds to the covariance of increments of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. Given the covariance of position of the OU process
\langle X(i\delta)X(j\delta)\rangle=\bar{D}\tau(e^{-|i-j|\delta/\tau_{x}}-e^{-|i+j|\delta/\tau_{x}})
, where
\bar{D}
is the diffusion coefficient and
\tau_{x}
is the position correlation time, one can deduce the covariance of increments:

C(i,j)=\langle(X((i+1)\delta)-X(i\delta))(X((j+1)\delta)-X(j\delta))\rangle.

(50)

The modulations correspond to the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross process as described in Sec.
E.5
.
In this case, the second moment
\langle X^{2}(t)\rangle
converges to
2\bar{D}\tau_{x}
at long times. In turn, the limit of the fourth moment is
\langle X^{4}(t\to\infty)\rangle=3\bar{D}^{2}\tau_{x}^{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\nu(1+\mu)}\right)
, where
1/\nu
measures the strength of diffusivity fluctuations and the initial shape of the distribution and
\theta
is half the ratio between the position correlation time and the diffusivity correlation time.
Therefore

\gamma(t\to\infty)=\frac{1}{\nu(1+\theta)},

(51)

which either vanishes when diffusivity is constant (
\nu\to\infty
) or when position correlation time is much larger than the diffusivity one
(\theta\to\infty)
(see
37
for a complete derivation).

Appendix D
Ergodicity Breaking parameter

When considering diffusion in heterogeneous media, it is important to
check whether a single trajectory is representative of the ensemble.
In other words, one should assess the ergodic behavior of the system. Within RMGPs, weak ergodicity breaking can be addressed in a general way. For this purpose, one can compare the MSD to its time-averaged counterpart, the Time-Averaged Mean-Squared Displacement (TAMSD) at lag-time
\tau
:
M_{N}(\tau)=\frac{1}{N-\tau}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N-\tau}(X_{n+\tau}-X_{n})^{2}
. To have ergodicity, two ingredients are required. First, the time average and the ensemble average should be equal in the long-time limit. Second, the time average should converge with probability one to the ensemble average, thus meaning the PDF of the time-average should converge to a Dirac delta function
\delta(M_{N}(\tau)-\langle X^{2}(\tau)\rangle)
as
N\to\infty
. The TAMSD can be
expressed as the quadratic form

M_{N}(\tau)=X_{N}^{\top}R_{\tau}X_{N},

(52)

where
R_{\tau}
is a matrix representing the
lag-differences
20
;
36
. The expectation can be computed using the trace trick and the law of total expectation

\langle M_{N}(\tau)\rangle=\textrm{Tr}(\langle J\rangle V_{\tau}),

(53)

where
V^{\tau}=\sqrt{C}^{\top}L^{\top}R_{\tau}L\sqrt{C}
and
L
is a lower triangular matrix with all non-zero elements being ones. Looking at
the first condition, the expectation of random modulations should
converge to a constant as
N\to\infty
. Another way to formulate this condition
is to say that the initial condition should not influence the long-time
behavior. As an example, the dependence of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck
process on its initial condition decays
exponentially fast. While the expectation of
\langle J_{i}\rangle
is not time invariant, the
process is ergodic in the weak sense because the asymptotic behavior of the MSD is independent of the initial condition.

To test the second condition, the ratio between the variance and the squared expectation of the time-average has been studied under the name of “Ergodicity-breaking parameter”, which can be computed within our framework.
From the trace trick and the law of total variance, one can show that the variance of TAMSD is

\textrm{var}(M_{N}(\tau))=2\textrm{Tr}(\langle JV_{\tau}JV_{\tau}\rangle)+\textrm{var}(\textrm{Tr}(JV_{\tau})),

(54)

where

\begin{split}\textrm{var}(\textrm{Tr}(JV_{\tau}))&=\langle\sum\limits_{i,j}J_{i}V_{ii}J_{j}V_{jj}\rangle-\langle\sum\limits_{i}J_{i}V_{ii}\rangle^{2}\\
&=\sum\limits_{i,j}V_{ii}V_{jj}\textrm{cov}(J_{i}J_{j}),\end{split}

(55)

from which one can express the ergodicity-breaking parameter for a lag-time
\tau
:

\mathcal{EB}^{\tau}=\frac{\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{N}(2V^{\tau}_{ij}V^{\tau}_{ji}+V^{\tau}_{ii}V^{\tau}_{jj})\,\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})+\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{N}2\langle J_{i}\rangle\langle J_{j}\rangle V^{\tau}_{ij}V^{\tau}_{ji}}{\left(\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{N}V^{\tau}_{ii}\langle J_{i}\rangle\right)^{2}},

(56)

where
V_{\tau}
is defined just below Eq. (
53
).
Figure 2.C of the main text shows perfect agreement between theory
and simulation for five representative models: fBm, DD-Exp, SD-Exp, sBm, CTRW. Usually,

\mathcal{EB}
is plotted for a fixed lag-time as a function of
N
. For uncorrelated displacements at

\tau=1
we have
V_{ij}=\delta_{ij}(1-\delta_{i1})\,\sigma^{2}/(N-1)
, such that
\mathcal{EB}
can be expressed as

\mathcal{EB}^{1}=\frac{\sum_{i=2}^{N}2(\textrm{var}(J_{i})+\langle J_{i}\rangle^{2})+\sum_{i,j=2}^{N}\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})}{\left(\sum_{i=2}^{N}\langle J_{i}\rangle\right)^{2}}.

(57)

When additionally the random modulations are stationary (i.e.,
\langle J_{i}\rangle=\langle J\rangle
and
\textrm{var}(J_{i})=\textrm{var}(J)
) we get

\mathcal{EB}^{1}=\frac{2}{N-1}\left(1+\frac{\textrm{var}(J)}{\langle J\rangle^{2}}\right)+\frac{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=2\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})}{(N-1)^{2}\langle J\rangle^{2}},

(58)

where the covariance of random modulations has been splitted into diagonal and non-diagonal parts.
The first term corresponds to the

1/N
decay, known for Brownian motion. The term
\frac{\textrm{var}(J)}{\langle J\rangle^{2}}
(the diagonal part of the covariance matrix) which accounts for uncorrelated random
modulations, does not change the scaling as a function of
N
but modifies its amplitude. The third term accounts for correlations of random
modulations, which may dominate at short time. For example, when the covariance decays exponentially, i.e.,

\textrm{cov}(J_{i},J_{j})=\textrm{var}(J)e^{-|i-j|\delta/\tau}
and
N\delta<\tau
,

\mathcal{EB}
can be almost constant. Therefore, TAMSD measurements on short trajectories displaying correlated random modulations are highly unreliable.

Appendix E
Simulation details

E.1
Continuous-time random walk (CTRW)

We consider a CTRW with a Gaussian jump PDF characterized by a diffusivity
D
and the Mittag-Leffler distributed waiting times with the parameter
\alpha
and timescale
T
(see Sec. I.A).
The CTRW has been simulated by taking a diagonal covariance matrix with elements
C_{ii}=D\delta
. The matrix
J
is constructed by generating a renewal process with Mittag-Leffler distributed waiting times with parameter
\alpha
(Sec.
A.1
for a definition). Then each modulation
J_{i}
is defined as the number of renewal events occurred between timestep
(i-1)\delta
and
i\delta
.
Parameters chosen are
D=1
,
\alpha=0.7
,
T=1
, and
\delta=1
.

E.2
Fractional Brownian motion (fBm)

We consider a fBm with generalized diffusion coefficient
D
and Hurst exponent
H
related to the anomalous diffusion exponent through
\alpha=2H
. The fBm is obtained by setting
C
to be the covariance matrix of the fractional Gaussian noise

C(i,j)=D\delta^{2H}\left(|i-j-1|^{2H}-2|i-j|^{2H}+|i-j+1|^{2H}\right).

(59)

The Cholesky decomposition of
C
is performed to obtain the square root
\sqrt{C}
in the form of a lower triangular matrix verifying
C=\sqrt{C}\sqrt{C}^{\top}
. The modulation matrix is the identity:
J=I
. Chosen parameters are with
D=1
,
\delta=1
, and
H=0.35
.

E.3
Scaled Brownian motion (sBm)

We also model sBm, a Markovian process with time dependent diffusivity
D(i)=D_{0}\alpha i^{\alpha-1}
, where
D_{0}
is the initial diffusion coefficient.
For sBm, the increments are independent so that the covariance matrix is diagonal with the elements
C_{ii}=2D_{0}\delta
. The modulations mimic the progressive cooling of the system through the deterministic relation
J_{ii}=\alpha i^{\alpha-1}
. The chosen parameter are
\alpha=0.7
,
D_{0}=1
, and
\delta=1
.

E.4
Switching Diffusivity (SD-Exp)

Diffusion with switching diffusivity alternates between two diffusion coefficients
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
with prescribed switching rates. SD-Exp is simulated by using a diagonal covariance matrix
C_{ii}=2D_{1}\delta
, the modulations alternate between two values. The diagonal elements
J_{ii}
are generated by a Markov chain dynamics, with a probability
p_{1}
to remain in state
1
with
J_{i}=1
and probability
p_{2}
to remain in state
2
with
J_{i}=D_{2}/D_{1}
. Initial state is chosen according to the equilibrium probabilities
p_{1}^{\textrm{(eq)}}=p_{1}/(p_{1}+p_{2})
and
p_{2}^{\textrm{(eq)}}=p_{2}/(p_{1}+p_{2})
.

E.5
Diffusing diffusivity (DD-Exp)

For DD-Exp we model the diagonal covariance matrix

C_{ii}=2\bar{D}\delta
, where
\bar{D}
is the equilibrium diffusion coefficient. The random modulations are obtained by rescaling the Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) process from
32
;
34
by
\bar{D}
, from which
J_{t}
follows

dJ_{t}=\frac{1}{\tau}(1-J_{t})dt+\sqrt{\frac{2J_{t}}{\nu}}dW_{t},

(60)

where
\tau
is the correlation time of random modulations,
\nu
controls the strength of fluctuations, and
dW_{t}
are the increments of the Wiener process. The diagonal elements of the random modulation matrix are obtained by simulation of the CIR process.
The simulation of CIR does not rely on an approximation scheme but takes advantage of the exact knowledge of the transition probabilities. The simulation is performed as follows:
The first modulation
J_{1}
is drawn from a Gamma distribution with the shape parameter
\nu
and the scale equal to
1
:

p(J_{1})=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)}J_{1}^{\nu-1}e^{-J_{1}}.

(61)

The subsequent modulations are obtained iteratively

J_{n+1}=J_{n}+\eta(J_{n},\delta)/(2c),

(62)

where
c=\tau(1-e^{-\delta/\tau})
and
\eta(J_{n},\delta)
is a non-central
\chi^{2}
random variable with the degree of freedom
2\nu
and the non-centrality parameter
2cJ_{n}e^{-\delta/\tau}

16
.

The parameters chosen for simulations are
\bar{D}=1
,
\nu=1
and
\delta=1
.
