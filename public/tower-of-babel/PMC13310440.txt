A study on the osteogenic effect of CGF membrane combined with bone graft in rabbit calvarial defects
Authors: Rong Ma, Liang Jiang, Yafeng Zhang, Weixuan Liu, Shile Liu, Yufei Zhang, Guangwei Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare the physicochemical properties, biocompatibility, barrier function, and bone regeneration performance of CGF membrane, heated‑compressed CGF membrane, and Bio‑Gide (BG) collagen membrane combined with bone graft in rabbit critical‑sized calvarial defects.MethodsIn vitro assessments included SEM, non‑enzymatic degradation, CCK‑8 assay, and Transwell migration assay using human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs). In vivo, 6‑mm critical‑sized calvarial defects were created in rabbits and assigned to five groups: blank control, bone graft (BO) alone, BO + CGF membrane, BO + heated‑compressed CGF membrane, and BO + BG membrane. Bone regeneration was evaluated by micro‑CT (BMD, Tb.N) and histology at 4th and 12th weeks. A linear mixed model was used to account for multiple defects per animal.ResultsAll three membranes showed favorable microstructure and biocompatibility, and significantly inhibited HGF migration. In vivo, BMD and Tb.N were significantly higher in the three membrane‑covered groups than in the blank and BO‑alone groups (P < 0.01), with no significant differences among membrane groups. Heated-compression did not improve functional performance.ConclusionWhen used with bone graft, CGF membrane yields similar bone regenerative structural outcomes to BG membrane in non‑load‑bearing calvarial defects. Direct osteogenic molecular markers (ALP, osteocalcin) were not analyzed. Conventional CGF membrane is a reliable autologous barrier candidate for small‑to‑medium defects (≤ 6 mm).

Bone defects represent a common yet clinically challenging condition, the repair of which is critical to functional recovery and quality of life in affected patients. Achieving efficient and stable regeneration in bone defect sites has long been a central focus in orthopedics, oral and maxillofacial surgery, and reconstructive medicine.

Currently, the most widely adopted clinical strategy for bone defect repair involves the implantation of bone substitute materials, such as autografts, allografts, or synthetic bone particles [1–3], combined with the use of barrier membranes to provide physical segregation [4]. This approach aims to maintain the defect space while guiding the preferential ingrowth of bone tissue [5]. An ideal membrane should possess the following characteristics: excellent biocompatibility [6, 7]; dimensional stability and high plasticity [8]; selective permeability [9]; and controllable degradation rate. However, none of the membranes currently used in clinical practice meet these “ideal” requirements.

Autologous Concentrated Growth Factor (CGF) contains various growth factors (GFs), platelets, leukocytes, and a fibrous network formed by fibrinogen, providing a matrix surface for cell adhesion and migration [10]. This allows CGF to remain integrated after transplantation into the defect site, thereby prolonging the activity of GFs [11]. The CGF membrane is prepared by compressing CGF gel, which rearranges the fibrin network into a more compact three-dimensional structure, improving handling characteristics and sustaining slow release of GFs compared with CGF gel [12].

This study compared CGF membrane, heated‑compressed CGF membrane, and BG membrane in a clinical simulation design combined with bone graft, focusing on microstructure, degradation profile, biocompatibility, anti‑fibroblast migration function, and bone regeneration efficacy. In this context, each experimental parameter was selected for its direct relevance to guided bone regeneration (GBR). SEM was used to assess the microarchitecture and porosity governing cell behavior. In vitro degradation provided insights into early structural stability. CCK-8 and Transwell assays evaluated biocompatibility and the physical barrier function against fibroblast infiltration. Micro-CT and histology visualized and qualitatively supported the extent and maturation of new bone formation.

In vitro experiments

SEM observation

The ultrastructure of three membranes was observed under scanning electron microscopy.

In vitro degradation

The 3 types of membranes were immersed in sterile EP tubes, with 10 ml of PBS buffer added, and placed in a 37℃ constant temperature box, replacing the PBS solution every day. The structure of the 3 membranes was observed at 2nd and 4th week using scanning electron microscopy to assess relative structural comparison.

CCK‑8 cytotoxicity assay

Three types of membranes were cut into 10 mm × 15 mm rectangles and immersed in 3 mL of DMEM culture medium containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics. The mixtures were stored in a 4℃ refrigerator for 24 h and 48 h respectively to prepare membrane extracts. Well-growing human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well, and cultured at 37℃ with 5% CO₂ for 24 h to allow cell adhesion. After adhesion, the original medium was discarded, and the wells were washed with PBS. The 96-well plate was returned to the incubator for continuous culture for 24 h. After the specified incubation period, CCK-8 solution and fresh DMEM culture medium were mixed at a volume ratio of 1:10, and 100 µL of the mixture was added to each well. The plate was incubated in the incubator for 2 h in the dark, and then a microplate reader was used to measure the optical density (OD) value of each well at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Transwell migration experiments

Using Transwell migration chambers, 3 types of membranes were cut to the size of the chamber bottom and laid flat on the bottom of the chambers, while the blank group had no membranes. 200µL of HGFs (1*104 /mL) without FBS was seeded in the upper chamber, and 600µL of DMEM culture medium containing 15% FBS was placed in the lower chamber. After 24 h of incubation in a constant temperature incubator, the membranes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Five fields of view were selected under a microscope for cell counting, and the average was calculated. (In this study, barrier function was strictly defined as the ability to inhibit HGF migration. No claims regarding permeability, pore interconnectivity, or selective exclusion were made.).

In vivo experiments

Research object

Eighteen male New Zealand white rabbits (provided by the Laboratory Animal Center of Ningxia Medical University, It has been approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University, Ethics Number: KYLL-2024-0040), weighing 2.5 ~ 3.0 kg.

Experimental methods

Preparation of rabbit CGF

Central auricular artery blood sampling for concentrated growth factor (CGF) preparation.Method same as experiment 1.2, CGF membrane, heated-compressed CGF membrane and BG membrane were infiltrated in normal saline for later use.

Animals and defect model

After general anesthesia takes effect (The hind legs of the rabbits were disinfected with 75% alcohol, and then Sumianxin II was administered intramuscularly at a dose of 0.2 mL/kg into the hind limb. After the onset of general anesthesia, the rabbits were placed in a prone position. The scalp and area behind the ears were shaved and disinfected with iodophor and 75% alcohol. A 2% lidocaine hydrochloride solution was diluted with 0.9% normal saline at a ratio of 1:4, and local infiltration anesthesia was performed at a dose of 0.2 mL/kg.), 4 defects of 6 mm size were prepared in the cranial of each rabbit at the upper left, lower left, upper right, and lower right positions, with at least 5 mm of bone tissue remaining between every two defects. The selection of a 6 mm diameter aimed to create a critical-sized defect model that would not heal spontaneously within the experimental timeframe, based on established criteria [13]. (Fig. 1). Each rabbit was randomly divided into groups using a random number table (source: SPSS26.0 built-in random number generator) with stratification by weight, and allocation concealment was maintained by an independent researcher not involved in surgery (via sealed opaque envelopes).

Postoperative treatment

Postoperatively, anti-lincomycin was injected for 5 days, iodophor disinfection was given to the surgical area. The rabbit feed was fed to observe the general condition of rabbit spirit, weight, diet and wound healing. During the observation period, 3 animals died(Decreased food intake and weight loss postoperatively, accompanied by infection, are likely attributed to inadequate aseptic technique during the procedure.). Replacement animals were randomly assigned by an independent researcher using the same randomization and allocation concealment protocol. Post-hoc analysis showed no significant increase in data variability. The final group sizes remained consistent (n = 3 for A, B,D, E group per time point, n = 4 for C group per time point, ), and data from deceased animals were excluded from analysis.

Eighteen rabbits were randomly divided into two groups, with each group being euthanized at 4th week and 12th week postoperatively (Following disinfection of the marginal ear vein with alcohol, the total calculated dose of pentobarbital sodium (390 mg/mL, 150 mg/kg) was administered intravenously in a rapid, single bolus. Within seconds, the animal lost consciousness, followed by muscle relaxation and respiratory arrest. Upon confirmation of maximal pupillary dilation, absence of pupillary light reflex, and cessation of palpable cardiac activity, cervical dislocation was performed by firmly stabilizing the head and applying a rapid, forceful traction and twist to the body to sever the spinal cord.). Specimens were collected for gross observation, micro-CT analysis, and histological examination using HE staining and Masson staining.

Statistical analysis

The data were statistically analyzed using SPSS 26.0 software. All continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̅ ± SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons, and repeated measures ANOVA was applied for time-point comparisons (4th week vs. 12th week). The significance level was set at P < 0.05, and exact P‑values and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for key parameters are reported throughout.

In this study, each 6‑mm bone defect was treated as an independent experimental unit for statistical analysis. Although four defects were created in the calvarium of each rabbit, they were separated by at least 5 mm, and randomization with allocation concealment was applied during grouping. To correct for the non‑independence of multiple defects per animal, a linear mixed model (LMM) with rabbit ID as a random effect was used. Specifically, for the primary outcome measures (bone mineral density [BMD] and trabecular number [Tb.N]), LMMs were applied to account for the hierarchical structure of the experiment, including multiple defects within the same rabbit and the partial sacrifice design. The fixed effects were treatment group (Groups A–E) and time point (4th weeks vs. 12th weeks); random effects were rabbit ID and defect ID (nested within rabbit). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05 for all tests.

SEM substructure (Fig. 2)

Under SEM, both the CGF membrane and heated-compressed CGF membrane exhibited a uniform fiber structure with consistent pore size (approximately 10 ~ 30 μm), forming a three-dimensional network. In contrast, the BG membrane displayed an irregular fiber thickness and heterogeneous pore distribution (approximately 5 ~ 15 μm).

In vivo experiments

Generally observed

All New Zealand rabbits regained consciousness from anesthesia post-surgery. Within the first 3 days post-operation, the rabbits appeared lethargic and ate less, but no significant weight loss occurred. By the first week post-operation, the incision sites had healed well, with no redness, swelling, blood oozing, or purulent discharge observed, and the sutures were removed.

Observing the osteogenesis of rabbit cranial (Fig. 5)

All incisions healed uneventfully without severe inflammation, infection, or membrane exposure. At the 4th week, no membrane residue was visible on the defect surfaces. Defect contours were clear across all groups. The central area of Group A (blank) appeared hollow and soft upon probing, with minimal peripheral bone ingrowth. Group B (BO only) presented with a palpable granular texture. Groups C, D, and E exhibited a hard texture and substantial bone tissue ingrowth. By the 12th week, defect margins became indistinct as surfaces were covered with a mixture of bone and soft tissues. Probing revealed a soft texture in Group A, firmer in Group B, and notably hard in membrane-covered groups (C, D, E). All groups showed increased tissue volume and firmer consistency compared to the 4th week.

Linear mixed models (LMM) were applied to evaluate the effects of treatment group and time on bone mineral density (BMD) and trabecular number (Tb.N). The results showed:

BMD: There were significant main effects of treatment group (F = 125.22, df₁ = 4, df₂ = 18.32, P < 0.001) and time (F = 44.00, df₁ = 1, df₂ = 5.72, P = 0.001), but the group × time interaction was not significant (F = 0.72, df₁ = 4, df₂ = 18.32, P = 0.591).Tb.N: Significant main effects were observed for treatment group (F = 201.08, df₁ = 4, df₂ = 22.00, P < 0.001) and time (F = 84.42, df₁ = 1, df₂ = 22.00, P < 0.001), while the interaction was not significant (F = 0.77, df₁ = 4, df₂ = 22.00, P = 0.559).

These findings indicate that both BMD and Tb.N were significantly influenced by treatment modality and time, with values increasing significantly from 4 to 12 weeks in all groups. The absence of significant group × time interactions suggests that the pattern of increase over the study period was comparable across all five experimental conditions.

Integration of histological and micro-CT findings

In the rabbit calvarial defect model, groups C (BO bone powder + CGF membrane), D (BO bone powder + heated-compressed CGF membrane), and E (BO bone powder + BG collagen membrane) demonstrated the highest bone mineral density (BMD) and trabecular number (Tb.N) values, with no statistically significant differences among them. However, all three membrane-covered groups showed statistically significant differences compared to the blank group (A) and the group treated with BO bone powder alone (B). Over time, BMD and Tb.N values increased significantly in all groups. Histological observations from HE and Masson staining were consistent with the quantitative micro-CT data. At 12 weeks, HE staining revealed more new bone formation, including mature osteocytes and Haversian-like structures, in groups C, D, and E than in groups A and B. Masson staining confirmed greater collagen fiber deposition in these membrane-covered groups, aligning with the micro-CT findings. Histological assessments were descriptive, and no histomorphometric quantification was performed.

Discussion

Determination of heat-compression parameters and defect model

Cai et al. [13] reported that heat-compression reduces CGF bioactivity, with prolonged heating increasing bioactivity loss while enhancing dissolution resistance. To balance these properties, a treatment condition of 90 °C for 5 s was selected in the present study based on their protocol [13] rather than systematic optimization. A critical limitation is that we did not validate growth factor activity or protein integrity of the heated-compressed CGF membrane; thus, findings for this membrane remain descriptive only. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that the heat-compressed membrane did not form a denser fibrous structure compared to conventional CGF, indicating that brief heating did not markedly alter its three-dimensional architecture. Furthermore, its in vitro and in vivo degradation profiles and barrier functions were comparable to those of untreated CGF and BG membranes, without significant superiority in barrier efficacy. The heat-compression process also requires specialized equipment and precise operational control, adding complexity to the preparation protocol. Collectively, these findings suggest that the marginal structural modification from brief heating did not translate into enhanced functional performance.

Regarding the bone defect model, a critical-size defect (CSD) is defined as the smallest defect that will not heal spontaneously within the animal’s natural lifespan [14]. Studies have confirmed that bone defects exceeding 5 mm in diameter do not heal spontaneously in the short term [15]. Accordingly, a 6 mm diameter defect was created in the rabbit calvarial model. The significantly lower bone mineral density (BMD) in the blank control group at 12th weeks confirmed that spontaneous healing was minimal in this study, validating the model for comparing membrane efficacy. At 12th weeks, BMD in membrane-covered groups (C, D, E) was significantly higher than in the blank group (A) and the BO-only group (B), indicating that both CGF and BG membranes enhance bone regeneration.

In summary, in the rabbit calvarial defect model, the combination of membrane materials (CGF, heat-compressed CGF, or BG collagen membrane) with BO bone powder promoted bone healing more effectively than BO alone or blank control. This was reflected in higher BMD and trabecular number (Tb.N) as well as more new bone and collagen deposition on histology.

Membrane structure, degradation, and barrier function

Autologous platelet concentrates (APCs), including PRP, PRF, and CGF, are prepared using different centrifugation protocols. CGF can be prepared as gels or membranes and is widely used in maxillofacial and oral tissue engineering [16–18]. Studies show that CGF enhances endothelial cell adhesion, promotes osseointegration, and reduces postoperative complications [19]. Combined with barrier membranes and bone graft materials in guided bone regeneration (GBR), CGF can mitigate bone resorption and improve regenerative outcomes [20].

In this study, gelatinous CGF was compressed into a membrane, with an additional group undergoing heat compression. A commercial BG membrane [21] served as control. Results indicated that the bone regeneration outcomes of CGF membrane, heated-compressed CGF membrane, and BG membrane were comparable, with no statistically significant differences. Although CGF membranes lack a bilayered design, their three-dimensional fibrin network effectively delayed cell invasion during the early healing phase.

Kabir [22] reported CGF membrane maintains integrity for 7 days in vitro. We did not perform mechanical testing; all observations were based on imaging and histological findings.

SEM observations revealed that both CGF and heated-compressed CGF membranes displayed a uniform three-dimensional fibrin network with consistent pore size, whereas BG membrane showed irregular fiber thickness and heterogeneous pores. Compression alone sufficiently organized the fibrin matrix, and brief hot pressing did not confer additional functional benefits.

In vitro degradation assays showed that both CGF-based membranes retained pore structure at 4th weeks, indicating reasonable structural stability during the early healing window. The progressive increase in BMD within each group from 4 to 12 weeks reflected ongoing bone mineralization and maturation, while the significantly higher BMD in membrane-covered groups indicated enhanced bone regeneration. Similarly, the increase in trabecular number (Tb.N) over time indicated improved trabecular connectivity and bone microstructure.

Therefore, CGF membrane is particularly suitable for small-to-medium non‑load‑bearing bone defects (diameter ≤ 6 mm) where early biological signaling is prioritized. For larger or load-bearing defects, additional structural reinforcement would be required.

Clarification of Barrier Function Terminology: In this study, “barrier function” specifically refers to the membrane’s ability to physically impede the migration of non-osteogenic cells (e.g., human gingival fibroblasts, HGFs) into the defect site, as demonstrated by the Transwell assay, thereby creating a protected space conducive to bone formation.

In vivo bone regeneration performance and temporal dynamics

In vivo, all three membranes were completely degraded by 4 weeks post‑implantation. In vitro degradation occurred in PBS without enzymatic or cellular components; thus, direct quantitative comparison between in vitro and in vivo degradation was not performed. The rapid in vivo degradation is likely driven by enzymatic and cellular processes: the material-induced inflammatory response recruits leukocytes and multinucleated giant cells [23], which secrete collagenases that degrade fibrin (CGF) and collagen (BG) [24], along with physiological factors such as pH changes and phagocytosis.

Although the physical barrier lasted only about 4 weeks, this period corresponds to the critical early bone regeneration phase (weeks 1–4), involving osteoblast recruitment, proliferation, and initial bone matrix deposition. Transwell assays confirmed all membranes significantly inhibited human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) migration. In vivo, membrane‑covered groups showed significantly higher BMD and trabecular number (Tb.N) at 4th weeks compared with uncovered controls, and bone regeneration continued to progress through 12th weeks. This indicates that the 4‑week barrier function provided a sufficient biological window to initiate osteogenesis.

CCK‑8 assays indicated relative growth rates (RGR) above 100% for all membranes, with CGF‑based membranes showing higher biocompatibility than BG membrane, attributable to their autologous origin and low immunogenicity [25]. These results suggest that all three membranes exhibited no cytotoxicity and possessed good biocompatibility. Heat compression did not enhance biological performance.

Transwell assays confirmed all membranes effectively blocked HGF penetration. In bone regeneration evaluation, CGF membrane, heated‑compressed CGF membrane, and BG membrane performed similarly in BMD and Tb.N, all significantly better than blank or BO‑only groups. Histological staining (HE and Masson) further confirmed enhanced new bone formation in membrane‑covered groups. Even after membrane degradation by 4th weeks, the bone regeneration trend continued through 12th weeks, underscoring that the early regenerative microenvironment established by the membrane is crucial for sustained bone healing.

Study limitations and clinical implications

The main limitation is the lack of mechanical testing (tensile strength, stiffness, collapse resistance), which precludes full physical characterization. Additionally, histological analysis was descriptive; histomorphometric quantification was not performed, and molecular osteogenic markers (e.g., ALP, osteocalcin) were not evaluated. The independent osteogenic effect of membranes without bone graft was not assessed. Furthermore, three animals died and were replaced, which may have introduced selection bias. The study remains preclinical with a small sample size. Future studies should include mechanical testing, histomorphometry, and clinical verification.

Clinical Relevance: The data support the use of conventional CGF membrane as a biocompatible alternative to commercial collagen membranes for the repair of small-to-medium, non‑load‑bearing bone defects (≤ 6 mm). Its autologous nature minimizes immunogenic risk. However, the findings do not support the use of CGF membranes for large defects (> 6 mm) or load-bearing bone defects due to insufficient mechanical data. This study only demonstrates comparable outcomes between CGF and BG membranes when used with bone graft.

Conclusion

When used with bone graft, CGF membrane, heated-compressed CGF membrane, and BG membrane provide similar bone regenerative outcomes in rabbit critical-sized calvarial defects.

Notably, CGF-based membranes exhibited higher relative cell proliferation than BG membrane, which may be attributable to their autologous origin, although the biological significance of this difference requires further clinical verification.

Given that heat compression added procedural complexity without improving performance, conventional CGF membrane appears to be a favorable bioactive material for bone defect repair. The advantage of CGF membrane lies in its ability to act as a temporary barrier during early healing while sustaining the release of bioactive signals.

In conjunction with BO bone graft, CGF membrane and BG membrane showed comparable bone regeneration outcomes in a rabbit calvarial defect model. Thus, CGF membrane may serve as a clinically viable alternative to BG membrane when used with bone graft in small defects (≤ 6 mm). Further studies are needed to evaluate its independent osteogenic effect.
