Allocating a scarce medical resource: liver transplant and lay individuals’ patient prioritisation
Authors: Siann Hirani, Lance Grove, Adrian Furnham, Alastair McClelland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is much debate on how scarce medical resources such as transplant organs should be allocated: in the UK, the NHS uses the principles of sickest first, prognosis and waiting list time – but not age [1]. Furthermore, it has recently been reported that in the UK younger patients tend to wait much longer than older patients for a liver transplant. The current study investigates younger and older lay people’s preferences for the allocation of liver transplants using four criteria,the patient’s age, time spent on the waiting list, pre-transplant life expectancy and post-transplant life expectancy.MethodsIn an online study, lay participants (N = 400) rated 16 hypothetical patients in terms of priority to receive an available donor liver, using only information on patients’ age, time spent on the waiting list, pre-transplant life expectancy and post-transplant life expectancy. The relative ‘importance’ of the criteria in making decisions was indexed using a standardised effect size measure (generalised eta-squared).ResultsPre-transplant life expectancy (‘sickest first’) was associated with the largest effect size, followed by post-transplant life expectancy (‘prognosis’), then age (‘youngest first’) and finally waiting list (‘first-come, first served’). In addition, participants assigned higher priority ratings to young patients, patients who had been on the waiting list for a long time, patients with a low pre-transplant life expectancy, and patients with a high post-transplant life expectancy. Both age groups prioritised younger over older patients, but younger participants prioritised younger over older patients to a greater extent than did older participants. There was no evidence for in-group bias. Additionally, younger participants showed a stronger prioritisation of patients with a long waiting time and patients with a low pre-transplant life expectancy compared to older participants.ConclusionsLay individuals were able to effectively use the four allocation principles in a practical scenario to assign priority ratings to potential liver transplant recipients. The findings are consistent with previous findings that lay individuals see ‘sickest first’ (pre-transplant life expectancy in this case) as being the most important criterion for the allocation of a scarce medical resource. However, the findings also reveal that both younger and older individuals support the notion that ‘youngest first’ is also an important criterion, something not currently reflected in the NHS allocation policy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01545-w.

Donor organs are scarce resources because the number of patients who need organ transplantation frequently exceeds the availability of donated organs [2]. This results in the challenge of deciding who should receive available organs [3]. Allocation decisions carry significant consequences since prioritising one patient over another for a transplant can determine their survival [4].

This paper concerns the allocation of scarce medical resources which is of concern to ethicists, medical practitioners and the general public and has attracted a great deal of research across disciplines and countries, particularly during the COVID pandemic [5–10]. We are not concerned with the clinical criteria used to place a patient on a transplant waiting list (details of the criteria used can be found in NHS England [11]) but rather patient selection for liver transplants once they are on a waiting list – a topic which has already attracted considerable attention particularly amongst clinicians and medical ethicists (e.g., [12, 13]).

Various schemes for the fair allocation of scare medical resources have been proposed (e.g., [14–16]). Persad et al. [17], in a celebrated and much quoted paper, discussed eight ethical principles for allocation, classified under four categories. Utilitarianism is a key philosophical theory, focused on maximising total benefits through decision-making, whereas Egalitarianism asserts equal worth for all individuals [18]. Persad et al. [17] identified two utilitarian allocation principles: ‘number of lives saved’ and ‘prognosis or life-years saved’, and two egalitarian principles: ‘first-come first-served’ and ‘lottery’ (i.e., random allocation). Beyond utilitarian and egalitarian principles, Persad et al. also discussed Prioritarianism as a critical consideration in resource allocation. Prioritarianism is concerned with prioritising the worst-off individuals, that is, prioritising the ‘sickest first’ and the ‘youngest first’. Persad et al. also considered the importance of promoting and rewarding Social Usefulness. This can be accomplished via the allocation principles of ‘reciprocity’ (i.e., rewarding past usefulness) and ‘instrumental value’ (i.e., prioritising future usefulness).

Recently, Emanuel and Persad [19] outlined procedural principles for allocating scarce medical resources: transparency, engagement, and evidence-responsiveness. Transparency involves making the weightings of ethical principles within allocation systems publicly available. Engagement requires the integration of lay individuals’ perspectives into allocation systems. Evidence-responsiveness entails regularly reviewing and adapting allocation systems in light of emerging evidence. To inform these procedural principles, current lay opinions of allocation principles must be ascertained. Support for the importance of engaging lay people in organ allocation decisions arises from the fact that transplantation is primarily funded by lay individuals [20], and Sypek et al. [21] argued that lay people are key stakeholders in organ transplantation, and their views should be respected. Additionally, the availability of donor organs is dependent on lay individuals’ willingness to altruistically donate their organs. Therefore, to increase future donor rates, it is critical that a publicly accepted allocation system is developed [3, 22, 23].

Lay preferences for allocating scarce medical resources

A number of researchers have investigated how lay people evaluate medical resource allocation principles. Some have been very small scale and/or qualitative in nature (e.g., , [24–26]). More recently, Krütli et al. [27] asked lay people, health professionals, and medical students to rate the fairness of allocation principles in three hypothetical scarce medical resource scenarios (kidney transplants, hospital beds, and joint replacements). In all three scenarios, lay participants rated ‘sickest first’ as the fairest allocation principle, followed by ‘first-come first-served’, and then ‘prognosis’. ‘Lottery’, ‘reciprocity’ and ‘instrumental value’ were the least favoured principles. Grover et al. [28] asked lay participants to rank ethical principles based on their perceived fairness in guiding the allocation of scarce medical resources. Consistent with Krütli et al. [27], ‘lottery’, ‘reciprocity’ and ‘instrumental value’ were ranked as the least fair principles. ‘Saves the most lives’ was the highest ranked principle, followed by ‘sickest first’. However, unlike Krütli et al., ‘youngest first’ and ‘prognosis’ were viewed as fairer than ‘first-come first served’. More recently, in partial replications of the Krütli et al. study, Yousef et al. [9] and Campbell-Hewson et al. [29] asked lay individuals (and other groups) to rank six ethical principles within three scenarios (Yousef et al.: hospital beds, cancer medication and transplant organ, Campbell-Hewson et al.: ICU beds, vaccine and transplant organ). As in the Krütli et al. study, for the transplant scenario lay individuals rated ‘sickest first’ as being the most important principle in both studies. In Yousef et al., ‘prognosis’ was given higher priority than both ‘first-come, first served’ and ‘youngest first’. In Campbell-Hewson et al., ‘first-come, first served’ was ahead of ‘prognosis’ and ‘youngest first’. The discrepancies in the findings may be due to methodological differences between studies. For instance, ranking principles required participants to prioritise principles in relation to each other, whereas rating principles involved participants to evaluate each principle independently. However, these studies all relied upon participants choosing from a set list of principles, with no opportunity for them to devise their own principles in open discussion. Oedingen et al. [30] rectified this by having focus groups decide on how to allocate organs without any provided list, and it was found that the participants (of their own volition) still opted for saving the greatest number of lives and treating the sickest patients first. Therefore, ‘sickest first’ is consistently given the highest priority – despite this being rejected by Persad et al. [17] as a valid selection criterion – something that has generated controversy (e.g., [31]).

Krütli et al. [27] and subsequent studies have investigated lay people’s evaluations of abstract principles of allocation. This may lack a sense of realism of allocating scarce resources to patients. Instead, it may be appropriate to assess directly lay people’s allocation preferences when faced with descriptions of individual patients. As opposed to evaluating abstract principles, evaluating patient descriptions may prompt individuals to consider the tangible impact of their decisions on patients, potentially providing a better understanding of lay beliefs regarding the fairest approach to allocating medical resources.

There have been a series of studies on ethical decision-making in which participants have been presented with descriptions of hypothetical patients (who varied on several, generally binary, characteristics) and asked to rank or rate them for treatment. For example, these studies revealed that lay people prioritised hypothetical patients for kidney failure treatment according to ‘instrumental value’ (e.g., patients’ intelligence) and ‘reciprocity’ (e.g., patients’ volunteering record) [4, 32–34]. Such studies have also found that lay people prioritise younger patients over older patients for a range of scarce medical resources, such as life-saving medical treatments (e.g., heart transplants), life-enhancing medical treatments (e.g., IVF), stigmatised treatments (e.g., HIV medication) and mental health treatments (e.g., anorexia treatment) [35–38]. This body of evidence suggests that lay people perceive ‘instrumental value’, ‘reciprocity’ and ‘youngest first’ as appropriate principles for medical resource allocation.

Howard et al. [39] investigated which principles were salient to lay people when allocating organ transplants to hypothetical patients. Importantly, participants favoured patients who were younger, had a longer waiting time, a lower pre-transplant life expectancy and a higher post-transplant life expectancy. This provides evidence that lay individuals prioritise hypothetical patients for organ transplants based on the ‘youngest first’, ‘first-come first-served’, ‘sickest first’ and ‘prognosis’ principles. Furthermore, they found that pre-transplant life expectancy was valued more highly than post-transplant life expectancy, suggesting that participants favoured allocation based on ‘sickest first’ more than ‘prognosis’, which is consistent with the findings from related studies [9, 27–30]. However, ethical values may vary between cultures and medical treatments as noted by Krütli et al. [27].

Several studies have investigated how organ allocation is evaluated by UK citizens. Ratcliffe et al. [26] found that British university employees prioritised post-transplant life-expectancy, followed by age and waiting time, when ranking allocation principles for allocating donor livers. Dolan and Shaw [24] found that UK focus group participants agreed that younger patients and patients with a low pre-transplant life expectancy should be prioritised for the allocation of kidney transplants. Waiting time was reported as being less important. However, discussion within the group may have altered the views of the participants, hence the apparent consensus in beliefs may be representing social desirability rather than true agreement. Nonetheless, Gibbons et al. [25] interviewed individuals from the UK who were waiting for or had received a kidney transplant and similarly found support for prioritising younger patients over the ‘first-come first served’ principle. However, these studies used small samples, and Gibbons et al. [25] involved patients rather than lay people. It is evident that there has been very little research into lay people’s preferences for organ allocation in the UK.

In Germany, Oedingen et al. [30] found that the focus groups in their study failed to agree on how to use the principles for multiple eligible patients and instead concluded that a combination would be the fairest. They also found that, despite participants not wanting to allocate organs based on age, younger patients were preferred because they were likely to have a better post-transplant life expectancy and because they had lived shorter lives. Similarly, in a study by Stahl et al. [40], US participants were asked to choose between pairs of hypothetical patients to receive an organ transplant. They found that patients with a lower post-transplant life expectancy needed a lower pre-transplant life expectancy in order to receive priority compared with patients with a higher post-transplant life expectancy. Moreover, older patients required a lower pre-transplant life expectancy to receive priority than did younger patients. These results underscore the integration of multiple patient characteristics in determining priority judgements, which many studies have failed to acknowledge.

‘Youngest first’ principle

Previous research findings suggest that lay people support the allocation of organ transplants based on the ‘youngest first’ principle [35–39]. However, there is evidence to suggest that in the UK, the allocation of liver transplants by the NHS currently prioritises older individuals, younger people wait, on average, 156 days longer for liver transplants than those over 60 [41].

There is some evidence to suggest that lay people’s own age influences their support of the ‘youngest first’ allocation principle. Diederich et al. [42] found that German individuals aged 18 to 29 tended to agree with the statement that ‘younger patients should be preferred to older patients’ in the allocation of donor kidneys, whereas individuals aged 30 to 80 tended to disagree with the same statement. Thus, young individuals favour the ‘youngest first’ allocation principle, whereas old individuals appear to disagree with it. This may indicate an in-group bias. Werntoft et al. [43] found that in interviews with elderly Swedish individuals (aged 66–100), 84% of the participants said neither young nor old patients should be prioritised for life-saving treatment; 8% favoured younger patients, and 8% favoured older patients. This suggests that old individuals do not necessarily support medical resource allocation by patients’ age. The current study explores whether lay people demonstrate an in-group allocation bias based on age.

Current study

The first aim of the current study is to investigate whether lay people in the UK agree with ‘youngest first’, ‘waiting time’, ‘sickest first’ and ‘prognosis’ as being important principles in allocating a liver for transplant – and the relative importance they attach to each principle. The second aim is to investigate whether patient characteristics interact in determining lay perceptions of priority (see [40]). The third aim of the current study is to investigate whether lay people’s age influences their support for the ‘youngest first’ principle with respect to liver transplant, given the apparent bias in the NHS in favour of older patients [41]. Participants were presented with a hypothetical scenario in which one liver is available for transplant, and a list of hypothetical patients to rate for priority to receive the organ. The hypothetical patient descriptions varied with respect to their age, time spent on the waiting list, pre-transplant life expectancy, and post-transplant life expectancy.

Method

Participants

Four-hundred participants were recruited online via Prolific (https://www.prolific.com/). Recruitment involved setting eligibility criteria on Prolific (i.e., ≥ 18 years old, living in the UK). Participants accepted invites from Prolific to voluntarily enrol in the current study and were compensated for their time. Enrolled participants were aged between 21 and 80 years (M = 44.10, SD = 15.65). One hundred and ninety-eight were female, 199 were male, two were other, and one preferred not to say. From the ethnicity data available from 394 participants, 340 were White, 22 were Asian, 16 were Black, 15 were Mixed, and one was Other. The participants in the sample were closely representative of the UK population in terms of ethnicity, with 82.0% of the UK population being White, and 86.0% of the current sample being White, and with respect to gender, with 51.0% of the UK population being female, and 49.5% of the current sample being female [44]. Participants were categorised into two age groups. Two-hundred participants were aged between 21 and 35 years (M = 29.54, SD = 3.97), 98 were female, 100 were male, one was other, and one preferred not to say. Two-hundred participants were aged between 46 and 80 years (M = 58.66, SD = 7.04), 100 were female, 99 were male, and one was other. A post hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power [45] and this indicated that the power to detect moderate effect sizes (ƒ = 0.25) with N = 400 at a significance level = 0.05 was 99.9%. Ethical approval was granted by the UCL Department of Experimental Psychology Ethics Committee (Number: EP/2018/007).

Design and materials

The between-subject variable was the age of the participants with two levels: younger (21–35 years old) and older (46–80 years old). The four within-subject variables were patient characteristics, each with two levels: age (younger 20–25 years vs. older 55–60 years), waiting time (6 months vs. 5 years), pre-transplant life expectancy (1–2 years vs. 7–8 years), and post-transplant life expectancy (10–12 years vs. 20–25 years). These four variables map onto ‘youngest first’, ‘first-come, first served’, ‘sickest first’ and ‘prognosis’ respectively. Sixteen hypothetical patient descriptions were created by uniquely combining the two levels of the four patients (see Supplementary Material). Randomly generated initials preceded patient descriptions to avoid assigning a gender or race to patients, to mitigate any potential gender or race bias. Participants rated the hypothetical patients for priority to receive a liver transplant on a Likert scale from 1 (“they should be given very low priority”) to 7 (“they should be given very high priority”).

Procedure

All participants completed the study online, via a computer, laptop, or mobile phone. The tasks were completed without a time limit to ensure that participants carefully considered their responses. Firstly, participants were presented with an information sheet and consent form. Once consent was given, participants reported their age and gender. They were then presented with the hypothetical scenario. They were told about the nationwide shortage of liver donors and that an NHS allocation committee decides who should receive these organs for transplant. Participants were asked to imagine that they were a member of this committee, and that one new liver had become available for transplantation. Participants were informed that their task was to rate 16 hypothetical patients who are waiting for a liver transplant on how much priority they believe each patient should receive for the transplantation. One patient description was presented at a time, each alongside a priority rating scale (see Fig. 1). Participants were randomly assigned to one of five groups, each presented with patient descriptions in a different random order to control for order effects. Discussion

Consistent with our hypotheses, participants prioritised: 1) younger patients over older patients, 2) patients with a long waiting time over patients with a short waiting time, 3) patients with a low pre-transplant life expectancy over patients with a high pre-transplant life expectancy, and 4) patients with a high post-transplant life expectancy over patients with a low post-transplant life expectancy following a liver transplant. These findings mirror those of Howard et al. [39], who found that Australian lay people preferentially allocated kidney transplants to hypothetical patients who were younger, had a longer waiting time, a lower pre-transplant life expectancy, and a higher post-transplant life expectancy. Thus, the current study extends these findings to the allocation of liver transplants.

‘Prognosis’

A moderate effect size was found for the influence of patients’ post-transplant life expectancy on priority ratings. Participants prioritised patients with a higher post-transplant life expectancy, suggesting that lay people support the ‘prognosis’ allocation principle for liver transplant allocation [17]. This decision was influenced by utilitarianism, because allocating the resource to an individual who will live for longer is expected to bring greater benefits to future society [7, 17].

In the UK, liver transplants are allocated to patients with the highest transplant benefit score (TBS) which is the difference between the patient’s predicted survival without the transplant over a five-year interval (i.e., pre-transplant life expectancy) and their predicted survival with the transplant over a five-year interval (i.e., post-transplant life expectancy) [48, 49]. Unexpectedly, post-transplant life expectancy had a greater influence on priority ratings for patients with a long waiting time than for patients with a short waiting time. Thus, participants appear to have based their priority judgements on the weighted information of patients’ post-transplant life expectancy and waiting time.

‘Sickest first’

We also found that patients’ pre-transplant life expectancy had the largest effect of all the investigated patient characteristics on priority ratings. Participants prioritised patients with a lower pre-transplant life expectancy, consistent with the ‘sickest first’ allocation principle, and following the philosophical principle of prioritarianism [17]. The large effect of pre-transplant life expectancy is in line with previous research which has indicated that lay individuals consider ‘sickest first’ to be one of the fairest allocation principles – and more so than ‘prognosis’ [27, 28, 39]. This decision is consistent with the current UK liver allocation scheme [48, 49].

However, lay people’s support of the ‘sickest first’ principle is at odds with the views of some ethicists, who argue that ‘sickest first’ is morally unjustifiable because it fails to account for prognosis, prioritising patients who may be worst-off in the future [17]. Nonetheless, participants prioritised patients with a low pre-transplant life expectancy (i.e., the sickest patients) over patients with a high pre-transplant life expectancy to a greater extent when patients’ prognosis was low compared to when it was high. This result is in accordance with Stahl et al. [40], who found that patients with a lower prognosis needed a lower pre-transplant life expectancy for lay people to allocate a transplant to them than did patients with a higher prognosis. Additionally, when allocations were based on ‘sickest first’, participants also considered patients’ age. That is, participants prioritised patients with a lower pre-transplant life expectancy to a greater degree when patients were younger than when they were older. This finding contrasts with Stahl et al. [40], who observed that older patients required a lower pre-transplant life expectancy than younger patients to receive priority by US citizens.

‘First-come first-served’

Participants only marginally prioritised patients with a long waiting time over those with a short waiting time. This result is consistent with the ‘first-come first-served’ allocation principle, reflecting an egalitarian perspective [17]. This finding implies that lay people from the UK do regard ‘first-come first-served’ as a reasonable principle for the allocation of liver transplants but suggests that the philosophical perspective of egalitarianism may not be as salient as utilitarianism and prioritarianism. This result is not consistent with Krütli et al. [27], who found that German lay individuals rated ‘first-come first-served’ as the second fairest principle for the allocation of kidney transplants, above ‘prognosis’.

Focus groups comprising lay individuals from the UK have provided insights into the rationale behind allocating by waiting list (e.g., [50]). Lay individuals supported the 'first-come first-served' approach, reporting its cultural acceptance in the UK, where queuing is a common practice. Further, this approach was seen as a way of avoiding difficult decision-making based on other patient characteristics. However, Persad et al. [17] argued that the ‘first-come first-served’ principle is unfair due to the potential for corruption within transplant waiting lists.

‘Youngest first’

Participants assigned higher priority ratings to younger patients over older patients, with a moderate effect size. This is consistent with the ‘youngest first’ allocation principle [17] and is in accordance with previous studies indicating that lay people prioritise hypothetical younger patients across a range of scarce medical treatments [36–39].

Prioritising younger patients over older patients may reflect utilitarian views that younger individuals may contribute more to society, by contributing to the economy and raising children [38]. However, patient age influenced priority ratings for both patients with low and high post-transplant life expectancy, suggesting that our participants held the prioritarian view that younger patients are worse off because they have lived fewer years than older patients [37]. This is consistent with the concept that older individuals have had their 'fair innings' in life [25, 51]. However, others argue that age-based prioritisation is ageist and unfair [52], proposing that pre-illness health, frailty and functional ability should guide transplant allocation instead [53].

The current NHS liver transplant allocation system appears to prioritise older patients [41] which conflicts with the salience of the ‘youngest first’ allocation principle among lay people, and with the government ban on all forms of age discrimination within the NHS [54]. This may undermine public trust in the fairness of the organ allocation system, which is the most common reason that people do not want to donate their organs [55], and thus may increase the scarcity of donor livers [3, 23]. Recent research suggests that age is not associated with expected post-transplant survival, while older age is associated with lower expected pre-transplant survival [56, 57]. Consequently, older patients may often have a higher TBS than younger patients, potentially leading to the prioritisation of older individuals.

Between-participant effects

Consistent with our hypothesis, there were differences in support for the ‘youngest first’ allocation principle on the basis of participants’ age. Younger participants prioritised younger patients over older patients more than did older participants. Werntoft et al. [43] found that older individuals in Sweden prefer not to allocate medical treatments by age. Diederich et al. [42] found that younger lay individuals in Germany agreed with the ‘youngest first’ allocation of kidney transplants, whereas older individuals tended to disagree with it, indicating an in-group bias in Germany.

These findings contrast with the current study’s observation that older participants prioritised younger patients over older patients in the UK, albeit to a lesser extent than younger participants. Participants in Diederich et al.’s and Werntoft et al.’s study evaluated patients simply categorised as ‘young’ or ‘old’, lacking information on the patients’ specific ages. This difference in methodology could potentially explain the differences in the findings, as participants in Diederich et al.’s and Werntoft et al.’s study may have had different perceptions of what ages constitute ‘young’ and ‘old’ compared to the specific ages given in the current study. Although in-group allocation biases have been found on the basis of other criteria, such as smoking habits and political beliefs, the current study did not find an in-group allocation bias on the basis of age [32], [58]. Unexpectedly, the effect of patients’ pre-transplant life expectancy and waiting time on priority ratings was also larger for younger participants than for older participants. Considering the age-related differences in allocation preferences, it is important to take into account the views of both young and old lay individuals when constructing allocation frameworks. This could potentially lead to the creation of fair systems that are reflective of the broader population’s values.

Limitations

Participants were asked to rate the priority that patients should receive for a liver transplant, a method that has limitations, as it allows participants to assign equal priority ratings to all patients, thus avoiding actually making a decision [28]. Consequently, the results may not fully represent participants’ views. An alternative methodology commonly used in this field involves participants ranking hypothetical patients in order of priority [34]. While the ranking task forces participants to make priority decisions, people express anxiety when ranking patients, potentially raising ethical concerns [28]. Additionally, using rankings would have limited the range of statistical analyses that could be performed, including the between-subject analyses [28]. Considering the limitations of the ranking task, a rating task was chosen for the current study. Additionally, the quantitative survey design of the current study captured participants’ initial opinions but did not allow for more considered judgments. Research using focus groups has shown that people’s views about healthcare priority-setting change after deliberation and discussion [59]. Future research should therefore examine whether lay preferences for liver allocation change after more time to reflect on decisions/further consideration.

Conclusion

The present study found that lay participants from the UK favoured a prioritarian approach to allocating livers for transplantation to hypothetical patients, as opposed to the utilitarian or egalitarian approaches that ethicists such as Persad et al. [17] have argued to be the fairest method of allocation. This is demonstrated by pre-transplant life expectancy having a larger effect on participants’ priority ratings than prognosis and waiting list time (representing a utilitarian and egalitarian approach respectively). This discrepancy between ethicists’ arguments and lay participants’ actual choices should be addressed in future research, as it raises important questions about who has the mandate to determine important public health policies such as the distribution of scarce organ transplants. Additionally, the present study reinforced the finding that younger hypothetical patients are given higher priority than older ones. Therefore, future research should also investigate the findings of Burns and Loader [41] that younger people wait significantly longer than those over 60 years of age, as it may be that the current system for the allocation of livers for transplantation does not align with public opinion.
