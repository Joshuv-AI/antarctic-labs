Electrothermal vacuum sublimation drying of graphene aerogels for high-temperature synthesis
Authors: Su-Fan Hu, Ming-Ze Yang, Pei-Yu Cao, Si-Fan Cheng, Ye-Chuang Han, Yu Gu, Zhong-Qun Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphene aerogels have emerged as versatile material platforms for diverse critical applications, owing to their ultralight, highly porous architecture and attractive mechanical, electrical, and thermal properties. However, the precise and scalable synthesis of graphene aerogels remains a key challenge, particularly in developing efficient drying strategies that preserve their delicate nanostructures. Here, we devise an electrothermal vacuum sublimation drying method that integrates rapid Joule heating with vacuum-driven phase-transition engineering, achieving a 417-fold increase in drying rate while consuming only 0.6% of the electrical energy used by conventional freeze-drying. The high-temperature and low-pressure electrothermal vacuum sublimation drying environment enhances carbon-water reactions and accelerates the decomposition of oxygen-containing functional groups, thus preserving the porous structure and improving the high-temperature stability of graphene aerogels. Furthermore, the resulting graphene aerogels can serve as localized high-temperature resistive heaters, reaching temperatures exceeding 3000 K for rapid graphitization of mesocarbon microbeads, shortening the process from days to 20 seconds. Beyond drying, the versatility of electrothermal vacuum sublimation drying is further demonstrated through applications in chemicals and nanomaterials synthesis. This study presents a versatile strategy that integrates physical phase transitions with chemical transformations, enabling the precise and scalable fabrication of graphene aerogels and other advanced material systems.

Introduction

Graphene aerogels (GAs), distinguished by their ultralight and three-dimensional porous networks, have emerged as a versatile platform for energy storage, environmental remediation, and applications in extreme environments1–7. Their intrinsic advantages, including exceptional mechanical, electrical, and thermal properties, further highlight their immense promise for various advanced applications. However, the development and practical implementation of GAs remain largely restricted to laboratory-scale investigations8–11. A critical challenge hindering their translation to industrial-scale production and commercial viability lies in the lack of precise and scalable fabrication strategies that can preserve their delicate nanostructures while ensuring cost efficiency and reproducibility12–14. Traditional fabrication methods, including freeze-drying, supercritical drying, and chemical vapor deposition (CVD), typically require prolonged drying, intricate operational protocols, and substantial capital investment. These factors collectively increase production costs and hinder the preservation of the 3D porous framework, a structure critical to the functional performance of GAs across diverse applications4,15–17. Addressing these constraints is pivotal to unlocking the broader technological utility of GAs.

Drying is a critical unit operation in GAs synthesis, which directly dictates their structural integrity, physicochemical properties, and ultimate functional performance18–27. From a physicochemical standpoint, this drying process poses a multiscale challenge: it requires precise control of heat and mass transfer dynamics to preserve the delicate 3D porous architecture of GAs. Current drying methods, such as freeze-drying and supercritical drying, rely on complex, time- and energy-intensive protocols to prevent structural collapse during solvent removal18–20. Supercritical drying mitigates capillary pressure at the liquid-gas interface by operating above the critical point of the working solvent (most commonly CO2), thereby preserving the intricate pore network of GAs. However, its inherent requirements for high-pressure conditions and lengthy solvent exchange cycles make this approach energy-intensive and incompatible with cost-effective, large-scale manufacturing21,22. Freeze-drying, which is based on the vacuum sublimation of ice crystals, faces nearly identical scalability limitations, as its required sustained low temperatures severely restrict drying rates and production throughput23,24. Both methods are fundamentally limited by diffusive mass transfer, a process that inherently operates on timescales of hours. Over this prolonged processing window, residual solvent and adventitious oxidation accumulate along pore walls, progressively eroding the long-range structural integrity of GAs that underpins their mechanical, electrical, and thermal performance25,26. This coupling of slow mass transfer and structural degradation frequently leads to poor energy utilization efficiency and non-uniform material structure, especially when scaling production from laboratory to industrial scale27. Therefore, an advanced processing strategy that enables rapid solvent removal under near-vacuum conditions, while eliminating the liquid-vapor interface, would represent a critical advancement toward scalable and efficient GAs production.

In this work, inspired by the phase diagram of water, we devise an electrothermal vacuum sublimation drying (EVSD) method that integrates rapid Joule heating with vacuum-driven phase-transition engineering (Fig. 1a). This electrothermal approach not only enables fast and energy-efficient drying of GAs by sublimation of ice within the fragile pores of GAs, effectively preserving their hierarchical 3D architecture, but also extends its utility beyond drying. Experimental results demonstrate that EVSD (operated at ~10 Pa, ~776 K) achieves a 417-fold increase in drying rate while consuming only 0.6% of electrical energy compared to that of freeze-drying approach (Fig. 1b). The high-temperature and low-pressure EVSD environment enhances the carbon-water reaction and accelerates the decomposition of oxygen-containing functional groups, thus preserving the porous structure and improving the high-temperature stability of GAs. As a proof of concept, we validated that EVSD-prepared GAs can serve as an advanced resistive heater material and to create localized high-temperature zones (>3000 K, Fig. 1c) by Joule heating. This high-temperature environment reduces the typically time- and energy-intensive graphitization of raw mesocarbon microbeads (MCMB) from days to just 20 s, yielding graphite with improved electrochemical performance as a lithium-ion battery anode. Moreover, the intrinsic high-temperature capability of EVSD (up to 1000 K) offers opportunities for process control, extending its functionality beyond drying, and unlocking functionalities such as chemical synthesis and nanomaterials fabrication (Fig. 1d). The broader applicability of EVSD is further demonstrated by the synthesis of wood-derived carbon sponges, high-surface-area porous carbons, and ultrafine metal nanocrystals. Therefore, this work presents a generalizable electrothermal processing strategy that couples physical phase change with chemical reaction, providing a scalable pathway for the fabrication of GAs and other advanced functional material systems.Fig. 1Principle and application of electrothermal vacuum sublimation drying (EVSD) for graphene aerogel (GA) preparation.a The phase diagram of water. Results and discussion

Application of EVSD for GAs preparation

To evaluate the performance of the EVSD method in GAs preparation, we systematically investigated its efficacy in preserving structural integrity and functional properties, and benchmarked it against conventional freeze-drying (FD) and forced air drying (FAD) methods in terms of drying efficiency and energy consumption. The custom-designed EVSD system was developed by modifying a commercial freeze-drying unit (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). This modification involved integrating a sealed electrothermal component within the vacuum chamber (Fig. 2a), enabling precise thermal control under sublimation conditions28. In a typical EVSD process (Supplementary Video 1), the frozen GA precursor was placed on a carbon felt inside the vacuum chamber (~10 Pa). The temperature of the carbon felt was adjustable, ranging from ambient to above 1000 K via control of the input power29. The ice crystals within the frozen GA, placed on carbon felt, underwent rapid sublimation from solid to gas due to a flash temperature rise of the carbon felt to 776 K (Supplementary Fig. 3). Subsequently, heat propagated gradually from the as-formed GA to the remaining ice crystal regions, until fully dried GAs was obtained (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 4). Both EVSD and FD effectively preserved the original hierarchical architecture of GAs, with minimal volume shrinkage (<8%) across all samples (Fig. 2c). In stark contrast, the FAD process induced irreversible structural collapse during the initial drying phase (Supplementary Fig. 5), leading to a dramatic volume reduction (>90%). This collapse arises from surface tension- and capillary-induced stresses exceeding the mechanical strength of the gel network during the drying process30,31. The EVSD method, by leveraging its vacuum environment and controlled electrothermal process, eliminates the formation of liquid water and thus avoids the associated surface tension or capillary forces that typically compromise the structural integrity of GAs. The EVSD method provides a robust technological foundation for the efficient and cost-effective preparation of GAs, as evidenced by its superior performance in both drying efficiency and energy consumption compared to conventional techniques. Specifically, EVSD achieves complete drying of GAs in merely 8 min, in stark contrast to the 2200 min required for FD and 630 min for FAD (Fig. 2d). To quantitatively evaluate the drying kinetics, we systematically fitted the drying curves (Supplementary Figs. 6–8 and Supplementary Table 1), and calculated the corresponding drying rates at varying degrees of dryness (Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table 2). EVSD-treated samples exhibit an optimal balance between rapid drying and minimal volume shrinkage (<8%), outperforming previously reported drying methods23,24,32–44 (Fig. 2e). To ensure an accurate energy comparison, we measured the total electricity consumption of each method throughout the entire drying process (Supplementary Fig. 10). The results show that EVSD requires merely 0.14 kW h of energy to achieve complete dryness of GAs, which is not only lower than the 22.98 kW h required for FD and 1.49 kW h for FAD (inset of Fig. 2e), but also comprehensively superior to that of supercritical drying (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Table 3). Collectively, these findings identify EVSD as a superior strategy that minimizes energy and time expenditures while effectively retaining the hierarchical porous structure of GAs, fully meeting the scalability requirements for future industrial-scale applications.

Characterization of aerogels

Beyond its exceptional drying efficiency, the EVSD approach which serve as a core implementation of our custom electrothermal processing platform, differs fundamentally from conventional drying techniques by integrating two synchronous processes: (i) controlled physical sublimation under reduced pressure to achieve efficient dehydration, and (ii) thermally activated chemical transformations that simultaneously tailor surface chemistry and even enable materials synthesis (Supplementary Fig. 12). This dual-function mechanism stands in clear contrast to traditional drying methods, which rely exclusively on phase-change thermodynamics with no concurrent chemical modification18. The effectiveness of EVSD was verified by a systematic comparison of GAs synthesized via electrothermal vacuum sublimation drying (EVSD-GA) and conventional freeze-drying (FD-GA). Macroscopic and microscale structural analyses confirm that EVSD effectively preserves the native 3D porous framework of GAs (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Figs. 13, 14). Nitrogen (N2) adsorption–desorption measurements revealed no significant difference in pore size distribution between EVSD-GA and FD-GA, with both centered at approximately 4 nm (Fig. 3c). Notably, the EVSD-GA exhibits a higher Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface area of 107.5 m2 g−1, approximately twice that of FD-GA (56.3 m2 g−1, Fig. 3d). Raman spectroscopy reveals minimal changes in the ID/IG ratio of EVSD-GA and FD-GA compared to raw graphene oxide (Fig. 3e), suggesting that structural defects of graphene (e.g., vacancies, topological defects) remain largely unhealed during hydrothermal and drying processes48,49. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis distinguishes their surface chemistry profiles. The C1s spectrum of EVSD-GA is dominated by C=C/C-C, with markedly reduced contributions from oxygen-containing functional groups (Supplementary Figs. 18–21), whereas FD-GA and raw graphene oxide (GO) exhibit higher oxygen content (Fig. 3f). The high oxygen content of GAs by conventional methods may compromise their structural stability (Supplementary Fig. 22) and typically necessitate additional annealing steps before application50–52. The EVSD process effectively minimizes surface oxygen-containing groups through thermal-driven bond breakage (Supplementary Fig. 23), achieving both efficient water removal and surface chemistry optimization45,53. Critically, the elevated temperatures achieved via our EVSD platform enable two synergistic functions in a single step: efficient ice sublimation under vacuum to preserve the 3D porous framework, and thermally driven chemical reactions to remove surface oxygen-containing groups. This synergistic dual-action mechanism, combining efficient and controllable physical phase transition and chemical reaction, enables precise tailoring of porosity, surface chemistry, and microstructural integrity in GAs and beyond. To further demonstrate the broad versatility of our EVSD method beyond GAs fabrication, we applied this method to multiple other material systems. These include emerging MXene-based aerogel (Fig. 3g, h), wood-derived carbon aerogels (Supplementary Fig. 24), high-surface-area porous carbons (Supplementary Fig. 25), and ultrafine metal nanocrystals (Supplementary Figs. 26 and 27)54–59. All of these materials are difficult or even impossible to efficiently fabricate with intact structure and tailored properties using conventional drying methods alone.

Mechanism analysis for GAs synthesis by EVSD

To elucidate the working mechanism of EVSD, we first characterized the drying kinetics in GAs fabrication. The EVSD process for GAs operates via a dual-process kinetic mechanism that integrates ice sublimation and water vapor diffusion. Drying curves for EVSD-fabricated GAs showed a strong temperature dependence of the dryness (Fig. 4a). As the processing temperature rose from 295 K to 776 K, the time needed to achieve the same drying degree dropped from 690 min to 8 min. This behavior aligns with the fundamental principles of sublimation: elevated temperatures provide sufficient energy to overcome the latent heat of sublimation, thereby accelerating ice removal from the aerogel’s porous network60,61. This trend was further confirmed by the calculated dehydration rate, which initially peaks and subsequently declines as the drying progresses (Fig. 4b). However, a deviation from this pattern emerges at higher temperatures and prolonged drying times, implying a transition from a sublimation-dominated to a diffusion-limited regime62. Given that the pore size of GAs (~4 nm) is orders of magnitude smaller than the mean free path of water molecules (~1 mm, Supplementary Fig. 28), which requires the use of the Knudsen diffusion model (Dk ∝ T0.5) rather than the free diffusion model (D ∝ T1.5) to describe this confined mass transport process63. Consequently, in the final drying stage, the rate-determining step shifts from surface-driven sublimation to the internal transport of water molecules through the aerogel’s complex pore network. We further established a quantitative mathematical model for the EVSD process through a mathematical model correlating overall drying duration with temperature. This model achieved a strong coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.9795), demonstrating a reliable agreement between theoretical predictions and experimental results (Fig. 4c). To validate its accuracy, we evaluated three alternative models: one relying solely on conductive heat transfer, another focusing exclusively on gas diffusion, and a third integrating both mechanisms (Supplementary Fig. 29). Our EVSD model reveal that the direct sublimation of frozen water within the GA scaffold via radiative heating, which is the core advantage of our electrothermal platform that circumvents liquid-phase surface tension and capillary stress to preserve the 3D hierarchical porosity of GAs. The EVSD process unfolds in two sequential stages (Fig. 4d): (i) thermal radiation energy is transferred from the carbon felt to the GA, driving sublimation of ice; (ii) the sublimated water vapor traverses the already dried region of the aerogel to reach the surface and diffuse into the vacuum environment64. A critical factor underpinning this process is the intrinsic coupling of thermal and mass transfer dynamics within the nanoscale porous medium, as supported by thermodynamic frameworks such as the offset activation energy model for porous materials20. Given the inherently low thermal conductivity of GAs (typically <0.05 W m−1 K−1)19,45,65, conductive heat transfer makes a negligible contribution to the overall process. Consequently, the energy required for sublimation is supplied almost entirely by radiative heat from the carbon felt. Combined experimental validation and theoretical modeling collectively define an optimal temperature window that balances rapid water vapor removal with minimal structural collapse, which is critical for scalable GA production. The experimentally determined processing window establishes a temperature-dependent predictive model for reaction kinetics, not only addressing the reproducibility and scalability challenges inherent in GAs production8–11, but also providing a foundation for the high-temperature Joule heating synthesis applications presented in subsequent sections.

Localized high-temperature field in GA

Leveraging the robust porous architecture and inherent high-temperature stability of EVSD-fabricated GAs, we further explored their utility as resistive heaters capable of attaining localized high temperatures through GA-mediated Joule heating. EVSD-prepared GAs show remarkable performance advantages over conventional heating approaches (Supplementary Table 5). Specifically, the GA maintains a porous architecture supported by graphite electrodes during the power-off state (Fig. 5a). Upon electrical activation, GA-mediated Joule heating generates a localized bright red-white emission region within itself (Fig. 5b), indicating the formation of a high-temperature zone. COMSOL Multiphysics simulations reveal the cross-sectional temperature distribution in GA under Joule heating, with the core reaching 3500 K while the surface stabilizes at 1778 K (Fig. 5c). The inset of Fig. 5c illustrates the radiation mechanisms associated with GA-mediated Joule heating, encompassing scattering, absorption, and dissipation. GA-mediated Joule heating achieves and maintains internal temperatures of ~3500 K without direct heating of the entire structure, stemming from two inherent properties of GA: (i) its high surface-to-volume ratio and interconnected porous structure enable efficient radiative heating, followed by repeated internal scattering and absorption of radiated energy; (ii) simultaneously, the low thermal conductivity of GA acts as an internal insulator, minimizing heat loss45,63,65. The electrothermal performance of GAs was systematically benchmarked against conventional resistive heating materials, such as carbon paper and graphene films. At identical input power, GAs exhibited higher peak temperatures (Fig. 5d), a result attributed to their outstanding electrothermal energy conversion efficiency. According to the Stefan-Boltzmann law49, which states that thermal radiation scales with the fourth power of absolute temperature, the observed radiation profiles closely match theoretical predictions (Supplementary Fig. 30). The core temperature of GAs exhibits a near-linear correlation with input power (Supplementary Figs. 31 and 32), indicating that the porous architecture efficiently suppresses excessive radiative losses, thereby improving electrothermal conversion efficiency. Given the stability of the high-temperature field is critically important, EVSD-GAs were thus tested under two of the most commonly used heating scenarios, including continuous heating and cycling pulsed heating. No obvious temperature fluctuations were observed during continuous heating, demonstrating excellent thermal stability of the GA materials (Fig. 5e). Comprehensive structural and chemical characterizations of EVSD-GA before and after continuous heating further confirmed its robust structural integrity under these operating conditions (Supplementary Fig. 33). Meanwhile, the electrodes closely related to the high-temperature field were also taken into consideration. By monitoring electrode temperature during heating and characterizing structural changes before and after operation, we confirmed that the electrodes can withstand prolonged continuous high-temperature exposure without performance degradation (Supplementary Fig. 34). The resistance evolution during this process reflects structural transformations, including pore collapse, densification, or modifications to the graphene sp2 hybridization network66,67. Continuous resistance monitoring provides an in situ, real-time metric to track material state and reaction kinetics during synthesis. Stability assessments further confirm that EVSD-GAs maintain consistent surface-core temperature gradients and resistance values after 2000 cycles of electrothermal shock (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Figs. 35, 36). In addition, combined with XRD, Raman, and SEM characterizations of EVSD-GAs before and after 2000 electrothermal shock cycles (Supplementary Fig. 37), the results demonstrate their robust durability for high-temperature applications. Comparative studies between FD-GAs and EVSD-GAs, as well as a widely used 3D carbon material (carbon felt), revealed that the electrothermal performance of GAs is largely insensitive to the drying method used, but is drastically superior to that of commercial carbon felt (Supplementary Fig. 38). Collectively, these findings establish GAs fabricated via our EVSD method as a reliable, high-performance resistive heating material for highly efficient electrothermal conversion in high-temperature synthetic applications.

Rapid graphitization of MCMB by GA-mediated Joule heating

Having validated the high-temperature electrothermal performance of GAs fabricated via EVSD, we next demonstrated the practical utility of this platform for ultrafast, high-temperature graphitization of raw mesocarbon microbeads (MCMB) via GA-mediated Joule heating (Fig. 6a). Notably, the graphitization process represents the most time- and energy-intensive step in synthetic graphite production, typically requiring prolonged processing times (tens to hundreds of hours) at high temperatures above 2500 K68,69. In this process, the GA simultaneously serves as a conductive substrate and an efficient localized heat generator. Upon applying an electrical current, the GA generates an instantaneous localized high-temperature field (up to 3500 K), with the generated heat rapidly transferred to the adjacent raw MCMB particles embedded within the GA matrix. This localized thermal field enables raw MCMB to overcome the activation energy barrier for graphitization, inducing rapid structural ordering within a short duration of 20 s. Compared to conventional high-temperature graphitization furnaces (e.g., resistance or induction furnaces), this approach eliminates the need for complex thermal field control systems, enabling direct and efficient energy transfer from the macroscopic thermal field to individual particles70,71. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns and Raman spectroscopy provided direct evidence of the rapid graphitization of raw MCMB (Fig. 6b). Specifically, the (002) diffraction peak of raw MCMB is broad and diffuse, centered at approximately 25.948°, indicative of a relatively large interlayer spacing (d002 ≈ 0.34310 nm) and low crystallinity. In contrast, the Joule-heating treated MCMB (JH-MCMB) exhibits a narrower and more intense (002) peak shifted to a lower 2θ angle (26.535°), corresponding to a reduced d002 spacing of 0.33565 nm, a clear hallmark of a high degree of graphitization. Microstructural characterization via scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) provides further validation of the rapid graphitization of raw MCMB (Fig. 6c). The JH-MCMB preserves the original spherical morphology of raw MCMB but exhibits a smoother surface and is characterized by densely stacked graphene layers. The SAED patterns of raw MCMB reveal diffuse, disordered diffraction spots, whereas those of JH-MCMB display sharp, well-defined diffraction rings corresponding to the (002) and (100) planes of graphite, providing direct evidence of its high crystallinity. To contextualize this approach within the broader landscape of graphitization methodologies, we compared the graphitization degree and synthesis duration of JH-MCMB with literature-reported benchmarks for graphite materials66,72–80 (Fig. 6d). The results indicate that JH-MCMB achieves a graphitization level comparable to or exceeding that of graphite produced via conventional methods within an ultrafast 20-s synthesis time. This highlights the exceptional efficiency of GA-mediated Joule heating for rapid, high-quality graphitization.

The structural characteristics of graphite directly influence its electrochemical performance in lithium-ion batteries, particularly the lithium-ion intercalation and deintercalation processes. To evaluate the practical utility of JH-MCMB, we assessed its electrochemical performance as an anode material for lithium-ion batteries. Before testing, JH-MCMB was coated with a carbon layer (JH-MCMB@C) to enhance electrical conductivity (Supplementary Fig. 39). All electrochemical tests were performed without any systematic optimization of the sample preparation or testing protocols. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves recorded at 0.1 mV s−1 reveal electrochemical reversibility in JH-MCMB@C comparable to commercial graphite (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 40). The broad cathodic peak near 0.2–0.3 V in the first scan corresponds to lithium-ion intercalation into graphite layers, while the well-matched anodic peak at ~0.1 V in subsequent scans reflects efficient deintercalation. The symmetry and reversibility of these redox peaks are consistent with structural stability and robust cycling behavior81. The initial charge-discharge profiles demonstrate a specific capacity of 358.4 mAh g−1 (Fig. 6f), approaching the theoretical capacity of graphite (372 mAh g−1)68, accompanied by an initial Coulombic efficiency (CE) of 93.54%. These electrochemical features align with the well-defined phase transitions observed during lithiation (Supplementary Figs. 41–44)81,82. Cycling tests showed negligible capacity fade over 50 cycles (inset of Fig. 6f), reinforcing the structural robustness of JH-MCMB@C compared with literature-reported benchmarks80,83–85 (Fig. 6g). These findings collectively validate the practical utility of GA-mediated Joule heating for ultrafast graphitization, offering a scalable pathway toward the synthesis of structure-controlled, high-performance graphite materials and beyond (e.g., refractory carbides (Supplementary Fig. 45)).

Overall, this study introduces the EVSD strategy that integrates rapid Joule heating with vacuum-driven phase-change engineering, enabling significant advancements in the precise and scalable fabrication of GAs for applications in high-temperature synthesis. The EVSD process demonstrates unprecedented efficiency, completing solvent removal within 8 min while consuming merely 0.6% of the energy required by conventional freeze-drying methods, all while maintaining exceptional structural integrity with minimal volume shrinkage (<8%) across all samples. Additional head-to-head comparisons with supercritical drying further validate the superiority of our electrothermal strategy in both life cycle assessment and techno-economic analysis. Beyond high-efficiency drying, the EVSD process integrates synchronous physical sublimation and thermally driven chemical modification, yielding GAs with optimized surface chemistry, enhanced specific surface area, and excellent high-temperature electrothermal stability. Leveraging these unique characteristics of EVSD-prepared GA, we demonstrate that it enables the creation of localized high-temperature zones (>3000 K) via GA-mediated Joule heating. This capability reduces the conventional graphitization process from hours to just 20 s, producing synthetic graphite with a high degree of long-range crystallographic order (graphitization degree > 97%) that delivers excellent electrochemical performance as an anode material for lithium-ion batteries. Notably, the high-temperature capability (up to 1000 K) of EVSD extends its utility beyond drying, unlocking functionalities such as surface modification, one-step chemical synthesis, and advanced nanomaterial fabrication. Collectively, this work directly addresses the long-standing key challenges in GAs manufacturing: scalability, energy efficiency, and structural fidelity, and establishes a robust foundation for future innovations in high-temperature synthesis and advanced functional materials fabrication.

Methods

Reagents and materials

Graphene oxide solution (10 mg mL−1) was purchased from Hangzhou Gaoxi Technology Co., Ltd. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, 99.8%) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. L-ascorbic acid (99%) and phenol-formaldehyde resin (BR grade) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Raw-MCMB (99.95%) was purchased from Guangzhou Nano Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. Cupric acetate monohydrate (Cu(CH3COO)2·H2O, AR) was purchased from National Pharmaceutical Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Chloroplatinic acid hexahydrate (H2PtCl6·6H2O, 99%) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. The balsa wood (Ochroma pyramidal) was purchased from Alibaba (Hangzhou, China) and was cut into cubes of 15 × 15 × 15 mm. Sodium chloride (NaCl, 99.7%) and acetic acid (CH3COOH, 99.5%) were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Sodium chlorite (NaClO2, 80%) was sourced from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 99.7%) was supplied by Guangdong Guanghua Sci-Tech Co., Ltd., and cellulose (95% purity) was acquired from Hefei BASF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Silica powder (1 ~ 5 μm, 99.99%) were obtained from Qinghe Enyi Metal Material Co., Ltd. Carbon black (VULCANXC72R, Cabot). Zirconium powder (40 nm, purity > 99.9%) was supplied by Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Chromium powder (300 mesh, 99.5%) was sourced from Shanghai Acmec Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Ammonium molybdate tetrahydrate (AR) was supplied by Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

Synthesis of graphene hydrogel

45 mL of GO solution (5 mg mL−1), 0.01 g of PVA, and 0.13 g of L-ascorbic acid were added to deionized water (3 mL), followed by ultrasonic dispersion for 30 min to obtain a stable GO dispersion. Subsequently, 7.5 mL of mixed dispersion was transferred into a hydrothermal reactor and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 353 K for 24 h to form the GO hydrogel. The resultant hydrogel was solvent-exchanged in deionized water to remove the majority of the L-ascorbic acid reducing agent. Four rounds of solvent exchange were performed, with each cycle involving immersion of the GO hydrogel in deionized water for 1 h.

Graphene hydrogel dried via EVSD

The obtained hydrogel was first frozen at 253 K for 2 h, and then transferred into the EVSD device. A low pressure of 10 Pa was achieved by pumping down the system. Subsequently, a current of 30 A was applied to heat the carbon felt to approximately 776 K, lasting for 8 min. The system was then backfilled with air until internal and external pressures were balanced, after which the sample was removed. This yielded a dried graphene aerogel (diameter is 16 mm; height is 8 mm).

Preparation of Ti3C2Tx MXene

Ti3C2Tx MXene was fabricated via selective etching of the Ti3AlC2 MAX phase. Typically, 1 g of Ti3AlC2 powder (200 mesh; 11 Technology Co., Ltd.) was immersed in a mixed solution of 17 mL HCl (36 wt%~38 wt%) and 3 mL HF (48 wt%~50 wt%), followed by magnetic stirring at 308 K for 24 h. The as-obtained multilayer Ti3C2Tx was then washed with deionized (DI) water and centrifuged at 5000 rpm to collect the precipitate, and this washing process was repeated 5 to 6 times until the pH of the supernatant reached 6 ~ 7, yielding an aqueous Ti3C2Tx slurry. Subsequently, 8 mL of the as-prepared Ti3C2Tx slurry was frozen at 2253 K for 2 h, followed by drying in an EVSD system at 423 K under 10 Pa. Finally, Ti3C2Tx MXene aerogel was obtained after complete drying.

Preparation of wood-derived carbon sponges

The pretreatment of wood was based on previously reported work86. The balsa wood cubes were first immersed in a 3 wt% NaClO2 solution under acetic acid conditions (pH = 4.6) and heated at 358 K for 12 h. After washing with hot deionized (DI) water, the wood samples were transferred into a 5 wt% NaOH solution and maintained at 353 K for 8 h. Subsequently, the samples were washed 5 times with hot DI water to remove residual chemicals, followed by EVSD treatment at 330 K to obtain a white wood sponge. The white wood sponge was placed in a rectangular mold filled with DI water and degassed using a vacuum device. To stabilize the expanded cuboid in fluctuating water conditions and facilitate the rapid escape of the expanded air bubbles from the water, the wood sponge cube was inserted into the water along the fiber direction and fully immersed. The sample was then frozen and subsequently dried at 1173 K using the EVSD method, which achieved complete carbonization in just 10 min.

Preparation of cellulose-based porous carbon

One gram of cellulose was added to 5 mL of 8 wt% NaOH aqueous solution, then the solution was frozen. Subsequently, the mixed frozen mixtures were dried and carbonized by the EVSD method at a temperature of 1073 K.

Preparation of metal nanoparticles

Synthesis of Cu nanoparticles: Cupric acetate monohydrate (Cu(CH3COO)2·H2O, 100 mg) was dissolved in deionized water (100 mL) to form a Cu(CH3COO)2 solution (5 mmol L−1). Then, the solution was frozen and then dried/pyrolyzed using the pulsed heating mode of the EVSD method, with a heating duration of 0.2 s and a cooling duration of 1.0 s, reaching a peak temperature of 773 K. Finally, the powdered Cu nanoparticles were obtained.

Synthesis of Pt nanoparticles: Chloroplatinic acid hexahydrate (H2PtCl6·6H2O, 51.8 mg) was dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water to form a solution with a concentration of 10 mmol L−1. Subsequently, 100 mL of graphene oxide (GO) dispersion (5 mg mL−1) was added to the above solution. The mixed solution was frozen, followed by thermal treatment using an EVSD apparatus. The thermal treatment was performed under pulsed heating mode, with a peak temperature of 1073 K, a heating duration of 0.2 s, and a cooling time of 5 s. Finally, the obtained black product was collected.

Preparation of refractory carbides

Synthesis of β-SiC: Silica powder and carbon black were mixed at a molar ratio of 1:1.25, and ball-milled at 300 rpm for 10 h, and obtained the mixed powders. Then, a hole with a diameter of 5 mm was drilled in the center of the EVSD-GA aerogel, and 100 mg of the mixed powders was loaded into the hole. Subsequently, the EVSD-GA was filled with powders and clamped with graphite electrodes. A current was applied to raise the core temperature to 1673 K and held for 10 s, then the power supply was cut off. Finally, collect the β-SiC sample after the electrodes were cooled down to room temperature.

Synthesis of ZrC: Zirconium powder and petroleum coke were mixed at a molar ratio of 1:1, then ball-milled at 300 rpm for 10 h to obtain a powder. Then, a hole with a diameter of 5 mm was drilled in the center of the EVSD-GA aerogel, and 100 mg of the mixed powders was loaded into the hole. Subsequently, the EVSD-GA loaded with the sample was clamped by graphite electrodes. A current was applied to raise the core temperature to 2273 K and held for 10 s, then the power supply was cut off. Finally, collect the ZrC sample after the electrodes were cooled down to room temperature.

Synthesis of α-MoC: Ammonium tetramolybdate was dissolved in deionized water, then mixed with amorphous carbon, and vacuum-dried at 333 K for 1 h to obtain dried powder. A hole with a diameter of 5 mm was drilled in the center of the EVSD-GA aerogel, and 100 mg of the mixed powder was loaded into the hole. The EVSD-GA loaded with the sample was clamped by graphite electrodes. A current was applied to raise the core temperature to 1573 K and held for 10 s, then the power supply was cut off. Finally, collect the α-MoC sample after the electrodes were cooled down to room temperature.

Synthesis of Cr3C2: Chromium powder and carbon black (CB) were mixed at a molar ratio of 3:2.2, then ball-milled at 300 rpm for 24 h to obtain a powder. A hole with a diameter of 5 mm was drilled in the center of the EVSD-GA, and the mixed powder was loaded into the hole. The EVSD-GA loaded with the sample was clamped by graphite electrodes. A current was applied to raise the core temperature to 2273 K and held for 30 s, then the power supply was cut off. Finally, collect the sample after the electrodes were cooled down to room temperature.

Battery assembly and testing

The composite electrodes were prepared by mixing anode materials (JH-MCMB@C), Super P, CMC, and SBR in a mass ratio of 95.5:1.5:1.2:1.8 with deionized water as solvent. The homogeneous slurry was uniformly coated onto copper foil using a doctor blade technique and subsequently dried in a vacuum oven at 353 K for 12 h. Li half-cells were assembled in an argon-filled glovebox (O2 and H2O < 0.1 ppm) using lithium metal as the counter electrode, Celgard 2500 polypropylene as the separator, and finally integrated into a CR2032 coin-type cell configuration. All electrochemical measurements were conducted at room temperature (RT). The rate performance, long-term cycling stability, and galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) were evaluated using the Neware Test System. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed on a CHI660D electrochemical workstation (Shanghai CH Instrument Co., Ltd., China), with the EIS carried out over a frequency range of 200 kHz to 10 mHz and an AC perturbation amplitude of 5 mV.

High-temperature Joule heating

The Joule heating process was conducted inside an argon-filled glovebox, with the sample directly clamped between two graphite electrodes featuring adjustable spacing, eliminating the need for additional conductive additives. The electrical current and voltage were controlled via a portable power supply unit. Temperature fluctuations during the pulsed Joule heating process were precisely monitored using a non-contact infrared pyrometer. To enhance temporal resolution, the system incorporated a high-speed data acquisition workstation. The pyrometer’s measurement range spans from 525 K to 3273 K. All temperature data recorded during the experiment corresponded to the state where the graphene aerogel (GA) temperature had stabilized and ceased fluctuating.

GA-mediated Joule heating for graphitization

First, raw MCMB is heated at a rate of 3 K/min under an air atmosphere to 573 K, held at this temperature for 4 h, and then cooled naturally to room temperature. Subsequently, the sample is heated at 3 K min−1 under a nitrogen atmosphere to 573 K, followed by heating at 1 K min−1 to 973 K, and then at 3 K min−1 to 1273 K, where it is held for 1.5 h before being cooled naturally. The first step, pre-oxidation, promotes cyclization of the polymer chains and stabilizes the structure. The second step, carbonization, further removes residual volatiles and forms a reinforced carbon network framework.

The graphitization process of raw MCMB involves loading the pre-treated raw MCMB (~0.5 g) inside a graphene aerogel, followed by applying Joule heating. The high-temperature physical field generated at the center of the graphene aerogel induces graphitization, resulting in JH-MCMB. JH-MCMB is added to a solution composed of 100 mg of phenolic resin and 30 mL of ethanol. The mixture is stirred at 400 rpm for 24 h under room temperature conditions. Subsequently, the solution is dried in an oven at 333 K to obtain JH-MCMB@C.

Characterizations

The surface morphology of the samples was examined using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (Zeiss Gemini SEM 500) operated at 10 kV acceleration voltage. Crystallographic information was obtained via XRD on a Rigaku Ultima-IV system equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). The resulting patterns were analyzed to determine the degree of graphitization using the Scherrer equation and Rietveld refinement method. Defect characterization and structural ordering were assessed through micro-Raman spectroscopy (Horiba XploRA Plus) using a 532 nm excitation laser. The spectra were deconvoluted to quantify the D/G band intensity ratios and crystallite size distributions. Nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms were measured at 77 K using a Quantachrome Tristar II 3020 analyzer. Before analysis, approximately 100 mg of each sample underwent degassing at 473.15 K for 3 h under continuous nitrogen flow (99.999% purity). This pretreatment ensured the removal of physiosorbed contaminants without altering the sample’s intrinsic properties. Specific surface areas were calculated via the BET method, while pore size distributions were derived from the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) model. XPS was performed using a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB Xi+ instrument with monochromatic Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV). All binding energies were calibrated using the C 1 s peak at 284.8 eV. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was conducted using a JEOL JEM-2100Plus instrument operating at 200 kV. Accelerating voltage was chosen to minimize electron beam damage to the carbonaceous samples.
