Coping with suicidal crisis in Kalaallit Nunaat (Greenland): inuit youth lived experiences of recovery and protective processes - a qualitative pilot study
Authors: Arnarak Patricia Bloch, C. V. L. Larsen, Tenna Jensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKalaallit Nunaat (Greenland) continues to experience a very high burden of suicide, while knowledge about culturally meaningful recovery processes remains limited. This qualitative pilot study explores how Inuit individuals with lived experience of suicide attempts describe coping, turning points, and pathways toward thriving lives. Six participants who had experienced suicide attempts in childhood, adolescence or early adulthood, took part in semi-structured interviews conducted in Kalaallisut. Data were analyzed using systematic text condensation. Findings show that recovery was not experienced as an individual trait but as a relational and culturally embedded process. Supportive relationships, cultural grounding, connections to nature and land based practices, language-accessible services, and opportunities for belonging and contribution functioned as key protective resources. Inuit worldviews, including understandings of Sila and Inua, and engagement with land-based practices supported emotional regulation, meaning-making, and hope.

Introduction

Kalaallit Nunaat (Greenland) continues to carry a very high burden of suicide [1,2]. This epidemiological pattern has been interpreted in relation to profound socio-cultural change, modernisation, and colonisation-related disruptions shaping mental health and social wellbeing in Kalaallit Nunaat [2,3]. Suicide prevention, even though it rests on knowledge about risk patterns, requires insights into culturally and societally relevant coping strategies that can be translated into prevention approaches aligned with community priorities and pathways to support [4].

Background

A scoping review of literature on suicide in Greenland concluded that research has been predominantly descriptive and quantitative [5]. Studies on protective factors and intervention evaluations are scarce, and community-level determinants have rarely been addressed [6]. In 2023, Naalakkersuisut- the Government of Greenland launched “Qamani Strategy for the Prevention of Suicide 2023–2028,” framing suicide prevention around local values and strengths and defining four focus areas: accessible help-seeking/contact options, citizen involvement, safety and wellbeing in schools and youth education, and destigmatization [7].

A circumpolar study of Indigenous suicide prevention interventions highlights the diversity of approaches while underscoring recurring challenges related to evaluation, scalability, and cultural fit, alongside the value of community-driven development [8]. In Alaska Native communities, intervention science has advanced “culture as intervention” approaches such as Qungasvik, a multi-level, community-directed cultural intervention developed to strengthen “reasons for life” and related protections among youth complementing intervention development, prevention science syntheses argue that a protective-factor framework can serve as a unifying, culturally responsive organising principle for suicide prevention, supporting measurement and action on connectedness, meaning, and culturally specific resources [9]. These orientations resonate with global guidance that emphasise multi-level prevention, including strengthening life skills among young people, responsible media practices, early identification and care, and restricting access to means. Components that can be adapted to local realities [10,11].

A further imperative in Indigenous suicide prevention research concerns ethics, governance, and self-determination. Inuit-led guidance articulates what equitable engagement requires, including recognition of Inuit knowledge on its own terms, accountable partnerships, and Inuit control over data and benefits [7,12,13] Within this landscape, qualitative research centreing lived experiences of coping, meaning-making, and turning points after suicide attempts can fill a critical evidence gap in Kalaallit Nunaat by strengthening the protective-factor knowledge base, supporting culturally congruent tool development, and aligning with strategic priorities emphasising citizen involvement, destigmatization, and accessible pathways to support [5,9].

AIM

This study aimed to document coping resources and turning points among people in Kalaallit Nunaat who have experienced suicide attempts and describe themselves as thriving today, and to translate these findings into actionable implications for prevention practice.

Research questions

The study asked: (I) How do participants describe making their lives comprehensible, manageable, and meaningful after suicide attempts? (II) Which resistance resources—relational, cultural, and place-based do they identify as protective, and how are these mobilised and sustained in everyday life? (III) What actionable implications do these lived experiences provide for ethically safe, culturally congruent, community-facing prevention and tool development in Kalaallit Nunaat?

Author positionality and study context

The first author is an Indigenous Kalaaleq Inuk. This positionality supported cultural, linguistic, and contextual understanding, and strengthened sensitivity to how participants narrated coping, meaning-making, and turning points in ways embedded in local relationships, place, and everyday life. At the same time, insider positionality is known to involve methodological complexities, including risks of role ambiguity, taken-for-granted knowledge, and potential bias in interpretation [14–16]. To address these challenges, an explicit reflexive stance was maintained throughout the research process, informed by qualitative methodology emphasising reflexivity, transparency, and the active examination of preunderstandings [17,18] as well as literature on managing insider roles through deliberate reflexive techniques [19,20]. These reflections also draw on Indigenous research methodologies that foreground relational accountability, community-embedded ethics, and respect for local knowledge systems [21–23]. Such approaches emphasise that knowledge is co-constructed within relationships and that researcher positionality must be acknowledged as an integral part of the analytic process. The study was supervised by two non-Indigenous senior researchers who also supervise the ongoing PhD project. They both have extensive experience as Arctic researchers, providing external academic oversight and triangulation of analytic decisions. The topic of suicide also carries personal significance for all three authors, as it is a pervasive and deeply felt issue in Kalaallit Nunaat, affecting individuals, families, and communities across generations. This awareness informed the study with a shared commitment to conducting the research with care, respect, and responsibility toward participants’ experiences and narratives. The collaboration between authors was conducted primarily in Dansh and English, while the interviews and initial meaning-making processes were rooted in Kalaallisut. This multilingual process required careful attention to translation, interpretation, and conceptual equivalence across languages, and was approached as part of the reflexive and analytic work.

Methods

Study design

This article reports a completed qualitative pilot study exploring coping resources and turning points among people in Kalaallit Nunaat who had experienced suicide attempt and who described thriving in their life today. All Kalaallisut quotes from the informants in this text were translated into English manually by the first author, as AI tools cannot directly translate from Kalaallisut. AI-assisted tools (Microsoft Copilot, accessed through a secured university account) were used in a limited capacity to support English language clarity and consistency, including light language editing of translated passages and selected assistance in phrasing. In some instances, Copilot was also used to support translation processes following manual translation, functioning as a supplementary linguistic tool. All original meanings were preserved, and all translations, interpretations, and final formulations were carefully reviewed and verified by the authors.

Setting and data collection period

Data collection was conducted in Kalaallit Nunaat between June and July 2023 and included participants from East (Ittoqqoormiit), North (Ilulissat), West (Nuuk), and South (Narsaq and the village Eqalugaarsuit).

Participants and recruitment

Eligibility criteria were: (i) living in Kalaallit Nunaat, (ii) self-identifying as having experienced suicide attempt in the past, and (iii) describing current improved wellbeing or thriving. A purposive sampling strategy was used to recruit variation in gender and age.

Participants were aged 25–43 years at the time of the interviews. All participants had experienced suicide attempts during childhood, adolescence or early adulthood, between the ages of 8 and 27. Participants were approached through invitation letters and received written oral information about the study aim, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Because the topic is sensitive, participants were informed in advance that interviews would focus on coping and recovery processes after suicidal crises rather than detailed descriptions of the suicide act.

Data collection procedures

Six individual semi-structured interviews were conducted by the first author. Interviews lasted 60–120 minutes and were conducted in Kalaallisut (Greenlandic). Three interviews were conducted online (two with participants located in Nuuk and one with a participant located in Ilulissat). The remaining interviews were conducted in person in Southern Kalaallit Nunaat. With participant permission, interviews were audio-recorded. Field notes were written after each interview to capture contextual observations and reflections relevant to interpretation. The knowledge presented rests on the time, trust, and stories shared by the six participants.

Interview guide and theoretical approach

The interview guide was originally inspired by Antonovsky’s concept of Sense of Coherence (SOC), [24] particularly its attention to how people experience life as comprehensible, manageable, and meaningful. However, during the development process, and in dialogue with senior researchers the guide evolved in a more exploratory direction to avoid constraining participants’ narratives within predefined categories. The final interview guide was deliberately designed to remain open, flexible, and grounded in participants’ own priorities and lived experiences.

In parallel, the study was informed by Thomas Joiner’s Interpersonal Theory of Suicide, [25] which highlights perceived burdensomeness, thwarted belonging, and acquired capability for suicide as central mechanisms in suicidal behaviour. Joiners’ work has also emphasised that openly addressing suicidal thoughts can be protective, as speaking about such thoughts may counteract isolation and shame.

The purpose of the interviews was to elicit participants’ experiences of healing and strengthening processes that led them to choose life over death. Questions explored inner emotional experiences, social relationships, main life activities, beliefs or guiding values, and perceived changes in coping strategies across mental/spiritual, physical, and social domains. The guide also addressed support from family, peers, community services, and health systems, as well as participants’ reflections on what they would advise others who struggle with suicidal thoughts.

The interview guide was developed collaboratively with senior researchers. While grounded in relevant theory, the analytical approach remained inductive, ensuring that locally rooted meanings and participants’ own narrative structures guided interpretation.

Transcription and data management

All interviews were transcribed verbatim (in kalaallisut) by the first author and checked for accuracy against the audio recordings. Identifying information was removed during transcription, and participants were assigned in codes. Audio files, transcripts, and analytic materials were stored securely with access restricted to the first author and will be deleted in accordance with regulatory requirements and applicable data protection requirements.

Ethics and participant safety

All participants provided informed consent. A preparedness plan was in place to safeguard participants in case interviews triggered distress, including the option to pause or stop the interview and to offer follow-up support/supervision and referral information if needed. None of the participants required supervision or additional follow-up during or after the interviews.

The study was approved by the Scientific Ethics Committee for Health Research (VEK) with approval no. 2023-21759 and registered with the Research & Innovation Organisation (RIO) at the University of Southern Denmark with confirmation ID 2023-21759. The project complied with Greenlandic data protection legislation and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Participants received written and oral information and provided informed consent prior to participation. Recruitment procedures ensured voluntariness and confidentiality.

Data analysis

Data was analysed using systematic text condensation (STC), [18] a pragmatic, stepwise approach inspired by phenomenological analysis and commonly applied in health research. Analysis proceeded through: (1) repeated reading of all transcripts to gain an overall impression and identify preliminary themes; (2) identification and sorting of meaning units relevant to the research questions; (3) condensation of meaning units into code groups and subgroups, with iterative comparison across participants; and (4) synthesis of condensates into analytically grounded descriptions and concepts capturing shared patterns and variation in coping resources and turning points [11].

Reflexivity

Continuity across interviewing, transcription, and analysis by the first author supported sustained immersion in the data and close attention to context and meaning. To strengthen interpretive accountability [18], emerging interpretations and analytic decisions were discussed with two senior researchers, providing critical reflection on assumptions, alternative readings, and the clarity of analytic descriptions. Reflexivity was maintained regarding how the first author’s positionality could shape, what participants chose to share, and what was foregrounded during interpretation; analytic transparency was supported through documentation of key coding and condensation decisions [18].

Results

Across the six interviews’ participants accounts converged on interconnected protective factors supporting movement from suicidal crisis toward recovery and present-day wellbeing. Courage to live after suicidal crisis did not emerge as a single internal trait, but as something fostered through relationships, cultural grounding, nature, and opportunities to belong and contribute. These resources interacted dynamically over time, forming multiple “paths to life” that participants drew on in different combinations depending on life circumstances and turning points.

Inuit worldview, Sila, and relationships with land as stabilising and restorative

Participants described cultural grounding as a protective force that supported coherence and identity after periods of crisis. Understandings of life and wellbeing were articulated through lived experience rather than abstract explanation, and were often accessed through engagement with literature, land, and what participants described in relation to Sila an Inuit understanding of the interconnected universe encompassing both the environment and inner life. One participant described how kalaallisut literature helped reconnect personal experience with a broader Inuit worldview:

“After I started reading books like Qooqa (The great hunter) and Angakkoq Papik (last shaman), I recognised the landscape that was described. The islands, the horizon disappearing into the sky the same places where I went reindeer hunting. I began to see how strong our ancestors were, how they survived in nature. That gave me respect, and from that grew a wish to treat myself with dignity and respect for where I come from.” (P2)

Another participant described Sila (Inuit understanding/perception of the universe) as an experience of deep interconnectedness, where inner life and the surrounding world move together:

“When we are out in nature, that is Sila. We are one people and one with the universe. My thoughts, my body, the land, the weather, and the silence are connected. Nothing stands alone. When I breathe, the world breathes with me, and that is when things inside me begin to calm down” (P6)

All six participants described relationships with land and experiences connected to Sila as core elements in their sensemaking of the world and as a stabilising and restorative space supporting emotional regulation and renewed meaning. Participants spoke about calm and silence in the mountains, by the sea, and the landscape offering relief from intrusive thoughts and inner turmoil. One participant explained the significance of nature, describing how going outdoors helps calm their nervous system today when painful memories from childhood arise.

“We had to run away a lot when I was growing up because my father was violent toward my mother. After we got a kind stepfather who took us dogsledding, I felt this enormous sense of safety seeing the horizon, feeling the clean and cold crisp air in my lungs, warmth in my body, completely safe. The feeling was so clear, and I still carry it inside me, therefore when I’m overwhelmed, I go out to the silence it’s here I can hear my thoughts and feelings.” (P5)

Land-based activities such as hunting, dogsledding, sailing, and other meaningful practices and other meaningful outdoor activities were portrayed as culturally resonant practices reconnecting body and mind and helping create balance. Nature functioned as both refuge and resource for grounding, perspective, and the rebuilding of daily coping. Inua (an Inuit understanding that all elements of existence from people and animals to rivers, mountains, and stones carry soul or life) as the understanding that all elements of the world carry life or spirit, and that wellbeing emerges through relationship rather than separation:

“Everything has Inua (the soul), people, animals, plants, rivers, mountains, even stones. That is why we feel wellbeing after being out in nature, because our soul speaks with the soul of nature. Together, without us realising it, things start to fall into place.” (P4)

Experiences of wellbeing described in relation to Sila and Inua resonate with broader understandings of how time spent in nature can support emotional regulation and positive affect, including through bodily processes associated with calm and balance. For participants, these effects were primarily understood as relational and meaningful, grounded in connection to land, culture, and life, rather than being explained solely through biological mechanisms. Across accounts, nature functioned as a stabilising and restorative space that supported emotional regulation, grounding and renewed meaning, often intertwined with cultural worldviews such as Sila and Inua.

Supportive relationships

A consistent theme was the healing impact of being met with respect and genuine human presence. All participants emphasised the importance of someone who listens without judgement and can hold honest, direct conversations about suicide. This kind of sharing could feel physically and emotionally relieving:

“Sometimes it just feels good letting the feeling, the words, out of your body; it seems like the system in you restarts when doing so.” (P5)

Participants also stressed that safety depended on not having to manage the listener’s emotions:

“Someone who, without fear, listens to you without you having to comfort that person afterwards.” (P4)

Safe and reliable adults within family networks or among professionals were described as anchors, especially when participants felt overwhelmed or isolated. Being “seen” and met as a whole person rather than a diagnosis enabled trust, openness, and gradual re-engagement with life.

Beyond close one-to-one relationships, participants also described how relational safety and recognition were often experienced through participation in shared activities and communal spaces. Wider community environments such as youth houses, community houses, and local gathering places were described as settings where belonging, support, and everyday meaning naturally unfolded. Community houses and sports houses, especially during Christmas events, functioned as accessible and inclusive meeting points that brought people together across age groups:

“Christmas atmosphere with a choir, cookies, handicraft sales, and bingo with big prises brought the whole town together, it was lovely.” (P1)

The youth club was frequently emphasised as a protective arena, an inclusive, low-threshold space where young people could “hang out” across age groups, supporting recognition, social learning, and a sense of growing into the community. One participant contrasted this with current age-restricted arrangements, suggesting that reduced cross-age mixing can limit opportunities for inclusion and being “taken in” by older peers:

“Back then, the youth club was for all ages, but today it has been changed into specific age groups. One of the best things for me was hanging out with people who were a little older, and it felt like a big deal to be accepted by that group. Later, when I got older myself, I remembered exactly how that felt, so I did the same for the younger ones who started coming to the club. They’re not always teenagers; some were children, age grouping does not suit us and didn’t exist before.” (P1)

Although communal experiences varied in form from seasonal celebrations to long-term involvement in local activities they served a common function: Creating opportunities to be included, recognised, and to contribute. One participant described how leading a dance group during adolescence offered a powerful sense of importance, pride, and connection to the wider community:

“When I was a teenager, I created my own choreography as a dance leader, and together with my dance group performed whenever the town came together for different events and activities. We felt so important, proud, and grown-up.” (P5)

Across accounts, communal activities such as choir, dance, sports, and shared local practices were described as meaningful pathways into connection and belonging. For this participant, feeling important, proud, and contributing through community performances appeared to strengthen self-worth during adolescence.

Alongside community participation, family life and close relational stability were described as central protective resources. Five participants spoke about the importance of a supportive partner, safety and acceptance at home, and patience over time as they moved through recovery:

“My wife was deeply patient with me. I have so much to thank her for; being consistent in her support, from reminding me why she and our children were the most important. Strange as it sounds, I needed to be reminded of that.” (P1)

“I had my wife to talk to about everything connected to my painful upbringing. I’m moved just thinking about her ability to put up with me when I couldn’t even put up with myself. We have six children today, and we are partners in our love and in our role as parents.” (P2)

For the five participants, the parental role and caring responsibilities created meaning and motivation to choose life. Five participants described how working through difficult experiences together with a life partner, rather than alone, helped reduce shame and supported a more stable daily life.

Taken together, these accounts position families and communal spaces as active arenas for healing, reassurance, and the long-term sustainability of coping with relational support often encountered in places and practices (e.g. youth club, rehearsals and performances, and communal gatherings) where participation enabled recognition, connection, and everyday meaning.

Culturally relevant trauma healing

Language access and cultural relevance in community services like Allorfik (National addiction centre) and municipal family support centres functioned as important protective factors. Being able to speak one’s own language in treatment settings enabled deeper emotional expression and reduced the distance between lived experience and professional support. Participants emphasised that it was not only access to services that mattered, but the form and content of the treatment itself. Several participants contrasted cultural and relationally grounded approaches with more certain standardised therapeutic formats that they experienced as less helpful in their specific contexts.

One participant described how conventional psychological sessions, structured around numerical self-assessments (e.g. rating feelings on a scale from 0–10), failed to capture the complexity of their lived experience. For one participant, encouragement from a high school principal to try a different treatment pathway led to engagement with Allorfik, which was experienced as significantly more meaningful:

“Here I could speak my own language with a kalaaleq inuk addiction counsellor. We wrote everything down absolutely all my feelings and sorted them together, how they affected me. The most important thing I learned was that it is me, and only me, who has responsibility for my own life. No one else can do it for me.” (P2)

Family-centred psychological support was also highlighted as particularly impactful. One participant described psychoeducational conversations that helped understand concepts such as the “window of tolerance,” (referring to the optimal arousal zone for emotional regulation) and why chronic suicidal thoughts can develop following complex and cumulative life experiences. One participant described the relief of having such thoughts normalised:

“When I was told that it is completely normal that I have chronic suicidal thoughts because of growing up with complex traumatic experiences... I lost my father in an accident, I have lost close relatives to suicide, and I was exposed to sexual abuse and neglect as a child that it is not strange that I have these thoughts.” (P3)

This reframing reduced shame and fear and enabled the participant to relate to suicidal thoughts without allowing them to dominate everyday life. Several participants described such psychological approaches, including psychoeducation and mindfulness, as helpful when they were delivered in ways that were culturally and relationally meaningful.

“I understood why my nervous system is often overactive, because my emotions are trying to protect me. Mindfulness helped me find calm and to understand that my fear is valid, but not the truth, because today I live without being in danger of those things happening again, but Its part of me and I cope with them.” (P3)

For others, Allorfik treatment facilitated processes of accountability, self-compassion, and reorientation toward a desired way of living. One participant described how experiences of exclusion at school generated feelings of shame that later contributed to substance use and violence in close relationships. Through treatment, these patterns were addressed and reframed:

“Through Allorfik, I had my substance use normalised as something I developed, not who I am. We worked with self-compassion and the way forward, how I want to live my life. I started following that: I earn my own money, I can go to the hairdresser, get my nails done if I want. Today I go to parties without drinking alcohol and still dance all night. I have been sober for many years, and I am an addiction counsellor today. I am proud of that.” (P5)

Changes in substance use and lifestyle were also associated with shifts in social belonging. One participant described losing friendships after stopping cannabis use and dealing, resulting in profound loneliness. Gradually, reconnection with culturally meaningful practices such as sailing and hunting opened new social spaces:

“All my friends disappeared when I stopped smoking and selling hash. I became very lonely. My wife stayed by my side. Little by little I started sailing again and going hunting. There I found new friends. We joke and laugh about the old days when we used to party, but today we are adults and take responsibility by teaching our sons how to hunt to bring food home to our families.” (P1)

Reconnection with culturally meaningful practices also extended into family life and everyday responsibilities. One participant described how treatment at Allorfik supported a deeper process of grief work and personal maturation, linked to both substance use and relational difficulties. The participant reflected on how unresolved childhood loss had shaped later behaviour, and how processing this grief enabled a shift toward adult responsibility and participation within the family:

“Eating together with the family after hunting became important. Allorfik helped me realise that I choose what kind of life I want to live, and why I behaved immaturely after losing my highly respected grandfather as a child. I never processed that grief and instead dulled it with hash and alcohol. When I worked through the grief, my brain grew into being able to think like an adult.” (P1)

After years of contact with psychiatric services without recognition of early sexual abuse, one participant described how biomedical approaches were experienced, leaving some central experiences unaddressed.

“I was in the system for 18 years and nobody ever asked me if I had been exposed to sexual abuse… In the end, I was told that the last thing we could try was electroconvulsive therapy. I said no.” (P6)

For this participant turning away from biomedical approaches opened space for exploring Inuit understandings of mental distress and healing. Through shamanistic practices and reflection on family lineage, the participant reframed fear and suffering as communicative rather than pathological:

“I started to delve into the Inuit worldview and how our ancestors treated mental illness… Through intuitive skills, I healed wounds from adverse childhood experiences.” (P6)

Another participant described a different, but related, form of culturally grounded healing gained through a community-based personal development course in adolescence:

“We hiked the mountain together, threw out heavy stones and screamed our pain… Our therapist was Kalaaleq Inuk and spoke our language, which was very supportive for us from the village.” (P4)

Although the activities and approaches differed, all six participants described healing processes that drew on culturally grounded frameworks, including language access, relational safety, land-based practices, and Indigenous understandings of emotional pain. These perspectives helped transform shame into meaning, connected past experiences with present identity, and opened pathways for agency and recovery beyond what standardised psychological formats could offer. Across accounts, one participant emphasised the importance of being met as a human being rather than as a diagnosis. The participant described how spiritual grounding and culturally rooted healing practices such as support groups, literature, poetry, songs, and elements of shamanic tradition provided strength, helped make meaning, and fostered continuity and connection beyond the immediate suicidal crisis. Taken together, these accounts suggest that both culturally grounded approaches and psychological or psychiatric support can play important roles in recovery when they are experienced as relevant, relational, and responsive to participants’ lived realities.

Choosing life: turning points and restored hope and resilience

All participants described that they experienced turning points that became important markers in their process toward healing. They described turning points as moments or gradual processes where something shifted in them and led them toward choosing life. These included finding renewed meaning, hope, and strength; acknowledging problems and daring to seek help; and making choices that pointed forward even when uncertainty about the future remained. All six participants told about developing self-compassion after crisis and integrating painful experiences into a life story that allowed dignity and growth rather than rupture. One participant described acceptance of the new personality as central to sustaining recovery:

“The most important thing for being able to maintain my recovery was that I accepted my new identity who I was, who I have become, and why I became the person I am so I could keep holding on to the version of me that has healed.” (P1)

Overall, these turning points were described as the results of changes supported by relationships, cultural grounding, nature, and belonging rather than functioning in isolation, these elements formed a dynamic, relational system. Through activities such as hunting, dance, communal gatherings, storytelling, therapy, treatment or spiritual practices, participants experienced connection, agency, and continuity, enabling them to rebuild hope and move forward in life.

Discussion

Inuit worldview, Sila, and relationships with land as stabilising and restorative

Rather than functioning as a neutral backdrop, what is often referred to as “nature” was described through relationships with land and Sila as living and relational presences that supported calmness, perspective, and self-regulation in moments of distress, as well as spaces where coherence could be gradually restored after periods of crisis. These accounts were closely connected to Inuit relational understandings of the world, particularly Sila and Inua, both of which emphasise the interconnectedness between inner life, land, weather, breath, and the vitality inherent in all elements of existence. Participants articulated how these frameworks were not abstract philosophical ideas but lived experiences encountered through the horizon, the silence, the movement of weather, and the sensation of being held by the land.

This interpretation aligns with those of circumpolar Indigenous scholars that describe land not simply as environment but as an active relational force in processes of healing, grounding, and meaning-making [26]. In this literature, land-connected practices support regulation through embodied engagement and sensory experience, which can be particularly relevant when distress is expressed somatically or through restlessness, grief, or dysregulated emotional states. Inuit youth wellbeing programmes similarly emphasise the importance of embedding healing within cultural and land-based practices from the outset culture as treatment rather than “culture added to treatment” [9].

The significance of place and land-connectedness is further underscored in circumpolar suicide prevention guidance such as RISING SUN, which highlights that wellbeing for Arctic Indigenous youth is shaped through relational, place-anchored, and culturally meaningful pathways [12]. The significance of these relational and land-connected experiences in our findings resonates with this emphasis by showing how participants experienced regulation, clarity, and renewed stability through relational encounters with land, weather, and silence.These were not merely aesthetic or recreational meetings but moments of grounding, reconnection, and continuity that supported movement away from suicidal crisis.

In a Kalaallit Inuit context, these descriptions also align with locally grounded health frameworks such as Peqqissuserput (Our Health), which conceptualises wellbeing as emerging from relationships with land, culture, community, and more-than-human worlds rather than existing within the individual alone [27]. Participants’ reflections that some traumatised children and youth find it difficult to benefit from Western-style, sit-and-listen learning environments further underscore the relevance of land-connected regulation and embodied practices. Six participants described that being on the land allowed emotions to settle, thoughts to clarify, and bodily tension to ease creating conditions for meaning-making that were difficult to access elsewhere.

These perspectives suggest that what is often translated as “nature” is more accurately understood through Sila, Inua, and relational connections with land. Nature helped participants establish continuity after experiences of rupture, reconnect with values and identities disrupted by crisis, and restore emotional balance in ways that were culturally congruent, relationally grounded, and experientially accessible. Rather than being peripheral or symbolic, nature emerged as a primary healing resource that supported resilience through presence, rhythm, and relational coherence.

A further interpretive insight concerns how nature offered a form of non-performative space, a setting in which participants did not need to manage social expectations, regulate others’ emotions, or perform identities. In this analysis, being alone on the land functioned as a space to breathe, relax, and simply “be,” where the usual demands of relational or institutional contexts were suspended. Read through Inuit relational concepts such as Sila and Inua, this aloneness can be understood not as withdrawal, but as being held within an animate world where land, weather, and living elements provide a sense of companionship and coherence. In this view, aloneness becomes a form of restorative presence rather than loneliness, a mode of grounding where emotional intensity can ease, perspectives can widen, and a sense of inner alignment can develop.

Supportive relationships

Our study demonstrates that respectful human presence and being listened to without judgement established decisive protective experiences. Participants emphasised the importance of having open and honest conversations about suicide, as well as of having reliable adults and professionals during their time of vulnerability. This suggests that supportive relationships require relational processes that restore trust, consistency, and hope. This aligns closely with Thomas Joiner’s Interpersonal Theory of Suicide [28,29] which identifies thwarted belonging and perceived burdensomeness as central drivers of suicidal desire. Participants’ descriptions of being listened to and taken seriously can therefore be understood as directly reducing these mechanisms. Open dialogue about suicidal thoughts does not increase risk; rather, research shows that it can interrupt isolation, reduce shame, and enhance insight [30,31].

Importantly, relational protection extended beyond one-to-one meetings. Support was also experienced through participation in shared activities and community spaces, highlighting that relational safety was often embedded in being with others, and taking part in everyday communal life. This resonates with circumpolar suicide prevention indicators identified in the RISING SUN initiative [12] which emphasise strengthened connectedness, peer and community support, and culturally safe environments as core components in effective interventions. Similar relational resilience processes are documented in Arctic youth research, where protective pathways frequently involve trusted adults, peer solidarity, and collective engagement [32,33].

Participants’ descriptions further highlighted that participation was a protective factor because it created opportunities to be valued, included, and recognised as meaningful contributors in community life. Protection was not solely about receiving care, but also about contributing, being useful, and feeling that one mattered to others. These findings align well-established research demonstrating that sense of belonging is a fundamental human motivation and a central determinant of mental health [34,35]. The experience of mattering and reciprocal contribution has been shown to reduce suicidal ideation and strengthen resilience, particularly among youth [25,36].

These community-based relational processes were supported by a variety of local contexts. Youth clubs, sports houses, community centres, and seasonal gatherings functioned as inclusive, low-threshold environments where participants could connect across age groups, observe and learn from older peers, and gradually grow into a sense of social place and identity. Such spaces created conditions in which participants could participate without performance pressure, build trust, and experience everyday recognition. This is also consistent with Greenlandic wellbeing frameworks such as Peqqissuserput, which emphasise relational health, community connectedness, and participation in meaningful activities as central to wellbeing [27]. Community-based initiatives such as Atatsimorluta Aalasa similarly operationalise participation, movement, and collective engagement as pathways to strengthened social cohesion and health [37]. Emerging Greenlandic scholarship underscores that structured community participation and collective activity reinforce shared identity, visibility, and mutual responsibility [38].

Within families, stability and close relational safety appeared as particularly powerful sources of protection. Participants described partner support, acceptance in the home, and shared processing of difficult experiences as essential to sustaining recovery over time. For some, caring responsibilities and everyday family roles generated renewed purpose and reinforced commitment to life. These accounts highlight how intimate relationships function as regulatory environments, where patience, reassurance, and continuity buffer shame and loneliness and stabilise recovery trajectories.

These findings highlight that protection emerges through relational presence embedded in community life. Supportive relationships, participation and belonging constitute a multi-layered relational infrastructure that reduces existential isolation, restores coherence, and re-establishes a sense of mattering within the social world. Supportive relationships, in this sense, function not as a singular resource but as an active, dynamic regulatory process shaped by people, places, and shared practices that together help individuals move away from suicidal crisis and toward renewed engagement with life.

Culturally relevant trauma healing

The present findings demonstrate that language access and cultural relevance in trauma treatment settings are not peripheral but central protective mechanisms. Community services such as Allorfik (the National Addiction Centre), municipal family support centres, and other local counselling contexts were described as meaningful not only because they were available, but because they were culturally and relationally congruent. Being able to speak one’s own language in treatment enabled deeper emotional articulation and reduced the distance between lived experience and professional support. This is consistent with growing literature demonstrating that language-congruent and culturally grounded care strengthens therapeutic alliance, trust, and treatment engagement in Indigenous contexts [39,40].

Culturally relevant addiction treatment has been shown to improve retention and long-term recovery outcomes, particularly when it integrates relational, community-based, and identity-affirming approaches [41,42]. These elements correspond closely with participants’ descriptions of accountability, self-compassion, grief processing, and reorientation toward adult responsibility. Importantly, participants emphasised that what mattered was not merely access to services, but whether the form of care resonated with their language, history, and worldview.

This emphasis on relational and epistemological alignment also sheds light on participants’ accounts of psychiatric care and medication. A distinctive contribution of the findings is how they complicate a narrow biomedical framing of distress. One participant described psychiatric medication as causing more harm than benefit, contrasted with healing through culturally grounded ancestral knowledge and spiritual practice. Such narratives do not determine clinical effectiveness, but they reveal how treatment may be experienced as misaligned when it fails to acknowledge trauma histories, spiritual frameworks, or culturally meaningful forms of regulation. This is connected to longstanding critiques of biomedical reductionism in mental health. Engel’s biopsychosocial model emphasised that suffering must be understood through psychological meaning and social context, not solely biological dysfunction [43]. Later work clarified that biopsychosocial practice requires relational engagement and reflexivity, not merely expanded diagnostic protocols [44,45]. In Nordic psychiatry, Aarre has similarly cautioned against over-medicalizing distress and over-relying on diagnostic categories [46,47]. Read alongside these perspectives, the present findings suggest that culturally grounded and relationally congruent pathways can be central to recovery, while clinical interventions may be helpful when experienced as respectful, context-sensitive, and dialogical.

The emphasis participants placed on being met as a person rather than as a diagnosis also resonates with Inuit health system scholarship. Akearok and colleagues argue that Inuit-centred health care approaches must prioritise relational safety, cultural continuity, and Inuit knowledge systems in order to reduce mistrust and improve outcomes [48]. Participants’ descriptions of shamanistic and spiritually anchored healing illustrate how culturally grounded meaning-making can function as a recovery mechanism, particularly when they experience that biomedical services have not helped. Indigenous mental health scholarship increasingly acknowledges the legitimacy of spiritual and land-based healing practices as complementary regulatory systems rather than cultural artefacts [26,49]. In this context, the evaluation discussion becomes directly relevant. Northern implementation partnership literature argues that evaluation criteria must reflect locally defined wellbeing indicators rather than externally imposed biomedical metrics [10,46]. The present findings therefore help specify culturally congruent domains that can guide both practice and feasible evaluation (e.g. language access, cultural safety, relational alignment, land-based regulation, belonging), including mixed-method approaches that incorporate story-based evidence alongside other indicators [9,12,48]. At the same time, broader European monitoring indicates rising mental health burden among children and young people [50,51], which may contribute to pressures toward quicker categorisation and medicalization. The present findings suggest that strengthening early relational, culturally grounded, and trauma-informed supports may offer alternative or complementary pathways that address distress before it becomes narrowly framed as disorder, while still maintaining access to clinical pathways when desired and helpful. Culturally relevant treatment in Kalaallit Nunaat emerges not merely as a question of translation or service provision, but of epistemological alignment. Treatment that recognises language, history, relational accountability, and Indigenous frameworks may function as an active mechanism of suicide prevention by restoring dignity, coherence, and agency within culturally meaningful worlds.

While the present study does not seek to undermine psychiatric services, particularly for individuals living with psychiatric disorders that require medical treatment, it is notable that none of the participants were psychiatric patients at the time of the interviews. One participant had previously been treated at the psychiatric department for long-term depression, understood in their own narrative as arising from unprocessed childhood sexual abuse, neglect, and violence rather than from an underlying psychiatric condition. This underscores that suicidal crises in Kalaallit Nunaat often unfold within complex social, relational, and historical contexts rather than solely through biomedical pathways. In a setting where mental health services face substantial workforce deficits, it is therefore significant that participants described meaningful healing processes occurring outside formal psychiatric care, through culturally grounded, relational, and land-connected resources. This does not diminish the value of psychiatry; rather, it highlights the need for respectful and collaborative complementary healing pathways. Psychiatric treatment remains essential for those who benefit from medication or require clinical stabilisation. At the same time, the findings illustrate that culturally anchored approaches drawing on language, relational safety, ancestral knowledge, and community-based practices can play a vital role in restoring coherence and intervention for individuals whose distress is rooted in trauma, social adversity, and disrupted belonging. Recognising this variety of healing pathways may strengthen suicide prevention by ensuring that biomedical and culturally grounded approaches are not positioned as oppositional, but as potentially complementary components of a broader, contextually responsive support system.

Choosing life turning points and restored hope and resilience

Understanding trauma responses, emotions, and bodily stress reactions was described as protective and linked to turning points. This strengthens the case for prevention that includes emotional literacy and life skills guidance early in the life course, especially for those experiencing early exposure to alcohol or substance use [52–54]. Such priorities are consistent with circumpolar calls to evaluate prevention using outcomes beyond suicide rates, including improved coping skills among children and youth, strengthened supportive environments, improved access to caring responses, and prevention of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and other sources of trauma [12,55,56]. The present findings add Greenland-specific descriptions of what these outcomes can look like in lived experience, and how they may contribute to recovery over time.

Turning points were described by participants as processes of reconnection to relationships, culture, land, Sila, and belonging, as well as the gradual rebuilding of daily life. This conceptualisation draws on ethnographically informed analytic approaches that attend to lived transitions and narrative re-orientation over time rather than discrete outcome moments [57]. Within resilience and recovery research, turning points have similarly been described as relationally embedded processes that emerge through shifts in meaning, identity, and social positioning rather than sudden change [58,59]. This view aligns with implementation research arguing that prevention should strengthen ongoing community work rather than relying solely on importing fixed interventions [10]. From an ethnographic perspective, attention to turning points enables identification of locally meaningful catalysts of change as they are narrated and interpreted by participants themselves. Practically, prevention and care pathways may therefore aim to create conditions that enable turning points through the promotion of safe relationships, culturally grounded spaces, and opportunities for participation.

Public health implications for suicide prevention in Kalaallit Nunaat

The present study contributes with locally grounded knowledge about protective processes following suicidal crisis in Kalaallit Nunaat. Rather than proposing a new intervention model, the findings specify contextually meaningful domains that can inform the strengthening of existing community, family, and service-based efforts. This underscores a broader need within Arctic suicide prevention: the development of knowledge and support systems generated within local contexts and responsive to Indigenous lifeworlds.

The study supports a public health approach to suicide prevention that strengthens relational environments, culturally congruent care, and community participation as foundational protective infrastructures. Developing evaluation approaches that are epistemologically aligned with these domains may help ensure that prevention efforts are both locally meaningful and systemically sustainable. As a practice-oriented synthesis of the study’s findings, the “Paths to Life” map is presented in Appendix A. The map translates central themes from qualitative material into a structured conversational tool intended to support therapeutic work with individuals experiencing suicidal thoughts and or have attempted suicide, particularly in phases where attention gradually shifts from validation of life pain toward motivation for life, hope, and future direction. Following completion of the study, the map has been independently reviewed by the leadership of Inulerivik, Greenland’s National Therapy and Counselling Centre [60]. The clinical lead, inuk psychologist, assessed the tool as addressing a concrete gap in current therapeutic practice, particularly regarding the motivational and hope-oriented components of suicide-preventive care. Specifically, the tool was highlighted as relevant in later phases of therapy, following repeated sessions centred on validation of suffering, when therapist and client begin to work more explicitly with motivation, hope, meaning, and direction. Inulerivik has expressed an intention to implement the map among its Greenlandic psychotherapists as a structured conversational aid in therapeutic pathways with individuals presenting with suicidal ideation, once clients are assessed as ready for such focus.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this pilot study is the depth of the six interviews and the coherence of protective processes across narratives, generating detailed Greenlandic insight into recovery mechanisms that remain under-described in suicide prevention research. The study is not intended for statistical generalisation but for analytic transferability, offering conceptually rich descriptions that may inform similar small-population and Indigenous contexts.

A core limitation is that the sample size is small and consists of individuals who are currently thriving following suicidal crisis. The findings therefore reflect retrospective narratives of recovery and may not capture the perspectives of those who remain in acute distress or who are disengaged from services. The analysis is based on self-reported accounts of turning points and protective processes. Such narratives are shaped by memory, meaning-making, and present identity, and therefore represent interpreted experience rather than direct causal evidence. While this is consistent with the ethnographically informed analytic approach, it limits conclusions about effectiveness of specific interventions.

Finally, although analytic reflexivity was applied throughout, alternative readings of the material are possible. The aim has been depth and contextual understanding rather than representativeness.

Conclusion

This qualitative pilot study documents coping resources and turning points among six Kalaallit Inuit participants who described their pathway from crisis to recovery. The findings indicate that recovery is fostered through relational safety, cultural grounding, connections to nature, belonging and participation, language access, and culturally relevant support. The findings also underscore the importance of expanding responses beyond narrow biomedical framings, recognising that some individuals experience medication and diagnostic pathways as unhelpful or harmful, while culturally grounded and relational pathways can be experienced as restoring meaning, coherence, and connection.

At the same time, the findings point to the importance of bridging biomedical and culturally grounded approaches rather than positioning them as opposing systems. While psychiatric treatment remains essential for individuals who require medical support, the present study illustrates that culturally anchored, relational, and land-connected forms of healing can also play a decisive role in restoring paths to life after suicide crisis. Future work should therefore explore how these approaches can complement one another in practice, ensuring that clinical care remains context-sensitive, respectful, and aligned with the lived worlds of those seeking support.

Future work should examine how these protective processes can be strengthened earlier in the life course and operationalized across municipal, educational, and service settings in Greenland.

This is particularly relevant in a context where much suicide related distress is socially and historically shaped rather than rooted in chronic psychiatric illness, and where healthcare workforce deficits mean that everyday settings frequently become the first and most accessible arenas for support. Strengthening protective relational environments across schools, youth programmes, municipal services, family support systems, and community-based initiatives may therefore represent a crucial public-health pathway for suicide prevention in Kalaallit Nunaat.

Community dissemination

Study findings were shared back to the community through oral presentations in Nuuk in February 2024 in collaboration with Kommuneqarfik Sermersooq (Municipality of Nuuk, Paamiut and Eastgreenland), including a public event attended by more than 100 citizens [61]. The first author presented the findings and facilitated dialogue together with the citizens, including panel with the participants from the study. Findings were later presented at Qamani’s national conference on suicide prevention in Nuuk, November 2025, where 2 of the participants from this study presented the results with the first author [62].

Prior publication

Prior to journal submission, a non- academic, community-oriented dissemination product based on the study findings (Paths to Life) (Appendix A) was shared publicly through the University of Southern Denmark (SIF) website under Centre for Public Health in Greenland and social media platforms on Facebook, Instagram and LinkedIn resources in December 2025, with the aim of providing accessible, hope-oriented knowledge during the winter period [63].
