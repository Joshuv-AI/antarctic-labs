Sort, Partition, Randomize: Optimal Binary Hypothesis Testing under Local Differential Privacy
Elena Ghazi, Jawad Nasser, Flavio Calmon, Ibrahim Issa

Abstract
We study optimal design of ε \varepsilon -locally differentially private mechanisms for binary hypothesis testing. Each observation is drawn from one of two known distributions P 0 , P 1 P_{0},P_{1} on a finite alphabet of size k k , privatized by a mechanism Q Q , and then used to infer which distribution generated the data. We measure testing utility using an f f -divergence—including total variation, KL, and hockey-stick divergences—between the two induced output distributions. Previous work established structural properties of optimal mechanisms, but only yielded exponential-time algorithms. We prove a sharp structure: for every ε \varepsilon and every f f -divergence objective, after sorting the alphabet by likelihood ratio, there exists an optimal mechanism that partitions the sorted alphabet into contiguous blocks and applies randomized response to the block label. We call this class Sort-Partition-Randomize (SPR). This characterization yields an exact dynamic program that computes an optimal mechanism in O ⁡ ( k 3 ) O(k^{3}) time, and more generally in O ⁡ ( ℓ ​ k 2 ) O(\ell k^{2}) time with an ℓ \ell -output budget. Our results make it possible to efficiently compute and characterize the exact optimum across the full privacy range, beyond asymptotic privacy regimes.



1
Introduction

Balancing privacy and utility is a central challenge in data disclosure control.
A canonical instance of this challenge is privacy-preserving binary hypothesis testing: a set of samples is drawn from one of two distributions, denoted by
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
, and each sample is randomized to ensure privacy.
An analyst then performs a hypothesis test to infer which distribution generated the samples given the privatized outputs.
A natural measure of how much testing power survives randomization is an
f
-divergence between the privatized output distributions induced by
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
.
Different choices of
f
capture different operational quantities: total variation determines the probability of correct guessing under a balanced prior (i.e., when the two hypotheses are equally likely),
E_{\gamma}
(or hockey-stick) divergences trace operating points along the Neyman–Pearson ROC curve, and KL divergence controls the exponential decay of error probabilities as the analyst accumulates samples
[
5
,
2
,
9
]
.

We study privacy-preserving binary hypothesis testing under local differential privacy (LDP).
LDP
[
8
,
11
]
is among the most stringent notions of privacy, requiring each sample to be randomized individually prior to disclosure.
However, this strictness comes at a substantial utility cost in statistical applications
[
6
,
3
]
.
A natural design objective is therefore to find LDP mechanisms that maximize an
f
-divergence between the privatized output distributions induced by
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
.

Formally, for
\nu\in\{0,1\}
, an observation
X\sim P_{\nu}
on a finite alphabet
\mathcal{X}
of size
k
is privatized through an
\varepsilon
-locally differentially private channel
Q(\cdot\mid x)
with finite output alphabet
\mathcal{Y}
, producing an output
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
. The
\varepsilon
-LDP constraint requires that for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,

\displaystyle Q(y\mid x)\leq e^{\varepsilon}Q(y\mid x^{\prime}).

(1)

Denote by
M_{\nu}:=Q^{\top}P_{\nu}
the output distribution induced by
P_{\nu}
and
Q
. Given a convex function
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
with
f(1)=0
, our goal is to design
Q
to maximize the
f
-divergence defined as:

\displaystyle D_{f}(M_{0}\|M_{1}):=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}M_{1}(y)f\!\Big(\tfrac{M_{0}(y)}{M_{1}(y)}\Big).

(2)

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}
be the set of
\varepsilon
-LDP channels (with input alphabet
\mathcal{X}
). We can formulate our optimization as:

\displaystyle\max_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}}D_{f}(Q^{\top}P_{0}\|Q^{\top}P_{1}).

(3)

We also consider the case in which the output alphabet is constrained to be less than or equal to a given size
\ell
(referred to as a communication constraint by
9
). To wit, we replace
\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}
in (
3
) by
\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,\ell}
, the set of
\varepsilon
-LDP channels with at most
\ell
outputs:

\displaystyle\max_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,\ell}}D_{f}(Q^{\top}P_{0}\|Q^{\top}P_{1}).

(4)

Despite the relative simplicity of the formulations, exact optimal mechanisms were previously understood only in special regimes: a binary-output mechanism for all
f
-divergences at sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, and randomized response on the original alphabet for KL at sufficiently large
\varepsilon

[
7
]
(the latter requires
\ell\geq k
in the constrained case). The intermediate regime, where privacy and utility must genuinely be balanced—and arguably the case of greatest practical interest—has remained computationally out of reach: although significant results on the structure of the optimizers have been derived by Kairouz et al.
[
7
]
and Pensia et al.
[
9
]
, the resulting algorithms are exponential in
k
(in the unconstrained output size case) or in
\ell
(in the constrained case).

Our dynamic program follows from a sharp structural characterization of the optimizers of (

), formally stated in Theorem

Beyond computational efficiency, SPR mechanisms yield a simple interpretable description of the optimal privatization strategy: aggregate symbols with similar likelihood ratios, then privatize only the resulting coarse label. Furthermore, the optimality of SPR mechanisms immediately implies that an optimal mechanism does not require more than

3)

We recover and extend optimality results when

Prior work.

7
showed that, for a broad class of utilities including
f
-divergences, an optimal
\varepsilon
-LDP mechanism may be chosen to be
staircase
: for each output
y
, the probabilities
Q(y\mid x)
take only two values,
\theta_{y}
and
e^{\varepsilon}\theta_{y}
, as
x
varies. This reduces the optimization over arbitrary mechanisms to a finite linear program with one variable for each possible high set
H\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, hence
2^{k}
variables. They also identified simple optimal mechanisms in extreme regimes: a binary-output mechanism for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
for any
f
-divergence, and randomized response on the original alphabet (i.e.,
k
-ary randomized response) for sufficiently large
\varepsilon
in the KL case, with distribution-dependent
\varepsilon
thresholds. Our work replaces this exponential search by an
O(k^{3})
dynamic program, giving exact optimization across the full privacy range.

9
studied the geometry of achievable pairs of output distributions under LDP and communication constraints. In the
\varepsilon
-LDP setting with an
\ell
-output budget, they showed that extreme achievable pairs can be realized by first quantizing the input using likelihood-ratio thresholds into at most
2\ell^{2}
intermediate symbols, and then applying an extreme
\varepsilon
-LDP channel from this intermediate alphabet to the
\ell
outputs. This gives an algorithm with runtime polynomial in
k^{\ell^{2}}
and
2^{O(\ell^{3}\log\ell)}

[
9
, Corollary 4]
, hence polynomial in
k
only when
\ell
is treated as a constant. Building on this geometric perspective and a perturbation-based argument, we sharpen the structure substantially: in the pure LDP,
f
-divergence setting, the intermediate quantizer and arbitrary private channel collapse to an SPR mechanism, yielding the
O(\ell k^{2})
dynamic program.

10
studied the non-private analogue and showed the optimality of likelihood-ratio quantizers for broad classes of binary testing and quantization problems. Our SPR mechanisms are the private counterpart: sort by likelihood ratio, partition into contiguous blocks, and then apply randomized response. As
\varepsilon\to\infty
, randomized response becomes deterministic, so SPR mechanisms recover the non-private likelihood-ratio quantizers.

2
Problem setup and SPR mechanisms

This section fixes notation and introduces the two structural objects on which the rest of the paper is built: staircase mechanisms and sort–partition–randomize (SPR) mechanisms.

Notation.

We recall the setup from Section
1
. An observation
X\sim P_{\nu}
for
\nu\in\{0,1\}
on a finite alphabet
\mathcal{X}
is privatized through an
\varepsilon
-LDP channel
Q
with finite output alphabet
\mathcal{Y}
, i.e.,
Q(y\mid x)\leq e^{\varepsilon}Q(y\mid x^{\prime})
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, producing an output
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
. The induced output marginals are
M_{\nu}:=Q^{\top}P_{\nu}
, and utility is measured by an
f
-divergence
D_{f}(M_{0}\|M_{1})
for a convex
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
with
f(1)=0
. The goal is to design
Q
to maximize
D_{f}(M_{0}\|M_{1})
.

Likelihood-ratio ordering and reduction of ties.

We first remove a few degenerate cases so that the likelihood-ratio order is strict, which is the setting used in intermediate arguments.
Deleting symbols with zero mass under both hypotheses and merging symbols with the same likelihood ratio
P_{0}(x)/P_{1}(x)
does not change the induced output pair
(M_{0},M_{1})
of any
\varepsilon
-LDP mechanism: within each likelihood-ratio class, replacing the corresponding rows by the appropriate weighted average preserves
\varepsilon
-LDP and leaves both induced marginals unchanged. Conversely, any mechanism on the reduced alphabet can be lifted to the original alphabet by copying its row to all symbols in the corresponding class. We relabel the reduced likelihood-ratio-sorted alphabet as
[k]
, and write
p,q
for the reduced distributions. Thus
k
may be smaller than
|\mathcal{X}|
, and

\displaystyle 0\leq r_{1}<r_{2}<\cdots<r_{k}\leq\infty,\qquad r_{i}:=\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}.

(5)

Output constraints and the joint range.

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}
denote the set of
\varepsilon
-LDP channels with input alphabet
[k]
. For
\ell\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,\ell}
denote the set of
\ell
-output
\varepsilon
-LDP channels,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,\ell}:=\Bigl\{Q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k\times\ell}:Q\mathbf{1}_{\ell}=\mathbf{1}_{k},\ Q\text{ is }\varepsilon\text{-LDP}\Bigr\},

(6)

and the fixed-output joint range

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(p,q):=\Bigl\{(Q^{\top}p,Q^{\top}q):Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,\ell}\Bigr\}.

(7)

We assume
\varepsilon>0
throughout. When
\varepsilon=0
, every feasible channel is input-independent, so
Q^{\top}p=Q^{\top}q
and every
f
-divergence objective is zero.

Staircase mechanisms.

For any channel
Q
, an output
y\in\mathcal{Y}
is
active
if
Q(y\mid\cdot)
is not identically zero.

Definition 2.1

(Staircase mechanism and high set)
.

An
\varepsilon
-LDP mechanism
Q
is a
staircase mechanism
if, for every active
y
and for all
x
and
x^{\prime}
,
Q(y|x)/Q(y|x^{\prime})\in\{e^{-\varepsilon},1,e^{\varepsilon}\}.
Moreover, for each
y
, we define the
high set

H_{y}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
as:

\displaystyle H_{y}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:Q(y|x)=\max_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}Q(y|x^{\prime})\}.

(8)

If
Q
is staircase, it will be convenient to express each active column
y
as

\displaystyle Q(i,y)=\theta_{y}\Bigl(1+(e^{\varepsilon}-1)\mathbf{1}\{i\in H_{y}\}\Bigr),\qquad\theta_{y}>0,\ H_{y}\subseteq[k].

(9)

We call
\theta_{y}
the column scale and
H_{y}
the
high set
of column
y
. When an active staircase column is constant, i.e.,
Q(i,y)\equiv\alpha_{y}>0
, we write it as

\displaystyle Q(i,y)=\alpha_{y}e^{-\varepsilon}\Bigl(1+(e^{\varepsilon}-1)\mathbf{1}\{i\in[k]\}\Bigr).

(10)

With this convention, every active high set is nonempty.

SPR mechanisms and example.

If
\tau:[m]\to[\ell]
is a deterministic map and
Q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k\times m}
, we write
\tau\circ Q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k\times\ell}
for the post-processed channel
(\tau\circ Q)(i,y):=\sum_{z:\tau(z)=y}Q(i,z)
. An
LR-contiguous partition
of
[k]
is a partition
\pi=(B_{1},\dots,B_{s})
into nonempty intervals in the likelihood-ratio order.

Definition 2.2

(Sort–partition–randomize mechanism)
.

A
sort–partition–randomize
(SPR) mechanism first sorts the input symbols as
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
by likelihood ratio
P_{0}(x_{i})/P_{1}(x_{i})
, partitions this ordered alphabet into contiguous blocks
\pi=(B_{1},\dots,B_{s})
, and then applies
s
-ary randomized response (RR) to the block label:

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}(j\mid x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{e^{\varepsilon}}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1},&x\in B_{j},\\[5.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1},&x\notin B_{j}.\end{cases}

(11)

Equivalently,
Q^{\pi}
is a staircase mechanism whose active high sets are exactly the likelihood-ratio-contiguous blocks
B_{1},\dots,B_{s}
. When needed, we may also view
Q^{\pi}
as a channel with a larger output alphabet by relabeling its
s
active outputs and padding the remaining columns with zeros.

Main results

This section collects the headline statements of the paper. Section
3.1
states the structural characterization of optimal mechanisms; Section
3.2
states the resulting polynomial-time algorithm; Sections
3.3
and
3.4
state consequences for
E_{\gamma}

3.1
SPR optimality theorem

Our main structural result is that, after sorting the alphabet by likelihood ratio, every extreme point of the fixed-output joint range
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(p,q)
is attained by an SPR mechanism.

Theorem 3.1

(Extreme points are sort–partition–randomize)
.

If
(u,v)\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(p,q)
is an extreme point, then there exists an LR-contiguous partition
\pi=(B_{1},\dots,B_{s})
of
[k]
such that
(u,v)
is induced by an
\ell
-output channel obtained from
Q^{\pi}
by relabeling its active outputs and padding with zero columns. In particular, necessarily
s\leq\ell
and
s\leq k
. Equivalently, every extreme point of
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(p,q)
is realized by a sort–partition–randomize mechanism.

Since every
f
-divergence is convex in the induced pair
(M_{0},M_{1})
, the maximum over
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(p,q)
is attained at an extreme point. Theorem
3.1
therefore yields the following optimality statement.

Corollary 3.2

(SPR optimality for
f
-divergences)
.

For every
f
-divergence, the following hold.

1.

Over all finite-output
\varepsilon
-LDP mechanisms, the optimum is attained by an SPR mechanism with at most
k
active outputs.

2.

For any output budget
1\leq\ell\leq k
,
\max_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,\ell}}D_{f}(Q^{\top}p\|Q^{\top}q)
is attained by an SPR mechanism with at most
\ell
active outputs.

3.2
Exact dynamic program

Corollary
3.2
reduces the search for an optimal mechanism to a search over LR-contiguous partitions of
[k]
. We exploit the additive decomposability of
f
-divergences across blocks to obtain an exact dynamic program.

For
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}
, let
\displaystyle\mu(z):=(q^{\top}z)\,f\!\left(\frac{p^{\top}z}{q^{\top}z}\right)
be the column score associated with the chosen
f
-divergence. For
1\leq a\leq b\leq k
, let

\displaystyle\mu[a:b]:=\mu\!\left(\bigl(1+(e^{\varepsilon}-1)\mathbf{1}\{a\leq i\leq b\}\bigr)_{i=1}^{k}\right).

(12)

Proposition 3.3

(Dynamic program with an output budget)
.

Fix
1\leq\ell\leq k
. For
1\leq s\leq\ell
and
s\leq i\leq k
, let
F[s,i]
be the maximum raw score over all partitions of
[1:i]
into exactly
s
nonempty contiguous blocks. Then
F[1,i]=\mu[1:i]
, and for
s\geq 2
,

\displaystyle F[s,i]=\max_{s-1\leq t<i}\Bigl\{F[s-1,t]+\mu[t+1:i]\Bigr\}.

(13)

The optimal
\ell
-output utility is
\displaystyle\max_{1\leq s\leq\ell}\frac{F[s,k]}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1}
.

Corollary 3.4

(Runtime)
.

For an output budget
1\leq\ell\leq k
, the optimal value and an optimal SPR mechanism can be computed in
O(\ell k^{2})
time and
O(\ell k)
space. In particular, the unconstrained-output problem is obtained by taking
\ell=k
, giving
O(k^{3})
time and
O(k^{2})
space.

The proofs of Proposition
3.3
and Corollary
3.4
, together with pseudocode for the algorithm, are given in Section
5
.

3.3
Closed-form optimal mechanism for
E_{\gamma}
-divergences

For
\gamma\geq 1
, define the
E_{\gamma}
- (or hockey-stick) divergence

\displaystyle E_{\gamma}(P_{0}\|P_{1}):=\sup_{A\subseteq\mathcal{X}}\bigl(P_{0}(A)-\gamma P_{1}(A)\bigr),

(14)

which corresponds to the convex function
f_{\gamma}(t)=(t-\gamma)_{+}
, with
\gamma=1
recovering total variation. The
E_{\gamma}
-divergence has a direct testing interpretation. If a test decides
P_{0}
on an event
A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
, then
M_{0}(A^{c})
is its missed-detection probability and
M_{1}(A)
is its false-alarm probability. Therefore

\displaystyle E_{\gamma}(M_{0}\|M_{1})=1-\inf_{A\subseteq\mathcal{Y}}\{M_{0}(A^{c})+\gamma M_{1}(A)\}.

(15)

Maximizing
E_{\gamma}
is equivalent to minimizing this weighted testing risk:
\gamma=1
gives total variation, while
\gamma>1
captures asymmetric costs. Such asymmetry is common in privacy-sensitive decisions (rare-event detection, medical screening, fraud or abuse detection, and content moderation) where false alarms and missed detections need not have comparable costs.

For the
E_{\gamma}
-divergences, the SPR dynamic program collapses to a closed form.

Definition 3.5

(Generalized binary mechanism)
.

With
S_{\gamma}:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:P_{0}(x)\geq\gamma P_{1}(x)\}
, the
generalized binary mechanism
is the binary-output channel
Q_{\gamma}
defined by

\displaystyle Q_{\gamma}(0\mid x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{e^{\varepsilon}}{1+e^{\varepsilon}},&x\in S_{\gamma},\\[6.88889pt]
\dfrac{1}{1+e^{\varepsilon}},&x\notin S_{\gamma},\end{cases}

\displaystyle Q_{\gamma}(1\mid x)

\displaystyle=1-Q_{\gamma}(0\mid x).

(16)

Theorem 3.6

(Optimal
E_{\gamma}
mechanism)
.

For every
\gamma\geq 1
and every
\varepsilon
-LDP mechanism
Q
with induced marginals
M_{0},M_{1}
,

\displaystyle E_{\gamma}(M_{0}\|M_{1})\leq\left(\frac{e^{\varepsilon}-1}{e^{\varepsilon}+1}E_{\gamma}(P_{0}\|P_{1})+\frac{1-\gamma}{e^{\varepsilon}+1}\right)_{+}.

(17)

The generalized binary mechanism
Q_{\gamma}
attains (
17
) with equality, and therefore maximizes
E_{\gamma}(M_{0}\|M_{1})
over
\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}
.

In the non-private problem, the event maximizing
P_{0}(A)-\gamma P_{1}(A)
is the threshold set
S_{\gamma}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:P_{0}(x)\geq\gamma P_{1}(x)\}
, obtained by selecting exactly the points where
P_{0}(x)-\gamma P_{1}(x)\geq 0
. Theorem
3.6
states that privacy does not change this threshold: the optimal private mechanism applies binary randomized response to the bit
\mathbf{1}\{X\in S_{\gamma}\}
. Notably,
S_{\gamma}
does not depend on
\varepsilon
: privacy leaves the classical likelihood-ratio threshold unchanged and privatizes only the resulting bit. This captures a private Neyman–Pearson lemma for the weighted-risk formulation. Since hockey-stick divergences also underlie privacy profiles and worst-case LDP contraction bounds
[
4
,
2
]
, this result identifies, for the fixed pair
(P_{0},P_{1})
, the
\varepsilon
-LDP channel that preserves the largest possible

-separation.

12
(Theorem 3) established the upper bound (
17

shows that the generalized binary mechanism

attains it. The argument is short: under

, the contribution of the output

is
M_{0}(0)-\gamma M_{1}(0)=\sum_{x}(P_{0}(x)-\gamma P_{1}(x))Q_{\gamma}(0\mid x)

3.4
Other objectives: Rényi divergences and
\ell_{r}
distance

SPR optimality applies to any objective maximized at extreme points of the joint-range polytope; the

dynamic program, however, requires an additive decomposition over contiguous blocks, as
f
-divergences have. Rényi divergences fit the same framework via a monotone reduction to an

-divergence; for

Proposition 3.7

(SPR optimality for Rényi divergences)
.

For every fixed finite order
\alpha>0
, the SPR optimality result and the
O(\ell k^{2})
dynamic program apply to maximization of
D_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

The case
\alpha=1
is KL divergence, an
f
-divergence. For
\alpha\neq 1
,

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(u\|v)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{y}u_{y}^{\alpha}v_{y}^{1-\alpha},

(18)

so maximizing
D_{\alpha}
is equivalent to maximizing the
f
-divergence with
f(t)=t^{\alpha}-1
for
\alpha>1
and
f(t)=1-t^{\alpha}
for
0<\alpha<1
; both are convex with
f(1)=0
.
∎

Proposition 3.8

(Optimal mechanism for
\ell_{r}
distance is binary)
.

For any
1\leq r\leq\infty
and any
\varepsilon
-LDP channel
Q
with at least two outputs,

\displaystyle\|Q^{\top}p-Q^{\top}q\|_{r}\leq 2^{1/r}\tanh(\varepsilon/2)\,d_{\rm TV}(p,q),\qquad 2^{1/\infty}:=1.

(19)

The maximum is achieved by binary randomized response applied to
S:=\{i\in[k]:p_{i}\geq q_{i}\}

Proof.

Let
\Delta:=Q^{\top}p-Q^{\top}q
and

\varepsilon

\displaystyle m\leq\tanh(\varepsilon/2)\,d_{\rm TV}(p,q).

(20)

The positive entries of

\displaystyle\|\Delta\|_{r}^{r}\leq m^{r}+m^{r}=2m^{r},

therefore,

\displaystyle\|\Delta\|_{r}\leq 2^{1/r}\tanh(\varepsilon/2)\,d_{\rm TV}(p,q).

(22)

For

\displaystyle\|\Delta\|_{\infty}\leq\tanh(\varepsilon/2)\,d_{\rm TV}(p,q).

(23)

For tightness, let

where

∎

4.1
Extreme refinements: from extreme points to LR-contiguous staircase channels

We first show that every extreme image point can be realized, up to deterministic post-processing, by a staircase channel whose active columns respect the likelihood-ratio order.

Proposition 4.1

(Extreme points admit LR-contiguous staircase refinements)
.

If
(u,v)\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(p,q)
is an extreme point, then there exist an integer
m\geq 1
, a staircase channel
\widetilde{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,m}
, and a deterministic map
\tau:[m]\to[\ell]
such that

\displaystyle(u,v)=\bigl((\tau\circ\widetilde{Q})^{\top}p,(\tau\circ\widetilde{Q})^{\top}q\bigr),

(24)

and every active column of
\widetilde{Q}
has an LR-contiguous high set.

The proof is given in Appendix
A.4
. Starting from an arbitrary extreme image point, we refine it to a staircase channel and then show that any noncontiguous active column creates a local obstruction that contradicts extremality. This step adapts the joint-range perturbation idea of
9
: we rule out such a column by producing two feasible induced pairs whose midpoint is the original pair.

4.2
Convex hull of SPR mechanisms

Once the active columns are LR-contiguous, the staircase channel decomposes into a convex combination of SPR mechanisms.

Proposition 4.2

(Convex hull of SPR channels)
.

Let
m\geq 1
, and let
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,m}
be a staircase channel. Assume every active column of
Q
has an LR-contiguous high set, with active constant columns represented by the high set
[k]
. Then there exist LR-contiguous partitions
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{N}
, channels
\widehat{Q}^{(1)},\dots,\widehat{Q}^{(N)}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,m}
, and coefficients
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{N}>0
with
\sum_{t=1}^{N}\beta_{t}=1
such that

\displaystyle Q=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\beta_{t}\widehat{Q}^{(t)},

(25)

where, for each
t
, the channel
\widehat{Q}^{(t)}
is obtained from the SPR mechanism
Q^{\pi_{t}}
by relabeling its active outputs into
[m]
and padding the remaining columns with zeros.

The proof is deferred to Appendix
B
. It rewrites
Q
as a weighted family of LR-contiguous intervals, uses the row-sum condition to show that every row sees the same total interval weight, and then peels this weighted interval system into layers, each layer being a partition of
[k]
. Each layer yields an SPR mechanism on the same output alphabet after relabeling and zero-padding. This peeling step can be viewed as a weighted flow-decomposition argument
[
1
]
specialized to interval families.

4.3
Nontrivial coarsenings are not extreme

Finally, we show that deterministically merging active outputs of an SPR mechanism does not create new extreme points.

Proposition 4.3

(Nontrivial coarsenings are not extreme)
.

Let
\pi=(B_{1},\dots,B_{s})
be an LR-contiguous partition of
[k]
, let
Q^{\pi}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,s}
be the associated SPR mechanism, and let
\tau:[s]\to[\ell]
be deterministic. If
t:=|\tau([s])|
satisfies
1<t<s
, then the image point

\displaystyle\bigl((\tau\circ Q^{\pi})^{\top}p,(\tau\circ Q^{\pi})^{\top}q\bigr)\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(p,q)

(26)

is not an extreme point of
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(p,q)
.

The proof is deferred to Appendix
C
. The key observation is that such a coarsened image decomposes into a randomized-response point on the coarser partition induced by
\tau
, together with a hypothesis-independent remainder coming from an input-independent channel. This gives a nontrivial convex decomposition inside
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(p,q)
.

4.4

Putting these three propositions together gives the main geometric statement: Theorem

The extreme-point characterization converts into optimal mechanisms, both with and without a communication constraint, via Corollary

Proof of Corollary
3.2

Fix any
\ell^{\prime}\geq 1

By convexity of

\displaystyle D_{f}(u\|v)\leq\sum_{t=1}^{N}\lambda_{t}D_{f}(u^{(t)}\|v^{(t)})\leq\max_{t}D_{f}(u^{(t)}\|v^{(t)}),

so some extreme point

satisfies

, that extreme point is induced by an SPR mechanism with at most

For the unconstrained claim, take

arbitrary: every finite-output

Fix an
f

5.1
Interval scores and DP recurrence

For
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}
, let
\displaystyle\mu(z):=(q^{\top}z)\,f\!\left(\frac{p^{\top}z}{q^{\top}z}\right)

f
-divergence. For

, let

\displaystyle\mu[a:b]:=\mu\!\left(\bigl(1+(e^{\varepsilon}-1)\mathbf{1}\{a\leq i\leq b\}\bigr)_{i=1}^{k}\right).

(29)

Thus

[a:b]

If

\displaystyle D_{f}\bigl((Q^{\pi})^{\top}p\|(Q^{\pi})^{\top}q\bigr)=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{s}\mu(B_{t})}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1},

5.2

Proof of Corollary

Sorting the input alphabet by likelihood ratio takes

6
Conclusion and limitations

We showed that the exact mechanism-design problem for binary testing under local privacy has a much smaller structure than the general staircase formulation suggests. After sorting by likelihood ratio, optimal mechanisms can be chosen by partitioning the ordered alphabet into contiguous blocks and applying randomized response to the block label (Section
4
), yielding an exact polynomial-time dynamic program (Section
5
). For
E_{\gamma}
-divergences this collapses to a closed-form binary mechanism, and the same machinery extends to Rényi divergences and
\ell_{r}
distance objectives (Sections
3.3
–
3.4
).

Our characterization is specific to simple binary hypothesis testing under pure, non-interactive
\varepsilon
-LDP on a finite alphabet. Extending the SPR geometry to multi-hypothesis or composite testing, approximate or Rényi local privacy, and interactive protocols remains open. On the algorithmic side, the
O(\ell k^{2})
dynamic program relies on the additive block decomposition of
f
-divergences; finding comparably efficient exact algorithms for broader quasi-convex objectives, such as Chernoff information, is an interesting direction. Another natural question is how to adapt the mechanism-design results when the hypotheses
P_{0},P_{1}
are not known exactly and must be estimated or specified through uncertainty classes.

A.1
Staircase refinement

The next lemma shows that any fixed-output
\varepsilon
-LDP channel can be refined into a staircase channel that merges back to the original one under a deterministic post-processing map. This lets us work at the staircase level and later transfer a contradiction back to the original image point. Example
A.3
below illustrates the construction on a small
5\times 2
channel.

Lemma A.1

(Nested staircase refinement)
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k\times\ell}
be a row-stochastic
\varepsilon
-LDP channel, where
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exist a staircase
\varepsilon
-LDP channel
\widetilde{Q}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k\times\widetilde{m}}
with output alphabet
\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}=[\widetilde{m}]
, and a deterministic map
\tau:\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}\to[\ell]
such that

\displaystyle Q(i,y)=\sum_{\tilde{y}:\tau(\tilde{y})=y}\widetilde{Q}(i,\tilde{y})\qquad(i\in[k],\ y\in[\ell]).

Moreover, for each
y\in[\ell]
, the high sets of the fine columns merged into
y
are nested.

Proof.

Zero columns need no refinement, so fix a nonzero column
Q(\cdot,y)
of
Q
.
Because
Q
is
\varepsilon
-LDP and this column is nonzero, every entry in it is positive.
Let

\displaystyle\theta:=\min_{i\in[k]}Q(i,y)>0

be its smallest entry. Then every entry in this column lies between
\theta
and
e^{\varepsilon}\theta
.

We now show that this column can be written as a sum of staircase columns with nested high sets.

Write each entry as

\displaystyle Q(i,y)=\theta\bigl(1+(e^{\varepsilon}-1)u_{i}\bigr),\qquad u_{i}\in[0,1],

where

\displaystyle u_{i}:=\frac{Q(i,y)-\theta}{(e^{\varepsilon}-1)\theta}\in[0,1].

Here,
u_{i}
records how much of the maximum possible “extra above the minimum” row
i
receives.

If
u_{i}=0
for all
i
, then the column is already constant:
Q(i,y)=\theta
for all
i
.
In that case we define a single fine column by

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}(i,(y,0)):=\theta\qquad(i\in[k]),

and there is nothing further to do for this
y
.

Otherwise, let

\displaystyle 0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}<\cdots<\lambda_{L}\leq 1

be the distinct values taken by the
u_{i}
’s. For each level
t=1,\dots,L
, let

\displaystyle H_{t}:=\{i\in[k]:u_{i}\geq\lambda_{t}\}.

Then

\displaystyle H_{1}\supseteq H_{2}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq H_{L},

because rows with a larger boost automatically belong to all smaller-boost groups.

Each
u_{i}
is obtained by adding the jump sizes
\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t-1}
for exactly those sets
H_{t}
that contain row
i
:

\displaystyle u_{i}=\sum_{t=1}^{L}(\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t-1})\mathbf{1}\{i\in H_{t}\}.

Now let
H_{0}=\varnothing
, set

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}:=\theta(\lambda_{t}-\lambda_{t-1})\qquad(t=1,\dots,L),

and

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}:=\theta(1-\lambda_{L}).

Substituting the expression for
u_{i}
into

Q(i,y)=\theta\bigl(1+(e^{\varepsilon}-1)u_{i}\bigr)
gives

\displaystyle Q(i,y)=\sum_{t=0}^{L}\alpha_{t}\Bigl(1+(e^{\varepsilon}-1)\mathbf{1}\{i\in H_{t}\}\Bigr).

For this fixed
y
, define the corresponding fine columns by

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}(i,(y,t)):=\alpha_{t}\Bigl(1+(e^{\varepsilon}-1)\mathbf{1}\{i\in H_{t}\}\Bigr)\qquad(t=0,\dots,L).

Then

\displaystyle Q(i,y)=\sum_{t=0}^{L}\widetilde{Q}(i,(y,t))\qquad(i\in[k]).

Apply the same construction to every nonzero column of
Q
, using for each column
y
its own levels
\lambda_{t}^{(y)}
, sets
H_{t}^{(y)}
, and coefficients
\alpha_{t}^{(y)}
.
The resulting fine columns form the refined channel
\widetilde{Q}
.
Let
\tau
send each fine column back to the original column it came from.
Thus,

\displaystyle Q(i,y)=\sum_{\tilde{y}:\tau(\tilde{y})=y}\widetilde{Q}(i,\tilde{y})\qquad(i\in[k],\ y\in[\ell]).

Each fine column has the form
\alpha\bigl(1+(e^{\varepsilon}-1)\mathbf{1}\{i\in H\}\bigr)
for some
\alpha\geq 0
and some set
H\subseteq[k]
, so it has staircase form.
Since within each original column the fine columns sum back to that column, summing over all original columns shows that each row sum of
\widetilde{Q}
equals the corresponding row sum of
Q
; hence
\widetilde{Q}
is row-stochastic. Finally, for a fixed original output
y
, the fine columns coming from
Q(\cdot,y)
have high sets among the sets
H_{t}^{(y)}
, and these sets are nested.
∎

Remark A.2
.

In the proof of Lemma

Elsewhere in the paper, when discussing staircase channels abstractly, we use the convention that an active constant column is represented with high set

A.3
Perturbation lemmas

The next step is to show that each forbidden local configuration makes the induced point in the joint range non-extreme. The general strategy is to build small feasible perturbations of the staircase channel that preserve row-stochasticity and
\varepsilon
-LDP, and then choose them so that the original point becomes a nontrivial midpoint of two distinct feasible image points.

We first record a simple perturbation criterion for staircase channels.

Lemma A.7

(Perturbation criterion for staircase channels)
.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k\times m}
be a row-stochastic staircase
\varepsilon
-LDP channel with
\varepsilon>0
. Let
D\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times m}
be supported on active columns of
Q
, and assume

\displaystyle D\mathbf{1}=0.

Assume also that for every modified column
y
, every row
h
that is high in column
y
, and every row
\ell
that is low in column
y
,

\displaystyle D_{h,y}\leq e^{\varepsilon}D_{\ell,y}.

(36)

Then
Q+\gamma D
is row-stochastic and
\varepsilon
-LDP for all sufficiently small
\gamma>0
.

Proof.

Since
D\mathbf{1}=0
, every row sum of
Q+\gamma D
equals the corresponding row sum of
Q
, hence equals
1
.

Because
D
is supported on active columns and every active staircase column is strictly positive entrywise, each modified entry of
Q
starts strictly positive. Since there are finitely many modified entries, there exists
\gamma_{0}>0
such that
Q+\gamma D\geq 0
for all
0<\gamma<\gamma_{0}
.

Fix a modified column
y
. In a staircase column, every entry is either
\theta_{y}
or
e^{\varepsilon}\theta_{y}
for some
\theta_{y}>0
. Thus:

•

if both rows are high, or both are low, then the two entries are equal, so the ordered privacy inequality is

\displaystyle Q_{i,y}\leq e^{\varepsilon}Q_{i^{\prime},y},

which is strict because
\varepsilon>0
;

•

if row
h
is high and row
\ell
is low, then

\displaystyle Q_{h,y}=e^{\varepsilon}Q_{\ell,y},

so this is the only potentially tight ordered privacy inequality;

•

if row
\ell
is low and row
h
is high, then

\displaystyle Q_{\ell,y}<e^{\varepsilon}Q_{h,y},

so that ordered inequality is also strict.

Therefore it is enough to check the high-to-low pairs. For such a pair,

\displaystyle Q_{h,y}=e^{\varepsilon}Q_{\ell,y},

so after perturbation we get

\displaystyle Q_{h,y}+\gamma D_{h,y}\leq e^{\varepsilon}\bigl(Q_{\ell,y}+\gamma D_{\ell,y}\bigr)

by (
36
).

All other ordered privacy inequalities are strict at
\gamma=0
, so by continuity they remain valid for all sufficiently small
\gamma>0
. Since there are only finitely many such inequalities, shrinking
\gamma_{0}
if necessary gives a single
\gamma_{0}>0
for which every privacy inequality holds for all
0<\gamma<\gamma_{0}
. Therefore
Q+\gamma D
is row-stochastic and
\varepsilon
-LDP for all sufficiently small
\gamma>0
.
∎

The next lemma adapts the midpoint perturbation argument used in the proof of Theorem 6 of
[
9
]
. There, Pensia et al. rule out a non-threshold deterministic channel by showing that the induced point in the fixed-output joint range is not extreme. We use the same perturbative idea here for staircase
\varepsilon

Lemma A.8

(Zigzag configuration implies non-extremality in the joint range)
.

Let
p,q\in\Delta_{k}
be in reduced strict extended likelihood-ratio order: all input symbols with

have been deleted, equal extended likelihood ratios have been merged, and

\displaystyle 0\leq r_{1}<r_{2}<\cdots<r_{k}\leq\infty,\qquad r_{i}:=\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}},

with the convention that
p_{i}/0=\infty
when
p_{i}>0
.
Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k\times m}
be a row-stochastic staircase
\varepsilon
-LDP channel with
\varepsilon>0
. If
Q
contains a zigzag configuration, then the induced point
(Q^{\top}p,Q^{\top}q)
is not an extreme point of

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{m}(p,q):=\{(T^{\top}p,T^{\top}q):T\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k\times m}\text{ is row-stochastic and }\varepsilon\text{-LDP}\}.

Equivalently, there exist row-stochastic
\varepsilon
-LDP channels
Q^{\prime},Q^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k\times m}
such that

\displaystyle(Q^{\top}p,Q^{\top}q)=\tfrac{1}{2}(Q^{\prime\top}p,Q^{\prime\top}q)+\tfrac{1}{2}(Q^{\prime\prime\top}p,Q^{\prime\prime\top}q),

and

\displaystyle(Q^{\prime\top}p,Q^{\prime\top}q)\neq(Q^{\prime\prime\top}p,Q^{\prime\prime\top}q).

Moreover, the channels
Q^{\prime}
and
Q^{\prime\prime}
may be chosen so that they differ from
Q
only in the two columns participating in the zigzag configuration.

Proof.

Let
a<b<c
and
j\neq j^{\prime}
witness the zigzag configuration, so that on rows
(a,b,c)
the columns
j
and
j^{\prime}
have patterns

\displaystyle 101,\qquad 010.

Let
e_{j},e_{j^{\prime}}
be the standard basis vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Since the likelihood ratios are strictly ordered and
a<b<c
, the rows
a
and
b
have finite likelihood ratio, hence
q_{a},q_{b}>0
. The row
c
may have finite likelihood ratio, in which case
q_{c}>0
, or it may be the unique infinite-likelihood-ratio endpoint, in which case
q_{c}=0
and
p_{c}>0
.

Define
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times m}
by

\displaystyle A_{a,j}=-1,\qquad A_{a,j^{\prime}}=1,\qquad A_{b,j}=\frac{q_{a}}{q_{b}},\qquad A_{b,j^{\prime}}=-\frac{q_{a}}{q_{b}},

\displaystyle B_{b,j}=q_{c},\qquad B_{b,j^{\prime}}=-q_{c},\qquad B_{c,j}=-q_{b},\qquad B_{c,j^{\prime}}=q_{b},

and all other entries equal to
0
.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
\cdots&-1&\cdots&+1&\cdots\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
\cdots&+\dfrac{q_{a}}{q_{b}}&\cdots&-\dfrac{q_{a}}{q_{b}}&\cdots\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
\cdots&0&\cdots&0&\cdots\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\end{bmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{bmatrix}\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
\cdots&0&\cdots&0&\cdots\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
\cdots&+q_{c}&\cdots&-q_{c}&\cdots\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
\cdots&-q_{b}&\cdots&+q_{b}&\cdots\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\end{bmatrix}.

When
q_{c}>0
, this
B
is exactly
q_{c}
times the original finite-ratio perturbation with entries
1,-1,-q_{b}/q_{c},q_{b}/q_{c}
. When
q_{c}=0
, it reduces to the endpoint perturbation supported only on row
c
.

Both
A
and
B
are supported on the active columns

and satisfy

\displaystyle A\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0},\qquad B\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}.

Moreover, in each modified column, every high-row perturbation is nonpositive and every low-row perturbation is nonnegative: for the rows not listed above, the perturbation is
0
. Hence the pairwise inequalities of Lemma
A.7
hold for both
A
and
B
. It follows that

\displaystyle Q+\gamma A,\qquad Q+\gamma B

are row-stochastic and
\varepsilon
-LDP for all sufficiently small
\gamma>0
.

Their images are

\displaystyle A^{\top}q=\mathbf{0},\qquad A^{\top}p=\left(-p_{a}+\frac{q_{a}}{q_{b}}p_{b}\right)(e_{j}-e_{j^{\prime}})=q_{a}(r_{b}-r_{a})(e_{j}-e_{j^{\prime}}),

and

\displaystyle B^{\top}q=\mathbf{0},\qquad B^{\top}p=(q_{c}p_{b}-q_{b}p_{c})(e_{j}-e_{j^{\prime}}).

\displaystyle\Delta_{ab}:=q_{a}(r_{b}-r_{a})>0,\qquad\Delta_{bc}:=q_{b}p_{c}-q_{c}p_{b}.

\displaystyle A^{\top}p=\Delta_{ab}(e_{j}-e_{j^{\prime}}),\qquad B^{\top}p=-\Delta_{bc}(e_{j}-e_{j^{\prime}}).

Appendix B
Convex hull of SPR mechanisms

We isolate the combinatorial core of the proof as a decomposition lemma for weighted interval families with constant coverage.

Lemma B.1

(Layer decomposition of constant-coverage interval families)
.

Suppose we have finitely many nonempty intervals
I_{y}\subseteq[k]
, indexed by
y\in A
, and positive weights
\theta_{y}>0
. Assume that for each
i\in[k]
, the total weight of intervals covering
i
,

\displaystyle h(i):=\sum_{y:\,i\in I_{y}}\theta_{y},

is the same positive constant. Then there exist subsets
\Pi_{1},\dots,\Pi_{N}\subseteq A
and numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}>0
such that:

1.

for each
t
, the intervals
\{I_{y}:y\in\Pi_{t}\}
form a partition of
[k]
;

2.

for each
y\in A
,

\displaystyle\theta_{y}=\sum_{t:\,y\in\Pi_{t}}\lambda_{t}.

The lemma says that if weighted intervals overlap in such a way that every position
i\in[k]
sees the same total weight, then the whole system can be peeled into layers, each of which is a partition of
[k]
. The coefficients
\lambda_{t}
record how much weight each layer contributes, and adding the layers back together recovers the original interval weights. This can be viewed as the standard flow-decomposition theorem
[
1
]
applied to the DAG that identifies each interval
[a:b]
with an edge
a-1\to b
.

Proof.

We peel off the interval family one partition layer at a time. At each stage, we keep track of the remaining weight on each interval and choose a collection of intervals that partitions
[k]
; we then subtract the same amount from every interval in that layer.

Formally, start with remaining weights
w_{y}:=\theta_{y}
. At any stage, let

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{cur}}(i):=\sum_{y:\,i\in I_{y}}w_{y}

be the total remaining weight covering
i
. Initially
h_{\mathrm{cur}}
is constant by assumption, and after each layer is removed it will remain constant.

As long as some
w_{y}
is positive, we build one layer from left to right. Since some positive-weight interval is nonempty, there is at least one point with positive remaining coverage. Because
h_{\mathrm{cur}}
is constant, this implies that
h_{\mathrm{cur}}(i)>0
for every
i\in[k]
, in particular for
i=1
. Hence there exists a positive-weight interval containing
1
, and since it is an interval, it must be of the form
[1:b_{1}]
for some
b_{1}
.
Now suppose we have already chosen disjoint positive-weight intervals

\displaystyle[1:b_{1}],\ [b_{1}+1:b_{2}],\ \dots,\ [b_{r-1}+1:b_{r}]

and that
b_{r}<k
. Because
h_{\mathrm{cur}}(b_{r})=h_{\mathrm{cur}}(b_{r}+1)
, the total remaining weight of intervals ending at
b_{r}
equals the total remaining weight of intervals starting at
b_{r}+1
:

\displaystyle\sum_{y:\,b_{r}\in I_{y},\ b_{r}+1\notin I_{y}}w_{y}=\sum_{y:\,b_{r}\notin I_{y},\ b_{r}+1\in I_{y}}w_{y}.

The last chosen interval ends at
b_{r}
, so the left-hand side is positive. Hence the right-hand side is also positive, and there exists a positive-weight interval starting at
b_{r}+1
. Choose one as the next block.

This shows that the construction cannot stop before reaching
k
. Therefore it produces a set of intervals
\{I_{y}:y\in\Pi_{t}\}
that partitions
[k]
.

Now let

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}:=\min_{y\in\Pi_{t}}w_{y}>0,

and subtract
\lambda_{t}
from the weight of every interval in this layer:

\displaystyle w_{y}\leftarrow\begin{cases}w_{y}-\lambda_{t},&y\in\Pi_{t},\\
w_{y},&y\notin\Pi_{t}.\end{cases}

Since the intervals in
\Pi_{t}
partition
[k]
, each point of
[k]
lies in exactly one of them, so
h_{\mathrm{cur}}(i)
decreases by exactly
\lambda_{t}
for every
i
. Thus
h_{\mathrm{cur}}
remains constant. Also, by the choice of
\lambda_{t}
, at least one interval in
\Pi_{t}
now has remaining weight zero. Therefore the process terminates after finitely many steps.

Finally, each time
I_{y}
appears in a layer
\Pi_{t}
, we subtract exactly
\lambda_{t}
from its remaining weight. Since
I_{y}
starts with weight
\theta_{y}
and ends with weight
0
, the total amount removed from it is

\displaystyle\theta_{y}=\sum_{t:\,y\in\Pi_{t}}\lambda_{t}.

This proves the lemma.
∎

\displaystyle Q(i,y)=\theta_{y}\Bigl(1+(e-1)\mathbf{1}\{i\in I_{y}\}\Bigr).

Equivalently,

\displaystyle Q=\frac{1}{D}\begin{bmatrix}10e&4e&3&7&7\\
10&4e&3e&7e&7\\
10&4&3&7e&7e\\
10&4&3&7e&7e\\
10&4&3&7e&7e\end{bmatrix}.

Each row sums to
1
, since for example

\displaystyle 10e+4e+3+7+7=14e+17=D,

and similarly for the other rows. The total weight of intervals containing each input is constant:

\displaystyle\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}=\frac{14}{D},\qquad\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}+\theta_{4}=\frac{14}{D},\qquad\theta_{4}+\theta_{5}=\frac{14}{D}.

Thus the weighted interval family has constant row-coverage. Now peel off partition layers:

•

First layer:

\displaystyle\Pi_{1}=\{I_{1},I_{4}\}=\{[1,1],[2,5]\},\qquad\lambda_{1}=\min\{\theta_{1},\theta_{4}\}=\frac{7}{D}.

Subtracting
\lambda_{1}
leaves

\displaystyle\theta_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{3}{D},\quad\theta_{2}^{\prime}=\frac{4}{D},\quad\theta_{3}^{\prime}=\frac{3}{D},\quad\theta_{4}^{\prime}=0,\quad\theta_{5}^{\prime}=\frac{7}{D}.

•

Second layer:

\displaystyle\Pi_{2}=\{I_{2},I_{5}\}=\{[1,2],[3,5]\},\qquad\lambda_{2}=\min\{\theta_{2}^{\prime},\theta_{5}^{\prime}\}=\frac{4}{D}.

Subtracting
\lambda_{2}
leaves

\displaystyle\theta_{1}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{3}{D},\quad\theta_{2}^{\prime\prime}=0,\quad\theta_{3}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{3}{D},\quad\theta_{4}^{\prime\prime}=0,\quad\theta_{5}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{3}{D}.

•

Third layer:

\displaystyle\Pi_{3}=\{I_{1},I_{3},I_{5}\}=\{[1,1],[2,2],[3,5]\},\qquad\lambda_{3}=\frac{3}{D}.

After subtracting
\lambda_{3}
, all remaining weights are zero.

So the original weights decompose as

\displaystyle\theta_{1}=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{3},\qquad\theta_{2}=\lambda_{2},\qquad\theta_{3}=\lambda_{3},\qquad\theta_{4}=\lambda_{1},\qquad\theta_{5}=\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3}.

Each layer
\Pi_{t}
gives an SPR mechanism
\widehat{Q}^{(t)}
, relabeled onto the common output alphabet
[5]
:

\displaystyle\widehat{Q}^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{e}{e+1}&0&0&\frac{1}{e+1}&0\\[4.0pt]
\frac{1}{e+1}&0&0&\frac{e}{e+1}&0\\
\frac{1}{e+1}&0&0&\frac{e}{e+1}&0\\
\frac{1}{e+1}&0&0&\frac{e}{e+1}&0\\
\frac{1}{e+1}&0&0&\frac{e}{e+1}&0\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\widehat{Q}^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac{e}{e+1}&0&0&\frac{1}{e+1}\\[4.0pt]
0&\frac{e}{e+1}&0&0&\frac{1}{e+1}\\
0&\frac{1}{e+1}&0&0&\frac{e}{e+1}\\
0&\frac{1}{e+1}&0&0&\frac{e}{e+1}\\
0&\frac{1}{e+1}&0&0&\frac{e}{e+1}\end{bmatrix},

\frac{1}{e+2}&0&\frac{e}{e+2}&0&\frac{1}{e+2}\\
\frac{1}{e+2}&0&\frac{1}{e+2}&0&\frac{e}{e+2}\\
\frac{1}{e+2}&0&\frac{1}{e+2}&0&\frac{e}{e+2}\\
\frac{1}{e+2}&0&\frac{1}{e+2}&0&\frac{e}{e+2}\end{bmatrix}.

With

\displaystyle\beta_{1}=\lambda_{1}(e+1)=\frac{7(e+1)}{D},\qquad\beta_{2}=\lambda_{2}(e+1)=\frac{4(e+1)}{D},\qquad\beta_{3}=\lambda_{3}(e+2)=\frac{3(e+2)}{D},

we have

\displaystyle\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+\beta_{3}=\frac{7(e+1)+4(e+1)+3(e+2)}{D}=\frac{14e+17}{D}=1,

and

\displaystyle Q=\beta_{1}\widehat{Q}^{(1)}+\beta_{2}\widehat{Q}^{(2)}+\beta_{3}\widehat{Q}^{(3)}.

Appendix C
Nontrivial coarsenings of SPR mechanisms

We show that a nontrivial deterministic merging of the outputs of an SPR mechanism never creates a new extreme point. The reason is that, after merging, one copy of each coarse output still carries the usual randomized-response signal, while the remaining merged copies contribute only hypothesis-independent noise.

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

Let
\pi=(B_{1},\dots,B_{s})
be an LR-contiguous partition of
[k]
, and let
Q^{\pi}
be the corresponding SPR channel. Equivalently,
Q^{\pi}
applies
s
-ary randomized response to the partition label:

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}(j\mid x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{e^{\varepsilon}}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1},&x\in B_{j},\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1},&x\notin B_{j}.\end{cases}

Let
\tau:[s]\to[\ell]
be a deterministic merging map, and write
\tau\circ Q^{\pi}
for the induced channel

\displaystyle(\tau\circ Q^{\pi})(y\mid x):=\sum_{j:\tau(j)=y}Q^{\pi}(j\mid x)\qquad(x\in[k],\ y\in[\ell]).

Write

\displaystyle A:=\tau([s])\subseteq[\ell]

for the coarse output labels that remain active, with
t:=|A|
. Assume
1<t<s
, so at least one pair of fine outputs is merged, but the channel is not collapsed to a single active output. For each
y\in A
, define

\displaystyle n_{y}:=|\tau^{-1}(y)|,\qquad C_{y}:=\bigcup_{j:\tau(j)=y}B_{j}.

Thus
n_{y}
is the number of fine labels merged into
y
, and the sets
\{C_{y}:y\in A\}
form the coarser partition of
[k]
induced by
\tau
.

We now define two
\ell
-output channels. The first is randomized response on the coarser partition
\{C_{y}:y\in A\}
, and the second is an input-independent channel capturing the remaining mass.

•

Let
R
be randomized response on the coarser partition:

\displaystyle R(y\mid x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1+(e^{\varepsilon}-1)\mathbf{1}\{x\in C_{y}\}}{e^{\varepsilon}+t-1},&y\in A,\\[8.0pt]
0,&y\notin A.\end{cases}

•

Let
U
be the input-independent channel

\displaystyle U(y\mid x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{n_{y}-1}{s-t},&y\in A,\\[8.0pt]
0,&y\notin A,\end{cases}\qquad x\in[k].

This is well defined because
\sum_{y\in A}(n_{y}-1)=\sum_{y\in A}n_{y}-t=s-t
.

Set
\displaystyle\alpha:=\frac{e^{\varepsilon}+t-1}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1}\in(0,1)
.
We claim that

\displaystyle\tau\circ Q^{\pi}=\alpha R+(1-\alpha)U.

Fix
x\in[k]
and
y\in[\ell]
. If
y\notin A
, then both sides are zero. Now suppose
y\in A
.

•

If
x\in C_{y}
, then among the
n_{y}
fine outputs merged into
y
, exactly one corresponds to the block containing
x
, and therefore has probability
\frac{e^{\varepsilon}}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1}
; the other
n_{y}-1
have probability
\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1}
. Hence

\displaystyle(\tau\circ Q^{\pi})(y\mid x)=\frac{e^{\varepsilon}+n_{y}-1}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1}=\alpha\frac{e^{\varepsilon}}{e^{\varepsilon}+t-1}+(1-\alpha)\frac{n_{y}-1}{s-t}.

•

If
x\notin C_{y}
, then none of the
n_{y}
fine outputs merged into
y
corresponds to the block containing
x
, so all
n_{y}
of them have probability
\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1}
. Therefore

\displaystyle(\tau\circ Q^{\pi})(y\mid x)=\frac{n_{y}}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1}=\alpha\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon}+t-1}+(1-\alpha)\frac{n_{y}-1}{s-t}.

This proves the claim.

Let

\displaystyle M_{0}:=((\tau\circ Q^{\pi})^{\top}p),\qquad M_{1}:=((\tau\circ Q^{\pi})^{\top}q),

and similarly let

\displaystyle M_{0}^{R}:=R^{\top}p,\quad M_{1}^{R}:=R^{\top}q,\qquad M_{0}^{U}:=U^{\top}p,\quad M_{1}^{U}:=U^{\top}q.

Since
U
is input-independent, we have
M_{0}^{U}=M_{1}^{U}
. Write this common distribution as
c:=M_{0}^{U}=M_{1}^{U}
, or equivalently,

\displaystyle c(y)=M_{0}^{U}(y)=M_{1}^{U}(y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{n_{y}-1}{s-t},&y\in A,\\[8.0pt]
0,&y\notin A.\end{cases}

Taking induced marginals under
p
and
q
in the identity

\displaystyle\tau\circ Q^{\pi}=\alpha R+(1-\alpha)U

gives

\displaystyle(M_{0},M_{1})=\alpha(M_{0}^{R},M_{1}^{R})+(1-\alpha)(c,c).

Both terms on the right lie in
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(p,q)
: the pair
(M_{0}^{R},M_{1}^{R})
comes from the
\varepsilon
-LDP channel
R
, and
(c,c)
comes from the input-independent channel
U
.

•

If
(M_{0}^{R},M_{1}^{R})\neq(c,c)
, this is already a nontrivial convex decomposition, so
(M_{0},M_{1})
is not extreme.

•

If
(M_{0}^{R},M_{1}^{R})=(c,c)
, then
c=M_{0}^{R}=M_{1}^{R}
, and
(M_{0},M_{1})=\alpha(c,c)+(1-\alpha)(c,c)=(c,c)
, so it suffices to show that
(c,c)
is not an extreme point of
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(p,q)
. For every
y\in A
,

\displaystyle R(y\mid x)\geq\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon}+t-1}\qquad\text{for all }x\in[k],

so

\displaystyle c(y)=M_{0}^{R}(y)\geq\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon}+t-1}>0.

Since
t=|A|>1
, choose distinct
y_{1},y_{2}\in A
and

\displaystyle 0<\eta<\min\{c(y_{1}),c(y_{2})\}.

Obtain two new probability distributions by moving mass
\eta
from
y_{2}
to
y_{1}
and in the opposite direction:

\displaystyle c^{+}(y)=\begin{cases}c(y_{1})+\eta,&y=y_{1},\\
c(y_{2})-\eta,&y=y_{2},\\
c(y),&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad c^{-}(y)=\begin{cases}c(y_{1})-\eta,&y=y_{1},\\
c(y_{2})+\eta,&y=y_{2},\\
c(y),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then
c^{+}\neq c^{-}
and

\displaystyle c=\frac{1}{2}(c^{+}+c^{-}).

Since
c^{\pm}
are probability distributions, the input-independent channels

\displaystyle U^{\pm}(y\mid x):=c^{\pm}(y)

are feasible, so
(c^{+},c^{+}),(c^{-},c^{-})\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(p,q)
. Therefore

\displaystyle(c,c)=\frac{1}{2}(c^{+},c^{+})+\frac{1}{2}(c^{-},c^{-})

is not an extreme point of
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(p,q)
.

In either case, the image point induced by
\tau\circ Q^{\pi}
is not an extreme point of
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}(p,q)
.
∎

Appendix D
Optimality of the generalized binary mechanism for
E_{\gamma}

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

Let

\displaystyle S_{\gamma}:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:P_{0}(x)\geq\gamma P_{1}(x)\}.

If
S_{\gamma}=\emptyset
or
S_{\gamma}=\mathcal{X}
, then
E_{\gamma}(P_{0}\|P_{1})=0
, and the theorem is immediate: in the second case, summing
P_{0}(x)\geq\gamma P_{1}(x)
over
x
gives
1\geq\gamma
, which together with
\gamma\geq 1
forces
\gamma=1
and
P_{0}=P_{1}
, hence
M_{0}=M_{1}
. So assume both
S_{\gamma}
and
S_{\gamma}^{c}
are nonempty.

12
(Theorem 3) show the upper bound in Theorem
3.6
. We show that the generalized binary mechanism attains this bound. Under
Q_{\gamma}
,

\displaystyle M_{0}(0)-\gamma M_{1}(0)=\sum_{x}\bigl(P_{0}(x)-\gamma P_{1}(x)\bigr)Q_{\gamma}(0\mid x).

By the Neyman-Pearson characterization of
E_{\gamma}
,

E_{\gamma}(P_{0}\|P_{1})=P_{0}(S_{\gamma})-\gamma P_{1}(S_{\gamma}),\qquad P_{0}(S_{\gamma}^{c})-\gamma P_{1}(S_{\gamma}^{c})=(1-\gamma)-E_{\gamma}(P_{0}\|P_{1}).

(37)

Splitting the sum over
S_{\gamma}
and
S_{\gamma}^{c}
and using (
37
), we get

\displaystyle M_{0}(0)-\gamma M_{1}(0)=\frac{e^{\varepsilon}}{1+e^{\varepsilon}}\bigl(P_{0}(S_{\gamma})-\gamma P_{1}(S_{\gamma})\bigr)+\frac{1}{1+e^{\varepsilon}}\bigl(P_{0}(S_{\gamma}^{c})-\gamma P_{1}(S_{\gamma}^{c})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\varepsilon}E_{\gamma}(P_{0}\|P_{1})+(1-\gamma-E_{\gamma}(P_{0}\|P_{1}))}{1+e^{\varepsilon}}=\frac{e^{\varepsilon}-1}{e^{\varepsilon}+1}E_{\gamma}(P_{0}\|P_{1})+\frac{1-\gamma}{e^{\varepsilon}+1}.

Since
E_{\gamma}(M_{0}\|M_{1})
is the supremum over all events, it is at least the value of the event
\{0\}
and also at least
0
(by taking the empty event). Hence

\displaystyle E_{\gamma}(M_{0}\|M_{1})\geq\max\{0,\,M_{0}(0)-\gamma M_{1}(0)\}=\left(\frac{e^{\varepsilon}-1}{e^{\varepsilon}+1}E_{\gamma}(P_{0}\|P_{1})+\frac{1-\gamma}{e^{\varepsilon}+1}\right)_{+}.

Combined with the upper bound, this proves equality and therefore optimality.
∎

We show that one can recover the bound from our results as well:

Corollary D.1

(Recovering the
E_{\gamma}
upper bound from SPR optimality)
.

SPR optimality implies

\displaystyle E_{\gamma}(M_{0}\|M_{1})\leq\left(\frac{e^{\varepsilon}-1}{e^{\varepsilon}+1}E_{\gamma}(P_{0}\|P_{1})+\frac{1-\gamma}{e^{\varepsilon}+1}\right)_{+}.

Proof.

Since
E_{\gamma}
is an
f
-divergence, it is enough to optimize over SPR mechanisms. Consider an arbitrary SPR mechanism induced by an LR-contiguous partition
\pi=(B_{1},\dots,B_{s})
into
s
blocks.

If
s=1
, then the SPR mechanism has a single output and is input-independent, so
M_{0}^{\pi}=M_{1}^{\pi}
. Since
\gamma\geq 1
, this implies
E_{\gamma}(M_{0}^{\pi}\|M_{1}^{\pi})=0
, which is at most the right-hand side of the claimed bound because the latter is nonnegative by definition of
(\cdot)_{+}
. Hence it remains to consider
s\geq 2
.

The induced output probabilities at the output corresponding to block
B
are

\displaystyle M_{0}^{\pi}(B)=\frac{1+(e^{\varepsilon}-1)P_{0}(B)}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1},\qquad M_{1}^{\pi}(B)=\frac{1+(e^{\varepsilon}-1)P_{1}(B)}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1}.

Therefore the contribution of this output to
E_{\gamma}(M_{0}^{\pi}\|M_{1}^{\pi})
is

\displaystyle\bigl(M_{0}^{\pi}(B)-\gamma M_{1}^{\pi}(B)\bigr)_{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bigl(1-\gamma+(e^{\varepsilon}-1)\bigl(P_{0}(B)-\gamma P_{1}(B)\bigr)\bigr)_{+}}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\varepsilon}-1}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1}\Bigl(P_{0}(B)-\gamma P_{1}(B)-\dfrac{\gamma-1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}\Bigr)_{+},

where the last equality factors out
e^{\varepsilon}-1>0
. Summing over blocks,

\displaystyle E_{\gamma}(M_{0}^{\pi}\|M_{1}^{\pi})=\frac{e^{\varepsilon}-1}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1}\sum_{B\in\pi}\Bigl(P_{0}(B)-\gamma P_{1}(B)-\dfrac{\gamma-1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}\Bigr)_{+}.

(38)

We upper-bound the partition score: Let
\mathrm{Pos}(\pi)
denote the blocks of
\pi
that contribute positively to the sum, i.e.,
\mathrm{Pos}(\pi):=\{B\in\pi:P_{0}(B)-\gamma P_{1}(B)>\frac{\gamma-1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}\}
.
If
\mathrm{Pos}(\pi)=\emptyset
, then the sum in (
38
) is zero.

Otherwise, since
|\mathrm{Pos}(\pi)|\geq 1
and
\dfrac{\gamma-1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{B\in\pi}\Bigl(P_{0}(B)-\gamma P_{1}(B)-\dfrac{\gamma-1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}\Bigr)_{+}

\displaystyle=\sum_{B\in\mathrm{Pos}(\pi)}\bigl(P_{0}(B)-\gamma P_{1}(B)\bigr)-|\mathrm{Pos}(\pi)|\,\dfrac{\gamma-1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{B\in\mathrm{Pos}(\pi)}\bigl(P_{0}(B)-\gamma P_{1}(B)\bigr)-\dfrac{\gamma-1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bigl(P_{0}(i)-\gamma P_{1}(i)\bigr)_{+}-\dfrac{\gamma-1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}

\displaystyle=E_{\gamma}(P_{0}\|P_{1})-\dfrac{\gamma-1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}.

Thus, in all cases,

\displaystyle\sum_{B\in\pi}\Bigl(P_{0}(B)-\gamma P_{1}(B)-\dfrac{\gamma-1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}\Bigr)_{+}\leq\Bigl(E_{\gamma}(P_{0}\|P_{1})-\dfrac{\gamma-1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}\Bigr)_{+}.

(39)

Combining (
38
) and (
39
) gives

\displaystyle E_{\gamma}(M_{0}^{\pi}\|M_{1}^{\pi})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{\varepsilon}-1}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1}\Bigl(E_{\gamma}(P_{0}\|P_{1})-\dfrac{\gamma-1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}\Bigr)_{+}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{\varepsilon}-1}{e^{\varepsilon}+1}\Bigl(E_{\gamma}(P_{0}\|P_{1})-\dfrac{\gamma-1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}\Bigr)_{+}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{e^{\varepsilon}-1}{e^{\varepsilon}+1}E_{\gamma}(P_{0}\|P_{1})+\frac{1-\gamma}{e^{\varepsilon}+1}\right)_{+}.

By SPR optimality for
f
-divergence objectives, the same bound holds for every
\varepsilon
-LDP mechanism.
∎

Equation (
38
) conveys the intuition behind the optimal mechanism being binary: the factor
\frac{e^{\varepsilon}-1}{e^{\varepsilon}+s-1}
decreases with the number of outputs, and the
\frac{\gamma-1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}
penalty scales with the number of outputs.
