 Background
Exploding Foil Initiator (EFI) systems function by launching a polymer flyer (often referred to as a slapper)
at a high explosive (HE) pellet, which is intended to shock-initiate the HE. Flyer characteristics (e.g., shape,
planarity, etc.) are extremely important in this respect, governing their ability to initiate the HE. Polymer
models currently used in these systems are largely unvalidated, potentially compromising the integrity of
magneto-hydrodynamic (MHD) predictions of EFI function. An improved EFI MHD modelling capability is
expected to: (1) expedite optimization of EFI-based initiation system design, (2) enable consideration of
EFI-related aging and corresponding lifetime predictions, and (3) potentially minimize the number of
costly experiments required to certify EFI designs. To address this knowledge gap, several years ago we
began a computational project to develop a higher-fidelity model for Kapton, a Polyimide copolymer most
commonly employed as the flyer material in EFI devices.

 Update: Strength Model Development for Polyimide Copolymers
2.1 Introduction
MHD modeling of EFI devices is primarily carried out via ALE3d simulations, which employs the Johnson-
Cook strength model 1 for the flyer material. Johnson-Cook is a constitutive model that was historically
developed to describe high-strain-rate deformation in metals. However, to describe stress relaxation
mechanisms within a glassy polymer like Kapton one needs to use a viscoelastic response model. The most
commonly used in this regard is the Mulliken-Boyce 2 (MB) model, which quantitatively describes the
stress-strain response over a wide range of strain-rates. The original MB paper published model
parameters for two polymers, i.e., Polycarbonate (PC) and Poly-methyl-methacrylate (PMMA), but not
polyimides like Kapton.
Since FY24 we have worked toward refining the Mulliken-Boyce (MB) model for Kapton (i.e., generate MB
parameters appropriate for Kapton). Atomistic modeling 3 gave us significant insight into the system’s
fundamental polymer physics (e.g., strength is partly mediated by Kapton’s entanglement network).
Particularly, (1) the various 𝛼𝛼 and 𝛽𝛽 (sub-Tg) relaxations that show up in dynamic mechanical analyzer
(DMA) type experiments on Kapton (where oscillatory strains are applied), 4 and (2) the conformational
flexibility or “chain stiffness” related quantities like persistence length that are related to the basic
tendency for chains to contract when stretched.

The MB model is a polymer-physics-based constitutive model that we have carefully analyzed (briefly
rehashed in this report) to appropriately apply to Polyimide copolymers.
 • The core principle breaks the model up into different relaxations and response regimes.
 • The principle linking these distinct relaxations (tied to molecular-level rotations) is that as a
 given relaxation freezes out (either at low T, or where a deformation is too fast to permit
 relaxation) it becomes mechanically “important”, providing resistance to strain or deformation.

The model incorporates a number of dashpot and Langevin spring components (Fig. 1(a)) to mimic these
regimes and relaxations 5 (Fig. 1(b)).

2.2 Elastic Response Regime and “Decompose-Shift-Reconstruct”

elastic response), with curves more appropriate to Kapton. To establish an elastic response, MB uses the
“DSR” method, a rate (or time)-temperature superposition approach, which ingests DMA data (closed
blue circles in Figure 3), decomposing a reference temperature-dependent elastic modulus curves 𝐸𝐸𝛼𝛼 (𝑇𝑇)
and 𝐸𝐸𝛽𝛽 (𝑇𝑇) into primary and secondary relaxations (represented by cubic spline fits). We note that Young’s
modulus for fully amorphous Kapton samples 7 closely matches storage modulus for 0°C <T< 400°C.

2.3 Development of Fitting Workflow
Comprehensive sensitivity studies completed during FY25 revealed that:
 (1a) Elastic-viscoelastic elements are operative in different (𝑇𝑇, 𝜖𝜖̇) regimes; at quasistatic rates the 𝛽𝛽
 element has essentially no contribution, whereas high rate regimes are more complex with both 𝛼𝛼
 and 𝛽𝛽 contributing to the yield profile.

whereas both relaxation terms are important at the ~ Kolsky-bar rate.
 (2) Flow response: The plastic flow regime (following the yielding profile) operates similarly, and can
 be effectively reshaped via “softening slopes” ℎ𝛼𝛼 and ℎ𝛽𝛽 , and saturation terms (softening evolves to
 a steady state value over time and ceases).
Figure 5 illustrates the capacity for this term to reshape the high-rate profile somewhat independently of
the low-rate response. The caveat here is that the low-rate response needs to be set first (i.e., in the
regime where only the family of 𝛼𝛼 parameters are important). Once set, the 𝛽𝛽 parameters can be set
without perturbing the low-rate response too much. In short, the workflow needs to work its way up in
strain rate.
 (3) Hardening: The strain hardening response at larger strains (for both low and high-rate response)
 are controlled by a plateau modulus (𝐶𝐶𝑅𝑅 ) and characteristic maximum extension (𝑁𝑁𝑙𝑙 ) closely
 related to physical entanglement behavior.

2.4 Fitting Process and Quality

2.4.1 Preamble
Since this effort was designed to predict mechanical response under shock compression, we required mid-
rate to high-rate compression data. Unfortunately such data was not available in the literature for Kapton,
which is typically used in a thin-film configuration. After a survey of the literature, the only PMDA-ODA
based polyimide for which relevant strain-rate-dependent data was available was Vespel, which comes in
a form much more conducive to Kolsky bar compression measurements. This was directionally aimed
towards setting a base PMDA-ODA polyimide model, that could then be subsequently refined for DuPont’s
specific Kapton formulation (i.e., supplemented with quasistatic and high-rate MD tension and
compression data).

2.4.2   Process
In this section we will briefly summarize the fitting process and provide a figure of merit. Given limited
time / significant time constraints, we opted to fit these parameters semi-manually, doing parameter
sweeps. A more robust parameterization would involve an ML-based automated fitting scheme. Sum of
error squared (across the whole stress strain curve), and deviations from the yield stress were used as a
guide when setting fitting parameters. Some key steps are outline below:
Step 1: Starting from the PC model (the more physical starting point), we swap in the DSR reference
modulus curve and TTS scheme derived from Kapton DMA data.
Step 2: Use rate-activated plasticity terms to fit the yield stress to experimental low-rate data (0.001 and
0.01 𝑠𝑠 −1 ). The activation energy barrier (Δ𝐺𝐺𝛼𝛼 ) is then used to capture the rate-dependence of the yield
stress, and the pre-factor used to effectively shift the yield stress values (decreasing 𝛾𝛾̇𝛼𝛼 increases the yield
stresses).
Step 3: Reshape the profile in the plastic flow and hardening regimes.
Step 4: Since we lacked Kolsky-bar data sufficiently separated by rate (1500 and 4000 𝑠𝑠 −1 ), we opted to
set Δ𝐺𝐺𝛼𝛼 = Δ𝐺𝐺𝛽𝛽 , and then match the yield stress with 𝛾𝛾𝛽𝛽 .
Some final manual refinements in parameters resulted in Figure 7 below. This is as good as we could do
without developing a more robust/automated fitting scheme (which should be possible via something like
Bayesian Optimization tools like BOWT). Admittedly, there are a few deficiencies.
First, there is a modest over-prediction in the fits to the Kolsky bar derived curves, that we were unable
to correct just manually adjusting parameters.
Second, the shape of the yielding profile is somewhat sharper than desired. This is not specific to our fit.
For instance, the original MB modelas well as the extended Varghese and Batra model 10 (as implemented
in ALE) produced the fits in Figure 8 from similar PC data. There is a distinctly sharp transition from elastic
to plastic flow in both, so this may be a pathology of the model form itself.
Moreover, there is a distinct under-prediction (rather than over-prediction) in their model fit. Finally, we
note that the model is suitable for low-rate response up to about 40 to 50 % true strain, past which the
model doesn’t harden enough. This compromise was made to capture the reduced degree of hardening
observed in the Kolsky bar data.

2.4.3 Concluding Thoughts and (possible) future work
Although we worked hard to drive forward efforts on the continuum-scale strength modeling front, the
model is not completely ready yet to be dropped into MHD modeling decks as a material model for a
Kapton flyer: specifically,
 (1) Damage is not yet incorporated into the model, although preliminary tests of the out of the box
 PC model with a failmodel 400 (‘Johnson-Cook Damage Model’) suggest the MB model form is
 likely stable when combined with the damage model. This model allows the material to “fail” and
 flow hydrodynamically; material failure occurs when 𝑑𝑑𝑡𝑡 ≥ 1
 𝜖𝜖̇𝑝𝑝 Δ𝑡𝑡
 𝑑𝑑𝑡𝑡+Δ𝑡𝑡 = 𝑑𝑑𝑡𝑡 + ,
 𝜖𝜖𝑓𝑓 (1)
 where 𝜖𝜖̇𝑝𝑝 Δ𝑡𝑡 is the plastic strain, 𝜖𝜖𝑓𝑓 is a failure strain, and material failure occurs when 𝑑𝑑𝑡𝑡 ≥ 1.
 (2) Thus far the model has “seen” rates up to ≤ 4 ⋅ 104 s-1 in compression data, and higher strain-
 rate exploration could not be completed by the end of FY25. There were challenges associated
 with consistently fitting tension and compression data (and shear data) consistently for Kapton /
 PMDA-ODA copolymers generally. Some of these issues arise from mechanical differences
 between fomulations that we did not anticipate at the start of this fitting exercise.
 (3) Manually tuning parameters is extremely inefficient for fitting across multiple strain
 configurations (shear, tension, and compression) and rates. Establishing fitting
 machinery/software (probably interfacing with a Bayesian Optimization tool like BOWT) should
 simplify this process significantly.
