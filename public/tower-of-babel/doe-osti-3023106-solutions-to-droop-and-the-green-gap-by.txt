Section I: Accomplishments & Milestone Update

Summary
 Long-wavelength (green, yellow, red) visible light-emitting diodes (LEDs) have
historically been inefficient compared to blue light-emitters in the III-nitrides. This is due to many
factors, including poor material quality due to the low growth temperatures required for high In-
composition InGaN quantum wells (QWs), and the polar nature of the III-nitride crystal structure.
Spontaneous and piezoelectric polarization in the III-nitrides causes enhanced quantum-confined
Stark effect (QCSE), which spatially separates electrons and holes and reduces radiative
recombination (ie: light output) from the devices. Polarization in the crystal also induces additional
potential barriers that electrons and holes must cross over before entering the QWs where they can
recombine to emit light. Both of these polarization effects worsen as emission wavelength
increases, making blue III-nitride LEDs are the most efficient and red III-nitride LEDs the least
efficient. The focus of this project has been solving the latter polarization-related issue through the
implementation of V-defects in long wavelength LEDs.
 V-defects are morphological defects which are commonly observed in c-plane III-nitrides.
They are observed as hexagonal pyramid-shaped depressions on the c-plane surface, with six
semipolar sidewalls. They typically form at the apex of threading dislocations (TDs) under
conditions of kinetically-limited growth and low growth-temperature. They were initially thought
to be detrimental to LED performance and much early work focused on eliminating them entirely
from III-nitride devices. However, over the past decade work has emerged that indicate that they
can improve LED performance by allowing electrons and holes to bypass the polarization-induced
barriers present in the c-plane and directly enter the QWs of an LED. This is due to the semipolar
nature of the V-defect sidewall: these sidewalls are thin and lack the polarization-induced barriers
which prevent carriers easily moving between layers. V-defects have since been determined to be
an efficient avenue by which to inject electrons and holes into the c-plane QWs where they can
recombine to emit light.
 Throughout this project we have explored lateral injection through a variety of methods:
simulation (Task 2), epitaxial growth of V-defect and non-V-defect LEDs (Tasks 1, 3, 4, 5, 6),
and advanced characterization methods (Task 7). All tasks have been completed. A description
of each task completed follows from this section.

Task 1
 The reference LEDs were grown and characterized in Y1 of the project, completing M1.0.
Task 1 is complete and J-V data for these reference blue and green LEDs is shown in Task 3. We
used references and carefully engineered V-defect LEDs to achieve a significant reduction in
forward voltage (~0.9 V reduction), completing M1.0 and M4.2. V-defects work by creating
lateral pathways for electrons and holes to reach the quantum wells. This lateral carrier injection
helps bypass the large voltage barriers that exist on the c-plane (growth plane) in long-wavelength
III-N LEDs enabling lower voltage operation and improved wall plug efficiency (WPE).

 Simulating a 3D V-defect with alloy fluctuations built into the model is computationally
prohibitive. As such, we’ve developed a model which parameterizes carrier mobility and lifetime
from 2D structures and then uses these values in the 3D simulation. While not perfect, this solution
should better resemble actual device performance.

the device base and active region are 10 µm, respectively. The c-plane structure of the LEDs

Task 3
 Task 3 was centered around metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) growth
studies for the optimization of V-defect LEDs. Task 3 has been completed. The summary of the
progress will proceed in the order of the subtasks.
 Subtask 3.1 focused on the formation of threading dislocations (TDs) and V-defects on
patterned sapphire substrate (PSS). V-defects typically form at the apex of TDs under kinetically-
limited growth conditions, as shown in Figure 3.1 [1]. The primary factor in their opening is low
growth temperature (< 900 °C), but other factors including growth rate, V/III ratio, Si-doping, and
carrier gas play a significant role and have been explored. Because V-defects form at the apex of
TDs, we have conducted experiments regarding increasing threading dislocation density (TDD)
and the resulting V-defect density (VDD) on both flat sapphire substrates (FSS) and PSS. We have
developed reproducible methods for both substrates.

 With repeatable V-defect formation achieved, further experiments related to controlling
VDD and V-defect size were conducted. For GaN grown on FSS, TDD can be controlled during
the nucleation and coalescence phases of the growth on the bare sapphire. This is primarily through
varying the V/III ratio during the coalescence phase of the growth. A schematic of how this occurs
is shown in Figure 3.3 (a). Increasing V/III ratio during the initial phases of growth results in more
vertical growth and an increased density of GaN islands. This results in a higher density of
coalescence boundaries and higher density of pure-edge TDs [3].
 V/III ratio can be effectively controlled by tuning the NH3 (the group-V precursor) flow.
X-ray diffraction (XRD) results across samples with various NH3 flows during the coalescence
phase are shown in Figure 3.3 (b). All samples measured were grown via MOCVD upon FSS.

coalescence layer in which the NH3 flow was varied, and concluded with 2 µm n-GaN with
[Si] = 5 ´ 1018 cm-2. 𝑇𝐷𝐷 ∝ (∆𝜔)5 , where ∆𝜔 is the full-width half-maximum of a given peak

 For a green LED, the ideal V-defect density is ~1 ´ 109 cm-2 and the ideal ratio of pitted to
planar surface area approaches 50% [4,5]. By tuning V/III ratio during the initial phases of GaN
epitaxy on FSS, V-defect density can be controlled well within the 108 - 1010 cm-2 range [2], and
the V-defect size and area density can be controlled with subsequent growth. However, for high

 13

efficiency LEDs high light-extraction is required and thus PSS should be used as a substrate as
opposed to FSS. PSS has been found to increase light extraction by nearly 50% [6], which would
result in increased external quantum efficiency (EQE) and wall-plug efficiency (WPE) as a result.
However, the TDD of GaN grown on PSS is typically in the 107 – 108 cm-2 range [2,7] and cannot
be tuned during the initial stages of growth if using GaN-on-PSS templates from an industrial
supplier. As such, a new method of TD formation must be used to open V-defects in sufficient
densities for high efficiency long wavelength LEDs.
 We have developed a novel method for the formation of pure-edge TDs on PSS. During
our initial V-defect formation experiments on PSS, similar structures to that shown in
Figure 3.2 (b) were grown and a variety of growth parameters were explored within the V-defect
formation layer. Increasing the disilane flow (the Si-precursor for n-type GaN) within the V-defect
formation layer was found to produce small surface depressions in addition to the large V-defects
expected, as shown in Figure 3.4 (a). Closer inspection revealed these surface depressions to be
aligned along the a-plane of the GaN surface, offset 30° from the hexagonal sides of the V-defect
surface depressions which are oriented along the m-planes. Further cross-section high-angular
annular dark field (HAADF) transmission electron microscope (TEM) imaging revealed these
surface depressions to result from half-loop dislocations, whose TD arms incline and extend
upward from the misfit segment where they are formed to the sample surface. This is shown in
Figure 3.4 (b).

 This finding indicates that TDs can be formed in addition to those formed at the hetero-
interface between GaN and its initial substrate. To explore how pure-edge half-loop dislocations
can be used to form V-defects in densities sufficient for a long wavelength LED, various growth
parameters were explored in the half-loop formation layer.
 The most effective parameters within the formation layer for controlling half-loop density
(“small V-defects” due to the surface depressions they produce) were found to be disilane flow,
growth temperature, and thickness of the formation layer, as shown in Figure 3.7. The results from
the disilane flow series indicate that high Si-doping is a hard requirement for half-loop formation.
At 5 sccm disilane flow in the formation layer, which is a bit higher than what is conventionally
used in the n-GaN of our LEDs at comparable Ga-precursor flows, the density of half-loops drops
below the already low density of TDs native to the GaN-on-PSS which result in large V-defects.
Growth temperature and layer thickness had predictable results: growing colder results in more
defect formation and growing the formation layer thicker allows for more opportunities for half-
loops to form. Both resulted in higher pure-edge half-loop TDD. By combining these parameters,

 16

TDD and resulting VDD can be controlled within the 108 – 1010 cm-2 range, allowing for a wide
range of VDD to be used for LED experiments.

 The final component to make this ideal for LED experiments surrounding V-defect density
and size is uniform V-defect size and distribution. When the temperature is reduced to form half-
loop TDs, existing TDs in the GaN-on-PSS template will open into V-defects due to the kinetically
limited growth regime. This results in a bimodal distribution in defect size. A different solution is
shown in Figure 3.8. After forming half-loops in the formation layer, increasing the growth
temperature and reducing the V/III ratio will effectively planarize over the sample surface, filling
in V-defects that may have opened while preserving the TDs from the substrate and formed by
half-loops. A subsequent n-GaN buffer may be grown at high temperature if desired to form an
accessible n-contact for LED fabrication if desired. Reducing the growth temperature once more
prior to SL or active region growth while keeping Si-doping in a more conventional regime

half-loops, into V-defects. A SL or additional GaN may be grown to enlarge the V-defects. The

uniform size, occurring in densities which closely match the sum of the large and small surface
depressions observed in Figure 3.8 (a).

 While LEDs are generally operated in forward bias conditions, their characteristics under

 To examine the electrical properties of the improved V-defect template structure, our
reference green LED recipe was grown on V-defect templates with these two SL growth
temperatures, both with and without the high temperature n-GaN layer before the SL. Radial
voltage data from fabricated devices taken at 20 A/cm-2 can be seen in Figure 3.16 (a). The sample
with no high temperature n-GaN pre-layer and the colder SL (250225BL) exhibited the best
voltage performance, with low forward voltage in the center of the wafer where V-defect density
is highest. For this sample, voltage increased as radial position increased as opposed to a radial
voltage decrease. This indicates there is predominantly lateral current injection via V-defect
sidewalls in this sample as opposed to vertical c-plane injection. For vertical c-plane injection,
forward voltage increases with increasing In-composition in the InGaN QWs due to the
polarization-induced barriers to carrier injection at the InGaN/GaN interface surrounding each
QW. These potential barriers increase with In-composition. In-composition in InGaN decreases
with growth temperature and our heater surrounds the wafer externally during MOCVD growth so
the outer radius is hotter than the wafer center. This results in a decrease in In-composition in the
QWs going outward on the wafer, which would result in a radial voltage decrease if only vertical
injection were considered. The radial voltage increase from a much lower voltage at the wafer
center that we observe indicates a large amount of lateral current injection at the wafer center via
V-defect sidewalls; voltage increases going outward on the wafer radius for our V-defect LEDs
because V-defect density decreases with increasing growth temperature.

 Going forward, experiments were conducted without any AlGaN layers. In addition to
removing the EBL, AlGaN caps were replaced with GaN caps and temperature-graded GaN
barriers. This was done to any other potential variables which could be contributing to increased
forward voltage and V-defect re-filling besides the GaN QBs themselves. The changes in device
structure can be seen in Figure 3.22. At the conclusion of AlGaN cap experiments, AlGaN caps
were determined to not contribute a significant voltage penalty but were omitted from QB
experiments to reduce their complexity.
 In the new structure, multi-quantum well (MQW) period was increased from six to eight
QWs. This was done because, assuming lateral carrier injection is occurring via the V-defect
sidewalls, additional QWs should not contribute additional voltage penalties due to the bypassing
of polarization-induced barriers. The thickness of planarizing p-GaN was increased from 30 nm to
60 nm, as it was found that with open V-defects thicker planarization layers were needed to avoid
device shorting and burn-out. Mg-doping was removed from the final QB as this improved light
output from devices, leaving the only p-type layers to be the p-GaN planarization layer and the
p++contact layer. To prevent InGaN decomposition without AlGaN capping layers, GaN was
continuously grown during the temperature ramp from the QW growth temperature, which range
from 800 °C to 830 °C depending on the target wavelength, to the high-temperature QB growth
temperature at 900 °C. This results in a temperature graded QB between the QW and high-
temperature QB that is 4.5 nm ~ 6 nm thick, depending on the duration of the temperature ramp
between steps.

 To conclusively determine if the high-temperature QB is responsible for excess forward
voltage and V-defect filling, green LEDs with varying high-temperature QB thickness were grown.
These devices were fabricated using UCSB’s standard QuickFab process with Ti/Au n-contacts
and Pd/Au p-contacts. Radial voltage data of fabricated devices at 20 A/cm2 drive current can be
seen in Figure 3.23. Reducing the thickness of the high-temperature QB from 8 nm to 2 nm
significantly improves the forward voltage of the device. This brought our V-defect green LED
forward voltage at 20 A/cm2 to under 2.8 V, around 0.1 V away from Nanchang University’s [11].
In addition, for all high-temperature QB thicknesses, forward voltage was observed to increase as
devices were tested outward on the wafer radius. This is to be expected because
 1. V-defect density is highest in the wafer center, as this is the farthest from the heating
 element during wafer growth and therefore the coldest.
 2. If lateral carrier injection through the V-defect sidewall is occurring, then we would expect
 to see forward voltage to increase as emission wavelength decreases and photon energy
 increases as polarization-induced barriers are circumvented.
 Conversely, with standard c-plane injection we would expect VF to decrease as emission
wavelength decreases and photon energy increases, as wavelength increases with increasing In-
composition in the QWs and the polarization-induced barriers that carriers must traverse to enter
QWs also increase with In-composition. Because V-defect density is variable across wafer, it is
hard to determine whether V-defect density or emission wavelength is contributing more to the
radial increase in VF at constant drive current. However, both are indicative of lateral carrier
injection through V-defects and are expected for a V-defect LED.

 Task 4 focused on lateral injector engineering, both through in-situ crystal growth and
ex-situ chemical processes in the UCSB Nanofabrication Facility. We have also conducted
extensive simulation work on laterally injected LEDs with an etch and re-growth of the p-side. V-
defects have been found to improve carrier injection into quantum wells (QWs) due to the lower
In-composition in the QWs on the sidewall of V-defects as well as lower polarization-induced
barriers due to the semipolar plane of the sidewalls. We believe that fully vertical sidewalls should
have equal or better carrier injection characteristics if properly engineered. Figure 4.1 shows the
three main types of lateral-injector LEDs (LI-LEDs) explored during Task 4, simulated using our
2D simulations with allow fluctuations.

 The 2D valence band and the 2D hole current density for the LI-LEDs is shown in
Figure 4.2. We observed that the quantum barriers present a large barrier to holes, preventing them
from injecting vertically into the wells. Thus, as shown in the 2D hole current density diagram,

most of the hole current is flowing through the sidewalls, injecting directly into the quantum wells
from the side, due to the low polarization induced barrier.

 However, to experimentally develop such a structure, a regrowth of the p-side is needed

silicon, we decided to test the effect of having a 3Å layer of silicon at a concentration of 3.33 ×

the IQE and J-V plots, we found that there was a negligible effect by the Si impurity. However,

needed to raise it the sheet density closer to the 1013 cm-2 range to match real-life regrowth
interfaces to confirm our results.

 To ensure that pure lateral injection is taking place, plans are in place to etch down the
p-GaN on an industry grown LED wafer using a low damage atomic layer etching, that has an etch
selectivity of GaN over AlGaN, to the EBL. Then UID GaN will be grown on the sample and a
similar pillar etch and regrowth process will be performed, where both the etch and regrowth
processes can be optimized. The UID GaN on the surface is to ensure that there are no carriers
transporting vertically into the active region and that all carriers in the active region are injected
only laterally to see if what simulations predict is truly possible. An industry sample is also being
used due to their high uniformity standards, which will allow for more accurate comparisons
between the various etch and regrowth conditions.
 Our etch and regrowth studies were promising, but although the devices showed a voltage
benefit, there was also significant leakage, as seen in Figure 4.21. Generally, it’s difficult to etch
and regrow through the active region. There’s also a significant concern of excess [Si] at the
growth interface. Since Si is the n-type dopant in GaN this will almost certainly change the
performance of the lateral injection pathway.
 In Year 3, we began work on a new method of lateral injector patterning but with a
substantially different approach. Instead of etched and regrown lateral injectors, we began
intentionally patterning V-defects using dielectric dots, which can be lithographically patterned
and then overgrown. If the overgrowth stopped just before planarization, the result should be
regularly spaced V-defects in arrays. Preliminary experiments worked with typical lithography
tools which don’t have the necessary resolution to reach optimal V-defect densities but have
developed holographic lithography techniques which should be able to reach the needed resolution
and V-pit density for complete and regularly spaced lateral injection. Our preliminary results are
shown in Figure 4.22.

 Figure 4.22: (a) The mask design used in exposure for the SiN array using the Heidelberg Maskless Aligner. (b)
 The resulting SiN-dot pattern after ion-beam deposition and liftoff, imaged via SEM. (c) An SEM micrograph of
 the sample after successful MOCVD regrowth. A regular array of V-defects is achieved, with additional V-defects
 in-between the hexagonal pattern originating from substrate dislocations in the GaN-on-PSS template.

 As a proof-of concept, to confirm that dielectric SiN patterning could indeed produce V-
defect arrays after MOCVD regrowth, experiments were conducted at the µm-scale. A mask was
designed with 1 µm diameter hexagons in 10 µm spaced hexagonal array to fully expose a 2” GaN-
on-PSS template from Enkris Semiconductor. The mask pattern is shown in Figure 4.22 (a).

After a standard solvent clean in acetone, isopropanol, and de-ionized water, a thin SPR 955CM-
0.9 photoresist was spun on and soft-baked at 95 °C for 90 seconds. The mask design was then
exposed on a Heidelberg Maskless Aligner to produce a uniform hexagonal array of openings in
the resist, hard baked at 110 °C for 90 seconds, and developed in AZ 300 MIF developer for 60s
before rinsing. After a UV-Ozone descum of the photoresist mask and native oxide removal in
HCl, a thin 30 nm SiN layer was deposited via ion-beam deposition. The dielectric film over the
resist-coated regions was lifted off using AZ NMP resist-stripper, followed by acetone,
isopropanol, and de-ionized water. An SEM micrograph of the sample surface after liftoff is shown
in Figure 4.22 (b).
 After successfully patterning the GaN-on-PSS template with a µm-scale SiN-dot array,
MOCVD regrowth experiments were conducted to produce a hexagonal array of V-defects. The
regrowth was a two step process of growing high temperature GaN, first with high V/III ratio and
then with low V/III ratio. The goal was to grow enough GaN to reach the top of the SiN dots under
high vertical growth rate conditions, followed by a lateral overgrowth where the dots were mostly
covered leaving a V-shaped pit over each of the SiN dots. The initial results can be seen in
Figure 4.22 (c). However, these V-defects are far too large for an actual V-defect engineered LED.
This will be addressed with longer overgrowth and small SiN dots. Overall this was a successful
proof-of-concept but more work will need to be done before this is to be realized in full LEDs.
 After successfully producing V-defect arrays at the µm-scale, with roughly 107 cm-2 areal
density, experiments began for lithographically defining V-defects with a density in the 109 cm-2
range, which is the density required for efficient use of V-defects for lateral injection in GaN LEDs.
New tools were needed to do this, as the Heidelberg Maskless Aligner cannot expose features
under 1 µm and even the 0.9 µm thick resist does not have the resolution for such fine features.
 Two GaN-on-PSS template from Enkris Semiconductor was used for this experiment. A
30 nm SiN layer was initially deposited with plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition
(PECVD), followed by a solvent clean in acetone, isopropanol, and de-ionized water, and a
dehydration bake at 115 °C exceeding 30 minutes. A 400 nm thick resist, THMR 3600HP, was
spun on, followed by a soft bake at 95 °C for 3 minutes. The sample was then exposed via
holographic lithography, where self-interference of a 350 nm laser is used to expose uniform lines
in the resist with a 240 nm pitch. The general process for holography is described earlier in this
task, with a schematic shown in Figure 4.13. Two 5 minute exposures were done on each sample,
with one sample rotated roughly 60° and the other rotated roughly 90° between subsequent
exposures. A 3 minute hard bake at 115 °C was then performed, followed by a 15 second develop
in AZ 300 MIF and gentle rinsing in de-ionized water. After a 20 second O2 plasma descum at
300 mT/100W in an asher, SEM images were taken to confirm the lithographic patterning was
successful. The results of these fabrication experiments are shown in Figure 4.23.
 To prepare for regrowth, the SiN must be etched. An Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP)
etch of the SiN was performed with 200 W power and 25 W bias, using O2 (5 sccm) and CF4 (50
sccm) as etch gases. The etch rate for SiN was found to be ~2.6 nm/s. This was followed by a dip
in piranha solution (H2SO4:H2O2 = 3:1) for 3 min to strip photoresist before rinsing and drying.
A final plasma descum was performed in the O2 asher for 2 min at 300 mT/100W. A resulting
SEM image of the SiN/GaN surface is shown in Figure 4.24.

Task 5
 Task 5 looks at developing to forward voltage III-N tunnel junctions for epitaxial current
spreading layers for III-N LEDs and cascaded LED structures for RGB white lighting. In Year 4,
we focused on both implementing novel tunnel junction device designs, as well as exploring
strategies to grow quality tunnel junctions without regrowth. Additionally, we explore how to
reduce turnaround time on tunnel junction experiments.
 Past UCSB work on tunnel junctions involved different designs, including both AlGaN and
InGaN polarization tunnel junctions. The Year 4 tunnel junction effort grew tunnel junction
structures on industry blue LED epi. The reference design utilized for the Year 4 tunnel junction
effort is shown in Figure 5.1, which employs both a n++ InGaN interlayer, highly doped
superlattice, and an AlGaN polarization barrier. Alloying compositions in Figure 5.1 are estimates
and need to still be explored to determine the real compositions of each alloyed structure. The
highly doped InGaN provides increased tunneling due to improved band alignment. A highly
doped Si superlattice was utilized to achieve high doping while partially maintaining adequate
surface morphology. AlGaN barrier provides electron sheet charge which we have previously
demonstrated can improve tunneling.

 The preliminary data for the reference structure can be shown in Figure 5.2, which shows
a variety of samples utilizing a variety of different growth conditions, with several devices form
each sample tested and plotted. We demonstrate a low voltage of 3.07 V for the tunnel junction
with the best growth conditions. Analysis is still required for understanding the mechanism for
increased tunnel junction performance.

 While previous efforts and Year 4’s preliminary results utilized regrowth techniques in
order to clean/prep the p-GaN surface for tunnel junction growth, in order for the technology to be
widely adopted in industry, it must be performed without a regrowth. Surface cleaning is
performed on the p-GaN surface to remove Mg on the surface of the substrate/LED. This is done
because the surface Mg can significantly compensate the n-dopants, reducing the band bending of
the tunnel junction and decreasing the band alignment for tunneling. As such, in-situ cleaning
methods need to be developed for the implementation of III-N tunnel junctions in industry. The
Y4Q3 effort began to explore different strategies for fully in-situ grown MOCVD tunnel junctions.
Tunnel junctions were regrown on top of industry blue LED wafers without the Mg cleaning
procedures previously developed in previous work as a reference. Then, two different treatments
were attempted: H2/Si2H6 etching treatment and a thin UID GaN Mg-capping layer, with results
shown on Figure 5.3. It appears that both treatments are effective in lowering the voltage of the
TJ-LED. More work is needed in order to fully optimize both techniques, and to also implement
both during the same in-situ cleaning. The outlined preliminary can be implemented in growing
cascaded LEDs with reasonable voltages without regrowth/ex-situ cleaning techniques.

 Figure 5.3: Preliminary J-V data of different in-situ p-GaN cleaning strategies, plotted in linear scale (left) and
 log-scale (right).

 Activation of Mg has historically been a challenge when growing and fabricating p-GaN
devices. For p-GaN buried with n-GaN, this is especially an issue due to difficulties in hydrogen
diffusion though n-GaN barriers. UCSB has done work in this field, such as select area growth of
LEDs and microLED sidewall treatments to allow for the diffusion of hydrogen out the sidewalls
of the LED. However, this method does not allow for the quicktest of LED epi after growth. This
is due to the difficulty of avoiding shorting the p and n of the LED when conducting quicktests.
As such, to get any electro-luminescence (EL) data from the TJ-LED growth one would require
the p-GaN to be at least partially active in order to successfully bias the p-i-n junction. Here we
demonstrate the possibility for maintaining active p-GaN by growth of a tunnel junction on top of
industry blue LED epi without performing the sidewall activation treatment. Growth of the tunnel
junction was performed under low V/III ratio to limit the hydrogen content in the boundary layer
and at the LED surface. As shown in Figure 5.4, the LED exhibits diode behavior, albeit at a

significant voltage penalty attributed to the partial re-passivation of the p-GaN. However, this
result opens the possibility of performing quick-test measurements with tunnel junction LEDs.
Further growth optimization needs to be performed to maximize the free hole concentration in
active buried p-GaN.

Task 7
 Task 7 focused on the identification and mitigation of non-radiative channels in III-nitride
LEDs. This was primarily through electron emission spectroscopy (EES) and microscopy (EEM).
Subtask 7.1 explored the role of the SL in impurity capture. In Year 1 of the project we found that
the inclusion of a pre-multi quantum well SL reduced the Shockley-Read Hall (SRH) and Trap-
Assisted Auger Mietner (TAAR) rates in green LEDs. This implies a reduction of defects in the
active region due to the inclusion of the SL. This work has been published in Ho, Wan Ying, et
al., "Detection of hot electrons originating from an upper valley at ∼1.7 eV above the Γ valley in
wurtzite GaN using electron emission spectroscopy." Physical Review B 107, 035303 (2023) [1].
This completed Subtask 7.1.
 Subtask 7.2 focused on the effect of LED designs on TAAR through EES measurements.
This subtask was progressed through investigating and imaging the pre-well SL with EES. The
results of these measurements are related to those regarding Subtask 7.1 and once again, reported
in the publication Ho, Wan Ying, et al. "Detection of hot electrons originating from an upper valley
at ∼1.7 eV above the Γ valley in wurtzite GaN using electron emission spectroscopy." Physical
Review B 107, 035303 (2023) [1]. In this work, green LEDs were systematically grown with
different superlattice periods and measured via EES to study the impact the SL has on the
recombination occurring in the active region. We found that by reducing the number of SL periods,
a new electron emission peak was observed. This new peak was ascribed to a hot electron
generating TAAR process. This indicated that inclusion of the SL helps capture impurities prior to
active region growth, where these impurities can act as undesired TAAR centers.
 59

 Subtask 7.3 was to develop advanced characterization methods for etched sidewalls and
regrown interfaces. These interfaces were characterized in Year 1. In addition, regrowth
experiments were conducted in tandem with Task 4, with some etch and regrowth LEDs being
successfully grown and fabricated. A majority of this work has been presented with the results of
Task 4, earlier in this report. While etch and subsequent regrowth was successful, the large density
of impurities at the regrowth interface led to devices with large leakage currents. We are currently
in the process of implementing new cleaning methods to help mitigate the impurities at the etched
sidewall prior to regrowth. Initial studies show a large success in removing the impurities at
regrown interfaces. These results have been published in Biegler, Allerman, and Speck, “Removal
of Si impurities from GaN regrowth interfaces using XeF2,” Applied Physics Letters 127, 072101
(2025) [2].
 Subtask 7.4 focused on the monitoring of SRH mechanisms. This was done in tandem
with Task 6. In Y1Q3 and Y3Q3 we conducted differential carrier lifetime measurements to
determine SRH, radiative, and 3-body Auger recombination coefficients in single QW violet, blue,
and green LEDs with and without doped barriers. We also conducted thermal EES measurements
on commercial green and blue LEDs in Year 4. The results of the thermal EES measurements,
along with state-of-the-art two-dimensional simulations show that SRH is the leading cause of
thermal droop, even in high quality LEDs. The results of this study have been published in Tak,
Tanay, et al., “Role of the junction voltage on the overflow current in light-emitting diodes,”
Physical Review Applied 24, 064025 (2025) [3].
 Subtask 7.5 was the EES study of green and blue LEDs with novel structures. Blue and
green LEDs were studied with EEM to experimentally verify lateral injection of carriers through
V-defect sidewalls in Year 2. Thermal measurements of blue and green LEDs were also conducted
in Year 4. Prior to EEM studies of the LEDs, we employed EEM to study the current spreading in
p-GaN. This study showed that current spreading through a resistive p-GaN layer on top of a
conductive n-GaN layer followed the analytical expressions derived by Joyce and Wemple as they
report in Journal of Applied Physics 41, 3818-3830 (1970) [4]. This experimental matching of the
current spreading model is reported in Ho, Wan Ying, et al. "Steady-State Junction Current
Distribution in p-n GaN Diodes Measured using Low-Energy Electron Microscopy
(LEEM)." Applied Physics Letters 123, 031101 (2023) [5]. This study was important to verify
Joyce and Wemple’s model, as that is what we use in our usual EES and EEM measurements to
determine the size of our exposed apertures to ensure there is sufficient current spreading across
them. Next, an EEM study was performed on a green V-defect engineered LED. During this study,
it was observed that there were dark spots in the electron emission micrographs which
corresponded to V-defects in the active region- allowing us a method to directly image the
microstructure of the active region of a device. Further, we observed strong electron emission from
the rims of these non-electron-emitting V-defects, that indicated a large present carrier density
around them. This large present carrier density around V-defects is a direct consequence of their
lateral injection of carriers and provided us with direct evidence of their beneficial lateral injection.
The results of this study were reported in Tak, Tanay, et al. “Injection mechanisms in a III-nitride
light-emitting diode as seen by self-emissive electron microscopy.” Physics Review Applied 20,
064045 (2023) [6].
 Subtask 7.6 involved the absolute quantification of TAAR. p-i-n structures of variable
p-layer thickness were grown and studied in Year 1 to serve as a proof of viability of absolute hot
electron measurements. Atomic layer etching was then used in Year 1 for the systematic etch-back

 60

of the p-region. We verified lateral current and carrier profile of LEDs using EEM and fabricated
EEM structure devices. In Year 3, p-i-n devices with varying i- and p-region thicknesses were
grown and measured with EES, where unexpected high energy electrons were detected at high
current densities. This was indicative of TAAR processes. This measurement of TAAR electrons
from a simple p-i-n diode, where one only expects simple drift-diffusion transport and
SRH/radiative recombination, was highly unexpected. The results of this study are reported in Tak,
Tanay, et al., "Trap-assisted Auger-Meitner recombination in GaN p-i-n diodes," Physical Review
B 111, 155308 (2025) [7]. Based on the results of the prior study, we looked into developing a
rate-based analysis of what happens in defect-assisted recombination cycles when the same defect
can have multi-phonon emission (MPE), radiative, and TAAR capture processes simultaneously.
This resulted in the defect-assisted recombination rate scaling up to the square of the carrier density,
instead of the linear dependence with carrier density that the conventionally used Shockley-Read-
Hall recombination model leads to. The results of this study are currently under review.
