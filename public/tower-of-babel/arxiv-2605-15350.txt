Stochastic Compositional Optimization via Hybrid Momentum Frank–Wolfe
El Mahdi Chayti

Abstract
Stochastic compositional optimization minimizes objectives of the form min 𝒙 ∈ 𝒳 ⁡ F ⁡ ( 𝒇 ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) , 𝒙 ) \min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}F({\bm{f}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{x}}) , where 𝒇 {\bm{f}} is accessible only through noisy stochastic queries. Existing methods for this problem assume that the outer function F F is continuously differentiable, which excludes many practically important applications such as robust max-of-losses, Conditional Value-at-Risk, and norm regularizers. We propose the Hybrid Momentum Stochastic Frank–Wolfe algorithm, which drops the smoothness assumption on F F . By combining a momentum-based Jacobian tracker with a Taylor-corrected function tracker, the algorithm feeds an entire stochastic linearization—rather than a single gradient—into a generalized linear minimization oracle. We establish an 𝒪 ( K − 1 / 4 ) \mathcal{O}(K^{-1/4}) convergence rate in the generalized Frank–Wolfe gap for non-convex objectives with L F L_{F} -Lipschitz outer functions, matching the optimal complexity for projection-free single-sample stochastic methods under expected smoothness. The analysis extends to heavy-tailed noise oracles with bounded r r -th moments for r ∈ ( 1 , 2 ] r\in(1,2] and recovers the deterministic rates of 24 as the noise vanishes.



1
Introduction

Modern machine learning increasingly demands optimization paradigms that go beyond classical Empirical Risk Minimization. Risk-averse learning, distributionally robust optimization, multi-task learning, and fairness-constrained training all give rise to objectives of the form

\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\varphi({\bm{x}}):=F({\bm{f}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{x}})\quad\text{where}\quad{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\tilde{{\bm{f}}}({\bm{x}};\xi)],

(1)

where
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is convex and compact,
{\bm{f}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a vector-valued inner mapping accessible only via stochastic queries, and
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a deterministic outer function. This is the
fully composite
formulation introduced in
11
and revisited in its projection-free variant by
(
24
)
(in the deterministic case); this formulation generalizes both classical compositional optimization
F({\bm{f}}({\bm{x}}))
and additive composite optimization.

Solving equation
1
stochastically poses a fundamental challenge: by Jensen’s inequality, naive plug-in estimators are biased,
\mathbb{E}[F(\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}};\xi),{\bm{x}})]\neq F(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}};\xi)],{\bm{x}})
. A line of work culminating in
7
;
4
;
26
addresses this by tracking the inner function with momentum-based estimators and feeding the result through the chain rule
\nabla\varphi({\bm{x}})=\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\nabla F({\bm{f}}({\bm{x}}))
. However, the chain rule
requires
F
to be differentiable
—an assumption that excludes the very examples that motivate fully composite optimization in the first place: max-type losses, CVaR,
\ell_{1}
regularizers, and constraint-penalty formulations.

24
resolved this in the deterministic setting through a
generalized linear minimization oracle
(GLMO), which minimizes
F
evaluated on a linearization of
{\bm{f}}
, treating
F
as a black box rather than differentiating through it. They explicitly leave the stochastic case as an open problem.

Contributions.

This paper addresses that open problem.

1.

We propose the
Hybrid Momentum Stochastic Frank–Wolfe

), the first stochastic projection-free method for fully composite problems with non-smooth outer

2.

We present two tracker variants offering a memory/assumption trade-off:
Variant I
(Polyak update; memoryless but requires
{\bm{f}}
Lipschitz);
Variant II

3.

Under heavy-tailed noise with bounded

convergence in the generalized Frank–Wolfe gap (Theorem

). At

\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/4})

(

primal rate (Theorem

\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/3})

5.

The algorithm interpolates with the deterministic regime: when the oracle is exact, setting the momentum parameters to one recovers the deterministic Basic Method of

together with its

Related work.

The deterministic foundation for our approach was established by
24
, who introduced the GLMO and analyzed the deterministic Basic and Accelerated methods for fully composite optimization. In a related vein,
11
investigated higher-order methods for this same problem class.

In the stochastic regime, early work by
25
introduced SCGD using a two-timescale stepsize schedule. This was later streamlined by
14
via the single-timescale NASA algorithm. Subsequent advancements include SCSC
(
7
)
, which achieved optimal SGD-like rates, and Linearized NASA
(
4
)
, which established level-independent rates. However, all of these methods rely on exact projections and cannot operate in the projection-free setting.

For projection-free stochastic compositional optimization, the current state-of-the-art is LiNASA+ICG
(
26
)
. While it achieves an
\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/4})
convergence rate, it suffers from a high per-iteration cost, requiring
\mathcal{O}(K^{3/2})
LMO calls (yielding an overall
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-4})
SFO and
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-6})
LMO complexity). Furthermore, it fundamentally requires the outer function
F
to be smooth. Similarly,
2
tackle the two-level case but impose strictly stronger assumptions. Crucially, both of these methods require differentiating through
F
, rendering them inapplicable to the non-smooth outer functions considered in our work.

Finally, our variance reduction techniques draw inspiration from the standard (non-compositional) stochastic Frank–Wolfe literature.
18
demonstrated an
\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/3})
rate for convex objectives using momentum tracking, while
28
achieved improved non-convex rates utilizing the STORM variance reduction technique originally developed by
10
. To correct the bias inherent in momentum tracking,
16
and
29
proposed using Hessian-vector products coupled with uniform random variables. This bias correction was recently refined by
6
using Beta-distributed random variables. We adapt both of these fundamental techniques to our fully composite framework, detailing a STORM-based acceleration in Appendix
K
and a Hessian-based acceleration in Appendix
L
.

Problem Setup and Assumptions

Notation.

We use
\|\cdot\|
for the Euclidean norm (Frobenius norm for matrices),
D_{\mathcal{X}}:=\max_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{X}}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|
for the diameter of
\mathcal{X}
, and
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for the all-ones vector.

Generalized Frank–Wolfe gap.
For
{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{X}
, the
generalized Frank–Wolfe gap
is

\hat{\Delta}({\bm{y}}):=\varphi({\bm{y}})-\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}F\big({\bm{f}}({\bm{y}})+\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}})({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}),\,{\bm{x}}\big)\geq 0,

(2)

which generalizes the classical FW gap i.e.: when
F({\bm{u}},{\bm{x}})\equiv u^{(1)}
,
\hat{\Delta}({\bm{y}})
reduces to
\max_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\langle\nabla f_{1}({\bm{y}}),{\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}\rangle
.

Note that by construction,
\hat{\Delta}({\bm{y}})\geq 0
, and
\hat{\Delta}({\bm{y}})=0
characterizes stationarity in the non-smooth setting
(
24
)
.

2.1
Assumptions

Assumption 1

(Domain compactness)
.

\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is convex and compact with diameter
D_{\mathcal{X}}<\infty
.

Assumption 2

(Outer function regularity)
.

F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is (i)
L_{F}
-Lipschitz in
{\bm{u}}
, (ii) monotone non-decreasing in
{\bm{u}}
(i.e.,
{\bm{u}}_{1}\leq{\bm{u}}_{2}
componentwise implies
F({\bm{u}}_{1},{\bm{x}})\leq F({\bm{u}}_{2},{\bm{x}})
), and (iii) subhomogeneous in
{\bm{u}}
:
F(\gamma{\bm{u}},{\bm{x}})\leq\gamma F({\bm{u}},{\bm{x}})
for all
\gamma\geq 1
.
F
is
not
assumed to be differentiable.

Assumption 3

(Inner function smoothness)
.

{\bm{f}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
L
-Lipschitz Jacobian:
\|\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})-\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}})\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq L\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|
for all
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{X}
.

Assumption 4

(Heavy-tailed stochastic oracle)
.

There exists
r\in(1,2]
and constants
\sigma_{f},\sigma_{g}\geq 0
such that for every
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
, the stochastic oracle returns unbiased estimates
\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}};\xi),\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}};\xi)
satisfying

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\|\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}};\xi)-{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})\|^{r}\leq\sigma_{f}^{r},\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\|\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}};\xi)-\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})\|^{r}\leq\sigma_{g}^{r}.

The classical bounded-variance setting corresponds to
r=2
.

The following two assumptions are used in specific results.

Assumption 5

(Lipschitz inner function)
.

{\bm{f}}
is
G
-Lipschitz:
\|{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}})\|\leq G\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|
for all
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{X}
. Used only for Variant I.

Assumption 6

(Convexity for the convex setting)
.

Each
f_{i}
is convex on
\mathcal{X}
and
F({\bm{u}},{\bm{x}})
is jointly convex in
({\bm{u}},{\bm{x}})
. Used only for Theorem
2
; combined with monotonicity of
F
, this implies
\varphi
is convex on
\mathcal{X}
.

Discussion of the assumptions.

Assumptions
1
,
2
(ii)–(iii),
3
, and
6
are standard in the deterministic fully composite optimization literature
(
24
;
11
)
. Assumption
4
is standard in stochastic optimization with heavy-tailed gradients and reduces to bounded variance at
r=2
. Assumption
5
is standard for stochastic gradient methods.

The single new condition in our setting is Assumption
2
(i), the Lipschitzness of
F
in
{\bm{u}}
. This is necessary because
F
is non-smooth: in the absence of differentiability, Lipschitzness is the natural quantitative substitute that controls the deviation between the GLMO evaluated on the exact and tracked surrogates (Lemma
5
). The Lipschitz constant
L_{F}
admits closed-form expressions for all the motivating examples below:
L_{F}=1
for max-of-losses,
\ell_{p}
-norm regularization, and composite regularization;
L_{F}\leq 1/(\alpha\sqrt{n})
for CVaR at level
\alpha
; and
L_{F}\leq\sqrt{(1+\rho)/n}
for
\chi^{2}
-DRO with radius
\rho
.

2.2
Composite Curvature

The composite curvature constant generalizes the standard FW curvature to the non-smooth outer setting:

\mathcal{S}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{X}\\
\gamma\in(0,1]\end{subarray}}\frac{2}{\gamma^{2}}\Big[F({\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{\gamma}),{\bm{y}}_{\gamma})-F({\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})+\gamma\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})({\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}}),{\bm{y}}_{\gamma})\Big],

(3)

where
{\bm{y}}_{\gamma}:={\bm{x}}+\gamma({\bm{y}}-{\bm{x}})
. Under Assumptions
1
–
3
,
\mathcal{S}\leq L_{F}LD_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}\sqrt{n}
(Proposition
1
in the appendix).

3
Algorithm

The algorithm maintains two stochastic trackers: a Polyak Jacobian tracker
{\bm{V}}_{k}
and a function-value tracker
{\bm{z}}_{k}
in one of two variants. At each step, the trackers define an affine surrogate
\tilde{l}_{k}({\bm{x}}):={\bm{z}}_{k}+{\bm{V}}_{k}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}_{k})
, fed into the GLMO.

Algorithm 1

Hybrid Momentum Stochastic Frank–Wolfe

1:

Input:

{\bm{y}}_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
, step-size sequences
\{\gamma_{k},\beta_{k},\rho_{k}\}\subset(0,1]
, total iterations
K
.

2:

Draw
\xi_{0}
; set
{\bm{z}}_{0}\!=\!\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{0};\xi_{0})
,
{\bm{V}}_{0}\!=\!\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{0};\xi_{0})
.

3:

for

k=1,\ldots,K

do

4:

Draw
\xi_{k}
at
{\bm{y}}_{k}
.

5:

Jacobian tracker:

{\bm{V}}_{k}=(1-\beta_{k}){\bm{V}}_{k-1}+\beta_{k}\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k};\xi_{k})
.

6:

Function tracker
(one variant):

Variant I (Polyak):

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{k}=(1-\rho_{k}){\bm{z}}_{k-1}+\rho_{k}\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k};\xi_{k}),

Variant II (Taylor):

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}_{k}=(1-\rho_{k})[{\bm{z}}_{k-1}+{\bm{V}}_{k}({\bm{y}}_{k}-{\bm{y}}_{k-1})]+\rho_{k}\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k};\xi_{k}).

7:

GLMO:

{\bm{x}}_{k+1}\in\argmin_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}F({\bm{z}}_{k}+{\bm{V}}_{k}({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}_{k}),\,{\bm{x}})
.

8:

Update:

{\bm{y}}_{k+1}=(1-\gamma_{k}){\bm{y}}_{k}+\gamma_{k}{\bm{x}}_{k+1}
.

9:

end

for

10:

Return:

{\bm{y}}_{K}
.

Variant trade-off.

Variant I is memoryless and requires only Lipschitz
{\bm{f}}
(Assumption
5
); its tracking error scales linearly with the step distance. Variant II uses the first-order Taylor extrapolation
{\bm{V}}_{k}({\bm{y}}_{k}-{\bm{y}}_{k-1})
to predict
{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k})
from
{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k-1})
, reducing the tracking error to a quadratic Taylor remainder; it eliminates Assumption
5
but requires storing the previous iterate.

Generalized LMO.

The GLMO at line 7 minimizes
F
evaluated on the affine surrogate
\tilde{l}_{k}({\bm{x}})
. As shown by
24
, this oracle is tractable for all the motivating examples below: it reduces to a small linear program (or, in the case of
\ell_{2}
-norm or
\chi^{2}
-DRO, a second-order cone program). Crucially, the GLMO handles the non-smoothness of
F
directly, without requiring a (sub)gradient.
We also note that the GLMO reduces to the classical LMO used in Frank–Wolfe methods
(
13
;
17
)
when
F({\bm{u}},{\bm{x}})\equiv u^{(1)}
.

4
Motivating Examples

We illustrate the GLMO on five paradigmatic non-smooth fully composite problems. In each case, the stochastic inner function is
{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}};\xi)]
.

Example 1

(Robust Minimax Optimization)
.

Consider
n
distinct competing objectives or losses,
f_{i}({\bm{x}})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{i}}[\tilde{f}_{i}({\bm{x}};\xi_{i})]
. We aim to minimize the worst-case expected loss over a structured domain (e.g., an
\ell_{1}
-ball for sparsity)
(
23
)
:

\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}f_{i}({\bm{x}}).

(4)

Here, the outer function is
F({\bm{u}})=\max_{i}u^{(i)}
, which is globally
1
-Lipschitz but non-differentiable.
Tractability:
The GLMO requires minimizing the maximum of
n
affine functions over
\mathcal{X}
, which trivially reformulates as a highly efficient Linear Program (LP) by introducing a single auxiliary scalar variable
(
24
)
.

Example 2

(Norm-Regularized Multi-Task Learning)
.

In multi-task learning, one often minimizes the norm of a vector of expected tasks to encourage joint sparsity or uniformly bounded errors across tasks
(
22
)
:

\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\|{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})\|_{p}.

(5)

Here,
F({\bm{u}})=\|{\bm{u}}\|_{p}
. For
p\geq 1
,
F
is globally Lipschitz but non-smooth at the origin.
Tractability:
For
p=1
or
\infty
, the GLMO reduces to an LP. For
p=2
, it translates to an SOCP, efficiently solvable via interior point methods.

Example 3

(Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR))
.

Risk-averse learning often minimizes the CVaR at level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, which focuses on the
\alpha
-fraction of the worst-case expected losses across
n
tasks
(
20
)
:

\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}({\bm{f}}({\bm{x}}))=\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\inf_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\tau+\frac{1}{\alpha n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max(0,f_{i}({\bm{x}})-\tau)\right\}.

(6)

Here,
F({\bm{u}})
encapsulates the CVaR operator.
Tractability:
Evaluating the GLMO simply requires minimizing a piecewise affine convex function over
\mathcal{X}
. By adding auxiliary variables for the rectified linear units (
\max(0,\cdot)
), the GLMO cleanly formulates as an LP
(
24
)
.

Example 4

(Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO))
.

In DRO, one minimizes the expected loss with respect to the worst-case distribution
\mathbf{q}
within a
\chi^{2}
-divergence ball of radius
\rho
around the uniform distribution
(
19
)
:

\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\mathbf{q}\in\Delta_{n},D_{\chi^{2}}(\mathbf{q},\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1})\leq\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}f_{i}({\bm{x}}).

(7)

Here,
F({\bm{u}})
is the supremum over the
\chi^{2}
ball.
Tractability:
Through convex duality, this minimax objective can be rewritten as a minimization over an affine surrogate, reducing the GLMO to a low-dimensional convex problem bounded by a quadratic constraint (an SOCP).

Example 5

(Composite Regularized Optimization)
.

Consider a standard expected risk minimization problem augmented with a deterministic, potentially non-smooth, convex regularizer
\Xi({\bm{x}})

(
27
)
:

\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\tilde{f}({\bm{x}};\xi)]+\Xi({\bm{x}}).

(8)

Here,
f({\bm{x}})=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{f}({\bm{x}})]
, and the outer function is explicitly dependent on
{\bm{x}}
:
F(u,{\bm{x}})=u+\Xi({\bm{x}})
.
Tractability:
The GLMO computes
\argmin_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\left[{\bm{z}}_{k}+\langle{\bm{V}}_{k},{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}_{k}\rangle+\Xi({\bm{x}})\right]
. Discarding constant terms, this strictly reduces to
\argmin_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\langle{\bm{V}}_{k},{\bm{x}}\rangle+\Xi({\bm{x}})
, which exactly recovers the highly tractable classical composite Frank–Wolfe oracle
(
27
)
.

Further examples covering fairness-constrained learning
(
1
;
12
)
and exact penalty methods
(
9
)
are deferred to Appendix
M
.

Theorem 1

(Non-convex convergence)

5.3
Deterministic Interpolation and Nonconvex Acceleration

A natural sanity check is that the algorithm reduces to the deterministic Frank–Wolfe of
24
when the oracle is exact. The following corollary makes this precise.

Corollary 1

(Deterministic interpolation)
.

Suppose the stochastic oracle is exact (
\sigma_{f}=\sigma_{g}=0
in Assumption
4
). Setting
\beta_{k}=\rho_{k}=1
for all
k
in Algorithm
1
makes the trackers exact:
{\bm{V}}_{k}=\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k})
and
{\bm{z}}_{k}={\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k})
for all
k\geq 1
. The algorithm then reduces to the Basic Method of
24
. With the deterministic step-size schedules, the iterates satisfy:

•

Convex case
(Assumption
6
,
\gamma_{k}=2/(k+2)
):

\varphi({\bm{y}}_{K})-\varphi({\bm{x}}^{*})\leq\frac{2\mathcal{S}}{K+1}.

•

Non-convex case
(
\gamma_{k}=1/\sqrt{k+1}
):

\min_{0\leq k\leq K}\hat{\Delta}_{k}\leq\frac{\Phi_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{S}(1+\ln(K+1))}{\sqrt{K+1}}.

These match Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 of
24
.

The corollary is immediate from the form of the algorithm and Lemma
5
: with exact trackers, the tracking-error contributions vanish and Lemma
5
reduces to the deterministic progress bound. See Appendix
J
for details.

Schedule choice across regimes.

The schedules in Theorems
1
and
2
are noise-aware: they shrink the momentum parameters
\beta_{k},\rho_{k}
as
K
grows in order to absorb the variance of the stochastic estimates. When
\sigma_{f}=\sigma_{g}=0
, no shrinking is needed: setting
\beta_{k}=\rho_{k}=1
recovers the deterministic regime, where Vladarean’s
\gamma_{k}
-only schedule is optimal. The algorithm thus interpolates smoothly: the same code, with three momentum parameters, recovers either the stochastic or the deterministic algorithm depending on how those parameters are set.

Acceleration under stronger smoothness.

The rates in Theorems
1
and
2
are tight under expected smoothness alone (Assumption
3
). When the stochastic Jacobian satisfies a stronger
r
-average smoothness
property (Assumption
7
in Appendix
K
), our framework integrates seamlessly with the STORM variance-reduction technique
(
10
;
16
)
. Specifically, replacing the Polyak Jacobian update by the STORM finite-difference correction (equation

Numerical Experiments

We evaluate the algorithm on three problems featuring non-smooth
F
and non-trivial constraint sets: robust minimax sparse regression, CVaR portfolio optimization, and robust low-rank matrix completion.

Algorithms.

We benchmark four algorithms, each using the same GLMO. (i)
Vanilla SCFW
: stochastic compositional FW with no variance reduction. (ii)
Clipped SCFW
: Vanilla SCFW with the stochastic Jacobian clipped at norm
C
(tuned per task). (iii)
Variant I
(this work, Polyak tracker). (iv)
Variant II
(this work, Taylor tracker). For Variants I and II, we use the theoretically-prescribed schedule at
r=2
:
\gamma=K^{-3/4}
,
\beta=\rho=K^{-1/2}
. We evaluate progress via the generalized FW gap
\hat{\Delta}_{k}
, plotted on log-log scale.

Tasks.

We consider the three following tasks:

Task 1:

Robust minimax sparse regression.
Given
m
data subsets
\{({\bm{a}}_{i,j},b_{i,j})\}_{j=1}^{N_{i}}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
, we solve

\min_{\|{\bm{x}}\|_{1}\leq\tau}\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\tfrac{1}{2N_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{i}}({\bm{a}}_{i,j}^{\top}{\bm{x}}-b_{i,j})^{2},

(11)

where the outer function
F({\bm{u}})=\max_{i}u^{(i)}
is non-smooth. The constraint
\mathcal{X}=\{{\bm{x}}:\|{\bm{x}}\|_{1}\leq\tau\}
admits an
\mathcal{O}(d)
LMO, and the GLMO is a small linear program in
{\bm{x}}
and an auxiliary scalar.

Task 2:

Risk-averse portfolio (CVaR).
Given asset return vectors
{\bm{r}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we solve

\min_{[{\bm{x}},y_{0}]\in\Delta_{d}\times[-1,1]}\Big\{y_{0}+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{r}}}\big[\max(0,-{\bm{r}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}-y_{0})\big]\Big\},

(12)

where
\Delta_{d}
is the probability simplex and
\alpha=0.95
. The outer function involves the non-smooth hinge operator. The GLMO is an LP.

Task 3:

Robust matrix completion.
Given partial observations
\Omega\subset[d]\times[m]
of a target matrix
{\bm{M}}
, we solve

\min_{\|{\bm{X}}\|_{*}\leq\tau}\tfrac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\Omega}|X_{ij}-M_{ij}|,

(13)

where the
\ell_{1}
loss
F({\bm{u}})=\|{\bm{u}}\|_{1}
is non-smooth and the nuclear-norm ball admits an LMO via top-singular-vector computation.

We first verify the convergence behavior on synthetic instances where ground-truth quantities are known and the noise is controlled. Task 1 uses
m=10
data subsets with
d=100
,
\tau=5
, and Gaussian inputs with task-dependent noise. Task 2 uses
d=50
assets with synthetically generated heavy-tailed daily returns. Task 3 uses a
30\times 20
matrix with rank
5
and
30\%

10
random seeds, are shown in Figure

.

Discussion.

Across both synthetic (Figure
1
) and real-world (Figure
2
) experiments, three observations are consistent. First, Vanilla SCFW fails to converge: the gap stagnates because plug-in stochastic estimates are biased through the non-linear
F
. Second, Clipped SCFW partially stabilizes the trajectory but plateaus well above the rate of the momentum-based variants and is sensitive to the clipping threshold. Third, both Variant I (Polyak) and Variant II (Taylor) achieve the predicted
\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/4})
rate.

On Task 1, the two variants perform essentially identically; on Tasks 2 and 3, Variant I slightly outperforms Variant II in the regime explored. Both tasks satisfy Assumption
5

\rho

5

7
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced the first stochastic projection-free algorithm for fully composite optimization capable of handling non-smooth outer functions. Our approach achieves the minimax optimal
\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/4})
rate for non-convex objectives and an
\mathcal{O}(K^{-1/3})
rate for convex objectives under standard finite-variance noise assumptions. Furthermore, the algorithm seamlessly adapts to the noise level, yielding a clean reduction to optimal deterministic rates when the stochastic noise vanishes.

Limitations and Future Work.

While our theoretical and empirical results demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed framework, several open challenges remain. First, our analysis assumes that the non-smooth outer function
F
is deterministic; extending our framework to the
doubly stochastic
regime—where
F
is itself an expectation—remains an important hurdle. Second, our current convergence guarantees hold in expectation. Deriving high-probability bounds, particularly in settings corrupted by heavy-tailed noise, is a crucial next step for robust deployment. Finally, a highly compelling direction for future research is extending our framework to distributed and federated learning settings, where projection-free methods for non-smooth compositional objectives could significantly reduce communication bottlenecks.

Lemma 10

(Decreasing-schedule tracking)

Set

We prove the Jacobian bound; the function tracker is analogous. From the recursion of Lemma

(25)

where

Appendix K
Acceleration via STORM

The Polyak Jacobian tracker yields rate
\mathcal{O}(K^{-(r-1)/(3r-2)})
. STORM replaces the Polyak update by a stochastic finite-difference correction that achieves
\mathcal{O}(K^{-(r-1)/(2r-1)})
, at the cost of
r
-average smoothness.

Assumption 7

(
r
-average smoothness)
.

There exists
\bar{L}>0
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\|\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}};\xi)-\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}};\xi)\|^{r}\leq\bar{L}^{r}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|^{r}
for all
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{X}
.

The STORM Jacobian update reuses
\xi_{k}
at
{\bm{y}}_{k}
and
{\bm{y}}_{k-1}
:

{\bm{V}}_{k}=\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k};\xi_{k})+(1-\beta)[{\bm{V}}_{k-1}-\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k-1};\xi_{k})].

(29)

The function tracker remains the Taylor-corrected Variant II.

Lemma 11

(STORM Jacobian tracking error)
.

Under Assumptions
3
,
4
,
7
with equation
29
and
\|{\bm{y}}_{k}-{\bm{y}}_{k-1}\|\leq\gamma D_{\mathcal{X}}

\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\delta}_{g,k}\|^{r}\leq(1-\beta)^{k}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\delta}_{g,0}\|^{r}+\frac{2^{r}C_{r}\bar{L}^{r}D_{\mathcal{X}}^{r}\gamma^{r}}{\beta}+C_{r}\sigma_{g}^{r}\beta^{r-1}.

Proof.

Subtracting
\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k})
from equation

:
\bm{\delta}_{g,k}=(1-\beta)\bm{\delta}_{g,k-1}+(1-\beta)\bm{\zeta}_{k}+\beta\bm{\xi}_{g,k}
, where
\bm{\zeta}_{k}:=[\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k};\xi_{k})-\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k-1};\xi_{k})]-[\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k})-\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k-1})]

\bm{\zeta}_{k}
and
\bm{\xi}_{g,k}
are mean-zero conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}

Appendix L
Hessian-Corrected Momentum and the Average-Smoothness Equivalence

We now present an alternative acceleration mechanism via a randomized Hessian–vector correction, and prove that bounded Hessian-noise moments imply
r
-average smoothness with explicit constants.

L.1
Bounded Hessian Noise Implies Average Smoothness

Assumption 8

(Bounded
r
-th moment of stochastic Hessian)
.

There exist
\sigma_{H}\geq 0
and
r\in(1,2]
such that
\tilde{\bm{f}}(\cdot;\xi)
is twice differentiable on
\mathcal{X}
a.s., with
\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\|\nabla^{2}\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}};\xi)-\nabla^{2}{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{r}\leq\sigma_{H}^{r}
for all
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
.

Proposition 2

(Hessian noise implies
r
-average smoothness)
.

Under Assumption
3
(which implies
\|\nabla^{2}{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq L
when
{\bm{f}}
is
C^{2}
) and Assumption
8
, Assumption
7
holds with
\bar{L}^{r}=2^{r-1}(\sigma_{H}^{r}+L^{r})
.

Proof.

Step 1.
Using
\|{\bm{A}}\|^{r}\leq 2^{r-1}(\|{\bm{A}}-{\bm{B}}\|^{r}+\|{\bm{B}}\|^{r})
:

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\|\nabla^{2}\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}};\xi)\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{r}\leq 2^{r-1}\sigma_{H}^{r}+2^{r-1}L^{r}=:\bar{L}^{r}.

Step 2.
The mean-value theorem gives, for each
\xi
a.s.,

\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}};\xi)-\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}};\xi)=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}+t({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}});\xi)[{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}]\,dt.

Step 3.
Taking the
r
-th power and applying Jensen on the uniform measure on
[0,1]
(since
s\mapsto s^{r}
is convex):

\|\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}};\xi)-\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}};\xi)\|^{r}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla^{2}\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}+t({\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}});\xi)\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{r}\,dt\cdot\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|^{r}.

Step 4.
Taking expectation and applying Fubini:

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\|\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}};\xi)-\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}};\xi)\|^{r}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\|\nabla^{2}\tilde{\bm{f}}(\cdot;\xi)\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{r}\,dt\cdot\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|^{r}\leq\bar{L}^{r}\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|^{r}.\qed

L.2
Hessian-Corrected Algorithm and Convergence

Let
\alpha_{k}\sim\mathrm{U}(0,1)
and
\zeta_{k}
be drawn independently of
\xi_{k}
from the Hessian oracle. Define
{\bm{y}}_{\alpha,k}:={\bm{y}}_{k-1}+\alpha_{k}({\bm{y}}_{k}-{\bm{y}}_{k-1})
and
\tilde{\bm{H}}_{k}:=\nabla^{2}\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{\alpha,k};\zeta_{k})
. The Hessian-corrected Jacobian update is

{\bm{V}}_{k}=(1-\beta){\bm{V}}_{k-1}+\beta\nabla\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k};\xi_{k})+(1-\beta)\tilde{\bm{H}}_{k}({\bm{y}}_{k}-{\bm{y}}_{k-1}).

(30)

Lemma 12

(Unbiasedness of Hessian–vector correction)
.

Conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
:
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{H}}_{k}({\bm{y}}_{k}-{\bm{y}}_{k-1})|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]=\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k})-\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k-1})
.

Proof.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus,
\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k})-\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k-1})=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}[\nabla^{2}{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{\alpha,k})]({\bm{y}}_{k}-{\bm{y}}_{k-1})
. By unbiasedness of the stochastic Hessian and conditional independence of
\alpha_{k},\zeta_{k}
:

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{H}}_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1},\alpha_{k}]=\nabla^{2}{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{\alpha,k}),

and taking iterated expectation completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 13

(Hessian-corrected Jacobian tracking error)
.

Under Assumptions
3
,
4
,
8
with
\xi_{k}\perp\zeta_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
and
\|{\bm{y}}_{k}-{\bm{y}}_{k-1}\|\leq\gamma D_{\mathcal{X}}

\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\delta}_{g,k}\|^{r}\leq(1-\beta)^{k}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\delta}_{g,0}\|^{r}+\frac{2^{r-1}C_{r}\bar{L}^{r}D_{\mathcal{X}}^{r}\gamma^{r}}{\beta}+C_{r}\sigma_{g}^{r}\beta^{r-1},

where
\bar{L}^{r}=2^{r-1}(\sigma_{H}^{r}+L^{r})
.

Proof.

Step 1: Decomposition.
Subtracting
\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k})
from equation
30
:

\bm{\delta}_{g,k}=(1-\beta)\bm{\delta}_{g,k-1}+(1-\beta)\bm{\zeta}_{H,k}+\beta\bm{\xi}_{g,k},

where
\bm{\zeta}_{H,k}:=\tilde{\bm{H}}_{k}({\bm{y}}_{k}-{\bm{y}}_{k-1})-(\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k})-\nabla{\bm{f}}({\bm{y}}_{k-1}))
. By Lemma
12
,
\mathbb{E}[\bm{\zeta}_{H,k}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]=\mathbf{0}
. By Assumption
4

\mathbb{E}[\bm{\xi}_{g,k}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]=\mathbf{0}

Step 2: Moment bound on
\bm{\zeta}_{H,k}
.
By Step 1 of the proof of Proposition
2
,
\mathbb{E}\|\nabla^{2}\tilde{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}};\zeta)\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{r}\leq\bar{L}^{r}
. Hence
\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\bm{H}}_{k}({\bm{y}}_{k}-{\bm{y}}_{k-1})\|^{r}\leq\bar{L}^{r}\gamma^{r}D_{\mathcal{X}}^{r}
. Since
\bm{\zeta}_{H,k}
is a centering of
\tilde{\bm{H}}_{k}({\bm{y}}_{k}-{\bm{y}}_{k-1})
, the standard centering inequality
\mathbb{E}\|{\bm{V}}-\mathbb{E}{\bm{V}}\|^{r}\leq 2^{r-1}\mathbb{E}\|{\bm{V}}\|^{r}

\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\zeta}_{H,k}\|^{r}\leq 2^{r-1}\bar{L}^{r}\gamma^{r}D_{\mathcal{X}}^{r}

Using conditional independence and

\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\delta}_{g,k}\|^{r}\leq(1-\beta)\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\delta}_{g,k-1}\|^{r}+2^{r-1}C_{r}(1-\beta)\bar{L}^{r}\gamma^{r}D_{\mathcal{X}}^{r}+C_{r}\beta^{r}\sigma_{g}^{r}.

Unrolling, with
\sum_{i}(1-\beta)^{k-i}\leq 1/\beta
