Dissipative phase decision without ground-state preparation
Hao-En Li, Yilun Yang, Lin Lin

Abstract
We propose a dynamical approach to identifying ground-state quantum phases through short-time dissipative cooling. Rather than determining the phase by preparing highly accurate approximations to ground states, we prepare a representative state of a candidate phase and monitor the early-time response of phase-sensitive observables under cooling dynamics tailored to the target Hamiltonian. For a class of phase-decision problems in which the relevant observables can be inferred from the low-energy manifold, and with jump operators implementable using only short-time Hamiltonian simulation, the dissipative evolution rapidly suppresses high-energy components and drives the system into a low-energy manifold whose observables already reveal the underlying ground-state phase, well before mixing to the steady state. We demonstrate this strategy for the frustrated J 1 J_{1} – J 2 J_{2} Heisenberg chain, the Kitaev honeycomb model, and the XXZ chain, including Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless and topological phase transitions. In particular, coarse filter resolutions and short evolution times suffice to recover phase-sensitive quantities such as the Luttinger parameter and topological diagnostics. We further provide theoretical justification that cooling dynamics with such jump operators can rigorously prepare low-energy manifolds for free-fermionic and free-bosonic systems, and investigate this mechanism for interacting fermionic systems. Our results suggest that phase decision is a plausible target for future utility-scale studies on early fault-tolerant quantum devices.



I
Introduction

Quantum many-body systems often exhibit a wide range of competing phases and phase transitions even at zero temperature
Sachdev 2011
;
Carr 2010
. Understanding such a ground-state phase diagram involves two distinct tasks. The first is to identify the relevant competing phases, and the second is to decide, for a given parameter point, which phase is realized. In many settings one already has plausible candidate phases, suggested for instance by effective theories, numerical evidence, or experiments, but deciding between them remains costly. We refer to the second task as the
phase-decision
problem, whose precise definition will be given in
Section

III
.

Our approach is motivated by the following classical example. To decide which of ice and liquid water is thermodynamically preferred at a given temperature, one could compare static properties such as the free energies of the two phases, as illustrated in
Fig.

1
a. Another approach is to start from the ice phase and ask whether it begins to melt. Once melting sets in, one need not wait for full equilibration to infer that the liquid phase is thermodynamically preferred. This dynamical perspective is illustrated in
Fig.

1
b. Computationally, one can monitor qualitative changes in microscopic observables during melting, such as the pair correlation function
Widom 2002
.

To solve the quantum phase-decision problem, one can again take either a static or a dynamical perspective, as illustrated in
Fig.

1
c,d. The static perspective prepares representative states for the candidate phases, estimates their energies accurately, and compares them. This static perspective has been widely used in classical quantum many-body computation

;

;

;

On a quantum computer, quantum phase estimation (QPE) can estimate energies with high precision, but it requires an initial state with sufficient overlap with the relevant eigenstate and circuit depth long enough to resolve the target energy splitting, which is demanding for small-gap phase decisions

;

;

;
Albash and Lidar 2018
.

This work instead explores the
dissipative
analogue of the dynamical perspective. Rather than resolving small energy differences between carefully prepared approximations to competing ground states, we ask whether short-time dissipative cooling of a representative state already contains enough information to decide the phase. In this picture, one starts from a representative state of one candidate phase, evolves it with dissipative cooling dynamics tailored to the target Hamiltonian
Diehl et al. 2008
;
Kraus et al. 2008
;
Verstraete et al. 2009
;
Zhou et al. 2021

;

;

;
Feldmeier et al. 2026
;
Molpeceres et al. 2026
, and monitors phase-sensitive observables. If these observables already enter the phase region associated with the ground state, the phase decision can be made before the dynamics reaches the steady state. This viewpoint is close in spirit to earlier works that use quench dynamics to probe phases

Dissipative protocols differ from quench probes because the evolution is biased toward lower energies, and become particularly attractive when the phase decision depends on low-energy information, but not on preparing a nearly exact ground state.

We emphasize that this dynamical perspective is not intended to solve arbitrary ground-state phase-diagram problems. Classical dynamics may be slowed by metastable behavior such as nucleation barriers

;

, and quantum many-body dynamics can likewise exhibit metastability

;

We thus focus on a narrower setting, in which dissipative cooling rapidly suppresses high-energy components and reaches a low-energy sector whose observables already distinguish the competing phases. Because the phase-decision task asks only for a binary answer, it may be more robust to noise than full state preparation, and is therefore a plausible target for future utility-scale studies on early fault-tolerant devices.

. If phase discrimination is already possible from low-energy states, one can instead use coarser filters and stop the dissipative evolution once the diagnostic has stabilized. Our work uses the dissipative constructions developed in
Ding et al. 2024
;

, but for a different objective and in a different regime. We employ jump operators implementable using only short-time Hamiltonian simulation, and study the resulting dissipative dynamics well before it mixes to the steady state. This pre-mixing regime has received much less analysis in the literature, and it raises two questions. First, does the dynamics already produce informative low-energy states in that regime? We answer this affirmatively for free fermions and free bosons, and argue that interacting fermionic systems generically combine aspects of both behaviors. Second, can such low-energy states decide phases in concrete settings? We show that using suitable diagnostics, phase decision problems can be solved in representative examples including the frustrated
J_{1}
–
J_{2}
Heisenberg chain, the Kitaev honeycomb model, and the XXZ spin chain. We emphasize that the present demonstrations are proof-of-principle tests in classically accessible regimes, which provide controlled settings for validation, before moving to more challenging interacting systems. Throughout, dissipative cooling is used as an algorithmic tool for accessing the low-energy physics of the target Hamiltonian, rather than for engineering exotic mixed-state phases

II
Dissipative cooling with realistic filters

The recently developed dissipative cooling dynamics
Ding et al. 2024
;
Zhan et al. 2026
are governed by the Lindblad equation
Lindblad 1976
;
Gorini et al. 1976
:

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}\rho}{{\mathrm{d}}t}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{L}}[\rho]=\underbrace{-\mathrm{i}[H,\rho]}_{\mathcal{L}_{H}[\rho]}+\underbrace{\sum_{a}\left(K_{a}\rho K_{a}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{K_{a}^{\dagger}K_{a},\rho\right\}\right)}_{\mathcal{D}[\rho]},

(1)

where the Lindblad superoperator
{\mathcal{L}}
, or the generator of the quantum Markov semigroup, consists of a coherent part
\mathcal{L}_{H}
and a dissipative part
\mathcal{D}
. Here,
\rho(t)
denotes the many-body density matrix, and
H=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\outerproduct{\psi_k}{\psi_k}
represents the system Hamiltonian with its spectral decomposition. The jump operators
K_{a}
are constructed from a set of coupling operators
A_{a}
using a filter function
f(s)

as follows:

\displaystyle K_{a}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s)e^{\mathrm{i}Hs}A_{a}e^{-\mathrm{i}Hs}{\mathrm{d}}s

(2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l}\widehat{f}(\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{l})\braket{\psi_{k}|A_{a}|\psi_{l}}\ket{\psi_{k}}\bra{\psi_{l}}.

In this expression,
\widehat{f}(\omega)
is the Fourier transform of the time-domain filter function
f(s)
, following the convention in
Chen et al. 2025a
;
Ding et al. 2024
:

\widehat{f}(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s)e^{\mathrm{i}\omega s}{\mathrm{d}}s.

(3)

The choice of filter function determines which transitions between energy eigenstates are allowed, depending on the values of the frequency-domain filter
\widehat{f}(\omega)
at the energy differences (Bohr frequencies)
\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{l}
. For ground-state preparation, we would like to allow such a transition from
\ket{\psi_{l}}
to
\ket{\psi_{k}}
only when it lowers the energy, namely when
\lambda_{k}<\lambda_{l}
. This can be ensured by requiring
\widehat{f}(\omega)=0
for all
\omega\geq 0
. We also need the first excited state to transition to the ground state, so we require
\widehat{f}(-\Delta_{H})>c
for some constant
c>0
, where
\Delta_{H}
is the energy gap between the ground state and the first excited state. To distinguish this from the protocol used in the rest of the manuscript, we refer to this setting as the
ideal
filter, see
Fig.

2(a)
.

For the ground-state simulation task, the total Lindblad simulation time is upper bounded by the mixing time, which is the time required for the system to evolve sufficiently close to its steady state regardless of the initial state. In other words, it measures how long the system takes to “forget” its starting point and settle into thermal or ground-state equilibrium. The mixing time is usually highly system dependent. Under the ideal filter, rigorous upper bounds on the mixing time of the dissipative cooling algorithm have been established for several classes of non-commuting Hamiltonians, including systems satisfying certain forms of the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH)
Ding et al. 2024
, free Majorana fermions, and weakly interacting spin and fermionic systems
Zhan et al. 2026
. Numerical evidence indicates that such protocols can be much more powerful in practice, and can prepare ground states of spin systems far beyond the weakly interacting regime, as well as ground, excited, and transition states in quantum chemistry settings
Zhan et al. 2026
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Li and Lin 2026
;
Watts et al. 2026
.

The end-to-end resource cost of dissipative cooling depends on several factors, including the choice of parameters in the filter function, the set of coupling operators
A_{a}
, the algorithm used to simulate the Lindblad dynamics, and the mixing time. The goal of this paper is not to provide concrete resource estimates, which will be left for future work. Instead, we focus on
design principles
for efficient dissipative cooling algorithms and their utility in solving phase-decision problems.

According to
Eq.

2
,
implementing the jump operators requires Heisenberg evolution of the coupling operators
A_{a}
under the system Hamiltonian, which uses Hamiltonian simulation as a subroutine. As a rule of thumb, the cost is therefore usually dominated by the total Hamiltonian simulation time.
In the ideal filter setting, constructing
K_{a}
requires truncating the integral in
Eq.

2
at a time
s_{\max}
that is inversely proportional to the energy gap
\Delta_{H}
of the Hamiltonian
Ding et al. 2024
;
Zhan et al. 2026
. This can become large near phase boundaries, where the gap closes, forcing a large
s_{\max}
and hence deep quantum circuits. On early fault-tolerant quantum devices, it is therefore desirable to consider a more realistic setup that resolves energies only up to some
\Delta=\Omega(1)
, with a corresponding family of filters satisfying

\widehat{f}(\omega)=\begin{cases}=1,&\omega<-\Delta,\\
\in[0,1],&\omega\in[-\Delta,\Delta],\\
=0,&\omega>\Delta,\end{cases}~\Delta=\mathcal{O}(1/s_{\max}).

(4)

Within the frequency window
[-\Delta,\Delta]
, the function
\widehat{f}(\omega)
is monotonically non-increasing.
A detailed construction of these
realistic filters
(
Fig.

2(a)
) with finite energy resolution can be found in Appendix
A
.
When
\Delta_{H}<\Delta
, if
\widehat{f}(\omega_{0})=0
for some Bohr frequency
\omega_{0}\in(-\Delta,0)
, the transitions between the corresponding states become effectively dark. Conversely, when
\widehat{f}(\omega_{0})>0
for some Bohr frequency
\omega_{0}\in(0,\Delta)
, the “heating leakage”
would allow these energy-raising transitions, which will compete with cooling processes.

Using realistic filters shortens the Hamiltonian simulation time needed to implement the jump operators, see
Fig.

2(b)
. Because of leakage, however, it cannot prepare the ground state exactly, even in the infinite-time limit of the Lindblad evolution. The first question is whether it can still drive the system efficiently into a low-energy manifold. The answer is yes, and we postpone the theoretical analysis of this question to
Section

IV
. We next show that, when realistic filters drive the system into a low-energy manifold, the Lindblad simulation time needed to estimate low-energy local observables can be much shorter than the mixing time to solve phase-decision problems. Since the total Hamiltonian simulation cost is roughly the product of the time needed to implement the jump operators and the Lindblad simulation time, our protocol can use considerably fewer resources than faithful state preparation with ideal filters.

III
Probing Quantum Phases with Dissipative Dynamics

III.1
Overview and problem setup

In this section, we give concrete examples of dissipative protocols for deciding between competing ground-state phases. Before turning to these examples, we formulate the phase-decision problem studied in this work.

Consider a family of Hamiltonians
H(\kappa)
parameterized by
\kappa\in\mathcal{I}\subset\mathbb{R}
. For the parameter region of interest, assume that one has already identified a finite set of candidate phases
\{\mathscr{P}_{i}\}
. We then choose a phase-sensitive diagnostic
\mathscr{O}:\mathscr{D}\to\varOmega
, where
\mathscr{D}
is the set of quantum states and
\varOmega
is the diagnostic space. In practice,
\mathscr{O}
is obtained by measuring suitable observables and post-processing the resulting data, and need not be an order parameter in the Landau sense. For instance, in the examples below,
\mathscr{O}
may be a fitted Luttinger parameter, or a topological invariant such as a Chern number. We assume that
\varOmega
contains disjoint regions
\{\varOmega_{i}\}
that can be used to label candidate phases
\{\mathscr{P}_{i}\}
.

For a target parameter
\kappa
, the phase-decision task is to determine which candidate phase contains the ground state of
H(\kappa)
. Given a representative initial state
\rho_{0}
and dissipative dynamics tailored to
H(\kappa)
, let
\rho_{\kappa}(t)
denote the evolved state. We say that the dissipative protocol succeeds at
\kappa
if there exists a time
t_{0}
such that for all
t\geq t_{0}
,

\mathscr{O}[\rho_{\kappa}(t)],\mathscr{O}[\rho_{\kappa,\mathrm{GS}}]\in\varOmega_{i},

(5)

where
\rho_{\kappa,\mathrm{GS}}
is the ground state of
H(\kappa)
. In other words, the transient dissipative state already enters the same diagnostic region as the ground state, so the ground-state phase at this
\kappa
can be identified without waiting for full convergence to the steady state. If the steady state of the Lindblad dynamics with ideal filters coincides with the ground state, then necessarily
t_{0}\leq t_{\mathrm{mix}}
, where
t_{\mathrm{mix}}
denotes the mixing time. The main question in this section is whether one can have
t_{0}\ll t_{\mathrm{mix}}
, even with realistic filters.

We examine three test cases. The first is the frustrated
J_{1}
–
J_{2}
chain
Majumdar and Ghosh 1969a
;
Majumdar and Ghosh 1969b
in
Section

III.2
, which exhibits BKT-type transitions with extended critical phases
Haldane 1982
.
We show that even with realistic filters, short-time dissipative dynamics can still resolve these phases.
In particular, we probe the phase diagram through spin-correlation functions and extract the Luttinger parameter
\mathcal{K}

Giamarchi 2003
;
Song et al. 2010
;
Rachel et al. 2012
to carry out the phase decision. We also benchmark the protocol against quench dynamics in Appendix
D.1
.

The second example is the two-dimensional Kitaev honeycomb model
Kitaev 2006
, an exactly solvable system exhibiting a topologically ordered phase.
Dissipation has long been regarded as a powerful resource for preparing topological states
Diehl et al. 2011
;
Bardyn et al. 2012
;
Bardyn et al. 2013
;
Budich et al. 2015
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Pokart et al. 2026
, and in particular for preparing the Chern insulators
Goldstein 2019
.
In
Section

III.3
, we demonstrate that by applying dissipative dynamics to the original gapless Hamiltonian without opening the spectral gap, one can extract a mixed-state Chern diagnostic with the expected finite-size phase label
using finite-resolution filters and short evolution time. This approach leverages topological diagnostics generalized to mixed states
Bardyn et al. 2013
;
Budich et al. 2015
;
Budich and Diehl 2015
;
Evered et al. 2025
.

Finally, in
Section

III.4
we consider the anisotropic XXZ spin chain
Franchini 2017
, which also exhibits a BKT-type phase transition. Its nearest-neighbor structure allows efficient tensor network simulations of the Lindblad dynamics. For a relatively large system (
L=40
), we find from tensor network simulations that a coarse filter resolution and a short evolution time suffice to recover the qualitatively correct correlation-function behavior associated with the respective phases. Adiabatic evolution
Farhi et al. 2000
;
Albash and Lidar 2018
provides an alternative route to preparing ground states or low-energy states, but it is often bottlenecked by the closing of the spectral gap at critical points. This is especially restrictive for BKT transitions, where the gap opens exponentially slowly as the coupling deviates from the critical value.
We benchmark our dissipative protocol against adiabatic evolution for the XXZ spin chain.
Within our maximum simulated evolution time, we find that the adiabatic dynamics fails to resolve the power-law decay of the correlation functions characteristic of the gapless phase.

III.2
Frustrated
J_{1}
–
J_{2}
Heisenberg chain

We first consider the antiferromagnetic
J_{1}
–
J_{2}
Heisenberg chain. This model incorporates frustrated next-nearest-neighbor interactions and is described by the Hamiltonian:

H=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left(J_{1}\mathbf{S}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{i+1}+J_{2}\mathbf{S}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{i+2}\right),\quad J_{2}/J_{1}\geq 0.

(6)

Here
\mathbf{S}_{i}=(S_{i}^{x},S_{i}^{y},S_{i}^{z})
are spin-
\frac{1}{2}
operators at site
i
. We adopt the periodic boundary condition (PBC) and the subscript
i
is defined modulo
L
.
When the frustration ratio
\kappa:=J_{2}/J_{1}\leq\kappa_{\rm crit}\approx 0.241

Okamoto and Nomura 1992
;
Giamarchi 2003
, the system is in the critical
Tomonaga–Luttinger liquid
(TLL) phase, while for
\kappa>\kappa_{\rm crit}
the system undergoes a BKT phase transition into a gapped dimerized phase called
valence bond solid
(VBS), see
Fig.

3(a)
.
For a given
\kappa
, we would like to decide whether the system is in the TLL or VBS phase based on measured observables. In principle, one could distinguish these phases by examining the decay properties of the spin correlation function
\left(\langle S_{i}^{z}S_{j}^{z}\rangle-\langle S_{i}^{z}\rangle\langle S_{j}^{z}\rangle\right)
, which decays algebraically in the TLL phase and exponentially in the gapped VBS phase. Near a BKT transition, however, the correlation length on the VBS side becomes exponentially large in
(\kappa-\kappa_{\rm crit})^{-1/2}
, and higher corrections on the TLL side can complicate finite-size scaling analysis
Bursill et al. 1995
.

This issue can be overcome by leveraging the fact that, within the TLL regime, the effective low-energy theory is described by a free bosonic conformal field theory (CFT) with a continuously varying Luttinger parameter
\mathcal{K}

Giamarchi 2003
;
Song et al. 2010
;
Rachel et al. 2012
. This parameter dictates the scaling behavior of the entanglement entropy even in interacting systems
Song et al. 2012
, and can be extracted numerically from bipartite
S^{z}
fluctuations, which are defined as the variance of the total
S^{z}
in a subsystem
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
of size
\ell
:

\mathcal{F}(\ell)=\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}}\left(\langle S_{i}^{z}S_{j}^{z}\rangle-\langle S_{i}^{z}\rangle\langle S_{j}^{z}\rangle\right),\quad\mathcal{A}_{\ell}=\{1,2,\dots,\ell\}.

(7)

Within the TLL phase,
\mathcal{K}
appears as the coefficient of the logarithmic term in
\ell
(see Appendix
C
for the detailed fitting procedure). By analyzing
\mathcal{F}(\ell)
, the collective behavior of the correlation functions is effectively amplified, allowing the phase to be identified more reliably near the critical point. The Luttinger parameter
\mathcal{K}>1/2
in the TLL phase, and
\mathcal{K}=1/2
at the BKT transition. In the gapped VBS phase,
\mathcal{F}(\ell)
saturates rather than growing logarithmically in the thermodynamic limit, so fitting the TLL form there should be viewed only as an effective finite-size diagnostic (and should satisfy
\mathcal{K}<1/2
) rather than interpreted literally as a Luttinger parameter.

We set the coupling operators as the valence bond operators
\{\mathbf{S}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{i+1}\}_{i=1}^{L}
and consider filter energy resolutions of
\Delta=1
and
\Delta=0.5
. We perform exact diagonalization simulations of Lindbladian dynamics with system size
L=14
. The initial state is chosen to be the ground state of the Majumdar–Ghosh point
J_{2}/J_{1}=1/2
in the VBS phase, which is one of the exact dimer-product ground states,
\ket{\psi_{\rm MG}}=\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}-\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}\right)\right]^{\otimes L/2}
.
As illustrated in
Fig.

3(b)
, we find that the combination of a relatively
coarse
filter resolution and a
short
dissipative evolution is sufficient to accurately distinguish the different phases, even within the critical region.
On the TLL side, the extracted Luttinger parameter consistently satisfies
\mathcal{K}>0.5
as expected. For target couplings in the VBS phase, the same fit yields an effective logarithmic coefficient below
0.5
. While the quality of the fit is high within the TLL regime, it degrades in the VBS phase due to the fact that the logarithmic scaling form is a property only of the critical TLL phase. In the thermodynamic limit, the bipartite fluctuations
{\mathcal{F}}(\ell)
shift from logarithmic divergence in the TLL phase to a constant saturation in the gapped VBS phase.

We also benchmark our protocol against quench dynamics in Appendix
D.1
. We find that the quench approach fails to detect signatures of the quantum phase transition within the simulated time scales, highlighting the comparative advantage of our dissipative method near a BKT transition.

III.3
2D Kitaev honeycomb model

The dissipative protocol naturally extends to two or higher dimensional systems. As an illustrative example, let us consider the Kitaev honeycomb model

\frac{1}{2}
Hamiltonian with anisotropic exchange on a honeycomb lattice:

H=-4\sum_{\alpha\in\{x,y,z\}}J_{\alpha}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle_{\alpha}}S_{i}^{\alpha}S_{j}^{\alpha},

(8)

where
\langle i,j\rangle_{\alpha}
denotes the nearest neighbor sites connected by an
\alpha
-type bond.
\alpha
can be
x
,
y
, or
z
depending on the direction of the bond, see
Fig.

. The model exhibits a rich phase diagram featuring Abelian and non-Abelian spin liquid phases depending on the relative strengths of the coupling constants
J_{x}
,
J_{y}
, and
J_{z}

Kitaev 2006
, as shown in
Fig.

4(b)
. In recent years, theoretical and experimental studies of this model have focused extensively on topological state preparation on quantum processors
Evered et al. 2025
;
Will et al. 2025
, as well as topological signatures in quench dynamics
Wang et al. 2017
;
Tarnowski et al. 2019
.

The Kitaev honeycomb model can be mapped to Majorana fermions coupled to a static
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge field. When restricted to the vortex-free sector and a fixed gauge, the Hamiltonian reduces to a quadratic Majorana fermion model

H=\mathrm{i}\sum_{\mathbf{s},\mu,\mathbf{t},\nu}J_{\alpha(\mathbf{s},\mu;\mathbf{t},\nu)}\eta_{\mathbf{s}\mu}\eta_{\mathbf{t}\nu}

(9)

where
\eta_{\mathbf{s}\mu}
are Majorana fermions satisfying the anticommutation relations
\{\eta_{\mathbf{s}\mu},\eta_{\mathbf{t}\nu}\}=\delta_{(\mathbf{s},\mu),(\mathbf{t},\nu)}
, and
\mu=A,B
are the two sites contained in the unit cell of the honeycomb lattice, as illustrated in
Fig.

4(a)
.
Applying a Fourier transform followed by a Bogoliubov transformation diagonalizes the system into a free-fermion model in momentum space, in which the independent quasiparticle degrees of freedom can be represented by the
B
-sublattice fermionic operators

H=\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)}(1-2c_{\mathbf{q}B}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{q}B}),

(10)

with the dispersion relation given by

g(\mathbf{q}):=J_{x}\cos q_{x}+J_{y}\cos q_{y}+J_{z}+\mathrm{i}(J_{x}\sin q_{x}+J_{y}\sin q_{y}),

(11)

and
c_{\mathbf{q}B}
,
c_{\mathbf{q}B}^{\dagger}
represent fermionic annihilation and creation operators of the Bogoliubov quasiparticles. For simplicity, we take the side length
L
of the supercell to be odd and
q_{x},q_{y}=\frac{2\pi n}{L}
with
n=-\frac{L-1}{2},\cdots,\frac{L-1}{2}
. The ground state is given by

\ket{\psi_{\text{GS}}}=\prod_{\mathbf{q}}c_{\mathbf{q}B}^{\dagger}\ket{0}.

(12)

Here
\ket{0}
is the vacuum state of
c_{\mathbf{q}B}
operators. The energy gap is given by
\min_{\mathbf{q}}2\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)}
. We refer to Appendix
E
for the details of the model and its diagonalization.

Here we are interested in the line
J_{x}=J_{y}=({1-J_{z}})/{2}
in the parameter space
(the dashed line in the phase diagram
Fig.

4(b)
).
At the critical point
J_{z}=0.5
, the model changes from the gapped Abelian phase for
J_{z}>0.5
to the gapless phase for
J_{z}<0.5
with time-reversal symmetry preserved.
Along this line, the gapped phase has the trivial parent-Hamiltonian Chern number
\mathcal{C}=0
. For
J_{z}<0.5
, the thermodynamic system is gapless; after adding a small time-reversal-breaking perturbation that opens a gap, one obtains a chiral non-Abelian spin liquid with
\mathcal{C}=\pm 1

Kitaev 2006
. In the present work we do not explicitly add such a perturbation. Instead, we use the Chern number of the mixed-state parent Hamiltonian constructed from the covariance matrix as a finite-size diagnostic that distinguishes the two regimes in the dissipative dynamics.

Readers may wonder whether our results contradict the no-go theorem for rapid preparation of topologically ordered states by quasi-local dissipative dynamics
König and Pastawski 2014
. In fact,
different operational notions of topological order lead to different preparation obstructions, for example those based on finite-depth local unitary circuits
Chen et al. 2011
, finite-depth circuits with measurements and feedforward
Tantivasadakarn et al. 2023
, or fast dissipative evolution
Coser and Pérez-García 2019

König and Pastawski 2014
; see Appendix
F.2

Kitaev 2006

Eq.

8

\Gamma

L
scales as
\mathcal{O}(1/L)
rather than staying open by a constant. Even so, as long as the finite-size parent Hamiltonian has an open purity gap at the evaluation time, the parent-Hamiltonian construction together with the FHS algorithm yields a well-defined Chern diagnostic, and we will show below that the protocol recovers the expected phase label even with a realistic filter of constant width.

Our last example is the XXZ Hamiltonian

H=\sum_{i}\left[J_{xy}\left(S_{i}^{x}S_{i+1}^{x}+S_{i}^{y}S_{i+1}^{y}\right)+J_{z}S_{i}^{z}S_{i+1}^{z}\right],

where

. The ground state of the XXZ model in one dimension is solvable via the Bethe ansatz
Yang and Yang 1966
and possesses a rich phase diagram
Langari 1998
;
Kitanine et al. 2002
;
Rakov et al. 2016
. In this study, we focus on the transition between two distinct regimes. When
J_{z}/J_{xy}>1
, the system resides in a gapped antiferromagnetic Néel phase. In the Ising limit
J_{xy}\to 0
, one of the two degenerate ground states reduces to the product state
|\psi_{\text{ini}}\rangle=\ket{\uparrow\downarrow\cdots\uparrow\downarrow}
. It is easy to prepare experimentally and serves as our initial state. At
J_{z}/J_{xy}=1
, the system undergoes a BKT liquid–solid phase transition.

, the system lies in the critical, gapless XX phase, which is a gapless Luttinger liquid; see
Fig.

6
.

We choose the
J_{z}=0

Section

III.2
and
Section

III.3
, respectively.
Although the fermionic operators are nonlocal in the spin representation because of the Jordan–Wigner strings, they allow for exact analytical treatment, whose performance will later be rigorously proved in
Section

IV.2
.
The spin coupling operators are strictly local in the spin basis, making them suitable for implementation without non-local operations.

The low-energy physics of the XX phase is also effectively captured by the TLL
theory. Within this regime, the Luttinger parameter
{\mathcal{K}}
varies continuously as a function of the ratio
J_{z}/J_{xy}

\mathcal{K}=\frac{1}{2}\left[1-\frac{\arccos\left(J_{z}/J_{xy}\right)}{\pi}\right]^{-1}.

(15)

Again, the Luttinger parameter can be fitted from the subsystem bipartite
S^{z}

\mathcal{F}(\ell)

Song et al. 2012
;
Song et al. 2010
;
Rachel et al. 2012
, the treatment of the subleading terms becomes considerably more subtle under OBCs in this regime. We therefore focus here on cases deep inside the phases, where the phase structure can be identified more directly from the scaling behavior of the correlation functions in the tensor-network based simulations where OBCs are employed.

We first analyze the performance of dissipative dynamics for both choices of coupling operators, and then benchmark the performance against adiabatic evolution.

III.4.3
Benchmark against adiabatic evolution

We benchmark the performance of the dissipative cooling protocol using spin coupling operators against adiabatic evolution. To simulate a scenario where the system properties are not known a priori, we employ a simple linear interpolation between the initial Ising Hamiltonian and the target XX Hamiltonian

represents the time variable across a total evolution time

Since this simple adiabatic path crosses a quantum critical point and ends in a gapless phase, one should not expect it to reproduce the target correlations on the short timescales accessible here. As shown in
Fig.

IV
Low-energy state preparation with realistic filters

The success of phase probing via dissipative dynamics with realistic filters depends on whether the dynamics prepares low-energy states. In this section, we analyze this question using a drift inequality and three representative regimes. The drift inequality is a Lyapunov-type operator bound that controls the steady-state population at high energies. We first apply this method to free fermions, where the mode decomposition yields a sharp cutoff at the filter resolution and exponential convergence into the corresponding low-energy subspace. We then study free bosons, where the ladder structure of each mode prevents such a strict cutoff and instead yields an exponentially decaying high-energy tail. Proofs are given in Appendix
B
, and additional numerical results appear in Appendix
D.3
. Finally, we discuss interacting systems, where local couplings no longer preserve independent mode occupations and numerical results indicate a population profile resembling the bosonic leakage mechanism.

IV.1
Drift inequality

We use a drift-inequality framework to control low-energy state preparation and convergence for dissipative dynamics with realistic filters. The key step is to construct a positive semidefinite drift operator
\mathsf{G}=\sum_{k}\mathfrak{g}_{k}\outerproduct{\psi_k}{\psi_k}
that is diagonal in the energy eigenbasis
\{\ket{\psi_{k}}\}
of the Hamiltonian
H
, and to establish an operator inequality of the form

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\mathsf{G}]\leq-a\mathsf{G}+bI,

(20)

for constants
a>0
and
b\geq 0
. This inequality gives a bound for
\Tr[\mathsf{G}\rho(t)]
, and the operator Markov’s inequality then bounds the steady-state population above an energy threshold
E
by

\Tr[\mathcal{P}_{>E}\rho_{\rm ss}]\leq\frac{b}{a\mathfrak{g}_{E}},\quad\text{if}\quad\mathfrak{g}_{E}=\inf_{k:\lambda_{k}>E}\mathfrak{g}_{k}>0.

(21)

Here,
\mathcal{P}_{>E}:=\mathbf{1}_{(E,\infty)}(H)
denotes the spectral projector onto the subspace of states with energy above
E
, and
\lambda_{k}
is the energy of the eigenstate
\ket{\psi_{k}}
.
The choice of
\mathsf{G}
is system-dependent.
For free fermionic systems, we use the canonical number operator
N_{\Delta}
that counts quasiparticle modes with excitation energies above the filter resolution
\Delta
. For single-mode free bosonic systems, we instead use an exponential moment of the Hamiltonian,
e^{\beta H}
, with a parameter
\beta>0
chosen below the stability threshold. Lemma
4
in Appendix
B
gives the finite-dimensional version of the argument, while the bosonic tail bound uses the corresponding unbounded drift estimate proved in Appendix
B.2
.

IV.2
Free fermions

We first consider a generic quadratic fermionic Hamiltonian

H=\sum_{i,j=1}^{L}F_{ij}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}

(22)

where
F
is a Hermitian coefficient matrix. We choose the coupling operators to be

\{c_{i}^{\dagger},c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}.

(23)

We assume that the coefficient matrix can be unitarily diagonalized as
F=U\text{diag}(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{L})U^{\dagger}
.
The Hamiltonian can then
be diagonalized as

H=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\varepsilon_{k}\widetilde{c}^{\dagger}_{k}\widetilde{c}_{k},\quad\widetilde{c}_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{L}U_{jk}c_{j},

(24)

and the ground state energy is given by
E_{0}=\sum_{\varepsilon_{k}<0}\varepsilon_{k}
.

Theorem 1

(Low-energy state preparation in free-fermion dissipative dynamics, informal)
.

Consider the free-fermion Hamiltonian
Eq.

22
and coupling operators

in the high-energy subspace above

The proof of

is deferred to Appendix
B.1
. Since

decays exponentially, we have
\Tr(\mc P_{>E_\Delta} \rho_{\rm ss})=0
in the infinite-time limit. Thus the steady state is strictly supported on the subspace with energy at most
E_{\Delta}
. The derivation uses the fact that the chosen coupling operators decouple the dynamics of the canonical fermionic modes
\widetilde{c}_{k}
. In this representation, the occupation of each canonical mode,
\langle{n}_{k}\rangle=\langle\widetilde{c}_{k}^{\dagger}\widetilde{c}_{k}\rangle
, converges exponentially toward the steady-state value

\braket{{n}_{k}}_{\text{ss}}=\frac{\widehat{f}(\varepsilon_{k})^{2}}{\widehat{f}(-\varepsilon_{k})^{2}+\widehat{f}(\varepsilon_{k})^{2}},

at a relaxation rate given by
\widehat{f}(-\varepsilon_{k})^{2}+\widehat{f}(\varepsilon_{k})^{2}

\Delta

For a gapped free-fermion system whose smallest quasiparticle excitation energy is
\Delta_{H}
, any initial state rapidly relaxes toward the ground state provided that the filter resolution satisfies
\Delta<\Delta_{H}
. In contrast, for a gapless system, a fixed resolution

IV.3
Free bosons

The reduction to independent two-level occupation dynamics is a special property of free fermions and does not hold in general. To investigate a more complex and representative scenario, we consider bosonic systems, where each bosonic mode contains an infinite ladder of coupled energy levels. Specifically, let us consider the following
L
-mode free bosonic Hamiltonian:

H=\sum_{i,j=1}^{L}h_{ij}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j},

(28)

where
h
is a real-symmetric positive definite matrix whose eigenvalues
\varepsilon_{i}>0
are the canonical mode frequencies. We choose the coupling operators to be

A_{i}=a_{i}+a_{i}^{\dagger},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,L.

(29)

In order to discuss the effect of realistic filters,
we assume that every relevant mode frequency satisfies
\varepsilon_{i}<\Delta
and that the filter has stronger cooling than heating at these frequencies, namely
0\leq\widehat{f}(\varepsilon_{i})<\widehat{f}(-\varepsilon_{i})\leq 1
.
This simple model can describe, for example, optical cavity modes coupled to external modes with both cooling and heating drives.
Since the cooling is stronger than heating, the corresponding Gaussian steady state is stable and takes the form of a squeezed thermal state when the coherent Hamiltonian contribution is included
Grynberg et al. 2010
;
Marian and Marian 1993
. Here we use the drift inequality to control the low-energy character of the steady state. For simplicity, we first analyze a single-mode bosonic system with Hamiltonian
H=\Omega a^{\dagger}a
with
\Omega>0
.

Theorem 2

(Low-energy state preparation in free-boson dissipative dynamics, informal)
.

Consider the single-mode free bosonic Hamiltonian and coupling operator
A=a+a^{\dagger}
, and assume
0\leq\widehat{f}(\Omega)<\widehat{f}(-\Omega)

0<\beta<\frac{1}{\Omega}\log\frac{\widehat{f}(-\Omega)}{\widehat{f}(\Omega)}

C_{\beta}>0
such that for any
E>0
, the tail population of the steady state above
E
can be bounded as

\Tr[\mathcal{P}_{>E}\rho_{\rm ss}]\leq C_{\beta}e^{-\beta E}.

(30)

The proof of the above theorem is deferred to

in Appendix

In contrast to the free-fermion case, the free-boson steady state does not exhibit a sharp energy cutoff; instead, the total population above some energy threshold
E
decays exponentially in
E
. The reason is that the jump operator
K=\widehat{f}(-\Omega)a+\widehat{f}(\Omega)a^{\dagger}
couples adjacent Fock levels of the same bosonic mode, so filter leakage propagates through the full Fock ladder rather than remaining confined to a finite set of two-level occupations.

We also obtain trace-distance convergence bounds to the steady state.
In the purely dissipative case, i.e.
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{D}

squeezed vacuum state

\mathcal{L}_{H}

Proposition 3

Consider the free-boson Hamiltonian in

IV.4
Interacting systems

So far, our analysis has focused on free systems. For interacting models, local coupling operators generally do not preserve the mode-by-mode decomposition that made the free-fermion dynamics exactly solvable. Instead, the same local observable can connect many-body eigenstates that differ in several single-particle occupations.
Consequently, the steady-state behavior under dissipative dynamics generated by realistic filters is expected to show leakage across the many-body spectrum. The resulting population profile can resemble the free-boson case, with a decaying tail, as schematically illustrated in
Fig.

11
.

In the drift inequality framework, we would like to choose
E_{0}
, and find
\beta>0
,
a_{\beta}>0
, and
b_{\beta}<\infty
such that

\mathsf{G}_{\beta}:=e^{\beta(H-E_{0})},\qquad\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\mathsf{G}_{\beta}]\leq-a_{\beta}\mathsf{G}_{\beta}+b_{\beta}I.

(32)

By the drift argument above, such an estimate would imply an exponential steady-state tail,

\Tr[\mathcal{P}_{>E}\rho_{\rm ss}]\leq C_{\beta}e^{-\beta(E-E_{0})}

(33)

for energies
E>E_{0}
and a constant
C_{\beta}
depending on the drift constants. Intuitively, the downward transitions should still dominate upward leakage outside the filter-resolution window. But proving
Eq.

32
requires quantitative control of the filtered transition rates in the many-body energy basis and we leave this problem for future work. Below, we provide numerical evidence for this exponential-tail picture.

Let us take a simple illustrative Hamiltonian
H=H_{0}+J_{z}V
with

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{L-1}\left(c_{i+1}^{\dagger}c_{i}+c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i+1}\right),

(34)

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{L-1}\left(n_{i+1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(n_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\right).

Here
n_{i}=c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{i}
. This is the spinless-fermion form of the open-boundary XXZ chain, up to the standard Jordan–Wigner transformation and an additive constant.
In

\langle\widetilde{n}_{i}\rangle

H_{0}
. We set the interaction strength
J_{z}=0.5
, while maintaining a filter resolution of
\Delta=0.5
, which remains larger than the free fermion spectral gap. The data show that the noninteracting canonical occupations are no longer sharply cut off at
|\varepsilon_{i}|=\Delta

These numerics are outside the direct scope of existing perturbative analyses for Lindblad-based thermal-state and ground-state preparation in weakly interacting fermionic systems, for

V
Discussion

We have shown that short-time dissipative cooling can probe ground-state quantum phases without full ground state preparation. The main cost can be measured by the total Hamiltonian simulation time, which is determined by the filter resolution used to construct the jump operators and by the dissipative evolution time. In our examples, finite-resolution filters already produce informative phase-sensitive diagnostics well before global mixing, including in BKT-type and covariance-matrix based topological phase decisions. Combined with recent progress on dissipative thermalization and ground-state preparation
Lloyd and Abanin 2026
;
Langbehn et al. 2026
;
Hahn et al. 2026
;
Zhan et al. 2026
;
Wang and Ding 2026
;
Slezak et al. 2026
;
Chen et al. 2026
, we argue that dissipative phase identification is a plausible target for early fault-tolerant quantum devices.

Metastability arises in many settings, including long-range interacting and periodically driven (Floquet) quantum many-body systems
Yin et al. 2025
;
Defenu 2021
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Collura et al. 2022
, and it has also received increasing attention in open quantum many-body systems
Macieszczak et al. 2016
;
Landa et al. 2020
;
Macieszczak et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2022
;
Schütz et al. 2016
;
Rakovszky et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Bergamaschi et al. 2025
. In such regimes, short-time dissipative dynamics may still carry phase information, but long-lived nonequilibrium sectors or local energy minima can obstruct reliable phase identification on practical timescales. Extending the method to these settings may require quantum analogues of enhanced-sampling methods, designed to move between metastable sectors, such as parallel tempering or replica exchange
Bello-Rivas and Elber 2015
;
Layden et al. 2023
;
Leng et al. 2026
;
Chen et al. 2025c
.

The examples studied in this work are benchmark applications and are classically accessible at the simulated sizes and in the exactly solvable or quasi-one-dimensional regimes considered here. A natural next step is to push toward more challenging regimes, such as frustrated two-dimensional
J_{1}
–
J_{2}
models or perturbed Kitaev honeycomb models, and to move from deep within a phase toward phase boundaries.
It remains an open question whether classical methods for these harder regimes retain polynomial scaling in the relevant length scales, or develop prohibitive dependence on the correlation length as the phase boundary is approached. For such applications, we do not expect that a quantum advantage can be established from
a priori
complexity-theoretic arguments. Instead, quantum advantages will likely be empirical and depend on the physical details of the model and the phase transition. A practical strategy is to begin with benchmark cases, then move toward phase boundaries and identify where such approaches can offer a genuine computational advantage.

where

•

•

The parameters

.

•

and

. More precisely, the second error-function factor changes from approximately

.

•

.

Appendix B
Theoretical results for dissipative dynamics with realistic filters

In this appendix, we present theoretical results on dissipative dynamics with realistic filters for free fermionic and bosonic systems. Our proof relies on a drift inequality that bounds high-energy populations and the convergence rate of the system, as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Let
H
be a finite-dimensional Hamiltonian with spectral decomposition
H=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\outerproduct{\psi_k}{\psi_k}
.
Let
\mathsf{G}:=\sum_{k}\mathfrak{g}_{k}\outerproduct{\psi_k}{\psi_k}
be diagonal in the Hamiltonian eigenbasis with non-negative eigenvalues
\mathfrak{g}_{k}\geq 0
for all
k
.
Assume that there exist constants
a>0
and
b\geq 0
such that
\mathsf{G}
satisfies the drift inequality

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\mathsf{G}]\leq-a\mathsf{G}+bI.

(39)

For any energy threshold
E
, define the projector onto the high-energy subspace as
\mathcal{P}_{>E}:=\mathbf{1}_{(E,\infty)}(H)=\sum_{k:\lambda_{k}>E}\outerproduct{\psi_k}{\psi_k}
, and
\mathfrak{g}_{E}:=\inf_{k:\lambda_{k}>E}\mathfrak{g}_{k}
.
If
\mathfrak{g}_{E}>0
, then for any initial state
\rho(0)
, we have the following bound on the high-energy population at time
t
:

\Tr[\mathcal{P}_{>E}\rho(t)]\leq\frac{1}{\mathfrak{g}_{E}}\left(e^{-at}\Tr[\mathsf{G}\rho(0)]+(1-e^{-at})\frac{b}{a}\right).

(40)

In particular, for the stationary state
\rho_{\rm ss}
, we have by taking the limit
t\to\infty
from
Eq.

40
that

\Tr[\mathcal{P}_{>E}\rho_{\rm ss}]\leq\frac{b}{a\mathfrak{g}_{E}}.

(41)

Proof.

Set
\mathsf{m}(t):=\Tr(\mathsf G \rho(t)).
Since
\rho(t)
solves the Lindblad equation, the Hilbert–Schmidt adjoint gives
\derivative{\mathsf m(t)}{t}=\Tr(\mathcal{L}^\dagger[\mathsf G] \rho(t)).
Since
\rho(t)
is positive semidefinite,
Eq.

39
gives

\derivative{\mathsf m(t)}{t}\leq-a\mathsf{m}(t)+b.

(42)

By Grönwall’s inequality, we have

\mathsf{m}(t)\leq e^{-at}\mathsf{m}(0)+(1-e^{-at})\frac{b}{a}.

(43)

By the definition of
\mathfrak{g}_{E}
, we have
\mathfrak{g}_{E}\mathcal{P}_{>E}\leq\mathsf{G}
. This can be viewed as the operator form of Markov’s inequality, which converts the moment bound to a tail bound by taking the trace against
\rho(t)
:

\Tr[\mathcal{P}_{>E}\rho(t)]\leq\frac{1}{\mathfrak{g}_{E}}\Tr[\mathsf{G}\rho(t)]\leq\frac{1}{\mathfrak{g}_{E}}\left(e^{-at}\Tr[\mathsf{G}\rho(0)]+(1-e^{-at})\frac{b}{a}\right).

(44)

Taking the limit
t\to\infty
gives the bound for the steady state
Eq.

41
.
∎

Let us consider a general quadratic fermionic Hamiltonian

(45)

and bulk coupling operators
\{c_{i}^{\dagger},c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}

\widetilde{c}_{k}^{\dagger},&\text{if }\varepsilon_{k}<0,\end{cases}

b_{k}\ket{\psi_{0}}=0
for all
k
, and the ground state energy is
E_{0}=\sum\limits_{k:\varepsilon_{k}<0}\varepsilon_{k}
. Here we use
n_{k}
to denote the quasiparticle number operator on the
k
-th canonical mode, i.e.

n_{k}:=b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}=\begin{cases}\widetilde{c}_{k}^{\dagger}\widetilde{c}_{k},&\varepsilon_{k}>0,\\
1-\widetilde{c}_{k}^{\dagger}\widetilde{c}_{k},&\varepsilon_{k}<0.\end{cases}

(49)

Let us denote the dissipation generator
with respect to the jump operator
K
as

\mathcal{D}_{K}[\rho]:=K\rho K^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{K^{\dagger}K,\rho\right\}.

(50)

Proposition 5
.

For the free-fermionic system defined above, with the coupling operators set
\{c_{i}^{\dagger},c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
and a filter satisfying
Eq.

4
, the generator can be decomposed into a sum of independent generators acting on each canonical mode

\mathcal{L}[\rho]=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\mathcal{L}_{k}[\rho],\quad\mathcal{L}_{k}[\rho]=-\mathrm{i}\lambda_{k}[n_{k},\rho]+\gamma_{c,k}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{b_{k}}[\rho]+\gamma_{h,k}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{b_{k}^{\dagger}}[\rho],

(51)

where

\gamma_{c,k}:=\widehat{f}(-\lambda_{k}),\quad\gamma_{h,k}:=\widehat{f}(\lambda_{k})

(52)

represent the cooling and heating strengths for the
k
-th mode. The steady state of the dynamics has the following tensor product structure:

\rho_{\rm ss}=\bigotimes_{k:\lambda_{k}>\Delta}\outerproduct{0}{0}_{k}\bigotimes_{k:\lambda_{k}\leq\Delta}\left(p_{k}\outerproduct{0}{0}_{k}+(1-p_{k})\outerproduct{1}{1}_{k}\right),\quad p_{k}=\frac{\gamma_{c,k}^{2}}{\gamma_{c,k}^{2}+\gamma_{h,k}^{2}}.

(53)

Moreover, the total energy converges to a value below
E_{0}+\sum_{\lambda_{k}\leq\Delta}\lambda_{k}
, and
\braket{n_{k}}
converges to
0
for all modes with
\lambda_{k}>\Delta
, both at the rate
e^{-t}
.

Proof.

In this canonical basis, the coupling operators
\{c_{i}^{\dagger},c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
can be expressed as

c_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{L}U_{ik}\widetilde{c}_{k}=\sum_{\varepsilon_{k}>0}U_{ik}b_{k}+\sum_{\varepsilon_{k}<0}U_{ik}b_{k}^{\dagger},\quad c_{i}^{\dagger}=\sum_{k=1}^{L}U_{ik}^{*}\widetilde{c}_{k}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\varepsilon_{k}>0}U_{ik}^{*}b_{k}^{\dagger}+\sum_{\varepsilon_{k}<0}U_{ik}^{*}b_{k}.

(54)

According to the Thouless theorem, the time evolution of the fermionic operators is given by
Thouless 1960

e^{\mathrm{i}Hs}b_{k}e^{-\mathrm{i}Hs}=e^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda_{k}s}b_{k},\quad e^{\mathrm{i}Hs}b_{k}^{\dagger}e^{-\mathrm{i}Hs}=e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda_{k}s}b_{k}^{\dagger},

(55)

according to the construction in
Eq.

2
, the jump operators corresponding to the coupling operators are

\displaystyle K_{i,+}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(s)e^{\mathrm{i}Hs}c_{i}^{\dagger}e^{-\mathrm{i}Hs}\differential s=\sum_{\varepsilon_{k}>0}U_{ik}^{*}{\gamma_{h,k}}b_{k}^{\dagger}+\sum_{\varepsilon_{k}<0}U_{ik}^{*}{\gamma_{c,k}}b_{k};

(56)

\displaystyle K_{i,-}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(s)e^{\mathrm{i}Hs}c_{i}e^{-\mathrm{i}Hs}\differential s=\sum_{\varepsilon_{k}>0}U_{ik}{\gamma_{c,k}}b_{k}+\sum_{\varepsilon_{k}<0}U_{ik}{\gamma_{h,k}}b_{k}^{\dagger},

where we denote the cooling and heating strength by

\gamma_{c,k}:=\widehat{f}(-\lambda_{k}),\quad\gamma_{h,k}:=\widehat{f}(\lambda_{k}).

(57)

The coherent part is diagonal in the canonical basis, and the dissipative part simplifies by the orthonormality of the columns of
U
, which cancels all off-diagonal mode terms:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{H}[\rho]=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}[H,\rho]=-\mathrm{i}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\lambda_{k}[n_{k},\rho],

(58)

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\mathcal{D}_{K_{i,+}}[\rho]=

\displaystyle\sum_{\varepsilon_{k}<0}\gamma_{c,k}^{2}\left(b_{k}\rho b_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k},\rho\}\right)+\sum_{\varepsilon_{k}>0}\gamma_{h,k}^{2}\left(b_{k}^{\dagger}\rho b_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\{b_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger},\rho\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\varepsilon_{k}<0}\gamma_{c,k}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{b_{k}}[\rho]+\sum_{\varepsilon_{k}>0}\gamma_{h,k}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{b_{k}^{\dagger}}[\rho],

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\mathcal{D}_{K_{i,-}}[\rho]=

\displaystyle\sum_{\varepsilon_{k}<0}\gamma_{h,k}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{b_{k}^{\dagger}}[\rho]+\sum_{\varepsilon_{k}>0}\gamma_{c,k}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{b_{k}}[\rho].

Putting these together, the total Lindbladian can be expressed as

\mathcal{L}[\rho]=-\mathrm{i}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\lambda_{k}[n_{k},\rho]+\sum_{k=1}^{L}\gamma_{c,k}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{b_{k}}[\rho]+\sum_{k=1}^{L}\gamma_{h,k}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{b_{k}^{\dagger}}[\rho]=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\mathcal{L}_{k}{[\rho]},

(59)

where

\mathcal{L}_{k}[\rho]:=-\mathrm{i}\lambda_{k}[n_{k},\rho]+\gamma_{c,k}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{b_{k}}[\rho]+\gamma_{h,k}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{b_{k}^{\dagger}}[\rho].

(60)

Since each
\mathcal{L}_{k}
acts only on mode
k
, the dynamics of any observable
O_{k}
supported on mode
k
is closed in the Heisenberg picture:

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}O_{k}(t)}{{\mathrm{d}}t}=\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[O_{k}]=\mathcal{L}_{k}^{\dagger}[O_{k}]=\mathrm{i}\lambda_{k}[n_{k},O_{k}]+\gamma_{c,k}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{b_{k}}^{\dagger}[O_{k}]+\gamma_{h,k}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{b_{k}^{\dagger}}^{\dagger}[O_{k}],

(61)

where
\mathcal{L}_{k}^{\dagger}
is the Hilbert–Schmidt adjoint of the superoperator
\mathcal{L}_{k}
, and
\mathcal{D}_{K}^{\dagger}[O]
is given by
K^{{\dagger}}OK-\frac{1}{2}\left\{K^{\dagger}K,O\right\}
.
Choosing
O_{k}=n_{k}
, we obtain

\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}_{b_{k}}[n_{k}]=b_{k}^{\dagger}n_{k}b_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\{n_{k},n_{k}\}\overset{\text{$n_{k}^{2}=n_{k}$}}{=}-n_{k},\quad\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}_{b_{k}^{\dagger}}[n_{k}]=b_{k}n_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{b_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger},n_{k}\}=1-n_{k}.

(62)

Therefore

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[n_{k}]=\mathcal{L}_{k}^{{\dagger}}[n_{k}]=\begin{cases}-n_{k},&\lambda_{k}>\Delta;\\
-n_{k}\left(\gamma_{c,k}^{2}+\gamma_{h,k}^{2}\right)+\gamma_{h,k}^{2},&\lambda_{k}\leq\Delta.\\
\end{cases}

(63)

If
\gamma_{c,k}^{2}+\gamma_{h,k}^{2}>0
, the stationary population for modes with excitation energies below
\Delta
is

\braket{n_{k}}=\frac{\gamma_{h,k}^{2}}{\gamma_{c,k}^{2}+\gamma_{h,k}^{2}}.

(64)

For the same one-mode generator, each off-diagonal matrix element in the occupation basis decays at rate
(\gamma_{c,k}^{2}+\gamma_{h,k}^{2})/2
up to the Hamiltonian phase. Thus each
\mathcal{L}_{k}
has a unique one-mode stationary state under the non-dark-mode condition above. Since the one-mode semigroups commute, the full steady state is the tensor product of these one-mode stationary states:

\rho_{\rm ss}=\bigotimes_{k:\lambda_{k}>\Delta}\outerproduct{0}{0}_{k}\bigotimes_{k:\lambda_{k}\leq\Delta}\left(p_{k}\outerproduct{0}{0}_{k}+(1-p_{k})\outerproduct{1}{1}_{k}\right),\quad p_{k}=\frac{\gamma_{c,k}^{2}}{\gamma_{c,k}^{2}+\gamma_{h,k}^{2}}.

(65)

Finally, for modes with
\lambda_{k}>\Delta
, we have by
Eq.

63
that

\braket{n_{k}}_{t}=\Tr(n_k \rho(t))\leq e^{-t}\Tr(n_k \rho(0)),

(66)

which converges to
0
with an exponential rate
e^{-t}
. For the total energy

\Tr(H \rho(t))=E_{0}+\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\langle n_{k}\rangle_{t}\leq E_{0}+\sum_{k:\lambda_{k}\leq\Delta}\lambda_{k}+\sum_{k:\lambda_{k}>\Delta}\lambda_{k}\langle n_{k}\rangle_{0}e^{-t},

(67)

which will converge to a value not exceeding
E_{0}+\sum_{k:\lambda_{k}\leq\Delta}\lambda_{k}
with an exponential rate
e^{-t}
.

∎

With Proposition
5
, we
are now ready to prove
1
. Indeed,
Eq.

63
is a drift inequality for the number operator
n_{k}
on each canonical mode. Since the energy eigenstates in free fermionic systems are Fock states in the canonical basis, and since the eigenvalues are the occupation-weighted sums of the excitation energies, it is natural to construct the drift inequality for the resolved-mode
number operator
N_{\Delta}:=\sum_{k:\lambda_{k}>\Delta}n_{k}
that counts the total occupation on modes with excitation energies above
\Delta
.

Theorem 6

(Rigorous
version of
1
)
.

In the presence of finite-resolution imperfection
Eq.

4
in the filter, for any initial state
\rho
, its high-energy population
{\mathrm{Tr}}(\mathcal{P}_{>E_{\Delta}}\rho(t))
decays with an exponential rate
e^{-t}
, where
E_{\Delta}:=E_{0}+\sum_{\absolutevalue{\varepsilon_k}\leq\Delta}\absolutevalue{\varepsilon_k}=E_{0}+\sum_{k:\lambda_{k}\leq\Delta}\lambda_{k}
. In particular, every stationary state
\rho_{\rm ss}
satisfies
\Tr(\mc P_{>E_\Delta} \rho_{\rm ss})=0
.

Moreover, whenever the normalized projection
\rho_{\leq E_{\Delta}}(t)
is defined, the state converges to its projection onto the low-energy subspace in trace distance exponentially fast. Specifically,

\lVert\rho(t)-\rho_{\leq E_{\Delta}}(t)\rVert_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{L}e^{-t/2}.

Proof.

We first show the convergence of high-energy population.
By
Eq.

63
, we have

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[N_{\Delta}]=\sum_{k:\lambda_{k}>\Delta}\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[n_{k}]=-\sum_{k:\lambda_{k}>\Delta}n_{k}=-N_{\Delta}.

(68)

The canonical Fock states
\ket{\mathbf{n}}=\prod_{k:n_{k}=1}b^{\dagger}_{k}\ket{0}_{b}
, with configurations
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},n_{2},\cdots,n_{L})\in\{0,1\}^{L}
, form an eigenbasis of
H
, and the corresponding eigenvalue is
E_{\mathbf{n}}=E_{0}+\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}n_{k}
. For any canonical Fock basis vector
\ket{\mathbf{n}}
in the range of
\mathcal{P}_{>E}
where
E\geq E_{\Delta}
, we have

E_{\mathbf{n}}>E_{\Delta}
and thus there must be at least one mode
k
such that
\lambda_{k}>\Delta
and
n_{k}=1
. Since both
\mathcal{P}_{>E}
and
N_{\Delta}
are diagonal in the canonical Fock basis, this gives the operator inequality

\mathcal{P}_{>E}\leq N_{\Delta},\quad\forall E\geq E_{\Delta}.

(69)

By
Lemma
4
, we have

\Tr(\mc P_{>E}\rho(t))\leq\Tr(N_\Delta\rho(t))\leq e^{-t}\Tr(N_\Delta\rho(0))\leq Le^{-t},\quad\forall E\geq E_{\Delta}.

(70)

Hence
\Tr[\mathcal{P}_{>E_{\Delta}}\rho_{\rm ss}]=0
for every stationary state
\rho_{\rm ss}
, which means that stationary states have no support in the high-energy subspace above
E_{\Delta}
.

Next we show convergence of the state to its low-energy projection in trace distance. We denote

\mathcal{P}_{\leq E_{\Delta}}=\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,E_{\Delta}]}(H)=1-\mathcal{P}_{>E_{\Delta}}
as the projection onto the low-energy subspace.
Whenever
p_{\Delta}(t):=\Tr(\mc P_{\le E_\Delta}\rho(t))>0
, we define the projection of
\rho(t)
onto the low-energy subspace as

\rho_{\leq E_{\Delta}}(t):=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\leq E_{\Delta}}\rho(t)\mathcal{P}_{\leq E_{\Delta}}}{p_{\Delta}(t)}.

(71)

Since
\sqrt{\rho(t)}\rho_{\leq E_{\Delta}}(t)\sqrt{\rho(t)}=\left(\sqrt{\rho(t)}\mathcal{P}_{\leq E_{\Delta}}\sqrt{\rho(t)}\right)^{2}/p_{\Delta}(t)
,
we get that the fidelity
F(\rho(t),\rho_{\leq E_{\Delta}}(t))=\Tr\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho(t)}\rho_{\leq E_{\Delta}}(t)\sqrt{\rho(t)}}=\sqrt{p_{\Delta}(t)}.

By the Fuchs–van de Graaf inequality
Fuchs and Van De Graaf 2002
and
Eq.

70
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\rho(t)-\rho_{\leq E_{\Delta}}(t)\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{1-F(\rho(t),\rho_{\leq E_{\Delta}}(t))^{2}}=2\sqrt{1-p_{\Delta}(t)}=2\sqrt{{\mathrm{Tr}}\left[\mathcal{P}_{>E_{\Delta}}\rho(t)\right]}\leq 2\sqrt{L}e^{-t/2}.

(72)

∎

B.2
Free bosonic systems

We begin with the single bosonic mode.
The Hamiltonian and the coupling operator are

H=\Omega a^{\dagger}a=:\Omega N~({\Omega>0}),\quad A=a+a^{\dagger}.

(73)

Here

N:=a^{\dagger}a

(74)

is the number operator. Using free-bosonic version of the Thouless theorem
Eq.

55
, we have

e^{\mathrm{i}Hs}ae^{-\mathrm{i}Hs}=e^{-\mathrm{i}\Omega s}a,\quad e^{\mathrm{i}Hs}a^{\dagger}e^{-\mathrm{i}Hs}=e^{\mathrm{i}\Omega s}a^{\dagger}.

(75)

Therefore, the jump operator is constructed as

K=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(s)e^{\mathrm{i}Hs}Ae^{-\mathrm{i}Hs}ds=\widehat{f}(-\Omega)a+\widehat{f}(\Omega)a^{\dagger}.

(76)

We assume a realistic filter with finite resolution
\Delta>\Omega
, i.e.,
0\leq\widehat{f}(\Omega)<\widehat{f}(-\Omega)\leq 1
. Below, we denote the cooling and heating strength by

\gamma_{c}:=\widehat{f}(-\Omega),\quad\gamma_{h}:=\widehat{f}(\Omega).

(77)

Before moving on to prove
8
, let us first analyze the steady state properties. Since the Hamiltonian is quadratic and the jump operator is linear, the Lindblad dynamics is quasi-free, so the steady state is a bosonic Gaussian state, which is completely characterized by the moments
\langle a\rangle
,
\langle a^{2}\rangle
, and
\langle N\rangle=\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle
. These expectation values will indeed converge exponentially fast to their steady values. The relevant calculations are collected in the following proposition.

Proposition 7
.

The quasi-free dynamics associated with
Eq.

73
has a bosonic Gaussian stationary state with
\langle N\rangle_{\rm ss}=\frac{\gamma_{h}^{2}}{\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2}}
,
\langle a^{2}\rangle_{\rm ss}=\frac{\gamma_{c}\gamma_{h}}{\gamma_{h}^{2}-\gamma_{c}^{2}-2\mathrm{i}\Omega}
, and
\langle a\rangle_{\rm ss}=0
. The convergence of these moments is exponential.

These properties have been extensively studied in previous works
Prosen and Seligman 2010
;
Barthel and Zhang 2022
. For completeness, we also include the derivation here.

Proof.

Similar to the fermionic case, we compute the derivatives of these observables in the Heisenberg picture. Let us denote
\braket{O}_{t}:=\braket{O}(t){=\Tr(\rho(t ) O)}
for an observable
O
.

The number operator
N
.

Using the commutation relations
[N,a]=-a
,
[N,a^{\dagger}]=a^{\dagger}
,
we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{K}^{\dagger}[N]=\frac{1}{2}K^{\dagger}[N,K]+\frac{1}{2}[K^{\dagger},N]K=(\gamma_{h}^{2}-\gamma_{c}^{2})N+\gamma_{h}^{2}.

(78)

Take expectation in state
\rho
, and we obtain

\derivative{\langle N\rangle}{t}=\Tr\!\left(\rho{\mathcal{L}}^{{\dagger}}[N]\right)=(\gamma_{h}^{2}-\gamma_{c}^{2})\langle N\rangle+\gamma_{h}^{2}.

(79)

Its solution is

\langle N\rangle_{t}=\frac{\gamma_{h}^{2}}{\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2}}+\left(\langle N\rangle_{0}-\frac{\gamma_{h}^{2}}{\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2}}\right)e^{-(\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2})t},\quad\langle N\rangle_{\rm ss}=\frac{\gamma_{h}^{2}}{\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2}}.

(80)

The two-photon coherence
a^{2}
.

The coherent contribution to the Heisenberg picture derivative is

\mathrm{i}[H,a^{2}]=\mathrm{i}\Omega[a^{\dagger}a,a^{2}]=\mathrm{i}\Omega(-2a^{2})=-2\mathrm{i}\Omega a^{2}.

(81)

For the dissipation, using the commutators
[a^{2},K]=2\gamma_{h}a
and
[K^{\dagger},a^{2}]=-2\gamma_{c}a
, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{K}^{\dagger}[a^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}K^{\dagger}[a^{2},K]+\frac{1}{2}[K^{\dagger},a^{2}]K=(\gamma_{h}^{2}-\gamma_{c}^{2})a^{2}-\gamma_{c}\gamma_{h}.

(82)

Therefore

\derivative{\langle a^2\rangle}{t}=\Tr(\rho\mc L^\dag[a^2])=(-2\mathrm{i}\Omega+\gamma_{h}^{2}-\gamma_{c}^{2})\langle a^{2}\rangle-\gamma_{c}\gamma_{h}.

(83)

The solution is

\langle a^{2}\rangle_{t}=\langle a^{2}\rangle_{\rm ss}+\left(\braket{a^{2}}_{0}-\langle a^{2}\rangle_{\rm ss}\right)e^{-\left(\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2}+2\mathrm{i}\Omega\right)t},\quad\langle a^{2}\rangle_{\rm ss}=\frac{\gamma_{c}\gamma_{h}}{\gamma_{h}^{2}-\gamma_{c}^{2}-2\mathrm{i}\Omega}.

(84)

The lowering operator
a
.

The coherent contribution is

\mathrm{i}[H,a]=\mathrm{i}[\Omega a^{\dagger}a,a]=\mathrm{i}\Omega(a^{\dagger}aa-aa^{\dagger}a)=-\mathrm{i}\Omega a,

(85)

and for the dissipative part

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}_{K}[a]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}K^{\dagger}[a,K]+\frac{1}{2}[K^{{\dagger}},a]K=\frac{\gamma_{h}^{2}-\gamma_{c}^{2}}{2}a.

(86)

Therefore

\displaystyle\derivative{\langle a\rangle}{t}=\Tr(\rho\mc L^\dag[a])=\left(-\mathrm{i}\Omega+\frac{\gamma_{h}^{2}-\gamma_{c}^{2}}{2}\right)\langle a\rangle

(87)

which gives

\langle a\rangle_{t}=\langle a\rangle_{0}e^{-\left(\mathrm{i}\Omega+\frac{\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2}}{2}\right)t},\quad\langle a\rangle_{\rm ss}=0.

(88)

∎

We discuss the steady state properties of the bosonic system with realistic filters. Similar to the free fermionic case, this can also be done by constructing a suitable drift operator
and applying the argument of Lemma
4
to obtain the exponential tail bound of the steady state population distribution.

Theorem 8

(Rigorous version of
2
)
.

Let
s:={\gamma_{h}}/{\gamma_{c}}={\widehat{f}(\Omega)}/{\widehat{f}(-\Omega)}<1
, and choose
\beta
such that

0<\beta<\frac{1}{\Omega}\log\frac{1}{s},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad s<e^{-\beta\Omega}.

(89)

Then there exists a constant
C_{\beta}>0
, such that for any
E>0
,

\Tr(\mc P_{>E} \rho_{\rm ss})\leq C_{\beta}e^{-{\beta E}}.

(90)

Proof.

We choose the drift operator to be
\mathsf{F}_{\beta}:=e^{\beta\Omega N}
, and denote
r_{\beta}:=e^{\beta\Omega}
.
The diagonal matrix elements of
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\mathsf{F}_{\beta}]
in the Fock basis are

\langle n|\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\mathsf{F}_{\beta}]|n\rangle=r_{\beta}^{n}(r_{\beta}-1)\left(\gamma_{h}^{2}(n+1)-\frac{\gamma_{c}^{2}}{r_{\beta}}n\right).

(91)

The only off-diagonal matrix elements are

\langle n+2|\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\mathsf{F}_{\beta}]|n\rangle=\langle n|\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\mathsf{F}_{\beta}]|n+2\rangle=-\frac{\gamma_{c}\gamma_{h}}{2}(r_{\beta}-1)^{2}r_{\beta}^{n}\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}.

(92)

Eqs.

91
and
92
follow from inserting
the action of the jump operator
K
on the Fock states

K\ket{n}=\gamma_{c}\sqrt{n}\ket{n-1}+\gamma_{h}\sqrt{n+1}\ket{n+1}.

(93)

Define the normalized drift

\widetilde{\mathsf{F}_{\beta}}:=\mathsf{F}_{\beta}^{-1/2}\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\mathsf{F}_{\beta}]\mathsf{F}_{\beta}^{-1/2}.

(94)

By
\mathsf{F}_{\beta}\ket{n}={r_{\beta}^{n}}\ket{n}
, the diagonal and off-diagonal elements of
\widetilde{\mathsf{F}_{\beta}}
are given by
Eqs.

95
and
96
:

(\widetilde{\mathsf{F}_{\beta}})_{n,n}=(r_{\beta}-1)\left(\gamma_{h}^{2}(n+1)-\frac{\gamma_{c}^{2}}{r_{\beta}}n\right),

(95)

(\widetilde{\mathsf{F}_{\beta}})_{n+2,n}=(\widetilde{\mathsf{F}_{\beta}})_{n,n+2}=-\frac{\gamma_{c}\gamma_{h}}{2r_{\beta}}(r_{\beta}-1)^{2}\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}.

(96)

Set

c_{n}:=\frac{\gamma_{c}\gamma_{h}}{2r_{\beta}}(r_{\beta}-1)^{2}\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}\geq 0,\quad c_{-2}=c_{-1}=0.

(97)

For any
\ket{\varphi}=\sum_{n}\varphi_{n}\ket{n}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
, the inequality
2|uv|\leq|u|^{2}+|v|^{2}
gives

\langle\varphi|\widetilde{\mathsf{F}_{\beta}}|\varphi\rangle\leq\sum_{n\geq 0}A_{n}|\varphi_{n}|^{2},

(98)

\displaystyle A_{n}

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{\mathsf{F}_{\beta}})_{n,n}+c_{n}+c_{n-2}\leq\left[(r_{\beta}-1)\left(\gamma_{h}^{2}-\frac{\gamma_{c}^{2}}{r_{\beta}}\right)+\frac{\gamma_{c}\gamma_{h}}{r_{\beta}}(r_{\beta}-1)^{2}\right]n+(r_{\beta}-1)\gamma_{h}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{c}\gamma_{h}}{r_{\beta}}(r_{\beta}-1)^{2}

(99)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{c}^{2}(r_{\beta}-1)}{r_{\beta}}(s+1)(r_{\beta}s-1)n+(r_{\beta}-1)\gamma_{h}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{c}\gamma_{h}}{r_{\beta}}(r_{\beta}-1)^{2}=:q_{\beta}n+d_{\beta}.

By
Eq.

89
,
q_{\beta}<0
and
d_{\beta}>0
. For any fixed
\kappa>0
, there exists
M=\lceil-(d_{\beta}+\kappa)/q_{\beta}\rceil
such that
A_{n}\leq-\kappa
for all
n\geq M
. Therefore, if we define

C:=\max\{0,\max_{0\leq n<M}(A_{n}+\kappa)\}\leq d_{\beta}+\kappa<\infty,

(100)

then for every
n
, we have by definition that
A_{n}\leq-\kappa+C\mathbf{1}_{n<M}
. Therefore, by the quadratic-form inequality
Eq.

98
,

\langle\varphi|\widetilde{\mathsf{F}_{\beta}}|\varphi\rangle\leq\sum_{n\geq 0}(-\kappa+C\mathbf{1}_{n<M})|\varphi_{n}|^{2}=-\kappa\lVert\ket{\varphi}\rVert^{2}+C\lVert\mathcal{P}_{n<M}\ket{\varphi}\rVert^{2}=\langle\varphi|(-\kappa I+C\mathcal{P}_{n<M})|\varphi\rangle.

(101)

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert
denotes the
\ell^{2}
norm and
\mathcal{P}_{n<M}
is the projection onto the span of
\ket{0},\ket{1},\ldots,\ket{M-1}
. Therefore we have the operator inequality on the space of
\ell^{2}
-integrable vectors:

\widetilde{\mathsf{F}_{\beta}}\leq-\kappa I+C\mathcal{P}_{n<M}.

(102)

Note that
\mathcal{P}_{n<M}=\mathbf{1}_{n<M}(N)
and
\mathsf{F}_{\beta}=r_{\beta}^{N}
, thus we have

\mathsf{F}_{\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{P}_{n<M}\mathsf{F}_{\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}}=r_{\beta}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{n<M}(N)=r_{\beta}^{N}\mathcal{P}_{n<M}\leq r_{\beta}^{M-1}\mathcal{P}_{n<M}\leq r_{\beta}^{M-1}I.

(103)

Thus conjugating
Eq.

\mathsf{F}_{\beta}^{1/2}
gives

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\mathsf{F}_{\beta}]\leq-\kappa\mathsf{F}_{\beta}+Cr_{\beta}^{M-1}I.

(104)

Thus the drift inequality holds with
\theta:=Cr_{\beta}^{M-1}
. The stationary bound follows from Lemma
4
, which gives

\Tr(\mathsf F_\beta\rho_{ss})\leq\frac{\theta}{\kappa}\leq\frac{d_{\beta}+\kappa}{\kappa}r_{\beta}^{-\frac{d_{\beta}+\kappa}{q_{\beta}}}.

(105)

By choosing e.g.
\kappa=-\frac{q_{\beta}}{2}
, we get
\Tr(\mathsf F_\beta\rho_{ss})\leq\left(1-\frac{2d_{\beta}}{q_{\beta}}\right)r_{\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{d_{\beta}}{q_{\beta}}}
. Note that
\lim_{\beta\downarrow 0}\left(-\frac{d_{\beta}}{q_{\beta}}\right)=\frac{s^{2}}{1-s^{2}}
, we have that the upper bound is finite for any
0\leq\beta<\frac{1}{\Omega}\log\frac{1}{s}
.

We can then obtain the tail bound for the steady state using Markov’s inequality. For any
E\geq 0
, we have by setting
\mathfrak{g}_{k}=e^{\beta\lambda_{k}}
and noting that
\mathfrak{g}_{E}\geq\inf_{\lambda>E}e^{\beta\lambda}=e^{\beta E}
in Lemma
4
that

\Tr(\mc P_{>E}\rho_{ss})\leq e^{-{\beta E}}\Tr(e^{\beta\Omega N}\rho_{ss})\leq e^{-{\beta E}}\frac{\theta}{\kappa}.

(106)

∎

Remark 9
.

In the purely dissipative case i.e.
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{L}_{H}=0
, the
steady state is actually analytically solvable and is given by
a squeezed vacuum state
\ket{\Psi_{s}}=(1-s^{2})^{1/4}\sum_{k\geq 0}(-s)^{k}\frac{\sqrt{(2k)!}}{2^{k}k!}\ket{2k}.

The physical occupation is
m=2k
, and the populations behave as

|\langle 2k|\Psi_{s}\rangle|^{2}=(1-s^{2})^{1/2}s^{2k}\frac{(2k)!}{4^{k}(k!)^{2}}\sim s^{m}k^{-1/2}.

(107)

Therefore the exact squeezed-state moment
\langle e^{\eta N}\rangle
is finite precisely for
e^{\eta}s<1
, i.e.

\eta<\log\frac{1}{s}.

Taking
\eta=\beta\Omega
gives the same admissible range as
Eq.

89
. The Lyapunov proof above could be viewed as the dynamical approach of the same exponential threshold, which is obtained without any prior knowledge of the exact steady state.

B.3
Additional results on free bosonic systems

Since the steady state is a Gaussian state, we could also understand the free bosonic systems by directly solving the steady state or the covariance matrix. In this section, we present some additional results about the steady state of the free bosonic system such as the trace-distance convergence and the generalization to the multimode case, which may be of independent interest.

B.3.1
Purely dissipative case

We first consider the purely dissipative case
i.e.,
\mathcal{L}_{H}=0
and
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{D}
. In this case, the steady state is a unique pure state in
\ker K
and can be characterized by a squeezed vacuum state
Marian and Marian 1993
;
Grynberg et al. 2010

\ket{\Psi_{\zeta}}:=S(\zeta)\ket{0}_{a}=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{2}(\zeta^{\ast}aa-\zeta a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger})\right)\ket{0}_{a},

(108)

where the squeeze parameter
\zeta
depends only on the leakage ratio:

\zeta=\arctanh s,\quad s:=\gamma_{h}/\gamma_{c}{<1}.

(109)

The Fock basis expansion of
\ket{\Psi_{\zeta}}
is

\ket{\Psi_{\zeta}}=(1-s^{2})^{1/4}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-s)^{n}\frac{\sqrt{(2n)!}}{2^{n}n!}\ket{2n}.

(110)

By Stirling’s formula

\frac{\sqrt{(2n)!}}{2^{n}n!}\sim n^{-1/4}
, the coefficients satisfy

|\langle 2n|\Psi_{\zeta}\rangle|\sim|s|^{n}n^{-1/4},\quad\langle 2n+1|\Psi_{\zeta}\rangle=0.

(111)

Since
0\leq s<1
, the Fock-state populations decay exponentially in
n
, up to a subleading algebraic prefactor.

It is useful for numerically simulating the profile of the steady-state photon number distribution. In particular, when
s=0
,
\ket{\Psi_{\zeta=0}}=\ket{0}_{a}
is the vacuum state. We derive
Eq.

110
and verify that
K\ket{\Psi_{\zeta}}=0
in Appendix
B.3.3
.

Since the steady state is a pure state, we can utilize the Fuchs–van de Graaf argument to further show the exponential convergence of the state
\rho(t)
to the steady state
\ket{\Psi}
in trace distance. Specifically, we have

Proposition 10

(Rigorous version of Proposition
3
,
with
\mathcal{L}_{H}=0
)
.

If the initial state
\rho(0)
satisfies that
\Tr(K^\dag K\rho(0))
is well-defined and finite, then the trace distance between
\rho(t)
and the steady state
\rho_{\rm ss}
decays exponentially with a rate
e^{-(\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2})t/2}
and an initial-state-dependent prefactor. Here
\rho_{\rm ss}
is the squeezed vacuum state
Eq.

110
.

Proof.

We define the “squeezed number operator” as follows

\widetilde{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-s^{2}}}(a+sa^{\dagger}),\quad\widetilde{N}=\widetilde{a}^{\dagger}\widetilde{a}=\frac{1}{\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2}}K^{\dagger}K.

(112)

Thus we have
\widetilde{N}\ket{\Psi_{\zeta}}=0
by
\ket{\Psi_{\zeta}}\in\ker K

. In fact, we can view the operator
\widetilde{a}
as a Bogoliubov-transformed bosonic annihilation operator, and we can verify that
\widetilde{a}
and
\widetilde{a}^{\dagger}
satisfy the canonical commutation relation (CCR)
[\widetilde{a},\widetilde{a}^{\dagger}]=1
and
[\widetilde{a},(\widetilde{a}^{\dagger})^{n}]=n(\widetilde{a}^{\dagger})^{n-1}
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{N}(\widetilde{a}^{\dagger})^{n}\ket{\Psi_{\zeta}}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{a}^{\dagger}\widetilde{a}(\widetilde{a}^{\dagger})^{n}\ket{\Psi_{\zeta}}=n(\widetilde{a}^{\dagger})^{n}\ket{\Psi_{\zeta}}.

(113)

Therefore we can define the orthonormal eigenbasis of
\widetilde{N}
as
\ket{n}_{\widetilde{a}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}(\widetilde{a}^{\dagger})^{n}\ket{\Psi}
, which is also a Fock basis for the bosonic mode.
The steady state
\rho_{\rm ss}=\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}=\outerproduct{0}{0}_{\widetilde{a}}
is the vacuum in this basis.
Thus, we can compute the dynamics of
\widetilde{N}
as follows:

\mathcal{D}_{K}^{\dagger}[\widetilde{N}]=(\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2})\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}_{\widetilde{a}}[\widetilde{a}^{\dagger}\widetilde{a}]=-(\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2})\widetilde{N}\implies\Tr(\rho(t) \wt N)=e^{-(\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2})t}\Tr(\rho(0) \wt N).

(114)

Let us write both
\rho_{\rm ss}
and
\widetilde{N}
in the squeezed Fock basis
\{|n\rangle_{\widetilde{a}}\}
:

I-\rho_{\rm ss}=I-\outerproduct{0}{0}_{\widetilde{a}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|n\rangle_{\widetilde{a}}\langle n|_{\widetilde{a}},\quad\widetilde{N}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n|n\rangle_{\widetilde{a}}\langle n|_{\widetilde{a}},

(115)

which implies that
I-\rho_{\rm ss}\leq\widetilde{N}
. Therefore, by the Fuchs–van de Graaf inequality
Fuchs and Van De Graaf 2002
, we have

\displaystyle d(\rho(t),\rho_{\rm ss})\leq\sqrt{1-\Tr(\dyad{0}_{\wt a} \rho(t))}\overset{\text{$1-\rho_{\rm ss}\leq\widetilde{N}$}}{\leq}\sqrt{\Tr(\wt{N} \rho(t))}=e^{-(\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2})t/2}\sqrt{\Tr(\wt{N} \rho(0))}.

(116)

Because
\Tr(\wt N\rho(0))=\frac{1}{\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2}}\Tr(K^\dag K \rho(0))
is well-defined and finite by the assumption, we get the required exponential convergence in the trace distance.
∎

The analysis above can be easily extended to multi-mode free bosonic systems. The Hamiltonian and the coupling operators are given by

H=\sum_{i,j=1}^{L}h_{ij}a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{j},\quad A_{i}=a_{i}+a_{i}^{\dagger},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,L,

(117)

where
h
is a positive definite real symmetric matrix and we choose a real orthogonal diagonalization
h=U\text{diag}(\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{L})U^{\dagger}
. We can define the canonical modes
\widetilde{a}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{L}U_{ji}a_{j}
such that
H=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\widetilde{a}_{i}
. The bosonic operators in the canonical mode basis still satisfy the CCR
[\widetilde{a}_{i},\widetilde{a}_{j}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{ij}
and
[\widetilde{a}_{i},\widetilde{a}_{j}]=0
. We have a similar decomposition of the Lindbladian as in the free fermion case Proposition
5
.

Proposition 11
.

For the multi-mode free bosonic system defined above, with the coupling operator set
\{a_{i}+a_{i}^{\dagger}\}_{i=1}^{L}
, assume
\gamma_{c,k}>\gamma_{h,k}\geq 0
for all
k
. For the purely dissipative dynamics generated by the filtered jumps, the Lindbladian can be decomposed as

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{D}=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\mathcal{D}_{J_{k}},\quad J_{k}=\widehat{f}(-\varepsilon_{k})\widetilde{a}_{k}+\widehat{f}(\varepsilon_{k})\widetilde{a}_{k}^{\dagger}.

(118)

The steady state is a tensor product of single-mode squeezed vacuum states
\rho_{\rm ss}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{L}\outerproduct{\Psi_i}{\Psi_i}
, where each
\ket{\Psi_{i}}
is determined by the corresponding filter leakage
s_{i}=\widehat{f}(\varepsilon_{i})/\widehat{f}(-\varepsilon_{i})
by
Eq.

110
.

Proof.

Using the Thouless theorem
e^{\mathrm{i}Hs}\widetilde{a}_{i}e^{-\mathrm{i}Hs}=e^{-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon_{i}s}\widetilde{a}_{i}
and expanding the coupling operators in the canonical mode basis, we can construct the jump operators as

K_{i}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(s)e^{\mathrm{i}Hs}A_{i}e^{-\mathrm{i}Hs}ds=\sum_{k=1}^{L}U_{ik}\widehat{f}(-\varepsilon_{k})\widetilde{a}_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{L}U_{ik}\widehat{f}(\varepsilon_{k})\widetilde{a}_{k}^{\dagger}=:\sum_{k=1}^{L}U_{ik}J_{k},

(119)

where we denote

J_{k}=\gamma_{c,k}\widetilde{a}_{k}+\gamma_{h,k}\widetilde{a}_{k}^{\dagger},\quad\gamma_{c,k}:=\widehat{f}(-\varepsilon_{k}),\quad\gamma_{h,k}:=\widehat{f}(\varepsilon_{k}).

(120)

The dissipator is given by
\mathcal{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathcal{D}_{K_{i}}
, which can be simplified using the orthogonality of
U
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}[\rho]=\sum_{k=1}^{L}J_{k}\rho J_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{J_{k}^{\dagger}J_{k},\rho\}=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\mathcal{D}_{J_{k}}[\rho].

(121)

We then look at the dynamics of the operators
\widetilde{a}_{i}
,
\widetilde{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\widetilde{a}_{i}
and
\widetilde{a}_{i}^{2}
on each canonical mode. This is equivalent to the single-mode case, and we have the following identities for the dissipator using the CCR (see also
Eq.

80

Eq.

83
and
Eq.

87
):

\mathcal{D}_{J_{i}}^{\dagger}[\widetilde{a}_{i}]=\frac{\gamma_{h,i}^{2}-\gamma_{c,i}^{2}}{2}\widetilde{a}_{i},\quad\mathcal{D}_{J_{i}}^{\dagger}[\widetilde{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\widetilde{a}_{i}]=(\gamma_{h,i}^{2}-\gamma_{c,i}^{2})\widetilde{a}_{i}^{\dagger}\widetilde{a}_{i}+\gamma_{h,i}^{2},\quad\mathcal{D}_{J_{i}}^{\dagger}[\widetilde{a}_{i}^{2}]=(\gamma_{h,i}^{2}-\gamma_{c,i}^{2})\widetilde{a}_{i}^{2}-\gamma_{c,i}\gamma_{h,i},

(122)

Also, similar to
Eq.

65
in the multi-site free fermion case, the steady state of this purely dissipative generator is a tensor product of single-mode squeezed vacuum states, each of which is determined by the corresponding filter leakage
s_{i}:=\gamma_{h,i}/\gamma_{c,i}
. Specifically,
we define
\ket{\Psi_{i}}
as in
Eq.

110
for the
i
-th mode corresponding to the
i
-th leakage ratio
s_{i}:=\gamma_{h,i}/\gamma_{c,i}=\widehat{f}(\varepsilon_{i})/\widehat{f}(-\varepsilon_{i})
. Then by the single-mode construction above in Proposition
10
,

J_{i}\ket{\Psi_{i}}=(\gamma_{c,i}\widetilde{a}_{i}+\gamma_{h,i}\widetilde{a}_{i}^{\dagger})\ket{\Psi_{i}}=0.

(123)

Let
\rho_{i}:=\outerproduct{\Psi_i}{\Psi_i}
, and
\rho_{\rm ss}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{L}\rho_{i}
is the steady state of the dynamics.

∎

Using the Fuchs–van de Graaf inequality, we can further show the exponential convergence of
\rho(t)
to
\rho_{\rm ss}
in trace distance.

Theorem 12
.

Assume
\gamma:=\min_{i}\left[\widehat{f}(-\varepsilon_{i})^{2}-\widehat{f}(\varepsilon_{i})^{2}\right]>0
and finite initial squeezed occupation
\sum_{i}\Tr(\wt N_i\rho(0))<\infty
, where
\widetilde{N}_{i}
is defined in the proof below. The trace distance between
\rho(t)
and the steady state
\rho_{\rm ss}
decays exponentially with a rate
e^{-\gamma t/2}
, where
\gamma=\min_{i}\left[\widehat{f}(-\varepsilon_{i})^{2}-\widehat{f}(\varepsilon_{i})^{2}\right]
.

Proof.

We can define the “squeezed number operator” for each mode as
\widetilde{N}_{i}=\frac{1}{\gamma_{c,i}^{2}-\gamma_{h,i}^{2}}J_{i}^{\dagger}J_{i}
, which satisfies

\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}[\widetilde{N}_{i}]=-(\gamma_{c,i}^{2}-\gamma_{h,i}^{2})\widetilde{N}_{i},\quad\widetilde{N}_{i}\ket{\Psi_{i}}=0.

(124)

Similar to Proposition
10
, we have
I-\rho_{i}\leq\widetilde{N}_{i}
and thus

I-\rho_{\rm ss}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{L}(I-\rho_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{L}\widetilde{N}_{i}

(125)

by the operator inequality
1-\bigotimes_{i=1}^{L}P_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{L}(1-P_{i})
for
P_{i}
being orthogonal projectors. Therefore, by the Fuchs–van de Graaf inequality
Fuchs and Van De Graaf 2002
, we have

\displaystyle d(\rho(t),\rho_{\rm ss})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1-\Tr(\rho_{\rm ss} \rho(t))}=\sqrt{\Tr((I-\rho_{\rm ss})\rho(t))}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{L}\Tr(\wt N_i \rho(t))}\leq e^{-\gamma t/2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{L}\Tr(\wt N_i \, \rho(0))},

(126)

where
\gamma=\min_{i}(\gamma_{c,i}^{2}-\gamma_{h,i}^{2})
.
∎

B.3.2
Free bosonic systems with both Hamiltonian and dissipative contributions

The fact that the equations of motion of the first and second moments (
a
,
a^{\dagger}a
,
a^{2}
) form a closed system is characteristic of quasi-free Lindblad dynamics in general. Under such dynamics, the linear jump operators preserve the Gaussianity of the state. If the initial state of the dissipative protocol is assumed to also be a Gaussian state, we can carry out a unified analysis for both the purely dissipative case and the case with Hamiltonian contribution by the recently developed techniques for analyzing the tight upper bound of the trace distance between two Gaussian states, for both bosonic and fermionic systems
Bittel et al. 2025a
;
Bittel et al. 2025b
.

Theorem 13

(
(
Bittel et al. 2025a
, Theorem 1)
)
.

Let
\rho(V,\mathbf{m})
and
\rho(W,\mathbf{t})
be two bosonic Gaussian states with covariance matrices
V
and
W
and first-moment vectors
\mathbf{m}
and
\mathbf{t}
. Then the trace distance between
\rho(V,\mathbf{m})
and
\rho(W,\mathbf{t})
is upper bounded by

d(\rho(V,\mathbf{m}),\rho(W,\mathbf{t}))\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}+1}{16}\Tr(\abs{V-W} \Xi^T (V+W) \Xi)+\sqrt{\frac{\min(\lVert V\rVert,\lVert W\rVert)}{2}}\lVert\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{t}\rVert,

(127)

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert
denotes the spectral norm, the matrix absolute value is defined as
\absolutevalue{A}=\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}
, and the first-moment vector
\mathbf{m}
and the positive semidefinite covariance matrix
V
are respectively defined as

\mathbf{m}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\langle a\rangle+\langle a^{\dagger}\rangle\\
-\mathrm{i}(\langle a\rangle-\langle a^{\dagger}\rangle)\end{pmatrix}=\sqrt{2}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\Re\langle a\rangle\\ \Im\langle a\rangle\endlx@physics@matrix),\quad\Xi=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}

(128)

and

V=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Var}(a)+\mathrm{Var}(a^{\dagger})+2\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle_{\rm c}+1&\mathrm{i}(\mathrm{Var}(a^{\dagger})-\mathrm{Var}(a))\\
\mathrm{i}(\mathrm{Var}(a^{\dagger})-\mathrm{Var}(a))&-(\mathrm{Var}(a)+\mathrm{Var}(a^{\dagger}))+2\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle_{\rm c}+1\end{pmatrix}

(129)

where we introduce the short-hand notations

\langle AB\rangle_{\rm c}:=\langle AB\rangle-\langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle,\quad\mathrm{Var}(A):=\langle A^{2}\rangle_{\rm c}=\langle A^{2}\rangle-\langle A\rangle^{2}.

(130)

Using
13
, we can analyze the convergence of the state
\rho(t)
to the steady state
\rho_{\rm ss}
for both the purely dissipative case and the case with Hamiltonian contribution, by analyzing the dynamics of the covariance matrix and the first-moment vector.

Theorem 14

(Rigorous version of Proposition
3
, with both Hamiltonian and dissipative contributions)
.

If the initial state
\rho(0)
is a Gaussian state, then

d(\rho(t),\rho_{\rm ss})\leq Ce^{-(\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2})t/2},

(131)

where
C
is a constant that depends on the initial state
\rho(0)
and the system parameters
\Omega,\gamma_{c},\gamma_{h}
, but is independent of time
t
. Here
\rho_{\rm ss}
is the squeezed thermal state with the stationary first and second moments computed in
Eqs.

80
,
84
and
88
.

Proof.

We calculate the difference of the covariance matrices
V(t)
and
V_{\rm ss}
by noticing that

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{ \mathrm{Var} (a)_t - \mathrm{Var} (a)_{\rm ss}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{$\langle a\rangle_{\rm ss}=0$ by \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:b_boson}}}{=}\absolutevalue{\langle a^2\rangle_t -\langle a^2\rangle_{\rm ss} - \langle a\rangle_t^2}

(132)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\lx@cref{creftypeplural\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:b2_boson} and\lx@nobreakspace\lx@cref{refnum}{eq:b_boson}}}{\leq}\absolutevalue{\langle a^2\rangle_{\rm ss} - \langle a^2\rangle_0 }e^{-(\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2})t}+\absolutevalue{\langle a\rangle_0}^{2}e^{-(\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2})t}.

Note that
\langle a^{2}\rangle=\Tr(\rho a^2)=\Tr((\rho a^2)^\dag)^{\ast}=\langle a^{{\dagger}2}\rangle^{\ast}
, thus similar to
Eqs.

83
and
87
, we can also have

\displaystyle\langle a^{{\dagger}2}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\langle a^{{\dagger}2}\rangle_{\rm ss}+{\left(\braket{a^{{\dagger}2}}_{0}-\langle a^{{\dagger}2}\rangle_{\rm ss}\right)e^{-(\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2}-2\mathrm{i}\Omega)t}},\quad\langle a^{{\dagger}2}\rangle_{\rm ss}=\frac{\gamma_{c}\gamma_{h}}{\gamma_{h}^{2}-\gamma_{c}^{2}+2\mathrm{i}\Omega}.

(133)

Moreover, for the connected correlation term, we have

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{ \braket{a^\dag a}_{\mathrm{c}, t}
- \langle a^\dag a\rangle_{\rm c, ss}}_{\mathrm{c},t}-\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle_{\rm c,ss}

(134)

\lVert V(t)-V_{\rm ss}\rVert\leq C_{1}^{\prime}e^{-(\gamma_{c}^{2}-\gamma_{h}^{2})t},

for some constant

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{m}_{t}-\mathbf{m}_{\rm ss}\rVert

Putting all the pieces together, and by

and the trace Hölder inequality, we get the convergence of

\displaystyle d(\rho(t),\rho_{\rm ss})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}+1}{16}\Tr(\abs{V(t)-V_{\rm ss}} \Xi^T (V(t)+V_{\rm ss}) \Xi)+\sqrt{\frac{\min(\lVert V(t)\rVert,\lVert V_{\rm ss}\rVert)}{2}}\lVert\mathbf{m}_{t}-\mathbf{m}_{\rm ss}\rVert

(137)

B.3.3
Details on the bosonic squeezed state

We first derive the explicit form of the steady state in the purely dissipative case

Proposition 15

(
Grynberg et al. 2010
)
.

For the squeezed vacuum state
\ket{\Psi_{\zeta}}
in

, we write the parameter

as

Proof of
Eq.

110
.

In the purely dissipative case, the steady state moments are given by

83

(139)

We solve for the parameter
\zeta

138

(141)

Plugging in the values of

\ket{\Psi_{\zeta}}=(1-s^{2})^{1/4}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-s)^{n}\frac{\sqrt{(2n)!}}{2^{n}n!}\ket{2n}.

(142)

We verify that
\ket{\Psi_{\zeta}}

\displaystyle K\ket{\Psi_{\zeta}}

\displaystyle={\gamma_{c}}(a+sa^{\dagger})\ket{\Psi_{\zeta}}

(143)

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-s)^{n+1}\sqrt{2n+2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{(2n+2)!}}{2^{n+1}(n+1)!}\ket{2n+1}-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-s)^{n+1}\sqrt{2n+1}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{(2n)!}}{2^{n}n!}\ket{2n+1}.

Note that

therefore the two sums cancel each other and we get

∎

In the numerical simulation in Sec.

\widehat{f}(-\Omega)=1
with
0\leq s<1
. We assume that the initial state has zero first moment, i.e.,
\langle a\rangle_{0}=0
, which means that
\langle a\rangle_{t}=0
for all
t\geq 0
and thus we do not need a displacement operator in the following discussion. For a systematic discussion on photon-number distribution in more general cases, we refer to
Marian and Marian 1993
.

A squeezed thermal state can be written as

\rho=S(\zeta)\rho_{\text{th}}S^{\dagger}(\zeta),

(144)

where

\rho_{\rm th}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\overline{n}^{n}}{(\overline{n}+1)^{n+1}}\ket{n}\bra{n},\quad\overline{n}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(2\mathsf{N}+1)^{2}-4|\mathsf{M}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{2},

(145)

with
\mathsf{N}=\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle
and
\mathsf{M}=\langle a^{2}\rangle
. The squeezing operator is
S(\zeta)=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{2}(\zeta^{\ast}aa-\zeta a^{\dagger}a^{\dagger})\right)
, and the squeezing parameter
\zeta
is determined by

\cosh 2r=\frac{2\mathsf{N}+1}{2\overline{n}+1},\quad{e^{2\mathrm{i}\theta}\sinh 2r=-\frac{2\mathsf{M}}{2\overline{n}+1}},\quad\zeta=re^{2\mathrm{i}\theta}.

(146)

Then the exact probability
\rho_{nn}(t)
can be computed by the following formula
Marian and Marian 1993
:

\rho_{n,n}=\bra{n}\rho\ket{n}=\frac{\mathsf{h}_{2}^{n/2}}{\mathsf{h}_{0}^{(n+1)/2}}\Theta_{n}\!\left(\frac{\mathsf{h}_{1}}{\sqrt{\mathsf{h}_{0}\mathsf{h}_{2}}}\right),

(147)

where
\Theta_{n}(x)
denotes the
n
-th Legendre polynomial and the variables
\mathsf{h}_{0}
,
\mathsf{h}_{1}
and
\mathsf{h}_{2}
are defined as

\mathsf{h}_{0}=(\mathsf{N}+1)^{2}-|\mathsf{M}|^{2},\quad\mathsf{h}_{1}=\mathsf{N}(\mathsf{N}+1)-|\mathsf{M}|^{2},\quad\mathsf{h}_{2}=\mathsf{N}^{2}-|\mathsf{M}|^{2}.

(148)

Using
Eq.

147
one could compute the photon number distribution at any time
t
using the moment information
\mathsf{N}(t)=\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle_{t}
and
\mathsf{M}(t)=\langle a^{2}\rangle_{t}
. This shows the exponentially decaying behavior of the population
\rho_{nn}(t)
in the Fock basis for bosonic squeezed thermal states.

Appendix E
Details in diagonalizing the Kitaev honeycomb model

In this section, we provide details on how to diagonalize and solve the Kitaev honeycomb model. Most of the relevant calculations can be found in the literature, for instance, in
Kitaev 2006
;
Cui et al. 2010
. We only include part of the calculations for completeness.

The Kitaev honeycomb model can be described by introducing four Majorana fermions on each site, normalized by
\{\eta_{i}^{a},\eta_{j}^{b}\}=\delta_{ij}\delta_{ab}
,

{S_{i}^{\alpha}=\mathrm{i}\eta_{i}^{\alpha}\eta_{i},\quad\alpha=x,y,z,}

(156)

together with the constraint
S_{i}^{x}S_{i}^{y}S_{i}^{z}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\eta_{i}^{x}\eta_{i}^{y}\eta_{i}^{z}\eta_{i}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{8}
. The gauge field is defined on the bonds as

\mathfrak{u}_{ij}:=2\mathrm{i}\eta_{i}^{\alpha}\eta_{j}^{\alpha}

(157)

if
i,j
are connected by an
\alpha
-type bond. It is easy to check that for any bonds
\langle i,j\rangle_{\alpha(i,j)}
and
\langle k,l\rangle_{\alpha(k,l)}
, the gauge field satisfies the constraint

[\mathfrak{u}_{ij},\mathfrak{u}_{kl}]=0,\quad[\mathfrak{u}_{ij},H]=0.

(158)

This means that each operator
\mathfrak{u}_{ij}
, together with the Hamiltonian can be simultaneously diagonalized in some choice of gauge. In particular, the eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{u}_{ij}
are
\pm 1
since
\mathfrak{u}_{ij}^{2}=1
(and which is where the name
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge field comes from). Therefore, we can treat
\mathfrak{u}_{ij}
as a classical variable and the Hamiltonian becomes quadratic in Majorana fermions which can be solved exactly in this background gauge field:

H=2\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\mathrm{i}J_{\alpha(i,j)}\mathfrak{u}_{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}=\sum_{i,j}\mathrm{i}J_{\alpha(i,j)}\mathfrak{u}_{ij}\eta_{i}\eta_{j}.

(159)

In the second expression the sum over
i,j
is over the two directed orientations of each nearest-neighbor bond.
From
Lieb 1994
;
Kitaev 2006
;
Cui et al. 2010
we know that the ground state of the system is in the vortex-free sector which means we can work within the sector where
\mathfrak{u}_{ij}=1=-\mathfrak{u}_{ji}
for all bonds, where
i
is the site on the
A
sublattice and
j
is the site on the
B
sublattice (this lattice as seen in
Fig.

4(a)
is bipartite and is actually bicolorable). The unit cell of the honeycomb lattice contains an
A
-color site and a
B
-color site. We denote the position of the unit cell by
\mathbf{s}
and the two sites in the unit cell by
(\mathbf{s},\mu)
where
\mu=A,B
. The Hamiltonian can be written as

H=\mathrm{i}\sum_{\mathbf{s},\mu,\mathbf{t},\nu}J_{\alpha(\mathbf{s},\mu;\mathbf{t},\nu)}\eta_{\mathbf{s}\mu}\eta_{\mathbf{t}\nu}.

(160)

In particular, if
(\mathbf{s},\mu)
and
(\mathbf{t},\nu)
are not nearest neighbors, then
J_{\alpha(\mathbf{s},\mu;\mathbf{t},\nu)}=0
. We see that because of the translation invariance,
\alpha(\mathbf{s},\mu;\mathbf{t},\nu)
(and thus
J
) only depends on
\mu
,
\nu
and
\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{s}
, therefore we can perform the Bloch–Floquet decomposition

J_{\mathbf{s},\mu;\mathbf{t},\nu}=J_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{s})=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot(\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{s})}\widehat{J}_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{q}),\quad\eta_{\mathbf{s},\mu}={\frac{1}{L}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{s}}a_{\mathbf{q},\mu}.

(161)

Here
\mathbf{q}=(q_{x},q_{y})
goes through the discretized first Brillouin zone of the reciprocal lattice. Here
q_{x}
and
q_{y}
are the components of
\mathbf{q}
in the basis of the reciprocal lattice vectors in the direction of
\mathbf{n}_{1}
and
\mathbf{n}_{2}
respectively.
q_{x},q_{y}=\frac{2\pi n}{L},n=-\frac{L-1}{2},\cdots,\frac{L-1}{2}
. We take
L
to be odd to make it convenient for looking up the
-\mathbf{q}
mode in the following calculations.
The inverse transforms are given by

\widehat{J}_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{q})=\sum_{\mathbf{t}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{t}}J_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{t}),\quad a_{\mathbf{q},\mu}={\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\mathbf{s}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{s}}\eta_{\mathbf{s},\mu}}.

(162)

The Fourier modes of the Majorana fermions satisfy the anti-commutation relation, and the momentum-conserving
\mathbf{q}
-mode operators are related by a Hermitian conjugation:

a_{\mathbf{q},\mu}^{\dagger}=a_{-\mathbf{q},\mu},\quad\{a_{\mathbf{q},\mu},a_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\nu}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\delta_{\mu,\nu},\quad\{a_{\mathbf{q},\mu},a_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\nu}\}=\{a_{\mathbf{q},\mu}^{\dagger},a_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\nu}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\delta_{\mu\nu},\text{~in particular,~}a_{\mathbf{q},\mu}^{2}=0~\text{for}~\mathbf{q}\neq\mathbf{0}.

(163)

The transformed Hamiltonian can be written as

H=\mathrm{i}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\sum_{\mu,\nu\in\{A,B\}}\widehat{J}_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{q})a_{\mathbf{q},\mu}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{q},\nu}=\mathrm{i}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\sum_{\mu,\nu\in\{A,B\}}\widehat{J}_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{q})a_{-\mathbf{q},\mu}a_{\mathbf{q},\nu}.

(164)

Because
J_{0A;\mathbf{t}A}=J_{0B;\mathbf{t}B}=0
(there is no bond there), we have
\widehat{J}_{AA}(\mathbf{q})=\widehat{J}_{BB}(\mathbf{q})=0
. For
\widehat{J}_{AB}(\mathbf{q})
, only three values of
\mathbf{t}
contribute:
\mathbf{t}=0
,
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{n}_{1}
and
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{n}_{2}
where
\mathbf{n}_{1}
and
\mathbf{n}_{2}
are the two primitive lattice vectors of the honeycomb lattice. We have

\widehat{J}_{AB}(\mathbf{q})=J_{x}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{1}}+J_{y}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{2}}+J_{z}=:\varepsilon(\mathbf{q})+\mathrm{i}\delta(\mathbf{q})=:g(\mathbf{q}).

(165)

\varepsilon(\mathbf{q})=J_{x}\cos q_{x}+J_{y}\cos q_{y}+J_{z},\quad\delta(\mathbf{q})=J_{x}\sin q_{x}+J_{y}\sin q_{y},\quad\widehat{J}_{BA}(\mathbf{q})=-\widehat{J}_{AB}^{*}(\mathbf{q})=-\varepsilon(\mathbf{q})+\mathrm{i}\delta(\mathbf{q}).

(166)

Correspondingly, we take
\mathbf{s}=(s_{x},s_{y})=s_{x}\mathbf{n}_{1}+s_{y}\mathbf{n}_{2}
where
s_{x},s_{y}=-\frac{L-1}{2},\cdots,\frac{L-1}{2}
.

We see that
\delta(\mathbf{q})
is odd and
\varepsilon(\mathbf{q})
is even in
\mathbf{q}
. Therefore
g(-\mathbf{q})=g^{\ast}(\mathbf{q}).
The Hamiltonian can be written as

H=\mathrm{i}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\left(g(\mathbf{q})a_{\mathbf{q},A}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{q},B}-g^{*}(\mathbf{q})a_{\mathbf{q},B}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{q},A}\right).

(167)

Applying the Bogoliubov transformation to decouple the
\mathbf{q}
and
-\mathbf{q}
modes in
Eq.

163
, we obtain

\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix c_{\bv q A} \\ c_{\bv q B}\endlx@physics@matrix)=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix u_{\bv q} & v_{\bv q} \\ v_{\bv q}^* & -u_{\bv q}^*\endlx@physics@matrix)\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix a_{\bv q, A} \\ a_{\bv q, B}\endlx@physics@matrix),\quad u_{\mathbf{q}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\quad v_{\mathbf{q}}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\sqrt{2}}e^{\mathrm{i}\arg g(\mathbf{q})},

(168)

H=\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)}(c_{\mathbf{q}A}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{q}A}-c_{\mathbf{q}B}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{q}B}).

(169)

The new operators also satisfy the canonical anti-commutation relations. The modes on two sublattices
A
and
B
are related by

c_{-\mathbf{q},A}=2u_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}v_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}c_{\mathbf{q},B}^{\dagger}
and
c_{\mathbf{q}A}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{q}A}=1-c_{-\mathbf{q},B}^{\dagger}c_{-\mathbf{q},B}
. The first relation is because

c_{-\mathbf{q},A}=u_{-\mathbf{q}}a_{\mathbf{q},A}^{\dagger}+v_{-\mathbf{q}}a_{\mathbf{q},B}^{\dagger}=u_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}a_{\mathbf{q},A}^{\dagger}-v_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}a_{\mathbf{q},B}^{\dagger}

(170)

and

2u_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}v_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}c_{\mathbf{q},B}^{\dagger}=2u_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}v_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}(v_{\mathbf{q}}a_{\mathbf{q}A}^{\dagger}-u_{\mathbf{q}}a_{\mathbf{q}B}^{\dagger})=2u_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}\left(\frac{1}{2}a_{\mathbf{q}A}^{\dagger}-u_{\mathbf{q}}v_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}a_{\mathbf{q}B}^{\dagger}\right)=u_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}a_{\mathbf{q},A}^{\dagger}-v_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}a_{\mathbf{q},B}^{\dagger}.

(171)

This implies

c_{\mathbf{q},A}=2u_{-\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}v_{-\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}c_{-\mathbf{q},B}^{\dagger}=-2u_{\mathbf{q}}v_{\mathbf{q}}c_{-\mathbf{q},B}^{\dagger}.

(172)

After relabeling
\mathbf{q}\mapsto-\mathbf{q}
in the first occupation term, we can express the Hamiltonian as

H=\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)}(1-2c_{\mathbf{q}B}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{q}B}).

(173)

Note that this Hamiltonian only depends on
c_{\mathbf{q}B}
operators which should be understood as quasiparticle degrees of freedom. The ground state is given by the fully filled state of the
c_{\mathbf{q}B}
modes, namely

\ket{\text{GS}}=\prod_{\mathbf{q}}c_{\mathbf{q}B}^{\dagger}\ket{0}.

(174)

The system is gapless if and only if there exists some
\mathbf{q}\in\text{BZ}
such that
{g}(\mathbf{q})=0
in the continuum limit.
This is equivalent to the inequalities
|J_{x}|\leq|J_{y}|+|J_{z}|
,
|J_{y}|\leq|J_{x}|+|J_{z}|
and
|J_{z}|\leq|J_{x}|+|J_{y}|
. Outside this parameter regime, the system is gapped.

Appendix F
Details of topological phase transitions in the Kitaev honeycomb model

F.1
Chern number and purity gap

We could probe the topological properties of the states along the dissipative dynamics by evaluating the Chern number of the covariance matrix
\Gamma
in the real space, defined as

\Gamma_{\mathbf{s}\mu,\mathbf{t}\nu}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\Tr\left(\rho[\eta_{\mathbf{s}\mu},\eta_{\mathbf{t}\nu}]\right)=\mathrm{i}\langle\eta_{\mathbf{s}\mu}\eta_{\mathbf{t}\nu}\rangle-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\delta_{\mathbf{s}\mu,\mathbf{t}\nu},\quad\mu,\nu=A,B,

(175)

which only involves the two-point correlation functions in the real space and can be efficiently measured in experiments such as quantum simulations on neutral atom platform
Evered et al. 2025
. We will discuss the details of the simulation of the dissipative dynamics in
Section

G.2
.

Because of translation invariance, we could write
\Gamma_{\mathbf{s}\mu,\mathbf{t}\nu}=\Gamma_{\mathbf{0}\mu,\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{s},\nu}=:\Gamma_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{s})
. Thus we can define the
2\times 2
Bloch covariance matrix
\Gamma(\mathbf{q})
for each
\mathbf{q}
in the first Brillouin zone, obtained from the Fourier transform

\Gamma_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{q})=\sum_{\mathbf{s}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{s}}\Gamma_{\mathbf{0}\mu,\mathbf{s}\nu}.

(176)

Here, with a slight abuse of notation, we use the same symbol
\Gamma
to denote both the real-space covariance matrix and the Bloch representation in the momentum space.

We define the parent Hamiltonian
H_{\rm p}
associated with the covariance matrix
\Gamma
as

\displaystyle H_{\rm p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t};\mu,\nu\in\{A,B\}}\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{s})\eta_{\mathbf{s}\mu}\eta_{\mathbf{t}\nu}=\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\sum_{\mu,\nu\in\{A,B\}}2\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{q})a_{-\mathbf{q}\mu}a_{\mathbf{q}\nu}

(177)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\sum_{\mu,\nu\in\{A,B\}}2\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{q})a_{\mathbf{q}\mu}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{q}\nu}=\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\mathbf{a}_{\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{a}_{\mathbf{q}},

where the Bloch Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q})
is a
2\times 2
Hermitian matrix defined as

\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q}):=2\mathrm{i}\Gamma(\mathbf{q})=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 2\I\Gamma_{AA}(\bv q) & 2\I\Gamma_{AB}(\bv q) \\ 2\I\Gamma_{BA}(\bv q) & 2\I\Gamma_{BB}(\bv q)\endlx@physics@matrix),\quad\mathbf{a}_{\mathbf{q}}:=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix a_{\bv qA} \\ a_{\bv qB}\endlx@physics@matrix).

(178)

We consider the single occupied band of
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q})
. Let
\ket{n(\mathbf{q})}
denote the normalized negative energy eigenstate of
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q})
, then the Berry potential or connection
\mathscr{A}
is defined as

\mathscr{A}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{q})=\innerproduct{n(\bv q)}{\partial_{q_\alpha}n(\bv q)},\quad\alpha=x,y,

(179)

and the gauge-invariant Berry curvature, or the flux density is given by

\mathscr{F}_{xy}(\mathbf{q})=\partial_{q_{x}}\mathscr{A}_{y}(\mathbf{q})-\partial_{q_{y}}\mathscr{A}_{x}(\mathbf{q}).

(180)

The Chern number is then given by the integral of the Berry curvature over the first Brillouin zone:

\mathcal{C}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\iint_{\rm BZ}\mathscr{F}_{xy}(\mathbf{q})\differential q_{x}\differential q_{y}.

(181)

The Chern number could also be formulated using the projection operator
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{q})=\ket{n(\mathbf{q})}\bra{n(\mathbf{q})}
onto the single-occupied band

Bardyn et al. 2013
. We note that for any pure state, the covariance matrix
\Gamma
and thus the Bloch covariance matrix
\Gamma(\mathbf{q})
satisfies
\Gamma^{2}=-\frac{1}{4}I
. This implies that
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q})=2\mathrm{i}\Gamma(\mathbf{q})
satisfies
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathbf{q})=I
. Therefore,
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q})
is a reflection and
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{q})
could be directly defined as
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{2}(I-\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q}))
without explicitly computing the eigenstates. For general mixed states, we need to “flatten” the energy spectrum of
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q})
by applying the sign transform

{\mathcal{H}}^{\flat}(\mathbf{q}):=\mathrm{sgn}(\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q}))

(182)

where
\mathrm{sgn}(\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q}))
is understood as a functional calculus with the
\mathrm{sgn}
function applied to the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q})
. The flattened
{\mathcal{H}}^{\flat}(\mathbf{q})
is now a reflection and the projection operator
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{q})
can be defined as
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{2}(I-{\mathcal{H}}^{\flat}(\mathbf{q}))
.
The Chern number can then be computed as

\mathcal{C}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\iint_{\rm BZ}\Tr\left(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{q})[\partial_{q_{x}}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{q}),\partial_{q_{y}}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{q})]\right)\differential q_{x}\differential q_{y}.

(183)

The formula
Eq.

183
can be generalized to the multi-occupied band case, where
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{q})
is the projection operator onto the occupied bands.

In practice, we are always working with a finite lattice, or equivalently, a discretized Brillouin zone. Due to the highly concentrated nature of the Berry curvature, a naive finite difference scheme for discretizing the Berry curvature may not be able to capture the spikes at the Dirac points, which could completely miss the topological properties. Instead, we adopt the more stable specialized Fukui–Hatsugai–Suzuki (FHS)
scheme for computing the Chern number on a discretized Brillouin zone, as described in
Fukui et al. 2005
. For simplicity, we only consider the single-occupied case. The FHS scheme defines the link variable as

\mathscr{U}_{x}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{\innerproduct{n(\bv q)}{n(\bv q + \bv x)}}{|\innerproduct{n(\bv q)}{n(\bv q + \bv x)}|},\quad\mathscr{U}_{y}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{\innerproduct{n(\bv q)}{n(\bv q + \bv y)}}{|\innerproduct{n(\bv q)}{n(\bv q + \bv y)}|},

(184)

where
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
denote one mesh step in the
q_{x}
and
q_{y}
directions, respectively. The discretized Berry curvature is then given by

\mathscr{F}_{xy}(\mathbf{q})=\log\left(\mathscr{U}_{x}(\mathbf{q})\mathscr{U}_{y}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{x})\mathscr{U}_{x}^{-1}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{y})\mathscr{U}_{y}^{-1}(\mathbf{q})\right),

(185)

where the principal branch of the logarithm is taken to ensure that
\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}}\mathscr{F}_{xy}(\mathbf{q})
is in the range
(-\pi,\pi]
. The Chern number is then given by
\mathcal{C}=(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\mathscr{F}_{xy}(\mathbf{q})
.
The FHS scheme is stable and gives an integer-valued lattice Chern number whenever the link variables are well defined. This allows us to reliably extract the topological properties even with a coarse discretization of the Brillouin zone.

It is also informative to analyze the purity spectrum of a state with covariance matrix
\Gamma
. We define the purity gap as the smallest eigenvalue of the positive semidefinite matrix
(2\mathrm{i}\Gamma)^{2}

\Delta_{P}(\Gamma)=\min\{\lambda\geq 0:\lambda\in\mathrm{Spec}[(2\mathrm{i}\Gamma)^{2}]\}=\min\{\lambda\geq 0:\lambda\in\mathrm{Spec}(-4\Gamma^{2})\}.

(186)

In particular, for a pure state,
\Delta_{P}(\Gamma)=1
and for a maximally mixed state,
\Delta_{P}(\Gamma)=0
.
As discussed in
Diehl et al. 2011
;
Bardyn et al. 2013
, two Gaussian states with correlation matrices
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
are
topologically equivalent
if they can be connected continuously without closing the bulk purity gap. Unlike the earlier work
Bardyn et al. 2013
, we focus here on dynamical evolution rather than only the non-equilibrium steady states. A transient closing of the purity gap during the dynamics signals a change in topological class and thus marks a possible dynamical topological transition. Tracking the purity spectrum therefore provides complementary information to the Chern number and helps distinguish true topological changes from finite-size or numerical artifacts.

F.2
A simple mechanism for dissipative preparation of Chern insulators

We explain how dissipative protocols can evade the unitary topological obstruction to preparing a Chern insulator. Consider a general two-band Chern insulator with Bloch Hamiltonian
Qi and Zhang 2011

H(\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{q})\cdot\bm{\sigma}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix d_z(\bv q) & d_x(\bv q) - \I d_y(\bv q) \\
d_x(\bv q) + \I d_y(\bv q) & -d_z(\bv q)\endlx@physics@matrix),\quad\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z}),

(187)

The Qi–Wu–Zhang model
Qi et al. 2006
, the Haldane model
Haldane 1988
, and the Kitaev honeycomb model with explicitly broken time-reversal symmetry
Kitaev 2006
can all be written in this form. If the two bands are separated, then
\absolutevalue{\mathbf d(\bv q)}\neq 0
throughout the Brillouin zone, and the topology is encoded by the normalized map

\widehat{\mathbf{d}}(\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{q})/\absolutevalue{\mathbf d(\bv q)}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Here
\mathbb{T}^{2}:=(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}
denotes the two-dimensional torus and
\mathbb{S}^{2}:=\{\absolutevalue{x}=1:x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\}
denotes the two-dimensional sphere.
Equivalently, the occupied-band projection is

\mathcal{P}_{-}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{2}(I-\widehat{\mathbf{d}}(\mathbf{q})\cdot\bm{\sigma})
, whose first Chern number is the degree of
\widehat{\mathbf{d}}
, up to the occupied-band sign convention.

For a free-fermionic Gaussian state with Bloch covariance matrix
\Gamma(\mathbf{q})
, the associated parent Hamiltonian is
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q})=2\mathrm{i}\Gamma(\mathbf{q})
, which we write as

\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q})\cdot\bm{\sigma}
.
If the state is pure, then
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q})^{2}=I
, equivalently
\absolutevalue{\mathbf h(\bv q)}=1
, and
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{2}(I-\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q}))

is a rank-one projection. Its Chern number is

\mathcal{C}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\iint_{\rm BZ}\Tr\left(\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{q})[\partial_{q_{x}}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{q}),\partial_{q_{y}}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{q})]\right)\differential q_{x}\differential q_{y}.

(188)

In the two-band case this equals the winding number, or degree, of

\mathbf{h}:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Thus the projection formula and the Bloch-sphere winding description give the same integer invariant for pure states.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{L}(\mathbf{q})
denote the finite-size data on an
L\times L
torus. By adding twists,
\mathcal{P}_{L}(\mathbf{q})
can be defined for continuous
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
. When the covariance matrix is pure,
\mathcal{P}_{L}(\mathbf{q})
is a projection, and the finite-size Chern number
\mathcal{C}_{L}
is defined by the same formula as in
Eq.

188
.

Under quasi-local unitary dynamics generated by a translation-invariant free-fermionic Hamiltonian, purity is preserved, so
\mathcal{P}_{L}(\mathbf{q};t)
remains a smooth family of projections. Quasi-locality gives uniform bounds, independent of
L
, on the relevant momentum derivatives over any finite time window
t\in[0,T]
with
T
fixed. Together with convergence of the finite-size covariance data and Chern integrands to the thermodynamic limit, this allows the use of dominated convergence and gives

\mathcal{C}(t)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathcal{C}_{L}(t)
.
For each fixed
L
, the map
\mathbf{q}\mapsto\mathcal{P}_{L}(\mathbf{q};t)
is a smooth homotopy from
\mathbb{T}^{2}
to the space of rank-one projections, naturally identified with
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Since the Chern number is invariant under such homotopies,

\mathcal{C}_{L}(t)=\mathcal{C}_{L}(0)
, and therefore
\mathcal{C}(t)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathcal{C}_{L}(t)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathcal{C}_{L}(0)=\mathcal{C}(0).

Thus quasi-local unitary evolution cannot transform a trivial pure state into a pure Chern insulator with a different Chern number in finite time.

Under quasi-local Lindblad dynamics, by contrast, the covariance matrix is generally mixed. Then
\mathcal{P}_{L}(\mathbf{q};t)=\frac{1}{2}(I-\mathcal{H}_{L}(\mathbf{q};t))

need not be a projection. In the two-band parameterization, the Bloch vector

\mathbf{h}_{L}(\mathbf{q};t)
is no longer confined to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, but may move inside the Bloch ball

\mathbb{B}^{3}=\{\absolutevalue{x}\leq 1:x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\}
.
The eigenvalues of
\mathcal{P}_{L}(\mathbf{q};t)
are

\frac{1}{2}(1\pm\absolutevalue{\mathbf h_L(\bv q;t)})
, so
\mathcal{P}_{L}
is a projection only when

\absolutevalue{\mathbf h_L(\bv q;t)}=1
.
Nevertheless, if
\mathbf{h}_{L}(\mathbf{q};t)\neq 0
for all
\mathbf{q}
, one can still define the topological invariant by normalizing

\mathbf{h}_{L}\mapsto\mathbf{h}_{L}/\absolutevalue{\mathbf h_L}
, or equivalently by using the corresponding flattened projection, as discussed in Appendix
F.1
. This requires a nonzero purity gap
\Delta_{P}(t)>0
. As long as this gap remains open, the normalized projection gives a smooth homotopy

\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
, with quasi-locality again ensuring bounded momentum derivatives, and its Chern number cannot change. A change is possible only when the purity gap closes, namely when

\mathbf{h}_{L}(\mathbf{q}_{*};t_{*})=0
at some momentum
\mathbf{q}_{*}
and time
t_{*}
. At this point the normalized vector, and hence the flattened projection, is undefined.
This is the topological mechanism by which quasi-local Lindblad dynamics can evade the unitary Chern-number obstruction
Goldstein 2019
: the mixed-state Chern number is not conserved because the state may leave the sphere of pure covariance matrices, pass through the interior of the Bloch ball, and become undefined at a purity-gap closing.

F.3
Probing the topological properties with realistic filters

In
Section

III.3
, we demonstrate that the ground-state topological phase of the Kitaev honeycomb model can be probed by computing the Chern number directly from finite-filter-resolution dissipative dynamics. Following the approach in
Bardyn et al. 2013
, computing the Chern number only requires distinguishing the momentum-space fermionic modes, and the excited and ground states of each mode from the real-space covariance matrix, or the parent Hamiltonian. This allows one to construct the flattened single-particle Hamiltonian and subsequently evaluate the topological invariant.

Recall that we choose the set of coupling operators to be
\{\sqrt{2}\eta_{\mathbf{s}\lambda}\}_{\mathbf{s},\lambda\in\{A,B\}}
, which implements fermionic bulk dissipation. Therefore, the mixing time of the dissipative dynamics toward the steady state is upper bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\log L)
(see Appendix
B.1
and
(
Zhan et al. 2026
, Theorem 1)
).

For the steady-state characterization, the results of Appendix
B.1
imply that the density matrix in the
c_{\mathbf{q}B}
-basis factorizes as

\rho_{\rm ss}=\bigotimes_{{2\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)}>\Delta}}\outerproduct{1}{1}_{\mathbf{q}B}\;\bigotimes_{{2\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)}\leq\Delta}}\left((1-p_{\mathbf{q}})\outerproduct{0}{0}_{\mathbf{q}B}+p_{\mathbf{q}}\outerproduct{1}{1}_{\mathbf{q}B}\right).

(189)

The occupation probability is

p_{\mathbf{q}}=\frac{\widehat{f}(-2\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)})^{2}}{\widehat{f}(-2\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)})^{2}+\widehat{f}(2\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)})^{2}}=\langle c_{\mathbf{q}B}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{q}B}\rangle_{\rm ss}.

(190)

We introduce the Majorana basis via

\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix c_{\bv qB}\\ c_{\bv qB}^\dag\endlx@physics@matrix)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 1&-\I\\1&\I\endlx@physics@matrix)\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix w_{\bv q,+}\\ w_{\bv q,-}\endlx@physics@matrix),\qquad\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix w_{\bv q,+}\\ w_{\bv q,-}\endlx@physics@matrix)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 1&1\\\I&-\I\endlx@physics@matrix)\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix c_{\bv qB}\\ c_{\bv qB}^\dag\endlx@physics@matrix).

(191)

The covariance matrix in this Majorana basis is

\Gamma^{w}_{(\mathbf{p},\bullet),(\mathbf{q},\circ)}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\big(\rho\,[w_{\mathbf{p},\bullet},w_{\mathbf{q},\circ}]\big)=\mathrm{i}\langle w_{\mathbf{p},\bullet}w_{\mathbf{q},\circ}\rangle-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\delta_{(\mathbf{p},\bullet),(\mathbf{q},\circ)},\quad\bullet,\circ=+,-.

(192)

Hence the steady-state Majorana covariance matrix is block-diagonal in momentum:

\Gamma^{w}_{\rm ss}=\bigoplus_{{2\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)}>\Delta}}\begin{pmatrix}0&-1/2\\
1/2&0\end{pmatrix}\;\bigoplus_{{2\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)}\leq\Delta}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1/2-p_{\mathbf{q}}\\
-1/2+p_{\mathbf{q}}&0\end{pmatrix}=:\bigoplus_{\mathbf{q}}\Gamma^{w}_{\rm ss}(\mathbf{q}).

(193)

Because
\Gamma^{w}_{\rm ss}(\mathbf{q})
is unitarily related to the Bloch covariance matrix
\Gamma_{\rm ss}(\mathbf{q})
in
Eq.

176
, the matrices
2\mathrm{i}\Gamma^{w}_{\rm ss}(\mathbf{q})
and
\mathcal{H}_{\rm ss}(\mathbf{q})=2\mathrm{i}\Gamma_{\rm ss}(\mathbf{q})
share the same spectrum. Hence

\lambda_{\pm}(\mathbf{q})=\pm\left(2p_{\mathbf{q}}-1\right),

(194)

with
p_{\mathbf{q}}=1
for fully occupied modes. In the ideal-filter limit
\lambda_{\pm}(\mathbf{q})=\pm 1
for all
\mathbf{q}
, so the steady state is pure and coincides with the ground state. For a realistic filter we must ensure the bands are correctly resolved, i.e. the positive and negative eigenvalues of
2\mathrm{i}\Gamma^{w}_{\rm ss}(\mathbf{q})
stay separated from zero, equivalently
p_{\mathbf{q}}>1/2
for all
\mathbf{q}
.

For the gapped topologically trivial phase (
J_{z}>0.5,J_{x}=J_{y}=\frac{1}{2}(1-J_{z})
), the minimal excitation energy
\min_{\mathbf{q}}2\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)}
is lower bounded by a positive constant independent of the system size. Therefore, if we choose
\Delta
to be smaller than this constant, the steady state for an exact finite-resolution cutoff is pure and coincides with the ground state. However, in the gapless topologically nontrivial phase (
J_{z}<0.5,J_{x}=J_{y}=\frac{1}{2}(1-J_{z})
), the minimal excitation energy
\min_{\mathbf{q}}2\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)}
scales as
\mathcal{O}(1/L)
according to
Eq.

165
, therefore we cannot prepare the pure ground state even in the infinite-time limit of the Lindblad dynamics with any finite
\Delta
for sufficiently large system size. This suggests that our
topological diagnosis essentially relies on the extended definition of the Chern number for mixed states.

Assuming the filter construction of Appendix
A
and accounting for measurement error in real-space correlations, we additionally require

p_{\mathbf{q}}=\langle c_{\mathbf{q}B}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{q}B}\rangle_{\rm ss}>\tfrac{1}{2}+\epsilon,

(195)

where
\epsilon
denotes the additive measurement error (including Fourier and Bogoliubov transformations). Equivalently,

\frac{\gamma_{h,\mathbf{q}}^{2}}{\gamma_{c,\mathbf{q}}^{2}}:=\frac{\widehat{f}(2\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)})^{2}}{\widehat{f}(-2\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)})^{2}}<\frac{1-2\epsilon}{1+2\epsilon}.

(196)

It suffices to consider the smallest mode energy
\min_{\mathbf{q}}2\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)}=\mathcal{O}(1/L)
in the gapless topological regime. Then one can approximate

\widehat{f}(\pm 2\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)})\approx\widehat{f}(0)\pm 2\widehat{f}^{\prime}(0)\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\mp\mathcal{O}\big(1/(L\Delta)\big).

(197)

Hence the margin
p_{\mathbf{q}}-1/2
at the lowest mode is only
\mathcal{O}(1/(L\Delta))
, which implies the covariance matrix elements must be measured to
\mathcal{O}(1/(L\Delta))
precision, or
\mathcal{O}(1/L)
precision when
\Delta
is fixed.

Appendix G
Solving fermionic quasi-free Lindblad dynamics

In this section,
we provide details on how to solve the Lindblad dynamics for fermionic quasi-free systems. In Appendix
G.1
we will discuss the XX model (i.e. the XXZ model in
Section

III.4
with
J_{z}=0
) with fermionic bulk cooling, and in Appendix
G.2
we will discuss the Kitaev honeycomb model with local Majorana cooling.

A system is called quasi-free if the Hamiltonian is quadratic and the jump operators are linear in the fermionic creation and annihilation operators. For comprehensive and detailed reviews on quasi-free systems, we refer readers to Refs.
Prosen 2008
;
Prosen and Žunkovič 2010
;
Barthel and Zhang 2022
. A key property of a quasi-free system is that the dynamics of the system can be fully characterized by the two-point correlation functions. In particular, if the system is particle-number preserving, it can be fully characterized only by the one-particle reduced density matrix (1-RDM).

G.1
The XX model with bulk cooling

G.1.1
Solving the 1-RDM dynamics

We recall that the XX model can be fermionized into a free fermionic model by the Jordan–Wigner transformation, and the resulting Hamiltonian is given by
Eq.

26
.
We consider the bulk cooling, where the coupling operators are chosen as the local fermionic creation and annihilation operators
\{c_{i},c_{i}^{\dagger}\}_{i=1}^{L}
. The dynamics of the system can be fully characterized by the 1-RDM
P(t)
,
which consists of two-point correlation functions of the following form
Li et al. 2025
:

P_{ij}(t)=\Tr(\rho(t) c_j^\dagger c_i),\quad i,j=1,\cdots,L.

(198)

We consider both open boundary conditions (OBCs) and periodic boundary conditions (PBCs). In the PBC case, the wraparound term introduces

S_{L}^{x}S_{1}^{x}+S_{L}^{y}S_{1}^{y}=-\frac{1}{2}(c_{L}^{\dagger}c_{1}+c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{L})\exp(\I\pi\sum_j c_j^\dag c_j),

(199)

and since we are working in the half-filling sector, we have
\exp(\I\pi\sum_j c_j^\dag c_j)=\exp(\I\pi L/2)=(-1)^{L/2}
. Thus the coefficient matrix of the Hamiltonian is given by

F={\frac{J_{xy}}{2}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&p\\
1&0&1&\cdots&0\\
0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&1\\
p&0&0&1&0\end{pmatrix}\text{\quad where\quad}p=\begin{cases}1&\text{{PBCs}, and $L=2\mod 4$;}\\
-1&\text{{PBCs}, and $L=0\mod 4$;}\\
0&\text{{OBCs}.}\end{cases}

(200)

For simplicity, we only discuss the case where
L
is a multiple of
4
and take
J_{xy}=1
. Then we have the following explicit form of the diagonalization of
F=U\Lambda U^{\dagger}
. For OBC,

\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\varepsilon_{i})=\mathrm{diag}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix- \cos\frac{\pi}{L+1},\cdots- \cos\frac{L\pi}{L+1}\endlx@physics@matrix),\quad U=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix
\sqrt{\frac{2}{L+1}} \sin\frac{j(L+1-k)\pi}{L+1}
\endlx@physics@matrix)_{j,k=1}^{L},

(201)

For PBC with
L=0\mod 4
,

\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\varepsilon_{i})=\operatorname{diag}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix- \cos\frac{\pi}{L},- \cos\frac{\pi}{L},- \cos\frac{3\pi}{L}, - \cos\frac{3\pi}{L},\cdots, - \cos\frac{(L-1)\pi}{L}, - \cos\frac{(L-1)\pi}{L} \endlx@physics@matrix),

(202)

U=\begin{pmatrix}|&|&&|\\
u_{1}&u_{2}&\cdots&{u_{L}}\\
|&|&&|\end{pmatrix},\quad u_{2m-1}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\sqrt{\frac 2L}\cos\frac{(L-j)(L-2m+1)}{L}\pi\endlx@physics@matrix)_{j=1}^{L},\quad u_{2m}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\sqrt{\frac 2L}\sin\frac{(L-j)(L-2m+1)}{L}\pi\endlx@physics@matrix)_{j=1}^{L}.

(203)

We define the transformed fermionic modes as

\tilde{c}_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{L}U_{jk}^{*}c_{j},\quad\tilde{c}_{k}^{\dagger}=\sum_{j=1}^{L}U_{jk}c_{j}^{\dagger},\quad H=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\varepsilon_{k}\tilde{c}_{k}^{\dagger}\tilde{c}_{k},

(204)

and assume that
L
is even for simplicity, then

\varepsilon_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\varepsilon_{L/2}<0<\varepsilon_{L/2+1}\leq\cdots\leq\varepsilon_{L}.

(205)

Thus the ground state of the system is given by the half-filling state

\ket{\psi_{0}}=\prod_{k=1}^{L/2}\tilde{c}_{k}^{\dagger}\ket{0}

(206)

and, for OBC, the spectral gap of the Hamiltonian
is given by

\Delta_{H}=\varepsilon_{L/2+1}-\varepsilon_{L/2}=2\sin\frac{\pi}{2(L+1)}\sim\frac{1}{L}\quad(L\to\infty).

(207)

Using
Eq.

55
, the jump operators can be expressed as

\displaystyle K_{i}^{+}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s)c_{i}^{\dagger}(s)\differential s=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s)(e^{\mathrm{i}Fs})_{ji}c_{j}^{\dagger}\differential s=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\widehat{f}(F)_{ji}c_{j}^{\dagger},

(208)

\displaystyle K_{i}^{-}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s)c_{i}(s)\differential s=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s)(e^{-\mathrm{i}Fs})_{ij}c_{j}\differential s=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\widehat{f}(-F)_{ij}c_{j}.

Therefore, the jump operators are also linear in the fermionic creation and annihilation operators, and the resulting Lindblad dynamics is quasi-free. In the Heisenberg picture, the Hilbert–Schmidt adjoint of
\mathcal{L}
is given by

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[O]=\mathrm{i}[H,O]+\sum_{i=1}^{L}\left(K_{i}^{+\dagger}OK_{i}^{+}-\frac{1}{2}\{K_{i}^{+\dagger}K_{i}^{+},O\}+K_{i}^{-\dagger}OK_{i}^{-}-\frac{1}{2}\{K_{i}^{-\dagger}K_{i}^{-},O\}\right).

(209)

Plugging in
O=c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{i}
, using the canonical anticommutation relations (CAR) to simplify, and then taking the expectation value with respect to the state
\rho(t)
, we get a closed-form equation for the 1-RDM
P(t)

Li et al. 2025

\frac{\differential}{\differential t}P(t)=-\mathrm{i}[F,P(t)]+M^{+}-\frac{1}{2}\{P(t),M^{+}+M^{-}\}

(210)

where the matrices
M^{+}
and
M^{-}
are given by

(M^{+})_{pq}=\sum_{k}\widehat{f}(F)_{pk}{\widehat{f}(F)^{*}_{qk}},\quad(M^{-})_{pq}=\sum_{k}\widehat{f}(-F)_{pk}{\widehat{f}(-F)^{*}_{qk}},\quad\text{i.e.}\quad M^{\pm}=\widehat{f}(\pm F)\widehat{f}(\pm F)^{\dagger}.

(211)

We choose the “pure Néel state” as the initial state.
In the fermionic language, it can be expressed as
\ket{\psi_{\text{ini}}}=\prod_{j=1}^{L/2}c_{2j-1}^{\dagger}\ket{0}_{c}
, and the corresponding 1-RDM is given by

P_{ij}(0)=\Tr(\ket{\psi_{\text{ini}}}\bra{\psi_{\text{ini}}} c_j^\dagger c_i)\bra{\psi_{\text{ini}}}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{i}=\delta_{ij}\cdot\begin{cases}1&i\text{ is odd}\\
0&i\text{ is even}\end{cases}.

(212)

Therefore
\Tr P(0)=L/2
and the initial state is half-filled.

In this case, the initial state is a Slater determinant state and in particular, a Gaussian pure state. Since the dynamics is quasi-free, the state at any time
t
is also Gaussian and the observables of interest can be computed from the 1-RDM
P(t)
using Wick’s theorem. For the spin correlation function
\langle S_{i}^{z}S_{j}^{z}\rangle
, when
i\neq j
,

\langle S_{i}^{z}S_{j}^{z}\rangle=\left\langle\left(c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right\rangle=\langle n_{i}\rangle\langle n_{j}\rangle-\langle c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}\rangle\langle c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{i}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}(\langle n_{i}\rangle+\langle n_{j}\rangle)+\frac{1}{4}=P_{ii}P_{jj}-|P_{ij}|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(P_{ii}+P_{jj})+\frac{1}{4},

(213)

and when
i=j
, using
n_{i}^{2}=n_{i}
,

\langle S_{i}^{z}S_{i}^{z}\rangle=\left\langle\left(c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\right\rangle=\langle n_{i}\rangle-\langle n_{i}\rangle+\frac{1}{4}=\frac{1}{4}.

(214)

Together with
\langle S_{i}^{z}\rangle=\langle n_{i}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}=P_{ii}-\frac{1}{2}
, we can obtain the connected spin correlation function
\langle S_{i}^{z}S_{j}^{z}\rangle-\langle S_{i}^{z}\rangle\langle S_{j}^{z}\rangle
which is needed to compute the subsystem bipartite spin fluctuation
\mathcal{F}(\ell)
.

We are also interested in the fidelity of the evolved state
\rho(t)
with respect to the true ground state
Eq.

206
, whose 1-RDM is given by

P_{ij}^{\text{GS}}=\bra{\psi_{\rm GS}}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{i}\ket{\psi_{\rm GS}}=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{L}U_{jk}^{*}U_{i\ell}\bra{\psi_{\rm GS}}\tilde{c}_{k}^{\dagger}\tilde{c}_{\ell}\ket{\psi_{\rm GS}}=\sum_{k=1}^{L/2}U_{jk}^{*}U_{ik}.

(215)

We note that in the limit of ideal filtering, we have
\widehat{f}(-F)=U\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 0&0\\0&I_{\frac L2}\endlx@physics@matrix)U^{\dagger}
and
\widehat{f}(F)=U\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix I_{\frac L2}&0\\0&0\endlx@physics@matrix)U^{\dagger}
, thus the steady state
P_{\rm ss}
of
Eq.

210
satisfies

-\mathrm{i}[F,P_{\rm ss}]+U\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix I_{\frac L2}&0\\0&0\endlx@physics@matrix)U^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{P_{\rm ss},I_{L}\}=0.

(216)

The solution of the Sylvester-type equation is given by

P_{\rm ss}=U\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix I_{\frac L2}&0\\0&0\endlx@physics@matrix)U^{\dagger}=P^{\text{GS}}.

(217)

Note that for free-fermionic mixed states, the trace distance to the ground state can be controlled by the trace-norm difference of the corresponding covariance data, see
(
Bittel et al. 2025b
, Theorem 1)
.
Therefore we can study the convergence of the evolved state to the true ground state by looking at the convergence of the 1-RDM
P(t)
to
P^{\text{GS}}
in the trace norm, as is done in
Fig.

7(c)
.

G.1.2
Derivation of error in 1-RDM

In this subsection we compute the difference between dissipative steady state and ground state expectation values of the 1-RDM
\braket{c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}}
.

Using
Eq.

201
for OBC, we can write

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}\rho\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l}U_{ik}^{*}U_{jl}{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(\tilde{c}_{k}^{\dagger}\tilde{c}_{l}\rho\right)=\sum_{k}U_{ik}^{*}U_{jk}\braket{\widetilde{n}_{k}}

(218)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\frac{2}{L+1}\sin\left(\frac{i(L+1-k)}{L+1}\pi\right)\sin\left(\frac{j(L+1-k)}{L+1}\pi\right)\braket{\widetilde{n}_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L+1}\sum_{k}\left[\cos\left(\frac{(i-j)(L+1-k)}{L+1}\pi\right)-\cos\left(\frac{(i+j)(L+1-k)}{L+1}\pi\right)\right]\braket{\widetilde{n}_{k}}.

Since
\braket{\widetilde{n}_{k}}_{\rm ss}-\braket{\widetilde{n}_{k}}_{\rm GS}=0
for all
|\varepsilon_{k}|>\Delta
, we have

\displaystyle\braket{c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}}_{\rm ss}-\braket{c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}}_{\rm GS}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L+1}\sum_{|\varepsilon_{k}|<\Delta}\left[\cos\left(\frac{(i-j)(L+1-k)}{L+1}\pi\right)-\cos\left(\frac{(i+j)(L+1-k)}{L+1}\pi\right)\right]\left(\braket{\widetilde{n}_{k}}_{\rm ss}-\braket{\widetilde{n}_{k}}_{\rm GS}\right)

(219)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\arcsin(\Delta)}^{\arcsin(\Delta)}\left[\cos\left((i-j)(\pi/2-x)\right)-\cos\left((i+j)(\pi/2-x)\right)\right]\varphi(x){\mathrm{d}}x

where

\varphi(x):=\widehat{f}(x)^{2}/\left(\widehat{f}(x)^{2}+\widehat{f}(-x)^{2}\right)-\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,0]}(x)
accounts for the difference between the realistic and ideal filters. In particular, for

q_{k}:=k\pi/(L+1)-\pi/2
, we have
\varphi(q_{k})=\left(\braket{\widetilde{n}_{k}}_{\rm ss}-\braket{\widetilde{n}_{k}}_{\rm GS}\right)
and
\varphi
is supported in
(-\Delta,\Delta)
for an exact finite-resolution cutoff.

According to
Eq.

65
, we have
\varphi(x)=-\varphi(-x)
and
|\varphi(x)|\leq 1
. When we focus on the bulk of the system, i.e., when
i+j
is large, the second term with
i+j
will vanish due to the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma. For the first term, when
i-j
is odd, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{-\arcsin(\Delta)}^{\arcsin(\Delta)}\cos\left((i-j)(\pi/2-x)\right)\varphi(x){\mathrm{d}}x\right|

(220)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{-\arcsin(\Delta)}^{\arcsin(\Delta)}\sin\left((i-j)x\right)\varphi(x){\mathrm{d}}x\right|=\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2}|i-j|),

for small
\Delta
. When
i-j
is even, then the integral vanishes since
\varphi(x)=-\varphi(-x)
. Therefore we obtain that
|\braket{c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}}_{\rm ss}-\braket{c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}}_{\rm GS}|=\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2}|i-j|)
for small
\Delta
.

G.2
The Kitaev honeycomb model with local Majorana cooling

G.2.1
Solving the covariance matrix dynamics

We next discuss the Kitaev honeycomb model, where the jump operators are chosen as the local Majorana operators
\{\eta_{\mathbf{s}A},\eta_{\mathbf{s}B}\}_{\mathbf{s}}
. We recall that, after fixing the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge and diagonalizing the Hamiltonian as discussed in Appendix
E
, the Kitaev honeycomb model can be written as a quadratic fermionic form
Eq.

160
. They are related to the fermionic operators in the momentum space as

{\eta_{\mathbf{s}A}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{s}}v_{\mathbf{q}}(c_{\mathbf{q}B}-c_{-\mathbf{q}B}^{\dagger}),\quad\eta_{\mathbf{s}B}=-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{s}}u_{\mathbf{q}}(c_{\mathbf{q}B}+c_{-\mathbf{q}B}^{\dagger})},\quad u_{\mathbf{q}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\quad{v_{\mathbf{q}}=\frac{\mathrm{i}e^{\mathrm{i}\arg g(\mathbf{q})}}{\sqrt{2}}}.

(221)

The inverse transformation is given by

c_{\mathbf{q}B}=v_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}a_{\mathbf{q}A}-u_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}a_{\mathbf{q}B}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\mathbf{s}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{s}}(v_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}\eta_{\mathbf{s}A}-u_{\mathbf{q}}^{\ast}\eta_{\mathbf{s}B})=-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\mathbf{s}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{s}}\left(v_{-\mathbf{q}}\eta_{\mathbf{s}A}+u_{-\mathbf{q}}\eta_{\mathbf{s}B}\right).

(222)

c_{\mathbf{q}B}^{\dagger}=v_{\mathbf{q}}a_{-\mathbf{q}A}-u_{\mathbf{q}}a_{-\mathbf{q}B}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\mathbf{s}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{s}}\left(v_{\mathbf{q}}\eta_{\mathbf{s}A}-u_{\mathbf{q}}\eta_{\mathbf{s}B}\right).

(223)

Here the jump operators are hybrid linear combinations of the fermionic creation and annihilation operators, thus the resulting Lindblad dynamics is still quasi-free. However, the dynamics of the 1-RDM is not closed anymore since the cross terms that contain the “anomalous” correlation functions
\langle c_{\mathbf{q}B}c_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}B}\rangle
cannot be omitted. Therefore, we need to consider the covariance matrix that contains more information than the 1-RDM
Barthel and Zhang 2022
;
Zhan et al. 2026
. For this, we define the Majorana operators and the corresponding Majorana covariance matrix in the momentum space as follows (see also
Eq.

191
)

\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix c_{\bv qB}\\ c_{\bv qB}^\dag\endlx@physics@matrix)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 1&-\I\\1&\I\endlx@physics@matrix)\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix w_{\bv q,+}\\ w_{\bv q,-}\endlx@physics@matrix),\quad\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix w_{\bv q,+}\\ w_{\bv q,-}\endlx@physics@matrix)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 1&1\\\I&-\I\endlx@physics@matrix)\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix c_{\bv qB}\\ c_{\bv qB}^\dag\endlx@physics@matrix),

(224)

\Gamma^{w}_{(\mathbf{p},\bullet),(\mathbf{q},\circ)}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\rho[w_{\mathbf{p},\bullet},w_{\mathbf{q},\circ}])=\mathrm{i}\langle w_{\mathbf{p},\bullet}w_{\mathbf{q},\circ}\rangle-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\delta_{(\mathbf{p},\bullet),(\mathbf{q},\circ)},\quad\bullet,\circ=+,-.

(225)

With a slight abuse of notation, we drop the superscript
w
and denote the momentum-space covariance matrix by
\Gamma=\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\langle[w_{\mathbf{p},\bullet},w_{\mathbf{q},\circ}]\rangle\right)_{(\mathbf{p},\bullet),(\mathbf{q},\circ)}
throughout the following discussion for brevity. This notation should be distinguished from the real-space covariance matrix
\Gamma=\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\langle[\eta_{\mathbf{s}\mu},\eta_{\mathbf{t}\nu}]\rangle\right)_{\mathbf{s}\mu,\mathbf{t}\nu}
discussed in
Section

F.1
.

The Hamiltonian can be rewritten as

{H=\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\left({\mathrm{i}}\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)}w_{\mathbf{q},+}w_{\mathbf{q},-}{-}\mathrm{i}\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)}w_{\mathbf{q},-}w_{\mathbf{q},+}\right)}

(226)

Accordingly, the coefficient matrix here is given by

{G_{(\mathbf{q},+),(\mathbf{q},-)}={\mathrm{i}}\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)},\quad G_{(\mathbf{q},-),(\mathbf{q},+)}={-}\mathrm{i}\absolutevalue{g(\bv q)},\quad G_{(\mathbf{p},\bullet),(\mathbf{q},\circ)}=0\text{ for $\mathbf{p}\neq\mathbf{q}$}.}

(227)

The matrix
G
is Hermitian and anti-symmetric (in particular, it is purely imaginary). Now using the Heisenberg evolution of the Majorana operators

w_{i}(t)=e^{\mathrm{i}Ht}w_{i}e^{-\mathrm{i}Ht}=\sum_{j}(e^{-2\mathrm{i}Gt})_{ij}w_{j},\quad\text{for}\quad H=\sum_{i,j}G_{ij}w_{i}w_{j},

(228)

together with
Eqs.

221
and
224
, we can express the jump operators as a linear combination of Majorana operators in the momentum space as

\displaystyle K_{\mathbf{s}A}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sqrt{2}\eta_{\mathbf{s}A}(s)f(s)\differential s

(229)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p},\bullet\in\{\pm\}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{s}}v_{\mathbf{q}}\left(-\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(-\mathbf{q},+),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}-\mathrm{i}\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(-\mathbf{q},-),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}+\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(\mathbf{q},+),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}-\mathrm{i}\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(\mathbf{q},-),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}\right){w_{(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}}

\displaystyle K_{\mathbf{s}B}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sqrt{2}\eta_{\mathbf{s}B}(s)f(s)\differential s

(230)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p},\bullet\in\{\pm\}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{s}}u_{\mathbf{q}}\left(-\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(-\mathbf{q},+),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}-\mathrm{i}\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(-\mathbf{q},-),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}-\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(\mathbf{q},+),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}+\mathrm{i}\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(\mathbf{q},-),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}\right){w_{(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}}.

We define

\displaystyle E_{(\mathbf{p},\bullet),\mathbf{s}A}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{s}}v_{\mathbf{q}}\left(-\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(-\mathbf{q},+),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}-\mathrm{i}\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(-\mathbf{q},-),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}+\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(\mathbf{q},+),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}-\mathrm{i}\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(\mathbf{q},-),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}\right),

(231)

\displaystyle E_{(\mathbf{p},\bullet),\mathbf{s}B}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{s}}u_{\mathbf{q}}\left(-\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(-\mathbf{q},+),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}-\mathrm{i}\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(-\mathbf{q},-),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}-\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(\mathbf{q},+),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}+\mathrm{i}\widehat{f}(-2G)_{(\mathbf{q},-),(\mathbf{p},\bullet)}\right),

and

D_{(\mathbf{p},\bullet),(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},\bullet^{\prime})}=\sum_{\mathbf{s}}E_{(\mathbf{p},\bullet),\mathbf{s}A}E_{(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},\bullet^{\prime}),\mathbf{s}A}^{\ast}+E_{(\mathbf{p},\bullet),\mathbf{s}B}E_{(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},\bullet^{\prime}),\mathbf{s}B}^{\ast}.

(232)

Then the dynamics of the covariance matrix is given by
(
Barthel and Zhang 2022
, Proposition 1)

\derivative{\Gamma}{t}=X\Gamma+\Gamma X^{T}+Y,\quad X=-2\mathrm{i}G-D^{R},\quad Y=D^{I}.

(233)

Here,
D^{R}
and
D^{I}
are the
element-wise
real and imaginary parts of
D
, respectively.
The full-filled state corresponds to

{\Gamma^{\rm GS}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 0& - I_{L^2\times L^2}/2\\ I_{L^2\times L^2}/2& 0\endlx@physics@matrix).}

(234)

Again, according to
(
Bittel et al. 2025b
, Theorem 1)
, the distance to the ground state in the trace norm of the many-body density matrix is controlled by the covariance-matrix discrepancy, which justifies the study of the convergence of the covariance matrix to
\Gamma^{\rm GS}
in
Fig.

17(c)
.

we introduce the coefficients

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}_{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(-v_{\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{v}_{-\mathbf{q}}-v_{-\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{v}_{\mathbf{q}}+u_{-\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{u}_{\mathbf{q}}+u_{\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{u}_{-\mathbf{q}}\Big),\quad\mathsf{B}_{\mathbf{q}}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(v_{\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{v}_{-\mathbf{q}}+v_{-\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{v}_{\mathbf{q}}+u_{-\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{u}_{\mathbf{q}}+u_{\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{u}_{-\mathbf{q}}\Big),

(242)

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(-v_{\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{v}_{-\mathbf{q}}+v_{-\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{v}_{\mathbf{q}}-u_{-\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{u}_{\mathbf{q}}+u_{\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{u}_{-\mathbf{q}}\Big),\quad\mathsf{D}_{\mathbf{q}}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(v_{\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{v}_{-\mathbf{q}}-v_{-\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{v}_{\mathbf{q}}-u_{-\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{u}_{\mathbf{q}}+u_{\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{u}_{-\mathbf{q}}\Big).

Thus we have the final linear mapping from
\widetilde{w}
to
w
:

\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix w_{ \bv q,+ } \\ w_{ \bv q,- }\endlx@physics@matrix)=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\mathsf A_{\bv q} & \I\mathsf C_{\bv q} \\
-\I\mathsf C_{\bv q} & \mathsf A_{\bv q} \endlx@physics@matrix)\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\wt w_{(\bv q,+)} \\ \wt w_{(\bv q,-)} \endlx@physics@matrix)+\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\mathsf B_{\bv q} & -\I\mathsf D_{\bv q} \\-\I\mathsf D_{\bv q} & - \mathsf B_{\bv q} \endlx@physics@matrix)\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\wt w_{(-\bv q,+)} \\ \wt w_{(-\bv q,-)} \endlx@physics@matrix)=:\mathsf{T}_{\mathbf{q}}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\wt w_{(\bv q,+)} \\ \wt w_{(\bv q,-)} \endlx@physics@matrix)+\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{q}}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\wt w_{(-\bv q,+)} \\ \wt w_{(-\bv q,-)} \endlx@physics@matrix).

(243)

Consequently, the two-point correlation function transforms as follows:

\mathsf{W}_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}=\mathsf{X}_{\mathbf{q}}\widetilde{\mathsf{W}}_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\mathsf{X}_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}^{\dagger},\quad\mathsf{X}_{\mathbf{q}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{T}_{-\mathbf{q}}&\mathsf{S}_{-\mathbf{q}}\\
\mathsf{S}_{\mathbf{q}}&\mathsf{T}_{\mathbf{q}}\end{pmatrix}.

(244)

Here we introduce

\quad\mathsf{W}_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{W}_{(-\mathbf{q},+),(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime},+)}&\mathsf{W}_{(-\mathbf{q},+),(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime},-)}&\mathsf{W}_{(-\mathbf{q},+),(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},+)}&\mathsf{W}_{(-\mathbf{q},+),(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},-)}\\
\mathsf{W}_{(-\mathbf{q},-),(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime},+)}&\mathsf{W}_{(-\mathbf{q},-),(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime},-)}&\mathsf{W}_{(-\mathbf{q},-),(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},+)}&\mathsf{W}_{(-\mathbf{q},-),(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},-)}\\
\mathsf{W}_{(\mathbf{q},+),(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime},+)}&\mathsf{W}_{(\mathbf{q},+),(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime},-)}&\mathsf{W}_{(\mathbf{q},+),(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},+)}&\mathsf{W}_{(\mathbf{q},+),(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},-)}\\
\mathsf{W}_{(\mathbf{q},-),(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime},+)}&\mathsf{W}_{(\mathbf{q},-),(-\mathbf{q}^{\prime},-)}&\mathsf{W}_{(\mathbf{q},-),(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},+)}&\mathsf{W}_{(\mathbf{q},-),(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},-)}\end{pmatrix}.

(245)

The expectation values for the ground state

\widetilde{\mathsf{W}}_{(\mathbf{q},\circ),(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\circ^{\prime})}=\matrixelement{\wt{\text{GS}} }{ \wt{w}_{(\bv q,\circ)} \wt{w}_{(\bv q',\circ')} }{\wt{\text{GS}}}=-\mathrm{i}\widetilde{\Gamma}_{(\mathbf{q},\circ),(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\circ^{\prime})}^{\rm GS}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{(\mathbf{q},\circ),(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\circ^{\prime})},

(246)

where the ground-state covariance matrix

{\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\rm GS}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 0& - I_{L^2\times L^2}/2\\ I_{L^2\times L^2}/2& 0\endlx@physics@matrix),}

(247)

which characterizes a fully occupied Fermi sea for the
\widetilde{c}
fermions (refer to
Eq.

234

244
