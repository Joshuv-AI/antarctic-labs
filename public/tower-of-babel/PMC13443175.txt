Surgical Treatment of Pathologic Spinal Fractures in Osteoporosis: Two Clinical Cases
Authors: Nurzhan Abishev, Talgat Kerimbayev, Daryn Borangaliyev, Alimkhan Yelubayev, Makar Solodovnikov, Mariya Dmitriyeva, Medet Toleubayev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoporotic spine fractures are among the most prevalent fractures in patients with osteoporosis and pose significant challenges to surgical treatment. Achieving stable fixation of osteoporotic bone is technically challenging and has become an increasing concern in spinal neurosurgery in the aging global population. Numerous studies have described the biomechanical benefits of fixation devices and techniques; however, no standard of care has been identified to provide distinct spinal stabilization in these patients. This study reviews the existing evidence and explores the complexities related to the treatment of pathologic spinal fractures in the context of systemic osteoporosis. Description of clinical cases: We present 2 cases of pathologic osteoporotic spinal fractures with spinal canal compression treated surgically using a modified method that incorporates cemented cannulated transpedicular screws and interbody spondylodesis with cage placement. Patients were followed up for 24 months after surgery. The Visual Analog Scale score improved significantly, decreasing from 7.3 points before surgery to 1.2 points immediately after surgery and to 0.5 points at the end of the follow-up. Similarly, the Oswestry Disability Index improved from 69% before surgery to 41% immediately after surgery and to 20% after 2 years. The Cobb angle improved from 38.0° preoperatively to 29.0° immediately after surgery. No bone resorption around the screws was observed during follow-up. The combination of transpedicular fixation with vertebroplasty is safe and effective for treating osteoporotic spinal fractures. This approach yields satisfactory clinical and radiographic results.

INTRODUCTION

Spinal osteoporotic fractures are the most prevalent bone fractures caused by minor impact or trauma in patients with osteoporosis.5) Osteoporotic fractures can occur in most skeletal bones except the skull. Nonetheless, they are most frequently observed in the thoracic and lumbar vertebrae, wrist bones, and femur. Osteoporosis ranks fourth among the diseases contributing to disability, following cardiovascular system, diabetes mellitus, and cancer.3)

Clinical classifications of these fractures in osteoporosis remain poorly defined. Surgical interventions, when combined with competent rehabilitation, are undoubtedly advantageous. However, under some conditions, such as severe osteoporosis with vertebral compression fractures, advanced methods, such as expandable stem screws or cement augmentation, may fail to provide sufficient fixation strength until spondylosis is achieved.18)

Osteoporosis is a critical factor affecting the diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment of vertebral fractures. Decreased bone mineral density is a substantial independent risk factor for metalwork failure in stabilizing surgeries.6) Additionally, osteoporotic spinal fractures can worsen sagittal balance, causing secondary traumatic kyphosis, exacerbating anterior displacement, and increasing the risk of a fracture cascade and non-union. This necessitates diagnosing and managing osteoporosis along with surgical treatment.9)

The thoracolumbar junction is particularly sensitive to sagittal balance disturbances, as this segment is often subjected to osteoporotic fractures. Vertebral fractures of this segment are accompanied by significant kyphotic deformity due to the biomechanical properties of the spine and can lead to spinal cord compression, neurological deficits, and eventually severe disability.

Various surgical treatment options for osteoporotic fractures are described in the literature, including cement vertebroplasty, kyphoplasty, decompression and stabilizing operations. The most common treatments for osteoporotic burst fractures accompanied by compression of nerve structures and kyphotic deformity included resection of the fractured vertebra, interbody spondylodesis at the fracture site with transpedicular fixation (TPF) of the 2 overlying and 2 underlying vertebrae, as well as cement augmentation. However, even in the most recent scientific publications, there are divergent opinions on the extent of TPF. Some advocate for short fixation with 4 screws—one level above and one level below the broken segment—while others recommend longer fixation with 2 screws at each level or even more. The choice of surgical extent also depends on the patient’s comorbidities, including both systemic and spinal conditions, which may influence the final consistency of the metal structure and the patient’s clinical status. This decision remains controversial on a case-by-case basis. Surgical management of osteoporotic vertebral fractures presents challenges, including the risk of metal structure failure, bone resorption, pseudoarthrosis, and adjacent segment fractures.

CASE REPORT

Clinical case 1

A 70-year-old woman presented with severe lumbar pain. Her medical history indicated that she underwent surgery at another hospital at her place of residence. This surgery involved a lateral approach, installation of an interbody cage, and decompression of the spinal canal structures (FIGURE 1). However, 6 months after surgery, she reported worsening thoracolumbar pain along with weakness in the lower extremities. The initial surgery did not account for the patient’s severe osteoporosis, the lack of TPF in the posterior musculoskeletal segment, and diabetes mellitus. During the preoperative period, the patient underwent glycated hemoglobin assessment, followed by blood glucose monitoring and adjustment. The target fasting blood glucose level was maintained below 7.5 mmol/L. To optimize glycemic control, medications with hyperglycemic effects, including glucocorticosteroids (dexamethasone, prednisolone) and sugar-containing agents (e.g., lactulose), were excluded from the treatment regimen.

Computed tomography images after interbody cage placement, 6 months after surgery. Cage migration is observed, along with fusion of multilevel pathologic fractures with spinal cord compression.

When the patient presented to our clinic, preoperative computed tomography (CT) scans of the thoracolumbar spine suggested migration of the previously installed cage. Furthermore, 2 additional vertebral levels had become involved in the pathologic fracture, leading to a kyphotic deformity (FIGURE 2).

Three-dimensional reconstructed computed tomography images.

Surgical intervention

The patient underwent surgery under combined general anesthesia with artificial lung ventilation in the abdominal position maintained using rollers to ensure physiological alignment. A reinfusion machine was used because of the high risk of intraoperative bleeding. After radiologic marking, an incision was made through the skin and subcutaneous fat in the projection of the pathologic fracture. The muscles with their fasciae were separated and retracted laterally using retractors. For fluoroscopic verification, guiding holes were created using a 13G Jamshidi bone needle and advanced through the stems into the vertebral bodies of Th8, Th9, Th10, Th11, L2, L3, L4, L5, and S1. These holes were tapped, and cannulated fenestrated screws were inserted and reinforced with cement augmentation (Vertplex) to attain firm fixation. Upon examination, the previously installed cage was found to have migrated, and substantial vertebral instability was observed. Finally, the cage was removed. The affected vertebra underwent microsurgical resection, along with discectomy of the superior and inferior intervertebral disc. Interbody spondylosis was conducted using the V-LIFT system under radiologic control to confirm the implant location. Surgical hemostatic material was applied to the bottom of the surgical wound. Layer-by-layer suturing was conducted, and active drainage was established. The surgery lasted 8 hours and 15 minutes, with an intraoperative blood loss of 1,500 mL. Decompression of the spinal canal structures and stabilization of the vertebral motor segments were achieved.

Clinical case 2

A 78-year-old woman was admitted with the following diagnosis: International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision T91.1, indicating a closed spinal cord injury. She presented with a compression fracture of the L1 vertebral body (type A3 per AO Spine Classification), related to late-stage osteoporosis and spinal cord compression. Neurological examination suggested a type D deficit according to the American Spinal Injury Association impairment scale and vertebroplasty of the L1 vertebra from 2020. Moreover, the patient presented complications, such as lumbar syndrome and myelopathy. During admission, she reported substantial pain, discomfort in the lumbar spine, weakness in the extremities, limited movement, decreased sensation, and numbness. Additionally, she experienced generalized weakness.

She had sustained a spinal cord injury after a fall and was initially hospitalized elsewhere with a diagnosis of a closed uncomplicated osteoporotic compression fracture of the L1 vertebral body (second degree). She underwent percutaneous vertebroplasty of the L1 vertebral body. Nonetheless, her condition gradually worsened, with new-onset weakness and numbness in the lower extremities and increased lower back pain. A CT scan of the thoracic and lumbar spine suggested a compression fracture of the L1 vertebra with spinal cord compression (type A3 per AO Spine Classification) and a systemic osteoporotic lesion in the spinal column (FIGURE 4).

Burst fracture of L1, type A3, with spinal cord compression (computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging scans before surgery).

Surgical intervention

Considering the pathological fracture of the L1 vertebra in systemic osteoporosis, anamnesis, clinical and neurological symptoms of myopathic syndrome, and pronounced lumbago, surgical intervention was conducted. A posterior median approach was selected, involving resection of the L1 vertebra with spinal cord decompression. A laminectomy of the L1 vertebra was conducted, and a titanium mesh was installed. TPF was conducted from Th11 to L3 vertebrae. Additionally, vertebroplasty with cemented substance was conducted for reinforcement at the Th10, Th11, Th12, L2, and L3 vertebrae. FIGURE 5 illustrates the CT images obtained on day 1 after surgery. In the first clinical case, the patient was mobilized on postoperative day 3, while in the second case, mobilization occurred on postoperative day 2. No thromboembolic complications were observed in either patient. The risk of thromboembolic events was assessed using the Caprini scale,7) and appropriate prophylactic measures were implemented. These measures included elastic bandaging of the lower extremities, in-bed physical therapy, guided mobilization during verticalization, early rehabilitation as tolerated, and the administration of low-molecular-weight heparins.

Results

The clinical efficacy of the surgery was examined by analyzing pain severity using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), quality of life assessment through the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and surgical complications. During the postoperative period, patients’ functional status was evaluated using the ODI, and functional recovery was assessed through a structured questionnaire administered throughout the observation period. Additionally, the overall patient condition was continuously monitored based on neurological status and findings from follow-up instrumental examinations, ensuring timely assessment and intervention.

Before surgery, the VAS score was 7.3 points and the ODI was 69%. The VAS score declined immediately after surgery to 1.2 points and improved to 0.5 points at the end of the follow-up. A systematic, stepwise, and tiered approach to analgesic therapy was implemented, incorporating nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, analgesic-antipyretics, opioids, local neuromuscular and joint blockades, and physiotherapeutic interventions to optimize pain management and enhance recovery. The ODI improved to 41% immediately after surgery and 20% at the 2-year follow-up. Radiological outcomes were evaluated through CT imaging, which facilitated the assessment of kyphotic deformities (measured by the Cobb angle), state of metal fixation, and any bone resorption around the screws.4) The Cobb angle was 38.0° before surgery, which improved to 29.0° immediately after surgery (FIGURE 6).

Computed tomography images on day 1 after surgery (Case 1).

Considering the patients’ systemic osteoporosis, old age, comorbidities, multiple fracture levels (Th12, L2), cortical and labral destruction due to mesh kedge implantation, and the increased risk of mechanical failure of metal fixation, multilevel TPF combined with vertebroplasty was performed. Additionally, CT scans revealed signs of spinal segment instability at L4–L5 and L5–S1, as well as a high incidence of instability in segments adjacent to the TPF. Consequently, stabilization was extended to 4 levels above and 5 levels below the TPF, with the L4–L5 and L5–S1 vertebroplasty segments being particularly prone to instability. This comprehensive approach led to significant clinical, radiological, and functional improvement, as confirmed by a 2-year follow-up.

At the 24-month follow-up, clinical examination and questionnaire responses suggested satisfactory results. Neurological examination, along with pain and disability assessments using the VAS (0.5 points) and ODI (21%) indicated satisfactory recovery. During follow-up, imaging confirmed spondylodesis, corrected kyphotic deformity in Case 1, and no bone resorption around the screws (FIGURE 7).

DISCUSSION

The surgical management of osteoporotic vertebral body fractures presents a multifaceted challenge in spinal neurosurgery. Most patients with osteoporotic spinal fractures respond well to conservative or minimally invasive approaches; however, some patients require more invasive surgical interventions to address complications, such as hyperkyphosis, pain, immobility, dyspnea, and neurological deficits. In these cases, the primary goal is to preserve or restore ambulatory function. In instances where vertebral body destruction leads to hyperkyphosis and spinal canal stenosis, extensive surgical intervention emerges as the only viable option.311)

Osteoporotic vertebral fractures are classified according to the AO Spine German Society for Orthopaedics and Trauma Surgery (DGOU) system. The surgical management of these fractures is aimed at stabilizing or restoring the biomechanically and anatomically correct alignment of the spine, with a primary focus on restoring sagittal balance, depending on the fracture type as per the DGOU classification. For OF 4 and OF 5 fractures, the primary surgical objective is decompression of the spinal canal and restoration of sagittal balance. In this context, the gold standard for surgical treatment—based on long-term outcomes and the prevention of complications—includes resection of bone fragments, interbody spondylodesis at the fractured vertebral level, and TPF with cement augmentation. This approach is particularly advantageous for patients with osteoporotic vertebral fractures, especially in cases of severe bone insufficiency, as it enhances spinal stability and reduces the risk of postoperative complications.15)

Researchers focus on optimizing stability in internal fixation, maintaining corrective effects, and mitigating complications in patients undergoing surgery for osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs). The difference in the scope of surgical interventions between the first and second cases was determined by the distinct preoperative conditions of each patient. In the first case, factors such as diabetes mellitus, degenerative changes in segments adjacent to the initially planned levels of TPF, and pathological fractures involving the Th12 and L2 vertebrae due to cage-mesh migration necessitated a more extensive surgical approach.

Transpedicular screw systems are the most widely used internal fixation devices because of their strong initial stability and effective coronal and sagittal correction. However, the negative correlation between osteoporosis severity and screw stability poses major challenges.13) To this end, innovative techniques aim to enhance screw stability in vertebral body reinforcement. These approaches involve the use of hydroxyapatite-coated screws, expansion screws, or reinforcement of screws with bone cement. Cement augmentation can be conducted either before screw placement or, in the case of end screws, after insertion, thus enhancing the extraction force and stability.12)

A prospective multicentre study suggested that posterior spondylodesis with cement augmentation—even without nerve decompression—is a viable option for patients with OVCFs exhibiting <40% intraspinal compression.10) Similarly, a retrospective study proposed that vertebral column resection combined with cement-augmented transpedicular screw fixation effectively restores spinal alignment in patients with severe compression fractures accompanied by kyphotic deformities, yielding favorable clinical outcomes.12)

Taken together, posterior spondylodesis with bone cement screws may emerge as a standard procedure for patients with OVCF along with severe osteoporosis. This approach effectively stabilizes fractures and improves clinical outcomes; however, its ability to correct kyphosis remains limited. Cases of persistent kyphotic deformity warrant additional interventions, such as osteotomy, particularly in those with sagittal imbalance.17)

Research comparing long-segment and short-segment fixation has yielded varying results. Short-segment fixation offers several advantages, including the preservation of spinal mobility and reduced operative time. However, this technique is associated with a higher risk of screw loosening and is less effective in correcting kyphotic deformities.2) Our empirical observations support the necessity of extended TPF with cement augmentation to enhance biomechanical stability and mitigate neurological deficits.1)

Additionally, the effectiveness of vertebroplasty combined with TPF has shown variability across different research studies.20) Consequently, no definitive consensus has been reached regarding the optimal surgical strategy for managing thoracolumbar osteoporotic compression fractures.

The mechanism of pedicle screw loosening in patients with diabetes remains incompletely understood. Screw stability is directly dependent on bone quality and density.14) However, the bones of individuals with diabetes exhibit several structural characteristics that predispose them to degradation, including reduced cortical thickness, increased porosity, and decreased overall strength.19) Prolonged hyperglycemia may negatively impact bone quality, further complicating screw stabilization in patients with diabetes and emphasizing the need for optimized fixation strategies.

In our case, no complications related to diabetes mellitus were noted. Wang et al.16) describe both complications directly affecting the formation of solid spinal fusion and the quality of TPF, as well as general surgical complications, including prolonged wound healing and infectious complications.8)

Fracture prevention at adjacent levels is achieved through multidisciplinary collaboration with specialists from related fields, including rheumatologists and endocrinologists, to ensure systemic osteoporosis treatment and improve bone mineral density. The risk of adjacent segment degeneration is assessed based on existing studies. In cases of high risk, a more extensive TPF approach is employed to enhance spinal stability and reduce complications.

The extended fixation strategy was designed to enhance spinal stability, reduce the risk of implant failure, and optimize long-term outcomes. By extending the fixation levels, the mechanical load was distributed more evenly across multiple spinal segments, thereby reducing stress on individual fixation points and decreasing the likelihood of structural compromise.

Existing studies emphasize the role of posterior spondylodesis with bone cement screws in OVCF treatment; however, potential limitations and drawbacks should be addressed. First, the follow-up periods in these trials are relatively short, failing to capture long-term outcomes or complications associated with the procedure. Long-term follow-up is warranted to assess the long-term durability and sustainability of treatment effects. Second, despite favorable clinical outcomes, previous trials may not adequately address procedural complications, such as infection, cement leakage, or adjacent segment degeneration. Third, the trials primarily describe the efficacy of posterior spondylodesis with bone cement screws, without direct comparison with alternative treatment modalities. This limits the ability to assess the relative merits of this approach compared with other surgical or conservative options. Future research should aim to address these gaps and provide more comprehensive analyses of the merits of posterior spondylodesis with bone cement screws in OVCF treatment.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. As a retrospective analysis, it necessitates future research employing a prospective, randomized design to achieve more precise and definitive conclusions. Additionally, due to the small sample size, nonparametric tests were used, which may have limited the statistical power of the analysis. Despite these constraints, our study highlights a potential treatment approach for OVCFs with kyphotic deformity, contributing to the ongoing exploration of optimal surgical strategies.

CONCLUSION

The findings of this case series indicate that TPF with vertebroplasty is a safe and effective surgical intervention for patients with severe osteoporotic fractures leading to neurological dysfunction and immobility, particularly when alternative treatments have proven inadequate. However, when determining the extent of fixation, it is essential to consider systemic conditions such as type 2 diabetes mellitus, spinal pathologies—including degenerative instability of spinal motion segments at similar levels—the location of the fracture, and the planned fixation levels. The procedure was associated with minimal surgical complications and no deterioration in neurological status. Additionally, significant improvements in segmental kyphosis and pain symptoms were observed. Given the rising prevalence of osteoporotic vertebral fractures, the role of spinal stabilization in enhancing patients’ quality of life is gaining increasing attention. TPF with vertebroplasty, when performed by an experienced neurosurgeon, represents a reliable treatment option for patients with severe osteoporotic fractures, sequential kyphosis, and spinal stenosis. However, individualized treatment planning remains essential. Patients should be thoroughly informed about the potential risks and benefits of the procedure. Ultimately, the primary goal of treatment is to preserve or restore the patient’s ability to ambulate and maintain functional independence.
