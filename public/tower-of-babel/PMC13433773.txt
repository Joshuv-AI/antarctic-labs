Generative-model-based null-space iterative reconstruction for atomic electron tomography with sparse data
Authors: Han Li, Wenting Cui, Wenda Su, Huan Lei, Kuang Yu, Jiali Yang, Jia Li, Lin Gan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Atomic electron tomography (AET) is a powerful technique for determining the three-dimensional atomic structure of matter in real space. However, conventional AET requires numerous projections across a wide angular range. The high dose and prolonged acquisition severely limit its application. Here, we propose an interpretable and universal algorithm for low-dose, fast and tilt-constrained AET: null-space iterative reconstruction (NSIRE), which uses an unsupervised diffusion model to iteratively compute null-space solutions of projection equations, thereby generating tomograms consistent with both projection constraints and atomic potential prior. NSIRE can resolve a wide range of complex materials under 6–12° sparse projection or within ±29° small tilt-range without retraining. Using the NSIRE, we determine the three-dimensional atomic structures of a 4-nm Pt nanoparticle with grain boundaries and a 3-nm PtCo nanoalloy (the smallest one resolved so far), achieving a root-mean-square displacement of <20 pm and high projection consistency, overcoming the scale, dose and time limitations of AET.

Introduction

Atomic electron tomography (AET) is an advanced technique for characterizing the three-dimensional (3D) internal structure and chemical composition of materials at the atomic scale1. AET typically employs an incoherent annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscope (ADF-STEM) to acquire two-dimensional (2D) projections of a sample at different tilt-angles, where the image intensity is increases with both thickness and atomic number (Z)2,3. This enables reconstruction of the 3D structure using an iterative algorithm based on the Radon transform, producing a 3D image whose computed projections match the experimental projections4,5. Mathematically, this process corresponds to solving an inverse problem. Iterative reconstruction techniques (IRTs), widely used in AET, include equally sloped tomography (EST)6–9, generalized Fourier iterative reconstruction (GENFIRE)10–15, and real space iterative reconstruction (RESIRE)16–23. These methods enable projection-consistent 3D reconstruction of the tomogram, but typically require numerous 2D projections acquired at small angular intervals (1–2°) over a wide angular range (±70°) to resolve reliable atomic structures. The high irradiation dose is prone to induce structural damage (with a typical dose approaching \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${10}^{6}{{\rm{e}}}/{{{{{\text {\AA}}} }}}^{2}$$\end{document}106e/Å2)10,11,22–24, thereby limiting AET applications to samples smaller than ~5 nm6–11,17–19. The lengthy acquisition process (often several hours) places stringent demands on sample stability, resulting in a low success rate of AET experiments and hindering dynamic or time-sensitive studies25,26. Increasing angular intervals and reducing the number of projections (known as under-sampling25,27) can lower the irradiation dose and accelerate data acquisition, but also render the inverse problem ill-posed, resulting in severe artifacts in the reconstructed tomograms and ultimately degrading the accuracy of atomic localization.

Incorporating prior information to constrain the solution space of the inverse problem helps mitigate the effects of data sparsity and enables high-quality 3D reconstructions. According to physical principle, atomic tomograms should contain only the 3D atomic potential convolved with the point spread function (PSF) of the microscope28–31 (hereafter referred to as the atomic potential); namely, the tomograms should conform to the atomic potential data distribution. This provides a precise and stringent constraint; however, conventional regularization methods cannot incorporate such complex priors32–34. Artificial intelligence has led to a paradigm shift in solving tomographic inverse problems. The most popular approach employs neural networks to construct an end-to-end mapping between the inputs (e.g., under-sampled tomograms27,29,35 or projections36) and the target outputs (e.g., fully-sampled tomograms), yielding high-quality reconstructions that respect the tomogram data priors. In the field of AET, Yang et al. used an end-to-end 3D U-Net for tomograms post-processing to remove artifacts according to atomicity and resolve the 3D structure of Pt nanoparticles29,37 at 6.5° angular intervals. However, neural network outputs do not guarantee projection consistency and may produce artifact-free tomograms that deviate from experimental observations (as we will show later). More importantly, in practice, these methods often encounter generalization problems when dealing with materials of different compositions, structures, or projection angles, reducing accuracy and requiring retraining for each new scenario.

Advances in generative artificial intelligence37–41 provide a novel approach to AET reconstruction. In this work, we propose a Null-Space Iterative REconstruction (NSIRE) algorithm based on diffusion model that leverages the mathematical properties of the null-space to constrain 3D reconstructions to the atomic manifold. This ensures strict projection consistency while incorporating atomic priors. The NSIRE efficiently explores the solution space by integrating stochasticity and determinism, enables accurate 3D reconstruction of single-elemental samples from sparse projection with ~12° intervals, maintaining stable performance for the multi-elemental samples outside the training distribution at ~6° intervals. Using NSIRE, we determine the 3D stacking fault structure of a 4-nm Pt nanoparticle and the 3D atomic structure of a 3-nm PtCo alloy nanoparticle (the smallest nanoalloy resolved by AET so far) under sparse projection conditions, with root-mean-square displacements of atomic coordinates reaching 13 and 17 pm, respectively. The electron dose and acquisition time are reduced by approximately 70% compared to traditional AET10,11,22–24, thereby significantly reducing sample damage and improving the success rate. Last, we determine the atomic structure of an irregular-shaped Pt nanoparticle within a small tilt range of ±29°, demonstrating NSIRE’s potential for AET under tilt-constrained in situ conditions.

However, simultaneously determining the number, types, and positions of atoms under sparse constraints constitutes a complex combinatorial optimization problem, limiting the development of atom-based tomographic reconstruction methods. Nevertheless, if the sampling process of the diffusion model is constrained to the null-space, artifact formation can be fundamentally suppressed while maintaining strict projection consistency.

Notably, generating a 3D voxel tomogram using the diffusion model directly requires substantial computational costs. Considering the similarity of tomogram features, we convert the single-channel 3D tomogram \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}_{3{{\rm{D}}}}{\mathbb{\in }}{\mathbb{R}}}^{{N}^{3}}$$\end{document}x3D\inRN3 into N/d 2.5D tomograms with d channels through dimensional compression, and all channels share identical convolution parameters. We choose d = 8 as it corresponds to the size of a single atom at the resolution typical in AET experiments (pixel size ~0.3 Å). The results show that the network can achieve continuous 3D reconstruction during sampling without requiring hyperparameter tuning49 (Fig. 2c, d). Moreover, when the projection information is insufficient or experimental errors, NSIRE can generate distinct features to prevent realistic but false outputs (Supplementary Figs. 7–9).

Figure 2e–g evaluate the performance of NSIRE on the amorphous Ta nanoparticle model and a crystalline Pt nanoparticle model of comparable thickness, the latter was obtained from molecular dynamics simulations. Multi-slice simulated tilt-series were generated for both models, with different uniformly spaced tilts spanning ±65°, and then were reconstructed using the pre-trained score network sθ. NSIRE can effectively reconstruct 3D atomic structures under different angular intervals less than 13°, achieving a root-mean-square displacement (RMSD) of ~20 pm and a common atomic ratio (CAR) exceeding 98%. This has important practical implications for characterizing small-sized nanoparticles, as they are difficult to stabilize at conventional doses (Supplementary Fig. 10). Furthermore, NSIRE can also perform well in amorphous structure, indicating that atomic priors introduced via diffusion models are less dependent on artificially applied structural biases, such as lattice assumptions (Fig. 2e–g). This generalization capability is essential for the accurate determination of previously unknown materials with novel structures. More importantly, NSIRE utilizes an unsupervised score network for sampling, which can accommodate changes in projection angles, missing-wedge ranges, etc. Therefore, compared with end-to-end neural networks that require predefined acquisition settings29,36, the NSIRE algorithm is highly flexible. Experimental parameters can thus be tuned flexibly according to the precision requirements (Supplementary Fig. 11). Further batch tests on simulated data of single-elemental particles (Supplementary Figs. 12–15), multi-elemental samples (Supplementary Figs. 16–19), and thin film (Supplementary Fig. 20) show that NSIRE consistently has stable and optimal performance under variations such as sample geometry, interatomic distance, and tilt range.

During the iterative sampling, NSIRE dynamically updates both the atomic intensities and atomic coordinates of Pt and Co, guided by the conditional constraint imposed by the projection equations. This enables the accurate reconstruction of PtCo atomic tomograms that are strictly consistent with the projection constraints (Fig. 5b–e). The atomic priors ensure that the atomic profile in the NSIRE tomogram remains isotropic and robust to projection sparsity, thereby enabling more accurate coordinate fitting than IRTs (Fig. 5f). The relative intensity of the 3D atomic potential for averaged Pt and Co atoms closely matches the Hartree-Fock theoretical calculations2 (Fig. 5g–i). These results demonstrate that the diffusion model, guided by experimental projection constraints, can adaptively generate atomic potentials that conform to physical principles for unknown atoms.

The atomic structure of the PtCo nanoparticle reconstructed by NSIRE exhibits an RMSD of 17.2 pm, with the CAR exceeding 98% (Table 4), and an average R1-factor error of 0.121 (Supplementary Fig. 28b). To assess the benefit of computing the null-space solution, we compared the reconstruction errors of NSIRE for different elements with those of traditional IRTs using multi-slice simulations. In IRTs tomograms relying solely on projection constraints, a large number of Co atoms are either missing or dislocated (Supplementary Fig. 29). This is because the weaker Co atoms exhibit higher positional error. Additionally, in under-sampled IRTs tomogram, high-contrast Pt atoms generate numerous ray-like artifacts with intensities comparable to the Co atomic peaks (Fig. 5f), which obscure the Co signal and lead to significant atom loss (the atomic recovery ratio is only 41.2%). The tomogram reconstructed by the end-to-end method29, without retraining for the Pt-Co system, exhibits a similar issue: the neural network fails to distinguish Co atomic peaks from Pt-induced artifacts (Supplementary Fig. 29). NSIRE iteratively samples the atomic manifold, effectively suppressing inappropriate null-space solutions, such as ray-like artifacts, while adaptively recovering the Co atomic potential. This significantly improves the reconstruction accuracy of Co atoms. NSIRE performance on complex materials

In addition to ultra-small nanoparticles, NSIRE also demonstrates excellent performance for fast AET of thicker samples. We extensively validate NSIRE using high-quality, publicly available experimental datasets. These datasets include a fivefold-twinned core–shell Pt@Pd nanoparticle20 (~5 nm, Fig. 6a–e, Supplementary Figs. 30–32), representing a class of material systems whose atomic numbers differ by one period; three CoNiRuRhPdAgIrPt high-entropy nanoalloys17,23 (5–9 nm, Fig. 6f–j, Supplementary Figs. 33–35), representing materials with complex 3D chemical distributions; an icosahedral Pd@Au nanoparticle19 (~5 nm, Fig. 6k–o, Supplementary Fig. 36) with amorphization and dislocations, representing non-platinum materials outside the training distribution; an amorphous ZrO2 nanoparticle21 (~10 nm, Fig. 6p–t, Supplementary Fig. 37), representing oxide materials. Discussion

In summary, we developed a NSIRE algorithm based on generative artificial intelligence for the AET with sparse data. During iterative reconstruction of under-sampled AET, the null-space component of the tomogram is constrained to the atomic manifold, ensuring that the reconstruction results remain consistent with both experimental projection constraints and atomic priors. Recent studies have clarified that the accuracy of AET is constrained by the information contained in the measured projections and the fidelity with which the reconstruction workflow preserves and transfers that information into an atomic model52,53. By embedding a sparse reconstruction kernel with a precise prior information into the current AET workflow52, NSIRE suppresses artifact formation at its origin and excludes a large number of erroneous solutions, thereby effectively extending the information limit of AET.

The NSIRE enables high-precision reconstruction of 3D atomic structures using as few as 10–20 images (angular interval: 6°–12°), as demonstrated by a twinned Pt nanoparticle, where the RMSD is only 13.2 pm and the common atomic ratio exceeds 99%. Even for samples outside the training set, such as PtCo or high-entropy nanoalloys, the NSIRE also significantly improves the reconstruction accuracy of the lighter atoms. Without the need of retraining, the NSIRE offers a flexible and fast approach to determine the 3D atomic structures of a wide range of materials of various structures and compositions, demonstrating strong generalizability. Due to the universality of atomicity in the microscopic world, this strategy of introducing the complex atomic potential priors through generative models can also be applied to other inverse problems in high-resolution electron microscopic imaging, enabling the precise characterization of materials.

It should be emphasized that this work mainly focuses on improving the ADF-based AET. NSIRE relies on RESIRE as linear generalized inverse for inversion and cannot be directly applied to nonlinear data such as HRTEM54, nor can it effectively reconstruct light-elemental atoms or large-sized samples whose thickness exceeds the depth-of-field. Moreover, when non-null-space artifacts such as sub-volume boundaries of thin film are present, the atomic prior is disturbed and leads to sub-optimal reconstruction. Finally, although we introduce an accelerated sampling technique, it still has higher computational consumption than IRTs and end-to-end networks, especially when the number of projections or the image size is large. Introducing more accurate physical models and more advanced generative models to address these issues remains the primary focus of future work.

Methods

Construction of atomic potential datasets

Due to the perturbation ability of the diffusion model, the atomic potential of any element can be utilized for training. Here the element Pt is chosen, and 10,000 atomic structural models of Pt nanoparticles are generated by cutting FCC super-cells of Pt using randomly shaped 3D surfaces29, with the FCC lattice constant of 3.912 Å and a random displacement of 22 pm. The shape of 3D volumes is smooth random 3D surface, with a volume size of 4–7 nm. Other parameters follow the default values in the code29. When the number of atomic structures reaches 5000, the performance of the model tends to stabilize (Supplementary Fig. 38).

Since the size of the thin sample is usually smaller than the depth of field of ADF-STEM, and considering that the PSF near the focal plane can be effectively approximated by a Gaussian function, the 3D atomic tomogram can be expressed as the linear convolution of the 3D atomic potential of the material and a Gaussian kernel function in an ideal state after rotational averaging. Furthermore, ab initio TEM studies have demonstrated that bonding has little effect on ADF-STEM image contrast55, enabling the 3D atomic potential of the sample to be derived from linear combinations of isolated atomic potentials \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sum }_{i}{V}_{i}({{{\boldsymbol{r}}}-{{\boldsymbol{r}}}}_{i})$$\end{document}∑iVi(r−ri). The score network is trained on eight RTX 2080 Ti GPUs for one day. Construction of generalized inverses of under-sampled tomography

To apply range-null-space decomposition, it is necessary to construct the generalized inverse of the projection process and accurately calculate the range-space component A−y of the tomogram. Filtered back-projection (FBP)56 is an efficient and accurate generalized inverse under fully-sampled conditions. However, FBP does not hold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\boldsymbol{AA}}}}^{-}{{\boldsymbol{A}}}={{\boldsymbol{A}}}$$\end{document}AA−A=A under sparse projection conditions. IRTs that minimize the difference between the observed and computed projections using the least squares method can satisfy the definition of generalized inverse under sparse projection conditions. Among them, simultaneous algebraic reconstruction technique (SART) and simultaneous iterative reconstruction technique (SIRT) are the fastest. However, their forward and backward projection steps rely on mathematical approximation, which is not precise enough for accurate 3D atomic structure reconstruction. Fourier-based iteration techniques, such as EST and GENFIRE12, use Fourier transforms for projection and back-projection and introduce oversampling57 in their implementation to achieve high accuracy, but require a large amount of memory to assemble the 3D Fourier array. This further reduces the efficiency of iterative sampling. We recommend using the RESIRE algorithm as A−. RESIRE inherits the oversampling method and iteratively updates using the exact Radon transposition (AT) in real space. The update steps are given below16:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}^{\left(k+1\right)}={{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}^{\left(k\right)}-{{\rm{\lambda }}}\,{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}^{{{\rm{T}}}}\left({{\boldsymbol{A}}}{{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}^{\left(k\right)}-{{\boldsymbol{y}}}\right)$$\end{document}xk+1=xk−λATAxk−yWhere \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{\lambda }}}=2/{nN}$$\end{document}λ=2/nN is the step length, n is the number of projections, and N is the size of the sinogram. It can be shown that RESIRE is essentially an affine transformation in the form of a matrix polynomial:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}^{-}{{\boldsymbol{y}}}={{\mathrm{lim}}}_{k\to \infty }{{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}^{\left(k+1\right)}={{\mathrm{lim}}}_{k\to \infty }\left\{\sum_{l=1}^{k}{{\rm{\lambda }}}{\left({{\boldsymbol{I}}}-{{\rm{\lambda }}}{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}^{{{\rm{T}}}}{{\boldsymbol{A}}}\right)}^{l-1}{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}^{{{\rm{T}}}}{{\boldsymbol{y}}}+{\left({{\boldsymbol{I}}}-{{\rm{\lambda }}}{{{\boldsymbol{A}}}}^{{{\rm{T}}}}{{\boldsymbol{A}}}\right)}^{k}{{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}^{(0)}\right\}$$\end{document}A−y=limk→∞xk+1=limk→∞∑l=1kλI−λATAl−1ATy+I−λATAkx(0)

Therefore, it is mathematically reasonable to treat RESIRE as the linear generalized inverse of tomography. To balance precision and efficiency, the number of iterations is set to 50. In this work, we use RESIRE to represent IRTs. For a Fourier based algorithm, range-null-space decomposition is still valid by simply replacing the Radon transform with the Fourier transform.

As the DDPM generation process (from noise to data) is approximately the inverse of the forward diffusion process (from data to noise), at least 1000 iterations are required to obtain high-quality samples, with each iteration involves 50 RESIRE calculations, making the process highly inefficient. Since typical AET experiments usually require several hours for projection collection, we believe that this calculation time is acceptable in practical applications.

It is worth noting that while many diffusion-model-based methods exist to deal with inverse problems involving noise, most of them rely on singular value decomposition (SVD) to cope with noise in the observed constraint62,63. However, due to the unbounded nature of the Radon operator A, SVD is difficult. Therefore, our iterative algorithm is mainly based on noise-free method41, and Poisson-Gaussian noise should be completely removed during tilt-series pre-processing (Supplementary Figs. 39), which causes a slight resolution degradation that can be counted into the B-factor.

Evaluation metrics

Unlike other tomography fields, the meaningful result of AET is the atomic structure fitted from the tomogram rather than the voxel image itself. Therefore, the most important evaluation metrics are root-mean-square displacement (RMSD) and the common atom ratio (CAR). For AET data which ground truth is known, common atoms are first searched for between the reconstructed structure and the model.
