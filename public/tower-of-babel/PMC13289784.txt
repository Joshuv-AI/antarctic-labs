Cation concentration-dependent reaction kinetics in single-atom catalysts for electrochemical CO2 reduction
Authors: Lingyi Kong, Zhe Chen, Yuheng Chen, Lingxiao Wang, Yu Wang, Yafei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrolyte cations are widely recognized as critical promoters in electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR), yet the field has largely focused on cation identity while overlooking a more fundamental and practical parameter, namely cation concentration. Whether continuously increasing cation concentration enhances catalytic activity remains an open and consequential question, and the lack of mechanistic understanding has limited the rational design of electrolyte environments. Here, we demonstrate that cation concentration is not merely a secondary parameter, but a decisive kinetic regulator that fundamentally governs CO2RR performance. By integrating constant-potential ab initio molecular dynamics simulations with experiments, we reveal a previously unrecognized nonmonotonic (“volcano-type”) dependence of catalytic activity on K+ concentration over Ni–N–C single-atom catalysts. At moderate concentrations, K+ promotes CO2 activation by restructuring the interfacial hydrogen-bond network and stabilizing key intermediates. Strikingly, further increasing K+ concentration leads to over-stabilization of *CO, impeding its desorption and suppressing overall reaction rates. This “double-edged” effect establishes an intrinsic trade-off between intermediate activation and product release. Experimental measurements directly validate this prediction, exhibiting a pronounced rise-and-fall trend in CO partial current density.

Introduction

Electrochemical carbon dioxide reduction reaction (CO2RR) is a promising technology that can convert electricity generated from renewable energy sources into carbon-based fuels and chemicals for storage.1,2 This carbon-negative process involves multiple proton-coupled electron transfer steps and can yield a variety of reduction products (e.g., CO, CH4, C2H4, and EtOH) when facilitated by efficient electrocatalysts.3,4 Although multi-electron products, particularly C2+ species, possess higher commercial value, to date only Cu-based catalysts have demonstrated the capability to drive multi-electron CO2 reduction,5 albeit with limited product selectivity and low energy conversion efficiency. In contrast, the conversion of CO2 to carbon monoxide (CO) via a simple two-electron (2e−) pathway offers higher selectivity and energy efficiency, rendering it a more practical route for industrial-scale CO2RR.6

In this context, silver and gold are widely regarded as promising catalysts of CO production owing to their appreciable activity and selectivity.7 However, the intrinsically high cost of these noble metals severely limits their large-scale application in CO2RR. Recently, low-cost single-atom catalysts (SACs) have attracted considerable attention as promising alternatives to noble-metal catalysts for CO2RR, owing to their maximal atom utilization and well-defined active sites.6,8 Among various SACs, nickel-nitrogen-carbon (Ni–N–C) SACs featuring NiN4 moieties have been extensively studied because of their high selectivity toward the 2e-CO2RR.9,10 For instance, Li et al. reported that Ni–N–C SACs can achieve near-unity CO selectivity with a current density of 28.6 mA cm−2 at −0.81 VRHE in CO2-saturated 0.5 M KHCO3.11 Similarly, Liu and co-workers designed atomically dispersed Ni on nitrogenated graphene as an efficient electrocatalyst for CO2 reduction to CO, which shows a faradaic efficiency (FE) of 97% and a current density of 22 mA cm−2 at a mild overpotential of 0.61 V.12 Despite their excellent selectivity, the relatively low current densities of Ni–N–C SACs remain insufficient for commercial applications, indicating substantial room for improving the kinetics of CO2RR on these catalysts.

In recent years, the influence of metal cations (e.g. K+, Na+, Li+, etc.) on the thermodynamics and kinetics of electrochemical CO2RR has emerged as an important research focus.13–16 Metal cations are generally recognized to modulate the hydrogen-bond network of water molecules, the electric field at the electrochemical double layer, and the local electronic structure of the catalyst, thereby influencing the adsorption of key intermediates and product selectivity of CO2RR.17–22 Wu et al. reported that in the absence of metal cations, the CO2RR on Ni–N–C SACs is even shut down while the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) becomes the dominant reaction, underscoring the essential role of metal cations in initiating CO2RR.23 Although the importance of metal cations has been well established, most previous studies have focused primarily on the effects of cation identity (i.e., ionic radius), whereas another crucial parameter, cation concentration, has received comparatively limited attention. Recently, several studies have shown that increasing the K+ concentration can further enhance the current density of Ni–N–C SACs for CO2-to-CO conversion, offering a new avenue for improving catalytic performance.23–25 However, the underlying mechanisms by which elevated cation concentrations influence the thermodynamics and kinetics of CO2RR remain unclear. More fundamentally, it is still unresolved whether the activity of Ni–N–C SACs continues to improve monotonically with increasing cation concentration. Addressing these questions is essential for advancing the practical application of Ni–N–C SACs in CO2RR and for achieving optimal catalytic performance through rational electrolyte design.

In this work, we employed constant-potential ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations in conjunction with a hybrid solvent model containing varying concentrations of potassium cations (K+) to systematically investigate the effect of cation concentration on the CO2RR performance of Ni–N–C SACs. Potassium ions (K+) were selected as the representative cations because potassium salts (e.g. KCl, KHCO3) are the most commonly used electrolytes in experimental studies. Our theoretical results reveal that increasing K+ concentration progressively disrupts the hydrogen-bond network of interfacial water and strengthens the interaction between CO2 and the Ni active center, thereby accelerating the kinetics of CO2 reduction to CO. However, at high K+ concentrations, the interaction between the Ni active center and the reaction product *CO is significantly enhanced, leading to an increase in the kinetic barrier for *CO desorption, suggesting that the overall reaction kinetics do not necessarily improve with increasing cation concentration. Consistent with these theoretical insights, our experimental measurements demonstrate that the partial current density of Ni–N–C SACs for CO2-to-CO conversion does not increase monotonically with cation concentration, instead reaching an optimum at intermediate K+ levels.

Results and discussion

Concentration effect of K+ on CO2 adsorption and activation

Structurally, Ni–N–C SACs predominantly feature two distinct NiN4 moieties according to different types of coordinating N atoms, namely pyridinic and pyrrolic N. Experimental studies have demonstrated that Ni–N–C SACs comprising pyridinic- and pyrrolic-type NiN4 both exhibit appreciable catalytic activity toward CO2RR.26–28 However, the contribution of the pyridinic-type NiN4 to CO2RR activity is still a matter of debate, as evidenced by the calculated thermodynamic barrier of >1.5 eV for the first elementary step of CO2 hydrogenation.11 This is likely attributable to substantial differences between the idealized pyridinic-type NiN4 models employed in theoretical simulations and the actual structures present in experiments. Consequently, elucidating the true active-site structure of pyridinic-type NiN4 has become a subject of ongoing theoretical investigations,29–31 although it is beyond the scope of the present work. In contrast, prior theoretical studies have consistently shown that the pyrrolic-type NiN4 moiety exhibits favorable catalytic activity for CO2RR.32 Accordingly, pyrrolic-type NiN4 (Fig. S1) is adopted here as a more appropriate model than pyridinic-type NiN4, serving as the theoretical active center structure of Ni–N–C SACs for investigating the relationship between CO2 reduction and cation concentration.

Stable adsorption and effective activation of CO2 on the catalyst surface are essential prerequisites for efficient CO2RR. Accordingly, constant-potential AIMD simulations were performed to investigate CO2 adsorption on Ni–N–C SACs at different K+ concentrations under pH 6.8 and an applied potential of −0.7 VRHE, consistent with experimental conditions.9–11 Four K+ concentrations were considered by introducing 0, 1, 2, or 3 K atoms into the supercell (Fig. S1). The introduced K atoms were ionized into K+ through electron transfer to the catalyst surface. Note that the 3K+ condition modeled here is close to a K+ concentration of 2 M, as used by Wu et al. in their experiments.23 As shown in Fig. 1a, CO2 adsorption in all four solvent environments (0K+, 1K+, 2K+, and 3K+) reached energetic equilibrium within 5 ps AIMD simulations. Notably, under the 0K+ condition, CO2 could not be stably adsorbed and tended to diffuse back into the solvent, as evidenced by a persistent Ni–C distance greater than 3 Å. Our AIMD simulations are in good agreement with experimental observations that Ni–N–C SACs are inactive for CO2RR in the absence of K+. In sharp contrast, upon introducing K+, irrespective of concentration, the Ni–C bond length fluctuated around ∼2 Å (Fig. 1b), indicating the formation of a chemical bond between CO2 and the Ni active site. These results demonstrate that K+ plays a crucial role in facilitating CO2 adsorption.

To evaluate the effect of K+ concentration, we then calculated the free-energy profiles for CO2 adsorption (* + CO2(g) → *CO2) using constrained constant-potential AIMD simulations with a slow-growth approach. As shown in Fig. 1c (more details can be found in Fig. S2–S4), the free-energy barrier for CO2 adsorption on Ni–N–C SACs decreases progressively with increasing K+ concentration, reaching 0.29, 0.20, and 0.10 eV under 1K+, 2K+, and 3K+ conditions, respectively. Remarkably, the adsorption process is endothermic under 1K+ conditions, but it becomes exothermic when the K+ concentration increases to 2K+ and 3K+. In contrast, when the adsorption configuration involves the O atom bonded to the Ni site, (i.e., OCO adsorption), negligible cation effects can be observed, with free energy barriers >0.80 eV (Fig. S5). This indicates that the OCO adsorption configuration is independent of cation concentration and energetically unfavorable.

To evaluate the degree of CO2 activation, we further analyzed the bending angle distribution of *CO2 obtained from AIMD trajectories. As shown in Fig. 1d, the most probable bending angle decreases from 136.55° at 1K+ to 135.38° at 2K+ and further to 133.88° at 3K+. Because a greater deviation from the linear 180° geometry corresponds to stronger activation, these results indicate enhanced CO2 activation at higher K+ concentrations. Meanwhile, increasing the K+ concentration leads to a gradual elongation of the total C–O bond, accompanied by a shortening of the Ni–C bond (Fig. S6). Overall, increasing K+ concentration improves both the kinetics and thermodynamics of CO2 adsorption and promotes CO2 activation, thereby facilitating subsequent CO2 reduction.

Concentration effect of K+ on CO2 reduction

Mechanistically, adsorbed *CO2 undergoes two proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) steps to form CO, with the first PCET step corresponding to the hydrogenation of *CO2 to *COOH generally recognized as the rate-determining step of CO2RR.26–30 To elucidate the effect of cation concentration on this critical step, we investigated the reaction kinetics for the hydrogenation process of *CO2 (*CO2 + H2O + e− → *COOH + OH−) under different K+ concentrations. As shown in Fig. 2a (more details can be found in Fig. S7–S9), under the 1K+ condition, the hydrogenation of *CO2 exhibits a relatively high free-energy barrier of 0.86 eV at −0.7 VRHE, indicating sluggish kinetics and a substantial obstacle to CO2RR. Notably, this barrier decreases to 0.66 and 0.44 eV under 2K+ and 3K+ conditions, respectively, demonstrating that increasing K+ concentration significantly facilitates *COOH formation. Moreover, statistical analysis of AIMD trajectories reveals a progressive shortening of the Ni–C bond length with increasing cation concentration (Fig. S10). This trend indicates that higher K+ concentrations strengthen the interaction between the *COOH intermediate and the Ni active center, thereby facilitating the reaction kinetics.

Free energy profiles for (a) *COOH formation, (b) *CO formation, (c) *CO desorption and (d) *H adsorption on Ni–N–C SACs under different K+ conditions.

Subsequently, the second PCET step, corresponding to the conversion of *COOH to adsorbed *CO (*COOH + e− → *CO + OH−) on Ni–N–C SACs, was investigated at −0.7 VRHE under different K+ concentrations. As shown in Fig. 2b and S11–S13, the free-energy barriers for this step are minimally affected by cation concentration, with values of 0.31, 0.38, and 0.32 eV for 1K+, 2K+, and 3K+, respectively. These relatively low barriers indicate facile *CO formation, confirming that this step is not rate-limiting and is largely insensitive to K+ concentration. The generated *CO must subsequently desorb from the Ni active site to release gaseous CO and regenerate the catalyst for the next CO2RR cycle. As presented in Fig. 2c and S14–S16, the calculated free-energy barriers for *CO desorption at −0.7 VRHE are 0.21, 0.27, and 0.39 eV for eV 1K+, 2K+, and 3K+ conditions, respectively. These results reveal that *CO desorption becomes progressively less favorable as cation concentration increases. Nevertheless, even under the 3K+ condition, the barrier remains sufficiently low to sustain efficient CO production. Overall, the presence of K+ stabilizes CO2 adsorption and significantly enhances CO2RR kinetics by lowering the energy barrier of the rate-determining *COOH formation step, although excessive cation concentration begins to impede *CO desorption.

In addition to CO2RR on Ni–N–C SACs, the competitive HER must be considered to evaluate catalytic selectivity under different K+ concentrations. Specifically, the PCET processes may occur either on adsorbed *CO2, leading to CO2 conversion, or directly on the Ni active center, resulting in competitive H2 generation. To assess this competition, we calculated the free-energy barrier of the Volmer step (*H2O + e− → *H + OH−) on the Ni active center at varying K+ concentrations (Fig. 2d and S17–S19). Constrained AIMD simulations at −0.7 VRHE reveal a slightly decreasing yet consistently high free-energy barrier (>1.2 eV) for the Volmer step as K+ concentration increases. Notably, these barriers are substantially higher than those associated with CO2 adsorption and reduction, indicating that the Ni active center effectively suppresses HER and thus maintains high catalytic selectivity for CO2RR at −0.7 VRHE. To ensure the accuracy of the kinetic descriptions of key steps in CO2RR, multiple independent constrained AIMD simulations were performed (Fig. S20). Indeed, negligible error fluctuations can be observed, which ensures the reliability of our theoretical results and mechanistic conclusions.

Origin of cation concentration effect on CO2RR

Understanding the role of cation concentration from the perspective of the interfacial microenvironment and electronic structure is essential for optimizing catalytic performance beyond the intrinsic properties of active sites. As shown in Fig. 3a and S21, we first examined the effect of K+ concentration on the interfacial microenvironment in the absence of CO2. The results indicate that the average number of hydrogen bonds of each explicit H2O molecule decreases progressively with increasing K+ concentration, being 3.02, 2.86, and 2.78 for 1K+, 2K+, and 3K+ conditions, respectively. This disruption of the hydrogen-bond network facilitates the transport of CO2 through the solvent, enabling easier access to the Ni active centers. In addition, K+ concentration markedly influences the spatial orientation of interfacial H2O molecules (Fig. S22). Under the 1K+ condition, most interfacial water molecules adopt an “O-down” configuration, whereas at 2K+ and 3K+ concentrations, a greater proportion of H2O molecules exhibit an “H-down” orientation. Correspondingly, the shortest distance between adsorbed CO2 and the hydrogen atoms of interfacial H2O decreases with increasing cation concentration (Fig. 3b). The prevalence of the “H-down” configuration promotes CO2 hydrogenation by shortening the proton-transfer distance, thereby lowering the kinetic barrier for CO2 hydrogenation under high-cation-concentration conditions.

The promotional effect of K+ concentration on CO2RR can also be elucidated from an electronic-structure perspective. Across the three K+ concentrations, a clear correlation is observed between the Ni–C bond length and the excess charge on the adsorbed *CO2 species (Fig. 3c). Under the 1K+ condition, as the Ni–C bond length varies from 1.90 to 2.04 Å, the charge transferred to *CO2 ranges from 0.59 to 0.67 e. In contrast, under 2K+ and 3K+ conditions, *CO2 gains a relatively larger amount of charge (0.61–0.72 e) from the catalyst. The increased charge transfer strengthens the interaction between *CO2 and the positively polarized Ni active center, thereby enhancing CO2 adsorption and activation. Besides, the dz2 orbitals of the Ni site gradually approach the Fermi level with cation concentration (Fig. S23), becoming more active occupied orbitals. After CO2 adsorption, these occupied orbitals disappear significantly, while new bonding orbitals emerge at positions far below the Fermi level, which suggests the formation of more stable chemical bonds.

To further quantify the Ni–*CO2 interaction at different K+ concentrations, we performed crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) analysis. A more negative integrated COHP (ICOHP) value corresponds to a stronger chemical bond. As shown in Fig. 3d, the number of bonding states below the Fermi level increases with rising K+ concentration. Consequently, the ICOHP value of the Ni–C bond shifts toward more negative values as cation concentration increases, indicating progressively strengthened catalyst–adsorbate interactions.

Theoretical prediction of the existence of an optimal cation concentration

The above DFT calculations, based on a systematic analysis of cation-concentration-dependent reaction pathways and kinetic responses, demonstrate the positive role of K+ in promoting CO2RR on Ni–N–C SACs. However, an important question remains: does continuously increasing K+ concentration always enhance CO2RR kinetics before the electrolyte reaches saturation? Indeed, we have found that the free-energy barrier for CO desorption increases with increasing K+ concentration. This trend originates primarily from the strengthened interaction between *CO and the Ni active site, as evidenced by the COHP analysis of the Ni–C(*CO) bond (Fig. S24). To further clarify whether the overall CO2RR kinetics would continue to improve at higher cation concentrations, we extended the concentration range to 4K+ and investigated the corresponding reaction mechanism (Fig. S25–S28). As shown in Fig. S26, the formation of *COOH becomes more kinetically favorable in the 4K+ environment, with a reduced free-energy barrier of 0.35 eV. However, the free-energy barrier for *CO desorption increases to 0.48 eV (Fig. S28), exceeding that of *CO2 hydrogenation. This shift indicates that, under the 4K+ condition, the rate-determining step changes from *CO2 hydrogenation to *CO desorption.

Fig. 4 summarizes the cation concentration-dependent free-energy barrier of each elementary step in the CO2RR pathway. Based on the trend obtained through linear fitting, the difference of the free-energy barriers between *CO2 hydrogenation and *CO desorption progressively decreases with increasing K+ concentration. At a concentration (Ccross) between 3K+ and 4K+, the free-energy barriers for *COOH formation and *CO desorption become comparable. Beyond this point, *CO desorption overtakes CO2 hydrogenation as the dominant rate-limiting step for CO2RR on Ni–N–C SACs, suggesting that excessive cation concentration would ultimately impede overall CO2RR kinetics. Overall, we can conclude that for CO2RR, a higher K+ concentration is not necessarily better, but rather there exists an optimal K+ concentration before electrolyte saturation, at which the overall reaction kinetics reach their maximum. It should be specified here that the calculated free-energy barriers are used primarily to identify relative changes in reaction kinetics and rate-limiting behavior, rather than to provide quantitatively exact activation energies under operating electrochemical conditions.

Our results are consistent with the Sabatier principle33 as the cation concentration can be linearly translated into the strength of the catalyst–adsorbate interactions, based on the clear linear relationship observed between ICOHP values and cation concentration (Fig. S29). Although our DFT/AIMD model does not explicitly include all aspects of the electrochemical environment, such as anions, potential-dependent double-layer structure, long-range space-charge effects, or local pH gradients, the systematic promotion of CO2 reduction kinetics with increasing local K+ concentration arising from stabilization of reaction intermediates and modification of the interfacial environment relies on relative trends that are expected to be robust.

Experimental validation of the existence of an optimal cation concentration

To verify whether there is indeed an optimal cation concentration for Ni–N–C SACs in catalyzing CO2RR, we experimentally prepared Ni–N–C SACs and evaluated their catalytic performance over a wide range of K+ concentrations. First, nickel tetraphenylporphyrin (NiTPP) molecules were loaded onto acid-treated carbon nanotubes (CNTs), followed by precise pyrolysis, resulting in the synthesis of Ni site-containing SACs (Ni–Npyrrolic@CNT, Fig. S30). In the XRD pattern (Fig. S31), the diffraction peaks of the (102) crystal plane of CNTs and the (111) crystal plane of metallic Ni overlap significantly, indicating that the formation of nickel nanoclusters or nanoparticles cannot be excluded. To resolve this ambiguity, we further employed aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-HAADF-STEM) for detailed characterization at the atomic resolution level. As shown in Fig. 5a, isolated bright spots are scattered across the CNTs surface, confirming atomic-level Ni dispersion and the absence of nanoclusters. A three-dimensional surface plot further visualizes the precise locations of Ni atoms (Fig. 5b). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping provides additional evidence for the uniform distribution of Ni and N atoms on the CNT surface (Fig. S32). In addition, the Ni 2p X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra (Fig. 5c) show that the Ni 2p3/2 peak is close to the Ni2+ peak, while the absence of the Ni0 peak supports the conclusion of atomically dispersed Ni. The N 1s spectrum reveals four types of nitrogen: pyrrolic N, Ni–N, pyridinic N, and graphitic N (Fig. 5d). Among these, pyrrolic N and Ni–N are dominant, indicating that controlled pyrolysis significantly preserves the coordination environment of Ni atoms within NiTPP molecules. Furthermore, soft X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS, Fig. 5e) at the N K-edge demonstrates that most nitrogen exists in the pyrrolic form. Overall, the above results demonstrate that we have successfully prepared Ni–N–C SACs featuring pyrrolic N atoms. In the Ni–Npyrrolic@CNT, the Ni and N contents are 0.59 wt% and 2.49 wt%, respectively. The BET surface area is 146.4 m2 g−1 (Fig. S33), and it contains mesopores with an average pore diameter of 21.5 nm, which may be a three-dimensional pore structure formed by the intertwining of carbon nanotubes.

(f) LSV curves in electrolytes with different K+ concentrations. (g) Faradaic efficiency of CO. (h) ECSA-normalized JCOversus K+ concentration. (i) TOF value under different K+ concentrations.

KCl solutions of varying molarities were then introduced into a neutral electrolyte (0.5 M KHCO3) to test the effect of K+ concentration on the electrocatalytic performance of as-obtained Ni–Npyrrolic@CNT toward CO2RR. As shown in Fig. 5f, when the KCl concentration increased from 0 to 1.5 M, the cathodic current increased sharply to several hundred mA. As the KCl concentration continued to rise beyond this point, the cathode current reached a plateau, with the products consisting primarily of CO and H2. As the KCl concentration increased to 1.5 M, CO efficiency continuously improved between −0.6 and −0.9 VRHE (Fig. 5g). Further increases in K+ concentration promoted hydrogen evolution, indicating that regulating K+ within an optimal concentration range (0−1.5 M) not only accelerates overall kinetic but also enhances CO selectivity. The electrochemical surface area (ECSA)-normalized current density (Fig. S34 and S35) for CO production (JCO) as a function of cation concentration is shown in Fig. 5h. As the K+ concentration increases from 0.5 M to 2.5 M, the ECSA-normalized JCO exhibits a steady increase, indicating that elevated cation concentrations enhance reaction kinetics. However, when the K+ concentration exceeds 2.5 M, a decline in JCO is observed, suggesting an inhibitory effect at higher concentrations. This adverse trend becomes more pronounced at more negative potentials. The turnover frequency (TOF) values showed a similar growth trend, further demonstrating the crucial role of K+ in accelerating reaction kinetics (Fig. 5i). Overall, the experimental results are in excellent agreement with the theoretical predictions, revealing a volcano-type dependence of CO production activity on cation concentration and confirming the existence of an optimal K+ concentration for maximizing CO2RR performance.

Conclusions

In summary, by means of constant-potential AIMD simulations, we comprehensively explored the effect of K concentration on the reaction kinetics of CO2RR over Ni–N–C SACs. Our theoretical results reveal that K+ concentration plays a pivotal role in stabilizing and activating adsorbed *CO2 by transferring more electrons. Further mechanistic simulations indicate that the kinetic process of *CO2 protonation to *COOH is more sensitive to changes in K+ concentration, enabling the overcoming of the original rate-determining step. However, the gradually enhanced Ni–C bond with K+ concentration makes *CO desorption kinetically unfavorable, which will replace *COOH formation as the new rate-determining step. As a result, excessively increasing the K+ concentration can become counterproductive for CO2RR, which indicates the presence of an optimal K+ concentration that balances the reaction kinetics for *COOH formation and *CO desorption. The cation concentration-dependent reaction kinetics is further supported by the experimentally measured partial current density of CO production. Our findings redefine the role of electrolyte composition by identifying cation concentration as a previously overlooked but general kinetic descriptor, and they establish a new paradigm for electrolyte engineering in CO2RR and other electrocatalytic reactions.

Methods

DFT computations and models

All atomic-scale simulations were performed using the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP) code with the projector augmented wave pseudopotential based on density functional theory (DFT).34,35 The revised Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (RPBE) functional was employed to describe the exchange–correlation interactions within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA).36,37 The kinetic energy cutoff of the plane wave was set to be 400 eV, and the convergence criterion for the residual forces and total energies was set to be 0.05 eV Å−1 and 10−5 eV, respectively. Bader charge calculation was performed to analyze the charge population and charge transfer.38 The integrated crystal orbital Hamilton population (ICOHP) was employed to evaluate the strength of the Ni–C bond.39 The empirical correction in Grimme's method (DFT + D3) was used to describe the van der Waals interactions.40 The water/Ni–N–C interface was constructed using 80 explicit water molecules (average density ∼1 g cm−3) on a rectangular Ni–N–C configuration containing 62 carbon atoms, 4 nitrogen atoms, 1 nickel atom, and 8 hydrogen atoms. The cell size of the model was 17.22 × 12.78 × 28 Å3, with a 15 Å-vacuum region along the z-direction to prevent interactions between the periodic slabs. A 1 × 1 × 1 Gamma-centered k-point mesh was employed to sample the Brillouin zone.41 Different amounts of potassium ions (K+) were introduced into the explicit water layer to simulate varying cation concentrations.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Constant-potential ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were performed using CP-VASP,42,43 which determines the target electrode potential based on the Fermi level that fluctuates with the number of electrons in the system. The implicit solvent environment was simulated using the VASPsol code,44 which treats the electrode–electrolyte interface as a polarizable continuum and places ionic counter-charges at the interface to maintain system electroneutrality. The canonical ensemble (NVT) was imposed by a Nose–Hoover thermostat at 300 K,45,46 and the electrode potential was preset to −0.7 VRHE for the equilibrium AIMD simulations. A 1.2 fs time step was used in AIMD simulations with the hydrogen mass set to 2 atomic mass units.47,48 A standard AIMD simulation of 10 ps was performed at 300 K to allow the solvation structures of cations to reach the relative equilibrium state for subsequent reaction kinetic calculations. The free energy profile was obtained via the thermodynamic integration within the constrained molecular dynamic framework (i.e., the slow-growth approach combined with the bule-moon ensemble). The increment of the collective variable (CV) was set to 0.0003 Å/step for the transition state search process.

In CP-VASP, the electrode potential (Uq) referenced to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) scale is defined as:1Uq (V vs. SHE) = ΦSHE − Φq/ewhere Φq is the electron energy, and ΦSHE is the absolute potential of the SHE, which is set to −4.6 V in this work.

Based on the experimental pH value of 6.8 (0.5 M KHCO3), we converted the electrode potentials to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale in all constant potential computational procedures using the following formula:2Uq (V vs. RHE) = Uq (V vs. SHE) + kBT × ln 10 × 6.8

Reagents and materials

Nickel tetraphenylporphyrin (NiTPP), potassium hydroxide (KOH), glycerol, N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and ethanol were obtained from Aladdin. Multi-walled carbon nanotubes (CNTs, purity >95%, length ∼50 µm and product number: 100240) were obtained from Xianfeng Nano. Nafion perfluorinated resin solution (5 wt% in lower aliphatic alcohols and water, contains 15–20% water) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. All the reagents were used as received without further purification. Deionized water was used throughout this study.

Pre-treatment of CNTs

500 mg of CNTs were dispersed in a beaker containing 49.5 mL of water and 49.5 mL of ethanol. Subsequently, 9 mL of HCl was added, and the mixture was sonicated for 30 minutes. It was then stirred and heated at 60 °C for 6 hours. Finally, the mixture was centrifuged and washed with deionized water until pH ≈ 7.0.

Experimental synthesis of Ni–Npyrrolic@CNT

70 mg of CNTs were dispersed in a solution containing 70 mL DMF by sonication for 30 min (solution A). Then, 70 mg of NiTPP was dispersed in a solution containing 140 mL DMF by sonication for 30 min (solution B). Solution B was then added slowly into solution A with continuous sonication for 30 min. The mixed solution was magnetically stirred for 24 hours at room temperature, after which it was centrifuged and washed twice with DMF, water and ethanol, respectively. Finally, the product was collected for future use after being freeze-dried for 24 hours. Then, the product was placed in a tube furnace, heated under Ar gas protection at a rate of 2 °C min−1 to 460 °C and then kept for 2 h, after which it was naturally brought to room temperature. The resulting sample was referred to as Ni–Npyrrolic@CNT.

Characterization

Powder X-ray diffraction patterns of samples were recorded on a Rigaku Miniflex-600 diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (Cu Kα, λ = 0.15406 nm, 40 kV and 15 mA). TEM images were recorded using a Hitachi-7650 operated at 100 kV. High-resolution TEM and HAADF-STEM images were recorded using a FEI Tecnai G2F20 S-Twin high-resolution transmission electron microscope working at 200 kV, as well as a JEOL JEM-ARM200F TEM/STEM with a spherical aberration corrector working at 200 kV. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, VG-Multilab2000) was performed by using Al Kα radiation (1486.71 eV). A gas chromatograph (PANNA A91) equipped with TCD and FID detectors was used to detect the generated gas. NMR spectra of the liquid products were acquired using a Bruker AVANCE Ⅲ 400MHZ spectrometer using dimethyl sulfoxide as an internal standard. The specific surface area of the materials was measured using a specific surface area analyzer (ASAP 2460), and the elemental composition of the materials was analyzed using an inductively coupled plasma emission spectrometer (Agilent 725-ES).
