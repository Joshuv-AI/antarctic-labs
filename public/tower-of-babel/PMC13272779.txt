Sustainable removal of Acid Black 172 from wastewater using Lantana camara derived biochar insights from adsorption and statistical physics modeling
Authors: Mohammod Hafizur Rahman, Ganesh Jethave, Umesh Fegade, Ayed Eid Alluqmani, Muhammad Ali Martuza, Md Ehtesamul Haque, Ziad Shatnawi, Ammar Fayez Al-Shayeb, Md Kamrul Islam, Md Arifuzzaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe discharge of dye-contaminated wastewater from textile and related industries represents a serious environmental concern due to the persistence, toxicity, and resistance of synthetic dyes to natural degradation. In this study, Lantana camara derived biochar (LCB) was investigated as a sustainable and low-cost adsorbent for the removal of Acid Black 172 (AB172) dye from aqueous solutions. The structural and surface properties of LCB were characterized using BET, FTIR, and HRTEM analyses, revealing a mesoporous structure with a surface area of 12.14 m² g⁻¹, pore diameter of 6.8 nm, and total pore volume of 0.031 cm³ g⁻¹. Batch adsorption experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of pH, contact time, adsorbent dosage, and temperature. The adsorption process showed strong pH dependence, achieving maximum dye removal at pH 3, with equilibrium reached within 90 min. The Langmuir model predicted a maximum adsorption capacity of 303.03 mg g⁻¹, indicating a strong affinity between AB172 molecules and LCB surface sites. Kinetic analysis revealed that the adsorption followed a pseudo-second-order model, while thermodynamic parameters confirmed that the process is spontaneous and endothermic. In addition to this, the use of statistical physics modeling helped understand more about the adsorption process, such as adsorption orientation, adsorption energy, and the mechanism of surface interaction. These aspects set apart the study from other studies on adsorption. In the case of AB172 adsorption, the process was mainly influenced by electrostatic attraction, pore filling, van der Waals force, and hydrogen bonding interactions. Regeneration studies demonstrated high stability of the adsorbent, maintaining over 91% removal efficiency after five adsorption–desorption cycles. Statistical physics modeling and experimental studies on adsorption help in gaining a better insight into the processes involved in the dye-biochar interactions than traditional adsorption experiments do. These findings highlight LCB as a promising, environmentally sustainable, and economically viable adsorbent for the treatment of dye-contaminated wastewater.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57437-x.Keywords: Biochar adsorption, Acid Black 172, Acid Black 172 dye, Wastewater treatment, Adsorption kinetics and isotherms, Statistical and physics modelingSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Water is one of the most important natural resources that help in maintaining ecological balance and civilization. But rapid industrialization and urbanization have greatly affected the quality of water due to the release of harmful pollutants including dyes, heavy metals, medicines, pesticides, and organic substances in water. Of these pollutants, artificial dyes pose a great threat due to their complex aromatic structure, toxicity, carcinogenic nature, chemical stability, and biodegradability. The textile wastewater consisting of azo dyes greatly reduces light intensity, causes photosynthesis inhibition, and depletes oxygen in water. The emission of dye-contaminated wastewater has significantly increased as a result of the widespread use of synthetic dyes in a variety of sectors, including printing, paper, and textiles. These colours may linger in water bodies and are often resistant to degradation, endangering human health and the environment. Therefore, it is crucial to identify sustainable and effective dye removal techniques in order to protect ecosystems and guarantee water quality. One of the major industrial sectors that contribute to the world economy includes the textile industry; however, it also poses as a significant contributor to water pollution owing to the use of artificial dyes in the dyeing process1. However, because of the harmful consequences of synthetic dyes, this thriving sector also carries a heavy environmental cost. Although these colours make fabrics more aesthetically pleasing, they seriously endanger human health and aquatic ecosystems. When they are released into bodies of water, they pollute the water, disturb aquatic life, and jeopardize the health of those who rely on these water supplies2. According to industry data, the world generates 1.8 to 1.9 × 106 tons of dyestuffs yearly, and about 11,000 distinct dyes and pigments are used, mostly in the food, textile, cosmetics, leather, paper, and plastics industries3. The majority of dyestuffs have unique properties, such as light fastness and chemical stability. Numerous dyes pose a serious risk to the aquatic environment because of their chemical makeup, which also has an adverse influence on plants, animal habitats, and human health4. The most popular fabric dye, AB172, is one of the main classes of these dyes and may irritate the respiratory system and be dangerous if breathed. Mutagenesis in certain scientific investigations can cause damage to the kidney, liver, reproductive system, heart, brain, and neurological system5,6. The incidence of dyestuffs in collecting waters slows down photosynthetic activity by blocking light transmission, apart after the rare studies that demonstrate particular dyes are detrimental. During the degradation process, dyes reduce the dissolved oxygen levels in the collected water, lowering the water quality standards for aquatic life7. It has negative visual and aesthetic effects that could be detrimental to both plants and fish health and reproduction8.

Researchers have been working nonstop to create efficient dye removal methods from contaminated effluents in response to this concerning circumstance. Numerous methods, from sophisticated oxidation processes to physical and chemical treatments, have been investigated throughout time. Even if these techniques are somewhat successful, they often have drawbacks of their own, such as high expenses, low efficiency, and the production of additional hazardous byproducts9–11. In the past, scientists have investigated a variety of materials in adsorption-based methods for wastewater color removal. Activated carbon, zeolites, and other types of clay are often used materials, each having unique benefits and drawbacks. Furthermore, methods including membrane filtration, coagulation, and adsorption have been used to address this widespread problem. Although these approaches have shown potential, there is an ongoing search for effective and sustainable substitutes that are both financially feasible and ecologically benign12. Biochar has become a promising adsorbent in recent years for the economical removal of dyes and other contaminants from wastewater. For of its larger surface area and abundance of serviceable groups, biochar a kind of charcoal produced from the pyrolysis of organic materials is a great choice for this application13–15. The novelty of this study lies in the sustainable conversion of invasive Lantana camara biomass into functional biochar for efficient Acid Black 172 removal, combined with detailed statistical physics modeling to elucidate adsorption mechanisms, adsorption energies, and molecular interaction behavior.

The use of Lantana camara biomass for biochar production was influenced by its abundance, quick propagation, invasiveness, and underutilization. Lantana camara is known to be an invasive plant that causes ecological imbalance due to its unchecked proliferation. The LC plant grow in the May - June month in India. Lantana camara is considered an invasive plant species with limited economic utilization and significant ecological impact in many regions. The plant harvested from the bottom of the stem; it will grow again and gain very fast. The biochar of LC plant is very economical than the commercially available activated carbon. In addition to its exceptional adsorption ability, LCB is also more affordable and environmentally favourable than other materials. Additionally, its porous nature ensures effective removal from polluted water sources by offering plenty of places for dye molecules to cling. LCB, which comes from renewable resources, may provide a long-term solution to the issue of dye pollution16–18. The article investigates the damaging effects of synthetic dyes, focusing on the environmental and human health risks associated with their unrestrained discharge. It examines and evaluates the benefits and drawbacks of the many methods and substances that have been used historically for color removal. After that, the paper concentrates on LCB’s potential as an adsorbent, explaining its special qualities that make it a desirable option for wastewater treatment. This study intends to make a substantial contribution to the continuing efforts to lessen the environmental impact of dye pollution by thoroughly assessing LCB’s efficacy in contrast to conventional materials. This study aims to open the door for creative and sustainable dye wastewater treatment solutions by thoroughly examining LCB’s adsorption properties and possible real-world applications.

There are now about 9000 distinct dyes in use, each of which represents a distinct kind of chemical application19–22. These harmful pollutants are often eliminated from water by a variety of physical and chemical methods, including adsorption and precipitation23–25. Electrocoagulation, photocatalysis, coagulation, distillation, ion exchange, reverse osmosis, precipitation, biodegradation, and many other established processes have some drawbacks. Environmental scientists are compelled to remove dyes from the environment due to their high toxicity and potential to cause cancer26–30. Among the available wastewater treatment technologies, adsorption has attracted considerable attention because of its operational simplicity, high efficiency, low cost, regeneration capability, and minimal generation of secondary pollutants. Compared with advanced oxidation, membrane filtration, and coagulation processes, adsorption using biochar-based materials offers a sustainable and environmentally friendly alternative for dye removal from contaminated water.

Material and method

Instrumentation

The morphology and structural properties were evaluated by field emission scanning electron microscopy (Carl Zeiss Model Supra 55 Field FE-SEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM; Jeol Jem 2100 Plus) equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis. FTIR spectra were recorded using a Bruker Alpha FTIR spectrometer in the range of 4000–400 cm⁻¹ to identify the functional groups present on the biochar surface and The Malvern Nano ZS instrument was used for the determination of zeta potential of the LCB. The Shimadzu UV spectrophotometer (UV mini-80 1240) and Digital pH-metre (Equip-Tronics model Eq. 610) were used the performance of experiment. BET surface area analysis was performed after degassing the samples at 120 °C for 6 h under vacuum conditions to remove physically adsorbed moisture and impurities.

Chemical and reagents

For experiments, high-purity (> 98%) precursor materials of reagent grade are purchased. Furthermore, Fischer Scientific in India provided sodium hydroxide pellets (NaOH), hydrochloric acid (HCl). The adsorption selectivity of the produced Lantana camara Biochar (LCB) was determined by means of a preliminary screening test involving several selected dyes. To guarantee their freshness and efficacy in our research projects, these reagents were used as soon as they were acquired. All processes in this study, including the synthesis of biochar, require de-ionized (MilliQ) water.

Preparation method of LCB

To examine its effectiveness in dye adsorption, LCB was made from the stems of Lantana camara trees for this investigation. Fresh stems of Lantana camara L. were collected from [Bhusawal, Maharashtra state, India] non-protected areas with appropriate permission from the relevant authority. The collection of plant material complied with applicable institutional and national guidelines and legislation. Lantana camara is an invasive species and is not listed as a threatened or endangered species under the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List or the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES). To maintain the ecological balance, developed tree stems and branches were first gathered. The stems and branches were picked with care and showed no symptoms of deterioration. The gathered tree stems were washed properly to get rid of any clinging materials, dirt, and debris. The stems were cleaned and then let to air dry in the sun to get the appropriate moisture content. A motorized cutter was used to cut the tree stems into smaller pieces when they were totally dried. To enable homogeneous pyrolysis, smaller pieces of stem and branches were converted into small fine particle. Pyrolysis, a procedure that involves heating organic materials with a restricted oxygen supply, was applied to the shredded tree stem particles. The stem and branches particles were burned to a temperature between 500 and 700 °C in a specifically made pyrolysis chamber for a predetermined amount of time, usually several hours. The organic material was converted into LCB while volatile chemicals were removed thanks to this well-regulated heating procedure27.

The LCB was allowed to progressively cool to ambient temperature after the pyrolysis. The LCB stabilized and its chemical and physical characteristics were improved for adsorption throughout this chilling period. A mortar and pestle were used to further grind the cooled LCB into a fine powder. To ensure homogeneity, the LCB powder was further filtered to produce particles in certain size extend, usually within 100–200 mesh size28. Before being used in a dye adsorption experiment, the manufactured LCB powder remained kept in sealed dish to avoid water related disturbance and preserve efficacy with adsorption.

Biochar Characterization: FTIR, TEM, and BET were used to characterize the produced LCB. These methods improved our knowledge of the LCB’s potential for dye adsorption by offering in-depth insights into its structural, morphological, and chemical characteristics29. The LCB’s specific surface area and porosity are determined by BET surface area analysis. Numerous active sites for adsorption were present, according to the research, which provided significant knowledge on the surface area. LCB’s large surface area indicated that it had a considerable ability to intermingle with dye molecules, which improve adsorption capability30. HRTEM was used to investigate the nanoscale morphological properties of the LCB. These pictures provide important details regarding the LCB surface’s adsorption capabilities, including its porosity, particle size allotment, and the existence of all nanoparticles or microstructures31. Such functional groups on the LCB surface were investigated using FTIR spectroscopy like (-OH), (C = O) groups etc. The LCB-AB172 interaction during adsorption were revealed by the attendance of these functional groups. Furthermore, the surface chemistry of the LCB was discovered through FTIR analysis, which clarified the kind of interactions between the LCB and the dyes32. A thorough description of the LCB material was produced by combining the findings from FTIR spectroscopy, HRTEM imaging, and BET surface area analysis.

Using biochar for batch adsorption of dye

Making of dye solution

The dye solution was prepared using a methodical way to guarantee accuracy and repeatability. To create the stock solution, the right amount of dye was dissolved in deionized water, guaranteeing a uniform combination. To get the appropriate concentrations for the adsorption studies, further dilutions were carried out precisely. The basis for precise and trustworthy batch adsorption tests was established by this strict preparation technique33.

Dynamic adsorption process monitoring

Real-time monitoring of the adsorption process was prioritized in this case. By ensuring dynamic agitation via the use of a shaker equipment, LCB and the dye were able to interact effectively. An additional degree of control was provided by continuously monitoring the pH levels throughout the experiment, which made it possible to comprehend the complex interactions between the LCB-AB172.

Quantification UV-Vis spectrophotometry utilization

Following adsorption, the solutions’ residual AB172 dye concentrations were computed with greater precision. At a certain wavelength, the absorption intensity was determined using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Such accurate assessment of the leftover AB172 dye concentration in each sample was made possible by the creation of an extensive calibration curve using standard solutions with known concentrations. This improved quantification method increased the findings’ dependability and provided a strong basis for interpreting the data. Essentially, this enhanced process for AB172 dye batch adsorption blends careful planning, dynamic testing, and sophisticated quantification methods. The end product is an advanced and accurate method that not singly improves the dependability of investigational results but also establishes a standard for further research for dye adsorption employing LCB related arena. Following equilibrium, the supernatant was gathered, and the UV-Vis technique was used to measure the AB172 content at λmax = 575 nm34.

Data analysis for AB172 dye adsorption on biochar

The Langmuir and Freundlich models, two advanced adsorption isotherm models, were used in a thorough examination of the gathered adsorption data. The complex adsorption behaviour of AB172 dye on the LCB surface was revealed thanks in large part to these models. This analytical method shed insight on the effectiveness of the adsorption process by offering a sophisticated knowledge of the interaction dynamics between the dye molecules and the LCB.

Result and discussion

Characterization result

HRTEM analysis showed that LCB exhibits a porous morphology characterized by flake-like pores with irregular mesoporous structures, which could enhance diffusion and adsorption of dye molecules onto the surface of the adsorbent. The porous structure identified from TEM images is consistent with BET results and suggests that there are available adsorption sites that contribute to effective dye adsorption. In addition, the FTIR results confirm that there are functional groups present in the samples, including –OH, C = O, and C-O groups, which play an important role in adsorption processes.

The HR-TEM low and high magnification pictures of LCB are shown in Fig. 1a and b. It is clear that the LCB has a flake type nanostructure. The Fig. 1c shows the Selected Area Electron Diffraction (SAED) image of HBK, it reveals the clear difference between the sphere’s centre and edges, suggesting that the LCB has a core-shell structure36,37. LCB has mesoporous structures, as shown by the type IV isotherm with an H3-type hysteresis loop seen in the N2 isotherm (Fig. 1d and f). Effect of pH

One crucial controlling aspect is the pH of the aqueous solution, which has a significant impact on adsorption. The effect of the solution’s initial pH (about 6) on the adsorption of AB172 after the aqueous solution was studied. A stable room temperature of 298 K (K) and an primary AB172 concentration of 50 mg/L were maintained while the pH standard values were adjusted throughout a broad ranging from 2.0 to 10. The dye adsorption rises with increasing pH, as seen in Fig. 3a. The highest AB172 adsorption is reached at pH 3.0 with a maximum adsorption of 98.48%. As a result, pH 3.0 was used for all further studies.

The anionic diazo dye adsorbed on the positively charged adsorbent surface and exhibit a strong electrostatic affinity at pH 3.0. There are more negatively charged sites and fewer positively charged ones when pH rises. Due to electrostatic repulsion, dye anions are unable to adhere to the negatively charged areas of the adsorbent. Furthermore, by generating ionic repulsion between the negatively charged surface and the anionic dye molecules, the presence of OH- ions at higher pH values results in decreased adsorption41. Moreover, the observed reduction in adsorption is partly explained by the absence of exchangeable anions on the adsorbent’s outer surface at higher pH values42.

Contact time influence

To find the equilibrium time for AB172 adsorption, the contact time was methodically changed from 0 to 90 min at various temperatures. The results revealed that AB172 adsorption increased steadily with contact duration, plateauing at 90 min. To assure correct results, the batches have been repeated at least three times. The ideal contact time for all ensuing tests was thus determined to be 90 min after a consistent elimination was obtained at this stage (Fig. 3b)43.

An adsorption curve in Fig. 6 illustrates the AB172 adsorbed per unit weight of LCB with time. LCB containing 50, 100, 150, and 200 mg were adsorbed for 90 min on 50 mL of a 50 mg/L AB172 aqueous solution. All experiments are carried out in three batches for getting the precise results. The adsorption process starts from the occupying the active sites by dye molecules which accelerating the rate adsorption. Longer adsorption times gradually occupied the active sites of the adsorbent particles, resulting in a lower adsorption rate and slope. As the surface of the adsorbent particles got closer to adsorption saturation, the adsorption amount finally reached a value that was almost constant. Because the adsorption and desorption processes coexisted, the adsorbed amount may change somewhat after adsorption saturation, although it was shown to be rather stable44.

Research on desorption and regeneration

To effectively recover dye ions and regenerate the adsorbent, desorption studies must be carried out. The choice of suitable solvents, which is impacted by the adsorption mechanism and the adsorbent’s type, is crucial to success of desorption process. Several potential eluents were evaluated, including deionized water, 0.1 M HCl, 0.1 M ethanol and a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of methanol and deionized water. Notably, a 1:4 (v/v) mixture of methanol and deionized water afforded the highest desorption efficiency, achieving 99% recovery of AB172. Furthermore, as an eluent for AB172 desorption, 0.1 M NaOH performed better than 0.1 M ethanol, 0.1 M HCl, and deionized water.

Restoring the adsorbent material during adsorption procedures is beneficial for lowering processing expenses and making dye recovery from the liquid phase easier. As illustrated in Fig. 9, the recycled adsorbent was employed for numerous adsorption-desorption cycles in this study with the solvents mentioned above. Interestingly, 0.1 M NaOH had a first cycle efficiency of 98% AB172 recovery, indicating that it is a viable alternative for adsorbent renewal. AB172 adsorption steadily decreases as the number of cycle’s increases. After five cycles, the adsorbent’s adsorption capacity dropped from 97.9 to 91.28% (Fig. 6). A 6% reduction in the adsorbent’s capacity to bind dye ions was found. In AB172 adsorption studies, the LCB used for five cycles with efficient results51.

Based on the results of adsorption-desorption tests, it was proved that LCB is characterized by high structural stability and regeneration ability under repetitive usage. The efficiency of adsorption showed insignificant change in five successive cycles, which confirmed the high stability of active adsorption centers as well as mechanical strength of the synthesized biochar.

Proposed adsorption mechanism

The adsorption process of AB172 on LCB occurs via several different interaction mechanisms. Under acidic pH conditions, the functional groups on the surface of LCB get protonated, creating positive adsorption sites on the surface that have a strong attraction towards negative sulfonate ions of AB172 molecules. Besides, the presence of hydroxyl and carbonyl functional groups on the biochar surface allows hydrogen bonding interactions with the dye molecules.

The mesoporous nature of LCB also plays an important role in the adsorption process via increased pore diffusion and availability of internal adsorption sites. Furthermore, the presence of aromatic structures in biochar may allow π-π interactions with the aromatic rings of AB172. Consequently, the entire adsorption process can be attributed to several different processes, including electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, pore filling, and weak intermolecular interactions. The adsorption of AB172 onto LCB is mainly governed by electrostatic attraction between negatively charged dye molecules and protonated functional groups on the biochar surface at acidic pH. In addition, mesoporous structures facilitate pore diffusion and adsorption accessibility, while hydroxyl and aromatic functional groups contribute through hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions52–54.

n parameter analysis

It is beneficial to assign further information on the adsorption process of AB172 on the LCB adsorbent since the parameter n has a steric meaning. Table 4 shows that all computed values of this parameter were less than unity, except for the AB172-LCB adsorption system at 298 K. This indicates that electrostatic repulsion initially kept the AB172 dye molecules separated in solution, preventing aggregation of the adsorbates.

The steric parameter n provides information regarding the interaction behavior between adsorbate molecules and adsorption sites on the adsorbent surface. The obtained n values (< 1) suggest that a single AB172 molecule may interact with multiple active sites through intermolecular interactions such as electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding. Variations in n values with temperature indicate that thermal effects influence adsorption site occupancy and molecular mobility during adsorption.

At temperatures between 298 and 318 K, the n values obtained varied from 0.37 to 0.41, showing a single adsorption site in a non-parallel orientation which shows that AB172 dye molecules may interact with a single adsorption site as a result of thermal agitation as the temperature increases, promoting binding. Figure 8a shows how temperature affects this parameter, showing different impacts on the adsorption of both dyes. In the adsorption of AB172 on LCB, for example, the parameter n rose as the temperature rose, indicating the role of thermal agitation in encouraging dye binding to the adsorption site. Regarding the adsorption of AB172 on LCB, both increases and reductions in the parameter n were noted56.

Nm and Qsat parameter analysis

Nm is defined as the density of the active sites in the LCB material used in AB172 adsorption process. Reductions in the Nm values as a function of increased temperature could possibly suggest some reduction in the accessibility of active adsorption sites due to increased molecular motions and reduced surface-molecule interaction as the temperature increases.

Qsat is the predicted maximum saturation adsorption capacity from statistical physics. Qsat value variations as a function of temperature would be influenced by the state of the adsorption equilibrium and intermolecular forces acting between AB172 molecules and adsorbent surface.

As the amount of dye molecules bound to each site diminishes, the accessible surface area on LCB may increase, exposing more active sites for dye adsorption. As previously indicated, dye adsorption from aqueous solutions on adsorbents has traditionally been examined using the Langmuir model8,18,20. Table 4 displays the Nm values at temperatures of 298, 308, and 318 K are 352.79, 242.19, and 108.03 mg/g, respectively (Fig. 8b). The statistical physics model, on the other hand, is more realistic as it takes into account that different external elements, such temperature and interaction energy, may influence the adsorption process by causing the creation of one (Langmuir instance), two, or more adsorbate layers. It was concluded that the formation of many layers during adsorption may be facilitated by the low interaction energies controlling dye clearance.

The number of layers generated by thermal agitation decreases as the temperature rises, as seen in Fig. 8c. The generated adsorption layers thin as a result of dye molecules moving erratically on chitosan’s adsorption surface due to temperature changes. At temperatures of 298, 308, and 318 K, the corresponding Qsat values were 264.50, 190.37, and 89.68 mg/g (Fig. 8c). According to Qsat measurements, AB172 molecules and LCB nanoparticles seldom ever interact as the temperature increases57.

In terms of industry, the use of biochar from the invasive species Lantana camara presents many environmental and economic benefits since it is cheap, readily available, and classified as an invasive species. Due to the easy preparation process, high adsorption efficiency, and effective regeneration, LCB can be used as a replacement for commercial activated carbon in the treatment of dye wastewater.

Conclusion

For this study, LCB was successfully prepared and used as an eco-friendly adsorbent in removing Acid Black 172 dye from an aqueous medium. The adsorption process was strongly affected by the pH, contact time, and adsorbent dosages, where the maximum adsorption occurred at an acidic environment. Adsorption isotherms and kinetics showed favorable monolayer adsorption characteristics, together with excellent correlation with the pseudo-second-order kinetic and Langmuir isotherm models.

Moreover, the thermodynamic results revealed that the process was spontaneous and endothermic. Also, modeling using statistical physics showed valuable information on adsorption site occupancy, adsorption energy, and interaction mechanism. The mechanism of adsorption was driven by electrostatic force, pore-filling mechanism, hydrogen bonding, and intermolecular forces between AB172 molecules and biochar surface groups. The use of statistical physics modeling together with traditional adsorption studies will give us insight into the relationship between dyes and biochar and could help in designing sustainable adsorbent materials based on biochar.

Additionally, the adsorbent demonstrated good regeneration efficiency and structural stability during multiple adsorption-desorption processes, suggesting practical applicability as a sustainable adsorbent. It can be noted that the present work provides insights about the potential use of Lantana camara biomass to produce biochar for wastewater treatment purposes.
