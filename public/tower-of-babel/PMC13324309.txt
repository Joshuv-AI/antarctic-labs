Intelligent responsive alloy scaffold temporally regulates the immune-osteogenic axis for the treatment of infectious bone defects
Authors: Dong Ruan, Zhiqiang Li, Xueqiang Deng, Hui Jiang, Xiaoyong Lan, Xiang Chen, Chengpeng Qiu, Foqiang Liao, Jiale Zhu, MingChuan Yu, Jilin Xu, Jun Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Excessive immune activation induced by persistent bacterial infection, which further impairs osteogenic function, is a crucial factor contributing to the poor healing of infectious bone defects. Therapeutic strategies targeting a single link often yield limited efficacy. Therefore, we developed an alloy scaffold bone graft (TCMP) with pH-responsive sequential regulation of the immuno-osteogenic axis. During the infection phase, the scaffold mainly releases copper ions to exert potent antibacterial activity, and simultaneously releases Pueraria lobata-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (PELNs) in a pH-responsive manner, thereby effectively suppressing the early inflammatory cytokine storm. In the osteogenesis phase, the scaffold switches its release pattern to sustained magnesium ion release, accompanied by low-level sustained release of PELNs, which targets mesenchymal stem cells, vascular endothelial cells and macrophages to promote the directional recruitment and migration of stem cells, accelerate their osteogenic differentiation, improve the inflammatory immune microenvironment and enhance angiogenesis; consistent with the physiological characteristics of extremely scarce osteoclasts in the early stage of implantation, Mg2+ exerts no regulatory effect on osteoclasts in this study. Mechanistically, TCMP mediates dual-pathway synergistic therapy for infectious bone defects by inhibiting the advanced glycation end products/receptor for advanced glycation end products/nuclear factor-κB (AGEs/RAGE/NF-κB) pathway and activating the hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) signaling pathway. In conclusion, the developed TCMP alloy scaffold integrates the “trinity” functions of antibacterial activity, anti-inflammation, and osteogenesis, comprehensively breaking the vicious cycle of bacterial infection-excessive inflammatory activation-impaired osteogenic capacity, and providing a therapeutic strategy with excellent translational potential for clinical practice.

Introduction

Infectious bone defect is a major challenge in clinical orthopedics. Impaired immune response and chronic inflammation exacerbate tissue damage and hinder bone healing processes [1]. It is characterized by persistent bacterial infection, excessive inflammatory response, impaired bone regeneration function, and immune microenvironment dysregulation [2,3]. Emerging evidence indicates that bacterial pathogens can trigger the release of a cascade of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) and induce immune response disorders, which subsequently enhance osteoclast activation, inhibit osteoblast function, and ultimately form a vicious cycle of bone destruction and regeneration impairment [4,5]. Pathogens and their metabolites disrupt the local redox balance, and the formation of bacterial biofilms further induces a hypoxic microenvironment in bone tissue [6]. Under hypoxic conditions, the stable expression of HIF-1α can induce the synthesis and secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), providing nutritional support for the osteogenic process [7]; meanwhile, it can activate related signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin, promote the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into osteoblasts, and enhance osteoblast activity and mineralization capacity [8,9]. Furthermore, the bone microenvironment under infectious conditions is a key regulatory site for bone regeneration and a core area for dynamic interactions between immune cells, cytokines, and bacterial virulence factors [10,11]. The early inflammatory storm during infection is a critical node determining whether bone tissue ultimately achieves healing or progresses to pathological bone loss: the early inflammatory storm triggered by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) disrupts the homeostasis of the bone microenvironment, induces oxidative stress, impairs bone regeneration function, and leads to delayed healing [12,13]. Notably, the persistent accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) occurs under chronic inflammatory conditions. After AGEs bind to the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), they can regulate the expression of Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and the activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes in macrophages and osteoclast precursors by activating the NF-κB pathway and NLRP3 inflammasomes, ultimately leading to the massive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [14,15]. In summary, the pathogenesis of infectious bone defects is significantly complex, and intervention strategies targeting only a single target or signaling pathway may limit the development of effective therapeutic regimens.

In recent years, research advances in bone grafting technology have primarily focused on enhancing the antibacterial activity and osteogenic efficacy of graft materials [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. For instance, composite biomaterials such as polylactic acid (PLA)-demineralized bone matrix (DBM) scaffolds loaded with gentamicin sulfate (GS) have demonstrated dual efficacy in controlling infection and promoting defect repair. Through localized targeted delivery of antibiotics, such scaffolds can achieve high antibacterial concentrations at defect sites while minimizing systemic toxicity [20]. Additionally, innovative strategies such as 3D-printed porous titanium prostheses combined with flap repair have shown promising prospects in the treatment of large-segment tibial defects, as they not only provide mechanical stability to the defect site but also significantly reduce the recurrence rate of infection [21]. Our research team has previously developed a Ti-3Cu alloy scaffold, which exhibits excellent antibacterial properties and mechanical stability [22]. However, a single Ti-3Cu alloy scaffold is insufficient to meet the complex therapeutic needs of infectious bone defects. Thus, the research team further developed a TCM composite scaffold with greater clinical translation potential, which significantly improves mechanical stability, antibacterial efficacy, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging capacity. This composite scaffold functions by synergistically integrating the bioactive properties of copper (Cu) and magnesium (Mg) into the titanium (Ti) matrix structure: titanium (Ti) provides structural integrity and biocompatibility, copper (Cu) endows the scaffold with antibacterial activity, and magnesium (Mg) regulates osteogenic function [23]. Previous studies have confirmed that copper ions (Cu2+) can disrupt bacterial biofilm structures, which is a core advantage in combating infections associated with bone defects [24]; magnesium ions (Mg2+), on the other hand, can regulate the bone tissue microenvironment (BTME) through integrin-mediated signaling pathways, specifically promoting the adhesion, recruitment, migration and osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), and thereby enhance osteogenic efficacy [25]. Furthermore, the controlled degradability of magnesium facilitates precise regulation of ion release rates, while simultaneously promoting angiogenesis and alleviating excessive inflammatory responses [26]. However, to address the inflammatory cytokine storm induced in the early stage of bacterial infection, the research team further introduced natural plant-derived vesicles—PELNs. As a novel bioactive component, PELNs are rich in osteo inductive microRNAs (miRNAs) and anti-inflammatory compounds in their extracellular vesicles, providing a new therapeutic target for enhancing bone regeneration [27]. In summary, the synergistic combination of TCMP is expected to become an effective therapeutic strategy for infectious bone defects.

Overall, the present study demonstrated that PELNs possess multi-pathway synergistic therapeutic potential for infectious bone defects. We developed an intelligently responsive alloy bone graft capable of temporally regulating the immune-osteogenic axis and established a dual-pathway synergistic therapeutic strategy for infectious bone defects. This therapeutic combination enables comprehensive intervention against infectious bone defects, with core advantages as follows: (1) In the early stage, it primarily releases copper ions and a high dose of PELNs; over time, it gradually transitions to sustained magnesium ion release accompanied by a low dose of PELNs; (2) It exhibits excellent antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and osteogenic efficacy; (3) It can effectively antagonize the early inflammatory cytokine storm by inhibiting the AGEs/RAGE/NF-κB signaling pathway and continuously drive osteogenesis by activating the HIF-1 signaling pathway (Scheme 1).Scheme 1Schematic illustration of the temporal regulation mechanism of TCMP in treating infectious bone defects.

Results and discussion

Isolation of PELNs and investigation into their therapeutic mechanisms for infectious bone defects

Pueraria lobata (Kudzu) is a medicinal herb rich in bioactive flavonoids and polysaccharides, which has been demonstrated to exert multiple pharmacological properties including anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects [28,29]. Recent studies have revealed its capacity to promote tissue repair, a property that may hold promise for the treatment of osteomyelitis and post-traumatic bone infections [30]. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, fresh Pueraria lobata was purchased commercially and first homogenized using a juicer, followed by sequential separation and purification via differential centrifugation and ultracentrifugation. Subsequently, ultra-performance liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry (UPLC-HRMS) was employed to analyze the bioactive components of PELNs. The extracted PELNs were visualized under transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which revealed a spherical morphology (Fig. 1B). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis showed that the particle size of PELNs was 139.4 ± 49.1 nm with a zeta potential of −34.93 ± 0.72 mV (Fig. 1C). To identify the bioactive components and determine their contents, UPLC-HRMS analysis was conducted. A total of 1292 compounds were identified, including 872 and 457 compounds detected in positive and negative ion modes, respectively (Table S2). Among them, Puerarin, Daidzin, Daidzein, Genistein, Calycosin and Formononetin were identified as the core active component group of PELNs, which are the classic dominant bioactive substances of Pueraria lobata. Combined with network pharmacology and transcriptomics verification, these components are the key substances for PELNs to regulate the AGEs/RAGE/NF-κB and HIF-1 signaling pathways, and synergistically mediate the anti-inflammatory and osteogenic effects of PELNs [[31], [32], [33], [34], [35]]. All identified compounds (integrating results from both ion modes) were annotated according to the NPClassifier classification system [36], with the proportion of each compound category presented in Fig. S1. For the base peak chromatograms (BPCs) of Pueraria lobata nanovesicles in positive and negative ion modes, peak shape validation and secondary spectrum analysis were performed for chromatographic peaks with high abundance. These peaks were then sequentially numbered in the positive and negative ion chromatograms, as shown in Fig. 1D and Fig. S2. A total of 26 characteristic peaks were labeled, and detailed information on the corresponding identified compounds is provided in Table S3. Fig. S3 displays the proportion of chemical categories among the compounds corresponding to the labeled characteristic peaks.

The synergistic mechanism of bioactive components in PELNs for the treatment of infectious bone defects is of great research value. In this study, network pharmacology was employed to systematically elucidate the potential mechanisms of PELNs in treating infectious bone defects, with the specific procedures and results as follows: After database collection, screening, and deduplication, 8307 potential targets of compounds in PELNs and 1092 disease-related targets of infectious bone defects were obtained. The intersection of potential compound targets and disease targets yielded 660 common targets, which were identified as potential core targets of PELNs for the treatment of infectious bone defects (Fig. 1E). To clarify the interaction relationships among core targets, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the String database. Results showed that the network contained 60 core nodes and 359 edges, with core nodes including CASP3, STAT3, IL-6, TP53, IL-1β, ALB, and JUN (Fig. 1F). Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation and enrichment analysis were performed on the core targets using relevant platforms, resulting in 4532 biological process (BP)-related terms, 303 cellular component (CC)-related terms, and 519 molecular function (MF)-related terms. The TOP 20 terms were selected for visual analysis based on P value (p < 0.05): In the BP category (Fig. 1G), the core targets were mainly involved in processes such as cellular response to chemical stimulus, response to oxygen-containing compound, and response to organic substance; in the CC category (Fig. 1H), the core targets were primarily localized in NF-kappaB p50/p65 complex, membrane-enclosed lumen, and intracellular organelle lumen; in the MF category (Fig. 1I), the core targets mainly possessed functions including enzyme binding, identical protein binding, and transcription factor binding. To reveal the overall metabolic pathway enrichment characteristics of core targets, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was further conducted to identify relevant metabolic pathways. Results of the enrichment analysis (Fig. 1J) showed that the metabolic pathways enriched by core genes included Advanced Glycation End Products (AGE-RAGE) signaling pathway in diabetic complications and HIF-1 signaling pathway. A compound-core target network was constructed (Fig. S4), which contained 212 nodes and 1330 edges. The network diagram showed complex regulatory relationships, including “multiple compounds corresponding to a single target” and “a single compound corresponding to multiple targets”. Based on the compound-core target data and the TOP 20 results of KEGG enrichment analysis, a key compound-core target-pathway network diagram was plotted (Fig. S5), which included 142 key compounds, 44 core targets, and 20 related pathways. These results indicated that PELNs may exert therapeutic effects on infectious bone defects through a synergistic mode of multi-compound, multi-target, and multi-pathway [37].

Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis was performed to further explore the therapeutic mechanism of PELNs in treating infectious bone defects. A total of 237 differential genes were screened out, including 102 upregulated differential genes and 35 downregulated differential genes (Fig. S6). Based on P value ranking (P < 0.05), the top 30 differential genes are presented in Fig. 1K, including Ccl2, Nfkbia, Nfkbiz, and other related genes. GO enrichment analysis (Fig. S7) showed that the core differential genes were closely associated with biological processes and functions such as response to lipopolysaccharide, interphase microtubule organizing center, and CXCR chemokine receptor binding. KEGG and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) enrichment analyses (Fig. 1L) revealed that PELNs may treat infectious bone defects through multiple signaling pathways, including the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway (Fig. 1M), HIF-1 signaling pathway (Fig. S8A), NF-kappa B signaling pathway (Fig. S8B), IL-17 signaling pathway (Fig. S8C), and TNF signaling pathway (Fig. S8D).

In summary, we successfully isolated PELNs, analyzed their bioactive components, and systematically explored the therapeutic mechanisms of PELNs in treating infectious bone defects.

Synthesis and characterization of TCMP

The preparation process of TCM graft loaded with PELNs (TCMP) is illustrated in Fig. 2A. Briefly, the TCM graft was first etched with NaOH to alter its surface planar structure and enhance surface roughness. Subsequently, the surface of the TCM graft was modified via dopamine polymerization, and finally, PELNs were conjugated to the TCM graft surface through 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DSPE). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed that the surface of the Ti graft exhibited a porous structure after NaOH etching (Fig. 2B), a rough structure after poly (dopamine) (PDA) coating modification (Fig. 2C), and a dense and uniform coating after PELNs conjugation (Fig. 2D). To further verify the successful loading of PELNs on the TCM graft surface, energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) was used to analyze the elemental distribution of TCMP (Fig. 2E). The uniform distribution of phosphorus (P) element is presumably related to the phospholipid components in PELNs. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis further confirmed the successful modification of PELNs on the TCM alloy surface: characteristic peaks of Ti (463.9 eV and 458.1 eV), Cu (951.5 eV and 931.7 eV), Mg (50.1 eV), and P (133.9 eV) were detected in the spectrum of TCM@PELNs, while the presence of C1s and O1s characteristic peaks was associated with the PDA modification on the TCM alloy surface (Fig. 2F–J). Fig. 2K shows the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of TCM, TCM/PDA, and TCM@PELNs. The C=O stretching vibration peak of lipid ester bonds appeared at 1740 cm−1, overlapping with the aldehyde peak of DSPE-PEG-CHO; a new C=N characteristic peak (approximately 1660 cm−1) was also detected. These results indicated that PELNs have been successfully loaded onto the TCM-PDA-DSPE-PEG-CHO substrate. In addition, the release behaviors of copper ions, magnesium ions, and PELNs from TCMP were detected. Fig. 2L demonstrates that the initial daily release rate of copper ions was significantly higher than that of magnesium ions. Fig. S9A shows the short-term release profiles of PELNs, Cu2+ and Mg2+ within 48 h. The results indicated that an obvious burst release of PELNs, Cu2+ and Mg2+ occurred under acidic conditions (pH 5.5), accompanied by accelerated ion release. In contrast, slow and sustained release of these bioactive components was observed in the physiological neutral environment (pH 7.2). The temporal sequential release behavior of Cu2+ and Mg2+ from TCMP. The underlying release mechanisms are attributed to the differential electrochemical corrosion characteristics and microscopic phase distribution of the two metal elements in the titanium matrix. Specifically, Cu exists in the form of Ti-Cu intermetallic compound phases with low electrochemical corrosion potential, which undergo preferential and rapid dissolution on the scaffold surface in the early stage, leading to a high burst release of Cu2+. Mg is dispersed as chemically active grains in the Ti matrix and is slightly wrapped by the matrix in the early stage, showing a relatively low release rate. With the depletion of surface Cu phases and the exposure of the scaffold's porous structure, the dispersed Mg grains undergo continuous hydrolytic degradation, resulting in a stable sustained release of Mg2+ in the late stage. This intrinsic degradation mechanism endows TCMP with the temporal sequential controlled release of metal ions, which is highly matched with the anti-infection and osteogenic repair stages of infectious bone defects. To mimic the in vivo pH variation during the repair of infectious bone defects, the release medium was initially adjusted to pH 5.5 and replaced with fresh medium at pH 7.2 on Day 4. Results from Supplementary Fig. S9B reveal the release behaviors as follows. In the early acidic stage (Days 1–4): copper ions were released rapidly at a high concentration, and plant-derived extracellular vesicles exhibited pH-responsive burst release, which well satisfies the demands of antibacterial and anti-inflammatory activities in the acute infection phase. In the later neutral stage (Days 4–14): upon switching the ambient pH to 7.2, the cumulative release of copper ions dropped drastically, whereas magnesium ions maintained stable sustained release. Meanwhile, plant-derived extracellular vesicles switched to low-level slow release, matching the osteogenesis-promoting requirements during the bone repair stage. To further approximate the complex microenvironment of infected bone defects, two additional release experiments were performed. First, 0.1–1.0 mM H2O2 was introduced into the release medium to mimic the oxidative stress characteristic of the infected niche. The respiratory burst of host immune cells transiently enriches H2O2 at the implant–tissue interface, accelerating oxidative dissolution of metal ions. H2O2 selectively accelerated Cu2+ release while leaving Mg2+ release largely undisturbed (Fig. S10). This behavior equips the TCMP system with an infection-responsive “sense-and-respond” capability—exploiting the biochemical hallmark of the pathological microenvironment to trigger Fenton-like catalysis and on-demand Cu2+ liberation precisely at the infection site. Sustained Mg2+ release is preserved, effectively decoupling early antibacterial action from subsequent osteogenesis and minimizing off-target cytotoxicity. Second, conditioned medium was generated by co-stimulating RAW264.7 macrophages with LPS (1 μg/mL) and Staphylococcus aureus, recapitulating the secretory landscape of the infected bone microenvironment enriched with pro-inflammatory cytokines, lactate, and reactive oxygen species. In this biochemically aggressive milieu, the TCMP system displayed more complex temporal release kinetics. Cu2+ and PELNs release was markedly accelerated, enabling rapid antibacterial and anti-inflammatory actions during early infection. Mg2+ release showed only a modest increase and preserved a sustained profile, providing a stable pro-osteogenic stimulus for later bone regeneration (Fig. S11). This differential release behavior demonstrates that TCMP senses and decodes multi-factorial inflammatory signals in a physiologically faithful context, selectively amplifying therapeutic components for infection control while safeguarding the osteogenic reservoir for subsequent repair. These results demonstrate that although a certain amount of magnesium ions is released at the early stage, copper ions together with plant-derived exosome-like nanoparticles exert potent antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects, which mask the functional efficacy of magnesium ions. In the later phase, as the cumulative release of copper ions gradually diminishes, sustainably released magnesium ions become the core functional factor promoting osteogenesis, thus achieving an obvious temporal shift in dominant functions. From the SEM images of the scaffold surface after immersion in different pH environments, it can be clearly observed that the number of vesicles on the scaffold surface decreases drastically after 3 days at pH 5.5, while vesicles remain abundant on the scaffold at pH 7.2 (Fig. S12). In addition, the high-resolution N 1s XPS spectra confirmed that the amount of Schiff base (C=N) bonds decreased significantly under acidic conditions (Fig. S13). As time progressed, the release rate of magnesium ions gradually exceeded that of copper ions. The release behavior of PELNs (Fig. 2M) exhibited a typical pH-responsive temporal release pattern matching the infectious bone defect microenvironment: In pH 5.5 solution (simulating early acute acidic infection), PELNs were rapidly and massively released (nearly complete within 8 days) to exert early anti-inflammatory effects; In pH 7.2 solution (simulating late neutral repair), PELNs maintained sustained slow release up to 24 days, providing low-dose continuous support for osteogenesis.

This dual-phase pH-responsive release fully supports our statement that PELNs are largely released early for anti-inflammation and slowly released late for osteogenesis. Meanwhile, we compared the antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and osteogenic efficacy of the early soaking solution (Days 1–4) and late soaking solution (Days 4–14) of TCMP. The selection of these two times windows was based on the inherent sequential release kinetics of TCMP and the classical pathological stages of infectious bone defect repair. Days 1–4 correspond to the acute infection and inflammatory storm stage of infectious bone defects, during which TCMP mainly releases high concentrations of Cu2+ and large amounts of pH-responsive PELNs; Days 4–14 correspond to the inflammation resolution and osteogenic initiation stage, during which Mg2+ release becomes dominant accompanied by low-level sustained PELNs release. The results showed that in terms of antibacterial activity and promotion of macrophage polarization (Fig. S14A and B), the efficacy of the early soaking solution was significantly superior to that of the late soaking solution; however, there was no significant difference in the ability to promote the osteogenesis of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) between the early and late soaking solutions (Fig. S14C and D). In summary, the above results confirmed the successful synthesis of TCMP, which has a time-dependent release characteristic: a large amount of copper ions and PELNs are released in the early stage, the release amount of magnesium ions exceeds that of copper ions with time, and PELNs can be continuously released in a small amount. Meanwhile, the antibacterial and anti-inflammatory efficacy of the early soaking solution of TCMP is superior to that of the late soaking solution, while there is no significant temporal difference in osteogenic efficacy.

Antibacterial detection in vitro

To investigate the in vitro antibacterial properties of the grafts, the film adhesion method was used for antibacterial performance testing. Laser confocal scanning microscopy (CLSM) images of live/dead bacterial staining were employed to evaluate bacterial viability, and SEM was used to observe the micro morphology of bacteria (Fig. 3A). The experimental results are as follows: First, the bacterial colony counts on agar plates (Fig. 3B) showed that compared with the pure Ti group, both the TCM group and TCMP group exhibited excellent antibacterial properties, while the TP group had poor antibacterial ability, presumably because PELNs themselves do not possess antibacterial activity. The CCK-8 assay results further confirmed this conclusion (Fig. S15A–D), with significantly decreased optical density (OD) values in the TCM group and TCMP group on both day 1 and day 3. In addition, this study further explored the ability of TCMP to eliminate pathogenic bacterial biofilms (Fig. 3C). Consistent with the aforementioned antibacterial experimental results, both the TCM group and TCMP group showed significant pathogenic biofilm elimination ability compared with the pure Ti group. The live/dead bacterial staining results (Fig. 3D) showed that bacteria treated with the TCM group and TCMP group emitted red fluorescence (a characteristic of dead bacteria), while those treated with the Ti group and TP group still emitted green fluorescence (a characteristic of live bacteria). This indicated that the TCM group and TCMP group could effectively kill bacteria inside the biofilm, suggesting that the TCM alloy has the potential to inhibit the proliferation of Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus). The SEM observation results further confirmed this conclusion (Fig. 3E): bacteria in the TCM group and TCMP group showed obvious fragmentation, while those in the Ti group and TP group maintained intact morphology, indicating that the TCM alloy exerts a direct killing effect on bacteria. To study the competitive colonization ability between cells and bacteria, BMSCs were co-inoculated with E. coli and S. aureus on the sample surface, and the competitive interaction between bacteria and BMSCs was observed by CLSM images. Fig. 3F shows that the number of bacteria on the surface of the Ti group and TP group was significantly higher than that of the TCM group and TCMP group, while the number of cells was significantly lower. In addition, the morphology of BMSCs in the Ti group and TP group was significantly abnormal. Previous studies have shown that bacteria can significantly affect cell morphology when co-cultured with cells [38]. Regulation of macrophage polarization in vitro

Macrophages play a crucial role in regulating immune responses and tissue repair, thereby modulating the process and outcome of bone repair. The functional polarization of macrophages into pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype is the core mechanism underlying these processes [39]。During infection or injury, M1 macrophages dominate the early inflammatory phase, but sustained M1 macrophage activation exacerbates tissue damage [40]. M2 macrophages emerge during the resolution phase of inflammation, which can inhibit inflammation and promote tissue regeneration [41]. In infectious bone loss, the accumulation of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages is associated with inflammatory progression, while M2-like macrophages play a key role in bone regeneration [42,43].

In this study, immunofluorescence, flow cytometry, and Western blot (WB) assays were performed to systematically evaluate the regulatory effect of the grafts on the polarization of RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 4A). First, the 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe was used to detect the reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging capacity of the grafts (Fig. 4B and C). The results showed that compared with the pure Ti group, the TCMP group exhibited the lowest green fluorescence intensity, indicating that TCMP has significant ROS scavenging capacity. Immunofluorescence analysis results (Fig. 4D–G) showed that compared with the pure Ti group, the TCMP group had the highest red fluorescence intensity of the M2 phenotype marker CD206, suggesting a significant increase in CD206 expression; in contrast, the TCMP group had the lowest red fluorescence intensity of the M1 phenotype marker inducible nitric oxide synthase (INOS), indicating a significant decrease in INOS expression. Quantitative analysis by flow cytometry further confirmed (Fig. 4H) that the expression of the M2 phenotype marker CD206 was significantly upregulated, while the expression of the M1 phenotype marker CD86 was significantly downregulated in the TCMP group, demonstrating that TCMP can effectively induce macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype. Western blot (WB) assay was used to detect the protein expression levels of CD206 (M2 marker) and CD86 (M1 marker) at the protein level. Consistent with the previous results, compared with the pure Ti group, the TCMP group showed significantly increased CD206 expression and significantly decreased CD86 expression (Fig. 4I, Fig. S16). The moderate macrophage polarization observed in the TCM group is derived from the ROS-scavenging and mild immunomodulatory effects of Mg2+, while the effect in the TP group is attributed to the intrinsic anti-inflammatory activity of PELNs. The superior effect of TCMP is the result of the synergistic interaction between metal ions and PELNs. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) assay was performed to detect the expression levels of INOS, CD206, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-10 (IL-10) at the RNA level (Fig. 4J–M). In this study, one of the core design principles of the TCMP scaffold is to leverage the sequential release of Mg2+ to satisfy the demands of different stages of bone repair. Mg2+ presents a low release amount in the early stage with limited functional contribution, while its release rate rises markedly in the middle and late stages, which highly coincides with the period of bone repair and remodeling. Accordingly, this study focuses on the osteogenic effect of Mg2+, which is consistent with the time window of material design. It is worth noting that existing literature has fully verified that apart from its osteogenic activity, Mg2+ also possesses anti-inflammatory effects and can induce the polarization of macrophages from the M1 phenotype to the M2 phenotype [25,44]. In this work, the TCMP scaffold exhibited prominent anti-inflammatory capacity and M2 polarization-inducing effects both in vitro and in vivo. We believe these effects result from the synergistic action of PELNs, Cu2+ and Mg2+: PELNs serve as the main contributor to early anti-inflammation, whereas Mg2+ consolidates and sustains a favorable immune-osteogenic microenvironment in the middle and late stages. Nevertheless, since Mg2+ is released simultaneously with PELNs and Cu2+, the relative contributions of the three factors to anti-inflammation and immune regulation cannot be precisely distinguished based on the available data, which is one of the limitations of this study. Follow-up research can systematically evaluate the independent anti-inflammatory and immune regulatory efficacy of Mg2+ by fabricating control scaffolds containing Mg2+ but devoid of PELNs alone, or by introducing exogenous Mg2+ concentration gradients into in vitro culture systems. Even so, the multiple functions of Mg2+ in this study are not neglected, but indirectly reflected through the overall comprehensive performance of the scaffold.

In summary, the above results demonstrated that the prepared grafts (Ti, TCM, TP, and TCMP) had good in vitro biosafety, among which the TCMP group exhibited the optimal osteogenic capacity.

Therapeutic effect of the sample in the Treatment of Bone Infection in vivo

To evaluate the early infection and inflammatory response, we detected the levels of inflammatory factors and infection-related indicators on postoperative day 7. The experimental results fully verified that TCMP exerts rapid antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects, which is highly consistent with the sequential release mechanism in this study. On postoperative day 7, histological examination revealed no bacterial colonization in the TCMP group. In contrast, the Ti group presented severe inflammatory response with extensive bacterial adhesion (Fig. S18 A). Compared with the Ti group, the count of bacterial colony-forming units (CFU) in the TCMP group was reduced by over 90%, with barely any viable bacteria left (Fig. S18 B). Meanwhile, TCMP significantly downregulated the expression levels of IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-6 (P < 0.001), effectively suppressing the early inflammatory storm (Fig. S24 B). The above results confirm that TCMP can rapidly exert antibacterial and anti-inflammatory functions in the early postoperative period, which lays an important foundation for the subsequent osteogenic repair at postoperative week 4 and week 8.

A SD rat model of infectious bone defect was established in this study, and histological staining was performed at 4 and 8 weeks postoperatively. (Fig. 6A). First, the in vivo antibacterial effect of the grafts was evaluated: PBS was used to rinse the bone defect site of SD rats, and the rinse solution was inoculated on agar plates for culture. Magnesium ions were released in a sustained and slow manner, consistent with the designed time window. Furthermore, the micron-scale pores formed locally on the scaffold after magnesium ion release may benefit the integration and remodeling of newly regenerated bone tissue by increasing the specific surface area and facilitating nutrient exchange. Micro-CT results of this study revealed that the bone formation indices in the TCMP group were significantly better than those in the other groups, and no pore-induced impairment of bone healing was observed. Nevertheless, the long-term evolutionary effects of these pores on bone microarchitecture and mechanical properties during extended follow-up remain to be further explored. In summary, these results indicated that TCMP has excellent in vivo antibacterial activity, osteogenic-promoting efficacy, and good biosafety.

Previous network pharmacology and transcriptome sequencing results have shown that the HIF-1 signaling pathway and AGE-RAGE signaling pathway play key roles in the pathophysiological process of infectious bone defects. Among them, the HIF-1 signaling pathway, as a core regulatory pathway for cells to respond to the hypoxic microenvironment, can coordinate the complex interaction between osteogenesis and angiogenesis, which is crucial for maintaining bone homeostasis and promoting bone repair. Hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), the oxygen-sensitive subunit of HIF-1, remains stable under hypoxic conditions and forms a dimer with HIF-1β, thereby activating the transcription of downstream angiogenesis-related target genes (e.g., VEGF) and osteogenesis-related target genes (e.g., RUNX2) [[50], [51], [52]]. Based on this, this study detected the expression of HIF-1 signaling pathway-related proteins, as well as osteogenic and angiogenesis-related proteins. WB assay results of rat bone tissue (Fig. 7F) showed that the gray values of HIF-1α, VEGF, Runx2, and OPN protein bands in the TCMP group were significantly higher than those in other groups; semi-quantitative analysis results further confirmed this trend (Fig. 7G–J). Immunofluorescence assay results (Fig. 7K) showed that the TCMP group had the highest red fluorescence intensity of α-Smad, suggesting that TCMP could create a favorable microenvironment for endothelial cell growth and enhance angiogenesis ability. Meanwhile, the red fluorescence intensity of OCN and OPN in the TCMP group was also significantly higher than that in other groups (Fig. 7L and M), indicating its optimal osteogenic capacity. In addition, the detection results of OCN, OPN, and α-Smad in the bone tissue of rats at 8 weeks postoperatively (Fig. S25 C-E) were consistent with the above conclusions. Furthermore, we also evaluated the in vitro angiogenic capacity. The results revealed that TCMP could effectively promote the migration and angiogenesis of HUVECs (Fig. S26).

AGEs and their receptor (RAGE) have been confirmed as key regulatory mediators in the progression of infectious bone defects. Their binding can activate the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway, leading to persistent chronic inflammation and impaired tissue repair function. AGEs are generated through non-enzymatic glycation of proteins, lipids, and nucleotides, and accumulate abnormally in aging and diabetic states. Binding of AGEs to RAGE can trigger the nuclear translocation of NF-κB, thereby upregulating the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) and osteoclast genesis-related factors (e.g., RANKL) [14,38]. This cascade reaction not only exacerbates bone resorption but also impairs osteoblast function, further hindering bone regeneration. Therefore, this study detected the expression of AGEs/RAGE/NF-κB signaling pathway-related proteins. WB assay results (Fig. 7N) showed that the protein expression levels of RAGE, p-P65, IL-6, and IL-1β in the TCMP group were significantly lower than those in other groups, while there was no significant intergroup difference in total P65 protein expression. Semi-quantitative analysis results showed (Fig. 7O–R) that the TCMP group had the lowest p-P65/P65 ratio, and the lowest protein expression levels of RAGE, IL-6, and IL-1β.

It should be noted that the NF-κB and HIF-1 signaling pathways do not follow a simple parallel additive relationship; instead, they constitute a well-documented bidirectional molecular cross-regulatory network. On the one hand, NF-κB has been identified as a key transcriptional activator of HIF-1α, and basal NF-κB activity is indispensable for HIF-1α protein accumulation under hypoxic conditions [53]. In the inflammatory microenvironment of infectious bone defects, this regulatory mechanism can partially explain why the activation of HIF-1α is subsequently enhanced after PELNs inhibit the AGEs/RAGE/NF-κB pathway. On the other hand, HIF-1α can also suppress NF-κB-dependent gene expression to prevent excessive inflammatory responses [54]. In bone tissue, HIF-1α has been confirmed to exert anticatabolic protective effects by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway [55]. In addition, there is a direct competitive relationship between NF-κB and HIF-1α at the level of transcriptional activity. NF-κB competes with HIF-1α for binding to the transcriptional coactivator p300, thereby blocking the transactivation function of HIF-1α on hypoxia-response elements [56]. In the hypoxic and inflammatory microenvironment induced by bacterial infection, the crosstalk between the HIF-1α and NF-κB pathways can also synergistically activate the inflammatory response of macrophages [57]. Collectively, the above evidence indicates that the interaction between NF-κB and HIF-1 is dynamic and complex. TCMP may synergistically modulate this cross-regulatory network through multi-target regulation, thereby breaking the vicious cycle of the imbalance between inflammation and bone repair. Future research should further explore how specific active components in PELNs precisely regulate the balance between NF-κB and HIF-1, and clarify the underlying molecular docking details and target selectivity.

In summary, these results indicated that in the animal model, TCMP could treat infectious bone defects by inhibiting the AGEs/RAGE/NF-κB signaling pathway, regulating macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, improving the local inflammatory microenvironment, and promoting osteogenesis and angiogenesis.

Conclusion

In summary, this study developed a pH-responsive TCMP graft enabling temporally-regulated release: PELNs with Cu2+ doping achieve pH-responsive rapid release in the acidic infectious microenvironment to yield strong antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects, while they transform into Mg2+-based sustained slow-release PELNs under the neutral reparative microenvironment to boost osteogenesis. TCMP demonstrated efficient antibacterial, anti-biofilm, anti-inflammatory, and osteogenic properties. In vivo, it achieved synergistic antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, osteogenic, and angiogenic effects via HIF-1 activation and AGE-RAGE inhibition. The core innovation of TCMP lies in the intelligent sequential responsive design rather than simple superposition of components. By coupling the differential degradation of Ti-Cu-Mg alloy and pH-responsive release of PELNs, the scaffold achieves stage-specific regulation of antibacterial, anti-inflammatory and osteogenic functions, and exerts dual-pathway synergistic therapeutic effects. This integrated design has significant advantages in precise regulation and clinical translation compared with single functional materials. This work presents a temporally-regulated immuno-osteogenic graft platform and a dual-pathway synergistic therapeutic strategy, offering novel insights for next-generation orthopedic implants.

Isolation and characterization of PELNs

The extraction of Pueraria lobata-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (PELNs) was performed with slight modifications based on a previously reported method [58]. To guarantee the batch-to-batch consistency and reproducibility of PELNs, a full-process standardized quality control system was established. All fresh Pueraria lobata was collected from a fixed authentic Dao-di producing area and harvested in autumn (the optimal period), and was authenticated as Pueraria lobata (Willd.) Ohwi via botanical identification; its moisture, total ash, and total flavonoid content all met the standards of the Pharmacopoeia of the People's Republic of China (2020 Edition). The entire extraction process adopted fixed parameters: consistent homogenization conditions, sequential centrifugation (1000×g for 10 min, 4000×g for 30 min, 10000×g for 1 h, 150000×g for 90 min, 4°C), 0.22 μm membrane filtration, and PBS resuspension were applied to all batches, with the same operator and equipment to avoid process-induced variation. Three independent batches of PELNs were verified for consistency: particle size (139.4 ± 49.1 nm, RSD <5%), zeta potential (−34.93 ± 0.72 mV, RSD <3%), and vesicle concentration (RSD <6%) were stable among batches. UPLC-HRMS analysis confirmed that the relative peak area of 26 core characteristic peaks had RSD <8%, and the relative contents of key bioactive components (flavonoids and polysaccharides) were consistent. Biological function validation showed no significant differences in the anti-inflammatory and osteogenic activities of the three batches (P > 0.05), ensuring the reproducibility of PELNs' core efficacy.

High-resolution mass spectrometry analysis of chemical components in PELNs

A total of 600 μL of the sample was placed in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf (EP) tube, and 400 μL of methanol was added and thoroughly mixed. Then, 200 μL of the aforementioned mixture was taken, mixed with 200 μL of 40% methanol aqueous solution, and vortexed evenly. The mixture was centrifuged at 16000×g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected for subsequent detection. Sample separation was performed using a Vanquish Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography (UHPLC) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany) coupled with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm). Primary and secondary mass spectra of the samples were acquired using a Q-Exactive HFX mass spectrometer, which was connected to the UHPLC system. Mass spectrometry data were collected in both positive and negative ion modes via an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Compound identification was achieved by searching the Traditional Chinese Medicine High-Resolution Mass Spectrometry Database, with the following criteria: primary mass error <25 ppm, and secondary fragment ion spectrum matching score >0.7. A higher score indicates higher spectrum similarity, and a matching score ≥0.7 is generally considered to ensure reliable identification results [[59], [60], [61], [62]].

Network pharmacology study of PELNs for the treatment of infectious bone defects

Potential targets of compounds were screened using HERB, BATMAN-TCM, TCMS, and CTD databases [[63], [64], [65], [66]]. Disease-related targets of infectious bone defects were obtained from TTD, ETCM, and CTD databases [67,68]. The intersection of potential compound targets and disease targets was identified, and the overlapping targets were imported into the String database [69] to acquire Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) relationships. The network topology structure was calculated using the R package igraph, and parameters such as Degree, Betweenness, and Closeness were statistically analyzed to screen out core targets. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed via the GO database (geneontology.org) [70], and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis was conducted using the KEGG database [71]. The GO enrichment analysis included three modules: Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC), while the pathway analysis focused on KEGG pathways. Based on the P value (P < 0.05), the top 20 (TOP20) data of BP, MF, CC, and KEGG pathways were selected respectively, and the results of pathway enrichment analysis were visualized using R language.

RNA sequencing

PELNs were co-cultured with cells for 2 days. The experiment consisted of 2 groups with 3 biological replicates per group. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed by Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened using the thresholds of p-value <0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was used for functional annotation of DEGs, and pathway analysis was conducted via the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was applied to evaluate whether predefined gene sets were significantly enriched in genomic data, thereby interpreting their biological significance.

Preparation of TCM alloys

The experimental procedure was slightly modified based on the method previously established by our research group [22]. Titanium (Ti) powder and copper (Cu) powder were mixed at preset mass ratios and subjected to wet ball milling in a planetary ball mill (Model QM-1SP4, Nanjing University Instrument Factory) with ethanol as the dispersion medium and stainless steel grinding balls. The ball milling parameters were set as follows: duration of 6 h, rotating speed of 250 r/min, with a 1-min dwell after every 30-min direction reversal. After wet ball milling, the mixture was allowed to stand, and the upper ethanol solution was decanted. The powder containing residual ethanol was dried in a vacuum drying oven for 24 h. Subsequently, the dried Ti-Cu mixed powder was dry-mixed with magnesium (Mg) powder at the predetermined mass ratio without adding ethanol or stainless steel grinding balls. The dry ball milling was conducted under the parameters: duration of 3 h, rotating speed of 250 r/min, and a 1-min dwell after every 30-min direction reversal, yielding a uniformly dispersed Mg-containing mixed powder. A hydraulic press (Model FY-30, Tianjin Sichuang Jingshi Technology Co., Ltd.) was used for compaction of a defined mass of the mixed powder. A stainless steel mold with an inner diameter of 11.5 mm was employed, and the compaction pressure was set to 6 MPa. Bidirectional compaction (forward and reverse directions) was performed with a holding time of 1 min. Thereafter, the green compacts were sintered in a microwave sintering furnace (Model NJZ4-3, Nanjing Jiequan Microwave Equipment Co., Ltd.). The green compacts were evenly placed in a high-temperature resistant insulation bucket filled with silicon carbide (SiC) particles. During sintering, the operating voltage was 380 V, the operating frequency was 2450 MHz ± 20 MHz, and the maximum microwave heating power was 3 kW. Meanwhile, the real-time temperature of the samples was monitored using an infrared thermometer. After sintering, the samples were cooled to room temperature along with the furnace, then ground sequentially with 80#, 400#, 800#, 1200#, 1500#, and 2000# carborundum sandpapers on a grinding machine. The ground samples were ultrasonically cleaned in ethanol for 10 min and then dried for subsequent use.

Preparation of TCMP composite alloys

TCM alloys were first ground with gradient sandpapers to remove the surface oxide layer, followed by alkali etching in 5 mol/L NaOH solution at 60°C for 24 h to construct a rough hydroxylated surface. The pretreated alloys were immersed in dopamine Tris-HCl buffer solution (2 mg/mL, pH 8.5) and incubated at room temperature for 24 h to form a polydopamine (PDA) coating. Distearoylphosphatidylethanolamine-polyethylene glycol-aldehyde (DSPE-PEG-CHO) was coupled via amidation reaction, thereby introducing pH-sensitive Schiff-base groups. The aldehyde groups (-CHO) of DSPE-PEG-CHO underwent a Schiff-base reaction with the amino groups (-NH2) on the surface of the PDA coating, generating imine bonds (-C=N-) for stable connection. The functionally modified TCM alloys were immersed in PELNs suspension (100 μg/mL) and incubated at 4°C for 12 h. Exosome loading was achieved by inserting the hydrophobic tail of DSPE into the exosomal phospholipid bilayer.

In vitro release behavior determination of Cu2+, Mg2+ and PELNs

To evaluate the release behaviors of Cu2+, Mg2+ and PELNs vesicles from the samples in vitro, while eliminating the interference of ions in the solution on the ion release of the samples, 0.9% NaCl solution was selected as the extraction medium for the ion release test. The functionally modified alloys were immersed in 1.5 mL of the above solution per sample, and the mouth of the immersion bottle containing the samples was sealed with plastic wrap before being placed in a constant temperature incubator at 37°C. To further approximate the complex microenvironment of infected bone defects, two additional release conditions were employed: (1) oxidative stress simulation, in which 0.1–1.0 mM H2O2 was introduced into the release medium to mimic the elevated ROS levels at the implant–tissue interface resulting from the host immune respiratory burst; and (2) inflammatory microenvironment simulation, in which conditioned medium was prepared by co-stimulating RAW264.7 macrophages with LPS (1 μg/mL) and Staphylococcus aureus for 24 h, followed by centrifugation and filtration to obtain a medium enriched with pro-inflammatory cytokines, lactate, and ROS. At predetermined time points, the concentrations of Cu2+ and Mg2+ in the supernatants were determined by flame atomic absorption spectrophotometry (AAS NovAA 800, Analytik Jena, Germany). For PELNs quantification (unit: μg/mL), the total phospholipid content was determined using a commercial total phospholipid assay kit based on ammonium molybdate colorimetry. Lecithin was used as the standard to establish a standard curve, and the absorbance of PELNs samples was measured at 550 nm using a microplate reader. The total phospholipid concentration was calculated and defined as the quantitative concentration of PELNs, which is a standardized method for plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles. The concentration of PELNs was detected by an in vitro spectrophotometer (ND-100C, Hangzhou Mio Instrument Co., Ltd.). All tests were repeated three times, and the average values were taken as the final results.

Antibacterial properties in vitro

The bacterial strains Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Escherichia coli (E. coli) were chosen for studying their antibacterial properties.

Spread-plate method

Bacteria were cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium at 37°C until reaching the logarithmic growth phase. A portion of the bacterial culture was serially diluted for subsequent experiments. Then, 1 mL of the diluted bacterial suspension and alloy stents subjected to different treatments were co-incubated in a 5 mL EP tube at 37°C for 24 h. After incubation, 50 μL of the bacterial solution was aspirated and evenly spread onto LB agar plates, which were then incubated at 37°C for another 24 h. Triplicate samples were set up for each group to ensure experimental reproducibility. Upon the bacterial colonies growing to a suitable state with uniform size and stable morphology, the agar plates were photographed, and the number of colonies (CFU) in each group was counted. The antibacterial efficiency was calculated based on the colony count results.

Bacterial morphology observation

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Thermo Scientific/Apreo 2S HiVac, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to observe the bacterial morphology and structural integrity. Suspensions of Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, diluted to 106 CFU mL−1, were separately added to alloy groups subjected to different treatments, followed by co-cultivation at 37°C for 24 h. After incubation, the samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 2 h, and then rinsed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.0) to remove residual fixative. Subsequently, gradient dehydration of bacteria was performed using ethanol solutions with increasing concentrations (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), with 10 min of dehydration at each concentration. After air-drying naturally, SEM observation was conducted. For transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation, the samples treated as described above were double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, air-dried on copper grids, and then TEM images were captured to observe changes in the ultrastructure of bacteria.

LIVE/DEAD stain

The samples used in the experiment were sterilized by ultraviolet (UV) irradiation (wavelength 254 nm) for 30 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli suspension (106 CFU mL−1, either mixed or single-species) was inoculated into 6-well cell culture plates, and 2 mL of alloy immersion extract (solvent: Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS)) from each group was added to the corresponding wells. The plates were incubated in a 37°C cell incubator with 5% CO2 for 24 h. After incubation, bacteria were stained using the Live/Dead Bacterial Staining Kit (BB41266, BestBioKit) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) to analyze the bacterial viability.

Cell experiments

Cell culture

Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were isolated from the femurs and tibias of Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (weighing 120 g, purchased from Jiangsu Huachuang Xinnuo Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd.). After removing the epiphyses of the bones under sterile conditions, the bone marrow cavity was flushed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.0) using a sterile syringe, and the flushing solution was collected into a centrifuge tube containing DMEM medium. Cells were harvested as pellets by centrifugation at 1000 r/min for 5 min. Subsequently, the cells were resuspended in complete DMEM medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and cultured in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. After 72 h of culture, BMSCs were passaged into DMEM medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin antibiotics via trypsin digestion. Passage 4 (P4) BMSCs were selected for subsequent experiments to ensure cell purity and stable biological characteristics.

CCK-8 cell proliferation assay

Cell viability was determined using the CCK-8 kit (Cat. No.: BMU106, Yakeyin Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), and the experimental procedures were performed strictly according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were digested, counted, and adjusted to a concentration of 5 × 104 cells/mL to prepare a single-cell suspension. A total of 100 μL of the cell suspension (containing 5 × 103 cells per well) was added to a 96-well cell culture plate, which was then incubated in a constant temperature incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 until cell adhesion. The original culture medium in the wells was aspirated and discarded, and 100 μL of alloy immersion extract (solvent: DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum) of each group was added to the corresponding wells, followed by incubation for different days under the same condition (37°C, 5% CO2). After incubation, the liquid in each well was removed, and 100 μL of CCK-8 working solution was added to each well. The plate was further incubated for 1 h at 37°C with 5% CO2. Finally, the optical density (OD) value of each well was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Live/dead cell staining assay

A portion of the aforementioned single-cell suspension was added to a 12-well cell culture plate and incubated in a 37°C, 5% CO2 constant temperature incubator until cell adhesion. After aspirating and discarding the culture medium in the wells, alloy immersion extract of each group (solvent: DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum) was added to each well separately, and the plate was incubated for another 24 h in the same incubator. After incubation, cells were stained using the Calcein-AM/PI Live-Dead Cell Double Staining Kit (Cat. No.: CA1630, Solarbio Co., Ltd.) in strict accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, and fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM).

Cell-bacteria Co-culture experiment

Samples were irradiated with 254 nm ultraviolet light at room temperature for 30 min for surface sterilization. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were seeded into 6-well cell culture plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and cultured in a 37°C, 5% CO2 cell incubator. After the cell adherence rate reached 80%-90% (approximately 24 h), 1 mL of mixed bacterial suspension of Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli (106 CFU mL−1) was added to each well, along with 200 μL of immersion extract of each alloy (the solvent of the extract was DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum). The cells were further incubated in the 37°C, 5% CO2 cell incubator for 48 h. After incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 10 min, washed three times with PBS, and then incubated with blocking solution containing Actin488 fluorescently labeled probe for 30 min. Subsequently, 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining solution was added for staining at room temperature for 20 min, followed by three additional washes with PBS to remove unbound staining solution. Finally, the cell morphology and fluorescence distribution were observed, and images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM). Image analysis was performed with ImageJ software.

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

Cell suspensions were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated in a cell culture incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 h until the cells adhered to the plate and reached 70%∼80% confluency. The complete medium in each well was aspirated, and the cells were treated accordingly based on experimental groups, followed by an additional 4 h of incubation. After discarding the treatment solution from each group, the cells were gently washed 2∼3 times with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) pre-warmed to 37°C to thoroughly remove residual medium and treatment solution, thereby avoiding interference with subsequent probe incubation. A 10 μmol/L DCFH-DA working solution diluted in serum-free medium was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 30 min. The DCFH-DA working solution was quickly aspirated, and the cells were gently washed 3 times with PBS to completely eliminate uninternalized free probes outside the cells. All operations were performed under strict light protection. Fluorescent images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM).

Immunofluorescence assay

RAW 264.7 macrophages (Cat. No.: BNCC337875, BeNa Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Henan, China) were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells/cm2. To simulate the infectious microenvironment of bone defects, cells were subjected to co-stimulation with Staphylococcus aureus and LPS (1 μg/mL) before treatment. After routine culture for 2 days, the cell samples were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 20 min, blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at 4°C for 2 h, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Subsequently, the cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against INOS (5 μg/mL, Abcam, Cat. No. ab178945, USA) or CD206 (5 μg/mL, Abcam, Cat. No. ab64693, USA). On the following day, the samples were treated with anti-mouse secondary antibody (5 μg/mL, R&D Systems, Cat. No. NL013, USA) at 37°C for 1 h. Finally, the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min in the dark, and images were observed and acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM).

Flow cytometry assay

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded onto the surface of samples in 6-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells/cm2. Cells were stimulated with Staphylococcus aureus/Escherichia coli and LPS (1 μg/mL) to mimic infectious conditions prior to sample treatment. After routine culture for 2 days, the cells in each group were collected and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C. Subsequently, the cells were stained with specific primary antibodies against CD86 and CD206 for 30 min at 4°C in the dark, washed gently twice with PBS, and then resuspended in 500 μL PBS. The cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, FACS Calibur, USA).

Western blot analysis

Total proteins were extracted from cells or tissues using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors. The cell lysates were centrifuged at 10000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected as the protein sample. After adjusting the protein concentration in the sample buffer, the proteins were fully denatured by heating in a 95°C water bath for 10 min. Subsequently, the proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) with gel concentrations adjusted according to protein molecular weights: 12%∼15% gels for small-molecular-weight proteins, 8%∼10% gels for medium-molecular-weight proteins, and 5%∼7% gels for large-molecular-weight proteins. The separated proteins on the gel were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes, which were then blocked with non-fat milk blocking solution at 4°C overnight. After blocking, the membranes were incubated with the corresponding primary antibodies at 4°C overnight, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. The related proteins detected in this experiment included: CD86, CD206, hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), β-actin, Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), osteopontin (OPN), receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), PP65, P65, interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH).

qRT-PCR

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed to detect the expression levels of target genes, including inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), CD206, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-10 (IL-10), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), collagen type Ⅰ (Col-1), osteocalcin (OCN), osteopontin (OPN), and Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), so as to evaluate the anti-inflammatory and osteogenesis-promoting effects of each group. Total RNA was extracted from each group using an RNA extraction kit (Cat. No.: 12183020, Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA quality was confirmed by spectrophotometry to assess purity and integrity. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Cat. No.: K16225-100T, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and qRT-PCR was carried out on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). The primer sequences are listed in Table S1. The expression levels of target genes were normalized using glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and β-actin as internal reference genes.

Alkaline phosphatase staining (ALP)

At 7 and 14 days after osteogenic induction, the activity of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) was detected in strict accordance with the instructions provided by the commercial kit. Adherent cells on the specimens were gently rinsed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), followed by staining using the BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit (Cat. No.: P0321S, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). The ALP staining results were observed and photographed using a camera and microscope for subsequent analysis.

Alizarin red staining (ARS)

Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining was used to evaluate the mineralization degree of the extracellular matrix. After 14 and 21 days of osteogenic induction of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), the samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min, followed by staining with 2% Alizarin Red solution (Cat. No.: ALIR-10001, OriCell) at 37°C for 30 min. After staining, excess dye was gently rinsed off with PBS, and the staining results were observed and recorded using a camera and microscope to assess the mineralization level.

Animal experiments

Surgical procedures

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats weighing 250-300 g were used to establish an infectious bone defect model. The rats were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane via inhalation. After anesthesia, the right hindlimb of each rat was shaved, and the surgical area was disinfected with 2% iodine tincture. A drill hole was made at the distal femur perpendicular to the long axis of the femur, and normal saline was continuously used for cooling during the drilling process to avoid thermal injury to bone tissue. Subsequently, 20 μL of staphylococcus aureus solution (1 × 108 CFU/mL) was dropped into the bone defect hole, and the alloy material was implanted. The rats were euthanized at 4 and 8 weeks after surgery, and specimens were collected to evaluate the antibacterial effect of the material and the osteogenic capacity during bone defect repair.

In vivo anti-inflammatory efficacy evaluation

On postoperative day 7, bone tissues around the bone defect area were collected from rats of each group (approximately 50–100 mg). The tissues were minced and mixed with RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors, followed by homogenization using a tissue homogenizer at 4°C. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, the supernatant was harvested. The total protein concentration was determined by the BCA method. The expression levels of IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-6 were detected using ELISA kits respectively. All procedures were performed in accordance with the kit instructions: Standards and diluted samples were added into pre-coated microplates and incubated at 37°C for 2 h. After three washes, biotinylated detection antibody was added, and the plates were incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Following plate washing, HRP-streptavidin was added and incubated at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. After five washes, TMB chromogenic solution was added, and the plates were incubated at room temperature for 15–30 min away from light. After reaction termination, the optical density (OD) value was measured at 450 nm. The concentration of each factor was calculated based on the standard curve and expressed as pg/mg protein. Each sample was arranged in three replicate wells, and the experiment was repeated three times.

In vivo antibacterial efficacy evaluation

At 4 and 8 weeks postoperatively, the rats were euthanized, and the implants were retrieved and immediately placed into sample collection tubes containing sterile PBS. The bacteria adhering to the implant surface were eluted by vigorous shaking and soaking. A 20 μL aliquot of the soaking solution was dropped onto agar plates for bacterial colony counting. Meanwhile, the remaining soaking solution was serially diluted, inoculated into liquid medium, and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. The optical density (OD) value was measured to quantitatively assess the infection degree of the implants. In addition, the bone tissue surrounding the implants was collected, decalcified, and subjected to histological staining and immunofluorescence staining to comprehensively evaluate the antibacterial, anti-inflammatory effects of the material and the bone tissue repair status. The histological staining included hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, Masson's trichrome staining, Goldner staining, and Giemsa staining. The indicators detected by immunofluorescence staining included CD86, CD206, collagen type Ⅰ (COL-Ⅰ), osteocalcin (OCN), chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan (CPN), and α-Smad.

Micro-CT analysis

The rats were euthanized at 4 and 8 weeks postoperatively, and the femurs containing the implants were then isolated, collected, and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) for 24 h to preserve tissue morphology. To evaluate the newly formed bone tissue around the implants, microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) scanning was performed on the femurs using a PerkinElmer Quantum GX2 system (PerkinElmer Enterprise Management (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.). The newly formed bone around the implants was assessed within the volume of interest (VOI), which was delineated centered on the implant. Quantitative micro-CT analysis was simultaneously conducted, including bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and trabecular number (Tb. N). In addition, three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction was performed for each sample to more intuitively evaluate the new bone formation around the implants.

In vivo biosafety detection

After euthanasia at 4 and 8 weeks postoperatively, blood samples were immediately collected from rats in different treatment groups. The serum/plasma was separated by centrifugation and used for the detection of hematological and biochemical parameters. Meanwhile, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues were harvested from each group of rats, fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned routinely. Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining was performed to evaluate the in vivo biocompatibility of the material through histomorphological observation.
