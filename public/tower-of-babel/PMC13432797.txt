Isobavachin Attenuates Renal and Cardiac Injury in Chronic Hyperuricemia via Modulation of NF‐κB and NLRP3 Signaling Pathways
Authors: Yongmei Li, Yuexin Xu, Wenjie Ye, Zeqi Yu, Zhenkun Wu, Xinyi Liu, Xuhong Huang, Jianxin Pang, Zean Zhao, Ting Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHyperuricaemia (HUA) is a recognized risk factor for cardiovascular and renal diseases. Isobavachin, a dihydroflavone derivative, demonstrates significant efficacy in reducing uric acid levels. This study investigated the nephroprotective and cardioprotective effects and molecular mechanisms of isobavachin. Through integrative network pharmacology, pathway enrichment analysis, and molecular docking, NF‐κB was identified as a potential target of isobavachin in the context of hyperuricemia‐related cardiac and renal injury. Isobavachin treatment inhibited the activation of the NF‐κB pathway, leading to cytoprotection in uric acid (UA)‐stimulated mRTEC and H9C2 cells. In chronic HUA mice, isobavachin treatment significantly improved renal pathology and reduced renal collagen deposition by inhibiting epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition. Isobavachin administration demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in attenuating myocardial injury, hypertrophy, and fibrotic remodeling. Notably, the anti‐inflammatory effects of isobavachin were particularly evident in both renal and cardiac tissues, primarily mediated through its dual regulatory capacity in suppressing NF‐κB pathway activation and NLRP3 inflammasome formation. In conclusion, isobavachin ameliorated cardiac and renal damage caused by chronic HUA, suggesting a new strategy to prevent HUA.

Introduction

Hyperuricemia (HUA) is a prevalent metabolic disorder characterized by abnormally elevated levels of uric acid (UA) [1]. Global epidemiological studies indicate a more than twofold increase in gout prevalence since the late 20th century [2, 3]. Besides, prolonged HUA significantly contributes to multisystem disorders, such as type 2 diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease, contributing to premature mortality [4, 5, 6]. Clinical evidence confirms HUA as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease and chronic kidney disease [7], underscoring the need for integrated management strategies targeting cardiorenal and metabolic risk factors throughout the disease continuum.

HUA is also a chronic inflammatory disease. Soluble UA has been demonstrated as a metabolism‐associated molecular pattern that functions as an inflammatory mediator, eliciting inflammatory responses in various organs throughout the body [8]. Elevated soluble UA contributes to HUA‐associated nephropathic changes by activating Toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4) in renal mesangial cells and renal proximal tubular epithelial cells, as well as triggering NOD‐like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) activation within macrophage populations [9]. Elevated circulating UA levels potently stimulate inflammasome signaling pathways within cardiac and vascular tissues. It is reported that UA can activate NLRP3 and damage mitochondria, resulting in myocardial injury [10]. Furthermore, elevated UA levels induce endothelial inflammation and accelerate atherosclerotic plaque development [11, 12]. Thus, urate‐lowering therapy may serve as an efficacious treatment for chronic inflammatory diseases associated with HUA.

Several clinical studies have demonstrated the positive role of urate‐lowering therapy in the management of chronic kidney disease [13, 14]. However, it remains controversial due to limitations in sample size and potential adverse drug effects. For instance, allopurinol failed to slow down the progression of eGFR decline, while benzbromarone is not indicated in patients with kidney stones [15, 16, 17]. In the latest consensus on HUA with high cardiovascular risk, only allopurinol was recommended as a first‐line drug for lowering UA, while febuxostat was noted to have a potential risk of cardiovascular death [18]. Thus, the selection of urate‐lowering drugs for patients with HUA combined with other systemic diseases is extremely limited.

Recently, discovering safer alternatives from natural products has gained momentum, such as cyanidin‐3‐O‐galactoside [19], apigenin [20], digallic acid [21], and so on. Isobavachin, an active ingredient derived from the traditional Chinese herb Psoralea corylifolia L., exhibits a diverse range of pharmacological effects. Notably, our previous study has confirmed its remarkable urate‐lowering potential [22, 23]. However, the effect of isobavachin on chronic HUA is unknown. This study was designed to investigate the protective effects of isobavachin against cardiac and renal injuries inducted by chronic HUA, and its underlying molecular mechanisms.

Materials and Methods

Network Pharmacology

Potential targets of isobavachin were predicted using the SwissTargetPrediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) and the Super‐PRED platform (https://prediction.charite.de/). The obtained targets were standardized to official gene symbols through annotation and correction via the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Disease‐related targets associated with “hyperuricemia”, “heart injury”, and “renal injury” were retrieved by searching the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and OMIM (https://www.omim.org/) databases using the corresponding keywords. All retrieved disease targets were merged, deduplicated, and further standardized using UniProt to ensure consistency in gene nomenclature. The potential targets of isobavachin were then mapped against the disease‐associated targets, and a Venn diagram was constructed to identify the overlapping genes (common targets) shared by the compound and the diseases.

To further investigate the protein‐protein interactions involved in the treatment of hyperuricemia‐related cardiac and renal injury by isobavachin, the drug‐intersection genes were imported into the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) to construct a protein‐protein interaction (PPI) network. Subsequently, the drug‐disease intersection genes were uploaded to the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/summary.jsp), and Genome Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (top 10 terms) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis (top 20 pathways, p < 0.01) were performed using DAVID 6.8 to predict the mechanism of action of isobavachin in treating hyperuricemia‐related cardiac and renal damage. Finally, molecular docking analysis between key targets and isobavachin was performed using Schrödinger Maestro 11.1.

Reagents and Antibodies

Isobavachin (> 98% purity by HPLC) was purchased from Chengdu HerbSubstance (Chengdu, China). Uric acid (UA) (> 98% purity by HPLC) was acquired from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and ATP were acquired from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Pyrrolidinedithiocarbamate ammonium (PDTC, > 98% purity by HPLC) was acquired from MCE (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Potassium oxonate (> 98% purity by HPLC), hypoxanthine (> 98% purity by HPLC), and allopurinol (> 98% purity by HPLC) were obtained from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). The primary antibodies used in this study were as follows: Fibronectin, E‐cadherin, Claudin 1, Caspase 1, nuclear factor kappa‐B (NF‐κB) p65, and inhibitor of κB‐α (IκB‐α) were acquired from Abmart (Shanghai, China); NLRP3 was obtained from Wanleibio (Shenyang, China); the antibody for α‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA) was procured from Bioworld, located in Nanjing, China. Transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF‐β1) and klotho were sourced from Abcam, based in Cambridge, UK. Additionally, apoptosis‐associated speck‐like protein containing a caspase recruitment domain (ASC) and interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) were acquired from Cell Signaling Technology, situated in Danvers, USA.

Cell Culture

Mouse renal tubular epithelial cells (mRTECs) were cultured in DMEM/F12, and H9C2 and iBMDM cells were maintained in DMEM. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and cells were cultured in an incubator at 37°C/5% CO2.

Cell Viability Analysis

mRTECs and H9C2 cells were seeded in 96‐well plates at a 50% density. After the cells had adhered to the cell wall, different concentrations of isobavachin were added. The UA stimulation groups were treated with UA (20 mg/dL) in the absence or presence of isobavachin. After 24 h of incubation, the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) solution was added and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. The absorbance of each well was then measured at 450 nm.

Calcein Acetoxymethyl Ester (Calcein AM)/Propidium Iodide (PI) Staining

The metabolic activity of viable cells was assessed using Calcein AM fluorescence staining, whereas PI was employed to identify membrane‐compromised dead cells. After the designated treatments, cellular specimens were gently washed with PBS and subsequently incubated in a combined staining solution containing Calcein‐AM and PI (both at a 1:1000 dilution; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 min. Fluorescent visualization was ultimately performed utilizing a Carl Zeiss epifluorescence microscope (Germany).

Inflammation Activation

Inflammation activation cell model was performed as described earlier [24]. Briefly, iBMDM cells were pretreated with LPS (5 μg/mL) in the absence or presence of isobavachin (20 μM) for 4 h, and then the cells were stimulated with ATP (5 mM) for another 1 h to activate inflammation. Cells were finally collected to analyze the mRNA associated with NF‐κB/NLRP3 by performing qRT‐PCR.

Induction of Chronic Hua Model in Mice

Male Kunming mice (20 ± 2 g) were supplied by the Experimental Animal Center of Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China). The mice were housed under standard conditions at the controlled temperature (25 ± 1°C) and humidity (50 ± 10%) with a constant 12‐h light/dark cycle and allowed free for regular chow and water. This study protocol received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Southern Medical University, and all the animal experiments in the present study were conducted according to the international guidelines. Forty‐eight mice were stratified into four experimental cohorts (n = 12 per group): (1) Normal control group (NC), (2) Hyperuricemic model group (HUA), (3) Allopurinol‐treated positive control group (AP, 5 mg/kg), and (4) Isobavachin‐treated group (ISO, 5 mg/kg). Following a 1‐h pretreatment period with respective compounds or 0.5% CMC‐Na vehicle, all groups except the NC group were subjected to intraperitoneal potassium oxonate injections combined with hypoxanthine gastric gavage administered daily for 8 consecutive weeks to establish chronic HUA, in accordance with the referenced methodology [25]. Physiological monitoring included weekly body weight measurements and biweekly serum UA quantification. During the terminal week, urine samples were collected via metabolic cages. After intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) on the last day, the hearts and kidneys of 6 mice in each group were perfused through the heart and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for pathological assessment. The remaining 6 mice in each group were sacrificed by euthanasia, and their orbital blood and tissues were collected for subsequent molecular analysis.

Biochemical Parameters Determination

Blood specimens, collected with or without 0.5% heparin anticoagulant, were processed by centrifugation (3000 g, 10 min) to isolate plasma or serum fractions. Similarly, urine samples were centrifuged under identical conditions to obtain clear supernatants. Quantitative analysis of UA concentrations in plasma and urine was performed using commercial UA assay kits (BioAssay Systems, Hayward, CA, USA). Concurrently, serum biochemical parameters including creatinine (CRE), creatine kinase (CK), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were quantified using a fully automated biochemical analyzer (Mindray BS–330E, Shenzhen, China).

Detection of Oxidative Stress Indexes in Kidney and Heart Tissues

The tissue samples were homogenized using cold saline, maintaining a tissue‐to‐saline ratio of 1:9, followed by centrifugation to isolate the supernatant. The protein concentration of the homogenate was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit, which was procured from Tiangen Biotechnology (Beijing, China). The malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) contents and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity were determined according to the manufacturer's instructions using MDA assay kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China), Cellular GSH assay kit, Total SOD assay kit and LDH assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China).

Echocardiography Evaluation

Echocardiography was conducted at the end of the 8th week using an ultrahigh‐frequency, high‐resolution small animal ultrasound imaging system (VEVO 2100, VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada). Prior to the examination, mice were fasted overnight, and hair was removed from the anterior chest to expose the imaging area. For ultrasound evaluation, animals were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) administered 10 min before the procedure. During the examination, insulation and appropriate subcutaneous rehydration and resuscitation care were provided. Measurements of diastolic and systolic left ventricular anterior wall (LVAW) thickness and left ventricular internal diameter (LVID) were obtained in each groups.

Histological Evaluation

Renal and cardiac tissue samples were immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for a minimum of 48 h at room temperature and subsequently embedded in paraffin. Sections with a thickness of 4 μm were prepared from these specimens for staining with Hematoxylin‐Eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome. The stained sections were then examined under a light microscope. Collagen deposition areas identified by blue were quantified and scored using Image J software.

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR (qRT‐PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from tissues or cells with a commercial kit (Foregene, Chengdu, China) following the manufacturer's specifications. For cDNA synthesis, 500 ng of purified RNA underwent reverse transcription using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan). Subsequent quantitative real‐time PCR analysis was performed on a Roche LightCycler 480II system (Roche, Switzerland) with Power SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher, USA). All qRT‐PCR primers (sequences in Table S1) were commercially synthesized by Tsingke Biotech (China). Target gene expression levels were normalized against β‐actin and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western Blot

Tissues were homogenized and cells were lysed in ice‐cold RIPA buffer, then vortexed (1 h, 4°C) and centrifuged (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4°C) to obtain protein supernatants. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Assay Kit, with equal amounts loaded onto 10% SDS‐PAGE gels for electrophoretic separation. Transfer to PVDF membranes was followed by 1 h blocking in 5% skim milk (room temperature). Membranes were probed with primary antibodies (overnight, 4°C), then incubated with secondary antibodies (1 h, room temperature) after three TBST washes. Chemiluminescent detection was performed using a Tanon imaging system, with band quantification conducted via Image J software.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 Software. All experimental data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). One‐way ANOVA was employed for comparisons between groups, and two‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA was used for body weight and UA levels monitoring, followed by Dunnett's multiple comparison test to determine statistical significance. Differences with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Network Pharmacology and Molecular Docking Predict Targets of Isobavachin in Hyperuricemia‐Related Cardiac and Renal Injury

To elucidate the potential therapeutic targets of isobavachin, target screening was performed using two publicly available databases based on its chemical structure (Figure 1A ), yielding a total of 200 candidate targets. By integrating data from GeneCards and OMIM, we identified 13,297, 10,478, and 1,455 targets related to cardiac injury, renal injury, and hyperuricemia, respectively. Intersection analysis between hyperuricemia‑associated cardiac and renal injury targets and isobavachin‑related targets revealed 42 overlapping genes (Figure 1B ). PPI networks of these 42 core targets were constructed using the STRING database. Network analysis highlighted NFKB1, PPARG, ESR1, MTOR, and PTGS2 as pivotal nodes (Figure 1C ). GO enrichment analysis indicated that these targets were predominantly involved in biological processes (BP) such as inflammatory response, DNA damage response, and canonical NF‑κB signal transduction, cellular components (CC) including the cytoplasm, and molecular functions (MF) such as protein binding (Figure 1D ). KEGG pathway analysis further demonstrated significant enrichment in the bile secretion and NF‑κB signaling pathways (Figure 1E ).

Collectively, these findings suggest that isobavachin may exert protective effects against hyperuricemia‑associated cardiac and renal injury by modulating NF‑κB‑driven inflammatory responses. To validate this hypothesis, molecular docking and inflammatory gene regulation assays were conducted. Docking simulations revealed that isobavachin strongly bound to NF‑κB (PDB: 6SH6), forming a π–π stacking interaction with ARG 204 and potential hydrogen bonds with GLN 398 and ASP 431 (Figure 1F ). In an LPS‑stimulated inflammatory cell model, isobavachin suppressed NF‑κB expression and downregulated the NF‑κB‑dependent NLRP3 inflammasome and IL‑1β production (Figure 1G–I ). These results demonstrate that isobavachin directly targets NF‑κB and alleviates inflammation, thereby mitigating hyperuricemia‑inducted cardiac and renal damage.

Isobavachin Protects Aagainst High‐UA‐Induced Damage and Inhibits UAInduced NF‐κB/NLRP3 Pathway Activation in mRTEC and H9C2 Cells

Our previous studies have demonstrated that, elevated UA stimulation resulted in significant damage to cardiomyocytes and renal tubular epithelial cells [25, 26]. In this study, the potential protective effects of isobavachin were investigated in mRTEC and H9C2 cell models. Initially, we assessed the cytotoxic effects of isobavachin on cells, observing significant reductions in cell viability at concentrations exceeding 60 μM. We found that isobavachin (5, 10, 20 μM) could alleviate the inhibition of cell viability induced by UA in a concentration‐dependent manner (Figure 2A,B ). Additionally, we utilized staining techniques involving Calcein‐AM and PI to differentiate between live and dead cells, respectively. Microscopic examination showed that UA stimulation caused marked morphological damage and a reduction in cell density, characterized by low‐density green fluorescence and scattered red fluorescence, indicating extensive cell death. In contrast, co‑treatment with isobavachin (10 and 20 μM) significantly increased cell survival and reduced UA‑induced damage compared with UA treatment alone (Figure 2C,D ). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that isobavachin effectively rescues UA‑induced cell death in vitro, highlighting its potential cytoprotective properties against hyperuricemia‑associated cellular injury.

As a dangerous signal, UA has been reported to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome by activating the NF‐кB signaling pathway [27]. Given that NF‑κB emerged as a central node in our network pharmacology analysis, we further investigated whether isobavachin exerts its protective effects by suppressing NF‑κB–mediated inflammatory signaling (Figure 2E,F ). In both mRTEC and H9C2 cells, UA stimulation significantly increased the phosphorylation levels of NF‑κB and IκB‑α, accompanied by a marked reduction in total IκB‑α expression. Consequently, the ratios of p‑NF‑κB/NF‑κB and p‑IκB‑α/IκB‑α were markedly elevated, indicating activation of the NF‑κB signaling pathway. Importantly, isobavachin treatment attenuated UA‑induced NF‑κB activation in both cell types. Since NF‑κB serves as a key upstream regulator of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, we next examined downstream NLRP3 signaling. Isobavachin significantly inhibited UA‑induced upregulation of NLRP3, ASC, caspase 1, and IL‑1β at the protein level. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that isobavachin suppresses hyperuricemia‑induced inflammation by inhibiting NF‑κB activation and subsequently downregulating the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling cascade, thereby protecting against cardiac and renal injury.

Isobavachin Reduces the UA Levels and Promotes the Urine UA Excretion

We investigated the therapeutic efficacy of isobavachin in a murine model of chronic HUA, with the experimental timeline schematically presented in Figure 3A. The chronic HUA model was established through daily oral administration of hypoxanthine combined with intraperitoneal potassium oxonate injections over an 8‐week induction period. Our results showed that HUA mice exhibited significantly elevated serum UA levels with progressive accumulation throughout the study period (Figure 3C ), concomitant with slow body weight gain relative to the NC group (Figure 3B ). Pharmacological intervention with isobavachin effectively normalized both serum UA concentrations and body weight in HUA mice, whereas allopurinol treatment failed to recover the body weight although allopurinol demonstrated a strong UA reduction effect. Consistent with our previous animal trials, allopurinol consistently suppressed body weight gain in this model, implying compound‐specific adverse effects independent of urate‐lowering activity [25]. We speculate that isobavachin maintains metabolic homeostasis via comprehensive renal protection to rescue nutrient absorption, while allopurinol may trigger gastrointestinal toxicity or dysregulated energy metabolism that counteract its uric acid‐lowering benefits. Future mechanistic investigations will explore the exact pathways underlying allopurinol‐induced weight loss and renal deterioration. In addition, urinary UA concentrations in the HUA model group demonstrated significantly reduced levels compared with the NC group, suggesting compromised renal UA clearance capacity (Figure 3D ). Isobavachin intervention effectively enhanced urinary UA elimination, while allopurinol administration paradoxically decreased urinary UA levels, likely attributable to the inhibitory effect of allopurinol on UA production.

Isobavachin Alleviates Kidney Damage in Chronic HUA Mice

We then assessed the protective effect of isobavachin on HUA‐induced kidney injury. Histopathological analysis using H&E staining and tubular injury scoring (Figure 4A,B ) revealed severe renal morphological alterations in the HUA group, characterized by inflammatory cell infiltration, renal tubular dilation, and disorganized epithelial cell arrangement. In comparison to the model group, isobavachin treatment significantly ameliorated renal pathological damage, whereas allopurinol administration was associated with more pronounced inflammatory infiltration. Furthermore, serum CRE and BUN levels were markedly elevated in the HUA group relative to the NC group, while isobavachin treatment significantly attenuated these elevations in HUA mice (Figure 4C,D ).

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying isobavachin's renoprotective effects, we examined the expression of kidney injury molecule‐1 (Kim‐1) and neutrophil gelatinase‐associated lipocalin (NGAL), two well‐established biomarkers of renal injury [26]. As illustrated in Figure 4E,F, mRNA expression levels of Kim‐1 and NGAL were significantly upregulated in HUA mice, and isobavachin treatment effectively reversed these abnormal increases. Klotho, a critical regulator of renal homeostasis, plays a pivotal role in mitigating oxidative stress and excessive inflammation, with its downregulation serving as a key indicator of renal injury [28]. We observed reduced mRNA and protein expression of renal Klotho in HUA mice, while isobavachin treatment significantly attenuated Klotho loss induced by HUA (Figure 4H ). However, allopurinol was unable to improve aforementioned indicators. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that isobavachin not only effectively mitigated HUA but also conferred significant protection against HUA‐induced renal injury in mice.

Isobavachin Attenuates Kidney Fibrosis in Chronic HUA Mice

Persistent HUA induces oxidative stress and an inflammatory environment, leading to apoptosis and necrosis of renal tubular epithelial cells, which release damage‐associated molecular patterns that exacerbate tissue damage [29]. To assess the renal oxidative stress and inflammatory status, we measured the levels of key biomarkers. As shown in Figure 5A–C, a significant increase in MDA content accompanied by decreased SOD activity and GSH content was observed, indicating the presence of oxidative stress in the kidneys. Notably, isobavachin administration effectively enhanced SOD activity and GSH production while reducing MDA levels. Furthermore, analysis of inflammatory mediators revealed elevated concentrations of IL‐1β, IL‐6, and tumor necrosis factor⁃α (TNF‐α) in the HUA group (Figure 5D). Importantly, isobavachin treatment markedly attenuated the expression of these pro‐inflammatory cytokines, thereby confirming its potent antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties.

The damage to kidney cells leads to the release of various pro‐fibrotic substances, including TGF‐β1, which can trigger renal tubular epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) and is considered a key characteristic of hyperuricemic nephropathy. Masson staining revealed significant collagen accumulation in the renal tubular interstitium of HUA mice, indicating renal fibrotic lesions. The area of renal fibrosis was effectively reduced after isobavachin treatment but was augmented after the administration of allopurinol (Figure 5E,F). Additionally, qRT‐PCR results revealed that the kidneys of HUA mice underwent EMT, with increased expression levels of Collagen I, Fibronectin, TGF‐β1, and α‐SMA, and a decreased expression level of E‐cadherin (Figure 5G). Isobavachin treatment effectively reversed these changes. We further detected the protein expression of renal Fibronectin, E‐cadherin, α‐SMA, Claudin‐1, and TGF‐β1. As shown in Figure 5H,I, isobavachin substantially reduced the increased levels of these proteins in mice with HUA and upregulated the level of E‐cadherin. However, allopurinol did not induce any significant changes in the mRNA or protein expression levels of these fibrosis‐related factors in HUA mice. Nevertheless, the kidneys of mice in the allopurinol group still exhibited extensive renal fibrosis, with higher expression levels of renal fibrosis‐related factors compared with those in the HUA group. These results demonstrated that isobavachin significantly ameliorated renal fibrosis in HUA mice.

Isobavachin Alleviates Left Ventricular Remodeling in Chronic HUA Mice

Left ventricular remodeling has been shown to occur in a mouse model of HUA induced by potassium oxonate and hypoxanthine [25]. To explore the effect of isobavachin on left ventricular remodeling, echocardiographic examination was performed after 8 weeks of modeling. As shown in Figure 6, the HUA group exhibited an increase in the wall thickness compared to the NC group, with correspondingly decreased inner diameter, while the thickening could be ameliorated by isobavachin and allopurinol intervention. Further analysis also showed that both isobavachin and allopurinol attenuated the excessive increase in LVAW and rescued the reduction in LVID in HUA mice.

Isobavachin Relieves Cardiac Injury in Chronic HUA Mice

We next investigated the effect of isobavachin on the heart in mice with HUA. H&E demonstrated marked inflammatory cell infiltration accompanied by cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and structural disarray in the HUA group relative to the NC group (Figure 7A ). Concurrently, the Masson staining also showed obvious collagen accumulation in heart tissues (Figure 7A ). Besides, the activity of cardiomyocyte necrosis markers serum CK, serum AST and cardiac LDH were significantly higher in HUA mice than those in normal mice, indicating that chronic HUA induces cardiac injury. The hearts in the HUA group also exhibited a pronounced oxidative stress state, as evidenced by a significant reduction in the activity of the SOD and the content of GSH, coupled with a marked elevation in MDA levels. Both isobavachin and allopurinol treatments significantly ameliorated cardiac injury and effectively attenuated the oxidative stress state in HUA mice (Figure 7B–G). Furthermore, the expression levels of cardiac pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, were substantially elevated, however, this upregulation was significantly mitigated by both isobavachin and allopurinol interventions (Figure 7H–J).

Isobavachin Alleviates Kidney and Heart Injury via Inhibition of NF‐κB/NLRP3 Pathway in Chronic HUA Mice

Inflammation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of HUA. Elevated UA levels can trigger inflammatory processes, fostering the development of a chronic inflammatory state that adversely affects multiple organ systems [30]. In this study, our HUA model demonstrated a significant upregulation of inflammatory cytokine expression in both renal and cardiac tissues. Administration of isobavachin elicited a marked downregulation of pro‐inflammatory cytokine expression, underscoring its therapeutic potential in chronic HUA models. Notably, while allopurinol treatment ameliorated cardiac inflammation, it failed to attenuate renal inflammation.

As shown in Figure 8, increased protein levels of NLRP3, ASC, Caspase 1 and IL‐1β were observed in the kidney and heart of HUA mice, they were restored by isobavachin administration. Consistently, the NF‐кB signaling pathway was activated in the HUA group, as evidenced by increased protein levels of p‐NF‐κB/NF‐κB, as well as elevated levels of p‐IκB‐α, and the decreased protein levels of IκB‐α. Isobavachin was demonstrated to significantly downregulate the protein levels of the NF‐κB signaling pathway. In contrast, allopurinol treatment modulated the expression levels of the NLRP3/ASC/Caspase 1 axis and the NF‐κB signaling pathway in the cardiac tissue of HUA mice, while exhibiting no discernible effects on renal tissue, suggesting that allopurinol specifically attenuates cardiac inflammation associated with HUA. Notably, isobavachin effectively inhibited the activation of the NF‐κB/NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, thereby significantly mitigating both renal and cardiac damage in HUA mice.

Discussion

Cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic diseases frequently exhibit significant overlap and coexistence within the same patient, with metabolic disorders playing a pivotal role in linking chronic kidney disease and cardiovascular disease [31]. The global prevalence of HUA has risen rapidly, and accumulating evidence underscores its association with cardiovascular and renal complications [7], further supporting the notion that UA is a key mediator in cardio‐renal interactions. Although UA metabolism disturbances are implicated in the pathogenesis of cardiorenal syndromes, the precise underlying mechanisms remain elusive. Consequently, the development of novel therapeutic strategies for the prevention and management of HUA and its associated organ complications is of paramount importance.

Isobavachin, a dihydroflavone derivative, was identified from a proprietary database of natural products based on its potent uricosuric activity [22]. Furthermore, isobavachin has been shown to exhibit multiple biological activities, including antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory effects [32], suggesting its therapeutic potential in alleviating UA‐inducted organ damage. To decipher the underlying mechanisms, network pharmacology was used to map the potential targets of isobavachin against hyperuricemia‐related cardiac and renal injury. This analysis identified 42 intersecting targets, with NF‐κB signaling emerging as the most prominent pathway governing the therapeutic effects. Mechanistically, we confirmed that isobavachin alleviates organ damage by inhibiting NF‐κB and suppressing the NF‐κB/NLRP3 inflammasome axis, providing a systematic pharmacological basis for its renocardiac protection.

Previously, numerous studies have predominantly focused on the protective mechanisms of urate‐lowering agents in hyperuricemic nephropathy, while their effects on cardiac injury have been largely overlooked. Omizo et al. demonstrated that febuxostat confers protection against both cardiac and renal injury in a 5/6 nephrectomy model with HUA, underscoring the pathological significance of xanthine oxidase in cardio‐renal interactions [33]. Additionally, another study revealed that benzbromarone attenuates myocardial fibrosis and ferroptosis in a urate oxidase knockout (Uox‐KO) mouse model treated with doxorubicin [34]. In the present study, we established a chronic HUA mouse model and investigated the effects of isobavachin on renal and cardiac injury. Our findings substantiated the efficacy of isobavachin in mitigating cardiac and renal damage inducted by elevated UA levels. These results highlight the critical role of urate‐lowering therapy in managing cardio‐renal syndrome.

The kidney is the primary organ responsible for regulating circulating UA, accounting for over 70% of UA excretion, and is particularly vulnerable to damage caused by chronically elevated UA levels [35]. High UA levels promote oxidative stress, deplete antioxidants such as GSH and SOD, and increase MDA levels, a marker of cell membrane lipid oxidation, indicating that prolonged HUA leads to cellular damage [26]. Specifically, in renal tubules, oxidative stress results in manifestations such as tubular necrosis and dilatation. Isobavachin ameliorated tubular injury and protected the kidneys in chronic HUA mice, owing to its flavonoid structure with highly efficient antioxidant capacity. Furthermore, renal tubulointerstitial inflammation and fibrosis are hallmark features of hyperuricemic nephropathy [36]. TGF‐β1 is recognized as the master regulator of fibrosis and is known to initiate renal tubular EMT, thereby mediating kidney fibrosis. Numerous studies have demonstrated that high UA levels upregulate TGF‐β1 expression in renal cells, inducing EMT in renal tubular cells through increased expression of mesenchymal cell markers [29]. Consistent with previous findings, our study revealed a significant increase in renal TGF‐β1 expression, accompanied by the onset of tubular EMT. Isobavachin treatment effectively attenuated renal tubular EMT, reduced renal interstitial collagen deposition, and alleviated renal fibrosis.

In recent years, accumulating evidence has highlighted the detrimental effects of UA on vascular and cardiac health. Elevated UA levels trigger chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, impairing vascular endothelial function and leading to cardiomyocyte damage and dysfunction. It was has been reported that hyperuricemic mice developed kidney damage, which impaired UA excretion. Additionally, the accumulation of circulating UA further exacerbated myocardial injury and promoted ventricular remodeling [25]. Myocardial damage can disrupt energy metabolism and alter the activity of oxidative stress‐related enzymes. CK, AST, and LDH, which are widely distributed in cardiomyocytes, serve as reliable markers of myocardial injury. In this study, pathological changes such as myocardial necrosis and interstitial fibrosis were observed in the hearts of HUA mice. Furthermore, the levels of myocardial metabolic enzymes and oxidative stress‐related indices were significantly elevated. Collectively, excessive UA inflicts damage on cardiomyocytes by disrupting cardiac energy metabolism, exacerbating oxidative stress, and modulating inflammatory responses, ultimately leading to myocardial necrosis, hypertrophy, and other pathological manifestations. Importantly, we found that urate‐lowering therapy with isobavachin or allopurinol effectively ameliorated myocardial injury and attenuated ventricular remodeling.

Chronic HUA results in prolonged exposure of multiple organs to elevated UA levels, triggering a cellular inflammatory response and subsequent tissue damage, which play pivotal roles in the pathological progression of HUA‐associated multi‐organ complications [37]. Soluble UA is widely recognized as a damage‐associated molecular pattern that promotes the transcriptional activation of NLRP3 inflammasome‐associated genes in a TLR4/NF‐κB pathway‐dependent manner [10, 38]. Upon initial NLRP3 activation, ASC adaptor proteins and pro‐caspase 1 are recruited to assemble the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to the conversion of the cytokine precursor pro‐IL‐1β into mature and biologically active IL‐1β [39]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that UA exerts potent pro‐inflammatory effects by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome, affecting various organs including the liver [40], heart [10], intestine [41], and kidneys [42]. Notably, UA‐induced cardiac and renal damage may involve a complex interplay between these organs, mediated by mechanisms such as hemodynamics, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress [31]. Our results showed that the natural product isobavachin effectively suppresses the NF‐κB signaling pathway and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby ameliorating cardiac injury and mitigating renal lesions. Thus, inhibition of NF‐κB signaling and NLRP3 inflammasome activation may represent a key therapeutic strategy for treating HUA‐induced cardio‐renal injury.

In this study, there are still some limitations. First, the HUA model is pharmacologically induced, which poorly mimics human spontaneous chronic hyperuricemia driven by long‐term dietary disorders, intrinsic renal urate transport defects, and slowly progressive cardiac and renal complications. Second, regarding the evaluation of the drugs. We administered only a single dose of 5 mg/kg isobavachin and allopurinol, which was based on the known effective dose in our previous related study [23], without performing a dose‐response experiment to determine their optimal therapeutic ranges. Moreover, neither compound was evaluated in normal mice to establish a rigorous in vivo control design, making it impossible to determine whether the effects in vivo on heart and kidney result from reduced uric acid levels or from their independent anti‐inflammatory actions. Consequently, we cannot determine whether the exacerbated kidney injury observed with allopurinol resulted from excessive drug dosage or prolonged treatment duration, or from toxic active metabolites produced by xanthine oxidase inhibition. It is a critical consideration for elucidating the mechanism by which allopurinol exacerbated renal injury in this study. Most importantly, this study lacks characterization of the pharmacokinetic profiles of isobavachin and allopurinol, including bioavailability, plasma exposure, and distribution in cardiac and renal tissue. This prevents us from directly confirming that orally administered isobavachin accumulates in target organs and thereby locally inhibits the NF κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway. Third, our evaluation of the heart was limited to examining pathological changes and structural alterations, we did not measure functional parameters such as ejection fraction and fractional shortening in order to comprehensively assess the effects of isobavachin on cardiac function in our HUA mice model. Future experiments will include multi‐dose validation, pharmacokinetic testing, and comprehensive cardiac function measurements to address these limitations.

In summary, our study demonstrates that chronic HUA can induce both cardiac and renal lesions, and the heart‐kidney interaction is likely mediated by systemic inflammation triggered by UA. Isobavachin exerts protective effects on the heart and kidneys in HUA by promoting UA excretion and inhibiting NF‐κB signaling and NLRP3 inflammasome activation (Figure 9). Thus, our findings suggest that promoting UA excretion while simultaneously inhibiting the inflammatory response may represent a novel therapeutic strategy to protect cardiac and renal damage in patients with chronic HUA.
