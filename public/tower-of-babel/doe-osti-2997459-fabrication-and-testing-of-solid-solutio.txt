 ABSTRACT

The demand for higher system thermal efficiencies requires the operation of power generation cycles and
heat conversion systems at progressively higher temperatures. As the system operating temperature
increases, existing materials may not provide adequate mechanical properties or environmental
compatibility or both. There is an increasing commercial interest in the development and deployment of
liquid-fueled Molten Salt Reactors (MSRs). Hastelloy®N, the highest performing candidate MSR
structural alloy, is not capable of operations at temperatures above 700°C, thus limiting the performance
of these systems. Using an Integrated Computational Materials Engineering (ICME)-approach and small
laboratory scale heats, ORNL developed a class of patented alloys covered by U.S. Patent 9, 435, 011
B2, “Creep-resistant, Cobalt-free alloys for high temperature, liquid-salt heat exchanger systems,” similar
to Hastelloy®N in that they are primarily solid solution strengthened. In contrast to precipitation
strengthened alloys, the microstructure of solid solution alloys and hence the high temperature mechanical
properties are stable for extended periods of time allowing long reactor operating life. The new alloys
have shown to possess good resistance to liquid fluorides at temperatures up to 850°C and have
significantly improved creep properties when compared to Hastelloy®N.

The purpose of the CRADA project was for ORNL to collaborate with Haynes International- a materials
producer, MetalTek International- a foundry, and Kairos Power – an advanced reactor developer – to
scale-up selected alloys, evaluate their properties, and identify one solid solution strengthened alloy that
can meet the property requirements for the reactor being developed by Kairos Power and other similar
liquid fluoride-salt cooled reactors. As part of the project, eight alloys were down-selected and fabricated
in larger industrial scale heats by Haynes International. Resistance to molten salt was evaluated in flowing
FLiNaK and FLiBe by Kairos Power using their Rotating Cage Loop (RCL) system. Accounting for iron
deposition during these tests, the new alloys displayed very low net mass change showing excellent
corrosion performance in molten salt. Creep properties evaluated at ORNL were found to be better than
that of Hastelloy®N and 316 stainless steel. Long-term stabilities of the alloys evaluated by Haynes
International showed that these alloys have excellent thermal stability in the temperature range 704.4-
815.6°C, with the change in strength and ductility being less than 10-15% after a 4000-hour exposure at
815.6°C. Autogenously Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW) welded samples showed less than 10%
change in yield strength / ultimate tensile strength / total elongation compared to the basemetal, indicating
that the alloys have excellent weldability. Three parts were successfully investment-cast using one alloy
with very little voiding showing feasibility of fabricating parts using the casting process.

This project enabled extensive interaction between the material producer Haynes International, casting
supplier MetalTek, and reactor developer Kairos Power. This facilitated testing of materials and
components produced using the newly developed alloys by the end-user. This allowed the generation of
critical dataset required for down-selection of a few promising alloys for further development. This data is
also currently being shared with other reactor designers for them to evaluate the suitability of this alloy
for their reactor design. The availability of this alloy will ultimately enable the design and development
and deployment of MSRs with increased temperature of operation and thus, improved efficiencies.

 1. INTRODUCTION

The demand for higher system thermal efficiencies requires the operation of power generation cycles and
heat conversion systems at progressively higher temperatures. As the operating temperature of the system
increases existing materials may not provide adequate mechanical properties or environmental
compatibility or both. This lack of materials with appropriate combination of mechanical properties and
environmental compatibility is evident in structural applications at temperatures above 700°C in liquid
Fluoride Salt Cooled High-Temperature Reactors (FHRs) and Concentrating Solar Power (CSP) systems
where liquid fluoride and/or liquid chloride salts are used for their high thermal capacity and conductivity
to store and transport heat. It has been shown that fluorides and chlorides can degrade the properties of
the material used for storage and transport due to corrosive effects thus affecting the performance and
lifetime characteristics. Thus, materials used for storage and transport for fluoride/chloride-salt must at
the minimum have good compatibility with the salt by exhibiting low corrosion rates at the required
temperatures. In addition, since many of the components will be subject to stresses in service, they need
to have sufficient strength to resist plastic deformation, and the resistance to time dependent creep
deformation at these temperatures, and stress levels. Achieving the combination of these properties is
challenging considering the expected lifetime of some of the components used in these systems are in the
range of 10-80 years.

Simultaneously achieving creep resistance and resistance to liquid fluoride salts at higher temperatures in
Ni-based alloys is challenging most commercial high temperature alloys are designed for oxidation
resistance with high levels of Cr additions and/or Al additions which is detrimental to alloys designed for
use in contact with molten salts. Hastelloy®N, developed at ORNL in the 1950s-60s is currently the
highest performance commercially available FHR structural alloy to contain liquid salts in such systems
for storage and transport. It was developed through an expensive, prolonged, Edisonian approach, to
balance strength, creep resistance, salt resistance, and oxidation resistance at temperatures. Although
Hastelloy®N has good resistance to corrosion by liquid salts, its creep properties are not sufficient for
operation at temperatures of 704°C and higher. This severely restricts the allowed temperatures and
pressures that can be used in the power generation system, thus resulting in poor efficiencies thus making
such technologies non-viable.

Using a computationally-guided approach, Oak Ridge National Laboratory has developed new solid
solution strength alloys in small laboratory-scale heats that show good resistance to corrosion by fluorides
and improved creep rupture life at temperatures up to 850°C which has been granted U.S. Patent 9, 435,
011 B2, “Creep-resistant, Cobalt-free alloys for high temperature, liquid-salt heat exchanger systems.” [1]
Table 1 shows the typical compositions of these alloys. As can be seen from the table the new alloys
include a wide range of additions of Ta and W, additions of Re and Ru along with higher carbon levels.
Although the examples show selected compositions, the claims allow a wide range of Mo, Ta, and W
levels thus requiring optimization of alloy compositions. In contrast to Ni-based alloys that are
strengthened with significant precipitation of coherent intermetallic ’ precipitations or carbides, these
alloys are primarily solid solution strengthened with a small amount of carbides for grain size control.

To realize the commercial and energy benefits possible from the use of these novel alloys in these
systems, the new alloys have to be available commercially in large volumes with well-demonstrated
properties. Although the alloys have been licensed, an important step in the commercialization of these
alloys is the fabrication of larger sized heats by an industrial partner and the demonstration of properties
in these heats. The purpose of this project is to evaluate the ease of fabrication of the most promising
alloys identified from these patent-pending compositions in a larger scale compared to laboratory
scale, to evaluate the high temperature strength, creep properties, and fluoride salt resistance in
static and flowing fluoride salts of heats of these alloys at relevant temperature, stress, and

 3

environmental conditions, and to down-select one alloy with good properties and evaluate
weldability and irradiation resistance of this alloy.

In this project, it was proposed that six alloys would be down-selected from the alloys identified in the
patent, larger sized heats would be fabricated, and alloy properties would be measured to enable
identification of at least one alloy capable of meeting the property requirements for use in disruptive, high
efficiency, high performance, FHR and CSP systems. It was anticipated that small modifications of the
compositions within the broad patent would be required to allow for easier processing of these alloys to
final forms or to increase their creep and/or molten fluoride salt resistance. This project would allow
accumulation and compilation of property data to provide them to potential end-users to educate them
about the availability of the new materials, and to identify early adopters of the new materials
technologies thereby enabling rapid commercialization.

 2. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

2.1 Task 1: Materials development, processing, and microstructural evaluation

Objective: The objective of this task was to down-select alloys from U.S. Patent 9,435,011 B2, for
fabrication by Haynes International into plates for testing using standard procedures used for nickel (Ni)-
based alloys.

Results: An initial set of compositions from U.S. Patent 9, 435, 011 B2, “Creep-resistant, Cobalt-free
alloys for high temperature, liquid-salt heat exchanger systems,” were evaluated by the team with respect
to their alloying element contents, existing creep properties from laboratory scale heats, and molten salt
resistance characteristics as highlighted in the original patent (Table 2.1.1). A set of six initial
compositions were identified within the broad composition range within the patent. Figure 2.1.1 shows
results from two alloy compositions that were considered with different amount of solid solution
strengthening phases. Note that the stability of Mu phase is affected by the changing composition of the
solid solution strengthening elements. These alloys were followed by a second batch of two new alloys.

Eight experimental alloy compositions, designated TCF-1 through TCF-8 were vacuum melted,
electroslag re-melted, hot-rolled and cold-rolled to plate (0.5” and 0.3” thick) and sheet (0.065” thick)
forms. These chemistries were chosen to broadly conform to alloying element ranges specified in the
patent US 943011B2 and were used to explore differing Mo/W ratios, C contents as well as minor
alloying element additions [1]. Photographs of processed plates and sheets of some of these alloys are
shown in Figs. 2.1.2 (a) and 2.1.2 (b). It should be noted that the alloys from U. S. Patent No.
9,435,011 and all the aforementioned experimental alloys are primarily strengthened by solid
solution strengthening due to the addition of alloying elements that dissolve in the primary phase.

2.2 Task 2: Salt preparation, capsule testing, and evaluation of properties in flowing fluoride salt

Objective: The key objective of this task was to evaluate the corrosion rate of selected alloys in flowing
molten salt that meets specifications.

Results: With guidance from industrial partners and other reactor designers, emphasis was placed on
testing in flowing molten salt rather than on capsule testing. In the first set of molten salt testing, coupons
of several Ni-based alloys fabricated by Haynes International were corrosion tested in a Rotating Cage
Loop (RCL) system at Kairos Power with FLiNaK salt at 650°C for 500 hours. Mass change results and
results from cross-sectional microscopy were characterized. In the second set of experiments, Rotating
Cage Loop tests were performed with FLiBe salt at 650°C for a total duration of 3000 hours. The results
from these tests are discussed below.

2.2.1. Mass change: Molten salt corrosion testing was performed in molten FLiBe salt at 650°C. Samples
of the alloys TCF-1 through TCF-6 along with those of 316H stainless steel and other reference alloys
were placed in stainless steel cages and exposed to flowing FLiBe salt at >3 m/s face velocity for a total
duration of 3000 hours. All Ni-based alloy samples showed mass gain due to deposition of Fe dissolved
in the molten salt. Once the mass of the Fe deposit was factored in, the samples of alloys TCF-1 through
TCF-6 suffered extremely low mass change. In contrast, there was no Fe deposit on the stainless steel
316H samples which suffered from weight loss.

2.2.2. Post-test microstructural characterization: Scanning electron microscopy and EDS revealed that
the samples of the alloys, TCF-1 through TCF-6 suffered less than 5-10 µm Cr depletion depths at
isolated locations. In contrast, the 316H stainless steel samples suffered Cr depletion up to 10-20 µm
across the surface. A representative SEM micrograph of the cross-section of the TCF-6 alloy sample
following the molten salt exposure is shown in Fig. 2.2.1. It can be observed that there is about a 4-5 µm
thick Fe layer was deposited on the Ni-based alloy sample surfaces. Underneath the Fe deposit, localized
accumulation of voids due to selective leaching / depletion of Cr can be found in the samples. The depth
of the layer with the voids shown in Fig. 2.2.1 compares well with the depth of the Cr depletion layer, as
tracked by EDS spectroscopy (see Fig. 2.2.2). These results demonstrate that the examined alloys possess
superior resistance to molten fluoride salt compared to code qualified 316 stainless steel.

2.3.2. Creep properties

Creep properties of alloys were evaluated using 0.25” diameter samples in an Argon atmosphere at
several temperature / stress combinations. Figure 2.3.1 shows results for rupture lives at two select
temperature / stress combinations --- 750 °C/15 Ksi (103.4 MPa) and 704°C / 19 Ksi (131 MPa). It can be
noticed that the properties can vary widely depending on the alloys. For example, TCF-3 and TCF-5,
show rupture lives (in hours) comparable to or better than a previously explored alloy P1. All the tested
alloys show substantially improved rupture lives compared to Hastelloy®N and the code-qualified
material, 316 H stainless steel. For comparison, 316H stainless steel has a rupture life of 162 hours at

2.3.3. Thermal Stability studies

Annealed sheet specimens (0.065” thick) of the alloys TCF-1 through TCF-8 were subject to long-term
thermal exposures of up to 4000 hours at 1200°F (648.9ºC), 1350°F (732.2ºC) and 1500°F(815.6ºC). Test
coupons were extracted after every 1000 hours of exposure at the indicated temperatures and examined
via optical metallography to monitor the changes in microstructure. All tested alloys demonstrated
excellent microstructural stability, with no new phase precipitation seen in any of the alloys and all the
alloys exhibiting little to no observable change in grain structure after the prolonged 4000-hour exposure
even at the highest test temperature. Microstructures from before and after the exposure of select alloys
are shown in Fig. 2.3.2 to support this assessment.

 3. SUMMARY AND FUTURE WORK

Eight experimental alloy compositions from U.S. Patent 9, 435, 011 B2, “Creep-resistant, Cobalt-free
alloys for high temperature, liquid-salt heat exchanger systems,” were down-selected and fabricated into
experimental heats by Haynes International for further testing and evaluation. The following are the
primary conclusions from this project.

 1. The down-selected alloys could be successfully scaled up and fabricated into plates and sheet
 demonstrating feasibility of manufacturing in a larger scale
 2. Resistance to molten salt was evaluated in flowing FLiNaK and FLiBe by Kairos power using
 their proprietary Rotating Cage Loop (RCL) system. Accounting for iron deposition from the
 molten salt, the new alloys showed very low net mass change proving their excellent resistance to
 molten salt corrosion.
 3. Creep properties evaluated at ORNL were found to be better than that of Hastelloy®N and 316
 stainless steel. 3-10X enhancement in rupture lives were achieved compared to Hastelloy® N at
 temperatures of interest.
 4. Long-term stabilities of the alloys at high temperatures evaluated by Haynes international showed
 that these alloys have excellent thermal stabilities in the temperature range 704.4-815.6°C, with
 the change in strength and ductility being less than 10-15% after a 4000-hour exposure at
 815.6°C.
 5. Autogenously GTAW welded samples showed less than 10% change in yield strength / ultimate
 tensile strength / total elongation compared to the base material, indicating that the alloys have
 excellent weldability.
 6. Three parts were successfully investment-cast using one alloy with very little voiding, showing
 feasibility of fabricating parts using the casting process.
 7. Two promising alloys with a good combination of properties were identified for further
 evaluation and next level of scale-up

Future work will focus on further scale-up of one or both of these promising alloys and property
evaluation. Specific welding and casting processes will be developed for identified alloys.
