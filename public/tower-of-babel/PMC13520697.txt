Lessons learned from the integration of virtual labs and at-home lab kits to enhance microbiology education during the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Beena G. Patel, John-Paul Vermitsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHands-on lab components are an integral part of biological science courses at the college level, as they develop students’ technical skills, problem-solving abilities, and critical-thinking skills. During the COVID-19 pandemic, instructors were required to devise strategies for providing hands-on lab experiences through remote instruction. This study reports the successful implementation of hands-on lab activities using at-home custom Carolina lab kits along with McGraw-Hill Connect Virtual Biology (MHCVB) Labs. A total of 220 students from 10 sections of microbiology courses were selected for MHCVB or without MHCVB. As part of the Fall 2020 course, three sections (66 students) were instructed to perform labs at home using lab videos. MHCVB Labs were used along with instructor demonstrations during the Spring 2021 semester. Students assessed their knowledge through pre- and post-quizzes and lab skills through lab reports. No significant differences were observed in student performance on pre-lab quizzes, post-lab quizzes, or laboratory reports between the Fall 2020 and Spring 2021 semesters. In contrast, significant improvements were observed in hands-on technical skills during Spring 2021. Evaluation of student images showed that streak-plate proficiency increased from 57.7 ± 2.5 to 80.1 ± 7.6 (P < 0.05). Similar improvements were observed in simple staining (57.3 ± 11.6 to 74.4 ± 6.4; P < 0.01) and Gram staining (55.0 ± 8.7 to 80.1 ± 6.4; P < 0.001). Student proficiency was assessed by normalizing total scores to a 100-point scale, enabling direct comparisons between groups. These results demonstrate that integration of virtual laboratory simulations enhances hands-on microbiology skill development in remote laboratory courses and supports blended laboratory models across instructional modalities.

INTRODUCTION

Many biological science courses, especially at the college level, have hands-on lab components that provide technical skills to help students understand complex concepts and develop problem-solving and critical-thinking skills (1). Since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in early 2020, numerous colleges and universities have experienced a substantial decline in enrollment, particularly among Black, Hispanic, first-generation, and Indigenous students. Given that these populations were also disproportionately affected by obstacles to remote laboratory instruction, addressing equitable access to hands-on learning emerged as a paramount priority during the transition to online STEM education (2–5). Some recent studies have shown that undergraduate online courses compromise student motivation (6, 7). However, due to the restrictions the pandemic placed on society in 2020 and 2021, many colleges and universities were forced into completely online modalities (8), including the use of virtual labs and/or hands-on lab kits, to help students gain the essential lab skills required for their science courses (9). In addition to the essential lab skills, it is important that the students understand the basics of working in a lab setting, including any instruments or protocols appropriate to the lab exercise being taught.

Several challenges to scientific education became evident during the early days of the pandemic, including a lack of faculty expertise in online teaching and less institutional support for students, potentially compromising their learning (10). Often, lab exercises require heavy faculty involvement and interactions with students, providing clear explanations of the outcomes and protocols for lab exercises and development of essential lab skills (11). In most undergraduate lab settings prior to the pandemic, the instructor provided face-to-face demonstrations of protocols and created open learning environments where students could ask questions in real time if they were not clear on how to complete the activities of the lab exercise. This is not possible in asynchronous online learning and challenging in synchronous online learning via platforms like Zoom, where direct interaction with students is non-existent or minimal. This lack of direct instructor interactions may increase student anxiety and have an overall negative effect on learning (12). Alternatives to face-to-face lab instruction have included instructor-led demonstration videos and virtual simulations to show new methods of preparing the student to complete the lab exercise (11, 13–16). Research has shown that the combination of demonstration videos and virtually simulated lab exercises established a greater success in students’ lab skills (17–19).

One suite of simulated lab exercises is McGraw-Hill’s Connect Virtual Biology Labs (MHCVB Labs), which offers an extensive list of simulated labs in the biology discipline. In one study, a non-major general biology lab course showed improvement in their exam scores through the use of MHCVB Labs (20). Students develop competency in microbiological identification, microscopic observation, aseptic culturing, and molecular techniques using research-based hands-on lab activities (21). This prompted us to investigate whether MHCVB Labs would have a positive impact on our students performing hands-on lab exercises within their homes. Community College of Philadelphia (CCP) chose to use a custom at-home lab kit from Carolina Biology for our Principles of Microbiology course in order to meet the Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs) for the course. The goal of this custom lab kit was to provide the students with hands-on experience at home that would parallel the experience they would have received in an in-person lab.

Students at CCP during the Fall 2020 semester were asked to complete weekly readings of lab exercises, watch Carolina Custom Lab Kit videos along with supplemental YouTube videos, and turn in pre-lab questions prior to completing an at-home lab exercise. However, in the Spring 2021 semester, we incorporated the MHCVB suite of labs that aligned with our SLOs, along with the at-home Carolina Custom Lab Kit. These simulated labs were incorporated directly into the online Canvas course, CCP’s Learning Management System (LMS). The MHCVB Lab suite allowed students to gain background knowledge and familiarize themselves with the protocols and equipment used to complete the at-home Carolina lab kit experiments. In this study, we chose to examine the students’ lab essential skills using the at-home Carolina lab kit during the Fall 2020 semester and added MHCVB Labs in the Spring 2021 semester to investigate any improvement in their knowledge and technical performance.

Intended audience

At CCP, students enrolled in the Principles of Microbiology course are completing an upper-level four-credit science course. This course is offered four times per year (Fall, Spring, Summer 7A, and Summer 7N semesters) with a limited class size of 24 students. CCP typically runs about 30 sections in an academic year. Within our college population, this course is often used to satisfy an applied science requirement for Allied Health Care students and/or as a transfer course to another institution.

PROCEDURE

Materials

BIOL-241 custom lab kit

Each student received a custom at-home Carolina distance learning lab kit sent to their home. The lab kit contained prepared media, stains, selective and differential media, biochemical assays, required reagents, consumables, and lyophilized bacterial cultures. All bacterial cultures are classified as BSL-1 per CDC guidelines. Students also received detailed lab protocols, safety instructions, personal protective equipment (PPE), and a Duo Scope Starter Kit 2-in-1 Microscope.

CCP’s BIOL-241 course committee worked with a representative from Carolina Biological to build the custom lab kit, aligning available materials with our SLOs. Once a custom lab kit was assembled, students were able to order their custom lab kit with a submission of a voucher code (provided by the College bookstore) via the Carolina Distance Learning website (https://www.carolinadistancelearning.com/). The custom lab kit was shipped directly from the Carolina warehouse to the student’s home using a common delivery service. Any issues with the contents of the kit were immediately resolved by Carolina. These custom lab kits were funded by CARES Act money from the federal government, administered by the College at no cost to the student.

McGraw-Hill Connect Virtual Biology Labs

CCP participates in First Day with McGraw-Hill for course materials for the Principles of Microbiology course. This means that all students taking this course have the e-Textbook and Connect (including MHCVB Labs) integrated into their Canvas course. This allows the course instructors to assign weekly virtual labs and assignments directly through the LMS system.

McGraw-Hill instructor information

McGraw-Hill offers deep integration linking of Connect into our LMS Canvas. Instructors must go through a one-time pairing process to link Connect with their corresponding Canvas section of the course. Virtual Labs are deployed from Connect to Canvas, which allows for a direct link to the Virtual Labs in Canvas and gradebook integration. Instructor resources from McGraw-Hill can be found at https://mhedu.force.com/CXG/s/article/Connect-Canvas-Resources-for-instructors?dc=Instructor_Resources.

McGraw-Hill student information

Students have access to Connect, including MHCVB Labs, on the first day of class. This allows no lag time in students being able to get started on the first day of class. McGraw-Hill also provides detailed first-day-of-class support, including specific LMS integration videos that walk through step-by-step directions on how to activate and register their personal account. The video can be found at the following link: http://video.mhhe.com/watch/xUs68jEUwVnAB2K64eWMgc. After completing a one-time registration with the provided voucher code for Connect, students can simply access all assignments directly through the Canvas LMS. Once the student has completed the assigned MHCVB Labs, the grade is automatically populated in the Canvas gradebook.

MHCVB Labs are fully online lab solutions that can be used as an online lab replacement, supplement, or make-up lab to bridge the gap between the classroom (lecture) and the lab. The simulations provided by MHCVB Labs guide students to learn the practical and conceptual skills needed in a lab setting while promoting an overall positive-feedback mechanism. The use of adaptive pre- and post-lab assessments allows the instructor to customize each lab assignment. All MHCVB Labs were designed to meet the standards for accessibility from the onset.

Weekly instructional sequence of laboratory work

Laboratory instruction was structured as a weekly sequence of guided laboratory activities delivered through Canvas. While there were implementation differences between Fall 2020 and Spring 2021, the laboratory content and learning objectives remained unchanged (Table 1).

During Weeks 1–7 of Fall 2020, students did not engage in hands-on laboratory activities. Instead, instruction focused on pre-laboratory preparation. Each week, students were required to read the assigned laboratory protocol and watch custom Carolina at-home lab kit instructional videos corresponding to that week’s laboratory topic. Week 1 centered on course orientation and lab kit information. Week 2 introduced lab safety and microscopy, followed by aseptic techniques and culturing in Week 3, streak plate techniques in Week 4, preparation of bacterial smears and simple staining in Week 5, differential staining methods in Week 6, and selective and differential media in Week 7 (Table 1).

Prior to the initiation of hands-on laboratory activities in Week 8, students completed a pre-lab quiz designed to assess their baseline knowledge of microbiological concepts, laboratory techniques, and safety practices relevant to the at-home lab kit. Hands-on laboratory experimentation commenced in Week 8 using Carolina at-home lab kits. Before conducting each activity, students viewed instructor-led demonstration videos aligned with all procedures outlined in the Lab Activities Workflow (Fig. 1). Hands-on work continued throughout the remainder of the semester, with students completing laboratory worksheets as formative assessments documenting procedural accuracy, data interpretation, and skill development. During Weeks 9–11, instruction emphasized the unknown identification of gram-positive and gram-negative organisms and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (Table 1). Upon completion of all at-home laboratory activities, students completed a post-lab quiz and a final lab report to measure their learning gains relative to the course laboratory learning objectives.

During Spring 2021, the laboratory content sequence and learning objectives remained the same as in Fall 2020, with the addition of structured virtual laboratory activities to reinforce conceptual understanding and procedural skills.

Canvas student information

During the first week of classes, students received an e-mail from the College bookstore containing a voucher code for a Carolina Custom Lab Kit and directions on how to order the kit from the Carolina Biologics website. To provide adequate lab experience to meet the SLOs of the Principles of Microbiology course, these lab kits were provided to the students at no cost by the College using CARES Act funds.

Students from Fall 2020 and Spring 2021 performed Carolina labs after the seventh week of the semester. The mid-semester start time of the Carolina labs allowed students to receive their lab kits in a timely manner. Giving students the flexibility of performing labs at home provides them sufficient time and opportunity to carry out their experiments. Students were expected to complete all Carolina labs in 7 days as demonstrated in the instructor demo video (Fig. 1). Students were instructed to watch a step-by-step lab video demonstration of each Carolina lab experiment. They became familiar with equipment and lab procedures prior to performing labs. A prelab quiz was available to students 1 week prior to the start of their Carolina labs. Once students completed their prelab quiz, they would complete their Carolina labs, followed by a post-lab quiz. A final lab report was submitted by the student as an assessment of completed labs. The instructor used a standardized rubric for grading the final lab report (Appendix S3).

Suggestions for determining student learning

Pre- and post-lab quizzes are used to assess SLOs (1–11).

An overall knowledge of practical microbiology was assessed with pre-lab and post-lab quizzes using multiple-choice questions. These questions were prepared to meet each of the SLOs of the Principles of Microbiology course. In addition, to avoid test-retest bias, we used an alternative form of the same test, although never identical, in pre-lab and post-lab quizzes (Appendix S4).

Final lab report was used to assess essential lab skills based on SLOs (3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, and 11).

Students were required to submit a final lab report on the experiments from the Carolina Labs, including images of their results and lab products, to exhibit their overall essential lab skills. Instructors evaluated all lab reports using a standardized rubric. In addition, all students were asked to give their feedback for future improvements (Appendix S5).

Sample data

Fundamentals of microscopy

Students used the Mini Duo-Scope provided in the Carolina lab kit and observed prepared slides. To record their observations, students used mobile devices to take pictures and inserted them into their lab worksheet.

Aseptic technique and use of different types of media

Aseptic transfer and subculturing of microorganisms are essential techniques in Microbiology. For microbiology experiments to be successful, students need to master aseptic techniques. The students in this lab activity reactivated lyophilized bacterial cultures and then subcultured them in broth and on agar plates. For their lab worksheets, students photographed media at 48 and 72 h to document the growth of organisms.

Streak-plate method

In this experiment, students mixed two known cultures (Serratia marcescens and Micrococcus luteus) from their pure cultures to create a mixed culture. Students followed the procedure for streaking the plate for isolation and subsequently incubated them for 48 and 72 h. Following the incubation period, students took pictures for assessment (Fig. 2).

Mixed cultures of Serratia marcescens and Micrococcus luteus.

Simple and Gram-staining techniques

In this experiment, students prepared slides for staining by heat fixation. Simple and Gram-staining techniques were performed on the following strains: S. marcescens, Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and M. luteus. Following the staining techniques, students observed their slides using 10× and 40× objectives and recorded their results on their lab worksheets.

Selective and differential media

In this experiment, students used E. coli, S. epidermidis, and M. luteus organisms and inoculated them on mannitol-salt agar, MacConkey agar, and eosin-methylene blue agar. Following incubation periods of 48 and 72 h, students recorded their results and interpretations on their lab worksheets.

Biochemical assays

In this experiment, students performed three of the most commonly used biochemical assays for E. coli, S. epidermidis, and M. luteus identification: triple sugar iron slants, oxidase test, and catalase test. Following incubation periods of 48 and 72 h, students recorded their results and interpretations on their lab worksheets.

Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion

In this experiment, students observed the effects of two different antimicrobial drugs on two common bacterial species: one gram-positive (S. epidermidis) and one gram-negative (E. coli). Students measured zones of inhibition around paper disks containing each of these antibacterial drugs (bacitracin disks and tetracycline disks) that have been incubated with each bacterial species. To determine the effectiveness of each drug against the two types of bacteria, students also compared the measurements to standard values and recorded their results (Appendix S7).

Safety issues

All organisms selected for these activities are characterized as BSL-1 according to CDC Guidelines (https://www.carolina.com/teacher-resources/Interactive/living-organism-care-guide-bacteria/tr10478.tr#biosafety). Students must follow all safety guidelines to ensure that the Carolina Lab Kit materials are safe to use and do not cause harm to members of the household. Before performing lab activities, students must watch videos on how to wear all proper PPE, prepare disinfectants, disinfect surface areas, and properly clean up and dispose of all materials (Appendix S2).

RESULTS

Field testing

In March 2020, many colleges and universities were forced to shut down all in-person learning and move to remote learning due to the COVID-19 pandemic. This proved to be problematic for some science-based courses with hands-on lab components. The technical skills developed during these hands-on lab exercises are critical to complementing the student learning outcomes. At Community College of Philadelphia, the Principles of Microbiology course had to be modified into a remote-learning course with a hands-on lab component that would be deployed for the Fall 2020 (3 sections; 66 students) and Spring 2021 (7 sections; 154 students) semesters. In this study, we explored the pre- and post-usage of McGraw-Hill Connect labs to enhance the technical skills and learning outcomes with the goal of improving the students’ successful completion and results of the hands-on lab exercises. This course used a custom-designed lab kit from Carolina Labs delivered to the students’ homes. The hands-on Carolina lab kit exercises were asynchronous and self-guided, with detailed directions and video demonstrations over the course of the semester. Students completed the lab exercises, recorded their data, completed pre- and post-lab quizzes, evaluated the streak-plate method, and submitted a final lab report for the Fall 2020 and Spring 2021 semesters. In the Spring 2021 semester, complimentary McGraw-Hill Connect Virtual Biology Labs were added to enhance the hands-on Carolina lab kit exercises.

Assessment of the students’ competency with the Carolina lab kit exercises was conducted through pre-lab quizzes, post-lab quizzes, and final lab reports of results and conclusions for both the Fall 2020 and Spring 2021 semesters. Comparisons of the results of assessments between the Fall 2020 (without MHCVB labs) and Spring 2021 (with MHCVB labs) semesters can be seen in Fig. 1. In this study, we saw an overall improvement in students’ scores using the MHCVB Labs.

The technical proficiency of students in the simple stain, Gram stain, and streak plate techniques was evaluated using a standardized scoring rubric to ensure uniformity across sections (Appendix S7). The rubric outlined specific performance criteria that were applied consistently to all student-submitted images. Two instructors participated in the assessment process and utilized the same rubric and scoring criteria. Student performance data from all course sections were compiled in a shared Excel worksheet for analysis. The utilization of a common rubric and multiple evaluators facilitated the consistent application of scoring criteria across sections.

Evidence of student learning

This study assessed the student learning outcomes of the course using several methods. The scores of pre- and post-lab quizzes, evaluation of streak-plate method, and a submitted lab report were compared for evidence of gains in competency from the Fall 2020 (without MHCVB Labs) semester to the Spring 2021 (with MHCVB Labs) semester. Student proficiency was evaluated by normalizing total scores to a 100-point scale, facilitating direct comparisons between groups. Figure 3 shows the comparison in scores of the pre-lab quizzes (Fall 2020, 80.3 ± 6.1; Spring 2021, 76.4 ± 3.7; P = 0.236), post-lab quizzes (Fall 2020, 89.3 ± 2.5; Spring 2021, 85.3 ± 2.3; P = 0.069), and lab report (Fall 2020, 82.7 ± 2.1; Spring 2021, 78.7 ± 4.8; P = 0.214) between the two semesters, indicating no statistically significant change in scores (P > 0.05) using the MHCVB Labs. However, assessing the students’ hands-on techniques by comparing images of the streak-plate method from the Carolina lab kit showed an increase in students’ scores from the Fall 2020 semester (57.7 ± 2.5) to the Spring 2021 semester (80.1 ± 7.6; P = 0.001). Student performance on microscopy-related laboratory techniques (Fig. 4) improved between the Fall 2020 and Spring 2021 semesters. Scores on the simple staining assessment increased significantly from Fall 2020 (57.3 ± 11.6) to Spring 2021 (74.4 ± 6.4; P < 0.01). Similarly, Gram-staining scores showed a significant improvement between Fall 2020 (55.0 ± 8.7) and Spring 2021 (80.1 ± 6.4; P < 0.001).

Assessing a course like Principles of Microbiology needs to include both a theoretical knowledge base and a hands-on lab competency. The shift to remote learning proved more problematic for the hands-on lab competency than for the theoretical knowledge. This study showed that the use of MHCVB Labs prior to completion of the hands-on labs significantly increased the competency of the hands-on techniques using the Carolina lab kit compared to the kit alone.

DISCUSSION

Pedagogical implications

The improvement in staining and isolation techniques suggests that MHCVB Labs provided students with a conceptual foundation before performing physical experiments, reducing cognitive load and improving performance (13, 18). This blended approach supports recommendations for integrating virtual and physical labs to maximize learning outcomes in STEM education (17, 20).

Providing lab kits at no cost through CARES Act funding eliminated financial barriers, which is critical for supporting underrepresented and first-generation students (2–4). Continued institutional investment in accessible resources and instructor training will be essential for sustaining effective hybrid and remote laboratory instruction.

Despite being situated in a pandemic context, the core pedagogical features of the intervention—scaffolded inquiry, emphasis on experimental reasoning, and formative feedback—align with best practices that continue to shape post-pandemic laboratory instruction.

Alternatively, many post-pandemic laboratory courses continue to incorporate hybrid elements, digital pre-lab activities, and flexible instructional designs, suggesting that insights from this study remain informative for ongoing curriculum development.

Possible modifications

As the Community College of Philadelphia looks to move back to on-campus activities, the Principles of Microbiology course will be offered in both in-person and hybrid formats. The use of MHCVB connect labs will continue to be used to see if there is an increase in the in-person lab technique competency. Future research should explore long-term impacts on student retention and success in microbiology and related disciplines.

Conclusion

The integration of McGraw-Hill Connect Virtual Biology Labs with Carolina Custom Lab Kits significantly improved students’ hands-on microbiology skills compared to using the kit alone. While theoretical knowledge remained consistent across semesters, technical competencies such as streak plating and staining showed marked improvement. This blended approach offers a sustainable model for remote and hybrid microbiology instruction. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the difficulty of developing hands-on laboratory skills in a fully remote environment. While conceptual learning outcomes were maintained, students demonstrated stronger technical competency when virtual laboratory simulations were used as preparatory tools before hands-on experiments. These findings support the continued use of blended laboratory approaches to enhance skill development in remote, hybrid, and in-person microbiology courses.
