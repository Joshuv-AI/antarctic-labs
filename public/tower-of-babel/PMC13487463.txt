A Safe and Effective Bivalent Vaccine Candidate Against Duck Enteritis Virus and Duck‐Origin Novel Goose Parvovirus
Authors: Liu Chen, Jionggang Hua, Weicheng Ye, Cun Zhang, Xinjin Li, Yinchu Zhu, Yuan Fu, Liang He, Suxin Huo, Zongxiao Wang, Zhuanli Wu, Tao Yun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSince 2015, duck‐origin novel goose parvovirus (NGPV) has caused severe economic losses to the Chinese duck industry, with no licensed vaccines or treatments available. Our previous study constructed a recombinant duck enteritis virus (rDEV) from an attenuated vaccine strain, which conferred full protection against virulent DEV challenge. NGPV VP2 is the major immunogenic structural antigen. In this study, we generated a recombinant virus rNGPV VP2‐Cre by inserting the NGPV VP2 gene into the rDEV genome. VP2 expression and virus‐like particle assembly were confirmed. Safety and efficacy were evaluated in ducklings. The results showed that rNGPV VP2‐Cre was safe, facilitated clearance of NGPV and provided strong protection against challenge with a highly virulent DEV strain. In summary, rNGPV VP2‐Cre represents a promising bivalent vaccine candidate for concurrent control of DEV and NGPV infections.Keywords: bivalent vaccine, duck enteritis virus, duck origin‐novel goose parvovirus, immunoprotection, virus‐like particles

Introduction

Short beak and dwarfism syndrome (SBDS), characterised by clinical signs such as diarrhoea, beak shrinkage, growth retardation, tongue exposure and osteoporosis, was first reported in mule ducks in southwestern France during 1971–1972 (Palya et al. 2009). Since 2015, outbreaks of SBDS have occurred in commercial flocks of young ducklings in China (Chen, Dou, et al. 2015; Chen et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2016). The causative agent of SBDS was identified as duck‐origin novel goose parvovirus (NGPV), a genetically variant strain related to Muscovy duck parvovirus (MDPV) and goose parvovirus (GPV). NGPV infection has gradually emerged in duck production regions of Shandong, Anhui, Fujian, Jiangsu, Guangdong, Guangxi, Sichuan, Jiangxi, Henan, Zhejiang, Hubei and Hebei provinces, as well as Beijing, Shanghai and Chongqing municipalities (Chen, Dou, et al. 2015; Chen et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2016, 2021; Chi and Lin 2024; Huang 2022; Qiao et al. 2024; Wei et al. 2024; Jiao et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2019; Bian 2019). Most infected ducks exhibit stunted growth, which increases culling rates and reduces production quality in duck flocks. In addition to China, similar outbreaks have been reported in Vietnam, Poland and Egypt in recent years (Huong et al. 2024; Matczuk et al. 2020; Eid et al. 2024; Soliman et al. 2020). These outbreaks cause significant economic losses and threaten the sustainable development of the duck breeding industry.

NGPV is the etiological agent of SBDS. It is an icosahedral, nonenveloped virus measuring approximately 20–22 nm in diameter (Li 2017). The virus belongs to the genus Dependovirus within the subfamily Parvovirinae (Chen, Dou, et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2016). This classification is similar to that of MDPV and GPV. The genomes of MDPV, GPV and NGPV show limited sequence divergence and are approximately 5.1 kb in length. The genome of NGPV consists of a single‐stranded DNA (ssDNA) molecule containing two open reading frames (ORFs). These ORFs are flanked by identical inverted terminal repeats at both termini. ORF1 and ORF2 encode two nonstructural proteins (NS1 and NS2) and three structural proteins (VP1, VP2 and VP3). Among these proteins, VP2 exhibits the highest immunogenicity. In addition, VP3 is the most abundant capsid protein and accounts for approximately 80% of the total capsid protein content. VP3 contains the major antigenic determinant that induces production of neutralising antibodies (Yu et al. 2016).

Similar to pseudorabies virus (PRV), duck enteritis virus (DEV), which belongs to the family Alphaherpesviridae, is considered an attractive viral vector for vaccine development. Several characteristics support this application. Live attenuated DEV vaccines are widely used to prevent lethal DEV infections in major duck‐producing regions. The virus has a relatively narrow host range, which contributes to favourable biosafety. In addition, the DEV genome contains multiple noncoding regions suitable for insertion of foreign genes and has a large genetic carrying capacity. Successful use of DEV as a viral vector has already been demonstrated. For example, a DEV‐based bivalent viral vector vaccine expressing avian influenza virus H5 antigen (rDEVus78Ha strain) has been approved as a Class I new veterinary pharmaceutical by the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of the People's Republic of China (Liu et al. 2011).

At present, no commercial vaccine is available for NGPV infection. Development of safe and effective vaccines, therefore, remains an urgent priority. In the present study, a recombinant DEV expressing the NGPV VP2 gene was generated using the infectious full‐length clone of the DEV vaccine strain pDEV‐EF1 (Chen et al. 2013; Chen, Yu, et al. 2015). The vaccine potential of the recombinant virus rNGPV VP2‐Cre, including safety, immunogenicity and protective efficacy, was subsequently evaluated in ducklings.

Materials and Methods

Viruses, Bacterial Strains and Plasmids

The bacterial strain pDEV‐EF1/GS1783, which harbours the full‐length infectious bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) clone of the DEV vaccine strain, along with the corresponding viruses rE1 and rDEV‐Cre, was previously constructed and stored in our laboratory (Chen et al. 2013; Chen, Yu, et al. 2015). The DEV CHv strain (CVCC AV1221), a highly virulent strain, was obtained from the National Center for Veterinary Culture Collection (CVCC) (Beijing, China). A highly virulent NGPV ZJ strain was isolated from clinical samples. The plasmids pEP‐BGH‐end and pCAGGS‐NLS/Cre were kindly provided by Dr. N. Osterrieder (Freie Universität Berlin, Berlin, Germany).

Cell, Culture Condition and Cell Line Information

Chicken embryo fibroblasts (CEFs) were isolated from 9‐ to 11‐day‐old specific‐pathogen‐free (SPF) embryonated eggs (Ningbo Chunpai Agricultural Technology Co. Ltd., Ningbo, China) following standard procedures. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco‐BRL, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) supplemented with 8% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin).

CEF line information was as follows: (i) Metadata: Species Gallus gallus domesticus; mixed‐sex whole embryonic tissue; primary CEFs; no RRID (only assigned to immortalised cell lines). (ii) Source and acquisition: SPF fertilised eggs were obtained from the above supplier with official SPF quarantine certification. (iii) Authentication and contamination detection: (a) Species identification was performed via chicken COI barcoding, showing 100% match with Gallus gallus GenBank reference sequences. (b) Fresh primary CEFs are not listed as misidentified/contaminated lines in the ICLAC database. (c) All CEF batches tested negative for mycoplasma by rRNA‐targeted PCR before experiments.

Construction of the Recombinant Vector BGH‐NGPV VP2

The NGPV VP2 gene was synthesised after codon optimisation based on the codon usage preference of ducks, as shown in Supporting Information S1. Kpn I and Not I restriction sites were introduced at the 5′ and 3′ termini, respectively. Gene synthesis was performed by Sangon Corporation (Shanghai, China) according to the NGPV VP2 sequence (GenBank: ATX74763.1). The plasmid BGH‐NGPV VP2 was constructed by inserting the NGPV VP2 gene into the pEP‐BGH‐end vector using Kpn I and Not I restriction sites.

Generation of the Recombinant DEV BAC Clones Delivering the NGPV VP2 Gene

The recombinant BAC clone was generated by inserting the expression cassette pCMV‐NGPV VP2‐BGH‐pA into the intergenic region between DEV US7 and US8 through two‐step Red‐mediated recombination (en passant) (Tischer et al. 2006, 2010) (Figure 1). Specifically, to construct pNGPV VP2, the pCMV‐NGPV VP2‐BGH‐pA expression cassette flanked by homology arms corresponding to DEV US7 and US8 was amplified from BGH‐NGPV VP2 using Rec‐s and Rec‐as primers (Table 1). The purified PCR product (3505 bp) was electroporated into GS1783/pDEV‐EF1 competent bacteria. The transformed bacteria were incubated for 2 h at 32°C and plated on LB agar supplemented with chloramphenicol (CAM, 34 μg/mL) and kanamycin (KAN, 50 μg/mL). The cultures were incubated until colonies appeared. A CAM/KAN‐resistant clone (pkan‐NGPV VP2) was identified as a positive clone by PCR using jd‐F and jd‐R primers (Table 1). Confirmation was further performed by restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis after BamH I digestion. The confirmed clone was subjected to a second red recombination step to remove the kanamycin resistance gene. Colonies that were resistant to chloramphenicol but sensitive to kanamycin were selected. Subsequently, the recombinant clone (pNGPV VP2) was verified by RFLP analysis using BamH I digestion. Additional confirmation was obtained by sequencing PCR products (3001 bp) amplified with jd‐F and jd‐R primers (Table 1), which flank the inserted NGPV VP2 sequence.

Rescue of Recombinant Viruses

The pNGPV VP2 plasmid was isolated from bacteria using the alkaline lysis method. Subsequently, 2 μg of pNGPV VP2 was transfected into CEFs cultured in 6‐well plates using the calcium phosphate precipitation method as previously described (Chen et al. 2013). After transfection, the cells were cultured until fluorescent plaques appeared. The cultures were maintained until 70%–80% cytopathic effect (CPE) was observed. The virus harvested from these cells was designated rNGPV VP2. To eliminate the influence of additional BAC sequences containing xgpt and gfp genes, the redundant sequence was removed through site‐specific recombination between flanking loxP sites. This excision was performed by co‐transfecting 4 μg of pNGPV VP2 and 1 μg of the Cre recombinase expression plasmid pCAGGS‐NLS/Cre into CEF cultures. One day after transfection, the culture medium was replaced with DMEM containing 1.5% methylcellulose (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Subsequently, several rounds of limiting dilution and plaque purification were performed. Typical DEV CPE lacking green fluorescent protein (GFP) expression was obtained. The accurate excision of redundant BAC sequences from rNGPV VP2‐Cre was confirmed by PCR amplification using the 18F/15R primer pair, followed by sequencing analysis.

Measurement of Viral Plaque Size

Plaque sizes of the recombinant virus rNGPV VP2 and the control virus rE1 were measured as described previously (Chen et al. 2013; Chen, Yu, et al. 2015). One hundred fluorescent plaques were randomly selected and photographed. Plaque sizes were measured using ImageJ software (version 1.42) (http://rsb.info.nih.gov/ij/). Statistical analysis was performed using a two‐tailed unpaired t‐test in GraphPad Prism 10 (San Diego, CA, USA).

Western Blot Analysis of NGPV VP2 Protein Expression

Expression of the NGPV VP2 protein was examined by Western blot analysis using an anti‐GPV VP2 monoclonal antibody (mAb) prepared in our laboratory (Chen et al. 2022). Specifically, CEFs were infected with rNGPV VP2 or rNGPV VP2‐Cre at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1.0. When most cells exhibited CPE, the cells were harvested, washed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and lysed with sample buffer. The lysates were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and transferred onto a nitrocellulose (NC) membrane (Millipore, Boston, MA, USA). The membrane was blocked with PBST (PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20) supplemented with 10% skimmed milk for 2 h at 37°C. The membrane was incubated with the primary antibody (anti‐GPV VP2 mAb, 1:500 dilution), followed by a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated goat anti‐mouse IgG secondary antibody (Santa Cruz, CA, USA). The signal was detected using Western Blot Hyper HRP Substrate (Takara Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Chemiluminescence signals were visualised with a Molecular Imager ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

NGPV VP2 Protein Localisation

CEFs were seeded on glass slides in 12‐well plates. The next day, cells were infected with rNGPV VP2‐Cre at an MOI of 0.02. Immunofluorescence assay (IFA) was performed at 24 h post‐infection. After two washes with PBS (pH 7.2), the cells were fixed with ice‐cold methanol–acetone (1:1). Following three additional washes, the cells were blocked with 1% BSA in PBST for 1 h at 37°C. The cells were then incubated sequentially with anti‐GPV VP2 mAb (1:100 dilution) as the primary antibody and Cy3‐labelled goat anti‐mouse IgG (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) as the secondary antibody. Finally, cell nuclei were stained with DAPI solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Fluorescence signals were visualised using an Eclipse TS2 inverted microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica TCS‐SP5) (Leica, Heidelberg, Germany) equipped with a 100× oil immersion objective.

Electron Microscopy

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

CEFs infected with rNGPV VP2‐Cre at an MOI of 0.01 were processed for sectioning at 48 h post‐infection. TEM was performed following the method described previously (Chen et al. 2021).

Immuno‐Electron Microscopy (IEM)

IEM was performed according to the method described previously (Shang et al. 2023). In brief, rNGPV VP2‐Cre‐infected CEFs were fixed at 48 h post‐infection with 4% paraformaldehyde in PHEM buffer (pH 6.9) containing 60 mM PIPES (piperazine‐N,N′‐bis(2‐ethanesulfonic acid)), 25 mM HEPES (4‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)‐1‐piperazineethanesulfonic acid), 10 mM EGTA (ethylene glycol‐bis(β‐aminoethyl ether)‐N,N,N′,N′‐tetraacetic acid) and 2 mM MgSO4 for 4 h at room temperature. CEFs were embedded in 10% (wt/vol) gelatin, cryoprotected overnight in 2.3 M sucrose containing 20% polyvinylpyrrolidone, frozen in liquid nitrogen and cryosectioned at −120°C using a Leica EM FCS cryoultramicrotome (Leica, Heidelberg, Germany). Thawed 90‐nm sections were quenched with 20 mM glycine for 5 min. After blocking with 3% BSA in PBS for 30 min, immunogold labeling was performed using GPV VP2 mAb (1:100 in 1% BSA) followed by 10‐nm gold‐conjugated protein A. The sections were stained with 1.8% methylcellulose and 0.4% uranyl acetate and observed using an HT7700 transmission electron microscope (HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan).

Evalution of Genetic Stablity of Recombinant Virus

The resuced rNGPV VP2‐Cre was labelled as first‐generation virus, and then the virus was re‐infected with CEFs to prepare the next generation of the virus until the virus was uploaded to the 20th passage. DNA was extracted from the 1st, 5th, 10th and 20th passages of each virus according to the instructions of the Animal Virus DNA/RNA Extraction Kit (Magnetic Bead) (Compurify Biotech Co. Ltd., Changzhou, Jiangsu, China) and PCR identification with jd‐F and jd‐R primers (Table 1) was performed. A multi‐step growth curve of virus titers at passages 1, 5, 10 and 20 for each virus was determined on CEFs. All experiments were repeated 3 times. Expression of VP2 proteins from different passages‐infected cells was analysed by Western blot.

Quantitation of Viral DNA

To determine viral DNA in swabs or tissues of experimental ducklings, SYBR real‐time PCR was performed as previously described (Shang et al. 2023). Briefly, viral DNA (vDNA) was isolated from NGPV‐infected swabs or tissues of ducklings using the Animal Virus DNA/RNA Extraction Kit (Magnetic Bead) (Compurify Biotech Co. Ltd., Changzhou, Jiangsu, China). One microliter of purified vDNA was diluted in nuclease‐free H2O and added to a 20 μL reaction mixture. The reaction mixture contained 10 μL qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Shanghai, China), 0.2 μL NGPV NS‐F primer (5′‐TACAATGGAACACAGAATWCC‐3′), 0.2 μL NGPV NS‐R primer (5′‐TCAGACACAACAGGAACWA‐3′) and 8.6 μL ddH2O. The NGPV NS‐F and NGPV NS‐R primers target the SF3 domain of the NGPV NS helicase gene. PCR conditions included an initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 50°C for 30 s.

Determination of Serum IgG Antibodies

IgG antibody titers against NGPV VP2 were determined using an indirect enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (iELISA) based on VP2 protein. The VP2 protein was expressed and purified with a His‐tag using a prokaryotic Escherichia coli expression system. The iELISA was performed according to the following procedure. Purified VP2 protein diluted in 0.05 M carbonate–bicarbonate buffer (CBS, pH 9.6) was added to 96‐well microplates (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) at a final concentration of 50 ng/well. The microplates were incubated overnight at 4°C and blocked with PBST containing 10% (w/v) skimmed milk and 10% BSA for 1 h at 37°C. Subsequently, 100 μL of 1:100 diluted serum samples, positive sera and negative sera were added to the corresponding wells. After incubation at 37°C for 1 h, the plates were washed three times with PBST. The wells were then incubated with 100 μL of HRP‐conjugated goat anti‐duck IgG (1:2000 dilution) (Kirkegaard and Perry Laboratories Inc., Gaithersburg, MD, USA). Finally, 100 μL tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) was added for colour development at 37°C for 10 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 μL of 2 M hydrochloric acid. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a FlexA‐200 microplate reader (Aosheng, Zhejiang, China).

Determination of Neutralising Antibody Levels in Serum

Virus‐neutralising (VN) antibodies were measured as previously described (Zhou et al. 2022). In brief, serum samples were heat‐inactivated at 56°C for 30 min. The inactivated sera were subjected to twofold serial dilution and mixed with culture medium containing NGPV ZJ at a dose of 200 ELD50 (50% embryo lethal dose) in equal volumes. After incubation at 37°C for 1 h, the mixtures were inoculated into 9‐day‐old embryonated Cherry Valley duck eggs. The eggs were incubated at 37°C with 50% humidity for 7 days. Mortality of duck embryos was recorded for each dilution, and the 50% neutralisation endpoint for each sample was calculated using the Reed–Muench method.

Animal Experiments

This study was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of ZAAS (permit number 2021ZAASLA66). The study was conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for Experimental Animals issued by the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People's Republic of China (Beijing, China). Throughout the study, all animals were maintained in a segregated facility. This arrangement reduced the risk of cross‐infection and ensured the reliability of the experimental results.

In the safety test, 50 healthy 2‐day‐old female mallard ducks (Anas platyrhyncha) were purchased from a commercial hatchery in Huzhou. Ducklings without prior immunisation were housed in SPF animal isolators equipped with negative‐pressure ventilation. Before the challenge, serum samples and anal swabs were collected to confirm the absence of NGPV and MDPV antigens and antibodies. Ducklings were randomly assigned to five groups (Groups 1–5) with 10 birds per group (n = 10). Groups 1 and 2 were intramuscularly immunised with 1 × 106 TCID50 of rNGPV VP2‐Cre and rDEV‐Cre, respectively. Groups 3 and 4 received 1 × 105 TCID50 of rNGPV VP2‐Cre and rDEV‐Cre, respectively. Group 5 served as the unvaccinated control and received 1 mL of culture medium. Clinical signs and mortality were monitored daily for 14 days after immunisation.

In the efficacy test, 120 healthy 2‐day‐old female mallard ducks, free of NGPV and GPV antigens and antibodies, were randomly allocated to four groups (Groups 1–4) with 30 birds per group (n = 30). Birds in Groups 1 and 2 were intramuscularly immunised with 1 × 105 TCID50 rNGPV VP2‐Cre (A) and 1 × 104 TCID50 rNGPV VP2‐Cre (B), respectively. Group 3 served as the unvaccinated control and received 1 mL PBS, whereas Group 4 remained untreated (NC). Ten ducklings from each group were randomly selected and marked for subsequent collection of anal swabs, throat swabs and serum samples. The remaining 20 ducklings were reserved for tissue collection (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney and intestine) for viral titre determination. At 8 days post‐immunisation (d.p.i), serum samples were collected from the 10 marked ducklings in Groups 1–3, labelled A (8 d.p.i), B (8 d.p.i) and PBS (8 d.p.i). Subsequently, all ducklings were challenged with 105.5 EID50 of the NGPV ZJ strain. Anal and throat swabs were collected at 1, 3, 5 and 7 days post‐challenge (d.p.c) from the marked ducklings. Serum samples were collected at 16 d.p.c, labelled A+NGPV (16 d.p.c), B+NGPV (16 d.p.c) and PBS+NGPV (16 d.p.c). Tissues from five ducklings in each group were collected at 1, 3, 5 and 7 d.p.c. Duodenum samples collected at 5 d.p.c were used for histological analysis. After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin embedding, sectioning and haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, the duodenum sections were examined microscopically. At 47 days after the NGPV challenge, the remaining ducklings in Groups 1 and 2 were boost‐immunised with 1 × 105 TCID50 and 1 × 104 TCID50 of rNGPV VP2‐Cre, respectively. Fourteen days after the booster immunisation, all ducks in Groups 1–3 were challenged intramuscularly with 100 × DLD50 of the highly virulent DEV CHv strain. Clinical signs and mortality were monitored daily for 14 days after challenge.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate or more (n ≥ 3 per group). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA). An unpaired two‐tailed Student t‐test and one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used for comparisons between two groups and among multiple groups, respectively. Differences were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. Statistical significance * is indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001; ns, not significant.

Results

Identification of BAC Mutants Recombinants Carrying NGPV VP2

pNGPV VP2 and the corresponding intermediate pkan‐NGPV VP2 were extracted and digested with BamH I, followed by electrophoretic analysis. The RFLP pattern obtained after BamH I digestion matched the predicted pattern based on the reference sequence of the DEV vaccine strain (GenBank: KF487736.1) (Figure 2A) (Yang et al. 2013). Furthermore, PCR products amplified from recombinant BAC DNA using jd‐F/jd‐R primers showed the expected size and sequencing results matched the predicted sequence (Figure 2B). These results indicate that the NGPV VP2 gene was correctly inserted into the target region of the DEV genome.

Fluorescent plaques appeared in CEFs 48 h after transfection of pNGPV VP2. Cells were harvested when 70%–80% of cells exhibited fluorescence. The recovered virus was designated rNGPV VP2 (Figure 3B). rE1 is the parental virus, as control(Figure 3A). To generate rNGPV VP2‐Cre without redundant BAC sequences, pCAGGS‐NLS/Cre and pNGPV VP2 were co‐transfected into CEFs. When typical DEV CPE appeared, plaques without green fluorescence were obtained after several rounds of limiting dilution and plaque purification. The purified virus was designated rNGPV VP2‐Cre (Figure 3C). PCR analysis confirmed deletion of the mini‐F sequence from rNGPV VP2‐Cre (data not shown).

Comparison of Plaque Size Between rNGPV VP2 and Parental Virus

Plaque areas of recombinant virus rNGPV VP2 and parental virus rE1 were compared at 2 d.p.i. Plaques formed by rNGPV VP2 were approximately 4.05% smaller than those of rE1. However, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.223) (Figure 4).

Expression and Localisation Analysis of NGPV VP2 in rNGPV VP2‐Cre‐Infected CEFs

VP2 protein expression in CEFs infected with rNGPV VP2‐Cre was firstly examined by Western blot analysis. Results showed that a specific protein band at approximately 72 kDa was detected in cells infected with rNGPV VP2‐Cre. This band was absent in cells infected with the control virus rDEV‐Cre (Figure 5). The observed molecular weight matched the predicted size of VP2.

Evalution of Genetic Stablity of Recombinant Virus

The genetic stability of recombinant rNGPV VP2‐Cre was evaluated by serial viral passaging. Three detection methods were adopted for validation: PCR to verify stable integration of the VP2 gene into the DEV genome, Western blot to examine VP2 protein expression in cells infected with viruses at different passages and determination of virus growth curve. Combined PCR and Western blot data confirmed sustained VP2 insertion within the DEV genome (Figure S1a,b). In addition, viral titers showed no obvious fluctuations at passages 1, 5, 10 and 20 (Figure S1c).

Animal Experiments

In the safety test, similar to the rDEV‐Cre immunisation groups, ducks immunised with rNGPV VP2‐Cre at doses of 1 × 106 TCID50/mL or 1 × 105 TCID50/mL showed no illness or mortality during the 14‐day observation period after immunisation (Table 2).

In the efficacy test, immunisation and challenge schedules for the duck assay followed the schematic diagram shown in Figure 8. Eight days after primary immunisation with rNGPV VP2‐Cre, ducklings were challenged with a highly virulent NGPV strain. Clinical symptoms in 10‐day‐old ducks infected with NGPV were not obvious. Discussion

Viral vector vaccines have been validated as powerful tools to contain viral pandemics. Adenovirus‐vectored COVID‐19 vaccines stand as a prominent success case, conferring 94% vaccine efficacy (VE) against symptomatic infection following two‐dose immunisation. This vaccine platform has played an indispensable role in curbing the global COVID‐19 pandemic (Brüssow 2023).

Currently, only a limited number of studies have investigated vaccines against NGPV. To date, only inactivated vaccines (Zhou et al. 2022) and subunit vaccines (Shang et al. 2023; Ning et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2024; Xiao et al. 2022) have been preliminarily evaluated. Similar to the subunit vaccine based on NGPV VP2 expressed in the insect‐baculovirus system (Xiao et al. 2022) the VP2 antigen delivered by the DEV vector can also self‐assemble into VLPs. VLPs are widely recognised to have multiple advantages in vaccine development. VLPs closely resemble the structure of natural virions but lack viral genetic material, which makes them highly immunogenic while maintaining a favourable safety profile.

Compared with inactivated vaccines and subunit vaccines, which usually require two to three rounds of immunisation, viral vector vaccines provide several advantages. First, viral vector vaccines can induce both humoral and cellular immune responses. They also generate strong and relatively long‐lasting immunity without requiring additional adjuvants. Second, ducklings younger than three weeks are the most susceptible to NGPV infection. The immune responses generated by subunit and inactivated vaccines in very young ducklings may not develop rapidly enough to prevent infection. In contrast, viral vector vaccines can provide earlier immune stimulation and faster protection in this highly susceptible population. Third, viral vector vaccines may simultaneously protect against two pathogens, which reduces immunisation frequency and overall vaccination cost in commercial duck production.

In the present study, NGPV detected in anal and throat swabs of the group immunised with 1 × 105 TCID50 rNGPV VP2‐Cre became undetectable at 5 d.p.c., and viral loads in tissues were markedly reduced; notably, VP2‐specific antibodies were not detected in ducklings at 8 days after primary immunisation with rNGPV VP2‐Cre, yet the vaccinated groups exhibited rapid viral clearance upon NGPV challenge. Several explanations may account for the low VP2 antibody levels detected at 8 d.p.i. as well as this discrepancy between undetectable serum VP2 antibodies and effective viral clearance. First, the humoral immune system of 2‐day‐old ducklings is still immature and the primary immune response may be dominated by cell‐mediated immunity; Like other herpesviruses vector, DEV has been confirmed to be capable of inducing robust CD8+ T‐cell responses and secretion of cytokines such as IFN‐γ (Kumar et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2026), which maybe be critical for clearing intracellular parvovirus infections. And the recombinant virus may still stimulate sufficient cellular immune response to confer protection against NGPV infection. Second, an 8‐day period after immunisation may be insufficient for ducklings to produce detectable high‐titre serum IgG. Third, we cannot exclude a potential contribution of mucosal immunity (e.g., secretory IgA) in the early clearance of NGPV from the digestive tract (Yang et al. 2009), although this was beyond the scope of the current study. Additional studies are therefore required to clarify the immune mechanisms responsible for protection and further evaluate the immune responses induced by this vaccine candidate; future investigations will specifically focus on evaluating the cell‐mediated immune responses elicited by rNGPV VP2‐Cre, including antigen‐specific T‐cell proliferation, cytokine profiling (e.g., IFN‐γ, IL‐4) and CTL activity against VP2, to fully elucidate its protective mechanisms.

During the design of the animal experiments, the age‐dependent susceptibility of ducklings to NGPV infection was carefully considered. Ducklings between one and three weeks of age are the most susceptible to infection, so vaccine efficacy against NGPV was evaluated eight days after primary immunisation. In addition, to observe clinical manifestations of SBDS after challenge with highly virulent NGPV, the booster immunisation was postponed until the ducks were 47 days old. This timing was selected because the duration of immunity induced by the DEV vaccine is approximately one month. Nevertheless, the optimal vaccination schedule (including how many doses? At what time schedule? Homologous or heterologous booster) requires further investigation and refinement in future studies (Brüssow 2023).

Since NGPV infection can cause intestinal barrier damage and diarrhoea in ducks (Liu et al. 2024), intestinal pathological examinations were also performed. These analyses showed that immunisation with 1 × 105 TCID50 rNGPV VP2‐Cre significantly reduced intestinal lesions caused by NGPV infection, further supporting the protective effect of this recombinant vaccine. Of course, the present evaluation of protective efficacy in this study was centred on viral clearance and acute intestinal injury within 14 days post‐challenge, without longitudinal tracking of productive performance parameters such as body weight gain, beak development and final culling rate. Given that the hallmark of SBDS is growth retardation and dwarfism, we fully recognise that long‐term monitoring is the ‘gold standard’ for verifying the vaccine's ability to reduce economic losses. Such studies are already planned in our ongoing research programme, with the aim of determining whether a single immunisation can effectively protect commercial meat‐type ducks until market age.

Furthermore, the GPV VP2 monoclonal antibody showed cross‐reactivity with NGPV VP2, which is likely due to the high sequence homology between these proteins. Based on this cross‐reactivity, it is reasonable to speculate that rNGPV VP2‐Cre may also provide protective effects against both DEV and GPV infections and possibly against Muscovy duck parvovirus (MDPV). This possibility is supported by the findings of Gan et al. (2024). Their study reported that the SBDS subunit vaccine (rBac‐JS01 VP2 strain), a recombinant baculovirus expressing SBDSV VP2 protein, provided effective and durable cross‐protection against GPV and partial cross‐protection against MDPV, although the protective efficacy against MDPV was somewhat lower than that observed against GPV (Gan et al. 2024). Of course, further independent GPV and MDPV challenge experiments are needed before confirmed this view.

Our study demonstrated that the rNGPV VP2‐Cre with 104 TCID50 dose conferred limited intestinal protection, whereas the 105 TCID50 dose was clearly effective, defines a critical floor for the minimum effective dose (MED) under experimental conditions. Given the potential for dosing inconsistencies in the field, a substantial safety margin must be incorporated into the commercial vaccination protocol. We recommend a minimum field dose of no < 2 × 105 TCID50 per bird. This also highlights the importance of developing thermostable lyophilised formulations to maintain vaccine potency and companion on‐site quality control tools (e.g., rapid test cards) to ensure adequate vaccine delivery to each duckling. Another problem that may arise in clinical application is that commercial duck flocks frequently carry maternally derived antibodies (MDA) against DEV and/or parvoviruses. The presence of MDA represents a potential confounding factor for live vectored vaccines, as they may neutralise the vaccine virus during initial replication or interfere with antigen presentation. This is an urgent subject for investigation. Future studies will employ commercial ducklings with defined MDA titers to evaluate whether vaccine efficacy is significantly compromised, and to explore strategies such as increasing the immunisation dose or employing mucosal routes of delivery to circumvent MDA interference.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study successfully constructed a recombinant duck enteritis virus (DEV) designated rNGPV VP2‐Cre, which harbours the novel goose parvovirus (NGPV) VP2 gene. The expressed VP2 protein was capable of self‐assembling into virus‐like particles. Additionally, the recombinant virus rNGPV VP2‐Cre demonstrated favourable safety profiles in ducklings, facilitated efficient clearance of NGPV and conferred complete protection against challenge with a highly virulent DEV strain. Collectively, these findings indicate that rNGPV VP2‐Cre serves as a promising bivalent vaccine candidate for the concurrent prevention and control of both DEV and NGPV infections in waterfowl.
