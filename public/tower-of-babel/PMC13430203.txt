SCLC humanized mice identify T cell infiltration phenotypes in response to combination immune-radiation therapies
Authors: Bell Xi Wu, Xiaozhuo Ran, Olivia Huang, Lifang Song, Vivek Philip, Adrian Sacher, Ming-Sound Tsao, Benjamin H Lok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMolecular small cell lung cancer (SCLC) subtypes (ASCL1, NEUROD1, POU2F3, and inflamed) display distinct immune contextures, but preclinical modeling remains limited. We evaluated peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) humanized mice (hu-mice) as a platform to capture subtype-specific tumor-immune interactions and therapeutic sensitivity to the triplet regimen: AZD1390 (ATM inhibitor), radiotherapy (RT), and durvalumab (aPDL1). SCLC cell lines representing all molecular subtypes were engrafted into hu-mice, and while T cell infiltration varied across subtypes, SCLC-inflamed cell lines exhibited the highest levels of infiltration. Infiltrating CD8+ T cells rapidly acquired exhaustion markers PD1, CD39, and TOX. Although aPDL1 monotherapy achieved tumor control only in the inflamed SBC5 cell line, combination AZD1390 and RT upregulated chemokine expression (CCL5 and CXCL10) and increased surface PDL1 to sensitize previously non-responsive xenografts in vivo. Responding xenografts showed enrichment of CD39+CD103+ tumor-reactive T cells with reduced terminal exhaustion (TCF1−TOX+). Overall, PBMC hu-mice may effectively recapitulate SCLC immune contextures and guide development of combinatorial strategies to overcome aPDL1 resistance.Keywords: DNA damage repair inhibition, radiosensitizers, combination immunotherapy, small cell lung cancer, humanized mouse models

Introduction

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a deadly form of lung cancer with poor prognosis.1 Despite high initial response to chemotherapy with selective ionizing radiation (IR), many patients inevitably relapse.2 Advances in immune-oncology (IO) have led to the approval of anti-PDL1 (aPDL1) immune checkpoint inhibitors with durable response in select SCLC patients. However, many patients still fail to benefit, with modest improvements of 2–3 months in overall survival, as reported in IMpower133 (placebo: 10.3 vs. atezolizumab: 12.3 months) and CASPIAN (placebo: 10.3 months vs. durvalumab: 13 months) phase 3 trials.3,4 As such, further strategies to identify responsive and transform nonresponsive phenotypes are needed to improve IO efficacy.

Novel radiosensitizers present a growing strategy to improve IO outcomes through induction of innate signaling mechanisms.5 DNA damage repair proteins such as PARP and ATR effectively control SCLC tumor growth preclinically, especially in combination with aPDL1.6,7,8 Similarly, ATM inhibition (ATMi) is tolerable in multiple advanced solid cancer phase 1 trials (NCT05678010, NCT04882917, and NCT02588105) with preclinical potential as a novel immunogenic radiosensitizer.9,10

Transcriptomic profiling of SCLC has identified subtypes based on expression of transcription factors (TFs) including ASCL1, NEUROD1, and POU2F3.11 A fourth “inflamed” subtype, lacking expression of all three TFs, has been associated with upregulation of inflammatory genes.12 Although incredible progress has been made in the development of SCLC syngeneic models, they fail to recapitulate the inflammatory tumor microenvironment (TME) seen within neuroendocrine-low subtypes (NE-low: POU2F3, inflamed) and are limited to neuroendocrine-high (NE-high: ASCL1, NEUROD1) models.13,14

PBMC humanized mice (hu-mice) can serve as an alternative platform to probe novel therapies in a human immune setting and understand the complex crosstalk between the cancer and engrafted immune cells.15,16,17,18 Despite its early development and increased adoption, PBMC hu-mice tumor immunity remains mostly uncharacterized.

Here, we extensively characterized SCLC cell-line-engrafted PBMC hu-mice and observed notable differences between splenic and infiltrating T cells. Infiltrating T cells between NE-high are phenotypically different from those within NE-low subtypes. Treatment with aPDL1 monotherapy and combination ATMi+IR+aPDL1 therapy resulted in unique cell-line-dependent responses and with changes in exhaustion and activation markers both within the spleen and tumor. Furthermore, combination treatment in responsive models led to an increase in tumor-specific CD8 T cells, as denoted by CD39 and CD103 expression and markedly decreased terminal exhaustion (TCF1−TOX+).19 Our data demonstrate the potential for PBMC hu-mice to accessibly probe infiltration kinetics within various SCLC subtypes and identify novel combination treatments capable of invigorating non-responsive tumors.

Results

PBMC hu-mice models T cell infiltration concordant with transcriptomically defined SCLC immunophenotypes

PBMC hu-mice are generated with a 2 Gy whole-body IR dose and subsequent i.v. injection of three million PBMCs into immunodeficient NSG mice. Following humanization, selected SCLC cell lines are subcutaneously engrafted (Figure 1A). Proportion of human CD45 (hCD45) of total CD45 was measured via flow cytometry from weekly tail vein bleeds. hCD45 was detected as early as week 1, with humanization achieved once 1% of peripheral CD45 composition was human to around 50% at week 3 onwards (Figure 1B). Further characterization confirmed the majority of engrafted human immune cells to be CD3+ T cells with an even distribution between CD4+ and CD8+ subsets, as previously seen in other PBMC hu-mice (Figure 1C).20 Expression of markers CD45RA and CCR7 further categorized T cells into memory (CD45RA−CCR7+), naive (CD45RA+CCR7+), effector memory re-expressing CD45RA (TEMRA; CD45RA+CCR7-), and effector memory (CD45RA−CCR7-; Figure S1A, left). Splenocyte analysis at hu-mice endpoints showed no CD127 expression in CD8 T cells, suggesting no full differentiation toward a memory phenotype (Figure S1A, right).

PBMC hu-mice ultimately succumb to graft-versus-host disease due to allograft interactions between engrafted human PBMCs and the murine host.22 Median survival of mice at the experimental start age of 7–8 weeks was 35 days (Figure S1B). As such, cell-line xenografts were collected at this endpoint for immune profiling. Interestingly, immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining of CD3+ T cells revealed PBMC hu-mice to model a spectrum of immunophenotypes depending on engrafted SCLC cell lines (Figure 1D). Subsequent quantification of IHC slides and flow cytometry analysis of percent CD3+/live cells across engrafted SCLC cell lines showed varying levels of T cell tumor infiltration (TINF) (Figures 1E and 1F). Of note, the SCLC-inflamed/triple-negative cell lines (H841, SBC5, and DMS114) had the highest T cell infiltration (30%–40%), thus biologically validating previous transcriptomic predictions of SCLC-I subtypes to be the most inflamed.12 However, recent studies have reported that SCLC-I-labelled cell lines may be SMARCA4-deficient pulmonary cancers rather than true SCLC, highlighting a potential limitation of this analysis.23 A heatmap of antigen presentation machinery (APM) genes adapted from Nabet et al.21 revealed similar patterns with NE-low (I, P) subtypes to have the highest transcriptomic abundance (Figure 1G). Surface flow staining of HLA class I expression showed a similar trend with high median fluorescence intensity (MFI) in NE-low cell lines, with the exception of SHP77, while cell lines such as H82 and H446, lacking strong APM transcriptional signatures, expressed no surface HLA (Figure 1H). Our results confirm a moderate correlation (R2 = 0.30) that was previously observed between HLA expression and T cell infiltration in SCLC (Figure S1C).24 Tumor volume plots of immunodeficient (NSG) vs. humanized xenografts also revealed the differences in tumor growth within NE-low SCLC cell lines (Figures S1D and S1E). Interestingly, SHP77 engrafted in humanized mice grew significantly faster than within NSG mice, possibly explained by “pseudo-progression,” given the high proportion of T cell infiltrate found within the tumor, with limited anti-tumor cytotoxicity. However, due to potential HLA mismatch in some cell lines, it remains unclear whether the difference is driven by truly anti-cancer response or an allograft effect (Table S1).

To address potential HLA mismatch causing allograft interactions between donor PBMCs and SCLC cell lines, we constructed genetic CRISPR knockouts (KOs) of B2M, a key subunit of the HLA class I complex, and saw complete HLA surface abrogation (Figure 1I). Control (sgLacZ) and KO (sgB2M) cell lines were subsequently engrafted in opposing flanks of hu-mice to control for potential differences in T cell clones (Figure 1J). Interestingly, KO of HLA did not lead to any significant changes in TINF in H841 (Figure 1K, left), SBC5 (Figure 1K, middle), and SHP77 (Figure 1K, right), suggesting that HLA mismatch allograft interactions are not responsible for the TINF observed in hu-mice. No significant difference in tumor growth between sgLacZ vs. sgB2M was observed, likely due to a lack of CD8 recognition after HLA loss (Figure S1F). Moreover, infiltrating CD8 T cell phenotypes denoted by markers PD1, TIM3, CD103, and CD39 did not differ significantly between control vs. B2M-KO xenografts (Figure S1G).

T cells are uniquely influenced by the tumor microenvironment

Having established the lack of allogenic influence in T cell infiltration phenotype and kinetics within PBMC hu-mice, we sought to further probe CD8 phenotypes through an activation/exhaustion panel (Table S2) and CD4 phenotype through a cytokine panel (Table S3). At the endpoint, spleen, skin, and TINF samples were collected (Figure 2A). To first understand whether subcutaneous skin microenvironment could confound TINF results, we probed for differences between paired skin and spleen samples. No significant differences were observed between CD8 T cell markers (Figure 2B, left). Scatterplot revealed a strong correlation (R2 = 0.78) between CD8 phenotypes within the two anatomical sites (Figure 2B, right).

Next, we compared splenic T cells with infiltrating T cells with gating strategy for markers shown in Figures S2A and S2B. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering shows distinct CD4 and CD8 T cell phenotypes between the spleen and TINF (Figure 2C). Cell lines were further labeled on the basis of the expression of NE TFs and infiltration status described in Figure 1D. Boxplot of exhaustion markers (PD1, CD39, and TOX) showed significantly higher expression in tumor infiltrating CD8 T cells, while activation markers (Ki67, IFNG, and TCF1) were significantly lower in comparison with splenic CD8 T cells (Figure 2D). Gating strategy for inactive CD4 T cells not expressing any cytokines (cytokine-neg) is shown in Figure S2C. The clear dichotomous expression of markers indicates that T cells are more active within the spleen and once infiltrated into the TME, they become exhausted, suggesting that the TME induces exhaustion that can be recapitulated within PBMC hu-mice.

Since SCLC has been transcriptomically categorized into subtypes on the basis of the expression of NE TFs, we sought to further segregate our cell lines into NE-low (SBC5 and H841) and NE-high (SHP77, H69, H446, and H1694). Principal-component analysis (PCA) plots show separation between infiltrating CD8, splenic CD8, and infiltrating CD4 T cells, whereas splenic CD4 T cells failed to separate (Figure 2E). This separation suggests that the markers used to probe these T cells can demarcate between SCLC subtypes, further showing the utility of the PBMC hu-mice in capturing subtype-specific tumor-T cell interactions. Subsequent volcano plots to identify T cell markers different between the NE subtypes uncovered infiltrating T cells within NE-high subtypes to possess the capability of secreting a larger variety of cytokines (Figure 2F). Further categorization into Th subtypes (Th1, Th2, and Th17) on the basis of cytokine expression showed similar levels across CD4 splenic T cells but significantly lower proportions of Th1 (p < 0.01) in NE-low cell lines (Figure S2D). Overall, in-depth immune phenotyping of PBMC hu-mice T cells shows that engrafted cell lines have unique effects on the infiltrating T cell behavior.

PBMC hu-mice cell-line xenografts have distinct responses to aPDL1 monotherapy and ATMi+IR combination therapy

Having characterized infiltration profiles in each SCLC cell line, we next evaluated their therapeutic response to aPDL1. As PBMC hu-mice have a relatively short experimental window, humanization was delayed until after cell line implantation and growth to 50 mm3. Lead shields were designed to protect the tumor from a 5 Gy whole-body dose of irradiation before i.v. injection of 3 × 106 PBMCs (Figure 3A). A 5 Gy dose was selected, as it led to faster engraftment (Figure S3A). Treatment was initiated once tumor reached 150 mm3 with aPDL1 administered twice a week i.p. for a total of three cycles or until mouse endpoint (Figure 3B). Tumor growth inhibition (TGI) scores of aPDL1 vs. IgG were calculated for murine syngeneic cell line (KP1) and humanized H82 (hu-H82), hu-H1048, hu-SHP77, and hu-SBC5 xenografts (Figure 3C). Murine aPDL1 (clone 80) was used for KP1 while durvalumab was administered in hu-mice. Hu-SBC5 showed the greatest degree of inhibition (47%), followed by hu-SHP77 (22%), then KP1 (10%), hu-H1048 (6.33%), and hu-H82 (0.32%). Tumor volume plots with t test at plotted days showed only hu-SBC5 to have statistical significance (Figure 3D). Improved tumor control of aPDL1 observed in hu-SBC5 was validated in a separate human donor (D2; Figure S3B).

DNA damage repair inhibition (DDRi) in combination with IR induces aberrant cell cycle progression after mitosis, leading to cGAS micronuclei translocation and STING activation.25 Subsequent transcription of inflammatory genes via cGAS-STING can synergize with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) and improve IO outcomes contributed by the upregulation of chemokines promoting T cell infiltration, such as CXCL10 and CCL5.8,26 Indeed, we observed significant increases in micronuclei formation relative to both ATMi and IR monotherapies upon IR in combination with the ATM inhibitor AZD1390 (ATMi+IR; Figure S3C). Additionally, ATMi+IR led to increased cGAS micronuclei translocation, as detected 48 h post-IR in SBC5 (Figure 3E). Heatmaps of normalized Z scores for CXCL10 and CCL5 transcriptional expression revealed increased levels upon combination ATMi+IR (COM) across multiple cell lines, as detected by qPCR (Figure 3F). cGAS KO in SBC5 and H1048 partially abrogated CCL5 and CXCL10 transcription, respectively (Figures S3D and S3E). Surface PDL1 detection with flow cytometry in treated cell lines displayed increased PDL1 expression relative to monotherapies (Figure 3G). Taken together, our in vitro data suggest synergistic potential of ATMi+IR in combination with aPDL1.

We have previously shown ATMi+IR to be an effective radiosensitizer for SCLC both in vitro and in vivo.9 To understand the effectiveness of a triple combination treatment regimen ATMi+IR+aPDL1, we treated mice concurrently with 20 mg/kg ATMi p.o. 1 h before a 3 Gy dose of IR given in three fractions for a total of 9 Gy (Figure 3H). 10 mg/kg aPDL1 was subsequently administered i.p. twice a week until endpoint or after three cycles. Tumor growth inhibition plots of ATMi+IR+IgG vs. ATMi+IR+aPDL1 at day 14 show the highest level of tumor control in hu-SBC5 (41%) and hu-SHP77 (33%) with minimal improvement in hu-H1048 (2.05%), hu-H82 (2.0%), and KP1 (1.9%; Figure 3I). Interestingly, baseline infiltration correlated with response to therapy as both SBC5 and SHP77 had at least 10% CD3/live at baseline before treatment. Tumor volume plots show significant reduction in tumor growth after ATMi+IR+aPDL1 administration in hu-SHP77 and hu-SBC5 xenografts, while no statistically significant reduction was seen in KP1, hu-H1048, or hu-H82 xenografts (Figure 3J). Reduction and lack of reduction in SBC5 and H82, respectively, was further validated in a second donor (D2; Figures 3K and S3F). Overall, our data suggests that the ATMi+IR can effectively sensitize selective cell lines to aPDL1, and this sensitization may be related to the presence of baseline T cell infiltration within PBMC hu-mice.

Splenic and infiltrating CD8 T cells in cell lines primed to aPDL1 have similar phenotypes

After characterizing tumor growth control across treatment groups in various SCLC cell lines, we sought to determine whether there were differences within the overall T cell population. We first measured total T cell infiltration in IgG-treated tumors relative to aPDL1 and saw no significant changes in infiltration (Figure S4A). Alternatively, IHC staining of CD3+ T cells in ATMi+IR+IgG- and ATMi+IR+aPDL1-treated tumors and subsequent quantification revealed greater infiltration in hu-SBC5 and hu-SHP77, while no significant changes were observed between treatment cohorts in hu-H82 (Figures 4A and S4B). Similar trends were seen with flow cytometry analysis of proportion of infiltrating T cells over all live cells with SBC5, SHP77, and the murine syngeneic cell line KP1 showing a significant increase in infiltration after ATMi+IR+aPDL1 administration (Figure 4B).

Next, to determine whether there were differences in phenotypes, we categorized treatment groups of cell lines into either aPDL1-primed or non-responsive. aPDL1-primed groups included those that could elicit a tumor control response with aPDL1 (SBC5-IgG, SBC5-ATMi+IR, and SHP77-ATMi+IR) and those that responded to aPDL1 (SBC5-aPDL1, SBC5-ATMi+IR+aPDL1, and SHP77-ATMi+IR+aPDL1) as the TME is primed to respond to aPDL1 therapy (Figure 4C). Conversely, non-responsive groups included models that were completely unresponsive in controlling tumor growth (all hu-H82, all hu-H1048, SHP77-IgG, and SHP77-aPDL1; Figure 4C). PCA plots show clustering of non-responsive and aPDL1-primed groups in both the spleen and TINF, suggesting distinct CD8 phenotype between groups (Figure 4D). Supervised heatmap analysis of marker Z scores shows unique expression patterns between aPDL1-primed and non-responsive groups (Figure S4C). Volcano plots of splenic CD8 T cells had increased expression of most markers within the non-responsive group, while within the TINF, cytotoxic markers (granzyme B and perforin) were upregulated and exhaustion markers (PD1, PDL1, and CD39) were also increased (Figure 4E). The high expression of PD1 and PDL1 within infiltrating T cells in the responsive groups offers a potential explanation to aPDL1 sensitivity.

To examine treatment-induced significant changes in T cell surface marker expression over time, SHP77 was selected because it contained treatment cohorts within both the aPDL1-primed and non-responsive groups. Bar plots reveal a trajectory in which treatment ATMi+IR+IgG led to a reduction of the proportion of TOX, CD45RA, and TIM3 within the TINF of hu-SHP77 to the expression levels found in the aPDL1-primed cell line SBC5 (Figure 4F), with the mean marker expression of aPDL1-primed and nonresponsive groups depicted by the dotted lines. By contrast, we observed the opposite pattern with markers CD39 and CD103 as upon treatment with ATMi+IR+IgG, SHP77 showed increased CD39 expression to levels similar to the aPDL1-primed SBC5, while CD103 was downregulated. Our results also suggest a possible systemic shift within the CD8 T cells as change in marker expression was observed within the spleen also, although not all markers followed this shift (Figure 4F, left). Within the TINF, the significant upregulation of CD39 could reflect the increase in infiltrating tumor-reactive T cells, as CD39+CD103+ double positivity is an established marker for this phenotype.19 Indeed, double-positive gating shows that tumor-infiltrating CD8 T cells were significantly increased (Figure 4G, left). Exhaustion is known to occur as a spectrum demarcated by TOX and TCF1 expression.27 Infiltrating CD8 T cells within the aPDL1-primed group appear to be less exhausted, as denoted by a lower TCF1-TOX+ population (Figure 4G, right), with gating shown in Figure S4D. Analysis of SHP77 infiltration within the non-responsive (IgG and aPDL1) and aPDL1-primed (ATMi+IR and ATMi+IR+aPDL1) groups revealed a significant decrease in the terminally exhausted TCF1−TOX+ T cells, but no significant changes in CD103+CD39+ T cells were observed (Figure S4E). Additionally, in vitro analysis of surface MHC class I expression was unchanged after ATMi+IR combination therapy in SCLC murine cell line KP1 and human cell lines H82 and H1048 (Figure S4F). This suggests the increased efficacy observed may be attributed not to upregulation of presentation machinery but rather to changes in neoantigen expression. In summary, treatment with ATMi+IR+IgG was able to sensitize specific cell lines (SHP77) to respond by changing surface expression of CD8 T cells resulting in an overall increase in tumor-reactive CD8 T cells and decreased terminal exhaustion.

Discussion

Here, we demonstrate the utility of the PBMC hu-mice in modeling infiltration kinetics at baseline and upon treatment within SCLC xenografts. SCLC research is limited by the lack of murine syngeneic models that reflect the major biological subtypes defined by the expression of TFs.11 Alternatively, studies of SCLC patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) have yielded valuable insights into SCLC heterogeneity and its relation to treatment resistance.28 Despite this, innovation is still needed to define the influence of novel immune-combination therapies in a preclinical setting. Recent studies have also shown multiple patient cohorts with unique responsiveness to aPDL1+chemo, predicted on the basis of inflammatory and/or neuroendocrine/non-neuroendocrine features that exist within SCLC.21 Infiltration kinetics at the baseline of the NE-high RPM and RPP syngeneic models have been observed to be immune-desert and unresponsive to immune checkpoint monotherapy.8,26 Our data show that the intrinsic properties of the engrafted cell line can influence the T cell infiltration in an HLA-independent manner and as such, contribute to IO response. As such, PBMC hu-mice can function as a companion model to better understand the complex interactions between T cell immunity and the SCLC tumor.

Expression of APM genes has also been suggested to correlate with immune infiltration and subsequent treatment response in SCLC patients.24 Here, we also observed SCLC cell lines with high HLA expression to moderately correlate with immune infiltration. KO of HLA expression did not influence the infiltration kinetics of hu-mice xenografts, suggesting that HLA expression may be a correlate rather than a determinant of T cell infiltration, highlighting a potential transcriptional repression mechanism that controls both APM and inflammatory genes in SCLC cell lines. However, there were exceptions, as not all models with high HLA expression showed increased T cell infiltration as shown within the SCLC-P cell lines. Since treatment-responsive xenografts possessed both baseline infiltration and surface HLA and PDL1 expression, treatment responsiveness of PBMC hu-mice may demonstrate the classical hallmarks of immunotherapy response, but it is important to note that CD8 T cells within the PBMC hu-mice may represent a hyperactive state not typically observed in patients.

Radiotherapy is selectively administered to SCLC patients, and combination therapy with immunogenic radiosensitizers plus ICB has potential for improved tumor control. AZD1390 is an orally available ATM inhibitor with penetration through the blood-brain barrier (BBB).29 BBB penetrance can be valuable for SCLC due to its frequent occurrence of brain metastases.1 We and others have previously shown ATM inhibitors to be effective radiosensitizers in SCLC, glioblastoma, and CNS-metastasized breast cancer.9,30,31 Additionally, the ubiquitous loss of p53 activity in SCLC can result in improved outcomes in combination with ATMi. Our data show combination ATMi+IR can lead to global increase in inflammation, as defined by chemokine expression levels across multiple cell-lines and result in systemic changes in CD8 T cell phenotypes. The induced damage could lead to increased neoantigen release rather than increased presentation, as observed in the increased total tumor-reactive CD39+CD103+ infiltrating T cells in our responsive hu-mice models and lack of increased MHC class I expression. Furthermore, decreased CD45RA surface expression was observed both peripherally and intratumourally, suggesting that T cells within hu-mice are increasingly exposed to their cognate antigen upon combination treatment, suggesting CD45RA as a candidate peripheral biomarker of immune-radiation combination, though prospective validation is required. Additionally, due to the allograft limitations of incomplete HLA matching between donor PBMCs and cell line MHC class I, the anti-tumor efficacy observed across SCLC remains difficult to fully interpret.

PBMC hu-mice offer promise as an alternative model, yet are constrained by a short experimental duration and allograft interactions. Nonetheless, these limitations could be mitigated through donor matching and increased replicates across multiple donors. Furthermore, as the role of tumor-associated macrophages within the TME becomes increasingly recognized, PBMC hu-mice are limited in modeling macrophage-T cell interactions due to the absence of a detectable CD3− population. Despite this, PBMC hu-mice can still explore the intrinsic properties of SCLC cell-lines and their interactions with T cells to ultimately corroborate transcriptomic findings, as demonstrated by the distinctive T cell infiltration and ICB response patterns that emerged in engrafted cell lines.

Limitations of the study

Our study is limited by the lack of complete donor-matching to engrafted cell line xenografts, potentially confounding interpretability of results. Additionally, the hyperactive peripheral PBMC component resulting in graft-versus-host-disease may not be reflective of normal immune physiology. Future work aims to donor-match PBMCs from SCLC patient donors with circulating tumor-derived xenografts generated in-house to further validate tested combination therapies.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell-lines and reagents

Human SCLC cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; H841, DMS114, H1048, H526, SHP77, H69, H82, H446, H1694). SBC5 cell line was gifted from Dr. Kazuhiro Yasufuku lab (University Health Network). SBC5, H526, SHP77, H69, and H446 cell-lines were maintained with RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. H841, H1048, and H1694 cells were cultured with HITES medium supplemented with 5% FBS. DMS114 cells were maintained with Waymouth’s MB 752/1 media supplemented with 10% FBS. Murine SCLC p53−/− Rb1−/−p130−/− cell line KP1 was gifted from Dr. Julien Sage and cultured with RPMI1640 medium supplemented with 10% BGS.32 (The source of cells is listed in the key resources table). Cell lines were authenticated via STR profiling with 100% match to supplier’s reference. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and confirmed negative.

AZD1390 was diluted in vehicle suspension formulated with 0.5% hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) + 0.1% (v/v) Tween 80 in double-distilled H2O.29 Anti-murine PD-L1 antibody (Clone 80, mIgG1 D265A, AstraZeneca), anti-human PD-L1 antibody (Durvalumab, AstraZeneca) and IgG (Bio X Cell) were diluted in PBS.

PBMC mice humanization studies

Blood samples were obtained from healthy donors following informed consent according to approved protocol by the University Health Network (REB #22–5423). Blood was collected in 10 mL EDTA tubes (BD Vacutainer).

Animal studies were conducted following the approved protocols by the Animal Care Committee (Animal Use Protocol #6091) at the Princess Margaret Cancer Center (Toronto, ON, Canada). The animal holding room is specific-pathogen free and was maintained at 21–22°C with relative humidity between 40% and 60% and a 12–12h light and dark photoperiod. The experimental and control mice were co-housed. To generate PBMC humanized mice, 8–10-week-old NSG (NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ; Jackson Laboratory; JAX005557) female mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory and given an initial 2 Gy whole-body radiation dose (GC40 irradiator). 3x106 density-gradient isolated PBMCs from a healthy donor were injected intravenously via tail vein 1h after irradiation. On the second day, 2.5x106 SCLC cells mixed with Matrigel in a 1:1 ratio at a final volume of 50uL were engrafted subcutaneously into mouse flanks. Tumors were measured using digital caliper and tumor volumes were calculated using the formula (lw2)/2, where l is length and w is width of the tumor. Animals’ tumor volumes were measured twice a week, and GvHD symptoms of weight, posture, activity, fur and skin were monitored weekly. Mice were humanely euthanized with CO2 when tumors reached the total volumetric endpoint of 1500 mm3 or when GvHD cumulative score reached 5.

Alternatively, PBMC humanized mice used for drug studies were generated from 8 to 10-week-old female NRG (NOD-Rag1null IL2rgnull; JAX007799) mice. 1–2 weeks after engraftment, subcutaneous tumors were shielded with lead and given whole-body radiation dose of 5 Gy (XRAD320). After engraftment and tumor size of 150-200 mm3, mice were randomized to respective tumor groups: IgG, anti-PD-L1 (PDL1), AZD1390 plus radiation (ATMi+IR), and ATMi+IR with anti-PDL1 (ATMi+IR+aPDL1). No specific inclusion and exclusion criteria were set. Tumor measurements were not blinded but confounders were minimized through selection of a single researcher for both tumor measurements and animal treatment.

In ATMi cohorts, 20 mg/kg AZD1390 was administered five times a week until previously described volumetric or GvHD endpoints. For combination therapy arms, mice were anesthetized using 2–5% isoflurane and tumors were irradiated once daily with 3 Gy for 3 fractions using XRAD320 X-ray irradiation (Precision X-ray). The mice were restrained in a custom-built lead shield, and tumors were irradiated from day 1–3 after randomization. AZD1390 was administered 1 h prior to IR. For aPDL1 administration, mice were dosed with 200ug of aPDL1 intraperitoneally twice a week for three cycles or until GvHD symptoms reached a cumulative score of 3.

Syngeneic in vivo studies

Syngeneic murine experiments were performed in female B6129F1/Tac (Taconic Bioscience) mice that were 6–8 weeks old at the time of KP1 cell implantation. The animal holding room is specific-pathogen free and was maintained at 21–22°C with relative humidity between 40% and 60% and a 12–12h light and dark photoperiod. The experimental and control mice were co-housed. Tumor volumes were calculated from digital caliper measurements using the formula volume = (lw2)/2. Mice were randomized into six treatment groups: vehicle, radiation, ATMi+IR, aPDL1, aPDL1 plus IR, and ATMi+IR+aPDL1 at tumor volumes of ∼100–150 mm3 for treatment efficacy experiments. No specific inclusion and exclusion criteria were set. For immune profiling, mice were randomized into treatment groups at tumor volumes of 200–250 mm3. Treatment scheduling follows the same protocol as PBMC humanized mice. Mice were humanely euthanized once tumor reached volumetric endpoint of 1500 mm3.

Method details

Immune profiling

To assess peripheral engraftment, murine blood was collected via tail vein bleeds once a week and analyzed using flow cytometry staining for murine CD45+ and human CD45+ cells.

To generate single-cell suspensions, tumors were harvested at day 35. Tumor volumes were different sizes depending on the cell-line at the time of collection (see Figure S1D for approximate size but note that tumor volume experiments were conducted separately from profiling experiments). All tumors isolated were included in profiling. Tumors were excised and divided into 1 × 1 mm pieces. The tumor homogenate was incubated with a digestion medium composed of RPMI1640 + DNase I (0.1 mg/mL) + collagenase IV (1 mg/mL) at 37°C for 30 min and mechanically passed through a strainer. Similarly, splenocytes were excised and divided into 1 × 1 mm pieces and mechanically passed through strainer. Homogenate was spun down at 300xG for 6min and subsequently incubated with red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer for 10 min. The cells were washed with PBS and resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS +2% FBS) for further analysis.

For cell staining, single cell suspensions were incubated with mixture of fixed viability dye FVS575v (BD 565694) and BD anti-human Fc block (BD 564219) for 25 min at room temperature. After centrifugation, supernatant was removed and cells were stained with primary antibody conjugated with fluorescence according to the standard protocol at 4°C, avoiding light (All panels and antibodies are listed in the Tables S2 and S3). Cells were washed with staining buffer twice and fixed with BD fixation buffer at 4°C for 10 min without light. The fixation buffer was removed, and cells were resuspended in staining buffer for flow cytometry (Symphony A3, BD) analysis until a minimum of 2500 human CD3+ cells were collected per sample with the resultant data analyzed by FlowJo v10. For analysis, dead and murine cells were gated out before profiling of T cell proportions and immunophenotypes.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates 24 h prior to irradiation and treated with AZD1390 2 h prior to 2–6 Gy dose of radiation. At selected timepoints after irradiation, cells were washed with PBS twice and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. After the fixation, cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 2 min and blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min. Subsequently, cells were blotted with primary antibody (anti-cGAS, Cell signaling technology 15102S) for 1 h and anti-rabbit secondary antibody conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 for 30 min. Nuclear DNA was stained with DAPI for 10 min. Images were captured by Leica SP8 confocal microscope and the number of cGAS+ micronuclei and primary nuclei were quantified with ImageJ. (The source of antibodies is listed in the key resources table).

Generation of CRISPR knockout cell-lines

The design of sgRNA targeting cGAS or B2M was performed using public software CRISPICK or CRISPOR (primers are listed in key resources table). Guide pairs were synthesized, annealed, and inserted into either lentiCRISPRv2 backbone or pSpCas9(BB)-2a-GFP backbone (addgene #48138) and then validated by sequencing. The HEK293T cells were transfected with constructed sgRNA plasmids and packaging plasmids and the supernatant containing lentiviral particles was collected after 48 h. SBC5, H1048, SHP77, and H841 cells were transduced with the lentiviral particles and underwent puromycin selection or fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS).

Western blot

Cell lysates were separated on 10–15% SDS–polyacrylamide gels with Laemmli buffer and Tricine buffer system. The proteins were then transferred onto the nitrocellulose membrane or PVDF membrane at 250 mA for 2 h or 100 mA for 8–12 h at 4°C. The membrane was incubated with blocking buffer (5% BSA or milk in TBST) for 1 h at room temperature followed by incubation with respective antibodies (key resources table) at 4°C overnight. After washing with TBST three times, the membrane was incubated with IRDye goat anti-rabbit 800CW and goat anti-mouse 680RD secondary antibodies (LI-COR Biosciences) or HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit or mouse antibody. Images were acquired using the Odyssey Clx infrared imaging system (LI-COR) or Chemidoc system (BIO-RAD) or iBright FL1500 and bands were quantified and normalized using ImageJ.

RNA isolation, reverse transcription and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

The total RNA was isolated using the High Pure RNA Isolation Kit (Roche Life Science, cat#11828665001) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was measured by NanoDrop 2000 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific).

Reverse transcription reactions were carried out using SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis SuperMix (Invitrogen, cat# 18080-400) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The obtained cDNA was diluted with H2O and used for qPCR. Gene-specific primers were used for PCR amplification and detection (primers listed in key resources table). The relative gene expression was normalized by the level of human GAPDH or murine Gapdh and calculated with RQ = 2−△△Ct.

Donor matching from isolated PBMC RNA and cell-lines was achieved using LinkSeq LinkSēq HLA-ABCDRDQDP SABR 384 Kit (ThermoFisher) following manufacturer instructions.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Tumor growth inhibition analysis

Tumor growth inhibition was calculated by comparing the average tumor volume (TV) of the treatment group with that of the control group at the timepoint.

Kaplan-Meier analysis

The time to reach a volumetric endpoint of 1500 mm3 was defined as the event. Log rank tests were used to evaluate differences between groups.

Statistical testing

All statistical analyses were performed with two-tailed unpaired t test or one-way ANOVA with data assumed parametric, not formally tested, or Wilcoxon rank-sum test with data assumed non-parametric, not formally tested in Graphpad Prism 10 or with R 4.4.0. Each data point is represented as an individual sample of tumour infiltration, spleen, or mouse. Abbreviations for significance are as follows: ns, not significant; p: ∗<0.05, ∗∗<0.01, ∗∗∗<0.001, ∗∗∗∗<0.0001.
