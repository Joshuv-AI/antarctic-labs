Letter to the Editor from Dandinoğlu: “Aggressive macroprolactinoma refractory to multiple lines of localized and systemic therapy”
Authors: Taner Dandinoğlu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor

I read with interest the recent case report by Konindala et al describing the therapeutic complexity of aggressive macroprolactinoma refractory to multiple lines of localized and systemic therapy. The report provides a valuable illustration of the challenges posed by refractory prolactinoma, including resistance to dopamine agonists, repeated local interventions, progressive mass effect, and the need for sustained multidisciplinary management [1].

I would like to highlight another clinically relevant and often underrecognized aspect of prolactinomas, particularly in male patients: skeletal morbidity. In the presented case, the patient had a prolonged disease course and developed central hypothyroidism, secondary adrenal insufficiency, and ultimately panhypopituitarism requiring hormone replacement; however, bone health evaluation was not reported. Given the recognized adverse effects of hyperprolactinemia, hypogonadism, and chronic pituitary hormone disturbances on bone metabolism, assessment of bone mineral density and fracture history may provide clinically relevant complementary information in such patients [2].

This point may be especially relevant in an older male patient, in whom baseline skeletal fragility may already be a concern. In this setting, failure to recognize bone involvement may carry important clinical consequences, as fragility fractures may further contribute to morbidity, functional decline, and loss of independence, particularly in older men [3]. Severe osteoporosis and multiple fragility fractures, including vertebral and hip fractures, have also been reported in male patients with prolactinoma, underscoring that delayed recognition of prolactinoma-related endocrine disturbance may lead to substantial skeletal burden [4].

I therefore believe that the clinical evaluation of patients with prolactinoma should extend beyond prolactin levels, radiologic progression, and mass effect alone. When clinically appropriate, a structured assessment of bone health, including fracture history and bone mineral density, may help identify an underrecognized source of long-term morbidity, particularly in men with prolonged disease duration and multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies.
