Synchronous primary breast invasive carcinoma and breast metastasis from high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma: a case report and literature review
Authors: Jingge Miao, Nianqiu Liu, Fuhong Gong, Jiayi Chen, Chenxi Wang, Mengping Lin, Na Li, Yilin Chen, Fei Ge, Wenlin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast metastasis from high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma is rare, and its coexistence with a synchronous primary breast carcinoma is exceptionally uncommon. Distinguishing metastatic ovarian carcinoma from primary breast carcinoma is clinically important because treatment strategies and prognosis differ substantially.Case presentationWe report a 48-year-old premenopausal woman who presented with pleural effusion, abdominal distension, bilateral adnexal masses, multiple breast nodules, and systemic lymphadenopathy. Pathological examination of the pelvic lesion supported high-grade serous carcinoma, whereas breast biopsy demonstrated two morphologically distinct adenocarcinoma components. Immunohistochemical profiling, including breast-lineage markers and Müllerian/ovarian markers, supported the coexistence of primary breast invasive carcinoma and metastatic ovarian carcinoma involving the breast.Intervention and outcomeThe patient was diagnosed with FIGO stage IVB high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma with breast, pleural, peritoneal, and lymph node involvement, synchronous with primary breast carcinoma. She received neoadjuvant paclitaxel plus carboplatin and achieved a partial response, followed by interval cytoreductive surgery, additional chemotherapy combined with bevacizumab, breast tumor excision, and maintenance therapy with fluzoparib plus bevacizumab. At the last follow-up on December 20, 2025, no evidence of disease progression was observed.ConclusionThis case highlights the diagnostic difficulty of breast lesions in patients with ovarian carcinoma. Morphology combined with an appropriate immunohistochemical panel is essential for distinguishing primary breast carcinoma from metastatic ovarian carcinoma. Multidisciplinary evaluation and genetic assessment should be considered in similar cases.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy among women worldwide and remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in this population (1). While primary breast cancers predominate, metastatic breast tumors are rare and typically originate from contralateral breast lesions. Metastases to the breast from extramammary solid tumors account for only 0.2%–1.1% of all cases (2).

Ovarian cancer—though not the most prevalent gynecological malignancy—is characterized by high aggressiveness, invasiveness, and recurrence rates. It carries the highest case-fatality rate among female reproductive tract cancers (3, 4), largely due to late detection when distant metastases are already present. Common metastatic sites include the liver (37.5%), lymph nodes (29.4%), lung (28.4%), bone (3.7%), and brain (1%) (5). In contrast, metastasis to the breast from ovarian cancer is exceedingly rare; such cases constitute just 0.03%–0.6% of all malignant breast neoplasms (6).

The coexistence of primary breast carcinoma and ovarian carcinoma metastasis to the breast creates a major diagnostic challenge. We present this case to highlight the value of integrating morphology, immunohistochemistry, imaging, and multidisciplinary assessment.

Case presentation

Patient information and clinical findings

A 48-year-old premenopausal woman presented with pleural effusion in February 2024. She had a 16-year history of smoking and no family history of breast or ovarian cancer. Initial thoracoscopic pleurectomy and pleural biopsy at an outside hospital revealed metastatic high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC).

On admission to our hospital in April 2024, physical examination revealed a firm mass in the left breast (~1.5 × 1.0 cm) and palpable bilateral axillary lymph nodes. Gynecologic examination revealed bilateral adnexal masses (left 12.7 × 8.4 cm, right 6.9 × 4.8 cm).

Ultrasound on April 3, 2024 demonstrated multiple hypoechoic nodules in both breasts and axillary regions (Figure 1). CT scan on April 15, 2024 revealed bilateral adnexal masses with diffuse pleural and peritoneal nodules, omental thickening, hepatic capsular lesions, and multiple enlarged lymph nodes (Figure 2).

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry

Pelvic biopsy on April 11, 2024 revealed high-grade serous adenocarcinoma with WT1+, PAX8+, CA125+, and Ki67 80% (Figures 3A–D). Left breast biopsy on the same day demonstrated two distinct components: primary breast invasive carcinoma (MG+, GATA3+, ER 5%, PR 50%, HER2 1+, Ki67 70%; Figures 4A–D) and metastatic Müllerian carcinoma (WT1+, PAX8+, CA125+). HE staining revealed two morphologically distinct adenocarcinoma components coexisting in the same lesion (Figure 5). Follow-up pelvic IHC confirmed ovarian origin of the metastatic component (Figure 6).

Molecular testing

Molecular testing revealed a pathogenic BRCA mutation and homologous recombination deficiency (HRD)-positive status. These findings supported the use of PARP inhibitor maintenance therapy after platinum-based chemotherapy and suggested a possible homologous recombination repair-deficient background in this patient.

Literature review

Search strategy and selection criteria

A focused literature search was conducted in PubMed to identify previously reported cases of ovarian, fallopian tube, or primary peritoneal carcinoma involving the breast and/or axillary lymph nodes. The search included all publications available from database inception to May 2026. The reference lists of relevant articles were also manually reviewed to identify additional eligible reports.

Articles were included if they met the following criteria: (1) the primary tumor was pathologically diagnosed as ovarian carcinoma, fallopian tube carcinoma, or primary peritoneal carcinoma; (2) breast involvement and/or axillary lymph node metastasis was clinically, radiologically, cytologically, or pathologically documented; and (3) sufficient clinicopathological information was available, including tumor histology, site of involvement, immunohistochemical findings, treatment, or clinical outcome. Articles were excluded if the reported breast lesion represented primary breast carcinoma without evidence of ovarian or Müllerian origin, if pathological or cytological confirmation was unavailable, or if essential clinical information was insufficient for analysis.

Summary of previously reported cases

The available literature confirms that metastasis of ovarian or Müllerian-origin carcinoma to the breast region is rare. Reported cases most commonly involved serous carcinoma, particularly high-grade serous ovarian or primary peritoneal carcinoma. The breast and/or axillary lesions were identified either at the initial diagnosis of ovarian carcinoma or during recurrence after prior treatment. Clinically and radiologically, these lesions often resembled primary breast carcinoma or breast cancer-associated nodal disease, making pathological confirmation essential.

Immunohistochemistry plays a central role in determining tumor origin. Müllerian markers, including PAX8, WT1, and CA125, are commonly used to support ovarian, fallopian tube, or primary peritoneal origin. In contrast, breast-lineage markers, including GATA3, mammaglobin, GCDFP-15, ER, and PR, are useful for supporting primary breast origin. Because individual markers may show overlapping or focal expression, interpretation should be based on a combined panel together with tumor morphology, imaging findings, and clinical history.

Most previously published reports described metastatic ovarian or primary peritoneal carcinoma involving the breast, axillary lymph nodes, or other supradiaphragmatic lymph nodes, without evidence of a synchronous primary breast carcinoma. In contrast, the present case is unusual because the breast biopsy revealed two morphologically distinct adenocarcinoma components: one compatible with primary breast invasive carcinoma and another morphologically discordant component showing Müllerian differentiation in the clinical context of advanced high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma. This coexistence creates a major diagnostic challenge and highlights the importance of integrating histopathology, immunohistochemistry, imaging, and clinical context when evaluating breast lesions in patients with ovarian carcinoma.

Discussion

The present case represents a rare and diagnostically challenging scenario in which primary breast invasive carcinoma coexisted with a morphologically distinct Müllerian-type carcinoma component in the breast in a patient with advanced high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma. As summarized in our literature review, metastasis of ovarian or primary peritoneal serous carcinoma to the breast and/or axillary lymph nodes has been reported only sporadically. These lesions may closely mimic primary breast carcinoma on clinical examination and imaging, leading to potential diagnostic pitfalls. The current case further expands this diagnostic spectrum because the breast biopsy revealed two distinct adenocarcinoma components, one compatible with primary breast invasive carcinoma and the other showing features suggestive of metastatic Müllerian/ovarian origin. This finding highlights the importance of careful morphological assessment combined with an appropriate immunohistochemical panel when evaluating breast lesions in patients with ovarian carcinoma.

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide and remains a major cause of cancer-related mortality (1). Although most malignant breast lesions represent primary breast carcinoma, metastatic tumors to the breast from extramammary malignancies are uncommon, accounting for less than 1% of all breast malignancies when contralateral breast cancer and lymphoma are excluded (2, 7). Among gynecologic malignancies, ovarian carcinoma metastasis to the breast or axillary lymph nodes is particularly rare, but it represents an important diagnostic pitfall because it may clinically and radiologically mimic primary breast carcinoma (8). Accurate distinction between primary breast cancer and metastatic ovarian carcinoma is essential, as these entities differ substantially in therapeutic strategy, surgical decision-making, and prognosis (7, 8).

High-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSC) is the most common and aggressive histological subtype of epithelial ovarian cancer, which accounts for approximately 70%–80% of epithelial ovarian cancers (9–11). Globally, ovarian cancer was responsible for 3.7% of new cancer cases and 4.7% of cancer-related deaths among women in 2020 (12), with the majority of patients diagnosed at an advanced stage when peritoneal dissemination or distant metastasis has already occurred (12). HGSC is characterized by rapid progression, marked genomic instability, and a strong propensity for transcoelomic, lymphatic, and hematogenous spread, most frequently to the liver, lymph nodes, lung, bone, and brain (5). In contrast, metastasis to the breast is exceedingly rare, representing less than 0.6% of malignant breast tumors (6).

Several risk factors have been associated with HGSC, including age over 60 years, a family history of breast or ovarian cancer, and infertility, whereas multiparity, breastfeeding, oral contraceptive use, late menarche, and early menopause are considered protective (13, 14). Notably, some of these reproductive and hormonal factors also influence breast cancer risk, reflecting shared etiologic pathways between ovarian and breast malignancies. Approximately 20% of ovarian cancers are hereditary, predominantly linked to germline mutations in BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes (15). BRCA1 mutation carriers have a lifetime ovarian cancer risk of 44–61%, whereas BRCA2 carriers have a risk of up to 24% (15). These mutations also confer familial risk of breast cancer, accounting for approximately 5–10% of cases, with cumulative risk by age 70 reaching ~65% for BRCA1 and ~47% for BRCA2 carriers (16, 17). Identification of these hereditary risk factors is clinically important, as it informs genetic counseling, surveillance strategies, and preventive interventions such as prophylactic salpingo-oophorectomy.

Together, these epidemiologic and genetic characteristics underscore why HGSC frequently presents at an advanced stage with widespread dissemination, and why rare metastatic sites—such as the breast and axillary lymph nodes—can occasionally be involved, creating diagnostic challenges when breast lesions are evaluated in patients with a history or suspicion of ovarian carcinoma.

Genetically driven pathogenesis along an “ovarian–breast axis” may underlie both primary and metastatic processes involving the breast. High-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSC) exhibits pervasive TP53 alterations, present in approximately 96% of cases according to TCGA data, while BRCA1/2 germline mutations occur in roughly 9% of patients (18–20). These shared genetic aberrations suggest overlapping molecular mechanisms between HGSC and breast neoplasia, emphasizing the clinical relevance of TP53- and BRCA-driven pathways.

Metastasis of ovarian carcinoma to the breast is exceedingly rare, typically occurring within two years of initial ovarian cancer diagnosis, with more than 60% of cases involving axillary lymph nodes (8, 21). Compared with primary breast cancers, metastatic breast tumors (MMTB), excluding contralateral breast tumors and lymphoma, remain uncommon (<1% of all breast malignancies) (22). Clinically and radiologically, MMTB often present as painless, firm nodules with well-circumscribed margins, occasionally bilateral, and usually lack typical features of primary breast carcinoma such as skin retraction or microcalcifications. Ultrasound typically demonstrates round or oval hypoechoic masses with clear borders. In our patient, multiple bilateral hypoechoic nodules in the breasts and axillary regions were consistent with these characteristics (23).

Differentiating primary from metastatic breast lesions is challenging, as imaging alone is often insufficient. Histopathological examination (HE staining) combined with immunohistochemistry is essential for definitive diagnosis. Markers including mammaglobin (MG)+, GATA3+, CK7/CK20, GCDFP-15/ER/PR support primary breast origin, whereas WT1+ and PAX8+ indicate Müllerian or ovarian origin (24–27). In the present case, left breast biopsy demonstrated a mixed immunophenotype with MG+/GATA3+/WT1+/PAX8+ (Ki67+, 70%), while pelvic biopsy showed WT1+/PAX8+ (Ki67+, 80%). HE staining revealed two morphologically distinct components: invasive ductal carcinoma coexisting with serous adenocarcinoma of ovarian or uterine origin, highlighting a rare scenario of synchronous primary and metastatic tumors within the breast.

Treatment strategies for HGSC and metastatic breast involvement depend on disease stage and patient condition. Surgery remains central for early-stage disease, while advanced cases often require neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by interval cytoreductive surgery upon achieving sufficient tumor shrinkage. Maintenance targeted therapies, particularly PARP inhibitors, have demonstrated benefit in patients with BRCA-mutated tumors, according to NCCN guidelines (28). Surgical management addressing both ovarian primaries and metastatic breast lesions has been associated with improved survival; conversely, patients who do not undergo surgery face up to an 88% higher risk of mortality from metastatic solid tumors involving the breast (29–31).

In this case, the patient was diagnosed with FIGO stage IVB high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma involving the ovaries, breast, lymph nodes, pleura, and peritoneum. She achieved a partial response after neoadjuvant paclitaxel plus carboplatin chemotherapy and subsequently underwent interval debulking surgery. Molecular testing revealed a pathogenic BRCA mutation and HRD-positive status, which provided a molecular rationale for PARP inhibitor maintenance therapy. The germline or somatic origin of the BRCA mutation should be interpreted according to the original molecular testing report. If a germline BRCA mutation was confirmed or suspected, genetic counseling and cascade testing for family members should be recommended.

Overall, this case highlights a rare diagnostic scenario in which primary breast invasive carcinoma coexisted with metastatic high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma involving the breast. In patients with ovarian carcinoma who present with breast lesions, metastatic ovarian carcinoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis, even when a synchronous primary breast carcinoma is present. Comprehensive evaluation integrating clinical findings, imaging, morphology, immunohistochemistry, and molecular testing is essential for accurate diagnosis and individualized treatment. Molecular testing for BRCA mutations and HRD status may further guide maintenance therapy and genetic counseling.
