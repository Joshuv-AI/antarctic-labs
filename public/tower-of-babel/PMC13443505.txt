Facile Synthesis of Primary Amines via Nitrogen Fixation in Charged Microdroplets
Authors: Rebekah Erin Strong, Owen L Looker, Abraham K Badu-Tawiah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary amines are one of the most versatile synthetic intermediates, but current methods rely on protecting groups in multistep reactions or corrosive reducing agents. Herein, we report an innovative synthetic method for primary amines based on accelerated microdroplet chemistry. The electrospray process allows in-situ ammonia generation from atmospheric nitrogen, reacting with alcohols of various properties (aliphatic and aromatic) to produce the corresponding amine. The alcohol to amine conversion rate improves (> 10×) when hydrazine is introduced as an ammonia source. Electronic and steric effects are also investigated. Overall, three spray conditions are investigated: (1) co-axial spray with hydrazine, (2) single emitter spray without hydrazine at high flowrates, and (3) single emitter spray without hydrazine at low flowrates. Material collection allowed NMR characterization of products, confirming ipso substitution. Our results suggest this method is a useful alternative to bulk synthesis, achieving primary amines under mild reaction conditions without corrosive reagents and exotic catalysts.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Mass spectrometry

Introduction

We introduce an innovative and direct process for the conversion of alcohols to primary amines in charged microdroplets, which is enabled by the in situ conversion of atmospheric nitrogen to ammonia. Charged microdroplets derived from electrospray have been shown to accelerate reactions known to have slow kinetics in bulk phase1. In particular, aqueous microdroplets exhibit a unique set of properties that are not observed in bulk reactions1–5. For example, due to the strong electric field at the air-water interface, species like free electrons, H3O+, hydroxyl radicals, and hydrogen peroxide are produced6. These reactive species contribute to the propagation of chemical reactions, leading to significantly faster rates, sometimes 106orders of magnitude rate increases7. To take advantage of these properties, we created a dual spray process to enable atmospheric nitrogen to be converted into ammonia, which subsequently reacted with alcohols to produce the corresponding amine in situ and in real-time during the microseconds timeframe of the electrospray process. Products were collected and characterized by NMR.

Primary amines are one of the most versatile functional groups in synthetic chemistry, validated by their prevalence in nearly all chemical industries8–14. Amines can be used as intermediates in several industries, including agriculture (pesticides, herbicides, fertilizers)10,11, dyes synthesis (azo-containing dyes)12, pharmaceutical13, and materials synthesis14. Despite their utility, current methods for achieving a primary amine moiety are limited to synthesis requiring multiple steps and the use of protecting groups. This is due to the strongly nucleophilic nature of primary amines; reaction conditions must be carefully selected to yield the best results8,9,15–17.

Cumbersome multistep synthesis includes the Gabriel synthesis, addition-elimination of the nitrogen source, or nucleophilic addition of azides or cyanides15,16. Often, these reactions will be followed up with hydrolysis (acidic or basic) or the use of strong reducing agents (i.e., LiAlH4 or NaBH4)15. Unfortunately, many of these reactions are laborious and/or cost intensive. More modern methods have shown the ability to synthesize a primary amine in a single step; however, these reactions use environmentally toxic catalysts18–20. This can be seen in methods such as the Buchwald-Hartwig synthesis and the Ullman coupling8. While they are significantly more accessible than traditional methods, these catalytic methods suffer from their lack of simplicity, versatility, and cost-intensive nature. Only a few attempts have been made to efficiently synthesize primary amines in microdroplets20–23 although we have seen demonstrations of C-N bond formation to afford secondary and tertiary amines24,25. Our research group has recently demonstrated the tunable nature of these reactions through a dual spray approach25.

Ammonia or ammonia surrogates are often exploited in methods used in synthesizing primary amines9. Generation of ammonia in-situ during a chemical reaction is a valuable capability. Even more so is the generation of ammonia via atmospheric nitrogen, or nitrogen fixation. While nitrogen fixation typically occurs in soil via the assistance of cyanobacteria, this process can also occur in the upper atmosphere, mediated by thermal plasma, or lightning26. In the production of ammonia for industrial use, ammonia is synthesized via the Haber-Bosch process, utilizing a solid support metal catalyst (usually iron oxides) to convert nitrogen gas into ammonia26–28. Recent studies have showcased the ability to achieve nitrogen fixation in charged microdroplet environment28,29. In these studies, Zare and coworkers catalyzed ammonia production through the use of an iron oxide solid support and thus demonstrated the Haber-Bosch process under electrospray conditions28. On the other hand, Fan and colleagues used ultrasonication of bulk water to promote ammonia production29.

In this work, we aim to overcome the challenges of primary amine synthesis and develop a direct synthetic pathway to primary amines using greener reagents. Recent works from our laboratory discuss the tunable nature of microdroplet chemistry achieved via the controlled exposure of the microdroplets to nonthermal plasma, which has included the controlled synthesis of various N-alkylated products from various benzylamines5,30. To expand on recent discoveries, we aim to develop a facile method to synthesize primary amines from alcohols. We rely on nitrogen fixation in charged microdroplets following the use of nitrogen nebulizing gas during the electrospray of the alcohols. The method is direct and does not require protection groups, making the microdroplet-based method simpler and greener compared with traditional bulk-phase synthetic methods.

Results

Optimization of co-axial spray

To enable effective mixing of reagents and to take advantage of accelerated reaction rates possible under the charged microdroplet environment, we developed a co-axial contained electrospray (ES) emitter (Fig. 1A) that delivered two reagents separately to the outlet of the ES emitter where microdroplets are formed. This contained-ES emitter was created in-house from a stainless-steel Swagelok T-element. The initial experiments used hydrazine (100 μM at 18 µL/min flow rate) as the ammonia source, which was sprayed from a fused silica capillary. The alcohol reactant (40 μM) was sprayed from an etched silica capillary at a flow rate of 18 μL/min. See Supporting Information Fig. S1 for details on how the etched silica was prepared in-house from a chemical etching process using 10% hydrofluoric acid. The two inner capillaries (fused and etched silica, 100 µm ID) were inserted into a larger outer capillary (fused silica, 450 µm ID). During the spray process, direct current (DC) high voltage ( ± 5 kV) was applied to the fused silica capillary. The spray process was assisted by nitrogen (N2) nebulizer gas input (150 PSI). The co-axial contained-ES emitter can be operated in two modes: (i) Type I mode (Fig. 1A, i), where the two inner capillaries were pushed slightly (~1 mm) outside of the outer capillary. This mode generates high-speed microdroplets (100 m/s)31 that offer microseconds reaction timescale. (ii) The Type II mode (Fig. 1A, ii), however, is created by recessing the inner capillaries into the outer capillary, which significantly slows down the microdroplets to allow longer reaction time on milliseconds timescale32,33. Preliminary experiments indicated the Type II mode of operation to provide better product conversion (Figs. S2 and S3). Therefore, all reactions were performed in Type II mode. All reagents were prepared in methanol:water (1:1, vol/vol) solution and used as the spray solvent.

The use of the etched capillary was not required for this conversion, but it provided a facile means to detect products via electron capture ionization, perhaps due to the presence of microplasma that can be generated from this conductive capillary having a sharper tip than the fused silica32. A typical mass spectrum recorded in the negative-ion mode when analyzing 4-bromophenol is provided in Fig. 1B, which shows abundant species at m/z 171 and 173 at approximately 1:1 isotopic ratio, confirming the presence of bromine in the corresponding amine product. Using this reaction, we optimized N2 gas pressure (Fig. S4) flowrate (Fig. S5), and spray voltage (Fig. S6) for the contained-ES source used to initiate the accelerated microdroplet chemistry. Scope Study

Using our optimized spray conditions, we explored the breadth of this primary amine synthetic method. We designed a library of starting materials consisting of aromatic and alkyl alcohols as well as thiols. Figure 2 summarizes our results from this scope study. When phenethyl alcohol (2; MW 122 Da) was sprayed in positive-ion mode, co-axially with hydrazine, we observed a peak at m/z 122 (Fig. 2B,i), which represents the protonated species of the expected phenethylamine product (2a; MW 121 Da) with structure confirmed via MS/MS as shown in Fig. 2B,ii. Assuming similar ionization efficiency, approximately 28.39% of the alcohol reactant was converted into the amine product. Phenylethylamine is useful for synthesizing psychiatric (bupropion) and decongestant (pseudoephedrine) medications. We then sprayed benzyl mercaptan (3; MW 124 Da) and observed a close to 42.67% conversion rate for the expected benzylamine product (Fig. 2C, i) detected at m/z 108. MS/MS spectrum characterizing benzylamine is provided in Fig. 2C, ii, which showed diagnostic fragment ion at m/z 91 indicating the presence of a benzylic moiety. In addition to 4-bromophenol (1), we tested another phenolic group via the analysis of 4-N,N-(dimethylamino)-phenol (4; MW 135 Da). Here, we observed the alcohol starting material in negative-ion mode and the expected p-(N,N-dimethylamino)- aniline (4a; MW 136 Da) was detected in positive-ion mode at m/z 137 (Fig. 2D, i). Again, the structure of this amine product was confirmed via MS/MS analysis as explained in Fig. 2D, ii. As part of the scope study, we tested two additional aliphatic alcohols, 3,5-dimethyl-1-hexyn-3-ol (5; MW 125 Da) and cyclohexanol (6; MW 100 Da). The sterically hindered alcohol 5 produced <7% conversion (Fig. 2E, i), with the product detected at m/z 126, while the cyclohexylamine (Fig. 2F, i) was produced at 87% conversion rate from cyclohexanol. The amine products (5a and 6a) from these aliphatic alcohols were characterized via tandem MS and are described in Fig. 2E, ii and 2F, ii, respectively. Cyclohexylamine can be used as an intermediate to synthesize medications to treat type II diabetes (acetohexamide) and anti-cancer agents (lomustine).

Mechanistic investigations

An aspect of the scope study was to quantitatively investigate how electron density and distribution affect reactivity by selecting electron withdrawing and electron donating groups, including 4-bromophenol (1), 4-nitrophenol (7), and 4-N,N-(dimethylamino)-phenol (4). As already discussed in Figs. 1B and 2B-F, we observed good conversion of the alcohols to their corresponding amines for most reactants, including the weak electron withdrawing 4-bromophenol and strong electron donating 4-N,N-(dimethylamino)-phenol reagents (Fig. 3). On the contrary, an order of magnitude decrease in product was observed for the strong electron withdrawing reagent 4-nitrophenol in negative-ion mode (see Fig. 3A, replicate analysis at Fig. S8). We hypothesize that this effect is due to the increased stabilization of the phenyl carbocation that occurs when an electron-donating group (e.g., dimethyl amino) is present, which is facilitated by positive induction mechanism compared with a negative effect from electron withdrawing nitro group. A unique quality of halogens when attached to an aromatic ring is that they exhibit an electron-donating effect via resonance. This partially explains the high relative abundance observed. Another reason for the high relative abundance of 4-bromoanilne may also be owed to the high ionization efficiency of the bromine group in comparison to the nitro and dimethyl amino groups. Aside from such electronic effects, we also investigated steric hindrance. For this, we employed 2-(tert-butyl)−4-methylphenol (8; MW 164 Da), 2,6-di-(tert-butyl)−4-methylphenol (9, MW 220 Da) and 4-methylphenol (10; MW 108 Da). Surprisingly, we observed an increase in the relative abundance of 2-(tert-butyl)−4-methylphenol compared with 4-methylphenol (Fig. S9B, and C), though the reaction efficiency of 4-methylphenol could not be determined as the starting material and product ionized in different ion modes. However, when 2,6-di-(tert-butyl)−4-methylphenol (10, MW 220 Da) is sprayed co-axially with hydrazine, we could not confirm the presence of the desired amine at m/z 220 at all. In fact, a background ion with similar mass shows up at the expected mass-to-charge, but when subjected to MS/MS, this species fragments differently than would be expected for product 2,6-di-(tert-butyl)−4-methylaniline (Fig. S9A). The reduced reactivity for 9 is expected due to the bulky nature of the tert-butyl groups; it occupies a chemical space in the x, y, and z planes, which interferes with the approaching amino group.

Following steric studies, we decided to probe the possible influence of radical species using a radical trapping reagent 4-(trifluoromethyl)-aniline. This experiment was motivated by two reasons: (1) our ability to detect reaction product 1a (4-bromoaniline; Fig. 1B) in the negative-ion mode as a radical anion, suggesting the presence of plasma with low-energy electrons and (2) the existence of a homolytic cleavage mechanism involving the hydrazine to afford the amino group that is used in the nucleophilic attack to generate the amine reaction product. Such homolytic cleavage reaction will involve radical intermediates5. To investigate these, 100 µM of the radical trap was added separately to the 40 µM alcohol (4-bromophenol) and the 100 µM hydrazine solutions. When added to the hydrazine, we observed a marked decrease in the overall conversion of 4-bromophenol into 4-bromoaniline (Fig. S10), which indicates that the source of the amino group was impeded by the presence of the radical trap. Access to the amino group can be impeded in two possible ways: (1) the radical trapping agent can react with the amino group to reduce its abundance in the microdroplet environment as confirmed by the detection of a peak at m/z 176, which corresponds to the addition of aminyl (•NH2) across 4-(trifluoromethyl)-aniline (Fig. S11), and/or (2) the radical trap can impeding the homolytic cleavage of hydrazine to yield ammonia. These theories are further justified as addition of 4-(trifluoromethyl)-aniline to the alcohol did not decrease product conversion to the same degree. Lastly, we used deuterated solvents (MeOD:D2O, 1:1, v/v) as the spray solvents to determine the position of amine insertion into the starting material. We expected to see a mass shift of +2 Da when the amine adds ortho or meta to the bromine. Conversely, if the amine adds ipso to the alcohol, we do not expect to see a large abundance of mass shift. In our experiment, we observed a minimal mass shift associated with the deuterated 4-bromoaniline, and the m/z 171/173 peaks still dominated the spectrum (Fig. S12). This data indicates that the amine is added ipso to the aromatic ring.

In-situ NH3 generation

The earlier discussions have focused on the use of hydrazine as ammonia source. However, the realization that ammonia can be synthesized in-situ in the charged microdroplet environment29,30 motivated us to remove the hydrazine from the spray and to utilize a single electrospray emitter (Fig. 4A) for the direct conversion of alcohols to primary amines. Here, we used 4-bromophenol (1) and electrosprayed it from MeOH/H2O (1:1, v/v) solution via the fused silica capillary in the presence N2 nebulizer gas (150 PSI). To our delight, the expected 4-bromoaniline (1a) product was detected at m/z 171 and 173 in high abundance (Fig. 4B, i) even in the absence of hydrazine. Under this spray condition, however, the formation of the primary amine product was accompanied by a new peak at m/z 343, 345, and 347 in relative ratios of 1:2:1 (see insert in Fig. 4B, i), respectively, indicating the presence of two bromine atoms in this molecule. As can be seen, this new product (m/z 345, 11) was detected at about 73% relative abundance with respect to the main amine product (m/z 171). Both species were detected as radical anions in the negative-ion mode. When hydrazine is sprayed co-axially, the relative abundance of the new product dropped from 73% to 5% (Figs. S13 and S14), indicating the charged microdroplet environment has changed significantly between the two spray conditions. The experiment was reproduced on a high resolution Orbitrap instrument, which provided molecular formula of C12H11NOBr2 (exact mass measured: 342.9229 Da versus expected 342.9219 Da, error is 5 ppm; see Fig. S15). We further characterized this product via MS/MS at m/z 345 (Fig. B,ii), which showed the loss of water to give a peak at m/z 327 as a minor fragment. The fragment from the water loss in turn dissociates to afford the main fragment ion at m/z 171 by releasing 4-bromobenzene. Further collisional activation in MS3 experiment at m/z 171 showed a prominent peak at m/z 79 (Fig. 4B, iii), which is assigned to bromide anion. These fragmentation patterns strongly suggest that the species at m/z 345 is related to the main 4-bromoaniline product, which is formed through a self-coupling chemistry (Fig. S15) to give 1,2-bis-(4-bromophenyl)-amine (MW 327 Da). The hydrated form (see structure in Fig. 4A (green color)) of this coupling product was detected at m/z 345 as supported by exact mass measurement and tandem MS analysis. The appearance of the coupling product was reproduced for two more substrates: 4-nitrophenol (12) and 4-methoxyphenol (13), both of which resulted in the production of the hydrated 1,2-bis-(4-nitrophenyl)-amine (12a) and 1,2-bis-(4-methoxyphenyl)-amine (13a), respectively (Fig. S17).

While the synthesis of 1,2-bis-(4-methoxyphenyl)-amine has useful application as a dye for perovskite solar cells, its appearance in the spray led us to further optimize our reactions to determine if we could control reaction conditions to exclusively produce the primary amine. Through systematic experiments, we optimized the single-capillary spray conditions resulting in optimal reagent flow rate of 2 µL/min, spray volage of −5 kV, and nebulizing gas pressure of 30 PSI (Fig. S18). Under this optimized reaction condition and in the absence of hydrazine, the 1,2-bis-(4-bromophenyl)-amine product was eliminated (Fig. 4B, iv and Fig. S19), enabling atmospheric N2 to be used for in-situ primary amine synthesis by electrospraying the corresponding alcohol. Given the production of 4-bromoaniline even without the hydrazine (as ammonia source), we sought to determine if the nitrogen fixation reaction was dependent on N2 nebulizing gas used for electrospray. To examine this possibility, we used carbon dioxide (CO2) as our nebulizing gas (Fig. S20); if the nebulizer gas is the main source of nitrogen, then we would expect the amine product at m/z 171/173 to disappear. However, when CO2 was used during the electrospray, we still observed the desired 4-bromoaniline product, although intensity reduced (Fig. S20C). Given this data, we believe that some of the nitrogen comes from the atmosphere surrounding the spray plume, which are oxidized in-situ to ammonia, which subsequently engages in nucleophilic attack on the phenyl cation derived from the loss of water from the alcohol. The in-situ generation of ammonia in the microdroplet environment was further confirmed by replacing the alcohol reactant with an aldehyde functional group, expecting to form an amide via an addition-elimination reaction. We used 4-bromobenzaldehyde (14; MW 184 Da) for this experiment prepared in methanol:water (1:1, v/v) and sprayed with N2 and CO2 nebulizer gases in the negative-ion mode as two separate experiments. The results from these experiments are summarized in Figs. S21 and S22, where we observed the expected 4-bromobenzamide product (14a; MW 199 Da) at m/z 199 in negative-ion mode. CID analysis revealed the loss of O = C = NH2 (MW 44 Da) to give a fragment at m/z 155, which subsequently dissociated in MS3 experiment to give the bromide anion at m/z 79. When sprayed with CO2 as nebulizing gas, we observed similar results, though with reduced signal intensity (Fig. S21C).

Product collection

Product collection was performed using an in-house-made collection setup comprising an external power supply, an external syringe pump, and a grounding device. A 40 µM solution of 4-bromophenol in methanol: water (1:1, v/v) was sprayed coaxially with 100 µM hydrazine in methanol: water (1:1, v/v). The hydrazine solution was sprayed through the fused silica capillary with constant DC voltage applied (−5.0 kV). N2 nebulizing gas was used at 5 PSI; increasing the nebulizing pressure during collection caused material to splash out of the collection vessel. The collection vial, containing 2 mL of water, was wrapped in aluminum foil and grounded via a copper wire connected to our grounding device. The product, which we expected to be 4-bromoaniline, was collected for 6 h. The collected crude product was subjected to NMR analysis, which confirmed ipso substitution of the amine for the hydroxyl group (Fig. S23). This suggests that no rearrangement occurs with the phenyl cation intermediate that forms. This further explains why benzenes substituted with electron-withdrawing groups were less efficient. Electron-donating group para to the alcohol presents the most stable resonance structural forms. The crude NMR analysis (Fig.S24) of the collected material also showed the presence of a secondary amine, confirming the presence of the coupled product 11, detected at m/z 345.

Mechanistic insight on microdroplet-based NH3 synthesis

Ammonia synthesis requires N2 and H2 sources. Traditionally, CH4 is used as the H2 source, which is reacted with steam under high temperature (> 700 °C) and pressure (< 25 atm) conditions. Recent studies have suggested that charged water microdroplets are able to facilitate one-electron reduction because of the unique air–water interface properties, including the highly active redox environment29,34,35. Combined with the high acidic environment, including the existence of interfacial hydronium ions (H3O+)36, it is believed that proton-coupled electron transfer reactions can enable the protonation of N2 to form H2N-NH2 and subsequently NH3. Alternatively, high abundant H2 has been detected to accompany the reduction of dissolved oxygen in water microdroplets generated from sonication37, a process similar to the electro-sonic spray used here for generating charged microdroplets. Such reactions can serve as a facile H2 source for NH3 production in the presence of N2. To evaluate the presence of ammonia in the charged microdroplets, we performed a colorimetric ammonia test on material collected after 12 h spray. First, we sprayed a blank methanol:water solution and determined ammonia concentration to be ≤0.25 ppm in the collected material (Fig. S25). Then, we performed a similar spray with 4-bromophenol added to the MeOH/H2O spray solution. In this case, ammonia concentration was determined to be between 1 and 2 ppm, which suggests that the amine products formed during the electrospray contributed significantly to the color change observed. Thus, the color change and product formation both emphasize the in-situ synthesis of ammonia from N2 during electrospray. Simultaneously, the alcohol (e.g., 4-bromophenol) is protonated by the acidic microdroplet environment (H3O+), causing water to leave, assisted by collisions in the Type II mode spray condition, which generates the corresponding carbocation (e.g., phenyl cation)38. The carbocation and ammonia react to afford the corresponding protonated primary amine. The introduction of hydrazine in the microdroplet environment is not required, but it is found to increase product formation, sometimes up to an order of magnitude, as shown for 4-bromophenol to 4-bromoaniline conversion. The two independent reaction pathways (ammonia synthesis and carbocation formation) occurring in the microdroplet environment to afford the final amine product are summarized in Fig. 5. An example of experimental evidence (Fig. S26) demonstrating the formation of the carbocation intermediate is shown for the analysis of 4-N,N-(dimethylamino)-phenol (4; MW 137 Da) in which the corresponding carbocation, N,N-(dimethylamino)benzylium, was detected at m/z 120 after water loss from the protonated pseudomolecular ion (m/z 138).

In conclusion, we have developed a facile method for synthesizing primary amines directly from alcohols using hydrazine in charged microdroplets. We have demonstrated the desired conversion using aromatic and alkyl alcohols, as well as thiols, with mild steric bulk tolerance. Hydrazine is not required for successful conversion, as in-situ oxidation of atmospheric nitrogen gas yields ammonia as our nitrogen source. In three independent experiments, the mechanism of this facile reaction has been revealed. First, we use 4-(trifluoromethyl)-aniline, capable of scavenging radicals, which showed the importance of the homolytic cleavage of hydrazine in catalyzing the start of the reaction. Next, we confirmed amine substitution to involve the ipso position using deuterated solvents. Finally, we showed that in the absence of an external ammonia source, nitrogen enters the reaction from the atmospheric air surrounding the microdroplets and not necessarily from the N2 nebulizing gas. Therefore, we believe nitrogen fixation occurs without the need for a solid support or a metal catalyst. Relative humidity can also affect product distribution by enabling in-situ generation of reactive oxygen species (e.g., H2O2)39. Our results highlight the potential to use nitrogen fixation in ambient air for reactions as the ammonia source. By utilizing this process under the microdroplet reaction condition, primary amines were synthesized without the need for multi-step reaction pathways, protection groups, metal catalysts, or corrosive oxidizing reagents. This method can be further utilized in green synthesis of nitrogenous intermediates (imines, amides, etc.) without a catalyst and external nitrogen sources. Aside from aromatic alcohols, aliphatic alcohols, and thiols, N-heterocycle substrates were also studied but detectable conversion to the corresponding primary amine was not observed, highlighting a potential limitation of the method. Product conversion rates were determined using the ion intensities of reactants and product(s), assuming that ionization efficiencies are similar. There is a risk of under- or overestimating product conversion rates if this condition is not satisfied. Data from steric studies also suggest poly-substituted phenols might have limited reactivity under this method although further experiments are needed. While hydrazine is not required for the formation of primary amine from alcohols, its use increased the overall yield of the reaction. When using hydrazine, however, it is important to note its toxicological effects.

Methods

Contained-electrospray emitter

The contained-electrospray source for plasma-microdroplet fusion reactions (Fig. 1) was constructed from a Swagelok cross-junction (1/16” tube o.d.) with compression fittings (Swagelok, Solon, OH). Sheath gas introduction occurred through stainless-steel tubing (1/16” OD x 0.20” ID) and a stainless-steel Swagelok reducing union (1/4” × 1/16” OD). To transfer solution-phase species via a spray capillary, a union assembly of polyether ether ketone (PEEK) was used to connect the capillary and syringe. Substrate solutions for conventional electrospray ionization were delivered co-axially through a deactivated fused silica capillary (100 μm ID), Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Substrate solutions for plasma-microdroplet fusing reactions were delivered through etched silica spray capillaries. For Type II operation, which was used most often in this study, species were delivered into the cavity of the outer deactivated fused silica capillary (pure microdroplet reaction) or the outer etched silica capillary (plasma-microdroplet fusion reaction) (450 µM ID, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The Type II operation mode allows extended droplet mixing via the discontinuous thin film on the outer capillary walls prior to exiting the contained-electrospray source. Capillaries were held in place by graphite ferrules (Restek, Bellefonte, PA). Type I and Type II operational modes are described in detail in previous work. Briefly, Type I operation occurs when the inner capillary protrudes from the outer ~1mm, allowing a microsecond droplet lifetime. Conversely, Type II operation occurs when the inner capillary is recessed ~6 mm into the outer capillary, creating a reaction cavity.

Etched silica capillary production

The production of etched silica capillaries for plasma-microdroplet fusion is described in detail in a fundamental study. Briefly, 10% v/v hydrofluoric acid is utilized to etch the top 3-5 mm of silica from the emitter tip after removal of the protective polyimide coating. Emitter tips were immersed in the etching solution for 24 hours, during which the hydrofluoric acid is drawn up the sides of the emitter via capillary action, resulting in an isotropic etching process. The process self-terminates when bulk silica is completely removed. After etching, a thorough washing procedure is followed. HF Acid Safety Statement: HF acid is very corrosive and hazardous due to the production of fluoride anion. Extreme care should be taken to prevent bodily exposure to HF vapor and liquid. HF solutions should be handled only in a well-ventilated hood with chemically resistant PTFE containers and butyl rubber gloves.

Mass spectrometry

Mass spectra were obtained using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Finnigan ion trap mass spectrometer (San Jose, CA). The emitter-to-MS inlet distance was held at 5 mm. Experimental parameters were optimized based on the average of at least 3 replicates. MS parameters were held at 200 °C transfer capillary temperature; 3 microscans; 100 ms ion injection time, and all spectra were recorded for a minimum of 30 s. Tandem MS was performed via CID for structural analysis using collision energies ranging from 15-40 (manufacturer’s units), which were optimized for each species. Data processing was performed using Thermo Fisher Scientific Xcalibur 2.2 SP1 software.

Reaction conditions: dual emitter setup

Through the fused silica capillary 100 µM hydrazine in water:methanol (1:1, v/v) was injected at a flow rate of 18 µL/min. Through a separate HF etched silica capillary 40 µM alcohol in water:methanol (1:1, v/v) was injected at a flow rate of 18 µL/min. Nitrogen nebulizing gas was applied at 150 PSI and DC voltage was applied to the fused silica capillary at 6.0 kV. Reactions were performed in Type II mode.

Reaction conditions: single emitter setup

Through the fused silica capillary 40 µM alcohol in water: methanol (1:1, v/v) was injected at a flow rate of 2 µL/min. Nitrogen nebulizing gas was applied at 30 PSI, and DC voltage was applied to the fused silica capillary at −5.0 kV. Reactions were performed in Type II mode.

Qualitative ammonia test

An API Ammonia (NH3/NH4+) test kit was purchased as a colorimetric test for the presence of ammonia in our product samples. The test was performed using 625 µL of the soft landing collected sample, one drop of solution #1, and one drop of solution #2. Solution #1 and Solution #2 were premade contents of the API test kit. The solution was agitated to thoroughly mix the sample and manufacturer solutions; this was the zero minute. The test solution was left undisturbed for five minutes, at which point the color change was evaluated using the color gradient cards provided in the test kit. The depth of color change was indicative of dissolved ammonia concentration, reported in parts per million (ppm).

Product collection

Product collection platforms consisted of the above mentioned contained electrospray device spraying vertically into a collection vial with a 2 mL water soft landing that was grounded via aluminum foil and a copper wire. Grounding was achieved using an external power supply with positive and negative current alligator clips connecting the collection vial and the wall socket ground. Applied voltage was applied to the fused silica capillary via the external power supply. Reagent solutions were introduced through the silica capillaries of the contained electrospray source via 20 mL plastic syringes of prepared reagent solution in methanol:water (1:1, v/v) at 100 µM (hydrazine) and 40 µM (alcohol) concentrations. External syringe pumps were used for extended setup use without the need to replenish for 12 hours. Upon completion, the collected samples were concentrated and dissolved in NMR solvent to be subject to NMR analysis.
