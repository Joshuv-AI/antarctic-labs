Clarifying dissemination outcomes: a taxonomy to advance dissemination science

Introduction

Dissemination research has grown substantially over the past two decades, yet the field still lacks shared language for specifying what dissemination strategies are designed to achieve [1, 2]. When a dissemination strategy fails to move evidence toward use, evidence-based interventions remain underused, populations who would most benefit are reached unevenly if at all, and inequities in access and care deepen. It is also difficult to determine why dissemination falls short. The evidence may never have been encountered by intended audiences, encountered but not understood, understood but not seen as relevant, or perceived as relevant but never acted upon. Without clearly defined outcomes, researchers cannot diagnose where dissemination breaks down, compare findings, or build cumulative evidence about how dissemination actually works [3]. This risk is visible in HIV prevention and care for adolescents and young adults, where effective interventions exist yet access remains markedly uneven, driven largely by systemic inequities [3–5]. Closing these gaps requires shared outcome language that can locate where dissemination stalls and guide strategies needed to address it.

Purtle and colleagues [6] observed that Proctor et al.‘s (2011) widely cited taxonomy of implementation outcomes does not address dissemination outcomes [7], and Baumann and colleagues’ [8] scoping review found significant heterogeneity in how dissemination outcomes were conceptualized and measured, limited consistency in terminology, and wide variation in level of analysis [see also [2, 9]. Throughout, we use “dissemination practice” to describe dissemination strategies used to share evidence with audiences and “dissemination science” to describe studying which dissemination strategies produce which desired outcomes for which audiences and under what conditions [10]. Although some empirical work has captured dissemination outcomes, these efforts remain fragmented. For example, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s (CDC’s) Diffusion of Effective Behavioral Interventions (DEBI) program documented reach to more than 11,300 agencies [11], and Knudsen and colleagues (2025) published a protocol for experimentally testing adaptive dissemination strategies to promote intervention adoption within HIV service organizations [12]. However, these examples remain isolated rather than applications of shared outcomes.

Dissemination outcomes are the effects of audiences encountering, processing, and responding to evidence, including changes in awareness, knowledge, perceptions, and motivation [13, 14]. Throughout this paper, “evidence” refers broadly to research findings, evidence-based interventions, guidelines, and other products of research being disseminated.1 Dissemination strategies may involve multiple audiences at individual, organizational, network, or policy levels, each with distinct pathways toward desired outcomes [1, 13]. Dissemination outcomes are conceptually distinct from dissemination mechanisms, which are the means by which dissemination strategies produce change [15], and from implementation outcomes, which follow once an audience decides to implement [7].

We propose a taxonomy of eight dissemination outcomes to address this gap in shared language. Our taxonomy focuses on outcomes of intentional, audience-specific dissemination, recognizing that this represents one component of how evidence moves toward use [16]. The taxonomy is informed by more than two decades of applied experience within the Adolescent Medicine Trials Network for HIV Interventions (ATN), a National Institutes of Health-funded research network addressing HIV prevention, care, and treatment for adolescents and young adults [17]. We offer this taxonomy not as a prescriptive framework but as a conceptual starting point to support rigorous study design [18].

Methods

Distinguishing dissemination outcomes from mechanisms and implementation outcomes

Developing a taxonomy of dissemination outcomes requires two conceptual distinctions that shape which outcomes belong and which do not. The first is between outcomes and mechanisms. Dissemination outcomes describe audience-level changes: whether audiences encountered, understood, trusted, or decided to act on evidence. Dissemination mechanisms describe the processes through which dissemination strategies produce those changes, such as emotional engagement, social influence, or cognitive elaboration [15]. This distinction is foundational to how we developed the taxonomy: mechanisms are causal pathways linking strategies to outcomes, not outcomes themselves. While existing frameworks include outcomes such as “reception” or “emotional reactions” as dissemination outcomes [8], we conceptualize these as mechanisms because they describe processes that may contribute to audience-level change rather than the change itself.

The second distinction is between dissemination outcomes and implementation outcomes. Proctor and colleagues defined implementation outcomes as effects of deliberate actions to implement new treatments, practices, and services [7], and proposed a taxonomy of eight such outcomes. Their taxonomy has provided essential conceptual infrastructure for implementation research. Although their adoption outcome encompasses intention, initial decision-making, and early use [7], the taxonomy does not differentiate audience-level processes that precede a formal commitment to implement. We therefore disaggregate what Proctor et al. grouped within adoption into upstream decisional commitment (Decision to Implement) and downstream enactment (adoption). This separation matters because Proctor and colleagues’ adoption outcome already folds intentions and the decision to implement into early use; bundled this way, it cannot pinpoint the boundary between dissemination and implementation. We locate the boundary between dissemination and implementation at the point of formal commitment, which we term Decision to Implement. This placement is consistent with how the broader literature treats adoption. Purtle and colleagues include adoption as a dissemination outcome [6], and many of the dissemination definitions cataloged by Baumann and colleagues frame adoption as the goal of dissemination [8]. We read this recurring emphasis as evidence that the decisive event is the commitment to act, which we isolate as the Decision to Implement rather than the enactment that follows. Locating the boundary here aligns dissemination science with decision science by treating the decision to implement as an observable commitment arising from decision processes, rather than conflating those processes with downstream execution. Dissemination outcomes describe how audiences encounter, process, and respond to evidence up to and including the Decision to Implement. Implementation outcomes, such as adoption, fidelity, penetration, and sustainability, describe the initial use and ongoing execution once implementation begins. Dissemination outcomes can also function as implementation determinants in widely used implementation frameworks. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [19, 20], for example, includes constructs aligned with our dissemination outcomes under source and evidence, recipient needs, and leadership engagement.

Taxonomy development process

Our team works in adolescent HIV research, with public health training and disciplinary expertise in social-health psychology, clinical and community psychology, implementation science, and/or infectious disease medicine. Two of us co-lead the ATN, two are members of the University of Illinois Chicago’s Center for Dissemination and Implementation Science, and all hold leadership roles in the ATN Communications and Dissemination Hub. Our positionality and applied experience shaped which outcomes we selected, how we categorized them, and which we prioritized.

To develop this taxonomy, we drew on existing dissemination scholarship, frameworks from related fields, and two decades of applied experience within the ATN. Scoping and systematic reviews are designed to map what a literature contains; [8, 21] our task was to propose and refine outcomes that do not yet exist as a coherent set in the dissemination literature. We drew purposively on scholarship across several domains to identify candidate outcomes, tested and refined them against applied cases, and iteratively consolidated them through author team discussion, consistent with the conceptual approach used in other taxonomy development efforts [7, 22, 23].

We began with existing reviews of dissemination frameworks [8, 24–27] and followed citation chains to identify primary sources. We consulted communication and persuasion theory [28–31], health behavior theory [32, 33], innovation adoption and diffusion research [34, 35], public policy theory [36], organizational readiness theory [37], and knowledge mobilization scholarship [38–40]. We also drew on the SPIRIT-ME action framework for academic-policy engagement [41], strategic messaging for policy change [42], equity-centered dissemination practice [43], designing for dissemination [44], and emerging dissemination science methodology [6, 14]. The Dissemination Research Logic Model (DRLM) developed within the ATN [15] also informed the taxonomy by specifying the relationships among dissemination determinants, strategies, channels, mechanisms, and outcomes, providing a structured framework within which to locate and sequence dissemination outcomes.

From the sources described above, we identified an initial set of fifteen candidate outcomes and iteratively refined distinctions through discussion among the author team (see supplementary table). We organized candidate outcomes around a central question: what must dissemination accomplish, sequentially, for evidence to move toward use? Sequencing the outcomes makes the taxonomy easier to follow and shows where progress can break down. The CFIR Outcomes Addendum [45] and the RE-AIM outcomes cascade [46] also present their outcomes in sequence. This question led us to focus on outcomes that reflect audience-level changes rather than the processes that produce them.

We tested definitions against three ATN studies (Table 1): Connect to Protect (C2P) (ATN 040) [47, 49], Project PrEPare (ATN 113) [49], and Tailored Motivational Interviewing (TMI) (ATN 146) [50]. We assessed whether the outcomes were distinguishable in practice and whether the dissemination-implementation boundary held. For each study, we asked whether a given outcome could be assessed independently of others, whether it required distinct measurement approaches, and whether collapsing two outcomes into one would obscure meaningful variation in how audiences responded to disseminated evidence. Where definitions overlapped or created ambiguity, we revised or consolidated. For example, we initially treated credibility and perceived fit as a single outcome but found that audiences could trust evidence while seeing no fit with their own context. This distinction has direct implications for which dissemination strategies would be needed. Engagement and secondary dissemination came up as candidates. Engagement refers to active audience interaction with disseminated evidence; secondary dissemination refers to audiences passing evidence on to others. We treated both as measurable indicators that span multiple outcomes rather than as distinct outcomes. Through this process, we consolidated from fifteen candidate outcomes to the eight presented here.

Results

A taxonomy of dissemination outcomes

Each outcome can be assessed at individual, organizational, system, or policy levels depending on study design and intended audiences. We intentionally modeled our approach on Proctor and colleagues’ taxonomy, seeking to provide comparable conceptual infrastructure for dissemination research [7]. We organize these outcomes from proximal to distal, reflecting the general sequence in which audiences encounter and respond to disseminated evidence: proximal outcomes describe whether evidence was encountered and processed, intermediate outcomes describe how audiences appraised it, and distal outcomes describe commitments to act. This ordering does not imply that all dissemination processes follow a fixed linear progression but can help researchers organize and describe their desired dissemination outcomes, including when designing tests of dissemination strategies. Below, we describe each dissemination outcome and illustrate how it could be assessed in practice by drawing on one of three ATN studies. Exposure

Evidence cannot influence anyone who never encounters it, which is why exposure appears first in persuasion models [51]. Exposure captures whether intended audiences encountered the evidence being disseminated, including whether they became aware of its existence. Mass media channels create exposure rapidly across large audiences, while interpersonal channels become more important in later stages [52]. Researchers assess exposure through indicators such as attendance records, download counts, or media coverage tracking. Exposure may also include connectivity; whether dissemination efforts created or strengthened relationships between evidence producers and potential users that enable ongoing access to evidence. Dissemination of C2P (ATN 040) coalition findings illustrates the measurement challenges exposure presents. Researchers could track whether health departments and community organizations encountered publications or presentations describing the model, and which types of organizations were and were not reached. But exposure metrics alone would not reveal whether organizations that downloaded a brief actually read it, whether the brief reached the individuals within those organizations who were positioned to act, or whether organizations serving the communities most affected by adolescent HIV were systematically more or less likely to be exposed. These gaps are why exposure is only the first outcome in this taxonomy.

Comprehension

Audiences must also understand what the evidence shows. Rogers distinguishes three types of knowledge in the innovation-decision process: awareness knowledge (knowing something exists), how-to knowledge (knowing how to use it correctly), and principles knowledge (understanding why it works) [34]. We locate awareness knowledge within Exposure and reserve Comprehension for how-to and principles knowledge, where audiences move beyond knowing evidence exists to understanding what it demonstrates and implications for practice. Comprehension follows exposure in McGuire’s persuasion sequence because cognitive processing must occur before attitudes can form [51]. Assessing comprehension requires more than recognition; it requires determining whether audiences can accurately describe the evidence and draw appropriate conclusions from it. Comprehension may also vary based on how evidence is communicated; materials designed for one audience may not translate for others with different professional backgrounds, language needs, or health literacy. Wherever audiences must interpret evidence collectively, comprehension also involves sensemaking [53], which is the process through which groups develop shared meaning from new information. This operates at any aggregate level, whether within a clinic team, across a health system, or among members of a guideline committee. TMI (ATN 146) demonstrated that brief training improved counselor competence in motivational interviewing with sustained gains over time. Disseminating these findings would require conveying not just that training worked but what the trial showed about dosage (a single training session with performance feedback) and durability (sustained gains at follow-up). Comprehension could be assessed by examining whether clinic leaders accurately understood these specific findings, for example, by asking them to describe the training model and its evidence base. A clinic leader who knew TMI existed but believed it required ongoing expert supervision would demonstrate exposure without comprehension, a distinction that would lead to different dissemination strategies than if the leader had never heard of the study at all.

Credibility

Audiences do not simply receive evidence; they evaluate whether to trust it. Credibility captures whether audiences trust both the evidence and its source, based on the perceived rigor of the methods, the reputation of the researchers and institutions involved, and whether the source seems to understand their context and populations [1]. Petty and Cacioppo’s elaboration likelihood model describes processing of persuasive messages as varying along a continuum [29]. Audiences who process evidence carefully evaluate methodological rigor, while those processing less carefully rely on heuristics such as source reputation or institutional prestige. Many audiences fall in the middle, recognizing the importance of rigor without claiming the expertise to evaluate it themselves. Dissemination strategies should attend to where audiences fall along this continuum, since the kinds of information needed to support credibility differ across positions. Evidence produced by researchers with no connection to a community, for instance, may face greater skepticism than evidence from researchers who have established relationships over time [54]. For TMI (ATN 146), clinic leaders receiving disseminated findings would judge whether the methods were rigorous and whether ATN investigators were credible researchers who understood real-world clinic constraints. A clinic leader who trusted the evidence but doubted the investigators understood the realities of under-resourced clinics would present a different dissemination challenge than one who questioned the trial design itself. Credibility assessments thus shape not only whether audiences accept evidence but which specific concerns dissemination strategies must address.

Salience

Salience captures whether audiences see the issue addressed by the evidence as a problem in their setting that warrants attention [28]. This judgment includes both whether audiences recognize a problem at all and how they rank it against competing concerns. Audiences may not see the issue as a problem in the populations they serve, may see it as a problem but not one they face, or may see it as a problem that ranks below other priorities. Each pattern represents a distinct dissemination challenge. Quinn and colleagues identified relevance, a term closely related to salience, as a critical criterion for health communication, finding that campaigns often failed because they did not address concerns audiences actually held [30]. At the policy level, Kingdon argues that issues reach decision-makers’ agendas only when three conditions align: the problem is recognized, a viable policy solution exists, and the political moment is receptive [36]. Salience also varies across audiences and over time as priorities shift. For C2P (ATN 040), salience could be assessed by examining whether health departments receiving disseminated findings viewed HIV prevention among adolescents as a current priority, and whether departments serving communities most affected by HIV saw the coalition model as addressing needs they recognized. A health department that trusted the evidence and understood the model but ranked adolescent HIV prevention below other competing priorities would represent a salience failure, not a credibility or comprehension failure, and would require dissemination strategies focused on agenda-setting rather than on strengthening evidence or clarifying findings.

Perceived fit

Audiences must also judge whether they can act on the evidence in their own setting and whether doing so offers advantage over current approaches. Tornatzky and Klein found that perceptions of compatibility with existing practices, relative advantage over current approaches, and complexity of use most consistently predict adoption decisions [35]. Someone may find evidence credible but see no feasible application because they face different constraints or perceive no advantage over what they currently do. We preserve a distinction between credibility and perceived fit because an audience may trust evidence while seeing no feasible application in their own setting, a distinction with direct implications for which dissemination strategies are needed. Judgments about fit do not require that an approach be a clear improvement, only that it be implementable; whether an alternative offers advantage is a separate question that perceived fit allows researchers to ask. Researchers may assess perceptions of compatibility, feasibility, and relative advantage separately or jointly, depending on the level of analysis and study design. Perceived fit may also reflect whose contexts shaped the original research; evidence developed in well-resourced academic medical centers may seem inapplicable to safety-net clinics or community-based organizations serving similar populations under different constraints. Salience and perceived fit do not always align. A practitioner may care deeply about a problem yet still conclude that the evidence was not developed for contexts like theirs. PrEPare (ATN 113) dissemination required FDA and CDC officials to judge whether the evidence fit the regulatory and guideline pathways available to them. An official who found the evidence credible and the issue salient but saw no clear pathway to translate it into regulatory action would present a perceived fit problem, signaling a need for dissemination strategies that addressed how the evidence connected to existing decision processes rather than evidence quality itself.

Leadership endorsement

In organizational, system, and policy settings, dissemination often must reach those with authority before others can respond, regardless of how favorable frontline staff or community members may be [55]. Leadership endorsement captures whether formal decision-makers or opinion leaders express support for evidence or authorize others to act on it. Endorsement can range from agreeing to review evidence to actively championing it but does not require commitment to sustained use. Opinion leaders may not hold formal authority but shape peer perceptions through credibility, network position, or professional standing; their endorsement can advance dissemination even when they cannot directly authorize action. PrEPare (ATN 113) dissemination included consultations with FDA and CDC officials. Their public support for reviewing adolescent PrEP data and willingness to champion adolescent-specific considerations within regulatory processes represented endorsement. An official who found the evidence compelling but declined to publicly support its consideration within regulatory processes would represent an endorsement failure, signaling a need for dissemination strategies that addressed institutional risk, political context, or the framing of evidence for regulatory audiences rather than the evidence itself.

Action readiness

Audiences may understand and trust the evidence, see it as relevant, and judge it applicable to their context, yet still not be positioned to act. Action readiness captures whether audiences both intend to act on evidence and feel prepared to do so, short of commitment. Ajzen identifies intentions as the immediate antecedent of behavior, and intentions should be specified with respect to a particular action (e.g., intentions to adopt, intentions to explore, intentions to recommend) [33]. Intentions form from attitudes toward the action, perceived social norms, and perceived behavioral control. Someone may view evidence favorably but lack intention to act because they perceive barriers, doubt their capacity, or feel no social pressure to respond. Weiner extends this to organizations, describing readiness as a shared psychological state with two components: commitment to change and confidence in the organization’s ability to carry it out [37]. Dissemination efforts may also change audiences’ capacity to act, for example by increasing skills, knowledge, or resources needed to use evidence, which the knowledge mobilization literature identifies as a distinct outcome of engagement [40]. Readiness also depends on whose capacity has been supported; organizations facing resource constraints may lack ability to act regardless of intentions. For TMI (ATN 146), action readiness could be assessed by examining whether clinic leaders who received disseminated findings intended to adopt the training model and felt their clinics had capacity to do so. A clinic leader who expressed strong interest in adopting TMI but reported that the clinic lacked protected time for staff training would demonstrate intention without preparedness, a pattern that would call for dissemination strategies addressing organizational capacity rather than evidence quality or relevance. We treat intention and perceived capacity as one outcome because both are proximal preparations for adoption that often co-occur; however, they may be assessed separately or jointly.

Decision to implement

This taxonomy culminates in a formal or explicit commitment to initiate implementation of evidence. Specifying the Decision to Implement clarifies where dissemination ends and implementation begins: our Decision to Implement captures the commitment that precedes Proctor and colleagues’ implementation outcomes [7], including adoption, which encompasses initial attempts to employ an innovation. The Decision to Implement takes different forms at different levels. For example, a clinician deciding to try an intervention with eligible patients, an organization formally approving a new program, a payer deciding to cover a service, a regulatory body expanding a labeling indication, or a guideline committee issuing a recommendation. At organizational and system levels, decisions to implement are shaped by institutional pressures. Organizations may decide to implement because regulators require it, because peer organizations have decided to implement, or because professional bodies endorse it [56]. The Decision to Implement also determines whose needs are centered. Decisions about which populations, settings, or indications to include shape who stands to benefit from evidence uptake. For PrEPare (ATN 113), the Decision to Implement included FDA expansion of the Truvada label to include adolescents, CDC inclusion of adolescents in updated PrEP guidelines, and payer decisions to approve coverage for adolescent PrEP. Each of these commitments carried equity implications: the FDA labeling decision defined which age groups fell within the approved indication, the CDC guideline update defined which populations were named in clinical recommendations, and payer coverage decisions defined which adolescents’ services would be eligible for reimbursement. Documenting these decisions as the final dissemination outcome, distinct from the delivery and quality assessments that follow, allows researchers to identify where in the pathway evidence stalled and for whom.

These eight dissemination outcomes operationalize the outcome component of the broader DRLM [15], which specifies the full causal pathway from what is being disseminated and its intended audiences through to dissemination outcomes. Figure 1 shows how dissemination outcomes relate to Proctor and colleagues’ implementation outcomes [7]. Decision to Implement marks the boundary where dissemination outcomes describe what happens as evidence reaches and influences potential adopters, while implementation outcomes typically occurring downstream describe what happens after the decision to implement has been made. Some outcomes appear in both domains but with different referents. For example, what implementation research captures as acceptability of a delivered intervention [7] disaggregates in dissemination into Credibility, Perceived Fit, and Salience, because audiences appraise trust in evidence, its fit with their setting, and its relevance as distinct judgements before committing to implement.

Discussion

This taxonomy offers a structured approach to specifying dissemination outcomes prospectively [2, 6, 8, 57]. Researchers can use these eight outcomes, from initial exposure through a formal decision to implement, to study the effects of dissemination processes, not just whether they occurred. Like Proctor and colleagues’ taxonomy of implementation outcomes [7], this framework provides common language that enables cumulative evidence building while also clarifying the boundary between dissemination and implementation.

Our taxonomy aligns with but also departs from the outcomes cataloged by Baumann and colleagues [8]. Their 15 outcomes include reception and emotional reactions, which we conceptualize as mechanisms rather than outcomes. Knowledge utilization, which is the most frequently identified outcome, spans both dissemination and implementation; we locate the boundary at Decision to Implement, treating utilization as an implementation outcome. We also disaggregate the broad outcome of “attitudes” into three functionally distinct outcomes (Credibility, Salience, and Perceived Fit) because aggregate attitude measures obscure which specific appraisals dissemination strategies need to address. An audience may trust evidence but not see it as relevant, or see it as relevant but not applicable to their context. These distinctions have direct implications for strategy selection and measurement.

Several outcomes identified in the broader knowledge mobilization literature warrant acknowledgment even though we do not include them as separate outcomes. Engagement (whether audiences actively interact with or participate in dialogue about evidence beyond passive exposure) has been identified as a distinct dissemination goal in community-engaged and knowledge mobilization frameworks that prioritize bidirectional exchange [38, 58, 59]. In participatory dissemination traditions, engagement may also be valued as an end in itself; here we treat it as a cross-cutting indicator because our focus is on outcomes that move evidence toward formal decisions to implement. We conceptualize engagement as a measurable indicator that spans multiple outcomes, particularly Exposure and Comprehension, rather than a standalone outcome. Similarly, secondary dissemination (whether audiences share, recommend, or discuss evidence with others in their networks) represents a multiplier effect central to diffusion processes [34]. Demand creation, in which dissemination generates bottom-up pressure for adoption among end-users or the public, complements the top-down dynamic captured by Leadership Endorsement and has been identified as a distinct goal in participatory dissemination research [58]. Future work should examine whether these dynamics require separate outcomes or whether existing outcomes adequately capture them when applied across different audience levels.

At the same time, social, political, and ideological contexts influence evidence uptake well beyond scientific merit [60, 61]. In adolescent HIV prevention, evidence must contend with stigma toward affected populations, discomfort with adolescent sexuality, and resistance to harm reduction approaches [62–65]. How evidence is framed, including which aspects are emphasized, what causal attributions are implied, and which values are invoked, affects credibility, salience, and perceived fit simultaneously [66, 67]. These contextual forces determine which dissemination outcomes can realistically be achieved, how stable those outcomes are over time, and who benefits from evidence uptake. Framing choices carry equity implications when they determine whose problems and whose solutions are centered, and credibility and leadership endorsement may prove fragile given the rapid spread of health misinformation [68, 69]. As shown in Table 2, we encourage researchers to apply an equity lens across all eight outcomes by assessing not only whether an outcome occurred but for whom: Who was exposed? Whose comprehension was assessed? Whose perceptions of fit were centered? Whose leadership endorsement was sought? Equity-centered dissemination practice [43, 70] and designing for dissemination to promote equitable impacts [44] call attention to how dissemination efforts may reproduce or reduce existing inequities.

Measurement approaches will vary by outcome and audience. Proximal outcomes like exposure can often be documented through administrative data such as attendance records, download counts, or distribution lists. Intermediate outcomes like salience and perceived fit may require qualitative or mixed-methods approaches that capture how audiences weighed evidence against their own priorities and constraints. Decisions to implement at the policy level may require tracking regulatory actions, guideline revisions, or budget commitments over time. Across all outcomes, measurement approaches should consider whose perspectives are captured and at what level of analysis. The field would benefit from measurement development comparable to what has occurred for implementation outcomes [71, 72].

Practical implications

This taxonomy has implications for how dissemination research is designed, reviewed, funded, and connected to related efforts across the translational pathway, particularly in the context of federal priorities that increasingly emphasize rigorous dissemination science. For researchers, specifying dissemination outcomes prospectively sharpens study design by clarifying what a dissemination effort is intended to accomplish, at what level, for which audiences, and what should be measured to determine whether it succeeded. Rather than proposing to “disseminate findings” as a general activity, researchers can specify which outcomes they intend to affect (e.g., exposure and comprehension among clinic leaders, perceived fit among policymakers) and select dissemination strategies and measurement approaches accordingly. This specificity also supports the design of dissemination experiments, which remain rare but are essential for building causal evidence about how dissemination works [2]. For reviewers and funders, the taxonomy offers a shared vocabulary for evaluating whether proposed dissemination plans are specific enough to generate interpretable findings or whether they remain vague and aspirational. Reviewers can ask: Which outcomes does this study target? Are the proposed measures appropriate for those outcomes? Does the study design support the intended level of inference? These questions parallel the specificity that implementation science now expects for implementation outcomes and strategies.

For practice and policy audiences, clearer specification of dissemination outcomes can support more actionable guidance on how evidence should be communicated to reach the right audiences, be seen as credible and relevant, and move toward use. When dissemination efforts fail, the taxonomy provides language for diagnosing where they failed: Was the evidence never encountered (exposure)? Encountered but not understood (comprehension)? Understood but not trusted (credibility)? Trusted but not seen as relevant or a priority (salience)? Seen as a priority but not feasible to act on (perceived fit)? Feasible but lacking leadership support (leadership endorsement)? Endorsed but lacking organizational capacity (action readiness)? Ready but no formal commitment made (decision to implement)? Each diagnosis points to different corrective strategies. This diagnostic precision is especially important when research enters adversarial settings where evidence is scrutinized in court cases, policy debates, or amid broader mistrust of health research [60].

Although we developed this taxonomy within U.S.-based dissemination science, the outcomes we describe map onto concerns central to knowledge mobilization and knowledge translation traditions in Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, and other contexts where research funding agencies expect explicit plans for moving evidence toward use [38, 41]. Shared outcome language across these traditions could support comparison and learning across contexts.

This taxonomy is designed to function as part of an integrated set of tools for dissemination science. The DRLM specifies the full causal pathway of the dissemination process, from what is being disseminated and its intended audiences through to dissemination outcomes; [15] this taxonomy operationalizes the outcome component of that model. These tools support researchers in planning what they are disseminating, to whom, through what strategies and channels, toward which outcomes, and with what level of empirical rigor.

Our eight dissemination outcomes are organized proximally to distally, in contrast to Proctor and colleagues’ implementation outcomes, which are sequential by category but not within categories. Dissemination and implementation are iteratively related, not linearly sequenced. Implementation generates new evidence, adaptations, and unintended consequences that themselves require dissemination, a dynamic articulated in the Dynamic Sustainability Framework [73] and the RE-AIM outcomes cascade [46]. Sustained progress on the evidence-to-practice gap depends on attending to each pass of this cycle, and these tools give researchers language for specifying what each pass should accomplish.

Limitations and future research

Several limitations warrant consideration. We have not yet validated this taxonomy empirically. We developed and illustrated it within the ATN, a U.S.-based research network focused on adolescent HIV, and empirical work is needed to assess whether these distinctions hold across health topics, settings, and populations. Global health contexts or settings with different infrastructures may surface outcomes not adequately captured here. Some outcomes may cluster together, prove redundant, or interact in ways that affect dissemination success. Leadership endorsement without organizational readiness, for instance, may produce commitments that cannot be sustained. Few validated measures exist specifically for dissemination outcomes, and measurement development remains a priority. Empirical testing could include studies that operationalize each outcome with validated measures, designs that compare dissemination strategies across multiple outcomes, and hybrid designs that link dissemination outcomes to downstream implementation outcomes. Commonly used frameworks also risk becoming reified without critical reflection [18], and we encourage researchers to apply this taxonomy thoughtfully rather than adopt it uncritically.

Conclusions

We intend this taxonomy as a starting point, not a definitive classification system. Researchers will find some outcomes more relevant than others depending on what they are disseminating, to whom, and toward what end. Specifying outcomes is most valuable when it prompts careful thinking about what dissemination is designed to accomplish rather than checklist compliance. Our hope is that this taxonomy, alongside the DRLM, provides language that supports clearer communication, enables comparison across studies, and helps distinguish where dissemination ends and implementation begins. As dissemination science continues to develop its own conceptual and methodological infrastructure, shared outcome language will be essential to building the cumulative evidence base the field requires. The utility of this taxonomy will be shaped by continued use, critique, and refinement.
