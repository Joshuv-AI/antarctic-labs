Retinal Vessel Traits and the Risk of Stroke: The Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging
Authors: Alexis O’Neil, Roshan A. Welikala, Sarah Barman, Christopher G. Owen, Alicja R. Rudnicka, Mohan Rakesh, Marie-Hélène Roy-Gagnon, David Maberley, Marie-Jeanne Kergoat, Nicole Paquet, Ellen E. Freeman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeRetinal vascular changes could serve as an early, easily visible indicator of cerebrovascular health. Prior research on retinal vessel traits and stroke has predominantly focused on diameter, while vessel area and tortuosity remain underinvestigated and have yielded inconsistent results. Our goal was to examine longitudinal associations between retinal vessel traits and incident stroke.DesignProspective cohort study.ParticipantsBaseline and 6-year follow-up data from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging Comprehensive Cohort (n = 30 097) were used.MethodsA deep learning algorithm called Quantitative Analysis of Retinal Vessel Topology and Size extracted data from retinal images including arteriolar and venular diameter, area, and tortuosity. Participants were asked if a doctor had previously diagnosed them with stroke. Logistic regression was used to adjust for demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors. Interactions with sex were also assessed.Main Outcome MeasuresStroke.ResultsHaving wider arterioles at baseline was associated with lower odds of stroke development over 6 years (odds ratio [OR] = 0.84, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.74‑0.95). Having larger venular area was associated with a higher odds of developing stroke (OR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.11‑2.63), whereas larger arteriolar area showed the inverse association (OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.44‑0.86). Statistically significant interactions were found between sex and both arteriolar and venular tortuosity (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThis study confirms that retinal vessel area and stroke are associated. Vessel area and arteriolar width are associated with the development of stroke, which may help to develop stroke prediction models in the future.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.

Stroke occurs when a cerebral infarction or hemorrhage disrupts blood flow and subsequently causes acute damage to the brain tissue.1 It is the second leading cause of death globally and the third leading contributor to disability, often resulting in cognitive or motor impairments.2 Most strokes stem from systemic vascular etiologies such as small vessel disease, large artery disease, or cardio-embolism and well-established risk factors including diabetes and hypertension.1

The retina shares anatomical, physiological, and embryological characteristics with the brain. Therefore, retinal vascular changes could serve as an early, easily visible indicator of cerebrovascular health.3,4 Systematic reviews have shown that a variety of quantitative retinal vessel measures have been associated with clinical and subclinical stroke, its recurrence, and stroke-related mortality.4,5 Specifically, narrower retinal arteriolar diameter and wider venular diameter have been linked to an increased risk of stroke across several prospective studies.6, 7, 8, 9 A meta-analysis of 6 cohort studies comprised of 20 798 subjects also confirmed that wider venular diameter was associated with incident stroke.10 Overall, these findings highlight the potential utility of retinal vessel diameter in assessing cerebrovascular risk.

While prior research on retinal vessel traits and stroke has predominantly focused on diameter, other parameters that reflect the overall retinal vessel pattern such as vessel area and tortuosity (curvature) remain underinvestigated and have yielded inconsistent results. For example, Cheung et al found null associations between arteriolar and venular tortuosity and incident stroke.9 However, in a study of individuals with type 2 diabetes, Sandoval-Garcia et al reported a positive association between arteriolar tortuosity and incident stroke.11 To the best of our knowledge, only one published study has investigated retinal vessel area and stroke reporting an inverse association.12

Given the few longitudinal studies that have examined retinal vessel traits like area and tortuosity, we aimed to examine the longitudinal associations between retinal vessel diameter, area, and tortuosity with incident stroke in a large cohort of participants from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA). We will also investigate differences by sex because they have previously been reported.6,10

Methods

Study Population and Design

Longitudinal analyses were conducted using data from the CLSA Comprehensive Cohort.13 This study includes 30 097 Canadian adults aged 45 to 85 years, recruited through random sampling of provincial health care databases and random digit dialing of landline phones. Baseline data were gathered between 2012 and 2015, with follow-up 2 data collected 6 years later, from 2018 to 2021. Data collection involved in-home interviews and physical assessments at CLSA data collection sites in cities across Canada, including Victoria, Vancouver, Surrey, Calgary, Winnipeg, Hamilton, Ottawa, Montreal, Sherbrooke, Halifax, and St. John's.

Participants aged 45 to 85 years were eligible if they lived in the community at recruitment, spoke English or French, and resided within 25 to 50 km of a data collection site. Exclusion criteria included cognitive impairment at recruitment, full-time membership in the Canadian Armed Forces, living on a federal First Nations reserve or settlement, residing in a long-term care facility at recruitment, or not being a permanent resident or citizen. The research adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent. Research Ethics Board approval was acquired for all CLSA sites in July 2010. The University of Ottawa Office of Research Ethics and Integrity gave approval for the present analysis in January 2023 (H-01-23-8331).

Data Collection

Retinal Images

Nonmydriatic images of the retina were taken in each eye at baseline using a Topcon TRC-NW8 fundus camera with an attached Nikon D90 camera. Images were centered on the macula and had a 45° field-of-view. Most images were saved in JPEG format with a resolution of 4288 × 2848 pixels. Images from one site were saved with a resolution of 4928 × 3264 pixels and were resized to 4288 × 2848 pixels to be consistent with the other images.

Retinal Vessel Traits

Retinal images were processed using a deep learning algorithm called Quantitative Analysis of Retinal Vessel Topology and Size (QUARTZ).14,15 Quantitative Analysis of Retinal Vessel Topology and Size analyzes vessels in fundus images across the inner retinal layer. These vessels, supplied by the ophthalmic artery via the central retinal artery, include arterioles, precapillaries and postcapillaries, and venules. Vessel width was measured using zero-crossings of the second derivative and tortuosity (curvature) was measured using a subdivided chord length method. These methods have been previously well documented and validated.15 In the CLSA data, QUARTZ can determine inadequate image quality with a sensitivity of 91% and a specificity of 100%. Quantitative Analysis of Retinal Vessel Topology and Size can also distinguish between an arteriole and a venule with 96% accuracy using a probability cutoff of 0.8.14 Thousands of measures of diameter, area, and tortuosity from across the entire retinal image were obtained. The mean diameter and tortuosity of each arteriole and venule were then summarized weighted by the vessel segment length for each image. Area was calculated from the average vessel width multiplied by length, summed across vessel segments. Diameter is measured in pixels, while area is presented in kilopixels.2 Tortuosity is a unitless measure.

Stroke, Demographic, Health, and Lifestyle Data

During the interviewer-led survey at baseline and follow-up, participants were asked “if a doctor had ever told them they had a stroke or CVA (cerebrovascular accident).” They were also asked “if a doctor had ever told them they had experienced a ministroke or TIA (transient ischemic attack)?” Mortality data were collected by proxy interviews. Primary and contributing causes of death were obtained. We defined a person as dying from stroke if either their primary or contributing cause of death was reported as stroke.

Participants were also asked about physician diagnoses of diabetes (none, type 1, type 2, or neither), heart disease, or high blood pressure (hypertension). Blood pressure was also measured using the VSM BpTRU monitor (Medaval). Six readings were acquired, and the average of the second through sixth measurements was used for analysis. High blood pressure was defined as either reporting a diagnosis of it or currently having elevated blood pressure (systolic blood pressure ≥130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥80 mmHg). Individuals without high blood pressure did not report a doctor diagnosis and had normal blood pressure (systolic blood pressure <130 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure <80 mmHg).

Weight was measured using a 140-10 Healthweigh Digital Physician Scale, while height was measured using a Seca 213 stadiometer. Body mass index was calculated as weight (kg)/height squared (m2) and classified according to the World Health Organization categories (underweight <18.5 kg/m2, normal weight 18.5–24.9 kg/m2, overweight 25.0–29.9 kg/m2, and obese ≥30.0 kg/m2).

Demographic information, including age, sex, income, and race/ethnicity, was collected at baseline through an interviewer-administered questionnaire. Household income was assessed by asking participants “What is your best estimate of the total household income received by all household members, from all sources, before taxes and deductions, in the past 12 months?” Participants were grouped into 5 categories: >$100K, $100 to $50K, $50K to $20K, <$20K, and Refused.

Smoking status was determined using answers from 2 questions: “Have you smoked at least 100 cigarettes in your life?” and “At the present time, do you smoke cigarettes daily, occasionally (at least once in last 30 days), or not at all (not in last 30 days)?” Current smokers were defined as those who had smoked ≥100 cigarettes and currently smoke daily or occasionally. Former smokers were those who reported smoking ≥100 cigarettes in life but had not smoked in the last 30 days. Nonsmokers were defined as individuals who had smoked <100 cigarettes in their lifetime. Similarly, frequency of alcohol consumption was assessed by asking participants “About how often during the past 12 months did you drink alcohol?” Responses were categorized into never, occasionally (0–3 times a month), weekly (between 1 and 5 times a week), or daily (≥6 times a week).

Blood Tests

Nonfasting blood samples were collected via venipuncture during the data collection site visit and analyzed at the respective site laboratory. Several soluble and cellular biomarkers were measured in serum including total cholesterol and the concentration of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein using the Cobas 8000 modular analyzer (Roche Diagnostics).

Statistical Analysis

Our primary outcome was nonfatal stroke. Incident nonfatal stroke was defined as the presence of a nonfatal stroke or cerebrovascular accident (CVA) at follow-up 2 in those without a stroke or CVA at baseline. Our secondary outcome was fatal or nonfatal stroke. Incident fatal or nonfatal stroke was defined as the report of a nonfatal stroke or CVA at follow-up 2 in those without a stroke or CVA at baseline or the presence of death by stroke as either a primary or contributing cause by the date of their follow-up 2 examination. Those with and without incident stroke were compared. Logistic regression models were used to assess the associations between each retinal vessel trait (diameter, tortuosity, and area) and the 6-year development of stroke. Each outcome’s model was run separately, containing both the arteriolar and venular trait as there is evidence to suggest a confounding effect between the vessels.3,16 Models were adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, income, hypertension, body mass index, history of heart disease, total cholesterol, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, diabetes, alcohol, smoking, and province. Effect modification by sex was assessed to determine whether the relationship between vessel traits and outcomes varied across subgroups. This was done by including interaction terms (e.g., sex × vessel diameter) in the statistical models and conducting stratified analyses. The complex survey design (analytic weights and strata) of the CLSA was accounted for in all analyses. Regression models were run using right eye data only because it was not possible to include both eyes and account for both weights and strata in the analyses. A P value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Multiple comparisons were not taken into account.17 Stata Version 16 was used for all analyses.

In supplemental analyses, we investigated whether retinal vessel traits were associated with the 6-year incidence of a transient ischemic attack, reported by 1.9% of the sample. There were no statistically significant associations between retinal vessel diameter, area, or tortuosity with incident transient ischemic attack (Table S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). In addition, to assure that area provides new information apart from diameter, we adjusted area models for diameter. All inferences remained the same.

Discussion

Our work examines the relationship between retinal vessel diameter, area, and tortuosity with the incidence of stroke. We observed that the venular area was associated with a higher odds of developing stroke, and both arteriolar area and diameter were inversely associated with the development of stroke. We also found strong sex differences in the association between arteriolar and venular tortuosity and stroke incidence.

Retinal vessel area has not often been examined.12,18, 19, 20 Area combines information about retinal vessel width and length into one measure. We previously reported that blood pressure, a strong risk factor for stroke,2 was associated with retinal vessel area, diameter, and tortuosity.20 Only one study, to our knowledge, has previously examined retinal vessel area and stroke.12 Yusufu et al reported that greater arteriolar vessel area density, as measured with the Retina-Based Microvascular Health Assessment System, was associated with a lower adjusted risk of incident stroke (hazard ratio = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.80‑0.93). Our findings are consistent with theirs. We found that greater arteriolar area was associated with a lower 6-year odds of developing stroke, while a greater venular area was associated with a higher 6-year odds of developing stroke. These associations were very strong and persisted after adjustment for a variety of potential confounding factors. Vessel area and vessel diameter are related to one another but they are not very correlated with correlation coefficients <0.2. Although arteriolar diameter and arteriolar area share similar results with stroke incidence in our data, the same cannot be said for venular diameter and venular area. Indeed, when we adjust area for diameter, it remains statistically significant. More research is needed on retinal vessel area to understand its biological meaning.

We also observed that wider arteriolar diameter was inversely associated with 6-year development of stroke such that a 1-pixel increase in diameter was associated with a 16% lower odds of developing a stroke. This is a moderately sized OR similar in size to what is seen for eating a diet high in fruit and vegetables and its association with stroke.21 Our results are consistent with longitudinal studies6, 7, 8,22 and 3 meta-analyses.23, 24, 25 Previous studies have established links between retinal arteriolar narrowing and known risk factors for stroke including hypertension, reduced large-artery compliance, increased carotid artery stiffness, and reduced aortic distensibility.3,20,26,27 Narrower arterioles may also reflect atherosclerotic changes in the vasculature;25 thromboembolism from atherosclerotic lesions can cause stroke.1 Furthermore, narrowing of retinal blood vessels may indicate cerebral artery stenosis (narrowing), which causes cerebral ischemia and ultimately stroke.28

We were unable to confirm the association between wider venular diameter and the higher risk of stroke reported in meta-analyses and several population-based studies.10,25 The OR was elevated at 1.07, but it was not statistically significant (P = 0.151). Quantitative Analysis of Retinal Vessel Topology and Size takes measures across the entire retinal image, whereas some other analysis software only takes measures near the optic disc. If venular widening is more pronounced near the optic disc in people at a risk of stroke, this may explain the discrepancy in our results. Wider retinal venules have been linked to hypoxia, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammation,29 which play an essential role in the development of stroke.30 We did find that having a larger venular area was associated with the 6-year development of stroke. Also, we previously reported using CLSA data that higher C-reactive protein levels, a marker of inflammation, were associated with venular area.20

There were sex differences in the association between tortuosity and the incidence of stroke. Having more tortuous (curved) arterioles was associated with the incidence of stroke but only in women (OR = 2.17, 95% CI: 1.02‑4.61). This was a statistically significant interaction. By contrast, the ORs for venular tortuosity were in opposite directions for men and women such that there was a higher risk of stroke in men (OR = 1.99, 95% CI: 0.61‑6.47) and a lower risk of stroke in women (OR = 0.38, 95% CI: 011‑1.35). Owen et al also observed using QUARTZ that more tortuous arterioles were associated with prevalent stroke in the EPIC Norfolk study but they did not report sex differences.31 Sandoval-Garcia et al also reported that more tortuous arterioles were associated with long-term risk of stroke in those with type 2 diabetes11 but they did not find sex differences. In contrast, in the Singapore Malay Eye Study, arteriolar tortuosity was not associated with the risk of stroke.9 Although no studies have reported sex differences between retinal vessel tortuosity and stroke, studies examining vessel diameter and stroke have reported sex differences. McGeechan et al10 observed that wider central retinal vein equivalent was associated with incident stroke in men only, but not women, after stratification. Conversely, Seidelmann et al6 assessed stroke as part of a composite outcome among other incident cardiovascular events and showed that narrower arterioles and wider venules were associated with cardiovascular events in women but not in men. Other studies have not reported statistically significant interactions between sex and retinal vessel diameter.10,32,33 Sex differences could indicate different biological mechanisms of stroke by sex34 or these could be chance findings.

The sex differences that we found are aligned with evidence suggesting a more widespread contribution of microvascular dysfunction in relation to cardiovascular disease in women compared to that in men.35 Women generally have more diffuse atherosclerosis and arteriolar dysfunction35 and stroke risk factors often have stronger associations with stroke in women than in men.34 Women also generally tend to have more tortuous arteries, potentially due to hormonal factors.36 Therefore, sex-specific differences in retinal microvascular geometric patterns may contribute to stroke risk. Biologically, our results are supported by evidence showing that more tortuous vessels are common in those with major risk factors for stroke, including hypertension and atherosclerosis, potentially due to increased mechanical stress.36,37 Highly tortuous vessels can restrict or occlude blood flow and may also encourage platelet activation and the formation of blood clots, contributing to stroke development.37,38 Thus, as a reflection of the cerebral and systemic microvasculature, more tortuous arterioles in the retina may represent underlying pathogenic processes related to stroke. Further research to clarify the role of retinal vessel tortuosity on the development of stroke and whether there are any sex differences is warranted.

The strengths of this study are the large sample size, the population-based sample, and the comprehensive data in the form of survey responses and physical assessments including retinal images. A limitation of this work is that stroke was assessed by self-report of a doctor’s diagnosis and we did not have any information on the type of stroke. The prevalence of stroke in the CLSA is 1.5%, which is almost identical to what was self-reported in the Canadian Community Health Survey (1.3%).39 The self-report of stroke has been reported to have a sensitivity of 80% and a specificity of 99%.40 Therefore, some people who actually had a stroke are likely to be misclassified as having reported not having a stroke. However, we would not expect the misclassification of stroke to vary by the level of retinal vessel characteristics. Another limitation is that our population is predominantly of a White, European ancestry background. Results may differ in populations with substantially different ethnic backgrounds. We did test 3 retinal vessel traits in their relationship with stroke and did not adjust for multiple comparisons. Therefore, there is a possibility that our findings are due to chance. Finally, we did not have data on axial length so were unable to convert pixels to microns. However, a rough estimate can be provided of how many microns a pixel equals in the CLSA. The average vertical optic disc diameter in the CLSA was 418 pixels, while the average vertical optic disc diameter in White people in a paper by Quigley et al was 1820 microns.41 Therefore, 1 pixel equals approximately 4.35 microns.

Conclusions

This study provides longitudinal evidence into the relationship between retinal vessel characteristics, including understudied traits such as tortuosity and area, with stroke incidence. More research on the timing of retinal vessel changes and their relationship to the development of stroke would be helpful for use in risk prediction models.12,42,43
