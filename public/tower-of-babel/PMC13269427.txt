On the conjugate weight function and ultradifferentiable classes of entire functions
Authors: Gerhard Schindl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe introduce the new notion of a conjugate weight function and provide a detailed study of this operation and its properties. Then we apply this knowledge to study classes of ultradifferentiable functions defined in terms of fast growing weight functions in the sense of Braun–Meise–Taylor and hence violating standard regularity requirements. Therefore, we transfer recent results shown by the author and D.N. Nenning from the weight sequence to the weight function framework. In order to proceed and to complete the picture we also define the conjugate associated weight matrix and investigate the relation to conjugate weight sequences via the corresponding associate weight functions. Finally, as it has already been done in the weight sequence case, we generalize results by M. Markin from the small Gevrey-setting and show how the corresponding non-standard ultradifferentiable function classes can be used to detect boundedness of normal linear operators on Hilbert spaces (associated with an evolution equation problem). On the one hand, when involving the weight matrix here the crucial information concerning regularity of the weak solutions can be expressed in terms of only one weight, namely of the given weight function. But, on the other hand, for the connection to the weighted entire setting the required conditions on the weight function are too restrictive in the general case.Keywords: Weight functions and weight sequences, Associated weight functions, Conjugate weight function, Growth and regularity properties for functions and sequences, Boundedness of linear operators

Introduction

This article is the direct continuation of the author’s (joint) works [12, 16, 17], and the aim is to transfer the main notions and results from the weight sequence case treated in [12] to the (ultradifferentiable) weight function setting.

In [12] we have been interested in introducing and studying ultradifferentiable function classes defined in terms of non-standard (small) weight sequences M. The goal was to emphasize the meaning of such “exotic classes” which are strictly smaller than the space of all real-analytic functions and, in particular, to generalize results by M. Markin from [10] dealing with small Gevrey sequences, i.e. when Gs:=(p!s)p∈N with 0<s<1, to more general (families) of weight sequences. In order to proceed we have introduced and investigated the notion of the conjugate weight sequence M∗, see [12, Sect. 2.5 and 2.6] and the summary in Sect. 4.3. It has turned out that the ultradifferentiable class defined in terms of a regular small M can be described (as l.c.v.s.) as the weighted space of entire functions defined in terms of the associated weight function ωM∗, see [12, Thm. 3.4]. Moreover, when considering on a Hilbert space H with norm ‖·‖ the evolution equation

with A being a normal (and closed) operator on H, then a priori regularity of the (weak) solutions y of (1.1) expressed in terms of a family F of regular small weight sequences can be used to detect boundedness of the operator A; see the main results [12, Thm. 4.8 and 4.9].

Classically, in the ultradifferentiable framework there also exists a second approach, namely dealing with a weight function ω:[0,+∞)→[0,+∞) (in the sense of Braun–Meise–Taylor) instead. This setting is more recent, its modern form is based on [3] and it is known that both methods are in general mutually distinct; see [2, 14]. Taking into account this observation it is then natural to study the following problem:

Transfer the information, definitions and notions from [12] to the weight function setting and then investigate the similarities and differences w.r.t. the weight sequence case.

The content of this article is to answer this question in detail. Indeed, the following two motivations are of particular interest:

First, in order to proceed it is required to introduce the notion of the conjugate weight function ω∗ and this operation seems to be new in the literature; see (3.1). The definition has been motivated by the Gevrey-type weights studied in Example 3.3. We work in a general setting, more precisely we consider weight functions in the general sense from [6, 17], see Definition 2.1. This gives the possibility to use the obtained information dealing with the conjugate operation ω↦ω∗ in different contexts, hence the new results admit applications to other weighted settings as well and we expect that ω∗ has meanings in various directions. However, in order to get that ω∗ is again a weight function one crucially requires t=o(ω(t)) as t→+∞, see Lemma 3.1, thus ω has to be growing fast and this condition is non-standard especially in the BMT-weight function setting.

Second, recall that with any weight function ω satisfying basic growth properties (“BMT-weights”) one can associate a weight matrix Mω={W(ℓ):ℓ>0}, i.e. a one-parameter family of weight sequences with parameter ℓ>0. Mω can be used equivalently to describe the corresponding ultradifferentiable spaces; this is shown in [14, 20] and see Sect. 5.2 for precise definitions and a summary. Thus it seems to be possible and reasonable to consider F=Mω in the results from [12, Sect. 4] generalizing M. Markin’s approach. In this situation the crucial information concerning regularity of (weak) solutions from (1.1) can be expressed in terms of one given weight function ω in a unified way. From this point of view the weight function setting seems to be more natural for the problem studied by M. Markin since by involving the matrix parameter the weight ω admits naturally more flexibility in order to test regularity of the solutions from (1.1).

Related to these comments and in order to complete the picture it is also the problem to see how the two notions of conjugation in the weight sequence and weight function setting are related to each other and how they are interacting. More precisely, the goal is to understand the connections between the functions ωM∗ and ωM∗ and between the matrices Mω∗:={(W(ℓ))∗:ℓ>0} and Mω∗.

The paper is structured as follows: First, in Sect. 2 we gather all relevant definitions and growth conditions concerning weight functions. In Sect. 3 we define the conjugate weight function ω∗ and provide a detailed study of this operation: In Sect. 3.1 we investigate basic properties of ω∗ and introduce also the double-conjugate ω∗∗; in Sect. 3.2 we show how the conjugate operation is interacting with the known growth relations between weight functions from Sect. 2.2. Then, in Sect. 3.3 we study the interaction of the conjugate with the generalized lower and upper Legendre envelopes ⋆ˇ and ⋆^ from [17], see the summary in Sect. 2.4 and the main result Proposition 3.9. In Sect. 3.4 we show how the conjugate operation is modifying the growth indices γ(·) and γ¯(·) for weight functions defined and studied in [6], see Theorem 3.12 (for precise definitions of γ(·) and γ¯(·) see Sect. 2.3). Section 4 is dedicated to the weight sequence setting; i.e. when ω≡ωM and M∈R>0N satisfies basic growth properties. In Corollary 4.10 it is shown that both ωM∗ and ωM∗ are simultaneously well defined. The main result is Theorem 4.15 (and its Corollary 4.17) where the expected relation between ωM∗ and ωM∗ is established. Analogously as before, in Lemma 4.11 we investigate the interaction with growth relations (between weight sequences) and Theorem 4.13 provides information about the interplay with the generalized lower and upper Legendre envelopes.

Then, in Sect. 5 we apply all this information to a BMT-weight function ω and its associated matrix Mω. We introduce the corresponding conjugate associated matrix Mω∗ (see (5.7)) and study its behavior in Proposition 5.5: On the one hand this set is always well defined but, on the other hand, the identity Mω∗=Mσ for a BMT-weight σ already implies the restriction that both matrices Mω and Mω∗ are constant, i.e. all sequence are equivalent, and hence one loses flexibility w.r.t. the matrix parameter.

Similarly, this happens in the main results Theorems 5.6 and 5.7 where it is shown that assumption γ¯(ω)<1 is convenient to obtain more information but also restrictive. In view of this it turns out that whenever the conjugate objects enjoy desired standard growth conditions, then this forces already for the originally given weight (matrix) the restriction mentioned before and the fact that the (ultradifferentiable) classes defined via ω (resp. equivalently Mω) can already be described and defined via a single weight sequence; see the comments in Remark 5.8 and Sect. 5.4. All results mentioned so far can have more applications for other analogously defined weighted settings since we exclusively investigate weights and their growth properties.

Moreover, in Sect. 6 we provide a concrete application and transfer the results from [12, Sect. 4] to the BMT-weight function setting. On the one hand it turns out that some statements hold without any further assumption which is basically due to the fact that (each) W(ℓ) is automatically very regular; i.e. it belongs to the set LC (see Sect. 4.1). But, on the other hand, the technical issue described in the previous paragraph (concerning Sect. 5) also yields difficulties and restrictions of the generality in this direction: By the explanations in (d) in Sect. 6 the technical result [12, Prop. 4.5] has to be modified resp. generalized accordingly; see Proposition 6.2. This result is required for constructing a crucial specific (uniform bound) sequence a for the matrix Mω which is then used to obtain Corollary 6.5 and Theorem 6.6 even in the general (non-constant) case. (Proposition 6.2 (also) slightly improves the results in [12, Sect. 4].) In Proposition 6.9 we construct a “non-constant example” of a weight function to which Theorem 6.6 can be applied; here ω=ωM with M satisfying appropriate growth conditions and Theorem 6.6 holds for the non-constant matrix MωM. However, in the second main result Theorem 6.8 when combining the information from Theorem 6.6 and the equivalence from [12, Thm. 3.4], see also Corollary 4.16 and Sect. 5.4, then by the required assumptions on the weight in the latter result establishing the isomorphism to certain weighted spaces of entire functions, again we have to restrict to the constant case. Therefore, the constructed example also illustrates the different nature of Theorems 6.6 and 6.8.

Finally, in Appendix A we study the property that ωM is slowly varying (see (2.3)) in terms of the defining sequence M; see Proposition A.1. This question is inspired by the reflexivity of a crucial growth relation from Sect. 2.2 and concrete applications of this technical statement are expected, especially when constructing (counter-)examples.

Ultradifferentiable Braun–Meise–Taylor classes as weighted entire spaces

We are interested in establishing an isomorphism between E[ω], defined via fast growing (non-standard) weights ω, and weighted spaces of entire functions expressed in terms of ω∗ or related conjugate (associated) weight functions. Thus the aim is to transfer the main statement [12, Thm. 3.4] from the weight sequence setting to the BMT-weight function setting. As in [12, Thm. 3.4] this isomorphism is provided via the extension operator E:f↦∑k=0+∞f(k)(x0)k!(z-x0)k, x0∈I fixed, z∈C. Here I⊆R denotes an interval and E is defined on E[ω](I,H); i.e. the functions f are considered to be H-valued with H a Hilbert space. These spaces are defined analogously to the usual scalar-valued Braun–Meise–Taylor classes from [3] and for precise definitions concerning the weight sequence spaces E[M](I,H) see [12, Sect. 2.3 and 2.4]. For the forthcoming comments and notions concerning weighted entire spaces, as in Corollary 4.16, we refer to [12, Sect. 3] and [24].

When inspecting the proof of [12, Thm. 3.4] it turns out that when choosing h:=1 there the first part can be transferred for any ω∈W0 satisfying t=o(ω(t)) as t→+∞: Indeed, by Lemma 5.4 each ω(W(ℓ))∗ is well defined and then, in the Roumieu case, for some A,ℓ>0 and all z∈C we have the estimate ‖E(f)‖≤2Aexp(ω(W(ℓ))∗(2|z|)). Similarly, in the Beurling case, for all ℓ>0 there exists A>0 such that this estimate is valid for any z∈C. Thus the operator E is continuous having a similar target space compared to the one from [12, Thm. 3.4]: More precisely, the parameter is subject to the weight sequences in the matrix Mω.

Note that for ω one might expect the corresponding “exponential-type weight function system” as target space and which seems to be more natural: In the Roumieu case that there exist C,ℓ>0 such that the bound on the right-hand side is given by Cexp(ℓω∗(|z|)) for all z∈C whereas in the Beurling case for all ℓ>0 there exists C>0 such that the previous estimate holds for all z∈C. Again the crucial parameter ℓ>0 is subject to the associated weight sequences.

But in view of Theorem 4.15 resp. Corollary 4.17, (ii) in Theorem 5.6 (especially (5.9)) a connection between both weighted entire notions without any additional assumption on ω is unclear.

However, in any case, by inspecting the second part of the proof where the continuity of the inverse (restriction) mapping is studied, it turns out that the log-convexity of the conjugate sequence M∗ is indispensable, see (4.3) and Lemma 4.3, and by (b) in Sect. 4.3 in the weight function case one requires then the log-concavity for (each) w(ℓ). But (iii)(a) in Theorem 5.6 yields the fact that under this assumption both Mω and Mω∗ are constant and ω satisfies (ω6) and this is too restrictive in the general case. Indeed, for applying (iii)(a) in Theorem 5.6 even the more general assumption that some ϑp(ℓ)pp is almost decreasing is sufficient to conclude.
