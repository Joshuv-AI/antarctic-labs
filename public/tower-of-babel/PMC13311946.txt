Context

Multimorbidity is the presence of two or more chronic health conditions within an individual. Delivering programmes that enhance health literacy and support self-management is recognised for improving patient outcomes for those living with multimorbidity. Self-management entails an individual’s active engagement in monitoring symptoms, recognising early signs of deterioration that may require medical intervention, managing treatment plans and initiating lifestyle changes that support better health outcomes. The high prevalence of multimorbidity and negative patient outcomes among culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) populations highlights the importance of nurturing the knowledge and skills to self-manage multimorbidity.1 However, there remains a gap in evidence regarding strategies to adequately support individuals from CALD backgrounds in developing self-management capabilities for multimorbidity.

Methods

This study2 used a qualitative descriptive design to explore the barriers and facilitators influencing self-management of multimorbidity among CALD migrants residing in an eastern province in Canada. Adopting purposive and snowball sampling techniques, 20 adult participants who self-identified as black or a person of colour, living with two or more self-reported chronic conditions, participated in the study. The sample consisted of 20 participants, comprising women (n=12) and men (n=8), the majority of whom were aged 40 years or older. Most participants self-identified as black (n=7), while the remainder identified as Indian (n=3), Sri Lankan (n=2), Iranian (n=2), Bangladeshi (n=2), Pakistani (n=2) and Afghani (n=2). Semi-structured interviews were used to gather data (6 virtual and 14 face-to-face). Reflexive thematic analysis was used to analyse data.

Findings

The findings reveal that CALD migrants experience significantly more barriers than facilitators in self-managing their multimorbidity. Barriers to self-management were identified, including challenges in navigating healthcare services, financial insecurity, language barriers, experiences of racism and limited genuine engagement by healthcare professionals. Combining traditional cultural practices, Western health management approaches and developing resilience to adapt self-management strategies were key facilitators identified. These findings underscore the imperative for implementing tailored interventions that address these barriers and nurture multimorbidity self-management supports that are responsive to the needs of CALD migrants.

Commentary

Providing responsive healthcare that meets the needs of a diverse and growing CALD patient population is a global health priority. However, evidence highlights systemic barriers to healthcare access and deficiencies in standards of care for CALD patients.3 4 It is essential to critically evaluate the cultural alignment of healthcare services, focusing efforts to enhance accessibility, inclusivity and cultural responsiveness. Culturally responsive practice is a complex intervention that involves adapting practices to reflect the cultural and linguistic needs of patients and requires individual practitioner capabilities, institutional support and is shaped by broader societal and political influences.5

Rising incidence of multimorbidity among CALD populations emphasises the importance of implementing targeted support for individuals managing these health conditions.1 However, Monari2 reports on the range of barriers that influence the self-management capabilities of CALD populations. Fostering preparedness for self-management of multimorbidity among this population necessitates tailored and culturally responsive practices that nurture health literacy and self-management skills. It requires meaningful engagement with patients to understand their culturally informed perceptions of multimorbidity, self-management and their expectations of healthcare services. Recognising, respecting and sensitively addressing these cultural nuances is crucial. Furthermore, tailoring and co-producing health literacy intervention strategies to meet the specific multimorbidity self-management needs of patients from CALD backgrounds is essential. Cultural values, health beliefs about illness and health-seeking behaviours influence how people access and what they expect from the content and delivery of health information, laying the foundation of understanding and applying this information.6 It is essential to overcome language barriers and implement effective cross-cultural communication strategies. Without these, there is a heightened risk of cultural misunderstandings and misinterpretation of health information, breaching human rights standards. Finally, involving CALD service users with lived experience, along with community and voluntary groups that support CALD populations, in the design of culturally responsive health literacy interventions is paramount.6
