Sinkhorn Treatment Effects: A Causal Optimal Transport Measure
Medha Agarwal, Alex Luedtke

Abstract
We introduce the Sinkhorn treatment effect, an entropic optimal transport measure of divergence between counterfactual distributions. Unlike classical quantities such as the average treatment effect, this measure captures differences across entire distributions. We analyze this divergence as a statistical functional and show it can be written as a smooth transformation of counterfactual mean embeddings with an appropriate kernel. This characterization allows us to establish first-order pathwise differentiability in general, and second-order pathwise differentiability under the null hypothesis of equal counterfactual distributions. Leveraging this smoothness, we construct debiased estimators and use them to obtain asymptotically valid tests for distributional treatment effects with a fixed entropic regularization parameter. Because the power of the test depends on this unknown parameter, we further propose an aggregated test that combines evidence across a grid of regularization choices. Experiments on simulated and image data demonstrate the practical advantages of our estimator and testing procedure.

Introduction

Estimating causal treatment effects is a central goal of causal inference
(
63
;
64
;
62
;
12
)
. In the canonical binary treatment setting, the scientific goal is the discrepancy between the counterfactual outcome distributions under treatment and control, denoted by
Y_{1}\sim P_{1}
(treatment) and
Y_{0}\sim P_{0}
(control). However,
P_{1}
and
P_{0}
are never observed via independent samples. Instead, we observe independent copies of
(X,A,Y)\sim P
, where treatment
A
and observed outcome
Y
may depend on confounders

. Under standard causal identifiability conditions (see
Section

2
),
P_{1}
and
P_{0}
can be learned from
P
. Most empirical work summarizes treatment effects through the average treatment effect (ATE),
\mathbb{E}_{P_{1}}[Y_{1}]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[Y_{0}]

(
30
)
.
While convenient for computation and inference, mean-based summaries can mask important effects that occur away from the center of the distribution. Empirical studies have shown that interventions may induce large distributional shifts despite a negligible ATE
(
9
)
.

These limitations motivate inference on the entire counterfactual outcome distributions, commonly referred to as distributional treatment effects (DTEs)
(
1
)
. Classical approaches target specific functionals of
P_{1}
and
P_{0}
, including quantiles
(
20
)
, cumulative distribution functions
(
13
)
—which imply results for the 1-Wasserstein distance in univariate outcome settings
(
41
;
5
)
—and probability density functions
(
60
;
33
)
. More recently, kernel-based methods estimate counterfactual distributions via kernel mean embeddings (KME) into characteristic reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS)
(
50
)
and quantify DTE using maximum mean discrepancy (MMD)
(
18
;
47
;
43
)
. Although MMD metrizes weak convergence, it induces a flat geometry on the space of probability measures and may fail to capture meaningful geometric discrepancies between counterfactual distributions, especially when their supports weakly overlap or separate. Related approaches based on
f
-divergence further require mutual absolute continuity, limiting applicability
(
33
)
.
Unlike
f
-divergences, MMD is finite for disjoint distributions; however, MMD saturates quickly as this disjointness increases, a phenomenon visualized in
Figure

1
.

Figure 1

To overcome these limitations, we propose measuring DTE via the Sinkhorn divergence
(
19
;
56
)
, a centered entropy-regularized optimal transport (EOT) cost, that respects the intrinsic geometry of the outcome space. Under identifiability, we define the
Sinkhorn treatment effect
(STE) as

\text{STE}(P)=\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(P_{1},\,P_{0}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(P_{1},\,P_{1}\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(P_{0},\,P_{0}\right).

(1)

For measures
\mu
and
\nu
, the EOT cost with quadratic cost function
c(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}
and
\varepsilon>0
regularization is

\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int c(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y)+\varepsilon\mathrm{KL}(\pi\mid\mu\otimes\nu),

(2)

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
denotes the set of couplings of
(\mu,\nu)
, and
\mathrm{KL}(\cdot\mid\cdot)
is the Kullback–Leibler divergence. Here
c
reflects the cost of moving mass between two outcome values, thereby encoding domain-specific notions of discrepancy between outcomes, while
\varepsilon
controls the degree of entropic smoothing. As
\varepsilon\to 0
, STE approaches the corresponding unregularized OT cost, and as
\varepsilon\to\infty
, it approaches the MMD distance corresponding to the Gibbs kernel
e^{-c/\varepsilon}
. More broadly, Sinkhorn divergence interpolates between the OT cost and MMD
(
19
)
.

We show that STE admits a pathwise differentiable representation, enabling construction of a doubly robust, asymptotically normal one-step estimator
(
55
)
. While this first-order estimator is degenerate under the null of equal counterfactual outcome distributions, mirroring known degeneracy phenomena for debiased MMD-based tests
(
50
)
, we further develop a second-order influence function-based correction, yielding a valid and powerful test with an
n
-rate limiting distribution.
With our asymptotic result, we construct a test with provable type I error control, for each fixed regularization parameter
\varepsilon>0
. Since
\varepsilon
is unknown in practice and an unfavorable choice may result in low power, we further propose STEAgg, a multiple testing procedure that combines evidence across a grid of
\varepsilon
values. This approach is inspired by aggregated kernel tests such as
66
, which combine MMD statistics across bandwidths while maintaining non-asymptotic type I error guaranties. We prove that STEAgg-based tests achieve nominal asymptotic type I error control.

Our Contributions.

1.

We introduce Sinkhorn treatment effects. Unlike prior causal OT approaches, ours allows for multivariate outcomes.

2.

We characterize the smoothness of the STE functional, showing it is first-order differentiable and second-order differentiable under the null of no DTE.

3.

We use this differentiability to construct efficient first- and second-order bias-corrected estimators of STE.

4.

We propose STE- and STEAgg-based hypothesis tests for the causal null, at a fixed
\varepsilon>0
and aggregated over a finite grid of
\varepsilon
values, respectively.

5.

We demonstrate the practical advantages of our approach on simulated and high-dimensional image data.

Sinkhorn Divergence.

The EOT problem defined in (
2
) between measures
\mu
and
\nu
admits a unique optimal coupling

. The dual EOT problem admits optimal potentials
(\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)},\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)})\in L^{1}(\mu)\times L^{1}(\nu)
called entropic potentials. The entropic potentials are unique up to an additive constant; see

B.2

\frac{d\pi^{(\mu,\nu)}}{d(\mu\otimes\nu)}=\exp\left(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\big(\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}\oplus\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}-c\big)\right),

(5)

where
(\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}\oplus\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)})(y_{1},y_{2})=\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}(y_{1})+\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}(y_{2})
, and

. Since
\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\mu\right)\neq 0
, the EOT cost is not a divergence. The Sinkhorn divergence
(
56

corrects this through the centering

{S}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)=\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}[\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\mu\right)+\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\nu,\,\nu\right)].

(6)

It admits dual representation
{S}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)=\mu\upsilon_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}+\nu\upsilon_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}
where
(\upsilon_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)},\upsilon_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)})
are the
centered entropic potentials

\upsilon_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}:=\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}-\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\mu)},\quad\upsilon_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}=\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}-\varphi_{0}^{(\nu,\nu)}.

Higher-order Efficient Influence Function.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a nonparametric statistical model and
\Phi:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}

For any regular parametric submodel

\tfrac{d}{dt}\Phi(P_{t})|_{t=0}=\langle\dot{\Phi}_{P},s\rangle_{L^{2}(P)}

This representation directly generates the usual notion of a gradient from calculus, with the EIF playing the role of a gradient and the score playing the role of a direction along which an input to a function is perturbed. The EIF can be used to construct a one-step estimator that corrects for first-order plug-in bias and is semiparametrically efficient under regularity conditions. However, for nonnegative functionals such as the STE, first-order linearization may be insufficient under boundary null hypotheses where the first-order term vanishes
(
42

Following

\ddot{\Phi}_{P}\in L^{2}(P^{\otimes 2})

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\Phi(P_{t})\Big|_{t=0}=\mathbb{E}_{P^{\otimes 2}}[\ddot{\Phi}_{P}(Z_{1},Z_{2})\,r(Z_{1},Z_{2})],

where

is the second-order score function
(
69

. For paths of the form
P_{t}=(1+ts)P
, this reduces to

. In a nonparametric model, there is only one second-order influence function, and this is known as the second-order

influence function
(
57

Section

B.1
.

An unfortunate fact uncovered in the literature is that most functionals of interest fail to be smooth enough to admit a second-order EIF
(
57
;
77

3
Sinkhorn Treatment Effect

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}
denote a data-generating distribution and fix entropic regularization
\varepsilon>0
. We define the Sinkhorn treatment effect from (
1

\mathscr{S}:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\mathscr{S}(P)=S_{\varepsilon}\circ J\circ\Psi(P).

In this chaining argument, first,
\Psi
maps
P
to the pair of counterfactual mean embeddings defined in (
4

J
is the canonical isometric embedding of
\mathcal{H}
in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
, as discussed in
Section

2
. For notational convenience, we use
J
to denote the induced canonical embedding of the product space
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
. Lastly,
S_{\varepsilon}
computes the Sinkhorn divergence between the counterfactual distributions.

The goal of this section is to characterize the smoothness properties of
\mathscr{S}
and leverage them for efficient semiparametric estimation. Our analysis builds on recent advances in the differentiability theory of EOT
(
24
;
36
)
. In particular,
24
analyze the Hadamard differentiability of the map
(\mu,\nu)\mapsto{S}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)
in the
\ell^{\infty}(B^{s})\times\ell^{\infty}(B^{s})
topology, where

is a unit ball in
\mathcal{C}^{s}(\mathcal{Y})
. Following this, we embed
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
into the normed linear space
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
via the KME, and view the Sinkhorn divergence as a smooth functional
S_{\varepsilon}:\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\subset\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\times\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\to\mathbb{R}

Appendix

C

We show that this smoothness propagates through the causal composition defining

and second-order pathwise differentiability under the null that

is termed as the Hadamard operator of Sinkhorn divergence in
36

25

T_{\mu}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}

T_{\mu}
has a nontrivial null space spanned by constant functions, rendering it non-invertible on
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})

T_{\mu}
is a well-defined linear operator on
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}
, and
(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1}
exists and is a bounded linear operator on
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}

(
38
, see Section 3 of)
.

Since
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}
is canonically dual to
\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
, the bilinear form
\gamma_{0}^{\prime}K_{\mu}\gamma_{0}
is well-defined for all
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
. As shown in
36

K_{\mu}
is precisely the Hadamard operator of Sinkhorn divergence, governing the second-order expansion of the Sinkhorn divergence. We denote by
k_{\mu}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
the associated symmetric kernel, representing the bilinear form
\gamma_{1}(K_{\mu}\gamma_{2})
as
(\gamma_{1}\otimes\gamma_{2})k_{\mu}
for
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
. The explicit form of
k_{\mu}
is derived in
Lemma

C.1
. Letting
\mathscr{H}_{0}:=\{P\in\mathcal{P}:P_{1}=P_{0}\}
with
(P_{1},P_{0})
as defined in (

), we now state our second-order pathwise differentiability result.

Theorem 3.2

(Second-order differentiability under the null)
.

At any
P\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
,
\mathscr{S}:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}
is second-order pathwise differentiable with the second-order efficient influence function

\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P}=\left(I-\left(I-(I-P_{X})P_{A\,|\,X}\right)P_{Y\,|\,A,X}\right)^{\otimes 2}g_{P},

(10)

where
g_{P}(z_{1},z_{2})=\omega_{P}(a_{1},x_{1})\,\omega_{P}(a_{2},x_{2})\,k_{P_{1}}(y_{1},y_{2})
for
z_{j}=(x_{j},a_{j},y_{j})
and
\omega_{P}(a,x)=(2a-1)/e_{P}(a\,|\,x)
.

For shorthand, we will occasionally write
g_{P}=\omega_{P}^{\otimes 2}\odot k_{P_{1}}
,
identifying
\omega_{P}^{\otimes 2}
and
k_{P_{1}}
with their canonical extensions
to
\mathcal{Z}^{2}
that ignore the
y
and

coordinates, respectively.

A second-order analysis of
\mathscr{S}
is possible under the null because the first derivative of the Sinkhorn divergence vanishes in these cases. This eliminates, through the second-order chain rule, the term that would ordinarily involve the second-order pathwise derivative of the KME map
\Psi
, whose presence would typically preclude the existence of a second-order EIF
(
77
, pp. 681–682)
.

4
One-Step Estimation of STE

We now leverage the first- and second-order EIFs of STE to construct one-step bias-corrected estimators of
\mathscr{S}(P)
. Let
P^{*}\in\mathcal{P}
be our data-generating distribution and
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
be independent draws from
P^{*}
, with corresponding empirical distribution denoted by
P_{n}
. Let
\widehat{P}
be an initial estimator of
P^{*}
constructed from an independent sample of size
n
. For notational ease, we present our one-step estimators under the sample splitting approach, assuming
P_{n}
and
\widehat{P}
are based on independent data. A cross-fitting approach
(
65
;
35
;
11
)
is recommended, and the resulting efficiency is discussed for both first- and second-order one-step estimators. Our test for the causal null is based on the second-order one-step estimator of
\mathscr{S}(P^{*})
for a fixed
\varepsilon>0
. To reduce sensitivity to the choice of the unknown entropic regularization parameter
\varepsilon
, we also provide to a way to aggregate tests over a finite grid of
\varepsilon
values.

4.1
One-Step Estimator

The one-step estimator of
\mathscr{S}^{*}:=\mathscr{S}(P^{*})
is defined as
\widehat{\mathscr{S}}:=\mathscr{S}(\widehat{P})+P_{n}\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}
. We study conditions under which a cross-fitted version of
\widehat{\mathscr{S}}
is asymptotically linear and semiparametrically efficient. As is standard in semiparametric theory, efficiency follows if
\widehat{\mathscr{S}}-\mathscr{S}(P^{*})=P_{n}\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}+o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
, in which case the asymptotic variance attains the efficiency bound
(
7
;
11
)
.
This expansion is obtained via the decomposition
\widehat{\mathscr{S}}-\mathscr{S}(P^{*})-P_{n}\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}=\mathcal{R}_{n}+\mathcal{D}_{n}
, where
\mathcal{R}_{n}:=\mathscr{S}(\widehat{P})+P^{*}\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}-\mathscr{S}(P^{*})
is the remainder term from first order von Mises expansion and
\mathcal{D}_{n}:=(P_{n}-P^{*})(\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}-\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}})
is a stochastic drift term. Under appropriate convergence rates for the nuisance components of
\widehat{P}
, we show that both terms are
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
in
Section

E.1
.
Consequently,
\widehat{\mathscr{S}}
is asymptotically linear, satisfying the weak convergence

\sqrt{n}\big(\widehat{\mathscr{S}}-\mathscr{S}^{*}\big)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\big(0,\mathbb{E}_{P^{*}}\big[\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}^{2}(Z)\big]\big).

(11)

This asymptotic normality enables the construction of asymptotically valid Wald-type confidence intervals for
\mathscr{S}^{*}
.

Under the null hypothesis (
P^{*}\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
), the first-order EIF is degenerate, i.e.
\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{P}=0
, since
\upsilon_{1}^{(P_{1},P_{1})}=\upsilon_{0}^{(P_{1},P_{1})}=0
. As a consequence, the first-order limit distribution in (
11
) collapses to a point mass, rendering the first-order inference invalid. To address this, we construct a second-order one-step estimator based on a Newton–Raphson bias correction, in the spirit of
57
, which attains
n
-rate convergence rate to
\mathscr{S}^{*}
under the null. Inference based on this second-order expansion yields asymptotically valid hypothesis tests for detecting DTEs, with the second-order one-step estimator serving as the test statistic.

The second-order one-step estimator for
\mathscr{S}^{*}
is defined as

\overline{\mathscr{S}}:=\mathscr{S}(\widehat{P})+P_{n}\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{U}_{n}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}},

(12)

where
\mathbb{U}_{n}
denotes the canonical U-statistic operator from the same samples as
P_{n}
. However, second-order pathwise differentiability of
\mathscr{S}
is only established under the null, and therefore the second-order EIF
\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}
need not be well-defined at a general
\widehat{P}\notin\mathscr{H}_{0}
. To address this, we construct an extension of the second-order EIF to distributions
P\notin\mathscr{H}_{0}
. This extension is defined by the same analytic expression as in (
10
), where for notational simplicity we denote this extension by the same symbol,
\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P}
. Since
k_{P_{1}}\neq k_{P_{0}}
when
P\notin\mathscr{H}_{0}
, the definition in non-null cases depends on the choice of counterfactual distribution used to construct the self-transport kernel. We fix this choice to
\widehat{P}_{1}
in our theoretical arguments for concreteness, though any convex combination of
\widehat{P}_{1}
and
\widehat{P}_{0}
would enjoy similar guarantees. Note that
\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P}
is already
P
-centered, and therefore, does not require further centering common in second-order one-step estimation
(
42
)
.

4.2
Inference Under Null

We now study the asymptotic properties of (
12
). Define the centering operator
C_{P^{*}}
such that for any measurable kernel
\ell:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
,
C^{*}\ell(z,z^{\prime})
is equal to

\ell(z,z^{\prime})-{P^{*}\ell(\cdot,z^{\prime})-P^{*}\ell(z,\cdot)}+[(P^{*})^{\otimes 2}\ell]\mathbbm{1},

where
\mathbbm{1}
is the constant one function.
The following breakdown is key to the inference of
\overline{\mathscr{S}}
:

\overline{\mathscr{S}}-\mathscr{S}^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{U}_{n}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}=\mathscr{D}_{n}+\mathscr{U}_{n}+\mathscr{R}_{n},

where
\mathscr{D}_{n},\mathscr{U}_{n},\mathscr{R}_{n}
are drift, U-process consistency, and remainder terms, respectively, and are defined as

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{n}

\displaystyle=(P_{n}-P^{*})\left(\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}+\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits\kern-0.99998pt\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}(z,\cdot)\,dP^{*}(z)\right),

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{U}_{n}(C_{P^{*}}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}-C_{P^{*}}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}),

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{S}(\widehat{P})+(P^{*}-\widehat{P})\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}+\frac{1}{2}(P^{*}-\widehat{P})^{2}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}-\mathscr{S}(P^{*}).

In the next result, we show that all three terms above are
o_{P}(n^{-1})
under suitable conditions. Consequently, the asymptotic distribution of
n(\overline{\mathscr{S}}-\mathscr{S}(P^{*}))
is determined by the limiting distribution of
n\mathbb{U}_{n}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}/2
. The drift term
\mathscr{D}_{n}
is
o_{P}(n^{-1})
provided that the integrand
\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}+\int\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}(z,\cdot)\,dP^{*}(z)
is
o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
in
L^{2}(P^{*})
norm (
Lemma

E.1
). This result follows from a conditioning argument that combines Chebyshev’s inequality with the dominated convergence theorem. We prove the desired rate on our integrand in
Lemma

E.5
.
The U-process term
\mathscr{U}_{n}
is also
o_{P}(n^{-1})
using similar arguments using Chebyshev’s inequality, only requiring
L^{2}(P^{*}\otimes P^{*})
convergence of
C_{P^{*}}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}
to
\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}
—see
Lemma

E.6
. Finally, the remainder term
\mathscr{R}_{n}
is
o_{p}(n^{-1})
under regularity conditions commonly satisfied by smooth statistical functionals
(
42
;
58
)
. We verify these conditions for
\mathscr{R}_{n}
in
Lemma

E.8
and discuss the feasibility of the required assumptions later in this section. Similar to the first-order one-step estimator, a cross-fitting approach for
\overline{\mathscr{S}}
is recommended
(
34
)
, as it can improve sample efficiency.

For brevity, we write
P_{Y\,|\,a,X}
for
P_{Y\,|\,A=a,X}
,
\hat{e}\,
for
e_{\widehat{P}}
, and
e^{*}
for
e_{P^{*}}
. Recall
k_{\mu}
is the kernel associated with operator
K_{\mu}
and defined in
Lemma

C.1
. We now state the main theorem on asymptotics of the second-order one-step estimator
\overline{\mathscr{S}}
.

Theorem 4.1

(Null distribution)
.

Let
P^{*}\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
. Suppose:

1.

the estimated nuisance parameters satisfy

P^{*}_{X}\left(\tfrac{\hat{e}(a\,|\,X)}{e^{*}(a\,|\,X)}-1\right)\big({\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,a,X}-P^{*}_{Y\,|\,a,X}}\big)k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(Y,y)=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

uniformly in
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,
a\in\{1,0\}
,

2.

\|C_{P^{*}}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}-C_{P^{*}}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(P^{*}\otimes P^{*})}\xrightarrow{p}0
, and

3.

the von Mises remainder satisfies
\mathscr{R}_{n}=o_{p}(n^{-1})
.

Then,

n\overline{\mathscr{S}}=\frac{n}{2}\mathbb{U}_{n}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}+o_{p}(1)\xrightarrow{d}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{2}(N_{j}^{2}-1),

(13)

where
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
are the eigenvalues of the integral operator
f\mapsto\int\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}(\cdot,z)\,f(z)\,dP^{*}(z)
, counted with multiplicity, and
\{N_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
are iid standard normal random variables.

The proof of
Theorem

4.1
is presented in
Section

E.2
.
We now discuss the feasibility of the conditions. We let the initial estimator
\widehat{P}
of the data-generating distribution
P^{*}
satisfies
\|\widehat{P}_{a}-P^{*}_{a}\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{-r})
for some
1/4<r<1/2
and
a\in\{1,0\}
.
The first condition is standard in the doubly robust causal inference literature
(
43
)
and is implied by
o_{p}(n^{-1/4})
convergence of the nuisance estimators in their appropriate norms. The second condition is a consistency requirement ensuring stability of the second-order term; it follows from convergence of
\omega_{\widehat{P}}^{\otimes 2}\odot k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}
to
\omega_{P^{*}}^{\otimes 2}\odot k_{P^{*}_{1}}
in
L^{2}(P^{*}\otimes P^{*})

over
\widehat{P}\in\mathcal{P}

Lemma

E.8

4.3
Formulation of Test

To test the null hypothesis that
P^{*}\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
, we use the test statistic
n\overline{\mathscr{S}}
. Under the null,
Theorem

4.1
establishes that
n\overline{\mathscr{S}}
converges weakly to a weighted sum of centered chi-squared distributions. A non-conservative test of nominal level
\alpha\in(0,1)
rejects the null hypotheses when
n\overline{\mathscr{S}}
exceeds the
(1-\alpha)
th quantile of the limiting distribution,
q_{1-\alpha}
. The weak convergence in (
13
) implies the corresponding test asymptotically attains nominal type I error:

\limsup_{n\to\infty}P^{*}(n\overline{\mathscr{S}}\geq q_{1-\alpha})=\alpha.

(14)

Moreover, if
\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}
is a consistent estimator of
q_{1-\alpha}
, then the same conclusion holds with
q_{1-\alpha}
replaced by
\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}
in (
14
).
To obtain such an estimator, we use the general approximation strategy of
27
, which was adapted to tests based on second-order influence functions in
42
. This approximates the spectrum of the operator
f\mapsto\int\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}(\cdot,z)f(z)dP(z)
through that of an empirical Gram matrix. In our case, this is the Gram matrix of the kernel
\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}
, which depends on
{P^{*}}
through three nuisance parameters: the propensity
e^{*}
, outcome regression model
P_{Y\,|\,A,X}^{*}
, and entropic potentials
(\upsilon_{1}^{(P_{1}^{*},P_{0}^{*})},\upsilon_{0}^{(P_{1}^{*},P_{0}^{*})})
. Since these nuisances are unknown, we estimate them to obtain
\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}
, form its empirical Gram matrix, and compute its eigenvalues
\{\hat{\lambda}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
. The limiting distribution is then approximated by
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{\lambda}_{j}(N_{j}^{2}-1)
, and its
(1-\alpha)
-th quantile
\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}
is obtained via Monte Carlo simulation. Under sufficient regularity—specifically, if the nuisance estimators converge at rates fast enough to guarantee operator-norm consistency of the plug-in kernel and continuity of the limiting spectral distribution—nominal type 1 error control follows by standard kernel spectral approximation and perturbation arguments
(
68
;
82
;
61
)
.

Regarding power, the proposed test will reject fixed alternatives
P^{*}\not\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
with probability tending to
1
. This follows from the relation
\overline{\mathscr{S}}=\widehat{\mathscr{S}}+\mathbb{U}_{n}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}/2
between the second- and first-order bias-corrected estimators, the approximate 1-degeneracy of
\mathbb{U}_{n}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}
resulting from its
\widehat{P}
-centering, and the asymptotic normality in (
11
)—see
Lemma

E.9
.

4.4
STEAgg: Max-Aggregated Test

In finite samples, power of the STE-based test can depend on the choice of entropic regularization parameter
\varepsilon
: small values cause dispersion in the asymptotic distribution of the U-statistic, while large values oversmooth the discrepancy and reduce power. To reduce sensitivity to this choice, we propose a multiple testing procedure, STEAgg, that aggregates evidence over a finite set of stable candidate regularization parameters
\Xi=\{\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{m}\}\subset(0,\infty)
. We also establish asymptotic nominal type I error guarantee for STEAgg.

For each
\varepsilon\in\Xi
, let
\mathcal{T}_{n,\varepsilon}=n\overline{\mathscr{S}}
denote the test statistic with explicit dependence on
n
and
\varepsilon
. Under the null,
Theorem

4.1
shows that
\mathcal{T}_{n,\varepsilon}\xrightarrow{d}W(\varepsilon)
, where
W(\varepsilon)
is the Gaussian chaos limit in (
13
), induced by the spectral decomposition of the degenerate kernel
\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}
. We define the aggregated test statistic

\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathrm{agg}}=\max_{\varepsilon\in\Xi}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{n,\varepsilon}}{q_{n,\varepsilon}},

(15)

where
q_{n,\varepsilon}
consistently estimates the
(1-\beta)
quantile of
W(\varepsilon)
for a pre-specified
\beta\in(0,1)
. Normalization by
q_{n,\varepsilon}
calibrates each statistic
\mathcal{T}_{n,\varepsilon}
to the scale of its own marginal null distribution. This ensures that aggregation compares evidence for different
\varepsilon
on a common scale, rather than allowing monotonic dependence on
\varepsilon
to dominate the maximum.

Theorem 4.2

(Asymptotic behavior of STEAgg)
.

If the null holds (
P^{*}\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
), the conditions of
Theorem

4.1
hold, and
q_{n,\varepsilon}\xrightarrow{P}q_{\varepsilon}>0
for each
\varepsilon\in\Xi
, then

\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathrm{agg}}\xrightarrow{d}W^{\mathrm{agg}}:=\max_{\varepsilon\in\Xi}W(\varepsilon)/q_{\varepsilon}.

Further, if
P^{*}\notin\mathscr{H}_{0}
is a fixed alternative, the nuisance estimators satisfy the conditions in
Lemma

E.2
, and
Lemma

E.3
, and, for each
\varepsilon\in\Xi
,
\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\varepsilon,P^{*}}\in L^{2}(P^{*}\otimes P^{*})
, and
\|{C_{P^{*}}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\varepsilon,\widehat{P}}-C_{P^{*}}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\varepsilon,P^{*}}}\|_{L^{2}(P^{*}\otimes P^{*})}=o_{p}(1)
, then the proposed test will reject the null with probability converging to
1
as
n\to\infty
.

The proof proceeds by first deriving the joint limit distribution of
(\mathcal{T}_{n,\varepsilon_{1}},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{n,\varepsilon_{m}})
. Then we apply Slutsky’s theorem together with the continuous mapping theorem to obtain the limiting distribution of the maximum—see
Appendix

H
.

5
Finite-Sample Estimation

We derive explicit, computable formulas for the first- and second-order one-step estimators,
\widehat{\mathscr{S}}
and
\overline{\mathscr{S}}
. Nuisance parameters are estimated on one data split, with all remaining computations performed on the empirical distribution of the other data. In finite samples, expectation operators in (
8
) and (
10
) are replaced by matrix operations, kernel evaluations by Gram matrices, and function evaluations by vectors, yielding concise, intuitive, and implementable formulas in closed form.

Sample splitting and nuisance estimation.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{1}=\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{2}=\{z_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be independent
samples from
P
, where
z_{i}=(x_{i},a_{i},y_{i})
. Using
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{2}
, we estimate the nuisances: the
propensity score
e_{P}
, outcome regression
P_{Y\,|\,A,X}
, centered entropic
potentials
(\upsilon_{1}^{(P_{1},P_{0})},\upsilon_{0}^{(P_{1},P_{0})})
, and self-transport entropic potential
\varphi_{1}^{(P_{1},P_{1})}
. Modern nonparametric learning methods can be used for estimating
e_{P}
and
P_{Y\,|\,A,X}
, while entropic potentials should be computed via
the Sinkhorn algorithm
(
14
)
. We denote the resulting estimators by
\hat{e},\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,A,X},\hat{\upsilon}_{1},\hat{\upsilon}_{0},\hat{\varphi}_{1}
. Let
\mathbf{1}_{n}
and
\mathbf{1}_{n\times n}
denotes the all-ones vector and matrix, respectively. Also define the data projection operator
\mathbf{D}_{A,X}
acting on tensors
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times n,p}
by
(\mathbf{D}_{AX}\mathbf{A})_{[i,:]}=\mathbf{A}_{[2-a_{i},i,:]}
.

Precomputations.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be the
n\times n
Gram matrix of
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{1}
. For
i\in[n]
and
a\in\{1,0\}
, define the nuisance evaluations

\mathbf{E}_{i}=\hat{e}(x_{i}),\,\,\mathbf{U}^{a}_{i}=\hat{\upsilon}_{a}(y_{i}),\,\,\mathbf{F}_{i}=\hat{\varphi}_{1}(y_{i}).

Let
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 2\times n}
collect evaluations of
\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,A,X}
, so
\mathbf{P}_{i,j,k}=\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,A,X}(y_{i}\,|\,2-j,x_{k})
, and define the marginal

\mathbf{P}^{1}
(resp.
\mathbf{P}^{0}
) by averaging the treatment (resp. control) slice of
\mathbf{P}
along along the third axis; e.g.,
\mathbf{P}^{1}_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}_{i,1,k}
.

Debiasing.

Using
Algorithms

1
and
2
in
Appendix

G
, we compute the first- and second-order EIF evaluations on
\mathcal{D}_{n}^{1}
, yielding
\mathbf{I}^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbf{I}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. In both cases, the intermediate steps
T_{i}
correspond to the four additive components of the operator
(I-P_{Y\,|\,A,X}+P_{Y,A\,|\,X}-P)
appearing in (
8
) and (
10
). In both algorithms, multiplication by matrix
\mathbf{P}
is done along the
Y
(first) axis of both matrices. The resulting one-step estimator is
\widehat{\mathscr{S}}_{n}=S_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{P}^{1},\mathbf{P}^{0})+\ (1_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{I}^{1})/n
, and the test statistic (

\overline{\mathscr{S}}_{n}=\widehat{\mathscr{S}}_{n}+\left(1_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{I}^{2}1_{n}-\text{Tr}(\mathbf{I}^{2})\right)/2n(n-1).

Treating the generic estimators that yield
\hat{e}
and
\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,A,X}
as black boxes, the procedure’s remaining complexity is

, with

O(n^{2}t)
for the Sinkhorn algorithm
(

)

for

. In particular, low-rank Nyström approximations to the Gibbs kernel matrix used in the Sinkhorn algorithm can reduce the per-iteration Sinkhorn cost from

to

is the Nyström rank. Similar Nyström-based ideas can also be used to accelerate the computations in
Algorithm

1
and
Algorithm

.

7
Conclusion

We propose a novel optimal transport-based DTE that addresses limitations of existing approaches. Unlike kernel-based measures such as MMD, which suffer from flat geometry under limited overlap, and

f
-divergence-based DTEs, which require mutual absolute continuity, our formulation faithfully lifts Euclidean geometry to probability spaces and naturally accommodates discrete outcomes.
Our contributions invite future work in several directions. One involves confronting the numerical instability that can arise from large eigenvalues of
(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1}

(
38
)
. Recent work proposes replacing the Sinkhorn kernel
k_{\mu}
with
\xi_{\mu}
to enable faster computations
(
36
, Lem. 12)
; fully establishing the theoretical validity of this substitution for STE is an open problem. Another is to scale inference to larger datasets. For this, we recommend extending our approach to batched Sinkhorn divergences
(
54
)
.

Appendix B
Background

B.1
Pathwise differentiability and EIF

We refer the reader to
43
for a detailed exposition of pathwise differentiability. We briefly recall the key definitions needed for our analysis.
As introduced in
Section

2
, let
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
denote our statistical model, where
\mathcal{Z}:=\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}\times\mathcal{Y}
is a Polish space equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{Z}}
. We assume that all distributions in
\mathcal{P}
are dominated by a
\sigma
-finite measure
\lambda
.

A parametric submodel
(P_{t}:t\in[0,\delta))\subset\mathcal{P}
with
P_{0}=P
is said to be
quadratic mean differentiable
(QMD) at
P
if there exists a (Fisher) score function
s\in L_{0}^{2}(P)
such that

\big\|\sqrt{p_{t}}-\sqrt{p}-ts\sqrt{p}\big\|_{L^{2}(\lambda)}=o(t),

where
p_{t}=dP_{t}/d\lambda
and
p=dP/d\lambda
. We denote by
\mathfrak{P}(P,\mathcal{P},s)
the collection of all QMD submodels at
P
with score function
s
. The set
\{s\in L_{0}^{2}(P):\mathfrak{P}(P,\mathcal{P},s)\neq\emptyset\}
is called the
tangent set
of
\mathcal{P}
at
P
. Its closed linear span in
L_{0}^{2}(P)
is the
tangent space
, denoted by
\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{P}
.

Let
\Phi:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert-valued parameter. We say that
\Phi
is
pathwise differentiable
at
P
, relative to the model
\mathcal{P}
, if there exists a continuous linear operator
D\Phi_{P}:\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{P}\to\mathcal{H}
such that, for all
(P_{t}:t\in[0,\delta))\in\mathfrak{P}(P,\mathcal{P},s)
,

\big\|\Phi(P_{t})-\Phi(P)-tD\Phi_{P}(s)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}}=o(t).

(16)

The operator
D\Phi_{P}
is called the
local parameter
of
\Phi
at
P
. Its image is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{H}
, denoted by
\dot{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
, and referred to as the local parameter space.
The Hermitian adjoint of
D\Phi_{P}
, denoted by
D^{*}\Phi_{P}:\mathcal{H}\to\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{P}
, is called the
efficient influence operator
. The operators
D\Phi_{P}
and
D^{*}\Phi_{P}
satisfy the adjoint relationship
\left\langle h,D\Phi_{P}(s)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\left\langle D^{*}\Phi_{P}(h),s\right\rangle_{L^{2}(P)}
for all
h\in\mathcal{H},\;s\in\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{P}
.
We say that
\Phi
admits an efficient influence function (EIF) at
P
if, for
P
-almost every
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, the map
h\mapsto D^{*}\Phi_{P}(h)(z)
defines a bounded linear functional on
\mathcal{H}
. In this case, by the Riesz representation theorem, there exists a function
\phi_{P}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{H}
such that

D^{*}\Phi_{P}(h)(z)=\left\langle h,\phi_{P}(z)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\quad\text{for all }h\in\mathcal{H}\text{ and }P\text{-a.e. }z.

(17)

Sufficient conditions for the existence of the EIF when
\mathcal{H}
and
\dot{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
are RKHS are given in
(
43
, Theorem 1)
. In the special case of real-valued parameters,
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}
, a function
\phi_{P}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
is an influence function of
\Phi
at
P
if the local parameter admits the representation

D\Phi_{P}(s)=\left\langle\phi_{P},s\right\rangle_{L^{2}(P)}.

The
efficient
influence function is the unique influence function that lies in the tangent space
\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{P}
, and can therefore be obtained as the orthogonal projection of any influence function onto
\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{P}
. For fully nonparametric models,
\dot{\mathcal{P}}_{P}=L_{0}^{2}(P)
, so the EIF reduces to
\phi_{P}-P\phi_{P}

(
7
)
.

Second-order scores and influence functions

Again, we consider a smooth one-dimensional parametric submodel
(P_{t}:t\in[0,\delta))\subset\mathcal{P}
through
P_{0}=P
, with corresponding densities
p_{t}
with respect to a common dominating measure. For a general treatment of higher-order scores and influence functions in
r
-dimensional submodels (
r\geq 1
), see
57
.

Let
s:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the first-order score function
s(z)=\tfrac{\partial t}{\partial t}p_{t}(z)\big|_{t=0}
. The second-order score function
(
80
;
69
;
77
)

r:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined through the perturbation of the product measure
P_{t}^{\otimes 2}=P_{t}\otimes P_{t}
. Specifically, it is the second derivative of the joint density relative to the baseline product measure
P^{2}
.
A direct calculation is presented below

\displaystyle r(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2p(z_{1})p(z_{2})}\left(\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\left(p_{t}(z_{1})p_{t}(z_{2})\right)\right)\Big|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2p(z_{1})}\frac{d^{2}p_{t}(z_{1})}{dt^{2}}\Big|_{t=0}+\frac{1}{2p(z_{2})}\frac{d^{2}p_{t}(z_{2})}{dt^{2}}\Big|_{t=0}+s(z_{1})s(z_{2}).

Thus, unlike the first-order score, the second-order score is not simply the second derivative of the univariate log-likelihood; rather, it arises from the second-order perturbation of the product measure. In particular, for a linear path
P_{t}=(1+ts)P
, this reduces to the product of scores:
r(z_{1},z_{2})=s(z_{1})s(z_{2})
.

The second-order influence function of
\Phi
at
P
is a symmetric measurable map
\ddot{\Phi}_{P}\in L_{0}^{2}(P^{\otimes 2})
such that
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\ddot{\Phi}_{P}]=0
and the following holds

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Phi(P_{t})\Big|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\iint\ddot{\Phi}_{P}(z_{1},z_{2})(s(z_{1})+s(z_{2}))\,dP(z_{1})\,dP(z_{2}),\quad\textrm{and }

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\Phi(P_{t})\Big|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\iint\ddot{\Phi}_{P}(z_{1},z_{2})\,r(z_{1},z_{2})\,dP(z_{1})\,dP(z_{2}).

(18)

Our presentation of second-order pathwise differentiability differs slightly from that of
57
, in that we carry an extra factor of
1/2
in our expression for
r
and
2
in our expression for the second-order part of
\ddot{\Phi}_{P}
. Concretely, in the notation of
57
, the second-order influence function of
\Phi
at
P
would be
\Gamma(\ddot{\Phi}_{P})
, where
\Gamma:L_{0}^{2}(P^{\otimes 2})\to L_{0}^{2}(P^{\otimes 2})
is the bijective map defined by

\displaystyle\Gamma(f):(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto\int[f(z_{1},z)+f(z,z_{2})]dP(z)+\frac{1}{2}\left[f(z,z_{2})-\int[f(z_{1},z)+f(z,z_{2})]dP(z)\right].

In the case where the first-order EIF is 0 a.s., the first-order part of
\ddot{\Phi}_{P}
is also
0
, and so our second-order EIF is twice that from
57
, that is,
\ddot{\Phi}_{P}=2\Gamma(\ddot{\Phi}_{P})
.

B.2
Entropic Optimal Transport

We briefly review entropic optimal transport. We refer to
39
and
14
for comprehensive treatments. Throughout, we work on the space of Borel probability measures
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
defined on the Polish space
(\mathcal{Y},d_{\mathcal{Y}})
.

Given
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
, the Kantorovich formulation of the optimal transport problem
(
32
;
79
)
with quadratic cost is

\text{OT}\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\frac{\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|^{2}}{2}d\pi(y_{1},y_{2}),

(19)

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
denotes the set of couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
, that is joint distributions on
\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}
with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
.
Despite its appealing geometric properties, classical OT poses significant computational and statistical challenges. In particular, the empirical plug-in estimator converges at the slow rate
n^{-1/d}
in dimension
d
, reflecting the curse of dimensionality
(
21
;
46
)
. Entropic optimal transport addresses these difficulties by introducing a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence-based regularization term. For
\varepsilon>0
regularization, the EOT problem is defined as

\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\frac{\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|^{2}}{2}d\pi(y_{1},y_{2})+\varepsilon\mathrm{KL}(\pi\mid\mu\otimes\nu),

(20)

where
\mathrm{KL}(\cdot\mid\cdot)
denotes the KL divergence. The regularized problem admits efficient solutions via the Sinkhorn algorithm, whose computational complexity scales quadratically in the sample size
(
14
)
.
The EOT problem enjoys strong duality, with dual formulation

\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)=\sup_{(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{0})\in L^{1}(\mu)\times L^{1}(\nu)}\left[\int\varphi_{1}\,d\mu+\int\varphi_{0}\,d\nu-\varepsilon\int\exp\left(\frac{\varphi_{1}\oplus\varphi_{0}-\|\cdot-\cdot\|^{2}/2}{\varepsilon}\right)d(\mu\otimes\nu)+\varepsilon\right],

(21)

where
(\varphi_{1}\oplus\varphi_{0})(y,y^{\prime})=\varphi_{1}(y)+\varphi_{0}(y^{\prime})
. The dual maximizers
(\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)},\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)})
, known as entropic potentials, satisfy the Schrödinger system

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\log\left(\int\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}(y^{\prime})-\|\cdot-y^{\prime}\|^{2}/2\right)\,d\nu(y^{\prime})\right)\right),

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\log\left(\int\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}(y)-\|y-\cdot\|^{2}/2\right)\,d\mu(y)\right)\right).

(22)

Note that
(\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)},\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)})
are unique up to an additive constant, meaning that if
(\tilde{\varphi}_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)},\tilde{\varphi}_{2}^{(\mu,\nu)})
is another pair of entropic potentials satisfying (
B.2
), there exists a constant
c>0
such that
(\tilde{\varphi}_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)},\tilde{\varphi}_{2}^{(\mu,\nu)})\equiv(\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}+c,\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}-c)
. Following
45
, we define unique potentials
(\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)},\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)})
such that—at an arbitrarily chosen reference point
y_{0}\in\mathcal{Y}
—
\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}(y_{0})=0
.

A limitation of EOT is that
\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\mu\right)\neq 0
, which precludes its direct use as a discrepancy measure. To remove this bias,
56
and
19
introduced the Sinkhorn divergence

{S}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)=\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)-\frac{1}{2}\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\mu\right)-\frac{\mathbbm{1}}{2}\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\nu,\,\nu\right).

Appendix C
Hadamard Differentiability of EOT

C.1
Setup

In this section, we present the results on the Hadamard differentiability of EOT objects, specifically, the entropic potentials and the EOT cost, when viewed as mappings on the Banach space
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\times\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
. As discussed in
Section

3
, the map
J
allows us to identify each probability measure in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
with a unique element of
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\times\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
. Working with this representation, we equip the space of measures with the product norm

\left\|(\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2})\right\|_{\infty}:=\left\|\gamma^{1}\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}\lor\left\|\gamma^{2}\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})},\quad(\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2})\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\times\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1}),

Related differentiability results have been established in closely related normed spaces. In particular, on
\ell^{\infty}(B^{s})\times\ell^{\infty}(B^{s})
, where
B^{s}=\{f\in\mathcal{C}^{s}(\mathcal{Y}):\|f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{s}(\mathcal{Y})}\leq 1\}
denotes the unit ball of the
s
-Hölder space
\mathcal{C}^{s}(\mathcal{Y})
,
24
proved first- and second-order Hadamard differentiability for the entropic potentials and the Sinkhorn divergence. More recently,
36
derived an explicit expression for the first-order Hadamard derivative of the entropic potentials and for the second-order derivative of the Sinkhorn divergence under the null in the Gaussian RKHS topology. In this section, we extend these Hadamard differentiability results for the relevant EOT objects to the Sobolev RKHS topology.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
be a reference measure, and define
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
at
\mu
by

\mathcal{P}_{\mu}=\left\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}):\text{supp}(\nu)\subset\text{supp}(\mu)\right\}.

We define the tangent cone to
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
at
\mu
by

\mathcal{M}_{0,\mu}=\overline{\left\{t(\nu-\mu):\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\mu},t>0\right\}}^{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}.

Tangent cone represents the collection of all admissible first-order perturbation directions at
\mu
induced by paths remaining in
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
, with the closure taken in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
topology. Hadamard differentiability at
\mu
is therefore understood with respect to perturbations along paths
(\mu_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R})\subset\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
, whose first-order increments lie in
\mathcal{M}_{0,\mu}
.

C.2
Sinkhorn Hadamard operator

Fix
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
and recall the operators
H_{\mu}
and
T_{\mu}
defined in (
9
). Since
T_{\mu}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, the operator
T_{\mu}
induces a well-defined operator on the quotient space
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}
, still denoted by
T_{\mu}:\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}
for convenience. Because
\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
is canonically dual to
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}
, the Sinkhorn operator is defined by

K_{\mu}:\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R},\quad K_{\mu}\gamma=\varepsilon(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1}[H_{\mu}\gamma].

This expression is well-defined because, by
38
, the inverse
(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1}
exists as a bounded operator on
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}
. Consequently, for any
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
, the evaluation of the Sinkhorn Hadamard operator
B_{\mu}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}):=\left\langle\gamma_{1},K_{\mu}\gamma_{2}\right\rangle
is well-defined by duality between
\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
and
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}
.

Moreover, the quotient space
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}
is isometrically isomorphic to the closed subspace of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})
consisting of functions orthogonal to constants (equivalently, functions with zero constant component). For any
\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
, the function
H_{\mu}\gamma
has zero constant component, hence belongs to this subspace and canonically represents its equivalence class
[H_{\mu}\gamma]\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}
. Accordingly, when it will not cause confusion, we write
(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1}H_{\mu}\gamma
instead of
(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1}[H_{\mu}\gamma]
.

Following
38
, we now refine this construction in the RKHS setting.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
denote the RKHS associated with the self-transport kernel
\xi_{\mu}
. The RKHS is well-defined because
\xi_{\mu}
is continuous, symmetric, and positive definite.
By construction, both
H_{\mu}
and
T_{\mu}
map into
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
. Since constant functions lie in
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
and satisfy
T_{\mu}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, the operator
T_{\mu}
preserves constants and therefore induces a well-defined operator on the quotient space
T_{\mu}:\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}
.
Similarly, because
H_{\mu}\gamma:\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
is injective and has no constant component when
\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
, the map
[H_{\mu}(\cdot)]:\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}
is also injective; for brevity, we will simply denote this map as
H_{\mu}:\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}
when clear from context.
The quotient space
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}
, equipped with the norm
\|[h]\|_{{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}}}=\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\|h-\lambda\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}
, is isometrically isomorphic to the closed subspace
{\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{\perp}:=\{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mu}:\left\langle h,\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}=0\}
.
Hence
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}
is itself a Hilbert space.
The spectrum of
T_{\mu}:\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}
is contained in
[0,q]
for some
q<1
, and so the spectrum of
(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1}
is contained in
[\varepsilon,\varepsilon/(1-q^{2})]

(
38
, Thm. 4.3)
.

We now derive a kernel representation of
B_{\mu}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
in terms of the inner product in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma C.1
.

The kernel
k_{\mu}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined so

k_{\mu}(y_{1},y_{2})=\left\langle(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}[\xi_{\mu}(y_{1},\cdot)],(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}[\xi_{\mu}(y_{2},\cdot)]\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}},

is symmetric positive semi-definite and, for any
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
, satisfies

B_{\mu}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}k_{\mu}(y,y^{\prime})\,d\gamma_{1}(y)\,d\gamma_{2}(dy^{\prime}).

A kernel representation satisfying the above display is not unique; in particular,
B_{\mu}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=\int\int[k_{\mu}(y,y^{\prime})+a(y)+b(y^{\prime})]\,d\gamma_{1}(y)\,d\gamma_{2}(dy^{\prime})
for any measurable functions
a,b:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

With a slight abuse of notation, we will use
K_{\mu}\gamma_{2}
to denote its representative element in
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\perp}\subset\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
. Then,
B_{\mu}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=\int K_{\mu}\gamma_{2}\,d\gamma_{1}
. Since
H_{\mu}\gamma_{1}
is a kernel mean embedding,
\int K_{\mu}\gamma_{2}\,d\gamma_{1}=\left\langle H_{\mu}\gamma_{1},K_{\mu}\gamma_{2}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}
. Because
H_{\mu}\gamma_{1},K_{\mu}\gamma_{2}\in\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{\perp}
, we have that

B(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=\left\langle H_{\mu}\gamma_{1},K_{\mu}\gamma_{2}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}=\left\langle[H_{\mu}\gamma_{1}],[K_{\mu}\gamma_{2}]\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}}.

Since
(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1}
is strictly positive,
(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}
exists on
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}
. This and its self-adjointness
(
38
, Prop. 4.2)
give

\displaystyle B_{\mu}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle=\left\langle[H_{\mu}\gamma_{1}],(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1}[H_{\mu}\gamma_{2}]\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}}=\left\langle(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}[H_{\mu}\gamma_{1}],(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}[H_{\mu}\gamma_{2}]\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}}.

Let
Q:\mathcal{H}_{\mu}\to\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}
be the standard quotient map such that
Q:h\mapsto[h]
. Since
Q
is a bounded linear map, it commutes with Bochner integration. That is for any Bochner integrable
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
-valued function
h
,
Q(\int h\,d\gamma)=\int Q(h)\,d\gamma
. The boundedness of
\xi_{\mu}
on compact
\mathcal{Y}
implies Bochner integrability of
h=\xi_{\mu}
, and then we have that
[H_{\mu}\gamma_{i}]=[\int\xi_{\mu}(\cdot,y)\,d\gamma_{i}(y)]=\int[\xi_{\mu}(\cdot,y)]\,d\gamma_{i}(y)
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Since
(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1}
is a bounded linear operator on
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}

(
38
, Thm. 4.3)
, we can exchange the operator
(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}
and this gives

(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}[H_{\mu}\gamma]=(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt\mathcal{Y}}[\xi_{\mu}(\cdot,y)]\,d\gamma(y)=\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt\mathcal{Y}}(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}[\xi_{\mu}(\cdot,y)]\,d\gamma(y).

Using the above, and the bilinearity and continuity of inner products, we have

\displaystyle B_{\mu}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt\mathcal{Y}}(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}[\xi_{\mu}(y_{1},\cdot)]d\gamma_{1}(y_{1}),\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt\mathcal{Y}}(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}[\xi_{\mu}(y_{2},\cdot)]d\gamma_{2}(y_{2})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt\mathcal{Y}}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt\mathcal{Y}}\left\langle(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}[\xi_{\mu}(y_{1},\cdot)],(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}[\xi_{\mu}(y_{2},\cdot)]\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}}d\gamma_{1}(y_{1})\,d\gamma_{2}(y_{2})

\displaystyle=:\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt\mathcal{Y}}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt\mathcal{Y}}k_{\mu}(y_{1},y_{2})\,d\gamma_{1}(y_{1})\,d\gamma_{2}(y_{2}).

Symmetry of
k_{\mu}
follows by the symmetry of inner products. To see that the kernel
k_{\mu}
is positive semi-definite, note that for every choice of points
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\in\mathcal{Y}
and vector
(c_{1},\dots,c_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}c_{i}c_{j}k_{\mu}(y_{i},y_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}c_{i}c_{j}\left\langle(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}[\xi_{\mu}(y_{i},\cdot)],(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}[\xi_{\mu}(y_{j},\cdot)]\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i}c_{i}\,(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1/2}[\xi_{\mu}(y_{i},\cdot)]\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}/\mathbb{R}}^{2}\geq 0.

∎

C.3
Hadamard differentiability of EOT potentials and divergence

Now we are ready to present the Hadamard differentiability results of entropic potentials and Sinkhorn divergence in the
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
topology. First, we characterize some properties of the entropic potentials without proof.

Lemma C.2

(Properties of entropic potentials)
.

Recalling that the cost function
c
is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
and
\mathcal{Y}
is compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for every
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
,

1.

23
: The entropic potentials
\big({\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)},\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}}\big)
are uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{H}
, and their norms satisfy
\big\|{\varphi_{i}^{(\mu,\nu)}}\big\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(1+\varepsilon^{-s+1}\right)
for
i\in\{1,0\}
, and the constant depends on
d
and
\left|\mathcal{Y}\right|
.

2.

24
: The map
(\mu,\nu)\mapsto\big({\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)},\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}}\big)
is continuous relative to the topology of weak convergence, that is if
(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
converges to
(\mu,\nu)
weakly as

, then
\big({\varphi_{1}^{(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})},\varphi_{0}^{(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})}}\big)\to\big({\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)},\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}}\big)
in
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
.

The second claim of
Lemma

C.2
follows directly from a similar continuity of entropic potentials in the
C^{k}(\mathcal{Y})
norm for any arbitrary
k\in\mathbb{N}

(
24
, Lem. 1)
because the Sobolev norm can be upperbounded by the
C^{s}(\mathcal{Y})
norm for the compact domain.

Lemma C.3

(Sobolev RKHS properties)
.

The following properties hold for Sobolev RKHS
\mathcal{H}=H^{s}(\mathcal{Y})
for
s>d/2
:

1.

For every smooth function
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
F(0)=0
, there exists a non-decreasing function
C_{F}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

\|F(h)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C_{F}\left(\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y})}\right)\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

2.

For all
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
, the pointwise product
h_{1}h_{2}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}
(defined a.e. in
\mathcal{Y}
). Further, there exists a constant
K_{2}\equiv K_{2}(s,d,|\mathcal{Y}|)
such that

\|h_{1}h_{2}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq K_{2}\|h_{1}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|h_{2}\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

3.

For
h\in\mathcal{H}
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{Y})
, define
T_{\gamma}h:y\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{Y}}e^{-\|y-y^{\prime}\|^{2}/2\varepsilon}\,h(y^{\prime})\,d\gamma(y^{\prime})
. Then for all
h,\gamma
, it holds that
T_{\gamma}h\in\mathcal{H}
and moreover, there exists a constant
K_{3}\equiv K_{3}(s,d,|\mathcal{Y}|)
such that

\left\|T_{\gamma}h\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq K_{3}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|\gamma\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H})}.

Proof.

The first result follows from Sobolev inequalities (Gagliardo–Nirenberg–Moser estimates)
(
22
;
52
;
49
)
for composition. Since
s>d/2
,
H^{s}(\mathcal{Y})\subset\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})

(
74
, Chap. 4, Prop. 1.3)
. Therefore, all
h\in\mathcal{H}
are continuous and hence bounded on
\mathcal{Y}
, i.e.
h\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y})
. For every smooth function
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
F(0)=0
, there exists a non-decreasing function
C_{F}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
\|F(h)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C_{F}(\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y})})\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}

(
73
, Chap. 13, Prop. 3.9)
.

Because we chose
s>d/2
,
\mathcal{H}
is a Banach algebra
(
2
, Thm. 4.39)
, which implies that for any
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
, the pointwise product
h_{1}h_{2}
(defined a.e. in
\mathcal{Y}
) belongs to
\mathcal{H}
. Further, there exists a constant
K_{2}\equiv K_{2}(s,d,\mathcal{Y})
, such that
\|h_{1}h_{2}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq K_{2}\|h_{1}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|h_{2}\|_{\mathcal{H}}
.

For the third result, the function
T_{\gamma}h
is well-defined because
\mathcal{H}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})
, so
h
has a continuous representative in
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})
. Let
k(y,y^{\prime})=e^{-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\|y-y^{\prime}\|^{2}}
. We have that

\displaystyle\left|T_{\gamma}h(y)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int k(y,y^{\prime})\,h(y^{\prime})\,d\gamma(y^{\prime})\right|\leq\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y})}\|\gamma\|_{\text{TV}}<\infty.

Now we show that
T_{\gamma}h\in\mathcal{H}
. Let
k(y,y^{\prime})=e^{-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\|y-y^{\prime}\|^{2}}
. For every
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
, the function
y^{\prime}\mapsto k(y,y^{\prime})
is
C^{\infty}
. For each
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,
T_{\gamma}h(y)=\gamma\left(k(y,\cdot)\,h\right)
. Since
y^{\prime}\mapsto k(y,y^{\prime})\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y})
and
\mathcal{H}
is an algebra
k(y,\cdot)h
belongs to
\mathcal{H}
. As a consequence,

\displaystyle\left|T_{\gamma}h(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|k(y,\cdot)h\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|\gamma\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq K_{2}\|k(y,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}\,\|\gamma\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}

\displaystyle\implies\|T_{\gamma}h\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y})}

\displaystyle\leq K_{2}M_{0}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}\,\|\gamma\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})},

where
M_{0}=\sup_{y}\|k(y,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}}<\infty
because
\mathcal{Y}
is compact. Similarly, for any multi-index
\alpha
, define
M_{\alpha}=\sup_{y}\|\partial^{\alpha}_{y}k(y,\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}}<\infty
. Using the same argument and knowing that
{\partial^{\alpha}T_{\gamma}h(y)}={\gamma(\partial^{\alpha}k(y,\cdot)h)}
, we have that
\|\partial^{\alpha}T_{\gamma}h\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y})}<\infty
. Then it holds that

\displaystyle\|T_{\gamma}h\|_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\left|\alpha\right|\leq s}\left\|\partial^{\alpha}T_{\gamma}h\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Y})}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathcal{Y}\right|^{1/2}\sum_{\left|\alpha\right|\leq s}\left\|\partial^{\alpha}T_{\gamma}h\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y})}

\displaystyle\leq K_{2}\left|\mathcal{Y}\right|^{1/2}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|\gamma\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}\sum_{\left|\alpha\right|\leq s}M_{\alpha}=:K_{3}\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|\gamma\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}.

The first inequality follows from elementary inequalities between
L^{2}
and
L^{\infty}
norms. The last inequality uses that
\mathcal{H}
is an algebra from previous result.
∎

Next, we prove the Hadamard differentiability of Sinkhorn divergence in the dual Sobolev RKHS topology. The Hadamard derivative is of the same form as presented in
24
, but in an ambient RKHS space (
\mathcal{H}
) rather than a dual Hölder space. We present a proof here for completeness.

Lemma C.4

(Hadamard derivative of
S_{\varepsilon}
)
.

For a fixed
\varepsilon>0
, the functional
S_{\varepsilon}:(\mu,\nu)\mapsto{S}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\nu\right),\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\subset\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\times\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\to\mathbb{R}
is Hadamard differentiable at
(\mu,\nu)
, tangentially to
\mathcal{M}_{0,\mu}\times\mathcal{M}_{0,\nu}
, with Hadamard derivative
S_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}[\mu,\nu]:\mathcal{M}_{0,\mu}\times\mathcal{M}_{0,\nu}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

S_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}[\mu,\nu](\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2})=\int\upsilon_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}\,d\gamma^{1}+\int\upsilon_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}\,d\gamma^{2}.

Proof.

By linearity of derivatives, it suffices to compute the derivative of
(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\alpha,\,\beta\right)
. Consider paths
(\mu_{t})_{t>0}
and
(\nu_{t})_{t>0}
in
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\nu}
respectively, such that
\mu_{t}=\mu+t\gamma^{1}_{t}
and
\nu_{t}=\nu+t\gamma^{2}_{t}
, with
\gamma_{t}^{i}\to\gamma^{i}
as
t\downarrow 0
in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
and some
(\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2})\in\mathcal{M}_{0,\mu}\times\mathcal{M}_{0,\nu}
. Then,

\frac{1}{t}\left(\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu_{t},\,\nu_{t}\right)-\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)\right)=\frac{1}{t}\left[\left(\mu_{t}\varphi_{1}^{(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})}+\nu_{t}\varphi_{0}^{(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})}\right)-\left(\mu\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}+\nu\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}\right)\right].

For any
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
, recall the density of the EOT plan

\xi^{(\mu,\nu)}(y_{1},y_{2})=\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}(y_{1})+\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}(y_{2})-\frac{\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|^{2}}{2}\right)\right).

From the optimality of
\left(\varphi_{1}^{(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})},\varphi_{0}^{(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})}\right)
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\left(\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu_{t},\,\nu_{t}\right)-\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{t}\left[{\left(\mu_{t}\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}+\nu_{t}\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}\right)-\left(\mu\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}+\nu\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\varepsilon}{t}\left[\int\int\xi^{(\mu,\nu)}(y_{1},y_{2})\,d\mu_{t}(y_{1})\,d\nu_{t}(y_{2})-1\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma_{t}^{1}\,\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}+\gamma_{t}^{2}\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}-t\varepsilon\int\int\xi^{(\mu,\nu)}(y_{1},y_{2})\,d\gamma_{t}^{1}(y_{1})\,d\gamma_{t}^{2}(y_{2}),

where the last equality follows by expanding
\mu_{t}
(and
\nu_{t}
) as
\mu+t\gamma_{t}^{1}
(and
\nu+t\gamma_{t}^{2}
), and noting that
\mu\left(\xi^{(\mu,\nu)}(\cdot,y_{2})\right)=1
and
\nu\left(\xi^{(\mu,\nu)}(y_{1},\cdot)\right)=1
for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}
. Consequently,

\liminf_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\left(\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu_{t},\,\nu_{t}\right)-\text{OT}_{\varepsilon}\left(\mu,\,\nu\right)\right)\geq\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\left(\gamma_{t}^{1}\,\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}+\gamma_{t}^{2}\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}-t\varepsilon\int\int\xi^{(\mu,\nu)}(y_{1},y_{2})\,d\gamma_{t}^{1}(y_{1})\,d\gamma_{t}^{2}(y_{2})\right).

We will show that the limit on the RHS is equal to
\gamma^{1}\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}+\gamma^{2}\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}
. We already have that

\displaystyle\left|(\gamma_{t}^{1}-\gamma^{1})\,\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}+(\gamma_{t}^{2}-\gamma^{2})\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\gamma_{t}^{1}-\gamma^{1}\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}\left\|\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}+\left\|\gamma_{t}^{2}-\gamma^{2}\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}\left\|\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\xrightarrow{t\downarrow 0}0

because
\gamma_{t}^{i}\to\gamma^{i}
as
t\to 0
in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
for
i\in\{1,2\}
and entropic potentials are uniformly bounded by the first part of
Lemma

C.2
. Therefore, it suffices to show that

\int\int\xi^{(\mu,\nu)}(y_{1},y_{2})\,d\gamma_{t}^{1}(y_{1})\,d\gamma_{t}^{2}(y_{2})=\mathcal{O}(1)\quad\text{as }t\downarrow 0.

(23)

We show this by using three properties for the Sobolev RKHS
\mathcal{H}
detailed in
Lemma

C.3
. Choosing
F(h)=e^{h}-1
in the first property gives us that there exists a non-decreasing function
C_{F}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for all

,

\|e^{h}-1\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C_{F}(\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y})})\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

Because the constant function
\mathbf{1}\in\mathcal{H}
, we have that

\|e^{h}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\|e^{h}-1\|_{\mathcal{H}}+\|\mathbf{1}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C_{F}(\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y})})(\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}+1).

(24)

The Sobolev embedding theorem
(
2
, Thm. 4.12)
gives that
\mathcal{H}
embeds continuously in
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Y})
. Further,
Lemma

Lemma C.5

(Hadamard differentiability of entropic potentials in
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})
)
.

The

Let
\mathcal{G}

36

Therefore,
\Phi
as a map
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\subset\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\times\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})

\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{1})\times\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{1})
.
∎

Lemma E.3

(Rate for first-order remainder term)
.

Let
P^{*}\notin\mathscr{H}_{0}
and
\|n^{r}(\widehat{P}_{a}-P^{*}_{a})\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(1)
for

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle P^{*}\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}

\displaystyle=\left(P^{*}-P^{*}_{A,X}\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,A,X}+P^{*}_{X}\widehat{P}_{Y,A\,|\,X}-\widehat{P}\right)\left((y,a,x)\mapsto\frac{a\,\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,x)}+\frac{(1-a)\,\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,x)}\right)

\displaystyle=P^{*}_{X}\left(\frac{e^{*}(1\,|\,\cdot)}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,\cdot)}P^{*}_{Y\,|\,1,\cdot}\,\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}+\frac{e^{*}(0\,|\,\cdot)}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,\cdot)}P^{*}_{Y\,|\,0,\cdot}\,\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}\right)-P^{*}_{X}\left(\frac{e^{*}(1\,|\,\cdot)}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,\cdot)}\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,1,\cdot}\,\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}+\frac{e^{*}(0\,|\,\cdot)}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,\cdot)}\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,0,\cdot}\,\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+P^{*}_{X}\left(\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,1,\cdot}\,\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}+\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,0,\cdot}\,\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}\right)-P_{1}\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}-P_{0}\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}.

Add and subtract
P_{1}^{*}\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}+P_{0}^{*}\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}
on the RHS to obtain

\displaystyle P^{*}\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}

\displaystyle=P^{*}_{X}\left(\frac{e^{*}(1\,|\,\cdot)}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,\cdot)}(P^{*}_{Y\,|\,1,\cdot}-\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,1,\cdot})\,\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}+\frac{e^{*}(0\,|\,\cdot)}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,\cdot)}(P^{*}_{Y\,|\,0,\cdot}-\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,0,\cdot})\,\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-P^{*}_{X}\left((P^{*}_{Y\,|\,1,\cdot}-\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,1,\cdot})\,\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}+(P^{*}_{Y\,|\,0,\cdot}-\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,0,\cdot})\,\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}\right)+(P_{1}^{*}-P_{1})\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}+(P_{0}^{*}-P_{0})\upsilon_{0}

\displaystyle=(P_{1}^{*}-P_{1})\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}+(P_{0}^{*}-P_{0})\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}

\displaystyle\quad+P^{*}_{X}\left(\left(\frac{e^{*}(1\,|\,\cdot)}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,\cdot)}-1\right)(P^{*}_{Y\,|\,1,\cdot}-\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,1,\cdot})\,\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}+\left(\frac{e^{*}(0\,|\,\cdot)}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,\cdot)}-1\right)(P^{*}_{Y\,|\,0,\cdot}-\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,0,\cdot})\,\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}\right)

From assumption, the second and third additive terms are
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
. The assumption is valid because the term is a product of estimation error of two nuisance parameters - propensity score
e_{P^{*}}
and outcome regression
P^{*}_{Y\,|\,A,X}
. The requirement of
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
convergence rate for this double product is standard. Therefore, now ignoring the established

term, it remains to show that

S_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{P}_{1},\widehat{P}_{0})-S_{\varepsilon}(P_{1}^{*},P_{0}^{*})-\left[(P_{1}^{*}-\widehat{P}_{1})\upsilon_{1}+(P_{0}^{*}-\widehat{P}_{0})\upsilon_{0}\right]=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

The above is true via the Hadamard differentiability of
(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto S_{\varepsilon}(\alpha,\beta)
.
For any
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
and
(\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2})\in\mathcal{M}_{0,\mu}\times\mathcal{M}_{0,\mu}
, consider paths of the form
\mu_{t}=\mu+t\gamma_{t}^{1}
and
\nu_{t}=\nu+t\gamma_{t}^{2}
such that
\gamma_{t}^{1}\to\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{t}^{2}\to\gamma_{2}
as
t\downarrow 0
in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
. Because
(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto S_{\varepsilon}(\alpha,\beta)
is second-order Hadamard differentiable with a bounded second-order Hadamard derivative, the following first-order expansion is immediate from the definition

S_{\varepsilon}(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})-S_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)-S_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}[\mu,\nu](\mu_{t}-\mu,\nu_{t}-\nu)=O(t^{2}).

Plugging in the form of

from
Lemma

C.4
, we have

S_{\varepsilon}(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})-S_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)-\left[(\mu_{t}-\mu)\upsilon_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}+(\nu_{t}-\nu)\upsilon_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}\right]=O(t^{2}).

Subtracting
t\gamma_{t}^{1}\upsilon_{1}^{(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})}+t\gamma_{t}^{2}\upsilon_{0}^{(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})}
from both LHS and RHS of the above display, we have that

S_{\varepsilon}(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})-S_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)-t\gamma_{t}^{1}\upsilon_{1}^{(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})}-t\gamma_{t}^{2}\upsilon_{0}^{(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})}=t\gamma_{t}^{1}(\upsilon_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}-\upsilon_{1}^{(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})})+t\gamma_{t}^{2}(\upsilon_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}-\upsilon_{0}^{(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})})+O(t^{2}).

From the Hadamard differentiability of the map
(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto(\upsilon_{1}^{(\alpha,\beta)},\upsilon_{0}^{(\alpha,\beta)})
at

and boundedness of the derivative, we have that the RHS in the above display is

. If
\|n^{r}(\widehat{P}_{a}-P^{*}_{a})\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(1)
for

Therefore,
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0.5}\left\|\mathfrak{g}(M_{b_{\hat{\mu}}}\gamma)\right\|_{\mathcal{G}}}\leq C_{1}\|\gamma\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{1})}
.

•

Controlling
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\left\|b_{\hat{\mu}}-b_{{\mu}}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})}}
.
From
24
, we know that the map
\Phi:\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})
given by
(\mu,\nu)\mapsto(\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)},\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)})
is Hadamard differentiable. Further the function
b:\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})
given by
u\mapsto\exp(u/\varepsilon)
is Fréchet differentiable on
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})
with derivative given by the linear operator
b^{\prime}[u](h)=\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\exp(u/\varepsilon)h
. Hence
b
is Hadamard differentiable and by chain rule, the mapping
(\mu,\nu)\mapsto(\exp(\varphi_{1}^{(\mu,\nu)}/\varepsilon),\exp(\varphi_{0}^{(\mu,\nu)}/\varepsilon))
is Hadamard differentiable.

This implies that the function
\mu\mapsto b_{\mu}
is Hadamard differentiable and there exists a continuous linear operator
b^{\prime}[\mu]:\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})
such that along the path
\mu_{t}=\mu+t\gamma_{t}
with
\gamma_{t}\to\gamma
in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
for some
\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{0,\mu}
,

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\left\|b_{\mu_{t}}-b_{\mu}-tb^{\prime}[\mu](\gamma_{t})\right\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})}}{t}=0.

By functional delta theorem, in supremum norm,

n^{r}(b_{\hat{\mu}}-b_{\mu})=b^{\prime}[\mu](n^{r}(\hat{\mu}-\mu))+o_{p}(1).

Because
b^{\prime}[\mu]
is a tight continuous operator,
b^{\prime}[\mu](n^{r}(\hat{\mu}-\mu))
is tight, and therefore
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\|(b_{\hat{\mu}}-b_{\mu})\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})}}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{-r})
.

•

Controlling
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\left\|\mathfrak{g}(M_{b_{\hat{\mu}}}\gamma)-\mathfrak{g}(M_{b_{{\mu}}}\gamma)\right\|_{\mathcal{G}}}
.
By
36
,
\|b_{\hat{\mu}}-b_{\mu}\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{-r})
immediately implies that
\left\|M_{b_{\hat{\mu}}}-M_{b_{{\mu}}}\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{1})\to\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{1})}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{-r})
.
As a consequence,
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25}\left\|\mathfrak{g}(M_{b_{\hat{\mu}}}\gamma)-\mathfrak{g}(M_{b_{{\mu}}}\gamma)\right\|_{\mathcal{G}}}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{-r})\|\gamma\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{1})}
.

Altogether, term (i) is
\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{-r})\|\gamma\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{1})}
.

Step 2: Control (ii).

We bound
\|H_{\mu}\|_{\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})}
as following. For any
\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})\subset\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{1})
,

\left\langle\gamma,H_{\mu}\gamma\right\rangle=\|M_{b_{\mu}}\gamma\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{1})}^{2}\leq C_{1}^{2}\|\gamma\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{1})}^{2}.

Therefore, the operator norm must be uniformly bounded.
For the difference term, we consider the following identity

\displaystyle\left\|(I-T_{\hat{\mu}}^{2})^{-1}-(I-T_{{\mu}}^{2})^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|(I-T_{\hat{\mu}}^{2})^{-1}\left[T_{\mu}^{2}-T_{\hat{\mu}}^{2}\right](I-T_{{\mu}}^{2})^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(I-T_{\hat{\mu}}^{2})^{-1}\right\|\left\|(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1}\right\|\left\|T_{\hat{\mu}}^{2}-T_{\mu}^{2}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(I-T_{\hat{\mu}}^{2})^{-1}\right\|\,\left\|(I-T_{\mu}^{2})^{-1}\right\|\,\left(\left\|T_{\hat{\mu}}\right\|+\left\|T_{\mu}\right\|\right)\,\left\|T_{\hat{\mu}}-T_{\mu}\right\|,

where all operator norms are on the common function space
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}
. As in Step 1, the operator norm of
(I-T_{\hat{\mu}}^{2})^{-1}
is uniformly bounded on
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}
.
38
show that the operator norm of
T_{\nu}
is uniformly bounded by
1
for all
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
. Since centering is a contraction,
\|{T_{\hat{\mu}}-T_{\mu}}\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}}\leq\|T_{\hat{\mu}}-T_{\mu}\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})}
. For any
f
inside a unit ball in
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})
,

\displaystyle\left\|(T_{\hat{\mu}}-T_{\mu})f\right\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})}

\displaystyle\leq\|H_{\hat{\mu}}M_{f}\hat{\mu}-H_{{\mu}}M_{f}{\mu}\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})}

\displaystyle\leq\|(H_{\hat{\mu}}-H_{\mu})M_{f}\hat{\mu}+H_{\mu}M_{f}(\hat{\mu}-\mu)\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})}

\displaystyle\leq\|H_{\hat{\mu}}-H_{\mu}\|_{\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})}\|M_{f}\hat{\mu}\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{1})}+\|H_{\mu}\|_{\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})}\|M_{f}(\hat{\mu}-\mu)\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{1})}.

Since
f
belongs to a compact subset in
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})
, again by
36
,
\|M_{f}\gamma\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{1})}\leq C_{1}\|\gamma\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{1})}
. Using this and the fact that
\|H_{\mu}\|_{\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})}\leq C_{1}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\left\|(T_{\hat{\mu}}-T_{\mu})f\right\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|H_{\hat{\mu}}-H_{\mu}\|_{\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})}+\|\hat{\mu}-\mu\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{G}_{1})}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{-r}).

Together, terms (i) and (ii) are
\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{-r})
and this completes the proof.
∎

Lemma E.5

(Rate for second-order drift term)
.

Let
P^{*}\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
and assume that
\|n^{r}(\hat{\mu}-\mu)\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(1)
for some
r>1/4
. Further, assume that the estimated nuisance parameters satisfy

\displaystyle P^{*}_{X}\left[x\mapsto\left(\frac{e_{\widehat{P}}(a\,|\,x)}{e_{P^{*}}(a\,|\,x)}-1\right)\big({\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,a,x}-P^{*}_{Y\,|\,a,x}}\big)k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(\cdot,y)\right]=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

uniformly in
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
a\in\{1,0\}
. Then the drift term satisfies

\mathscr{D}_{n}=(P_{n}-P^{*})\left(\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}(\cdot)+\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits\kern-0.99998pt\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}(z,\cdot)\,dP^{*}(z)\right)=o_{p}(n^{-1}).

Proof.

Since
\widehat{P}
and
P_{n}
are constructed from independent samples, the random function
\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}(\cdot)+\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits\kern-0.99998pt\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}(\cdot,z)\,dP^{*}(z)
is independent of
P_{n}
. By
Lemma

E.1
, it suffices to show that
\big\|{\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}(\cdot)+\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits\kern-0.99998pt\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}(\cdot,z)\,dP^{*}(z)}\big\|_{L^{2}(P^{*})}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
to show that
\mathscr{D}_{n}=o_{p}(n^{-1})
.
Recall from (
8
) and (
10
) the formulations of
\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}
and
\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}
. Using
Q
operator notation in (
37
), at evaluation points
z_{j}=(x_{j},a_{j},y_{j})
for
j=1,2
in
\mathcal{Z}
, we have that

\displaystyle\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}

\displaystyle=Q_{\widehat{P}}f_{\widehat{P}},\quad f_{\widehat{P}}(z_{1})=\frac{a_{1}}{\hat{e}(1\mid x_{1})}\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}(y_{1})+\frac{1-a_{1}}{\hat{e}(0\mid x_{1})}\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}(y_{1}),

\displaystyle\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}

\displaystyle=Q_{\widehat{P}}^{\otimes 2}g_{\widehat{P}},\quad g_{\widehat{P}}(z_{1},z_{2})=\omega_{\widehat{P}}(a_{1},x_{1})\,\omega_{\widehat{P}}(a_{2},x_{2})\,k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y_{1},y_{2}),

where
\omega_{\widehat{P}}(a,x)=\tfrac{a}{\hat{e}(1\mid x)}-\tfrac{1-a}{\hat{e}(0\mid x)}
.
Note that, with
k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)\otimes\omega_{\widehat{P}}:(y^{\prime},a^{\prime},x^{\prime})\mapsto k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,y^{\prime})\otimes\omega_{\widehat{P}}(a^{\prime},x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}(\cdot)+\int\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}(z,\cdot)\,dP^{*}(z)=Q_{\widehat{P}}f_{\widehat{P}}+(Q_{\widehat{P}}\otimes Q_{P^{*},\widehat{P}})g_{\widehat{P}}

\displaystyle=Q_{\widehat{P}}\left[(y,a,x)\mapsto\frac{a}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,x)}\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}(y)+\frac{1-a}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,x)}\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}(y)+\omega_{\widehat{P}}(a,x)\,Q_{P^{*},\widehat{P}}\,\left(k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)\otimes\omega_{\widehat{P}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=Q_{\widehat{P}}\left[(y,a,x)\mapsto\frac{a}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,x)}\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}(y)+\frac{1-a}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,x)}\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}(y)+\left(\frac{a}{\hat{e}(1\mid x)}-\frac{1-a}{\hat{e}(0\mid x)}\right)\,Q_{P^{*},\widehat{P}}\,\left(k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)\otimes\omega_{\widehat{P}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=Q_{\widehat{P}}\left[(y,a,x)\mapsto\frac{a}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,x)}\zeta_{1}(y)\right]+Q_{\widehat{P}}\left[(y,a,x)\mapsto\frac{1-a}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,x)}\zeta_{2}(y)\right],

where

\zeta_{1}(y):=\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}(y)+Q_{P^{*},\widehat{P}}\left(k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)\otimes\omega_{\widehat{P}}\right)\quad\textrm{and }\quad\zeta_{2}(y):=\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}(y)-Q_{P^{*},\widehat{P}}\left(k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)\otimes\omega_{\widehat{P}}\right).

Expanding the form of
Q_{\widehat{P}}
, we have that the above is equal to

\displaystyle\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}(z)+\int\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}(z^{\prime},z)\,dP^{*}(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,x)}(I-\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,A,X})\left[(y^{\prime},a^{\prime},x^{\prime})\mapsto\zeta_{1}(y^{\prime})\right](z)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1-a}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,x)}(I-\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,A,X})\left[(y^{\prime},a^{\prime},x^{\prime})\mapsto\zeta_{2}(y^{\prime})\right](z)

\displaystyle\quad+(I-\widehat{P}_{X})\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,1,X}\left[(y^{\prime},a^{\prime},x^{\prime})\mapsto\zeta_{1}(y^{\prime})\right](z)

\displaystyle\quad+(I-\widehat{P}_{X})\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,0,X}\left[(y^{\prime},a^{\prime},x^{\prime})\mapsto\zeta_{2}(y^{\prime})\right](z).

Note that if
\|\zeta_{1}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}|\zeta_{1}(y)|=o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
, then

\displaystyle\left\|z\mapsto\frac{a}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,x)}(I-\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,A,X})\left[z^{\prime}\mapsto\zeta_{1}(y^{\prime})\right](z)\right\|_{L^{2}(P^{*})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{a^{2}}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,x)^{2}}\left(\zeta_{1}(y)-\int\zeta_{1}(y^{\prime})\hat{p}_{Y\,|\,A,X}(y^{\prime}\,|\,a,x)\,dy^{\prime}\right)^{2}dP^{*}(z)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\zeta_{1}\|_{\infty}^{2}\int\frac{a^{2}}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,x)^{2}}\,dP^{*}(x,a,y)\leq\frac{2}{E}\|\zeta_{1}\|_{\infty}^{2},

and

\displaystyle\left\|(I-\widehat{P}_{X})\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,1,X}\left[z^{\prime}\mapsto\zeta_{1}(y^{\prime})\right]\right\|_{L^{2}(P^{*})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,1,X}\left[z^{\prime}\mapsto\zeta_{1}(y^{\prime})\right]\right\|_{L^{2}(P^{*})}+\left\|\widehat{P}_{X}\widehat{P}_{Y\,|\,1,X}\left[z^{\prime}\mapsto\zeta_{1}(y^{\prime})\right]\right\|_{L^{2}(P^{*})}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\zeta_{1}\|_{\infty}.

Similarly, the same upperbounds hold for terms involving
\zeta_{2}
, and therefore,

\left\|\dot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}(\cdot)+\int\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}(z^{\prime},\cdot)\,dP^{*}(z^{\prime})\right\|_{L^{2}(P^{*})}\lesssim\|\zeta_{1}\|_{\infty}+\|\zeta_{2}\|_{\infty}.

It remains to show that both
\|\zeta_{1}\|_{\infty}
and
\|\zeta_{2}\|_{\infty}
are
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.
For any
y
,
\zeta_{1}(y)
can be simplified as follows:

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}(y)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}(y)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(P^{*}-P^{*}_{A^{\prime},X^{\prime}}\widehat{P}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,A^{\prime},X^{\prime}}+P^{*}_{X^{\prime}}\widehat{P}_{Y^{\prime},A^{\prime}\,|\,X^{\prime}}-\widehat{P}\right)\left[(y^{\prime},a^{\prime},x^{\prime})\mapsto\left(\frac{a^{\prime}}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,x^{\prime})}-\frac{1-a^{\prime}}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,x^{\prime})}\right)k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,y^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}(y)+P^{*}_{X^{\prime}}\left[x^{\prime}\mapsto\frac{e^{*}(1\,|\,x^{\prime})}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,x^{\prime})}P^{*}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,1,x^{\prime}}\,k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)-\frac{e^{*}(0\,|\,x^{\prime})}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,x^{\prime})}P^{*}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,0,x^{\prime}}\,k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)\right]

\displaystyle\quad-P^{*}_{X^{\prime}}\left[x^{\prime}\mapsto\frac{e^{*}(1\,|\,x^{\prime})}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,x^{\prime})}\widehat{P}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,1,x^{\prime}}\,k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)-\frac{e^{*}(0\,|\,x^{\prime})}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,x^{\prime})}\widehat{P}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,0,x^{\prime}}\,k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+P^{*}_{X^{\prime}}\left[x^{\prime}\mapsto\widehat{P}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,1,x^{\prime}}\,k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)-\widehat{P}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,0,x^{\prime}}k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)\right]-(\widehat{P}_{1}-\widehat{P}_{0})\,k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}(y)-K_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(\widehat{P}_{1}-\widehat{P}_{0})(y)

\displaystyle\quad+P^{*}_{X^{\prime}}\left[x^{\prime}\mapsto\left(\frac{e^{*}(1\,|\,x^{\prime})}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,x^{\prime})}-1\right)P^{*}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,1,x^{\prime}}\,k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)-\left(\frac{e^{*}(0\,|\,x^{\prime})}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,x^{\prime})}-1\right)P^{*}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,0,x^{\prime}}\,k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)\right]

\displaystyle\quad-P^{*}_{X^{\prime}}\left[x^{\prime}\mapsto\left(\frac{e^{*}(1\,|\,x^{\prime})}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,x^{\prime})}-1\right)\widehat{P}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,1,x^{\prime}}k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)-\left(\frac{e^{*}(0\,|\,x^{\prime})}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,x^{\prime})}-1\right)\widehat{P}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,0,x^{\prime}}k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+(P_{1}^{*}-P_{0}^{*})k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)

Since the null holds by assumption,
P_{1}^{*}=P_{0}^{*}
, and so the last additive term is zero. Combining the terms above

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}(y)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}(y)-K_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(\widehat{P}_{1}-\widehat{P}_{0})(y)

\displaystyle\quad+P^{*}_{X^{\prime}}\left[x^{\prime}\mapsto\left(\frac{e^{*}(1\,|\,x^{\prime})}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,x^{\prime})}-1\right)\left(P^{*}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,1,x^{\prime}}-\widehat{P}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,1,x^{\prime}}\right)k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)\right]

\displaystyle\quad-P^{*}_{X^{\prime}}\left[x^{\prime}\mapsto\left(\frac{e^{*}(0\,|\,x^{\prime})}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,x^{\prime})}-1\right)\left(P^{*}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,0,x^{\prime}}-\widehat{P}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,0,x^{\prime}}\right)k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)\right].

A similar calculation yields

\displaystyle\zeta_{2}(y)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}(y)+K_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(\widehat{P}_{1}-\widehat{P}_{0})(y)

\displaystyle\quad-P^{*}_{X^{\prime}}\left[x^{\prime}\mapsto\left(\frac{e^{*}(1\,|\,x^{\prime})}{\hat{e}(1\,|\,x^{\prime})}-1\right)\left(P^{*}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,1,x^{\prime}}-\widehat{P}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,1,x^{\prime}}\right)k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+P^{*}_{X^{\prime}}\left[x^{\prime}\mapsto\left(\frac{e^{*}(0\,|\,x^{\prime})}{\hat{e}(0\,|\,x^{\prime})}-1\right)\left(P^{*}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,0,x^{\prime}}-\widehat{P}_{Y^{\prime}\,|\,0,X^{\prime}}\right)k_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(y,\cdot)\right].

The last two terms in both
\zeta_{1}
and
\zeta_{2}
involve products of estimation errors for propensity score
e_{P^{*}}
and the outcome regression
P^{*}_{Y\,|\,A,X}
. By assumption, these terms are
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
uniformly over
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, and hence negligible. It therefore remains to control the leading components:
\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}-K_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(\widehat{P}_{1}-\widehat{P}_{0})
and
\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}+K_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(\widehat{P}_{1}-\widehat{P}_{0})
. Consider the breakdown

\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}-K_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(\widehat{P}_{1}-\widehat{P}_{0})=\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}-K_{P_{1}^{*}}(\widehat{P}_{1}-\widehat{P}_{0})+(K_{P_{1}^{*}}-K_{\widehat{P}_{1}})(\widehat{P}_{1}-P_{1}^{*})-(K_{P_{1}^{*}}-K_{\widehat{P}_{1}})(\widehat{P}_{0}-P_{0}^{*}),

and

\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}+K_{\widehat{P}_{1}}(\widehat{P}_{1}-\widehat{P}_{0})=\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}+K_{P_{1}^{*}}(\widehat{P}_{1}-\widehat{P}_{0})-(K_{P_{1}^{*}}-K_{\widehat{P}_{1}})(\widehat{P}_{1}-P_{1}^{*})+(K_{P_{1}^{*}}-K_{\widehat{P}_{1}})(\widehat{P}_{0}-P_{0}^{*}).

From
Lemma

E.4
, we have that for each
i,j\in\{1,0\}

\left\|(K_{\widehat{P}_{i}}-K_{P_{i}^{*}})(\widehat{P}_{j}-P^{*}_{j})\right\|_{\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})}\leq\left\|K_{\widehat{P}_{i}}-K_{P_{i}^{*}}\right\|_{\mathcal{M}_{0}(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})/\mathbb{R}}\left\|\widehat{P}_{j}-P^{*}_{j}\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H})}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{-2r}).

Since
r>1/4
, we have that
(K_{\widehat{P}_{1}}-K_{P_{1}^{*}})(\widehat{P}_{a}-P_{a}^{*})
is
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
in the
L^{2}(P^{*})
sense.
Finally, by
Corollary

C.7
, the quantities
\widehat{\upsilon}_{1}-K_{P_{1}^{*}}(\widehat{P}_{1}-\widehat{P}_{0})
and
\widehat{\upsilon}_{0}+K_{P_{1}^{*}}(\widehat{P}_{1}-\widehat{P}_{0})
constitute the second-order remainder terms in the Hadamard expansion of the centered entropic potentials. From assumption,
\|n^{r}(\widehat{P}_{1}-P_{1}^{*})\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}
and
\|n^{r}(\widehat{P}_{0}-P_{0}^{*})\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}
are
\mathcal{O}_{p}(1)
for some
r>1/4
. Then by functional delta theorem and second-order Hadamard differentiability of entropic potentials
(
24
, Theorem 4)
, these remainders are
\mathcal{O}_{p}(n^{-2r})
in
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Y})
topology, and hence
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
in
L^{2}(P^{*})
norm.
∎

Lemma E.6

(Rate for second-order U-process term)
.

If
\|C_{P^{*}}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}-C_{P^{*}}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(P^{*}\otimes P^{*})}\xrightarrow{p}0
, then the U-process term

\mathscr{U}_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{U}_{n}\left(C_{P^{*}}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}-C_{P^{*}}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}\right)=o_{p}(n^{-1}).

Proof.

Denote
f_{n}:=C_{P^{*}}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}-C_{P^{*}}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{P^{*}}
. We will show that for every
\delta>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|n\mathbb{U}_{n}f_{n}\right|>\delta\right)\leq\frac{C}{\delta^{2}}{\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(P^{*}\otimes P^{*})}}.

Because
C_{P^{*}}\ddot{\mathscr{S}}_{\widehat{P}}
is
P^{*}
-degenerate,
\mathbb{E}_{P^{*}}\left[U_{n}f_{n}\,|\,\widehat{P}\right]=0
. Let
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
be the iid samples from
P^{*}
used to construct
\widehat{P}
. By Chebyshev’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|n\mathbb{U}_{n}f_{n}\right|>\delta\,|\,\widehat{P}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[(n\mathbb{U}_{n}f_{n})^{2}\,|\,\widehat{P}\right]}{\delta^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left({n\choose 2}^{-1}\sum_{i<j}f_{n}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\right)^{2}\Big|\,\widehat{P}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2n}{\delta^{2}(n-1)}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{n}(Z_{1},Z_{2})^{2}\,|\,\widehat{P}\right]\leq\frac{C}{\delta^{2}}\left\|f_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}(P^{*}\otimes P^{*})}^{2}\quad\text{a.s. for some }C>0.

Taking expectation on both sides,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|n\mathbb{U}_{n}f_{n}\right|>\delta\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\left|n\mathbb{U}_{n}f_{n}\right|>\delta\,|\,\widehat{P}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left(1,\frac{C}{\delta^{2}}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(P^{*}\otimes P^{*})}\right)\right].

Since
\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(P^{*}\otimes P^{*})}\xrightarrow{p}0
, the integrand on the right converges to
0
in probability. Since it is also dominated by the constant function
1
, by the dominated convergence theorem, the right-hand side converges to
0
as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Lemma E.7

(Second-order remainder for the Sinkhorn divergence)
.

Consider paths
\mu_{t}=\mu+t\gamma_{t}^{1}
and
\nu_{t}=\mu+t\gamma_{t}^{2}
in
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}
such that
\gamma_{t}^{i}\to\gamma^{i}
in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
, for

i=1,2
, with
\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{0,\mu}
. Suppose that
S_{\varepsilon}:(\mu,\nu)\mapsto S_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)

is twice differentiable along the path
t\mapsto(\mu+t\gamma_{t}^{1},\mu+t\gamma_{t}^{2})
for
t
small enough, and its second derivative satisfies the local
Lipschitz bound

\left|S_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}[\mu_{t},\nu_{t}](h_{1},h_{2})-S_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}[\mu_{t},\mu_{t}](h_{1},h_{2})\right|\leq C_{1}\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|^{2}_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}\|\nu_{t}-\mu_{t}\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})}

for all
h_{1},h_{2}
in
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
and some constant
C_{1}>0
. Then

S_{\varepsilon}(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})-\frac{t^{2}}{2}(\gamma_{t}^{1}-\gamma_{t}^{2})K_{\mu}(\gamma_{t}^{1}-\gamma_{t}^{2})=O(t^{3}).

Proof.

Define

Appendix F
One-Step Estimator of MMD Distribution Treatment Effect

As a benchmark, we also compute first- and second-order one-step estimators for the maximum mean discrepancy between counterfactual outcome distributions; we call this the MMD treatment effect (MTE). The corresponding EIFs are derived using the same framework as for the Sinkhorn divergence; however, smoothness with respect to the underlying kernel mean embeddings is immediate for MMD due to its quadratic form.

The (squared) MMD functional is defined as

\mathscr{M}:P\in\mathcal{P}\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\text{MMD}^{2}\left(\psi^{1}(P)),\,\psi^{0}(P)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\psi^{1}(P))-\psi^{0}(P))\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{G}},

(38)

where recall that
\mathcal{G}
is the Gaussian RKHS with kernel
g_{\varepsilon}(y_{1},y_{2})=\exp(-\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|^{2}/2\varepsilon)
and KME operator
\mathfrak{g}(\mu)=\int g_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,y)d\mu(y)
.

Lemma F.1

(MMD first-order local parameter, Ex. 3 in
43
)
.

The parameter

is pathwise differentiable at
P\in\mathcal{P}
with first-order EIF given by

\dot{\mathscr{M}}_{P}=(I-(I-(I-P_{X})P_{A\,|\,X})P_{Y\,|\,A,X})f,

where

Proof.

This lemma follows by applying the automatic differentiation algorithm in Thm. 1 of
44

Recall that

is the symmetric, one-degenerate kernel

(T_{\varepsilon}f)(z)=\int h_{\varepsilon}(z,z^{\prime})f(z^{\prime})\,dP^{*}(z^{\prime})

Since

is symmetric,

is compact and self-adjoint on

and an orthonormal family

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j,\varepsilon}^{2}<\infty

Because

is one-degenerate, every eigenfunction corresponding to a nonzero eigenvalue belongs to

Let

(G(\phi_{j,\varepsilon}))_{j\geq 1}
are i.i.d. standard normal random variables. Let

denote the second-order Wiener–Itô integral with respect to

I_{2}(h_{\varepsilon})=I_{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j,\varepsilon}\left(\phi_{j,\varepsilon}\otimes\phi_{j,\varepsilon}\right)\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j,\varepsilon}I_{2}\left(\phi_{j,\varepsilon}\otimes\phi_{j,\varepsilon}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j,\varepsilon}\left(G(\phi_{j,\varepsilon})^{2}-1\right).

(39)

The above is the spectral representation of the so-called Gaussian chaos random variable

I.3
Acceleration recommendations

We now make the computational bottlenecks of the second-order one-step STE estimator explicit and summarize practical acceleration strategies. From
Section

5
, the total computational complexity is
O(n^{2}(n+t))
, where
n
is the sample size and
t
is the number of Sinkhorn iterations. This cost is driven primarily by the Sinkhorn algorithm, which contributes
O(n^{2}t)
, and Algorithm 2, which contributes
O(n^{3})
. In addition, once the Gram matrix of the second-order EIF has been formed, the second-order bias-correction term requires a U-statistic computation, which incurs an additional quadratic computational load. We now discuss standard acceleration techniques that can be incorporated directly into our framework. While we expect these strategies to yield empirical gains similar to those reported in the existing literature, a full asymptotic analysis of the resulting accelerated procedures is beyond the scope of the present paper.

Sinkhorn algorithm.

To accelerate the Sinkhorn step, several complementary strategies are available.
Greenkhorn
(
4
)
replaces full alternating row/column normalizations by greedy coordinate updates, which often improves practical performance while retaining the same dense-kernel representation. Batch Greenkhorn methods
(
37
)
extend this idea by updating batches of coordinates and provide refined convergence guarantees. These approaches aim to reduce the amount of scaling work required for convergence, but they do not by themselves eliminate the
O(n^{2})
cost of storing and multiplying by a dense Gibbs kernel. To address this bottleneck, Nyström-Sinkhorn
(
3
)
approximates the Gibbs kernel
K_{ij}=e^{-C_{ij}/\varepsilon}
using a low-rank Nyström factorization, reducing per-iteration kernel-vector multiplication costs and memory usage from
O(n^{2})
to
O(nr)
, where
r
is the approximation rank. These approaches can be incorporated into our one-step STE estimator to reduce the per-iteration computational cost.

Algorithm 2 acceleration.

A natural way to reduce the
O(n^{3})
cost of
Algorithm

2
is to replace the dense discretized self-transport kernel
\mathbf{X}
, defined in line 3 by

\mathbf{X}_{ij}=\exp\!\left\{\frac{\mathbf{F}_{i}+\mathbf{F}_{j}-\mathbf{G}_{ij}}{\varepsilon}\right\},

with a rank-
r
Nyström approximation
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=CW^{\dagger}C^{\top}=UV^{\top}
with
r\ll n
. This is well aligned with prior work on scalable entropic OT, where low-rank Nyström approximations are used to compress the Gibbs kernel while preserving the Sinkhorn scaling structure
(
3
)
. In our setting, the cubic bottleneck arises because line 5 requires solving a dense
n\times n
linear system,

\mathbf{M}=\bigl(I-(\mathbf{P}^{1}\odot\mathbf{X})^{2}\bigr)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}/n\right),

and because the subsequent contractions
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{M}
in lines 8-10 amount to repeated dense matrix multiplications, each of overall order
n^{3}
. Replacing
\mathbf{X}
by
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
lowers both costs: first, if
A:=\mathbf{P}^{1}\odot\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
is represented in low-rank form as
A=\tilde{U}V^{\top}
, then

A^{2}=(\tilde{U}V^{\top})(\tilde{U}V^{\top})=\tilde{U}BV^{\top},\qquad B:=V^{\top}\tilde{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},

so
\bigl(I-A^{2}\bigr)^{-1}
can be applied via the Woodbury identity using only an
r\times r
inner solve, reducing line 5 from
O(n^{3})
to
O(nr^{2}+r^{3})
after the low-rank factors are formed. Second, every multiplication by an
n\times n
matrix generated from
\mathbf{X}
can be evaluated through the factorization
UV^{\top}
, costing
O(nr)
per vector and
O(n^{2}r)
per matrix block, so the contractions
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{M}
drop from
O(n^{3})
to
O(n^{2}r)
. The remaining operations in the algorithm: forming
\mathbf{X}
, applying
\mathbf{\mathbf{D}}_{A,X}
, and elementwise products are at most quadratic, so under a Nyström representation the total complexity of the second-order routine is reduced from
O(n^{3})
to roughly
O(n^{2}r+nr^{2}+r^{3})
, with memory reduced from
O(n^{2})
to
O(nr)
. Therefore, a Nyström approximation to the discretized Sinkhorn self-transport kernel is a direct and computationally efficient way to scale the second-order correction while retaining the kernel structure induced by the entropic potentials.

U
-statistic computation.

The
O(n^{2})
complexity of the U-statistic, standard in kernel two-sample testing, can be mitigated using linear-time approximations
(
26
)
or incomplete U-statistics
(
67
)
. Both can be directly substituted into the second-order bias-correction term of the STE one-step estimator.
