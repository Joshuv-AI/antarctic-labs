Impulsivity facets and internet gaming disorder symptoms in male adolescents with ADHD: an exploratory cross-sectional study from Turkiye
Authors: Sarper İçen, Şefika Nurhüda Karaca Cengiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescents with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) are at elevated risk for problematic gaming. Although impulsivity has been linked to problematic gaming, it is unclear whether different impulsivity facets show distinct associations with heterogeneous DSM-5 Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) symptom domains.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 45 male adolescents with ADHD (ages 12–17) presenting with excessive gaming concerns were recruited from two child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinics in Turkiye (December 2025–January 2026). ADHD diagnoses and comorbidity screening were confirmed with the K-SADS-PL-DSM-5-T semi-structured interview; comorbid psychiatric disorders expected to meaningfully affect impulsivity or gaming-symptom reporting were excluded. Impulsivity was assessed using the Barratt Impulsiveness Scale-11-SF (BIS-11-SF) (attentional, motor, non-planning) and IGD symptoms using the IGDS-27 (nine DSM-5 criteria). Spearman correlations were computed between impulsivity indices and IGD total/domain scores, with uncertainty quantified using 5,000-sample bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Selected exploratory comparisons of dependent overlapping correlations were also conducted. Age, ADHD pharmacotherapy status, and parental psychiatric history were examined in sensitivity analyses.ResultsMost participants reported gaming > 4 h/day on weekdays (73.3%) and weekends (75.6%). IGDS-27 total score was positively associated with non-planning impulsivity (ρ = 0.56, 95% CI [0.30, 0.74]), attentional impulsivity (ρ = 0.52, 95% CI [0.23, 0.74]), and motor impulsivity (ρ = 0.45, 95% CI [0.16, 0.69]). In a multivariable linear regression including all three impulsivity facets simultaneously, only non-planning impulsivity remained independently associated with IGD symptom severity (B = 4.15, 95% CI [1.63, 6.67], p = .002; model R² = 0.45). Selected exploratory comparisons suggested stronger associations of non-planning impulsivity with problems and deception than with preoccupation or escape. Age was not significantly correlated with impulsivity or IGD severity, and sensitivity analyses did not indicate differences by ADHD pharmacotherapy status or parental psychiatric history.ConclusionsIn this clinical sample of male adolescents with ADHD, IGD symptom severity was positively associated with all three impulsivity facets in bivariate analyses, while non-planning impulsivity retained an independent association in multivariable analysis after accounting for shared variance among facets. Background

Gaming is a common leisure activity in adolescence, yet excessive and poorly controlled gaming can result in clinically significant impairment [1]. Because adolescence involves increasing academic demands, shifting sleep schedules, and growing autonomy in routines, persistent gaming can be particularly disruptive when it displaces sleep, schoolwork, and offline social and physical activities [2]. Reflecting growing clinical concern, the DSM-5 introduced Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) as a condition warranting further study [3], and the ICD-11 later formalized Gaming Disorder [4]. Epidemiologic evidence indicates a higher prevalence of problematic gaming among adolescent males [5, 6].

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a prevalent neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity [3]. Across adolescent studies, ADHD symptoms and/or diagnosis have been repeatedly associated with higher levels of problematic gaming and IGD symptoms [7–9]. This co-occurrence is clinically important because gaming-related impairment may add to academic, family, and social difficulties already common in ADHD. Recent reviews further suggest that the ADHD–gaming relationship is clinically meaningful and multifactorial, involving impulsivity, inattention, emotional dysregulation, reward-related vulnerability, and social or family-contextual factors [10–12]. ADHD-related self-regulatory difficulties may increase susceptibility to problematic gaming, while excessive gaming may, in turn, aggravate attentional difficulties [9, 10, 12]. Neurobiological and clinical studies likewise support partially overlapping vulnerability profiles, including impulsivity-related differences in ADHD with comorbid IGD symptoms [13, 14].

Importantly, clinically characterized samples are already represented in this literature. In a clinically diagnosed sample of children and adolescents with ADHD, Berloffa et al. [15] found high IGD burden and showed that ADHD youth with gaming-related problems had greater symptom severity, functional impairment, and broader emotional and social difficulties. In a treatment-seeking clinical sample of patients with gaming disorder, Cabelguen et al. [7] reported a substantial rate of ADHD symptom positivity and identified impulsivity-related negative urgency and low self-esteem as factors associated with ADHD–gaming comorbidity. More recently, Zhuang et al. [14] showed that adolescents with ADHD and comorbid IGD differed from those with ADHD alone on several experimentally assessed dimensions of impulsivity, including response inhibition, delay discounting, and risky decision-making. Together, these findings strengthen the view that impulsivity and related self-regulatory vulnerabilities are central to ADHD–IGD overlap rather than peripheral correlates.

Impulsivity is therefore a plausible shared feature of both ADHD and IGD [8, 12, 16, 17]. Conceptually, impulsivity is multidimensional—often operationalized as attentional, motor, and non-planning facets—and these dimensions may plausibly relate differently to problematic gaming behaviors [18]. In parallel, DSM-5 IGD criteria capture heterogeneous symptom domains, including preoccupation, tolerance-like experiences, withdrawal, impaired control, gaming for escape, and continued gaming despite harm [2]. This heterogeneity raises the possibility that specific impulsivity facets may show domain-specific associations that are obscured when analyses rely only on total impulsivity or total IGD scores. Although impulsivity is generally associated with IGD severity in adolescents, evidence is mixed regarding whether attentional, motor, or non-planning facets show the strongest and most consistent links [18, 19]. Moreover, Ghiaccio et al. [12] noted that the reviewed studies generally did not adequately differentiate impulsivity subtypes, particularly in terms of cognitive versus motor components.

Despite review-level and clinically based evidence linking ADHD and problematic gaming, the existing literature remains methodologically heterogeneous, with substantial variation in case definitions and in the instruments used to operationalize problematic gaming, internet addiction, IGD, and GD. Prior work has also mainly emphasized prevalence, broad clinical correlates, or task-based group differences rather than whether self-reported attentional, motor, and non-planning impulsivity are differentially associated with specific DSM-5-aligned IGD symptom domains in clinically characterized adolescents with ADHD. Thus, although the broader association between ADHD, impulsivity, and problematic gaming is well supported, an important question remains regarding whether distinct impulsivity facets map onto different IGD symptom domains within this clinical population.

In this study, we aimed to examine associations between impulsivity (BIS-11-SF) and IGD symptoms (IGDS-27; nine DSM-5-aligned domains) in a clinical sample of male adolescents with ADHD. Given the higher prevalence of problematic gaming among males and our clinic sampling frame, we focused on male adolescents. We prespecified a primary expectation that higher overall impulsivity would be associated with greater overall IGD symptom severity. As a secondary, hypothesis-generating aim, we conducted an exploratory examination of facet-by-domain associations to generate preliminary evidence and inform future studies.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study with an exploratory analytic design. The primary analysis tested the prespecified association between BIS-11-SF total and IGDS-27 total. Secondary analyses examined correlations between BIS-11-SF total/subscales and IGDS-27 total/domain scores as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Participants and procedure

Participants were recruited from child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinics in December 2025 and January 2026. Eligible adolescents were male patients with ADHD for whom excessive gaming was identified during routine outpatient assessment as a prominent current clinical concern based on adolescent self-report, caregiver report, and clinician judgment. Because this was a naturalistic clinical study, “prominent clinical concern” was determined pragmatically during routine assessment rather than by a single predefined cutoff.

All participants had a clinical diagnosis of ADHD, combined presentation, confirmed by trained child and adolescent psychiatrists using the Turkish adaptation of the Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Age Children—Present and Lifetime Version for DSM-5 (K-SADS-PL-DSM-5-T). To reduce heterogeneity and limit major psychiatric confounding in this small exploratory study, adolescents with psychiatric comorbidities were excluded, with the exception of specific phobia, which was considered less likely than the other excluded disorders to substantially influence impulsivity or gaming-symptom reporting. Of 59 adolescents with ADHD assessed for eligibility, 14 (23.7%) were excluded because of comorbidities, including oppositional defiant disorder (n = 5), depressive disorder (n = 5), social anxiety disorder (n = 3), and obsessive-compulsive disorder (n = 1). Thus, the final sample comprised 45 adolescents, including 6 with specific phobia.

No a priori power calculation was conducted because analyses were exploratory; the sample reflects consecutive eligible patients during the recruitment window. The final sample comprised 45 male adolescents with ADHD (M age = 14.10 years, SD = 1.25). Participants received no compensation. Written informed consent/assent was obtained from adolescents and their legal guardians. Ethical approval was obtained from the Eskişehir City Hospital Scientific Research Ethical Committee (ESH/BAEK 2025/282), which approved both sites after local administrative approval from the second hospital.

Gaming frequency was evaluated separately for weekdays and weekends, initially coded as up to one hour = 1, 1–2 h = 2, 2–3 h = 3, 3–4 h = 4, and more than 4 h = 5, later re-grouped as more than 4 h/day versus 4 h/day or less. Current ADHD pharmacotherapy status was extracted from electronic medical records and coded as yes (1) for participants receiving methylphenidate or atomoxetine, and no (0) otherwise. Parental psychiatric disorders were assessed based on parent report. Questionnaires were self-administered in the clinic setting.

Measures

Diagnostic assessment. Current ADHD diagnosis and comorbidity screening were conducted using the semi-structured clinical interview originally developed by Kaufman et al. (1997) and later adapted to DSM-5, which was validated for use in the Turkish population as K-SADS-PL-DSM-5-T [20, 21].

Impulsivity. Impulsivity was assessed using the 15-item Barratt Impulsiveness Scale—Short Form (BIS-11-SF), adapted by Spinella (2007) from the original BIS-11 [22, 23]. The BIS-11-SF yields a total score and three 5-item subscales (attentional, motor, and non-planning impulsivity). Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater impulsivity (including reverse-scored items reflecting lower impulsivity). In the original study, principal components analysis supported the expected three-factor structure; the factors explained 33.4% of the variance, and internal consistency for the total score was good (α = 0.81). Convergent validity was supported by a strong correlation with the BIS-11 total score (r = .94, p < .001) [23]. The Turkish adaptation study [24] also supported the three-factor structure and reported Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.82 for the total score, 0.64 for attentional impulsivity, 0.70 for motor impulsivity, and 0.80 for non-planning impulsivity. In the present sample, internal consistency was good for the BIS-11-SF total score (Cronbach’s α = 0.83) and acceptable for the non-planning (α = 0.72) and attentional (α = 0.72) subscales, while internal consistency for the motor impulsivity subscale was questionable (α = 0.67).

Internet Gaming Disorder Symptoms. We selected the IGDS-27 because the primary aim of this study was exploratory symptom-domain mapping (i.e., DSM-5 criterion-level patterns) rather than diagnostic classification. The IGDS-27 assesses nine DSM-5–aligned IGD criteria with three items per domain, which allowed domain-level analyses in relation to impulsivity facets: preoccupation (salience/mental occupation), tolerance (need for increasing time), withdrawal (negative affect when unable to game), persistence (unsuccessful control attempts), escape (mood regulation), displacement (reduced other interests), problems (continued use despite harm), deception (concealment/lying), and conflict (jeopardized roles/relationships) [25]. Items are rated on a 6-point frequency scale (0 = never to 5 = almost daily). Criterion scores range from 0 to 15 and the total score from 0 to 135, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. In the original validation study, internal consistency for the IGDS-27 total score was excellent (α = 0.94), and a second-order factor structure (nine criteria loading onto a higher-order IGD factor) showed acceptable fit (χ²(315) = 959.42, CFI = 0.991, WRMR = 1.005, RMSEA = 0.047 [90% CI 0.043–0.050]) [25]. Criterion-related validity was supported by associations with gaming time (r = .23) and psychosocial correlates such as loneliness (r = .41) and aggression (r = .60) [25]. The Turkish adaptation and validation of the measure were conducted in two studies, which reported good psychometric properties for the polytomous form [26, 27]. In the present sample, the IGDS-27 demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.92). For descriptive purposes only, we derived a non-diagnostic DSM-5-aligned symptom-domain endorsement count from the IGDS-27. Because each domain is represented by three items, a domain was considered descriptively endorsed when at least one corresponding item was rated as occurring once a week or more (score ≥ 4). The number of descriptively endorsed domains was then summed to yield a 0–9 count. This operationalization was used only to characterize symptom burden and should not be interpreted as a formal diagnostic classification or validated cutoff.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in SPSS (Version 22.0). Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables; continuous variables are reported with both mean (SD) and median (IQR), and categorical variables as n (%). Normality was evaluated using Shapiro–Wilk tests and visual inspection of histograms. Because several variables showed non-normal distributions, rank-based nonparametric methods were used for the primary bivariate analyses. No missing data were present.

The primary analysis tested the prespecified association between overall impulsivity and overall IGD symptom severity using Spearman’s rank correlation (BIS-11-SF total with IGDS-27 total). Secondary exploratory analyses examined Spearman correlations between BIS-11-SF total and subscale scores (attentional, motor, non-planning) and IGDS-27 total and domain scores. Associations between age and impulsivity/IGD scores were also examined using Spearman correlations. Exploratory sensitivity analyses used Mann–Whitney U tests to examine group differences by ADHD pharmacotherapy status (methylphenidate/atomoxetine: yes vs. no) and parental psychiatric history (yes vs. no), selected a priori as potentially relevant clinical/family-context variables.

Correlations are reported as effect sizes (Spearman’s ρ). To quantify uncertainty, 95% bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap confidence intervals were estimated for Spearman correlations using 5,000 bootstrap resamples. Because a relatively large number of criterion-level correlations were examined, these analyses were treated as exploratory and interpretation prioritized effect sizes and bootstrap confidence intervals. As a secondary check for multiplicity, Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR; q = 0.05) adjusted p values were also calculated for the exploratory BIS×IGDS outcome correlation set (40 tests: 4 BIS indices × 10 IGDS-27 outcomes) and reported in Supplementary Table S1. As an additional post hoc exploratory step, we conducted a small number of direct comparisons between selected dependent overlapping correlations using cocor [28]. Because exhaustive pairwise testing across all facet-by-domain associations would have generated an excessive number of comparisons in this modest sample, we restricted these analyses to a few clinically interpretable contrasts showing relatively larger apparent differences in the correlation matrix.

To complement bivariate analyses and account for shared variance among impulsivity facets, a multivariable linear regression model was fitted with IGDS-27 total score as the dependent variable and BIS-11-SF attentional, motor, and non-planning subscale scores entered simultaneously as predictors. We report unstandardized and standardized coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, and model fit indices (R²/adjusted R² and overall F test). Given the exploratory design and modest sample size, this model was used to examine independent association patterns rather than to support causal inference. Regression assumptions were evaluated using visual inspection of residual plots (linearity, homoscedasticity, and residual normality), Cook’s distance (influence), and variance inflation factors (multicollinearity).

Results

Sample characteristics and gaming exposure

The sample comprised 45 male adolescents with ADHD (ages 12–17) in Grades 6–11 (M grade = 8.60, SD = 1.37; M age = 14.10, SD = 1.25). Most reported gaming more than 4 h/day on weekdays (33/45, 73.3%) and weekends (34/45, 75.6%). Regarding ADHD pharmacotherapy (methylphenidate/atomoxetine), 17 participants (37.8%) were receiving treatment, and 28 (62.2%) were not. In family history indicators, at least one parent reported a psychiatric diagnosis in 11 (24.4%) participants.

Descriptive statistics (including central tendency and dispersion) for all scale scores are presented in Table 1. The IGDS-27 total score showed a broad distribution in this clinically selected sample (mean = 57.00, SD = 29.08; median = 49.00; IQR = 44; range = 1–116), with no marked skewness (skewness = 0.19). For interpretability, the mean IGDS-27 total score corresponds to an average item score of 2.11 across the 27 items. In this study, the IGDS-27 polytomous total score was treated as a continuous indicator of DSM-5–aligned IGD symptom severity rather than as a validated case-defining cutoff. Because the study aimed to examine criterion-level symptom patterns and did not apply an impairment-based diagnostic algorithm, participants were not classified as “at-risk” or “disordered” on the basis of the total score alone.

Impulsivity facets and IGD symptom domains

IGDS-27 total score was positively associated with BIS-11-SF total impulsivity (ρ = 0.62, BCa 95% CI [0.38, 0.78]) and with each impulsivity facet, including motor (ρ = 0.45, BCa 95% CI [0.16, 0.69]), attentional (ρ = 0.52, BCa 95% CI [0.23, 0.74]), and non-planning impulsivity (ρ = 0.56, BCa 95% CI [0.30, 0.74]) (Table 2). Direct comparisons of dependent overlapping correlations did not show significant differences between the associations of IGD total score with motor, attentional, and non-planning impulsivity (all ps ≥ 0.438).

At the IGD domain level, exploratory analyses showed heterogeneous association patterns across DSM-5–aligned symptom domains and impulsivity facets (Table 2). To examine whether a few clinically interpretable associations with relatively larger apparent differences differed in magnitude, we conducted a limited number of post hoc comparisons of dependent overlapping correlations. Non-planning impulsivity was more strongly associated with the IGD problems domain than with either the escape domain (ρ = 0.62 vs. 0.21; Williams’ t(42) = 2.43, p = .019; Zou 95% CI for the difference: 0.07 to 0.75) or the preoccupation domain (ρ = 0.62 vs. 0.31; Williams’ t(42) = 2.36, p = .023; Zou 95% CI: 0.04 to 0.59). Non-planning impulsivity was also more strongly associated with deception than with escape (ρ = 0.56 vs 0.21; Williams’ t(42) = 2.14, p = .038; Zou 95% CI: 0.02 to 0.67). In addition, motor impulsivity was more strongly associated with displacement than with preoccupation (ρ = 0.47 vs. 0.17; Williams’ t(42) = 2.08, p = .044; Zou 95% CI: 0.01 to 0.59). These limited comparisons should be interpreted as exploratory.

Because a relatively large number of exploratory correlations were examined, we additionally applied Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction. Across 40 exploratory Spearman tests, 31 associations remained significant after Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction (q < 0.05; Supplementary Table S1).

Multivariable regression analysis

To complement the bivariate correlations, we conducted a multivariable linear regression with IGDS-27 total score as the dependent variable and BIS-11-SF motor, attentional, and non-planning impulsivity subscales entered simultaneously (see Table 3). The overall model was significant, F(3, 41) = 11.29, p < .001, explaining 45.2% of the variance in IGDS-27 total score (R² = 0.452; adjusted R² = 0.412). Visual inspection of residual diagnostics (histogram, normal P–P plot, and standardized residuals vs. predicted values) did not indicate major violations of linearity, homoscedasticity, or residual normality. No highly influential observations were observed (maximum Cook’s distance = 0.217).

Secondary analyses and sensitivity checks

In a secondary analysis, age was not significantly correlated with impulsivity scores or overall IGD severity. Specifically, age was not associated with BIS-11-SF total score (ρ = 0.13, p = .385) or IGDS-27 total score (ρ = −0.19, p = .210). At the domain level, correlations between age and IGD criteria were not statistically significant; the largest one was a negative association with Preoccupation (ρ = −0.27, p = .079).

Exploratory Mann–Whitney U tests indicated no differences in impulsivity or IGD severity by (a) parental known psychiatric diagnosis (yes: n = 11 vs. no: n = 34) or (b) ADHD treatment status (yes: n = 17 vs. no: n = 28). For example, IGDS-27 total score did not differ by parental psychiatric history (U = 175.5, Z = − 0.30, p = .761) or current ADHD pharmacotherapy (U = 206.0, Z = − 0.75, p = .454). Similarly, BIS-11-SF total score did not differ across these groupings (ps ≥ 0.145).

Discussion

The present study found that impulsivity was broadly associated with Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) symptom severity in a clinical sample of male adolescents with ADHD. IGDS-27 total score was positively correlated with BIS-11-SF total and all three impulsivity facets (motor, attentional, and non-planning), supporting the view that impulsivity is relevant to problematic gaming in this high-risk group. In a multivariable regression model including all three BIS-11-SF facets simultaneously, only non-planning impulsivity remained independently associated with IGD symptom severity, whereas attentional and motor impulsivity did not. This suggests that overlap among impulsivity facets contributed to the bivariate association pattern. At the domain level, exploratory analyses did not support a uniform pattern across DSM-5–aligned IGD symptom domains. Rather, in a small number of post hoc direct comparisons, non-planning impulsivity was more strongly associated with the IGD problems domain than with the escape and preoccupation domains, and more strongly associated with deception than with escape. In addition, motor impulsivity was more strongly associated with displacement than with preoccupation.

Our results converge with and extend prior literature [18, 19], emphasizing that impulsivity in IGD is multifaceted and underscoring cross-study heterogeneity regarding which impulsivity facet appears most salient across studies. The multivariable model nonetheless suggests that non-planning impulsivity showed the clearest independent association with overall IGD symptom severity in this sample after accounting for shared variance among BIS facets. This pattern is broadly compatible with prior work showing that multiple impulsivity domains are relevant to IGD [18], while also aligning with the heterogeneity noted in the Nuske et al. meta-analysis [19], which cautioned against strong subtype-specific conclusions due to between-study variability and limited multivariable evidence.

Prior work supports a broad association between IGD and multiple domains of impulsivity, although facet patterns vary across samples and measures. Studies have reported higher BIS total/subscale scores in IGD groups, and associations between IGD severity and attentional and non-planning impulsivity in clinical or community samples [29, 30]. Complementing self-report work, adolescent findings linking IGD to decisional impulsivity on behavioral tasks suggest that future-oriented self-control processes may also be implicated [31]. Measurement-focused analyses integrating multiple impulsivity frameworks further suggest that different impulsivity components may relate to gaming involvement rather than disorder-level thresholds, supporting symptom- and domain-level approaches rather than treating impulsivity as unitary [32].

In our study, non-planning impulsivity showed stronger associations with problems and deception than with escape or preoccupation, while motor impulsivity showed a stronger association with displacement than with preoccupation. Facet profiles also differ by context. For example, in a large college sample, motor impulsivity showed stronger associations with several IGD dimensions than non-planning, and tolerance showed minimal correlations [33]. Differences across studies may reflect sample characteristics (clinical ADHD adolescents vs. non-clinical young adults), symptom operationalization (DSM-5–aligned criteria vs. alternative multidimensional scales), and construct measurement (task-based decisional indices vs. BIS non-planning, which captures self-reported planfulness).

Interestingly, although selected domain-level associations differed in strength, the correlations of IGD total severity with motor, attentional, and non-planning impulsivity did not differ significantly. This pattern suggests that facet-specific differentiation may be more visible at the level of individual IGD symptom domains than at the level of global IGD severity. Clinically, these findings suggest that assessing impulsivity multidimensionally may be useful in adolescents with ADHD and gaming-related difficulties.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the sample consisted exclusively of male adolescents to reduce confounding due to the highly unbalanced sex distribution; therefore, findings cannot be generalized to female adolescents with ADHD. This sample also had very high gaming exposure, indicating a high-risk clinical subgroup that may further limit generalizability. Moreover, the study used restrictive psychiatric exclusion criteria to reduce heterogeneity in a small exploratory clinical sample. Although this may have improved internal interpretability, it also reduced clinical representativeness and limited external validity. Of 59 adolescents assessed for eligibility, 14 (23.7%) were excluded because of psychiatric comorbidities, while 6 participants with specific phobias were retained. Because psychiatric comorbidity is common in routine ADHD outpatient settings, these findings may not fully generalize to broader clinically representative ADHD populations.

Second, the modest sample size (N = 45) limited statistical power and precision, particularly for small effects. A post hoc sensitivity analysis (G*Power) indicated that, with two-tailed α = 0.05 and 80% power, the study was able to detect correlations of approximately |ρ| = 0.39 or larger. Accordingly, some smaller associations may have gone undetected. However, not all non-significant exploratory associations should be interpreted in the same way. Given the modest sample size, some may reflect limited power to detect small effects. However, other null findings may also indicate that certain impulsivity facets are only weakly related, or not meaningfully related, to particular IGD symptom domains in this sample. In particular, several non-significant associations were accompanied by relatively small point estimates, which is more compatible with little or no substantial relationship, whereas others showed positive point estimates with wide confidence intervals, suggesting residual uncertainty. Accordingly, the present findings do not support a uniform association pattern across all impulsivity facets and IGD domains.

Third, reliance on self-report measures of both impulsivity and IGD symptoms may have inflated associations due to shared-method variance, and we did not include behavioral tasks assessing inhibition or decision-making. Accordingly, the observed correlations may be inflated relative to associations estimated using multi-informant or behavioral measures. Interpretation of motor-impulsivity findings should be cautious because internal consistency for the BIS-11-SF motor subscale was only questionable in the present sample. We also did not measure ADHD symptom severity, which may confound impulsivity-IGD associations. Some observed associations may partly reflect shared variance with overall ADHD severity rather than facet-specific impulsivity effects.

A further measurement-related limitation concerns the choice of IGD instrument. This study used the IGDS-27, a DSM-5–aligned instrument, because our aim was exploratory mapping of impulsivity facets across DSM-5 symptom domains rather than ICD-11-based diagnostic classification. However, this choice also introduces limitations. DSM-5 IGD criteria are broader than the more condensed ICD-11 gaming disorder framework, and some DSM-5-aligned domains may be less disorder-specific. In addition, measurement quality varies across available IGD instruments, and the IGDS-27 has been rated less favorably than some alternatives in comparative reviews [34]. Accordingly, our domain-level findings should be interpreted as exploratory DSM-5-based symptom-pattern findings rather than definitive evidence about ICD-11 gaming disorder structure. In addition, our symptom-domain endorsement count was a descriptive proxy derived from IGDS-27 response frequencies rather than a validated diagnostic algorithm, and no impairment-based diagnostic classification was established.

Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about directionality, and symptom-level analyses involved multiple tests; these patterns should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, the correlation-comparison analyses were selected post hoc and were not intended as an exhaustive test of all possible facet-by-domain differences. Future studies should replicate these exploratory correlation-comparison findings in larger samples and use multivariable symptom-domain models to test whether these patterns remain after accounting for shared variance.

Conclusions

In this clinical sample of male adolescents with ADHD, overall IGD symptom severity showed moderate-to-strong associations with self-reported impulsivity across all BIS-11-SF facets, and non-planning impulsivity showed an independent association in the multivariable model. At the symptom-domain level, a small number of exploratory post hoc comparisons suggested that non-planning impulsivity was more strongly associated with problems than with escape or preoccupation, and with deception than with escape, while motor impulsivity was more strongly associated with displacement than with preoccupation. These findings should be interpreted as exploratory due to the modest sample size, multiple testing, reliance on self-report instruments, and cross-sectional design. Replication in larger, sex-balanced samples incorporating ADHD symptom severity indices, clinician-rated outcomes, and behavioral measures of impulsivity is needed to clarify the specificity and directionality of these associations and to determine whether impulsivity profiles can inform targeted prevention and intervention strategies for gaming-related problems in ADHD.
