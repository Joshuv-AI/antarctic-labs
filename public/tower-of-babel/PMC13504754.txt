Robot-assisted sacroiliac screw fixation for tile type C posterior pelvic ring injuries: a comparative study
Authors: Bin Luo, Yan Huang, Yongjun Wu, Shengkang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the clinical efficacy and safety of robot-assisted versus conventional fluoroscopy-guided percutaneous sacroiliac screw fixation for Tile type C posterior pelvic ring injuries.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 60 patients with Tile type C posterior pelvic ring injuries treated at our institution from January 2021 to December 2023. Patients were stratified into two groups based on surgical technique: a robot-assisted group (n = 31, TiRobot-assisted percutaneous sacroiliac screw fixation) and a conventional group (n = 29, C-arm fluoroscopy-guided percutaneous sacroiliac screw fixation). Group allocation reflected the surgical pathway available during the study period rather than randomisation. The following parameters were compared between groups: operative time, intraoperative fluoroscopy frequency, intraoperative blood loss, screw placement accuracy, number of guide-wire adjustments, fracture healing time, complications, and Majeed scores at final follow-up.ResultsAll patients completed 12–24 months of follow-up (mean 15.6 months). No significant difference in operative time was observed between the robot-assisted group (78.5 ± 15.2 min) and the conventional group (82.3 ± 18.6 min) (P > 0.05). The robot-assisted group demonstrated significantly fewer intraoperative fluoroscopy exposures (8.2 ± 2.1 vs. 18.6 ± 4.5, P < 0.001), fewer guide-wire adjustments (1.3 ± 0.6 vs. 4.2 ± 1.8, P < 0.001), and less intraoperative blood loss (45.2 ± 12.8 mL vs. 68.5 ± 18.3 mL, P < 0.001). The excellent-and-good screw placement rate was 97.96% (48/49) in the robot-assisted group versus 86.36% (38/44) in the conventional group (P = 0.042). No significant difference was found in fracture healing time. Majeed functional scores at final follow-up were higher in the robot-assisted group (85.6 ± 8.2) than in the conventional group (81.2 ± 9.5) (P = 0.049). No severe complications occurred in the robot-assisted group, whereas one case of L5 nerve-root irritation associated with screw malposition occurred in the conventional group.ConclusionFor Tile type C posterior pelvic ring injuries, robot-assisted percutaneous sacroiliac screw fixation was associated with higher screw placement accuracy, lower intraoperative radiation exposure, fewer guide-wire adjustments, reduced blood loss, and favourable early functional outcomes compared with conventional fluoroscopy-guided fixation. These findings, derived from a single-centre retrospective cohort, should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and warrant confirmation in prospective multicentre studies.Keywords: Pelvic fracture, Sacroiliac screw, Robot-assisted surgery, Tile type C, Minimally invasive

Introduction

Pelvic fractures account for 1–3% of all fractures and predominantly result from high-energy trauma, frequently accompanied by severe haemodynamic instability and high mortality rates [1, 2]. According to the Tile classification, type C pelvic fractures represent vertically and rotationally (horizontally) unstable injuries characterised by complete disruption of both the anterior and posterior pelvic rings, resulting in complete loss of pelvic ring integrity in all planes [3]. For such injuries, restoration of pelvic ring stability is the cornerstone of treatment, with sacroiliac screw fixation serving as a primary method for posterior ring stabilisation and achieving widespread clinical application [4, 5].

Percutaneous sacroiliac screw fixation offers significant advantages including minimal surgical trauma, reduced blood loss, and rapid recovery, and has become a standard surgical approach for treating posterior pelvic ring injuries [6]. However, the complex anatomy of the sacroiliac region and the proximity of critical neurovascular structures present considerable technical challenges. Conventional fluoroscopy-guided screw placement is limited by insufficient accuracy, excessive fluoroscopy exposure, and a prolonged learning curve [7, 8]. Reported screw malposition rates with conventional techniques range from 2 to 15%, potentially leading to serious complications such as neural injury [9].

Recent advances in robot-assisted orthopaedic surgical systems have provided new technological solutions for precise sacroiliac screw placement. The TiRobot system is an orthopaedic surgical robot platform independently developed in China, which has received approval from the National Medical Products Administration and achieved extensive clinical application [10]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that robot-assisted technology can enhance screw placement accuracy, reduce intraoperative fluoroscopy exposures, and minimise radiation exposure [11–13]. More recently, comparative studies of the TiRobot system and of other robotic platforms have further supported these findings for sacroiliac screw fixation [11, 13, 14]. However, published reports specifically addressing robot-assisted surgery for Tile type C pelvic injuries remain limited, and most existing studies lack a detailed consideration of surgeon experience.

This study retrospectively analysed clinical data from our institution comparing robot-assisted versus conventional fluoroscopy-guided percutaneous sacroiliac screw fixation for Tile type C posterior pelvic ring injuries. We evaluated clinical efficacy and safety outcomes, examined the potential influence of surgeon experience, and sought to provide preliminary evidence to inform clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Study population

A retrospective analysis was conducted on 60 consecutive patients with Tile type C posterior pelvic ring injuries treated in the trauma orthopaedic department of our institution from January 2021 to December 2023.

Inclusion criteria

(1) age 18–65 years; (2) Tile type C pelvic fracture confirmed on preoperative CT by two independent senior trauma surgeons; (3) posterior ring injury requiring sacroiliac screw fixation; (4) follow-up duration ≥ 12 months; and (5) complete clinical records.

Exclusion criteria

(1) old or malunited pelvic fractures (> 3 weeks from injury); (2) pathological fractures; (3) severe medical comorbidities precluding surgical intervention; and (4) prior sacroiliac joint surgery.

Patients were divided into two groups according to the surgical technique used during the study period: a robot-assisted group (n = 31) treated with TiRobot-assisted percutaneous sacroiliac screw fixation, and a conventional group (n = 29) treated with C-arm fluoroscopy-guided percutaneous sacroiliac screw fixation. Allocation to a particular group was determined by the equipment and surgical pathway available at the time of treatment rather than by randomisation; this is acknowledged as a limitation of the retrospective design. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, injury severity score (ISS), fracture subtype, and time from injury to surgery, showed no significant differences between groups (P > 0.05), confirming comparability (Table 1). This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed under general anaesthesia. For patients with concomitant anterior ring injuries, anterior ring reduction and fixation was performed first (symphyseal separation managed with plate fixation; pubic rami fractures managed with an internal fixator [INFIX] or plate fixation), followed by posterior ring sacroiliac screw fixation. In both groups, 7.3-mm cannulated screws (Implants were supplied by Double Medical Technology Inc., Xiamen, Fujian, China)were used.

Robot-assisted group

After pelvic reduction was achieved in the supine position, a patient tracker was secured to the iliac crest. C-arm fluoroscopy was performed in anteroposterior, inlet, and outlet views, followed by three-dimensional image reconstruction. Digital images were transmitted to the robotic workstation (TiRobot, Beijing TINAVI Medical Technologies, Beijing, China) for screw-trajectory planning. The entry point and screw direction were determined, and a 7.3-mm cannulated screw was selected for each planned trajectory. The robotic arm automatically advanced to the planned position. After fluoroscopic verification, a guide wire was inserted along the guiding sleeve, with subsequent fluoroscopic confirmation of satisfactory position. Screw depth was measured, the tract was tapped, and the cannulated screw was inserted. S1 and/or S2 screws were placed according to the injury pattern.

Conventional group

After pelvic reduction was achieved in the supine position, C-arm fluoroscopy was used to localise the entry point. Under fluoroscopic guidance in anteroposterior, inlet, and outlet views, the guide wire was manually inserted and adjusted iteratively until a satisfactory position was achieved. A true lateral (lateral sacral) view was additionally obtained in every case to confirm that the guide wire and screw remained within the bony corridor and had not breached the anterior sacral cortex or the iliac cortical density line. After confirming that the guide wire had not penetrated the sacral cortex, screw depth was measured, the tract was tapped, and a 7.3-mm cannulated screw was inserted.

Postoperative management

Postoperative protocols were identical for both groups. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered within 24 h after surgery, and low-molecular-weight heparin was administered subcutaneously for deep venous thrombosis prophylaxis. Bed-based functional exercises commenced on postoperative day 1. Partial weight-bearing with crutches began at 2 weeks postoperatively, with gradual progression to full weight-bearing at 6–8 weeks according to fracture healing.

Surgeon information

The robot-assisted group was managed by three attending surgeons, each with experience of more than 50 pelvic fracture surgeries and more than 30 robot-assisted procedures (mean 58 ± 12 pelvic fracture cases). The conventional group was managed by two attending surgeons, each with experience of more than 100 pelvic fracture surgeries and more than 80 sacroiliac screw fixations (mean 130 ± 15 pelvic fracture cases). For each robot-assisted surgeon, fluoroscopy exposure during the initial 10 cases was approximately 12 ± 2 times, stabilising at approximately 8 ± 2 times thereafter. The conventional-group surgeons had completed their learning curves, with relatively stable performance. Because surgical technique and surgeon experience were not independent in this cohort (the more experienced surgeons used the conventional technique and the less experienced surgeons used the robot), the effect of the robotic system cannot be statistically separated from the effect of operator experience. This important confound is addressed in the Discussion and Limitations.

Outcome measures

Surgical parameters: operative time (from completion of pelvic reduction to completion of screw insertion), intraoperative fluoroscopy frequency, number of guide-wire adjustments, and intraoperative blood loss. A guide-wire adjustment was defined as any withdrawal and repositioning of the guide wire after its initial advancement, recorded prospectively in the operative note and verified against intraoperative fluoroscopic images. Intraoperative blood loss was calculated from the volume of fluid in the suction canister (after subtracting irrigation fluid) plus the estimated blood content of surgical gauze, weighed at the end of the procedure.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean±standard deviation and compared between groups using independent-samples t-tests. Categorical variables are presented as frequency (percentage) and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the modest sample size and the non-randomised design, all comparisons should be regarded as exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Results

Surgical parameters and screw distribution by level

Both groups completed the surgical procedures successfully. In the robot-assisted group, 49 screws were placed in 31 patients: 31 S1 screws (one per patient) and 18 S2 screws. In the conventional group, 44 screws were placed in 29 patients: 29 S1 screws (one per patient) and 15 S2 screws. S2 screws were added when a single S1 screw was judged to provide insufficient stability for the posterior injury pattern. No significant difference in operative time was found between groups (P > 0.05). The robot-assisted group demonstrated significantly fewer intraoperative fluoroscopy exposures, fewer guide-wire adjustments, and less blood loss than the conventional group (P < 0.001 for all; Table 2). Screw position assessment

Postoperative CT evaluation of screw positioning according to the Smith grading system revealed the following distribution in the robot-assisted group: grade 0, 42 screws (85.7%); grade 1, 6 screws (12.2%); grade 2, 1 screw (2.0%); and grade 3, 0 screws. The excellent-and-good placement rate was 97.96% (48/49). In the conventional group, the distribution was: grade 0, 28 screws (63.6%); grade 1, 10 screws (22.7%); grade 2, 4 screws (9.1%); and grade 3, 2 screws (4.5%). The excellent-and-good placement rate was 86.36% (38/44). The difference in excellent-and-good placement rates between groups was statistically significant (χ²=4.125, P = 0.042; Table 4).

Sacral morphology

Preoperative CT was reviewed for features of sacral dysmorphism (collinearity of the S1 body with the iliac crests, presence of mammillary tubercles, an acute or reversed alar slope, residual S1 disc, and non-circular upper sacral foramina). Sacral dysmorphism was identified in 7 of 31 patients (22.6%) in the robot-assisted group and 6 of 29 patients (20.7%) in the conventional group, with no significant difference between groups (P > 0.05). In dysmorphic sacra, S1 trans-sacral trajectories were avoided and oblique S1 and/or S2 screws were used as appropriate. Among the 13 patients with dysmorphic sacra, the single grade 2 malposition in the robot-assisted group and 3 of the 6 malpositioned screws in the conventional group occurred in dysmorphic anatomy, consistent with the recognised technical difficulty of screw placement in these patients.

Follow-up results

All patients completed follow-up for 12–24 months (mean 15.6 months). Fracture healing time was 10.2 ± 2.1 weeks in the robot-assisted group and 10.8 ± 2.5 weeks in the conventional group, with no significant difference (t = 1.024, P = 0.310). At final follow-up, Majeed functional scores were higher in the robot-assisted group (85.6 ± 8.2) than in the conventional group (81.2 ± 9.5) (t = 2.012, P = 0.049). In the robot-assisted group, 16 patients achieved excellent results, 12 good, and 3 fair, for an excellent-and-good rate of 90.3%. In the conventional group, 12 patients achieved excellent results, 11 good, 5 fair, and 1 poor, for an excellent-and-good rate of 79.3% (Table 5).

Complications

No severe complications, including neural or vascular injury, occurred in the robot-assisted group; one case of superficial wound infection was treated successfully with dressing changes. In the conventional group, one patient developed L5 nerve-root irritation (lower-extremity paraesthesia and pain); CT imaging demonstrated screw malposition with cortical perforation (Smith grade 3). Symptoms resolved completely after three months of conservative management, including neurotrophic therapy and physiotherapy. Two cases of superficial wound infection in the conventional group were treated successfully with dressing changes. The incidence of complications showed no significant difference between groups (P > 0.05). No internal fixation loosening or breakage occurred in either group during follow-up.

Discussion

Screw accuracy and the influence of surgeon experience

Tile type C pelvic fractures are completely unstable injuries with posterior ring disruption involving sacroiliac joint dislocation, sacral fractures, or iliac fractures [17]. The posterior ring transmits the majority of axial load between the spine and the lower limbs, so restoration of its stability is critical for patient outcomes [18]. Percutaneous sacroiliac screw fixation has become a standard approach for posterior ring stabilisation owing to its minimally invasive nature; however, conventional fluoroscopy-guided techniques present substantial technical challenges. The sacroiliac screw corridor is narrow and obliquely oriented, and lies adjacent to the L5 and S1 nerve roots and the sacral plexus.

In the present cohort, the robot-assisted group achieved a 97.96% excellent-and-good rate compared with 86.36% in the conventional group (P = 0.042), and this advantage was observed even though the robot-assisted surgeons were less experienced (mean 58 pelvic fracture cases) than the conventional-group surgeons (mean 130 cases). It is tempting to interpret this as evidence that the robotic system compensates for limited surgeon experience. However, we wish to be explicit that our study cannot support that as a firm conclusion. Because the less experienced surgeons used the robot and the more experienced surgeons used the conventional technique, surgeon experience and surgical technique are completely confounded; the design cannot separate a true technology effect from differences between these specific operators, and the comparison is further limited by the small, non-randomised sample. The most we can say is that the robotic workflow may reduce the dependence of screw accuracy on operator experience—a hypothesis that is biologically and mechanically plausible, consistent with prior reports [11, 14], and worth testing directly in an experience-stratified, randomised design. We have therefore reframed this observation as hypothesis-generating rather than as a demonstrated finding.

Wang and colleagues [11] demonstrated, in a prospective randomised controlled study, that robot-assisted sacroiliac screw fixation significantly improves screw placement accuracy, and Li and colleagues [14] reported similar gains in accuracy and reductions in fluoroscopy with the TiRobot system. Our results are directionally consistent with these reports. The robotic system is designed to achieve accurate guide-wire placement through preoperative three-dimensional trajectory planning and intraoperative navigation, reducing reliance on iterative fluoroscopic correction.

With respect to guide-wire adjustments, the robot-assisted group required significantly fewer adjustments (1.3 ± 0.6) than the conventional group (4.2 ± 1.8), a 69% relative reduction. Fewer adjustments may reduce the risk of bone-tract widening and loss of screw purchase from repeated passes [19], a particular concern in patients with compromised bone quality, in whom posterior pelvic bone density is itself an important determinant of screw anchorage [20].

Radiation exposure is another important consideration in sacroiliac screw fixation. Conventional techniques require repeated fluoroscopy to confirm guide-wire position, exposing both the surgical team and the patient to ionising radiation [21]. In this study, the robot-assisted group required approximately 56% fewer intraoperative fluoroscopy exposures (8.2 ± 2.1 vs. 18.6 ± 4.5). Because we recorded fluoroscopy frequency rather than dose, this should be read as a surrogate for radiation exposure rather than a direct dose measurement; nonetheless, the reduction is consistent with the wider navigation literature, although not all comparative studies have found a radiation advantage for 3D over 2D techniques [22]. Over a career, cumulative occupational radiation is a recognised concern for trauma surgeons, and any reproducible reduction is clinically meaningful.

Operative time

Operative times did not differ significantly between groups (robot-assisted 78.5 ± 15.2 min vs. conventional 82.3 ± 18.6 min; P = 0.384). Although robot-assisted procedures require additional time for equipment setup, patient registration, and three-dimensional image acquisition, the rapid and precise guide-wire placement and the minimal need for adjustment appear to offset this, resulting in comparable total operative times. This is consistent with the meta-analysis of Al-Naseem and colleagues [23], which found that robot-assisted technology did not increase operative time. In our cohort, mean operative time in the robot-assisted group fell from 87 ± 18 min for the earliest cases to 74 ± 12 min for later cases, suggesting a learning-curve effect; this within-group comparison is descriptive and was not formally tested.

Clinical efficacy

The Majeed score is a standard instrument for assessing functional recovery after pelvic fracture, covering pain, standing, sitting, sexual function, and work capacity [16]. At final follow-up, the robot-assisted group had higher Majeed scores (85.6 ± 8.2 vs. 81.2 ± 9.5; P = 0.049). The difference, while statistically significant at the conventional threshold, is modest in absolute terms and close to the boundary of significance, and its clinical importance relative to the minimal clinically important difference for the Majeed score is uncertain. The higher excellent-and-good rate (90.3% vs. 79.3%) did not reach significance (P = 0.232). Any functional advantage most plausibly reflects more accurate screw positioning and more stable fixation, but residual confounding by surgeon experience and unmeasured factors cannot be excluded.

Fracture healing time did not differ between groups (10.2 ± 2.1 vs. 10.8 ± 2.5 weeks; P = 0.310). This suggests that, once adequate reduction and stable fixation are achieved, the method of screw insertion does not materially affect healing, which is primarily governed by reduction quality and fixation stability—both satisfactory in both groups here.

Safety

Neural injury is among the most serious complications of sacroiliac screw fixation. Reported neural injury rates with conventional techniques range from 0.5 to 7.7% and correlate with the degree and location of screw malposition [24]. In this study, one conventional-group patient (3.4%) developed L5 nerve-root irritation associated with a grade 3 malposition (cortical perforation), with complete resolution after conservative management. No neural injury occurred in the robot-assisted group. Although the between-group difference in complication rates was not statistically significant—expected given the small sample—the higher placement accuracy in the robot-assisted group is mechanistically linked to a lower risk of cortical breach and therefore of neural injury.

Intraoperative blood loss was significantly lower in the robot-assisted group (45.2 ± 12.8 vs. 68.5 ± 18.3 mL; P < 0.001), a 34% relative reduction, most plausibly attributable to fewer guide-wire passes and puncture sites and hence less repetitive soft-tissue trauma. For pelvic fracture patients, reduced blood loss is clinically relevant, although the absolute volumes here are small.

S2 screw placement deserves particular comment. S2 screws can provide additional fixation but are technically more demanding because the S2 corridor is narrower than that of S1 [25]. In this cohort, the robot-assisted group placed 18 S2 screws with a 100% excellent-and-good rate and no malposition, whereas the conventional group placed 15 S2 screws with an 86.7% excellent-and-good rate (2 malpositioned). These numbers are small and the comparison is descriptive, but they are consistent with the report of Auron and colleagues [26], in whose series robotic guidance was associated with fewer fluoroscopy exposures and adjustments and no malposition.

Cost–benefit considerations and clinical implementation

Robot-assisted systems require substantial capital investment and maintenance, which is the principal barrier to wider adoption. A full health-economic evaluation was beyond the scope of this study, and the following remarks are qualitative. Potential offsets include reduced cumulative radiation exposure for staff and patients, and a possible reduction in malposition-related complications, the treatment of which can be costly. The suggestion that robotic assistance might shorten the surgeon learning curve and allow less experienced surgeons to approach the results of experienced surgeons is, as noted above, a hypothesis rather than a finding of this study; if confirmed, it could be particularly relevant to centres with limited access to high-volume pelvic surgeons. Formal cost-effectiveness analysis is required before any such claim can inform policy.

From the patient’s perspective, the observed Majeed difference (81.2 to 85.6) is numerically modest, and we are cautious about over-interpreting it. If real and reproducible, even small functional gains may be meaningful at the population level, particularly for younger patients returning to work, but this requires confirmation in adequately powered studies with patient-reported outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-centre retrospective study with non-randomised group allocation and consequent potential for selection bias. Second, surgical technique and surgeon experience are completely confounded, so the independent effect of the robotic system cannot be isolated; this is the most important limitation and tempers all between-group comparisons. Third, the sample is small (n = 60), limiting statistical power and precision, and several comparisons are descriptive. Fourth, radiation exposure was approximated by fluoroscopy frequency rather than measured dose. Fifth, follow-up was relatively short (12–24 months), so longer-term functional and implant outcomes remain to be defined. Adequately powered, prospective, multicentre randomised trials—ideally stratified by surgeon experience and incorporating measured radiation dose, patient-reported outcomes, and formal cost-effectiveness analysis—are needed to confirm the present observations.

Conclusions

In this single-centre retrospective cohort of Tile type C posterior pelvic ring injuries, robot-assisted percutaneous sacroiliac screw fixation was associated with higher screw placement accuracy (97.96% excellent/good), approximately 56% fewer intraoperative fluoroscopy exposures, fewer guide-wire adjustments, lower intraoperative blood loss, and favourable early Majeed functional scores, with no severe complications. We observed that less experienced surgeons using the robot achieved screw accuracy at least comparable to that of more experienced surgeons using the conventional technique; however, because technique and experience were confounded in this design, this observation is hypothesis-generating and does not establish that the robotic system reduces dependence on surgeon experience. These findings should be confirmed in prospective, multicentre, randomised studies before robot-assisted fixation is recommended for broad adoption on the basis of an experience-independent advantage.
