Sustainable Ag–Cu bimetallic nanozymes derived from Juglans regia husk exhibit multifunctional catalytic, antimicrobial, and antioxidant activities
Authors: Sona Ayadi Hassan, Leila Rezaei Somee, Parinaz Ghadam, Ahya Abdi Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid emergence of multidrug-resistant microorganisms, oxidative stress, and heavy metal pollution necessitates the development of sustainable and efficient therapeutic agents. Nanozymes, as enzyme-mimicking nanomaterials, offer high stability and tunable catalytic activity. In this study, we report a rapid, green, and cost-effective strategy for synthesizing multifunctional Ag–Cu alloy-like nanozymes using the aqueous extract of the green husk of Juglans regia (walnut), an abundant agricultural byproduct. Comprehensive physicochemical characterization confirmed the formation of spherical, crystalline Ag–Cu nanozymes with a uniform elemental distribution and an average core size of ~ 17 nm. The phytochemicals from the extract served as both producing and capping agents, ensuring colloidal stability and enhancing catalytic efficiency. The synthesized nanozymes exhibited robust peroxidase- and oxidase-like activities with favorable kinetic parameters. The nanozymes demonstrated optical stability, which serves as a key indicator of their structural integrity. Beyond their catalytic performance, the Ag–Cu nanozymes displayed broad-spectrum antimicrobial efficacy against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, including multidrug-resistant microorganism strains, as well as significant antifungal activity against Candida albicans and mature biofilms. The antioxidant capacity of the nanozymes was also evaluated, revealing radical scavenging activity attributed to surface-bound biomolecules and the intrinsic redox properties of the bimetallic core. The enhanced biological and catalytic performance of the Ag–Cu nanozymes, compared to monometallic counterparts, suggests a synergistic effect arising from their alloy-like structure and nanoscale dimensions. Introduction

Humanity is increasingly confronted by a web of interconnected global health and environmental challenges, including the accelerating crisis of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), heavy metal contamination, and oxidative stress. The excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) overwhelms intrinsic cellular antioxidant defenses, resulting in cellular and tissue damage1. Simultaneously, the rapid emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens, often reinforced by strong biofilm formation, has rendered conventional antimicrobial treatments progressively ineffective. Such recalcitrance arises from intrinsic antibiotic resistance, metabolic persistence, and horizontal gene transfer. Traditional antimicrobial strategies frequently fail due to systemic toxicity, poor bioavailability, high production costs, and limited scalability2.

Nanotechnology offers powerful opportunities to address these challenges through the rational design of nanoscale materials endowed with unique catalytic and biological functionalities. Among these, nanozymes (NZs)—nanomaterials displaying intrinsic enzyme-like catalytic activity—have emerged as promising alternatives to natural enzymes due to their superior stability, cost-effectiveness, and robustness3. NZs exhibit diverse catalytic behaviors (e.g., peroxidase, oxidase, haloperoxidase) that enable applications in biosensing, environmental remediation, oxidative stress regulation, and antimicrobial therapy4,5. Within the biomedical domain, the subclass of nanozybiotics—nanozymes with intrinsic antimicrobial activity—has attracted considerable attention for integrating catalytic and bactericidal properties6. For example, dextran-coated Fe3O4 NZs catalytically activate H2O2 to degrade biofilm exopolysaccharides, weakening bacterial biofilm integrity7. Likewise, biogenic Ag NZs effectively inhibit biofilm formation by pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa8. Overall, nanozybiotics present multifunctional platforms for combating MDR pathogens, mitigating oxidative stress, and addressing biofilm-associated infections. Recently, NZ-based artificial organelles have further demonstrated long-term catalytic stability, multifunctionality, and recyclability9.

Despite these advances, most conventional synthesis routes for NZs—such as chemical reduction, hydrothermal methods, and laser ablation—rely on toxic reducing or capping agents, require high energy input, and involve complex operational procedures. These drawbacks limit their sustainability, scalability, and environmental compatibility. At the same time, the growing accumulation of agricultural waste (AW) presents a serious ecological problem, highlighting the need for sustainable upcycling routes that transform low-value biomass into high-value functional materials10,11. The use of AW as a renewable precursor for NZ synthesis provides dual benefits: reducing pollution caused by biomass disposal and furnishing inexpensive, eco-friendly, and phytochemical-rich sources of natural reducing/stabilizing agents. Phytoconstituents such as polyphenols, organic acids, and polysaccharides naturally facilitate green reduction and stabilization during nanoparticle (NP) formation. In this context, Juglans regia green husks, notably rich in bioactive compounds including fatty acids, juglone, and polysaccharides, represent an ideal candidate for sustainable nanomaterial synthesis.

To enhance NZ catalytic performance, recent research trends emphasize multimetallic systems—particularly alloy or core–shell architectures—capable of synergistically improving electron transfer, surface energy, and structural stability12–16. Alloying multiple metallic components is thus an established approach for amplifying the functional diversity of NZs in biomedical and environmental applications. However, a critical scientific gap remains in the current literature. Existing works on Ag and Cu nanostructures synthesized via green routes largely focus on synthetic simplicity or baseline antibacterial activity, frequently overlooking the mechanistic aspects of alloy formation, catalytic synergy, and enzyme-like kinetics17–19. Moreover, while Juglans regia extracts have been employed individually for preparing Ag or CuO NPs8,20, no report to date has presented a comprehensive development of a biogenic bimetallic Ag–Cu alloy-like nanozybiotic system derived solely from Juglans regia extract, nor an in-depth examination of its synergistic catalytic mechanism and dual antioxidant/antibiofilm functionality. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop a sustainable synthesis protocol that not only valorizes AW but also elucidates the kinetic and mechanistic drivers of bimetallic synergy to overcome the limitations of single-metal green nanomaterials.

To address this problem statement, this study introduces a novel, sustainable, and cost-effective biosynthetic route for the rapid fabrication of Ag–Cu alloy-like nanozybiotics using aqueous extract of Juglans regia green husks as a sole biomediating agent. This approach simultaneously addresses key limitations of conventional NZ synthesis—toxicity, complexity, and high energy consumption—while valorizing AW into high-value nanocatalytic materials. The specific research highlights and novelties of this work are threefold: (1) For the first time, we report the synthesis of an Ag–Cu alloy- like nanozybiotic exclusively using Juglans regia green husk extract, leveraging its unique phytochemical profile for stabilizing the bimetallic structure; (2) we systematically describe, for the first time, how this biogenic alloy governs catalytic synergy, enzyme-mimetic behavior, and kinetic enhancement, providing a mechanistic understanding absent in previous green-synthesized systems; and (3) we demonstrate the comprehensive multifunctionality of these biogenic alloy-like nanozybiotics through a rigorous evaluation of antioxidant properties, antimicrobial performance, and anti-biofilm efficacy (Fig. 1). This work thus establishes an environmentally benign and versatile catalytic platform for tackling urgent biomedical and environmental challenges through green nanotechnology innovation.

Results and discussion

Biosynthesis and characterization mechanism

The successful biosynthesis of Ag–Cu NZs was initially indicated by a distinct color change in the reaction mixture compared to the control samples (Juglans regia (J. regia) husk extract and precursor solutions) (Fig. 2a–d). A rapid color shift observed within approximately 10 min indicated the immediate reduction of metal ions by bioactive phytochemicals (e.g., phenolics and carboxylates) present in the green husk extract21,22. To ensure complete reduction and stabilization, the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 24 h. The proposed mechanism involves the simultaneous reduction of Ag⁺ and Cu2+ ions, facilitated by their similar standard reduction potentials (0.33–0.80 V)23. The reduced atoms initially form nanoclusters, which aggregate and grow into the final alloy nanostructures, leveraging the diverse functional groups of the plant extract as both reducing and capping agents.

Optical properties and plasmonic behavior

The optical characteristics of the synthesized Ag–Cu NZs were elucidated via UV–Vis spectroscopy, as illustrated in Fig. 2e. The resulting spectrum displays a single, broad Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) band centered at approximately 511 nm. This peak position lies distinctly between the characteristic SPR maxima of pure Ag NPs (typically 400–420 nm)8,24 and pure Cu NPs (560–590 nm)25,26. The absence of discrete peaks attributable to individual metallic phases, coupled with this intermediate spectral signature, provides compelling evidence for the formation of a homogeneous Ag–Cu alloy-like rather than a physical mixture of segregated metal particles.

The observed spectral features can be attributed to three primary mechanisms:

The presence of a unified SPR band indicates a homogeneous interaction between Ag and Cu atoms at the electronic level. The observed red-shift relative to pure Ag NPs is ascribed Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) to the hybridization of conduction electrons from both metals, which alters the dielectric function of the NP27. Such electronic coupling is a definitive hallmark of bimetallic alloy formation, distinguishing it from simple core–shell or mixed structures.

The significant red-shift and spectral broadening are further influenced by the local refractive index surrounding the NPs. The dense coating of phytochemical capping agents—primarily phenolics and proteins derived from J. regia—increases the local dielectric constant around the metallic core. According to Mie theory, this change in the local environment induces a bathochromic (red) shift in the SPR wavelength28.

To assess structural integrity, the optical properties were monitored over a one-month period. The SPR peak remained stable at ~ 511 nm, exhibiting negligible shifts in position or changes in intensity throughout the monitoring duration. This exceptional stability confirms the formation of a thermodynamically stable Ag–Cu alloy-like core, which effectively mitigates the rapid oxidation of Cu and the aggregation of Ag particles commonly observed in physically mixed systems.

Particle size and morphology

DLS analysis revealed a hydrodynamic diameter of 77.3 nm with a PDI of 0.55. This high PDI value signifies a moderate-to-broad particle size distribution, which we acknowledge as a limitation inherent to this green synthesis approach due to the complex mixture of reducing agents in the plant extract (Fig. 3a). This polydispersity aligns with FESEM/TEM observations, where the discrepancy between DLS (77.3 nm) and the metallic core size ~ 17 nm (15.79 ± 2.75 nm) is attributed to the thick layer of biomolecules (proteins, polysaccharides) from the J. regia extract surrounding the core29–31. FESEM micrographs (Fig. 3b and c) revealed predominantly spherical NZs, with minor aggregation observed after drying on the substrate.

Crystalline structure and structural validation

The crystalline structure was investigated by XRD (Fig. 4a). The diffraction pattern exhibits four main peaks at approximately 38°, 44°, 64°, and 77°, corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of a face-centered cubic (FCC) structure. These peaks align with the JCPDS (Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards) database for Ag (JCPDS No. 04–0783). Crucially, no distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to separate metallic Cu or Cu oxide phases were observed. The absence of secondary phases, combined with the similarity in lattice parameters and atomic radii between Ag and Cu (< 15% difference), supports the formation of a homogeneous, alloy-like Ag–Cu structure rather than a physical mixture of separate phases.

To further validate the structural homogeneity, STEM-High-Angle Annular Dark-Field (STEM-HAADF) imaging (Fig. 5e) and elemental mapping were employed. STEM-EDX line-scanning across individual NZs (Fig. 2S) demonstrated a uniform Ag:Cu atomic ratio of 1:2. Surface chemistry

FTIR analysis (Fig. 4b) identified the functional groups responsible for stabilization. A broad band at 3400 cm−1 corresponds to O–H and N–H stretching from phenolic and amine groups in the J. regia extract. Peaks at 2900 cm−1 (C–H), 1600–1700 cm−1 (C=O), and 1000–1300 cm−1 (C–N, C–O) confirm the presence of biomolecules on the surface. Minor fluctuations observed above 3800 cm−1 were attributed to instrumental noise and do not correspond to specific chemical vibrations. The O- and N- containing functional group originate from the surface-bound capping agents, confirming that the phytochemicals anchor to the alloy-like metallic core without altering its crystalline structure32,33.

Enzymatic activity and kinetic analysis

The Ag–Cu NZs exhibited dual enzyme-mimicking activities, functioning as both peroxidase and oxidase mimics. The peroxidase-like activity was evaluated by the colorimetric oxidation of TMPD in the presence of H2O2, and quantified in Units per milligram (U/mg). One Unit (U) was defined as the amount of NZs required to produce 1 µmol of oxidized product per minute under standard conditions, calculated using the extinction coefficient of oxidized TMPD34.

The Ag–Cu NZs exhibited a specific peroxidase-like activity of 0.004 U/mg and an oxidase-like activity of 0.0008 U/mg. The peroxidase reaction reached a plateau within 5 min, indicating rapid kinetics, whereas the oxidase reaction proceeded more gradually over 20 min. This suggests that while both pathways operate concurrently, the peroxidase pathway dominates due to the higher reactivity of H2O2 compared to molecular oxygen.

Kinetic parameters were determined using the Michaelis–Menten model and Lineweaver–Burk plots (Fig. 6 and Table 1). In the peroxidase-like reaction, the apparent Michaelis constant (Km) for H2O2 (72.44 mM) was significantly higher than that for TMPD (0.89 mM). Since Km is inversely proportional to substrate affinity, indicates a much higher affinity of Ag–Cu NZs for TMPD than for H2O2. This high affinity facilitates rapid oxidation of the chromogenic substrate at catalytic sites.

In contrast, the oxidase-like activity exhibited lower Km for TMPD (0.38 mM), suggesting tight binding of TMPD even in the absence of H2O2. The maximum reaction rate (Vmax) for oxidase activity (0.042 µM/min/mg) was much higher than that for peroxidase activity (0.01 µM/min/mg). However, direct comparison of Vmax values across different oxidants (molecular O2 vs. H2O2) is not straightforward, as the reaction mechanisms and rate‑limiting steps differ. Thus, while TMPD binding appears strong in both pathways, the catalytic turnover rates are not directly comparable under these assay conditions.

Moreover, previous studies have reported that Ag and Cu NPs exhibit both H2O2-dependent peroxidase-like and oxidase-like activities35,36. Notably, Ag-based bimetallic systems, including Ag–Fe and Ag–Cu composites, display higher reaction rates, lower apparent Km values, and improved catalytic stability compared to monometallic NZs, potentially due to electronic interactions between the constituent metals37.

The Lineweaver–Burk plots (Fig. 7) intersected at a single point to the left of the y-axis, indicating a random sequential mechanism. This implies that both substrates (TMPD and H2O2) can adsorb onto the heterogeneous alloy surface in any order before electron transfer occurs, facilitated by multiple active sites.

The high catalytic efficiency is attributed to the synergistic interaction between the two metals. It is proposed that Cu atoms act as primary active sites for H2O2 activation, undergoing redox cycling (Cu0/Cu+/Cu2+) to generate hydroxyl radicals (·OH) via a Fenton-like reaction. Meanwhile, Ag atoms serve as efficient electron-transfer mediators, facilitating the rapid oxidation of TMPD. The homogeneous alloy structure ensures intimate contact between Ag and Cu, lowering the activation energy for electron transfer and enhancing catalytic turnover18,19,37.

Antimicrobial, antibiofilm, and antifungal activities

The antimicrobial efficacy of the synthesized Ag–Cu NZs was evaluated against MDR and standard bacterial strains. The MIC values for Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa ) PAO1 and Escherichia coli (E. coli) were determined to be 9.8 µg/mL, whereas Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis) exhibited higher MICs of 29.3 µg/mL and 88 µg/mL, respectively (Fig. 8a; Table 2). Notably, the MDR strain P. aeruginosa TAG48 required a significantly higher MIC of 266 µg/mL.

To elucidate the synergistic potential of the Ag–Cu NZs, its antimicrobial performance was benchmarked against monometallic Ag and Cu NPs reported in recent literature, utilizing comparable green synthesis protocols and bacterial strains (Table 2). The Ag–Cu NZs demonstrated superior efficacy compared to their monometallic counterparts. For instance, while monometallic Cu NPs and Ag NPs exhibited MIC values of 133 µg/mL20 and ≥ 333 µg/mL8, respectively, against P. aeruginosa PAO1, the Ag–Cu NZs achieved an MIC of 9.8 µg/mL. This represents a 10- to 30-fold enhancement in potency. A similar trend was observed for S. aureus, where monometallic Cu NPs showed an MIC of 133 µg/mL20, whereas the Ag–Cu NZs required only 29.3 µg/mL. This enhanced efficacy is attributed to the homogeneous alloy structure, which ensures intimate atomic contact between Ag and Cu, facilitating a dual-attack mechanism that is more effective than the single-pathway action of monometallic NPs.

The antibiofilm activity of the Ag–Cu NZs further underscores this synergistic effect. As detailed in Table 3, the MBIC and MBEC values of Ag–Cu NZs were significantly lower than those of monometallic NPs. For example, Ag–Cu NZs eradicated P. aeruginosa 137 biofilms at an MBEC of 12.3 µg/mL, whereas monometallic Cu NPs achieve eradication at 333 µg/mL. This confirms that the bimetallic alloy structure significantly enhances Exopolysaccharide (EPS) penetration.

The antibacterial and anti-biofilm activities of the synthesized Ag–Cu NZs were assessed against S. aureus ATCC 25,923 and P. aeruginosa PAO1. The MIC, MBC, MBIC, and MBEC values were determined and compared with standard antibiotics, as detailed in Table 4. Against S. aureus ATCC 25,923, the Ag–Cu NZs exhibited an MIC of 29.3 μg/mL and an MBC of 88 μg/mL. While ciprofloxacin demonstrated lower MIC (0.5 μg/mL) and MBC (4 μg/mL) values, indicating higher intrinsic bactericidal potency, the Ag–Cu NZs still showed significant activity. Notably, the MBIC and MBEC values for Ag–Cu NZs were both 111 μg/mL, suggesting a moderate ability to inhibit and eradicate pre-formed biofilms compared to ciprofloxacin (MBIC: 32 μg/mL; MBEC: 512 μg/mL). Interestingly, while ciprofloxacin was highly effective at inhibiting biofilm formation (low MBIC), it required a much higher concentration to eradicate existing biofilms (high MBEC), whereas Ag–Cu NZs showed a balanced profile with equal MBIC and MBEC values.

Against P. aeruginosa PAO1, a Gram-negative pathogen known for its intrinsic resistance, the Ag–Cu NZs demonstrated an MIC of 9.8 μg/mL and an MBC of 29.3 μg/mL. When compared to standard antibiotics, the Ag–Cu NZs were significantly more potent than cefixime (MIC: 128 μg/mL; MBC: 256 μg/mL). Overall, while the Ag–Cu NZs did not consistently outperform the most potent antibiotics like ciprofloxacin in terms of MIC values, they demonstrated broad-spectrum activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains. Furthermore, their ability to act as both biofilm inhibitors and eradicators (with equal MBIC/MBEC values) highlights a unique mechanistic profile that may complement existing antibiotic therapies, particularly against resistant strains such as those treated with cefixime.

The observed antimicrobial activity is multifactorial, involving physical membrane disruption, oxidative stress, and potential metal ion toxicity. While literature widely proposes that NZs generate ROS leading to bacterial death6,18, this study did not directly quantify ROS or ion leaching. Therefore, the observed efficacy is presented as a consequence of the combined physicochemical properties of the alloyed nanostructures, including their small size, high surface-to-volume ratio, and catalytic activity. Future studies will employ ROS scavenger assays and Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) to decouple the contributions of ROS generation versus metal ion release.

Additionally, the antifungal properties of the biogenic Ag–Cu NZs were assessed against pathogenic Candida strains. The NZs exhibited MIC values of 12.3 µg/mL for Candida albicans (C. albicans) 30 and 29, and 1.3 µg/mL for C. albicans 11 (Table 5). These values are comparable to or lower than those reported for other nanomaterials, such as VO-Cu2+44, and Ag NPs8, further supporting the broad-spectrum antimicrobial potential of the synthesized Ag–Cu NZs.

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activity of the synthesized Ag–Cu NZs, evaluated using the DPPH radical scavenging assay demonstrated an IC50 value of 158.2 ± 13.9 µg/mL (Fig. 9). While the extensive washing process removed loosely bound phytochemicals, the retained antioxidant capacity likely stems from phenolic and flavonoid compounds firmly anchored to the NP surface. Furthermore, the bimetallic core may contribute through intrinsic redox activity, potentially mimicking the function of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase8,48. However, it is important to acknowledge that this study relied solely on the DPPH assay. Since DPPH measures hydrogen atom transfer or electron donation capacity in organic solvents, it may not fully represent the antioxidant behavior in biological aqueous environments or other radical species. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, and future studies should employ a broader panel of assays (e.g., FRAP, ABTS, ORAC) to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the antioxidant profile.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully validated the hypothesis that synergistic interactions between Ag and Cu in an alloy-like structure can significantly enhance the catalytic and biological functionalities of NZs compared to their monometallic counterparts. We developed a novel, rapid, and cost-effective green biosynthesis strategy utilizing J. regia green husk extract—an abundant agricultural byproduct—as a sole reducing and capping agent. This approach not only valorizes AW but also eliminates the need for toxic chemical reagents, aligning with sustainable nanotechnology principles.

Our key findings demonstrate the successful fabrication of spherical, crystalline Ag–Cu NZs with a uniform core size of ~ 17 nm. The synthesized NZs exhibited robust dual enzyme-mimicking activities (peroxidase and oxidase-like) with favorable kinetic parameters, notably a high affinity for the substrate TMPD. Furthermore, the Ag–Cu NZs displayed exceptional optical stability over one month and potent broad-spectrum antimicrobial efficacy against MDR bacterial strains and mature biofilms, with MBEC comparable to standard antibiotics. These improvements underscore the advantage of the bimetallic alloy- like structure in facilitating electron transfer and increasing surface reactivity.

Despite these promising results, several limitations and future directions warrant consideration. First, the antioxidant capacity was assessed solely using the DPPH assay. As DPPH primarily reflects electron-donating capacity in organic media, it may not fully capture the antioxidant behavior in complex biological systems. Therefore, future studies should employ a broader panel of assays (e.g., Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP), 2,2′-Azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid (ABTS), and Oxygen Radical Absorbance Capacity (ORAC)) to provide a more comprehensive evaluation. Second, the synthesized NZs exhibited a PDI of 0.55, indicating a moderate-to-broad size distribution. This polydispersity is a known limitation of green synthesis methods using complex plant extracts, which can lead to variations in nucleation and growth kinetics. Future optimization of reaction conditions or the use of purified biomolecules may help narrow this distribution. Third, while XRD and STEM-EDX data strongly suggest an alloy-like structure, definitive confirmation of atomic-level mixing and oxidation states (e.g., via X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy [XPS]) was not performed in this study. Therefore, the term ‘alloy’ is used to describe the homogeneous distribution of elements, with the understanding that advanced spectroscopic validation remains a priority for future work. Fourth, direct quantification of intracellular ROS levels and metal ion leaching kinetics was beyond the scope of the current study; future work will employ ROS scavenger assays and ICP-MS to decouple the contributions of oxidative stress from ion toxicity. Finally, to assess clinical translational potential, comprehensive biocompatibility and cytotoxicity studies on normal mammalian cell lines are essential to establish a therapeutic window. Moving beyond in vitro assays, future studies should evaluate the efficacy and stability of these NZs in complex biological environments and explore their practical application in in vivo models for wound healing or environmental remediation.

Overall, this work establishes a sustainable and versatile platform for transforming AW into high-performance nanozybiotics. The combination of green synthesis, enhanced catalytic synergy, and multifunctional biological activity positions these Ag–Cu NZs as promising candidates for next-generation biomedical and environmental applications.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Green husks of Juglans regia were collected as agricultural waste from Shahriar Gardens (35.659527° N, 51.059527° E). Silver nitrate (AgNO3) and copper (II) acetate (Cu(CH3COO)2; ≥ 99%, Merck, Germany) served as precursors for Ag–Cu NZ synthesis. Triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC; ≥ 98%, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH; ≥ 95%, Merck, Germany) were employed for antibiofilm and antioxidant assays, respectively. Additional reagents included ascorbic acid (≥ 99.6%, Sigma-Aldrich), N, N, N′, N′-tetramethyl-p-phenylenediamine (TMPD; ≥ 98%, Sigma-Aldrich), sodium acetate (CH3COONa; ≥ 99%, Sigma-Aldrich), and glacial acetic acid (100%, Drm Chem, Iran). Culture media, including Potato Dextrose Broth (PDB), Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB), Mueller–Hinton Agar (MHA), and Mueller–Hinton Broth (MHB), were sourced from Ibresco (UK) and Sigma-Aldrich (Germany), while Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium was obtained from Gibco (USA). All materials were handled in strict accordance with laboratory biosafety protocols to ensure reproducibility.

Bacterial strains and culture media

An MDR strain of Pseudomonas aeruginosa (TAG48) was utilized in this study. Additionally, Pseudomonas aeruginosa 137 and Staphylococcus aureus 141, isolated from cystic fibrosis (CF) patients, were included. Candida albicans strains used for antifungal assays were isolated from ocular infections. These clinical isolates were kindly provided by Dr. Ahya Abdi Ali and previously identified via 16S rRNA sequencing8,20,49. Standard reference strains, including Bacillus subtilis (ATCC 6633), Escherichia coli (ATCC 25,922), Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 25,923), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 (ATCC 15,692), were acquired from the Iranian Biological Resource Center (IBRC).

Methods

Extract preparation

Green husks of Juglans regia were washed thoroughly with soap and tap water, soaked in distilled water, and shade-dried at ambient temperature (20–25 °C) to prevent photodegradation. The dried biomass was milled into a fine powder (0.2–0.5 mm particle size) using a coffee grinder (N-96, Naniwa, Iran). This particle size was selected to optimize extraction kinetics while ensuring practical handling. A concentrated extract was prepared by adding 0.4 g of dried powder to 14.5 mL of boiling distilled water, a solid-to-liquid ratio established in our previous optimization studies24 as optimal for bioactive phytochemical yield. Maintaining this ratio ensured consistency with prior work on Ag NZs. The mixture was heated in a boiling water bath for 10 min, sealed with aluminum foil and a cotton ball, and then filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The filtrate was used immediately for Ag–Cu NZs biosynthesis.

Synthesis and characterization of Ag–Cu NZs

To synthesize Ag–Cu NZs, 150 µL of the plant extract was added to a 10 mL mixture containing 1 mM AgNO3 and 1 mM Cu(CH3COO)2. The reaction was incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 24 h. Successful synthesis was confirmed by a distinct color change compared to controls (extract + water, or metal salts alone)28.

The product was purified via centrifugation (≈9.86 × 103 g for 30 min at 4 °C) (5810R, Eppendorf, Germany) washed, and re-centrifuged (≈1.2 × 104 g for 30 min) (MiniSpin, Eppendorf, Germany). The resulting pellet was dried at 70 °C, re-dispersed in distilled water, and sonicated for 2 h (15 min on/5 min off cycles) (Elmasonic S60, Germany) to ensure colloidal stability. The final concentration was adjusted to 1 mg/mL, which remained stable for at least 24 h.

Following sonication, the optical properties and Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) of the Ag–Cu NZs were recorded at a concentration of 200 µg/mL and in the 200–800 nm range by UV–Visible (UV-1800 Shimadzu) spectrophotometer. The blank sample was distilled water. The spectrum was analyzed by UV-Probe software (https://www.ssi.shimadzu.com/products/uv-vis/uv-vis-nir-spectroscopy-software/uvprobe/index.html). Hydrodynamic diameter, the polydispersity index (PDI), and particle size distribution were determined by Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) (VASCO2, Cordouan Tech, France). The sample preparation for DLS analysis was the same as UV-spectroscopy. Prior to any electron microscopy analysis, one drop of homogenized and colloidal sample of Ag–Cu NZs was placed on a piece of aluminum foil and it was dried under fume hood. The Elemental composition was assessed by Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDX) (AP1.3, Moxtek, Orem, Utah, USA). Morphology and size of the particles were observed using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) at 3–10 kV (MAIA3, TESCAN, Kohoutovice, Czech Republic). Atomic lattice structures and defects were examined via High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HRTEM) at 200 kV (FEI TECNAI F20) with Digital Micrograph 3.6.1 software (https://www.gatan.com/products/tem-analysis/digitalmicrograph-software) for automated electron tomography. For the HRTEM measurements, a droplet of the NZ solution was placed onto a 3-mm carbon-coated copper grid. An absorbent paper was used to eliminate any excess solution, and the sample was allowed to dry under vacuum at room temperature (25 ºC). The electron microscopy micrographs were processed and analyzed thoroughly by Digimizer 6.5.1 (https://www.digimizer.com/) and ImageJ 1.51 (https://imagej.net/ij/) for plotting size distribution histograms of Ag–Cu NZ. The chemical composition of the synthesized NZs was analyzed using an EDX detector attached to the Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) operating in Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy (STEM) mode. Crystalline phases were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis performed at 40 kV and 40 mA with a step size of 0.02° and detection angle from 10º to 80º (Rigaku, Tokyo, Japan). The results of XRD were analyzed by Xpert High Score Plus 3.0 software (https://www.malvernpanalytical.com/). For Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis, the pellet of Ag–Cu NZs was prepared, and it was dried in the heat block for 30 min. One mg of dried Ag–Cu NZs was mixed with dried KBr (100–200 mg). Surface capping molecules and associated phytochemicals were characterized in the range 400–4000 cm−1 (TENSOR 27, Bruker, Massachusetts, USA) and the spectrum was analyzed by OPUS software (https://www.bruker.com/en/products-and-solutions/infrared-and-raman/opus-spectroscopy-software.html).

Enzymatic activity assays

The catalytic activity of Ag–Cu NZs was evaluated using TMPD as a chromogenic substrate. All assays were performed in 0.2 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.0), which was selected consistent with the acidic optimum typical of peroxidase-mimicking NZs. For peroxidase-like activity, Ag–Cu NZs (1 mg/mL) prepared in sodium acetate buffer (0.2 M, pH 4.0) were incubated with TMPD (0.8 mM) and different concentrations of H2O2 (10–100 mM). Also, for the second substrate, the NZ enzymatic reaction was performed using varying TMPD concentrations (0.1–1.1 mM) and a constant H2O2 concentration (0.1 mM). The reaction was monitored at 612 nm (the maximum absorbance of oxidized TMPD) by recording absorbance changes every 30 s for 20 min using an Enzyme Linked Immuno Sorbent Assay (ELISA) microplate reader. Absorbance was measured using the BioTek Epoch Microplate Spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, USA).

(C1) TMPD + H2O2, (C2) NZs + H2O2, and (C3) NZs + TMPD.

Antimicrobial and antibiofilm assays

Following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines50, the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) was determined using the micro-broth dilution technique in polystyrene 96-well microtiter plates. The antibacterial activity of Ag–Cu NZs was evaluated against four standard bacterial strains, including Bacillus subtilis (ATCC 6633), Escherichia coli (ATCC 25,922), Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 25,923), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 (ATCC 15,692), as well as a MDR strain, Pseudomonas aeruginosa TAG48, isolated from sputum24. Initially, 100 µL of MHB was dispensed into each well. Subsequently, 100 µL of Ag–Cu NZs at a concentration of 800 µg/mL was added to the first column of each row, followed by serial dilution through the ninth well by transferring 100 µL sequentially. The bacterial inoculum, pre-cultured to 108 Colony Forming Unit / mL (CFU/mL), was diluted to achieve a final concentration of 106 CFU/mL, and 100 µL of this inoculum was added to the wells. The last three columns were designated as controls: 100 µL bacteria + 100 µL MHB for bacterial growth control, 100 µL MHB + 100 µL Ag–Cu NZs for sample control, and 200 µL MHB as the culture control. In contrast to the previously reported protocol24, the extract of green husk from Juglans regia was omitted from the samples; thus, extract-related control groups were unnecessary. The microtiter plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight, and wells without visible bacterial growth were recorded as MIC values51. All assays were performed in triplicate.

The wells containing concentrations higher than the determined MIC were sub-cultured to evaluate bacterial viability. The Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC) was defined as the lowest concentration capable of producing a 3-log reduction (99.9% killing) in the initial bacterial inoculum. All experiments were performed in triplicate52.

For Antibiofilm assays, Pseudomonas aeruginosa 137 and Staphylococcus aureus 141 isolated from the sputum of patients with CF were selected to evaluate the antibiofilm activity of Ag–Cu NZs and to determine the corresponding Minimum Biofilm Inhibitory Concentration (MBIC) and Minimum Biofilm Eradication Concentration (MBEC). These strains were chosen due to their strong biofilm-forming capacity. According to CLSI guidelines50, each well of the microtiter plates was filled with 100 µL of TSB supplemented with 0.2% glucose. Serial dilutions of Ag–Cu NZs (50 ng/mL to 333 µg/mL) were prepared and added to the wells. The 10th, 11th, and 12th columns were designated as controls for bacterial growth, Ag–Cu NZs’ sterility, and medium sterility, respectively. Subsequently, 100 µL of overnight bacterial cultures adjusted to a standard concentration of 108 CFU/mL were added up to the 10th well. The microtiter plates were incubated at 37 °C for 20 h. After incubation, planktonic cells were carefully removed, and the wells were washed with 0.9% normal saline. Then, 170 µL of TSB containing 0.2% glucose along with 30 µL of freshly prepared 1% TTC solution were added to each well. The microtiter plates were further incubated for 5 h at 37 °C under dark conditions with shaking at 150 RPM in a shaker incubator. Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 27,853 were used as standard biofilm-producing reference strains.

For MBEC determination, a similar procedure was followed. Biofilm-producing bacteria were first cultured for 24 h at 37 °C. Each well of the microtiter plates was filled with 100 µL of TSB supplemented with 0.2% glucose, followed by the addition of 100 µL of bacterial suspension (108 CFU/mL). The microtiter plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C to allow biofilm formation at the bottom of the wells. After incubation, planktonic cells were removed, and the wells were gently washed and air-dried under sterile conditions. Serial dilutions of Ag–Cu NZs (50 ng/mL to 333 µg/mL) were then added to the wells. The final three columns served as controls (bacterial growth control, Ag–Cu NZ sterility control, and medium control). The microtiter plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The staining procedure was subsequently performed following the same protocol described for the MBIC assay49,53.

Antifungal assays

The MIC against Candida albicans was determined according to CLSI guidelines50. For antifungal evaluation, three pathogenic strains of Candida albicans isolated from patients’ infected eyes were used. The fungal isolates were cultured overnight in PDB at 37 °C with shaking at 150 RPM in a shaker incubator (WIS-20, Germany). Standardized fungal suspensions (0.5 McFarland ≈ 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL) were prepared in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium. Initially, each well of the microtiter plates was filled with 100 µL of RPMI medium. Serial dilutions of the biosynthesized Ag–Cu NZs (50 ng/mL to 333 µg/mL) were then prepared. Subsequently, 100 µL of each fungal suspension (106 CFU/mL) was added to the wells. The microtiter plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h54.

Antioxidant assays

The free radical scavenging activity of Ag–Cu NZs was evaluated using the DPPH assay. Ag–Cu NZ solutions with concentrations ranging from 100 to 800 µg/mL were prepared. Subsequently, 100 µL of each concentration was mixed with 0.1 mM DPPH solution (freshly prepared in methanol to obtain a final volume of 1 mL). Ascorbic acid, at the same concentration range (100–800 µg/mL), was used as the standard reference. The reaction mixtures were kept in the dark for 100 min at 25 °C. Statistical analysis

The IC50 value for the Ag–Cu NZs was estimated from the dose–response curve of radical scavenging activity versus concentration.

All experiments, including antimicrobial and antioxidant assays, were performed in triplicate. Antioxidant activity results for Ag–Cu NPs are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The IC50 value was determined from the concentration–response curve using linear interpolation of inhibition percentages. Descriptive statistics were calculated using Microsoft Excel 2016. MIC and MBC were determined based on macroscopic observation of growth/turbidity relative to untreated controls. MBIC and MBEC were assessed using the TTC colorimetric method; biofilm inhibition was defined by a reduction in red formazan intensity compared to the control, while complete eradication was indicated by the absence of color change. Particle size distribution was analyzed from SEM micrographs by measuring 100 randomly selected NPs using Digimizer software. The average particle size was reported (mean ± SD, n = 100). EDX point analyses were conducted at three distinct locations to verify elemental composition reproducibility. No inferential statistical tests were performed for qualitative or semi-quantitative measurements.
