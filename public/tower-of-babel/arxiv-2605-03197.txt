Completely-positive non-signalling non-Markovian dynamics
Serhii Kryhin, Vivishek Sudhir

Abstract
We define non-Markovian quantum dynamics as evolution in which the current state depends on all past states, and completely characterize its structure under the assumptions of complete positivity and non-signalling. The resulting continuous-time dynamics is an integro-differential equation that augments the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad equation with a memory integral, and is capable of describing the quantum state of systems exposed to noise with any integrable power spectral density with no further approximations. We then establish a formalism to evaluate multi-time correlations of measurement outcomes in this general setting, obviating the need for a regression theorem. As an application, we derive the emission spectrum of a driven two-level system coupled to a non-Markovian bath: the familiar Mollow triplet acquires a frequency-dependent linewidth that encodes the memory of the bath. Our work provides a rigorous yet transparent description of the quantum state of non-Markovian systems, opening the door for state estimation and state-based quantum control beyond the Markovian regime.

A class of non-Markovian processes.

In classical probability theory, a non-Markovian process is, informally, a random process whose
current value depends on all of its past values. A classical dynamical system is said to be
non-Markovian if its state is a non-Markovian process, i.e. if its current state depends on all of its
past states. It is this notion of non-Markovianity that we wish to extend to the quantum setting.

To wit, we consider a quantum system whose density matrix
\hat{\varrho}_{n}

\hat{\varrho}_{n+1}=\mathcal{K}_{n+1}[\hat{\varrho}_{n},..,\hat{\varrho}_{0}].

(1)

is distinct from approaches where non-Markovian dynamics is defined in terms of the history
of control operations

Our concern is to characterize the structure of the maps

,

Axioms for the maps
\mathcal{K}_{n}
.

We are interested in the maps
\{\mathcal{K}_{n}\}
in
Eq.

1
, each a multi-variable map
acting on
n
states, that satisfy some natural axioms.

A natural demand is that the maps
\mathcal{K}_{n}
are compatible with the system being part of a larger Hilbert space, which is axiomatized by the notion of complete positivity.
In the familiar case of a single-variable map
\mathcal{K}_{1}
, it is completely positive (CP)
if
\mathcal{K}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{m}

is positive for all
m
, where
\mathcal{I}_{m}
is the identity map on an
m
-dimensional Hilbert space.
The extension to multi-variable maps is similar
Ando and Choi 1986
:
a map of
n+1
variables
\mathcal{K}_{n+1}[\hat{\varrho}_{n},\ldots,\hat{\varrho}_{0}]
is
CP iff., for any set of
n+1
positive block-matrices
\hat{\varrho}_{m}
(with
(i,j)
’th block

\hat{\varrho}_{m,ij}
,
0\leq m\leq n
), the block-matrix with
(i,j)
’th block
\mathcal{K}_{n+1}[\hat{\varrho}_{n,ij},\ldots,\hat{\varrho}_{0,ij}]
is also positive.
It captures the essential requirement that the maps
\mathcal{K}_{n}
produce positive states even when
each of their arguments is possibly entangled with a spectator ancilla.

Ando and Choi
Ando and Choi 1986
have shown that any multi-variable CP map
can be represented as a sum of multi-variable CP maps that are homogeneous in each of their arguments.
(or anti-homogeneous, which we ignore in the context of maps between quantum states).
That is, if
\mathcal{K}
is a multi-variable CP map, then there exists a decomposition

\mathcal{K}[\hat{\varrho}_{n},\ldots,\hat{\varrho}_{0}]=\sum_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}}\mathcal{K}_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}}[\hat{\varrho}_{n},\ldots,\hat{\varrho}_{0}],

(3)

where
m_{i}
are positive integers and
\mathcal{K}_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}}
is homogeneous
of degree
m_{i}
in the
i
’th argument, i.e.

\begin{split}&\mathcal{K}_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}}[\hat{\varrho}_{n},\ldots,z\hat{\varrho}_{i},\ldots,\hat{\varrho}_{0}]\\
&\qquad\qquad=z^{m_{i}}\mathcal{K}_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}}[\hat{\varrho}_{n},\ldots,\hat{\varrho}_{i},\ldots,\hat{\varrho}_{0}].\end{split}

(4)

for any
z\in\mathds{C}
.
This is the structural implication of complete positivity.

In addition to complete-positivity, we demand that the maps
\mathcal{K}_{n}
are non-signaling (NS).
The single-variable map
\mathcal{K}_{1}
is NS
Ghirardi et al. 1988
;
Gisin 1989
;
Simon et al. 2001
iff.
it is convex-linear, i.e.

\mathcal{K}_{1}[\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\hat{\varrho}_{\alpha}]=\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathcal{K}_{1}[\hat{\varrho}_{\alpha}]
, for any states
\hat{\varrho}_{\alpha}
and any probabilities

\{p_{\alpha}\}
.
Convex-linearity ensures that statistically equivalent ensembles cannot
be distinguished by the map
\mathcal{K}_{1}
, so no subsequent local measurement
can reveal which ensemble was remotely prepared; this is precisely non-signaling.
We can
extend the notion of non-signaling to multi-variable maps by demanding that they are convex-linear
in each of their arguments, i.e.

\begin{split}\mathcal{K}_{n+1}&\left[\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\hat{\varrho}_{n,\alpha},\cdots,\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\hat{\varrho}_{0,\alpha}\right]\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad=\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\mathcal{K}_{n+1}[\hat{\varrho}_{n,\alpha},\ldots,\hat{\varrho}_{0,\alpha}],\end{split}

(5)

for any states
\hat{\varrho}_{m,\alpha}
and any probabilities
\{p_{\alpha}\}
.

Convex-linearity can be conveniently reformulated in terms of sub-normalized states.
Note that the single-variable map
\mathcal{K}_{1}
is defined on the set of normalized states.
The normalized states form the base of a cone of sub-normalized
states
\{p\hat{\varrho}:p\in[0,1],\Tr\hat{\varrho}=1\}
.
Any convex-linear map on the base of a convex cone has a unique extension
to the whole cone which is homogeneous of degree one (see End Matter).
That is,
\mathcal{K}_{1}
has a unique extension to sub-normalized states:

\mathcal{K}_{1}[p\hat{\varrho}]=p\mathcal{K}_{1}[\hat{\varrho}]
for any
p\in[0,1]
.
It can be shown that convex-linear multi-variable maps have a similar extension, i.e.

\mathcal{K}_{n+1}[p\hat{\varrho}_{n},..,p\hat{\varrho}_{0}]=p\,\mathcal{K}_{n+1}[\hat{\varrho}_{n},..,\hat{\varrho}_{0}],

(6)

for any
p\in[0,1]
and any normalized states
\{\hat{\varrho}_{n}\}
(see End Matter).
This is an equivalent formulation of non-signaling and has the interpretation that
if the state
\hat{\varrho}_{0}
is initialized with probability
p
, represented by the sub-normalized
state
p\hat{\varrho}_{0}
, then the consequent evolution will happen with the same probability
p
.

Structure of CPNS maps.

We now identify the most general form of completely-positive non-signaling (CPNS) maps,
i.e. those that satisfy
Eqs.

3
,
4
and
6
.
Note that
Eq.

6
constrains the values of
m_{i}
that can appear in the expansion
in
Eq.

3
:
only the terms with
\sum_{i}m_{i}=1
satisfy
Eq.

6
for all
0\leq p\leq 1
, so

m_{i}=\delta_{ik}
for some
0\leq k\leq n
; i.e. any multi-variable CP
map
\mathcal{K}[\hat{\varrho}_{n},\ldots,\hat{\varrho}_{1}]
is a sum of one-argument maps
\mathcal{K}_{0,..,m_{k}=1,..,0}[\hat{\varrho}_{k}]
.
But since each of such CP maps is also linear, according to
Ando and Choi 1986
, it has a Kraus representation.
Thus, an arbitrary CPNS map
\mathcal{K}_{n+1}
can be expressed in the form

\mathcal{K}_{n+1}[\hat{\varrho}_{n},..,\hat{\varrho}_{0}]=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\sum_{\alpha}V^{\dagger}_{\alpha nm}\hat{\varrho}_{m}V_{\alpha nm},

(7)

where the operators
V_{\alpha nm}
satisfy

\sum_{\alpha}V_{\alpha nm}V^{\dagger}_{\alpha nm}=p_{nm}\hat{I}
with

p_{nm}\geq 0
and
\sum_{m=0}^{n}p_{nm}=1
,
to ensure that
\mathcal{K}_{n+1}
is trace-preserving.
This structure subsumes the Kraus representation of a single-variable (
n=0
)
CP map.

Continuous-time limit.

To elucidate the continuous-time dynamics corresponding to the discrete-time CPNS maps in

Eq.

7
, we suppose that the evolution is continuous in the sense that

\hat{\varrho}_{n+1}=\hat{\varrho}_{n}+O(\Delta t)
,
where
\Delta t=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
, and take the limit
\Delta t\to 0
.

Continuity of the evolution constrains the scaling of the operators
V_{\alpha nm}

with
\Delta t
: the required continuous behavior can only be produced if

\begin{split}V_{\alpha nm}=\begin{cases}1+\Delta t\,{L}_{0}(t_{n})+O(\Delta t^{2});&\alpha=0,m=n\\
\sqrt{\Delta t}\,L_{\alpha}(t_{n})+O(\Delta t);&\alpha\neq 0,m=n\\
\Delta t\,{R}_{\alpha}(t_{n},t_{m})+O(\Delta t^{2});&\alpha\neq 0,m<n,\end{cases}\end{split}

(8)

where
{L}_{\alpha}(t),{R}_{\alpha}(t,t^{\prime})
are operators independent of
\Delta t
,
and
\sum_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}(t,t^{\prime})R^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(t,t^{\prime})=p(t,t^{\prime})\hat{I}
where
p(t,t^{\prime})\geq 0

is the rate density for invoking the state
\hat{\varrho}(t^{\prime}<t)
in the evolution of
\hat{\varrho}(t)
.

The terms in
Eq.

8
represent qualitatively different contributions
to
\hat{\varrho}_{n+1}
.
The terms with
m=n
represent the action of Kraus operators on the
current state
\hat{\varrho}_{n}
, and are thus the usual Markovian contribution to the dynamics
exhibiting their well-known scaling with
\Delta t

Manzano 2020
.
The terms with
m<n
represent the action of Kraus operators on all past states

\{\hat{\varrho}_{m}\}_{m<n}
, and are thus the non-Markovian contribution.
The scaling with
\Delta t
here leads to the
Kraus sum scaling as
\sum_{m}^{n}(\Delta t)^{2}
, but since there are
n\sim 1/\Delta t
terms in the sum,
the total contribution of this non-Markovian part scales as

\Delta t
, ensuring a well-defined continuous-time limit.

Substituting the parametrization for
V_{\alpha nm}
into
Eq.

7
and taking the limit
\Delta t\to 0
leads to an integro-differential equation for
\hat{\varrho}(t)
:

\frac{\partial\hat{\varrho}}{\partial t}=L_{0}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{\varrho}(t)+\hat{\varrho}(t)L_{0}(t)+\sum_{\alpha\neq 0}L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{\varrho}(t)L_{\alpha}(t)\\
+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\differential{t'}\sum_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(t,t^{\prime})\hat{\varrho}(t^{\prime})R_{\alpha}(t,t^{\prime}).

(9)

Splitting
L_{0}(t)=G(t)+iH(t)
into Hermitian
G(t)
and
H(t)
and imposing
\Tr[\partial_{t}\hat{\varrho}(t)]=0
fixes
G(t)
.
After the explicit separation of the Markovian and non-Markovian contributions, the non-Markovian master equation attains its final form:

\frac{\partial\hat{\varrho}}{\partial t}=(\mathcal{L}\hat{\varrho})(t)+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\differential{t^\prime}(\mathcal{R}\hat{\varrho})(t,t^{\prime}).

(10)

The generator for Markovian part of evolution has a form
(\mathcal{L}\hat{\varrho})(t)=-i[H(t),\hat{\varrho}(t)]+\sum_{\alpha}\!\left(L^{\dagger}_{\alpha}\hat{\varrho}L_{\alpha}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{L_{\alpha}L_{\alpha}^{\dagger};\hat{\varrho}\}\right)
, which is the familiar GKSL generator.
The operators
L_{\alpha}=L_{\alpha}(t)
may depend explicitly on the current time.
The non-Markovian part of the evolution is governed by

(\mathcal{R}\hat{\varrho})(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{\alpha}\left(R_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\varrho}(t^{\prime})R_{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\{R_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}^{\dagger};\hat{\varrho}(t)\}\right),

(11)

where
R_{\alpha}=R_{\alpha}(t,t^{\prime})
are allowed to depend on both current and retarded times.
The structure of
Eq.

11
looks like that of a GKSL generator, but the jump term
acts on the retarded density matrix
\hat{\varrho}(t^{\prime})
and the anti-commutator
on the current state
\hat{\varrho}(t)
;
this asymmetry is not incidental, but is a direct consequence of complete positivity
and non-signalling. (See Sec. I of Supplemental Information
Sup
for details.)
This structure is subtly different from other proposals for non-Markovian
dynamics
Nakajima 1958
;
Zwanzig 1960
;
Shabani and Lidar 2005
;
Vacchini 2016
, but it is this difference that guarantees
that
Eq.

10
represents a CPNS evolution.

The construction above is valid whenever
R_{\alpha}(t,t^{\prime})
are bounded and
L_{\alpha}(t)

are closed
Lindblad 1976
;
Davies 1979
;
Dunford and Schwartz 2009
.
The converse is true in the sense that any evolution of the form in
Eq.

10

can be expressed as
Eq.

7
, whenever
L_{\alpha}(t)
and
R_{\alpha}(t,t^{\prime})
are
bounded and
p(t,t^{\prime})
is integrable on the interval
[0,t]
.
However, for unbounded
L_{\alpha}(t)
, there is no general result, similar in spirit
to
Davies 1979
, that would establish the converse.

Continuous-time dynamics of observables.

The non-Markovian master equation

Equation

10
defines a linear map
\hat{\varrho}(t_{0})\mapsto\hat{\varrho}(t)=\mathcal{K}_{t,t_{0}}\hat{\varrho}(t_{0})
on the set of states, where
\mathcal{K}_{t,t_{0}}
is the
continuous-time version of the map
\mathcal{K}_{n}
in
Eq.

1
.
The non-Markovian nature of the evolution implies that
\mathcal{K}_{t,t_{0}}
does not satisfy the composition law, i.e.

\mathcal{K}_{t_{2},t_{0}}\neq\mathcal{K}_{t_{2},t_{1}}\circ\mathcal{K}_{t_{1},t_{0}}

for
t_{2}>t_{1}>t_{0}
in general.
This means that, although the adjoint map
\mathcal{K}_{t,t_{0}}^{*}
formally evolves observables by
\hat{X}(t_{0})\mapsto\hat{X}(t)=\mathcal{K}_{t,t_{0}}^{*}\hat{X}(t_{0})
,
the equations of motion for observables do not close, and there is no regression theorem for correlation functions of observables.
Nevertheless, correlation functions can be defined
and evaluated by specifying measurement interventions and propagating the
measurement-conditioned state in the intervening duration using
Eq.

10
.

Instantaneous measurements.

We first consider the case of an instantaneous measurement defined by
the state transformation
\hat{\varrho}(t)\mapsto(\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}\hat{\varrho})(t)=J_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\varrho}(t)J_{\alpha}
,
where
J_{\alpha}
is an effect operator corresponding to the measurement outcome
\alpha
.
This measurements defines a classical random variable
I_{\alpha}(t)\in\{1,0\}
,
depending on whether
\alpha
is observed or not.
Its classical expectation is given by
\mathds{E}[I_{\alpha}(t)]=\Tr\!\left[(\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}\hat{\varrho})(t)\right]
.
Its correlation function
\mathds{E}[I_{\alpha}(t+\tau)I_{\beta}(t)]
corresponds to the action of
the corresponding operator at each of the two times.
Since
I_{\alpha}(t)
and
I_{\beta}(t)
are valued in
\{1,0\}
,
the probability
P[I_{\beta}(t)=1]=\mathds{E}[I_{\beta}(t)]
.
Then

\mathds{E}[I_{\alpha}(t+\tau)I_{\beta}(t)]=\mathds{E}[I_{\alpha}(t+\tau)\mid I_{\beta}(t)=1]P[I_{\beta}(t)=1],

where the conditional expectation value is given by

\mathds{E}[I_{\alpha}(t+\tau)\mid I_{\beta}(t)=1]=\Tr[\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}\hat{\varrho}(t+\tau\mid I_{\beta}(t)=1)],

(12)

with
\hat{\varrho}(t+\tau|I_{\beta}(t)=1)
the state of the system at the time
t+\tau

conditioned on
I_{\beta}(t)=1
at the time
t
.
The event

implies that

is applied at time

to

\hat{\varrho}(t)
; thus the conditional state

\hat{\varrho}(t^{\prime}|I_{\beta}(t)=1)=\begin{cases}(\mathcal{J}_{\beta}\hat{\varrho})(t)/P[I_{\beta}(t)=1],&t^{\prime}=t,\\
\hat{\varrho}(t^{\prime}),&t^{\prime}<t.\end{cases}

(13)

By causality, measurements performed in the future cannot retroactively affect the current state
of the system, so the state at

remains unchanged.
The conditional state

is obtained by evolving the system in the state

to

\partial_{\tau}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(t+\tau)=(\mathcal{L}\hat{\varrho}_{t})(t+\tau)+\int_{t_{0}}^{t+\tau}\differential{t^\prime}(\mathcal{R}\hat{\varrho}_{t})(t+\tau,t^{\prime}),

(14)

where
\hat{\varrho}_{t}(t^{\prime})=\hat{\varrho}(t^{\prime}|I_{\beta}(t)=1)
and the initial conditions at time

are given by
Eq.

13
.
This state then allows to evaluate
Eq.

12
and
therefore
\mathds{E}[I_{\beta}(t+\tau)I_{\alpha}(t)]
.

A more general non-binary random variable can be thought of as a weighted sum of binary-valued
random variables

, which corresponds to a measurement defined by the operator
\mathcal{J}=\sum_{\alpha}\nu_{\alpha}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}
.
Clearly, its expectation value is
\mathds{E}[I(t)]=\Tr[(\mathcal{J}\hat{\varrho})(t)]
,
and the correlation function is given by

\mathds{E}[I(t+\tau)I(t)]=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\nu_{\alpha}\nu_{\beta}\mathds{E}[I_{\alpha}(t+\tau)I_{\beta}(t)].

(15)

The bilinearity of
Eq.

15

\nu_{\alpha}
and the linearity of

allows to define a conditional state
\hat{\varrho}_{J,t}(t^{\prime})

to compute
\mathds{E}[I(t+\tau)I(t)]

\mathds{E}[I(t+\tau)I(t)]=\Tr[(\mathcal{J}\hat{\varrho}_{J,t})(t+\tau)],\quad\tau>0.

(16)

The state
\hat{\varrho}_{J,t}(t+\tau)
is a result of evolution according to
Eq.

14

to
t+\tau
, where
\hat{\varrho}_{t}(t+\tau)=\hat{\varrho}_{J,t}(t+\tau)
and the initial conditions at time

\hat{\varrho}_{J,t}(t^{\prime})=\begin{cases}(\mathcal{J}\hat{\varrho})(t),&t^{\prime}=t,\\
\mathds{E}[I(t)]\hat{\varrho}(t^{\prime}),&t^{\prime}<t.\end{cases}

(17)

A particularly useful special case is a weak measurement of an observable
\hat{X}
.
It is obtained by specializing to

{(\mathcal{J}\hat{\varrho})(t)=(x+\hat{X})^{\dagger}\hat{\varrho}(t)(x+\hat{X})}

{\Tr[(\mathcal{J}\hat{\varrho})(t)]\approx x^{2}+2x\real\!\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{X}\hat{\varrho}(t)]}

We interpret this as defining a current of large mean value

Subtracting the mean and rescaling defines the diffusive current

{I_{X}(t)\equiv x^{-1}(I(t)-x^{2})}

The two-time correlation of this current is given, to leading order in

\mathds{E}[I_{X}(t+\tau)I_{X}(t)]=\Tr[(\mathcal{X}\hat{\varrho}_{X,t})(t+\tau)],\quad\tau>0,

where we define
(\mathcal{X}\hat{\varrho})(t)\equiv\hat{X}^{\dagger}\hat{\varrho}(t)+\hat{\varrho}(t)\hat{X}
.
The quantity
\hat{\varrho}_{X,t}(t+\tau)
is defined by evolving
\hat{\varrho}_{X,t}(t)
from time
t
to
t+\tau
according to
Eq.

14
, where
\hat{\varrho}_{t}(t+\tau)=\hat{\varrho}_{X,t}(t+\tau)
, and the initial conditions are given by

\hat{\varrho}_{X,t}(t^{\prime})=\begin{cases}(\mathcal{X}\hat{\varrho})(t),&t^{\prime}=t,\\
2\real\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{X}\hat{\varrho}(t)]\hat{\varrho}(t^{\prime}),&t^{\prime}<t.\end{cases}

(19)

Continuous-time measurement.

So far, the measurement intervention is assumed to be instantaneous.
However, the scope of
Eq.

16
and
Eq.

14
can be expanded to
time-continuous measurements using the method described in Ref.
Landi et al. 2024
.
A time-continuous measurement can be modeled by incorporating it into the continuous dynamics of the
system: the measurement at each time is described by
(\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{(c)}\hat{\varrho})(t)\differential{t}=L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\varrho}L_{\alpha}(t)\differential{t}
, where
L_{\alpha}
are some Lindblad operators
that appear in
Eq.

10
.
The back-action of the measurement is automatically included in the dynamics.
As before, a weighted current
I^{(c)}(t)=\sum_{\alpha}\nu_{\alpha}I_{\alpha}^{(c)}(t)
can be defined,
corresponding to the operation
\mathcal{J}=\sum_{\alpha}\nu_{\alpha}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}
.
It has the expectation value
\mathds{E}[I^{(c)}(t)]=\mathrm{Tr}[(\mathcal{J}^{(c)}\hat{\varrho})(t)]
.
Its correlation function can be expressed as

\mathds{E}[I^{(c)}(t+\tau)I^{(c)}(t)]=\Tr[(\mathcal{J}\hat{\varrho}_{J,t}^{(c)})(t+\tau)]+K\delta(t),

(20)

where
K=\sum_{\alpha}\mathrm{Tr}[\nu_{\alpha}^{2}L_{\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\varrho}L_{\alpha}]
.
The additional contribution
K\delta(t)
is the
O(dt^{-1})
contribution that arises
from
\tau=0
, where two measurements overlap.
The conditional state
\hat{\varrho}_{J,t}^{(c)}(t+\tau)
is the state evolved from
t
to
t+\tau
by
Eq.

14
with the same initial conditions at time
t
as in
Eq.

17
, but with
\mathcal{J}^{(c)}
and
I^{(c)}
being substituted for
\mathcal{J}
and
I
respectively.
Similarly, continuous-time weak measurement of the observable
\hat{X}
can be described by the corresponding current with boundary conditions identical to
Eqs.

18
and
19
.

Application to a two-level system.

We illustrate the above formalism on a driven two-level system (TLS) coupled to a bosonic bath.
We model it by the Hamiltonian
H(t)=\omega_{0}\sigma^{\dagger}\sigma+\Omega\sigma e^{i\nu t}+\mathrm{h.c.}
, where
\sigma
is the annihilation operator of
the TLS,
\omega_{0}
is the splitting between its two levels, and
\Omega e^{i\nu t}
is a time-dependent drive.
Its interaction with a bosonic bath is described by the interaction Hamiltonian

H_{I}=g(\sigma^{\dagger}E+\sigma E^{\dagger})
, where
E=\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{\omega}f_{E}(\omega)b(\omega)
with
b(\omega)
the annihilation operator of the bath mode
distributed according to
f_{E}
, and
g
is the coupling strength.
We assume that the bath is stationary, i.e.
\Tr_{\mathrm{B}}[b^{\dagger}(\omega)b(\omega^{\prime})\hat{\varrho}_{\mathrm{B}}]=n(\omega)\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})
, where
n(\omega)
is the average
occupation number of the bath.
A standard textbook exercise in the weak-coupling limit
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
of applying the Born approximation, but crucially, not the Markov approximation, leads directly
to
Eq.

10
where the non-Markovian contribution is given by

Eq.

11
with
{R_{1}(t,t^{\prime})=S_{E}^{1/2}(t-t^{\prime})\sigma^{\dagger}}
and
{R_{2}(t,t^{\prime})=S_{E}^{1/2}(t-t^{\prime})\sigma}
,
where
S_{E}(t)=2g^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,|f_{E}(\omega)|^{2}n(\omega)\cos((\omega-\omega_0)t)

is the correlation function of the bath operator.
Bochner’s theorem guarantees
S_{E}(t)\geq 0
for all times, ensuring
that
\sum_{i}R_{i}^{\dagger}(t,t^{\prime})R_{i}(t,t^{\prime})=S_{E}(t-t^{\prime})\hat{I}
is
positive semi-definite, guaranteeing that the dynamics is CP.
The Markovian limit is recovered when
|f_{E}(\omega)|^{2}n(\omega)=\mathrm{const}
, i.e. white,
and
S_{E}(t)\propto\delta(t)
.
(See Sec. II of Supplemental Information
Sup
for details.)
We further include Markovian dissipation by adding Lindblad operators

L_{1}=\sqrt{\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}}\sigma^{\dagger}
and
L_{2}=\sqrt{\bar{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{M}}}\sigma

to the master equation.
The resulting form of
Eq.

10
is a Volterra integro-differential equation of
the second kind, which can be solved (see Sec. III of Supplemental Information
Sup
).

We consider the continuous weak measurement of the operator
\sigma
, which defines the
emission spectrum
S(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos(\omega\tau)\mathds{E}[I_{\sigma}(t+\tau)I_{\sigma}(t)]
.
Using the formalism developed above, and the solution of the master equation, we can evaluate
the emission spectrum.
In the limit of small detuning and large pumping rate, it takes the form
(see Sec. III of Supplemental Information
Sup
for details)

S(\omega+\omega_{0})=\mathrm{Re}\!\left[\frac{1}{4\lambda_{0}}+\frac{1}{8\lambda_{+}}+\frac{1}{8\lambda_{-}}\right],

(21)

where

\lambda_{0}\approx-i\omega+\Gamma_{\mathrm{T}}
,
and

{\lambda_{\pm}\approx-i\omega\pm 2i\Omega+3\gamma^{\prime}_{\mathrm{T}}(\omega)/2}
,
with
\gamma^{\prime}_{\mathrm{T}}(\omega)=(\Gamma_{\mathrm{M}}+\bar{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{M}}+\frac{4}{3}\Gamma_{\mathrm{NM}}+\frac{2}{3}\gamma_{\mathrm{NM}}(\omega))/2
and
{\Gamma_{\mathrm{T}}=\gamma^{\prime}_{\mathrm{T}}(0)}
.
This is the non-Markovian analog of the Mollow triplet
Mollow 1969
: the three-peak
structure of the spectrum is preserved, but each peak acquires a frequency-dependent
linewidth governed by
\gamma_{\mathrm{NM}}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}S_{E}(t)e^{i\omega t}\differential{t}
,
directly encoding the memory of the non-Markovian bath.
This is qualitatively similar to the phenomenological description of non-Markovian damping of a
harmonic oscillator by a frequency-dependent damping rate
Saulson 1990
, but our formalism
not only derives it from first principles, but also enables assigning a legitimate notion of a
quantum state to the system at all times, in contrast to the phenomenological approach.

Conclusion.

We have defined a novel class of discrete-time non-Markovian quantum stochastic processes on
the space of quantum states, and characterized its form under the assumption of complete positivity
and non-signalling.
Its continuous-time limit, when it exists, is given by a non-Markovian generalization of the
GKSL equation given by
Eq.

10
.
This framework is directly applicable to open quantum systems driven by noises with
arbitrary integrable power spectral density, and allows a consistent definition of the
quantum state of such a system at all times.
We have shown how this state determines the results of continuous measurements of the system,
circumventing the failure of the regression theorem in the non-Markovian regime, and allowing all
operationally meaningful predictions to be extracted from the theory.
All of this is illustrated on the example of a driven two-level system coupled to a bosonic
bath without the Markov approximation; the resulting non-Markovian analog of the Mollow triplet
encodes the memory of the bath in the frequency-dependent linewidth of each peak.

Open problems include the extension of the formalism to deal with noises with
non-integrable power spectral density (such as
1/f
noise), which will enable the consistent
assignment of a quantum state to such systems at all times; and a full quantum trajectory
description of such a system, which will enable full state estimation and feedback control of
non-Markovian systems.

Extension of an affine map on the base of a convex cone to the whole cone

The requisite characterization, namely
Theorem

1
below, can be extracted from standard
results at the intersection of convex analysis and functional analysis
Asimow and Ellis 1980
.
Here we give a self-contained elementary proof.
The following definitions, required to state the result, are standard in convex analysis
Rockafeller 1970
.

A
cone

C
is a subset of a real Banach space
V
such that if
x\in C
,
then
\alpha x\in C
for all
\alpha>0
.
The cone has a
base
iff. there exists a continuous linear functional
f:V\to\mathds{R}

such that
f(x)>0
for all
x\in C
; then the base of the cone with respect to
f
is the set

B_{f}(C)=\{x\in C\mid f(x)=1\}
, which is a subset of
C
.

A
convex set

S
is a set
such that if
x,y\in S
, then
\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\in S
for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

A
convex cone
is a cone that is also a convex set.
Let
C
be a convex cone. Then for any
x,y\in C
and
\alpha,\beta>0
, we have
\alpha x+\beta y\in C
since

\alpha x+\beta y=(\alpha+\beta)[\tfrac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}x+\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}y]
,
which is
(\alpha+\beta)
, a positive number,
times a convex combination of
x
and
y
.
Thus, a convex cone is closed under addition and positive scalar multiplication.

The base of a convex cone is a convex set. Suppose
b,b^{\prime}\in B_{f}(C)
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Then
\lambda b+(1-\lambda)b^{\prime}\in C
by the convexity of the cone; furthermore, since
f
is linear,

f(\lambda b+(1-\lambda)b^{\prime})=\lambda f(b)+(1-\lambda)f(b^{\prime})=\lambda\cdot 1+(1-\lambda)\cdot 1=1
; so
\lambda b+(1-\lambda)b^{\prime}\in B_{f}(C)
.

Lemma 1
.

Every nonzero
x\in C
can be uniquely expressed as
x=\alpha b
for some
\alpha>0
and
b\in B_{f}(C)
.

Proof.

Take
\alpha=f(x)
and
b=x/f(x)
.
Since
f(b)=f(x/f(x))=f(x)/f(x)=1
, we have
b\in B_{f}(C)
.
∎

An
affine
(or convex-linear) map
h
between convex sets is a map such that for all
x,y
in the domain and
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

h(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)=\lambda h(x)+(1-\lambda)h(y)
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
C
be a convex cone with a base
B_{f}(C)
, and let
h:B_{f}(C)\rightarrow B_{f}(C)
be a bounded affine
map. Then there exists a unique bounded linear map
\tilde{h}
on the cone such that
(i)
\tilde{h}(\alpha x)=\alpha\tilde{h}(x)
for all
x\in C
and
\alpha>0
;
(ii)
\tilde{h}|_{B_{f}(C)}=h
; and
(iii)
f(\tilde{h}(x))=f(x)
for all
x\in C
.

Proof.

Define
\tilde{h}:C\to C
by

\tilde{h}(x)=f(x)\,h\!\left(\frac{x}{f(x)}\right).

This is well-defined:
f(x)>0
implies
x/f(x)\in B_{f}(C)
, so
h(x/f(x))\in B_{f}(C)\subset C
.

The boundedness of
\tilde{h}
follows from
\|\tilde{h}(x)\|=|f(x)|\|h(x/f(x))\|\leq C_{h}\|x\|
, where
C_{h}
is the norm of
h
.

The conditions (i), (ii), and (iii) are easy to verify:

(i) For
\alpha>0
:
\tilde{h}(\alpha x)=f(\alpha x)\,h\!\left(\frac{\alpha x}{f(\alpha x)}\right)=\alpha f(x)\,h\!\left(\frac{x}{f(x)}\right)=\alpha\,\tilde{h}(x)
,
using the linearity of
f
.

(ii) For
b\in B_{f}(C)
:
\tilde{h}(b)=1\cdot h(b)=h(b)
.

(iii)
f(\tilde{h}(x))=f\!\left(f(x)\,h\!\left(\frac{x}{f(x)}\right)\right)=f(x)\,f\!\left(h\!\left(\frac{x}{f(x)}\right)\right)=f(x)
,
since
h(x/f(x))\in B_{f}(C)
implies
f(h(x/f(x)))=1
.

Linearity.
We first show
\tilde{h}(x+y)=\tilde{h}(x)+\tilde{h}(y)
for
x,y\in C
.
Set
\alpha=f(x)
,
\beta=f(y)
, so that
x/\alpha,\,y/\beta\in B_{f}(C)
.
Since
C
is a convex cone,
x+y\in C
, and by linearity of
f
,
f(x+y)=\alpha+\beta
; thus
by the definition of
\tilde{h}
:

\tilde{h}(x+y)=(\alpha+\beta)\,h\!\left(\frac{x+y}{\alpha+\beta}\right).

The last factor can be simplified by writing

\frac{x+y}{\alpha+\beta}=\underbrace{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}_{\lambda}\frac{x}{\alpha}+\underbrace{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}_{1-\lambda}\frac{y}{\beta}

and using the affineness of
h
:

\begin{split}\tilde{h}(x+y)=(\alpha+\beta)\left[\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\,h\!\left(\frac{x}{\alpha}\right)+\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\,h\!\left(\frac{y}{\beta}\right)\right]=\alpha\,h\!\left(\frac{x}{\alpha}\right)+\beta\,h\!\left(\frac{y}{\beta}\right)=\tilde{h}(x)+\tilde{h}(y).\end{split}

Combined with (i), this gives
\tilde{h}(\alpha x+\beta y)=\alpha\,\tilde{h}(x)+\beta\,\tilde{h}(y)
for all
\alpha,\beta>0
, so
\tilde{h}
is linear on
C
.

Uniqueness
follows from
Lemma

1
and (i), (ii):
let
h^{\prime}:C\rightarrow C
be any linear map satisfying (i), (ii), then

h^{\prime}(x)=h^{\prime}\!\left(f(x)\cdot\frac{x}{f(x)}\right)\overset{(\mathrm{i})}{=}f(x)\,h^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{x}{f(x)}\right)\overset{(\mathrm{ii})}{=}f(x)\,h\!\left(\frac{x}{f(x)}\right)=\tilde{h}(x).

∎

The set of normalized quantum states forms the base of the convex cone of
sub-normalized states, with the trace
\mathrm{Tr}
identified as the linear functional
f
that defines the base.
With this identification,
Theorem

1
immediately applies to the single-variable map
\mathcal{K}_{1}

if
\mathcal{K}_{1}

For the multi-variable map
