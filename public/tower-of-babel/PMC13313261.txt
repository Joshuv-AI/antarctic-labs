Ab Osterhaus: My life in virology
Authors: Albert Osterhaus
Published: 2026

In Amsterdam during the 1950s and 1960s, I grew up with a strong fascination for animals and their well-being. This enthusiasm inspired me to study veterinary medicine at Utrecht University in The Netherlands, during which time an internship in Marian Horzinek’s newly founded Virology lab provided an opportunity to learn fundamental virological techniques. After graduating with my veterinary degree in 1974, I spent some time working as a veterinarian, enabling me to observe the profound effects of viral outbreaks on both wild and domestic animals. This deepened my commitment to virology and vaccine research.

Marian Horzinek subsequently offered me a PhD position, which provided considerable autonomy to explore the discovery of animal viruses in both wild and domestic animal species. I started by identifying previously unknown animal viruses. Notably, I described the first papillomavirus of a non-mammalian host, which caused skin papillomas on the legs and heads of finches. My doctoral research involved the development of a vaccine for a newly identified feline coronavirus (FIP virus). This project proved to be a pivotal moment in my scientific development. The candidate FIP vaccine we developed, generated high levels of virus-neutralizing antibodies but unexpectedly made animals more susceptible to severe disease following later exposure to the virus. This effect, known as vaccine-mediated enhancement, has also been observed in several other experimental virus vaccines and may result from unwanted virus-specific antibody or T cell responses. This is an issue that I also encountered during subsequent work on other candidate virus vaccines, like a vaccine candidate for feline lentivirus (FIV), a virus responsible for an AIDS-like disease in cats.

Upon completing my PhD, I joined the National Institute of Public Health (RIVM) in The Netherlands. Among my responsibilities was ensuring that vaccines produced for the Dutch population were free from extraneous viruses originating from the animal cell substrates used in their production. At that time, most RIVM viral vaccines were produced on cells from various SPF animal species, which allowed me a unique opportunity to search for new viruses across a broad range of animal species. My interest thus increasingly focused on viruses capable of crossing species barriers.

With my growing research group at RIVM, we identified the first documented cases of human and animal hantavirus infections in The Netherlands and discovered several viruses in monkeys, dogs, and rabbits among other species. These achievements led Marian Horzinek to offer me a part-time professorship focused on virus discovery, complementing my vaccine-related work at RIVM. This work also uncovered several new animal viruses, including the discovery of a morbillivirus – phocine distemper virus (PDV) – which devastated the harbor seal population in northwestern Europe in 1988 and later in 2002. After identification of the virus, we proved its causative role in the first epizootic using an experimental morbillivirus vaccine in seronegative captive seals that were subsequently challenged with PDV and were protected, thus fulfilling Koch’s postulates. We published the key findings on both outbreaks in Nature and Science, respectively, and the first discovery of the morbillivirus as the cause of recent seal deaths gained widespread international attention. This also led to new research collaborations for which I visited regions such as Lake Baikal, the Caspian Sea and the coastal waters of Mauretania, where related morbillivirus infections in endangered seal species were detected. Despite initial debate over whether pollution or PDV was the primary cause, our feeding experiments with juvenile seals in a seal center in The Netherlands, led by Lenie ‘t Hart, showed that pollution may indeed increase susceptibility to infections, possibly contributing to seal mass mortalities. International media coverage of this research brought significant public interest in seals and seal rehabilitation, prompting the release of rehabilitated seals from the Dutch center onto the French coast with Brigitte Bardot. Likewise, we released rehabilitated highly endangered Mediterranean monk seals in Greece alongside the Agha Khan, his Princes, and Melina Mercouri.

At RIVM, we also established the first laboratory in The Netherlands dedicated to producing monoclonal antibodies against various viruses, including polio, influenza, and rabies viruses. These monoclonal antibodies became invaluable tools in vaccine development and epidemiological studies. Hilary Koprowski, interested in this work, invited me to the Wistar Institute in Philadelphia to collaborate with Tad Wiktor on therapeutic monoclonal antibodies targeting rabies. After a rewarding period at the Wistar Institute, I returned to The Netherlands and refocused my expanding RIVM group on better understanding mechanisms of viral pathogenesis and vaccine-mediated immune protection. I began a highly productive collaboration with Bror Morein in Sweden, resulting in our first publication on ISCOM-adjuvanted vaccines in Nature. This early success prompted us to start a company in Sweden that, after extensive collaborative research, eventually led to the proprietary adjuvant “Iscom Matrix,” which is now used in commercial human and animal vaccines.

In the meantime, I moved with several RIVM group members to Rotterdam, where I became Head of Virology at the Erasmus Medical Center (EMC). In this role, I managed the hospital’s diagnostic virology laboratory and initiated an experimental “Viroscience” laboratory. We implemented a rigorous quality system for all virological diagnostic procedures, which formed the foundation for Viroclinics – a commercial company providing virological assays for clinical trials by the pharmaceutical industry. The independent revenue generated by Viroclinics, along with funding from several EU and other international projects that we had started, enabled the Viroscience group to invest in fundamental research and targeted studies on emerging viral diseases such as SARS, the H1N1 ‘swine flu’ pandemic, and MERS. This expanding Viroscience team welcomed back several Dutch virologists with international experience, including Ron Fouchier, Bernadette van den Hoogen, Guus Rimmelzwaan, Bert Niesters, Thijs Kuiken, and several visiting scientists such as Derek Smith and Martin Ludlow, each of whom brought unique and complementary expertise to a dynamic environment. Together with the other members of the group, we made pioneering discoveries of new or previously unrecognized viruses in humans and animals, and on their pathogenesis, epidemiology and control measures.

In 1997, together with Jan de Jong and Wellina Lim, a colleague from Hong Kong, we discovered that an influenza A virus that had caused the death of a young boy in a local hospital was a subtype avian H5N1 strain. At that time, when it was not yet generally accepted that avian influenza viruses could directly cross the species barrier to humans, we had a hard time convincing reviewers of Nature that indeed this avian virus could have crossed the species barrier with fatal consequences. It was not until additional 17 patients had become infected, five of whom also died, that we realized that we were facing a pandemic threat. Nearly all the infected people had a history of visiting live bird markets in Hong Kong. Apparently, the virus had not spread from human to human. Subsequently, different influenza H5N1 viruses caused extensive outbreaks in poultry and wild bird populations, sporadic cases in wild terrestrial and aquatic mammals, and most recently large outbreaks in cattle in the USA. In the latest decades, we have thus witnessed an emerging H5N1 panzootic, and although associated subsequent sporadic human cases have generally been relatively mild, H5N1 viruses continue to pose a pandemic threat to humans. Groundbreaking studies by Ron Fouchier and colleagues in the lab identified a small number of mutations that rendered an H5N1 virus airborne transmissible between ferrets. These experiments, although carried out with all the necessary safeguards and permissions, sparked a huge international discussion about the justification of gain-of-function experiments, a debate that continues to the present.

The development of a world-class research program on influenza virus proved invaluable when a large avian influenza outbreak occurred in poultry in 2003 in The Netherlands. We identified a highly pathogenic avian influenza A virus of subtype H7N7 not only in the affected chickens but also in poultry workers who had handled infected animals. Most of these individuals developed mild symptoms, although one fatal case occurred in a veterinarian with pneumonia in combination with acute respiratory distress syndrome. The potential of H7N7 viruses to cause disease in several mammalian species means that this subtype represents yet another pandemic threat to humans.

Soon thereafter, together with pediatricians in the EMC hospital, our group discovered a new virus in children with acute respiratory symptoms: human metapneumovirus (HMPV). We later showed that hMPV, like the closely related respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), causes serious respiratory problems not only in young children worldwide but also in older adults and immunocompromised individuals. Consequently, with the successful development and introduction of vaccines and human monoclonal antibodies for RSV, it is expected that these products will also soon follow for HMPV.

The emergence of SARS in 2003 led to our participation in a WHO coordinated international consortium of laboratories investigating the etiological agent responsible for SARS. This resulted in four major papers in Nature, Science, the New England Journal of Medicine and the Lancet that identified a new human coronavirus (later renamed SARS-CoV-1) as the cause of SARS. Among our activities was providing proof via experimental infection of macaques that the newly discovered SARS-associated coronavirus was the etiological agent of this disease and not HMPV that had also been found in several SARS patients in Hong Kong. Together with Bart Haagmans, we also successfully tested an antiviral and a vaccine candidate in the macaque model. These eventually were not needed, as the outbreak was brought under control with the implementation of strict isolation measures and rapidly developed diagnostic tests.

About 10 y later in close collaboration with Dr Zaki in Saudi Arabia, our lab identified a second previously unknown coronavirus in a 60-year-old patient who had died with ARDS: the Middle East Respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV). Again, in close collaboration with several other groups, we developed and distributed the first diagnostic tests, conducted the first epidemiological studies, identified the dromedary camel as the intermediate host, identified the DPP4 as viral receptor and tested, together with the group of Gerd Sutter in Munich, the first experimental MVA-based MERS vaccine candidate in seronegative camels with Quim Segales in Barcelona. This vaccine candidate is now in clinical trials in humans in Germany.

These collective efforts earned me the nickname “virus collector” in the journal Science and significantly advanced our understanding of viral pathogenesis, whilst contributing to the development of novel intervention strategies and supporting health authorities such as the WHO in combating viral disease outbreaks worldwide. As a result, I also became director of several WHO reference centers, chairman of the Scientific Veterinary Committee and of the BSE/TSE group of the EU. We also rewrote the textbook understanding of measles pathogenesis with respect to the early stages of infection and subsequent immunosuppression with Rik de Swart and Geert van Amerongen.

With the help of Eric Claassen, an expert in the valorization of scientific data, and the increasing scientific productivity of the laboratory, we established several additional spin-off companies together employing several hundreds of people, which enabled the effective testing of new vaccination and antiviral strategies for the pharmaceutical industry. The Dutch Ministry of Economic Affairs awarded us for these activities with a 1 million Euro “Valorization Award.” At the same time, I was heavily criticized by Dutch media for having a conflict of interest by collaborating as an academic scientist with pharmaceutical industry. Fortunately, this was categorically contradicted by the Board of EMC and by the Dutch Minister of Health. Despite facing criticism from the media, I have never hesitated to fulfill my responsibilities as a scientist by actively informing the public via participation in talk shows and other media events about the significance of viral infections and promoting intervention strategies, including the use of vaccines.

After retiring from EMC in Rotterdam, I took on the role of Guest-Professor at the University of Veterinary Medicine Hannover. Together with longtime collaborators Guus Rimmelzwaan and Martin Ludlow, we built a new group that continued research on virus discovery, viral pathogenesis, and the development of intervention strategies such as vaccination against emerging viruses. Besides discovering several new viruses, this work has focused on viral pathogenesis, developing and testing next-generation vaccine candidates as well as studying the possible protective or harmful effects of antiviral cell-mediated immunity. During the COVID-19 pandemic, much of this work focused on the pathogenesis of SARS-CoV-2 infections and novel-generation vaccines and monoclonal antibodies to combat pandemic threats.

Most recently, I have joined Gülsah Gabriel’s group at the Leibniz Institute of Virology in Hamburg, where I am involved in acquiring and managing new European and other externally funded projects.

Finally, I have (co-)authored over 1,400 peer-reviewed papers with more than 100,000 citations and an H index above 160, while I am a co-inventor on several major patents. I have chaired ESWI (eswi.org) and GOHC (gohc.org) for a long time, received notable awards, held senior editorial roles, and am presently also Chief Editor of One Health Outlook (Springer Nature). I belong to both the Dutch and German Academies of Sciences and am Commander of the Order of the Dutch Lion.

Most rewarding though has been that many former students and postdocs from my group now lead research teams worldwide, advancing research on viral pathogenesis and intervention strategies for humans and animals as part of the “One Health” initiative.
