Social norms and the decline in US cigarette smoking: evidence from 30 years of US representative surveys
Authors: David R Strong, John P Pierce, Karen Messer, Brian Dang, Sara McMenamin, Matthew D Stone, Dennis R Trinidad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChanging social norms on cigarette smoking are proposed as a primary strategy to reduce smoking behaviour.MethodsUsing core questions in 30 years of tobacco use supplements (TUS) to the Current Population Survey (CPS), we develop a state-specific, injunctive social norm scale of Willingness to Restrict Smoking (WTRS) and test its association with smoking behaviours. Respondents (n=1 462 050) to US national and state representative TUS-CPS conducted at nine regular intervals from 1992 to 2022.ResultsRespondents were queried on their WTRS in 10 different settings, which varied somewhat over time. Psychometric analyses of 10 items supported a single primary dimension that accounted for 74% (SD: 7.3%) of estimated common variance. Using item response theory methods, we calibrated scores across different years and demonstrated consistency of measurement over time (differential test functioning (DTF) across adjacent years = <1% difference in scores). The resulting WTRS scale had good reliability (omega-h mean = 0.75, SD=0.04 and alphamean=0.81, SD=0.03). Construct and concurrent validity of the scale were shown with regression analyses with outcomes of current cigarette smoking (p<0.001) and presence of home smoking restrictions (p<0.001). An 87% correlation between state-averaged smoking prevalence and WTRS score was observed over the 30-year period. The adjusted OR (AOR) of being a person who smokes for people in states with the lowest versus highest quartiles of states for the WTRS score was 0.74 (95% CI 0.69 to 0.76) which can be compared with the same OR between the lowest and highest quartiles of cigarette prices. (AOR=0.84, 95% CI 0.84 to 0.92).ConclusionsThis WTRS social norm scale was likely influenced by state tobacco control programmes targeting social norms and appears to be a strong indicator of declining smoking prevalence across 30 years of US data.

Introduction

Social psychologists have long noted that people usually do not make their decisions without looking to others for guidance on their thoughts and actions.13 The study of norms has been an active research topic in a number of disciplines4 and, while definitions vary somewhat across disciplines, common elements include that social norms are shared understandings within groups of what constitutes appropriate behaviour for group members. The groups studied have ranged from small friendship groups5 to large groups such as societies.6 Descriptive norms are those that people learn from observing others and these are differentiated from injunctive norms which people learn from the degree of social approval or sanction of behaviours by group members.7 Norms have also been differentiated on whether they are perceived (reported beliefs) or objective (assessed by behaviours).8 Group members comply with norms because either they have internalised them or they want to avoid group disapproval/sanctions or, in some cases, legal sanction.9 10 Nations have been usefully ranked on a tightness-looseness continuum of injunctive normative rules by tallying the number of norms which are strongly enforced and on tolerance for deviance.11 12 US states have been shown to have reliable rankings by the tightness-looseness of their normative rules.13

Interventions have targeted social norm change in order to effect population behaviour change.1420 It has been argued that success will depend on changing a norm that significantly affects behaviour (strong norm) and one that does not fade over time (stable norm).21 Cigarette smoking offers an interesting case study for such interventions. Although there was public health consensus that smoking caused disease in 1964,22 it was not until 1981 that the health risk to people who do not smoke from exposure to secondhand smoke was first documented,23 24 and the 1986 US Surgeon-General’s report concluded that such exposure was a causal agent.25 The first large scale interventions which explicitly aimed to change societal norms as a way of reducing involuntary exposure to secondhand smoke were included in the American Stop Smoking Intervention Study (ASSIST) (1991–1998),26 which funded interventions in 17 US states, using all other US states as controls, and the ongoing California Tobacco Control Program (CTCP, 1989–present).27

The formal evaluation of ASSIST included the 1991 development of a Tobacco Use Supplement (TUS) to the Current Population Survey (CPS), a large monthly federal survey that provided representative estimates for US states. Measurement of social norms was a key evaluation component for which six questions were allocated in the TUS questionnaire core.28 The questions assessed the respondent’s willingness to restrict smoking in a series of six different settings or locations (eg, hospitals, shopping malls). When aggregated at the state level, these were thought to reflect the injunctive norm of the disapproval of smoking in these settings. The average state-level willingness to restrict smoking in these settings increased throughout the 1990s.29

After the year 2000, the locations queried changed resulting in 10 settings queried across the 30-year period (1992–2022). In this paper, we used overlapping questions in each survey to anchor new locations that were queried and so develop a single dimensional WTRS scale that can be used to assess national and state estimates at each time point. We theorise that a valid WTRS scale will have lower scores among people who smoke currently and higher scores among those who restrict smoking in their home. We hypothesise that, over the 30-year period, the trends in the WTRS measure would be significantly related to smoking trends after controlling for known predictors30 such as cigarette price and socio-demographic factors. We also hypothesise that, over the study period and across all 50 states, the average state-wide WTRS score will be highly correlated with the statewide smoking prevalence.

Materials and methods

Data source

We use the publicly available repeated cross-sectional TUS-CPS between 1992 and 2022.31 The US Census Bureau conducts an ongoing, rolling monthly survey (CPS) of ~54 000 households that assesses labour force characteristics at the state and national level for the civilian, non-institutionalised US population. Beginning in 1992, the National Cancer Institute (NCI) coordinated a regular series of TUS to the CPS. Each TUS consists of three independent monthly samples (4 months apart) and typically spans two calendar years with sample sizes of ~150 000. TUS were undertaken in 1992–1993, 1995–1996, 1998–1999, 2001–2002, 2006–2007, 2010–2011, 2014–2015, 2018–2019 and 2022 and involved a total of 1 462 050 respondents. Response rates were over 60% for each of these surveys. Person-level replicate weights adjust for sampling design and non-response to provide both state and national-level estimates.32

Cigarette smoking measures

Respondents were asked ‘whether they had smoked 100 cigarettes in their lifetime’. Those who had not were categorised as ‘never smokers’. Those who had smoked 100 cigarettes in their lifetime were asked ‘whether they now smoked every day, some days, or not at all’ and were categorised as either ‘current smokers’ or ‘former smokers’.

Willingness to Restrict Smoking measures

From 1992 to 2022, the TUS core generally included six social norm questions that all started with the stem ‘Do you think that smoking should be…’ followed by specific settings. Response categories for each item were: 1 = ‘allowed in all areas’, 2 = ‘allowed in some areas’, 3 = ‘not allowed at all’. Smoking restrictions at home

Participants responded to the question ‘Which statement best describes the rules about smoking in your home’: (1) ‘Smoking is allowed anywhere’, (2) ‘Smoking is allowed in some places or at some times’ and (3) ‘No-one is allowed to smoke anywhere’.

State cigarette price

State level cigarette prices for the first of each TUS-CPS survey years were obtained from published annual data33 and are included as online supplemental eTable 1.

Population sociodemographic characteristics

Across the 30-year time period, the TUS-CPS used standard questions to identify respondent age (which we categorised into 18–34 years, 35–49 years, 50–64 years and 65+ years), sex, educational level (binarised to ‘less than college’ and college graduate). For race/ethnicity, Hispanic/Latinx origin or descent was first determined. Non-Hispanics were then categorised as follows: black; Asian/Pacific Islander; white; and other/mixed.

Analytical plan

We focus on scale construction rather than confirmatory model testing so that we are concerned with relative rather than absolute fit of models. Graded item response models (GRM) fit with ‘mirt’ software in R34 using TUS-CPS sampling weights, were used to describe the association between the willingness to restrict smoking in various settings and a common underlying tendency to restrict smoking in public places. We assessed the presence of a single primary dimension underlying responses via exploratory factor analysis. Exploratory graph analysis (EGA) was conducted using a network model,35 which however could not incorporate the sampling weights. Comparison of EGA fit to correlated multifactor or hierarchical bifactor models were favoured when Total Entropy Fit Index (TEFI) was lowest.36 37 Goodness of fit to factor models was described with the Standardised Root Mean Squared Residual (SRMSR), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker Lewis Index (TLI). Omega reliability38 estimates and comparison of the percentage of variance explained were used to assess the strength of a single primary dimension. We evaluated the relative strength of association between each item and levels of the overall scale (ie, discrimination), as well as breakpoints on the overall scale where the endorsement of subsequent item response options became more probable than the previous option (ie, severity threshold).39 The statistical code to generate the scale is presented in online supplemental eTable 2.

Consistency of measurement over time

To rule out possible bias in the scale over time, differential test functioning (DTF) using ‘mirt’ and TUS-CPS sampling weights tested whether social norm questions elicited different response patterns across years despite theoretically equal underlying homogeneity. Pairs of adjacent years with sufficient overlapping scale item reports were compared using the preceding year as reference group for each analysis of DTF.40 In order to estimate comparable levels of the WTRS over time, responses to smoking restrictions in ‘bars and cocktail lounges’ were used as an anchor item in all analyses to establish a common metric.41 Anchor items were chosen based on availability and exploratory non-parametric models suggesting consistent performance between years.42 Using the metric of the original raw scale, signed DTF (sDTF) tested if one or more survey years yielded consistently higher scores on some items despite similar levels of WTRS in adjacent years. For example, should the sDTF be 1, the composite WTRS score in the reference year would, on average, be one point higher than in the previous year survey. Unsigned DTF (uDTF) captured the average area between test curves showing expected total scores as a function of levels of WTRS. uDTF values quantified whether the test curves from reference years had more overall separation on average than previous years.43 For example, should the uDTF be 0, the relationship between increasing levels of WTRS and increasing expected scores on the scale would be the same in the reference and previous year. The per cent difference in scores between groups in WTRS scores between years reflected the magnitude of estimated differences between years. TUS-CPS sampling weights were used for factor analysis, GRM, DTF and estimation of means, but EGA was unweighted due to software limitations. Finally, a common score for comparisons across survey waves44 was computed using a calibration of all surveys and respondents from all years45 46 and, for respondents, non-administered items across waves were assumed missing at random.47

Demographic correlates and associations with restrictions at home and smoking status

With individual WTRS scores as the outcome, a series of linear models evaluated associations with individual sociodemographic characteristics, then individual reports of personal home restrictions on smoking and current cigarette smoking status. Models evaluating associations between restrictions at home or smoking status with WTRS scores adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics and tertile of state-level smoking prevalence. As previously presented in a study by Stone et al,48 the 50 US states were categorised into tertiles based on average smoking prevalence in 1992–2001 TUS-CPS surveys. Models controlled for time-invariant variation between states’ characteristics by grouping states within tertiles of prevalence when estimating associations with individual-level social norms. Finally, with individual smoking status (ie, current cigarette smoking) as the outcome, we assigned the state average estimates of WTRS scores to each resident and used logistic regression to estimate the strength of association between state average social norms and an individual’s current smoking status, controlling for socio-demographics and state-level cigarette prices. All regression models used TUS-CPS sampling weights.

Results

Changes in settings where smoking should not be allowed 1992–2022

Figure 1A presents the weighted estimates for the response ‘not allowed at all’ for each setting queried in each survey year. In 1992/1993, WTRS was already very high for hospitals (74.8%) and over 50% for indoor settings (workplaces, sporting events, shopping malls). There was a marked and somewhat parallel increase in the WTRS in each setting queried through 2010–2011, with some stabilisation in later years.

Across a range of model fit evaluation metrics, unidimensional models for each survey year fit the data reasonably well: the average SRMSR was 0.05 (range=0.03–0.08), the RMSEA was 0.10 (range=0.02–0.13), TLI was 0.95 (range=0.95–0.98) and CFI was 0.97 (range=0.95–0.99). However, a hierarchical bifactor model with a single primary dimension that allowed additional residual correlations among sets of items demonstrated improved fit compared with unidimensional models (χ2=85 094 1310.20, df=10, p<0.001) when analysing all respondents (n=1 462 050). The bifactor model indicated the presence of some residual item correlations after accounting for the primary dimension with the largest residual correlation (r=0.37) between items assessing willingness to restrict smoking in bars and casinos. Mean omega-hierarchical was 0.75 (SD=0.04) suggesting reliability of scores from the primary construct after accounting for residual variability. Percentage of variance explained by the single primary ‘smoking should not be allowed’ construct averaged 75% (range=61–85%) across years. EGA results compared a network model with multiple separate but correlated communities to a network configured to have a hierarchical structure with a single primary dimension organising smaller communities of correlated items. Relative fit of network models supported the robustness of the single primary dimension, with the TEFI for the bifactor model (−12.159) being lower than that for the separate correlated dimensions model (0.28). Each item had strong loadings (range=0.66–0.96) on a single primary factor and contributed substantial common variance (communalities (h2) range=0.43–0.92). The GRM parameters from all respondents across years largely paralleled observed item frequencies. Spearman rank correlation between observed item scores and model derived expected ‘a posteriori’ scores was r=0.94. Model estimated reliability was >0.80 for the majority of the observed score range.

Scoring the WTRS scale

With 10 settings potentially queried in each of the multiple survey waves, response scores within each wave were scaled from 10 to 30. A score of 10 indicated the selection of ‘1=allowed in all areas’ for all settings queried, while a score of 30 indicated the selection of ‘3=not allowed at all’ for all settings. The survey weighted average for the raw sum of the WTRS scale across waves was 25.49 (SD=4.72). In 1992/1993, the overall US score was 24.5 (95% CI 24.5 to 24.6), in 2001/2002 it was 26.0 (95% CI 26.0 to 26.1) and in 2022 it was 27.3 (95% CI 27.3 to 27.4) (figure 1B) and the pattern can be seen to reflect the changes in individual settings in figure 1A.

Measurement consistency over survey waves

Further support for a single scale came from 11 estimates of DTF scores for adjacent survey years (table 2). For there to be confidence in a single scale, the sDTF scores should differ by less than one point which is consistent with the conditional SE of measurement computed across a range of WTRS scores (median=0.78; Q1, Q3=0.28, 1.37).48 The observed sDTF range was 0.11–0.17, well within this criterion. Further, for those with comparable levels on the WTRS scale, the difference between the expected scores on the scale and the overall scores (uDTF%) should differ by <1%48 and this also occurred (range=0.09–0.95%). Sensitivity analyses using the work setting as an alternative yielded a similar sDTF range of 0.13–0.08. sDTF and uDTF values suggest the test curves from reference years provided consistent measurement and increase confidence in direct comparison of WTRS scores across years.

Concurrent validity

Sociodemographic associations with WTRS scale

With WTRS as the outcome, individual level characteristics were related to WTRS scores in a regression model. Each older age group was significantly more willing to restrict smoking than those aged 18–34 years (online supplemental eTable 3). Females were significantly more willing to restrict smoking than males. Compared with non-Hispanic (NH) white respondents, WTRS was higher among NH blacks, and substantially higher among NH Asians and in Hispanics. Respondents without a college education were less willing to restrict smoking than those who were college educated.

Smoking status associated with WTRS scale

Individual level smoking status was related to the WTRS score levels as outcome (weighted mean=25.49; SD=4.72) after adjustment for age, sex, race-ethnicity, education and state tertile of 1992–2001 smoking prevalence. Overall, compared with people who have never smoked, the mean estimate on the scale was considerably lower with each smoking status level: people who smoked cigarette previously (b=−0.96, SE=0.03, p<0.001; d=−0.20); people who smoke cigarettes some-days (b=−2.21, SE=0.05, p<0.001; d=0.47); and people who smoke cigarettes daily (b=−4.01, SE=0.03, p<0.001; d=0.85) (online supplemental eFigure 1). Within each smoking status category, the scale increased over time (F(24,241) = 24.33, p<0.001). The largest change was seen for people who smoke daily where mean scores on the WTRS scale were below 22 in 1992–1993 but increased to 24.78 by 2022.

Home smoking restrictions and WTRS scale

Individual scores on the WTRS scale varied with reported restrictions of smoking in the home (online supplemental eFigure 2). Compared with those who with no restrictions on smoking in their homes, those who restricted smoking in certain areas or for specific people had higher scores on the scale (b=2.16, SE=0.02, p<0.001; d=0.46), with even higher scores observed for those who had a smoke-free home (b=4.24, SE=0.02, p<0.001; d=0.90). Over time, there was a trend for increased WTRS scale across these categories (F(16,250) = 56, p<0.001), with the largest change in the scale occurring among those who had no restrictions on smoking in their own home (mean score 1992/1993=22.2, increased to 24.4 in 2022).

Association of WTRS score with state smoking prevalence

Figure 2 depicts a scatterplot showing the state-average WTRS social norm score and the average smoking prevalence for each of the 50 US states. Scores within each decade are colour-coded. All 50 states progressed on WTRS over time and there is a strong negative correlation (r=−0.875) between state-average WTRS score and smoking prevalence over the three-decades. In a logistic regression model with individual cigarette smoking status as the response, both a one-unit change in the state-average WTRS score (adjusted OR (AOR)=0.90, 95% CI 0.88 to 0.90, p<0.001) and a one cent change in state price of cigarette packs (AOR=0.98, 95% CI 0.998 to 0.99, p<0.001) were associated with change in prevalence (online supplemental eTable 4).

Interpretability of these results is hindered because of the different range of units used for each of the variables. Accordingly, we standardised effect sizes for both the cigarette price variable and state-averaged WTRS score (table 3). Compared with respondents who lived in the quartile of states with the lowest cigarette prices, the odds of smoking for residents of states with the highest cigarette price were 16% lower (AOR=0.84, 95% CI 0.84 to 0.92). Discussion

Over the 30 years since 1992, the US population as a whole became increasingly willing to restrict cigarette smoking across all settings queried. The settings most likely to have restrictions endorsed were hospitals and children’s playgrounds, while bars and casinos were the least likely. Across these different settings and years, an underlying WTRS construct formed a scale with excellent psychometric properties: factor analysis and EGA demonstrated a strong, single primary dimension with good reliability; DTF demonstrated that the scale had excellent homogeneity across surveys. Concurrent validity was demonstrated by expected robust associations between scores on the scale with smoking status and the presence of home restrictions. Average scores on this WTRS social norm scale varied consistently across both smoking status and home restriction categories in expected ways, with increases in scores occurring within each increasing category, consistently over time. This WTRS scale meets both of Bicchieri’s criteria21 for a norm that can be effectively targeted with an intervention. It is a strong norm as it is followed by the majority of the population and is significantly associated with behaviour and it is a stable norm that does not fade over time. Indeed, over this period, the odds of being a smoker were much higher for residents in the lowest compared with the highest quartile of state WTRS scores.

In earlier research, Nyborg and Rege49 postulated that the increased protection of people who do not smoke could be explained by a change in social norms among people who smoke cigarettes, who became more considerate and less willing to expose people who do not smoke to secondhand smoke in different settings over time. Similarly, we showed that over the 30-year period there was a large increase in the willingness of people who smoke daily to restrict smoking in a number of settings. However, at any point in time, the willingness among people who smoke to restrict smoking remained far below that of people who do not smoke. The much higher levels of willingness by people who do not smoke to restrict smoking in a given location would seem to be an indication of their greater willingness to show disapproval of anyone who smokes in that setting, and may explain the increased ‘considerateness’ of people who smoke.

Prior to the scientific evidence about the harmful effects of exposure to secondhand smoke, a 1978 Roper report to the tobacco industry50 indicated that the changing social norm among people who do not smoke on exposure to secondhand smoke could be a major threat to the tobacco industry’s future cigarette sales. There was a large increase in the injunctive norm that people ought not to smoke in public settings after the 1986 Surgeon-General’s report concluded that secondhand smoke caused lung cancer in people who do not smoke.25 The California Tobacco Control Program (CTCP) viewed increasing this social norm as the necessary precursor to the passage of ordinances/laws to protect people who do not smoke.27 Willingness to restrict smoking in bars was low in California when bars and cocktail lounges were included in the 1994 California smoke-free workplace law.29 Because of this, the legislation allowed a 3-year phase-in time to allow the CTCP to build community support. During this time, the CTCP conducted mass media campaigns that featured bar workers who do not smoke pleading their case to have a smoke-free workplace. Also, CTCP mounted a large outreach to business owners to elicit and share data to alleviate concerns with the implementation of the smoke-free law.51 52 By 1998–1999, there had been a 61% increase in support for prohibiting smoking in bars and cocktail lounges in California compared with only a 15% increase in the rest of the country.29

A strength of this study is that the WTRS score is aggregated from responses of large representative samples to provide a state-level measure of the injunctive norm that smoking should not be allowed in various public settings. This can support future investigation of temporal associations between trends in norms and co-occurrence of events, such as the different timing of state adoption of new laws and ordinances or shifts in patterns of tobacco consumption. It is possible that changing social norms may explain the declines in cigarette smoking initiation that have also been seen among the states with little history of state-level tobacco control efforts.53 Between 2002 and 2026, 27 other US states enacted comprehensive smoke-free workplace laws that required 100% smoke-free air in all indoor workplaces including restaurants and bars.54

One limitation is that social norms were assessed in a limited number of settings, and some settings were no longer assessed during periods when the willingness to restrict had broad public support. It is possible that other settings may reveal sources of variability not consistent with the general primary dimension underlying this scale. However, scores had associations with reported restrictions implemented in adults’ homes and differed by smoking status in ways expected, supporting the validity of the WTRS. The limited number and inconsistent types of settings assessed require assumptions about items not presented and may have limited the potential precision of the WTRS scale. Our scale would be strengthened by further validation with more detailed assessments of social norm attitudes, reasons for suggesting restrictions and perceptions of harms from secondhand smoke exposure. Additional exploration of differences by sociodemographic characteristics and sensitivity to regional policy differences would further support the validity and utility of this scale.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that questions asked in the large US nationally representative tobacco surveillance system form a psychometrically valid and reliable ‘WTRS’ social norm scale. The WTRS provides a quantitative measure of the injunctive norm that people who smoke should not be allowed to expose people who do not smoke to secondhand smoke. Development and validation of this scale will allow investigation of the role of this social norm in the different rates of decline in cigarette smoking observed in subgroups of the US population. With further validation, changes in the scale may be used to assess the effectiveness of different tobacco control interventions.
