Radiofrequency Septal Reduction After Transcatheter Mitral Valve Replacement to Treat Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Obstruction
Authors: Praneeth Katrapati, Marvin Eng, Philip Gideon, Michael F Morris, Paul Sorajja, Roderick Tung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR) has emerged as an important treatment for mitral stenosis. Radiofrequency catheter ablation has been shown to be effective as a pre-emptive strategy to mitigate left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction.Case SummaryA 78-year-old man undergoes a TMVR. He develops significant LVOT obstruction. Radiofrequency ablation is used as a therapeutic procedure.DiscussionLVOT obstruction is an important complication of TMVR. History of Presentation

A 78-year-old man presented to the emergency department with a chief complaint of shortness of breath and worsening dyspnea on exertion over the past 2 to 3 months. In addition, he had increasing malaise and fatigue during this time period. His physical examination revealed mild distress, tachycardia, lung crackles, and 2+ lower extremity edema.

Past Medical History

His medical history included coronary artery bypass grafting and bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement 7 years prior, reduced ejection fraction (35%-40%), persistent atrial fibrillation, and severe mitral stenosis.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included acute-on-chronic systolic heart failure exacerbation, atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, severe mitral valve stenosis, and pulmonary hypertension.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiogram confirmed severe mitral annular calcification, severe mitral stenosis, and a peak left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) gradient of 2 mm Hg at baseline. Cardiac computed tomography performed for transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR) planning demonstrated a neo-LVOT of 219 mm2 and a left ventricular anterobasal septal thickness of 13 mm.

Management

The patient underwent a successful TMVR using a 29-mm SAPIEN (Edwards Lifesciences) in the mitral position,1 although follow-up echocardiography the following day revealed elevated gradients in the LVOT (peak = 65 mm Hg) (Figure 1A). Coronary anatomy was not deemed suitable for alcohol septal ablation, given severe stenosis of the native arteries (Figure 2). Radiofrequency catheter ablation was therefore planned for septal reduction.2,3

A transseptal approach was implemented, given recent data suggesting improved safety with this method,4 along with the use of a steerable sheath (Agilis; Abbott) and an irrigated ablation catheter (TactiFlex; Abbott). Targeted ablation was delivered with direct visualization under intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) guidance with a zero-fluoroscopic approach to achieve localized akinesis of the basal septum and the closest contact point to the skirt of the TMVR. A total of 23 lesions were delivered for approximately 30 to 60 seconds per lesion, with increased echogenicity visualized on ICE as an indicator of lesion formation and depth to mitigate systolic narrowing of the neo-LVOT in closest proximity to the skirt of the anteroseptal aspect of the TMVR (Figure 3). Adequate lesion application was qualified by elimination of local electrograms on the ablation catheter as well as visualization to cover the septal and anteroseptal left ventricle as seen on ICE, which was further confirmed by localized septal thickening after ablation. Intraprocedural LVOT gradient monitoring was not performed.

The patient's preprocedural electrocardiogram revealed atrial fibrillation with a baseline left bundle branch block with a QRS duration of 170 milliseconds. Given this conduction abnormality, consideration was not given to worsening of conduction delay as a consequence of ablation, with a QRS duration of 175 milliseconds on postprocedure electrocardiogram.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Postprocedural transthoracic echocardiogram the day after ablation demonstrated a significant reduction in the mean LVOT gradient (65-22 mm Hg) (Figure 1B). The patient recovered without complications, including no stroke or transient ischemic attack, and was discharged home. Follow-up echocardiography performed 45 days postablation revealed persistent reduction in the peak LVOT gradient (17 mm Hg) (Figure 1C).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of septal reduction therapy using radiofrequency energy as a therapeutic strategy for severe LVOT obstruction after TMVR. LVOT obstruction is an important complication of TMVR, commonly defined as a peak LVOT gradient >30 mm Hg or >10 mm Hg increase in LVOT gradient after TMVR.5 Preprocedural cardiac computed tomography is used to help predict the risk of LVOT obstruction after TMVR via calculation of the anticipated neo-LVOT. The risk of LVOT obstruction is significantly increased in patients with a predicted neo-LVOT <200 mm2; however, as evident in our patient in whom the predicted neo-LVOT was 219 mm2, such cutoffs have limitations when considered as binary thresholds.

The mainstay strategies for septal reduction therapy are septal myectomy and alcohol septal ablation. Septal ablation is often limited by coronary anatomy, whereas myectomy is often inherently contraindicated in patients who qualify for TMVR due to surgical risk. LVOT obstruction from hypertrophied septum and anterior mitral leaflets remains the most important limitation of TMVR. Various strategies such as laceration of the anterior mitral leaflet to prevent outflow obstruction, SEptal Scoring Along the Midline Endocardium, and balloon-assisted translocation of the mitral anterior leaflet have been developed to mitigate the anterior transposition of the aortomitral curtain into the LVOT that can occur during TMVR.6 These electrosurgical techniques remain limited in their use due to their relative complexity, as well as imprecision of the target location and limited effectiveness in various patients (eg, heavy leaflet calcification).

Radiofrequency ablation has been introduced as an alternative by Killu et al among others.3,7 While these reports have described preprocedure radiofrequency ablation, our case describes success of this approach as a potential rescue strategy. Radiofrequency ablation has the advantages of being highly targeted without the constraints of coronary anatomy, especially with the use of ICE. Concurrent electroanatomical mapping can further help identify the conduction system, which lowers the risk of iatrogenic heart block. Inherent risks include myocardial edema leading to worsening LVOT obstruction, thrombosis from the myocardial injury leading to systemic embolism, and collateral injury (eg, inadvertent mitral leaflet damage). With limited application of radiofrequency ablation for septal reduction, a multidisciplinary heart team approach is prudent and further investigation is needed.

Conclusions

LVOT obstruction remains a significant anatomic limitation in patients undergoing TMVR. This case illustrates the feasibility and novelty of septal reduction therapy with conventional radiofrequency ablation catheters in patients who develop significant LVOT obstruction after TMVR intervention as a bailout therapy.
