Puerarin ameliorates obesity-induced spermatogenic dysfunction by targeting GRP78/StAR to increase testosterone synthesis
Authors: Yongyi Pan, Yaoxuan Li, Kongwei Huang, Qiumei Huang, Zhenhui Fu, Hui Wu, Jiameng Yang, Yunqian Cai, Weiqing Qian, Xiaohan Cao, Xiaocan Lei, Lintao Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTestosterone synthesis dysfunction caused by obesity is a major contributor to male infertility, but effective therapeutic options remain limited. Puerarin (Pue), a bioactive compound from Pueraria lobata, has lipid-lowering and reproductive-protective properties. This study aimed to investigate the mechanisms by which Pue ameliorates obesity-associated spermatogenic impairment.MethodsA high-fat diet (HFD) mouse model was used to assess the effects of Pue supplementation on lipid metabolism, testosterone levels and spermatogenesis. Network pharmacology, transcriptomics, and activity-based protein profiling (ABPP) analyses were performed to explore potential pathways and protein targets. In addition, pull-down assays, immunofluorescence, and cellular thermal shift assays (CETSA) were used to validate target engagement, and the role of the identified target was further tested in palmitic acid (PA)-induced TM3 cells by using protein target inhibitors and protein target overexpression to verify the ability of Pue to ameliorate obesity-related testosterone synthesis dysfunction.ResultsPue supplementation markedly alleviated obesity-induced metabolic disorders by reducing body weight gain, adiposity, and hepatic lipid deposition while improving serum triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels. Pue supplementation preserved testicular and epididymal morphology and significantly increased the serum testosterone level, sperm count, and sperm motility. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that Pue restored the expression of steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR), cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 1 (CYP11A1), 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (3β-HSD), and cytochrome P450 family 17 subfamily A member 1 (CYP17A1). Pull-down assays, molecular docking, and CETSAs revealed that glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78) is a Pue-binding target, with Pue forming hydrogen bonds at the TYR-65 residue. Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and colocalization assays verified the specific GRP78–StAR interaction. Pue binding enhanced the chaperone activity of GRP78, stabilized StAR and upregulated its expression. The inhibition of GRP78 reduced StAR expression and testosterone secretion in TM3 cells, whereas Pue supplementation reversed these effects, and GRP78 overexpression further enhanced the therapeutic effect of Pue.ConclusionPueraria root alleviates obesity-induced spermatogenic dysfunction by improving lipid metabolism, upregulating the expression of key testosterone enzymes, and promoting testosterone synthesis through the targeting of GRP78 by StAR expression. These findings reveal a novel mechanism through which Pue restores androgen biosynthesis and provide a scientific basis for the development of Pueraria-derived interventions for obesity-associated male infertility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08089-y.

Introduction

Obesity is recognized by the World Health Organization as a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by excessive fat accumulation. Obesity not only increases the risk of developing metabolic diseases but also adversely affects male reproductive function, posing a significant threat to global public health [1]. Notably, the prevalence of obesity among reproductive-aged men has nearly tripled over the past three decades, paralleling the global rise in male infertility [2]. Numerous studies have shown that obese men exhibit significantly impaired semen parameters, including reduced semen volume, sperm concentration, and total sperm count, with the severity of reproductive dysfunction positively correlated with increasing body mass index [3–5]. Given the increasing prevalence of obesity and its associated reproductive impairments, identifying effective interventions to mitigate obesity-related male reproductive dysfunction is of critical clinical importance and represents an urgent public health priority.

Obesity-induced testosterone synthesis dysfunction is closely associated with spermatogenic dysfunction [6]. Testosterone biosynthesis depends critically on key regulatory proteins such as StAR, CYP11A1, 3β-HSD, and CYP17A1 [7]. Studies have shown that obesity downregulates the expression and activity of these enzymes at both the transcriptional and translational levels, thereby compromising testosterone synthesis and contributing to reproductive impairment [8]. Despite these insights, effective therapeutic strategies to restore testosterone synthesis function remain limited [9]. Natural products, particularly those derived from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), have attracted significant interest because of their multitarget properties [10]. In this context, natural products with both antiobesity and reproductive protective properties are of particular interest.

Pueraria is derived from the root of the leguminous plant Pueraria lobata. Its wide range of applications are attributed to its diverse pharmacological properties, including combating atherosclerosis, hypertension, and diabetes [11]. Notably, puerarin (Pue) is one of its main active constituents. In addition to its potent antiobesity effects, Pue also shows promise for treating reproductive dysfunction. Research has demonstrated that in the context of busulfan-induced spermatogenic dysfunction, supplementation with Pue can effectively ameliorate this dysfunction by suppressing oxidative stress and apoptotic processes within the testes [12]. Furthermore, in cadmium-exposed rats, supplementation with Pue upregulated the expression of glycolytic genes and increased the protein levels associated with lactate production [13]. However, most existing studies are limited to animal or in vitro models, and direct clinical evidence supporting the effects of Pue on human male reproductive function remains insufficient; moreover, its efficacy in ameliorating obesity-induced testosterone synthesis dysfunction has yet to be clarified.

In this study, the potential mechanisms through which Pue alleviates obesity-induced spermatogenic dysfunction were investigated. Through comprehensive network pharmacology, transcriptomic, and ABPP analyses, this study aimed to characterize the direct binding targets and elucidate the molecular pathways underlying the beneficial effects of Pue. These results provide a robust theoretical foundation for the application of Pue in the prevention and treatment of obesity-induced spermatogenic dysfunction.

Materials and methods

Animal models and procedures

Three-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Hunan Slaike Jinda Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. and housed under SPF conditions (22 ± 1 °C, 12-h light‒dark cycle). After 1 week of adaptation, the mice were randomly divided into four groups: control (Ctrl, n = 10), obesity (HFD, n = 10), Pue-treated obesity (HFD + Pue, n = 10), and positive control (HFD + Ot, n = 10). The mice received oral administrations of Pue, orlistat (Ot), or vehicle for 8 consecutive weeks. The animal experimental protocol was approved by the People’s Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee (Approval NO: KY-GZR-2025-111).

Dosage information

Male mice were randomly divided into two groups during the experiment. The control group (n = 10) was fed a conventional maintenance diet for 12 weeks. The HFD group (n = 30) was fed a diet for 12 weeks, the detailed nutritional composition, energy density, and commercial source (catalog number where applicable) of both diets are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

During the treatment phase, HFD-fed mice were randomly allocated into three groups: HFD (n = 10), HFD + Pue (100 mg/kg, n = 10) [14], and HFD + Ot (60 mg/kg, n = 10). Pue was dissolved in saline and administered by daily oral gavage for 8 weeks. Ot (Hunan Mingrui Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Hunan, China) served as the positive control and was administered at 60 mg/kg/day for 8 weeks [15]. The control and HFD groups were maintained on their respective diets throughout the same period.

Oral glucose tolerance test

One day prior to sacrifice, the mice underwent an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) following a 12 h fast. Glucose (2.0 g/kg) was administered via oral gavage, and blood glucose levels were measured (Roche ACCU-CHEK® Performa blood glucose metre) at 15, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min postadministration [16]. Glucose tolerance was assessed by calculating the area under the curve (AUC).

Lee’s index and adipose tissue measurements

Body weight (BW) was recorded weekly throughout the 8-week intervention. Body length (BL) was measured under light anaesthesia (1% propofol, 0.1 mL/10 g, i.p.) from the tip of the nose to the anus. Waist circumference was measured immediately after BL at identical time points. For adipose tissue measurements, the mice were euthanized, and the visceral and subcutaneous fat pads (including the epididymal, perirenal, and inguinal depots) were dissected and weighed using an analytical balance. Body fat percentage was calculated as (abdominal fat mass/BW) x100%.

Blood biochemistry assay

Blood was collected, left to stand at 4 °C for 3 h, and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm (~ 1000 × g) for 15 min to isolate serum. Serum biochemical parameters, including cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), insulin (Ins), testosterone, and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels, were measured using commercial assay kits (Biosino BioTechnology and Science, China).

Analysis of reproductive tissues and sperm

After 8 weeks of treatment, the mice were anaesthetized, and relevant tissues were collected for evaluation. The cauda epididymis was carefully minced and placed in a petri dish containing 1.5 mL of saline. The sperm were then transferred to a haemocytometer. Morphological evaluation of the sperm was performed using eosin staining. Additionally, the total sperm count and the number of sperm with abnormalities were quantified using a haemocytometer under a 40× magnification microscope. The observation method followed the same protocol as described previously [18]. A total of 300 sperm cells were counted on each slide, and the percentage of deformed sperm was calculated. The percentage of motile sperm was visually evaluated in each sample by viewing a drop of sperm suspension diluted with Tris buffer solution (3.63 g of Tris[hydroxymethyl]aminomethane, 0.50 g of glucose, 1.99 g of citric acid, and 100 mL of distilled water) on a prewarmed (37 °C) slide under a prewarmed coverslip with light microscopy at 400x magnification. Motility estimations were performed from four different fields in each sample. The mean of the four estimations was used as the final motility score [19].

Liver, adipose, testes and epididymis histopathology

For haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, liver, adipose, testis and epididymis tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, dehydrated, cleared, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 3 μm. The sections were dried at 65 °C for 1 h and stained with haematoxylin, acid‒alcohol, and eosin. The slides were sealed with neutral balsam. For oil red O staining, liver tissues were fixed, incubated in 20% sucrose at 4 °C overnight, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, and cryosectioned. Sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, rinsed, air-dried, and stained with oil red O as previously described [20].

Cell culture

TM3 and HEK293T cells were purchased from Cellcook Biotech Co., Ltd. and cultured in Dubecco’s modified Eagle’s medium nutrient mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12) supplemented with 5% certified foetal bovine serum and 2.5% horse serum at 37 °C in a humid atmosphere with 5% CO2. TM3 cells were first seeded on a 6-well plate for the treatments and grown for 24 h. Subsequently, 0.4 mM palmitic acid (PA) and 1 µM Pue were added to the medium for 24 h, PA was used to mimic obesity-induced lipid accumulation in Leydig cells, thereby establishing an in vitro model of obesity-associated impairment of testosterone synthesis [21].

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8)

A CCK-8 kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, Cat. No. C0039) was used to evaluate the viability of TM3 cells. After being plated into 96-well plates at a seeding density of 2000 cells per well, the TM3 cells were incubated for 24 h. Then, the cells were treated with 0.4 µM PA and different concentrations of Pue or Pue-P for 72 h. CCK8 reagent was added to the cells, and they were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Finally, the OD values were measured at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Immunohistochemical analysis (IHC) of testes

Testicular tissues were fixed with 4% (w/v) PFA for 24 h at 4 °C, followed by sequential dehydration in 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100% ethanol (1 h per step), clearing in xylene (30 min × 2), and embedding in paraffin wax (melting point 56–58 °C). A series of 4-µm sections was prepared via a rotary microtome and placed onto a polylysine-coated slide. For immunohistochemical staining, the sections were deparaffinized by baking at 65 °C for 30 min, rehydrated through decreasing concentrations of ethanol (100% to 75%), and subjected to antigen repair by heating in a microwave (95 °C, 20 min) in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After cooling, nonspecific binding was blocked with a PBS solution containing 5% (w/v) BSA at 37 °C for 1 h. The primary antibodies were then incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the sections were washed with PBS three times for 5 min each and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h with an HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody. The antigen‒antibody complex was visualized using 3,3′-DAB chromogens (containing 0.003% H₂O₂, 0.05% w/v in 0.05 M Tris–HCl buffer, pH 7.6) at room temperature for 5 min, followed by counterstaining with haematoxylin (0.5% w/v, 2 min). The sections were then dehydrated through increasing concentrations of ethanol (75% to 100%), cleared in xylene (5 min × 2), and mounted with neutral resin. Stained sections were observed under a light microscope (Olympus BX53) equipped with a digital camera (Olympus DP74). Five randomly selected fields at 400× magnification were analysed per sample using ImageJ software (NIH) to quantify the intensity of positive staining. All primary and secondary antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

RT‒qPCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from the tissues using TRIzol® reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and then converted into cDNA by reverse transcription reagent kits (Monad, Wuhan, China). The primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The mRNA expression levels of genes were normalized to those of GAPDH. The primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 3.

Western blot analysis

Testis tissues were lysed using RIPA buffer (Boster B logical Technology, Wuhan, China) supplemented with PMSF and a phosphatase inhibitor. Total protein was extracted, and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit. The protein samples were loaded onto SDS‒PAGE gels and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The proteins on the membrane were incubated with the appropriate primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by subsequent incubation with the corresponding secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The signals were detected using a chemiluminescence imaging system.

Network pharmacology database and software

PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), PharmMapper (http://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/), Swiss Target Prediction (https://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), UniProt (http://www.uniprot.org/), GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org), DisGeNET (https://www.disgenet.org/), the DrugBank platform (https://go.drugbank.com/), and the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) were used.

Network pharmacology experimental methods

By searching the keywords “Pueraria lobata” or “Pueraria lobata radix” in the TCMSP database, information on its active ingredients was obtained. In accordance with the oral bioavailability (OB) and drug-like property (DL) of the pharmacokinetic parameters, the chemical components meeting both an OB ≥ 20% and a DL ≥ 0.18 were screened. After comparison and sorting, the active components of Pue were obtained by reference to the relevant literature.

The identification of potential drug targets was conducted through the utilization of databases such as PubChem, PharmMapper, and Swiss Target Prediction. All identified targets were subsequently annotated with official information via the UniProt Knowledgebase search tool, leveraging data from the UniProt protein database. The targets with “humans” as the research species were collected, and the action target of the active ingredient of Pue was determined. In the GeneCards, DisGeNET, and DrugBank databases, the keyword “obesity” was entered to identify obesity-related targets. The obesity targets from the three major databases were combined, and duplicate genes and false-positive genes were removed. Matching the collected obesity target with the active ingredient target of Pue, the common target was the potential obesity inhibition target of Pue. The selected therapeutic targets were further analysed by submitting them to the STRING database to construct a protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network, which was visualized using Cytoscape 3.8.0. To elucidate the biological roles of these targets, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed. In the final phase, key genes associated with the most significant pathways were selected for molecular docking studies, which were conducted using PyMOL 2.4.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

RNA sequencing of the testicular samples was performed by Novogene Corporation (Beijing, China). To assess RNA purity and concentration, we used a NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (Implen Inc.). In accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol, the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (California, USA) was used to construct the transcriptome sequencing library. Each sample contained 3 µg of RNA, and an index barcode was added to the sequences to uniquely identify each sample. The indexed samples were then clustered using the cBot Cluster Generation System and TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina, California, USA). After library preparation, sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads.

Synthesis of the Pue probes

Pue (400 mg, 0.92 mmol, 1.00 eq) was dissolved in 12 mL of DMF in an ice-water bath at 0 °C to prepare a mixed solution. Anhydrous K₂CO₃ (244 mg, 1.84 mmol, 2.00 eq) and Alkyne (221 mg, 0.92 mmol, 1.00 eq) were added portionwise to the above mixed solution with stirring at 1000 rpm (~ 112 × g). After stirring the reaction mixture at 0 °C for 5 min, it was transferred to an oil bath at 50 °C and reacted for 5 h, with the completion of the reaction monitored by LC-MS. The resultant product was then placed in a dialysis bag and subjected to continuous dialysis against deionized water for 48 h for desalting and DMF removal, followed by concentration via PEG-mediated dialysis and further purification via column chromatography (dichloromethane/methanol = 10/1). The final product was freeze-dried and stored in a desiccator for subsequent use.

Pull-down assay

TM3 cells were divided into a control group and a competition group. Cells were incubated with Pue-P for 2 h in the absence or presence of excess Pue, respectively. After incubation, the cells were harvested and total cellular proteins were extracted, with equal amounts of proteins (after protein quantification) taken for fluorescent labeling. A premixed click reaction cocktail (containing 50 mmol/L Azide-Biotin, 100 mmol/L THPTA, 1 mmol/L sodium ascorbate, and 1 mmol/L copper sulfate) was added to the protein lysate, and the reaction was carried out overnight at 4 °C. Proteins were precipitated with pre-chilled acetone (at a volume ratio of 1:4, lysate to acetone) at -20 °C for 6 h, and the precipitate was collected by low-speed centrifugation. The precipitate was resuspended and incubated with streptavidin magnetic beads at 4 °C for 6 h with gentle rotation, then dissolved in 1× loading buffer and denatured at 95 °C for 5 min. The samples were loaded and subjected to 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and the labeled proteins were visualized using a fluorescent imaging system.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

TM3 cells were treated with Pue (1 µM) or DMSO and incubated for 12 h. The cells were collected, washed with PBS, and then resuspended to a density of 5 × 106 cells/ml in PBS supplemented with protease inhibitor. One hundred microlitres of each cell suspension was then dispensed into PCR tubes, heated at 44–60 °C by a thermal cycler for 3 min and immediately lysed by freeze-thawing in liquid nitrogen. The cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 15,000 rpm (~ 21,000 × g) for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were subsequently analysed by immunoblotting.

Immunofluorescence experiments

Cells were supplemented with or without Pue-P for 6 h on Petri dishes, washed with precooled PBS buffer, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and then blocked with foetal bovine serum. After the blocking buffer was removed, the cells were incubated with a fluorescence-tagged primary antibody overnight at 4 °C and, the next day, were washed with 0.1% Tween in PBS prior to incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h. After staining with DAPI, fluorescence images were obtained by scanning laser confocal microscopy.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

HEK293 cells were transfected with the Flag-GRP78 plasmid. At 24 h post-transfection, the cells were lysed by brief sonication on ice in immunoprecipitation (IP) buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, and 0.1% Triton X-100) supplemented with a complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (where applicable). Cell lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm (~ 25,200 × g) for 30 min at 4 °C, and the clarified supernatant was carefully collected. Immunoprecipitation was carried out by incubating the supernatants with Anti-FLAG M2 monoclonal antibody for 1 h at room temperature with gentle end-over-end rotation, followed by incubation with protein A/G agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4 °C overnight with continuous gentle rotation. The beads were washed three times with cold IP buffer, and the immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted in 2× SDS sample buffer and denatured at 95 °C for 5 min. The eluted samples were loaded onto gels and analyzed by Western blotting.

Over-expression of GRP78 (OE-GRP78)

TM3 cells (50–70% confluence) were treated with a GRP78 overexpression plasmid (PCMV-MCS) from Escherichia coli (synthesized by VectorBuilder, Guangzhou, China), and a scrambled sequence was used as a control. Briefly, 4 µL of Lipo 3000™ and 4.5 µL of PCMV-MCS (2.5 µg) were incubated in Opti-MEM for 20 min at room temperature. The mixture was added to cells cultured in 6-well plates containing 2 mL of serum-free DMEM. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS), and the cells were further treated for 24 h.

MS analysis

The pulled-down beads were subsequently washed ten times with cold 50 mM Tris-Cl (pH = 7.5), dissolved in mixed solution (8 M urea, 25 mM Tris-Cl (pH 7.5), and 10 mM EDTA) and analysed on a high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled to an Orbitrap Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To generate an extracted ion chromatogram, the raw data were processed using Seguesi and Proteome Discoverer software (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Data analysis

Statistical evaluation of datasets with a normal distribution was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0), regardless of whether the data were dependent or independent. The results are presented as the mean ± SD. The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test was used to evaluate the normality of the data, and the Bartlett’s test was applied to assess homoscedasticity. Pearson correlation analysis was performed through the Omic Studio platform available at https://www.omstudio.cn. Partial correlation analysis was conducted with SPSS and Python libraries. Statistical significance was assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s posthoctests or Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn posthoctests. **p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Screening of active ingredients and targets of Pue

To investigate the potential molecular targets of Pue for the management of obesity, a network pharmacology analysis was conducted (Fig. 1). We identified 348 Pue-related genes through PubChem and other databases and obtained 2,653 obesity-related genes from the GeneCards database. We constructed a Venn diagram and screened out 184 common targets (Fig. 1A). A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed for these 184 common targets (Fig. 1B), with the top 50 hub genes shown in Fig. 1C. The top 30 targets for the antiobesity effects of Pue are shown in Fig. 1D. We subsequently performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis on these therapeutic targets and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis to predict potential antiobesity target-related signalling pathways (Fig. 1E–F). The results indicated that the antiobesity targets of Pue were enriched not only in lipid metabolism-related pathways involving adipokines and insulin resistance but also in steroid hormone biosynthesis pathways.

Pue prevents high-fat-diet-induced obesity and improves lipid metabolism

Pue is the active component in kudzu root (Fig. 2A). To investigate whether Pue ameliorates lipid metabolism abnormalities in obese mice, we established an obesity model by feeding mice a HFD (Fig. 2B). Compared with those in the Ctrl group, the body weights of the mice in the HFD group were markedly greater (Fig. 2C). After 6 weeks of Pue supplementation, the body weights of the mice in the HFD group were significantly lower (body weight and Lee’s index) (P < 0.05; Fig. 2D–E). Concurrently, the body fat percentage and abdominal fat mass in the HFD group exceeded those in the Ctrl group, with marked elevations in the lipid metabolism markers TG, TC, and low-density LDL and a decrease in HDL (P < 0.05; Fig. 2F–H). Haematoxylin‒eosin (H&E) staining revealed hypertrophic adipose tissue with disorganized cellular arrangement and variable morphologies. Pue supplementation significantly improved lipid metabolism abnormalities and lipid deposition in obese mice, with the HFD + Ot group showing a similar trend (Fig. 2I). To investigate whether obesity affects the level of insulin resistance in mice, we observed significantly lower serum insulin levels and impaired glucose tolerance in the HFD group than in the Ctrl group. Pue supplementation alleviated insulin resistance in obese mice (P < 0.05; Fig. 2J–K). Additionally, compared with the Ctrl group, the high-fat diet group exhibited elevated serum ALT and AST levels (P < 0.05; Fig. 2L). Further analysis via H&E staining of liver tissue revealed significant vacuolation and severe fatty degeneration in obese mice. Oil Red O staining confirmed lipid deposition in the livers of high-fat-diet-fed mice, whereas supplementation with Pue reversed this phenomenon (Fig. 2M–N).

Pue restores spermatogenesis and sperm quality in obese mice

To investigate whether Pue ameliorates spermatogenic dysfunction in obese mice, we examined testicular parameters associated with spermatogenesis. Compared with the Ctrl group, the HFD group exhibited a significant reduction in the testis index (testis/body weight ratio) (Fig. 3A-B), and the epididymis index was also markedly decreased (P < 0.05, Fig. 3C). Following Pue supplementation, testicular size and weight significantly increased and approached normal levels. H&E staining of the testes and epididymis revealed that in the Ctrl group, the tissue structure was normal, with intact seminiferous tubules. Germ cells at different developmental stages were uniformly distributed and tightly packed, with numerous mature sperm visible within the tubular lumen. In contrast, the HFD group exhibited histological abnormalities characterized by morphological distortion of the seminiferous tubules. Germ cells were disorganized and loosely arranged, with expanded intercellular spaces and deformed cell membranes accompanied by vacuolation and atrophy. Germ cells detached and underwent necrosis, leading to a reduced number of germ cells within the tubules and a significant decrease in mature sperm. Pue supplementation increased both the spermatogenic cell number and sperm count, alleviating HFD-induced testicular damage (Fig. 3D). Moreover, H&E staining of the epididymis revealed narrowed luminal spaces and reduced sperm counts within the tubules of HFD-fed mice. However, after Pue or Ot supplementation, the epididymal tubules appeared dilated, and more sperm were observed within the tubules (Fig. 3E).

To further evaluate the effects of Pue on sperm quality in obese mice, we subsequently stained sperm samples from the epididymides of obese mice. The results revealed that the sperm density was significantly lower in the HFD group than in the control group, which was accompanied by abnormal sperm tail morphology (Fig. 3F–G), whereas the sperm morphology in the Pue group remained intact. Through quantitative analysis of sperm, we found that compared with a HFD, Pue supplementation increased the total sperm count and reduced the abnormal sperm rate while increasing the forward motility rate (P < 0.05; Fig. 3H–J). Additionally, Pue supplementation significantly elevated serum testosterone levels in obese mice (Fig. 3K).

Pue significantly increases the expression of testosterone synthase in obese mice

Transcriptomic analysis was employed to explore the potential mechanism of HFD-induced male reproductive impairment and the restorative effect of Pue. Compared with the Ctrl group, 676 upregulated and 402 downregulated DEGs were identified in obese mice, and 679 upregulated and 702 downregulated DEGs were identified between the Pue and obese groups (Fig. 4A). In total, 109 genes were differentially expressed across the three groups, among which 47 DEGs showed expression trends in obese mice that were reversed by Pue treatment towards Ctrl levels (Fig. 4B). We further performed functional enrichment analysis on the basis of the KEGG and GO databases (Fig. 4C–D). Most notably, the DEGs were significantly enriched in metabolic pathways such as energy metabolism, the insulin metabolism pathway and steroid hormone biosynthesis. In addition, the expression of 12 DEGs in the above pathways was significantly restored upon Pue administration. TCluster analysis of these DEGs revealed that proteins associated with the testosterone synthesis pathway (StAR, CYP11A1, and CYP17A1) were downregulated in response to a HFD, an effect that was alleviated by Pue supplementation (Fig. 4E). The expression levels of key testosterone synthesis-associated genes/proteins (StAR, CYP11A1, CYP17A1, and 3β-HSD), which are enriched in lipid and steroid hormone synthesis pathways according to GO/KEGG analyses, were detected in mouse testicular samples by qRT‒PCR, WB and IHC (P < 0.05; Fig. 4F–G) and were consistent with the testicular transcriptome data, indicating that Pue enhances testosterone levels possibly by rescuing the impaired expression of these key testosterone synthesis-related enzymes in obese mice. Next, we analysed the associations between serum testosterone levels and indicators of obesity diagnosis, male reproductive function, and the expression levels of enzymes involved in testosterone synthesis. The results revealed significant positive correlations between serum testosterone levels and male reproductive function indicators (testosterone level, sperm count, and sperm motility), testosterone synthesis-related enzymes, and high-density lipoprotein levels. Conversely, serum testosterone levels were negatively correlated with obesity diagnostic indicators (body weight, fat mass, and Lee’s index), the sperm abnormality rate, hepatic metabolic markers (AST and ALT), and lipid metabolism hormone levels (TC, TG, and LDL) (Fig. 4H).

On the basis of the results of the cell viability analysis, 0.4 mM PA and 1 nM Pue were selected as the optimal drug concentrations for the subsequent experiments (Fig. 5A). Transcriptome sequencing revealed a total of 860 differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The Venn diagram and heatmap of all the DEGs are shown in Fig. 5B and C, respectively. Notably, KEGG enrichment analysis of all the DEGs (Fig. 5D) and clustering analysis of the DEGs involved in the steroid hormone biosynthesis pathway revealed that both groups were enriched in the steroid hormone biosynthesis pathway, with significant upregulation of genes related to testosterone synthesis (Fig. 5E–F). Subsequent validation via Western blotting and qPCR confirmed that Pue supplementation upregulated the mRNA and protein expression levels of StAR, CYP11A1, 3β-HSD and CYP17A1 in TM3 cells (P < 0.05; Fig. 5G–I).

GRP78 is identified as a target of Pue-P by ABPP

To identify the molecular targets of Pue in PA-treated TM3 cells, we synthesized a clickable alkyne-tagged probe, Pue-P, for ABPP analysis (Fig. 6A). Because probe validity depends on preserved biological activity, we verified that Pue-P exerted effects on TM3 cell viability comparable to Pue (Fig. 6B). We utilized Pue-P to identify cellular targets of Pue through in situ ABPP experiments (Fig. 6C). Finally, we performed in situ labelling of Pue-P in TM3 cells, and the results indicated that Pue-P demonstrated optimal labelling efficiency at a concentration of 200 µM (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, coincubation with excess Pue competed with Pue-P for in situ labelling in TM3 cells (Fig. 6E). The Pue-P probe was coincubated with PA-induced TM3 cells, and 59 specific protein targets were identified through mass spectrometry screening. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that these proteins were enriched primarily in pathways such as metabolic and endocrine regulation, protein synthesis and processing (Fig. 6F). PPI network analysis was subsequently performed on these protein targets (Fig. 6G).

To identify proteins directly regulated by Pue in energy metabolism and to further focus on the target proteins, we integrated network pharmacology targets and ABPP targets. Analysis revealed EGFR and HSPA5 (GRP78) as common target proteins, with GRP78 ranking in the top five among the 59 high-confidence potential targets identified by mass spectrometry (Fig. 6H, Supplementary Table 4). Molecular docking using AutoDock Tools confirmed that Pue binds more stably to GRP78, with a binding energy of − 8.9 kcal/mol (compared with − 8.1 kcal/mol for EGFR) (Fig. 6I, Supplementary Tables 5–6). Finally, molecular docking results indicated that Pue forms hydrogen bond interactions with residues in all three active pockets of the GRP78 protein, including HIS-252, ARG-97, and TYR-65 (Fig. 6I). On the basis of these findings, we selected GRP78 as our primary potential target for subsequent validation.

To further confirm that GRP78 is a binding target of Pue, we stained Pue-P and GRP78 with fluorescent dyes. We incubated TM3 cells treated with PA with Pue-P, and the proteins subsequently labelled by Pue-P were click-conjugated to the fluorescent dye TAMRA-azide. We incubated TM3 cells treated with PA with Pue-P, and the proteins subsequently labelled by Pue-P were click-conjugated to the fluorescent dye TAMRA-azide, revealing the colocalization of GRP78 with Pue-P to support their interaction and binding (Fig. 6J). A pulldown experiment revealed significant enrichment of GRP78 compared with that in cells supplemented with DMSO or Pue alone, confirming effective Pue-GRP78 binding, and preincubation with Pue reduced GRP78 precipitation, further demonstrating competitive binding properties (Fig. 6K). CETSA revealed that compared with the DMSO treatment, the addition of Pue significantly increased the thermal stability of GRP78 (Tm = 3.87 °C), suggesting the presence of a binding interaction (Fig. 6L–N). Finally, to investigate the specific amino acid residues of GRP78 that bind Pue, we mutated the two amino acid residues (HIS-252 and TYR-65) at the TYR-65 site that strongly interact with Pue and transfected them into TM3 cells. The results revealed that GRP78 expression was nearly absent after mutation of the TYR-65 site (Fig. 6O).

Pue binds to GRP78 and upregulates StAR expression

StAR is a known client protein of GRP78. Immunofluorescence and Co-IP experiments revealed an interaction between GRP78 and StAR (Fig. 7A and B). Moreover, in the PA-induced lipid accumulation TM3 cell model, Pue significantly increased the mRNA, protein expression and fluorescence levels of both GRP78 and StAR (P < 0.05; Fig. 7C–E, Supplementary Fig. 1). Given that molecular chaperones cannot directly regulate testosterone synthesis, we hypothesize that Pue indirectly modulates StAR stability and activity by interacting with the chaperone GRP78. To validate the role of GRP78 as a key target for Pue-mediated regulation of testosterone biosynthesis, we established two TM3 cell models: one in which YUM70 was used to inhibit the ATPase activity of GRP78 and the other in which GRP78 was overexpressed via plasmid transfection. The results revealed that in YUM70-inhibited cells, the number of intracellular disulfide bonds in unfolded proteins markedly decreased (P < 0.05; Fig. 7F), and this change was accompanied by decreased StAR expression and testosterone secretion, which were reversed by Pue supplementation (P < 0.05; Fig. 7G–H). Conversely, GRP78 overexpression significantly increased the intracellular protein disulfide content (P < 0.05; Fig. 7I) and markedly increased StAR expression and testosterone secretion levels (P < 0.05; Fig. 7J–K).

Discussion

Obesity is closely associated with reduced testosterone levels, with approximately 52% of obese men exhibiting hypogonadism, and a positive correlation exists between BMI and the severity of testosterone deficiency [22]. Research suggests that both total and free testosterone levels are significantly lower in obese men than in those with normal weight [23]. Obesity-related impairment in testosterone synthesis underscores the importance of understanding its biological and pathological mechanisms to identify potential therapeutic targets. Pue has demonstrated potential in mitigating chronic metabolic disorders [24].

Although there are no clinical studies on Pue and its effects on testosterone synthesis dysfunction, research on Pue has shown positive effects on plasma lipid profiles and a substantial reduction in oxidative stress and inflammatory biomarkers [25]. Through comprehensive retrieval and screening across multiple databases, we revealed that Pue and obesity-related targets are not only enriched in lipid metabolism-related pathways but also significantly enriched in steroid hormone biosynthesis pathways, suggesting their potential involvement in regulating androgen synthesis. These findings are biologically meaningful, as testosterone synthesis in Leydig cells depends critically on this pathway, involving key enzymes such as StAR, CYP11A1, 3β-HSD and CYP17A1 [26]. In obesity, excessive lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction collectively impair this biosynthetic cascade [26]. For example, oxidized LDL and saturated fatty acids disrupt mitochondrial integrity and downregulate StAR expression, thereby limiting cholesterol transport into mitochondria, which is the rate-limiting step of steroidogenesis [27]. Consequently, a reduction in StAR-mediated cholesterol delivery leads to decreased substrate availability for CYP11A1, resulting in attenuated testosterone output [28]. Our enrichment results suggest that Pue may protect against Leydig cell steroidogenesis by restoring the expression of steroidogenic enzymes.

To validate the predicted targets and pathways of Pue in the treatment of obesity-associated testosterone synthesis dysfunction, we established a high-fat-diet-induced obesity mouse model and investigated the effects of Pue on glucolipid metabolism and spermatogenic function. Our results demonstrate that Pue supplementation significantly ameliorated high-fat-diet-induced metabolic dysfunction. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies reporting the metabolic protective effects of Pue in obesity [29]. Importantly, Pue supplementation restored testicular architecture and spermatogenic epithelium integrity, alleviated seminiferous tubule atrophy, reinstated the orderly arrangement of germ cell layers, and expanded the epididymal lumen, suggesting a marked recovery of spermatogenic capacity. Such protective effects may be mechanistically linked to the upregulation of key steroidogenic enzymes (StAR, CYP11A1, 3β-HSD and CYP17A1), thereby enhancing testosterone biosynthesis and re-establishing the androgenic microenvironment required for germ cell proliferation and differentiation [30]. Adequate testosterone levels are indispensable for Sertoli cell function, maintenance of the spermatogenic epithelium, and completion of spermiogenesis, whereas androgen deprivation results in seminiferous epithelium regression and impaired sperm production [30]. In addition, studies have indicated that compared with a high-fat diet, supplementation with Pue not only increased the sperm density within the epididymis and reduced the proportion of abnormal sperm but also elevated serum testosterone levels. Furthermore, sperm quality improved in tandem with increasing serum testosterone levels, suggesting that the restoration of spermatogenic function is closely linked to the recovery of androgen levels. Testosterone is a well-established key regulator of germ cell proliferation, meiosis, and sperm release; insufficient androgen levels lead to reduced spermatogenesis, increased abnormal sperm morphology, and impaired fertility. Therefore, the improvement in epididymal sperm quality following Pue administration reflects both direct protection of the spermatogenic epithelium and restoration of testosterone-dependent signalling pathways that regulate germ cell maturation. By improving Leydig cell function and steroidogenic activity, Pue may establish a favourable testicular endocrine environment, promoting the production of morphologically normal and viable sperm [31]. This translates histological and molecular-level recovery into functional reproductive outcomes [32]. This study further revealed that genes that are differentially regulated by Pue supplementation under obese conditions are significantly enriched in the steroid hormone biosynthesis pathway. The expression of genes associated with testosterone synthesis within this pathway were significantly upregulated, which localized to Leydig cells [33]. The biosynthesis of testosterone in testicular cells is tightly regulated by a series of key steroidogenic enzymes. Among these, StAR mediates the rate-limiting transport of cholesterol from the outer to the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it is subsequently converted into testosterone under the catalysis of CYP11A1, 3β-HSD and CYP17A1 [34]. Through transcriptomic sequencing analysis and validation experiments, this study further confirmed that Pue supplementation significantly upregulated the expression levels of StAR, CYP11A1, 3β-HSD, and CYP17A1. Similar results were observed with Ot supplementation, which is consistent with previous literature reports [35]. These findings indicate that Pue promotes testosterone biosynthesis by enhancing the steroidogenesis mechanism in Leydig cells.

To elucidate the molecular targets of Pue in the treatment of obesity-related testosterone synthesis dysfunction, we used network pharmacology and ABPP technology to screen for Pue-binding proteins in TM3 cells. Pull-down, CETSA and molecular docking experiments confirmed the binding between Pue and GRP78. GRP78, which is widely recognized for its persistent overexpression in tumour cells, promotes cell migration, tissue invasion, and metastasis through interactions with proteins such as MMP2 and LRP-1 [36]. In nontumour cells, however, GRP78 typically does not directly execute its physiological functions but acts as a molecular chaperone by regulating client proteins, including structured and intrinsically disordered proteins. GRP78 undergoes ATP-driven conformational changes and collaborates with cochaperones such as Cdc37, Hsp70, and p23 to achieve allosteric regulation of client protein recognition, loading, folding, and stability [37]. Previous studies have indicated that GRP78 serves as both a biomarker of endoplasmic reticulum stress and a key regulator of energy expenditure [38, 39]. Studies report that GRP78 upregulation during the early stages of ER stress buffers protein misfolding and restores protein homeostasis [40]. Gómez et al. reported that chronic ER stress induced by repeated low doses leads to transcriptional feedback inhibition of specific UPR target genes, including Hspa5, with this inhibitory effect also observed in the livers of obese mice [41]. Similarly, in db/db (diabetic obese) mice and HepG2 cells in vitro, oleic acid treatment reduced GRP78 expression and inhibited insulin-AKT pathway activation [42]. Consistent with these findings, we observed that palmitic acid treatment reduced GRP78 expression in TM3 cells, whereas Pue supplementation upregulated GRP78 expression. These findings suggest that the mechanism through which Pue affects obesity-associated testosterone synthesis dysfunction may involve GRP78 regulation.

Previous studies have reported that StAR is a client protein that is regulated by GRP78 [43]. Specifically, GRP78 functions as a molecular chaperone by directly interacting with mature membrane-localized receptors, thereby maintaining the conformational stability of StAR and promoting its folding from a precursor protein into a mature protein with a cholesterol transport function, thus sustaining testosterone synthesis. Knockdown of GRP78 results in dramatic decreases in StAR expression and activity [43]. In this study, immunofluorescence staining revealed spatial colocalization of StAR and GRP78. Furthermore, Co-IP experiments confirmed their interaction. Following GRP78 inhibition, the intracellular disulfide bond content was significantly reduced, and StAR expression was downregulated, which is consistent with previous reports [44]. However, supplementation with Pue ameliorated these effects. Notably, testosterone levels in cell supernatants also returned to normal, indicating that the therapeutic effect of Pue on obesity-related testosterone synthesis dysfunction primarily depends on its binding to and functional upregulation of GRP78. Conversely, GRP78 overexpression enhanced the therapeutic effects of Pue, further confirming that GRP78 is a key mediator of its therapeutic effect.

In this study, the therapeutic mechanism of Pue was investigated using only high-fat diet-induced obese mice and cellular models. Whether similar therapeutic effects and their underlying mechanisms exist for other physical factors (thermal stimulation and radiation effects), chemical methods (chemotherapy drugs), or surgical methods (in male infertility models induced by experimental cryptorchidism or surgically induced varicocele) requires further exploration in future research. Additionally, we identified potential targets by intersecting target proteins identified via ABPP mass spectrometry with therapeutic targets from network pharmacology. EGFR, another target of this interaction, has not yet been thoroughly explored. Research has indicated that excessive EGFR activation in the granulosa cells of PCOS patients leads to GJA1 phosphorylation, which ultimately prevents oocytes from receiving sufficient maturation signals, resulting in impaired oocyte maturation [45]. Furthermore, in sperm capacitation, EGFR is a key regulator of both capacitation and the acrosome reaction [46]. These findings suggest that EGFR may also be a potential target through which Pue ameliorates obesity-induced male spermatogenic dysfunction, although definitive binding experiments were not performed in this study. Furthermore, whether the other high-confidence targets identified by ABPP represent potential targets for Pue merits exploration in future research.

Overall, these findings underscore the multifaceted role of Pue in modulating disease processes and support its potential as a therapeutic candidate for the prevention and treatment of obesity-related testosterone dysfunction. Although our results elucidate important pathways influenced by Pue, additional studies are necessary to clarify the exact molecular mechanisms underlying its effects on testosterone biosynthesis. Future studies should employ mechanistic approaches, such as analysing structural changes in StAR before and after treatment, to validate the functional significance of the observed disulfide bond alterations. Furthermore, incorporating female models in subsequent research is crucial for assessing whether Pue exhibits similar or distinct mechanisms of action.

Conclusion

In summary, our results indicate that supplementation with Pue can effectively counteract obesity and ameliorate testosterone synthesis dysfunction in mice. Through this intervention, we observed the restoration of testicular and epididymal tissue structure, supporting the ability of the Leydig cells of the testes to perform normal testosterone synthesis and promote spermatogenesis. Through transcriptomics analysis, we have provided insights into the potential molecular mechanisms through which Pue exerts its beneficial effects on obesity-related testosterone dysfunction. Further ABPP analysis revealed that Pue promotes testosterone synthesis by binding to and upregulating the activity of molecular chaperones. Collectively, these research outcomes establish a foundational understanding of how Pueraria might serve as a functional food or therapeutic agent capable of alleviating obesity-related testosterone synthesis dysfunction. These results warrant further investigation to translate these benefits into clinical applications.
