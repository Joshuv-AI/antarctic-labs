Eisenmenger Syndrome Complicated by Hemorrhagic Hereditary Telangiectasia
Authors: Frazer A Tessema, Madhusudan Ganigara, Orly Leiva, Kenneth S Cohen, Gene Kim, Luis Landeras, Stephen G Pophal, Michael G Earing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Eisenmenger syndrome is an advanced stage of an unrepaired congenital heart defect with a large shunt. Early in life, it is a left-to-right shunt, but over time, there is development of severe irreversible pulmonary hypertension, resulting in shunt reversal and multiple associated complications, particularly chronic cyanosis and complex hematologic consequences with associated risk for bleeding and thrombosis. Hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT) is a syndrome characterized by multiple arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) that lack intervening capillaries, resulting in direct connections between the arteries and veins. These malformations lead to multiple complications including cyanosis, pulmonary hypertension, and risk for stroke and hemorrhage.

We present the case of an adult woman with Eisenmenger syndrome and concurrent genetically confirmed HHT who presented with acute gastrointestinal bleed, hypotension, and worsening cyanosis.

History of Presentation

This was a 43-year-old woman with Eisenmenger syndrome secondary to unrepaired large ventricular septal defect (VSD). As a child and young adult, the patient experienced cyanosis and exercise intolerance but was able to accomplish most activities of daily living. Given her severe pulmonary hypertension, she was started on sildenafil in her 30s, which improved her overall functional capacity. During adulthood, she was noted to have superficial telangiectasias, persistent thrombocytopenia, and recurrent epistaxis. This prompted concern for hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT), which was later confirmed with genetic testing at age 37 that revealed a pathogenic variant in the ACVRL1 gene, specifically variant c.1231C>T (p.Arg411Trp). Further work-up after diagnosis revealed AVMs in the lungs, brain, liver, and gastrointestinal tract. The patient had known complications of HHT including hemoptysis, hematemesis, and epistaxis. She also had chronic iron deficiency anemia requiring multiple transfusions. Six years before presentation, she experienced a cerebrovascular accident (CVA) involving the right occipital lobe secondary to a septic embolic in the setting of possible endocarditis. All prior medical care was obtained in Ecuador.

The patient presented to our emergency department with reports of recurrent hematemesis and melena. She had significant oxygen desaturation—to 75% on room air by pulse oximetry and reported to be 84% at baseline. She was hypotensive, with a blood pressure by cuff of 86/48 mm Hg. She was not tachypneic or afebrile, and appeared comfortable at rest. Heart rate was borderline-elevated at 90 to 100 beats/min. On examination, she had clubbing of fingernails and toenails, multiple superficial telangiectasias, and a loud P2. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated marked anemia (hemoglobin: 6.4 g/dL) and thrombocytopenia (platelet count: 125,000 μL).

Differential Diagnosis

Initial differential included acute hemorrhage secondary to known pulmonary or gastrointestinal AVMs related to HHT leading to both hypotension and worsening right-to-left shunting at the level of her VSD and an associated decrease in oxygen-carrying capacity with a drop in hemoglobin. Given her history of Eisenmenger syndrome and a previous CVA related to septic emboli, also in the differential was possible pulmonary hemorrhage, gastrointestinal bleed, and intracranial hemorrhage. Less likely was potential sepsis and intracerebral abscess.

Investigations

The patient was placed on oxygen via nasal canula with minimal to no improvement in her oxygen saturation, reflecting her known right-to-left intracardiac shunt. Two blood cultures were negative. Initial white blood cell count (4,000 μL) was normal. Computed tomography angiography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis demonstrated multiple areas of AVMs in both lungs (Figures 1 and 2). The largest AVM noted was in the left lower lobe with a feeding vessel measuring 2.5 to 3 mm in diameter. The stomach demonstrated similar findings: innumerable hypervascular foci throughout, consistent with AVMs (Figure 3). Several vascular malformations were seen in the large bowel and cecum. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a large perimembranous VSD with low-velocity right-to-left shunting, consistent with Eisenmenger syndrome (Figure 4). Left and right ventricular systolic function were normal. Both main and proximal pulmonary arteries were severely dilated. Chest x-ray demonstrated cardiomegaly, enlarged pulmonary arteries, and nodules consistent with AVMs.

Management

The patient was transfused with packed red blood cells (RBCs). She then underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy, demonstrating scattered nonbleeding angioectasias in her oropharynx and esophagus. There were multiple medium-to-large angioectasias found throughout the gastric fundus and body that bled with contact and were actively oozing when visualized. Coagulation of approximately 10 to 15 of the largest lesions using argon plasma was then successfully performed, and pantoprazole was initiated.

Hematology recommended administering bevacizumab—a VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) inhibitor, which has previously reduced episodes of epistaxis, telangiectasias, and iron-deficiency anemia in HHT.1,2 There is some evidence that bevacizumab may modulate TGF (transforming growth factor)-β activity, and previous data in patients with comorbid HHT and pulmonary hypertension has demonstrated that 86% of patients had a defect in TGF-β signaling.3,4 Bevacizumab dosing was set at 1 mg/kg, compared to the usual 5 mg/kg, given the patient's delicate hemodynamics.

Right heart catheterization confirmed the presence of Eisenmenger syndrome with associated severe pulmonary hypertension and right-to-left shunting (see Table 1). The mean pulmonary artery pressure was 61 mm Hg with pulmonary resistance of 7.5 Wood units (10.28 indexed units). There was resting right-to-left shunting with a pulmonary-to-systemic blood flow of 0.85. Selective angiograms in both pulmonary arteries confirmed bilateral pulmonary AVMs. Given persistent hypotension, midodrine was started to help stabilize systemic vascular resistance, which later allowed reinitiation of the pulmonary vasodilator sildenafil and eventually, macitentan as well. In an effort to optimize oxygen carrying capacity, packed RBCs and iron infusions were given—maintaining a hemoglobin of 17 g/dL. Given the patient's history of CVA, septic emboli, and right-to-left shunting, she remained at risk for thromboembolism; nonetheless, the risk of anticoagulation in the setting of known AVMs and recent gastrointestinal bleed were felt to outweigh the benefit. Intravenous filters were placed to avoid risk for air emboli. Discussion did take place about coil embolization of her pulmonary AVMs, but given her persistent right-to-left shunting at her VSD level, benefit of this procedure alone to lower her risk for stroke or improve her oxygen saturation was felt to be low.

Ultimately after 13 days, the patient was discharged home with stable blood pressure 100/54 mm Hg and oxygen saturation 80% on room air. Bevacizumab dosing was increased to 5 mg/kg outpatient.

Outcome and Follow-Up

One year after initial presentation, the patient remains on bevacizumab, macitentan, sildenafil, midodrine, pantoprazole, and iron without recurrence of gastrointestinal bleeding. With initiation of pulmonary vasodilator therapy, her functional capacity on 6-minute walk (400 m) and oxygen saturation on room air (86%) have improved.

Discussion

Patients with Eisenmenger syndrome are at risk for both cyanosis and bleeding, as are patients with HHT—albeit by different pathophysiological mechanisms. These factors lead to complex, overlapping scenarios that can worsen both conditions.

Patients with Eisenmenger syndrome are at increased risk for bleeding because of both thrombocytopenia and dysfunctional platelets.5 In addition, they have low circulating levels of vitamin K–dependent clotting factors, factor V, and von Willebrand factor.5 Bleeding in this population can be life threatening and involve severe hemoptysis. At same time, patients with Eisenmenger syndrome are at risk for development of secondary erythrocytosis because of elevated erythropoietin production, a physiologic response of chronic tissue hypoxemia. This results in increasing circulating RBC and hemoglobin concentration, with an overall effect of increased oxygen-carrying capacity and delivery to tissues.

HHT patients are also at risk for both cyanosis and bleeding. They can develop AVMs in the lungs, liver, gastrointestinal tract, and brain. Gastrointestinal AVMs often cause significant bleeding with associated anemia, requiring iron replacement therapy and intervention.6 Pulmonary AVMs create high-flow, right-to-left shunts with a wide variety of clinical manifestations including hypoxia, pulmonary hypertension, stroke, and brain abscess. Less commonly, individuals with pulmonary AVMs present with hemoptysis or intrapulmonary hemorrhage.6 Pulmonary hypertension is another pulmonary vascular manifestation of HHT, although much less common than pulmonary AVMs.7 Pulmonary hypertension can result from systemic arteriovenous shunting in the liver leading to increased cardiac output, and can be clinically and histologically indistinguishable from idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension.7 Increased cardiac output from AVMs was also likely responsible for the pulmonary resistance of 7.5 Wood units—a value lower than would be typically expected for a patient with only Eisenmenger syndrome.

Conclusions

Our multidisciplinary team needed to weigh the interplay of complex, overlapping factors related to our patient's care. In setting of her Eisenmenger syndrome secondary to her unrepaired VSD, she had marked right-to-left shunting with associated chronic cyanosis. In turn, she had developed secondary erythrocytosis as a compensatory measure to improve oxygen delivery to her tissues. Unfortunately, in the setting of her HHT and gastrointestinal AVMs, she developed a significant gastrointestinal bleeding, which resulted in marked lowering of her oxygen delivery to her tissues, as demonstrated by her drop in oxygen saturation. This gastrointestinal bleeding was exacerbated by bleeding diathesis associated with Eisenmenger syndrome. Gastrointestinal bleeding and diathesis were exacerbated by thrombocytopenia, dysfunctional platelets, as well as low circulating levels of vitamin K–dependent clotting factors, factor V, and von Willebrand factor.5

In this setting, our teams targeted a higher than usual hemoglobin target of >17, which was key to improving oxygen delivery to her tissues and is standard treatment for Eisenmenger syndrome. The higher goal gave valuable time to allow the effects of other medications to take place, specifically bevacizumab and additional pulmonary vasodilators. The patient's challenging clinical care adhered to current best practice guidelines for management of HHT and Eisenmenger syndrome.8,9
