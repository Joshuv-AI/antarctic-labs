1 Abstract
Radiation propagates throughout the Universe and is often the only information we have from
distant astronomical objects. Additionally, radiation plays a key role in systems ranging from
the interior of stars to the establishment of shocks at the edges of forming galaxies to neutron
star mergers. Here radiative processes, absorption, emission, and scattering, are fundamental to
transport of momentum and energy, as well as, the overall evolution of these and other astrophysical
systems. In some cases, radiation-dominant systems are also sufficiently dense and ionized, such
that, the ions are strongly coupled (ie electrostatic energy >> thermal energy or Γ >> 1), which
will affect radiative processes and the transport of radiation. During this project, we developed
a computational ray-tracing algorithm to study radiation transport through a simulated neutron
star capsule implosion under conditions relevant to Omega-60 laser experiments.

2 Summary of Project Activities
This simulation work consisted of the development and application of a computational ray-tracing
algorithm to study radiation transport through a simulated neutron star capsule implosion under
conditions relevant to Omega-60 laser experiments. The capsule was modeled in two dimensions as
a series of concentric rings composed of plastic (CH), nickel (Ni), and helium (He), each character-
ized by distinct absorption and emission properties. The objective was to calculate the radiation
intensity along individual rays passing through the capsule and to aggregate these results into a
total intensity observable at a detector located some distance away.
 The methodology combined analytical derivations with numerical implementation. First, we de-
rived equations for calculating ray–capsule intersections, path lengths through each material layer,
and the corresponding attenuation and emission contributions to intensity. These expressions were
then integrated into a Python algorithm that systematically computed the evolution of intensity
as a ray traversed the capsule. The algorithm was extended to handle multiple rays, enabling the
calculation of total detector intensity.
 Validation was carried out by comparing numerical results from the code with analytical so-
lutions for a representative test case involving a ray at a specified angle. The results matched
exactly, confirming the correctness and robustness of the approach. The framework successfully
reproduced the path lengths, layer-by-layer intensity contributions, and total detector intensity,
and also generated visualizations of both ray trajectories and intensity profiles.
 The study demonstrated that this algorithm provided a reliable and flexible tool for modeling
radiation transport in implosion experiments. It established a foundation for future extensions,
including the incorporation of Doppler shifting effects, the transition to full 3D simulations, and
the use of tabulated absorption and emissivity data for more realistic material modeling. Through
these developments, the work laid the groundwork for connecting controlled laboratory-scale laser
implosions with astrophysical phenomena and advancing the predictive capabilities of radiation
transport modeling.
