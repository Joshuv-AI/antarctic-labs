Abstract
. A framework for risk-averse optimization problems is introduced that is resilient to ambiguities in the true form of the underlying probability distribution. The focus is on problems with partial differential equations (PDEs) as constraints, although the formulation is more broadly applicable. The framework is based on combining risk measures with problem relaxation techniques, and it builds off of previous advances for risk-neutral problems. This work advances the existing theory with strengthened Γ \Gamma -convergence results, novel existence results and first-order optimality criteria. In particular, the theoretical approach naturally accommodates infinite-dimensional probability spaces; no finite-dimensional noise assumption is needed. The framework blends aspects of both distributionally robust optimization (DRO) and distributionally optimistic optimization (DOO) approaches. The DRO aspect facilitates strong out-of-sample performance, while the DOO aspect takes care of adversarial and outlier data, as illustrated with numerical examples.



1.
Introduction

In a wide variety of applications in financial planning, operations management,
engineering design, and statistical modeling, it is critical to account for
uncertainty in a conservative, risk-averse manner.
Risk measures are a well-established tool in stochastic optimization to arrive
at decisions that minimize exposure to exceptionally poor outcomes
[
55
]
.
Given a random variable
\xi
defined on a probability space
(\Xi,{\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{P})
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, and a
coherent risk measure
[
56
, Section 6.3]

{\mathcal{R}}:L^{p}(\Xi,{\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{P})\to\mathbf{R}
, a typical risk-averse stochastic program can
be written in the form

(1.1)

\min_{z\in{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}}\mathcal{R}[f(\xi,z)].

Here the deterministic control variable
z
belongs to an admissible
subset
{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}
of some Banach space
Z
, and
f:\Xi\times Z\to\mathbf{R}

is integrable for each
z\in Z
.

In practice, the precise form of the uncertainty itself
is often unsettled; i.e. the sample space
\Xi
and probability measure
\mathbb{P}
are
themselves uncertain.
The assumed probability distribution might
not accurately reflect the true variability, or extreme outcomes
may appear outside the nominal support of the distribution. These inaccuracies could
arise from measurement error or adversarial corruption, for example.
Ignoring these possibilities can lead to optimization solutions
that perform poorly under real-world conditions, particularly
in the risk-averse setting
[
35
]
.

A common approach to account for such meta-uncertainty is
to consider distributionally robust optimization (DRO) formulations,
where one minimizes an expected value over a collection
\{\mathbb{P}_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}

of plausible measures, which is termed the ambiguity set.
In risk-averse approaches, worst-case scenarios are
considered, which yields a minimax problem

(1.2)

\min_{z\in{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}}\sup_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}\int_{\Xi}f(\xi,z)d\mathbb{P}_{i}.

The literature on DRO is quite vast; we refer to the reviews

[
11
,
45
,
46
,
39
]

and references therein.

It is well known that minimizing a coherent measure of risk
{\mathcal{R}}
is itself
an example of a distributionally robust optimization problem.
For example, if one takes
{\mathcal{R}}=\operatorname{CVaR}_{\beta}
, i.e. the Conditional Value-at-Risk
at level
\beta\in(0,1)

[
50
,
49
]
, then (
1.1
) becomes

(1.3)

\min_{z\in{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}}\operatorname{CVaR}_{\beta}(f(\xi,z))=\min_{z\in{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}}\inf_{\gamma\in\mathbf{R}}\Big(\gamma+\frac{1}{1-\beta}\int_{\Xi}(f(\xi,z)-\gamma)_{+}\,d\mathbb{P}\Big).

By the Fenchel-Moreau theorem
[
8
, Theorem 9.3.5]
, (
1.3
) is equivalent to

(1.4)

\min_{z\in{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{\beta}}\int_{\Xi}\theta(\xi)f(\xi,z)\,d\mathbb{P},

where the so-called risk envelope

\Theta_{\beta}=\Big\{\vartheta\in L^{\infty}(\Xi,{\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{P})\,\Big|\,\int_{\Xi}\vartheta(\xi)\,d\mathbb{P}=1,\,0\leq\vartheta\leq(1-\beta)^{-1}\,\mathbb{P}\text{ a.s.}\Big\}

plays the role of the ambiguity set.
For more general coherent risk measures, an analogous result can be found in

[
56
, Theorem 6.7]
.

In the context of statistical estimation, DRO is known to exhibit strong out-of-sample performance; it allows one to hedge against
common issues such as overfitting and “distributional shifts”—deviations
between the distributions observed in the model training and
model deployment environments.
We refer to the comprehensive discussion in
[
14
]

for more details.
On the other hand, DRO can be ill-suited for problems with contaminated or outlier data.
At scenarios
\xi
that cause the function

f(\xi,z)
to be exceptionally large (or possibly unbounded) at certain
control values
z
(termed “endogenous” outliers by Jiang and Xie

[
31
]
) the DRO approach
necessarily leads to higher objective function values, owing to the supremum
in (
1.2
).

An alternative approach for problems with data corruption is
distributionally optimistic optimization (DOO).
Generally speaking, DOO seeks best-case scenarios, for example, by replacing the supremum in (
1.2
) with an infimum.
Although the literature is not as vast as for DRO, optimistic approaches are also well-studied;
see
[
12
,
27
,
1
,
24
]
, for example. They are also closely connected to techniques from robust statistics
[
29
,
40
]
, such as using a trimmed loss
[
6
]
, as recently pointed out by
the authors in
[
31
]
and
[
15
]
. This connection
can help explain the success
of these approaches in the presence of outlier data.
The work in
[
3
]
was the first example of an optimistic approach to
stochastic optimization problems with
partial differential equations (PDEs) as constraints.

An interesting and relatively unexplored approach to stochastic optimization
is to combine DOO with DRO to simultaneously
improve both resilience to corrupted data and out-of-sample performance.
A recent example is from
[
31
]
, who proposed
combining a “favorable
\operatorname{CVaR}_{\beta}
” with a DRO approach based on measuring
ambiguity sets with the Wasserstein
\infty
-distance.
Similar approaches can be found in
[
44
,
42
,
58
,
15
]
.
Distributionally robust chance constrained programs
[
26
,
19
,
57
]

can also be classified in this way.

The current work presents a novel approach to combining robust and optimistic frameworks.
The approach is based on employing both risk measures and Rockafellian relaxation techniques.
Although the framework is more general, our focus is on
problems that are constrained by PDEs.
In this setting, the generic stochastic optimization problem (
1.1
)
is modified to include the composition of a
(random-valued) control-to-state map
s(\xi,z)
with a quantity-of-interest
map
g
, as well as an additional control penalty term.

In particular, this work uses the coherent risk measure
{\mathcal{R}}=\operatorname{CVaR}_{\beta}
(Eq. (
1.3
)),
which originated in financial mathematics and portfolio optimization
[
50
,
49
]
.
The authors in
[
34
]
were the first to use
{\mathcal{R}}=\operatorname{CVaR}_{\beta}
in the
context of PDE constrained optimization (PDECO). Analysis and optimization algorithms
can be found in
[
36
]
and
[
37
,
22
,
10
]
,
respectively. Risk measures for PDECO have also been used in conjunction with reduced order
models
[
61
,
28
]
and
tensor-train decompositions
[
4
]
. Application areas include
building and shape design
[
33
,
16
,
32
]
,
noise reduction
[
61
]
, and digital twins
[
2
]
.
These lists are far from exhaustive, however, and risk-averse PDECO is a continually evolving field.

Rockafellian relaxation is an optimistic approach to stochastic optimization
based on ideas originating in
[
47
,
48
]
. Rather than replacing the
supremum in (
1.2
) with an infimum (or suitable reformulation thereof),
the approach seeks best-case scenarios by not only optimizing over the control
variable
z
, but also jointly with a choice of model perturbations
[
54
]
.
Similar ideas based on minimizing “bivariate functions” and “perturbation functions” can
also be found in
[
9
]
and
[
62
]
, respectively.
Rockafellians are intimately related to Lagrangian relaxations and dual problems, as discussed
in
[
53
, Chapter 5]
and
[
20
]
.
The work in
[
3
]
established theoretical foundations as well as practical algorithms for Rockafellian relaxation applied to risk-neutral
PDECO problems, building off of prior work for
finite dimensional
problems in
[
52
]
. The numerical examples
in
[
3
]
additionally showcased that
Rockafellian relaxation enables recovery of the optimal control to an
uncorrupted

optimization problem, even when solving with corrupted data.
See also recent work applying Rockafellian relaxation to handle erroneous training labels in deep learning
[
18
]
, as well as extensions to problems with arbitrary Borel distributions and discontinuous integrands (including chance-constrained programs)
[
59
]
.

The first of two main objectives of the present work is to introduce a number of
theoretical advances from the work in
[
3
]
.
We prove novel existence results for Rockafellians constrained by PDEs, and, under
appropriate regularity assumptions on the control-to-state map, we rigorously
derive first-order optimality conditions.
Importantly, our construction allows for infinite-dimension (in the Schauder sense) probability spaces.
In contrast to many presentations in the literature, no finite-noise
assumption is needed, cf.
[
35
]
.
We also present a unified theory for
\Gamma
-convergence of corrupted Rockafellians
to the original, uncorrupted one in the limit of vanishing data corruption. As is well-known,
this guarantees preservation of convergence of minimizers, demonstrating the method’s consistency.

The second main objective is to illustrate with numerical examples the practical utility
of Rockafellian relaxation for risk-averse PDECO problems with corrupted data. This builds
off of the numerical examples presented in
[
3
]
in the risk-neutral setting.
We observe that the method can recover the optimal control of a risk-averse problem
in the presence of endogenous outliers
[
31
]
caused by corrupted
data samples.
Additionally, we illustrate how the combined DOO and DRO approach can uncover “hidden” possibilities:
using the
\operatorname{CVaR}_{\beta}
risk measure and minimizing over the Rockafellian decision variable allow
for the recovery of controls that
would
be optimal, were the problem data different.
The principal utility of the combined approach is that it can equip decision makers with
insight into the sensitivity of optimal control strategies under
changing levels of
both
problem corruption and risk-aversion.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce
some notations and preliminary definitions. Section
3
studies the original uncorrupted problem together with natural modifications that prepare the ground for the introduction of corruption. Section
4
constitutes the main part of this work, in which we develop a systematic framework to analyze corrupted versions of the problems introduced previously. In particular, we introduce several constructions that allow us to handle arbitrary sample spaces, which may be infinite-dimensional and of infinite measure. Then, we prove the existence of minimizers, first-order optimality conditions, and the convergence of corrupted minimizers to uncorrupted minimizers. In Section
5
, we particularize the results given in Section
4
to some cases of interest. In particular, as mentioned earlier, we complete the analysis made in
[
3
]
for corruption to the continuous distribution and to the support of distributions. Moreover, we illustrate how focusing on particular instances enables us to relax assumptions and strengthen the obtained results.
Section
6
contains two numerical examples of Rockafellian relaxation in practice, and Section
7
finishes with some concluding remarks.

2.
Notation and preliminaries

Let
({\rm Z},\|\cdot\|_{{\rm Z}})
be a reflexive Banach space,
({\rm U},\|\cdot\|_{{\rm U}})
be an arbitrary Banach space, and
(\Xi,{\mathcal{A}},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space whose sample space is equipped with a norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\Xi}
. Let
{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}\subset{\rm Z}
be a closed convex subset of optimization variables. We consider functions
f_{0}:{\rm Z}\to\overline{\mathbf{R}}
,
g:{\rm U}\to\overline{\mathbf{R}}
, and
s:\Xi\times{\rm Z}\to{\rm U}
, which satisfy the following assumptions.

Assumption 2.1

(Properties of the solution map
s
)
.

(1)

s(\cdot,z):\Xi\to{\rm U}
is
{\mathcal{A}}
-measurable, for every
z\in{\rm Z}
.

(2)

If both
\xi_{\varepsilon}\to\xi
in
\Xi
and
z_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup z
in
{\rm Z}
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, then

s(\xi_{\varepsilon},z_{\varepsilon})\rightharpoonup s(\xi,z)\quad\text{ in }\penalty\ {\rm U}\,.

Assumption 2.2

(Properties of
f_{0}
and
g
)
.

(1)

f_{0}
is proper:
f_{0}(z)>-\infty\quad\forall\,z\in{\rm Z}
and
f_{0}(z)<+\infty
for some
z\in{\rm Z}
.

(2)

Both
f_{0}
and
g
are sequentially weakly lower semicontinuous; that is, respectively,

\displaystyle z_{\varepsilon}\overset{{\rm Z}}{\rightharpoonup}z\implies\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}f_{0}(z_{\varepsilon})\geq f_{0}(z)\,,

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}\overset{{\rm U}}{\rightharpoonup}u\implies\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}g(z_{\varepsilon})\geq g(z)\,.

Now, let us introduce some notation that will be used throughout the paper. Given an arbitrary space
X
and a subset
S\subset X
, we denote by
\imath_{S}
the indicator function of
S
, namely, the function
X\to\mathbf{R}
defined by
\imath_{S}(x)=0
for
x\in S
and
\imath_{S}(x)=+\infty
for
x\notin S
. Similarly, let
\chi_{S}
denote the characteristic function of
S
, that is, the function that takes the value
1
in
S
, and
0
otherwise. We also denote by
{\rm E}_{S,0}
the extension-by-zero operator from
S
to
X
. More precisely, given a function
f:S\to\overline{\mathbf{R}}
, its extension
{\rm E}_{S,0}(f):X\to\overline{\mathbf{R}}
coincides with
f
on
S
, and is identically zero on
X\setminus S
.

We also recall the definition of the positive part function
(\,\cdot\,)_{+}:\mathbf{R}\to\mathbf{R}
, given by

x_{+}\,:=\,\frac{|x|+x}{2}\,=\,\max\,\{0,x\}\qquad\forall\,x\in\mathbf{R}\,,

which enjoys the following useful lower semicontinuity property: for any sequence of real numbers
(x_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
such that
\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}x_{\varepsilon}\geq x
for some
x\in\mathbf{R}
, one has

(2.1)

\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\;(x_{\varepsilon})_{+}\geq x_{+}.

We now introduce the notions of convergence that we will consider throughout the paper. In the following,
X
and
Y
denote Banach spaces,
\phi:X\times Y\to\mathbf{R}
denotes a map, whereas
(\phi_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
is a sequence of maps from
X\times Y
to
\mathbf{R}
.

Definition 2.1

(Mosco convergence)
.

The sequence
(\phi_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
Mosco-converges to
\phi
if the following conditions hold:

(1)

For all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
there is a sequence
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
in
X\times Y
converging strongly to
(x,y)
such that

(2.2)

\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\,\phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\leq\phi(x,y)\,.

(2)

For all sequences
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup(x,y)
in
X\times Y
, there holds

(2.3)

\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\,\phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\geq\phi(x,y)\,.

These are called limit superior and limit inferior conditions, respectively.

We also introduce a slight modification of the definition of
\Gamma
-convergence, which we refer to as weak-strong
\Gamma
-convergence. This notion will be particularly useful in our context (cf. Theorem (
4.3
)).

Definition 2.2

(Weak-strong
\Gamma
-convergence)
.

The sequence
(\phi_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
weak-strong
\Gamma
-converges to
\phi
, denoted by
\phi_{\varepsilon}\overset{\Gamma}{\rightharpoondown}\phi
, if the following conditions hold:

(1)

For all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
, there exists a sequence
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
in
X\times Y
converging strongly to
(x,y)
such that

(2.4)

\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\,\phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\leq\phi(x,y)\,.

(2)

For all sequences
x_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup{}x\in X
and
y_{\varepsilon}\to y\in Y
, there holds

(2.5)

\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\,\phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\geq\phi(x,y)\,.

The main difference between this and the usual notion of
\,\Gamma
-convergence lies in the topology under which convergence is understood: here, convergence in
X
and
Y
is taken with respect to the weak and strong topologies, respectively, rather than the strong topology on the product space as a whole. This gives rise to a somewhat stronger notion of generalized convergence, which sits strictly between Mosco and Gamma convergence. As a consequence, and as expected, the two notions are generally not equivalent.

These notions of convergence are naturally suited to the optimization framework. Indeed, control sequences typically exhibit weak convergence. The following proposition provides a concrete illustration of these concepts.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(x_{\varepsilon}^{\star},y_{\varepsilon}^{\star})_{\varepsilon}
be a sequence in
X\times Y
with
x_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\in\operatorname{argmin}\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)
. Assume that the sequence
(\phi_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
converges to
\phi
in one of the notions defined above, and that
(x_{\varepsilon}^{\star},y_{\varepsilon}^{\star})_{\varepsilon}
converges to
(x^{\star},y^{\star})\in X\times Y
with respect to the corresponding topologies. Then,
x^{\star}\in\operatorname{argmin}\phi(x)
.

Proof.

Given
(x,y)\in X\times Y
, there holds
\phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon}^{\star},y_{\varepsilon}^{\star})\leq\phi_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
. Then, for any sequence
(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
satisfying the limit superior condition associated with
(x,y)
, we obtain

\phi(x^{\star},y^{\star})\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon}\,\phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon}^{\star},y_{\varepsilon}^{\star})\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon}\,\phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon}\,\phi_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon},y_{\varepsilon})\leq\phi(x,y)\,.

This completes the proof.
∎

To conclude this section, we recall the definition of a Rockafellian associated with an optimization problem (cf.
[
47
,
48
]
).

Definition 2.3

(Rockafellian)
.

For Banach spaces
X
and
Y
, a function
\varphi:X\to\overline{\mathbf{R}}
, and a generic optimization problem
\min_{x\in X}\varphi(x)
, a bivariate function
\Phi:X\times Y\to\overline{\mathbf{R}}
is called a Rockafellian for the problem, anchored at
\overline{y}\in Y
, if

\Phi(x,\overline{y})=\varphi(x)\qquad\forall\,x\in X\,.

Note that Rockafellians are not unique; for a given optimization problem, there exist infinitely many Rockafellians associated with it. This non-uniqueness provides significant flexibility, allowing one to design Rockafellians that can better accommodate approximations, perturbations, or numerical regularizations than the original problem. In particular, by carefully choosing the Rockafellian, one can simplify the analysis of convergence, stability, or sensitivity of approximate solutions.

3.
The uncorrupted problem

In this section, we study the optimization problem

(3.1)

\min_{(z,\gamma)\in{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}\times\mathbf{R}}\varphi(z,\gamma)=f_{0}(z)+\gamma+\kappa\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big((g\circ s)(\cdot,z)-\gamma\big)_{+}\big]\,,

together with a slight variant incorporating a smoothing of the positive-part function. Moreover, we introduce the corresponding Rockafellian formulations for each problem, thereby setting the stage for the corruption framework developed in Section
4
.

3.1.
The original optimization problem

Our first result establishes the existence of a solution to (
3.1
).

Proposition 3.1
.

If
{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}
is bounded in
{\rm Z}
or
f_{0}
is coercive, then the problem (
3.1
) has a minimizer.

Proof.

We first prove that
{\rm Z}\times\mathbf{R}\ni(z,\gamma)\mapsto{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big((g\circ s)(\cdot,z)-\gamma\big)_{+}\big]
is weakly lower semicontinuous. Let
(z_{n},\gamma_{n})\in{\rm Z}\times\mathbf{R}
be a sequence such that
z_{n}\rightharpoonup z
in
{\rm Z}
and
\gamma_{n}\rightharpoonup\gamma
in
\mathbf{R}
. Notice that since
\mathbf{R}
is finite-dimensional, the latter means that
\gamma_{n}\to\gamma
strongly. Now, using Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
, we have that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\big\{(g\circ s)(\xi,z_{n})-\gamma_{n}\big\}\geq(g\circ s)(\xi,z)-\gamma
for a.e.
\xi\in\Xi
. Then, by (
2.1
), we get

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\big((g\circ s)(\xi,z_{n})-\gamma_{n}\big)_{+}\geq\big((g\circ s)(\xi,z)-\gamma\big)_{+}\quad\text{for a.e.}\penalty\ \penalty\ \xi\in\Xi\,.

An straightforward application of Fatou’s lemma implies that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big((g\circ s)(\cdot,z_{n})-\gamma_{n}\big)_{+}\big]\geq{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big((g\circ s)(\cdot,z)-\gamma\big)_{+}\big]\,,

which means that
(z,\gamma)\mapsto{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big((g\circ s)(\cdot,z)-\gamma\big)_{+}\big]
is weakly lower semicontinuous, as desired. Since
f_{0}
is weakly lower semicontinuous as well (cf. Assumption
2.2
), we conclude that
\varphi
is weakly lower semicontinuous. Moreover,
{\rm Z}\times\mathbf{R}\ni(z,\gamma)\mapsto\varphi(z,\gamma)+\imath_{{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}}(z)
is coercive provided that
{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}
is bounded or
f_{0}
is coercive. Thus, by applying
[
8
, Theorem 3.2.5]
, it follows that
\varphi
admits a minimizer in
{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}\times\mathbf{R}
, i.e., problem (
3.1
) has a solution.
∎

Ahead of passing to a corrupted Rockafellian framework and intending to maintain the notation as clear as possible, we introduce a slight variation of the so-called reduced objective function. Specifically, we define
\mathscr{J}
as the function that assigns to each
(z,\gamma)\in{\rm Z}\times\mathbf{R}
the random variable

\mathscr{J}(z,\gamma):=g(s(\cdot,z))-\gamma\,.

For
\mathbf{t}:\Xi\to\Xi
, we denote
\mathscr{J}(z,\gamma;\mathbf{t})=\mathscr{J}(z,\gamma)\circ\mathbf{t}
and
\mathscr{J}_{+}(z,\gamma;\mathbf{t})=(\mathscr{J}(z,\gamma;\mathbf{t}))_{+}
. We further define
f:{\rm Z}\times\mathbf{R}\to\overline{\mathbf{R}}
as

f(z,\gamma):=f_{0}(z)+\gamma\,.

Under this notation, the objective functional may be rewritten as

\varphi(z,\gamma)=f(z,\gamma)+\kappa\,{\mathbb{E}}[\mathscr{J}_{+}(z,\gamma)]\,.

Furthermore, we introduce a suitable change of measure to ease the task of formalizing corruption to the continuous probability distribution:

Assumption 3.1

(Change of measure)
.

(1)

There exists another
\sigma
-finite measure
\mu
defined on
(\Xi,{\mathcal{A}})
.

(2)

\mathbb{P}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
.

From now on, we assume that
\Xi
carries the measure
\mu
. Accordingly, all measure-related properties are to be interpreted with respect to
\mu
instead of
\mathbb{P}
. We let
\rho
denote the Radon–Nikodym derivative
d\mathbb{P}/d\mu
and
{\mathcal{I}}
denote the integral operator
X\mapsto\int_{\Xi}X\,d\mu
. In addition, given
\mathrm{t}:\Xi\to\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{t}:\Xi\to\Xi
, we denote the translations
\wp(\mathrm{t})=\rho+\mathrm{t}
and
\omega(\mathbf{t})=I+\mathbf{t}
, where
I:\Xi\to\Xi
denotes the identity map. In particular, this enables us to rewrite the objective functional as

\varphi(z,\gamma)=f(z,\gamma)+\kappa\,{\mathcal{I}}\left[\mathscr{J}_{+}(z,\gamma)\,\rho\right]=f(z,\gamma)+\kappa\,{\mathcal{I}}\left[\mathscr{J}_{+}\big(z,\gamma;\omega(0)\big)\,\wp(0)\right].

The notation on the right-hand side of the last equality is suggestive of the form that the Rockafellian will take. In fact, for
q\in(1,+\infty)
and
q^{\prime}\in(1,+\infty)
, we introduce the reflexive Banach spaces

(3.2)

{\rm T}:={\rm L}^{q}(\Xi)\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{T}:={\rm L}^{q^{\prime}}(\Xi;\Xi)\,,

and define the announced Rockafellian functional
\Phi:{\rm Z}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}\times\mathbf{T}\to\overline{\mathbf{R}}
by

(3.3)

\Phi(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t}):=\begin{cases}f(z,\gamma)+\kappa\,{\mathcal{I}}\left[\mathscr{J}_{+}\big(z,\gamma;\omega(\mathbf{t})\big)\wp(\mathrm{t})\right],&\text{ if }(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})=(0,0)\text{ a.s.}\,,\\
+\infty&\text{ otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

which is clearly anchored at
(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})=(0,0)
. Thus, the Rockafellian problem associated with (
3.1
) is

(3.4)

\min\Big\{\Phi(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\penalty\ :\penalty\ (z,\gamma,\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\in{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}\times\mathbf{T}\Big\}\,.

Proposition 3.2
.

If
{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}
is bounded in
{\rm Z}
or
f_{0}
is coercive, then the minimization problem (
3.4
) has at least one solution.

Proof.

Let
(z^{\star},\gamma^{\star})\in{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}\times\mathbf{R}
be a solution of (
3.1
), whose existence is guaranteed by Proposition
3.1
. Since
\Phi(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})=+\infty
whenever
(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\neq(0,0)
, it follows that

\Phi(z^{\star},\gamma^{\star};0,0)=\varphi(z^{\star},\gamma^{\star})\leq\varphi(z,\gamma)=\Phi(z,\gamma;0,0)\leq\Phi(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\qquad\forall\,(z,\gamma)\in{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}\times\mathbf{R}\,,

which means that
(z^{\star},\gamma^{\star};0,0)
is a solution to (

∎

3.2.
The
\delta
-smoothed uncorrupted problem

The non-smoothness of the positive part function introduces non-differentiability in the integral term of (
3.1
), which poses significant challenges when deriving optimality conditions—particularly in nonconvex settings, where certain regularity assumptions on the functional are essential. To address this issue, we introduce a smoothing of the positive part function, which ensures enhanced regularity of the integral term, namely Fréchet differentiability. This smoothing strategy can be traced back, for instance, to the work of Chen and Mangasarian in
[
17
]
. Our approach can be seen as a generalization of the smoothed primal formulation studied by Kouri and Surowiec in
[
34
]
, as both the mapping
g
to the quantity-of-interest and the solution operator
s
considered here are generic. In this way, we consider a function
\zeta:\mathbf{R}\to\mathbf{R}
satisfying the following assumption.

Assumption 3.2

(Properties of
\zeta
)
.

(i)

\zeta
is continuous and bounded on the real line.

(ii)

\zeta
is nonnegative and
\int_{\mathbf{R}}\zeta(x)\,dx=1
.

(iii)

\int_{\mathbf{R}}\zeta(x)|x|\,dx<+\infty
.

(iv)

Either
\int_{\mathbf{R}}\zeta(x)x\,dx\leq 0
or
\int_{-\infty}^{0}\zeta(x)|x|\,dx=0
.

(v)

The set
\left\{x\in\mathbf{R}\penalty\ :\penalty\ \zeta(x)>0\right\}
is connected.

Under this assumption, we define a net of smoothings of the positive part
(\,\cdot\,)_{+,\delta}:\mathbf{R}\to\mathbf{R}
, given by

(3.5)

(x)_{+,\delta}:=\int_{-\infty}^{x}A_{\delta}(\tau)\,d\tau\,,\quad\text{where}\quad A_{\delta}(\tau):=\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\frac{1}{\delta}\,\zeta\left(\frac{\sigma}{\delta}\right)\,d\sigma\,,

for all
\delta>0
. The smoothing proposed by Chen and Mangasarian in
[
17
]
serves as an example of this, since it can be understood as the one obtained by considering
\zeta(x)=e^{-x}\,(1+e^{-x})^{-2}
, which certainly satisfies our assumptions.

Through a direct computation, one verifies that
(\,\cdot\,)_{+,\delta}
is at least twice continuously differentiable, with derivatives

(3.6)

(x)_{+,\delta}^{\prime}=A_{\delta}(x)\quad\text{ and }\quad(x)^{\prime\prime}_{+,\delta}=\frac{1}{\delta}\,\zeta\left(\frac{x}{\delta}\right)\qquad\forall\,x\in\mathbf{R}\,.

Now, we collect some important facts about the smoothing for later use.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider
\zeta
satisfying Assumption
3.2
. Then, the following statements hold:

(i)

\left\{(\,\cdot\,)_{+,\delta}\right\}_{\delta}
converges uniformly to
(\,\cdot\,)_{+}
as
\delta\downarrow 0
, with linear order.

(ii)

Given
\delta>0
, either
(\,\cdot\,)_{+,\delta}\leq(\,\cdot\,)_{+}
or
(\,\cdot\,)_{+}\leq(\,\cdot\,)_{+,\delta}
.

(iii)

Let
(x_{\delta})_{\delta}
be a sequence of real numbers whose limit inferior is bounded below by
x\in\mathbf{R}
. Then

\liminf_{\delta}\;(x_{\delta})_{+,\delta}\geq x_{+}\,.

(iv)

For all
\delta>0
,
(\,\cdot\,)_{+,\delta}
is Lipschitz continuous, with uniform Lipschitz constant
1
.

Proof.

To prove the first assertion, we first establish upper and lower bounds for the difference
(x)_{+,\delta}-(x)_{+}
. Given
x\in\mathbf{R}
, integrating by parts (
3.5
), we have

(3.7)

(x)_{+,\delta}=-\int_{-\infty}^{x}A_{\delta}^{\prime}(\tau)\,\tau\,d\tau+A_{\delta}(x)\,x\,.

By definition of
A_{\delta}
and (
3.6
), we get

(x)_{+,\delta}-(x)_{+}\leq-\delta\int_{-\infty}^{x/\delta}\sigma\,\zeta(\sigma)\,d\sigma\leq\delta\,\Delta_{2}\,,

with
\Delta_{2}=\int_{-\infty}^{0}|\sigma|\zeta(\sigma)\,d\sigma
. Similarly, when
x>0
, from (
3.7
), we obtain

\displaystyle(x)_{+,\delta}-(x)_{+}

\displaystyle=-\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{\tau}{\delta}\,\zeta(\tau/\delta)\,d\tau+(A_{\delta}(x)-1)\,x

\displaystyle\geq-\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{\tau}{\delta}\,\zeta(\tau/\delta)\,d\tau-\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{x}{\delta}\,\zeta(\tau/\delta)\,d\tau\geq-\delta\int_{\mathbf{R}}\sigma\,\zeta(\sigma)\,d\sigma\,,

where in the last inequality we have used that
x>0
together with the natural change of variables. When
x\leq 0
, by definition, there holds

(x)_{+,\delta}-(x)_{+}\geq 0\,.

The last two inequalities give
(x)_{+,\delta}-(x)_{+}\geq-\delta\,\Delta_{1},

with
\Delta_{1}=\max\left\{\int_{\mathbf{R}}\sigma\zeta(\sigma)\,d\sigma,0\right\}
, thereby proving the estimate

(3.8)

-\delta\,\Delta_{1}\leq(x)_{+,\delta}-(x)_{+}\leq\delta\,\Delta_{2}\,,

which, in turn, provides a uniform bound on the absolute value of the difference
(x)_{+,\delta}-(x)_{\delta}
. Thus, letting
\delta\to 0
yields assertion
(i)
. The second statement follows directly from comparing (
3.8
) with Assumption
3.2
, item
(iv)
. Notice that the options in this statement are mutually exclusive; otherwise, the smoothing would coincide pointwise with the positive part, which leads to a contradiction, since the zero function does not integrate to
1
.

For the third item, from the uniform bounds in (
3.8
) and (
2.1
), we obtain

\liminf_{\delta}\;(x_{\delta})_{+,\delta}\geq\liminf_{\delta}\,\big[(x_{\delta})_{+}-\delta\Delta_{1}\big]=\liminf_{\delta}\;(x_{\delta})_{+}\geq x_{+}\,.

Finally, the uniform Lipschitz continuity of the mapping
x\mapsto(x)_{+,\delta}
follows from its differentiability and
\|A_{\delta}\|_{{\rm L}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R})}\leq 1
. Then, given
x,y\in\mathbf{R}
, we have

\big|(x)_{+,\delta}-(y)_{+,\delta}\big|\leq\|A_{\delta}\|_{{\rm L}^{\infty}(x,y)}\,|x-y|\leq|x-y|\,,

which proves the third assertion and concludes the proof.
∎

Naturally, this smoothing induces a family of “objective” functionals
\left\{\varphi^{\delta}\right\}_{\delta>0}
, defined by

(3.9)

\varphi^{\delta}(z,\gamma)=f(z)+\kappa\,{\mathcal{I}}\left[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}\big(z,\gamma;\omega(0)\big)\,\wp(0)\right],

to which we refer as
\delta
-smoothed objective functionals. In turn, just as in Section
3.1
—and more precisely, in (
3.3
)— one can associate to
\varphi^{\delta}
a functional
\Phi^{\delta}:{\rm Z}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}\times\mathbf{T}\to\overline{\mathbf{R}}
, given by

\displaystyle\Phi^{\delta}(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f(z,\gamma)+\kappa\,{\mathcal{I}}\left[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}\big(z,\gamma;\omega(\mathbf{t})\big)\,\wp(\mathrm{t})\right],&\text{ if }(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})=0\text{ a.s.}\,,\\
+\infty\,,&\text{ otherwise}\,,\end{array}\right.

which we call
\delta
-smoothed Rockafellian
. It is clear that it is a Rockafellian anchored at
(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})=(0,0)
for
\varphi^{\delta}
. Finally, we remark that
\varphi^{\delta}
admits a minimizer in
{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}\times\mathbf{R}
, which follows from the same reasoning used in the proof of Proposition
3.1
. Consequently, by the same argument as in Proposition
3.2
, it follows that
\Phi^{\delta}
also admits a solution.

4.
Unified analysis for corrupted Rockafellians

This is the main section of the paper. Here, we study the problems arising from simultaneous corruptions of the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
and the support of its distribution. This approach is motivated by the work in
[
3
]
, where the authors carry out a convergence analysis for these two types of corruptions separately in the expected-value setting. Subsequently, in Sections
5.2
and
5.3
, we address the corresponding separated scenarios for the CVaR functional by particularizing the results developed in the present section.

We first develop a framework that encompasses the types of corruption described above and provides the theoretical foundation for their analysis. Next, we study the existence of solutions to the corrupted problems associated with (
3.9
) and (
3.2
). We then derive first-order optimality conditions under suitable differentiability assumptions on the operators. We end this section by establishing a generalized convergence result in the sense of weak-strong
\Gamma
-convergence (see Definition
2.2
).

4.1.
Preliminary constructions and the Rockafellian problem

We begin by defining the space of probability densities

(4.1)

{\rm P}:=\left\{\varrho:\Xi\to\mathbf{R}_{+}\penalty\ \Big|\penalty\ \varrho\in{\rm L}^{\infty}(\Xi)\quad\text{ and }\quad\int_{\Xi}\varrho(\xi)\,d\mu(\xi)=1\right\},

and note that the Radon–Nikodym derivative

introduced in the previous section belongs to this space.

The natural definition of the corrupted objective functional—by analogy with the one adopted in
[

(4.2)

where

and

represent a corruption map and a corrupted distribution, respectively. However, since we aim to work on a general underlying measure space
\Xi
, this notion of corrupted objective functional turns out to be slightly inadequate. Indeed, in order to establish the existence of solutions to the associated minimization problem, we require the availability of suitable compact embeddings (cf. Assumption
4.2
), which, in practice, are not attainable when
\Xi
is infinite-dimensional. We shall return to this issue and provide further insight into this setting in Remark

First, in order to accurately represent corruptions of
\rho
acting in our

-smoothed functional

Assumption 4.1
.

There exists a sequence of measurable subspaces

According to Assumption

Now, putting

We note that functions in

and

Comparing with

,
\wp_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{t})=\rho_{\varepsilon}+\mathrm{t}

\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z,\gamma)=f(z,\gamma)+\kappa\,{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}\big[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z,\gamma;\omega_{\varepsilon}(0))\,\wp_{\varepsilon}(0)\big]\,,

where

(\rho_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
and
(\eta_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}

We thus obtain our first corrupted minimization problem to study:

\min\left\{\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z,\gamma)\penalty\ :\penalty\ (z,\gamma)\in{\rm Z}_{\rm ad}\times\mathbf{R}\right\}.

As expected, we have the following result:

Proposition 4.1

If

is bounded in

Proof.

It follows from a slight modification of the proof of Proposition

∎

Next, to define a Rockafellian associated with

, we must define appropriate spaces for the perturbation variables. To this end, consider nets of reflexive Banach spaces
\left\{{\rm W}_{\varepsilon}\right\}_{\varepsilon>0}
and
\left\{\mathbf{W}_{\varepsilon}\right\}_{\varepsilon>0}

\Xi_{\varepsilon}\to\mathbf{R}
and
\Xi_{\varepsilon}\to\Xi

We assume that they satisfy the following assumption.

Assumption 4.2
.

For every

(1)

{\rm W}_{\varepsilon}
is compactly embedded into
{\rm L}^{q}(\Xi_{\varepsilon})
.

(2)

\mathbf{W}_{\varepsilon}
is compactly embedded into
{\rm L}^{q^{\prime}}(\Xi_{\varepsilon};\Xi)

Due to this assumption, and recalling the definition of the extension-by-zero operator (cf. Section
2

(4.8)

\displaystyle{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\mathrm{t}:\Xi\to\mathbf{R}\penalty\ \big|\penalty\ \mathrm{t}\lvert_{{}_{\Xi_{\varepsilon}}}\in{\rm W}_{\varepsilon}\oplus{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}^{\rho}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathrm{t}\lvert_{{}_{\Xi_{\varepsilon}^{c}}}\in{\rm L}^{q}(\Xi_{\varepsilon}^{c})\Big\}

\displaystyle={\rm E}_{\,\Xi_{\varepsilon},0}({\rm W}_{\varepsilon})\oplus{\rm E}_{\,\Xi_{\varepsilon},0}({\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}^{\rho})\oplus{\rm E}_{\,\Xi_{\varepsilon}^{c},0}\big({\rm L}^{q}(\Xi_{\varepsilon}^{c})\big)\,,

where

(4.9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}:

\displaystyle=\Big\{\mathbf{t}:\Xi\to\Xi\penalty\ :\penalty\ \mathbf{t}\lvert_{{}_{\Xi_{\varepsilon}}}\in\mathbf{W}_{\varepsilon}\oplus{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}^{\eta}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{t}\lvert_{\Xi_{\varepsilon}^{c}}\in{\rm L}^{q^{\prime}}(\Xi_{\varepsilon}^{c};\Xi)\Big\}

In practice, if the choice of spaces includes any of the added elements, one can force a direct sum by deleting the finite-dimensional spaces generated by these elements, and so the subsequent analysis is also valid.

We imbue these spaces with the norm deduced from the direct sum decompositions. More precisely, given

The use of compactly embedded spaces is, in broad terms, a way to enforce stronger regularity on the admissible functions. This additional regularity is what ultimately enables the existence of minimizers for the associated Rockafellians (see Theorem

Lastly, we consider a function

We are finally in a position to associate a Rockafellian functional with

(4.10)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}\big(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t}\big)

\displaystyle:=f(z,\gamma)+\kappa\,{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}\left[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}\big(z,\gamma;\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{t})\big)\,\wp_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{t})\right]+\theta_{\varepsilon}\,{\mathcal{H}}(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})

\displaystyle+\imath_{{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}\cap\left({\rm P}-\rho_{\varepsilon}\right)}(\mathrm{t})+\imath_{\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}}(\mathbf{t})\,.

We notice that

is anchored at

(cf. (

(4.11)

\min\Big\{\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}\big(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t}\big):\quad(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\in{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}\times\mathbf{T}\Big\}\,.

The following growth condition guarantees that the integral term in (

Assumption 4.3

There exists a function
\varrho:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)

\big|(g\circ s)(\xi,z)\big|\leq\varrho(\|z\|_{{\rm Z}})\,\|\xi\|_{\Xi}\qquad\forall\,(\xi,z)\in\Xi\times{\rm Z}\,.

\mathscr{J}(z,\gamma;\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{t}))\lvert_{{}_{\Xi_{\varepsilon}}}\in{\rm L}^{q^{\prime}}(\Xi_{\varepsilon})

Remark 4.1
.

We end this section by giving some insight into the aforementioned setting. At first glance, the framework presented herein may appear excessively complicated or overly elaborate, but we shall see that its implications make it a reasonable approach to adopt. For instance, this approach may be helpful when
\Xi
cannot be embedded in a finite-dimensional space (e.g. a space of sequences), as it enables us to study a limiting problem written in such a space through problems posed in finite-dimensional spaces. In this case, identifying function spaces
\Xi\to\mathbf{R}
and
\Xi\to\Xi
that are compactly embedded in
{\rm L}^{q}(\Xi,\mathbf{R})
and
{\rm L}^{q}(\Xi;\Xi)
is a difficult task, since classical Rellich-type compactness results depend heavily on the properties of
\Xi
, particularly on its dimension and size (see e.g.
[
30
,
13
,
51
,
21
,
25
]
). As we shall see in Section
4.2
, the lack of a compactness property hinders the proof of existence of a minimizer for the smoothed-corrupted problems, as the
{\rm L}^{q}
-topology turns out not to be strong enough. To overcome this difficulty, we can take the sequence
\left\{\Xi_{\varepsilon}\right\}_{\varepsilon>0}
to be finite-dimensional which, in real applications, makes it possible to find function spaces that possess the desired compactness properties (cf. Assumption
4.2
).

Remark 4.2
.

Notice that if
\Xi
turns out to be included in a finite-dimensional space and has finite measure, we can take every subspace
\Xi_{\varepsilon}
to be
\Xi
itself. In this sense, our framework includes the case in which a finite-dimensional noise assumption is made (see
[
5
, Part I, Section 2]
and the references therein). We consider this particular case in Section
5.1
.

4.2.
Existence of solution

The following result concerns the existence of a minimizer for each problem associated with the smoothed-corrupted Rockafellians.

Theorem 4.1
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
and suppose that Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
,
3.1
,
3.2
,
4.1
,
4.2
and
4.3
hold. Additionally, assume that
f_{0}
is coercive or
{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}
is bounded. Then, there exists

(z^{\star},\gamma^{\star};\mathrm{t}^{\star},\mathbf{t}^{\star})\in{\rm Z}_{\rm ad}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}

such that

\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z^{\star},\gamma^{\star};\mathrm{t}^{\star},\mathbf{t}^{\star})\leq\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\qquad\forall\,(z,\gamma,\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\in{\rm Z}_{\rm ad}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}\times\mathbf{T}\,.

Proof.

We begin by defining the auxiliary functional
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}:{\rm Z}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}\to\overline{\mathbf{R}}
given by

\widetilde{\Phi}_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t}):=\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}\lvert_{{\rm Z}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}}(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})+\imath_{{\rm Z}_{\rm ad}}(z)\,,

which, by definition, agrees with
\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
in
{\rm Z}_{\rm ad}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}
. We further realize that, if
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
has a minimizer
(z^{\star},\gamma^{\star};\mathrm{t}^{\star},\mathbf{t}^{\star})\in{\rm Z}_{\rm ad}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}
, we have

\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z^{\star},\gamma^{\star};\mathrm{t}^{\star},\mathbf{t}^{\star})=\widetilde{\Phi}_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z^{\star},\gamma^{\star},\mathrm{t}^{\star},\mathbf{t}^{\star})\leq\widetilde{\Phi}_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z,\gamma,\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})=\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\,,

for all
(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\in{\rm Z}_{\rm ad}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}
. Moreover, since
\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})=+\infty
whenever
(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\not\in{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}
, it holds

\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z^{\star},\gamma^{\star};\mathrm{t}^{\star},\mathbf{t}^{\star})\leq\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\qquad\forall\,(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\in{\rm Z}_{\rm ad}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}\times\mathbf{T}\,.

Thus, it suffices to show the existence of such a minimizer for
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
in order to conclude the proof. We do so in what follows.

We start by proving that
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
is weakly lower semicontinuous; in doing so, we pay special attention to the integral term. Consider sequences
(z_{n})_{n\in\mathbf{N}}
in
{\rm Z}
,
(\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbf{N}}
in
\mathbf{R}
,
(\mathrm{t}_{n})_{n\in\mathbf{N}}
in
{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}
, and
(\mathbf{t}_{n})_{n\in\mathbf{N}}
in
\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}
, converging weakly to
z\in{\rm Z}
,
\gamma\in\mathbf{R}
,
\mathrm{t}\in{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}
, and
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}
, respectively. If
(z_{n},\mathrm{t}_{n})_{n\in\mathbf{N}}
does not have a subsequence contained in
{\rm Z}_{\rm ad}\times({\rm P}-\rho_{\varepsilon})
, at least one of the indicator terms in the auxiliary Rockafellian equals
+\infty
, and the property follows. Otherwise, there exists a (not relabeled) subsequence such that
z_{n}\in{\rm Z}_{\rm ad}
and
\rho_{\varepsilon}+\mathrm{t}_{n}\in{\rm P}
, for all
n\in\mathbf{N}
. We may assume that the limit inferior
\displaystyle\liminf_{n}\;\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z_{n},\gamma_{n};\mathrm{t}_{n},\mathbf{t}_{n})
is attained along this subsequence.

Notice that
\big(\mathrm{t}_{n}|_{\Xi_{\varepsilon}}\big)_{n\in\mathbf{N}}
is weakly convergent in
{\rm W}_{\varepsilon}\oplus{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}^{\rho}
. Since the latter is compactly embedded in
{\rm L}^{q}(\Xi_{\varepsilon})
, strong convergence (up to subsequences) implies almost everywhere pointwise convergence in
\Xi_{\varepsilon}
. Hence, there exists a subsequence
(\mathrm{t}_{n^{\prime}})_{n^{\prime}}
satisfying

(4.12)

\rho_{\varepsilon}(\xi)+\mathrm{t}_{n^{\prime}}(\xi)\xrightarrow{n^{\prime}\to\infty}\rho_{\varepsilon}(\xi)+\mathrm{t}(\xi)\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ for a.e.}\penalty\ \penalty\ \xi\in\Xi_{\varepsilon}\,.

Similarly, since
\mathbf{t}_{n^{\prime}}\rightharpoonup\mathbf{t}
in
\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}
, using the compact embedding, we are able to find a subsequence (not relabeled) pointwise convergent to
\mathbf{t}
. Then,

\eta_{\varepsilon}(\xi)+\mathbf{t}_{n^{\prime}}(\xi)\xrightarrow{n^{\prime}\to\infty}\eta_{\varepsilon}(\xi)+\mathbf{t}(\xi)\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ for a.e.}\penalty\ \penalty\ \xi\in\Xi_{\varepsilon}\,,

which combined with the fact that
z_{n}\rightharpoonup z
in
{\rm Z}
, and employing Assumption
2.1
, yields

s(\eta_{\varepsilon}(\xi)+\mathbf{t}_{n^{\prime}}(\xi),z_{n^{\prime}})\rightharpoonup s(\eta_{\varepsilon}(\xi)+\mathbf{t}(\xi),z)\quad\text{ in }\penalty\ {\rm U}\,,\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ for a.e.}\penalty\ \penalty\ \xi\in\Xi_{\varepsilon}\,.

Since
g
is sequentially weakly lower semicontinuous (cf. Assumption
2.2
), the above convergence implies that

\liminf_{n^{\prime}\to\infty}\penalty\ \Big((g\circ s)(\eta_{\varepsilon}(\xi)+\mathbf{t}_{n^{\prime}}(\xi),z_{n^{\prime}})\Big)\geq(g\circ s)(\eta_{\varepsilon}(\xi)+\mathbf{t}(\xi),z)\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ for a.e.}\penalty\ \penalty\ \xi\in\Xi_{\varepsilon}\,.

Using that
\gamma_{n^{\prime}}\to\gamma
in
\mathbf{R}
, the monotonicity and continuity of
(\,\cdot\,)_{+,\delta}
, and the foregoing inequality, we arrive at

(4.13)

\liminf_{n^{\prime}\to\infty}\penalty\ \big(\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z_{n^{\prime}},\gamma_{n^{\prime}};\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{t}_{n^{\prime}}))\big)(\xi)\geq\big(\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z,\gamma;\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{t}))\big)(\xi)\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ for a.e.}\penalty\ \penalty\ \xi\in\Xi_{\varepsilon}\,.

Bearing in mind (
4.12
) and (
4.13
), and using the fact that
\rho_{\varepsilon}+\mathrm{t}_{n^{\prime}}\geq 0
, a straightforward application of Fatou’s Lemma yields

\liminf_{n^{\prime}\to\infty}\penalty\ {\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}\big(\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}\big(z_{n^{\prime}},\gamma_{n^{\prime}};\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{t}_{n^{\prime}})\big)\,\wp_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{t}_{n^{\prime}})\big)\geq{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}\big(\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}\big(z,\gamma;\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{t})\big)\,\wp_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{t})\big)\,.

This, together with the weak lower semicontinuity of
f_{0}
(see Assumption
2.2
) and
{\mathcal{H}}
, yields

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\penalty\ \widetilde{\Phi}_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z_{n},\gamma_{n};\mathrm{t}_{n},\mathbf{t}_{n})=\liminf_{n^{\prime}\to\infty}\penalty\ \widetilde{\Phi}_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z_{n^{\prime}},\gamma_{n^{\prime}};\mathrm{t}_{n^{\prime}},\mathbf{t}_{n^{\prime}})\geq\widetilde{\Phi}_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\,,

so
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
is weakly lower semicontinuous. In turn, it is straightforward to verify that
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
is coercive, since
{\mathcal{H}}
is coercive in
{\rm T}\times\mathbf{T}
and either
f
is coercive in
{\rm Z}\times\mathbf{R}
or the admissible set
{\rm Z}_{\mathrm{ad}}
is bounded. Consequently, noting that
{\rm Z}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}
is a reflexive Banach space, the weak lower semicontinuity and coercivity of
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
allow us to invoke a particular case of the Weierstrass minimization theorem (see
[
8
, Theorem 3.2.5]
) to establish the existence of a minimizer. Finally, since
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
is not identically equal to
+\infty
, any minimizer must satisfy
(z^{\star},\gamma^{\star},\mathrm{t}^{\star},\mathbf{t}^{\star})\in{\rm Z}_{\rm ad}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}
. According to the initial remark, this completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

We finish this section by commenting that, as seen in the previous proof, instead of introducing Assumption
4.2
, one might incorporate compact operators into the objective functional. This would allow us to dispense with the assumption that there is an actual embedding into the spaces
{\rm L}^{q}(\Xi_{\varepsilon})
and
{\rm L}^{q}(\Xi_{\varepsilon};\Xi)
. We do not adopt this approach because, as far as we see, it would heavily modify the analysis of convergence to the original problem. So far, we see two possible options: First, also including compact operators in the original Rockafellian, as arguments of
\omega
and
\wp
, would help the convergence analysis and would not modify the fact that we have Rockafellians anchored at
0
. The second option is only including them in the corrupted Rockafellians, and then deducing suitable conditions under which the results can still be carried out. One possible advantage of considering compact operators instead of an embedding is that it may allow us to strengthen the convergence results, from weak-strong Gamma convergence to Mosco convergence. This claim comes from the fact that compact operators upgrade weak convergence to strong convergence. In our setting, we are using this fact to leverage weak convergence in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, but we are not able to take advantage of this fact when proving convergence (see proof of Theorem
4.3
). If this could be overcome, one gets Mosco convergence, which is the strongest among these types of generalized convergence.

4.3.
Optimality conditions

Having established the existence of minimizers for our perturbed Rockafellian problems, we now aim to derive first-order optimality conditions. We use the following notation for differentials: given an operator
A:X\to Y
between Banach spaces, we denote its directional derivative at
x\in X
in the direction
\underline{x}\in X
by
\mathbf{D}A(x)[\underline{x}]
. Furthermore, if
A
is Fréchet differentiable, we denote its Fréchet derivative at
x
by
\mathbf{D}A(x)\in\mathscr{L}(X,Y)
. When
X
is a product space, namely
X=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}X_{i}
, we denote by
\mathbf{D}_{x_{i}}A(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})[\underline{x}_{i}]
the derivative with respect to the variable
x_{i}
. Notice that, if
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})
,

\mathbf{D}A(x)[\underline{x}]=\mathbf{D}_{x_{1}}A(x)[\underline{x}_{1}]+\mathbf{D}_{x_{2}}A(x)[\underline{x}_{2}]+\cdots+\mathbf{D}_{x_{\ell}}A(x)[\underline{x}_{\ell}]\,.

Given a convex set
S
of a vector space
X
, the normal cone of
S
at
x\in S
is denoted by
N_{S}(x)
. Moreover, we use the following convention for adjoint notation: given vector normed spaces
X
,
Y
, and
Z
, and given an operator
G\in\mathscr{L}(X,Y)
, we define
G^{*}:\mathscr{L}(Y,Z)\to\mathscr{L}(X,Z)
by
G^{*}(F):=F\circ G
for all
F\in\mathscr{L}(Y,Z)
. In particular, for
Z=\mathbf{R}
and
F\in Y^{\prime}=\mathscr{L}(Y,\mathbf{R})
, this reduces to the standard adjoint
G^{*}:Y^{\prime}\to X^{\prime}
. Finally, we denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the duality pairing of a space and its topological dual, omitting the underlying spaces when clear from the context.

Now, notice that (
4.11
) can be rewritten equivalently as

4.4.
Weak-strong Gamma convergence

The following result concerns the convergence of our family of smoothed-corrupted Rockafellians to the original one, in the sense of weak-strong Gamma convergence, under a reasonable assumption on the behavior of corruption. This generalized notion of convergence is of interest because it preserves minimizing sequences, in the sense given in Proposition
2.1
.

In addition, it is worth mentioning that the following convergence theorem does not rely on existence nor optimality conditions of solutions, and so its hypothesis are milder than those of Theorems
4.1
and
4.2

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
,
3.1
,
3.2
and
4.1

\rho\in{\rm P}\cap{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}
for all

and consider corruptions

(4.16)

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\,\theta_{\varepsilon}=+\infty\,,

and

(4.17)

\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}\overset{\Gamma}{\rightharpoondown}\Phi^{\delta}

This, together with the weak convergence of

to
z
and the properties of
s
, gives

s(\eta_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}+\mathbf{t}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}},z_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\rightharpoonup s(I+\mathbf{t},z)\quad\text{ in }\penalty\ {\rm U},\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{a.e. in }\Xi\,.

Furthermore, using the weak convergence of
(\gamma_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
, weak lower semicontinuity of
g
, and the continuity and monotonicity of
(\,\cdot\,)_{+,\delta}
, respectively, we obtain that

(4.18)

\liminf_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z_{\varepsilon^{\prime}},\gamma_{\varepsilon^{\prime}};\omega_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\mathbf{t}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}))\geq\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z,\gamma;\omega(\mathbf{t}))\,.

From the fact that convergence in
{\rm T}
implies almost everywhere pointwise convergence, and since
\mathrm{t}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\in{\rm P}-\rho_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
, it follows that

\rho_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}+\mathrm{t}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\xrightarrow{\penalty\ \varepsilon^{\prime}\penalty\ }\rho+\mathrm{t}\quad\text{ and }\quad\rho+\mathrm{t}\geq 0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ a.e. in }\Xi\,.

Moreover, according to the approximation property of the subspaces
\Xi_{\varepsilon}
(cf. (
4.3
)), we get

\chi_{{}_{\Xi_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}}}(\rho_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}+\mathrm{t}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\xrightarrow{\penalty\ \varepsilon^{\prime}\penalty\ }\rho+\mathrm{t}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ a.e. in }\Xi\,.

This, together with (
4.18
) and an application of Fatou’s lemma, yields

\liminf_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\,{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\big[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z_{\varepsilon^{\prime}},\gamma_{\varepsilon^{\prime}};\omega_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\mathbf{t}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}))\,\wp_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\mathrm{t})\big]\geq{\mathcal{I}}[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z,\gamma;\omega(\mathbf{t}))\,\wp(\mathrm{t})]\,.

Next, we treat the penalty term. We distinguish two cases: when
(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})=(0,0)
and when
(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\neq(0,0)
. In this way, we notice that

\liminf_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\,\theta_{\varepsilon}\,{\mathcal{H}}(\mathrm{t}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}},\mathbf{t}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\geq\begin{cases}0&\text{if }(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})=(0,0)\,,\\
+\infty&\text{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

where the first case follows from the nonnegativity of
{\mathcal{H}}
and the penalty parameters, and the second one follows from the properties of the limit inferior. Therefore, the limit inferior condition follows from this, the weak lower semicontinuity of
f_{0}
, and the fact that we are working in the minimizing sequence indexed by
\varepsilon^{\prime}
.

In second place, we prove the limit superior condition (cf. (
2.4
)). Fixed
(z,\gamma,\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\in{\rm Z}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}\times\mathbf{T}
, we must find a sequence converging to it, satisfying the bound in (
2.4
). We distinguish two cases. First, when
(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})=(0,0)
a.e. in
\Xi
, we choose the sequence
(z_{\varepsilon},\gamma_{\varepsilon},\mathrm{t}_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{t}_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}=(z,\gamma,\rho-\rho_{\varepsilon},I-\eta_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
, which is clearly convergent. Under this choice, we have

\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z_{\varepsilon},\gamma_{\varepsilon};\mathrm{t}_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{t}_{\varepsilon})=f(z,\gamma)+{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}\big[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z,\gamma;\omega(\mathrm{t}))\wp(\mathbf{t})\big]\,.

We remark that the fact that the indicator terms vanish comes precisely from the enrichment of the spaces (see (
4.8
) and (
4.9
)) and the assumption that
\rho\in{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}
. If this was not the case, the desired bound would not hold. The approximation property of
\Xi_{\varepsilon}
and the
Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem
yield

{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}\big[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z,\gamma;\omega(\mathrm{t}))\,\wp(\mathbf{t})\big]\xrightarrow{\penalty\ \varepsilon\penalty\ }{\mathcal{I}}\big[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z,\gamma;\omega(\mathrm{t}))\,\wp(\mathbf{t})\big]\,,

where we used that, by definition,
{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}(Y)={\mathcal{I}}(\chi_{{}_{\Xi_{\varepsilon}}}Y)
, for any
Y:\Xi\to\mathbf{R}
. It follows from the above arguments that

\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z_{\varepsilon},\gamma_{\varepsilon};\mathrm{t}_{\varepsilon},\mathbf{t}_{\varepsilon})\xrightarrow{\penalty\ \varepsilon\penalty\ }\Phi^{\delta}(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\,.

The remaining case is when
(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})
is not essentially zero. According to the definition of
\Phi^{\delta}
, the condition is tautological. This concludes the proof.
∎

We end this section by proving that the smoothing does not interfere with the convergence. Furthermore, we are able to prove Mosco convergence to the original Rockafellian functional.

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
,
3.1
and
3.2
hold. Then, the sequence of smoothed Rockafellians
\left\{\Phi^{\delta}\right\}_{\delta}
Mosco converges to
\Phi
, as
\delta\to 0
, in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

Proof.

First, to prove the limit inferior condition (cf. (
2.3
)), given a sequence
(z_{\delta},\gamma_{\delta},\mathrm{t}_{\delta},\mathbf{t}_{\delta})_{\delta}
weakly converging to
(z,\gamma,\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})
in
{\rm Z}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}\times\mathbf{T}
, we distinguish between two cases: If
(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})
is not zero, no weakly convergent sequence
(\mathrm{t}_{\delta},\mathbf{t}_{\delta})_{\delta}
can be identically zero. Therefore, up to deleting finite terms, we may assume that any sequence weakly converging to
(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})
does not have zero terms. A simple comparison of the involved Rockafellians (cf. (
3.3
) and (
3.2
)) gives the limit inferior condition in this case. On the other hand, if
(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})
is essentially zero, we may assume that any weakly convergent sequence
(\mathrm{t}_{\delta},\mathbf{t}_{\delta})_{\delta}
, up to deleting finite terms, is identically zero, because otherwise the condition is trivially verified. Then, by recalling the definition of the Rockafellians (cf. (
3.3
) and (
3.2
)), we have

\Phi^{\delta}(z_{\delta},\gamma_{\delta};0,0)=f(z_{\delta},\gamma_{\delta})+\kappa\,{\mathcal{I}}\left[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z_{\delta},\gamma_{\delta},\omega(0))\,\wp(0)\right]\,,

and

\Phi(z,\gamma;0,0)=f(z,\gamma)+\kappa\,{\mathcal{I}}\left[\mathscr{J}_{+}(z,\gamma,\omega(0))\,\wp(0)\right]\,.

In this way, resorting to the reasoning of the previous proof (cf. Theorem
4.3
), we may establish that

\liminf_{\delta}\mathscr{J}(z_{\delta},\gamma_{\delta},\omega(0))\,\wp(0)\geq\mathscr{J}(z,\gamma,\omega(0))\,\wp(0)\quad\text{a.e. in }\Xi\,.

Moreover, according to item
(iii)
in Lemma
3.1
, the foregoing inequality implies that

\liminf_{\delta}\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z_{\delta},\gamma_{\delta},\omega(0))\,\wp(0)\geq\mathscr{J}_{+}(z,\gamma,\omega(0))\,\wp(0)\quad\text{a.e. in }\Xi\,.

Just as in the previous results, a straightforward application of Fatou’s lemma and the continuity properties of
f
(cf. Assumption
2.2
) give the condition.

We now turn to the verification of the limit superior condition (cf. (
2.2
)). The case
(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\neq(0,\mathbf{0})
is immediate. The remaining case follows by considering the constant sequence
\{(z,\gamma,\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})\}_{\delta}
and invoking the uniform upper bound provided in (
3.8
) to control the corresponding limit superior. We omit further details for the sake of brevity. This completes the proof.
∎

5.
Particular Cases

In this section, we explore different cases that arise in applications. The analysis developed in Section
4
is adapted to each context, leading to various simplifications. Specifically, we first present the modifications of the framework when the sample space
\Xi
is a finite-dimensional space with finite measure. We then consider corruptions of continuous probability distributions, and finally address the scenario in which only the support of the probability distribution is corrupted.

5.1.
Finite-dimensional underlying measure space of finite measure

In this subsection, we focus on the particular case in which the sample space
\Xi
is finite-dimensional and
\mu(\Xi)<+\infty
. This setting provides a simple framework where several technical difficulties encountered in the general case (cf. Section
4
) disappear. In fact, as previously observed, in this situation we may consider the subspaces
\widetilde{\Xi}_{\varepsilon}=\Xi
in Assumption
4.1
and, since
\Xi
has finite measure, we can put
\Xi_{\varepsilon}=\Xi
for all
\varepsilon>0
. This gives us a simplified version of the spaces introduced in Section
4
. Namely,

{\rm P}_{\varepsilon}={\rm P}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{T}\,.

In this way, noting that
{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}={\mathcal{I}}
, the smoothed-corrupted objective functional (
4.6
) reads:

\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z,\gamma)=f(z,\gamma)+\kappa\,{\mathcal{I}}[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z,\gamma;\omega_{\varepsilon}(0))\,\wp_{\varepsilon}(0)]\,,

for all
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
, where
\{\rho_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}\in{\rm P}
and
\{\eta_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}\in\mathbf{T}
represent the corruption densities and the corruption maps, respectively. Notice that, according to Proposition
4.1
, this functional has a minimizer.

We continue by describing how Assumption
4.2
is simplified. First, we observe that there are Banach space isomorphisms

(5.1)

[{\rm L}^{q}(\Xi)]^{n}\xrightarrow{\,\sim\,}{\rm L}^{q}(\Xi;\Xi)\quad\text{ and }\quad[{\rm L}^{q^{\prime}}(\Xi)]^{n}\xrightarrow{\,\sim\,}{\rm L}^{q^{\prime}}(\Xi;\Xi)\,,

where
n:=\dim\,\Xi
. If
q^{\prime}\geq q
, we only need to assume that there is a function space
\widetilde{\rm W}
with compact inclusion in
{\rm L}^{q}(\Xi)
(cf. Assumption
4.2
). In fact, by finite-dimensionality, there is a space
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}
of functions
\Xi\to\Xi
, isomorphic to
[\widetilde{\rm W}]^{n}
. We then have

(5.2)

\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}\xrightarrow{\sim}[\widetilde{W}]^{n}\hookrightarrow[{\rm L}^{q}(\Xi)]^{n}\,.

Then, according to (
5.1
), (
5.2
), and the canonical inclusion
{\rm L}^{q^{\prime}}(\Xi)\hookrightarrow{\rm L}^{q}(\Xi)
, the choice

{\rm W}=\widetilde{\rm W}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{W}=\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}

satisfies Assumption
4.2
. The case where
q\geq q^{\prime}
is analogous. In the remaining of this section, we assume that the spaces
{\rm W}
and
\mathbf{W}
have been obtained through this construction, thereby dispensing with Assumption
4.2
. Consequently,
{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}
(cf. (
4.8
) and (
4.9
)) become

{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}={\rm W}\oplus\mathrm{span}\{\rho_{\varepsilon}\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{W}\oplus\mathrm{span}\{I,\eta_{\varepsilon}\}\,.

Therefore, the Rockafellian functional defined in (
4.10
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})=

\displaystyle f(z,\gamma)+\kappa\,{\mathcal{I}}[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z,\gamma;\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{t}))\,\wp_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{t})]+\theta_{\varepsilon}\,{\mathcal{H}}(\mathrm{t},\mathbf{t})

\displaystyle+\imath_{{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}\cap({\rm P}-\rho_{\varepsilon})}(\mathrm{t})+\imath_{\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}}(\mathbf{t})\,.

Of course, the results presented in Section
4
remain valid, with the corresponding modifications to the notation. In particular, the existence of a solution (cf. Theorem
4.1
), the optimality conditions (cf. Theorem
4.2
), and weak-strong
\Gamma
-convergence (cf. Theorem
4.3
) still hold.

5.2.
Corruption to continuous probability distibution

Now, we aim to reduce the general setting presented in Section
4
to the case where we only consider corruptions to continuous probability distributions. In this scenario, we maintain the space of probability densities
{\rm P}
defined in (
4.1
) with respect to the measure
\mu
. Furthermore, in contrast to the case studied in Section
5.1
, we do have to use the auxiliary spaces
\Xi_{\varepsilon}
(cf. Assumption
4.1
) so that we can construct the spaces
{\rm P}_{\varepsilon}
(cf. (
4.4
)) which represent the spaces of corrupted probability densities in
\Xi_{\varepsilon}
. However,
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
(cf. (
4.5

\varepsilon>0
, we let

be a corrupted density. Then, the smoothed-corrupted objective functional (cf. (
4.6

\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z,\gamma)=f(z,\gamma)+\kappa\,{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}\big[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z,\gamma)\,\wp_{\varepsilon}(0)\big]\,,

where we recall that

4.2
that
\mathbf{W}_{\varepsilon}
is no longer required, and it suffices to consider
{\rm W}_{\varepsilon}
. This is reflected in the spaces
{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}

and satisfies: if
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm T}}(t_{k})\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}0

in
{\rm T}
. In this case, as mentioned when
{\mathcal{H}}
was introduced, one may take
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm T}}
to be the
{\rm T}
-norm. Thus, the Rockafellian functional associated with

is given by
\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}:{\rm Z}\times\mathbf{R}\times{\rm T}\to\overline{\mathbf{R}}
, defined as

(5.3)

\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}\big(z,\gamma;\mathrm{t}\big):=f(z,\gamma)+\kappa\,{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}\left[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z,\gamma)\,\wp_{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{t})\right]+\theta_{\varepsilon}\,{\mathcal{H}}_{{\rm T}}(\mathrm{t})+\imath_{{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}\cap\left({\rm P}-\rho_{\varepsilon}\right)}(\mathrm{t})\,.

Note also that Assumption
4.3
ensures that the integral in this functional is finite. Finally, we remark that the full Assumption
2.1
is not required. In fact, it suffices to consider the following hypothesis.

Assumption 5.1
.

(1)

s(\cdot,z):\Xi\to{\rm U}

{\mathcal{A}}
-measurable, for every

.

(2)

. This relaxation arises from the fact that we are now considering only corruptions to the continuous distribution, rather than both types of corruptions, so there are no sequences in the component associated with

.

5.3.
Corruption to support of the probability distibution

As a last instance of analysis, we study the case in which we are only in presence of corruption to the support of the idealized probability distribution
\rho
. In this situation, the spaces
{\rm P}_{\varepsilon}
(cf. (
4.4
)),
{\rm T}
,
{\rm W}
(cf. Assumption
4.2
) and
{\rm T}_{\varepsilon}
are no longer required, as all of them regard corruption to the probability distribution. In this way, recalling that
\wp(0)=\rho
, the objective functional (cf. (
4.6
)) takes the form

\varphi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z,\gamma)=f(z,\gamma)+\kappa\,{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z,\gamma;\omega_{\varepsilon}(0))\,\rho]\,.

In addition, we observe that the function
{\mathcal{H}}
now depends only on
\mathbf{T}
, thus the hypotheses on it are accordingly simplified. More precisely, we require a map
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{T}}:\mathbf{T}\to\mathbf{R}
which is weakly lower semicontinuous, coercive, vanishes at
0
, and satisfies: if
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{T}}(\mathbf{t}_{k})\xrightarrow{k}0
, then
\mathbf{t}_{k}\xrightarrow{k}0
in
\mathbf{T}
. In essence, these are the same requirements as before, and one may again take it to be an appropriate norm on
\mathbf{T}
.

The Rockafellian functional
\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}:{\rm Z}\times\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{T}\to\mathbf{R}
now reads (cf. (

))

(5.4)

\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}(z,\gamma;\mathbf{t})=f(z,\gamma)+\kappa\,{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z,\gamma;\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{t}))\,\rho]+\theta_{\varepsilon}\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{T}}(\mathbf{t})+\imath_{\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}}(\mathbf{t})\,.

An important observation is that, in this case, the construction of a family of subspaces of finite measure (cf. discussion after Assumption
4.1
) is not needed. Indeed, we notice that

{\mathcal{I}}_{\varepsilon}[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z,\gamma;\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{t}))\rho]\leq{\mathcal{I}}[\mathscr{J}_{+,\delta}(z,\gamma;\omega_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{t}))\rho]=\int_{\Xi}(g(s(\xi+\eta_{\varepsilon}(\xi)+\mathbf{t}(\xi)))-\gamma)_{+,\delta}\,d\rho\,.

This, according to Assumption
4.3
and the discussion following it, shows that the integral is finite. Furthermore, in a situation where Assumption
4.2
is satisfied in
\Xi
(for instance, when
\Xi
is finite-dimensional), there is no need whatsoever to consider the sequence
(\Xi_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
. Namely, Assumption
4.1
is not needed.

(\eta_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
such that
\eta_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}
, for each
\mathbf{T}_{\varepsilon}>0
. Assume that

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\theta_{\varepsilon}=+\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\,\theta_{\varepsilon}\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{T}}(I-\eta_{\varepsilon})=0\,.

Then, under the previously discussed assumptions, we have weak-strong
\Gamma
-convergence of
\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}
to
\Phi^{\delta}
.

Proof.

The proof is essentially identical to that of Theorem
4.3
, with the difference that now, as a result of the absence of corruption to the distribution, the reasoning leading to (
4.18
) is greatly simplified. The rest of the proof works verbatim.
∎

6.
Numerical examples

We consider two stochastic optimization problems constrained by elliptic boundary value problems.
In each case, we model a different corruption scenario. In the first, samples from a normally distributed random vector
are corrupted by introducing samples with increased variance. In the second, a truncated
exponential random variable is corrupted by increasing the likelihood of tail events.

6.1.
Example 1

For our first numerical example, we consider the stochastic optimal control problem

(6.1)

\min_{z\in L^{2}(0,1)}\varphi(z),\qquad\varphi(z)=\operatorname{CVaR}_{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|s(\xi,z)-u_{\star}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}\right)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|z\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}

where
u(\cdot,\xi):=s(\xi,z)
is the solution operator to the one-dimensional boundary value problem

(6.2)

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{dx}\Big(a(x,\xi)\frac{d}{dx}u(x,\xi)\Big)=z(x)

\displaystyle,\qquad(x,\xi)\in(0,1)\times\Xi

\displaystyle u(0,\xi)=u(1,\xi)=0

,

The diffusion coefficient is given by

a(x,\xi)=e^{\sigma\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}\sin(x/\sqrt{\lambda_{k}})\xi_{k}},\qquad\lambda_{k}=\frac{4}{(2k-1)^{2}\pi^{2}},

where each
\xi_{k}
is independent and normally distributed with mean-zero and unit variance. Here the argument of the exponential
function is a truncated Kosambi-Karhunen-Loéve expansion
[
41
, Theorem 2.3]

for a rescaled Brownian motion on the interval
(0,1)
.
We take
\sigma=0.4
and consider
d=50
expansion terms.
For the control regularization term in the objective function, we take
\alpha=10^{-5}
, while
the target function
u_{\star}(x)=1
.

To numerically solve the optimization problem, we discretize the expectation value in the objective
function with a sample-average approximation using
N=5000
samples.
Similar to the numerical experiments reported in

[
10
, Section 5.2]
, we employ
the “nested quantile estimation” (NQE)
strategy described in
[
10
, Algorithm 2]
, which
amounts to an alternating coordinate descent algorithm
[
53
, Section 1.J]
on the
variables
z
and
\gamma
.
After mollifying the function
(x)_{+}
with

(6.3)

(x)^{\delta}_{+}:=x+\delta\ln(1+e^{-x/\delta})

(from
[
34
, Eqn. (4.6)]
), the optimization problem for fixed
\gamma
becomes

\min_{z\in L^{2}(0,1)}\left(\frac{1}{1-\beta}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\|s(\xi^{(i)},z)-u_{\star}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}-\gamma\Big)_{+}^{\delta}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|z\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}\right),

which is numerically solved using SciPy’s implementation of the L-BFGS-B algorithm with the default settings
[
60
]
.
In turn, for fixed
z
, the optimization problem

\min_{\gamma\in\mathbf{R}}\left(\gamma+\frac{1}{1-\beta}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\|s(\xi^{(i)},z)-u_{\star}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}-\gamma\Big)_{+}^{\delta}\right)

is solved using the bisection method to find the critical point. Note that this latter optimization problem
is relatively cheap; the bulk of the computational expense requires less than a single objective function calculation.
The NQE algorithm terminates when the absolute difference in optimal
\gamma
values between successive iterations is smaller
than a specified tolerance, which we set to
10^{-4}
. In all numerical examples, the mollification parameter

\delta=10^{-3}
.

The state equation (
6.2
) and the corresponding adjoint equation are discretized with
a second-order finite difference method with uniform grid spacing
h=1/128
; the resulting tridiagonal
systems are solved with SciPy’s banded direct solver.
All
L^{2}
norms are approximated with the composite trapezoidal rule.

To introduce data corruption into the stochastic optimization problem
(
6.1
), we select the first
M\in\mathbf{N}
samples
(M<N)

and increase their variance through the map
\xi\mapsto 5\xi
, which amounts to setting

\sigma=2
for those samples.

For the values
\beta\in\{0.1,0.5,0.9\}
, we
solve both the uncorrupted version of (
6.1
)
and the corrupted version at various corruption levels,
defined as

(6.4)

\text{\% corruption}:=100\cdot M/N.

Figure
1
shows that even with only 1% of the samples corrupted,
the optimal control
z^{\ast}_{\rm corrupted}
is significantly different
than the uncorrupted one
z^{\ast}_{\rm true}
for all values of
\beta
. The differences
grow with increasing corruption.

Example 1: Optimal controls for the true, uncorrupted problem, as well as for the corrupted

To recover
z^{\ast}_{\rm true}
in the presence of data corruption, we define the Rockafellian

(6.5)

\Phi(z,t):=\min_{\gamma\in\mathbf{R}}\left(\gamma+\frac{1}{1-\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(\frac{1}{N}+t_{i}\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{2}\|s(\xi^{(i)},z)-u_{\star}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,1)}-\gamma\Big)^{\delta}_{+}\right)+\theta\|t\|_{1}+\iota_{\Delta}(p+t).

where
t\in\mathbf{R}^{N}
,
\theta>0
,
\Delta
is the set of probability vectors, and every component of
p\in\mathbf{R}^{N}
equals
1/N
.
Following the numerical experiments in
[
52
]
and
[
3
]
,
the
l_{1}
norm is chosen for the perturbation variable
t
because of its well known sparsity-promoting property.
We also borrow from
[
52
]
and
[
3
]
the numerical optimization strategy
to minimize
\Phi
, which involves an alternating-direction (ADI) heuristic in which we first fix
t=0
and compute

z^{\ast}\in\mathop{{\mathrm{argmin}}}\nolimits\Phi(z,0)
using the NQE described above. Using the result, we then solve the linear program

t^{\ast}\in\mathop{{\mathrm{argmin}}}\nolimits\Phi(z^{\ast},t)
using SciPy’s implementation of the simplex method. This process is repeated until the
absolute
l_{1}
distance between successive
t^{\ast}
values is smaller than
10^{-3}
.

The constraint that
p+t\in\Delta
implies the
pointwise bounds
-1/N\leq t_{i}\leq 1-1/N
for each
1\leq i\leq N
. As in the
numerical examples in
[
3
]
, in practice we find better results after imposing the tighter bounds
-1/N\leq t_{i}\leq 1/N
,
as this prevents the linear program solver from “greedily” identifying a small handful of samples
\xi^{(i)}
at which

\Big(\frac{1}{2}\|s(\xi^{(i)}-u_{\star}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}\Big)^{\delta}_{+}

is smallest and then subsequently removing the other samples (including potentially uncorrupted, “clean” samples).

(6.8)

\displaystyle-\Delta u(x,\xi)+v(\xi)\cdot\nabla u(x,\xi)=f(x)+z(x)

\displaystyle,\qquad(x,\xi)\in\Omega\times\Xi

\displaystyle u(x,\xi)=0

\displaystyle,\qquad(x,\xi)\in\partial\Omega\times\Xi,

where

equals 20. The target function in the objective function

.

\xi

7.
Conclusions

We introduce a framework for risk-averse optimization that is robust to ambiguities
in the true form of the underlying problem’s probability distribution.
The framework combines problem relaxation techniques (an example of DOO) with
the Conditional Value-at-Risk (an example of DRO), although other coherent measures of risk
could easily be incorporated.
The work advances previously established
theoretical foundations for risk-neutral problems with strengthened
\Gamma
-convergence
results, novel existence theorems (which can accommodate infinite-dimensional
probability spaces), and first-order optimality criteria.

Numerical experiments on model stochastic PDECO problems illustrate the benefit of the “blended”
DOO and DRO approach. By perturbing
both
the parameter
\beta
in

\operatorname{CVaR}_{\beta}
and the regularization parameter
\theta
inherent to Rockafellians,
decision makers can explore the sensitivity of optimal control strategies to changing
levels of risk aversion
and
problem corruption.
This is consistent with the notion that, on the whole, optimization technology is
more a tool for identifying possibilities than an “oracle” for producing a definitive
answer
[
53
, Preface]
.

One natural extension for future research is to consider Rockafellian relaxation
for state-constrained optimization problems in the presence of distributional
ambiguity. Another important direction is to develop theory for how to best optimize
the bivariate objective functions that arise in Rockafellian relaxation.
Lastly, the technique can be used in a variety of applications, for example,
topology optimization, structural weakness identification, and trajectory
optimization.
