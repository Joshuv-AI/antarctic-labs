Accurately modeling resting-brain functional connectivity using hypergraph neural field-Fourier deep neural network
Authors: Jichao Ma, Jiebin Luo, Dandan Liu, Xi’an Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRevealing the relationship between resting-state human brain structure and function is a central question for understanding brain cognition and neuropsychiatric disorders, while the problem still remains largely unanswered. Graph diffusion (GD) model predicts functional connectivity (FC) from structural connectivity (SC), which makes the assumption that current signals of each brain region conform to exponential decay once activated. However, information interaction between brain regions is ignored, resulting in none of negative correlations in FC. To overcome the challenge, we establish hypergraph neural field (HNF) model to depict information interaction between excitatory and inhibitory neurons between brain regions. Then, taking excitatory membrane potentials, calculate Pearson correlation coefficients between brain regions to get interactive connectivity (IC). Although the mean of Pearson correlation coefficients between IC and FC is relatively low (0.4), it is slightly higher than that achieved by the graph neural field model, and IC exhibits a non-negligible amount of negative correlations. Furthermore, we propose hypergraph neural field-Fourier deep neural network (HNF-FDNN) model, in which Fourier deep neural network (FDNN) integrates low- and high-frequency components spectral information, thereby enhancing HNF model in the representation of FC and significantly improving prediction accuracy. We test HNF-FDNN model on Human Connectome Project S1200 release including 90 regions of interests, which contains 100 train subjects and 100 test subjects. The mean value of Pearson correlation coefficients reaches 0.8168 in the 100 test subjects, exceeding GD model (0.5499). Meanwhile, HNF-FDNN model shows the stronger robustness and stability. The study emphasizes the potential of combining the brain activity signals and machine learning methods for modeling FC. It is beneficial for exploring formation of cognition and mechanism of neuropsychiatric disorders.Keywords: Structural connectivity, Functional connectivity, Hypergraph neural field, Negative correlation, Deep neural networkSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Neuroscience

Introduction

The brain consists of numerous interconnected regions that form a complex network, supporting a wide range of functions through dynamic interactions1,2. Nevertheless, the underlying mechanism by which such a relatively static anatomical structure gives rise to diverse functional processes remains elusive. In recent years, predicting FC from SC has become a central topic in neuroscience, offering insights that enhance theoretical models and guide surgical strategies for the diagnosis and treatment of neurological disorders. Moreover, this line of research introduces new perspectives for exploring mental diseases such as schizophrenia3–5, epilepsy6–8, and Alzheimer’s disease9–11.

In recent years, numerous strategies have been developed to investigate the SC–FC relationship. Although ample evidence indicates that structurally linked brain regions often display strong FC12,13, a puzzling phenomenon persists: certain brain regions exhibit strong FC even when their SC is weak or absent. To address this discrepancy, researchers have drawn on complex network theory, employing biologically meaningful network metrics to describe neural signal transmission and to construct topological models for estimating FC14–18. For instance, Sporns and colleagues identified graph-theoretic measures to account for FC, such as node degree19,20, search information and path transitivity21,22, as well as shortest paths23. However, these correlations provide only partial insight into FC and fall short of fully explaining how FC emerges from SC. At the same time, neural mass models have been used to simulate FC24–27. These approaches employ differential equations—such as dynamic mean-field model, the Wilson-Cowan equations and Hodgkin-Huxley model—to describe the collective dynamics of neuronal populations 28–31. Nonetheless, they neglect inter-module information transfer and often require computationally expensive iterative optimization, which limits both efficiency and accuracy. In addition, machine learning approaches have attempted to directly map SC to FC using matrix transformations32–34 or spectral mappings35–37, where FC is modeled as a combination of linear and nonlinear functions of SC along with higher-order connectivity weights38–42. Although these methods achieve competitive predictive performance, they do not clarify the underlying biological processes and generally require retraining when applied to different brain connectivity datasets. Furthermore, graph diffusion (GD) model43,44 is proposed by Abdelnour, et al, in fact that the eigenvalues of graph Laplacian of SC have an exponential relationship with the eigenvalues of empirical FC. It has shown potential advantages in predicting FC with few model parameters. However, it only predicts positive correlations between brain regions.

To remedy the above limitation, we integrate neural mass models with machine learning models. Selen, et al.45, derive graph neural field model, inspired by the Wilson-Cowan equations, to illustrate the interplay between brain regions from the excitatory-inhibitory balance, which demonstrates underlying mechanism of self-organization of connectome harmonics. Whereas, hypergraph can connect two vertices without anatomical connectivity, which reflects the indirect connection. Thus, we derive hypergraph neural field model to accurately depict excitatory and inhibitory membrane potentials in each brain region. Then, taking excitatory membrane potentials, calculate Pearson correlation coefficients to get IC. Although a large number of negative connections occur in IC, it is obtained from excitatory membrane potentials and has a significant difference from FC obtained by Blood Oxygenation Level-dependent. Thus, we introduce Fourier deep neural network to address the cross-modal transformation from neural activity to hemodynamic responses.

Deep neural network (DNN), characterised by multiple hidden layers, are widely employed for complex pattern recognition and predictive modelling tasks. Compared with shallow neural networks, the deeper architecture of DNN allows them to capture more intricate feature representations, thereby enhancing both accuracy and generalisation. Recently, graph signal processing methods designed for time-varying datasets on graph structures have been increasingly applied in brain network studies. In particular, graph Fourier transform has proven highly effective in elucidating mental disorders and the organisation of brain networks. Building on this, we explore Fourier deep neural network, which incorporate Fourier feature mappings composed of sine and cosine functions to expand the representational power of DNN. This approach not only mitigates spectral bias but also enables the model to learn high-frequency components with greater efficiency.

Accordingly, we propose hypergraph neural field-Fourier deep neural network (HNF-FDNN) model to predict FC from SC. Experimental results, comprising 90 regions of interests, reveal that the proposed HNF-FDNN model outperforms GD model, while the FC predicted by HNF-FDNN model exhibits strong stability.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose HNF-FDNN model for accurate prediction of FC from SC. Firstly, HNF model, derived from the Wilson–Cowan equations describing information interaction between excitatory and inhibitory neural populations, provides excitatory membrane potentials that is correlated to form IC. Then, FDNN is employed to enhance frequency expression and characterize the mapping from IC to FC.

HNF model extends graph neural field, which constructs hypergraph network to capture latent interaction between brain regions and more accurately represent membrane potentials. In addition, FDNN can compensate for spectral shifts while preserving high-frequency information during connectivity analysis. Experiments, in the empirical HCP S1200 release, demonstrate that HNF-FDNN model significantly outperforms GD model in predicting FC. Meanwhile, comparison with a recent SC-to-FC prediction approach further confirms the advantage of the proposed framework. Zalesky et al.47 demonstrated that a DNN predicts individual FC from SC with r = 0.5 and σ = 0.1 compared to empirical measurements.

HNF-FDNN model achieves a satisfactory performance in predicting FC, but several issues remain. Firstly, HNF model involve numerous parameters that need to be tuned during computation, making it difficult to obtain the optimal solution. Secondly, HNF-FDNN model establishes an IC–FC relationship that lacks clear physiological interpretation, and its generalizability to other datasets and parcellations remains unknown. These issues will be addressed in future work.

Methods

Connectome dataset

In this section, we conduct data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) S1200 release, which includes structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI), resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rsfMRI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) from 1,012 participants with complete imaging datasets48. Identically, all data is preprocessed using the Minimal Preprocessing Pipeline49, which involves correcting for spatial artefacts and distortions, reconstructing the cortical surface, aligning different modalities (including sMRI, rsfMRI, and DTI), and nonlinear transformation to MNI152 standard space.

Differently, for diffusion MRI data processing, we employ the fibre orientation distribution algorithm provided by MRtrix3 to perform probabilistic tractography for each participant50. Following white matter pathway reconstruction using this algorithm, the SC matrix is constructed for each participant, where each matrix element value corresponds to the number of fibre streamlines tracked between two different brain regions. Then, the rsfMRI data undergo additional preprocessing: (1) in 0.01–0.1 Hz, band-pass filtering to suppress scanner drift and high-frequency noise while retaining neural-relevant low-frequency fluctuations; (2) global signal regression for cortical signals to reduce systemic physiological artifacts; (3) temporal standardization (z-score normalization) of time series; (4) parcellation into 90 cortical regions using the Automated Anatomical Labeling atlas51; (5) concatenation of time series across four scanning sessions. The FC matrix is constructed by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients between the time series of all brain regions. The processed results are illustrated in Fig. 5.

The 90-region parcellation of the HCP dataset is widely used, as it provides a balanced spatial resolution that preserves large-scale network organization while reducing the complexity of SC–FC modeling and machine learning.

Negative functional connectivity remains a complex phenomenon and is generally considered to arise from a combination of neural and non-neural factors. On the one hand, negative correlations are often interpreted as reflecting functional segregation or competitive interactions between large-scale brain networks, such as the anti-correlated organization between the default mode network and task-positive networks. On the other hand, physiological noise (e.g., respiration and cardiac fluctuations), head motion, and global signal variations may also introduce widespread patterns of correlation or anti-correlation across the brain.

Preprocessing procedures, particularly global signal regression (GSR), can substantially influence the presence and magnitude of negative correlations. Although previous studies have shown that GSR may alter the distribution of correlations and, under certain conditions, introduce negative components, negative correlations cannot be solely attributed to GSR. The global signal itself contains a mixture of motion-related, physiological, and potentially neural contributions, and its removal or retention can affect the resulting connectivity structure. Moreover, stable patterns of negative connectivity have been observed under different preprocessing strategies, including those without GSR, suggesting that negative correlations likely emerge from multiple interacting factors rather than from a single preprocessing step.

In light of these considerations, GSR is adopted in this study. First, it remains one of the most widely used preprocessing strategies in functional connectivity analysis and is effective in reducing global non-neural noise, such as motion- and respiration-related signals. Second, under this commonly used framework, negative correlations constitute an important characteristic of empirical functional connectivity. Therefore, a model capable of capturing both positive and negative correlations is essential for a more comprehensive characterization of brain functional network organization.

Proposed in the 1970s by Hugh Wilson and Jack Cowan, the model uses coupled nonlinear differential equations to capture how the average activity levels of neural populations evolve over time. By incorporating feedback loops and sigmoidal activation function, the Wilson–Cowan framework explains phenomena such as oscillations, pattern formation, and stable states of neural activity. It has become a fundamental tool for studying brain rhythms, neural circuits, and cognitive processes.

Fourier deep neural network

By computing Pearson correlation coefficients of excitatory membrane potentials between brain regions, IC is obtained. Although there is a certain degree of correlations between IC and FC, discrepancies between the two still exist.

In recent years, machine learning methods have been widely adopted for modeling complex data relationship, demonstrating notable improvements in accuracy and robustness. DNN, as a representative approach, can automatically learn nonlinear mapping from large-scale datasets and captures intricate patterns without manual feature selecting. It also provides high flexibility and scalability, with performance improving as more data available. Moreover, hierarchical feature extraction enhances accuracy and robustness, facilitating generalization across related tasks. Prior studies have shown their superior performance in image recognition, speech analysis and natural language processing. Therefore, we employ DNN to infer FC from IC.
