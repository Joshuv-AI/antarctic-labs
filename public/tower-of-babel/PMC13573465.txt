Body mass index, nutritional knowledge, and eating attitudes in dancer and non-dancer students
Authors: Caifang Qiu, Hui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis cross-sectional comparative study examined differences in body mass index (BMI), nutritional knowledge, and eating behaviors between dancer and non-dancer university students. Given the physical and aesthetic demands of dance, understanding potential differences in health-related characteristics in this population is of particular importance.MethodsSeventy female undergraduates (35 dancers and 35 non-dancers, aged 17–22 years) were recruited. Height, weight, and BMI were measured using the InBody 970 analyzer. Nutritional knowledge was assessed using the Chinese version of the General Nutrition Knowledge Questionnaire (C-GNKQ), and disordered eating risk was evaluated using the Eating Attitudes Test-26 (EAT-26)©. Group differences were analyzed using independent t-tests, with effect sizes (Cohen’s d) calculated to estimate the magnitude of differences.ResultsDancers were significantly taller than non-dancers (P = 0.003, d = 0.74) and had a lower BMI (P = 0.023, d = 0.82). No statistically significant difference was observed in overall C-GNKQ scores (P = 0.128, d = 0.37), although dancers scored higher in the dietary knowledge subscale (P = 0.037, d = 0.52). EAT-26© scores were higher in dancers, but the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.147, d = 0.35). Additional Spearman correlation analyses revealed no significant associations among BMI, C-GNKQ scores, and EAT-26© scores (P > 0.05).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13104-026-07932-1.Keywords: Dancer, BMI, GNKQ, EAT-26©, Eating behaviors

Introduction

Dance is a distinctive form of artistic expression that requires a high level of physical fitness, technical skill, and aesthetic awareness. The discipline places considerable physical demands on performers, often requiring dancers to maintain a specific physique to meet both technical and aesthetic standards [1]. These demands create a complex relationship between physical health, nutritional knowledge, and eating behaviors, highlighting the importance of understanding how these factors interact, particularly in light of growing concerns regarding eating disorders within the dance community [2].

Dancers typically undergo intensive training programs that emphasize strength, flexibility, and a lean body composition. Within such environments, weight management often becomes a central concern, which may lead to restrictive dietary practices aimed at meeting professional and aesthetic expectations. Many professional dancers report dissatisfaction with their bodies despite maintaining a lean physique and a normal Body Mass Index (BMI), frequently expressing a desire to be thinner. Consequently, dancers are considered a population at elevated risk for the development of eating disorders [3]. Previous studies have also shown that dancers commonly experience body image concerns and may adopt unhealthy eating behaviors in pursuit of the ideal ballerina appearance [4, 5].

BMI serves as a widely used indicator of body composition and is commonly employed to classify individuals as underweight, normal weight, overweight, or obese [6]. Previous studies have consistently reported that dancers tend to exhibit lower BMI values and reduced body fat compared with non-dancers and other athletes [7]. For example, research on adolescent dancers reported an average BMI of 17.9 ± 2.2 kg/m2, accompanied by significantly lower body fat mass compared with a control group [8]. Similarly, a study of young female professional dancers found an average BMI of 19.79 kg/m2 and an average body fat percentage of 15.92% [9]. These characteristics likely reflect both training demands and aesthetic expectations in dance [10]. However, a lower BMI does not necessarily indicate better health and may sometimes be associated with unhealthy weight management practices or nutritional deficiencies [6, 11]. Therefore, a more comprehensive perspective is needed to evaluate dancers’ health, in which nutritional knowledge represents an important additional dimension.

Nutritional knowledge plays an important role in shaping eating behaviors among dancers and may influence both performance and recovery. The General Nutrition Knowledge Questionnaire (GNKQ) is a commonly used instrument for assessing dietary awareness [6]. Although dancers often demonstrate some level of nutritional knowledge, evidence suggests that this knowledge does not always translate into healthier eating practices [2, 12]. For instance, collegiate dancers have been reported to show lower awareness of eating disorders and to participate less frequently in nutrition-related education compared with administrators and coaches [13]. Despite familiarity with dietary recommendations, many dancers continue to adopt unhealthy eating patterns due to pressures to maintain a specific body image [4, 14]. Moreover, dancers have been reported to have approximately three times the risk of developing eating disorders compared with non-dancers, with an overall prevalence of 12.0% [15, 16]. These findings highlight the complexity of eating behaviors in physically demanding populations and suggest that knowledge alone may be insufficient to promote healthy dietary habits.

Although previous studies have documented lower BMI, body image concerns, and an elevated risk of disordered eating among dancers, relatively few studies have simultaneously evaluated body-related indicators, nutritional knowledge, and eating attitudes within the same population. Moreover, most existing studies have focused on professional or elite dancers, whereas evidence from university dance students remains limited. A more integrated assessment of these factors may provide additional insight into the health-related characteristics of this population.

Consequently, this study aimed to compare the BMI, nutritional knowledge, and eating attitudes of dancer and non-dancer university students, as well as to explore the relationships among these variables. It seeks to provide a more comprehensive understanding of health-related characteristics among female dance students.

Methods

This cross-sectional comparative study recruited 70 female undergraduates through voluntary self-enrollment via an on-campus online platform, dividing them into two equal groups: 35 dancers and 35 non-dancers. Participants were recruited through voluntary self-enrollment via an on-campus online platform.

This design was selected to compare health-related characteristics between groups within a natural educational setting. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Normal University. Before enrollment, all participants were informed about the study’s objectives, procedures, and the measures to ensure confidentiality.

All participants were from the same academic year and were of similar age. The dancer group consisted of students majoring in Chinese dance (classical and folk dance) who had received systematic dance training as part of their university curriculum. In contrast, the non-dancer group comprised students from other majors who had not received any formal dance training. None of the participants had a background in professional training for other sports. Aside from regular physical education classes at the university, participants did not engage in structured athletic training or consistent fitness programs. Participants were generally healthy and free from known chronic diseases at the time of the study.

Height, weight, and BMI were measured using the InBody 970 (InBody Co., Ltd., Cerritos, California, USA). Although the InBody analyzer can provide detailed body composition measures, BMI was selected as the primary indicator due to its widespread use in epidemiological and comparative studies. The Chinese version of the General Nutrition Knowledge Questionnaire (C-GNKQ) was used to evaluate dietary awareness [17]. Section A assessed participants’ understanding of the nutritional advice typically provided by experts. Section B evaluated their knowledge of food classification, specifically whether participants could accurately identify foods belonging to different groups. Section C examined food choices and required participants to respond according to the information provided rather than their personal preferences. Section D assessed participants’ awareness of health problems or diseases related to nutrition.

In addition, the Eating Attitudes Test-26 (EAT-26©) [18] was used to assess the risk of disordered eating, with scores greater than 20 indicating a higher risk. Dancers were also asked specific questions about their lifestyle and training, including the frequency and nature of dance-related injuries sustained during the previous year. Detailed descriptions of the procedures are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Data collection was conducted over one week, with participants completing the questionnaires in a quiet setting under the supervision of a researcher. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics and independent t-tests to compare group differences between dancers and non-dancers. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Effect sizes for group differences were calculated using Cohen’s d. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 [19]. With 35 participants in each group, an alpha level of 0.05, and a medium effect size (d = 0.5), the achieved statistical power was 0.84. Given the exploratory nature of this study, this sample size was considered sufficient to detect moderate group differences.

Results

Anthropometric characteristics

As shown in Table 1, dancers were significantly taller and exhibited a lower BMI than non-dancers. The difference in BMI was associated with a large effect size (d = 0.82), indicating a substantial difference between groups. Differences in current weight were not statistically significant, although non-dancers tended to have higher values. Dancers also reported a significantly lower minimum weight (P = 0.028), while no significant differences were observed in highest or ideal weight. These findings indicate that dancers generally exhibit a leaner anthropometric profile compared with non-dancers. Nutritional knowledge and eating attitudes

The GNKQ results showed no statistically significant difference in overall nutritional knowledge scores between dancers and non-dancers (Table 2). Among the GNKQ subcomponents, dancers scored significantly higher in specific dietary knowledge (Score-C) (P = 0.037); the observed difference corresponded to a moderate effect size (d = 0.52). No statistically significant differences were observed between dancers and non-dancers for the Score-A, Score-B, and Score-D. The statistically significant difference observed in Score-C indicates higher dietary knowledge in this specific domain among dancers.

Nutritional knowledge and eating attitudes in dancers and non-dancers

Regarding eating behavior, dancers showed a higher mean EAT-26© score than non-dancers, but this difference was not statistically significant (Table 2). Analysis of specific eating patterns indicated that 37.14% of dancers reported binge eating episodes 2–3 times per month, compared with 14.29% of non-dancers. Additionally, 17.14% of dancers reported binge eating once per month, compared with 25.71% of non-dancers. More frequent binge eating (2–6 times per week) was reported by 5.71% of dancers and 2.86% of non-dancers. Overall, no significant differences were observed in general nutritional knowledge or disordered eating risk between groups, although differences emerged in specific knowledge domains. These findings might be relevant for designing targeted nutrition education strategies in dance training settings.

In addition, Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to examine the relationships among BMI, GNKQ scores, and EAT-26 scores. No significant correlations were observed between BMI and GNKQ scores (r = 0.015, P = 0.905), BMI and EAT-26 scores (r = 0.093, P = 0.447), or GNKQ scores and EAT-26 scores (r = 0.112, P = 0.356).

Eating behaviors

In this study, dancers were more likely to report extreme weight management behaviors, including the use of laxatives, diet pills, or excessive exercise (Table 3). Most participants in both groups reported never engaging in self-induced vomiting for weight control (88.57% in each group). Among dancers, 8.57% reported vomiting once per month, compared with 2.86% of non-dancers. Additionally, 2.86% of participants in each group reported vomiting 2–3 times per month. Weekly vomiting was reported by 5.71% of non-dancers but was not reported among dancers.

Regarding the use of laxatives, diet pills, or diuretics, 80% of dancers and 91.43% of non-dancers reported never using these methods. Occasional use (once per month) was reported by 8.57% of dancers and 2.86% of non-dancers. Weekly use was reported by 2.86% of dancers and 5.71% of non-dancers.

For exercise aimed at weight control, 28.57% of dancers reported engaging in more than 60 min of exercise once per month, compared with 31.43% of non-dancers. Additionally, 25.71% of dancers reported exercising 2–3 times per month for weight control, compared with 11.43% of non-dancers. Weekly exercise for weight control was reported by 17.14% of dancers and 8.57% of non-dancers, suggesting relatively higher levels of physical activity reported by dancers.

Regarding weight loss, 14.29% of dancers and 22.86% of non-dancers reported losing 20 pounds or more at least once in the past six months, whereas 85.71% of dancers and 77.14% of non-dancers reported never experiencing such weight loss. Few participants from either group engaged in daily or multiple times weekly exercise or significant weight loss efforts, suggesting that while both groups showed interest in weight management, it was not performed excessively.

Daily routine and training patterns

In addition, data on the exercise routines of dancers were collected (Supplementary Materials, Tables S1 and S2). On average, dancers reported 6.88 h of sleep per night and had a mean dance experience of 8.8 years. Weekly training time averaged 16.76 h, supplemented by 2.26 h of non-dance physical activities. The most commonly reported injuries were joint sprains and muscle strains, each affecting 40% of dancers. Other injuries included soft tissue injuries (28.57%), ligament strains (14.29%), intercostal nerve cramps or disc problems (14.29%), chronic inflammation (8.57%), fractures (2.86%), and dislocations (2.86%).

Discussion

Our study compared BMI, nutritional knowledge, and eating attitudes between female university dancers and non-dancers, revealing important differences in anthropometric and behavioral characteristics. Dancers were significantly taller than non-dancers and maintained a lower BMI. In addition, dancers reported a lower minimum body weight, which may reflect both selection processes in dance training and aesthetic demands emphasizing a lean body composition. Although differences in current, highest, and ideal weights were not statistically significant, the consistently leaner profile of dancers aligns with previous reports showing that ballet and contemporary dancers tend to maintain lower BMI and body fat compared with non-dancer peers [20]. While such a physique may benefit performance, it also raises concerns regarding energy availability, menstrual health, and musculoskeletal vulnerability.

The GNKQ results showed no statistically significant difference in overall nutritional knowledge scores between dancers and non-dancers. However, dancers scored significantly higher in the dietary knowledge subscale (Score-C), while no differences were observed in other subscales. These findings suggest that group differences in nutritional knowledge might be domain-specific rather than general. The mean EAT-26© score was also higher in dancers, while the difference was not statistically significant, and therefore no group difference in disordered eating risk can be concluded, At the same time, the absence of significant correlations among BMI, nutritional knowledge, and EAT-26 scores, this interpretation should be considered exploratory and warrants further investigation. The coexistence of similar overall nutritional knowledge levels and non-significant differences in disordered eating risk may suggest that nutritional knowledge alone is insufficient to explain variability in eating-related behaviors. This discrepancy might be explained by the influence of sociocultural and performance-related pressures in dance environments, where maintaining a lean physique is often prioritized [21, 22]. These observations may also be interpreted within the context of aesthetic-sport-related sociocultural pressures, where body appearance is often closely linked to perceived performance and professional success. Such pressures may contribute to increased body image concerns and influence eating-related behaviors independently of nutritional knowledge. Under such conditions, dietary behaviors might be shaped more strongly by body image concerns and external expectations than by formal nutritional knowledge, a pattern that has also been observed in athletic populations where eating behaviors are closely linked to psychological factors [23–25]. This might help explain why increased exposure to dietary information does not necessarily translate into healthier eating practices among dancers. This finding extends the existing literature by showing that differences in nutritional knowledge do not necessarily correspond to differences in eating behaviors, highlighting a potential disconnect between knowledge and behavior.

Specific eating behaviors further illustrate this risk. Dancers reported higher engagement in binge eating episodes, occasional use of laxatives, diet pills, or diuretics was also more frequent among dancers. Moreover, 17.14% of dancers exercised more than 60 min per day for weight control once per week compared with 8.57% of non-dancers. These behavioral patterns might be influenced by a combination of aesthetic expectations, performance-related demands, and internalized body image pressures. In dance training contexts, frequent feedback on body shape and weight might reinforce restrictive or compensatory eating behaviors. Over time, such pressures might contribute to the adoption of maladaptive weight control strategies, even in individuals with adequate nutritional knowledge. Similar patterns have been reported in international studies showing a higher prevalence of risky eating behaviors among dancers [26].

Lifestyle and recovery-related factors provide important context for interpreting the observed patterns and might act as modifying variables that influence the translation of nutritional knowledge into eating behaviors, thereby contributing to variability in health-related outcomes among dancers. Dancers averaged 6.88 h of sleep per night while engaging in approximately 16.76 h of weekly dance training and reported a high incidence of joint sprains (40%) and muscle strains (40%). This combination of limited recovery, heavy physical load, and potential undernutrition might increase the risk of fatigue, injury, and low energy availability, thereby placing additional stress on both physical and psychological health. Implementing recovery management strategies, including adequate sleep, training load monitoring, and injury prevention programs, might help mitigate these risks among dancers. Although these variables were not formally included in statistical models, they might help explain variability in eating behaviors and health-related outcomes among dancers.

In summary, compared with non-dancers, dancers exhibited lower BMI and minimum body weight and scored higher in one domain of nutritional knowledge. Although no significant differences were observed in overall nutritional knowledge or EAT-26© scores, dancers reported a higher frequency of certain weight-control behaviors. These findings provide a descriptive comparison of anthropometric characteristics, nutritional knowledge, and eating attitudes in female university dancers and non-dancers.

Taken together, this study provides an integrated comparison of body composition, nutritional knowledge, and eating behaviors in dancer and non-dancer populations, highlighting a potential disconnect between knowledge and behavior that might not be captured by traditional assessments focusing on a single domain. These observations might have implications for health management in this population, particularly in addressing the mismatch between knowledge and behavior. From a practical perspective, interventions should extend beyond traditional nutrition education to incorporate psychological support and body image management strategies. Reducing excessive emphasis on appearance-based standards within training environments might also be critical in promoting healthier behavioral patterns. The findings of this study should be interpreted with caution given the cross-sectional design and relatively small sample size. Future longitudinal and intervention-based studies are needed to better understand the relationships among BMI, nutritional knowledge, and eating behaviors and to determine whether improvements in nutritional knowledge translate into meaningful behavioral changes over time.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Participants were recruited from a single university and consisted exclusively of female students, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The relatively small sample size may also reduce statistical power, particularly for detecting differences in behavioral outcomes. In addition, potential confounding factors, including dietary intake, physical activity levels, socioeconomic background, prior dieting history, and menstrual status, were not assessed or controlled for, which may have influenced the observed outcomes.

Some variables were assessed using self-reported questionnaires, which may be subject to reporting bias. Data collection was conducted over a short period, which may not fully capture variability in participants’ behaviors. Furthermore, dance majors may represent a specific group with distinct training backgrounds and body image expectations, potentially introducing selection bias.

The cross-sectional design of this study precludes causal inference and does not allow the prediction of future changes in BMI, nutritional knowledge, or eating behaviors. Consequently, the temporal relationships among these variables cannot be established. In addition, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied despite the analysis of multiple outcomes and subscales, which may increase the risk of type I error. Confidence intervals were not reported, which may limit the precision of the estimated effects.

Future studies employing larger and more diverse samples, as well as hypothesis-driven designs and advanced statistical models (e.g., regression or mediation analyses), are warranted to further explore the relationships between nutritional knowledge, eating behaviors, and related lifestyle factors.
