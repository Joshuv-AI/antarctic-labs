Reverse Diagnostic Thinking for Aortic Coarctation Using the Renal Artery Bilateral Parvus et Tardus as a Clue: A Case Report
Authors: Yan‐li Chen, Jin‐hua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn young patients with hypertension or stroke, the presence of bilateral parvus et tardus waveforms in the renal arteries should prompt reverse tracing to rule out aortic coarctation. This simple ultrasound clue enables early, non‐invasive screening and prevents missed diagnoses.Keywords: case report, cerebral infarction, coarctation of the aorta, diagnostic thinking, parvus‐tardus waveform, ultrasonography

Introduction

Coarctation of the aorta (CoA) is a congenital malformation of the aorta that often leads to secondary hypertension and is easily missed in adults because of insidious symptoms [1]. When CoA is first manifested as complications such as hypertension or even cerebral infarction, the core of clinical diagnosis is to identify the underlying aortic cause from these common symptoms. During this process, parvus‐tardus waveform in the renal artery spectrum becomes key clues that reveal distal hemodynamic alterations due to CoA [2]. This paper describes a case of adult CoA with hypertension and cerebral infarction onset and systematically demonstrates an efficient diagnostic path to derive the upstream cause from downstream effects by reverse tracing the diagnosis process of aortic lesions starting from parvus‐tardus waveform, which provides a reference thinking paradigm for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of such diseases [3, 4].

Case Presentation/Examination

The patient, a 32‐year‐old man, was admitted to the hospital for “sudden right limb weakness with slurred speech for 35 hours”. 34 h prior, he had weakness in the right limb, unsteady holding, delayed walking, slurred speech, difficulty in expression, loss of consciousness, headache, nausea, and vomiting. He had had uncontrolled hypertension with a maximum systolic blood pressure of more than 180 mmHg for 10 years prior. There was no family history of hypertension, early‐onset coronary heart disease, stroke, or congenital heart disease.

Physical examination revealed that the patient's vital signs were as follows: body temperature, 36.5°C; pulse, 80 beats/min; breathing, 18 breaths/min; and blood pressure, 172/100 mmHg for the upper limbs and 112/68 mmHg for the lower limbs. Laboratory test results after admission revealed that routine blood levels, coagulatory function, thyroid function, glycated hemoglobin levels, infectious marker levels, markers of the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system, and anticardiolipin antibody levels were not obviously abnormal.

Imaging examinations followed a reverse thought process:

In the morning of August 1, 2025, the patient underwent renal artery ultrasound examination, and the blood flow spectrum at the beginning of each renal artery showed parvus‐tardus waveform changes (Figure 1A,B); the acceleration was delayed, and the resistance index (RI) decreased. Further abdominal aortic ultrasound revealed low‐velocity, low‐resistance biphasic waves in the blood flow spectrum (Figure 2). Further two‐dimensional ultrasound examination of the descending aorta (suprasternal fossa section) revealed that the origin of the descending aorta was narrowed, with an inner diameter of 6.4 mm at the narrowest point (Figure 3A), high‐velocity turbulence with color Doppler (Figure 3B), and a spectral Doppler measuring peak flow velocity of 3.6 m/s and a pressure difference of 52 mmHg (Figure 3C). In the afternoon of August 1, 2025, the patient underwent thoracoabdominal CT angiography. This examination revealed limited severe stenosis of the lumen at the beginning of the descending aorta, with a diameter of approximately 5.8 mm at the narrowest point (Figure 4), local tortuosity, and an irregular canal wall.

Differences in imaging measurements: In this case, the inner diameter of the narrowest part of the descending aorta was 6.4 mm on ultrasound and 5.8 mm on CTA. A difference of about 0.6 mm between different imaging techniques is completely normal and acceptable. In particular, this is a minimal difference that cannot affect clinical management. Moreover, usually CT is more precise, exposes the patient to radiation, but it is necessary before interventional or surgical procedures to allow the surgeon or vascular radiologist to properly plan the treatment.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment Related to the Case)

The differential diagnosis considered essential hypertension, renovascular disease, and aortic coarctation. Investigations included renal artery Doppler ultrasound, abdominal aortic ultrasound, transthoracic echocardiography, and CT angiography. Treatment during hospitalization focused on acute cerebral infarction management, as surgical correction of CoA was deferred until neurological stabilization.

Given that the patient was in the acute phase of cerebral infarction, immediate aortic surgery was extremely risky. After multidisciplinary discussion, it was decided to prioritize secondary prevention of stroke and neurological rehabilitation. After his neurological function stabilized, the patient was transferred to a higher‐level hospital for further treatment on August 14, 2025.

Follow‐up diagnosis and treatment: We learned that the patient underwent thoracic aortic stent‐graft intraluminal isolation on August 26, 2025, after being transferred to the hospital. However, on the second day after surgery (August 27), a new right cerebral infarction and lower limb artery embolism occurred, and femoral artery embolectomy was urgently performed. This information came from the patient's family and the patient's condition during the follow‐up visit to the outpatient clinic of our hospital. Last follow‐up: The patient was discharged from the hospital in stable condition. At the last follow‐up visit in our hospital (September 9, 2025), his neurological function was restored compared with that before, but he still experienced sequelae such as weakness in the limbs, choking, and coughing after drinking water.

Discussion

It should be emphasized that the coarctation of the aorta in this case was diagnosed by ultrasound, not by routine physical examination, reflecting that the disease is prone to clinical miss, and the vigilance of the sonographer is crucial. For patients with suspected renal vascular hypertension, if Doppler ultrasound finds that the bilateral renal arteries have parvus‐tardus waveform, even if the stenosis is not directly detected, coarctation of the aorta needs to be included in the differential diagnosis, which helps to avoid missed diagnosis. Several previous reports have highlighted the value of the tardus‐parvus renal Doppler pattern in diagnosing aortic coarctation [5, 6, 7, 8]. At the same time, it is necessary to face the limitations of ultrasound: for example, diseases such as arteritis or distal aortic stenosis may produce similar waveforms, and factors such as obesity and intestinal gas may interfere with image quality. Therefore, comprehensive differentiation must be performed in combination with clinical information and other imaging tests [9]. It is also noteworthy that the aortic waveform in this case showed a low resistance index, which further supports the presence of a proximal stenosis (CoA) rather than intrinsic renal disease. This finding serves as an additional clue in the reverse diagnostic pathway.

CoA leads to cerebral infarction is not a single mechanism, but a combination of multiple pathological processes. The core mechanism is that long‐term uncontrolled upper limb hypertension can directly damage cerebral blood vessels, causing intracranial arteriole hyaline and microaneurysm formation. Hemodynamic disorders of the aortic arch (including local turbulence and abnormal pressure distribution) cause intracranial blood vessels to be in an abnormally hyperperfused state for a long time, which significantly increases the risk of intracranial aneurysm. The turbulence inherent in the coarctation site creates conditions for in situ thrombosis, and theoretically a detached embolus can lead to cerebral embolism. Although there is limited evidence of direct thromboembolism, it is a clear consensus that persistent hemodynamic abnormalities and vascular dysfunction are still common in patients after CoA repair, and that there are lifelong systemic cardiovascular and neurological risks [10, 11, 12]. The clinical manifestations of this patient are the result of the combination of the above mechanisms.

This case is a model of diagnostic thinking. Its success is not based on sophisticated technology but on a deep understanding of the pathophysiological changes in small slow waves following a series of purposeful and systematic imaging investigations. This kind of reverse thinking, from the “cause of the cause,” breaks the conventional model of headache treatment, showing how to start with a local functional abnormality, reason layer‐by‐layer like a detective, and finally reveal the global cause. This process (renal artery → abdominal aorta → descending aorta) can be considered a standardized pathway for screening for the cause of unexplained hypertension or stroke in all young people [13].

While ultrasound guides the diagnostic direction, CTA provides precise anatomical confirmation and preoperative evaluation. This demonstrates the superiority of combining functional screening (ultrasound) with anatomical confirmation (CTA) in modern imaging, which is used sequentially and complementarily in the diagnostic process [14].

The most important takeaway from this case is that the value of diagnostic ideas is sometimes greater than that of the diagnosis itself. Improving the awareness and ability of clinicians and sonographers to recognize parvus‐tardus waveform and initiate reverse tracking is key to improving the detection rate of treatable diseases such as CoA. We strongly recommend that this diagnostic process be performed in young people with stroke and refractory hypertension [15, 16, 17].
