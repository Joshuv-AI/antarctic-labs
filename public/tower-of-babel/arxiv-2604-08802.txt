Abstract
During disasters, cascading failures across power grids, communication networks, and social behavior amplify community fear and undermine cooperation. Existing cyber-physical-social (CPS) models simulate these coupled dynamics but lack mechanisms for active intervention. We extend the CPS resilience model of Valinejad and Mili (2023) with control channels for three agencies, communication, power, and emergency management, and formulate the resulting system as a three-player non-zero-sum differential game solved via online actor–critic reinforcement learning. Simulations based on Hurricane Harvey data show ∼ \sim 70% mean fear reduction with improved infrastructure recovery; cross-validation in the case of Hurricane Irma (without refitting) achieves ∼ \sim 50% fear reduction, confirming generalizability.



I
Introduction

When a major disaster strikes, physical infrastructure (power grids, hospitals), cyber systems (news outlets, social media), and human behavior (fear, cooperation) interact in tightly coupled feedback loops
[
13
,
12
]
. False news spreads much faster than factual content
[
18
,
8
]
, amplifying community fear and reducing compliance with evacuation orders
[
11
]
. These cascading cross-domain interactions are a central challenge in disaster resilience
[
4
]
.

I-A

Problem and Motivation

Existing resilience frameworks treat the physical, cyber, and social domains largely in isolation
[
12
]
. What is missing is a
unified and actionable
framework that (i) captures cross-domain feedback in real time and (ii) prescribes how response agencies should deploy resources. These form the general goals of this work. Towards this end,
Valinejad and Mili
[
16
]
developed a cyber-physical-social (CPS) model coupling ten community-level indicators into a system of coupled differential equations, validated against social-media text analysis and FEMA records (
R^{2}>0.8
). However, the model is
purely descriptive
in that it does not prescribe interventions.

I-B

Proposed Approach

We close this gap by adding an
active control layer
to the CPS model. Three disaster-response agencies—a
communication agency
(counter-messaging and risk communication), a
power utility
(crew dispatch and grid restoration), and an
EMS agency
(ambulance, shelter, and supply deployment)—each choose a resource-deployment level, forming a three-player
non-zero-sum (NZS) differential game

[
2
,
5
]
. The optimal policies satisfy coupled Hamilton–Jacobi (HJ) equations, which are intractable in closed form for the nonlinear CPS dynamics. We therefore adopt the online actor-critic RL architecture of Vamvoudakis and Lewis
[
17
]
, part of the broader adaptive dynamic programming (ADP) paradigm
[
9
,
15
]
. Each agency maintains a
critic
(value-function approximator) and an
actor
(policy approximator), both continuously updated using the known CPS model, converging to approximately Nash equilibrium policies
[
7
]
.

I-C

Contributions

The main contributions of this paper are:

1.

A
control-affine extension
of the cyber-physical-social (CPS) model of Valinejad and Mili
[
16
]
that augments the original open-loop dynamics with three additive intervention channels, counter-messaging, power restoration, and emergency medical service (EMS) deployment.

2.

A
three-player NZS differential game
with per-agency cost functions and cross-coupling penalties, solved online via a

3.

\sim

\sim
50% (Irma), with cross-disaster generalization using a single set of fitted parameters.

(1)

III-A

CPS Dynamics

The uncontrolled dynamics follow the validated CPS model of
[
16
]
. All states are normalized to
[0,1]
.

III-A
1
Logistic Activation

CPS drivers are activated through a logistic threshold map:

\psi(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\kappa(z-\zeta)}},\hskip 18.49988pt\psi^{c}(z)=1-\psi(z),

(2)

where
\kappa>0
controls steepness and
\zeta\in(0,1)

III-A
2
Social-Layer Dynamics

For the social states
\theta\in\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}
, the model prescribes diffusion-based relaxation toward a mixed target
[
16
]
:

\dot{\theta}=\alpha_{\theta}\bigl(\Gamma(\hat{\theta},\theta)-\theta\bigr),

(3)

where
\alpha_{\theta}>0
is a diffusion rate and
\hat{\theta}\in[0,1]
is a target constructed from CPS drivers. The amplification map is

\begin{split}\Gamma(\hat{\theta},\theta)&=\eta_{\theta}\bigl[x_{5}\bigl(1-(1-\theta)(1-\hat{\theta})\bigr)\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt+(1-x_{5})\hat{\theta}\,\theta\bigr]+(1-\eta_{\theta})\hat{\theta},\end{split}

(4)

with
\eta_{\theta}\in[0,1]
weighting the influence of risk perception
x_{5}
. Each target is a convex combination of logistic transforms of driver variables. The fear target, for example, is

\begin{split}\hat{x}_{1}&=\iota_{1}\psi^{c}(x_{6})+\iota_{2}\psi^{c}(x_{4})+\iota_{3}\psi^{c}(x_{8})\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt+\iota_{4}\psi(P^{S})+\iota_{5}\psi^{c}(x_{3})\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt+\iota_{6}\psi^{c}(x_{7})+\iota_{7}\psi^{c}(C^{+}),\end{split}

(5)

where
P^{S}(t)\in[0,1]
is an exogenous disaster-severity signal,
C^{+}(t)\in[0,1]
is a news-positivity signal, and
\sum_{k}\iota_{k}=1
. States
x_{4}
–
x_{7}
evolve via gated relaxation to analogous targets
[
16
]
. For instance:

\dot{x}_{4}=\eta_{P}\,\psi^{c}\!(x_{1})\!\left[\frac{\iota_{11}\psi(x_{9})+\iota_{12}\psi(x_{8})+\iota_{13}\psi^{c}\!(P^{S})}{3}-x_{4}\right]\!,

(6)

Similarly, risk perception evolves as

\begin{split}\dot{x}_{5}&=\eta_{R}\,\psi(x_{1})\psi^{c}(x_{6})\psi^{c}(x_{2})\\
&\;\times\!\left[\frac{\iota_{14}\psi^{c}\!(x_{8})\!+\!\iota_{15}\psi(P^{S})\!+\!\iota_{16}\psi(x_{10})}{5}\right.\\
&\;\left.\hskip 9.24994pt+\frac{\iota_{17}\psi^{c}\!(x_{9})\!+\!\iota_{18}\psi^{c}\!(C^{+})}{5}-x_{5}\right]\end{split}

(7)

where the
\psi/\psi^{c}
prefactors act as gating functions modulating relaxation rates. Cooperation and learning evolve similarly:

\begin{split}\dot{x}_{6}&=\eta_{C}\,\psi(x_{1})\psi(x_{3})\\
&\;\times\!\left[\frac{\iota_{19}\psi^{c}\!(x_{8})+\iota_{20}\psi(P^{S})+\iota_{21}\psi^{c}\!(x_{4})+\iota_{22}\psi(x_{2})}{4}-x_{6}\right]\!,\end{split}

(8)

\dot{x}_{7}=\eta_{L}\,\psi(x_{3})\!\left[\frac{\iota_{23}\psi(x_{6})+\iota_{24}\psi(x_{2})+\iota_{25}\psi^{c}(x_{10})}{3}-x_{7}\right]\!.

(9)

Uncontrolled social states
x_{2}

are forward-invariant in

III-B

Control-Affine Extension

We augment the open-loop CPS model with the three agency-specific control channels. Each control enters as an additive term on the state equations it physically affects:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\alpha_{x_{1}}\!\bigl(\Gamma(\hat{x}_{1},x_{1})-x_{1}\bigr)}_{\text{CPSS drift}}-\;\beta_{1}\,u_{1},

(10)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{8}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{8}(d_{8}-x_{8})+\beta_{8}\,u_{2},

(11)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{9}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{9}(d_{9}-x_{9})+\beta_{9}\,u_{3},

(12)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{10}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{10}(d_{10}-x_{10})-\beta_{10}\,u_{1},

(13)

where
\gamma_{k}>0
is a response rate,
d_{k}(t)\in[0,1]
is the exogenous baseline (
d_{8}
: grid damage profile;
d_{9}
: nominal EMS deployment;
d_{10}
: misinformation rate), and
\beta_{k}>0
are control-effectiveness coefficients. Notably, Player 1 (communication) has
dual coupling
:
u_{1}
suppresses both fear (
x_{1}
) and fake news (
x_{10}
). States
x_{2}
–
x_{7}
receive no direct input but are influenced
indirectly
through the coupled dynamics (e.g., restoring power reduces the fear target via
\psi^{c}(x_{8})
in (
5
)). The input vectors in (
1
) are

g_{1}=-\beta_{1}e_{1}-\beta_{10}e_{10},\hskip 9.24994ptg_{2}=\beta_{8}e_{8},\hskip 9.24994ptg_{3}=\beta_{9}e_{9}.

(14)

(A2)

Regularity.

f
and
g_{j}
are locally Lipschitz in
x
; the critic basis
\phi(x)
is
C^{1}
with bounded Jacobian.

(A3)

Slowly-varying exogenous signals.

The horizon is partitioned into windows
\{[t_{\ell},t_{\ell+1})\}
of length
\Delta
. Within each window,
d(t)\approx d(t_{\ell})
is frozen, yielding an autonomous drift
f_{\ell}(x):=f(x,d(t_{\ell}))

III-C

NZS Game Formulation

Each of the three agencies, communication (
i{=}1
), power (
i{=}2
), and EMS (
i{=}3
), independently minimizes its own infinite-horizon cost
[
17
]
:

J_{i}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\Bigl(Q_{i}(x)+R_{ii}\,u_{i}^{2}+\!\sum_{j\neq i}R_{ij}\,u_{j}^{2}\Bigr)dt,

(15)

with state penalties encoding each player’s resilience priorities:

\displaystyle Q_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=q_{1,1}\,x_{1}^{2}+q_{1,10}\,x_{10}^{2},

(16)

\displaystyle Q_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=q_{2,1}\,x_{1}^{2}+q_{2,8}\,(1{-}x_{8})^{2},

(17)

\displaystyle Q_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=q_{3,1}\,x_{1}^{2}+q_{3,4}\,(1{-}x_{4})^{2}+q_{3,9}\,(1{-}x_{9})^{2}.

(18)

All players penalize fear (
x_{1}
); the communication agency additionally penalizes misinformation (
x_{10}
), the power utility penalizes grid deficit (
1{-}x_{8}
), and EMS penalizes health and service deficits (
1{-}x_{4}
,
1{-}x_{9}
).
R_{ii}>0
penalizes own effort;
R_{ij}\geq 0
(
j\neq i
) encode inter-agency cost coupling.

The Hamiltonian for player
i
is

\begin{split}H_{i}&=Q_{i}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{3}R_{ij}\,u_{j}^{2}\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt+\nabla V_{i}(x)^{\top}\!\Bigl(f(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{3}g_{j}\,u_{j}\Bigr).\end{split}

(19)

and the stationarity condition
\partial H_{i}/\partial u_{i}=0
yields the Nash feedback policy:

u_{i}^{*}(x)=-\frac{1}{2R_{ii}}\,g_{i}^{\top}\nabla V_{i}(x),\hskip 9.24994pti=1,2,3.

(20)

Substituting (
20
) into (
19
) produces coupled nonlinear HJ equations

0=H_{i}\bigl(x,\nabla V_{i},u_{1}^{*},u_{2}^{*},u_{3}^{*}\bigr),\hskip 9.24994pti=1,2,3,

(21)

which are intractable in closed form for the nonlinear CPS dynamics. When the optimum violates a bound, saturation
\mathrm{proj}_{[0,\bar{u}_{j}]}(u_{i}^{*})
is applied, yielding a near-Nash policy
[
17
]
.

IV
Online Actor–Critic Solution

Following
[
17
]
, each player
i
maintains a critic (value-function approximator) and actor (policy approximator), both continuously updated using the known CPS model.

IV-A

Critic Approximation and Update

For each player
i
, the value function is parameterized as

\hat{V}_{i}(x)=\hat{W}_{c,i}^{\top}\phi(x),

(22)

where
\phi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a continuously differentiable basis (constant, linear, and quadratic monomials;
p=1+10+55=66
for
n=10
) and
\hat{W}_{c,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are critic weights. The approximate value gradient is

\nabla\hat{V}_{i}(x)=\nabla\phi(x)\,\hat{W}_{c,i},

(23)

where
\nabla\phi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{10\times p}
is the Jacobian of
\phi
.

The Hamiltonian residual (continuous-time Bellman error) is

\begin{split}\varepsilon_{c,i}&=Q_{i}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{3}R_{ij}\,\hat{u}_{j}^{2}\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt+\hat{W}_{c,i}^{\top}\nabla\phi(x)^{\top}\!\Bigl(f(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{3}g_{j}\hat{u}_{j}\Bigr).\end{split}

(24)

The critic weights are updated via normalized gradient descent
[
17
]
:

\dot{\hat{W}}_{c,i}=-\alpha_{c,i}\,\frac{\sigma_{i}}{(\sigma_{i}^{\top}\sigma_{i}+1)^{2}}\,\varepsilon_{c,i},

(25)

where
\alpha_{c,i}>0
is the critic learning rate and the regressor vector is

\sigma_{i}=\nabla\phi(x)^{\top}\!\Bigl(f(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{3}g_{j}\hat{u}_{j}\Bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

(26)

Evaluating
\sigma_{i}
requires the known drift
f
, making this a
model-based
scheme.

IV-B

Actor Parameterization and Update

Each player
i
maintains an actor whose structure mirrors the Nash policy (
20
):

\hat{u}_{i}(x)=-\frac{1}{2R_{ii}}\,g_{i}^{\top}\nabla\phi(x)\,\hat{W}_{a,i},

(27)

with actor weights
\hat{W}_{a,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The actor update drives
\hat{W}_{a,i}
toward the critic-implied policy weights
[
17
]
:

\dot{\hat{W}}_{a,i}=-\alpha_{a,i}\bigl(\hat{W}_{a,i}-\hat{W}_{c,i}\bigr),

(28)

where
\alpha_{a,i}>0
and
\alpha_{c,i}>\alpha_{a,i}
ensures two-timescale separation so the actor asymptotically tracks the Nash policy
[
17
]
.

IV-C

Initialization, Exploration, and Convergence

(A4)

Admissible initialization.

We initialize
\hat{W}_{a,i}(0)=0
, giving
\hat{u}_{i}\equiv 0
. This is admissible because the open-loop dynamics are inherently stable (relaxation toward targets in
[0,1]^{10}

[
16
]
), so the costs (
15
) are finite under
u\equiv 0
.

During an initial exploration phase
[0,T_{\mathrm{ex}}]
, a bounded probing signal is added to each control channel:

u_{i}(t)\leftarrow\hat{u}_{i}(x(t))+n_{i}(t),\hskip 9.24994pt\|n_{i}(t)\|\leq\bar{n},

(29)

where
n_{i}(t)
is a sum of sinusoids with incommensurate frequencies and
\bar{n}\ll\min_{j}\bar{u}_{j}
ensures that the perturbed policy remains non-destabilizing.

(A5)

Persistence of excitation (PE).

There exist
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},T_{0}>0
such that for each player
i
,

\mu_{1}I\preceq\!\int_{t}^{t+T_{0}}\!\frac{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i}^{\top}}{(\sigma_{i}^{\top}\sigma_{i}+1)^{2}}\,d\tau\preceq\mu_{2}I,\;\;\forall\,t\geq 0.

(30)

Under
(A2)
,
(A4)
, and
(A5)
, the critic and actor weights converge to a neighborhood of the Nash equilibrium strategies
[
17
,
7
]
, with the neighborhood size determined by the basis approximation error.

The logistic parameters (

Empirical Near-Nash Verification

The actor–critic framework of Vamvoudakis and Lewis
[
17
]
guarantees convergence to a Nash equilibrium under ideal conditions (exact function approximation and persistent excitation). In practice, the finite basis, limited trajectory, and control saturation introduce approximation gaps. Rather than assert near-Nash optimality, we measure it via a unilateral deviation test.

V-J
1
Exploitability Metric

We define the
exploitability
of the learned joint policy
(\hat{\pi}_{1},\hat{\pi}_{2},\hat{\pi}_{3})
as

\mathcal{E}=\max_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}\sup_{\pi_{i}^{\prime}}\frac{J_{i}(\hat{\pi}_{i})-J_{i}(\pi_{i}^{\prime};\hat{\pi}_{-i})}{|J_{i}(\hat{\pi}_{i})|},

(31)

where
\hat{\pi}_{-i}
denotes the other players’ converged policies held fixed. At a true Nash equilibrium,
\mathcal{E}=0
.

at each of three scales (
\epsilon\in\{5\%,10\%,20\%\}
of
\|\hat{W}_{a,i}\|
), then roll out the perturbed joint policy over the 17-step data horizon and record the minimum cost
J_{i}^{\mathrm{dev}}
.

Persistence-of-Excitation Diagnostics

The convergence guarantee of the actor-critic scheme requires the PE condition (
30
): the running covariance of the normalized regressor
\bar{\sigma}_{i}=\sigma_{i}/(\sigma_{i}^{\top}\sigma_{i}+1)
must remain positive definite. With
p=66
basis functions and only
T=17
time samples, at most
\mathrm{rank}=17
directions can be excited, so the full PE condition (
\mu_{1}I\preceq\cdot
) is
not
achieved in this experiment.

Figure
6
plots the minimum eigenvalue of the running average covariance
\bar{C}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=0}^{t}\bar{\sigma}_{k}\bar{\sigma}_{k}^{\top}
and of a sliding-window variant. The minimum eigenvalue is numerically zero throughout, confirming that the
p=66
basis is not fully excited by 17 samples. However, the effective rank reaches 17 (out of 66) by the end of the exploration phase, and the Bellman residuals (Fig.
4
) decrease steadily, indicating that the active subspace relevant to the learned value functions is adequately explored. This is consistent with the observation that overparameterized quadratic bases can still yield good approximate solutions when the value function has effectively low-dimensional structure
[
17
]
.

V-L

Control Saturation and State Projection

The Nash policy (
20
) is derived for unconstrained controls, while execution applies saturation to
[0,\bar{u}_{j}]
and the [0,1]-projection of assumption
(A1)
. We quantify how often these constraints are active.

Control saturation.

Over the 17-step horizon, each player’s control hits the lower bound (
u_{j}=0
) on 5.9% of timesteps (the first step, before learning produces nonzero weights). No control reaches the upper bound (
u_{j}=\bar{u}_{j}
). Hence saturation introduces negligible discrepancy between the unconstrained Nash policy and the executed policy.

State projection.

The
[0,1]
-projection of controlled states activates on 58.8% of steps for
x_{1}
(fear is driven to 0), 35.3% for
x_{10}
(fake-news intensity driven to 0), 11.8% for
x_{8}
(power pushed to 1), and 0% for
x_{9}
. The high activation rate for
x_{1}
and
x_{10}
reflects the controller’s aggressive fear-suppression strategy. Because projection acts as a one-sided barrier (clamping to 0 when the controller overshoots), the effective dynamics in this regime are smooth, the state simply remains at the boundary until the drift pulls it back. A smooth alternative (e.g., sigmoid saturation
x_{k}\mapsto 1/(1+e^{-\lambda(x_{k}-0.5)})
) would avoid the nonsmooth switch at negligible computational cost; we leave this for future work.

VI
Conclusion

We formulated decentralized disaster resource allocation as a three-player NZS differential game on validated CPS dynamics and solved it via online actor–critic RL. Beyond achieving
\sim
70% fear reduction on Harvey and
\sim
50% on Irma (without refitting), the framework yields three structural insights for disaster policy: (i) counter-messaging is the highest-leverage intervention due to its dual coupling to fear and misinformation, (ii) EMS availability is limited by physical channel capacity rather than optimization or cost weighting, and (iii) the game-theoretic formulation produces the most effort-efficient policies among all tested controllers, outperforming both centralized and heuristic baselines. These findings demonstrate that the CPS coupling structure itself—not just the choice of optimizer—determines which agencies can most effectively reduce community fear. Future work will extend to model-free settings, incorporate inter-agency communication constraints, and validate on additional disaster scenarios.
