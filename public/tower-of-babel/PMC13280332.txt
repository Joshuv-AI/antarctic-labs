Preoperative planning software as a digital health tool in orthopedics: Surgeon-reported benefits in shoulder arthroplasty☆
Authors: Maud Reynier, Sarah Shank, Matthew Venegoni, Matthias Regling, Arthur de Gast
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/hypothesisPreoperative planning software has gained traction in shoulder arthroplasty, enabling surgeons to better visualize patient's anatomy and optimize implant positioning. Widespread adoption of such tools depends not only on their efficacy but also on surgeon satisfaction and perceived utility in day-to-day practice. However, data on surgeons' satisfaction and perceived utility of such software remain limited. This survey aimed to evaluate surgeons' overall satisfaction, perception and the willingness to recommend the software as a training tool for fellows.Materials and methodsA retrospective observational survey was conducted to evaluate overall satisfaction and usage patterns among orthopedic surgeons using pre-operative 3D planning software (Blueprint®). All surgeons were contacted via email with a web-based questionnaire. Reponses were analyzed with descriptive statistics to assess overall surgeon satisfaction, level of agreement with predefined statements, likelihood of future use and willingness to recommend the software to others.ResultsThe web-based questionnaire was distributed to 1100 orthopedic surgeons between September 30, 2024, and November 11, 2024. 312 responses were received, of which 273 were evaluable responses. Most respondents were low-volume or medium-volume surgeons, and high-volume surgeons were underrepresented (<7.7%). 270 responses were collected from surgeons evaluating their overall satisfaction, with 97% of the feedback being positive. 96% of surgeons said the planning software boosted their confidence in their preoperative plan and 33% during surgery, and 86% reported lower stress compared to performing the procedure without it.ConclusionsThis survey suggests that pre-operative 3D planning software (Blueprint®) is a well-received digital solution in shoulder arthroplasty, with perceived benefits regarding surgical planning, surgeon's stress level, confidence and training.

Introduction

Total shoulder arthroplasty, including anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (ATSA), and reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RTSA) as well as revision surgery, has become increasingly common as the demand for improved upper limb function and pain relief continues to grow in an aging, yet active population [1], [2]. Although effective, these procedures remain technically demanding and highly dependent on patient anatomy, particularly in cases with complex glenoid deformities, rotator cuff pathology, and bone loss [3]. Improper implant positioning, glenoid component loosening, and soft tissue imbalance are among the leading causes of early revision [3]. Over the past decade, three-dimensional (3D) preoperative planning software has emerged to help address patient anatomy and improve outcomes by enhancing visualization, templating components, and providing predictive modeling of implant fit [4], [5].

The benefits of such software have been well-documented [6], [7] and as the field of orthopedic surgery increasingly embraces data-driven, precision-based care, the adoption of digital tools for planning and decision-making aligns with broader trends in surgical innovation [8], [9].

Among these tools, Blueprint® 3D Planning software (Stryker, Tornier SAS, Montbonnot Saint Martin, France; further referred to as the planning software) (Fig. 1) has gained prominence due to its integration of preoperative CT imaging with a user-friendly interface that allows surgeons to simulate component placement and sizing in ATSA and RTSA.Fig. 1Screenshot of the Blueprint® software showing the planification of the glenoid side.Fig. 1

Screenshot of the Blueprint® software showing the planification of the glenoid side.

Prior studies have demonstrated that planning software allows for easier identification of Walch glenoid types to help facilitate diagnoses and implant selection [10] and allows for improvement of virtual glenoid positioning and influences the decision-making process. It also has been shown to increase the ability to closely replicate the preoperative plan and allows surgeons to potentially save critical surgery time by eliminating intraoperative sizing steps [11], [12].

However, widespread adoption of such tools depends not only on their efficacy but also on surgeon satisfaction and perceived utility in day-to-day practice.

This survey was designed to evaluate the overall satisfaction and usage patterns of the planning software among orthopedic surgeons globally, with particular attention to surgeon confidence and stress level and willingness to recommend its use to others. By gathering retrospective data from a large cohort of surgeons' users, this survey aims to provide insights into why surgeons choose to integrate digital planning tools into their practice, and to identify potential barriers to broader adoption.

Materials and methods

This was a retrospective, observational, web-based survey designed to collect orthopedic surgeons' feedback on the planning software, including overall satisfaction, agreement with predefined statements, likeliness of future use, educational value and willingness to recommend it to others. A total of 10 questions were sent to orthopedic surgeons performing shoulder arthroplasty procedures and registered in the Blueprint® Online Management System.

Prior to completing the survey, the participants were presented with the purpose of the survey and general privacy statements.

Between September 30 and November 11, 2024, the web-based survey was shared via the Qualtrics survey platform (Qualtrics, Provo, UT, USA) and distributed to 1100 users (orthopedic surgeons) globally. Surgeons were eligible to participate regardless of geographical location, institutional affiliation or surgical volume. Participation was voluntary and all responses were anonymized to ensure confidentiality.

The survey consisted of multiple-choice and Likert scale questions, organized into four thematic domains: (1) General information and surgical volume: procedure type performed, number of surgeries performed per year and type of procedure (i.e., ATSA, RTSA, revision cases), and frequency of planning software use; (2) Satisfaction; (3) Personal experience: impact on confidence and stress level; and (4) Willingness to recommend. There were open fields for additional comments.

The survey was designed to take no longer than 3–6 min to complete and was available in English. Responses were excluded if they were incomplete (e.g. no answer to the primary questions on satisfaction or no answer to all questions), inconsistent (e.g. conflicting volume data or free text mentioning they are not surgeons or users) or clearly erroneous (e.g. duplicate entries). In case duplicate answers were identified without other reasons for exclusion, the lowest rating was retained in the final dataset.

The threshold for acceptance was predefined as a general satisfaction rate of at least 80% among respondents. Satisfaction was assessed using a single-item question (“Please indicate your overall satisfaction with the Blueprint 3D Planning Software”), which was measured with a 7-point Likert-type scale. Among the response options, ‘completely satisfied’, ‘mostly satisfied’ and ‘somewhat satisfied’ were considered positive responses; all others were classified as neutral or negative responses. Missing responses were excluded from analysis. To achieve a ± 5% margin of error at a 95% confidence level, a minimum of 246 responses was required. The analysis was one-sided: only satisfaction levels exceeding 80% were considered acceptable.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants' characteristics and survey responses. Categorical variables (e.g., satisfaction levels, usage frequency) were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Data was managed and analyzed in Microsoft Excel v2504. No identifying data were collected, and the survey was exempt from Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval due to its anonymous and non-interventional nature.

In 262 from the 270 responses for satisfaction (97%), the surgeons gave a positive rating regarding their overall satisfaction with the planning software (Fig. 4). Specifically (in ranked order), 60.1% of surgeons (n = 164) indicated they were ‘mostly satisfied’, while an additional 29.3% (n = 80) reported being ‘completely satisfied’. Finally, 6.6% of surgeons (n = 18) stated they were ‘somewhat satisfied’ with the software overall. In contrast, negative ratings were seen in a total of 5 responses (< 2%): 3 surgeons indicated they were ‘somewhat dissatisfied’, whereas one response each was received with ‘mostly dissatisfied’ and ‘completely dissatisfied’ ratings. In the remaining response, a neutral rating (i.e., neither satisfied nor dissatisfied’) was given.Fig. 4Overall satisfaction.Fig. 4

Surgeons who indicated being ‘somewhat dissatisfied’, ‘mostly dissatisfied’ or ‘completely dissatisfied’ were given the opportunity to specify the reasons for their dissatisfaction in an open-comment field. All five surgeons who gave a negative rating provided specific reasons for their dissatisfaction. One surgeon, who reported to be ‘completely dissatisfied’ experienced restricted access to the software. This issue appeared to be a single case and support was provided to the clinician to address it. Three surgeons who were ‘somewhat dissatisfied’ highlighted limitations related to the software accessibility and compatibility, particularly the inability to use the software on their mobile phone and limited post-planning review capabilities. One of these respondents also noted the software's limited use for specific clinical situations, such as fractures or revisions cases (not yet available at the time of the survey). Another surgeon, who was ‘mostly dissatisfied’, reported delays in the planning process and frequent CT-scan upload rejections.

The set of predefined statements in the survey focused on aspects related to usability and surgeon experience, including perceived ease of use, confidence, and general stress levels (Fig. 5). The responses suggest a generally favorable surgeons' perception of the planning software's ease of use (90% agreement). Also, a clear majority of surgeons indicated that, compared to performing the same procedure without the planning software, its use increased their confidence in the pre-operative plan (96% agreement) and the execution of the procedure during surgery (94% agreement). Finally, 86% of surgeons expressed agreement that the planning software reduces their general stress level in comparison to the same surgical procedure without using the software.Fig. 5Surgeon's perceptions of the software's functionality on usability, confidence, and stress reduction.Fig. 5

Surgeon's perceptions of the software's functionality on usability, confidence, and stress reduction.

The last questions of the survey focused on the use of the planning software as a training tool and the likeliness for the surgeons to recommend it to others.

Regarding the surgeons' satisfaction with the use of the planning software to train fellows, students or staff (Fig. 6), positive feedback was received in 83.5% of responses; in contrast, 0.7% of the surgeons expressed complete dissatisfaction. Discussion

This international survey highlights strong surgeon satisfaction and high acceptance of this planning software as a digital health tool in shoulder arthroplasty. Surgeons appreciated its ease of use and its impact on confidence and stress level. These findings are consistent with prior studies that underscore the growing integration of enabling technologies in orthopedic workflows, particularly when perceived as user-friendly and impactful on decision-making [14], [15]. It should be noted that the existing literature on preoperative planning in shoulder arthroplasty includes studies evaluating a range of CT-based 3D planning software platforms, including both manufacturer-specific and independent solutions.

Most surgeons who responded were low-volume surgeons, performing fewer than 10 shoulder arthroplasties per year. This demographic characteristic is particularly relevant considering previous findings demonstrating that patient-specific preoperative planning has the greatest impact on this group [16], [17]. In the study by Min et al., the use of preoperative planning software significantly improved both glenoid classification and surgical planning accuracy, with the most pronounced benefit observed among low-volume surgeons [18], [19]. These results suggest that advanced planning tools can serve as valuable support systems for surgeons with less frequent exposure to shoulder arthroplasty, helping to bridge the gap with expert-level decision-making.

However, as these studies were conducted using different planning platforms and prosthesis systems, caution is warranted when extrapolating software-specific results to CT-based planning tools more broadly. Given the high percentage of low-volume surgeons in our survey cohort, this reinforces the importance of integrating such technologies into clinical practice [6].

Another key insight from this survey is the perceived reduction in intraoperative stress and the corresponding increase in surgeon confidence when using this planning software. Digital platforms that support visualization and case rehearsal may reduce cognitive load during surgery; a factor associated with improved focus and reduced error risk [20], [21]. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing improved surgeon confidence and perceived preparedness when using preoperative planning or decision-support tools, regardless of the specific software platform evaluated. These perceived benefits suggest broader implications not only for intraoperative performance but also for surgeon well-being. Orthopedic surgery is associated with high physical and psychological demands, and tools that reduce stress may contribute to better long-term health outcomes and lower burnout risk among surgeons [22].

.The educational potential of the planning software also emerged as a significant theme. In this survey, surgeons frequently noted its value in communicating surgical goals to trainees and facilitating interactive learning. It can help residents and fellows to visualize complex anatomy and understand surgical strategies before entering the operating room—an approach grounded in principles of deliberate practice and experiential learning [23].

.A limitation of this survey research is that it primarily focused on surgeons' experience and agreement with statements regarding the planning software, without incorporating clinical outcome data. While multiple studies report improvements in preoperative decision-making, planned implant positioning, and intraoperative accuracy—often in combination with patient-specific instrumentation or navigation comparative evidence demonstrating improvements in long-term patient-reported outcomes, complication rates, or implant survivorship attributable to planning software alone remains limited. Consequently, the results cannot be directly extrapolated to objective surgical performance or patient outcomes.

Furthermore, this survey was addressed to surgeons who had previously used Blueprint planning software in their clinical practice, and because the survey primarily included low-volume surgeons who had used this software, selection bias may limit the generalizability of the findings. The reliance on self-reported feedback may also have introduced recall or reporting bias, potentially overestimating perceived benefits. Additionally, no comparison with alternative software solutions was included, limiting the ability to contextualize these findings. Finally, because the evaluation was based on a single survey time point, user feedback may not reflect changes that occur with increasing experience or along the learning curve, and these temporal effects were not captured.

Future work should focus on further evaluating the objective impact of this digital planning tools in shoulder arthroplasty on surgical metrics, patient outcomes, and training effectiveness. Additionally, research into economic value, integration/ coordination with health systems, and barriers to adoption will be critical to guide broader implementation.

Despite interesting feedback on intraoperative experience, its validity and significance remain uncertain. In some cases, existing data in literature supports evidence, while in others, the lack of scientific and clinical studies prevents definitive conclusions.

Conclusion

This survey shows a high level of satisfaction among orthopedic surgeons using the Blueprint® 3D preoperative planning software, especially among low-volume surgeons. The planning software was perceived as easy to use, helpful for preoperative planning and valuable for subjectively reducing stress and increasing confidence during surgery. Its educational potential was also widely recognized. However, as all respondents were surgeons using the planning software, the results may reflect a positive bias. Perceived benefits on clinical outcomes remain uncertain and require further objective validation.
