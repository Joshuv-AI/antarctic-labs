Effect of an out-of-pocket insulin cap in Medicare part D
Authors: Yevgeniy Feyman, Thomas Buchmueller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOut-of-pocket (OOP) costs can often impede patients' access to prescription drugs. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 (IRA) limited OOP costs for insulin products covered by the Medicare Part B and Part D programs to no more than $35 for a one-month supply.MethodsThis analysis uses prescription drug data from Medicare Part D and commercially insured patients along with a difference-in-differences specification to estimate changes in OOP costs among Medicare Part D enrollees after policy implementation.ResultsWe estimate that the Part D enrollees most likely to be affected by the policy saw a $34.71 reduction in average OOP costs per prescription across all insulins compared to what might have occurred absent the policy in 2023.ConclusionThese findings suggest that targeted limits on OOP costs for high-value drugs can reduce the cost burden for patients.Keywords: Medicare, insulin, prescription drugs, Inflation Reduction Act, part D, out-of-pocket spending

Introduction

Over 38 million Americans (11.6% of the US population) have diabetes; more than 7 million use insulin daily to manage their blood sugar levels.1 Although insulin is critical for patients who require it to manage their illness, it can be expensive. List prices have increased over time, nearly doubling between 2012 and 2016, before stabilizing in recent years.2 High insulin prices can reduce adherence, which, in turn, can lead to complications that are costly to treat and severely impact patients' health and quality of life.3

Diabetes is more prevalent among Medicare beneficiaries than in the general population (nearly 30% compared to 11.6% among all Americans).4 Among Medicare beneficiaries, prevalence rates are higher among American Indian/Alaska Natives, Black, Hispanic, and Asian/Pacific Islander beneficiaries than among their White counterparts.4 From 2009 to 2019, the percentage of Medicare Part D enrollees facing high out-of-pocket (OOP) costs for insulin grew substantially and higher OOP costs were associated with lower levels of self-reported adherence5 and satisfaction.6 In 2020, Medicare Part D enrollees paid more than $1 billion in OOP costs for insulin.7

Diabetes can be treated with different types of insulin products, which vary based on how quickly they work, when they reach their peak, and how long they last. Many individuals require regimens of 2 insulin products, used at mealtime (bolus) and once per day (basal), simultaneously. Insulin products may be rapid acting (begin working in 15 minutes after injection), regular or short acting (begin working within 30 minutes), intermediate acting (begin working within 2-4 hours), and long-acting (begin working within several hours). In addition, insulin products vary based on whether they are modified versions of the insulin hormone (analog insulin; typically, newer products) or are the insulin hormone itself (human insulin; typically, older products). While human insulins are less expensive than long or short-acting analog insulins,8 they are less clinically preferred.9

Recognizing the importance of insulin affordability, the federal government has worked to reduce the OOP cost of insulin products for Medicare beneficiaries. On January 1, 2021, the CMS Innovation Center launched the Part D Senior Savings (PDSS) model.10 Under this model, Part D plans could voluntarily limit insulin cost-sharing to $35 for a month's supply.11 Model evaluations have found both increases in insulin fills and lower OOP costs.12-15

Building on this, the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) of 2022 included provisions to limit OOP costs of insulin for Medicare enrollees in all Part D plans. Starting January 1, 2023, people enrolled in a Medicare Part D prescription drug plan (PDP) or a Medicare Advantage plan with prescription drug coverage (MA-PD) have no deductible for covered insulin products and have a copayment cap of $35 per month supply of each covered insulin product. With the $35 cap applying to all Part D enrollees, CMS terminated the PDSS at the end of 2023. Since July 2023, insulin products covered under Medicare Part B are also capped at $35 for a month's supply.

Research exists on similar OOP insulin caps enacted by states,16-18 and the PDSS.12-15 Assessing the effects of the new cap under the IRA is important for 2 reasons. First, because Medicare enrollees may respond differently than other populations to changes in OOP costs, the experience of populations affected by state-level bans may not generalize to the IRA's cap. Second, while the PDSS exposed a large portion of Medicare beneficiaries to a cap, this policy was not implemented across all Part D plans, and did not cover all insulins. Thus, the effects of the PDSS are likely different than the IRA's insulin OOP cap.

Recent analyses have found increases in prescription fills under the $35 insulin cap,19 and have described broad trends in insulin out-of-pocket costs over time for Medicare beneficiaries.20 Most directly related to our work, Hong et al.21 used segmented regressions to assess changes in OOP spending among Medicare patients from 2021 (implementation of PDSS) through 2023 (implementation of the IRA OOP cap).

Our analysis builds on previous work by focusing on the patient group directly affected by the IRA's $35 OOP cap, and is the first to apply quasi-experimental methods to assess how Medicare enrollees' OOP spending for insulin products changed after implementation of the $35 cap. This will help inform whether the policy achieved the intended goal of reducing OOP costs for Part D enrollees using insulin. Furthermore, our analysis provides a starting point for future work examining possible effects on insulin adherence and health outcomes in the Medicare population.

Data and methods

Data

The analysis uses 2 sources of data. The first is IQVIA's PayerTrak data, which provides OOP amounts paid per adjudicated prescription at the point of sale. The IQVIA data directly capture approximately 90% of retail pharmaceutical sales; IQVIA uses a proprietary projection methodology to impute the remaining 10%. The data used in this analysis represent calendar years 2021 to 2023—2 years prior and 1 year after the IRA Part D $35 insulin cap went into effect. We analyze payments for all insulins together, as well as by insulin source and type (eg, human vs analog and short-acting vs long-acting; see Insulin Identification). Data for prescriptions filled using Medicare Part D and by commercial insurance are aggregated to the state/month level. With 2 payer types, 51 states (including DC) and 36 months, the largest sample we analyze has 3672 observations.

IQVIA data allows the use of commercially insured patients as a comparison group for Part D enrollees collected in an identical way. However, the IQVIA data has an important limitation: it is not possible to distinguish among different types of Medicare enrollees. Specifically, while the IQVIA data includes claims for enrollees qualifying for Medicare's Low-Income Subsidy (LIS) as well as those enrolled in the PDSS, these groups of enrollees are not separately identified. LIS enrollees, who in 2023 represented over 27% of Part D enrollees, face minimal cost-sharing for all prescription drugs.22,23 As a result, LIS enrollees would mostly be unaffected by the IRA's $35 OOP limit. Individuals enrolled in the PDSS model, as noted, already benefited from an OOP cap of $35 for a month's supply of insulin. Altogether, among Part D enrollees who filled insulin prescriptions in 2023, roughly 75% were either in PDSS plans or were receiving LIS protections. By grouping LIS and PDSS enrollees with other enrollees for whom insulin costs were not previously capped, analyses based on IQVIA data alone will underestimate the effect of the IRA's $35 OOP limit for Part D beneficiaries whose OOP costs are not limited by other policies.

To address this limitation, we also incorporate data from the Common Medicare Environment (CME) and 100% Medicare Part D Prescription Drug Event (PDE) data for the same years. We identify PDEs filled by LIS enrollees based on whether the individual was LIS at the time of the fill. We identify PDSS enrollees based on the Part D contract and plan that paid for the PDE, using landscape files from CMS to identify participating contracts and plans.

Identifying these enrollees allows us to distinguish the effect of the $35 insulin cap from other policies. The main limitation of the PDE data is that it does not allow for a non-Medicare comparison group. Together, the 2 data sets allow us to estimate changes in OOP costs for the Part D enrollees most likely to benefit from the $35 insulin cap—enrollees without LIS protections and those not in plans participating in the PDSS—while accounting for market-level trends unrelated to the policy and state-level caps. To make the 2 datasets fully comparable, we restrict the PDE data to fills in retail pharmacies, and limit geographies to Medicare enrollees in the 50 states plus the District of Columbia.

Study population

We study all Medicare Part D enrollees from 2021 through 2023 who filled at least one prescription for insulin during that time. In the IQVIA data, we also include all individuals with commercial insurance who filled insulin prescriptions over the same period.

Insulin identification

To identify fills for insulin products, we take 2 approaches. In IQVIA data, insulin products are defined by their Uniform System of Classification 5-digit codes (USC5). USC5 codes combine unique therapeutic categories, pharmacology, chemical structure, and indication.24 In PDE data, we use National Drug Codes (NDCs) to identify insulin products. Full lists of the USC5 categories and NDCs used to define insulin products are available in Appendix Tables S1 and S2, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Using the IQVIA data, we estimate a series of linear difference-in-differences regressions using a two-way fixed effects specification, with the commercially insured population as a control group. We estimate these regressions by aggregating fills to the state-year-month-payer type level, where payer type represents either commercial or Medicare Part D. The specifications include state-by-payer type and calendar month fixed effects.

Because diabetes is a widely treated condition among people with commercial insurance, but the IRA's $35 cap only applied to Medicare beneficiaries, the trends for commercially insured patients provide a relevant theoretical counterfactual. To assess the validity of the commercial group as a counterfactual, we examine pre-trends for both Medicare and commercial patients and conduct sensitivity analyses.

Because the IQVIA data capture claims for all Medicare Part D enrollees (including those unaffected by the $35 cap), the estimates will understate the policy's impact. We estimate 2 separate single-difference specifications for the years 2022-2023 using IQVIA data on commercial claims and PDE data on claims for non-LIS, non-PDSS Medicare enrollees. Each specification includes state and calendar month fixed effects with average OOP as the outcome, weighted by the number of prescriptions in the cell; standard errors are clustered by state. We then calculate the difference in the coefficients from these 2 estimates.25 Thus, our primary result is obtained from a manually estimated two-period difference-in-differences analysis using these 2 data sets. Standard errors are calculated using methods for comparing coefficients from different models.26

We note that though this approach is less common in the difference-in-differences literature, it is similar to estimating a two-period difference-in-differences by hand. Assumptions of parallel pre-trends are required, as well as similar measurement of the outcome. To ensure the latter, we restrict our analysis only to retail pharmacies. We present the full regression specifications in Appendix B.

Limitations

Our analysis has several limitations. First, while we conduct a variety of sensitivity tests, as with all quasi-experimental analyses, it is impossible to verify that the estimated associations are causal. Second, while IQVIA data is well-documented and standardized, the payer source for some prescription fills in the data may be miscoded. If this is random, it would attenuate our primary estimates. Third, while PDE data is the authoritative source of Medicare Part D prescriptions, because the first year of any policy may face implementation challenges, data errors, and processing errors on the part of payers and pharmacies may be present and could affect our estimates based on the PDE data. Fourth, we identify PDSS fills based on the contract plan under which they were filled. However, not all fills in a contract-plan participating in PDSS are covered by PDSS. This means that our estimates may be biased downward. Fifth, several pharmaceutical manufacturers instituted copayment limits in the commercial market for their insulin products at participating pharmacies, which took effect at various times during 2023.27 While this does not appear to affect our estimates based on examination of pre-trends, it could further bias our estimates downward. Sixth, the IRA's OOP cap applied to monthly equivalent supplies of insulin (eg, a $35 cap for 30 days, $70 cap for 60 days). Our analysis focused on OOP spending per prescription. If the number of days supplied on an average fill increased (decreased), our results would understate (overstate) the impact of the IRA. However, our estimates likely approximate the change in monthly fill cost, given that the most common type of fill is for a 30-day supply.28 Lastly, the IRA introduced a variety of changes to the Part D benefit, including a new maximum OOP requirement, an expansion of LIS coverage, and negotiated prices for selected drugs. Some of these provisions will affect plan design, utilization, and outcomes among Part D beneficiaries. These changes were not in effect during the time of our analysis, but would potentially confound longer-term analyses. Thus, we focus on changes in insulin OOP in the first year and do not address the total OOP burden for Part D enrollees.

In the pre-cap period, over 90% of commercial and Medicare Part D prescriptions for insulin were analog rather than human insulin products. A higher share of prescriptions was for long-acting than fast-acting analog products among both commercial prescriptions (37.7% vs 36.1%) and in Medicare Part D (46.3% vs 25.7%). While among commercial prescriptions, human insulin had slightly higher OOP than analog ($47.70 vs $43.03), Part D beneficiaries paid less for human insulin compared to analog ($30.64 vs $38.39). These differences were directionally similar for the non-LIS/non-PDSS Part D population.

Before the IRA cap took effect, the non-LIS/non-PDSS Part D population had higher OOP costs for insulin than the overall Medicare group or the commercial population. For example, for analog insulins, the non-LIS and non-PDSS population paid $92.42 on average, while the commercial population paid $43.03, and the overall Medicare population paid just under $40.

Figure 1 displays monthly data on the mean OOP cost per insulin fill for Medicare Part D and commercially insured patients as measured in the IQVIA data. This provides visual evidence that after the $35 cap went into effect, average OOP costs for Medicare Part D enrollees fell both in absolute terms and relative to the counterfactual implied by the commercial market. In 2021 and 2022 (prior to the IRA's $35 cap), both commercial and Medicare Part D OOP costs follow a similar seasonal pattern (suggesting that the parallel pre-trends assumption for a difference-in-differences analysis holds). This occurs because when deductibles reset for most plans in January of each year, individuals are responsible for all of their applicable drug costs until they reach their deductibles. For Part D enrollees, the mean OOP amount paid per insulin fill was roughly $15 higher in January than in December. Overall, in those years, OOP costs were consistently higher for commercial patients ($46.81) than for Part D patients ($37.76). However, Part D enrollees not receive LIS or PDSS protections paid substantially more in OOP ($88.38).

In 2023, the trend for commercial patients is consistent with the seasonal pattern observed in 2021 and 2022. However, after implementation of the pre-deductible $35 cap for Medicare patients, OOP costs no longer spike in January, though mean OOP costs still trend down throughout the year, from $32.09 in January to $26.34 in December. This may be due to some Part D enrollees eventually entering the catastrophic phase of the Part D benefit design, where they face lower OOP costs overall.

As demonstrated in Appendix Table S1, the decline in OOP payments varied by categories of insulin. For human insulins, the estimated reduction ranged from $2.56 (SE: $0.65) for combination insulins to $6.44 (SE: $0.85) for fast-acting insulins. Among analog insulins, OOP reductions ranged from $4.38 (SE: $0.43) for fast-acting insulins to $11.78 (SE: $1.35) for long-acting insulins with GLP-1s.

Because IQVIA data includes fills by LIS enrollees and individuals enrolled in PDSS plans, the estimates in Table 3 and Appendix Figure S1 understate the changes in OOP costs among the population facing uncapped insulin costs prior to the IRA. To estimate our model on the group most affected, we combine estimates from 2 separate single-difference regressions using the years 2022 and 2023—one for the commercial IQVIA data and one for the PDE data restricted to the non-LIS and non-PDSS population (these primary results are available in Table 3). In the commercial market, the change in OOP from 2022 to 2023 was −$2.64 (SE: $0.39). In the Part D market, among non-LIS, non-PDSS enrollees, OOP fell by $37.35 (SE: $2.04). The estimated two-period difference-in-differences for the enrollees directly affected by the policy is the difference between these 2 changes: −$37.35—(−$2.64) = −$34.71 (SE: $2.67). Given that the non-LIS and non-PDSS population had a mean OOP of $88.38 prior to the cap (Table 1), this represents a 39% reduction in mean OOP. The estimated changes in OOP costs varied considerably across insulin types, ranging from −$9.58 (SE: $1.92) for intermediate-acting human insulin to −$66.98 ($2.70) for long-acting analog insulin with a GLP-1.

Sensitivity analyses and validation

We tested the sensitivity of our results to alternative modeling assumptions and specifications.

First, to ensure that our difference-in-differences specification was not picking up a pre-existing trend, we estimated a placebo test assuming that the IRA's $35 cap went into effect in 2022. This produced a small and non-significant coefficient (−$0.87, SE: $0.75), supporting our research design (Table 2).

Second, because 27 states29 implemented OOP caps for insulin that apply to non-ERISA commercial health plans, we separately examined states with and without such caps. The pattern in overall trends was unchanged when doing so (Appendix Figure S1). Regression models limited to states without insulin caps in the commercial market (Appendix Table S2), indicated little difference in the estimates of OOP costs for Medicare Part D enrollees after the $35 cap was in effect for either set of states. This suggests that pre-existing policies did not confound our primary analysis.

Third, because there may be broader trends for diabetes overall, we examined OOP costs for non-insulin drugs for diabetes. These results (Appendix Figure S2) indicate that OOP costs were trending similarly for Part D and commercial insurance, with no change starting in 2023 (non-insulin drugs are listed in Appendix Table S3). Specifically, the lack of a decline in OOP costs per fill for non-insulin diabetes medications, while we observed a decline in OOP costs per fill for insulin, suggests that our results are not driven by a diabetes-specific trend.

Discussion

The IRA's $35 cap on insulin OOP was implemented to help reduce the cost burden of insulin for Medicare beneficiaries. In this novel difference-in-differences analysis, we found that for Medicare Part D enrollees without other protection against high OOP costs, the IRA's $35 cap was associated with a reduction of $34.71 in mean OOP costs per fill of insulin, a 39% reduction. Changes in OOP costs were nearly twice as large (−$66.98) for long-acting insulins with GLP-1 medications. For comparison, this average reduction represents 64% of average OOP insulin spending per prescription among Part D enrollees in 2020.7

Our results illustrate that prior to the implementation of the $35 cap, seasonal trends in OOP per fill for insulin among Part D enrollees generally followed seasonal trends among commercial enrollees, largely as a function of resetting annual deductibles in both types of coverage. After the cap was implemented, there was much less variation in OOP costs per fill of insulin over the calendar year. Thus, in addition to reducing overall spending, the policy had the added effect of smoothing spending, which may be an important benefit for beneficiaries who are liquidity-constrained.30

Because the policy was implemented recently, we are unable to investigate longer-term effects on utilization or health outcomes. Changes in utilization and health outcomes may take years to observe. While initial work found increases in utilization in the short-term,19 future research can measure the full effect of the IRA's $35 cap. Specifically, because our findings suggest larger changes for certain insulins, future work could focus on whether utilization of more clinically appropriate insulins was affected and whether this led to disproportionately improved health outcomes.

While other changes under the IRA that affect plan design, utilization, and outcomes were not in effect and therefore could not have affected our estimates in the first year of the insulin OOP cap, these changes will complicate future analyses. Disentangling the effects of different components of the IRA will be an important challenge in assessing the longer-term effects of the insulin OOP cap.

Our findings showing reduced OOP spending per fill among Part D enrollees are consistent with research investigating the effect of state-level OOP caps,16 which found reductions among commercial enrollees more exposed to OOP costs. Though not directly comparable to our analysis, existing work by Hong et al.21 measured quarterly insulin spending, finding a $58.59 reduction in quarterly insulin spending. Given that insulin fills are most commonly for one month's supply,28 this suggests a monthly reduction of around $19.50, less than our estimate of $34.71. This makes sense given that Hong et al. were unable to exclude PDSS beneficiaries and did not use a comparator group.

While these results do not measure monthly or total OOP burden among Medicare beneficiaries, taken together, our findings and existing work on state-level OOP caps suggest that targeted policies focused on reducing OOP costs among users of high-value therapies can have large effects.

Conclusion

The IRA's $35 cap on OOP for insulin drugs among the Part D population was effective at substantially reducing OOP burden among enrollees facing high OOP costs. This provides a template for other potential approaches that can be used to reduce OOP cost burden among life-saving therapies where adherence may be a concern.
