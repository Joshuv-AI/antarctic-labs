Allele-specific methylation of MRP3 drives mating type determination in a marine diatom
Authors: Antonella Ruggiero, Francesco Manfellotto, Svenja Mager, Federica di Costanzo, Tobias Gerber, Andrea Broccoli, Viviana Di Tuccio, Tomohiro Nishimura, Marco Sollitto, Leslie Pan, Alberto Pallavicini, Monia T. Russo, Remo Sanges, Maria I. Ferrante
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sex determination mechanisms exhibit remarkable diversity across eukaryotic lineages. In the pennate diatom Pseudo-nitzschia multistriata, sex is determined by MRP3, a gene with unknown function, which is monoallelically expressed in sexually competent mating type plus (MT+) cells and silenced in MT− cells. Here, we describe an allelic-specific methylation pattern at the sex locus, with a hypomethylated allele specific to the MT+ which is transcriptionally active, while the other hypermethylated MRP3 alleles are transcriptionally silent. In addition, the genome-wide methylation landscape remains stable throughout the life cycle. Treatment with a methylation inhibitor leads to MRP3 activation and partial sex reversal, suggesting an active role of DNA methylation in the sex determination mechanism. Long-read sequencing improved the MT-determining region resolution, highlighting a gene-poor, repeat-rich area that likely represents a primordial MT-determining locus. Our results provide insights into the mechanisms of sex determination and suggest that DNA methylation in diatoms has a more complex role than silencing repetitive regions.

INTRODUCTION

Mechanisms of sex determination are highly diverse and remain largely underexplored in protists. In unicellular organisms, the genes required for determining cell identity and directing sexual development are encoded in a specialized region of the genome known as mating type (MT) locus (1, 2) comparable to sex chromosomes. Unicellular organisms can shift between sexual and asexual phases, alternating haploid and diploid stages in their life cycle. Excluding a few species where only vegetative growth has been reported, the sexual phase is mandatory for most of the diatoms (3, 4). Diatoms are unicellular photosynthetic microalgae generating about 20% of the oxygen produced on Earth, as much as the entire world’s rainforests (5). Despite their ecological importance, very little is known on the molecular controls of the life cycle transitions and on the mechanisms involved in sex determination (6). Diatom cells are encased in a siliceous cell wall, known as the frustule, which consists of two valves, the smaller hypotheca, which fits inside the larger epitheca (7). This rigid frustule creates a constraint during cell division. Specifically, during each mitotic division, each of the two daughter cells inherits a valve of different sizes and builds the second valve inside the one inherited, and this leads to a progressive reduction in the cell size of the population. This progressive miniaturization of the population can lead to extinction, and it is counteracted by the process of sexual reproduction (6). Therefore, in diatoms, sexual reproduction serves two functions: It introduces genetic diversity through recombination, and it secures species persistence by restoring cell size and maintaining population viability. The ability to enter the sexual cycle is linked to cell size reduction, with cells becoming sexually competent only once they reach the sexualization size threshold (SST). Large cells are incapable of forming gametes, and mating can happen only once the cells reach a specific size and the opposite MT is present (Fig. 1).

Main phases of a pennate diatom life cycle.

Below a species-specific SST, cells can initiate sexual reproduction when a partner of the opposite MT is present. Gametes (red and blue spheres) represent the only haploid stage in the diatom life cycle. Following mating, the resulting zygote (green spheres), initially a soft, expandable stage, develops into an auxospore. Within the auxospore, a large initial cell with a rigid silica cell wall is formed, restoring maximum cell size and allowing the vegetative cycle to resume.

How diatom cells can perceive their size and determine whether to mate or not is still an open question. Among possible factors playing a role in this process, we tested the possibility of an epigenetic control. Epigenetic mechanisms influence gene expression by regulating chromatin structure and DNA accessibility (8–10) and, in general, provide the plasticity to combine genomic and environmental signals. They are also known to play a role in sex determination. Methylation is a heritable DNA modification involved in the repression of transposable elements, gene expression control, embryo development, and sex determination in animals and plants (11–13). In the process of DNA methylation, a methyl group is transferred to the fifth carbon of cytosine. We tested our hypothesis on Pseudo-nitzschia multistriata, a pennate planktonic diatom (14), which is so far the only diatom species where an MT locus and an MT-determining gene, MRP3, have been identified (15). Comparative transcriptomic analysis of sexually competent cells from opposite MTs revealed five MT-specific genes. The genes were named according to their MT-specific expression: MT Related Plus (MRP1, MRP2, and MRP3), expressed in MT+ strains, and MT Related Minus (MRM1 and MRM2) expressed in MT− strains (15). Among them, MRP3 determines MT identity and is expressed monoallelically exclusively in MT+ strains. MRP3 overexpression in MT− strains causes sex reversal: They mate with cells of the identical MT and display an inverted trend of expression of the MT Related (MR) genes (15). Different allelic variants of MRP3 have been described. These alleles were characterized by polymorphic elements, in the region upstream of MRP3, containing triplet repeats of different length or single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and, in some cases, a transposase remnant, whose role in the regulation of the gene transcription remains to be clarified. We had discovered distinctive structural differences in a specific allele (called A) found exclusively in MT+ in strains from the Gulf of Naples (15); however, extending the analysis of the MRP3 genomic region to additional strains from different geographical locations (16, 17), we were unable to identify a genomic structure that could universally represent an MT-specific signature. In all the 127 strains analyzed in total in (15–17), we always found a transcriptionally active (TA) allele always present in MT+ and transcriptionally inactive (TI) alleles that could be present in both MTs. In this work, we describe the methylation landscape at genomic scale in sexually competent and non sexually competent cells and investigated the role of DNA methylation in the sex competence acquisition and sex determination mechanism in P. multistriata. We report that the methylation profile is stable during size reduction, excluding its role in the former process. We find hypermethylation of the MRP3 TI alleles and hypomethylation of the TA allele which defines the MT+, linking the methylation to gene silencing. Treatment with the methylation inhibitor 5-azacytidine (5-aza-dC) induced demethylation at several CpG (C—phosphate—G) sites, causing the hypermethylated TI allele to become hypomethylated. This change was associated with increased MRP3 expression, resulting in partial sex reversal, from the genetic MT− to the phenotypic MT+. These findings suggest a mechanism with inherited methylation playing an important role in maintenance of allele-specific gene expression.

RESULTS

Methylation landscape in life cycle transitions

In P. multistriata, large cells (L) are defined as cells that are not capable of sexual reproduction and range in size from 80 to 55 μm. Below 55 μm, which is the SST for this species, cells become sexually competent and are classified as small (S). To investigate the potential role of DNA methylation in the transition to sexual competence and in sex determination, we profiled cytosine methylation at single-base resolution using enzymatic methyl sequencing (EM-seq) (18). We analyzed cells of opposite MTs above and below the SST, using 14 samples: four wild strains from the Gulf of Naples, Italy, two wild strains from New Zealand (NZ), and six strains from crosses, of which two were collected above (L) and below (S) the SST (fig. S1 and table S1).

We first explored the P. multistriata methylation landscape, identifying methylation in the CpG, CHG and CHH contexts. The vast majority of methylated cytosines were found within CpG dinucleotides, in agreement with what was found in other diatoms (19–21) with about ∼5 to 10% of CpGs methylated in the 59-Mb P. multistriata nuclear genome across the 14 samples analyzed (Fig. 2A and table S2).

About 70% of CpGs that are found in repeat regions were methylated, while we found methylated only 0.8% of CpG that are located in Transcription Start Sites (TSS), 0.7% in Transcription Termination Sites (TTS), and between 1 and 1.5% in genes (table S3). The genome-wide methylation level in CHG and CHH contexts was below 1% in all analyzed samples (Fig. 2A and table S2) and always higher in repeats (tables S4 and S5).

A principal components analysis (PCA) (Fig. 2B) indicated that, on the basis of the methylation profile, samples are separated according to the geographical location of origin and to the pedigree to which they belong (fig. S1A). We next focused on genes exhibiting high methylation levels [highly methylated genes (HMGs)]to investigate whether methylation is associated with transcriptional variation across strains, cell sizes, and MTs. On the basis of the genome-wide methylation distribution, we defined HMGs as genes with methylation levels above 10% (table S6). Some of these genes showed a partial overlap with repeats (table S6). To evaluate how the methylation status affects the gene expression of HMG, we explored their expression levels across multiple strains and conditions available from previous studies, including different sizes and MTs (Fig. 2C). No general trend emerged across these subsets. In the list of HMGs, we found MRP3, the sex-determining gene for this species, which is expressed exclusively in small MT+ cells (Fig. 2C). We quantified the specificity of MRP3 expression by correlating it against the small MT group and found a high correlation with small MT+ strains and an anticorrelation with small MT− strains (Fig. 2D). We next looked for a correlation between expression and methylation of all the genes in the genome, taking advantage of data from three strains for which we have both RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and EM-seq data, the large MT+ MF11L, the small MT+ 1364-6, and the small MT− 1365-11. MRP3 has similar methylation levels in the first two, but it is expressed only in the small MT+, while it is more methylated and not expressed in the MT− (Fig. 2E). We assessed the global linear relationship between gene body methylation and expression across all genes and found no correlation (R ≈ 0) (Fig. 2E). However, this result is likely reflecting the high proportion of unmethylated genes in the genome, which dilutes the signal from methylated genes. This suggests that the global methylation expression relationship is weak, and meaningful associations may be restricted to the subset of methylated genes. We therefore grouped the genes in highly methylated (>10%) and low or not methylated (<10%) and inspected gene expression differences. Gene expression differed significantly between the two groups in all samples (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, adjusted P < 0.001), with HMGs showing lower median expression (Fig. 2F). Although gene expression differed significantly between the two groups, the effect sizes were small (Cliff’s δ ranging from −0.12 to −0.21), indicating modest but consistent reductions in expression among HMGs.

Of the HMGs, MRP3 is the only one showing a different pattern between the two MT+ and the MT−, with the methylation levels being half in the MT+ with respect to the MT− (Fig. 2E). No similar trend was found for the other MR genes (Fig. 2E). Next, we sought to determine whether there was differential methylation in specific genomic regions across conditions. To this end, we quantified the distribution of differentially methylated regions (DMRs). DMRs were enriched in highly repeated regions. No DMRs were found in the CpG context when comparing cells of different sizes, indicating that the general global CpG methylation patterns in a given strain remain stable during size reduction throughout the life span. When comparing opposite MTs, we found 218 DMRs, of which only eight contained genes (table S7). Among these genes, we found MRP3. However, when comparing randomly selected MT− versus MT−, we also found 170 DMRs, which only contained seven genes (table S8). The two DMR sets do not overlap. MRP3 is therefore the only gene differentially methylated and differentially expressed between MTs. This finding has a relevant implication as it suggests that the P. multistriata sex locus is epigenetically marked.

MRP3 differential methylation is allele specific and inherited

We have shown that in P. multistriata, the MRP3 gene can have different alleles, but only one, inherited exclusively by MT+ strains, is transcriptionally active (15). The alternative allele in MT+ is not transcribed; therefore, the gene has a monoallelic expression. We observed a different methylation pattern of the MRP3 locus in MT+ and MT− (Fig. 3A). In particular, in MT− strains, which do not express MRP3, both alleles are hypermethylated (about 77% average CpG methylation), while in MT+, which expresses MRP3 monoallelically, only one allele is methylated (about 42% average CpG methylation) (Fig. 3A and table S9). MRP3 expression is absent in MT−, both above and below SST, and present only in small, sexually competent MT+ cells (Fig. 3C), while the methylation profile is maintained between large and small cells (Fig. 3A). By comparing SNPs on the reads, it is possible to deduce that the transcribed MRP3 allele is the one that is hypomethylated (Fig. 3B and fig. S2). Monoallelic expression and differential methylation of MRP3 were confirmed in strains from a distant population. In MT+ strains isolated from the Gulf of Naples or from the Gulf of Mexico (16), the MT+ transcribed allele always bore some structural differences when compared to all other nontranscribed alleles (fig. S3). The MT+ NZ5 strain, from New Zealand, deviates from this pattern as it has two alleles with no major structural differences (Fig. 3D and fig. S3). We showed that in this MT+ NZ5 strain, MRP3 retained a monoallelic expression (Fig. 3E) and was up-regulated with respect to an NZ MT− strain (Fig. 3F), and methylation on its two alleles was not identical (Fig. 3G). Also in this case, nucleotide variations along the sequence allowed to demonstrate that the expressed allele is the one with reduced methylation (Fig. 3, E and G). Our finding suggests that the difference in the methylation pattern between MT+ and MT− could play a role in sex determination rather than in sexual competence acquisition and that the MRP3 expression is linked to its methylation profile.

The putative MT locus displays rearrangements in the different alleles

In the reference genome, obtained from the MT+ strain B856 with the Illumina technology (22), MRP3 is contained in a 32-kb gene-poor, repeat-rich region. We sought to explore this region with long-read sequencing and performed Nanopore sequencing of the MT+ strain AB6 (with MRP3 alleles A/B; table S1 and fig. S3). The new assembly was of 65 Mb, coherent with the current estimate of 59 Mb (22), with improved contiguity (Fig. 4A and table S10). The reference genome strain B856 had A/M alleles for MRP3 (15), so this was the first time that we assembled the B allele. MRP3 containing contigs from separated haplotypes are shown in Fig. 4B. The new assembly showed that the coding region of MRP3 in the B allele is interrupted by a simple repeat (Fig. 4B). This has been verified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification in independent strains. Moreover, the new assembly reveals a different size of the putative MT locus, with an interval between the two flanking regions of ∼25 kb for the A allele and of 37 kb for the B allele, due to translocations and insertion of repetitive sequences. The only other gene in the region, 0020760, is absent from the A allele and is close to the MT locus boundary in the B allele with respect to the reference scaffold, due to a translocation (Fig. 4B). We looked at 0020760 expression and found that the gene can be expressed in both MTs and with all the different combinations of alleles for the samples available (fig. S4 and table S11), although there is a tendency to see it expressed when the B allele is present, suggesting that its expression might be facilitated when the gene position is closer to a region less heavily methylated.

Impairing methylation unlocks MRP3 expression and induces partial sex reversal

To test the effect of DNA methylation on sex determination, we treated three different MT− strains, 1339-5, 1339-1, and VD10, with two DNA methyltransferase inhibitors, 5-aza-dC and 5-aza-2′-deoxycytidine (5-aza-2-dC), which are well characterized cytidine analogs that become incorporated into DNA or DNA/RNA respectively but cannot be methylated (23). The inhibitor 5-aza-2-dC did not affect cell growth and did not appear to be effective in reducing methylation (fig. S5). On the other hand, treatment with 5-aza-dC significantly reduced growth rates of P. multistriata strains (Fig. 5A); however, cell morphology remained unaffected, and cells remained viable. The effect of 5-aza-dC on DNA methylation was evaluated 3 days after treatment, the time point at which cells were collected for DNA and RNA extraction. Strain 1339-5 treated with 5-aza-dC was able to produce sexual stages when crossed with MT− and with MT+ strains, indicating partial sex reversal (Fig. 5B). This was not observed in the other two strains, 1339-1 and VD10, which could only mate with the opposite MT. A decrease in genome-wide DNA methylation was observed in the three strains treated with 5-aza-dC but not with 5-aza-2-dC (tables S12 to S15). Strains 1339-5 and 1339-1 showed reductions of ∼32 and 26%, respectively. In contrast, in strain VD10, methylation levels were reduced by about 18%. We next examined the methylation profile of the MRP3 gene in the treated strains. As expected, methylation levels at the MRP3 locus dropped markedly in strains 1339-5 and 1339-1, which showed the most pronounced methylation decrease, while in VD10, the methylation profile of the control and the treated strain was very similar (Fig. 5C). We note that strain 1339-1 has a B/B genotype (fig. S3); therefore, its MRP3 CDS is interrupted by a repeat causing a frameshift. We searched for positions in MRP3 where methylation levels were significantly deviating across all conditions (control versus 5-aza-dC versus 5-aza-2-dC). We found 33 positions where one of the conditions varied significantly from all others (controls: 7, 5-aza-dC: 22, 5-aza-2-dC: 4). Six of seven of the variable positions for the control condition were in VD10, suggesting an overall different methylation distribution compared to the other two strains. Eleven of the 22 variable positions found in 5-aza-dC condition were associated with strain 1339-5, the only strain which could mate with both MTs. In 1339-5, the positions that changed were close to one another and were located at the beginning of the gene body (genomic positions 92724/92727 and 93406/93430/93468) (fig. S5E, bottom), suggesting that methylation at these bases might be of particular importance for MRP3 expression and thereby sex determination.

To determine whether sex reversal in 1339-5 was accompanied by changes in the expression of MR genes, we measured transcript levels using quantitative PCR (qPCR; Fig. 5D). In strain 1339-5, which underwent sex reversal, we observed a clear up-regulation of MT+ associated genes following demethylation. Specifically, MRP1, MRP2, and MRP3 were consistently up-regulated in the treated MT− cells compared to untreated controls, linking the phenotypic switch to MRP3 activation. Notably, the expression of MT− marker genes remained unchanged. In contrast, the two strains that did not undergo sex reversal (1339-1 and VD10) showed only a limited transcriptional response. In these strains, only MRP1 was significantly up-regulated by more than fourfold, while MRP2 and MRP3 did not exhibit a notable change. 5-aza-dC treatments were repeated for confirmation on three more MT- strains: Partial sex reversal was observed in all of them, albeit with a lower efficiency when crossing with MT−, along with MRP3 induction (fig. S6).

Next, we tested whether MRP3 expression was maintained or not after removal of 5-aza-dC in 1339-5 and in VD10. After treating the cells with 5-aza-dC for 3 days, we washed and transferred them into a fresh, drug-free medium. As for the experiments described above, MRP3 expression increased 10-fold in strain 1339-5 treated with 5-aza-dC in comparison to the untreated control, whereas for strain VD10, the increase in MRP3 expression in the sample treated with 5-aza-dC in comparison to the untreated control was minimal (fig. S7). Five days after the washout, both MT− strains resumed growth, dividing around four times, and in 1339-5, MRP3 expression was reduced to half compared to the levels observed in drug-treated cells (fig. S7). These results suggest that the methylation pattern at MRP3 in MT cells is reestablished through mitotic divisions following drug removal, indicating that de novo methylation could occur. While the role of de novo methylation in diatoms is still under investigation, in the P. multistriata genome, we found the methylation regulators reported for other diatoms (table S16) (24).

DISCUSSION

Allele-specific methylation of MRP3

DNA methylation is known to frequently play a role in sex determination mechanisms in eukaryotes (25). Here, we demonstrate a critical role of methylation in sex determination in the marine diatom P. multistriata, where allele-specific hypermethylation of the sex-determining gene MRP3 silenced its expression. In the MT−, both alleles are hypermethylated, and the gene is not expressed, whereas in MT+, the presence of one hypomethylated allele ensures its expression and drives sex determination (Fig. 6). There are few examples of these mechanisms in other systems. An epigenetically regulated, monoallelic sex-specific expression is typical of genomic imprinting, a mechanism which, in plants and animals, involves differential allele expression dependent on epigenetic marks inherited from one or the other of the two parents, maintained throughout the life span (26). MRP3 is monoallelically expressed and epigenetically marked, with its methylation status maintained during the whole vegetative cycle of P. multistriata; however, in this case, the methylation control appears to be due to an allele effect rather than a parental effect. In any case, because the allele methylation is maintained in large and small cells, in P. multistriata, DNA methylation is not a major regulatory mechanism in the switch to sexual competence. MRP3 expression becomes active at the SST switch in the MT+, and we therefore hypothesize that a putative transcription factor recognizing the MRP3 promoter becomes active at the size switch in both MTs, but it can only access the unmethylated allele in the MT+, leading to restricted expression in the MT+ and subsequent activation of the MT+ genetic program. In a transcriptomic study in the diatom Cylindrotheca closterium, 21 genes were found up-regulated in cells below the SST compared to cells above SST, including genes encoding DNA binding proteins which might provide cues to solve the pending question of size sensing and control (27). Additional experiments will be needed to test this hypothesis and identify putative candidates, such as RNA-seq and/or proteomic analyses comparing small versus large cells. MRP3 is only conserved in the genus Pseudo-nitzschia and in the phylogenetically close genus Fragilariopsis (15), and no sex determination mechanism has been identified in Cylindrotheca as yet; therefore, similarities between the two systems remain to be determined.

Structural variations allow to distinguish MT+ specific alleles from alleles shared by both MTs in Neapolitan and Gulf of Mexico strains, but not in a New Zealand strain analyzed in this study, which has two structural identical but differentially methylated and transcriptionally different alleles. This indirectly suggests that there might be subtle variations upstream of MRP3 or other as yet unidentified mechanisms that link lack of methylation and transcriptional activation to the MT+ specific allele. Dedicated experiments should be designed to address these questions, including the study of other epigenetic mechanisms, as discussed below, further comparison of the locus structure in different strains, and a careful dissection of the sequences surrounding MRP3.

In strains 1339-5, MFT13, MNF2, and MNF9, methylation inhibition experiments induced partial sex reversal: Some cells maintained their genetic identity, while others transitioned from a genetic MT− to a phenotypic MT+. EM-seq data for 1339-5 indicate that the 5-aza-dC treatment did not completely erase methylation, as reported in similar studies (28, 29). It is plausible that in a subset of cells, the demethylation was not sufficient to allow MRP3 expression, and these cells therefore continued to behave like MT−. Sex reversal was not observed in two other treated strains, 1339-1 and VD10, and two different explanations can be found. First, an uneven drug efficiency was observed, with a level of demethylation ranging between 32 and 18%, even if all cells were dark synchronized and treated in parallel. We note that dark synchronization is not always effective in arresting P. multistriata cells in G1. In VD10 cells, MRP3 methylation levels did not appear to be substantially different in the control and in the 5-aza-dC–treated samples, indicating that, in this case, the drug was not effective. Strain 1339-1, on the other hand, showed a higher reduction of genome-wide methylation, with levels similar to those of 1339-5. However, 1339-1 is homozygous for the B allele, and long-read sequencing indicated that the B allele has an insertion of a repetitive sequence interrupting the MRP3 coding sequence. This strain therefore has a different, repeat-containing transcript, and we hypothesize that this might be unstable or that the repeat impairs reactivation. While we cannot exclude a function for the transcript, this result supports the idea that the sex reversal is an effect of the expression of a functional MRP3 protein and not related to other general perturbations induced by the drug treatment. Coherently, the MRP1 and MRP2 genes, which act downstream of MRP3 (15), are concomitantly activated in strain 1339-5. The four MR genes and the region in which they are contained do not show any methylation. After removal of 5-aza-dC, MRP3 was found to be partly resilenced. This could be due either to the ability of the cells to restore DNA methylation or to the fact that the least affected cells, with little loss of methylation, are those that resume growth after drug removal. The first scenario cannot be tested yet due to our limited understanding of DNA methylation regulators in diatoms.

A search in the PLAZA Diatoms 1.0 platform for proteins involved in establishing, maintaining, or removing cytosine methylation (24) indicates that P. multistriata, like other diatoms, lacks the DNMT1 paralog, which is required for methylation maintenance, as well as the de novo methyltransferase paralog DNMT3, which is missing in P. multiseries but present in Phaeodactylum tricornutum and Fragilariopsis (30). However, P. multistriata does have DNMT5, the most conserved and functionally validated DNA methyltransferase in diatoms, which is essential for maintaining CpG methylation and silencing transposable elements (24). Methylation marks can be copied at every cell division (31), and it remains to be understood which is the mechanism of protection that maintains the P. multistriata MT+ specific allele unmethylated during meiosis, and how the non-A alleles are specifically targeted for methylation. In plants, the paternally or maternally inherited allele remains unmethylated through a balance of active demethylation, chromatin remodeling, and histone modifications (12). In the diatom P. tricornutum, histone modifications have been shown to influence genome stability, gene expression, and environmental adaptation (19, 30, 32), while no data are available in Pseudo-nitzschia genus. The presence of histone marks on the MRP3 gene and in the surrounding region should be tested. Alternatively, a different content of repetitive elements between the A allele and the other alleles could be linked to differential methylation, as discussed in the next paragraph.

DNA methylation remains a fundamental epigenetic mechanism for regulating gene expression, acting as a repressive mark at promoters, but its effects can vary on the basis of genomic location and chromatin state. In P. multistriata, as reported for other species, DNA methylation was mostly occurring on repetitive sequences and almost negligible for coding genes. The comparison of methylation and transcription data for coding sequences from the same strain suggested a weak trend between the gene methylation and the absence of transcription. Although the interplay between DNA methylation and histone modifications in diatoms remains poorly understood, our findings suggest that methylation plays a more complex role than merely silencing repetitive sequences, highlighting the need for further investigation into its regulatory functions. Ge et al. (33) reported that DNA methylation mediates active recombination suppression independently of structural divergence among alleles within the MT locus of the green alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, supporting a role for this epigenetic mechanism in regulating recombination at sex-determining regions.

MT locus structure

In the present study, we provide evidence that P. multistriata harbors a primordial sex-determining region, gene poor, repeat rich, and with a distinctive methylation profile. As nonrecombining regions of sex chromosomes, this region appears to have accumulated repetitive sequences, linked to recombination suppression (34). Repetitive sequences are usually silenced by DNA methylation and/or histone modifications and play a role in determining evolutionary changes in sex chromosomes. Long-read sequencing indicated that, compared to the region present in the reference assembly, variations, translocations, and small inversions are present in the different alleles, as expected for regions where recombination has been lost. The putative MT-determining region in the A allele, with transcribed MRP3, appears to be 12 kb shorter than the region found in the B allele, missing mostly repetitive sequences. Whether the presence of these sequences could be linked to heavier methylation and eventually to MRP3 silencing remains to be investigated.

We have already reported loss-of-function mutations in the nontranscribed MT− alleles of three P. multistriata resequenced strains (17), and by sequencing the genome of a B allele–containing strain, we now observed a disruptive insertion in the MRP3 coding sequence, confirming that disruptive variations can accumulate in allelic variants that bear a nontranscribed MRP3 sequence. MRP3 monoallelic expression (35) and differential methylation were confirmed in New Zealand strains, which are the most genetically distant P. multistriata strains from the Gulf of Naples strains, as shown in a population genomic study (17). This confirms that the mechanism identified in this study is not restricted to a specific population. Long-read sequencing also provided the valuable opportunity to produce a new genome assembly and new gene models, improved with respect to the reference data produced over 10 years ago.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Strains, culture condition, and experimental crosses

P. multistriata strains were isolated from the long-term monitoring station MareChiara (LTER-MC) in the Gulf of Naples (36) and from the coasts of New Zealand (35), and F1 strains were manually isolated from experimental crosses of strains from LTER-MC (table S1). Individual P. multistriata cells were isolated from the water sample with a micropipette and grown in f/2 medium supplemented with silicates (37) at 18°C, 50 μmol photons m−2 s−1, and a 12-hour light:12-hour dark photoperiod. To test sex compatibility and assess the strains MT, they were crossed with reference MT− and MT+ strains isolated at LTER-MC using the same procedure described in (38). Crosses were carried out in six-well culture plates (Corning Costar) filled with 3 ml of f/2 medium. Exponentially growing cultures of pairs of strains were inoculated at a final cell concentration of ∼6 × 103 cells ml−1. Culture plates were incubated at the culture conditions indicated above for 5 days and inspected daily to check for the presence of sexual stages. Single large F1 cells were isolated with a micropipette.

The isolated F1 initial cells (around 75 μm in length) were grown under the mentioned culture conditions for 6 months to reduce their size. F1 large (L, above 55 μm) and small (S, below 55 μm) cells were isolated from the mixed-size culture using a micropipette. Cultures of large and small F1 cells were collected in duplicate and processed for DNA and RNA extraction.

DNA extraction

Before extraction of DNA, strains were grown in f/2 medium supplemented with antibiotics, as described in (39). The absence of bacteria was tested as described in (40). Genomic DNA was extracted following the protocol described in (41) and quantified using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies), while integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

MT-determining region characterization

PCRs to amplify the region around the triplet repeats were performed using WonderTaq (Euroclone SPA). PrimeSTAR GXL DNA Polymerase (Takara Bio, USA) was used in all other PCRs, and when the fragments with the triplet repeats had to be amplified for subsequent Sanger sequencing, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, using the primers reported in (15, 16). The MRP3 (GenBank ID MW116958) amplicons were obtained with a couple of primers that amplified the complete coding sequence, FMrp3Eco and RMrp3Sma (15). Sanger sequencing was performed on an Applied Biosystems (Life Technologies, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) 3730 Analyzer 48 capillaries using the primers reported in (15, 16).

Enzymatic methyl sequencing library preparation and sequencing platform

Libraries for 24 samples (table S1) were prepared using the NEBNext Enzymatic Methyl-seq Kit (New England Biolabs, Massachusetts, USA) following the manufacturer’s large insert libraries protocol. On the basis of the quality assessment, the samples were standardized depending on the available amount of DNA in a volume of 50 μl, including a spike-in of pUC and Lambda DNA provided with the kit, as a control for methylation efficiency as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The samples were fragmented using the Covaris S2 system, with settings to achieve an average fragment size of 350 to 400 bp. The samples were individually barcoded during the PCR using the PCR cycle numbers as per the manufacturer’s protocol depending on used input. The libraries were quantified using the Qubit HS DNA assay following the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and molarity of the libraries were assessed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer with the DNA HS Assay kit as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The assessed molarity was used to equimolarly combine individual libraries into pools for sequencing. All samples were sequenced with the Illumina NextSeq 550/2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using a 300-cycle kit and a read-length of 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw data are available with the accession number PRJNA889340.

Sequencing data processing and methylation extraction

The raw reads were quality checked with fastqc v0.11.9 (42). Mapping and methylation extraction and quantification were done with Bismark v0.23.0, using bismark_genome_preparation with default parameters to index the reference genome (22), then reads were aligned with default parameters (table S17), and the resulting bam files were deduplicated. Validation of the files was done with the ValidateSamFile program v2.26.6 of Picard (Broad Institute, 2019), and mapping statistics were called with qualimap v2.2.2 (43). Methylation was extracted with the bismark_methylation_extractor with --comprehensive option.

To determine the average methylation level genome wide as well as in different genomic features [CDS, exon, 5′ untranslated region (5′UTR), genes, mRNAs, repeats, 3′UTR, TSS, and TTS], the methylation level of each cytosine of a certain context within a feature was determined (methylated reads divided by total reads, only considering sites with at least five reads), and then the average was calculated. PCA was performed in R (v 4.4.3) with methylKit v1.32.1 (44) using the Bismark cytosine reports as input. Only CpG context was considered for the analysis. To ensure high-quality data, CpG sites with a minimum coverage of 10× in all samples were retained. Coverage normalization was applied using default scaling factors to account for differences in sequencing depth. To mitigate potential PCR bias, sites with coverage exceeding the 99.9th percentile were excluded. All the samples were merged into a single database, and SD was calculated for each CpG site based on its methylation percentage. According to the variance distribution curve, the top 50,000 hypervariable CpG sites were selected for PCA to minimize background noise. PCA results were visualized with the ggplot2 package (45).

Identifying HMGs

CpG methylation levels in each gene and for each sample were determined via ViewBS v0.1.11 MethHeatmap command. Since global CpG methylation levels in the samples ranged between 7.6 and 9.8%, we considered only genes with a methylation level >10% in all of the samples as highly methylated. HMGs were screened for overlap with repetitive elements. First, to prevent overestimation of overlapping repetitive elements, these were collapsed using bedtools v 2. 30. 0, with the merge function (46). Subsequently, the percentage of overlapping between genes and the collapsed repetitive elements was calculated with bedtools intersect.

Determining DMRs

DMRs were determined with the R package DMRcaller v1.26.0 and Bismark cytosine reports. DMRs were determined for comparison of small cells (libraries MF9SMALL1, MF9SMALL2, MF11SMALL1, and mf11small2_new) versus large cells (libraries MF9BIG1, MF9BIG2, MF11BIG1, and MF11BIG2), as well as MT− samples (13395_Control, 13391_Control, VD2, VD10_Control, MF1, NZ10, MF9BIG1, MF9BIG2, MF9SMALL1, MF9SMALL2, and 1365_11_l67) versus MT+ samples (MF11BIG1, MF11BIG2, MF3, MF11SMALL1, mf11small2_new, and NZ05, 1364_6_l68).

Valid DMRs in CpG context needed to have a P value of ≤0.01, a minimum size of 50 bp, a cytosine count of 5, a minimum of five reads per cytosine, and a minimum proportional difference in methylation level of 30% between the comparing groups (small versus large cells and MT− versus MT+ samples). As suggested for CpG DMRs by Catoni and coauthors (47), a noise filter method with triangular kernel for noise filtering was used and the Score test as statistical test. For DMRs in the CHG context, the same parameters were used with the only modification of allowing DMRs with a difference minimum of 20% between the compared conditions. Last, for CHH context DMRs, the bins method was used (with a bin size of 100), and minimal methylation difference was reduced to 10% difference between compared conditions.

For the comparison of large versus small cells, an additional analysis was done for DMRs in the CpG context, accepting DMRs with only a single differentially methylated cytosine between the two conditions and a minimal proportion difference required of only 10%. With the bedtools v2.30.0 (46) intersect command (applying –wa and –wb options), we determined DMRs overlapping with genes.

Evaluating gene expression levels for HMG genes

The gene expression of the HMG genes was visualized for 33 samples from published and newly generated bulk RNA-seq datasets (22, 48) (E-MTAB-12676) using the R package “pheatmap.” To inspect whether gene expression patterns might be influenced by methylation levels of HMRs, we categorized the samples in four groups (MT+/small, MT+/large, MT−/small, and MT−/large) and correlated (Pearson) each sample’s gene expression with a group-specific pseudo-bulk (expression level 10 or 0). The correlation results for only the small MT groups were visualized as boxplots using “ggplot2.”

Correlating gene expression and methylation levels within the same samples

The methylation and gene expression levels within three samples were investigated as an alternative approach to defining the list of genes whose gene expression is potentially influenced by methylation levels. Some samples were measured in replicates, and the expression or methylation levels were averaged before the analysis. A correlation (Pearson) test [cor.test()] was performed resulting in values close to 0, indicating no correlation between gene expression and methylation on a global level. To lower the effect of the much higher number of nonmethylated genes in the distribution we binned the data into methylated (>10% methylated) versus low or not methylated (<10% methylated) and visualized the gene expression as boxplots. The expression distributions were compared by a Wilcox test, and we adjusted the P value with the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method. To estimate the effect size, we used the R package effsize and calculated the cliff’s delta (MF11_large: −0.116, 1364_6: −0.170, and 1365_11: −0.207).

Identifying positions in MRP3 that vary across strains and treatments

To identify deviations in methylation levels at specific genomic positions within MRP3, we first calculated the average methylation level and its variability at each position across all samples. For every genomic position, the mean normalized methylation value and corresponding SD were computed. Next, we quantified how strongly each individual methylation measurement deviates from the typical distribution at its respective position by calculating a z-score. The z-score was defined as the difference between the observed normalized methylation value and the position-specific mean, divided by the position-specific SD: z = (normalized observed methylation − mean methylation at each position)/SD at each position. This standardization expresses each methylation value in terms of SDs from the position-specific average, allowing direct comparison across sites with different baseline methylation levels and variability. Positions were classified as showing significant deviation when the absolute z-score exceeded 1.8 (|z| > 1.8). Thus, methylation levels that differed by more than 1.8 SDs from the mean distribution at a given site were considered substantially altered relative to the expected variation at that position.

DNA methylation regulators

DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) orthologs in P. multistriata were determined by searching in the PLAZA Diatoms 1.0 platform (49) the gene IDs based on table 1 in (32), which summarizes putative methylation regulators (proteins to set, maintain, or remove cytosine methylation) in three different diatom species.

Nanopore sequencing and assembly

DNA from the AB6 strain was used for library preparation according to the Ligation Sequencing DNA V14 kit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK), following the provided MinION user manual (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK). Briefly, DNA repair and ends preparation were performed using the NEB Next FFPE DNA Repair kit (New England Biolabs, Massachusetts, USA) and the NEB Next Ultra II End Repair/dA-tailing Module (New England Biolabs, Massachusetts, USA), respectively, and the adaptor was ligated using NEB Next Quick T4 Ligase (New England Biolabs, Massachusetts, USA). Purification was performed at every step using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Inc., Brea, USA). After library preparation, sequencing was conducted using R10.4.1 FLO-MIN114 flow cells with a MinION Mk1b device. Sequence reads were basecalled using Dorado v7.3.11 integrated into the MinKNOW software (v24.02.8; Oxford Nanopore Technologies), yielding 3.01 million reads.

For each dataset, k-mer frequencies were calculated with Jellyfish (50), and histograms were generated using the -histo command. These histograms were then analyzed with GenomeScope v2.0 (51) to estimate genome size, repeat content, and heterozygosity. Reads were then assembled using Hifiasm v0.25 (52) using the --ont parameter. Two different haplotypes were generated and evaluated for completeness and contiguity. However, given the suboptimal results, the two haplotypes generated were concatenated, and the purging step was repeated using purge_dups v1.2.5 (51). This approach led to an improved quality and accuracy, as assessed by Quast v5.0.2 (53) and by completeness metrics using BUSCO (54) compleasm (55) and Merqury (56). Merqury revealed high consensus accuracy, and the AB6 primary haplotype showed a Quality Value (QV) of 52.83, corresponding to a per-base error rate of 5.22 × 10−6, indicating high base-level accuracy (QV > 50). Before submission, the genome assembly was screened for exogenous contamination using FCS-GX v0.5.5 (57). On the basis of the FCS-GX results, flagged contigs as putative contaminants were removed or trimmed before downstream analyses. Raw data have been deposited with the Bioproject ID PRJNA1329128, and the assembly ID GCA_058380785.1.

Genome annotation

Repetitive elements were annotated using a combination of homology-based and de novo approaches. For de novo prediction, a custom repeat library was generated with EDTA v2.2.2 (58). This library was then used with RepeatMasker to detect previously unidentified repetitive sequences in the genome. The homology-based analysis was carried out with RepeatMasker v4.1.8 (http://repeatmasker.org/), using the RepBase library to identify sequences homologous to known repetitive elements. Protein-coding genes were annotated using BRAKER3 (59), an integrated gene prediction pipeline that leverages both protein and transcriptomic evidence. Protein data were obtained from annotated genomes within the Bacillariophyceae family available in the National Center for Biotechnology Information Datasets (60), while RNA-seq datasets used can be found in the Bioproject PRJNA287788. Functional annotation of the predicted genes was performed using InterProScan (61) integrating multiple publicly available databases (table S18).

Syntenic analysis

Synteny analysis of the genomic regions containing MRP3 among the scaffold 147 of P. multistriata B856 (alleles A/M) and alleles A and B of the AB6 strain was performed. Protein sequences spanning from gene 0020660 to gene 0020800 of the scaffold 147 were used to identify the homologous genes for comparison. Synteny maps were generated and visualized with the SimpleSynteny webserver (https://dveltri.com/simplesynteny) using default parameters, enabling the construction of comparative diagrams that link homologous genes across alleles. The SimpleSynteny output was used to produce the image in Fig. 4B with edits to represent the gene structures and the length of the different segments.

DNA methylation inhibition and wash-out assay

5-aza-dC (CAS:320-67-2. Sigma-Aldrich, Milwaukee, USA) and 5-aza-2 dc (CAS: 2353-33-5. Sigma-Aldrich, Milwaukee, USA) were prepared in 50% dimethyl sulfoxide and 50% ice-cold MilliQ water and used at 20 μM final concentration. Lower doses were tested, but no effect was observed neither on cell growth nor on gene expression levels. Stock solutions were stored at −80°C; aliquots were thawed on ice before treatment to prevent drug instability and breakdown. P. multistriata cells were synchronized with prolonged darkness for 36 hours. Following reillumination, 5-aza-dC and 5-aza-2 dc were added once a day for 3 days in the light cycle. Cell count was performed every day using the Malassez counting chamber. At the end of the treatment, cells were harvested for DNA and RNA extraction. To test sex reversal, a few microliters of the control and treated strains were incubated with strains of the same and opposite MTs under standard conditions for 5 days and inspected daily using a DM3000B Leica microscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) to check for the presence of sexual stages and capture images. Treatments for strains 1339-1, 1339-5, and VD10 were repeated three times, and treatments for MNF2, MNF3, and MFT13 were repeated twice. For the wash-out assay, after 5-aza-dC treatment, cells were washed in f/2 medium and incubated for 5-day recovery time. At the end of the recovery time, cells were harvested for gene expression analysis.

RNA extraction, RNA-seq, and gene expression analysis

RNA extractions were performed as described (62). RNA quantity was determined using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and integrity using a 2100 Bioanalyzer System (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). RNA-seq library preparation and sequencing libraries for three samples (table S1) were performed at the Genecore Facility of the European Molecular Biology Laboratory (EMBL) in Germany as described in (48). Raw data are available in ArrayExpress with the accession number E-MTAB-12676. A total of 0.5 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed using the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Gene expression levels were evaluated by qPCR using primers reported in (15, 16). The qPCR experiments were performed in triplicate in a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using PowerTrack SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The reference genes used were TUB-A and CDK-A (63). The relative gene expression quantification was calculated with the following formula: relative expression ratio of the gene of interest is 2−∆CT with ∆CT = Ct(target gene) − CT(reference gene).

Gene fold change relative expression was made following the ∆-∆-Ct method. The results are the mean SD of at least three separate experiments, measuring each parameter by triplicate (n = 3).

Statistical analysis

To determine whether there were significant differences in gene expression, between the different strains and the conditions, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was performed. Pairwise post hoc Tukey tests were applied for all multiple comparison procedures when differences were found significant (P < 0.05). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v.10.2.1. Growth rates were calculated as the slope of the linear regression of ln-transformed cell abundance versus time during the exponential growth phase. Differences in growth rates between treatments were evaluated using a two-tailed Student’s t test.
