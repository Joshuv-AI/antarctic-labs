Person‐Centered Care Planning for People With Multiple Chronic Conditions: An Environmental Scan of Models and Approaches
Authors: Annette M Totten, David A Dorr, Arlene S Bierman, Kisha Coa, Chandler Atchison, Cynthia Davis‐O'Reilly, Katherine D Peak, Shannon Sweeney, Deborah J Cohen, Ana R Quiñones
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionPerson‐centered care planning (PCCP) involves active collaboration between people seeking care, clinical teams, and others to co‐create longitudinal treatment plans. It is a crucial part of care for people with multiple chronic conditions (MCCs) and other complex needs. Despite the widespread acceptance of the concept, the use of PCCP in the US is variable. Many effective models of PCCP exist but uptake has been limited.ObjectiveWe sought to identify and assess current models and approaches for PCCP by performing a multi‐component environmental scan.MethodsWe conducted targeted literature reviews based on streamlined systematic review methods and qualitative interviews with key informants, identified for their relevant expertise and frontline knowledge of PCCP models.ResultsIn all, 966 abstracts and 187 full articles met review criteria. We identified 40 models with elements fitting into 7 categories of how they differed from usual care (adding People/Roles, innovative Payment/Incentives, novel Technology, decision support Tools, Services, Functions, and Changing Focus). Most were multi‐component models with evidence of effectiveness in outcomes that address the quintuple aim. Barriers to adoption and integration across sectors were primarily lack of time and appropriate payment mechanisms. A small set of measures for PCCP was identified that focused primarily on patient experience and goal setting. Similarly, a small number of PCCP models connected with social services organizations to address health‐related social needs and highlighted challenges with data sharing and payment as well as growing collaborations in community care hubs to address social needs. KI interviews echoed and deepened our understanding of these findings by contextualizing experiences with barriers and factors facilitating the delivery of PCCP.DiscussionPCCP is a key aspect of high‐quality care for people with MCCs; uptake is limited with barriers related to time and resources. Facilitators include alignment with healthcare system objectives and practices, and ready‐to‐deploy resources that enable coordinated PCCP care across providers. To promote greater adoption of PCCP concerted efforts are needed to share implementation details, success stories, and build the case for PCCP with patients, their families, and healthcare system leaders.Keywords: care planning, multiple chronic conditions, older adults, person‐centered care, practice change, scoping review

Introduction

Person‐centered care planning (PCCP) is a process of active collaboration and shared decision‐making among health and social service professionals, people seeking care, their families, and caregivers to develop a feasible, person‐centered longitudinal treatment plan that fits into the person's life context. PCCP considers the whole person—their social circumstances, goals, and ultimately what matters most to them.

PCCP frequently targets patients with serious illnesses but is particularly well attuned to addressing the needs of older adults managing multiple chronic conditions (MCC) [1, 2, 3]; yet, it is not standard practice, and uptake is slow [4, 5].

We identified PCCP approaches across diverse US healthcare settings and factors to facilitate adoption as an integral component of routine practice for people with MCC. We conducted an environmental scan to identify and characterize recent PCCP models, describe implementation experiences, and summarize evidence of effectiveness. Two rapid scans on complementary topics followed: one to describe measures of PCCP implementation and another on healthcare collaborations with social services organizations essential to addressing health‐related social needs (HRSN) as part of PCCP. Reports for each scan were posted on the AHRQ and John A. Hartford Foundation websites [6, 7] as components of a larger project.

Methods

The main scan topic was defined in the project contract, while the two rapid scan topics were identified through technical expert panel (TEP), project leadership, and partner input. All scans were multicomponent, consisting of targeted literature reviews using streamlined systematic review methods [8], and qualitative interviews with key informants with relevant expertise.

The main scan addressed the questions: which models of PCCP have been implemented for adults with MCC in the US? and, what implementation barriers and facilitators have been reported for identified models? The rapid scans addressed the questions: which measures are used to assess PCCP implementation? and, which PCCP models incorporate collaborations between healthcare organizations and social services organizations to address HRSN?

Targeted Literature Review

We provide brief summarization of methods here with more detail in Appendices A and B. Search strategies were based on definitions of key concepts mapped to medical subject headings (MeSH) and key words (Appendix Table A‐1). An expert librarian executed searches of MEDLINE for articles published January 2008–April 2024. The searches for the rapid scans built on main searches but were topically focused. Consequently, we did not set a start date and included articles published through November 2024.

Several key articles published prior to 2008 were obtained from bibliographies of included articles if they were necessary for understanding models. We supplemented MEDLINE with gray literature to include unpublished reports and non‐peer‐reviewed electronic sources.

Abstracts were triaged by a single reviewer. Full texts were dual‐reviewed with disagreements adjudicated by the team. We included articles for PCCP models implemented in the US for adults (≥ 18 years) with MCC in outpatient, long‐term care, or residential care settings; we excluded inpatient and emergency departments. For studies that evaluated outcomes, we included findings on costs, quality of care, patient/clinician experience, and equity. The measures rapid scan focused on measures specific to PCCP implementation. When searching for examples of HRSN addressed in PCCP, we included collaborations with social services organizations but did not include programs run by healthcare organizations themselves. Detailed inclusion/exclusion criteria provided in Appendix Table B‐1.

We limited data abstraction to settings, populations, and care components focusing on: (1) how the model differed from usual care; (2) implementation context, barriers, and facilitators; (3) results of evaluations and (4) additional information specific to the rapid scans. Abstracted data tables are available in the full reports [6, 7, 9, 10, 11, 12].

In assessing how models differed from usual care, we identified all elements that characterized them, and one or two of these that were essential—which we termed defining elements—meaning, to remove them would fundamentally alter the program and constitute a different approach to care. Designation of defining elements was made by the team after reviewing texts and consulting model developers and the TEP.

Key Informant Interviews

We purposively selected clinical leaders, clinical teams, patients, researchers, community‐based organization and federal agency representatives as key informants (KIs) [13]. We aimed to maximize variation in expertise, including experience implementing PCCP, familiarity with federal and state policies, organizational procedures, clinical practices that affect implementation, knowledge of PCCP‐specific measures or of social services organization collaborations. AHRQ partners, project leadership, and the TEP informed selection of KIs.

We developed semi‐structured interview guides for each scan (available in the full report [6, 7].) and tailored guides based on KI expertise. We used an iterative sampling strategy, analyzing interviews when completed, allowing us to refine data collection and interview guide as needed and use what we learned to inform selection of subsequent KIs. Interviews lasted ~60 min, were conducted using video conferencing software, audio‐recorded with permission, professionally transcribed, de‐identified, and reviewed for accuracy. Interviews were conducted July 2024–February 2025.

We analyzed all interviews using a five‐step process outlined by Miller and Crabtree: Describing, Organizing, Connecting, Corroborating/Legitimating, and Representing the account [14]. We reviewed transcripts for emerging patterns, and refined findings based on group discussion. We examined each interview and conducted a cross‐case analysis to compare interviewee experiences. Findings were integrated into a cross‐KI summary that highlighted similarities and differences and identified factors connected to variations.

Results

We included sources and articles as follows: 58 articles on the identified PCCP models; 9 documents on PCCP implementation measures; and 26 sources on social services collaborations.

All 40 models contained multiple elements across categories. The most common defining element was People/New Roles. Some roles supported interdisciplinary team care, others added pairs of professionals (e.g., nursing and social work) while several added a lay person (e.g., community health worker). Models that added People also added other elements, most frequently Functions (e.g., case management). The second largest group of models was defined by Changing Focus. Most focused on patient goals, values, and preferences, such as Age Friendly Health Systems, a comprehensive model implementing the 4 M geriatrics framework (What Matters Most, Medication, Mentation, Mobility). Examples of Evidence on PCCP Effectiveness

For 27 of the 40 models, we identified recent evaluations that assessed included outcomes. We summarized these results to provide an overview of the body of evidence about PCCP effectiveness across outcomes reflecting costs, satisfaction, quality of care, and equity (Appendix Table C‐1).

In approximately two‐thirds of these studies, PCCP resulted in better or similar outcomes than the comparator. While not comprehensive, this suggests that PCCP models—which varied in how and where they were implemented—were broadly successful relative to usual care, and responsive to the needs of people managing MCC.

We abstracted barriers and facilitators to implementing PCCP reported in the published literature and grouped these into themes. An abbreviated set that typified the range of experienced barriers and facilitators in a variety of settings is presented in Tables 2 and 3. The complete list is available in the full report [6, 7].

Reported challenges with implementing PCCP spanned routine issues of integrating a new approach into practice (Identifying Program Targets and Challenges with Implementation) to more fundamental roadblocks (Misalignment with Existing Policies, Instilling Culture Change), and cautionary lessons of ensuring up‐front investments are made (Changing Roles and Organizational Structures, Need for More Resources). Many reported challenges with staff turnover, shortages, and having sufficient time to meaningfully engage in PCCP. Perhaps the most difficult challenge involved changing culture and convincing leaders to invest in systems that are navigable, comprehensive, and coordinated for people with MCC.

Facilitators were nearly the converse of reported barriers. These included aspects that made it possible to deliver PCCP (Flexibility to Manage Team Roles and Functions, Effective Team Composition, Effective Implementation) or needed structural changes (Attaining Culture Change, Additional Resources, Alignment with Policies). Several models noted that aligning program goals with healthcare system mission and workflow was key to their success. Similarly, healthcare organizations oriented to person‐centered care, prevention, avoiding unnecessary care, and rewarding team care were fertile ground for adoption of PCCP models.

Collaborations to Address HRSNs Results

We identified a small number of examples of collaboration between healthcare and social services organizations as part of PCCP in the second rapid scan. The importance of identifying social drivers that impact health outcomes was underscored along with the challenges encountered when trying to meet needs. The descriptions highlighted challenges related to data sharing and payment but also noted evolving collaborations, such as using community care hubs to connect individuals with relevant social care support services.

Key Informant Interview Results

We interviewed 18 KIs for the main scan: seven KIs represented patient, research, healthcare, social services organization perspectives and six government KI representatives from federal agencies. Five total KIs were interviewed for the rapid scans.

KIs provided insights that aligned with and expanded findings from the literature. They described the value of PCCP, highlighted barriers to implementation, and emphasized the importance of addressing health disparities. Notable findings are summarized here with full findings and quotations in Appendix Table A‐4.

Key Informant Findings

Variety of Ways to Deliver PCCP

KIs described a multitude of models; no two people cited the same model. All models emphasized asking questions to learn about patient goals in a way that made patients feel heard. KIs stated that getting to know their patients more deeply was critical and formed the basis to create longer term care plans. KIs mentioned using motivational interviewing and narrative medicine to elicit responses and needed to give time to the individual to describe their wishes, actively listen, and document goals. KIs noted a lack of training to engage in conversations and needed resources to direct patients to supportive services. Greater patient awareness was also highlighted to help patients understand PCCP.

Time and Misaligned Incentives in Current Payment Models Are Barriers

KIs consistently noted that the greatest barrier to implementing PCCP was the time it required, and challenges associated with being compensated for providing this kind of care. KIs felt far removed from downstream cost savings of preventing avoidable and costly urgent or intensive care, making documentation of any return on investment challenging.

I was thinking one of the real challenges is if [a clinician] decided to spend more time with one of their patients you'll actually get dinged in your P&L [profit & loss] statement even if [those] two hours prevented the person from ricocheting between the skilled nursing facility and the hospital. You could have actually saved your health system $20,000, $30,000, a $100,000. But none of that money will ever flow back down to you. No one's ever going to say thank you for doing that. You will in fact get dinged for doing that. I think the incentives are actually quite contrary. —Health services researcher

Internal Federal Opportunities for Expansion of PCCP

Government KIs saw opportunities to expand PCCP aligned with the strengths and foci of each of their agencies. These ideas capture how to expand PCCP in various ways, including making PCCP a requirement of funding, creating more educational materials, utilizing technology to facilitate PCCP, and using policy levers of measurement and payment to change behavior. This underscores the importance of aligning financial incentives. For example, CMS continues to use payment incentives to change behavior by including care planning and processes to improve quality of care with reimbursement codes and payment methodologies.

Importance of Addressing Health Disparities

One of the patient KIs we spoke with described the importance of addressing health disparities with the delivery PCCP by meeting people where they are and tailoring care to what matters most. A key requirement is tailoring care to ensure that, “equity is just baked in.”

I want to say the old adage meet people where they are, but that's really what you have to do. And they're maybe bringing the services to the person if they need it. We can do that using physical transportation, whether bringing the person to the resource or bringing the resource to the person. Ideally, a lot of things can be done virtually…we have to be sensitive to the way that people receive information best, whether it's over Zoom or reading like my mom likes to do. —Person with MCC and patient engagement consultant

Adoption of Goal‐Setting Measures Is Limited

KIs reported there is no specific measure developed to assess the quality of PCCP. Some existing measures, such as the person‐centered care goal‐setting measure set, are aligned with components of PCCP. KIs identified factors related to adoption worth considering as measurement efforts progress.

The goal‐setting measure set has limited adoption because, according to a KI from the National Committee for Quality Assurance (NCQA), many organizations are “not ready” to adopt this measure set and it “may take people some time” to implement them. To promote adoption, this KI suggests incorporating relevant measures into core sets and payment models such as state value‐based payments, the Merit‐based Incentive Payment System (MIPS), and Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set (HEDIS).

KIs identified two factors to consider when developing and adding new measures for clinicians and healthcare teams: measurement burden and time. Clinicians feel burdened by measures that require them to track everything they are doing and not what is most clinically meaningful. One way to minimize documentation burden is to design a PCCP tool in EHRs where completion serves as a process measure and documentation is tied closely to clinical workflow to make it seamless.

The barrier really is the time, the additional time to have these conversations and finding a place in your system to document it and then having it be able to be transferred and reported out. A lot of times this depends on funding, who you have on staff, you have to do a complete build‐out…making sure that they have the necessary fields to document this in a reportable manner that can be transferred out. But that is one of the biggest issues for sites really, if they have the team to be able to make the changes in their existing systems. —NCQA representative

KIs note that while most EHRs allow for extensions and adaptations, this requires motivation from systems, resources to ease implementation and facilitate changes, and the lack of documentation requirements for PCCP. Additionally, many individuals see clinicians using different EHRs; the lack of interoperability places a barrier to data sharing and care coordination. However, a CMS KI noted that an enhanced care planning data element is under consideration for the standardized set of data elements for nationwide health information exchange in the US Core Data for Interoperability (draft USCDI v6 [15]). EHR infrastructure is evolving to improve interoperability of digital health records with adoption of standards to maximize sharing across systems to ensure “the users of the technology, the workforce, people who are capturing that information, whether it be the physicians or the nurses and having that be easily available in their workflow and not be burdensome.”

Collaborations With Social Services Organizations

KIs reported similar processes for identifying and addressing HRSNs. They described challenges to partnerships between clinical organizations and community‐based organizations, including a lack of willingness to collaborate and understanding around payment. KIs also noted that funding, programs, policies and partnerships are crucial and emphasized the need for sharing successes to grow this work.

Similar Processes of Identifying and Addressing HRSNs

KIs reported that community organizations use similar processes for identifying HRSNs and use a standard script to do this. We spoke with one KI leader in one Area Agency on Aging (AAA) who noted that staff are trained to listen for needs outside of the primary reason for contact, connect individuals to a broader set of services, and recognize when a home visit is needed. KIs pointed out that clinicians and individuals in need of services are often unaware of all the services a community has to offer. Staff are critical to understanding the complex and evolving web of services available in local communities critical to supporting complex MCC needs.

To promote the identification of HRSNs in clinical settings, a health and social care integration consultant KI described the importance of the new community health integration billing codes. These codes require the use of an evidence‐based screening tool for HRSNs, such as the Accountable Health Communities Tool. To bill, “clinicians need to determine that the social need has a direct impact on the ability of the provider to treat or diagnose a condition.” Additional training is available to clinicians on how to utilize this screening tool, make referrals to community‐based organizations, and bill for services.

Funding, Programs, Policies, and Partnerships Are Critical to Adoption

KIs described the importance of funding, programs, policies, and partnerships to facilitate PCCP that addresses identified social needs. One KI provided an example from one of the emerging models—the GUIDE program (Guiding an Improved Dementia Experience)—launched by CMS in July 2024. This program provides payments for a comprehensive package of care coordination, care management, and fosters partnerships with community‐based organizations to connect to services such as transportation.

I think that you're going to need a lot of additional models such as the GUIDE program. The reason being, I applaud CMS and ACL [Administration for Community Living], they have worked very hard to get physician fee schedules, to get different funding mechanisms so that we can access [the funding]. —AAA Leader

To utilize these program resources with patients, KIs reminded us that healthcare organizations need to leverage partnership with community‐based organizations and/or develop in‐house expertise. Both options require leaders to have knowledge of the programs, workforce, clinical champions, value‐based risk modeling, and billing support needed to adopt and implement social needs screening and support.

Challenges of Partnerships

KIs described several challenges for clinical organizations in partnering with community‐based social services organizations. One challenge is the lack of knowledge that clinical leaders have about their ability to contract with community‐based organizations.

The providers will say yes [to contracting with a community‐based organization], and then their risk management team will be like, “Well, wait, we never heard of contracting with a community‐based organization, that can't be allowed.” Then I've got to give them the rules and they're like, “Wow, this is truly a CMS document? So yeah, I guess we can.” And then we start talking. So sometimes it's getting past those hurdles. —Healthcare and social care integration consultant

Education is needed at multiple levels to help clinical organizations understand the contracting process with community‐based organizations. Helping organization leaders understand the costs, return on investment for these partnerships, and how to bill for services partners provide (e.g., aggregate billing) is critical (Integration Consultant).

In addition to developing contracts, we heard that making referrals, sharing patient information, and being HIPAA compliant is a challenge. Many community‐based organizations do not use EHRs. Concerns were raised about attention to, and use of the information community‐based organizations share with a clinical organization after they work with one of their clients. The AAA KIs recognized that “it is work to work with us.” Nevertheless, they felt this collaboration was important and were working toward implementing an EHR to continue to expand access to social services.

Sharing Stories of Success

KIs thought having more stories about successful implementation of PCCP that addressed identified HRSNs was important as inspiration and information on how to do this work.

The one thing that I feel that we're lacking in terms of speed and adoption is clearly documenting successes from the field. Because clinicians like evidence, they want to see, “Are my peers doing this? And who are the peers that are doing this? And oh, they're doing it, and I trained there, so I should be doing it.” We have failed to capture and highlight those successes. That is something that we're focused on…documenting what has worked, highlighting what has worked, and sharing that with others so they can replicate. —Healthcare and social care integration consultant

Discussion

This multi‐component environmental scan identified and categorized PCCP models, showing many viable models and overall effectiveness of models. Some have existed for decades and have been adopted across organizations, while others are newer and still accumulating evidence. We identified evidence for multiple approaches and constellations of elements employed to implement PCCP in very different settings consistent with the heterogenous nature of US health systems, affording the opportunity for practices and systems to implement models tailored to their unique needs including resources, workforce, people and communities served. Better ways to classify, measure, and integrate models could help organizations identify approaches that fit their structure and those of their patients. Additionally, a longer time horizon with commitment to adapting and improving uptake of PCCP while generating evidence on effectiveness, characteristic of learning health system approaches, could help PCCP become standard care.

The increasing prevalence of MCC only amplifies the need for PCCP and the urgency to adopt it as routine care. While demand for PCCP should drive continued adaptations of models and iterative improvements, this is contingent on people with MCC and their care networks being aware of these approaches. Expanding campaigns like Patients for Primary Care, and efforts by groups like AARP have the potential to build the case for PCCP.

Most models incorporate strategies to reorient healthcare to be more whole‐person oriented instead of single‐disease focused. They address deficiencies or attempt to modify selected aspects of care. A few models advocate for a major overhaul of the healthcare system—either a transformational healthcare revolution, such as Minimally Disruptive Medicine [16], or changes that significantly move the focus from acute to preventive care and expand the scope of care like Whole Health for Life [17]. While change can happen incrementally, it is worth considering whether small modifications are sufficient to improve care for complex patients. Furthermore, advances in digital health and digital health solutions including eCarePlans, self‐management support, remote patient monitoring, and telehealth as components of PCCP models may help facilitate their adoption in the future. Development of measures to assess PCCP quality, as well as furthering and formalizing partnerships with trusted community organizations are crucial to expanding the availability of PCCP and reorienting care toward treating the whole person. In the short term, sharing details as implementation adapts and matures, and compiling stories of success will aid continued adoption and scaling of PCCP. Long‐term investments in interdisciplinary teams and strengthening primary care also need to be prioritized, possibly assisted with technology to improve access and safety [18, 19].

One limitation of this scan is there may be models and perspectives that were not included, particularly if published details are unavailable. Much of the academic literature focuses on outcomes at the expense of implementation details and word limit constraints may limit disclosure of crucial details. Authors should be encouraged or required to provide detailed accounting of the groundwork, challenges, workarounds, and elements helpful to developing and launching PCCP models. Iterative, rapid adjustments are critical to a learning health system; however, these are infrequently documented and shared. The scan was limited to models implemented in the US; future work should explore features of PCCP models for people living with MCC in other countries. Additionally, there is opportunity for cross border learning to better develop evidence about what works in model design and implementation in different systems. Finally, assessing the impact of healthcare system organization and structure was not strictly in scope and future work should examine more fully whether and how these aspects influence adoption and uptake.

Conclusion

This environmental scan details experiences with multiple models developed to provide comprehensive, coordinated care that maximizes the wellbeing of people living with MCC. There is no shortage of innovative approaches and decades of implementation experience. Models incorporate multiple elements that strive to enhance care and address deficiencies in how care is routinely provided.

Research is available on most models demonstrating their effectiveness. It is encouraging that there are many promising ways to deliver whole‐person care resulting in favorable outcomes. The fact that none are widely applied is sobering. Interviews and published studies document the many challenges that continue to hinder progress and suggest that advocating for change needs to consider model content and what is necessary to facilitate uptake and sustainability of models across healthcare settings. Scale and spread of PCCP will necessitate applying learning health system approaches and agile implementation to generate the needed evidence for adoption.
