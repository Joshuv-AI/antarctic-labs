Modeling Patterns of Population Exposure With a Multiscale Framework Utilizing LOTOS‐EUROS Chemical Transport Model, Mobility Surveys and Particulate Toxicity
Authors: Valeria Solórzano‐Araque, Andrés Yarce Botero, Jhon Edinson Hinestroza‐Ramirez, Fernando Fernandez‐Restrepo, Sebastian Carmona Estrada, Pablo Alberto Osorio‐Marulanda, Lisseth Cruz‐Ruiz, Santiago Isaza‐Cadavid, O. L. Quintero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study proposes a high‐resolution, multi‐component framework to estimate human exposure to air pollution in tropical urban environments, addressing complex topography and limited historical mobility and exposure data. This framework is applied to the city of Medellín in the Aburrá Valley in Colombia. To assess population exposure to particulate matter, numerical simulations, mobility and toxicity data were integrated. The LOTOS‐EUROS Chemical Transport Model, driven by the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model, was used to simulate air quality at a spatial resolution of 1 km × 1 km for the year 2019. Mobility dynamics were derived from the Origin‐Destination Survey (ODS), comprising over 180,000 trips across 240 traffic analysis zones. The utilisation of this data set facilitated the estimation of time‐weighted exposure levels for diverse demographic groups during weekdays. The morpho‐chemical properties of the particulate matter samples were analyzed to determine the presence of metals and carbonaceous fractions due to significant health implications. The exposure model is a weighted model that integrates exposure time, PM2.5 and PM10 concentration, and cytogenotoxic indicators. The application of cluster analysis to the available data, resulted in the identification of areas of elevated health risk, indicating that the central and southern zones, approximately 60% of the metropolitan population and main highway corridors, exhibited mean particulate matter exposure levels that exceeded 40 μg/m3 during peak hours, thus surpassing the WHO air quality guidelines. These zones demonstrated the highest levels of exposure, as indicated by cluster significance levels, suggesting more epidemiological studies and public health interventions.

Introduction

Long term exposure to ambient air pollution has been associated with a range of adverse health effects in both outdoor (Beevers et al., 2013) and indoor (Hwang et al., 2018; Soares et al., 2014) environments. These impacts have been widely studied in the literature (Dionisio et al., 2017; Li et al., 2019) including respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, and increased mortality rates. Human exposure occurs through inhalation, dermal contact, or ingestion of pollutants (Gault et al., 2013). Exposure depends on four main factors: (a) the concentration of contaminant, (b) the location of the exposed individual, (c) the duration of exposure, and (d) the pulmonary ventilation rate (Borghi et al., 2021); the last one is a characteristic of interest because it can increase or decrease the inhalation of pollutants.

Direct measurements of personal exposure to fine particulate matter PM2.5 and other pollutants using portable sensors, offer high accuracy and spatial representativeness (Hwang et al., 2018). However, such methods are usually resource‐intensive, time‐limited and restricted to specific population groups (Kitagawa et al., 2022). Consequently, their generalization over broad spatiotemporal personal exposure remains limited.

An alternative approach involves the use of large‐scale mathematical models that combine meteorological data, emission inventories, and boundary conditions to simulate atmospheric dynamics and chemistry. These simulations are then coupled with information about population distribution and human activity to estimate exposure risk or exposure levels across different regions and times. Examples of exposition studies using Chemical Transport Models (CTMs) include the ensemble of CMAQ (Community Multi‐scale Air Quality Model) and KCLurban (Beevers et al., 2013) models, the creation of models such as EXPAND (EXposure model for Particulate matter and Nitrogen Oxides) (Soares et al., 2014), AirGIS (Khan et al., 2019), the coupling of models such as WRF (Weather Research and Forecasting Model) with air quality, population distribution and human exposure (APEX) (Dionisio et al., 2017), the use of LURM (Land use regression Models) (Ciro & Barriga, 2018) and the WRF/CMAQ/WRF‐CHEM (WRF‐Chemistry) coupling (Kitagawa et al., 2022).

In addition, mobility patterns and daily schedules are essential for accurate exposure estimation; however, this data collection is often difficult, and is not available with the detailed information required. Common alternatives include activity diaries (Hwang et al., 2018) recording exposition and the use of massive surveys of human activity such as ATUS or the consolidated human activity database (George & McCurdy, 2011) or synthetic data sets generated via statistical models (Gurram et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2018). These approaches account for sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, socio‐economic status, transportation mode, among others) which influence behavior and exposure heterogeneity across population subgroups.

Urban pollutant concentrations tend to peak near high traffic roads and industrial areas compared to rural areas during rush hours (Zhao et al., 2021). These can be attributed to direct emission from transportation and industry, as well as secondary formation through atmospheric transport and chemical reactions (Kitagawa et al., 2022; Li et al., 2019). Among the air pollutants of greatest public health concern, particulate matter (PM), ozone (O3), carbon monoxide (CO), sulfur dioxide (SO2), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), lead (Pb), volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) (Han & Naeher, 2006). This study is focused on PM2.5 and PM10 due to their role in major pollution episodes in the metropolitan area of the Aburrá Valley (Velásquez‐García et al., 2024). Epidemiological studies have shown that exposure to particulate matter PM2.5, PM10 can cause severe health conditions such as asthma, non‐asthmatic respiratory symptoms, impaired lung function, cardiovascular mortality and morbidity (Baklanov et al., 2007).

Recent studies highlight the severity of this issue in Latin American cities. For instance (Kephart et al., 2023), found that 85% of urban residents in 326 cities live in areas with NO2 concentrations above WHO (World Health Organization) guidelines (World Health Organization, 2021), highlighting the need for greater greenness and reduced vehicle reliance to mitigate NO2 exposure. Marín et al. (2025) showed that a 5 µg/m3 increase in PM2.5 correlates with a 15% rise in cardio‐respiratory mortality in Bucaramanga and a 6%–7% in Medellín, emphasizing the need for high‐resolution exposure modeling in lower‐middle‐income urban settings. Other international study, such as the Stochastic Human Exposure and Dose Simulation (SHEDS‐PM) model in Philadelphia (Kruize et al., 2003) and EXPOLIS in European cities (BURKE et al., 2001), have reported median daily PM2.5 population exposure ranging from 13 to 43 µg/m3.

Atmospheric pollution is a major global driver of environmental health degradation. In urban contexts, poor air quality significantly contributes to respiratory diseases worldwide. To respond to this issue, exposure models integrate chemistry‐transport models (Lopez‐Restrepo et al., 2022; Manders et al., 2017) and pollutant concentration data to estimate the levels of pollutants individuals encounter. By incorporating daily activities and mobility patterns, these models help quantifying individual exposure and inform targeted public health strategies (S. Jonkers A.M.M. Manders‐Groot, 2016; Navarro‐Martínez et al., 2025).

This study proposes a framework to build a human exposure model for atmospheric pollution specifically designed for the tropical urban environments of the Aburrá Valley. The proposal involved refining techniques for modeling atmospheric transport and chemical dynamics in complex topographies, characteristic of our region. We then integrated this with human activity data and the morpho‐chemical and cytogenotoxic characteristics of pollutants. This integration allowed us to apply a risk weighting to the zonal pollutant concentrations, thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of exposure.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 describes the methods and materials, including the configuration of the coupled model WRF–LOTOS‐EUROS simulations applied to the Aburrá Valley and the processing of intra‐urban mobility data derived from the 2017 Origin–Destination Survey, the framework used for risk modeling, incorporating morphochemical and cytogenotoxic characteristics of particulate matter. Section 3 reports the results of the exposure and risk analysis, highlighting the influence of mobility patterns and pollutant concentrations. Finally, Sections 4 and 5 provide the discussion and conclusions, emphasizing the implications and relevance of this work for urban environmental health management.

Methods and Materials

This paper summarizes part of the research programs “Human Exposure Models to Atmospheric Pollution in Urban Areas as a Decision‐Making Tool” (Exposure to Pollutants Regional Research ExPoR2) and its twin Estimating urban pollution using surface, in situ, and remotely sensed measurements and data assimilation (4DAir‐MOLIS), specifically adapted to Colombian conditions (Montilla‐Rosero et al., 2026). This model serves as a supportive tool for the design and decision‐making processes with respect to mitigation and prevention measures. Atmospheric pollution constitutes a significant detriment to environmental health, with poor urban air quality being the leading cause of respiratory diseases around the world. The National Institute of Health (INS) of Colombia https://www.ins.gov.co/ identifies atmospheric pollution as the main environmental health hazard in the country.

Exposure models represent a fundamental tool in the field of environmental health management. The purpose of these models is to predict the concentrations of atmospheric pollutants within a specific domain. It should be noted that these concentrations can vary significantly over a distance of tens of meters. The objective of these models is to determine the manner in which the spatial heterogeneity of pollutants and human activity patterns interact to determine the pollutant loads to which individuals are exposed in their daily lives. Exposure models integrate atmospheric pollutant concentrations and their dispersion dynamics with the assimilation of data from monitoring stations and human activity models to estimate zonal exposure levels for human populations.

Although directly modeling human exposure was a core aim, this research output, by providing more accurate spatio‐temporal pollutant concentrations, indirectly supports the development of sophisticated human exposure models. The emphasis on high‐resolution modeling in complex terrains allows for a more precise estimation of where and when populations are exposed to air pollution, crucial for epidemiological studies and public health interventions. This aligns with broader research that demonstrates the association between long‐term PM2.5 exposure and cardio respiratory mortality in Colombian cities (Rodriguez‐Villamizar et al., 2023).

Chemical Transport Modeling

LOTOS‐EUROS (LOng Term Ozone Simulation–EURopean Operational Smog) is a three‐dimensional chemical transport model (CTM) designed for large‐scale simulations of air pollution in the lower troposphere. This CTM simulates atmospheric concentrations in the lower troposphere driven by meteorological data, inventory emissions (Biogenic and Anthropogenic), and boundary conditions from coarser CTMs used as boundary conditions (S. Jonkers A.M.M. Manders‐Groot, 2016). LOTOS‐EUROS is a computer program that solves a differential equation that integrates several operators—transport, chemistry, emissions and deposition—applying them sequentially over a 3D grid extending across the domain of interest. Horizontal transport is driven by the meteorological wind components (U and V), while the vertical wind (W) is inferred from the divergence of these horizontal winds when using ECMWF meteorological inputs. The phenomenon of vertical mixing due to turbulence is addressed through the utilization of a vertical diffusion operator. The chemistry module accounts for atmospheric production and loss through gas‐phase reactions, using a Carbon Bond Mechanism with 81 reactions (Schaap et al., 2008). Aerosol interactions are described using the ISORROPIA model (Fountoukis & Nenes, 2007). Dry deposition is represented through a resistance‐based parameterization (Wichink Kruit et al., 2012), while wet deposition includes scavenging of gases below the cloud (Schaap et al., 2004).

For the meteorological drivers the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model (Skamarock et al., 2019) was coupled with the Chemical Transport Model (CTM) LOTOS‐EUROS for simulating air pollution in regions with complex topography in the same configurations of previous publications over the Aburrá Valley (Hinestroza‐Ramirez et al., 2023). This coupling provides a more precise representation of both meteorology and pollutant dispersion compared to models relying on default or coarser meteorological inputs (e.g., from ECMWF reanalysis) using previous configurations for this CTM (Hinestroza‐Ramirez et al., 2023; Lopez‐Restrepo et al., 2022).

These simulation domains consisted of 4 nested domains, shown in Figure 1: the first with lower resolution (0.27°×0.27°) is positioned centered over Colombia, the second is positioned over the Andes and has a resolution of (0.09°×0.09°), the third domain is located over the department of Antioquia with a resolution of 0.03°×0.03° and finally, the fourth domain contains the region of the Aburrá Valley, which is the region of interest and has a resolution of 0.01°×0.01°. Biogenic emissions for all domains were obtained from the MEGAN and MACC/CAMSGFAS emissions inventory (Petrescu et al., 2012) and anthropogenic emissions were obtained from the EDGAR global emissions inventory (Petrescu et al., 2012). LOTOS‐EUROS Previous Validations

The LOTOS‐EUROS CTM has previously been validated for Colombia, and more specifically in the Aburrá Valley, with data on particulate matter pollutants collected from the SIATA network (Lopez‐Restrepo et al., 2020). Aburrá Valley has a network of air quality monitoring equipment that is certified for use, in addition to a high‐resolution network composed of low‐cost sensors (Lopez‐Restrepo et al., 2021). The experiments previously made in this area with the model were focused on emission estimation of PM2.5 and PM10 using data assimilation techniques specifically LENtKF. Without Assimilation the quantitative validation shows for PM10: Mean Fractional Bias (MFB) = −1.5, RMSE ≈ 49.6 μg/m3, Correlation Factor (CF) ≈ 0.33 and for PM2.5: MFB = −1.6, RMSE ≈ 46.2 μg/m3, CF ≈ 0.33. Assimilation, reduced RMSE for PM10 by 12% and for PM2.5 by 15%, improved bias, and produced emission fields more consistent with observed urban pollution patterns. Forecasts were sensitive to the day of initialization and the inheritance scheme, with hourly inheritance and weekday starts performing best. In the validation with low‐cost sensors, the LOTOS‐EUROS achieved strong correlation with official monitoring stations, especially in the high‐quality subset. Assimilation of low‐cost data outperformed official stations, with lower RMSE, higher correlations (up to R = 0.82), and superior detection of pollution warning events. Also different configurations of the LOTOS‐EUROS models were explored such as, for example, with different meteorological drivers such as the model WRF (Hinestroza‐Ramirez et al., 2023) because the previous forcing meteorological fields came from the ECMWF global model which had a coarse resolution that had low representativity of the topography for this region of a deep seated valley. Across both evaluation periods, WRF‐driven simulations consistently outperformed ECMWF‐driven ones in terms of RMSE, correlation, and spatial realism of PM2.5. WRF better represented daily cycles, vertical structure, and valley‐bottom pollution peaks, while ECMWF often misplaced high concentrations in unrealistic locations.

Modeling Human Activity

To modeling the human activity in the simulation region, the 2017 origin‐destination survey of the metropolitan area of the Aburrá Valley (Área Metropolitana del Valle de Aburrá, 2017a) was used. The metropolitan area of the Aburrá Valley is an administrative region in Antioquia, Colombia. Composed of 10 cities shown in the zoom image of 1: Barbosa, Bello, Caldas, Copacabana, Girardota, Envigado, Itagüí, Sabaneta, La Estrella and Medellín. This region is around the valley of the Medellín River, a deep mountain canyon that has an elongated and narrow shape, with an average width of 6 km, although it can reach 8–10 km at its widest point. The population in this metropolitan area is about 4 million people, making it the second most densely populated area in Colombia in which the citizens are exposed to severe air quality deterioration events, caused by high concentrations of particulate matter due to its location, weather conditions, and human activities.

The destination survey inventory, which includes the daily activities carried out by a data set of individuals during the day, is shown in Figure 2. The places where the individuals were present, the time elapsed during periods of mobility, the reason for the trips, the type of transport used, and its demographic and socio‐economic characteristics are specified in the data set. The survey was applied to 16,340 households in urban and rural areas of the Aburrá Valley, with 36,364 residents in 10 different municipalities. Statistical sampling was carried out employing systematic sampling in the macro zones (which represent subzones of Medellin and municipalities that are part of the metropolitan area) and randomly in the most populated sectors of the rural macro zones. The survey result has a statistical validation with a confidence interval of 90% and a probable error of 10%. Subsequently, the data is processed to find the population density in each macro zone within a temporal resolution of 1 hr since this is the maximum temporal resolution reached by the LOTOS‐EUROS model, that is, how many people are present in each zone at 1 hr of the day.

Example of mobility patterns extracted from the 2017 Origin‐Destination Survey for four macro zones in the Aburrá Valley: (a) Popular, (b) El Poblado, (c) Laureles–Estadio, and (d) Itagüí. The figures illustrate the spatial distribution and intensity of travel flows originating from each zone, highlighting demographic and geographic mobility differences across the metropolitan area.

Given that the segmentation provided by the survey considers only municipalities and does not account for the different macrozones within them, it was necessary to spatially disaggregate the population data from the municipal level to the macrozone level. This disaggregation process considered the area of each macrozone, the total area of its corresponding municipality, and was conducted under the assumption of uniform population density across each municipality.

The hypothesis of uniform population density implies that the population is evenly distributed throughout the territory of a municipality. Under this assumption, the proportion of the total population assigned to a macrozone is directly proportional to the area that the macrozone occupies within the municipality.

Based on this, the interpolation of the number of people in each macrozone is defined as follows. Let Nmacrozone be the variable representing the number of people in a given macrozone, and Nmunicipality the total population of the corresponding municipality.

Let ai be the proportion of the municipality's area occupied by the macrozone. The population in the macrozone is then calculated using a scaling factor λ, defined as: (1) λ=ai (2) Nmacrozone=Nmunicipality×λ

This formulation reflects the relationship between the number of people in a macrozone and the total population of the municipality, with the lambda factor λ serving as an adjustment based on the spatial proportion of the macrozone.

Modeling Outdoor Exposition

The most straightforward way to conceptualize exposure is “The replicated microenvironment monitoring model” which is a location where pollutant concentrations exhibit homogeneity and can vary over time, encompassing a four‐dimensional space (Duan, 1982). Since an individual can traverse multiple microenvironments, it is possible to group them. In this particular scenario, the Aburrá Valley is organized into macrozones. Consequently, all data points derived from the LOTOS‐EUROS model within the macrozone's boundaries are averaged to determine gas concentration values.

The overall exposure experienced by a population during their daily activities (or over a specified period) is represented as the linear combination of concentrations at various time intervals, multiplied by the corresponding duration spent by individuals in each microenvironment (Duan, 1982). It is defined as: (3) E¯ˆ=∑kcˆkt¯k where t¯k is the average time in the kth microenvironment averaged over the target population, and cˆk is the average concentration in the zone.

The present study focuses mainly on average exposure, achieved through the average concentrations and taking exposure duration as 1 hr within each macrozone. We add a multiplicative factor to Equation 3, representing the population density or the number of people in the area at a specific time and, when there are low concentrations but many people present, the value correspond to a higher exposure factor, resulting in the following equation: (4) E¯ˆ=∑kcˆkt¯kP where P is the population density, t¯k is the average time in the k microenvironment averaged over the target population and cˆk is the average concentration in the respective macrozone.

The proposed formulation of the exposure model takes into account the concentration of PM2.5, PM10 and the number of people in the area of interest resulting from the activity model. Figures 5 and 6 show the results of the concentration model. The mathematical formulation of the exposure is calculated as: (5) Exposure(t,z)PM10=Nper(t,z)×PM10(t,z) (6) Exposure(t,z)PM2.5=Nper(t,z)×PM2.5(t,z) where z is the zone of analysis (reference macrozone), t is the hour of day to be analyzed, and Nper is the number of people in that zone.

Collected samples underwent morphochemical analysis with two main objectives: (a) to count the particles and (b) to classify them morphochemically. Particles were grouped into six categories: mineral, organic/biogenic, tire wear, metallic, titanium‐rich paint, and salts (Figure 4 Right). In parallel, elemental composition (e.g., O, C, Si, Fe, Al, Ca) was quantified.

To support spatial analysis, only three of the nine available measurements (ME22MA P1, ME22 TLY P1, and ME22TG P1) were selected, as they represent distinct urban and topographic contexts.

Two interpolation techniques were applied to estimate the composition of PM across macrozones. It is important to note that the use of these methodologies was necessary due to the complete independence of the experimental data from the exposure model. Implementing these methods was expected to improve the characterization of the pollutants to which inhabitants are exposed. Moreover, the samples were collected as cumulative measurements, meaning that there is no associated temporal resolution. Due to these constraints, spatial interpolation was the most appropriate strategy to estimate pollutant distributions based solely on spatial variability. Mountain Method, a radial basis function expressed as: (14) mg(v)=∑i=1NPiexp−‖v−xi‖2CPi where xi is the data location, Pi the measured value, and C a user‐defined smoothing parameter.

Results

Exposure to Pollutants Model: Particulate Matter PM2.5 and PM10 Concentrations

All concentrations from the LOTOS‐EUROS model are spatiotemporal interpolated to cells within a specific area for each hour of the day. Subsequently, they were averaged again to find the maximum and minimum values in the valley for each gas. The values found for PM2.5 ranged between 1.00μ g/m3 and 30μ g/m3, and for PM10, they ranged between 1.22μ g/m3 and 67.73μ g/m3. Concentrations are evaluated at 3 different local times: 6 h, 12 h, and 18 hr. In the morning, concentrations tend to be lower; as they approach midday, concentrations tend to increase. Finally, approaching nighttime hours, they decrease again, presenting a marked difference between diurnal and nocturnal cycles; this behavior is shown in Figure 5.

Time Activity Patterns

The Origin‐Destination Survey exhibits typical weekday behavior. The main reasons for movements are work, study, and errands. The hours with the highest flow of people are between 6 and 8:30 and between 17 and 18:30 local time. 71% of the trips have Medellín Municipality as their destination, most of the trips are undertaken using emission motorized transport modes, including taxis, motorcycles, cars or buses, connecting the central area of Medellín with the southern municipalities of the Aburrá Valley. High concentrations of trips are observed in La Candelaria, El Poblado, Laureles/Estadio, and Robledo, as well as in central Itagüí, Envigado, and Sabaneta. Additionally, the central zone of Bello, located to the north, registers significant flows.

Exposition Factor

High levels of the exposure factor are primarily found in the city center, and most of these areas are within the jurisdiction of the municipality of Medellín. Some areas in the south and north of the Aburrá Valley exhibit a moderate exposure factor. Depending on human activity, more affected zones increase around midday and decrease as people return home. PM2.5 and PM10 behave similarly during daytime and nighttime periods. The sum of fine and coarse particulate matter was calculated to determine the total exposure presented in Figure 6; however, the behavior of this factor remains very similar to that of the individual elements. Areas such as La Candelaria, Guayabal, Belen, and Laureles in Medellín and the center of Itagüí and Sabaneta in the southern part of the Valley could be harmful for humans, especially around noon.

Areas with a substantial degree of exposure predominantly exist in the urban core, and two pivotal factors significantly contribute to this phenomenon. The first factor pertains to the high population density prevalent in these areas, typically within zones exhibiting elevated values of the exposure factor, which encompass commercial and industrial regions. It is customary for a considerable number of individuals to commute to these places to initiate their workday. Moreover, the intensified demand for transportation contributes to higher levels of pollutant emissions.

It is noteworthy how, at 6 a.m., areas with higher pollution exposure concentrate in a small zone in downtown Medellín. This may indicate the significant mass movement toward Medellín from peripheral municipalities. In fact, the activity model provides hints of the existence of “bedroom communities” these municipalities, with extensive residential areas, are primarily used for rest. Given that in the Aburrá Valley, residents of municipalities located to the south have higher economic capacity, this could explain the increase in private transport usage and higher concentrations in that area. Additionally, they also have industrial areas.

Compromised air quality remains a paramount environmental factor contributing to global morbidity and mortality (Cohen et al., 2017), with atmospheric pollution alone accounting for an estimated 9 million deaths annually worldwide (Burnett et al., 2018). In Colombia, the National Institute of Health (2018) identifies poor air quality as the primary driver of environmental health deterioration. Consistent with other forms of environmental health degradation, the spatial dynamics and patterns of pollutant emissions in urban areas exacerbate economic disparities, frequently transforming exposure inequities into issues of social justice (Tessum et al., 2019).

In the Aburrá Valley, data from 2008 to 2017 revealed approximately 3.5 million cases of respiratory illnesses linked to poor air quality, with the risk of disease episodes increasing by nearly 4% for every 10 μg/m3 rise in PM10 concentrations (AMVA, 2019). Furthermore, urban atmospheric pollution exposure in Medellín and three other major Colombian cities is associated with increased hospital consultations for respiratory and circulatory conditions (Rodríguez‐Villamizar et al., 2018). Notably, in three of these cities, exposure to industrial‐source atmospheric pollution has been linked to acute childhood leukemia (Rodriguez‐Villamizar et al., 2020).

Risk Mapping

The integration of atmospheric exposure with potential biological effects was carried out by combining modeled pollutant concentrations with toxicological response functions. Specifically, the LOTOS‐EUROS output was used to derive median concentrations per macrozone, which were then normalized against established hourly regulatory limits (Andreau et al., 2012) to represent the relative intensity of exposure as shown in Figure 7. These normalized exposures were subsequently weighted using tissue‐specific cell death rates obtained from experimental studies, yielding an indicator that reflects the relative potential for biological impact across macrozones.

Spatial risk map of PM2.5‐induced cell damage across the study area. Each pixel shows the median LOTOS–EUROS concentration, normalized to the hourly legal limit and multiplied by the corresponding tissue‐specific cell‐death rate. Warmer colors mark macrozones with the greatest potential biological impact.

The results highlight consistently higher values near the tram stations, where, as expected due to the mountain method, the combined concentration–toxicity signal reaches its maximum. This area includes the neighborhoods of Buenos Aires, La Milagrosa, and Caicedo, corresponding to macrozone 15, which shows the highest composite score. Macrozone 19 follows, including La Candelaria, the Administrative Center, and La Alpujarra, with a score of 5.4. Macrozones 20, 12, and 21 present lower but still notable values of 3.7, 1.6, and 1, respectively. Although these areas are also within the jurisdiction of the municipality of Medellín, they are less severely affected. They include neighborhoods such as Castropol, Barrio Colombia, San Joaquín, Laureles/Estadio, and Los Colores.

Rather than providing a direct prediction of health outcomes, this index offers a comparative perspective on where modeled pollutant concentrations and toxicological weighting coincide most strongly. It serves to highlight areas where the potential for biological stress may be elevated and where additional monitoring or more detailed epidemiological studies could be most informative.

Discussion

By combining high‐resolution simulations, measurement data from the atmospheric pollutant monitoring network, and human mobility patterns within the Aburrá Valley, we have constructed an exposure model that distinguishes the nominal exposure level received by residents of different neighborhoods in the region. To this model, we added a “hazard” weighting for pollutants based on the morpho‐chemical characteristics of contaminants. This generated a hypothetical risk index assigned to various urban sectors of the region. The increased resolution of pollutant estimates and their associated hazards to which Aburrá Valley residents are exposed provides us with tools to understand the mortality patterns related to atmospheric pollution observed in previous studies by the Metropolitan Area. It also offers a new tool for designing mitigation strategies, prioritizing locations where residents receive the highest pollutant loads. Future developments may incorporate higher‐resolution information on daily time‐use patterns to refine our risk estimates and identify opportunities for more targeted public policies aimed at enhancing human health.

Exposition studies aim to elucidate the contact of humans, animals, or ecosystems with potentially harmful substances and the subsequent impact on their integrity. To comprehensively understand human exposure to atmospheric pollutants, it's crucial to move beyond measurements and estimations reflecting only homogeneous mixing conditions, a limitation inherent in data derived from urban monitoring networks. Instead, a more granular approach is needed to capture pollutant loads resulting from fine‐scale variations and individual activity patterns. While exposure will always be influenced by individual idiosyncrasies, it is possible to approximate exposure patterns that reveal vulnerabilities beyond those suggested by monitoring network averages.

The Aburrá Valley's atmospheric pollutant monitoring network, operated by the Sistema de Alerta Temprana del Valle de Aburrá (SIATA), stands as Colombia's most technologically advanced, boasting superior coverage and spatial sampling density. Through data assimilation techniques developed within this project (Lopez‐Restrepo et al., 2021, 2022), the valuable data from this network was integrated into chemistry and transport models. This generates high‐resolution temporal and spatial simulations representing zonal concentrations of atmospheric pollutants. Advances in disaggregating mobile‐source emission inventories (Hernández et al., 2022) allow us to refine the atmospheric pollution burden that vehicular flow represents for each zone within the Aburrá Valley. Complementing advancements in atmospheric chemical species simulation, we have also progressed in modeling meteorology in tropical urban environments (Henao et al., 2020) and in the capability to feed high‐resolution meteorological data into chemistry and transport models such as LOTOS‐EUROS (Hinestroza‐Ramirez et al., 2023).

A common method for estimating urban pollutant exposure is the use of Land Use Regression Models (LURM) ((Amini et al., 2017); see also (Beelen et al., 2014), ESCAPE project). LURMs interpolate pollutant concentrations through multiple regressions with land cover variables. In this project, we implemented an alternative approach based on high‐resolution simulations with chemistry‐transport models to estimate zonal concentrations. This method, also used by Soares et al. (2014), involves greater computational cost than LURMs. However, due to the abrupt geography of the Aburrá Valley, we considered it essential to incorporate atmospheric dynamics, which LURMs do not capture. This choice is supported by observations of unusually high pollutant levels in southern Belén neighborhood, an area with low industrial activity and traffic. These levels are consistent with circulation patterns that trap particulates in this sector during certain times of day. Belén also reports one of the highest pollution‐attributed mortality rates in the valley (Área Metropolitana del Valle de Aburrá & de Investigación en Higiene y Gestión Ambiental GHYGAM, 2019). The regulation of industrial emissions and the transition to sustainable energy and transportation methods have the potential to reduce harmful Particulate Matter (PM) concentrations and, consequently, the exposure factor.

The level of pollutant exposure depends not only on zonal concentrations at different times of the day but also on the intersection between human density and pollutant density. To approximate human occupation patterns within the Aburrá Valley zones, we utilized information from the “Origen Destino 2017” survey conducted by the Aburrá Valley Metropolitan Area (Área Metropolitana del Valle de Aburrá, 2017b). This enabled us to estimate population density per sub‐zone (see Annexes 8 and 9) to combine these densities with pollutant concentrations. Although we attempted to extract zonal patterns of Colombian activity from the National Survey on Time Use (Departamento Administrativo Nacional de Estadística (DANE), 2020), the information contained there did not provide a geospatial application compatible with the models in their current iteration. Additional efforts are underway beyond the scope of this project to complement exposure models with higher‐resolution information on human activity.

Conclusions

This study identified areas of high exposure to fine and coarse PM10 and PM2.5 in the urban area of Medellín and the Metropolitan Area, advancing our understanding of human exposure to pollution in this region. The utilisation of large‐scale chemical transport models, such as LOTOS‐EUROS, facilitates the simulation of emission, transportation, chemical reactions and deposition processes. This paper's methodological approach enables the integration of simulated concentrations over time with daily population density dynamics. Consequently, it provides a quantitative framework for assessing exposure to pollutants in this specific region of Colombia. The absence of data represents a significant challenge in such contexts. The model's applicability to other cities in the country is a potential avenue for future research.

Estimating human exposure to atmospheric pollutants in urban environments characterized by abrupt topography and imperfect emission inventories, such as the Aburrá Valley, presents considerable challenges. These difficulties stem from the need to mathematically represent highly complex processes and to adapt tools originally developed for relatively simpler environments with more complete observational data sets. The computation of this exposition factors have limitation inherent in data derived from urban monitoring networks, a more granular approach is needed to capture pollutant loads resulting from fine‐scale variations and individual activity patterns.

Through this research, we solidified the implementation of the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model at a local scale for simulating meteorological dynamics with high spatial resolutions. This was coupled with chemistry and transport models (WRF‐Chem or WRF‐LOTOS‐EUROS), providing the latter with improved meteorological representation that, in turn, enhanced the representation of atmospheric species dynamics (Hernández et al., 2022; Hinestroza‐Ramirez et al., 2023). Improvements in the performance of chemistry and transport models within the Aburrá Valley were also achieved by utilizing emission inventories with higher resolution and lower uncertainty than those provided by the global EDGAR (v4.3) inventory (Lopez‐Restrepo et al., 2021). Through methods of spatial and temporal disaggregation of the official particulate emission inventory for the Aburrá Valley Metropolitan Area, and the use of road network and vehicular flow data, we refined emission estimates corresponding to vehicular sources. When coupled with chemistry and transport models (WRF‐Chem), this allowed us to construct zonal estimates of pollutant loads (Hernández et al., 2022).

While we acknowledge that this model still requires further refinement with additional sources of information, regarding human activity and occupation, we have established a robust tool for comprehending the atmospheric pollution burdens to which the citizens of the Aburrá Valley are routinely exposed. Given the focus on enhancing human well‐being in the region and the relevance to other tropical contexts, further investigation is warranted.
