Genome-wide identification and gene expression analyses of GH3 gene family under abiotic stress in Phyllostachys edulis
Authors: Yuan Xie, Lianting Wang, Ying Hu, Shinan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Gretchen Hagen 3 (GH3) family plays an essential role in plant growth, development and resistance to environmental stress. Although, the members in GH3 family have been reported in some monocots, their exploration in Phyllostachys edulis needs to be further explored.ResultsIn the present study, we identified totally 23 GH3 genes from P. edulis at the genomic level, which were categorized into two subfamilies (I and II) based on phylogenetic analysis. Subsequent analyses concentrated on the molecular features of these genes, including their gene structure, conserved motifs, cis-acting elements in promoters, and syntenic regions in the genome. Furthermore, qRT-PCR results indicated that most PeGH3 genes were extensively expressed across all the tested tissues. Meanwhile, some of them were also responsive to abiotic stresses such as salt and drought, as well as hormone treatments including naphthalene acetic acid (NAA) and salicylic acid (SA) based on RNA-seq data analysis. According to the tissue-specific and stress-responsive expression results, PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 were likely to play significant roles in plant growth and in response to abiotic stress. Subcellular localization analysis demonstrated that these four PeGH3 proteins were predominantly localized in the cytoplasm and nucleus. To further clarify their function in stress tolerance, PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 were expressed in yeast, which proved their ability to enhance yeast tolerance to salt and drought stresses.ConclusionsThis research enhances the understanding of PeGH3 genes and provides genetic resources for breeding bamboo plants with improved resistance to stress, especially to salt and drought.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09256-5.Keywords: Phyllostachys edulis, GH3 gene family, Expression analysis, Stress tolerance

Background

Phytohormones govern a spectrum of plant processes that are both unique and intricately intertwined during the entire life cycle [1, 2]. Among them, auxin plays indispensable roles in plant growth and development, including phototransduction, gravitropism, lateral-root formation and growth, cell division and differentiation, and responses to environmental cues [1, 3]. Most of these processes are regulated by some auxin-responsive genes including the Gretchen Hagen 3 (GH3) family. The GH3 gene encodes an enzyme known as auxin amide synthetase [4], which has a vital catalytic role in facilitating the binding of auxin to small molecule substrates containing a cyclohexyl moiety, including compounds like amino acids and jasmonic acid [5]. This gene family is widely distributed across the plant kingdom. Till the present, GH3 genes have been detected in a variety of species, such as Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, Zea mays, and Glycine max [4, 6–8]. The number of the GH3 family members varies significantly among species, ranging from 3 (Physcomitrella patens) to 41 (Saccharum spontaneum), and there is no clear association with genome size or morphological complexity [9, 10]. According to sequence similarities and substrate specificities, the GH3 family is primarily categorized into three subfamilies: I, II and III. Subfamilies I and II are conserved across all land plants, while subfamily III is absent in monocots (e.g., Z. mays and O. sativa) but present in dicots (e.g., A. thaliana), implying a possible eudicot-specific expansion [4, 6, 8]. Recent studies have further identified subfamily IV in woody plants species such as Citrus clementina, Eucalyptus grandis and Prunus persica [11], suggesting a potential role in lignification or long-term development processes. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the expansion and functional diversification of the GH3 gene family are not random but are closely correlated with plant lineage, evolutionary history, and ecological adaptation [12]. The presence or absence of specific subfamilies may show unique physiological or developmental requirements of different plant groups. Further comparative and functional studies are conducive to elucidating the precise roles of each subfamily and their contributions. However, for the time being, only a few GH3 genes have been functionally characterized.

The GH3 family members play different roles in plant development [13]. In some species, AtGH3-2/6/9 (A. thaliana) and SLGH3-5 (Solanum lycopersicum) negatively regulate the number of primary roots (PR), lateral (LR) and adventitious roots (AR) through decreasing the IAA content [14–16]. Similarly, OsGH3-2/8/13 can influence the abundance of PR, LR, and AR, as well as root hairs (HR) in rice [17–19]. Moreover, AtGH3-2/6 can positively regulate the hypocotyl elongation, while OsGH3-1/2/5/13 can influence leaf development and inclination [15, 18–21]. Beyond the vegetative development, GH3 genes are also implicated in reproductive organ development. For example, AtGH3-10/11 is required for the accumulation of JA-IIe in flower buds [22]. OsGH3-5 results in the thin-seed phenotype by interacting with OsARF19 [20]. CcGH3 (Capsicum Chinese L.), VvGH3-1 (Vitis vinifera L.), SiGH3-2 (S. lycopersicum L.), and RiGH3.1 (Rubus idaeus H.) modulated fruit ripening through regulating endogenous IAA levels [23–26]. Under adverse environmental conditions including drought, salinity, and high temperature, plants employ complex signaling networks involving hormones for their adaption and survival. The GH3 gene family has been indicated to make a vital impact on these stress responses. In A. thaliana, AtGH3-5 enhances resistance to temperature and drought stresses through modulating the SA/ABA signaling pathway [27]. In rice, OsGH3-13 confers drought tolerance by altering the IAA level [19]. Similarly, via the regulation of antioxidant enzyme, cotton GhGH3-5 (Gossypium hirsutum) exerts a positive effect on coping with drought and salt stresses [14]. The obtained findings underscore the dual role of GH3 genes in both normal development and stress adaptation. Further exploration of their molecular mechanisms may unravel additional interactions with other signaling pathways, providing novel genetic tools for enhancing plants resistance to abiotic stresses.

Phyllostachys edulis is the most economically and ecologically vital bamboo species in Asia, which is attributed to its rapid biomass production, soil-erosion control, and high lignocellulose feedstock [28–30]. As extreme weather events are more frequent, water scarcity and temperature fluctuations increasingly restrict the cultivation of bamboo forest and shoots. Throughout its growth and development stages, bamboo must adjust to a variety of adverse stressors, including diseases, drought, cold, and salinity [28, 31]. As a result, cultivating stress-resistant bamboo cultivars is essential to address the challenges posed by these stressors. The identification and characterization of resistance genes are of significant importance for advancing molecular breeding techniques in bamboo. Genome-wide investigation of stress-responsive GH3 gene family members can contribute to developing more resilient bamboo varieties, eventually improving their survival and performance under adverse conditions. Consequently, we identified the GH3 family members based on the whole-genome data of P. edulis and performed detailed analyses of their gene structure, chromosomal distribution, evolutionary relationships, and cis-regulatory elements in promoters. Additionally, the expression patterns of these genes across various tissues and in response to different abiotic stresses were investigated using public database data and qRT-PCR techniques. Moreover, the findings in this study can provide valuable insights into future research aimed at understanding the molecular mechanisms by which GH3 genes contribute to stress responses in bamboo.

Results

Identification of PeGH3 genes in P. edulis and their physicochemical characterization.

Totally 23 GH3 family members were identified in P. edulis, 19 of which were designated as PeGH3-1 to PeGH3-9 (subgenome C), and PeGH3-10 to 19 (subgenome D) based on their subgenomic origins and chromosomal localization. The remaining 4 members that could be anchored to the scaffold level were named PeGH3-20 to PeGH3-23. Key physicochemical properties of these proteins including the CDS length, amino acid number, molecular weight, theoretical isoelectric point (pI), and predicted subcellular localization were summarized in Table 1. The CDS lengths of the PeGH3 genes ranged from 789 bp (PeGH3-22) to 2481 bp (PeGH3-19), corresponding to amino acid lengths spanning from 262 aa to 826 aa. Consistently, the molecular weights varied from 29.12 kDa (PeGH3-22) to 92.67 kDa (PeGH3-19), while the pI values ranged from 5.25 (PeGH3-15) to 8.13 (PeGH3-17). Subcellular localization prediction indicated that 73.9% of the PeGH3 proteins were likely localized in the nucleus, while the remaining 26.1% showed distinct locations; besides, five proteins (PeGH3-2, PeGH3-10, PeGH3-14, PeGH3-16, and PeGH3-23) were predicted to be localized in the chloroplast, and one protein (PeGH3-19) was localized in the plasma membrane (Table 1).

Phylogenetic relationships and classification of GH3 proteins

To investigate the potential function of PeGH3 proteins, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the full-length protein sequences of 68 members of the GH3 family, including 29 from A. thaliana (AtGH3), 13 from rice (OsGH3), 12 from Z. mays (ZmGH3) and 23 from P. edulis (PeGH3). The results suggested that that all GH3 proteins could be distinctly clustered into three subfamilies (I, II and III) (Fig. 1). Obviously, subfamily III did not contain any PeGH3, OsGH3, or ZmGH3 proteins, and comprised solely 10 AtGH3 members. Subfamily I included 6 PeGH3 proteins (PeGH3-1, PeGH3-5, PeGH3-12, PeGH3-13, PeGH3-15, and PeGH3-21), 4 OsGH3 proteins (OsGH3-3, OsGH3-5, OsGH3-6, and OsGH3-12), 5 ZmGH3 proteins (ZmGH3-1, ZmGH3-4, ZmGH3-9, ZmGH3-10, and ZmGH3-12) and 2 AtGH3 proteins (AtGH3-10 and AtGH3-11). All the remaining GH3 proteins from A. thaliana, O. sativa, Z. mays, and P. edulis were assigned to subfamily II. Generally, the PeGH3 proteins exhibited a closer association with OsGH3 proteins than with those from the other two species. For example, PeGH3-5 and PeGH3-15 were clustered closely with OsGH3-5, while PeGH3-8 and PeGH3-18 formed a well-supported clade with OsGH3-8. These topological relationships demonstrated that these orthologous pairs might share conserved biological functions.

Phylogenetic, gene structure and conserved motif analysis of PeGH3 genes

In order to systematically characterize the structural features and functional conservation of the P. edulis GH3 gene family, the exon-intron structure and protein motif information were integrated across all the 23 members (Fig. 2). These genes were categorized into three distinct classes based on their structural characteristics (Fig. 2A), and the classification was largely consistent with phylogenetic clustering presented in Fig. 1. Totally 10 conserved motifs, designated as motif 1 to motif 10, were identified in the PeGH3 proteins (Fig. 2C). Ten proteins from subgroup 1 of Group III along with two proteins (PeGH3-9 and PeGH3-23) from Group II contained all motifs. Besides, 6 genes including PeGH3-1, PeGH3-5, PeGH3-7, PeGH3-12, PeGH3-13, and PeGH3-15 from Group III, were shown to lack only motif 6. By contrast, PeGH3-21 lacked four motifs (motifs 5, 6, 7, and 9), while PeGH3-20 lacked motifs 1, 3, 5, and 9. Obviously, PeGH3-22 possessed the fewest motifs, with only motifs 7 and 10 being detected. Additionally, the PeGH3 genes exhibited substantial variation in the number of exons, ranging from 2 (PeGH3-8 and PeGH3-18) to 11 (PeGH3-17) (Fig. 2B). Nine genes contained 3 exons, whereas 5 genes had either 4 or 5 exons. Furthermore, 11 genes (eg. PeGH3-6, PeGH3-16) lacked both 5’ and 3’ UTRs, whereas PeGH3-11 and PeGH3-21 lacked the 5’ UTR or 3’ UTR. The divergent exon-intron structures among different phylogenetic subgroups suggested that these PeGH3 genes underwent structural diversification during evolution.

Chromosomal location and synteny analysis of PeGH3 genes

Chromosomal localization analysis suggested that the PeGH3 genes were unevenly distributed across 9 of the 24 chromosomes of P. edulis. To be specific, chromosome Ped1D harbored the largest number of PeGH3 genes (4), while chromosomes Ped01C and Ped07D each contained 3 members (Fig. 3). Furthermore, chromosomes Ped05C, Ped07C, and Ped05D each harbored 2 PeGH3 genes, whereas only one PeGH3 gene was mapped to each of Ped03C, ChrPed11C, and ChrPed03D. These results suggested that half of the PeGH3 genes were concentrated on chromosomes Ped01 and Ped07. Furthermore, intraspecific synteny analysis revealed 18 pairs of collinear genes in P. edulis (e.g. PeGH3-1 and PeGH3-13, PeGH3-1 and PeGH3-5, PeGH3-3 and PeGH3-10), all of which originated from segmental duplication events (Fig. 4A). No tandem duplication events were detected among these genes, implying that segmental duplication was the primary evolutionary mechanism driving the expansion of the PeGH3 gene family. In addition, the evolutionary conservation of PeGH3 genes was assessed through synteny analysis between P. edulis and three representative plant species: A. thaliana, O. sativa, and Z. mays. The results showed that only 7 collinear gene pairs were identified between P. edulis and the dicot A. thaliana (Fig. 4B). By contrast, a substantially higher number of collinear pairs were detected between P. edulis and the two monocot species: 18 pairs with O. sativa and 29 pairs with Z. mays (Fig. 4C, D). These findings underscored a high level of evolutionary conservation of GH3 family proteins among the monocot species.

Cis-element composition and functional categorization of promoters

The 2000 bp upstream regions of the 23 PeGH3 genes were examined to identify cis-acting regulatory elements (CREs) and investigate their underlying biological functions and regulatory mechanism. A total of 50 CREs were identified and then divided into four functional categories: development, light responsiveness, hormone responsiveness, and stress responsiveness (Fig. 5). Among them, stress-responsive elements accounted for the highest proportion (43%), followed by hormone-responsive elements (27.4%) and light-responsive elements (26.1%). The development-responsive elements had the lowest proportion (3.5%). The hormone-responsive elements included ABA-responsive elements (ABRE), auxin-responsive elements (AuxRR-core and TGA-element), GA-responsive elements (GARE-motif, P-box, and TATC-box), methyl jasmonate (MeJA) responsive elements (CGTCA-motif and GACG-motif), and SA responsive elements (TCA). All the 23 genes contained ABA-responsive elements, whereas 19 of them harbored MeJA-responsive elements excluding PeGH3-7, PeGH3-9, PeGH3-21, and PeGH3-23. Additionally, 13 genes contained a small number of auxin-responsive elements, and 11 possessed a few SA-responsive elements, whereas 9 had a few GA-responsive elements. Specifically, 23, 17, 12, and 12 genes contained defense and stress (STRE, TC-rich repeats, and DRE1), anaerobic (ARE), drought (MBS), and low-temperature (LTR) responsive elements, respectively. Furthermore, all genes except PeGH3-4 possessed the MYB or MYC transcription factor binding sites. The obtained results demonstrated that PeGH3 genes might be involved in the responses of plants to multiple hormones and various environmental stresses, potentially modulated by these diverse cis-acting regulatory elements.

Expression analysis of PeGH3 genes

Flower buds, leaves, roots, rhizomes, bamboo shoots, culms, branches and seeds were examined by qRT-PCR to explore the transcript levels of PeGH3 genes in various organs. The results indicated significant differences in the expression of PeGH3 genes across different tissues, with the majority of genes expressed in all the tested tissues (Fig. 6). PeGH3-1 and PeGH3-15 showed relatively higher expression in leaves, while PeGH3-10 displayed the highest expression in bamboo shoots (Fig. 6A, B). Moreover, PeGH3-6, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, PeGH3-15, and PeGH3-18 displayed higher expression levels in flower buds (Fig. 6C), while PeGH3-8 and PeGH3-18 exhibited higher expression levels in culms (Fig. 6D). In addition, PeGH3-4, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 showed higher expression levels in seeds (Fig. 6E), while PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 revealed higher expression levels in branches, roots, and rhizomes (Fig. 6F, G, H). Notably, the expression level of PeGH3-22 was extremely low and even not detected in all the tested tissues. These findings suggested that the expression patterns of PeGH3 genes were diverse, and they might play diverse roles in regulating different tissues.

To further investigate the responses of PeGH3 genes to various abiotic stresses and hormones, their expression profiles were analyzed based on published transcriptome data (Fig. 7). In this study, a two-fold change in gene expression in the treated plants relative to the control (untreated) was considered a significant difference. The expression levels of PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, PeGH3-17, and PeGH3 -18 were significantly induced, while PeGH3-13 exhibited remarkable inhibition under salt and drought stresses (Fig. 7A). Moreover, PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, and PeGH3-14 showed higher expression levels under drought stress compared to salt stress. Under heat stress, PeGH3-1, PeGH3-5, PeGH3-11, PeGH3-12, PeGH3-13, PeGH3-15, and PeGH3-21 exhibited a decreasing expression trend, whereas PeGH3-4 and PeGH3-14 displayed an opposite trend (Fig. 7B). Under cold stress, several upregulated genes including PeGH3-1, PeGH3-2, PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-13, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 were identified (Fig. 7C). Moreover, the number of PeGH3 genes responding to NAA treatment was shown to be greater than that responding to GA treatment. PeGH3-3, PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-9, PeGH3-11, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 were strongly induced solely by NAA treatment. Both PeGH3-9 and PeGH3-23 were induced, while only PeGH3-14 was inhibited by GA treatment (Fig. 7D). Besides, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-15, and PeGH3-18 displayed an upregulation trend under ABA treatment (Fig. 7E). The expression levels of PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, PeGH3-17, and PeGH3-18 were remarkably upregulated by SA treatment, whereas those of PeGH3-5 and PeGH3-13 were significantly downregulated (Fig. 7E). These findings implied that PeGH3 genes were involved in different biological processes under abiotic stresses and hormone treatments.

Subcellular localization of PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18

According to the above expression analysis of PeGH3 genes in different tissues, as well as abiotic stress conditions and hormone treatments, it was speculated that PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 might play a predominant role. To conduct a more detailed investigation of their subcellular location, transient expression assays were performed in the epidermal cells of N. benthamiana. The obtained results indicated that the GFP signal in the control group was distributed throughout the cytoplasm and nucleus (Fig. 8). Furthermore, the GFP signals of the four PeGH3-GFP fusion proteins exhibited a similar distribution pattern, showing diffuse localization in both the cytoplasm and nucleus (Fig. 8). To confirm these findings, we harvested N. benthamiana leaves expressing the GFP control as well as the four PeGH3-GFP fusion proteins for subcellular fractionation assays. Western blot experiments demonstrated that no breakdown products of the four expressed PeGH3-GFP proteins when probed with anti-GFP antibodies (Fig. S1). These results confirmed that PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 were indeed localized in both the cytoplasm and nucleus.

Salt and drought stress assays of PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 in yeast

Since PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 genes strongly responded to NaCl and drought stresses, the validation in yeast expression experiments was performed. As displayed in Fig. 9A, both control and four transgenic strains could grow under 0 M, 0.5 M, and 1.0 M NaCl treatments. The growth of control and four transgenic strains remained similar under 0 M and 0.5 M NaCl conditions. Nevertheless, the four transgenic strains exhibited better growth relative to the control under 1.0 M NaCl treatment, suggesting that the PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 genes enhanced salt stress tolerance in transgenic yeast cells. Additionally, all the control and transgenic strains could grow under 0 mM, 30 mM, 60 mM, 90 mM, and 120 mM PEG treatments, while a gradual reduction in growth was observed with rising PEG concentrations (Fig. 9B). Under the five PEG treatments, the PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, and PeGH3-14 overexpressing strains exhibited better growth than the control. Our results suggested that the PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, and PeGH3-14 genes enhanced drought stress tolerance in transgenic yeast cells.

Discussion

Auxin plays a vital role in all aspects of plant growth and development, and the finding has also been demonstrated in P. edulis [32, 33]. The GH3 gene family is one of the primary early response gene families to auxin in plant growth, encoding an enzyme involved in auxin conjugation [13]. This gene family has been demonstrated to regulate the homeostasis of auxin distribution in various plant species [34]. Nevertheless, the GH3 genes in P. edulis have not yet been investigated. In view of the significant ecological and economic roles of P. edulis, the investigation of GH3 genes from this species is conductive to its cultivation and breeding. In this study, 23 GH3 gene family members were identified in P. edulis. The results indicated that the number of GH3 genes in P. edulis was significantly greater than in other monocots including Setaria italica (17), Z. mays (13), Hordeum vulgare (9), Sorghum bicolor (11), Brachypodium distachyon (8), and O. sativa (12) [12]. However, the genome size of P. edulis (2051 Mb) is smaller than that of H. vulgare (4790 Mb) [35, 36]. Therefore, there was no direct correlation between the number of GH3 genes and genome sizes, consistent with the previous report by Kong et al. [12]. Furthermore, the evolution of a species is more closely related to the number of gene families than to the size of genome [37]. Compared with other Gramineae species, the GH3 gene family in P. edulis was relatively conserved, while the number had increased significantly. The expansion and contraction of gene families can exert a vital effect on phenotypic adaptation during speciation [38], which may also elucidate why bamboo species differ markedly from other plants in the same family.

The published GH3 members from eudicots and monocots have been classified into three subfamilies (I, II, and III) [4, 6, 8]. To explore the evolutionary relationships of PeGH3 members in P. edulis, a phylogenetic tree was constructed by using GH3 proteins from A. thaliana, O. sativa, Z. mays, and P. edulis. All the GH3 members were categorized into three distinct groups (Fig. 1). The PeGH3 members were assigned to either subfamily I or subfamily II, with the number of PeGH3 genes in subfamily II (17) remarkably exceeding that in subfamily I (6). Obviously, subfamily III contained only 10 AtGH3 members, which have been presumed to be specific to eudicot plants [6]. These findings demonstrated that the members of subfamily I and II were identified, while no members of subfamily III were observed in the PeGH3 genes of P. edulis. This result aligned with observations from other Poaceae plants including O. sativa, and Z. mays [7, 12]. Moreover, all PeGH3 members were divided into three major groups. PeGH3-19 and PeGH3-20 from group I, and PeGH3-7, PeGH3-9, PeGH3-17, PeGH3-22, and PeGH3-23 from group II significantly distanced from other PeGH3 genes. Studies have indicated that the types and numbers of motifs are the important basis for the gene family classification. But PeGH3-9, PeGH3-19, and PeGH3-23 had similar motif types and numbers to members from group III. Besides, differences in gene structures including exon-intron and UTR among gene family members are also considered tools for phylogenetic grouping. In our study, there were obvious differences in the numbers of exons/introns and UTR of PeGH3 genes belonging to the different groups. It was speculated that these differences in gene structure and motifs led to the different members of group I and II from other PeGH3 genes, suggesting that they had undergone functional diversification during evolution [39, 40].

Gene expression patterns are typically closely related to gene functions. It has been reported that the expression patterns of the GH3 family are diverse across different plant species [41, 42]. The GH3 homologs from potato are mainly expressed in flowering organs and fruits, while OsGH3.1, OsGH3.4, OsGH3.5, and OsGH3.8 from rice, as well as CaGH3-2, CaGH3-6, and CaGH3-7 from pepper, and CsGH3.3 and CsGH3.10 from tea were mainly expressed in flowers [41, 43]. Moreover, AtGH3-10 and AtGH3-11 have been demonstrated to be involved in stamen development, while OsGH3-8 participates in panicles differentiation [22, 44]. In P. edulis, most PeGH3 genes were expressed in flowers, leaves, roots, rhizomes, culms, bamboo shoots, and seeds, even though they exhibited the lowest transcript levels in leaves. This pattern was consistent with observations in Z. mays and Medicago sativa [45, 46]. Six of the PeGH3 genes, especially PeGH3-15 and PeGH3-18, displayed higher expression levels in flower buds (Fig. 6C). Clearly, PeGH3-15 together with AtGH3-10 and AtGH3-11 belonged to the same clade, while PeGH3-18 was closely related to OsGH3-8 based on the phylogenetic analysis, suggesting that the PeGH3-15 and PeGH3-18 might participate in the flower development of P. edulis [13, 47]. Differently, MdGH3s from apple and TaGH3s from wheat were highly expressed in roots [1, 48]. Moreover, OsGH3.2 from rice, CsGH3.3, CsGH3.5, CsGH3.10, and CsGH3.16 from tea, as well as CaGH3-3, CaGH3-4, and CaGH3-8 from pepper also revealed similar expression patterns [41, 43, 49]. Studies have indicated that OsGH3-2/8/13 in rice significantly influenced the development of PR, LR, AR, and HR [17–19]. In the meanwhile, OsGH3-5 and OsGH3-13 exhibited the opposite function in seed development [19, 20]. It was found in this study that PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, and PeGH3-18 all exhibited higher expression levels in branches, roots, and rhizomes, while PeGH3-4, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 displayed higher expression levels in seeds, suggesting that their potential roles in the development of roots, rhizomes, branches, and seeds. Similar to ZmGH3-1, ZmGH3-6, ZmGH3-10, and ZmGH3-11 from Z. mays, as well as CmaGH3-2 and CmaGH3-2 from Cucurbita maxima, PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-9, PeGH3-14, PeGH3-15, and PeGH3-18 exhibited higher expression levels in culms [45, 46], implying a possible role of these genes in culm development. Collectively, similar to GH3 members from other species, the function of PeGH3 genes in regulating the plant growth and development was conserved, although the specific genes exerting vital roles differed. In addition, the expression location of the genes is crucial for the investigation of their functions [43]. According to previous studies, AtGH3.11 from A. thaliana was located in the cytoplasm, nucleus, and reticulum, while CaGH3-5/3–7 from Capsicum annuum were found to be located in the membrane and chloroplast [43]. By contrast, SIGH3.15 from Solanum lycopersicum was expressed in the nucleus and cytoplasm [50]. Similarly, in this study, four PeGH3 proteins were all detected in the nucleus and cytoplasm. As a result, the GH3 homologs in different species exhibited various traits, further indicating that they might have diversified functions.

Transcription factors (TFs) play an essential role in regulating plant growth, development, and stress resistance through the modulation of gene expression [51]. Therefore, analyzing the regulatory elements of promoters can strengthen our capability of predicting gene expression under various growth conditions and stresses. Studies have demonstrated that the promoters of GH3 genes contain responsiveness elements for abiotic stress (such as MBS, LTR, and TC-rich) and hormones (such as ABRE, TGACG motif, and SARE) [1]. Additionally, these corresponding regulatory elements were also present in the promoters of PeGH3 genes. Among them, the expression levels of seven, seven, three, and three PeGH3 genes were altered by NAA, SA, ABA, and GA treatments. In A. thaliana, only AtGH3-12 (subfamily III) is involved in the SA signaling pathway, while AtGH3 members from subfamily II play vital roles in IAA adenylation or amino acid conjugation reactions [5, 52, 53]. Similarly, PeGH3-3, PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-9, PeGH3-11, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 from subfamily II responded to exogenous NAA, exhibiting increased expression, which was not coincidental with some plants like Z. mays [45]. Obviously, different from A. thaliana, PeGH3-5 and PeGH3-13 from subfamily I, as well as PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, PeGH3-17, and PeGH3-18 from subfamily II responded to exogenous SA, and the genes from the two subfamilies exhibited opposite expression trends. ABA, a key phytohormone involved in plant defense, was also impacted by GH3 gene expression; overexpression of OsGH3-2 in rice leads to reduced ABA levels [18]. By contrast, ZmGH3.7 (a homolog of OsGH3-2) was significantly induced by ABA treatment [45]. Nevertheless, only PeGH3-8, PeGH3-15, and PeGH3-18 were responsive to ABA treatment and were upregulated in P. edulis. Moreover, the expression levels of 9, 7, 6, and 6 PeGH3 genes were significantly altered by heat, cold, NaCl, and PEG treatments, respectively. Specifically, PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 were all upregulated in response to NaCl and PEG stresses. Furthermore, the overexpression of these four genes significantly enhanced the NaCl and PEG tolerance of yeast. Similarly, ZmGH3-1, ZmGH3-2, ZmGH3-8, and ZmGH3-9 exhibited responses to NaCl, and PEG stresses [12]. In rice, the overexpression of OsGH3-2 or OsGH3-13 enhances drought tolerance, while OsGH3-2 and OsGH3-8 are also significantly upregulated under salt stress [6, 18, 54]. Interestingly, PeGH3-19 and PeGH3-20 were not detected in the above-mentioned abiotic stresses, suggesting that they might be pseudogenes or had unique expression patterns not examined in our study [55, 56]. Collectively, these findings suggested that PeGH3 genes might exhibit both conservation and diversification in their functions. Furthermore, they suggested that the GH3 genes exerted a significant role in growth and development, also contributing to the responses to various stresses across different species. Further experiments will be conducted to investigate their functions.

Conclusion

To conclude, in this study, 23 homologs of the GH3 gene family were identified in P. edulis. We characterized their chromosomal distribution, gene structure, phylogenetic relationships, and cis-regulatory elements. In addition, expression analysis suggested that PeGH3 genes were involved in the plant growth and responses to various stresses. Furthermore, as demonstrated by heterologous expression analysis in yeast, PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 exhibited the increased tolerance to salt and drought stresses. However, future research must focus on the functional validation of these key genes to confirm their roles. Our findings provide a foundation for the further exploration of the role of PeGH3 genes in the development and stress resilience of P. edulis, and offer valuable implications for bamboo breeding programs.

Materials and methods

Identification of GH3 genes in P. edulis

In order to identify GH3 members in P. edulis, the genome, genome annotation files, and protein sequences of P. edulis were obtained from the BambooBase database (https://bamboo.genobank.org/Blast.html). These sequences were screened through the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) with Pfam number PF03321, which contains the conserved GH3 domain through the HMMER software with an E-value < 1e-5 [57]. Furthermore, GH3 proteins from A. thaliana and O. sativa served as reference sequences for a BLAST search against the P. edulis protein dataset, with an E-value ≤1e-5 as the parameter based on TBtools, aiming to retrieve homologous genes [58]. The results from both search methods were integrated, and redundant sequences were removed. Candidate GH3 members were subsequently submitted to the NCBI’s Conserved Domain Database (CDD) (E-value < 0.01), where sequences with incomplete N/C terminals were manually excluded [59]. Eventually, the GH3 gene family members of P. edulis were identified. Additionally, the physicochemical properties of these PeGH3 proteins were explored by employing the ExPASy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/proparam/).

Gene structure, conserved motif and phylogenetic analysis

To analyze the gene structure and evolutionary relationships of GH3 genes in P. edulis, exon-intron maps were constructed using TBtools software, with reference to the genome sequences and coding sequences (CDS). Conserved motifs within the PeGH3 protein sequences were identified using the MEME tool (http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme) [59], with the number of motifs set to 10 and the motif length constrained to 6 − 50 amino acids, while other parameters were set to default. In terms of the phylogenetic analysis, a total of 68 GH3 protein sequences from four species including A. thaliana (20), Z. mays (12), O. sativa (13), and P. edulis (23) were aligned using MEGA 7.0 software [60]. Then, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method with a bootstrap value set to 1000 and other parameters set to default values [41]. In addition, the resulting phylogenetic tree was visualized and optimized with Evolview (https://evolgenius.info//evolview-v2/#login).

Cis-acting regulatory elements and synteny analysis

TBtools software was used to extract the 2000 bp genome sequences upstream of the ATG start codon. Subsequently, these promoter sequences were submitted to the PlantCARE database (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) for predicting cis-acting regulatory elements, including stress responsiveness, hormone responsiveness, and TF binding elements. Furthermore, the duplication pattern and synteny of the PeGH3 genes were explored by utilizing the Multiple Collinearity Scan toolkit (MCScanX) with E-value < 1e-10 [61].

RNA and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Different tissues of P. edulis including flowers, leaves, roots, rhizomes, culms, bamboo shoots, branches and seeds were collected from the Zhoucun village of Rongshui county in Guangxi province to explore the tissue-specific expression patterns of PeGH3 genes. P. edulis is a common species, and the specimens were collected from a cultivated forest with the owner’s permission. The specimens were deposited at the Herbarium of School of Forestry, Guangxi University, with accession numbers LSN2024112101 to LSN2024112108, and identified by Prof. Dayong Huang from the Guangxi Key Laboratory of Superior Timber Trees Resource Cultivation at the Guangxi Forestry Research Institute. Total RNA was extracted using a kit from Guangzhou Magen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). Complementary DNAs (cDNAs) were synthesized through the PrimeScript TM FAST RT reagent Kit (Takara, Dalian, Japan). Next, qRT-PCR was performed to determine the relative gene expression levels using the TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II FAST qPCR (Takara, Dalian, Japan). The amplification reactions were conducted on a LightCycler 480 instrument with gene-specific primers, and each reaction included three biological replicates to guarantee reproducibility. The relative expression level of each gene was calculated based on the 2−∆∆CT method [62]. The primers used for qRT-PCR were listed in Table S1, with PeNTB serving as the internal control [63].

Gene expression analysis according to RNA-seq data

Transcriptome data were obtained from leaf tissues of P. edulis seedlings subjected to various treatments, including 100mM gibberellin (GA), 200 µM abscisic acid (ABA), 1 mM salicylic acid (SA), 5 µM naphthalene acetic acid (NAA), 20 mM sodium chloride (NaCl), 25% polyethylene glycol (PEG), cold stress (4℃), and heat stress (42℃) (PRJNA413166, PRJNA715101, PRJNA767196, PRJNA1289125, and PRJNA707140). The PeGH3 genes were identified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs) based on a log2 fold change (log2FC) in the expression level of ≥ 1 or ≤ -1, with FPKM values of at least 1. The expression abundance of PeGH3 genes, quantified as transcripts per million reads (TPM) values was calculated separately and employed to generate the heat map of gene expression.

Subcellular localization and Western blot analysis of candidate genes

The coding sequences (CDS) of PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 excluding the terminator were ligated into the pCAMBIA1302 vector. Subsequently, both the reconstructed and empty vectors were introduced into Agrobacterium GV3101, which were later injected into the leaves of one-month-old tobacco plants. After 24 h of incubation in dark, GFP signals were observed through a laser confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Western blot assays were conducted following the protocol described by Zhu et al. [64]. In brief, 1 g of leaf powder from three different infected tobacco plants was mixed with 1 mL of IP lysis buffer containing a Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Bimake, Houston, TX, USA). The crude slurry was subjected to incubation on ice for 30 minutes, with vertexing every 10 minutes. After centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4°C, the supernatant was collected. Afterwards, 30 µL of the supernatant was mixed with 10 µL of 4 × protein loading buffer, boiled at 100°C for 10 min, and later analyzed using sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). The proteins separated on the gel were transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, IPVH00010). Next, the membrane was sequentially incubated with mouse anti-GFP IgG as the primary antibody and HRP goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) as the secondary antibody. Finally, immunoreactive proteins were visualized using an ECL Substrate Kit (Advansta, K-12045) in accordance with the manufacturer’ s instructions.

Verification of salt and drought stress tolerance of candidate genes in yeast

Four genes including PeGH3-4, PeGH3-8, PeGH3-14, and PeGH3-18 were individually ligated into the pYES2 vector. Then, the recombinant vectors were transformed into the yeast strain INVSC1, and the transformed cells were spread on the corresponding selective yeast medium (SD-Ura medium). After being incubated at 30 ℃ for 3 days, single colonies were picked for PCR identification to confirm positive clones. To assess the salinity and drought stress, yeast cells were serially diluted ten-fold and incubated in SG/-Ura liquid medium with varying concentrations of NaCl (0, 0.5 mol/L, 1.0 mol/L, 1.5 mol/L, and 2.0 mol/L) and PEG (0, 30 mmol/L, 60 mmol/L, 90mM, and 120mM) at 30 °C for 16 h (OD ≈ 0.6). Finally, the growth status of the yeast colonies was observed and then photographed to evaluate stress tolerance after the growth at 30 °C for 3 to 5 days.
