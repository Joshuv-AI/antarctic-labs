A Key Distinction in EVT Decision-Making: Accurately Measured Core Volume Is a Prognostic Marker and Accurately Measured Penumbra Volume Is a Predictive Biomarker
Authors: Amir Molaie, Jeffrey L. Saver
Published: 2026

We write to correct an increasingly frequent misunderstanding regarding the appropriate use of imaging biomarkers in endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) decision-making for patients with acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusions presenting with large ischemic cores.1–9 This infelicity has arisen due to the failure of some investigators to appreciate the important distinction between prognostic versus predictive biomarkers.

Accordingly, prognostic markers should be used to guide risk stratification, while predictive markers should be used to guide personalized treatment selection. In actuality, biomarkers will almost always have some degree of prognostic value and some degree of predictive value, but frequently one aspect is predominant.

Applying this framework to reperfusion therapy decision-making, it is clear that accurately measured ischemic core volume is primarily a prognostic, and not predictive, biomarker. Over a wide range, from 0 to 150 mL, increasing ischemic core volumes are associated in a monotonic fashion with reduced functional independence outcomes.14 For example, in the HERMES (Highly Effective Reperfusion Evaluated in Multiple Endovascular Stroke Trials) pooled trial analysis, every 10-mL increase in core volume was associated with a 23% reduction in the odds of a functional independence outcome.15 However, patients with all core sizes benefited from treatment. In pooled analyses of small-moderate and large core trials, rates of functional independence (modified Rankin Scale score, 0–2) dropped with increasing core size from 0 to 150 mL in both EVT and medical management (MM) groups (prognostic utility) but remained higher at each step with EVT than MM (the absence of predictive utility): core volume of 0 to 70 mL-EVT 46% versus MM 27%, core volume of 70 to 100 mL-EVT 25% versus MM 9%; and core volume of 100 to 150 mL-EVT 11% versus MM 6%.16,17 Over this range, ischemic core volumes do not modify the relative benefit of EVT (though they do reduce the absolute benefit).

The available evidence also strongly indicates that penumbra size is primarily a predictive, but not prognostic, biomarker. In small-to-moderate core patients in the National Institutes of Health DEFUSE 2 trial (Diffusion and Perfusion Imaging Evaluation for Understanding Stroke Evolution 2) using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) diffusion and perfusion assessment, patients with target mismatch benefited substantially from reperfusion (9-fold increase in odds of favorable clinical outcome), while patients without target mismatch did not benefit and tended to be harmed by reperfusion (5-fold decrease in odds of favorable clinical outcome).18 The same is true for large core patients. In the FRAME study (French Acute Multimodal Imaging Study to Select Patients for Mechanical Thrombectomy), large core patients with the presence of a mismatch benefitted substantially from reperfusion (7-fold increase in odds of good clinical outcome), while patients without a target mismatch did not benefit from reperfusion.10 Furthermore, the extent of benefit from reperfusion correlated with the pretreatment penumbra volume in a monotonic manner; for example, in patients with small penumbra size (mismatch ratio >1.2), reperfusion was associated with only a 4-fold increase in odds of good clinical outcome, while, in patients with large penumbra size (mismatch ratio >1.8), reperfusion was associated with a 17-fold increase in odds of good clinical outcome. In a meta-analysis of randomized EVT trials, the extent of penumbral salvage reducing infarct growth correlated strongly with the extent of EVT benefit on outcome.19 For every 4.5-mL reduction in infarct growth by EVT, the clinical benefit on functional independence of EVT compared with MM increased by an absolute 10%.

The MR CLEAN-LATE randomized trial (Multicenter Randomized Clinical Trial of Endovascular Stroke Treatment in the Netherlands for Late Arrivals) provides additional compelling evidence of the distinct roles of core volume and penumbra size as biomarkers, having tested each concurrently in the same trial.20 Penumbra volumes strongly modified the association of EVT with outcomes (Pinteraction<0.001), with the largest effect size among patients with penumbras of ≥120 mL. Core volume did not modify the EVT effect.

With this background, we can turn in an informed manner to the results and the reception of the recent 6 positive large core trials.21 Before the results came in, there was concern that ischemic core volume might behave in large core patients like a predictive rather than prognostic variable, with magnified core sizes limiting the ability of EVT to confer benefit. Loss of benefit was indeed seen at extremely large core volumes, >200 mL. However, over a large core volume range lower than that extreme, between 0 and 150 mL, the presence of a large core did not modify relative treatment benefit; core size was a prognostic, not predictive, variable.

Importantly, showing that one biomarker has prognostic value but does not modify treatment benefit has no bearing on whether another biomarker has predictive value and an important role to play in treatment decision-making. Unfortunately, in the field’s excitement at learning that core size was not a predictive variable, many somehow reached the conclusion that penumbra size must also not be a predictive variable, an obvious logical fallacy. In fact, the initially available evidence from the positive large core trials suggested that penumbra size does affect the extent and even the possibility of benefit from EVT.22 Among 863 patients pooled from 3 RCTs, best estimates indicated that patients benefited from EVT if mismatch was present, with EVT versus MM increasing the odds of functional independence more than 3-fold. In contrast, patients did not benefit from EVT if a mismatch was absent, with no difference occurring in functional independence between patients treated with EVT versus MM.

Curiously, the recently published ATLAS (Analysis of Mechanical Thrombectomy for Large Core Ischemic Stroke) individual patient-data meta-analysis reported results discrepant with prior reports from individual trials, indicating that penumbra size did not modify the relative benefits of EVT treatment.23 One plausible explanation for this discordance is that the more recent meta-analysis may have relied unduly upon inexact perfusion computed tomography (CT) rather than more reliable diffusion MRI measures of core volume. Diffusion-weighted MRI assessment of core directly indexes accumulated bioenergetic failure through the time of imaging. In contrast, perfusion CT assessment of core does not directly index cellular energetic failure and reflects physiologic status only at the single timepoint of imaging acquisition. As a result, CT perfusion is susceptible to overestimation of core, particularly in the early time window.24 While potentially of modest impact in small core settings, CT overestimation of core size is likely to be more consequential in large core settings, where it will incorrectly categorize substantial volumes of tissues that are actually still salvageable as irreversibly infarcted. By definition, true ischemic core is irreversibly fully infarcted, rather than entirely or partially salvageable, and cannot be benefited by early reperfusion. Further development of CT techniques that more directly index tissue bioenergetic failure, including net water uptake25 and volumetric assessment of severe hypodensity (attentuation ≤26 Hounsfield units),26 may enable CT to more precisely delineate penumbra in large core ischemic strokes. It is also important to recognize that the recent ATLAS study did not actually perform an analysis of potential treatment effect modification by presence versus absence of penumbra (mismatch ratio >1.0 versus ≤1.0), but rather examined substantial versus modest penumbra (mismatch ratio >1.2 versus ≤1.2).

It is helpful to consider correctly-measured ischemic core size as a backward-looking biomarker, reflecting the patient’s course up until the time of EVT decision-making, and penumbra size as a forward-looking biomarker, indicating the patient’s potential courses after EVT decision-making. If extensive injury has already occurred, one may still consider going forward, but, if there is no salvageable tissue left to save, EVT should not be pursued.

The faulty reasoning that because accurately measured core size does not modify treatment benefit, accurately measured penumbra size must also not modify treatment benefit, has become surprisingly widespread. Most worryingly, it is implicitly present in the recently issued 2026 American Heart Association acute ischemic stroke management guidelines.27 The guidelines section on patient selection for EVT gives a strong, class 1A, recommendation for pursuing EVT in large core patients in both the 0 to 6- and 6 to 24-hour windows without indicating that it is reasonable to not pursue EVT in large core patients who have MRI-indicated absence of penumbra because they will not benefit and indeed may be harmed by therapy. (A generic class 2A recommendation for perfusion imaging in the extended window does appear earlier in the guideline but without any specific guidance on how to use it in EVT selection.)

Randomized trials focused specifically on this question are needed. The recently initiated OUTER LIMITS study (Endovascular Therapy for Large Core Patients With Uncertain Response to Thrombectomy) is addressing this uncertainty by evaluating EVT in patients with infarct core >70 mL without core-penumbra mismatch and in patients with extreme core volumes exceeding 125 mL with or without core-penumbra mismatch (Unique identifier: NCT06961487). Importantly, advocating for continued consideration of core-penumbra interactions in decision-making for EVT is not intended to restrict access to brain-saving therapy for patients who would benefit but rather to reduce exposure to potentially brain-injuring therapy for patients who would not benefit and may be harmed.
