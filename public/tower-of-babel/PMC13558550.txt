Social inequality and the mental health of Chinese youth
Authors: Yuanchao Wang, Yiqing Zhang, Yining Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The mental health of young people serves as a social barometer, reflecting societal conditions. However, the relationship between social inequality and youth mental health remains a contested issue. This study uses data from the China General Social Survey (CGSS) 2021 and 2023, focusing on Chinese youth aged 18–30, to analyze the association between social inequality and their mental health, along with its group heterogeneity. This study reveals an overall inverted U-shaped pattern between social inequality and mental health of this demographic. Regarding group heterogeneity, an inverted U-shaped pattern is found among males and those with non-agricultural hukou; a positive association is found among youth within the system; and no significant association is found among females, youth with agricultural hukou and youth outside the system. The findings indicate that youth mental health is the outcome of the interplay between the “tunnel effect” and the “relative deprivation effect” and identify a potential a “system justification” effect among youth outside the system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58041-9.

Introduction

Young people are at the convergence of multiple life tasks, including education, employment, housing, and childbearing. They are highly sensitive to changes in their social environment, which makes them more prone to various forms of psychological distress1. As a result, mental health has become a particularly salient issue for them. Youth form the backbone of economic and social development. Therefore, their mental health is not only associated with their personal growth and quality of life, but also closely linked to long-term accumulation of human capital, innovation capacity, and sustainable development. Furthermore, the mental health of youth serves as an important window into a society’s developmental state and its underlying tensions. It acts as a “social barometer” and is a key indicator for observing social well-being and assessing long-term social development.

For these reasons, youth mental health has long attracted sustained attention in the academic literature. Most existing studies adopt a micro-level perspective, examining how factors such as gender, age, family socioeconomic status, lifestyles, psychological traits, and social support shape mental health outcomes2–5. In contrast, macro-level research on mental health focuses on the role of social structural factors, with particular emphasis on social inequality. On this issue, the literature remains contested, with two main viewpoints currently: one view argues that social inequality intensifies social comparison and relative deprivation, thereby undermining mental health; the other view suggests that, under certain social conditions, social inequality can be interpreted as a signal of social mobility opportunities, which may foster optimistic expectations and exert a positive influence on it.

It should be noted that the above two competing viewpoints are primarily based on studies conducted in developed countries or regions such as Europe, the United States, and Japan, while research on developing countries remains relatively scarce. As the world’s largest developing country, China offers both practical urgency and theoretical value as a research subject. On the one hand, China has undergone profound social transformation over the past decade. Along with slowing economic growth and external shocks, intergenerational mobility has declined, and the wealth gap between social classes has continued to widen6,7, all of which pose serious challenges to the mental health of youth. On the other hand, the pattern of social inequality in China and its effect on public perceptions of fairness have unique characteristics. China’s Gini coefficient has remained above the internationally recognized alarm level of 0.4 since 2000, yet the economy and society have stayed stable over a long period8. This coexistence of a high Gini coefficient and social stability challenges the traditional theoretical logic that links “objective inequality – relative deprivation – social instability”9. Furthermore, research indicates that Chinese citizens’ perception of fairness is jointly moderated by horizontal social comparison and vertical social comparison. When individuals derive strong satisfaction from comparing themselves with their own impoverished past from over a decade ago, the perceived inequality generated by horizontal social comparison is significantly weakened8. The rapid and sustained economic growth in China over recent decades has substantially improved the living standards of a large share of its population, generating a strong sense of vertical satisfaction. As a result, even with considerable objective inequality, overall social stability has been maintained. Therefore, when examining the relationship between social inequality and youth mental health in China, existing Western theories cannot be directly applied. Instead, the underlying mechanisms should be investigated within China’s unique context.

Accordingly, this study uses data from the 2021 and 2023 Chinese General Social Survey (CGSS) to examine the association between social inequality and youth mental health within the Chinese context.

Literature review

What factors shape mental health? Answers to this question mainly adopts two perspectives: micro-level and macro-level. The majority of studies have focused on micro-level factors, including demographic characteristics, lifestyle and behaviors, psychological traits, and social support. Research on demographic factors shows that female, older youth, youth with siblings, and youth with lower social capital are more likely to face mental health challenges2. Research on lifestyle find that sufficient sleep benefits mental health10, while poorer physical conditions, such as higher BMI, lower lung capacity and endurance, have negative effects11. Research on psychological traits indicates that cognitive flexibility, emotion regulation and psychological resilience can reduce depression and anxiety12,13. Finally, research on social support suggest that social networks protect mental health by strengthening resilience or easing stress from negative social events4,5.

Unlike the micro-level perspective, macro-level research pays greater attention to the effects of social structural factors on mental health. Within this line of inquiry, social inequality has been widely discussed due to its rising trend across the globe. However, research in this field remains inconclusive, giving rise to two strands of inquiry. The first, the “adverse effect” perspective, posits that rising social inequality undermines mental health, a conclusion supported by studies from multiple countries including those in Europe and the United States14, Japan15, and other countries. This negative effect is often explained through theories of “relative deprivation” and “status anxiety”. Relative deprivation refers to a negative psychological experience in which individuals perceive themselves to be deprived after comparing their situation with a reference group and finding themselves at a disadvantage. This perception can lead to negative emotions such as helplessness, depression, and anxiety16,17. If people take the average social income as their reference, the widening income gap concentrates wealth at the top, thereby inevitably increasing the proportion of those below the average (the relatively deprived) and lowering society’s average happiness18. In addition, income inequality reinforces social hierarchies and makes status competition more intense across society, which gives rise to widespread status anxiety. This refers to individuals’ ongoing concerns about others’ evaluations and the risk of failure in competition, thus leading to a decline in average social well-being19. In the Chinese context, the country’s social transformation has gradually revealed a trend of class rigidity and widening wealth gaps between social classes6,7. The “long shadow effect” of family background on personal income has become increasingly evident20, making it significantly harder for individuals to achieve upward mobility through their own efforts, which in turn lowers their expectations for mobility. At the same time, Internet technology has made the lifestyles of the formerly inaccessible advantaged classes visible to ordinary individuals. This not only expands the reference groups for social comparison but also easily triggers upward social comparison21. Such cross-class comparisons continuously intensify youth’s sense of relative deprivation and status anxiety. Moreover, while the main explanation in the past for China’s high Gini coefficient yet social stability was vertical satisfaction9, today’s young people lack the memory of moving from conditions of scarcity to economic improvement that their parents’ generation experienced. As a result, their fairness perceptions are shaped more by horizontal comparisons with peers or higher social classes. This makes their perception of social inequality and class rigidity more direct and acute, while their sense of relative deprivation grows even stronger due to the absence of a buffer from vertical satisfaction. Therefore, in contemporary China, social inequality may be perceived as a sign of class rigidity, evoking strong feelings of relative deprivation among disadvantaged groups. Thus, there is likely a significant negative association between social inequality and the mental health of youth.

In contrast, the second strand of inquiry is the positive effect. It suggests that rising social inequality may enhance residents’ subjective well-being and thereby improve mental health, which can be explained by the “tunnel effect”. This phenomenon supported by studies in rural China22 and several Eastern European countries23. This idea comes from a classic example proposed by Hirschman and Rothschild24. In a traffic jam within a two-lane tunnel, when the adjacent lane begins to move, drivers may feel optimistic because they expect their own lane to start moving soon. Applied to that, when individuals interpret widening income gaps as a signal of future income improvement, it can raise optimistic expectations about social mobility opportunities, which may enhance well-being and mental health. The tunnel effect depends on the condition that society genuinely offers perceptible opportunities for upward mobility. In contemporary China, this condition still holds. Therefore, the tunnel effect may still exist in China today. First, although China’s economic growth has slowed, the transition to high-quality development continues to create new and high-quality job opportunities24. Additionally, a number of studies based on Chinese data suggest that education remains an effective means for achieving class mobility25,26. Good job opportunities and education provide a realistic basis for young people to have optimistic expectations of social mobility. Second, China’s “common prosperity” strategy has made the benefits of social development more equitably accessible to all, leading to higher perceptions of fairness. Studies show that, compared to middle-aged and older groups, Chinese youth (aged 18–44) exhibit a stronger sense of social fairness and a greater consensus on the meaning of common prosperity. Nearly 60% of youth report experiencing upward mobility and hold optimistic expectations about future mobility27. This indicates that youth have not lost confidence in social mobility, suggesting that the tunnel effect may still exist. Finally, although the internet has broadened the scope of social comparison, a significant proportion of youth still prefer offline socializing, with their social circles formed around classmates, colleagues, and partners based on shared interests28. These offline, strong-tie networks are highly homogeneous. As a result, youth social comparison is largely limited to peers with similar backgrounds29. In summary, considering China’s visible channels of social mobility, young people’s high sense of fairness, positive expectations of mobility, and homogeneous social comparisons, the positive association between social inequality and youth mental health may still persist.

Although the two perspectives above offer different views for understanding the relationship between social inequality and mental health, existing study still has clear limitations. First, the law revealed by Piketty in Capital in the Twenty-First Century, that the rate of return on capital persistently exceeds the economic growth rate, is exacerbating global wealth and income inequality, a trend that is becoming increasingly prominent in China as well. Over the past decade, China has undergone rapid social transformation. Alongside slower economic growth and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, intergenerational mobility has declined6, while wealth gaps across social strata have continued to widen7. As a result, long-term and structural social inequality has become increasingly visible. However, most existing studies rely on data from developed countries such as Europe, the United States, and Japan, or on cross-national datasets, with limited attention to China and other developing countries. Second, a key reason for macro inequality of its impact on mental health is that existing studies often treat it as a homogeneous overall effect, with limited tests of group heterogeneity. But individuals’ positions within the social structure shapes their social environment, level of risk, and reference groups for social comparison, thereby leading to differential mental health effects of social inequality. Finally, recent studies find that as social inequality increases, the level of mental health exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship30,31. This evidence points to a nonlinear perspective of understanding its effect. However, whether this relationship holds universally, particularly in a rapidly transforming society like China, what its specific form and critical threshold are, and whether it varies across different youth groups remains insufficiently examined in the existing literature.

Therefore, this study focuses on China, where the social environment has undergone major change. Using data from the 2021 and 2023 Chinese General Social Survey (CGSS), it examines how macro-level social inequality affects the mental health of Chinese youth and whether this effect differs across social groups.

Theoretical hypothesis

The relationship between social inequality and youth mental health

Based on the literature review, the tunnel effect and the relative deprivation effect coexist among youth in China. On one hand, youth generally hold high expectations for upward mobility and believe in the possibility of changing their fate through self-driven paths, which forms the foundation of the tunnel effect. On the other hand, youth lack the vertical satisfaction that serves as a psychological buffer, making them more sensitive to perceptions of social unfairness, which is more likely to trigger relative deprivation. Therefore, the impact of income inequality on youth mental health may be a dynamic balance process, where the forces of the tunnel effect and the relative deprivation effect compete and offset each other. Notably, Hirschman and Rothschild (1973) already pointed out this effect that in the early stages, this effect causes individuals to tolerate social inequality due to optimistic expectations about future mobility. However, if social mobility channels stay blocked for a long time and expectations are repeatedly unmet, the initial positive attitude will turn into frustration24. This conclusion suggests that there may be an inverted U-shaped relationship between social inequality and mental health31. When the level of inequality is low, the tunnel effect may dominate. However, as inequality worsens and exceeds a certain point, causing mobility expectations to drop and the sense of deprivation from social comparison to outweigh hope, the relative deprivation effect will dominate, and mental health will then worsen.

Based on this, this study proposes Hypothesis 1: There is an inverted U-shaped relationship between social inequality and the mental health of youth in China.

Group heterogeneity in the relationship between social inequality and youth mental health

The disagreement about the impact of social inequality on mental health may be due to the differences between social groups. In China, there are three main social classification criteria: First, gender, where traditional cultural norms such as “men work outside the home, women work inside the home” have fostered distinct gender role divisions and the resultant gender inequality. Second, hukou, a dual urban-rural system that has shaped disparities in economic development and social networks between urban and rural areas, thereby differentiating youth with agricultural hukou and non-agricultural hukou. The third factor is the system divide. Market reforms in China have created a divided labor market, with noticeable differences in income, benefits, and rights protection between youth within the system and those outside it. Therefore, this study examines heterogeneity among youth along three key dimensions of social stratification in the Chinese context: gender, hukou, and system position.

Group heterogeneity based on gender

The impact of social inequality on the mental health of youth may exhibit significant gender heterogeneity, primarily driven by two pathways: family wealth inheritance and gender role division. First, from the perspective of family wealth inheritance, due to traditional son preference, males are the primary recipients of intergenerational transmission of family wealth and assets32. Although the concept of gender equality has become mainstream in contemporary society, a “son preference” viewpoint is still fairly common in less economically developed areas33,34. As a result, male youth more directly inherit and perceive significant disparities in the economic status of their families of origin. They also develop a clearer awareness of the mechanisms of social exclusion established by privileged groups, making their mental health closely associated with social inequality. In contrast, the intergenerational status inheritance for females is generally weaker than for males, and the economic capital they receive from their families of origin shows relatively smaller variation. This leads to a relatively ambiguous perception of overall societal wealth disparity among females. Second, from the perspective of gender role division, influenced by the traditional notion of “men work outside the home, women work inside the home,” males typically face greater pressure to achieve economic success, with their self-worth more closely tied to occupational status and income35. Consequently, males are more likely to perceive macro-level income inequality as a signal directly relevant to their own developmental prospects, experiencing fluctuations in social equality more acutely. In contrast, despite a significant increase in female labor force participation, women remain the primary bearers of family caregiving responsibilities36,37. The dual role of women in both public and private spheres makes their well-being and self-worth come from various sources, not just the economic side, but also family relationships and emotional fulfillment. Therefore, the impact of social inequality on women’s mental health may be lessened by other factors, and the relationship may not be significant.

In summary, given that male youth are more sensitive to social inequality in both wealth inheritance and role expectations while also lacking effective buffers, and that female youth tend to rely on more diverse sources of personal value and receive weaker intergenerational resource transfers, males tend to perceive social inequality more clearly whereas females perceive it less clearly. Based on this, the study proposes Hypothesis 2.1: Social inequality is significantly associated with the mental health of male youth, but not with that of female youth.

Group heterogeneity based on hukou

Since the 21st century, the Chinese government has implemented a series of policies to promote rural development and urban-rural integration. These policies have boosted the rural economy and relatively moderate socio-economic stratification in rural society. Research indicates that since 2012, the primary source of income inequality in China has shifted from the urban-rural gap to disparities within urban areas38. This suggests that rural youth are situated in an environment with relatively low social differentiation, whereas urban youth reside in a context of high social differentiation. Therefore, in the rural social environment, where social networks are close-knit and homogeneous, individuals tend to compare themselves with those they interact with most frequently and intimately, such as relatives, former classmates, and neighbors39. This kind of parallel comparison within homogeneous groups overlooks nationwide macro-level income disparities, thereby buffering the psychological impact of social inequality. As a result, the relationship between social inequality and the mental health of youth with agricultural hukou may not be significant. In contrast, urban residents exist in a highly stratified social structure. Their frame of reference extends beyond neighbors and relatives to include colleagues, peers, and even strangers encountered through social media. This broader scope of reference groups makes upward social comparisons more likely21, enabling them to perceive social inequality more acutely.

Thus, the study proposes Hypothesis 2.2: Social inequality is significantly associated with the mental health of youth with non-agricultural hukou, but not with that of youth with agricultural hukou.

Group heterogeneity based on system position

Since the 1980s, alongside China’s transition from a planned economy to a market economy, the non-state sector has gradually expanded. However, public ownership has been maintained, resulting in the current dual labor market structure characterized by the coexistence of state (within the system) and non-state (outside the system) sectors. This division results in differences between the two groups in terms of wage stability, benefits, job risks, and social identity and prestige40. Due to this difference, state-owned units provide social resources and protections to their employees, bringing positive psychological benefits like well-being, thus creating the “institutional shelter” effect41. This effect greatly reduces the fear of social inequality among youth within the system, making them more likely to interpret observed income gaps as positive signals of a dynamic socioeconomic environment and potential future development opportunities. Especially with the impact of external factors such as the pandemic, China’s economic environment has been in a downturn, and many companies, in order to survive, have had to cut salaries and lay off employees. The stability of the state-owned sector has become more prominent, which may enhance the psychological sense of security and satisfaction among youth within the system. Thus, it can be inferred that for youth within the system, there may be a positive association between social inequality and their mental health. For youth outside the system, they are fully exposed to market competition. When inequality is low, it may be seen as a sign of economic vitality. However, as inequality increases and reaches a certain point, it will be interpreted as a hopeless future for career development and the solidification of social classes, with strong “relative deprivation” taking the lead, so the association between the two may shift toward a negative direction.

Based on the above analysis, this study proposes Hypothesis 2.3: Among youth within the system, there is a significant positive association between social inequality and mental health. For youth outside the system, the association between the two may follow an inverted U-shaped pattern.

Data and variables

Data sources

This study utilises data from the Chinese General Social Survey (CGSS) for the years 2021 and 2023. The CGSS is designed by the Survey and Data Center of Renmin University of China and carried out nationwide. It is a cross-sectional survey that takes individuals as the main units of analysis. The project is conducted every one or two years and adopts a multi-stage, stratified probability sampling strategy proportional to population size. Overall, this dataset is highly nationally representative.

Following Arnett’s (2000) theory of emerging adulthood42, this study defines the age range of youth as 18 to 30 years. Arnett notes that “For most people, the transition from emerging adulthood to young adulthood intensifies during the late twenties and is largely completed by age 30.” This age demarcation has been adopted by many subsequent studies43,44. During this life stage, young people have moved beyond the daily protection of their families but have not yet fully assumed adult social roles, while also finding themselves at the convergence of multiple life tasks. The combination of this transitional social position and the accumulation of multiple pressures makes their mental state particularly sensitive to the impact of structural factors such as social inequality and class rigidity. Consequently, the effects of social inequality may be significantly magnified within this group. Accordingly, this study retains the sample of youth aged 18 to 30, resulting in a final sample size of 2,797.

Variable measurement

The dependent variable in this study is mental health. It is derived from the survey item: “During the past four weeks, how often have you felt depressed or discouraged?” The responses are coded as a continuous variable ranging from 1 to 5. Higher values indicate a lower frequency of depressive or discouraged feelings, which corresponds to a higher level of mental health.

The key independent variable is social inequality, measured by the provincial-level Theil Index. Henri Theil introduced this index in 1967 based on the concept of entropy in information theory. It captures the degree of uneven distribution of income or resources across populations or regions. The index ranges from 0 to positive infinity. Larger values indicate higher levels of social inequality.

Following prior research, this study includes ten common variables as control variables: gender, hukou, education level, age, marital status, log of personal annual income, log of provincial GDP per capita, urban–rural per capita disposable income ratio, individual physical health, and survey year. In addition, considering the potential impact of family background and migration experience on youth mental health, this study specifically includes two control variables: migration experience and socioeconomic status before age 15. Since the migration experience variable has a small number of missing values in the CGSS data, we applied multiple imputation to address the missing data, thereby preserving the sample size and ensuring the validity of the analysis. Regarding migration experience, existing literature indicates that China’s migrant population exhibits a higher prevalence of depressive symptoms than the national adult average45. And social exclusion, including lack of labor rights, social stigmatization, and discrimination, can harm the mental health of migrants46,47. Regarding family background, low family socioeconomic status is closely associated with mental health problems such as depression and anxiety among adolescents. This association is mediated by pathways including reduced psychological resilience and hope, increased loneliness, heightened school refusal tendencies, and experiences of discrimination48,49. First, this study uses an OLS regression model to examine the effect of social inequality on youth mental health. It divides the sample by gender, hukou, and system position, and applies OLS regression models to analyze the group heterogeneity in the relationship between social inequality and youth mental health.

Table 5 presents the relationship between social inequality and mental health among youth within and outside the system. Model 1 shows that for youth within the system, there is a significant positive association between social inequality and mental health. After controlling for other factors, a one-unit increase in the Theil Index is associated with an average increase of 0.46 in mental health (p = 0.029). Model 2 shows that among youth outside the system, no significant statistical association is found between social inequality and mental health (b = 0.04, p = 0.508). These findings differ from the original hypothesis. Although the positive association between social inequality and mental health among youth within the system aligns with our expectation, while no significant effect is observed among youth outside the system.

This result can be explained by System Justification Theory50. System Justification Theory suggests that people in general, whether they benefit from or are harmed by the current system, tend to uphold it. Research has found that disadvantaged groups sometimes show even greater support for the status quo than advantaged groups do51–53. This mindset helps disadvantaged individuals avoid the penalties that come from challenging the system. It also brings them psychological benefits, such as greater satisfaction and happiness, while reducing negative emotions like anger and anxiety54,55. Youth outside the system, who operate in a highly competitive and uncertain environment, tend to reduce cognitive dissonance by accepting and rationalizing existing inequalities. More specifically, they are likely to attribute their personal development to factors such as industry cycles, company decisions, or individual skills, rather than to macro-level structural inequality, which is difficult to change. This cognitive strategy helps reduce negative emotions and anxiety. Therefore, there is no significant association between macro-level social inequality and the mental health of youth outside the system.

Robustness checks

To ensure the reliability of the above findings, this study conducted two robustness checks. First, given that the dependent variable can also be treated as an ordinal variable, we employed an ordered logistic regression model to test whether the results are sensitive to model specification. Second, the mental health of respondents within the same province may exhibit within‑group correlation, which violates the assumption of independent and identically distributed errors. To mitigate this potential spatial dependence, we estimated the models using cluster‑robust standard errors clustered at the province level. Detailed results of both checks are presented in Supplementary Tables 1–8.

Our robustness checks confirm that, under both the ordered logit model and cluster-robust standard errors, the overall sample continues to show an inverted U-shaped relationship between social inequality and mental health. In the subgroup analyses, the inverted U-shaped relationship remains significant among male youth but not among female youth; it is significant among youth with non-agricultural hukou but not among those with agricultural hukou; and social inequality is positively associated with mental health among youth within the system while showing no significant association among youth outside the system. These findings are consistent with those reported earlier, indicating that the main results of this study are not sensitive to model specification or standard error estimation and are therefore robust.

Conclusions and discussion

This study uses data from the CGSS 2021 and 2023 to examine the impact of social inequality on the mental health of Chinese youth and its group heterogeneity. The main findings are as follows.

First, there is an inverted U-shaped relationship between social inequality and the mental health of youth. This result indicates that the tunnel effect and the relative deprivation effect coexist and compete dynamically. Youth mental health reflects the shifting balance between these two psychological forces. When the level of social inequality is relatively low, the tunnel effect plays a dominant role over the relative deprivation effect, and individuals tend to interpret income growth among others as a signal that their own future income may also improve. However, when social inequality rises to a higher level and surpasses a critical threshold, relative deprivation and status anxiety become stronger, which weakens positive expectations and leads to a decline in mental health31.

Second, the impact of social inequality on the mental health of youth is not uniform but shows clear group heterogeneity. From the gender perspective, the sense of achievement among male youth is more closely associated with occupational status and economic income. As a result, they are more likely to interpret macro-level income inequality as a signal directly related to their own development prospects and are more sensitive to changes in overall social inequality. Consequently, the association between social inequality and the mental health of male youth is significant. In contrast, female youth derive their sense of well-being from more diverse sources, which reduces their sensitivity to perceptions of macro-level social inequality. As a result, no significant statistical association has been found between social inequality and the mental health of female youth. From the hukou perspective, youth living in highly stratified urban contexts engage more frequently in social comparison and are therefore more sensitive to changes in macro-level social inequality. Consequently, the association between social inequality and their mental health is significant. However, youth with agricultural hukou live in a social environment with relatively low social stratification. They mainly compare themselves with local residents who have similar backgrounds. This “parallel comparison” pattern reduces the psychological impact of broader social inequality. Therefore, no significant statistical association has been found between social inequality and the mental health of rural youth. From the system perspective, due to the “institutional shelter” effect, increased social inequality is positively associated with the mental health of youth within the system. In contrast, there is no significant statistical association between social inequality and their mental health. One possible explanation is the system justification effect, meaning that they tend to accept and rationalize existing inequality to reduce cognitive dissonance.

The conclusions of this research have several implications. First, this research integrates the tunnel effect and the relative deprivation effect into a unified framework. The findings show that these two psychological mechanisms operate at the same time and form a dynamic process of competition. Furthermore, this study reveals that the operation of these two effects exhibits group heterogeneity. For male youth and urban youth, no significant association has been observed between social inequality and mental health. In contrast, for female youth and youth with agricultural hukou, no significant association between social inequality and mental health is observed. This suggests that future research should shift from asking “which effect is correct” to asking “which effect is activated for specific groups and under particular conditions”. In addition, the findings show that excessively high social inequality eventually damages the mental health of youth. This means that governments should adopt policies that promote equal opportunities and reduce barriers to social mobility, which can protect youth mental health and contribute to sustainable social development. Finally, for the groups whose mental health is not significantly affected by social inequality, such as youth outside the system, their situation should not be overlooked simply because the statistical results are not significant. Instead, it is important to recognize that they may rely on internal psychological adjustment to cope with external risks. Therefore, it is necessary to improve the social security system and reduce the uncertainty they face in life and development. This can lower their need to rely on system justification as a psychological defense and help protect their mental health at a fundamental level.

Finally, several limitations of this study should be noted. First, because this study uses cross-sectional data, the limitations of the data structure make it difficult to address endogeneity issues such as omitted variable bias and reverse causality through methods like fixed-effects models or the inclusion of lagged terms. As a result, the findings can only speak to statistical correlations between the variables rather than deterministic causal relationships. Future research could attempt to obtain panel data and adopt more rigorous causal identification methods to further extend this line of inquiry. Second, due to the questionnaire design of the CGSS database, the survey includes only a single self-rated mental health item and does not contain detailed depression or anxiety scales. Therefore, we were obliged to use this single indicator as the operational measure of mental health. Future research may draw on data that include professional mental health scales to further improve the measurement of mental health indicators.
