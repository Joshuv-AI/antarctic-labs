On the S S -version of some special elements in commutative rings
D. Bennis, A. Bouziri, S. D. Kumar, T. Singh

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce and study the S S -versions of several fundamental elements in commutative rings. Specifically, for a commutative ring R R with identity and a multiplicative subset S S , we define and investigate the notions of S S -invertible, S S -idempotent, S S -von Neumann regular, and S S - π \pi -regular elements. We establish their basic properties, interrelations, and structural inclusions, and use them to characterize classes of rings. Special attention is given to the uniform S S -counterparts of Boolean and π \pi -regular rings, where we provide examples distinguishing these from their classical analogues. Several transfer results under homomorphisms and direct product constructions are established, and connections with existing S S -counterparts (uniformly S S -von Neumann regular, uniformly S S -Artinian, etc.) are highlighted. Throughout the paper, we point out several open problems, offering directions for further research.

Introduction

Throughout this paper,

The generalization of classical notions using multiplicative subsets has attracted significant attention from researchers in homological algebra and ring theory in recent years. This approach was initiated by Anderson and Dumitrescu in 2002 when they introduced the concept of

-Noetherian rings, defined as rings in which every ideal

[

]
,

-coherent rings and modules

]

Although many classical results have

[
7

It is also worth noting that recent attempts to study
S
-Noetherian and

[
4

5
,

In this paper, we aim to introduce and study the
S
-version of certain special elements in commutative rings. Specifically, we investigate the

The paper is organized into two sections. In the first section, we introduce and study

Recall from
[
8
]
that an element
a\in R
is called
S
-idempotent
if there exists
s\in S
such that
sa=a^{2}
. In this section, we introduce and investigate the
S
-versions of invertible, von Neumann regular, and
\pi

Definition 2.1
.

Let
S
be a multiplicative subset of a commutative ring
R
. Then,

1.

An element
a\in R
is said to be
S
-invertible
if
ab\in S
for some
b\in R
; that is, there exist
b\in R
and
s\in S
such that
ab=s
.

2.

An element
a\in R
is said to be
S
-von Neumann regular
if there exist
b\in R
and
s\in S
such that
sa=a^{2}b
.

3.

An element
a\in R
is said to be
S
-
\pi
-regular
if there exist
b\in R
,
s\in S
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq 1
, such that
sa^{n}=a^{2n}b
.

We denote by
\mathrm{S\text{-}u}(R)
(respectively,
\mathrm{S\text{-}idem}(R)
,
\mathrm{S\text{-}vnr}(R)
,
\mathrm{S\text{-}\pi\text{-}reg}(R)
) the set of all
S
-invertible (respectively,
S
-idempotent,
S
-von Neumann regular,
S
-
\pi
-regular) elements of

.

For each

, we denote by
s\text{-}\mathrm{u}(R)
(respectively,
s\text{-}{\rm idem}(R)
,
s\text{-}{\rm vnr}(R)
,
s\text{-}\mathrm{\pi\text{-}reg}(R)
) the set of all elements of
R
that are
S
-invertible (respectively,
S
-idempotent,
S
-von Neumann regular,
S
-
\pi
-regular) with respect to

.

Remarks 2.2
.

The following assertions are straightforward to verify.

1.

Any element
s\in S
is
S
-invertible, since
s=s\cdot 1
.

2.

Any invertible (resp., idempotent, von Neumann regular,
\pi
-regular) element of

is
S
-invertible (resp.,
S
-idempotent,
S
-von Neumann regular,
S
-
\pi
-regular). The converses, however, do not hold in general. Moreover, the
\mathrm{u}(R)
-invertible (resp.,
\mathrm{u}(R)
-von Neumann regular,
\mathrm{u}(R)
-
\pi
-regular) elements are precisely the invertible (resp., von Neumann regular,
\pi
-regular) elements of
R
.

3.

A
\mathrm{u}(R)
-idempotent element need not be idempotent. For example, let
R
be a von Neumann regular ring that is not Boolean. Then there exists
a\in R
which is not idempotent. By
[
1
, Theorem 2.2]
, there exists an invertible element
u\in R
such that

. Hence,
a
is a
\mathrm{u}(R)
-idempotent element that is not idempotent.

In fact, it follows from
[
1

that the
\mathrm{u}(R)
-idempotent elements are exactly the von Neumann regular elements of

.

4.

Every
S
-invertible element of

is
S
-von Neumann regular. Indeed, if
ab=s
for some

sa=a^{2}b
.

5.

Every
S
-idempotent element of

is
S
-von Neumann regular, and every
S
-von Neumann regular element is
S
-
\pi
-regular.

Thus, we have the following inclusions:

S\subseteq\mathrm{S\text{-}u}(R),\quad{\rm idem}(R)\cup S\subseteq\mathrm{S\text{-}idem}(R)\subseteq S\cup\mathrm{Z}(R),

{\rm vnr}(R)\cup\mathrm{S\text{-}u}(R)\cup\mathrm{S\text{-}idem}(R)\subseteq\mathrm{S\text{-}vnr}(R)\subseteq\mathrm{S\text{-}u}(R)\cup\mathrm{Z}(R),

\mathrm{\pi\text{-}reg}(R)\cup\mathrm{S\text{-}vnr}(R)\cup\mathrm{S\text{-}nil}(R)\subseteq\mathrm{S\text{-}\pi\text{-}reg}(R)\subseteq\mathrm{S\text{-}u}(R)\cup\mathrm{Z}(R).

where

\mathrm{S\text{-}nil}(R)=\{r\in R\mid sr^{n}=0\text{ for some }n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\text{ and }s\in S\}.

The following Example

shows that an
S
-invertible element does not necessarily belong to

.

Example 2.3
.

Let

be an integral domain, and let

be a prime element; let

and set

. Then

is an

-invertible element which is not in

.

Recall from
[

]
that an element

is said to be
S
-zero if there exists

.

Let

. An element

For a ring
R
, we denote by
{\rm Max}(R)
the set of all maximal ideals of
R
. The following proposition highlights the connection between the classical notions and their
m
-relative counterparts, where
M
ranges over
{\rm Max}(R)
.

Proposition 2.4
.

The following assertions hold:

1.

\{0\}=\bigcap_{M\in{\rm Max}(R)}M\text{-}{zero}(R)
;

2.

\mathrm{u}(R)=\bigcap_{M\in{\rm Max}(R)}M\text{-}\mathrm{u}(R)
;

3.

{\rm nil}(R)=\bigcap_{M\in{\rm Max}(R)}M\text{-}{\rm nil}(R)
;

4.

{\rm idem}(R)\subseteq\bigcap_{M\in{\rm Max}(R)}M\text{-}{\rm idem}(R)\subseteq{\rm vnr}(R)
;

5.

{\rm vnr}(R)=\bigcap_{M\in{\rm Max}(R)}M\text{-}{\rm vnr}(R)
.

Proof.

(1) Let
a\in\bigcap_{M\in{\rm Max}(R)}M\text{-}{zero}(R)
. For each maximal ideal
M
, there exists an element
t_{M}\notin M
such that
t_{M}a=0
. Since the ideal generated by the family
\{t_{M}:M\in{\rm Max}(R)\}
is equal to
R
, there exist elements
r_{1},\dots,r_{n}\in R
and maximal ideals
M_{1},\dots,M_{n}\in{\rm Max}(R)
such that

1=r_{1}t_{M_{1}}+\cdots+r_{n}t_{M_{n}}.

It follows that

a=r_{1}t_{M_{1}}a+\cdots+r_{n}t_{M_{n}}a=0.

(2) Let
a\in\bigcap_{M\in{\rm Max}(R)}M\text{-}\mathrm{u}(R)
. For each maximal ideal
M
, there exist
t_{M}\notin M
and
x_{M}\in R
such that
ax_{M}=t_{M}
. As in (1), the family
\{t_{M}:M\in{\rm Max}(R)\}
generate the unit ideal, so

1=r_{1}t_{M_{1}}+\cdots+r_{n}t_{M_{n}}

for some
r_{i}\in R
and
M_{i}\in{\rm Max}(R)
. Hence,

1=r_{1}ax_{M_{1}}+\cdots+r_{n}ax_{M_{n}}=a(r_{1}x_{M_{1}}+\cdots+r_{n}x_{M_{n}}),

which shows that
a\in\mathrm{u}(R)
.

(3) Let
a\in\bigcap_{M\in{\rm Max}(R)}M\text{-}{\rm nil}(R)
. For each maximal ideal
M
, there exists
t_{M}\notin M
and
p_{M}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
t_{M}a^{p_{M}}=0
. Since the family
\{t_{M}:M\in{\rm Max}(R)\}
generate the unit ideal, there exist
r_{1},\dots,r_{n}\in R
and
M_{1},\dots,M_{n}\in{\rm Max}(R)
such that

1=r_{1}t_{M_{1}}+\cdots+r_{n}t_{M_{n}}.

Set
N=p_{M_{1}}+\cdots+p_{M_{n}}
. Then,

a^{N}=a^{N}(r_{1}t_{M_{1}}+\cdots+r_{n}t_{M_{n}})=\sum r_{i}a^{N}t_{M_{i}}=0,

since each
t_{M_{i}}a^{p_{M_{i}}}=0
, and
a^{N}=a^{q}a^{p_{M_{i}}}
for some
q\geq 0
. Hence
a\in{\rm nil}(R)
.

(4) The first inclusion is clear. Let
a\in\bigcap_{M\in{\rm Max}(R)}M\text{-}{\rm idem}(R)
. Then for each
M
, there exists
t_{m}\notin M
such that
t_{M}a=a^{2}
. As above, we write

1=r_{1}t_{M_{1}}+\cdots+r_{n}t_{M_{n}}.

Then,

a=r_{1}t_{M_{1}}a+\cdots+r_{n}t_{M_{n}}a=r_{1}a^{2}+\cdots+r_{n}a^{2}=(r_{1}+\cdots+r_{n})a^{2},

so
a\in{\rm vnr}(R)
. If
a
is regular, then

1=(r_{1}+\cdots+r_{n})a.

(5) This follows by using a similar argument as in the previous statements.

∎

Proposition 2.5
.

The following assertions are equivalent:

1.

R
is an
S
-field.

2.

There exists
s\in S
such that every element of

is either
S
-invertible or
S
-zero with respect to

.

Proof.

1.\Rightarrow 2.
Assume that
(0)
is
S
-maximal with respect to some
s\in S
. Let
a\in R
be a non-
S
-zero element. Since
(0)\subseteq Ra
, and
(0)

S

. But

S
-zero, so

. Hence,

2.\Rightarrow 1.
Let

We define a subset
A\subseteq R
to be
S

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring, and let
S
be a multiplicative subset of
R
.

1.

If
a,b\in R

.

2.

If

S

.

3.

Let

) for some

, and

(resp.,

4.

The subsets

,
\mathrm{S\text{-}idem}(R)
,
S\text{-}\mathrm{vnr}(R)
, and

5.

The intersection
\mathrm{S\text{-}vnr}(R)\cap\mathrm{S\text{-}nil}(R)
is

\mathrm{S\text{-}idem}(R)\cap\mathrm{S\text{-}nil}(R)

6.

Let
s,t\in S
. If the set
\{s^{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is finite, then the set

is uniformly

-torsion, and so is
s\text{-}{\rm idem}(R)\cap t\text{-}{\rm nil}(R)

Proof.

1. Let
s\in S
and

such that
(ab)c=t
, where

. Thus,

, which implies that

is

.

2. If
se=e^{2}
, then:

(s-e)^{2}=s^{2}-2se+e^{2}=s(s-e).

Thus,
s-e
is also
S
-idempotent.

3. Let
a\in R

4. Let

6. This follows similarly to (3), using the finiteness of the set

Theorem 2.7

Let
R

1.

a\in\mathrm{S\text{-}vnr}(R)
.

2.

There exist
s\in S
and

such that
sa=a^{2}u
.

3.

There exist

,

, and

.

There exist

and

and

.

There exist
s\in S

sab=0
and

.

Proof.

1.\Rightarrow 2.
Let
s\in S
and
x\in R
such that
sa=xa^{2}
. Let

. By proposition
2.6
, we have

and

Assume that

for some

and

. Let

and

such that

. Set

, so we have

, and

3.\Rightarrow 4.

Assume (3) holds, i.e.,
sa=ue
for some
u\in\mathrm{S\text{-}u}(R)
and
e\in\mathrm{S\text{-}idem}(R)
. Let

and
t\in S

, and let

. Then, for

since

Thus,

4.\Rightarrow 5.

This is clear.

5.\Rightarrow 1.
Assume

and
sab=0
for some
b\in R
,
s\in S
. Let
v\in R
and

. Then,

Thus,

so

∎

Recall from

Theorem 2.8
.

Let

be a commutative ring and let

-von Neumann regular element. Then, there exist

and

and

. Moreover, if

. In particular, if

is a regular element, then

, and hence

Proof.

Let
a\in R
be an
S
-von Neumann regular element. Then there exist
b\in R
and
s\in S
such that

. Set

. We compute:

xa^{2}=ab^{2}a^{2}=a(ba)^{2}=(ba)^{2}a=s^{2}a,\quad\text{so }s^{2}a=xa^{2},

and

ax^{2}=a(ab^{2})^{2}=a^{2}b^{4}=s^{2}b^{2}=s^{2}x,\quad\text{so }s^{2}x=ax^{2}.

Thus, such an

exists.

Now, suppose

and
s^{2}y=ay^{2}
. Then:

s^{4}y=s^{2}ay^{2}=xa^{2}y^{2}=x(a^{2}y)y=s^{2}xya=ax^{2}ya=(a^{2}y)x^{2}=s^{2}ax^{2}=s^{4}x.

Hence,

. In particular, if

is a regular element, then cancellation implies

, and the uniqueness of

Following Zhang
[
17

, a ring

is said to be
uniformly
S
-von Neumann regular
if there exists an element
s\in S
such that every element of
R
is
S
-von Neumann regular with respect to
s
. Moreover, by
[
17
, Proposition 3.10]
, one has that
R_{S}
is von Neumann regular if and only if every element of
R
is
S
-von Neumann regular.

Similarly, at first glance, it may seem natural to consider the class of rings in which every element is
S
-
\pi
-regular. However, this situation occurs precisely when the localization
R_{S}
is
\pi
-regular. Indeed, if every element
a\in R
is
S
-
\pi
-regular, then by definition there exist
s\in S
,
b\in R
, and

such that

sa^{n}=a^{2n}b
. Then, for any
t\in S
, we have

\Bigl(\tfrac{a}{t}\Bigr)^{n}=\tfrac{sa^{n}}{st^{n}}=\tfrac{a^{2n}b}{st^{n}}=\Bigl(\tfrac{a}{t}\Bigr)^{2n}\tfrac{t^{n}b}{s},

which shows that
\tfrac{a}{t}
is
\pi
-regular in
R_{S}
. Conversely, suppose that
R_{S}
is
\pi
-regular. Then for each
a\in R
there exist
n\geq 1
and
\tfrac{b}{t}\in R_{S}
such that

\Bigl(\tfrac{a}{1}\Bigr)^{n}=\Bigl(\tfrac{a}{1}\Bigr)^{2n}\tfrac{b}{t}.

Clearing denominators, we obtain
sa^{n}=a^{2n}b^{\prime}
, for some
s\in S
and

. Hence

is
S
-
\pi
-regular.

Thus, the property that "every element is
S
-
\pi
-regular" does not provide anything essentially new. In contrast, the situation is different for

-idempotent elements, since, as shown in Example

, the fact that every element of

is Boolean.

were uniformly
S
-Boolean, then it must be with respect to
s
, since
R
is not Boolean.
But

s(1+\overline{x})=s+s\overline{x}=(1+\overline{x})^{2}=1+\overline{x}^{2}=1+s\overline{x},

which implies
1=s
, a contradiction.
Thus
R
is not uniformly
S
-Boolean as desired. Furthermore, observe that
R_{S}=R
is not a Boolean ring. This shows that the condition "every element of
R
is
S
-idempotent" does not necessarily imply that the localization
R_{S}
is Boolean, in agreement with the remark preceding Definitions
3.1
.

For the other counterexamples, we will use the following result.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
R
be the direct product of a family of commutative rings
\{R_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and let
S
be a multiplicative subset of
R
.
Then

1.

R
is (uniformly)
S
-Boolean if and only if, for each
i\in I
,
R_{i}
is (uniformly)
S_{i}
-Boolean.

2.

R
is uniformly
S
-von Neumann regular if and only if, for each
i\in I
,
R_{i}
is uniformly
S_{i}
-von Neumann regular.

3.

If
I
is finite, then
R
is uniformly
S
-
\pi
-regular if and only if, for each
i\in I
,
R_{i}
is uniformly
S_{i}
-
\pi
-regular.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Proposition
2.20
.
∎

We now give an example of a uniformly
S
-
\pi
-regular ring which is not
\pi
-regular.

Example 3.7
.

Let
R^{\prime}
be a
\pi
-regular ring and let
R^{\prime\prime}
be a ring which is not
\pi
-regular.
Set
R=R^{\prime}\times R^{\prime\prime}
and
S=S^{\prime}\times\{0\}
, where
S^{\prime}
is any multiplicative subset of
R^{\prime}
.
Then
R
is uniformly
S
-
\pi
-regular with respect to
(1,0)
, but it is not
\pi
-regular.

Finally, we give an example of a uniformly
S
-
\pi
-regular ring which is not uniformly
S
-von Neumann regular. Notice that a ring
R
is von Neumann regular (resp.,
\pi
-regular) if and only if it is uniformly
\mathrm{u}(R)
-von Neumann regular (resp., uniformly
\mathrm{u}(R)
-
\pi
-regular), where
\mathrm{u}(R)
denotes the group of units of
R
.

Example 3.8
.

Let
R^{\prime}
be a
\pi
-regular ring and let
R^{\prime\prime}
be a
\pi
-regular ring which is not von Neumann regular.
Set
R=R^{\prime}\times R^{\prime\prime}
and
S=S^{\prime}\times\mathrm{u}(R^{\prime\prime})
, where
S^{\prime}
is any multiplicative subset of
R^{\prime}
.
Then
R
is uniformly
S
-
\pi
-regular with respect to
(1,1)
, but it is not uniformly
S
-von Neumann regular.

Recall that a ring
R
is called
S
-Artinian if every descending sequence
\{I_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of ideals of
R
is
S
-stationary. This means there exist a positive integer
k
and an element
s\in S
such that for all
n\geq k
,
sI_{k}\subseteq I_{n}

[
15
]
.

A ring
R
is said to be uniformly
S
-Artinian if the element
s
in the definition above can be chosen independently of the descending sequence; that is, there exists
s\in S
such that every descending sequence
\{I_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of ideals of
R
is
S
-stationary with respect to
s

[
22
]
.

Proposition 3.9
.

The following statements hold true.

1.

If
R
is
S
-Artinian, then every element of
R
is
S
-
\pi
-regular; equivalently,
R_{S}
is a
\pi
-regular ring.

2.

Any uniformly
S
-Artinian ring is uniformly
S
-
\pi
-regular.

Proof.

1. Assume that
R
is
S
-Artinian. Let
r\in R
and consider the descending chain of ideals

Rr\supseteq Rr^{2}\supseteq Rr^{3}\supseteq\cdots.

By the
S
-Artinian property, there exist a positive integer
k
and an element
s\in S
such that for all
n\geq k
,
sRr^{k}\subseteq Rr^{n}
. In particular,
sRr^{k}\subseteq Rr^{2k}
, which shows that
r
is
S
-
\pi
-regular. The equivalence with
R_{S}
being
\pi
-regular follows from the discussion preceding Definitions
3.1
.

2. This follows by a similar argument.
∎

Of course, a uniformly
S
-
\pi
-regular ring need not be uniformly
S
-Artinian; one can construct a counterexample using arguments similar to those in Examples
3.7
and
3.8
.

Recall from
[
1
, Theorem 2.6]
that a commutative ring
R
is Boolean if and only if every element of
R
is either idempotent or nilpotent; that is,
R={\rm idem}(R)\cup{\rm nil}(R)
. Replacing Boolean with
S
-Boolean, we have:

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
S
be a multiplicative subset of
R
containing no units except
1
. Then
R=\mathrm{S\text{-}idem}(R)\,\cup\,{\rm nil}(R)
if and only if
R
is an
S
-Boolean ring.

Proof.

Assume that
R=\mathrm{S\text{-}idem}(R)\cup{\rm nil}(R)
. Then
U(R)\subseteq\mathrm{S\text{-}idem}(R)
. Let
a\in U(R)
. Then
a^{2}=sa
for some
s\in S
, which implies
a=s
, so
U(R)=\{1\}
. Consequently,
{\rm nil}(R)=\{0\}
since
U(R)+{\rm nil}(R)=U(R)
. Therefore,
R=\mathrm{S\text{-}idem}(R)
, and hence
R
is an
S
-Boolean ring. The converse is immediate.
∎

It is not clear whether the condition on
S
in the Proposition
3.10
can be removed, nor whether
{\rm nil}(R)
can be replaced with
\mathrm{S\text{-}nil}(R)
; that is, if
R=\mathrm{S\text{-}idem}(R)\cup\mathrm{S\text{-}nil}(R)
, is
R
necessarily an
S
-Boolean ring? This question remains an open problem.

Another result from
[
1
, Theorem 2.7]
states that for a commutative ring
R
with at least one nonzero zero-divisor, it suffices for
R
to be Boolean that every zero-divisor is idempotent. Currently, we are unable to prove an
S
-version of this result; however, we can state the following partial result:

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
s\in S
. Assume that
Z(R)
contains a nonzero element
x\neq s
. If
\mathrm{Z}(R)\subseteq s\text{-}{\rm idem}(R)
, then
s^{2}y\in S
for every
y\in R\setminus Z(R)
. Consequently,
R
is uniformly
S
-von Neumann regular.

Proof.

Let
x\in Z(R)\setminus\{0\}
. Since
x\in s\text{-}{\rm idem}(R)
, we have
x^{2}=sx
.
By Proposition
2.6
, we also have
s(s-x)=(s-x)^{2}
.

Now let
y\in R\setminus Z(R)
. Then both
xy
and
(s-x)y
belong to
Z(R)^{*}\subseteq s\text{-}{\rm idem}(R)
, so

sxy=(xy)^{2}=sxy^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad s(s-x)y=((s-x)y)^{2}=s(s-x)y^{2}.

Since
y
is regular, it follows that

sx=sxy\quad\text{and}\quad s(s-x)=s(s-x)y.

Adding these equalities, we obtain

s^{2}=sx+s(s-x)=sxy+s(s-x)y=(sx+s(s-x))y=s^{2}y.

Hence, for any
y\in R\setminus Z(R)
, we have
s^{2}y=s^{2}y^{2}
. Therefore,
R
is uniformly
S
-von Neumann regular with respect to
s^{2}
.
∎

It is well known that, over a commutative Boolean ring, or more generally over a von Neumann regular ring, every primary ideal is maximal (and hence prime). In Theorem
3.12
, we extend this result to the
S
-setting.

Recall from
[
11
, Definition 2.1]
that a proper ideal
P
of a ring
R
, disjoint from
S
, is said to be
S
-primary
if there exists
s\in S
such that for all
a,b\in R
, whenever
ab\in P
, either
sa\in P
or
sb\in{\rm rad}(P)
, where
{\rm rad}(P)
denotes the radical of
P
.

Also recall that any
S
-maximal ideal is
S
-prime
[
16
, Proposition 10]
.

Theorem 3.12
.

Let
R
be a (uniformly)
S
-Boolean ring and let
P
be an
S
-primary ideal of
R
. Then
P
is an
S
-maximal ideal of
R
, and hence
S
-prime.

Proof.

Suppose
P
is
S
-primary with respect to some
t\in S
. Let
Q
be an ideal such that
P\subseteq Q
and
Q\cap S=\emptyset
. We claim that
tQ\subseteq P
.

Take
x\in Q
. Since
R
is
S
-Boolean, there exists
s\in S
such that
x^{2}=sx
.
Hence,
(s-x)x=0\in P
. By the
S
-primaryness of
P
, either
tx\in P
or
(t(s-x))^{n}\in P
for some
n\geq 1
.

Assume that
(t(s-x))^{n}\in P
. Then, since
P\subseteq Q
, we also have
(t(s-x))^{n}\in Q
.
But note that
(t(s-x))^{n}=t^{n}s^{n}+rx
for some
r\in R
. As
x\in Q
, it follows that
t^{n}s^{n}=(t(s-x))^{n}-rx\in Q
. But
t^{n}s^{n}\in S
, so
t^{n}s^{n}\in Q\cap S
, contradicting the assumption that
Q\cap S=\emptyset
. Thus, we must have
tx\in P
.

Therefore,
tQ\subseteq P
, which shows that
P
is
S
-maximal.
∎

By following similar arguments as in the proof of Theorem
3.12
, one can show the same result under the weaker assumption that
R
is uniformly
S
-von Neumann regular (or even just that every element of
R
is
S
-von Neumann regular).

We conclude with the following result, which can be seen as an
S
-version of primary decomposition over Boolean rings.

Theorem 3.13
.

Assume that
R
is uniformly
S
-Boolean with respect to some
s\in S
with
s^{2}=s
.
If
I
is an ideal of
R
disjoint from
S
, then

s\cdot\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\supseteq I\\
M\in{\rm Max}_{S}(R)\end{subarray}}M\;\subseteq\;I\;\subseteq\;\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\supseteq I\\
M\in{\rm Max}_{S}(R)\end{subarray}}M,

where
{\rm Max}_{S}(R)
denotes the set of all
S
-maximal ideals of
R
.

Proof.

Set

J=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\supseteq I\\
M\in{\rm Max}_{S}(R)\end{subarray}}M.

Clearly,
I\subseteq J
. We claim that
sJ\subseteq I
. Suppose, for contradiction, that
sJ\nsubseteq I
. Then there exists
x\in J
with
sx\notin I
. Consider the family of ideals

\mathcal{F}=\{\,K\subseteq R\mid I\subseteq K,\;sx\notin K,\;K\cap S=\emptyset\,\}.

Clearly,
\mathcal{F}\neq\emptyset
since
I\in\mathcal{F}
. Moreover, every chain in
\mathcal{F}
has an upper bound in
\mathcal{F}
(the union). By Zorn’s lemma,
\mathcal{F}
has a maximal element, say
M
.

We now show that
M
is an
S
-prime ideal. Let
a,b\in R
with
ab\in M
. If neither
sa\in M
nor
sb\in M
, then by maximality of
M
in
\mathcal{F}
we must have

sx\in M+Rsa\quad\text{and}\quad sx\in M+Rsb,

so

sx=m+r_{1}sa\quad\text{and}\quad sx=m^{\prime}+r_{2}sb

for some
m,m^{\prime}\in M
and
r_{1},r_{2}\in R
. Hence
sx^{2}\in M
. Since
R
is uniformly
S
-Boolean, we have either
x^{2}=sx
or
x^{2}=x
. In both cases, it follows that
sx\in M
, contradicting the fact that
M\in\mathcal{F}
. Therefore, either
sa\in M
or
sb\in M
, and thus
M
is
S
-prime with respect to
s
.

By Theorem
3.12
, in a uniformly
S
-Boolean ring every
S
-prime ideal is
S
-maximal. Hence
M
is
S
-maximal. But then
sx\in J\subseteq M
, which contradicts
sx\notin M
.

Therefore,
sJ\subseteq I
, completing the proof.
∎
