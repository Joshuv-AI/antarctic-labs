Executive Summary
This work shows that two strategies—(1) improved forecasting of price-responsive electric
vehicle (EV) charging and (2) flattening EV charging price-response over similarly priced
times—can greatly increase the percentage of personal light-duty vehicles that can participate in
time-of-use or real-time price electric vehicle managed charging (EVMC) and by so doing
contribute to bulk power system cost reductions. Furthermore, the outcomes for price-responsive
EVMC can be similar to or exceed those of direct load control (DLC) up to high participation
levels, especially when charging is scheduled within single sessions using coarse price signals
(time-of-use rates).

Light-duty vehicle EVMC, whether price-responsive or DLC, can reduce power system costs by
better aligning EV charging with locations and times of low energy cost or infrastructure use.
Coordinating charging across thousands to millions of electric vehicles while preserving mobility
service is a barrier to realizing the value of EVMC. Price-responsive dispatch mechanisms like
time-of-use rates and real-time prices can be attractive compared to DLC because they only
require one-way communications and can schedule individual vehicle charging with full
knowledge of both flexibility and price. However, increasing participation in price-responsive
mechanisms is known to induce costly electricity consumption spikes when many EVs respond
to the same price signal at the same time.

We quantify the ability of improved EVMC forecasting to sustain savings from price-responsive
mechanisms beyond the limits observed in previous work of 4% to 14% of light-duty vehicles
participating (Hale et al. 2022a). Perfect forecasting of price-responsive EV load makes time-of-
use and real-time price bulk power system production cost savings similar to those of DLC with
up to 27% to 45% or more light-duty vehicles participating, where the lower bound is observed
when EV charging is scheduled over an entire week, and the upper bound corresponds to
scheduling EV charging within individual sessions (Figure ES-1). Additionally, flattening price-
response to time-of-use rates enables production cost savings with light-duty vehicle time-of-use
participation up to 45% for both within-week and within-session scheduling, and this could be
value-competitive with DLC over that full range if the incremental costs of DLC are more than
$10–30/vehicle-yr. This increase in effective price-responsive EVMC represents an additional 3
to 5 percentage points of production cost savings achievable without DLC.

These results are based on simulations of an envisioned New England power system in which
84% of annual within-system generation is sourced from wind, solar, biomass, hydropower, and
nuclear generators. Although we would expect the quantitative results to vary from system to
system and with changes to the EV modeling methods, we expect the qualitative results to stand,
that is, with accurate forecasting and response flattening, time-of-use EVMC can be value-
competitive with DLC managed charging approaches with active participation levels
approaching or around up to half of the light-duty vehicle fleet.

1 Introduction
Electrifying transportation increases the share of energy service demand that can be supplied by
low-carbon electricity (Mai et al. 2018; Murphy et al. 2021; Jenkins et al. 2021; Williams et al.
2021; Browning et al. 2023). The cost of supplying electric vehicle (EV) charging can be reduced
if EV charging occurs in locations and at times of high variable generation or low electricity
infrastructure utilization (Muratori and Mai 2020; Borlaug et al. 2021; Borlaug et al. 2020;
Anwar et al. 2022). We refer to mechanisms for aligning EV charging with these low-cost times
as EV managed charging (EVMC). Electric light-duty vehicles (LDVs) are especially well suited
for EVMC because most are parked at least 95% of the time (Wood et al. 2023).

Realizing the benefits of EVMC entails coordinating the charging of thousands to millions of
vehicles to minimize charging costs while ensuring EV drivers can complete all desired trips. The
numerous mechanisms for coordinating EV charging described in the literature generally fall into
two high-level categories, which we refer to and compare as direct load control (DLC) and price-
responsive dispatch. DLC directly schedules vehicle charging to achieve a desired aggregate
response. For example, DLC can coordinate EV charging with availability of bulk power system
supply-side resources, or to flatten peak electricity load. Because this process minimally involves
two-way communication between vehicles or chargers and grid operators, DLC is often
facilitated by a third party responsible for presenting a megawatt-scale aggregate of individual
vehicle charging flexibility to the power system operator. The third-party “aggregator” then takes
responsibility for fulfilling the resulting dispatch requests by scheduling individual vehicle
charging. In contrast, price-responsive dispatch mechanisms can use one-way communication of
time-varying prices to individual vehicles, chargers, or drivers. Price-responsive mechanisms rely
on individual behavioral or automated responses to communicated prices to coordinate charging
demand. For example, electricity tariff designs like time-of-use (TOU) rates and hourly real-time
prices (RTP) offered to electric utility customers are price-responsive dispatch mechanisms that
incentivize EV charging during lower-price hours.

The primary contribution of this report is to show how price-responsive mechanisms with one-
way communication and lower implementation costs than DLC can overcome documented issues
with electricity demand spikes resulting from simultaneous price-response. Overcoming these
issues makes price-responsive mechanisms preferable to DLC at higher shares of LDVs
participating in EVMC. Two strategies are particularly effective in integrating double-digit
percentage shares of LDVs in price-responsive EVMC: (1) simultaneous responses to price
minima/maxima are forecast and anticipated in bulk power system dispatch and (2) individual
vehicle charging is flattened over the same-priced hours when EVs are plugged in. These
findings differ from recent work (The AES Corporation and Camus 2024; EnergyHub, Inc.
2024) suggesting more DLC-like active management will be necessary in the coming decade for
managing EV charging to avoid costly simultaneous response to prices. This report softens that
prescription by further exploring an earlier finding that DLC and price-responsive mechanisms
both have advantages and disadvantages (Hale et al. 2022a).

Scenarios developed in this work directly compare our formulation of DLC to price-responsive
mechanisms in a realistic bulk power system model. There are coordination mechanisms in the
literature that blend DLC and price-responsive approaches by sending individualized price

 1

signals (like price-responsive mechanisms) to achieve a coordinated outcome (like DLC).
However, such methods are mostly iterative and can thus be considered a variant of, or a specific
way to implement, DLC (Xi and Sioshansi 2014; Cutler et al. 2021; Najafi and Fripp 2023).
Noniterative mechanisms that send individualized price signals to consumers or devices can
produce a coordinated shed response (Cole, Morton, and Edgar 2014), but we are unaware of any
such mechanisms able to coordinate energy shifting. Thus, we subsume mechanisms that send
individualized price signals to achieve a coordinated outcome under the broader category of DLC
in this study and proceed comparing price-responsive and DLC mechanisms.

The primary advantage of DLC is that, because it directly coordinates EV charging load with
electricity supply, it is guaranteed to lower overall electricity costs no matter how many vehicles
participate. Its cost reductions can therefore serve as a benchmark for the cost reductions
achieved by other coordination mechanisms at high EVMC participation levels. The
disadvantages of DLC stem from the need to aggregate charging flexibility across many EVs to
facilitate two-way communication between charging controls and grid operation software.
Aggregation is necessary because grid operation software is designed to optimize hundreds to
thousands of megawatt-scale devices. For example, FERC Order 2222 specifies a minimum
aggregation size of 0.1 megawatt (MW) (Federal Energy Regulatory Commission [FERC] 2020),
not thousands to millions of kilowatt-scale devices (i.e., EVs).

Aggregation to present DLC to the bulk power system is challenging because of the requisite
costs of software, communications, and controls, and the complications introduced by
aggregation mathematics. Aggregation mathematics present an inherent challenge we find likely
requires significantly reducing the flexibility of the aggregate resource offered to the bulk power
system (Hale et al. 2022a), disadvantaging DLC. As an example, simply adding up the charging
flexibility of all participating vehicles overstates aggregate flexibility by obscuring vehicle-level
constraints that link the ability to accept charge (or not) with availability of a charger (or not).

Any aggregation process that ignores this loss of information is likely to incorporate a significant
degree of error in fulfilling aggregate load shift requests. Hao et al. (2013) show it is sometimes
possible to bound aggregate flexibility with mathematical models that present either strictly less
or strictly more flexibility than what is actually available. However, that method’s bounds are not
tight enough to be practicable for multihour energy shifting (Hale et al. 2022b). Thus, this study,
similar to Hale et al. (2022a), handles the aggregator’s information loss problem by discounting
aggregate power and energy bounds to ensure aggregate dispatch requests can, with high
probability, be fulfilled with low error.

In summary, DLC’s disadvantages are increased cost of enabling two-way communication,
increased computational complexity, and an inability to represent the full flexibility of individual
EVs in megawatt-scale aggregations.

Price-responsive dispatch mechanisms such as TOU and RTP have two primary advantages—
ability to access an individual EV’s full scheduling flexibility and lower cost to implement than
DLC—because they require only one-way communications and localized controls. Their primary
disadvantage is that large quantities of price-responsive load can induce new and potentially
costly-to-serve load spikes at times forecast to have low prices (Hale et al. 2022a). This issue is
an example of the general problem with price-taking resource dispatch: eventually the quantity of

 2

participating resources is large enough to materially affect prices, which can change the relative
categorization of times as “low-price” or “high-price”. The deficiencies of price-taking price-
responsive dispatch can occur at high levels of participation (not generally seen currently for
EVMC) because price-responsive mechanisms are based on the assumption that the participating
load is not large enough to significantly impact generator dispatch decisions. There is evidence in
the literature that price-responsive mechanisms can provide savings even if they are large enough
to affect generator dispatch decisions and that there is a breaking point beyond which price-
responsive mechanisms increase, rather than decrease, power system costs (Xi and Sioshansi
2014; Muratori and Rizzoni 2016; Mahmud, Ravishankar, and Hossain 2020). This breaking
point can also be thought of as the EVMC participation level at which price-making dispatch
mechanisms (in our modeling, DLC) are preferred to price-taking dispatch mechanisms (TOU
and real-time price, RTP).

This study quantifies how improved forecasting of EVMC price-responsive dispatch increases
the effectiveness and performance of TOU and RTP relative to DLC at higher participation rates.
Our previous work (Hale et al. 2022a) pessimistically fixed the day-ahead unit commitment
decisions used to estimate production costs with TOU and RTP EVMC based on the unmanaged
EV charging profile, an assumption we refer to in this study as a “no-foresight forecast.” The no-
foresight forecast resulted in TOU and RTP EVMC increasing production costs at participation
rates beyond 2% to 14% of LDVs. However, this assumption is unrealistically pessimistic,
especially for TOU rates that are in place for years—the responses to those rates are easily
observed and incorporated into day-ahead load forecasts. In this study, we therefore bound the
possible effectiveness of price-responsive dispatch mechanisms by also simulating day-ahead unit
commitment decisions using EV charging profiles that incorporate the effects of optimally
scheduling all participating vehicles’ charging based on TOU or RTP, which we refer to as the
perfect-foresight forecast.

We evaluate up to 45% of LDVs (100% of modeled EVs) participating in EVMC with both price-
responsive (i.e., TOU, RTP) and DLC dispatch mechanisms in a realistic, low-carbon bulk power
system simulated using a commercial production cost model, PLEXOS (Energy Exemplar).
Previous research has often focused on coordinating flexible loads on smaller or synthetic
systems (Cutler et al. 2021; Dall’Anese et al. 2018), modeled aggregate EVMC without ensuring
that aggregate dispatch requests could be fulfilled by individual participating vehicles (Pertl et al.
2019; Szinai et al. 2020), or scheduled EV charging only within a day (Liu et al. 2012; Wood et al.
2017; Wood et al. 2023). In contrast, this study leverages an envisioned future New England
power system extracted from the Interconnections Seam Study (National Renewable Energy
Laboratory n.d.) production cost model for 2038 (Bloom et al. 2022); aggregates electric LDV
charging flexibility starting from realistic, heterogeneous, vehicle-level charging profiles (Yip et
al. 2023); scales aggregate resources to ensure aggregate dispatch feasibility (Hale et al. 2022a);
and allows EV charging to be scheduled across an entire week while fulfilling all scheduled
driving trips. Although the model is specifically calibrated for an envisioned Independent System
Operator of New England (ISO-NE) system, methods and results are generalizable to other bulk
power systems integrating increasing shares of EVMC. By combining these improvements over
current practice with no-foresight and perfect-foresight forecast bracketing of TOU and RTP
EVMC performance, this study provides a realistic accounting of how well price-responsive
EVMC dispatch mechanisms compare to DLC across a wide range of participation rates in an
envisioned future power system with high renewable energy shares.

2 Methods
This study adjusts and extends the workflow of Hale et al. (2022a) to estimate the value of
perfect-foresight forecasts of price-responsive EVMC, compared to both no-foresight forecasts
of price-responsive EVMC and a low-error DLC formulation, in a production cost model.
Sections 2.1 to 2.4 present methods developed in the previous study as needed to understand this
study’s results. The production cost modeling implementation, which differs in some ways from
that of Hale et al. (2022a), is described in Section 2.5. Section 2.6 presents our scenario
framework. Additional methodological information is available in Hale et al. (2022a) and in
Appendix A and Appendix B.

2.1 Study Setting
We examine EVMC simulated in a production cost model of an envisioned ISO-NE 2038 bulk
power system derived from the Interconnections Seam Study (SEAMS) (Bloom et al. 2022). We
chose ISO-NE as our study region because it is a well-defined, moderately sized system that is
relatively easy to isolate from the rest of the Eastern Interconnection and Quebec by fixing
hourly imports and exports. In the 2038 scenario selected from SEAMS, 84% of ISO-NE within-
system generation is sourced from wind, solar, biomass, hydropower, and nuclear generators. As
in Bloom et al. (2022) and Hale et al. (2022a), production cost modeling is nodal and uses the
commercial PLEXOS software (Energy Exemplar n.d.).

We add electric LDV load for 2038 based on the “All LDV Sales EV by 2035” scenario described
by Yip et al. (2023), which captures current county-level heterogeneity in EV ownership and
adoption and then transitions county-level LDV EV sales shares to 100% by 2035 at rates
aligned with state and federal policy goals. As described by Yip et al. (2023), the TEMPOTM
model creates realistic sample vehicle mobility demand data by modeling household vehicle
adoption considering county-specific demographics, vehicle registrations, and weather and
assigning weekly mobility demand profiles and sample weights that are also consistent with those
variables. For this study, TEMPO also provides a vehicle-level weekly EV charging time series
consistent with the weekly mobility demand profiles using different charging strategies. The
unmanaged charging scenario in this study uses TEMPO’s Immediate charging strategy, which
assumes that vehicles plug in and charge after every trip until they are fully charged or until
another trip begins.

Table 1 summarizes the vehicle-level TEMPO data used for this study. Because all modeled EVs
make up 45% of projected New England LDVs, in what follows EVMC participation rates of 5%,
30%, 60%, and 100% correspond to participating EVs being 2%, 14%, 27%, and 45% of LDVs,
respectively.

 2.2 Individual Electric Vehicle Flexibility Models
We represent the ability to flexibly schedule EV charging for inflexible vehicle trips by pairing a
deferred charging strategy with the Immediate charging strategy for the same vehicle. The
deferred strategy is the most delayed charging profile that still enables all trips, whereas the
Immediate strategy is, by definition, the earliest possible charging. Figure 1 shows state of charge
(SOC) profiles for one vehicle-week labeled Immediate, Delayed, and Min Charges & Delayed.
Pairing the Immediate profile with the Delayed profile defines “within-session” flexibility, which
assumes the number of charging sessions and the amount of energy delivered in each charging
session stay the same, but energy delivery can be scheduled across the entire vehicle dwell
period. Pairing the Immediate profile with the Min Charges & Delayed strategy allows more
flexible “within-week” charging deferral. This study assumes ubiquitous charger access for all
strategies, that dwelling vehicles can charge at the power level of the last charger used in the
Immediate strategy, and that all charging strategies for all sample vehicles start and end each
week with full batteries.

 2.4 Price-Responsive Dispatch Mechanisms
We compare two price-responsive dispatch mechanisms, RTP and TOU, to our previously
described DLC mechanism. In both price-responsive mechanisms, we schedule individual EV
charging to minimize charging costs subject to Eqs. 2.3–2.5, 2.9, 2.10, and 2.12 for each
vehicle. 2 The only difference between the two price-responsive mechanisms is their hourly price
profiles. The PLEXOS day-ahead model simulated using the unmanaged EV charging profile
supplies the hourly RTP profile. We calculate TOU rates to minimize the mean absolute
difference between the resulting TOU price schedule and the hourly RTP subject to structural
constraints on number of seasons and blocks, minimum number of days in a season, and
minimum number of hours in a block. In this study, we use the TOU-1-2 rate described in Hale

Because we only use one TOU rate in this study, in what follows, we refer to the TOU-1-2 rate

Because individual vehicles (or drivers or chargers) achieve RTP- and TOU-based EVMC by
scheduling charging to communicated prices, RTP and TOU can access individual vehicles’ full
flexibility. Price-responsive mechanisms’ ability to access each individual vehicle’s full
flexibility is an advantage compared to scaled forms of aggregate DLC. However, many vehicles
receiving the same prices are incentivized to act synchronously. This can result in large load
spikes as vehicles respond to RTP minimums, or large jumps or drops in EV charging at the
times when TOU rates change. This study mitigates TOU charging spikes by flattening EV
price-response with a $1/(MW/h) ramp penalty. The ramp penalty creates a preference, for
example, for an EV needing p kWh to charge at p/4 kW in 4 equally priced hours (for a total
ramp penalty of $0.0005p) rather than p in the first hour and 0 in the following 3 hours (for a
total ramp penalty of $0.002p). Although not widely implemented today, such a management
strategy could be implemented by local controllers aware of vehicles’ planned departure times
and desired SOCs, in addition to the TOU rate schedule. Our results show that this flattening of
EVMC response to TOU rates is a primary way price-responsive mechanisms can work at higher
shares of LDVs participating in EVMC.

 2.5 Production Cost Modeling Implementation
This study uses production cost modeling in up to three nested scheduling stages in the PLEXOS
software to simulate the impact of different EVMC participation, flexibility, and dispatch
mechanism scenarios on bulk power system operating costs. Stages 1 and 2 mimic two-
settlement wholesale electricity market formulations common in North America, including in
ISO-NE. Stage 1 is a day-ahead unit commitment and economic dispatch simulation that fixes
most unit commitments for Stage 2 based on nodal transmission constraints and fixed load
forecasts. Stage 2 is a real-time simulation that accepts Stage 1 commitment and dispatch
decisions as input and then responds to actual load conditions by changing the commitment
status of fast-starting combustion turbines and adjusting committed generators’ dispatch levels.
All scenarios use Stage 1 and Stage 2 simulations, with different Stage 1 results feeding into
Stage 2 based on the EVMC dispatch mechanism and forecast type. Only low-error DLC (DLC-
LE) scenarios start with Stage 0, which performs regional, day-ahead unit commitment and
economic dispatch including the aggregate, megawatt-scale DLC-LE flexibility resources
described in Section 2.3. This fixes the DLC-LE EV load profiles for the subsequent Stage 1 and
Stage 2 simulations, thereby managing the computational challenge of optimizing EV charging
load with generator commitment decisions before adding in nodal transmission constraints. 4 The
coarser transmission representation used in Stage 0 both reduces computational time and

response in Stages 1 and 2.

The price-responsive dispatch mechanism simulations (RTP and TOU) also partition EV load
into participating (flexible) and nonparticipating (fixed) portions. Fixed EV load is aggregated by

charging costs based on the flexibility constraints Eqs. 2.3–2.5, 2.9, 2.10, and 2.12, and their

penalty described previously to flatten the aggregate response. Although we apply price-response
flattening via ramp penalty for both RTP and TOU, it has a greater impact on the TOU results
because TOU prices are constant for multihour periods.

We simulate two forecast types for the price-responsive dispatch mechanisms: no-foresight and
perfect-foresight, by using different Stage 1 results as input into Stage 2. Stage 2 always uses the
actual price-responsive EV load simulated for the specified mechanism (RTP or TOU), flexibility
type (within-session or within-week), and participation level. No-foresight uses the Stage 1
simulation with unmanaged EV load to establish initial commitment and scheduling decisions for
Stage 2. This is a worst-case forecast, in which the day-ahead unit commitment does not
anticipate how EVs will respond to RTP or TOU prices. Perfect-foresight uses the same EV load
profiles as Stage 2 in Stage 1. Thus, Stage 2 is initialized with commitment decisions and
dispatch levels that already anticipate the exact shape of EV load—a best-case forecast.

3 Results
The results provide a detailed picture of the pros and cons of RTP, TOU, and DLC as EVMC
dispatch mechanisms at different levels of participation (from 2.5% to 45% of LDVs) and with
different levels of charge scheduling flexibility (within-session and within-week). We examine
the value of the mechanisms with perfect-foresight in Section 3.1, examine the value of perfect-
foresight compared to no-foresight, i.e., forecast value, in Section 3.2, describe implementation
challenges associated with RTP in Section 3.3, and consider the value of price-responsive
mechanisms compared to DLC, given the additional complexity and costs associated with
implementing DLC, in Section 3.4. A full table of results is available in Appendix C.

3.1 Production Cost Savings of Price-Responsive Mechanisms
Perfect-foresight provides production cost savings at higher participation rates than the
same load integrated with no-foresight. Even with perfect-foresight, there is a maximum
participation rate beyond which price-responsive charging provides low or negative
savings.
Perfect-foresight of price-responsive EV load increases the participation rates at which price-
responsive mechanisms produce production cost savings comparable to or better than those
achieved by DLC. As in our previous study (Hale et al. 2022a), in this study (Table A.2) we find
negative or low savings (less than 35% of DLC’s per-vehicle value) for 14% of LDVs
scheduling within-session or within-week charging on an RTP or TOU rate and integrated into
grid operations with no-foresight (from −$96/vehicle-yr for within-week RTP to $12/vehicle-yr
for within-session TOU compared to $31/vehicle-yr for within-session DLC and $60/vehicle-yr
for within-week DLC). Incorporating perfect-foresight of price-responsive EV load changes this
result. Figure 2 shows price-responsive mechanisms can yield positive production cost savings
for all studied participation rates (up to 45% of LDVs) when scheduling within-session
flexibility. For within-week flexibility, 14% to 27% of LDVs responding to RTP or TOU rates
provide per-vehicle production cost savings similar to those achieved by DLC (Figure 2).

Although foresight greatly increases the relative savings of price-responsive dispatch, there is
still a maximum amount of participation beyond which more coordinated DLC mechanisms are
necessary to achieve production cost savings. In our simulations, perfect-foresight of RTP or
TOU response is insufficient to obtain significant savings from within-week EVMC with 45% of
LDVs responding. Although our study did not include enough electric vehicles to reach a similar
limit for within-session flexibility, the trajectory of RTP value between 27% and 45% of vehicles
participating suggests that such a limit does exist (Figure 2). Furthermore, the study assumption
of ubiquitous charging, by distributing charging across many sessions (e.g., 31 charging sessions
in 1 week [Figure 1]), within which charging is less flexible, on average, than if fewer, longer-
dwell charging sessions are chosen, might also lead to overestimates of the ability of price-
responsive mechanisms to deliver value with high within-session EVMC participation rates.

3.4 Price-Responsive Mechanisms Value-Competitive with DLC,
 Especially Within-Session
With perfect forecasts, price-responsive mechanisms are value-competitive with DLC at
participation rates of at least 45% of LDVs for within-session flexibility, and up to 27% of
LDVs for within-week flexibility. Although TOU never maximizes production cost
savings, it might provide the most value over a wide range of participation rates after
accounting for implementation costs.
Improved forecasting increases the EVMC participation levels at which price-responsive dispatch
mechanisms are competitive with DLC. Table 4 shows DLC is the highest-value mechanism
only at high participation rates, specifically 45% LDV participation for within-session flexibility
and 27% LDV participation and above for within-week flexibility. However, price-responsive
mechanisms can still be value-competitive with DLC, even when they yield lower savings. DLC
requires frequent two-way communication and coordination, which implies higher enablement
costs. For DLC to be the preferred mechanism, its additional savings must exceed its additional
costs when compared to alternatives. To estimate how much value could be extracted from the
higher costs of DLC, it is instructive to quantify differences between DLC savings and preferred
price-responsive mechanism savings. For within-session flexibility at 45% LDV participation
and within-week flexibility at 27% LDV participation, the TOU mechanism, although not
preferred overall, is the preferred price-responsive mechanism and only reduces savings
compared to DLC-LE by $3/veh-yr or 13% (DLC $23 vs. TOU $20/veh-yr) and $12/veh-yr or
27% (DLC $45 vs. TOU $33/veh-yr), respectively (Table 4). This suggests that incremental
enablement costs for within-session DLC compared to a well-designed TOU tariff must be tens
of dollars per vehicle-year or less to be value-competitive with TOU rates. A similar result holds
for within-week DLC, but over a narrower band of participation levels, as only DLC is able to
effectively schedule the within-week charging flexibility of 45% of the LDV fleet to achieve
overall production cost savings of nearly 10% (Figure 5).

A TOU rate can also be value-competitive with RTP. TOU rates are coarser and updated much
less frequently than RTPs, which means communications, controls, and response forecasting can
be simpler, less costly to implement, and less sensitive to communication disruptions. And
although RTP is the highest-value mechanism at low participation rates, as participation rates
increase, the flattened TOU responses implemented in our study become preferred. TOU
(perfect-foresight) outperforms RTP (perfect-foresight) in total and incremental savings value for
27%–45% LDV participation for within-week flexibility and at 45% LDV participation for
within-session flexibility (Figure 5). And although RTP responses can greatly increase maximum
system-level ramp rates, TOU reduces maximum system-level ramp rates compared to the
unmanaged scenario in all but one scenario (Table A.2, also see Figure 4 for the 27% LDV
within-week scenario).

This suggests TOU responses are less likely than RTP responses to stress power system
flexibility, especially at moderate to high participation rates and assuming local controls are
implemented to use more of the dwell period than the minimum necessary to reach desired states
of charge.

4 Discussion and Conclusions
Improved forecasting of price-responsive EVMC increases bulk power system production cost
savings and makes price-responsive EVMC on RTP or TOU rates preferable to DLC at higher
participation and flexibility levels. On the envisioned ISO-NE bulk power system, perfect-
foresight RTP is preferred to DLC with up to 27% of LDVs participating in within-session
managed charging, and perfect-foresight price-responsive strategies are value-competitive up to
at least 27% and 45% participation for within-week and within-session EVMC scheduling,
respectively. Improved forecasting provides more savings as the amount of EVMC flexibility and
the time granularity of prices increases. Even with improved forecasting, value can be contingent
on the rest of the power system: for example, with 27% of LDVs scheduling charging across the
week in response to an RTP forecast, savings can be realized only if generators provide additional
operational flexibility to serve increased load ramp rates.

We demonstrate that price-responsive dispatch of increasing shares of EVMC can be better
achieved with a combination of two strategies: (1) flattening the response through managed TOU
and (2) accurate price-response forecasting. Price-responsive managed TOU dispatch could be
preferable to more complicated DLC approaches if operational flexibility is difficult to procure,
if bulk power systems do not provide multiday schedules and pricing, or if costs of DLC
implementation exceed additional savings compared to managed TOU responses. We find that
the additional costs of the most valuable mechanisms, which are within-week flexibility
exercised via RTP at low participation rates and via DLC at high participation rates, must be less
than tens of dollars per vehicle-year, and in some cases can only be a few dollars per vehicle-
year, to outperform simpler mechanisms like flattened (managed) TOU and within-session
flexibility. Although many North American utilities are still piloting EVMC, Baltimore Gas and
Electric’s recently expanded EVMC program forecasts costs of around $50/vehicle-year for
incremental participation in 2026–2027, excluding customer participation incentives for a DLC-
like implementation with 20,000–30,000 participating vehicles (Michel 2024). This suggests a
range of costs of tens of dollars per vehicle-year may be policy-relevant as EVMC programs are
scaled up beyond the pilot level.

Whether price-responsive EVMC can achieve savings similar to those seen in this study under
perfect-foresight is contingent on how well real-life forecasting approximates perfect-foresight.
Contemporary power system operator experience and forecast quality suggest high-quality
forecasting is feasible. Best-practice load forecasting and machine learning methods exhibit
around 1% normalized mean average error in hour-ahead and 2%–4% normalized mean average
error in day-ahead load forecasts (Bryce et al. 2023). EV loads are still small in North American
jurisdictions compared to future EV adoption projections, but if similar forecasting errors can be
achieved for price-responsive EV loads, implemented results should be closer to perfect-
foresight than to no-foresight. Also, although the quantitative savings per vehicle-year results of
this study are particular to the system studied and all of our modeling assumptions, we would
expect our high-level findings to hold qualitatively for all bulk power systems. That is, well-
forecast TOU responses based on individual vehicles charging at power levels moderated to use
more of the dwell period than minimum-time charging can provide significant savings up to
moderate shares of the LDV fleet. The additional value achievable by more complex mechanisms
like RTP and DLC is likely insufficient to merit the additional cost and complexity in many but

 18

not all circumstances on bulk power systems, and applying DLC to a portion of the EV fleet is
required at sufficiently high managed charging participation rates.

This study made several key assumptions that could be relaxed in future work. Setting the
baseline charging profile assuming ubiquitous charging with vehicles immediately plugging in
after every trip results in an unrealistically high number of plug-ins per vehicle and per week
(Smart and Salisbury 2015). This also likely enhances the contrast seen in this study between
within-session and within-week charging flexibility. On the other side of the ledger, assuming
charging can be managed any time the vehicle is parked overstates the within-week flexibility
resource, which is also impacted by the assumption that all vehicles start and end the week with a
full SOC.

In focusing on bulk power system LDV EVMC production cost savings, this work does not
estimate other potential EVMC savings or EVMC enablement costs. Regarding savings, we do
not investigate EVMC’s potential to displace long-term power generation investments or
EVMC’s ability to reduce distribution system costs, both of which can provide significant
additional savings highly dependent on system particulars but likely to be in the range of tens to
hundreds of dollars per vehicle-year (Anwar et al. 2022). Reduced distribution system costs are
cited in industry studies as a major reason for preferring DLC-like active management of EV
charging at lower participation shares (The AES Corporation and Camus 2024; EnergyHub, Inc.
2024). We also do not include load or managed charging for medium- and heavy-duty EVs.
Regarding costs, we do not estimate the absolute or relative costs of enabling EVMC through
TOU, RTP, or DLC mechanisms.

This study also analyzes different dispatch mechanisms in isolation, but it could be that,
especially after enablement costs and EV owner preferences are accounted for, a mix of dispatch
mechanisms would provide more value for lower cost and with higher overall participation.
Finally, we chose a specific strategy, applying a $1 per MW/h ramping penalty in the price-
responsive dispatch algorithm to penalize changing charging power across equally priced TOU
periods, to mitigate TOU rebound effects. We heuristically discounted the megawatt-scale power
and energy bounds of aggregated DLC to reduce expected dispatch error rates to below 5%. In
both cases, other mitigation approaches might perform as well or better or be easier to implement
in practice.

Appendix A. Additional Information on Methods
Figure A.1 shows the high-level workflow used in Hale et al. (2022a) and in this study to translate
TEMPO™ county-level vehicle adoption and charging profile results into PLEXOS models and
the results described in the main text. The workflow starts with a TEMPO vehicle adoption
scenario. Once this is fixed, TEMPO runs three charging strategies per sample vehicle:
Immediate, Delayed, and Min Charges & Delayed. The TEMPO charging profiles are in the form
of lists of charging events for single weeks, but the PLEXOS production cost model requires
hourly aggregate profiles for a whole year. The Python package dsgrid-flex performs the
necessary translations via several processing steps. First, lists of charging events are transformed
into hourly profiles, one profile for each sample vehicle and charging strategy. Pairs of these
profiles (Immediate and Delayed for within-session flexibility, Immediate and Min Charges &
Delayed for within-week flexibility) are then used to construct individual flexibility models
(Section 2.2). dsgrid-flex also creates scaled aggregate flexibility models (Section 2.3) and
dispatches individual flexibility models against hourly price profiles to simulate RTP and TOU
response (Section 2.4). The unmanaged Immediate profiles, price-responsive managed profiles,
and aggregate, battery-like, megawatt-scale flexibility models are then translated into a form
ingestible by PLEXOS using another National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) Python package
called beetle. Up to this point, the workflows for Hale et al. (2022a) and this study are identical.

Appendix B. Time-of-Use Rates and Price-Responsive
Dispatch
For the TOU dispatch mechanism modeled in the main text, we construct TOU rates that attempt
to mimic hourly RTP patterns as closely as possible while enforcing a season and block structure.
Seasons, defined as a set of contiguous days, and blocks, defined as a set of contiguous hours,
are chosen because these commonly feature in retail TOU prices with on-peak, mid peak, and/or
off-peak rates that vary by season and times of day. Although our optimization formulation is
general and could be used to construct any number of rates with different numbers of seasons,
blocks, and restrictions on season and block length, in this study we analyze a TOU rate with one
season and two blocks.

The TOU rate we construct is intended to represent only the wholesale energy portion of a typical
retail tariff. Other common retail tariff components other than wholesale energy include
distribution, transmission, and line items to cover specific utility programs; these costs are not
reflected in our rates except to the extent transmission congestion is captured in nodal PLEXOS
simulations. Based on our modeling framework, we derive the wholesale energy portion of rates
from the hourly real-time prices in the day-ahead PLEXOS model with unmanaged EV charging
loads. Seasons are defined as groups of contiguous days that can wrap around the first day of the
year (i.e., December 31 and January 1 can be in the same season). Blocks are defined separately
for weekdays and weekend days, but in both cases, they consist of groups of contiguous hours
that can wrap around at midnight (i.e., 11 p.m.–12 a.m. and 12 a.m.–1 a.m. can be in the same
block). Seasons must persist for a minimum number of days (not applicable to our 1-season TOU
rate); blocks must be at least as long as a minimum number of hours (4 in our TOU rate).

The resulting TOU rate used in this study is in Table A.1. Consistent with average wholesale
energy costs, TOU rates are lower overnight than in the daytime and are lower on the weekend
than on weekdays. For both weekends and weekdays, lower rates start at midnight. The transition
to daytime prices happens at 7 a.m. on weekdays and at 9 a.m. on weekends. Overall, the prices
range from 2.9 cents/kWh overnight on weekends to 5.5 cents/kWh during the day on weekdays.
