A hybrid system enables plasmid copy number control in yeast
Authors: Anni Li, Qingyang Zhao, Zhunyi Yang, Jiesheng Cheng, Dong Xu, Yueyao Zhang, Ting He, Bingzhao Zhuo, Xing Zhao, Xiaolin Rao, Hui Wang, Lizhu Chen, Zhouqing Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Synthetic biology demands plasmid with tunable copy numbers. While dynamic plasmid copy number (PCN) regulation has been engineered in prokaryotes, a parallel capability remains unavailable in eukaryotic systems. Here, we develop a programmable PCN platform for S. cerevisiae based on the endogenous 2μ plasmid. The p2μ-Cir0 system achieves up to 20 copies per cell with enhanced homogeneity and stability, supporting 60-fold higher expression than chromosomal integration. Addition of a CEN element enables switchable PCN transitions from 1 to 38 copies, and Leu2d-based selection further raises copy number to ~70 and expression to ~110-fold. We demonstrate broad utility in tRNA phenotyping, GLP-1 precursor, 2-phenylethanol, β-carotenoid and ergothioneine production, and long-term strain preservation. These results establish a well quantified PCN regulation toolkit for yeast, addressing instability from multiple copies and enabling gene dosage control across DNA, RNA, and protein levels.

Introduction

Saccharomyces cerevisiae (S. cerevisiae) and Escherichia coli (E. coli) are predominant chassis organisms in fundamental research and industrial biotechnology. Plasmids are indispensable in both systems for DNA manipulation, genome engineering, and controlling gene expression levels1–3. While E. coli benefits from a versatile plasmid toolkit with replicons covering a wide copy number range4,5, the options for S. cerevisiae remain limited. Conventional yeast episomal plasmids are primarily of two types6: Yeast Centromere plasmids (YCps), which contain a centromere (CEN) and an autonomously replicating sequence (ARS) and maintain low copy numbers (1–5 copies/cell) with high segregation stability but often provide insufficient gene dosage for high-level expression7,8; and Yeast Episomal plasmids (YEps) harbor a 2μ origin, which replicate at higher copy numbers (~20 copies/cell) but suffer from greater instability during long-term cultivation9. Plasmid copy number (PCN) and stability are thus critical yet under-optimized parameters for advanced yeast engineering10,11. Expanding the repertoire of yeast plasmids with tunable copy numbers and enhanced stability is therefore essential to unlock more complex and rigorous synthetic biology applications4.

Dynamic control of PCN, which is highly compatible with regulatory layers at transcriptional, translational and post-translational stages12,13, overcomes the limitation of canonical plasmids with fixed PCN, constituting a powerful and modular strategy for flexible regulation of protein expression, metabolic engineering and synthetic gene circuits4,14,15. So far, dynamic PCN control has only been achieved by modulating the RNA-p/RNA-i balance for ColE1 plasmid or Rep proteins for pSC101 plasmid, which are both designed for E. coli16,17. By aTc-mediated full induction of RNA-p, the PCN of ColE1 could increase from 1.4 to roughly 50 copies per cell16. A profound understanding of the replication regulation mechanisms of ColE1 and pSC101 plasmids is essential for the success of the aforementioned dynamic regulatory work. Developing dynamic PCN control strategies in microorganisms beyond E. coli, especially in eukaryotic organisms like S. cerevisiae, has become an important direction in synthetic biology tools.

Although the Flp-based PCN amplification mechanism of the endogenous yeast 2μ plasmid was well characterized18–20, no work on dynamic PCN regulation based on such a mechanism has been reported thus far. Most existing efforts have focused on modifying the 2μ derived YEps. For instance, truncating the marker gene promoter on YEps or increasing antibiotic concentrations enables a ~ 20-fold increase in PCN, but careful engineering of promoter length and antibiotic concentrations is needed to achieve the best performance21. However, YEps rely on endogenous 2μ for replication and segregation but compete with it for intracellular resources, leading to instability akin to plasmid incompatibility observed in prokaryotes that will lead to loss of these engineered YEps within as few as 20 generations22,23. Meanwhile, the large-scale industrial use of antibiotics is neither permissible nor cost-effective24. This underscores the absence of an effective, inducible regulatory switch for yeast plasmids comparable to those available in prokaryotic systems4.

To bridge this technological gap, we developed a novel switchable-copy-number plasmid system with enhanced stability and elevated PCN. We first engineer a high-copy-number vector with improved stability based on the endogenous 2μ plasmid of S. cerevisiae. The incorporation of a centromeric (CEN) element then creates a foundation for inducible copy number switching. Further optimization via marker promoter truncation enables the generation of the YTp-L variant, which achieves a higher PCN. Using fluorescence quantified by flow cytometry, we show that our system enables programmable transitions across a wide copy number range (1–109 copies/cell), while exhibiting significantly greater population stability than conventional YEp vectors. As illustrated, we present a switchable genetic tool for S.cerevisiae that integrates high copy number with robust stability, which facilitates strain preservation and fundamental investigations into plasmid maintenance and provides a quantitative means to correlate gene dosage with transcriptional, translational, and metabolic outputs for synthetic biology.

Results

Engineering the p2μ-Cir0 system to enhance PCN and stability

A uniform plasmid background could be conducive to the PCN and stability engineering5. To achieve this, it is necessary to eliminate the endogenous 2μ plasmid, which is rarely lost naturally (loss frequency <0.01% per generation25). Thus, we employed CRISPR-Cas9 to excise the endogenous 2μ origin of replication (ori), generating a 2μ-free host strain (Cir0) (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1). Next, we constructed a shuttle vector, p2μ, by engineering the endogenous 2μ sequence. The engineered plasmid carries elements for propagation in E. coli and a HIS3 selectable marker for yeast (Supplementary Fig. 3). Then, p2μ was transformed into both Cir0 and Cir+ strains to assess its PCN and segregation stability. Interestingly, the transformation efficiency of p2μ was approximately three-fold higher in Cir+ than in Cir0 strains (Supplementary Fig. 4), though absolute efficiency in Cir0 remained robust. We hypothesize that this difference stems from the time required for p2μ to establish its self-encoded replication and segregation machinery (Flp, Rep1, Rep2, and Raf1) in Cir0 cells, whereas these proteins are already present in Cir+ cells27.

Furthermore, we employed mCherry fluorescence to characterize p2μ, in comparison to YCp-type plasmid pRS413 and the YEp-type plasmid pRS423. The distinct color contrast of mCherry under blue light illumination enabled direct visual differentiation of strains harboring various plasmids, with p2μ-Cir0 exhibiting the highest intensity (Fig. 1c). To quantitatively assess PCN, we employed both quantitative PCR (qPCR) and flow cytometric measurement, normalizing all values to a genomically integrated single-copy reference. Our analysis revealed a proportional relationship between the flow cytometry and the qPCR measurements, with a consistent ratio of approximately 3:1 (Fig. 1d). This discrepancy suggests a higher transcriptional output per DNA copy for episomal plasmids compared to the chromosomally integrated reference. Notably, the PCN of the pRS423-mCherry dropped significantly from the reported about 20 to 8.8 (Supplementary Fig. 5a), highlighting the substantial metabolic burden imposed by heterologous gene expression on the PCN. In contrast, our p2μ vector maintained a higher copy number. This was particularly enhanced in the Cir0 strain (~17 copies), likely due to the absence of competition with the endogenous 2μ plasmid.

Flow cytometric analysis of population heterogeneity demonstrated that the p2μ-Cir0 system exhibited a narrower fluorescence distribution (Coefficient of Variation, CV = 15%) than both p2μ-Cir+ (CV = 18%) and the pRS423 control (CV = 23%) (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. 6). To rigorously assess segregational stability, all strains were subjected to serial passaging in non-selective medium, with plasmid retention rates quantified at intervals (Supplementary Fig. 7). Even under initial selection, p2μ-Cir0 and pRS413 exhibited a similar proportion of His+ cells (approximately 80%), while the proportion for p2μ-Cir+ was slightly lower, but pRS423 had a His+ proportion of less than 50% (Fig. 1f). This was consistent with the observation that a population of non-fluorescent cells was detected in the pRS423-mCherry group shown in Fig. 1e, indicating that a substantial number of cells had lost their plasmids even in the selective medium. Notably, this non-fluorescent population was not a contamination of plasmid-free cells (Supplementary Fig. 5b). After 4 rounds of serial transfers, p2μ in Cir0 and Cir+ demonstrated significantly higher retention frequencies of 14% and 10%, respectively, markedly outperforming pRS423, which declined to merely 1.3% (Supplementary Fig. 8). By replacing the HIS3 selection marker with hygromycin B resistant gene, the results in Supplementary Fig. 5c, d also support the superior stability of p2μ-Cir0 system after eliminating the plasmid-free population by high concentration of hygromycin B. These results are consistent with the plasmid stability data determined by flow cytometry.

Collectively, our p2μ plasmid, particularly in the Cir0 background, exhibits a combination of high copy number (~17 copies per cell), enhanced population homogeneity, and improved copy-number maintenance, surpassing conventional YEps. It supports protein expression levels up to 56-fold higher than a single chromosomal integrant, providing a robust genetic tool for S. cerevisiae.

A hybrid plasmid system enabling switchable copy number control

The stability of p2μ-Cir0 provides an ideal foundation for developing dynamic PCN control in yeast. An optimal system should maintain a true single-copy state before induction, then enable robust amplification via a specific molecular switch.

We first questioned whether a dynamic regulatory system could be established using YEps. We engineered a derivative of the YEp pRS425 by integrating a CEN/ARS element to enforce low-copy propagation, as centromeric function dominantly suppresses 2μ-mediated replication28. The transition to multicopy propagation was designed by inactivating the centromere through inducible transcriptional interference mediated by the Pgal1 promoter, a method previously used for aneuploidy construction29. However, this construct (Pgal1-CEN-2μ ori) failed to function as intended. It maintained a basal copy number of ~3 before induction and showed no significant amplification thereafter (Supplementary Fig. 9). We hypothesized that the 2μ system retains partial functionality before induction, leaving the plasmid in a mixed mode of replication and segregation. Furthermore, the endogenous 2μ plasmid likely lacks sufficient ability to fully amplify the copy number of YEps (with the 2μ origin only) upon transcriptional inactivation of the centromere.

To engineer a dynamically switchable system, we therefore turned our focus to the p2μ-Cir0 system. The amplification of the 2μ plasmid is mediated by Flp recombinase through a site-specific recombination mechanism, which is critical for high-copy-number maintenance30. To establish a stringent single-copy control, a CEN sequence flanked by I-SceI recognition sites was inserted into the FLP coding sequence, thereby disrupting the amplification machinery (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Galactose-induced expression of I-SceI (integrated at the HO locus) was designed to excise the CEN cassette, and a functional FLP gene was restored, as verified by PCR (Supplementary Fig. 10b).

Rep1 and Rep2, two transcriptional regulators encoded by and essential for the endogenous 2μ plasmid, form a complex that suppresses Flp31. We speculated that basal expression of Rep1 and Rep2 during the single-copy state strongly represses FLP transcription, thereby delaying plasmid amplification even after an intact FLP sequence has been restored (Supplementary Fig. 10c). In this construct, a Leu2d marker was added to the plasmid backbone of Supplementary Fig. 10a to provide an additional driving force for PCN increase, yet amplification remained markedly delayed, supporting the role of Rep1/Rep2-mediated repression. To overcome this, we replaced the native FLP promoter with the constitutive, medium-strength Pcyc1. This design served three purposes: to minimize transcriptional interference with the centromere before induction; to ensure robust expression of full-length FLP after induction; and to mitigate repression by the Rep1/Rep2 complex. Consequently, we called this system Yeast PCN-switchable plasmid (YTp-C, where C denotes constitutive FLP expression) (Fig. 2a). The YTp-C plasmid was maintained at a single copy in glucose medium and could be induced to ~38 copies per cell by galactose (Fig. 2c, d). The complete kinetics of the copy number changes during the entire induction process are presented in Supplementary Fig. 11. We also tested the effect of promoter strength on system performance (Supplementary Fig. 12). Replacement of the native promoter with either the minimal Pleu2d or the strong constitutive Ptdh3 resulted in suboptimal amplification profiles. To uncouple CEN excision from FLP expression but maintain a high Flp level, we integrated another copy of FLP driven by the inducible Pgals into the genome, which was named the YTp-I system (I denotes inducible expression of FLP) (Fig. 2b). Here, galactose induction simultaneously triggers I-SceI-mediated CEN excision and Flp expression, leading to replication derepression and an increase in PCN to ~22 copies (Fig. 2d). Similarly, we utilized mCherry fluorescence to measure the PCN and population heterogeneity by flow cytometry (Figs. 2d, e). Both YTp-C and YTp-I showed more centralized populations after induction than the conventional YEp pRS423, with PCN ranging from 1 to 105 and 67, respectively (Fig. 2d, e, Supplementary Fig. 13). The induced cultures showed a marked increase in red fluorescence under blue light (Fig. 2e).

We next assessed segregation stability by serial passaging in non-selective medium (Fig. 2f). Initially, YTp-C and YTp-I showed higher retention (84% and 73%, respectively) than pRS423 (45%). Despite higher copy numbers, the engineered YTp-C and YTp-I demonstrated greater stability, especially YTp-C, which retained 60% of its plasmids after four consecutive passages without selection (Fig. 2f). For YTp-C, nearly all cells were fluorescence-positive even after 8 consecutive passages without selection (Supplementary Fig. 14). This stability surpassed that of CEN/ARS plasmids, underscoring the system’s capacity for plasmid maintenance and its potential utility for protein and metabolite production.

The YTp-C and YTp-I systems present the programmable plasmid systems for yeast featuring temporally controlled PCN and superior stability. These switchable systems enable precise transition from single-copy to multicopy state through time-dependent induction.

PCN can be super-elevated by Leu2d-mediated metabolic selection

To further increase PCN, we attempted to leverage extra driving forces. Inspired by reports that truncating selection marker promoters can elevate YEp copy numbers21, we engineered a truncated LEU2 allele (designated Leu2d), shortening its promoter from 405 bp to 29 bp (Fig. 3a). This Leu2d allele was introduced into the basal “Pgal-CEN-2μ ori” system, whose PCN is not switchable (Supplementary Fig. 15). The truncated promoter reduced the expression of Leu2p and thus the efficiency of leucine biosynthesis. Consequently, when cultured in leucine-deficient medium, strains with a low plasmid copy number showed severely impaired growth, while high-copy-number variants proliferated robustly, enforcing plasmid maintenance through metabolic selection (Fig. 3b). This leucine auxotrophy-driven PCN amplification was a multi-generational process, gradually reaching a maximum of ~150 copies/cell (Supplementary Fig. 15d). Notably, even at this high copy number, plasmid maintenance imposed no measurable burden on host growth under selective conditions (Supplementary Fig. 15c). Based on this metabolic selection principle, we engineered two advanced plasmid vectors, YTp-CL and YTp-IL (L is for Leu2d), by incorporating the Leu2d cassette into the YTp-C and YTp-I backbones, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 16). These constructs enable programmable transitions across three distinct replication states (Fig. 3c): (i) a single-copy state maintained by the CEN element; (ii) a low-copy state achieved upon galactose-induced excision of CEN by I-SceI; and (iii) a high-copy state triggered by leucine deprivation. Quantitative analysis confirmed this programmable control (Fig. 3d). Under uninduced conditions, both plasmids maintained single-copy levels (1.3 and 1.0 copies/cell for YTp-CL and YTp-IL, respectively). Leucine deprivation drove amplification to 68 (YTp-CL) and 50 (YTp-IL) copies per cell. Correspondingly, mCherry expression exceeds that of chromosomally integrated controls by 109-fold (YTp-CL) and 101-fold (YTp-IL). Notably, the ratio of fluorescence intensity (flow cytometry) to gene copy number (qPCR) for YTp-CL decreased to approximately 1.6, suggesting a declining marginal contribution of each additional plasmid copy to the total protein output at very high copy numbers. To further illustrate the temporal control of this system, the full kinetics of copy number changes during the induction time course are presented in Supplementary Fig. 17.

On the other hand, the high-copy-number populations exhibited markedly improved homogeneity, with coefficients of variation (CV) of 11% and 12% (Supplementary Fig. 18). Quantitative stability assessment during serial passaging revealed distinct profiles: YTp-IL retention dropped sharply from 81.7–15.3%, whereas YTp-CL declined from 92.2–34.4%, showing relatively better maintenance (Fig. 3f). This reduced stability of both Leu2d-bearing constructs compared to their parental vectors (YTp-C and YTp-I) is likely attributable to the metabolic burden of sustained high-copy replication. Once selective pressure is alleviated, plasmid-free cells gain a pronounced growth advantage32, accelerating plasmid loss (Supplementary Fig. 19). However, both YTp-CL and YTp-IL still maintained significantly higher stability than conventional traditional YEps. Notably, under non-selective, single-copy conditions, the initial retention of YTp-CL and YTp-IL was comparable to that of their parents, confirming that the stability difference emerges specifically under high-copy, selective pressure.

The YTp-CL and YTp-IL systems further elevate the PCN in yeast to nearly 70 and recombinant protein expression levels ~110-fold above genomic integration, providing robust dynamic regulatory tools for protein expression and other applications.

Dynamic expression of RNA and protein

We sought to characterize the potential of our PCN-switchable system for phenotyping multi-copy genes. tRNA genes represent a diverse multicopy gene family, with their promoters encoded by internal tRNA sequences33. This characteristic renders it challenging to modulate specific tRNA levels through conventional promoter engineering34. Thus, we exploited the YTp-CL system to overexpress all valine-coding tRNAs by amplifying their gene dosage. The valine in yeast is decoded by three tRNA genes: tV(CAC), tV(TAC), and tV(AAC), with native genomic copy numbers of 2, 2, and 14, respectively35. As outlined in Fig. 4a, each gene was cloned into the YTp-CL vector and transformed into a Pgal1-I-SceI host. Under uninduced conditions, the PCNs of tRNA-bearing plasmids remained comparable to those in the empty vector control (Supplementary Fig. 20). Following the induction procedure in Fig. 3c, elevated PCN led to increased tRNAVal transcript levels. The PCNs increased by 27-, 37-, and 64-fold for tV(CAC), tV(TAC), and tV(AAC), respectively. The corresponding transcript levels rose by 32-, 10-, and 11-fold compared to the uninduced state (Fig. 4b). This discrepancy between DNA copy number amplification and transcript accumulation suggests potential differences in transcriptional efficiency, post-transcriptional regulation, or cellular tolerance among these tRNA isoacceptors, although they decode the same amino acid. Notably, under nocodazole stress, strains overexpressing different tRNAVal isoforms exhibited divergent phenotypes: the tV(CAC)-overexpressing strain slightly outperformed the vector control, whereas tV(TAC) was severely impaired (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 21). We further extended the application of our system to produce the therapeutically relevant peptide glucagon-like peptide-1 (9–37) [GLP-1(9–37)], a direct precursor to the long-acting antidiabetic drug semaglutide36. To facilitate detection and purification, we constructed a fusion protein consisting of an N-terminal hexahistidine (6 × His) tag, mCherry fluorescent protein, and the target peptide, with these domains separated by an enterokinase-cleavable DDDDK site (Fig. 4d). This expression cassette was introduced into a protease-deficient yeast strain (yps1Δ pep4Δ)37, and the intact peptide product was verified by LC-MS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 22). Induction of the YTp-CL system enhanced both PCN and peptide yield, leading to 22-fold and 42-fold increases, respectively (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 23). To further limit proteolytic degradation, we engineered a host strain by additionally deleting the protease gene PRB138. This modification boosted peptide accumulation to 119-fold (Fig. 4e). Absolute quantification showed that the recombinant peptide constituted 6.8% and 11.2% of the total cellular protein in the respective host strains (Fig. 4f). Following 48 h cultivation in a 5 L fermenter and subsequent nickel-affinity purification, the final target protein concentration was determined to be 373 mg/L by BCA assay. This yield surpasses those reported for previous yeast expression systems, even after normalization to cell mass or culture volume39,40.

Together, this work demonstrates a dual-purpose tunable plasmid copy number system for functional genomics and bioproduction. By combining dynamic gene dosage control with tailored host engineering, the platform effectively addresses potential transcriptional/translational limitations, proving its value for both fundamental genetic studies and applied metabolic engineering.

YTp system enhances the production of diverse metabolites

To demonstrate the broad utility of the YTp platform in synthetic metabolism, we applied it to produce three high-value compounds with distinct biochemical origins: 2-phenylethanol (a rose-scented aromatic alcohol), β-carotene (a tetraterpenoid pigment), and ergothioneine (a sulfur-containing amino acid derivative). 2-Phenylethanol (2-PE) is a valuable rose-scented aroma compound widely used in fragrance and cosmetics; its global market was valued at USD 33.58 billion in 202541. In yeast, 2-PE is biosynthesized from L-phenylalanine via the Ehrlich pathway, a three-step conversion catalyzed by phenylalanine transaminase (encoded by ARO8 and ARO9), phenylpyruvate decarboxylase (ARO10), and alcohol dehydrogenases (such as ADH2-5) (Fig. 5a)42. To engineer 2-PE production, we constructed the YTp-CL vector harboring ARO9 and ARO10 driven by PGK1 and TDH3 promoters. In uninduced conditions without leucine selection, the system maintained a baseline PCN of 0.4 copies/cell, producing 40.2 mg/L 2-PE in shake-flask culture (Fig. 5b). Galactose induction coupled with leucine selection pressure increased the PCN to 27.5 copies/cell (68.75-fold increase), which drove a concomitant rise in 2-PE titer to 840.8 mg/L, a 20.9-fold enhancement over the baseline (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Fig. 24).

Next, we investigated the biosynthesis of the complex tetraterpenoid β-carotene. Codon-optimized variants of yeast CrtE, CrtYB, and CrtI were integrated into the pRS423 vector and transformed into the host strain ANY05143,44. The resulting transformants exhibited pronounced colony color heterogeneity; only a subset showed the yellow pigmentation characteristic of β-carotene accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 25a). This result indicates that conventional YEps are inefficient for maintaining such a metabolically burdensome heterologous pathway. To address this, we expressed the same biosynthetic cassette using the YTp-CL system, generating strain ANY693. This strain showed markedly improved phenotypic homogeneity compared to the pRS423-based control (Supplementary Fig. 25b). In the YTp-CL system, the plasmid maintained a basal copy number of 0.34 prior to induction. Upon induction, the copy number increased 32-fold to 10.7, which drove a 9-fold enhancement in β-carotene titer, reaching 17.1 mg/L (Fig. 5c). This comparatively modest enhancement in both PCN and titer than 2-PE biosynthesis is likely attributable to a greater metabolic burden imposed by the β-carotene biosynthetic pathway on the host cell45.

Further, we engineered the biosynthesis of ergothioneine (EGT), a high-value thiol-histidine derivative with notable antioxidant and cytoprotective activities46. The NcEGT1 and CpEGT2 genes were assembled into the YTp-CL system47, yielding strain ANY694, which produced 9.2 mg/L EGT under uninduced conditions. Upon induction, the PCN increased by 35-fold to 15 copies, yet EGT titers rose only 1.9-fold to 17.5 mg/L (Fig. 5d). This disproportionate response implies that pathway flux is limited primarily by precursor availability, notably L-histidine and S-adenosylmethionine (SAM)48. Combinatorial engineering of precursor supply modules may therefore be necessary to maximize the production capacity of the YTp system.

In contrast to YTp-CL, conventional 2μ origin-based vectors like pRS423 (Supplementary Fig. 26) and engineered high copy plasmid with truncated hygromycin promoter21 (Supplementary Fig. 27) exhibit restricted scalability in both copy number and metabolic output. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the YTp system enables a dynamically tunable strategy to enhance metabolic flux across diverse biochemical pathways through programmable copy number control. The differential yield responses reveal the complex, non-linear coupling between gene dosage, enzyme abundance, and precursor availability among reconstituted pathways. Using the same YTp-CL vector, the dosage‑dependent increase in 2‑PE yield upon PCN induction identifies the copy numbers of ARO9 and ARO10 as critical rate‑limiting nodes. In contrast, the marginal enhancement in EGT yield suggests that EGT production requires additional factors. Together, these findings underscore the platform’s capacity to map the hierarchy of bottlenecks across reconstituted pathways.

Dynamic PCN control preserves storage viability

The continuous metabolic stress or toxicity imposed by high-copy plasmids often triggers cell death upon revival from freeze-thaw cycles, a common instability in high-producing strains49–51. To investigate whether dynamic copy number regulation preserves host fitness, we evaluated the viability of five engineered strains (producing GLP-1, 2-PE, β-carotene, and EGT) under two distinct states: the pre-induction single-copy state and the post-induction high-copy production state (Fig. 6a). Significant differences in survival rates were observed following cryopreservation at −80 °C for 24 h (Fig. 6b). Strains maintained in the single-copy state exhibited exceptionally high viability with negligible fitness loss. In contrast, all high-copy production strains, with the exception of the 2-PE producer, displayed a significant decline in survival. The viability gap between the two states varied depending on the target product, ranging from a 1.1- to 6.6-fold difference. This viability advantage became more pronounced after five consecutive freeze-thaw cycles, where the survival gap between the two states expanded to a range of 1.2- to 20.2-fold (Fig. 6c). Notably, this fitness loss did not always align with maximum specific growth rates (Fig. 6d, μmax). This feature provides a reliable foundation for standardized cell banking and long-term preservation of engineered microbial chassis.

Discussion

The endogenous 2μ plasmid of S. cerevisiae is a natural high-copy genetic element. In this study, we engineered it into the p2μ-Cir0 backbone and its switchable derivatives (YTp-C, YTp-I, YTp-CL, YTp-IL). These systems provide a convenient and powerful platform for bioproduction, enabling over 100-fold enhancements in recombinant protein expression and up to several dozenfold increases in the titers of high-value metabolites. Notably, the protein output per plasmid copy in our systems significantly exceeds that of a single genomic integrant. When combined with the enhanced stability demonstrated by YTp-C, plasmid-based expression offers superior efficiency, convenience, and scalability. Thus, our work establishes a modular platform for high-level gene expression with broad utility, poised to assist in strain preservation and functional phenotyping, improve therapeutic protein production, and boost metabolic yields in industrial settings.

Dynamic control of gene expression is a fundamental challenge in eukaryotic synthetic biology, as current strategies are inherently limited by competing constraints. Inducible promoters (e.g., Pgal1) couple expression control to specific carbon sources that compromise host fitness52; RNA interference provides reversible regulation but lacks genetic stability53; and genomic multi-copy integrations offer stability at the cost of dynamic tunability54. To address these limitations, we present YTp, the first programmable plasmid combining galactose-inducible PCN amplification with the native stability of endogenous 2μ backbone, ensuring robust and homogeneous plasmid maintenance across populations. This system provides a versatile DNA-copy-level tool that is capable of integrating regulatory inputs from transcriptional, translational, and post-translational modification levels.

When different genes or pathways were inserted into the same YTp backbone, they yielded different PCNs (ranging from 10.7–27 copies, Fig. 5) and titers (ranging from 17.1–840 mg/L, Fig. 5). For example, the plasmid YTp-CL has a PCN of 68 in the empty vector state (Fig. 3), but after the introduction of an indicated pathway, its PCN stabilizes at 27.5 for 2-PE and 15.5 for EGT. The observed differences in PCN do not appear to correlate with plasmid size (e.g., plasmids carrying different tRNA genes, or those carrying 2-PE versus EGT, have comparable sizes yet exhibit different PCNs), but perhaps quantified the metabolic tolerance of wild-type cells toward different metabolites. We therefore propose that PCN is more likely constrained by the host cell’s intrinsic biosynthetic capacity: the cell should strike a trade-off between maintaining its own viability and product synthesis.

In this study, galactose-induced formation of the Flp recombinase effectively amplified the copy number of the 2μ plasmid. The two engineered systems, YTp-C and YTp-I, differ solely in Flp levels yet exhibit distinct PCNs and genetic stabilities, indicating that Flp level is a critical determinant for regulating both the PCN and stability of the 2μ plasmid. Notably, the native 2μ plasmid maintains its high copy number and stability not only through Flp-mediated site-specific recombination but also via a highly optimized regulatory network involving Rep1, Rep2, and Raf126. Therefore, systematic engineering of these four proteins holds promise for programmable and precise control over 2μ PCN and stability, thereby introducing a new layer of regulation for synthetic biology chassis platforms based on the 2μ backbone.

While the YTp system was primarily validated in S. cerevisiae, its functionality across divergent genetic backgrounds was exemplified by the successful adaptation of the YTp-CL platform in the W303 strain (Supplementary Fig. 28), demonstrating that its utility is not limited to a specific laboratory lineage. Beyond this intra-specific versatility, the modular nature of our engineering framework is further underscored by the successful implementation of an orthogonal Cre/loxP-based architecture (Supplementary Fig. 29). This expansion not only confirms the generalizability of our design principles but also will enable the simultaneous and independent regulation of multiple genetic modules. The system utilizes the complete replication and regulatory elements of the endogenous 2μ plasmid, which exhibit high functional conservation across the yeast kingdom. This offers a particularly promising solution for non-model yeasts—such as the probiotic Saccharomyces boulardii, the industrial host Pichia pastoris, or the pathogen Candida albicans—which often suffer from inefficient genetic tools, challenging genome editing, and a lack of stable, high-copy expression vectors55–57. Our work offers an alternative strategy for these genetically recalcitrant yeasts: establishing a high-expression heterologous gene platform via straightforward transformation rather than complex genomic integration. Furthermore, with the chromosomal tethering of Rep1-Rep2 complexes58 and the FLP/FRT system’s broad eukaryotic utility59, the 2μ plasmid system shows potential applications across eukaryotes.

Methods

Strains and growth conditions

The yeast strains BY4741 and BY4742, which are derived from S288C, were used as the hosts in this study. Based on them, a series of strains was constructed, which are listed in Supplementary Data 1.

For routine growth, yeast were cultured at 30 °C in YPD medium (20 g/L glucose, 20 g/L peptone, 10 g/L yeast extract). For transformant selection and PCN induction, synthetic complete (SC) medium was used, which was deficient in uracil, methionine, lysine, leucine, histidine, adenine and tryptophan (SC-Ura-Met-Lys-Leu-His-Ade-Trp) with appropriate supplements added as required by specific selection markers. The carbon source in SC medium was adjusted based on experimental needs: 20 g/L glucose for standard cultivation, 20 g/L galactose to induce GAL1- and GALs-promoter-driven expression, and 20 g/L raffinose to prevent the repression of galactose-inducible promoters by glucose. All liquid yeast cultures were incubated at 30 °C with orbital shaking at 220 rpm.

Escherichia coli strains (DH5α and XL-10) used for plasmid construction and propagation were grown in LB medium (20 g/L bacto-peptone, 10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L NaCl) at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. Transformed E. coli was selected using LB medium supplemented with 100 mg/L carbenicillin.

Plasmid assembly strategies

All plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 2. Key plasmids are visualized in Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 16, and their sequences are provided in the Source Data files. Plasmids were constructed using PCR, homologous recombination, and Gibson assembly. The full 2μ sequences were amplified by PCR from regions spanning between the two inverted repeats. Key primers are listed in Supplementary Data 3. All plasmids were verified by Sanger sequencing (Sangon) or Nanopore sequencing (Tsingke).

Construction of Cir0 strains using CRISPR/Cas9

The Cas9 plasmid was constructed based on the pRS416 backbone, with Cas9 expression driven by the GAL10 promoter and terminated by the CYC1 terminator. Two sgRNAs (with N20 sequences 5’-GGAAGACAATGTATGTATTT-3’ and 5’-GAAAATCACGTAATACTTCT-3’) were designed to target the sites flanking the 2μ ori. This plasmid was transformed into BY4741 and BY4742. Single colonies from the transformation plates were first pre-cultured in SC-Ura medium containing raffinose, then induced in SC-Ura medium containing galactose. Cells were subsequently plated onto selective medium (SC-Ura). Single colonies were screened by colony PCR, and those confirmed to have lost the endogenous 2μ plasmid were designated as Cir⁰ strains (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Flow cytometry analysis

Overnight yeast cultures were diluted to an initial OD600 of 0.1 in 3 mL of fresh medium and incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 8 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 1660 × g for 1 min, washed twice with 800 μL of 50 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.5), and finally resuspended in the same buffer to maintain cellular integrity during analysis.

Fluorescence intensity measurement was performed by flow cytometry using a Quanteon cell analyzer (ACEA Bioscience) equipped with an automatic sampler. The mCherry fluorescence was excited with a 561 nm laser and detected using a 610/20 nm bandpass filter. For each sample, ≥10,000 events were recorded; no gating strategy was applied, as fluorescence measurements were performed on the total cell population. Single-cell fluorescence data were acquired using NovoExpress software (Version 1.6.2) and further analyzed in FlowJo (version 10.8.1).

Plasmid copy number was calculated as the ratio of mCherry mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) in engineered strains to that of a reference strain harboring a single chromosomal copy of mCherry (denoted as the “one-copy reference”). This normalization accounts for variations in cellular physiology and instrument settings between experiments. All measurements were performed in biological triplicates.

qPCR assay for quantifying the plasmid copy number

Plasmid copy numbers were quantified by quantitative PCR (qPCR) using the total yeast DNA extracted using the phenol-chloroform method. The yeast total DNA was diluted to 100 μg/mL and used for qPCR analysis. Genomic and reporter plasmid sequences were amplified using primers targeting the genomic ALG9 region (for: 5’-CACGGATAGTGGCTTTGGTGAACAATTAC-3’ and rev: 5’-TATGATTATCTGGCAGCAGGAAAGAACTTGGG-3’), and the plasmid AMP region (for: 5’-TGACTTGGTTGAGTACTCACC-3’ and rev: 5’-TGTCACGCTCGTCGTTTGGTA-3’), respectively. Copy numbers were quantified using the 2–ΔΔCt method.

Plasmid stability experiment

The stability of plasmid expression was evaluated by plate counting. Specifically, the strain was transformed with the corresponding plasmids, a single colony was picked and cultured overnight in selective medium, marking today as day 0. Cells were harvested at the mid-log phase (OD600 0.6 ~ 0.8), 8 h after subculture into fresh medium. Approximately 200 cells were plated onto YPD medium, and single colonies were allowed to grow for 48 h at 30 °C. This plate was then replica-plated onto selective medium plates. The plasmid retention rate at day 0 was calculated as the ratio of colony-forming units (CFUs) on the selective medium to those on the YPD medium. To assess stability over time, the Day 0 liquid culture was subcultured into non-selective YPD medium at an OD600 of 0.1. This culture was then subjected to the same plating, replica-plating, and CFU counting procedure described above. This sequential subculturing and assay process was repeated daily, and plasmid retention rates were determined for Days 1, 2, 3, and 4.

tRNA extraction and quantification

Total RNA was isolated from yeast cells using the TRIzol-based phenol-chloroform method. The cell pellet was washed twice and resuspended in 150 μL of lysis buffer (containing 0.1 M lithium acetate and 0.5% SDS), followed by incubation at 70 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, 450 μL of TRIzol LS reagent was added and vortexed for 15 s, followed by the addition of 150 μL chloroform and inversion mixing for 15 s. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The aqueous phase was collected by centrifugation at 13,500 × g for 30 min, and RNA was precipitated by adding 450 μL of isopropanol. The RNA pellet was collected by centrifugation at 9390 × g for 20 min and washed twice with 75% ethanol. Finally, the RNA was dissolved in 25 μL of RNase-free water and immediately used for cDNA synthesis. RNA concentration and purity were determined by spectrophotometric measurement, with all samples exhibiting A260/A230 ratios > 2 and A260/A280 ratios > 2. cDNA synthesis was performed using a commercial kit (Novoprotein, Suzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To ensure the isolated RNA was DNA-free, about 2 μg of each sample underwent DNase I treatment at 37 °C for 30 min. The DNase reaction was halted by adding 50 mM EDTA and heating the sample at 65 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, RNA was reverse-transcribed using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Novoprotein).

Individual tRNAs were quantified by qPCR using 1 μL of 1:10 diluted cDNA template with gene-specific primers (Supplementary Data 3). Expression values were normalized to the geometric mean of four reference genes (ALG9, TAF10, TFC1, and UBC6)60. Data were derived from two biological replicates, each with three technical replicates.

Extraction, purification and analysis of GLP-1

Each transformant was inoculated at an OD600 of 0.1 into 50 mL of the corresponding medium in a shake flask and grown overnight, and a 5 mL aliquot was used for protein sample preparation. For protein extraction, cultures were centrifuged and washed twice with 1 mL double-distilled water (ddH2O). The pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of 0.2 M NaOH and 100 µL ddH2O, incubated at room temperature for 5 min, then neutralized with 100 µL of 0.2 M HCl. The sample was centrifuged at 9390 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was aspirated, and the pellet was resuspended in Simple Buffer (10 μL per OD600 unit). Then, 100 µL of glass beads were added to each tube, and cells were lysed using a bead beater (30 s ON, 60 s OFF, 6 cycles at 1470 × g). The lysates were boiled at 100 °C for 10 min, centrifuged at 20,600 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was loaded for analysis.

For Western blot analysis, protein samples were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore). The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: Anti-β-Tubulin (Cat# M20005F, Lot 10268577; ABmart) and Anti-His-tag (Cat# 30402ES60, Lot H6227050; Yeasen). After washing, the membrane was incubated at room temperature for 2 h with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies—anti-rabbit for His-tag and anti-mouse for β-tubulin—both diluted 1:10,000 in TBST containing 5% skim milk. Enhanced chemiluminescence signals were detected using Immobilon ECL Ultra Western HRP Substrate (Millipore), followed by chemiluminescent imaging.

To purify target proteins, cells (150–200 OD600 units) were harvested and lysed in PBS using 200 µL of glass beads and 500 µL of lysis buffer, followed by mechanical disruption in a pre-chilled bead beater (6 cycles of 20 s at 5.5 m/s, with 1 min intervals at 4 °C). The supernatant was incubated with pre-equilibrated His-Pur Ni-NTA resin (LABLEAD) at 4 °C for 2 h with gentle agitation. The resin was then washed with wash buffer (PBS with 100 mM imidazole). The target proteins were eluted with 250 mM imidazole. The eluate was pooled, desalted, and concentrated using a 10 kDa MWCO Pierce™ protein concentrator (Millipore) by centrifugation at 10,000 × g. Protein purity and concentration were assessed by SDS-PAGE and the Bradford method, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 23). Subsequently, the target protein sample was sequenced using the timsTOF PRO system (Bruker, Beijing). Raw files were analyzed using PEAKS Studio 13 (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc.). Database searching was performed against a custom database containing the target GLP-1 analogue sequence together with the S. cerevisiae UniProt database and contaminant proteins. Trypsin was used with semi-specific digestion and up to three missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation (Cys) was set as a fixed modification, whereas N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation were set as variable modifications. The precursor and fragment mass tolerances were 20 ppm and 0.05 Da, respectively. Identifications were filtered at 1% peptide and protein FDR, with a minimum peptide length of 6 amino acids and at least one unique peptide required for protein identification. Supplementary Fig. 22 illustrates that the final purified GLP-1 analogue maintained an identical sequence to GLP-1 (EGTFTSDVSSYLEGQAAKEEFIAWLVRGRG).

Extraction and analysis of 2-PE

The induced strains were transferred into liquid medium for overnight culture, then the seed culture was transferred into 250 mL flasks containing 50 mL of medium with an initial OD600 of 0.2. The two-phase shake-flask fermentation was initiated by adding 5 mL of isopropyl myristate. The cultures were incubated in a shaker at 30 °C, 220 rpm for 48 h. After cultivation, the yeast broth was centrifuged, and the supernatant was analyzed for aromatic compound concentrations.

For 2-PE analysis, the organic phase was centrifuged and collected at 4100 × g for 10 min for Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC−MS) analysis. The yield of 2-PE was measured using Agilent MassHunter Acquisition software for GC/MS (Version 12.0). An Agilent Technologies 7820A GC system equipped with an HP-5 column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) was used. The sample (1 μL) was injected in split mode (1:20), and the GC oven temperature program was applied with 3 mL/min nitrogen as carrier gas: 100 °C for 1 min, 10 °C/min to 270 °C, an increase of 30 °C/min to 320 °C, hold for 15 min. The injector temperature was 250 °C. The structure of 2-PE was further analyzed using a GC/MS-QP2010 Plus instrument of Shimadzu (Japan) with a quadrupole mass analyzer (QMA) and a HP-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). The mass scan range was 50 − 800 m/z. A 2-PE standard (GC grade) was purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China) and used for compound identification and quantification. All of the GC analyses were quantified using a six-point calibration curve ranging from 100–2000 mg/L (R2 = 0.9729).

Fermentation, extraction and HPLC quantification of carotene

The yeast strains were cultured in 50 mL of SC-His or SC-Leu medium at 30 °C (220 rpm) for 72 h. A 10% aliquot of the total culture was harvested by centrifugation (4100 × g, 2 min) and washed twice with sterile deionized water. Cell pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of methanol:dimethyl sulfoxide (1:1, v/v) with 100 μL of 0.5 mm glass beads. Mechanical lysis was performed using a Bioprep-24R homogenizer (Hangzhou, Allsheng) with 10 cycles of 45 s shaking followed by 20 s pausing. The lysate was incubated at 55 °C for 10 min with intermittent vortexing and centrifuged at 20,600 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected as the crude β-carotene extract and stored at −20 °C until analysis. β-carotene quantification was performed using an Agilent 1220 HPLC system equipped with a diode-array detector (DAD) and an InfinityLab Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 4 μm particle size; Agilent Technologies). For each sample, 20 μL of extract was injected onto the column, and separation was achieved using an isocratic mobile phase of methanol:acetonitrile:dichloromethane (42:42:16, v/v/v) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min for 25 min. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and absorbance was monitored at 450 nm. β-carotene was identified by comparing retention times with an authentic standard and quantified by integrating peak areas using Agilent OpenLab CDS software (version 2.4).

Fermentation, extraction and HPLC quantification of ergothioneine

Overnight-cultured yeast was transferred into a shake flask containing 50 mL of the corresponding medium. After 48 h of cultivation, an additional 5 mL of 20% glucose was supplemented. Cells were harvested at 24 h post-glucose supplementation. A 1/10 aliquot was collected with 150 μL of 0.5 mm glass beads, and cell disruption was performed (5 cycles of 45 s shaking followed by 20 s pausing). 1 mL of ddH2O was added for thorough dissolution, followed by centrifugation at 13,500 × g for 5 min. Ergothioneine quantification was performed using an Agilent 1220 HPLC system equipped with a diode-array detector (DAD) and an InfinityLab Poroshell 120 Hilic column (3.0 × 100 mm, 2.7 μm particle size; Agilent Technologies). The mobile phase consisted of 5 mM lithium acetate and acetonitrile in a ratio of 15:85. Separation was performed at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min for 25 min. The column temperature was maintained at 30 °C, and absorbance was monitored at 254 nm by using the same machine that analyses carotene. Ergothioneine was identified by comparing retention times with an authentic standard and quantified by integrating peak areas using Agilent OpenLab CDS software (version 2.4).

Evaluation of strain viability via repeated freeze-thaw assays

Strain pre-culture. S. cerevisiae strains were streaked from glycerol stocks onto solid media and incubated at 30 °C for 48 h. Single colonies were inoculated into liquid media and cultured to the stationary phase. Cultures were then diluted to OD600 = 2. For cryopreservation, 200 µL of diluted culture was mixed with 200 µL of 30% (v/v) sterile glycerol in cryotubes.

Freeze–thaw robustness assay. Cryotubes were subjected to repeated freeze–thaw cycles, including storage at −80 °C for at least 24 h, followed by thawing at room temperature for 1 h. After thawing and vortexing, aliquots were collected for viability counting, and the remaining culture was returned to −80 °C for subsequent cycles. Baseline viability (cycle 0) was determined using freshly prepared cryotubes prior to the first freezing step.

Viability measurement and statistical analysis. Thawed cultures were serially diluted in sterile water to an effective OD600 of 0.0002, and 200 µL of each dilution was plated onto solid media. Colonies were counted after forming distinct single colonies at 30 °C for colony-forming unit (CFU) counting. All experiments were performed with 3 independent biological replicates. Survival rates were calculated as CFU/mL relative to the initial time point (Cycle 0). Data are presented as mean ± SD.

Growth curve determination of the strain

The growth curve assay was performed as follows. Each strain was first streaked from the stock culture onto the appropriate solid medium. After single colonies appeared, a single colony was inoculated into liquid medium and cultured to the stationary phase. The culture was then diluted in the same medium to an OD600 of 0.1. Aliquots of 100 µL were transferred to the wells of a 96-well microplate, and cell growth was monitored by measuring OD600 using a BioTek Epoch 2 microplate reader at defined time intervals. All manipulations were performed under sterile conditions. Specific measurement parameters: 30 °C; run time 72 h; measurement interval 30 min; shaking frequency 282 cpm; wavelength: 600 nm. Growth curve data were processed using Gen5 software (version 3.11).

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. All colonies were randomly picked for experiments. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
