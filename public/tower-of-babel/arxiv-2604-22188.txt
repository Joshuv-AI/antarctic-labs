Optimal Investment and Entropy-Regularized Learning Under Stochastic Volatility Models with Portfolio Constraints
Thai Nguyen, Pertiny Nkuize

Abstract
We study the problem of optimal portfolio selection under stochastic volatility within a continuous-time reinforcement learning framework with portfolio constraints. Exploration is modeled through entropy-regularized relaxed controls, where the investor selects probability distributions over admissible portfolio allocations rather than deterministic strategies. Using dynamic programming arguments, we derive the associated entropy-regularized Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation, whose Hamiltonian involves optimization over probability measures supported on a compact control set. We show that the optimal exploratory policy takes the form of a truncated Gaussian distribution characterized by spatial derivatives of the solution of the resulting nonlinear quasilinear parabolic partial differential equation. Under suitable structural conditions on the model coefficients, we prove the existence of classical solutions to this nonlinear HJB equation for the value function. We then establish a verification theorem and analyze the policy-improvement structure induced by the entropy-regularized Hamiltonian, showing how the resulting sequence of PDEs provides a continuous-time interpretation of actor–critic learning dynamics. Finally, our PDE analysis with a semi-closed form of optimal value and optimal policy enables the design of an implementable reinforcement learning algorithm by recasting the optimal problem in a martingale framework.

Introduction

The problem of optimal portfolio selection in continuous time originates from the seminal works of
[
42
,
43
]
, which established expected-utility maximization as a cornerstone of mathematical finance. In this framework, an investor dynamically allocates wealth between a risk-free asset and one or several risky assets to maximize the expected utility of terminal wealth or intertemporal consumption. The associated Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation yields a nonlinear parabolic partial differential (PDE) equation whose explicit solution can be derived under power or logarithmic preferences in complete diffusion markets. The mathematical foundations of this theory were rigorously developed in
[
29
,
59
,
22
,
50
]
and further systematized in
[
30
]
. In these classical formulations, the optimal strategy is characterized by a deterministic feedback control—often referred to as the Merton proportion—under the assumption that model coefficients are perfectly known.

In realistic investment environments, however, portfolio decisions are subject to institutional and regulatory constraints. Short-selling prohibitions, borrowing limits, and leverage caps restrict the admissible set of strategies and fundamentally alter the geometry of the optimization problem. Constrained portfolio optimization has been extensively studied in
[
15
,
16
,
53
,
31
,
14
]
, where convex-analytic and duality techniques were employed to characterize optimal strategies under compact or convex control sets. The presence of constraints often induces boundary solutions and transforms the associated HJB equation into a fully nonlinear variational inequality; see also
[
6
]
. The interaction between state constraints and control compactness plays a central role in the analytical treatment of such problems. Another essential extension concerns stochastic volatility. Empirical evidence of volatility clustering and time-varying uncertainty motivated diffusion-based stochastic volatility models, most notably
[
25
]
. Optimal portfolio selection in stochastic volatility environments has been investigated in
[
33
,
9
,
39
,
32
,
8
]
. In such models, volatility evolves as an additional state variable, possibly correlated with asset returns, and markets typically become incomplete when volatility risk cannot be perfectly hedged. The resulting value function depends jointly on wealth and volatility factors, and the HJB equation becomes multi-dimensional and fully nonlinear. Explicit or semi-explicit solutions are rare and generally rely on specific structural assumptions.

Despite these structural refinements, classical portfolio theory relies heavily on the assumption that drift and volatility parameters are known. In practice, however, estimating expected returns is notoriously difficult. This issue was emphasized early by
[
44
]
and later analyzed empirically in
[
46
]
. Small estimation errors in the drift parameter may generate substantial deviations in optimal portfolio weights, a phenomenon often referred to as the mean-estimation problem. Robust control approaches, pioneered in
[
24

and developed further in
[
40
,
41

[
7

, provide an alternative representation in which ambiguity is embedded directly into the objective functional.

While robust and Bayesian approaches remain model-based, recent developments in reinforcement learning (RL) advocate a model-free paradigm. RL, systematically developed in
[
54
]
, enables an agent to learn optimal behavior through interaction with the environment via exploration and exploitation. Continuous-time perspectives were proposed in
[
19
]
, suggesting a connection between stochastic differential equations and learning dynamics. A growing body of work has incorporated learning mechanisms and data-driven approaches to model financial decision-making and market dynamics. In the context of market microstructure,
[
11

[

[

[

. These contributions, however, are predominantly developed in discrete-time or simulation-based settings.

A major breakthrough in continuous-time reinforcement learning is the entropy-regularized framework introduced in
[

,
56
]
.
The theoretical foundations of continuous time RL were substantially advanced in
[
26
,
27
]
, where martingale methods were used to derive policy evaluation, policy gradient, and actor–critic algorithms in continuous time. These results place reinforcement learning firmly within the framework of stochastic analysis and dynamic programming. In the context of portfolio optimization,
[
17

studied entropy-regularized learning of Merton-type strategies in incomplete markets and identified biased Gaussian exploratory policies. In a similar vein,
[
58
]
recently addressed the continuous-time mean-variance problem in regime-switching markets with unobservable states, utilizing a semi-analytical approach for actor-critic learning. Complementarily,
[
10

[

, while
[

The idea of replacing pointwise controls with measure-valued controls originates in the classical theory of relaxed stochastic control, notably in
[
35
]
and in the monograph
[
22
]
. In that literature, relaxed controls were introduced primarily as a compactification device: enlarging the admissible control set to probability measures restores convexity and compactness properties that may fail in the original formulation. This enlargement is essential for proving the existence of optimal controls and the stability of value functions. The entropy-regularized reinforcement learning formulation builds upon this relaxed-control framework but introduces a structurally significant ingredient: an entropy penalty in the performance criterion. The entropy term discourages overly concentrated control distributions and promotes systematic exploration of the action space. Unlike classical relaxed control, where measure-valued controls are mainly technical tools, the entropy-regularized framework interprets them as intrinsic to the learning mechanism. Exploration thus becomes part of the optimization objective itself, implying important structural consequences. In particular, the associated HJB equation involves optimization over probability measures, and the entropy term induces additional convexity and smoothing effects in the Hamiltonian. When the exploration weight vanishes, the formulation converges to the classical deterministic control problem, thereby establishing a rigorous bridge between reinforcement learning and stochastic optimal control. Related connections between entropy regularization and stochastic control have also been discussed in
[
55
,
51
]
.

Despite the rapid development of entropy-regularized reinforcement learning in continuous time, existing studies typically consider either complete markets, unconstrained investment problems, or simplified volatility structures. The existing works, e.g.
[
26
,
27
,
10
]
, do not investigate but rather assume the existence of solutions for the resulting PDEs, which are generally highly non-linear in nature. Therefore, a rigorous analytical treatment combining stochastic volatility, explicit portfolio constraints, and entropy-regularized exploration within a nonlinear PDE framework remains largely absent from the literature. From a mathematical standpoint, entropy-regularized portfolio optimization leads to a fully nonlinear parabolic HJB equation, whose Hamiltonian involves optimization over probability measures supported on constrained sets. We recall that the analytical theory of nonlinear parabolic equations originates in
[
36
,
34
,
38
]
and the viscosity-solution framework of
[
13
,
12
]
. Probabilistic representations through backward stochastic differential equations, developed in
[
48
,
21
,
45
]
, further clarify the link between dynamic programming and nonlinear PDEs.

The present paper addresses this structural gap at a rigorous analytical level. Building on the entropy-regularized exploration paradigm of
[
57
]
, which captures the correlation between the risky asset and the factor variable
[
17
]
, as well as the martingale-based continuous-time reinforcement learning framework of
[
26
]
, we develop a continuous-time reinforcement learning formulation of optimal investment under stochastic volatility with explicit portfolio constraints. Our contributions are fivefold. First, we derive the entropy-regularized Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation associated with the constrained stochastic-volatility problem and explicitly characterize the optimizer in the Hamiltonian. We show that, under compact portfolio constraints, the optimal exploratory control distribution is a truncated Gaussian whose mean and variance depend on the spatial derivatives of the value function. The variance is proportional to the temperature parameter and inversely proportional to the curvature of the value function, thereby linking exploration intensity to risk aversion and the local concavity of the value function. Second, we establish the existence of a classical solution to the nonlinear parabolic HJB equation under suitable structural conditions on the model coefficients. After transforming the value function via a homothetic ansatz, the problem reduces to a one-dimensional quasilinear parabolic equation with nonlinear gradient terms and logarithmic entropy contributions. Using the theory of nonlinear parabolic equations in Hölder spaces and structural growth conditions of Ladyzhenskaya–Solonnikov type
[
36
]
, we prove that the reduced equation admits a solution in Hölder space. Third, we prove a verification theorem linking the PDE solution to the stochastic control problem. Under admissibility conditions on the exploratory policy class, we show that the candidate value function satisfies the dynamic programming principle and dominates the performance functional of any admissible exploratory distribution. Moreover, equality holds for the truncated Gaussian policy derived from the Hamiltonian maximization. This establishes optimality in the class of relaxed entropy-regularized controls. Finally, we analyze the structure of policy improvement induced by the entropy-regularized Hamiltonian. The optimal distribution at each iteration depends on the gradient and Hessian of the current value function, which, in turn, solves a nonlinear PDE parameterized by the policy moments. This leads naturally to a sequence of coupled parabolic PDEs corresponding to successive policy updates. We show that, under suitable compactness and monotonicity conditions, this sequence yields a policy improvement scheme consistent with the continuous-time actor–critic paradigm. Our PDE analysis enables us to design an interpretable actor–critic algorithm that learns both the value function and the exploratory policy. Numerical experiments show that the critic parameters converge stably toward their theoretical values and that the learned stochastic policy behaves consistently with the optimal truncated-Gaussian form derived from the HJB equation.

The closest work is the seminar paper
[
17
]
. While the exploratory formulation is similar to that in
[
17
]
, our analysis with portfolio constraints is substantially different. In addition, our analysis rigorously addresses several important issues regarding the existence of solutions to the corresponding PDEs that are not studied in
[
17
]
. In particular, by combining nonlinear PDE analysis, relaxed stochastic control, and entropy-regularized reinforcement learning in a stochastic-volatility environment with portfolio constraints, the present work provides a mathematically grounded bridge between continuous-time learning theory and constrained stochastic control. A central component of our contribution is the establishment of a continuous-time policy improvement theorem at the PDE level. Starting from an admissible exploratory policy, we associate a value function that solves a corresponding parabolic partial differential equation. The entropy-regularized Hamiltonian then induces an explicit update of the exploratory distribution through a maximization step depending on the spatial derivatives of the current value function. This generates a sequence of parabolic PDEs, each corresponding to the evaluation of an updated policy. We show that this iterative scheme produces a monotone improvement in the performance functional and remains well-posed under suitable structural conditions on the coefficients and the constraint set. The entropy term plays a crucial stabilizing role, ensuring the regularity of the Hamiltonian and preventing the degeneracy of the optimization step. In the vanishing-temperature limit, the exploratory distributions concentrate, and the scheme converges toward the classical deterministic feedback control, thereby recovering the Merton-type solution. Compared to the recent work
[
10
]
where a complete financial market is considered for a logarithmic or quadratic utility function, our model with stochastic volatility implies an incomplete market with a focus on nonlinear PDE analysis. We remark that by setting the risk aversion
\eta=1
and a deterministic volatility, we can recover the results obtained for logarithmic utility in
[
10
]
.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows:
Section 2 introduces the stochastic-volatility market model and the admissible exploratory policy class. We also establish the well-posedness of the exploratory wealth dynamics and the admissibility conditions for truncated Gaussian policies.
Section 3 is devoted to the analytical study of the reduced partial differential equation and establishes the existence of classical solutions under suitable structural growth conditions, together with the regularity required for feedback optimality.
Sections 4 and 5 provide the verification theorem, prove the optimality of the truncated Gaussian exploratory policy, and analyze the limiting regime near the zero-temperature limit.
Section 6 develops the policy evaluation and policy-improvement framework associated with the entropy-regularized Hamiltonian.
Section 7 presents numerical illustrations of the proposed learning framework. Section 8 concludes and discusses possible extensions. Finally, extra arguments can be found in the Appendix.

2.1
Exploratory wealth dynamics

In RL settings where the parameters of the underlying model are unknown, dynamic learning becomes crucial. Dynamic learning refers to the ability of an agent to adapt and learn from her interactions with an environment where the underlying model is not known. In such cases, the agent must employ exploration strategies to interact with the environment, gather data, and learn through trial and error. In particular, at each time

that are characterized as follows:

Definition 1
.

The policy
\lambda
is admissible if it satisfies the following properties:

(i)

For each
(s,x,y)\in[t,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}
,
\lambda(\cdot|s,x,y)
is a density function on
[a,b]
a.s;

(ii)

\lambda(\cdot|s,x,y)
is a measurable mapping from
[t,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}\,\,\text{to}\,\,[a,b];

(iii)

the SDE (
2.5
) admits a unique strong, non negative solution for any initial
(t,x,y)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}
;

(iv)

For some
1<q<\frac{1}{1-\eta}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\int_{0}^{T}qm(1-\eta)|{\cal E}(\lambda_{s})|\mathrm{d}s}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[(U(X^{\lambda}_{\mathchoice{T}{T}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle T$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle T$}}}))^{q}\right]<\infty.

We remark that condition
(iv)
is a technical assumption that allows us to apply Fatou’s Lemma in the verification theorem below. When the density
\lambda
is independent of the wealth level, i.e.
\lambda_{t}=\lambda(\pi|t,y)
, Proposition
1
below shows that the SDE (
2.5
) admits a unique strong solution that satisfies
X^{\lambda}_{s}\geq 0
for
t\leq s\leq T
.

Proposition 1
.

Assume that Condition
(SV)
holds. Let
\lambda(\cdot|t,y)
be a probability density function on
[a,b]
with mean
m(t,y)
and variance
M(t,y)
. Suppose that
{\cal E}(.)
is uniformly bounded and that
m
and
M
are deterministic, continuous, and differentiable in both variables, satisfying
m^{2}(t,y)+M(t,y)>0,\,\text{for all }t\text{ and }y
. Then,
\lambda
is admissible.

Proof.

We rewrite (
2.5
):

\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{\lambda}=\big(r+(\mu-r)\,m(t,y_{t})\big)X_{t}^{\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(y_{t})X_{t}^{\lambda}\big(m(t,y_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\sqrt{M(t,y_{t})}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{W}_{t}\big)\,,

(2.7)

which can be rewritten as

\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{\lambda}=\check{k}_{t}X_{t}^{\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}t+\check{h}_{t}X_{t}^{\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\check{W}_{t},

(2.8)

where

\check{k}_{t}=r+(\mu-r)\,m(t,y_{t})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\check{h}_{t}=\sigma(y_{t})\sqrt{m^{2}(t,y_{t})+M(t,y_{t})},

and

\check{W}_{\mathchoice{u}{u}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle u$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}}=\int^{u}_{\mathchoice{0}{0}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}}\,\left(\frac{m(t,y_{t})}{\sqrt{m^{2}(t,y_{t})+M(t,y_{t})}}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\frac{\sqrt{M(t,y_{t})}}{\sqrt{m^{2}(t,y_{t})+M(t,y_{t})}}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{W}_{t}\right),\quad u\in[0,T],

is a Wiener process. Note that the processes
(\check{k}_{t})_{\mathchoice{0\leq t\leq T}{0\leq t\leq T}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 0\leq t\leq T$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 0\leq t\leq T$}}}
and
(\check{h}_{t})_{\mathchoice{0\leq t\leq T}{0\leq t\leq T}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 0\leq t\leq T$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 0\leq t\leq T$}}}
are continuous. Therefore, by Itô’s formula,
there exists a strong solution to (
2.8
) which is given by

X_{t}^{\lambda}=X_{0}^{\lambda}\,\exp\left\{\int^{t}_{0}\big(\check{k}_{u}-\check{h}^{2}_{u}/2\big)\mathrm{d}u+\int^{t}_{0}\check{h}_{u}\mathrm{d}\check{W}_{\mathchoice{u}{u}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle u$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}}\right\}.

(2.9)

To show uniqueness, we set

\tau_{n}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:|\check{k}_{t}|+|\check{h}_{t}|\geq n\}\wedge T,

(2.10)

which is a stopping time. Taking into account that
\max_{\mathchoice{0\leq t\leq T}{0\leq t\leq T}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 0\leq t\leq T$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 0\leq t\leq T$}}}\big(|\check{k}_{t}|+|\check{h}_{t}|\big)<\infty
a.s., we get that
\tau_{n}\to T
a.s. as
n\to\infty
. Now, set

\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{\lambda,n}=\check{k}^{n}_{t}X_{t}^{\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}t+\check{h}^{n}_{t}X_{t}^{\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\check{W}_{t},

where

\check{k}^{n}_{t}=\check{k}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}
and

\check{h}^{n}_{t}=\check{h}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}
. This equation has a unique solution which is

X_{t}^{\lambda,n}=X_{0}^{\lambda}\,\exp\left\{\int^{t}_{0}\big(\check{k}^{n}_{u}-(\check{h}^{n}_{u})^{2}/2\big)\mathrm{d}u+\int^{t}_{0}\check{h}^{n}_{u}\mathrm{d}\check{W}_{\mathchoice{u}{u}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle u$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}}\right\}\,.

Note that on the interval
[0,\tau_{n}]
, the processes
X_{t}^{\lambda,m}

coincide with (
2.9
) for any
m\geq n
.
Let
\zeta_{t}
be a solution of (
2.8
); then, as
n\to\infty

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{0\leq t\leq T}|\zeta_{t}-X_{t}^{\lambda,n}|>0\right)=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}<T)\to 0\,.

So, any solution
\zeta
of the equation (
2.8
) can be represented as the uniform limit in probability of the processes
X_{t}^{\lambda,n}
, so
\zeta
coincides with (
2.9
), hence uniqueness.

It remains to prove that

\mathbb{E}\left[(U(X^{\lambda}_{T}))^{q}\right]<\infty
for some
1<q<\frac{1}{1-\eta}
. Note first that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
U(x)^{q}\leq Cx^{q(1-\eta)}
for all
x>0
. Hence, it suffices to prove that

\mathbb{E}\left[(X^{\lambda}_{T})^{q(1-\eta)}\right]<\infty.

To this end, we consider the localized processes
X^{\lambda,n}_{\tau_{n}}
. We will show that

\underset{n}{\sup}\mathbb{E}\left[(X^{\lambda,n}_{\tau_{n}})^{q(1-\eta)}\right]<\infty.

Indeed, from the explicit representation of the wealth process, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X^{\lambda,n}_{\tau_{n}})^{q(1-\eta)}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[(X_{0}^{\lambda})^{q(1-\eta)}\,\exp\left\{\int^{\tau_{n}}_{0}q(1-\eta)\big(\check{k}^{n}_{u}-(\check{h}^{n}_{u})^{2}/2\big)\mathrm{d}u+\int^{\tau_{n}}_{0}q(1-\eta)\check{h}^{n}_{u}\mathrm{d}\check{W}_{\mathchoice{u}{u}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle u$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}}\right\}\bigg]

\displaystyle=(X_{0}^{\lambda})^{q(1-\eta)})\mathbb{E}\exp\left\{\int^{\tau_{n}}_{0}q(1-\eta)\big(\check{k}^{n}_{u}-(\check{h}^{n}_{u})^{2}/2\big)+\frac{(q(1-\eta)\check{h}^{n}_{u})^{2}}{2}\mathrm{d}u\right\},

where we used the fact that the stochastic exponential is a true martingale under Novikov’s condition.

Now, using the bounds
|\check{k}^{n}_{u}|\leq n
,
|\check{h}^{n}_{u}|\leq n
, for
u\in[0,\tau_{n}]
we obtain:

\mathbb{E}[(X^{\lambda,n}_{\tau_{n}})^{q(1-\eta)}]\leq(X_{0}^{\lambda})^{q(1-\eta)}\exp\left\{Tq(1-\eta)\left(n-\frac{n^{2}}{2}(1-q(1-\eta))\right)\right\},

which is finite for every
n
and
q(1-\eta)<1
.
Using the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we can conclude that
\mathbb{E}\left[(X^{\lambda}_{T})^{q(1-\eta)}\right]<\infty
,
which completes the proof.

∎

Corollary 1
.

Assume that Condition
(SV)
holds and
\lambda\sim\mathcal{N}(\alpha(t,y),\beta(t,y))_{[a,b]}
is a Gaussian density truncated in
[a,b]
with
a<b
fixed. Suppose further that

(a)

For all
(t,y)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}
,
\beta(t,y)>0
and
\sup_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}}\beta(t,y)\leq C
for some constant
C>0
independent of
t
;

(b)

there exists
\iota>0
such that
\alpha(t,y)\in[a+\iota,b-\iota]
for all
(t,y)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
.

Then the exploration policy
\lambda(\cdot|t,y)
is admissible and

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\int_{0}^{T}qm(1-\eta)\,|\mathcal{E}(\lambda_{s})|\,ds\right)\right]<\infty
for any
0<q<(1-\eta)^{-1}.

Proof.
Let
\lambda
be a truncated Gaussian distribution
\lambda
that satisfies all conditions (a)-(b) of Corollary
1
. We recall first that the entropy of
\lambda
is given by

\displaystyle\ln\!\big(\sqrt{2\pi e}\,\beta(t,y_{t})Z\big)+\frac{A\varphi(A)-B\varphi(B)}{2Z},

(2.11)

where
Z=\Phi(B)-\Phi(A)
and

A=\frac{a-\alpha(t,y_{t})}{\beta(t,y_{t})},\qquad B=\frac{b-\alpha(t,y_{t})}{\beta(t,y_{t})}.

We verify all the conditions in Definition
1
. Indeed, Conditions (i) and (ii) are immediate. Note that by (b), we have
A<0<B
uniformly. From
\beta(t,y_{t})Z\leq\frac{C(b-a)}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
, it can be seen that the first term in (
2.11
) is integrable on
[0,T]
. For the second term, we simply note that

\frac{A\varphi(A)-B\varphi(B)}{2Z}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbb{E}[U^{2}\mid A\leq U\leq B]-1\Big),

with
U\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).
Since
A\leq 0\leq B
, it follows that

-\frac{1}{2}\leq\frac{A\varphi(A)-B\varphi(B)}{2Z}\leq 0.

Therefore, the entropy term is uniformly bounded and hence integrable on
[0,T]
. Combining both terms, we conclude that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\int_{0}^{T}qm(1-\eta)\,|\mathcal{E}(\lambda_{s})|\,ds\right)\right]<\infty,

which establishes the admissibility of
\lambda(t,y_{t})
by using Proposition
1
.

∎

2.3
Recursive entropy-penalized optimization problem

Due to the exploration terms, the entropy-regularized optimization problem (
2.6
) does not admit closed-form solutions, except in special cases, for example, when
\eta=1
and the utility function is logarithmic (see
[
10
]
). To make learning more efficient, we consider the following recursive entropy-penalized objective

J^{\lambda}_{t}=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}m[(1-\eta)J^{\lambda}_{s}+1]{\cal E}(\lambda_{\mathchoice{s}{s}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle s$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}})\mathrm{d}s+U(X_{\mathchoice{T}{T}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle T$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle T$}}}^{\lambda})\bigg|{\cal F}_{t}\right].

(2.12)

The recursive form (
2.12
) includes the entropy in an endogenous way; see e.g.
[
17
]
for a similar setting. In particular, it combines the terminal expected utility (the second term) and the utility-dependent (the first term) factor on exploration, which is determined recursively. In
I
, we discuss how the recursive framework developed in this paper can also be used to address the Merton problem, where the agent no longer optimizes a trade-off
between exploration and exploitation but still benefits from an exploratory parameterization of the
policy inherited from the entropy-regularized formulation.

The recursive form (
2.12
) can be connected to a BSDE via the martingale representation theorem as follows:

Lemma 1
.

For any given policy
\lambda
, consider
J^{\lambda}
defined in (
2.12
). Define the martingale

\displaystyle M_{t}=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}m\left[(1-\eta)J^{\lambda}_{s}+1\right]\mathcal{E}(\lambda_{s})\mathrm{d}s+U(X_{T}^{\lambda})\bigg|{\cal F}_{t}\right],\quad t\in[0,T].

(2.13)

Then there exists a predictable process
Z_{t}

such that

\displaystyle M_{t}=M_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}Z_{s}\cdot\mathrm{d}\textbf{W}_{s},\quad t\in[0,T],

(2.14)

where
\cdot
denotes the inner product,
\textbf{W}_{s}=(\bar{W}_{s},W_{s},\hat{W}_{s})^{\top}
, and
\top
denotes the transposition. Furthermore, it holds that

\displaystyle J^{\lambda}_{t}

\displaystyle=M_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}m\left[(1-\eta)J^{\lambda}_{s}+1\right]{\cal E}(\lambda_{s})\mathrm{d}s,

(2.15)

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}J^{\lambda}_{t}

\displaystyle=-m\left[(1-\eta)J^{\lambda}_{t}+1\right]{\cal E}(\lambda_{t})\mathrm{d}t+Z_{t}\cdot\mathrm{d}\textbf{W}_{t}.

(2.16)

In such a context of recursive utility, the term
m[(1-\eta)J^{\lambda}+1]
represents the generator, which encapsulates the relationship between immediate utility and future continuation utility, while the process
Z
corresponds to the hedging portfolio, capturing the sensitivity of the utility to variations in the underlying stochastic factors; see e.g;
[
20
,
21
]
. Observe that (
2.16
) is a linear BSDE whose unique solution (see e.g.
[
21
]
) can be expressed in the following form

J^{\lambda}_{t}=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\int_{t}^{s}m(1-\eta){\cal E}(\lambda_{u})\mathrm{d}u}m{\cal E}(\lambda_{s})\mathrm{d}s+e^{\int_{t}^{T}m(1-\eta){\cal E}(\lambda_{u})\mathrm{d}u}U(X^{\lambda}_{T})\bigg|{\cal F}_{t}\bigg].

(2.17)

Therefore, the investor’s objective is now stated as follows:

\underset{\lambda\in{\cal H}_{[}a,b]}{\sup}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\int_{t}^{s}m(1-\eta){\cal E}(\lambda_{u})\mathrm{d}u}m{\cal E}(\lambda_{s})\mathrm{d}s+e^{\int_{t}^{T}m(1-\eta){\cal E}(\lambda_{u})\mathrm{d}u}U(X^{\lambda}_{T})\bigg|X_{\mathchoice{t}{t}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}}^{\lambda}=x,y_{\mathchoice{t}{t}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}}=y\bigg].

(2.18)

Remark 1
.

Under some smoothness assumptions, using Proposition 4.3 in
[
21
]
, we can link the BSDE (
2.16
) with the following PDE:

\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}+\mathcal{A^{\lambda}}J(t,x,y)+m{\cal E}(\lambda_{t})[(1-\eta)J(t,x,y)+1]=0,\quad J(T,x,y)=U(x),

(2.19)

where, for each fixed
\pi\in[a,b]
and
v
, a smooth function, the local operator
\mathcal{A}^{\pi}
is defined by

\mathcal{A}^{\pi}v:=(r+(\mu-r)\pi)x\,v_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(y)x^{2}\pi^{2}\,v_{xx}+\varpi(y)\,v_{y}+\frac{1}{2}\delta^{2}(y)\,v_{yy}+\rho\,\delta(y)\sigma(y)x\pi\,v_{xy},

and the relaxed operator
\mathcal{A}^{\lambda}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\lambda}v=\int_{a}^{b}\mathcal{A}^{\pi}v\,\lambda(\pi)\,d\pi.

(2.20)

Then, by the Feynman-Kac formula (see
[
21
]
or Theorem
9
), the solution to the PDE (
2.19
) can be expressed as:

\displaystyle J(t,x,y)=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t}^{T}e^{\int_{t}^{s}m(1-\eta){\cal E}(\lambda_{u})\mathrm{d}u}m{\cal E}(\lambda_{s})\mathrm{d}s+e^{\int_{t}^{T}m(1-\eta){\cal E}(\lambda_{u})\mathrm{d}u}U(X^{\lambda}_{T})\bigg|X^{\lambda}_{t}=x,y_{t}=y\bigg].

(2.21)

Remark 2
.

For
\eta=1
and
U(x)=\ln x
, (
2.18
) becomes

\underset{\lambda\in{\cal H}_{[}a,b]}{\sup}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}m{\cal E}(\lambda_{\mathchoice{s}{s}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle s$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}})\mathrm{d}s+U(X_{\mathchoice{T}{T}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle T$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle T$}}}^{\lambda})|X_{\mathchoice{t}{t}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}}=x,Y_{\mathchoice{t}{t}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}}=y\right],

(2.22)

which is studied in
[
10
]
. If
m=0
, then we are back to the classical Merton problem.

2.4
Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman Equation and optimal control distribution

In the rest of the paper, we will investigate the exploratory optimization problem (
2.21
) using the stochastic dynamic programming principle. The optimal value function is given by

\displaystyle V(t,x,y;m):=\underset{\lambda\in{\cal H}_{\mathchoice{[a,b]}{[a,b]}{\lower 0.5382pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle[a,b]$}}{\lower 0.5382pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle[a,b]$}}}}{\sup}J^{\lambda}(t,x,y;m)

(2.23)

To this end, we rewrite the process (
2.5
) and the second equation in (
2.1
) in the following matrix form:

d\zeta_{\mathchoice{t}{t}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}}=\mathbf{a}(\zeta_{\mathchoice{t}{t}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}}^{\lambda},\lambda)\mathrm{d}t+\mathbf{b}(\zeta_{\mathchoice{t}{t}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}}^{\lambda},\lambda)\mathrm{d}\textbf{W}_{\mathchoice{t}{t}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}},\quad\textbf{W}_{\mathchoice{t}{t}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}}=\left(\begin{matrix}\bar{W}_{\mathchoice{t}{t}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}}\\
W_{\mathchoice{t}{t}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}}\\
\hat{W}_{\mathchoice{t}{t}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}}\end{matrix}\right),

(2.24)

where the coefficients
a
and
b
are defined for any state vector
\zeta=(x,y)^{\top}
and policy
\lambda
as

\mathbf{a}(\zeta,\lambda)=\left(\begin{matrix}(r+(\mu-r)Mean(\lambda))x\\
\varpi(y)\end{matrix}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad b(\zeta,\lambda)=\left(\begin{matrix}0&\sigma(y)xMean(\lambda)&\sigma(y)x\sqrt{Var(\lambda)}\\
\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\delta(y)&\rho\delta(y)&0\end{matrix}\right).

Now, for any vector
\mathbf{q}=(\mathbf{q}_{\mathchoice{1}{1}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 1$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 1$}}},\mathbf{q}_{\mathchoice{2}{2}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 2$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 2$}}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and for the symmetric matrix

\textbf{M}=(M_{\mathchoice{i,j}{i,j}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle i,j$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle i,j$}}})_{\mathchoice{1\leq i,j,\leq 2}{1\leq i,j,\leq 2}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 1\leq i,j,\leq 2$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 1\leq i,j,\leq 2$}}}
, we set the Hamiltonian function as

H(J,\zeta,\textbf{q},\textbf{M}):=\sup_{\mathchoice{\lambda\in{\cal H}_{\mathchoice{[a,b]}{[a,b]}{\lower 0.5382pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle[a,b]$}}{\lower 0.5382pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle[a,b]$}}}}{\lambda\in{\cal H}_{\mathchoice{[a,b]}{[a,b]}{\lower 0.5382pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle[a,b]$}}{\lower 0.5382pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle[a,b]$}}}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\lambda\in{\cal H}_{\mathchoice{[a,b]}{[a,b]}{\lower 0.37672pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle[a,b]$}}{\lower 0.37672pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle[a,b]$}}}$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\lambda\in{\cal H}_{\mathchoice{[a,b]}{[a,b]}{\lower 0.37672pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle[a,b]$}}{\lower 0.37672pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle[a,b]$}}}$}}}H_{\mathchoice{0}{0}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}}(\lambda,J,\zeta,\textbf{q},\textbf{M}),

(2.25)

where

H_{\mathchoice{0}{0}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}}(\lambda,\zeta,J,\textbf{q},\textbf{M}):=\textbf{a}^{\top}(\zeta,\lambda)\textbf{q}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{tr}[\textbf{b}\textbf{b}^{\top}(\zeta,u)\textbf{M}]+m{\cal E}(\lambda)[(1-\eta)J+1].

(2.26)

The HJB equation associated with Problem (
2.23
) is given by

\begin{cases}V_{\mathchoice{t}{t}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle t$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}}(\zeta,t)+H(V,\zeta,V_{\zeta}(\zeta,t),V_{\mathchoice{\zeta,\zeta}{\zeta,\zeta}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\zeta,\zeta$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\zeta,\zeta$}}}(\zeta,t))=0,\quad t\in[0,T),\\[5.69054pt]
V(\zeta,T)=\frac{x^{1-\eta}-1}{1-\eta},\quad\zeta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\,,\end{cases}

(2.27)

where
V_{\mathchoice{\zeta}{\zeta}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\zeta$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\zeta$}}}(\zeta,t)=(V_{\mathchoice{x}{x}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle x$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}},V_{\mathchoice{y}{y}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle y$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle y$}}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and

V_{\mathchoice{\zeta\zeta}{\zeta\zeta}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\zeta\zeta$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\zeta\zeta$}}}(\zeta,t)=\left(\begin{matrix}V_{\mathchoice{xx}{xx}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle xx$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle xx$}}}&V_{\mathchoice{xy}{xy}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle xy$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle xy$}}}\\
V_{\mathchoice{xy}{xy}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle xy$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle xy$}}}&V_{\mathchoice{yy}{yy}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle yy$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle yy$}}}\end{matrix}\right)\,.

Note that in this case, the function
H_{\mathchoice{0}{0}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}}
defined in (
2.26
) is of the form

\displaystyle H_{\mathchoice{0}{0}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 0$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 0$}}}(\lambda,J,\zeta,\textbf{q},\textbf{M})

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}((r+(\mu-r)\pi)xq_{\mathchoice{1}{1}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 1$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 1$}}})\lambda(\pi)\mathrm{d}\pi+\varpi(y)q_{\mathchoice{2}{2}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 2$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 2$}}}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(y)\int_{a}^{b}x^{2}\pi^{2}\lambda(\pi)\mathrm{d}\pi\textbf{M}_{\mathchoice{11}{11}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 11$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 11$}}}

\displaystyle+\int_{a}^{b}\rho\delta(y)\sigma(y)x\lambda(\pi)\mathrm{d}\pi\textbf{M}_{\mathchoice{12}{12}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 12$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 12$}}}+\frac{1}{2}\delta^{2}(y)\textbf{M}_{\mathchoice{22}{22}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle 22$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle 22$}}}+m{\cal E}(\lambda)[(1-\eta)J+1]

and the value function
V(t,x,y;m)
solves the following HJB PDE

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}+\underset{\lambda\in{\cal H}_{[}a,b]}{\sup}\bigg(\mathcal{A^{\lambda}}V(t,x,y)+m{\cal E}(\lambda_{t})[(1-\eta)V(t,x,y)+1]\bigg)=0,

(2.28)

with terminal condition
V(T,x,y;m)=\frac{x^{1-\eta}-1}{1-\eta}
. The optimal policy
\lambda^{0,[a,b]}
is characterized by the following:

Lemma 2
.

In the exploratory constrained setting, the optimal density
\lambda^{0,[a,b]}
to the HJB equation (
2.28
) is a normal distribution with mean
\alpha
and variance
\beta^{2}
, truncated on the interval

Theorem 4
.

A function

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{T}|X^{\lambda}_{s},y_{s}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[J^{\lambda}(T,X^{\lambda}_{T},y_{T};m)+\int_{t}^{T}m[(1-\eta)J^{\lambda}(s^{\prime},X^{\lambda}_{s^{\prime}},y_{s^{\prime}};m)+1]{\cal E}(\lambda_{s^{\prime}})\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}|X^{\lambda}_{s},y_{s}\bigg]

7.2
State process simulation

Accurate simulation of the variance factor is crucial for the stability of the learning algorithm. In our SV model, since both the control and value functions depend nonlinearly on
y
, any numerical bias or loss of positivity in its simulation propagates directly into the policy updates. Recall that the diffusion coefficient of the factor is of square-root type,
c_{v}(y)=k_{1}\sqrt{y+\delta_{*}},

, which degenerates near zero.
For such processes, the standard Euler–Maruyama discretization may produce negative values with positive probability.
This may lead to numerical instability, especially since the functions
\sqrt{y+\delta_{*}}
and
\sqrt{y^{2}+\sigma_{*}^{2}}
are repeatedly evaluated during learning.
Ad–hoc truncation or reflection schemes may restore positivity,
but they introduce bias and may significantly distort the dynamics,
which is particularly problematic in reinforcement learning where small errors accumulate over many iterations.

To overcome this issue, we adopt the inverse–Gaussian variance (IVI) step introduced in
[
1
]
.
This scheme is specifically designed for square-root diffusions and yields an explicit update in terms of an inverse–Gaussian random variable.
Importantly, it preserves positivity by construction and avoids artificial boundary corrections.
This ensures numerical robustness and consistency with the theoretical continuous-time dynamics. Note that for a given
y_{k}
, the value of
y_{k+1}
can be simulated by the IVI procedure as metioned above. However, the logreturn process
\log S
is correlated with the stochastic volatility process
y
which continuously depends on
y_{t}
, for
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
. To maintain the stability, we take
y_{bar}=(y_{k}+y_{k+1})/2
, set

\sigma_{bar}=\sqrt{y_{bar}^{2}+\sigma_{*}^{2}}
,

\mu_{bar}=\mu(t_{k}+\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta t,y_{bar})
,
and use a single Brownian driver correlated with the factor increment. This yields a stable mid–point update for
\log S
over
[0,T]
.

shows that the singular term

\frac{1-\eta}{2}\,m\ln m\,(T-t)
originates exclusively from the entropy–induced
exploratory variance.
This contribution is therefore retained explicitly in the value proxy.
The remaining higher–order correction terms in the asymptotic expansion
(e.g.

They correspond to finer deterministic corrections that do not affect the leading-order
exploratory behavior and would unnecessarily complicate the learning procedure.
Instead, the functions

and
M^{\psi}
are trained to capture the dominant
state-dependent structure of the value function beyond the variance effect. Following Lemma

, the optimal control
\lambda_{t}\in[0,1]
is modeled as a truncated normal:

\lambda_{t}^{\theta}\sim{\cal N}\big(\mu_{\theta}(t,y_{t}),\,\sigma_{\theta}(t,y_{t})^{2}\big)_{\mathchoice{[0,1]}{[0,1]}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle[0,1]$}}{\lower 0.75346pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle[0,1]$}}},

with

\mu_{\theta}(t,y)=\frac{\sqrt{y+\delta_{*}}}{\sqrt{y^{2}+\sigma_{*}^{2}}}\;\frac{\theta_{4}+\theta_{5}\,L^{\theta}(t)}{\eta},\qquad L^{\theta}(t)=\frac{-\theta_{1}+\theta_{1}e^{\theta_{0}(T-t)}}{-\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}e^{\theta_{0}(T-t)}},

Conclusion

We study a continuous-time portfolio optimization problem under stochastic volatility, portfolio constraints, and entropy-regularized exploration.
We proved the well-posedness of the associated Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation by establishing the existence and uniqueness of a classical solution in the spirit of
[
36
]
, allowing us to characterize the value function in closed form.
This analytical structure enabled us to design an interpretable actor–critic algorithm that learns both the value function and the exploratory policy.
Numerical experiments show that the critic parameters converge stably toward their theoretical values and that the learned stochastic policy behaves consistently with the optimal truncated-Gaussian form derived from the HJB equation.
Our framework opens several directions for future work, including an extended empirical evaluation, the incorporation of additional sources of risk such as stochastic interest rates or ambiguity aversion, and the development of higher-dimensional versions of the learning algorithm.
These aspects will be addressed in subsequent research

Lemma 4

(Donsker–Varadhan variational formula
[
18
]
)
.

Let
(\Theta,\mathcal{F},P)
be a probability space, and let
h:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function such that
e^{h}\in L^{1}(P)
. Then

\ln\mathbb{E}_{P}\!\left[e^{h}\right]=\sup_{Q\ll P}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{Q}[h]-{\bf KL}(Q\|P)\right\},

(A.1)

where
{\bf KL}(Q\|P)
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence defined by

{\bf KL}(Q\|P):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q}\!\left[\ln\!\left(\frac{dQ}{dP}\right)\right],&Q\ll P,\\[5.16663pt]
+\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Moreover, the supremum is attained uniquely at the probability measure
Q^{*}
defined by

\frac{dQ^{*}}{dP}=\frac{e^{h}}{\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{h}]}.

(A.2)

Proof.

Let
Q\ll P
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q}[h]-{\bf KL}(Q\|P)=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\!\left[h-\ln\!\left(\frac{dQ}{dP}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\!\left[\ln\!\left(e^{h}\frac{dP}{dQ}\right)\right].

By Jensen’s inequality, since
\ln
is concave,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q}\!\left[\ln\!\left(e^{h}\frac{dP}{dQ}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\ln\mathbb{E}_{Q}\!\left[e^{h}\frac{dP}{dQ}\right]=\ln\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{h}].

Hence

\mathbb{E}_{Q}[h]-{\bf KL}(Q\|P)\leq\ln\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{h}]

for every
Q\ll P
, which proves

\sup_{Q\ll P}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{Q}[h]-{\bf KL}(Q\|P)\right\}\leq\ln\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{h}].

Now define
Q^{*}
by

\frac{dQ^{*}}{dP}=\frac{e^{h}}{\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{h}]}.

Since
\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{h}]<\infty
,
Q^{*}
is a probability measure and
Q^{*}\ll P
. Moreover,

\ln\!\left(\frac{dQ^{*}}{dP}\right)=h-\ln\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{h}],

so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q^{*}}[h]-{\bf KL}(Q^{*}\|P)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Q^{*}}[h]-\mathbb{E}_{Q^{*}}\!\left[\ln\!\left(\frac{dQ^{*}}{dP}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Q^{*}}[h]-\mathbb{E}_{Q^{*}}[h-\ln\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{h}]]=\ln\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{h}].

Therefore, the supremum is attained at
Q^{*}
, and (
A.1
) follows.

Finally, since equality in Jensen’s inequality holds if and only if

e^{h}\frac{dP}{dQ}

is
Q
-almost surely constant, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

e^{h}\frac{dP}{dQ}=C\quad Q\text{-a.s.},

which is equivalent to

\frac{dQ}{dP}=\frac{e^{h}}{C}.

Since
Q
is a probability measure, integrating with respect to
P
yields

1=\int_{\Theta}\frac{e^{h}}{C}\,dP=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{h}]}{C},

hence
C=\mathbb{E}_{P}[e^{h}]
, and therefore
Q=Q^{*}
. This proves uniqueness.

Connection to the Merton problem

As pointed out in
[
10

, the recursive entropy-penalized optimization (
2.18
) considered in this paper does not fully recover the classical Merton problem. In particular, as noted in Remark
2
, when
\eta=1
, one recovers the logarithmic case, while the extension to power utility remains open within this framework. In order to address this issue, we propose an alternative approach inspired by the policy parametrization developed throughout the paper. The key idea is the following: we no longer consider the trade-off between exploration and exploitation through an entropy penalty in the objective function, but we still allow the agent to use a randomized (exploratory) policy. In other words, exploration is preserved at the level of the control parametrization, but it is no longer penalized. More precisely, we consider the optimization problem

V^{\mathrm{Merton}}(t,x,y)=\sup_{\lambda^{\mathrm{Merton}}\in\mathcal{H}_{[a,b]}}\mathbb{E}\left[U\big(X_{T}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{Merton}}}\big)\,\middle|\,X_{t}^{\lambda^{\mathrm{Merton}}}=x,\;Y_{t}=y\right].

(I.1)

Contrary to the entropy-regularized problem (
2.18
) studied in the main text, no entropy term appears in the objective function. However, we keep the same class of parameterized policies. In particular, based on Corollary
1
, we consider controls of the form

\lambda^{\mathrm{Merton}}=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\alpha^{\mathrm{Merton}}(t,x,y),\frac{m}{\eta\sigma^{2}(y)}\right)_{[a,b]},

(I.2)

where
\alpha^{\mathrm{Merton}}
is a deterministic function and
m>0
controls the level of exploration. Therefore, even though the objective is not entropy-penalized, the agent still explores through the variance of the policy.

\Upsilon
