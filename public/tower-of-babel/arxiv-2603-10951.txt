1.
Introduction

A classical result of Dirac
[
6
]
states that every graph on
n\geq 3
vertices with minimum degree at
least
n/2
contains a Hamilton cycle, i.e. a cycle on
n
vertices. A closely related result is
the famous theorem of Komlós, Sárközy and Szemerédi
[
16
]
which states
that, for any
\gamma>0
and
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a
n_{0}
such that every graph
on
n\geq n_{0}
vertices
with minimum degree at least
{(1/2+\gamma)n}
contains every spanning tree
with maximum degree at most
\Delta
. The bound on the maximum degree of the tree was later improved by the same authors to
O(n/\log n)

[
15
]
.
In this paper, we investigate Dirac-type conditions for the embedding of bounded
degree trees in digraphs.

One of the earliest Dirac-type results for digraphs is due to
Ghouila-Houri
[
7
]
, who showed that every strongly connected digraph
D
on
n
vertices
with
{\delta^{0}(D)\geq n/2}
has a directed Hamilton cycle (for all notation, see Section
3
). A generalization of this theorem, due to DeBiasio and Molla
[
5
]
and
DeBiasio, Kühn, Molla, Osthus and Taylor
[
4
]
states that
\delta^{0}(D)\geq n/2+1
suffices to guarantee all orientations of the Hamilton cycle in an
n
-vertex digraph
D
, if
n
is sufficiently large, and moreover, the term
+1
is only needed for the antidirected Hamilton cycle. With respect to spanning trees,
Mycroft and Naia
[
20
]
showed that
for any
\gamma>0
and
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
n_{0}
such
that any digraph
D
on
n\geq n_{0}
vertices with
\delta^{0}(D)\geq(1/2+\gamma)n

contains every oriented tree
T
on
n
vertices with
\Delta(T)\leq\Delta
, which is reproved in this work. Kathapurkar and Montgomery
[
10
]
improved this, relaxing the maximum degree bound for the tree to
O(n/\log n)
. In conclusion, both for Hamiltonicity and for spanning tree universality, the bounds on the minimum degree in the graph setting correspond to the bounds on the minimum semidegree in the digraph setting (if we disregard the
+1
term for antidirected Hamilton cycles).

This changes in the setting of oriented graphs. Kelly, Kühn and Osthus
[
12
]

showed that for any
{\gamma>0}
, every sufficiently large oriented graph
G
on
n
vertices
with
{\delta^{0}(G)\geq(3/8+\gamma)n}
contains a directed Hamilton
cycle. An exact result for sufficiently
large oriented graphs was later proved by Keevash, Kühn and
Osthus
[
11
]
, and Kelly
[
13
]
proved
asymptotically optimal results for Hamilton cycles with any orientation. This was recently improved by
Wang, Wang and Zhang
[
24
]
who showed that every
sufficiently large oriented graph with minimum semidegree at least
{\left\lceil(3n-1)/8\right\rceil}

contains every possible orientation of a Hamilton cycle.

To our knowledge, until now no corresponding results are known for the spanning tree problem in oriented graphs.
We show that in this setting, the minimum semidegree threshold for containment of bounded-degree oriented
tree asymptotically coincides with the threshold for oriented Hamilton cycles.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
\gamma>0
and
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
n_{0}=n_{0}(\gamma,\Delta)

such that the following holds for every
n\geq n_{0}
. If
G
is an oriented graph
on
n
vertices with
{\delta^{0}(G)\geq(3/8+\gamma)n}
, and
T
is an oriented
tree on
n
vertices with
\Delta(T)\leq\Delta
, then
G
contains a copy of
T
.

The following construction due Kelly
[
13
, Proposition 4]
, which improves earlier
constructions of Häggkvist
[
8
]
and Keevash, Kühn, and Osthus
[
11
]
,
shows that the bound on the minimum semidegree in
Theorem
1.1
is asymptotically tight. Let
n=8k+4
, for some
{k\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Consider the oriented graph
G
on
n
vertices such that
the vertex set
V(G)
is partitioned into
four sets
X,Y,W
and
Z
, each with exactly
2k+1
vertices. The sets
X
and
Z

each induce a regular tournament. All arcs from
X
to
W
, from
W
to
Z
,
from
Z
to
Y
, and from
Y
to
X
are present in
G
. Finally, the pair
(Y,W)
induces
a regular bipartite tournament. Note that
G
has minimum semidegree exactly

3n/8-1/2
,
and that it does not contain any antidirected path on more than
3n/4
vertices. In particular,
the graph
G
does not contain the antidirected Hamilton path. Recently, this
construction was extended to all
n
by
Wang, Wang and Zhang
[
24
, Proposition 1.2]
.

Two open problems arise from our work. The first is determining the exact threshold for
an oriented graph to contain bounded degree trees, and the second is determining the asymptotic threshold for trees with
maximum degree that grows with
n
. In particular, it seems natural to ask the following.

Question 1.2
.

Is it true that for
\gamma>0
there are
c>0
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that the following
holds for every
n\geq n_{0}
? Every oriented graph
G
on
n
vertices with
{\delta^{0}(G)\geq(3/8+\gamma)n}
contains
a copy of each oriented tree
T
on
n
vertices with
{\Delta(T)\leq cn/\log n}
.

If true, this would be best possible apart from a constant factor. Let
c
be a sufficiently large constant.
As observed by Komlós, Sárközy and Szemerédi
[
16
]
, a random graph on
n
vertices obtained by selecting
each edge independently with probability
0.9
has with high
probability (w.h.p.) minimum degree at least
0.8n
, and moreover w.h.p. it does not contain the tree
T
obtained by taking a star
of degree
\log n/c
and attaching to each of its leaves a star of degree
cn/\log n
. Let
G
be a graph satisfying both of these properties.
Then a random orientation of
G
yields w.h.p. an oriented graph with minimum semidegree at least
0.39n
, and clearly this oriented
graph contains no orientation of
T
.

1.1.
Robust expansion

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we will establish a version of it for
robust out-expanders. Given
\nu,\tau>0
, we say that a digraph
D
on
n
vertices
is a
robust
(\nu,\tau)
-out-expander
if for every set
S\subseteq V(D)

with
{\tau n\leq|S|\leq(1-\tau)n}
, at least
|S|+\nu n
vertices each receive at least
\nu n

arcs from
S
. The notion of robust out-expanders was introduced
by Kühn, Osthus and Treglown
[
18
]
to prove Theorem
1.3
below
and has found many applications in extremal problems in digraphs. In the statement of the following
theorem and in the rest of the paper, we use the notation
x\ll y

to indicate that
there is an increasing function
f
such that,
for any given
y
, whenever
we choose
{x\leq f(y)}
, all calculations
and inequalities
involving these constants in the sequence are valid.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
18
]
)
.

Let
1/n\ll\nu\leq\tau\ll\gamma<1
. Let
D
be a a robust
(\nu,\tau)
-out-expander
on
n
vertices with
{\delta^{0}(D)\geq\gamma n}
. Then
D
contains a
directed Hamilton cycle.

We prove that in the same setting,
D
also contains all bounded degree oriented spanning trees.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
1/n\ll\nu\leq\tau\ll\gamma,\Delta^{-1}
.
Let
D
be a a robust
(\nu,\tau)
-out-expander on
n
vertices with
{\delta^{0}(D)\geq\gamma n}
, and let
T
be an oriented tree on
n

vertices with
{\Delta(T)\leq\Delta}
. Then
D
contains a copy of
T
.

Now, Theorem
1.1
follows immediately from Theorem
1.4
and an application
of part (b) of the following known result.

Lemma 1.5

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
1/n\ll\nu\ll\tau\leq\gamma
and let
D
be a digraph on
n
vertices. If

(1)

D

satisfies
{\delta^{0}(D)\geq(1/2+\gamma)n}
,
or

(2)

D

is an oriented graph with
{\delta^{0}(D)\geq(3/8+\gamma)n}
,

then
D
is
a robust
(\nu,\tau)
-out-expander.

Clearly, Theorem
1.4

together with part (a) of Lemma
1.5
implies
Mycroft and Naia’s above-mentioned result
[
20
]
on bounded degree oriented spanning trees in digraphs.

2.
Overview

For better readability, we first sketch a proof for a variant of Theorem
1.4
where
T
is
only almost-spanning. Then we present the adjustments that are necessary so
the proof works for spanning trees.

2.1.
Proof for almost spanning trees

We use the Digraph Regularity Lemma.
For an appropriate choice of
constants
\varepsilon\ll d\ll\nu\leq\tau\ll\gamma
, we find an
\varepsilon
-regular
partition
\{V_{0},\dots,V_{k}\}
of
V(D)

(see Section
4
). The corresponding reduced digraph
R

inherits the minimum semidegree and
expansion of
D
(see Lemma
4.2
).

As it is typical in applications of the Regularity Lemma, we need to find an assignment
{\varphi:V(T)\rightarrow V(R)}
that distributes
the vertices of
T
in a balanced way among the vertices of
R
. To find such
\varphi
, we use a randomised approach.
Consider, as a toy example, the case of
T
being an oriented
path
{(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n})}
. We start by
choosing
\varphi(v_{1})\in V(R)
uniformly at random. Then, for
1\leq t\leq n-1
, we choose

\varphi(v_{t+1})
uniformly at random either from the in- or from the out-neighbourhood of
\varphi(v_{t})
, according to the orientation of
T
. This gives a
random walk in
R
that follows the pattern of the oriented path. To the best of our knowledge, this idea was first used by Mycroft and Naia
[
20
]

in a slightly different setting. Since we require
\varphi
to be well-balanced, we keep track of the visitation time
X_{t}
of this walk
(i.e,
|\varphi^{-1}(X)|
at time
t
) for
X\in V(R)
, which we expect to be roughly
n/k
once the
random walk mixes into a uniform distribution.

Now the
cherry property
(see Definition
6.1
) becomes important.
It can be viewed as a directed analogue of being connected and non-bipartite in graphs, which are properties known to relate to
mixing of (undirected) random walks.
It turns out that if
H
is a regular digraph on
k
vertices that possesses the
cherry property then the
variable
X_{t}
is w.h.p. close to a uniform sampling in
V(H)
as long as
t
is large enough compared
to
k
(see Proposition
6.3
).
We also show that any robust expander
with non-vanishing minimum semidegree, as the one from Theorem
1.4
, has a spanning regular subdigraph having the cherry property (see Proposition
6.2
). In particular, this yields a regular subdigraph of
R
with the cherry property.
Consequently, if performed on this subdigraph, the random walk mixes and w.h.p. we obtain a well balanced assignment
\varphi
.
We then complete the embedding
T

by greedily following
\varphi
. The properties of the regular partition guarantee that this greedy embedding works.

Now, if
T
is an arbitrary oriented tree with bounded maximum degree (not necessarily a path), we modify our randomised approach as follows. We choose an arbitrary root
r
of
T
and fix a top-bottom ordering
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
of
V(T)
. We start by assigning
v_{1}
uniformly at random to a vertex of
V(R)
. Then, for
1\leq t\leq n-1
, we choose uniformly at random an out-neighbour
V^{+}
and in-neighbour
V^{-}
of
\varphi(v_{t})
. We assign all out-neighbours of
v_{t}
to
V^{+}
and all in-neighbours to
V^{-}
.
A martingale analysis shows that we can find a well-balanced assignment
\varphi
using this approach.

2.2.
Proof for spanning trees

To embed spanning oriented trees we must overcome three problems. First, we have to use the Blow-Up lemma in order to fill up the clusters, second, we have to incorporate the exceptional vertices, and third, we need to find a perfectly balanced assignment. We will discuss each of these issues separately.

2.2.1.
Using the Blow-Up Lemma

The greedy embedding procedure discussed above only works if the number of unused vertices in each cluster
V_{i}

is much larger than
\varepsilon|V_{i}|
, which is no longer the case when
T
is spanning. To solve this problem we invoke the Blow-up Lemma, in a version due
to Csaba (see Lemma
4.3
).

To be able to apply the Blow-up Lemma, we need to make two modifications to our approach.
First, we find a directed Hamilton cycle
C
in
R
, which exists by Theorem
1.3
,
and relocate some vertices to
V_{0}
to make the arcs of
C
super-regular. Second,
we must ensure that many designated special arcs of
T
are mapped by
\varphi
to the arcs of
C
in
a balanced way. We call this the
absorbing property
.
These special arcs are chosen according to the structure of
T
.
We distinguish three cases, depending
on whether
T
has many leaves, many constant length
bare paths
(i.e. paths on degree-2 vertices of
T
) of the same orientation,
or many
switches
(sources or sinks of degree two). We only explain here the many-paths case, as it contains the main ideas of the proof.
Also, we will assume that each of these paths starts with an out-vertex.
In this case, the special arcs are the arcs of selected bare paths.

To obtain an assignment with the absorbing property, we replace the random greedy assignment used in the almost-spanning case
with a
semi-random assignment
(see Definition
7.1
),
which is defined as follows. We perform the random greedy process described above,
only that whenever
the starting vertex of a special path is assigned, we deterministically assign the rest of the path only using edges of
C
.
In Proposition
7.2
,
we show that w.h.p. this semi-random assignment
\varphi
satisfies the absorbing property and also gives each cluster
roughly the same amount of vertices of
T
.

Moreover, we can show that
\varphi
assigns roughly the same number of
starting vertices of the special paths to each cluster of
R
. This property will be important in the next subsection.

2.2.2.
Incorporating the exceptional vertices

The second issue we need to address in order to embed spanning trees is that we must incorporate
the exceptional vertices from
V_{0}
into
\varphi
. This will be done in Section
8.4
, using
skewed-traverses (see Figure
1
), which were introduced by Kelly
[
12
]
. For each exceptional
vertex
v\in V_{0}
we find two clusters
V_{i^{+}}
and
V_{i^{-}}
such that
v
sends many arcs to

V_{i^{+}}
and receives many arcs from
V_{i^{-}}
. These clusters exists because of the high minimum semidegree of
D
.

Moreover, as established in the previous subsection, many special paths start at
V_{i^{-}}
. Let
P=(v_{0},\dots,v_{\ell-1})
be one of them.
We incorporate
v
by reassigning
v_{1}
to
v
and
v_{2}
to
V_{i^{+}}
. However,
note that this modification is only valid if
V_{i^{-}+3}
is in the out-neighbourhood of
V_{i^{+}}
; otherwise the
reassignment would violate the arc-preserving property of
\varphi
. Since this condition need not always hold,
we use skewed-traverses to adjust the assignment of
some vertices of
P
(see Figure
2
). This method
works only when
\ell
is a multiple of
|V(R)|
and, therefore, we can incorporate only
\xi n\ll\varepsilon n
vertices (while
V_{0}
can have up to
\varepsilon n
vertices).

To solve this issue, we use an idea that has also appeared in
[
12
,
13
,
22
]
. We split
T
into two linear
subtrees
T_{A}
and
T_{B}
(see Lemma
3.7
). We also split
D

into two subdigraphs
D_{A^{\prime}}
and
D_{B^{\prime}}
such that both remain robust expanders with linear
minimum semidegree (see Lemma
5.2
). We then apply the Diregularity Lemma
to
D_{B}
with parameter
\varepsilon
, obtaining an exceptional set
V_{0}
with at most
\varepsilon n
vertices.
Next, we apply the Diregularity Lemma to
D[A\cup V_{0}]
, this time with parameter
\xi
instead
of
\varepsilon
, yielding an exceptional set
U_{0}
with at most
\xi n
vertices. Finally,
we define
D_{A}=D_{A^{\prime}}\setminus U_{0}
and
D_{B}=D_{B^{\prime}}\cup U_{0}
. In this way, and at the cost of having to connect
T_{A}

and
T_{B}
later, we have no exceptional vertices in
D_{A}
and only a very small set
U_{0}
of exceptional vertices in
D_{B}
, which
we can then incorporate using the method of skewed-traverses. The steps outlined above are carried out
in Sections
8.1
,
8.2
,
8.4
.

2.2.3.
Obtaining a perfectly balanced assignment

Finally, the last issue in embedding spanning trees is obtaining a perfectly balanced assignment
\varphi
.
We do this by modifying
\varphi
via skewed-traverses. This balancing step is very similar to the
incorporation of exceptional vertices and is presented in Section
8.5
.
Combined with the properties of
\varphi
, this procedure allows us to balance
\varphi
without
significantly disturbing the absorbing property.

With all issues resolved, we obtain a perfectly balanced assignment
\varphi
that incorporates all the exceptional
vertices and still satisfies the absorbing property. We can now apply the Blow-up Lemma
to embed
T_{B}
into
D_{B}
. Now, we need to embed
T_{A}
into
D_{A}
and connect the two pieces.
We proceed in the same way as for
T_{B}
.
We find a perfectly balanced assignment
\varphi_{A}
of
T_{A}
to the
corresponding reduced graph of
D_{A}
.
The only difference is that we ensure the root of
T_{A}
is assigned
to a cluster that receives (or sends) many arcs to the vertex where the root of
T_{B}
was embedded.
We can then apply the Blow-up Lemma to embed
T_{A}
into
D_{A}
, finishing the embedding of
T
.

3.
Preliminaries

3.1.
On sequences of numbers

Given a number
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
[n]

the set
{\{1,\dots,n\}}
. We will
use the following equality.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any two sequences of numbers
(a_{i})_{i\in{[\ell]}}

and
(b_{i})_{i\in[\ell]}
we have

\left(\sum_{i\in[\ell]}a_{i}b_{i}\right)^{2}=\sum_{i\in[\ell]}a_{i}^{2}\sum_{i\in[\ell]}b_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in[\ell]}\big(a_{i}b_{j}-a_{j}b_{i}\big)^{2}.

3.2.
On probability and concentration inequalities

We say that an event depending on
n
occurs
with high probability
if its
probability tends to
1
as
n
goes to infinity. Given a random variable
X
,
we write
\mathbb{E}[X]
for its expected value. We say that
X
follows a binomial
distribution with parameters
n
and
p
if it counts the number of successes
in
n
independent experiments, each of which has probability
p
of success.
We use the following Chernoff-type bound (see, for instance,
Corollary 2.3 of
[
9
]
).

Lemma 3.2

(Chernoff’s bound)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
0<p<1
, and
0<\varepsilon\leq 3/2
.
If
X
follows a binomial distribution with parameters
n
and
p
, then

(1)

\Pr(|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\geq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[X])\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3}\mathbb{E}[X]\right).

Let
(A_{i})_{i\in[k]}
be a collection of increasing events, and
define
X=\sum_{i\in[k]}\mathds{1}_{A_{i}}
. If these events are mutually
independent,
we can apply a Chernoff-type bound to estimate the lower tail of
X
. When the
events are ‘mostly’ independent,
we use the following Janson-type
bound (see, for instance, Theorem 2.14 of
[
9
]
).

Lemma 3.3

(Janson’s inequality)
.

Let
(A_{i})_{i\in[k]}
be a collection of increasing events.
Let

Z=\sum_{i\in[k]}\mathds{1}_{A_{i}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta=\sum_{i\in[k]}\sum_{i\sim j}\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{j}),

where the sum is over non-ordered pairs
\{i,j\}
of dependent events,
with
i\neq j
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(Z\leq\mathbb{E}[Z]-t\right)\leq\exp\left(-\dfrac{t^{2}}{2(\mathbb{E}[Z]+\Delta)}\right).

A sequence of random variables
(X_{i})_{i=0}^{k}
is a
martingale
if

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}|X_{0},\dots,X_{i-1}]=X_{i-1}
, for every
i\in[k]
. We say that
a martingale
(X_{i})_{i=0}^{k}
is
c
-Lipschitz
if
{|X_{i}-X_{i-1}|\leq c}
,
for all
{i\in[k]}
. We will use the following Azuma-type bound
for martingales (see, for instance, Theorem 2.25 of
[
9
]
).

Lemma 3.4

(Azuma’s inequality)
.

Let
c>0
and let
(Y_{i})_{i=0}^{k}
be a
c
-Lipschitz martingale.
Then, for any
\lambda>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}(|Y_{k}-Y_{0}|\geq\lambda)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2kc^{2}}\right).

3.3.
On multigraphs

A
multigraph
is a graph that allows multiple edges between the same pair of vertices.
The
multiplicity
of a multigraph
G
, denoted by
\mu(G)
, is the maximum number
of edges joining the same pair of vertices.
Given an integer
k
, a
k
-edge colouring
of a multigraph
G
is
a map
c:E(G)\rightarrow[k]
with
c(e)\neq c(f)
for any adjacent edges
e,f\in E(G)
.
The
chromatic index
of a multigraph
G
, denoted by
\chi^{\prime}(G)
, is the smallest integer
k

for which a
k
-edge colouring of
G
exists.
We will use Vizing’s Theorem
[
23
]
.

Theorem 3.5

(Vizing’s Theorem)
.

For every loopless multigraph
G
, we have

\chi^{\prime}(G)\leq\Delta(G)+\mu(G).

3.4.
On digraphs

For a digraph
D
, we denote by
V(D)
its vertex set
and by
A(D)
its arc set. If
(u,v)\in A(D)
,
we say that
v
is an
out-neighbour
of
u
, and
u

is an
in-neighbour
of
v
. For
{v\in V(D)}
,
the set
N^{+}(v)

. The

of

The

and the

defined analogously. The

of

is the minimum over all the in- and all the out-degrees of the vertices. A

in a digraph is a vertex with out-degree

is a vertex with in-degree

We will use the symbols

If
{X\subseteq V(D)}
, then for every vertex

.

The

of a digraph is a graph obtained by replacing each arc by an edge.
We write

for the maximum degree of its underlying multigraph.
An

in both directions. Note that the underlying multigraph of an oriented graph has multiplicity one, i.e., it is a graph.
In this case we simply refer to it as the

.
An oriented graph is

if each vertex is either a source or a sink.

An

is a sequence of distinct vertices

such that for

or
(v_{i+1},v_{i})
exists in

.
Two oriented paths in

if the pattern
of arc directions along the paths is the same, up to reversing the order
of the vertices.
A

of length

is an oriented path where all arcs follow the same direction.
A

of length

of distinct vertices
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
such that
(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in A(D)
, and

{(v_{k},v_{1})\in A(D)}
.
An

in a digraph

on

{(v_{1},\dots,v_{n},v_{n+1}=v_{1})}
such that for every

or
(v_{i+1},v_{i})
is an arc of

. A

in

is an oriented Hamilton cycle where

As usual, if

Lemma 3.6

We will see next that in every oriented tree with many bare paths, many of these paths share

We also need to split our tree into two linear sized subtrees so that both contain the same structure.

4.
The Diregularity Lemma

Let
D
be a directed graph, let
\varepsilon>0
, let
{\diamond\in\{+,-\}}
and let
X,Y\subseteq V
be disjoint
and nonempty. The
out-density

d^{+}(X,Y)
and
in-density

d^{-}(X,Y)
of the pair
(X,Y)
are defined by

d^{\diamond}(X,Y)=\frac{e^{\diamond}(X,Y)}{|X||Y|},

where
e^{\diamond}(X,Y)
counts the edges between
X
and
Y
in the direction indicated by
\diamond
.
A set
{X^{\prime}\subseteq X}

is
\varepsilon
-significant
if
{|X^{\prime}|\geq\varepsilon|X|}
.
The pair
(X,Y)

is
(\varepsilon,\diamond)
-regular

if
{|d^{\diamond}(X,Y)-d^{\diamond}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})|\leq\varepsilon}
for
all
\varepsilon
-significant subsets
{X^{\prime}\subseteq X}

and
{Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y}
. If furthermore, for some
d\geq 0
, we
have
{\deg^{\diamond}(x,Y)\geq(d-\varepsilon)|Y|}
, for all
x\in X
, we say
that the
pair
(X,Y)
is
(\varepsilon,\diamond,d)
-super-regular
.

The Regularity Lemma of Szemerédi
[
21
]
states that
every large graph can be partitioned into a bounded number of
sets, most of which are pairwise
\varepsilon
-regular. We will use the
following version of this lemma for digraphs, obtained by Alon
and Shapira
[
2
]
.

Lemma 4.1

(Degree form of the Diregularity Lemma
[
2
]
)
.

For every
0<\varepsilon<1
and
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, there are integers
M_{0}
and
n_{0}

such that the following holds for all
n\geq n_{0}
and
0<d\leq 1
.
If
D
is a digraph on
n
vertices, then there is a partition
\{V_{0},\dots,V_{k}\}
of
V(D)
, and
a spanning subgraph
D^{*}
of
D
such that:

(1)

m_{0}\leq k\leq M_{0}
,

(2)

|V_{0}|\leq\varepsilon n
and
|V_{1}|=\dots=|V_{k}|
,

(3)

\deg_{D^{*}}^{\diamond}(v)>\deg_{D}^{\diamond}(v)-(d+\varepsilon)n
,
for all
v\in V
and
\diamond\in\{+,-\}
,

(4)

V_{i}
is an independent set in
D^{*}
, for all
i\in[k]
, and

(5)

for all

(V_{i},V_{j})
is

(\varepsilon,+)
-regular in

with density either

or at least

.

We refer to the sets

as

, and call

and its vertices as

. The

of

.
The

-regular with density at least

.

It is known that the reduced digraph inherits the minimum semidegree
of

(scaled down to its order). Moreover, Kühn, Osthus, and Treglown
[

]

showed that the reduced digraph also inherits the robust out-expansion property.

Lemma 4.2

(
[

Let

-out-expander on

Let

and
{\{V_{0},\dots,V_{k}\}}
be a regularised digraph
of

.

-reduced digraph of

5.
Robust expansion and skewed-traverses

Given a digraph
D
on
n
vertices, a set
{X\subseteq V(D)}
, and a number
\nu>0

the
\nu
-robust out-neighbourhood
of
X
,
denoted by
\textsc{RN}^{+}_{\nu,D}(X)
, is the set consisting of
all vertices in
D
that receive at least
\nu n
arcs from
vertices in
X
, i.e.

\textsc{RN}^{+}_{\nu,D}(X)=\{x\in V(D):|N^{-}(x)\cap X|\geq\nu n\}.

Recall that
D
is a
robust
(\nu,\tau)
-out-expander
, for some
\tau>0
,
if
|\textsc{RN}^{+}_{\nu,D}(X)|\geq|X|+\nu n
,
for all
X\subseteq V(D)
with
\tau n<|X|<(1-\tau)n
. We define the
notions of
robust in-neighbourhood
and
robust in-expander
analogously.
Finally, we say that
D
is a robust
(\nu,\tau)
-expander
if it is a robust
(\nu,\tau)
-out-expander
and also a robust
(\nu,\tau)
-in-expander. It is known
that robust out-expansion implies robust in-expansion (see Proposition 48 in
[
22
]
).
Moreover, between any pair of vertices in a robust out-expander, there exists a directed path of constant length.

Lemma 5.1

(DeBiasio; Taylor (see Lemma 58 in
[
22
]
))
.

Let
0<\nu\leq\tau\ll\gamma<1
.
Let

be a robust
(\nu,\tau)
-out-expander on
n

\delta^{0}(D)\geq\gamma n
. Then

(1)

is a robust
(\nu^{3},2\tau)
-in-expander, and

(2)

for every pair of distinct vertices

, there is a directed path
of length
{\left\lceil 1/\nu\right\rceil}
from
u
to
v
.

By combining an application of the Diregularity Lemma with a straightforward use of Chernoff’s bound (Lemma
3.2
), Taylor showed (Lemma 60 in
[
22
]
) that every dense robust expander can be split
into two dense robust expanders of approximately equal size. The next lemma generalises this result
to partitions of arbitrary proportions. The proof is an immediate adaptation of Taylor’s and introduces no new ideas,
so we omit the details.

.

Let
0<1/n\ll\nu^{\prime}\ll\nu\leq\tau\ll\tau^{\prime}\ll\mu,\gamma<1

Let

be a digraph on

and suppose that

(\nu,\tau)
-expander. Then
there is a partition
{\{A,B\}}
of

such that

(1)

,

(2)

|N^{\diamond}(v,A)|\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}|A|
for all

and

,

(3)

|N^{\diamond}(v,B)|\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}|B|
for all

and
\diamond\in\{+,-\}
, and

(4)

D[A]
and

are robust
(\nu^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})
-expanders.

6.
Random oriented walks

Let
P=(p_{i})_{i\in[m]}
be an oriented path and let
\diamond_{i}\in\{+,-\}

be such that
p_{i+1}\in N^{\diamond_{i}}(p_{i})
for all
i\in[m-1]
. Given a
digraph

on
k
vertices, the
P
-random walk starting at
{u\in V(H)}
is a random sequence
of vertices
(X_{i})_{i\in[m]}
in
V(H)
such that
{X_{1}=u}
and given
an outcome
{X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell}}
with
{\ell<m}
, we choose
{X_{\ell+1}}

uniformly at random among the vertices of
N^{\diamond_{i}}(X_{\ell})
. We denote
the probability distribution induced by this process by
{\mathbb{P}_{P,u}}

or
\mathbb{P}_{u}
when

is clear from context.

We will show that our host graphs, namely robust expanders of a certain minimum semidegree, have the property that the distribution of the last vertex
X_{m}
of a
P
-random walk tends to a uniform distribution. For this, the following property will be useful.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
{\alpha>0}
and let
D
be a digraph on
k
vertices. For any disjoint sets
{X,Y\subseteq V(D)}

and
\diamond\in\{+,-\}
, we define

\mathcal{C}^{\diamond}(X,Y)=\big\{(x,y,z):x\in X,y\in Y,z\in N^{\diamond}(x)\cap N^{\diamond}(y)\big\}.

We say
D

has the
\alpha
-cherry property
if for every partition
\{X,Y\}
of the
vertices
V(D)
and each

\diamond\in\{+,-\}
,
we have
|{\mathcal{C}^{\diamond}(X,Y)}|\geq\alpha|X||Y|k
.
Moreover, we say that
D
has the
cherry property

if
{\mathcal{C}^{\diamond}(X,Y)}\neq\emptyset
for all
\diamond\in\{+,-\}
.

Let us explain why the cherry property is a natural concept
when considering mixing of random walks. In the
undirected world, it is well known that a random walk in a graph mixes well if the host
graph is regular, connected and non-bipartite, and it is easy to check that a graph
satisfies the (analogous undirected) cherry property if and only if it is
connected and non-bipartite. In direct analogy, we show in Proposition
6.3
that the mixing of the random walk in the directed setting is likely to happen in regular digraph with the cherry property. In Proposition
6.2
we show that a linear minimum semidegree and robust expansion guarantees the existence of a regular subgraph with the cherry property. We now state these two results.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
0<1/k\ll\nu\leq\tau\ll\gamma<1
.
Let
D
be a digraph on
k
vertices
with
{\delta^{0}(D)\geq\gamma k}
and suppose that
D
is a robust
(\nu,\tau)
-expander.
Then there exists
H\subseteq D
that
is regular and has the cherry property.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
H
be a regular digraph on
k
vertices that
satisfies the cherry property. Moreover, let
{P=(v_{i})_{i\in[m]}}
be an oriented

{u\in V(H)}
, the

-random walk
{(X_{i})_{i\in[m]}}
starting at

\max_{v\in V(H)}\left|\mathbb{P}_{u}(X_{m}=v)-\frac{1}{k}\right|\leq\exp\left(-\frac{m}{4k^{5}}\right).

in Subsection

and Proposition

6.1.
Finding a regular subgraph with the cherry property

We show the existence of the subgraph

of

from Proposition
6.2
in three steps: first we show

has the cherry property, then we show a random subgraph of

has the cherry property since this property is monotone.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
0<\nu\leq\tau\ll\gamma<1
. Let

be a robust
(\nu,\tau)
-expander
with
{\delta^{0}(D)\geq\gamma k}
.
Then

has the
2\nu^{3}
-cherry property.

Proof.

Let
\{X,Y\}
be a
partition of
V(D)
, and let
{\diamond\in\{+,-\}}
. We can assume that
{|X|\geq|Y|}
.
First assume that
|Y|>\tau k
. Since

is a robust
(\nu,\tau)
-expander, we have

|\textsc{RN}_{\nu}^{\diamond}(X)|+|\textsc{RN}_{\nu}^{\diamond}(Y)|\geq|X|+|Y|+2\nu k=(1+2\nu)k.

|\textsc{RN}_{\nu}^{\diamond}(X)\cap\textsc{RN}_{\nu}^{\diamond}(Y)|\geq 2\nu k.

By considering each vertex in

Now, assume that
{|Y|\leq\tau n\leq\gamma k/2}
. Let

, and choose an arbitrary
{y\in Y}

and thus

∎

Given a digraph
D
and

the random subgraph of

obtained by selecting each arc independently with probability

.
We next show that if

is a digraph satisfying the cherry property robustly,
then

also satisfies the cherry property for a wide range of values of

,
via a straightforward application of Janson’s inequality.

Lemma 6.5
.

For every
\alpha=\alpha(k)\in(0,1]
and
p=p(k)\in(0,1)

satisfying
{\alpha^{2}pk/\log k\rightarrow\infty}
as
k\rightarrow\infty
,
the following holds.
If
D
is digraph on
k
vertices with the
\alpha
-cherry property then with
high probability
D_{p}
has the
\alpha p^{2}/2
-cherry property.

Proof.

Throughout this proof, for a partition
\{X,Y\}
of
V(D)
and
\diamond\in\{+,-\}
,
we write
\mathcal{C}^{\diamond}(X,Y)
and
\mathcal{C}_{p}^{\diamond}(X,Y)
with the index signaling whether we are in
D

or
D_{p}
.
First, fix a partition
\{X,Y\}
of
V(D)
with
|X|\leq|Y|
, and
let
\diamond\in\{+,-\}
. The variable
Z^{\diamond}_{X,Y}=|\mathcal{C}_{p}^{\diamond}(X,Y)|
is then a sum of
indicator functions for each cherry in
\mathcal{C}^{\diamond}(X,Y)
.
As

has the
\alpha
-cherry property, we have

\alpha|X||Y|k\leq|\mathcal{C}^{\diamond}(X,Y)|\leq|X||Y|k
,
and consequently,

(3)

\alpha p^{2}|X||Y|k\leq\mathbb{E}[Z^{\diamond}_{X,Y}]\leq p^{2}|X||Y|k.

We aim to apply Janson’s inequality (Lemma
3.3
) for estimate of the lower tail of
Z^{\diamond}_{X,Y}
.
For this, define
\Delta
as the expectation of pairs of distinct cherries in
\mathcal{C}_{p}^{\diamond}(X,Y)
that have one arc in common.
The terms which contribute to
\Delta
come from

(x,y_{1},y_{2},z)
with
(x,y_{i},z)\in\mathcal{C}_{p}^{\diamond}(X,Y)

for

, or

{(x_{i},y,z)\in\mathcal{C}_{p}^{\diamond}(X,Y)}

for

.
Therefore we have that

\Delta\leq p^{3}|X|^{2}|Y|k+p^{3}|X||Y|^{2}k\leq p^{3}|X||Y|k^{2},

since
|X|+|Y|=k
. Note that the
\alpha
-cherry property can only hold for

and therefore
{pk\geq\alpha^{2}pk\gg\log k}
for sufficiently large

, which entails that

\mathbb{E}[Z^{\diamond}_{X,Y}]\overset{\eqref{eq:exp_Z}}{\leq}p^{2}|X||Y|k\leq p^{3}|X||Y|k^{2}.

So Janson’s inequality with

implies that

\mathbb{P}\left(Z^{\diamond}_{X,Y}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z^{\diamond}_{X,Y}]}{2}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(\alpha p^{2}|X||Y|k)^{2}}{8(\mathbb{E}[Z^{\diamond}_{X,Y}]+\Delta)}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}p|X||Y|}{16}\right).

Taking the union bound over all
X
with at most
k/2
vertices, and
\diamond\in\{+,-\}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(D_{p}\text{ does not have the }\alpha p^{2}/2\text{-cherry property})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\diamond\in\{+,-\}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\cup Y=V(D)\\
|X|\leq{\left\lfloor k/2\right\rfloor}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\Big(Z^{\diamond}_{X,Y}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z^{\diamond}_{X,Y}]}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\binom{k}{i}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}pi(k-i)}{16}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\exp\left(i\log k-\frac{\alpha^{2}pik}{32}\right)

\displaystyle\leq k\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}pk}{64}\right)

which tends to
0
since
\alpha^{2}pk/\log k\rightarrow\infty
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.
∎

Lemma 6.6

Let

Let

We will construct a family

of arc-disjoint

Suppose we already defined

Let

, for all

We claim

contains a set of pairwise vertex-disjoint directed paths
{P_{1},\dots,P_{m}}

of length

.
To see this, note that for each

-expander. This holds

).
Then
{C_{i}=M_{i}\cup\bigcup_{j\in[\ell]}P_{j}}

Moreover, since

6.2.
From the random directed walk to the uniform distribution

In this subsection we prove Proposition
6.3
, which measures
how close a random directed walk gets to the uniform distribution. For the sake of explanation, consider an oriented path

P=(p_{i})_{i\in[m]}
, a vertex
u
, and the
P
-random walk starting at
u
. Moreover let
f(v)=\mathbb{P}_{u}(X_{i})
. In the next result we show that, in
a digraph with the cherry property, the maximum difference of
f
among a pair of
vertices is to some extent achieved by a pair of vertices with a common
out(in)-neighbor. So if
X_{i}
gets to that common neighbor, then the probabilities of those said vertices being reached at step
i+1
will get closer to each other.

An interesting aspect of regular digraphs is that if you choose a
vertex
u
uniformly at random and a vertex
v
uniformly at random in
N^{+}(u)
,
then the distribution of
v
itself is uniform. This simple fact greatly simplifies the analysis of the proof of Proposition
6.3
because it gives us a clean way of describing how close
X_{i}
is to the uniform distribution in terms of how close
X_{i-1}
is. We couple that intuition with an analysis of the
L_{2}
-distance of
X_{i}
to the uniform distribution and a convenient Cauchy-Schwarz type argument, where the cherry property appears.
We remark that a similar proof strategy was used by Mycroft and Naia
[
20
]
.

Proof of Proposition
6.3
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that
H
is
d
-regular.
Let
P
be given as in the proposition, and
let
\diamond_{i},\circ_{i}\in\{+,-\}
be such that
p_{i+1}\in N^{\diamond_{i}}(p_{i})
and
p_{i-1}\in N^{\circ_{i}}(p_{i})
for all suitable
i
.
Let
(X_{i})_{i\in[m]}
be the given
P
-random walk starting at
u
.
We wish to bound the distance from the distribution of
X_{i}
to the
uniform distribution, and will employ the squared euclidean
distance to this aim. So for each
i\in[m]
, set

m(X_{i})=\sum_{w\in V(H)}\left(\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=w)-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{2}.

Let
2\leq i\leq m
.
For each
{v\in V(H)}
let
f(v)=\mathbb{P}(X_{i-1}=v)-\frac{1}{k}
.
As
H
is
d
-regular, we have

\displaystyle m(X_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in V(H)}\left(\left(\sum_{v\in N^{\circ_{i}}(w)}\frac{\mathbb{P}(X_{i-1}=v)}{d}\right)-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in V(H)}\left(\sum_{v\in N^{\circ_{i}}(w)}\frac{1}{d}f(v)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38092pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Prop \ref{prop:cauchy}}}}{{=}}\sum_{w\in V(H)}\left(\sum_{v\in N^{\circ_{i}}(w)}\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{v\in N^{\circ_{i}}(w)}f(v)^{2}-\frac{1}{2d^{2}}\sum_{u,v\in N^{\circ_{i}}(w)}(f(u)-f(v))^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in V(H)\\
v\in N^{\circ_{i}}(w)\end{subarray}}f(v)^{2}-\frac{1}{2d^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in V(H)\\
u,v\in N^{\circ_{i}}(w)\end{subarray}}(f(u)-f(v))^{2}.

Observe that in the first term of the last line of the above equation, we are summing
f(v)^{2}

exactly
d
times for each
v\in V(H)
, and divide the result by
d
, so this term equals
\sum_{v\in V(H)}f(v)^{2}=m(X_{i-1})
. Thus

(4)

m(X_{i})=m(X_{i-1})-\frac{1}{2d^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in V(H)\\
u,v\in N^{\circ_{i}}(w)\end{subarray}}(f(u)-f(v))^{2}.

Now, let
M=\max_{u,v\in V(D)}|f(u)-f(v)|
. We will use the fact
that
H
has the cherry property to prove that the sum on the right hand side
of (
4
) is at least
{\left(\frac{M}{k}\right)^{2}}
.
So, let
(v_{i})_{i\in[k]}
be a non-decreasing ordering of
V(H)

according to the values of
f
. Note
that
{M=f(v_{k})-f(v_{1})=\sum_{i\in[k-1]}(f(v_{i+1})-f(v_{i}))}
.
Hence, there exists
j\in[k-1]
such that
f(v_{j+1})-f(v_{j})\geq M/(k-1)

and consequently
f(v_{\ell})-f(v_{i})\geq M/(k-1)
for every
{i,\ell\in[k-1]}

with
i\leq j<\ell
. Now let
X=\{v_{1},...,v_{j}\}
and
Y=V(H)\setminus X
.
Since
H
has the cherry property, there exist
u\in X
,
v\in Y
and

w\in V(H)
such that
u,v\in N^{\circ_{i}}(w)
. So,

(5)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in V(H)\\
u,v\in N^{\circ_{i}}(w)\end{subarray}}(f(u)-f(v))^{2}\geq\left(\frac{M}{k-1}\right)^{2}\geq\left(\frac{M}{k}\right)^{2}.

We claim that
M\geq\max_{v\in V(H)}|f(v)|
. Indeed, if
f
is
identical to
0
this holds trivially. Otherwise,
let
{u\in V(H)}
such that
|f(u)|=\max_{v\in V(H)}|f(v)|
.
Given that
{\sum_{v\in V(H)}f(v)=0}
, there exists
{u^{\prime}\in V(H)}

with
f(u^{\prime})
having opposite sign of
f(u)
.
Hence,
{M\geq|f(u)-f(u^{\prime})|\geq|f(u)|=\max_{v\in V(H)}|f(v)|}
.
Furthermore, by an averaging argument, there
exists
u\in V(H)
such that
f(u)^{2}\geq m(X_{i-1})/k
.
Thus,
M\geq\max_{v\in V(H)}|f(v)|\geq\sqrt{m(X_{i-1})/k}
.
Combining all of this with (
4
) and (
5
), we get

m(X_{i})\leq m(X_{i-1})-\frac{M^{2}}{2d^{2}k^{2}}\leq m(X_{i-1})-\frac{m(X_{i-1})}{2d^{2}k^{3}}\leq m(X_{i-1})\left(1-\frac{1}{2k^{5}}\right)

and consequently

m(X_{i})\leq m(X_{1})\left(1-\frac{1}{2k^{5}}\right)^{m-1}\leq m(X_{1})\exp\left(-\frac{m}{4k^{5}}\right),

where we used that
1-x\leq e^{-x}
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
in the last inequality.
Notice that
X_{1}
is deterministically equal to
u
. Therefore,

m(X_{1})=\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{2}+\frac{k-1}{k^{2}}<1.

We finish the proof by deducing that

\max_{v\in V(H)}\left|\mathbb{P}(X_{m}=v)-\frac{1}{k}\right|\leq m(X_{m})\leq\exp\left(-\frac{m}{4k^{5}}\right).

∎

7.
Semi-random assignment

Let
T
,
H
be oriented graphs. An
assignment
of
T
to
H

is a mapping
{\varphi:V(T)\rightarrow V(H)}
that preserves arcs,
i.e.
{xy\in A(T)}
implies
{\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\in A(H)}
. An assignment
is also called a homomorphism in other contexts.
Given a vertex
{v\in V(H)}
, a set
{S\subseteq V(T)}
and an assignment
\varphi

of
T
to
H
, we are interested in the quantity
|\varphi^{-1}(v)\cap S|
when
T
is a tree and
H
is a regular digraph with
the cherry property. In the proof of our main result,
H
is the reduced digraph and we use
the assignment to guide the actual embedding.

A natural first approach would be to perform a randomised greedy embedding and use the mixing property of the random walks to show that each cluster of the reduced digraph is assigned roughly the same amount of vertices. This would indeed be sufficient for embedding almost spanning trees. However, since we are working with spanning trees, we need to assign an exact amount of vertices to each cluster, and we also need to deal with the exceptional vertices of the regular partition. For this reason we will work with a semi-random assignment. This will have enough randomness to obtain a roughly balanced assignment and enough structure to correct the imbalance.

We call any ordering of the vertices of a rooted tree where each vertex is preceded by its parent a
top-down ordering
.

8.3.
Preliminary assignment.

We will use Proposition
7.2

to find a preliminary assignment of the tree
T_{B}
into the reduced graph
R_{B}
.
This initial assignment ensures that almost all the
vertices of
T_{B}
are well-distributed across the vertices of the reduced graph.
Furthermore, it has the property that some special subtrees of
T_{B}
, selected
according to the type of
T
, are well-distributed along the arcs of the
Hamilton cycle
H_{B}
. This prepares the ground for the
later incorporation of the exceptional vertices into the assignment, the
correction of its imperfections, and the embedding of
T_{B}
.

More precisely,
based on the type of
T
,
we define two collections of subtrees
T_{B}
, denoted by
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{R}
.
The collection
\mathcal{P}
will be used in the embedding of
T_{B}
, while

\mathcal{R}
will be used later to incorporate the
exceptional vertices, and to adjust the initial assignment of
T_{B}
.
We first describe how these sets are selected according to the type of
T
,
and then show how to apply Proposition
7.2
to
obtain the desired preliminary assignment.

Leafy trees.

If
T
is leafy, then we let

\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{R}
consist of a collection of subtrees induced by a set of
leaves and their parents, defined as follows. We say that a leaf in an oriented
tree is an
out-leaf
if it has out-degree equal to one, and an

if it has in-degree equal to one.
For each
{\diamond\in\{+,-\}}

let

of all
\diamond
-leaves of

Let

be such that

\mathcal{S}
of
2
-vertex subtrees formed by the set
P
of parents of leaves in
L^{\circ}

together with one of their leaves in
L^{\circ}
. Then
{|\mathcal{S}|\geq\lambda n/(2\ell\Delta)}
. Being a subset of a tree,
the set

with

.
Let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
consist of all trees from
\mathcal{S}
that meet

.
Then the leaves in trees of

are pairwise at distance

4
. Moreover, let

\mathcal{P}
be any set of size
{\left\lceil|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|/2\right\rceil}
of

, and set
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{S^{\prime}}\setminus\mathcal{P}

Then, we have

(19)

|\mathcal{P}|\geq|\mathcal{R}|\geq\left\lfloor\frac{|\mathcal{S}^{\prime}|}{2}\right\rfloor\geq\left\lfloor\frac{|\mathcal{S}|}{4}\right\rfloor\geq\frac{\lambda n}{8\ell\Delta}-1\geq\frac{\lambda n}{16\ell\Delta}\gg\delta n.

Bare trees.
Assume that
T
is bare. In this
case, the sets
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{R}
consist of
a collection of vertex-disjoint directed bare paths defined as follows.
Let

the tree

contains a set of

Splitting each of these paths into

and ignoring the first one,
we obtain a set of paths on
p

at distance at least

We then distribute
these subpaths evenly between the sets
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{R}
, discarding one of them if necessary.
Then

|\mathcal{P}|=|\mathcal{R}|\geq\frac{\lambda n}{4\ell}\Big({\left\lfloor\frac{\ell}{p}\right\rfloor}-1\Big)-1\geq\frac{\lambda n}{10p}\gg\frac{\delta n}{p}.

Switchy trees.
If

is switchy, then

\mathcal{P}

4

By (T3), the tree

We delete some of the members of

4

Now that the collections

and

By Proposition

8.5.
Obtaining a balanced assignment.

We now adjust the assignment
\varphi^{\prime}
to obtain a perfectly balanced
assignment. For this, we will reassign some vertices from the
subtrees of
\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}
. At the end of this process, we will have obtained a new
assignment
{\varphi^{\prime\prime}:V(T_{B})\rightarrow V(R_{B})\cup U_{0}}
such that the
vertices of
T_{B}
are perfectly balanced across
{V(R_{B})\cup U_{0}}
.

We start by setting
{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(v)=\varphi^{\prime}(v)}
,
for every
v\in V(T_{B})
, and
\mathcal{R}^{*}=\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}
. Then,
we choose clusters
V_{i^{>}}
and
V_{i^{<}}
such
that

|(\varphi^{\prime\prime})^{-1}(V_{i^{>}})|>|V_{i^{>}}|\quad\text{and}\quad|(\varphi^{\prime\prime})^{-1}(V_{i^{<}})|<|V_{i^{<}}|.

These clusters exist unless the assignment
\varphi^{\prime\prime}
is already perfectly balanced (in which case we stop). Next,
we describe how to decrease the number of vertices assigned
to
V_{i^{>}}
by one and increase the number assigned
to
V_{i^{<}}
by one, without affecting the number of
vertices assigned to the other clusters.
Throughout the process, we maintain the property that at most
one vertex from
V(\mathcal{R}^{*})
in each cluster is reassigned.

The process to
improve the assignment depends on the type of
T
.

Leafy and switchy trees.
If
T
is leafy or switchy,
we reassign certain leaves or switches to adjust
the assignment. Suppose first that the root of each tree in
\mathcal{R}^{*}
is an out-vertex in this tree.
If there exists an arc
from
V_{i^{>}-1}
to
V_{i^{<}}
, we can simply reassign
one out-leaf or out-switch vertex assigned from
V_{i^{>}}

to
V_{i^{<}}
(and leave all other assignment as they are). However, this arc may not exist and therefore, we again use skewed-traverses. Let

W_{1}=\Big((V_{i^{>}-1},V_{i_{1}}),(V_{i_{1}-1},V_{i_{2}}),\dots,(V_{i_{t}-1},V_{i^{<}})\Big)

be a
(V_{i^{>}-1},V_{i^{<}})
-skewed-traverse of
length
t\leq\lceil 2/\nu^{\prime}\rceil+1
,
which exists by Lemma
5.3
. For convenience, let
i_{0}=i^{>}-1
,
and
i_{t+1}=i^{<}
. Then, for each
{j\in[t]}
, we select one
vertex
{v\in\textsc{Root}(\mathcal{R}^{*})}
which is assigned to
V_{i_{j-1}}
by
\varphi^{\prime\prime}
.
Observe that
such a vertex
v
always exists. Indeed, by (
22
), and since we reassign only one
vertex from
{(\varphi^{\prime\prime})^{-1}(V_{i_{j-1}})\cap\textsc{Root}(\mathcal{R}^{*})}
,
for each pair of indices
(i^{>},i^{<})
, and the number of such pairs is bounded above
by
8\varepsilon_{A}k_{B}n
, we have
|(\varphi^{\prime\prime})^{-1}(V_{i_{j-1}})\cap\textsc{Root}(\mathcal{R}^{*})|>0
.

By (A6’), the child
y
of
v
is assigned
to
V_{i_{j-1}+1}
by
\varphi^{\prime\prime}
.
Hence, by setting
\varphi^{\prime\prime}(y)=V_{i_{j}}

we decrease the number of vertices assigned
to
V_{i_{j-1}+1}
by one, and increase the number assigned
to
V_{i_{j}}
by one. Moreover, we remove from
\mathcal{R}^{*}
the subtree whose
root is
x
.
For an illustration, see Figure
3
.
Note that at the end, in each cluster we reassigned at most one vertex
from
V(\mathcal{R}^{*})
.
Furthermore, the number of vertices
assigned to
V_{i^{>}}
decreased by one and the number of
vertices assigned to
V_{i^{<}}
increased by one.

The procedure is analogous if the root of each tree in
\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}
is an in-vertex
in this tree.

Bare trees.
If
T
is bare,
we use the paths in
\mathcal{R}^{*}
to balance the assignment. First,
assume that the paths in
\mathcal{R}^{*}
start with an out-vertex. We choose
a vertex
x\in\textsc{Root}(\mathcal{R}^{*})
that is the starting vertex of a path
Q=x_{0}x_{1}\dots x_{p-1}\in\mathcal{R}^{*}
,
such that
x_{0}
is assigned to
V_{i^{>}-1}
. By similar calculations
as in the leafy and neutral case, we can show that such a path
Q

always exists.
If there is both an arc from
V_{i^{>}-1}
to
V_{i^{<}}
, and an arc
from
V_{i^{<}}
to
V_{i^{>}+1}
, then we can reassign
the second vertex
x_{1}
of
Q
(which by (A6’) is currently assigned to
V_{i^{>}}
) to
V_{i^{<}}
, thus decreasing the number of vertices assigned to
V_{i^{>}}
by
one and increasing the number assigned to
V_{i^{<}}
by one, without
affecting the other clusters.
However, one or both of these arcs may be absent, and so,
we again use skewed-traverses.
Let

W_{1}=\Big((V_{i^{>}-1},V_{i_{1}}),\dots,(V_{i_{t_{1}}-1},V_{i^{<}})\Big)\quad\text{and}\quad W_{2}=\Big((V_{i^{<}},V_{i^{\prime}_{1}}),\dots,(V_{i^{\prime}_{t_{2}}-1},V_{i^{>}+1})\Big)

be a
(V_{i^{>}-1},V_{i^{<}})
-skewed-traverse of length
t_{1}
and a

(V_{i^{<}},V_{i^{>}+1})
-skewed-traverse of length
t_{2}
,
respectively, with
t_{1},t_{2}\leq\lceil 2/\nu^{\prime}\rceil+1
.
We start by reassigning the vertices
{x_{1},\dots,x_{t_{1}k_{B}}}

in a way that every cluster receives the same amount of these vertices.
Namely, for each
j\in[t_{1}]
, we reassign the
path
x_{1+(j-1)k_{B}},\dots,x_{jk_{B}}
to the Hamiltonian path
induced by
V_{i_{j}},\dots,V_{i_{j}-1}
. We then assign
x_{1+t_{1}k_{B}}

to
V_{i^{<}}
. Notice that by doing this we decreased the
number of vertices assigned to
V_{i^{>}}
and increased the number of
vertices assigned to
V_{i^{<}}
by one, without affecting the other clusters.
However, this new assignment is not valid. To fix this, we reassign
the vertices
x_{t_{1}k_{B}+1,\dots,(t_{1}+t_{2})k_{B}}
as we did before,
but using
W_{2}
. Then we obtain a valid assignment,
because
x_{(t_{1}+t_{2})k_{B}+1}
, by (A6’), is assigned to
V_{i^{>}+1}
,
the vertex
x_{(t_{1}+t_{2})k_{B}}

is now assigned to
V_{i^{\prime}_{t_{2}}-1}
, and
(V_{i^{\prime}_{t_{2}}-1},V_{i+1})\in W_{2}
.
Moreover, note that the reassignment of
x_{t_{1}k_{B}+1},\dots,x_{(t_{1}+t_{2})k_{B}}

does not affect the number of vertices assigned to each cluster.
Therefore, we obtained a new assignment such that the number of vertices
assigned to
V_{i^{>}}
decreased by one, and the number of vertices
assigned to
V_{i^{<}}
increased by one.

The procedure is analogous when the paths in
\mathcal{R}^{*}
start with an in-vertex.

By repeating this procedure until there is no pair of clusters
V_{i^{>}}

and
V_{i^{<}}
with
{|(\varphi^{\prime\prime})^{-1}\cap V_{i^{>}}|>|V_{i^{>}}|}

and
{|(\varphi^{\prime\prime})^{-1}(V_{i^{<}})\cap V_{i^{<}}|<|V_{i^{<}}|}
, we obtain
an assignment

\varphi^{\prime\prime}:V(T_{B})\rightarrow V(R_{B})\cup U_{0}

such that, for all
i\in[k_{B}]
, the following holds.

(A1”)

|(\varphi^{\prime\prime})^{-1}(V_{i})\cap\textsc{Root}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})|=\frac{|\mathcal{P}^{\prime}|}{k_{B}}\pm n^{1-\beta}
,

(A2”)

|(\varphi^{\prime\prime})^{-1}(V_{i})\cap V(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})|=\frac{|V(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})|}{k_{B}}\pm n^{1-\beta}
,

(A3”)

|(\varphi^{\prime\prime})^{-1}(V_{i})|=|V_{i}|
, and

(A4”)

every arc of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is assigned to an arc of
H_{B}
.

8.6.
Embedding of
T
.

Our final goal is to embed
T
in
D
. First we will embed
T_{B}
in
D_{B}
, and then
we will embed
T_{A}
in
D_{A}
.

Embedding of
T_{B}
.
We start by setting the stage for the application
of the Blow-up Lemma. Recall that
U_{0},V_{1},\dots,V_{k_{B}}
is a partition
of
V(D_{B})
such that all
(V_{i},V_{j})
are
\varepsilon_{B}
-regular with density
0
or
d\pm\varepsilon_{B}
,
and all
(V_{i},V_{i+1})
are
(\varepsilon_{B},d)
-super-regular.
Also recall that in the incorporation
of
U_{0}
(see (
21
)), for each
u\in U_{0}

we
chose
V_{i_{v}^{+}}
and
V_{i_{v}^{-}}
with

|N^{+}_{G_{B}}(u,V_{i^{+}})|\geq\frac{\gamma|V_{i^{+}}|}{8}\quad\text{and}\quad|N^{-}_{G_{B}}(u,V_{i^{-}})|\geq\frac{\gamma|V_{i^{-}}|}{8}.

We apply the Blow-up Lemma (Lemma
4.3
) with

\Delta,\quad K_{1}=K_{2}=1,\quad K_{3}=2,\quad\text{and}\quad c=\frac{\gamma}{8}.

The constants
\varepsilon_{B}
and
\varepsilon^{*}
will play the role of
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
.
Note that we have the following hierarchy of constants

0<\varepsilon_{B}\ll\varepsilon^{*}\ll\delta\ll d\ll\Delta^{-1},K_{1}^{-1},K_{2}^{-1},K_{3}^{-1},c.

Let
D_{B}^{\prime}
be the digraph obtained by removing, for each
v\in U_{0}
,
all arcs incident to
v
except those going to
V_{i_{v}^{+}}
or coming
from
V_{i_{v}^{-}}
. In addition, we remove all arcs between pairs of clusters
(V_{i},V_{j})

for which
(V_{i},V_{j})
is not an arc of
R_{B}
.
In the application of the Blow-up Lemma, the underlying
graph of
D_{B}^{\prime}
plays the role of
D
, and the tree
T_{B}
plays the role
of
H
. The set
U_{0}
will correspond to the exceptional set
V_{0}
.
The partition
L_{0},\dots,L_{k_{B}}
of
V(T_{B})
is the partition induced
by the assignment
\varphi^{\prime\prime}
.
Furthermore, we set
\psi(v)=\varphi^{\prime\prime}(v)
, for all
{v\in U_{0}}
,
and
I=L_{0}\cup V(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
.

With the stage prepared, we now verify that conditions (C1)–(C9)
of the Blow-up Lemma (Lemma
4.3
) hold for this setting. By (
18
), we have

|L_{0}|=|U_{0}|\leq 8\varepsilon_{A}n_{B}\leq dn_{B}
,
and thus (C1) is satisfied. Condition (C2) holds
by the definition of
I
. Conditions (C3), (C6), and (C7) hold by the
construction of
\varphi^{\prime\prime}
. Condition (C4) holds because
\varphi^{\prime\prime}
assigns
to
U_{0}
only vertices of in- or out-degree at most 2.
Therefore, for all
i\in[k_{B}]
,

|N_{T_{B}}(L_{0})\cap L_{i}|\leq 2|L_{0}|\leq 14\varepsilon_{A}n_{B}\leq d|V_{i}|.

We now verify condition (C5). The choice of the sets
D_{i}
,
for every
i\in[k_{B}]
, depends on the type of
T
.

Leafy trees.
If
T
is leafy, then for
each
i\in[k_{B}]
we select a collection
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}_{i}
of
\delta|V_{i}|/2
subtrees
of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
whose root was assigned to
V_{i}
. We
set
D=\bigcup_{i\in[k_{B}]}V(\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}_{i})
, and for each
i\in[k_{B}]
we
set
{D_{i}=D\cap L_{i}}
. Since
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
consists of leaves and their parents,
by (A6”), we have
|D_{i}|=\delta|V_{i}|
, for every
{i\in[k_{B}]}
.
So, for every
{i\in[k_{B}]}
we have

|N_{T_{B}}(D)\cap L_{i}|=|N_{T_{B}}(D_{i-1})\cap L_{i}|+|N_{T_{B}}(D_{i+1})\cap L_{i}|=\delta|V_{i}|.

Consequently,

\big||N_{T_{B}}(D)\cap L_{i}|-|N_{T_{B}}(D)\cap L_{j}|\big|=0<\varepsilon_{B}|V_{i}|
for every
1\leq i<j\leq k_{B}
, and thus (C5) holds.

Bare trees.
If
T
is bare, then, for
each
i\in[k_{B}]
, we select a collection
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}_{i}
of

\frac{\delta|V_{i}|}{pk_{B}}
subtrees of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
whose root was
assigned to
V_{i}
.
We set
D=\bigcup_{i\in[k_{B}]}V(\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}_{i})
,
and, for each
i\in[k_{B}]
, we set
D_{i}=D\cap L_{i}
. Since every subtree
in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is a directed path of length
p
, and by (A6”)
these paths wind around the Hamilton cycle
H_{B}
, it follows
that
{|D_{i}|=\delta|V_{i}|}
, for every
{i\in[k_{B}]}
. Moreover, for
every
{i\in[k_{B}]}
,

|N_{T_{B}}(D)\cap L_{i}|=|N_{T_{B}}(D_{i-1})\cap L_{i}|+|N_{T_{B}}(D_{i+1})\cap L_{i}|=\delta|V_{i}|,

so again, condition (C5) holds.

Switchy trees.
If
T
is switchy, then, for
each
i\in[k_{B}]
, select a collection
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}_{i}
of
\frac{\delta|V_{i}|}{4}

subtrees of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
whose root was assigned to
V_{i}
.
We set
D=\bigcup_{i\in[k_{B}]}V(\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}_{i})
,
and, for each
i\in[k_{B}]
, we set
{D_{i}=D\cap L_{i}}
. Since
all paths in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime\prime}
have the same orientation and length
4
, and
they wind around
H_{B}
, by (A6”), it follows that
|D_{i}|=\delta|V_{i}|
, for
all
i\in[k_{B}]
. Moreover, they are perfectly distributed over
H_{B}
.
Therefore, for every
i\in[k_{B}]
,

|N_{T_{B}}(D)\cap L_{i}|=|N_{T_{B}}(D_{i-1})\cap L_{i}|+|N_{T_{B}}(D_{i+1})\cap L_{i}|=\delta|V_{i}|,

and hence, condition (C5) holds.

It only remains to check conditions (C8) and (C9). For
i\in[k_{B}]
, let
{E_{i}\subseteq V_{i}}

with
|E_{i}|=\varepsilon^{*}|V_{i}|
be given. Since
|I|=|L_{0}|+|V(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})|\gg\delta n
,
we can choose an arbitrary set
{F_{i}\subseteq L_{i}\cap(I\setminus D)}

consisting of leaves or internal vertices of paths
with
|F_{i}|=|E_{i}|
. Let
{\psi_{i}:E_{i}\rightarrow F_{i}}
be
any bijection. To verify that (C8) holds for this choice, let
v\in E_{i}
,
and let
j\in[k_{B}]
such that
L_{j}
contains a neighbour of
\psi_{i}(v)
.
By (A6”), all edges incident to
F_{i}
are assigned to
the Hamilton cycle
H_{B}
. Therefore,
(V_{i},V_{j})
is

(\varepsilon_{B},d)
-super-regular. Consequently,
{|N_{D_{B}^{*}}(v)\cap V_{j}|\geq(d-\varepsilon_{B})|V_{j}|}
, and (C8) holds.
Finally, let
F=\bigcup_{i\in[k_{B}]}F_{i}
. As the vertices in
F_{i}

have degree at most
2
, it follows that

|N_{T_{B}}(F)\cap L_{i}|\leq 2|E_{i}|\leq 2\varepsilon^{*}|V_{i}|

and so (C9) holds with
{K_{3}=2}
.

Since conditions (C1)–(C9) are satisfied, the Blow-up Lemma (Lemma
4.3
) gives
a copy of
T_{B}
in
D_{B}^{*}
. By the construction of
\varphi^{\prime\prime}
, this copy
corresponds to an oriented copy of
T_{B}
in
D_{B}
.

8.7.
Embedding of
T_{A}
.

To finish the embedding of
T
, we
need to embed
T_{A}
into
D_{A}
. Let
{v\in V(D_{B})}
be the vertex that hosts
the root
r_{B}
of
T_{B}
.
As
{|N^{\pm}_{G}(v,A)|\geq\frac{\gamma}{4}|A|}
, by (D2), there
are
U_{i_{v}^{+}}
and
U_{i_{v^{-}}}
with

|N^{+}(v,U_{i_{v}^{+}})|\geq\frac{\gamma|U_{i_{v}^{+}}|}{8}\quad\text{and}\quad|N^{-}(v,U_{i_{v}^{-}})|\geq\frac{\gamma|U_{i_{v}^{-}}|}{8}.

If
r_{A}
is an in-neighbour of
r_{B}
, we find a preliminary
assignment
{\varphi_{A}:V(T_{A})\rightarrow V(R_{A})}
as we did
for
T_{B}
, with the additional requirement that
\varphi_{A}(r_{A})=U_{i_{v}^{-}}
.
Otherwise, i.e. if
r_{B}
is in an out-neighbour of
r_{B}
, we
ensure that
\varphi_{A}(r_{A})=U_{i_{v}^{+}}
. Note that in either case
the desired assignment exists because
the root of
T_{A}
was assigned uniformly
at random in a cluster of
R_{A}
(see Definition
7.1
(2)).
Since
D_{A}
has no exceptional vertices, we can skip the incorporation
of exceptional vertices step and obtain a perfectly balanced assignment,
just as we did for
T_{B}
. This is possible because the total imbalance
of the preliminary assignment
\varphi_{A}
is
\sum_{i\in[k_{A}]}\big||\varphi_{A}^{-1}(U_{i})|-|U_{i}|\big|\leq k_{A}n^{1-\beta}\ll\varepsilon_{A}n_{A}
.
Now, let
D_{A}^{\prime}
be the digraph obtained from
D_{A}
by adding

v
and all the arcs from
v
to
U_{i^{+}}
and from
U_{i^{-}}
to
v
. Moreover,
we remove all arcs between pairs of clusters
(U_{i},U_{j})
for which
(U_{i},U_{j})
is not an arc of
R_{A}
.
We then finish by
applying the Blow-up Lemma with the underlying graph of
D_{A}^{\prime}
playing the role of
D

and treating the root
r_{B}
as an exceptional vertex, i.e. we set
L_{0}=\{r_{B}\}
and

\psi(r_{B})=v
.
Hence, we find a copy of
T_{A}
in
D_{A}
, thus completing the embedding
of
T
into
D
.
