Research progress and trends on DNA methylation research in non-small cell lung cancer: a bibliometric analysis
Authors: Mengmeng Chen, Yaming Liu, Fengfeng Xu, Jincan Zhang, Congwen Zhuang, Meiqing Zhang, Kaixiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDNA methylation plays a key role in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) pathogenesis. This study aimed to explore the global research landscape and frontier trends of DNA methylation and NSCLC through bibliometric analysis.MethodsA literature search was conducted in the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC), focusing on articles published from January 2008 to April 2025. The bibliometric analysis utilized VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and the R package “bibliometrix” to visualize collaborations, keyword co-occurrences, and emerging research trends.ResultsThe analysis identified 573 articles authored by 4,356 researchers across 2,524 institutions in 174 countries. China (n=288), the USA (n=77), and South Korea (n=33) were the countries with the highest number of published articles, with Nanjing Medical University in China (n=74) emerging as the leading institution. Clinical Cancer Research had the highest total link strength in co-occurrence networks. High-impact authors included Esteller Manel and Herman James G. Keyword clustering identified seven major themes: Hypermethylation in NSCLC, DNA methylation in therapy against NSCLC, DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) in NSCLC, DNA methylation as a biomarker in early diagnosis for NSCLC, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in NSCLC, cigarette smoke-related abnormal DNA methylation, and DNA methylation in other cancers. Notably, since 2022, the emergence of “activation” and “transcription” continued through to 2025.ConclusionsThis bibliometric analysis highlights the important role of DNA methylation in NSCLC. Moving forward, research efforts should focus on elucidating the specific molecular mechanisms within the broader epigenetic network of NSCLC, rather than studying DNA methylation in isolation. Such an approach may enhance the efficacy of therapies targeting epigenetic regulators and modifiers.

Key findings

What is known and what is new?

• DNA methylation is a well-established epigenetic mechanism in NSCLC pathogenesis, with recognized roles in gene silencing, early diagnosis, and therapeutic targeting.

• This comprehensive bibliometric study [2008–2025] maps the global research landscape, identifying leading countries (China, USA, South Korea), institutions (Nanjing Medical University), and influential authors. Seven major research clusters are delineated, revealing the intellectual structure of the field. Notably, emerging keywords “activation” and “transcription” [2022–2025] signal a paradigm shift from descriptive methylation patterns toward mechanistic investigations of epigenetic regulation.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Future research should transition from analyzing single-gene methylation to investigating the complex epigenetic networks and dynamic transcriptional regulation in NSCLC. A key priority will be to conduct mechanistic studies on how DNA methylation functionally interacts with other epigenetic regulators, including histone modifiers and chromatin remodelers, in order to inform the design of more effective, next-generation epigenetic combination therapies.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the most common type of lung cancer, accounting for approximately 85% of all cases (1,2). Its rising incidence and mortality rates represent a significant global public health challenge (3,4). Although progress has been made in traditional treatments such as surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, patient prognosis remains unsatisfactory (5). This persistent clinical challenge has motivated investigations into the molecular mechanisms underlying tumor progression and treatment resistance. Among these mechanisms, DNA methylation—an essential epigenetic modification—has attracted considerable research interest (6). By regulating gene expression and influencing key biological processes such as cell proliferation and apoptosis, DNA methylation plays a crucial role in tumorigenesis (7).

In recent years, the literature examining the relationship between DNA methylation and the overall risk of lung cancer, including NSCLC, has expanded significantly (8). Numerous studies have identified specific genes, of which its methylation status correlates with NSCLC occurrence, progression, and treatment response. For example, aberrant methylation of the p16INK4a gene is implicated in NSCLC pathogenesis and serves as a potential biomarker for early diagnosis and prognosis (9). For example, aberrant methylation of the p16INK4a gene is implicated in NSCLC pathogenesis and serves as a potential biomarker for early diagnosis and prognosis (10). However, despite extensive research on DNA methylation in NSCLC, comprehensive evaluations synthesizing the evolving research landscape remain limited.

Bibliometric analysis offers a quantitative approach to examine academic literature, revealing developmental characteristics and patterns within a research field (11). By evaluating parameters such as publication volume, citation metrics, authorship, and thematic focus, this methodology can elucidate research trends, identify emerging topics, and forecast future directions. This study therefore employs bibliometric analysis to explore the global research landscape and frontier trends in DNA methylation and NSCLC. We present this article in accordance with the BIBLIO reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0414/rc).

Methods

Search strategies and data collection

We conducted a systematic literature search using the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC), chosen for its focus on high-quality, globally peer-reviewed academic publications. WoSCC primarily includes traditional scholarly literature, such as journal articles, conference proceedings, and books (11). Moreover, the database is multidisciplinary and comprehensive, offering a complete citation network and key bibliometric indicators [e.g., Journal Citation Reports (JCR), Impact Factor (IF), and H-index] (11). Therefore, in line with previous studies (11-13), WoSCC was selected as the source for obtaining global academic information to perform the bibliometric analysis. Retrieve publications from January 1, 2008 to April 21, 2025. The search formula was as follows: TS = ((“DNA Methylation” OR “DNA Methylations” OR “Methylation DNA” OR “Methylations, DNA”) AND (“Carcinoma, Non-Small-Cell Lung” OR “Carcinoma, Non Small Cell Lung” OR “Carcinomas, Non-Small-Cell Lung” OR “Lung Carcinoma, Non-Small-Cell" OR “Lung Carcinomas, Non-Small-Cell” OR “Non-Small-Cell Lung Carcinomas” OR “Non-Small-Cell Lung Carcinoma” OR “Non Small Cell Lung Carcinoma” OR “Carcinoma, Non-Small Cell Lung” OR “Non-Small Cell Lung Carcinoma” OR “Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer” OR “Nonsmall Cell Lung Cancer”)). To ensure high-quality visualization results, the literature type in this study is limited to “article”, and the language type is set as “English”. To avoid database renewal-related deviations, we performed the literature retrieval on a single day (April 21, 2025). The complete bibliographic data were automatically retrieved in plain text format without manual intervention. This dataset comprised publication and citation counts, titles, author and institutional affiliations, corresponding countries/regions, keywords, and journal information. As all records originated from a single database, issues of duplication or internal inconsistency did not arise.

Statistical analysis

During the visualization analysis process, a comprehensive examination of academic data was conducted using three powerful bibliometric analysis tools: VOSviewer (version 1.6.20), CiteSpace (version 6.3.R1), and the R package “bibliometrix” (version 4.4.1).

VOSviewer served as a versatile tool for mapping collaborative networks, enabling the visualization of institutional cooperation, author collaboration, co-authorship patterns, citation networks, and co-citation relationships (14). This allowed us to examine the complex interconnections between authors, institutions, and publications in the field of DNA methylation in NSCLC. In the network visualizations, node size represents the volume of publications, line thickness indicates the strength of connections between entities, and node colors distinguish different clusters or temporal patterns. To investigate emerging trends and research hotspots, keyword co-occurrence analysis was conducted using VOSviewer and keyword burst detection using CiteSpace (15). For the CiteSpace analysis, the following parameters were configured: the time slice spanned from January 2008 to April 2025, with keywords selected as the node type. For keyword co-occurrence mapping, a 1-year time slice interval was applied. The top 5 most cited keywords per slice were retained as nodes, and the network was pruned using the Pathfinder algorithm combined with the Pruning Merged Network method. Visualization was then performed to generate burst word timeline diagrams.

The R package “bibliometrix” was employed for additional bibliometric analyses and visualization of publication patterns (16). To quantify academic impact, metrics such as the H-index, G-index, and M-index were employed, as referenced in existing literature (17). These metrics are critical for assessing the contributions of researchers and predicting future scientific output. Additionally, journal quality and impact were assessed using JCR quartile rankings and IF. The JCR quartiles (Q1–Q4) categorize journals by their relative IF within specific disciplines, serving as benchmarks for academic influence (18).

An overview of publications

This study examined 573 publications and all of them are articles (Figure 1A). A survey showed that a total of 4,356 authors from 2,524 institutions contributed to the creation of these 573 manuscripts. These works were published in 217 different journals and cited 18,727 references. From 2008 to 2024, the overall publication trend had shown a yearly increase, with the highest number of publications in 2020, reaching a total of 53 articles (Figure 1B).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study provides a comprehensive bibliometric overview of DNA methylation research in NSCLC spanning 2008–2025, mapping the global research landscape, delineating thematic clusters, and identifying emerging trends. Specifically, this evolution is characterized by a recent growing emphasis on hypermethylation in NSCLC, DNA methylation in therapy against NSCLC, DNMTs in NSCLC, DNA methylation as a biomarker in early diagnosis for NSCLC, SNPs in NSCLC, cigarette smoke-related abnormal DNA methylation, and DNA methylation in other cancers. Since 2022, the emergence of “activation”, and “transcription” continued through to 2025, which are indicative of anticipated future research directions.

China emerged as the leading contributors to this research, both in terms of publication volume and citations. The dominance of China, with institutions such as Nanjing Medical University, reflects its well-established research infrastructure, significant funding in DNA methylation in NSCLC research. The reason may be the serious situation of NSCLC in China. Recent years have witnessed a rising trend in both lung cancer incidence and mortality rates in China. According to 2020 global cancer statistics from the International Agency for Research on Cancer, China recorded approximately 820,000 new lung cancer cases and 715,000 lung cancer-related deaths (19). NSCLC estimates ~85% of all lung cancers (20). Notably, currently available treatments are generally considered noncurative (21). This prevalence status lead to a growing attention from researchers and policy makers in China on diagnosis and treatment for NSCLC (22). The most impactful contributors, such as Esteller Manel and Herman James G., have significantly shaped the field of DNA methylation in NSCLC. Esteller Manel is a professor and director of Josep Carreras Leukaemia Research Institute. His research focuses on promoter hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes as a common hallmark of human tumors, including NSCLC (23). Dr. Herman James G. from the University of Pittsburgh established first the methylation-specific polymerase chain reaction (MSP) (24). Recently, his team proposed the multiplex digital MSP (mdMSP) platform for simultaneous analysis of four methylation biomarkers for liquid-biopsy-based detection of NSCLC, exhibiting superior clinical performance when compared to traditional MSP assays for noninvasive detection of early-stage NSCLC (25).

Research hotspots

Cluster 1 (red): hypermethylation in NSCLC

This cluster focuses on DNA hypermethylation in early-stage NSCLC, with strong linkages to “gene-expression” and “tumor-suppressor”. Research highlights that promoter hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes (e.g., SOX1, EPAS1) is a key mechanism for transcriptional silencing, correlating with poor survival (26,27). The trend suggests these signatures hold potential for risk stratification, but current limitations in cohort size and specificity indicate a future research need for large-scale validation before clinical adoption as prognostic biomarkers.

Cluster 2 (blue): DNA methylation in therapy against NSCLC

Centered on therapeutic targeting, this cluster connects DNA methylation to “gefitinib” and “acquired-resistance” (28). It reveals that resistance to epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) tyrosine kinase inhibitors is mediated by aberrant RNA methylation regulators (e.g., KIAA1429, NSUN2/YBX1/QSOX1 axis) (29,30). This suggests a growing frontier in combinatorial epigenetic therapy, aiming to reverse drug resistance by targeting methylation pathways alongside traditional inhibitors.

Cluster 3 (green): DNMTs in NSCLC

This cluster highlights the role of DNMTs, particularly DNMT1, in NSCLC pathogenesis. Keywords like “pathway” and “down-regulation” indicate a focus on how DNMT dysregulation drives genomic instability (31). While DNMT inhibitors (e.g., azacytidine, decitabine) are established in hematology, the unclear efficacy in NSCLC points to a significant research gap, urging future studies to elucidate the specific role of DNMTs in lung cancer for targeted intervention (31,32). Thus, more studies should be conducted to elucidate the role of DNMTs in NSCLC and ascertain the efficacy and safety profile of DNMT inhibitors.

Cluster 4 (yellow): DNA methylation as a biomarker in early diagnosis for NSCLC

Strongly associated with “biomarkers”, “plasma”, and “diagnosis”, this cluster underscores the translational potential of methylation signatures (33). Studies like the mdMSP assay for SOX17/CDO1/TAC1/HOXA7 demonstrate high sensitivity in liquid biopsies (25). Furthermore, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that elevated blood DNA methylation levels are significantly associated with increased overall lung cancer risk, with particularly robust evidence from prospective cohort studies (8). The trend indicates a shift towards non-invasive, methylation-based early detection platforms that could surpass traditional invasive techniques.

Cluster 5 (purple): SNPs in NSCLC

This cluster links genetic polymorphisms (e.g., EGFL7/miR-126 rs2297538, WDR74 rs11231247) to NSCLC risk and poor prognosis. The co-occurrence of “poor prognosis” and “polymorphisms” suggests an emerging interface between genetic susceptibility and epigenetic regulation, providing new insights into personalized risk assessment (34). These results provide new insights into the pathogenesis of NSCLC.

Cluster 6 (brilliant blue): cigarette smoke-related abnormal DNA methylation

Focused on “cigarette-smoking” and “inflammation”, this cluster explores how tobacco induces methylation alterations (e.g., p16 hypermethylation, LKB1 repression) to promote carcinogenesis and drug resistance (31,35). Future research is expected to delve deeper into the mechanistic hierarchy of CpG site alterations under chronic smoke exposure.

Cluster 7 (orange): DNA methylation in other cancers

This cluster expands the scope to “breast-cancer” and “colorectal-cancer”, indicating that methylation research in NSCLC is part of a broader pan-cancer epigenetic landscape (36). The integration of machine learning for methylation signature analysis suggests a future trend towards multi-cancer diagnostic and classification platforms (37). Despite implementation challenges inherent to novel technologies, methylation profiling has become an established approach that will see expanding clinical adoption as a cancer diagnostic modality across diverse malignancies.

Emerging research frontiers

From 2008 to 2014, research centered on the detection of p16INK4a promoter methylation in NSCLC, as evidenced by the prominence of the term “genes” (38). The focus was predominantly on evaluating the potential of p16 methylation as a biomarker in various biospecimens, such as serum, sputum, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. This trend underscores an early-phase emphasis on identifying single-gene epigenetic markers for diagnostic purposes. The widespread investigation of p16 suggests that initial epigenetic research in NSCLC was largely gene-centric, aimed at translating isolated methylation events into clinical biomarkers. However, the recognition of its limited sensitivity for screening also reflected a key limitation of this period’s focus, which was the search for robust diagnostic markers.

Between 2015 and 2021, the keywords shifted to “epigenetics” and “identification”, indicating a significant expansion of the research paradigm. The focus evolved from single genes to a system-wide epigenetic perspective. Research during this phase aimed to identify the complex landscape encompassing not only DNA methylation, but also histone modifications and non-coding RNAs (39). The emphasis on “identification” highlights the application of omics technologies for comprehensive profiling. This period was characterized by exploring the synergistic crosstalk between different epigenetic layers, such as the cooperative silencing involving DNA methylation and microRNAs (miRNAs) (40). The overarching theme shifted from finding singular markers to mapping the integrated epigenetic architecture of NSCLC, paving the way for developing multi-targeted therapeutic strategies (41). The key challenge emerging from this expanded view was how to effectively translate this complex landscape into actionable biomarkers and therapies.

Since 2022, the recent emergence of “activation” and “transcription” signals a further evolution towards understanding the functional and regulatory outcomes of the epigenetic machinery. The research focus is on elucidating how epigenetic alterations, particularly DNA methylation, directly exert their oncogenic effects through transcriptional silencing of tumor suppressor genes and the resultant oncogene activation (42). The growing interest in DNMT3B, regulated by transcription factors like FOXO3a, exemplifies this trend towards investigating the dynamic regulation of epigenetic writers themselves (31). This reflects a deeper mechanistic inquiry: rather than merely cataloging changes, research is now more concentrated on the upstream regulators and downstream transcriptional consequences of epigenetic dysregulation. The prominence of “transcription” underscores a shift towards understanding epigenetic changes as integral components of the transcriptional regulatory network in NSCLC, as highlighted by studies on the interplay between DNA methylation and miRNAs (43). This trend points towards future research that will likely focus on dissecting the causal epigenetic-transcriptional circuits driving NSCLC, beyond correlations.

Limitations

In this bibliometric analysis of DNA methylation and NSCLC, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, reliance on the WoSCC and restriction to English-language publications may have excluded relevant research indexed in other databases or published in other languages, potentially underrepresenting contributions from non-English-speaking regions. Second, bibliometric indicators (e.g., citation counts, H-index) are citation-dependent and may not fully capture the impact of recent high-quality studies that have not yet accrued substantial citations, which could affect the identification of emerging research fronts and the ranking of countries, institutions, journals, and authors. These limitations underscore the importance of adopting a more inclusive and nuanced approach in future bibliometric studies to enrich the understanding of the evolving landscape in this research domain.

Conclusions

This bibliometric analysis underscores the pivotal role of DNA methylation in NSCLC. Research hotspots are focused on hypermethylation in NSCLC, DNA methylation in therapy against NSCLC, DNMTs in NSCLC, DNA methylation as a biomarker in early diagnosis for NSCLC, SNPs in NSCLC, cigarette smoke-related abnormal DNA methylation, and DNA methylation in other cancers. Future research should focus on elucidating the specific molecular mechanisms within NSCLC’s broader epigenetic network, rather than concentrating on DNA methylation in isolation. A deeper understanding of how DNA methylation functions within this integrated epigenetic context may enhance the efficacy of therapies targeting epigenetic regulators. These research directions hold significant promise for improving clinical management and prognosis in NSCLC.
