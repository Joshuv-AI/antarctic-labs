Association of the C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index with new-onset myocardial infarction risk: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Lijun Meng, Jing Yang, Shouling Wu, Shuohua Chen, Yuntao Wu, Qi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the association between the C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index (CTI) and risk of new-onset myocardial infarction (MI). We conducted a prospective cohort study among participants enrolled in the Kailuan Study in Tangshan City, Hebei Province, China, who underwent the physical examination between June 2006 and October 2007 (n = 101,510). After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 94,509 participants were included in the final analysis.The subjects were stratified into four groups according to baseline CTI quartiles: Q1 (CTI < 7.89), Q2 (7.89 ≤ CTI < 8.54), Q3 (8.54 ≤ CTI < 9.19), and Q4 (CTI ≥ 9.19). New-onset myocardial infarction was the main endpoint of the follow-up, which lasted until December 31, 2021.Hazard ratios (HR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated using the multivariate-Cox proportional hazards regression model. The cumulative incidence function was constructed with the Kaplan–Meier method, and log-rank tests were performed to assess between-group differences. During a median follow-up of 15.01 (14.65, 15.20) years, 2,062 new-onset myocardial infarctions were documented. As CTI levels increased, the cumulative incidence rates of MI in the Q1 to Q4 groups were 0.86%, 1.60%, 2.12%, and 3.16%, respectively. After adjusting for relevant confounders, Cox proportional hazards regression analysis showed that compared with the Q1 group, the HR (95% CI) for new-onset myocardial infarction in the Q2 to Q4 groups was 1.42 (95% CI 1.21–1.67), 1.69 (95% CI 1.45–1.98), and 2.04 (95% CI 1.74–2.40), respectively. Each 1-unit increment in CTI was associated with a 29% increase in the risk of new-onset MI. Elevated CTI levels are independently associated with a higher risk of new-onset MI.Keywords: Myocardial infarction, C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index, Inflammation, Metabolic disorder, Risk predictionSubject terms: Cardiology, Diseases, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) is a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, imposing a substantial public health burden. It is one of the most prevalent cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) and a leading cause of mortality and disability worldwide1. Although conventional risk factors, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, and smoking, play a pivotal role in risk assessment, some individuals develop MI without these classic risk factors, implying the involvement of other incompletely understood pathophysiological mechanisms.Emerging evidence indicates that chronic low-grade inflammation and insulin resistance(IR) are not only independent risk factors but also mutually exacerbate each other through complex signaling networks, forming an “inflammation-metabolism vicious cycle”. This cycle is of critical importance in the initiation and progression of atherosclerosis, acting synergistically as a core pathological driver of MI2,3. Accordingly, the C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index (CTI) was proposed. By integrating C-reactive protein (CRP) and the Triglyceride-Glucose(TyG) index,CTI simultaneously captures two core pathological processes: inflammation and metabolic disturbance. Prior studies have confirmed4–6 the CTI’s strong predictive capacity for stroke and coronary heart disease, supporting its potential as a novel composite biomarker. However, evidence linking CTI to MI risk—especially in large-scale Chinese populations—remains scarce.Using data from the large prospective Kailuan Cohort, this study aimed to examine the association between the CTI index and the risk of new-onset MI in Chinese adults and to evaluate its incremental predictive value beyond single biomarkers. Our objective is to provide evidence-based support for the early risk stratification and primary prevention of MI.

Methods

Study participants

Participants were selected from the Kailuan Study database (n = 101,510).Inclusion criteria: Individuals who completed the 2006–2007 physical examination.Exclusion criteria: (1) Individuals missing data for triglyceride (TG), C-reactive protein (CRP), or fasting blood glucose (FBG)(n = 3270); (2) History of CVD at baseline(n = 3396); (3) History of cancer(n = 335). A total of 94,509 participants were finally included.

Data collection and definitions

Epidemiological surveys and anthropometric measurements were performed as described in our previous publications7. For biochemical analyses: Participants fasted for at least 8 h prior to examination. Approximately 5 mL of fasting blood was drawn from the elbow vein the following morning. After centrifugation, the supernatant serum was collected for analysis. Biochemical indicators, including FBG, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, and CRP, were measured using the Hitachi 7600 automatic biochemical analyzer8. We measured FBG using the hexokinase assay and measured TG and HDL-C using the enzyme colorimetric assay. All tests were performed by professional laboratory technicians strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions. Smoking history definition:in the past year, the average number of cigarettes smoked at least 1 per day, lasting more than 1 year.Drinking history definition: the average daily drinking was more than 1 standard drinking volume (1 standard drinking volume is equivalent to 45 ml liquor/360 ml beer/120 ml fruit wine), for more than 1 consecutive year, or has been abstained from alcohol for less than 1 year.Income level definition: ≥ 800 yuan/month and < 800 yuan/month. Educational attainment definition: high school or above and junior high school or below. Physical exercise definition: ≥ 3 sessions per week, each lasting ≥ 30 minutes9; BMI definition: body mass (kg)/height2 (m2)10; Diagnoses of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia followed Chinese guidelines11–13.

Calculation of the CTI index

The CTI index was calculated using the formula: 0.412 × ln[CRP (mg/L)] + ln[TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL)/2]14. TG (mmol/L) was converted to mg/dL by multiplying by 88.57,FBG (mmol/L) was converted to mg/dL by multiplying by 18.02.Participants were stratified into four quartile groups: quartile 1 (Q1) (CTI < 7.89), quartile 2 (Q2) (7.89 ≤ CTI < 8.54), quartile 3 (Q3) (8.54 ≤ CTI < 9.19), and quartile 4 (Q4) (CTI ≥ 9.19).

Follow-up period and outcomes

Follow-up began at the 2006–2007 examination and ended at the first diagnosis of new-onset MI, death, or December 31, 2021.Only the first MI event was recorded. MI diagnosis was based on WHO criteria15,16. Event data were retrieved from the Kailuan Social Security Information System. Trained medical professionals collected data by reviewing records at Kailuan Group hospitals, with diagnoses confirmed by physicians based on inpatient medical records.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SAS 9.4 software. For quantitative variables, normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, with intergroup comparisons performed using ANOVA. The measurement data of non-normal distribution were expressed as M (P25, P75), and the non-parametric rank sum test was employed for the purpose of comparing the data across groups. Categorical variables are expressed as constituent ratios or rates (%), and X2 test was used for comparison among groups. Cumulative incidence curves were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method, and inter-group differences were compared using the log-rank test. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards models analyzed the impact of different CTI groups on the risk of new-onset MI. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was applied to explorethe continuous relationship between CTI and MI risk. The robustness of the correlation between the CTI index and the incidence of new-onset MI was also examined. Initially, the CTI index was incorporated into the model as a continuous variable. Secondly, to eliminate the possibility of reverse causality, the primary analyses were repeated, with participants who experienced MI events during the initial 2 years of follow-up excluded from the analysis. Subgroup analyses were conducted following stratification by age, sex, BMI, baseline FBG, smoking status, and drinking status. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed for independent factors identified in multivariate analysis, with area under the curve (AUC) calculated to assess each factor’s discriminatory ability for new MI. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Association of the CTI index with new-onset MI events

With a median follow-up duration of 15.01 (14.65, 15.20) years, 2,062 cases (2.18%) of new-onset MI occurred. The number of incident MI cases in Q1 to Q4 was 242(1.02%), 433 (1.83%), 570 (2.41%), and 817 (3.46%), respectively. With incidence densities of 0.71 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI 0.63–0.81), 1.29 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI 1.18–1.42), 1.73 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI 1.59–1.88), and 2.53 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI 2.36–2.71), respectively. Log-rank test results showed that compared with the Q1 group (0.86%), the cumulative incidence rates of new-onset MI in the Q2, Q3, and Q4 groups were 1.60%, 2.12%, and 3.16%, respectively. Comparisons of cumulative incidence rates of new-onset MI events among groups revealed statistically significant differences (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1).

Cumulative incidence curve of new-onset diagnosed MI in CTI groups.

Furthermore, considering deaths during follow-up (2,062 cases) as competing risks, we conducted additional analysis using a competing risks model for mortality. After implementing the necessary adjustments to account for the potential confounders, the findings remained consistent and robust: the highest CTI group (Q4) was an independent risk factor for new-onset MI (HR:1.97, 95% CI 1.68–2.32, P < 0.0001) (Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the primary analysis results, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses: (1) Excluding patients with new-onset myocardial infarction events at the first 2-year follow-up; (2) Excluding patients who developed cancer during follow-up; (3) Excluding patients taking antihypertensive, antidiabetic, or lipid-lowering medications at baseline; (4) Excluding patients with abnormally elevated C-reactive protein (> 10 mg/L); (5) Excluding patients with severe renal impairment (eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73m2). The results of sensitivity analyses were consistent with the primary findings (Table 4).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age,sex, BMI, baseline FBG,smoking status, and drinking status. No significant interaction effects were observed across all subgroups (all P for interaction > 0.05).CTI (Q4 vs Q1) was significantly associated with higher MI risk in subgroups including age < 45 years, females, BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2, smoking status, drinking status, and non-diabetic status. However, this association was not observed in the subgroup with FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (P = 0.137). To further investigate the CTI index’s role in new-onset MI risk among different fasting blood glucose status, we separately plotted restricted cubic spline for baseline FBG levels < 5.6 mmol/L (Fig. 2B) 、 5.6 ≤ FBG < 7.0 mmol/L (Fig. 2C) and FBG > = 7.0 mmol/L(Fig. 2D) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study, founded upon the large-scale prospective Kailuan Cohort, systematically demonstrates for the first time in the Chinese adult population a positive association between a novel composite indicator—the C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index (CTI)—and the potential for incidental myocardial infarction(MI).After adjusting for confounding factors, we adjusted for metabolic covariates such as diabetes and lipid parameters to isolate the independent effect of CTI. While this may attenuate the observed association by accounting for part of the mechanistic pathway, it strengthens the inference that CTI provides information beyond these traditional factors,each one-standard-deviation increase in CTI was associated with a 29% higher risk of MI. Subsequent subgroup analyses indicated that this correlation was most evident in individuals aged < 45 years, women, smokers, drinkers, and those without diabetes. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated a linear positive relationship between CTI and MI risk. Importantly, CTI provided significant incremental predictive value beyond traditional risk factor models, outperforming either the TyG index or CRP alone.

The TyG index functions as a surrogate marker for insulin resistance (IR), while CRP has been identified as a biological marker of persistent, subclinical, low-grade inflammation. The evidence suggests that both have been independently associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease17–19. Evidence indicates that elevated TyG index correlates with increased CVD incidence risk and mortality in both the general population and specific subgroups such as those with hypertension or diabetes19,20. In a similar vein, the CRP has been demonstrated to possess the capacity to independently predict CVD outcomes, with a particular emphasis on the occurrence of recurrent stroke and diabetic complications21.It is well-established that inflammation and IR are independent CVD risk factors that exert a synergistic effect on the pathogenesis of CVD22,23. Building upon this, the CTI integrates these two pathways and has demonstrated predictive value for cardiovascular events in non-CKM populations5,6. By combining inflammation and IR, the CTI captures dual pathophysiological processes and exhibits strong predictive value for stroke and coronary heart disease. For instance, Tang et al.4 confirmed that elevated CTI levels correlate with increased stroke risk among hypertensive adults in China.In addition, Huo et al.5reported significant associations between CTI and stroke in both normoglycemic and prediabetic populations. In a cohort of U.S. subjects, Xu et al6. documented a positive linear relationship between CTI and CHD, thereby enhancing its utility for screening purposes in the general population. Recent research frontiers have expanded beyond single CTI measurements, focusing instead on the long-term health impacts of cumulative exposure and dynamic trajectory evolution. For instance, Ma et al24. confirmed that participants with moderately elevated stable CTI levels or moderately elevated CTI levels demonstrated significantly higher CVD risk in comparison to those with consistently low CTI levels.

From a pathophysiological perspective, C-reactive protein promotes atherosclerosis formation through three primary mechanisms25: (1) inducing endothelial dysfunction; (2) enhancing the deposition of oxidized lipoproteins; (3) facilitating leukocyte infiltration.Concurrently, the TyG index, a marker of insulin resistance, has been demonstrated to impair peripheral glucose metabolism and directly compromise vascular endothelium through the mechanisms of oxidative stress and lipotoxicity26. The precise mechanisms through which CTI contributes to an increased risk of MI remain to be elucidated. However, the following factors may offer a potential explanation for this association. IR and chronic inflammation have been demonstrated to impair vascular endothelial integrity, reduce nitric oxide utilization efficiency, disrupt normal coagulation mechanisms, and accelerate atherosclerosis progression. These factors, in turn, have been shown to significantly increase the risk of MI27,28. Furthermore, inflammation exacerbates IR by amplifying systemic inflammatory responses through tissue-derived mediators. This mutual reinforcement creates a vicious cycle that further elevates MI risk29. Concurrently, inflammation and IR destabilize atherosclerotic plaques, increasing rupture risk and subsequent thrombosis formation, thereby promoting MI30. In conclusion, patients suffering from IR and inflammation frequently exhibit comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, and obesity. These conditions are well-established risk factors for MI31–35.

Subgroup analysis revealed that the highest quartile Q4 of CTI was associated with increased MI risk across most subgroups, indicating strong predictive universality of CTI. Particularly in the younger subgroup (< 45 years), the Q4 group exhibited a hazard ratio as high as 2.401. This supports the life course hypothesis of CVD, suggesting that risk accumulates over years of exposure and is more modifiable before irreversible damage occurs36, implying that CTI may serve as a useful tool for early identification of high-risk young individuals. The relationship between CTI and MI risk demonstrated elevated HR values in the female subgroup. The potential explanations for this phenomenon include sex differences in hormonal regulation, fat distribution, or immune responses,with women exhibiting stronger correlations with IR than men37. Consequently, clinicians should prioritize the monitoring of CTI levels in female patients to effectively mitigate the risk of myocardial infarction. Notably, in the subgroup of participants with diabetes (FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L), the correlation between CTI and MI was not statistically significant. (P = 0.137). This finding aligns with Huo et al.'s5 study: elevated CTI levels have been demonstrated to correlate with an increased risk of stroke in individuals with normal glucose regulation and prediabetes across a range of glycemic states, but this association is not observed in diabetic populations. Possible explanations include:individuals with diabetes often have pre-existing severe vascular pathology, and other strong risk factors (such as blood glucose variability, pancreatic dysfunction, and diabetes duration) may mask CTI’s marginal contribution. Alternatively, under diabetic conditions, the interaction pathway between inflammation and IR may reach saturation, or other dominant pathological mechanisms may be at play. Second, competing risks may exist: individuals with diabetes often have multiple comorbid risk factors (e.g. hypertension, dyslipidemia, kidney disease), which collectively influence MI risk and may dilute the independent contribution of CTI to some extent. This finding does not diminish the value of CTI but rather underscores the potential importance of prediabetes intervention and MI prevention.

This study expands current knowledge in several critical aspects. First, it is the first to validate the association between CTI and MI in a large, general adult population in China. Second, through rigorous statistical metrics, it demonstrates that CTI provides a modest but statistically significant incremental predictive value for MI risk beyond traditional risk factor models. These observations suggest the potential for CTI to function as a pragmatic clinical instrument, offering a promising avenue to enhance the existing framework of cardiovascular risk stratification.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in our study. First, the observational design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between CTI and MI. Second, our study was based on a single baseline measurement of CTI and its components (CRP, FBG, TG). This approach cannot account for potential changes in these biomarkers over the long follow-up period, which might be influenced by lifestyle modifications or medication use (e.g.statins, anti-diabetic, or anti-inflammatory drugs), and thus may not reflect the cumulative or dynamic exposure accurately. Third, despite extensive adjustments, residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as detailed dietary habits, family history of CVD, medication adherence, and the presence of chronic inflammatory conditions (or use of immunomodulatory drugs) cannot be ruled out. The lack of information on anti-inflammatory drug use is particularly relevant given CRP’s sensitivity to inflammation. Fourth, our prediction model was not directly compared against established cardiovascular risk scores (e.g.Framingham, Pooled Cohort Equations), limiting the assessment of its practical incremental utility in clinical risk stratification. The observed improvement in C-index, while statistically significant, is modest, and its clinical impact remains to be determined. Fifth, the generalizability of our findings is constrained by the specific nature of the Kailuan cohort, which consists predominantly of male industrial workers from northern China. Caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to women, other ethnic groups, or general Western populations. Finally, the results of subgroup analyses should be interpreted with caution due to the lack of adjustment for multiple comparisons, and they serve primarily for hypothesis generation rather than definitive mechanistic conclusions.

In summary, elevated CTI positively correlates with new-onset MI risk. CTI may aid in identifying high-risk individuals for new-onset MI, warranting its long-term monitoring in clinical practice.However, given the modest improvement in discrimination and the lack of comparison with established risk scores, the current findings do not immediately advocate for a change in clinical practice. Further validation in diverse populations and direct comparison with existing models are needed to ascertain its clinical utility.
