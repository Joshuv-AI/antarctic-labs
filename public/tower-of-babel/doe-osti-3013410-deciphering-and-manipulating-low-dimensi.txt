Final Technical Report
 Award Number: SC0021421
 Principle Investigator: Ming Yi (Rice University)
 Project Title: Deciphering and Manipulating Low Dimensional Magnetism

Executive Summary
This program investigates the electronic structure and collective quantum phenomena of correlated
magnetic materials using advanced angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) and
complementary probes, with a focus on three interrelated material families: (i) the semiconducting
van der Waals magnet Cr₂Ge₂Te₆, (ii) metallic Fe-based van der Waals magnets including Fe₃GeTe₂
Fe₃GaTe₂, and Fe5GeTe₂, and (iii) kagome magnets such as FeGe, FeSn, and CsV₃Sb₅/CsCr₃Sb₅.
In Cr₂Ge₂Te₆, our work established how spin excitations develop across a dimensional crossover
as spins establish correlation to form long range order, providing a clean platform to isolate
correlation effects. In metallic Fe-based magnets, we uncovered the dichotomy between flat and
dispersive bands, revealed momentum-dependent electronic reconstructions tied to magnetic order,
and demonstrated reversible, non-volatile electronic switching near room temperature,
highlighting the strong coupling among spin, charge, and lattice degrees of freedom in metallic
ferromagnets. In kagome magnets, we identified charge density wave formation, symmetry
breaking, flat-band renormalization, and field-induced momentum-dependent electronic
anisotropy, elucidating how geometric frustration and electronic correlations conspire to generate
emergent quantum states. Collectively, these results establish a unified microscopic framework for
understanding correlation-driven electronic reconstruction, symmetry breaking, and collective
order across semiconducting and metallic magnetic systems, advancing DOE mission goals in
quantum materials discovery and control.

Accomplishments
The overarching goal of this project was to bridge the gap between first-principles theory and
experimentally measured electronic, electric, and optical properties in two-dimensional magnetic
materials by directly probing the single-particle spectral function using angle-resolved
photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES). This goal has been met and, in several respects, exceeded.
First, regarding goal (i)—providing fundamental understanding of the origin of magnetism in bulk
(three-dimensional) materials—the project delivered a comprehensive microscopic picture of
correlation-driven electronic reconstruction in semiconducting, metallic, and kagome magnets. In
bulk and quasi-3D systems such as Cr₂Ge₂Te₆, Fe₃GeTe₂, Fe₃GaTe₂, and kagome FeGe, ARPES
measurements revealed the coexistence of itinerant and local-moment behavior, flat-band
formation, and strong band renormalization beyond density functional theory predictions, directly

identifying many-body effects responsible for magnetic ordering. These results provided
quantitative benchmarks for advanced theoretical approaches beyond conventional DFT.

Second, goal (ii)—understanding the evolution of magnetism from the three-dimensional limit
toward the two-dimensional regime—was achieved through systematic studies of van der Waals
magnets and thin-film kagome systems. The work established how reduced dimensionality
enhances correlation effects, stabilizes flat electronic bands, and modifies exchange interactions,
leading to distinct magnetic and charge-ordered ground states. Notably, the identification of flat-
band dichotomy, selective band renormalization, and symmetry breaking in Fe-based van der
Waals magnets and kagome lattices clarified how magnetic interactions evolve as interlayer
coupling is suppressed, directly addressing the dimensional crossover central to the proposal.

Third, goal (iii)—investigating tunability and external perturbations—was addressed through
direct measurements of electronic responses to temperature, magnetic field, lattice defects, and
reduced dimensionality. The project uncovered magnetic-field-induced momentum-dependent
symmetry breaking in CsV₃Sb₅, vacancy-induced suppression of charge density wave order in
FeGe, and reversible non-volatile electronic switching in van der Waals Fe5GeTe₂ ferromagnets,
demonstrating strong and controllable coupling between electronic structure and magnetic order.
These results go beyond static characterization and establish concrete pathways for externally
tuning magnetism in correlated materials including the development of novel experimental
techniques such as magnetoARPES.

Overall, the project successfully fulfilled its original objectives by establishing ARPES as a
quantitative bridge between theory and experiment in two-dimensional magnetic systems. The
methodologies developed and insights gained now provide a broadly applicable framework for
understanding and controlling emergent quantum phenomena in correlated magnets, directly
supporting materials-by-design efforts for future quantum and spintronic applications.

Project Activities
The central hypothesis of this project was that the electronic, magnetic, and optical properties of
two-dimensional and quasi-two-dimensional van der Waals magnets are governed by many-body
interactions that are inadequately captured by conventional density functional theory, and that
direct measurement of the single-particle spectral function via angle-resolved photoemission
spectroscopy (ARPES) can provide the critical experimental constraints needed to bridge theory
and materials-by-design efforts. The project further hypothesized that magnetism in these systems
evolves from the three-dimensional bulk limit toward the two-dimensional regime, and that

external perturbations—such as temperature, magnetic field, lattice defects, and reduced
dimensionality—can be used to systematically tune magnetic order through their impact on the
electronic structure.

To test these hypotheses, the project employed a combination of high-resolution ARPES, magneto-
ARPES, temperature-dependent measurements across magnetic and charge-ordering transitions,
and controlled comparisons between bulk crystals, thin films, and van der Waals materials. These
measurements were complemented by neutron scattering, transport, and theoretical modeling
beyond standard DFT, including correlation-informed approaches, to establish a quantitative link
between measured band structures and magnetic interactions. The experimental strategy
emphasized systematic trends across three material families—semiconducting magnets
(Cr₂Ge₂Te₆), metallic Fe-based van der Waals magnets (Fe₃GeTe₂, Fe₃GaTe₂, Fe5GeTe₂), and
kagome magnets (FeGe, FeSn, CsV₃Sb₅/CsCr₃Sb₅)—allowing common physical mechanisms to
be explored across chemically and structurally distinct systems.

In the area of semiconducting magnets, we identified a two-step electronic response associated
with the dimensional crossover of spin correlation formation. In Cr₂Ge₂Te₆, ARPES measurements
revealed distinct electronic reconstructions accompanying the development of magnetic
correlations, establishing a direct link between electronic structure and magnetic dimensionality
(Wu et al., Phys. Rev. B 2024). Furthermore, we discovered a massive electron–plasmon coupling
enabled by the emergence of a Van Hove singularity in this system via surface-dosing, highlighting
the role of electronic topology in enhancing many-body interactions (Guo et al., in preparation).

In metallic Fe-based van der Waals magnets, our ARPES studies demonstrated that magnetism is
governed predominantly by local moments rather than itinerant electrons, contrary to prevailing
assumptions (Wu et al., Phys. Rev. B 2024). We further discovered a reversible electronic
switching behavior associated with vacancy ordering in Fe₅GeTe₂, which directly modifies the
electronic structure and topological character through the creation and destruction of a
geometrically frustrated lattice (Wu et al., Nat. Commun. 2024). Importantly, we showed that the
presence or absence of this frustrated sublattice has a direct and controllable impact on the
magnetic ordering of the Fe sublattice, establishing lattice geometry as a powerful tuning
parameter for magnetism in this material family (Wu et al., arXiv:2508.03029).

In kagome magnets, the project led to several major discoveries. We identified charge density wave
(CDW) order emerging within the magnetically ordered phase of FeGe (Teng et al., Nature 2022)
and demonstrated that magnetic order plays a critical role in tuning the energy position of the

kagome Van Hove singularity, thereby playing an important role in the CDW instability (Teng et
al., Nat. Phys. 2023). By growing high-quality thin films of the kagome magnet FeSn, we achieved
direct spectroscopic visualization of the electronic response associated with magnetic order and
established the local-moment nature of the kagome flat band (Ren et al., Nat. Commun. 2024). In
the Cr-based kagome metal CsCr₃Sb₅, we discovered that moderate electron correlations shift the
kagome flat band to near the Fermi level, enabling its active participation in the emergent magnetic
order (Guo et al., Nat. Commun. 2025). Most recently, in work to appear in Nature Physics, we
developed and applied magneto-ARPES to directly observe electronic signatures of time-reversal
symmetry breaking associated with the proposed loop-current order in CsV₃Sb₅, providing a direct
spectroscopic probe of this elusive ordered state.

Overall, the work conducted under this project resulted in 16 published peer-reviewed papers,
including in Nature, Nature Physics, Nature Communications, Physical Review Letters, and
Physical Review B, with one additional paper in press, one under review, and one in preparation.
During the funding period, the PI also delivered 69 invited talks at international conferences,
workshops, and seminars, broadly disseminating the project’s findings to the condensed matter
and quantum materials communities.

c. Networks or collaborations fostered
Many new collaborations were fostered. The work on elucidating electronic switching behavior
in Fe5GeTe2 grew into a large collaboration due to the many experimental probes that we needed
to use to identify the complex physics associated with the vacancy ordering. These included
neutron scattering collaboration with Huibo Cao at Oak Ridge National Lab, Scanning Tunneling
Microscopy collaboration with Eduardo da Silva Neto at Yale University, second harmonic
oscillation measurements with Liang Wu at University of Pennsylvania, synchrotron-based x-ray
diffraction at CHESS, and Jianxin Zhu at Los Alamos National Laboratory for first principles
calculations.
