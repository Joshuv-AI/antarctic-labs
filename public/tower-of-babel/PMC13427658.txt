Development and independent validation of a hypoxemia risk prediction model (HAPPY-12K) for sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy: a multicentre prospective cohort study
Authors: Nana Li, Xiaoshuang Xu, Maojie Xue, Jiacheng Zhou, Xiang Wang, Junbei Wu, Leyi Chen, Yuqing Yang, Jiajin Chen, Tianlin Zhou, Jigang Zhang, Yanna Si, Yanxiang Miao, Guoxiang Fan, Dong Shao, Xin Liu, Liang Sun, Chaolin Guo, Feng Chen, David C Christiani, YunHong Lu, Hui Peng, Xiaofei Cao, Ruyang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundHypoxemia is a common and serious complication during sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy for out- and in-patients. Though diagnostic and severity scoring systems of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and difficult airway assessment (DAA) are widely used to assess hypoxemia risk, there is no exclusively designed prediction model and convenient tool in real-world practice. We aimed to develop and validate a robust and accurate hypoxemia risk prediction model for pre-operative use in this context.MethodsUsing data from out-patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy between May 2020 and November 2023 across seven hospitals in China with diverse regional and ethnic backgrounds, we developed and independently validated a hypoxemia risk prediction model for sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy (HAPPY-12K). The model was developed to pre-operatively predict occurrence of hypoxemia during sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy, defined as SpO2 falling below 95% for a duration exceeding 10 s. HAPPY-12K was a logistic regression model incorporating eight predictors: body mass index, Mallampati grade, limited jaw protrusion, short thyromental distance, large tongue, history of snoring, short neck with large circumference and pre-operative mean arterial pressure. The model was constructed by a well-established 3-D modeling strategy composed of Double types of effects, Double steps of screening, and Double steps of modeling. The discriminative ability was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The model calibration was examined through calibration slope, expected-to-observed (E:O) ratio and Brier score. For clinical utility, decision curve analysis was performed to assess net benefit (NB) and net reduction (NR). Furthermore, we systematically compared HAPPY-12K with other newly developed models using scores or raw variables from questionaries of OSA and DAA using DeLong’s test. This study is registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300074128).FindingsWe included 11,957 patients, divided into a Training Set (n = 2,518, hypoxemia rate 10.37%), and five validation sets (n = 9,439, hypoxemia rate raining from 8.40% to 28.45%). HAPPY-12K was developed in a Han Chinese population and exhibited satisfactory discrimination ability with AUCs ranging from 0.818 to 0.895 in external populations of the same ethnicity, and an acceptable AUC of 0.771 in a Uygur Chinese population. Although its Brier scores were satisfactory across all ethnic populations, HAPPY-12K displayed acceptable calibration (calibration slope < 1.2) in external Han Chinese populations, and good calibration (E:O ratio = 0.962) in independent homogenous populations comparable to the training set. The average NB and NR were 45.2‰ and 59.5%, respectively. It was estimated that HAPPY-12K would identify over half a million patients with truly developing hypoxemia during sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy and could help avoid over six million unnecessary interventions annually in China. Meanwhile, a head-to-head comparison revealed that HAPPY-12K outperformed other models. HAPPY-12K has been implemented as an interactive online tool available at http://bigdata.njmu.edu.cn/HAPPY-12K/.InterpretationHAPPY-12K could enable efficient and precise hypoxemia risk assessment before sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy, providing timely alerts for high-risk outpatients.FundingNational Natural Science Foundation of China; Noncommunicable Chronic Diseases-National Science and Technology Major Project; Science and Technology Project of Jiangsu Disease Control and Prevention Administration; Science and Technology Development Project of Nanjing Medical University; Priority Academic Program Development of Jiangsu Higher Education Institutions; and Outstanding Young Level Academic Leadership Training Program of Nanjing Medical University.Keywords: Hypoxemia, Risk prediction model, Sedated, Gastrointestinal endoscopy, Online tool

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Hypoxemia is a common and clinically relevant complication during sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy, with potential consequences that include procedure interruption, airway intervention, and serious cardiopulmonary or neurological events. Current risk assessment mainly relies on subjective clinical judgment, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) screening tools or difficult airway assessment (DAA) tools. However, these tools are not specially designed for hypoxemia risk prediction undergoing sedated endoscopy. We searched PubMed from database inception to June 20, 2026, for English-language papers using the terms “hypoxemia” OR “hypoxia” OR “oxygen desaturation”, and “gastrointestinal endoscopy” OR “gastroscopy” OR “colonoscopy” OR “endoscopic sedation”, and “prediction model” OR “risk prediction” OR “risk score” OR “nomogram” OR “machine learning”. Our search yielded 34 results. Existing prediction studies have been limited due to small sample sizes, single-center designs, restricted anesthetic protocols, or insufficient external validation.

Added value of this study

HAPPY-12K is a hypoxemia risk prediction model, accompanied by a free online tool, developed and externally validated based on approximately 12,000 patients undergoing sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy, through a multicentre prospective cohort study. By incorporating eight accessible pre-operative predictors, HAPPY-12K enables rapid and accurate pre-procedural risk stratification. Furthermore, model recalibration across regional datasets improved its generalizability in different clinical settings.

Implications of all the available evidence

HAPPY-12K is developed and independently validated through a large-sample multicentre prospective cohort study, and exhibits accurate and reliable predictive value for hypoxemia risk prior to sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy. It enables clinicians to implement targeted preventive strategies for high-risk patients, potentially improving safety outcomes and optimizing resource use in endoscopic practice.

Introduction

Early detection of gastrointestinal tumors is essential for improving prognosis. Gastrointestinal endoscopy, the gold standard for diagnosis, plays a pivotal role in screening and therapeutic intervention.1 Sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy enhances patient comfort, attenuates physiological stress responses, and facilitates procedural performance; It is therefore the preferred clinical approach for most patients.2,3 Nevertheless, hypoxemia remains a frequent and potentially life-threatening complication in sedated gastrointestinal endoscopic procedures. Its incidence has been reported to range from 8.4% to 33.5%.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Once hypoxemia occurs during gastrointestinal endoscopy, immediate intervention is often required, including pausing the procedure, mask ventilation, or even endotracheal intubation.11 Although these rescue measures are essential, they significantly disrupt the procedural workflow, increase technical difficulty, and may compromise diagnostic or therapeutic completeness due to examination interruption. Importantly, routine supplemental oxygen during gastroscopy, while maintaining peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) within the normal range, may mask underlying hypoventilation or apnea, thereby delaying the recognition of hypoxemia.12 By the time a decline in SpO2 is detected, patients may have already experienced prolonged hypoxia, particularly those with limited respiratory reserve. Alarmingly, hypoxemia accounts for approximately one-third of anesthesia-related malpractice claims in non-operating room settings.13 Furthermore, prolonged hypoxemia is associated with serious outcomes, including myocardial ischemia, irreversible neurological damage, and even death.14, 15, 16 Therefore, accurate pre-procedural identification of patients at high risk of hypoxemia is of paramount importance. Early risk stratification would enable anesthesiologists to implement graded preventive strategies (e.g., high-flow oxygen therapy, airway protection, or modification of sedation depth) before adverse events occur. In this context, the development of a dedicated hypoxemia risk assessment tool tailored to sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy represents a critical unmet clinical need, providing an objective framework for peri-procedural decision-making and proactive intervention.

Currently, risk stratification for hypoxemia during sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy relies predominantly on anesthesiologists’ comprehensive clinical judgment. Key evaluation parameters include fundamental indicators such as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status Classification System and body mass index (BMI), along with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)-specific screening tools.17,18 However, these methods fail to adequately account for procedure-specific factors in sedated endoscopy, such as the impacts of bite block placement, scope insertion, left lateral decubitus positioning, and abdominal compression on airway management. Consequently, their ability to accurately predict hypoxemia in this specific procedural context remains limited. To date, high-quality prediction models for hypoxemia during sedation for gastrointestinal endoscopy are scarce. An earlier study, although it preliminarily explored risk factors such as age, BMI, and habitual snoring, was constrained by its small sample size (n = 308) and single-center design. Moreover, the absence of external validation substantially limited the model’s clinical generalizability.9 Recently, a retrospective study attempted to develop a prediction model for middle-aged and elderly outpatients undergoing sedated gastroscopy using machine learning methods. However, this model was based solely on data from patients receiving etomidate-propofol combined induction anesthesia and did not encompass other widely used anesthetic regimens, such as propofol-only induction. Furthermore, the external validation of this model was derived from a single regional hospital, lacking extensive multi-center validation with large-scale samples.19 In addition, the performance of clinical prediction models may vary substantially across different regions because of heterogeneity in patient characteristics, clinical practice patterns, and baseline outcome rates. Differences in the incidence of hypoxemia across institutions or geographic areas may lead to miscalibration when a model developed in one population is applied to another. Therefore, recalibration strategies are often necessary to adjust the model to local outcome prevalence and improve its predictive accuracy in new settings.

We aimed to develop and validate a robust and accurate hypoxemia prediction model for sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy.

Methods

In response to these unmet clinical needs, we conducted a prospective multicenter study with a large sample size to develop an independently validated hypoxemia risk prediction model for gastrointestinal endoscopy sedation, which is the first model based on about 12K patients (HAPPY-12K). The model incorporates objective parameter from difficult airway assessment (DAA) tools (e.g., difficult mask ventilation and difficult intubation) with validated OSA screening instruments (STOP-Bang questionnaire and NoSAS score) to enable rapid outpatient risk stratification. In addition, model recalibration was performed in external datasets to account for regional heterogeneity in hypoxemia incidence and to optimize model calibration across different geographic areas. The derived risk profiles will facilitate tailored clinical decision-making, including high-flow nasal oxygen preoxygenation, anesthesia protocol optimization, and procedure modality selection.

The study was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300074128), and adheres to the TRIPOD guideline for reporting.20

Definition of primary outcome

There remains no global consensus on the diagnostic threshold for hypoxemia during procedural sedation, with SpO2 cutoffs of 90%, 92%, 94% and 95% all reported in published literature.8,21,22 Among these cutoffs, 90% and 95% are the most frequently adopted in clinical studies. Therefore, the primary outcome of our study was the occurrence of hypoxemia during sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy, defined as a drop in SpO2 below 95% for a duration lasting more than 10 s.18 Our definition was determined according to the actual clinical environment of outpatient sedated endoscopy and existing clinical evidence. (i) Given the relatively limited facilities and resuscitation resources in outpatient settings, using a loose threshold SpO2 < 95% enables the anesthesiologist to intervene promptly with adequate time (e.g., adjusting airway support or increasing oxygen supply), before hypoxemia progresses to more severe stage. (ii) To reduce false-positive alarms caused by transient factors such as brief positional changes or temporary monitor signal interference, a hypoxemic event was defined as lasting over 10 s. This criterion enhances detection specificity and ensures that only events with meaningful clinical implication are captured. We also performed sensitivity analysis using a strict threshold SpO2 < 90%.

In addition, EtCO2 monitoring is more sensitive for early detection of respiratory depression than SpO2, and its value in identifying hypoventilation before overt hypoxemia is well recognized. However, our study was conducted in an outpatient gastrointestinal endoscopy setting, where resources and monitoring configurations vary across clinical centers. To maintain methodological consistency and improve generalizability to routine clinical practice, we used continuous SpO2 monitoring, which is universally available in almost all endoscopy units.

Sample size estimation

Sample size was estimated using the R package pmsampsize.23 The incidence of hypoxemia during sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy was determined based on multiple sources. Our previous study reported an incidence of 17%.18 However, the incidence varies considerably due to heterogeneity of population characteristics, anesthesia protocols and study designs. Based on several studies published by representative journals in the field of gastrointestinal endoscopy, the incidence ranges from 8.4% to 21.2%, with a median 11%,6, 7, 8, 9, 10 which is slightly lower than our pilot finding. To ensure a conservative estimation of sample size, the expected incidence was set at 11%. Regarding the number of predictors, we totally collected 29 preoperative factors in the questionnaire. Predictors were defined as factors either with significant main effects or two-way interactions. Since, we needed to evaluate both main effects and all pairwise interactions. The total number of indicators was 435, the sum of 29 and C(29, 2), where C(·) denotes the combination function. For HAPPY-12K, we assumed 30 predictors in the model. We anticipated the concordance index (C-index) of 0.80 and applied a shrinkage factor of 0.9 (the default in pmsampsize), indicating that the predictor effect in the model would need to be shrunk by 10% to mitigate overfitting. The random-number seed was set to be 260,423 to allow reproducible simulation of data and to approximate the Cox-Snell R2 based on the given C-index and incidence. Under these assumptions, the estimated sample size was 2097. Accounting for a 15% rate of missing data, the final sample size for model development was adjusted to 2468.

Study populations

This multicenter prospective cohort study consecutively enrolled outpatients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy between May 2020 and November 2023 at seven hospitals: (i) The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (collecting data in three sequential batches), (ii) Nanjing First Hospital, (iii) Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, (iv) The Second People’s Hospital of Changzhou, (v) Subei People’s Hospital, (vi) Jiangsu Taizhou People’s Hospital, and (vii) Xinjiang Kezhou People’s Hospital. Participants from Hospitals 1–6 are of Han Chinese ethnicity. Hospitals 1–4 are located in Southern Jiangsu, which is the economically developed and urbanized region, whereas, Hospitals 5–6 represent Northern Jiangsu, a less economically developed area. Jiangsu Province, one of China’s most populous provinces, experiences substantial population movement both intra-provincially and inter-provincially, making it broadly representative of the Han Chinese population. In contrast, Hospital 7 exclusively enrolled outpatients of Uygur Chinese ethnicity, the third largest ethnic minority group in China, the vast majority of whom reside in Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region.

Patient inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: outpatients aged ≥ 18 years, with ASA physical status classification I-III, scheduled for gastrointestinal endoscopy. Eligible participants were also required to have complete preoperative assessments, laboratory examinations, medical records, and relevant medical histories.

Exclusion criteria included: (i) severe pre-existing respiratory insufficiency; (ii) concomitant severe dysfunction of vital organs (e.g., heart failure, liver or kidney failure); (iii) mental disorders; (iv) known allergy to propofol or its emulsifiers; and (v) acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding.

Variables collected in the questionnaire

A total of 34 variables were collected and categorized into seven domains. Where applicable, explicit measurement criteria and numeric thresholds were used to ensure objectivity and quantification. (i) Demographic and anthropometric characteristics: sex, age, smoking status, height, weight and BMI; (ii) Major comorbidities and health conditions: diagnosed according to established clinical criteria including hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, infection of the upper respiratory tract, cardiac functional assessment (e.g., patient’s physical exercise tolerance) and ASA physical status (grade I-Ⅲ); (iii) Preoperative auxiliary examination abnormalities: electrocardiography diagnosis (classified as normal or abnormal based on automated machine readings followed by physician confirmation), chest computed tomography (CT) diagnosis (classified as presence or absence of pulmonary inflammation based on radiology reports). Notably, although chest CT examination was mandatory for all outpatients before admission during the pandemic of COVID-19. Subsequent analysis deleted CT diagnosis, since it is not a routine examination for outpatients; (iv) OSA-related clinical history: snoring, snoring loudly (audible with the door closed), discovered sleep apnea; (v) Airway assessments: neck circumference (>40 cm defined as short neck with large circumference), thyromental distance, large tongue (defined during relaxed tongue position at the level of the lower teeth; positive if the tongue was observed above the occlusal surface of the mandible), Mallampati grade, limited jaw protrusion, limited mouth opening, beard, lack of teeth; (vi) Baseline vital signs: heart rate, systolic blood pressure, and SpO2; (vii) Intraoperative anesthetic and procedural data: induced dose of propofol, total dose of propofol, endoscopic operation time and gastric retention, which were not preoperative information and were removed before subsequent analysis. Totally, 29 variables were remained for model developed and validation.

Anesthesia protocol and operating procedures

A uniform anesthesia protocol and operating procedures were adopted across all participating hospitals. Sedation was administered exclusively by trained anesthesiologists. Throughout the procedure, all outpatients were positioned in the left lateral decubitus position with knees slightly bent.

In all seven endoscopy units, it is routine practice to sedate patients undergoing endoscopic procedures with propofol alone. The induction dose of propofol was 1–2 mg/kg based on the patient’s actual body weight; for these obese patients, the dose was calculated based on ideal body weight. Gastrointestinal endoscopic procedures were initiated only when the patients exhibited no response to verbal stimuli, had absent corneal and eyelash reflex, showed no movement upon jaw thrust, and did not respond to firm pressure applied to the posterior border of the mandibular ramus. This depth of sedation corresponded to a modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (modified OAA/S) score of 1 or lower. Additional propofol (10–20 mg per dose) was administered intraoperatively based on the patient’s response to painful stimuli.

Vital signs were monitored using standard ASA monitors, including non-invasive blood pressure, electrocardiography and SpO2. All patients received oxygen inhalation via bilateral nasal cannula at 5–7 L/min throughout the procedure. In the event of a decrease in SpO2 below 95% lasting more than 10 s, the anesthesia provider performed the necessary airway modification, including chin lift, jaw thrust or insertion of a nasal airway. If these measures were insufficient to improve ventilation, the digestive endoscopist halted the operation, removed the endoscope, and administered Bag-Valve-Mask ventilation.

Missing value imputation

Missing values were addressed using multiple imputation implemented with the R package mice. To prevent potential data leakage, imputation was performed separately for each hospital before model development and validation. Continuous variables were imputed using random forest regression, while categorical and ordinal variables were imputed using random forest classification. Due to very low missing rate (Supplementary Table S1), five imputed datasets were generated with fifty iterations to ensure convergence. The random forest-based approach was chosen for its ability to capture complex relationships without relying on parametric assumptions.

HAPPY-12K development and validation

The workflow of our study was presented in Fig. 1. To develop and validate HAPPY-12K, we applied the 3-D modeling strategy proposed in our previous study.24, 25, 26, 27 The 3-D modeling strategy, composed of including Double Types of Effects, Double Steps of Screening, and Double Steps of Modeling, has been successfully applied to build prognostic models for glioma, breast cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and lung cancer.24, 25, 26, 27

Flow chart of model development and validation of HAPPY-12K.

Double types of effects

Our objective was to identify predictors with either main effects or two-way interactions. (i) For the main effect analysis, we utilized the easy-to-interpret logistic regression model and adopted forward stepwise regression strategy to screen out predictors with significant main effects, while minimizing collinearity. (ii) For the interaction analysis, we fixed these predictors with main effects in the model and used the same way to search for the significant pairwise interactions.

Double steps of screening

We adopted a two-step design consisting of the discovery and validation phases, to screen for robust predictors. (i) In the discovery phase, we utilized some outpatients (n = 2518) from Hospital 1 (Batch 1) to identify significant predictors. (ii) In the validation phase, we confirmed their significance using the other outpatients (n = 2327) from Hospital 1 (Batch 1). Significant predictors were ultimately retained in the model if they met all of the following criteria: (i) P ≤ 0.05 in the discovery phase; (ii) P ≤ 0.05 in the validation phase; (iii) consistent direction of effects in both phases.

Double steps of modeling

HAPPY-12K was developed and subsequently validated across diverse populations (Table 1). (i) In the model development step, HAPPY-12K was fitted using significant and robust predictors with main effects or interactions in Training Set consisting of 2518 outpatients from Hospital 1 (Batch 1). (ii) In the model validation step, we assessed its performance across various populations with different enrollment time periods, hospital locations, geographic regions and ethnicities. Another 2327 participants from Hospital 1 (Batch 1) served as Testing Set 1. Then, HAPPY-12K was internally validated using outpatients enrolled during the same period. For external validation, an independent 2179 patients from Hospital 1 (Batch 2) comprised Testing Set 2, which was used to validate the model in the outpatients enrolled in a different time period. Testing Set 3 consisted of 4227 Southern Jiangsu participants from Hospital 1 (Batch 3) and Hospital 2–4, externally evaluating model performance across different time periods and hospital locations. Testing Set 4 was composed of 587 Northern Jiangsu outpatients from Hospitals 5–6, further assessing the model in another geographic region. In contrast to Testing Set 1–4, which were of Han Chinese ethnicity, Testing Set 5 consisted of 119 outpatients of Uygur Chinese ethnicity from Hospital 7, and served only for a preliminary trans-ethnic validation, due to its small sample size.

Sensitivity analysis

We acknowledge that our primary definition of hypoxemia may capture relatively mild events. Therefore, we developed and validated the model using a more stringent threshold (SpO2 < 90% lasting > 10 s), following the same workflow described above. We did not collect information using the cutoff of either 94% or 92%, since the former is close to the 95%, and the latter is close to 90%.

Given the elevated risk of desaturation in elderly patients (≥65 years, WHO criteria28) and those with high BMI (≥28 kg/m2, Chinese criteria29), we further performed subgroup analyses stratified by age and BMI to evaluate model performance in these subpopulations. The results of subgroup analyses were visualized using the R package forestplot.

Model evaluation

Model discrimination

The discriminative ability was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated by the R package pROC, and its 95% confidence intervals (CI) was estimated by 2000 bootstrap samples.30 Additionally, sensitivity (recall), specificity, positive predictive value (PPV, also referred to as precision), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy were also evaluated at the threshold that maximized the Youden’s index. The Youden’s index, defined as sensitivity + specificity-1, measures the maximum potential effectiveness of a prediction model by balancing the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity.

However, given the low incidence of hypoxemia (ranging from 10% to 30% across datasets), we also reported precision–recall curves (PRC) alongside the ROC curves to account for the uncertainty due to class imbalance, which was implemented by R package PRROC. Furthermore, traditional accuracy can be misleading in the analysis of imbalanced data. Therefore, we primarily evaluated model performance using the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC), the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), balanced accuracy and the F1 score.

Model calibration

Model calibration was assessed by the calibration slope, expected-to-observed (E:O) ratio, and the Brier score. The predicted probability of hypoxemia was calculated from a logistic regression model, logit(probability) = α + β × LP, where LP is the weighted linear combination of predictors. The weight of each predictor corresponds to its coefficient derived from the logistic regression model fitted in Training Set. A calibration curve was plotted to visualize the agreement between average predicted (expected) number of events and the average observed number of events. Brier score represents the average squared difference between the predicted probability and the actual binary outcome. Perfect calibration corresponds to a calibration slope of 1, an E:O ratio of 1, and a Brier score of 0.

To account for geographic and ethnic heterogeneity across different hospitals, which led to substantial difference in hypoxemia incidence (Table 1), we additionally performed model recalibration.31, 32, 33 First, the simplest form of recalibration was applied to address the calibration-in-the-large (intercept calibration) by merely updating the α to adjust the difference of baseline hypoxemia incidence between training set and testing set, while all coefficients of predictors were preserved from Training Set. Second, the logistic recalibration (slope & intercept recalibration) applied a uniform correction factor (β) to the coefficients of predictors to adjust for heterogeneity of effects of predictors between training set and testing set. Model recalibration was demonstrated to significantly improve the regional model performance.34

Clinical utility

The decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to quantitatively assess the clinical utility of HAPPY-12K, including identifying the net benefit (NB) of identifying high-risk patients who truly needed the intervention and net reduction (NR) of unnecessary interventions.

Model comparison

Since no validated hypoxemia risk model currently exists for outpatients undergoing sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy, we selected five widely used clinical risk assessment tools from related domains sharing common pathophysiological pathways.16,33, 34, 35 These tools were originally developed as diagnostic and severity scoring systems of OSA and DAA, rather than formal prediction models. The scoring systems of five tools are summarized as follows:

(i)NoSAS questionnaire35: neck circumference > 40 cm (4 points), BMI 25–30 kg/m2 (3 points), BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 (5 points), snoring (2 points), age > 55 years (4 points), male sex (2 points).

NoSAS questionnaire35: neck circumference > 40 cm (4 points), BMI 25–30 kg/m2 (3 points), BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 (5 points), snoring (2 points), age > 55 years (4 points), male sex (2 points).

(ii)STOP-Bang questionnaire36: one point is added for each of the following: snoring, tiredness, observed apnea, high blood pressure, BMI > 35 kg/m2, age > 50 years, neck circumference > 40 cm, and male sex.

STOP-Bang questionnaire36: one point is added for each of the following: snoring, tiredness, observed apnea, high blood pressure, BMI > 35 kg/m2, age > 50 years, neck circumference > 40 cm, and male sex.

We previously developed an unvalidated prediction model, denoted NoSAS + MMP, by applying logistic regression model to the scores from NoSAS questionnaire and the MMP.18 For head-to-head comparison, we then reproduced these five tools strictly according to their original scoring systems, and newly developed five models using their scores by logistic regression model (NoSAS, STOP-Bang, DMV, DI and MMP). In addition, we developed another five prediction models solely based on the same set of original variables (without categorization), denoted NoSAS∗+MMP∗, NoSAS∗, STOP-Bang∗, DMV∗, and DI∗. Beyond the aforementioned metrics used in model evaluation, we further compared these different models using the net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination index (IDI).40

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) among groups. Categorized variables were characterized by frequency (n) and proportion (%) and were compared using Chi-square test among groups. Odds ratio (OR) and its 95% CI of each predictor was derived from logistic regression model. All the statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant, unless otherwise specified.

Results

Participant characteristics

We enrolled a total of 11,957 participants. The study comprised a training set (n = 2518), in which the hypoxemia incidence rate was 10.37%, and five testing sets (n = 9439) with incidence rates ranging from 8.40% to 28.45%. As shown in Table 1, significant differences were observed for demographic, medical history, and clinical characteristics across the datasets, indicating substantial population heterogeneity. Detailed information was presented in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

HAPPY-12K exhibited acceptable calibration across the Han Chinese populations in Training Set and Testing Sets 1–4, with all calibration slopes remaining below 1.2. The only exception was the Testing Set 5, which comprised a small sample of Uygur Chinese patients (Supplementary Table S8, Fig. 3).

For example, in Combined Testing Set 1–5, the Brier score of 0.100 was less than 0.1310, which equals 15.49% × (1–15.49%). Clinical utility of HAPPY-12K

DCA demonstrated that using HAPPY-12K to guide clinical decisions would yield substantial benefit compared with strategies of intervening for all patients or intervening for none. As shown in Fig. 4, the NB plot displays the threshold probability on the x-axis (representing the minimum risk at which a clinician would intervene) and the NB on the y-axis (which balances true positives against false positives weighted by the threshold). At a clinically reasonable threshold (Pt) of 0.3, selected based on clinical consensus, HAPPY-12K identified additional 49.67 true-positive patients per 1000 patients who should receive intervention across different datasets, compared with the “intervene for none” strategy (Supplementary Table S12). Conversely, relative to the “intervene for all” strategy, HAPPY-12K avoided 59.96% of unnecessary clinical interventions while ensuring that no high-risk patients were missed in Combined Testing Set 1–5 (Supplementary Table S13). By varying the threshold probability from 0.1 to 0.6, decision curves for HAPPY-12K predominantly remained higher than other strategies across a spectrum of threshold probabilities, indicating its consistent clinical utility and suitability for implementation (Figs. 4 and 5). HAPPY-12K achieved an average NB of 45.21‰ and an average NR of 59.54% in Combined Testing Set 1–5 (Supplementary Tables S12 and S13). A national survey in China estimated that 12,301,350 patients receive sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy across the whole nation annually.3 HAPPY-12K would identify over half a million (556,144 = 12,301,350 × 45.21‰) patients with truly developing hypoxemia, adjusted for false positives. Meanwhile, assuming an incidence rate of hypoxemia was 15.49% (Combined Testing Set 1–5), compared to the most extreme strategy of intervening in every patient, HAPPY-12K could help avoid over six million (6,189,702 = 12,301,350 × (1–15.49%) × 59.54%) unnecessary interventions.

Sensitivity analysis of HAPPY -12K performance

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses stratified by BMI and age showed that HAPPY-12K exhibited similar performance across different subgroups in Combined Testing Set 1–5. The AUC values were 0.816, 0.798, 0.751 and 0.873 in the obese, non-obese, elderly and non-elderly populations (Supplementary Table S14), respectively, indicating relatively stable discrimination. The model calibration remained consistently acceptable (calibration slope < 1.15), similar to those in the general population (Supplementary Table S15).

All newly developed models, including HAPPY-12K, displayed reduced AUC in the elderly subgroup, a high-risk population41 where accurate prediction is most needed. This deviation might be caused by two factors. (i) The hypoxemia incidence (19.54%) of elderly population in Combined Testing Set 1–5 is higher than that (10.36%) of the general population in Training Set, indicating substantial population heterogeneity. (ii) We retrained all models separately for elderly and non-elderly populations in Training Set (Supplementary Table S16). The effects and significances of predictors in the model for elderly population differed from those in the original model derived from the general population. For HAPPY-12K, five main-effect predictors and the interaction pair were not significant in the elderly population. Furthermore, the effects of seven predictors changed by more than 25%. In contrast, only one predictor was not significant and its effect changed by > 25% in the non-elderly population. Hence, HAPPY-12K, along with other models, lost predictive ability in the elderly population.

Although we observed differences in the predictor effects between the elderly and non-elderly populations, this heterogeneity was not robustly significant. Therefore, we did not identify any factor having a significant interaction with age through Training Set and Testing Set 1 (Supplementary Table S16). In future, all models need to be tailored to the elderly population.

Alternative hypoxemia definition

When a more stringent definition of hypoxemia (SpO2 < 90%) was applied, HAPPY-12K outperformed other existing models and maintained acceptable discriminative ability (AUC = 0.827, Supplementary Table S17), acceptable calibration (calibration slope = 0.798, E:O ratio = 0.889, Brier score = 0.033, Supplementary Table S18) and good E:O ratio of 1.023 with intercept recalibration (Supplementary Table S19), in Combined Testing Set 1–5.

Model comparison of HAPPY-12K with other newly developed models

Predictive factors

In our study, we developed a series of hypoxemia risk prediction models using scores or raw variables from the scoring systems for OSA and DAA. Totally, 17 predictors were involved in these models and could be categorized into four categories: basic characteristics, sleep apnea symptoms, airway features and hemodynamic indicators (Supplementary Table S20). HAPPY-12K comprises eight predictors, six of which are airway features and hemodynamic indicators. STOP-Bang also consists of eight predictors, half of which are sleep apnea symptoms and airway features. While, NoSAS + MMP is composed of predictors from NoSAS questionnaires and Mallampati grade. BMI was the most frequently used predictor, followed by age, history of snoring, short neck with large circumference, and Mallampati grade.

Model performance

Seven models (HAPPY-12K, NoSAS + MMP, NoSAS∗+MMP∗, NoSAS, NoSAS∗, STOP-Bang∗ and DMV∗) exhibited acceptable model discrimination with AUC ≥ 0.77 in all testing sets (Fig. 2). However, HAPPY-12K possessed significantly (P ≤ 0.05) higher discriminative ability than these models (Supplementary Table S6) in all testing sets, except the small-sample Testing Set 5. Meanwhile, HAPPY-12K appeared better reclassification ability than the other six competing models. Based on NRI statistics in Combined Testing Set 1–5, HAPPY-12K correctly reclassified 15.2–19.9% more patients into right risk categories, compared to NoSAS + MMP and NoSAS∗+MMP∗. Further, its advantage was even more pronounced compared with the remaining four models, with NRIs ranging from 58.8 to 78.9% (Supplementary Table S21). Based on IDI statistics, the predicted probabilities from HAPPY-12K for hypoxemic (or non-hypoxemic) patients were averagely 1.3–1.6% higher (or lower) than those from NoSAS + MMP and NoSAS∗+MMP∗. Similarly, its superiority is even clearer compared with the other four models, with IDIs ranging from 5.0 to 11.4% (Supplementary Table S21). In addition, although HAPPY-12K shown comparable sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, accuracy (Supplementary Table S7) and AUPRC (Supplementary Fig. S3) with NoSAS + MMP and NoSAS∗+MMP∗, it presented slightly higher balanced accuracy, F1 score, MCC and AUPRC (Supplementary Table S7, Supplementary Fig. S3), adjusted for the low incidence rate of hypoxemia. HAPPY-12K, NoSAS + MMP and NoSAS∗+MMP∗ displayed similar model calibration (Fig. 3) and comparable clinical utility (Figs. 4 and 5).

For the stringent definition of hypoxemia (SpO2 < 90%), HAPPY-12K (AUC = 0.827) outperformed NoSAS + MMP (AUC = 0.799) and NoSAS∗+MMP∗ (AUC = 0.805) in discriminative ability (Supplementary Table S17).

Based on comprehensive evaluation of model discrimination, calibration and clinical utility, HAPPY-12K emerges as the optimal newly developed model, followed by NoSAS∗+MMP∗ and NoSAS + MMP.

Online software deployment

To simplify the application of the HAPPY-12K in clinical settings, we deployed a free online software tool, which is accessible at http://bigdata.njmu.edu.cn/HAPPY-12K/. Users only need to upload the values of the clinical variables required by HAPPY-12K to directly obtain the predicted probability of hypoxemia.

To stratify patients into low-, medium-, and high-risk groups based on the predicted probability from HAPPY-12K, we first applied the Youden’s index derived from Training Set to determine the optimal threshold for classifying high-risk individuals. Specifically, the threshold that maximized the Youden’s index was selected as the cutoff for high risk, denoted as Phigh. Patients with a predicted probability ≥ Phigh were assigned to the high-risk group. Among the remaining patients, we used the median predicted probability as the cutoff to distinguish the low-risk group from the medium-risk group.

As demonstrated by our results, HAPPY-12K developed in Training Set maintains satisfactory discrimination in heterogeneous external populations, but shows some degree of miscalibration in terms of E:O ratio. However, intercept recalibration effectively addresses this issue. Therefore, we provide the original model predictions by default, together with an option for intercept recalibration allowing users to supply the expected incidence rate for their local settings. Note that recalibration does not change the discriminative ability of HAPPY-12K. Users are advised to re-evaluate calibration after recalibration to ensure adequate performance in their specific population.

Here is a real-world example. Consider an outpatient with the following characteristics: BMI of 35 kg/m2, MAP of 102 mmHg, neck circumference of 41 cm, Mallampati grade of I, limited jaw protrusion, self-reported history of snoring, a thyromental distance of 7 cm, no evidence of a large tongue, and entering this information into the online tool yields a predicted probability of 92.1% for hypoxemia during outpatient sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy. This means that among 1000 patients with the same profile, approximately 921 would be expected to develop hypoxemia. Consequently, this patient is classified into the high-risk group.

To ensure objectivity and quantification for definition of predictors used in the online tool, the web interface was enhanced with hover-over tooltips and simple diagrams to clarify the definitions of each predictor in a busy clinical setting, thereby reducing inter-observer bias.

Discussion

Hypoxemia was a critical adverse event during sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy, often caused by many factors, e.g., airway obstruction or respiratory depression.42,43 Therefore, it is essential to comprehensively identify hypoxemia associated factors and formally develop a prediction model to target these high-risk patients and tailor anesthesia protocols. In our large-scale multicenter study involving nearly 12,000 patients, we evaluated 29 various factors with prior predictive information, meanwhile, newly developed and externally validated a series of hypoxemia risk prediction models predominantly in Han Chinese populations from different regions of Jiangsu Province, with a small-sample Uyghur Chinese population from Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region. HAPPY-12K demonstrated satisfactory discrimination, acceptable calibration and good clinical utility even among diverse populations. Generally, HAPPY-12K outperformed other newly developed models by a head-to-head comparison.

Notably, NoSAS∗+MMP∗ is second only to HAPPY-12K, with a very small gap and almost comparable performance. (i) In terms of accessibility, they share four predictors (BMI, history of snoring, short neck with large circumference and Mallampati grade). NoSAS∗+MMP∗ has two additional demographic predictors (age and sex); whereas HAPPY-12K has three additional airway-feature predictors (limited jaw protrusion, large tongue, and short thyromental distance) and one additional hemodynamic indicator (pre-operative MAP). Indeed, all outpatients scheduled for sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy are required to have their blood pressure assessed. Thus, pre-operative MAP is already inherent to the standard procedure without requiring additional time. For the other three airway features, a competent anesthesiologist needs only a glance to assess them, which takes no more than 30 s. Therefore, this approach does not significantly increase the burden of clinical assessment in real-world practice. In fact, such a small investment of time can yield great clinical returns, making it worthwhile. (ii) In terms of clinical benefits, given the extremely large number of outpatients undergoing sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy, although HAPPY-12K shows only relatively modest advantages in the statistics used to characterize model performance, the absolute number of additional patients who could benefit from it is by no means negligible. Here, we still assume there are 12,301,350 patients receiving sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy across China annually, as estimated in year 2016,3 with a hypoxemia incidence rate of 15.49%. Compared with NoSAS∗+MMP∗, HAPPY-12K has an NRI of 15.2%, which is the sum of −30.37% for hypoxemia group and 45.54% for non-hypoxemia group. HAPPY-12K slightly underestimates the probability of hypoxemia and is more conservative than NoSAS∗+MMP∗. It moves down the predicted probability for 578,639 (12,301,350 × 15.49% × −30.37%) patients in hypoxemia group, and for 4,734,278 (12,301,350 × (1–15.49%) × 45.54%) patients in non-hypoxemia group, and yields a net number of correctly reclassified individuals of up to 4.16 million (4,734,278–578,639 = 4,155,639). Nevertheless, NRI counts every correct reclassification equally, without accounting for the clinical cost of false positives. In contrast, NB and NR explicitly incorporate a weighting factor derived from the decision threshold (Pt), which penalizes false positives relative to true positives. Hence, HAPPY-12K can identify an additional 12 thousand (556,144–544,089 = 12,055) patients with truly developing hypoxemia, and can avoid an additional 78 thousand (6,189,702–6,111,733 = 77,969) unnecessary interventions. The predicted number of sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy by 2030 will be 24,816,636,3 which is double that of year 2016. By then, HAPPY-12K will bring double the benefits. In brief, there is a trad-off between model accessibility and model performance. NoSAS∗+MMP∗ has slightly better convenience in use, whereas HAPPY-12K has slightly better accuracy. We acknowledge that both are satisfactory models. To support flexible clinical application, we have also deployed a free online tool for NoSAS∗+MMP∗, allowing rapid risk evaluation in busy clinical settings.

The clinical countermeasure for a patient is personalized, which depend on the predicted risk. Typically, there are three levels of clinical interventions. (i) For low-risk patients, routine vital sign monitoring combined with low-flow oxygen therapy is sufficient. (ii) For moderate-to-high-risk patients, it is necessary to prepare in advance high-flow nasal cannula oxygen therapy devices, nasopharyngeal airways and non-invasive respiratory support equipment, with continuous intensive monitoring provided by anesthesiologists with extensive clinical experience throughout the process. (iii) For extremely high-risk patients, relevant tracheal intubation instruments should be equipped in advance. The advanced airway management team should be called to stand by simultaneously. And, it is advisable to transfer the patients to the operating room for gastrointestinal endoscopy if necessary, so as to ensure that all types of rescue resources can be allocated and used in a timely manner.

The predictive power of HAPPY-12K stems from the integration of predictors that reflect the multifactorial nature of procedure-induced hypoxemia. The model incorporates eight independent predictors covering a basic characteristic (BMI), sleep apnea symptoms (history of snoring), airway features (short neck with large circumference, limited jaw protrusion, large tongue, short thyromental distance and Mallampati grade) and a perioperative hemodynamic indicator (MAP). Airway features are anatomical indicators, well-recognized risk factors for difficult airways. A high Mallampati grade indicates latent oropharyngeal collapse, while limited jaw protrusion restricts intraoperative airway opening. Short thyromental distance, enlarged tongue, and thick short neck collectively narrow upper airway space and reduce airway stability, significantly increasing the susceptibility to airway obstruction and oxygen desaturation under sedation. Obesity further contributes to airway narrowing, reduced lung compliance, and increased sedation risks due to anatomical changes.44,45 High BMI, a key risk factor for hypoxemia, correlates strongly with adverse events during sedation.46,47 Obese patients often exhibit airway narrowing due to submental fat, pharyngeal tissue enlargement, and large tongue.48 Fat deposition in the neck and chest also restricts neck mobility, short thyromental distance, and high Mallampati grade, all of which elevate sedation risks. Additionally, chest wall fat reduces lung compliance, functional residual capacity, and expiratory reserve volume, leading to rapid SpO2 decline during apnea or hypoventilation.49 Short neck with large circumference, often associated with snoring, exacerbates oropharyngeal collapse during sedation.9,50

Elevated preoperative MAP was novel predictor identified in our study, and was only used in HAPPY-12K. It indicates underlying cardiovascular vulnerability and unrecognized sleep-disordered breathing, whose pathophysiological basis lies in two aspects: (i) Hypertensive patients typically present endothelial dysfunction, impaired left ventricular diastolic function, and decreased baroreflex sensitivity, leading to weakened hemodynamic compensatory capacity.51,52 Under propofol-induced vasodilation and myocardial depression, these patients are prone to hypotension and inadequate tissue perfusion, which triggers ventilation-perfusion mismatch, and may exacerbate respiratory depression through a hypoxia-perfusion mismatch cycle. (ii) Elevated preoperative MAP can serve as an important clinical marker of undiagnosed OSA, since over 30% of adults with hypertension are estimated to have OSA.53 Among patients with drug-resistant hypertension, the prevalence of OSA is estimated to be approximately 80%.54 Recurrent nocturnal hypoxemia in OSA activates the sympathetic nervous system, induces oxidative stress, and stimulates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), all of which contribute to sustained hypertension.55, 56, 57 Collectively, elevated preoperative MAP is not merely a transient hemodynamic abnormality, but a comprehensive indicator of latent cardiovascular dysfunction and occult OSA.

Notably, large tongue and short thyromental distance exhibited an antagonistic interaction. Two potential explanations are proposed. (i) A floor effect: when thyromental distance is severely shortened, the pharyngeal cross-sectional area is already critically reduced, such that further tongue enlargement no longer proportionally increases hypoxemia risk, as the risk curve plateaus. (ii) A preventive intervention effect: patients with both large tongue and short thyromental distance exhibit obvious high-risk airway features, which are readily recognized during preoperative evaluation. Such recognition tends to prompt anesthetists to adopt more proactive airway management strategies, including reduced sedation titration, prophylactic oxygen supplementation, jaw thrust maneuvers, and intensive monitoring. These preventive interventions may partially offset the additional anatomical risk associated with the coexistence of the two abnormalities, thereby resulting in an antagonistic interaction rather than a synergistic interaction. Ideally, all models should predict risk under a hypothetical “no-intervention” scenario. However, observed outcomes may be influenced by clinical interventions. Since this is a real-world study, and all procedures were performed exactly as in routine clinical practice, we did not restrict anesthesiologists from applying preventive protective measures when facing high-risk patients. If the second speculation is correct, the incidence of hypoxemia would be underestimated under preventive interventions. However, we are unable to determine which of the two explanations is the true underlying cause. Nevertheless, this question is not one that a prediction model is intended to answer.

Hypoxemia and apnea are critical during procedural sedation, particularly in obese patients with increased upper airway resistance and sleep apnea predisposition.58,59 Studies suggest that many patients experiencing hypoxemia during sedation may have undiagnosed sleep apnea,60 raising the question of whether higher scores on these questionnaires correlate with a greater risk of hypoxemia. In our study, the hypoxemia group shared characteristics with high-risk OSA populations, such as higher BMI, short neck with large circumference, large tongue, and history of snoring. This supports the inclusion of OSA risk evaluation in preoperative assessments for obese patients.61 We newly developed several other hypoxemia risk prediction models using scores or raw variables in OSA-related questionnaires (STOP-Bang and NoSAS). These models also showed satisfactory discriminative ability, which align with our previous model NoSAS + MMP.18 Nevertheless, their performance was inferior to HAPPY-12K. This may be attributed to two key factors: (i) Factors in these two questionnaires are basic characteristics and sleep apnea symptoms, which fails to adequately reflect the potential drug–physiological interactions during sedation, consequently leading to undetected cases of non-OSA-hypopnea syndrome patients with elevated pharmacological sensitivity. However, HAPPY-12K incorporates a special factor, preoperative MAP. As aforementioned, its elevation reflects underlying cardiovascular vulnerability and undiagnosed sleep-disordered breathing. Such patients are at higher risk for adverse drug–physiological interactions during sedation, including exaggerated hypotension and hypoxemia, because their compensatory mechanisms are already compromised. (ii) Some factors (e.g., daytime tiredness, and discovered sleep apnea) in the questionnaires are subjective measurements, which may be biased by a patient’s self-perception.

Baillard et al. identified DMV and DI as risk factors for hypoxemia during general anesthesia induction.38 Besides, DMV was also a predictor of airway complications.62 DI indicators primarily assess airway exposure ease, but in sedated patients maintaining spontaneous breathing, broader factors such as BMI and history of snoring may have a greater impact on hypoxemia risk. Though our newly developed models related with DMV and DI displayed acceptable discriminative ability, which aligns with Baillard’s study,38 they underperformed compared to HAPPY-12K, likely because they omitted two factors (short neck with large circumference and large tongue), which had strong effect on hypoxemia.

Our study was initiated in year 2020 with the primary objective of investigating hypoxemia incidence under a standardized sedation protocol. The decision to use propofol as the sole sedative agent was based on clinical availability, widespread practice, and the necessity for methodological consistency across the multi-year study period.

At the time of study initiation (2020), alternative sedatives were not yet suitable for adoption. The first formulation of remimazolam (Ruibaining) was approved in China only in December 2019, and its indication was initially limited to gastroscopy sedation; approval for colonoscopy was not granted until June 2020. Another formulation (Ruima) became available only in July 2020 with an initial indication restricted to colonoscopy. During the early post-marketing stage in 2020, both formulations had extremely limited clinical accessibility, uneven regional supply, and insufficient real-world experience for routine gastrointestinal endoscopy. Ciprofol, although approved for sedation in non-intubated procedures in 2020, did not receive its official drug approval certificate until February 2022. Consequently, it was not widely available in clinical settings and had not yet been promoted for routine gastrointestinal endoscopy sedation before 2022. In contrast, propofol remains the most widely used sedative in Chinese outpatient endoscopy practice. Choosing propofol as the sole anesthetic agent allowed us to minimize confounding and maintain strict control over study conditions from 2020 onwards. Introducing the novel agents (e.g., ciprofol or remimazolam) whose long-term safety and hypoxemia profiles were just beginning to be explored, would have introduced significant heterogeneity and compromised the longitudinal consistency of our data.

Furthermore, to ensure internal validity, we intentionally avoided concomitant opioids. Opioids vary in potency, onset time, and individual response, and their use would have introduced additional confounding that could obscure the true relationship between propofol monotherapy and hypoxemia. Instead, we maintained a uniform propofol-only protocol, which, at the time of study initiation, reflected a widely adopted real-world practice in many outpatient endoscopy settings across China, particularly for diagnostic procedures.

In summary, our choice of propofol as the sole sedative was not a dismissal of the potential benefits of newer agents or opioid-based regimens. Rather, it was a methodological decision driven by the timeline of drug market entry, the need for a consistent and controlled study design, and the relevance to the standard of care at the time the study was conceived. This approach ensured the internal validity and cross-center comparability of our findings while providing a clinically meaningful baseline for future comparisons with emerging sedatives.

In conclusion, our study has several strengths. (i) This is the first study exclusively designed to develop and validate a hypoxemia risk prediction model. We indeed evaluated a series of newly developed models and confirmed HAPPY-12K’s superiority in model discrimination, model calibration, clinical utility and external generalizability through diverse populations. (ii) We innovatively applied a 3-D modeling strategy to develop HAPPY-12K to optimize predictor selection, model performance and robustness. The 3-D modeling strategy has successfully promoted four prediction models, i.e., APOLLO,24 ARTEMIS,25 ATHENA26 and Prophet.27 HAPPY-12K again demonstrated general applicability of the strategy. (iii) The large-sample and multi-region data consisting of about 12,000 patients enhances the robustness and generalizability of the model across multiple clinical settings. In addition, the Uygur Chinese population provides an opportunity for preliminary exploration of the model’s potential applicability in populations with different ethnic backgrounds. (iv) To ensure objectivity and quantification for definition of predictors, we utilized the acknowledged definitions to predictors. For example, short neck with large circumference was defined as neck circumference > 40 cm. The is a well-recognized cutoff, widely adopted in relevant clinical and epidemiological studies. Also, it can be measured objectively and conveniently with a soft tape. Another example is the evaluation of large tongue. We used a standardized observational method: subjects were asked to relax their tongue at the level of the lower teeth, and a tongue above the occlusal surface of the mandible was regarded as large tongue.55 Although this assessment relies on visual inspection, the procedure was strictly standardized, and all examinations were performed by well-trained investigators to reduce inter-observer variation as much as possible. (v) An interactive online tool was developed to facilitate clinical application of HAPPY-12K and NoSAS∗+MMP∗. The web interface was enhanced with hover-over tooltips and simple diagrams to clarify the definitions of each predictor in a busy clinical setting, thereby reducing inter-observer bias.

However, we acknowledge some limitations. (i) The discrimination of HAPPY-12K, along with other newly developed models, declined in elderly outpatients. The root cause is the substantial heterogeneity between non-elderly and elderly populations resulting in the failure of some existing predictors in the elderly population. Therefore, a critical future direction involves external validation of HAPPY-12K across diverse populations and clinical settings, coupled with dynamic model updating by learning from new data to refine its performance. A practical and feasible approach is to optimize the modeling framework. For variable selection, forward stepwise regression is a classical approach, but it may be less robust than modern regularization techniques (e.g., LASSO and elastic net) in terms of selection stability. We adopted the “Double Steps of Screening” strategy to partially mitigates this issue. For modeling method, although logistic regression ensures interpretability, machine learning methods (e.g., XGBoost) might better capture predictors with more complex effects (e.g., nonlinear effects, high-order interactions), potentially improving both discrimination and generalization performance. In the future, we plan to optimize HAPPY-12K using more advanced approaches. (ii) Findings are specific to gastroscopy under sedation; applicability to major surgeries (e.g., thoracic procedures) remains unvalidated. (iii) The cohort excluded ASA IV–V patients, who face higher hypoxemia risks. These high-risk patients typically undergo un-sedated exams or operating room-based anesthesia. (iv) Hypoxemia in our study was defined as SpO2 < 95% sustained for at least 10 s. This time-based criterion likely excludes transient, clinically insignificant oxygen desaturations caused by momentary physiological fluctuations. Compared with the definition of SpO2 < 90%, adopting SpO2 < 95% as an early warning threshold inevitably leads to a relatively high incidence of hypoxemic events, which is exactly what we anticipate for outpatient settings with limited resuscitation resources: early detection, risk alert and prompt intervention. Nevertheless, we have to admit that under the current relatively loose definition, there may still be clinically insignificant desaturation events. Furthermore, our definition is strongly supported by a recent multicenter randomized controlled trial,22 in which Lee et al. defined hypoxemia during procedural sedation for gastrointestinal endoscopy as SpO2 ≤ 95% for more than 5 s. The same threshold was also adopted by Baillard et al.38 Although their study focused on hypoxemia during general anesthesia induction, deep sedation for gastrointestinal endoscopy and general anesthesia share essentially identical core risks: both can induce drug-mediated respiratory depression and upper airway collapse. The underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of hypoxemia are highly consistent across the two clinical scenarios. Taken together, our definition of hypoxemia using 95% as a threshold is clinically reasonable. This alert threshold allows HAPPY-12K to provide early warning and creates a safety buffer for timely clinical action, thereby preventing serious adverse events. (v) There is no universal definition of hypoxemia in procedural sedation; 95% and 90% are commonly used thresholds for respiratory adverse events. Therefore, we did not collect data for SpO2 < 92% or 94%. Both loose (SpO2 < 95%) and stringent cutoffs (SpO2 < 90%) confirmed the robust model performance of HAPPY-12K. (vi) We validated the HAPPY-12K in a small-sample of 119 Uygur Chinese patients. Although its discriminative ability is quite acceptable (AUC = 0.771), while 95% CI was wide due to the limited number of hypoxemia events (n = 10). This serves as a preliminary trans-ethnic validation, and highlights the necessary of validation in large-sample populations in future. (vii) Clinically, the most important task is to discriminate between patients with and without hypoxemia. It is commendable that HAPPY-12K exhibits highly stable and satisfactory discriminative ability. Although its calibration was unacceptable in several inhomogeneous external datasets (i.e., Testing Set 3–5), it was a common phenomenon when a prediction model was applied to heterogeneous populations. This limitation may be partly attributable to differences between the training and testing sets in baseline hypoxemia incidence and patient characteristics. Therefore, HAPPY-12K should not be regarded as a fully plug-and-play tool for direct application in all external populations without prior evaluation. Nevertheless, by merely given the local hypoxemia incidence rate, the simplest intercept recalibration substantially improved the predictive accuracy of HAPPY-12K, which was implemented in the free online tool. It is worth mentioning that the model with slope and intercept recalibration perfectly addressed the issue, but external validation of the recalibrated model in the homogenous populations is warranted. Hence, we strongly suggest that the local setting should be assessed before implementation in a new population. The simplest way is to evaluate the hypoxemia incidence using existing large-scale studies or high-quality literature. If the hypoxemia incidence deviates substantially from 10.4%, intercept recalibration of HAPPY-12K improves model performance. Another more sophisticated way is slope and intercept recalibration using a small number of local samples (e.g., n = 80, ten times the number of predictors).63 Indeed, as the renowned British statistician George Box once said, “all models are wrong, but some are useful.” Given the widespread population heterogeneity and the inability to obtain samples that fully represent all types of populations, applying HAPPY-12K offers a cost-effective way to identify high-risk individuals in real-world practice, provided that recalibration is performed when necessary.

HAPPY-12K is a reliable hypoxemia risk prediction model for sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy, developed and validated using large-sample, multi-region populations.
