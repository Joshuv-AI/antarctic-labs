Intermuscular dissection by transanal endoscopic operation for a rectal lesion
Authors: Agustin Vaccaro, Esteban Gonzalez Salazar, Lucas Caram, Juan Pablo Campana, Ricardo Mentz, Gustavo Rossi
Published: 2026

The authors present the case of a 46-year-old male patient who underwent a transanal endoscopic operation (TEO) with intermuscular dissection for a mid-rectal lesion.

Colonoscopy revealed a vegetative lesion approximately 5 cm in size on the right posterolateral wall, and pathology findings were consistent with a tubulovillous adenoma. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a T1N0 tumor, with a negative circumferential resection margin (CRM) and no evidence of extramural venous invasion (EMVI).

The operative time was 180 min. The surgical procedure was successfully completed without intraoperative complications; estimated blood loss was minimal and not quantifiable. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 1 without any complications.

The patient was discharged on postoperative day 1, and no postoperative complications were observed.

Intermuscular dissection has emerged in recent years as an advanced resection technique for the treatment of rectal lesions, through either endoscopic or surgical approaches. This technique is based on resecting lesions along the dissection plane between the circular and longitudinal muscle layers [1].

Its foundation is based on achieving the deepest possible resection margin without compromising the total mesorectal excision plane. This approach allows for adequate submucosal staging while preserving the integrity of the rectal wall, making it potentially curative for T1 rectal cancer [2].

In addition, it avoids distortion of the total mesorectal excision plane that could be caused by a full-thickness resection, which could significantly impact surgical outcomes in the presence of adverse histopathological features [3, 4].

One of the main technical challenges of this technique is that it is performed through a transanal endoscopic platform, where instrument mobility and working space are limited. In this setting, precise identification and maintenance of the intermuscular plane between the circular and longitudinal muscle layers may be demanding, particularly in larger lesions. Hemostasis within this plane and closure of the rectal wall defect can also be challenging after wider resections. Careful dissection and meticulous hemostasis are therefore essential. For defect closure, barbed sutures may be a useful adjunct, allowing continuous tension-free closure and facilitating reconstruction of larger defects.

Conclusion

Intermuscular dissection of rectal lesions represents a valuable minimally invasive diagnostic and therapeutic alternative for early-stage lesions, allowing complete resection with an adequate specimen for pathological assessment, without compromising the anatomy for a potential future rectal resection.
