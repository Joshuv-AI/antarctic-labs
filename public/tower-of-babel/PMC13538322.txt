Measles Epidemiology, Transmission, and Surveillance Characteristics in Ethiopia, 2018–2024
Authors: Habtamu Milkias Wolde, Youngoh Bae, Ali Raza, Seung Won Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEthiopia remains a high-burden measles setting, yet the drivers of protracted transmission and regional susceptibility remain poorly understood. This study analyzes seven years of national surveillance data to characterize demographic shifts, disease severity, and the impact of system performance on outbreak dynamics.MethodsA longitudinal analysis of confirmed measles cases from 2018 to 2024 was conducted. Age-specific attack rates (AR) and case fatality rates (CFR) were calculated, and time-varying reproduction numbers (Rt) were estimated to assess transmission intensity. The relationship between surveillance latency (onset-to-confirmation) and outbreak characteristics, including duration and size, was evaluated.ResultsA total of 69,866 confirmed cases were analyzed, of which 12.5% were laboratory confirmed and 87.5% were epidemiologically linked. The highest burden was observed among children aged 1–4 years (AR: 195 per 100,000). Substantial regional variation was evident, with the median age of infection at 2.8 years in pastoralist regions (e.g., Somali) and 7.2 years in urban settings (e.g., Addis Ababa). The overall CFR was 0.8%, peaking at 1.12% among infants aged < 9 months. Transmission intensity remained elevated during epidemic peaks (Rt: 1.8–2.4). The median onset-to-confirmation delay was 18 days, exceeding the estimated measles serial interval. While longer outbreaks were associated with larger outbreak size, no significant association was observed between surveillance delay and outbreak duration.ConclusionPersistent measles transmission in Ethiopia appears to be driven primarily by regional immunity gaps among susceptible pediatric populations, while prolonged surveillance delays remain an important operational concern for timely outbreak detection and response. Elimination efforts should prioritize strengthening routine immunization, closing regional immunity gaps, and improving surveillance timeliness. These findings also provide practical insights for other low- and middle-income countries seeking to strengthen measles control programs and surveillance systems in heterogeneous transmission settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44197-026-00594-z.

Introduction

Measles is a highly contagious viral disease transmitted through respiratory droplets and remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality globally despite the availability of a safe and effective vaccine. Infection typically presents with fever, rash, cough, coryza, and conjunctivitis, and may lead to severe complications including pneumonia, encephalitis, and death, particularly among young children, malnourished populations, and individuals with limited access to healthcare [1, 2]. Globally, measles vaccination is estimated to have prevented nearly 60 million deaths between 2000 and 2024; however, measles continues to resurge in many regions due to stagnating routine immunization coverage, immunity gaps, and disruptions to health services [3, 4].

Recent global surveillance data indicate a marked increase in measles cases since 2019, with the African Region accounting for a substantial proportion of reported outbreaks and deaths [3–5]. Declines in routine measles-containing vaccine (MCV) coverage, compounded by population growth and delayed supplementary immunization activities (SIAs), have contributed to the accumulation of susceptible individuals and recurrent outbreaks [4–6]. Understanding the age distribution of measles cases is critical, as shifts in the age profile of infection often reflect underlying immunity gaps and changes in vaccination program performance [7, 8].

Ethiopia remains one of the countries in sub-Saharan Africa experiencing persistent measles transmission. Despite the introduction of routine measles vaccination in the Expanded Program on Immunization and repeated SIAs, large outbreaks continue to occur across multiple regions. National and subnational studies have consistently reported that measles predominantly affects children, but the affected age groups vary across time and settings. Surveillance analyses from Addis Ababa and other regions have documented a substantial burden among children aged under 5 years, as well as notable proportions of cases among school-aged children and adolescents, suggesting incomplete or waning immunity in older age groups [9–11]. Similar findings have been reported elsewhere in Africa, where shifts toward older age groups have been associated with suboptimal routine coverage and delayed vaccination [7–12].

However, the existing evidence base in Ethiopia is largely derived from regional analyses, short time periods, or studies that combine laboratory-confirmed, epidemiologically linked, and clinically compatible cases without a systematic focus on confirmed measles. Few studies have examined national surveillance data over multiple years to characterize age-specific patterns, regional heterogeneity, temporal dynamics, and surveillance performance indicators such as reporting delays. Moreover, while attack rates and case fatality ratios are frequently cited in outbreak reports, comprehensive national analyses that relate these measures to age and vaccination status using confirmed case definitions remain limited.

High-quality measles surveillance is a cornerstone of measles control and elimination strategies. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends timely case detection, laboratory confirmation, and detailed analysis of surveillance data to guide outbreak response and immunization strategies [2, 13]. Delays between symptom onset, specimen collection, and laboratory reporting can undermine outbreak detection and response efforts, yet these indicators are rarely quantified in published analyses from high-burden settings [14]. In the context of Ethiopia’s large and diverse population, national-level analyses are essential to identify priority age groups and geographic areas for targeted interventions.

To address these gaps, we analysed national measles surveillance data from Ethiopia between 2018 and 2024 to describe the age profile of measles outbreaks and age-specific attack rates and case fatality rates among confirmed cases. We further examined the regional distribution of cases, temporal trends, and delays across key steps of the surveillance process which is barely studied in Ethiopia. By leveraging a comprehensive national line-list over six years, this study provides the most detailed assessment to date of age-specific measles epidemiology in Ethiopia and offers evidence directly relevant for strengthening routine immunization, optimizing SIAs, and improving measles surveillance performance.

Methods

Study Setting and Period

This study included all of the administrative regions in Ethiopia. The country has 12 regions and two city administrations as of 2023. The national Public Health Emergency Management and its regional branches under the Ethiopian Public Health Institute are the primary bodies overseeing surveillance activities of diseases in Ethiopia including measles. Additionally, surveillance focal points and teams are established within healthcare facilities to identify and report surveillance diseases as well as take part in emergency response activities whenever outbreaks are detected. For the purpose of contextualization and comparisons, the regions and city administrations in Ethiopia were categorized into two settings: pastoralist/emerging and urban/agrarian regions. Afar, Somali, Gambella and Benishangul Gumuz regions were categorized under pastoralist/emerging regions while Addis Ababa, Dire Dawa, Harari, Oromia, Tigray, South Ethiopia, Central Ethiopia, South West Ethiopia and Sidama Regions were classified under urban/agrarian regions. This study covered the period between 2018 and 2024.

Data Source

We analysed national measles surveillance data collected in Ethiopia between 2018 and 2024. The dataset included individual-level records of suspected measles cases reported through the routine surveillance system, with information on demographics, clinical outcomes, vaccination status, geographic location, and key surveillance dates.

Case Definition

Confirmed measles cases were defined according to national and WHO surveillance guidelines [15]. Laboratory-confirmed cases were defined as cases with a positive measles-specific IgM results while epidemiologically linked cases were defined as suspected cases epidemiologically linked to a laboratory-confirmed measles case. Cases classified as compatible, discarded, or pending were excluded from the analysis. Hence, confirmed cases included laboratory confirmed and epidemiologically linked cases.

Definition of Outbreak Episodes and Analysis of Outbreak Persistence

Outbreak episodes were defined at the regional level as periods of sustained measles transmission following at least two consecutive weeks of zero reported cases. To reduce the influence of short-term fluctuations, weekly case counts were visually inspected and minor irregularities smoothed where necessary. Multi-peak waves occurring without a clear interruption (i.e., without ≥ 2 consecutive zero-case weeks) were treated as a single outbreak episode. Outbreak duration was defined as the number of weeks between the first and last reported case within each outbreak episode. The unit of analysis was the region - outbreak episode, with each observation representing a distinct outbreak within a region. To assess the association between early outbreak detection delays and outbreak persistence, we fitted negative binomial regression models, with outbreak duration (in weeks) as the outcome variable. This approach was chosen to account for overdispersion in the duration data. Models were adjusted for outbreak size (total number of cases) and region as a fixed effect. Early outbreak delay was defined as the median onset-to-confirmation delay during the first two weeks of each outbreak episode.

Age data Processing and Imputation

Age was recorded in years and months. A single age variable expressed in months was constructed using reported age in years or months. Cases with both age in years and months recorded as zero were treated as missing. Missing age values were imputed using the median age (in months) among confirmed cases within each region. For regions with entirely missing age data, the national median age was used. To assess the robustness of this approach, sensitivity analyses were conducted using complete-case data and alternative imputation scenarios assigning lower (25th percentile) and upper (75th percentile) regional age values for missing observations. Age was subsequently categorized into epidemiologically relevant groups based on Ethiopia’s vaccination schedule: <9 months, 9–11 months, 12–59 months, 5–14 years, and ≥ 15 years.

Epidemic Curve

An epidemic curve was constructed using weekly aggregated confirmed cases, based on the date of symptom onset. Weeks were defined using a standard epidemiological week starting on Monday (ISO 8601). To assess geographic variations, national trends were further disaggregated into regional epidemic curves.

Attack rate and case Fatality rate Estimation

Attack rates (AR) were calculated as the number of confirmed measles cases divided by the corresponding population, expressed per 100,000 population. Regional and age-specific attack rates were estimated using stratified population denominators. 95% confidence intervals (CI) for attack rates were calculated using the Poisson distribution method. Case fatality rates (CFR) were calculated as the proportion of deaths among confirmed cases with a known clinical outcome (“Alive” or “Died”). To ensure statistical robustness, 95% CIs for the CFR were estimated using the Clopper-Pearson (Exact) binomial method. Regional and age-specific attack rates (AR) were calculated using population denominators derived from WorldPop (www.worldpop.org) unconstrained subnational estimates for Ethiopia (2018–2024). All AR units are standardized to cases per 100,000 population. To assess population protection, we compared these rates against the established measles herd immunity threshold of 95%.

Surveillance Delays

The operational performance of the measles surveillance system was assessed by quantifying delays across key stages of the diagnostic pathway. Four time-intervals were defined: (i) delay from symptom onset to first presentation at a health facility (healthcare-seeking delay), (ii) delay from facility presentation to specimen collection, (iii) delay from specimen collection to laboratory result reporting, and (iv) the total delay from symptom onset to laboratory confirmation. The latter represents the cumulative time during which transmission may occur prior to formal case recognition. To ensure data quality, all delay variables were subjected to plausibility filtering. Negative values and delays exceeding 60 days were considered biologically or operationally implausible and were excluded. The number and proportion of excluded records were assessed prior to filtering, including their distribution by year and region. Given the right-skewed distribution of delay metrics, system performance was summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR).

To evaluate the impact of reporting delays on transmission dynamics, a delay-adjusted sensitivity analysis was conducted. The empirical median delay from symptom onset to laboratory confirmation was estimated from the cleaned dataset and used to shift onset dates forward, generating an adjusted incidence time series. The time-varying reproduction number (Rt) was then re-estimated using this delay-adjusted incidence.

Statistical Analysis and Visualization

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.1). The raw surveillance dataset underwent a multi-stage cleaning process to ensure analytical validity. Duplicate records were removed using unique identifiers, onset dates, and geographic location. Missing age values were imputed using region-specific medians, while other key variables with substantial missingness (> 5%) were categorized as “Unknown” to preserve case structure. For temporal variables, plausibility filters were applied: negative intervals and delays exceeding 60 days were considered implausible and excluded from delay analyses. Descriptive statistics are presented as counts, proportions, medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and rates per 100,000 population. Age-specific attack rates (AR) and case fatality rates (CFR) were calculated with 95% confidence intervals (CI) using the Clopper–Pearson exact method.

Transmission intensity was assessed by estimating the time-varying effective reproduction number (Rt) using the EpiEstim package. Rt was derived from daily incidence based on symptom onset, with zero-case days included to maintain a continuous time series. A parametric serial interval distribution (mean: 12.5 days; standard deviation: 3.4 days) was assumed. Estimates were generated using a sliding window approach, with a 14-day window for the primary analysis and a 7-day window for sensitivity analysis. Estimation assumed constant reporting completeness, consistent with national Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) implementation. To assess the influence of reporting delays, a sensitivity analysis was performed by shifting onset dates forward using the empirical median delay from symptom onset to laboratory confirmation, and Rt was re-estimated under the same assumptions.

Data visualization included epidemic curves, population pyramids for age–sex distributions, horizontal bar charts for AR and CFR with 95% CIs, and boxplots for surveillance delays.

Results

Descriptive Characteristics of Confirmed Measles Cases

A total of 92,687 measles cases were reported through the national measles surveillance system from all regions in Ethiopia between 2018 and 2024, among which 69,866 were confirmed measles cases. Of the total confirmed cases, 12.5% were laboratory-confirmed and 87.5% were epidemiologically linked. The median age of confirmed cases was 4 years (IQR [2–7.6]). Children aged 12–59 months accounted for the largest proportion of cases 42%, followed 5 -14-year-old (30%) (Table 1). Males comprised 49.2% of cases, while 46.3% were female. Overall, 39.7% of confirmed cases were reported as unvaccinated, 32.9% had received at least one dose of measles-containing vaccine, and vaccination status was unknown for 27.4%. A total of 487 deaths were reported, corresponding to a case fatality ratio of 0.74%.

Temporal Distribution of Measles Cases

The temporal analysis of confirmed measles cases in Ethiopia from 2018 to 2024 revealed a significant resurgence of the disease, characterized by persistent seasonal cycles and a sharp multi-year escalation in incidence. Following a period of relatively low transmission between 2018 and 2020, the country experienced a dramatic surge beginning in 2021, with confirmed cases increasing nearly fivefold by 2022. This upward trend peaked in 2023, which recorded the highest annual burden within the study period. The decline observed in late 2024 may reflect a combination of reactive vaccination activities, regional outbreak dynamics, reporting variations, or other unmeasured factors (Fig. 1).

The epidemic curve demonstrated a distinct bimodal seasonal pattern. Primary transmission peaks consistently occurred during the spring months (March to May), followed by a secondary, lower-intensity surge in the autumn (September to November). These peaks often aligned with the dry seasons and periods of increased population mobility. The overall crude incidence rate per 100,000 population showed a corresponding increase, rising from 0.53 in 2020 to a peak of 2.13 in 2023, highlighting a period of widespread community transmission and frequent regional outbreaks.

Age and sex Profiles of Confirmed Measles Cases in Ethiopia 2018–2024

The national age distribution reveals a high-intensity pediatric burden, with a significant peak in cases occurring between the ages of one and four years (Fig. 6A). While the disease burden remains concentrated in young children, the wide-range analysis confirms measles transmission across the entire life course, including cases in adults aged up to 60 years.

This national trend is deconstructed by region in Fig. 6B, which highlights the geographical disparities in the age of infection. In emerging regions such as Gambella (2.4 years) and Afar (3.1 years), the interquartile range of the boxplots is narrow and centered on the preschool years. Conversely, highland and urban regions like Addis Ababa (7.2 years) show significantly broader distributions, suggesting that immunity gaps are more multi-generational in these settings compared to the strictly pediatric burden seen in pastoralist areas (Fig. 2).

The demographic analysis, visualized through a population pyramid, indicates a relatively symmetrical distribution of confirmed measles cases between males and females across all age cohorts. The largest volume of cases for both sexes was concentrated in the 12–59-month age group, which accounted for approximately 15,000 male and 14,000 female cases. As age increased beyond five years, the number of confirmed cases declined progressively, though the balance between sexes remained consistent in both the 5–14 year and ≥ 15-year categories. Similarly, in the youngest cohorts, the burden among infants aged < 9 months and 9–11 months showed no significant tendency for either sex. Overall, these findings suggest that while age is a primary determinant of infection risk in this outbreak, the disease impacted male and female children with nearly equal frequency (Fig. 3).

Age-Specific Attack Rates and CFRs

Age-specific analysis revealed that the burden of measles was disproportionately concentrated in young children. The highest attack rates were recorded among children aged 12–59 months and 9–11 months (190 and 170 per 100,000, respectively). Infants younger than 9 months experienced an attack rate of 155 per 100,000 population. Similarly, the CFR was highest in children under five years of age, peaking at nearly 1.0% in those under 60 months. In contrast, individuals aged 15 years and older exhibited both the lowest attack rates and the lowest CFR (0.4%), suggesting a significant shift in risk profile as age increases (Fig. 4).

Fig. 4Comparison attack rates and case fatality rates among different age groups

Comparison attack rates and case fatality rates among different age groups

Outcome status was available for the majority of cases, although completeness varied mostly by year (Table S6). The observed case fatality ratio (CFR) among cases with known outcomes was 0.74%. Under a conservative assumption that no deaths occurred among cases with missing outcomes, the minimum CFR was 0.70%. Assuming a higher mortality among cases with missing outcomes (twice the observed death rate), the CFR increased slightly to 0.78%. These findings indicate that CFR estimates were robust to plausible variations in outcome completeness.

Regional Analysis of Disparities in Burden and Severity of Measles

The geographical distribution of confirmed measles cases revealed profound disparities in both transmission intensity and clinical outcomes across Ethiopia’s administrative regions. Nationally, the measles attack rate (AR) was recorded at 67.17 per 100,000 population, but this burden was disproportionately concentrated in emerging/pastoralist regions. Gambella and South West Ethiopia emerged as the primary hotspots, experiencing the highest relative incidence with attack rates of 324.61 and 308.51 per 100,000, respectively figures nearly five times the national average (Table 2; Fig. 5).

Fig. 5Regional measles attack rates in Ethiopia, 2018–2024

Regional measles attack rates in Ethiopia, 2018–2024

The severity of the disease, measured by the Case Fatality Rate (CFR), also varied widely among regions. While the national CFR was 0.74% (95% CI: 0.68–0.81), several regions reported fatality rates that significantly exceeded the World Health Organization’s 1.0% threshold. The most severe outcomes were documented in Benishangul Gumuz (3.07%), Gambella (2.86%), Afar (2.21%), and Somali (1.99%). Notably, high attack rates did not always correlate with high mortality; for instance, South West Ethiopia maintained a relatively low CFR (0.33%) despite its high incidence. The geographical clustering of high-mortality outbreaks in pastoralist and border regions may suggest unique structural challenges, including limited healthcare access and delayed response (Table 2; Fig. 6).

Surveillance Performance and System Delays

The temporal performance of the national surveillance system varied across stages of the reporting cascade (Fig. 7). Among cases with complete data, the median delay from symptom onset to facility presentation was 2 days (IQR: 1–4), and the median interval from facility visit to specimen collection was 1 day (IQR: 0–3).

Laboratory turnaround time, defined as the interval from specimen collection to result reporting, was estimated among cases with complete laboratory records (n = 5,701), with a median of 5 days (IQR: 3–8). The total delay from symptom onset to laboratory confirmation was estimated among cases with available onset and result dates (n = 33,447), yielding a median of 18 days (IQR: 12–25).

Data completeness varied over time, particularly for specimen collection dates, which declined from near-complete reporting in 2019–2020 (> 99%) to below 11% during 2022–2023, before improving in 2024. Consequently, the proportion of cases with complete surveillance timelines decreased in later years and also varied by region (Supplementary Table S3 and S4). However, these differences were broadly similar across geographic and demographic groups (Supplementary Table S5). In addition, 2,274 (2.5%) records exceeded the 60-day plausibility threshold and were excluded from the delay analysis; this proportion was low and showed no substantial variation across years or regions (Supplementary Table S7).

Outbreak Dynamics and Effective Reproduction Number

The transmission intensity of measles varied over time, with the effective reproduction number (Rt) frequently exceeding the epidemic threshold of 1.0 (Fig. 8). Rt peaked between approximately 1.8 and 2.4 during major epidemic waves in 2019 and 2021, indicating sustained transmission during these periods. Elevated Rt values were maintained for several consecutive weeks, consistent with prolonged phases of epidemic growth.

Periods in which Rt declined below 1.0 were also observed, indicating temporary reductions in transmission. Overall, the temporal patterns of Rt demonstrated recurrent cycles of expansion and decline throughout the study period. The overall temporal trends were consistent when estimated using a longer 14-day sliding window (Supplementary Figure S1).

The delay-adjusted sensitivity analysis should be interpreted as a simplified robustness assessment rather than a formal nowcasting approach, as it does not fully correct for right truncation, changing reporting completeness, or missing onset dates.

Association Between Surveillance Latency and Outbreak Duration

A total of 62 outbreak episodes were included in the analysis, with a median duration of 10 weeks (IQR: 5–16 weeks). Using negative binomial regression to account for overdispersion, early outbreak delay was not significantly associated with outbreak duration (IRR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.97–1.01, p = 0.195). In contrast, outbreak size was strongly associated with longer duration, with each additional 100 cases corresponding to approximately an 8.6% increase in outbreak duration (p < 0.001). Several regions exhibited shorter outbreak durations relative to the reference region, although these differences likely reflect heterogeneity in surveillance and response dynamics. These findings were consistent with results from linear regression models (Table 3, Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

The results of this seven-year longitudinal study provide a comprehensive assessment of measles epidemiology in Ethiopia. It reveals that persistent measles transmission in Ethiopia appears to be driven primarily by regional immunity gaps among susceptible pediatric populations, while prolonged surveillance delays remain an important operational concern for timely outbreak detection and response. Despite sustained efforts to improve immunization coverage, the frequent occurrence of outbreaks with peak Rt values of 2.4 indicates that population immunity remains significantly below the 95% threshold required for herd protection.

A central theme of our findings is the disproportionate burden of disease and mortality among the youngest age cohorts. The age-specific attack rates (AR) peaked sharply in children aged 12–59 months, confirming that this group remains the primary reservoir for measles transmission in Ethiopia. Of even greater clinical concern is the high case fatality rate (CFR) of 1.12% among infants under 9 months. This group is particularly vulnerable as they are not yet eligible for routine MCV1 and likely possess insufficient maternal antibodies, a phenomenon observed in other high-prevalence settings where maternal immunity wanes earlier than in low-transmission contexts [16, 17]. The demographic pyramid further illustrates this, showing a broad base of pediatric cases that suggests the Zero-Dose child population remains the most critical hurdle for elimination [4, 18, 19].

Our analysis revealed two distinct epidemiological signatures within Ethiopia. In emerging and pastoralist regions like Somali and Afar, the low median age of infection (2.8–3.1 years) and high attack rates point toward a classic failure of routine service delivery. Conversely, in urban centers and highland regions such as Addis Ababa and Amhara, the shift toward an older median age (over 7 years) and a broader age distribution indicates a residual susceptibility in school-aged children [20]. This suggests that while routine immunization is reaching more infants in these areas, historical gaps in Supplementary Immunization Activities (SIAs) have allowed a cohort of older susceptible children to accumulate, who then serve as reservoirs for transmission during outbreaks [19, 21].

Although surveillance delays frequently exceeded the measles serial interval, we did not observe a statistically significant association between early outbreak delay and outbreak duration in adjusted models. This may reflect the influence of unmeasured factors such as response intensity, vaccination campaigns, and heterogeneity in transmission dynamics across regions. Nevertheless, the observed delay distributions remain operationally important, as delays approaching or exceeding one serial interval are theoretically consistent with continued transmission before control measures can take effect [14, 22, 23].

To achieve the 2030 measles elimination goals, the focus must expand from simply increasing MCV1 coverage to optimizing the speed of the public health response. The regional differences found in this study suggest that a one-size-fits-all approach to SIAs is inefficient. Instead, pastoralist regions require intensified routine services and mobile outreach, while urban centers need age-expanded catch-up campaigns targeting school-aged populations. Furthermore, reducing the 18-day total onset-to-confirmation delay through decentralized testing or digital reporting could potentially close the gap on Rt, allowing interventions to move from reactive to proactive [8].

The findings of this study have important implications for measles prevention and surveillance programs in Ethiopia and other low- and middle-income countries with heterogeneous vaccination coverage and variable surveillance capacity. The marked regional differences in age-specific burden and transmission intensity highlight the need for geographically tailored immunization strategies rather than uniform national approaches. Strengthening routine childhood immunization, identifying zero-dose populations, and improving outbreak preparedness in high-risk regions remain critical priorities. In addition, the prolonged delays observed across the surveillance cascade underscore the importance of timely case detection, laboratory confirmation, and reporting for effective outbreak monitoring and response. Although surveillance delays were not independently associated with outbreak duration in adjusted analyses, improving surveillance timeliness may still enhance situational awareness and operational readiness during outbreaks. The analytical framework used in this study may also help inform surveillance evaluations and measles elimination strategies in similar resource-constrained settings.

This study has several strengths. It provides a seven-year longitudinal assessment of measles dynamics in Ethiopia, offering a comprehensive view of temporal trends. The exclusive use of confirmed cases enhances the validity of findings compared with studies that include suspected cases. However, the analysis is subject to limitations. Surveillance data are prone to under-reporting and incomplete vaccination histories, which may affect interpretation. In addition, data on the timing and nature of public health response activities (e.g., outbreak declaration, reactive vaccination, and response deployment) were not available, precluding direct assessment of the relationship between case confirmation and response initiation. As a result, interpretations regarding the impact of reporting delays on transmission dynamics are inferential rather than empirically established. The inclusion of both laboratory-confirmed and epidemiologically linked cases may introduce some heterogeneity in delay measurements, although both are part of standard surveillance definitions and reflect routine reporting practice. Future studies incorporating subnational supplementary immunization activity coverage data could further clarify the effects of vaccination campaigns relative to underlying transmission patterns.

Conclusion

Between 2018 and 2024, measles transmission in Ethiopia showed patterns consistent with sustained endemic circulation and a high pediatric burden. Using a large dataset of confirmed cases, we observed persistent periods where the effective reproduction number remained above 1, alongside median reporting delays of approximately 18 days. These delays exceed the estimated serial interval, suggesting that surveillance and response activities may not consistently occur within a single transmission cycle.

Marked regional differences in age distribution highlight heterogeneity in population immunity. Younger median ages in pastoralist and emerging regions contrast with older age profiles in urban and agrarian settings, while the substantially higher age observed in Tigray likely reflects disruption to routine services during the 2020–2022 conflict.

Together, these findings underscore the need for a dual strategy: strengthening surveillance timeliness to enable more rapid response, and addressing subnational immunity gaps through targeted vaccination of zero-dose and under-immunized populations. Improving both the speed and reach of interventions will be critical to interrupt transmission and advance toward measles elimination.
