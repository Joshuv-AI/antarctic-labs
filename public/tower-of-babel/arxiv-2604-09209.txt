An automated method for planetary nebula detection with SIGNALS: first applications to NGC 4214 and NGC 4449 2025 An automated method for planetary nebula detection with SIGNALS: first applications to NGC 4214 and NGC 4449 – References
Nancy Yang, Johanna Hartke, Martin Bureau, Chiara Spiniello, Louis-Simon Guité, Guy Flint, Magda Arnaboldi, Ana Inés Ennis, R. Pierre Martin, Thomas Martin, Carmelle Robert, Laurie Rousseau-Nepton, Lucas M. Valenzuela, Sébastien Vicens-Mouret

Abstract
Utilising the optical imaging Fourier transform spectrograph SITELLE, the Star-formation, Ionized Gas and Nebular Abundances Legacy Survey (SIGNALS) is designed to study the connection between star-forming regions and their environments. Targeting 31 31 local star-forming galaxies, its data products also lend themselves to planetary nebula (PN) surveys. We present here a new pipeline to find PNe using automated emission-line diagnostics and morphology tests, that is able to distinguish PNe from contaminants with an accuracy similar to that of past visual methods. We also perform thorough completeness tests using mock PNe inserted into the data cubes with full spectra. We apply these tools to a pilot sample of two dwarf irregular galaxies from the SIGNALS survey, NGC 4214 and NGC 4449, with other galaxies to follow. For these two galaxies, we identify 25 25 PNe (including 6 6 new discoveries) and 23 23 PNe (including 13 13 new discoveries), respectively, and calculate PN luminosity function distances of 3.09 − 0.46 + 0.25 3.09^{+0.25}_{-0.46} and 3.91 − 0.52 + 0.33 3.91^{+0.33}_{-0.52} Mpc, respectively, the latter consistent with previous estimates. We also calculate the bolometric PN specific frequency of our galaxies ( α bol \alpha_{\mathrm{bol}} ), as well as a newly defined V V -band PN specific frequency ( α V \alpha_{\mathrm{V}} ) based solely on the galaxies’ total luminosities in that band.



1
Introduction

Planetary nebulae are the progeny of low- to intermediate-mass (
\approx 1
–
8

{\rm M}_{\odot}
) stars at the end of their asymptotic giant branch phases
(
45
, e.g.)
. Planetary nebulae have varying lifetimes of
\sim 10,000
yr before their ionised shells dissipate. They are almost always brightest in the [O
iii
]
\lambda
5007 emission line, although exceptions exist, such as very low-excitation planetary nebulae with cool central stars that emit more in the H
\alpha
line
(
29
)
. The exact strength of the [O
iii
]
\lambda
5007 line depends on the metallicity, initial mass, and temperature of each star, but a high-metallicity planetary nebula can emit up to
15\%
of its central star’s luminosity in the [O
iii
]
\lambda
5007 line alone
(
22
)
. The bright [O
iii
]
\lambda
5007 emission allows planetary nebulae to be detected out to distances of
\approx 100
Mpc
(
31
)
. Planetary nebulae can also be detected in galaxy haloes, where the stellar continua are faint. They are therefore popular tools to trace the kinematics of galaxies to large galactocentric radii
(
3
;
75
;
32
;
5
, e.g.)
.

Planetary nebulae also serve as a secondary distance indicator, through the planetary nebula luminosity function.
12
showed that there is a lower limit to the absolute magnitudes of observed planetary nebulae (i.e. there is a maximum planetary nebula brightness) which is nearly universal across galaxies, regardless of morphological type. By fitting a measured planetary nebula luminosity function, this lower limit can be determined and a distance modulus inferred. First described in
12
and adapted for a varying faint-end planetary nebula luminosity function slope by
47
, the planetary nebula luminosity function is described by

N(M_{5007})=c_{1}\,e^{c_{2}\,M_{5007}}\left(1-e^{3(M^{*}_{5007}-M_{5007})}\right)\,,

(1)

where
M_{5007}
is the absolute magnitude of the [O
iii
]
\lambda
5007 line,
c_{1}
is a normalisation constant,
c_{2}
is the faint-end slope index and
M^{*}_{5007}
is the absolute magnitude of the bright-end cut-off. The
{m}_{5007}
(apparent) magnitude approximates the
V
-band (apparent) magnitude one would observe if the total [O
iii
]
\lambda
5007 line emission was distributed over the
V
band, and can be obtained through the integrated [O
iii
]
\lambda
5007 flux,
{F}_{5007}
, via the Jacoby relation
(
39
)
:

{m}_{5007}=-2.5\,\log\left(\frac{{F}_{5007}}{\mathrm{erg~s}^{-1}~\mathrm{cm}^{-2}}\right)-13.74\,.

(2)

Planetary nebula luminosity function distances can be accurately derived (uncertainties
<10\%
) for distances of up to
\approx 20
Mpc
(
15
;
38
)
and have been found to be of comparable precision and accuracy to those derived from Cepheids and tip of the red giant branch methods (see
17
for a recent comparison).

While the planetary nebula luminosity function is effective as a distance indicator, the physics behind the universality of the bright-end cut-off remains unclear. Naively, older stellar populations should have fainter planetary nebulae, as older stars exit the main sequence at lower masses and lower progenitor masses produce fainter planetary nebulae
(
50
)
. Yet, observations have shown that the bright-end cut-off is always at the same absolute magnitude, so there must be some mechanism(s) for old stellar populations to produce bright planetary nebulae
(
39
)
. Recent simulations suggest that these bright planetary nebulae may form within older stellar populations of high metallicity, as a result of metal-rich stars
(
81
)
.
Because metal-rich stars evolve more slowly than metal-poor stars of the same mass, they form planetary nebulae later and with a higher initial mass, leading to brighter planetary nebulae.
Metallicity is also predicted to affect the absolute magnitude cut-off, such that
M^{*}_{5007}
increases with decreasing metallicities
(
11
)
. In turn, one could constrain the metallicity of a galaxy by assuming a distance and fitting for
M^{*}_{5007}
.

Another way to investigate stellar populations with planetary nebulae is to use the bolometric specific frequency of planetary nebulae, that is the number of planetary nebulae per unit bolometric luminosity, defined as

\alpha\equiv\frac{N_{\mathrm{PN}}}{L_{\mathrm{bol}}}\,,

(3)

where
{N}_{\mathrm{PN}}
is the total number of planetary nebulae in a galaxy and
{L}_{\mathrm{bol}}
is the galaxy’s total bolometric luminosity.

In practice,
N_{\mathrm{PN}}
can only be determined over a limited range of magnitudes, down from the bright cut-off. A more practical definition is thus given by

\alpha_{\Delta m}=\frac{1}{L_{\mathrm{bol}}}\int^{m^{*}_{5007}+\Delta m}_{m^{*}_{5007}}N(m)\mathrm{d}m\,,

(4)

where
N(m)
is the number of planetary nebulae between (apparent) magnitudes
m
and
m+\mathrm{d}m
and
\Delta m=2.5
is commonly used in the literature.

This specific frequency
\alpha
has long been shown observationally to correlate with galaxy colour
(
58
;
13
;
14
;
10
)
, such that redder galaxies produce fewer planetary nebulae (per unit luminosity). However, stellar population modelling produces the opposite prediction, i.e. redder galaxies should have more planetary nebulae
(
10
)
.

The specific frequency can also vary as a function of galactocentric radius within a galaxy
(
36
;
47
;
33
;
6
)
.

Traditionally, planetary nebula candidates have been identified using the on-band/off-band method, for which follow-up spectroscopy is required to confirm the planetary nebula classifications and measure their velocities. Slitless spectroscopy instruments like the Planetary Nebula Spectrograph (PN.S;
23
) offer a more efficient solution, providing planetary nebula diagnostics and accurate velocity measurements from a single observation
(
16
;
63
, e.g.)
.

A further improvement is offered by integral-field spectrographs: planetary nebulae no longer require dedicated surveys but can be found in integral-field spectrograph data cubes as by-products. Indeed, since the first planetary nebula study using integral-field spectrograph
(
71
)
, there have been many more (e.g.
44
;
72
; but see also the reviews by
64
and
34
).

Nevertheless, the effectiveness of most integral-field spectrographs is limited by their generally small fields of view, often necessitating numerous pointings for single targets and thus large mosaics
(
17
)
. SITELLE
(
24
)
is an imaging Fourier transform spectrometer with a very large field of view (
11′\times 11′
) mounted at the Canada–France–Hawaii Telescope. Its spectral coverage and field of view lend themselves perfectly to planetary nebula studies. In particular, the wide field of view allows to study planetary nebulae in the outskirts of galaxies, where they stand out easily from the faint stellar continua.

The Star formation, Ionized Gas, and Nebular Abundances Legacy Survey (Star formation, Ionized Gas, and Nebular Abundances Legacy Survey;
65
) targeted
31
local (distances
\approx 1
–
12
Mpc) star-forming galaxies with SITELLE. Most past planetary nebula surveys have focused on early-type galaxies, for their lack of contaminating [O
iii
]-sources
(
16
;
19
;
62
, e.g.)
.
A planetary nebula survey of the Star formation, Ionized Gas, and Nebular Abundances Legacy Survey galaxies will allow to probe planetary nebula population (and associated planetary nebula luminosity function) changes with e.g. galaxy morphology (from dwarf irregular galaxies to grand-design spirals), stellar mass and stellar metallicity. So far, the planetary nebula populations of only M 31
(
53
)
and NGC 4214
(
83

have been catalogued using Star formation, Ionized Gas, and Nebular Abundances Legacy Survey data. To expand these studies to the entire survey, a more automated pipeline for planetary nebula detection is required. This is the primary goal of this work.

This paper thus sets out in detail the automated methods we developed to construct planetary nebula catalogues of Star formation, Ionized Gas, and Nebular Abundances Legacy Survey galaxies. For illustrative purposes, we also applied those methods to the galaxies NGC 4214 and NGC 4449. We describe the data and calibration methods in Section
2
. Section
3
covers planetary nebula detection, the elimination of contaminants and the construction of mock planetary nebula catalogues to estimate the completeness of observations. We present our results for NGC 4214 and NGC 4449 separately in Section
4

\alpha

Data and calibrations

2.1
SITELLE

As mentioned above, SITELLE is an imaging Fourier transform spectrometer with an
11′\times 11′
field of view (sampled with
0\aas@@fstack{\prime\prime}32\times 0\aas@@fstack{\prime\prime}32
spaxels) mounted at the Cassegrain focus of the Canada–France–Hawaii Telescope
(
24
)
. The design is that of an off-axis Michelson interferometer. A beam splitter directs the light onto two different mirrors: the fixed mirror and the scanning mirror. The two beams meet again at the beam splitter, where they interfere and form two new beams, each recorded with its own camera. The scanning mirror can be moved in small steps, changing the optical path difference between the two beams.

An interferogram cube is obtained by a series of short exposures (
\approx 1
min each), in between which the scanning mirror moves by a few microns. The spectral resolution can be fine-tuned to any resolution in the range
1
to
10,000
by changing the number of steps of the scanning mirror. A Fourier transform then returns a data cube in which two dimensions are spatial and the third is spectral (wave number). This results in an instrumental line spread function that is a sinc as a function of wave number
(
54
)
rather than the more typical Gaussian as a function of wavelength. Emission (and absorption) lines broadened by the Doppler effect have a Gaussian shape, which when observed with SITELLE is convolved into a "sincgauss" shape.

Early on during the commissioning of SITELLE, the image quality was found to degrade towards the edges of the field of view, where the point spread function becomes elongated. As mentioned in
53
and
24
, the extent of this distortion changes from image to image, so it cannot be corrected. Fortunately, however, more recent data suffer less from this issue, thanks to an update to the SITELLE optics
(
61
)
.

2.4
Sky-line velocity map

All velocities quoted in this paper have had a barycentric velocity correction applied, based on the start time of the observations.

As described in detail in
53
, SITELLE’s wavelength calibration relies on a high spectral resolution data cube of a laser source, taken with the telescope pointing at the zenith. However, due to temperature variations and the changing direction of the gravity vector, laser cubes obtained for different pointings show (absolute) velocity calibration errors of up to
25
km s
-1

(
28
)
. In addition, the exact wavelength of the laser used for the calibration has some uncertainty, as it is dependent on the observing conditions. The data reduction pipeline assumes the manufacturer-stated wavelength of
543.5
nm, but a
0.1
nm error leads to a
55
km s
-1
offset
(
52
)
.

To achieve a better accuracy, a fit to the Meinel OH sky lines can be performed in the SN3 filter. Indeed, these should have zero velocity, allowing to map the velocity offset across the SITELLE FOV. As the bright central regions of galaxies do not have clear sky lines, we can not constrain the velocity offsets there by direct fits to the spectra. Nevertheless, we can constrain the velocity offsets at the edges of the field of view and fit a model to those measurements (as a function of position), thus allowing to predict the offset in the central regions. The details of the model and the initial parameters of the fit can be found in
53
, and they have been implemented in
Outils de Réduction de Cubes Spectraux
(
ORCS
;
51
). Here we sampled each cube on a
40\times 40
grid, masking the
5\%
brightest pixels. The resulting sky velocity maps are presented in Fig.
3
. The standard deviation of the residuals from the fit is
\approx 4
km s
-1
in both cases, and these have been propagated into our final velocity uncertainties.

3
Planetary nebula pipeline

The construction of our planetary nebula catalogues can be broken down into two main steps: i) finding planetary nebula candidates and ii) distinguishing planetary nebulae from contaminants. In this section, we describe these two steps in detail. We start with the creation of a two-dimensional (2D) image from which we detect emission-line sources. The emission lines are then fitted and diagnostic diagrams are used to weed out H
ii
regions and supernova remnants. Finally, we remove sources whose morphologies are inconsistent with a point-source. See Fig.
4
for an overview of our planetary nebula detection pipeline, including map-making and calibration steps.

Figure 4:
Schematic illustration of the Star formation, Ionized Gas, and Nebular Abundances Legacy Survey planetary nebula identification pipeline.

3.1
Source identification

To identify emission-line objects, we created a so-called detection map using an inbuilt function of
ORCS

(
51
)
, as used in
53
. The process is similar to median filtering. At each spaxel, the median spectrum of a
9\times 9
pixel
2
"background" spatial box (excluding the central
3\times 3
pixel
2
) is first subtracted from the median spectrum of a
3\times 3
pixel
2
spatial box, both centred on the spaxel under consideration.
The highest flux at any wave number of this background-subtracted spectrum is then assigned to the spaxel. This procedure therefore reveals sources that are brighter than their surroundings in at least one (wave-number) frame. By applying this procedures to our SN2 data cubes, we highlight sources that are bright in either [O
iii
]
\lambda
5007, [O
iii
]
\lambda
4959 or H
\beta
. The box sizes quoted here are the default options, chosen to fit the typical seeing of SITELLE observations (
\approx 1″
or
\approx 3
pixels). While the sizes can be changed, they are restricted to odd numbers of pixels and therefore cannot be precisely fine-tuned.

We search for objects in the SN2 detection map using the DAOFIND algorithm
(
76
)
as implemented in python by
9
. This finds local maxima with peak fluxes above a certain threshold flux. The routine
DAOStarFinder
also returns two different roundness measures for each detected source, but we opted not to use those in the following steps as both measures turned out to be correlated with magnitude. We therefore proceed with the source centroids only, and (re-)fit their roundnesses later.

Running the algorithm on the detection maps yields
\approx 5000
sources for each galaxy. Most of these sources are associated with H
ii
regions, but using the detection map is nevertheless preferable to using a simple map of the [O
iii
]
\lambda
5007 emission, as H
ii
regions are very bright and extended in [O
iii
]. The detection map is better at revealing point sources, which is extremely useful when searching for planetary nebulae; any source bright and compact in [O
iii
] should also be prominent in the detection map. To illustrate this, we show a comparison of (part of) the deep image (constructed by integrating over all frames of the interference data cube before Fourier transforming), the [O
iii
] map and the detection map of NGC 4449 in Fig.
5
.

3.2
Source spectra and photometry

We use
ORCS

(
51
)
for emission-line fitting, which relies on the
curve_fit
function of the
SciPy
library
(
84
)
. For each emission-line source identified in the detection map, we extract a spectrum from a circular aperture of
3
pixels in radius, from which we subtract the median spectrum from a background annulus of inner radius
8
and outer radius
10
pixels. The typical full width at half maximum is
\approx 3
pixels, but we purposefully keep the aperture small to minimise contamination from other sources
(
74
)
. This is the same aperture used by
83
and increases the signal-to-noise ratio (
S/N
;
35
). The small aperture does introduce the need for an aperture correction, which we determine by constructing the curves of growth of round, isolated sources. 1.35\pm 0.11

When fitting emission lines,
ORCS
has the option to fit the velocity and velocity dispersion of each line separately. We did not use this option, however, as we assume planetary nebula emission lines are from a single source (superpositions are unlikely in these low-mass galaxies). This has the added benefit of yielding more robust velocity measurements, as all the lines can be used simultaneously to determine a single velocity and velocity dispersion.

Good initial guesses of the velocities of the sources are required for the fits to be accurate.
To illustrate this, we generated mock PN spectra with varying background noise. We set the spectral resolution to match that of the datacubes of NGC 4449, which is the SIGNALS standard. We then fitted these spectra with a range of initial velocity guesses, to test the limits within which the fits still converge to the correct velocity. We show the results in Fig.
6

\approx 300
km s
-1
of the truth, which is a wider range than the velocity dispersion of the galaxies studied. For SN3, the allowed range is smaller and an initial guess within
\approx 100
km s
-1
of the truth is required. This is due to the SN3 cube having a higher spectral resolution, which is SIGNALS standard practice. In contrast to the strong dependence on spectral resolution, there is only a small dependence on signal-to-noise ratio.

Contaminant elimination

3.3.1
Emission-line ratio diagnostics

The next step is to distinguish planetary nebulae from other emission-line sources. The most common contaminants are H
ii
regions and supernova remnants, although background intermediate-redshift galaxies are also occasionally detected in [O
iii

(

4

We use the demarcation line of

43

68

For most candidates, both the [N

The use of two diagnostic diagrams means that we have two intermediate classifications that may not always match, and that need to be consolidated into one final conclusion. To avoid confusion, we only discuss the intermediate classifications in this section and consolidate the results in Section
3.3.3

3.3.2
Source morphology

Spatially unresolved objects like planetary nebulae should be spatially identical to the point spread function, and should therefore appear as circular Gaussian-shaped objects. As H
ii
regions and supernova remnants are usually spatially extended, the extents and shapes of emission-line sources are of great help to remove potential contaminants remaining after applying the aforementioned emission-line ratio diagnostics. However, the SITELLE point spread function varies across the field of view, objects being more distorted toward the edges. A point source located far from the data cube centre could therefore appear more extended and elongated, making it more difficult to distinguish it from an intrinsically extended source. We have therefore attempted to map the point spread function variations across the field of view, so that we can still apply compactness and shape criteria to determine whether a planetary nebula candidate (based on emission-line ratios) is indeed a spatially unresolved planetary nebula.

We map the variations of the point spread function in SN2 by quantifying the departures from perfect 2D Gaussians of sources across the field of view. We use all the stars from the
GAIA
Data Release 3
(
30
)
catalogue that are located within the SITELLE field of view and have
m_{G}>17
(to avoid saturated stars). We filter for stars using
classprob_dsc_combmod_star

>0.995
, which indicates the probability of any source being a single star.

We then fit the stars’ shapes on the SN2 continuum map using 2D Gaussians, and define a distortion parameter
\mathcal{D}\equiv 1-\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{b}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}}
, where
\sigma
is the standard deviation of the best-fitting Gaussian and a and b refer to the best-fitting Gaussian major and minor axis, respectively. A perfectly round source has
\mathcal{D}=0
while an elongated source has
0<\mathcal{D}<1
. We then fit a polynomial surface to all the measurements across the field of view, yielding the 2D distortion map (
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fit}}
) shown in Fig.
9
for each of NGC 4214 and NGC 4449. Each map informs us on how elongated an intrinsically round source appears in our data.

3.4
Velocity calibration

While the SN3 initial velocities were based on the uncalibrated SN2 velocities (Section
3.2
), the resulting fitted SN3 velocities are usually of a higher precision and can be calibrated using the skyline method described in Section
2.4
. The exceptions to this are sources that have
S/N<3
in the H
\alpha
line, for which the SN2-based velocities generally remain more reliable. Nevertheless, the SN2 filter has a much lower spectral resolution and, more importantly, it lacks (detected) sky lines required for a velocity calibration. We thus calibrate the SN2 velocities of these planetary nebulae by finding the average offset between the uncalibrated SN2 velocities and the fully calibrated SN3 velocities of nearby objects. This method is very similar to that used by
83
, but we nevertheless explain it in full here as a few modifications were made.

First, we compile a catalogue of sources that are reliably detected (flux
S/N>3
) in both [O
iii
] and H
\alpha
, so that these sources have velocities estimated using both the SN2 (
v_{\mathrm{SN2}}
) and the SN3 (
v_{\mathrm{SN3}}
) filters. These sources are not required to have planetary nebula-like emission or be point sources. As we can use the sky-line calibration for SN3, we treat the calibrated SN3 velocities as the ground truths. For each planetary nebula that does not have a velocity derived using the SN3 filter, we search for objects within a radius of
150
pixels, iteratively increasing that radius by
100
pixels at a time, if necessary, until at least
20
sources are found. We then calculate the median
v_{\mathrm{SN3}}-v_{\mathrm{SN2}}
of these sources, and use that as a correction factor for the planetary nebula SN2 velocity. For error propagation, the standard deviation of all the
v_{\mathrm{SN3}}-v_{\mathrm{SN2}}
measurements is taken as the uncertainty of the correction factor.

This method assumes that the extra correction to the SN2-derived velocities varies smoothly across the field of view,
but we can test its accuracy by also applying it to sources which do have SN3 (sky line-based) velocities. The outcome of this is shown in Fig.
11
for NGC 4449, revealing that the corrected SN2 and SN3 velocities of the sources follow the
1:1
line, with a standard deviation of
\approx 20~\mathrm{km~s}^{-1}
. The velocities of sources detected only in SN2 may therefore be slightly less reliable. In this work, we quote the SN3 velocities for sources with
S/N>3
in the H
\alpha
line, SN2 velocities otherwise.

3.5
Mock catalogue and recovery tests

To better understand our recovery rate and to define a completeness limit for our planetary nebula catalogues, we generate mock populations of planetary nebulae. As planetary nebulae are the progeny of stars, their distribution should to first order reflect that of the underlying stellar light. We therefore start by performing surface photometry of each galaxy, and use that to dictate where the mock planetary nebulae are inserted spatially. We first mask out sources identified as stars by
GAIA
, as these do not belong to the galaxy. To avoid being biased by emission-line regions, we use a map of the galaxy (stellar) continuum emission in SN2. We then carry out surface photometry, fitting the surface brightness in elliptical annuli of constant ellipticity, position angle and centre. We use
12
annuli, increasing the semi-major and semi-minor axes logarithmically.
The relative surface brightness of each annulus determines the number of mock planetary nebulae it receives. Within each elliptical annulus, the positions of the mock planetary nebulae are randomised. To test the planetary nebula recovery as a function of apparent magnitude, we also randomly sample the magnitudes from an assumed Ciardullo-like planetary nebula luminosity function
(
12
)
. In doing so, we need to assume a bright-end cut-off and therefore a distance to each galaxy, but this does not affect the completeness limit inferred, which is at a much fainter magnitude. The distance used for each galaxy is as listed in Table
1
.

To insert each planetary nebula in a data cube, we create a 2D Gaussian spatial model using
astropy
, with input parameters as determined from foreground stars. For each mock planetary nebula, we take the fitted
\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}
and
\mathcal{D}
at its input position, using the maps created in Section
3.3.2
.

The spectra of the mock planetary nebulae are scaled versions of each other, in the sense that the [O
iii
] flux matches that of the sampled
{m}_{5007}
. We select input emission-line ratios, such that a source with these emission lines would be located in the appropriate region of each BPT diagram, and assign the same ratios to each mock planetary nebula.
ORCS
is then used to simulate SN2 and SN3 spectra at spectral resolutions matching that of the real data cubes, assuming the same velocity offsets and broadening as the real planetary nebulae.

Finally, the mock planetary nebulae are inserted into the real data cubes, to best recreate the conditions under which real planetary nebulae are searched for (e.g. same noise and contaminant sources). To ensure sufficient numbers of mock planetary nebulae (and thus reliable statistics) but avoid source confusion, for each galaxy we create
3
unique sets of mock data cubes with
\approx 300
planetary nebulae in each.

Using the same methods as for the real catalogue, we create a detection map using
ORCS
, run our source detection algorithms using
photutils
and carry out emission-line fitting with the same parameters as for the real data. We then cross-match the input mock planetary nebulae with the recovered sources. Fig.
12
shows how our mock planetary nebulae populate the [N
ii
]-BPT emission-line ratio diagnostic diagram, where the input emission-line ratios are indicated by the red star.
The colour coding of the data points shows that the scatter of the mock planetary nebulae about the input emission-line ratios increases with faintness (larger magnitudes) and proximity to the galaxy centre (increased contamination due to bright H

regions).

3.6
PNLF and the
\alpha
parameter

The planetary nebulae luminosity function specifies the number of planetary nebulae within a certain
{m}_{5007}
range. It has empirically been shown to function as a distance indicator through its bright-end cut-off, applicable to both early- and late-type galaxies. It is described by Eq.
1
, but we use the simplified version that keeps the faint-end slope index fixed at
c_{2}=0.307
and we assume an absolute magnitude of the bright-end cut-off
M^{*}_{5007}=-4.47

(
15
)
. The only parameter we fit for is the apparent magnitude of the bright-end cut-off (
m^{*}_{5007}
), for which we use the method of maximum likelihood
(
12
)
as implemented by
72
. By treating the planetary nebula luminosity function as a probability distribution, we avoid binning the planetary nebulae in magnitude. For the initial guess of the distance modulus we assume the distance listed in Table
1
for each galaxy, which is the mean of the distances listed in the NASA/IPAC Extragalactic Database. For NGC 4214 and NGC 4449, these come from a combination of methods including TRGB.

The specific frequency of planetary nebulae, also referred to as the
\alpha
parameter, is defined in Eqs.
3
and
4
. Although
\alpha
is traditionally defined using a bolometric luminosity, it has become common practice to use
B
or
V
band photometry with a bolometric correction, as described in
10
. We also follow this convention and calculate
\alpha_{\mathrm{bol}}
parameters based on
V
-band photometry from
18
, using the integrated magnitudes obtained from their infrared apertures as they most closely match the areas over which we detect planetary nebulae (see Fig.
1
). We assume distances as listed in Table.
1
, rather than the ones derived from PNLF fitting. In addition to
\alpha_{\mathrm{bol}}
, we provide an
\alpha_{V}
parameter for each galaxy, which simply uses the
V
-band luminosity without a bolometric correction.

The number of planetary nebulae we recover is only a subset of the entire population of planetary nebulae, even at bright magnitudes. This needs to be accounted for when calculating the
\alpha
parameter. For each galaxy, we determine the recovery rate using the mock catalogue of planetary nebulae. This recovery rate as a function of magnitude is then used to scale the number of recovered planetary nebulae to what should be more representative of the full population. To facilitate comparisons between surveys of varying depth, the
\alpha
parameter is often defined up to
0.5
or
2.5
magnitudes from
m^{*}_{5007}
. It should be noted that with a completeness threshold defined at
50\%

5.2
Flux and planetary nebula luminosity function uncertainties

The planetary nebula
{m}_{5007}
listed in Tables
3
and
4
were calculated using the
Hubble Space Telescope
-derived calibration factors (Section
2.3
). Additional flux uncertainties are introduced by the aperture correction, and as the correction factors were calculated using isolated undistorted objects, they may not be accurate for sources closer to the edges of the fields of view, where the point spread functions are more distorted. Background subtraction is also very important for star-forming galaxies such as those from Star formation, Ionized Gas, and Nebular Abundances Legacy Survey. There is much contaminant emission from a variety of sources (principally H
ii
regions and supernova remnants), including in the [O
iii
] line that can in turn be over- or under-subtracted. Additionally, while we do correct for foreground Milky Way extinction, we do not account for extinction within the host galaxy. All these factors can influence the derived planetary nebula apparent magnitudes, in turn impacting the derived planetary nebula luminosity function distances.

The planetary nebula luminosity function distances are also affected by incompleteness. Our mock planetary nebula catalogues have shown that we are unable to recover most of the planetary nebulae in the central regions of our galaxies (Fig.
13
). If the brightest planetary nebulae of a galaxy remain undetected because of this, the resulting fit to the planetary nebula luminosity function would overestimate the galaxy distance. Conversely, the presence of contaminants can lead to an underestimation of the galaxy distance, if bright compact H
ii
regions are confused for planetary nebulae.

5.3
Velocity fitting

As discussed in Section
3.2
, the fitting software
ORCS
requires a rather precise initial guess for the input velocity of a source. The precision required increases with increased spectral resolution. While we do not expect this to have impacted the results of this paper, it could potentially be a problem for galaxies with higher rotational velocities. The way the velocity guess in SN3 is now implemented, three different guesses are compared, and the best one is selected from the reduced
\chi^{2}
. In galaxies with higher velocities, a larger number of guesses over a wider range of velocities may need to be compared.

For some noisy spectra or spectra of very faint objects, even with the aforementioned three velocity guesses, a noise peak sometimes gets fitted and an incorrect flux and velocity are returned. These bad fits can be difficult to identify, as the associated uncertainties are relatively small (
S/N\approx 2
). We have attempted to minimise the impact on our planetary nebula catalogue by only quoting
v_{\mathrm{H\alpha}}
for planetary nebulae which have
S/N>3
in the H
\alpha
line, and using
[\textup{O\,{iii}}]
velocities otherwise (see Tables
3
and
4
). For each galaxy, there are six (bona fide or possible) planetary nebulae for which this applies. One of these, NGC4214-Y6, still has an outlying
v_{[\textup{O\,{iii}}]}
(compared to the rest of the catalogue) and may therefore be a contaminant.

The required precision for fitting is only a challenge because we are looking at faint point-sources that have velocities distinct from that of the diffuse gas. For other science done using the Star formation, Ionized Gas, and Nebular Abundances Legacy Survey data, initial velocity guesses can be easily sampled from velocity maps created using diffuse emission and H
ii
regions.

We would like to repeat the caveat that the calibration of
v_{[\textup{O\,{iii}}]}
, as described in Section
3.4
, relies on the assumption that the velocity offset
v_{\mathrm{SN3}}-v_{\mathrm{SN2}}
varies smoothly across the FOV.

6
Conclusions

In this paper we have described in detail a new automated pipeline to detect planetary nebulae in integral-field spectrograph data, particularly suited to the Star formation, Ionized Gas, and Nebular Abundances Legacy Survey data obtained with SITELLE. Compared to previous methods which rely on visual inspection of [O
iii
] flux maps or similar
(
83
, e.g.)
, this pipeline mostly eliminates the need for visual inspection. This speeds up the process considerably, while also making the results more objective and replicable.

We applied our new pipeline to the star-forming galaxies NGC 4214 and NGC 4449. In NGC 4214, we recovered
19
known planetary nebulae and discovered
6
(possible) new ones. We derived a PNLF distance of
3.09^{+0.25}_{-0.46}
Mpc, in agreement with previous distance measurements. In NGC 4449, we confirmed
10
previously reported planetary nebulae and discovered
13
(possible) new ones. We derived a PNLF distance of
3.91^{+0.33}_{-0.52}
Mpc, also in agreement with previous distance measurements.

As became apparent in the tests we ran using mock planetary nebulae (Fig.
13
), detecting planetary nebulae superposed or close to bright star-forming regions is challenging even with the Star formation, Ionized Gas, and Nebular Abundances Legacy Survey IFS data. This is reflected in the spatial distributions of the recovered planetary nebulae, as shown in the left panels of Figs.
14
and
16
. However, because the mock planetary nebula samples allow us to robustly quantify the recovery rates, we can correct for this when calculating the planetary nebula specific frequencies (
\alpha
parameters). Additionally, SITELLE’s wide field of view allows us to detect planetary nebulae to greater galactocentric distances, where contaminants are also less prominent. Of course, the Star formation, Ionized Gas, and Nebular Abundances Legacy Survey data also yield the planetary nebula emission-line ratios, allowing to reject compact contaminants, and to measure velocities. It is therefore highly complementary to narrow-band
Hubble Space Telescope
planetary nebula surveys.

Although our planetary nebula detection pipeline is meant to be generic, small changes may be necessary to apply it to the other Star formation, Ionized Gas, and Nebular Abundances Legacy Survey galaxies, to optimally handle different galaxy morphologies. For example, even though this functionality already exists in the current version of the pipeline, the initial velocity guesses required for the emission-line fits may have to be sampled from wider velocity ranges. Galaxies at larger distances may also prove more challenging, as larger fractions of the contaminants will appear spatially unresolved and only smaller (i.e. brighter) portions of the planetary nebula luminosity functions will be probed, resulting in proportionally fewer planetary nebulae detected. This will however be partially compensated for brighter galaxies, as they inherently harbour more planetary nebulae.

Overall, the new method developed in this paper is bound to lead to hundreds of new planetary nebula discoveries in a range of star-forming galaxies, most of which have never been surveyed for planetary nebulae before.
