Mothers’ experiences during prenatal and childbirth journey in Bali: a qualitative study
Authors: Ni Made Dian Kurniasari, Kazuko Tanaka, Desak Nyoman Widyanthini, Ni Luh Putu Suariyani, Rina Listyowati, Akimi Urayama, I Md.Ady Wirawan, Koichi Yoshimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndonesia has made substantial progress in reducing maternal mortality, with the Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR) declining from 446 per 100,000 live births in 1990 to 305 per 100,000 live births in 2015 [1]. Nevertheless, further improvements in the quality of maternal healthcare are needed. This study sought to investigate the experiences of Balinese mothers and their perceptions regarding care during pregnancy and childbirth.MethodsWe used a qualitative descriptive study design with in-depth semi-structured interviews to explore experiences of Balinese mothers and their perceptions of the perinatal care they received during pregnancy and childbirth. Nineteen Balinese women who had given birth in Bali were recruited for the study. Data were collected from August 2017 to January 2018 in Badung Regency, Gianyar Regency, Karangasem Regency and Denpasar City in Bali Province and were analysed using the qualitative content analysis method.ResultsThe analysis revealed one core category, “Mothers’ preferences and aspirations for childbirth,” and four major categories that characterize Balinese mothers’ experiences. The core category highlighted the importance of acknowledging women’s wishes in shaping positive birth experiences; mothers felt supported and confident when respected. The four major categories were: desire for a convenient and comfortable birth environment, support from community midwives, dissatisfaction with hospital care, and the stages leading to childbirth. Balinese women typically choose hospitals for delivery due to the availability of superior facilities, yet they frequently reported dissatisfaction with the attitudes and communication of health professionals. Furthermore, a significant lack of maternal knowledge and empowerment was observed, with many women tending to passively accept medical recommendations.ConclusionsAlthough mothers preferred hospitals for safety, many reported dissatisfaction with poor communication and negative attitudes from health professionals. Strengthening respectful communication, supporting shared decision-making, and expanding midwife-led, culturally sensitive care may enhance maternal satisfaction and birth experiences.

Background

Indonesia has made substantial progress in reducing maternal mortality from 446 per 100,000 live births in 1990. This progress was associated with improvements in maternal healthcare services, including an increase in the proportion of women receiving at least four antenatal care visits from 63% in 1994 to 77% in 2017 and an increase in deliveries assisted by skilled providers from 36.5% in 1994 to 91% in 2017 [2, 3]. However, despite these improvements, the 2015 Intercensal Population Survey reported a Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR) of 305 per 100,000 live births, indicating that significant challenges remain [1]. Although the availability and accessibility of professional maternal services have increased, improving the quality of care remains an important concern in maternal healthcare. Globally, substantial evidence highlights that non-respectful treatment and mistreatment of women during childbirth remain pervasive issues, particularly in Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) [4]. Understanding the local context of these experiences, such as in Bali, is therefore crucial for designing effective interventions.

The World Health Organization has formulated strategies to ensure that every pregnant woman and newborn receives quality care throughout pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period. Good quality care can avoid maternal mortality and morbidity, bring positive experiences of childbirth, and produce satisfactory outcomes for women and children. Good quality of care, as defined by the WHO, should be safe, effective, timely, efficient, equitable, and people-centred to achieve the desired health outcomes [5]. Therefore, the quality of care for women is multidimensional and extends beyond the care provided in healthcare facilities [5, 6]. It should be women-centred in that it considers the needs and preferences of women as well as their culture.

Maternal satisfaction is a key factor in assessing the quality of care and can be influenced by various factors [7, 8]. According to the WHO framework on quality of care, as described in an original article by Donabedian [9], assessing maternal satisfaction has a wide domain within the overall health system [5]. It also focuses on the measured experience of care, highlighting effective communication, respect, dignity, and emotional support for women and their families as healthcare users [6].

Most low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) do not have optimal maternal care services. A previous study showed that the major determinant of maternal satisfaction with the quality of care in LMICs was the process of care, emphasising the importance of health professionals in the delivery of services that are responsive, respectful, communicative, and considerate of women’s needs. Studies conducted in Indonesia also found that women preferred traditional birth attendants to midwives because they had good interpersonal skills and were caring, patient, and respectful of local customs [10, 11]. Nurse-midwives were found to have difficulty coping with the cultural beliefs of women and their families [12]. These findings indicate the need to increase the prominence of women-centred care delivery among health professionals.

Bali is one of the most developed provinces in Indonesia, with robust economic and infrastructural growth primarily driven by its tourism sector. Furthermore, this sector creates a high demand for labour, leading to significant population growth through migration and immigration. These demographic dynamics place additional pressure on the healthcare system, necessitating that the government provide access to high-quality health services, including maternal and child health services.

While Bali’s strong tourism economy suggests high overall development, this prosperity may mask significant disparities in public health access and quality for local mothers. Therefore, capturing the actual experiences of Balinese mothers is critical to identifying specific gaps in the public system and ensuring equitable care for a growing and diverse population. While previous studies provide valuable quantitative data on facility use and general satisfaction in Indonesia [13], research focusing specifically on maternal satisfaction remains scarce. Existing quantitative data, including studies comparing satisfaction across different health insurance schemes in Aceh [14], and recent reports from community health centres [15], generally report favourable satisfaction levels and a positive correlation between service quality and patient satisfaction.

However, the focus of these previous quantitative studies may not fully capture the subtle, qualitative aspects of care or the lived experience, particularly the influence of different insurance backgrounds or the reasons for maternal passivity despite reporting high satisfaction. A critical research gap thus remains: the lack of in-depth insights into Balinese mothers’ specific experiences. Specifically, existing research has not fully explored how cultural context, hierarchical communication, and mothers’ unique expectations influence their perceptions of care quality in the Balinese setting. This study aims to fill this gap by providing qualitative insight into the nuanced experiences of mothers in Bali.

Little is known about mothers’ experiences and satisfaction during pregnancy and childbirth in Bali and Indonesia, especially regarding care delivered by health professionals. With growing concern about the provision of high-quality and respectful care, measuring mothers’ perspectives and subsequent satisfaction with care delivery is anticipated to provide indications to drive future maternal and childcare services. This would lead to positive pregnancy outcomes and increased utilisation of healthcare services for mothers and children. This study is a part of a larger study aimed at exploring maternal and health professional’ perspectives concerning prenatal care and childbirth services in Bali [16]. This sub-study specifically focused on exploring experiences of Balinese mothers and their perceptions of perinatal care during pregnancy and childbirth.

Methods

Study design

We used a qualitative descriptive study design with in-depth semi-structured interviews to explore the experiences of Balinese mothers and their perceptions of the perinatal care they received during pregnancy and childbirth. This approach is well-suited for directly describing and clarifying personal experiences within the specific cultural context of Bali, ensuring that findings remain close to the participants’ own language and perspectives. Qualitative description represents the methodological category with the least level of inference among qualitative methods [17], and in-depth semi-structured interviews constitute the empirical backbone of qualitative research in the social sciences [18].

The conceptual foundation of this study was guided by Donabedian’s quality framework (Structure, Process, and Outcome) [19] and the WHO quality care dimensions. These models served two primary purposes: (1) they structured the development of the semi-structured interview guide, ensuring comprehensive coverage from infrastructure (Structure) to patient-provider interactions (Process) and perceived experiences (Outcome); and (2) they provided a framework for the qualitative content analysis and discussion, ensuring that the identified categories (e.g., respect and communication) were systematically linked to established quality dimensions.

To further assess midwifery care and focus our objectives, we also incorporated Halldorsdottir and Karlsdottir’s theory [20] and the concept of woman-centred care [21]. We asked participants about their satisfaction or dissatisfaction with the attitudes and services of health professionals during the perinatal period. The reporting of these results adhered to the COREQ guidelines.

Setting

In Bali, between 2016 and 2018, obstetricians had the highest proportion of first-time mothers attending their deliveries (56.4%), followed by midwives (40.4%), which highlights the shifting trend towards specialized facility-based care in the region [22]. The proportion of mothers who used obstetrician services increased from year to year, while that of midwives decreased; however, the need for midwives remained high. The data for this research were collected in Badung Regency, Gianyar Regency, Karangasem Regency, and Denpasar City (the island’s capital) in Bali Province, with Denpasar City having the highest population density in the region due to population growth [23].

Sample recruitment

The research participants were mothers who had recently given birth. We did not utilize key informants (i.e., experts or leaders) but focused on recruiting those with direct lived experience. This study recruited Balinese women who were: (i) aged 18–45 years, (ii) had given birth in Bali, (iii) consented to participate in the study, and (iv) whose youngest child was under 6 months. Balinese women used both obstetricians and midwives for antenatal checkups and childbirth, allowing for a comparison of obstetricians and midwives based on their perinatal experiences.

Participants were recruited through convenience sampling with the assistance of community midwives across four districts (Denpasar, Badung, Gianyar, and Karangasem). Midwives acted as key gatekeepers for the recruitment of participants. This strategy was chosen because midwives, who often serve as the primary maternal health professionals, possess trusted local status and established relationships within the Balinese community, which was essential for facilitating access and trust for the in-depth interviews. The midwives identified potential participants from their client registers/records (not hospital registers) and screened them against the inclusion criteria. The midwives approached the identified women sequentially to ask for their consent to be contacted by the research team. This method aimed to include women with diverse experiences, encompassing those who had both normal and complicated pregnancies or deliveries, to capture a broad range of perceptions regarding maternal care. Nineteen participants were recruited. The researchers collected data from August 2017 to January 2018.

Data collection

The quality of midwifery services is a key aspect of a woman’s experience of childbirth [20], and recent studies in policy and services surrounding maternity care have recommended a woman-centred approach [24, 25]. Consequently, we designed the interview guide with reference to the theory of midwife professionalism [20] and the concept of woman-centred care [21]. The first draft of the interview guide was prepared and discussed among the researchers. The interview guide was then pilot-tested with two Balinese women who met the inclusion criteria but were not part of the final study sample. Feedback from this pilot led to specific adjustments to ensure the guide was culturally and contextually appropriate. These included tailoring probing questions to reflect the various settings of maternal service delivery, such as community health centres, private midwifery practices, and hospitals. Additionally, we incorporated locally familiar terms, rephrased sensitive questions related to childbirth experiences to be more respectful, and modified the sequencing of questions to align with Balinese communication norms and storytelling styles. The data collected during this pilot phase were used solely to refine the protocol and were excluded from the final analysis to maintain methodological rigor.

Semi-structured, face-to-face interviews were conducted with the 19 participants to explore their satisfaction or dissatisfaction with perinatal care during their childbirth experiences. Prior to the interviews, background information was obtained, including age, length of marriage, reproductive history, place of delivery, and antenatal care. The interview guide included questions on the reasons for selecting a childbirth facility and the participants’ satisfaction or dissatisfaction with the attitudes and services provided by health professionals during the perinatal period, such as their knowledge and skills, communication skills, and partnership with the women and their families.

All participants provided written informed consent prior to the interviews. To ensure comfort and privacy, interviews were conducted at a time and location of the participants’ choosing, typically their homes. If a health centre or midwifery clinic was selected, the interview was held in a private, designated room to guarantee confidentiality and ensure that participants felt secure discussing sensitive topics without fear of being overheard. With the participants’ consent, the interviews were recorded using an IC recorder, and contemporaneous notes were taken. Each interview lasted for approximately one hour. Data collection was characterised by openness to new ideas among the interviewers and follow-up probing questions during the interviews. Data collection ceased at 19 interviews, as theoretical saturation was achieved and no further new themes or insights emerged relevant to the study aims.

Data analysis

Data were independently analysed using qualitative content analysis in accordance with Graneheim and Lundman’s methods [26]. This approach is a flexible method for analysing textual data [27] and is a research technique used for identifying replicable and valid inferences from data in a specific context [28]. The analysis commenced with a progression from manifest to latent content. Specifically, verbatim transcripts were read multiple times to achieve a sense of the whole. Meaning units were identified, condensed, and assigned codes. To ensure transparency in the abstraction process, these codes were constantly compared for similarities and differences; those sharing common core meanings were grouped into sub-categories. These sub-categories were then further abstracted into four major categories. Finally, a single core category was derived to capture the latent meaning across the entire dataset. For instance, codes reflecting unequal power dynamics were grouped into the sub-category of “Hierarchical Communication,” which contributed to the broader category of “Information and Communication.”

After completing the interviews, the researchers involved in conducting the interviews immediately reviewed and discussed the childbirth experiences described by the participants.

Data Processing and Translation: The recordings were transcribed verbatim into Indonesian by the first author and subsequently translated into English by an expert translator to ensure interpretive consistency. This primary translation was done by a single translator to maximize reliability [29]. To verify accuracy, a subset of the translated transcripts underwent back-translation by the bilingual first author. The original and back-translated texts were then compared by the research team, and any semantic discrepancies or nuances were resolved through consensus. Finally, the English transcripts were given to the Japanese researcher, who checked and compared them with their field notes to facilitate the subsequent analysis.

All researchers who had interviewed participants repeatedly read the verbatim records in order to familiarise themselves with the participants’ expressions and then extracted and summarised related text to avoid changing the individual participants’ overall meanings. Researchers performed the analysis separately, discussed the results, and reached consensus within the research team [28, 30]. The five phases of analysis [31] began with the researchers familiarising themselves with the data and identifying the preliminary codes. The codes were integrated into subcategories until the main categories that described the investigated phenomena emerged, as well as the key elements of interest. We developed subcategories and then compared the categories generated from the English datasets [29]. The categories were refined, reviewed, and returned to the data to ensure consistency. Triangulation, including reflexive field notes, was used to reduce the effect of research bias on establishing confirmability. The interview data, data analysis products, and data reconstruction products were provided to the research team for verification. To ensure dependability, field notes and individual interview data were compared to examine data consistency [32, 33].

Trustworthiness

To ensure trustworthiness, we employed analyst triangulation during data analysis. All Indonesian co-authors and the lead Japanese author met weekly during the analysis phase, challenging interpretations and ensuring consensus on the emerging codes and categories.

Ethical considerations

Given the hierarchical culture prevalent in the Balinese healthcare setting, specific measures were implemented to manage cultural sensitivities and ensure truly voluntary participation. The voluntary nature of the study and the right to withdraw at any time were emphasized multiple times during the informed consent process to overcome potential pressure stemming from the cultural tendency toward obedience or respect for the referring midwives. Additionally, the interview guide utilized culturally appropriate phrasing that focused on describing personal experiences and process, rather than direct evaluation or criticism of health professionals, allowing participants to discuss sensitive topics more comfortably.

The researchers adhered to the recommendations of the Declaration of Helsinki for conducting this research. Ethical approval was obtained from the Faculty of Medicine, Udayana University/Sanglah Hospital (493/UN.14.2/KEP/2017) and Yamaguchi Prefectural University (No. 27–45). Before data collection, the purpose and aim of the research were explained to the participants, and they were informed of their rights. Written and verbal information was provided to the participants, and written consent was obtained before the interview. Participants were also assured that their responses would be kept confidential; all collected information, including audio recordings and transcripts, were securely stored and accessible only to the research team. This study is part of a project in 2016–2021, which was implemented in cooperation with Udayana University and Yamaguchi Prefectural University.

Clarification of terminology

Throughout this manuscript, the term ‘midwives’ refers exclusively to certified professional midwives. The inclusive term ‘health professionals’ is used consistently to refer collectively to all clinical healthcare staff involved in maternal care, including midwives, nurses, and obstetricians/general practitioners, unless the context explicitly specifies a single profession.

Results

Participant characteristics

The participants’ average age was 27.7 (± 6.8) years. Of the 19 participants, 11 (57.9%) had a high school education, and 4 (21.1%) had a tertiary education. Thirteen (68.4%) participants were housewives, five (26.3%) were working in the private sector, and one (5.3%) was a farmer. Sixteen participants were Hindu, two were Muslim, and one was Christian.

The average length of marriage among the participants was 4.7 (± 5.9) years, and the average age of their husbands was 32 (± 9.8) years. Eight women (42.1%) were first-time mothers, and the other 11 (57.9%) were multipara. Two mothers had given birth more than three times.

In terms of the choice of birthing facilities, over half (57.9%) of the participants gave birth to their last child in a private hospital, five (26.3%) mothers gave birth in a midwives’ clinic, and three (15.8%) gave birth in a primary healthcare centre. Twelve (63.2%) mothers gave birth vaginally, and the other seven (36.8%) had caesarean sections. The majority of mothers (94.7%) had an Indonesian Maternal and Child Health handbook and consultations (89.5%) with health professionals (Tables 1 and 2).

Categories

Qualitative content analysis yielded four major categories and one core category related to Balinese mothers' experiences and perceptions of perinatal care quality. The core category, which illustrates the overall structure of the mothers' experiences, is "The mother's preferences and aspirations for childbirth." This core is comprehensively shaped by the four major categories and their corresponding subcategories (Fig. 1).

This centrality reflects the core category that acknowledging participants’ wishes by health professionals and family members during childbirth and the postnatal period is paramount. When their wishes were considered, participants felt relaxed, and their desires could also guide birth partners or health professionals in providing care according to maternal needs.

The four constituent categories are: (1) The desire for a convenient and comfortable place to give birth, (2) Encouragement and support from community midwives, (3) Dissatisfaction with hospital experiences, and (4) The different stages leading up to birth.

Mothers’ desires and wishes for childbirth

Participants generally emphasised the importance of health professionals and family members acknowledging their wishes during childbirth and the postnatal period. Desire for a convenient and comfortable place to give birth

This study found that mothers’ choice of birthplace was driven by the consideration of having a convenient place that met the mothers’ needs, health condition, and economic situation. Mothers also sought a place that brought comfort to them, their baby, and their family. Mothers chose a familiar place based on their previous experience or where a relative or sibling had given birth. In addition, they chose health centres close to their residence.Primary Health Centre, this is the closest to my home, all my relatives have been born here (IND2).

Discussion

The findings of this qualitative study provide valuable context and nuance to the existing discussion on maternal satisfaction in Indonesia. While quantitative reports often indicate high satisfaction rates, our in-depth findings suggest that underlying factors, such as hierarchical communication and low expectations of care, may influence how satisfaction is reported. This highlights the importance of interpreting existing quantitative satisfaction data with caution and critical reflection.

While data collection for this study was conducted in 2017–2018, the qualitative findings on health professionals’ attitudes, communication gaps, and systemic power dynamics remain highly relevant, as these issues persist and continue to challenge the achievement of respectful maternity care globally and in Indonesia. Moreover, maternal experiences, beliefs, and socio-cultural contexts tend to evolve slowly; therefore, insights into these dynamics retain their relevance over time and continue to inform current maternal health practice and outcomes.

The results of this study showed that participants generally experienced anxiety about giving birth. They obediently followed the instructions of the midwives and rarely questioned their suggestions. They also faced challenges in accessing information and maintained low expectations for higher-quality health services.

Women in this study described the delivery of a safe, healthy baby and their well-being as the main outcomes of satisfaction. They were afraid of adverse outcomes during childbirth, so they expected to give birth in good health facilities and obey health professionals. A previous study also showed that maternal and infant survival and safety were influential factors in satisfaction [34].

The finding that mothers rarely questioned the advice they received from their caregivers and displayed obedient behaviour warrants critical discussion. While professional compliance is generally positive, this passivity suggests the existence of hierarchical power dynamics between health professionals and patients, a known challenge in many low- and middle-income country (LMIC) healthcare settings [35]. This reluctance to engage is not merely a personal preference but is likely rooted in structural barriers, including lower educational attainment, socio-economic vulnerability, and a fear of retribution or poor treatment if they challenge authority. The strong emphasis by participants on family members acknowledging their wishes is consistent with growing evidence regarding the crucial role of social support during labour. Research on labour companionship, for example, highlights how a trusted companion can enhance maternal autonomy and protect against non-respectful care, especially in low-resource settings [35]. This reinforces our argument that support, and the acknowledgement of wishes are key to patient-centered care.

The pervasive hesitation of mothers to question or communicate their wishes to health professionals, a finding consistent with other studies in hierarchically structured settings, warrants deeper discussion of systemic power dynamics. The findings of this study suggest that participants experienced the healthcare system as operating within a strong professional authority model, where health professionals are seen as the sole possessors of knowledge and institutional power. This structural imbalance places the mothers in a submissive role. Our findings, such as reports of “traumatic reprimand during delivery” and “health professionals with little empathy,” illustrate how this hierarchy is actively enforced. Such authoritative and punitive behaviours transform the clinical setting into an intimidating environment where seeking clarification is perceived as challenging authority. Consequently, women hesitate to question health professionals not due to a lack of wishes, but out of fear of negative reactions or further psychological distress.

Furthermore, the demographic profile of our participants adds valuable context. The majority of mothers in our sample had low to moderate levels of education—a factor known to exacerbate hierarchical power dynamics and contribute to lower health literacy. This disparity may compound the feeling of subservience, suggesting that communication barriers are heavily influenced by socio-demographic factors that limit maternal agency.

We argue that this observed ‘obedience’ may reflect low expectations of care rather than genuine satisfaction or patient autonomy. When mothers expect minimal involvement, receiving basic information or being treated neutrally may be perceived as a high-quality interaction, even though they remain minimally empowered to manage their pregnancy. As noted by WHO [36, 37], patients have a fundamental right to participate actively in their care. To move beyond mere compliance toward true patient-centred care, interventions must address both the patient’s right to participate and the health professionals’ behaviour. Actively encouraging questions and shared decision-making is vital, as increased engagement builds trust, improves safety, and leads to more informed care decisions and positive health outcomes.

Women might not expect respectful care due to their reluctance to question health professionals, a finding that may reflect broader social and health-system norms that do not prioritise respectful care [34]. Shakibazadeh et al. conducted a review on respectful care during childbirth and found that women in LMICs were less likely to prioritise their personal choice and participate in decision-making for their childbirth compared to women in high-income countries [38]. Mothers’ sociodemographic characteristics were considered predictors of their willingness to participate in medical encounters [37]. Our study was based on mothers from low-middle-income families and low-middle education backgrounds, which might have limited the women’s access to accurate information and led to a low expectation of quality of care. This is also consistent with a study in LMICs that found that low levels of education were associated with low expectations of care [39]. Low expectations might also be due to a lack of knowledge about what establishes good quality of care, limited awareness regarding standards and client rights, repeated acquaintance with low quality of care, and reluctance to express critical comments or personal desires [39, 40]. Patients with low literacy have less knowledge about their medical illnesses and experience more difficulty navigating the healthcare system.

While the findings underscore the need for women to be knowledgeable about their health and care options, a primary implication is the need for interventions focused on maternal empowerment. Education should not only provide information but also equip women with the confidence and skills to actively participate in shared decision-making and address the hierarchical power dynamics observed. Furthermore, these findings emphasize that any educational intervention must be balanced by systemic improvements and mandatory training for health professionals to ensure they actively encourage women’s participation and practice respectful communication. This would increase their ability to manage their pregnancy, leading to positive birth experiences while they continue to consider and follow health professionals’ suggestions. Providing patient education and allowing more time for information exchange between health professionals and mothers during healthcare meetings can increase mothers’ autonomy and empowerment during their pregnancy and childbirth journey [34]. Provision of information in pregnancy should include physiological, behavioural, social, cultural, and biomedical components and should value embodied and cultural knowledge, as well as biomedical evidence [41].

Proximity and quality of care influence the choice of facility

In this study, primary health centres and midwifery services were frequently utilized by participants for antenatal care, whereas hospitals were predominantly selected for childbirth. For antenatal care, mothers generally preferred facilities located close to their homes, such as primary health centres and midwifery services.

The finding that some mothers preferred to deliver in hospitals because of the perceived higher quality of care, even when primary care facilities (Puskesmas/midwifery clinics) were available, requires contextualization within Indonesia’s National Health Insurance Scheme (JKN). While JKN is designed with Primary Health Centres (PHCs) acting as gatekeepers [42], the mothers’ choices suggest that perceived differences in quality of care and safety—sometimes facilitated by private payments outside the JKN referral mechanism or utilization of the emergency clause—still drive decisions regarding facility choice. This indicates that while the system is designed to promote PHC use, the perceived quality gap remains a significant factor in women’s choice of delivery setting. A previous study in Indonesia reported that women with JKN coverage were more likely to deliver in hospitals and receive care from obstetricians/gynaecologists or general practitioners than women without insurance [43].

Women in this study were less satisfied if they were received in overcrowded hospitals. This study also found that mothers typically opted to give birth in a hospital to prepare for any potential abnormalities that may arise during pregnancy or delivery. However, they were often dissatisfied with the attitudes of health professionals in hospital settings.

Need for humane and caring health professionals

Although the women reported gratitude and relief that both the mother and newborn were well after birth, a secondary aspect that influenced their experience was the care provided by the health professionals. Some mothers also highlighted the need for high-quality and respectful care from health professionals. Mothers noted that interpersonal communication and demonstrations of ‘caring’ by health professionals and facility staff influenced their experience, although they did not necessarily determine overall satisfaction.

Generally, mothers consistently reported that experiences of care such as (i) effective communication, (ii) respect and dignity, and (iii) emotional support are crucial. The poor attitude of health professionals, such as ineffective communication (impolite, frowning, whispering, and non-explaining procedures), neglect (not attending to clients promptly, not involving clients in their care), unfriendliness (shouting, ignoring), and financial factors are major factors that make women uncomfortable. The characteristics, attitudes, and behaviours of health professionals and informal support providers are important to pregnant women. Respectful interaction and supportive social environments free from violence and discrimination between health professionals and mothers will heighten mothers’ self-esteem, dignity, and autonomy during pregnancy [44, 45]. This will increase the likelihood of positive birth experiences and satisfaction with the quality of care among mothers.

Client satisfaction with healthcare is subjective and interlinked with expectations and outcomes of care. The fact that having a companion improves maternal satisfaction underlines the importance of allowing a person to choose to accompany them during birth. This finding is consistent with those of other studies. Interpersonal relationships are important contributors to patient satisfaction. Mothers who had negative experiences during the care process reported dissatisfaction more frequently than those who had no such experience [46].

Achievement of higher quality, women-centred perinatal care

The finding that mothers value the acknowledgment of their wishes (as a key aspect of respectful care) necessitates practical steps to enhance maternal autonomy and participation in the birthing process. One fundamental tool for addressing this need is the birth plan. Promoting the use of birth plan documents is recommended as a crucial strategy to allow women to express their preferences, needs, desires, and expectations regarding the birth process, thereby promoting respectful women-centred perinatal care.

Limitations

First, the small and relatively homogeneous sample (n = 19 Balinese mothers from a specific socio-economic context) may limit the transferability of the findings to other regions and populations in Indonesia, although they provide valuable insights into maternal experiences within this specific cultural context.

Second, the data were collected between 2017 and 2018. Although socio-cultural norms and communication patterns may change gradually, subsequent health policy and system changes may have influenced more recent maternal experiences. Participants had different financing arrangements, including JKN coverage, self-payment, and private services, which may have influenced their experiences.

Third, the qualitative interviews may have been influenced by social desirability bias. Given the cultural norms of respect for authority identified in this study, some participants may have been reluctant to express dissatisfaction with healthcare services.

Finally, this study focused solely on mothers’ perspectives and did not include the views of health professionals or policymakers. Future research incorporating multiple stakeholder perspectives would provide a more comprehensive understanding of maternal healthcare experiences and service delivery in Indonesia.

The findings demand targeted interventions to bridge the gap between maternal desires and care delivery. Practically, our study suggests the potential for the following improvements:

Conclusions

This study underscores the significant influence of proximity and quality of care on expectant mothers’ choices of healthcare facilities in Bali, Indonesia. While primary health centres and midwifery services are frequently visited for antenatal care, hospitals remain the preferred option for deliveries due to the perceived need for better facilities in case of complications. However, despite this preference, many mothers expressed dissatisfaction with the attitudes and communication styles of health professionals, highlighting the importance of respectful, empathetic, and supportive interactions with health professionals. Improving interpersonal communication is essential, as respectful care is associated with greater patient satisfaction and reduced psychological trauma during childbirth.

The findings also revealed a critical gap in mothers’ empowerment and access to information. Although many women demonstrated strong trust in health professionals, their tendency to follow advice without question suggests limited active participation in decision-making. Enhancing maternal knowledge and encouraging shared decision-making may increase patient satisfaction, strengthen trust, and improve health outcomes. Additionally, culturally sensitive care, such as accommodating requests for female caregivers or non-hospital birth environments, may further support women’s preferences and comfort.

Low expectations of healthcare quality appeared to be more common among mothers from lower socioeconomic and educational backgrounds. This highlights the need for comprehensive educational initiatives, such as antenatal workshops and community-based programmes, which address physiological, behavioural, social, cultural, and biomedical aspects of childbirth. These programmes should aim to strengthen women’s confidence, autonomy, and cultural identity within the healthcare system.

In conclusion, improving the quality of maternal healthcare requires a dual strategy: strengthening the interpersonal and communication skills of health professionals and empowering mothers through education, culturally sensitive care, and active involvement in decision-making processes. Considering the high regard for midwives in Bali, expanding midwife-led models of care within hospital settings may offer a pathway to more woman-centred, respectful, and satisfying perinatal care experiences. By addressing these dimensions, the maternal healthcare system can better promote positive birth experiences, improve satisfaction, and enhance trust among mothers.
