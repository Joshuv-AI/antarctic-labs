SPIRAL: A Closed-Loop Framework for Self-Improving Action World Models via Reflective Planning Agents
Yu Yang, Yue Liao, Jianbiao Mei, Baisen Wang, Xuemeng Yang, Licheng Wen, Jiangning Zhang, Xiangtai Li, Hanlin Chen, Botian Shi, Yong Liu, Shuicheng Yan, Gim Hee Lee

Abstract
We introduce SPIRAL , a S elf-improving P lanning and I terative R eflective A ction World Modeling closed- L oop framework that enables controllable long-horizon video generation conditioned on high-level semantic actions. Existing one-shot video generation models operate in open-loop, often resulting in incomplete action execution, weak semantic grounding, and temporal drift. SPIRAL formulates ActWM as a closed-loop think–act–reflect process, where generation proceeds step by step under explicit planning and feedback. A PlanAgent decomposes abstract actions into object-centric sub-actions, while a CriticAgent evaluates intermediate results and guides iterative refinement with long-horizon memory. This closed-loop design naturally supports RL evolving optimization, improving semantic alignment and temporal consistency over extended horizons. We further introduce the ActWM-Dataset and ActWM-Bench for training and evaluation. Experiments across multiple TI2V backbones demonstrate consistent gains on ActWM-Bench and mainstream video generation benchmarks, validating SPIRAL ’s effectiveness.

1
Introduction

Video provides a high-dimensional, continuous, and dynamically structured representation of the visual world, making it a natural medium for modeling physical and interactive environments. Recent advances in video generation
(
35
;
4
;
7
)
have demonstrated strong capability in learning complex spatiotemporal distributions, shifting research from static content modeling toward dynamic visual modeling. Building on this progress, video world models extend video generation to controllable world simulation
(
1
)
, emphasizing human-in-the-loop interaction and world state evolution driven by temporal sequences and agent actions.

Existing approaches
(
5
)
typically realize interaction through low-level control,
e.g.
, keyboard/mouse inputs or explicit motion trajectories, which directly specify object movements but offer limited expressiveness for abstract, high-level semantic behaviors. In this article, we explore a complementary interaction paradigm that treats language descriptions as high-level semantic actions, enabling object-centric behavioral control over extended temporal horizons in generated videos. We refer to this paradigm as the Action World Model (ActWM), which semantically extends world models driven by low-level controls.

While this formulation can be viewed, at a surface level, as a form of text-and-image-to-video (TI2V) generation, ActWM emphasizes action-conditioned evolution of subjects and scenes, rather than the synthesis of globally plausible scenes or brief motions from static prompts. Consequently, naively reducing ActWM to a standard TI2V task and directly applying TI2V models introduces fundamental challenges.
Incomplete Action Execution
arises because high-level semantic actions require persistent execution over long horizons, yet existing models often terminate prematurely (Fig.
1
a).
Action Hallucination and Weak Semantic Grounding
occur without explicit planning, resulting in motions that contradict instructions or fail to act on target objects (Fig.
1
a).
Long-horizon Temporal Incoherence
stems from the lack of explicit state representation and memory, causing object and scene drift over time (Fig.
1
a). More fundamentally, these failures are exacerbated by
Open-loop Error Accumulation
, as most TI2V methods generate without intermediate feedback or correction.

To address these challenges, we propose
SPIRAL
, an agentic, self-improving framework with reflective planning agents for ActWM.
SPIRAL
replaces open-loop, one-shot generation with a
closed-loop think–act–reflect
paradigm. Given a high-level semantic action prompt, a VLM-driven
PlanAgent
grounds the action in the visual context and decomposes it into object-centric, step-wise sub-actions. Video generation is reformulated as a multi-step, autoregressive, chunk-wise process, with iterative refinement guided by a
CriticAgent
that evaluates action fidelity and visual consistency, while a memory module preserves long-horizon object and scene consistency. Crucially, this closed loop induces stochastic action trajectories and deterministic critic rewards, enabling RL optimization and continual
Progressive-Evolution
. Together,
SPIRAL
achieves precise and sustained grounding of high-level semantic actions into coherent, long-horizon video dynamics.

Beyond pipeline design, realizing
SPIRAL
in practice requires adapting general VLMs into action planning and video critic experts, and transforming generic TI2V models into step-wise ActWMs. To this end, we introduce ActWM-Dataset, constructed by re-annotating existing action–video pairs into multi-step procedures, comprising 24,616 tasks with 118,156 step-level annotations. The dataset supports fine-tuning of PlanAgent and adaptation of video world models, while the CriticAgent is trained via supervised distillation from large VLMs followed by reward modeling to provide reliable verification. To evaluate ActWM, we further introduce ActWM-Bench, a benchmark for assessing long-horizon action grounding and temporal consistency.

Extensive experiments demonstrate that
SPIRAL
can be seamlessly integrated with diverse TI2V models, yielding consistent improvements on ActWM-Bench and VBench, with ablations validating the roles of individual agents and the RL continuous training pipeline.
Our key contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose
SPIRAL
, a closed-loop, agentic framework that extends one-shot open-loop video generation with reflective planning agents for ActWMs.

•

We introduce a large-scale dataset and benchmark for ActWMs, enabling step-wise training and evaluation.

•

We further adopt a closed-loop training scheme that combines SFT with RLVR, leading to consistent improvements across diverse TI2V backbones.

Agents for Video Generation.

Recent work has explored incorporating chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning and intermediate planning into video generation to improve consistency and controllability
(
14
)
. These approaches typically decompose high-level prompts into structured intermediate signals, and have evolved from pre-planning
(
23
)
or post-refinement
(
48
)
toward “thinking while generating” paradigms
(
13
)
. Representative examples include EditThinker
(
21
)
, which frames image editing as iterative reasoning, and VideoAgent
(
32
)
and CoAgent
(
45
)
, which cast video generation as a closed-loop, agentic process with planning, synthesis, and verification. In contrast to these works, we explicitly formulate a closed-loop framework with reflective planning agents and an explicit memory module, enabling sustained grounding of high-level actions over long temporal horizons.

Video World Models.

Video world models aim to learn environment dynamics by predicting future observations conditioned on past observations and actions
(
15
)
. Early studies focused on games and simulated environments
(
2
)
, with recent progress extending to robotics
(
46
;
24
)
and real-world settings
(
42
)
. With advances in video foundation models
(
43
;
35
)
, recent works improved long-horizon generation quality
(
27
;
38
)
, inference efficiency
(
41
;
7
)
, action controllability
(
33
)
, and physical fidelity
(
1
)
. Unlike prior works that primarily use text prompts to describe global scenes or short-term changes
(
17
)
, our work focuses on persistent, object-grounded control, treating language as high-level semantic actions driving long-horizon behavior.

Self-Evolving Video Generation.

Self-evolving paradigms have been widely studied in code generation, question answering, and agentic search
(
47
;
18
;
37
)
. In video generation, recent efforts have begun to explore iterative refinement and planning for long-horizon synthesis. VideoAgent
(
32
)
introduces a self-improving planning framework using VLM guidance and environment feedback, while VISTA
(
26
)
proposes a test-time multi-agent self-improvement framework based on structured prompt refinement. Unlike prior test-time or heuristic self-improvement approaches, our method enables closed-loop reinforcement learning with critic-derived signals, allowing policies to progressively evolve through group-relative optimization.

3
Method

3.1
Overview

Problem Formulation.

We formulate long-horizon, action-conditioned video generation as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) defined by the tuple
\mathcal{T}=\langle g,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{V}\rangle
.
Given a global goal
g
, the objective is to synthesize a coherent video trajectory
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{T}\}
guided by a sequence of structured steps
\mathcal{S}=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{T}\}
.
At each timestep
t
, the system jointly generates the plan
s_{t}
and video segment
v_{t}
conditioned on historical context, maximizing cumulative rewards that reflect action completion and temporal coherence.

Overall Framework.

To ensure long-term consistency and controllability, we propose a hierarchical, closed-loop framework (Fig.
2
) that operates
iteratively
:

(1)
a
PlanAgent
(
\pi_{\text{plan}}
), serving as the high-level policy, employs CoT reasoning to decompose the global goal
g
into structured atomic plans
s_{t}
;

(2)
a
World Model
(
\pi_{\text{wm}}
) functions as the execution policy, synthesizing video segments
v_{t}
conditioned on the plan
s_{t}
and context memory
\mathcal{M}_{t-1}
;

(3)
a
CriticAgent
(
\mathcal{C}
) evaluates action-video alignment via a reward
r_{t}=\mathcal{C}(v_{t},s_{t})
, triggering a feedback loop for refinement upon detecting failures;

(4)

World Memory
accumulates successful transitions as
\mathcal{M}_{t}=\mathcal{M}_{t-1}\cup\{(s_{t},v_{t})\}
to maintain global coherence.
Collectively, this cycle instantiates a
think–act–reflect
loop, advancing beyond one-shot generation toward a robust plan-and-verify formulation.

3.2
PlanAgent: Structured Reasoning and Planning

Directly conditioning a world model on a high-level goal
g
in a one-shot manner often leads to temporal hallucinations or incomplete execution.
To mitigate this, we employ a
PlanAgent
(parameterized by a VLM
\pi_{\text{plan}}
) to explicitly decompose abstract goals into executable action sequences.

Formally, given a goal
g
and memory context
\mathcal{M}
, the agent employs Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning to synthesize a structured plan sequence:

\small\mathcal{S}=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{T}\},\hskip 9.24994pt\mathcal{S}\sim\pi_{\text{plan}}(\cdot\mid g,\mathcal{M})

(1)

Each atomic plan is defined as a tuple
s_{t}=(a_{t},c_{t}^{pre},c_{t}^{post})
, where
a_{t}
denotes the language action instruction, while
c_{t}^{pre}
and
c_{t}^{post}
represent physical pre- and post-conditions.
For instance, for an action
a_{t}
=
‘open the jar’
, the agent specifies
c_{t}^{pre}
=
‘jar closed’
and
c_{t}^{post}
=
‘lid removed’
.
Crucially, this CoT paradigm compels the agent to explicitly reason about causal dependencies, ensuring that planned actions are physically feasible rather than merely semantically relevant.

The PlanAgent undergoes a hierarchical training process: we first conduct
Instruction Tuning (IT)
on our curated
ActWM-Dataset
(Sec.
4.1
) to instill CoT-based structured planning capabilities, followed by
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
to enforce alignment with physical reality and temporal logic.
This regimen empowers the agent to not only capture structured causal dependencies but also effectively mitigate logical hallucinations in complex tasks.
Detailed implementation details are provided in Appendix
A.1
.

3.3
Action-Conditioned World Model

The
World Model
serves as an executor that translates each atomic plan
s_{t}
into pixel-level dynamics.
We instantiate this component as a video diffusion policy
\pi_{\text{wm}}
.
To ensure long-term consistency, the synthesis of the current video segment
v_{t}
is conditioned on both the action instruction
a_{t}
and retrieved historical context from the World Memory:

\small v_{t}\sim\pi_{\text{wm}}(\cdot\mid a_{t},\text{Encoder}(\mathcal{M}_{t-1}))

(2)

The instruction
a_{t}
specifies the intended motion or interaction, while the historical memory
\mathcal{M}_{t-1}
, stored as visual key frames or latent KV-caches, provides contextual guidance via encoded representations.
Generation is performed in a streaming manner, where each segment corresponds to a single planned step, and the World Memory preserves motion continuity across the long-horizon sequence.

To bridge the gap between generic short-clip generators and procedural long-horizon action generation, we adopt a
Streaming Long-Tuning
strategy.
Crucially, our framework supports the
plug-and-play
integration of arbitrary world models; by performing Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on
ActWM-Dataset
(Sec.
4.1
), we empower generic T2V and I2V backbones to follow step-wise action instructions over extended horizons (see Appendix
A.2
for a concrete instantiation). To systematically evaluate action controllability under this setting, we further introduce the
ActWM-Bench
(Sec.
4.2
), which assesses a range of modern world models within our closed-loop framework.

3.4
CriticAgent: Reward and Closed-Loop Feedback

Despite structured planning, open-loop generation remains susceptible to execution failures, such as missed actions, post-condition violations, and physical inconsistencies.
To address this, we employ a
CriticAgent
(parameterized by a VLM
\mathcal{C}
) to enforce closed-loop verification and feedback.

For each generated segment
v_{t}
, the CriticAgent assesses its alignment with the plan
s_{t}
across five key dimensions:
action adherence, object interaction, goal achievement, temporal coherence, and physical realism
.
It yields a scalar reward
r_{t}\in[0,1]
quantifying action execution quality, alongside textual feedback
f_{t}
for diagnosis:

\small r_{t},f_{t}=\mathcal{C}(v_{t},s_{t})

(3)

Leveraging these signals, we implement a dual-level feedback mechanism:

(1) Inner Loop (Local Refinement):
If
r_{t}
falls below a threshold
\tau
, indicating minor artifacts or partial action completion, the feedback
f_{t}
is used to refine the instruction
a_{t}
(e.g., emphasizing unmet post-conditions) for immediate regeneration via the World Model;

(2) Outer Loop (Global Replanning):
If generation fails consecutively for
K
attempts, implying an infeasible plan (e.g., missing pre-conditions or incorrect action ordering), the failure propagates to the PlanAgent to trigger the replanning of trajectory
\mathcal{S}
starting from step
t
.

To ensure robust verification, the CriticAgent undergoes a two-stage training regimen: first, we distill judgments from large VLMs (e.g., Gemini-3-Pro) via
Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)
; subsequently, we refine the model via
Pairwise Reward Modeling (RM)
using a Bradley-Terry objective to enhance discriminative accuracy.
Detailed implementation protocols are provided in Appendix
A.3
.

3.5
Progressive-Evolving: Closed-Loop GRPO Training

While inference-time feedback mitigates immediate errors, our objective is to internalize such corrections to permanently enhance the World Model.
To this end, we propose a
Progressive-Evolution
strategy using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), enabling closed-loop reinforcement learning driven by critic-derived signals.

Formally, initialized from the supervised
\pi_{\text{wm}}
, the World Model policy
\pi_{\theta}
undergoes iterative refinement leveraging stochastic plans from
\pi_{\text{plan}}
and rewards from
\mathcal{C}
.
For a plan step
s_{t}
, we sample a group of
G
video trajectories
\{v_{t,i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
via the previous policy
\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}
. The CriticAgent evaluates each sample, assigning a reward set
\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
.
We then compute the advantage
A_{i}
using group-wise normalization:

\vskip-5.69054pt\small A_{i}=\frac{r_{i}-\text{mean}(\{r_{1},\dots,r_{G}\})}{\text{std}(\{r_{1},\dots,r_{G}\})+\delta}

(4)

The World Model parameters
\theta
are updated by maximizing the GRPO objective:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{t}\sim\pi_{\text{plan}}(\cdot\mid g)\\
v_{t,i}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot\mid s_{t})\end{subarray}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\Big(\rho_{t,i}A_{i},

(5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{clip}(\rho_{t,i},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\,A_{i}\Big)-\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{wm}}\right)\Bigg]

where
\rho_{t,i}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v_{t,i}\mid s_{t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(v_{t,i}\mid s_{t})}

is the importance sampling ratio between
\pi_{\theta}
and
\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}
for sample
v_{t,i}
,

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\cdot)
denotes the KL divergence,
\epsilon
and
\beta
are hyper-parameters controlling policy clipping and regularization strength, respectively.

This closed-loop optimization effectively distills the PlanAgent’s reasoning knowledge and the CriticAgent’s physical verification into the World Model’s generative policy.
Moreover, this framework naturally facilitates
Curriculum Learning
. By progressively increasing the complexity of plans, the World Model evolves from generating atomic actions to executing complex, long-horizon procedural tasks. Additional implementation details are provided in Appendix
A.4
.

4
Dataset and Benchmark

4.1
ActWM-Dataset Construction

To support framework training, we present
ActWM-Dataset
, which provides structured
\langle g,\text{CoT},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{V}\rangle
supervision. It integrates global reasoning with step-wise action sequences grounded in video segments, providing a robust foundation for long-horizon planning and consistent video generation.

Data Sourcing and Preprocessing.
To ensure a diverse coverage of real-world tasks, we aggregate videos from four datasets: Ego4D
(
11
;
31
)
, Ego-Exo4D
(
12
)
, COIN
(
34
)
, and EPIC-KITCHENS
(
10
)
. These sources provide a broad range of procedural activities across egocentric and exocentric viewpoints. To remove redundant or irrelevant content, we apply an automated shot detection pipeline. This process filters out low-quality or ambiguous clips, retaining only those segments that strictly align with significant action boundaries for annotation.

Hierarchical Annotation Pipeline.

We develop an automated pipeline to convert raw video clips into structured annotations
\langle g,\text{CoT},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{V}\rangle
, effectively bridging visual signals with explicit step-wise actions. The process consists of two strategic stages:

Stage 1: Step-wise Action Annotation.

This stage instantiates the structured step tuple
s_{t}=(a_{t},c_{t}^{pre},c_{t}^{post})
from visual observations. By taking video clips
v_{t}
as input, we leverage a VLM (e.g., GLM-4.5V
(
44
)
) to identify fine-grained action instructions
a_{t}
(specifying verb-object-tool interactions) and infers physical state transitions
(c_{t}^{pre},c_{t}^{post})
. This yields a sequence
\mathcal{S}
of actions that explicitly models the physical dependencies.

Stage 2: CoT Planning Generation.
We employ an LLM to orchestrate global reasoning logic based on the goal
g
and sequence
\mathcal{S}
. By synthesizing a reasoning narrative (e.g.,
<think> ... </think>
), the model transforms disjoint steps into a coherent, logically grounded plan, providing intermediate supervision for long-horizon CoT-based planning.

Quality Verification and Human Alignment.

To ensure dataset integrity, we implement a hierarchical verification pipeline that integrates VLM filtering with human evaluation. Specifically, Qwen3-VL-235B
(
3
)
is employed as a verifier to filter samples with suboptimal video–text alignment. Subsequent human verification on a random subset yields a 93% alignment rate, confirming that our annotations faithfully capture both action semantics and causal dependencies. This validation ensures a reliable foundation for training agents and world models.

Dataset Statistics and Analysis.

ActWM-Dataset
comprises 24,616 procedural tasks, yielding 118,156 step-level action-video pairs. As illustrated in Fig.
3
, the dataset exhibits substantial diversity across three pivotal dimensions: (1)
Scenes
, spanning a wide range of environments from indoor to outdoor settings; (2)
Perspectives
, balancing egocentric and exocentric views to facilitate cross-view generalization; and (3)
Complexity
, covering a broad spectrum of temporal durations and task-step lengths. Its scale and structured nature make
ActWM-Dataset
uniquely positioned to support the development of planning-oriented agents and action-conditioned video generation.

4.2
ActWM-Bench Construction

To systematically evaluate the long-horizon controllability and physical plausibility of world models, we introduce
ActWM-Bench
, a comprehensive evaluation suite featuring hierarchical difficulty levels and multi-dimensional metrics.

Eval Prompt Suite.

We curate 300 evaluation prompts stratified into three difficulty levels to probe temporal generalization:
Simple
(1-3 action steps,
<20
s video duration),
Medium
(3-5 steps, 20-40s), and
Hard
(
>5
steps,
>40
s).
To ensure robust coverage, the suite spans diverse
scenes
(indoor, outdoor),
perspectives
(egocentric, exocentric), and
procedural activities
(e.g., sports, maintenance, cooking).

Evaluation Metrics.

We adopt a hybrid evaluation protocol.
We report
Static
and
Dynamic Quality
using VBench
(
19
)
to assess general visual fidelity.
However, since our task focuses on procedural action-conditioned video generation, such generic metrics are insufficient to evaluate whether actions are correctly executed.

Thus, our primary evaluation target is
Action Quality
.
To this end, we introduce a multi-agent action evaluator with GPT-5 as the reasoning engine, which performs comprehensive evaluation via multi-agent collaboration and cross-verified chain-of-query reasoning.
The agent evaluates Action Completeness, Motion Smoothness, Object Interaction, and Physical Fidelity. Details are provided in Appendix
B
.

Experiments

This section outlines the experimental setup, evaluates the PlanAgent and CriticAgent, and analyzes the Progressive-Evolution framework via our
ActWM-Bench
and ablations.

5.1
Experimental Settings

Implementation Details.

We summarize the implementations of our key components.

(1) PlanAgent
is built upon Qwen3-VL-8B
(
3
)
and trained on
ActWM-Dataset
with LoRA in two stages:
Instruction Tuning (IT)
to induce CoT reasoning and structured plan formatting, followed by
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
to better align generated plans with physical feasibility.

(2) CriticAgent
also undergoes a two-stage training.
We first conduct
Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)
by distilling the judgments of a strong model (Gemini-3-Pro) on videos generated by diverse baselines (e.g., Sora, CogVideoX, SkyReels) from VideoVerse
(
36
)
.
We then perform
Reward Modeling (RM)
with the Bradley-Terry objective on the GAIA dataset
(
9
)
, focusing on preference learning for action quality.

(3) Action-Conditioned World Model
uses LongLive (T2V) and SVI (I2V) as base generators; we apply
Streaming Long-Tuning
on
ActWM-Dataset
to equip them with action-following capabilities.

Evaluation Benchmarks.

We evaluate different components of our framework on three benchmarks.

(1) EgoPlan-Bench

(
8
)
assesses the
PlanAgent
on long-horizon procedural planning, utilizing task success rate as the primary metric.

(2) VideoGen-RewardBench

(
25
)
evaluates the
CriticAgent
as a video reward model, quantifying its alignment with human judgments via preference accuracy.

(3) ActWM-Bench (Ours)
holistically benchmarks
Action-Conditioned World Models
across multiple dimensions, including visual quality, temporal coherence, and action quality under varying difficulty levels.

Evaluations on PlanAgent

Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced
SPIRAL
, an agentic framework for Action World Modeling that formulates action-conditioned video generation as a closed-loop think–act–reflect process. By combining planning, feedback, memory, and RL optimization, our approach enables reliable long-horizon execution of high-level semantic actions. Experiments across multiple TI2V backbones, together with the proposed ActWM-Dataset and ActWM-Bench, demonstrate consistent improvements in action fidelity and temporal consistency, validating the effectiveness of closed-loop action-centric video generation.

Impact Statements

This paper introduces SPIRAL, a closed-loop think–act–reflect framework for action-conditioned video generation, together with the ActWM-Dataset and ActWM-Bench, with the goal of improving long-horizon action fidelity, temporal consistency, and the evaluation of Action World Models.
The primary objective of this work is to advance methodological research in video world models, agentic generation, and reinforcement learning–based optimization, which may support future studies in simulation, embodied AI, and interactive systems.

All datasets in this work are constructed by re-annotating existing publicly available datasets.
No new raw video content is collected, and no additional personal or sensitive data are introduced beyond those present in the original sources; accordingly, the data construction process follows established practices in prior research on video generation and procedural action understanding.

As with prior work on generative video models, the techniques studied here could in principle be applied in broader content generation settings.
However, this work focuses on action execution, physical fidelity, and benchmark-driven evaluation, and does not introduce capabilities specifically designed for identity manipulation, targeted deception, or surveillance.
We therefore believe that the ethical considerations and potential societal impacts of this work are consistent with those commonly associated with advances in generative video modeling and agent-based learning.

Architecture and Training Configuration.

We instantiate the PlanAgent using Qwen3-VL-8B
(
3
)
as the backbone. To enable structured planning while preserving pre-trained multimodal capabilities, we apply Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to all linear layers in the attention and feed-forward networks.

Training proceeds in two sequential stages:
Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)
and
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
.
(1) SFT
optimizes the model to internalize Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning and the required strict JSON schema.
(2) DPO
aligns outputs with physical plausibility and temporal logic by maximizing the margin between valid and flawed plans. Both stages use the AdamW optimizer with a cosine scheduler and a global batch size of 8 (via gradient accumulation). We set the context window to 8,192 tokens to accommodate interleaved visual history and extensive reasoning traces. Table
5
summarizes the hyperparameters.

DPO Preference Construction.

A critical component is constructing high-quality preference pairs
(y_{w},y_{l})
to suppress hallucinations and physical violations. Unlike standard datasets containing only ground truth, we employ a Teacher-Student Distillation pipeline to synthesize these pairs from
ActWM-Dataset
.

Chosen Response (
y_{w}
):
We prompt a strong teacher (GPT-5.1) to generate a Chosen response containing a rigorous CoT rationale (in
<think>
tags) decomposing the goal, followed by a valid JSON plan. The teacher ensures the plan successfully realizes the global goal depicted in the ground-truth video.

Rejected Response (
y_{l}
):
We prompt the teacher to generate a Hard Negative that starts plausibly but fails to achieve the goal due to injected errors, such as missing pre-conditions (e.g., pouring without opening), temporal disorder, or object hallucinations.

This Model-in-the-Loop synthesis ensures rejections are subtle and contextually relevant, providing stronger learning signals than random negative sampling. All generated data is filtered for strict schema adherence before training.

Inference Logic.

The PlanAgent operates in two inference modes:
Standard Planning Mode
. In the initial phase, the agent receives the goal and visual observations (current frame plus uniformly sampled history). We generate the action plan via temperature sampling, parsing the output to extract the thought process and structured
steps
list
\mathcal{S}
. The agent is constrained to output only the
<think>
block and JSON object to facilitate parsing.
Closed-Loop Re-planning Mode
. Upon failure detection by the CriticAgent, the PlanAgent enters a re-planning state using a structured
Failure Analysis
context. This context includes the
execution history
, the
failed attempt
, and the
critical diagnosis
. The logic enforces a Diagnose and Fix approach: the agent must rewrite the failed step to address feedback and revise subsequent steps for logical continuity. The output starts strictly from the failed step ID to ensure seamless integration.

A.2
WorldModel Implementation Details

Our framework adopts a
flexible, model-agnostic paradigm
for the Action-Conditioned World Model (
\pi_{\text{wm}}
), designed to support the
plug-and-play
integration of arbitrary Text-to-Video (T2V) or Image-to-Video (I2V) backbones. The core of our approach lies in utilizing the structured instruction-action pairs from
ActWM-Dataset
to perform Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), thereby endowing generic video generators with fine-grained, long-horizon instruction-following capabilities.

Model Instantiation.

To demonstrate the effectiveness of our SFT strategy, we present a concrete instantiation using Stable-Video-Infinity
(
22
)
, which leverages the Wan2.1-I2V-14B
(
35
)
as the foundational backbone. To adapt this model for precise action control while preserving its massive pre-trained generative prior, we apply Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to the query, key, value, output projection, and feed-forward network layers (
q,k,v,o,ffn
) of the DiT blocks. We set the LoRA rank
r=64
and alpha
\alpha=128
. The model processes video data encoded by the Wan2.1 VAE and text instructions via the T5-XXL encoder. Training is conducted using DeepSpeed Stage 2 with BF16 mixed precision to optimize memory efficiency.

Progressive Resolution Training.

Since
ActWM-Dataset
contains videos with diverse aspect ratios, we adopt a Progressive Resolution Training strategy to achieve high-fidelity generation across different spatial dimensions. We categorize the training data into resolution buckets and fine-tune the model in three sequential stages, progressively increasing spatial complexity while decaying the learning rate:
(1) Stage 1 (Base Adaptation):
The model is trained on
480\times 480
square clips with a learning rate of
1.0\times 10^{-4}
for 10 epochs to establish basic instruction alignment;
(2) Stage 2 (Rectangular Adaptation):
We increase the resolution to
640\times 480
with a reduced learning rate of
6.0\times 10^{-5}
for 3 epochs;
(3) Stage 3 (High-Res Adaptation):
The model is fine-tuned on
832\times 480
inputs with a learning rate of
4.0\times 10^{-5}
for 3 epochs. This curriculum allows the model to adapt to new spatial distributions and instruction complexities without catastrophic forgetting of the base motion dynamics.

(1) SFT

. Adhering to the VideoVerse protocol, we synthesize a diverse corpus of video samples using a suite of state-of-the-art world models, including CogVideoX-1.5-5B
(

)
, SkyReels-V2-14B
(
6

, HunyuanVideo
(

)
, OpenSora2.0
(
30

, Wan2.1-14B
(
35

(

(
29
)
. We utilize Gemini-3-Pro as the oracle judge to generate comprehensive Chain-of-Thought (CoT) critiques and scalar scores across five key dimensions, including
Action Adherence
,
Object Interaction
,
Goal Achievement
,
Temporal Coherence
, and
Physical Realism
. These rich signals are distilled into the CriticAgent to instill a robust evaluation schema.
For

(

This contrastive objective compels the agent to scrutinize fine-grained motion details rather than relying on coarse scene context, significantly mitigating hallucination rates in complex action scenarios.

Inference Logic.

The CriticAgent operates within our closed-loop system to provide actionable, structured feedback:

Multi-Dimensional Scoring.
Given a generated video segment
v_{t}
and the corresponding plan step
s_{t}
, the agent evaluates their alignment. The inference output is constrained to a structured JSON object containing scalar scores
r\in[0,1]
for each dimension alongside a textual
reasoning
field justifying the judgment.

Thresholding and Feedback.
We define a success threshold
\tau=0.7
for the primary metric,
Action Adherence
. If the score falls below
\tau
, the feedback loop is triggered. Crucially, the CriticAgent generates a
revised action instruction
, explicitly refining the prompt to address missing or violated conditions (e.g., “Ensure the hand clearly grasps the handle before lifting”).

Streaming Evaluation.

A.4
Closed-Loop GRPO Implementation Details

We provide a rigorous mathematical formulation of our
Progressive-Evolution via Closed-Loop GRPO Training
strategy. To enable Reinforcement Learning on the generative World Model, we first frame the denoising process as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). Subsequently, we detail the Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE) formulation required to inject exploration noise for Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), followed by the specific objective functions.

Video Generation as a Markov Decision Process.

While the high-level framework operates on plan steps
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, the low-level generation of a single video segment
v_{t}
involves an iterative denoising process. We formulate this inner loop as a finite-horizon MDP defined by the tuple
\mathcal{M}_{\text{denoise}}=\langle\mathbf{S},\mathbf{A},P,R\rangle
:

•

State Space (
\mathbf{S}
):
A state is defined as
\mathbf{x}_{\tau}=(\mathbf{c}_{t},\tau,\mathbf{z}_{\tau})
, where
\tau
represents the current denoising timestep (from
K
down to
0
),
\mathbf{z}_{\tau}
denotes the noisy video latent, and
\mathbf{c}_{t}=(s_{t},\text{Encoder}(\mathcal{M}_{t-1}))
encodes the atomic plan
s_{t}
and memory context
\mathcal{M}_{t-1}
at the
t
-th step.

•

Action Space (
\mathbf{A}
):
The action
\mathbf{a}_{\tau}
corresponds to the World Model’s prediction at step
\tau
. For a Rectified Flow model,
\mathbf{a}_{\tau}=\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau})
predicts the velocity field
\mathbf{u}_{\tau}
, transitioning the state from
\mathbf{z}_{\tau}
to
\mathbf{z}_{\tau-1}
.

•

Transition Probability (
P
):
The transition
P(\mathbf{x}_{\tau-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{\tau},\mathbf{a}_{\tau})
is governed by the sampling solver (defined via SDEs below).

•

Reward Function (
R
):
We employ a sparse reward structure where signals are provided only at the terminal state
\tau=0
(the clean video
v_{t}
). The reward is determined by the CriticAgent
\mathcal{C}
:

R(\mathbf{x}_{\tau})=\begin{cases}\mathcal{C}(v_{t},s_{t})&\text{if }\tau=0\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(6)

Stochastic Exploration via SDEs.

Standard sampling in Rectified Flow models follows an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE):
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{u}_{t}\mathrm{d}\tau
. However, GRPO requires stochastic exploration to generate diverse trajectories
\{v_{t,i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
for advantage estimation. To enable this, we follow DanceGRPO
(
40
)
to adopt a reverse-time
Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE)
formulation.

Inspired by recent stochastic sampling theories, we introduce a diffusion term into the flow matching process. The reverse SDE for generation is given by:

\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}_{\tau}=\underbrace{\left(\mathbf{u}_{\tau}(\mathbf{z}_{\tau})-\frac{1}{2}\eta_{\tau}^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\log p_{\tau}(\mathbf{z}_{\tau})\right)}_{\text{Drift Term}}\mathrm{d}\tau+\underbrace{\eta_{\tau}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}}_{\text{Diffusion Term}}

(7)

where
\mathbf{u}_{\tau}
is the velocity predicted by
\pi_{\theta}
,
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}
denotes a standard Wiener process (Brownian motion), and
\eta_{\tau}
is a time-dependent scalar controlling the stochasticity. The score term
\nabla\log p_{\tau}(\mathbf{z}_{\tau})
guides the sample towards the data distribution, derived analytically from the Gaussian assumption
p_{\tau}(\mathbf{z}_{\tau})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{z}_{\tau}\mid\alpha_{\tau}\mathbf{x},\sigma_{\tau}^{2}\mathbf{I})
as:

\nabla\log p_{\tau}(\mathbf{z}_{\tau})=-\frac{\mathbf{z}_{\tau}-\alpha_{\tau}\mathbf{x}_{\text{pred}}}{\sigma_{\tau}^{2}}

(8)

By solving Eq. (
7
) numerically, we can sample a group of diverse candidates conditioned on the exact same plan, satisfying the exploration requirement for RL.

Shared Noise Initialization.

A critical implementation detail is the initialization of the starting noise
\mathbf{z}_{K}
. As demonstrated by
(
40
)
, using independent noise for each group member leads to
reward hacking
, where the model overfits to specific noise artifacts.
Therefore, we enforce a
Shared Noise
strategy:

\forall i\in\{1,\dots,G\}:\quad\mathbf{z}_{K,i}=\mathbf{z}_{\text{shared}},\quad\text{where }\mathbf{z}_{\text{shared}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})

(9)

This ensures that the variance in rewards
r_{i}
arises solely from the stochastic sampling path (Eq. (
7
)) rather than initial conditions, stabilizing the gradient estimation.

Objective Function.

We optimize the World Model
\pi_{\theta}
using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). For a plan
s_{t}
, we sample a group of
G
trajectories
\{v_{t,i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
using the old policy
\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
. The advantages are computed via group-wise normalization:

A_{i}=\frac{r_{i}-\mu_{G}}{\sigma_{G}+\delta},\quad\text{where }\mu_{G}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}r_{j},\quad\sigma_{G}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}(r_{j}-\mu_{G})^{2}}

(10)

The final objective function maximizes the surrogate gain with importance sampling clipping and KL-regularization:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{t}\sim\pi_{\text{plan}}(\cdot\mid g)\\
v_{t,i}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot\mid s_{t})\end{subarray}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\Big(\rho_{t,i}A_{i},\mathrm{clip}(\rho_{t,i},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\,A_{i}\Big)-\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{wm}}\right)\Bigg]

(11)

where
\rho_{t,i}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v_{t,i}\mid s_{t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(v_{t,i}\mid s_{t})}
is the importance sampling ratio. The KL term
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\mathrm{wm}})
ensures the policy does not deviate excessively from the initial supervised World Model
\pi_{\mathrm{wm}}
.

Appendix B
Evaluation Details for Action Quality

Single-turn, direct QA evaluations often lack reasoning depth and fail to scrutinize fine-grained visual details. To address these limitations, we design a multi-agent evaluation system and adopt the Chain-of-Query mechanism
(
16
)
to capture the nuanced alignment between generated videos and text instructions. Specifically, we employ a four-stage pipeline to assess the action quality between generated videos and text prompts. Initially, the video description agent (VLM) processes video frames alongside action decomposition data (action steps and post-conditions) to generate a chronological description, verifying alignment with action steps and checking post-condition satisfaction. Subsequently, based on video descriptions, two specialized assistant agents (LLMs) raise queries based on distinct evaluation dimensions: one verifies whether all action steps are executed and post-conditions met to identify omissions, while the other scrutinizes object interaction realism, transition smoothness, and physical fidelity. Following this, the answer agent (VLM) reviews the video to address the synthesized chain of queries from both assistant agents; it performs rigorous verification to tabulate the completion status for each action step, analyzes overall flow coherence, and provides detailed answers to the inquiries. Finally, the score agent (VLM) computes four quantitative metrics, i.e., Action Completeness, Object Interaction, Action Smoothness, and Physical Fidelity, to produce a complete report, thereby ensuring comprehensive and accurate evaluation through multi-agent collaboration and cross-verified chain-of-query reasoning. We elaborate on the detailed definition of these quantitative metrics below:

Action Completeness.
Measures the proportion of action steps actually completed in the video. An action step is considered complete only if it is executed and all post-conditions are met. This metric reflects whether the video fully presents all action steps required by the prompt.

Motion Smoothness.
Evaluates the coherence and fluidity of action sequences, scored on a scale of 1-5. This metric focuses on the quality of transitions between different action steps, the presence of abrupt cuts or jumps, the continuity of motion, and inter-frame consistency.

Object Interaction.
Assesses the correctness and realism of object/tool usage in the video, scored on a scale of 1-5 (or N/A for cases without object interaction). Evaluation criteria include the method of grasping/holding, contact quality, accuracy of usage, and the plausibility of the object’s physical behavior.

Physical Fidelity.
Evaluates whether actions adhere to real-world physical laws and natural motion patterns, scored on a scale of 1-5. It scrutinizes the realism of body mechanics, the accuracy of gravity and physics, the plausibility of object deformations, and the presence of artifacts or clipping.

Appendix D
Limitation

While
SPIRAL
demonstrates strong performance in long-horizon action-conditioned video generation, it still has several limitations.
First, the framework relies on a closed-loop think–act–reflect process with explicit planning and verification, which introduces additional system complexity and may increase inference latency compared to one-shot generation paradigms.
A promising direction for future work is to further amortize planning and verification into the world model itself, reducing the need for repeated external reasoning during generation. Second,
SPIRAL
depends on learned PlanAgent and CriticAgent models to decompose actions and assess execution quality.
Although effective in practice, errors or ambiguities in planning or critique may propagate across steps in long-horizon generation, suggesting opportunities to improve robustness through stronger joint training or more compact feedback representations. Third, the current action formulation focuses on sequential, high-level semantic actions and does not explicitly model fine-grained control signals or tightly coupled physical interactions.
Extending the framework to support richer action representations or tighter integration with low-level motion modeling remains an open direction.
