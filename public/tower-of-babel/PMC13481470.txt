Multi-omics Integration of Single-cell and Spatial Transcriptomics with Tissue and Urine Proteomics Identifies TRIM28 as a Translationally Relevant Candidate Regulator in Acute Kidney Injury
Authors: Kangyu Wang, Hao Wang, Yalong Zhang, Zijian Zhang, Changhong Xu, Hong Zhao, Jiaxuan Ma, Jiangwei Man, Li Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute kidney injury (AKI) lacks disease-modifying therapies, partly because cell type–specific injury programs remain incompletely resolved and tissue mechanisms are not readily translated into clinically accessible biomarkers. We constructed a cross-scale, multi-omics atlas to prioritize candidate regulator linking renal injury circuitry with urine-detectable signals. Human single-cell, bulk, and spatial transcriptomics were integrated with mouse renal ischemia–reperfusion injury (RIRI) kidney proteomics and clinical urine proteomics. Overlap of differentially expressed genes and differentially abundant proteins nominated candidates, which were ranked by a random forest model. Immune remodeling was assessed by deconvolution and pathway enrichment, with spatial localization supported by reference-based deconvolution. Therapeutic tractability was explored by structure-based virtual screening, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA). TRIM28 was evaluated in HK-2 hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) and mouse RIRI models using genetic perturbation, expression validation, and in vitro pharmacologic evaluation. Integration converged on five genes (TRIM28, HNRNPH1, ARHGEF10L, C1RL, and UCHL3), with TRIM28 showing the highest feature importance and links to inflammatory, immune, metabolic, and proliferative programs. AKI exhibited intensified intercellular communication and an innate-skewed immune landscape. TRIM28 was robustly upregulated in HK-2 H/R and mouse RIRI kidneys and was also directly detected in the human urine proteome. In HK-2 cells, TRIM28 knockdown dampened, whereas overexpression amplified, IL-17–linked inflammatory signaling and apoptotic responses. Docking-prioritized HY-N10592 improved viability, reduced H/R-associated TRIM28 induction, IL-17–linked output, and apoptotic marker activation, and showed CETSA-supported cellular engagement of TRIM28. This integrative framework prioritizes TRIM28 as a candidate regulator linking tubular injury mechanisms with clinically relevant urinary signals and nominates HY-N10592 as a candidate chemical tool with CETSA-supported cellular target engagement for further mechanistic and in vivo evaluation in AKI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-026-02521-7.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a clinical syndrome defined by an abrupt decline in renal function over a short time frame, resulting in impaired clearance of metabolic waste and disruption of fluid, electrolyte, and acid–base homeostasis [1]. AKI remains a common and devastating complication in hospitalized and critically ill patients worldwide, imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems and long-term patient outcomes [2]. Etiologically, AKI arises from diverse insults, including renal ischemia–reperfusion injury, nephrotoxic exposures, urinary tract obstruction, and systemic inflammatory responses [3]. These triggers initiate a cascade of pathological alterations—tubular epithelial injury, interstitial edema, microvascular endothelial dysfunction, and immune-cell infiltration—that converge on compromised renal perfusion and filtration, ultimately manifesting as a rapid decline in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) [4, 5]. Epidemiological studies indicate that AKI affects all age groups, yet its incidence and severity are disproportionately higher in older individuals, patients with chronic comorbidities, and those admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) [6]. Given its rapid progression and narrow therapeutic window, early recognition and timely intervention are critical; conversely, delayed diagnosis or inadequate management increases the risk of persistent renal dysfunction and accelerates progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [7].

Current AKI management is largely supportive and etiology-directed. Supportive care focuses on optimizing volume status, maintaining hemodynamic stability, and correcting electrolyte and acid–base disturbances, accompanied by appropriate nutritional support [8]. Etiologic interventions aim to identify and eliminate precipitating factors, such as relieving urinary tract obstruction, restoring renal perfusion, and discontinuing or substituting nephrotoxic agents [9]. In critically ill patients with refractory volume overload, severe metabolic derangements, or uremia-related complications, renal replacement therapy (RRT)—including intermittent hemodialysis and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT)—is frequently required to stabilize the internal milieu and support recovery of kidney function [10]. Increasing attention has also been directed toward post-AKI care, including longitudinal assessment of renal recovery, mitigation of CKD transition, and management of associated complications such as hypertension, anemia, and elevated cardiovascular risk [11]. Nevertheless, despite advances in monitoring and supportive strategies, AKI incidence and mortality remain unacceptably high, underscoring the urgent need to elucidate its molecular pathophysiology and to develop clinically actionable biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

GSEA analysis highlighted the enrichment of the IL-17 signaling pathway in response to changes in TRIM28 expression. This observation prompted us to examine IL-17-associated inflammatory outputs in the HK-2 cell model under H/R conditions. In this context, IL-17 immunoreactivity was used as an inflammation-linked readout informed by transcriptomic pathway enrichment, rather than as a definitive surrogate for full canonical IL-17 receptor pathway activation in tubular epithelial cells. Our findings show that perturbing TRIM28 expression bidirectionally modulates IL-17 immunoreactivity, supporting a potential role for TRIM28 in amplifying IL-17-linked inflammation under ischemic stress. However, many prior AKI studies have been confined to single-omics layers or bulk tissue measurements, which can be confounded by shifts in cellular composition and often fail to resolve cell type–specific injury states. Moreover, mechanistic insights derived from tissue-based analyses do not necessarily translate into clinically accessible biomarkers, and the correspondence between tissue-level molecular perturbations and urinary-detectable signatures remains insufficiently defined.

The advent of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) provides unprecedented resolution to characterize cell type–specific dynamics during AKI progression and repair, uncover rare or transitional cellular populations, and delineate rewired intercellular communication networks [12]. When combined with bulk transcriptomic profiling, scRNA-seq enables cross-scale inference that bridges tissue-level signals to cellular programs, thereby refining the identification of key transcriptional regulators and molecular networks that drive AKI [13]. Importantly, integrating transcriptomic discoveries with orthogonal omics layers—particularly proteomics—can strengthen causal plausibility and improve robustness by validating convergent RNA–protein signals [14].

Building on these advances, we integrated publicly available scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq datasets with in-house proteomic profiling of kidney tissues from a murine renal ischemia–reperfusion injury model, together with urinary omics data from clinical samples. This multi-layered framework was developed to delineate cell type–specific injury and repair programs, validate key pathways through concordant transcriptomic and proteomic signals, and prioritize urinary-detectable candidates with translational relevance. By mapping transcriptional signatures and pathway perturbations in key cellular subpopulations alongside dynamic protein changes in experimental kidneys and molecular features measurable in human urine, we aim to delineate core regulatory circuits governing tubular injury and regeneration and to nominate candidate biomarkers that bridge mechanistic discovery and clinical utility. Collectively, this integrated “mechanistic discovery–clinical validation” strategy provides a systems-level perspective on AKI pathophysiology and establishes a data-driven foundation for earlier diagnosis and precision therapeutic intervention.

Materials and Methods

Data Acquisition

Gene expression datasets were obtained from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) public repository. The AKI-related scRNA-seq dataset GSE210622 was downloaded, including 6 control samples and 8 AKI samples. The bulk transcriptomic dataset GSE232404 was downloaded, comprising 5 control samples and 5 AKI samples. In addition, the spatial transcriptomic dataset GSE226533, which contains two AKI kidney tissue samples, was retrieved. Raw data (including count matrices and accompanying annotations) were collected and subsequently processed for scRNA-seq, bulk RNA-seq, and spatial transcriptomic analyses, respectively.

Quality Control of scRNA-seq Data

Three standard QC metrics were used, including the number of detected genes (nFeature_RNA), total UMI counts (nCount_RNA), and the fraction of mitochondrial reads (percent.mt). Cells were kept for downstream analyses only when they satisfied the following thresholds: nFeature_RNA between 500 and 6,000, nCount_RNA between 1,000 and 50,000, and percent.mt < 15% [15].

Doublets were detected using DoubletFinder (v2.0.4), with the optimal pK value selected via paramSweep and find.pK, and an expected doublet rate of 5%. Predicted doublets were excluded before downstream analyses. Because several omics components of this study were exploratory and constrained by public-data availability or available clinical samples, formal prospective power calculations were not performed for all datasets. Accordingly, the integrative conclusions were based on cross-layer convergence across independent data sources and orthogonal validation rather than on any single dataset alone.

Dimensionality Reduction, Clustering, and Cell-type Annotation

The quality-controlled scRNA-seq expression matrix was normalized using Seurat’s LogNormalize method. Cell-cycle scores were computed using CellCycleScoring, and highly variable genes were identified with FindVariableFeatures. The matrix was then scaled with ScaleData, regressing out potential confounders such as mitochondrial gene proportion and cell-cycle scores [16].

We applied PCA to the scaled dataset and retained 30 principal components for subsequent neighborhood construction and clustering. Cell communities were identified via a graph-based approach implemented with FindNeighbors and FindClusters, and the results were displayed using UMAP [17].

Cell identities were assigned using canonical marker genes from the CellMarker and PanglaoDB databases, with SingleR used as an auxiliary annotation tool. Final cell-type labels were determined by consensus between marker-based and algorithm-assisted annotation [18].

Cell–cell Communication Analysis

CellChat was used to model cell–cell signaling from normalized scRNA-seq data and annotated cell identities [19]. Based on curated ligand–receptor pairs, communication probabilities were estimated and network-level features (information flow, strength, and frequency) were quantified. Network comparisons between control and AKI groups were then conducted to highlight pathways and cellular sources/targets driving disease-related rewiring.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis and Integrative Scoring

To assess changes in immune, inflammation, and metabolism-related pathways across cell subpopulations, gene set enrichment analysis was performed using the irGSEA package. AUCell, UCell, singscore, and ssGSEA were applied to each cell subset to compute enrichment scores for predefined gene sets from the MSigDB database [20]. To enhance robustness, results from the four algorithms were integrated using Robust Rank Aggregation (RRA), and the derived rankings and P values were used as composite indicators of pathway activity. The most significantly enriched gene sets for each cell subpopulation were retained for downstream interpretation.

Machine Learning Analysis

To identify key genes closely associated with AKI, a random forest classifier was constructed using the randomForest package. The normalized gene expression matrix was used as input features, and sample labels (AKI vs. control) were used as outcomes. The model was built with 1,000 trees, and performance was evaluated using cross-validation. Feature importance was assessed using the built-in %IncMSE metric, and the top-ranked genes contributing most to classification were retained as candidate markers [21]. These candidates were further prioritized based on biological relevance and pathway-level evidence from downstream analyses.

Immune Infiltration Analysis

Immune cell composition was estimated using CIBERSORT with the LM22 leukocyte signature matrix to infer the relative proportions of 22 immune cell subsets from the transcriptomic profiles of GSE232404. Deconvolution results were subjected to statistical quality assessment, and qualified samples were retained for downstream analyses [22]. Group-wise comparisons of immune infiltration and correlation analyses between gene expression and immune cell proportions were subsequently performed.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

Samples in the bulk transcriptome cohort were dichotomized according to the expression of each candidate key gene (high vs. low). Pathway-level differences between groups were assessed by GSEA using annotated gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, v7.0). Enrichment statistics were derived by permutation testing, yielding normalized enrichment scores (NES) and adjusted P values. Significance was defined using an FDR threshold of < 0.25 (or < 0.05 where applicable), and significantly enriched pathways were ranked and interpreted for biological relevance [23].

Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA)

GSVA was applied to estimate pathway activity on a per-sample basis using MSigDB gene sets. GSVA scores were computed with the GSVA R package and compared between AKI and control groups to identify differentially activated pathways.

Spatial Transcriptomic Data Analysis

Spatial transcriptomic data from GSE226533 were processed using the Seurat spatial workflow. Raw UMI count matrices, tissue images, spot coordinates, and scale factors were imported into Seurat. Normalization and variance stabilization were performed using SCTransform, followed by PCA based on highly variable genes [24, 25]. A selected number of principal components were used for nonlinear dimensionality reduction with UMAP and Louvain clustering to identify spatially distinct expression patterns. Clustering results were interpreted in conjunction with histological images and compared with nephron structures (e.g., glomeruli and proximal tubules) to characterize spatial heterogeneity in AKI.

RCTD Deconvolution Analysis

To map single-cell transcriptional information onto spatial transcriptomic profiles, Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD) was applied to deconvolve cell-type composition within spatial transcriptomic sections. Annotated scRNA-seq data were used as a reference to construct cell–type–specific expression signatures, which were then projected onto the spatial transcriptomic dataset to estimate the relative proportions of each cell type within individual spots [26]. Posterior probabilities computed by RCTD were used to assess assignment confidence, thereby enabling reconstruction of the spatial distribution patterns of distinct cellular subpopulations across kidney tissue.

Pathway Activity Inference (PROGENy) in Bulk and Spatial Data

Pathway activity inference was performed using PROGENy (Pathway RespOnsive GENes) with the progeny package in R. PROGENy computes activity scores for canonical pathways (e.g., TNFα/NF-κB, TGF-β, PI3K,

MAPK, p53, Hypoxia, and WNT) based on weighted expression of downstream responsive genes.

For bulk RNA-seq (GSE232404), PROGENy scores were calculated from the.

normalized expression matrix and compared between the AKI and control groups. For spatial transcriptomics (GSE226533), spot-level PROGENy scores were computed from the SCTransform-normalized expression matrix (SCT assay) and visualized as spatial feature maps and pathway heatmaps. RCTD-derived cell-type proportions were visualized alongside PROGENy scores to aid interpretation of compartment-specific signaling states.

Proteomic Profiling of Mouse Kidneys Following Renal Ischemia–reperfusion Injury

Animals and RIRI Model

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–25 g) were purchased from the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with free access to food and water. Mice were assigned to a sham group (n = 3) or a renal ischemia–reperfusion injury (I/R) group (n = 3). The renal I/R model was generated as previously described. Briefly, animals were anesthetized with isoflurane and body temperature was maintained at 37 °C using a heating pad throughout the procedure. Both kidneys were approached dorsally, and bilateral renal pedicles were clamped with microvascular clips for 45 min to induce ischemia, followed by clip removal to initiate reperfusion. After 24 h of reperfusion, mice were euthanized and kidneys were harvested. Sham-operated mice underwent the same surgical steps except for pedicle clamping. Renal cortical tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Protein Extraction, Digestion, and LC–MS/MS Analysis

Approximately 50 mg of renal cortical tissue was ground in liquid nitrogen and lysed in urea-based lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. The lysates were sonicated on ice and centrifuged to remove debris, and the supernatants were collected for protein quantification using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were subjected to reduction, alkylation, and overnight tryptic digestion according to the standard workflow. The resulting peptides were desalted using a Strata X SPE column and reconstituted in solvent A for LC–MS/MS analysis.

The tryptic peptides were separated on a NanoElute UHPLC system using a reversed-phase analytical column (25-cm length, 100 μm inner diameter). Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid and 2% acetonitrile in water, and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. Peptides were eluted with the following gradient: 6%–24% B from 0 to 14 min, 24%–35% B from 14 to 16 min, 35%–80% B from 16 to 18 min, and 80% B from 18 to 20 min, at a constant flow rate of 500 nL/min.

Mass spectrometric analysis was performed on a timsTOF Pro instrument operated in dia-PASEF mode. Raw data were processed using Spectronaut (v.18) with the Pulsar search engine against the Mus musculus UniProt database. Carbamidomethylation on cysteine was set as a fixed modification, whereas oxidation of methionine and acetylation of the protein N-terminus were set as variable modifications. Up to two missed cleavages were allowed. The false discovery rate was controlled at < 1% at the protein, peptide, and PSM levels. Differentially abundant proteins were identified based on |log2 fold change| > 1 and FDR < 0.05. Functional enrichment analyses and protein–protein interaction network construction were subsequently performed using clusterProfiler and STRING, respectively [27].

Proteomic Profiling of Urine Samples From Patients with AKI

Clinical Sample Collection

A total of 20 healthy volunteers (control group) and 20 patients diagnosed with AKI (AKI group) were enrolled. AKI was defined according to the KDIGO 2012 criteria (an increase in serum creatinine by ≥ 0.3 mg/dL within 48 h or ≥ 50% from baseline). Midstream morning urine (10–20 mL) was collected from each participant. Samples were centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles. The studies involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Lanzhou University Second Hospital (Approval No. 2024 A-445). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before urine collection. Sample Preparation and Proteomic Analysis

Urine samples were thawed on ice and centrifuged to remove residual debris. When necessary, urinary proteins were concentrated prior to digestion. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of urinary protein were subjected to reduction, alkylation, and overnight tryptic digestion according to the standard workflow. The resulting peptides were desalted using a Strata X SPE column and reconstituted in solvent A for LC–MS/MS analysis.

The tryptic peptides were separated on a NanoElute UHPLC system using a reversed-phase analytical column (25-cm length, 100 μm inner diameter). Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid and 2% acetonitrile in water, and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. Peptides were eluted with the following gradient: 6%–24% B from 0 to 14 min, 24%–35% B from 14 to 16 min, 35%–80% B from 16 to 18 min, and 80% B from 18 to 20 min, at a constant flow rate of 500 nL/min.

Mass spectrometric analysis was performed on a timsTOF Pro instrument operated in dia-PASEF mode. Raw data were processed using Spectronaut (v.18) with the Pulsar search engine against the Homo sapiens UniProt database. Carbamidomethylation on cysteine was set as a fixed modification, whereas oxidation of methionine and acetylation of the protein N-terminus were set as variable modifications. Up to two missed cleavages were allowed. The false discovery rate was controlled at < 1% at the protein, peptide, and PSM levels. Differentially abundant proteins were identified using |log2 fold change| > 0.8 and FDR < 0.05 [28].

Cross-species and Cross-layer Integrative Analysis

To link experimental findings with clinically relevant biomarkers, integrative analyses were performed between the mouse kidney tissue proteome and the human urine proteome. Differentially abundant mouse proteins were mapped to human orthologs using biomaRt and the Ensembl database. Cross-species pathway alignment was conducted using KEGG Orthology and Reactome annotations to identify jointly enriched pathways (Fisher’s exact test, FDR < 0.05).

A three-layer “tissue–urine–pathway” network was constructed using STRING and Cytoscape based on differentially abundant proteins and their pathway relationships. Molecules upregulated in both mouse kidney tissue and human urine were defined as candidate translational biomarkers. Spearman correlation analyses were performed between these biomarkers and clinical indicators of kidney injury (serum creatinine), prioritizing key factors linked to injury severity [29].

Small-molecule Screening and Molecular Docking Analysis

Structure-based virtual screening and molecular docking were performed using two compound libraries from MedChemExpress: the HYL001P Bioactive Compound Library (~ 27,000 compounds) and the HY-L032V Fragment Library (~ 23,000 compounds). The crystal structure of human TRIM28/TIF1B was used as the receptor. Protein preparation, including hydrogen addition, removal of crystallographic water molecules, and energy minimization, was performed using the Schrödinger Protein Preparation Wizard, and a docking grid was generated around the predicted binding pocket of TRIM28.

All compounds were prepared using the Schrödinger LigPrep module for three-dimensional structure generation and energy minimization. Molecular docking was carried out using Schrödinger Glide with a hierarchical workflow (HTVS, SP, and XP). Based on docking scores, the top 200 candidates from each library were retained for further evaluation. The binding modes of high-scoring compounds were visually inspected in Schrödinger Maestro.

Among the screened compounds, HY-N10592 (Herbacetin-3-sophoroside-8-glucoside) from the HYL001P library showed the best predicted binding affinity, with a docking score of − 14.557, and was therefore selected for subsequent molecular dynamics simulation and experimental validation.

Molecular Dynamics Simulation

Molecular dynamics simulation was performed using GROMACS 2022.3 to further assess the conformational stability and interaction dynamics of the HY-N10592–TRIM28 complex. The docked complex was used as the starting structure. The protein was described using the amber99sb-ildn force field, and ligand parameters were generated using AmberTools22 with the GAFF force field and Gaussian 16 W. The system was solvated in a cubic box with the TIP3P water model, and Na+ ions were added to neutralize the system.

After energy minimization, the system was equilibrated under the NVT and NPT ensembles for 100,000 steps each at 300 K and 1 bar. A 100 ns production simulation was then carried out with a time step of 2 fs. Trajectory analysis included RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, SASA, intermolecular hydrogen bonds, free energy landscape, and MM/GBSA binding free energy calculations to evaluate the stability and interaction behavior of the HY-N10592–TRIM28 complex.

Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA)

A cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) was performed to evaluate the cellular engagement of HY-N10592 with TRIM28. HK-2 cells were treated with 20 µM HY-N10592 or an equal volume of DMSO for 6 h, followed by H/R treatment. Cells were then collected, resuspended in PBS, aliquoted into PCR tubes, and heated at the indicated temperatures for 3 min. After cooling to room temperature, the samples were lysed by two freeze–thaw cycles and centrifuged at 17,000 × g for 20 min. The resulting supernatants were collected for Western blot analysis. Soluble TRIM28 levels at each temperature point were detected by immunoblotting to compare the thermal stability profile of TRIM28 between the DMSO- and HY-N10592-treated groups.

The following reagents were purchased from Servicebio (Wuhan, China): DAPI nuclear staining solution (cat. no. G1012), hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining reagents (cat. no. G1076), and periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining kit (cat. no. G1008). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) detection was performed using a commercially available kit (Bioss, Beijing, China; cat. no. PV-0023). HY-N10592 (MedChemExpress; cat. no. HY-N10592) was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a stock solution and diluted in culture medium to the indicated final concentrations (20 µM unless otherwise specified). N-acetylcysteine (NAC; Sigma; cat. no. A0150000) was dissolved in sterile water and used at a final concentration of 5 mM. For cell-based assays, the final DMSO concentration was kept identical across groups (≤ 0.1%, v/v).

Cell Culture, H/R Model, and Experimental Grouping

HK-2 human proximal tubular epithelial cells (Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank) were cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂. Cells were used for subsequent experiments when they reached approximately 70–80% confluence.

To mimic ischemia–reperfusion–like injury, an H/R model was established. Briefly, during hypoxia, cells were switched to glucose-free, serum-free medium and cultured under hypoxic conditions (1% O₂, 5% CO₂, 94% N₂), followed by replacement with complete medium and reoxygenation under normoxia. The specific procedures for H/R induction were performed as previously described [30].

To interrogate the functional role of TRIM28, stable TRIM28 knockdown and overexpression in HK-2 cells were generated and analyzed under normoxia and H/R conditions. The knockdown experiment included four groups: HK2-Vector, TRIM28-Sh3, HK2-Vector-HR, and TRIM28-Sh3-HR. The overexpression experiment included HK2-Vector, TRIM28-OE, HK2-Vector-HR, and TRIM28-OE-HR, where “-HR” indicates exposure to H/R under the corresponding genetic background.

For pharmacological intervention, cells were allocated into five groups: NC, NC + HY, H/R, H/R + HY, and H/R + NAC (positive control). HY-N10592 was applied at the indicated concentrations (5–20 µM; 20 µM was used unless otherwise stated). N-acetylcysteine (NAC; final concentration: 5 mM) served as a positive control. For both HY-N10592 and NAC, drugs were added 6 h before hypoxia and maintained throughout the subsequent hypoxia and reoxygenation procedures. Vehicle controls received an equivalent volume of solvent.

Mouse Renal Ischemia–reperfusion Injury Model and Grouping

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–25 g) were obtained from the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Animals were maintained in an SPF facility with free access to food and water under controlled environmental conditions (approximately 26 °C, ~ 50% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle). Mice were fasted for 12 h prior to surgery. Anesthesia was induced with 3% isoflurane and maintained at 1%, and adequate depth of anesthesia was verified by the absence of the toe-pinch reflex.

Renal ischemia–reperfusion injury (RIRI) was established via a dorsal surgical approach by clamping both renal pedicles for 45 min, followed by 24 h of reperfusion. Throughout surgery, mice were kept on a warming pad to maintain normothermia. Animals were allocated to either a sham group or a renal ischemia–reperfusion injury (RIRI) group. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Lanzhou University Second Hospital (Approval No. D2026-003) and performed in accordance with institutional animal-care regulations. The study was conducted and reported following the ARRIVE guidelines.

H&E Staining, PAS Staining, and Immunohistochemistry

Mouse kidneys were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, cleared, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4-µm sections. Hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining was carried out following the protocol provided with the commercial reagents. Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining was performed using a ready-to-use kit to evaluate tubular histopathological alterations, particularly changes in the brush border and basement membrane.

Histopathological assessment was performed by two independent observers, with tubular injury scored semi-quantitatively based on criteria such as tubular dilation, epithelial necrosis, brush border loss, and cast formation, as described previously.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for TRIM28 expression was conducted on deparaffinized and rehydrated sections, followed by heat-induced antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase was quenched with 3% H₂O₂, and non-specific binding was blocked with 5% BSA. Sections were incubated with anti-TRIM28 primary antibody (Abcam, ab109545) overnight, followed by secondary antibody incubation and DAB visualization. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin. IHC quantification was performed using ImageJ by measuring positive staining area or integrated optical density (IOD), presented as TRIM28-positive area (%).

Quantitative Real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted with minor modifications to our previously reported protocol [30]. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol and reverse-transcribed to generate cDNA. PCR amplification was performed on a real-time PCR instrument using a SYBR Green–based detection system. β-actin served as the housekeeping control, and relative transcript abundance was determined using the 2^−ΔΔCt approach. Primer sequences are provided in Table S3.

Western Blotting

Cells were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk or BSA as appropriate, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TRIM28, IL-17, ACT1/TRAF3IP2, NF-κB p65, phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser468), Bcl-2, Bax, cleaved caspase-3, and β-actin, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system and captured for analysis.

Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ and normalized to β-actin as the loading control. For phosphorylated proteins, phospho-signal changes were interpreted together with the corresponding total protein levels. All experiments were repeated independently at least three times. For proteins with similar apparent molecular weights, immunoblotting was performed on parallel membranes prepared from the same lysates to avoid signal overlap and ensure unambiguous band assignment. Band identity was verified according to the expected molecular weight and antibody specificity before densitometric quantification.

Cell Viability Assay (CCK-8)

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Briefly, HK-2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 1 × 10^4 cells per well and pretreated with HY-N10592 (5–20 µM) for 6 h, followed by hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R). After reoxygenation, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Immunofluorescence Staining

HK-2 cells on glass-bottom dishes were fixed (4% paraformaldehyde), permeabilized (1% Triton X-100), and blocked (5% BSA) after H/R. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and then with fluorescent secondary antibodies in the dark, with DAPI counterstaining when indicated. Confocal microscopy was used for imaging.

Statistical Analysis

Unless otherwise specified, all statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.2.2). Differential expression in scRNA-seq data was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in Seurat or the MAST model as appropriate. Differential analyses for bulk transcriptomic and proteomic datasets were conducted using limma, edgeR, or DESeq2, with the choice of method determined by data characteristics and distributional assumptions. Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, and significance was generally defined as FDR < 0.05. Correlation analyses were performed using Spearman’s or Pearson’s correlation coefficients as indicated, and two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Cohort

A total of 20 healthy volunteers and 20 patients with AKI were included in the urine proteomics cohort. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Briefly, age, sex distribution, and anthropometric indices (height, weight, and BMI) were comparable between groups. In line with the diagnostic definition, AKI patients exhibited markedly elevated serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen. In addition, AKI patients showed higher uric acid and fasting blood glucose levels and lower serum albumin. NT-proBNP was available in a subset of participants and showed an increasing trend in the AKI group.

Data Quality Control and Integration Yielded a High-quality Single-cell Atlas

To construct a reliable single-cell transcriptomic dataset for downstream analyses, we performed standardized quality control across all samples based on raw UMI count matrices. After excluding low-quality cells based on QC thresholds (low gene detection/UMI counts and high mitochondrial transcript proportion) and further removing predicted doublets, we retained 112,912 high-quality cells for subsequent.

analyses. The distributions of nCount_RNA, nFeature_RNA, and percent.mt before and after filtering showed improved uniformity across samples, supporting effective removal of low-quality profiles (Supplementary Fig. S1A–B).

We next identified 2,000 highly variable genes as informative features for dimensionality reduction (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Principal component analysis captured the major sources of transcriptomic variation, with the elbow plot indicating that the leading components explained most of the variance (Supplementary Fig. S1D). To further mitigate inter-sample batch effects, we applied Harmony-based.

integration on the PCA space. Visualization of embeddings before and after correction demonstrated markedly improved cross-sample mixing (Supplementary Fig. S1E–F), indicating successful batch-effect reduction and establishing a robust basis for clustering and downstream biological interpretation.

Cell–type–specific Functional Programs and Intensified Intercellular Signaling Characterize AKI

To define the functional specialization of each renal cell population under injury, we assessed pathway enrichment based on cell–type–specific differential expression and summarized the major functional modules (Fig. 1E). Clear compartment-specific signatures were observed. PCT cells were primarily associated with transport-related programs (e.g., vitamin and vascular transport), LOH cells showed preferential enrichment of pathways linked to ion transport and tubular acid–base regulation, and podocytes were strongly associated with processes involved in glomerular development and filtration barrier organization. Collectively, these results indicate that AKI-related transcriptional remodeling is organized by nephron compartment, spanning transport dysfunction, filtration-barrier impairment, and structural remodeling.

Given that AKI is a multicellular process, we next compared cell–cell communication landscapes between control and AKI conditions using inferred ligand–receptor interactions. Compared with controls, AKI samples exhibited both a higher number of detected communications and increased average interaction strength (Fig. 2A–B), suggesting a global amplification of intercellular signaling during injury. Network summaries further highlighted particularly prominent interactions involving PCT and LOH cells (Fig. 2C). In addition, PCT-to-ENDO and PCT-to-LEUK communication was strengthened in AKI, consistent with enhanced epithelial–vascular and epithelial–immune crosstalk accompanying proximal tubular injury.

Cross-platform Integration and Machine-learning Prioritization Identify TRIM28 as a Key AKI-associated Gene

To identify robust AKI-associated candidates supported across multiple molecular layers, we first examined transcriptional alterations in progenitor-like cells by comparing AKI and control conditions, which yielded 3,596 differentially expressed genes (Fig. 3A). In parallel, differential expression analysis of the bulk transcriptomic dataset GSE232404 identified 6,045 DEGs, capturing consistent global expression shifts between groups (Fig. 3B–C). To enhance biological confidence and reduce platform-specific noise, we next integrated evidence across transcriptomic and proteomic layers by intersecting DEGs from the single-cell and bulk analyses with differentially abundant proteins from two independent proteomic resources (mouse kidney tissue proteome, pro1; human urine proteome, pro2). This cross-layer strategy converged on 130 candidate genes showing concordant alterations at both RNA and protein levels (Fig. 3D), nominating a refined set of high-confidence AKI-associated molecules. We then applied a random forest model to prioritize candidates by feature importance. This analysis highlighted five top-ranked genes—TRIM28, HNRNPH1, ARHGEF10L, C1RL, and UCHL3—as the most informative features associated with AKI (Fig. 3F). Among them, TRIM28 exhibited the highest importance score, supporting its selection for subsequent functional validation and small-molecule screening. Importantly, TRIM28 was not prioritized on the basis of transcriptomic evidence alone; it was also directly detected in the human urine proteome, where it showed significant differential abundance between AKI and control samples (Fig. 3E), thereby strengthening its positioning within the tissue–urine translational framework.

These findings are consistent with an AKI immune landscape characterized by myeloid-skewed inflammatory activation accompanied by relative depletion or suppression of selected lymphocyte compartments.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis Links Key Genes to Inflammatory, Immune, Metabolic, and Proliferative Programs in AKI

To gain mechanistic insight into the biological processes associated with the prioritized genes, we stratified samples into high- and low-expression groups for each gene and performed GSEA (Fig. 6A–E). TRIM28-high samples showed significant enrichment of pathways related to DNA replication, IL-17 signaling, and complement and coagulation cascades (Fig. 6A), suggesting that elevated TRIM28 is coupled to proliferative/replication programs and activation of inflammatory and complement–coagulation responses in AKI. HNRNPH1-high samples were enriched for T cell receptor signaling, PPAR signaling, and IL-17 signaling (Fig. 6B), implicating this gene in transcriptional states linked to T-cell activation alongside metabolic regulation. For ARHGEF10L, high expression was associated with enrichment of Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, RNA polymerase, and NOD-like receptor signaling (Fig. 6C), consistent with connections to innate immune sensing and helper T-cell–related immune programs. C1RL-high samples were characterized by enrichment of PPAR signaling, HIF-1 signaling, and NOD-like receptor signaling (Fig. 6D), aligning with metabolic adaptation, hypoxia-related responses, and inflammatory signaling. UCHL3-high samples were enriched for Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, Rap1 signaling, and Relaxin signaling (Fig. 6E), suggesting potential involvement in immune differentiation programs and signaling pathways relevant to vascular or stress responsiveness.

Overall, these GSEA results provide pathway-level support that the five key genes converge on core AKI-related programs, including inflammation/immune regulation, metabolic remodeling, and proliferative or stress-response states, reinforcing their potential functional relevance in disease progression.

GSVA Further Delineates Hallmark-level Functional Phenotypes Linked to Key Genes

To complement GSEA and quantify pathway activity at the Hallmark level, we performed GSVA to evaluate functional states associated with high versus low expression of each prioritized gene (Fig. 6F–J). Consistent with its GSEA profile, TRIM28-high samples exhibited prominent activation of cell-cycle and proliferative programs, particularly MYC_TARGETS_V2 and E2F_TARGETS (Fig. 6F), supporting a link between TRIM28 and replication/proliferation-associated transcriptional activity in AKI. HNRNPH1-high samples were strongly associated with INTERFERON_ALPHA_RESPONSE and MYC_TARGETS_V2 (Fig. 6G), suggesting concomitant engagement of interferon-related signaling and growth-related transcriptional programs. A similar pattern was observed for ARHGEF10L (Fig. 6H) and UCHL3 (Fig. 6J), each showing positive associations with INTERFERON_ALPHA_RESPONSE and MYC_TARGETS_V2, further connecting these genes to interferon-driven states alongside proliferative remodeling.

In contrast, C1RL-high samples were characterized by enrichment of IL6_JAK_STAT3_SIGNALING and the REACTIVE_OXYGEN_SPECIES_PATHWAY (Fig. 6I), implicating C1RL in inflammatory amplification via the JAK/STAT3 axis and oxidative stress–associated injury responses. Overall, GSVA results were concordant with GSEA and collectively indicate that these five genes map onto core AKI-relevant functional programs, including proliferative remodeling, interferon/inflammation signaling, and oxidative stress responses, providing coherent pathway-level support for downstream mechanistic and therapeutic interrogation.

Key Genes Align With Established AKI Markers and Position TRIM28 Within a Disease-relevant Regulatory Network

To relate the five prioritized genes to known AKI biology, we curated 20 AKI-relevant genes from GeneCards that were robustly expressed in our dataset and compared their expression between control and AKI samples (Fig. 7A). Multiple established markers showed significant differential expression, including PKD1, PKD2, CEBPA, TP53, RUNX1, FLT3, ACE, and NPM1, supporting their involvement in AKI-associated transcriptional remodeling in our cohort. We next constructed a correlation matrix between the five key genes (TRIM28, HNRNPH1, ARHGEF10L, C1RL, UCHL3) and the curated AKI marker set (Fig. 7B). TRIM28 exhibited broad, significant correlations with numerous AKI-associated genes, whereas the remaining key genes displayed more modular, marker-cluster-specific correlation patterns. This structure suggests that the five candidates may map to distinct functional nodes within the AKI regulatory landscape, with TRIM28 occupying a comparatively central position. Given its top-ranked importance in the machine-learning prioritization, we further examined TRIM28 at the single-gene level. TRIM28 expression was negatively correlated with CEBPA (r=-0.787, P = 0.0069; Fig. 7C) and showed a strong positive correlation with PKD2 (r = 0.965, P = 6.3 × 10 − 9; Fig. 7D). Together, these associations suggest that TRIM28 is embedded within established AKI marker-linked programs, potentially aligning with PKD2-associated injury/repair signaling while exhibiting an opposing relationship with CEBPA-related regulatory states that may influence inflammatory balance and cell-fate decisions during AKI.

Single-cell Pathway Activity Profiling Links Key Genes to Immune, Metabolic, and Proliferative Programs and Supports the Pharmacologic Tractability of TRIM28

To systematically relate the five key genes to immune-, metabolic-, and signaling-associated functional states, we applied AUCell to quantify Hallmark and KEGG gene set activity at single-cell resolution and summarized these relationships in a gene–pathway bubble plot (Fig. 8D). In this visualization, pathways are displayed along the x-axis and the five genes along the y-axis; bubble color represents the log-fold change of pathway activity, bubble size reflects FDR, and an annotation bar categorizes pathways into immune, metabolism, signaling, and proliferation modules.

Across genes, we observed broadly elevated associations with multiple AKI-relevant programs, with particularly strong signals in immune/inflammatory and growth-related pathways, including allograft rejection, IL6_JAK_STAT3_SIGNALING, INTERFERON_ALPHA_RESPONSE, oxidative phosphorylation, NOTCH signaling, and MYC targets. These convergent patterns suggest that TRIM28, HNRNPH1, ARHGEF10L, C1RL, and UCHL3 are embedded in coordinated transcriptional remodeling that integrates cytokine and interferon signaling, metabolic rewiring, and cell-cycle/proliferative regulation within the AKI microenvironment.

Importantly, when these pathway-level associations were considered alongside our structure-based virtual screening results, TRIM28 emerged as a top-ranked candidate with potential pharmacologic tractability. Its consistent linkage to immune and proliferative programs, together with the presence of a defined small-molecule binding pocket, supports the feasibility of developing pathway–target–drug intervention strategies centered on TRIM28.

Spatial Transcriptomics Resolves Anatomically Structured Clusters and Maps Renal Cell-type Distributions by RCTD Deconvolution

To extend our single-cell findings into tissue context, we analyzed the spatial transcriptomic dataset GSE226533. Quality assessment based on UMI distributions across spots indicated relatively uniform sequencing depth between the two kidney sections, while localized UMI-enriched regions were apparent and broadly coincided with anatomical compartment boundaries (Fig. 9A). After normalization and variance stabilization with SCTransform, we performed PCA followed by UMAP embedding and Louvain clustering, which resolved five spatial clusters with distinct transcriptional profiles (Fig. 9B, left). Mapping these clusters back onto the tissue images revealed spatially coherent and anatomically interpretable patterns, including separation across cortex versus medulla and structured heterogeneity within cortical subregions (Fig. 9B, right).

We next inferred spot-level cell-type composition using RCTD (spacexr) with our annotated scRNA-seq atlas as the reference signature matrix. Deconvolution revealed pronounced spatial heterogeneity across major renal populations, including DCT, PCT, LOH, ENDO, MES, and PODO (Fig. 9C). As expected, PCT/DCT/podocyte signals predominated in cortical regions, whereas LOH, stromal populations, and subsets of immune cells were enriched toward the medulla and corticomedullary junction. These spatial distributions are concordant with our scRNA-seq annotations and provide tissue-level support for compartment-specific remodeling during AKI.

Spatial Pathway Activity Profiling and Tissue Localization of Key Regulators Reveal Compartment-specific Signaling Reprogramming

To characterize signaling states in situ, we used PROGENy to infer pathway activity in the spatial transcriptomic dataset, incorporating RCTD-estimated cell-type proportions for each spot. The resulting heatmap revealed pronounced, cell–type–dependent differences in pathway activity across the tissue (Fig. 9D). Notably, progenitor-like cells displayed markedly elevated WNT activity, consistent with engagement of regenerative and cell-state plasticity programs during AKI, potentially reflecting stem-like features, epithelial–mesenchymal transition–related processes, and injury–repair dynamics. In contrast, MES, ENDO, and LEUK populations exhibited relatively higher activity across multiple inflammation- and stress-associated pathways, whereas tubular epithelial compartments such as PCT showed comparatively attenuated activity across several pathways, underscoring functional divergence among renal compartments during injury and recovery.

We next mapped the spatial expression of the five prioritized genes to define their tissue distribution and cellular context. Spatial feature maps demonstrated non-uniform expression of TRIM28, HNRNPH1, ARHGEF10L, C1RL, and UCHL3 across both kidney sections, with discrete regions forming prominent high-expression “hotspots” (Fig. 10A–E). Because the GSE226533 dataset contained AKI samples only and did not include matched control kidneys, these spatial feature plots were intended to illustrate intra-AKI localization patterns rather than direct control-versus-AKI spatial differences. These hotspots overlapped with spatial domains enriched for PCT, MES, and progenitor-like signatures. Bubble plots further quantified the fraction of expressing cells and average expression across cell types (Fig. 10F–J), showing that TRIM28, HNRNPH1, and UCHL3 were most prominent in PCT, progenitor-like cells, and subsets of LEUK/MES, whereas C1RL and ARHGEF10L showed relatively higher expression in immune- and stromal-associated compartments.

Together, integrating PROGENy-inferred pathway activity with spatial gene expression indicates that these key regulators are concentrated in cell populations and tissue regions undergoing substantial signaling reprogramming, reinforcing their putative roles in AKI-associated microenvironment remodeling and network rewiring.

Structure-based Virtual Screening, Molecular Dynamics Simulation, and CETSA Support HY-N10592 as a Candidate TRIM28-engaging Compound

Building on our multi-omics integration and machine-learning analyses that prioritized TRIM28 as a top candidate target, we conducted structure-based virtual screening to identify small molecules with putative activity against human TIF1B/TRIM28. The TRIM28 crystal structure (PDB: 7Z36) was used as the receptor and processed using a standard protein preparation workflow, including hydrogen addition, removal of crystallographic waters, hydrogen-bond network optimization, and energy minimization. A docking grid was defined around a predicted binding pocket, with key surrounding residues including ALA218, VAL250, ALA186, and ARG38. Two MedChemExpress libraries (HYL001P Bioactive Compound Library Plus and HY-L032V Fragment Library) were then screened on the Schrödinger platform using a hierarchical docking pipeline (HTVS → SP → XP). Compounds were ranked by docking score, and the top 200 candidates from each library were retained for downstream evaluation.

Among the shortlisted hits, HY-N10592 (from the HYL001P library) exhibited the strongest predicted binding to TRIM28 (docking score = − 14.557) and adopted a pose that filled the pocket with extensive contacts. Structural visualization placed HY-N10592 within a central groove of TRIM28 (Fig. 11A), and the 2D interaction map identified multiple hydrogen-bonding and noncovalent interactions involving PHE23, GLU29, LYS84, LEU192, CYS196, GLY197, ARG200, CYS214, HIS216, and VAL250 (Fig. 11B). Surface and pocket close-up inspection further supported that HY-N10592 was accommodated within a largely hydrophobic cavity and formed a densely packed interaction interface, including multiple hydrogen bonds and a π–π stacking interaction with HIS216 (Fig. 11C), indicating a favorable docking geometry.

To further evaluate whether the predicted HY-N10592–TRIM28 complex remained stable under dynamic conditions, we performed molecular dynamics simulation. The RMSD profile indicated that the ligand-bound complex remained relatively stable throughout the simulation after initial equilibration (Fig. 11D). Residue-level fluctuation analysis showed that most regions displayed limited flexibility, with higher mobility restricted to selected segments of the protein (Fig. 11E). The Gibbs free energy landscape supported the presence of a dominant low-energy conformational basin, consistent with a comparatively stable binding state (Fig. 11F). In addition, the number of intermolecular hydrogen bonds remained sustained during the simulation (Fig. 11G), while gyration-related parameters and solvent-accessible surface area analyses were consistent with maintenance of a compact conformational state of the complex (Fig. 11H–I). Together, these molecular dynamics results support the dynamic stability of the predicted HY-N10592–TRIM28 interaction.

To move beyond in silico prioritization, we next performed a cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) in HK-2 cells to assess whether HY-N10592 engages TRIM28 in a cellular context. CETSA showed that HY-N10592 altered the thermal stability profile of TRIM28, providing cellular target-engagement support consistent with the docking and molecular dynamics results (Fig. 11J–K).

Taken together, the docking score, interaction density, molecular dynamics-supported complex stability, and CETSA-supported cellular target engagement support HY-N10592 as a candidate TRIM28-engaging compound and provide a rational basis for subsequent functional validation. However, direct binding specificity and the extent of TRIM28-dependent pharmacologic activity remain to be established, and off-target effects cannot be excluded without further orthogonal validation, including biophysical binding assays and competition-based studies.

TRIM28 is Robustly Induced in Both an HK-2 H/R Model and a Mouse RIRI Model

To experimentally validate our bioinformatics-driven prioritization, we first assessed TRIM28 expression in an HK-2 H/R model. qRT–PCR demonstrated that TRIM28 mRNA was significantly increased following H/R compared with normoxic controls (NC) (Fig. S3A). Consistently, western blotting showed elevated TRIM28 protein abundance in the H/R group (Fig. S3B), and densitometric quantification confirmed this increase (Fig. S3C). Immunofluorescence further supported these findings, showing stronger TRIM28 staining after H/R together with significantly higher quantified signal intensity (Fig. S3D–E). Collectively, these results indicate that TRIM28 is consistently upregulated under ischemia-like stress at both transcript and protein levels in vitro.

We next evaluated TRIM28 expression in vivo using a mouse RIRI model. Compared with sham controls, RIRI kidneys exhibited clear tubular injury and disrupted architecture. H&E and PAS staining confirmed successful injury induction (Fig. S3F) and were accompanied by significantly increased tubular injury scores (Fig. S3G). In the same sections, TRIM28 immunohistochemistry showed stronger staining in RIRI kidneys relative to sham (Fig. S3F), and quantitative IHC analysis confirmed a significant increase in TRIM28 levels in vivo (Fig. S3H). Thus, both the cellular H/R system and the animal RIRI model consistently demonstrate TRIM28 induction across orthogonal assays, supporting TRIM28 activation as a reproducible molecular event associated with ischemic kidney injury rather than a platform-specific artifact.

Notably, the concordant upregulation of TRIM28 in cells and tissues provides a foundation for mechanistic investigation. In view of our upstream multi-omics results implicating immune remodeling and inflammation-associated pathways, these validations motivated subsequent gain- and loss-of-function studies to test whether TRIM28 directly modulates H/R-induced inflammatory and apoptotic responses. In parallel, we advanced to pharmacological perturbation using the candidate compound HY-N10592 to evaluate the pharmacologic tractability of the TRIM28 axis in AKI.

TRIM28 Potentiates H/R-induced IL-17–linked Inflammatory Signaling and Output in HK-2 Cells

Motivated by the enrichment of the IL-17 signaling pathway in our transcriptomic analyses (Sect. 3.6), we selected an IL-17–linked inflammatory readout for functional evaluation in HK-2 cells under H/R conditions. Given that tubular epithelial cells are not canonical IL-17-producing cells, this analysis was intended to capture an inflammation-associated output linked to transcriptomic IL-17 pathway enrichment, rather than to define the full upstream–downstream architecture of canonical IL-17 signaling. We first generated stable HK-2 lines with TRIM28 knockdown or TRIM28 overexpression. Western blotting confirmed efficient TRIM28 suppression in the TRIM28-Sh3 line and marked TRIM28 upregulation in the TRIM28-OE line relative to the corresponding vector controls (Fig. 12A), with densitometric analyses further supporting successful model construction (Fig. 12B–C).

We then examined whether TRIM28 perturbation modulates representative IL-17-associated inflammatory signaling events under normoxia and following H/R stimulation. In the TRIM28 knockdown setting, basal TRIM28 suppression did not produce obvious changes in IL-17 immunoreactivity, ACT1 expression, NF-κB p65 phosphorylation, or total NF-κB levels under normoxia. After H/R stimulation, however, vector control cells showed clear increases in IL-17 immunoreactivity, ACT1 expression, and p-p65 levels, whereas TRIM28 knockdown significantly attenuated these responses, with no obvious change in total NF-κB abundance (Fig. 12D–E). In contrast, in the TRIM28 overexpression setting, H/R-induced increases in IL-17 immunoreactivity, ACT1 expression, and p-p65 were further enhanced by TRIM28 overexpression, again without clear alteration in total NF-κB levels (Fig. 112F–G).

TRIM28 Amplifies H/R-induced Apoptotic Signaling in HK-2 Cells

Given our enrichment analyses implicating apoptosis-related pathways in AKI (Sect. 3.3), we next investigated whether TRIM28 modulates apoptotic phenotypes in the HK-2 H/R model. We first examined canonical apoptosis-associated proteins in TRIM28 knockdown cells. Western blotting indicated that H/R triggered a marked apoptotic response, characterized by increased BAX and cleaved caspase-3 together with decreased anti-apoptotic Bcl-2. Importantly, this H/R-induced shift was significantly blunted by TRIM28 knockdown, as reflected by reduced BAX and cleaved caspase-3 levels and partial restoration of Bcl-2 expression (Fig. 13A). Densitometric quantification corroborated that TRIM28 knockdown mitigated the H/R-driven amplification of apoptotic signals (Fig. 13B).

Conversely, TRIM28 overexpression intensified the apoptotic response to H/R. In TRIM28-OE cells exposed to H/R, western blotting showed higher levels of BAX and cleaved caspase-3 and lower Bcl-2 abundance compared with vector controls (Fig. 13C), with densitometric analysis confirming the same trend (Fig. 13D).

We further assessed apoptosis by immunofluorescence staining of BAX. H/R increased BAX signal intensity overall; under H/R conditions, TRIM28 knockdown reduced BAX fluorescence (Fig. 13E) and its quantified intensity (Fig. 13G). In contrast, TRIM28 overexpression further increased BAX staining relative to the corresponding H/R control (Fig. 13F), consistent with fluorescence quantification (Fig. 13H).

Together, these complementary assays support that TRIM28 abundance is positively associated with H/R-induced apoptotic signaling in HK-2 cells: TRIM28 reduction attenuates, whereas TRIM28 elevation exacerbates, apoptotic marker activation under ischemia-like stress.

HY-N10592 Protects HK-2 Cells from H/R-induced Injury and Shows CETSA-supported Engagement of TRIM28

To assess the protective potential of HY-N10592 against H/R-induced tubular epithelial injury, we first evaluated its concentration-response profile using a CCK-8 viability assay across the indicated dose range (Fig. 14A; Supplementary Fig. S4). Among the tested concentrations, 20 µM HY-N10592 produced the most pronounced improvement in viability relative to the H/R group and was therefore selected as the working concentration for subsequent experiments.

We next examined whether HY-N10592 modulates TRIM28 and the IL-17-associated inflammatory signaling axis under H/R conditions. Immunoblotting showed that TRIM28 protein abundance was markedly increased in the H/R group compared with the NC and NC + HY groups (Fig. 14B), and treatment with HY-N10592 (20 µM) significantly reduced this H/R-associated induction, with an inhibitory effect comparable to NAC (Fig. 14B, D). We then further assessed representative downstream mediators related to IL-17-linked inflammatory signaling. Compared with the NC and NC + HY groups, the H/R group exhibited clear increases in IL-17 immunoreactivity, ACT1 expression, and phosphorylated NF-κB p65 (p-p65), whereas total NF-κB levels showed no obvious change (Fig. 14C). HY-N10592 treatment significantly attenuated the H/R-induced increases in IL-17, ACT1, and p-p65, again with an effect broadly comparable to NAC, while total NF-κB abundance remained largely unchanged across groups (Fig. 14C, E).

To determine whether HY-N10592 also affects apoptotic signaling, we quantified canonical apoptosis-related proteins. Consistent with prior results, H/R increased BAX and cleaved caspase-3 and decreased Bcl-2 (Fig. 14F). HY-N10592 shifted these markers in a protective direction, lowering BAX and cleaved caspase-3 while partially restoring Bcl-2 expression, which was supported by densitometric quantification (Fig. 14F–G).

Discussion

AKI is a clinically heterogeneous syndrome characterized by abrupt loss of renal function, rapid progression, and limited disease-modifying therapies. Despite improved recognition and staging under KDIGO criteria, AKI continues to impose high short-term morbidity and mortality and confers substantial long-term risks, including CKD progression, cardiovascular events, and all-cause mortality [31]. Current clinical management remains largely supportive—optimizing hemodynamics, avoiding nephrotoxins, and initiating renal replacement therapy when indicated—highlighting an urgent need to define actionable molecular circuits that govern injury and repair [32]. In this study, we integrated single-cell transcriptomics, bulk transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, mouse kidney tissue proteomics, and clinical urine proteomics to construct a cross-layer atlas of AKI-associated remodeling. This framework prioritized five key genes (TRIM28, HNRNPH1, ARHGEF10L, C1RL, and UCHL3), with TRIM28 emerging as a top-ranked candidate node. We further explored the tractability of TRIM28 through structure-based virtual screening and provided initial experimental support linking TRIM28 perturbation to inflammatory and apoptotic phenotypes in ischemia-like injury models. Collectively, our results support a “mechanistic discovery–clinical readout” strategy that bridges tissue-level injury programs with urinary-detectable molecular changes.

Rather than relying on any single omics layer, our results are strengthened by convergent signals observed across cellular, tissue, spatial, and biofluid readouts, which helps reduce platform-specific noise and improves biological interpretability. At the single-cell level, AKI was characterized by marked tubular remodeling, including a contraction of proximal tubular epithelial populations alongside an apparent expansion of immune and stromal compartments, consistent with injury-associated shifts in cellular composition [33]. Ligand–receptor inference further supported heightened epithelial–immune and epithelial–vascular crosstalk, suggesting that tubular stress is accompanied by coordinated microenvironmental signaling changes during injury [14, 34]. Complementing these transcriptomic observations, mouse kidney proteomics captured broad disturbances spanning stress responses, metabolic reprogramming, and inflammatory pathways, whereas human urine proteomics provided a clinically accessible proxy of injury-associated molecular output. Importantly, by intersecting transcriptomic perturbations with differential protein abundance in experimental kidney tissue and patient urine, we prioritized candidates supported by multi-layer concordance and then refined this set using machine-learning–based feature ranking. This cross-validation strategy emphasizes reproducibly perturbed molecules that generalize across modalities and compartments, thereby increasing confidence that the nominated candidates reflect core AKI remodeling programs rather than dataset- or platform-specific artifacts. Importantly, TRIM28 itself was directly detected in the human urine proteome and showed significant differential abundance, strengthening its relevance as a translationally convergent candidate linking tissue-level injury biology with clinically accessible urinary signals.

Spatial transcriptomics further contributed anatomical context by revealing non-uniform distributions of key genes and pathway activities within AKI tissues. When coupled with RCTD deconvolution using scRNA-seq references, spatial sections showed compartment-structured patterns consistent with known nephron architecture and injury-associated remodeling [35]. Importantly, because the GSE226533 spatial transcriptomic dataset contained AKI samples only and did not include matched control kidneys, these data primarily support interpretation of intra-tissue heterogeneity and localization of key regulators within AKI kidneys, rather than definitive AKI-versus-control spatial differences. Nevertheless, the spatial localization of TRIM28 and other prioritized genes to regions enriched for tubular and stromal signatures provides an additional layer of evidence linking these molecules to tissue microenvironments undergoing prominent signaling reprogramming.

Among the prioritized genes, TRIM28 emerged with the highest importance score in our classification framework and showed convergent support across transcriptomic and proteomic layers. TRIM28 is widely recognized as a transcriptional and chromatin-associated regulator with established roles in coordinating gene programs through epigenetic and post-translational mechanisms [36, 37]. In our data, elevated TRIM28 expression aligned with programs related to proliferation and stress responses, and it was co-expressed with multiple AKI-relevant genes, supporting the notion that TRIM28 may represent an important regulatory component within injury-associated networks. To move beyond association, we combined cell type–resolved single-cell analysis with functional perturbation experiments in HK-2 cells subjected to H/R. In the scRNA-seq dataset, TRIM28 expression was increased across multiple renal cell populations in AKI, with particularly evident elevation in tubular epithelial compartments, especially PCT and LOH, supporting that TRIM28 induction is not explained solely by global compositional shifts. In parallel, TRIM28 knockdown attenuated, whereas TRIM28 overexpression amplified, inflammatory and apoptotic readouts under H/R, providing experimental support that TRIM28 contributes functionally to epithelial injury phenotypes. TRIM28 was also markedly upregulated in the mouse RIRI model, further supporting its relevance to ischemic kidney injury in vivo. Taken together, these findings support that TRIM28 induction is at least partly epithelial-intrinsic, while also occurring in selected mesenchymal and immune-associated compartments. However, because functional perturbation was performed only in tubular epithelial cells, the role of TRIM28 in non-epithelial compartments should currently be interpreted as spatial/transcriptional association rather than direct functional evidence.

Innate immune activation and the accompanying cytokine/chemokine milieu are now viewed as early organizing events in AKI, shaping both the extent of tubular injury and the trajectory of subsequent repair [13, 38]. In our bulk transcriptomic deconvolution analyses, AKI samples showed an innate-skewed pattern characterized by increased macrophage and neutrophil signatures and reduced lymphocyte-associated signatures, consistent with a shift toward an inflammatory microenvironment. Correlation analyses suggested that the prioritized genes captured distinct immune contexts, implying that they may participate in or reflect different immune–epithelial states during AKI. Pathway-level analyses (GSEA/GSVA) further indicated convergence of the key genes on inflammatory and stress-associated signaling programs, including IL-17–related signatures, interferon-associated programs, and proliferative modules. These findings support the concept that AKI involves coordinated, multi-compartment remodeling that integrates epithelial stress, immune activation, and repair-associated programs rather than a purely passive loss of tubular cells. Balzer and colleagues further showed that proximal tubule (PT) cells in AKI do not simply undergo a passive numerical decline; instead, they transition into distinct injury- and repair-associated states. Notably, a “failed-repair/pro-fibrotic” PT subpopulation exhibited marked upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines and was closely associated with heightened myeloid-cell infiltration [39].

Notably, IL-17–related signatures emerged repeatedly at the transcriptomic pathway level. Motivated by these findings, we evaluated an IL-17–linked inflammatory readout in HK-2 cells under H/R and observed that TRIM28 perturbation bidirectionally modulated this readout, suggesting that TRIM28 may participate in IL-17–associated inflammatory responses during ischemia-like stress. Given that tubular epithelial cells are not canonical IL-17-producing cells and that IL-17-associated biology spans both renal parenchymal and immune compartments, these observations are best interpreted as evidence that TRIM28 impacts an IL-17–linked inflammatory program rather than establishing a complete mechanistic chain. To further define the signaling context, we examined representative downstream mediators associated with IL-17-related inflammatory signaling and found that ACT1 expression and phosphorylated NF-κB p65 (p-p65) were regulated in parallel with TRIM28 perturbation under H/R conditions, whereas total NF-κB levels showed no obvious change. Because ACT1 is a proximal adaptor in canonical IL-17 receptor signaling and NF-κB is a major downstream inflammatory effector, the concordant modulation of these molecules provides additional pathway-level support that TRIM28 is linked to IL-17-associated inflammatory signaling in tubular epithelial cells. Nevertheless, these data should still be interpreted as partial mechanistic support rather than a full dissection of the canonical IL-17 signaling cascade. Broader pathway interrogation, together with immune–epithelial interaction models and orthogonal pathway-engagement assays, will be required to more fully clarify how TRIM28 interfaces with IL-17–linked inflammatory networks in AKI.

Beyond mechanistic prioritization, our study further explored the pharmacological tractability of TRIM28. Structure-based virtual screening identified HY-N10592 (Herbacetin-3-sophoroside-8-glucoside) as a top-ranked docking candidate with a favorable predicted interaction profile within a putative TRIM28 pocket. In HK-2 cells, HY-N10592 attenuated H/R-associated apoptotic marker changes and reduced TRIM28 abundance together with suppression of the IL-17/ACT1/p-p65 inflammatory signaling readout, while total NF-κB levels remained largely unchanged; these effects were broadly comparable to those of the antioxidant positive control. Consistent with the target-engagement evidence presented in Sect. 3.14, CETSA showed that HY-N10592 altered the thermal stability profile of TRIM28 in a cellular context, thereby providing support beyond docking alone for a TRIM28-linked pharmacologic effect. These data strengthen the interpretation that HY-N10592 acts, at least in part, through a TRIM28-linked pharmacologic mechanism and support its use as a candidate chemical tool for probing TRIM28-centered biology in AKI. However, neither in vivo pharmacologic testing nor in vivo genetic rescue/conditional validation was performed in the present study, and therefore the therapeutic relevance of HY-N10592 and the TRIM28 dependence of its effects remain to be established in vivo. Nevertheless, CETSA does not by itself establish binding specificity or fully define the extent to which the observed protective effects are exclusively TRIM28-dependent. Definitive characterization will still require orthogonal biophysical binding assays, broader target-engagement validation, and ideally genetic–pharmacologic interaction studies to determine whether HY-N10592 effects are diminished when TRIM28 is suppressed.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, although we integrated multiple datasets, some omics layers were constrained by relatively small sample sizes and heterogeneous backgrounds, particularly the spatial transcriptomic dataset, which included only two AKI samples without matched controls. In addition, formal prospective power calculations were not performed for all study components because several datasets were exploratory and availability-limited. Larger, well-characterized cohorts with standardized clinical phenotyping, balanced sampling designs, and prospective statistical planning will be required to confirm the generalizability and statistical robustness of our findings [40]. Second, although TRIM28 was directly detected in the human urine proteome and showed significant differential abundance, the causal relationship between renal tissue abundance and urinary release remains unclear. Future studies incorporating matched tissue and urine samples from the same clinical cohort or longitudinal sampling designs could provide stronger translational support and help clarify the tissue-to-urine dynamics of TRIM28 and related candidates. Third, while computational deconvolution methods provided insight into immune infiltration and pathway activity, these methods rely on gene set assumptions and may not fully capture complex immune states [41]. Although single-cell and spatial analyses indicated that TRIM28 expression is not restricted to tubular epithelium, functional validation in this study was limited to HK-2 cells. Additional perturbation studies in stromal and immune-relevant compartments will be required to define the compartment-specific functions of TRIM28 during AKI. Orthogonal immunological profiling, such as flow cytometry and multiplex immunohistochemistry, will be valuable for validating these findings. Finally, although TRIM28 perturbation experiments in vitro support its role in regulating inflammatory and apoptotic pathways, and its induction was confirmed in the mouse RIRI model, the present study did not include in vivo genetic modulation of TRIM28 or in vivo pharmacologic testing of HY-N10592. Therefore, TRIM28 should currently be considered a candidate therapeutic target rather than a fully validated in vivo driver of AKI, and further in vivo proof-of-concept studies—including conditional genetic models, rescue strategies, and pharmacologic intervention experiments—will be required to determine whether TRIM28-directed modulation or HY-N10592 confers therapeutic benefit.

In conclusion, by integrating single-cell, bulk, and spatial transcriptomics with mouse kidney tissue and human urine proteomics, we delineated multi-layer remodeling programs in AKI and prioritized TRIM28 as a convergent node associated with inflammatory and apoptotic responses. Our functional perturbation experiments support a contributory role of TRIM28 in ischemia-like tubular injury, and our virtual screening and in vitro pharmacological data nominate HY-N10592 as a candidate small-molecule tool/lead for further validation. Together, this work provides a systems-level framework that links tissue mechanisms to urinary readouts and supports TRIM28-centered hypotheses for biomarker development and therapeutic exploration in AKI.

Conclusion

In this study, we integrated human AKI single-cell, bulk, and spatial transcriptomics with mouse renal ischemia–reperfusion proteomics and clinical urine proteomics to delineate AKI-associated cellular and molecular remodeling. Cross-layer validation together with machine-learning prioritization identified TRIM28 as a translationally relevant candidate regulator, supported by concordant evidence across omics modalities, direct detection in the human urine proteome, and enrichment of inflammation-related pathways, including IL-17–associated gene programs. TRIM28 was upregulated in HK-2 hypoxia/reoxygenation and in the mouse RIRI model, and genetic perturbation in HK-2 cells modulated IL-17–linked inflammatory signaling and apoptosis-related phenotypes. In addition, the docking-prioritized compound HY-N10592 reduced injury-associated signals in vitro and showed CETSA-supported cellular engagement of TRIM28, supporting its use as a candidate chemical tool for further mechanistic and in vivo evaluation. Collectively, our findings position TRIM28 as a tractable node linking tubular injury with clinically accessible urinary signals and support further evaluation of TRIM28 as a potential therapeutic target in AKI. However, further in vivo proof-of-concept intervention studies will be required to determine whether TRIM28-directed modulation confers therapeutic benefit.
