Transcriptome sequence resource for the hazelnut powdery mildew pathogen Erysiphe corylacearum
Authors: Ulku Baykal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Erysiphe corylacearum is the main causal agent of hazelnut powdery mildew, causing substantial damage in the Black Sea region of Türkiye. Despite its economic importance, molecular resources for this obligate biotrophic pathogen remain severely limited. This study presents the first comprehensive transcriptomic dataset for E. corylacearum obtained through Illumina sequencing of mRNA from naturally infected hazelnut leaves. Using targeted epidermal peeling to enrich fungal material while minimizing host contamination, over 66 million high-quality paired-end reads were generated. De novo Trinity-based assembly yielded an initial set of 135,404 unigenes for annotation, and the final NCBI-cleaned TSA submission contains 100,863 transcript sequences. Functional annotation assigned database matches to 71% of the initial unigene set, including sequences related to pathogenicity, sexual compatibility, and reproduction. The dataset also includes 10,821 high-confidence intra-sample sequence variants retained after filtering the original SNP call set against the cleaned FASTA using transcript-identifier, coordinate, and REF-allele checks, providing a candidate resource for future marker development, diagnostic assays, and comparative analyses pending validation across additional isolates. This transcriptomic resource will facilitate investigations into pathogen biology, host-pathogen interactions, and improved disease management strategies.

Background & Summary

Hazelnut (Corylus avellana L.) is a globally significant agricultural crop, with Türkiye producing approximately 70% of the world’s supply1. Powdery mildew, caused primarily by Erysiphe corylacearum, represents one of the most economically important diseases affecting hazelnut production worldwide2–4. The disease manifests as characteristic white, powdery fungal colonies on the surface of leaves, petioles, and occasionally on developing nuts, leading to reduced photosynthetic efficiency, premature defoliation, and yield losses that can exceed 50% under favorable conditions for disease development5.

E. corylacearum exhibits typical characteristics of powdery mildew fungi, including obligate biotrophy, ectophytic mycelial growth with specialized feeding structures (haustoria) that penetrate host epidermal cells, and prolific production of asexual spores (conidia) that facilitate rapid disease spread6. The obligate biotrophic nature of powdery mildews, including E. corylacearum, presents significant challenges for molecular studies, as they cannot be cultured axenically and produce limited biomass that is intimately associated with host tissues7. As with other powdery mildew fungi, these biological constraints have limited the development of genomic and transcriptomic resources for E. corylacearum. However, unlike model species such as Blumeria graminis f.sp. hordei and Erysiphe necator, for which complete genome sequences are available, molecular resources for E. corylacearum remain scarce8–10.

Metatranscriptomic approaches offer a powerful solution for studying obligate biotrophic pathogens by enabling simultaneous capture and analysis of gene expression from naturally infected environmental samples11. Although previous studies have successfully applied these methods to powdery mildew species under controlled laboratory conditions, such as Podosphaera xanthii on cucurbits grown in tissue culture12, the application to field-collected environmental samples presents additional technical challenges including variable pathogen biomass, mixed microbial communities, and host tissue contamination. Recent advances in sample enrichment techniques have demonstrated the potential to overcome these limitations and obtain high-quality pathogen transcriptomes directly from naturally infected field material.

This study reports the first comprehensive transcriptomic resource for E. corylacearum obtained through Illumina sequencing and de novo assembly of RNA extracted from naturally infected hazelnut leaves using an innovative environmental sampling approach. The methodology employed targeted epidermal peeling to enrich for fungal material while minimizing host contamination, enabling successful transcriptomic characterization directly from field samples. The initial assembly comprised over 135,000 assembled unigenes for annotation and exploratory analysis, while the final NCBI-cleaned TSA submission contains 100,863 transcript sequences after removal of non-target sequences flagged during contamination screening. This transcriptomic dataset will facilitate investigations into pathogen biology, host-pathogen interactions, and the development of improved disease management strategies for sustainable hazelnut production. The functionally annotated transcripts and intra-sample sequence variants retained after filtering the original call set against the cleaned FASTA provide valuable resources for hazelnut breeding programs, supporting resistance gene discovery, molecular diagnostics, and population monitoring efforts.

Methods

E. corylacearum-infected hazelnut leaves were collected from commercial orchards in Giresun Province, Black Sea region of Türkiye, during the peak infection period in July 2023. Sampling targeted fully expanded young leaves exhibiting extensive powdery mildew coverage to maximize pathogen biomass recovery. To enrich for fungal material and minimize host tissue contamination, pathogen-rich epidermal layers were isolated using a modified peel method13. The adaxial surface of infected leaves was carefully coated with clear nail base coat and allowed to polymerize completely. The resulting film, containing embedded fungal structures, was gently peeled away using sterile forceps and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Total RNA was extracted from frozen epidermal peels using the Plant/Fungi Total RNA Purification Kit (Norgen Biotek Corp., Canada) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Genomic DNA contamination was eliminated through on-column treatment with RNase-free DNase I for 15 minutes at 25 °C. RNA quality and quantity were assessed using a NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Despite optimization efforts, the challenging nature of environmental sampling from field-collected material yielded RNA with relatively low integrity (RIN = 2.9), which is typical for obligate biotrophic pathogens extracted from naturally infected tissue. This necessitated careful adaptation of downstream protocols to accommodate partially degraded RNA while maintaining transcript representation.

cDNA libraries were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) with modifications to accommodate partially degraded RNA. Starting with 1 µg of total RNA per sample, mRNA enrichment was performed using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads with two rounds of purification to maximize recovery of intact transcripts. Fragmentation time was reduced to 5 minutes at 94 °C to prevent over-fragmentation of already compromised RNA. First-strand cDNA synthesis employed random hexamer primers and extended incubation conditions, followed by second-strand synthesis with dUTP incorporation. After end-repair, 3′ adenylation, and adapter ligation, size selection targeted fragments of 300–400 bp using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA). Library amplification was optimized to 12 PCR cycles to balance yield with minimal amplification bias. Final library quality was verified on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer before sequencing on an Illumina platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads.

Raw sequencing reads were processed through a comprehensive quality control pipeline. Initial quality assessment was performed using FastQC14, followed by adapter removal and quality trimming with Trimmomatic15 using parameters: LEADING:3, TRAILING:3, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:20, MINLEN:50. This processing retained over 97% of raw reads while significantly improving quality metrics. To eliminate host-derived sequences, cleaned reads were aligned to the Corylus avellana reference genome using Bowtie216 with sensitive local alignment parameters. Unmapped read pairs, representing putative pathogen sequences, were extracted for transcriptome assembly.

De novo transcriptome assembly was performed using Trinity17 with default parameters optimized for fungal transcriptomes and the --jaccard_clip option enabled to handle potential fusion transcripts arising from incomplete RNA. To reduce redundancy in the assembled sequences, transcripts were clustered using CD-HIT-EST18 at 95% sequence identity, followed by Corset19 clustering based on shared read support, yielding 135,404 final unigenes. Before TSA deposition, the assembled transcriptome was screened using the NCBI Foreign Contamination Screen. Sequences flagged as non-target or contaminant-derived were removed or trimmed according to NCBI recommendations, and sequences shorter than 200 nt after trimming were excluded. This produced the final cleaned TSA submission containing 100,863 transcript sequences.

Functional annotation of the initial 135,404-unigene pre-NCBI-cleaning assembly employed a multi-faceted approach to maximize annotation coverage and accuracy. Sequence homology searches were conducted against the NCBI non-redundant (Nr) and nucleotide (Nt) databases using DIAMOND20 with an E-value threshold of 1e-5. Protein domain identification utilized HMMER searches against the Pfam database21. Gene Ontology (GO) terms were assigned using Blast2GO22 incorporating both sequence homology and protein domain information. Metabolic pathway reconstruction was performed through KEGG Automatic Annotation Server (KAAS)23 using bidirectional best hit methodology. Additionally, unigenes were classified into orthologous groups through comparison with the KOG database. Gene expression levels were quantified using RSEM24 with abundance reported as Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) to facilitate comparison across transcripts of varying lengths.

Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) discovery followed established best practices for RNA-seq variant calling. Cleaned reads were aligned to the assembled transcriptome using STAR25 in two-pass mode to improve splice junction detection. After duplicate marking with Picard tools and split N cigar processing, variant calling was performed using GATK HaplotypeCaller26 in RNA-seq mode. High-confidence SNPs were filtered based on quality metrics including QD > 2.0, FS < 30.0, MQ > 40.0, and ReadPosRankSum > -8.0. Because the SNPs were originally called and quality-filtered against the pre-deposition assembly, the resulting records were then filtered against the final cleaned pre-submission FASTA corresponding to the deposited TSA rather than recalled de novo. Records were matched using the original assembly identifiers retained in the cleaned FASTA. A record was kept only when the corresponding transcript was present, the reported 1-based position fell within the retained sequence, and the nucleotide at that position matched the reported REF allele; records failing any of these checks were removed. When multiple cleaned FASTA records shared the same identifier prefix, a variant was counted once if at least one record passed the coordinate and REF checks. Genotypes 1|1 were classified as homozygous, whereas 0|1 and 1|2 were classified as heterozygous. Variant allele frequency (VAF) analysis was restricted to biallelic 0|1 sites with usable read depth (DP) and non-reference depth (DV), with VAF calculated as DV/DP; multiallelic 1|2 sites were excluded because DV does not resolve the two alternate alleles separately.

A set of 37,899 orphan/unannotated transcripts retained in the final NCBI-cleaned TSA and lacking significant matches to public sequence databases was further characterized through a preliminary in silico pipeline. Open reading frames and protein sequences were predicted using TransDecoder (https://github.com/TransDecoder/TransDecoder). Predicted proteins were screened for N-terminal signal peptides using SignalP 6.027 (Eukarya mode). Signal-peptide-positive proteins were evaluated for transmembrane helices downstream of the predicted cleavage site using DeepTMHMM28. Proteins lacking additional transmembrane regions were assessed for effector-like features using EffectorP 3.029. A high-priority candidate subset was defined by combining predicted secretion, absence of transmembrane regions, EffectorP positivity, small size (≤300 aa), and cysteine enrichment (≥3% Cys).

Data Records

The dataset described in this Data Descriptor is available through the NCBI Sequence Read Archive, GenBank, and Zenodo. Raw sequencing reads have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under accession SRP61098030, with run accession SRR35105981, BioProject PRJNA1310008, and BioSample SAMN50763195. The final cleaned TSA assembly, generated after NCBI Foreign Contamination Screen-based removal of non-target sequences, has been deposited in GenBank under accession GLIQ00000000; the version described in this paper is GLIQ0100000031. This deposited TSA dataset contains 100,863 transcript sequences. The SNP dataset retained after filtering the original call set against the cleaned FASTA comprises 10,821 sites that passed transcript-identifier, coordinate, and REF-allele checks and is available from Zenodo under https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.1705459232.

Assessment of the transcriptome assembly revealed robust statistics supporting high quality reconstruction despite the challenging starting material (Table 2). The initial clustered assembly comprised 135,404 unigenes with an N50 of 1,843 bp and maximum transcript length of 13,513 bp, providing the basis for annotation and downstream exploratory analyses (Fig. 2a). Following NCBI Foreign Contamination Screen-based removal of non-target sequences and required trimming, the final cleaned TSA submission contains 100,863 transcript sequences. The effective redundancy reduction through clustering approaches is demonstrated in Fig. 2b. BUSCO33 analysis of the initial clustered assembly yielded 89.1% complete BUSCOs (ascomycota_odb10; n = 1,706 orthologs). To evaluate the impact of NCBI Foreign Contamination Screen-based cleaning, BUSCO analysis was repeated on the final cleaned TSA assembly using the ascomycota_odb10 dataset containing 1,706 conserved fungal orthologs. This analysis revealed 87.4% complete BUSCOs (14.0% single-copy, 73.4% duplicated), with 7.7% fragmented and 4.9% missing BUSCOs (Table 3, Fig. 3). The slight reduction in complete BUSCOs after NCBI FCS-based cleaning is consistent with the removal or trimming of flagged sequences, while the final cleaned TSA assembly still retained high fungal gene-content recovery. The cleaned assembly retained high BUSCO completeness comparable to published powdery mildew transcriptome resources, although the high duplicated BUSCO fraction indicates redundancy and/or biological complexity typical of de novo metatranscriptomic assemblies. The high proportion of duplicated complete BUSCOs should therefore be interpreted cautiously. In a de novo metatranscriptomic assembly, duplicated BUSCOs may arise from transcript isoforms, allelic variation, paralogous transcripts, assembly redundancy, mixed genotypes within the infection, or residual metatranscriptomic complexity. Taxonomic profiling of annotated sequences showed that approximately 95% of assigned matches corresponded to fungal proteins, including target powdery mildew fungi and other environmental fungi, with limited non-fungal representation (<5%) (Fig. 4a). In Fig. 4a, this fungal-enriched fraction is represented by the combined target powdery mildew and other environmental fungal assignments, while the smaller non-fungal fraction corresponds to host- or environment-associated matches. The majority showed highest similarity to Erysiphe species and related powdery mildews. This validates the effectiveness of the targeted epidermal peeling approach for obtaining high-quality pathogen transcriptomes from naturally infected field material. Notably, 43.47% of the initial unigene set (58,856 of 135,404 unigenes) lacked significant matches to sequences in public databases. Expression quantification demonstrated successful capture of the full dynamic range of transcript abundance (Fig. 6a). FPKM values spanned five orders of magnitude, from less than 0.1 to over 1,000, enabling detection of both highly abundant and rare transcripts (Fig. 6b). The expression distribution showed expected patterns with many moderately expressed genes and progressively fewer genes at expression extremes. Alignment of reads back to the assembled transcriptome achieved an overall mapping rate of 87.06%, further supporting assembly quality and completeness. Normalized transcript-body coverage analysis across 100,863 transcripts (the final NCBI-submitted assembly after NCBI Foreign Contamination Screen-based removal or trimming of flagged sequences) revealed no strong positional end bias (end-bias ratio = 1.04; Table 6, Fig. 6c), indicating that the low RNA integrity (RIN = 2.9) did not introduce severe systematic coverage skew across the assembled transcriptome. Separately, the unannotated transcript fraction was examined for candidate effector-like proteins. Of the 58,856 unigenes lacking significant database matches in the initial assembly, 37,899 were retained in the final NCBI-cleaned TSA and met the ORF-prediction input criteria. This cleaned-TSA orphan subset was used for the preliminary in silico characterization of orphan transcript-derived proteins (Table 7, Fig. 8).

Of these, 1,028 carried predicted N-terminal signal peptides according to SignalP 6.0 (Eukarya mode)27, representing 7.59% of the predicted orphan protein set. DeepTMHMM28 analysis showed that 1,017 of the SignalP-positive proteins lacked additional transmembrane helices downstream of the predicted cleavage site. Effector-like proteins were then predicted using EffectorP 3.029, which predicts apoplastic and cytoplasmic effector candidates in fungi and oomycetes. This analysis identified 741 candidate secreted effector-like proteins from the SignalP-positive, TM-filtered orphan protein set, representing 72.86% of the secreted, TM-filtered set and 5.47% of all predicted orphan proteins. These included 387 cytoplasmic-only candidates (52.23%), 153 apoplastic-only candidates (20.65%), and 201 dual apoplastic/cytoplasmic candidates (27.13%). Usage Notes

This comprehensive transcriptomic dataset for E. corylacearum provides a foundational resource for diverse research applications in plant pathology, fungal biology, and crop protection. Researchers interested in comparative genomics can utilize these data to investigate evolutionary relationships among powdery mildew species, identify lineage-specific genes, and trace the evolution of pathogenicity mechanisms. The high proportion of taxonomically restricted sequences offers opportunities to discover novel E. corylacearum-specific factors that may contribute to host specialization on hazelnut.

The environmental origin of this transcriptomic dataset provides unique advantages over laboratory-based studies. The transcriptome captures gene expression patterns occurring under natural field conditions, including responses to environmental stresses, host-pathogen dynamics, and potential interactions with other microorganisms present in the phylloplane. This makes the dataset particularly valuable for understanding the ecology and natural pathogenesis strategies of E. corylacearum in commercial hazelnut production systems.

For functional studies, the annotated transcriptome provides numerous candidates for experimental characterization. Putative effector proteins can be identified based on characteristics such as small size, high cysteine content, presence of secretion signals, and lack of homology to known proteins. Genes involved in nutrient acquisition, particularly those encoding transporters and enzymes for utilizing host-derived nutrients, offer insights into the biotrophic lifestyle. Additionally, transcripts related to sexual reproduction and mating type determination can facilitate studies of population biology and genetic diversity in field populations.

Functional annotation, Gene Ontology, KEGG, KOG, and Pfam summaries reported in this Data Descriptor refer to the explicitly identified initial 135,404-unigene pre-NCBI-cleaning assembly and should not be interpreted as annotation statistics for the final cleaned TSA. In contrast, the orphan-transcript protein-prediction and effector-candidate analyses presented in Table 7 and Fig. 8 were performed on the 37,899 unannotated transcripts retained in the final NCBI-cleaned TSA.

The 10,821 retained high-confidence SNP records represent a subset of the original calls from a single metatranscriptomic sample obtained from naturally infected hazelnut leaves, selected by filtering against the cleaned FASTA. Because the reads were not remapped and variants were not recalled against the cleaned TSA, these records should be regarded as a filtered subset of the original calls rather than a de novo variant call set generated directly from the final reference. The observed polymorphisms cannot be assigned to a single source and may arise from mixed infection, allelic variation, allele-specific expression, or mapping to closely related paralogous transcripts. The predominance of heterozygous calls (9,825 heterozygous vs. 996 homozygous sites) is compatible with several of these possibilities. Among 9,755 biallelic 0|1 sites, 49.83% occurred within VAF 0.40–0.60, and the distribution was centered near 0.46 (mean = 0.4716, median = 0.4615; Fig. 7). This pattern indicates balanced representation at many sites but does not distinguish among diploid allelic variation, co-infection, allele-specific expression, or paralogous mapping. Although population-level comparisons are not feasible from a single sample, the filtered sites provide candidates for future validation, strain tracking, molecular diagnostics, and population studies in E. corylacearum.

Applied researchers can leverage this resource for developing improved disease management strategies. The transcriptome provides targets for host-induced gene silencing approaches, where essential pathogen genes are silenced by expressing corresponding RNA interference constructs in transgenic hazelnut plants. Similarly, unique pathogen proteins absent from the host can serve as targets for novel fungicides with reduced off-target effects. The dataset also enables development of molecular diagnostics for early disease detection and pathogen monitoring.

Users should consider several technical aspects when utilizing these data. Although the transcript-body coverage analysis did not detect strong systematic terminal bias, the low RNA integrity value (RIN = 2.9) may still affect completeness or quantification of individual transcripts. Researchers requiring full-length sequences for specific genes should validate transcript completeness through independent methods such as RACE-PCR. Additionally, as this dataset represents a single sampling time point during active infection, genes expressed exclusively during other lifecycle stages such as sexual reproduction or overwintering may be underrepresented.

While the enrichment strategy successfully minimized contamination (>95% fungal sequences), users should be aware that trace amounts of sequences from other leaf-associated microorganisms may be present due to the environmental sampling approach. Researchers should independently verify the organismal origin of sequences of interest, particularly for genes lacking clear homology to known fungal sequences. However, this environmental context also provides opportunities to study natural microbial community interactions that influence pathogen biology under field conditions. Expression values reflect the specific infection stage and environmental conditions at the time of sampling and may vary under different circumstances.

For optimal utilization of this resource, integration of these data with other genomic resources is recommended as they become available. Future genome sequencing of E. corylacearum will enable improved transcript models and identification of non-coding regulatory elements. Comparative transcriptomics across multiple infection stages, host genotypes, and environmental conditions will reveal dynamic aspects of gene expression not captured in this single snapshot. Time-course studies following infection progression would be particularly valuable for understanding pathogenicity mechanisms and host-pathogen interactions.
