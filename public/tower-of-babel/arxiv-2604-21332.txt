Disentangling new physics with quantum entanglement in t ​ t ¯ t\bar{t} production at future lepton colliders


Abstract
We investigate quantum entanglement and Bell-inequality violation in top-antitop pair production at future lepton colliders such as the International Linear Collider (ILC) and multi-TeV muon colliders. Within the Standard Model (SM), the process proceeds through s s -channel γ \gamma and Z Z exchange and exhibits characteristic spin-correlation patterns that encode a non-trivial amount of entanglement. We then examine how these features are modified in several well-motivated extensions of the SM: (i) a neutral scalar mediator that couples to charged leptons and top quarks via Yukawa interactions and contributes as an additional s s -channel exchange; (ii) the minimal gauged U ​ ( 1 ) B − L U(1)_{B-L} model, which introduces a new neutral gauge boson Z ′ Z^{\prime} coupling vectorially to SM fermions; and (iii) a Randall-Sundrum scenario, in which the exchange of massive Kaluza-Klein gravitons arising from a warped extra dimension induces additional spin-dependent interactions. For all cases, we evaluate quantum-information observables including the entanglement marker, the concurrence, and the maximal Clauser-Horne-Shimony-Holt parameter, and study their dependence on the center-of-mass energy, scattering angle, and model parameters. We find that, relative to the SM expectation, the entanglement is typically reduced in the scalar-mediator scenario, while sizable deviations can arise in the U ​ ( 1 ) B − L U(1)_{B-L} and Randall-Sundrum cases for phenomenologically relevant regions of parameter space. These results demonstrate the potential of quantum-information observables as sensitive probes of new neutral interactions and extra-dimensional dynamics in future lepton colliders.

Introduction

Quantum entanglement is one of the most fundamental features of quantum mechanics.
It refers to the phenomenon in which multiple quantum systems form a single global quantum state that cannot be
described as a product of independent states of its subsystems.
Although physical observables are measured locally on each subsystem, the measurement outcomes exhibit strong
correlations that cannot be explained within classical physics.
Such non-classical correlations originate from the global structure of the quantum state itself.
Well-known low-energy realizations of quantum entanglement include entangled photon pairs
[
1
,
2
]

and ultracold atomic systems
[
3
]
, where highly controlled experimental environments have enabled precise
tests of entanglement generation and its properties.

Quantum entanglement, however, is a universal phenomenon that arises in all systems governed by quantum mechanics and
is by no means restricted to low-energy physics.
In particular, heavy particles produced in high-energy processes provide a unique arena for studying entanglement: due to the
short-distance nature of the interactions and the rapid decay of unstable particles, phase information and spin correlations of the
quantum state can be preserved and accessed experimentally.
From this perspective, high-energy scattering processes offer a new experimental and theoretical stage for exploring quantum
entanglement.

Indeed, correlations among spin degrees of freedom in particle-antiparticle pair production have long been studied in
high-energy physics.
In processes such as top-quark pair production and electroweak reactions, the spin states of the produced particles can be
reconstructed from their decay products, enabling systematic analyses of spin correlations within the density-matrix formalism
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
.
These spin correlations reflect the underlying interaction dynamics of the system and describe quantum correlations in a broad sense,
not necessarily limited to quantum entanglement.
Experimentally, spin correlations in top-antitop production have already been measured at the Tevatron in proton-antiproton
collisions by the D0 and CDF collaborations
[

Collider (LHC) in proton-proton collisions by the ATLAS and CMS
experiments

Building on this progress, quantum correlations in top-antitop production at the
LHC, including quantum entanglement and Bell-inequality violation, have attracted
considerable attention in recent years.
Using Run-2 LHC data, Afik et al.
[

demonstrated that quantum
entanglement in
t\bar{t}

marking the first proposal to measure entanglement in a quark-antiquark system
and the highest-energy realization of such a study to date.
Subsequent works have systematically investigated both the structure of

Importantly, recent measurements by ATLAS and CMS

observability of quantum correlations in the SM at high energies.

While top-antitop production has been extensively studied at hadron colliders
such as the LHC, where a wide variety of quantum-mechanical observables have been
analyzed, complementary studies have also been carried out at lepton colliders.
Lepton colliders provide an exceptionally clean experimental environment, with
precisely controlled initial states and production mechanisms dominated by
electroweak interactions rather than strong dynamics.
These features make them an ideal setting for high-precision investigations of
spin correlations and quantum correlations in heavy-particle production.

Motivated by these advantages, detailed analyses of quantum spin observables in
top-quark pair production at lepton colliders have been performed in recent
years, clearly demonstrating their potential and relevance
[
30

The clean environment and tunable kinematics of lepton colliders allow for a
transparent interpretation of spin-correlation effects and provide a natural
laboratory for studying quantum entanglement in

Similar quantum-information-oriented analyses have also been
carried out for other fermion-antifermion production processes at lepton

In particular, systematic studies of spin correlations, quantum entanglement,

experimentally accessed at Belle II experiment as well as at tau-charm factories.
Tau-lepton pairs therefore provide another well-controlled system for testing

interaction structures influence their manifestation.

A more general and systematic approach to quantum correlations in

Early progress in this direction explored quantum tomography of collider

More recently, a fully automated framework was presented to construct

Related studies have also investigated optimized spin bases for

2
Formalism: Quantum-information observables

2.1
Spin density matrix and correlation coefficients

Spin correlations in a top-antitop system can only be fully specified once nine independent parameters
are fixed, as is the case for any two-qubit state.
A convenient way to encode these degrees of freedom is through the standard decomposition of the spin density matrix,

\rho=\frac{1}{4}\left(\mathbf{1}\otimes\mathbf{1}+\mathcal{B}_{1}\cdot\bm{\sigma}\otimes\mathbf{1}+\mathcal{B}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}\otimes\bm{\sigma}+\mathcal{C}\cdot\bm{\sigma}\otimes\bm{\sigma}\right),

(2.1)

where the vectors
\mathcal{B}_{1}=(B_{1i})
and

\mathcal{B}_{2}=(B_{2i})
, with
i=1,2,3
, quantify, respectively,
the top and antitop polarizations along the spatial axes,
and
\mathcal{C}=(C_{ij})
, with
i,j=1,2,3
,
contains nine parameters and encodes all possible spin-spin correlations.
Here,
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2},\sigma^{3})
denotes the Pauli matrices.
To give operational meaning to these components, one must choose a spatial triad.
In what follows we adopt the customary
orthonormal basis built from the top-quark momentum direction,

\hat{k}=\text{top direction},\qquad\hat{r}=\frac{\hat{p}-\hat{k}\cos\theta}{\sin\theta},\qquad\hat{n}=\frac{\hat{p}\times\hat{k}}{\sin\theta},

(2.2)

with
\hat{p}
the incoming
l^{-}

direction, and with
\theta
defined through
\cos\theta=\hat{k}\cdot\hat{p}
in the
t\bar{t}

rest frame.
The geometric configuration of these vectors is depicted in Fig.
1
.

Figure 1
:
Orthonormal basis
(\hat{r},\hat{n},\hat{k})
in the
t\bar{t}
rest frame,
defined with respect to the top-quark direction
\hat{k}
and beam direction
\hat{p}
.

2.2
Entanglement measures and Bell inequality

To quantify bipartite entanglement between the two spin-
1/2
systems,
we use the concurrence
C
introduced by Wootters
[
48
]
.
For a given two-qubit density matrix
\rho
, we define the
spin-flipped density matrix as

\tilde{\rho}=(\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y})\,\rho^{*}\,(\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y}),

(2.3)

where the asterisk denotes complex conjugation in the computational basis.
The non-Hermitian matrix

R=\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho}\tilde{\rho}\sqrt{\rho}}

(2.4)

has four real, non-negative eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
(
i=1,\dots,4
), which we
order such that
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{3}\geq\lambda_{4}
.
The concurrence is then given by

C=\max\left\{0,\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{4}\right\}.

(2.5)

It satisfies
0\leq C\leq 1
, with
C=0
if and only if the state is separable,
and
C=1
for maximally entangled Bell states.

In collider environments, however,
C
is not an ideal observable.
Extracting them from data typically inflates the impact of statistical fluctuations; related estimators have
been shown to suffer from an intrinsic upward bias
[
22
]
.
For this reason, it is advantageous to work with alternative quantities that retain sensitivity to quantum
correlations while being more robust experimentally.
As established in Refs.
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
,
the so-called entanglement markers
can be built from the diagonal entries of
\mathcal{C}
:

\displaystyle D^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}(C_{kk}+C_{rr}+C_{nn}),

(2.6)

\displaystyle D^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}(C_{kk}-C_{rr}-C_{nn}),

(2.7)

\displaystyle D^{(r)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}(-C_{kk}+C_{rr}-C_{nn}),

(2.8)

\displaystyle D^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}(-C_{kk}-C_{rr}+C_{nn}).

(2.9)

Here, the indices
i,j=1,2,3
correspond to the orthonormal basis

(\hat{r},\hat{n},\hat{k})
, respectively.
A convenient sufficient criterion for entanglement in (
2.1
) is

D_{\min}\equiv\min\{D^{(1)},D^{(k)},D^{(r)},D^{(n)}\}<-\frac{1}{3}.

(2.10)

This inequality underlies the recent experimental demonstrations of top-quark entanglement in
pp

collisions
[
28
,
29
]
, where
D^{(1)}
becomes the smallest of the four markers near threshold.

While (
2.10
) is normally only sufficient, it becomes necessary when both top and antitop polarizations
vanish,
\mathcal{B}_{1}=\mathcal{B}_{2}=0
, and
\mathcal{C}
is diagonal. In that case, the concurrence reduces to

C=\tfrac{1}{2}\max\bigl(0,-1-3D_{\min}\bigr),

(2.11)

which makes it explicit that nonzero entanglement appears precisely when
D_{\min}<-\tfrac{1}{3}
.

The nonlocal properties of the two-qubit state
\rho
can be probed by Bell inequalities.
The simplest and most widely studied version is the Clauser-Horne-Shimony-Holt (CHSH) inequality
[
49
]
,
formulated in terms of spin measurements performed independently on the two qubits.
One may imagine two distant observers, Alice and Bob, who each choose between two possible spin-measurement
directions.
Let Alice’s choice of axes be the unit vectors
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\prime}
, and Bob’s be
\hat{b}
and
\hat{b}^{\prime}
.
For a given pair of measurement directions, the spin-spin correlation is defined as

E(\hat{a},\hat{b})=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho(\hat{a}\cdot\bm{\sigma})\otimes(\hat{b}\cdot\bm{\sigma})\right],

(2.12)

where
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
are the Pauli matrices.
The CHSH Bell parameter
B
is constructed as

B=E(\hat{a},\hat{b})+E(\hat{a},\hat{b}^{\prime})+E(\hat{a}^{\prime},\hat{b})-E(\hat{a}^{\prime},\hat{b}^{\prime}).

(2.13)

Local hidden-variable theories impose the CHSH bound
[
50
]

|B|\leq 2,

(2.14)

whereas quantum mechanics allows values up to the Tsirelson limit
2\sqrt{2}

[
51
]
.
This inequality thus provides a sharp criterion for diagnosing nonclassical correlations in a two-qubit system.

For an arbitrary mixed two-qubit state, the maximal quantum violation
B_{\max}
obtainable by optimizing over all measurement directions can be expressed entirely in terms of the correlation matrix
\mathcal{C}
defined in Eq. (
2.1
).
Introducing

U=\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathcal{C},

(2.15)

and denoting its eigenvalues by
u_{1}\geq u_{2}\geq u_{3}\geq 0
, the Horodecki criterion
[
52
]
states

B_{\max}=2\sqrt{u_{1}+u_{2}},

(2.16)

and CHSH violation occurs if and only if

B_{\max}>2\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad u_{1}+u_{2}>1.

(2.17)

In collider applications,
B_{\max}
provides a clean and basis-independent diagnostic of the quantum nonlocality present in the spin state of the produced pair.
The correlation matrix
\mathcal{C}
can be reconstructed experimentally from angular distributions in the decay products of the top and antitop quarks,
as discussed for example in
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
.
In the present theoretical work, we compute
\mathcal{C}
and consequently
B_{\max}
directly from the density matrix (
2.1
).

2.3
Construction of the spin density matrix from helicity amplitudes

We now specialize the general two-qubit formalism to the process

l^{-}l^{+}\to t\bar{t}

(l=e,\mu)
in the helicity basis.
Throughout this paper, helicities are denoted by
\pm
, corresponding to

\pm\tfrac{1}{2}
.

We denote the helicity amplitudes for this process by

\mathcal{M}(\lambda_{l^{-}},\lambda_{l^{+}};\lambda_{t},\lambda_{\bar{t}})
,
where
\lambda_{l^{-}}
,
\lambda_{l^{+}}
,
\lambda_{t}
, and
\lambda_{\bar{t}}

represent the helicities of the incoming lepton, incoming antilepton,
top quark, and antitop quark,
respectively.

Since we consider unpolarized initial leptons, we start directly from the
spin-averaged quantity.
We define the (unnormalized) spin matrix of the final
t\bar{t}
system as

R_{\lambda_{t}\lambda_{\bar{t}},\,\lambda_{t}^{\prime}\lambda_{\bar{t}}^{\prime}}\equiv\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\lambda_{l^{-}},\lambda_{l^{+}}}\mathcal{M}(\lambda_{l^{-}},\lambda_{l^{+}};\lambda_{t},\lambda_{\bar{t}})\,\mathcal{M}^{*}(\lambda_{l^{-}},\lambda_{l^{+}};\lambda_{t}^{\prime},\lambda_{\bar{t}}^{\prime}),

(2.18)

where
(\lambda_{t},\lambda_{\bar{t}})
and
(\lambda_{t}^{\prime},\lambda_{\bar{t}}^{\prime})
label
the row and column of the
4\times 4
matrix
R
.

The normalized spin density matrix relevant for entanglement observables is
obtained by dividing by the spin-summed rate,

\rho_{\lambda_{t}\lambda_{\bar{t}},\,\lambda_{t}^{\prime}\lambda_{\bar{t}}^{\prime}}=\frac{R_{\lambda_{t}\lambda_{\bar{t}},\,\lambda_{t}^{\prime}\lambda_{\bar{t}}^{\prime}}}{\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda_{t},\lambda_{\bar{t}}}R_{\lambda_{t}\lambda_{\bar{t}},\,\lambda_{t}\lambda_{\bar{t}}}}\,,

(2.19)

so that
\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho=1
by construction.

Once
\rho
is obtained, we decompose it in the Pauli basis as in Eq. (
2.1
).
The coefficients
B_{1i},B_{2i}
and
C_{ij}
are extracted from
\rho
via

\displaystyle B_{1i}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\rho\,(\sigma_{i}\otimes\mathbf{1})\right],

\displaystyle B_{2j}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\rho\,(\mathbf{1}\otimes\sigma_{j})\right],

\displaystyle C_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\rho\,(\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{j})\right].

(2.20)

For practical calculations, it is convenient to write the density matrix
explicitly in the helicity basis of the
t\bar{t}
system.
We choose the spin quantization axis along the top-quark momentum.
We order the basis states as

|1\rangle=|+,-\rangle,\qquad|2\rangle=|+,+\rangle,\qquad|3\rangle=|-,-\rangle,\qquad|4\rangle=|-,+\rangle,

where the first (second) entry denotes the helicity of the top (anti-top)
quark.
Since the spin quantization axis is defined by the top-quark momentum,
the helicity of the anti-top quark is evaluated with respect to the same axis.
As a result, the helicity states of the antitop quark correspond to spin projections
opposite to its momentum; namely, a positive (negative) helicity antitop has a
negative (positive) spin projection along its direction of motion.

In this basis, the density matrix can be written as

\rho=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{+-,+-}&\rho_{+-,++}&\rho_{+-,--}&\rho_{+-,-+}\\
\rho_{++,+-}&\rho_{++,++}&\rho_{++,--}&\rho_{++,-+}\\
\rho_{--,+-}&\rho_{--,++}&\rho_{--,--}&\rho_{--,-+}\\
\rho_{-+,+-}&\rho_{-+,++}&\rho_{-+,--}&\rho_{-+,-+}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.21)

For brevity we denote these elements as

\rho_{ij}

(i,j=1,\dots,4)
.
Since the density matrix is Hermitian,
\rho_{ij}=\rho_{ji}^{*}
,
it is convenient to express the polarization vectors and the
spin-correlation matrix in terms of the real and imaginary parts
of the matrix elements.
Using Eq. (
2.20
), we obtain

\displaystyle B_{1r}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{13}+\rho_{24}),

\displaystyle B_{1n}

\displaystyle=-2\,\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{13}+\rho_{24}),

\displaystyle B_{1k}

\displaystyle=\rho_{11}+\rho_{22}-\rho_{33}-\rho_{44},

(2.22)

\displaystyle B_{2r}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{12}+\rho_{34}),

\displaystyle B_{2n}

\displaystyle=-2\,\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{12}+\rho_{34}),

\displaystyle B_{2k}

\displaystyle=\rho_{11}-\rho_{22}+\rho_{33}-\rho_{44},

(2.23)

and

\displaystyle C_{rr}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{14}+\rho_{23}),

\displaystyle C_{rn}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{23}-\rho_{14}),

\displaystyle C_{rk}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{13}-\rho_{24}),

(2.24)

\displaystyle C_{nr}

\displaystyle=-2\,\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{14}+\rho_{23}),

\displaystyle C_{nn}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{23}-\rho_{14}),

\displaystyle C_{nk}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{24}-\rho_{13}),

(2.25)

\displaystyle C_{kr}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{12}-\rho_{34}),

\displaystyle C_{kn}

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{34}-\rho_{12}),

\displaystyle C_{kk}

\displaystyle=\rho_{11}-\rho_{22}-\rho_{33}+\rho_{44}.

(2.26)

In the following, the correlation coefficients
C_{ij}
are computed explicitly
for each theoretical scenario considered in this work.
To this end, in Sec.
4
we evaluate the helicity amplitudes for

l^{-}l^{+}\to t\bar{t}
in the SM and in the benchmark extensions,
and construct the spin matrix
R
defined in Eq. (
2.18
).
From the resulting density matrix
\rho
, the coefficients
C_{ij}
are
extracted using Eqs. (
2.24
)-(
2.26
) and subsequently employed in
Sec.
5
to evaluate the entanglement markers, the concurrence,
and the maximal CHSH parameter.

3
Models

In this section we specify the three scenarios considered in the paper and collect the interactions
relevant for
l^{-}l^{+}\to t\bar{t}

(l=e,\mu)
.

3.1
Scalar mediator extension of the SM

We first consider the SM extended by a
SU(2)
doublet scalar field
\Phi
with
U(1)_{Y}
charge
{1/2}
that
couples to a charged lepton
l=e,~\mu
, and to the top quark via Yukawa interactions.
The relevant Lagrangian terms are

\mathcal{L}\supset-m_{\Phi}^{2}\Phi^{\dagger}\Phi+\left\{-Y_{l}\,\bar{l}_{L}\Phi l_{R}-Y_{t}\,\bar{q}_{L}\tilde{\Phi}t_{R}+h.c.\right\},

(3.1)

where
\tilde{\Phi}=i\sigma_{2}\Phi^{*}
,
and
Y_{l}
and
Y_{t}
denote Yukawa couplings.
We assume that
\Phi
develops no vacuum expectation value.

In this scenario,
l^{-}l^{+}\to t\bar{t}
receives, in addition to the SM

\gamma
and
Z
exchange, an
s
-channel scalar-exchange contribution
proportional to
Y_{l}Y_{t}
, which we will denote by
\mathcal{M}^{(\Phi)}
.
Its helicity structure differs from that of vector and axial-vector exchange,
and in particular allows same-helicity final states that are suppressed in
the pure SM.
As we will see, this feature has a direct impact on the entanglement pattern
of the final-state spins.

3.2
Minimal gauged
U(1)_{B-L}
model

The second scenario is the minimal gauged
U(1)_{B-L}
extension of the
SM
[
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
,
45
,
46
]
.
The gauge group is enlarged to

SU(3)_{C}\times SU(2)_{L}\times U(1)_{Y}\times U(1)_{B-L},

(3.2)

and the SM fermions carry
B-L
charges equal to their baryon
(B)
minus lepton
(L)

number.
Anomaly cancellation requires the introduction of three right-handed neutrinos.
The
U(1)_{B-L}
symmetry is spontaneously broken by a scalar field
with
U(1)_{B-L}
charge
+2
, giving mass to the new gauge boson
Z^{\prime}
and generating
Majorana masses for the right-handed neutrinos.

The gauge-kinetic terms involving the two Abelian factors are

\mathcal{L}\supset-\frac{1}{4}B_{\mu\nu}B^{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{4}Z^{\prime}_{\mu\nu}Z^{\prime\mu\nu}-\frac{\sin\chi}{2}B_{\mu\nu}Z^{\prime\mu\nu},

(3.3)

where
B_{\mu}
and
Z^{\prime}_{\mu}
are the gauge fields of
U(1)_{Y}
and
U(1)_{B-L}
,
respectively, and
\chi
parametrizes kinetic mixing.
After electroweak and
B-L
symmetry breaking, the neutral gauge bosons mix
into the photon, the SM
Z
and the heavy
Z^{\prime}
boson.
In the simplest limit of vanishing kinetic mixing at the
B-L
breaking scale,
the mass eigenstate
Z^{\prime}
couples vectorially to fermions with strength
proportional to their
B-L
charge.

For the purposes of this work, we assume
\chi=0
in which the
relevant couplings of
Z^{\prime}
to leptons
and top quarks are

\mathcal{L}\supset-g_{B-L}Q_{B-L}^{l}\,\bar{l}\gamma^{\mu}l\,Z^{\prime}_{\mu}-g_{B-L}Q_{B-L}^{t}\,\bar{t}\gamma^{\mu}t\,Z^{\prime}_{\mu},

(3.4)

where
g_{B-L}
denotes the gauge coupling constant of the
U(1)_{B-L}
symmetry,
and
Q_{B-L}^{l}=-1
and
Q_{B-L}^{t}=1/3
are the corresponding
B\!-\!L
charges.
The mass and the
U(1)_{B-L}
coupling,

m_{Z^{\prime}}
and
g_{B-L}
, are treated as free parameters within the
experimental bounds.

In this scenario, the
l^{-}l^{+}\to t\bar{t}
amplitude receives a new
s
-channel
contribution from
Z^{\prime}
exchange, which interferes with the SM photon and

Z
amplitudes.
We denote this contribution by
\mathcal{M}^{(Z^{\prime})}
.

3.3
Randall-Sundrum model

As a third benchmark scenario, we consider the RS model with a single
warped extra dimension
[
47
]
.
In this framework, the five-dimensional spacetime is a slice of
anti-de Sitter space compactified on an
S^{1}/\mathbb{Z}_{2}
orbifold,
bounded by a Planck (UV) brane and a TeV (IR) brane.
The non-factorizable warped metric generates an exponential hierarchy
between the electroweak and Planck scales, thereby providing a geometrical
solution to the gauge hierarchy problem.

A characteristic feature of the RS model is that the zero-mode graviton and
its KK excitations have distinct wave-function profiles along
the extra dimension.
While the graviton zero mode is localized near the Planck brane and couples
to SM fields with the usual Planck-suppressed strength,
the KK gravitons are localized toward the TeV brane.
As a consequence, their couplings to SM fields residing on the TeV brane
are significantly enhanced and become relevant for collider phenomenology.

The effective interaction between the KK gravitons and the SM fields can be
written as
[
53
,
54
]

\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}=-\frac{1}{\bar{M}_{\rm Pl}}T^{\mu\nu}(x)\,h^{(0)}_{\mu\nu}(x)-\frac{1}{\Lambda_{\pi}}T^{\mu\nu}(x)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}h^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}(x),

(3.5)

where
h^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}
denotes the
n
-th KK graviton mode,

T^{\mu\nu}
is the energy-momentum tensor of the SM fields,
and
\Lambda_{\pi}=\bar{M}_{\rm Pl}e^{-\kappa r_{c}\pi}
is an effective
interaction scale of order the TeV scale with
\bar{M}_{\rm Pl}=2.4\times 10^{18}~{\rm GeV}

being the reduced Planck mass.
The contribution of the graviton zero mode is negligible for collider
processes, whereas each KK graviton couples to SM fields with strength
suppressed only by
\Lambda_{\pi}
.

The masses of the KK gravitons are given by

m_{n}=x_{n}\,\kappa\,e^{-\kappa r_{c}\pi},

(3.6)

where
x_{n}
are the roots of the Bessel function
J_{1}(x_{n})=0
,
with
x_{1}\simeq 3.83
,
x_{2}\simeq 7.02
,
x_{3}\simeq 10.17
, etc.
Once the mass of the lightest KK graviton
m_{1}
is fixed,
the entire KK spectrum is determined by
m_{n}=m_{1}(x_{n}/x_{1})
.
It is convenient to trade the parameters of the RS geometry for the
phenomenological input parameters
m_{1}
and
\kappa/\bar{M}_{\rm Pl}
,
with theoretical consistency requiring the five-dimensional curvature
\kappa
to be

\kappa/\bar{M}_{\rm Pl}\ll 1
,
typically
\kappa/\bar{M}_{\rm Pl}\lesssim 0.1

[
54
]
.

In the RS scenario, the process
l^{-}l^{+}\to t\bar{t}
receives additional
contributions from
s
-channel exchange of virtual KK gravitons.
The corresponding invariant amplitude can be written schematically as

\mathcal{M}^{(G)}=A(s)\,T^{\rm in}_{\mu\nu}(p_{1},p_{2})\eta^{\mu\rho}\eta^{\nu\sigma}\,T^{\rm out}_{\rho\sigma}(k_{1},k_{2}),

(3.7)

with
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
(
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
) denoting the momenta of the initial (final) particles, and

A(s)=-\frac{1}{\Lambda_{\pi}^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s-m_{n}^{2}+im_{n}\Gamma_{n}},\quad s=(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}=(k_{1}+k_{2})^{2},

(3.8)

where
\Gamma_{n}
denotes the total decay width of the
n
-th KK graviton given by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n}(h^{(n)}\rightarrow yy)={m_{n}x_{n}^{2}\over 16\pi}\left({\kappa\over\bar{M}_{\rm pl}}\right)^{2}\sum_{y}\Delta_{n}^{yy}\,,

(3.9)

with
\Delta_{n}^{yy}
being a dimensionless coefficient
for each decay mode, whose explicit forms are given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{\gamma\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{5},\quad\Delta_{n}^{gg}=\frac{8}{5},\quad\Delta_{n}^{\nu\bar{\nu}}=\frac{1}{10},

(3.10)

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{VV}

\displaystyle=c_{V}\,\sqrt{1-4r_{V}}\left(\frac{13}{12}+\frac{14}{3}r_{V}+4r_{V}^{2}\right),\quad(V=W,Z),

(3.11)

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{HH}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{30}(1-4r_{H})^{5/2},

(3.12)

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{f\bar{f}}

\displaystyle=c_{f}\,(1-4r_{f})^{3/2}\left(1+\frac{8}{3}r_{f}\right),\quad(f=l,q),

(3.13)

with

c_{W}=\frac{2}{5},\quad c_{Z}=\frac{1}{5},\quad c_{l}=\frac{1}{10},\quad c_{q}=\frac{3}{10},

(3.14)

and
r_{y}=m_{y}^{2}/m_{n}^{2}
for each SM particle
y
.
Expressions for leptons and quarks are for one flavor.

The energy momentum tensors are given by

\displaystyle T^{\text{in}}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\bar{v}(p_{2},\lambda_{l^{+}})\,V^{\text{in}}_{\mu\nu}\,u(p_{1},\lambda_{l^{-}}),

(3.15)

\displaystyle T^{\text{out}}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\bar{u}(k_{1},\lambda_{t})\,V^{\text{out}}_{\mu\nu}\,v(k_{2},\lambda_{\bar{t}}).

(3.16)

with

\displaystyle V^{\text{in}}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left[(p_{1}-p_{2})_{\mu}\gamma_{\nu}+(p_{1}-p_{2})_{\nu}\gamma_{\mu}+\eta_{\mu\nu}\left(-\frac{1}{2}(p_{1}-p_{2})_{\rho}\gamma^{\rho}\right)\right],

(3.17)

\displaystyle V^{\text{out}}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left[(k_{1}-k_{2})_{\mu}\gamma_{\nu}+(k_{1}-k_{2})_{\nu}\gamma_{\mu}+\eta_{\mu\nu}\left(-\frac{1}{2}(k_{1}-k_{2})_{\rho}\gamma^{\rho}+m_{t}\right)\right],

(3.18)

where
\gamma^{\mu}
are the gamma matrices,
m_{t}
denotes the top-quark mass,

\eta_{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)
is the Minkowski metric,
and
u(p,\lambda)
and
v(p,\lambda)
denote helicity spinors
given in Eqs. (
4.6
)–(
4.9
) in Sec.
4.1
.

The exchange of spin-2 KK gravitons induces characteristic angular
distributions and helicity structures that are qualitatively different
from those arising from scalar or vector mediators.
As a result, the RS model can lead to distinctive modifications of the
spin correlations and quantum-entanglement properties of the produced

t\bar{t}
system.
In our analysis, we include KK modes up to
i=4
, since higher modes
develop large decay widths and cease to appear as well-defined resonances,
making their individual contributions difficult to identify.

4
Helicity amplitude and density matrix for
l^{-}l^{+}\to t\bar{t}

In this section, we present the helicity amplitudes for
l^{-}l^{+}\to t\bar{t}

(l=e,\mu)

in the SM and in the new-physics scenarios. The construction of the spin
density matrix and quantum-information observables has been defined in
Sec.
2
.

4.1
Kinematics and spinor conventions

We work in the center-of-mass frame of the incoming leptons, with 4-momenta

\displaystyle p_{1}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{s}}{2}(1,0,0,1),

(4.1)

\displaystyle p_{2}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{s}}{2}(1,0,0,-1),

(4.2)

\displaystyle k_{1}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{s}}{2}(1,\beta\sin\theta\cos\phi,\beta\sin\theta\sin\phi,\beta\cos\theta),

(4.3)

\displaystyle k_{2}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{s}}{2}(1,-\beta\sin\theta\cos\phi,-\beta\sin\theta\sin\phi,-\beta\cos\theta),

(4.4)

where

\beta=\sqrt{1-\frac{4m_{t}^{2}}{s}}

(4.5)

is the top-quark velocity in the center-of-mass frame.
The angle
\theta
is the scattering angle of the top quark with respect to
the
l^{-}
beam,
taken along the
z
axis.
The azimuthal angle
\phi
is measured around the beam axis from the
x
axis, so that the scattering plane is specified by the plane spanned by
the incoming
lepton
momentum and the top-quark momentum.

We use spinors in the helicity basis for a fermion with mass
m
and four-momentum

p^{\mu}=(E,\bm{p})
, where
E=\sqrt{|\bm{p}|^{2}+m^{2}}
, given by

\displaystyle u(p,+1/2)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\sqrt{E-|\bf p|}\,\chi(\hat{\bm{p}})\\
-\sqrt{E+|\bf p|}\,\chi(\hat{\bm{p}})\end{pmatrix},

(4.6)

\displaystyle u(p,-1/2)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{E+|\bf p|}\,\xi(\hat{\bm{p}})\\
\sqrt{E-|\bf p|}\,\xi(\hat{\bm{p}})\end{pmatrix},

(4.7)

\displaystyle v(p,+1/2)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\sqrt{E+|\bf p|}\,\xi(\hat{\bm{p}})\\
\sqrt{E-|\bf p|}\,\xi(\hat{\bm{p}})\end{pmatrix},

(4.8)

\displaystyle v(p,-1/2)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{E-|\bf p|}\,\chi(\hat{\bm{p}})\\
-\sqrt{E+|\bf p|}\,\chi(\hat{\bm{p}})\end{pmatrix},

(4.9)

where
\hat{\bm{p}}=\bm{p}/|\bm{p}|
is the direction of the three-momentum.
The two-component helicity spinors are

\chi(\hat{\bm{p}})=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\\
e^{i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\xi(\hat{\bm{p}})=\begin{pmatrix}-e^{-i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\\
\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix},

(4.10)

with
(\theta,\phi)
the polar and azimuthal angles of
\hat{\bm{p}}
.
For the present calculation, we choose the scattering plane to coincide with the
x
-
z
plane.
Accordingly, the initial
lepton and antilepton
are taken along the
+z
and
-z
directions, corresponding to
(\theta,\phi)=(0,0)
and
(\pi,0)
, respectively.
The final-state top quark is assigned
(\theta,\phi)=(\theta,0)
, while the anti-top quark is assigned
(\pi-\theta,\pi)
.
This choice entails no loss of generality, since the azimuthal dependence is absorbed into the definition of the orthonormal basis
(\hat{r},\hat{n},\hat{k})
introduced in Eq. (
2.2
). Consequently, all spin-dependent observables depend only on the polar angle
\theta
.

Our convention for the fermion spinors is based on the HELAS convention
[
55
]
.
However, it differs from the HELAS convention by an overall sign in the right-handed particle spinors.
This difference originates from a convention-dependent phase choice in the definition of the spinors.
In the present work, we adopt a convention in which this sign is explicitly retained.
With this choice, the resulting density matrix satisfies the relation,

R_{\lambda_{t}\lambda_{\bar{t}},\lambda^{\prime}_{t}\lambda^{\prime}_{\bar{t}}}=R_{\lambda_{\bar{t}}\lambda_{t},\lambda^{\prime}_{\bar{t}}\lambda^{\prime}_{t}}.

(4.11)

if the interaction is CP invariant
[
5
]
.
Although the interactions considered in this work are not CP invariant,
we adopt this convention so that the above relation holds in the CP-conserving limit,
such as in QCD.

4.2
SM, scalar,
Z^{\prime}
and graviton contributions to the amplitude

The full helicity amplitude can be written as

\mathcal{M}({\lambda_{l^{-}},\lambda_{l^{+}};\lambda_{t},\lambda_{\bar{t}}})=\mathcal{M}^{\rm(SM)}({\lambda_{l^{-}},\lambda_{l^{+}};\lambda_{t},\lambda_{\bar{t}}})+\mathcal{M}^{(X)}({\lambda_{l^{-}},\lambda_{l^{+}};\lambda_{t},\lambda_{\bar{t}}}).

(4.12)

As introduced above, we denote the helicities
-1/2
and
+1/2
by
-
and
+
, respectively.
Here
\mathcal{M}^{(\rm SM)}
denotes the SM contribution, while

\mathcal{M}^{(X)}
represents the contribution from physics beyond the SM,
with
X=\Phi
for the scalar mediator,
X=Z^{\prime}
for the
U(1)_{B-L}
model,
and
X=G
for the RS scenario, respectively.

The first term in the right-hand side is of SM, which is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(\rm SM)}(+,-;\pm,\pm)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}\,\sin\theta\,\left[e^{2}Q_{l}Q_{t}+{s\,g_{Z}^{Rl}\over 2}\frac{g_{Z}^{Lt}+g_{Z}^{Rt}}{s-m_{Z}^{2}+im_{Z}\Gamma_{Z}}\right],

(4.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(\rm SM)}(-,+;\pm,\pm)

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}\,\sin\theta\,\left[e^{2}Q_{l}Q_{t}+{s\,g_{Z}^{Ll}\over 2}\frac{g_{Z}^{Lt}+g_{Z}^{Rt}}{s-m_{Z}^{2}+im_{Z}\Gamma_{Z}}\right],

(4.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(\rm SM)}(+,-;\pm,\mp)

\displaystyle=\left(1\pm\cos\theta\right)\left[e^{2}Q_{l}Q_{t}+{s\,g_{Z}^{Rl}\over 2}\frac{\,g_{Z}^{Lt}(1\mp\beta)+g_{Z}^{Rt}(1\pm\beta)}{s-m_{Z}^{2}+im_{Z}\Gamma_{Z}}\right],

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(\rm SM)}(-,+;\pm,\mp)

\displaystyle=(1\mp\cos\theta)\left[\,e^{2}Q_{l}Q_{t}+{s\,g_{Z}^{Ll}\over 2}\frac{g_{Z}^{Lt}(1\mp\beta)+g_{Z}^{Rt}(1\pm\beta)}{s-m_{Z}^{2}+im_{Z}\Gamma_{Z}}\right],

where
m_{Z}
and
\Gamma_{Z}
is the mass and the decay width of
Z
boson, and

\displaystyle\displaystyle Q_{l}=-1,\quad Q_{t}=\frac{2}{3},

(4.17)

\displaystyle\displaystyle g_{Z}^{La}={e\over\cos\theta_{W}\sin\theta_{W}}\left(T_{3}^{a}-Q_{a}\sin^{2}\theta_{W}\right),\quad g_{Z}^{Ra}=-{eQ_{a}\sin\theta_{W}\over\cos\theta_{W}},

(4.18)

with
a=l,t
and

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{3}^{l}=-{1\over 2},\quad T_{3}^{t}={1\over 2}.

(4.19)

The additional contributions from the scalar are as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(\rm\Phi)}(\pm,\pm;\pm,\pm)

\displaystyle=-{s\over 2}\frac{\,Y_{l}Y_{t}\,(1-\beta)}{s-m_{\Phi}^{2}+im_{\Phi}\Gamma_{\Phi}},

(4.20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(\rm\Phi)}(\pm,\pm;\mp,\mp)

\displaystyle={s\over 2}\frac{\,Y_{l}Y_{t}\,(1+\beta)}{s-m_{\Phi}^{2}+im_{\Phi}\Gamma_{\Phi}},

(4.21)

For the benchmark choice
m_{\Phi}=200~\mathrm{GeV}
adopted in our numerical analysis,
the channel
\Phi\to t\bar{t}
is closed kinematically, and we assume no other relevant decay modes.
Hence
\Phi
decays only into
l^{-}l^{+}

(l=e,\mu)

\Gamma_{\Phi}\simeq\Gamma(\Phi\to l^{-}l^{+})=\frac{|Y_{l}|^{2}\,m_{\Phi}}{16\pi}.

(4.22)

For the
U(1)_{B-L}
scenario, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(Z^{\prime})}(\pm,\mp;+,+)

\displaystyle=\pm\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}\sin\theta\frac{s\,g_{B-L}^{2}Q^{l}_{B-L}Q^{t}_{B-L}}{s-m_{Z^{\prime}}^{2}+im_{Z^{\prime}}\Gamma_{Z^{\prime}}},

(4.23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(Z^{\prime})}(\pm,\mp;-,-)

\displaystyle=\pm\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}\sin\theta\frac{s\,g_{B-L}^{2}Q^{l}_{B-L}Q^{t}_{B-L}}{s-m_{Z^{\prime}}^{2}+im_{Z^{\prime}}\Gamma_{Z^{\prime}}},

(4.24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(Z^{\prime})}(\pm,\mp;+,-)

\displaystyle=(1\pm\cos\theta)\frac{s\,g_{B-L}^{2}Q^{l}_{B-L}Q^{t}_{B-L}}{s-m_{Z^{\prime}}^{2}+im_{Z^{\prime}}\Gamma_{Z^{\prime}}},

(4.25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(Z^{\prime})}(\pm,\mp;-,+)

\displaystyle=(1\mp\cos\theta)\frac{s\,g_{B-L}^{2}Q^{l}_{B-L}Q^{t}_{B-L}}{s-m_{Z^{\prime}}^{2}+im_{Z^{\prime}}\Gamma_{Z^{\prime}}},

(4.26)

where
m_{Z^{\prime}}
is the mass of
Z^{\prime}
boson and
\Gamma_{Z^{\prime}}
is the decay width of
Z^{\prime}
given by
\Gamma_{Z^{\prime}}={13\over 24\pi}g_{B-L}^{2}m_{Z^{\prime}}
.
This has the same Lorentz structure as photon exchange but with different
couplings and a massive propagator.

For the RS scenario, the additional contribution arises from

s
-channel exchange of KK gravitons.
The corresponding helicity amplitudes can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(G)}(\pm,\mp;+,+)

\displaystyle=\mp{s^{2}\beta\over 4}A(s)\,\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}\,\sin\theta\cos\theta,

(4.27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(G)}(\pm,\mp;-,-)

\displaystyle=\mp{s^{2}\beta\over 4}A(s)\,\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}\,\sin\theta\cos\theta,

(4.28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(G)}(\pm,\mp;+,-)

\displaystyle=\mp{s^{2}\beta\over 8}A(s)\,(2\cos^{2}\theta\pm\cos\theta-1),

(4.29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(G)}(\pm,\mp;-,+)

\displaystyle=\pm{s^{2}\beta\over 8}A(s)\,(2\cos^{2}\theta\mp\cos\theta-1).

(4.30)

Unlike the scalar and vector mediators, the exchange of a spin-2 KK graviton
induces helicity structures characteristic of its tensorial coupling to the
energy-momentum tensor.
As seen from Eqs. (
4.27
)–(
4.30
),
the angular dependence exhibits nontrivial combinations such as

\cos^{2}\theta
and
\sin\theta\cos\theta
, which are absent in scalar or
vector exchanges.

Moreover, the overall energy dependence encoded in
A(s)
differs
significantly from that of scalar or vector mediators, effectively scaling as
s
at the amplitude level.
This distinct behavior reflects the
higher-dimensional origin of the interaction and can lead to qualitatively
different modifications of spin correlations and entanglement observables,
especially at high energies.

While the
U(1)_{B-L}
contribution has the same Lorentz structure as photon
exchange, differing only by the couplings and the massive propagator,
the RS contribution represents a genuinely new tensor interaction.
As a consequence, the RS scenario provides a qualitatively distinct benchmark
for studying the sensitivity of quantum-entanglement observables to the spin
and Lorentz structure of new physics.

6
Discussion and conclusions

In this work, we have performed a systematic study of quantum entanglement
and Bell-inequality violation in
l^{-}l^{+}\to t\bar{t}

(l=e,\mu)
at future lepton colliders
such as the ILC and multi-TeV muon colliders,
within the SM and three representative extensions involving neutral

s
-channel mediators of different Lorentz structures.
Working in a unified density-matrix framework, we evaluated the entanglement marker,
the concurrence, and the maximal CHSH Bell parameter on equal footing.

Our analysis reveals that new interactions do not merely enhance or suppress
entanglement in a uniform way, but rather reorganize the spin correlations
in characteristic patterns determined by their Lorentz structure.
The scalar mediator leads to an overall reduction of entanglement,
reflecting its helicity structure and the absence of strong interference
with SM vector amplitudes.
In contrast, the
U(1)_{B-L}
model produces energy-dependent distortions
through interference effects, resulting in a redistribution of entangled regions
in the
(\sqrt{s},\theta)
plane.
The RS scenario exhibits the most distinctive behavior,
where the tensorial nature of KK graviton exchange induces qualitatively new
high-energy structures in the spin-correlation pattern.

We further find that the CHSH parameter provides information complementary to that of the entanglement marker and concurrence.
In the SM, as well as in the
U(1)_{B-L}
and RS scenarios, the maximal CHSH parameter exceeds the classical bound

B_{\max}=2
over broad angular regions, indicating persistent Bell-inequality violation throughout much of the accessible phase space.
By contrast, in the scalar-mediator model the violation is much more limited:
for
\sqrt{s}=500
and
1000~\mathrm{GeV}
, the CHSH parameter stays below the classical bound over the entire angular range,
whereas clear violation appears only at higher center-of-mass energies, where it is confined to narrow angular intervals.
This behavior reflects the distinct Lorentz structures of the underlying interactions: vector and tensor exchanges efficiently generate
strong nonlocal spin correlations over wide kinematic domains, whereas the scalar contribution enhances them only selectively and becomes more visible at high energies.
Taken together, the entanglement marker, concurrence, and CHSH parameter therefore provide complementary diagnostics of
how new physics reshapes quantum correlations in heavy-fermion production.

Future lepton colliders provide a uniquely clean environment for probing
quantum entanglement with
t\bar{t}
final state.
In particular, polarized beams would allow direct control over the initial-state
helicity configuration and could significantly enhance the sensitivity to the
chiral and Lorentz structure of new interactions
[
59
]
.
Incorporating realistic reconstruction and detector effects will be essential
for assessing the experimental feasibility of measuring these quantum-information
observables in practice.
These results highlight the potential of quantum-information observables
as conceptually novel and practically powerful probes of new physics in
top-quark pair production.
