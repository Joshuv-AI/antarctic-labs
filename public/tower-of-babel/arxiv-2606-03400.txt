Monadic and cylindric expansions of bounded implication algebras
Joseph McDonald

Abstract
. Implication algebras were introduced by Abbott as algebraic models of the operation of Boolean implication in the classical propositional calculus. In this work, we study additional operators and constants on bounded implication algebras by introducing monadic and cylindric implication algebras. It is demonstrated that the category 𝐌𝐈𝐀 \mathbf{MIA} of monadic implication algebras is isomorphic to the category 𝐌𝐁𝐀 \mathbf{MBA} of monadic Boolean algebras and moreover, that the category 𝐂𝐈𝐀 \mathbf{CIA} of I I -dimensional cylindric implication algebras is isomorphic to the category 𝐂𝐁𝐀 \mathbf{CBA} of I I -dimensional cylindric Boolean algebras. As an application of the obtained categorical isomorphisms, we provide spectral duality results for I I -dimensional cylindric implication algebras along the lines of Bezhanishvili and Holliday’s spectral duality for Boolean algebras combined with McDonald’s extension of their duality to monadic and I I -dimensional cylindric Boolean algebras. Keywords: Bounded implication algebra, Monadic and cylindric Boolean algebra; Duality theory.



1.
Introduction

Implication algebras were introduced by Abbott
[
3
]
as algebraic models of the operation of Boolean implication in the classical propositional calculus. An
implication algebra
is a binar
\langle A;\cdot\rangle
satisfying the contraction, quasi-commutativity, and exchange equations. Abbott demonstrated that every implication algebra can be converted into a semi-Boolean algebra (i.e., a join-semilattice with a greatest element in which every principal filter forms a Boolean algebra) and conversely, that every semi-Boolean algebra gives rise to an implication algebra under the semi-Boolean algebra polynomial
\pi(x,y):=(x\vee y)^{\prime}_{y}
where
(x\vee y)^{\prime}_{y}
is the Boolean complement of
x\vee y
in the principal filter generated by
y
. Abbott additionally stated (without giving an explicit proof) that by adjoining a certain constant to an implication algebra (resulting in a bounded implication algebra), which determines the least element in the induced join-semilattice, then one obtains a Boolean algebra, and moreover, that every Boolean algebra
B
can be converted into a bounded implication algebra
\langle B;\pi_{c},0\rangle
under
\pi_{c}(x,y):=x^{\prime}\vee y
. Section 2 of this paper is concerned with giving explicit constructions of these latter claims and then extending these results to that of an isomorphism between the category
\mathbf{IA_{0,1}}
of bounded implication algebras and the category
\mathbf{BA}
of Boolean algebras.

We then investigate bounded implication algebras within the setting of monadic and cylindric Boolean algebras. Monadic Boolean algebras were introduced by Halmos
[
5
]
as algebraic models of the classical single-variable predicate calculus. A
monadic Boolean algebra
consists of a Boolean algebra
B
equipped with a closure operator
\exists\colon B\to B
, known as a
quantifier
, whose closed elements form a Boolean subalgebra. Cylindric Boolean algebras were introduced by Henkin, Monk, and Tarski
[
6
,
7
]
as algebraic models of the classical
n
-variable predicate calculus with identity. An
I-dimensional cylindric Boolean algebra
consists of a Boolean algebra
B
equipped with a family
(\exists_{i})_{i\in I}\colon B\to B
of pairwise commuting quantifiers and a family
(\delta_{i,k})_{i,k\in I}
of constants, known as the
diagonal elements
, which are subject to certain conditions that algebraically model identity in a first-order language. Closely related to the monadic and cylindric Boolean algebras are the polyadic Boolean algebras introduced by Halmos
[
5
]
as algebraic models of the classical
n
-variable predicate calculus without identity.

In Section 3, we introduce monadic implication algebras. After establishing some of their basic algebraic properties, we demonstrate that the category
\mathbf{MIA}
of monadic implication algebras is isomorphic to the category
\mathbf{MBA}
of monadic Boolean algebras. Monadic implication algebras have been considered elsewhere (such as in
[
1
]
), however they study monadic implication algebras from the perspective of universal quantifiers (i.e., interior operators whose open elements form Boolean subalgebras), whereas we study monadic implication algebras from the perspective of existential quantifiers. More importantly, whereas the monadic implication algebras considered here are bounded, the monadic implication algebras considered in
[
1
]
are not assumed to be bounded. Finally, we introduce
I
-dimensional cylindric implication algebras. It is demonstrated that the category
\mathbf{CIA}
of
I
-dimensional cylindric implication algebras is isomorphic to the category
\mathbf{CBA}
of
I
-dimensional cylindric Boolean algebras.

In Section 4, as an application of the obtained categorical isomorphisms, we provide a duality result for
I
-dimensional cylindric implication algebras along the lines of Bezhanishvili and Holliday’s spectral duality for Boolean algebras
[
4
]
combined with McDonald’s extension of their duality to monadic Boolean algebras and
I
-dimensional cylindric Boolean algebras
[
9
]
.

2.1.
Implication algebras and semi-Boolean algebras

In this subsection, we discuss some basic details of implication algebras and describe the construction given in
[
3
]
which associates to every implication algebra, a semi-Boolean algebra.

Definition 2.1
.

An
implication algebra
is a binar
\langle A;\cdot\rangle
satisfying:

(1)

(x\cdot y)\cdot x=x
(contraction)

(2)

(x\cdot y)\cdot y=(y\cdot x)\cdot x
(quasi-commutativity)

(3)

x\cdot(y\cdot z)=y\cdot(x\cdot z)
(exchange)

The following are immediate consequences of the axioms of implication algebras.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
3
]
)
.

Any implication algebra satisfies:

(1)

x\cdot(x\cdot y)=x\cdot y

(2)

x\cdot x=(x\cdot y)\cdot(x\cdot y)

Proof.

Definition
2.1
(1) yields
x\cdot(x\cdot y)=((x\cdot y)\cdot x)\cdot(x\cdot y)=x\cdot y
which implies
x\cdot x=((x\cdot y)\cdot x)\cdot x=(x\cdot(x\cdot y))\cdot(x\cdot y)=(x\cdot y)\cdot(x\cdot y)
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.3

(
[
3
]
)
.

Any implication algebra
A
admits of a constant
c_{1}\in A
satisfying
x\cdot x=c_{1}
,
x\cdot c_{1}=c_{1}
, and
c_{1}\cdot x=x
.

Proof.

Proposition
2.2
along with Definition
2.1
(2) yields the following equality:

x\cdot x=(x\cdot y)\cdot(x\cdot y)=((x\cdot y)\cdot y)\cdot((x\cdot y)\cdot y)=((y\cdot x)\cdot x)\cdot((y\cdot x)\cdot x)=(y\cdot x)\cdot(y\cdot x)=x\cdot x.

This implies the existence of a constant
c_{1}=x\cdot x
that is independent of
x
so that
c_{1}
is well-defined. Moreover, we have
c_{1}\cdot x=(x\cdot x)\cdot x=x
by Definition
2.1
(1) and
x\cdot c_{1}=x\cdot(x\cdot x)=x\cdot x=c_{1}
by Proposition
2.2
(1).
∎

Lemma 2.4

(
[
3
]
)
.

Every implication algebra
A
determines a poset
\langle A;\preceq\rangle
under
x\preceq y
iff
x\cdot y=c_{1}
. Moreover, the constant
c_{1}\in A
is the greatest element in this poset. In addition,
\langle A;\oplus\rangle
is a join-semilattice under
x\oplus y:=(x\cdot y)\cdot y
.

A
semi-Boolean algebra
is a bounded join-semilattice
\langle B;\vee,1\rangle
such that
{\uparrow}x=\{y\in B:x\leq y\}
is a Boolean algebra for each
x\in B
. The following associates to each implication algebra, a semi-Boolean algebra.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
A
be an implication algebra and let
\langle A;\oplus,c_{1}\rangle
be the bounded join-semilattice obtained from
A
via Lemma
2.4
. Then
\langle{\uparrow}z;\oplus,\odot,-,z,c_{1}\rangle
is a Boolean algebra for any
z\in A
under
x\odot y:=(x\cdot(y\cdot z))\cdot z
and
-x:=x\cdot z
.

Theorem 2.6

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
A
be a semi-Boolean algebra and let
z\in A
. Then
\langle{\uparrow}z;\pi_{c}^{*}\rangle
is an implication algebra under
\pi_{c}^{*}(x,y):=(x\vee y)^{\prime}_{z}
where
(x\vee y)^{\prime}_{z}
is the Boolean complement of
x\vee y
in the Boolean algebra
{\uparrow}z
.

Definition 2.7
.

A
bounded implication algebra
is an implication algebra
A
equipped with a constant element
c_{0}\in A
satisfying
c_{0}\cdot x=1
for all
x\in A
.

Note that for any bounded implication algebra
A
, the constant
c_{0}\in A
is the least element in the poset
\langle A;\preceq\rangle
. The following is due to Abbott
[
3
]
but was stated without proof. Hence, we fill in the details for the sake of clarity.

Theorem 2.8
.

If
B
is a Boolean algebra,
\langle B;\pi_{c}\rangle
is a bounded implication algebra under
\pi_{c}(x,y):=x^{\prime}\vee y
.

Proof.

We first verify that
\pi_{c}
satisfies the contraction equation by checking that
\pi_{c}(\pi_{c}(x,y),x)=x
. The definition of
\pi_{c}
, De Morgan’s identities, the absorption identities, and the fact that
′
is an involution yields:

\pi_{c}(\pi_{c}(x,y),x)=\pi_{c}(x^{\prime}\vee y,x)=(x^{\prime}\vee y)^{\prime}\vee x=(x^{\prime\prime}\wedge y^{\prime})\vee x=(x\wedge y^{\prime})\vee x=x.

We now verify the quasi-commutativity equation by checking that
\pi_{c}(\pi_{c}(x,y),y)=\pi_{c}(\pi_{c}(y,x),x)
:

\displaystyle\pi_{c}(\pi_{c}(x,y),y)

\displaystyle=\pi_{c}((x^{\prime}\vee y),y)=(x^{\prime}\vee y)^{\prime}\vee y=(x^{\prime\prime}\wedge y^{\prime})\vee y=(x\wedge y^{\prime})\vee y

\displaystyle=(x\vee y)\wedge(y^{\prime}\vee y)=(x\vee y)\wedge 1=x\vee y.

Since
x\vee y=y\vee x
, reversing the above calculation applied to
y\vee x
yields
y\vee x=\pi_{c}(\pi_{c}(y,x),x)
and hence we obtain
\pi_{c}(\pi_{c}(x,y),y)=\pi_{c}(\pi_{c}(y,x),x)
. Finally, we now verify that
\pi_{c}
satisfies the exchange equation by checking that
\pi_{c}(x,\pi_{c}(y,z))=\pi_{c}(y,\pi_{c}(x,z))
. The calculation proceeds as follows:

\displaystyle\pi_{c}(x,\pi_{c}(y,z))

\displaystyle=\pi_{c}(x,y^{\prime}\vee z)=x^{\prime}\vee(y^{\prime}\vee z)=(x^{\prime}\vee y^{\prime})\vee z=(y^{\prime}\vee x^{\prime})\vee z

\displaystyle=y^{\prime}\vee(x^{\prime}\vee z)=y^{\prime}\vee(\pi_{c}(x,z))=\pi_{c}(y,\pi_{c}(x,z)).

This completes the proof that
\langle B;\pi_{c}\rangle
is an implication algebra. To see that
\langle B;\pi_{c}\rangle
is bounded, observe that
\pi_{c}(0,x)=0^{\prime}\vee x=1\vee x=1
for all
x\in B
.
∎

Theorem 2.9
.

Every bounded implication algebra can be converted into a Boolean algebra.

Proof.

The proof follows from Theorem
2.5
by setting
z=c_{0}
so that
{\uparrow}z=A
.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}=\langle B;\wedge,\vee,^{\prime},0,1\rangle

\mathfrak{B}^{+}=\langle B;\pi_{c},0\rangle

\mathfrak{B}=(\mathfrak{B}^{+})_{+}

Proof.

Note that the carrier set of
\mathfrak{B}
is identical to the carrier set of
(\mathfrak{B}^{+})_{+}
and
x\leq y
iff
\pi_{c}(x,y)=1
iff
x\preceq y
. Moreover,
x^{\prime}=x^{\prime}\vee 0=\pi_{c}(x,0)=-x
as well as:

\displaystyle x\wedge y

\displaystyle=(x^{\prime}\vee y^{\prime})^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}\vee(y^{\prime}\vee 0))^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}\vee(y^{\prime}\vee 0))^{\prime}\vee 0=\pi_{c}((x^{\prime}\vee(y^{\prime}\vee 0)),0)

\displaystyle=\pi_{c}(\pi_{c}(x,y^{\prime}\vee 0),0)=\pi_{c}(\pi_{c}(x,\pi_{c}(y,0)),0)=x\odot y

\displaystyle x\vee y

\displaystyle=(x\vee y)\wedge 1=(x\vee y)\wedge(y\vee y^{\prime})=(x^{\prime\prime}\vee y)\wedge(y\vee y^{\prime})=(x^{\prime\prime}\wedge y^{\prime})\vee y

\displaystyle=(x^{\prime}\vee y)^{\prime}\vee y=\pi_{c}(x^{\prime}\vee y,y)=\pi_{c}(\pi_{c}(x,y),y)=x\oplus y.

This completes the proof.
∎

Definition 2.13
.

Let

3.1.
Monadic and cylindric Boolean algebras

In this subsection, we describe some of the basic aspects of monadic and cylindric Boolean algebras. For more details pertaining to the former, consult
[
5
]
and for the latter, consult
[
6
,

]
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
monadic Boolean algebra
is an algebra
\langle A;\wedge,\vee,^{\prime},0,1,\exists\rangle
such that:

(1)

\langle A;\wedge,\vee,^{\prime},0,1\rangle
is a Boolean algebra;

(2)

\exists\colon A\to A
is a unary operator, known as a
quantifier
, satisfying:

(a)

\exists 0=0
and
x\leq\exists x
;

(b)

\exists(x\wedge\exists y)=\exists x\wedge\exists y
.

The motivating example of a monadic Boolean algebra arises by considering the function space from a non-empty set into a complete Boolean algebra.

Example 3.2
.

Let
X
be a non-empty set and let
B
be a complete Boolean algebra. Then the algebra
\langle B^{X};\cdot,+,-,c_{0},c_{1}\rangle
where
B^{X}
is the function space from
X
to
B
and

(1)

(f\cdot g)(x)=f(x)\wedge g(x)
;

(2)

(f+g)(x)=f(x)\vee g(x)
;

(3)

-f(x)=f(x)^{\prime}
;

(4)

c_{0}(x)=0
,
c_{1}(x)=1
,

is a Boolean algebra, known as the
full functional Boolean algebra
determined by
X
and
B
. Defining:

\exists\colon B^{X}\to B^{X};\hskip 11.38092pt(\exists f)(x)=\bigvee\text{ran}(f)=\bigvee\{f(x):x\in X\},

induces a quantifier on
B^{X}
making
\langle B^{X};\cdot,+,-,c_{0},c_{1},\exists\rangle
a monadic Boolean algebra, known as the
full functional monadic Boolean algebra
determined by
X
and
B
.

Definition 3.3
.

An
I-dimensional cylindric Boolean algebra
is an algebra:

\langle A;\wedge,\vee,^{\prime},0,1,(\exists_{i})_{i\in I},(\delta_{i,k})_{i,k\in I}\rangle

satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

\langle A;\wedge,\vee,^{\prime},0,1,\exists_{i}\rangle
is a monadic Boolean algebra for each
i\in I
;

(2)

\exists_{i}\exists_{k}x=\exists_{k}\exists_{i}x
for all
i,k\in I
;

(3)

(\delta_{i,k})_{i,k\in I}
is a family of constants, known as the
diagonal elements
, satisfying:

(a)

\delta_{i,k}=\delta_{k,i}
and
\delta_{i,i}=1
;

(b)

i,l\not=k\Rightarrow\exists_{k}(\delta_{i,k}\wedge\delta_{k,l})=\delta_{i,l}
;

(c)

i\not=k\Rightarrow\exists_{i}(d_{i,k}\wedge x)\wedge\exists_{i}(d_{i,k}\wedge x^{\prime})=0
.

We call the reduct
\langle A;\wedge,\vee,^{\perp},0,1,(\exists_{i})_{i\in I}\rangle
the
I-dimensional diagonal-free cylindric Boolean algebra reduct
of
A
provided conditions 1-2 are satisfied.

Example 3.4
.

I
-dimensional cylindric Boolean algebras arise from taking the Boolean algebra induced by the function space
X^{I}
where
X
is a non-empty set. Each
f\in X^{I}
is an
I
-indexed family and for any subset
F\subseteq X^{I}
, its cylindrification
\exists_{i}F
is the set of all such indexed families
g
that agree with some
f\in F
expect possibly at the coordinate
i
. The diagonal
\delta_{i,k}
is then given by
d_{i,k}=\{f:f(i)=f(k)\}
.

3.2.
Monadic implication algebras

We now introduce monadic implication algebras and establish some of their basic properties.

Definition 3.5
.

A
monadic implication algebra
is a bounded implication algebra
A
equipped with an additional operator
\nabla\colon A\to A
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

\nabla c_{0}=c_{0}
and
x\cdot\nabla x=c_{1}
;

(2)

\nabla((x\cdot(\nabla y\cdot c_{0}))\cdot c_{0})=((\nabla x\cdot(\nabla y\cdot c_{0}))\cdot c_{0})
.

The following collects some useful facts about monadic implication algebras.

Proposition 3.6
.

In any monadic implication algebra,
\nabla
preserves the constant
c_{1}
and is idempotent.

Proof.

Part 2 of Definition
3.5
(1) yields
c_{1}\cdot\nabla c_{1}=c_{1}
and Proposition
2.3
gives
\nabla c_{1}\cdot c_{1}=c_{1}
and hence
\nabla c_{1}=c_{1}
. To see that
\nabla
is idempotent, observe that by Definition
2.7
, Proposition
2.3
, and Definition
3.5
(2):

(1)

\nabla x=c_{1}\cdot\nabla x=(c_{0}\cdot\nabla x)\cdot\nabla x=(\nabla x\cdot c_{0})\cdot c_{0}=(c_{1}\cdot(\nabla x\cdot c_{0}))\cdot c_{0}

Since
\nabla c_{1}=c_{1}
, Definition
3.5
(2) along with Equation (1) yields:

\nabla\nabla x=\nabla((c_{1}\cdot(\nabla x\cdot c_{0}))\cdot c_{0})=(\nabla c_{1}\cdot(\nabla x\cdot c_{0}))\cdot c_{0}=(c_{1}\cdot(\nabla x\cdot c_{0}))\cdot c_{0}=\nabla x.

This completes the proof.
∎

If
A
is a monadic implication algebra, let
\text{ran}(\nabla):=\{\nabla x:x\in A\}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

In any monadic implication algebra
A
, we have:

\text{ran}(\nabla)=\{x\in A:\nabla x=x\}.

Proof.

Suppose that
x\in\text{ran}(\nabla)
so that
x=\nabla y
for some
y\in A
. Then since
\nabla
is idempotent by Proposition
3.6
, we obtain
\nabla x=\nabla\nabla y=\nabla y=x
, as desired. The
\{x\in A:\nabla x=x\}\subseteq\text{ran}(\nabla)
inclusion is trivial.
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

Any monadic implication algebra satisfies:

(1)

if
x\cdot\nabla y=c_{1}
, then
\nabla x\cdot\nabla y=c_{1}
;

(2)

if
x\cdot y=c_{1}
, then
\nabla x\cdot\nabla y=c_{1}
.

Proof.

For condition 1, assume that
x\cdot\nabla y=c_{1}
so that by Theorem
2.9
we have
(x\cdot(\nabla y\cdot c_{0}))\cdot c_{0}=x
. Then by Definition
3.5
(2) together with the fact that
\nabla
is an involution yields:

\nabla x=\nabla(x\cdot(\nabla y\cdot c_{0}))\cdot c_{0}=(\nabla x\cdot(\nabla\nabla y\cdot c_{0}))\cdot c_{0}=(\nabla x\cdot(\nabla y\cdot c_{0}))\cdot c_{0}

which by Theorem
2.9
implies
\nabla x\cdot\nabla y=c_{1}
. For condition 2, assume that
x\cdot y=c_{1}
and observe that we have

by Definition

(1). Then
x\cdot\nabla y=c_{1}
and hence by the first part of this this proof, we have
\nabla x\cdot\nabla y=c_{1}

∎

Any monadic implication algebra satisfies
\nabla(\nabla x\cdot c_{0})=\nabla x\cdot c_{0}

Proof.

Since
(\nabla x\cdot c_{0})\cdot\nabla(\nabla x\cdot c_{0})=c_{1}

(2)

((\nabla x\cdot c_{0})\cdot(\nabla x\cdot c_{0}))\cdot c_{0}=c_{1}\cdot c_{0}=c_{0}=\nabla(((\nabla x\cdot c_{0})\cdot(\nabla x\cdot c_{0}))\cdot c_{0})=(\nabla(\nabla x\cdot c_{0})\cdot(\nabla x\cdot c_{0}))\cdot c_{0}.

Equation (2) then implies that
\nabla(\nabla x\cdot c_{0})\cdot(\nabla x\cdot c_{0}))=c_{1}

If
A
is a monadic implication algebra,

Proposition 3.11

Any monadic implication algebra satisfies
\nabla((x\cdot y)\cdot y)=((\nabla x\cdot\nabla y)\cdot\nabla y)

Proof.

Conclusions

We have extended the work of Abbott
