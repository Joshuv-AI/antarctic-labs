Diagnostic performance of package insert-based screening for potential drug–drug interactions in ICU patients receiving injectable medications: a retrospective diagnostic accuracy study using Lexi-Interact as the comparator in Japan
Authors: Takahiro Kato, Noriko Yasukawa, Eriko Uno, Kenta Kanamori, Eizo Watanabe, Yukihiro Hamada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNo established gold standard exists for identifying potential drug–drug interactions (pDDIs). In Japan, package inserts are commonly used for pDDI screening in clinical practice, whereas internationally curated databases such as Lexi-Interact serve widely as clinical decision support tools. However, the performance of package insert-based pDDI identification in intensive care unit (ICU) settings has not been fully evaluated. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to evaluate the concordance of package insert-based screening for detecting pDDIs in ICU patients receiving injectable medications, using Lexi-Interact as the comparator.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included adult ICU admissions involving patients aged ≥ 18 years at two ICUs of a tertiary hospital. Clinically significant pDDIs among injectable drugs were assessed at three predefined timepoints (day 2, day 5, and the day before ICU discharge). Package insert-based pDDI identification was compared with Lexi-Interact as the comparator. The primary outcome was the negative predictive value (NPV) of package insert-based screening for clinically significant pDDIs. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and weighted kappa in relation to Lexi-Interact as a pragmatic comparator. Robustness was assessed via ICU admission-level cluster bootstrap analysis with 10,000 resamples.ResultsAmong 345 eligible ICU admissions, package insert-based screening yielded an NPV of 0.98, a sensitivity of 0.80, a specificity of 0.91, and a PPV of 0.51 compared with Lexi-Interact. Agreement between the two sources was moderate (weighted kappa = 0.57). Bootstrap analysis confirmed stable estimates of diagnostic performance. Interactions identified exclusively by Lexi-Interact were predominantly category C, with only one category D interaction and no category X interactions.ConclusionsIn ICU settings, package insert-based identification of pDDIs involving injectable drugs showed high NPV, sensitivity, and specificity relative to Lexi-Interact. These findings suggest that package insert-based screening may serve as a practical initial screening approach with substantial concordance with Lexi-Interact classifications in ICU pharmacotherapy. Background

Critically ill patients in intensive care units (ICUs) frequently experience polypharmacy, resulting in a higher incidence of potential drug–drug interactions (pDDIs) compared with non-ICU settings. The number of administered medications is positively associated with the number of pDDIs [1], and pDDIs contribute to emergency department visits and hospital admissions [2]. In older adults, pDDIs have been implicated in hospitalizations, with DDI-related events substantially increasing the odds of adverse drug event (ADE)-related admissions [3]. ADEs arising from DDIs are associated with prolonged hospital stays [4]. In a prospective study of patients with sepsis, pDDIs were identified in 80% of cases [5]. In the ICU, major pDDIs—such as those causing international normalized ratio (INR) prolongation, acute kidney injury, or QT-interval prolongation—resulted in actual ADEs in 65% of affected patients, prolonged ICU length of stay, and were deemed preventable in approximately 75% of instances [6]. These data highlight the critical need for accurate pDDI identification in ICU settings.

Recent studies report that customizing computerized DDI alerts in ICUs can reduce unnecessary alerts and alert burden by approximately 12%, underscoring how excessive or clinically irrelevant alerts impair workflow efficiency and compromise patient safety [7]. Effective screening strategies must, therefore, balance high sensitivity with practical usability in routine ICU care.

In Japan, package inserts remain the primary source for pDDI identification in clinical practice [8, 9]. Internationally, clinical decision support tools such as Lexi-Interact are widely used, although discrepancies in pDDI detection exist across databases [10–12]. Lexi-Interact has shown superior performance in multiple studies, with higher sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values compared with other interaction databases [13–16].

Despite the concurrent use of regulatory package inserts and database-driven tools in daily practice, it remains uncertain how well package insert-based screening identifies combinations that are unlikely to be classified as clinically meaningful pDDIs by comparator databases in ICU patients.

No study to date has reported evaluating the performance of Japanese package insert information for pDDI detection in ICU patients through direct comparison with Lexi-Interact or similar international databases. This evidence gap is especially pertinent in the acute ICU setting, where therapy predominantly involves injectable medications and where pharmacokinetic profiles and interaction mechanisms differ markedly from those in general wards. Determining the reliability of package insert information for interaction detection under ICU-specific conditions is fundamental to ensuring safe and effective pharmacotherapy. Such clarification would inform whether package insert-based screening can function as a practical initial screening approach for identifying combinations less likely to be classified as clinically significant pDDIs by a comparator database, and guide the appropriate integration of additional databases into clinical decision-making.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the diagnostic performance of Japanese package insert-based screening in detecting clinically significant pDDIs among ICU patients receiving injectable medications. Lexi-Interact, a widely used clinical tool, served as the comparator. Particular emphasis was placed on the ability of package insert-based screening to identify combinations that were unlikely to be classified as clinically significant pDDIs by Lexi-Interact.

Methods

Study design

In this single-center retrospective cohort study, we analyzed routinely collected electronic medical record data to evaluate the detection of pDDIs in critically ill patients. We compared pDDI information from Japanese drug package inserts with that from Lexi-Interact (Lexicomp/UpToDate, accessed May 31, 2024). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Aichi Medical University Hospital (approval number: 2023-148). Informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design.

Patients

Eligible ICU admissions were those involving adults aged ≥ 18 years who were admitted for at least 3 days to the general ICU or the emergency and critical care center ICU at Aichi Medical University Hospital between January 1 and December 31, 2022, to enable consistent evaluation at predefined timepoints (day 2, day 5, and the day before ICU discharge [Pre-discharge]). The general ICU primarily admits critically ill patients transferred from hospital wards or requiring postoperative care after major surgery. In contrast, the emergency and critical care center ICU primarily admits patients directly from the emergency department, including those with severe trauma, sepsis, or other acute medical emergencies. Given that annual ICU admissions at our institution substantially exceeded the required sample size, we implemented a predefined sampling strategy: consecutive eligible ICU admissions were selected in chronological order, with the first 400 ICU admissions initially extracted. The unit of analysis in this study was ICU admission. Patients with multiple ICU admissions during the study period were considered independent observations.

Previous ICU studies have reported a mean of 1.84 ± 1.09 pDDIs per patient, with approximately 80% of patients experiencing at least one pDDI [5]. Given the substantial number of annual ICU admissions at our institution and the complexity of constructing and evaluating all possible drug-pair combinations at multiple timepoints, we adopted a predefined consecutive sampling strategy. The first 400 eligible ICU admissions were extracted as a pragmatic dataset to enable detailed analysis while maintaining feasibility. The robustness of the estimates was subsequently evaluated using bootstrap resampling.

Drug selection

The analysis was restricted to injectable medications to capture real-world pharmacotherapy during the acute phase of ICU care and to ensure a standardized evaluation of pDDIs by minimizing variability related to oral drug absorption and administration conditions. In the acute ICU setting, injectable drugs predominate, because they enable rapid onset, flexible dose titration, and reliable therapeutic effects while avoiding oral drug-specific interactions (e.g., impaired absorption, chelation, or gastric pH alterations).

Before querying Lexi-Interact, drug names were standardized to their generic names. When necessary, synonymous names, salt forms, and formulation-specific names were mapped to the corresponding active ingredients used in Lexi-Interact to ensure consistent drug identification.

Injectable drugs not listed in Lexi-Interact could not be evaluated for pDDIs and were, therefore, excluded from the analysis, because Lexi-Interact was used as the comparator. These included drugs for which no corresponding entries were available in Lexi-Interact, as well as drugs with complex formulations that could not be mapped to a single active ingredient.

Intravenous fluids (e.g., Ringer’s solutions and maintenance fluids), total parenteral nutrition, and amino acid solutions were also excluded, owing to their complex compositions, which complicate reliable interaction assessment as single agents, and inconsistent mapping to unique drug entities in Lexi-Interact.

Identification of pDDIs

pDDIs were evaluated at three predefined timepoints: day 2 and day 5 after ICU admission, and Pre-discharge. These timepoints were selected to represent key phases of ICU pharmacotherapy, including the early phase (day 2), the midpoint of ICU stay (day 5), and Pre-discharge. For each ICU admission and timepoint, we generated a list of administered drugs and constructed all possible pairwise combinations of drugs co-administered at that timepoint.

Timepoint selection was informed by a prior methodological approach that used median ICU length of stay to define a representative evaluation point [8]. In our cohort, the median ICU length of stay was 5 days; thus, day 5 represented the midpoint of the stay.

pDDIs were evaluated using Lexi-Interact (UpToDate®, accessed May 31, 2024). Interactions rated as categories C (monitor therapy), D (consider therapy modification), or X (avoid combination) were classified as clinically significant pDDIs, whereas categories A (no known interaction) and B (no action needed) indicated no clinically relevant interaction.

Japanese package insert data were extracted from an electronic database compiling interaction information from official package inserts issued by the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA; accessed June 30, 2024). In Japanese package inserts, pDDIs are described within regulatory labeling, primarily under sections such as “contraindications” and “precautions for co-administration.” In this study, these categories were operationally interpreted as signals requiring avoidance or caution, and were mapped to a dichotomized framework to enable comparison with Lexi-Interact.

Interactions described as “Precautions for co-administration” were mapped to category D, and those labeled “contraindicated” were mapped to category X. For the primary analyses, interactions were subsequently dichotomized as positive (presence of a clinically significant pDDI) or negative (no interaction). Drug names were standardized to generic names prior to interaction screening. When necessary, salt forms or formulation variants were mapped to their corresponding generic active ingredients to ensure consistency with Lexi-Interact database entries. Statements describing physical or chemical incompatibilities (e.g., precipitation or instability during co-administration) were excluded from the analysis, because we focused on pharmacological pDDIs.

Drugs were excluded only when no corresponding entry was available in Lexi-Interact or when the formulation consisted of multiple components for which a single active ingredient could not be clearly defined.

Outcomes

For Lexi-Interact, categories C, D, and X were considered positive, whereas categories A and B were considered negative. For Japanese package inserts, interactions described as “Precautions for co-administration” or “contraindicated” were considered positive signals. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), in accordance with the STARD 2015 guidelines [17]. Given that the primary objective was to evaluate whether package insert-based screening could identify combinations that are unlikely to be classified as clinically significant pDDIs by Lexi-Interact, emphasis was placed on NPV and sensitivity. The primary outcome was the NPV of package insert-based screening for clinically significant pDDIs. Secondary outcomes included sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and overall agreement with Lexi-Interact. Agreement in interaction severity was assessed using weighted kappa statistics. For this analysis, Lexi-Interact categories were grouped as 0 (no interaction/A/B), 1 (C/D), and 2 (X), whereas package insert categories were grouped as 0 (no interaction), 1 (precautions for co-administration), and 2 (contraindicated), with interpretation based on the Landis and Koch criteria [18].

Internal validity

We applied an ICU admission-level cluster bootstrap with 10,000 resamples to assess the robustness of the primary outcome and diagnostic performance metrics. Bootstrap distributions yielded mean estimates and 95% confidence intervals.

Sensitivity analysis

Analyses limited to injectable drugs may underestimate pDDI profiles after oral reintroduction, because oral medications are often withheld during the acute phase of ICU admission, but may be resumed later. Accordingly, we conducted a Monte Carlo simulation to model oral medication resumption. Candidate oral medications were selected based on a previous report [19]. Timepoint-specific probabilities of oral medication use (on day 2, day 5, and pre-discharge) were determined through expert consensus at the study institution. An initial proposal was developed by a Japanese Society of Critical Care Medicine-certified intensive care pharmacy specialist with more than 5 years of ICU experience. The proposal was then reviewed and refined through iterative discussions with pharmacists working in the general ICU (GICU) and emergency and critical care center ICU (EICU) at our institution, including an emergency medicine-certified pharmacist. These pharmacists operate as a unified critical care team and share expertise across both units, ensuring that the consensus reflected pharmacotherapy practices in intensive care and emergency settings.

The probability structure was defined separately for disease-specific conditions and for other conditions. For general conditions, baseline probabilities of oral medication use were specified at each predefined timepoint (day 2, day 5, and pre-discharge) using percentile-based estimates (P5, P50, and P95). For disease-specific conditions, probability estimates were defined using median values (P50) with disease-specific adjustment factors.

In the simulation, oral medications were assigned to each ICU admission at each timepoint based on these predefined probabilities. To reflect real-world prescribing patterns, simulated oral medications that overlapped with already administered injectable drugs were excluded.

We evaluated three scaling scenarios (0.8 × , 1.0 × , and 1.2 × baseline probabilities) to address uncertainty in probability estimates. For each ICU admission, simulated oral medications were generated, integrated with injectable drug data, and assessed for pDDIs using the same approach as the primary analysis. In each scenario, 10,000 ICU admission-level cluster bootstrap replications were performed. Detailed parameter values are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Evaluation of potentially missed interactions

We identified drug pairs flagged by Lexi-Interact but not by package inserts to quantify the risk of missed interactions when relying on package inserts. Pairs with a frequency of ≥ 0.5% were selected to reflect clinically meaningful exposures for descriptive presentation. All interaction pairs, including those with lower frequencies and higher severity (category D or X), were retained in the overall analysis but may not have been presented in the table. Severity distribution of these potentially missed interactions was described according to Lexi-Interact categories.

Statistical analysis

The primary analysis was conducted at the drug-pair–timepoint level, in which all possible drug pairs co-administered at each predefined timepoint were evaluated independently. Because multiple observations were derived from the same ICU admission across timepoints, an additional ICU admission–timepoint-level analysis was performed to reflect clinical decision-making. At each timepoint, an ICU admission was classified as positive if at least one clinically significant pDDI was identified based on Lexi-Interact (categories C, D, or X). Package insert-based screening was considered positive when at least one interaction categorized as “contraindicated co-administration” or “precaution for co-administration” was detected at the same timepoint. Diagnostic performance metrics were recalculated using this ICU admission–timepoint-level definition. ICU admissions were evaluated only at timepoints that occurred during their ICU stay; therefore, patients who were discharged or deceased before day 5 contributed data only for the available earlier timepoints.

Continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges or means with standard deviations, as appropriate. Diagnostic performance metrics were calculated using Lexi-Interact as the comparator. Lexi-Interact was selected as the comparator, because it provides a structured severity classification and evidence grading system and is widely used in clinical decision support internationally. However, it should be interpreted as an operational comparator rather than as a definitive standard. All analyses were conducted using EZR version 4.3.1 (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan) [20] and R version 4.3.1.

To evaluate diagnostic performance at the clinical decision-making level, an additional ICU admission–timepoint-level analysis was conducted at each predefined timepoint (day 2, day 5, and pre-discharge). A total of 473 ICU admission–timepoint observations were included. The prevalence of clinically significant pDDIs according to Lexi-Interact was 50%. Using this ICU admission–timepoint-level definition, package insert-based screening demonstrated a sensitivity of 0.94, specificity of 0.70, PPV of 0.76, and NPV of 0.92. The negative likelihood ratio was 0.08, and agreement between the two systems showed substantial concordance (weighted kappa = 0.62). The underlying contingency tables for the drug-pair-timepoint-level and ICU admission–timepoint-level analyses are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Overall frequency of pDDIs

A total of 6,034 drug-pair–timepoint observations were evaluated for pDDIs. Clinically significant pDDIs were identified in 979 observations (16.2%) using package insert information and in 628 observations (10.4%) using Lexi-Interact.

Discussion

In this study, package insert-based screening exhibited a high NPV for potential pDDIs classified as categories C, D, or X by Lexi-Interact. Drug pairs not flagged by package inserts were infrequently categorized as interacting by Lexi-Interact. Because NPV depends on outcome prevalence, the high NPV observed in this study should be interpreted in the context of the relatively low prevalence of Lexi-Interact-defined pDDIs in this cohort. In settings with a higher prevalence of clinically significant pDDIs, the NPV may be lower, which may limit the generalizability of these findings.

To complement prevalence-dependent metrics, likelihood ratios provide a more robust assessment of diagnostic performance. In particular, the low negative likelihood ratio observed in this study supports concordance with Lexi-Interact in identifying combinations less likely to be classified as clinically significant pDDIs, independent of prevalence. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that package insert-based screening may help identify drug combinations that are unlikely to be classified as clinically significant pDDIs by Lexi-Interact in ICU patients receiving injectable medications.

Because no true gold standard exists for pDDI detection, we selected Lexi-Interact—reported to have relatively high diagnostic performance across multiple metrics [14]—as a comparator. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as measures of concordance rather than as definitive measures of diagnostic accuracy. Although package insert-based screening identified more interactions overall, it achieved high sensitivity, specificity, and NPV. This pattern aligns with a conservative framework designed to prioritize broad detection of potential interaction signals in high-risk settings such as the ICU.

Notably, interactions detected only by Lexi-Interact were predominantly category C, whereas higher-severity interactions (categories D or X) were rare. This suggests that pDDIs missed by package inserts were generally less severe per Lexi-Interact classification, supporting concordance with Lexi-Interact for higher-severity combinations in this context, rather than providing definitive safety assurance.

From a clinical workflow perspective, ICU pharmacists routinely screen for pDDIs amid complex polypharmacy and time-sensitive decisions. Tools with high NPV enable efficient identification of combinations less likely to be classified as clinically significant by the comparator, allowing clinicians to prioritize those requiring detailed evaluation. Package insert-based screening may thus function as an initial triage step, supplemented by database tools such as Lexi-Interact for assessing interaction severity and clinical relevance.

In contrast, the lower PPV indicates that package insert-based screening overidentifies interactions relative to Lexi-Interact, reflecting a conservative regulatory approach that favors sensitivity. Although this strategy may reduce the likelihood of overlooking Lexi-Interact-defined interactions, it increases the alert burden, potentially contributing to cognitive workload and alert fatigue in ICU practice [7]. Consistent with this interpretation, the negative likelihood ratio was low (LR− = 0.08), indicating that a negative package insert screen substantially reduced the probability of Lexi-Interact-defined interactions. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in the underlying objectives of the two sources, with package inserts prioritizing safety and Lexi-Interact emphasizing clinically contextualized interaction assessment.

A key question is whether screening could rely solely on database tools such as Lexi-Interact. However, in many healthcare systems, regulatory (package insert) information remains the primary reference for medication use, and practice must align with it. Exclusive reliance on package inserts may result in uniform responses without adequate prioritization of clinical relevance, as these documents often provide limited severity stratification. Consequently, pharmacists and clinicians must interpret flagged interactions cautiously, integrating contextual factors.

A two-step approach—initial screening guided by regulatory interaction information followed by database-based stratification—may represent a pragmatic strategy to balance patient safety, regulatory consistency, and clinical efficiency in ICU pharmacotherapy, although its applicability may vary depending on clinical setting and patient population. These findings indicate that regulatory labeling-based screening and database-driven tools show complementary patterns of pDDI detection, with their complementary strengths best applied to screening and prioritization within ICU medication-safety workflows.

Agreement between package insert information and Lexi-Interact was moderate, aligning with prior reports of variability among drug interaction databases, including Lexi-Interact and Micromedex [16, 21]. These discrepancies in pDDI classification appear to stem from fundamental differences across knowledge bases rather than limitations unique to regulatory drug information. Therefore, discordance between the two resources should not be interpreted as definitive true-positive or false-negative classifications, but rather as reflecting differences in evidence synthesis and curation approaches. More broadly, discrepancies between the two information sources should be interpreted as differences in interaction detection rather than as definitive evidence of inaccuracy in either source. Because no true gold standard exists for pDDI identification, the diagnostic performance metrics in this study should be interpreted with caution. Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were calculated against Lexi-Interact as operational comparators rather than as definitive references.

In this context, “false positives” and “false negatives” reflect discordance between two information sources with different objectives and evidence frameworks, rather than definitive misclassification. Accordingly, these metrics should be interpreted as measures of concordance with the comparator database rather than as measures of absolute diagnostic accuracy.

Notably, this study was designed to evaluate the comparative detection performance of two information sources rather than to estimate the incidence of pDDIs. Therefore, the observed frequencies should not be interpreted as quantitative estimates of incidence but as characteristics of the study cohort.

Furthermore, because ICU pharmacotherapy is highly complex and context-dependent, and because healthcare systems, institutional practices, and resource availability vary across settings, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings beyond the present study environment.

Such differences likely reflect distinct approaches to evidence synthesis and curation. Package inserts derive interaction data primarily from nonclinical studies, pharmacokinetic trials, regulatory approval dossiers, and postmarketing surveillance, prioritizing reproducibility and safety as official regulatory documents [22, 23]. In contrast, Lexi-Interact synthesizes published literature, case reports, pharmacological mechanisms, and expert opinion to inform clinical decision-making, although detailed criteria for inclusion and severity classification remain undisclosed [24]. Notably, interaction information in Japanese package inserts is not structured as a graded clinical decision tool but is presented as regulatory safety information, which differs fundamentally from database-based classification systems. Therefore, the mapping used in this study represents an operational simplification required for comparative analysis.

Collectively, these findings support a complementary model for pDDI screening. In settings where regulatory labeling serves as the primary medication reference, package insert data can provide a practical initial screening approach with substantial concordance with Lexi-Interact, whereas database resources can facilitate prioritization based on severity and clinical context. Implementing such a structured workflow may support medication review and prioritization while optimizing efficiency in ICU pharmacotherapy.

Overall, these findings delineate the distinct yet synergistic roles of regulatory drug information and clinical decision support databases in pDDI screening, advocating a complementary workflow in ICU settings.

This study has several methodological strengths. It is the first to directly compare regulatory labeling-based pDDI screening with an internationally recognized interaction database in an ICU cohort. The analysis was restricted to injectable medications, reflecting real-world pharmacotherapy during the acute phase of critical illness. Methodologically, we adopted a diagnostic test accuracy framework consistent with STARD recommendations, with internal validity supported by ICU admission-level cluster bootstrap resampling and robustness evaluated through Monte Carlo-based sensitivity analyses.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, institutional factors—including patient case-mix, treatment protocols, and formulary choices—may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings. In addition, routine pDDI screening at the study institution relied primarily on package insert information, which may have introduced information bias and further restricted generalizability. Notably, one drug pair listed as contraindicated in package inserts (dobutamine–norepinephrine) was observed in practice. In ICU management of shock, this combination is sometimes used clinically with careful monitoring when indicated. We did not assess the appropriateness or clinical outcomes of such administrations. Furthermore, because consecutive patients were selected from the beginning of the year rather than across the entire calendar year, potential seasonal variation in ICU case-mix may not have been fully captured. In addition, ICU admissions with a length of stay of less than 3 days were excluded to enable consistent evaluation at predefined timepoints, which may have excluded early deaths and early discharges, and could have influenced the observed pDDI patterns.

Second, the analysis was restricted to injectable medications during the acute phase of critical illness; therefore, potential interactions involving chronic oral medications may have been underestimated, even with simulation-based sensitivity analyses. Third, detailed data on administration timing were unavailable, so pDDIs that could be mitigated by temporal separation may have been misclassified as concurrent. In addition, the use of predefined timepoints may not fully capture transient pDDIs occurring immediately after drug initiation. Fourth, the lack of a true gold standard and the use of Lexi-Interact as a comparator—based on its reported high diagnostic performance—may have introduced comparator bias; discrepancies between tools should be interpreted accordingly. In addition, interaction information was extracted from database versions accessed in 2024, whereas the patient cohort was derived from 2022. Because historical versions of interaction databases are not readily accessible, the most recent versions were used for analysis; therefore, temporal updates in interaction databases may represent a potential limitation of this retrospective study.

Finally, actual adverse drug events were not assessed, so the clinical impact of detected pDDIs cannot be directly inferred. Accordingly, our findings reflect potential rather than confirmed clinical harm.

Conclusion

In ICU patients receiving injectable medications, package insert-based screening demonstrated a high NPV for identifying combinations that were unlikely to be classified as clinically significant pDDIs by Lexi-Interact. Although package inserts identified more interactions overall, their high NPV compared with Lexi-Interact suggests that they may function as a practical initial screening tool in critical care pharmacotherapy.

Database resources such as Lexi-Interact provide additional context regarding interaction severity and clinical relevance. Therefore, the complementary use of regulatory drug information and clinical decision support databases may provide a useful framework for pDDI screening in ICU practice, although these findings should be interpreted as concordance with a comparator database rather than as definitive diagnostic accuracy or safety assurance, and further validation in diverse clinical settings is warranted.
