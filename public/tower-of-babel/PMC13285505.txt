Gamabufotalin suppresses pancreatic cancer through redox-homeostasis disruption by G6PD downregulation
Authors: Die Xu, Xiaowei Wang, Yisi Cai, Piao Zheng, Lemei Zhu, Weijun Peng, Yinghui Song, Bolin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, highlighting the critical need for novel therapeutic strategies. Gamabufotalin (CS-6), a bufadienolide compound, has been shown to exert significant inhibitory effects on a variety of cancers.ObjectiveThis study investigates the potential therapeutic effects and underlying molecular mechanisms of CS-6 in pancreatic cancer.MethodsThe anti-cancer properties of CS-6 were assessed through both in vivo and in vitro experiments. Network pharmacology and untargeted metabolomics were employed to identify candidate molecular targets of CS-6. Molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and CETSA were used to support potential target engagement between CS-6 and G6PD. G6PD expression was evaluated through public databases and immunohistochemistry. NADPH levels, ROS accumulation, apoptosis, and autophagy were measured using NADPH assays, western blot, flow cytometry, and transmission electron microscopy. Transcriptomic analysis and western blot were conducted to explore the downstream signaling pathways affected by G6PD downregulation.ResultsCS-6 inhibited pancreatic cancer cell growth, migration, and invasion, while promoting apoptosis. In vivo, CS-6 suppressed tumor growth and induced cell death. Elevated G6PD expression in pancreatic cancer was associated with poor prognosis. CS-6 treatment reduced NADPH levels, increased ROS accumulation, and was associated with autophagy induction, accompanied by decreased G6PD protein expression. Furthermore, CS-6 inhibited the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, and G6PD knockdown recapitulated this effect.ConclusionCS-6 treatment is associated with G6PD downregulation, redox-homeostasis disruption, and suppression of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby contributing to autophagy-related cell death, apoptosis, and inhibition of pancreatic cancer progression. These findings highlight CS-6 as a promising therapeutic candidate for pancreatic cancer treatment, with G6PD as a potential molecular target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08441-2.Keywords: Pancreatic cancer, Gamabufotalin, G6PD, Oxidative stress, Autophagy

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) remains one of the most aggressive malignancies globally, with increasing incidence and mortality rates [16]. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) accounts for approximately 95% of all cases [9, 40]. While surgical resection is effective for early-stage patients, approximately 80% are diagnosed at advanced stages, where treatment is limited to systemic therapies. Alarmingly, the five-year survival rate remains as low as 13%, underscoring the limited clinical benefits of current treatment options [35, 41]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop novel and effective therapeutic strategies for PC.

Targeting programmed cell death is a major strategy in the development of anti-cancer therapies [18]. This process includes not only classical apoptosis but also other forms, such as autophagy-dependent cell death [26]. Autophagy plays a dual role in tumorigenesis: it helps cancer cells adapt to stress and acquire drug resistance, yet excessive or prolonged activation can lead to cell death [5, 6, 11, 12]. Recent studies suggest that various natural compounds exhibit significant anti-cancer effects by simultaneously inducing both apoptosis and autophagic cell death [24, 25, 32, 42]. Therefore, the development of therapeutic agents that can trigger both processes, especially those derived from natural products, has become a promising approach to enhancing anti-cancer efficacy.

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) provides a rich source of natural compounds with potential anti-cancer properties [50]. Chan Su, a well-known TCM, is traditionally used for reducing inflammation and alleviating pain [14]. Gamabufotalin (CS-6), a bufadienolide derived from Chan Su, has a distinct structure and has been shown to inhibit cell growth and induce both apoptosis and autophagy in various cancers [19, 23, 30, 34, 46, 47]. However, the specific role and molecular targets of CS-6 in PC remain unclear. Therefore, this study aims to explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-PC effects of CS-6.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

For experimental purposes, Gamabufotalin (CS-6) (purity ≥ 98%) was initially dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 50 mM stock solution, which was aliquoted and stored at − 20 °C to avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles and maintain its bioactivity. Detailed information regarding CS-6 and the antibodies used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Cell lines and culture

The human PC cell lines Panc-1 and Patu-8988t, as well as the pancreatic ductal epithelial cell line HPDE6-C7, were obtained from the Cell Bank of Central South University.

Panc-1 and Patu-8988t cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM, while HPDE6-C7 cells were maintained in MEM (both from Gibco, USA). Each medium was supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics. All cell lines were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere, with routine medium changes.

Cell proliferation assays

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit‑8 (CCK‑8; NCM Biotechnology, C6005, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Approximately 5,000 cells per well were seeded in 96‑well plates, with three replicate wells per condition. To evaluate the time‑ and dose‑dependent effects of CS‑6, cells were treated with a series of CS‑6 concentrations (0, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320, 640 nM) for either 24–48 h. After the indicated treatment, CCK‑8 reagent was added, and the cells were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and relative cell viability was calculated by normalizing the values to those of control wells, which were set as 100%.

In the colony formation assay, 500 cells per well were seeded in 6‑well plates and cultured in complete medium. After 24 h of attachment, cells were treated with different concentrations of CS‑6 (0, 20, 40 nM) for 10 days. After 10 days of culture, the medium was removed, and the cells were gently washed with PBS. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 20 min. Excess stain was removed by rinsing with distilled water, and the plates were air‑dried. Colonies were counted manually or using ImageJ software. The colony formation efficiency was calculated as (number of colonies/number of cells seeded) × 100%.

Cell migration and invasion assays

The wound healing assay was performed to evaluate cell migration. PC cells were seeded in six‑well plates and cultured until reaching 100% confluence. A straight scratch was created using a sterile 10 µL pipette tip. Debris was removed by washing twice with PBS, and the cells were then incubated in medium containing 2% fetal bovine serum (low‑serum condition) with or without various concentrations of CS‑6 (0, 10, 20 nM). The 2% FBS medium alone served as the control to distinguish the contribution of proliferation inhibition from that of migration inhibition. Wound closure was monitored at 0 and 24 h post‑scratching. Phase‑contrast images were captured at each time point using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The wound area was measured using ImageJ software. The percentage of wound closure was calculated as (area at 0 h – area at 24 h) / area at 0 h × 100%. Each experiment was performed independently three times, and three random fields per well were quantified.

Transwell invasion assays were performed using 24-well Transwell chambers (Corning, USA). The upper surface of the insert membrane was pre‑coated with 100 µL of Matrigel (diluted 1:8 with serum‑free medium) and incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. Then, 1 × 10⁵ PC cells suspended in 200 µL of serum‑free medium containing various concentrations of CS-6 were seeded into the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with 600 µL of medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and the same concentration of CS-6. After 24 h of incubation, non‑invading cells on the upper surface were gently removed with a cotton swab. Invaded cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Images of randomly selected fields were captured under an inverted microscope, and the number of invaded cells was counted. The relative invasion rate was calculated as (number of invaded cells in the treatment group/number in the control group) × 100%. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Cytoskeleton staining

PC cells were seeded on confocal Petri dishes and cultured for 24 h to allow attachment, followed by treatment with CS-6 for 48 h. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. For cytoskeleton staining, they were incubated with phalloidin (Beyotime, C2203S, China) and DAPI (Meilunbio, MA0127, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Flow cytometry

For cell cycle analysis, cell cycle distribution was assessed using a Cell Cycle Kit (MULTISCIENCES, Zhejiang, China) according to the manufacturer’s live cell protocol. Briefly, cells were harvested, washed once with PBS, and resuspended in 1 mL of DNA staining solution containing 10 µL of permeabilization solution. After vortex mixing, cells were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Cells were then centrifuged, the supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in PBS.

For apoptosis assessment, commercial kits from MULTISCIENCES (Zhejiang, China) were used. Apoptosis was analyzed by dual staining with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI), distinguishing early apoptotic from late apoptotic/necrotic cells. Viable cells were first gated on FSC‑A/SSC‑A to exclude debris. Doublets were excluded using FSC‑A/FSC‑H, and at least 10,000 single cells were collected per sample. For apoptosis detection (Annexin V‑FITC/PI), quadrants were set using FITC‑only and PI‑only single‑stained controls, together with a blank tube (unstained cells) as a completely negative control.

For ROS detection, intracellular ROS levels were measured using DCFH‑DA (Beyotime, S0033S, China). After treatment, cells were incubated with 10 µM DCFH‑DA at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. Cells were then washed three times with serum‑free medium.

After staining and washing, flow cytometry data were collected using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA) and analyzed with FlowJo V.10.8.1 software.

Network pharmacology analysis

Candidate targets of CS-6 were identified through the SEA website. First, the SMILES string of the compound (Gamabufotalin) was obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The SMILES string was then submitted to the SEA website (https://sea.bkslab.org/) for target prediction. Finally, predicted targets were filtered to retain only those with the species specified as Homo sapiens to ensure reliability and accuracy.

Untargeted metabolomics and data analysis

Approximately 1 × 10⁷ Panc-1 cells per group were collected after treatment with vehicle or 40 nM CS-6 for 48 h (n = 6 per condition), and metabolites were extracted with 80% methanol. All chromatographic separations were performed using an UltiMate 3000 UHPLC System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). An ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) was used for the reversed-phase separation. The column oven was maintained at 45 °C. Gradient elution conditions were set as follows: 0–1.5 min, 5% B; 1.5–3 min, 5%–30% B; 3–5 min, 30%–60% B; 5–7 min, 60%–80% B; 7–12 min, 80%–100% B; 12–16 min, 100% B; 16–16.5 min, 100%–5% B. A high-resolution mass spectrometer Q-Exactive Orbitrap (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to detect metabolites eluted from the column. The Orbitrap was operated in both positive and negative ion modes with spray voltages of 3500 V and 3100 V, respectively. Raw MS data were preprocessed using XCMS software (v1.50.1). The online KEGG and HMDB databases were used to annotate the metabolites by matching the exact molecular mass data (m/z) of samples with those from the databases. Differential metabolites were identified through partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) with criteria of VIP > 1.0 and p ≤ 0.05, and pathway enrichment analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst 3.5.

RNA-sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from Panc‑1 cells with or without G6PD knockdown using TRIzol Reagent (TransGen Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by purification with a nucleic acid purification kit (Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., China). RNA concentration and purity were measured using a K5500 micro-spectrophotometer (Kaiao, Beijing, China), and RNA integrity was assessed with an Agilent 2200 TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis (Biowest Agarose, Biowest, Spain). Poly(A)+ mRNA was enriched and fragmented to approximately 200 bp. First- and second-strand cDNA synthesis, adaptor ligation, and low-cycle PCR amplification were performed using the Illumina® Stranded mRNA Prep Ligation kit (Illumina, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform with paired-end 150 bp reads using the NovaSeq Reagent Kit (Illumina, USA). Three biological replicates were included for each condition. Sequencing was performed by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Sequencing data were processed using fastp for quality control. Gene-level read counts were obtained with HTSeq (v0.12.4). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2, with adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log₂FC| ≥ 1 as thresholds. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted using the clusterProfiler package in R (Bioconductor), focusing on KEGG pathway enrichment and visualization.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was conducted using MOE 2019 software. The small molecule CS-6 (PubChem CID: 259803) and the target protein G6PD (PDB ID: 7UAG) were converted to PDB format. CS-6 was subjected to energy minimization, followed by docking with the preprocessed G6PD protein. Binding affinity was assessed based on docking scores, and the results were visualized using PyMOL 2.6.0 and Discovery Studio 2019.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using GROMACS 2022.3. Ligands were preprocessed with AmberTools22 to assign GAFF force fields and RESP charges via Gaussian 16 W, incorporating electrostatic potential data into topology files. Simulations were performed under constant temperature (300 K) and pressure (1 bar) using the Amber99sb-ildn force field and TIP3P water model, with Na+ ions added to neutralize the system charge. Energy minimization was followed by 100,000 steps of NVT and NPT equilibration (coupling constant: 0.1 ps; duration: 100 ps). A 100 ns production run was performed over 5,000,000 steps.

The Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA)

CETSA was conducted with slight modifications to established protocols. Briefly, Panc-1 cells were treated with either 0 or 40 nM CS-6 for 2 h, washed with PBS, and lysed. The resulting lysates, supplemented with protease inhibitors, were aliquoted and heated at temperatures of 46, 48, 50, 52, 54, and 56 °C for 3 min. After heating, all samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles. The soluble protein fractions were then prepared in loading buffer and analyzed by western blot [43].

Transfection

For G6PD overexpression, the full-length human G6PD cDNA (GenBank: NM_000402.4) was cloned into the lentiviral vector GV492 (GeneChem, Shanghai, China). For G6PD knockdown, PC cells were transduced with G6PD-targeting shRNA using the lentiviral vector GV493 (GeneChem, Shanghai, China). The corresponding lentiviruses were transduced into PC cells according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Forty-eight hours post-transduction, stable cell populations were selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin (Biosharp, Hefei, China). Overexpression and knockdown efficiencies were confirmed via western blot analysis. The shRNA sequences used were as follows: G6PD-shRNA1 (5′-GTCGTCCTCTATGTGGAGAAT-3′), G6PD-shRNA2 (5′-CCCTATATTTATGGCAGCCGA-3′), and negative control shRNA (shNC) (5′-CGCTGAGTACTTCGAAATGTC-3′).

Immunohistochemical analysis and H&E staining

Human and murine PC tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, then embedded in paraffin. Sections were prepared for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC). For IHC, sections underwent deparaffinization, rehydration, and blocking before incubation overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting G6PD and Ki-67, followed by a 60-minute incubation with the appropriate secondary antibodies. Staining was visualized under a Leica optical microscope. Human tumor paraffin-embedded tissue samples were collected with approval from the Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (Approval No. LYF20240243). Two qualified pathologists independently evaluated the staining in a blinded manner. Staining intensity was scored from 0 to 3 (0, no color; 1, light yellow; 2, light brown; 3, brown), and the percentage of positive cells was scored from 0 to 4 (0, < 5%; 1, 5–25%; 2, 25–50%; 3, 51–75%; 4, > 75%). The two scores were multiplied to give a final score (range, 0–12) [37].

Western blot analysis

PC cells were lysed in a buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Signal detection was performed using the Bio-Rad imaging system [10].

Tumor xenograft experiments

All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, China (Approval No. 20240600), and with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health (NIH). Male BALB/c nude mice (5 weeks old) were acclimated for one week and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. PC xenografts were established by subaxillary injection of 2 × 106 Panc-1 cells. When tumor volume reached approximately 50 mm³, mice were randomly divided into three groups and received daily intraperitoneal injections for two weeks: vehicle control (2% DMSO), CS-6 (2.5 mg/kg), or CS-6 (5 mg/kg). CS‑6 was dissolved in DMSO to prepare a 50 mg/mL stock solution, which was then diluted with saline to working concentrations of 0.5 mg/mL and 1 mg/mL (final DMSO concentration ≤ 2%). Each mouse received 100 µL of the respective CS‑6 solution or vehicle (2% DMSO in saline) via intraperitoneal injection. Tumor growth and general health were monitored every other day. At the study endpoint, all mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation.

Serum biochemical testing

Blood was collected from mice via orbital puncture, and serum samples were obtained through centrifugation. Liver and kidney function indicators were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer (Toshiba, Japan).

NADPH and NADP+/NADPH ratio assay

NADPH levels and the NADP+/NADPH ratio in PC cells were measured using the NADP+/NADPH assay kit (Beyotime, S0179, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

PC cells were washed twice with cold PBS, fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h, and subsequently treated with 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h. After graded acetone dehydration, samples were embedded in resin. Ultrathin sections were cut, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and observed under a JEM1400 TEM (JEOL, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. One-way ANOVA and two-tailed Student’s t-tests were applied as appropriate. Results are expressed as mean ± SD based on a minimum of three independent experiments. Statistical significance was defined as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, with n.s., non-significant.

Results

CS-6 suppresses pancreatic cancer cell growth and triggers apoptosis

The inhibitory effect of CS-6 (Fig. 1A) on PC cell proliferation was assessed using CCK-8 and colony formation assays. The results demonstrated a dose-dependent suppression of proliferation. The IC50 values for CS-6 were 40.82 nM at 24 h and 31.48 nM at 48 h in Panc-1 cells, and 61.06 nM and 48.23 nM at 24 and 48 h, respectively, in Patu-8988t cells. For normal pancreatic ductal epithelial cells (HPDE6-C7), the IC50 values were significantly higher, exceeding 1 µM at 24 h and reaching 131.1 nM at 48 h, indicating selective cytotoxicity towards cancer cells (Fig. 1B).

As presented in Fig. 1C and D, CS-6 substantially reduced colony formation in both Panc-1 and Patu-8988t cells. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant increase in the percentage of Panc-1 and Patu-8988t cells in the G2/M phase after CS-6 treatment (Fig. 1E-F). These results suggest that CS-6 induces G2/M phase arrest, thereby inhibiting cell cycle progression and proliferation. Additionally, flow cytometry analysis demonstrated a significant induction of apoptosis in both cell lines following CS-6 treatment.

These results collectively indicate that CS-6 effectively suppresses PC cell growth and induces apoptosis.

CS-6 inhibits the migration and invasion of pancreatic cancer cells in vitro

Fluorescence staining revealed significant morphological changes in PC cells treated with CS-6, including increased pseudopodia formation and irregular cell edges, indicating disruption of cytoskeletal remodeling (Fig. 2A-B). This alteration may contribute to reduced cell adhesion and motility.

Given that the IC20 concentration of CS-6 had minimal impact on cell viability, we further investigated its role in cell migration and invasion using wound healing and transwell assays (Fig. S2). Functional assays demonstrated that CS-6 significantly inhibited the migratory capacity of Panc-1 and Patu-8988t cells, as evidenced by slower gap closure in wound healing assays (Fig. 2C, D, and G). In Transwell assays, CS-6 markedly reduced the number of cells invading through membranes, indicating a reduction in invasive potential (Fig. 2E, F, and H). Notably, the inhibitory effects were dose-dependent, with higher concentrations resulting in more pronounced suppression.

These results suggest that CS-6 significantly inhibits the migration and invasion of PC cells.

CS-6 exerts anti-pancreatic cancer effects in vivo

Based on the in vitro findings, the potential antitumor efficacy of CS-6 was further evaluated in a subcutaneous xenograft model in nude mice (Fig. 3A). As shown in Fig. 3B-D, tumor volumes and weights were significantly reduced in CS-6-treated Panc-1 xenograft mice, compared with the vehicle group. To assess the safety of CS-6, body weight was monitored throughout the treatment period. No significant loss of mouse body weight was observed at therapeutically effective doses (Fig. 3E). Histological examination of major organs (liver, spleen, and kidneys) via H&E staining revealed no evident pathological changes at a dose of 5 mg/kg CS-6 (Fig. 3F). Additionally, serum biochemical analyses of liver and kidney function markers showed no significant evidence of hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity at the therapeutic dose (Fig. 3G–L), supporting the favorable in vivo safety profile of CS-6.

To further investigate the in vivo effects of CS-6, histopathological analysis, IHC, and TUNEL assays were performed on tumor tissues. H&E staining revealed notable structural disruption and necrotic regions in tumors from CS-6-treated mice (Fig. 3M). IHC staining for Ki-67 demonstrated a significant reduction in tumor cell proliferation following CS-6 treatment (Fig. 3M, N). Consistently, TUNEL assays revealed a substantial increase in apoptotic cells within the tumor tissue (Fig. 3M, O), indicating that CS-6 effectively induces tumor cell apoptosis in vivo.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that CS-6 exhibits potent anti-PC activity in vivo while maintaining a favorable safety profile, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic candidate.

CS-6 may exert its anti-cancer effects in pancreatic cancer by targeting G6PD

To further elucidate the underlying mechanism of CS-6 in PC, computational target prediction methods were employed to identify its potential molecular interactors. Target screening (Table S1) revealed that CS-6 may interact with multiple protein targets, with particular attention given to glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD). Consistently, our previous study suggested that G6PD is a potential target of CS-6 in non-small cell lung cancer [7] (Table S3). G6PD is the rate-limiting enzyme in the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), playing a pivotal role in maintaining intracellular NADPH levels, regulating redox homeostasis, and supporting nucleotide biosynthesis [2]. Previous studies have shown that G6PD is highly expressed in various cancer types, contributing to metabolic reprogramming and antioxidant defense in tumor cells, thereby promoting tumor progression [28, 31, 33].

Based on these findings, we hypothesized that G6PD may be involved in the anti-cancer effects of CS-6 and its regulation of metabolic reprogramming in PC cells. To test this hypothesis, untargeted metabolomic profiling was conducted on Panc-1 cells treated with CS-6. A total of 270 significantly altered metabolites were identified between the control and CS-6-treated groups. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these differential metabolites revealed that CS-6 modulates several metabolic pathways, including caffeine metabolism and the PPP (Fig. 4A–B). These results suggest that CS-6 may interfere with PC cell metabolism by regulating PPP activity.

To further investigate the potential interaction between CS-6 and G6PD, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and CETSA were performed. Molecular docking predicted that CS-6 may bind to G6PD with a binding energy of − 7.7 kcal/mol. The LYS-171 residue on the G6PD protein forms a hydrogen bond interaction with CS-6 (Fig. 4C).

RMSD analysis showed that the G6PD-CS-6 complex fluctuated within 0.5 nm, suggesting relative stability of the predicted complex (Fig. 4D). The G6PD-CS-6 complex maintained a stable and compact structure during the simulation, as indicated by the radius of gyration (Rg) curve fluctuating between 3.66 and 3.82 nm without significant shifts. Moreover, the binding of CS-6 did not induce major alterations to the overall structure of the G6PD protein (Fig. 4E). RMSF analysis showed minor fluctuations, with values consistently staying within 0.8 nm (Fig. 4F). The maximum number of hydrogen bonds formed between G6PD and CS-6 reached four, suggesting possible hydrophilic interactions (Fig. 4G). The free energy landscape of the CS-6-G6PD complex revealed a nearly singular minimum energy cluster (Fig. 4H), further supporting the strong stability of the complex.

Additionally, CETSA was conducted to assess cellular target engagement between CS-6 and G6PD. CS-6 treatment increased the thermal stability of G6PD, as evidenced by slower degradation at high temperatures (Fig. 4I, J).

These results collectively suggest that G6PD may be a functionally relevant candidate target involved in the anti-cancer activity of CS-6 in PC.

G6PD expression levels in PC were analyzed using public databases such as TCGA and CPTAC. To validate these findings, IHC staining was performed on PC tissue samples to assess G6PD protein expression. The results showed significantly higher G6PD protein levels in tumor tissues than in adjacent normal pancreatic tissues (Fig. 5D–E), consistent with the public database analyses. This further supports the elevated expression of G6PD in PC.

To investigate the relationship between G6PD expression and clinical staging or patient prognosis, a stratified analysis was conducted using clinical data from TCGA. The results showed that G6PD expression was significantly higher in patients with advanced-stage PC (stage III–IV) than in those with early-stage disease (Fig. 5F), suggesting that G6PD may be involved in tumor progression. Additionally, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis suggested that patients with high G6PD expression had significantly shorter progression-free survival (PFS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) compared to those with low G6PD expression (Fig. 5G, H), indicating a strong correlation between elevated G6PD levels and poor prognosis.

CS-6 induces oxidative stress and autophagic cell death in association with G6PD reduction in pancreatic cancer.

To further investigate whether CS-6 regulates G6PD in PC, the impact of CS-6 on G6PD protein expression was assessed. Western blot analysis showed a reduction in G6PD protein levels following CS-6 treatment (Fig. 6A, B). Given that G6PD regulates a key control point in the PPP [2], NADPH levels were measured post-treatment. CS-6 treatment led to reduced NADPH production (Fig. 6C) and increased the NADP+/NADPH ratio (Fig. 6D). A deficiency in NADPH can result in ROS accumulation and oxidative stress, both of which are closely associated with cancer progression [15, 29]. Flow cytometry analysis showed that CS-6 treatment significantly increased ROS levels in PC cells (Fig. 6E, F).

ROS accumulation can disrupt cellular redox balance and induce autophagic cell death [21]. We further examined the effect of CS-6 on autophagy in PC cells. Western blot analysis showed that CS-6 treatment increased the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and reduced p62 protein levels, suggesting activation of autophagy-related processes (Fig. 6G). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) also revealed the presence of autophagic vacuoles in CS-6-treated PC cells (Fig. 6H). To determine whether autophagy was involved in CS-6-induced cell death, the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) was co-administered with CS-6. Compared with CS-6 treatment alone, co-treatment with CQ further increased LC3-II accumulation (Fig. S3A). Moreover, CCK-8 assay showed that CQ treatment partially rescued CS-6-induced cell death (Fig. S3B), suggesting that autophagy contributed to cell death to some extent.

These findings suggest that CS-6 treatment is associated with oxidative stress and autophagy-related cell death, which may be linked to G6PD reduction.

G6PD is involved in autophagy, cell death, and growth inhibition in pancreatic cancer cells

The role of G6PD in autophagy, cell death, and PC cell growth inhibition was further investigated. Stable G6PD knockdown was established in PC cells, and its effects on cellular phenotypes were evaluated through in vitro functional assays. Efficient G6PD knockdown in PC cells was confirmed by western blot analysis (Fig. 7A and Fig. S1A).

The impact of G6PD knockdown on cell proliferation was assessed using CCK-8 and colony formation assays. The results demonstrated that reduced G6PD expression inhibited PC cell growth (Fig. 7B-D and S1B-C, E). Flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant increase in apoptosis following G6PD knockdown (Fig. 7E, F, and S1D, F).

Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis showed that G6PD knockdown notably elevated ROS levels in PC cells (Fig. 7G, H, and S1G, H). Western blot analysis indicated an increased LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and decreased p62 expression, suggesting enhanced autophagy. TEM revealed the presence of autophagosomes, further supporting an association between G6PD knockdown and autophagy-related changes in Panc-1 cells, which may be associated with increased oxidative stress.

To further validate that CS-6 exerts its effects through G6PD, G6PD overexpression (OE) was performed in AsPC-1 cells, which exhibited the lowest G6PD expression among the PC cell lines tested (Fig. S4A). The OE efficiency was confirmed by western blot (Fig. S4B). The CCK-8 assay showed that G6PD OE partially rescued CS-6-induced cell death (Fig. S4C), supporting that G6PD is a functionally relevant downstream mediator of CS-6 activity.

These findings further indicate that G6PD may be functionally involved in CS-6 activity and may contribute to the regulation of autophagy, apoptosis, and growth inhibition in PC cells.

CS-6 treatment and G6PD knockdown suppress the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in pancreatic cancer cells

To further explore downstream signaling pathways associated with G6PD downregulation, RNA sequencing was performed on PC cells with and without G6PD knockdown.

RNA sequencing identified 902 significantly altered genes in G6PD-knockdown Panc-1 cells, with a threshold of |log₂FC| ≥ 1 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05. Compared to the control group, 640 genes were upregulated, while 262 genes were downregulated (Fig. 8A, B). Enrichment analyses for GO functions and KEGG pathways were conducted on the 902 DEGs. GO enrichment analysis indicated that the DEGs were primarily enriched in the biological process (BP) category, including processes such as developmental regulation and multicellular organismal processes (Fig. 8C). KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, among others (Fig. 8D).

Consistent with previous reports that CS-6 can regulate the PI3K/AKT pathway [34], the RNA sequencing results were validated through western blot analysis. Western blot analysis showed that both CS-6 treatment and G6PD knockdown inhibited the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 8E, F).

These results suggest that both CS-6 treatment and G6PD knockdown suppress the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in PC cells, indicating that G6PD may be involved in CS-6-mediated inhibition of PI3K/AKT signaling.

Discussion

This study investigated the potential of CS-6, a bufadienolide derived from Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), as a novel therapeutic agent for pancreatic cancer (PC). CS-6 inhibits PC progression, an effect associated with G6PD downregulation, disruption of redox homeostasis, suppression of PI3K/AKT signaling, and induction of autophagy-related cell death and apoptosis (Fig. 9).

The mechanism underlying the inhibitory effect of CS-6 on PC progression. CS-6 inhibits PC progression in association with G6PD downregulation, disrupted redox homeostasis, and suppression of PI3K/AKT signaling, thereby contributing to autophagy-related cell death and apoptosis

TCM provides a rich source of natural compounds, many of which are characterized by multi-target activities and favorable safety profiles [8, 24, 25]. CS-6, a natural compound from toad venom, exhibits potent anti-cancer effects across multiple cancer types. It induces cell death processes such as apoptosis and autophagy by modulating key signaling pathways, including NF-κB, STAT3, PI3K/AKT, and mTOR [19, 23, 30, 34, 46, 47]. In vitro studies demonstrated that CS-6 inhibits PC cell proliferation and migration while inducing apoptosis. In vivo, CS-6 effectively suppressed PC xenograft growth within the therapeutic dose range, with no significant side effects in nude mice. These results indicate that CS-6 suppresses PC growth while maintaining a favorable safety profile in vivo. Our previous studies suggested that G6PD may be a potential target of CS-6 [8]. Network pharmacology and untargeted metabolomics predicted G6PD as a candidate target protein of CS-6 in PC, and subsequent experiments supported that CS-6 can bind to G6PD. G6PD, the rate-limiting enzyme in the pentose phosphate pathway, utilizes NADP+ to generate NADPH, which provides reducing power for nucleotide and lipid synthesis as well as antioxidant defense [2]. G6PD expression is significantly elevated in tumors compared to normal tissues, and this heightened expression is often associated with poor overall survival [4, 13, 20,28, 31, 33, 36]. Public database analysis and IHC confirmed G6PD upregulation in PC, correlating with poor prognosis. Functional assays revealed that G6PD knockdown promotes cell death in PC cells. Western blot analysis further showed that CS-6 reduces G6PD protein levels. Notably, G6PD overexpression (OE) partially rescued CS-6-induced cell death. Therefore, we hypothesize that CS-6 exerts its anti-PC effects, at least in part, by regulating G6PD.

Previous studies have shown that G6PD knockdown disrupts redox homeostasis and induces autophagy in PC [13]. Autophagy plays a dual role in tumor cells, exerting either cytoprotective or cytotoxic effects depending on cellular context [3]. Emerging evidence indicates that pharmacological induction of autophagic cell death represents a promising therapeutic strategy for PC. Several natural compounds have been shown to exert anti-PC effects by promoting autophagy-related cell death through diverse signaling pathways, including ROS accumulation and suppression of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling [22, 38, 48, 49]. Our results demonstrate that CS-6 induces autophagy in PC cells. Given that G6PD maintains NADPH levels and redox homeostasis, CS-6-mediated G6PD downregulation may contribute to ROS accumulation. Consistently, we observed that CS-6 treatment significantly elevates intracellular ROS levels. As previously reported [13], G6PD knockdown disrupts redox balance and induces autophagy. Based on these findings, CS-6-induced ROS accumulation appears to be associated with autophagy-related changes. To further determine whether CS-6-induced autophagy leads to autophagic cell death, we performed functional experiments using CQ to block autophagic flux. Our findings indicate that autophagy contributes to CS-6-induced cell death, rather than merely accompanying changes in autophagy markers. Rescue experiments using a ROS scavenger (e.g., N-acetylcysteine) are needed to definitively establish the causal role of ROS in this process.

RNA sequencing performed in G6PD-knockdown PC cells revealed notable enrichment in the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. In PC, the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is widely involved in tumor cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and drug resistance, serving as a key molecular network driving tumor progression [17, 44]. Targeting the PI3K/AKT pathway has shown potential to inhibit tumor growth and enhance chemosensitivity, highlighting its therapeutic relevance in PC [27, 39]. Consistently, previous reports have shown that CS‑6 can regulate the PI3K/AKT pathway [34], and our study further demonstrates that both CS‑6 treatment and G6PD knockdown suppress PI3K/AKT signaling in PC cells, indicating that G6PD may be involved in CS-6-mediated inhibition of PI3K/AKT signaling. However, the precise relationship between ROS accumulation, PI3K/AKT inhibition, and autophagic cell death remains to be further dissected.

While CS-6 shows promise against PC, this study has some limitations. First, although several pathways associated with the anti-pancreatic cancer effects of CS-6 have been partially elucidated, these findings still require further validation through rescue experiments, such as treatment with a ROS scavenger (e.g., N-acetylcysteine) or PI3K/AKT pathway inhibitors. Specifically regarding the involvement of G6PD, its overexpression only partially rescued CS-6–induced cell death, indicating partial dependence. Moreover, we did not assess whether CS-6 directly inhibits G6PD enzymatic activity. Future studies using enzymatic activity assays and biophysical binding assays are needed to determine whether CS-6 directly binds to G6PD and affects its enzymatic activity, and rescue experiments with mutant or shRNA-resistant G6PD constructs are required to establish the mechanistic role of G6PD in CS-6 action fully. Second, using a single xenograft model in vivo limits the generalizability across various PC subtypes. Future research should employ more clinically relevant models, such as patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models or organoids [1, 45]. Lastly, the pharmacokinetic profile of CS-6 remains insufficiently characterized, which is a critical step before clinical trials.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that CS-6 inhibits PC progression, an effect associated with downregulation of G6PD. CS-6 exerts inhibitory effects that are associated with reduced G6PD protein levels and disrupted redox homeostasis, which may contribute to autophagy-related cell death and apoptosis in PC cells. Our findings also indicate that CS-6 suppresses the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, possibly in association with G6PD downregulation. Together, these findings highlight CS-6 as a promising therapeutic candidate for PC treatment and suggest G6PD as a potential molecular mediator of its anti-cancer activity.
