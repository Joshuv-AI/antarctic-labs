Complex Chronic Conditions in the PICU: Single-Center Retrospective Study in Central-Southern China, 2020–2025
Authors: Xun Li, Haipeng Yan, Xiao Li, Xinping Zhang, Jiaotian Huang, Jinpeng Gan, Ting Luo, Xiangyu Wang, Longlong Xie, Yufan Yang, Haohao Zou, Zhenghui Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVES:Complex chronic conditions (CCCs) are increasingly prevalent in the PICU. Identifying high-risk patterns of CCC and comorbidities is essential for patient risk stratification and targeted management. In this study, we aimed to describe the distribution and patterns of CCCs among children admitted to our tertiary PICU in China and to identify the types and combinations of conditions associated with a higher risk of adverse outcomes.DESIGN:Retrospective cohort study, January 2020 to February 2025.SETTING:Single center, at a tertiary referral PICU in Central-Southern China.PATIENTS:Children older than 28 days, up to 18 years old.INTERVENTIONS:None.MEASUREMENTS AND MAIN RESULTS:CCCs were defined according to the Pediatric Complex Chronic Conditions Classification System Version 2. Of 9589 PICU admissions, 49% had one or more CCCs. Admissions with CCCs accounted for 69.8% of the adverse outcomes, 70.8% of in-hospital deaths, 58% of multiple organ dysfunction cases, and 57.6% of total patient days. Admissions with multiple CCC diagnoses were associated with higher odds of adverse outcomes compared with admissions with a single CCC diagnosis (p < 0.0001). Transplantation and metabolic CCCs were associated with greater odds of adverse outcomes (odds ratios [OR], 6.06 and 5.27, respectively; p < 0.05). The most common co-occurring CCC categories were malignancy and hematologic/immunologic disorders. Among the most frequent combinations, the co-occurrence of metabolic and malignancy CCC was associated with greater odds of adverse outcomes (OR, 8.54 [95% CI, 4.48–16.29]). Admission with CCC was associated with pneumonia and sepsis (p < 0.05).CONCLUSIONS:In our 2020–2025 experience, admission of patients with CCCs accounted for most of the adverse PICU outcomes. The odds of adverse outcomes varied across CCC types and combinations. Hence, incorporating CCCs into a stratified management approach across our healthcare system may support more effective planning and delivery of care.

Over the last decade, increasingly around the world, children with medical complexity (CMC) form an important part of the pediatric intensive care practice (1–5). These patients often present with multifaceted health issues and prolonged hospitalizations and pose unique challenges in diagnosis, treatment, and continuity of care (5–8). For example, to classify CMC, various definitions and classification systems, such as complex chronic conditions (CCCs) and the pediatric medical complexity algorithm, yield different cohorts when applied to over 290,000 pediatric patients in the U.S. Virtual Pediatric Systems database for 2017–2019 (9). In this context, the term CCC is now more commonly used: the original definition arose from a 1980–1997 cohort study and was “any medical condition that can be reasonably expected to last at least 12 months (unless death intervenes) and to involve either several different organ systems or one organ system severely enough to require specialty pediatric care and probably some period of hospitalization in a tertiary care center” (10, 11). Now, despite the growing importance of CCCs in PICUs, data from China are limited.

Therefore, we aimed to describe PICU patients with CCCs managed in a single center in central-southern China. Our objective was, first, to identify the types of CCC. Second, we examined the combinations of problems associated with greater odds of adverse outcomes.

METHODS

Our retrospective study was approved by the Hunan Children’s Hospital (HCH) Institutional Review Board (IRB, “Clustering Patterns and Prognostic Impact of Complex Chronic Conditions in the PICU”; Approval Number: HCHLL-2025-272) on August 14, 2025. The need for informed consent was waved by the IRB because of the retrospective design. The study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the hospital IRB and was fully compliant with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975.

The cohort comprised medical records from the PICU at HCH for the period January 2020 to February 2025. HCH is a major tertiary referral center that serves a large pediatric population across central-southern China. We included children who were younger than 18 years and older than 28 days. Study data were extracted from the electronic medical records and included demographic characteristics, admitting department, PICU length of stay (LOS), and admission and discharge diagnoses with corresponding International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes, and discharge status. For children with multiple PICU admissions, each admission was treated as an independent observation in the analysis.

Definitions and Outcomes

We defined CCCs according to the 2014 “Pediatric Complex Chronic Conditions Classification System Version 2” (11). That is, we used the ICD-10 code list for CCCs (11), and via code matching using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), we classified patient data into 12 categories according to the classification system. The CCC diagnoses and corresponding medical records were reviewed by two researchers to verify whether they met the definition for CCCs. Diagnoses initially identified as CCCs but actually representing acute conditions that developed during the current hospitalization were excluded. The number of CCCs per patient was calculated based on the number of distinct CCC categories represented.

The main outcome variable was a composite endpoint of adverse outcome, defined as in-hospital death or discharge following withdrawal of advanced life-sustaining treatment. In our PICU, many families of critically ill children choose to take their child home for palliative care when the condition reaches a terminal stage. Therefore, using in-hospital mortality alone as the primary outcome may underestimate the severity of outcomes. Discharge following withdrawal of advanced life-sustaining treatment in the PICU reflects both a high risk of death after discharge and a subgroup of patients with persistent critical illness despite treatment during hospitalization. Accordingly, this condition was also classified as an adverse outcome. The secondary outcome was multiple organ dysfunction (MOD) occurring during hospitalization. To capture clinical deterioration attributable to the index illness, preexisting chronic dysfunction present at baseline was excluded from the assessment of MOD, whereas acute worsening of chronic dysfunction was included. The diagnostic criteria for MOD are provided in the Appendix S1 (https://links.lww.com/PCC/C752). Our definition of “MOD” was not a formal diagnosis of other uses of MODS in the literature. In-hospital death and LOS were examined as additional outcome measures.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were presented as absolute counts and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Between-group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs to assess associations between CCC and outcomes. A co-occurrence matrix was generated using the tidyverse package in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) to identify the most common CCC categories and diagnoses. Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the distribution of CCCs and outcomes among admissions admitted from different departments, across PICU-dominant disease subgroups, and between the COVID-19 and post-COVID periods. All hypothesis tests were two-tailed with a type I error rate fixed at 5%. Analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute). Figures were created in R 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing ) and GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

RESULTS

The 2020–2025 study cohort included 9589 admissions corresponding to 8872 unique patients (Fig. 1). Among the admissions, 4694 of 9589 admissions (49.0% [95% CI, 48.0–50.0%]) had at least one CCC diagnosis. Of these, 3335 of 4694 admissions (71.0%) had a single CCC diagnosis, and 1359 of 4694 admissions (29.0%) had multiple CCC diagnoses. The median number of CCCs was 1 (IQR, 1–2), with a maximum of 4. Respiratory was the most prevalent category in the cohort, accounting for 30.4% of CCC admissions, followed by malignancy (23.1%) and metabolic disorders (14.2%).

Table 1 shows the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population. Classification as CCC, as opposed to not, was associated with being younger (median age 1.5 vs. 2.7 yr; p < 0.0001). Overall, most admissions were admitted directly to the PICU (70.7%). In both admissions with and without CCC, pneumonia was the most frequent admitting diagnosis. The overall prevalence of adverse outcomes was 3.5%. On comparing the CCC group with non-CCC admission, any CCC was associated with a higher proportion of cases with adverse outcomes: 5.0% vs. 2.1%; p value of less than 0.0001. Other associations on comparing any CCC with non-CCC grouping, included a higher proportion of in-hospital death, 1.8% vs. 0.7% (p < 0.0001); a higher proportion with MOD, 22.2% vs. 15.4% (p < 0.0001); and a longer median hospital LOS, 15 vs. 10 days (p < 0.0001). Admissions with CCC, as opposed to without, was also associated with higher prevalence of hepatic, renal, and coagulation abnormalities (see Table S1, https://links.lww.com/PCC/C752). Admissions with CCC accounted for 236 of 338 adverse outcomes (69.8%), 85 of 120 in-hospital deaths (70.8%), and 1044 of 1799 admissions complicated by MOD (58%). The total patient days of the population were 163,628, of which the CCC admissions accounted for 57.6%. Admissions with multiple CCC diagnoses, compared with admissions with single CCC, were also associated with worse outcomes (all p < 0.05).

Risk According to CCC Categories

Table 2 presents the odds of adverse outcomes and MOD associated with various CCC categories. Among the 12 CCC categories, compared with admission without any CCC, greater odds of adverse outcomes were present for transplantation (OR, 6.06 [95% CI, 2.1–17.49]), metabolic conditions (OR, 5.27 [95% CI, 3.83–7.25]), and neurologic or neuromuscular conditions (OR, 4.04 [95% CI, 2.74–5.97]). Similarly, there were greater odds of MOD for transplantation (OR, 11.95 [95% CI, 5.83–24.50]), and technology dependence (OR, 3.34 [95% CI, 2.26–4.94)]. Last, for admissions in patients with one CCC, the odds of an adverse outcome were 2.09 (95% CI, 1.61–2.71), and for admissions in patients with multiple CCCs, the odds of an adverse outcome were 3.49 (95% CI, 2.62–4.65).

Figure 2B also shows the ORs of adverse outcomes for each CCC category, stratified by the presence of a single CCC vs. multiple CCCs. On broad inspection, co-occurrence of CCCs was associated with greater odds of adverse outcomes in comparison with occurrence of CCC alone. Last, Figure 2C shows the most frequent individual CCC diagnoses and their associated adverse outcome prevalence. Congenital malformations of the larynx (ICD-10 code Q31) were the most prevalent CCC diagnosis, although we failed to identify an association with adverse outcomes. The second most common CCC was malignant neoplasm (ICD-10 code C), which was associated with greater odds of adverse outcome (OR, 3.63 [95% CI, 2.66–4.97]). The top 10 most frequent co-occurring CCC diagnoses are presented in Table S2 (https://links.lww.com/PCC/C752).

In the study cohort, the most common technology-dependence conditions were bone-marrow transplant rejection (n = 27, adverse outcome prevalence [adv%] = 7.4%), ileostomy status (n = 24, adv% = 0), and tracheostomy status (n = 20, adv% = 0). For posttransplant-related conditions, bone marrow transplant rejection (n = 27, adv% = 7.4%), liver transplant rejection (n = 6, adv% = 33.3%), and kidney transplant rejection (n = 2, adv% = 0) were observed.

Subgroup Analysis

Subgroup analysis showed that the distribution of CCCs and CCC-related outcomes varied by admitting department (see Table S3, https://links.lww.com/PCC/C752). Admission after surgery was associated with the highest rate of CCCs, whereas admission with any CCC coming via PICU or internal medicine was associated with high proportions of adverse outcomes and MOD. Admissions with sepsis or pneumonia also showed higher proportions of CCCs, multiple CCCs, and MOD compared with those without these conditions (p < 0.05; see Tables S4 and S5, https://links.lww.com/PCC/C752). Among admissions with CCCs, sepsis was associated with higher proportions of adverse outcomes (p < 0.0001), but we failed to identify such an association with pneumonia. Subgroup analysis also examined the distribution of CCCs during and after the COVID-19 pandemic; admission during the pandemic was associated with higher proportions of adverse outcomes among the CCC population (p < 0.05; see Table S6, https://links.lww.com/PCC/C752). Also, during the pandemic, pneumonia occurred in 33.3% of the CCC population and 19.8% in the non-CCC population (see Table S7, https://links.lww.com/PCC/C752).

DISCUSSION

In this single-center, retrospective study covering 62 months (2020–2025), we found that close to half of our PICU admissions had one or more CCCs. Among admissions with CCCs, 29% had multiple CCCs, and this grouping as opposed to a single CCC was associated with worse outcomes. Transplantation and metabolic CCCs were associated with greater odds of adverse outcomes. The most common co-occurring CCC categories were malignancy and hematologic/immunologic. Among the most frequent combinations, the co-occurrence of metabolic and malignancy CCC was associated with greater odds of adverse outcomes.

During 2020–2025, half of the admissions to our PICU in central-southern China had CCCs, which is comparable to the rate in PICUs in Brazil (59.7%) during the COVID-19 pandemic and in the United States (57.7%) in 2017–2019 (2, 9). Although based on general pediatric inpatient populations rather than PICU cohorts, data from the Kids’ Inpatient Database in the United States from 2009 to 2022 suggest that the distribution of CCC types has changed over time (12). The predominant types of CCCs in the PICU also differed across studies. An observational study from 19 PICUs in Argentina (2015–2016) found that the predominant CCC type was cardiovascular (22.4%), followed by neuromuscular (18.8%) (1). In our cohort, respiratory (30.4%) and malignancy (23.1%) CCCs were most common, and neurologic CCCs accounted for 9.9%. These differences may reflect history or regional and temporal variations in healthcare systems, emphasizing the need for context-specific care planning and resource allocation.

Our data show that among all CCC categories, respiratory and renal/urologic CCCs were associated with lower odds of adverse outcomes, whereas metabolic and neurologic/neuromuscular CCCs were associated with greater odds. Advancements in organ support within PICUs, particularly for respiratory and renal systems, have improved outcomes for children with primary dysfunction in these organs (13, 14). However, children with neurologic/neuromuscular and metabolic CCCs pose particular challenges in pediatric critical care, and their greater odds of adverse outcomes have been reported worldwide (15–17). These conditions often involve complex pathophysiology, limited reversibility, and a lack of targeted therapies. Neurologic CCCs, for example, may impair airway protection, increase vulnerability to infections, and complicate sedation and ventilation strategies (17, 18). Similarly, metabolic conditions can be rapidly progressive, difficult to diagnose in acute settings, and require highly specialized management that may not be readily available in all centers. The persistently high risk of adverse outcomes in these groups highlights a critical need for earlier identification, multidisciplinary protocols, and disease-specific PICU care pathways.

Despite their low prevalence, admissions in technology-dependent and transplantation-related CCCs were associated with greater odds of adverse outcomes. These patients require complex, continuous support and remain vulnerable despite specialized outpatient care (19, 20). Historically, in our healthcare system, as in many others, these children are managed through specialized care models that include home health support and individualized care plans. Still, our findings highlight the need for enhanced early-warning systems, dedicated PICU protocols, and anticipatory care planning that integrates both inpatient and outpatient teams. Notably, the prevalence of technology-dependent children in our cohort was lower than that reported in other countries (21). This difference may relate to regional healthcare practice patterns; however, cross-national comparisons and analyses of the underlying causes warrant further investigation.

The accumulation of multiple CCCs is not random, as many patients have disease and/or etiologies that arise as complications. For example, leukemia and lymphoma may occur in thalassemia due to shared hematopoietic origins (22); mitral valve insufficiency may lead to pulmonary hypertension; and genetic syndromes often involve multiple organ systems. As the number of affected systems increases, so does the clinical complexity, care coordination burden, and risk of treatment-related complications. Our study shows that children with multiple CCCs face substantially higher risks of adverse outcomes, underscoring the challenges PICUs must be prepared to address.

In our cohort, the presence of CCCs was associated with pneumonia and sepsis, and patients with CCCs who developed pneumonia or sepsis experienced high rates of MOD. Together, these findings underscore that CCCs may not only modify the course of these acute illnesses but also predispose children to their development in the first place. For example, children with neurologic CCCs may be at increased risk for aspiration pneumonia, while immunocompromised patients are particularly susceptible to severe sepsis. Accordingly, the management of acute conditions in children with CCCs should begin with risk-adapted strategies even in early stages of illness, including preventive interventions, close monitoring, and timely escalation of care, to reduce progression to critical illness. Care coordination models have been developed for both specific CCC populations and PICU patients with CCCs (23–26). Moving forward, it is important to adapt and integrate these models according to local healthcare resources and to evaluate their effectiveness. Furthermore, these models may need to evolve over time in response to changes in patient populations, healthcare system conditions, and clinical decision-making influenced by available resources and technology.

Our study has several limitations. First, as a single-center study at a tertiary referral hospital, selection bias may exist, and the patient population may not represent other hospital types or regions in China. Second, the CCC classification includes a broad range of diagnoses with variable severity and resource needs. Some, like congenital malformations of the larynx and exomphalos, may have limited clinical impact. Further studies should stratify CCCs by severity and critical care demand for application in risk evaluation. Third, the baseline variables and outcomes were limited and may not fully reflect the disease burden of CCCs in the PICU. Future studies should include risk-adjustment and assessment tools (27) to better evaluate patient characteristics and outcomes. Fourth, due to limited data in this retrospective study, we were unable to distinguish between elective and nonelective PICU admission. Additionally, CCC classification continues to evolve. For example, version 3 of the Pediatric CCC System is based on ICD-10-CM (28), whereas we used version 2 because our dataset was coded in ICD-10 (11). Differences between versions should be considered when comparing our study with historical studies. Finally, research should extend beyond risk stratification to identify specific CCC types posing distinct technical or care challenges in the PICU, guiding targeted protocols, training, and system improvements. Incorporating family-centered interventions may also help address unique needs and improve outcomes (29).

In conclusion, in our PICU center in central-southern China, 2020–2025, admissions in patients with CCCs accounted for most adverse outcomes. The associated odds of these outcomes varied by the type and combination of CCCs, with CCCs involving the metabolic and neuromuscular systems posing significant challenges for PICU care. To better address the care needs of high-prevalence and high-risk CCCs, targeted PICU protocols and dedicated research initiatives should be developed.
