Final Report on

 Quantifying Uncertainties in Earth's Energy Budget by Cloud Feedback and Ocean Heat
 Uptake Using E3SM-Slab Ocean Configurations

Objectives of the Research

To improve predictive skills of Earth System Models, we need to better understand processes
that control Earth's energy budget via ocean, atmosphere, and cryosphere interactions. Simulated
energy budget in comprehensive Earth System Models shows a wide range, leading to large
uncertainties in predicting Earth system dynamic and thermodynamic variations and associated
social-economic impacts. Uncertainties in cloud feedback have been identified as the main cause
of the large inter-model spread, but oceanic adjustments, especially those associated with ocean
heat uptake (OHU) and the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), also play an
important role. In this research, we focus on understanding the individual and combined roles of
cloud feedback and ocean adjustments on modulating Earth's energy balance.

The proposed research is motivated by our overarching hypothesis that oceanic adjustment is a
key source of uncertainty, in addition to those associated with the cloud feedback; further, the
ocean adjustment and associated OHU work through the cloud feedback to modulate Earth's
energy budget and temperature variations. We test this hypothesis using numerical experiments
where we systematically enable and disable cloud feedback in conjunction with perturbations to
OHU.

Specific objectives of proposed research are:

1) Identify interaction between cloud feedback, OHU characteristics, the AMOC, and their
respective roles in modulating Earth's energy balance and temperature variations.

2) Determine individual contribution from each process and their time-dependency using E3SM-
slab ocean configurations.

3) Quantify the degree to which surface temperature and its spatiotemporal variability are caused
by distinct OHU patterns in E3SM-SOM.

Publications Resulted from This Research:

The role of cloud feedbacks in Arctic response to forcing variability remains unclear. Traditional
feedback analysis diagnoses the net cloud feedback as strongly positive in the tropics but either
weak or negative in the Arctic, suggesting that arctic response would be amplified if cloud
feedbacks were suppressed. However, in cloud-locking experiments using the slab ocean version
of E3SM, we find that suppressing cloud feedbacks results in a substantial decrease in Arctic
response. We identified mechanisms contributing to this counter-intuitive result. Our results
suggest that traditional attributions based on local feedback analysis overlook key interactions
between extra-polar cloud changes, poleward AHT, and non-cloud feedbacks in the Arctic.

These new insights are summarized in a peer-reviewed publication:

 Li, Q., K. C. Armour, W. Cheng, L. Thompson, J. Lu, J. Zhang, B. E. Harrop, O. A. Garuba,
 Y. Luo (2025). Extra-polar cloud feedbacks as a driver of Arctic amplification.
   https://doi.org/10.1175/JCLI-D-24-0724.1.

In a separate study, we use a passive–active decomposition to assess the relative contributions of
ocean circulation and passive ocean temperature changes to polar ocean heat transport (OHT)
anomalies. Passive temperature changes are defined as those thermally forced by the atmosphere
in the absence of circulation changes. In this passive–active decomposition, an advective term
involving both circulation and passive temperature changes remains ambiguous — classifying it
as active implies circulation changes dominate Southern Ocean OHT anomalies, whereas
classifying it as passive implies temperature changes dominate. However, both interpretations
imply that ocean circulation changes have a much weaker effect on polar OHT anomalies than
inferred from the standard decomposition. These results help reconcile conclusions from studies
using the standard decomposition with those using passive tracer methods to assess the role of
circulation changes in polar OHT anomalies. This is published as:

 Li., Q., W. Cheng, K. C. Armour, L. Thompson, O. A. Garuba (2025). Revisiting the role of
 ocean circulation changes in polar ocean heat transport anomalies under global warming.
   https://doi.org/10.1175/JCLI-D-25-0135.1.
