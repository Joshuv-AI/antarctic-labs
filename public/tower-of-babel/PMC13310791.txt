Baclofen Misuse in Clinical Practice: A Case Report on Management Strategies
Authors: Mostafa Yousef, Mohamed Elsayed, Ahmed Hosney Nada, Olivier Uwishema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBaclofen, a GABA_B receptor agonist, is increasingly linked to misuse and severe withdrawal symptoms, including acute neuropsychiatric manifestations. We present a 26‐year‐old male exhibiting severe substance use disorder and recurrent depressive episodes, accompanied by profound insomnia, suicidal ideation, and visual hallucinations. He had been taking baclofen in high doses (200 mg/day) for 2–3 years, and he was still using cocaine and opioids from time to time. There was no acute intoxication, and the lab tests and electrocardiography were normal. It was thought that he was going through baclofen withdrawal, so he was given lorazepam 2 mg, which was slowly reduced over 2 weeks, leading to clinical stabilization. This case emphasizes baclofen withdrawal as a potentially fatal factor in psychiatric decline and underscores the necessity for prompt identification, meticulous observation, and tailored intervention particularly in patients with psychiatric disorders and comorbid substance use.Keywords: baclofen, benzodiazepines, dependence, lorazepam, withdrawal treatment

Introduction

Substance dependence, or substance use disorder (SUD), is a condition that shows itself through cognitive, behavioral, and physical symptoms. It means that a person keeps using a psychoactive substance even though it has serious negative effects on their life. It usually includes tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, and a compulsive pattern of use [1, 2]. There are many different reasons why people become dependent on drugs. At first, using drugs activates the basal ganglia, which causes a huge release of dopamine and a very intense feeling of happiness [3]. When used for a long time, the brain's stress system, especially in the extended amygdala, gets too active [3]. At this point, people usually use the drug not to feel good, but to ease the pain and anxiety that come with withdrawal [4]. Chronic substance use exacerbates the dysfunction of the prefrontal cortex, leading to compulsive drug‐seeking behavior [1].

To treat substance dependence, an individualized, evidence‐based approach is needed [5]. This means using behavioral therapy, medication, and psychosocial support to help with withdrawal symptoms and deal with underlying psychological problems [5].

Baclofen is a GABA_B receptor agonist that boosts inhibitory signaling, which makes muscles relax and the central nervous system less active [6]. Prolonged baclofen use may result in physical dependence; therefore, abrupt cessation should be avoided because it can precipitate severe and potentially fatal withdrawal syndrome. This case report outlines a pharmacological management approach for a patient with confirmed baclofen use and dependence.

Case History/ Examination

A 26‐year‐old male exhibited an acute psychiatric crisis marked by suicidal ideation, insomnia, and perceptual disturbances. He lived with his parents and received financial support from unemployment benefits (Bürgergeld, Germany) since he was unfit for work because of health problems. He was single, had no children, and received most of his emotional support from his mother.

The patient had previously attempted suicide in August 2025 and had a history of recurrent depressive episodes. He had earlier declared his intention to self‐harm by severing his radial artery, and at the time of presentation, he was exhibiting active suicidal ideation with imminent risk. Additionally, he had a history of self‐harming behaviors that were not suicidal. A previous delirium episode was recorded in 2017. He reported experiencing significant sleep disturbances, including persistent visual hallucinations and complete insomnia since October 23, 2025. An emergency transfer for detoxification and psychiatric stabilization was started due to the severity of the symptoms and the immediate danger.

The patient had a recorded history of serious substance use disorder (C2 classification). He stated that he uses cocaine regularly at around 2 g per week (not every day) and has sporadic opioid use, while denying any current alcohol intake. Several previous detoxification attempts have been mentioned. At the time of evaluation, he was not visibly intoxicated.

His medical history was notable for prolonged baclofen use at a daily dose of 200 mg since 2017. Initially, the medication was used intermittently; however, during the last 2 years prior to presentation, it had been taken continuously. There was no history of seizures.

Differential Diagnosis

Given the constellation of acute neuropsychiatric symptoms—insomnia, visual hallucinations, and suicidal ideation in the context of polysubstance use—the following differential diagnoses were considered:

Baclofen withdrawal syndrome: The leading working diagnosis given 2–3 years of high‐dose baclofen use (200 mg/day) with recent dose reduction and temporal alignment of acute psychiatric symptoms. Investigations and Treatment

Vital Signs and Physical Examination on Admission

• Heart Rate: 98 bpm (mild tachycardia).

• Blood Pressure: 142/88 mmHg (elevated, consistent with autonomic dysregulation).

• Temperature: 37.1°C (normal).

• Respiratory Rate: 18 breaths/min (normal).

• Oxygen Saturation: 98% on room air.

• Physical signs: Fine tremor in hands; mild to moderate diaphoresis (sweating).

Laboratory and Imaging Results

• Complete Blood Count: Normal.

• Basic Metabolic Panel (electrolytes, renal function, liver function): All within normal limits.

• Urine Drug Screen: Positive for cocaine metabolites; trace opioid metabolites detected.

• 12‐lead Electrocardiogram: Normal (no QT prolongation, no arrhythmia).

• Thyroid‐Stimulating Hormone (TSH): Normal (rules out thyroid‐related mood/anxiety symptoms).

Mental State Examination

• Appearance: Unkempt, visibly distressed.

• Behavior: Restless, intermittently agitated; poor eye contact; increased psychomotor activity.

• Speech: Normal rate and volume; coherent but occasionally circumstantial.

• Mood: Patient reported feeling “hopeless and desperate”.

• Affect: Dysphoric and restricted.

• Thought Form: Coherent and goal‐directed; no formal thought disorder detected.

• Thought Content: Active suicidal ideation with specific intent (patient mentioned intention to sever radial artery); preoccupation with guilt, worthlessness, and nihilistic ideation.

• Perception: Visual hallucinations reported (described as fleeting shadows and geometric patterns, particularly in low‐light environments); no auditory or tactile hallucinations.

• Cognition: Oriented to person, place, and time (3/3); normal digit span; no obvious memory impairment detected on bedside testing.

• Insight and Judgment: Poor insight regarding substance use; patient recognized need for help but showed ambivalence about treatment adherence.

Standardized Assessments

• Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol Scale (CIWA‐Ar, adapted): Patient scored in the moderate‐to‐severe range on general withdrawal symptom assessment.

• Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9): Score of 24/27 (severe depression) during stabilization phase, consistent with major depressive episode with psychotic features.

• Suicide Risk Assessment: High risk initially; risk stratification improved to moderate risk by Day 7 of admission and low risk by Day 14.

During the clinical evaluation, the patient showed signs of severe distress, exhibiting notable insomnia, visual hallucinations, and thoughts of suicide. Even with no signs of severe intoxication or metabolic issues, his mental condition was viewed as unstable, requiring immediate psychiatric assistance and careful observation.

Lorazepam was started at 2 mg, followed by gradual tapering over 2 weeks.

Conclusion and Results (Outcomes and Follow Up)

The administration of lorazepam 2 mg, progressively reduced over a fortnight, resulted in clinical stabilization, presumably via the modulation of GABAergic neurotransmission.

A structured post‐detoxification follow‐up plan is essential given the complexity of this case, encompassing pharmacological, psychiatric, and psychosocial domains:

Short‐term (Weeks 1–4): Daily nursing and psychiatric assessment during inpatient stay; close monitoring of lorazepam taper tolerance and withdrawal symptom rebound; urine drug screening to confirm abstinence from cocaine and opioids; repeated suicide risk stratification with documentation; re‐evaluation of sleep and perceptual symptoms to confirm resolution of withdrawal‐related hallucinations.

Medium‐term (Months 1–3): Outpatient psychiatric follow‐up at least bi‐weekly for the first month, then monthly; initiation or optimization of antidepressant therapy targeting the recurrent depressive disorder once the patient is medically stable and abstinent; referral to a specialized addiction medicine clinic or dual‐diagnosis program; structured psychotherapy (e.g., cognitive‐behavioral therapy for substance use and depression); family psychoeducation, particularly engaging the mother as primary support.

Discussion

Baclofen withdrawal typically presents with a constellation of symptoms including seizures, autonomic dysregulation (tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia), tremor, and agitation. This case is notable because our patient presented predominantly with psychiatric symptoms—severe insomnia, visual hallucinations, and active suicidal ideation—in the ABSENCE of seizures, marked vital‐sign abnormalities, or abnormal laboratory findings. This psychiatric phenotype of baclofen withdrawal can easily be missed or misattributed to primary psychotic disorder, major depressive episode with psychotic features, or cocaine‐induced psychosis, particularly in polysubstance users where diagnostic differentiation is challenging.

Early recognition of baclofen withdrawal as a potential cause of acute psychiatric decompensation in patients with known high‐dose baclofen use is critical. In the present case, the CIWA‐Ar was employed as a general tool for assessing withdrawal severity and not specifically for alcohol withdrawal. The clinical response to benzodiazepines (lorazepam in our case) supports, but does not definitively prove, baclofen withdrawal as the primary driver; other psychiatric conditions also respond to benzodiazepines. Nevertheless, structured benzodiazepine taper with careful psychiatric monitoring successfully de‐escalated this patient's presentation.

Pharmacological Mechanism

Baclofen is a GABA_B receptor agonist. Chronic high‐dose use leads to adaptive downregulation of GABA_B receptors and compensatory upregulation of excitatory glutamatergic and noradrenergic signaling [7]. Upon dose reduction or cessation, loss of GABAergic inhibition creates a state of central nervous system hyperexcitability, manifesting as autonomic instability, seizure risk, and—as in this case—acute psychiatric symptoms including insomnia, hallucinations, and suicidal ideation [8, 9].

Benzodiazepines such as lorazepam enhance GABAergic neurotransmission via GABA_A receptors, providing symptomatic relief and preventing escalation to seizures during withdrawal [8]. In our patient, lorazepam was initiated at 2 mg and gradually tapered over 14 days, a duration consistent with standard clinical practice for baclofen‐withdrawal management (typical taper: 7–21 days depending on severity and clinical response).
