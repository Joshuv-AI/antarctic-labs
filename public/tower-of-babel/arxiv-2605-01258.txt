Toward the Goldilocks blind compression of quantum states
Hyunho Cha, Chae-Yeun Park, Jungwoo Lee

Abstract
Quantum autoencoders (QAEs) are learning architectures that compress quantum data into a low-dimensional latent state while preserving the information needed for reconstruction. We study blind single-copy compression of quantum states through a k k -qubit bottleneck and investigate the minimal circuit width required to attain the information-theoretic optimum under average infidelity. Between the conventional architecture, which is narrow but nonuniversal, and fully general completely positive and trace preserving (CPTP) realizations, which are universal but overparameterized, we identify a Goldilocks regime. We prove that for every distribution of pure n n -qubit states, there exists a QAE with exactly k k encoder ancillas and n n decoder ancillas that achieves the optimal fidelity over all CPTP encoder–decoder pairs. The encoder-side statement is sharp in that we construct source families for which every optimal scheme necessarily uses at least k k encoder ancillas, thereby determining the universal encoder threshold exactly. On the decoder side, we show that isometric decoders are exactly optimal for several analytically tractable source families, but we also exhibit an explicit counterexample demonstrating that decoder isometry is not universally sufficient. Nevertheless, numerical experiments indicate that the performance gap is practically negligible.

Introduction

The rapid development of controllable quantum platforms has brought into focus a central challenge in quantum physics, namely how researchers can manipulate and preserve information encoded in exponentially large Hilbert spaces with finite and noisy resources. Manipulation of quantum states serves as the structural foundation for diverse fields, ranging from quantum simulation of many-body dynamics and chemistry to quantum computation, communication, and sensing

For near-term devices in particular, the available number of high-fidelity qubits and coherent operations remains a dominant bottleneck

]

This necessitates architectures that use the quantum hardware at an appropriate scale by being neither underpowered for the task nor overprovisioned in ways that amplify noise.

Compression is the canonical response to resource scarcity in classical information theory

Experimental demonstrations of this compression have also been realized

Within this landscape, quantum autoencoders (QAEs) were proposed as a route to compression of quantum data that may not admit efficient classical descriptions

-qubit input state into a

QAEs have been studied theoretically and deployed in diverse physical contexts

These developments demonstrate that QAE-style compression is becoming an architectural primitive for quantum technologies.

A major open issue, however, concerns resource optimality. Most QAE implementations adopt a unitary–trace architecture with a circuit acting on the system and a small number of ancillas (or auxiliary quantum systems), which can severely restrict the set of realizable encoder and decoder channels. From the perspective of quantum channel theory, the most general encoder and decoder are
completely positive and trace preserving
(CPTP) maps

;

;
31
;

Indeed, recent fully quantum variational autoencoder frameworks have proposed universal constructions capable of representing arbitrary CPTP maps by introducing sufficiently large ancillas

[
47
;
54
;
6

This motivates a fundamental question: for a given objective, what is the minimal circuit width required to achieve the information-theoretic optimum?

In this work, we investigate this question for blind, single-copy quantum state compression under the
infidelity
loss
[
46
;
30
;
11
]
.
In essence, for the infidelity objective there is a
Goldilocks
architecture wide enough to reach the information-theoretic optimum, yet narrow enough to avoid the redundant ancilla overhead.

On the encoder side, we determine the ancilla cost sharply. Specifically, we prove matching sufficient and necessary conditions on the encoder ancilla size. In this sense, the encoder ancilla requirement is characterized exactly. This singles out a natural minimal encoder architecture that is expressive enough to attain the optimum while avoiding unnecessary overhead.

We further provide both analytical and numerical evidence for a conceptually appealing practical principle. Although our counterexample demonstrates that, in blind single-copy compression with infidelity loss, an isometric decoder need not always attain the exact theoretical optimum, numerical results suggest that restricting the decoder to be isometric nevertheless yields empirically near-optimal performance. We prove exact optimality of isometric decoders for several sources with closed-form distributions, and we find consistent numerical support on datasets constructed from MNIST-encoded quantum states
[
42
]
, where the observed performance gap is negligible. This makes isometric decoders particularly attractive in practice.
They offer near-optimal performance while reducing model size and computational overhead.

Preliminaries and prior work

For a positive integer
d
, let
H_{d}\cong\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space and let
L(H_{d})
denote the linear operators on
H_{d}
. A qubit is a two-dimensional quantum system, so an
n
-qubit register has Hilbert space
H_{2^{n}}\cong(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
and hence dimension
2^{n}
. For quantum registers
A,B,\ldots
, we write
H_{A},H_{B},\ldots
for the associated Hilbert spaces and
H_{AB}:=H_{A}\otimes H_{B}
for the composite system. If the same physical space is viewed under two factorizations, we write for example
AB\equiv CD
. We use the computational basis
\{|x\rangle:x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\}
of an
n
-qubit system (equivalently
\{|j\rangle:0\leq j\leq 2^{n}-1\}
after fixing the binary-to-integer identification). In Dirac notation,
|\psi\rangle
is a
ket
,
\langle\psi|:=|\psi\rangle^{\dagger}
is the corresponding
bra
, and
\langle\phi|\psi\rangle
is the inner product. State vectors are always taken to have unit norm.

A pure state is represented by a unit vector
|\psi\rangle
, or equivalently by the rank-one
density operator

|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
. More generally, a mixed state on a Hilbert space
H
is a density operator
\rho\in L(H)
, meaning
\rho\geq 0
and
\Tr(\rho)=1
. Tensor products are used for spaces, state vectors, and operators. For a bipartite state
\rho_{AB}\in L(H_{AB})
, the reduced state on
A
is
\rho_{A}:=\Tr_{B}(\rho_{AB})
, where
\Tr_{B}
is the partial trace. Operationally, this is exactly the operation of discarding subsystem
B
. Likewise
\rho_{B}:=\Tr_{A}(\rho_{AB})
.

A
superoperator
is a linear map between operator spaces. The physical maps considered here are quantum channels, namely CPTP maps
\Phi:L(H_{A})\to L(H_{B})
. Trace preservation means
\Tr[\Phi(X)]=\Tr[X]
, while complete positivity means that
\Phi\otimes\mathrm{id}_{R}
sends positive operators to positive operators for every auxiliary register
R
. Closed-system evolution is unitary: if
U\in\mathrm{U}(d)
, the unitary group on
H_{d}
, then the induced channel is the conjugation map

\Ad_{U}(\rho):=U\rho U^{\dagger}.

More generally, every CPTP map admits a
Kraus representation

\Phi(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}K_{i}\rho K_{i}^{\dagger},\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{r}K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}=I,

and, equivalently, a Stinespring realization by adjoining an ancilla in a fixed state, applying a joint unitary, and partially tracing out an environment. The smallest possible number
r
of Kraus operators is the
Kraus rank
of
\Phi
.

A more detailed mathematical formulation can be found in Appendix
B
.

Prior architectures.

Among the many QAE constructions studied in the literature, two architectures are especially relevant for the resource question considered here. The
conventional QAE
introduced in
[
57
;
30
;
9
;
10
;
51
;
64
;
70
]
uses an
n
-qubit unitary on the input register, discards
n-k
qubits to obtain a
k
-qubit latent register, and reconstructs by adjoining
n-k
fresh ancillas followed by another
n
-qubit unitary. Anticipating the
(n,k,n_{B},n_{E})
notation introduced in Section
3
, this is the
(n,k,0,n-k)
architecture. Its main virtue is its small circuit width, since both the encoder and decoder act on only
n
qubits. However, it constrains the encoder to be a unitary-on-system followed by a partial trace with no additional encoder ancilla, and it constrains the decoder to be an isometric channel. Accordingly, it is not universal for blind single-copy compression through a
k
-qubit bottleneck.

At the opposite end of the expressivity spectrum,
[
67
]
proposed the
\zeta
-QVAE
, a quantum variational autoencoder with mixed latent representations and an explicit latent-space regularization term. In that framework, both the encoder and decoder are modeled as general CPTP maps via
Stinespring dilations

[
65
]
with sufficiently large ancillas, so the construction is universal. Specialized to the compression setting studied here, one convenient realization corresponds to
n_{B}=n+2k
encoder ancillas and
n_{E}=2n+k
decoder ancillas, so that both associated unitaries act on
2(n+k)
qubits. Thus the conventional QAE and the
\zeta
-QVAE represent the two extremes relevant to our analysis. The former is resource-efficient but nonuniversal, whereas the latter is universal but substantially wider than may be necessary. This contrast motivates the main question of the present work, namely whether there exists a strictly smaller universal architecture for blind single-copy compression under the infidelity objective.

3
Main results

Overview.

In this section, we establish four primary results. First,
k
encoder ancilla qubits and
n
decoder ancilla qubits always suffice to match the best possible CPTP encoder–decoder pair. Second, the encoder count
k
is unavoidable in the worst case. Third, the smallest physical decoder, an isometric decoder with
n-k
ancilla qubits, is not always optimal. Fourth, despite this counterexample, isometric decoders are near-optimal when the source is concentrated near a
2^{k}
-dimensional subspace.

For an
n
-qubit source distribution
\mu
over pure states
|\psi\rangle\in H_{2^{n}}
and integers
n>k\geq 1
, we evaluate an encoder
\mathcal{E}:L(H_{2^{n}})\to L(H_{2^{k}})
and a decoder
\mathcal{D}:L(H_{2^{k}})\to L(H_{2^{n}})
by the average fidelity

F_{\mu}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}):=\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu}\bigl[\langle\psi|(\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E})(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle\bigr].

(3.1)

The corresponding average infidelity is
L_{\mu}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}):=1-F_{\mu}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D})
.

Lemma
3.2
implies that allowing arbitrary fixed mixed ancilla states does not change the optimal average fidelity.
Thus, without loss of generality, we may fix the encoder and decoder ancilla states to all-zero states for the purpose of infidelity minimization. We maintain this convention throughout the remainder of this work.

Remark 3.3
.

At first glance,
[

may appear to contradict Lemma
3.2
, since they report an advantage
from using mixed reference states. There is, however, no contradiction, because the optimization problem studied in
[

is different from the problem considered here.
Lemma

applies to our separately linear objective

[

Our first theorem identifies a universal Goldilocks architecture by showing that

Theorem 3.4

(Universal sufficiency of
(k,n)
ancillas)
.

For every distribution
\mu
of pure
n
-qubit states, there exists an
(n,k,k,n)
-QAE attaining

\max_{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}}F_{\mu}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}),

where the maximum ranges over all CPTP encoders
\mathcal{E}:L(H_{2^{n}})\to L(H_{2^{k}})
and decoders
\mathcal{D}:L(H_{2^{k}})\to L(H_{2^{n}})

Proof sketch.

By a Choi-matrix reformulation, the fidelity functional is separately linear in
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{D}
. Hence an optimum can be chosen with both channels extreme. Extreme encoders
L(H_{2^{n}})\to L(H_{2^{k}})
have Choi rank at most
2^{n}

L(H_{2^{k}})\to L(H_{2^{n}})
have Choi rank at most

\widetilde{U}:H_{2^{n}}\to H_{2^{k}}\otimes H_{2^{n}},\qquad\widetilde{V}:H_{2^{k}}\to H_{2^{n}}\otimes H_{2^{k}},

which extend to unitaries on
n+k

The universal sufficiency theorem is sharp on the encoder side. The source family below already forces
k
encoder ancillas.

Theorem 3.5

(Informal)
.

For every choice of input size
n
and bottleneck size
k<n
, there is a source
distribution of pure
n
-qubit states for which fewer than
k
encoder ancillas
are insufficient to reach the best possible reconstruction fidelity.
More precisely, for this source, no
(n,k,n_{B},n_{E})
-QAE with

can attain
the optimum value of
F_{\mu}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D})
over all CPTP encoders and decoders

In other words, the

Proof sketch.

The proof uses a one-parameter source family
\{\mu_{1,\varepsilon}\}_{0<\varepsilon<1}
, whose construction is given in Appendix
E

differences point in many possible directions. A compression scheme that is optimal must therefore preserve the reference state perfectly while also
handling these hidden directions as well as the
k
-qubit bottleneck allows.
The optimal behavior has a simple operational form. The channel keeps the part
of the input lying in a subspace of the same size as the bottleneck and sends
the remaining part back to the reference state. In other words, the best channel
for this source is a “reset” channel.
The key point is that implementing this reset behavior through the bottleneck requires a sufficiently rich encoder. It can be shown that any architecture
with
n_{B}<k
cannot be optimal for this source. A detailed restatement and full proof are given in Appendix

Corollary 3.6

(Sharp universal encoder threshold)
.

For blind single-copy compression under the infidelity objective,

For the source
\mu_{1,\varepsilon}

The next theorem shows that, although isometric decoders need not be exactly optimal in general, they remain near-optimal whenever the average source state is well concentrated on its top
2^{k}

Theorem 3.12

(Informal)
.

For blind single-copy compression, restricting the decoder to be an isometry is near-optimal whenever the source is close to an
m
-dimensional subspace, where
m=2^{k}
is the latent dimension. More precisely, if
\eta_{m}
denotes the weight of the average source state outside its top
m
eigenspaces, then the best isometric decoder achieves at least a fraction

1-\bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{m}\bigr)\eta_{m}

of the unrestricted optimum average fidelity.

A detailed restatement and full proof are given in Appendix
H
.

5
Discussion

Limitations.

This work restricts attention to blind single-copy compression of pure states, with performance measured solely by reconstruction infidelity. Accordingly, our results should not be interpreted as statements about other objectives, such as latent space regularization, representation learning quality, robustness, or trainability. Such objectives may be valuable in practice, but they lie beyond the scope of the present theory and may modify the optimal resource counts identified here. The conclusions should also be distinguished from results in the literature that optimize different loss functions or assume different informational access. Furthermore, our empirical validation uses an artificially engineered quantum dataset, which is useful for probing the low-dimensional concentration regime but does not adequately represent the entanglement structures of physical quantum data, such as many-body ground states.

Our main results are resource-theoretic statements about blind single-copy compression under the infidelity objective. We identify an architecture that is wide enough to attain the optimum over all CPTP encoder–decoder pairs, yet narrower than fully general models. In particular,
k
encoder ancillas and
n
decoder ancillas are universally sufficient, and the encoder threshold is sharp. The decoder side remains less completely characterized. Specifically, the tight universal decoder ancilla lower bound as a function of
n
and
k
remains open. Because infidelity is the primary objective in many compression tasks, the ancilla counts identified here provide a natural baseline. A promising direction is to study how additional objectives, including explicit regularization and other desiderata for representation learning, alter the architecture or lead to genuinely different optimal tradeoffs.

Appendix B
Basic concepts

B.1
Hilbert spaces, qubits, registers, and Dirac notation

A finite-dimensional Hilbert space is a finite-dimensional complex vector space equipped with an inner product. Throughout,
H_{d}\cong\mathbb{C}^{d}
denotes a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space and
L(H)
denotes the linear operators on
H
. In Dirac notation, a vector is written as a ket
|\psi\rangle
, its conjugate transpose is the bra
\langle\psi|:=|\psi\rangle^{\dagger}
, and the inner product of
|\phi\rangle
and
|\psi\rangle
is
\langle\phi|\psi\rangle
. A vector represents a physical pure state only when it has unit norm,
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1
.
For a vector
|v\rangle
, we write
|v\rangle^{\perp}
for the subspace orthogonal to
|v\rangle
.

A qubit is a system with Hilbert space
H_{2}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}
and computational basis
\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}
. An
n
-qubit register has Hilbert space

H_{2^{n}}\cong(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n},

so an
n
-qubit system has dimension
2^{n}
. For quantum registers
A,B,\ldots
, we write
H_{A},H_{B},\ldots
for the corresponding Hilbert spaces. Composite systems are modeled by tensor products, so
H_{AB}:=H_{A}\otimes H_{B}
. The same physical space may be reinterpreted under a different factorization, written for example as
AB\equiv CD
.

Tensor products are used not only for Hilbert spaces but also for states and operators. Thus
|\psi\rangle_{A}\otimes|\phi\rangle_{B}
is a joint pure state on
AB
, often abbreviated as
|\psi\rangle_{A}|\phi\rangle_{B}
, and
X_{A}\otimes Y_{B}
is an operator on
H_{AB}
. The computational basis of an
n
-qubit system is
\{|x_{1}\cdots x_{n}\rangle:x_{i}\in\{0,1\}\}
, equivalently
\{|j\rangle:0\leq j\leq 2^{n}-1\}
after identifying a binary string with its integer value.

B.2
Pure states, mixed states, density operators, and reduced states

A pure state may be described by a unit vector
|\psi\rangle
, but the object acted on by channels and partial traces is the corresponding density operator
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\in L(H)
. These are different mathematical objects:
|\psi\rangle
is a vector in
H
, whereas
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
is an operator on
H
. In particular,
|\psi\rangle
and
e^{i\theta}|\psi\rangle
are different vectors but determine the same density operator.

A mixed state on
H
is a density operator
\rho\in L(H)
, meaning a positive semidefinite operator with unit trace,
\rho\geq 0
and
\Tr(\rho)=1
. Equivalently, a mixed state can be written as a convex combination of pure states,

\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|,\qquad p_{i}\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{i}p_{i}=1.

The same density operator may arise from many different ensembles
\{p_{i},|\psi_{i}\rangle\}
.

For a bipartite state
\rho_{AB}
, the reduced state (or marginal) on subsystem
A
is defined by the partial trace over
B
:

\rho_{A}:=\Tr_{B}(\rho_{AB})=\sum_{j}(I_{A}\otimes\langle j|_{B})\,\rho_{AB}\,(I_{A}\otimes|j\rangle_{B}),

where
\{|j\rangle_{B}\}
is any orthonormal basis of
H_{B}
. The result is basis independent. Likewise
\rho_{B}:=\Tr_{A}(\rho_{AB})
. On simple tensors one has

\Tr_{B}(X_{A}\otimes Y_{B})=X_{A}\,\Tr(Y_{B}),

and linearity extends this to all operators. Operationally, taking the partial trace is exactly the same as discarding the corresponding subsystem. Thus
\rho_{A}
is the state accessible to an observer who keeps only subsystem
A
.

B.3
Quantum channels, unitary evolution, CPTP maps, and channel representations

A superoperator is a linear map
\Phi:L(H_{A})\to L(H_{B})
. The physically allowed maps are quantum channels, namely CPTP maps. Complete positivity means that for every auxiliary Hilbert space
H_{R}
and every positive semidefinite operator
X\in L(H_{A}\otimes H_{R})
,

(\Phi\otimes\mathrm{id}_{R})(X)\geq 0.

This is the correct positivity condition because a system may be entangled with external reference systems. Trace preservation means

\Tr[\Phi(X)]=\Tr[X]

for every operator
X
.

Closed-system evolution is unitary. The unitary group on
H_{d}
is

\mathrm{U}(d):=\{U\in L(H_{d}):U^{\dagger}U=UU^{\dagger}=I_{d}\}.

If
U\in\mathrm{U}(d)
, the corresponding unitary channel is conjugation by
U
,

\Ad_{U}(\rho):=U\rho U^{\dagger}.

General open-system evolution is modeled by CPTP maps rather than only by unitary channels.

Every CPTP map
\Phi:L(H_{A})\to L(H_{B})
admits a Kraus representation

\Phi(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}K_{i}\rho K_{i}^{\dagger},\qquad K_{i}:H_{A}\to H_{B},\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{r}K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}=I_{A}.

The minimal number
r
of nonzero Kraus operators in such a representation is the Kraus rank of
\Phi
.

Equivalently, Stinespring’s theorem states that there exist an auxiliary Hilbert space
H_{E}
and an isometry
V:H_{A}\to H_{B}\otimes H_{E}
such that

\Phi(\rho)=\Tr_{E}\!\bigl(V\rho V^{\dagger}\bigr).

After adjoining an ancilla in a fixed state and extending the isometry to a unitary on a larger space, this becomes the familiar unitary-plus-ancilla-plus-partial-trace realization

\Phi(\rho)=\Tr_{E}\!\left[U\bigl(\rho\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|_{E}\bigr)U^{\dagger}\right].

Concretely, if we write
\Phi
in Kraus form as

\Phi(\rho)=\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}K_{i}\rho K_{i}^{\dagger},

choose an orthonormal family
\{|i\rangle_{E}\}_{i=0}^{r-1}
in the auxiliary space
H_{E}
, and define

V=\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}K_{i}\otimes|i\rangle_{E},

then
V:H_{A}\to H_{B}\otimes H_{E}
is an isometry (because
\sum_{i}K_{i}^{\dagger}K_{i}=I_{H_{A}}
), and

\displaystyle V\rho V^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}K_{i}\otimes|i\rangle_{E}\right)\rho\left(\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}K_{j}^{\dagger}\otimes\langle j|_{E}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{r-1}K_{i}\rho K_{j}^{\dagger}\otimes|i\rangle\!\langle j|_{E}.

Taking the partial trace over
H_{E}
gives

\displaystyle\Tr_{E}\!\bigl(V\rho V^{\dagger}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}(I_{H_{B}}\otimes\langle k|_{E})\Bigl(\sum_{i,j=0}^{r-1}K_{i}\rho K_{j}^{\dagger}\otimes|i\rangle\!\langle j|_{E}\Bigr)(I_{H_{B}}\otimes|k\rangle_{E})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,k=0}^{r-1}K_{i}\rho K_{j}^{\dagger}\,\langle k|i\rangle\langle j|k\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}K_{i}\rho K_{i}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\Phi(\rho).

B.4
Blind single-copy compression and Haar-random pure states

In blind compression, the encoder is not told which source state was supplied. It must apply the same encoding channel to every input state. In single-copy compression, only one copy of the source state is available. Thus blind single-copy compression from
n
to
k<n
qubits asks for channels

\mathcal{E}:L(H_{2^{n}})\to L(H_{2^{k}}),\qquad\mathcal{D}:L(H_{2^{k}})\to L(H_{2^{n}}),

that reconstruct an unknown pure
n
-qubit state as well as possible after passing through a
k
-qubit bottleneck.

Haar measure on
\mathrm{U}(d)
is the unique probability measure invariant under left and right multiplication by fixed unitaries. The induced measure on pure states is obtained by applying a Haar-random unitary to a fixed reference vector. For example, if
U\sim\mathrm{Haar}
on
\mathrm{U}(d)
, then
U|0\rangle
is Haar-random on the unit sphere of
H_{d}
. More generally, if
S\subseteq H
is a subspace, a Haar-random pure state in
S
is one drawn from the unique unitarily invariant probability measure on the unit sphere of
S
.

(Choi representation)
.

\Phi:L(H_{d_{1}})\to L(H_{d_{2}})

\Lambda(\Phi):=\sum_{i,j=0}^{d_{1}-1}|i\rangle\!\langle j|\otimes\Phi(|i\rangle\!\langle j|)\in L(H_{d_{1}}\otimes H_{d_{2}}).

(C.1)

The
Choi rank
(or
Kraus rank
) of a linear map
\Phi

Theorem C.2
.

Let
\Phi:L(H_{A})\to L(H_{B})

\rho\in L(H_{A})
,

\Phi(\rho)=\Tr_{A}\bigl(\Lambda(\Phi)(\rho^{T}\otimes I_{B})\bigr),

(C.2)

where the transpose is taken in the computational basis.

Definition C.3

(Link product)
.

For
\Lambda_{1}\in L(H_{B}\otimes H_{C})
and
\Lambda_{2}\in L(H_{A}\otimes H_{B})
, define

\Lambda_{1}\star\Lambda_{2}:=\Tr_{B}\Bigl((I_{A}\otimes\Lambda_{1})(\Lambda_{2}^{T_{B}}\otimes I_{C})\Bigr),

(C.3)

where
T_{B}
denotes partial transpose over
H_{B}
.

Theorem C.4

(
[

]
, Theorem 1)
.

If
\Phi_{1}:L(H_{A})\to L(H_{B})
and
\Phi_{2}:L(H_{B})\to L(H_{C})
are linear maps, then

\Lambda(\Phi_{2}\circ\Phi_{1})=\Lambda(\Phi_{2})\star\Lambda(\Phi_{1}).

(C.4)

Proposition C.5
.

For any source distribution
\mu
, the functional
F_{\mu}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D})
from Eq. (

) extends to a separately linear continuous functional on the vector spaces of linear maps
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

For a pure state

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E})(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)

\displaystyle=\Tr_{A}\Bigl((\Lambda(\mathcal{D})\star\Lambda(\mathcal{E}))(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|^{T}\otimes I)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\Tr_{A}\Bigl(\Tr_{C}\bigl[(I_{A}\otimes\Lambda(\mathcal{D}))(\Lambda(\mathcal{E})^{T_{C}}\otimes I)\bigr](|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|^{T}\otimes I)\Bigr).

(C.5)

The right-hand side is bilinear in
\Lambda(\mathcal{E})
and
\Lambda(\mathcal{D})
, hence separately linear in
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{D}
. Averaging over

∎

C.2
Proof of Lemma

We recall and prove Lemma

.

Proof.

Let

M_{\mathrm{mix}}:=\max_{U,V,\sigma_{B},\tau_{E}}F_{\mu}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{U,\sigma_{B}},\mathcal{D}_{V,\tau_{E}}\right),\qquad M_{0}:=\max_{U,V}F_{\mu}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{U,|0^{n_{B}}\rangle\langle 0^{n_{B}}|},\mathcal{D}_{V,|0^{n_{E}}\rangle\langle 0^{n_{E}}|}\right).

Both maxima exist because the relevant unitary groups and state spaces are compact,
and the objective is continuous in all parameters.

Since
|0^{n_{B}}\rangle\langle 0^{n_{B}}|
and
|0^{n_{E}}\rangle\langle 0^{n_{E}}|
are
particular density operators, we immediately have

M_{0}\leq M_{\mathrm{mix}}.

It remains to prove the reverse inequality.

Fix arbitrary unitaries

and arbitrary density operators
\sigma_{B},\tau_{E}
.
Choose spectral decompositions

\sigma_{B}=\sum_{r}p_{r}|\beta_{r}\rangle\langle\beta_{r}|,\qquad\tau_{E}=\sum_{s}q_{s}|\eta_{s}\rangle\langle\eta_{s}|,

with
p_{r},q_{s}\geq 0
and
\sum_{r}p_{r}=\sum_{s}q_{s}=1

\mathcal{E}_{r}:=\mathcal{E}_{U,|\beta_{r}\rangle\langle\beta_{r}|},\qquad\mathcal{D}_{s}:=\mathcal{D}_{V,|\eta_{s}\rangle\langle\eta_{s}|}.

By linearity,

\mathcal{E}_{U,\sigma_{B}}=\sum_{r}p_{r}\mathcal{E}_{r},\qquad\mathcal{D}_{V,\tau_{E}}=\sum_{s}q_{s}\mathcal{D}_{s}.

Applying separate linearity twice gives

F_{\mu}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{U,\sigma_{B}},\mathcal{D}_{V,\tau_{E}}\right)=\sum_{r,s}p_{r}q_{s}\,F_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{r},\mathcal{D}_{s}).

Hence there exist indices

such that

F_{\mu}\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{U,\sigma_{B}},\mathcal{D}_{V,\tau_{E}}\right)\leq F_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{r_{\ast}},\mathcal{D}_{s_{\ast}}).

Now choose unitaries
W_{B}
on
H_{B}
and
W_{E}
on

satisfying

W_{B}|0^{n_{B}}\rangle=|\beta_{r_{\ast}}\rangle,\qquad W_{E}|0^{n_{E}}\rangle=|\eta_{s_{\ast}}\rangle,

and define

\widetilde{U}:=U(I_{A}\otimes W_{B}),\qquad\widetilde{V}:=V(I_{C}\otimes W_{E}).

Then, for every input state
\rho_{A}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\widetilde{U},|0^{n_{B}}\rangle\langle 0^{n_{B}}|}(\rho_{A})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{D}\!\left[\widetilde{U}(\rho_{A}\otimes|0^{n_{B}}\rangle\langle 0^{n_{B}}|)\widetilde{U}^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{D}\!\left[U(\rho_{A}\otimes W_{B}|0^{n_{B}}\rangle\langle 0^{n_{B}}|W_{B}^{\dagger})U^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{D}\!\left[U(\rho_{A}\otimes|\beta_{r_{\ast}}\rangle\langle\beta_{r_{\ast}}|)U^{\dagger}\right]

Likewise, for every input state

Therefore

Since the mixed-ancilla implementation

was arbitrary, it follows that

M_{\mathrm{mix}}\leq M_{0}.

Together with

.

C.3
Extreme points and low-rank implementations

Lemma C.6

There exists an optimal pair
(\widehat{\mathcal{E}},\widehat{\mathcal{D}})

Proof.

Appendix D
Haar analysis and mixtures with perfectly compressible priors

We write
d:=2^{n}
and
m:=2^{k}
for the source and bottleneck dimensions.

Lemma D.1
.

Let
|\psi\rangle
be Haar-random on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

(i)

For all operators
X,Y\in L(\mathbb{C}^{d})
,

\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim\mathrm{Haar}}\bigl[\langle\psi|X|\psi\rangle\,\langle\psi|Y|\psi\rangle\bigr]=\frac{\Tr(XY)+\Tr(X)\Tr(Y)}{d(d+1)}.

(D.1)

(ii)

If
|\psi\rangle
is Haar-random on
H_{A}\otimes H_{B}
and
\rho_{A}:=\Tr_{B}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)
, then

\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim\mathrm{Haar}}\Tr(\rho_{A}^{2})=\frac{\dim H_{A}+\dim H_{B}}{1+\dim H_{A}\dim H_{B}}.

(D.2)

Proposition D.2

(Optimal fidelity for the Haar prior)
.

For the Haar prior on pure states in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, one has

\sup_{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}}F_{\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D})=\frac{d+m^{2}}{d(d+1)}.

(D.3)

An optimal encoder–decoder pair is obtained by identifying
\mathbb{C}^{d}\cong\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d/m}
and setting

\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\Tr_{B}(\rho),\qquad\mathcal{D}(\sigma)=\sigma\otimes\frac{I_{B}}{d/m}.

(D.4)

By Haar symmetry one may replace
I_{B}/(d/m)
in Eq. (
D.4
) by any pure state on
H_{B}
. In particular, a rank-one decoder is optimal.

Proof.

Let
\Phi:=\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}
. Choose Kraus operators
\{E_{j}\}_{j}
for
\mathcal{E}
and
\{D_{i}\}_{i}
for
\mathcal{D}
, and write
K_{ij}:=D_{i}E_{j}
. For a pure input
|\psi\rangle
,

\langle\psi|\Phi(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle=\sum_{i,j}\left\lvert\langle\psi|K_{ij}|\psi\rangle\right\rvert^{2}.

Applying Eq. (
D.1
) with
X=K_{ij}
and
Y=K_{ij}^{\dagger}
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim\mathrm{Haar}}\bigl[\langle\psi|\Phi(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle\bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d(d+1)}\sum_{i,j}\Bigl(\Tr(K_{ij}K_{ij}^{\dagger})+\left\lvert\Tr K_{ij}\right\rvert^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d+1}+\frac{1}{d(d+1)}\sum_{i,j}\left\lvert\Tr(D_{i}E_{j})\right\rvert^{2}.

(D.5)

Now
E_{j}D_{i}
is an
m\times m
matrix, so

\left\lvert\Tr(D_{i}E_{j})\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert\Tr(E_{j}D_{i})\right\rvert^{2}\leq m\,\Tr(D_{i}^{\dagger}E_{j}^{\dagger}E_{j}D_{i}).

Summing over
i,j
yields

\sum_{i,j}\left\lvert\Tr(D_{i}E_{j})\right\rvert^{2}\leq m\sum_{i}\Tr\!\left(D_{i}^{\dagger}\Bigl(\sum_{j}E_{j}^{\dagger}E_{j}\Bigr)D_{i}\right)=m\sum_{i}\Tr(D_{i}^{\dagger}D_{i})=m^{2}.

Substituting into Eq. (
D.5
) gives the upper bound in Eq. (
D.3
).

For achievability, identify
\mathbb{C}^{d}\cong\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d/m}=H_{A}\otimes H_{B}
, take
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\Tr_{B}(\rho)
, and
\mathcal{D}(\sigma)=\sigma\otimes I_{B}/(d/m)
. If
\rho_{A}:=\Tr_{B}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)
, then

\langle\psi|\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|))|\psi\rangle=\Tr\!\left(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\Bigl(\rho_{A}\otimes\frac{I_{B}}{d/m}\Bigr)\right)=\frac{1}{d/m}\Tr(\rho_{A}^{2}).

Averaging and using Eq. (
D.2
) with
\dim H_{A}=m
and
\dim H_{B}=d/m
gives

\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim\mathrm{Haar}}\bigl[\langle\psi|\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|))|\psi\rangle\bigr]=\frac{1}{d/m}\cdot\frac{m+d/m}{d+1}=\frac{d+m^{2}}{d(d+1)}.

This proves Eq. (
D.3
). Finally, because the Haar measure is invariant under unitary rotations on
H_{B}
, replacing
I_{B}/(d/m)
by any pure state on
H_{B}
leaves the average fidelity unchanged.
∎

Proposition D.3

(Mixtures with a perfectly compressible prior)
.

Let
\mu^{\prime}=\mu_{\mathrm{pc}}
be a prior on pure states in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that there exist a unitary
U\in\mathrm{U}(d)
, a fixed reference state
|\varphi_{\mathrm{ref}}\rangle
, and an
m
-dimensional subsystem
C
with

U|\psi\rangle_{A}=|\psi^{\prime}\rangle_{C}\otimes|\varphi_{\mathrm{ref}}\rangle_{D}\qquad\text{for all }|\psi\rangle\in\supp(\mu^{\prime}).

Let
\mu=c\,\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}+(1-c)\mu^{\prime}
with
0<c<1
. Then the maps

\mathcal{E}(\rho):=\Tr_{D}(U\rho U^{\dagger}),\qquad\mathcal{D}(\sigma):=U^{\dagger}\bigl(\sigma\otimes|\varphi_{\mathrm{ref}}\rangle\!\langle\varphi_{\mathrm{ref}}|\bigr)U

simultaneously maximize the fidelity for the Haar part and reconstruct every state in
\supp(\mu^{\prime})
perfectly. Consequently,

F_{\mu}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D})=1-c\,\frac{(d+m)(d-m)}{d(d+1)}.

(D.6)

Proof.

The chosen pair reconstructs
\mu^{\prime}
perfectly by construction, so its contribution to the average fidelity is
1-c
. On the Haar component the same pair is of the form considered in Proposition
D.2
, hence contributes
c(d+m^{2})/(d(d+1))
. Therefore

F_{\mu}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D})=(1-c)+c\,\frac{d+m^{2}}{d(d+1)}=1-c\,\frac{d^{2}-m^{2}}{d(d+1)},

which is exactly Eq. (
D.6
).
∎

Appendix E
The source

Throughout this appendix we set

d:=2^{n},\qquad m:=2^{k},\qquad q:=d-1,\qquad H_{\perp}:=|0\rangle^{\perp}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d},\qquad\dim H_{\perp}=q.

For
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and a unit vector
|\varphi\rangle\in H_{\perp}
, write

|\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\rangle:=\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}|0\rangle+\sqrt{\varepsilon}|\varphi\rangle,

and define

F_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}):=\mathbb{E}_{\varphi}\bigl[\langle\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)|(\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E})(|\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\rangle\!\langle\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)|)|\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\rangle\bigr],

E.1
Two preliminary lemmas

Lemma E.1
.

Let
K\neq\varnothing
be compact, and let
A,B,C:K\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded and continuous. Define

f(\varepsilon):=\max_{x\in K}\bigl(A(x)+\varepsilon B(x)+\varepsilon^{2}C(x)\bigr),\qquad\varepsilon\geq 0.

If

A_{\max}:=\max_{x\in K}A(x),\qquad S:=\{x\in K:A(x)=A_{\max}\},

then

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{f(\varepsilon)-A_{\max}}{\varepsilon}\leq\sup_{x\in S}B(x).

(E.2)

Proof.

For
\varepsilon>0
, choose
x_{\varepsilon}\in K
such that

f(\varepsilon)=A(x_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon B(x_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon^{2}C(x_{\varepsilon}).

Since
A(x_{\varepsilon})\leq A_{\max}
,

\frac{f(\varepsilon)-A_{\max}}{\varepsilon}\leq B(x_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon C(x_{\varepsilon}).

(E.3)

Choose a sequence
\varepsilon_{r}\to 0^{+}
along which the left-hand side tends to the limsup. By compactness of
K
, some subsequence of
x_{\varepsilon_{r}}
converges to a point
x_{*}\in K
. Passing to that subsequence and using continuity of
A,B,C
gives

A(x_{*})=A_{\max},\qquad x_{*}\in S.

Taking the limit in Eq. (
E.3
) along the same subsequence yields

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{f(\varepsilon)-A_{\max}}{\varepsilon}\leq B(x_{*})\leq\sup_{x\in S}B(x),

which proves Eq. (
E.2
).
∎

Lemma E.2
.

For every fixed CPTP map
\Phi:L(\mathbb{C}^{d})\to L(\mathbb{C}^{d})
, the function

\varepsilon\longmapsto F_{\varepsilon}(\Phi):=\mathbb{E}_{\varphi}\bigl[\langle\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)|\Phi(|\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\rangle\!\langle\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)|)|\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\rangle\bigr]

is a polynomial of degree at most
2
in
\varepsilon
. More precisely, there exist real numbers
A(\Phi),B(\Phi),C(\Phi)
such that

F_{\varepsilon}(\Phi)=A(\Phi)+\varepsilon B(\Phi)+\varepsilon^{2}C(\Phi)\qquad\text{for all }\varepsilon\in[0,1],

and

A(\Phi)=\langle 0|\Phi(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)|0\rangle.

Proof.

Write

A_{0}:=|0\rangle\!\langle 0|,\qquad A_{1}:=|\varphi\rangle\!\langle\varphi|,\qquad C_{+}:=|0\rangle\!\langle\varphi|,\qquad C_{-}:=|\varphi\rangle\!\langle 0|.

Then

|\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\rangle\!\langle\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)|=(1-\varepsilon)A_{0}+\varepsilon A_{1}+\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)}\,(C_{+}+C_{-}).

Because
(X,Y)\mapsto\Tr(\Phi(X)Y)
is bilinear, the quantity

\Tr\bigl(\Phi(|\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\rangle\!\langle\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)|)|\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\rangle\!\langle\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)|\bigr)

is a finite linear combination of monomials in
(1-\varepsilon)
,
\varepsilon
, and
\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)}
. Any term containing unequal numbers of
|\varphi\rangle
and
\langle\varphi|
picks up a nontrivial phase under
|\varphi\rangle\mapsto e^{i\theta}|\varphi\rangle
and hence has zero Haar average. The surviving terms have equal numbers of
|\varphi\rangle
and
\langle\varphi|
, so they contribute only powers
\varepsilon^{0},\varepsilon^{1},\varepsilon^{2}
. At
\varepsilon=0
the state is
|0\rangle
, giving the formula for
A(\Phi)
.
∎

E.2
Asymptotic separation for the conventional architecture when
(n,k)=(2,1)

We first analyze the conventional architecture in the minimal nontrivial case. Here

|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}|00\rangle+\sqrt{\varepsilon}|\varphi\rangle,\qquad|\varphi\rangle\in\Span\{|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle\},\qquad\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert=1,

and
|\varphi\rangle
is Haar-random in the three-dimensional orthogonal complement of
|00\rangle
.

Let
U,V\in\mathrm{U}(4)
be the encoder and decoder unitaries of a conventional
(2,1,0,1)
-QAE. Define the decoder isometry

W:=V(I\otimes|0\rangle):H_{2}\to H_{4},\qquad W^{\dagger}W=I_{2},

and for
b\in\{0,1\}
set

\widetilde{K}_{b}:=(I\otimes\langle b|)U:H_{4}\to H_{2},\qquad K_{b}:=W\widetilde{K}_{b}:H_{4}\to H_{4}.

Then

\Phi_{U,V}(\rho)=\sum_{b=0}^{1}K_{b}\rho K_{b}^{\dagger}.

(E.4)

Because
U
is unitary,

\widetilde{K}_{b}\widetilde{K}_{b^{\prime}}^{\dagger}=(I\otimes\langle b|)UU^{\dagger}(I\otimes|b^{\prime}\rangle)=\delta_{bb^{\prime}}I_{2},

so

K_{b}K_{b^{\prime}}^{\dagger}=\delta_{bb^{\prime}}P,\qquad P:=WW^{\dagger}.

(E.5)

Thus
P
is the rank-
2
projector onto
\ran(W)
. If we set

Q_{b}:=K_{b}^{\dagger}K_{b},

then each
Q_{b}
is a rank-
2
projection,
Q_{0}Q_{1}=0
, and trace preservation implies

Q_{0}+Q_{1}=I_{4}.

(E.6)

For a pure input
|\psi\rangle
,

\langle\psi|\Phi_{U,V}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle=\sum_{b=0}^{1}\left\lvert\langle\psi|K_{b}|\psi\rangle\right\rvert^{2}.

(E.7)

For each fixed
(U,V)
, define

f_{U,V}(\varepsilon):=\mathbb{E}_{\varphi}\bigl[\langle\psi|\Phi_{U,V}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle\bigr].

The Kraus pair
(K_{0},K_{1})
may be mixed by an arbitrary unitary
S\in\mathrm{U}(2)
without changing the channel, and under this mixing the vector

v(\psi):=(\langle\psi|K_{0}|\psi\rangle,\langle\psi|K_{1}|\psi\rangle)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}

transforms as
v(\psi)\mapsto Sv(\psi)
, leaving Eq. (
E.7
) unchanged. We may therefore choose the Kraus gauge so that

\langle 00|K_{0}|00\rangle=\sqrt{f_{0}}\geq 0,\qquad\langle 00|K_{1}|00\rangle=0,\qquad f_{0}:=\sum_{b=0}^{1}\left\lvert\langle 00|K_{b}|00\rangle\right\rvert^{2}\in[0,1].

(E.8)

If
f_{0}=0
, then the constant term of
f_{U,V}(\varepsilon)
is
0
, so the pair is clearly suboptimal. Henceforth assume
f_{0}>0
.

For
b\in\{0,1\}
define

f_{b}^{(1)}(\varphi):=\langle\psi|K_{b}|\psi\rangle.

Expanding,

f_{b}^{(1)}(\varphi)=(1-\varepsilon)\langle 00|K_{b}|00\rangle+\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)}\bigl(\langle 00|K_{b}|\varphi\rangle+\langle\varphi|K_{b}|00\rangle\bigr)+\varepsilon\langle\varphi|K_{b}|\varphi\rangle.

Since

f_{b}^{(1)}(\varphi)=f_{b}^{(2)}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,f_{b}^{(3)}(\varphi)+\varepsilon f_{b}^{(4)}(\varphi)+o(\varepsilon),

where

f_{b}^{(2)}:=\langle 00|K_{b}|00\rangle,\qquad f_{b}^{(3)}(\varphi):=\langle 00|K_{b}|\varphi\rangle+\langle\varphi|K_{b}|00\rangle,\qquad f_{b}^{(4)}(\varphi):=\langle\varphi|K_{b}|\varphi\rangle-f_{b}^{(2)}.

\displaystyle\left\lvert f_{b}^{(1)}(\varphi)\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lvert f_{b}^{(2)}\right\rvert^{2}+2\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\Re\!\bigl((f_{b}^{(2)})^{*}f_{b}^{(3)}(\varphi)\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad+\varepsilon\Bigl(\left\lvert f_{b}^{(3)}(\varphi)\right\rvert^{2}+2\Re\!\bigl((f_{b}^{(2)})^{*}f_{b}^{(4)}(\varphi)\bigr)\Bigr)+o(\varepsilon).

Taking the Haar expectation removes the

\mathbb{E}[|\varphi\rangle]=0

\mathbb{E}\left\lvert f_{b}^{(1)}(\varphi)\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert f_{b}^{(2)}\right\rvert^{2}+\varepsilon\left(\mathbb{E}\left\lvert f_{b}^{(3)}(\varphi)\right\rvert^{2}+2\Re\!\bigl((f_{b}^{(2)})^{*}\mathbb{E}[f_{b}^{(4)}(\varphi)]\bigr)\right)+o(\varepsilon).

Summing over

f_{U,V}(\varepsilon)=f_{0}+\varepsilon\sum_{b=0}^{1}\left(\mathbb{E}\left\lvert f_{b}^{(3)}(\varphi)\right\rvert^{2}+2\Re\!\bigl((f_{b}^{(2)})^{*}\mathbb{E}[f_{b}^{(4)}(\varphi)]\bigr)\right)+o(\varepsilon).

Let

the projector onto
\Span\{|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle\}

in this three-dimensional subspace,

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\langle u\,|\,\varphi\rangle\langle v\,|\,\varphi\rangle\bigr]=0\qquad\text{for all }u,v,

by invariance under the global phase
|\varphi\rangle\mapsto e^{i\theta}|\varphi\rangle
. Therefore

\mathbb{E}\left\lvert f_{b}^{(3)}(\varphi)\right\rvert^{2}=\frac{\left\lVert\Pi K_{b}^{\dagger}|00\rangle\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\Pi K_{b}|00\rangle\right\rVert^{2}}{3}.

(E.11)

Set

p_{0}:=\langle 00|P|00\rangle,\qquad q_{b}:=\langle 00|Q_{b}|00\rangle,\qquad q_{0}+q_{1}=1.

Then by Eqs. (
E.5
) and (

\left\lVert K_{b}^{\dagger}|00\rangle\right\rVert^{2}=\langle 00|P|00\rangle=p_{0},\qquad\left\lVert K_{b}|00\rangle\right\rVert^{2}=\langle 00|Q_{b}|00\rangle=q_{b}.

\displaystyle\sum_{b=0}^{1}2\Re\!\bigl((f_{b}^{(2)})^{*}\mathbb{E}[f_{b}^{(4)}(\varphi)]\bigr)

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1-\max_{U,V}f_{U,V}(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}\geq 1.

The identity choice

E.16
).

For unrestricted CPTP encoder–decoder pairs, consider the projector

\Pi^{\prime}:=|00\rangle\!\langle 00|+|01\rangle\!\langle 01|

and the channel

\Phi(\rho)=\Pi^{\prime}\rho\Pi^{\prime}+\Tr\bigl((I-\Pi^{\prime})\rho\bigr)|00\rangle\!\langle 00|.

It admits an encoder with Kraus operators

E_{0}=|0\rangle\!\langle 00|+|1\rangle\!\langle 01|,\qquad E_{1}=|0\rangle\!\langle 10|,\qquad E_{2}=|0\rangle\!\langle 11|,

and a decoder with a single Kraus operator

D_{0}=|00\rangle\!\langle 0|+|01\rangle\!\langle 1|.

Write

|\varphi\rangle=\varphi_{1}|01\rangle+\sqrt{1-\left\lvert\varphi_{1}\right\rvert^{2}}\,|\chi\rangle,\qquad|\chi\rangle\in\Span\{|10\rangle,|11\rangle\},\qquad\left\lVert\chi\right\rVert=1.

Then

\Pi^{\prime}|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}|00\rangle+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\varphi_{1}|01\rangle,\qquad(I-\Pi^{\prime})|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-\left\lvert\varphi_{1}\right\rvert^{2})}\;|\chi\rangle,

and therefore

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\Phi(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\Pi^{\prime}|\psi\rangle\right\rVert^{4}+(1-\varepsilon)\left\lVert(I-\Pi^{\prime})|\psi\rangle\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\varepsilon\bigl(1-\left\lvert\varphi_{1}\right\rvert^{2}\bigr)+o(\varepsilon).

Since
\mathbb{E}\left\lvert\varphi_{1}\right\rvert^{2}=1/3
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\langle\psi|\Phi(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle\bigr]=1-\frac{2}{3}\varepsilon+o(\varepsilon),

(E.17)

which is strictly better than the conventional-architecture coefficient in Eq. (
E.16
).

E.3
A universal first-order lower bound for all factorized channels

In the remainder of this appendix, let
\Phi=\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}
be an arbitrary factorized channel with

\mathcal{E}:L(\mathbb{C}^{d})\to L(\mathbb{C}^{m}),\qquad\mathcal{D}:L(\mathbb{C}^{m})\to L(\mathbb{C}^{d}),\qquad\Phi(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)=|0\rangle\!\langle 0|.

Choose Kraus operators
\{E_{\ell}\}_{\ell}
for
\mathcal{E}
and
\{D_{j}\}_{j}
for
\mathcal{D}
, and relabel the Kraus family
\{D_{j}E_{\ell}\}_{j,\ell}
of
\Phi
as
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
. Each
A_{i}
has rank at most
m
.

Lemma E.3

(Block form)
.

For every
i
there exists
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

A_{i}|0\rangle=\alpha_{i}|0\rangle.

With respect to the decomposition
\mathbb{C}^{d}=\Span\{|0\rangle\}\oplus H_{\perp}
, we may write

A_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{i}&v_{i}^{\dagger}\\
0&B_{i}\end{pmatrix},\qquad v_{i}\in H_{\perp},\qquad B_{i}:H_{\perp}\to H_{\perp},

and the trace preserving condition becomes

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert^{2}=1,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}v_{i}=0,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{r}\bigl(v_{i}v_{i}^{\dagger}+B_{i}^{\dagger}B_{i}\bigr)=I_{H_{\perp}}.

(E.18)

In particular,

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left\lVert v_{i}\right\rVert^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left\lVert B_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=d-1.

(E.19)

Proof.

Because

|0\rangle\!\langle 0|=\Phi(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}A_{i}|0\rangle\!\langle 0|A_{i}^{\dagger},

each positive semidefinite summand on the right has support contained in
\Span\{|0\rangle\}
. Hence
A_{i}|0\rangle\in\Span\{|0\rangle\}
, proving the first claim. Writing the block decomposition and comparing the matrix blocks in
\sum_{i}A_{i}^{\dagger}A_{i}=I_{d}
gives Eq. (
E.18
). Taking traces yields Eq. (
E.19
).
∎

Lemma E.4
.

Define

S:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left\lVert v_{i}\right\rVert^{2},\qquad T:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left\lVert B_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

Then

F_{\varepsilon}(\Phi)=1-2\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon}{d-1}\left(S+2\Re\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}^{*}\Tr(B_{i})\right)+o(\varepsilon).

(E.20)

Equivalently,

c(\Phi):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1-F_{\varepsilon}(\Phi)}{\varepsilon}=2-\frac{1}{d-1}\left(S+2\Re\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}^{*}\Tr(B_{i})\right).

(E.21)

Proof.

Fix a unit vector
|\varphi\rangle\in H_{\perp}
and set

a:=\sqrt{1-\varepsilon},\qquad b:=\sqrt{\varepsilon},\qquad x_{i}(\varphi):=\langle v_{i}\,|\,\varphi\rangle,\qquad y_{i}(\varphi):=\langle\varphi|B_{i}|\varphi\rangle.

Then

\langle\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)|A_{i}|\psi_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\rangle=a^{2}\alpha_{i}+ab\,x_{i}(\varphi)+b^{2}y_{i}(\varphi).

Since

\langle\psi|\Phi(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left\lvert\langle\psi|A_{i}|\psi\rangle\right\rvert^{2},

we obtain

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{E}_{\varphi}\Bigl[\left\lvert a^{2}\alpha_{i}+ab\,x_{i}(\varphi)+b^{2}y_{i}(\varphi)\right\rvert^{2}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left(a^{4}\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert^{2}+a^{2}b^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left\lvert x_{i}(\varphi)\right\rvert^{2}+2a^{2}b^{2}\Re\bigl(\alpha_{i}^{*}\mathbb{E}[y_{i}(\varphi)]\bigr)\right)+o(\varepsilon).

The terms of order
a^{3}b
and
ab^{3}
vanish after Haar averaging:
\mathbb{E}[x_{i}(\varphi)]=0
by symmetry, and
\mathbb{E}[x_{i}(\varphi)^{*}y_{i}(\varphi)]=0
because
x_{i}(\varphi)^{*}y_{i}(\varphi)
acquires a nontrivial phase under
|\varphi\rangle\mapsto e^{i\theta}|\varphi\rangle
. By the first moments of Haar measure on the unit sphere of
H_{\perp}
,

\mathbb{E}[x_{i}(\varphi)]=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}\left\lvert x_{i}(\varphi)\right\rvert^{2}=\frac{\left\lVert v_{i}\right\rVert^{2}}{d-1},\qquad\mathbb{E}[y_{i}(\varphi)]=\frac{\Tr(B_{i})}{d-1}.

Using
a^{4}=(1-\varepsilon)^{2}=1-2\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})
,
a^{2}b^{2}=\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)=\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})
, and
\sum_{i}\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert^{2}=1
, we obtain Eq. (
E.20
). Equation (
E.21
) is immediate.
∎

Lemma E.5

(Universal first-order lower bound)
.

For every factorized channel
\Phi=\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}
as above,

c(\Phi)\geq\frac{d-m}{d-1}.

(E.22)

Proof.

Let

I_{+}:=\{i:\alpha_{i}\neq 0\}.

If
i\in I_{+}
, then
|0\rangle\in\im(A_{i})
and
\rank(A_{i})\leq m
, so
\rank(B_{i})\leq m-1
. Hence

\left\lvert\Tr(B_{i})\right\rvert^{2}\leq\rank(B_{i})\,\left\lVert B_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq(m-1)\left\lVert B_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\qquad(i\in I_{+}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}^{*}\Tr(B_{i})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in I_{+}}\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert\,\left\lvert\Tr(B_{i})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i\in I_{+}}\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{i\in I_{+}}\left\lvert\Tr(B_{i})\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{(m-1)\sum_{i\in I_{+}}\left\lVert B_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}\leq\sqrt{(m-1)T}.

Using
S+T=d-1
from Eq. (
E.19
),

S+2\Re\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}^{*}\Tr(B_{i})\leq d-1-T+2\sqrt{(m-1)T}.

The right-hand side is maximized at
T=m-1
, where it equals
d+m-2
. Substituting into Eq. (
E.21
) gives

c(\Phi)\geq 2-\frac{d+m-2}{d-1}=\frac{d-m}{d-1},

which proves Eq. (
E.22
).
∎

Define the optimal first-order coefficient

c_{*}:=\frac{d-m}{d-1}.

(E.23)

The explicit reset construction below attains this bound.

E.4
Classification of first-order-optimal channels

Proposition E.6
.

Let
\Phi=\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}
factor through
\mathbb{C}^{m}
and satisfy
\Phi(|0\rangle\!\langle 0|)=|0\rangle\!\langle 0|
. If
c(\Phi)=c_{*}
, then

\Phi(\rho)=\Phi_{P}(\rho):=P\rho P+\Tr\bigl((I_{d}-P)\rho\bigr)|0\rangle\!\langle 0|

(E.24)

for some rank-
m
orthogonal projection
P
with
|0\rangle\in\im(P)
.

Lemma E.7
.

Let
B\neq 0
be a linear operator on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space. If

\left\lvert\Tr(B)\right\rvert^{2}=\rank(B)\,\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},

then there exist a scalar
\beta\neq 0
and an orthogonal projection
Q
of rank
\rank(B)
such that

B=\beta Q.

Proof.

Let
r:=\rank(B)
, and let
Q
be the orthogonal projection onto
(\ker B)^{\perp}
. Then

\rank(Q)=\dim\bigl((\ker B)^{\perp}\bigr)=\rank(B)=r.

Moreover,
B=BQ
.
Now equip the operator space with the Frobenius inner product

\langle X,Y\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}:=\Tr(X^{\dagger}Y).

Since
Q=Q^{\dagger}
, and since the trace is cyclic in finite dimensions, we get

\Tr(B)=\Tr(BQ)=\Tr(QB)=\langle Q,B\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}.

Hence, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\left\lvert\Tr(B)\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert\langle Q,B\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}\right\rvert^{2}\leq\left\lVert Q\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

Because
Q
is an orthogonal projection,

\left\lVert Q\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\Tr(Q^{\dagger}Q)=\Tr(Q^{2})=\Tr(Q)=\rank(Q)=r.

Therefore

\left\lvert\Tr(B)\right\rvert^{2}\leq r\,\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

By hypothesis, equality holds. So equality holds in the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and therefore
B
is a scalar multiple of
Q
:

B=\beta Q

for some
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
. Since
B\neq 0
, we must have
\beta\neq 0
.
∎

Lemma E.8

(The equality family)
.

Assume
c(\Phi)=c_{*}
. After multiplying each Kraus operator
A_{i}
by a phase of modulus
1
, we may assume:

(i)

each
\alpha_{i}
is real and nonnegative.

(ii)

if
\alpha_{i}=0
, then
B_{i}=0
.

(iii)

if
\alpha_{i}>0
, then there exists a rank-
(m-1)
orthogonal projection
P_{i}
on
H_{\perp}
such that

B_{i}=\alpha_{i}P_{i}.

Moreover,

\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}=1,\qquad\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}v_{i}=0,\qquad\sum_{i}\bigl(v_{i}v_{i}^{\dagger}+\alpha_{i}^{2}P_{i}\bigr)=I_{H_{\perp}}.

(E.25)

Proof.

Because
c(\Phi)=c_{*}
, every inequality in the proof of Lemma
E.5
is saturated. In particular,

d-1-T+2\sqrt{(m-1)T}

must attain its maximum at the actual value of
T
, so
T=m-1
.

Equality in

\sum_{i\in I_{+}}\left\lvert\Tr(B_{i})\right\rvert^{2}\leq(m-1)\sum_{i\in I_{+}}\left\lVert B_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq(m-1)T

forces
B_{i}=0
whenever
\alpha_{i}=0
, and for every
i\in I_{+}
,

\left\lvert\Tr(B_{i})\right\rvert^{2}=(m-1)\left\lVert B_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2},\qquad\rank(B_{i})=m-1.

By Lemma
E.7
, for each such
i
there exist
\beta_{i}\neq 0
and a rank-
(m-1)
projection
P_{i}
such that
B_{i}=\beta_{i}P_{i}
.

Equality in the Cauchy–Schwarz step

\sum_{i}\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert\,\left\lvert\Tr(B_{i})\right\rvert\leq\left(\sum_{i}\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{i}\left\lvert\Tr(B_{i})\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}

shows that there exists
c\geq 0
with
\left\lvert\Tr(B_{i})\right\rvert=c\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert
for every
i
. Using
\sum_{i}\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert^{2}=1
and
\sum_{i}\left\lvert\Tr(B_{i})\right\rvert^{2}=(m-1)^{2}
, we find
c=m-1
. Since
\left\lvert\Tr(B_{i})\right\rvert=(m-1)\left\lvert\beta_{i}\right\rvert
, this implies
\left\lvert\beta_{i}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert
for every
i\in I_{+}
.

Finally, equality throughout

\Re\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{*}\Tr(B_{i})\leq\left\lvert\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{*}\Tr(B_{i})\right\rvert\leq\sum_{i}\left\lvert\alpha_{i}\right\rvert\,\left\lvert\Tr(B_{i})\right\rvert

implies that
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{*}\Tr(B_{i})
is real and nonnegative, and that all complex numbers
\alpha_{i}^{*}\Tr(B_{i})
have a common phase. Multiplying each
A_{i}
by an individual unit-modulus scalar does not change the channel, so we may choose those phases to make every
\alpha_{i}
real and nonnegative. Then
\Tr(B_{i})
has the same phase, hence is also real and nonnegative, which forces
\beta_{i}=\alpha_{i}
. This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma E.9
.

Under the hypotheses of Lemma
E.8
, all nonzero projections
P_{i}
are equal.

Proof.

Set
P_{i}:=0
when
\alpha_{i}=0
, and define

U_{i}:=\im(P_{i})\subset H_{\perp},\qquad N:=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}P_{i},\qquad W:=\im(N)=\Span\Bigl(\bigcup_{i}U_{i}\Bigr).

If the nonzero projections are not all equal, then at least two distinct
(m-1)
-dimensional subspaces occur among the
U_{i}
, and therefore

\dim W\geq m.

(E.26)

We will show that this contradicts the bottleneck dimension.

Step 1: the image of a factorized channel has dimension at most
m^{2}
.

Since
\Phi=\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}
, the image of
\Phi
as a linear map on the operator space is contained in the linear subspace
\mathcal{D}(L(\mathbb{C}^{m}))
. Hence

\dim\im(\Phi)\leq\dim L(\mathbb{C}^{m})=m^{2}.

(E.27)

Step 2: a block formula for
\Phi
.

Write an arbitrary
\rho\in L(\mathbb{C}^{d})
in block form as

\rho=\begin{pmatrix}a&x^{\dagger}\\
y&Z\end{pmatrix},\qquad a\in\mathbb{C},\quad x,y\in H_{\perp},\quad Z\in L(H_{\perp}).

Using the block decomposition from Lemma
E.3
together with
B_{i}=\alpha_{i}P_{i}
, a direct multiplication yields

A_{i}\rho A_{i}^{\dagger}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{i}^{2}a+\alpha_{i}v_{i}^{\dagger}y+\alpha_{i}x^{\dagger}v_{i}+v_{i}^{\dagger}Zv_{i}&\alpha_{i}^{2}x^{\dagger}P_{i}+\alpha_{i}v_{i}^{\dagger}ZP_{i}\\
\alpha_{i}^{2}P_{i}y+\alpha_{i}P_{i}Zv_{i}&\alpha_{i}^{2}P_{i}ZP_{i}\end{pmatrix}.

Summing over
i
, using
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}v_{i}=0
and

\sum_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{\dagger}=I_{H_{\perp}}-N,

we obtain

\Phi(\rho)=\begin{pmatrix}a+\Tr\bigl((I_{H_{\perp}}-N)Z\bigr)&x^{\dagger}N+\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}v_{i}^{\dagger}ZP_{i}\\
Ny+\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}P_{i}Zv_{i}&T(Z)\end{pmatrix},

(E.28)

where

T(Z):=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}P_{i}ZP_{i}.

(E.29)

Step 3: a lower bound on
\dim\im(\Phi)
.

From Eq. (
E.28
) we introduce the four linear subspaces

V_{00}:=\Span\{|0\rangle\!\langle 0|\},

V_{01}:=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&x^{\dagger}N\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}:x\in H_{\perp}\right\}=\{|0\rangle\!\langle w|:w\in W\},

V_{10}:=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
Ny&0\end{pmatrix}:y\in H_{\perp}\right\}=\{|w\rangle\!\langle 0|:w\in W\},

V_{11}:=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&T(Z)\end{pmatrix}:Z\in L(H_{\perp})\right\}.

These subspaces live in disjoint matrix blocks, so their sum is direct. In particular,

\dim\im(\Phi)\geq 1+\dim V_{01}+\dim V_{10}+\dim V_{11}

(E.30)

once we know that all four subspaces are contained in
\im(\Phi)
.

The inclusions
V_{00},V_{01},V_{10}\subseteq\im(\Phi)
are immediate from Eq. (
E.28
). The inclusion
V_{11}\subseteq\im(\Phi)
is slightly less immediate. Fix
Z\in L(H_{\perp})
and set

\rho_{Z}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&Z\end{pmatrix}.

Then

\Phi(\rho_{Z})=\begin{pmatrix}\Tr\bigl((I_{H_{\perp}}-N)Z\bigr)&\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}v_{i}^{\dagger}ZP_{i}\\
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}P_{i}Zv_{i}&T(Z)\end{pmatrix}.

Define

c_{Z}:=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}P_{i}Zv_{i}.

For each
i
,
P_{i}Zv_{i}\in\im(P_{i})\subseteq W
, hence
c_{Z}\in W
. Since
N
is positive semidefinite and
\im(N)=W
, the restriction
N|_{W}:W\to W
is invertible. Therefore there exists
y_{Z}\in W
with
Ny_{Z}=c_{Z}
, and so

\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
c_{Z}&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
Ny_{Z}&0\end{pmatrix}\in V_{10}.

Next define

r_{Z}^{\dagger}:=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}v_{i}^{\dagger}ZP_{i}.

If
u\in W^{\perp}
, then
P_{i}u=0
for every
i
, hence
r_{Z}^{\dagger}u=0
. Equivalently, there exists
u_{Z}\in W
such that

r_{Z}^{\dagger}=u_{Z}^{\dagger}\Pi_{W},

where
\Pi_{W}
is the orthogonal projection onto
W
. Again using invertibility of
N|_{W}
, choose
x_{Z}\in W
with
Nx_{Z}=u_{Z}
. Since
N=N\Pi_{W}=\Pi_{W}N
and
N
is self-adjoint,

x_{Z}^{\dagger}N=u_{Z}^{\dagger}\Pi_{W}=r_{Z}^{\dagger}.

Therefore

\begin{pmatrix}0&r_{Z}^{\dagger}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&x_{Z}^{\dagger}N\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in V_{01}.

Also,

\begin{pmatrix}\Tr((I_{H_{\perp}}-N)Z)&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in V_{00}.

Subtracting these three elements from
\Phi(\rho_{Z})\in\im(\Phi)
shows that

\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&T(Z)\end{pmatrix}\in\im(\Phi),

and therefore
V_{11}\subseteq\im(\Phi)
.

Now
\dim V_{01}=\dim V_{10}=\dim W
. Choose an index
i_{0}
with
\alpha_{i_{0}}>0
. For every
X\in L(U_{i_{0}})
,

\langle X,T(X)\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}\left\lVert P_{i}XP_{i}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\geq\alpha_{i_{0}}^{2}\left\lVert P_{i_{0}}XP_{i_{0}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\alpha_{i_{0}}^{2}\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

Hence
T
is injective on
L(U_{i_{0}})
, so

\dim V_{11}=\rank(T)\geq\dim L(U_{i_{0}})=(m-1)^{2}.

Substituting into Eq. (
E.30
),

\dim\im(\Phi)\geq 1+2\dim W+(m-1)^{2}.

If the
P_{i}
are not all equal, then by Eq. (
E.26
)

\dim\im(\Phi)\geq 1+2m+(m-1)^{2}=m^{2}+2,

contradicting Eq. (
E.27
). Therefore all nonzero
P_{i}
coincide.
∎

Proof of Proposition
E.6
.

By Lemmas
E.8
and
E.9
, there exists a single rank-
(m-1)
orthogonal projection
P_{\perp}
on
H_{\perp}
such that

B_{i}=\alpha_{i}P_{\perp}\qquad\text{for all }i.

Set

P:=|0\rangle\!\langle 0|+P_{\perp}.

Then
P
is a rank-
m
orthogonal projection with
|0\rangle\in\im(P)
.

Choose a unitary matrix
U=(u_{ji})
on the Kraus index space such that the new coefficients

\alpha^{\prime}_{j}:=\sum_{i}u_{ji}\alpha_{i}

satisfy
(\alpha^{\prime}_{1},\alpha^{\prime}_{2},\dots,\alpha^{\prime}_{r})=(1,0,\dots,0)
. Define the mixed Kraus family

A^{\prime}_{j}:=\sum_{i}u_{ji}A_{i}.

The channel is unchanged. Because
B_{i}=\alpha_{i}P_{\perp}
, the lower-right blocks transform as

B^{\prime}_{j}=\sum_{i}u_{ji}B_{i}=\left(\sum_{i}u_{ji}\alpha_{i}\right)P_{\perp}=\alpha^{\prime}_{j}P_{\perp}.

Hence

B^{\prime}_{1}=P_{\perp},\qquad B^{\prime}_{j}=0\quad(j\geq 2).

Also,

v^{\prime}_{1}=\sum_{i}u_{1i}v_{i}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}v_{i}=0

by Eq. (
E.25
). Therefore

A^{\prime}_{1}=P,\qquad A^{\prime}_{j}=|0\rangle\!\langle v^{\prime}_{j}|\quad(j\geq 2).

Using again Eq. (
E.25
),

\sum_{j\geq 2}v^{\prime}_{j}{v^{\prime}_{j}}^{\dagger}=I_{H_{\perp}}-P_{\perp}=I_{d}-P

on
H_{\perp}
. Thus

\displaystyle\Phi(\rho)

\displaystyle=P\rho P+\sum_{j\geq 2}|0\rangle\!\langle v^{\prime}_{j}|\,\rho\,|v^{\prime}_{j}\rangle\!\langle 0|

\displaystyle=P\rho P+\Tr\bigl((I_{d}-P)\rho\bigr)|0\rangle\!\langle 0|,

which is exactly Eq. (
E.24
).
∎

E.5
Factorizations of reset channels

Lemma E.10

(Kraus operators of the identity channel)
.

Let
C
be finite dimensional, and let
\{K_{t}\}_{t}
be Kraus operators of the identity channel on
L(C)
:

\sum_{t}K_{t}XK_{t}^{\dagger}=X\qquad\text{for all }X\in L(C).

Then every
K_{t}
is a scalar multiple of the identity on
C
.

Proof.

Fix a unit vector
|\psi\rangle\in C
and apply the channel identity to
X=|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
:

|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|=\sum_{t}K_{t}|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|K_{t}^{\dagger}.

The right-hand side is a sum of positive semidefinite rank-one operators, while the left-hand side has one-dimensional support
\Span\{|\psi\rangle\}
. Therefore every vector
K_{t}|\psi\rangle
belongs to
\Span\{|\psi\rangle\}
. As this holds for every unit vector, each
K_{t}
is a scalar multiple of the identity.
∎

Proposition E.11

(Factorizations of a reset channel)
.

Let
P
be a rank-
m
orthogonal projection on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with
|0\rangle\in\im(P)
, and define

\Phi_{P}(\rho):=P\rho P+\Tr\bigl((I_{d}-P)\rho\bigr)|0\rangle\!\langle 0|.

Assume

\Phi_{P}=\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E},\qquad\mathcal{E}:L(\mathbb{C}^{d})\to L(\mathbb{C}^{m}),\qquad\mathcal{D}:L(\mathbb{C}^{m})\to L(\mathbb{C}^{d})

are CPTP. Then:

(i)

there exists a unitary
U:\im(P)\to\mathbb{C}^{m}
such that

\mathcal{E}(X)=UXU^{\dagger}\qquad\text{for all }X\in L(\im(P)).

(ii)

the decoder is exactly the inverse isometry channel,

\mathcal{D}(Y)=U^{\dagger}YU\qquad\text{for all }Y\in L(\mathbb{C}^{m}).

(iii)

the encoder has Kraus rank

\operatorname{rank}_{\mathrm{Kraus}}(\mathcal{E})=d-m+1.

Proof.

Let
C:=\im(P)\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
. For every

We provide an alternative proof of the isometric-decoder part of Proposition

Let
|0_{m}\rangle
denote the first standard basis vector of
\mathbb{C}^{m}

|0\rangle\in\im(P)
and
\rank(P)=m

|u_{1}\rangle=|0\rangle

Then

V^{\dagger}V=I_{m}
, and
V|0_{m}\rangle=|0\rangle

\mathcal{V}(X):=VXV^{\dagger},\qquad X\in L(\mathbb{C}^{m}),

and the channel
R:L(\mathbb{C}^{d})\to L(\mathbb{C}^{m})
by

R(Y):=V^{\dagger}YV+\Tr\bigl((I_{d}-P)Y\bigr)|0_{m}\rangle\!\langle 0_{m}|.

|f_{1}\rangle,\dots,|f_{d-m}\rangle
of
\im(I_{d}-P)
. Then
R
has Kraus operators

K_{0}:=V^{\dagger},\qquad K_{j}:=|0_{m}\rangle\!\langle f_{j}|\quad(1\leq j\leq d-m),

so
R
is CPTP. Moreover,

(R\circ\mathcal{V})(X)=X\qquad\text{for all }X\in L(\mathbb{C}^{m}).

Now set

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}:=\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{V}:L(\mathbb{C}^{m})\to L(\mathbb{C}^{m}),\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}:=R\circ\mathcal{D}:L(\mathbb{C}^{m})\to L(\mathbb{C}^{m}).

Both maps are CPTP, and

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}\circ\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}=R\circ\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{V}=R\circ\Phi_{P}\circ\mathcal{V}=R\circ\mathcal{V}=\mathrm{id}_{L(\mathbb{C}^{m})}.

Thus
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
is a CPTP channel with a CPTP left inverse. By the standard reversibility characterization of left-invertible channels (
[
55
, Theorem 18 and Remark 19]
,
[
50
, Theorem 2.1]
, and
[
52
, Theorem 10.1]
), such a channel must be of the form

\mathcal{D}\circ\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}=\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{V}=\Phi_{P}\circ\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}.

Therefore, for every

\mathcal{D}(X)=\mathcal{D}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(W^{\dagger}XW)\bigr)=\mathcal{V}(W^{\dagger}XW)=VW^{\dagger}XWV^{\dagger}.

Setting

gives an isometry

and

This is exactly the isometric form asserted in Proposition

.

Appendix G
Non-isometric decoders can outperform isometric ones for a fixed suboptimal encoder

The counterexample in Appendix
F
is about
globally optimal
encoder–decoder pairs. A much simpler phenomenon already appears if the encoder is held fixed but suboptimal.

Let
n=2
and
k=1
, and consider the source distribution consisting of

|\psi_{0}\rangle=|00\rangle,\qquad|\psi_{1}\rangle=|11\rangle,

sampled with probabilities
1-\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon

0<\varepsilon<1/2
. With an optimal encoder one can achieve perfect reconstruction using, for example, the encoder whose Kraus operators are

K_{0}=|0\rangle\!\langle 00|+|1\rangle\!\langle 11|,\qquad K_{1}=|0\rangle\!\langle 01|+|1\rangle\!\langle 10|,

and the isometric decoder

\mathcal{D}(\rho)=W\rho W^{\dagger},\qquad W=|00\rangle\!\langle 0|+|11\rangle\!\langle 1|.

Now instead fix the suboptimal encoder that discards the input and always outputs the maximally mixed qubit state
I_{2}/2
. If the decoder is allowed to be non-isometric, it can ignore its input and deterministically output
|00\rangle
, giving expected fidelity

(1-\varepsilon)\cdot 1+\varepsilon\cdot 0=1-\varepsilon>\frac{1}{2}.

Suppose instead that the decoder is restricted to be an isometry
W
. Then the reconstructed output state is always

W\frac{I_{2}}{2}W^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{2}P,\qquad P:=WW^{\dagger},

where
P
is a rank-
2
projector. For any pure state
|\psi\rangle
,

\langle\psi|\frac{1}{2}P|\psi\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Hence the expected fidelity is at most
1/2
. So for this fixed suboptimal encoder, non-isometric decoders strictly outperform all isometric decoders. This is weaker than Proposition
3.7
, but it pinpoints the failure of a tempting proof strategy already noted in spirit by
[
1
]

Appendix H
A source-dependent multiplicative guarantee for isometric decoders

Isometric decoders are not universally sufficient for exact optimality, but one can still prove a source-dependent approximation guarantee in the same blind single-copy setting.
Let
d:=2^{n}
and
m:=2^{k}
.
For a source distribution
\mu
over pure states
|\psi\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, define

F^{\star}(\mu):=\sup_{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}}F_{\mu}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}),\qquad F^{\star}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mu):=\sup_{\mathcal{E},\,V^{\dagger}V=I_{m}}F_{\mu}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D}_{V}).

Also let

\bar{\rho}:=\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu}[|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|],\qquad s_{m}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}(\bar{\rho}),\qquad\eta_{m}:=1-s_{m},

where all eigenvalues are listed in nonincreasing order and padded with zeros when necessary.
For each rank-
m
orthogonal projector
P
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, define

p_{P}(\psi):=\langle\psi|P|\psi\rangle,\qquad Y_{P}:=\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu}\Bigl[(1-p_{P}(\psi))P|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|P\Bigr].

Definition H.1
.

For finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces
A
and
B
, we say that a state

\rho\in L(A\otimes B)
is separable if it can be written as

\rho=\sum_{x=1}^{N}p_{x}\,\sigma_{x}\otimes\tau_{x},\qquad p_{x}\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{x=1}^{N}p_{x}=1,

where each
\sigma_{x}
is a density operator on
A
and each
\tau_{x}
is a density operator on
B
.

Finally, define

B_{\mu}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}P=P^{\dagger}=P^{2}\\
\rank(P)=m\end{subarray}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu}\bigl[p_{P}(\psi)^{2}\bigr]+\lambda_{\max}(Y_{P})\right)

and

U_{\mu}:=\sup\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}(\Omega):\begin{array}[]{l}\Omega\in L(\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes H_{E}),\ \Omega\geq 0,\ \Tr(\Omega)=1,\\[1.99997pt]
\Tr_{E}(\Omega)=\bar{\rho},\ \Omega\text{ separable},\\[1.99997pt]
H_{E}\text{ finite dimensional}\end{array}\right\}.

3.12
.

Theorem H.2
.

With the notation above,

\frac{F^{\star}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mu)}{F^{\star}(\mu)}\geq\frac{B_{\mu}}{U_{\mu}}.

Moreover,

U_{\mu}\leq s_{m},\qquad B_{\mu}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)s_{m}^{2}+\frac{s_{m}}{m},

\frac{F^{\star}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mu)}{F^{\star}(\mu)}\geq\frac{B_{\mu}}{s_{m}}\geq 1-\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\eta_{m}.

We divide the proof into four steps.

Step 1: the unrestricted optimum is at most
U_{\mu}
.

Fix any encoder
\mathcal{E}:L(\mathbb{C}^{d})\to L(\mathbb{C}^{m})
and decoder
\mathcal{D}:L(\mathbb{C}^{m})\to L(\mathbb{C}^{d})

W:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes H_{E}

\mathcal{D}(\sigma)=\Tr_{E}\bigl(W\sigma W^{\dagger}\bigr),

where
H_{E}
is finite dimensional, and set

P:=WW^{\dagger}.

Then
P
is a rank-
m
orthogonal projector on
\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes H_{E}
.
For a pure input state
|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
and a latent state
\sigma\in L(\mathbb{C}^{m})

\sigma

\mathbb{C}^{m}

\im(P)

\lambda_{\max}\bigl(P(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\otimes I_{E})P\bigr)=\lambda_{\max}\bigl((\langle\psi|\otimes I_{E})P(|\psi\rangle\otimes I_{E})\bigr).

matrix whose
x
-th column is
w_{x}
.
Then

\bar{\rho}=AA^{\dagger},\qquad\Omega=BB^{\dagger}.

Define the Gram matrices

H:=A^{\dagger}A,\qquad G:=B^{\dagger}B,\qquad K_{xy}:=\langle b_{x}\,|\,b_{y}\rangle.

The nonzero eigenvalues of
AA^{\dagger}
and
A^{\dagger}A
coincide, and likewise for
BB^{\dagger}
and
B^{\dagger}B
.
Hence the nonzero eigenvalues of
\bar{\rho}
are the eigenvalues of
H
, and the nonzero eigenvalues of
\Omega
are the eigenvalues of
G
.
Moreover,

G_{xy}=\langle w_{x}\,|\,w_{y}\rangle=\langle u_{x}\,|\,u_{y}\rangle\langle b_{x}\,|\,b_{y}\rangle=H_{xy}K_{xy},

so

G=H\circ K=K\circ H,

where
\circ
denotes the entrywise product.
Because
K
is a Gram matrix of unit vectors, it is positive semidefinite and has diagonal entries equal to
1
.
Thus the Schur multiplier

\Phi_{K}(X):=K\circ X

is completely positive by the Schur product theorem, and it is both unital and trace preserving because
K_{xx}=1
for all
x
.
Its Hilbert–Schmidt adjoint is

\Phi_{K}^{\dagger}(X)=\overline{K}\circ X,

and
\overline{K}
is again a correlation matrix, so
\Phi_{K}^{\dagger}
is also positive, unital, and trace preserving.
Now let
X
be any Hermitian matrix.
By the Ky Fan variational principle,

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}(X)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq Q\leq I\\
\Tr(Q)=m\end{subarray}}\Tr(QX).

Applying this to
G=\Phi_{K}(H)
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}(G)

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq Q\leq I\\
\Tr(Q)=m\end{subarray}}\Tr\bigl(Q\,\Phi_{K}(H)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq Q\leq I\\
\Tr(Q)=m\end{subarray}}\Tr\bigl(\Phi_{K}^{\dagger}(Q)\,H\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq Y\leq I\\
\Tr(Y)=m\end{subarray}}\Tr(YH)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}(H).

Because the eigenvalues of
H
are the nonzero eigenvalues of
\bar{\rho}
, the right-hand side equals
s_{m}
.
Therefore

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}(\Omega)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}(G)\leq s_{m}.

As this holds for every feasible
\Omega
, we conclude that

U_{\mu}\leq s_{m}.

Step 3: the isometric optimum is at least
B_{\mu}
.

Fix a rank-
m
orthogonal projector
P
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and a unit vector
|\xi\rangle\in\im(P)
.
Choose an isometry
V_{P}:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that

V_{P}V_{P}^{\dagger}=P,\qquad V_{P}|0\rangle=|\xi\rangle.

Define an encoder and decoder by

\mathcal{E}_{P,\xi}(\rho):=V_{P}^{\dagger}P\rho PV_{P}+\Tr\bigl((I-P)\rho\bigr)|0\rangle\!\langle 0|,\qquad\mathcal{D}_{V_{P}}(\sigma):=V_{P}\sigma V_{P}^{\dagger}.

The map
E_{P,\xi}
is CPTP because it is the sum of two completely positive maps and

\Tr\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{P,\xi}(\rho)\bigr)=\Tr(P\rho P)+\Tr\bigl((I-P)\rho\bigr)=\Tr(\rho).

For a pure input state
|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
, write
p:=p_{P}(\psi)=\langle\psi|P|\psi\rangle
.
Using
V_{P}V_{P}^{\dagger}=P
and
V_{P}|0\rangle\!\langle 0|V_{P}^{\dagger}=|\xi\rangle\!\langle\xi|
, we obtain

(\mathcal{D}_{V_{P}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{P,\xi})(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)=P|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|P+(1-p)|\xi\rangle\!\langle\xi|.

Therefore

\langle\psi|(\mathcal{D}_{V_{P}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{P,\xi})(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle=p^{2}+(1-p)\left\lvert\langle\xi\,|\,\psi\rangle\right\rvert^{2}.

Averaging over
\mu
gives

F_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{P,\xi},\mathcal{D}_{V_{P}})=\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu}[p_{P}(\psi)^{2}]+\langle\xi|Y_{P}|\xi\rangle.

Since
Y_{P}
is supported on
\im(P)
,

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\xi\rangle\in\im(P)\\
\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert=1\end{subarray}}F_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{P,\xi},\mathcal{D}_{V_{P}})=\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu}[p_{P}(\psi)^{2}]+\lambda_{\max}(Y_{P}).

Taking the supremum over all rank-
m
projectors
P
proves that

F^{\star}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mu)\geq B_{\mu}.

Step 4: an explicit lower bound on
B_{\mu}
.

Let
P_{\star}
be the orthogonal projector onto the span of the eigenvectors of
\bar{\rho}
corresponding to its
m
largest eigenvalues.
Then

\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu}[p_{P_{\star}}(\psi)]=\Tr(P_{\star}\bar{\rho})=s_{m}.

Because
Y_{P_{\star}}\geq 0
and
Y_{P_{\star}}
is supported on the
m
-dimensional space
\im(P_{\star})
,

\lambda_{\max}(Y_{P_{\star}})\geq\frac{1}{m}\Tr(Y_{P_{\star}})=\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu}\bigl[p_{P_{\star}}(\psi)-p_{P_{\star}}(\psi)^{2}\bigr].

Hence

\displaystyle B_{\mu}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu}\bigl[p_{P_{\star}}(\psi)^{2}\bigr]+\lambda_{\max}(Y_{P_{\star}})

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu}\bigl[p_{P_{\star}}(\psi)^{2}\bigr]+\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu}[p_{P_{\star}}(\psi)].

(H.1)

By Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu}\bigl[p_{P_{\star}}(\psi)^{2}\bigr]\geq\left(\mathbb{E}_{|\psi\rangle\sim\mu}[p_{P_{\star}}(\psi)]\right)^{2}=s_{m}^{2}.

Substituting this and
\mathbb{E}[p_{P_{\star}}(\psi)]=s_{m}
into Eq. (
H
) yields

B_{\mu}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)s_{m}^{2}+\frac{s_{m}}{m}.

Combining Steps 1–4, we obtain

\frac{F^{\star}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mu)}{F^{\star}(\mu)}\geq\frac{B_{\mu}}{U_{\mu}}\geq\frac{B_{\mu}}{s_{m}}\geq\frac{\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)s_{m}^{2}+\frac{s_{m}}{m}}{s_{m}}=\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)s_{m}+\frac{1}{m}=1-\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\eta_{m}.

∎

Remark H.3
.

The converse
F^{\star}(\mu)\leq s_{m}
is optimal as a function of
\bar{\rho}
alone.
Indeed, if

\bar{\rho}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(\bar{\rho})|i\rangle\!\langle i|

in an orthonormal eigenbasis and
\mu
is the orthogonal eigenensemble that draws
|i\rangle
with probability
\lambda_{i}(\bar{\rho})
, then a blind encoder can transmit the states
|1\rangle,\ldots,|m\rangle
perfectly through the
m
-dimensional bottleneck and map every
|i\rangle
with
i>m
to a fixed latent basis state.
With the obvious decoder, the resulting average fidelity is exactly
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}(\bar{\rho})=s_{m}
.
Thus any further improvement of the explicit bound

\frac{F^{\star}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mu)}{F^{\star}(\mu)}\geq 1-\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\eta_{m}

must use information beyond the spectrum of
\bar{\rho}
or must strengthen the lower bound on
F^{\star}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mu)
.

Appendix I
MNIST data preparation

Objects and dimensions.

Each (rescaled) MNIST image is represented as a real matrix

x\in[0,1]^{28\times 28}.

Fix integers
n>k\geq 1
, and let
d=2^{n}<784
. We map each image to a pure
n
-qubit state represented by a complex vector

\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{d},\qquad\|\psi\|_{2}=1.

Our goal is to concentrate most of the probability mass
\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\psi_{j}|^{2}
into the first
m
entries.
This data preparation scheme is designed to facilitate near-perfect reconstruction via a QAE with a bottleneck dimension of
2^{k}
. By concentrating the signal power into the low-frequency subspace, we ensure that the reconstruction infidelity is small. This approach enhances the sensitivity of our benchmarks, as small variations in model performance become more discernible when the total error magnitude is low.

Unitary-scaled 2D FFT feature map.

We compute a 2D discrete Fourier transform of the image with scaling
[
15
]
:

F:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{784}}\texttt{FFT2}(x)\ \in\mathbb{C}^{28\times 28}.

We then apply an FFT shift

\tilde{F}:=\texttt{fftshift}(F),

which permutes frequency bins so that the DC (zero-frequency) component is moved to the center of the
28\times 28
grid.

Low-frequency-first ordering.

Let the center index be
(c_{y},c_{x})=(14,14)
. For each frequency bin
(y,x)\in\{0,\dots,27\}^{2}
, define the squared radius from the center

d(y,x)^{2}:=(y-c_{y})^{2}+(x-c_{x})^{2}.

We define an ordering (permutation)
\pi
of the
784
grid points by sorting bins in increasing order of
d(y,x)^{2}
(with stable tie-breaking). Flattening
\tilde{F}
into a length-
784
vector and reordering by
\pi
yields a sequence

(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{784})\in\mathbb{C}^{784},

where
a_{1}
is the DC component, and increasing indices correspond to increasing spatial frequency. This low-frequency-first rule reflects the empirical fact that low spatial frequencies often capture most of the reconstruction-relevant content of images.
We retain only the first
d
coefficients
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})
. This discards higher-frequency coefficients (lossy truncation).

Head/tail placement and energy allocation.

Split the retained coefficients into a “head” and “tail”:

u:=(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{C}^{m},\qquad v:=(a_{m+1},\dots,a_{d})\in\mathbb{C}^{d-m}.

We build an unnormalized state
\widehat{\psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
by placing the head coefficients in the first
m
amplitudes and the tail coefficients next (optionally rescaled):

\widehat{\psi}_{j}=\begin{cases}u_{j},&1\leq j\leq m,\\
\alpha\,v_{j-m},&m+1\leq j\leq d.\end{cases}

Given a target head energy fraction
e\in(0,1)
, we choose
\alpha\geq 0
so that, after normalization, the fraction of probability mass in the head equals
e
. Specifically, we choose
e=0.9
and set

\alpha=\sqrt{\frac{1-e}{e}}\sqrt{\frac{\|u\|^{2}}{\|v\|^{2}}}.

Normalization and output state.

Finally we normalize to obtain a valid quantum state

\psi:=\frac{\widehat{\psi}}{\|\widehat{\psi}\|_{2}}.

In non-degenerate cases, the construction ensures

\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\psi_{j}|^{2}=e,

so a projective measurement onto the head subspace (spanned by the first
2^{k}
computational basis vectors) succeeds with probability
e
. Moreover, because the head entries store the lowest-frequency Fourier coefficients, truncating to the first
2^{k}
amplitudes corresponds to keeping a low-frequency approximation of the image, which is a simple proxy for preserving reconstruction-relevant information.
