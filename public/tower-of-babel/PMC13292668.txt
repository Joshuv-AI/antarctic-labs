Anticoccidial efficacy of a cinnamon-Rumex nervosus phytobiotic mixture on growth performance, carcass traits, cecal morphology, and cecal health in Eimeria tenella-challenged broilers☆
Authors: Mohammed M Qaid, Maged A Al-Garadi, Abdulaziz A Al-abdullatif, Rashed A Alhotan, Abdulrahman S Alharthi, Mutee Murshed, Jameel Al-Tamimi, Saleh Al-Quraishy

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Eimeria tenella challenge significantly impaired growth performance, gut morphology, and cecal health.•Relative to the infected untreated control (IUC), dietary supplementation with the cinnamon–Rumex nervosus mixture reduced oocyst shedding by up to 75.8%.•Phytogenic treatments achieved “marked” anticoccidial activity (ACI: 162-168).•High-dose phytogenic most effectively improved cecal morphology and reduced wall thickness.•Low-dose phytogenic yielded the best growth performance among phytogenic groups.Keywords: Anticoccidial and performance indices, Broilers, Cecal histomorphology, Eimeria tenella oocyst shedding, Phytogenic feed additive

Introduction

Coccidiosis, caused by protozoan parasites of the genus Eimeria, remains one of the most economically important enteric diseases affecting the global poultry industry (Aziz-Aliabadi et al., 2026; Carvalho et al., 2026; Llalla Vidal et al., 2025; Mathis et al., 2025). Among the seven recognized Eimeria species infecting chickens, Eimeria tenella is considered one of the most pathogenic, primarily targeting the ceca, where it induces severe hemorrhagic typhlitis, cecal lesions, epithelial destruction, impaired nutrient utilization, bloody diarrhea, and marked reductions in growth performance and feed efficiency (Choi et al., 2021; Kang et al., 2026; Qasem et al., 2020). Consequently, coccidiosis results in substantial economic losses through mortality, depressed productivity, impaired flock uniformity, and increased treatment and management costs (Chen et al., 2025a; Mohammed et al., 2025; Sharma and Kim, 2024).

Current control programs rely largely on synthetic anticoccidial drugs and ionophores incorporated into poultry feeds (Debbou-Iouknane et al., 2026; Qasem et al., 2020; Tian et al., 2026). However, prolonged and extensive use of these compounds has contributed to the emergence of resistant Eimeria strains, concerns regarding drug residues in poultry products, and increasing restrictions on antimicrobial growth promoters in many countries (Bharti et al., 2025; El-Saadony et al., 2026). These limitations have intensified interest in natural and sustainable alternatives capable of controlling coccidiosis while simultaneously supporting intestinal health and productive performance.

Phytogenic feed additives have emerged as promising candidates due to their wide spectrum of biological activities, including antimicrobial, antiparasitic, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory properties (Akdemir Evrendilek, 2026; Al-Garadi et al., 2025; Cojocariu et al., 2026; Sookying et al., 2026). In poultry, several phytogenic compounds have demonstrated the ability to interfere with Eimeria development, reduce oocyst output, protect intestinal tissues, and improve productive performance during coccidial infection (Galamatis et al., 2025a; Geng et al., 2024; Hascoët et al., 2025). These effects are commonly associated with modulation of oxidative stress, stabilization of intestinal epithelial integrity, suppression of inflammatory responses, inhibition of parasite sporulation, and enhancement of nutrient absorption capacity. Previous poultry studies have also demonstrated that phytogenic compounds derived from Cinnamomum verum and Rumex nervosus possess beneficial biological activities against coccidiosis and other enteric disorders in chickens (Banday et al., 2024; El-Hamid et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2026; Orimaye et al., 2024; Rehman et al.; Valdez et al., 2023).

Cinnamomum verum (cinnamon) is one of the most extensively studied phytogenic additives in poultry nutrition and contains high concentrations of cinnamaldehyde, eugenol, and other phenolic compounds with potent antimicrobial and antiparasitic activities (Batiha et al., 2020; Kowalska et al., 2021; Qaid et al., 2021a). Previous studies reported that cinnamon supplementation reduced Eimeria oocyst shedding, alleviated lesion severity, and improved cecal health in challenged broilers, although the overall anticoccidial response remained moderate under severe infection conditions (Qaid et al., 2021a). Mechanistically, cinnamaldehyde has been shown to disrupt protozoal cell membranes, inhibit sporulation, modulate intestinal microbiota, and reduce oxidative damage within intestinal tissues (Batiha et al., 2020; Kowalska et al., 2021).

Similarly, Rumex nervosus, a medicinal plant distributed throughout the Arabian Peninsula and neighboring regions, contains abundant phenolic acids, flavonoids, tannins, and gallic acid derivatives associated with antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and mucosal protective activities (Alsayadi et al., 2025; Mohamed Gohar et al., 2025; Qaid et al., 2021b). Previous investigations demonstrated that Rumex nervosus supplementation reduced oocyst shedding and partially improved intestinal pathology in Eimeria tenella-infected broilers, primarily through attenuation of oxidative stress and preservation of intestinal integrity (Qaid et al., 2021b; Qasem et al., 2020). In addition, phenolic constituents from Rumex species may help maintain epithelial barrier function and reduce inflammatory tissue damage during enteric infections.

Despite the documented biological activities of cinnamon and Rumex nervosus individually, information regarding their combined application against avian coccidiosis remains limited. Therefore, the present phytogenic blend was formulated based on the complementary modes of action of both botanicals. Cinnamon-derived compounds are mainly associated with direct antiparasitic and antimicrobial effects, whereas Rumex nervosus contributes strong antioxidant and mucosal protective activities. Combining these phytogenic sources may therefore provide additive or synergistic benefits through simultaneous suppression of parasite development, mitigation of intestinal inflammation, and improvement of epithelial recovery and nutrient absorptive capacity. Similar enhanced responses have been reported for other multi-component phytogenic formulations used against coccidiosis in broilers (Geng et al., 2024; Hascoët et al., 2025; Zaman et al., 2012), supporting the concept that phytogenic combinations may exert broader biological efficacy than single-herb applications.

In addition to their broad biological activities, phytogenic compounds may also improve intestinal functionality by enhancing villus development, reducing crypt hyperplasia, modulating gut microbiota, and limiting oxidative and inflammatory damage, thereby supporting epithelial integrity and productive performance in broilers (Azzam et al., 2020; Galamatis et al., 2025a; Oni and Oke, 2025). Preservation of intestinal architecture is particularly important during Eimeria infection because epithelial disruption and cecal inflammation directly impair nutrient utilization and growth performance.

Therefore, the present study hypothesized that dietary supplementation with a cinnamon-Rumex nervosus phytogenic blend would improve resistance to Eimeria tenella infection through complementary antiparasitic and gut-protective mechanisms, including suppression of oocyst shedding, reduction of cecal tissue damage, preservation of intestinal morphology, and improvement of growth efficiency. Accordingly, this study evaluated graded dietary levels of a cinnamon bark and Rumex nervosus leaf mixture in experimentally challenged broilers, with emphasis on parasitological responses, clinical indicators, growth performance, cecal morphometry, cecal histomorphology, and anticoccidial index (ACI) as indicators of phytogenic efficacy under controlled challenge conditions.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

All procedures were approved by the King Saud University (KSU) Scientific Research Ethics Committee (KSU-SE-20-44) and complied with institutional animal care and ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

Experimental design, birds, and management

The experimental design consisted of six dietary treatments, each with five replicate pens, and each replicate pen contained five birds, resulting in a total of 150 birds (6 treatments × 5 replicates × 5 birds = 150 one-day-old Ross 308 broiler chicks (44.06 ± 0.33 g)). A completely randomized design was used. Birds were reared to 21 d of age in a climate-controlled facility in galvanized wire cage batteries for the entire experimental period at Zoology department, KSU under standard husbandry conditions. Cages (50 × 50 × 30 cm) provided 0.25 m² per replicate (0.05 m²/bird). Temperature was maintained at 35°C on day 1 and reduced gradually to 24°C by day 21; lighting was 24 h for week 1 and 23L:1D thereafter. Feed and water were provided ad libitum. Birds were vaccinated at the hatchery against infectious bronchitis (IB), infectious bursal disease (IBD), and Newcastle disease (ND) according to commercial practice.

Diets and treatments

As shown in Supplementary Table S1, corn-soybean meal diets were formulated to meet or exceed Ross 308 requirements for starter (0-14 d) and grower (15-21 d) phases and were offered as mash throughout the experiment. The phytogenic mixtures containing cinnamon and Rumex nervosus leaf powder were top-dressed onto the basal diets at the designated inclusion levels from day 1 and continued throughout the trial as protective natural anticoccidial treatments. Chemical characterization of the mixture is presented in Supplementary Tables S2-S4. The phytogenic mixtures are characterized by a carbohydrate-rich, fiber-dense composition with a phenolic profile supporting its multifunctional bioactivity in poultry nutrition. The major bioactive compounds identified included cinnamaldehyde, gallic acid, phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and fatty acid methyl esters, which are known for their antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and anticoccidial activities. These phytochemicals were naturally incorporated into the experimental diets through the dietary inclusion of the phytogenic mixture and may have contributed to the observed improvements in growth performance, oxidative status, and suppression of Eimeria tenella infection.

The phytogenic inclusion levels were selected based on preliminary individual trials and published literature demonstrating their safety and potential anticoccidial and antioxidant activities in broilers (Qaid et al., 2021a, 2021b). The standard anticoccidial drug treatment (STD-C) consisted of Toltrazuril (“TolicoxR”) administered orally via drinking water at a dose of 7 mg/kg body weight from day 15, coinciding with the Eimeria tenella challenge, until day 21 of age, according to the manufacturer’s recommendations and previously published studies evaluating anticoccidial efficacy (Na et al., 2026).

Treatments (Table 1) comprised: phytogenic low (PL-C; 2 g cinnamon + 1 g Rumex nervosus/kg), medium (PM-C; 4 + 3 g/kg), and high (PH—C; 6 + 5 g/kg) under challenge; a standard anticoccidial drug group (STD-C; 25 mg/1 ml Toltrazuril (7 mg/kg BW) under challenge; an infected untreated control (IUC); and an unchallenged untreated control (UUC). This design enabled dose- dependent evaluation against both infected and non-infected baselines.

Preparation of Eimeria tenella inoculum and challenge

Un-sporulated oocysts of Eimeria tenella were obtained from naturally infected chickens, sporulated in 2.5% potassium dichromate at 25°C for 72 h, purified by salt flotation, and stored at 4°C (El-Ashram and Suo, 2017). Identity was confirmed by morphology, lesion pattern, and internal transcribed spacer 1 partial region (ITS-1 region) analysis by our colleague's researchers at the parasitology laboratory at the Department of Zoology, KSU. To ensure infectivity, oocysts were passaged 2-3 times in coccidia-free broilers. A preliminary trial established a dose inducing consistent cecal lesions without excessive mortality. At day 15, challenged groups received 1 mL oral gavage containing 2 × 104 sporulated Eimeria tenella oocysts/bird; following Wang et al. (2026), UUC received distilled water.

At 21 d of age (7 d post-infection "7 DPI "), one bird per replicate cage (n = 5 per treatment) was randomly selected for necropsy, fasted (10 h), weighed, and processed. Birds were humanely slaughtered by severing the jugular vein, then defeathered and eviscerated. Carcass weight was recorded immediately, and dressing percentage was calculated as (carcass weight / pre-slaughter weight) × 100, according to Al-abdullatif et al. (2025). Breast and legs were weighed and expressed as a proportion of carcass weight (carcass part weight / carcass weight × 100) following Mousapoor et al. (2023).

Cecal morphometry and histomorphometric indices

Cecal morphometric measurements included absolute cecal weight, cecal length, and cecal wall thickness for each slaughtered bird, without normalization to body weight, according to Nerida et al. (2024). Cecal length (cm) was measured using a ruler, whereas cecal weight (g) was determined using an electronic balance. Cecal wall thickness was estimated as the weight-to-length ratio (g/cm), which was used as an indicator of cecal tissue hypertrophy and inflammation. For cecal histomorphometric, cecal segments (approximately 2 cm) were collected, rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, paraffin-embedded after dehydration in graded ethanol, sectioned (5 μm) using a microtome, mounted on glass slides in a 50°C water bath, and stained (hematoxylin and eosin: H&E). Villus height, villus width, and crypt depth were measured using image analysis (Nikon, Japan). Clinical evaluation, oocyst shedding, and anticoccidial indices

Clinical signs and mortality were recorded daily and survival rate computed as the percentage of surviving birds relative to the initial number. Fecal bloody diarrhea, a typical clinical sign of coccidiosis, was scored daily from 4 to 7 post-infection (0-4 scale), where 0 = normal feces, 1-4 = increasing numbers of bloody fecal spots. Then, fecal bloody diarrhea reduction (%) was calculated relative to the infected untreated control (IUC) group and presented descriptively (Galamatis et al., 2025a).

Excreta samples were collected from each replicate pen, thoroughly mixed, and suspended in 10 mL of water. Oocyst output per gram (OPG) was determined at 7 DPI using a McMaster-type counting method as described by Qaid et al. (2021b). A 10-µL aliquot was examined under an Olympus Corporation light microscope, and results were expressed as OPG output.

The inhibition rate (%) was calculated as the relative reduction in oocyst shedding compared with the infected untreated control group and was used as an indicator of anticoccidial efficacy, reflecting suppression of parasite replication and potential environmental contamination. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using (SAS, 2012), SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) under a completely randomized design (CRD), with dietary treatment considered as a fixed effect. Statistical analyses were performed using the General Linear Model (GLM) procedure. The statistical model used was.

Where Yij represents the observed value of the dependent variable, μ is the overall mean, Ti is the fixed effect of the ith treatment, and eij is the random residual error.

Prior to analysis, data were evaluated for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and for homogeneity of variances. Percentage variables, including survival rate (%), oocyst inhibition rate (%), and oocyst value (%), were arcsine square-root transformed before statistical analysis to satisfy assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity. However, untransformed means are presented in the tables and figures for ease of interpretation.

Continuous variables that met parametric assumptions, such as performance indices and carcass traits, were analyzed by one-way ANOVA using the GLM procedure. When significant treatment effects were detected (P < 0.05), means were separated using Tukey’s multiple range test. Results are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Because fecal bloody diarrhea scores and lesion scores represented ordinal qualitative data, they were analyzed using nonparametric procedures rather than parametric ANOVA. Specifically, data were subjected to the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test to identify differences among treatment groups.

Replicate pen served as the experimental unit for growth performance traits, whereas the individual bird served as the experimental unit for carcass characteristics, cecal morphometry, histomorphological measurements, lesion scores, fecal scores, and parasitological assessments.

In addition to overall treatment comparisons, orthogonal contrasts were retained only for continuous parametric variables to evaluate the infection effect by comparing the infected untreated control with the uninfected untreated control (UUC vs. IUC), and the treatment effect under challenge conditions by comparing the IUC group with all challenged treated groups collectively (IUC vs. challenged treated groups), whereas they were not applied to ordinal qualitative nonparametric score data. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Cecal histomorphometric indices

As shown in Table 3, Eimeria tenella challenge severely impaired cecal architecture, as shown in IUC by reduced villus height (635 μm), VSA (0.245 mm²), and VH/CD ratio (8.80), along with increased villus width (119 μm) and crypt depth (90.3 μm), indicating villus atrophy and crypt hyperplasia, reflecting compromised absorptive capacity and increased epithelial turnover. Phytogenic supplementation significantly improved all parameters (P < 0.0001). Villus height increased markedly in all phytogenic groups (875-904 μm), exceeding both IUC and STD-C (775 μm), with PH—C showing the highest value and VSA closest to UUC. Crypt depth was reduced (77.0-80.2 μm), and villus width remained moderate, reflecting reduced inflammation and improved epithelial stability. Consequently, VH/CD ratio was highest in PL-C (12.5), followed by PH—C and PM-C, all significantly exceeding both IUC and STD-C, indicating superior intestinal efficiency. Although STD-C partially improved morphology, it remained inferior to phytogenic treatments in key indices. Overall, phytogenic supplementation, particularly at higher levels, effectively restored intestinal structure and, in some parameters, outperformed the standard anticoccidial.

Fecal bloody diarrhea score and bloody diarrhea reduction

Bloody diarrhea scores were affected by treatment (P = 0.009). The IUC group exhibited the highest bloody diarrhea score, whereas all phytogenic-treated groups and the STD-C group showed markedly lower scores with corresponding reductions ranging from 80.6 to 93.5%. The greatest reduction was observed in STD-C, followed by PH—C. No bloody diarrhea was observed in the UUC group

Fecal oocyst count and oocyst inhibition rate

As shown in Table 4 and Fig. 4, The IUC group showed the highest oocyst count (3.37 × 10⁵ OPG feces) and no inhibition, confirming extensive Eimeria tenella replication. All phytogenic-treated groups significantly reduced oocyst shedding (0.82 × 10⁵ OPG feces) and increased inhibition rates compared with IUC, with PH—C showing the greatest efficacy among phytogenic treatments (75.8% inhibition). However, the STD-C treatment achieved the strongest suppression overall, with the lowest oocyst count (0.21 × 10⁵ OPG) and highest inhibition rate (93.8%).

Survival rate: Survival rate was reduced in the IUC group (95%) compared with the UUC group (100%), confirming the negative impact of Eimeria tenella infection. Dietary supplementation with the cinnamon–Rumex nervosus phytogenic blend significantly improved relative BWG compared with IUC, with PL-C exhibiting the greatest improvement among phytogenic treatments. However, the standard anticoccidial treatment maintained the highest relative BWG values overall (Fig. 5).

Anticoccidial index (ACI): The ACI values confirmed the severe negative impact of Eimeria tenella infection in the IUC, which exhibited the lowest ACI (83.6), indicating poor health status and severe disease challenge. In contrast, all treated challenged groups showed markedly improved ACI values, reflecting enhanced survival, growth performance, and reduced lesion severity and oocyst shedding. All phytogenic treatments (PL-C, PM-C, PH—C) significantly achieved “marked” activity (162.3–167.8), fell within the marked ACI category (160-180). Among the phytogenic treatments, PH—C achieved the highest ACI (167.8), followed by PL-C (165.7) and PM-C (162.3), demonstrating moderate anticoccidial efficacy. However, the STD-C produced a significantly greater ACI (187.5, excellent efficacy), approaching the UUC (200.0), which represented the optimal health and performance status.

Overall, ACI values demonstrated that Eimeria tenella infection severely impaired bird health in the IUC group, whereas all phytogenic treatments improved anticoccidial performance by enhancing survival and growth while reducing lesion severity and oocyst shedding, with PH—C showing the best efficacy among phytogenic groups, although STD-C remained superior overall.

Discussion

Coccidiosis caused by Eimeria tenella remains a major constraint in broiler production due to intestinal damage, impaired nutrient utilization, oxidative stress, and depressed productive performance (Choi et al., 2021; Mohammed et al., 2025; Qaid et al., 2021a; Qasem et al., 2020). In the present study, the infected untreated control (IUC) group exhibited severe clinical and parasitological manifestations, including elevated oocyst shedding, bloody diarrhea, lesion severity, impaired growth performance, and marked deterioration of cecal morphology and histoarchitecture, confirming the severity of the experimental challenge model.

Dietary supplementation with the cinnamon–Rumex nervosus phytogenic blend effectively alleviated these adverse effects by improving parasitological responses, clinical signs, growth performance, and cecal integrity. All phytogenic-treated groups reduced oocyst shedding and improved anticoccidial index values, with PH—C exhibiting the strongest anticoccidial response. Although the standard anticoccidial treatment remained superior, the phytogenic blend demonstrated substantial biological efficacy under challenge conditions.

Growth performance responses followed a similar pattern, with Eimeria tenella challenge markedly impairing BWG, FCR, PI, and EPEF in the IUC group. Phytogenic supplementation alleviated these adverse effects and partially restored productive efficiency under challenge conditions, particularly at the low inclusion level (PL-C), which produced the most favorable PI and EPEF values among phytogenic treatments. Nevertheless, the standard anticoccidial treatment remained superior in feed efficiency and overall production efficiency, likely because of its greater efficacy in suppressing parasite replication and minimizing intestinal damage. The improvements in PI and EPEF observed in phytogenic-treated birds may be associated with improved nutrient utilization, preservation of intestinal absorptive capacity, and attenuation of oxidative and inflammatory damage within cecal tissues. Similar improvements in productive efficiency indices following phytogenic supplementation during coccidial challenge have been reported in broilers supplemented with phytogenic blends and essential oils, where enhanced growth performance and feed utilization were linked to improved intestinal integrity and reduced parasite burden (Geng et al., 2024; Hascoët et al., 2025). The absence of additional performance enhancement at higher inclusion levels may further suggest the existence of an optimal phytogenic supplementation threshold, as previously reported for several phytogenic feed additives (Ntsongota et al., 2025; Rakhmatullin et al., 2026).

The infected untreated control group had a significantly lower carcass weight, and the treated groups had a slightly higher carcass weight, which suggests that infection slowed overall growth while dietary changes helped prevent weight loss without changing carcass distribution. This is in line with what Čobanović et al. (2024) found, which showed that healthy broilers have higher carcass weights than infected untreated birds. In addition, Qaid et al. (2022) and Al-Baadani et al. (2026) found that emeriosis negatively affects bird carcass weight, carcass yield, and most carcass traits. However, in the current study, treatment had little effect on carcass traits, which means that neither the Eimeria tenella challenge nor the phytogenic supplementation had a big effect on carcass composition.

Similarly, cecal morphometric measurements demonstrated that phytogenic supplementation attenuated the infection-induced increase in cecal weight and wall thickness, both of which are commonly associated with inflammation and tissue hypertrophy. The normalization of cecal wall thickness observed particularly in PH—C may reflect reduced inflammatory infiltration and improved tissue recovery. These effects are likely associated with the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of cinnamaldehyde, gallic acid derivatives, flavonoids, and tannins present in the phytogenic mixture. Cecal histomorphological findings provide mechanistic support for these responses. Phytogenic supplementation increased villus height, reduced crypt depth, and improved villus height-to-crypt depth ratio, indicating enhanced absorptive capacity and reduced epithelial turnover. These improvements suggest better preservation of intestinal functionality and epithelial recovery during coccidial challenge. Interestingly, some histomorphological improvements observed in phytogenic-treated groups were comparable to or greater than those of the standard anticoccidial treatment, suggesting additional trophic effects of the phytogenic blend on intestinal structure beyond direct parasite suppression (Abd El-Hack et al., 2024; Galamatis et al., 2025a, 2025b; Hascoët et al., 2025).

The observed protective effects are likely associated with the complementary biological activities of the phytogenic constituents. Cinnamon contains cinnamaldehyde, eugenol, and related polyphenols with antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antiparasitic activities capable of disrupting parasite membranes, inhibiting sporulation, and limiting oxidative damage within intestinal tissues (Batiha et al., 2020; Kowalska et al., 2021; Qaid et al., 2021a). In parallel, Rumex nervosus contains abundant flavonoids, tannins, anthraquinones, gallic acid derivatives, and other phenolic compounds known for antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and mucosal protective activities (Alsayadi et al., 2025; Islam et al., 2025; Qaid et al., 2021b). Tannins may interfere with parasite metabolism and colonization, whereas flavonoids and phenolic acids may reduce oxidative stress and preserve epithelial barrier integrity through modulation of inflammatory responses and reactive oxygen species scavenging (Li et al., 2026). The integration of these mechanisms may explain the reduced lesion severity, lower oocyst shedding, improved cecal morphology, and enhanced productive performance observed in the present study.

Comparable responses have been reported for several phytogenic anticoccidial systems rich in phenolics, flavonoids, alkaloids, tannins, terpenoids, and essential oils (Yuke et al., 2026). For example, Calotropis procera extract demonstrated marked inhibition of Eimeria tenella sporulation and sporozoite viability (Murshed et al., 2025), whereas a multi-herbal formulation containing Trachyspermum ammi, Calotropis procera, Azadirachta indica, and Nicotiana tabacum reduced infection severity and improved broiler performance in challenged birds (Zaman et al., 2012). Similarly, phytogenic blends enriched with flavonoids, alkaloids, and essential oils significantly reduced oocyst shedding and improved gut health and anticoccidial indices in broilers (Geng et al., 2024; Hascoët et al., 2025). These findings support the concept that phytogenic combinations possessing multiple bioactive classes interventions may provide broader biological efficacy.

The improvements in bloody diarrhea scores and lesion severity further confirm the protective role of the phytogenic blend against cecal damage induced by Eimeria tenella. The marked reduction in lesion severity observed in PH—C suggests enhanced preservation of cecal epithelial integrity and reduced inflammatory injury. Similar improvements in intestinal integrity following phytogenic supplementation have previously been associated with attenuation of oxidative stress and stabilization of epithelial tissues (Kaschubek et al., 2018; Toschi et al., 2022).

Overall, the present findings indicate that the cinnamon–Rumex nervosus phytogenic blend exerted significant anticoccidial and gut-protective effects in Eimeria tenella-challenged broilers through combined antiparasitic, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and epithelial protective mechanisms. Although its efficacy remained lower than that of the standard anticoccidial drug, the phytogenic blend substantially improved cecal health, productive performance, and intestinal morphology under challenge conditions. These findings support the growing evidence that strategically formulated phytogenic combinations may represent promising sustainable alternatives for coccidiosis management in poultry production. Nevertheless, the present study was limited to the early post-infection period and did not include molecular, microbiological, or microbiome analyses, which restricted deeper mechanistic interpretation and broader field applicability. Therefore, future studies should evaluate longer challenge periods, dose optimization, synergistic phytogenic strategies, microbiome modulation, molecular pathways associated with intestinal protection and immunity, and economic feasibility under commercial production conditions

Conclusion

In conclusion, dietary supplementation with a cinnamon-Rumex nervosus phytogenic blend effectively mitigated the adverse effects of Eimeria tenella infection in broiler chickens. The treatment significantly reduced oocyst shedding, improved inhibition rates, and alleviated clinical signs, including lesion severity and bloody diarrhea. Improvements in growth performance, feed efficiency, cecal histomorphology, and cecal integrity accompanied these benefits. Among the tested inclusion levels, the high dose (PH—C) demonstrated the strongest anticoccidial and gut-protective effects, whereas the low dose (PL-C) yielded the most favorable growth performance, indicating a dose-dependent response with an optimal inclusion threshold. Although the standard anticoccidial drug remained superior in overall efficacy, the phytogenic blend consistently achieved “marked” anticoccidial activity. Therefore, this phytogenic mixture represents a promising natural alternative for coccidiosis control, with potential to reduce reliance on conventional anticoccidial drugs. Further long-term and mechanistic studies under field conditions are recommended to validate and optimize its practical application.
