On Quantum Learning Advantage Under Symmetries
Tuyen Nguyen, Mária Kieferová, Amira Abbas

Abstract
Symmetry underlies many of the most effective classical and quantum learning algorithms, yet whether quantum learners can gain a fundamental advantage under symmetry-imposed structures remains an open question. Based on evidence that classical statistical query ( 𝖲𝖰 \mathsf{SQ} ) frameworks have revealed exponential query complexity in learning symmetric function classes, we ask: can quantum learning algorithms exploit the problem symmetry better? In this work, we investigate the potential benefits of symmetry within the quantum statistical query ( 𝖰𝖲𝖰 \mathsf{QSQ} ) model, which is a natural quantum analog of classical 𝖲𝖰 \mathsf{SQ} . Our results uncover three distinct phenomena: (i) we obtain an exponential separation between 𝖰𝖲𝖰 \mathsf{QSQ} and 𝖲𝖰 \mathsf{SQ} on a permutation-invariant function class; (ii) we establish query complexity lower bounds for 𝖰𝖲𝖰 \mathsf{QSQ} learning that match, up to constant factors, the corresponding classical 𝖲𝖰 \mathsf{SQ} lower bounds for most commonly studied symmetries; however, the potential advantages may occur under highly skewed orbit distributions; and (iii) we further identify a tolerance-based separation exists, where quantum learners succeed at noise levels that render classical 𝖲𝖰 \mathsf{SQ} algorithms ineffective. Together, these findings provide insight into when symmetry can enable quantum advantage in learning.

Introduction

Symmetry has long played a central role in the design and analysis of machine learning models. In classical learning, widely used architectures such as convolutional and graph neural networks encode translation and permutation invariance as geometric inductive biases, yielding significant improvements in efficiency and accuracy
[
18
,
29
,
9
,
7
]
. Beyond empirical performance, a growing body of theoretical work has demonstrated that encoding symmetry as an architectural inductive bias can reduce sample complexity and tighten generalization bounds, thereby providing a principled explanation for the observed success of symmetry-aware models
[
12
,
19
,
21
,
27
]
. The importance of symmetry extends just as strongly to the quantum setting. Quantum models are inherently constrained by group symmetries, from the representation theory of quantum states to the design of equivariant quantum neural networks
[
26
,
28
,
22
]
. Symmetry-inspired constructions of quantum circuits have been proposed as remedies to central obstacles in quantum neural networks, such as barren plateaus
[
20
]
and excessive local minima
[
4
]
. These proposals suggest that encoding symmetry into circuit architectures can stabilize optimization landscapes, reduce the number of parameters, and capture structural regularities in data, closely mirroring classical geometric deep learning
[
9
]
.

Despite these promising directions, it remains poorly understood whether symmetry can deliver a quantum advantage over its classical counterpart. This motivates a fundamental question:

Can symmetry enable quantum advantage in learning tasks?

This question has been partially addressed in recent work
[
10
]
, which studies the classical simulability of quantum neural networks designed to avoid barren plateaus. That work shows that enforcing symmetry, such as
\mathbb{S}_{n}
-equivariance, can provably improve trainability, but may simultaneously restrict expressivity to the extent that the resulting models become efficiently classically simulable. It remains unclear, however, whether this phenomenon extends beyond equivariant quantum neural networks to more general quantum learning algorithms.

In contrast, existing results indicate that learning symmetric function classes can be exponentially hard in the statistical query (
\mathsf{SQ}
) model
[
17
]
. The
\mathsf{SQ}
framework captures a broad family of classical learning algorithms, including gradient-based methods, convex optimization, and Markov-chain Monte Carlo
[
24
]
. In particular, it demonstrates that while symmetry can dramatically reduce the amount of data required to represent a target function, the computational cost of learning such functions may remain prohibitively large.
[
17
]
proved that imposition of symmetry on a function class only reduces the computational complexity of learning by a factor proportional to the size of the group.

In this work, we ask: can
quantum statistical query
(
\mathsf{QSQ}
) algorithms exploit the problem symmetry better? The
\mathsf{QSQ}
model, introduced by
[
5
]
, extends the classical statistical query (
\mathsf{SQ}
) framework of
[
16
]
with the goal of identifying potential quantum advantages in learning classical function classes. Our aim is therefore to have a direct, task-matched comparison between the quantum and classical settings for learning symmetric functions, as studied in
[
17
]
. We compare the two models through their
query complexity
, defined as the number of queries to the respective statistical oracles required to learn a target function class to a prescribed accuracy. Each query is answered up to a tolerance parameter
\tau
, which controls the precision of the statistical information returned by the oracle. Here,
\mathsf{Stat}
denotes the classical statistical oracle and
\mathsf{Qstat}
its quantum analogue; formal definitions are given in a later section. This query-complexity perspective offers a clean, information-theoretic measure of learning power, which we will employ to rigorously compare the capabilities of classical and quantum models.

1.1
Our results

We provide a unified characterization of how group-invariant structure governs the learnability of both classical and quantum models within the statistical query framework. Our results reveal a sharp dichotomy: while symmetry can enable dramatic quantum advantages in special cases, it also imposes fundamental limitations that collapse quantum learning to classical hardness in broad regimes. Our main contributions are summarized below.

Permutation-invariant separation.

We begin by identifying a setting that admits an exponential separation between quantum and classical statistical query models.

{thrm}

[Permutation-invariant separation (Informal; see Thms.
3.1
and
3.1
)]
a

There exists a permutation-invariant function class such that, for any tolerance parameter
\tau\in(0,1/2)
, the class can be learned exactly by a
\mathsf{QSQ}
algorithm using
\mathcal{O}(n)
queries to
\mathsf{Qstat}
, whereas any
\mathsf{SQ}
algorithm requires at least
\tau^{2}\exp\!\left(n^{\Omega(1)}\right)
queries to
\mathsf{Stat}
to achieve sufficiently small classification error.

A lower-bound under general symmetry.

We then move beyond permutation invariance to arbitrary group actions. Here, we develop an orbit-based analysis that yields lower bounds for both classical and quantum statistical query models. These lower bounds reveal that the learnability of symmetric function classes is fundamentally controlled by the induced orbit structure of the group action.

{thrm}

[
\mathsf{QSQ}
lower bounds under general symmetry (Informal; see Thms.
3.2
and
3.2
]
Let
G
be a symmetry group acting on
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}^{n}
via a representation

\rho:G\to\mathrm{GL}(\mathcal{X})
, and let

\mathcal{C}_{\rho}=\{f:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}\mid f(\rho(g)\cdot x)=f(x)\ \text{for all }g\in G,\,x\in\mathcal{X}\}

denote the corresponding class of symmetric Boolean functions.
Let
\mathcal{O}_{\rho}
be the set of orbits induced by the action of
G
, and define the orbit distribution

p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}(O)={|O|}/{|\mathcal{X}|},\forall O\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}.

Any classical
\mathsf{SQ}
algorithm that learns
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
to constant error with tolerance
\tau
requires at least

\Omega\!\left(\frac{\tau^{2}}{\|p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2}}\right)

queries. Moreover, there exists
\mathsf{QSQ}
algorithms that learn
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
with the same tolerance
\tau
requires at least

\Omega\!\left(\frac{\tau^{2}\,|\mathcal{X}|}{\max_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}|O_{k}|}\right)

queries.

A direct consequence of the above bounds is that, for most commonly studied symmetry groups, the quantum and classical lower bounds coincide up to constant factors. At the same time, our analysis identifies highly skewed orbit distributions as a structural regime in which such an advantage may arise.

{remark}
[Informal; see Rems.
3.2
and
3.2
]
For most commonly studied symmetry groups, the query complexity lower bound for
\mathsf{QSQ}
learning matches the classical
\mathsf{SQ}
lower bound up to constant factors. However, an asymptotic quantum advantage may arise when the induced orbit distribution is highly skewed.

Tolerance-based separations.

Finally, we identify a quantum advantage through the tolerant parameter. In particular, quantum learners can succeed in tolerance regimes that render classical
\mathsf{SQ}
algorithms ineffective.

{thrm}

[Tolerance-based advantage (Informal; see Thm.
3.3
]
Let
\mathcal{S}
be a subclass of symmetric Boolean functions with disjoint supports, each having measure
\zeta
. If
\zeta<0.4
, then for any tolerance
\tau
satisfying

2\zeta<\tau<\sqrt{2\zeta-\zeta^{2}}
, the following hold:

•

Classical hardness:
No classical
\mathsf{SQ}
algorithm can learn
\mathcal{S}
with tolerance
\tau
.

•

Quantum learnability:
There exists a
\mathsf{QSQ}
algorithm that learns
\mathcal{S}
with tolerance
\tau
using
O(\log|\mathcal{S}|)
queries.

Background

2.1
Notation

Throughout the paper, we use uppercase letters such as
M
,
A
, and
U
to denote matrices, with
I
representing the identity matrix of appropriate dimension. The symbol
\mathbf{1}
denotes the all-ones column vector, with dimension clear from context. For two quantum states (density matrices)
\rho
and
\sigma
, their trace distance is defined as

d_{\text{tr}}(\rho,\sigma)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}\Tr\!\left[\sqrt{(\rho-\sigma)^{\dagger}(\rho-\sigma)}\right],

where
\|\cdot\|_{1}
is the Schatten
1
-norm. For a matrix
M
, we denote by
\|M\|
its operator norm, i.e., the largest singular value of
M
. For an event, we write
\mathds{1}(\cdot)
for its indicator function, which returns
1
if the event occurs and
0
otherwise. We use
\text{Unif}(S)
to denote the uniform distribution over a finite set
S
, with
\text{Unif}(\{0,1\}^{n})
representing the uniform distribution over
n
-bit strings in particular.

2.2
Quantum Statistical Queries Model

The statistical query (
\mathsf{SQ}
) model
[
16
]
captures learning algorithms that access
a target function only through expectations of bounded query functions, rather than labeled samples.
The
quantum statistical query
(
\mathsf{QSQ}
) model
[
5
,
6
]

is a quantum analogue in which classical expectations are replaced by expectation values of quantum observables.

Formally, let
\mathcal{X}
be
\{0,1\}^{n}
and a concept class
\mathcal{C}:=\{f:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}\}
. The goal is to identify an unknown target concept
f\in\mathcal{C}
under a distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{X}
. In the classical
\mathsf{SQ}
framework, rather than examining a sequence of labeled samples
(x,f(x))
, the learner interacts with a
statistical oracle

\mathsf{Stat}
is defined as follows

{defn}
[Statistical Oracle] Let
\phi:\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}\to[-1,1]
be a query function and a tolerance parameter
\tau>0
. For a target concept
f\in\mathcal{C}
, the statistical oracle returns

\mathsf{Stat}(\phi,\tau)=\alpha\quad\text{such that}\quad\Big|\alpha-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[\phi(x,f(x))]\Big|\leq\tau.

With a slight abuse of notation, we denote by
\mathsf{Stat}(\tau)
a query to any map
\phi
satisfying
\phi:\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}\to[-1,1]
.

The quantum statistical query (
\mathsf{QSQ}
) model generalizes this framework to the quantum setting. Instead of classical labeled examples, information about the target concept is encoded in the
quantum example state

\ket{\psi_f}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sqrt{\mathcal{D}(x)}\ket{x,f(x)},

which is a superposition of labeled samples. This quantum example state is well-motivated in quantum computing and has many interesting complexity-theoretic applications
[
2
]
. Same as
\mathsf{SQ}
, a
\mathsf{QSQ}
learner interacts with the state only through a

quantum statistical oracle
.

{defn}

[Quantum Statistical Oracle]
Let
O\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n+1}\times 2^{n+1}}
be any observable with operator norm
\|O\|\leq 1
, and let
\tau>0
.
For a target concept
f\in\mathcal{C}
encoded in the state
\ket{\psi_{f}}
, the quantum statistical oracle returns

\mathsf{Qstat}(O,\tau)=\alpha\quad\text{such that}\quad\big|\alpha-\langle\psi_{f}|O|\psi_{f}\rangle\big|\leq\tau.

With a slight abuse of notation, we denote by
\mathsf{Qstat}(\tau)
a query to any observable
O
satisfying
\|O\|\leq 1
.

If
O
is diagonal in the computational basis,
\mathsf{Qstat}
reduces exactly to a
\mathsf{Stat}
query.
Hence, the
\mathsf{QSQ}
model strictly generalizes
\mathsf{SQ}
.
Any potential quantum advantage must therefore arise from the use of

non-diagonal observables
, which can access global interference or entanglement-induced
correlations unavailable to classical
\mathsf{SQ}
algorithms.
At the same time, the
\mathsf{QSQ}
model is that the learner itself remains a classical randomized algorithm. It never manipulates quantum states directly, but only receives approximate numerical values
\alpha
returned by the oracle.

In this paper, we focus on lower bounds on the query complexity of the
\mathsf{QSQ}
model, which is the number of oracle calls
\mathsf{Qstat}(O,\tau)
required to complete a learning task, and on how this complexity compares to that of the classical
\mathsf{SQ}
model on the same problem. Our main technical tool is the
quantum statistical dimension
(
\mathsf{QSD}
), introduced in
[
6
]
.
The standard approach reduces learning to an associated decision problem
(distinguishing an unknown state
\rho\in\mathcal{C}
from a fixed reference state

\sigma\notin\mathcal{C}
) and lower bounds the decision complexity via
\mathsf{QSD}
. Hence, bounding
\mathsf{QSD}
provides a direct measure of the complexity of learning in the
\mathsf{QSQ}
model. Formal reduction statements are deferred to Appendix
A
.

{defn}

[Quantum statistical dimension (
\mathsf{QSD}
)]
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a set of quantum states and let
\sigma\notin\mathcal{C}
.
For a distribution
\mu
over
\mathcal{C}
, define

\kappa_{\tau}\text{-frac}(\mu,\sigma)=\max_{\|O\|\leq 1}\mathbb{P}_{\rho\sim\mu}\!\left[\big|\Tr[O(\rho-\sigma)]\big|>\tau\right],\qquad\mathsf{QSD}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C},\sigma)=\sup_{\mu}\big(\kappa_{\tau}\text{-frac}(\mu,\sigma)\big)^{-1}.

Intuitively,
\mathsf{QSD}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C},\sigma)
measures how many distinct observables are required,
at tolerance
\tau
, to reliably separate typical states in
\mathcal{C}
from
\sigma
. A large
\mathsf{QSD}
implies that no single query can distinguish more than a small fraction of the class, forcing many oracle queries. The relation of
\mathsf{QSD}
and
\mathsf{QSQ}
is then presented in the following lemma.

{lmma}
[
\mathsf{QSD}
lower bounds
\mathsf{QSQ}
learning complexity]

Let
\varepsilon\geq\tau>0
and
\delta\in(0,1/2)
.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a concept class of states and let
\sigma\notin\mathcal{C}
be a reference state such that

\min_{\rho\in\mathcal{C}}d_{\mathrm{tr}}(\rho,\sigma)\;>\;2(\tau+\varepsilon).

Then the
\mathsf{QSQ}
learning complexity satisfies

\mathsf{QSQ}^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C})\;\geq\;(1-2\delta)\,\mathsf{QSD}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C},\sigma)\;-\;1.

where we denote
\mathsf{QSQ}^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C})
as the number of
\mathsf{Qstat}(\tau)
queries made by a
\mathsf{QSQ}
algorithm that on input
\rho
outputs
\pi\in\mathcal{C}
such that
d_{\text{tr}}(\pi,\rho)\leq\epsilon
with probability
\geq 1-\delta
.

This lemma shows that lower bounds on the quantum statistical dimension
\mathsf{QSD}
translate directly into lower bounds on the
\mathsf{QSQ}
learning complexity. However, directly computing
\mathsf{QSD}
is often difficult. Following
[
6
]
, we present a lower-bound technique that will be central in our later symmetry arguments.

{lmma}
[Variance bound
[
6
]
]

Let
\mu
be a distribution over
\mathcal{C}
and let
\sigma=\mathbb{E}_{\rho\sim\mu}[\rho]\notin\mathcal{C}
.
Define

\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(\mathcal{C}):=\sup_{\|O\|\leq 1}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\rho\sim\mu}[\Tr(O\rho)^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}_{\rho\sim\mu}\Tr(O\rho))^{2}\Big).

Then for every
\tau\in(0,1]
,

\mathsf{QSD}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C},\sigma)\;\geq\;\Omega\!\left(\frac{\tau^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(\mathcal{C})}\right).

This variance bound analyzes the fluctuations of measurement outcomes across an ensemble of states: if, for every observable
O
, the variance of
\Tr[O(\rho-\sigma)]
over
\rho\in\mathcal{C}
is small, then no single query can reliably distinguish members of
\mathcal{C}
from the reference state
\sigma
, forcing the learner to make many queries.

3
Main Results

In this section, we study quantum statistical query (
\mathsf{QSQ}
) learning under symmetry across three regimes.
In Section
3.1
, we show that for a permutation-invariant function class,
quantum learners achieve an exponential separation over classical
\mathsf{SQ}
learners by leveraging
quantum Fourier sampling to uncover hidden parity structures that induce exponentially small
classical correlations.
In Section
3.2
, we extend the analysis to general symmetry groups and establish
matching lower bounds for
\mathsf{QSQ}
and
\mathsf{SQ}
learning, showing that the orbit structure induced by
the group action constrains both models equally in most of the commonly studied symmetry groups, but the asymptotic advantage may arise under a highly skewed orbit distribution. Finally, in Section
3.3
, we examine the role of the tolerance parameter and
demonstrate that quantum learners can succeed at tolerance levels where all classical
\mathsf{SQ}

learners fail.

3.1
Learning permutation-invariant functions

We begin with the special case of permutation-invariant function classes, which
provide a clean setting for illustrating separations between classical
\mathsf{SQ}
and
quantum
\mathsf{QSQ}
learning.
While permutation-equivariant quantum neural networks have been shown to enjoy favorable
optimization and generalization properties
[
26
]
,
recent results demonstrate that such models with bounded observables are
classically simulatable
[
10
]
.
In contrast, we show that under the permutation-invariant function class, the
\mathsf{QSQ}
model can yield exponential advantages over classical
\mathsf{SQ}
learning.

To make this separation concrete, we consider a simple permutation-invariant
Boolean function class defined on graphs.
Let
A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
be the adjacency matrix of a directed graph sampled
uniformly at random.
Define the degree-count vector
c_{A}\in[n]^{n+1}
, where
[c_{A}]_{i}
records the
number of nodes with out-degree
i-1
.
For each subset
S\subseteq[n+1]
, define

g_{S}(A)\;=\;\sum_{i\in S}[c_{A}]_{i}\bmod 2.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{n}=\{g_{S}:S\subseteq[n+1]\}
.
Each function in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is Boolean-valued and invariant under
permutations of the graph vertices.
Moreover,
\mathcal{C}_{n}
can be represented exactly by two-hidden-layer graph
neural networks (GNNs)
[
17
, Lemma 15]
, a widely used
architecture for processing graph-structured data.
Consequently, our results imply that for function classes representable by
two-hidden-layer GNNs, there exist function families that are provably hard to
learn in any
\mathsf{SQ}
model, yet admit efficient learning algorithms in the
\mathsf{QSQ}

model.

Note that each function
g_{S}
is essentially a parity over selected entries of the
degree-count vector
c_{A}
. As a result, the class
\mathcal{C}_{n}
inherits the characteristic hardness of
parity functions in the statistical query model, leading to the following lower
bound.

{thrm}
[Classical Hardness, A variant of Theorem 3 in
[
17
]
] Let
\tau>0
. Any
\mathsf{SQ}
algorithm that learns
\mathcal{C}_{n}
up to classification error
\epsilon
sufficiently small (
\epsilon<1/4
) requires at least
\Omega\!\left(\tau^{2}\exp\!\big(n^{\Omega(1)}\big)\right)

\mathsf{Stat}(\tau)
queries.

Proof Sketch.

This
\mathsf{SQ}
lower bound follows the argument of
[
11
, Theorem C.4]
, which relies on the

pairwise independence
property of the function class.
Let
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution over graphs
A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
.
For any
g\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, define the corresponding labeled distribution

\mathcal{D}_{g}:=\{(A,\,y):A\sim\mathcal{D},\,y=g(A)\},

that is,
\mathcal{D}_{g}
represents samples drawn from
\mathcal{D}
whose labels are generated by
g
.
Let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{C}_{n})}
denote the mixture distribution obtained by first sampling
g\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{C}_{n})
and then setting
y=g(A)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{C}_{n}
forms a
(1-\eta)
–pairwise independent function family: with probability at least
(1-\eta)
over independently drawn graphs
A,A^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}
,
whenever
\hat{c}_{A}\neq\hat{c}_{A^{\prime}}
and
[\hat{c}_{A}]_{i}=[c_{A}]_{i}\bmod 2
,
the joint distribution
(g_{S}(A),g_{S}(A^{\prime}))
for a uniformly random
g_{S}\sim\mathcal{C}_{n}
equals

\mathrm{Unif}(\{0,1\}^{2})
.
Under this condition, any
\mathsf{SQ}
learner that distinguishes the target-labeled distribution

\mathcal{D}_{g^{*}}
(for an unknown
g^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
) from the random mixture

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{C}_{n})}
with classification error at most
1/4
using queries of tolerance
\tau

requires at least
\tfrac{\tau^{2}}{2\eta}
statistical queries.

Finally, using the concentration properties of Erdős–Rényi random graphs,
we can show that the constructed class
\mathcal{C}_{n}
satisfies

(1-\mathcal{O}(\exp(-n^{\Omega(1)})))
–pairwise independence.
A detailed derivation can be found in
[
17
, Appendix E.1]
.
∎

In contrast, we present a
\mathsf{QSQ}
algorithm that learns
\mathcal{C}_{n}
with sample complexity polynomial in
n
, yielding a sharp separation between the classical

\mathsf{SQ}
and quantum
\mathsf{QSQ}
learning models. At a high level, the algorithm proceeds in two stages: an efficient state preparation procedure that coherently computes

c_{A}\bmod 2
, followed by a quantum Fourier sampling routine. To begin, we observe that each function in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
can be expressed as

g_{S}(A)=\hat{S}\cdot\hat{c}_{A}\bmod 2,

where
[\hat{c}_{A}]_{i}=[c_{A}]_{i}\bmod 2
and
[\hat{S}]_{i}=1
if
i\in S
and
0
otherwise. Thus, learning a target function
g_{S}
reduces to identifying the hidden binary string
\hat{S}
, which can be efficiently recovered using quantum Fourier sampling
[
8
]
.

We first define
x_{A}=\text{vec}(A)
as the column-stacked vectorization of
A
. Since
A
is sampled uniformly from
\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
, access to a
\mathsf{Qstat}
oracle provides quantum examples of the form

\ket{g_S}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n^{2}}}}\sum_{A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}}\ket{x_A}\ket{g_S(A)}.

Therefore, in order to evaluate the function
g_{S}(A)
efficiently, it suffices to compute the corresponding input
\hat{c}_{A}
from the encoding
\ket{x_A}
. The following lemma shows that there exists an efficient quantum circuit that prepares
\hat{c}_{A}
given access to the state
\ket{g_S}
.

{lmma}

[State preparation] Let
\ket{g_{S}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}}\ket{x_A}\ket{g_{S}(A)}
, where
N=2^{n^{2}}
. There exists a
\text{poly}(n)
-gate quantum circuit
\mathsf{PREP}
such that

\mathsf{PREP}\ket{g_{S}}\ket{0^{\otimes n + n\lfloor\log(n)\rfloor+ 1}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}}\ket{x_A}\ket{\hat{c}_{A}}\ket{g_{S}(A)}.

Next, we show that using a quantum Fourier sampling circuit, we could estimate the Fourier mass of any function
f
on a subset of
\{0,1\}^{m}
using a single query of
\mathsf{Qstat}
.

{lmma}

Let
f:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}
and let
\ket{\psi_f}\;=\;2^{-n/2}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{m}}\ket{x}\ket{f(x)}
be the corresponding quantum example state, for any subset
T\subseteq\{0,1\}^{m}
, there is an observable
M_{T}
with
\|M_{T}\|\leq 1
such that
\Tr[M_{T}\outerproduct{\psi_f}{\psi_f}]\;=\;\sum_{S\in T}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}
.
Consequently, a single call to the
\mathsf{Qstat}(M_{T},\tau)
returns a
\tau
-estimate of
\sum_{S\in T}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}
.

With these lemmas at hand, we can have the guarantee of
\mathsf{QSQ}
algorithm as follows:

{thrm}
[Quantum Guarantee]
For any
\tau\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
, there exists a
\mathsf{QSQ}
algorithm that exactly learns
\mathcal{C}_{n}
using
\mathcal{O}(n)
queries to
\mathsf{Qstat}(\tau)
, together with an additional
\mathrm{poly}(n)
number of gates and ancilla qubits.

Proof Sketch.

First, we show that the relevant symmetry statistics of the input can be coherently computed.
Given quantum examples
\ket{g_S}
, Lemma
3.1
constructs a polynomial-size quantum circuit
\mathsf{PREP}
that computes the degree-count vector modulo
2
, mapping

\ket{g_S}\;\mapsto\;\ket{g'_S}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n^{2}}}}\sum_{A}\ket{x_A}\ket{\hat c_A}\ket{g_S(A)}.

This step preserves coherence and enables subsequent Fourier analysis on the hidden structure. Second, we exploit the fact that learning
g_{S}
reduces to identifying the unknown binary string
\hat{S}
. And under the Walsh–Hadamard Fourier expansion, the Fourier influence
\mathrm{Inf}_{i}(g_{S})
equals
1
if
i\in\mathrm{supp}(\hat{S})
and
0
otherwise. By Lemma
3.1
, for each coordinate
i
there exists an efficiently implementable observable whose expectation equals
\mathrm{Inf}_{i}(g_{S})
. Thus, a single
\mathsf{QSQ}
query per coordinate suffices to distinguish whether
i\in\mathrm{supp}(\hat{S})
, provided the tolerance satisfies
\tau<\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Combining the Fourier-sampling observable with the state-preparation circuit yields a family of observables
\{O_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n+1}
, each implementable in polynomial time.
Querying all
O_{i}
recovers
\hat{S}
exactly using
\mathcal{O}(n)

\mathsf{Qstat}
queries, completing the proof.
∎

We defer the full proofs to Appendix
B.1
for better readability.

3.2
Learning general symmetries

In the previous section, we established an exponential separation between quantum and classical models in learning permutation-invariant functions.
We next extend our analysis beyond permutation invariance to arbitrary symmetry groups. Specifically, we consider the problem of learning symmetric Boolean functions defined as follows:

{defn}
[Symmetric Boolean functions] Let input distribution
\mathcal{D}
be uniform over
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}^{n}
. For a given symmetry group
G
with
representation
\rho:G\to GL(\{0,1\}^{n})
, the class of symmetric Boolean functions is defined as:

\mathcal{C}_{\rho}=\{f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}:\forall g\in G,\forall x\in\mathcal{X}:f(\rho(g)\cdot x)=f(x)\}.

(1)

To establish the hardness of
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
, we consider the concept of the
orbit representation
of a group action. Given a symmetry group
G
acting on the input space
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}^{n}
via a representation
\rho:G\to\mathrm{GL}(\mathcal{X})
, the action partitions the domain into
orbits
under
G
, defined as

\mathcal{O}_{\rho}:=\left\{\mathcal{O}(x):=\{\rho(g)\cdot x\mid g\in G\}\,\middle|\,x\in\mathcal{X}\right\}.

Functions in the symmetric class
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
are necessarily
constant on orbits
. This structure directly induces correlations between function values: for inputs
x,x^{\prime}
belonging to the same orbit, their function values are identical across all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
; for inputs in distinct orbits, their joint distribution over
(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))
is uniform if
f\sim\text{Unif}(\mathcal{C}_{\rho})
. Consequently, the
pairwise correlation
between functions in
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
is tightly governed by the
distribution of orbit sizes
. Specifically, defining the orbit distribution
p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}(O)=|O|/|\mathcal{X}|
, we show that
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
forms a
(1-\|p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2})
-pairwise independent function family. This means that when the orbit distribution is nearly uniform (i.e.,
\|p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2}\ll 1
), the function class exhibits
low pairwise correlation
, which is a key indicator of learning hardness under classical statistical query models. In particular, we present this notion in the following lemma. For completeness, we include a proof in Appendix
B.2
.

{lmma}

Consider
\|p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}(|O_{k}|/|\mathcal{X}|)^{2}
, with
(1-\|p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2})
over the choice of
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, for every
f\in\text{Unif}(\mathcal{C}_{\rho})
the distribution of
(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))
is equal to uniform distribution over
\{0,1\}^{\otimes 2}
.

Based on Lemma
3.2
, Kiani
et. al.

[
17
]
established a lower bound
\Omega\!\left({\tau^{2}}/{\|p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2}}\right)
on the query complexity of learning under the classical
\mathsf{SQ}
model, showing that the hardness of learning
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
is governed by the orbit distribution induced by the group action. In particular, for the cyclic group the number of distinct orbits is
\Theta(2^{n}/n)
, yielding
\|p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2}=\Theta(n/2^{n})
and an exponential lower bound. We therefore ask whether the
\mathsf{QSQ}
model, by allowing non-diagonal observables, can exploit orbit structure beyond what is achievable with diagonal (classical) queries, or whether the same orbit-induced constraints fundamentally limit quantum learnability.

Diagonal Observables.

We first restrict to the set of diagonal observables
O
. Note that the
\mathsf{QSQ}
model with access only to diagonal observables reduces to the classical
\mathsf{SQ}
model. Our goal is to recover the classical hardness result of
[
17
]
within the
\mathsf{QSD}
lower bound.

To do so, we employ Lemma
2.2
. Given the
\mathsf{Qstat}
accessing quantum example state of

\ket{\psi_f}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{X}|}}\ket{x,f(x)},

for
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
, we have:

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{C}_{\rho})=\mathbb{E}_{f}[\Tr[O\ket{\psi_f}\bra{\psi_f}]^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}_{f}[\Tr[O\ket{\psi_f}\bra{\psi_f}]])^{2}

(2)

We consider diagonal observables
O
of the form
O=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\phi(x,f(x))\ket{x, f(x)}\bra{x, f(x)}
for
\phi:\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}\to[-1,1]
as
\|O\|\leq 1
. Thus, we have:

\Tr[O\ket{\psi_f}\bra{\psi_f}]=\frac{1}{{|\mathcal{X}|}}\sum_{x}\phi(x,f(x)):=\Phi_{f}

Then, we can write
\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{C}_{\rho})
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{C}_{\rho})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{f}[\Phi^{2}_{f}]-\mathbb{E}_{f}[\Phi_{f}]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{f^{\prime}}[\Phi_{f^{\prime}}]

(3)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{f,f^{\prime}}[\Phi_{f}\cdot\Phi_{f}-\Phi_{f}\cdot\Phi_{f^{\prime}}]

(4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}}\sum_{x,x^{\prime}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{f}[\phi(x,f(x))\cdot\phi(x^{\prime},f(x^{\prime}))]-\mathbb{E}_{f}[\phi(x,f(x))]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{f^{\prime}}[\phi(x^{\prime},f^{\prime}(x^{\prime}))]\right)

(5)

Using Lemma
3.2
, we have with

(1-\|p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2})
over the choice of
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, the distribution of
(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))
will act as
\text{Unif}(\{0,1\})^{\otimes 2}
. In such case,

\mathbb{E}_{f}[\phi(x,f(x))\cdot\phi(x^{\prime},f(x^{\prime}))]=\mathbb{E}_{y,y^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}}\phi(x,y)\cdot\phi(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}_{f}[\phi(x,f(x))]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{f^{\prime}}[\phi(x^{\prime},f^{\prime}(x^{\prime}))]

the second equality holds because
f,f^{\prime}
are drawn independently from
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
. Thus, with probability of
(1-\|p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2})
, this term vanishes. Therefore,

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{C}_{\rho})=\Theta(\|p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2})

(6)

From this analysis, we can obtain the
\mathsf{QSQ}
lower bound for the diagonal
O
as follows:

{thrm}

Let
\tau<0.096
and
O
be any diagonal observable with
\|O\|\leq 1
. for every
\mathsf{QSQ}
algorithm restricted to such observables that learn
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
to trace distance below
0.05
with probability of 0.99, the number of queries to
\mathsf{Qstat}(\tau)
is at least

\Omega\left(\frac{\tau^{2}}{\|p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2}}\right).

Proof.

We first convert the learning problem to a decision problem between
\rho_{f}=\ket{\psi_f}\bra{\psi_f}
where
f
is any state in
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
, and an adversarial state
\sigma=\mathbb{E}_{f\sim\mathcal{C}_{\rho}}[\ket{\psi_f}\bra{\psi_f}]
. Using Lemma
2.2
, we have

\mathsf{QSQ}^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C}_{\rho})\geq(1-2\delta)\mathsf{QSD}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C}_{\rho},\sigma)-1.

To apply this relationship, we need
\min_{f}d_{\text{tr}}(\rho_{f},\sigma)>2(\tau+\epsilon)
. We observe that:

d_{\text{tr}}(\rho_{f},\sigma)\geq 1-\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{f^{\prime}}[|\innerproduct{\psi_f}{\psi_{f'}}|^{2}]}\geq 1-\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}.

Fixing
\epsilon=0.05
and
\delta=0.01
, then the Lemma
2.2
holds if
\tau<0.096
. Along with Lemma
2.2
and (
6
), we complete the proof.
∎

This is unsurprisingly equal to the bound in the classical
\mathsf{SQ}
model
[
17
]
. However, the power of
\mathsf{QSQ}
models comes from their capability to use entanglement induced from non-diagonal observable
O
. Thus, we are rather interested in the general design of
O
, where we could utilize entanglement power.

General Observables.

In this case, we still use the variance bound method to lower bound
\mathsf{QSD}
, thereby
\mathsf{QSQ}
. The lower bound is presented in the following theorem

{thrm}

Let
\tau<0.096
. There exists a family of observables
O
such that
\|O\|\leq 1
and for every
\mathsf{QSQ}
algorithm restricted to observables in such family that learns
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
to the trace distance below
0.05
with probability of
0.99
, the number of queries to
\mathsf{Qstat}(\tau)
is at least

\Omega\left(\frac{\tau^{2}|\mathcal{X}|}{\max_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}|O_{k}|}\right).

Proof Sketch.

Full details of the proof can be found in Appendix
B.3
. Following the same overall strategy as Theorem
3.2
, we extend the argument to handle a general observable
O
. For an arbitrary observable
O
, we analyze
\mathrm{Var}_{f}(\Tr[O\ket{\psi_f}\bra{\psi_f}])
. Using the phase-kickback trick, we expand
\ket{\psi_f}\bra{\psi_f}
in a basis that separates the uniform component from the phase-encoded component and decompose
O
accordingly. This reduces the variance calculation to a sum of terms involving products of matrix elements over group orbits.

On the other hand, by exploiting the pairwise independence of
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
(Lemma
3.2
), most mixed terms vanish in expectation unless the corresponding inputs lie in the same orbit. As a result, all the related terms from different orbits cancel, and only terms
associated with orbit-wise structure remain. These surviving terms can be bounded using Cauchy–Schwarz and the operator-norm constraint on
O
, yielding

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{C}_{\rho})\;\leq\;\frac{\max_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}|O_{k}|}{|\mathcal{X}|},

where
O_{k}
ranges over the orbits of the symmetry group. Then, we design a family of
O
such that this inequality is tight, as such

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{C}_{\rho})=\Theta\left(\frac{\max_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}|O_{k}|}{|\mathcal{X}|}\right).

(7)

Finally, we apply Lemma
2.2
to complete the proof.
∎

Discussion.

Theorem
3.2
extends the
\mathsf{QSQ}
lower bound to arbitrary observables, while Theorem
3.2
recovers the classical
\mathsf{SQ}
hardness of
[
17
]
. A direct comparison yields

\|p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\frac{|O_{k}|^{2}}{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}\;\leq\;\frac{\max_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}|O_{k}|}{|\mathcal{X}|},

where we use
\sum_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}|O_{k}|=|\mathcal{X}|
. Combined with (
6
) and (
7
), this shows that allowing general
\mathsf{Qstat}
queries can increase the variance of statistical estimates relative to the classical
\mathsf{Stat}
model. Since variance controls the correlation strength accessible to statistical queries, this amplification improves statistical distinguishability. However, for commonly studied symmetry groups, the above inequality is tight up to constants, implying that the
\mathsf{QSQ}
lower bound cannot asymptotically exceed its classical
\mathsf{SQ}
counterpart. In contrast, when the orbit distribution is highly skewed, the gap between
\|p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2}
and
\max_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}|O_{k}|/|\mathcal{X}|
can grow, allowing non-diagonal observables to induce substantially larger variance. These are formally stated in the following remarks.

{remark}
[Matching lower bounds under orbit regularity]
For group actions satisfying

\|p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2}=\Theta(\max_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}|O_{k}|/|\mathcal{X}|)
,
including cyclic shifts and other orbit-regular symmetries, the
\mathsf{QSQ}
and classical
\mathsf{SQ}
query complexity lower bounds match up to constant factors.

{remark}
[Advantage under skewed orbit distributions]
If a group action has an orbit
O^{\star}
of size
|O^{\star}|=\sqrt{|\mathcal{X}|}
and all remaining orbits has the size of
1
, then

\|p_{\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\|_{2}^{2}=\Theta(1/|\mathcal{X}|),
and
\max_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}|O_{k}|/|\mathcal{X}|=\Theta(1/\sqrt{|\mathcal{X}|}),

so the
\mathsf{Stat}
yield variance
\Theta(1/|\mathcal{X}|)
, while the
\mathsf{Qstat}
can achieve variance
\Theta(1/\sqrt{|\mathcal{X}|})
.

However, it remains unclear to what extent such skewed orbit structures arise in practical learning problems. Identifying natural data distributions or symmetry groups that violate the orbit-regularity condition, and determining whether they can be exploited algorithmically, remains an open question.

3.3
Possible Advantages on Tolerance Parameter

While the variance bound provides a powerful tool for establishing general lower bounds on
\mathsf{QSQ}
learning, it does not capture potential advantages that may arise through the tolerance parameter. In this section, we identify a regime where the tolerance parameter
\tau
creates a strict separation between quantum and classical learnability. We show that for function classes with specific orbit structures, there exists a window of tolerance values where classical
\mathsf{SQ}
learners necessarily fail, yet
\mathsf{QSQ}
learners can efficiently identify the target function. We present the full proof in Appendix
B.4
.

{thrm}

[Tolerance-Based Quantum Advantage]
Let
\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
be a class of symmetric Boolean functions under group representation
\rho
. Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\rho}
be a subclass of functions such that every
f\in\mathcal{S}
is non-zero on a set of inputs
S_{f}\subset\mathcal{X}
with measure
\mu(S_{f})=\frac{|S_{f}|}{|\mathcal{X}|}=\zeta
. Assume that for any distinct
f,g\in\mathcal{S}
, their supports are disjoint (
S_{f}\cap S_{g}=\emptyset
).

If the orbit size fraction
\zeta
satisfies
\zeta<0.4
, then there exists a non-empty interval of tolerance parameters
\tau\in(\tau_{\mathsf{SQ}},\tau_{QSQ})
where:

1.

\mathsf{SQ}
Failure:
No classical
\mathsf{SQ}
algorithm can learn
\mathcal{S}
with tolerance
\tau
.

2.

\mathsf{QSQ}
Success:
There exists a
\mathsf{QSQ}
algorithm that learns
\mathcal{S}
with tolerance
\tau
using
O(\log|\mathcal{S}|)
queries.

Specifically, the separation holds for any
\tau
such that
2\zeta<\tau<\sqrt{2\zeta-\zeta^{2}}.

Proof Sketch.

For functions in
\mathcal{S}
, which differ from the zero function only on a fraction
\zeta
of the domain, every such expectation differs from that of the zero
function by at most
2\zeta
. Consequently, when the query tolerance
\tau
exceeds this scale, the
\mathsf{SQ}
oracle can respond consistently as if the target
were the zero function, rendering all functions in
\mathcal{S}
indistinguishable and learning impossible.

In contrast, the
\mathsf{QSQ}
model allows access to global quantum observables. The
quantum example states
\ket{\psi_f}
and
\ket{\psi_0}
corresponding to a
function
f
and the zero function have overlap

\innerproduct{\psi_0}{\psi_f}=1-\zeta
, which implies a trace distance

d_{\mathrm{tr}}(\ket{\psi_f},\ket{\psi_0})=\sqrt{1-|\langle\psi_{f}\mid\psi_{0}\rangle|^{2}}=\sqrt{2\zeta-\zeta^{2}}.

By the optimality of the Helstrom measurement, there exists an observable whose
expectation values on these two states differ by
\sqrt{2\zeta-\zeta^{2}}
.
Therefore, as long as the tolerance
\tau
is below this trace-distance scale,
a
\mathsf{QSQ}
learner can reliably distinguish
f
from the zero function.

Since the classical indistinguishability threshold scales as
2\zeta

while the quantum distinguishability threshold scales as
\sqrt{2\zeta-\zeta^{2}}
,
there exists a broad regime of
\tau
for which classical
\mathsf{SQ}
learning fails
but
\mathsf{QSQ}
learning succeeds, yielding the claimed separation.
∎

4
Conclusion and Open Problems

We studied the role of symmetry in quantum learning within the quantum statistical query (
\mathsf{QSQ}
) framework, characterizing when symmetry enables a quantum advantage and when it collapses to classical hardness. We exhibited an exponential separation between
\mathsf{QSQ}
and
\mathsf{SQ}
learners for permutation-invariant functions, proved matching query-complexity lower bounds for most commonly studied symmetries, and identified highly skewed orbit distributions as a source of asymptotic advantage. We also established a tolerance-based regime in which quantum learners succeed at noise levels that defeat classical
\mathsf{SQ}
algorithms. Together, these results delineate both the power and the limitations of quantum learning under symmetry constraints.

At the same time, our results highlight several important limitations. First, the
\mathsf{QSQ}
framework assumes access to prepared quantum examples, yet the efficient and scalable preparation of such states is itself a nontrivial task
[
23
,
25
]
. In practice, this preparation cost may offset theoretical advantages; for example, the spacetime volume of state-loading circuits is expected to scale exponentially with the system size
[
15
,
1
]
. Second, although our analysis identifies highly skewed orbit distributions as a condition for an asymptotic quantum advantage, it remains unclear whether such symmetry structures arise naturally in practical learning problems. Third, while the tolerance-based separation demonstrates a form of quantum advantage, realizing this advantage in practice typically requires implementing the Helstrom measurement, which is generally difficult to perform efficiently
[
14
]
. Finally, our analysis is carried out under the uniform input distribution, which provides a clean and tractable setting for establishing lower bounds. Extending these results to more general distributions remains an open problem, and it is unclear whether the same separation or collapse phenomena persist beyond the uniform case.

Appendix A
Extended background of Quantum Statistical Learning Framework

We first introduce the quantum statistical query
(\mathsf{QSQ})
learning model following the definitions and notations in
[
6
,
5
]
. Extended from the classical statistical query model (
\mathsf{SQ}
)
[
16
]
,
\mathsf{QSQ}
brings simple yet powerful tools to understand the advantage of quantum examples in machine learning
[
5
]
. In the classical
\mathsf{SQ}
framework, a concept class is given by functions
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\{c:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}\}
. The goal is to identify an unknown target concept
c^{\ast}\in\mathcal{C}
under an unknown distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{X}
. Rather than observing labeled samples
(x,c^{\ast}(x))
, the learner interacts with a
statistical query oracle

\mathsf{Stat}
. For a query function
\phi:\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}\to[-1,1]
and a tolerance parameter
\tau>0
, the oracle returns

\mathsf{Stat}(\phi,\tau)=\alpha\quad\text{such that}\quad\Big|\alpha-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[\phi(x,c^{\ast}(x))]\Big|\leq\tau.

The learner adaptively chooses a sequence of
m
queries
\{(\phi_{i},\tau_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
and, based on the responses, outputs a hypothesis
h:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
satisfying a classification error
\varepsilon
:
\mathbb{P}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[h(x)\neq c^{\ast}(x)]\leq\varepsilon
. Within the
\mathsf{SQ}
framework, the complexity is measured not by raw samples but by the number of queries
\mathsf{Stat}
. With a slight abuse of notation, we denote by
\mathsf{Stat}(\tau)
a query to any map
\phi
satisfying
\phi:\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}\to[-1,1]
.

The quantum statistical query (
\mathsf{QSQ}
) model generalizes this framework to the quantum setting. Instead of classical labeled examples, information about the target concept is encoded in the
quantum example state

\ket{\psi_{c^\ast}}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sqrt{\mathcal{D}(x)}\;\ket{x,c^\ast(x)}.

Same as classical
\mathsf{SQ}
models, a
\mathsf{QSQ}
learner does not directly manipulate these states, but through a
quantum statistical oracle

\mathsf{Qstat}
, defined as follows.

{defn}

[Quantum Statistical Oracle, restate Definition
2.2
]
Let
O\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n+1}\times 2^{n+1}}
be any observable with operator norm
\|O\|\leq 1
, and let
\tau>0
.
For a target concept
c^{\ast}\in\mathcal{C}
encoded in the state
\ket{\psi_{c^\ast}}
, the quantum statistical oracle returns

\mathsf{Qstat}(O,\tau)=\alpha\quad\text{such that}\quad\big|\alpha-\langle\psi_{c^{\ast}}|O|\psi_{c^{\ast}}\rangle\big|\leq\tau.

With a slight abuse of notation, we denote by
\mathsf{Qstat}(\tau)
a query to any observable
O
satisfying
\|O\|\leq 1
.

A crucial aspect of the
\mathsf{QSQ}
model is that the learner itself remains a classical randomized algorithm. It never manipulates quantum states directly, but only receives approximate numerical values
\alpha
returned by the oracle. In this sense, the
\mathsf{QSQ}
model is a precise mathematical analogue of the
\mathsf{SQ}
model, with expectations over labeled examples replaced by expectation values of observables:

\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(x,c^{\ast}(x))}[\phi(x,c^{\ast}(x))]}_{\text{$\mathsf{SQ}$ framework}}\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad\underbrace{\operatorname{Tr}(O\ket{\psi_c^\ast}\bra{\psi_c^\ast})}_{\text{$\mathsf{QSQ}$ framework}}.

The key distinction in the quantum setting comes from the design of the observable
O
. Note that, when
O
is diagonal with entries given by
\phi(x,c^{\ast}(x))
, the
\mathsf{QSQ}
oracle reduces to the classical
\mathsf{SQ}
oracle, since

\langle\psi_{c^{\ast}}|O|\psi_{c^{\ast}}\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[\phi(x,c^{\ast}(x))].
Hence, no quantum advantage is possible. The only potential source of quantum advantage
in the
\mathsf{QSQ}
framework comes from the ability to query with non-diagonal observables
O
that enable access to non-classical correlations. This allows the quantum learner to extract correlations accessible to any classical
\mathsf{SQ}
procedure, and therefore represents the precise point where quantum resources can outperform classical ones.

Throughout this paper, we focus on lower bounds on the query complexity of the
\mathsf{QSQ}
model, which is the number of oracle calls
\mathsf{Qstat}(O,\tau)
required to complete a learning task, and on how this complexity compares to that of the classical
\mathsf{SQ}
model on the same problem. Establishing such lower bounds, however, is highly non-trivial. Earlier approaches based on differential privacy and communication-complexity arguments yielded only linear lower bounds in the input size
n
, far weaker than the hardness expected for many fundamental learning problems
[
3
,
5
]
. To overcome this limitation, Arunachalam, Havlíček, and Schatzki introduced the
Quantum Statistical Dimension
(
\mathsf{QSD}
)
[
6
]
, which is a combinatorial parameter that extends the classical statistical dimension to the quantum setting and provides a unified framework for proving stronger
\mathsf{QSQ}
lower bounds. Leveraging
\mathsf{QSD}
alongside variance- and correlation-based arguments, they established tight bounds for several canonical tasks, including purity testing, shadow tomography, learning coset states, planted biclique states, and degree-2 function classes. In the following section, we formally define
\mathsf{QSD}
and describe two key techniques that enable us to derive lower bounds on
\mathsf{QSD}
, and consequently on the query complexity of the
\mathsf{QSQ}
model.

The standard methodology for proving lower bounds of
\mathsf{QSQ}
is to reduce a learning problem to an associated
decision problem
, and then to analyze the hardness of the decision problem using combinatorial parameters. Central among these parameters is the quantum statistical dimension (
\mathsf{QSD}
), which extends the classical notion of statistical dimension to the quantum setting. Intuitively,
\mathsf{QSD}
quantifies the number of observables that are needed to reliably distinguish states from a given concept class, and hence provides a direct measure of the complexity of learning in the
\mathsf{QSQ}
model. Beyond
\mathsf{QSD}
, two key techniques based on variance analysis and average correlation are employed to obtain sharper estimates and to capture the structural properties of the concept class under consideration.

Formally, let
\mathcal{C}
be a concept class of
n
-qubit quantum states,
\sigma
be an
n
-qubit state with
\sigma\notin\mathcal{C}
, and
\tau>0
a query tolerance, we consider a quantum statistical decision problem for
(\mathcal{C},\sigma)
defined as: for an unknown state
\rho
, given
\mathsf{Qstat}(\tau)
access to
\rho
decide if
\rho\in\mathcal{C}
or
\rho=\sigma
. Shown in
[
6
]
, the complexity of this decision task is captured by the
Quantum Statistical Dimension
(
\mathsf{QSD}
), which is formally defined as follows:

{defn}

[Quantum Statistical Dimension (
\mathsf{QSD}
), restate Definition
2.2
]
For a distribution
\mu
over
\mathcal{C}
and
\sigma\notin\mathcal{C}
be an
n
-qubit state, define the
maximum covered fraction

\kappa_{\tau}\text{-frac}(\mu,\sigma)\;=\;\max_{\|O\|\leq 1}\mathbb{P}_{\rho\sim\mu}\!\left[\,\big|\Tr[O(\rho-\sigma)]\big|>\tau\,\right],

where the maximization is over all observables
O
with operator norm
\|O\|\leq 1
.
The
quantum statistical dimension
is

\mathsf{QSD}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C},\sigma)\;:=\;\sup_{\mu}\,\bigl[\kappa_{\tau}\text{-frac}(\mu,\sigma)\bigr]^{-1}.

Intuitively,
\mathsf{QSD}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C},\sigma)
measures the smallest expected number of observables needed to reliably distinguish any
\rho\in\mathcal{C}
from
\sigma\notin\mathcal{C}
using tolerance
\tau
. A large
\mathsf{QSD}
implies that many observables are required, which translates into a high
\mathsf{QSQ}
complexity for learning.

The connection between
\mathsf{QSD}
and
\mathsf{QSQ}
learning is established in two steps. First, one shows that
learning a concept class is at least as hard as deciding membership in that class
. This means that if we can learn the class
\mathcal{C}
, then, in particular, we can solve the decision problem of

or equals some fixed state

{lmma}

[Learning is as hard as deciding

]
]

\mathsf{QSQ}^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C})\;\geq\;\mathsf{QQC}^{\delta}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C},\sigma)-1,

where
\mathsf{QQC}^{\delta}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C},\sigma)

\mathsf{Qstat}(\tau)
queries needed to decide whether

.

This lemma establishes a reduction: lower bounds for decision problems automatically imply lower bounds for
\mathsf{QSQ}
learning. The next step is to show that the complexity of such decision problems is itself controlled by the
\mathsf{QSD}

{lmma}
[
\mathsf{QSD}
lower bounds decision complexity
[
6
]
]
If an algorithm solves the decision problem of whether
\rho\in\mathcal{C}
or
\rho=\sigma\notin\mathcal{C}
with success probability at least
1-\delta
, then

\mathsf{QQC}^{\delta}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C},\sigma)\;\geq\;(1-2\delta)\,\mathsf{QSD}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C},\sigma).

Taken together, the lemmas show the complete chain of reductions:

\mathsf{QSD}\;\;\longrightarrow\;\;\text{Decision Complexity}\;\;\longrightarrow\;\;\text{$\mathsf{QSQ}$ Learning Complexity}.

Thus, by proving that
\mathsf{QSD}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C},\sigma)
is large for a given concept class, we immediately obtain a lower bound on the
\mathsf{QSQ}
complexity of learning that class. Combining these two lemmas, we get the proof for Lemma

.

While
\mathsf{QSD}
provides a powerful conceptual tool, it is often difficult to compute directly. To make the framework usable in practice, we need bounding techniques that relate
\mathsf{QSD}
to more tractable combinatorial parameters. Arunachalam
et al.

[
6
]
introduced two techniques, namely
variance bound
and
average correlation
, to understand this quantity. They are presented in the following lemmas.

{lmma}

[Variance bound
[
6
]
]
For a concept class
\mathcal{C}
and a distribution
\mu
over
\mathcal{C}
such that
\sigma=\mathbb{E}_{\rho\in\mu}[\rho]\notin\mathcal{C}
, define

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{C})\;=\;\sup_{\|O\|\leq 1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\rho\sim\mu}\!\left[\Tr[O\rho]^{2}\right]-\left(\mathbb{E}_{\rho\sim\mu}\Tr[O\rho]\right)^{2}\right).

Then, we have:

\mathsf{QSD}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C},\sigma)\;\geq\;\Omega\!\left(\frac{\tau^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{C})}\right).

{lmma}

[Average-correlation lower bound for
\mathsf{QSD}

[
6
]
]

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of
n
-qubit states and let
\sigma\notin\mathcal{C}
be full rank. For
\rho\in\mathcal{C}
define the

\sigma
-centered,
\sigma
-normalized deviation

\hat{\rho}\;:=\;\rho\,\sigma^{-1}-\mathbb{I}.

For any finite subfamily
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
, define its
average correlation
w.r.t.
\sigma
as

\gamma(\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\sigma)\;:=\;\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|^{2}}\sum_{\rho_{i},\rho_{j}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\bigl|\Tr\!\bigl[\hat{\rho}_{i}\,\hat{\rho}_{j}\,\sigma\bigr]\bigr|.

For a base family
\mathcal{C}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
and threshold
\tau>0
, set

\kappa^{\gamma}_{\tau}\text{-frac}(\mathcal{C}_{0},\sigma)\;:=\;\max_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{0}}\left\{\frac{|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|}{|\mathcal{C}_{0}|}\;:\;\gamma(\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\sigma)>\tau\right\},\qquad\mathsf{QAC}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C},\sigma)\;:=\;\sup_{\mathcal{C}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{C}}\bigl(\kappa^{\gamma}_{\tau}\text{-frac}(\mathcal{C}_{0},\sigma)\bigr)^{-1}.

Then for every
\tau\in(0,1]
, the quantum statistical dimension satisfies

\mathsf{QSD}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C},\sigma)\;\geq\;\mathsf{QAC}_{\tau^{2}}(\mathcal{C},\sigma).

The variance method analyzes the fluctuations of measurement outcomes across an ensemble of states: if, for every observable
O
, the variance of
\Tr[O(\rho-\sigma)]
over
\rho\in\mathcal{C}
is small, then no single query can reliably distinguish members of
\mathcal{C}
from the reference state
\sigma
, forcing the learner to make many queries. The average correlation method, by contrast, considers pairwise overlaps among the states in
\mathcal{C}
: if different
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
yield highly correlated responses to all observables, then even collective querying cannot separate them efficiently, and the
\mathsf{QSD}
must be large.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}\sum_{x,v\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\sum_{x^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in O_{k}}\ket{x, v}\bra{x', v'}

(29)

Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f}[\Tr[O_{2}\otimes O_{2}\cdot\rho^{f}_{2}\otimes\rho^{f}_{2}]]-(\Tr[O_{f}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{f}[\rho^{f}_{2}]])^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}\sum_{x,v\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\sum_{x^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in O_{k}}\bra{x}O_{2}\ket{x'}\cdot\bra{v}O_{2}\ket{v'}

(30)

(3)
\boldsymbol{i=j=3}
:

Same as the case
i=j=2
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f}[\Tr[O_{3}\otimes O_{3}\cdot\rho^{f}_{3}\otimes\rho^{f}_{3}]]-(\Tr[O_{f}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{f}[\rho^{f}_{3}]])^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}\sum_{x^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\sum_{x,v\in O_{k}}\bra{x}O_{3}\ket{x'}\cdot\bra{v}O_{3}\ket{v'}

(31)

(4)
\boldsymbol{i=2,j=3}
or
\boldsymbol{i=3,j=2}
:

As both
\mathbb{E}_{f}[\rho^{f}_{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{f}[\rho^{f}_{3}]=0
, we are only interested in
\mathbb{E}_{f}[\rho^{f}_{2}\otimes\rho^{f}_{3}]
or
\mathbb{E}_{f}[\rho^{f}_{3}\otimes\rho^{f}_{2}]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f}[\rho^{f}_{2}\otimes\rho^{f}_{3}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}\sum_{x,x^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{f}[(-1)^{f(x^{\prime})+f(v)}]\ket{x, v}\bra{x', v'}

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}\sum_{x,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\sum_{x^{\prime},v\in O_{k}}\ket{x, v}\bra{x', v'}

(33)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f}[\rho^{f}_{3}\otimes\rho^{f}_{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}\sum_{x,x^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{E}_{f}[(-1)^{f(x)+f(v^{\prime})}]\ket{x, v}\bra{x', v'}

(34)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}\sum_{x^{\prime},v\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\sum_{x,v^{\prime}\in O_{k}}\ket{x, v}\bra{x', v'}

(35)

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f}[\Tr[O_{2}\otimes O_{3}\cdot\rho^{f}_{2}\otimes\rho^{f}_{3}]]-(\Tr[O_{f}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{f}[\rho^{f}_{2}]])^{2}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}\sum_{x,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\sum_{x^{\prime},v\in O_{k}}\bra{x}O_{2}\ket{x'}\cdot\bra{v}O_{3}\ket{v'}

(36)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f}[\Tr[O_{3}\otimes O_{2}\cdot\rho^{f}_{3}\otimes\rho^{f}_{2}]]-(\Tr[O_{f}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{f}[\rho^{f}_{3}]])^{2}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}\sum_{x^{\prime},v\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\sum_{x,v^{\prime}\in O_{k}}\bra{x}O_{3}\ket{x'}\cdot\bra{v}O_{2}\ket{v'}

(37)

(5)
\boldsymbol{i=4,j=2,3}
:

We first consider

\mathbb{E}_{f}[\rho^{f}_{4}\otimes\rho^{f}_{2}]=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}\sum_{x,x^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{f}[(-1)^{f(x)+f(x^{\prime})+f(v^{\prime})}]\ket{x, v}\bra{x, v'}=0

(38)

The second equality holds because
\mathbb{E}_{f}[(-1)^{f(x)+f(x^{\prime})+f(v^{\prime})}]=0
based on the observation that, if there are at least 2 variables that belong to the same orbit, the expectation is reduced to
\mathbb{E}_{f}[(-1)^{f(x)}]
and equals
0
. Otherwise, all three variables belong to different orbits, using the same reasoning as Lemma
B.3
, the distribution of
(f(x),f(x^{\prime}),f(v^{\prime}))
will be uniform distribution over
\{0,1\}^{\otimes 3}
and
\mathbb{E}_{f}[(-1)^{f(x)+f(x^{\prime})+f(v^{\prime})}]=0
.

Similarly, we have:

\mathbb{E}_{f}[\rho^{f}_{4}\otimes\rho^{f}_{3}]=0

(39)

Putting all this together in (
18
), we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{C}_{\rho})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4|\mathcal{X}|}\sum_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\sum_{x,v\in O_{k}}\bra{x}O_{3}\ket{u}\cdot\bra{v}O_{3}\ket{u}+\bra{u}O_{2}\ket{x}\cdot\bra{u}O_{2}\ket{v}

(40)

\displaystyle\quad+\bra{x}O_{3}\ket{u}\cdot\bra{u}O_{2}\ket{v}+\bra{u}O_{2}\ket{x}\cdot\bra{v}O_{3}\ket{u}

(41)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4|\mathcal{X}|}\sum_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\left(\sum_{x\in O_{k}}\bra{x}O_{3}\ket{u}+\bra{u}O_{2}\ket{x}\right)^{2}

(42)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}\sum_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\left(\sum_{x\in O_{k}}\mathrm{Re}(\bra{u}O_{2}\ket{x})\right)^{2}

(43)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}\sum_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}\left(\sum_{x\in O_{k}}|\bra{u}O_{2}\ket{x}|\right)^{2}

(44)

Here, we apply some facts that
O_{3}=O^{\dagger}_{2}
and
\mathrm{Re}(z)\leq|z|
for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Next, applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to (
44
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{C}_{\rho})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}\sum_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}|O_{k}|\sum_{x\in O_{k}}|\bra{u}O_{2}\ket{x}|^{2}

(45)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\max_{k}{|O_{k}|}}{|\mathcal{X}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\bra{u}O_{2}\ket{x}|^{2}

(46)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\max_{k}{|O_{k}|}}{|\mathcal{X}|}\|O_{2}\ket{u}\|^{2}_{2}

(47)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\max_{k}{|O_{k}|}}{|\mathcal{X}|}\|O_{2}\|^{2}

(48)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\max_{k}{|O_{k}|}}{|\mathcal{X}|}

(49)

Now we tighten the inequality by choosing an explicit construction of
O_{2}
. Let
O^{\star}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}
be an orbit of maximum size,

|O^{\star}|=\max_{O_{k}\in\mathcal{O}_{\rho}}|O_{k}|.

Define the normalized uniform superposition over this orbit by

\ket{w}\;:=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{|O^{\star}|}}\sum_{x\in O^{\star}}\ket{x}.

Let
\ket{u}
denote the fixed reference basis state used in the block decomposition of the observable.
We define

O_{2}\;:=\;\ket{u}\!\bra{w},\qquad O_{3}\;:=\;O_{2}^{\dagger}=\ket{w}\!\bra{u}.

Then
\|O_{2}\|=1
, and for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\bra{u}O_{2}\ket{x}=\bra{w}x\rangle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|O^{\star}|}},&x\in O^{\star},\\[6.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Substituting this into the variance expression yields

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{C}_{\rho})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}\left(\sum_{x\in O^{\star}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|O^{\star}|}}\right)^{2}=\frac{|O^{\star}|}{|\mathcal{X}|},

Thus, the variance bound is tight and
\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{C}_{\rho})=\Theta(\max_{k}|O_{k}|/|\mathcal{X}|)
.

Finally, using Lemma
A
,
A
,
2.2
, we finish the proof.

B.4
Proof of Theorem
3.3

Our proof is based on the reduction to distinguishing two pure states. In this setting, a common figure of merit is the trace distance
d_{\text{tr}}(\cdot,\cdot)
, which is attained by the
Helstrom measurement
. Concretely, letting
X:=\ket{\psi_f}\!\bra{\psi_f}-\ket{\psi_g}\!\bra{\psi_g}
, the optimal observable is the sign of
X
, i.e., the difference of the projectors onto the positive and negative eigenspaces of
X
(Jordan–Hahn decomposition). Because our
\mathsf{QSQ}
oracle accepts arbitrary bounded observables, querying with this optimal observable realizes the information-theoretic limit for distinguishing
\ket{\psi_f}
from
\ket{\psi_g}
. We formalize this below.

{lmma}

Let
\ket{\psi_f}
and
\ket{\psi_g}
be two quantum states, there always exists an optimal measurement
O^{*}
such that:

|\Tr[O^{*}(\outerproduct{\psi_f}{\psi_f}-\outerproduct{\psi_g}{\psi_g})]|=2d_{\text{tr}}(\ket{\psi_f},\ket{\psi_g})

Proof.

The construction of
O^{*}
relies on the Jordan–Hahn decomposition, which we aim to find the projection to the positive and negative parts of
X=\ket{\psi_f}\bra{\psi_f}-\ket{\psi_g}\bra{\psi_g}
.

Here we define:

\ket{\phi}=\frac{\ket{\psi_f}+\ket{\psi_g}}{\sqrt{2+2\innerproduct{\psi_f}{\psi_g}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\ket{\phi^{\perp}}=\frac{\ket{\psi_f}-\ket{\psi_g}}{\sqrt{2-2\innerproduct{\psi_f}{\psi_g}}}

It is easy to check that
\innerproduct{\phi}{\phi^\perp}=0
. Denote
\ket{P}=\frac{\ket{\phi}+\ket{\phi^\perp}}{\sqrt{2}}
and
\ket{P^\perp}=\frac{\ket{\phi}-\ket{\phi^\perp}}{\sqrt{2}}
we have:

X\ket{P}=d_{\text{tr}}(\ket{\psi_f},\ket{\psi_g})\ket{P}\quad\text{and}\quad X\ket{P^\perp}=-d_{\text{tr}}(\ket{\psi_f},\ket{\psi_g})\ket{P\perp}

Thus, we observe that if
O^{*}:=\ket{P}\bra{P}-\ket{P^\perp}\bra{P^\perp}
, we have:

\Tr[O^{*}X]=2d_{\text{tr}}(\ket{\psi_f},\ket{\psi_g}).

We finish the proof.

∎

Thus, the proof for Theorem
3.3
proceeds by establishing an upper bound on the distinguishing power of any classical
\mathsf{SQ}
query and a lower bound on the distinguishing power of specific Helstrom measurements.

Classical
\mathsf{SQ}
Hardness.

Consider any two functions
f,g\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{0\}
(where
0
denotes the zero function). For a classical statistical query defined by a bounded function
\phi:\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}\to[-1,1]
, the difference in expected values is:

\Delta_{SQ}(f,g)=\left|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[\phi(x,f(x))]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[\phi(x,g(x))]\right|

(50)

Since
f
and
g
differ only on the union of their supports
S_{f}\cup S_{g}
, and are identical (both zero) elsewhere, the expectation difference is non-zero only on this region.

\Delta_{SQ}(f,g)=\left|\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}\sum_{x\in S_{f}\cup S_{g}}(\phi(x,f(x))-\phi(x,g(x)))\right|

(51)

The maximum possible value of the term
(\phi(x,y)-\phi(x,y^{\prime}))
is 2 (since
\phi\in[-1,1]
). Thus, we can bound the statistical distance by the measure of the support. Comparing any function
f\in\mathcal{S}
to the zero function
g=0
:

\Delta_{SQ}(f,0)\leq\frac{|S_{f}|}{|\mathcal{X}|}\cdot 2=2\zeta.

(52)

If the query tolerance
\tau>2\zeta
, the oracle can validly return the value corresponding to the zero function for every query, regardless of whether the true target is
f
or
0
. Specifically, the oracle can define a “null” response
\alpha_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\phi(x,0)]
. Since
|\alpha_{0}-\mathbb{E}_{x}[\phi(x,f(x))]|\leq 2\zeta<\tau
,
\alpha_{0}
is a valid response for any
f\in\mathcal{S}
. Consequently, the learner effectively observes responses consistent with the zero function for all targets, making learning impossible.

\mathsf{QSQ}
Learnability.

We now construct a quantum learner. The quantum example state for a function
f
is
|\psi_{f}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{X}|}}\sum_{x}|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle
.
To distinguish
f
from the zero function state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
, we compute their inner product. Since
f(x)\in\{0,1\}
, the states are orthogonal on inputs
x
where
f(x)=1
, and identical where
f(x)=0
:

\langle\psi_{f}|\psi_{0}\rangle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\langle f(x)|0\rangle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}\sum_{x\notin S_{f}}1=1-\zeta.

(53)

The trace distance
d_{tr}
, which governs the optimal distinguishability via Helstrom measurements, is given by:

d_{tr}(|\psi_{f}\rangle,|\psi_{0}\rangle)=\sqrt{1-|\langle\psi_{f}|\psi_{0}\rangle|^{2}}=\sqrt{1-(1-\zeta)^{2}}=\sqrt{2\zeta-\zeta^{2}}.

(54)

By Lemma
B.4
, there exists an observable
O^{*}
such that the gap in expectation values between
|\psi_{f}\rangle
and
|\psi_{0}\rangle
is exactly
2d_{tr}=2\sqrt{2\zeta-\zeta^{2}}
. However, to strictly separate
f
from
0
given a tolerance
\tau
, we require that the tolerance be smaller than the signal strength. In the
\mathsf{QSQ}
model, successful identification is possible if the trace distance exceeds
\tau
(allowing the oracle to return distinct ranges).
Thus, provided
\tau<\sqrt{2\zeta-\zeta^{2}}
, a
\mathsf{QSQ}
algorithm can distinguish
f
from
0
.

The Separation Window.

A quantum advantage exists if the condition for classical failure (
\tau>2\zeta
) and quantum success (
\tau<\sqrt{2\zeta-\zeta^{2}}
) can be simultaneously satisfied. This requires:

2\zeta<\sqrt{2\zeta-\zeta^{2}}

(55)

Squaring both sides (valid since
\zeta>0
):

4\zeta^{2}<2\zeta-\zeta^{2}\implies 5\zeta^{2}<2\zeta\implies\zeta<\frac{2}{5}=0.4.

(56)

Thus, for any orbit fraction
\zeta<0.4
, there exists a valid gap. For example, if we choose functions supported on orbits of size
\zeta=0.2
,
\mathsf{SQ}
fails for any
\tau>0.4
, while
\mathsf{QSQ}
succeeds up to
\tau\approx 0.6
. The quantum learner can proceed by a tournament method: for any candidate
f\in\mathcal{S}
, it applies the optimal observable to reject the hypothesis
f
or
0
. Since pairwise trace distances between disjoint support functions scale similarly, the class is efficiently learnable.
