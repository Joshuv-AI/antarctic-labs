 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Safety testing and postirradiation examination were conducted on two uranium carbide and uranium oxide
(UCO) tristructural-isotropic (TRISO) fuel compacts from Capsule 5 of the AGR-5/6/7 irradiation
experiment. Compact 5-5-3 was safety tested at 1600°C for approximately 300 h. Compact 5-6-3 was
examined in the as-irradiated condition using deconsolidation–leach–burn–leach, Irradiated Microsphere
Gamma Analyzer (IMGA) measurements, x-ray computed tomography, and scanning electron
microscopy with energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDS). Compact 5-5-3 exhibited strong
fission product retention during the 1600°C safety test. No 85Kr was detected above the measurement
limit, and cumulative 134Cs release was only 0.045 particle equivalents, providing no evidence of full
TRISO or SiC failure. Releases of 90Sr and 154Eu were also low. Although the total silver release was
small, the 110mAg release rate increased progressively after approximately 100 h at 1,600°C. This behavior
differs from most previous AGR 1600°C safety tests, in which the measured silver release was dominated
by 110mAg that had already passed through the SiC during irradiation and was released from the outer
pyrolytic carbon layer and compact matrix during heatup. Destructive postirradiation examination of as-
irradiated Compact 5-6-3 displayed fission product retention after irradiation. Minimal actinides were
recovered during deconsolidation, preburn leaching, and matrix postburn leaching. Approximately seven
particle equivalents of uranium and plutonium were recovered after particle postburn leaching. The
recovery of this kernel inventory in the particle postburn leach is most likely indicative of handling
damage rather than in-pile SiC failures. IMGA survey measurements of 3260 Compact 5-6-3 particles
identified no low-144Ce or low-137Cs population, and the 137Cs measured-to-calculated distribution was
centered near unity. Longer gamma counting of 45 particles produced a narrow 154Eu distribution centered
at a measured-to-calculated ratio of 0.94 with no low-europium particles. X-ray computed tomography
examination of three representative particles showed typical irradiation-induced buffer densification and
separation of the buffer from the inner pyrolytic carbon layer without coating failure. SEM/EDS
examination of two representative particles likewise showed intact structural coating layers and no
significant high-Z fission product redistribution into the coatings. Overall, the AGR-5/6/7 Capsule 5
results reported herein demonstrate strong fuel performance under relatively low-temperature, low-burnup
irradiation conditions and during subsequent heating to 1600°C.

 1. INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND

The US Department of Energy Advanced Gas Reactor Fuel Development and Qualification (AGR)
Program was established to develop, irradiate, and collect qualification basis data on high-performance
tristructural-isotropic (TRISO)–coated particle fuel for use in high-temperature gas-cooled reactors. The
AGR irradiation and postirradiation examination (PIE) campaigns have progressively evaluated fuel
performance over a broad range of irradiation temperatures, burnups, fast neutron fluences, and
postirradiation safety-test conditions. AGR-5/6/7 was the final irradiation experiment in the AGR fuel
qualification series and utilized production-scale UCO TRISO fuel fabricated to the AGR-5/6/7 fuel
specification (Marshall 2020; Pham et al. 2021; Demkowicz et al. 2026).

PIE of AGR-5/6/7 is being performed to establish particle failure fractions, quantify fission product
retention and redistribution, characterize irradiation-induced microstructural evolution, and evaluate fuel
performance during high-temperature safety testing. Postirradiation safety testing provides a direct
measure of TRISO fuel performance under temperatures representative of a postulated high-temperature
gas-cooled reactor depressurization conduction cooldown event. At Oak Ridge National Laboratory
(ORNL), safety testing is performed in the Core Conduction Cooldown Test Facility (CCCTF) located in
the Irradiated Fuels Examination Laboratory (IFEL). Irradiated fuel compacts are heated under flowing
helium, typically to temperatures between 1600°C and 1800°C for approximately 300 h. Gaseous fission
product release is continuously monitored using liquid-nitrogen-cooled traps for 85Kr, while condensable
fission products released from the compact are collected on deposition cups that are periodically
exchanged during the test. The combination of 85Kr and cesium release indicates the type of coating
failure: significant 85Kr release indicates loss of the hermetic TRISO coating layers, whereas cesium
release without corresponding krypton release can indicate isolated SiC failure (Hunn et al. 2014a).
Following completion of AGR-1 and AGR-2 testing, obsolete CCCTF control hardware and software
were replaced with a National Instruments LabVIEW-based control system that consolidated furnace
monitoring and control and incorporated automated routines for furnace evacuation and purging, helium
flow control, and operation of the liquid-nitrogen-cooled fission gas traps. These upgrades improved
system reliability and preserved the CCCTF capability for AGR-5/6/7 safety testing (Cureton et al. 2022).

Destructive PIE complements safety testing by determining the locations and inventories of actinides and
fission products remaining within the compact and individual particles and by evaluating irradiation-
induced changes in the TRISO coating layers. Deconsolidation–leach–burn–leach (DLBL) analysis is
used to quantify actinides and fission products accessible outside intact SiC and to identify particles with
compromised coatings. The Irradiated Microsphere Gamma Analyzer (IMGA) provides particle-by-
particle gamma spectrometry to identify particles with anomalous fission product inventories. Selected
particles can subsequently be examined by x-ray computed tomography (XCT) and electron microscopy
to evaluate coating integrity, irradiation-induced microstructural evolution, and fission product
redistribution. This combination of safety testing and destructive PIE has been applied throughout AGR-
1, AGR-2, and AGR-5/6/7 and allows engineering-scale release measurements to be related to individual
particle behavior and failure mechanisms (Demkowicz et al. 2015; Stempien et al. 2021; Cureton et al.
2025).

The AGR-5/6/7 fuel particles were fabricated by BWX Technologies using production-scale coating
equipment and contained UCO kernels with TRISO coatings similar in design to the AGR-2 UCO fuel
(Marshall 2020). AGR-5/6/7 was designed to span a broader irradiation-condition envelope than the
earlier AGR experiments. AGR-1 UCO compacts were irradiated to approximately 11%–20% fissions per
initial metal atom (FIMA) at time-average, volume-average (TAVA) temperatures of approximately
950°C–1140°C, while AGR-2 UCO compacts reached approximately 7%–13% FIMA at TAVA
temperatures of approximately 990°C–1300°C (Demkowicz et al. 2015; Stempien et al. 2021). AGR-
5/6/7 included fuel irradiated at substantially lower temperatures and under high-temperature margin
conditions.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS*

2.1 SAFETY TESTING METHODS

Safety testing in ORNL’s CCCTF (Figure 2-1) was accomplished using the same methods that were used
for safety testing of the first AGR program irradiation experiment (AGR-1) (Baldwin et al. 2012). A
compact was placed in a graphite holder that positions the compact in the furnace and simulates the
graphite that surrounds the compacts in a prismatic block reactor. A water-cooled deposition cup located
near the top of the tantalum-lined furnace chamber collected vaporized metallic elements that escaped
from the compact and the encompassing graphite holder.

 Figure 2-1. Schematic of the CCCTF safety-test furnace.

For the isothermal safety tests, deposition cups were periodically removed and replaced with new cups
using a maximum exchange interval of about 24 h. Shorter exchange intervals were used for the first few
cups removed during heatup to the isothermal test temperature to better track higher rate changes during
this period. Cups were monitored with gamma spectrometry for fission products with relevant gamma
signatures after removal from the furnace to track safety-test progress, with particular emphasis on the
collected cesium inventory, which would indicate compromise of the SiC layer because cesium is well
retained by an intact SiC layer but not by the dense pyrolytic carbon (PyC) layers (Hunn et al. 2014b).
The presence of 85Kr in the helium sweep gas was continuously monitored by gamma spectrometry using
a charcoal trap cooled with liquid nitrogen to collect the 85Kr in the exhaust gas. Significant 85Kr release
would indicate coincident compromise of all three hermetic TRISO layers—inner pyrolytic carbon
(IPyC), SiC, and outer pyrolytic carbon (OPyC). After each safety test was completed, additional analyses
were performed to measure actinides and fission products on the deposition cups and other CCCTF

furnace internals (graphite holder, tantalum furnace liner, and tantalum gas inlet line). These data were
used to determine the cumulative release from the compact of each detected nuclide.

Actinide and fission product measurements were converted to fractions of the total compact inventory, or
compact fraction, using the standard AGR PIE approach (Hunn et al. 2013a). This method used the total
compact inventory of each nuclide estimated from physics calculations (Sterbentz 2020), which provided
calculated values for the nuclide inventories at three specific times after the end of the irradiation, also
called the end of life (EOL): namely, 1 day after EOL, 1 year after EOL, and 2 years after EOL.
Radionuclide quantities measured by gamma spectrometry (e.g., 85Kr, 110mAg, 134Cs, 137Cs, 154Eu, and
155
 Eu) or chemical separation and beta spectrometry, in the special case of 90Sr, were decay-corrected to 1
day after EOL (July 23, 2020, at 07:10 UTC) and divided by the calculated total nuclide inventories at 1
day after EOL to determine the inventory fraction measured. For stable nuclides such as 104Pd and for
actinides (e.g., 235U, 236U, 238U, 239Pu, and 240Pu), quantities measured by mass spectrometry were divided
by the calculated total nuclide inventory at 1 year after EOL to determine the measured compact fraction.
Calculations showed that inventories of many stable nuclides and actinides in the fuel increased
significantly over the first year after the compacts were removed from the reactor because of generation
of these stable nuclides via decay chains from radionuclides present in the irradiated fuel at EOL. The
calculated inventories 1 year after EOL were used to determine the compact fractions because further
increase in the calculated inventories of these stable nuclides and actinides after 1 year was negligible
(Hunn et al. 2013a), and the mass spectrometry analysis of AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-6-3 was performed
more than 1 year after EOL. To facilitate assessment of fission product release relative to individual
particle failure, inventory values have been analyzed in terms of a particle equivalent value, which factors
in the estimated number of particles within the compact. For the compacts from Capsule 5, an average of
3392 particles was utilized for this calculation (Pham et al. 2021).

The efficiency of fission product collection on the deposition cups varied for different elements due to the
variability in transport of these elements out of the graphite holder and onto the water-cooled cups. The
cumulative fraction of each fission product measured on the deposition cups—relative to the total released
from the compact and measured on the cups, tantalum internals, and graphite holder at the end of each
safety test—provided a value for the average effective deposition cup collection efficiency for that fission
product. The compact release during each deposition cup residence period was estimated by multiplying
the amount of each fission product measured on the cup by the average effective collection efficiency for
that fission product, and this estimated compact release during each deposition cup residence period was
plotted to illustrate the time-dependent release from the compact. The accuracy of this method for
estimating time-dependent fission product release from the compact is usually good for fission products
like 110mAg, 134Cs, and 137Cs, whose transport times to the cups are relatively short compared with the
deposition cup residence period. Silver and cesium typically exhibit high deposition cup collection
efficiencies that do not vary significantly after the furnace reaches the safety-test temperature. Some
fission products (e.g., 90Sr, 154Eu, and 155Eu) are held up in the graphite holder and transport relatively
slowly to the deposition cups. For these fission products, the time-dependent plots of the fractional
releases during safety testing are often not representative of the time-dependent release from the
compact—especially during the early stages of the safety test, when an apparent increase in the release
rate may be dominated by the changing concentration profile in the graphite holder. However, because the
average effective deposition cup collection efficiencies are based on the cumulative release from the
compact to all of the internal furnace components where fission products primarily accumulate, the values
plotted for the final deposition cup accurately represent the total compact release fractions.

2.2 DECONSOLIDATION–LEACH–BURN–LEACH AND IRRADIATED MICROSPHERE
 GAMMA ANALYZER METHODS

Destructive examination was performed using the same methods that were developed for destructive PIE
of as-irradiated AGR-1 and AGR-2 compacts, and the comprehensive details of the equipment and
methods have been reported previously (Hunn et al. 2013a). Figure 2-2 is a flow diagram of the typical
process for compact DLBL integrated with a gamma survey of the particle inventory using the ORNL
IMGA. Deconsolidation-and-leach solutions were analyzed by gamma and mass spectrometry, providing
information about actinides and fission products that were present outside of the intact particles. Sources
of the dissolved actinides and fission products in deconsolidation-and-leach solutions include (a) nuclides
that diffused through intact SiC during irradiation or safety testing but were still retained within the
compact matrix, (b) nuclides related to uranium contamination present in the compact matrix and/or
OPyC at the time the compact was fabricated, (c) external contamination from sources present in the hot
cell, or (d) particles with compromised SiC (with or without coincident compromise of the other TRISO
coating layers) that exhibited abnormally low retention of actinides and fission products due to defective,
failed, or broken layers.

 Figure 2-2. Process flow for DLBL and IMGA.

as an acid-soluble oxide in the kernel and is typically well retained by normal SiC. Table 2-1 defines
terminology used herein for the different types of compromised SiC (with or without coincident
compromise of the other TRISO coating layers) that may be observed.

2.3 MATERIALOGRAPHY AND XCT METHODS

Select SPs and RS particles that had been gamma counted with IMGA were examined by XCT and/or
cross-sectioned for materialography. For materialography, particles were embedded in epoxy in small
aluminum holders and polished to near midplane with a Buehler MiniMet 1000 grinder/polisher using the
methods developed for AGR-1 PIE (Hunn et al. 2013a). Final polish was typically achieved with a
0.5 µm diamond film. Polished cross sections were imaged with a Leica DMI5000 optical microscope.

Particles designated for XCT were embedded in epoxy in a Lucite holder (Figure 2-3a), which was
removed from the hot cell and loaded into a tungsten-shielded enclosure (Figure 2-3b) designed for use in
an Xradia MicroXCT-400 (Figure 2-3c), which was custom designed by ORNL and Xradia for imaging
TRISO fuel particles. The Versa line of instruments based on the MicroXCT-400 is now sold
commercially by Zeiss, and a Versa 620 system was also used in this work. For 3D imaging, each particle
was rotated around an axis perpendicular to the x-ray beam, and radiographs were obtained at multiple
angles of rotation. Tomographic reconstruction was performed to determine the relative x-ray density of
each minimum resolvable volume element (voxel) in the cylindrical volume of rotation. From these data,
2D tomograms could be produced for any planar section. In addition to these tomograms, 3D
representations were produced using various software packages for 3D visualization of tomographic
datasets. Details of the XCT methods are available in previous reports (Hunn et al. 2013a).

2.4 SCANNING ELECTRON MICROSCOPY AND ENERGY-DISPERSIVE X-RAY
 SPECTROSCOPY METHODS

Analysis was performed with a Tescan Vega scanning electron microscope equipped with an Oxford
Ultim Max 65 silicon drift detector for EDS using the Oxford AZtec analysis software suite. This SEM
and imaging suite replaced the JEOL JSM-6390 SEM and reflects new capabilities and minor changes to
standard practices relative to SEM analysis at ORNL in the AGR-2 irradiation campaign. Further details
on the implementation of the new system are reported elsewhere (Cureton et al. 2025). The SEM was
located in a contamination control area in Room 120 of ORNL’s IFEL. The SEM analysis was performed
on the mounts previously imaged by optical microscopy. To facilitate analysis, the mounts were
transferred out of the IFEL hot cell to the charging area, where initial decontamination was performed to
reduce radiological contamination. The mounts were then transferred to a hood located in Room 120 for
final radiological decontamination and surface cleaning. The mounts underwent ultrasonic cleaning in
deionized water, were rinsed with isopropyl alcohol, and were wiped with a cotton swab. These cleaning
processes were repeated three times. Following cleaning, the samples were smeared and surveyed prior to
being released from the hood. After the samples were released, their surfaces were checked with an
optical microscope located in the contamination control area. If the sample surfaces were free of
significant debris, then they were secured onto an aluminum SEM stub using conductive carbon tape and
colloidal graphite to facilitate SEM analysis. SPs previously encased in nonconducting epoxy from XCT
analysis were coated with a thin layer of carbon (~5–10 nm) using a Cressington Series 108 sputter coater
to create a conductive surface to mitigate charging effects.

Two complementary SEM imaging modes—secondary electron (SE) imaging and backscattered electron
(BSE) imaging—were performed, as they provide different and complementary information on particle
cross sections. The SE signal was surface sensitive, which allowed for identification of surface features
and any irregularities. The SE imaging aided in differentiating surface debris from embedded fission
product features distributed throughout the TRISO layers. The SE micrographs were obtained using a
5 keV accelerating voltage with a probe current of 300 pA and a working distance of 5 mm. The BSE
signal was dependent on the atomic number (Z) of the elements near the surface of the sample. Because
the features appeared as bright spots, the high-Z actinides and fission products embedded in the relatively
low-Z TRISO layers could be readily identified. The BSE micrographs were obtained using a 20 kV
accelerating voltage with a probe current of 3 nA and a working distance of 10 mm.

Image montage pairs of the same areas were acquired using both imaging modes to ensure that surface
features (such as loose debris) were not misinterpreted as embedded features. A general image set was
taken for each particle cross section. The image set typically consisted of a montage of more than 150
micrographs—each with an 80 × 80 μm field of view—stitched together to form a high-resolution
panorama of the entire particle. Additional images of selected areas of interest were also acquired when
necessary. Two or more locations were chosen on each particle cross section for EDS analysis to gain an
understanding of the distribution of actinides and fission products in the TRISO layers after irradiation
and safety testing. Point-ID analysis was used to identify local compositions in high-Z features. The
Point-ID analysis was acquired under the same conditions as images acquired in the BSE mode, which
resulted in a sufficient count rate. The general acquisition parameters used for Point-ID analysis were to
collect spectra within an energy range of 0–20 keV with 10 eV/channel, 4,096 channels, a unitless process
time of 2, and a live time of 30 s. Data acquired by Point-ID analysis were processed using standardless
analysis, and significant contributions from the surrounding matrix were included in the data due to the
submicron size of the analyzed features; therefore, information on feature composition was not
quantitative. In addition, minor constituents present in the high-Z actinide and fission product features
may not be observed due to sensitivity limitations of the analysis. However, the Point-ID analysis
information on feature composition was useful for qualitative classification of the features, which
supported identification of general trends and comparison of the features observed in different locations in
the TRISO layers.

Independent measurements of as-irradiated AGR-5/6/7 fuel support this interpretation. Precision gamma

generally had 110mAg measured-to-calculated ratios of approximately 0.7–0.8, while only a very small

irradiation despite compact measured-to-calculated ratios below unity. Previously reported DLBL
examinations of as-irradiated Capsule 5 fuel also indicated minimal 110mAg transport through intact SiC
during irradiation. No 110mAg was detected in the DLBL solutions from Compacts 5-5-2 and 5-3-1, while
Compact 5-1-2 contained only 3.89 × 10-4 of its calculated 110mAg inventory, corresponding to
approximately 1.32 particle equivalents (Stempien and Cai 2025). Even this small measured inventory did

from transport through intact SiC. These results indicate that very little silver migrated through the SiC
during low-temperature Capsule 5 irradiation. Thus, Compact 5-5-3 entered the 1600°C test with a
relatively small inventory of readily releasable 110mAg outside the SiC layers.

While 110mAg release from intact particles is well documented during safety testing at 1800°C, these
results differ from most AGR-1 and AGR-2 compacts previously safety tested at 1600°C. AGR-1 Variant
3 provides a particularly useful comparison because its SiC microstructure is more similar to that of
AGR-5/6/7 fuel than that of the AGR-1 Baseline fuel. Quantitative image analysis gave an average SiC
grain area of approximately 0.188 µm2 for AGR-1 Variant 3 compared with approximately 0.172 µm2 for
AGR-5/6/7, indicating that the AGR-5/6/7 coating was slightly finer grained (Montoya et al. 2026).

AGR-1 Variant 3 Compact 4-1-2 provides a useful comparison because its SiC microstructure was similar
to that of AGR-5/6/7, with mean grain areas of approximately 0.188 and 0.172 µm², respectively
(Montoya et al. 2026). However, Compact 4-1-2 experienced a higher irradiation temperature (~1042°C
TAVA) than Compact 5-5-3 (773°C TAVA), resulting in greater 110mAg transport during irradiation and a
larger silver inventory in the OPyC and compact matrix at the start of safety testing. Most of the 110mAg
released from Compact 4-1-2 was collected early in the 1600°C test, after which the release rate
continued to decrease (Hunn et al. 2013b). In contrast, Compact 5-5-3 retained a larger fraction of its
silver within the particles during irradiation, providing a larger internal silver concentration when heated
to 1600°C; this greater driving force may explain the sustained increase in 110mAg release rate during the
latter portion of the test. Thus, despite their similar SiC grain structure, the different release kinetics could
reflect differences in irradiation history and the silver concentration available to drive transport rather
than SiC microstructure alone.

As mentioned, the low irradiation temperature of Compact 5-5-3 substantially reduced this normally
dominant contribution from silver already located outside the SiC. As a result, the contribution from
110m
 Ag transported through intact SiC during the isothermal hold became observable in the release-rate
measurements. This is most clearly illustrated by Figure 3-2, where the steadily increasing 110mAg release
rate after approximately 100 h contrasts with the decreasing release rates typically observed after the
initial silver inventory outside the SiC is depleted in other AGR safety tests. The total release remained
small, and differences in irradiation history and initial silver distribution prevent a direct quantitative
comparison of SiC silver permeability among these fuels. Nevertheless, these results provide evidence for
active 110mAg transport through intact AGR-5/6/7 SiC at 1600°C without evidence of SiC or full TRISO
failure.

Cesium exhibited a similar stage dependence. Only 0.14 particle equivalents of 134Cs and 0.16 particle

retain much of its cesium during irradiation (Stempien et al. 2023). Nevertheless, the combination of very

no indication of multiple full TRISO failures during irradiation. Most of the cumulative uranium and

SiC failures in pile or damage to particles after deconsolidation and preburn leaching.

measurements therefore provide the primary evidence that little silver had migrated through the SiC
during low-temperature Capsule 5 irradiation.

The low 90Sr and europium inventories outside the particles are also consistent with prior results. As-

also demonstrated increasing release of strontium and europium with irradiation temperature, with
inventories frequently retained in the OPyC and compact matrix after transport through intact SiC
(Demkowicz et al. 2015). The low inventories measured before particle burn leach for Compact 5-6-3 are
therefore reflective of its relatively low irradiation temperature.

only 0.46 particle equivalents, providing little indication that these fission-product inventories were
significantly affected by failed particles. Compared with Compact 5-6-3, the higher 90Sr and 154Eu

irradiation temperature and burnup. The much larger 110mAg inventory outside the particles in Compact 2-

measurement of Compact 5-6-3 prevents a useful quantitative comparison based on its 110mAg detection
limits.

These DLBL results are generally consistent with the Compact 5-5-3 safety-test results described in
Section 3. The low as-irradiated inventories of 90Sr and 154Eu outside the particles in Compact 5-6-3 are
consistent with the small releases of these nuclides observed during the 1,600°C safety test of Compact
5-5-3. The Compact 5-6-3 110mAg DLBL measurement itself is not sufficiently sensitive to constrain the
as-irradiated silver inventory; however, the PGS results and DLBL measurements of other low-
temperature Capsule 5 compacts indicate that little 110mAg was present outside the SiC at the end of
irradiation (Walker et al. 2025; Stempien and Cai 2025).

 5. IMGA SURVEY

The IMGA survey provided a particle-by-particle assessment of gamma-emitting fission product
inventories for 3,260 particles recovered from Compact 5-6-3. As described in Section 2.2, relative 144Ce
and 137Cs inventories are particularly useful for identifying particles with abnormal fission product
retention. Cesium is normally well retained by intact SiC; thus, a particle with a depleted cesium
inventory can indicate a compromised SiC layer. Cerium is more strongly retained within the particle, and
a depleted 144Ce inventory can indicate a particle with a leached kernel reflective of TRISO failure.

The normalized 144Ce activity distribution for Compact 5-6-3 is shown in Figure 5-1. The 3260 particles
displayed a distribution centered near the population average, with no distinct low-144Ce population. The
absence of particles with depleted 144Ce inventories indicates that no particles with full TRISO failures
remained in the population surveyed by IMGA. This result is consistent with the small uranium inventory
measured during the deconsolidation and preburn leaches discussed in Section 4.

A subset of 45 particles from Compact 5-6-3 was subsequently counted with a 2 h live time to improve

Particle counting began on March 27, 2025, approximately 1,708 days after the AGR-5/6/7 end of

quantification would therefore have required impractically long counting times. The lack of particle-level
110m

than evidence for either retention or release.

and September 2024 because of the long interval between irradiation and examination; 110mAg had
remained detectable in Compact 5-1-2 particles counted more than a year earlier (Stempien and Cai
2025). Thus, neither the ORNL Compact 5-6-3 particle measurements nor the later Idaho National

Laboratory Capsule 5 measurements provide a useful quantitative particle-level constraint on silver

 6. XCT ANALYSIS OF RANDOMLY SELECTED PARTICLES

XCT was performed on three RS particles from Compact 5-6-3. No particles with anomalous fission
product inventories were identified during the IMGA survey described in Section 5; therefore, XCT was
used to examine the 3D morphology of representative as-irradiated particles rather than particles selected
as potential failures.

Particle 563-RS08, shown in Figure 6-1, exhibited morphology typical of irradiated UCO TRISO fuel.
The buffer had densified relatively uniformly around the kernel and largely separated from the IPyC,
while retaining broad areas of residual attachment and smaller tendrils of attached buffer. These residual
attachments produced localized circumferential tearing within the densified buffer. Similar buffer
densification, separation from the IPyC, and residual attachment have been commonly observed in
irradiated AGR-1 and AGR-2 UCO particles (Demkowicz et al. 2015; Stempien et al. 2021). The
remaining structural coating layers were intact, and no evidence of particle failure was observed.

 500 μm

Figure 6-1. Tomographic cross section through the midplane of particle 563-RS08 displaying typical behavior
 of densified buffer and some residual attachment between the buffer and IPyC layers.

Particle 563-RS19, shown in Figure 6-2, exhibited similar buffer densification and localized tearing but
had a more pronounced geometric feature. A substantial facet in the coating layers was located adjacent to
a flat region of the kernel, resulting in locally thinner PyC layers on that side of the particle. Despite the
local distortion and reduced PyC thickness, the structural coating layers remained continuous, and no
evidence of layer cracking or particle failure was observed.

Particle 563-RS27, shown in Figure 6-3, also exhibited typical as-irradiated morphology, including
relatively uniform buffer densification and separation from the IPyC. No unusual structural features or
indications of coating failure were observed.

Overall, the XCT examination showed a consistent structural response among the three Compact 5-6-3
particles. Buffer densification and localized tearing associated with residual buffer/IPyC attachment were
evident, but the IPyC, SiC, and OPyC layers remained intact in all three particles. Particle 563-RS19
demonstrates that appreciable local coating faceting and PyC thinning could be accommodated without

observable damage to the SiC layer under the relatively low-temperature, low-burnup irradiation
conditions of Compact 5-6-3. Although only three particles were examined, the absence of structural
evidence for coating failure is consistent with the IMGA results in Section 5 and the low pre-IMGA
survey uranium and cesium inventories measured by DLBL in Section 4.

 7. SEM ANALYSIS OF RANDOMLY SELECTED PARTICLES

Two RS particles were selected from as-irradiated Compact 5-6-3 for SEM analysis based on the results
from gamma spectrometry. Table 7-1 lists the M/AA and M/C values for relevant fission product
inventories within the RS particles subjected to SEM analysis. Generally, particles had been selected with
bounding M/C values for silver because silver retention was shown to be sensitive to irradiation
temperatures (Gerczak et al. 2018; Stempien et al. 2021), and therefore extremes in 110mAg M/C reflect
bounding conditions within a compact. However, as discussed in Section 5, all particles from Compact
5-6-3 had silver inventories below the MDL due to radioactive decay. The particles selected displayed a
range in 154Eu M/C from 0.92 to 0.94, with particles 563-RS15 and 563-RS24 representing “Average Eu”
particles.

Overviews of 563-RS15 and 563-RS24 are shown in Figures 7-1 and 7-2, respectively, as examples of
average particles. These overviews highlight some of the commonly observed structural features such as
the crack around the circumference of the kernel/buffer boundary and the internal buffer tearing around
the circumference of the buffer/IPyC interface, which was seen in both particles analyzed. Figures 7-3
and 7-4 show enlarged SE images of the highlighted regions in both particle overviews. There were
consistent levels of porosity in the IPyC and at the IPyC/SiC interface in both AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-6-3
particles. At the IPyC/SiC boundary, there were PyC inclusions in the first few microns of the SiC layer.
Additionally, there were many low-Z inclusions or material pullout or porosity randomly distributed
throughout the SiC layer. The OPyC was fully bonded to the SiC layer for all AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-6-3
particles analyzed herein.

Figures 7-5 and 7-6 show enlarged BSE images of the highlighted regions in the particle overviews,
which show some of the commonly observed high-Z features. All layers of the AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-6-3
particles analyzed showed no significant high-Z material distribution. Throughout all layers, there are
only faint features that are primarily Al-Si with a U peak that is not significantly above the background,
as shown in the EDS spectra of Figure 7-7, suggesting these features were artifacts from polishing. The
mount used to cross-section particles is aluminum, and both uranium and silicon are primary constituents
within the particle; these elements were most likely redistributed during polishing. In the buffer layer of
particle 563-RS15, there were Al-Si-U features on one side of the particle, as shown in the overview
image; the corresponding EDS spectra are shown in Figure 7-8. There was also Na-Cl contamination
found throughout all layers of the particles, which is indicative of the epoxy material used for mount
preparation. Aluminum was found in the tear between the buffer and IPyC, and steel was found at the
IPyC/SiC boundary, as shown in Figure 7-9; the corresponding EDS spectra are shown in Figure 7-10.
These features are again associated with sample preparation, during which the aluminum was transferred
from the mount housing. All these features are the result of the metallographic preparation and are not
indicative of particle behavior during irradiation.

 8. CONCLUSIONS

Safety testing and destructive PIE of AGR-5/6/7 Capsule 5 fuel demonstrated strong TRISO fuel
performance under the relatively low-temperature and low-burnup irradiation conditions examined in this
report. Compact 5-5-3 was safety tested at 1600°C for approximately 300 h without evidence of SiC or
full TRISO failure. Krypton-85 remained below the detection limit, and cumulative 134Cs release was only
0.045 particle equivalents. Releases of 90Sr and 154Eu were also low and consistent with the limited
inventories transported outside the particles during low-temperature irradiation. Total release of 110mAg
was relatively small, but the release rate increased after approximately 100 h at 1600°C. DLBL
measurements indicate that Capsule 5 fuel contained minimal silver outside intact SiC at the end of
irradiation. The increasing release rate is therefore consistent with continued 110mAg transport through
intact SiC during the safety test. This behavior contrasts with many previous AGR-1 and AGR-2 1600°C
safety tests, where inventories of silver that accumulated outside the SiC during irradiation and were
released during heatup may have obscured a smaller through-SiC contribution.

Destructive examination of as-irradiated Compact 5-6-3 revealed minimal uranium or plutonium
accessible before particle post burn leach, whereas approximately six to seven particle equivalents of
kernel material were recovered from the particle postburn leach. The concentration of this inventory in the
final DLBL stage, together with the absence of a corresponding population of anomalous particles during
IMGA, indicates that the cumulative postburn inventory is most likely not indicative of in-pile particle
failure.

No low-144Ce or low-137Cs particles were identified among the 3260 particles surveyed by IMGA. Long-
count 154Eu measurements of 45 particles also showed a narrow distribution with no depleted population.
Quantitative particle-level 110mAg measurements were not possible because radioactive decay before
analysis resulted in inadequate measurement sensitivity. XCT and SEM/EDS examinations of
representative particles showed typical irradiation-induced buffer densification and buffer/IPyC
separation, and the structural coating layers remained intact. SEM/EDS showed no significant high-Z
fission product redistribution into the structural coatings, which is consistent with the relatively low
irradiation temperature and burnup of Compact 5-6-3.

The AGR-5/6/7 Capsule 5 fuel presented herein exhibited excellent fission product retention and no
evidence of widespread SiC or full TRISO failure during irradiation, and Compact 5-5-3 maintained these
retention barriers during 1600°C safety testing. The very low irradiation temperatures of Capsule 5 reduce
the sensitivity of conventional post-irradiation indicators of SiC failure because limited fission product
mobility can suppress the depleted-Cs signature normally used for particle identification. In this context,
the very low cesium release measured during the 1600°C safety test of Compact 5-5-3 provides especially
strong evidence for good SiC performance during irradiation. The safety test results also demonstrate that
110m
 Ag remains mobile through intact irradiated AGR-5/6/7 SiC at 1600°C. Continued comparison with
fuel irradiated at higher temperatures and burnups will help define the conditions controlling fission
product redistribution and release across the AGR-5/6/7 experiment.

 APPENDIX B. IMGA DATA
The tables in this appendix document the IMGA data for key gamma-emitting fission products. Values
are reported as measured activities in units of Bq that have been decay-corrected to 1 day after EOL and
as various unitless ratios, as described in Section 2.2. Summary values at the bottoms of the tables for
maximum, minimum, mean, and standard deviation indicate the degree of particle-to-particle variation in
these values. The M/A values—for example, Equation (2.1)—are indicative of particle-to-particle
variation in isotopic content. For particles with negligible radionuclide release, this particle-to-particle
variation may be related to variation in fissionable material and/or burnup. Fissionable material in an as-
fabricated particle may vary due to variation in kernel size (e.g., spherical kernels with different diameters
or fragmented kernels), variation in kernel uranium fraction, and/or variation in uranium enrichment.
Variation in local neutron fluence will cause a variation in burnup between spatially separated particles
even if the physical properties of the particles are identical. The M/AA values were adjusted using a
normally well-retained nuclide, usually 137Cs, to attempt to separate the variation in M/A as a function of
fission product retention from the variation as a function of fissionable material and/or burnup. This
makes the M/AA values—for example, Equation (2.5)—indicative of the fission product retention in a
particle relative to the average of all measured RS particles. Particles with M/AA near unity for a given
nuclide exhibited close to average retention of that nuclide. However, if a given nuclide was released
from a large fraction of particles, then an M/AA value of unity does not indicate good retention of that
nuclide. Such was often the case for 110mAg in particles from the AGR-1 and AGR-2 experiments
(Demkowicz et al. 2016; Hunn et al. 2018). The M/C values—for example, Equation (2.4)—indicate the
particle inventory relative to the average calculated inventory (Appendix C). For well-retained nuclides,
the mean M/C values are typically 1.00 ± 0.10, in which the variation is mostly due to error in the
calculated average inventories stemming from uncertainty in the values used for the calculation. For the
AGR-1 and AGR-2 irradiations, the mean M/C values for 125Sb and 154Eu were consistently offset from
unity by more than 10% due to larger errors in the calculated values.

All particles from Compact 5-6-3 are reported in the following tables as having a less-than value for
110m
 Ag. These less-than values are based on the MDL for 110mAg at the time the particles were counted. As
discussed in Section 5, this was due either to a low 110mAg compact/particle inventory after irradiation or
to the time elapsed between EOL and IMGA counting, which caused much of the short-lived radioisotope
to decay.
