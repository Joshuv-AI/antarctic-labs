Algebroid Desingularizable Poisson Structures
Shane Rankin

Abstract
. We introduce algebroid desingularizable Poisson manifolds , a class of Poisson manifolds induced by symplectic Lie algebroids with almost-injective anchors, generalizing structures including log-symplectic, b m b^{m} -symplectic, E E -symplectic manifolds, and hypersurface algebroids. We show that the dual of real, finite-dimensional, non-abelian, reductive Lie algebras never admit such algebroids. We finish by giving two infinite families of 2 2 -step nilpotent Lie algebras, one of which is desingularizable, and one of which is not.

1.
Introduction and Results

Generically symplectic Poisson structures arise naturally across mathematical physics. The dynamics of pseudo-Riemannian manifolds
[
8
]
, Painlevé equations
[
7
]
, and the restricted three-body problem
[
14
]
each produce Poisson structures that are necessarily degenerate: the dual 2-form to the Poisson bivector does not exist as a smooth section of the cotangent bundle. In favorable situations, however, one can construct a new vector bundle whose sections carry a Lie bracket analogous to the Lie bracket on vector fields, and on which this dual
2
-form exists as a smooth, closed, nondegenerate element. Such a bundle is a
symplectic Lie algebroid
.
Symplectic Lie algebroids were introduced by Nest and Tsygan
[
15
]
, who showed they admit Fedosov deformation quantization and canonically induce a Poisson structure on the base manifold.

Subsequent work has shown that many important families of Poisson manifolds arise this way: log-symplectic manifolds,
b^{m}
-Poisson manifolds, and
E
-manifolds each admit a symplectic Lie algebroid realizing their Poisson structure, see
[
13
,
6
,
5
]
. In the physics literature, these structures have been studied under the name quasi-symplectic manifolds in connection with deformation quantization and BRST theory
[
12
]
. More recently, deformation quantization of the underlying Poisson structure via Toeplitz operators on the inducing algebroid has been developed in
[
4
]
. In
[
1
]
, Bischoff and Witte study the case in which the anchor map of the algebroid is generically an isomorphism onto the tangent bundle, and extract substantial information about the underlying Poisson structure from the algebroid’s symplectic geometry.

The common thread running through all of these examples is that the anchor map is injective on an open dense subset of the base manifold. Such algebroids are called almost-injective, and we call a Poisson manifold
algebroid desingularizable
or just
desingularizable
if it is induced by a symplectic Lie algebroid with an almost-injective anchor. This notion unifies the examples above under a single definition and raises a natural question: which Poisson manifolds are desingularizable?
We investigate this question for linear Poisson structures, that is, for duals of finite-dimensional real Lie algebras equipped with the Kirillov-Kostant-Souriau Poisson structure. A necessary condition for desingularizability is that the Lie algebra is not nonabelian reductive; we prove the following:

Theorem
.

If
\mathfrak{g}
is a real, finite-dimensional, non-abelian, reductive Lie algebra, then
\mathfrak{g^{*}}
is non-desingularizable.

The proof proceeds by showing that the existence of a desingularizing algebroid forces the existence of a nonzero abelian ideal of the Lie algebra
T_{0}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, which is impossible when
\mathfrak{g}
is semisimple, and which persists to the reductive case by restricting to the semisimple part.
In dimensions three or less, non-abelian reductivity turns out to be the only obstruction:

Corollary
.

In dimension
\leq 3
,
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is desingularizable if and only if
\mathfrak{g}
is not semisimple.

We prove this by giving an explicit construction of a desingularizing algebroid for every non-semisimple real Lie algebra of dimension at most three, using the classification of such algebras together with a general criterion that reduces the problem to finding an appropriate decomposition of the Poisson bivector.
However, semisimplicity is not the complete picture in higher dimensions. We give two families of examples demonstrating this. First, for each
n\geq 1
, we show that the dual of the

(2n+1)
-dimensional Heisenberg Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}
is desingularizable. In contrast, we exhibit a
6
-dimensional
2
-step nilpotent Lie algebra whose dual is non-desingularizable despite not being semisimple:

Theorem
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the real Lie algebra with basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{6}
, with nonvanishing structure constants
C_{12}^{4}=C_{13}^{5}=C_{23}^{6}=1
. Then
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is non-desingularizable.

The proof is topological in flavor; any desingularizing algebroid would have to be a rank
2
trivial bundle over
\mathfrak{g}^{*}\cong\mathbb{R}^{6}
, and the image of the anchor, when restricted to an embedded
S^{2}
, would provide a global framing of
TS^{2}
, contradicting non-parallelizability. We then remark that taking the product of this example with any manifold with the zero Poisson structure produces another counter example. Combining these results, we conclude with the following open question:

Question
.

What conditions on a real, finite-dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
are necessary and sufficient to ensure that
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is desingularizable?

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the framework of desingularizable Poisson manifolds, including foundational examples and basic structural properties. Section 3 turns to the linear case: Section 3.1 contains the non-desingularizability theorems for reductive Lie algebras; Section 3.2 classifies the three-dimensional case; and Section 3.3 gives the Heisenberg positive example, followed by the six-dimensional counterexample.

2.1.
Background

Throughout this paper
M

Definition 1
.

A
Poisson algebroid
is a Lie algebroid
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot])
over
M
, with a choice of
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}\in\Gamma(\bigwedge^{2}\mathcal{A})
satisfying
[\pi_{\mathcal{A}},\pi_{\mathcal{A}}]=0
, where the bracket is the Nijenhuis-Schouten extension of the bracket on
\Gamma(\mathcal{A})
.

Often, we will simply write
(\mathcal{A},\pi_{\mathcal{A}})
when the anchor map and bracket are clear from context. In the case that
\mathcal{A}
is chosen to be the tangent bundle to
M
, this exactly recovers the standard notion of a Poisson bivector, i.e. an element
\pi\in\Gamma(\bigwedge^{2}TM)
such that

\pi(df,dg)=\{f,g\},\qquad\forall f,g\in C^{\infty}(M)

where
[\pi,\pi]=0
is equivalent to the Jacobi identity of
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
. In the case that
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}
is nondegenerate the map
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{\sharp}
is an isomorphism of cochain complexes. In this case of invertibility, there is a well-defined dual element to
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{\sharp}
, denoted
\omega_{\mathcal{A}}=\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}
that plays the role of a symplectic structure.

Definition 2
.

A
symplectic Lie algebroid
is a Lie algebroid
\mathcal{A}
, with a choice of closed, nondegenerate element
\omega_{\mathcal{A}}\in\Gamma(\bigwedge^{2}\mathcal{A}^{*})
.

(\mathcal{A},\pi_{\mathcal{A}})

\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\omega_{\mathcal{A}}\in\Gamma(\bigwedge^{2}\mathcal{A}^{*})
satisfies

Proof.

See

*Proposition 72.
∎

In light of this, we will call a Poisson algebroid

that any symplectic Lie algebroid

The question driving this article is understanding which Poisson manifolds arise this way if one insists that

2.2.
Desingularization Algebroids

Definition 4
.

A Lie algebroid
(\mathcal{A},\rho,[\cdot,\cdot])
over
M

if the anchor map
\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{A}\rightarrow TM
is injective on an open dense set of

\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Gamma(\mathcal{A})\rightarrow\Gamma(TM)

Proposition 5
.

If
(\mathcal{A},\rho_{\mathcal{A}},[\cdot,\cdot])
is almost-injective, then the maps on sections
\wedge^{k}\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Gamma(\bigwedge^{k}\mathcal{A})\rightarrow\Gamma(\bigwedge^{k}(TM))
are injective for all
k\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let
S\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{p\in M|\rho_{p}\text{ is injective}\}
, and fix
k\geq 0
, as well as
X\in\ker(\wedge^{k}\rho)
. If
p\in S
, then
(\wedge^{k}\rho)_{p}
is injective as well, as it’s the exterior power of an injective linear map between finite-dimensional vector spaces. The condition that

in conjunction with injectivity of
\wedge^{k}\rho
at points of

forces

to vanish on

as well. Since
X
is a smooth function vanishing on an open dense set, it must vanish globally, hence
\wedge^{k}\rho
is injective as a map of sections.
∎

Symplectic manifolds are the proto-typical example, where the algebroid can be taken to be the tangent bundle with the identity as the anchor map. In fact, we have the following:

Proposition 7
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\rho,\pi_{\mathcal{A}})
be a desingularizing algebroid for a Poisson manifold
(M,\pi)
, and suppose that
\pi
is invertible, i.e.

is symplectic. Then
\mathcal{A}\cong TM
as Lie algebroids.

Proof.

First, we claim that for any

\rho_{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{A}_{x}\rightarrow T_{x}M
is surjective. Toward this, fix

\pi
is invertible, the map
\pi_{x}^{\sharp}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T_{x}^{*}M\rightarrow T_{x}M
is a surjection, hence there is some
\alpha\in T_{x}^{*}M
such that
v=\pi_{x}^{\sharp}(\alpha)

\rho(\pi_{\mathcal{A}})=\pi
, we have that
v=\pi_{x}^{\sharp}(\alpha)=\rho_{x}((\pi_{A}^{\sharp})_{x}((\rho^{*})_{x}(\alpha)))

\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{A})\geq\mathrm{rank}(TM)

\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{A})=\mathrm{rank}(TM)
, and so
\rho_{x}
is a fiberwise isomorphism globally. Since
\rho
is a morphism of Lie algebroids, the result follows.
∎

Proposition 10
.

If
(M,\pi)

\mathrm{rank}(\pi_{x}^{\sharp})\leq\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{A})\qquad\forall x\in M,

\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{A})=\max_{L\in\mathcal{L}}\{\dim(L)\}

Let

3.
Linear Poisson Structures

Given a real finite-dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, one can associate a canonical Poisson structure to the dual
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, by defining the Poisson bracket for
f,g\in C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
as

\{f,g\}(\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\langle\xi,[d_{\xi}f,d_{\xi}g]\rangle

for any
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. This is commonly referred to as the Kirillov-Kostant-Souriau or KKS Poisson structure, and the symplectic leaves of the foliation agree with the coadjoint orbits. After choosing a basis of
\mathfrak{g}
, one obtains structure constants
C_{ij}^{k}
where
[e_{i},e_{j}]=C_{ij}^{k}e_{k}
which we can use to write the KKS Poisson bivector. If
x_{i}
are linear coordinates on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, then we have

\pi_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}=\sum_{i<j}C_{ij}^{k}x_{k}\partial_{x_{i}}\wedge\partial_{x_{j}}.

3.1.
Reductive Lie algebras

It was observed in
[
12
]
*Proposition 2.3 that
\mathfrak{so}(3)^{*}
does not admit a symplectic Lie algebroid inducing its Poisson structure, and a similar proof works for
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
based on the explicit geometry of the coadjoint orbits.

Definition 19
.

We say that a real finite-dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is
desingularizable
if
(\mathfrak{g^{*}},\pi_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}})

non-desingularizable

We will use the identification between
(T_{0}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\pi})

(\mathfrak{g},[\cdot,\cdot])

Proposition 20

There is an isomorphism of Lie algebras
(T_{0}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\pi})\cong(\mathfrak{g},[\cdot,\cdot])

Proof.

First, note that
T_{0}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is a Lie algebra with bracket inherited from the Poisson bracket

on

since
T_{0}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is the isotropy Lie algebra of
\pi_{0}^{\sharp}
. For any
x\in\mathfrak{g}
, let
\hat{x}\in C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
denote the map
\hat{x}(p)=\langle p,x\rangle
where the angle brackets denote the dual pairing between
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. Define a map

\displaystyle\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathfrak{g}

\displaystyle\rightarrow T_{0}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto(d\hat{x})_{0}.

This map is linear and injective, and hence an isomorphism of vector spaces. Note that since
\hat{x}
is linear, we have that for any
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
,
(d\hat{x})_{\xi}=(d\hat{x})_{0}
since the derivative is constant. Moreover, that constant is
x
, since
(d\hat{x})_{0}(v)=\langle v,x\rangle
, and so
x
and
(d\hat{x})_{0}
are identified under the isomorphism
(\mathfrak{g}^{*})^{*}\cong\mathfrak{g}
. Now fix
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
and note that we have that

\displaystyle\varphi([x,y])=d_{0}(\widehat{[x,y]})=d_{0}(\{\hat{x},\hat{y}\})=[d_{0}\hat{x},d_{0}\hat{y}]_{\pi}=[\varphi(x),\varphi(y)],

thus
\varphi
is an isomorphism of Lie algebras.
∎

Lemma 21
.

Suppose that
(\mathcal{A},\rho,\pi_{\mathcal{A}})
is a desingularizing algebroid for
(\mathfrak{g}^{*},\pi)
for
\mathfrak{g}
a finite dimensional real Lie algebra, and define
\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{\sharp}\circ\rho^{*}
. Then
\ker(\Phi_{0})
is an abelian Lie ideal of
(T^{*}_{0}\mathfrak{g}^{*},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\pi})
.

Proof.

At the origin we have the induced map between the isotropy Lie algebras of
\pi^{\sharp}_{0}
and
\rho_{0}
, namely the map

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T_{0}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\ker(\rho_{0})

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\mapsto(\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{\sharp}\circ\rho^{*})_{0}(\alpha).

First, note that this map is well-defined as given
v=\Phi_{0}(w)
, we have that
\rho_{0}(v)=\pi_{0}^{\sharp}(w)=0
, since
\pi_{0}^{\sharp}
is the zero map, and thus
v\in\ker(\rho_{0})
. We claim that this map is a morphism of Lie algebras. First, choose
\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}\in T_{0}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, and choose local extensions
\alpha,\beta\in T^{*}U
for
0\in U\subset\mathfrak{g}^{*}
with
U
open. Then, using that
\rho
is injective on sections, we have that

\displaystyle\rho(\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{\sharp}(\rho^{*}([\alpha,\beta])))

\displaystyle=\pi^{\sharp}([\alpha,\beta])

\displaystyle=[\pi^{\sharp}(\alpha),\pi^{\sharp}(\beta)]

\displaystyle=[\rho(\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{\sharp}(\rho^{*}(\alpha))),\rho(\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{\sharp}(\rho^{*}(\beta)))]

\displaystyle=\rho([\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{\sharp}(\rho^{*}(\alpha)),\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{\sharp}(\rho^{*}(\beta))]),

giving us the following equality on sections:

\Phi([\alpha,\beta])=(\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{\sharp}\circ\rho^{*})([\alpha,\beta])=[\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{\sharp}(\rho^{*}(\alpha)),\pi_{\mathcal{A}}^{\sharp}(\rho^{*}(\beta))]=[\Phi(\alpha),\Phi(\beta)].

Since both brackets are isotropy Lie algebra brackets, their value is independent of choice of local extension, and so by taking constant extensions we see that evaluating the above expression at zero on both sides gives us

\Phi_{0}([\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}])=\Phi_{0}([\alpha,\beta]_{0})=\Phi([\alpha,\beta])(0)=[\Phi(\alpha),\Phi(\beta)](0)=[\Phi_{0}(\alpha_{0}),\Phi_{0}(\beta_{0})],

thus
\Phi_{0}
is a morphism of Lie algebras, and hence
\ker(\Phi_{0})
is an ideal of
T_{0}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. Fix a global trivialization of
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
as well as one for
\mathcal{A}\cong\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
where
k=\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{A})
. Any
u_{0},v_{0}\in\ker(\Phi_{0})=\ker(\rho_{0}^{*})
, we may extend to globally constant sections
u,v
, i.e. for any
x\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}

u(x)=(u_{0},x)
and
v(x)=(v_{0},x)
, which are indeed smooth global sections of
T^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. Define
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
via

\displaystyle f(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\pi_{x}(u,v)=\langle x,[u,v]\rangle=(\pi_{\mathcal{A}})_{x}(\rho_{x}^{*}(u),\rho_{x}^{*}(v))

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the dual pairing between
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, and we identify
u,v
with the corresponding element of
\mathfrak{g}
. From the dual pairing perspective, we have that
f
is a linear map that vanishes at the origin. We can regard
\rho^{*}_{x}(u)
as a smooth map
\mathfrak{g}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by
x\mapsto\rho_{x}^{*}(u)
, and we have that
\rho^{*}_{0}(u)=\rho^{*}_{0}(u_{0})=0
. By Hadamard’s lemma we have that there must exist smooth functions
g_{i}^{u}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\rho^{*}_{x}(u)=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}x_{i}g_{i}^{u}(x)\quad\forall x\in\mathfrak{g^{*}}.

We analogously find smooth
g^{v}_{j}
such that
\rho^{*}_{x}(v)=\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}x_{j}g^{v}_{j}(x)
, and so we have that

f(x)=(\pi_{A})_{x}(\rho_{x}^{*}(u),\rho_{x}^{*}(v))=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}x_{i}x_{j}(\pi_{\mathcal{A}})_{x}(g_{i}^{u}(x),g_{j}^{v}(x))\quad\forall x\in\mathfrak{g}^{*},

however
f
is linear, and this shows that
f
vanishes to at least order 2 at the origin. As a consequence, we must have that
f
vanishes identically, i.e.

0=\langle x,[u,v]\rangle\quad\forall x\in\mathfrak{g}^{*},

and so
[u,v]=0
by nondegeneracy of the pairing. Since
u,v
were constant extensions,
[u_{0},v_{0}]=0
, hence
\ker(\Phi_{0})
is abelian.
∎

Theorem 22
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional real semisimple Lie algebra. Then
\mathfrak{g}
is non-desingularizable, that is,
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is not in the image of
\mathscr{D}_{ai}
.

Proof.

Suppose a desingularizing algebroid
\mathcal{A}
exists. Since
\mathfrak{g}
is semisimple, the maximal coadjoint orbit dimension is given by
\dim(\mathfrak{g})-\mathrm{rank}(\mathfrak{g})
, see
[
11
]
*Theorem 0.7, which by Proposition
10
is equal to
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{A})
. Using rank-nullity on the map
\Phi_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T^{*}_{0}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\rightarrow\ker(\rho_{0})
we see

\displaystyle\dim(\mathfrak{g})

\displaystyle=\dim(\ker(\Phi_{0}))+\dim(\im(\Phi_{0}))

\displaystyle\leq\dim(\ker(\Phi_{0}))+\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=\dim(\ker(\Phi_{0}))+(\dim(\mathfrak{g})-\mathrm{rank}(\mathfrak{g})),

thus
1\leq\mathrm{rank}(\mathfrak{g})\leq\dim(\ker(\Phi_{0}))
. By Lemma
21
, we must then have that
\ker(\Phi_{0})
is a nonzero abelian ideal of
T_{0}^{*}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, which by Proposition
20
, we can identify with
\mathfrak{g}
, which is impossible as
\mathfrak{g}
is semisimple.
∎

Theorem 23
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a real, finite-dimensional, non-abelian, reductive Lie algebra. Then
\mathfrak{g}
is non-desingularizable, that is,
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is not in the image of
\mathscr{D}_{ai}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathfrak{g}
is non-abelian, we may write
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{s}\oplus\mathfrak{z}
where
\mathfrak{s}
is semisimple, and
\mathfrak{z}
is abelian. Dualizing, we have
\mathfrak{g}^{*}=\mathfrak{s}^{*}\times\mathfrak{z}^{*}
with
\pi_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}=\pi_{\mathfrak{s}^{*}}+\pi_{\mathfrak{z}^{*}}=\pi_{\mathfrak{s}^{*}}+0
. The result then follows from using Proposition
16
alongside Theorem
22
.
∎

In contrast, if one drops the insistence of
\rho
being almost-injective, one can always find a symplectic Lie algebroid inducing a given linear Poisson structure, see
[
17
]
*Section 2.3.

3.2.
Low-Dimensional Lie algebras

In dimension
3
or less, we can exactly classify which Lie algebras are desingularizable:

Dimension 1:
The only one dimensional Lie algebra (up to isomorphism) is the abelian one, and by Proposition
9
it is desingularizable.

Dimension 2:
Up to isomorphism, there are two Lie algebras of dimension two. The abelian one is desingularizable, again by Proposition
9
. The other Lie algebra is also desingularizable, and is in fact
b
-symplectic. An explicit construction of a desingularizing algebroid is provided in
[

]
*Example 1.1.

Dimension 3:

We claim that all non-semisimple Lie algebras of dimension
3
are desingularizable, and we can prove this uniformly.

Lemma 25
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a non-abelian Lie algebra, and denote by
\pi
the KKS Poisson bivector on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. Then the set of points
\mu\in\mathfrak{g^{*}}
such that
\pi_{\mu}=0
is nowhere dense in
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.

Proof.

By definition,
\pi
vanishes at
\mu\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
if and only if for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
we have that
\langle\mu,[x,y]\rangle=0
, and so
\mu
must lie in
(\mathfrak{g}/[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}])^{*}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. Since
\mathfrak{g}
is non-abelian this subspace is a proper subspace, which is closed and has empty interior, thus nowhere dense.
∎

Theorem 26
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a real 3 dimensional Lie algebra. If
(\mathfrak{g}^{*},\pi)
admits two vector fields
V,W
such that
\pi=V\wedge W
and
\langle V,W\rangle_{C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})}
is involutive, then
\mathfrak{g}
is desingularizable.

Proof.

If
\pi\equiv 0
, then the zero Lie algebroid is trivially a desingularizing algebroid, so suppose that
\pi
is nontrivial, or equivalently, that
\mathfrak{g}
is non-abelian. We will build a desingularizing algebroid directly. Let
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
be coordinates on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, and write out
V=V_{1}\partial_{1}+V_{2}\partial_{2}+V_{3}\partial_{3}
,
W=W_{1}\partial_{1}+W_{2}\partial_{2}+W_{3}\partial_{3}
. Now consider the trivial
\mathbb{R}^{2}
bundle
\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, and define

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}

\displaystyle\rightarrow T\mathfrak{g}^{*}

\displaystyle(a,b,p)

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{1\leq i\leq 3}(aV_{i}(p)+bW_{i}(p))\partial_{i}|_{p}

Let
X,Y
be a global frame of
\mathcal{A}
for which
X(p)=(1,0,p)
and
Y(p)=(0,1,p)
, so we have that
\rho(X)=V
and
\rho(Y)=W
. We claim that
\rho
is almost-injective, or injective on sections. Toward this, suppose that
\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(\psi_{1}X+\psi_{2}Y)=\psi_{1}V+\psi_{2}W=0
, and consider the set
Q=\{p\in\mathfrak{g^{*}}|V_{p}\wedge W_{p}=0\}
. By Lemma
25
, this set is closed and nowhere dense. As such,
U\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathfrak{g}^{*}\setminus Q
is an open dense set on which
V\wedge W\neq 0
, and thus are linearly independent. We then have for any
p\in U
,
\psi_{1}(p)V_{p}+\psi_{2}(p)W_{p}=0
, which by linear independence forces
\psi_{i}(p)=0
for any such
p\in U
. Since the
\psi_{i}
are smooth and vanish on the open dense set
U
, they must identically vanish, and so
\rho
is almost-injective. For the bracket of
\mathcal{A}
, for
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{A}
, set

[s_{1},s_{2}]_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\rho_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}([\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(s_{1}),\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(s_{2})]_{T\mathfrak{g}^{*}}).

This is well-defined as
\rho_{\mathcal{A}}
is injective on sections and the involutivity condition on
V
and
W
ensures that
[\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(s_{1}),\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(s_{2})]\subset\im(\rho_{\mathcal{A}})
. By construction,
\rho_{\mathcal{A}}
is a Lie algebra morphism, and the Leibniz rule follows as for any
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
we have

\displaystyle[s_{1},fs_{2}]_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\rho_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}([\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(s_{1}),f\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(s_{2})])

\displaystyle=\rho_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(f[\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(s_{1}),\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(s_{2})]+(\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(s_{1})(f))\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(s_{2}))

\displaystyle=f\rho_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}([\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(s_{1}),\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(s_{2})])+(\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(s_{1})(f))\rho_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(s_{2}))

\displaystyle=f[s_{1},s_{2}]_{\mathcal{A}}+(\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(s_{1})(f))s_{2}.

The triple
(\mathcal{A},\rho_{\mathcal{A}},[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{A}})
is then a Lie algebroid over
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. This algebroid is in fact Poisson, with
\mathcal{A}
-Poisson bivector given by
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}=X\wedge Y
, which is closed for dimension reasons, and manifestly nondegenerate. We finally have that
\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(\pi_{\mathcal{A}})=\rho(X)\wedge\rho(Y)=V\wedge W=\pi
, so
\mathcal{A}
desingularizes
(\mathfrak{g}^{*},\pi)
.
∎

3.3.
Nilpotent Lie algebras

In dimensions 3 or less, semisimplicity of
\mathfrak{g}
is enough to detect whether or not
\mathfrak{g}
was desingularizable. We now give two families of examples of
2
-step nilpotent Lie algebras, one desingularizable, and one not, to indicate that this fails in higher dimensions. Isomorphism class
1
from Table
1
is
2
-step nilpotent, and admits a ready generalization to all dimensions. A familiar representative of this family is the Heisenberg Lie algebra, and we can construct a desingularizing algebroid for all finite-dimensional real Heisenberg Lie algebras.
Let
\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}
be the real Lie algebra generated by

\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}=\langle e_{1},...,e_{n},e_{n+1},....,e_{2n},e_{2n+1}\rangle

with non-vanishing structure constants given by

C_{k,n+k}^{2n+1}=1\qquad\forall 1\leq k\leq n.

Let
\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}^{*}
have dual linear coordinates given by
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2n+1}
, i.e.
x_{i}(e_{j})=\delta_{ij}
. The Poisson structure dual to the bracket is given by the bivector

\pi_{\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}x_{2n+1}\partial_{i}\wedge\partial_{n+i}

Define
\mathcal{A}_{n}
as the trivial
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
vector bundle over
\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}^{*}
with anchor map

\displaystyle\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{A}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}^{*}

\displaystyle\rightarrow T\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}^{*}

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{2n},x_{1},\dots,x_{2n+1})

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\lambda_{i}x_{2n+1}\partial_{i}+\sum_{i>n}\lambda_{i}\partial_{i}

and bracket given by

[s_{1},s_{2}]_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\rho^{-1}([\rho(s_{1}),\rho(s_{2})]_{T\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}^{*}})

Proposition 31
.

\mathcal{A}_{n}
is a desingularizing algebroid for
\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}^{*}
for all
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

We claim the map on sections induced by the anchor map is
C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{h}^{*}_{2n+1})
-linear. To see this, fix
s\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A}_{n})
and
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{h}^{*}_{2n+1})
. Then at a point
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{2n+1})\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}_{2n+1}
, write
s(y)=(s_{1}(y),\dots,s_{2n}(y))
, and we have

\displaystyle\rho(fs)(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}(f(y)s_{i}(y))y_{2n+1}\partial_{i}|_{y}+\sum_{i>n}(f(y)s_{i}(y))\partial_{i}|_{y}

\displaystyle=f(y)\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}s_{i}(y)y_{2n+1}\partial_{i}|_{y}+\sum_{i>n}s_{i}(y)\partial_{i}|_{y}\right)

\displaystyle=f(y)\rho(s)(y)

\displaystyle(f\rho(s))(y).

Moreover, we have that
\rho
is almost injective. To see this, again let
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{2n+1})\in\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}^{*}
, and suppose that

\rho(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{2n},y)=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\lambda_{i}y_{2n+1}\partial_{i}|_{y}+\sum_{i>n}\lambda_{i}\partial_{i}|_{y}=0.

Using the fact that the
\partial_{i}
form a basis of
T_{y}\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}^{*}
, we must have that
\lambda_{i}=0
for all
i
for any
y\in\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}^{*}
such that
y_{2n+1}\neq 0
. This is an open dense set of
\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}^{*}
, as it’s the complement of the hyperplane defined by
y_{2n+1}=0
. We now claim that
[\rho(\mathcal{A}_{n}),\rho(\mathcal{A}_{n})]\subset\rho(\mathcal{A}_{n})
, that is the image of the anchor map is an involutive distribution. The image is generated by elements of the form
\{x_{2n+1}\partial_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{\partial_{j}\}_{j=n+1}^{2n}
. It suffices to check that the bracket of any two generators lies in the image of
\rho
, since for any smooth functions
f,g\in C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}^{*})
we have

[fX,gY]=fg[X,Y]+(fX(g))Y-(gY(f))X.

If we take two generators from the first set, we have
1\leq i,j\leq n

\displaystyle[x_{2n+1}\partial_{i},x_{2n+1}\partial_{i}]

\displaystyle=x_{2n+1}^{2}[\partial_{i},\partial_{j}]+(x_{2n+1}\partial_{i}(x_{2n+1}))\partial_{j}-(x_{2n+1}\partial_{j}(x_{2n+1}))\partial_{i}

\displaystyle=x_{2n+1}^{2}(0)+(x_{2n+1}\delta_{i,2n+1})\partial_{j}-(x_{2n+1}\delta_{j,2n+1})\partial_{i}

\displaystyle=(x_{2n+1}\delta_{i,2n+1})\partial_{j}-(x_{2n+1}\delta_{j,2n+1})\partial_{i},

where
\delta_{s,t}=1
if
s=t
, and
0
otherwise. Since
i,j\neq 2n+1
for this set, the terms above indeed vanish. If two generators are from the second set, then the bracket vanishes automatically.
Finally, suppose that we have a generator from each set. Then we have

\displaystyle[x_{2n+1}\partial_{i},\partial_{j}]

\displaystyle=x_{2n+1}[\partial_{i},\partial_{j}]+x_{2n+1}\partial_{i}(1)\partial_{j}-\partial_{j}(x_{2n+1})\partial_{i}

\displaystyle=x_{2n+1}(0)+x_{2n+1}(0)\partial_{j}-\delta_{j,2n+1}\partial_{i}

\displaystyle=-\delta_{j,2n+1}\partial_{i},

and since
j\leq 2n
, or equivalently
\partial_{2n+1}
is not in the image of
\rho
, the claim follows. With this, it’s clear that
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{A}}
is well-defined, and that
\rho
and the bracket provide a Lie algebroid structure on
\mathcal{A}
. Explicitly, if we let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2n}
be the canonical generating sections of
\mathcal{A}
, that is

X_{i}(p)=(0,\dots,1,\dots,0,p),

where the
1
is in the
i
-th place, then we have that

\rho(X_{i})=\begin{cases}x_{2n+1}\partial_{i}\quad&\text{if }1\leq i\leq n\\
\partial_{i}&\text{if }i>n\end{cases}

and

[X_{i},X_{j}]_{\mathcal{A}_{n}}=0\quad\forall 1\leq i,j\leq 2n.

For arbitrary sections
s=\sum_{i}f_{i}X_{i}
and
t=\sum_{i}g_{i}X_{i}
, we have that the bracket is given by

[s,t]_{\mathcal{A}}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq 2n}\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}f_{i}x_{2n+1}\partial_{i}(g_{j})-g_{i}x_{2n+1}\partial_{i}(f_{j})+\sum_{i>n}f_{i}\partial_{i}(g_{j})-g_{i}\partial_{i}(f_{j})\right)X_{j}.

The only thing left to see is that this carries a nondegenerate Poisson structure inducing
\pi_{\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}}
. Consider the
\mathcal{A}
-bivector

\pi_{\mathcal{A}}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}\wedge X_{n+i}\in\Gamma(\bigwedge^{2}\mathcal{A}_{n}).

By construction,
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}
is nondegenerate as the
X_{i}
are globally linearly independent and non-vanishing, and we have that

\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(\pi_{\mathcal{A}})=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(X_{i})\wedge\rho_{\mathcal{A}}(X_{n+i})=\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}(x_{2n+1}\partial_{i})\wedge\partial_{n+i}=\pi_{\mathfrak{h}_{2n+1}^{*}}

so by Remark
13
,
\pi_{\mathcal{A}}
is Poisson.
∎

Now, consider the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, generated over
\mathbb{R}
by
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{6}\}
with nonzero structure constants

C_{12}^{4}=C_{13}^{5}=C_{23}^{6}=1,

which indeed define a 2-step nilpotent Lie algebra. Let
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
have linear coordinates

x_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=e_{i}^{*}\quad y_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=e_{3+i}^{*}\qquad 1\leq i\leq 3.

In these coordinates, the KKS bivector takes the form

\pi_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}=y_{1}\partial_{x_{1}}\wedge\partial_{x_{2}}+y_{2}\partial_{x_{1}}\wedge\partial_{x_{3}}+y_{3}\partial_{x_{2}}\wedge\partial_{x_{3}}.

Note that for
\xi=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, we have that

\mathrm{rank}((\pi_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}^{\sharp})_{\xi})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }y_{1}=y_{2}=y_{3}=0\\
2&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

We claim that this Poisson manifold is non-desingularizable.

Lemma 32
.

Let
S^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
F=\mathbb{R}^{3}\times S^{2}
be the trivial rank
3
bundle, and for any point
p=(x,y,z)\in S^{2}
, define

E_{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{v\in F_{p}|v\cdot(z,-y,x)=0\}.

Then the collection of the
E_{p}
define a smooth subbundle
E
of
F
and
E\cong TS^{2}
.

Proof.

Consider the matrix

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&-1&0\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SO}(3),

and define

\displaystyle\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}F

\displaystyle\longrightarrow F

\displaystyle(p,v)

\displaystyle\mapsto(p,Av).

After identifying
TS^{2}
with the set
\{(v,p)\in F|v\cdot p=0\}
, we claim that
\Phi
restricts to an isomorphism
TS^{2}\cong E
. Given
v\in T_{p}S^{2}
, with
p=(x,y,z)
, we have that

\Phi_{p}(v)\cdot(z,-y,x)=\Phi_{p}(v)\cdot Ap=(Av)\cdot(Ap)=v\cdot p=0,

thus
\Phi_{p}(v)\in E_{p}
. This map is injective as
A
is invertible, and hence by rank-nullity for all
p\in S^{2}
,
\Phi_{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T_{p}S^{2}\rightarrow E_{p}
is a linear isomorphism. Since
\Phi|_{TS^{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}TS^{2}\rightarrow E
is a smooth bundle map with fiberwise isomorphisms, it is an isomorphism of vector bundles.
∎

Theorem 33
.

\mathfrak{g^{*}}
as above is non-desingularizable.

Proof.

Suppose toward a contradiction that such a desingularizing algebroid
(\mathcal{A},\rho,\pi_{\mathcal{A}})
exists. By the above remark, and Proposition
10
, it must have rank
2
, and as it’s over
\mathfrak{g}^{*}\cong\mathbb{R}^{6}
it must be trivializable. Let
\{X,Y\}
be the canonical global frame for
\mathcal{A}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, i.e. at a point
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, we have that

X(\xi)=(1,0,\xi),\quad Y(\xi)=(0,1,\xi),

and let
V=\rho(X),W=\rho(Y)
. By assumption, we then have that

\pi_{\mathfrak{g}*}=V\wedge W.

Let
f_{i},g_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{\mathfrak{g}^{*}})
be such that

V=f_{i}\partial_{x_{i}},\quad W=g_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}.

We claim that such an expression is valid, i.e.
V
and
W
have no nonzero components in the
\partial_{y_{i}}
. Toward this, suppose
\xi\in U
, where
U
is the open dense set on which
\rho_{\xi}
is injective. From the proof of Proposition
10
, we have that at such a point
\im(\rho_{\xi})=\im(\pi_{\xi}^{\sharp})
, which from the expression of the bivector above we can see lies in
\mathrm{span}\{\partial_{x_{i}}|_{\xi}\}
for any
\xi
. Since this holds on an open dense set, any coefficient function on the
\partial_{y_{i}}
must vanish on this set, forcing them to globally vanish by continuity. Expanding
V\wedge W
in this basis and comparing to
\pi_{\mathfrak{g}^{*}}
we arrive at the system

\begin{cases}f_{1}g_{2}-f_{2}g_{1}=y_{1}\\
f_{1}g_{3}-f_{3}g_{1}=y_{2}\\
f_{2}g_{3}-f_{3}g_{2}=y_{3}\end{cases}.

Define smooth maps
F,G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathfrak{g}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
by

\displaystyle F=(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}),\quad G=(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}),

and note that using the cross-product in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
along with the above relations we have

(F\times G)(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})=(y_{3},-y_{2},y_{1}).

If we consider the set

M\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{(0,0,0,y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}|y_{1}^{2}+y^{2}_{2}+y_{3}^{2}=1\}\ \cong S^{2},

we have that

\|(F\times G)|_{M}\|=1,

thus for all points on
M\cong S^{2}
, we must have that
F
and
G
are linearly independent, and as such are non-vanishing. These provide a global framing for a trivial plane bundle
E
over
S^{2}
. Explicitly, if we let
F
denote the trivial rank
3
bundle over
M
, then this plane bundle is defined over
p=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\in M
via

E_{p}=\{v\in F_{p}|v\cdot(y_{3},-y_{2},y_{1})=0\}.

However, by Lemma
32
, this bundle is necessarily non-trivializable as it is isomorphic to
TM\cong TS^{2}
, a contradiction.
∎

By Proposition
16
, taking the product of this example with any manifold with the zero Poisson structure gives infinitely many non-examples.
