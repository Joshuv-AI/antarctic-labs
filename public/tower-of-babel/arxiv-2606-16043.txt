Bias-Reduced GEE via Adjusted Estimating Equations, with Odds-Ratio Extensions
Anestis Touloumis

Abstract
Generalized estimating equations (GEE) are widely used for correlated data, but with small to moderate numbers of independent clusters the ordinary GEE regression estimators can be substantially biased. We develop a first-order bias-reduction principle for GEE by viewing the estimator as a clustered-data M M -estimator and deriving an adjustment to the estimating equations that targets the leading bias term while accounting for the dependence of the working covariance on the mean parameters. The resulting class includes three bias-reduced estimators and three one-step bias-corrected analogs, nesting the bias-corrected estimator of 18 and the bias-reduced and bias-corrected estimators of 23 as special cases. The framework applies to general response types through correlation-coefficient parameterizations for the association structure and extends to correlated binary data through pairwise odds-ratio parameterizations, yielding the first bias-reduced and bias-corrected GEE estimators under this parameterization, for which the marginal-mean compatibility constraints are far less restrictive than those of correlation-coefficient parameterizations, making them better suited for small-sample settings. Under standard regularity conditions, all six estimators share the same asymptotic distribution as the ordinary GEE. Simulation studies show that the proposed estimators reduce bias while maintaining efficiency and coverage close to those of ordinary GEE across a range of settings, and a clinical trial analysis illustrates the proposed estimators in practice. Software is available in the R package geer .



Keywords:
bias reduction, generalized estimating equations, adjusted estimating equations, M-estimation, correlated data, odds-ratio parameterization

Introduction

Generalized estimating equations (GEE), introduced by
15
, are a standard approach for the analysis of longitudinal and other clustered data. In the GEE framework, the marginal mean is specified through a generalized linear model, while within-cluster dependence is represented through a low-dimensional working association structure. Under mild regularity conditions, GEE yields consistent and asymptotically normal estimators of the regression parameters with valid large-sample standard errors, even when the working association is misspecified. The finite-sample behavior of GEE estimators has received less systematic study, despite growing evidence that bias can be substantial when the number of clusters is small or moderate, which affects Wald-type inference
(
23
;
20
;
10
;
11
)
.

Finite-sample bias has long been recognized in likelihood-based inference, and a large literature exists on bias reduction and correction for maximum likelihood estimators, including
7
,
6
,
9
, and
5
. Extending these ideas to GEE is appealing but nontrivial because GEE does not arise from a genuine likelihood and the association structure may be misspecified.
23
adapted the approaches of
7
and
9
to obtain bias-corrected and bias-reduced GEE estimators, respectively.
18
noted that their construction relies on correct specification of the association structure and derived a one-step bias-corrected estimator that remains valid under misspecification. A further limitation, not previously noted, is that treating the working covariance matrices as fixed with respect to the marginal means introduces an additional problem even under correct specification of the association structure. In particular, the bias-reduced estimators of
23
can fail to achieve first-order bias reduction whenever the variance depends on the mean, as it does for binary and Poisson responses. Our naive bias-reduced and bias-corrected estimators subsume those of
23
as special cases and clarify precisely when first-order bias reduction holds. The one-step estimator of
18
arises as a special case of our robust construction. Because all existing bias-corrected estimators require a well-defined ordinary GEE estimator as a starting point, they can encounter numerical difficulties in challenging settings such as separation or near-separation in binary data
(
1
)
, where the ordinary GEE estimator may be numerically unstable or difficult to obtain.

For binary responses, a further limitation of existing work is that all bias-correction and bias-reduction methods have focused on correlation-coefficient parameterizations of the association. Feasible correlations are constrained by the marginal means
(
24
;
3
;
4
)
, and in small to moderate samples these constraints can cause correlation estimates to fall outside the admissible range or the working correlation matrix to become nonpositive definite, precisely in the settings where bias reduction is most needed. Pairwise odds-ratio parameterizations
(
16
;
2
)
avoid these constraints and offer a more stable alternative for modeling association in marginal binary regression, yet no bias-reduction or bias-correction methodology for GEE has been developed under this parameterization, a gap the present paper addresses.

We address these gaps by developing a unified first-order bias-reduction framework for GEE that subsumes and extends existing bias-correction and bias-reduction methods as special cases, embedding the GEE estimator in the clustered-data
M
-estimation framework and building on the robust bias-reduction methodology of
14
. We derive an explicit target adjustment to the GEE estimating equations that accounts for the dependence of the working covariance matrices on the mean parameters and, from this target, construct three implementable bias-reduced estimators and their one-step bias-corrected analogs. The robust variant replaces unknown covariance matrices with unbiased estimators and achieves first-order bias reduction regardless of whether the working association is correctly specified. Under correct specification, the naive variant uses the model-based working covariance and can offer efficiency gains relative to the robust variant, while the empirical variant replaces expectations with sample analogs and shares the robustness of the robust variant. Under standard regularity conditions, all six estimators are first-order equivalent in distribution to ordinary GEE. The one-step bias-corrected estimators provide a computationally convenient alternative that avoids iterating the adjusted estimating equations. Under correlation-coefficient parameterizations, the robust one-step estimator coincides with the estimator of
18
, and the naive estimators nest those of
23
as special cases in which the working covariance matrices are treated as fixed with respect to the mean parameters.

We further extend the framework to odds-ratio parameterizations for correlated binary data, yielding the first bias-reduced and bias-corrected GEE estimators under this parameterization. In challenging settings with correlated binary responses, the proposed estimators may encounter numerical difficulties, and the companion paper by
28
addresses this setting through a penalized GEE framework that provides existence guarantees. An open-source implementation is available in the
R
package
geer
.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
presents the theoretical developments, derives the ideal adjustment, and introduces the robust, naive, and empirical bias-reduced estimators together with their bias-corrected variants under both correlation-coefficient and odds-ratio parameterizations. Section
3
reports simulation studies for correlated binary and Poisson responses that compare competing estimators in terms of bias, efficiency, and coverage. Section
4
illustrates the methodology using a clinical trial application. Section
5
summarizes the findings and discusses directions for future research. Derivations, implementation details, and extended numerical results for the simulation study and the application are provided in the Supporting Information.

GEE Setup

Consider a longitudinal study with
N
independent subjects. For subject

i=1,\ldots,N

(
j=1,\ldots,n_{i}

\mu_{ij}=E(Y_{ij}\mid\mathbf{x}_{ij})

is a
p

n_{i}\times p

g(\mu_{ij})=\eta_{ij}=\mathbf{x}_{ij}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta},

where
g(\cdot)
is a known link function and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is the regression parameter vector.

Let
\operatorname{Var}(Y_{ij})=\phi h(\mu_{ij})
, where
h(\cdot)
is a known variance function and

\mathbf{A}_{i}
for the

diagonal matrix with

\mathbf{V}_{i}=\phi\,\mathbf{A}_{i}^{1/2}\mathbf{R}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\mathbf{A}_{i}^{1/2}
. Given
\phi
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
,
15
defined the GEE estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}
as
the solution of

\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{G}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{U}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{D}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}\mathbf{S}_{i}=\mathbf{0}_{p},

where

\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}
the

\partial\mu_{ij}/\partial\eta_{ij}

and

\sqrt{N}
-consistent estimators

Under standard regularity conditions,

, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{D}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}\mathbf{D}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}E(\mathbf{U}_{i}\mathbf{U}_{i}^{\top})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{D}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}\operatorname{cov}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{i}\right)\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}\mathbf{D}_{i}
. This is consistently estimated by the sandwich estimator

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{R}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}^{-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}^{-1},

(1)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1}
are obtained by replacing (
\boldsymbol{\beta}
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
,
\phi
) with (
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}
,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
,
\widehat{\phi}
) and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1}
additionally replaces
\operatorname{cov}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{i}\right)
by
(\mathbf{Y}_{i}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i})(\mathbf{Y}_{i}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i})^{\top}
. In small samples,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{R}
can underestimate sampling variability and inflate type I error. We therefore use the small-sample adjusted estimator of
21
,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{M}=\frac{n^{\star}-1}{n^{\star}-p}\frac{N}{N-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{R}+\lambda\xi\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}^{-1},

(2)

where
n^{\star}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}n_{i}
,
\lambda=\min\{0.5,p/(N-p)\}
, and
\xi=\max\{1,\operatorname{tr}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}^{-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{1})/p\}
. The estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{M}
remains positive definite whenever
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{0}
is positive definite.

For vector and matrix derivatives, we adopt the narrow definition of
19
. If
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{c})^{\top}
is a vector function of
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{d})^{\top}
, then
\partial\mathbf{a}/\partial\mathbf{b}^{\top}
denotes the
c\times d
matrix with
(i,j)
entry
\partial a_{i}/\partial b_{j}
. If
\mathbf{A}
is a matrix function of a vector
\mathbf{b}
, then
\mathcal{D}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{b})=\partial\,\operatorname{vec}\left({\mathbf{A}}\right)/\partial\mathbf{b}^{\top}
.

2.2
The Bias-Reducing Adjustment and Its Target

The derivations assume the
M
-estimation regularity conditions of
14
and the assumptions of Theorem 2 of
15
.

Treating
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\phi
as known,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}
is an
M
-estimator with bias expansion
E(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}-\boldsymbol{\beta})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G}}+O(N^{-3/2})
, where

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G}}=-\left(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{p}^{\top}\right)\left[\left(\mathbf{1}_{p}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\right)\circ\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G1}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{1}_{p}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{G}\right)\circ\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G2}}\right]\mathbf{1}_{p},

(3)

with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{D}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{D}_{i}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G1i}}\operatorname{cov}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{i}\right)\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}\mathbf{D}_{i},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G1i}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{n_{i}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{\star}+\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\star}\right)\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}+\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n_{i}}\right)\mathbf{K}_{n_{i}}\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\star},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G2}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{D}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{D}_{i}\right)^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G1i}}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G2i}}\right)\mathbf{D}_{i},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G2i}}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}\right)\mathbf{K}_{n_{i}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{\star}+\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\star}\right).

The definitions of
\mathbf{K}_{n_{i}}
,
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{\star}
and
\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\star}
, and a detailed derivation of (
3
) are given in Supporting Information, Web Appendix B. We treat
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G}}
as the ideal target and construct practical adjustments
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{BR}}
that approximate it. Following
14
, we define bias-reduced estimators as solutions of

\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{BR}}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{G}}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{BR}}=\mathbf{0}_{p},

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{BR}}
depends on
\boldsymbol{\beta}
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
,
\phi
and the data, and its derivatives with respect to
(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\phi)
are
O_{p}(1)
. When
condition

E(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{BR}})=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G}}+O(N^{-1/2})

(5)

holds,
E(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{BR}-\boldsymbol{\beta})=O(N^{-3/2})
and
N^{1/2}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{BR}-\boldsymbol{\beta})\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}_{p}\!\left(\mathbf{0}_{p},\lim_{N\to\infty}N\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{G}\right)
. A proof sketch of asymptotic normality is given in Web Appendix E of the Supporting Information.

The corresponding one-step bias-corrected estimator,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{BC}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\!\bigl(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}\bigr)\,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{BR}}\!\bigl(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}\bigr),

(6)

has bias of order
o(N^{-1})

(
14
)
and the same asymptotic distribution. Both
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{BR}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{BC}
may use
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{R}
or
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{M}
for inference, with
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}
replaced by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{BR}
or
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{BC}
as appropriate. When nuisance parameters are unknown, we follow
23
and
18
and replace
(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\phi)
by
\sqrt{N}
-consistent estimators
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\widehat{\phi})
, either fixed at the ordinary GEE values or updated jointly with

2.3
Three Approximations to the Ideal Adjustment

The ideal target
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G}}
in (
3
) depends on the unknown covariance matrices
\{\operatorname{cov}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{i}\right):i=1,\ldots,N\}
. We consider three implementable adjustment vectors satisfying (
5
): a robust adjustment based on unbiased covariance estimates, a naive adjustment that requires correct specification of the association structure, and an empirical adjustment based on sample analogs throughout.

Robust adjustment.
Replacing each
\operatorname{cov}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{i}\right)
by the unbiased analog
\mathbf{S}_{i}\mathbf{S}_{i}^{\top}
gives

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{R}}=-\left(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{p}^{\top}\right)\left\{\left[\left(\mathbf{1}_{p}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\right)\circ\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{R1}}\right]+\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\mathbf{1}_{p}\otimes\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{e}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\right)\right]\circ\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G2}}\right\}\mathbf{1}_{p},

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{R1}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbf{D}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{D}_{i})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G1i}}\,\mathbf{S}_{i}\mathbf{S}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}\mathbf{D}_{i}
and
\mathbf{e}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{U}_{i}\mathbf{U}_{i}^{\top}
is the empirical analog of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}
. Because
E(\mathbf{S}_{i}\mathbf{S}_{i}^{\top})=\operatorname{cov}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{i}\right)
, we have
E(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{R}})=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G}}
exactly, so (
5
) holds. The robust bias-reduced (RBR) estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{RBR}
solves

\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{R}}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{G}}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{R}}=\mathbf{0}_{p}

and does not require correct specification of the working association structure.

Naive adjustment.
Assuming
\mathbf{V}_{i}=\operatorname{cov}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{i}\right)
for all
i
, the target

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G}}
simplifies to

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{N}}=-\left(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{p}^{\top}\right)\left[\left(\mathbf{1}_{p}\otimes\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\right)\circ\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{N1}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{N2}}\right)\right]\mathbf{1}_{p},

(8)

where

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{N1}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{D}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{D}_{i}\right)^{\top}\left[\mathbf{K}_{n_{i}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{\star}\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}-\left(\mathbf{I}_{n_{i}}\otimes\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}\right)\mathbf{K}_{n_{i}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{\star}-\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n_{i}}\right)\mathbf{K}_{n_{i}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{\star}\right]\mathbf{D}_{i}

arises from derivatives of the mean component, and

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{N2}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{D}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{D}_{i}\right)^{\top}\left[\mathbf{K}_{n_{i}}\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\star}\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}-2\left(\mathbf{I}_{n_{i}}\otimes\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}\right)\mathbf{K}_{n_{i}}\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\star}+\left(\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n_{i}}\right)\mathbf{K}_{n_{i}}\mathbf{A}_{i}^{\star}\right]\mathbf{D}_{i}

captures the dependence of the variance function
h(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i})
on
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. The naive bias-reduced (NBR) estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{NBR}
solves
\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{N}}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{G}}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{N}}=\mathbf{0}_{p}
. Under misspecification of the association structure, first-order bias reduction holds only when

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G}}-\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{N}}=O\left({N^{-1/2}}\right).

(9)

Supporting Information, Web Appendix D provides an example in which condition (
9
) holds under misspecification. This implies that NBR can achieve bias reduction despite incorrect working association specification, a pattern confirmed by the simulation study in Section
3
.

Setting
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{N2}}=\mathbf{0}_{p}
recovers the estimator of
23
, which treats
\mathbf{V}_{i}
as fixed with respect to
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and does not in general deliver first-order bias reduction for mean-dependent variance functions, regardless of whether the working association is correctly specified. Under working independence,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{NBR}
reduces to the bias-reduced GLM estimator of
9
.

Empirical adjustment.
Let
\mathbf{j}=\mathcal{D}\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{G}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
,
\mathbf{d}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{vec}\!\left(\left[\mathcal{D}\mathbf{U}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\right]^{\top}\right)\mathbf{U}_{i}^{\top}
, and
\mathbf{u}=\left[\mathcal{D}\mathbf{j}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\right]^{\top}
be empirical analogs of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G1}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G2}}
(see Web Appendix B of the Supporting Information). The empirical adjustment is

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{E}}=-\left(\mathbf{I}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{p}^{\top}\right)\left[\left(\mathbf{1}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{j}^{-\top}\right)\circ\mathbf{d}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{1}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{j}^{-1}\mathbf{e}\,\mathbf{j}^{-\top}\right)\circ\mathbf{u}\right]\mathbf{1}_{p},

(10)

and the empirical bias-reduced (EBR) estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{EBR}
solves
\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{E}}=\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{G}}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{E}}=\mathbf{0}_{p}
. Although
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{E}}
satisfies (
5
) under the conditions of
14
, it can differ from
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{R}}
by zero-mean terms that can inflate finite-sample variability. See Web Appendix D of the Supporting Information for an example. We therefore recommend
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{RBR}
as the default and treat
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{EBR}
as a fallback when the robust procedure is numerically unstable.

2.4
Binary Responses: Odds-Ratio Parameterization

For correlated binary responses, pairwise odds-ratio parameterizations
(
16
;
2
)
avoid the restrictive feasibility constraints on correlation coefficients
(
3
;
4
)
, making them better suited for measuring the association of correlated binary responses. The dispersion parameter is fixed at
\phi=1
, the nuisance vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
collects pairwise odds ratios from within-subject
2\times 2
tables, and the working covariance
\mathbf{V}_{i}
is specified through
\mu_{ijj^{\prime}}=\Pr(Y_{ij}=Y_{ij^{\prime}}=1)
, with the
(j,j^{\prime})
th off-diagonal element of
\mathbf{V}_{i}
equal to
\mu_{ijj^{\prime}}-\mu_{ij}\mu_{ij^{\prime}}
. For subject
i
and times
j\neq j^{\prime}
,

\mu_{ijj^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{f_{ijj^{\prime}}-\sqrt{f^{2}_{ijj^{\prime}}-4\alpha_{ijj^{\prime}}(\alpha_{ijj^{\prime}}-1)\mu_{ij}\mu_{ij^{\prime}}}}{2(\alpha_{ijj^{\prime}}-1)}&\mbox{if $\alpha_{ijj^{\prime}}\neq 1$},\\[6.0pt]
\mu_{ij}\mu_{ij^{\prime}}&\mbox{if $\alpha_{ijj^{\prime}}=1$},\end{cases}

where
f_{ijj^{\prime}}=1-(1-\alpha_{ijj^{\prime}})(\mu_{ij}+\mu_{ij^{\prime}})
and
\alpha_{ijj^{\prime}}
is the odds ratio for the pair
(j,j^{\prime})
. When
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is unknown, it can be estimated via additional estimating equations
(
16
;
2
)
or via the marginalized odds-ratio approach of
26
, as implemented in
28
for correlated binary responses.

The bias-reduction framework of Section
2.2
applies unchanged, but
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G1}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G2}}
must be rederived because
\mathbf{V}_{i}
no longer decomposes as
\mathbf{A}_{i}^{1/2}\mathbf{R}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\mathbf{A}_{i}^{1/2}
. The odds-ratio counterparts
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G1}}^{OR}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G2}}^{OR}
are given in Supporting Information, Web Appendix C. The robust, naive and empirical adjustments under odds ratios are obtained from (
7
), (
8
) and (
10
), respectively, by replacing the correlation-based components with their odds-ratio counterparts.

Bias Correction

Using (

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{RBC}
coincides with the robust bias-corrected estimator of
18
, and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{NBC}
recovers the estimator of
23
when
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{N2}}=\mathbf{0}_{p}
.

2.6
Limitations and Practical Guidance

Three limitations are worth noting. First, first-order bias reduction is not guaranteed for the naive estimators NBR and NBC unless the association structure has been modeled correctly or condition (
9
) holds. Second, for correlated binary responses the odds-ratio parameterization of Section
2.4
should be preferred, because under correlation-coefficient parameterizations, the working correlation matrix may violate the Fréchet bounds and lead to inadmissible values of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
as noted by
24
,
3
, and
4
. Third, when the ordinary GEE solution
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}
does not exist, the bias-corrected estimators RBC, NBC and EBC are unavailable. In such cases the fully iterated bias-reduced estimators should be initialized as described in Algorithm
1
, and for binary responses the penalized GEE framework of
28
provides a complementary approach with existence guarantees.

For routine applications we recommend RBR with the small-sample covariance adjustment of
21
and an odds-ratio parameterization for correlated binary responses. NBR may be useful when the working association model is believed to be close to correct, while EBR is mainly a fallback when RBR is numerically unstable. Fully iterated estimators are preferred when feasible, and one-step corrections are useful when
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{G}
exists and the iterative algorithm converges rapidly.

2.7
Software

All proposed estimators are implemented in the
R
package
geer
, available from CRAN. The function
geewa()
fits marginal models under correlation-coefficient parameterizations, while
geewa_binary()
fits correlated binary-response models under odds-ratio parameterizations, with odds ratios estimated via the marginalized approach of
28
. In both functions, the argument
method
selects ordinary GEE, the fully iterated bias-reduced estimators, or their one-step bias-corrected counterparts. The default is
method = "gee"
, and users seeking robust bias-reduction or bias-correction should specify
method = "brgee-robust"
or
method = "bcgee-robust"
, respectively.

3
Simulation Study

This section reports simulation results assessing the finite-sample performance of bias reduction for GEE with correlated binary and Poisson responses. We compare ordinary GEE with the three proposed fully iterated bias-reduced estimators: RBR, NBR, and EBR. One-step bias-corrected variants are omitted because their simulation results closely track the fully iterated counterparts. We also omit the naive estimators of
23
since they correspond to the special case
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{N2}}=\mathbf{0}_{p}
of our naive adjustment (Section
2.3
).

For each configuration, we generated 10,000 Monte Carlo replications. For each regression parameter and method, we recorded the Monte Carlo bias (
\times 100
), empirical standard error (ESE), empirical coverage (EC, %) of nominal
95\%
Wald confidence intervals based on
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{M}
, and the convergence proportion (CP, %). We also computed two relative efficiency measures. The relative efficiency (RE) is the ratio of the theoretical standard error derived from
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{M}
to the ESE, with values close to 1 indicating that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{M}
accurately reflects the true sampling variability. The simulated relative efficiency (SRE) is the ratio of the MSE of the ordinary GEE estimator under the working independence model to the MSE of the estimator under consideration, where
\text{MSE}=\text{Bias}^{2}+\text{ESE}^{2}
with Bias on the original scale, with the GEE estimator under the working independence model serving as a common baseline across all simulation configurations. An estimator was declared to have converged if the iterated estimating equations satisfied a relative tolerance of
10^{-6}
within at most
500
iterations,
\max_{k}|\widehat{\beta}_{k}|<10^{2}
to flag numerical divergence, and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{M}
could be formed without numerical warnings. Replications failing any of these criteria were treated as nonconverged.

) need not require correct specification of the working association structure, as discussed in Web Appendix D. EBR performs comparably to RBR under complete data, with similar SRE values to those of RBR.

in all cases, confirming its suitability as the default estimator regardless of the working association specification.

N=20

0.37
. These results confirm the recommendation in Section
2.6
that EBR should be treated as a fallback rather than a default.

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{M}

95\%

\beta_{2}
at
N=20
, particularly under the MCAR scenario. Coverage improves steadily with
N
for all three estimators. As expected, differences across methods diminish as
N
increases, and all four estimators perform virtually identically by
N=500
, confirming that the benefits of bias reduction are concentrated when the number of clusters is small to moderate.

5
Summary and Future Directions

Viewing GEE as an
M
-estimation problem provides a unified route to bias reduction and bias correction through adjusted estimating equations. Within this framework, we derived an explicit condition for bias reduction and used it to construct three estimators, robust, naive, and empirical, together with one-step bias-corrected counterparts. All procedures share the first-order asymptotic distribution of ordinary GEE but differ in how the ideal adjustment is approximated and, consequently, in their robustness and efficiency properties. The construction is valid for correlation-coefficient parameterizations and extends naturally to odds-ratio parameterizations for correlated binary responses. It also clarifies how several existing proposals fit within the framework. Under correlation-coefficient parameterizations, the bias-corrected estimator of
18
coincides with our RBC estimator, while the naive bias-reduced and bias-corrected estimators of
23
arise as special cases of our NBR and NBC estimators when the working covariance is treated as fixed with respect to the mean parameters. Under this simplification, first-order bias reduction is not guaranteed for mean-dependent variance functions, regardless of whether the working assumption for the association structure is correctly specified, a limitation that the robust and empirical estimators of the present framework avoid.

Across the simulation designs considered, the robust bias-reduced (RBR) estimator retained the large-sample properties of ordinary GEE while achieving smaller finite-sample bias and comparable or improved mean squared error. The benefits were clearest for correlated binary responses under odds-ratio parameterizations, where ordinary GEE exhibited appreciable small-sample bias. The naive estimator can be attractive when the working association is plausibly correct, whereas the empirical estimator tracked RBR closely in complete data but proved less stable in the smallest samples under missingness for correlated binary responses. These results support RBR as a practical default when sample sizes are small to moderate. For Wald-type inference, we recommend the small-sample adjusted covariance estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{M}
, which provided reliable calibration across settings. Software implementing all procedures is available in the
geer
package.

Several directions for further work are suggested. First, adjusted estimating equations motivate a reexamination of model-selection and goodness-of-fit criteria for correlated data, including QIC, QICu, and CIC
(
22
;
8
;
13
;
12
)
, whose standard derivations rely on unadjusted estimating equations. Second, extending the framework to ordinal and nominal multinomial responses would broaden applicability, though the derivatives required for the adjustment terms may be challenging to obtain in closed form when association is parameterized through local odds ratios
(
26
)
. Finally, combining bias reduction with the existence guarantees of the penalized GEE framework of
28
within a single approach remains an open question. The main obstacle is that the ideal bias-reduction adjustment depends on the true covariance matrices of the responses, which are unknown and must be estimated, whereas the PGEE penalty depends only on the model-based covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}
and its derivatives. Reconciling these two adjustments without sacrificing the finiteness guarantees of the penalized framework remains a worthwhile direction for future research.
