Activating bulk S/Se/Te positive electrodes by acidic binder-induced Cu corrosion for wide-temperature Na-Chalcogen batteries
Authors: Chengxing Lu, Boyu Li, Qun Li, Shuaishuai Liu, Jinnuo Cheng, Jiahao Yan, Zemin Wang, Tong Liu, Jinkai Wang, Qing Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sluggish kinetics and shuttle effects hinder high-energy rechargeable sodium-chalcogen batteries, requiring nanostructured positive electrodes with excess conductive additives. Here, we show that an acidic binder-induced corrosion strategy enables activity in bulk S/Se/Te positive electrodes. Carboxylic acid-rich binders create an acidic microenvironment (pH≈3) during processing, triggering spontaneous formation of copper chalcogenide interlayers on Cu current collectors. These interlayers reconstruct into active Cu2X (X = S/Se/Te) catalysts during cycling. Using Na-Se batteries, experimental and DFT analyses confirm Cu2Se reduces the liquid-solid conversion barrier by ~24.1% and accelerates polyselenides conversion. Polar groups in the binders enhance chemisorption, reducing the shuttling effect. Consequently, bulk Se with alginic acid binder achieves 96% theoretical utilization at 0.1 A g‒1, rate capability of 508 mAh g‒1 at 20 A g‒1, stable cyclability at 5 A g‒1 over 16,000 cycles, and low-temperature operation at 0.1 A g‒1 (94% capacity retention at −20 °C). The strategy of transforming binders into active interface modulators provides a generalizable approach for other chalcogens, enabling practical and host-free rechargeable metal batteries with chalcogen positive electrodes.

Introduction

Room-temperature sodium-chalcogen batteries (RT-SCBs), utilizing sodium metal negative electrodes and chalcogen positive electrodes (S, Se, Te), have emerged as promising alternatives to lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) owing to their elemental abundance, low cost, and high theoretical specific energy. For example, Na-S systems offer specific capacities up to 1675 mAh g‒1 and specific energies of 1274 Wh kg‒1 1,2. However, their practical application still faces significant challenges. The intrinsically low electronic conductivity of sulfur (~5 × 10‒28 S m‒1 at 25 °C) severely limits both electron transport in the positive electrode and active materials utilization3,4. Although the conductivity of selenium (~1 × 10‒3 S m‒1) and tellurium (~2 × 102 S m‒1) has improved, their atomic utilization still requires significant improvement5,6. Further complications arise from the shuttle effect of soluble polychalcogenide intermediates, which causes active material loss and capacity fading, coupled with substantial volume changes during sodiation/desodiation that threaten positive electrode structural integrity and cycling stability7. Additionally, sluggish reaction kinetics fundamentally limit rate capability and practical viability. These issues collectively hinder the technological advancement of RT-SCBs.

To address these practical challenges, the prevailing core strategy involves confining chalcogens (S, Se, Te) within micro/nanostructured carbon hosts to construct composites8. This architectural design significantly enhances electrode conductivity and improves electron transport efficiency. Furthermore, introducing heteroatoms or polar components into the carbon framework strengthens chemisorption of soluble polychalcogenide intermediates, thereby effectively suppressing the shuttle effect9. To accelerate chalcogen redox kinetics, catalytically active species are incorporated as functional carriers, or composites are engineered with highly conductive substrates (e.g., hierarchically porous carbon, MXenes)10–13. These approaches synergistically optimize battery reaction kinetics by reducing activation energy barriers and enhancing charge transfer rates. For example, Li et al. developed an Fe-Co dual-atom catalyst supported on N-doped hollow carbon nanospheres, which effectively facilitates the sulfur redox reaction kinetics14. Although the aforementioned strategies effectively enhance the electrochemical performance of RT-SCBs, the introduction of host materials inevitably reduces the mass percentage of active materials while substantially increasing manufacturing costs due to their intricate synthesis procedures. Consequently, it has become imperative to develop alternative strategies that enable high utilization efficiency of bulk chalcogen materials without relying on additional carriers or catalysts.

In Na-chalcogen battery systems, copper (Cu) current collectors play a critical role in enabling efficient electrochemical utilization of bulk chalcogenide positive electrode materials. For example, Li et al. demonstrated a Cu-driven conversion mechanism that directly employs bulk S/Se as active material15. These chalcogens undergo in situ electrochemical transformation into active Cu2S/Cu2Se components. The resultant Na-S battery delivers a capacity retention of 1045 mAh g‒1 after 700 cycles at 2 A g‒1. However, under the same conditions, Na-Se batteries exhibited a significantly lower capacity output (~100 mAh g‒1), indicating the low reaction kinetics between Cu and Se due to the low oxidation capability of Se. In our earlier research on Na-Se batteries, we demonstrated that the Cu-driven conversion reaction could accelerate the transformation kinetics of polyselenides, thereby significantly improving sodium storage performance16. Further advancing this concept, Chen et al. engineered a fast ion conductor (amorphous Cu1.4Te) via in situ electrochemical reconstruction of micro-sized Te on Cu current collectors. This design boosted electrode ionic conductivity, enabling a high-performance K-Te battery operable at −40 °C through synergistic electrolyte optimization17. Recently, Qiao’s team introduced the concept of facet engineering and revealed the selective regulatory effect of the Cu (111) facet on the sulfur redox reaction18. The Cu (111) facet can not only induce the generation of a stable intermediate phase NaCu5S3, but also successfully establish a full solid-solid conversion pathway, significantly improving the reaction kinetics and ion transport efficiency. Although the Cu-driven conversion mechanism has been demonstrated to significantly enhance the electrochemical performance of chalcogenide electrodes, there is still a lack of in-depth investigations into how this conversion mechanism can be effectively triggered. More importantly, the inconsistent reaction kinetics of various chalcogen increase the difficulty of finding a generalization to other chalcogens that is applicable to all RT-SCBs. From this consideration, the binder may provide potential functions to activate the Cu current collector. As one of the key components of the electrode, the binder is uniformly dispersed with chalcogen particles, and can regulate the microenvironment of positive electrodes and the interaction towards the current collector. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the binder’s role in the Cu-driven conversion mechanism, particularly how it affects the initial state of the active material/current collector interface and subsequent electrochemical reconstruction, is crucial for further improving the performance of RT-SCBs.

In this work, we demonstrate that carboxylic acid-based binders (e.g., alginic acid (AA), polyacrylic acid (PAA)) with an acidic microenvironment can accelerate the reaction kinetics in all chalcogen (S, Se, Te) positive electrodes even under bulk phase (micron sizes). The acidic condition facilitates the pre-formation of a corrosion interlayer (CuX, X = S, Se, Te) at the chalcogen active material/Cu current collector interface. This pre-formed interfacial layer will serve as nucleation seeds, significantly facilitating subsequent electrochemical restructuring into highly stable and electrochemically active Cu2X phases. The resulting Cu2X not only delivers electrochemical capacity as an active material but more critically serves as an efficient catalyst, significantly facilitating polychalcogenide conversion kinetics. This dual functionality enhances both reaction efficiency and electrochemical reversibility. Furthermore, the inherent abundance of polar functional groups (e.g., -COOH, -OH) in these binders facilitates strong chemisorption of polychalcogenide intermediates, effectively suppressing their dissolution and shuttle effects. Coupled with their robust mechanical properties that maintain electrode structural integrity, this synergistically enhances capacity retention and cycling stability. Specifically, the Na-Se battery employing AA as the binder and micron-sized bulk Se as the active component achieves a discharge capacity of 508 mAh·g‒1 at 20 A·g‒1 and maintains 89% capacity retention over 16,000 cycles at 5 A·g‒1, and also demonstrates extended operation at −20 °C with a capacity of 577 mAh g−1 at 0.1 A g−1.

Results and discussion

Acidic binder selection and induced corrosion engineering at Cu interfaces

Initially, six distinct binders were systematically screened: AA, PAA, sodium alginate (AANa), sodium polyacrylate (PAANa), sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMCNa), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF). This selection was designed to establish a complete comparative system spanning from strongly acidic (AA, PAA), through weakly ionic (AANa, PAANa, CMCNa), to completely inert (PVDF) binders. The rationale originates from the initial observation that among common commercial binders, only PAA significantly enhanced the performance of bulk chalcogenide electrodes. This performance enhancement was hypothesized to originate from the acidic microenvironment created by its free carboxylic acid groups. To validate this, we introduced AA as an extended acidic binder example and PAANa as a critical control to isolate the effect of H⁺ from the carboxylate moiety. Figure 1a schematically illustrates their structural distinctions, revealing AA and PAA as carboxylic acid-rich polymers dominated by -COOH/-OH functional groups. To mask the acidic protons, their sodium salt counterparts (AANa and PAANa) were comparatively evaluated alongside conventional CMCNa and PVDF binders. The Fourier‑transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra in Fig. 1b further validate the characteristic functional groups consistent with the molecular architecture of each binder. To accommodate the significant volume changes in chalcogenide electrodes during cycling, the selected binders must possess good mechanical properties. Consequently, the mechanical strengths of different binders were systematically evaluated via nano-indentation tests. Load-displacement curves were analyzed to determine the hardness and reduced modulus of each binder (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Notably, electrodes incorporating AA and PAA binders exhibited significantly enhanced modulus and hardness values relative to their counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Beyond requiring robust mechanical properties, the pH of binder-derived slurries critically governs pre-corrosion behavior on Cu current collectors. pH measurements of binder solutions (Supplementary Fig. 2) reveal that AA and PAA yield acidic slurries (pH ≈ 3), while others exhibit neutral or weakly alkaline properties. This acidity originates from the dissociation of free -COOH groups in AA/PAA molecules. Taking the Se-based electrode as an example, as shown in Fig. 1c, the acidic microenvironment facilitates the formation of copper selenide (CuxSe) corrosion layer on the Cu substrate during the slurry preparation. Subsequent electrochemical cycling progressively reconstructs this interphase into stable Cu2Se catalytic species, and this electrochemical reconstruction mechanism will also be verified in the subsequent mechanistic analysis.

Figure 1d shows X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of Se-AA-Cu and Se-AANa-Cu electrodes. Se-AANa-Cu electrodes exhibited crystal structures consistent with those of pure Se (PDF#06-0362) and pure Cu (PDF#03-1018), with no impurity peaks detected. In contrast, Se-AA-Cu electrodes displayed additional diffraction peaks at 2θ = 25.0°, 27.8°, 28.7°, and 31.2° assigned to the (101), (200), (111), and (210) planes of Cu3Se2 (PDF#71-0045), respectively, while a characteristic peak at 2θ = 27.1° was simultaneously observed for the (111) plane of Cu2Se (PDF#88-2043), beyond those of the Se and Cu phases. These findings indicate that the acidic microenvironment generated by the AA binder facilitates the formation of CuxSe corrosion layers at the interface. Remarkably, acidic exposure triggers the evolution of ~200 nm Cu particles (Fig. 1e,f) on Cu substrate surfaces, exhibiting agglomerated architectures comprising primary nanocrystallites (Fig. 1g). The phase purity and elemental homogeneity of these metallic Cu particles were unambiguously verified through combined energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Fig. 1h) and post-Se-removal XRD pattern (Supplementary Fig. 3). Figure 1i shows a transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image displaying the morphological characteristics of the copper-selenium composite phase formed at the corrosion interface. This phase originates from the solid-state diffusion reaction of copper ions released during corrosion with selenium upon contact. The selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns provide clear evidence for the crystallographic features of these composite phases. The measured interplanar spacings of diffraction rings are in good agreement with standard references for Cu2Se and Cu3Se2 (Fig. 1i), establishing perfect consistency with XRD results (Fig. 1d) that collectively confirm their polycrystalline nature. The HAADF-STEM image (Fig. 1j) and the corresponding elemental mapping confirm the homogeneous spatial distribution of Se and Cu within the copper-selenium composite phase. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, the acid-corroded Cu surface becomes smoother compared to the pristine fresh Cu surface. This phenomenon is likely attributed to the preferential dissolution of the protruding parts of the Cu surface, which diffuse into the electrode. Pre-corrosion of the Cu surface also occurred in S and Te electrode systems. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5, the Cu surface exhibits varying degrees of corrosion, with the corrosion layer compositions identified as copper sulfide (CuS) and copper telluride (Cu2.8Te2), respectively. The S, Se, and Te used in this study are all micron-scale bulk materials (Supplementary Fig. 6). The formation of the corrosion layer promotes the further coupling of Cu with bulk materials to form stable Cu2X catalysts, thereby accelerating the conversion of polychalcogenides and significantly enhancing the utilization of micron-scale bulk materials and their sodium storage capacity.

To elucidate the mechanism of acidic binder-induced Cu corrosion and the formation of CuxSe, the effects of the acidic environment and temperature on the formation of copper chalcogenides were systematically investigated. In the Se-Cu system, XRD analysis of electrodes prepared with the acidic AA binder and dried at 25 ± 2 °C confirmed the formation of Cu2Se, along with Cu3Se2 and Cu1.8Se phases (Supplementary Fig. 7a). In contrast, electrodes using the neutral AANa binder exhibited only diffraction peaks corresponding to pure Se and Cu (Supplementary Fig. 7b), even after drying at 60°C, demonstrating that an acidic environment is essential for triggering the Se-Cu reaction at 25 ± 2 °C. A consistent trend was observed in the Te-Cu system (Supplementary Fig. 7c and 7d), where only the AA binder (dried at 25 ± 2 °C) induced the formation of CuxTe, whereas no copper telluride phases were detected with the AANa binder, even after thermal treatment at 60 °C. Notably, the S-Cu system exhibits a slightly different but complementary trend. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7e, the AA binder promoted the formation of CuS at 25 ± 2 °C, while the AANa binder required drying at 60 °C for CuS to form, consistent with the reported strong sulfiphilicity of Cu19,20. In comparison, Se and Te did not react with Cu under neutral conditions even at 60 °C, which can be attributed to the decreasing oxidizability of chalcogens with increasing atomic number. These results indicate that the acidic environment enhances the oxidizability of S/Se/Te, thereby lowering the reaction energy barrier and overcoming the thermodynamic limitation posed by the positive redox potential of Cu, enabling the spontaneous formation of copper chalcogenides at 25 ± 2 °C.

To gain fundamental insight into the interfacial reaction mechanism at the atomic level, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations on both Cu (220) and Cu (111) surface models (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Data 1). On the Cu (220) surface, Se adsorption alone triggers significant surface reconstruction, spontaneously giving rise to a Cu-Se cluster featuring a Cu:Se ratio of 4:1 (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b, e). Remarkably, co-adsorption with H+ further promotes this reconstruction, driving the clusters stoichiometry toward Cu2Se (2:1 composition) (Supplementary Fig. 8d, f). The calculated Cu-Se bond lengths (2.23-2.71 Å) in these clusters match well with bulk Cu2Se (2.53 Å). Thermodynamically, the formation energy (Ef) of Cu2Se clusters is strongly negative (−32.36 eV) even without H+, confirming spontaneous formation feasibility. Critically, H+ presence drastically reduces Ef to −56.83 eV, powerfully promoting Cu2Se generation. Electronic structure analysis through density of states (Supplementary Fig. 9) reveals significant Se-4p and Cu-3d orbital hybridization between −4 to −2 eV, indicating strong covalent Cu-Se bonding via p-d hybridization. Conversely, the Cu (111) surface shows minimal reconstruction despite enhanced Se adsorption energy with H+ (−2.07 eV to −4.20 eV), highlighting the facet-dependent reactivity where Cu (220) is particularly susceptible to corrosion and cluster formation. These computational results demonstrate that H+ not only facilitates but is essential for driving extensive surface reconstruction and drastic reduction in formation energy, enabling spontaneous CuxSe formation through strong interfacial bonding at 25 ± 2 °C.

Electrochemical performance for Na-Se batteries

The electrochemical performance of bulk Se with AA, PAA, AANa, PAANa, CMCNa, and PVDF binders was evaluated in CR2032-type coin cells with Na metal as negative electrode in the potential window of 0.4-3.0 V (versus Na+/Na). Figure 2a compares the rate performances of the bulk Se with different binders. Among them, the bulk Se positive electrode using AA and PAA binders with corrosion layers exhibits favorable capacity retention and relatively high specific discharge capacities at various current densities. In the AA binder system, the bulk Se delivers reversible capacities of 634, 633, 632, 629, 625, 617, 600, and 508 mAh g−1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20 A g−1, respectively. Notably, the reversible capacity of 650 mAh g−1 can be recovered when the specific current is returned to 0.1 A g−1, reaching 96.3% of the theoretical capacity of Se (675 mAh g−1). It is worth noting that even the AA electrode obtained at 25 ± 2 °C can achieve comparable capacity and rate performance (Supplementary Fig. 10). In contrast, the PAA binder assisted bulk Se electrode also maintains a high specific capacity of 566 mAh g−1 under identical conditions, whereas the control groups using conventional binders (including AANa, PAANa, CMCNa, and PVDF) exhibit capacities below 170 mAh g−1. Notably, this favorable performance is also observed with citric acid, a small-molecule organic acid binder that creates an acidic environment. Its electrode exhibits comparable capacity and rate capability (Supplementary Fig. 11), further validating the universality of free carboxylic acid groups in enhancing performance. We also evaluated control electrodes incorporating pre-synthesized Cu2Se nanowires (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b)21. However, these electrodes delivered negligible capacities below 50 mAh g−1 (Supplementary Fig. 12c, d) in both configurations (AANa/Cu and AA/Al), highlighting the key limitation of physical catalyst blending. This discrepancy demonstrates that acidic binders (e.g., AA, PAA) and their in situ formed corrosive interlayers play a key role in optimizing electrode interface kinetics and enhancing Se utilization. The corresponding galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) profiles of the different binders at various rates are presented in Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 13. Supplementary Fig. 14 shows the GCD profiles of bulk Se with different binders in the initial five cycles at 0.1 A g−1. The profiles exhibit relatively distinct charge-discharge plateaus, which correspond to the occurrence of Se redox reactions. The pre-corrosion engineering for acidic binder adopted in this work improves both capacity and rate capability of bulk Se materials. As shown in Fig. 2c, the micron-scale bulk Se developed herein exhibits rate performance comparable to recently reported RT Na-Se batteries utilizing catalyst supports16,22–29. Considering the significant differences in capacity, this study only conducted cycle performance tests on bulk Se with AA and PAA binders. As shown in Fig. 2d, the initial Coulombic efficiencies (ICE) of bulk Se with AA and PAA binders reached as high as 92.4% and 95.6%, respectively. Moreover, after 100 cycles at a low specific current of 0.1 A g−1, their reversible capacities attained 673 mAh g−1 and 636 mAh g−1, which are even higher than initial capacities due to the gradual activation process. Furthermore, they also exhibited good long-term cycling stability at a high specific current of 5 A g−1. As shown in Fig. 2e, the bulk Se with PAA binder exhibits a high reversible specific capacity of 490 mAh g−1 and capacity retention of 92% after 10,000 cycles with a low capacity decay rate of 0.0008% per cycle. Similarly, bulk Se with AA binder shows an even higher reversible capacity of 566 mAh g−1 after 10,000 cycles, with capacity retention and decay rate comparable to those of the PAA binder system. More remarkably, the AA binder system maintains stable cycling up to 16,000 cycles, retaining a reversible capacity of 544 mAh g−1 with 89% capacity retention and a per cycle decay rate of 0.0007%. Herein, the bulk Se electrode with AA/PAA binder delivers a stable cycling performance over 10,000 cycles. Its cycling stability and capacity decay behavior are competitive compared with previously reported Se-based positive electrodes for RT Na-Se batteries (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 1)16,22–39. The system also demonstrates competitive advantages over state-of-the-art Li-Se batteries in key metrics (Supplementary Table 2). Such stable long-term cycling performance implies that severe parasitic reactions and rapid electrode failure are effectively suppressed. To further substantiate that the acidic microenvironment does not introduce additional failure mechanisms via electrolyte decomposition, we conducted a post-mortem analysis of the electrolyte stability. Raman spectroscopy was employed to compare a fresh 1 M NaPF6 in DME electrolyte with an electrolyte in which a Se-AA electrode had been immersed for 24 h. The resulting spectra (Supplementary Fig. 15) are virtually identical, showing no detectable additional peaks or shifts characteristic of solvent degradation or salt decomposition. This direct evidence confirms the chemical compatibility of the acidic binder with the electrolyte. The origin of this compatibility lies in the immobilized nature of the carboxylic acid protons within the solid polymer matrix after electrode drying, which prevents continuous leaching of H+ into the bulk electrolyte during cycling. Consequently, the initial acidic condition crucial for interface engineering does not perpetually drive detrimental side reactions, thus ensuring the observed favorable electrochemical stability.

To further explore the feasibility of bulk Se with AA binder for low-temperature energy storage systems, its rate capability and cycling performance at −20 °C were systematically evaluated (Fig. 2g). After 50 stable cycles at 0.1 A g−1 under 25 ± 2 °C, the battery was subjected to low-temperature rate capability testing. At −20 °C, it retained 577 mAh g−1 at 0.1 A g−1 with only 6% capacity decay compared to the room-temperature baseline, while delivering 438 mAh g−1 even at a high specific current of 1 A g−1, demonstrating robust low-temperature kinetics. Upon extended cycling at 1 A g−1 for 1000 cycles, the capacity stabilized at 402 mAh g−1. Notably, the battery maintained performance consistency despite an unexpected power interruption followed by temperature recovery (to ambient) and system reboot at the 450th cycle, highlighting its structural integrity and resilience to operational disruptions.

Electrochemical reaction kinetics test and analysis

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were performed on bulk Se with various binders at different scanning rates ranging from 0.2 to 1.0 mV s−1 to further explore the Na+ diffusion properties and Se conversion kinetics. As revealed by the comparative CV curves of bulk Se electrodes with different binders at 1.0 mV s−1 (Fig. 3a), the acidic binder systems (AA/PAA) exhibit higher peak currents and larger integrated areas, signifying accelerated Se conversion kinetics and enhanced sodium storage capacity. These findings are fully consistent with their favorable rate capability demonstrated in Fig. 2a, further validating the significant advantages of acidic binders (AA/PAA) in optimizing the electrochemical performance of bulk Se materials. Figure 3b shows the contour plots of CV curves for bulk Se with AA binder. Three obvious reduction peaks at ∼1.6 V (R1), ∼1.0 V (R2), and ∼0.6 V (R3) correspond to the conversion of Se to high-order Na2Sex (4 ≤ x ≤ 8), Na2Se2, and low-order Na2Se, respectively. Two typical oxidation peaks at ∼1.5 V (O1) and ∼1.9 V (O2) correspond to the conversion of Na2Se/Na2Se2 to Na2Sex and Se, respectively40. This multi-stage redox behavior demonstrates the sequential phase transformation pathway during sodium-ion insertion/extraction in Se-based electrodes. It is worth noting that the redox processes are also coupled with partial redox reactions of Cu2Se, which will be verified by subsequent ex situ characterizations. The b value obtained by linear fitting of the log(i) vs. log(v) relationship effectively distinguishes the charge storage mechanism of the redox peaks (battery-type diffusion control or capacitor-type surface control behavior)41. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 16, the calculated b-values of 0.85 (R3) and 0.73 (O1) for the dominant redox peaks indicate a hybrid diffusion-capacitive storage mechanism with pseudocapacitive dominance. As demonstrated in Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 18, the pseudocapacitive ratio of Se electrodes with AA and PAA acidic binders reaches up to 97% at a scan rate of 1.0 mV s−1, which is significantly higher than that of Se electrodes with other binders. This high pseudocapacitance signifies abundant, efficiently utilized redox-active sites in bulk Se, stemming from the initially formed CuxSe interlayer and subsequent in situ electrochemically derived nanoscale Cu2Se catalyst. This catalyst simultaneously functions as an active phase and mediates the electrochemical dissociation of bulk Se into surface-anchored nano-agglomerates (a structural evolution confirmed in subsequent mechanistic analyses). Critically, this process collectively enables enhanced exposure of Na+ storage active sites alongside accelerated polyselenide conversion kinetics. These synergistically coupled effects constitute the fundamental mechanistic basis for the high discharge capacity and satisfactory rate performance of acidic-binder-equipped bulk Se positive electrodes.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) pattern of fresh bulk Se electrodes with different binders (Fig. 3d) reveals three characteristic regions: the high-frequency intercept on the Z’-axis corresponds to the ohmic resistance (Rs) of separator/electrolyte, the mid-to-high frequency semicircle to the charge transfer resistance (Rct) at the electrode-electrolyte interface, and the low-frequency slope to the Warburg impedance (Zw) related to Na+ solid-state diffusion. The EIS data were fitted using the equivalent circuit shown in Supplementary Fig. 19a. To exclude the influence of the Na metal counter electrode, we measured a Na | |Na symmetric cell, which exhibited negligible impedance (~0.2 Ω, Supplementary Fig. 19b), confirming that the recorded Rct originates predominantly from the working electrode. Notably, bulk Se employing AA and PAA acidic binders exhibits significantly lower Rct values (3 Ω and 4 Ω, respectively) than those of AANa (11 Ω), PAANa (8 Ω), CMCNa (7 Ω), and PVDF (6 Ω) systems. To further validate the assignment of these processes, we performed distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis. The DRT spectra (Supplementary Fig. 19c,d) resolve five distinct peaks, corresponding to high-frequency polarization (P1, P2), mid-frequency charge-transfer (P3, P4), and low-frequency diffusion (P5) processes42. Electrodes with acidic binders demonstrate a substantial reduction in peak intensities and integrated areas across all frequency regions (P1 to P5), independently confirming their favorable charge-transfer kinetics and reduced interfacial resistances. This result collectively demonstrates that the CuxSe interfacial catalyst induced by acidic binders can effectively reduce the charge transfer energy barrier. The relatively low Rct values observed even at open-circuit voltage (OCV) can be attributed to the pre-formed conductive CuxSe interlayer, which provides efficient charge transport pathways prior to electrochemical cycling.

To gain deeper insights into the Na-Se redox mechanism and Cu2Se phase evolution, ex situ XRD, X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS), and TEM were employed to investigate the bulk Se positive electrode with AA binder during charge/discharge processes. Supplementary Fig. 24 displays the ex situ XRD patterns of the bulk Se positive electrode with AA binder at the first complete sodium deintercalation state. Upon complete discharge, the diffraction peaks of Se and Cu3Se2 disappeared completely. Only peaks corresponding to Cu2Se and the newly formed Na2Se were observed. This Na2Se peak is attributed to sodium selenite (Na2SeO3, PDF#32-1153) formed due to the oxidation of Na2Se in air. In the subsequent full charge state, the peaks associated with Na2Se vanish, accompanied by the intensified peaks of Cu2Se. This indicates that the coupled Cu atoms within the electrode directly react with Na2Se to form Cu2Se, while the residual Na2Se ultimately transforms into amorphous Se. Figure 4c further shows the ex situ XRD patterns at the 21st cycle steady state. Similarly, the diffraction peaks associated with Na2Se (Na2SeO3) gradually emerge with weakened Cu2Se peaks during discharge, while the charging process shows the opposite trend. This crystallographic evolution aligns precisely with that observed during the first cycle, confirming the electrode’s robust cycling stability. Collectively, the overall electrochemical reaction processes involve two distinct redox pathways: one mediated by Cu2Se and the other dominated by Se species. In contrast, ex situ XRD patterns of the bulk Se positive electrode with AANa binder did not exhibit similar reaction behaviors or pathways (Supplementary Fig. 25), which is ascribed to the weak binding ability of AANa toward NaPSe, leading to severe active material loss. Even though the Cu2Se catalyst was detected during the reaction, it failed to suppress the shuttle effect of NaPSe. This result confirms that acidic binders and their pre-corrosion effect on the Cu current collector are crucial for the efficient utilization of bulk Se.

To eliminate interference from the Cu current collector, ex situ XPS analysis was performed on the cycled bulk Se electrode after deliberate removal of the Cu current collector, aiming to further verify its phase transformation mechanism. Figure 4d shows the high-resolution Se 3 d spectra of bulk Se with AA binder at different charge-discharge states. During discharge, elemental Se progressively transforms into polyselenides and Na2Se. Conversely, during charging, Na2Se and polyselenides gradually convert back to Se alongside Cu2Se formation16. In contrast, no discernible Se 3 d signal was detected in the ex situ XPS for the bulk Se electrode with AANa binder after the 21st cycle (Supplementary Fig. 26). The Cu LMM Auger signals were further measured to distinguish between Cu(0) and Cu(I) valence states. As evidenced by Fig. 4e, the Cu LMM Auger peak at 918 eV is assigned to Cu(I) in Cu2Se46. Quantitative comparison of the peak area ratios revealed that Cu(0) became the predominant species at the fully discharged state, while Cu(I) dominated at the fully charged state. This result provides direct evidence for the reciprocal transformation between Cu2Se and metallic Cu during cycling. HRTEM images of the sample at fully discharged and charged states provide further verification. As shown in Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 27, the Na2Se, Cu2Se, and Cu phases are unambiguously identified via lattice spacing measurements in the fully discharged state. Upon full charging (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 28), the Cu2Se phase remains detectable, consistent with XRD results. Notably, the electrochemically reconstructed Cu2Se catalyst shows polycrystalline characteristics with several crystal planes, including the (220) and (111) planes, coexisting and potentially interacting synergistically. Subsequent computational analysis further confirms that the (220) plane possesses a unique atomic pathway enabling rapid Na+ transport, acting as a dominant high-activity surface. The synergistic interaction between different crystal planes collectively enhances overall catalytic activity and reaction kinetics. As depicted in Fig. 4g,i, their corresponding EDS elemental mapping shows homogeneous distributions of Se and Cu elements, indicating that Cu gradually disperses evenly within the electrode after cycling, while effectively confining Se species.

The electrodes cycled for 2000 and 10,000 times were disassembled to further investigate the good redox durability of Se and its structural evolution during cycling. Supplementary Figs. 29–31 present the SEM images of the electrode surface, interior, and Cu interface at different cycle numbers, respectively. The SEM images of the electrode surface reveal that good flatness is maintained after both 2000 and 10,000 cycles, demonstrating the reliable adhesive capability of the functional group-rich acidic binder towards bulk Se particles and its effectiveness in ensuring electrode structural stability. The cracks observed at low magnification are likely caused by desiccation cracking resulting from electrolyte evaporation. Furthermore, as cycling proceeds, the micron-sized bulk Se particles are observed to gradually fragment into smaller particles. This phenomenon occurs throughout the entire electrode interior, resulting in particles with sizes ≤ 1 μm (Supplementary Fig. 30). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 31, the Cu substrate becomes smoother overall with electrochemical cycling, and the Cu particles attached to its surface also become finer and more well-defined. Supplementary Fig. 32 further presents the TEM images of the electrode material after 10,000 cycles. These images clearly reveal that the nanoparticles formed by the fragmentation of the bulk Se during the cycling are predominantly composed of Cu2Se, and Na2Se is dispersed within these nanoparticles. This phenomenon further demonstrates that the acidic binder and the in situ generated Cu2Se can work synergistically to effectively confine NaPSe, suppress its shuttle effect, and thus enhance the cycling stability of the electrode.

Under the typical testing condition of 5 A g−1 with a mass loading of 1.2 mg cm−2, the electrode operated at an areal current density of ~6.0 mA cm−2 and delivered an areal capacity of ~0.69 mAh cm−2. To ensure sodium metal negative electrode stability under such high areal current densities, a dual-separator configuration consisting of a PP membrane and a glass fiber (GF) separator was employed. Single-separator control experiments demonstrated the inability of either configuration to sustain stable operation. Specifically, cells with a GF separator alone sustained merely a few cycles, while those with a PP separator alone exhibited micro-short circuits during the initial cycle (Supplementary Fig. 33b, c). Such results provide clear confirmation of the dual-separator configuration’s necessity47.

Post-mortem analysis of cycled sodium metal negative electrodes provided further mechanistic insights. In the dual-separator system, the sodium surface exhibited typical roughening following repeated Na stripping and plating, yet retained its structural integrity without the formation of large dendrites that could penetrate the separator (Supplementary Fig. 34a–c). In contrast, the single GF separator system exhibited more severe inhomogeneous deposition and large granular structures, accompanied by sodium particle deposition on the GF separator. This deposition progressively extended toward the Se positive electrode side with continued cycling, ultimately leading to short circuits (Supplementary Fig. 34d–f). Notably, sodium metal deposition also gradually formed on the outer GF separator in the dual-separator system. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 33a, a silvery deposition ring appeared on the GF separator near the Se positive electrode side after 2000 cycles, which expanded toward the center after 10,000 cycles. XRD analysis confirmed this deposit to be metallic sodium (Supplementary Fig. 33d), while SEM showed its surface exhibited a flat morphology similar to the initial sodium foil (Supplementary Fig. 34g-i). The PP separator effectively prevented direct contact between the newly deposited sodium and the Se positive electrode. This protection, combined with the uniform sodium deposition on the GF separator, collectively ensured durable and stable battery cycling.

Furthermore, elemental analysis (EDS) of the separators revealed clear Se and Cu signals in the single GF separator system (Supplementary Fig. 35a, b), confirming severe polyselenide shuttling and its detrimental side reactions with the sodium negative electrode that compromised interface stability. Conversely, no detectable Se or Cu signals were found on the outer GF separator in the dual-separator system (Supplementary Fig. 35c, d), demonstrating effective shuttle suppression and the provision of a stable interfacial environment for the sodium metal.

To further substantiate the stability of the sodium metal negative electrode under the demanding conditions relevant to this work, we conducted additional electrochemical analyses. Specifically, Na | |Na symmetric cells were tested at a current density of 6 mA cm−2 with a fixed areal capacity of 0.7 mAh cm−2 per Na plating/stripping cycle. The cell demonstrated stable operation for over 5000 Na deposition/stripping cycles, with the potential hysteresis gradually decreasing and stabilizing upon prolonged testing (Supplementary Fig. 36a–d). This decreasing overpotential suggests an activation process and improved electrode kinetics, indicating the durability of sodium metal plating/stripping under these stringent conditions. EIS measurements on the Na | |Na symmetric cell after 5000 Na deposition/stripping cycles revealed a low charge-transfer resistance, indicating a stable interfacial behavior of the sodium metal negative electrode (Supplementary Fig. 37). Furthermore, the long-term sodium metal plating/stripping reversibility was quantified using Na | |Al (carbon-coated Al foil) cells. The cell exhibited stable Na plating/stripping performance over 3000 Na deposition/stripping cycles, with an average Coulombic efficiency (CE) of 99.937% (Supplementary Fig. 38). These results collectively confirm the highly reversible and stable nature of the sodium metal negative electrode under the applied high current densities and capacities, supporting the long-term Na plating/stripping stability observed in the full cells.

It is noteworthy that the introduction of Cu is essential for significantly enhancing the utilization of bulk Se. When aluminum (Al) is employed as the current collector, the sodium storage capacity of bulk Se is only maintained at approximately 25 mAh g−1 (Supplementary Fig. 39), which starkly contrasts with the performance after Cu introduction. This finding strongly validates that the corrosion engineering on the Cu surface plays a crucial role in optimizing the electrochemical performance of bulk Se. Supplementary Fig. 40a compares the initial charge-discharge profiles of Se-AA-Cu and Se-AA-Al electrodes. The Se-AA-Al electrode exhibits a significant potential drop (75 mV overpotential) during selenium reduction and a potential surge (52 mV overpotential) during oxidation, with these high overpotentials indicating sluggish selenium redox kinetics and high interfacial energy barriers. In contrast, the Se-AA-Cu electrode does not show any measurable overpotential. The smooth and flat plateaus signify a kinetically favorable and highly efficient redox process. Additionally, electrodes with neutral binders all exhibit varying degrees of overpotential (Supplementary Fig. 40b). These results directly demonstrate that the presence of Cu2Se facilitates the reduction of activation energy barriers for Se species conversion and accelerates their transformation, thereby enabling higher utilization of bulk Se.

To further validate the significance of the pre-corrosion engineering on the Cu surface, the aqueous solvent used in the slurry preparation was replaced with N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 41, when the AA binder was dissolved in NMP, the measured pH value was neutral, and no characteristic diffraction peaks of copper selenide (CuxSe) were observed in the XRD pattern, which fully demonstrates that the slurry prepared with NMP does not corrode Cu foil. Furthermore, the bulk Se positive electrode fabricated with this slurry exhibits a discharge specific capacity of only ~90 mAh g−1. These results demonstrate that the pre-corrosion treatment of Cu can promote the penetration of Cu species into the electrode interior and facilitate the formation of stable Cu2Se catalytic material under electrochemical driving forces. This catalytic material can not only serve as a capacity carrier for sodium storage but also effectively accelerate the redox reaction kinetics of selenium species, significantly enhancing the electrode performance.

To substantiate the favorable catalytic activity of Cu2Se, comparative Gibbs free energy profiles (ΔG) for stepwise polyselenide conversion were analyzed with and without Cu2Se catalysts (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 43). In catalyst-free systems, while the Se8 → Na2Se8 transition is thermodynamically spontaneous, subsequent conversion steps exhibit substantial energy barriers. Specifically, the liquid-to-solid conversion (Na2Se4 → Na2Se2) acts as the rate-determining step with a Gibbs free energy barrier of ΔG = 10.64 eV, which constitutes the fundamental origin of polyselenide shuttling. Upon introduction of Cu2Se catalysts, the energy landscape is significantly optimized, with all conversion barriers being effectively reduced below 9.0 eV. Most notably, the critical liquid-solid transition barrier decreases to 8.08 eV, representing a substantial reduction of approximately 24.1% and consequently ceasing to be rate-limiting. This confirms that Cu2Se optimizes the overall reduction pathway of Se species by accelerating the liquid-solid conversion kinetics.

Based on the projected crystal orbital Hamiltonian population (pCOHP) analysis with the weighted state density of overlapping groups, the catalytic modulation mechanism of Cu2Se on the bonding behavior of Na2Sex was further revealed. To gain comprehensive insights into the bond evolution across the entire conversion pathway, we systematically analyzed all key intermediates (from Na2Se to Se8) before and after adsorption on Cu2Se (Supplementary Fig. 44 and Supplementary Table 4). The results demonstrate consistent bond elongation in both Na-Se and Se-Se bonds upon adsorption, accompanied by structural transformations where cyclic polyselenide configurations open and form direct bonds with surface Cu atoms. As shown in Fig. 5d, the Na-Se bond length in Cu2Se-adsorbed Na2Se4 expands to 3.1 Å, exhibiting significant structural relaxation compared to free-standing Na2Se4 (2.9 Å). Integrated COHP analysis (Fig. 5e) reveals a 13.5% reduction in bonding strength (iCOHP = −0.45) for the Cu2Se-Na2Se4 system relative to its isolated counterpart (iCOHP = −0.52), demonstrating the catalyst-induced weakening of Na-Se covalent interactions through interfacial charge redistribution. This universally observed bond-weakening effect across all selenium intermediates substantially enhances the kinetics of liquid-to-solid NaPSe conversion by systematically lowering the energy barriers for polyselenide deposition and dissociation. Crucially, analogous trends are observed in Na2Se adsorption systems, where Cu2Se mediates bond elongation (2.6 Å→3.9 Å) and iCOHP attenuation (−1.03 → −0.11), confirming its dual functionality in simultaneously catalyzing the oxidative dissolution of Na2Se and mitigating kinetic limitations in selenium oxidation reactions (SeOR) via synergistic electronic-geometric modulation. These comprehensive analyses establish that Cu2Se catalyzes the polyselenide conversions through universal bond-weakening effects across all reaction intermediates, fundamentally altering the reaction pathway and dramatically reducing the overall energy barriers.

To further elucidate how Cu2Se reduces charge transfer barriers and facilitates Na+ transport, both of which are key factors enabling the observed high-rate capability, we performed additional DFT calculations (Supplementary Data 1). Analysis of the Cu2Se (220) surface reveals distinctive atomic channels (Supplementary Fig. 45a) that provide optimized pathways for Na+ migration. DFT optimization confirms Na+ can stably reside within these channels (Supplementary Fig. 45b), while Bader charge analysis demonstrates highly efficient charge transfer wherein each Na atom loses ~1 electron to adjacent Se atoms (Supplementary Fig. 45c), thereby reducing charge transfer resistance at the electrode-electrolyte interface. Most notably, CI-NEB calculations reveal a low diffusion barrier of only 0.08 eV for Na+ migration within these channels (Supplementary Fig. 45d, e), confirming Cu2Se’s role as a fast ionic conductor that enables rapid Na+ transport. These findings provide atomic-scale insight into how the unique structural and electronic properties of Cu2Se synergistically enhance both charge transfer and ion diffusion kinetics.

Collectively, the synergistic interplay of enhanced polyselenide chemisorption, dramatically reduced conversion barriers (~24.1% decrease), catalyst-induced bond-weakening effects, and facilitated Na⁺ transport with low diffusion barriers underpins the favorable electrochemical performance of acidic-binder-enabled Na-Se batteries, which is manifested as near-theoretical capacity utilization, high-rate capability, and long cyclability.

These findings collectively demonstrate the universality and effectiveness of the copper pre-corrosion engineering across various chalcogen-based battery systems.

To further evaluate the practical viability of our acidic binder-induced interfacial engineering strategy, we systematically investigated the electrochemical performance under high-mass-loading conditions. For the Se-AA positive electrode (Supplementary Fig. 52a), an areal capacity of approximately 1.6 mAh cm−2 was achieved with a mass loading of 2.5 mg cm−2 at 0.2 A g−1, corresponding to a high specific energy of 690 Wh kg‒1 based on the active material mass (excluding packaging material). When the loading was increased to 4.3 mg cm−2, the areal capacity rose significantly to 2.7 mAh cm−2 while maintaining a specific energy of 675 Wh kg‒1 (active material level, excluding packaging). To pursue even higher areal capacity and specific energy, we extended this investigation to the S-AA system, which possesses a higher theoretical capacity. Notably, with a substantial S loading of 6.1 mg cm‒2, the electrode achieved a favorable areal capacity of 6.0 mAh cm−2 while delivering a good specific energy of 1030 Wh kg‒1 (active material level, excluding packaging) (Supplementary Fig. 52b). These results demonstrate that our strategy maintains its effectiveness even under practically relevant loading conditions.

When compared with state-of-the-art lithium-ion systems, our sodium-chalcogen positive electrodes demonstrate comparable performance in both specific energy and cycling stability. As summarized in Supplementary Table 7, our Na-Se and Na-S systems achieve specific energy values of 690 and 1030 Wh kg‒1, respectively, based on the active material mass (excluding packaging material), which are competitive than some reported lithium-ion positive electrodes including Ni-rich layered oxides (∼640-880 Wh kg‒1) and organic materials (∼229-609 Wh kg‒1). Both systems maintain cycling durability with capacity decay rates of 0.0008% per cycle (Na-Se) and 0.01% per cycle (Na-S), which are lower than those of typical lithium-ion batteries (0.011–0.092% per cycle). This combination of high specific energy and reliable cycling stability positions our sodium-chalcogen systems as highly competitive candidates for next-generation energy storage applications where both high energy content and long service life are paramount.

Discussion

In summary, we present an acidic binder-induced corrosion engineering strategy for bulk S/Se/Te positive electrodes in RT-SCBs. By employing carboxylic acid-rich binders (AA/PAA), an acidic microenvironment (pH ≈ 3) is created during electrode processing, which spontaneously triggers the pre-formation of CuxS/CuxSe/CuxTe and Cu NPs interlayers on Cu current collectors via corrosion. These interlayers serve as nucleation seeds for in situ electrochemical reconstruction into highly active Cu2X catalysts during cycling. The resultant Cu2X phases not only participate in electrochemical reactions as active phases but also act as high-efficiency catalysts, mitigating the shuttle effect by accelerating the conversion kinetics of polychalcogenides. Meanwhile, acidic binders enhance the system performance through their polar groups (-COOH/-OH), enabling strong chemisorption of soluble intermediates while tolerating volume changes via robust mechanical properties. This synergistic mechanism enables the bulk Se positive electrode with AA binder to deliver a high rate capability of 508 mAh·g‒1 at 20 A·g‒1, favorable cycling stability over 16,000 cycles with 89% capacity retention at 5 A·g‒1, and good low-temperature operation (438 mAh·g‒1 at 1 A·g‒1, −20 °C). The generalizability of this strategy to other chalcogens is further validated in Na-S and Na-Te systems, achieving high capacities of 1291 mAh·g‒1 and 645 mAh·g‒1 at 0.1 A·g‒1, respectively. DFT calculations quantitatively confirm that Cu2Se catalysts reduce the energy barrier for liquid-to-solid conversion of polyselenides by ~24.1%, while the acidic binders exhibit favorable adsorption energies toward Na2Se6 intermediates. This work clarifies the transition of binders from passive adhesives to active interface modulators, offering a cost-effective and scalable pathway to practical sodium-chalcogen batteries without relying on complex nanostructured hosts or exotic catalysts.

Methods

Fabrication and testing of positive electrodes for RT Na-S/Se/Te batteries

The electrodes were fabricated by mixing bulk sulfur (S, 99.9%, Aladdin), selenium (Se, 200 mesh, 99.9%, Aladdin), or tellurium (Te, 200 mesh, 99.9%, Aladdin), conductive carbon black Super P (99.8%, Canrd), and binders (AA (99.9%, Alfa), PAA (99.9%, Mw 450,000, Aladdin), AANa (99.9%, Aladdin), PAANa (99.9%, Mw 3,000,000, Aladdin), CMCNa (99.9%, Mw 250,000, Aladdin), PVDF (99.5%, Mw 1,100,000, Canrd)) at a weight ratio of 7:2:1, followed by grinding with deionized water (laboratory-made) or N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, 99.9%, Aladdin) at a solvent-to-solid weight ratio of 5:1 to form a slurry. The slurry was coated onto the Cu current collector using a doctor blade at a wet thickness of 35 μm, resulting in single-side coated electrodes. The Cu current collector (thickness: 9 μm, purity: 99.8%, Canrd) was used as received without any pretreatment. After coating, the electrodes were dried at 60°C in a vacuum oven for 12 h. The dried film was punched into 12 mm diameter electrodes (area ≈ 1.13 cm2) using a manual disc cutter, achieving a mass loading of 1-1.5 mg cm−2. CR2032-type coin cells were assembled using 316 stainless-steel cases and springs (15.4 × 1.1 mm). Cells were assembled in a glove box with O2 and H2O contents below 0.1 ppm, using sodium metal (99.9%, Aladdin) as the negative electrode. The sodium metal was received as rods or sheets, rolled with a stainless steel rod into thin slices (thickness ~0.258 mm), and then punched into discs of 7.9 mm diameter inside an argon-filled glove box. The surface oxide layer was carefully scraped off using a stainless steel blade before use. The clean Na discs were used as negative electrodes without further treatment. GF (Whatman GF/D, 19 mm diameter, thickness 0.67 mm) and PP (Celgard 2400, 19 mm diameter, thickness 25 μm) were used as separators, and the electrolyte consisting of 1 M sodium hexafluorophosphate (NaPF6) dissolved in 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) (Dodochem, 99.9 %). The volume of electrolyte added per cell was 200 μL. The visual cells employed 20 mL transparent glass vials, with electrodes secured by red copper (pure copper) clips and hermetically sealed in an argon-filled glovebox. The preparation of Na2Se6 solution was carried out by stirring Na and Se in a molar ratio of 2:6 in DME at 80 °C for 12 h.

Characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analyses were conducted using Hitachi 7500 and JEM-2200HR (JEOL), respectively. For SEM‑EDX measurements, an accelerating voltage of 10 kV was applied. The crystal phase of the products was analyzed by XRD (Empyrean) over the 2θ range of 5–80°, with a scan rate of 2° min−1 and a step size of 0.02°. XPS (Kratos Axis SUPRA+) was employed to analyze the surface chemical state of the products. XPS samples were prepared inside an Ar‑filled glovebox (O2, H2O < 0.1 ppm) and transferred to the analysis chamber via a sealed, vacuum‑compatible transfer vessel to avoid air exposure. The spectra were acquired using monochromated Al Kα radiation (hν = 1486.6 eV) with a beam power of 150 W. Energy calibration was performed by referencing the adventitious carbon C 1 s peak to 284.8 eV. All binding energies were normalized using the same reference. FTIR was recorded on a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer using the KBr pellet method over the wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm−1. UV‑vis absorption spectra were measured on a UV-2600 spectrophotometer using quartz cuvettes over the wavelength range of 200-600 nm.

Electrochemical measurements

Galvanostatic charge/discharge tests were performed using a NEWARE Battery Test System (Shenzhen, China) at a potential window of 0.4-3 V (vs Na+/Na). For the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) measurements, the cells were discharged/charged at 0.1 A g−1 with a current pulse duration of 10 min followed by a relaxation period of 30 min. Data points were recorded at a rate of 1 point per second (1 Hz). To validate the condition of potential-concentration linearity required for reliable diffusion coefficient calculations, we confirmed that the applied current pulse duration (τ = 600 s) was sufficiently short compared to the characteristic diffusion time (τ ≪ L2/D), and the cell potential during each titration step exhibited a linear relationship with the square root of time over the entire pulse period (R2 > 0.99). This linearity confirms that the diffusion process follows Fick’s second law under the applied conditions. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were carried out in the potential range of 0.4–3.0 V (vs Na+/Na) at scan rates of 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mV s−1. EIS was subsequently performed at OCV in potentiostatic mode with an AC amplitude of 10 mV over a frequency range of 0.01 Hz to 100 kHz, acquiring 5 data points per decade (fitting results are detailed in Supplementary Tables 8–10). Prior to the measurement, the cell was allowed to stabilize at OCV for 1 hour to reach a quasi‑stationary condition. CV and EIS measurements were conducted on a Princeton electrochemical workstation (PARSTAT MC, Princeton, USA). For Na | |Na symmetric cells, two identical sodium metal electrodes (thickness ~0.258 mm, diameter 7.9 mm) were assembled into CR2032 coin cells using the same electrolyte (1 M NaPF6 in DME) and separators (PP and GF) as the full cells. Galvanostatic cycling was performed at a current density of 6 mA cm−2 with a fixed areal capacity of 0.7 mAh cm−2 for each plating/stripping half‑cycle. The voltage hysteresis was recorded over 5000 cycles. EIS was conducted on the symmetric cells after cycling to evaluate the interfacial stability. For Na | |Al cells, carbon‑coated Al foil (thickness 15 μm, purity 99.8%, Canrd) was punched into discs (diameter 12 mm) and used as the working electrode, with sodium metal as the counter electrode. The cells were assembled with the same electrolyte and separators. Sodium was plated onto the Al electrode at a current density of 6 mA cm−2 for a fixed capacity of 0.7 mAh cm−2, followed by stripping to a cutoff potential of 1 V (vs. Na+/Na). The CE was calculated as the ratio of the stripping capacity to the plating capacity. The average CE was determined over 3000 cycles. All electrochemical experiments were repeated using at least three independently fabricated cells under identical conditions. The data presented in the manuscript are from a single representative cell that exhibited median performance characteristics among the replicates. Room-temperature cycling tests were performed under ambient laboratory conditions at an average temperature of 25 ± 2 °C. The reported specific energy values were calculated based on the active material mass only (excluding packaging). The capacity retention and per-cycle decay rate reported for long-term cycling were calculated starting from the 91st cycle after the rate-performance test, i.e., from the 91st to the final cycle.

Theoretical calculations

The adsorption energies between different binder and Na2Se6 intermediate are calculated by Gaussian 09 with the framework of DFT by employing B3LYP/6-31 + G (d, p) level of theory. The dispersion correction term of Grimme-D3 was incorporated to address the noncovalent interactions48. The vibrational frequency calculations are performed to confirm the stability of the optimized structures and further obtain the Gibbs free energy corrections at 298.15 K and 1 atm pressure.

All the DFT calculations related to Cu2Se were conducted by Vienna Ab initio Software Package (VASP) within projected augmented wave (PAW) method49. For the exchange-correction functional, the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) generalized gradient approximation (GGA) is employed50. The energy cut-off for the plane-wave basis set is 550 eV. For the structure relaxation, the used K-point meshes are 13×13×13 and 1 × 1 × 1 Gamma-centered for the bulk Cu2Se and (220) Cu2Se surface with Cu-termination, respectively. There exists 15 Å vacuum in the surface model to avoid the interaction between the periodic images. Four atomic layers in the bottom of the slabs are kept frozen to keep the slabs stable.
