A note on the multiple zeta functions and their variants at identical arguments
Pawan Singh Mehta

Abstract
. In this article, we study the multiple zeta functions (MZF) and some of its variants at identical arguments. Using the harmonic product, these functions can be expressed as polynomials in the Riemann zeta function. Firstly, we note that an explicit description of the coefficients of these polynomials can be given in terms of the values of the complete Bell polynomials. This for instance, easily leads to the complete description of the singularities of these functions. But more importantly, this enables us to establish a functional relation between MZF, Multiple t t -functions (M t t F) and their star variants at identical arguments.

1.
Introduction

Throughout this article, we denote the set of non-negative integers by
{\mathbb{N}}
.
For an integer
r\geq 1
, the depth
r
multiple zeta function is defined by

\zeta_{r}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}):=\sum_{n_{1}>\cdots>n_{r}>0}\frac{1}{n_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots n_{r}^{s_{r}}},

on the open set

U_{r}:=\{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r}:\Re(s_{1}+\cdots+s_{i})>i\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq r\}.

We also set
\zeta_{0}(\varnothing):=1
, in the depth
0
case. In depth
1
, we get back the classical Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
. In the last three decades, the multiple zeta functions and their special values have been studied extensively. However, their origin can be traced back to Euler. While studying the special values of the Riemann zeta function, he noticed that

(1)

\displaystyle\zeta(s_{1})\zeta(s_{2})=\zeta_{2}(s_{1},s_{2})+\zeta_{2}(s_{2},s_{1})+\zeta(s_{1}+s_{2}),

for
s_{1},s_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}
, with
\Re(s_{1}),\Re(s_{2})>1
.
This product formula is known as the harmonic product formula (or, the stuffle product formula) and can be easily generalised for the multiple zeta functions.

Some of the natural variants of the multiple zeta functions are the multiple zeta-star functions (MZSF), the multiple
t
-functions (M
t
F) and the multiple
t
-star functions (M
t
SF), and these functions are defined on
U_{r}
by

\zeta^{\star}_{r}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}):=\sum_{n_{1}\geq\cdots\geq n_{r}\geq 1}\frac{1}{n_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots n_{r}^{s_{r}}},

t_{r}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}):=\sum_{n_{1}>\cdots>n_{r}>0}\frac{1}{(2n_{1}-1)^{s_{1}}\cdots(2n_{r}-1)^{s_{r}}},

and

t^{\star}_{r}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}):=\sum_{n_{1}\geq\cdots\geq n_{r}\geq 1}\frac{1}{(2n_{1}-1)^{s_{1}}\cdots(2n_{r}-1)^{s_{r}}},

respectively, for any integer
r\geq 1
. As before, in the depth
0
case, we set these functions to be
1
. The MZSF and M
t
SF satisfy similar harmonic product formula. For example,

(2)

\displaystyle\zeta^{\star}(s_{1})\zeta^{\star}(s_{2})=\zeta^{\star}_{2}(s_{1},s_{2})+\zeta^{\star}_{2}(s_{2},s_{1})-\zeta^{\star}(s_{1}+s_{2}),\text{ for $\Re(s_{1}),\Re(s_{2})>1$}.

The meromorphic continuation of these functions to
{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
have been studied by many authors. A particular case, which is of our interest in this article, is obtained by considering these meromorphic functions at the points of the form
(s,\ldots,s)
with
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We use the notation
f_{r}(s)
to denote the function
f_{r}(s,\ldots,s)
, where
f
is any one of
\zeta,\zeta^{\star},t,t^{\star}
. Hoffman
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
computed some special values of these functions at positive integers. For example,

(3)

\displaystyle\zeta_{r}(2)=\zeta_{r}(2,\ldots,2)=\frac{\pi^{2r}}{(2r+1)!},\quad t_{r}(2)=t_{r}(2,\ldots,2)=\frac{\pi^{2r}}{2^{2r}(2r)!},

and

(4)

\displaystyle t^{\star}_{r}(2)=t^{\star}_{r}(2,\ldots,2)=\frac{\pi^{2r}}{2^{2r}(2r)!}(-1)^{n}E_{2n},

where
E_{n}
is the
n
-th Eulerian number. Muneta
[
11
]
also obtained some special values of the multiple zeta-star function at positive integers. A more systematic study of the multiple zeta functions and the multiple Hurwitz zeta functions at identical arguments was initiated by Kamano
[
7
]
. He computed the special values of these functions at non-positive integers by using the harmonic product.

One can view
\zeta_{r}(s)
and its variants in terms of the symmetric functions in infinite number of variables. To see this, for an integer
r\geq 1
, let
e_{r}
denote the elementary symmetric function and
h_{r}
denote the complete symmetric function in
n
variable, defined by

e_{r}=e_{r}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}):=\sum_{n\geq n_{1}>n_{2}>\cdots>n_{r}>0}x_{n_{1}}x_{n_{2}}\cdots x_{n_{r}},

and

h_{r}=h_{r}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}):=\sum_{n\geq n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq\cdots\geq n_{r}\geq 1}x_{n_{1}}x_{n_{2}}\cdots x_{n_{r}}.

Then for
\Re(s)>1
, we have

\zeta_{r}(s)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}e_{r}\left(\frac{1}{1^{s}},\frac{1}{2^{s}},\ldots,\frac{1}{n^{s}}\right),\quad\zeta^{\star}_{r}(s)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}h_{r}\left(\frac{1}{1^{s}},\frac{1}{2^{s}},\ldots,\frac{1}{n^{s}}\right),

and

t_{r}(s)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}e_{r}\left(\frac{1}{1^{s}},\frac{1}{3^{s}},\ldots,\frac{1}{(2n-1)^{s}}\right),\quad t^{\star}_{r}(s)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}h_{r}\left(\frac{1}{1^{s}},\frac{1}{3^{s}},\ldots,\frac{1}{(2n-1)^{s}}\right).

For an integer
r\geq 1
, if
p_{r}
denote the symmetric function of
r
-th power sum in
n
variables, defined by

p_{r}=p_{r}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{r},

then we have the following well-known Newton’s identities:

\displaystyle re_{r}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}(-1)^{j-1}e_{r-j}p_{j}\ \text{ and }\ rh_{r}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}h_{r-j}p_{j}.

These identities give rise to the following recursive formulae

(5)

r\zeta_{r}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}(-1)^{j-1}\zeta_{r-j}(s)\zeta(js),\quad rt_{r}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}(-1)^{j-1}t_{r-j}(s)t(js),

and

(6)

r\zeta^{\star}_{r}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\zeta^{\star}_{r-j}(s)\zeta(js),\quad rt^{\star}_{r}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}t^{\star}_{r-j}(s)t(js).

Note that these identities are also consequences of (
1
) and (
2
). With these recurrence formulae, we can write
\zeta_{r}(s)
and its variants, in terms of the usual Riemann zeta function. For example,

(7)

\zeta_{2}(s)=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\zeta(s)^{2}-\zeta(2s)\right\},\quad\zeta_{3}(s)=\frac{1}{6}\left\{\zeta(s)^{3}-\zeta(s)\zeta(2s)+2\zeta(3s)\right\},

and

(8)

\zeta^{\star}_{2}(s)=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\zeta(s)^{2}+\zeta(2s)\right\},\quad\zeta^{\star}_{3}(s)=\frac{1}{6}\left\{\zeta(s)^{3}+3\zeta(s)\zeta(2s)+2\zeta(3s)\right\}.

In
[
8
, eq. 2.13]
, Matsumoto, Matsusaka and Tanackov wrote them in the following form:

(9)

\displaystyle\zeta_{r}(s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{q})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{q}\\
{\bm{\beta}}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{q})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{q}\\
q\leq r,\ \alpha_{1}\beta_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{q}\beta_{q}=r\end{subarray}}C({\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}})\prod_{j=1}^{q}\zeta(\alpha_{j}s)^{\beta_{j}},

where
C({\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\beta}})
are some rational numbers, which were not expressed explicitly though. Note that knowing these constants explicitly gives a complete description of the special values of
\zeta_{r}(s)
in terms of the special values of
\zeta(s)
. Firstly, we note that an explicit description of these coefficients can be given in terms of the values of the complete Bell polynomials.

Theorem 1
.

Let
r\geq 1
be an integer and
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
s>1
. Then

(10)

\displaystyle\zeta_{r}(s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{r}\geq 0,\\
c_{1}+2c_{2}+\cdots+rc_{r}=r\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{r+c_{1}+c_{2}+\cdots+c_{r}}}{1^{c_{1}}c_{1}!\ 2^{c_{2}}c_{2}!\cdots r^{c_{r}}c_{r}!}\zeta(s)^{c_{1}}\zeta(2s)^{c_{2}}\cdots\zeta(rs)^{c_{r}},

and

(11)

\displaystyle t_{r}(s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{r}\geq 0,\\
c_{1}+2c_{2}+\cdots+rc_{r}=r\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{r+c_{1}+c_{2}+\cdots+c_{r}}}{1^{c_{1}}c_{1}!\ 2^{c_{2}}c_{2}!\cdots r^{c_{r}}c_{r}!}t(s)^{c_{1}}t(2s)^{c_{2}}\cdots t(rs)^{c_{r}}.

Remark 1
.

In
[
8
, Theorem 4.2, 4.3]
, Matsumoto, Matsusaka and Tanackov studied the singularities of
\zeta_{r}(s)
and showed that
\zeta_{r}(s)
has only polar singularities at
s=1/k
of order
[r/k]
for any
1\leq k\leq r
, where
[x]
denotes the largest integer
\leq x
. Moreover, they also deduced the leading coefficient in the Laurent expansion of
\zeta_{r}(s)
at these points. Using (
10
), we can immediately recover their result to show that if
r=kl+m
with
0\leq m<k
, then

\zeta_{r}(s)\sim\frac{(-1)^{(k+1)l}}{k^{l}l!}\zeta_{m}\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)(sk-1)^{-l},

around
s=1/k
. A short proof is given in Section
3
.

Using the recurrence relation (
6
), we also prove the following theorem for the star variants.

Theorem 2
.

Let
r\geq 1
be an integer and
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
s>1
. Then

(12)

\displaystyle\zeta^{\star}_{r}(s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{r}\geq 0,\\
c_{1}+2c_{2}+\cdots+rc_{r}=r\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{1^{c_{1}}c_{1}!\ 2^{c_{2}}c_{2}!\cdots r^{c_{r}}c_{r}!}\zeta(s)^{c_{1}}\zeta(2s)^{c_{2}}\cdots\zeta(rs)^{c_{r}},

and

(13)

\displaystyle t^{\star}_{r}(s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{r}\geq 0,\\
c_{1}+2c_{2}+\cdots+rc_{r}=r\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{1^{c_{1}}c_{1}!\ 2^{c_{2}}c_{2}!\cdots r^{c_{r}}c_{r}!}t(s)^{c_{1}}t(2s)^{c_{2}}\cdots t(rs)^{c_{r}}.

The formulae (
11
) and (
13
) can be found in
[
4
, Corollary 3.1-3.2]
, but novelty of our method is that we prove these results using the Bell polynomials. A brief discussion on the Bell polynomials is given in the next section. Our proof involving the Bell polynomials leads us to many other interesting consequences. For example, we get the following seemingly new relation among
\zeta_{r}(s)
,
\zeta_{r}^{\star}(s)
,
t_{r}(s)
and
t_{r}^{\star}(s)
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
r\geq 1
be an integer. Then for any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
s>1
, we have

t_{r}(s)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\frac{(-1)^{r-j}}{2^{(r-j)s}}\zeta_{j}(s)\zeta^{\star}_{r-j}(s)\ \text{ and }\ t^{\star}_{r}(s)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\frac{(-1)^{r-j}}{2^{(r-j)s}}\zeta_{r-j}(s)\zeta^{\star}_{r}(s).

Remark 2
.

The left hand side and the right hand side of each of (
10
), (
11
), (
12
) and (
13
) have meromorphic extensions. Therefore, these identities can be considered as identities of meromorphic functions on
{\mathbb{C}}
and can be used to study
\zeta_{r}(s)
,
t_{r}(s)
and their star-variants at non-positive integer points.

One can easily see that
\zeta_{r}(2k),t_{r}(2k),\zeta_{r}^{\star}(2k),t_{r}^{\star}(2k)\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{*}\pi^{2k}
and
\zeta_{r}(-2k)=t_{r}(-2k)=\zeta_{r}^{\star}(-2k)=t_{r}^{\star}(-2k)=0
for all
k\geq 1
. But here, we also look at the value at
0
. We prove that
\zeta_{r}(0)
and
\zeta^{\star}_{r}(0)
are non-zero rational numbers, whereas
t_{r}(0)=t^{\star}_{r}(0)=0
.
As a corollary to the proof of Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
, we obtain the exact values in terms of certain binomial coefficients. Many such interesting results are included in Section

.

We conclude this section by mentioning that Merca
[

]
obtained the following similar looking expression for
\zeta(2k)
, for an integer

:

\displaystyle\zeta(2k)

\displaystyle=k\pi^{2k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}\geq 0,\\
c_{1}+2c_{2}+\cdots+kc_{k}=k\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{k+c_{1}+\cdots+c_{k}}(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{k}-1)!}{3!^{c_{1}}c_{1}!\ 5!^{c_{2}}c_{2}!\cdots(2k+1)!^{c_{k}}c_{k}!}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{2k}}{2(2^{2k}-2)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}\geq 0\\
c_{1}+2c_{2}+\cdots+kc_{k}=k\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{k+c_{1}+\cdots+c_{k}}(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{k})!}{3!^{c_{1}}c_{1}!\ 5!^{c_{2}}c_{2}!\cdots(2k+1)!^{c_{k}}c_{k}!}.

2.
Preliminaries

We first recall the definition of the Bell polynomials. Let
n\geq k\geq 1
be integers. The partial Bell polynomial
\mathbf{B}_{n,k}:=\mathbf{B}_{n,k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-k+1})
is a polynomial in (infinitely many) indeterminates
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
, defined by the coefficients of the formal double series

\displaystyle\phi(t,u):=\exp\left(u\sum_{m\geq 1}x_{m}\frac{t^{m}}{m!}\right)=1+\sum_{n\geq k\geq 1}\mathbf{B}_{n,k}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}u^{k}=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}u^{k}\mathbf{B}_{n,k}\right)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

It is natural to set
{\bf B}_{0,0}=1
. Note that by the above definition,
\mathbf{B}_{n,k}=0
for all
1\leq n<k
. It is easy to see that

{\bf B}_{1,1}=x_{1},{\bf B}_{2,1}=x_{2},{\bf B}_{2,2}=x_{1}^{2},{\bf B}_{3,1}=x_{3},{\bf B}_{3,2}=3x_{1}x_{2},{\bf B}_{3,3}=x_{1}^{3}.

In general, one can see that for
n\geq 1
,

{\bf B}_{n,1}=x_{n},{\bf B}_{n,n}=x_{1}^{n}.

The partial Bell polynomials satisfy many recurrence formulae and special values of these functions are well-studied (for more details see
[
2
]
).
One often assigns the weight
i
, to the indeterminate
x_{i}
and then the weight of a monomial is defined to be the sum of the weight of each of the indeterminate present in that monomial (counted with multiplicity). We now recall the following key theorem of Bell
[
1
]
, after whom these polynomials are named.

Theorem 4
.

For integers
n\geq k\geq 1
, the partial Bell polynomial
\mathbf{B}_{n,k}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
k
and weight
n
, and has integer coefficients. More precisely,

\mathbf{B}_{n,k}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n-k+1})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}\geq 0,\\
c_{1}+2c_{2}+3c_{3}+\cdots+nc_{n}=n,\\
c_{1}+c_{2}+\cdots+c_{n}=k\end{subarray}}\frac{n!}{(1!)^{c_{1}}c_{1}!\ (2!)^{c_{2}}c_{2}!\cdots(n!)^{c_{n}}c_{n}!}x_{1}^{c_{1}}x_{2}^{c_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{c_{n}}.

One of the many remarkable application of these Bell polynomials is that they can be used to express Faà di Bruno’s formula for the derivative of the composition of two functions (see
[
6
]
). This then leads to many interesting applications of Faà di Bruno’s formula (see also
[
9
]
).

For an integer
n\geq 1
, the complete Bell polynomial
\mathbf{Y}_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is defined by

\mathbf{Y}_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{B}_{n,k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-k+1}).

For example,

\mathbf{Y}_{1}(x_{1})=x_{1},\ \mathbf{Y}_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2},\ \mathbf{Y}_{3}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=x_{1}^{3}+3x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3},

and in general, using Theorem
4
, we have

(14)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}\geq 0,\\
c_{1}+2c_{2}+3c_{3}+\cdots+nc_{n}=n\end{subarray}}\frac{n!}{(1!)^{c_{1}}c_{1}!\ (2!)^{c_{2}}c_{2}!\cdots(n!)^{c_{n}}c_{n}!}{x_{1}}^{c_{1}}{x_{2}}^{c_{2}}\cdots{x_{n}}^{c_{n}}.

It is easy to see from this expression that for any
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
,

(15)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{n}(\alpha x_{1},\alpha^{2}x_{2},\ldots,\alpha^{n}x_{n})=\alpha^{n}\mathbf{Y}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}).

From the definition of the partial Bell polynomials, we see that the generating function for the complete Bell polynomials is given by

(16)

\displaystyle\phi(t,1)=\exp\left(\sum_{m\geq 1}x_{m}\frac{t^{m}}{m!}\right)=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbf{Y}_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

It is natural to set
{\bf Y}_{0}=1
. Note that we also have

(17)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{n}(x_{1}+y_{1},x_{2}+y_{2},\ldots,x_{n}+y_{n})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\mathbf{Y}_{j}(x_{1},x_{2}\ldots,x_{j})\mathbf{Y}_{n-j}(y_{1},y_{2}\ldots,y_{n-j}).

3.
Proof of the theorems

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Since
\zeta_{r}(s)
and
t_{r}(s)
satisfy the same recurrence formula (
5
), we give the key steps of the proof of (
10
). Recall (
5
) for
\zeta_{r}(s)
, namely,

r\zeta_{r}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}(-1)^{j-1}\zeta_{r-j}(s)\zeta(js).

For a real number with
s>1
, assume that
a_{r}=(-1)^{r}\zeta_{r}(s)
and
b_{r}=\zeta(rs)
for all
r\geq 1
. If we set
a_{0}=1
, then we have

(18)

ra_{r}=-\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{r-j}b_{j}.

To prove the required result, it is enough to write
a_{r}
in terms of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{r}
using the above recurrence formula. First, consider the formal power series

\displaystyle p(x):=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n},\quad\text{and}\quad q(x):=\sum_{n\geq 1}b_{n}x^{n}.

From recurrence formula (
18
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}na_{n}x^{n-1}=-\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n-i}b_{i}x^{n-1}=-\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{n-i}b_{i}x^{n-1}=-\sum_{i\geq 1}b_{i}\sum_{n\geq i}a_{n-i}x^{n-1}.

Now,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{n\geq 1}na_{n}x^{n-1}=-\sum_{i\geq 1}b_{i}\sum_{n\geq i}a_{n-i}x^{n-1}&=-\sum_{i\geq 1}b_{i}\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}x^{n+i-1}=-g(x)\sum_{i\geq 1}b_{i}x^{i-1}=-\frac{p(x)q(x)}{x}.\end{split}

Therefore,

p^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{p(x)q(x)}{x}.

The solution of this differential equation is given by

\displaystyle p(x)=\exp\left(-\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{b_{n}}{n}x^{n}\right).

By comparing the above equation with (
16
), we get for every integer
r\geq 1
,

\displaystyle a_{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r!}\mathbf{Y}_{r}\left(-0!b_{1},-1!b_{2},\ldots,-(r-1)!b_{r}\right).

By substituting the values of
a_{i}
’s and
b_{i}
’s in the above equation we get

(19)

\displaystyle\zeta_{r}(s)=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}\mathbf{Y}_{r}\left(-0!\zeta(s),-1!\zeta(2s),\ldots,-(r-1)!\zeta(rs)\right).

Now using (
14
), we get the desired expression (
10
). Similarly, taking
a_{r}=(-1)^{r}t_{r}(s)
and
b_{r}=t(rs)
, we get the identity

(20)

\displaystyle t_{r}(s)=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}\mathbf{Y}_{r}\left(-0!t(s),-1!t(2s),\ldots,-(r-1)!t(rs)\right),

which gives (
11
), using (
14
).
∎

Remark 3
.

As
\mathbf{Y}_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2},\ \mathbf{Y}_{3}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=x_{1}^{3}+3x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}
, we get back (
7

for

6

\displaystyle r\zeta^{\star}_{r}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\zeta^{\star}_{r-j}(s)\zeta(js)\ \text{ and }\ rt^{\star}_{r}(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}t^{\star}_{r-j}(s)t(js).

\displaystyle\zeta^{\star}_{r}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r!}\mathbf{Y}_{r}\left(0!\zeta(s),1!\zeta(2s),\ldots,(r-1)!\zeta(rs)\right).

Similarly, taking

\displaystyle t^{\star}_{r}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r!}\mathbf{Y}_{r}\left(0!t(s),1!t(2s),\ldots,(r-1)!t(rs)\right).

Using (

), we get the desired expressions (

) and (

). This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let
s>1
be a real number. It is easy to note that

(23)

\displaystyle t(s)=t^{\star}(s)=\left(1-{2^{-s}}\right)\zeta(s).

Now we use (
23
) and (
17
), together with (
15
), in (
20
) to write

\displaystyle t_{r}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}\mathbf{Y}_{r}\left(-0!t(s),-1!t(2s),\ldots,-(r-1)!t(rs)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}\mathbf{Y}_{r}\left(-0!(1-2^{-s})\zeta(s),-1!(1-2^{-2s})\zeta(2s),\ldots,-(r-1)!(1-2^{-rs})\zeta(rs)\right)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} =\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{r}{j}\frac{1}{2^{(r-j)s}}&\mathbf{Y}_{j}(-0!\zeta(s),-1!\zeta(2s),\ldots,-(j-1)!\zeta(js))\times\\
&\mathbf{Y}_{r-j}\left(0!{\zeta(s)},1!{\zeta(2s)},\ldots,(r-j-1)!{\zeta(js)}\right).\end{aligned}

Now using (
19
) and (

Corollary 1
.

Let
r\geq 1
be an integer. Then

(25)

\displaystyle\zeta_{r}(0)=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{4^{r}}\binom{2r}{r}\ \text{ and }\ \zeta^{\star}_{r}(0)=-\frac{1}{r2^{2r-1}}\binom{2r-2}{r-1}.

Proof.

Using (
19
), we have

\displaystyle\zeta_{r}(0)=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}{\mathbf{Y}_{r}\left(-0!\zeta(0),-1!\zeta(0),\ldots,-(r-1)!\zeta(0)\right)}=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}\mathbf{Y}_{r}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1!}{2},\ldots,\frac{(r-1)!}{2}\right).

By definition,

\mathbf{Y}_{r}\left(\frac{0!}{2},\frac{1!}{2},\ldots,\frac{(r-1)!}{2}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mathbf{B}_{r,k}\left(\frac{0!}{2},\frac{1!}{2},\ldots,\frac{(r-1)!}{2}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\frac{\mathbf{B}_{r,k}\left({0!},{1!},\ldots,{(r-1)!}\right)}{2^{k}}.

It is well known that (see
[
2
, Chapter 5]
)

\mathbf{B}_{r,k}\left({0!},{1!},\ldots,{(r-1)!}\right)=|s(r,k)|,

where
|s(r,k)|
is the unsigned Stirling number of the first kind given by rising factorial, i.e.,

(26)

\displaystyle x\left(x+1\right)\cdots\left(x+r-1\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}{|s(r,k)|}{x^{k}}.

Therefore, we have

\mathbf{Y}_{r}\left(\frac{0!}{2},\frac{1!}{2},\ldots,\frac{(r-1)!}{2}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\frac{|s(r,k)|}{2^{k}}=\prod_{k=0}^{r-1}\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right),

and

\mathbf{Y}_{r}\left(-\frac{0!}{2},-\frac{1!}{2},\ldots,-\frac{(r-1)!}{2}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\frac{(-1)^{k}|s(r,k)|}{2^{k}}=\prod_{k=0}^{r-1}\left(k-\frac{1}{2}\right),

Hence, we get

\zeta_{r}(0)=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}\prod_{k=0}^{r-1}\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right),\quad\zeta^{\star}_{r}(0)=\frac{1}{r!}\prod_{k=0}^{r-1}\left(k-\frac{1}{2}\right).

Simplifying the above identities, we get the desired formulae.
∎
