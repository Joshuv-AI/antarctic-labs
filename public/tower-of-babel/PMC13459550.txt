MSstatsQC-ML: A Supervised Machine Learning Approach to Monitor System Suitability and Quality Control in Mass Spectrometry-Based Proteomics
Authors: Eralp Dogu, Shantam Gupta, Roger Olivella, Eduard Sabido, Olga Vitek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mass spectrometry offers numerous ways to analyze the composition, function, and interactions of complex proteomes. Unfortunately, it suffers from the variation introduced by technological artifacts, which reduces the reproducibility and reliability of the results. In order to detect and minimize deviations from optimal performance, researchers monitor standard mixtures of proteins or peptides and various associated metrics using statistical summaries. Although this approach to monitoring multiple analytes and metrics is often beneficial, most of these methods do not scale well to multivariate situations. In this paper, we present MSstatsQC-ML, a machine learning approach to quality control that optimizes decision-making from standard mixtures with many analytes and metrics. MSstatsQC-ML combines machine learning classifiers with experimental design strategies to simulate possible suboptimal MS runs that have not yet been observed. For training the classifiers, the proposed approach incorporates informative features from metrics. Analysis of longitudinal values of each feature allows us to interpret the root causes of suboptimal performance and helps to design preventive actions. In evaluations on quality control data from discovery and targeted proteomic experiments, MSstatsQC-ML reduced error rates of detecting suboptimal performance and outperformed traditional approaches. MSstatsQC-ML is available as part of the open-source MSstatsQC R/Bioconductor package.

Introduction

Mass spectrometry-based proteomics enables the quantitative profiling of a growing number of biological samples and analytes in data-dependent acquisition (DDA), selected reaction monitoring (SRM), and data-independent acquisition (DIA) workflows. Recent studies reported up to 10,000 proteins detected with fractionated or single-shot proteome analyses − and processed millions of spectra. − Such large-scale studies must overcome the nuisance variation that inevitably accumulates throughout sample collection, storage, processing, and data acquisition. −

To ensure the quality of the resulting data sets, the efforts of system suitability (monitoring the performance of the measurement system) and quality control (QC, monitoring the quality of the acquired data) in a proteomic experiment must address three key aspects. The first aspect is the selection of a quality assurance procedure. Many large-scale workflows rely on QC runs, i.e., profiles of standard protein or peptide mixtures interleaved between biological samples, or on standards spiked into biological samples in known concentrations. − The choice of the standard, sample preparation, and test frequency depends on the analyst’s expertise and the history of the instrument’s performance. Accumulated over time, QC data provide valuable longitudinal insights into data quality.

The second aspect of quality assurance involves the generation of appropriate metrics, such as retention time, intensity, or peak shape-related parameters. − Substantial efforts have focused on identifying metrics that are relevant to instrument performance based on (1) chromatography monitoring by evaluating peak retention times, peak intensities, peak widths, and peak areas of peptide standards; (2) electrospray stability monitoring by assessing MS1 and MS/MS ion injection times and the number of MS1 and MS/MS scans; and (3) mass spectrometer performance by evaluating the mass accuracy of peptide standards. Many efforts in metric generation have focused on DDA − and SRM , workflows.

The third aspect of quality assurance is the statistical analysis of the QC data, which detects deviations from desirable performance and helps implement preventive and corrective actions. A QC run produces high-dimensional data, corresponding to multiple metrics and peptides, that are specific to the instrument and to the experimental workflow. Traditional methods of statistical process control in proteomics rely on univariate or low-dimensional multivariate summaries of data from the standards. These include arithmetic mean, moving range, Hotelling’s T 2, multivariate cumulative sum (MCUSUM), , or multivariate exponentially weighted moving averages (MEWMA). , These statistical methods learn patterns of variation in these summaries from a large-scale guide set of good quality QC runs and detect deviations from optimal performance in the subsequent runs. This is typically implemented in a visual manner using control charts. ,,, Such analyses have been proposed for DDA, ,, SRM, ,, and DIA acquisitions.

A standard QC run may monitor dozens of peptides and a number of metrics simultaneously. The numbers can increase depending on the workflow, instrument, and laboratory-specific parameters and needs. Unfortunately, the traditional statistical approaches and control charts do not scale to the multivariate nature of proteomic measurements. In particular, small-scale guide sets may not contain examples of all types of disturbances for all analytes and metrics. As a result, the decision-making process can be poorly calibrated in cases of previously unseen patterns, − leading to undetected problems or false alarms. Moreover, there is no generally accepted approach for integrating the univariate or low-dimensional summaries across the large number of peptide and protein standards, metrics, and types of deviations from optimal performance into a single decision regarding the quality of a mass spectrometry run. Although multivariate methods for proteomics have been proposed, e.g., based on unsupervised Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and anomaly detection , and based on supervised classification, − these methods do not benefit from the insights gained by advances in traditional statistical methods for system suitability and process control and use only the observed and manually annotated data to detect deviations in performance.

This work aims to overcome the above shortcomings. We propose leveraging the advantages of low-dimensional summaries used in traditional statistical process control while integrating them in a multivariate manner into single-point decision-making with supervised machine learning. A difficulty of the proposed approach is in the appropriate and investigation-specific training of the machine learning classifier. We propose to train the classifier on a combination of small-scale experimental data acquired as part of the investigation under optimal performance, augmented with synthetic data that exemplify unseen but potentially likely deviations from optimal performance. In evaluations on quality control data from discovery and targeted proteomic experiments, the proposed approach was able to detect multiple patterns (step shifts, linear drifts, and isolated outliers) of suboptimality in time and outperformed traditional methods of statistical process control. The proposed approach is implemented in the open-source R/Bioconductor package MSstatsQC.

Background

Notation

Denote t = 1,2,···,T as the time stamp of a QC run, i = 1,2,···,I as the index of a metric (such as retention time or peak area), and j = 1,2,···,J as the index of a peptide standard used for QC. Denote X tij as the output of a QC run at time point t of metric i of peptide j, stored in a three-dimensional T × I × J array X. We assume that under optimal performance, peptide j has a constant metric-specific expected value across all time points and a constant covariance across all metrics.

Univariate Summary Statistics at a Single Time Point

To capture changes in instrument performance, we employ univariate summary statistics as the initial features. Following established protocols in proteomics QC, ,, each observation is first scaled relative to the median and median absolute deviation (MAD) of the guide set. To ensure that these features are suitable for the downstream classifier and follow a near-normal distribution, we apply the Yeo–Johnson transformation. Unlike the Box-Cox transformation, the Yeo–Johnson method effectively handles zero or negative values, which are common in scaled QC metrics. Detailed mathematical formulations of these transformations are provided in the Expanded Technical Background in the Supporting Information (Section 1.1).

Univariate Summary Statistics at Multiple Time Points

Beyond single-point observations, MSstatsQC-ML incorporates summary statistics that leverage neighboring time points to detect subtle longitudinal deviations. We utilize moving ranges (MR) to monitor shifts in metric variation over time. Additionally, we implement time-weighted statistics, specifically cumulative sums (CUSUM), , to identify low-magnitude drifts in both the mean and variation of each metric that might otherwise be missed by univariate thresholds. We employ two types of CUSUM features: CUSUM m (Positive and Negative) to detect subtle increasing or decreasing trends in the expected value (mean) of a metric and CUSUM v to detect subtle shifts in the dispersion (variation) of a metric. These features allow the algorithm to detect the temporal context of each run, enabling earlier detection of instrument drifts. Detailed recurrence relations and the specific parameter settings for these statistics are provided in the Experimental Procedures in the Supporting Information (Section S2).

Decisions from Univariate Summary Statistics

The univariate summary statistics are typically visualized in the form of graphs, called control charts, where the x-axis is the time stamp of the QC run and the y-axis is a univariate summary statistic. The charts served as the basis for quality control decisions. For each metric i and peptide j, we denote a decision regarding the quality of the measurement at time t, with a class label C tij . C tij = 0 when the summary statistic is below a predefined threshold (indicating no evidence of a failed run for this metric and peptide). C tij = 1 when the summary statistic exceeds the threshold (indicating a failed run for this metric and peptide).

To determine the decision threshold, we view [X 1ij ,···,X Tij ] as a random three-dimensional sample from a probability distribution f (typically Normal). The thresholds are chosen to control the false positive rate, i.e., the probability of a measurement being wrongly labeled as failed. Since the true probability distribution f is unknown, the approach requires us to estimate its parameters. The parameters are estimated from a reference or guide set of size T g , available before collecting the QC data, assumed to contain only good quality runs, and summarized in the T g × I × J matrix X g .

This control-chart-based decision-making is difficult to scale to multiple peptides, metrics, and patterns of deviations. For example, a workflow monitoring 8 peptides and 4 metrics per peptide would require 8 control charts to monitor changes in the mean of the metrics and 8 additional control charts to monitor changes in the variability of each metric. Although control charts may be visually summarized in the form of heatmaps, , heat maps do not formally control the overall error rate of false alarms.

Multivariate Summary Statistics

Many industries use more formal decision-making from multiple analytes and metrics based on low-dimensional multivariate summaries. For example, Hotelling’s T 2 summary statistic summarizes multiple metrics of a peptide calculated by comparing the deviation from the mean vector, scaled by the inverse of the covariance matrix, to detect shifts in the mean across multiple metrics. When the statistic for a peptide j at time t exceeds a prespecified threshold, all the metrics for that peptide are considered failed. The use of such summaries has previously been proposed in mass spectrometry-based proteomics. Researchers also proposed more complex time-weighted multivariate summaries. Unfortunately, these multivariate statistics do not scale to aggregate the information from multiple peptides.

Decisions from Multivariate Summary Statistics

Decisions from multivariate summary statistics are made similarly to univariate statistics by displaying them as a function of time stamps of the QC run on a control chart. Unfortunately, control charts with multivariate summaries, such as T 2, have several drawbacks. First, T 2 summaries assume that the metrics follow a multivariate normal distribution. As a result, T 2 values are sensitive to deviations from this assumption. Second, multivariate summaries are effective only in certain types of deviations from optimal performance. For example, T 2 is effective in detecting large isolated shifts, while time-weighted control charts are effective in detecting subtle drifts. Finally, multivariate summaries require the estimation of more parameters of the probability distribution f and need a large guide set. In general, control charts from multivariate summaries are useful only in experiments with a moderate number of analytes and metrics.

Supervised and Unsupervised Machine Learning Methods

Supervised and unsupervised machine learning (ML) methods , classify QC runs as pass and fail and can overcome the challenges associated with univariate and low-dimensional summary statistics. Older supervised ML methods relied on logistic regression. Subsequently, the R package TargetedMSQC implemented support vector machines (SVM), logistic regression, random forest (RF), and K-nearest neighbor (KNN) for targeted mass spectrometry-based workflows. A supervised synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) coupled with an XGBoost algorithm was proposed in isotopically labeled experiments with previously documented failed runs. This method generates data for the minority class using interpolation between similar data points; thus, it needs at least a small set of suboptimal runs and cannot generate data for unseen patterns of suboptimality. An unsupervised local outlier detection method considered the similarity between runs based on a set of metrics and helped detect unusual runs without requiring a guide set. Similarly, QC-ART used a dynamic linear model with unsupervised learning to address typical instrument-related variations over time. The Seaop software analyzed QC data with ensembles of unsupervised learning methods. A visual semisupervised method to classify QC runs for DDA acquisitions has also been proposed.

The biggest challenge for the ML methods above is the need for ad hoc training of a model for each experimental setting. Methods must learn from a training set of good examples of historically documented optimal (and, in many cases, suboptimal) QC runs. Ideally, these examples are (1) accurate, i.e., include correctly annotated QC runs, (2) balanced, i.e., include enough examples for both optimal and suboptimal QC runs, and (3) complete, i.e., include QC runs that reflect all types of deviations from optimal performance. Creating such experimental training data sets is difficult in proteomics, where some types of failure are rare and where we cannot control the responsiveness of the metrics to various types of failure. Therefore, experimental training sets are often insufficient for training a high-accuracy classifier, even with a relatively large number of QC runs.

This paper investigates the use of a modified supervised machine learning method for quality control of proteomics and addresses the above weaknesses. First, to overcome the difficulties of training ML models, particularly in detecting previously unobserved patterns of failure, we revisit the approach of synthetic data generation. Similar to ref , we oversample previously observed patterns of optimal and suboptimal behavior. Our oversampling approach mimics potential failure types and generates random patterns. Moreover, the proposed approach uses statistical principles of experimental design to augment the training set with additional synthetic QC data, train the model on the synthetic data, and enable the detection of plausible but previously unobserved patterns of failure in real-time QC runs. Finally, unlike ML methods previously used in proteomics, the proposed approach implements feature engineering to include traditional univariate statistical summaries as features in the ML models. We show that feature engineering boosts our ability to detect deviations from optimal performance, such as subtle drifts.

Algorithm

MSstatsQC-ML combines synthetic data generation, feature engineering, and supervised machine learning to estimate the probability of QC run failure. The algorithm is overviewed in Figure in Supporting Information (Section S2) and detailed below.

Overview of the proposed MSstatsQC-ML workflow. The MSstatsQC-ML workflow consists of two main phases: training and deployment. In the training phase, input data are preprocessed and augmented with simulated QC runs in order to cover possible failure types. Additional statistical features are also engineered in this phase. Next, the simulated data set is used for model development. The hyperparameters are tuned based on random search, and the model is trained, validated, and tested in this phase. In the deployment phase, the trained model is deployed to characterize the experimental data in real-time monitoring. MSstatsQC-ML reports the performance of the classifier and annotates the experimental data. Numbers in circles indicate algorithms detailed in Supporting Information Section S2.

Input Similarly to the approaches relying on univariate or multivariate summary statistics, MSstatsQC-ML takes as input data sets from DDA, DIA, or SRM workflows, and QC metrics such as retention time, mass accuracy, etc. MSstatsQC-ML can analyze various types of metrics, including quantitative and categorical, that are specific to a given laboratory, instrument, and experiment type. The metrics can be produced by data processing tools such as Skyline, MaxQuant, Spectronaut, and QCloud2 or other tools that detect and identify analytes and quantify their mass spectrometric signals.

The required input is a guide data set, i.e., a set of measurements at times t = 1,2,···,T g of metrics i = 1,2,···,I of a peptide j = 1,2,···,J, summarized in a T g × I × J array X g . The guide set contains high-quality QC runs, typically determined by an expert. An optional input to MSstatsQC-ML is a suboptimal data set, summarized in a T s × I × J array X s . This data set exemplifies suboptimal QC runs, where all the measurements are also annotated by an expert as good or bad.

Step 1: Preparing training set MSstatsQC-ML processes the metric values using a robust center-scale function (that outputs Z tij ) and then applies the Yeo–Johnson power transformation (that converts Z tij into W tij as in eq S1 in Supporting Information Section S1) as part of the preprocessing step in Figure . We then engineer each metric to add additional temporal features that can detect various problems. This two-step transformation approach is used in the guide set X g by preprocessing each metric separately per peptide j and across all the time points to contribute to Dengg in Figure . The preprocessing algorithm is also used to generate synthetic suboptimal time points Dengs , which are based on random disturbances created from X g and distortion patterns given by a 2 I factorial design matrix DM. In order to augment the experimental training set with synthetic observations, the algorithm takes as input the number of time points in the guide set T g , the simulation size T, i.e., the number of runs to be generated synthetically per peptide), and disturbance limits, i.e., boundaries a and b of possible disturbances on the standardized scale. Defaults for these parameters are set to T = 500, a = 2, and b = 5 to mimic large shifts in instrument performance. The disturbance limits a and b in Algorithm 2 are defined relative to the baseline standard deviation (σ) of the guide set, ensuring that the classifier’s sensitivity is calibrated to the inherent noise of the instrument. Specifically, a small shift [1.5, 2] targets failures beginning at 1.5σ and extending to 2σ, while a large shift [2, 5] covers more pronounced deviations up to 5σ. For instance, in a mass accuracy context with an observed σ of 1 ppm, these settings correspond to shifts of 1.5–2 ppm and 2–5 ppm, respectively. This standardized approach allows MSstatsQC-ML to maintain consistent statistical rigor across different instruments and metrics, regardless of their absolute units.

The two data structures Dengg and Dengs of size ((3 · (2 I – 1) · T) × (5 · I + 1)) are the basis of training. Here, the constant 3 represents three different disturbance types (step to detect sudden changes in the mean of a metric, linear to detect upward/downward drift in the mean of a metric, and fluctuation to detect an increase in the variability of a metric). Constant 2 represents a binary outcome for a time point of PASS or FAIL, which is also used in the factorial design matrix DM to contribute to generation of random patterns of failures in Dengs . The same dimensions are shared in both Dengg and Dengs to ensure class balance. Constant 5 is the number of features per metric. Each metric is defined by raw measurement (for example, retention time, mass accuracy, etc.), moving range, cumulative sum for positive drifts, cumulative sum for negative drifts, and cumulative sum for variation. Similarly, a data preprocessing step is applied to the suboptimal data set X s to convert this data structure into Wengs of size ((3· T s ) × (5 · I + 1)). If available, this data structure is combined into the training data D eng along with Dengg and Dengs . The details of the data preprocessing procedures and algorithms are given in Supporting Information (Supplementary Figure S1 and Algorithms S1–S3).

Step 2: Training Random Forest MSstatsQC-ML uses random forest as the default classifier. To train, develop, and test the random forest model, D is split into three data sets (default 60% of time points for training, 20% for model development, and 20% for model validation), as illustrated in Figure , training step. The hyperparameters of the random forest, specifically the number of decision trees and the number of features randomly sampled as candidates at each node per decision tree, are autotuned by the random discrete parameter search using a Cartesian grid search strategy to perform a random search of all the combinations of your hyperparameters. The stopping rules for this search are based on AUC values by adding a stopping tolerance of 0.002, which specifies the tolerance value by which a model must improve before training ceases.

As part of developing the algorithm, we investigated various alternatives to the random forest classifier, such as neural networks. The random forest-based approach provided a shorter run time and was less computationally expensive. Moreover, it was robust to missing values and outliers and could handle lower degrees of imbalanced class labels. Finally, since understanding the factors contributing to a suboptimal time point is critical in practice, the interpretability of the results helped identify the root causes of suboptimality, as described below.

Step 3: Detecting failures in new runs The model needs to be trained separately for every data set, as shown in Figure . After a valid model with high performance is obtained, it is deployed to predict new QC runs in the unlabeled experimental data set that we expect to contain the same metrics as the historical data. We first apply the same data-preprocessing approach (Algorithm S1 in Supporting Information) and then calculate the same SPC-based additional statistical features per peptide as in Algorithms S3 in the Supporting Information. The experimental data set per peptide is then ready for label prediction. The summaries from the prediction step flag poor-quality runs per peptide and display them on a heatmap called decision map. A decision map includes a row for each peptide and shows the probability of failure per time point for this particular peptide. The color palette is defined to represent a gradient between 0 (white) and 1 (red). Therefore, darker regions of the plot indicate a higher probability of failure. Each probability per time point per peptide is predicted by the trained model for the particular deployment data set aggregated over all of the metrics.

Although insights regarding the sources of suboptimal performance are critical, most metrics, such as accuracy, F-1 score, etc., cannot be used for explaining the reasons for a specific prediction. MSstatsQC-ML uses a heatmap called root causes map for explaining the outcome of models. It is built from the values of the features per metric per time point and per peptide and highlights the measurements of each statistical feature over time per peptide. The colors represent the relative intensity of centered/scaled metrics and engineered features from the metrics. Higher values are darker and indicate relationships to the root cause of the problem. Lower values are lighter and represent optimal performance.

The inclusion of all statistical features per metriccomprising the transformed raw measurement, moving range, and three distinct cumulative sumsrepresents a strategic design choice intended to ensure comprehensive sensitivity to diverse failure modes. Rather than relying on a dominant subset of variables, this approach ensures that the classifier is trained to monitor all features, each of which addresses a theoretically distinct pattern of suboptimality. Consequently, the resulting root cause maps serve as a dynamic and local form of importance, allowing users to identify the specific drivers of failure for each individual run in real time.

Output MSstatsQC-ML outputs the probability of failure of each peptide per run aggregated over the metrics. The results of this assessment, including visualizations that help to identify the starting points of poor performance, are summarized in a report.

Implementation

The proposed approach is implemented in the open-source R/Bioconductor software MSstatsQC, available at DOI: 10.18129/B9.bioc.MSstatsQC. It is offered as a stand-alone solution or can be integrated with automated pipelines such as Panorama AutoQC or QCloud2.

The implementation takes as input a guide data set and an optional suboptimal data set. The rows of the arrays must be annotated with t as the descriptor of AcquiredTime of the QC run, as well as important facts (such as calibration or column change) about a particular QC run may be included as the annotations column. Each array must be annotated with Peptide or another analyte for which the metrics are collected. The data sets need to be in long format, where inputs are AcquiredTime, Peptide, Annotations, and QC Metrics. QC metrics can be any QC metric of interest, denoted in columns following the Annotations.

The main functions include MSstatsQC.ML.trainR­() for training and MSstatsQC.ML.deployR­() for deployment. The h2o package in R is used to train the model and evaluate its performance. At a minimum, h2o requires using Java 8–14, 64-bit JDK, and R version 3 or later. Our experience indicates that 50 to 200 trees yield a good balance between the area under curve (AUC) and processing time for experimental data sets. MSstatsQC.ML.trainR­() allows users to specify simulation size and the number of folds for cross-validation. However, as the method includes random hyperparameter search, instead of increasing the number of folds, we recommend increasing the simulation sizes, where we observed faster computational times. Our experience indicates that simulation sizes over n = 500 for each problem type give acceptable performance (AUC > 0.9). We provide an additional R function called MSstatsQC.ML.sim.size.detectR() to investigate the best simulation size. Since we label each peptide per QC run, the random forest model can handle individual missing metrics.

The user should upload a deployment data set in the same format as the input data, where label predictions are needed. The implementation outputs a decision map that shows the probability of failures per time point per peptide aggregated over all metrics and root cause maps that show the values of features (raw and engineered) per metric per peptide over time.

We evaluated the performance of the method on a PC equipped with a 1.6 GHz CPU and 8 GB of RAM. We tested the algorithm with data sets that include up to ten metrics and eight peptides. We observed 3992 and 7291 s to train the model for simulation sizes of 100 and 500, respectively, while the computation time was 44 and 170 s with five metrics and eight peptides for the same simulation setting.

Results

MSstatsQC-ML Decreased the Rate of Incorrect Decisions, as Compared to Control Charts

We used synthetic data sets to demonstrate how MSstatsQC-ML effectively detected deviations from optimal performance. The classifier was trained based on the DDA-QCloud guide set. Synthetic drifts in RT and mass accuracy were generated for three peptides: SLHTLFGDELCK, VPQVSTPTLVEVSR, and YICDNQDTISSK between the 25th and 50th QC runs (Figure ).

In evaluations on the synthetic data set, MSstatsQC-ML demonstrated significant improvements over traditional univariate monitoring approaches, such as those implemented in MSstatsQC 2.0. While previous iterations provide decision maps that summarize independent control chartssuch as CUSUM and moving rangethey often necessitate manual heuristics, such as requiring a FAIL signal from more than 50% of monitored peptides, to mitigate the excessive false alarms inherent in monitoring many analytes separately in Supplementary Figure S2a. This figure shows only warning signs for RT, while a considerably large shift was observed in >30% of the analytes (shown in river and radar plots in Supplementary Figures S2b and c. In contrast, MSstatsQC-ML consolidates all peptide-metric features into a single calibrated probability score, enabling the detection of subtle, subthreshold failures that do not cross traditional heuristic boundaries. Furthermore, the transition from binary labels to a continuous failure probability allows for the identification of shift types; for example, a gradual increase in probability over time effectively characterizes a linear drift. This diagnostic information is otherwise unavailable in univariate frameworks without intensive investigation of individual charts.

Next, we also analyzed synthetic QC test data with random forest without simulation and feature engineering (Figure b). The heat map shows the probability of failure versus time for the artificial QC data set. Dark red tiles show higher probability of failure. While we observed higher probability of failure for the peptides that were shifted, such as SLHTLFGDELCK, we also observed a higher rate of false positives for the observations without alterations, such as the time between the 1st and 25th QC runs for peptide HLVDEPQNLIK. Moreover, we observed a higher rate of false negatives in the altered measurements. We trained the random forest model using simulations of optimal and suboptimal runs (1000 simulations for each shift type). Here, no feature engineering was used. The results are summarized in Figure c. Apparently, the simulation helped us decrease false negatives in the altered regions. However, we were unable to address the type of disturbance as all altered observations had similar degrees of probability of failure. We trained the random forest model, allowing only feature engineering in the training set (Figure d) for an alternative approach. This approach significantly decreased the rate of false positives but did not help with false negatives. We were only able to detect a clear pattern of suboptimality in peptide SLHTLFGDELCK (between the 25th and 50th QC runs). Other types of disturbances were not detected. Finally, we combined simulation and feature engineering as proposed in MSstatsQC-ML and trained the random forest model for the same data set (Figure e). We observed proper discrimination between optimal and suboptimal measurements in peptides SLHTLFGDELCK, VPQVSTPTLVEVSR, and YICDNQDTISSK. The prespecified patterns (linear drift, fluctuation, and step changes) were also clearly observed. Additionally, we were able to control the rates of false positives and false negatives. Here, the change point (25th run) and peptides with retention time and mass accuracy shifts were clearly separated. It is quite clear that YICDNQDTISSK experiences a subtle drift as the color slowly darkens, SLHTLFGDELCK experiences a step shift as a sudden color change occurs, and VPQVSTPTLVEVSR experiences a subtle drift for a short time and tends to go back to its initial state after the drift. Other peptides show no sign of suboptimality, as they were not altered during the synthetic data generation.

Larger Simulation Sizes Improved Detection of Known False Alarms in DDA-QCloud and SRM-QCloud Data Sets

Selection of the simulation size is a major concern for simulation-based algorithms. Moreover, the performance of the algorithm depends on the guide set size and the magnitude of shifts. In order to analyze these effects, we conducted performance analysis for different simulation sizes (T), guide set sizes in number of QC runs, and two different magnitudes of shift: small shift (a = 1.5, b = 2) and large shift (a = 2, b = 5). Each performance experiment was repeated 3 times, and mean test AUC performances and standard deviation of AUC values are summarized in Figure for DDA-QCloud data and Supporting Information Figure S2 for SRM-QCloud data. The observed values are also provided in Supporting Information Tables S1 and S2. Higher mean AUC values and lower standard deviations are obtained, especially for the larger simulation sizes, larger guide sets, and large shift magnitudes. The performance of the method is relatively low (lower mean AUC and higher variability) with small simulation sizes, small guide sets, and small magnitudes of shift.

Effect of simulation size for the DDA-QCloud data set: (a) AUC performance for small changes (a = 1.5, b = 2). (b) AUC performance for large changes (a = 2, b = 5). Facets summarize results for mean AUC, standard deviation (SD) of AUC, guide set size, and simulation size.

While the performance of the classifier improves with larger reference data, we observed that high detection accuracy (AUC > 0.90) can be maintained even with enough experimental data. Based on our evaluations across various experimental scales, we recommend a minimum of 20 high-quality QC runs to establish an initial baseline for new instruments or methods. In scenarios where the guide set is small, increasing the simulation size (e.g., T ≥ 500) is critical to provide the model with sufficient synthetic examples of failure patterns to compensate for the lack of experimental diversity. This flexibility allows MSstatsQC-ML to be deployed shortly after instrument setup without requiring an extensive historical record.

Conclusions

Analysis of QC data acquired on a daily basis helps to discover underlying causes of undesirable variation. As the data structures of longitudinal QC acquisitions are inherently complex, high-dimensional, and include various data types and violations of statistical assumptions, the traditional practice of monitoring each metric and analyte separately is often ineffective, suffers from multiplicity of decisions, and increases false alarms. MSstatsQC-ML effectively analyzes such complex longitudinal QC data and transforms such highly multivariate information into actionable strategies for quality improvement. The approach presented in this paper helps detect different types of existing and potential problems.

MSstatsQC-ML relies on a guide set (examples of good performance) and annotated runs (examples of suboptimal performance) if available. Since the method relies on a set of baseline data, it is highly important to generate high-quality optimal QC data and to annotate historical problems in detail to generate high-quality suboptimal synthetic data. The guide set can be updated regularly to dynamically reflect accumulated system information to the classifier. Updating the guide set requires a full retraining of the model rather than an incremental update. This retraining process is also essential following significant instrument maintenancesuch as a column changeto ensure that the classifier accurately reflects the updated system suitability baseline and maintains its predictive rigor. MSstatsQC-ML offers high flexibility in metric selection, allowing users to tailor the monitoring process to the specific strengths or weaknesses of their instrumentation. If a particular metric is known to be unreliable, it can be excluded from the input data set; conversely, new quantitative or categorical metrics can be added to the workflow. It should be noted that because the classifier integrates all features into a consolidated decision boundary, any modification to the set of input metrics necessitates a full retraining of the model. This ensures the failure probabilities remain accurately calibrated to the current feature space, replacing the need for manual, per-metric threshold adjustments common in univariate frameworks.

Two key features of MSstatsQC-ML are the use of univariate statistics as additional features and the augmentation of the training set with synthetic examples of previously unseen disturbances. This blends classical principles of longitudinal monitoring with simulation and enables sensitive discovery of unseen patterns of suboptimality, even with a small-scale guide set. The proposed approach is easily transferable between instruments, laboratories, and experiments by retraining the classifier on relevant past examples. As a supplementary example, we applied MSstatsQC-ML to the CPTAC SRM system suitability data set (Study 9.1 Site 54), previously analyzed in ref . The results are presented in Supporting Information Figure S4. The guide set for this data set consisted of only 20 runs, with a known shift occurring around run 20 out of 46 total runs. Despite the limited guide set, MSstatsQC-ML successfully detected the shift, and the root cause maps correctly identified changes in the total area. The details of this example are available in the MSstatsQC vignette on Bioconductor.

MSstatsQC-ML successfully operationalizes high-dimensional machine learning for routine analytical diagnostics by coupling global feature attribution with localized anomaly mapping. While global feature importance confirms that the classifier actively leverages a comprehensive suite of base and engineered metrics during synthetic training (e.g., Supporting Information Figure S4b), such aggregate global summaries are insufficient for day-to-day instrument troubleshooting. By integrating run-specific root cause maps (e.g., Supporting Information Figure S4c–S4f) for deployment, the framework successfully translates complex, multidimensional anomaly scores into localized, actionable insights, empowering researchers to pinpoint the exact source of an instrument failure during routine quality control.

The MSstatsQC-ML framework highlights a critical distinction between synthetic training design and empirical deployment. Although real-world LC-MS failures exhibit high cross-metric covariance, simulating this correlation during training artificially restricts the learned decision boundary to a multicollinear subspace, severely impairing model generalizability. By deliberately employing an orthogonal perturbation strategy during synthetic data generation, we force the classifier to robustly partition the entire multidimensional feature space. As validated by the empirical QCloud data sets, this orthogonal training paradigm effectively anchors the decision boundary, yielding a highly stable model capable of accurately classifying the complex, correlated multivariate anomalies observed in routine practice.

One limitation of the proposed approach is the set of predefined hyperparameters (number of trees, number of folds, and maximum depth of a tree) of the random forest. Users can easily tune the hyperparameters using the functions provided in the R package. Another potential limitation is the training time of the classifier. Our evaluations showed that an increase in the number of metrics and the number of historical QC runs exponentially increases the training times. As an extension to the current implementation, the selection of a subset of features and a subset of runs can reduce the computational burden for large data sets.
