Chiral chromophore engineered donor for constructing circularly polarized organic long-persistent luminescence exciplex
Authors: Xin Yan, Yunliang Ji, Zhisheng Gao, Hengyu Cao, Jianwei Li, Fei He, Peng Zhang, Minyue Cai, Guangyao Guo, Huanhuan Li, Hui Li, Gaozhan Xie, Ye Tao, Runfeng Chen, Yizhou Zhang, Wei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Constructing chiral organic long-persistent luminescence materials has garnered considerable attention owing to their extraordinary ultralong emission duration but has thus far achieved limited success. Herein, we present a straightforward approach to develop chiral chromophore engineered donor for exploring chiral exciplex system, which exhibits a blue circularly polarized exciplex emission peaked at ~ 440 nm, as well as an ultralong yellow organic long-persistent luminescence from the triplet emission of chiral chromophore with emission peak at 540 nm and ultralong duration of 90 minutes. Particularly, the exciplex system achieves mirror-symmetric chiroptical signal with the dissymmetry factor of 7.8×10-3. Theoretical and experimental analyses reveal that the phenomenon is attributed to the charge transfer process that facilitates exciplex formation, followed by effective energy transfer from the exciplex to the chiral chromophore. The developed exciplex enables the applications of multi-level information encryption and three-dimensional display objects. This work presents a significant insight into advancing organic chiral afterglow materials with ultralong duration, unlocking broad application potentials across various domains.

Introduction

The development of circularly polarized organic afterglow materials that exhibit simultaneous circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) and afterglow emission has recently attracted tremendous attention1–8. Because of the unique advantages, including chiral emission and long lifetime of triplet excitons, these materials hold wide promise for applications ranging from data encryption, chiral afterglow display to intelligent optoelectronic devices9–15. Numerous types of circularly polarized organic afterglow materials have been reported, which lean upon varied strategies including host-guest doping16,17, polymerization18–20, chiral crystal engineering21, as well as crystallization and clusters-inducement22. Although substantial processes have been made, the afterglow durations typically last for several seconds to a few minutes due to the fast triplet exciton decay process, and the intersystem crossing (ISC) is also not favorable to generate the triplet excitons because of a large singlet (S1)-triplet (T1) energy gap23,24 (Fig. 1a, left). Organic long-persistent luminescence (OLPL), emitting from the exciplex of donor-acceptor systems, has proven to be an effective strategy for generating hour-long afterglow emissions25–31. Principally, photo-induced charge transfer (CT) and separation (CS) produce the long-lived radical cations and anions (Fig. 1b); then, the radical cations and anions gradually recombine to enable the OLPL phenomenon; moreover, the exciplex nature endows a rapid ISC and reverse ISC process32–34. Recent advancements in OLPL have enabled materials with diverse emission colors and tunable durations through various strategies, which include crystallization29, host–guest doping35,36, carbon dot fabrication33,37, and polymerization strategies38,39. Despite attaining great success, the integration of CPL into OLPL emission for the creation of circularly polarized -OLPL (CP-OLPL) materials with ultralong durations has yet to be fully realized. This challenge is largely attributable to the lack of effective design strategies for combining CPL characteristics with the long-lasting emission properties of OLPL systems.

Notably, a chiral exciplex sensitizer was successfully developed by doping a chiral electron-donating molecule into an achiral acceptor; the sensitized CPL emission from an achiral emitter was achieved using this constructed chiral exciplex sensitizer as host40. Vitalized by this chiral exciplex host, we hypothesized that a CP-OLPL exciplex could be established by designing chiral donors and/or acceptors, thereby enabling the realization of chiral persistent luminescence with ultralong durations. In this work, a pair of chiral donors (Fig. 1c) has been synthesized by attaching the donor (D) of N, N-dimethylaniline (DMB) to the chiral chromophore (C) of an axial chiral binaphthol derivative (R(S)-2OBN). Principally, N, N-dimethylaniline moiety is a typical donor to fabricate the exciplex, which is crucial for facilitating efficient CT to produce stable radical cations and anions when doped into bis(diphenylphosphoryl)dibenzo[b,d]thiophene (PPT) acceptor26,28. The exciplex film could be excited by UV light and the electron could be easily separated through CT (Fig. 1b, ①) that arise between the donor and acceptor; then, a continual process of CS (②) and charge recombination (CR, ③) are occurred to produce OLPL exciplex emission41,42; finally, the luminescent chiral binaphthol derivative serves as an energy harvester to capture the OLPL exciplex emission through energy transfer (ET, ④) from 1CT of exciplex to the triplet state of chiral donor (T1D), yielding an exceptionally CP-OLPL emission from chiral binaphthol chromophore (Fig. 1a, right). Excitingly, the resulting chiral exciplex achieved a blue CPL emission with a luminescent peak at ~440 nm and dissymmetry factor ( | glum | ) of 7.8×10-3, as well as an ultralong yellow CP-OLPL with main emission peaks at ~540 nm, |glum| of 4.7×10-3 and durations up to 90 min. This work successfully proposes a chiral donor engineering strategy to build a valid CP-OLPL exciplex, paving the way to cultivate chiral afterglow materials with ultralong durations.

Results

Preparation and characterization of chiral donor

A pair of chiral chromophore engineered donor was easily constructed by attaching R(S)-2OBN to DMB through a one-step Suzuki coupling reaction (Supplementary Fig. 1). The chemical structures and purities of these compounds were confirmed by 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, high-resolution mass spectra (Supplementary Figs. 2–10), as well as chiral high-performance liquid chromatogram spectra (Supplementary Fig. 11). The enantiomeric excess (ee) values of R-2OBNMB and S-2OBNMB, calculated as 99.95% and 99.25% respectively, are sufficiently high for photophysical investigations. The R(S)-2OBNMB exhibits outstanding thermal stability (Supplementary Fig. 12), as evidenced by decomposition temperature values exceeding 357 °C and a melting point surpassing 115 °C. The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels of R(S)-2OBNMB were measured by cyclic voltammogram (CV) and ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy43 (UPS) measurements (Supplementary Figs. 13–14), respectively. Both the CV and UPS measured energy levels support the formation of an exciplex between R(S)-2OBNMB and PPT (Supplementary Table 1, Figs. 13b and 14d). This result implies a viable potential for the formation of an exciplex between R(S)-2OBNMB and PPT. To further understand the exciplex formation ability between R(S)-2OBNMB and PPT, natural transition orbitals (NTOs) analyses were calculated. The ground-state structure of the exciplex is optimized through alignment between the electrostatic potential (ESP) maximum of R-2OBNMB and the ESP minimum of PPT44,45 (Supplementary Table 2 and Fig. 15). The highest occupied NTO is distributed over R-2OBNMB, while the lowest occupied NTO is located on the PPT; these results indicate the feasibility of generating an exciplex (Supplementary Fig. 16).

Photophysical and theoretical investigations of chiral donor

The photophysical properties of donor R(S)-2OBNMB were thoroughly investigated in tetrahydrofuran (THF) solution. R(S)-2OBNMB exhibits a maximum absorption peak around 320 nm (Supplementary Fig. 17), and a blue fluorescence emission at 420 nm (Fig. 2a). Under cryogenic conditions at 77 K, vibrant yellow luminescence is observed in delayed photoluminescence (PL) spectra, characterized by prominent emission peaks at 570 nm and lifetimes of ~2 s in R- and S-2OBNMB (Fis. 2a, b). Notably, R(S)-2OBNMB displays almost identical steady-state photoluminescence (SSPL) and delayed PL spectra to those of the chiral binaphthol chromophore, indicating that the introduction of DMB has a minimal impact on the optical properties of chiral chromophore (Supplementary Fig. 18). This limited effect on the photophysical properties was further confirmed by the theoretical calculations, showing minimal distributions of frontier molecular orbital on the DMB (Supplementary Fig. 16). Photophysical properties of CP-OLPL exciplex

The CP-OLPL exciplex R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT was easily prepared by heating a mixture of R(S)-2OBNMB and PPT in a molar ratio of 1:99 under a nitrogen atmosphere. The 1H NMR spectrum of the R-2OBNMB sample heated at 240 °C (Supplementary Fig. 20) is very similar to the pristine sample, ruling out the possibility of decomposition at high temperatures. The fabricated films indicate an amorphous feature as verified by the discernible diffraction peaks (Supplementary Fig. 12c). R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films exhibit the ultraviolet absorption band peaking at ~320 nm, which is similar to that of PPT acceptor (Supplementary Figs. 21–22), specifying that the absorption of the exciplex films mainly originates from PPT. Moreover, no additional peaks in the range of 400–600 nm are found (Supplementary Fig. 22), excluding the absorption from charge-transfer complex species. The SSPL spectra of R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films show a similar emission peak at ~440 nm, accompanied by two distinct peaks in the 500 to 600 nm range (Fig. 3a). After a 10 ms delay, the main emission band at 440 nm almost disappears, while the peaks at ~540 nm and ~576 nm become dominant. In comparison to the SSPL spectra of the R(S)-2OBNMB and PPT (Fig. 2a), the 440 nm emission peak of R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films is red-shifted. Although this emission peak at 440 nm closely resembles the phosphorescence of PPT, its intensity remains unchanged under air and nitrogen (N2) atmospheres (Supplementary Figs. 23-24). This result clearly excludes the influence of PPT phosphorescence46. Furthermore, the delayed PL spectra of R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films are identical to those of the delayed PL spectra of R(S)-2OBNMB at 77 K. These findings suggest that the 440 nm (2.79 eV) emission in R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films arises from the exciplex formation due to the CT between R(S)-2OBNMB and PPT (Fig. 3a), while the peaks at ~540 nm and ~576 nm are attributed to the triplet emission of R(S)-2OBNMB28,46. Compared to the SSPL and delayed PL spectra of 1 mol% R-2OBNMB/PPT in THF solution (10-5 M) at 77 K (Supplementary Fig. 25), the 1 mol% R-2OBNMB/PPT film shows an obvious exciplex emission with much enhanced triplet emission. The largely improved delayed PL emission under N₂ than that in oxygen (O₂) atmosphere, indicating again that OLPL emission originates from the triplet excitons of R(S)-2OBNMB. The double logarithmic fitting of the decay profiles of R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films adheres to the Debye-Edwards law (t-m)42,46–48, with the initial segment corresponding to the fast exponential phosphorescence of inherent the triplet emission of R(S)-2OBNMB and the second segment representing the LPL emission with typical power-law emission from the recombination of long-lived CS states28,42 (Fig. 3b). Notably, a yellow afterglow photograph can be taken for more than 90 minutes, and the OLPL emission spectra of 1 mol% R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films were detectable for the same duration (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Fig. 27). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 28, the encapsulated 1 mol% R-2OBNMB/PPT film exhibits acceptable stability, maintaining 74% of its OLPL intensity and an OLPL duration exceeding 1000 s after 15 days of storage under ambient conditions. Moreover, the SSPL and delayed PL intensities show only a moderate decrease, retaining ~70% of the initial value after 4 h irradiating with 365 nm UV light at a power of 100 W (Supplementary Fig. 29), which suggests a good stability of the doped film49. Despite non-radiative decay promoted by water and oxygen, the unencapsulated R-2OBNMB/PPT film maintains detectable OLPL emission but shows a significantly shortened duration time when immersed in water (Supplementary Fig. 30).

To confirm the existence of radical cations and anions formed from the CS states, electron spin resonance measurements were performed, which shows a strong signal after the UV light excitation (Fig. 3e). More importantly, the CPL spectra of R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films reveal an intense and mirror signal with prominent emission peaks at ~440 nm with corresponding |glum| of 7.8×10-3 (Fig. 3f, g), respectively. Compared to the glum of R(S)-2OBNMB in solution (Supplementary Fig. 19), the enhanced CPL emission in R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films is due to the rigid matrix formed by PPT, which effectively prevents the non-radiative transition of R(S)-2OBNMB. Besides, both the CPL and DC spectra (Fig. 3f) clearly show a shoulder peak at 540 nm with glum of 4.7×10-3, which corresponds to the OLPL signal observed in the exciplex film, confirming the presence of a chiral triplet emission component. Expectantly, these results experimentally indicate the successful creation of a CP-OLPL exciplex with a duration of up to one hour. More excitingly, the duration of the fabricated R-2OBNMB/PPT exciplex is longer than that of the three-component doped system containing chiral R-2OBN chromophore, DMB donor, and PPT acceptor, which shows the duration of ~1000 s (Supplementary Fig. 31). This suggests the merits of a chiral chromophore-engineered strategy for developing CP-OLPL system.

To verify the exciplex emission nature of R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films, the photophysical properties of R(S)-2OBNMB/PMMA films were studied. The SSPL and delayed PL spectra of these films display a fluorescence emission at ~420 nm with a short lifetime of 7.13 ns and a low-temperature phosphorescence emission at ~535 nm, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 32–33), which are similar to those of R(S)-2OBNMB in THF solution. The time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) profile of S-2OBNMB/PMMA film only exhibits a gradual decrease in intensity over time, without the emergence of any new emission peaks (Supplementary Figs. 34-35). The relative CPL intensity at ~540 nm and corresponding g value show a significant enhancement at 77 K compared to room temperature (Supplementary Fig. 36). This observed enhancement may stem from the severe restriction of molecular thermal motion under cryogenic conditions (77 K)50. Besides, this emission peak is identical to that of the triplet emission of the R(S)-2OBNMB donor (Figs. 2a and 3a), suggesting the presence of delayed CPL emission. To eliminate the influence of fluorescence on the CP-OLPL of 1 mol% R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films, a long-wavelength ( > 450 nm) passing optical filter was used to take the CPL spectra. The CPL spectra exhibit a distinct emission peak at ~540 nm with a maximum glum of 4.7 × 10⁻³ (Supplementary Fig. 37). To further demonstrate the long-lived nature, the delayed CPL spectra were recorded. After 200 ms delayed time, no fluorescence peak is achieved and in contrast, a distinct emission peak at ~540 nm is found51 (Supplementary Fig. 38). Moreover, under an external magnetic field of 1.6 T (Supplementary Fig. 39), the CPL signal at 540 nm shows a pronounced enhancement compared with that measured without the external magnetic field, while the g value remains at the 10⁻³ level. This enhancement arises from the magnetic-field-induced promotion of radiative transitions in the donor triplet state52, further confirming its long-lived triplet-state characteristic. These results verify the inherent CP-OLPL emission nature of R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films. These results certify that the 440 nm emission of R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films should be derived from the exciplex, and the ultimate yellow CP-OLPL emission at 540 nm should arise from the triplet emission of the chiral donor.

To investigate the photophysical behaviors of the CP-OLPL emission, a TRPL profile was recorded (Fig. 3h) of the S-2OBNMB/PPT film. This analysis indicates a significant spectral transformation over time within the CP-OLPL system, characterized by two emission bands over 20 ms, further suggesting the presence of multiple emission centers28,46. During the initial 0–10-3 ms interval, the ~430 nm exciplex emission dominates the luminescent process. As the delayed time increased from 10-3 to 2 ms, the exciplex emission gradually diminished, and the localized triplet emission of S-2OBNMB donor became the predominant emission band after a 2 ms delay. This transition from exciplex to localized triplet emission of S-2OBNMB effectively explains the observed CP-OLPL emission in S-2OBNMB/PPT film, resulting in a noticeable yellow emission from S-2OBNMB.

An extensive set of experiments was systematically conducted on S-2OBNMB/PPT film to delve into the impact of varying doping concentrations of chiral donor, temperature, excitation power, and time on the CP-OLPL emission. The excitation-delayed PL mappings of 1 mol% R and S-2OBNMB/PPT films are quite homologous (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 40), showing the main emission bands located in the 540–600 nm range with the optimal excitation wavelength at ~380 nm. Among the doped films with varied doping concentrations, 1 mol% S-2OBNMB/PPT film shows the longest OLPL duration (Fig. 4c). An increase in the concentration of S-2OBNMB reduces molecular spacing between S-2OBNMB and PPT, significantly enhancing the probability of radical cation and anion recombination, which shortens the OLPL duration to less than 160 s53. Expectantly, the OLPL duration increases as both the excitation power density and time are extended. CP-OLPL exceeding 3500 s is enabled following an excitation period of 300 s (Fig. 4d). The phenomenon is attributable to an increase in charge carriers generated through CS progress, which accordingly enhances the OLPL properties54,55. This result provides further evidence for the presence of an intermediate state of the exciplex. Mechanism of OLPL emission

To reasonably elucidate the emission mechanism of the CP-OLPL system, the energy levels of the S1 and T1 states of S-2OBNMB, PPT, and S-2OBNMB/PPT exciplex were calculated based on the SSPL, delayed PL, and OLPL spectra of the 1 mol% S-2OBNMB/PPT film53. According to Supplementary Fig. 16, the calculated energy levels of chiral donor S-2OBNMB are 2.95 eV and 2.29 eV for the lowest singlet excited state (S1D) and the lowest triplet excited state (T1D), respectively; also, the energy levels of PPT are simulated to be 3.76 eV and 2.91 eV for the lowest singlet excited state (S1A) and the lowest triplet excited state (T1A), respectively. Therefore, the CP-OLPL emission process following the photoexcitation by UV light was illustrated in Fig. 4e. After the photoexcitation, the electron is effectively separated with the aid of the CT process between S-2OBNMB and PPT; then the separated electron hops among the PPT acceptor and following the gradual electron and hole recombination between PPT and S-2OBNMB to generate the OLPL exciplex emission. The efficient spatial separation of the HOMO on the donor and the LUMO on the acceptor within the exciplex minimizes the energy gap between its ¹CT and ³CT states, enabling similar ¹CT and ³CT energy levels. Because the 1CT (2.79 eV) and 3CT of the exciplex are higher than T1D (2.29 eV), the generated OLPL exciplex emission is efficiently transferred to T1D through the ET process; and, the large energy gap (0.5 eV) between the 1CT and T1D prevents the reversed ET from T1D of S-2OBNMB to 1CT of the exciplex. These combined effects confer bright and ultralong CP-OLPL emission from the triplet state of S-2OBNMB.

Application of CP-OLPL materials

To demonstrate the applicability of the CP-OLPL material, various potential applications of multi-level information encryption and 3D display objects have been fabricated (Fig. 5). A chiral-selective response information encryption platform has been constructed by integrating both left- and right-handed circularly polarized filters with CP-OLPL materials composed of 1 mol% and 5 mol% R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films (Fig. 5a). Owing to the chiral-selective transmission characteristics of circularly polarized filters, CPL signals with the same handedness as the filter are transmitted, whereas those with the opposite handedness are effectively blocked. Upon turning off the excitation light 1 min later, afterglow patterns with different brightness are achieved (Patterns 2 and 4) through covering different circularly polarized filters, suggesting the inherent nature of CPL emission. Furthermore, owing to the concentration-dependent OLPL lifetimes, 5 mol% R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films show much shorter OLPL emission than 1 mol% R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films (Fig. 4b). Therefore, emission patterns changed from Pattern 2 to 3 and Pattern 4 to 5 are observed after the removal of UV light excitation for 5 min; notably, compared to afterglow brightness of 1 mol% R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films, the much shorter duration time of 5 mol% R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films endow afterglow patterns with much enhanced contrast variations between R-2OBNMB/PPT and S-2OBNMB/PPT films (Fig. 5a, Supplementary movie 1). In contrast, when the CP-OLPL decay process is recorded without a polarizer, no discernible difference in brightness can be found in the 1 mol% and 5 mol% R(S)-2OBNMB/PPT films throughout the entire decay period (Supplementary movie 2). The ability to distinguish patterns under different filters and delayed times emphasizes the advantages of CP-OLPL films for information encryption. Furthermore, 3D display objects have been fabricated through a digital light processing printing method. Discussion

In conclusion, this work presents a valuable strategy for designing the chiral chromophore-engineered donor R(S)-2OBNMB. By combining R(S)-2OBNMB with a PPT acceptor, a CP-OLPL exciplex system has been successfully achieved, exhibiting a blue exciplex emission with a luminescent peak at ~440 nm and |glum| of 7.8×10-3, as well as an ultralong yellow CP-OLPL with main emission peaks at 540 nm and durations extending up to 90 min. Systematic experimental results demonstrate that the blue emission originates from the exciplex emission formed through efficient CS and gradual CR processes between R(S)-2OBNMB and PPT. Importantly, the effective ET from the exciplex to the chiral chromophore enables the CP-OLPL emission. These investigations provide a valid strategy for exploring chiral donors to facilitate an exciplex-based CP-OLPL system, which may promote the discovery of advanced chiral afterglow materials with prolonged durations and broad application potential.

Methods

Materials

Chemical reagents, unless otherwise specified, were purchased from Daicel, Energy Chemical, or Acros and used without further purification. The R-6,6’-dibromo-2,2’-bis(methoxy)-binaphthyl and S-6,6’-dibromo-2,2’-bis(methoxy)-binaphthyl were purchased from Daicel with a purity of 99% ee. N, N-dimethyl-4-biphenylboronic acid (purity: 98%), 2,8-dibromodibenzo[b,d]thiophen (purity: 98%), and diphenylphosphine chloride (purity: 97%) were purchased from Energy Chemical. The tetrakis(triphenylphosphine)palladium (purity: 99.99%) was purchased from Acros. Manipulations involving air-sensitive reagents were performed under an argon atmosphere.

General synthesis of R(S)-2OBNMB

R(S)-6,6’-dibromo-2,2’-bis(methoxy)-binaphthyl (0.20 g, 0.37 mmol), N,N-dimethyl-4-biphenylboronic acid (0.204 g, 0.82 mmol), and tetrakis(triphenylphosphine) palladium (Pd[P(C6H5)3]4, 10 mg, 0.086 mmol) were added into a 50 mL round-bottomed flask under an argon and light-shielded atmosphere. Subsequently, 10 mL of toluene solution and 1 mL of potassium carbonate (K2CO3) aqueous solution (2 mol/L) were rapidly injected into the reaction flask. The reaction mixture was stirred for 48 h at 90 °C. After the reaction, the mixture was extracted three times using water (30 mL) and dichloromethane (CH2Cl2, 3 × 30 mL). The organic phase was collected and dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate (Na2SO4). After filtration and solvent evaporation, the crude product was purified through silica gel column chromatography using CH2Cl2/petroleum ether (PE) as eluent, resulting in brown solid. Yield: 65 mg (28%) for R-2OBNMB and 83 mg (36%) for S-2OBNMB.

General synthesis of PPT

2,8-dibromodibenzo[b,d]thiophen (1.71 g, 5.00 mmol) was dissolved in 50 mL of anhydrous tetrahydrofuran (THF) under Ar and cooled to −78 °C in a dry ice/acetone bath. n-BuLi (7.50 mL, 12.00 mmol, 1.60 M in hexane) was then added dropwise. Two hours later, diphenylphosphine chloride (2.30 mL, 13.00 mmol) was injected into the system at −78 °C and the mixture was stirred at this temperature for 1 h, after which the temperature was allowed to increase to room temperature for 12 h. Then, the reaction was quenched with 5 mL of water and the mixture was extracted three times using water (30 mL) and CH2Cl2 (3 × 30 mL). The organic phase was collected and followed by oxidation with 5 mL 30% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) aqueous solution. After being stirred overnight at room temperature, the reaction mixture needs to be extracted three times again by water (30 mL) and CH2Cl2 (3 × 30 mL). The organic phase was collected and then dried with anhydrous Na2SO4. After filtration and solvent evaporation, the crude product was purified through silica gel column chromatography using CH2Cl2/methanol as eluent, resulting in 1.70 g of white solid with a yield of 58%.

The preparation of CP-OLPL exciplex films

1 mg of R(S)-2OBNMB, and 94 mg of PPT were placed into the 0.8 × 1.2 × 0.4 cm3 quartz grooves and heated to 240 °C for 1 minute to obtain the homogeneously melting mixture in the glovebox with nitrogen atmosphere. Subsequently, with the withdrawal of the heating source, the liquid mixture is able to cool rapidly and form transparent films. By encapsulation procedure using epoxy resin, the chiral exciplex films with stable optical properties were achieved after ultraviolet curing.

The process of 3D printing technology

the 1 mol% R-2OBNMB/PPT film was first mixed and melted into a glass mold. Then the melted film was carefully detached from the mold and ground into a homogeneous powder. Then, this powder was incorporated into a resin and thoroughly stirred to ensure its uniform dispersion, thereby producing a 3D-printable ink. Using a UV-curable 3D printing technique of digital light processing, different topological structures could be achieved.
