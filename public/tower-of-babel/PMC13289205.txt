Advances in immunotherapies in ovarian cancer
Authors: Ruveyda Ayasun, Dmitriy Zamarin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOvarian cancer (OC) remains one of the deadliest gynecologic malignancies, and, despite the presence of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in nearly half of cases, immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown only modest clinical benefit. These observations have stimulated interest in antigen-directed immunotherapies, including adoptive cell therapies (chimeric antigen receptor T cell, TIL therapy), bispecific antibodies, cancer vaccines, cytokine-based agents, and antibody–drug conjugates. Multiple targets, such as mesothelin, folate receptor-α, HER2, MUC16, EpCAM, and claudin-6, are currently under clinical investigation. In this review, we summarize the current landscape of immunotherapy in OC and examine the biological and clinical factors that have limited therapeutic efficacy to date. We also discuss emerging strategies aimed at overcoming resistance, including rational combinations and biomarker-driven approaches. Finally, we highlight the critical role of integrated genomic and immune profiling to elucidate mechanisms of response and resistance and to guide the next generation of immunotherapeutic strategies for OC.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is one of the deadliest gynecologic cancers. In the USA, it is expected that in 2025 about 20,890 women will be newly diagnosed, and around 12,730 will die from this disease.1 Even with progress in treatment, prognosis is still poor, and only about 25% of patients live longer than 10 years after diagnosis. The fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Tumors of the Female Genital Tract describes five main histologic types of ovarian carcinoma: high-grade serous, low-grade serous, mucinous, endometrioid, and clear cell.2 Among these, high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSOC) is the most common.

Standard therapy for newly-diagnosed advanced OC includes neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) with platinum-taxane doublet followed by interval cytoreductive surgery or primary cytoreductive surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy with the optional addition of bevacizumab in combination with chemotherapy and as maintenance.3 More recently, the introduction of maintenance treatments with poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, alone or together with bevacizumab, has resulted in modest improvement in survival, though the vast majority of patients still eventually experience cancer recurrence and succumb to disease.46

About half of ovarian tumors contain tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). Patients with intraepithelial TILs live longer, suggesting that the disease has immune activity and may be susceptible to immunotherapy.7 In the last 10 years, immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) changed the treatment of several solid tumors, including some gynecological cancers. However, in OC, the efficacy of ICI has in general been limited to a small fraction of patients.8 9 It is important to highlight that recently the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved pembrolizumab in combination with paclitaxel with or without bevacizumab for programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)-expressing platinum-resistant ovarian carcinoma based on the KEYNOTE-B96 trial, but even there the median progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) benefit was limited to 1.1 months and 4.2 months, respectively.10

OCs express multiple shared tumor antigens, creating possibilities for antigen-directed treatment. These include cell-based therapies such as chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy, bispecific antibodies, vaccines, cytokines, and antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs). Targets such as mesothelin, folate receptor alpha, HER2, MUC16, EpCAM, and claudin-6 (CLDN6) have been explored in a number of clinical trials.1113

In this review, we describe the present status of immunotherapy in OC. We discuss biological and clinical reasons for why the efficacy of ICIs has been limited and potential strategies to improve outcomes. We further highlight the importance of the identification of mechanisms of response and resistance using genomic and immune profiling to guide treatment decisions and combination approaches.

Overview of immunotherapy strategies in ovarian cancer

Immune checkpoint inhibitors

Tumor-infiltrating T cells carry prognostic significance across multiple cancers. Their ability to kill cancer cells depends on their functional states, which are shaped by T cell receptor (TCR) and peptide-major histocompatibility complex (MHC) interactions, as well as other signals in the tumor microenvironment (TME). Long-term exposure to antigen in the TME leads to T cell differentiation from activated states to early dysfunction and eventual terminal dysfunction/exhaustion. Dysfunction is marked by activation of transcriptional programs and expression of inhibitory receptors like programmed cell death receptor 1 (PD-1), LAG3, 2B4, TIM3, TIGIT, and CD39 which dampen T cell activity, leading to production of lower levels of effector cytokines like tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα), interferon-gamma (IFNγ), and interleukin-2 (IL2) and decreased cytolytic activity.1418 Drugs that block immune checkpoints, known as ICIs, have been explored as cancer therapeutics. Antibodies against cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), PD-1, and PD-L1 are already approved for several cancers, and studies of other ICIs are ongoing.

Although ICIs have changed treatment for many solid tumors, the benefit from PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in OC has been limited to a small fraction of patients, even when used in the upfront setting. In newly diagnosed advanced OC, the phase III IMagyn050/GOG 3015/ENGOT-ov39 trial evaluated atezolizumab added to chemotherapy and bevacizumab but showed no improvement in PFS or OS in the overall population. A modest PFS benefit was observed in patients with PD-L1-positive tumors without an OS advantage, including no benefit in BRCA-mutated or homologous recombination deficient (HRD) tumors. Similarly, other phase II and III frontline PD-L1 inhibitor trials have not demonstrated clear clinical benefit1923 (table 1).

In recurrent OC, multiple trials evaluating ICI monotherapy or combinations—including KEYNOTE-100, JAVELIN Ovarian 200, and NINJA—demonstrated low response rates (objective response rate (ORR) 8–22%) and no survival advantage over standard therapy, with only modestly improved efficacy in PD-L1-expressing subgroups.24 25 In KEYNOTE-100, PD-L1 Combined Positive Score (CPS) was the biomarker most associated with response, with higher ORR in patients with CPS >10 (10%) compared with CPS <1 (4.1%)2628 (table 1). Positive results recently emerged from the phase III ENGOT-ov65/KEYNOTE-B96 trial, in which anti-PD1 antibody (pembrolizumab) added to weekly paclitaxel±bevacizumab significantly improved PFS in platinum-resistant recurrent OC, both in the PD-L1 CPS ≥1 subgroup and in the overall population. The combination also conferred a clinically meaningful OS benefit in PD-L1 CPS ≥1 patients (18.2 vs 14.0 months; HR 0.76; p=0.0053), with subsequent analyses confirming OS benefit in the total population, while maintaining a manageable safety profile.10

ICIs have also been tested in combination with PARP inhibitors. While preclinical data were promising, large clinical trials so far failed to demonstrate substantial benefit. In the phase III DUO-O trial, the addition of anti-PDL1 antibody (durvalumab) and PARP inhibitor (olaparib) to chemotherapy and anti-VEGF (bevacizumab) in newly diagnosed OC yielded only modest benefit in patients with HRD-positive cancer, which was largely attributed to olaparib rather than durvalumab.29 In the phase III ATHENA-COMBO trial, rucaparib plus anti-PD1 antibody (nivolumab) did not improve PFS compared with rucaparib alone.30 In KEYLYNK-001, pembrolizumab plus olaparib maintenance improved PFS when compared with chemotherapy alone in newly diagnosed advanced OC, however, it is once again unclear whether the benefit was predominantly driven by olaparib as maintenance with pembrolizumab alone had no PFS benefit.31 In the phase III FIRST (ENGOT-ov44) trial, anti-PD1 antibody (dostarlimab) and PARP inhibitor (niraparib) maintenance after chemotherapy in newly diagnosed advanced OC demonstrated modest improvement in PFS without an OS benefit32 (table 1).

New studies are now looking at ICI combinations with other immune drugs or targeted agents. In NRG-GY003, nivolumab plus ipilimumab achieved higher ORR (31% vs 12%) and longer PFS (3.9 vs 2.0 months) than nivolumab alone.33 Combinations with bevacizumab have also shown some promise, including a phase II study of pembrolizumab combined with bevacizumab and metronomic cyclophosphamide, demonstrating an ORR of 47.5% and a median PFS of 10 months34 and a phase II trial of nivolumab and bevacizumab demonstrating an ORR of 28.9% with higher activity in platinum-sensitive versus platinum-resistant disease (40% vs 16.7%).35

Overall, although the majority of trials evaluating ICIs as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy have demonstrated limited activity in OC, these findings should not discourage further investigation. T cell exhaustion is a key immune resistance mechanism prevalent across all cancers, including OC, and lack of response to ICI in OC implies that T cell exhaustion is not the only mechanism of immune resistance that should be targeted. Ongoing studies are focusing on dual checkpoint blockade, antiangiogenic combinations, and novel immune and targeted agents. Finally, since ovarian tumors may lack effective T cell priming and pre-existing cancer-directed T cell pool, new efforts are also exploring immunotherapies that directly target OC antigens, as discussed below.

Antibody–drug conjugates

ADCs are a class of therapeutic agents based on an antibody linked to a cytotoxic or immune-modulating “payload”, allowing for delivery of the payload to the tumor cells expressing the target antigen (figure 1A). After antigen binding, ADCs are usually internalized by endocytosis, releasing the drug inside the tumor cells.36

(B) Bispecific T cell engager therapy; bispecific antibodies simultaneously bind a tumor-associated antigen on cancer cells and CD3 on T cells, forming an immune synapse that redirects polyclonal T cells to kill tumor cells without requiring prior antigen-specific priming. In OC, most ADCs that have been tested to date have carried cytotoxic payloads. Common tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) that have been targeted with ADCs include folate receptor alpha (FRα), HER2, cadherin-6 (CDH6), CLDN6, and sodium-dependent phosphate transport protein 2B. The most clinically advanced ADC is mirvetuximab soravtansine, which targets FRα and has been approved for therapy of FRα-high platinum-resistant OC based on survival benefit when compared with investigator’s choice chemotherapy.37 Fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd), an ADC targeting HER2 has received histology-agnostic approval from the FDA for all HER2-expressing tumors. In the Phase II DESTINY-PanTumor02 trial, in the OC cohort with HER2 3+ expression, T-DXd demonstrated an ORR of 63.6%.38

Besides direct cytotoxic drug delivery, antibodies in ADCs can engage Fcγ receptors on myeloid cells. This opens the possibility of delivering immune-activating payloads directly to myeloid cells in an antigen-independent manner.39 One example of such a strategy is the conjugation of antibodies with stimulator of interferon genes (STING) agonists, which activate innate immune signaling, but have a limited therapeutic window with systemic administration. Preclinical studies demonstrate that STING agonist-conjugated ADCs can effectively trigger adaptive immune responses.40 A phase I trial (NCT05514717) is ongoing to evaluate XMT-2056, a HER-2-directed STING agonist ADC in HER2-positive cancers.

These studies highlight that ADCs can deliver not only cytotoxic drugs but also immune-modulating agents. This strategy can be used alone or in conjunction with other immunotherapies by helping to remodel the immunosuppressive TME and increase tumor sensitivity to immune-mediated killing.

Bispecific T cell engagers

T cell-engaging (TCE) bispecific antibodies are engineered protein adapters that bind TAA on cancer cells and a surface marker (commonly CD3) on T cells, forming an immune synapse, resulting in T cell activation and cytolytic function (figure 1B).41

Blinatumomab, which targets CD19 in refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, is the first FDA-approved TCE.42 Because TCEs can activate polyclonal T cells, they may be useful in tumors with low neoantigen load, such as OC. Selecting a tumor antigen with high tumor specificity is critical to avoid on-target off-tumor toxicity.

An example of a TCE that is being explored in OC is REGN4018 (ubamatamab), a human bispecific antibody that targets MUC16 and CD3. A phase I trial (NCT03564340) has evaluated ubamatamab alone and in combination with anti-PD1 blockade (cemiplimab) in recurrent OC, with preliminary results demonstrating acceptable safety and dose-dependent activity.43

Other TCEs under study in OC target CLDN6, mesothelin, LYPD1, and others.4446 Results of a phase I trial of CLDN6-targeting bispecific antibody, BNT142, were presented at the 2025 ASCO Meeting (NCT05262530). This therapy uses lipid nanoparticle-formulated messenger RNA encoding a bispecific antibody against CLDN6 and CD3. In CLDN6-positive OC, partial responses were seen and the disease control rate (DCR) reached 75%.47 Another agent, XmAb541, is a bispecific antibody targeting CLDN6 and CD3 which showed high specificity for CLDN6 with limited cross-reactivity to other claudins such as CLDN9. A phase I trial (NCT06276491) of this drug in advanced solid tumors is currently ongoing.48 Overall, these early successes are exciting, as they demonstrate that T cell redirection with TCEs in OC can actually lead to responses and clinical benefit, providing a strong rationale for further exploration of these modalities, possibly in combination with other strategies to improve T cell infiltration and function.

Adoptive T cell therapies

ACTs aim to overcome weak endogenous tumor-specific T cell responses by expanding antigen-specific T cells ex vivo and infusing them into patients, targeting cancer cells and potentially stimulating broader antitumor immunity. Various ACT approaches have been explored in OC.

Chimeric antigen receptor T cells

CAR-T therapy is an approach where patient T cells are genetically engineered to express artificial receptors that recognize surface TAAs without dependence on HLA-antigen presentation12 49 (figure 2A–B). CAR-T cells consist of three main components: an extracellular antigen-recognition domain, a transmembrane anchor, and an intracellular signaling module.49 The CAR recognition domain typically consists of an antigen-targeting single-chain variable fragment linked via a hinge or spacer to the intracellular signaling module that contains activation motifs such as CD3ε, and costimulatory domains to enhance T-cell activation, persistence, and efficacy.49 TCR fusion constructs (TRuCs) represent a different CAR-T design. In this approach, T cells express a fusion protein that connects an antibody-based antigen-binding domain with a TCR subunit, usually CD3ε. Unlike CAR-T cells, TRuC is part of the natural TCR complex, and when it binds antigen, the full TCR signaling machinery is activated50 (figure 2A). A phase I/II trial of gavocabtagene autoleucel (NCT03907852), a TRuC targeting mesothelin, demonstrated safety and efficacy of such an approach in 30 patients including OC, where the overall response rate was 20%, DCR was 77%, and 6-month survival was 70%.51

CAR-T cells can be further engineered to secrete cytokines or antibodies, augmenting antitumor activity and remodeling the TME. A phase I clinical trial tested intravenous and intraperitoneal infusion of IL-12-secreting MUC16 CAR-T cells in recurrent OC. The treatment was safe without lymphodepleting chemotherapy, however, the introduction of lymphodepleting chemotherapy resulted in dose-limiting toxicity (NCT02498912),52 highlighting the need for further refinement of armored CAR-Ts.

Combination therapies of CAR-Ts with other immunotherapies are also being explored. A phase I trial (NCT05057715) is evaluating mesothelin-targeted CAR-T cells combined with VCN-01, an oncolytic adenovirus that selectively replicates in RB1-deficient tumors and degrades tumor stroma to enhance immune infiltration.53 A CLDN6-targeted CAR-T cell therapy combined with a CLDN6 RNA vaccine demonstrated a 37% ORR and a 67% DCR in patients with CLDN6-expressing tumors, including OC, with the vaccine promoting CAR-T expansion and persistence (NCT04503278).11

CAR natural killer (NK) cells have emerged as an alternative platform that may offer inherent advantages as CAR effectors: they mediate innate antitumor cytotoxicity through multiple activating receptors independent of the CAR and could be derived from allogeneic sources such as cord blood without inducing graft-versus-host disease. In a landmark phase I/II trial, Liu et al administered HLA-mismatched anti-CD19 CAR-NK cells derived from cord blood to 11 patients with relapsed or refractory CD19-positive B-cell malignancies. The NK cells were engineered to coexpress anti-CD19 CAR, IL-15 to support in vivo persistence, and an inducible caspase 9 safety switch. Of the 11 patients treated, 8 (73%) responded, with 7 achieving complete remission. These results highlight the potential of off-the-shelf allogeneic CAR-NK cells as a safer and more readily manufacturable cellular therapy platform, and efforts to extend this approach to solid tumors, including OC, are underway.54

Engineered TCR T cell therapy

Engineered TCR T-cell therapy involves transduction of T cells with tumor antigen-specific TCRs (figure 2A–2C). Because antigen recognition is MHC restricted, this approach requires HLA matching, potentially limiting patient eligibility. However, unlike CAR-T cells, TCR-engineered T cells can target intracellular antigens presented as peptide–MHC complexes. Examples of those include shared neoantigens, such as those derived from recurrent oncogenic mutations, and other shared TAAs, such as germline antigens (eg, MAGE-A4, NY-ESO-1). NY-ESO-1 is a cancer–testis antigen expressed in approximately 40–43% of epithelial OCs, making it an attractive target for immunotherapy.55 A phase I clinical trial (NCT02366546) investigated NY-ESO-1–specific TCR–engineered T cells with endogenous TCR knockdown in patients with NY-ESO-1–positive solid tumors. Among the six patients treated at the 5×10⁹ cell dose level, three achieved objective tumor responses.56 MAGE-A4 is another cancer germline antigen expressed in approximately 47–57% of ovarian serous carcinomas, positioning it as a clinically relevant target for a substantial subset of patients.57 Afamitresgene autoleucel (afamicel) is an affinity-enhanced TCR-engineered product targeting MAGE-A4 that has received FDA approval for the treatment of synovial sarcoma,58 highlighting the rationale for TCR therapy against this target in other tumors, including OC.

Targeting of shared neoantigens has also demonstrated early signs of efficacy in clinical studies. In a phase I/II trial (NCT05194735), TCR-T cells targeting shared KRAS, TP53, and EGFR mutations demonstrated a favorable safety profile through dose escalation to 65.5×10⁵ cells/kg. Preliminary efficacy was observed, with responses in 20% and disease control of 85.7% of patients in the lymphodepletion cohort.59

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes

TIL therapy is a type of cell-based immunotherapy that uses the T cells isolated and cultured from the patient’s tumor, followed by infusion back into the patient (figure 2D). Recently, the FDA approved lifileucel (Amtagvi, developed by Iovance) for patients with unresectable or metastatic melanoma.60

In OC, early studies of TIL therapy were conducted in Japan in the 1990s, demonstrating that patients who received TILs after completing initial adjuvant chemotherapy had improved PFS.61 In a 2018 pilot trial, six patients with metastatic platinum-resistant OC were treated with TILs after lymphodepleting chemotherapy and decrescendo intravenous IL-2. Stable disease was observed for at least 3 months in all six patients. Infused TILs showed antitumor activity, but high expression of exhaustion markers such as LAG-3 and PD-1 suggested that immune checkpoints may limit efficacy.62 In 2020, a study combined TIL therapy with checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD1 antibody nivolumab and anti-CTLA4 antibody ipilimumab) in six patients with advanced high-grade serous OC. One patient had a partial response, and five had stable disease lasting up to 12 months. Adding ipilimumab improved TIL expansion and tumor reactivity, and the combination was deemed to be feasible and safe.63

A 2024 phase I/II trial (NCT04766320) tested a modified TIL regimen without intravenous IL-2. Instead, it used low-dose cyclophosphamide for lymphodepletion and PD-1 blockade. Among 14 evaluable patients with advanced gynecologic cancers (including OC), five had objective responses, including three complete responses. TILs showed long-term persistence and proliferation, and the treatment was manageable in terms of safety.64

Overall, these small studies show that TIL therapy has potential for improving outcomes in OC. Since surgical resection is a standard part of OC care, it is prudent to explore TIL therapy in the context of standard treatment regimens as a potential strategy to increase the number of cures after upfront therapy

Cancer vaccines and oncolytic viruses

Oncolytic viruses represent an emerging therapeutic approach for OC based on selective infection and destruction of tumor cells while modifying the TME and activating antitumor immune responses (figure 2).65

In the phase II non-randomized VIRO-15 trial, intraperitoneal Olvimulogene nanivacirepvec (Olvi-Vec, a modified vaccinia virus) followed by platinum-doublet chemotherapy (±bevacizumab) in 27 heavily pretreated patients with platinum-resistant or platinum-refractory OC demonstrated ORR of 54% (95% CI 33% to 74%) and median PFS of 11.0 months (95% CI 6.7 to 13.0).66 These results suggest that Olvi-Vec–primed immunochemotherapy might overcome platinum resistance/refractoriness and the randomized phase III OnPrime/GOG-3076 study is currently enrolling (NCT05281471).

Cancer vaccines aim to increase existing antitumor immune responses or to prime new T cell responses against tumor antigens. Different vaccine platforms include peptide vaccines, RNA vaccines in liposomal nanoparticles, dendritic cell (DC) vaccines, and whole tumor cell vaccines (figure 3).

In OC, several vaccine methods have been tested. In the phase IIb randomized VITAL trial, gemogenovatucel-T (Vigil), an autologous tumor cell vaccine transfected with a plasmid encoding granulocyte-monocyte colony-stimulating factor and short hairpin RNA to knock down TGFβ1/2, has been evaluated as maintenance in 91 patients with newly diagnosed stage III/IV OC in CR after surgery and platinum-based chemotherapy. The study demonstrated a trend toward improved recurrence-free survival favoring Vigil compared with placebo (median recurrence-free survival 11.5 vs 8.4 months).67

An autologous DC-based vaccine (DCVAC) in combination with first-line carboplatin and paclitaxel chemotherapy showed safety and improved PFS in a randomized phase II trial (SOV01, NCT02107937). Improved outcomes were seen in patients with low tumor mutational burden (TMB) and poor CD8+ T cell infiltration. In these patients, DCVAC improved T cell effector activity and tumor-specific killing in blood, suggesting that “cold” ovarian tumors may benefit from vaccines to initiate immune responses.68

Another approach employed in recurrent OC used autologous DCs pulsed with oxidized autologous whole-tumor lysate, given by intranodal injection. This vaccine induced T cell responses against tumor antigens and was linked with longer survival in patients who developed immune responses. Vaccination also triggered T cells against neoepitopes arising from tumor mutations, including new T cell clones with higher avidity to known epitopes.69

Despite immunogenicity, ORR to vaccines in the setting of advanced/recurrent disease remain low. Meta-analyses show modest clinical effect, with pooled ORRs around 4%, though with high safety and evidence of longer survival in some patients.70 It is likely that vaccines may carry the highest potential in the setting of minimal residual disease such as maintenance. Other strategies include combinations of vaccines with other immunotherapies, and several studies are ongoing.

Biomarkers

Predictors of response and resistance to ICIs and other immunotherapies in OC remain poorly defined. The genomic landscape of OCs appears to influence cancer immune recognition, though only a limited number of genomic markers have been validated to be predictive of immunotherapy benefit.

A notable number of endometrioid and clear cell OCs harbor mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR) and demonstrate improved responsiveness to ICIs. Beyond dMMR, ovarian clear cell carcinomas (ccOCs) appear to be more immunogenic and generally more responsive to ICIs.337173 A recent study further demonstrated that ccOCs carrying PPP2R1A mutations are more likely to respond to ICIs, although the mechanistic basis for this remains unclear.74

Biomarkers of immunotherapy response in HGSOC, the most common subtype, remain undefined. TMB, a recognized biomarker of ICI response in other cancers, has limited utility in HGSOC given its typically low mutational load. Instead, HGSOC is characterized by pronounced genomic instability, manifesting as copy number alterations and chromosomal rearrangements. Distinct genomic subtypes of HGSOC are associated with different immune phenotypes: HRD tumors show inflammatory signaling and infiltration by highly differentiated, dysfunctional CD8+ T cells, whereas non-HRD tumors exhibit TGF-β–mediated immunosuppression and immune exclusion.75 Despite these features, neither TMB, HRD, nor BRCA1/2 mutation status has been correlated with meaningful clinical benefit from ICI therapy in OC and therefore should not be used for patient selection.19 20 76 The apparent paradox between HRD-driven immunogenicity and lack of ICI responsiveness may be explained by frequent loss of heterozygosity at the HLA locus and pronounced immune heterogeneity across tumor sites, both of which likely contribute to immune evasion.75

PD-L1 is an established biomarker of ICI benefit in several cancers, including NSCLC and urothelial carcinoma, but its role in OC is less certain. As highlighted above, current approval of pembrolizumab in OC is limited to the patients with PD-L1-expressing tumors, though the degree of enrichment for patients with benefit was modest.10 While PD-L1 alone is unreliable, its predictive potential may improve when integrated with other immune parameters. For example, PD-L1 expression combined with PD-1+ TILs strongly correlated with favorable prognosis in several studies.7779 Emerging evidence indicates that spatial interactions of immune populations in the TME may serve as better predictors of prognosis and response to immunotherapy than presence or absence of individual immune populations or markers such as PD-L1. For example, biomarker analyses from a combination trial of PARP inhibitor and anti-PD-1 demonstrated that spatial interactions between CD8+PD-1+ T cells and PD-L1+ macrophages are associated with improved response.80 Similarly, Duraiswamy and colleagues demonstrated that polyfunctional PD-1+ T cells cluster within intraepithelial niches where they interact with PD-L1+ antigen-presenting cells (APCs), suggesting that T cell–APC cross-talk governs T cell functional states.81 In contrast, colocalization of CXCR3+ Tregs with CXCR3+ effector T cells within OC was shown to be associated with suppression of effector T-cell proliferation and IFN-γ secretion.82 Contribution of stromal components to ICI response have also been highlighted by several studies. Perez-Villatoro et al presented a single-cell atlas of metastatic HGSOC from 280 patients, demonstrating that immune cell coinfiltration at the tumor-stroma interface predicts outcomes.83 Finally, analysis of OC samples collected within the context of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy trial (NeoPembrOV) revealed that tumor-stroma proportion (TSP) is prognostic in HGSOC treated with chemo-immunotherapy, and pembrolizumab-induced CD8+ T cell increase occurred only in low-TSP tumors.84

For antigen-targeted therapies such as CAR-T cells and bispecific antibodies, biomarkers of response remain under active investigation. The most fundamental determinant is tumor antigen expression: high and homogeneous antigen expression improves efficacy, whereas heterogeneity drives antigen escape and resistance. Coexpression of multiple antigens may inform combinatorial targeting strategies to enhance tumor coverage. Product-related biomarkers such as the proportion of early memory T cells in the CAR T-cell product, CAR T-cell expansion and persistence post-infusion, and cytokine profiles (eg, IFN-γ secretion on antigen encounter) are associated with clinical response and are being explored as monitoring tools.85 Dynamic biomarkers such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and CA-125 kinetics may assist in response monitoring, though their predictive value for antigen-targeted therapies is not yet established.

Mechanisms of immune resistance

Resistance to immune recognition and elimination in OC arises from both cancer cell–intrinsic and TME–driven mechanisms. In addition to T cell exhaustion, other mechanisms include but are not limited to low TMB and limited neoantigen presentation, T cell exclusion via vascular barriers, immunosuppressive myeloid compartment (tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs)), TGFβ-driven stromal barriers, Treg cells, epigenetic silencing of immune signaling, and metabolic immunosuppression (lipid metabolism, indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase).7 17 86

One of the most common intrinsic mechanisms is reduced expression of MHC-I, observed in ∼60% of epithelial OCs. This reduction occurs through loss of heterozygosity, decreased transcription of MHC-I–related genes, or defects in antigen-processing machinery components.87 These alterations can be classified as “hard” (irreversible, due to structural genetic loss) or “soft” (potentially reversible, mediated by epigenetic regulators or cytokine signaling). Standard NACT with carboplatin and paclitaxel has been shown to suppress HLA-I expression, impairing T cell recruitment and activation within the TME.88 Strategies to restore or enhance MHC-I expression during or following chemotherapy may therefore improve antitumor immune responses in these patients.

Multiple immune and stromal components contribute to immune evasion in OC, including MDSCs, TAMs, dysfunctional T cells, cytokines, and soluble mediators.89 MDSCs, in particular, exert broad immunosuppressive functions by inhibiting effector T cells and NK cells. They are recruited and activated in response to inflammatory cytokines such as IFNγ, TNFα, and IL-6. In OC, IL-6 is associated with increased MDSC accumulation, poor prognosis, and accelerated tumor progression.90

Recent studies have also highlighted a role for tumor-intrinsic cytokine signaling in shaping the TME. Mollaoglu et al demonstrated that OC cells are a significant source of IL-4, transcriptionally regulated by GATA6.91 Tumor-derived IL-4 fosters an immunosuppressive milieu characterized by M2-polarized TAMs and reduced T-cell infiltration. Importantly, combining anti-PD-1 therapy with IL-4 receptor blockade significantly delayed tumor progression, underscoring the translational potential of this strategy.

Future directions and conclusions

Despite clear evidence of immunogenicity in OC, the benefit of ICIs is still limited, with low ORRs and lack of improvement in OS in most studies. Potential mediators of resistance include the immunosuppressive TME, tumor heterogeneity, and genomic instability that drives evolution of immune resistance. Biomarkers predictive of response to ICIs in OC are lacking and PD-L1 expression has not been shown to consistently correlate with treatment response. New biomarkers are focusing on broader immunophenotyping, such as CD8+ T cell density and their spatial distribution, as well as combining genetic and TME profiling to guide patient selection and trial design. An immunogenomic analysis of paired pretreatment and post-treatment samples by Jimenez-Sanchez et al demonstrated that NACT induces local immune activation in HGSOC, including increased NK cell infiltration and oligoclonal T-cell expansion.92 Critically, the study showed that immune-cell-excluded and inflammatory microenvironments coexist within the same individual and even within the same tumor site. NACT was shown to potentiate the immunogenicity of immune-excluded tumors, providing a mechanistic rationale for incorporating immunotherapy in the neoadjuvant window.

Combination strategies with different agents such as cytokines are under intensive study to overcome primary and acquired immune resistance.24 93 Early studies of nemvaleukin alfa, an engineered IL-2, in combination with pembrolizumab showed encouraging ORR and disease control, however, the phase III ARTISTRY-7 trial (NCT05092360) was terminated earlier because interim analysis did not show significant benefit in OS compared with the investigator’s choice of chemotherapy (10.1 months vs 9.8 months).94 IMNN-001 is an IL-12 gene therapy delivered intraperitoneally that showed a 13-month OS benefit (46.0 vs 33.0 months; HR 0.69) when added to neoadjuvant/adjuvant chemotherapy in the randomized phase I/II OVATION-2 trial of 112 patients with newly diagnosed advanced OC. The encouraging results have led to the ongoing pivotal phase III OVATION-3 trial evaluating this combination in front-line treatment of advanced epithelial OC.95 Nogapendekin alfa inbakicept (N-803), an IL-15 receptor superagonist, was FDA-approved in 2024 for intravesical use in combination with BCG for BCG-unresponsive non-muscle invasive bladder cancer based on the QUILT-3.032 trial, which demonstrated a 62% complete response rate with durable responses. IL-15 activates NK cells and CD8+ T cells without expanding immunosuppressive regulatory T cells, making it an attractive candidate for locoregional delivery in OC, where intraperitoneal administration could similarly exploit direct access to the TME while minimizing systemic toxicity.96

Approaches targeting immunosuppressive cells in TME, like regulatory T cells, M2 macrophages, and MDSCs, are also under investigation. Targeting of the myeloid compartment is particularly important given its critical role in the establishment of immune exclusion.

Better understanding of TME evolution and resistance mechanisms in response to therapy is needed for developing new immunotherapy agents and improving patient selection. While the majority of novel therapies enter evaluation in advanced/recurrent settings, in OC, the neoadjuvant therapy setting is an attractive opportunity to test novel agents and perform detailed genomic and TME analyses to study the biologic impact of novel drugs and potential mechanisms of resistance. Multiple laparoscopic biopsies obtained in such settings can help capture tumor heterogeneity.97 98 High-content analyses performed on matched tissue samples at laparoscopy and debulking surgery can demonstrate how tumor evolves under therapeutic pressure and reveal resistance mechanisms.
