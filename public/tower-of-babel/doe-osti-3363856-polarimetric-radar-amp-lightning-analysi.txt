Final Technical Report for DE-SC0021247
Marcus van Lier-Walqui (Columbia University)

What were the major goals of the project

In this project we proposed to study the microphysics and dynamics of isolated deep convective
storms in the Houston region with a combined and complimentary observational and modeling
approach. Specifically, we proposed to track cells via polarimetric radar and lightning mapping
array (LMA) observations, capturing details of storm evolution for the full lifecycle of an isolated
updraft cell. LMA analysis would be improved to better illuminate how the charging structure
responds to individual storm updraft thermals; these insights would be related back to
polarimetric radar observations, which are only available at coarser temporal resolutions,
typically. The observational analysis will be compared to a parallel analysis of storms simulated
by a numerical weather prediction model, the NASA-unified Weather Research and Forecasting
model (NU-WRF). This model was to be modified with a microphysics scheme with prognostic
lightning and an in-line polarimetric radar forward operator (POLARRIS). We proposed to unify
the observational and model analysis within a conceptual framework of the evolution of
thunderstorm microphysics and dynamics. This proposed methodology was motivated by our
hypothesis that storm evolution is best understood and observed at the cell- and thermal-sclaes.
Our initial radar and model analysis will be used to help inform TRACER mission decisions that
will target storm cells in the Houston region. The ultimate goal is to observe, understand, and
model the microphysics and dynamics of deep convective storm updrafts at their native cell- and
thermal-scales.

What was accomplished under these goals?

Our group performed technical and scientific support for the TRACER field campaign, running
forecast simulations using the NASA Unified Weather Research and Forecasting model
(NU-WRF) in a newly developed version that incorporated explicit electrification and aerosol
interactions within its microphysics scheme. These simulations were used to help guide radar
scan strategies, observational resource allocation, and research flights during the TRACER field
campaign. The same model version was used in subsequent research studies (see associated
publications).

One simulation-based study focused on simulating the effect of land surface and
TRACER-measured aerosol properties using simulations at resolutions equivalent to modern
numerical weather prediction models (a higher resolution 1km inner domain nested within a
larger 3km outer domain). This study (Iguchi et al., 2025) found a complex interplay between the
effect of land usage (urban, rural, agricultural), with the urban environment found to be important
in the timing of afternoon convection. Aerosol effects, by contrast, affected the strength of
convection, with the most realistic case tested (aerosols and urban land usage) matching
observations best.

Another study (Hernandez-Deckers et al., 2026) submitted for peer review and available in
preprint, focused in a more detailed way on the impacts of aerosols on thermal-scale properties
of convection. For this study, the same NU-WRF version was used at near-LES-scale
resolutions (200 meters), with rising thermals circulations tracked in 3D and analyzed in
composite. Results showed microphysical sensitivity to aerosol perturbation, as well as changes
to electrification, whereas invigoration processes were evident but not generally very strong.

Polarimetric observations were also analyzed together with lightning for a large number of
storms observed during TRACER, and tracked using radar reflectivity in Bruning et al. (2024).
This study found that lightning was not found in the absence of a polarimetric differential
reflectivity and specific differential phase signature, and that neither aerosol conditions nor
environmental conditions were highly predictive of lightning activity. Despite the relationship
between radar polarimetry and lightning, correlations were not as strong as between
polarimetric varibles themselves, suggesting that lightning is dependent perhaps on
hydrometeors that are not visible in polarimetric radar variables (e.g. supercooled cloud water
and cloud-sized ice particles).

The project also supported participation in the BAMS article (Jensen et al., 2025) describing the
TRACER field campaign. Finally, there are at least two articles still in preparation based on work
funded by this project.

What opportunities for training and professional development has the project provided?

This work contributed to the development of a postdoctoral researcher (K. Brunner) a PhD
student (J. Souza), and a masters student (S. Gardner), all at Texas Tech University.
