Advances of oral micro- and nanomedicines for modulating intestinal barriers in the treatment of intestinal diseases
Authors: Mei Yang, Yuanyuan Zhu, Qian Li, Zhichao Deng, Chenxi Xu, Xiaodan Wei, Yana Ma, Chenguang Ding, Mingxin Zhang, Shanbo Ma, Mingzhen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe oral delivery of drugs for treating intestinal diseases, such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and colorectal cancer (CRC), is hindered by four primary intestinal barriers: chemical, microbial, mechanical, and immunological barriers. Micro- and nanoparticles have garnered significant attention as platforms for intestinal-targeted delivery due to their small size and unique structural composition, which enhance drug accumulation and prolong residence time at the site of action, thereby facilitating localized therapy. Oral micro/nanomedicines have emerged as a promising strategy to overcome these barriers and enable the selective targeting of drugs to pathological sites within the intestine. This review systematically examines the chemical, microbial, mechanical, and immune barriers of the intestinal mucosa encountered in intestinal diseases, introduces oral micro/nanomedicines designed to target these barriers, discusses their latest advancements in the context of intestinal diseases, and ultimately provides insights into the challenges faced by oral micro/nanomedicine.Key words: Oral administration, Micro/nanomedicine, Inflammatory bowel disease, Colorectal cancer, Chemical barrier, Microbial barrier, Mechanical barrier, Immunological barriers

Introduction

The gastrointestinal tract (GIT), as the largest organ system in the human body, plays a pivotal role in maintaining tissue homeostasis1. Compromised intestinal barrier function has been associated with several disease states, both intestinal and systemic, including localized gastrointestinal disorders such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), colorectal cancer (CRC), irritable bowel syndrome, and intestinal infections, as well as systemic conditions encompassing neurological disorders2, metabolic diseases3, and cardiovascular diseases4.

With the in-depth research on intestinal barrier function, the intact intestinal mucosal barrier is now recognized to consist of four components: the chemical barrier, microbial barrier, mechanical barrier, and immune barrier (Fig. 1)5. The chemical barrier primarily refers to substances secreted by the GIT, including gastric acid, bile, digestive enzymes, and lysozyme. The microbial barrier maintains the balance of gut microbiota. The mechanical barrier is composed of the mucus layer, intestinal epithelial cells with tight junctions (TJs), and the submucosal lamina propria. The immune barrier is mainly made up of gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) and diffuse immune cells6. In intestinal disorders such as IBD and CRC, the integrity and functionality of the intestinal barrier are compromised, leading to manifestations such as abnormal intestinal pH, altered transit time, dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, increased permeability, and heightened immune responses7,8. These pathological changes collectively characterize intestinal barrier dysfunction. The impairment of intestinal barrier function not only serves as the foundation for the onset of intestinal diseases but also acts as a critical driver in their progression. Therefore, developing therapeutic agents targeting the structural and functional integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier holds significant guiding importance for treating intestinal disorders.

Given the GIT's inherent capacity to absorb most small molecules and nutrients, oral administration currently accounts for 90% of marketed pharmaceuticals9. Compared to parenteral routes (e.g., intravenous, subcutaneous, and intramuscular injections), oral administration offers distinct advantages, including non-invasiveness, high patient compliance, cost-effectiveness, simplified mass production, and particular suitability for chronic conditions and cancer requiring prolonged therapy10. However, the development of oral pharmaceuticals faces multifaceted challenges stemming from the GIT's complex physiological and physicochemical environment11. Critical barriers include the four-layered gastrointestinal defenses (e.g., chemical, microbial, mechanical, and immune barriers), such as the acidic pH in the stomach, enzymatic degradation, the presence of the mucus layer, and hepatic first-pass metabolism, which collectively reduce drug bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy.

The emergence of micro/nanotechnology has opened new avenues for advancing oral drug delivery systems12. Engineered micro/nanomedicines demonstrate transformative potential through five key mechanisms: 1) Enhanced drug penetration through harsh gastric acid and intestinal mucosal barriers; 2) Improved targeting specificity and biosafety profiles; 3) Optimized drug solubility and extended plasma half-life; 4) Controlled drug release with prolonged retention at pathological sites; 5) Minimizing systemic clearance through precision delivery13. These technological advancements address fundamental limitations of conventional oral formulations while preserving the inherent advantages of oral administration.

In this review, we systematically examine and summarize how oral micro/nanomedicines are used in intestinal diseases, focusing on the role of intestinal barriers. First, we delineate the four major intestinal barriers confronting oral drug delivery. Next, we critically examine cutting-edge research strategies employed by oral micro/nanomedicines to overcome these barriers, along with their demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in preclinical and clinical models of intestinal pathologies. Finally, the challenges and opportunities that oral micro/nanomedicine faces in clinical transformation and industrial production in the future are presented and elaborated. This review aims to provide a deep understanding of intestinal barrier function in intestinal diseases and facilitate the development of more effective oral drugs for repairing intestinal barrier function.

The intestinal barrier to oral administration

The chemical barrier

The intestinal mucosal chemical barrier is primarily composed of chemical substances secreted by the GIT, including gastric acid, bile, various digestive enzymes, lysozyme, mucopolysaccharides, glycoproteins, and glycolipids14. Among these, gastric acid can kill acid-intolerant bacteria entering the GIT and inhibit bacterial adhesion and colonization. Bile excreted into the intestine binds to endotoxins in the intestinal lumen to form poorly absorbable complexes, preventing endotoxin absorption from the intestine. Digestive juices can dilute toxins and flush the intestinal lumen. Lysozyme destroys bacterial cell walls, leading to bacterial lysis and elimination6. Therefore, the chemical barrier of the intestinal tract is a crucial component of the body's defense system, protecting against the intrusion of harmful substances. However, it constitutes a significant barrier to oral drug delivery, encompassing pH variation, enzymatic degradation, and short intestinal retention time, thereby compromising bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy15,16 (Fig. 2). pH adaptation

Oral medications must endure the dynamic pH fluctuations in the GIT, shifting from the acidic environment of the stomach (pH 1–3) to pH 6 in the duodenum, pH 7.4 in the terminal ileum, and pH 6–8 in the colon17. Notably, GIT pH values exhibit significant variability not only between individuals but also across different disease states. In patients with IBD and CRC, disruptions in luminal pH often occur due to factors such as dietary changes, altered mucosal bicarbonate and lactate production, bacterial fermentation of carbohydrates, and modified mucosal absorption of short-chain fatty acids18. For example, clinical data from ulcerative colitis (UC) patients indicate substantially decreased pH levels in the right colon, whereas limited research on Crohn's disease has reported conflicting results regarding pH alterations19. Regardless of these complexities, many researchers have leveraged the pH gradient in the colon to design targeted nanodrug-delivery systems that enable site-specific drug release in this intestinal segment20.

Enzymatic resistance

The GIT harbors diverse digestive enzymes, such as salivary amylase in the oral cavity, pepsin and gastric lipase in the stomach, and trypsin in the intestines of which significantly impact drug bioavailability. Drugs are highly susceptible to these enzymes21. The colon, meanwhile, hosts over 400 species of aerobic and anaerobic microorganisms that produce hydrolytic and reductive metabolic enzymes, catalyzing the metabolism of exogenous substances and other biomolecules. Various polysaccharides can only be metabolized in the colon by anaerobic bacteria and are stable in the stomach and intestine, making it possible for colon-targeted drug delivery22.

Transit and retention optimization

GIT transit time is a pivotal parameter in the development of a drug delivery system. The small intestine typically exhibits a transit time of around 4 h, with inter-individual variability ranging from 1 to 6 h. In stark contrast, colonic transit time shows substantial divergence, spanning from 1 to 3 days23. It is imperative to note that numerous factors modulate GIT transit dynamics, including dietary composition, fasting/feeding status, physical activity levels, psychological stress, and colonic pathologies. Patients with active UC experience significantly accelerated colonic transit compared to healthy individuals. Diarrheal episodes, prevalent among patients with IBD and CRC, further enhance GIT peristalsis, thereby reducing overall transit time24,25. Additionally, dosage form characteristics play a decisive role: larger particles generally have shorter transit times than smaller ones due to mechanical interactions with the intestinal mucosa26. A decreased GIT transit time directly impacts the retention and clearance of orally administered drug delivery systems, potentially undermining their therapeutic efficacy at the target site10. Consequently, when designing micro/nanomedicine delivery systems, comprehensive consideration of both particle size and transit time kinetics is essential to optimize site-specific drug retention and bioavailability.

The microbial barrier

The microbial barrier, primarily constituted by the gut microbiota, consists of diverse symbiotic bacteria that form a microbial shield through interactions with intestinal epithelial cells. These microbes are stratified into two ecological niches: the mucosal microbiota (dominated by Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus) and the luminal microbiota (enriched in Escherichia coli and Enterococcus), collectively adhering to the intestinal mucosal layer to establish a multilayered microbial barrier27. Gut microbiota can directly or indirectly affect the bioavailability of oral drugs, such as influencing drug metabolism through the gut microbial enzyme activity, and affecting oral drug transport via substrate competition or regulating the expression of transport proteins28. The microbial barrier serves as the core of the intestinal barrier, as the mechanical barrier, chemical barrier, and immune barrier can all be regulated by microorganisms.

Microbial communities interact with intestinal epithelial cells via two mechanisms: directly through pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)-bacterial molecular motifs that bind to pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) like nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain receptors (NODs) and toll-like receptors (TLRs) on or within epithelial cells; and indirectly through metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids and bile acids29. Upon PAMP recognition, PRRs activate intracellular signaling pathways via ligands, transcription factors, and kinases, triggering gene expression changes that modulate levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, antimicrobial peptides, and immunoreceptors-processes critical for maintaining intestinal homeostasis and barrier function30,31 (Fig. 3).

Certain bacterial metabolites produced by fermenting complex carbohydrates, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like acetate, propionate, and butyrate, play a critical role in maintaining intestinal health. SCFAs exert their effects by binding to G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) on intestinal cells and altering gene expression through inhibiting histone deacetylases (HDACs), processes essential for mitigating local inflammation, defending against pathogen invasion, and preserving intestinal barrier integrity32,33. Additionally, other microbiota-derived metabolites-including indole derivatives and bile acid metabolites-regulate intestinal permeability and barrier function by influencing the expression of tight junction proteins, further reinforcing the structural and functional integrity of the intestinal epithelium29,34. These metabolic interactions highlight the intricate crosstalk between gut bacteria and host cells, whereby microbial byproducts act as key modulators of mucosal homeostasis and immune surveillance35.

Gut dysbiosis, defined as alterations in gut bacterial composition and functional capacity, adversely impacts host health. Such dysbiosis shifts disrupt the intestinal microbial barrier, enabling pathogens to produce toxins that damage the epithelial barrier, promote systemic translocation of bacteria and metabolites, and thereby enhance infection susceptibility while inducing systemic inflammation33. Clinically, gut dysbiosis has been closely linked to a spectrum of disorders, including IBD, diabetes, obesity, liver diseases, and neurodegenerative conditions. Notably, emerging evidence highlights its pivotal role in oncogenesis: bacterial infections, such as Helicobacter pylori, drive tumorigenesis through mechanisms like activating the β-catenin signaling pathway, while CRC patients exhibit enriched abundances of specific bacteria-Bacteroides fragilis, Fusobacterium nucleatum, etc., in their fecal microbiota36. Thus, oral drugs targeting the microbial barrier for treating intestinal diseases primarily focus on restoring intestinal dysbiosis, such as through direct probiotic administration or micro/nano delivery systems, with the dual objectives of increasing the abundance of beneficial bacteria and their metabolites while reducing that of pathogenic bacteria and their metabolites.

The mechanical barrier

The intestinal mechanical barrier, also known as the physical barrier, is composed of the intestinal mucosal mucus layer, epithelial cells with TJs, the epithelial basal lamina, and the submucosal lamina propria37. This barrier functions as an essential defense mechanism, inhibiting the infiltration of pathogenic microorganisms and harmful substances into the gut, while simultaneously facilitating the selective passage of nutrients38 (Fig. 4).

Intestinal mucus is a complex hydrogel secreted by goblet cells and submucosal glands, composed of proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, antibodies, electrolytes, bacteria, and cellular debris, with mucins serving as the most abundant constituents. Most mucins are glycosylated, conferring a negative charge, and contain regularly spaced hydrophobic domains that exhibit high avidity for hydrophobic particles39. These structural features endow mucins with high avidity for both hydrophilic and hydrophobic interactions, enabling mucus to form a stratified barrier: a loosely adherent luminal mucus layer and a firmly adherent basal mucus layer closely associated with the epithelial surface. The basal mucus layer acts as a protective shield, physically blocking pathogens, digestive enzymes, and toxins from reaching the gastrointestinal epithelium, while the luminal layer traps luminal contents and facilitates their transit40. The mucus layer serves as a habitat for gut microbiota and functions as a natural barrier to prevent bacterial infections within the intestinal niche, thereby regulating the balance between mucosal immunity and external stimuli. Therefore, the integrity of the mucus layer is of great importance to maintain intestinal homeostasis, which is majorly dependent on the crosstalk of the structural component of mucus41.

The intestinal epithelium comprises a heterogeneous population of epithelial cells, including secretory lineages-such as mucin-producing goblet cells, antimicrobial peptide-secreting Paneth cells, chemosensory tuft cells, hormone-secreting enteroendocrine cells, and absorptive enterocytes (intestinal epithelial cells)42. Among these, enterocytes, goblet cells, and microfold (M) cells are central to maintaining gut homeostasis: enterocytes mediate nutrient absorption and barrier integrity; goblet cells secrete mucus to form a protective gel layer; and M cells facilitate antigen sampling from the intestinal lumen, collectively forming a multifunctional physical and immunological network29. Adjacent epithelial cells are interconnected by apical lateral TJs, a complex structure composed of junctional adhesion molecules, occludin, claudins, and tricellulin43. These TJs are critical for the paracellular barrier, regulating the selective passage of ions, solutes, and water between cells while restricting the translocation of pathogens and macromolecules. Dysfunction of TJ proteins-characterized by reduced expression or structural defects-compromises epithelial permeability, a hallmark of conditions like IBD, necrotizing enterocolitis, and intestinal ischemia‒reperfusion injury44. Increased gut permeability in these disorders presents a therapeutic opportunity: micro/nanomedicines engineered to leverage heightened epithelial permeability can enable targeted delivery to inflamed or damaged intestinal tissues, facilitating localized drug release and potentially enhancing treatment efficacy in diseases characterized by TJ barrier dysfunction as a core pathogenic mechanism.

Immune barrier

The intestinal mucosal immune barrier is primarily composed of GALT and disseminated immune cells. GALT, which includes intraepithelial lymphocytes, lamina propria lymphocytes, and intestinal lymph nodes, houses abundant lymphoid tissues and cells distributed among intestinal mucosal epithelial cells, within the lamina propria, and in the submucosa, providing niches for key immune cell populations-T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, mast cells, and innate lymphoid cells (ILCs)-each endowed with specialized functions45. Through intricate innate and adaptive immune mechanisms, this barrier precisely discriminates between commensal bacteria and pathogens to maintain intestinal immune homeostasis46 (Fig. 5).

Intestinal epithelial cells regulate innate and adaptive immunity. Intestinal epithelial cells condition dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages towards a tolerogenic phenotype through the production of TSLP, transforming growth factor-β (TGFβ) and retinoic acid (RA). These DCs promote the differentiation of naive CD4+ T cells into regulatory T (TReg) cells and the maturation of B cells into IgA-secreting plasma cells. In the innate immune system, innate immune cells-including macrophages, dendritic cells, and natural killer cells-employ PRRs, such as TLRs and NODs, to actively engage in innate immune responses. These receptors recognize conserved microbial-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs), such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and peptidoglycan, triggering intracellular signaling cascades like NF-κB and MAPK pathways. This activation induces the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-8) and antimicrobial peptides (e.g., defensins, cathelicidins), which directly eliminate pathogens while regulating inflammatory dynamics to maintain immune balance47. Macrophages also sense perturbations via TLRs, activating intracellular pro-inflammatory signaling pathways that drive pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, IL-12, IL-18, IL-23) and chemokine production, thereby facilitating neutrophil recruitment to the intestinal lamina propria. Neutrophils, in turn, aid in recruiting additional immune cells and promote mucosal healing through the release of mediators essential for inflammation resolution. Additionally, activated innate immune cells generate substantial TNF-α, leading to enhanced reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, elevated chemokine levels, and other pro-inflammatory responses48.

The lamina propria serves as a critical effector site for adaptive immune responses, housing antigen-specific lymphocytes and antibody-secreting plasma cells. T lymphocytes, upon activation by APCs, differentiate into effector subsets (e.g., Th1, Th17) to eliminate invasive pathogens or regulatory T cells (Tregs) to dampen excessive inflammation49. Under the co-stimulation of dendritic cells and T cells, B lymphocytes undergo isotype switching to differentiate into IgA-producing plasma cells. These cells are fundamental to mucosal immunity, as they play a crucial role in pathogen neutralization and immune exclusion at the epithelial surface50. Memory B/T cells in the lamina propria ensure rapid, antigen-specific recall responses, balancing protective immunity with tolerance to commensal microbiota and dietary antigens.

In IBD, immune dysregulation is characterized by a complex bidirectional interaction between the innate and adaptive immune systems. Initially, abnormalities within the innate immune system, such as the excessive secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines by macrophages (including IL-23 and TNF-α), precipitate disorders in the adaptive immune response47,51,52. These disorders manifest as imbalances in Th1 and Th2 cell regulation, as well as in the transformation of Th17 and Treg cells. The ensuing polarization towards Th1 and Th17 cell phenotypes leads to the overexpression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-2353. Concurrently, a functional deficiency in Tregs undermines immune suppression, thereby promoting Th17 differentiation and the emergence of IL-17+ and FoxP3+ T cell subsets. These adaptive responses worsen innate immune damage by causing intestinal cell apoptosis, reducing junctional proteins, and decreasing Paneth cell antibacterial peptides, weakening the intestinal barrier and sustaining inflammation. Collectively, these processes compromise intestinal barrier integrity, perpetuating a self-sustaining cycle of inflammation. Following abnormal immune activation, cytokine dysregulation, and ROS accumulation, damage to intestinal tissue54. Thus, therapeutic strategies targeting macrophage-mediated immune regulation and ROS scavenging are critical for managing IBD.

In CRC, the immunosuppressive microenvironment is a key barrier for tumor immunity55. Due to the regulation of immune response activated by tumor invasion, the CRC microenvironment gradually becomes immunosuppressive, which helps tumor cells evade immune response56. In advanced CRC, malignant tumors recruit Tregs and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) to suppress anti-tumor immune responses and promote tumor progression57. In addition, several inhibitory receptors, such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4), are known to be expressed on the surface of T cells and then bind to programmed cell death ligand-1 (PD-L1), which is constitutively expressed on the surface of cancer cells, inducing inhibition of immune responses58. Therefore, regulating the immune microenvironment is crucial for the treatment of CRC.

Oral micro/nanomedicine modulating the chemical barrier in intestinal disease

Chemical barriers pose the most significant challenge for oral drugs, and they are also the primary consideration in the design of oral carriers. To overcome the chemical barrier of the GIT, an oral micro/nanomedicine that travels from the stomach through the intestines has certain requirements: (1) It needs an acid-resistant carrier and can withstand degradation by digestive enzymes. (2) It requires prolonged retention in the intestine. Generally, single nanoparticles cannot satisfy these conditions, so most systems adopt micro-nano structural models, where the microstructure protects the nanoparticles until they reach the intestinal site for drug release. Additionally, the larger size of micro-structures can increase their residence time in the intestine.

Acid or enzyme digestion resistance

For acid-resistant and digestive enzyme-resistant micro/nano materials, polymers are widely used at present, including pH-sensitive biocompatible polymers, polysaccharides, and natural compounds. pH-sensitive biocompatible polymers include common commercial enteric coatings such as cellulose acetate phthalate59, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose acetate succinate (HPMCAS-HF)60,61, PCADK62, and methacrylic acid copolymers (Eudragit), which are used to coat the surfaces of nanoparticles to prevent degradation by gastric acid or digestive enzymes in the GIT. Among them, Eudragit is the most widely used pH-dependent coating polymer for oral drug delivery systems, such as Eudragit®S-100 (soluble at pH > 7.0)63, 64, 65, Eudragit® L100 (soluble at pH > 6.0)66, and Eudragit FS30D21 (soluble at pH > 6.5)67. Zhu et al.67 leveraged Eudragit® FS30D21 to develop an oral vaccine for genitorectal viral infections. Eudragit FS30D21 is pH-sensitive, dissolving in intestinal fluids at pH > 7.0 (found only in the terminal ileum), which prevents premature drug release. Eudragit FS30D-coated PLGA nanoparticles effectively protect vaccines and target them to the large intestine, inducing colorectal immunity in mice comparable to that induced by colorectal vaccination, which protects against rectal and vaginal viral challenges21.

Natural polysaccharides offer advantages such as high bioavailability, good biocompatibility, non-toxicity, low cost, and FDA-approved clinical applications. Currently, various natural polysaccharides have been extensively explored for oral colon-targeted drug delivery in micro/nanoparticle systems, including chitosan68, 69, 70, 71, 72, alginate73,74, guar gum75, reteograded starch76, pectin77, 78, 79, 80, 81, hyaluronic acid82,83, xanthan gum84, xylan85,86, fucoidan (FUC) combined with chitosan oligosaccharide (COS)87, eucommia ulmoides polysaccharide88, hydroxyethyl starch (HES)89, and lentinan (LNT)90, as shown in Table 168, 69, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76, 77, 78, 79, 80, 81, 82, 83, 84, 85, 86, 87, 88, 89, 90, 91, 92, 93. These polysaccharides not only possess the characteristics of acid resistance and resistance to digestive enzyme degradation themselves, but also have the property of colon-specific degradation. They generally act through swelling/erosion, degradation by colonic pH and enzymes, or specific bacterial-mediated processes, which can target and deliver drugs to specific colonic sites15. For instance, LNT, a natural food-based polysaccharide composed of β-glucan, exhibits dual capabilities of gastric acid resistance and targeting specificity. It remains stable in the stomach, resisting degradation by acid and enzymes. Mechanistically, LNT can specifically bind to Dectin-1 receptors on macrophages and undergo degradation via intestinal microbial β-glucanase. Lin et al.90 developed advanced macrophage-targeted oral LNT-based nanoparticles loaded with Budesonide (BUD) for the treatment of UC (Fig. 6A).

Among them, a chitosan cationic polysaccharide is most widely used in oral micro/nanomedicines for intestinal diseases due to its unique properties, such as binding to negatively charged intestinal mucus membranes to prolong retention time and enhance cellular uptake, as well as biodegradability via glycosidic bond hydrolysis by colonic microflora enzymes, which is crucial for intestinal targeting68,71. Chitosan can be used alone or in combination with other polysaccharides to coat the surface of nanoparticles for protection94,95. For instance, Li et al.96 developed an oral infliximab (IFX)-loaded nano-in-microparticle system based on chitosan/carboxymethyl chitosan (CMC) and alginate (Alg) for the IBD therapy. Chitosan can also form nanoparticles alone for drug delivery. For example, in CRC treatment, chitosan nanoparticles enable sustained and targeted delivery of anticancer agents. Chen et al.70 developed chitosan nanoparticles encapsulating both photothermal (IR780) and photodynamic (5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA)) reagents. Stable in acidic gastric conditions, these nanoparticles enhance oral absorption and local accumulation in mouse colon tumors (CT-26 cells) after oral gavage, boosting colorectal oral therapeutic capacity (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, chitosan carries a positive charge and can form stable network-structured hydrogels with various anionic polymers through electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic effects, etc. Examples include chitosan/alginate hydrogels97, chitosan/gelatin hydrogels98, and chitosan/carrageenan hydrogel99, which form microspheres to protect and deliver nanodrugs to the intestinal site. Among them, alginate/chitosan hydrogels have been widely applied to load nanoparticles for delivery to the colon83,100, 101, 102, 103, 104.

In recent years, driven by their diverse functional properties, natural-source compounds with acid and digestive enzyme resistance have been increasingly explored as carriers for oral nanomedicines, such as microbial membrane-derived and lignin-derived nanoparticles. Microbial membrane-based micro/nanocarriers like yeast cell walls105, spore shells106, and bacterial biofilms107 not only protect oral drug delivery systems but also endow them with intestinal targeting, colonization capabilities, and the ability to modulate gut microbiota. For example, yeast cell walls composed of β-glucan are stable in the GIT due to the absence of degrading enzymes, can be recognized by macrophage receptors for immune modulation, and contain mannose as a prebiotic to regulate the microbiome108, 109, 110 (Fig. 6C). Clostridium butyricum is a strictly anaerobic spore-forming bacillus that can produce butyrate to maintain intestinal barrier function. Its dormant spores, which are more resistant to environmental insults than living bacteria, can immediately germinate and enter a state of normal growth in oxygenated intestinal fluid. Deng et al.111 surface-modified Clostridium butyricum with hyaluronic acid (HA). The dormant spores, together with the surface HA coating, can effectively protect the bacterium against the complex gastrointestinal environment. By utilizing both the immunosuppressive property of HA and the butyrate-producing capacity of C. butyricum, they alleviated intestinal inflammation, restored the intestinal barrier, and reduced interstitial inflammation as well as pathological damage to extraintestinal organs through remodeling microbial metabolites and decreasing microbial translocation.

Additionally, some oral nanomedicines enhance their stability in the GIT through intermolecular reactions. Pan et al.112 developed acid-resistant antioxidant nanoparticles (EGA NPs) via a “disintegration and reorganization” strategy. Using epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) and 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA), the team exploited pH-responsive degradation of EGCG's ester bond under acidic conditions to release polyphenolic building blocks. These blocks, along with 5-ASA and formaldehyde, were reorganized into oligomers through phenol-aldehyde condensation and Mannich reactions. The self-assembled EGA NPs maintain gastrointestinal stability while retaining EGCG's antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities for colitis treatment (Fig. 6D). Lee et al.113 prepared nanoparticles through the nano-aggregation of an amphiphilic conjugate between HA and bilirubin. In this system, the combination of bilirubin core drug and steric hindrance of the self-assembled nanostructure might exert a synergetic effect on enhancing the resistance of oral HABN to hyaluronidase and finally exerting its anti-inflammatory ability for colitis by targeting and regulating the intestinal immune and gut microbiome.

Prolonged intestinal retention time

Prolonged retention of oral drug delivery systems in the intestinal tract is critical for enhancing therapeutic efficacy against intestinal diseases, and multiple strategies have been developed, including electrostatic adsorption, microparticulate systems, hydrogen bonding interactions, and microbial colonization. Nanoparticles modified with positively charged polymers like chitosan or poly-l-lysine (PLL) can electrostatically adhere to the negatively charged intestinal mucosal layer, such as mucins, thereby enhancing muco-adhesion61,114. Microparticulate systems, owing to their larger size, can reduce drug absorption in the small intestine and prolong retention in the colon, favoring accumulation in inflamed intestinal tissues through physical entrapment or gravity-driven deposition in mucosal folds. For example, Zhang et al.103 developed a novel oral nano-in-micro system for the efficient colonic delivery of curcumin (Cur). They encapsulated HA/zein complex nanoparticles loaded with Cur into alginate/chitosan hydrogel microparticles (Cur@NMPs). Oral administration of Cur@NMPs enabled rapid release of Cur at the colonic site. NMPs exhibited significant colon retention and bioadhesive capacity in colon tissues, and significantly mitigated colitis symptoms in DSS-induced UC mice by inhibiting the TLR4/NF-κB pathway (Fig. 7A). Lin et al.115 encapsulated Cas9/sgRNA ribonucleoprotein (RNP) nanoclusters (NCs) with outer membrane vesicles derived from Escherichia coli (dOMVs), and then further encapsulated them into calcium alginate microspheres (CAMs). CAMs can protect them from degradation by gastric acid, enabling efficient delivery of RNP NCs@dOMVs to the intestines. RNP NCs@dOMVs can traverse the mucosal barrier, target inflammatory macrophages, and effectively perform genomic editing of TNF-α in the nucleus for the treatment of colitis.

Hydrogen bonding-driven coatings represent another effective strategy, enabling prolonged adhesion to the intestinal surface and sustained drug release. Zhao et al.116 developed a bio-adhesive liquid coacervate via hydrogen bonding-driven nanoparticle self-assembly to improve oral drug retention in the gut. The core-shell nanoparticles, with hydrophobic cores and catechol-functionalized PEG chains, form pH/salt-stable coacervates (NPA2) through fluid-phase separation. These adhere to intestinal mucosa via reversible wet bio-adhesion, retaining drugs for >2 days. In a DSS-colitis rat model, NPA2 sustained dexamethasone release, reduced inflammation, restored gut microbiota, and minimized systemic exposure, highlighting its potential for IBD and other gastrointestinal therapies (Fig. 7B).

The adhesion of a probiotic bacterium to the host intestinal tract can retard its transit through the gut, enhancing its effectiveness. Inspired by this property, Wang et al.117 engineered Bifidobacterium longum (BL) as a living microcarrier, conjugating hyaluronic acid-bilirubin nanomedicine (HABN) to its surface to form BL@HABN. This system not only prolonged gastrointestinal retention but also modulated gut microbiota composition, providing a synergistic approach for IBD therapy (Fig. 7C). Moreover, beyond nanoparticles, such strategies can also be extended to enhancing the intestinal colonization time of probiotics through various surface coatings. To improve the survival and colonization capacity of probiotics, Zhao et al.118 modified the surface of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum KLDS1.0386 (LP) with a tannic acid-Fe3+ (TA-Fe) crosslinked network, and further applied an outer layer of soybean isolate protein-xanthan gum (SX) to form a bilayer microcapsule (LP@TA-Fe/SX). Compared with unmodified LP, the survival rate of LP in LP@TA-Fe/SX increased by 8.5-fold, while its colonization capacity in the colon was extended to 48 h, significantly alleviating DSS-induced colitis.

Summary

Oral micro/nanomedicines encounter several challenges in overcoming chemical barriers, such as the degradation of carriers and drugs due to gastric acid and digestive enzymes, non-specific release triggered by pH gradients in the GIT, and inadequate targeting capability resulting from brief intestinal retention times. To address these challenges, several strategies have been proposed. These include the utilization of pH-sensitive synthetic polymers (e.g., the Eudragit series) and natural polysaccharides (e.g., chitosan, alginate) as carriers, which offer resistance to acid and enzymes and enable colon-specific degradation through microbial enzyme or pH-driven mechanisms, thereby facilitating targeted release. Additionally, intestinal retention time can be extended through approaches such as micro-nano composite structures (where microcarriers shield nanoparticles), electrostatic adsorption using cationic polymers, or hydrogen bond-driven bioadhesion. Furthermore, the use of microbial membrane-derived carriers (e.g., yeast cell walls) and molecular self-assembly technologies (e.g., EGA nanoparticles) can enhance the stability and targeting efficacy of the delivery system.

Oral micro/nanomedicine modulating microbial barrier in intestinal disease

Current therapeutic approaches targeting the gut microbiota, mainly including dietary interventions119, 120, 121, probiotic supplementation122,123, and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)124, exhibit variable efficacy in managing intestinal diseases, underscoring the need for improved strategies30. However, these approaches have certain limitations: probiotics are prone to inactivation under harsh physiological conditions such as strong gastric acid and digestive enzymes in the GIT; dietary regulation plays a supporting role but lacks precise intervention; fecal transplantation faces ethical controversies and regulatory gaps125. The advancement of nanotechnology facilitates both direct and indirect approaches for oral drug targeting of the microbial barrier. We can employ nanotechnology to engineer probiotics, directly delivering these modified microorganisms to intestinal sites. This approach enhances their ability to regulate gut microbiota and its metabolites as well as their other therapeutic functions. Furthermore, we can engineer diverse oral drug delivery systems, such as utilizing natural biomaterials and bioactive compounds as micro/nanocarriers. Direct modulating intestinal microenvironment

Despite the challenges in oral delivery of probiotics, which face the GI tract's harsh environment leading to poor bioavailability and limited colonization, probiotic therapy remains the most effective method for directly regulating the gut microbiota, as these live microorganisms show significant potential in preventing and treating various intestinal diseases through mechanisms such as colonizing the gut to regulate microecological balance136, enhancing mucosal barriers137, inhibiting pathogens, and regulating immune responses123,138, 139, 140, 141, 142. Engineered probiotics, created via genetic or chemical modification to enhance stress resistance, target specificity, or functional outputs, offer a promising solution143. Such modifications overcome natural probiotics' limitations and boost clinical utility. Integrating these engineered strains with nanotechnology aims to create multifunctional platforms that synergize microbial and synthetic components, addressing unmet needs in intestinal disease management and advancing microbiota-based therapies toward translation.

Currently, multiple engineered probiotics have been used for the treatment of intestinal diseases in animals. Bifidobacterium longum, a beneficial bacterial strain naturally present in the human gut, has been engineered by researchers Cao et al.126 to create a probiotic formulation (BL@B-SA50) modified with artificial enzymes, designed to reconstitute immune homeostasis in IBD patients. These enzymes are conjugated to the bacterial surface using C18-PEG-B linkers, which serve to protect the bacteria within the gastrointestinal environment. The biocompatible enzymes effectively scavenge excess ROS in the gut and reduce the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This anti-inflammatory action enhances the survival of the probiotic, thereby accelerating the repair of the intestinal barrier and facilitating the restoration of gut microbiota homeostasis (Fig. 8A). Xie et al.127 developed an oral nanosystem EcN@Fe-TA@mGN, coating EcN with Fe3+-tannic acid (Fe-TA) networks and carboxymethylated β-glucan (mGN) to enhance gastrointestinal stress resistance and colonization. This system improves probiotic survival and intestinal retention, with colon enzymes degrading mGN to expose Fe-TA for intestinal adhesion and sustained retention; degraded mGN fragments become short-chain fatty acids that synergize with EcN to regulate microbiota and alleviate colitis (Fig. 8B). Niu et al.128 constructed an oral colon-specific drug delivery system (CBS-CS@Lipo/Oxp/MTZ) by covalently conjugating Clostridium butyricum spores (CBS) with chitosan (CS)-coated liposomes loaded with chemotherapeutic drug oxaliplatin (Oxp) and anti-anaerobic drug metronidazole (MTZ). After oral administration, CBS germinates into CB and colonizes the colon, where colon-specific β-glucosidase triggers CS degradation to achieve dual colon-targeted release of liposomal drugs. The released liposomes penetrate tumor tissues via CS-mediated permeability enhancement to eliminate local tumor-associated bacteria, while CB regulates gut microbiota to block pathogen translocation to tumors, eliminating intratumoral pathogens and immunosuppressive cells. Lipopolysaccharides from MTZ-killed Fusobacterium nucleatum and tumor antigens from Oxp-induced immunogenic cell death synergize to enhance CD8+ T cell infiltration and reactivate antitumor immunity (Fig. 8C).

Indirect modulating gut microenvironment

Nature-derived biological components and bioactive compounds have provided important inspiration for the regulation of gut microbiota. Biologically derived vesicles belong to nanoscale membranous vesicle structures, which internally carry various biomolecules such as nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids144. These vesicles can not only participate in immune regulation processes but also form dynamic interactive relationships with gut microbiota. By regulating the composition and functions of the microbiota, they further maintain the homeostatic balance of the gut microbial ecosystem145. These mainly include plant-derived nanoparticles, bacterial-derived vesicles, and biofilm-coated nanomaterials.

Plant-derived nanoparticles

Plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (PELNs) are vesicular structures composed of a phospholipid bilayer, containing lipids, proteins, nucleic acids, and bioactive compounds146. They can be extracted from various plant tissues (such as garlic, ginger, bitter melon) and have been widely applied due to their remarkable advantages of diverse sources, natural biocompatibility, and ease of large-scale preparation147. Orally administered garlic-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (GENs) alleviate colonic inflammation by inhibiting the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB-mediated signaling pathway and regulating the gut microbiota. GENs increase the relative abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Lachnospiraceae, reduce the proportion of potential pathogens like Helicobacter, ameliorate DSS-induced dysbiosis, and promote the biosynthesis of SCFAs, thereby effectively curbing inflammation progression129. Ginger-derived exosome-like nanoparticles (GELNs) are preferentially internalized by lactobacilli via lipid-based recognition, delivering microRNAs that target Lactobacillus rhamnosus to boost indole-3-carboxaldehyde (I3A) production. This process activates the IL-22 signaling pathway, which strengthens tight junction proteins, reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines, and modulates gut microbiota130. Gao et al.131 extracted extracellular vesicles from bitter melon (MCEVs) using centrifugation techniques. These vesicles reduce harmful bacteria like Escherichia–Shigella and boost beneficial ones such as Muribaculum and Alistipes, restoring microbial balance. MCEVs also increase intestinal indole metabolites, aiding in inflammation control by adjusting gut microbiota and metabolites (Fig. 9A).

Inulin and xylan, derived from plant sources, have been utilized in the synthesis of nanoparticles for the delivery of various drugs aimed at treating CRC by modulating gut microbiota in conjunction with chemotherapy. Zhu et al.148 developed nanoparticles based on an inulin-ursodeoxycholic acid conjugate (UIRN) for encapsulating the anti-colon cancer drug regorafenib (REG). UIRN not only significantly enhanced intratumoral drug concentration but also improved gut microbiota composition by increasing the abundance of probiotics, reducing the proportion of tumor-promoting bacteria, and elevating levels of short-chain fatty acids. Additionally, when used in combination with chemotherapeutic and immunotherapeutic agents, UIRN demonstrated considerable adjuvant therapeutic effects. Another nanoparticle formulation, based on a xylan-stearic acid conjugate (SCXN), was employed to deliver capecitabine (Cap). Upon oral administration, SCXN prolonged drug retention in the bloodstream and increased intratumoral drug concentration. Concurrently, SCXN facilitated the proliferation of probiotics and the production of short-chain fatty acids, thereby significantly enhancing antitumor immunity and increasing the tumor inhibition rate from 5.29% to 71.78%149.

Bacterial-derived vesicles

Gut microbiota secrete abundant extracellular vesicles (Gm-EVs), which play a critical role in bacteria-to-bacteria and bacteria-to-host communications. Zu et al.150 used tea leaf lipid/pluronic F127-coated curcumin nanocrystals (CN@Lp127s) to train the gut microbiota of mice. They compared different groups, including those receiving FMT from healthy mic, Gm-EVs from healthy mice, FMT from CN@Lp127-trained healthy mice, and Gm-EVs from CN@Lp127-trained healthy mice. The results showed that oral Gm-EVs from CN@Lp127-trained healthy mice exhibited a superior therapeutic effect on UC. It not only alleviated colonic inflammation, promoted mucosal repair, and regulated the gut microbiota, but also modulated purine metabolism to reduce uric acid levels, resulting in robust improvement of UC.

Additionally, Bacterial membrane vesicles (BMVs), as a critical component of postbiotics, are being regarded as potential alternatives to probiotics. These membrane-bound vesicular structures actively secreted by bacteria can enrich functional substances from parental bacteria (such as antimicrobial peptides, enzymes, signaling molecules, and lipid metabolites), circumventing challenges like low survival rate in the gastrointestinal acidic environment and limited colonization ability of traditional probiotics151. Orally administered Roseburia intestinalis-derived extracellular vesicles (R-EVs) significantly accumulate in inflamed colonic tissues, repairing intestinal damage by inhibiting inflammatory cytokines and increasing the expression of mucin and tight junction proteins. Additionally, the Ile-Pro-Ile (IPI) sequence in R-EVs effectively regulates gut microbiota dysbiosis and enhances the abundance of Bifidobacterium in the microbial community132. Similarly, extracellular vesicles from Akkermansia muciniphila (Akk EVs) contain bioactive molecules that enhance intestinal barriers, prevent colitis, and repair colon tissue. They boost mucin MUC2 levels, improve mucus layer integrity, reduce permeability, and fortify the intestinal barrier. Akk EVs increase the proportion of Firmicutes while downregulating Proteobacteria, thus improving gut microbiota composition133. Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG)-derived extracellular vesicles (LGG-EVs) alleviate intestinal inflammation by inhibiting the activation of the TLR4–NF-κB–NLRP3 axis. After administration, LGG-EVs decrease the abundance of Helicobacter and Escherichia–Shigella while upregulating Muribaculaceae, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, and Akkermansia, reshaping the gut microbiota of DSS-induced colitis mice and altering intestinal microbial metabolic pathways134 (Fig. 9B).

Biofilm-coated nanomaterials

Cell membrane-coated nanodrugs offer excellent cell affinity and can specifically bind with biomacromolecules, making them ideal for treating intestinal diseases152. Inspired by probiotics' role in easing intestinal diseases, probiotic membrane-coated nanodrugs are crucial for rebalancing intestinal microbiota and restoring metabolic functions. A biomimetic nanozyme (CF@EM) from Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 enhances anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects through targeted intestinal colonization, boosting Lactobacillus and Muribaculum levels while reducing Clostridium-sensu-stricto and Escherichia–Shigella. CF@EM also regulates active metabolites like secondary bile acids, offering multi-target therapy by maintaining microbial balance and normalizing intestinal metabolites107 (Fig. 9C).

The intestinal microbiota is mainly composed of bacteria, but fungi like Saccharomyces cerevisiae also play a crucial role in maintaining gut health and influencing disease. Saccharomyces cerevisiae is effective in delivering bioactive compounds due to its compatibility and microbiota-regulating properties. Li et al.110 developed a CM@YM composite by encapsulating Cur-MPN in yeast microcapsules, which protects it from the gastrointestinal environment and enhances targeting and retention in inflamed colons. This formulation reduces intestinal inflammation, repairs the intestinal barrier, and promotes a healthy microbiota by scavenging ROS, lowering pro-inflammatory cytokines, and increasing SCFA content. The synergistic effect of the above multiple pathways significantly improves the therapeutic effect on IBD (Fig. 9D).

Summary

Existing therapies targeting the gut microbiota, including probiotic supplementation, dietary interventions, and fecal microbiota transplantation, present notable limitations. Probiotics are susceptible to inactivation within the harsh environment of the GIT due to strong acids and digestive enzymes, resulting in low bioavailability and limited colonization capacity. Dietary interventions primarily serve an auxiliary role and lack precise intervention capabilities. Furthermore, FMT is fraught with ethical controversies and regulatory gaps, impeding its widespread application. These challenges obstruct the precise modulation of the intestinal microbiota and the targeted treatment of diseases.

To address these challenges, oral micro/nanomedicines have emerged as effective solutions through both direct and indirect regulation of the intestinal microenvironment. In the realm of direct regulation, nanotechnology is employed to engineer probiotics by enhancing their stress resistance, targeting capabilities, and colonization potential through methods such as artificial enzyme modification and the application of surface coatings, including Fe-TA networks and carboxymethylated β-glucan. These modifications enable probiotics to survive within the intestinal environment and perform functions such as regulating microbiota balance, repairing intestinal barriers, and inhibiting inflammation. Conversely, for indirect regulation, natural-sourced micro/nano carriers are utilized. These include plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles, such as those from garlic, ginger, and bitter melon, bacterial membrane vesicles secreted by species like Roseburia intestinalis and Akkermansia muciniphila, and biofilm-coated nanomaterials, including those coated with Escherichia coli membranes or yeast cell walls. These carriers facilitate precise modulation of the intestinal microbial barrier by altering the composition of the gut microbiota, enhancing the production of beneficial metabolites like short-chain fatty acids, and suppressing inflammatory signaling pathways, thereby contributing to the amelioration of intestinal diseases.

Oral micro/nanomedicine modulating the mechanical barrier in intestinal disease

The intestinal mechanical barrier functions to prevent the intrusion of pathogenic microorganisms and harmful substances while selectively allowing nutrient passage, serving as a crucial physical barrier between the host and external stimuli to ward off threats from unwanted antigens like pathogens and particulate matter. The mucus layer and intestinal epithelial cells are pivotal components of this barrier, compelling the current design of oral micro/nanomedicines to possess strong intestinal mucosal penetration capabilities and the ability to cross the intestinal epithelial cells for better access to the lamina propria and interaction with immune cells.

Mucus-penetration

The intestinal mucus, with a bulk viscosity around 1000 times that of water153, poses a significant challenge and opportunity for nanoparticle-based therapeutics. Its porous yet adhesive matrix can trap nanoparticles through size exclusion, electrostatic interactions with negatively charged mucin moieties, or hydrophobic binding to mucin domains. Orally delivered nanoparticles typically face three fates: (1) rapid transit with chyme and fecal excretion; (2) temporary retention in the loosely adherent mucus until mucus turnover; (3) or deeper penetration into the basal mucus or epithelial uptake, which is critical for therapeutic efficacy22. The mucus traps nanoparticles via hydrophobic forces, hydrogen bonds, and electrostatic interactions, facilitating their elimination and impeding accumulation at intestinal inflammatory sites. For example, catechol can enhance the mucoadhesion of polymeric materials by forming covalent but not covalent bonds with mucin. Zhang et al.154 developed colon-targeted nanomicelles using catechol groups, which exhibited mucoadhesive effects and azo reductase-triggered drug release profiles for curcumin delivery in UC treatment. This nanosystem significantly enhanced cellular uptake of nanoparticles and their prolonged retention on colonic mucosa, which was mediated by the strong mucoadhesive property of the catechol structure.

To further enhance the mucosal penetration ability of nanoparticles, strategies have focused on altering nanoparticle properties such as hydrophilicity, shape, size, charge, and rigidity. Bao et al.155 demonstrated that shape and rigidity-tuned α-lactalbumin nanotubes exhibit enhanced transport across intestinal mucus and cellular barriers (Fig. 10), while Guo et al.156 showed that particle shape impacts mucus permeation, transepithelial transport, and bioavailability. These studies underscore the importance of deep mucus penetration for improving the efficacy of orally delivered nanoparticle-based therapeutics targeting intestinal diseases.

Pluronic F127 (PF127), an FDA-approved copolymer, is frequently used to modify the surfaces of nanoparticles. It can effectively weaken the physicochemical interactions between nanotherapeutics and mucin glycoproteins, thereby improving their mucus permeation capacity157. Xie et al.158 developed PF127-functionalized silk fibroin nanoparticles loaded with resveratrol (RSV) for oral treatment of UC. The hydrophilic segments of PF127 prevented the nanoparticles from aggregating in simulated intestinal fluid, maintaining stable particle size. In vitro studies used HEC hydrogel, which shares similar microrheological characteristics and chemical composition with intestinal mucus, to mimic the movement of nanoparticles in mucus. PF127-modified nanoparticles exhibited relatively free diffusion in this model, and results showed that PF127 significantly enhanced the mucus-penetrating properties of the nanoparticles. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that PF127-functionalized nanoparticles possessed potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. Similarly, their team further modified PF127 onto the surface of different nanoparticles, such as gold shell (AuS)-polymeric core nanotherapeutics loaded with curcumin (CUR) (P127-AuS@CURs)159 and mulberry biomass-derived nanomedicines loaded with resveratrol nanocrystals (RNs) (FP127@RN-MLNs)160. Due to the unique fluorine effect, this modification significantly improved their stability in the colon and enhanced their mucus infiltration and mucosal penetration capacities, thereby greatly strengthening the therapeutic efficacy of oral nanomedicines against UC.

Proteolytic enzymes such as bromelain (BRO) have also been used to enhance mucus penetration. Wilcox et al.161 investigated poly(acrylic acid) nanoparticles decorated with bromelain (PAA-BRO) and found that PAA-BRO could reduce mucus gel strength and promote glycoprotein release from the mucus network without disrupting the gel structure, thus enhancing nanoparticles permeability while preserving mucosal protection.

Enhancing intestinal epithelial cells crossing

Once micro/nanomedicines penetrate the mucus layer, their interaction with intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) becomes crucial. The absorption of oral nanomedicines in IECs primarily relies on two passive transport pathways: transcellular and paracellular transport162. In transcellular transport, nanomaterial endocytosis mainly occurs via macropinocytosis, clathrin-mediated endocytosis, caveolae-mediated endocytosis, and non-receptor-mediated endocytosis163. In contrast, paracellular transport involves nanomaterials passing through intercellular spaces by disrupting TJs, which are key for hydrophilic molecule passage41,51,164 (Fig. 11). After successfully traversing the mucus and cellular barriers, nanoparticles are absorbed into the systemic circulation. They are transported to the liver via the hepatic portal veins, where they undergo hepatic first-pass metabolism. Research showed that some nanomaterials, such as anionic nanoparticles165 and polymer films with surface nanotopography166, can damage the IEC barrier by affecting tight junctions, enhancing the transport of oral proteins or other macromolecular drugs in the GIT.

In IBD patients, the intestinal epithelium is characterized by excessive apoptosis of IECs, epithelial defects and dysfunction of TJs, contributing to increased permeability of the intestinal barrier and a loss of barrier function40,167. While this breakdown facilitates the enhanced penetration of oral nanomedicines into the intestinal tissue, it simultaneously necessitates that these therapeutic agents actively participate in barrier restoration. Wang et al.168 developed an oral nanomedicine (OPEN) embedding pifithrin-α (PFTα) to treat IBD by inhibiting excessive IECs apoptosis. Oral PFTα is prone to protonation and metabolic elimination in the stomach, while OPEN stabilizes PFTα in mesoporous nanoparticles (MNBs) to avoid leakage, enabling precise targeting of IBD lesions. OPEN reduces IEC apoptosis, promotes IEC proliferation, inhibits ROS and inflammatory factors, and restores intestinal microbiota balance by suppressing p53 activity (Fig. 12A).

In light of the readily modifiable characteristics of nanoparticles, current mainstream design strategies have focused on enhancing the active targeting function of nanoparticles to intestinal epithelial cells to further improve the delivery efficiency of oral nanomedicines. For instance, in colitis or CRC, receptors such as transferrin receptor (TfR), folate receptor (FR), CD44, and CD98 are overexpressed on the surfaces of colonic epithelial cells and macrophages169, 170, 171. Modifying the surfaces of oral nanomedicines with corresponding ligands can significantly enhance their ability to target intestinal epithelial cells and reduce side effects. Chen et al.172 developed a novel targeted delivery system by coating negatively charged HA onto positively charged tannic acid (TA)/berberine (BBR) self-assemblies, leveraging HA-CD44 interaction to target inflamed colonic epithelial cells and pro-inflammatory macrophages. HA-coated TB (HTB) enhanced cellular uptake and accumulation in inflamed colon lesions. In DSS-induced acute colitis, HTB treatment suppressed proinflammatory cytokines, restored tight junction proteins, normalized gut microbiota, and exhibited anti-inflammatory effects (Fig. 12B). This approach aims to deliver therapeutic agents directly to epithelial cells, promoting healing and maintaining barrier integrity.

Alternatively, certain materials such as chylomicrons (dietary lipids packaged into nanoparticles by enterocytes) enter lymphatic vessels beneath the epithelium, known as lacteals. These materials are transported through the lymphatic system via a network of lymph nodes into the downstream systemic circulation, bypassing hepatic first-pass metabolism173. The chylomicron transport pathway offers significant advantages for oral micro/nanomedicines. Leveraging this feature, Kim et al.174 designed oral nanoparticles for lymphatic-mediated systemic delivery by decorating their surface with bile acids (BA). Utilizing the ileocyte-specific apical sodium-dependent bile acid transporter (ASBT), BA-conjugated nanoparticles mimic dietary lipid absorption: digested fats are internalized via ASBT, reassembled into triglyceride-rich chylomicrons within enterocytes, and processed by the endoplasmic reticulum/Golgi apparatus. These fatty nanoparticles bypass hepatic metabolism by entering lymphatic vessels, significantly enhancing oral bioavailability through the chylomicron transport pathway (Fig. 12C).

Summary

The mucus layer and the tight junctions between intestinal epithelial cells represent significant obstacles for oral micro/nanomedicines in surmounting the intestinal mechanical barrier. The mucus layer entraps nanoparticles through mechanisms such as size exclusion, electrostatic interactions with negatively charged mucins, and hydrophobic binding. Consequently, nanoparticles are either rapidly excreted with chyme, temporarily retained within the loosely adherent mucus layer and subsequently cleared during mucus turnover, or encounter difficulty in penetrating deeply into the basal mucus or being internalized by epithelial cells. Conversely, the tight junctions between intestinal epithelial cells limit the passage of substances through intercellular spaces, while the efficacy of transcellular transport is further influenced by interactions between nanoparticles and epithelial cells. Notably, under inflammatory conditions, increased apoptosis of epithelial cells and compromised barrier function may facilitate penetration; however, these conditions also necessitate the involvement of drugs in barrier repair.

To address these challenges, current solutions primarily focus on enhancing the penetration of mucus and the translocation across intestinal epithelial cells. In terms of mucus penetration, the efficiency is augmented by modulating the physicochemical properties of nanoparticles, including hydrophilicity, shape, size, charge, and rigidity. For instance, surface modification with Pluronic F127 reduces interactions with mucins; bromelain is employed to decrease mucus gel strength while preserving its protective function; and α-lactalbumin nanotubes, optimized for shape and rigidity, demonstrate enhanced penetration through mucus and cellular barriers. Regarding the translocation across intestinal epithelial cells, both transcellular pathways (such as macropinocytosis and clathrin-mediated endocytosis) and paracellular pathways (such as the disruption of tight junctions) are exploited. Furthermore, targeted modifications, such as HA conjugation to target CD44 receptors, enhance specificity and uptake by inflamed epithelial cells. Additionally, utilizing the chylomicron transport pathway, exemplified by bile acid-modified nanoparticles that mimic lipid absorption, can circumvent hepatic first-pass metabolism. Meanwhile, certain nanomedicine strategies continue to be developed and refined.

Oral micro/nanomedicine modulating immune barrier in intestinal disease

Developing the oral drug delivery system that targets macrophages and responds to ROS holds significant promise for the treatment of IBD and CRC. In the pathological mechanisms underlying IBD and CRC, both innate and adaptive immune responses within the intestinal mucosa are pivotal, forming a disease-specific intestinal mucosal immune barrier. UC is associated with excessive ROS production and overexpression of inflammatory markers in colonic epithelial cells and macrophages175. Similarly, CRC primarily stems from a long-term inflammatory microenvironment characterized by dysregulated local immune responses in the colorectum. Many innate immune cells, mainly M1 macrophages and neutrophils, are recruited to the colorectal epithelial cells. Their continuous production of inflammatory factors leads to severe inflammation and intestinal immune dysregulation, ultimately promoting colorectal tumorigenesis57,104,135,176, 177, 178. Therefore, developing the oral drug delivery system that targets immune cells and responds to ROS holds significant promise for the treatment of IBD and CRC.

Targeted immune cells

Macrophages, as the initial responders in the innate immune system to nanomaterials and the main cells producing ROS, have become a focal point for cell-targeting strategies in nano-based therapies for IBD. Activated macrophages express numerous ligands and receptors on their surfaces, such as cationic amino acid transporter protein 2 (CAT-2), dectin-1, macrophage galactose-type C-type lectin (MGL-2) receptor, mannose receptors, and lactoferrin receptor16,20,179,180. These can be exploited as molecular targets to anchor drug delivery systems through specific interactions.

For instance, CAT-2 is highly expressed when macrophages polarize into the M1 phenotype. Arginine, an important modulator of macrophages, is transported through CAT-2. Li et al.181 designed an l-arginine-modified selenium nanozyme (Se-CA) that targets M1 macrophages and scavenges ROS for the targeted oral treatment of UC. Se-CA can target M1 macrophages by binding to CAT-2 on the surface of M1 macrophages. Oral administration of Se-CA effectively treats colitis through its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, inhibition of ferroptosis, and regulation of intestinal flora (Fig. 13A). Galactose receptors on macrophages can specifically recognize, bind to, and internalize endogenous and exogenous d-galactose and N-acetyl galactosamine. Zeeshan et al.182 have documented the development of galactose-conjugated poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles (GAL-PLGA). The oral administration of GAL-PLGA facilitates targeted delivery to macrophages at inflamed sites through macrophage MGL-2 receptors. Zu et al.183 demonstrated that exosomes derived from tea leaves exhibit surface galactose groups, which are internalized via MGL-2 receptors on macrophages. Furthermore, these exosomes have been shown to decrease ROS production, suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, and promote the secretion of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 by macrophages. Orally administered NTs effectively mitigate inflammatory bowel responses, restore compromised colonic barriers, and enhance the diversity and abundance of gut microbiota, thereby preventing or alleviating inflammatory bowel disease and colitis-associated colon cancer (Fig. 13B). The mannose receptor (MR), a member of the C-type lectin superfamily, is predominantly expressed on the surface of immune cells such as macrophages and DC cells. Oral nanodrug delivery systems modified with mannose have emerged as a feasible therapeutic approach. Sun et al.93 used mannosylated decamethylenediamine-grafted carboxymethyl inulin amphiphilic acid as a template to prepare mannosylated nanoparticles (Man-NPs) via a self-assembly method. In vitro results showed that Man-NPs were more efficiently internalized by activated macrophages, while in vivo studies demonstrated their effective accumulation at the inflamed sites of mice with colitis.

Natural cell membrane-coated nanoparticles possess the ability to evade clearance and target inflammatory sites. Macrophage membranes, in particular, not only preserve drug activity but also exhibit targeting effects due to their innate inflammation-directed chemotactic property. Bao et al.135 constructed a biomimetic nanomedicine for treating IBD by encapsulating mouse cathelicidin-related antimicrobial peptide (mCRAMP, a homolog of LL-37)-modified polydopamine nanoparticles (PDA NPs) with macrophage membranes (PCM NPs). This strategy integrates the anti-inflammatory and immune-regulatory functions of PDA NPs with the microbiota-modulating activity of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs). The resulting biomimetic nanomedicine enables efficient targeting to colonic inflammatory sites, where it modulates mucosal immune homeostasis and optimizes intestinal microbiota, thereby significantly enhancing the therapeutic efficacy against colitis in mouse models (Fig. 13C).

Although neutrophils also play an important role in innate immunity, there are relatively few studies on developing oral micro/nanomedicines targeting them. Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs) are reticular structures released by neutrophils, mainly composed of DNA, histones, granule proteins, and enzymes. Their role in intestinal diseases has attracted extensive attention in recent years184. Many studies have shown that excessive formation of NETs or impaired clearance can increase inflammation levels and cause local tissue or mucosal damage185. Dong et al.186 encapsulated Staphylococcal nuclease (SNase), an enzyme with strong DNA-degrading ability, in sodium alginate and calcium chloride hydrogels to prepare an oral nanoformulation (ALG-SNase) for the treatment of ulcerative colitis. Oral administration of ALG-SNase nanoparticles can reduce the levels of NETs in the colon of UC mice, effectively alleviating the clinical colitis index and tissue inflammation in UC mice. Additionally, SNase nanoparticles can reduce intestinal permeability, increase tight junction proteins in colonic tissues, and regulate the expression of proinflammatory cytokines.

Modulating immune microenvironment

IBD is characterized by the infiltration of various inflammatory cells at the disease sites, with excessive production of ROS by these cells serving as a key hallmark (see Section 2.4 for details). Therefore, various strategies aimed at modulating immune cell activity, scavenging ROS, and employing ROS-responsive oral micro/nanomedicines hold significant therapeutic potential for the treatment of IBD. For example, Rahman et al.187 reported the development of a nanomedicine consisting of bilirubin (BR)-conjugated low-molecular-weight water-soluble chitosan nanoparticles (LMWC-BRNPs). The LMWC component enhances the mucoadhesive properties of the nanoparticles through electrostatic interactions. Treatment with LMWC-BRNPs has been shown to restore intestinal barrier function, inhibit the activity of pro-inflammatory macrophages, expand regulatory T cell populations, and protect intestinal homeostasis in a DSS-induced colitis model (Fig. 14A). Min et al.188 created immune-modulating antioxidant nanocarriers (ICANs) by incorporating ROS-scavenging cerium nanoparticles (CeNPs) into mesoporous silica and coating them with polyacrylic acid (PAA). This design maintains high ROS-scavenging efficiency in artificial gastrointestinal fluids. Tang et al.189 synthesized a series of dihydromyricetin-based nano-assemblies with excellent antioxidant activities and high gastrointestinal dispersibility. As oral formulations, these nanoassemblies effectively alleviate oxidative stress and inflammation in IBD.

Chemoresistance and immunosuppression hinder CRC treatment, prompting research to improve chemotherapy and immunotherapy and enhance tumor penetration. Cao et al.190 developed a nanomotor made of mesoporous manganese oxide, loaded with mitochondrial sonosensitizers, and functionalized with silk fibroin and chondroitin sulfate. Encapsulated in chitosan-alginate hydrogel for oral CRC treatment, this nanomotor uses hydrogen peroxide and ultrasound to penetrate mucus and tumors effectively. It triggers a Fenton-like reaction in the tumor environment, producing hydroxyl radicals and oxygen for combined chemodynamic and sonodynamic therapy. When paired with anti-PD-L1, it increases the CD8+/CD4+ T cell ratio and reduces immunosuppressive Treg cells. These findings indicate that the combination of CS-ID@NM and immune checkpoint blockers can activate T cell-mediated immune responses, enhance systemic antitumor immunity, provide long-term immune memory effects, and prevent tumor recurrence. Similarly, Zu et al.191 created nanoparticles (Ce6/R837@Lp127NPs) by encapsulating chlorin e6 and imiquimod with lipids and pluronic F127. These nanoparticles, when exposed to ultrasound, effectively penetrate colonic mucus and tumors. They induce immunogenic cell death in colorectal tumors through sonodynamic therapy, with R837-generated neoantigens acting as in situ vaccines. Combined with immune checkpoint blockade, this treatment inhibits primary tumors, eradicates distant ones, and modulates gut microbiota. Additional, Li et al.192 developed an innovative method of using selenium nanoparticles loaded extracellular vesicles to bind with interleukin-32 and engineered probiotic Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (SeNVs@NE-IL32-EcN), which enhance the immune response mediated by CD8+T cells and overcome immune therapy resistance, significantly improved the proliferation and activity of CD8+T cells, and reduced tumor progression in humanized mouse models (Fig. 14B).

Disruption of the TRAP1 gene, which encodes a mitochondrial chaperone in tumor cells, leads to the translocation of cyclophilin D in tumor cells, thereby causing the continuous opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore. This process enhances chemotherapy-induced cell necrosis and promotes immune responses. Based on this finding, Zhao et al.193 developed an oral CRISPR-Cas9 delivery system, HTPBD, which can target and disrupt the TRAP1 gene in CRC. This system is capable of penetrating the intestinal mucus layer and accumulating in CRC tissues through epithelial transcytosis. In orthotopic, chemoresistant, and spontaneous CRC models, it enhances chemotherapeutic efficacy by overcoming chemoresistance and activating the tumor immune microenvironment, exhibiting remarkable synergistic antitumor effects. Thus, it provides a promising therapeutic strategy for the clinical management of CRC.

Probiotics play essential roles in immune modulation. Combining probiotics with cancer vaccines potentially can achieve a synergistic effect194. Xu et al.195 encapsulated Lactobacillus rhamnosus and Bifidobacterium longum with lipid membranes for the treatment of CRC. In a subcutaneous transplanted colon cancer mouse model, the probiotic formulations exhibited strong preventive and therapeutic effects; when combined with tumor nanovaccines, their preventive and therapeutic efficacy was further enhanced. These probiotic formulations can act as immune adjuvants to enhance innate immune responses and also serve as in situ tumor vaccines (Fig. 14C).

CRC progression is heavily influenced by a chronic inflammatory environment and immune imbalance. Another approach involves treating CRC by modulating the immune-inflammatory environment and immune balance. Cao et al.196 developed oral fullerene tablets (OFTs) that target the colorectal area to reduce inflammation and treat CRC effectively. These tablets reduce ROS, prevent mutations in wild-type P53, and inhibit NF-κB and STAT3 inflammatory pathways in CRC mice. As a result, OFTs decrease pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages and neutrophils at the tumor site, restoring immune balance and inhibiting CRC.

Summary

The dysfunction of the intestinal mucosal immune barrier in intestinal diseases is marked by a disruption in the balance between innate and adaptive immune responses. This imbalance is characterized by excessive production of ROS, overexpression of pro-inflammatory markers, and significant infiltration of pro-inflammatory immune cells, such as M1 macrophages and neutrophils. These immune cells persistently secrete inflammatory mediators, resulting in chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation, which further exacerbate intestinal damage or facilitate tumor progression. The aberrant activation of these immune cells and the disrupted immune microenvironment not only impede the precise targeting of drugs but may also diminish therapeutic efficacy due to heightened inflammatory responses.

To address these challenges, oral micro/nanomedicines are primarily engineered to target aberrant immune cells and modulate the immune microenvironment. In targeting macrophages, specific receptors that are abundantly expressed on their surfaces are employed to enable precise delivery through receptor-ligand interactions. Furthermore, macrophage membrane-coated nanomedicines leverage natural chemotaxis to localize inflammatory sites, thereby augmenting drug accumulation within immune cells. Regarding the modulation of the immune microenvironment, strategies encompass scavenging excessive ROS, adjusting the immune-inflammatory milieu and immune balance, and incorporating probiotics as immune adjuvants to enhance innate immune responses. Collectively, these methodologies aim to restore immune homeostasis, attenuate inflammation, and impede disease progression.

Multifunctional oral micro/nanomedicines modulating multiple barriers

Intestinal mucosal homeostasis is maintained by dynamic interactions among gut microbiota, intestinal mucus layer, intestinal epithelial cells, and host immune defense. A reduction in intestinal barrier integrity permits luminal antigens, such as microbes and toxins, to pass through the intestinal mucosa into the circulation, disrupting gut mucosal homeostasis and leading to chronic inflammation and malabsorption197. The four intestinal barrier functions interact with and influence each other. Within the physical barrier, intestinal epithelial cells can secrete mucus and antimicrobial peptides, thereby enhancing the function of the chemical barrier198. They can also release cytokines to activate immune cells, strengthening the immune barrier199. Moreover, changes in gut microbiota and their metabolites can affect mucus secretion, intercellular tight junctions, intestinal epithelial permeability, and intestinal immunity200. Through these complex interactions, the barriers collectively maintain intestinal homeostasis, preventing pathogen invasion and inflammatory responses. In different disease states, the characteristics of intestinal barrier function vary, and the oral medication strategies we design also differ accordingly, as shown in Table 339,201, 202, 203, 204. When designing oral micro/nanomedicines, we should comprehensively consider the interactions between these four barrier functions under different disease states, and develop more effective therapeutic strategies for intestinal diseases. For example, Zhao et al.205 engineered ferulic acid-derived lignin nanoparticles (FALNPs) for colitis management. These nanoparticles inherently possess chemical stability, enabling them to withstand the acidic environment of the GIT, thus overcoming the chemical barrier. Moreover, FALNPs can target the intestinal mucosa, where they scavenge excessive ROS, repair epithelial integrity, and restore the mechanical barrier. Additionally, by modulating gut microbiota composition and suppressing inflammatory signaling pathways, FALNPs concurrently address the microbial barrier (dysbiosis) and inflammatory activation. In summary, FALNPs exhibit a multi-barrier-modulating capacity, simultaneously surmounting chemical barriers, rejuvenating the mechanical barrier, and regulating immune and microbial barriers to treat colitis (Fig. 15A).

Similarly, Li et al.206 developed a “three-birds-one-stone” probiotic formulation, Se@EcN-C2/A2, for colitis treatment. Specifically, Se nanoparticles were intracellularly synthesized in EcN via biomineralization, followed by surface coating with glycol chitosan and sodium alginate. This design allowed Se@EcN-C2/A2 to first overcome the chemical barriers of the GIT through the protective polymer coating. Functionally, Se@EcN-C2/A2 scavenged ROS, inhibited the Toll-like receptor and NF-κB signaling pathways to suppress inflammatory responses, and promoted the polarization of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages toward anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes (targeting the immune barrier). Meanwhile, viable EcN probiotics modulated gut microbial dysbiosis by reducing Escherichia–Shigella abundance and enriching beneficial Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium (addressing the microbial barrier). Thus, Se@EcN-C2/A2 simultaneously tackled chemical, immune, and microbial barriers to alleviate colitis symptoms (Fig. 15B).

Currently, other oral micro/nanomedicines for treating intestinal diseases are mainly designed for 2 out of the 4 barriers. Wang et al.207 designed a sequential nanomedicine that simultaneously restores intestinal tight junction proteins (the mechanical barrier), modulates gut microbiota (the microbial barrier), and alleviates inflammation. Wu et al.208 synthesized the oral nanomedicine MON-PAMAM@SASP. This formulation scavenges ROS in colitic tissues to regulate the immune microenvironment (the immune barrier) and concurrently modulates gut microbiota composition (the microbial barrier), thus addressing two key barriers synergistically.

In conclusion, the four intestinal barriers—chemical, mechanical, microbial, and immune—are not isolated entities; rather, they sustain intestinal homeostasis through intricate interactions. The functional imbalance of these barriers constitutes the fundamental mechanism underlying the onset and progression of various intestinal diseases. Consequently, oral micro/nanomedicines that target a single barrier often fail to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes. In contrast, multifunctional systems that incorporate the synergistic regulation of multiple barriers have demonstrated considerable advantages. Future research should focus on further elucidating the dynamic interrelationships among these barriers and designing oral micro/nanomedicine systems capable of precise and synergistic regulation of multiple barriers. This approach will be pivotal in overcoming existing therapeutic limitations and advancing effective treatment of intestinal diseases.

Challenges and future perspectives

The intestinal barrier, encompassing chemical, biological, mechanical, and immune barriers, safeguards the GIT but concurrently limits the efficacy of oral drugs for intestinal diseases. In this review, we summarize the notable advancements in oral micro/nanomedicine that have enabled therapeutic agents to surmount these barriers through strategic innovations, significantly improving therapeutic outcomes and fostering novel treatment modalities: (1) Overcoming the chemical barrier: Employing acid-resistant and digestive enzyme-resistant materials, combined with extended intestinal retention designs, protects drugs from degradation by gastric acid and digestive enzymes, ensuring sufficient delivery to diseased sites. (2) Modulating the biological barrier: Engineered probiotics directly regulate gut microbiota and metabolites, while plant/bacteria-derived vesicles and biomembrane-modified nanoparticles indirectly normalize the microenvironment via microbiome-host interactions. (3) Penetrating the mechanical barrier: Surface modifications enhance the mucus-penetrating capacity of nanoparticles, whereas epithelial cell-targeting facilitates transcellular transport. (4) Remodeling the immune barrier: Strategies such as macrophage-targeted drug delivery or ROS scavenging reprogram the immune microenvironment.

Despite significant advancements, oral micro/nanomedicines encounter numerous challenges in the treatment of intestinal diseases, which simultaneously highlight critical directions for future development.

(1)Overcoming the multiple barriers of the intestinal mucosa is essential. To effectively reach the intestinal site, oral micro/nanomedicines must traverse various barriers. Currently, most systems achieve only primary targeting at the tissue or organ level, lacking the precision necessary for cellular or subcellular targeting. Therefore, the development of cascaded targeting mechanisms and multi-stimulus responsive platforms is crucial to facilitate simultaneous multi-target drug delivery and the regulation of the disease microenvironment205,206,208.

(2)There is an urgent imperative to advance the development of innovative and personalized oral micro/nano delivery systems. Traditional micro/nanocarriers are predominantly composed of inorganic or organic materials, which inherently pose toxicity risks. Consequently, the utilization of innovative natural carriers, such as probiotics, presents a promising alternative. Following appropriate modifications, these carriers are capable of traversing the intestinal barrier and effectively delivering therapeutic agents to specific intestinal sites209. Additionally, through engineering modifications, probiotics can be employed to load a variety of drugs210,211. Nonetheless, challenges such as the long-term safety of these natural carriers remain to be addressed. Furthermore, given the considerable variability in patients' gastrointestinal tracts-including differences in pH, peristalsis rate, mucosal structure, and microbiota composition, these factors significantly influence the absorption, distribution, and metabolism of drugs. Therefore, it is imperative to develop personalized delivery systems tailored to the unique gastrointestinal characteristics of individual patients to improve therapeutic outcomes.

(3)The precise regulation of the stability of oral micro/nanomedicine systems is crucial. These systems require site-specific drug release to minimize systemic side effects, necessitating a careful equilibrium between gastrointestinal stability (to prevent premature release) and lesion responsiveness (to ensure effective drug delivery). Stimuli-responsive systems that leverage disease-specific signals, such as enzymes, pH, glutathione (GSH), and ROS, offer promising solutions in this regard.

(4)Establishing standards for the safety evaluation of oral micro/nanomedicines is crucial212. The potential toxicity of oral micro/nanomedicines, coupled with the complexity of assessing their biocompatibility and toxicity within the intricate gastrointestinal environment, underscores the urgent need for standardized safety evaluation methodologies. A critical aspect of developing such standards involves the incorporation of assessments related to the biocompatibility, dose-dependent toxicity, and long-term toxicity of nanomaterials using large animal models. This approach is justified by the greater physiological, metabolic, and gastrointestinal similarities between large animals and humans, compared to small animal models213. Some nanomaterials are non-toxic in rodent animals but toxic in large animal models214. Establishing safety evaluation standards that incorporate large animal models for oral micro/nanomedicines can facilitate the early identification of potential risks associated with oral micro/nanomedicines, thereby ensuring patient safety and fostering the sustainable development of these therapeutic modalities.

(5)Industrialization challenges represent significant obstacles to the clinical translation of oral micro/nanomedicines. The multilevel functionalization of these systems significantly increases production costs and complicates quality control, thereby hindering clinical translation. Despite the considerable advancements achieved in the development of micro/nanomedicines currently undergoing clinical trials or present in commercial pipelines, such as mesalamine nanoparticles for ulcerative colitis, which are in Phase II clinical trials, and Onivyde (irinotecan liposomes), which has received approval for the treatment of metastatic pancreatic cancer-numerous challenges persist in the clinical translation of other micro/nanomedicines. At the regulatory level, the submission of safety data derived from large animal models, along with evidence demonstrating clinical advantages over traditional formulations, is mandated. For large-scale production, challenges encompass difficulties in scaling up complex processes, inconsistencies in batch-to-batch uniformity regarding particle size distribution and drug encapsulation efficiency, and elevated costs. Regarding formulation stability, oral micro/nanomedicines must endure extreme gastrointestinal pH, digestive enzymes, and microbiota environments, while avoiding aggregation, sedimentation, or drug leakage during storage, thereby imposing stringent requirements on carrier materials, such as pH-responsive polymers and natural biofilms. Quality control necessitates stringent standards for nanoscale characteristics, encompassing quantitative detection methodologies for critical parameters such as particle size distribution, surface charge, drug loading, and release kinetics, to ensure batch consistency.

(6)Artificial intelligence (AI) technology has significantly advanced the development of oral micro/nanomedicines for the treatment of intestinal diseases. Through the application of machine learning algorithms, researchers are able to optimize both the micro/nanomedicines and their delivery mechanisms215. AI models, including artificial neural networks (ANNs) and convolutional neural networks (CNNs), play a crucial role in automatically identifying disease states and predicting optimal drug delivery strategies, thereby enhancing the precision and efficacy of treatments216. Additionally, AI technology facilitates real-time monitoring of disease progression and allows for the adjustment of treatment plans based on individual patient conditions, thus enabling personalized therapeutic approaches217. Furthermore, AI-driven nanorobots are capable of autonomously navigating complex biological environments to achieve precise drug delivery218. Looking ahead, sustained efforts are necessary to foster innovation in this domain to support its broader clinical application.

These approaches will advance oral micro/nano drugs as therapeutic platforms for gut microbiome regulation and immune-metabolic intervention, accelerating clinical translation.
