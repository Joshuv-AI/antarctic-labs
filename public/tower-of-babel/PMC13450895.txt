Maternal patterning in early Drosophila embryos: how much information can single maternal gradients supply?
Authors: Eric Wieschaus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAnterior-posterior patterning in Drosophila relies on positional information supplied by the graded distributions of three maternally supplied transcription factors originally identified in large-scale mutagenesis screens. Here, I examine the development of embryos in which these three maternal gradients have been flattened, but set at different levels. Following such manipulations, expression patterns at the blastoderm stage are uniform along the anterior-posterior axis, but their specific nature depends on the combination and concentration level of the three systems. Each of the three gradients are then examined in contexts in which the others are flattened but set at different levels. This reveals that each system generates a greater fraction of the wild-type pattern than that deleted when that system is removed by loss-of-function mutations, with the exact pattern depending on the levels to which the two remaining flattened systems are set. These simplified patterns are then used to analyze interactions between the maternal systems and to investigate how the pattern of gap and pair rule genes are modulated by network interactions downstream of the maternal inputs themselves.Keywords: Maternal gradients, Drosophila, Pattern formation, Bicoid, Capicua, Nanos

INTRODUCTION

The past 40 years have seen extraordinary advances in our understanding of the role that maternal factors play in the early development of embryos from many species. This is particularly true in Drosophila, in which information establishing the anterior-posterior (AP) pattern is supplied by gradients of three distinct maternally supplied transcription factors: Bicoid (Berleth et al., 1988), Hunchback (Tautz et al., 1987) and Capicua (Jimenez et al., 2000). The RNAs encoding these three transcription factors are maternally supplied and are translated after fertilization. Their final graded distribution depends on distinct cellular mechanisms involving RNA localization, translational control and protein stability. The simplest perhaps is represented by the RNA encoding Bicoid (Bcd), which is localized to the anterior end of the egg during oogenesis and is released for translation after fertilization (Frohnhofer and Nusslein-Volhard, 1986). Bicoid protein is thought to diffuse from its source of synthesis to form a gradient that peaks at the anterior end of the egg and is detectable to at least 70% egg length (EL) (Driever and Nüsslein-Volhard, 1988; Gregor et al., 2007; Little et al., 2011). The maternal Hunchback (Hb) gradient is derived from a uniform maternal RNA, translation of which is blocked by Nanos protein (Nos; Hülskamp et al., 1989, 1990). The RNA encoding Nanos is localized in germline polar granules that accumulate in the posterior region of the embryo (Gavis and Lehmann, 1992). Maternal Hb levels are therefore high in the anterior and fall to form a steep gradient in the middle region of the egg. Loss-of-function mutations in Nanos (nosBN) result in high maternal Hb levels along the entire AP axis of the embryo. The transcription factor associated with a third maternal system, Capicua (Cic), is derived from a uniformly distributed maternal RNA that is post-translationally regulated by the receptor tyrosine kinase Torso (Sprenger et al., 1989; Casanova, 1990). Torso signaling is activated at both the anterior and posterior poles of the egg and results in ppERK-mediated phosphorylation of Cic, triggering its rapid release from DNA (Keenan et al., 2020; Patel et al., 2021) and subsequent degradation in the cytoplasm (Grimm et al., 2012). Cic levels are normally high in the middle of the embryo and grade off to low levels at the two poles. Loss-of-function mutation in the Torso pathway (tor, or tsl, encoding Torso-like) result in uniform high levels of Cic protein along the AP axis of the embryo.

A major attraction of the AP system in Drosophila is that the molecular components are well characterized and that the three systems are believed to represent the only maternal information for AP patterning in the egg. This belief is based largely on extensive saturation mutagenesis experiments for maternal-effect mutations (Schupbach and Wieschaus, 1986, 1991; Nusslein-Volhard et al., 1987), which have consistently identified mutations only in the three maternally supplied transcription factors and in gene products associated with their localization and graded distributions. The arguments from saturation mutagenesis experiments have been supported by embryos from females triply mutant for all three maternal components. Such bcd nosBN tsl embryos show uniform AP differentiation (Lehmann and Frohnhöfer, 1989) and uniform gene expression patterns at the blastoderm stage (Petkova et al., 2019). They provide no evidence of residual maternal inputs from genes not detected in the previous mutagenesis screens. A criticism of this approach is that the mid-embryonic cell fates assayed in bcd nosBN tsl embryos might be relatively insensitive to quantitative variation in inputs. To address this issue, I have generated a broad range of states by modulating the levels of the three transcription factors while keeping their distributions uniform. The differing expression patterns zygotically produced in those embryos mimic different positions along the AP axis of the embryo, but expression patterns remain uniform, arguing strongly against the existence of other, yet unknown, maternal inputs.

That the three known inputs represent the only maternal sources of AP position formation in the embryo therefore opens an alternative strategy for evaluating the role of each system in establishing pattern. In most early analyses, the role of a gene was characterized by the elements of the final pattern that are missing when its activity is eliminated. In this study, I examine embryos in which information about position along the AP axis is provided by only a single maternal patterning system while the other two remain uniform but can be set at different levels. Such ‘Bicoid only’, ‘Torso only’ and ‘Nanos only’ genotypes generate substantially larger regions of precise pattern than the regions deleted in loss-of-function alleles. Although a normal sequence of pattern elements is established by each gradient operating singly, the specific range of cell fates depends on the uniform level to which the two remaining systems are set. These simplified patterns are then used to analyze interactions between the maternal systems and their immediate targets and investigate how the patterns of gap and pair rule genes are modulated by network interactions downstream of the maternal inputs themselves.

RESULTS

AP patterning in the absence of maternal cues

To characterize the range of uniform ground-state embryos achievable by flattening the three maternal gradients associated with AP patterning, I first re-examined the phenotype of embryos from bcd nosBN tsl mothers. Such embryos lack maternal Bcd protein and are predicted to have uniform high levels of maternal Hunchback and Capicua. The only indication of AP polarity are the pole cells, which are unaffected by the maternal genes that directly govern AP pattern in the somatic region of the blastoderm (Lehmann and Nüsslein-Volhard, 1986). At the blastoderm stage, bcd nosBN tsl embryos showed uniform levels of the proteins encoded by the gap gene Kr and the pair rule gene runt (Fig. 1). They did not express Hb, Kni or Gt. This expression pattern corresponds to the pattern at approximately 53% EL measured from the anterior pole (Dubuis et al., 2013a), similar to cells that would give rise to anterior abdomen (Lohs-Schardin et al., 1979). Consistent with this view, when normal larvae would have hatched, embryos from bcd nosBN tsl females formed small balls of cuticle with a single, uniform lawn of abdominal denticles (Fig. 1).

It might be argued that the apparent uniform expression pattern in bcd nosBN tsl embryos is a feature of the middle region of the embryos and that other regions might be more sensitive to inhomogeneities in maternal cues. To test this possibility, I varied the level of Hb and Cic while maintaining their uniform distribution to shift cell fates to other regions of the embryo. Two strategies were used to reduce maternal Hb in a bcd nosBN tsl background. The first incorporated a transgene (nosTCEIIUC:AG 3′Tub, hereafter referred to as nosTub) that expresses low uniform levels of Nos protein along the AP axis of the embryo, thereby substantially repressing translation of Hunchback protein (Gavis et al., 2008). When introduced into bcd nosBN tsl mothers, the resultant embryos formed cuticle lawns similar to those produced by bcd nosBN tsl without the transgene, although likely of a more posterior segmental identity. At the beginning of cycle 14, they expressed Kni rather than Kr, with some low level of Gt at later stages (Fig. 1), a behavior of cells at approximately 62% EL, corresponding to the 4th or 5th abdominal segment. A stronger posterior shift was observed when maternal Hb was totally eliminated using germline clones (Chou and Perrimon, 1996), which are simultaneously homozygous for bcd hb nosBN and tsl (see Materials and Methods). Such embryos expressed high levels of Giant at the beginning of cycle 14 (Fig. 1), a pattern that would normally be seen at approximately 69% EL, in precursors for abdominal segment six.

Cell fates in uniform embryos were shifted to even more posterior cell fates when Capicua activity was reduced using cic1, a hypomorphic allele with a lesion in the 5′ untranslated region of the gene (Jimenez et al., 2000; Roch et al., 2002) that lowers protein levels to 15% of that in wild type (see Materials and Methods and Table S1 for quantification). Given the close proximity of the cic and nos genes on the third chromosome, in these experiments I substituted nosBN with an allele of oskar (osk) that blocks nos RNA localization and translation (Ephrussi et al., 1991). Embryos from such bcd osk cic tsl mothers did not make abdominal cuticle and did not express Kr, Kni or Gt. Instead, they showed high, uniform levels of zygotic Hb and Brachyenteron (Byn), a combination unique to the proctodeum (approximately 85% EL).

In all cases described above, zygotically active genes were either not expressed, or expressed uniformly along the AP axis of the embryos. That uniform patterns are produced when the three known maternal AP system are flattened to different levels argues that uniformity is not a peculiarity in the middle region of the embryo. The absence of all AP patterning in such embryos argues strongly against the existence of yet-unidentified sources of maternal AP positional information. The experiments that follow use the uniform ‘ground state’ patterns to analyze the patterns generated by each of the three maternal gradients in a context in which they provide the only source of AP positional information.

Bicoid-only embryos

Localization of bcd RNA at the anterior end of the embryo is required for anterior structures, and embryos from mutant bcd females lack all head and thoracic structures (Frohnhofer and Nusslein-Volhard, 1986). A complementary phenotype was produced in embryos from females homozygous for nosBN tsl, in which the only spatial information is supplied by the Bcd gradient. Such embryos formed head and thorax, sometimes followed by a small patch of abdominal denticles with no obvious segmental subdivision (Fig. 2; see also Frohnhofer and Nusslein-Volhard, 1986). The failure to form abdomen or express abdominal gap genes might be due to the high ectopic levels of maternal Hb present in abdominal region of embryos from females mutant for nos (Hülskamp et al., 1989). Since these inhibitory levels are not present in wild-type embryos, the high levels in nosBN tsl embryos make it difficult to evaluate the normal contribution of Bcd to abdominal patterning.

To investigate the potential impact of Bcd on abdominal patterning, maternal Hb levels were reduced using the same strategies employed to reduce Hb levels in bcd nosBN tsl embryos. When maternal Hb levels were lowered but not completely eliminated using the nosTub construct, nosBN tsl embryos formed a normally segmented abdomen from A1 to A5 (Fig. 1). Complete elimination of maternal Hb using germline clones (hb nosBN tsl) yielded a normal abdominal pattern to A6. In both cases, a corresponding expression of the proteins encoded by abdominal gap genes and pair rule genes was observed at the blastoderm stage, indicating that the Bcd gradient can establish normal abdominal patterning up to A6, but that the exact pattern of gene expression and cell fates is sensitive to the level of uniform maternal Hb.

To characterize the pattern generated in Bcd-only embryos in greater detail, protein expression was examined at the blastoderm stage using antibodies to the proteins encoded by eight target genes [Hkb, Otd (also known as Oc), Btd, Gt, Kni, Hb, Runt, Eve], the expression boundaries of which in wild-type embryos define positions from 10% to 90% EL (Fig. 2B-D). Several generalizations emerged from this analysis. The first is that embryos from Bcd-only mothers failed to express proteins normally restricted to the polar-most regions of the embryo, 15-20% from the anterior or posterior pole. These included the anterior and posterior cap-like domains of Hkb, the posterior cap-like domains of Hb and the posterior-most stripes of Eve and Run (Fig. S3A). All the remaining proteins and boundaries were expressed in Bcd-only embryos in their normal sequence relative to each other, although the positions were sometimes shifted. In the anterior regions of the embryo, the boundaries of Otd, Btd, Gt and Hb showed shifts of about 4-5% EL to the anterior (Fig. 2B-D, Table S1). Because these experiments eliminated Torso signaling as well as Nanos and maternal Hb, the only source of positional information in these embryos is Bcd. The shifts suggest that the enhancers for anteriorly expressed genes require higher levels of Bcd to activate expression in the absence of maternal Hb and Torso, confirming earlier observations of Simpson-Brose et al. (1994). In the posterior of the embryo, abdominal domains of Kni and Gt shifted in the posterior direction. This was associated with more gradual slopes at the posterior boundaries of Knirps (Fig. 2C) and Giant (Fig. 2D), resulting in much broadened expression domains, with the Giant domain extending all the way to the pole of the embryo.

Although the numbers, staging and orientation of embryos in this experiment were not sufficient for detailed analyses of precision (Dubuis et al., 2013a; Petkova et al., 2019; Haroush et al., 2023), Bcd-only embryos were used to check whether elimination of the other two maternal systems increases variability in the expression boundaries established by Bcd. In the anterior regions, standard deviations of boundary positions were small and the slopes were steep. Position uncertainties in Bcd-only embryos, defined as the standard deviation in intensity divided by the local slope (Dubuis et al., 2013a; Table S1), were only slightly larger than those in wild type (1.8% versus 1.2%) when expressed in terms of percentage EL. In the posterior region of the embryo, where slopes were less steep, border locations were less reproducible. The anterior margin of Gt provided the most posterior boundary that was examined in Bcd-only embryos (Table S1). The standard deviation at the boundary position (0.21%) was not very different from values measured in other regions of the embryo (Table S1). The shallower slope in this region, however, gave a positional error of 4% EL, corresponding to an uncertainty of four cell diameters. Although this is three-fold larger than the precision errors observed in wild-type GT borders, the difference is largely due to the shallow slope.

Direct or indirect responses to Bicoid gradient

Bicoid-only embryos from germline clones provide an opportunity to evaluate Bcd patterning in embryos that lack specific downstream components. One possibility, proposed by Struhl et al. (1992), is that Bcd patterning in the posterior half of the embryo depends on a sequential cascade. Once activated in the anterior region of the egg, this cascade might run independently of direct Bcd input. In such cascades, eliminating the first step in the cascade should block patterning in the abdomen. Instead, I found that the sequenced gap protein expression observed in Bicoid-only embryos does not depend on an inductive role of Hunchback, the first protein expressed. Bcd-only embryos with a homozygous hb mutation made cuticle in which the hb pattern deletions are followed by normally segmented denticle patterns characteristic of the posterior abdomen (Fig. 3A) and such embryos activated Kr, Kni and Gt in domains posterior to where Hb would have been expressed (Fig. 3B). Similarly, when Giant, the posterior-most pattern gap protein expressed in Bcd-only embryos, was eliminated, more anterior regions of the final pattern still formed normally (Fig. 3A, rightmost embryo), arguing against a cascade mechanism initiated from the posterior end. The zygotic pair rule protein Runt has also been proposed to play a major repressive role in establishing Bcd-dependent boundaries (Chen et al., 2012). The Bicoid-only germline clones used in Fig. 2B were heterozygous for runt and protein expression of Hkb, Otd, Btd, Hb, Kni and Gt was quantified in the resultant hemizygous embryos (Table S1). This revealed that removal of zygotic Runt activity shifts boundaries of most of the markers by a cell diameter (1%) to the posterior, with an increased positional error of 0.6% EL. Overall, the strongest, most consistent effect of removing Runt is a shift in position rather than an increase in variability.

Patterns in Bcd-only embryos may depend on more complex network interactions than simple relays and their detection might require deletion of multiple gap genes. To approach this problem, I used CRISPR to introduce a knirps mutation in the FRT hb nosBN tsl stock used to generate germline clones. One-quarter of the Bcd-only embryos from germline clones made using this stock would be expected to be homozygous for both hb and kni. Although the final cuticle patterns in such embryos did not show denticle patterns that were obviously segmental, I found that at blastoderm and gastrula stages Kr and Gt were still expressed in domains very similar to zygotically wild-type Bicoid-only embryos. The boundary positions showed minor shifts relative to where they would have occurred were wild-type alleles of the gap genes hb and kni present (Fig. 3B, Table S2), but, compared to the values in Bicoid-only embryos, the shifts were not associated with increased variability in position (Table S2), as might be expected if network interactions involving kni and hb played essential roles in correcting positional errors made in direct reading of the Bcd gradient. I conclude that embryos from Bcd-only embryos generate reproducible patterns that closely mirror those in wild type, even though they completely lack maternal inputs from Torso and Nanos, any residual maternal Hunchback activity, and any zygotic contributions from hunchback and knirps.

Patterning in Torso-only embryos

Loss-of-function mutations in any of the three well-characterized components of the pathway [the RTK receptor Torso (Tor), and the extracellular signals Trunk (Trk) and Torsolike (Tsl)] produce embryos that lack certain anterior structures, show only six pair-rule stripes and fail to express posterior telson markers (Fig. 4A; see also Schupbach and Wieschaus, 1986). To investigate the positional information provided by Torso signaling, the Torso-only phenotypes of bcd nosBN females carrying various mutations that alter the levels of other maternal components were examined. The simplest genotype, bcd nosBN with wild-type levels of the other components, produced small ‘bicaudal’ embryos with two posterior telson structures in mirror-image symmetry. There were no obvious abdominal segments between the two sets of posterior spiracles and anal plates, although an occasional patch of denticle or scar tissue was observed. At blastoderm stage, embryos showed mirror-image domains of Kni, Gt and Hb (Fig. 4B) as well as polar domains of Hkb, Tll and Byn (Fig. S3B). Since such ‘Torso-only’ mothers lack Nanos activity, the failure to make more anteriorly situated abdominal segments may reflect the high levels of maternal Hb. To test this hypothesis, I progressively lowered maternal Hb, shown in the composite panels of Fig. 4B; 50% reductions associated with maternal heterozygosity for hb resulted in the formation of a broad lawn of denticles between the anterior and posterior anal plates (Fig. 4B). Introduction of the nosTub construct transformed this denticle domain into a mirror-image pattern of two to four abdominal segments on each side of the mirror-image plane (Fig. 4B). When using germline clones (FRT bcd hb nosBN) to eliminate all maternal Hb expression, this pattern became less variable, with three intact segments and a mirror image always in the 6th abdominal belt (Fig. 4B). At the blastoderm stage, such embryos showed mirror-image patterns of Gt, Hb and Hkb expression and a pair rule pattern corresponding to the observed cuticle phenotype. The extension of the pattern into the abdomen did not require zygotic Hb expression (Fig. 4B). Torso-only embryos zygotically mutant for hb formed bicaudal embryos with the mirror-image plane in the 6th abdominal denticle belt, even though they lacked the A8 denticle belt and posterior spiracles. I conclude that graded positional information can be provided by Torso from the posterior pole to the 6th segment and to at least to 70% EL.

In Torso-only embryos with reduced Cic, the mirror-image pattern was maintained, but the broad middle region of the embryo was unpatterned, no cuticle formed and the mirror image consisted of posterior markers characteristic of proctodeum and posterior midgut fates (Fig. 4C).

Patterning in Nanos-only embryos

Nanos repression of maternal Hb translation lowers the level of maternal Hb and allows Kni and Gt expression. The role of Nanos appears to be specific for the abdomen and embryos from nos mutant females have normal head and thorax and normal anal plates and posterior spiracles (Lehmann, 1988). Although Nanos protein is dispensable for abdominal pattern if maternal Hb is eliminated (Tautz, 1988; Irish et al., 1989; Struhl, 1989), its graded distribution in wild-type embryos implies that it may still contribute to the final pattern of the embryo.

Struhl and co-workers' characterization of embryos from bcd tsl mothers (Struhl et al., 1992) provided the first estimate of positional information supplied by the Nos gradient alone. Nanos-only embryos lack head and tail structures and make abdomens with five to seven denticle belts, as illustrated in Fig. 5A. This variability may reflect a reduced selection for Nanos precision, given its dispensability as long as the embryo has wild-type levels of Bicoid and Torso signaling. Struhl et al. (1992) showed that patterning in Nanos-only embryos depends on a maternal Hb gradient produced by Nanos regulation. In the current study, immunostaining revealed a strong anterior maternal Hb gradient in cycle 13 that gradually decayed early in cycle 14 (Fig. 5B). The gradual loss of maternal Hb was associated with a rise of Kr and Runt expression in the anterior of half of embryo. This expression was followed by a temporal sequence in which increasingly posterior domains of Kni and Gt were established, followed eventually by a dorsal posterior patch of zygotic Hb. These zygotic expression patterns were mimicked by reporter constructs (Schroeder et al., 2004, 2011) that reproduce the normal expression patterns of the genes in wild-type embryos (Fig. S2), suggesting that the Nanos-only pattern observed in bcd tsl embryos involves the same cis regulatory elements that govern normal abdominal patterning.

The analysis of Nanos-only embryos was extended to a synthetic system using a transgene (nos-bcd3′UTR) that localizes nos RNA at the anterior rather than the posterior of the egg (Gavis and Lehmann, 1992). When this transgene was introduced into a bcd nosBN tsl background, embryos produced a cuticle pattern similar to, although slightly smaller than, that produced by bcd tsl females, generally five rather than six abdominal segments (Fig. 5C). The most striking feature of such embryos is that, based on their position in the eggshell, the polarity of the pattern was the opposite of that observed in wild-type and bcd tsl embryos. At the blastoderm stage, Gt ant Kr had switched positions such that Kr was adjacent to the pole cells that mark the posterior of the egg. These polarity alterations are consistent with localization of Nanos to the anterior rather than the posterior pole of the egg and complement experiments of Lehmann and Frohnhöfer (1989), in which posterior cytoplasm containing Nos activity was injected into the middle region of bcd nosBN tsl embryos, inducing the formation of mirror-image abdominal segments. The difference between the complete polarity reversals observed here and the mirror-image pattern observed by Lehmann and Frohnhofer argue that the location where Nos activity is introduced provides the essential cue for polarization of the subsequent pattern.

Patterning in Nanos-only embryos depends on Cic and Hb levels. Heterozygosity for cic1 in bcd tsl females shifted the posterior-most pattern elements from the 7th abdominal denticle belt to the 8th abdominal segment with fully formed spiracles with a corresponding enlargement of the posterior Hunchback expression domain (Fig. 5D). Reducing Hb maternal dosage in Nanos-only embryos using the nosTub construct reduced the number of segments formed (Fig. 5E), consistent with the classic model in which the maternal Hb gradient generated by Nanos provides essential positional information controlling abdominal patterning. The classic model does not exclude the possibility that the pattern depends on targets in addition to maternal Hb. To test this possibility, I examined germline clones homozygous for bcd hb tsl, which lack maternal Hb but still have wild-type Nanos activity. If Hb is the only target of Nanos regulation, we would expect its elimination to block all Nanos-dependent patterning. I found, however, that the lawn of denticle formed in such embryos is followed by a single denticle belt of normal morphology (Fig. 5F,G). A similar pattern is mentioned by Struhl et al. (1992) in their analysis of Nos-dependent pattern. To characterize the pattern in more detail, I examined protein expression at the blastoderm stage and found that such embryos show uniform expression along 80% of the AP axis, but show a clearly defined posterior zone of patterned pair rule protein expression consistent with the single isolated denticle belt observed in cuticle preparations (Fig. 5G). Since all patterning was eliminated in embryos from bcd hb nosBN tsl germline clones that lack Nos activity (Fig. 5H), I conclude that Nos must have other targets in addition to maternal Hb. A possible clue to the complexity of Nanos signaling comes from certain alleles of Nanos (e.g. nosL7) that have lost the ability to downregulate Hb but retain other Nanos activities, such as germline survival (Kobayashi et al., 1996; Deshpande et al., 1999; Arrizabalaga and Lehmann, 1999). I found that embryos from triple-mutant females carrying the nosL7 allele rather than nosBN showed flattened uniform patterns of gene expression in the anterior embryo, but, like the hb bcd tsl germline clones, showed a narrow band of posterior patterning for Runt and Even skipped (Fig. 5I). These results argue that Nanos activity is complex and that different regions of the protein may be essential for different target interactions, even during segmental patterning (Arrizabalaga and Lehmann, 1999).

Nanos regulation of Capicua levels

The sensitivity of Nanos signaling to Capicua levels depicted in Fig. 5D parallels observations of Cinnamon et al. (2004) who found interactions between Nanos and the Torso pathway in the expression of Torso targets like Tailless and Huckebein. To test whether the sensitivity of the Nanos-only phenotype reflects a direct interaction, I examined Cic protein levels in embryos that lack Bicoid and Torso signaling but retain wild-type levels of Nanos. Consistent with this hypothesized interaction, I found that, although embryos showed flattened high CIC levels at the anterior end, a gradient in Cic persisted at the posterior end of the embryo (Fig. 6A). This residual gradient was eliminated when Nos signaling was eliminated from the double-mutant combination. These experiments argue that the posterior Nos gradient has effects on Capicua, in addition to its well-characterized effect on maternal Hunchback. These data contrast with those of Cinnamon et al. (2004), who report Nos effects on Cic in otherwise wild-type embryos at both the anterior and posterior poles. Although they favor models in which Nos modulates MAPK activity and/or its nuclear entry, in the bcd tsl double mutants I have examined, pole-specific MAPK activity should be absent.

Nanos regulation of maternal Hb translation involves binding of Nanos and its co-partner Pumilio to the 3′ UTR of hb RNA (Murata and Wharton, 1995). Since the Cic 3′UTR lacks canonical PUF-binding sites (Gerber et al., 2006), how it might be regulated by Nanos is unclear. I compared the dynamics of the regulation of Cic by Nanos with the regulation of Hb by Nanos, using bcd tsl embryos, in which any input from the Torso pathway is eliminated (Fig. 6B). Both the Hb and Cic gradients were visible in nuclei during cleavage stages 11-13, although the expression boundary where Hb falls to 50% of its peak was positioned much farther anterior than the Cic boundary (49% EL versus 85% EL).

The impact of Nanos on Capicua distribution may provide an explanation for a long mystery associated with Torso signaling. In embryos from cic1 homozygous females, expression of posterior markers such as Hkb and Byn expanded to the anterior while maintaining a sequence mimicking that observed at the posterior end of wild-type embryos (Fig. 6C). The expanded domains of protein expression were associated with the formation of a large posterior midgut (Fig. 6D). The expansion did not depend on information from Torso signaling and the double mutant cic1 tsl has a phenotype identical to the cic1 mutant alone. This double-mutant phenotype is essential for establishing the downstream epistatic relationship between Cic and Torso signaling (Paroush et al., 1997), but has been puzzling given that spatial patterning persists despite elimination of positional information supplied by Torso. Because the cic1 mutation does not remove all Cic activity, the residual levels might be low enough to allow expression of posterior targets, but sufficiently graded in response to Nanos for that expression to be patterned. To test this model, I used osk mutants to remove Nos activity from cic1 tsl embryos and found that the posterior pattern seen in cic1 tsl embryos is lost. In such embryos, no posterior midgut formed (Fig. 6D) and in the ectoderm Hb and Runt were uniformly expressed to the posterior pole (Fig. 6E). Endodermal markers such as Hkb were no longer expressed at the posterior pole in dorsal and lateral regions of the embryo, although some expression of Hkb was observed in the ventral cells and mesoderm (Fig. 6F). Since zygotic Cic expression is graded along the dorsal ventral axis, it is possible that Hkb, which is normally induced by low expression at the posterior pole, might be triggered in these genotypes by the low levels present in mesoderm. This ventral expression does not seem to be relevant for the AP pattern observed in cic tsl embryos. Since AP pattern is eliminated in osk cic tsl mutants, I conclude that Nanos supplies a second direct input regulating Cic distribution along at the posterior pole.

DISCUSSION

The experiments described in this paper build on classic analyses carried out in the Drosophila community in the late 1980s and 1990s. During this period, the essential features of the three AP pathways were defined molecularly and the phenotypes associated with mutants described. The most straight-forward strategy for evaluating the role of genes at the time was based on the consequences of their removal. Although powerful, the logic behind this approach is always indirect, given that the phenotype one sees is produced by the genes that remain, not the gene that was removed. The approach, however, worked remarkably well and attests to the skilled phenotypic characterizations by the labs who carried out the initial analyses. As a strategy, classic genetic analyses based on loss of function may be less useful in analysis for which redundancy or network interaction play significant roles. That reasoning led me to attempt a strategy based on Bicoid-only, Torso-only and Nanos-only embryos in which all other inputs had been eliminated. A number of earlier studies used double- and triple-mutant genotypes implicit in this approach (Schupbach and Wieschaus, 1986; Lehmann and Nusslein-Volhard, 1987; Nüsslein-Volhard et al., 1987; Struhl et al., 1992). The analyses presented in this article build on those experiments, although with the exception of Struhl et al. (1992) the data were generally not presented in the context of the single remaining wild-type activity.

This approach is only possible in a system for which all inputs are known. One goal of my initial analysis was to test for additional unknown AP patterning inputs using triple mutants to flatten all three known maternal AP gradients. The uniform patterns of protein expression observed in such embryos argues against the existence of other inputs. The embryos thus produced can be treated as ground states allowing the pattern contributions of the individual maternal components to be re-introduced and evaluated. The simplicity of single gradients allows input/output relationships of each maternal system to be analyzed uncomplicated by redundancies and interactions between maternal systems. My analysis of the effects of Nanos on Cic protein levels, for example, avoids the confounding effects of other maternal signaling systems, explaining perhaps the subtle differences with that of Cinnamon et al. (2004). In their analysis of single nos and osk mutants, effects of Nos on Cic levels were measured in the context of wild-type Torso regulation of the same protein. A second striking feature of the simplified systems is their sensitivity to dosage. Although strong dosage effects have been well described for Bicoid (Frohnhofer and Nusslein-Volhard, 1986; Driever and Nüsslein-Volhard, 1987; Liu et al., 2013), they have not been documented for Nanos and maternal Hb, Torso and Capicua. In these cases, loss-of-function alleles behave as recessive mutations, and heterozygotes are viable and fertile, and do not show shifts in expression patterns comparable to those associated with altered Bcd dosage. The dosage effect I observed suggests that cell fate depends on ratios and that the cis regulatory regions of many target genes measure directly or indirectly all three morphogens.

Redundancy and establishing reproducible embryonic patterns

Reducing maternal information to single inputs also provides a useful opportunity to identify points in the patterning process where multiple inputs contribute to the same final developmental outcome. My experiments identify the abdomen and posterior midgut as two points where the same single developmental pattern can be driven by alternate maternal inputs. In the abdomen, I show that a normal sequence of abdominal segments can be established by activity of any one of the three known maternal inputs. Although in no case is the abdominal pattern complete, there is overlap between the three patterns generated, an overlap which centers on the abdominal regions of segments 5 and 6. It is interesting that, in wild type, abdominal segments 5 and 6 define a region of the embryo where all maternal gradients are present but at low levels.

Patterning at the posterior pole of the embryos provides a second and unexpected case of redundancy in patterning inputs. Morphogenetic movements at the posterior pole of the Drosophila embryo depend on the establishment of domains with distinct cell fates. These include the posterior midgut (Hkb, Fog, Kr), the proctodeal hindgut domain (Byn, Wg) and ectodermal derivatives such as anal plates, posterior spiracles and tracheae. The loss of all posterior structures in torso mutants was interpreted based on the role of Torso in downregulating Capicua at the posterior pole. The finding that Capicua is also downregulated by Nanos suggests that posterior patterning has multiple inputs and that either the Torso or Nanos gradients can establish the normal sequence via their regulation of Capicua. Understanding the relationship between Hb- and Nos-generated Cic gradients might build on mutants like nosL7 that block Nanos regulation of Hb levels but retain its germline survival functions and roles in posterior patterning.

Direct and indirect responses to information content of gradients

The boundaries generated in Bcd-only embryos define positions from 27% to 67% EL. Because Bcd is the only source of positional information in these embryos, the boundaries potentially define the range of Bcd concentrations that can be meaningfully translated into patterns of gene expression. In principle, these expression boundaries can be established directly by Bcd since measurement of the precision of the Bcd gradient indicates that its concentration supplies information about position to 1% accuracy (Gregor et al., 2007). Alternatively, even if the gradient itself is reproducible, cells may not be able to measure it precisely enough. A resultant ‘sloppy’ estimate of Bcd concentration might still provide triggers for a downstream network of Bcd targets to generate precise final patterns (Chen et al., 2012; Briscoe and Small, 2015).

Understanding patterning therefore requires sorting out the direct and indirect aspects that translate maternal pattern into patterns of discrete zygotic gene expression. My data are not sufficiently extensive for a deep quantification of precision and accuracy (Dubuis et al., 2013a; Petkova et al., 2019; Haroush et al., 2023), but the variability I measured in boundary position does not seem very different from those measured in previous studies in wild-type embryos, even in circumstances such as the Bcd-only embryos where multiple gap genes are eliminated. Gap gene-dependent shifts (Jäeckle et al., 1986; Jaeger et al., 2004, 2011; Haroush et al., 2023) have been interpreted as evidence that interactive networks generate the pattern, although the limited time for the evolving interactions to establish pattern represents one challenge for such models. Moreover, the impact on final pattern of removing individual components is small, generally 1-2% EL even when, as shown here, double mutants are used to remove multiple network components. Notably, other double mutants may have stronger effects on the expression of the remaining gap genes (see Clyde et al., 2003). A parallel analysis of the 11 double, triple and quadruple gap gene phenotype might provide unbiased insights into network interactions. It is also curious that the reported interactions are generally not reciprocal, e.g. Hb negatively regulates Kr and Kni, but neither of those genes has comparable effects on Hb expression (Haroush et al., 2023).

An alternative model for measuring small concentration differences concentrations builds on the observation that all components in the gap gene network are themselves direct targets of Bicoid with enhancers that bind Bcd with concentration-dependent affinities (Hannon et al., 2017). Although any individual enhancer might have difficulty in assessing Bcd concentration, it would be aided by ‘knowing’ the estimations being made by similar enhancers in the same nucleus. In this view, gap gene ‘interactions’ can be regarded as independent, additive measurements of Bcd communicated between gap genes by interactions at their enhancers (Wieschaus, 2016). A formally similar solution to the measuring problem has been proposed by Gregor et al. (2007), but in that case it postulated adjacent nuclei communicate with each other, rather than enhancers within the same nucleus.

I favor models in which the patterning capacity of the maternal gradients reflect their direct interactions with downstream targets rather than relay mechanisms and network interactions. Given the rapidity of its embryonic development, evolution in Drosophila may have maximized accuracy of the initial interactions between maternal transcription factors and their downstream enhancers. Understanding how enhancers achieve this precision is a prerequisite for the more challenging investigation of the subsequent interactions among the targets that may further contribute to this precision. Imaging of transcriptional outputs in living embryos (Garcia et al., 2013), carefully timed measurements of enhancer accessibility and nucleosome position (Blythe and Wieschaus, 2016; Degen et al., 2025) and measurements of transcription factor concentrations and distribution in the nucleus (Gregor et al., 2007; Mir et al., 2018) make the maternal gradients in the early Drosophila embryo an especially tractable system for such analyses.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Genetics and embryo collections

The embryos examined in these experiments were derived from 28 distinct maternal genotypes carrying between one and four maternal-effect mutations (Table S3). Chromosomes with multiple maternal-effect mutations were generated by classic meiotic mutations. Because homozygotes in inbred lines often show substantially reduced viability and fecundity, in most cases multiple lines for each genotype were maintained and high-yield experimental females produced by crossing males and females from independent lines. Germline clones were produced in FRT ovoD females using an X-linked chromosome supplying Flipase activity (Chou and Perrimon, 1996). Experimental females (e.g. y w Flipase/+; FRT e tsl4 ovoD/FRT hb) were subjected to three 1-h 37°C heat shocks as larvae to induce mitotic recombination. Females from OreR or Canton S lines were used as wild-type controls.

Two chromosomes merit special mention. Because the torso-like phenotype depends on the somatic genotype, germline clones homozygous for tsl are normal (Stevens et al., 1990). To eliminate tsl activity in females carrying germline clones for hunchback and other maternal-effect mutations, the tsl4 allele was crossed onto the FRT ovoD chromosome using X-ray-induced recombination in males. Germline clones in experimental females carrying that chromosome and an FRT chromosome bearing hb and various other maternal-effect mutations (e.g. FRT e tsl4 ovoD/FRT hbFB nosBN tsl4) develop in a somatic environment homozygous mutant for tsl and show the tsl phenotype as well as any germline phenotype associated with the germline clone.

The close proximity of knirps, the FRT site and the hunchback locus made it impossible to obtain a recombination chromosome carrying all three. Instead, CRISPR was used to generate a new kni mutation in an existing FRT hb nosBN tsl chromosome previously made using meiotic recombination. The guide RNA was generously supplied by Michal Levo (Columbia University, NY, USA). The resultant kni mutation (called kni10C) replaces the wild-type knirps upstream regulatory and coding sequences with a 2attp-dsRed cassette (Levo et al., 2022). The embryos homozygous for the new allele do not show detectable Knirps protein and in trans-heterozygotes with previously characterized knirps alleles produces a cuticle phenotype equivalent to null alleles at the locus.

Immunostaining

Expression profiles were characterized using immunostaining following standard heat-based fixation protocols (Müller, 2008). Unless otherwise indicated, polyclonal antibodies were commissioned and produced by Genscript Biotech Corporation (Dubuis et al., 2013b; Petkova et al., 2019; Keenan et al., 2022). Their specificity was validated in the Wieschaus lab by Reba Samanta based on two criteria: (1) the patterns obtained following immunostaining had to correspond to expression patterns previously published using in situ hybridization or antibodies, and (2) most importantly, expression had to be eliminated in embryos homozygous mutant for null alleles of the genes encoding the protein antigen. Antibodies produced in this manner included mouse and guinea pig anti-Hunchback, rabbit anti-Kruppel, rat anti-Knirps, guinea pig anti-Giant, rat anti-Huckebein, guinea pig or rat anti-Brachyenteron, rabbit and guinea pig anti-Runt, rat anti-Buttonhead. Monoclonal mouse anti-Evenskipped and mouse anti-Paired/Pax were obtained from the Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank (DP2B8 and DP201, respectively), rat anti-Orthodenticle from Robert Finkelstein (formerly, University of Pennsylvania, PA, USA; now NINDS Division of Extramural Research, NIH), and rabbit anti Capicua from Stanislas Shvartsman (Princeton University, NJ, USA).

For each experiment and each genotype, approximately 200 embryos were mounted between two coverslips in Aqua-Poly/Mount from Polysciences, Inc. The embryos were mounted in rows that allowed efficient confocal imaging and ensured that all embryos were examined. Surface and optical cross-sections were collected using a Leica SP5 confocal microscope and in a given experiment between 20 and 50 embryos were recorded and analyzed in detail. In cases in which the embryo was sufficiently flattened by the coverslip such that the surface and cross-sectional views completely overlapped, figures were prepared using only the surface view. When the embryos were not sufficiently flattened to allow inferring the overall shape of the embryo from the surface view, confocal images are presented as composites, consisting of the surface view layered onto a cross-sectional view. These composites were created manually and the contributions of the two original images are preserved. Embryo images made by combining two layers can be distinguished based on the dark line near the perimeter of the embryo separating the two layers. Examples of such layered embryos with composite lines are shown in Figs 2A, 3B and 6C,F. Embryos shown only in surface view were used in Fig. 4B,D and clearly lack the composite lines separating the two layers of composite images.

Quantification of expression profiles were made using the original images of the optical cross-section plane. The embryos shown in the figures adjacent to the measured expression profiles are generally shown in cross-section (Figs 2B and 6A,B; Fig. S1).

Quantification of expression profiles

Expression profiles in immunostained embryos were recorded in medial optical sections and nuclear intensities and measured along the AP axis of the embryo using image analysis tools built into ImageJ. Specifically, a line approximating the width of the nuclei was manually drawn through the nuclear layer on the dorsal side of the embryo from the anterior most point in the embryo to the posterior-most somatic point underlying the pole cells. The length of the line varied between embryos but was approximately 500 µm. Intensity profiles along the line were measured in ImageJ and transferred to Excel, where positions along the line in microns were converted to percentage egg length by dividing each position by the length of the line. Positions as percentage egg length were then binned to define 100 units along the AP axis of the embryo. The intensities per bin provide the core data for subsequent analyses and were calculated by averaging the intensities falling within each bin.

For zygotic expression, background levels were subtracted and intensities normalized for each protein relative to the peak values measured between 10% and 90% EL. Because all proteins studied have a region with no expression in the middle region of the embryo, background levels were defined as the minimal intensity value measured between 20% and 80% EL.

In the experiments measuring the impact of kni and hb mutation on gap protein expression in embryos from Bcd-only germline clones, all zygotic genotypes were immunostained in the same tube. To allow comparison of levels, intensity profile in the hb and kni hb were normalized to the non-mutant ‘Bcd-only’ control embryos.

Because nuclear density at the blastoderm stage varies along the AP axis (Blankenship and Wieschaus, 2001; Keränen et al., 2006), quantification of maternal Cic and Hb protein levels were normalized by dividing the Cic or Hb intensity profile by the intensity profile of DAPI fluorescence in the same optical section. The resultant values were then normalized to set the peak intensity values for each embryo and each immunostaining to 1, and lowest values to zero. Final profiles are presented in figures as averages from between five and 28 single embryo profiles, with the number of embryos examined given in the figure itself or in the figure legend, and either standard deviations and standard errors. The number of embryos in each experiment and the numerical data are also presented in Tables S1 and S2.
