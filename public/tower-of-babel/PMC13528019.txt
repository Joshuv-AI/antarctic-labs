Exploring quality of life and physical-physiological characteristics in obese patients with and without lipedema: insights from the LipObes study
Authors: Ceren Gursen, Jasmien Cools, Lode Claes, An De Groef, Mira Meeus, Laura Spincemaille, Fran Pouchele, Sarah Thomis, Véronique Cornelissen, Nele Devoogdt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLipedema is a prevalent chronic condition in women, characterized by a painful and symmetrical accumulation of adipose tissue primarily in the lower limbs. Its diagnosis is based on specific clinical characteristics; however, these characteristics lack robust scientific validation. Furthermore, lipedema is frequently misdiagnosed as obesity. This study aims to compare quality of life and physical and psychological characteristics between patients with obesity with and without lipedema.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 30 patients with obesity and lipedema (Lip-Obes group) and 29 patients with only obesity (nonLip-Obes group). Quality of life, body composition (BMI, fat free mass, fat mass, waist-to-hip ratio, waist-to-height ratio, leg volume), pain (pain pattern, pressure pain thresholds, pain interference, pain distribution, symptoms of neuropathic pain), physical functioning (hand grip strength, quadriceps strength, functional exercise capacity, functional mobility and physical activity level), and psychosocial functioning (pain catastrophizing, depression, anxiety, and stress, body image dissatisfaction, self-efficacy, and eating difficulties) were assessed using clinical measurements and self-reported outcomes. Statistical analyses were performed using independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsCompared to the nonLip-Obes group, the Lip-Obes group showed greater impairments in quality of life (p < 0.05). Despite similar body composition variables, the Lip-Obes group had lower waist-to-hip, waist-to-height, upper leg-to-waist, and lower leg-to-waist ratios, as well as higher total limb volume than the nonLip-Obes group. Additionally, the Lip-Obes group reported higher pain intensity, lower pressure pain thresholds in the arms and legs, and greater pain interference than the nonLip-Obes group (p < 0.05). Although hand-grip strength and physical activity levels were comparable, the Lip-Obes group exhibited lower quadriceps strength, functional exercise capacity, and functional mobility (p < 0.05). Additionally, the Lip-Obes group reported higher pain catastrophizing, greater body image dissatisfaction, and more severe eating difficulties than the nonLip-Obes group (p < 0.05). No significant differences were found in depression, anxiety, stress, or self-efficacy between groups (p > 0.05).ConclusionThis cross-sectional study highlights the complex nature of lipedema, providing preliminary evidence of differences in quality of life and distinct body composition and physical and physiological characteristics between patients with obesity with and without lipedema. These results emphasize the need for further research to identify diagnostic biomarkers for lipedema through in-depth investigations. Future studies should also focus on developing and optimizing a multidisciplinary treatment approach tailored to the unique characteristics of patients with lipedema.Keywords: Lipedema, Obesity, Diagnosis, Body composition, Pain, Physical functioning, Psychological functioning

Background

According to the consensus document of the International Lipoedema Association (ILA) published in 2020, lipedema is a chronic condition characterized by the disproportionate and bilateral accumulation of subcutaneous adipose tissue, primarily in the lower legs, hips, and buttocks [1]. The upper arms may also be affected, while the trunk, hands, and feet are typically spared [1, 2]. Although the etiology of lipedema is not fully understood, potential mechanisms include vascular and lymphatic dysfunction, hormonal changes, alterations in the extracellular matrix, and genetic factors [3]. Prevalence estimates suggest lipedema affects 11% to 18% of women, with rare occurrence in men [4–6].

The absence of specific biomarkers or imaging techniques complicates the diagnosis of lipedema, which currently relies on clinical examination, making it vulnerable to misdiagnosis—especially as obesity [7, 8]. In addition to fat accumulation, patients with lipedema frequently report pain, tenderness, and bruising in affected areas [7, 9]. However, coexisting obesity can obscure the visual signs of lipedema and contribute to diagnostic delays [10]. This diagnostic challenge highlights the importance of better characterizing the differences between patients with obesity and coexisting lipedema versus those with obesity alone.

Lipedema has been associated with reduced quality of life [7, 11, 12], yet only one study has directly compared quality of life between patients with lipedema and those with obesity [13]. That study found that women with lipedema experienced greater impairments in daily functioning and psychosocial well-being, even after adjusting for BMI [13]. Despite this, evidence remains limited regarding how lipedema uniquely impacts physical and psychological health compared to obesity.

Body composition data in lipedema are also inconclusive. Although 76–88% of patients with lipedema have a BMI above 30 kg/m² [14, 15], findings on fat mass and fat-free mass are inconsistent across studies [8, 16, 17]. While some studies found higher fat mass in patients with lipedema compared to healthy controls, others did not detect differences when compared to BMI-matched individuals [8]. Yet, significant differences in fat distribution have been found between patients with lipedema versus patients with obesity. Patients with lipedema often appear normal above the waist but have disproportionately enlarged legs, whereas visceral and subcutaneous fat tissue is distributed globally and proportionately in patients with obesity [8, 18, 19]. However, detailed analyses using regional fat ratios (e.g., upper leg/waist) remain absent from the literature.

The 2024 guideline of the German Society of Phlebology and Lymphology states that pain is consistently present in patients with lipedema [7]. Tissue hypoxia and low-grade inflammation are considered primary mechanisms underlying pain in lipedema [7]. Additionally, mechanical alterations in the subcutaneous adipose tissue i.e., increased tissue stiffness, may disrupt nerve function, leading to altered peripheral sensory processing in patients with lipedema [2, 3]. Pain in the adipose tissue is a hallmark symptom of lipedema and is typically absent in individuals with obesity. This pain is often described as dull and pressure-induced [3, 20], which helps distinguish lipedema from obesity, where such localized pain is not commonly reported. Although pressure sensitivity is a hallmark symptom in patients with lipedema [21, 22], the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood, and comparative data involving patients with obesity are limited. Therefore, assessing pressure pain thresholds in regions of fat accumulation in both groups is essential to objectively quantify this symptom in lipedema and to better characterize its specificity in obese patients with lipedema. Patients with lipedema also frequently report neuropathic pain symptoms such as burning or tingling [3, 20], yet comparative studies on pain profiles between lipedema and obesity are scarce. Therefore, quantifying pain through objective measures like pressure pain thresholds is essential to improving our understanding of lipedema-related pain.

Lipedema may also impair physical functioning due to pain, disproportionate leg volumes, and possible loss of muscle mass [23, 24]. Only one study has compared muscle strength and functional capacity between lipedema and obesity, finding lower quadriceps strength and six-minute walk distance in the former group [24]. However, data on handgrip strength, functional mobility, and physical activity levels remain lacking.

Previous research suggests that patients with lipedema face problems in psychological functioning such as depression, anxiety, stress, and eating difficulties, frequently attributed to social stigma [13, 25]. A study by Erbacher reported that approximately 80% of patients with lipedema exhibited heightened psychological distress even before the onset of lipedema-related symptoms [26]. Notably, mental well-being is often more severely affected than the physical condition in lipedema [27]. Patients with lipedema are frequently stigmatized by medical professionals and others, who may perceive them as obese or lazy [6]. Consequently, they may develop feelings of shame, guilt, and helplessness, which can contribute to psychological symptoms [12]. Additionally, many patients with lipedema often report low self-acceptance and satisfaction with their bodies, partly influenced by societal ideals favoring a slim, long-legged body shape [26]. To date, only one study has compared psychological symptoms between lipedema and obesity, reporting higher levels of depression and anxiety in patients with lipedema [13]. However, differences in pain catastrophizing, body image dissatisfaction, self-efficacy, and problems in eating difficulties between obese patients with lipedema and those without lipedema have not yet been thoroughly investigated.

Given the gaps in the literature, further studies are essential to better characterize the distinct physical and psychological profiles of patients with lipedema and obesity. Such understanding is critical for improving diagnostic accuracy and optimizing treatment strategies [1, 2, 7, 8, 13]. The aim of this study is to compare quality of life, body composition, pain, physical functioning (muscle strength, functional exercise capacity, functional mobility, and physical activity level), and psychological functioning (pain catastrophizing, depression, anxiety, stress, body image dissatisfaction, self-efficacy, and eating difficulties) between obese patients with and without lipedema, using clinical and self-reported measures. We hypothesize that patients with lipedema and obesity will demonstrate distinct profiles in these domains compared to those with obesity alone. Specifically, we expect differences in pain characteristics, physical functioning, and psychological outcomes, but not in physical activity levels.

Methods

Study design and setting

The LipObes study is a cross-sectional study and reported based on the principles of the “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE)” statement [28]. This study was conducted at the Center for Lymphedema and the Obesity Clinic of the University Hospital of Leuven (UZ Leuven) in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of UZ/KU Leuven (S66542). All patients provided signed informed consent prior to participating in the study.

Patients

A total of 59 patients were recruited between August 2022 and December 2023 through research advertisements on social media, approved by the ethical committee, as well as through consultations at the Center for Lymphedema and the Obesity Clinic at UZ Leuven. Patients were screened by the same clinical team at the Center for Lymphedema (J.C., S.T., and N.D.) and at the Obesity Clinic (L.C. and V.C.) to maintain consistency in determination of the eligibility. Patients with upcoming appointments at the two centers were contacted by phone and informed about the study. If they agreed to participate, an informed consent form was signed and the measurements were conducted following their consultation with the medical doctor.

The LipObes study consisted of two groups: (1) patients with obesity and lipedema (the Lip-Obes group) and (2) patients with obesity without lipedema (the nonLip-Obes group). Inclusion criteria for both groups were age of 18 years or older, a BMI > 30 kg/m² as the presence of obesity has been defined based on the World Health Organization classification, and female gender. For the Lip-Obes group, patients were also required to meet two additional criteria: (1) disproportionate fat distribution (fat deposition at the hips and legs with a normal appearance above the waist) and (2) the presence of bilateral pain in the soft tissues of the affected regions. These criteria were determined based on the international guidelines [1, 7] and the clinical expertise of the researchers. For the nonLip-Obes group, additional inclusion criteria were: a global and proportional distribution of body fat and no bilateral pain in the soft tissues of the lower limbs. Exclusion criteria for both groups were having a history of liposuction or bariatric surgery.

Assessments

In this study, data collection was carried out using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) system. The assessment consisted of three components: the collection of the sociodemographic and medical characteristics, a clinical evaluation, and the completion of self-reported outcome measures. The clinical evaluation for the lipedema group was conducted by a physiotherapist (JC) specialized in lipedema at the Center for Lymphedema at UZ Leuven. For the non-lipedema group, a physiotherapist (LC) specialized in obesity performed the clinical evaluation at the Obesity Clinic at UZ Leuven. All patients were also asked to complete electronic self-administered questionnaires via the REDCap system. If patients were unable to complete the electronic questionnaires, paper versions were provided.

The sociodemographic and medical characteristics of the sample included age, educational level (low level of education/high level of education), working situation (working/not working/retired), living together with a partner (yes/no), and smoking (yes/no). Patients who graduated from high school or university were categorized as having a high educational level, while those who graduated from primary or secondary school were categorized as having a low educational level. The Comorbidity Questionnaire was used to determine the presence of comorbidities and the prevalences of the cardiovascular, respiratory, metabolic, and psychological problems were recorded as yes/no [29].

All outcome measures are described in detail in Tables 1 and 2. The outcome measures of the study were categorized in 6 groups: (1) quality of life by the Lipoedema Functioning, Disability and Health Questionnaire–Lip-ICF Questionnaire [30]; (2) body composition i.e., BMI and obesity grades based on BMI (Grade I: BMI 30.0–34.9 kg/m², Grade II: BMI 35.0–39.9 kg/m², and Grade III: BMI ≥ 40.0 kg/m²); fat free mass and fat mass by the Bioimpedance Analysis [31]; waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) [32]; waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) [33]; leg volume by the circumference measurements [34]; and upper leg/waist ratio, lower leg/waist ratio, and lower leg/upper leg ratio by the circumference measurements (3), pain characteristics i.e., pain pattern by the Numeric Rating Scale–NRS in rest and during activity; pressure pain thresholds by the digital algometer [35]; pain interference and pain distribution by the Brief Bain Inventory–BPI [36]; signs and symptoms of neuropathic pain by the Douleur Neuropathic 4–DN4 [37], (4) physical functioning i.e., hand grip strength by a JAMAR dynamometer [38]; quadriceps strength by the Wall Sit Test [39] and by the isokinetic dynamometer [40], functional exercise capacity by the 6MWDT [41]; functional mobility by the Timed Up and Go test–TUG [42]; and physical activity level by the International Physical Activity Questionnaire–IPAQ [43], and (5) psychological functioning i.e. pain catastrophizing by the Pain Catastrophizing Scale–PCS [44]; depression, anxiety, and stress by the Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale–DASS-21 [45]; body image dissatisfaction by the Body Image Scale–BIS [46]; self-efficacy by the General Self-efficacy Scale–GSE [47]; and eating difficulties by the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire–EDE-Q [48].

The isokinetic test was performed at an angular velocity of 30 degrees per second. The patient sits on the device with the hip positioned at 90° flexion and the knee at 60° flexion. The length of the torque arm is adjusted so that the lower edge of the pad sits two finger widths above the upper edge of the lateral malleolus. The axis of the torque arm is aligned with the knee joint’s flexion-extension axis.

Test is performed on the right side for all patients. Except for one patient with knee pain, for whom the left side was tested. The patient is given maximum encouragement to perform the test. Four trials are conducted, with a 20-second pause between each. The highest result among the four trials is recorded as the final value.

To determine the percentage of the norm for quadriceps strength, predicted force values are calculated based on the patient’s age, weight (in kg), gender (1 for men, 0 for women), and the side tested. The formulas are used as follows:

‘Predicted quadriceps force right leg = -2,21*age + 1,78*weight + 55,9*gender + 124Nm’ and

Predicted quadriceps force left leg = -3,01*age + 1,70*weight + 56,8*gender + 158Nm’.

The actual measured force is then expressed as a percentage of these predicted values to assess the patient’s quadriceps strength relative to the norm.

In the 6MWT, the patient is instructed to walk as far as possible within a 6-minute period, covering the distance between the designated start and end lines. The patient receives encouragement at each minute to maintain her effort.

The total distance walked in meters is recorded.

The percentage of the norm value is determined based on the patient’s height (cm), weight (kg), age and gender (1 = man, 0 = woman). The following formula is used: ‘Predicted walking distance = 218 + (5,14*height – 5,32*age) – (1,80*weight) + (51,31*gender)’

The percentage of the norm value was then derived by comparing the measured walking distance with this predicted value.

Statistical analysis

Due to the exploratory nature of this study, a sample size calculation was not conducted. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics for continuous variables are presented as the mean and standard deviation for normally distributed data, or as the median and quartiles (Q1 and Q3) for non-normally distributed data. For categorical variables, frequencies and percentages are reported. The normality of the distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Group characteristics were compared using independent t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables. When the distribution of the variable was not normal, a non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was applied. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variables, depending on the number of categories within the variable. A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Quality of life

The Lip-Obes group exhibited significantly higher total and domain scores (physical function, mental function, general tasks/ household activities, mobility and life domains/ social life score) on the Lip-ICF Questionnaire suggesting that the Lip-Obes group has a poorer quality of life.

Body composition

No significant differences were found between the two groups in body composition measures such as BMI values and grades i.e., Grade I, II, and III, fat-free and fat mass. However, the lower WHR and WHtR, higher total leg volume, lower upper leg-to-waist and lower leg-to-waist ratios were found in the Lip-Obes group, compared to the nonLip-Obes group.

Pain characteristics

The Lip-Obes group showed significantly higher pain intensity scores both at rest and after activity compared to the nonLip-Obes group. Regarding pain pressure thresholds, the Lip-Obes group had significantly lower pressure pain thresholds at the m. triceps, m. quadriceps, and m. tibialis anterior than the nonLip-Obes group. A higher pain interference was found for the Lip-Obes group in comparison with the nonLip-Obes group.

Further analysis of pain distribution revealed that, in the Lip-Obes group, a significantly lower proportion of patients reported widespread pain (11% vs. 45%) and localized pain in any area (33% vs. 75%) compared to the nonLip-Obes group.

Neuropathic pain characteristics and associated sensory symptoms were significantly more prevalent in the Lip-Obes group compared to the nonLip-Obes group. The majority of patients in the Lip-Obes group reported at least one neuropathic pain symptom (82%) and associated sensory symptom (92%), whereas 30% and 35% of patients in the nonLip-Obes group reported at least one neuropathic pain symptom and associated sensory symptom, respectively.

Physical functioning

Significant lower quadriceps muscle strength and functional exercise capacity was observed in the patients of the Lip Obes group. For functional mobility, the TUG test score was significantly higher in the Lip-Obes group, compared to the nonLip-Obes group. Daily physical activity levels were higher in the Lip-Obes group, especially the amount of time spent at moderate activity.

Psychological functioning

Patients in the Lip-Obes group had significantly higher pain catastrophizing and body image dissatisfaction scores compared to those in the nonLip-Obes group. Additionally, the Lip-Obes group scored higher on total problematic eating difficulties scores, as well as in the restraint, shape concern, and weight concern domains, compared to the nonLip-Obes group. However, no significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of depression, anxiety, stress symptoms or self-efficacy.

Discussion

This study is the first to comprehensively compare quality of life and physical and psychological characteristics of obese patients with lipedema to those without lipedema, utilizing both clinical measurements and self-reported outcomes. The findings of this cross-sectional study confirm several hypotheses. Obese patients with lipedema demonstrated lower quality of life than those without lipedema. Regarding the body composition including fat distribution, obese patients with lipedema exhibited smaller waist measurements, larger hip circumferences, and increased lower limb volume. Pain characteristics also differed between the groups, with obese patients with lipedema experiencing more severe pain intensity both during rest and activity, lower pressure pain thresholds in the arms and legs, more pain interference in their daily life, less frequent widespread pain or pain beyond the fat accumulation region, and more frequent neuropathic pain related symptoms. In terms of physical functioning, obese patients with lipedema showed reduced quadriceps muscle strength, diminished functional exercise capacity, and lower functional mobility compared to those without lipedema. Finally, regarding psychological functioning, lipedema patients with obesity reported higher levels of pain catastrophizing, greater dissatisfaction with body image, and more severe eating difficulties, further highlighting the multifactorial challenges faced by this population.

Although the two groups were comparable in terms of the prevalence of cardiovascular comorbidities, the Lip-Obes group has less frequent arterial hypertension (13% versus 24%) than the nonLip-Obes group. These results are consistent with the findings of the other studies [20]. As patients with lipedema have a disproportional (gynoid type) fat distribution, it has been hypothesized that this type of fat distribution in lipedema would not have effect the metabolic health [20].

Although quality of life is an important measure for assessing well-being of patients with lipedema, it is not frequently reported as an outcome, as highlighted in a scoping review [23]. The results of our study strongly suggest that obese patients with lipedema have a more impaired quality of life compared to those without lipedema. This finding aligns with a previous study [13], which after adjusting for BMI, reported that patients with lipedema experience greater functional impairments in household activities, societal participation, and mobility subdomains of the World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule (WHO-DAS II) than patients with obesity or overweight individuals [13]. Additionally, other studies compared quality of life of patients with lipedema to that of the general population [49] or the BMI-matched controls [50] have revealed that patients with lipedema have lower quality of life scores. In our study, the lowest scores on the Lip-ICF questionnaire were observed in the mental function subdomain among obese patients with lipedema. This is an expected finding, as the physical appearance associated with lipedema is a distressing factor, leading to more mental function challenges than physical function [51]. This result is consistent with the study by Hamatschek et al. [50], which showed that patients with lipedema experience significant functional difficulties, particularly related to mental distress.

As expected, no significant differences in body composition parameters such as BMI, fat-free mass, and fat mass were observed between obese patients with lipedema and those without lipedema. While previous studies have compared lipedema patients to control groups that included individuals with overweight and obesity [52, 53], there remains a need for direct comparisons with well-defined and homogenous groups of obese patients without lipedema, particularly regarding body composition parameters. Reported mean BMI values for patients with lipedema range between 30.8 and 37.0 kg/m² [49, 50, 54]. Angst et al. [55] noted that 88.4% of patients with lipedema had a BMI exceeding 30 kg/m². In our study, the majority of patients with lipedema (73%) were classified as having Grade II or Grade III obesity (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m²). Although BMI is frequently emphasized as an indicator of metabolic status in patients with lipedema, its validity for this purpose has been questioned. A recent study suggested that BMI may lack construct validity, as it increases with the disproportionate accumulation of subcutaneous adipose tissue, even in patients with lipedema classified as metabolically normal [33]. Consequently, distinguishing whether an elevated BMI is attributable solely to lipedema or to a combination of lipedema and coincident obesity can be challenging. Furthermore, bioimpedance analysis in the present study did not reveal significant differences in fat mass or fat-free mass between the groups. This finding indicates that the total amount of fat is similar in both groups. It aligns with previous research that reported no significant differences in overall fat mass or fat-free mass between patients with lipedema and BMI-matched controls using bioimpedance spectroscopy [8].

On the other hand, the differences in body composition parameters related to fat distribution —such as the lower WHR and WHtR, higher total lower limb volume, and higher upper leg-to-waist and lower leg-to-waist ratios in the Lip-Obes group compared to the nonLip-Obes group—were consistent with both the literature and the study hypothesis. Only one prior study has investigated differences in WHR between lipedema and obesity [14]. Dudek et al. [49] reported an average WHR of 0.78 in patients with lipedema, closely aligning with the present study’s finding of 0.80 (± 0.09) in the Lip-Obes group. These results confirm that, despite similar BMI values, obese patients with lipedema exhibit smaller waist circumferences and larger leg circumferences compared to those without lipedema. This confirms the importance of assessing fat distribution patterns for diagnosing lipedema. Recent evidence also suggests that WHtR may better assess obesity in patients with lipedema. For instance, in one study, BMI classified 24% of patients as having Grade I obesity, while WHtR classified only 15%. Similarly, BMI identified 16% of patients with Grade II obesity, whereas WHtR identified only 2.3% [33]. These findings indicate that relying solely on BMI may overestimate obesity in patients with lipedema.

Pain is a key feature distinguishing lipedema from obesity, however it has been rarely investigated objectively yet [20]. In patients with lipedema, pain is often characterized by dull, pressure-induced nociceptive pain and burning, tingling, or stabbing sensations indicative of neuropathic pain [3, 20]. Chronic inflammation in lipedema may affect the function of small or medium sized C/Aδ-fibres, which are believed to transmit pressure and thermal stimuli [53]. We found that obese patients with lipedema reported higher pain intensity and interference, lower pressure pain thresholds at muscles such as the triceps brachii, quadriceps femoris, and tibialis anterior, and more frequent neuropathic pain symptoms compared to those without lipedema. In our study, unlike their non-lipedema counterparts, obese patients with lipedema experienced less widespread pain, with pain localized to areas of fat accumulation. These findings confirm a more severe and distinct pain profile in lipedema. Pressure pain thresholds provide objective evidence of pressure sensitivity, likely linked to local inflammation and mechanical hyperalgesia in lipedema, as suggested in previous research [21, 22]. Interestingly, while a prior study reported higher pressure pain thresholds at m. quadriceps femoris non-obese patients with lipedema compared to BMI- and WHtR-matched controls [2], we found a two-fold decrease in pressure pain thresholds across all measured points in the arms and legs. In addition, pressure sensitivity was strongest at the m. triceps brachii in obese patients with lipedema despite disproportionate fat accumulation in thigh. Given all this, the presence of pressure sensitivity warrants further investigation in obese and non-obese patients with lipedema. Furthermore, it is important to note that we did not assess pain intensity before or after the 6-minute walk test, which could have influenced due to exercise-induced pain exacerbation in this population.

In our study, obese patients with lipedema exhibited significant lower levels of physical functioning compared to those without lipedema. Muscle strength in patients with lipedema had not been extensively studied previously. Our findings confirm this hypothesis, with quadriceps muscle strength significantly lower in the LipObes group. In contrast, hand grip strength did not differ between the groups, likely because the upper extremity are not typically affected by pain in lipedema. To our knowledge, only one study by van Esch-Smeenge et al. [24] has compared muscle strength between patients with lipedema and obesity. Consistent with our results, they reported lower quadriceps muscle strength and a lower percentage of the normative value in the lipedema group compared to the obesity group (269 N, 67% in the lipedema group versus 400 N, 100% in the obesity group) [24]. Functional exercise capacity in patients with lipedema has been investigated in only two prior studies [24, 55]. Those studies reported lower mean walking distances in patients with lipedema (437 m and 494 m) compared to the distance observed in our study (514 m). In the study of van Esch-Smeenge [24], although 6MWDT distance did not differ significantly between patients with lipedema and obesity, it was clinically lower in the lipedema group (494 m versus 523 m). Functional mobility, which had not been previously measured in patients with lipedema, was also assessed in our study. As expected, the LipObes group demonstrated significantly poorer functional mobility, as evidenced by longer duration of the TUG test compared to the nonLip-Obes group. This impairment may be attributed to the reduced lower limb muscle strength and higher pain catastrophizing observed in the Lip-Obes group. Regarding physical activity levels, no significant differences were anticipated between the groups, and this was largely supported by the findings, except for the moderate MET-minutes/week score. However, the Lip-Obes group demonstrated approximately three times higher total MET-minutes/week score compared to the nonLip-Obes group. These results should be interpreted with caution due to missing values in the nonLip-Obes group and misinterpretations when completing the IPAQ. In addition, the literature indicates that self-reported questionnaires have important limitations, including recall bias, missingness, and low precision for measuring moderate and vigorous physical activities [56, 57].

This study supported the hypothesis that obese patients with lipedema experience more pronounced psychological functioning problems—such as higher levels of pain catastrophizing, body image dissatisfaction, and eating difficulties—compared to those without lipedema, consistent with existing literature. Patients with lipedema tend to magnify or ruminate on pain, and prior studies have documented higher prevalence rates of depressed mood, lower emotional well-being, and heightened depressive symptoms in this population [49, 58]. Challenges such as pain, muscle weakness, reduced functional exercise capacity, and disproportionate body shape may place patients with lipedema at a higher risk of experiencing psychological symptoms, including poor body image, distress and eating disorders [10, 49]. In our study, the prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress ranged from 35% to 46%, aligning with previous reports of depression rates between 31% and 59% [49–51]. However, DASS-21 scores did not differ significantly between the two groups, suggesting comparable levels of psychological symptoms. Despite elevated pain catastrophizing, self-efficacy levels remained comparable between groups, indicating that patients with lipedema retain confidence in managing challenging situations. Body image dissatisfaction was significantly higher in the Lip-Obes group, with 27 of 30 patients reporting severe dissatisfaction, which is an important finding of the Lip-Obes study. This finding supports that the disproportionate body shape may cause negative body image in patients with lipedema and negative criticisms from their social contacts and health care professionals, may lead to eating disorders and isolation [10, 59].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, it is the first to use objective measurements in combination with self-reported tools to comprehensively compare quality of life, and physical and psychosocial characteristics between obese patients with lipedema and those without lipedema. Second, patients in both study groups were strictly determined using standardized diagnostic criteria based on the literature and clinical expertise. Third, both groups were comparable in terms of age and BMI. These similarities enhance the validity of the comparisons and minimize potential confounding factors. In the literature, there is no lipedema-specific quality of life questionnaire. To address this gap, the study adapted the Lymphoedema Functioning, Disability and Health Questionnaire for Lower Limb Lymphoedema (Lymph-ICF-LL), by replacing the term “lymphoedema” with “lipedema” in each question. While this adaptation provided a practical solution, the lack of a validated lipedema-specific quality of life tool may affect the precision of the results.

The first limitation of our study is the assignment of one assessor for per study group (Lip-Obes or nonLip-Obes group). To minimize potential measurement variability, both assessors underwent standardized training prior to data collection. However, this allocation of one assessor per group could still introduce subtle inconsistencies between the groups. The second limitation relates to the use of self-reported questionnaires, which were distributed to participants without in-person guidance. As a result, some patients may have misinterpreted questions, particularly in the IPAQ, leading to invalid data for certain variables. Additionally, it is well-documented that individuals often overestimate their physical activity levels on the self-reported outcome measures [60]. The third limitation concerns missing data, as some patients did not complete all the questionnaires or provided only partial responses. This missing data could introduce selection bias [61], as those who did not complete the questionnaires might be more likely to have a lower overall health status. Consequently, the findings related to psychological functioning may underestimate the true burden of lipedema. The forth limitation is the absence of lipedema stage classification (stage 1–4), which may have provided additional insights into the relationship between disease severity and the assessed outcomes. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable insights and provides strong preliminary findings for future research.

Clinical implications and further research suggestions

The findings of this study offer valuable insights that can be directly applied to clinical practice. First, the distinct characteristics in terms of disproportionate fat distribution, more localized and severe pain and more frequent neuropathic pain symptoms identified in obese patients with lipedema can aid in differentiating them from those without lipedema. Assessing muscle strength and functional exercise capacity in relation to normative values during early physiotherapy consultations could provide valuable insights for tailored physical therapy interventions aimed at improving physical functioning in patients with lipedema [24]. Given the multimodal nature of lipedema, a multidisciplinary treatment approach is essential. Besides pain management and exercise therapy aimed at improving adaptive coping strategies and boosting the self-efficacy of patients with lipedema, a management program for patients with lipedema should also focus the improvement of psychological problems such as body image dissatisfaction and eating problems.

Further research with larger sample sizes and longitudinal design is needed to determine distinct characteristics of lipedema and to assess the diagnostic potentials of various physical and psychological characteristics. Future studies should also consider using both BMI and WHR/ WHtR to minimize the risk of overestimating obesity in patients with lipedema. This combined approach may provide a more accurate understanding of the metabolic status and aid in tailoring appropriate therapeutic strategies. We also recommend to perform segmental Bioimpedance Analysis in order to better determine the differences in fat-free and fat mass between lipedema and obesity. As pain characteristics in lipedema remain speculative, further research should investigate the peripheral (i.e., nociceptive and neuropathic) and central sensory processing mechanisms in patients with lipedema based on the Quantitative Sensory Testing protocol of the German Research Network on Neuropathic Pain (DFNS) [62]. In addition, future studies are encouraged to explore the relationship between pain intensity and psychological factors such as catastrophizing, as this may help explain the higher pain levels observed in patients with lipedema. Future studies should consider using objective tools i.e., accelerometers in combination with physical activity questionnaires to accurately measure physical activity level in lipedema. Lastly, in addition to physical and psychological characteristics, the distinct systemic nature of lipedema compared to obesity should be explored through measurements of metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers.

Conclusion

This study highlights key differences including disproportionate fat distribution, localized pain in fat accumulation, reduced functional exercise capacity, greater pain catastrophizing, and poorer body image in obese patients with lipedema. These findings underscore the need for a multifactorial assessment to improve phenotyping, enhance understanding of the underlying pathophysiology, and optimize management strategies for lipedema. Further research is crucial to identify diagnostic biomarkers and develop a multidisciplinary treatment approach tailored to the needs of patients with lipedema.
