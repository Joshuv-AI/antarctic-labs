Integrating ESG indicators and behavioral factors to estimate the mental health index in Italy
Authors: Resta Emanuela, Logroscino Giancarlo, Peter Preethymol, Costantiello Alberto, Leogrande Angelo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article examines the relationship between behavioral and Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) variables and the Mental Health Index (MHI) of Italian regions from 2016 to 2023. The use of descriptive statistics, cluster analysis, and forecasting models is employed for descriptive and exploratory purposes, as well as for forecasting regional disparities in mental health. Relative stability at the state level and persistent territorial variability are evident in the MHI derived from the ISTAT-BES indicators, with the North and Centre performing better than the South, except for notable improvements in disadvantaged regions such as Calabria and Campania. Behavioral factors, particularly sedentariness, smoking, and multimorbidity, are consistently associated with adverse trends for mental health, while adequate nourishment and exposure to urban green spaces are found to be protective. ESG-related factors, including occupational security and judicial efficacy, also contribute to these inequalities, attributing mental health status as part of the more general system effects of institutions and environments. Cluster analysis identifies distinct regional typologies based on aggregated behavioral and ESG patterns, while the predictive model highlights multimorbidity, smoking, and sedentary behavior as the most relevant factors negatively associated with the Mental Health Index (MHI), and urban green availability as a key factor positively associated with it. They point out that Italian mental health disparities are not only associated with behavioral determinants of individuals, but must be addressed from a multi-faceted ESG perspective. Policy reactions also mean that public health responses must attend not only to lifestyle risk factors but also structural determinants and incorporate active living promotion, reduction of smoking prevalence, investment in green infrastructures, and improvement of governance efficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13033-026-00716-x.Keywords: Mental health index; ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance); Regional disparities; Machine learning analysis; Public policy and well-being

Introduction

The present research contributes to the existing literature by integrating conceptual, empirical, and methodological advances within a single analytical framework. First, from a conceptual perspective, the study introduces an ESG-based approach to the analysis of mental health inequalities, explicitly combining environmental quality, governance efficiency, and social conditions—dimensions that are typically examined separately in prior Italy-focused studies on health disparities [1, 2]. Second, from an empirical standpoint, the paper provides an updated regional panel analysis for the period 2016–2023 based on ISTAT-BES data, offering recent and consistent evidence on territorial heterogeneity across Italian macro-regions, including the more advanced North, the transitional Centre, and the structurally weaker South and Islands [3, 4]. Third, from a methodological perspective, the study adopts a combined clustering–prediction design, using k-means clustering to identify six homogeneous regional typologies and predictive modeling to assess the relative importance of the main determinants of the Mental Health Index. Within this framework, multimorbidity, smoking, and sedentariness emerge as the most relevant negative predictors, while urban green availability represents a key positive factor. Overall, the novelty of the paper lies not in each element taken in isolation, but in their joint integration—an approach that has not been previously applied in an integrated way to the analysis of mental health inequalities across Italian regions—thereby linking ESG conditions, regional disparities, and the main drivers of mental health outcomes, and supporting policy implications aligned with the SDGs, including health, sustainable cities, and social justice [5, 6].

Methodological integration of clustering and predictive modelling to analyze ESG and mental health interlinkages in Italian regions

This study adopts a two-stage analytical framework to investigate regional patterns of mental health in Italy and their relationships with behavioral and Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) factors. In the first stage, unsupervised clustering methods are used to identify homogeneous typologies in mental health across Italy. Six clustering methods are used, namely Density-Based Clustering, Fuzzy C-Means Clustering, Hierarchical Clustering, Model-Based Clustering, Random Forest Clustering, and K-Means Clustering. The selection of clustering methods is informed by their performance across multiple evaluation criteria, including R2, AIC, BIC, Silhouette Score, Dunn Index, Entropy, and Calinski-Harabasz Index. The K-Means Clustering method is found to be the most suitable for this study, as concluded by Resta et al. [7]. The Elbow Method is used to determine the most suitable clusters for representing the Italian regions as homogeneous groups in terms of mental health typologies, considering both behavioral and ESG factors, as well as the distinction between northern and southern Italian regions, and it is concluded that six clusters are enough.

Defining the ESG and behavioral foundations of mental health across Italian territories

This section aims to demarcate the different variables used to measure the Mental Health Index (MHI) in relation to ESG behavioral, environmental, and social factors. The MHI measures the psychological well-being of individuals aged 14 and older. The Mental Health Index (MHI) is derived from the Mental Health (MH) component of the SF-36 (36-Item Short Form Health Survey), a widely used psychometric instrument in population-based studies. The index is based on the aggregation of responses to five specific items capturing psychological distress and well-being, including dimensions related to anxiety and depression. The resulting score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating better mental health conditions and higher levels of psychological well-being [8]. Major variables include sedentariness, alcohol consumption, smoking, nutrition, multimorbidity, availability of urban green spaces, job security, and the length of civil trials. These factors are associated with mental health in different ways. Behavioral factors like smoking and alcohol consumption have strong correlations with psychological well-being [9, 10]. In contrast, socioeconomic factors and access to green spaces are positively associated with psychological well-being [11]. Therefore, the variables provide a multidimensional ESG approach to analyzing territorial determinants of psychological well-being. See to Table 1.

The pattern of mental health in Italian regions has been complex, reflecting overall improvements in mental health accompanied by persisting regional disparities. While overall mental health has increased slightly, the traditional division between the north and the south, defined by economic strength and disadvantage, has persisted [12]. Regional disparities in mental health in Italy have been influenced by outliers, underscoring the importance of local governance and policy interventions, irrespective of macroeconomic factors [7]. Between 2016 and 2023, most regions showed improvements in the Mental Health Index (MHI), with increases ranging from + 0.1 to + 2.0, indicating a 0.5% to 2.5% increase in overall mental health [13]. While the overall increase has been significant in Calabria, other regions that have shown considerable improvements in mental health include Toscana, Marche, Umbria, Valle d’Aosta, and Campania [7, 14]. There have been slight declines in some traditionally higher-performing regions, including Trentino-Alto Adige, Liguria, Friuli-Venezia Giulia, and Abruzzo [15, 16]. While regional disparities in mental health in Italy have continued, the faster rates of improvement in southern and insular regions indicate a pattern of convergence, driven by policy interventions.

Comparative evaluation of clustering algorithms: evidence for the superiority of k-Means

To identify the best clustering technique for analyzing territorial data on mental health issues and ESG factors, six clustering algorithms were comparatively evaluated. These clustering algorithms include Density-Based Clustering, Fuzzy C-Means, Hierarchical Clustering, Model-Based Clustering, Random Forest Clustering, and K-Means. Each clustering algorithm was evaluated using a set of traditional performance indicators that cover both statistical and structural properties of clustering results. Statistical properties used in the clustering algorithm evaluation include the coefficient of determination (R2), Akaike information criterion (AIC), and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). In contrast, structural properties include the Silhouette score, Dunn score, Pearson’s Gamma, and entropy. Past studies have shown that comparative clustering algorithms can be used to analyze ESG indicators and territorial data [17–19]. Among the six clustering algorithms used in this study, the K-Means algorithm showed the best-balanced results across all clustering algorithm evaluation criteria. For example, the K-Means algorithm showed the highest normalized coefficient of determination (R.2) and high scores in the Silhouette and Calinski-Harabasz evaluations. In addition, the low AIC and BIC values indicate that the K-Means algorithm achieves the best balance between clustering quality and complexity [20, 21]. Therefore, the K-Means algorithm yielded the best clustering results when evaluating territorial data on mental health issues and ESG factors. Table 3

The comparative evaluation clearly illustrates the superiority of K-Means Clustering to partition geographical units along mental health and ESG-related indicators. Among the six algorithms experimented with, K-Means consistently achieved first- or second-best ranking for the majority of the statistical, geometric, and structural indices, such as R2, Silhouette measure, Dunn index, and Calinski-Harabasz index. Due to its ability to explain maximum variance while creating compact and well-separated clusters, it is particularly efficient in this multidimensional study [21]. Even when evaluated by indices such as AIC and BIC, which are traditionally penalty-imposing, K-Means was competitive and thus further contributed to its robustness. The six-cluster configuration, having been validated by both criterion-based choice and t-SNE representation, aids the interpretative meaning of spatial mental health patterns. Overall, K-Means is found to emerge as the most balanced and robust algorithm for uncovering hidden structures in complex data, making it the optimal choice for the purposes of this study’s classification and building typology [24].

Predictive modeling of mental health: comparative insights from behavioral and ESG determinants

The growing use of predictive modeling in mental health research highlights the need for robust methods capable of capturing the complex interactions among individual behaviors, clinical conditions, and broader ESG-related factors. Mental health outcomes increasingly reflect the interplay between biological vulnerability, lifestyle habits, and structural contexts [25]. Traditional approaches often fail to represent this complexity, encouraging the use of advanced machine learning and regression techniques such as regularized regression, neural networks, ensemble methods, and K-Nearest Neighbors [26]. Model performance is evaluated through indicators including MSE, RMSE, MAE, MAPE, and R2, which help identify the variables most strongly associated with mental health outcomes [27]. Model performance was assessed using a holdout validation framework. Specifically, 20% of the data was reserved as a test set, while the remaining observations were used for training and validation. Given the limited sample size, no cross-validation procedure was implemented. Performance metrics were computed on held-out data to ensure out-of-sample evaluation and reduce the risk of overfitting.

Integrating ESG frameworks into mental health policy: a national and regional strategy for Italy

The integration of environmental, social, and governance (ESG) factors into the mental health care policy in Italy requires a coordinated national-regional approach to address the existing territorial disparities. The mental health outcomes remain characterized by spatial disparities, with northern regions performing better than central and southern regions. This situation requires a governance strategy that takes a holistic approach to address the behavioral, environmental, and social determinants [41]. At the national level, the policy should aim to improve mental health infrastructure and address disparities in access to mental health care, with a focus on the disadvantaged regions of Campania, Calabria, and Sicily. ESG-aligned financial instruments, such as social and green bonds, can be used to fund the construction of sustainable health care infrastructure, telemedicine, and community mental health services [42]. Employee well-being can also be incorporated into ESG factors to address stress and burnout (Han & Lee, 2024). At the regional level, investments in green areas, pollution control, and sustainable urban planning can help improve the environment and, in turn, promote psychological well-being [43] (See Fig. 3).

Discussion: governance, environment, and lifestyle drivers of mental well-being

The results are consistent with the growing and strengthening consensus in the literature that mental health outcomes are associated not only with individual behaviors but also by structural, environmental, and institutional factors. Given the observational nature of the data, the results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships. More recent studies have progressively emphasized the importance of social determinants of health and the role of governance in explaining regional disparities in psychological well-being. In the Italian scenario, Bruzzi et al. [12] and Landi et al. [44] report a strong positive correlation between health inequalities and regional disparities in institutional capacity, healthcare systems, and public resource allocation. In line with these interpretations, the current study shows that regions with more advanced institutions of governance and healthcare systems report more favorable and stable mental health outcomes. At the same time, more recent studies have progressively emphasized the importance of environmental factors in influencing psychological well-being. The presence of urban green spaces, overall urban quality, and environmental sustainability have been found to have a protective role in mental health, through mechanisms related to stress relief, social interaction, and support for healthy lifestyle behaviors. The current study confirms these findings by reporting a statistically significant positive correlation between the presence of urban green spaces and mental health outcomes. On the other hand, behavioral risk factors such as smoking, lack of physical activity, and multimorbidity are still significantly associated with unfavorable outcomes for mental health, as reported in previous studies. However, it is important to note that territorial health disparities are not fixed. The improvement in health outcomes in disadvantaged areas shows that health interventions and system changes are associated with changes in mental health outcomes, underscoring the need for integrated health intervention policies with governance and environmental approaches. In terms of policy formulation, the study’s findings suggest that mental health policies should not be limited to the healthcare system or to individual behavior change. Instead, policymakers should take a holistic approach that seeks to minimize behavioral risks and maximize environmental quality and system performance. Green infrastructure, equal access to healthcare, and preventive public health policies may have a pivotal role in reducing territorial disparities in mental health outcomes. In policy formulation, the ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) framework can serve as a useful starting point for public health policymakers seeking to integrate public health goals with sustainable development and equity objectives.

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the analysis is based on observational regional data, which does not allow for causal inference but only the identification of statistical associations. Second, the relatively small sample size (Italian regions over time) may limit the robustness and generalizability of the predictive models. Third, the use of aggregated regional indicators may mask within-region heterogeneity and individual-level variability. Finally, although the ESG framework provides a comprehensive perspective, it may not capture all relevant determinants of mental health. Future research could address these limitations by using micro-level data, longitudinal designs, and alternative modeling approaches.

Conclusions

Mental health outcomes in Italy are associated with complex interactions among behavioral, environmental, and governance factors rather than by individual behaviors. While the overall Mental Health Index in Italy has remained fairly stable from 2016 to 2023, there are still large regional gaps, with northern regions performing better than southern regions. The predictive model shows that multimorbidity, smoking, and a sedentary lifestyle are the most important negative predictors of mental health, while the presence of urban green spaces is a protective factor. Cluster analysis shows that the mental health of regions is more closely associated with ESG-related behavioral factors than with geographical factors. Taken together, these results emphasize the need for a holistic policy strategy that combines lifestyle changes with environmental upgrades and enhanced institutional capacity to address territorial gaps in mental health.
