RUNX1‐Driven CPXM1 Transcription Promotes Malignant Phenotypes and EMT in Gastric Cancer Cells
Authors: Ping Jia, Fengming Xu, Tao Zhou, Pingping Wang, Huiping Chen, Jing Zhang, Xiaochen Yuan, Haoxun Xu, Yu Ouyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGastric cancer (GC) remains a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide, with current treatment strategies largely limited to surgery, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy. Although the glycoprotein CPXM1 has been implicated in tumorigenesis, its functional role and underlying molecular mechanisms in GC remain unclear. In this study, we demonstrated that CPXM1 is significantly upregulated in GC tissues, and that elevated CPXM1 expression is associated with an unfavorable prognosis in GC patients. Both in vitro and in vivo functional assays revealed that CPXM1 significantly promotes GC cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. Mechanistically, CPXM1 was found to facilitate epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT), a critical process driving cancer progression. Furthermore, we identified the transcription factor RUNX1 as a direct upstream regulator of CPXM1, which binds to the CPXM1 promoter and transcriptionally activates its expression. Collectively, these findings delineate a previously unrecognized RUNX1/CPXM1/EMT axis that contributes to GC malignant progression, highlighting CPXM1 as a potential diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target.Keywords: CPXM1, EMT, gastric cancer, RUNX1, transcription

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a major cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide, with nearly one million new cases reported in 2022, particularly in Eastern Asia [1]. Although surgical resection combined with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy has improved outcomes, the prognosis for advanced GC remains poor because of recurrence, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Therefore, identifying key molecular drivers of GC progression is critical for developing new diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Carboxypeptidase X, M14 family member 1 (CPXM1) is a collagen‐binding glycoprotein of the M14 metallocarboxypeptidase family [7]. CPXM1 dysregulation has been linked to myelodysplastic syndromes [8], polycystic ovary syndrome [9], and multiple cancers. Its function appears context‐dependent, acting as either a tumor promoter or suppressor depending on tumor type [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. In GC, elevated CPXM1 expression has been associated with poor prognosis and immune infiltration, although its precise role remains unclear [15].

Epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a key process driving cancer metastasis and therapeutic resistance [16]. During EMT, epithelial cells lose polarity and adhesion while acquiring migratory and invasive properties, accompanied by reduced E‐cadherin and increased N‐cadherin and Vimentin expression [17, 18, 19, 20]. Because EMT contributes to metastasis and treatment resistance, identifying upstream EMT regulators may provide novel therapeutic opportunities in GC.

In this study, we found that CPXM1 is significantly upregulated in GC tissues and associated with advanced stage and poor prognosis. Functional experiments demonstrated that CPXM1 promotes GC cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis by inducing EMT. Mechanistically, RUNX1 directly binds to the CPXM1 promoter and transcriptionally activates its expression. Together, these findings identify a novel RUNX1/CPXM1/EMT axis in GC progression and suggest CPXM1 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.

Material and Methods

Data Sources and Bioinformatics Analysis

Publicly available mRNA expression data for GC and matched clinical information were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) via the UCSC Xena platform (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). In addition, three independent Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets—GSE13911, GSE54129, and GSE79973—were retrieved from https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/. Genes with no expression variation among samples were excluded for quality control, and the remaining expression data were normalized via log2(x + 1) transformation.

Differential expression analysis was performed using the R packages limma and DESeq2. Prior to identifying significantly altered genes, batch effects were corrected using the ComBat algorithm. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) > 1 and an adjusted p‐value < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed.

Functional enrichment of the identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was assessed via Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, with significance defined as p < 0.05. Dot plots for significant GO terms were generated using the enrichplot package.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the fgsea package with gene sets obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB; dataset: c5.all.v7.0.entrez.gmt). Genes were ranked according to log2FC, and enrichment scores for each gene set were calculated and visualized using enrichplot.

Bioinformatics Analysis of Public scRNA‐Seq Dataset

Publicly available single‐cell RNA‐seq data from GC tissues were obtained from the GEO database. For the epithelial‐cell analysis, the GSE183904 dataset was used because it contains both primary gastric tumor and normal gastric tissue samples with raw gene‐by‐cell count matrices. Sample annotations were retrieved from the GEO SOFT metadata. Raw count matrices were processed using Python (Scanpy v1.12). Cells were first annotated into major cell lineages according to canonical marker genes. Epithelial cells were identified based on epithelial markers including EPCAM, KRT8, KRT18, KRT19, CDH1, MUC1, and CLDN18, and were further grouped according to tissue origin as tumor epithelial or normal epithelial cells.

For dimensionality reduction and visualization of the epithelial compartment, raw count matrices of epithelial cells were extracted and combined across samples. Genes detected in fewer than 10 cells were removed. Counts were normalized to 10,000 transcripts per cell and log‐transformed using log1p normalization. Highly variable genes were selected, and the data were scaled before principal component analysis. A nearest‐neighbor graph was constructed using the top 30 principal components, followed by UMAP visualization and Leiden clustering. CPXM1 expression was visualized on the UMAP embedding and compared between tumor epithelial and normal epithelial cells. Statistical comparison of CPXM1 expression between the two groups was performed using a two‐sided Mann–Whitney U test.

For pathway analysis, tumor epithelial cells from GSE183904 were stratified into CPXM1‐positive and CPXM1‐zero groups. Differential expression ranking was performed using log‐normalized expression values, and GSEA was conducted using preranked gene lists against MSigDB Hallmark and KEGG gene sets. KEGG over‐representation analysis was additionally performed using genes upregulated in CPXM1‐positive tumor epithelial cells.

Patients and Tissue Samples

From 2018 to 2020, a total of 10 paired human GC and adjacent non‐tumor tissues were collected at Taizhou Second People's Hospital. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Taizhou Second People's Hospital, and all participants provided written informed consents in advance.

Animal Experiments

Four‐week‐old BALB/c nude mice were purchased from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China). For the subcutaneous tumor model, mice were injected subcutaneously with 1 × 106 transfected GC cells. Tumor length and width were measured weekly, and the tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume = (width2 × length)/2. After 4 weeks, mice were sacrificed, and tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed.

For the in vivo metastasis model, transfected luciferase‐labeled GC cells were injected into the tail vein of BALB/c nude mice. Four weeks later, the mice were intraperitoneally injected with D‐luciferin sodium salt (Genomeditech, China). Then, an IVIS imaging system (Caliper Life Sciences, USA) was employed to detect the metastasis lesions. Subsequently, the lungs were harvested for photography and H&E staining.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM, SPSS, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between two groups were assessed using the Student's t‐test. The correlation between CPXM1 and RUNX1 expression levels was evaluated using Pearson's correlation analysis. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and survival differences were compared with the log‐rank test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001).

Results

CPXM1 Expression Is Upregulated in Gastric Cancer and Predicts Poor Prognosis

Through an integrated analysis of publicly available gastric cancer (GC) datasets from the GEO database (GSE13911, GSE54129, and GSE79973) and TCGA, we identified 11 consistently upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Intersection with prognostic data from The Human Protein Atlas database further refined this set to nine genes significantly associated with poor prognosis (ADAM12, APOC1, BGN, COL11A1, CPXM1, CST1, CTHRC1, FNDC1, and SULF1) (Figure 1A,B). Among these genes, CPXM1 remains relatively understudied in GC. Available evidence indicates that CPXM1 expression is associated with poor prognosis and correlates with immune and stromal cell infiltration, suggesting a potential role in GC pathogenesis [15]. Pan‐cancer analysis revealed that CPXM1 expression is significantly elevated in many malignancies (Figure S1), including multiple gastrointestinal malignancies, such as esophageal carcinoma, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, colon adenocarcinoma, and rectal adenocarcinoma (Figure 1C), supporting its putative oncogenic function. Consistently, CPXM1 expression was significantly elevated in the GEO datasets GSE13911, GSE54129, and GSE79973 (Figure 1D).

To validate these findings from public databases, we examined CPXM1 expression in our institutional cohort of GC patient samples using qPCR. The results confirmed that CPXM1 mRNA levels were significantly higher in tumor tissues compared to matched non‐tumor tissues (Figure 1E). Similarly, CPXM1 expression was markedly elevated in multiple GC cell lines relative to the normal gastric epithelial cell line GES‐1 (Figure 1F). Western blot analysis further confirmed the upregulation of CPXM1 protein in GC tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues (Figure 1G,H).

Further analysis revealed that CPXM1 expression increased significantly with advancing GC clinical stage, suggesting its role in promoting malignant progression (Figure 1I). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis of the TCGA‐STAD dataset indicated that CPXM1 has strong diagnostic potential for GC (Figure 1J). Furthermore, survival analysis demonstrated that high CPXM1 expression was significantly associated with poorer overall survival (OS), progression‐free survival (PFS), disease‐free survival (DFS), and recurrence‐free survival (RFS) in patients with GC as well as in several other cancer types (Figure 1K and Figure S2).

Collectively, these findings indicate that CPXM1 is significantly upregulated in GC and may serve as a valuable biomarker for both diagnosis and prognosis. Based on this clinically relevant expression pattern, we next investigated whether CPXM1 plays a functional role in regulating malignant phenotypes of GC cells.

Single‐Cell RNA‐Seq Analysis Reveals Increased CPXM1 Expression in Tumor Epithelial Cells

To further clarify the cellular distribution and functional relevance of CPXM1 in GC tissues, we analyzed the GSE183904 single‐cell RNA‐seq dataset. Major cell populations were annotated using canonical marker genes, and epithelial cells were identified based on established epithelial markers. We then focused on the epithelial compartment and compared CPXM1 expression between tumor‐derived epithelial cells and adjacent normal epithelial cells.

UMAP visualization showed the distribution of epithelial cells from tumor and adjacent normal gastric tissues. CPXM1 expression was detectable in a subset of epithelial cells and was more frequently observed in tumor epithelial cells than in adjacent normal epithelial cells. Quantitatively, tumor epithelial cells showed significantly higher CPXM1 expression than normal epithelial cells (mean log‐normalized expression: 0.0079 vs. 0.0045; Mann–Whitney U test, p = 1.39 × 10−6; Figure S3A–F). The fraction of CPXM1‐positive epithelial cells was also higher in tumor epithelial cells than in normal epithelial cells.

These results suggest that CPXM1 expression within the epithelial compartment is associated with tumor‐derived epithelial cells in GC. To further evaluate whether CPXM1 can be secreted by GC cells, we compared CPXM1 protein levels in the supernatants of GC cell lines and normal gastric epithelial cell lines by Western blot (Figure S3G). Although GC cells were not the predominant source of CPXM1, they remained capable of secreting CPXM1 into the extracellular environment. Together with our functional assays, these single‐cell data support the involvement of CPXM1 in malignant epithelial states and GC progression.

CPXM1 Drives GC Cell Proliferation, Invasion and Metastasis In Vitro

To determine whether the elevated CPXM1 expression observed in GC contributes functionally to tumor progression, we first established stable CPXM1‐knockdown and CPXM1‐overexpressing HGC27 and AGS cell lines using lentiviral vectors. The transfection efficiency was confirmed by both RT‐qPCR and Western blot analysis (Figure 2A,B). Functional assays revealed that CPXM1 significantly promotes the malignant phenotype of GC cells in vitro. CCK‐8 and colony formation assays demonstrated that CPXM1 overexpression enhanced cell proliferation and clonogenic capacity, whereas CPXM1 knockdown yielded the opposite effect (Figure 2C–E). The impact of CPXM1 on metastatic potential was assessed using wound healing (Figure 2F,G) and Transwell assays (Figure 2H–J). The results showed that CPXM1 overexpression markedly enhanced migratory and invasive capacities of GC cells, as evidenced by accelerated wound closure and an increased number of cells traversing the matrigel‐coated or uncoated membranes. In contrast, CPXM1 depletion potently suppressed these aggressive behaviors.

(H–J) Transwell assays were used to examine cell migration and invasion. Scale bar: 100 μm. Data are presented as mean ± SD. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Collectively, these data indicate that CPXM1 contributes to multiple malignant phenotypes in GC cells. Notably, although CPXM1 modulation affected proliferative and clonogenic capacities, its effects on migratory and invasive behaviors appeared more pronounced, suggesting that CPXM1 may preferentially promote an EMT‐associated aggressive phenotype while also supporting tumor cell growth. Because in vitro assays cannot fully recapitulate tumor growth and metastatic colonization in a physiological context, we next evaluated the role of CPXM1 in vivo using xenograft and experimental metastasis models.

CPXM1 Promotes Tumor Growth and Metastasis In Vivo

To validate the pro‐tumorigenic role of CPXM1 observed in vitro, we conducted a series of in vivo experiments using nude mouse models. In the subcutaneous xenograft model, GC cells with CPXM1 knockdown formed significantly smaller and lighter tumors compared to the control group (Figure 3A,B). Tumor growth curves, generated from regular measurements of tumor volumes, indicated that CPXM1 knockdown markedly suppressed in vivo tumor growth (Figure 3C). Consistently, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for the proliferation marker Ki67 revealed a significantly lower proportion of Ki67‐positive cells in the CPXM1‐knockdown group, indicating reduced tumor cell proliferation (Figure 3D,E).

Tumor volume and weight were measured. (D, E) Ki‐67 expression in xenograft tissues was analyzed by IHC and quantified. Scale bar: 20 μm. (F–I) Bioluminescence imaging was used to evaluate lung metastasis after tail vein injection of tumor cells. Representative lung images and quantification are shown. White arrows indicate liver metastases; black arrows indicate lung metastases. Scale bar: 2.5 mm. (J) Quantification of metastatic lung nodules. (K, L) CPXM1 expression in metastatic lung tissues was detected by qRT‐PCR and Western blotting. Data are presented as mean ± SD; *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

We further investigated the role of CPXM1 in tumor metastasis using an experimental lung metastasis model via tail vein injection. In vivo bioluminescent imaging revealed that CPXM1 knockdown significantly reduced the bioluminescent signals corresponding to metastatic tumor burden in the lungs (Figure 3F,I). These observations were confirmed by histopathological analysis; as hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of lung tissues demonstrated a substantial decrease in the number of metastatic nodules in the CPXM1‐knockdown group compared to the controls (Figure 3G,H,J).

To confirm that CPXM1 knockdown was maintained in lung metastatic lesions, we microdissected visible metastatic nodules from mouse lung tissues and examined CPXM1 expression by qRT‐PCR and Western blot (Figure 3K,L). Both analyses showed that CPXM1 expression was markedly reduced in metastatic lesions from the shCPXM1 group compared with the control group, supporting effective CPXM1 knockdown in the in vivo metastasis model.

In summary, our in vivo findings demonstrate that CPXM1 supports tumor growth and markedly enhances metastatic colonization in GC models. Together with the in vitro data, these results suggest that CPXM1 functions as a malignancy‐promoting factor, with a particularly prominent role in invasion and metastasis. Given the prominent effect of CPXM1 on migratory, invasive, and metastatic phenotypes, we next explored whether CPXM1 regulates molecular programs associated with tumor dissemination.

CPXM1 Promotes the EMT Phenotype of GC Cells

Having established that CPXM1 promotes GC cell aggressiveness, particularly migration, invasion, and metastasis, we next aimed to elucidate the molecular programs underlying these phenotypes. We first performed bioinformatic analysis on the TCGA STAD cohort. Patients were stratified into high and low CPXM1 expression groups, and DEGs between the two groups were identified. Subsequent Gene Ontology (GO) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed that these DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways associated with epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT), including REGULATION OF CELL ADHESION and EPITHELIAL TO MESENCHYMAL TRANSITION (Figure 4A–C and Figure S4A). As shown in Figure 4D and S5A, several other oncogenic pathways were also enriched, including the canonical WNT signaling pathway, transforming growth factor beta receptor signaling pathway, regulation of MAPK cascade, and ERK1 and ERK2 cascade. These findings suggest that CPXM1 may be associated with extracellular adhesion‐related signaling and downstream EMT‐regulatory pathways, including WNT, TGF‐β, MAPK, and ERK signaling, all of which have been reported to directly or indirectly participate in EMT regulation [21, 22]. To determine whether the EMT‐related programs observed in bulk transcriptomic analysis could also be detected at the tumor epithelial cell level, we further analyzed CPXM1‐expressing tumor epithelial cells using scRNA‐seq data. Tumor epithelial cells were stratified into CPXM1‐positive and CPXM1‐zero groups. Gene set enrichment analysis showed that CPXM1‐positive tumor epithelial cells were significantly enriched for epithelial‐mesenchymal transition and other malignant phenotype‐associated programs, including TNF‐alpha/NF‐kB signaling and hypoxia (Figure S5B). These scRNA‐seq findings complement the bulk transcriptomic analysis and support the association between CPXM1 expression and EMT‐like malignant states in GC tumor epithelial cells. EMT is a well‐established driver of tumor invasion and metastasis in GC [23, 24, 25]. Currently, no studies have reported the role of CPXM1 in modulating the EMT phenotype. Based on the pathway enrichment results, we next investigated whether CPXM1 regulates EMT to exert its pro‐tumorigenic effects.

(B–D) GO enrichment analyses revealed significant enrichment of EMT‐related and other signaling pathways. (E) Western blot analysis showed that CPXM1 knockdown altered EMT marker expression in GC cells.

To definitively establish a causal relationship, we examined the expression of key EMT markers following CPXM1 modulation. Western blot analysis demonstrated that CPXM1 knockdown led to downregulation of EMT‐related transcription factors Snail, Slug, Twist, accompanied by a significant upregulation of the epithelial marker E‐cadherin and decreased expression of mesenchymal markers, including N‐cadherin and Vimentin (Figure 4E).

Collectively, these findings provide strong evidence that CPXM1 promotes the malignant progression of GC by activating the EMT program. Although these findings indicated that CPXM1 promotes EMT‐associated malignant progression, the mechanism responsible for CPXM1 upregulation in GC remained unclear. We therefore investigated the upstream transcriptional regulators that may drive CPXM1 overexpression.

RUNX1 Acts as a Direct Transcriptional Activator of CPXM1

To further elucidate the upstream regulatory mechanisms driving CPXM1 overexpression in GC, we focused on identifying its transcriptional regulators, as dysregulation of transcription factors (TFs) is a common mechanism of oncogene activation [26, 27]. Using the JASPAR database [28], we predicted 586 potential TFs for CPXM1. By intersecting this list with TCGA‐STAD DEGs, genes positively correlated with CPXM1 expression, and a curated list of known TFs, we identified four candidate TFs: ETS1, RUNX1, SNAI1, and ZNF281 (Figure 5A). Among these, RUNX1 was selected for further investigation because it showed the strongest positive correlation with CPXM1 expression in clinical GC tumor tissues (R = 0.29, p < 0.05) (Figure 5B), its significant upregulation in GC tissues (Figure 5C), its association with poor OS, PFS, PPS in GC patients (Figure 5D,E), and the lack of prior reports linking it to the CPXM1 transcriptional regulation in GC.

(C) CPXM1 expression in TCGA‐STAD tissues. (D, E) Kaplan–Meier analysis of OS, FPS, and PPS according to CPXM1 expression. (F, G) RT‐qPCR and Western blot analyses of CPXM1 expression after RUNX1 knockdown or overexpression in HGC27 and AGS cells. (H–K) ChIP‐qPCR and JASPAR analyses identified RUNX1 binding sites within the CPXM1 promoter, with enrichment detected at the −800 to −600 bp region. (L) Schematic of wild‐type and mutant CPXM1 promoter luciferase reporters. (M) Dual‐luciferase assays confirmed RUNX1‐mediated activation of the CPXM1 promoter. Modulation of RUNX1 expression in GC cell lines via knockdown or overexpression led to corresponding changes in CPXM1 mRNA and protein levels, confirming its regulatory role (Figure 5F,G). To further verify the transcriptional regulation of CPXM1 by RUNX1, we conducted chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) followed by qPCR. Immunoprecipitation with an anti‐RUNX1 antibody specifically enriched DNA fragments encompassing the CPXM1 promoter, as detected by subsequent qPCR, providing direct evidence that RUNX1 binds to the CPXM1 promoter in cells (Figure 5H). To elucidate the precise binding site, we employed JASPAR to predict a potential RUNX1‐binding motif located at positions −729 to −719 bp upstream of the CPXM1 transcription start site (TSS) (Figure 5I). ChIP‐qPCR using primers spanning ten 200‐bp fragments across the 2 kb promoter region indeed demonstrated significant enrichment specifically at the −800 to −600 bp fragment, which includes the predicted binding site, compared with the IgG control (Figure 5J,K).

To functionally validate that this binding directly transactivates the CPXM1 promoter, we performed a dual‐luciferase reporter assay. A fragment of the CPXM1 promoter containing the predicted RUNX1‐binding site was cloned upstream of the firefly luciferase gene in the pGL3‐Basic vector. A mutant reporter construct, in which the core RUNX1‐binding motif was disrupted by site‐directed mutagenesis, was also generated (Figure 5L). These constructs were co‐transfected into GC cells together with a RUNX1 overexpression plasmid or an empty vector control, along with a renilla luciferase plasmid for normalization. Luciferase activity measurement revealed that RUNX1 overexpression significantly increased the activity of the reporter containing the wild‐type CPXM1 promoter, whereas this transcriptional activation was abrogated upon mutation of the RUNX1‐binding site (Figure 5M).

In summary, our findings establish RUNX1 as a direct transcriptional activator of CPXM1, binding to a specific promoter element located at −729 to −719 bp to drive its overexpression in GC, thereby uncovering a critical regulatory layer within the RUNX1/CPXM1/EMT oncogenic axis. Having established RUNX1 as a direct transcriptional activator of CPXM1, we next asked whether CPXM1 functionally mediates the pro‐malignant effects of RUNX1 in GC cells.

The RUNX1/CPXM1 Axis Governs EMT and Malignant Progression of GC Cells

To determine whether RUNX1 promotes GC malignancy through CPXM1‐dependent mechanisms, we performed a series of rescue experiments in HGC27 and AGS cells. Four experimental groups were established, including control cells, CPXM1 knockdown (shCPXM1) cells, RUNX1 overexpression (ov‐RUNX1) cells, and cells with concurrent CPXM1 knockdown and RUNX1 overexpression (shCPXM1 + ov‐RUNX1).

Functional assays demonstrated that CPXM1 knockdown significantly suppressed GC cell proliferation and migratory/invasive capacities compared to the control cells. Crucially, these inhibitory effects were substantially alleviated by concomitant RUNX1 overexpression. Specifically, the proliferation defect and reduced colony‐forming ability induced by CPXM1 silencing were partially restored in the rescue group (Figure 6A–C). Similarly, the impaired migratory and invasive phenotypes observed upon CPXM1 knockdown were largely reversed by ectopic RUNX1 expression (Figure 6D,E).

The RUNX1/CPXM1 axis promotes EMT and malignant progression in GC cells. (A–C) CCK‐8 and colony formation assays showed that the RUNX1/CPXM1 axis enhanced GC cell proliferation. (D, E) Transwell assays demonstrated increased migration and invasion. Scale bar: 200 μm. (F) Western blot analysis confirmed that the RUNX1/CPXM1 axis regulates EMT in HGC27 and AGS cells. Data are presented as mean ± SD; *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

At the molecular level, Western blot analysis of EMT‐related markers revealed a consistent pattern. CPXM1 knockdown alone resulted in upregulation of the epithelial marker E‐cadherin and downregulation of the mesenchymal markers N‐cadherin and Vimentin, accompanied by reduced expression of the key EMT‐associated transcription factors Snail, Slug, and Twist. Notably, this shift toward an epithelial phenotype was reversed upon RUNX1 overexpression in CPXM1‐knockdown cells, restoring a mesenchymal‐like protein expression profile (Figure 6F).

Collectively, these results indicate that the pro‐tumorigenic effects of RUNX1 in GC are mediated predominantly through its transcriptional target CPXM1. The RUNX1/CPXM1 axis drives malignant progression by activating the EMT program in GC cells.

The RUNX1/CPXM1 Axis Drives GC Progression In Vivo

To validate the tumor‐promoting function of the RUNX1/CPXM1 axis in a physiological context, we performed xenograft experiments in nude mice using stable HGC27 cells with distinct genetic modifications, including control (shCtrl), CPXM1 knockdown (shCPXM1), RUNX1 overexpression (ov‐RUNX1), and combined CPXM1 knockdown with RUNX1 overexpression (shCPXM1 + ov‐RUNX1).

In the subcutaneous tumor model, tumors formed from shCPXM1 cells exhibited significantly reduced growth rates and lower final tumor weights compared with controls. Conversely, ov‐RUNX1 tumors exhibited accelerated growth. Critically, the growth inhibition induced by CPXM1 knockdown was substantially rescued by concomitant RUNX1 overexpression (Figure 7A–C).

The RUNX1/CPXM1 axis promotes tumor growth and metastasis in vivo. (A–C) Subcutaneous xenograft assays were performed using stable HGC27 cell lines with CPXM1 knockdown and/or RUNX1 overexpression. Tumor weight and growth curves are shown. (D, E) In vivo bioluminescence imaging evaluated experimental metastasis after tail vein injection of luciferase‐labeled cells. (F, G) CPXM1 expression in metastatic lung tissues was analyzed by qRT‐PCR and Western blotting. Data are presented as mean ± SD. One‐way ANOVA was used for statistical analysis; **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

In the experimental metastasis model, tail vein injection of shCPXM1 cells resulted in a remarkable reduction in lung metastatic burden, as assessed by in vivo bioluminescent imaging. Notably, this suppression of metastasis was largely reversed upon RUNX1 overexpression in the CPXM1‐knockdown background (Figure 7D,E).

To further verify the genetic manipulations in the in vivo rescue model, we also microdissected visible lung metastatic nodules from the mice shown in Figure 7D and examined CPXM1 expression by qRT‐PCR and Western blot. CPXM1 expression was markedly reduced in the shCPXM1 group compared with the control group, whereas RUNX1 overexpression increased CPXM1 expression. In the shCPXM1 + ov‐RUNX1 group, CPXM1 expression was partially restored compared with the shCPXM1 group (Figure 7F,G). These results confirmed that the RUNX1/CPXM1 axis was effectively modulated in the lung metastatic lesions used for the in vivo rescue experiments.

Taken together, these in vivo findings conclusively demonstrate that RUNX1 promotes GC growth and metastasis predominantly through its downstream effector CPXM1, underscoring the critical role of the RUNX1/CPXM1 signaling axis in driving malignant progression.

Discussion

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a major cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide [1], and the prognosis of patients with advanced disease is still unsatisfactory largely due to metastasis and therapeutic resistance, despite progress in surgery, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy [28]. The marked heterogeneity of GC limits therapeutic efficacy and highlights the need to identify novel molecular drivers. In this study, we focused on Carboxypeptidase X, M14 family member 1 (CPXM1), a molecule with biological complexity, acting as a tumor‐promoter or suppressor depending on the malignancy [29]. Although CPXM1 has been associated with poor prognosis in GC [15], its expression pattern, biological role, and regulatory mechanism have not been fully clarified.

Our data demonstrated that CPXM1 is significantly upregulated in GC tissues and that high CPXM1 expression is associated with advanced tumor stage and unfavorable survival. Importantly, CPXM1 should not be regarded as a strictly tumor‐cell‐specific marker. As a secreted collagen‐binding glycoprotein, CPXM1 may reflect both cancer‐cell‐intrinsic and microenvironmental components. Consistently, our single‐cell RNA‐seq analysis showed that CPXM1 was detectable in tumor epithelial cells but was also expressed in stromal fibroblast‐like populations, suggesting that stromal CPXM1 may also participate in remodeling the tumor microenvironment. Nevertheless, CPXM1‐positive tumor epithelial cells exhibited enrichment of EMT, matrix remodeling, focal adhesion, and malignant phenotype‐associated transcriptional programs. Together with our in vitro and in vivo experiments, these findings support the view that CPXM1 is a tumor‐associated extracellular factor contributing to GC aggressiveness through both cancer‐cell‐intrinsic and microenvironment‐related mechanisms.

Functionally, CPXM1 promoted GC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and metastasis, with the most prominent effects observed in EMT‐associated phenotypes. The changes in proliferation and colony formation were relatively moderate, whereas alterations in migration, invasion, metastatic burden, and EMT marker expression were more pronounced. This suggests that CPXM1 may primarily function as an EMT‐associated driver of invasive progression rather than as a classical proliferation‐specific oncogene. Nevertheless, EMT‐enhanced cellular plasticity and survival during dissemination may indirectly contribute to tumor expansion in vivo. Given its extracellular localization, CPXM1 may initiate intracellular EMT programs by remodeling the extracellular matrix or modulating tumor cell‐matrix interactions. Although the precise receptor remains unknown, enrichment analyses indicated that high CPXM1 expression was associated with cell adhesion, WNT, TGF‐β, and MAPK/ERK‐related pathways, supporting a potential outside‐in signaling model where CPXM1 transmits microenvironmental cues into GC cells.

We further identified RUNT‐related transcription factor 1 (RUNX1) as a direct upstream regulator of CPXM1. Although previous reports on RUNX1 in GC are controversial, ranging from EMT promotion to tumor suppression [29, 30, 31], our ChIP and dual‐luciferase reporter assays provided direct mechanistic evidence that RUNX1 binds to the CPXM1 promoter at the −729 to −719 bp region and activates CPXM1 transcription; mutation of this motif abolished transactivation. Collectively, supported by functional rescue experiments, our study defines a novel RUNX1/CPXM1/EMT axis in GC (Figure 8). These findings deepen the understanding of GC metastasis and suggest that targeting this axis may serve to suppress metastasis and overcome therapy resistance. Future studies should identify CPXM1‐interacting receptors and evaluate small‐molecule inhibitors or biologics targeting this axis, either as standalone treatments or in combination with existing therapies.

RUNX1 transcriptionally activates CPXM1, promotes EMT, and enhances the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of gastric cancer cells.
