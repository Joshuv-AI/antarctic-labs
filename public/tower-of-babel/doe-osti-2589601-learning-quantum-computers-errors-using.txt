ABSTRACT
Learning and reducing the errors and noise in quantum computing systems is necessary for achieving
quantum computation’s promise. However, rapid advances in experimental quantum computing
are making this task increasingly difficult, because state-of-the-art systems now contain hundreds
of qubits and many characterization techniques are hard to apply at this scale. Furthermore,
complex kinds of errors in these systems, such as crosstalk and non-Markovian effects, must be
understood and decreased, but these errors are challenging to study with most existing methods. In
this project, we explored using neural networks for scalable characterization of complex errors in
quantum computers. We proposed and demonstrated characterizing a quantum computer’s errors
with neural networks that have interpretable parameters corresponding to the rates of different kinds
of errors, within a sparse Lindbladian parameterization for errors. To enable scaling to many qubit
systems, these networks then predict how these errors combine within quantum circuits and impact
their outcomes using an efficient approximations. We demonstrated these networks ability to learn
coherent crosstalk errors and context-dependent errors in a simulated 4-qubit system.

INTRODUCTION AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF RESULTS

Recent rapid improvements to quantum computing hardware [11, 22, 1, 3, 29, 7] have been enabled
by techniques for learning about the errors that limit their performance [15]. However, new error
learning techniques that can be applied to hundreds or thousands of qubits will be necessary to
continue this pace of progress. The most widely-used approaches to in situ error learning are based
on tomography [15, 31, 30]. In tomographic methods, such as process tomography [15] and gate set
tomography (GST) [15, 31, 30], matrices with a priori unknown elements represent each imperfect
operation (e.g., gates) and these unknown parameters are fit to data, typically using conventional
statistical methods. Although the foundational tomography methods are exponentially costly in
the number of qubits (𝑛) [15, 31], methods based on reductions in the size of tomography’s model
[15, 39, 18, 12, 14, 13] have enabled studying errors in systems with tens to over a hundred qubits
[22, 13, 14, 12]. However, scaling characterization techniques to many-qubit systems has so far
relied on models that exclude important kinds of errors. Most notably, sparse Pauli noise learning
methods have been used to study many-qubit systems [15, 39, 18, 12, 14, 13]. These methods
rely on randomization techniques that make all errors appear Pauli stochastic [24, 41, 16, 17] and
so they cannot learn the rates of any other kinds of errors, including coherent errors—which are
ubiquitous in contemporary systems [30, 33].

An intriguing alternative route to studying errors in large-scale quantum computers is to use
(classical) machine learning (ML) methods, which can potentially learn the structures in a system’s
errors from data rather than assuming them a priori. ML methods have been designed and applied
to a variety of tasks in quantum science—including control [32, 4, 9], compilation [28], state
tomography [38, 37, 6], and many-body problems [5]. Furthermore, artificial neural networks have
been shown to be a promising method for scalable predictions of a quantum computer’s failure
rates (i.e., fidelities) on quantum circuits [21, 8, 20, 19], a problem known as quantum capability
learning. However, understanding a system’s errors means having a description of the kinds and
magnitudes of the errors in that system that is (i) understandable by quantum computing scientists
and engineers and (ii) relatable to that system’s physical processes, so that their cause can be
assessed (and, ideally, the errors reduced). Black-box neural networks for quantum capability
learning have shown promise, but do not fulfill this need.

In this work we present an approach to learning a quantum computer’s errors using quantum
physics aware neural networks (QPANNs) [20]. QPANNs are neural networks that were recently
designed for quantum capability learning and that contain an internal, interpretable representative of
a quantum computer’s errors in terms of sparse Lindbladians [2, 27, 30, 25]. We adapt QPANNs to
the task of scalable simulation of noisy Clifford circuits (rather than only circuit fidelity prediction)
and show how to extract the rates of different kinds of errors from a trained QPANN. Our QPANNs
are able to learn certain kinds of non-Markovian errors, as well as complex kinds of Markovian
errors, such as crosstalk [35, 30, 33, 13, 14, 12, 34], and we demonstrate this using simulated
data. In particular, we show how QPANNs can learn crosstalk (and discuss how they can learn
non-Markovian errors) using simulated data. Our results are for a 4-qubit system, but all aspects
of the QPANN architecture and workflow (e.g., data processing) are scalable. Our results therefore
show that QPANNs provide a potential route to efficient characterization of many-qubit systems
even in the presence of complex and poorly-understood errors and noise.

DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT AND
METHODOLOGY

QPANNS [20] are neural networks that combine general neural network methods together with
efficient approximations to the theory of how errors occur and combine within quantum circuits
containing only Clifford gates [27]. The internal structures of QPANNs use the sparse 𝑛-qubit
Lindbladian representation of errors in quantum computers [2, 27, 30]. In this representation, the
error in a layer of gates is modelled by an error channel E = exp( 𝜖® · 𝐺) ® occurring after the layer’s
intended unitary, where 𝐺® = (𝐺 1 , . . . , 𝐺 𝐾 ) is a vector of 𝐾 = poly(𝑛) elementary errors and 𝜖® are
the corresponding rates of those errors. The elementary errors used within a QPANN is one its
hyperparameters, and the choice (which could be automated using hyperparameter tuning) impacts
what physical effects the created QPANN can accurately model.

A QPANN’s elementary errors are chosen from a basis (G) for 𝑛-qubit Lindbladians (a O (16𝑛 )-
dimensional space) that divides any Lindbladian into 4𝑛 − 1 Hamiltonian 𝐻 𝑃 and 4𝑛 − 1 Pauli
stochastic 𝑆 𝑃 components each indexed by a non-identity 𝑛-qubit Pauli operator 𝑃 (and defined
by 𝐻 𝑃 [𝜌] = −𝑖[𝑃, 𝜌] and 𝑆 𝑃 [𝜌] = 𝑃𝜌𝑃 − 𝜌), as well as two other kinds of errors (called Pauli
correlation and active errors) that are not used in this work [2]. This basis has three properties that
are important in our context: (i) sparsity—error processes in real quantum computers are expected
to be sparse in this basis [2, 27]; (ii) efficiency—the approximate effect on circuit outcomes of
errors that are sparse in this basis can be computed efficiently [27]; and (iii) interpretability—
these rates of errors in this basis are physically interpretable [2, 30], i.e., many of these errors are
familiar to quantum physicists (e.g., gate over- or under-rotation errors) and are directly attributable,
by quantum computing experimentalists and engineers, to particular physical imperfections (e.g.,
imperfectly calibrated gate pulses).

A QPANN models a noisy quantum computer by predicting the outcomes when some quantum
circuit is run on that system. A QPANN is constructed from two sequential components. The first
part of a QPANN (N1 ) is a neural network that takes a circuit 𝑐 (represented as a tensor [20, 21])
and predicts the rates of each error 𝐺® in 𝑐 versus time—or, more precisely, circuit layer index. That

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

We now demonstrate QPANNs and explore their accuracy using simulated data with crosstalk errors.
Crosstalk errors [35] are common in contemporary quantum computers [30, 33, 13, 14, 12, 34] and
are difficult to characterize with existing techniques, particularly if they are coherent. A layer of
gates contains crosstalk errors if there are errors that interact two or more qubits which should not
be interacting and/or when the errors that occur on a qubit depend on what gate is being applied to
other qubits [35]. These errors are difficult to characterize because cannot be accurately learned
by studying individual qubits or pairs of qubits in isolation, so they cannot easily be learned with
unscalable characterization techniques. Pauli noise learning methods have been used to study
crosstalk in many-qubit systems [15, 39, 18, 12, 14, 13], but that is only possible with the aid of
Pauli randomization methods that make coherent errors appear Pauli stochastic [24, 41, 16, 17],
and they therefore cannot distinguish between crosstalk errors of different kinds.

We applied QPANNs to learn coherent crosstalk errors in a hypothetical 4-qubit system. We
considered a system with three gates: 𝜋/2 rotations around the 𝑋 and 𝑌 axes of each qubit, and
controlled-𝑍 (𝐶 𝑍) gates between adjacent qubits in a ring. We simulated this system for an error
model in which each gate experiences stochastic and Hamiltonian errors that commute with the
gate’s unitary (coherent and stochastic over- and under-rotation errors for the 𝑋 and 𝑌 rotations—
i.e., 𝐻 𝑋 or 𝐻𝑌 and 𝑆 𝑋 or 𝑆𝑌 —and 𝐻 𝐼 𝑍 , 𝐻 𝐼 𝑍 , 𝐻 𝑍 𝑍 , 𝑆 𝐼 𝑍 , 𝑆 𝑍 𝐼 , and 𝑆 𝑍 𝑍 for the 𝐶 𝑍 gates). In addition
to these local (i.e., non-crosstalk) errors, each single-qubit gate causes Hamiltonian errors on its
two neighbouring qubits (an effect that can be caused by gate pulse spillover) and each 𝐶 𝑍 gate
causes a 4-qubit 𝐻 𝑍 𝑍 𝑍 𝑍 error.

ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS

The outcomes and impacts of this projects include a proof-of-principle demonstration that QPANNs
can learn errors in quantum computers, and a proof-of-principle implementation of QPANNs in
open-source software (available at https://github.com/sandialabs/pyGSTi). This work will continue
in a 3-year LDRD, that will develop QPANNs from a small proof-of-principle into reliable digital
twins for quantum computers. The results presented in this report will form the basis of an article
for submission to a peer-reviewed journal. Finally, we anticipate that QPANNs could become a
powerful tool for understanding and improving state-of-the-art quantum computing hardware, and
that after further development in a full LDRD project that they will be ready for deployment, to
support the development of quantum computing technologies.

CONCLUSION

In this work, we have proposed using QPANNs to characterizing complex errors in large-scale
quantum computers, and we have demonstrated the promise of this idea. We demonstrated that
QPANNs can accurately learn coherent crosstalk errors—including height-weight errors that impact
many qubits—and that they outperform even an idealized version of localized tomography, which is
the conventional approach to characterizing errors in many-qubit systems using unscalable methods.
These simulations focused on what are called intrinsic errors, where each error is unambiguously
assignable to a single gate, and where all errors where small. These choices enabled us to
sidestep the subtle issue of gauge freedom in error models for quantum computers [31] and to
use a first-order approximation, respectively. The natural approach to addressing gauge freedom
in the context of QPANNs is to use a first-order gauge invariant (FOGI) parameterization of
sparse 𝑛-qubit Lindbladians [26, 30]. However, such parameterizations remove some of the spatial
locality used in the QPANN’s structure, so it is an open questions as to how best to integrate FOGI
parameterizations and QPANNs. To enable QPANNs to accurately learn in the presence of larger
errors—or to obtain very high-accuracy models—we expect that we will need to go beyond the
first-order approximation used within the second half of the QPANN. However, higher-order (e.g.,
2nd order) and efficient approximations exist [27], and could be integrated into a QPANN.
The QPANN architecture is scalable, but the QPANNs that we demonstrated in this work are not
scalable because they predict the probabilities of all 2𝑛 outcomes of a quantum circuit. This is
straight forward to address in principle, by instead predicting only a polynomially-sized subset of
all 𝑛-bit strings—e.g., only those that were observed in an experiment need be predicted to compute
some loss functions, like the likelihood—or a polynomial number of Pauli observables. This is,

 12

however, a little complex to implement in practice and remains for future work. Another interesting
question pertaining to scaling QPANNs is the amount of training data that will be required. We
conjecture that the amount of data required to train QPANNs will grow only slowly (and perhaps
not all) with system size, because 𝑛 bits of information are obtained from each circuit execution
and many physically well-motivated choices for 𝐺® [2] and the other hyperparameters of a QPANN
result in O (𝑛) growth in the number of model parameters. However, testing this conjecture, with
both simulated and experimental data, is needed.
QPANNs can learn some kinds of non-Markovian error: the QPANN architecture is designed to
enable the learning of any kind of non-Markovianity in which the rates of errors in the sparse
Lindbladian representation depend on some measurable classical context variable. This is because
any such variable can be included within the input of a QPANN. However, not all non-Markovian
errors can be represented in this way, such as leakage outside of the computational space. More
significant structural changes to QPANNs (e.g., extensions beyond the sparse 𝑛-qubit Lindbladian
parameterization of errors) may be needed to enable QPANNs to accurately model all the kinds of
non-Markovianity that are significant in experimental quantum computing systems.

we will only have partial and perhaps inaccurate a priori information about the elementary errors
that occur in that system. In such a scenario, we anticipate that tuning of the set 𝐺® will be necessary
to obtain a QPANN that accurately models the system. We do not explore doing this herein.

The QPANN is trained on the data for the training circuits, with the validation data used to decide
when to stop model training (we use early stopping and restore the weights at which the validation
loss is minimized). We use the mean squared error as our loss function, and the adam optimizer
to fit the model. Test data is only used for final model evaluation. Moreover, note that herein our
primary metric for success is whether the internal parameters of the QPANN accurately represent
the true errors in the system—and the QPANN has no direct access to the values of the system’s
error rates even in the training.
