Hidden horn: misdiagnosed ectopic pregnancy in a unicornuate uterus
Authors: Mahdhir Amat Tugiman, Pankhuri Punj, Shavershree Govender
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital uterine anomalies occur as a result of a failure of the fusion and resorption of the Müllerian ducts in utero. Although Müllerian anomalies are relatively uncommon, they can complicate reproductive health and even cause death.We report a case of an asymptomatic Caucasian woman in her 30s who was misdiagnosed with a tubal ectopic pregnancy at 12 weeks 0 days during a routine dating scan, ultimately revealing a right-sided unicornuate uterus with a non-communicating rudimentary horn. The diagnosis of the Müllerian anomaly was made based on two-dimensional sonography and laparoscopy but was eventually confirmed with MRI. Due to the high risk of uterine rupture, intrauterine growth restriction and preterm birth, it was felt continuation of the pregnancy was not advisable. Thus, the woman underwent a laparotomy, during which a left hemi-hysterectomy of the rudimentary horn was performed and the pregnancy was terminated.

Introduction

The female internal reproductive organs develop via the fusion and resorption of paired Müllerian ducts in utero. Congenital uterine anomalies occur because of a failure anywhere along these processes. Although Müllerian anomalies are relatively uncommon, they can complicate reproductive health. A unicornuate uterus or a type II uterine anomaly using the American Society for Reproductive Medicine classification, is a lateral fusion defect when one side of the Müllerian tract fails to develop, forming a rudimentary horn.1 Unicornuate uterus is believed to comprise 2.4%–13% of all Müllerian anomalies.2 The incidence of ectopic pregnancy in a rudimentary uterine horn is approximately 1 in 76 000–150 000.3 This variation in the incidence of ectopic pregnancy is understandably due to difficulties in diagnosis.

The most significant complication of an ectopic pregnancy in a rudimentary uterine horn is rupture, which may lead to fatal haemoperitoneum.4–6 Thus, early identification and management are key to avoiding such complications and minimising maternal morbidity.

We present a case of a woman at 12 weeks’ gestation with a rudimentary horn pregnancy, following two previous uncomplicated term pregnancies.

Case presentation

A Caucasian female in her 30s, Gravida 4, Para 2, Miscarriage 1, referred from a rural centre for evaluation of a suspected ectopic pregnancy, presented to the emergency department after a routine dating ultrasound scan at 12 weeks 0 days of gestation. The patient was asymptomatic.

She had one previous miscarriage at 8 weeks and subsequently two spontaneous vaginal deliveries (SVDs) at term. There was no other relevant gynaecological history. She is a non-smoker with a prepregnancy body mass index of 24.

Transabdominal and transvaginal two-dimensional ultrasound scans were performed. No intrauterine gestation was identified (figure 1) but a 97 mm×90 mm×75 mm live extra-uterine pregnancy was visualised in the left adnexa (figure 2) and initially presumed to be tubal. There was no clinical or sonographic evidence of a rupture. The patient was then urgently referred to the emergency department after this incidental finding of an ectopic pregnancy.

Transvaginal ultrasound demonstrating an empty uterine cavity with no intrauterine gestation identified.

Transvaginal ultrasound demonstrating a live extra-uterine gestation in the left adnexa measuring 97 mm×90 mm×75 mm, initially presumed to represent a tubal ectopic pregnancy.

On presentation, the patient was haemodynamically stable with normal vital signs. Abdominal examination revealed a soft, non-distended abdomen with no tenderness, rebound tenderness or guarding. A pelvic examination was deferred, given the absence of vaginal bleeding or pain and the patient’s stable condition. A bedside ultrasound confirmed a gestational sac with a fetus, which was believed to be ectopic.

MRI services were not available at the time of review. Thus, given that the patient had a quantitative beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (b-hCG) of 253 850, which was disproportionately high for a suspected tubal ectopic pregnancy, this raised a suspicion for an alternative diagnosis. The possibility of a Müllerian anomaly was discussed with the patient. An examination under anaesthesia and laparoscopy were conducted. Prior, the patient was counselled that the procedure would be abandoned if the suspicion held true, for which she would be referred to a tertiary centre for further management.

At laparoscopy, a 360-degree view of the pelvis revealed a normal abdominal cavity, normal right-sided uterus, fallopian tube and ovary. There was a mass tissue arising from the superolateral aspect of the visualised uterus, possessing its own fallopian tube and ovary, with uncertain intracavitary communication (figures 3 and 4). The left ovary and fallopian tube were normal (figure 5). Laparoscopic images suggested a Müllerian anomaly, specifically, uterine didelphys or a communicating or non-communicating uterine horn at the time of the procedure.

Laparoscopic view showing a left-sided rudimentary uterine horn containing a gestational sac.

Laparoscopic view demonstrating a left-sided rudimentary uterine horn containing a gestational sac adjacent to a right unicornuate uterus.

Laparoscopic view of left ovary and left fallopian tube, which appear anatomically normal.

Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary centre for more specialised care where a non-contrast MRI scan was arranged, with the aim of further planning and confirmation. The images were compared with prior ultrasound findings from before. The MRI scan (figures 6 and 7) showed a pregnancy in the left rudimentary horn, with the placenta centred posteriorly, with a thinned 3 mm myometrium anteriorly, which did not demonstrate any communication with the endometrial cavity. The cervix and endometrial cavity were deviated to the right (right-sided unicornuate uterus inferolateral to the rudimentary horn) with an endometrial thickness of 20 mm. She had bilateral ovaries which appeared unremarkable. There was no free fluid and both her kidneys appeared unremarkable. The findings were consistent with a right-sided unicornuate uterus with a non-communicating rudimentary horn containing the pregnancy.

Coronal pelvic MRI showing a gestational sac within a left-sided rudimentary horn.

Sagittal pelvic MRI demonstrating a gestational sac within a left-sided rudimentary uterine horn, with no communication with the endometrial cavity.

Outcome and follow-up

With a thinned 3 mm myometrium anterior to the placenta, she was perceived to be at a high risk of uterine rupture. After further discussions with the patient, a decision was made to proceed with laparotomy, during which a left hemi-hysterectomy of the rudimentary horn was performed to terminate the pregnancy. A left salpingectomy was performed with conservation of the left ovary. The rudimentary horn was then excised from the medial attachment to the uterus, leaving the right uterus intact (figure 8).

Intraoperative view following excision of the left rudimentary uterine horn, with preservation of the right unicornuate uterus.

On histology, the left fallopian tube was an intact fimbriated tube measuring 50 mm×7 mm. The serosal surface appeared slightly congested, and no focal lesions were identified. The left rudimentary horn ectopic pregnancy was an aggregate of fragments of tissue measuring 160 mm×110 mm×20 mm. Foetal parts were identified; with foot length of 10 mm. Microscopically, the sections showed fragmented myometrium with neovascularisation, containing chorionic villi and the implantation site. No clear trophoblast and villi were seen permeating through the myometrium in areas, in keeping with placenta increta. The sampled serosal surface was intact within the sections examined. Features diagnostic of gestational trophoblastic disease were not seen. Overall, histology confirmed ectopic products of conception with placenta increta.

The patient retains fertility potential as the right uterus, fallopian tube and ovaries are preserved.

Discussion

Signs and symptoms of an ectopic pregnancy typically involve abnormal vitals such as tachycardia, low blood pressure, lower abdominal pain and vaginal bleeding. Misdiagnosis of a tubal ectopic pregnancy can happen due to a Müllerian anomaly. Even with previous successful deliveries, uterine anomalies cannot be ruled out. Transperitoneal migration of sperm from the contralateral fallopian tube is required for a non-communicating rudimentary horn pregnancy to occur7 8; which is believed to account for approximately 50% of all spontaneous human pregnancies.9 While there have been case reports of successful pregnancies in non-communicating rudimentary horns, the risk of uterine rupture, fetal growth restrictions and preterm birth remains high. These complications are mainly due to abnormal uterine vasculature and reduced myometrial mass.10 Thus, to reduce the risk of future ectopic pregnancies and menstrual issues, some authors suggest removing the non-communicating tubes.11

The two previous SVDs were possible since the right fallopian tube communicates with the normal uterus. Thus, the zygote and subsequently the placenta could implant directly in the uterus, with no complications during the pregnancy. Ultrasound scans done for her two previous pregnancies also failed to identify the pre-existing Müllerian anomaly, which suggests the difficulty in assessing anatomical variations with the imaging method.

Due to the rarity and complexity of the condition, awareness and timely management are key in optimising both maternal and foetal outcomes. While two-dimensional sonography may have missed the diagnosis in this case, its role in detecting an abnormality remains understated. Tsafrir et al7 proposed a sonographic criterion for a presumptive diagnosis of rudimentary horn pregnancy, which includes (1) a pseudopattern of an asymmetrical bicornuate uterus, (2) absent visual continuity tissue surrounding the gestational sac and the uterine cervix and (3) the presence of myometrial tissue surrounding the gestational sac. Mavrelos et al12 also added some further diagnostic features: (1) a single interstitial portion of the fallopian tube in the main uterine body; (2) a gestational sac, mobile and separate from the uterus, surrounded by myometrium and (3) a vascular pedicle joining the gestational sac to the unicornuate uterus. These sonographic criteria can help distinguish a rudimentary horn pregnancy from a tubal pregnancy, where a ring of myometrium surrounding the gestational sac is absent. Unfortunately, the reporting radiologist was not able to appreciate these features, and thus, a presumptive diagnosis of ectopic pregnancy was made.

Furthermore, while not seen in the patient, renal anomalies such as pelvic kidney and horse-shoe kidneys are associated with 30%–50% of cases with patients with Müllerian anomalies. For a patient with a unicornuate uterus, the most common renal abnormalities include an ipsilateral renal and ureter agenesis.13 Thus, it is important to investigate the kidneys once a Müllerian anomaly has been identified.

Conclusion

This case highlights the complexities involved in diagnosing congenital uterine anomalies, particularly in asymptomatic patients. The initial misdiagnosis of an ectopic pregnancy illustrates the necessity for thorough imaging and evaluation. The implications of such anomalies can significantly affect reproductive outcomes and warrant ongoing research and awareness. Increased documentation and understanding of such cases are essential for improving clinical management and outcomes in affected individuals.

Learning points

Congenital uterine anomalies should be considered in the differential diagnosis of late-presenting ectopic pregnancy, since they may allow pregnancy progression beyond the gestational age typically seen in tubal ectopic pregnancies.
