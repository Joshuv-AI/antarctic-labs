Research note: occurrence, molecular characterization, and clinical associations of avian intestinal spirochetosis in laying hens in India
Authors: Chanthirasekaran Raju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Avian intestinal spirochetosis (AIS) is an enteric disease of laying hens caused by pathogenic Brachyspira species and is likely underrecognized in typical on-farm conditions. Despite India being a major egg-producing country, organized data on AIS in Indian poultry remain limited. This targeted investigation described Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) detection of Brachyspira spp., associated field observations, and preliminary species-level evidence in clinically suspected commercial layer flocks in South and North India. Cecal mucosal scrapings were collected from 59 recently deceased laying hens from 12 commercial farms (33 sheds; 25 to 105 weeks of age) and applied to FTA cards. Samples were screened by PCR targeting the Brachyspira 16S rRNA gene and the NADH oxidase (nox) gene; selected amplicons were sequenced and analyzed phylogenetically. Brachyspira spp. were detected in 83.3% of farms, 82% of sheds, and 67.8% of samples in this targeted investigation of clinically suspected flocks. Detection was numerically higher in older flocks, ranging from 37.5% in birds aged 25 to 40 weeks to 84.6% in birds aged 71 to 105 weeks; however, this age related trend was not statistically significant. PCR-positive flocks commonly exhibited loose droppings, wet litter, vent pasting, dirty, fecal-stained eggs (approximately 1 to 5%) of total eggs, and only modest reduction (3 to 5%) in egg production in about one-third of affected flocks. nox sequencing of selected PCR-positive samples identified sequences consistent with Brachyspira pilosicoli, Brachyspira intermedia, Brachyspira pulli, and Brachyspira alvinipulli, providing preliminary evidence of multiple Brachyspira spp. among sequenced samples.

Introduction

Avian intestinal spirochetosis (AIS) is a chronic enteric disease of commercial laying hens caused by colonization of the lower intestine, especially the ceca, by anaerobic spirochetes of the genus Brachyspira. In affected flocks, AIS is usually expressed as a persistent production problem rather than an acute outbreak. Soft to watery droppings, wet litter, vent pasting, fecal staining of eggshells, and delayed or reduced egg production are typical findings. Because these signs are nonspecific and overlap with nutritional, parasitic, bacterial, and management-related disorders, AIS may remain undetected unless Brachyspira infection is specifically investigated (Medhanie et al., 2013).

The importance of AIS lies in its sustained effect on egg output and egg quality. Experimental infection with B. intermedia increased fecal water content and reduced egg production in laying hens, supporting the relationship between intestinal colonization, wet droppings, and production loss (Hampson and McLaren, 1999). In commercial flocks, losses arise from reduced flock output, fecal-stained or downgraded eggs, and delayed recognition. Thus, AIS is best regarded as a production-limiting disease that can persist with modest but cumulative losses rather than as a rapidly fatal condition.

Several Brachyspira species have been described in poultry. B. pilosicoli and B. intermedia are generally considered the principal pathogenic species in laying hens, (Hampson and McLaren, 1999), whereas B. alvinipulli is also recognized as an avian pathogen but is reported less frequently.

Diagnosis of AIS has traditionally relied on clinical signs combined with culture or microscopic detection of spirochetes in cecal samples. Culture remains useful, but Brachyspira organisms are slow-growing, fastidious anaerobes, and phenotypic differentiation is limited. Molecular diagnostics, particularly Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) assays targeting Brachyspira 16S rRNA and NADH oxidase gene (nox) followed by sequencing, have improved analytical sensitivity and enabled species-level identification.

In India, the significance of AIS in commercial layer production remains insufficiently defined. Despite the scale of the Indian poultry sector, published information on Brachyspira infection in layer flocks is limited. A recent Indian report identified B. pilosicoli in commercial layers and associated infection with dysbiosis, dirty eggs, production decline, and cecal lesions (Vigneshkumar et al., 2025). However, the broader occurrence of Brachyspira infection and the species diversity present in clinically suspected Indian layer flocks remain poorly characterized. Therefore, the objective of this Research Note was to describe the occurrence of AIS in commercial layer flocks in India, document associated field observations, and provide preliminary nox-based species-level evidence. To our knowledge, this study provides the first Indian report of B. alvinipulli and B. pulli detected among selected sequenced samples from layer flocks.

Materials and methods

Study design and flock selection

A targeted field investigation was conducted during 2023 in commercial layer farms in Namakkal, Hyderabad, and Barwala. Farms were selected based on field reports of wet litter, loose droppings, increased dirty eggs, or unexplained reduction in egg production. The study included 12 farms and 33 sheds containing Babcock BV 300 laying hens aged 25 to 105 weeks and reared in raised platform cages. This was not a random prevalence survey; results were interpreted as targeted surveillance of clinically suspected flocks.

Sample collection and farm data

Cecal mucosal scrapings were obtained from 59 recently deceased layer hens (routine on-farm mortalities) following standard diagnostic protocols for AIS. During necropsy, the ceca were aseptically opened, and mucosal scrapings were collected using sterile instruments. Each sample was immediately placed on FTA cards (QIAcard FTA™ Classic; formerly Whatman FTA Classic; QIAGEN, Germany) to preserve DNA integrity. Farm-level information was recorded, including flock age, housing system, litter condition, egg production relative to breed standards, the occurrence of dirty eggs, and observable clinical signs.

DNA extraction

We extracted genomic DNA from FTA card punches using an elution-based protocol, then purified it with a bead-based method. This method was optimized to enhance the recovery of bacterial DNA from preserved biological material. The purified DNA samples were stored at −20 °C until further analysis.

PCR detection of Brachyspira spp.

PCR assays were employed to detect Brachyspira spp. at both the genus and species levels. Genus‑level detection targeted the Brachyspira 16S rRNA gene using primers BRANCHY‑F (5′‑TTGCTACTGGTTCTTGGCCTG‑3′) and BRANCHY‑R (5′‑CTTCTATAAGTTCAACACCTAT‑3′) as previously described (La et al., 2003; Westerman et al., 2013). Species‑level identification was performed using primers specific to the NADH oxidase (nox) gene (La et al., 2003). Each PCR run included appropriate positive and negative controls to ensure assay reliability and accuracy.

Sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

Following PCR detection, a subset of PCR-positive samples was selected for nox gene sequencing. The amplified products were submitted for commercial Sanger sequencing, and resulting sequences were identified via BLASTn comparison against NCBI GenBank entries. For phylogenetic analysis, representative nox gene sequences of Brachyspira spp. were retrieved from GenBank and aligned with the field sequences using the ClustalW algorithm implemented in MegAlign program (Lasergene v6, DNASTAR). A distance-based neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was then constructed using the same software to infer relationships among the field sequences and reference strains, supporting preliminary species-level assignments and genetic relatedness to reference sequences. The partial nox gene sequences generated in this study were deposited in GenBank under accession numbers PZ051560, PZ161337, PZ161338 and PZ161339.

No Brachyspira culture or histopathology was performed. Samples were obtained from birds that died naturally, and no live birds were handled, treated, or euthanized for the study. Results were summarized descriptively at farm, shed, and bird levels.

Data analysis

Associations between Brachyspira PCR status and categorical variables were evaluated using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because multiple birds were sampled from some farms and sheds, observations may not have been fully independent; therefore, inferential comparisons were interpreted cautiously.

Results and discussion

PCR testing of cecal mucosal scrapings demonstrated frequent detection of Brachyspira DNA in this targeted set of clinically suspected layer flocks (Table 1). Brachyspira spp. were detected on 10 of 12 farms (83.3%), in 27 of 33 sheds (81.8%), and in 40 of 59 birds (67.8%), with within-farm positivity ranging from 50% to 100%. These values should be interpreted as diagnostic findings from farms submitted because avian intestinal spirochetosis (AIS) was considered a field possibility, not as prevalence estimates for the Indian layer industry. The birds sampled were recently deceased hens from farms reporting wet litter, loose droppings, dirty eggs, or reduced egg production, and the sampling design therefore enriched the study population for flocks in which Brachyspira infection was plausible. This distinction is important for applying the data in practice. The findings support Brachyspira PCR testing in the diagnostic work up of clinically suspected problem layer flocks, whereas estimation of national occurrence would require random, adequately powered sampling of clinically normal and affected farms. The field signs recorded in PCR-positive flocks were consistent with the syndrome usually associated with AIS, although none was sufficiently specific to support diagnosis without laboratory testing. Wet litter and semisolid to watery droppings were reported on 10 of 12 farms (83.3%), and all flocks with obvious loose or watery droppings were PCR-positive for Brachyspira. In raised platform cage systems, the absence of the characteristic cone-shaped manure accumulation in 22 of 33 sheds (66.7%) was consistent with persistently wet, poorly formed droppings and inadequate manure drying. These observations are consistent with the expected effects of lower intestinal colonization on fecal consistency can translate directly into wet litter, vent contamination, and manure-management problems. Similar signs, including moist feces, pasty vents, and fecal-stained eggs, have been described in layer flocks affected by intestinal spirochetosis (Swayne et al., 1992; Stephens and Hampson, 1999).

Dirty eggs were recorded on all farms at low levels (1 to 5%), but Brachyspira spp. were not detected in 6 flocks in which dirty eggs were the only reported abnormality. This observation is useful diagnostically because eggshell soiling in commercial layers is multifactorial. It may follow loose droppings, but it may also reflect cage design, ventilation, water leakage, manure handling, diet, or other management conditions. Thus, dirty eggs alone should not be treated as a specific field indicator of Brachyspira infection. Its diagnostic value is greater when it occurs together with persistent loose droppings or wet litter. In this data set, overt loose droppings showed a significant association with PCR status: all flocks with obvious loose droppings were PCR-positive, whereas flocks without this sign were negative (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.046). Because multiple birds came from the same farms and sheds, this association should still be interpreted cautiously, but it supports targeted PCR testing when persistent loose droppings and wet litter are present.

Production effects were present but generally modest, which is compatible with AIS as a chronic production problem rather than a severe acute disease. All farms showed some reduction below breed standards, but only 4 of 12 farms (33%) had a 3 to 5% reduction; the remaining farms had minor shortfalls of <1%. Across affected flocks, production losses were approximately 0.5 to 4%. These findings align with previous field and experimental work linking intestinal Brachyspira detection with loose droppings, dirty eggs, and reduced laying performance, but they do not establish Brachyspira as the sole cause of the observed production changes (Bano et al., 2008; Medhanie et al., 2013). In commercial layer farms, egg output can be influenced by age, nutrition, water quality, concurrent enteric disease, heat stress, housing, and flock management. Mortality was recorded as a field observation on some farms, but mortality rates were not quantified; therefore, an AIS-specific effect on mortality cannot be inferred from these data.

Detection was numerically higher in older hens, ranging from 38% in young layers aged 25 to 40 weeks to 69% in mid-cycle flocks aged 41 to 70 weeks and 85% in hens aged 71 to 105 weeks. However, this trend was not statistically significant (chi-square test, χ² = 4.03, df = 2, P = 0.13; Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact P = 0.15). Age should therefore be described only as a numerical trend in this targeted data set, not as a confirmed epidemiological association or risk factor. The apparent increase in older hens may reflect cumulative exposure or longer time at risk, as suggested in earlier work, but the present study was too small and too clustered to establish that relationship statistically (Stephens and Hampson, 1999; Medhanie et al., 2013).

The sequencing results provided preliminary species-level evidence from a limited subset of PCR-positive samples. Six PCR-positive samples were selected for nox gene sequencing. Four samples from a farm in South India yielded high-quality partial nox sequences suitable for detailed analysis, GenBank submission, and phylogenetic reconstruction, and were deposited under accession numbers PZ051560, PZ161337, PZ161338 and PZ161339. Two additional samples from another farm in North India yielded partial nox sequences of sufficient quality for BLASTn-based species identification, but they were not included in the phylogenetic analysis or submitted to GenBank. The nox gene was appropriate for this purpose because variation in this marker has been used to distinguish closely related Brachyspira taxa more effectively than genus-level detection alone (Atyeo et al., 1999). Nevertheless, because only a small selected subset was sequenced, species frequency, regional distribution, and wider epidemiological patterns cannot be inferred.

BLASTn comparisons identified different Brachyspira species among the selected sequenced samples, including B. pilosicoli, B. intermedia, B. pulli, and B. alvinipulli. The sequence assignments were KEM-0831 as B. pilosicoli, KEM-083D as B. intermedia, KEM-083E as B. pulli, and KEM-083N as B. alvinipulli. BLASTn comparisons supported preliminary species level assignments, with identities ranging from 92 to 100%, depending on the sample and reference sequence. Some partial nox comparisons showed 91.9 to 92.2% similarity to B. pilosicoli reference strains and 84.1 to 85.0% similarity to B. alvinipulli; KEM-083N shared around 93.1% sequence identity with published B. alvinipulli references. Pairwise sequence comparisons in the partial nox region showed limited nucleotide variation among the sequenced samples, with 0 to 4 substitutions per 100 bp, and overall divergence in the partial nox region was approximately 3 to 8%. These data support preliminary species-level assignment among the sequenced samples, but they should not be converted into species percentages because not all PCR-positive samples were sequenced.

Phylogenetic analysis of the partial nox sequences supported the BLASTn assignments and placed the field sequences within corresponding reference lineages (Fig. 1). The B. pilosicoli sequence clustered within the B. pilosicoli clade together with reference strains from Europe and Australia, whereas the B. alvinipulli sequence grouped within the B. alvinipulli lineage. Field sequences assigned to B. intermedia and B. pulli aligned with their respective reference clusters. These findings are relevant because organized information on Brachyspira species in Indian layer flocks remains limited, and earlier Indian work focused mainly on B. pilosicoli (Vigneshkumar et al., 2025). Importantly, detection of different Brachyspira species among selected samples does not demonstrate mixed infection within individual birds. No bird in this study was shown to carry more than one Brachyspira species. The appropriate interpretation is that different species were identified among selected sequenced samples from clinically suspected flocks, providing preliminary evidence of flock-level species diversity, but not individual-bird coinfection. This cautious interpretation is consistent with the broader limitations of the study. Farms were selected based on clinical suspicion, samples were obtained from recently deceased birds (fewer than two birds per shed on average), and observations were not fully independent because of clustering within farms and sheds. PCR detected Brachyspira DNA in cecal mucosal scrapings, but did not confirm active mucosal colonization, intestinal lesions, or causation of the field signs. Culture and histopathology were not performed. Broader random surveys, supported by histopathology, culture, or wider sequencing, are needed to estimate industry-level detection and clarify disease attribution. Within these constraints, the findings support targeted PCR testing as a practical diagnostic tool when layer flocks show persistent wet litter, loose droppings, dirty eggs, and modest production losses, and they provide preliminary nox-based evidence of multiple Brachyspira species among selected samples from clinically suspected Indian layer flocks.
