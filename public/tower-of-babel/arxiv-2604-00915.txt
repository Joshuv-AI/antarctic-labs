Orthogonal Learner for Estimating Heterogeneous Long-Term Treatment Effects
Haorui Ma, Dennis Frauen, Valentyn Melnychuk, Stefan Feuerriegel

Abstract
Estimation of heterogeneous long-term treatment effects (HLTEs) is widely used for personalized decision-making in marketing, economics, and medicine, where short-term randomized experiments are often combined with long-term observational data. However, HLTE estimation is challenging due to limited overlap in treatment or in observing long-term outcomes for certain subpopulations, which can lead to unstable HLTE estimates with large finite-sample variance. To address this challenge, we introduce the LT-O-learners (Long-Term Orthogonal Learners), a set of novel orthogonal learners for HLTE estimation. The learners are designed for the canonical HLTE setting that combines a short-term randomized dataset 𝒟 1 \mathcal{D}_{1} with a long-term historical dataset 𝒟 2 \mathcal{D}_{2} . The key idea of our LT-O-learners is to retarget the learning objective by introducing custom overlap weights that downweight samples with low overlap in treatment or in long-term observation. We show that the retargeted loss is equivalent to the weighted oracle loss and satisfies Neyman-orthogonality, which means our learners are robust to errors in the nuisance estimation. We further provide a general error bound for the LT-O-learners and give the conditions under which quasi-oracle rate can be achieved. Finally, our LT-O-learners are model-agnostic and can thus be instantiated with arbitrary machine learning models. We conduct empirical evaluations on synthetic and semi-synthetic benchmarks to confirm the theoretical properties of our LT-O-learners, especially the robustness in low-overlap settings. To the best of our knowledge, ours are the first orthogonal learners for HLTE estimation that are robust to low overlap that is common in long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Estimating heterogeneous
long-term
treatment effects (HLTEs) from short-term experiments is a common problem in marketing, economics, and medicine
[
17
,
57
,
2
]
. In many real-world applications, outcomes of interest materialize only after substantial delays or incur high measurement costs. As a result, randomized experiments typically record only short-term surrogate outcomes, and must be combined with auxiliary data sources that capture long-term outcomes in order to estimate HLTEs
[
8
,
9
,
11
]
.

Example.
In oncology drug development, randomized clinical trials of newly developed cancer therapies often rely on short-term surrogate endpoints, such as tumor shrinkage or biomarker responses, because outcomes like overall survival may take years to observe
[
4
]
. As a result, long-term outcomes for the new therapy are typically unavailable at the time of the trial. To assess long-term treatment effects, researchers therefore combine short-term randomized trial data with historical patient records or cancer registries that contain long-term survival outcomes, where survival can be modeled as a function of observed surrogate endpoints.

Formally, the task of estimating long-term treatment effects is typically formalized as follows
[
10
,
56
,
2
]
: a short-term randomized dataset
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, in which treatment assignment is randomized and surrogate outcomes are observed, and a long-term observational dataset
\mathcal{D}_{2}
, in which long-term outcomes are recorded but treatment assignment may be missing or unobserved (e.g., because it is a new medical treatment or marketing intervention that was not yet applied before). The two datasets have shared covariates, which then enable identification and estimation of long-term treatment effects under suitable assumptions.

Several methods have been developed for estimating long-term treatment effects
[
1
,
10
,
2
, e.g.,]
, but they primarily focus on the
average
long-term treatment effect (ALTE) rather than the HLTE. To our knowledge, learners designed for the canonical HLTE, i.e. with surrogacy and comparability assumption
[
2
]
, have not been explicitly studied in the literature. Nevertheless, standard confounding adjustment strategies can be adapted to derive a class of meta-learners

However, the meta-learners discussed above fail to address challenges due to

[

. Broadly, overlap refers to the availability of comparable samples across relevant regimes for individuals with similar covariates. In the HLTE setting, overlap arises in two different forms. (i)
Limited treatment overlap:
in the short-term experimental dataset

Limited long-term outcome overlap:
unique to the long-term setting is that some subpopulations may have a low probability of observing long-term outcomes in the historical dataset
\mathcal{D}_{2}

In this paper, we address these challenges by proposing the
LT-O-learners
(Long-Term-Orthogonal-Learners), a novel class of orthogonal meta-learners for estimating HLTEs from a short-term randomized dataset
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and a long-term observational dataset
\mathcal{D}_{2}
. The key idea behind our LT-O-learners is to explicitly account for both (i) limited treatment assignment overlap and (ii) limited long-term outcome overlap. To this end, our LT-O-learners
retarget
the learning objective through carefully designed overlap weights that downweight samples with weak treatment overlap or low probability of observing long-term outcomes, thereby stabilizing the second-stage learning problem. We show that the retargeted loss is equivalent to the weighted oracle loss. Then, we instantiate three variants: (1)
LT-O-TO-learner
, (2)
LT-O-LO-learner
, and (3)
LT-O-DO-learner
with different weightings tailored for different overlap regimes. We further provide an error bound for the LT-O-learners and give the conditions under which a quasi-oracle convergence rate can be achieved. Importantly, our LT-O-learners are fully model-agnostic: they can be instantiated with arbitrary machine learning models (e.g., neural networks) for both nuisance estimation and outcome learning, making them broadly applicable in high-dimensional HLTE settings from practice.

Research gap:
Existing methods for HLTE estimation fail to address the challenge due to (i) limited treatment overlap and (ii) limited long-term outcome issue in finite samples (see Table

Setup:
We follow the canonical long-term outcome setting
2
,
10
, but adapt it from ALTEs to HLTEs. We thus consider two datasets: a short-term experimental dataset
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and a long-term historical observational dataset
\mathcal{D}_{2}
, with
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
units respectively.
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
differ in terms of which variables are observed (see Figure
1
).

Let
A\in\{0,1\}
be the treatment variable,
X\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
be pre-treatment covariates,
S\in\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}
be the short-term surrogate variable,
Y\in\mathbb{R}
be the long-term outcome, and
R\in\{0,1\}
be the dataset indicator, denoting whether the observation belongs to
\mathcal{D}_{1}
(
R=0
) or
\mathcal{D}_{2}
(
R=1
).

The covariates
X
and short-term surrogates
S
are observed in both
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
. The long-term outcome
Y
is observed only in
\mathcal{D}_{2}
. The treatment
A
is only measured in the experimental dataset
\mathcal{D}_{1}
but
not
in
\mathcal{D}_{2}
(e.g., because this is a new treatment that was not applied previously). Formally, we can combine
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
to build a unified dataset with

\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{1}\cup\mathcal{D}_{2}=\{(X_{i},(1-R_{i})A_{i},S_{i},R_{i},R_{i}Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}

Nuisance functions:
Our method relies on a set of nuisance functions.

\displaystyle\pi_{s}(s,x)=\Pr(A=1\mid S=s,X=x,R=0),

(1)

\displaystyle\pi(x)=\Pr(A=1\mid X=x,R=0),

(2)

\displaystyle\rho_{s}(s,x)=\Pr(R=1\mid S=s,X=x),

(3)

\displaystyle\rho(x)=\Pr(R=1\mid X=x),

(4)

\displaystyle h(s,x)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S=s,X=x,R=1],

(5)

\displaystyle\mu(a,x)=\mathbb{E}\big[h(S,X)\mid A=a,X=x,R=0\big].

(6)

We let
\eta
denote the complete set of nuisance functions, i.e.,

\eta=(\pi(x),\pi_{s}(s,x),\rho(x),\rho_{s}(s,x),h(s,x),\mu(a,x)).

(7)

Causal estimand:
We build upon the potential outcome framework
[
46

and aim to estimate the HLTE in the experimental dataset, given by

\displaystyle\tau^{0}(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X=x,R=0\right].

(8)

This estimand captures how long-term treatment effects vary at the individual or subgroup level as a function of covariates

2

For all

h(s,x)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S=s,X=x,R=1]

\bullet

Long-term T-learner (LT-T-learner):
The simplest approach is to estimate the conditional response functions
\mu_{a}(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[h(S,x)|A=a,X=x,R=0\right],\;a\in\{0,1\}
. Then, we obtain the
plug-in
HLTE estimator:
\hat{\tau}^{0}_{T}(x)=\hat{\mu}_{1}(x)-\hat{\mu}_{0}(x)
. We name it the long-term T-learner following the standard HTE setting
[
36
]
. This T-learner plugs-in the estimated
\hat{\mu}
directly into the identification in Eq. (
9
). However, this induces the so-called "plug-in bias"
[
34
]
, which means that the estimator inherits the entire estimation error of the nuisance functions
\hat{\mu}_{1}
.

To mitigate the plug-in bias in standard HTE estimation, literature often employs two-stage learners that regress a constructed pseudo-outcome on covariates
[
36
,
15
]
. Here, we formulate the long-term analogues of these learners as follows:

\bullet

Long-term RA-learner (LT-RA-learner):
The RA-learner follows a two-stage pseudo-outcome regression. It fits estimates
(\hat{h},\hat{\mu}_{1},\hat{\mu}_{0})
in the first stage. In the second stage, it regresses the regression-adjusted pseudo-outcome on
\mathcal{D}_{1}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RA}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{RA}}-g(X))^{2}\right],

(10)

where
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{RA}}=A(\hat{h}(S,X)-\hat{\mu}_{0}(X))+(1-A)(\hat{\mu}_{1}(X)-\hat{h}(S,X)).

\bullet

Long-term IPW-learner (LT-IPW-learner):
The IPW-learner uses inverse-propensity-based pseudo-outcome:

\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{IPW}}=\left(\frac{A}{\hat{\pi}(X)}-\frac{1-A}{1-\hat{\pi}(X)}\right)\hat{h}(S,X)
, and the second stage loss is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IPW}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{IPW}}-g(X))^{2}\right]

(11)

However,
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{IPW}}
contains terms like
\frac{1}{\hat{\pi}(X)}
(or
\frac{1}{1-\hat{\pi}(X)}
), which is highly unstable in limited treatment overlap regimes.

Why do we need orthogonal learners:

Although with a two-stage framework, the LT-RA-learner and LT-IPW-learner still have two limitations: (i) the second-stage loss only uses data from
\mathcal{D}_{1}
, and (ii) they are still sensitive to the nuisance estimation errors
\hat{\eta}-\eta
. To overcome these limitations, current state-of-the-art two-stage methods for treatment effect estimation (either standard or long-term) use the so-called
orthogonal learners

[
13
,
19
]
. A learner with loss function
\mathcal{L}(g,\eta)
is defined as orthogonal if the Gateaux derivative of the loss w.r.t. the nuisance parameters vanishes at the truth:

\displaystyle D_{\eta}D_{g}\mathcal{L}(g,\eta)[\hat{g}-g,\hat{\eta}-\eta]=0.

(12)

Intuitively, orthogonality ensures that the gradient of the loss function is insensitive to perturbations in the nuisance parameters
[
13
]
. This allows faster convergence of the target estimator even with a slow convergence of the nuisances. Furthermore, orthogonal loss typically utilizes the full population, instead of a conditional population as in Eq. (
11
), Eq. (
10
). In the section below, we propose our LT-O-learners, a class of orthogonal learners for HLTE estimation, which are designed to be robust against nuisance errors and stable in low-overlap regimes.

5
Our LT-O-learners

Main idea of our LT-O-learners:
Existing studies have shown that, orthogonal learners without sample reweighting are usually hurt by low-overlaps under finite samples
[
18
,
21
,
22
]
(See Appendix
C.1
for the variance analysis of the unweighted LT-O-DR-learner). Our goal is to design learners that are both orthogonal and robust against low-overlap regimes. To do this, the key idea is to downweight the samples with (i) low treatment or (ii) low long-term outcome overlap. Conceptually, this corresponds to optimizing a weighted oracle risk of the form

\mathcal{L}_{\omega}^{\text{oracle}}(g)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\omega(\pi(X),\rho(X))\cdot(\tau^{0}(X)-g(X))^{2}\mid R=0\bigr],

where
\omega(\pi(X),\rho(X))>0
represents a
weighting function
that
retargets
the objective to downweight the samples with low overlap while preserving the target HLTE
\tau^{0}(x)
. A simple example of such a weighting function is

\displaystyle\omega(\pi(x),\rho(x))=\underbrace{\pi(x)(1-\pi(x))}_{\text{treat. overlap}}\cdot\underbrace{\rho(x)}_{\text{long-term outcome overlap}}\hskip-8.5359pt

(13)

The loss
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}^{\text{oracle}}
is an oracle objective because it depends on the true HLTE
\tau^{0}(x)
, which is our target estimand and thus unobserved in practice. Hence, our goal is to construct a loss function
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(g;\eta)
that is equivalent to this oracle objective and can be optimized using observed data. This requires a non-trivial derivation to ensure that the resulting loss (i) shares the same minimizer as the oracle loss and (ii) is Neyman-orthogonal w.r.t. the nuisance functions.

Overview:
In Section
5.1
, we show how we orthogonalize the oracle weighted loss. In Section
5.2
, we give the algorithm for training the HLTE estimator. Then, in Section
5.3
, we give specific instantiations of our LT-O-learners for different types of low-overlap regimes. Finally, in Section
5.4
, we show that the estimator achieves quasi-oracle convergence rate under mild conditions.

5.1
Orthogonalizing the weighted oracle loss

In this section, we derive the orthogonal loss that has the same minimizer as the weighted oracle loss, under true nuisance
\eta
and model class
\mathcal{G}
. Following a similar approach as in

5.2
Algorithm

Based on the proposed loss function in Eq. (
15
), we estimate the HLTE following a two-stage procedure (described below), which uses a
sample-splitting

[
13
,
35
,
39
,
19
]
. Specifically, we randomly split the samples from
\mathcal{D}=\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
into two disjoint parts
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{A}\cup\mathcal{D}_{B}
, with
|\mathcal{D}_{A}|=N-n,|\mathcal{D}_{B}|=n
. Then, our LT-O-learners proceed as follows

Stage
1
:
We fit the nuisance functions on
\mathcal{D}_{A}
data with arbitrary machine learning models to obtain the nuisance estimators
\hat{\eta}=(\hat{\pi},\hat{\pi}_{s},\hat{\rho},\hat{\rho}_{s},\hat{h},\hat{\mu})
.

Stage
2
:
We calculate the estimated LT weighting function and pseudo outcome with the estimated nuisance function in stage 1 on
\mathcal{D}_{B}
: For
i=1,\ldots,n
, we compute

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}^{*}_{i}=\omega^{*}(Z_{i};\hat{\eta}),\;\;\;\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{i}=\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{LT}}(Z_{i};\hat{\eta}).

(19)

Then, using the nuisance estimates in Eq. (
19
), we can solve the HLTE estimator by minimizing the empirical loss via

\displaystyle\hat{g}=\argmin_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\omega}^{*}_{i}g(X_{i})^{2}-2\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{i}g(X_{i}).

(20)

Note:
In practice, we implement the two-stage learner with cross-fitting
[
13
]
, instead of the two-samples-splitting strategy. This means we split data into
K
folds (e.g.,
K=5
) and then repeat the procedure above
K
times: In each iteration, we select one of the
K
folds as
\mathcal{D}_{B}
and treat all the remaining
K-1
folds as
\mathcal{D}_{A}
. The final estimator is then the average of the
K
estimators obtained from each fold.

5.3
Instantiations

Our LT-O-learners allow customized weighting functions
\omega(\pi(x),\rho(x))
to explicitly account for different forms of overlap limitations, including (i) treatment overlap and (ii) long-term outcome overlap. Below, we present instantiations tailored to common overlap scenarios.

\bullet

LT-O-DR-learner:
When we trivially set
\omega\equiv 1
, i.e. no overlap (NO) weighting in the loss, the learner uses efficient influence function of the ALTE from
10
,
2
. As a result, we obtain the LT-O-DR-learner under the standard surrogacy model. Substituting
\omega\equiv 1
into Eq. (
15
) and Eq. (
17
), we achieve the loss as follow:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}(g,\eta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)g(X)^{2}-2\mathcal{T}_{DR}\cdot g(X)\right],

(21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}(Z;\eta)

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}(R=0)\hat{\tau}_{\mathrm{AIPW}}(Z;\eta)+\mathds{1}(R=1)\psi_{\mathrm{obs}}(Z;\eta),

(22)

Problems of the LT-O-DR-learner:
Although orthogonal, the LT-O-DR-learner does not apply any sample-weighting strategy, which leads to large finite-sample variance
[
18
]
. To show this limitation, we analyze the variance of pseudo-outcome
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}
and explain why it has high variance when there is low overlap in Appendix
C.1
. As a result, the LT-O-DR-learner is not robust to low overlaps.

\bullet

LT-O-TO-learner:
This variant is designed for when only treatment overlap (TO) is problematic (e.g., because
\mathcal{D}_{1}
is small or high-dimensional, so that some subgroups have a low propensity score). We choose the weighting function
\omega(x)=\pi(x)(1-\pi(x))
, which downweights samples with limited treatment overlap.

Under this choice, the corresponding long-term weighting function simplifies to
\omega^{*}=\mathds{1}(R=0)(A-\pi(X))^{2}
. As a result, the second-stage objective reduces to a special case that is equivalent to an R-learner-style objective
[
40
]
but adapted to the long-term setting (see Appendix
B.1
for details):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\pi(1-\pi)}(g;\eta)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathds{1}(R=0)\bigl((h(S,X)-m(X))-(A-\pi(X))g(X)\bigr)^{2}-2\mathds{1}(R=1)\psi_{t}(Z;\eta)\,g(X)\Bigr],

where
m(X)=\mathbb{E}\left[h(S,X)\mid X,R=0\right]
, and
\psi_{t}(Z;\eta)=\frac{1-\rho_{s}}{\rho_{s}}\bigl(\pi_{s}-\pi\bigr)(Y-h).

\bullet

LT-O-LO-learner:
This learner is intended for settings where only long-term outcome overlap (LO) is low. In this case, we choose the weighting function
\omega(x)=\rho(x)
to downweight samples with a low probability of observing long-term outcomes. Then, the corresponding LT weighting function and the LT-pseudo-outcome can be computed via

\displaystyle\omega^{*}

\displaystyle=(R-\rho(X))^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{LT}}

\displaystyle=(R-\rho(X))^{2}(\mu_{1}(x)-\mu_{0}(x))+\begin{cases}\rho(x)\frac{A-\pi(X)}{\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))}(h(S,X)-\mu(A,X))&\text{if }R=0,\\
\rho(X)\psi_{\mathrm{obs}}(Z;\eta),&\text{if }R=1.\end{cases}

\bullet

LT-O-DO-learner:
Finally, we present an instantiation of our estimator for settings in which both treatment overlap and long-term outcome overlap are low. Here, we choose the weighting function
\omega(x)=\pi(x)(1-\pi(x))\rho(x)
, which jointly downweights samples with weak treatment overlap and low probability of observing long-term outcomes. The resulting LT weights and LT pseudo-outcomes are given by

\displaystyle\omega^{*}(Z;\eta)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\rho\big[\rho\pi(1-\pi)+(1-2\pi)(A-\pi)\big],&\text{if }R=0,\\
\pi(1-\pi)(1-\rho)^{2},&\text{if }R=1,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{LT}}(Z;\eta)

\displaystyle=(\mu(1,X)-\mu(0,X))\omega^{*}(Z;\eta)+\begin{cases}\rho(A-\pi)\big(h(S,X)-\mu(A,X)\big),&\text{if }R=0,\\
\rho\pi(1-\pi)\psi_{\mathrm{obs}}(Z;\eta),&\text{if }R=1.\end{cases}

(Error bounds)

Under the assumptions stated above, we have the following error bound:

\|\hat{g}-g_{0}\|^{2}\lesssim R_{g}+\|\hat{\mu}-\mu\|_{4}^{2}(\|\hat{\rho}-\rho\|_{4}^{2}+\|\hat{\pi}-\pi\|_{4}^{2})+\|\hat{h}-h\|_{4}^{2}(\|\hat{\pi}_{s}-\pi_{s}\|_{4}^{2}+\|\hat{\rho}_{s}-\rho_{s}\|_{4}^{2})+\|\hat{\pi}_{s}-\pi_{s}\|_{4}^{4}+\|\hat{\rho}_{s}-\rho_{s}\|_{4}^{4}

Proof.

C.4

Corollary 5.4

(Quasi-oracle rate condition)
.

Let the oracle rate be
R_{g}=O_{p}(n^{-r_{g}})
. We assume the following error rates of the nuisance estimators:

\|\hat{\mu}-\mu\|_{4}=O_{p}(n^{-k_{\mu}})
,

\|\hat{\pi}-\pi\|_{4}=O_{p}(n^{-k_{\pi}})
,

\|\hat{\rho}-\rho\|_{4}=O_{p}(n^{-k_{\rho}})
,

\|\hat{h}-h\|_{4}=O_{p}(n^{-s_{h}})
,

\|\hat{\pi}_{s}-\pi_{s}\|_{4}=O_{p}(n^{-s_{\pi}})
,

\|\hat{\rho}_{s}-\rho_{s}\|_{4}=O_{p}(n^{-s_{\rho}})
.
Then, our estimator achieves a quasi-oracle rate if

\displaystyle\max\{k_{\mu}+k_{\pi},k_{\mu}+k_{\rho},2k_{\pi},2k_{\rho},s_{h}+s_{\pi},s_{h}+s_{\rho}\}\leq\frac{r_{g}}{2}.

Theoretical benefits:
The above bound shows that the total error is decomposed into two components: (1) a quasi-oracle rate
R_{g}
, which represents the best possible performance given estimated nuisance
\hat{\eta}
, and (2) the nuisance penalty terms. Crucially, due to Neyman-orthogonality, the contribution of nuisance estimation error enters
only
through higher-order interaction terms rather than linearly, even when nuisance functions are estimated at relatively slow rates (e.g., at nonparametric rates like
n^{-1/4}
)
[
13
,
19
]
. This property permits the use of flexible machine learning models for nuisance estimation without compromising the convergence rate of the HLTE estimator.

Experiments

In this section, we conduct experiments to empirically validate our theoretical results and to demonstrate the benefits of our proposed LT-O-learners in low-overlap settings. The experiments serve two main purposes: (1)
to show that the limited overlap is a primary source of estimation instability under finite samples in standard learners
; and (2)
to demonstrate that our proposed learner addresses this challenge through Neyman-orthogonality and our overlap weighting

We evaluate the three instantiations of our LT-O-learners (see Section
5.3
). There is no direct benchmark specifically designed for our HLTE setting. Therefore, as baselines, we derive and adapt standard meta-learners, namely, a LT-T-learner, LT-RA-learner, and LT-IPW-learner to our setting (see Section

E

A.1
Identification

In this section, we briefly demonstrate the identifiability of the heterogeneous long-term treatment effects under the surrogacy model. The identification of average long-term treatment effects is first shown by
[
2
]
. We make a trivial extension to the heterogeneous setting.

Lemma A.1

(Identification of HLTE)
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
(Consistency),
3.2
(Positivity),
3.3
(Experimental Unconfoundedness),
3.4
(Surrogacy), and
3.5
(Comparability), the heterogeneous long-term treatment effect
\tau^{0}(x)
is identified as:

\displaystyle\tau^{0}(x)=\mu(1,x)-\mu(0,x),

(23)

where
\mu(a,x)=\mathbb{E}[h(S,X)\mid A=a,X=x,R=0]
and
h(s,x)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S=s,X=x,R=1]
.

Proof.

We begin with the definition of the causal estimand of interest:

\displaystyle\tau^{0}(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y^{1}-Y^{0}\mid X=x,R=0].

(24)

To estimate
\tau^{0}
, it suffices to identify the conditional expectation of the potential outcome
\mathbb{E}[Y^{a}\mid X=x,R=0]
for any
a\in\{0,1\}
.

First, using Assumption
3.1
(Consistency) and Assumption
3.3
(Unconfoundedness), we can relate the potential outcome to the conditional expectation given the treatment assignment
A=a
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{a}\mid X=x,R=0]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y^{a}\mid A=a,X=x,R=0]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid A=a,X=x,R=0].

(25)

Then, we apply the tower law for expectation by conditioning on the surrogate variable
S
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y\mid A=a,X=x,R=0]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S}\Big[\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S,A=a,X=x,R=0]\Big|A=a,X=x,R=0\Big].

(26)

We invoke Assumption
3.4
(Surrogacy), which states that
A\mathrel{\perp\mspace{-10mu}\perp}Y\mid S,X,R=0
. This allows us to remove the conditioning on
A
in the inner expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S,A=a,X=x,R=0]=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S,X,R=0].

(27)

Since the long-term outcome
Y
is not observed in the experimental dataset
\mathcal{D}_{1}
(
R=0
), we require Assumption
3.5
(Long-term outcome comparability), specifically
Y\mathrel{\perp\mspace{-10mu}\perp}R\mid S,X
, to transport the outcome model from the observational dataset
\mathcal{D}_{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S,X,R=0]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S,X,R=1]

\displaystyle=h(S,X).

(28)

Substituting this back into Eq. (
26
), we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y^{a}\mid X=x,R=0]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S}\Big[h(S,X)\Big|A=a,X=x,R=0\Big]

\displaystyle=\mu(a,x).

(29)

Hence:

\displaystyle\tau^{0}(x)=\mu(1,x)-\mu(0,x).

(30)

∎

A.2
Efficient influence function

What is the efficient influence function (EIF)?

The EIF is a fundamental concept in semiparametric statistics that describes the sensitivity of an estimator to the underlying data distribution. Formally, consider a functional
\psi(\mathbb{P})
of the data distribution
\mathbb{P}
((in our case
\psi(\mathbb{P})=\mathbb{E}_{S}[\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S,X,R=1]|A=1,X=x,R=0]-\mathbb{E}_{S}[\mathbb{E}[Y\mid S,X,R=1]|A=0,X=x,R=0]
)). The influence function
\phi(Z)
of an estimator
\hat{\psi}
is defined as the Gateaux derivative of the functional at
\mathbb{P}
in the direction of a point mass
\delta_{Z}

[
26
]
:

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\psi((1-\epsilon)\mathbb{P}+\epsilon\delta_{Z})-\psi(\mathbb{P})}{\epsilon}=\phi(Z).

(31)

To illustrate this in our context, consider the Average Long-Term Treatment Effect (ALTE),
\tau=\mathbb{E}[\tau^{0}(X)]
. The EIF for the ALTE in the standard surrogacy model is given by the centered version of the doubly-robust pseudo-outcome derived in Eq. (
22
):

\displaystyle\phi_{ALTE}(Z)=\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}(Z;\eta)-\tau.

(32)

First derived by
10
,
2
, this EIF defines the
efficiency bound
of the ALTE estimation. An estimator is asymptotically efficient (i.e., it achieves the lowest possible asymptotic variance, known as the Cramér-Rao lower bound) if and only if it is asymptotically linear with influence function equal to the EIF
[
5
,
52
]
.

Properties of EIF

The EIF has two properties that are critical for constructing robust two-stage learners:

1.

Mean Zero:
The expectation of the influence function is zero, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\phi(Z)]=0
. This implies that the uncentered EIF (or pseudo-outcome), i.e.
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}(Z)
, is an unbiased estimator of the target parameter:
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}(Z)]=\tau
.

2.

Neyman-orthogonality:
The most important property is that the EIF is insensitive to perturbations in the nuisance parameters
\eta
. Mathematically, the Gâteaux derivative of the expected EIF with respect to the nuisance functions is zero:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(Z;\eta+r(\hat{\eta}-\eta))\right]\bigg|_{r=0}=0.

(33)

This
orthogonality
condition implies that the estimation error of the nuisance functions
\hat{\eta}-\eta
affects the final target estimate
\tau
only at a second-order rate (i.e.,
\|\hat{\eta}-\eta\|^{2}
). This allows the final estimator to be still
\sqrt{n}
-consistent even if the nuisance components are estimated at slower rates (e.g.,
n^{-1/4}
), which is common when using machine learning methods
[
13
]
.

Connection between EIF and DR-pseudo-outcome

In our setting, we estimate a
conditional parameter

\tau^{0}(x)
instead of a scalar parameter
\tau^{0}
. The connection between the EIF and the
EIF-based pseudo-outcome
(or equivalently, DR-pseudo-outcome) used in DR-learner
[
33
]
is that the EIF-based pseudo-outcome is constructed to be the
uncentered
EIF of the corresponding average effect, conditioned on covariates
X
.

Specifically, let
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}(Z)
be the uncentered EIF for the average long-term effect, we use it to approximate the unobserved individual treatment effect
Y(1)-Y(0)
because:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}(Z)\mid X=x,R=0]=\tau^{0}(x)

(34)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}(Z)\mid X=x,R=1]=0.

(35)

By defining a loss function
\mathcal{L}(g)=\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}(R=0)g(X)^{2}-2\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}g(X)]
, the minimizer is the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}|X,R=0]
, which recovers
\tau^{0}(x)
. Crucially, because
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}(Z)
inherits the orthogonality property of the EIF, the second stage regression retains robustness against nuisance estimation errors.

Semantics of Neyman-orthogonality: Functional vs. Loss

In this paper we frequently use the concept of Neyman-orthogonality. We point out that actually they differ in their target object and semantic implication:

•

Functional Orthogonality:

Primarily used for scalar parameters
\theta
(e.g., ATE) defined by a moment condition
\mathbb{E}[\psi(Z;\theta,\eta)]=0
. Orthogonality requires
\partial_{\eta}\mathbb{E}[\psi(Z;\theta_{0},\eta_{0})]=0

[
13
]
. It ensures that nuisance estimation error
\hat{\eta}-\eta
only has second-order impact on the target parameter
\hat{\theta}
.

•

Loss Orthogonality:

Used for learning target functions
g(\cdot)
(e.g., CATE/HLTE) via risk minimization
\min_{g}\mathcal{L}(g,\eta)
. Orthogonality requires the
cross-derivative
to vanish:
D_{\eta}D_{g}\mathcal{L}(g_{0},\eta_{0})=0

[
19
]
.It ensures that distortions in
\hat{\eta}
may change the shape of the loss landscape but do not shift the
location
of the minimizer
g^{*}
to the first order.

A.3
EIF of the
\omega
-weighted average long-term effects

The efficient influence function for the parameter
\tau^{0}_{\omega}
in Eq. (
14
) is given by:

\displaystyle\phi(Z;\eta)=\frac{1}{p_{0}D_{\omega}}\Bigg[

\displaystyle\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\big(\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}(Z;\eta)-\tau^{0}_{\omega}\big)+\mathds{1}(R=1)\omega(X)\psi_{obs}(Z;\eta)+\big(\tau^{0}(X)-\tau^{0}_{\omega}\big)\Omega(Z;\eta)\Bigg],

(36)

where
p_{0}=P(R=0)
and
D_{\omega}=\mathbb{E}[\omega(X)|R=0]
. For simplicity, we use the shorthand
\omega(X)\equiv\omega(\pi(X),\rho(X))
. The components are defined as:

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}(Z;\eta)

\displaystyle=\mu(1,X)-\mu(0,X)+\frac{A}{\pi(X)}(h(S,X)-\mu(1,X))-\frac{1-A}{1-\pi(X)}(h(S,X)-\mu(0,X))

\displaystyle\psi_{obs}(Z;\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\rho_{s}(S,X)}{\rho_{s}(S,X)}\left(\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)-\pi(X)}{\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))}\right)\big(Y-h(S,X)\big)

(37)

\displaystyle\Omega(Z;\eta)

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}(R=0)\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\pi}(A-\pi(X))+(1-\rho(X))\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\rho}(R-\rho(X)).

(38)

Lemma A.2

(Neyman-orthogonality)
.

\phi(Z;\eta)
is the efficient influence function, which satisfies the Neyman-orthogonality condition with respect to the complete set of nuisance functions
\eta=(\pi(x),\pi_{s}(S,X),\rho(x),\rho_{s}(S,X),h(s,x),\mu(a,x))
. That is, the Gâteaux derivative of the expected EIF vanishes at the true nuisance parameters:

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(Z;\eta+r\delta)\right]\big|_{r=0}=0,

(39)

for any valid direction
\delta=\hat{\eta}-\eta
.

We provide a proof in Appendix
C.2
.

B.1
Derivation of the LT-O-TO-learner

To derive the LT-O-TO-learner, we set the weighting function to
\omega(X)=\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))
to specifically target treatment overlap. We substitute this into the LT-weighting function
\omega^{*}(Z;\eta)
in Eq. (
16
) and LT-pseudo-outcome
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{LT}}(Z;\eta)
in Eq. (
17
).

First, the residual term
\Omega(Z;\eta)
simplifies to:

\displaystyle\Omega(Z;\eta)

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}(R=0)\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\pi}(A-\pi(X))=\mathds{1}(R=0)(1-2\pi(X))(A-\pi(X)).

(40)

Then, we substitute this back into
\omega^{*}
:

\displaystyle\omega^{*}(Z;\eta)

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}(R=0)\big[\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))+(1-2\pi(X))(A-\pi(X))\big]=\mathds{1}(R=0)(A-\pi(X))^{2}.

(41)

Next, we derive the LT-pseudo-outcome
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{LT}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{LT}}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}(R=0)\Big[\pi(1-\pi)\hat{\tau}_{\mathrm{AIPW}}+(\mu_{1}-\mu_{0})(1-2\pi)(A-\pi)\Big]+\mathds{1}(R=1)\pi(1-\pi)\psi_{\mathrm{obs}}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}(R=0)\Big[(\mu_{1}-\mu_{0})(A-\pi)^{2}+(A-\pi)(h(S,X)-\mu(A,X))\Big]+\mathds{1}(R=1)\frac{1-\rho_{s}}{\rho_{s}}\big(\pi_{s}-\pi\big)\big(Y-h\big)

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}(R=0)(A-\pi)\Big[(\mu_{1}-\mu_{0})(A-\pi)+h(S,X)-A\mu_{1}-(1-A)\mu_{0}\Big]+\mathds{1}(R=1)\frac{1-\rho_{s}}{\rho_{s}}\big(\pi_{s}-\pi\big)\big(Y-h\big)

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}(R=0)(A-\pi)\Big[h(S,X)-\underbrace{(\pi\mu_{1}+(1-\pi)\mu_{0})}_{=\mathbb{E}[h(S,X)\mid X,R=0]:=m(X)}\Big]+\mathds{1}(R=1)\underbrace{\frac{1-\rho_{s}}{\rho_{s}}\big(\pi_{s}-\pi\big)\big(Y-h\big)}_{:=\psi_{t}}.

(42)

Plugging
\omega^{*}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{LT}}
into the loss
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}=\mathbb{E}_{n}[\omega^{*}g^{2}-2\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{LT}}g]
and completing the square for the
R=0
component yields the final LT-O-TO-learner loss function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\pi(1-\pi)}(g;\eta)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathds{1}(R=0)\bigl((h(S,X)-m(X))-(A-\pi(X))g(X)\bigr)^{2}-2\mathds{1}(R=1)\psi_{t}(Z;\eta),g(X)\Bigr].

(43)

This example best demonstrates the advantage of weighting: After reweighting, the inverse propensities of treatment
A
get eliminated. Therefore, the LT-O-TO-learner avoids division by treatment propensities, which makes the learner more robust against low treatment overlap.

Remark:
The LT-O-TO-learner is very similar to the standard R-learner
[
40
]
, whose second-stage loss is defined via
(Y-m(X)-(A-\pi(X))g(X))^{2}
. We point out that the
\mathds{1}(R=0)
component of our LT-O-TO-learner merely replaces
Y
with the surrogate index
h
. To account for the first-order error of
h
, the
\mathds{1}(R=1)
is added to correct this nuisance estimation error, ensuring the orthogonality of the loss.

C.1
Variance analysis

Here, we formally state the variance of the long-term DR-pseudo-outcome:

Lemma C.1

(Variance of DR-pseudo-outcome)
.

The variance of DR-pseudo-outcome is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}\mid X)\geq\underbrace{(1-\rho(X))\cdot\Sigma_{t}(X)}_{\text{treatment overlap}}+\underbrace{\rho(X)\cdot\Sigma_{\text{o}}(X)}_{\text{long-term outcome overlap}}

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}(X)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\left(\frac{1}{\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))}\right)^{2}\sigma_{h}(A,X)\,\big|\,X\Bigr]

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\text{o}}(X)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S|X}\left[\left(\frac{1-\rho_{s}(S,X)}{\rho_{s}(S,X)}\left(\frac{\pi_{s}-\pi}{\pi(1-\pi)}\right)\right)^{2}\sigma_{y}(S,X)\right],

where
\sigma_{h}(X,A)=\mathrm{Var}(h(S,X)|X,A,R=0)
,
\sigma_{y}(S,X)=\mathrm{Var}(Y|S,X,R=1)
.

This is similar to the variance analysis from
18
. When the treatment overlap
\pi(x)(1-\pi(x)
is close to zero, both
\Sigma_{t}(x)
and
\Sigma_{o}(x)
become very large. When there is limited long-term outcome overlap, namely
\rho(x)
close to zero (and thus for
\rho_{s}(S,X)
as well), we have that
\rho(x)\Sigma_{o}(x)
scales roughly with
\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{\rho_{s}(S,X)}|X=x]
. Therefore,
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}
has a high variance when at least one of the overlaps is low. As a result, the LT-O-DR-learner is
not
robust against low overlap.

Proof.

By the Law of Total Variance, conditioning on the dataset indicator
R
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}\mid X)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{R}[\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}\mid X,R)\mid X]+\mathrm{Var}_{R}(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}\mid X,R]\mid X)

(52)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{R}[\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}\mid X,R)\mid X]

(53)

where the inequality holds because variance is non-negative. Expanding the expectation over
R\in\{0,1\}
with
\rho(X)=\mathbb{P}(R=1|X)
, we obtain:

\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}\mid X)\geq(1-\rho(X))\underbrace{\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}\mid X,R=0)}_{\text{(A)}}+\rho(X)\underbrace{\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}\mid X,R=1)}_{\text{(B)}}

(54)

Part (A): Treatment Overlap Instability (
R=0
).

In the experimental dataset (
R=0
),
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}
reduces to the AIPW estimator:

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{\mathrm{AIPW}}=\frac{A-\pi(X)}{\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))}(h(S,X)-\mu(A,X))+\mu(1,X)-\mu(0,X):=Z_{0}+\mu(1,X)-\mu(0,X).

(55)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}\mid X,R=0))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\tau}_{\mathrm{AIPW}}\mid X,R=0))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\left(\frac{A-\pi(X)}{\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))}(h(S,X)-\mu(A,X))\mid X,R=0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{A}[\text{Var}(Z_{0}\mid A,X)]+\text{Var}_{A}(\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Z_{0}\mid A,X]}_{=0})

(56)

The inner expectation is zero because
\mu(A,X)=\mathbb{E}[h(S,X)|A,X]
. Thus, we are left with the expected variance:

\Sigma_{t}(X):=\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}\mid X,R=0))=\mathrm{Var}(Z_{0}\mid X)=\mathbb{E}_{A|X}\left[\left(\frac{A-\pi(X)}{\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))}\right)^{2}\mathrm{Var}(h(S,X)\mid A,X)\right]

(57)

This term scales with
\frac{1}{\pi(X)^{2}(1-\pi(X))^{2}}
, causing high variance under limited treatment overlap.

Part (B): Long-term outcome Overlap Instability (
R=1
).

In the observational dataset (
R=1
),
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}
uses the observation proxy
\psi_{\mathrm{obs}}
. Let:

Z_{1}=\frac{1-\rho_{s}(S,X)}{\rho_{s}(S,X)}W_{\pi}(S,X)(Y-h(S,X)),

where
W_{\pi}(S,X)=\left(\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)-\pi(X)}{\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))}\right)
.
Conditioning on the surrogate
S
, we get:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Z_{1}\mid X,R=1)=\mathbb{E}_{S}[\mathrm{Var}(Z_{1}\mid S,X,R=1)]+\mathrm{Var}_{S}(\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}\mid S,X,R=1]}_{=0}).

(58)

The inner expectation is zero because
h(S,X)=\mathbb{E}[Y|S,X,R=1]
. Thus:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{o}(X):=\mathrm{Var}(Z_{1}\mid X)=\mathbb{E}_{S|X}\left[\left(\frac{1-\rho_{s}(S,X)}{\rho_{s}(S,X)}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)-\pi(X)}{\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))}\right)^{2}\mathrm{Var}(Y\mid S,X)\right]

(59)

This term scales with
\frac{1}{\rho_{s}(S,X)^{2}}
, causing high variance under limited long-term outcome overlap.

Combining (A) and (B) together yields the lower bound:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{DR}}\mid X)\geq(1-\rho(X))\Sigma_{t}(X)+\rho(X)\Sigma_{o}(X)

(60)

C.2
Neyman-orthogonality

Let
\partial_{g}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(g,\eta)=S(g,\eta)\cdot\dot{g}(X)
be the derivative of the loss. By definition, the loss in Eq. (
15
) is orthogonal if we can show Neyman-orthogonality of the following term:

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\mathbb{E}[S(g,\eta+r\delta)]\big|_{r=0}=0,\quad\forall\delta=\hat{\eta}-\eta.

(61)

The reason is that
\dot{g}(X)
is irrelevant w.r.t. the nuisance
\eta
. The term can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S(g,\eta)]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)(g(X)-\hat{\tau}_{AIPW})-\mathds{1}(R=1)\omega(X)\psi_{obs}-\big((\mu(1,X)-\mu(0,X))-g(X)\big)\Omega(Z;\eta)\Big].

(62)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}(Z;\eta)

\displaystyle=\mu(1,X)-\mu(0,X)+\frac{A}{\pi(X)}(h(S,X)-\mu(1,X))-\frac{1-A}{1-\pi(X)}(h(S,X)-\mu(0,X))

\displaystyle\psi_{obs}(Z;\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\rho_{s}(S,X)}{\rho_{s}(S,X)}\left(\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)-\pi(X)}{\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))}\right)\big(Y-h(S,X)\big)

(63)

\displaystyle\Omega(Z;\eta)

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}(R=0)\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\pi}(A-\pi(X))+(1-\rho(X))\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\rho}(R-\rho(X)).

(64)

1. Orthogonality w.r.t. outcome nuisances
\mu(a,x)
:

The nuisance
\mu(a,x)
appears in
\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}
and
(\mu(1,X)-\mu(0,X))\Omega(Z;\eta)
. Let
\mu_{r}=\mu+r\delta_{\mu}
.

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\mathbb{E}[S]\Big|_{r=0}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\partial_{r}\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}+(\delta_{\mu}(1)-\delta_{\mu}(0))\Omega(Z;\eta)\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\left(\delta_{\mu}(1)-\delta_{\mu}(0)-\frac{A}{\pi}(\delta_{\mu}(1))+\frac{1-A}{1-\pi}(\delta_{\mu}(0))\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\omega(X)(1-\rho(X))\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{\mu}(1)-\delta_{\mu}(0)-\frac{A}{\pi}(\delta_{\mu}(1))+\frac{1-A}{1-\pi}(\delta_{\mu}(0))\mid X,R=0\right]\right]

(65)

Here we use the property that
\rho(x)=\mathbb{E}[R\mid X]
(by definition), and
\mathbb{E}\left[\Omega\mid X\right]=0
(see Eq. (
C
). Conditioning on
X
and
R=0
, and using
\mathbb{E}[A|X,R=0]=\pi(X)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\delta_{\mu}(1)-\delta_{\mu}(0)-\frac{A}{\pi}(\delta_{\mu}(1))+\frac{1-A}{1-\pi}(\delta_{\mu}(0))\mid X,R=0]=\delta_{\mu}(1)(1-\frac{\pi}{\pi})-\delta_{\mu}(0)(1-\frac{1-\pi}{1-\pi})=0.

(66)

Therefore, we have that
\partial_{r}\mathbb{E}[S]\Big|_{r=0}=0
.

2. Orthogonality w.r.t. surrogate outcome
h(s,x)
:

The nuisance
h
appears in both
\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}
and
\psi_{obs}
.
For the AIPW term, the derivative contribution is:

\displaystyle D_{1}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\left(\frac{A}{\pi}\delta_{h}-\frac{1-A}{1-\pi}\delta_{h}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[(1-\rho(X))\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[\omega(X)\frac{A-\pi}{\pi(1-\pi)}\delta_{h}\mid X,R=0\right]\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[(1-\rho(X))\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[\omega(X)\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)-\pi}{\pi(1-\pi)}\delta_{h}\mid X,R=0\right]\right],

(67)

For the
\psi_{obs}
term, we make use of the density ratio property that
\frac{dP(S\mid R=1,X)}{dP(S\mid R=0,X)}=\frac{\rho_{s}(S,X)}{1-\rho_{s}(S,X)}\frac{P(R=0|X)}{P(R=1|X)}
to transform the measure from the observational to the experimental sample:

\displaystyle D_{2}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=1)\omega(X)\frac{1-\rho_{s}(S,X)}{\rho_{s}(S,X)}\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)-\pi(X)}{\pi(1-\pi)}(-\delta_{h})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[\rho(X)\frac{1-\rho_{s}(S,X)}{\rho_{s}(S,X)}\omega(X)\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)-\pi}{\pi(1-\pi)}\delta_{h}\mid X,R=1\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[(1-\rho(X))\omega(X)\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)-\pi}{\pi(1-\pi)}\delta_{h}\mid X,R=0\right]\right].

(68)

Thus
D_{1}+D_{2}=0
.

3. Orthogonality w.r.t.
\pi(x)
:

Let
\pi_{r}(X)=\pi(X)+r\,\delta_{\pi}(X)
, then we take the derivative w.r.t.
r
:

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\mathbb{E}[S(g,\eta+r\delta_{\pi})]\Big|_{r=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathds{1}(R=0)\,\partial_{r}\omega_{r}(X)\Big|_{r=0}\,\big(g(X)-\hat{\tau}_{AIPW,0}\big)-\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\,\partial_{r}\hat{\tau}_{AIPW,r}\Big|_{r=0}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathds{1}(R=1)\,\partial_{r}\omega_{r}(X)\Big|_{r=0}\,\psi_{obs,0}+\mathds{1}(R=1)\omega(X)\,\partial_{r}\psi_{obs,r}\Big|_{r=0}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}\Big[\big((\mu(1,X)-\mu(0,X))-g(X)\big)\,\partial_{r}\Omega_{r}(Z)\Big|_{r=0}\Big].

(69)

First,
\partial_{r}\omega_{r}(X)|_{r=0}=\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\pi}(X)\,\delta_{\pi}(X)
. Then

\displaystyle F_{1}:=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathds{1}(R=0)\,\partial_{r}\omega_{r}(X)\Big|_{r=0}\,\big(g(X)-\hat{\tau}_{AIPW,0}\big)\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathds{1}(R=0)\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\pi}(X)\,\delta_{\pi}(X)(g(X)-\tau^{0}(X))\Big]

(70)

Second, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\partial_{r}\hat{\tau}_{AIPW,r}\Big|_{r=0}\mid X\right]

(71)

\displaystyle=(1-\rho(X))\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{r}\Bigl(\frac{A}{\pi_{r}(X)}\Bigr)\Big|_{r=0}(h(S,X)-\mu(1,X))-\partial_{r}\Bigl(\frac{1-A}{1-\pi_{r}(X)}\Bigr)\Big|_{r=0}(h(S,X)-\mu(0,X))\mid X,R=0\right]

\displaystyle=(1-\rho(X))\mathbb{E}\left[A\frac{-1}{\pi(X)^{2}}\delta_{\pi}(X)(h(S,X)-\mu(1,X))-(1-A)\frac{1}{(1-\pi(X))^{2}}\delta_{\pi}(X)(h(S,X)-\mu(0,X))\mid X,R=0\right]

\displaystyle=(1-\rho(X))\delta_{\pi}(X)\Biggl(\frac{-1}{\pi(X)^{2}}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[A(h(S,X)-\mu(1,X))\mid X,R=0\right]}_{=0}-\frac{1}{(1-\pi(X))^{2}}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[(1-A)(h(S,X)-\mu(0,X))\mid X,R=0\right]}_{=0}\Biggr)

\displaystyle=0

(72)

The last equation is zero because
\forall a\in\{0,1\},\quad\mathbb{E}\left[h(S,X)-\mu(a,X)\mid A=a,X,R=0\right]=0
. Hence

\displaystyle F_{2}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\omega(X)\mathds{1}(R=0)\partial_{r}\hat{\tau}_{AIPW,r}\Big|_{r=0}\right]=0.

(73)

From the property in Eq. (
C
), we have that
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=1)\psi_{obs}\mid X\right]=0
. Furthermore, with the exact same derivation, we also have
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=1)\partial_{r}\psi_{obs,r}\mid X\right]=0
(because the
Y-h(S,X)
is mean zero). Hence, the second row in Eq. (
69
) can be simplified to

\displaystyle F_{3}:=-

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathds{1}(R=1)\,\partial_{r}\omega_{r}(X)\Big|_{r=0}\,\psi_{obs,0}+\mathds{1}(R=1)\omega(X)\,\partial_{r}\psi_{obs,r}\Big|_{r=0}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\partial_{r}\omega_{r}(X)\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=1)\Big|_{r=0}\,\psi_{obs,0}\mid X\right]+\omega(X)\,\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=1)\partial_{r}\psi_{obs,r}\Big|_{r=0}\mid X\right]\Big]=0.

(74)

Finally, we address the terms involving
\partial_{r}\Omega_{r}|_{r=0}
.
Since
\Omega_{r}=\mathds{1}(R=0)\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\pi_{r}}(A-\pi_{r}(X))+(1-\rho(X))\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\rho}(R-\rho(X))
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\Omega_{r}\Big|_{r=0}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}(R=0)\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\omega}{\partial\pi^{2}}(X)\,\delta_{\pi}(X)\,(A-\pi(X))-\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\pi}(X)\,\delta_{\pi}(X)\right].

(75)

Conditioning on
X
and
R=0
, and using
\mathbb{E}[A-\pi(X)\mid X,R=0]=0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\partial_{r}\Omega_{r}\Big|_{r=0}\ \Bigm|\ X\right]

\displaystyle=(1-\rho(X))\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\omega}{\partial\pi^{2}}(X)\,\delta_{\pi}(X)\,\mathbb{E}[A-\pi\mid X,R=0]-\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\pi}(X)\,\delta_{\pi}(X)\right]

\displaystyle=-(1-\rho(X))\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\pi}(X)\,\delta_{\pi}(X).

(76)

Therefore,

\displaystyle F_{4}:=-\mathbb{E}\Big[\big((\mu(1,X)-\mu(0,X))-g(X)\big)\,\partial_{r}\Omega_{r}\Big|_{r=0}\Big]

\displaystyle=(1-\rho(X))\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\tau^{0}(X)-g(X)\big)\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\pi}(X)\delta_{\pi}(X)\Big].

(77)

Putting everything together, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\mathbb{E}[S(g,\eta+r\delta_{\pi})]\Big|_{r=0}=

\displaystyle F_{1}+F_{2}+F_{3}+F_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathds{1}(R=0)\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\pi}(X)\,\delta_{\pi}(X)(g(X)-\tau^{0}(X))\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[(1-\rho(X))\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\pi}(X)\,\delta_{\pi}(X)\big(\tau^{0}(X)-g(X)\big)\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(78)

4. Orthogonality w.r.t.
\pi_{s}(s,x),\rho_{s}(S,X)
:

Let
\pi_{r}(S,X)=\pi_{s}(S,X)+r\,\delta_{\pi_{s}}(S,X)
. Since
\pi_{s}(S,X)
only appears in
\psi_{obs}
, therefore

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\psi_{obs,r}\Big|_{r=0}=\left(\partial_{r}(\frac{1-\rho_{s}(S,X)}{\rho_{s}(S,X)}\left(\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)-\pi(X)}{\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))}\right))\Big|_{r=0}\right)\big(Y-h(S,X)\big).

(79)

Conditioning on
(S,X,R=1)
, we have
\mathbb{E}[Y-h(S,X)\mid S,X,R=1]=0
, and therefore by tower law

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\mathbb{E}[S(g,\eta+r\delta_{\pi_{s}})]\Big|_{r=0}=-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=1)\omega(X)\,\partial_{r}\psi_{obs,r}\Big|_{r=0}\right]=0.

(80)

The same derivation applies for
\rho_{s}(S,X)
.

5. Orthogonality w.r.t.
\rho(x)
:

Let
\rho_{r}(X)=\rho(X)+r\,\delta_{\rho}(X)
. The nuisance
\rho(X)
appears in
\omega(X)
and in
\Omega(Z;\eta)
, but not in
\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}
or
\psi_{obs}
.
Differentiating the gradient yields two types of terms:

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\mathbb{E}[S(g,\eta+r\delta_{\rho})]\Big|_{r=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\partial_{r}\omega_{r}(X)\Big|_{r=0}\cdot\big(\mathds{1}(R=0)(g-\hat{\tau}_{AIPW})-\mathds{1}(R=1)\psi_{obs}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}\Big[\big((\mu(1)-\mu(0))-g\big)\,\partial_{r}\Omega_{r}\Big|_{r=0}\Big].

(81)

For the first term, using properties in Eq. (
51
) and Eq. (
C
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\partial_{r}\omega_{r}(X)\Big|_{r=0}\cdot\big(\mathds{1}(R=0)(g-\hat{\tau}_{AIPW})-\mathds{1}(R=1)\psi_{obs}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{X}\Bigg[\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\rho}(X)\,\delta_{\rho}(X)\;\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathds{1}(R=0)\big(g(X)-\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}\big)-\mathds{1}(R=1)\psi_{obs}\mid X\Big]\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{X}\Bigg[\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\rho}(X)\,\delta_{\rho}(X)\;\Big(\Pr(R=0\mid X)\mathbb{E}[g(X)-\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}\mid X,R=0]-\Pr(R=1\mid X)\mathbb{E}[\psi_{obs}\mid X,R=1]\Big)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{X}\Bigg[\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\rho}(X)\,\delta_{\rho}(X)\;(1-\rho(X))\big(g(X)-\tau^{0}(X)\big)\Bigg].

(82)

For the second term, only the second component of
\Omega
depends on
\rho(X)
:

\displaystyle\Omega_{r}(Z)=(1-\rho_{r}(X))\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\rho_{r}}(X)\big(R-\rho_{r}(X)\big).

(83)

Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{r}\Omega_{r}|_{r=0}\mid X\right]=(1-\rho_{r}(X))\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\rho_{r}}(X)\cdot-\delta_{\rho}

(84)

Since
(\mu(1,X)-\mu(0,X))-g(X)
is
X
-measurable,

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\Big[\big((\mu(1)-\mu(0))-g\big)\,\partial_{r}\Omega_{r}\Big|_{r=0}\Big]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{X}\Big[\big((\mu(1,X)-\mu(0,X))-g(X)\big)\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\partial_{r}\Omega_{r}\Big|_{r=0}\ \Bigm|\ X\right]\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\Big[\big((\mu(1,X)-\mu(0,X))-g(X)\big)\,(1-\rho(X))\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\rho}(X)\,\delta_{\rho}(X)\Big].

(85)

Adding the terms (
82
) and (
85
) together, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\mathbb{E}[S(g,\eta+r\delta_{\rho})]\Big|_{r=0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\Big[(1-\rho(X))\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\rho}(X)\,\delta_{\rho}(X)\Big\{\big(g(X)-\tau^{0}(X)\big)+\big(\mu(1,X)-\mu(0,X)-g(X)\big)\Big\}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\Big[(1-\rho(X))\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\rho}(X)\,\delta_{\rho}(X)\cdot 0\Big]=0.

(86)

Combining these results, the total derivative
\partial_{r}\mathcal{E}(\eta+r\delta)|_{r=0}=0
, proving Neyman-orthogonality.

C.3
Proof of Theorem
5.2

The loss
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(g;\eta)
is, up to a constant (w.r.t.
g
), equivalent with:

\mathcal{R}_{\omega}(g;\eta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\big(\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}-g\big)^{2}-2\mathds{1}(R=1)\omega(X)\psi_{obs}\cdot g+\Omega\big((\mu(1,X)-\mu(0,X))-g\big)^{2}\right].

(87)

Recall that

\psi_{obs}(Z;\eta)=\frac{1-\rho_{s}(S,X)}{\rho_{s}(S,X)}\left(\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)-\pi(X)}{\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))}\right)\big(Y-h(S,X)\big).

(88)

Hence, the conditional expectation of
\mathds{1}(R=1)\psi_{obs}(Z;\eta)
given
X
equals zero:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=1)\psi_{obs}(Z;\eta)\mid X\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=1)\frac{1-\rho_{s}(S,X)}{\rho_{s}(S,X)}\left(\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)-\pi(X)}{\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))}\right)\big(Y-h(S,X)\big)\Bigg|X\right]

(89)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=1)\frac{1-\rho_{s}(S,X)}{\rho_{s}(S,X)}\left(\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)-\pi(X)}{\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))}\right)\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y-h(S,X)\mid S,X,R=1]}_{=0}\Bigg|X\right]

(90)

\displaystyle=0.

(91)

Note that the conditional expectation of
\Omega(Z;\eta)
given
X
is also zero:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Omega(Z;\eta)\mid X\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\pi}(A-\pi(X))+(1-\rho(X))\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\rho}(R-\rho(X))\Bigg|X\right]

(92)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\pi}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)(A-\pi(X))\mid X\right]+(1-\rho(X))\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\rho}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[R-\rho(X)\mid X\right]}_{=0}

(93)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\pi}\Pr(R=0|X)\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[A-\pi(X)\mid X,R=0\right]}_{=0}=0.

(94)

Hence, the risk function simplifies to

\mathcal{R}_{\omega}(g;\eta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\big(\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}(Z;\eta)-g(X)\big)^{2}\right],

(95)

where

\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}(Z;\eta)=\mu(1,X)-\mu(0,X)+\frac{A}{\pi(X)}(h(S,X)-\mu(1,X))-\frac{1-A}{1-\pi(X)}(h(S,X)-\mu(0,X)).

(96)

We further write the Eq. (
95
) as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\omega}(g;\eta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\big(\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}(Z;\eta)-g(X)\big)^{2}\right]

(97)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\big(\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}(Z;\eta)-\tau^{0}(X)+\tau^{0}(X)-g(X)\big)^{2}\right]

(98)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\bigl(\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}(Z;\eta)-\tau^{0}(X)\bigr)^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\bigl(\tau^{0}(X)-g(X)\bigr)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+2\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\bigl(\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}(Z;\eta)-\tau^{0}(X)\bigr)\bigl(\tau^{0}(X)-g(X)\bigr)\right]

(99)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\bigl(\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}(Z;\eta)-\tau^{0}(X)\bigr)^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\bigl(\tau^{0}(X)-g(X)\bigr)^{2}\right]

(100)

\displaystyle=p_{0}\mathbb{E}\left[\omega(X)\bigl(\tau^{0}(X)-g(X)\bigr)^{2}\mid R=0\right]+\text{Const}_{g}

(101)

\displaystyle\propto p_{0}\mathbb{E}\left[\omega(X)\bigl(\tau^{0}(X)-g(X)\bigr)^{2}\mid R=0\right]=\mathcal{L}^{\text{oracle}}_{\omega}(g;\eta).

(102)

The reason the cross term in the decomposition is zero is because

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\bigl(\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}(Z;\eta)-\tau^{0}(X)\bigr)\bigl(\tau^{0}(X)-g(X)\bigr)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\bigl(\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}(Z;\eta)-\tau^{0}(X)\bigr)\bigl(\tau^{0}(X)-g(X)\bigr)\mid X\right]\right]

(103)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)\bigl(\tau^{0}(X)-g(X)\bigr)\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}(Z;\eta)-\tau^{0}(X)\mid X,R=0\right]}_{=0}\right]

(104)

\displaystyle=0.

(105)

Therefore we successfully show that
\mathcal{R}_{\omega}(g;\eta)
has the same minimizer as the oracle loss
\mathcal{L}^{\text{oracle}}_{\omega}(g;\eta)
. Since
\mathcal{R}_{\omega}(g;\eta)
is also equivalent with
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(g;\eta)
, we finish the proof.

C.4
Proof of Error bounds

C.4.1
Assumptions

Here, we give the assumptions for deriving the error bound.

Assumption C.2

(Nondegenerate overlap)
.

There exists
\alpha_{\pi}>0,\alpha_{\rho}>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall x,\quad\alpha_{\pi}<\pi(x)<1-\alpha_{\pi},\quad\rho(x)>\alpha_{\rho}.

(106)

Assumption C.3

(Boundedness)
.

(i) All nuisance estimators are bounded, i.e.,
\exists M>0,r>0
, s.t.
\forall\hat{\eta}\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\eta,\mathcal{G})
,
\|\hat{\eta}\|\leq M
. (ii) All random variables are bounded.
(iii)
\exists\alpha_{\omega}>0,r>0
, s.t.
\forall\hat{\eta}\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\eta,\mathcal{G})
, we have
\hat{\omega}(X)>\alpha_{\omega}
.

Assumption C.4

(Smoothness of
\omega
)
.

The weighting function
\omega(x)
is lower bounded by a positive
\alpha_{\omega}>0
and has at least a second-order derivative. Furthermore, the spectral radius of the Hessian matrix below is always bounded:

\displaystyle\text{sup}_{X\in\mathcal{X}}\lambda_{\max}(\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla^{2}_{\eta}\omega^{*}(Z;\eta)\mid X\right])<+\infty.

(107)

Assumption C.5
.

\exists\alpha>0,r>0
,
s.t.

\forall\hat{\eta}\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\eta,\mathcal{G})
,

\displaystyle\text{inf}_{g\in\mathcal{G}/\{g_{0}\}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\omega^{*}(Z;\hat{\eta})(g(X)-g_{0}(X))^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[(g(X)-g_{0}(X))^{2}\right]}=\alpha>0,

(108)

Assumptions
C.2
,
C.3
,
C.4
are standard in causal inference literature
[
19
,
35
]
. Assumption
C.5
is easy to satisfy since
\omega^{*}(Z;\eta)=\mathds{1}(R=0)\omega(X)+\Omega(Z;\eta)
, with
\omega(X)>\alpha_{\omega}>0
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\Omega(Z;\eta)\mid X\right]=0
.

C.4.2
Proof

We adopt a similar proof as in
[
19
,
39
]
to derive the error bound for orthogonal learners. We use (functional) Taylor-expansion w.r.t.
g
to the population loss
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(g,\hat{\eta})
around
g_{0}
to get that
\exists\bar{g}\in B(g_{0},\mathcal{G})
s.t.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(\hat{g},\hat{\eta})=\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(g_{0},\hat{\eta})+D_{g}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(g_{0},\hat{\eta})[\hat{g}-g_{0}]+\frac{1}{2}D_{g}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(\bar{g},\hat{\eta})[\hat{g}-g_{0},\hat{g}-g_{0}].

(109)

We can compute the second-order term:

\displaystyle D_{g}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(\bar{g},\hat{\eta})[\hat{g}-g_{0},\hat{g}-g_{0}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t_{1}\partial t_{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\omega}^{*}(\widehat{\mathcal{T}}-\bar{g}(X)-t_{1}(\hat{g}(X)-g_{0}(X))-t_{2}(\hat{g}(X)-g_{0}(X))\right]\Bigg|_{t_{1}=0,t_{2}=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\omega}^{*}(\hat{g}(X)-g_{0}(X))^{2}\right]

(110)

With the assumption that

\displaystyle\text{inf}_{g\in\mathcal{G}/\{g_{0}\}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\omega^{*}(g(X)-g_{0}(X))^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[(g(X)-g_{0}(X))^{2}\right]}=\alpha>0,

(111)

We have

\displaystyle D_{g}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(\bar{g},\hat{\eta})[\hat{g}-g_{0},\hat{g}-g_{0}]\geq\alpha\|\hat{g}-g_{0}\|_{2}^{2}.

(112)

We substitute the inequality back into Eq. (
109
) and achieve an primary upper bound for
\|\hat{g}-g_{0}\|_{2}^{2}
:

\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\hat{g}-g_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(\hat{g},\hat{\eta})-\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(g_{0},\hat{\eta})}_{:=R_{g}\text{ oracle excess risk}}-D_{g}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(g_{0},\hat{\eta})[\hat{g}-g_{0}]

(113)

In the following proof, we show that
D_{g}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(g_{0},\hat{\eta})[\hat{g}-g_{0}]
can be decomposed into second-order cross multiplication of nuisance errors.

\displaystyle D_{g}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}(g_{0},\hat{\eta})[\hat{g}-g_{0}]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\underbrace{\mathds{1}(R=0)\hat{\omega}(X)\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}+\mathds{1}(R=1)\hat{\omega}(X)\hat{\psi}_{obs}}_{\text{Term (1)}}+\underbrace{\big((\hat{\mu}_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{0}\big)\hat{\Omega}-\hat{\omega^{*}}g_{0}}_{\text{Term (2)}}\right]

(114)

We first analyze Term (1). The AIPW pseudo-outcome
\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}
can be decomposed into:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}\mid X,R=0\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\mu}_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{0}+\frac{A}{\hat{\pi}}(\hat{h}-\hat{\mu}_{1})-\frac{1-A}{1-\hat{\pi}}(\hat{h}-\hat{\mu}_{0})\mid X,R=0\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\underbrace{\hat{\mu}_{1}(X)-\hat{\mu}_{0}(X)+\frac{A}{\pi}(h-\hat{\mu}_{1}(X))-\frac{1-A}{1-\pi}(h-\hat{\mu}_{0}(X))}_{\tau^{0}(X)}\mid X,R=0\Bigr]

(115)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\underbrace{\left(\frac{A}{\pi}-\frac{A}{\hat{\pi}}\right)(h-\hat{\mu}_{1})-\left(\frac{1-A}{1-\pi}-\frac{1-A}{1-\hat{\pi}}\right)(h-\hat{\mu}_{0})}_{\text{Bias}(\mu,\pi)}\mid X,R=0\Bigr]

(116)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{A}{\hat{\pi}}-\frac{1-A}{1-\hat{\pi}}\right)\delta_{h}(S,X)\mid X,R=0\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tau^{0}(X)+\text{Bias}(\mu,\pi)+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)}{\hat{\pi}(X)}-\frac{1-\pi_{s}(S,X)}{1-\hat{\pi}(X)}\right)\delta_{h}(S,X)\mid X,R=0\right]}_{\text{First-order bias}}.

(117)

The first brace equals
\tau^{0}(X)
because the use of the true propensity score
\pi
eliminates the bias from
\hat{\mu}
(double robustness):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\mu}_{1}+\frac{A}{\pi}(h-\hat{\mu}_{1})\mid X,R=0\right]

(118)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mu}_{1}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[A|X,R=0]}{\pi}(\mathbb{E}[h|X,A=1,R=0]-\hat{\mu}_{1})

(119)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mu}_{1}+\frac{\pi}{\pi}(\mu_{1}-\hat{\mu}_{1})=\mu_{1}.\quad(\text{Same goes for }\mu_{0})

The second brace is the product of nuisance error between

\pi

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\pi(X)}-\frac{1}{\hat{\pi}(X)}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[(h(S,X)-\hat{\mu}_{1})\mid X,A=1,R=0\right]

(122)

\displaystyle=\pi(X)\left(\frac{\hat{\pi}(X)-\pi(X)}{\pi(X)\hat{\pi}(X)}\right)(\mu_{1}(X)-\hat{\mu}_{1}(X))=\frac{\hat{\pi}(X)-\pi(X)}{\hat{\pi}(X)}(\mu_{1}(X)-\hat{\mu}_{1}(X)).

Since

is bounded from 0 and 1 (overlap assumption), there exists a constant

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\left(\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)}{\hat{\pi}(X)}-\frac{1-\pi_{s}(S,X)}{1-\hat{\pi}(X)}\right)\delta_{h}(S,X)\mid X\right]

Then, we analyze the correction term
\hat{\psi}_{obs}

,
C_{\pi}(S,X):=\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)-\pi(X)}{\pi(X)(1-\pi(X))}
and analyze the conditional expectation of

given

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=1)\hat{\omega}(X)\hat{\psi}_{obs}\mid X\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=1)\hat{\omega}(X)\hat{\gamma}(S,X)\left(\frac{\hat{\pi}_{s}(S,X)-\hat{\pi}(X)}{\hat{\pi}(X)(1-\hat{\pi}(X))}\right)(Y-\hat{h})\mid X\right]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=1)\hat{\omega}(X)\hat{\gamma}(S,X)\hat{C}_{\pi}(S,X)(-\delta_{h}(S,X))\mid X\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\frac{\hat{\gamma}(S,X)}{\gamma_{0}(S,X)}\hat{\omega}(X)\hat{C}_{\pi}(S,X)\delta_{h}(S,X)\mid X\right].

Here we make use of the density ratio property that

to transform the measure from the observational to the experimental sample.
Combining the two parts above, we have:

Term (1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\hat{\omega}(X)\hat{\tau}_{AIPW}+\mathds{1}(R=1)\hat{\omega}(X)\hat{\psi}_{obs}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\hat{\omega}(X)\left(\tau^{0}(X)+\text{Bias}(\mu,\pi)+\delta_{h}(S,X)\underbrace{\left(\left[\frac{\pi_{s}(S,X)-\hat{\pi}(X)}{\hat{\pi}(1-\hat{\pi})}\right]-\frac{\hat{\gamma}}{\gamma_{0}}\hat{C}_{\pi}\right)}_{\text{Residual }\mathcal{R}_{h}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}(R=0)\hat{\omega}(X)\tau^{0}(X)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[C_{0}|\hat{\pi}-\pi|\left(|\hat{\mu}_{1}-\mu_{1}|+|\hat{\mu}_{1}-\mu_{1}|\right)\right]

Appendix E
Baselines

We briefly explain the baseline weighted learners used in the experiments.

Weighted RA-learner:
For the weighted version of the learner, we simply add weights to all the samples during the second-stage pseudo-outcome regression. Specifically, the
weighted
RA-learner minimizes the following loss function:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{\omega,RA}}(g;\eta)=\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\omega(X)(g(X)-\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{RA}})^{2}\mid R=0\right],\;g\in\mathcal{G}

(171)

Weighted DR-learner:
The weighted DR-learner minimizes the following loss function in the second stage:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{\omega,DR}}(g;\eta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\omega(X)\bigl(\mathds{1}(R=0)g(X)^{2}-2\mathcal{T}_{DR}\cdot g(X)\bigr)\right],\;g\in\mathcal{G}

(172)

Appendix F
Implementation

All experiments are run on Intel Core Ultra 7 155U (1.70 GHz) CPU and 32GB RAM. Data processing and model training are all implemented with Python. We provide the source code at
https://anonymous.4open.science/r/LT-learner-for-Long-term-HTE-5041
.

Model architectures:
We instantiate a separate multi-layer perceptron (MLP) for each sub-regression task. Neural networks that estimate propensity nuisance functions
(\rho,\rho_{s},\pi,\pi_{s})
have two middle layers and a sigmoid output transformation at the end. Networks used to estimate outcome-related nuisances, as well as the second-stage predictor optimized under the meta-learner objective, consist of four hidden layers. All hidden layers use ReLU activations. Each learner instantiates the subset of these networks required by its algorithm. This ensures that all baselines use the same architecture. As a result, any differences in performance can be attributed to the meta-algorithm itself rather than model capacity.

Training:
Hidden-layer widths are set to either
(20,10)
(two-layer MLPs) or
(20,20,10,10)
(four-layer MLPs). Nuisance networks are trained for 20 epochs with batch size 64 and learning rate
10^{-3}
. The second-stage network is trained for 40 epochs with learning rate
10^{-3}
.
