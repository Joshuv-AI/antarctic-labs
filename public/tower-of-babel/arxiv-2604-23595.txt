Plug-and-Play Consistency Models for MIMO Channel Estimation
Jinlong Li, Peng Yang, Zehui Xiong, Xianbin Cao

Abstract
Consistency models (CMs) learn a consistent mapping from multiple noise levels to the data endpoint and can therefore perform generative inference in one or a few steps. This property makes them attractive as learned priors for low-latency inverse problems. Multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) channel estimation under limited pilot overhead can be formulated as a high-dimensional linear inverse problem with an explicit measurement matrix, where data consistency alone is often insufficient for stable angular-domain channel recovery. This paper applies the plug-and-play consistency model (PnP-CM) framework to pilot-aided MIMO channel estimation. The PnP-CM inference procedure enforces the pilot observation model in the data-consistency update and invokes a pretrained CM denoiser in the prior update, thereby recovering the angular-domain channel vector within a small number of outer iterations. Preliminary experiments validate the feasibility of using CMs as low-latency channel-estimation priors and show that adaptive parameter scheduling and cross-scenario robustness remain important directions for further improvement.



I
Introduction

Massive antenna arrays, wideband transmission, and increasingly stringent pilot-overhead constraints make channel estimation a high-dimensional inverse problem with limited observations. Conventional estimators usually rely on explicit statistical models or handcrafted structural assumptions, whereas neural-network-based approaches can learn more flexible channel distributions directly from data. Along this line, generative models move beyond deterministic regression and seek to model the underlying channel distribution. Gaussian mixture models
[
11
]
, mixtures of factor analyzers
[
7
]
, generative adversarial networks
[
2
]
, and variational autoencoders
[
3
]
have all been investigated as data-driven priors for wireless channel estimation.

Among existing generative approaches, diffusion and score-based methods have attracted substantial attention. Score-based generative models were introduced for MIMO channel estimation in
[
1
]
, where recovery is formulated as posterior sampling conditioned on observations. Diffusion priors have further been used for high-dimensional channel estimation
[
14
]
, low-complexity angular-domain recovery
[
6
]
, and plug-and-play (PnP) channel estimation in millimeter-wave and terahertz ultra-massive MIMO systems
[
9
]
. These studies indicate that diffusion models can represent complex high-dimensional channel distributions and provide more flexible priors than purely sparse or low-order statistical models.

The performance of diffusion-based methods, however, typically relies on multi-step sampling or iterative posterior correction, which increases online latency and computational cost. This issue is particularly relevant for channel estimation, where fast online processing is often required
[
6
,
5
]
. CMs learn a mapping from any point on a noise trajectory to the clean data endpoint and can approximate diffusion-style generation in one or a few steps
[
12
]
. They are therefore well suited as generative priors for low-latency channel estimation.

A generative prior alone is not sufficient for pilot-constrained channel recovery; it must be combined with the explicit observation model. PnP methods provide a general framework for inverse problems by alternating between data-consistency updates and pretrained prior modules
[
4
]
. In this framework,
[
8
]
interprets CMs as plug-and-play priors and incorporates them into PnP alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM), achieving a favorable tradeoff between reconstruction quality and inference cost with few iterations. Since pilot-aided MIMO channel estimation naturally admits a linear measurement formulation, this paper introduces PnP-CM into angular-domain MIMO channel estimation, where a CM learns the angular-domain channel prior and the data-consistency step incorporates pilot observations.

II
System Model and Consistency Model Prior

II-A

MIMO System Model

Consider a MIMO system with
N_{t}
transmit antennas and
N_{r}
receive antennas. During channel estimation, let
M_{t}
and
M_{r}
denote the numbers of transmit and receive training scans, respectively, so that the total pilot overhead is
M=M_{t}M_{r}
. The received signal is

\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{RF}}^{H}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X}_{p}+\mathbf{N},

(1)

where
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{r}\times N_{t}}
is the spatial-domain channel matrix,
\mathbf{X}_{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{t}\times M_{t}}
is the transmit pilot matrix,
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{RF}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{r}\times M_{r}N_{\mathrm{RF}}^{r}}
is the training combiner, and
\mathbf{N}\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{r}N_{\mathrm{RF}}^{r}\times M_{t}}
is the noise matrix.

To exploit the angular structure of large-scale MIMO channels, discrete Fourier transform (DFT) dictionaries
\mathbf{F}_{t}
and
\mathbf{F}_{r}
are introduced, and the angular-domain channel is defined as

\mathbf{H}_{a}=\mathbf{F}_{r}^{H}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{F}_{t}.

(2)

Substituting (
2
) into (
1
) and vectorizing the observation gives

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{n},

(3)

where
\mathbf{h}=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{H}_{a})\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{t}N_{r}\times 1}
is the vectorized angular-domain channel,
\mathbf{y}=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{Y})\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{t}M_{r}N_{\mathrm{RF}}^{r}\times 1}
is the received observation vector, and

\mathbf{A}=\left(\mathbf{X}_{p}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{F}_{t}}\right)\otimes\left(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{RF}}^{H}\mathbf{F}_{r}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{M_{t}M_{r}N_{\mathrm{RF}}^{r}\times N_{t}N_{r}}

(4)

is the equivalent measurement matrix. The vector
\mathbf{n}=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{N})
denotes the vectorized measurement noise.

II-B

Consistency Model Prior

A CM is a fast generative model that learns a mapping
f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
from an arbitrary noise level on a probability-flow ordinary differential equation (PF ODE) trajectory to the same clean data endpoint
[
12
]
. For any two time instants
t,t^{\prime}\in[\epsilon,T]
on the same PF ODE trajectory, the CM satisfies the self-consistency constraint

f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)=f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t^{\prime}},t^{\prime}),\quad\forall t,t^{\prime}\in[\epsilon,T].

(5)

Here,
\epsilon
is a small positive constant. Since the trajectory is close to the data endpoint at
t=\epsilon
, the consistency function should satisfy the boundary condition

f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{\epsilon},\epsilon)=\mathbf{x}_{\epsilon}.

(6)

To enforce this boundary condition in the neural parameterization,
[
12
]
uses a skip-connection form

f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t)=c_{\mathrm{skip}}(t)\mathbf{x}+c_{\mathrm{out}}(t)F_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t),

(7)

where
F_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},t)
is an unconstrained neural network, and
c_{\mathrm{skip}}(t)
and
c_{\mathrm{out}}(t)
are differentiable functions satisfying
c_{\mathrm{skip}}(\epsilon)=1
and
c_{\mathrm{out}}(\epsilon)=0
. Thus,
f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{\epsilon},\epsilon)=\mathbf{x}_{\epsilon}
at the data endpoint.

Unlike conventional diffusion models that rely on long reverse sampling chains, CMs can perform one-step inference and may use a small number of refinement steps when needed
[
12
]
. They can be trained either by consistency distillation from a pretrained diffusion model or by independent consistency training
[
12
]
. This low-step inference property makes CMs suitable for channel-estimation tasks with strict latency requirements. If the clean angular-domain channel is regarded as the data variable
\mathbf{h}_{0}
, then for a noisy intermediate estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{h}}_{t^{\star}}
derived from pilot observations, a CM-based estimate can be written as

\widehat{\mathbf{h}}_{0}=f_{\theta}(\widehat{\mathbf{h}}_{t^{\star}},t^{\star}).

(8)

The angular-domain matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{a}
and the spatial-domain channel can then be recovered from
\widehat{\mathbf{h}}_{0}
. It should be noted, however, that pilot noise is introduced in the observation domain, whereas CMs are typically trained with additive perturbations in the channel-variable domain. Therefore, an intermediate estimate obtained from the observation model does not necessarily follow the same Gaussian perturbation model used during CM training, and its effective noise level depends on both the measurement matrix and the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).

III
PnP-CM for MIMO Channel Estimation

This section applies PnP-CM to the pilot-aided MIMO channel-estimation problem in (
3
). Because the number of pilot observations is often smaller than or comparable to the channel dimension, data consistency alone may lead to unstable estimates. PnP-ADMM alternates between an explicit data-consistency update and a learned prior update, providing a natural way to combine the measurement model with a pretrained CM prior
[
4
,
8
]
.

Under complex Gaussian measurement noise
\mathbf{n}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{n}^{2}\mathbf{I})
, the maximum a posteriori estimate of the angular-domain channel vector can be written as

\widehat{\mathbf{h}}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{h}}\frac{1}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}\|_{2}^{2}+g(\mathbf{h}),

(9)

where
g(\mathbf{h})
denotes the angular-domain channel prior. Introducing auxiliary variables
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{x}
with the constraint
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{x}
gives

\min_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{z})+g(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{x},

(10)

where
f(\mathbf{z})=(2\sigma_{n}^{2})^{-1}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}
. With the scaled dual variable
\mathbf{u}
, the standard ADMM updates are

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbf{z}}f(\mathbf{z})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x}^{(k)}+\mathbf{u}^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}}g(\mathbf{x})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\mathbf{z}^{(k+1)}-\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u}^{(k)}\|_{2}^{2},

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{(k)}+\mathbf{z}^{(k+1)}-\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}.

(13)

The
\mathbf{z}
subproblem has the closed-form solution

\mathbf{z}^{(k+1)}=\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\mathbf{A}^{H}\mathbf{A}+\rho\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\mathbf{A}^{H}\mathbf{y}+\rho(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{u}^{(k)})\right).

(14)

In the implementation, the noise-variance factor is absorbed into an SNR-dependent effective penalty parameter. Hence, the linear data-consistency step is written as

\mathbf{z}=\left(\mathbf{A}^{H}\mathbf{A}+\rho\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{A}^{H}\mathbf{y}+\rho(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\widehat{\mathbf{u}})\right),

(15)

and the resulting linear system is solved by the conjugate-gradient (CG) method.

To align the ADMM iterations with the CM noise schedule, the algorithm uses a reverse time index. Let
N
be the number of outer iterations. The variables are initialized as
\mathbf{x}_{N}=\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{u}_{N}=\mathbf{0}
,
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{N}=\mathbf{0}
, and
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{N}=\mathbf{0}
, and the loop proceeds for
n=N-1,\ldots,0
. The data-consistency update at the
n
th reverse step is

\mathbf{z}_{n}=\left(\mathbf{A}^{H}\mathbf{A}+\rho_{n+1}\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{A}^{H}\mathbf{y}+\rho_{n+1}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{n+1}-\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{n+1})\right).

(16)

The proximal operator in the
\mathbf{x}
subproblem is replaced by a pretrained CM denoiser. Following the active-noising strategy in PnP-CM
[
8
]
, a noise level
t_{n+1}
is selected and

\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},t_{n+1}^{2}\mathbf{I})

(17)

is sampled. The CM input is then constructed as

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{n}=\mathbf{z}_{n}+\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{n+1}+\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}.

(18)

The ADMM variables
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{z}
, and
\mathbf{u}
are complex vectors of length
N_{r}N_{t}
, whereas the CM network is a two-dimensional convolutional network operating on a two-channel representation of the angular-domain channel matrix. Before the CM call, the vector is reshaped into a complex angular-domain matrix and split into real and imaginary channels; after denoising, the output is recombined into a complex matrix and vectorized back to the ADMM variable domain. Let
\mathrm{unvec}(\cdot)
denote the column-major reshaping from a length-
N_{r}N_{t}
vector to an
N_{r}\times N_{t}
complex matrix, and define

\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{H})=\begin{bmatrix}\Re\{\mathbf{H}\}\\
\Im\{\mathbf{H}\}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times N_{r}\times N_{t}}.

(19)

The CM denoiser used in PnP can then be expressed as the vector-to-vector composite operator

\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(\mathbf{v},t)=\mathrm{vec}\left(\mathcal{C}^{-1}\left[f_{\theta}\left(\mathcal{C}(\mathrm{unvec}(\mathbf{v})),t\right)\right]\right).

(20)

The prior update is therefore

\mathbf{x}_{n}=\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{n},t_{n+1}).

(21)

The scaled dual variable is updated as

\mathbf{u}_{n}=\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{n+1}+\mathbf{z}_{n}-\mathbf{x}_{n}.

(22)

In addition, a Nesterov-type momentum extrapolation is applied to accelerate convergence:

\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{n}=\mathbf{x}_{n}+\beta_{n+1}(\mathbf{x}_{n}-\mathbf{x}_{n+1}),\quad\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{n}=\mathbf{u}_{n}+\beta_{n+1}(\mathbf{u}_{n}-\mathbf{u}_{n+1}).

(23)

The final angular-domain channel estimate is
\widehat{\mathbf{h}}=\mathbf{x}_{0}
. Algorithm
1
summarizes the inference procedure.

IV
Numerical Results

This section presents preliminary numerical results for PnP-CM on angular-domain MIMO channel estimation. The experiments first examine the training convergence of the CM prior, then evaluate estimation performance with respect to SNR and pilot ratio, and finally study cross-scenario generalization and the behavior of the outer PnP iterations. Let
\mathbf{h}=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{H}_{a})
. The normalized mean-square error (NMSE) is defined as

\mathrm{NMSE}(\widehat{\mathbf{h}},\mathbf{h})=\frac{\|\widehat{\mathbf{h}}-\mathbf{h}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{h}\|_{2}^{2}},

(24)

and is reported in decibels as
10\log_{10}(\mathrm{NMSE})
.

IV-A

Experimental Setup

The experiments use Raymobtime s002 60-GHz ray-tracing data to construct MIMO channel samples
[
10
]
. Raymobtime provides ray-tracing path parameters, including path powers, phases, and angles of arrival and departure. Each sample is synthesized as a narrowband geometric multipath channel,

\mathbf{H}=\sqrt{\frac{N_{t}N_{r}}{L}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\alpha_{\ell}\mathbf{a}_{r}(\phi_{\ell}^{r},\theta_{\ell}^{r})\mathbf{a}_{t}^{H}(\phi_{\ell}^{t},\theta_{\ell}^{t}),

(25)

where
L
is the number of valid paths,
\alpha_{\ell}
is determined by the path power and phase, and
\mathbf{a}_{r}(\cdot)
and
\mathbf{a}_{t}(\cdot)
are the receive and transmit uniform planar array (UPA) responses, respectively. The transmitter and receiver use
8\times 8
and
4\times 4
UPAs with half-wavelength antenna spacing, respectively. Thus,
N_{t}=64
,
N_{r}=16
, and the angular-domain channel dimension is
N=N_{t}N_{r}=1024
. The deduplicated s002 dataset contains 11092 training samples, 1386 validation samples, and 1387 test samples. To assess cross-scenario generalization, 2000 samples from the Raymobtime s007 scenario are also used as an out-of-distribution test set.

The transmit and receive codebooks consist of constant-modulus random phase vectors with 4-bit phase quantization. The number of receive radio-frequency chains is
N_{\mathrm{RF}}^{r}=1
, and the pilot-symbol amplitude is set to one. The pilot ratio is defined as
\alpha=M/N
, where
M=M_{t}M_{r}
is the number of measurements. Since
M_{t}
and
M_{r}
must be integers, the implementation searches for a balanced transmit/receive scan configuration closest to the target
\alpha
. For each setting, 100 samples are used for end-to-end estimation and the results are averaged.

The CM prior is trained following the principles of CMs and improved consistency training
[
12
,
13
]
. The network backbone is a two-dimensional U-Net with two-channel
16\times 64
input and output tensors. The base channel width is 64, the channel multipliers are
[1,2,2]
, each layer contains two residual blocks, and self-attention is used at the low-resolution level. The model has 6.80 million trainable parameters. Boundary-condition preconditioning is used with
\sigma_{\mathrm{data}}=0.02228
and boundary time
\epsilon=0.002
. Independent consistency training uses the Pseudo-Huber loss with smoothing coefficient 0.0244, noise interval
[0.002,0.13]
, noise-grid parameter
\rho=7
, and a discretization size increasing from
s_{0}=10
to
s_{1}=640
. The optimizer is RAdam with learning rate
10^{-4}
, batch size 64, and 50000 training steps. An exponential moving average (EMA) with decay 0.9999 is used for evaluation.

PnP-CM inference uses four outer ADMM iterations, and each iteration invokes the CM denoiser once. The linear data-consistency subproblem is solved by CG with maximum 80 iterations and tolerance
10^{-6}
. The noise-level sequence
\{t_{n}\}
, penalty sequence
\{\rho_{n}\}
, and momentum sequence
\{\beta_{n}\}
are empirically selected on the validation set for each SNR and then fixed for the corresponding test setting.

IV-B

Training Convergence

Training and test loss curves of the CM.

Fig.
1
shows the training and test loss curves of the CM. Both curves decrease rapidly during the early stage and then enter a stable regime. The training loss decreases from 21.999 to 0.0428, while the test loss decreases from 22.011 to 0.0459. The small gap between the two curves in the converged regime indicates that the current CM can stably fit the angular-domain channel distribution of the s002 scenario.

IV-C

SNR and Pilot Ratio

Fig. 2:
NMSE versus SNR under different pilot ratios on the s002 validation set.

Fig.
2
compares the estimation performance on the s002 validation set under different SNRs and pilot ratios. For a fixed pilot ratio, the NMSE decreases as the SNR increases. For a fixed SNR, a larger pilot ratio yields a lower reconstruction error. When
\alpha=1.0
, the NMSE improves from
-9.34
dB at
-5
dB SNR to
-21.89
dB at 20 dB SNR. When the actual pilot ratios are approximately 0.779 and 0.586, the NMSE values at 20 dB SNR are
-18.54
dB and
-14.27
dB, respectively, showing that the learned prior can still be effectively combined with data consistency under moderate pilot compression.

The performance degrades substantially when the pilot ratio is further reduced. At an actual pilot ratio of approximately 0.391, the NMSE at 20 dB SNR is
-10.97
dB; at an actual pilot ratio of approximately 0.198, it is
-6.12
dB. This result indicates that in severely undersampled regimes, the estimation error is mainly limited by insufficient observation information, and increasing SNR cannot fully compensate for the loss of pilot dimensions.

IV-D

Cross-Scenario Generalization

Fig. 3:
Channel-estimation NMSE evaluation under different environments with the pilot ratio fixed at
\alpha=1.0
.

To evaluate distribution transfer, the model trained on s002 is directly tested on the s007 scenario with the pilot ratio fixed at
\alpha=1.0
. As shown in Fig.
3
, the NMSE on s007 is significantly higher than that on s002 over the entire SNR range. At
-5
dB SNR, the NMSE values on s002 and s007 are
-9.34
dB and
-3.92
dB, respectively. At 20 dB SNR, they are
-21.89
dB and
-11.29
dB, respectively. The generalization gap increases from about 5.42 dB to about 10.60 dB as SNR increases.

This result suggests that the current CM prior is strongly dependent on the angular-domain statistics of the training scenario. Because s007 and s002 have different propagation environments and angular path distributions, the pretrained prior cannot fully convert the improved observation quality at high SNR into estimation gains. For practical deployment, a generative prior trained on a single scenario may therefore suffer from distribution shift.

IV-E

PnP Iteration Behavior

Fig. 4:
NMSE evolution over the outer iterations of PnP-CM.

Fig.
4
shows the outer-iteration curves for different SNRs when
\alpha=1.0
. The NMSE decreases almost monotonically with the iteration index, indicating that alternating between data consistency and CM denoising progressively improves the estimate. At
-5
dB SNR, the average NMSE decreases from
-4.33
dB to
-9.34
dB after four iterations. At 20 dB SNR, it decreases from
-9.04
dB to
-21.89
dB. The gain is larger at high SNR because the data-consistency subproblem provides more reliable observation correction.

Most of the improvement is obtained within the first two or three iterations, after which the marginal gain decreases. This behavior is consistent with the design goal of CMs as low-step generative priors.

V
Conclusion

This paper introduced CMs and the PnP-CM inference framework into pilot-aided MIMO channel estimation. The method alternates between a data-consistency update associated with the linear observation model and a pretrained CM denoising prior, producing an angular-domain channel estimate within a small number of outer iterations. To interface the vector-valued ADMM variables with the convolutional CM network, the complex vector is reshaped into an angular-domain matrix and split into real and imaginary channels before denoising, and the network output is then recombined and vectorized back to the optimization domain.

Experiments on Raymobtime 60-GHz data show that PnP-CM achieves stable NMSE improvement with increasing SNR under full and moderately compressed pilot configurations, and that the outer iterations progressively reduce the estimation error. These results support the feasibility of using CMs as low-latency generative priors within explicit observation constraints. At the same time, the current results reveal two main limitations. First, the PnP noise levels, penalty parameters, and momentum coefficients still need to be empirically selected for different SNRs. Second, cross-scenario testing shows a clear performance drop, indicating that a CM prior trained on a single scenario depends strongly on the propagation distribution. Future work will investigate adaptive parameter scheduling, multi-scenario training, and robust channel estimation methods that combine PnP-CM with structured physical priors.
