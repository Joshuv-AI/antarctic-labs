Interfering with innate immunity: type I IFNs and avian influenza susceptibility
Authors: Stuart G Tangye, Cindy S Ma
Published: 2026

Type I interferons (IFNs) are central to early antiviral innate immunity, being produced rapidly by diverse cell types to restrict viral replication.1 The importance of type I IFNs in host defence against infections became apparent in studies of mice treated with neutralising antibodies (Ab) against type I IFN, or rendered genetically deficient for the type I IFN receptors (IFNα/βR). Infection of these mice with a wide range of viruses resulted in severe morbidity and/or mortality.2 In contrast, control of bacterial or other non-viral pathogens in IFNα/βR-deficient mice was largely intact, while infection of mice lacking the type II IFNγR with these viruses was mild.2 Thus, type I IFNs, rather than IFNγ, play non-redundant roles in anti-viral immunity, at least in mice.

The discovery of humans with inborn errors of immunity (IEI) affecting type I IFN function refined the role of type I IFN in host defence. Individuals with deleterious variants in IFNAR1 or IFNAR2 are profoundly susceptible to adverse reactions following immunisation with live attenuated vaccines (LAV); some also developed disease after influenza or HSV1 infection.3 These findings confirmed the fundamental role of type I IFN in human immunity but also revealed a narrower susceptibility to viral infection than predicted by murine studies.

Susceptibility to infectious diseases can also occur in individuals who develop neutralising autoantibodies (autoAbs) against specific cytokines.4 An early indication of this was the finding of autoAbs against type I IFN in a patient with generalised VZV-induced disease, but no significant history of viral infections.5 Neutralising anti-type I IFN autoAbs have also been detected in other clinical settings including APECED (autoimmune polyendocrinopathy candidiasis ectodermal dystrophy) due to pathogenic AIRE variants and SLE.1 However, at this time the clinical significance of these autoAbs in terms of viral susceptibility was unclear.1 However, this all changed with the COVID-19 pandemic. International efforts to identify risk factors for severe SARS-CoV-2 infection led to the seminal findings that neutralising anti-type I IFN autoAbs caused ∼10–15% of cases of critical life-threatening COVID-19 pneumonia, and up to 20% of fatalities.1 Since then, neutralising anti-type I IFN autoAbs have been associated with severe disease induced by Middle Eastern respiratory virus, rare arboviruses, tick-borne encephalitis virus, West Nile virus (WNV), or exposure to LAV for WNV or chikungunya virus.1,6,7

The H7N9 subtype of avian influenza A virus (IAV) has caused five “bird flu” epidemics in China between 2013 and 2017. While zoonotic infection with H7N9 IAV is rare (<0.1% of the population), the case fatality rate is ∼40%.8 Thus, risk factors for infection, morbidity and mortality need to be identified. To address this, Chen et al., in the August issue of eBioMedicine, assessed the prevalence of type I IFN autoAbs in the context of H7N9 IAV infection. The cohorts studied were Chinese Han patients with confirmed H7N9 IAV infection, Chinese poultry workers with a high probability of exposure to H7N9 IAV, and close contacts to infected individuals as healthy controls. Similar to data from analysing severe COVID-19,1 nearly 20% of patients with H7N9 influenza had detectable levels of neutralising autoAbs against IFNα, IFNω or both, while only 0.7% of poultry workers and 1.5% of close contacts had these autoAbs.8 The prevalence of neutralising anti-type I IFN autoAbs was significantly associated with age, being 3-fold higher in people with H7N9 influenza over 70 years (44%) compared to those under 70 years (14%). Strikingly, the presence of neutralising type I IFN autoAbs was associated with ∼10–25-fold increased risk of H7N9 IAV infection.8

Despite the increased prevalence of neutralising type I IFN autoAbs in individuals with H7N9 influenza, there was no statistical association between these autoAbs and case fatality rate (i.e. 47% vs 41% deaths in autoAb-positive vs autoAb-negative cases).8 However, data relating to length of hospitalisation, need for ventilation, ICU admission and other treatments for individual patients was not available. This is a notable limitation of the study. Consequently, more granular analyses of these factors may reveal significant differences between disease course and case fatality rates for autoAbpos vs autoAbneg cases.

Molecular mechanisms underlying the generation of type I IFN autoAb are incompletely known, but impaired expression of AIRE in the thymus - due directly to pathogenic variants in AIRE or secondary to disrupted NFκB signalling from variants in NFKB2, RELB or MAP3K14 (encoding NIK) - and thus aberrant T cell tolerance, is a notable contributor.9 As none of the patients studied by Chen et al. harboured these or related genetic variants,8,9 it is unlikely that an undiagnosed known IEI resulted in type I IFN autoAbs and heightened susceptibility to H7N9 influenza.

The findings presented by Chen et al.8 add to the significant amount of data implicating autoAbs against type 1 IFNs in the pathogenesis of severe infection by some viruses, and by extension the fundamental role of type 1 IFNs in host defence against respiratory viruses, arboviruses, and LAV viruses.1,6,7,9 This study,8 together with a recent report of fatal H5N1 avian influenza in an elderly male with high titres of neutralising type I IFN autoAbs,10 indicate that screening for such neutralising autoAbs may identify individuals at risk of developing severe sequelae following infection with specific viruses, thereby informing management during pandemics, and enabling implementation of strategies to minimise exposure and/or disease morbidity. Despite being discovered in 1957,1 it is striking that it took a recent global pandemic for the unequivocal function of type I IFNs in anti-viral immunity to be crystalised.1,6,9 Future studies investigating risk factors for viral pathogenesis will thus require complementary approaches assessing contributions of molecular (genetic), cellular, and serological (autoAb) mechanisms.
