Efficacy and safety of low-dose combination urate-lowering agent therapy versus standard-dose monotherapy in patients with hyperuricemia and/or gout: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Takashi Sakaguchi, Hisatomi Arima, Makoto Okada, Taichi Maruyama, Ayako Goto, Kazutoshi Ito, Reimi Tsurusawa, Tamami Ueno, Kazumi Kawano, Makiko Abe, Toshiki Maeda, Kosuke Masutani, Chikara Yoshimura, Shunsuke Funakoshi, Shinichiro Nagamitsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTreatment to lower uric acid levels is usually administered using monotherapy. However, this may be insufficiently effective for all patients. We hypothesized that a low-dose combination of drugs with different mechanisms of action might be more effective than standard-dose monotherapy. To test this theory, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis.MethodWe searched MEDLINE and the Cochrane Library to identify randomized controlled trials that compared combination therapy involving low-dose urate-lowering drugs with standard-dose monotherapy. The primary outcome was serum uric acid levels at the end of treatment, and the data were analyzed using a random-effects model. Adverse events were assessed qualitatively.ResultsThis review included four trials involving 342 participants. Statistically significant reductions in serum uric acid levels were observed with low-dose combination therapy compared to monotherapy (mean difference: −0.47 mg/dL; 95% confidence interval: −0.68 to −0.26). No statistically significant heterogeneity was observed (I² = 0.0%). No serious adverse events were reported in either group.ConclusionsThe results suggest that low-dose combination therapy with urate production inhibitors and urate excretion promoters may be more effective at lowering serum uric acid levels compared with standard-dose monotherapy. However, as the assessment of adverse events was qualitative and the certainty of the safety evidence was extremely low, it remains unclear whether there are differences in the safety profiles between treatment strategies. Further randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05082-x.Keywords: Urate-lowering therapy, Low-dose combination therapy, Systematic review, Meta-analysis, Randomized controlled trials

Introduction

Gout is closely related to hyperuricemia, and it is estimated that approximately 54 million people worldwide suffer from this condition [1]. Urate-lowering therapy has been recommended for patients with hyperuricemia accompanied by symptoms such as gout, tophi, and urinary tract stones [2]. Xanthine oxidase inhibitors are often used as single agents. However, there is a certain number of patients who do not achieve sufficient results with monotherapy [3]. According to a previous study, approximately 60% of patients treated with allopurinol 100–300 mg/day and approximately 30% of those treated with febuxostat 80 mg/day do not reach the recommended target. These findings suggest that, while combination therapy may not be required for all patients, a clinically meaningful subset may benefit from its use [4]. Combination therapy with urate-lowering drugs is recognized in clinical guidelines, including the 2012 and 2020 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) guidelines for the management of gout, as an effective treatment strategy for patients who cannot achieve target serum urate levels with monotherapy [5, 6]. Similarly, the 2020 ACR guidelines acknowledge that in patients who show only a partial response to xanthine oxidase inhibitors, the combination of uricosuric agents and xanthine oxidase inhibitors may improve control of serum uric acid levels. However, as the evidence is limited and fragmented, the guidelines do not provide formal, graded recommendations regarding combination therapy. Recently, in the field of antihypertensive therapy, it has been reported that combining drugs with different mechanisms of action at low doses can achieve a greater antihypertensive effect than increasing the dose of a single agent, without increasing adverse events [7]. Although xanthine oxidase inhibitors and uricosuric agents have different mechanisms of action, they complement one another. Consequently, this principle may also apply to uric acid-lowering therapy. However, whilst the clinical utility of combination therapy is recognized, no rigorous and comprehensive evaluation has yet been conducted to determine whether low-dose combination therapy with urate-lowering drugs is superior in efficacy and non-inferior in safety compared to standard-dose monotherapy. The available evidence is scattered across individual trials that differ in the specific combination therapies evaluated, the patient populations studied, and the outcome measures used. Therefore, to fill this evidence gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, focusing specifically on the comparison between low-dose combination therapy and standard-dose monotherapy.

Methods

Intervention and comparisons

The interventions under consideration were combination therapies involving low-dose urate-lowering therapy; specifically, these were defined as the combination of febuxostat 20 mg and benzbromarone 25 mg, or the combination of allopurinol 100 mg and benzbromarone 20 mg. The control groups received standard-dose monotherapy with febuxostat 40 mg or allopurinol 300 mg. All four trials enrolled patients with hyperuricemia. One of these trials included patients with concomitant gout, one was restricted to patients with concomitant hypertension, and the remaining two trials were restricted to patients with hyperuricemia alone. Baseline serum uric acid levels in each trial ranged from 6.9 to 9.47 mg/dL, and the follow-up periods varied from 9 days to 9 months. Outcomes

The primary outcome measure was serum uric acid levels at the end of the treatment period. For each randomized controlled trial, the difference in serum uric acid levels between groups at the end of treatment and its standard error were calculated, and the results were pooled using a random-effects model. Adverse events were secondary outcome measures; these were summarized descriptively based on the reports from each study but were not pooled.

Data sources, search strategy

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA statement and pre-registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42023449104) and can be accessed at: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42023449104 [8]. The following two changes were made to the registration protocol, both of which were implemented prior to data analysis. Firstly, as the methods of reporting adverse events differed across the studies included in the analysis, it was not possible to quantitatively pool the safety data; consequently, adverse events were reclassified from primary endpoints to secondary endpoints. Secondly, comparisons with the placebo group or the untreated group were excluded, and the analysis was restricted to a comparison between low-dose combination therapy and standard-dose monotherapy. This is because this comparison is of the greatest clinical significance for patients whose serum uric acid levels remain above the treatment target despite monotherapy. As all these changes were implemented prior to data analysis, it is considered unlikely that these changes caused any bias in the results. The MEDLINE and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials databases were searched from their inception through January 21, 2025 (see Supplementary Table 1 for details of the search strategy). No language restrictions were applied. Only randomized controlled trials comparing low-dose combination therapy with standard-dose monotherapy were included. Trials comparing combination therapy with placebo, or direct comparison between different combination therapies, were excluded.

Screening and data extraction

A two-stage screening process was conducted. First, five reviewers (MO, TM, AG, KI, and RT) independently conducted literature screening. Titles and abstracts were reviewed to assess relevance and identify potentially eligible studies. Subsequently, we obtained the full-text articles of the selected studies. Due to the differing expertise of team members, two reviewers (TS and HA) independently evaluated these articles to confirm their eligibility for inclusion and conducted data extraction. Differences of opinion were resolved through discussion. Studies and reviews that may contain relevant data or information were retained from the outset. All potentially relevant articles were evaluated in full text.

The following data were extracted from all included studies: study characteristics (study design, patient selection, number of patients, randomized treatment, follow-up period, and endpoints); baseline characteristics (age, sex, serum uric acid levels, and comorbidities); and outcome measures. The missing standard deviation was estimated from the available statistical data. Bias risk and study quality were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool version 2 (RoB 2) for randomized trials [9]. Two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. Risk-of-bias results are summarized in Supplementary Table 2 and visualized in Supplementary Figure 1, generated using robvis [10].

Protocol deviations

Prior to data analysis, two amendments were made to the registered protocol. Firstly, as the methods of reporting adverse events differed between the included studies, quantitative pooling of safety data was not possible. Consequently, adverse events were reclassified from primary endpoints to secondary endpoints. Secondly, comparisons with the placebo or untreated groups were excluded, and the analysis was restricted to a comparison between low-dose combination therapy and standard-dose monotherapy. This was because this comparison is of the greatest clinical importance for patients whose serum uric acid levels exceed the treatment target despite receiving monotherapy.

Data extraction from figures

When serum uric acid levels were not explicitly stated in text or tables but were shown in graphs, data were extracted from the graph using WebPlotDigitizer version 5 (https://automeris.io/WebPlotDigitizer/). Two reviewers independently extracted values from the same figures, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Where error bars were displayed, their type was verified prior to data extraction. In the case of Berg H 1990, the error bars represented standard errors (SE). As these were to be used in the meta-analysis, the mean difference and its standard error were calculated directly from the extracted values.

Data standardization

Serum uric acid concentrations were standardized to mg/dL for all analyses. Values reported in µmol/L were converted to mg/dL using the conversion factor (mg/dL = µmol/L / 59.48).

Statistical analysis

In each randomized controlled trial, we calculated the between-group difference in serum uric acid levels at the end of treatment and its standard error. Heterogeneity between trials was assessed using the I2 statistic. Funnel plots were used to assess the possibility of publication bias. A sensitivity analysis was conducted excluding crossover trials. STATA release 14 (STATA Corp, College Station, TX) was used for statistical analyses.

Certainty assessments

The certainty of evidence was assessed considering study quality, consistency of results, and precision of estimates.

Results

Description of included trials

A total of 153 documents were extracted from the initial search, and after excluding 29 duplicates, 124 citations were screened (Supplementary Figure 3). Most of these documents were excluded during the initial screening for appropriate reasons. The remaining 16 documents were selected for full-text review, and after a second screening, 12 full-text articles were excluded for appropriate reasons. Four trials including 342 patients were deemed eligible for analysis.

Table 1 shows characteristics of included trials. All studies included patients with hyperuricemia and/or gout, but one study included those with hyperuricemia complicated with hypertension. Average serum uric acid levels at baseline were 6.9–9.47 mg/dL. The follow-up duration was 9 days to 9 months. Mean patient age was 37.7 - 48.6 years. Most trials dominantly included male patients.

Serum uric acid levels

Compared with monotherapy, low-dose combination therapy was associated with a statistically significant reduction in serum urate levels at the end of treatment of −0.47 mg/dL (95% confidence interval −0.68 to −0.26) (Fig. 1). The I-squared value was 0.0%, indicating that there was little heterogeneity between studies. As the number of studies included was small (n = 4), it was not possible to reliably assess publication bias (Supplementary Figure 2). Sensitivity analysis excluding crossover trial demonstrated similar findings (−0.45 mg/dL, 95% confidence interval −0.67 to −0.22) (Supplementary Figure 4).

Complications and serious adverse events in each trial are summarized in Table 2 by randomized group. The available data did not suggest marked differences between the standard-dose monotherapy group and the low-dose combination therapy group in terms of non-serious adverse events (gout attacks, liver dysfunction, kidney stones, etc.); however, given that adverse events were assessed qualitatively rather than quantitatively due to heterogeneous reporting across trials, definitive conclusions regarding the comparative safety of these two treatment strategies cannot be drawn from the present meta-analysis.

Assessment of reporting bias

The assessment of publication bias was limited due to the small number of studies. We minimized bias through a comprehensive search of multiple databases and clinical trial registries.

Assessment of evidence certainty

All four studies included were classified as having ‘some concerns’ in the overall risk of bias assessment. In three of the four studies (Ohta Y et al. 2017, Berg H 1990, Mertz DP 1976), concerns were identified in the area of the randomization process (D1), and in all four studies, concerns were identified in the area of deviation from the intended intervention (D2). For the remaining domains—namely, missing outcome data (D3), measurement of outcomes (D4), and selection of reported results (D5)—the risk of bias was assessed as low in all included studies. Detailed results are presented in Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Figure 1.

The certainty of the evidence regarding the primary outcome (serum uric acid levels at the end of treatment) was assessed as ‘low’. This is due to uncertainty arising from the small number of trials and limited sample sizes, in addition to concerns regarding the risk of bias across all included studies, although no heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%) was observed. The certainty of the evidence regarding the secondary outcome (adverse events) was assessed as ‘very low’. This was partly due to the fact that reports of adverse events in all trials were qualitative. A summary of Findings table generated using the GRADE approach is presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Discussion

Regarding safety, although adverse events were assessed qualitatively, the certainty of the evidence was very low. Consequently, it is not possible to draw any conclusions as to whether the safety profile of combination therapy differs from that of monotherapy. There were no signs of heterogeneity, and publication bias could not be reliably assessed due to the small number of included studies (n = 4).

Previous meta-analytic evidence suggested dual therapy demonstrated superior efficacy compared to monotherapy but had a higher risk of adverse events than most monotherapies (Yu-Jiun Lin, et al., 2020) [11]. In our meta-analysis, treatment with two drugs at low doses showed superiority over monotherapy at standard doses in maintaining uric acid-lowering efficacy without increasing side effects. Low-dose combination therapy may be a new effective and safe treatment option for the management of hyperuricemia.

The following may be proposed as a hypothetical mechanism to explain the observed efficacy and safety of low-dose combination therapy. It is known that combining drugs with different mechanisms of action may avoid excessive inhibition of each pathway while achieving additive or synergistic effect. Dispersed inhibition across multiple pathways has been proposed as the mechanism by which low-dose combination therapy may demonstrate efficacy with fewer side effects by suppressing physiological dysfunction and the onset of side effects caused by excessive inhibition of a single pathway [12]. In contrast, doubling the dose of monotherapy does not double the efficacy while drug-specific side effects may increase in a dose-dependent manner [13]. However, explanations of these mechanisms remain purely hypothetical and have not been directly verified in the studies in question. Consequently, further research is required to substantiate these mechanisms.

Although a reduction of 0.47 mg/dL in serum uric acid levels may appear modest in absolute terms, it is considered clinically significant from the perspective of treatment strategies based on target levels for hyperuricemia. Current guidelines recommend a target serum uric acid level of less than 6.0 mg/dL for patients with gout, and less than 5.0 mg/dL for patients with tophi or those experiencing frequent attacks. The studies included in the analysis showed that baseline serum uric acid levels ranged from 6.9 to 9.47 mg/dL, which exceeds the recommended treatment target of 6.0 mg/dL. Therefore, reducing these levels by a further 0.47 mg/dL may offer meaningful additional benefit to patients whose levels remain above the treatment threshold despite receiving a standard monotherapy dose. This could contribute to achieving the target uric acid level and potentially reduce the long-term risk of gout attacks and urate crystal deposition. Indeed, previous studies have shown that maintaining serum uric acid levels below the therapeutic target is associated with a reduction in the frequency of gout attacks, the shrinkage of tophi, and a lower risk of long-term complications such as chronic gouty arthritis [14]. However, it should be noted that this meta-analysis did not conduct a quantitative assessment of these or other long-term clinical outcomes (such as renal function and long-term safety). Consequently, it remains unclear whether a reduction in serum uric acid levels leads to significant improvements in these clinical endpoints. The combination therapies evaluated in this trial were febuxostat 20 mg plus benzbromarone 25 mg, or allopurinol 100 mg plus benzbromarone 20 mg; these were compared with standard-dose monotherapy defined as febuxostat 40 mg or allopurinol 300 mg. These low-dose combination therapies represent a rational approach from the perspective of their mechanisms of action. This is because xanthine oxidase inhibitors suppress the production of uric acid, whilst benzbromarone promotes the excretion of uric acid by the kidneys. However, as benzbromarone is not approved in some countries, regional disparities in drug availability may limit the generalizability of these therapies.

Study limitations

A notable limitation of this systematic review is that the literature search was restricted to MEDLINE and CENTRAL and did not include other major databases such as Embase. Consequently, relevant studies may have been overlooked, which could lead to selection bias or compromise the comprehensiveness of the evidence. Therefore, the findings of this review should be interpreted with caution. To minimize this risk, future systematic reviews on this topic should adopt a more comprehensive search strategy that incorporates additional databases.

One limitation of this meta-analysis is that only four studies met the inclusion criteria, and the total number of participants across all included studies was just 342. Given the limited number of studies and the relatively small overall sample size, the statistical power of the pooled estimates is reduced. Furthermore, as a risk of bias was identified in the majority of the included studies, these results should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, although a funnel plot was used to assess publication bias, the ability to detect asymmetry in the funnel plot was significantly limited because only four studies were included in the meta-analysis. Consequently, the possibility of publication bias cannot be ruled out. Another important limitation is that the follow-up periods of some included trials were somewhat short, making it impossible to determine long-term effects from the data. The follow-up periods across the included studies varied considerably, ranging from 9 days to 9 months. Furthermore, the patient populations differed from study to study, including patients with hyperuricemia alone, those with both hyperuricemia and gout, and those with both hyperuricemia and hypertension. As these differences in follow-up periods and patient characteristics constitute factors of clinical heterogeneity, they should be taken into account when interpreting the results of the meta-analysis. Furthermore, compliance with uric acid lowering therapy was not recorded in most included studies. Two of the included trials were conducted in 1976 and 1990, and clinical practice, diagnostic criteria, and treatment standards for hyperuricemia have evolved considerably since that time. This may limit the comparability of these older studies with more recent trials. Although we were unable to perform a sensitivity analysis excluding these studies due to the limited number of included trials, this temporal heterogeneity should be considered when interpreting the findings of the present meta-analysis. The included studies comprised two febuxostat-based combination therapies and two allopurinol-based combination therapies. Although it would have been desirable to perform subgroup analyses stratified by drug type, this was not possible as only two studies were available for each subgroup, resulting in a significant lack of statistical power. Therefore, this pharmacological heterogeneity should be considered as a further limitation when interpreting the results of this meta-analysis. Furthermore, a significant proportion of the pooled effect estimates may be influenced by a single study with a substantial impact. In light of this, the overall conclusions should be interpreted with caution. In addition, adverse events across the included trials were reported in a heterogeneous manner, precluding quantitative pooling of safety data. Consequently, safety outcomes were evaluated qualitatively, which limits the strength of conclusions regarding comparative safety. Future trials should adopt standardized reporting of adverse events to enable more rigorous safety analyses.

Conclusions

As this meta-analysis is based on only four randomized controlled trials involving a total of 342 participants, the following conclusions should be interpreted with caution. The results suggest that low-dose combination therapy involving uric acid production inhibitors and uric acid excretion enhancers may be more effective than standard-dose monotherapy at reducing serum uric acid levels.

It remains unclear whether the safety profile of low-dose combination therapy differs from that of standard-dose monotherapy, as the assessment of adverse events was qualitative and the certainty of the safety evidence was extremely low. To resolve this question, randomized controlled trials with sufficient statistical power and pre-specified, quantitatively assessed safety outcomes are required.
