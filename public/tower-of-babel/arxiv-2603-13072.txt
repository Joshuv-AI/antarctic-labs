Practical framework for simulating permutation-equivariant quantum circuits
Su Yeon Chang, Martin Larocca, M. Cerezo

Abstract
Understanding which subclasses of quantum circuits are efficiently classically simulable is fundamental to delineating the boundary between classical and quantum computation. In this context, it is well known that certain tasks based on permutation-equivariant unitaries–i.e., n n -qubit circuits whose action commutes with the qubit-permuting representation of the symmetric group S n S_{n} –can be simulated in polynomial time. However, existing approaches scale as 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 7 ) \mathcal{O}(n^{7}) , and can rapidly become prohibitively expensive. In this work, we introduce a practical algorithm for simulating S n S_{n} -equivariant circuits under the assumption that the gate generators are at most k k -local, with k ∈ 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) k\in\mathcal{O}(1) . The resulting method runs in 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ω + 1 ) \mathcal{O}(n^{\omega+1}) time for constant depth, where ω \omega is the matrix multiplication exponent, significantly lowering the polynomial degree compared to existing techniques. Finally, we numerically validate this scaling by simulating the dynamical evolution of the Lipkin–Meshkov–Glick model, and show that for n = 512 n=512 spins, a standard laptop can compute the concurrence of the evolved state in under two minutes.

In this section, we will present an efficient algorithm that can be used to estimate

. To begin, let us note that Eqs. (
14
) and (

\displaystyle f(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{p_{\lambda}=1}^{m_{\lambda}}\Tr[\rho_{\lambda}^{p_{\lambda}}U_{\lambda}^{\dagger}O_{\lambda}U_{\lambda}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}\Tr[\left(\sum_{p_{\lambda}=1}^{m_{\lambda}}\rho^{p_{\lambda}}_{\lambda}\right)\widetilde{O}_{\lambda}]\;,

(26)

with
\widetilde{O}_{\lambda}=U_{\lambda}^{\dagger}O_{\lambda}U_{\lambda}
the Heisenberg evolved operator within each irrep. In this equation,
\rho_{\lambda}^{p_{\lambda}}
denotes the projection of
\rho
onto the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}^{p_{\lambda}}
. Note that the initial state
\rho
is not necessarily
S_{n}
-equivariant, and therefore cannot, in general, be block-diagonalized in the Schur basis, as illustrated in Fig.

. This is precisely why we work in the Heisenberg picture of backward-in-time evolution of measurement operators.

In what follows, we divide the task of estimating
f(\rho)
into two parts. First, we will focus on the evaluation of
\widetilde{O}_{\lambda}
, which will require us to compute the projection of
O

H_{\ell}
into the irreps and Heisenberg evolve the measurement operator. Second, we will discuss how to obtain
\rho_{\lambda}^{p_{\lambda}}
.

For the first task, one needs to block-diagonalize the circuit generator and the measurement operators in the Schur basis.
As a solution to this problem, Ref.
5
proposed an algorithm to compute the matrix elements of any arbitrary
S_{n}
-equivariant operator using tensor network diagram contraction, with computational complexity of
\order{n^7}
. Although this complexity scales polynomially with respect to the system size
n
, it can rapidly become prohibitively expensive to deploy at even modest system sizes. Our approach is to instead focus on block-diagonalizing in the basis obtained from the canonical Young tableau for specific
S_{n}
-equivariant operators that are physically motivated, rather than trying to work with arbitrary ones (see Refs.
57
;
58
for similar approaches).

Our decision is motivated by the fact that, in practice, most of the
S_{n}
-equivariant generators in
\mathcal{G}_{S_{n}}
are at most two-local

;
19
;
46
;
72
. For instance, if we are implementing a Trotterized evolution, the terms in a physical Hamiltonian are one- or two-bodied. Hence, one possible choice for the set of local
S_{n}
-equivariant generators
\mathcal{G}
is

\mathcal{G}_{S_{n}}=\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{j}\;,\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq k<j\leq n}P_{j}P_{k}\right\}\,,

(27)

where
P_{j}\in\{X,Y,Z\}
is a fixed Pauli matrix at the
j
-th qubit.
Similarly, we can also consider a set of equivariant observables
\chi
, given by

\chi=\mathcal{G}_{S_{n}}\cup\left\{\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}P_{j}\;\right\}\,,

(28)

which also contains the global measurement operators.

Here, we also find it important to note that the choice of generators
\mathcal{G}_{S_{n}}
, while being local, leads to subspace-universal circuits within each invariant subspace. That is, unitaries as in Eq. (
9
) with generators taken from
\mathcal{G}_{S_{n}}
will be subspace controllable and can generate any arbitrary unitaries in each subspace
\lambda
(see Theorem 1 in Ref.
39
).

III.1
S_{n}
-equivariant operators in Schur basis

Let
A
be an
S_{n}
-equivariant operator such as those in Eqs. (
27
) and (
28
). As previously mentioned, in the Schur basis,
A
admits the block-diagonal form
A\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda}\mathds{1}_{m_{\lambda}}\otimes A_{\lambda}
, where the matrix entries at position
(q_{\lambda},q^{\prime}_{\lambda})
, denoted as
\left(A_{\lambda}\right)_{q_{\lambda},q^{\prime}_{\lambda}}
, can be found as the inner product between
A
and two Schur basis states within a fixed multiplicity. In particular, we will focus on the case when
p_{\lambda}=p_{\lambda}^{0}
, so that

\left(A_{\lambda}\right)_{q_{\lambda},q^{\prime}_{\lambda}}=\langle\lambda,p^{0}_{\lambda},q_{\lambda}\hskip 1.0pt|A|\hskip 1.0pt\lambda,p^{0}_{\lambda},q^{\prime}_{\lambda}\rangle.

(29)

Thus, we can explicitly compute these matrix elements by considering the action of
A
on the symmetric and antisymmetric parts of the canonical tableau.

We first consider
A
to be solely composed of Pauli-
Z
operators.
As the canonical Schur basis
\ket{\lm, p^0_\lm, q_\lm}
is characterized by the Hamming weight of the computational basis in the symmetric part, given by
h_{\lambda,q_{\lambda}}=q_{\lambda}+m
, Pauli-
Z
operators act diagonally in the Schur basis. Using the eigenvalues of
A
expressed as a function of the Hamming weight in the computational basis (c.f. Lemma 4 in Ref.
39
), the matrix elements in each irrep take the form

\displaystyle\left(A_{\lambda}\right)_{q_{\lambda}q^{\prime}_{\lambda}}=\frac{n^{2}-n-4nh_{\lambda,q_{\lambda}}+4h_{\lambda,q_{\lambda}}^{2}}{n(n-1)}\delta_{q_{\lambda}q^{\prime}_{\lambda}}\,,

(30)

for
A=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{j<k}Z_{j}Z_{k}
;

\displaystyle\left(A_{\lambda}\right)_{q_{\lambda}q^{\prime}_{\lambda}}=\ \left(1-\frac{2h_{\lambda,q_{\lambda}}}{n}\right)\delta_{q_{\lambda}q^{\prime}_{\lambda}}\,,

(31)

for
A=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}Z_{i}
; and

\displaystyle\left(A_{\lambda}\right)_{q_{\lambda}q^{\prime}_{\lambda}}=(-1)^{h_{\lambda,q_{\lambda}}}\delta_{q_{\lambda}q^{\prime}_{\lambda}}\,,

(32)

for
A=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}
.

Next, we analyze the operators composed of Pauli-
X
operators, beginning with the operator
A=X^{\otimes n}
. The action of
A
simply flips each of the computational basis bits, while changing the sign of the antisymmetric state. As a result, we find

\displaystyle A\Big(\ket{\Psi}^{\otimes m}

\displaystyle\otimes\ket{\Sigma^{(n-2m)}_{q_\lm}}\Big)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\ket{\Psi}^{\otimes m}\otimes\ket{\Sigma^{(n-2m)}_{n-2m -q_\lm}}\;.

(33)

Therefore, each block
\lambda
has an anti-diagonal structure, with entries consisting solely of
1
or
-1
:

\left(A_{\lambda}\right)_{q_{\lambda}q^{\prime}_{\lambda}}=(-1)^{m}\delta_{q_{\lambda},n-2m-q^{\prime}_{\lambda}}\,.

(34)

Then, taking
A=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
, the antisymmetric part
\ket{\Psi}
always vanishes, and the action on the symmetric part shifts the Hamming weight by
\pm 1
, yielding

\displaystyle\left(A_{\lambda}\right)_{q_{\lambda}q^{\prime}_{\lambda}}\!=\!\frac{\alpha^{-}(\lambda,q_{\lambda})}{n}\delta_{q_{\lambda}-1,q^{\prime}_{\lambda}}\!+\!\frac{\alpha^{+}(\lambda,q_{\lambda})}{n}\delta_{q_{\lambda}+1,q^{\prime}_{\lambda}}\,,

(35)

where we define the coefficients

\displaystyle\alpha^{-}(\lambda,q_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{q_{\lambda}(n-2m-(q_{\lambda}-1))}\,,

(36)

\displaystyle\alpha^{+}(\lambda,q_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(q_{\lambda}+1)(n-2m-q_{\lambda})}\,.

(37)

In this case, each block
\lambda
will be a tridiagonal matrix with the main diagonal hollow and
\order{2n}
non-zero off-diagonal elements.

The previous examples illustrate a general pattern: Schur blocks of at most two-local
S_{n}
-equivariant operators, as well as the global
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}
ones, are sparse—being diagonal, anti-diagonal or banded—with at most
\order{n}
entries per block. As a result, all blocks
A_{\lambda}
can be constructed with total classical cost
\order{n^2}
.
The representations for all other operators of interest, as well as additional details, are provided in Appendix
A
.

III.2
Classical simulation of
S_{n}
-equivariant circuits

Using the results from the previous section, we now have a practical way to compute the Heisenberg-evolved measurement operators
\widetilde{O}_{\lambda}
on each of the size
d_{\lambda}\times d_{\lambda}
blocks, for all
\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor+1
distinct irrep labels
\lambda
.

Specifically, we begin by projecting the observable
O
and each circuit generator onto each irrep blocks using the techniques previously described. Then, let us focus on a single irrep. If we compute the single layer exponential
e^{-i(H_{\ell})_{\lambda}}
and use matrix product in the Heisenberg evolution, the naive cost would scale as
\order{L d_\lm^3}
with dominating matrix exponentiation cost. For two-local symmetrized Pauli strings, the irreducible blocks
(H_{\ell})_{\lambda}
are, in the worst case, banded Hermitian matrices with constant bandwidth. Hence, they can be diagonalized in
\order{d_\lm^2}
time, allowing us to express them as

(H_{\ell})_{\lambda}=Q_{\ell,\lambda}\Lambda_{\ell,\lambda}(Q_{\ell,\lambda})^{\dagger},

(38)

with
Q_{\ell,\lambda}
unitary and
\Lambda_{\lambda,\ell}
diagonal. Then, the unitary in the irrep is obtained as

e^{-i(H_{\ell})_{\lambda}}=Q_{\ell,\lambda}\,e^{-i\Lambda_{\ell,\lambda}}\left(Q_{\ell,\lambda}\right)^{\dagger}\,,

(39)

where we recall that the exponentiation of a diagonal matrix has a cost of
\order{d_\lm}
. Thus, given access to Eq. (
38
), we can compute a single layer Heisenberg evolution in time
\order{d_\lm^\omega}
with
\omega
the matrix multiplication exponent, taking a value between 2.37 and 3 depending on the method used. Summing over irreps, layers, and taking into account the cost of diagonalizing all generators leads to the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(Complexity of Heisenberg evolution)
.

Consider an
S_{n}
-equivariant circuit
U
with a set of one- and two-local generators defined as in Eq. (
27
), and an
S_{n}
-equivariant observable
O
from the set Eq. (
28
). The Heisenberg-evolved operator
U^{\dagger}OU
can be classically evaluated in the Schur basis with time cost scaling as

N_{\rm C.T.}\in\order{n^3 + L n^{\om+ 1}}\;,

(40)

and the memory cost scaling as

N_{\rm memory}\in\order{n^3}\;.

(41)

The first term in Eq. (
40
) arises from the diagonalization of the one- and two-body generators in Schur basis. For each irrep
\lambda
, this requires
\order{d_\lm^2}
operations, and summing over contributions across all irreps yields
\sum_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(n-2m+1)^{2}\in\order{n^3}
. The second term accounts for the matrix multiplication cost to obtain
U_{\lambda}
, as well as
U_{\lambda}^{\dagger}O_{\lambda}U_{\lambda}
across all irreps. For each irrep, this costs
\order{L d_\lm^\omega}
, leading to the total complexity
\sum_{\lambda}Ld_{\lambda}^{\omega}\in\order{L n^{\omega+ 1}}
. In addition, the diagonalizing matrices
Q_{\ell,\lambda}
of dimension
d_{\lambda}\times d_{\lambda}
must be stored for all
\lambda
and
\ell
, resulting in the memory cost of
\order{n^3}
. Here, we underline that the theorem assumes that the circuit generators are drawn from a fixed set. If, instead, each layer involve a new linear combination of generators from the set, the diagonalization should be performed independently at each layer, resulting in an overall time complexity of
\order{Ln^3 + L n^{\omega+ 1}}
and a memory complexity
\order{Ln^3}
.

A priori, the diagonalization of the circuit generators needs to be performed only once as a preprocessing step for each value of
n
. Hence, assuming access to such diagonalization, the cost of simulation per layer is simply given by
\order{ n^{\om+ 1}}
. However, as we will observe in Section
IV
, the practical runtime scales more favorably than the theoretical upper bound.

At this point, we note that the results in Theorem
1
are closely related to those in Lemmas 3 and 4 in Ref.
5
, but refined here for circuits generated by at most two-local Pauli generators. In fact, below we show how to extend our theorem for
k
-local Pauli generators with
k\in\order{1}
.

To finish, we note that if the components
\sum_{p_{\lambda}=1}^{m_{\lambda}}\rho^{p_{\lambda}}_{\lambda}
are known, the previous algorithm readily allows us to compute
f(\rho)
with an additional step of matrix multiplication and tracing. While in general, we might not have access to the decomposition of the input state within each irrep, in some special cases we can efficiently compute them. This is, for instance, the case when
\rho
is
S_{n}
-equivariant itself, then
\rho^{p_{\lambda}}_{\lambda}=\rho_{\lambda}
(i.e., the same component repeats across multiplicities) or when it is a simple state such as
\rho=\ket{0}\!\bra{0}^{\otimes n}
. Here, the following corollary follows.

Corollary 1
.

Assume that we are given access to the components of the general input state
\rho
within each irrep, as well as to the diagonalized circuit generators. Then, we can compute
f(\rho)
for an
L
-layered circuit with
L\in\mathcal{O}(1)
in time scaling as

N_{\rm C.T.}\in\order{ n^{\om+ 1}}\;.

(42)

More generally, we will see below that if
\rho
is a generic state that lives in a quantum computer, we can obtain its irrep projections via classical shadows techniques. This incurs some additional complexity in the form of classical shadows samples, as well as some classical post-processing. Still, even in this case our algorithm remains efficient across the board. We explore this setting in Section
III.4
, with additional details given in Appendix
D
.

III.3
Generic
k
-local symmetrized Pauli operators

Until now, we have restricted our analysis to the case when the elements in
\mathcal{G}
are at most two-local, where we can analytically find their irrep projections. In this section, we extend the framework to symmetrized
k
-local Pauli strings
\mathcal{T}_{S_{n}}(P_{\bm{k}})
with
k\in\order{1}
. In particular, we refer the reader to Appendix
B
for an explicit algorithm that numerically evaluates the matrix expression of
\mathcal{T}_{S_{n}}(P_{\bm{k}})
in the Schur basis for an arbitrary
\bm{k}
.

The following theorem states the computational complexity bound for evaluating the matrix representation of the
k
-local symmetrized Pauli strings in the Schur basis.

Theorem 2
.

Consider a symmetrized Pauli operator
\mathcal{T}_{S_{n}}(P_{\bm{k}})
with
P_{\bm{k}}
defined in Eq. (
4
). Given that
k\in\order{1}
, the total time complexity to find the matrix representation in the Schur basis scales at most as
\order{n^2}
.

As a result, for constant locality
k
, the computational cost of evaluating the matrix elements of
k
-local symmetrized Pauli strings is negligible compared to the cost of computing
U_{\lambda}
for all irrep labels
\lambda
.
Moreover, for a
k
-local Pauli string, the corresponding matrix block
H_{\lambda}
is at most
k
-banded. Therefore, its eigendecomposition can be performed with a computational cost that also scales at most as
\order{n^2}
, and the overall time complexity stated in Theorem
1
remains valid. We note that this bound is tighter compared to the bound of
\order{n^4}
provided in Appendix E of Ref.
5
.
In contrast, for generic Pauli strings with
k\in\order{n}
, we recover the time complexity of
\order{n^4}
.

III.4
Initial state acquisition via classical shadows

In this section, we discuss how a quantum computer can be used to estimate the irrep projection of
\rho
via classical shadows
26
. As usual, it is fundamental to pick a shadow tomographic procedure that aligns well with the information we are trying to extract. For
S_{n}
-equivariant quantum simulations, the initial state can be efficiently acquired using PI-CS, a shadow protocol tailored to permutation-invariant circuits
57
. We briefly review the PI-CS protocol and analyze the associated resource requirements, thereby completing the complexity analysis for
S_{n}
-equivariant simulations over generic states. Additional details on PI-CS are provided in Appendix
D
.

We start by considering an arbitrary initial state
\rho
, which is not necessarily
S_{n}
-equivariant. The last equality in Eq. (
26
) implies that it is sufficient to retrieve the sum of the initial states over multiplicities for each irrep block
\lambda
3
3

3

The fact that we do not require each individual projection onto the multiplicity blocks is crucial, as that would require us to store an exponential amount of information.
. A variant of PI-CS, dubbed Deep PI-CS, performs this task by block-diagonalizing the initial state with the quantum Schur transform (QST) circuit
U_{\rm QST}
of depth scaling linearly with respect to system size
n
and polylogarithmically in the inverse precision
6
;
7
;
41
;
45
. This leads to the following theorem for the quantum sample complexity:

Lemma 1

(Quantum circuit and sample complexity for generic states)
.

Given a generic state
\rho
, the expectation value of a set of
M
distinct
S_{n}
-equivariant observables
\{O_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
can be estimated up to additive accuracy
\epsilon
, and with a success probability
1-\delta
, via deep PI-CS using a number of quantum samples scaling as

N_{\rm Q.S.}\in\order{\log(\frac{M}{\dl})\frac{n^2}{\ep^2} \max_i{\norm{O_i}^2_{\infty}}}\;,

(43)

where
\norm{\cdot}_{\infty}
denotes the spectral norm of the matrix. The procedure requires a quantum Schur transform circuit of depth
\order{n \poly\left(\log(\epsilon^{-1}_{\rm QST})\right)}
where
\epsilon_{\rm QST}
denotes the implementation error of the Schur transform.

If the initial state is
S_{n}
-equivariant, it can also be decomposed as
\rho\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda}\mathds{1}_{m_{\lambda}}\otimes\rho_{\lambda}
with
\rho_{\lambda}
a
d_{\lambda}\times d_{\lambda}
matrix as well. In this specific case, we can use shallow PI-CS, or symmetrized PI-CS. Here, there is no need to apply a QST, reducing the quantum circuit complexity. We find that the following lemma holds.

Lemma 2

(Quantum circuit and sample complexity for
S_{n}
-equivariant states)
.

Given an
S_{n}
-equivariant state, the expectation value of a set of
M
distinct
S_{n}
-equivariant observables
\{O_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
can be estimated up to additive accuracy
\epsilon
, and with a success probability
1-\delta
, via symmetrized PI-CS using a quantum circuit of constant depth, i.e.,
N_{\rm Q.T.}\in\order{1}
, and a number of quantum samples scaling as

N_{\rm Q.S.}\in\order{\log\left(\frac{M}{\delta}\right)\frac{n}{\ep^2} \max_i{\norm{O_i}_F^2} }

(44)

where
\norm{\cdot}_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm of the matrix.

At this point, it is important to note that unlike deep PI-CS, in which additional quantum circuit and qubits are required, the symmetrized PI-CS requires no additional quantum resources. However, while the shadow post-processing is negligible in deep PI-CS, the symmetrized PI-CS does incur non-trivial classical overhead as one must invert the measurement channel and compute the relevant Clebsch–-Gordan coefficients. This adds an extra classical runtime cost of at least
\order{n^6}
, leading to a trade-off between quantum and classical resource consumption. More details on the PI-CS can be found in Appendix
D
and
E
.

IV
Numerical simulation

To benchmark the classical simulation algorithm introduced in Section
III
, we consider the task of ground state preparation via a digitized adiabatic quantum computing (AQC) protocol
1
;
2
;
8
. The AQC approach relies on the adiabatic theorem
13
, which states that a system initialized in the ground state of the initial Hamiltonian
H_{0}
remains in its instantaneous ground state provided the Hamiltonian governing the system varies sufficiently slowly compared to the inverse of the minimum energy gap
\Delta
between the ground state and the first excited state throughout the full evolution.

Given a target Hamiltonian
H_{1}
, the system is initially prepared in the ground state of a simple initial Hamiltonian
H_{0}
, and then driven adiabatically towards the ground state of
H_{1}
.
To this end, we introduce a schedule
s(t):[0,T]\to[0,1]
, satisfying the boundary condition
s(0)=0
and
s(T)=1
, where
T
denotes the total annealing time. The system dynamics is governed by a time-dependent Hamiltonian constructed as a linear interpolation between the initial and target Hamiltonians as

H(t)=\left(1-s(t)\right)H_{0}+s(t)H_{1}\;.

(45)

Ideally, the evolution operator can be written as:

U(T)=\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{T}H(t)dt\right)\;,

(46)

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the time-ordered operator.
While AQC is naturally implemented on analog quantum simulators, an equivalent procedure can be realized on the digital quantum hardware by discretizing the continuous time evolution.
In the digitized implementation, the continuous time evolution is approximated by a sequence of discrete time steps:

U(T)\approx\prod_{j=1}^{L}e^{-iH(t_{j})\Delta t}\,,

(47)

where
L
is the number of time steps,
\Delta t=\frac{T}{L}
the size of the time step, and
t_{j}=j\Delta t
discrete times. Each short-time exponential can be approximated by first- or second-order Trotter-Suzuki decomposition.
The final time evolution operator approximately follows the adiabatic trajectory up to controllable Trotter or non-adiabatic errors.

Throughout this work, we choose the initial
S_{n}
-equivariant Hamiltonian to be
H_{0}=-\sum_{i}^{n}X_{i}
, which is a standard and widely adopted choice in AQC protocols. The system is initialized in the ground state of
H_{0}
,

\rho_{0}=\outerproduct{\psi_0}{\psi_0}
, where
\ket{\psi_0}=\ket{+}^{\otimes n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}\ket{j}
, which corresponds to an equiprobable superposition over all computational basis states. Then, we take our target
S_{n}
-equivariant Hamiltonian to be that of the Lipkin–Meshkov–Glick (LMG) model
47
, a paradigmatic collective-spin model with infinite-range interactions. The Hamiltonian reads

H_{\rm LMG}=-\frac{J}{n}\sum_{i<j}\left(X_{i}X_{j}+\gamma Y_{i}Y_{j}\right)+h_{z}\sum_{i}Z_{i}\;

(48)

where
J
sets the interaction strength,
\gamma
controls anisotropy between
X
and
Y
couplings, and
h_{z}
is a longitudinal field along the
z
direction.

Since the initial state
\rho_{0}
lies in the fully symmetric irrep subspace labeled by
\lambda=(n,0)
, and both
H_{0}
and
H_{\mathrm{LMG}}
are
S_{n}
-equivariant, the dynamics remain confined to this symmetric subspace throughout the adiabatic evolution. This restriction is not merely an artificial simplification, but rather arises naturally in several physically relevant settings
37
;
70
. The LMG model within the symmetric sector has been extensively studied in the thermodynamic limit, and its ground-state properties are well understood using semiclassical approaches
23
;
24
;
64
.

In order to verify the fidelity of our classical simulation, we numerically compute several observables whose behavior has been characterized analytically in the aforementioned references. All simulations were performed using the Julia
11
on a 12-core CPU system with 36 GB of memory. The number of adiabatic time steps is chosen to scale linearly with respect to the system size,
L\in\order{n}
, to ensure the convergence of the digitized adiabatic evolution

. Although this scaling is adapted empirically without computing a rigorous bound based on the minimum spectral gap, the numerical results presented validate this choice.

V
Discussion

Understanding the classical simulability of families of quantum circuits is fundamental to determining the boundary between what is classical and what is genuinely quantum. Here, the gold standard is obtaining classical algorithms which scale polynomially with the system size. However, as any practitioner can attest, “polynomial scaling” does not always imply practical tractability. Indeed, even modest improvements in polynomial scaling and prefactors can determine whether a given circuit is simulable, or not, in practice. This is precisely the goal of our paper, as we have presented a more efficient algorithm to simulate physically motivated
S_{n}
-equivariant quantum circuits. Not only is our reported worst-case scaling better than that of previous techniques, but the actual clock-time obtained from the practical implementation of our algorithm shows that this bound is loose, and that the scaling is much more favorable than that of our theorems.

Moving forward, we present two future research directions. The first is to extend our methods to
S_{n}
-equivariant systems of qudits with
d>2
. In this setting, the irrep label
\lambda
is partitioned into
d
parts,
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
, which can make it harder to construct explicit Schur basis states. We expect that such a change will increase the time and memory complexity. Here, we also note that given an initial state, its irrep description can still be easily acquired using PI-CS
57
.

Next, we leave for future work the comparison of physical wall-clock execution time between our classical simulation algorithm and the corresponding implementation on a quantum computer. Here, we expect that quantum deployment of
S_{n}
-equivariant quantum circuits could be difficult in most architectures, as one needs to create all-to-all entanglement among the qubits. If entangling gates must be executed sequentially, this could incur deeper circuits and longer wall-clock times. However, some architectures, such as those based on trapped ions, can exploit all-to-all interaction via shared motional modes—often referred to as a quantum bus—together with Mølmer–Sørensen type entangling gate
50
. Such architectures could potentially be more amenable for
S_{n}
-equivariant circuits. Hence, one can readily see that a fair comparison between classical and quantum simulation run-times will ultimately be hardware dependent. Still, we expect that
S_{n}
-equivariant circuits could constitute a practical case where shadows plus classical simulation could scale more favorably than most quantum implementations
17
.

Theorem 3

Proof.

The proof exploits two facts: (i) the canonical Schur basis states consist of an antisymmetric register of
m
singlet pairs and a fully symmetric register of
n-2m
qubits; (ii) the symmetrized Pauli string
\mathcal{T}_{S_{n}}(P_{\bm{k}})
is permutation-invariant. Therefore, the action of
\mathcal{T}_{S_{n}}(P_{\bm{k}})
depends only on how many Pauli operators of each type act on the singlet pairs and on the
\ket{0}
/
\ket{1}
positions in the symmetric register. We thus decompose the action of
\mathcal{T}_{S_{n}}(P_{\bm{k}})
into an antisymmetric contribution and a symmetric contribution, and count all admissible assignments combinatorially.

As stated in Eq. (
23
) of the main text, the canonical Schur basis is defined as

\ket{\lambda,p_\lambda^0,q_\lambda}=\ket{\Psi}^{\otimes m}\otimes\ket{\Sigma_{q_\lambda}^{(n-2m)}},

where
\ket{\Psi}=(\ket{01}-\ket{10})/\sqrt{2}
is the two-qubit singlet state and
\ket{\Sigma_{q_\lm}^{(n-2m)}}
is the normalized symmetric state of Hamming weight
q_{\lambda}
on
n-2m

\mathcal{T}_{S_{n}}(P_{\bm{k}})
is invariant under permutations, it suffices to count the number of ways Pauli operators can be assigned to these two sectors.

In what follows, we will decompose our proof into studying how an operator
\mathcal{T}_{S_{n}}(P_{\bm{k}})
acts over the Schur basis, first studying its action on the antisymmetric part, then on the symmetric part, and keeping track of phases, combinatorial factors and normalization.

We thus begin by considering the action of a symmetrized Pauli on the antisymmetric singlet pairs. For this purpose, consider a single singlet pair,
\ket{\Psi}
. As the operator
\mathcal{T}_{S_{n}}(P_{\bm{k}})
is fully symmetric while the state
\ket{\Psi}
is antisymmetric, the action of all mixed Pauli pairs cancel and only identical Pauli contribute non-trivially. For instance, one can verify that the following cases hold

\mathcal{T}_{S_{2}}(P\otimes Q)\ket{\Psi}=\begin{cases}\ket{\Psi},&P=Q=I,\\
-\ket{\Psi},&P=Q\in\{X,Y,Z\},\\
0,&P\neq Q\text{ and }P,Q\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}.\end{cases}

The next step is to study how symmetrized Paulis act on the symmetric register. Due to the permutation symmetry of the operators and the state, it suffices to evaluate the action of the symmetrized Pauli string solely on a representative computational basis

At this point we note the action of

\frac{(n-2m-q_{\lambda})!}{(k_{X}-2a_{X}-s_{X})!(k_{Y}-2a_{Y}-s_{Y})!(k_{Z}-2a_{Z}-s_{Z})!(n-2m-q_{\lambda}-k+2\norm{\vec{a}}_{1}+\norm{\vec{s}}_{1})!}\;.

\mathcal{V}

\mathcal{M}
is invertible, allowing one to explicitly construct its inverse.

\hat{\rho}_{V,w}=\mathcal{M}^{-1}_{\mathcal{V}}\left(V^{\dagger}\Pi_{w}V\right)\;.

(63)

By repeating this procedure
N
times, one collects
N
independent snapshots with the corresponding measurement data
\{V,\ket{w}\}
.
Since
\hat{\rho}_{V,w}
forms unbiased estimators of
\rho
, we can compute an unbiased estimator
\hat{o}_{i}
for the expectation value of the observable
O_{i}
given by

\hat{o}_{i}=\Tr[\hat{\rho}_{V,w}O_{i}]=\Tr[O_{i}\mathcal{M}^{-1}(V^{\dagger}\Pi_{w}V)]\;,

(64)

satisfying
\mathbb{E}_{V\sim\mathcal{V},w}[\hat{o}_{i}]=\Tr[\rho O_{i}]
.

In particular, the quantum channel
\mathcal{M}
defines an operator subspace
\mathcal{L}_{\rm vis}
, so-called visible operator space
61
, consisting of all the operators that can be estimated via the classical shadow:

\mathcal{L}_{\rm vis}=\mathfrak{Im}(\mathcal{M})={\rm span}\{V^{\dagger}\Pi_{w}V\;\lvert\;V\in\mathcal{V},\penalty\ \ket{w}\in\mathcal{W}\}\;\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\;,

(65)

where
\mathfrak{Im}(\mathcal{M})
denotes the image of
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is the space of linear operators acting on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.

A central goal of the classical shadow is to minimize the number of snapshots
N
required to accurately estimate the expectation values. This is controlled by the variance of the estimator,

{\rm Var}[\hat{o}_{i}]=\mathbb{E}_{V\sim\mathcal{V},w}[\hat{o}_{i}^{2}]-\left(\mathbb{E}_{V\sim\mathcal{V},w}[\hat{o}_{i}]\right)^{2}\leq\norm{O_i}^{2}_{\rm shadow}

(66)

where
\norm{O_i}^{2}_{\rm shadow}
denotes the shadow norm defined as

\norm{O_i}^{2}_{\rm shadow}=\max_{\sigma:{\rm state}}\mathbb{E}_{V\thicksim\mathcal{V},\penalty\ w}\left[\Tr[\mathcal{M}^{-1}(V^{\dagger}\Pi_{w}V)O]^{2}\right]\;.

(67)

The previous equation showcases that the shadow norm explicitly depends on the unitary ensemble
\mathcal{V}
as well as the measurement basis
\mathcal{W}

57
;
65
;
34
;
66
.

This leads to an upper bound on the number of snapshots
N
required for the classical shadow protocol to accurately estimate the set of expectation values
\{\Tr[\rho O_{i}]\}_{i=1}^{M}
up to an (additive) error
\epsilon
and the success probability of at least
1-\delta\in[0,1]
as follows

N\in\order{\frac{\log(M/\dl)}{\ep^2} \max_{i = 1, \dots M} \norm{O_i}^2_{\rm shadow}}\;,

(68)

Appendix D
Deep permutation-invariant classical shadow

In this section, we provide a review of a variant of classical shadows, tailored for quantum circuits with symmetry, specifically permutation group. We focus on Permutation Invariant Classical Shadow (PI-CS)
57
, which is applied to a block-diagonalized quantum state by preparing the initial state
\rho
and implementing the Schur transform on a quantum computer.

D.1
Symmetric classical shadow

Given a group
G
with unitary representation
R\to\mathbb{U}(\mathcal{H})
, the goal of symmetric classical shadow protocol is to choose the measurement primitives
\mathcal{V}
and the set of projections
\mathbf{P}
such that the corresponding visible operator space satisfies

\mathcal{L}_{\rm vis}=\mathcal{L}^{G}=\left\{A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\;\middle\lvert\;[A,R(g)]=0,\penalty\ \forall g\in G\right\}\;,

(69)

that is, all operators in the visible space (c.f. Eq. (
65
)) should be equivariant with respect to
G
. Here, we denote
\mathcal{L}^{G}=\text{comm}(G)
to explicitly express that it is a space of linear operators.

Figure 6:
Schematic diagram of Permutation Invariant Classical Shadow.
The initial state
\rho
is transformed into a block-diagonal form by the quantum circuit responsible for the Schur transform
U_{\rm QST}
. We perform the permutation invariant classical shadow by measuring only the irrep register
\lambda
and the dimension register
q_{\lambda}
, while leaving the multiplicity register
p_{\lambda}
untouched.

Let us formalize the symmetric classical shadow framework for estimating the expectation value
\Tr[\rho O_{i}]
of a quantum state
\rho
, where the observables
\{O_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,M}
are equivariant with respect to a symmetry group
G
. By complete reducibility of finite-dimensional unitary representations, there exists a basis set
\{\ket{\lm, p_\lm, q_\lm}\}_{\lambda,p_{\lambda},q_{\lambda}}
in which the unitary representation
R
admits an isotypic decomposition

R(g)\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda}r_{\lambda}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{\lambda}}\;,

(70)

where
r_{\lambda}
is the irrep of dimension
m_{\lambda}:=\text{dim}(r_{\lambda})
and
d_{\lambda}:=\text{mult}(r_{\lambda})
denotes its multiplicity.
Accordingly, the Hilbert space admits the corresponding isotypic decomposition

\mathcal{H}\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda}\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda}\left(S_{\lambda}\otimes W_{\lambda}\right)\;,

(71)

where
S_{\lambda}
is the space of the irrep
r_{\lambda}
and
W_{\lambda}\cong\text{Hom}_{G}(S_{\lambda},\mathcal{H})
is the multiplicity space, i.e., the space of
G
-equivariant linear maps from
S_{\lambda}
to
\mathcal{H}
whose dimension equals the multiplicity
d_{\lambda}
.
Under the same basis change, the
G
-equivariant operator
A
admits a block-diagonal decomposition of the form

A\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda}\mathds{1}_{m_{\lambda}}\otimes A_{\lambda}\;,

(72)

where
A_{\lambda}
acts only on the multiplicity space
W_{\lambda}
, and therefore has dimension
d_{\lambda}
.

We assume that the isotypic basis can be decomposed into a tensor product of separate registers as
\ket{\lm, p_\lm, q_\lm}=\ket{\lm}\otimes\ket{p_\lm}\otimes\ket{q_\lm}
. We will refer to each register as irrep, multiplicity and dimension registers, respectively, with
p_{\lambda}\in[m_{\lambda}]
and
q_{\lambda}\in[d_{\lambda}]
. Throughout, the terminology “multiplicity” and “dimension” refer to the block structure of
G
-equivariant operators (i.e., the commutant of the group action), rather than to the decomposition of the group representation itself, in order to remain consistent with the main text.
In particular, storing a basis label
q_{\lambda}
requires
\lceil\log_{2}(d_{\max})\rceil
qubits, where
d_{\max}=\max_{\lambda}(d_{\lambda})
denotes the largest irrep dimension of the group-equivariant operators. For example, in the case of the permutation group, the largest irrep dimension of the
S_{n}
-equivariant operators scales as
d_{\max}=\max_{\lambda}(d_{\lambda})=n+1
. Hence, the dimension register consists of
\lceil\log_{2}(n+1)\rceil
qubits, while the associated Hilbert space has dimension
n+1
. Finally, we emphasize that the definitions of dimension and multiplicity differ from those used in the original study
57
. In this work, we define these notions for the irrep blocks of a commutant algebra whereas Ref.
57
defines them for irreducible components in unitary representations of the group. These roles are effectively interchanged between the two contexts, which can lead to confusion if not carefully distinguished. In particular, what we denote as the largest dimension of the group-commutant irrep,
d_{\max}
, corresponds to the largest multiplicity of a group irrep underlying the shadow protocol.

The key idea of the symmetric classical shadow is to perform the measurement only on the irrep and dimension registers, while leaving the multiplicity register untouched. Accordingly, we consider random unitaries
V\in\mathcal{V}
that has the form
V=\mathds{1}\otimes\mathds{1}\otimes V_{\lambda}
, where
V_{\lambda}
acts solely on the dimension register, as illustrated in Fig.
6
.

The symmetric shadow protocol proceeds as follows:

1.

Apply a quantum circuit
U_{\rm block}
to block-diagonalize the initial state.

2.

Measure the irrep register
\ket{\lm}
, obtaining the irrep label
\lambda
.

3.

Apply a random unitary
V_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{V}
to the dimension register
\ket{q_\lm}
.

4.

Measure the dimension register, yielding outcome
q_{\lambda}
.

Within this procedure, the corresponding projective measurement operators are given by:

\mathbf{P}^{G}=\left\{\Pi_{\lambda,q_{\lambda}}\right\}_{\lambda,q_{\lambda}}=\left\{\sum_{p_{\lambda}=1}^{m_{\lambda}}\outerproduct{\lm}{\lm}\otimes\outerproduct{p_\lm}{p_\lm}\otimes\outerproduct{q_\lm}{q_\lm}\right\}_{q_{\lambda}=1}^{d_{\lambda}}\;,

(73)

and given the unitary primitives
\mathcal{V}
, the resulting visible space
\mathcal{L}_{\rm vis}
contains operators:

V^{\dagger}\Pi_{\lambda,q_{\lambda}}V=\sum_{p_{\lambda}=1}^{m_{\lambda}}\outerproduct{\lm}{\lm}\otimes\outerproduct{p_\lm}{p_\lm}\otimes V_{\lambda}^{\dagger}\outerproduct{q_\lm}{q_\lm}V_{\lambda}=\outerproduct{\lm}{\lm}\otimes\mathds{1}\otimes V_{\lambda}^{\dagger}\outerproduct{q_\lm}{q_\lm}V_{\lambda}\,.

(74)

Therefore,
\mathcal{L}_{\rm vis}\subseteq{\rm comm}(G)
, which consists of
G
-equivariant observables of the form in Eq. (
72
). Thus, it suffices to apply standard classical shadow techniques—such as local or global Clifford measurements—on
q
-qubits of the dimension register.

Mathematically, the expectation value can be written as a sum over the irreps:

o=\Tr[\rho O]=\sum_{\lambda}\Tr[\rho\left(\mathds{1}^{m_{\lambda}}\otimes O_{\lambda}\right)]=\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{p_{\lambda}=1}^{m_{\lambda}}\Tr[\rho^{p_{\lambda}}_{\lambda}O_{\lambda}]\;.

(75)

Here
\rho_{\lambda}^{p_{\lambda}}
denotes the
p_{\lambda}
-th diagonal multiplicity block, defined as the restriction of the state
\rho
to the irrep subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}^{p_{\lambda}}={\rm span}\{\ket{\lm, p_\lm, q_\lm}\}_{q_{\lambda}=1}^{d_{\lambda}}
. More precisely,
\rho_{\lambda}^{p_{\lambda}}
is an operator acting on
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}^{p_{\lambda}}
, i.e,
\rho_{\lambda}^{p_{\lambda}}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}^{p_{\lambda}})\cong\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{d_{\lambda}})
, and is obtained by projecting
\rho
onto the
p_{\lambda}
-th copy of the irrep as

\rho_{\lambda}^{p_{\lambda}}=(\bra{\lambda, p_\lm}\otimes\mathds{1})\,\rho\,(\ket{\lm, p_\lm}\otimes\mathds{1})\;,

(76)

where we denote
\ket{\lambda, p_\lm}
the orthonormal basis living in the multiplicity space,
\mathbb{C}^{m_{\lambda}}
.

It is worth noting that, in Ref.
57
, the authors restrict attention to symmetric states of the form
\rho\cong\bigoplus_{\lambda}\mathds{1}_{m_{\lambda}}\otimes\rho_{\lambda}
, for which the reduced state
\rho_{\lambda}
is identical across all multiplicity sectors. In this case, the sum in Eq. (
75
) simplifies to
o=\sum_{\lambda}m_{\lambda}\Tr[\rho_{\lambda}O_{\lambda}]
as the state
\rho_{\lambda}
is repeated
m_{\lambda}
times. However, for a general input state
\rho
, the reduced states
\rho^{p_{\lambda}}_{\lambda}
corresponding to different multiplicity labels
p_{\lambda}
need not coincide, as visually explained in Fig.
1c
.

Upon measuring the irrep label, the outcome label
\lambda
is obtained with probability
p(\lambda)=\Tr[\tau_{\lambda}]
where we denote
o_{\lambda}=\Tr[\tau_{\lambda}O_{\lambda}]
with
\tau_{\lambda}=\sum_{p_{\lambda}=1}^{m_{\lambda}}\rho_{\lambda}^{p_{\lambda}}
. If
\rho
is also
G
-equivariant, the probability simplifies to
p(\lambda)=m_{\lambda}\Tr[\rho_{\lambda}]
, which is presented in Ref.
57
. Tracing out the multiplicity space yields the normalized reduced state
\tilde{\tau}_{\lambda}=\tau_{\lambda}/\Tr[\tau_{\lambda}]
. Applying the classical shadow protocol on the dimension register then allows one to construct an unbiased estimator
\hat{o}_{\lambda}
of
o_{\lambda}=\Tr[\tilde{\tau_{\lambda}}O_{\lambda}]
such that the total expectation value is recovered as
o=\sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda)o_{\lambda}
.

It follows that the unbiased estimator
\hat{o}
of
o
has variance defined as

{\rm Var}[\hat{o}]_{\rm{block-CS}}=\left(\sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda){\rm Var}[\hat{o}_{\lambda}]_{\rm{CS}}\right)+{\rm Var}_{\lambda}o_{\lambda}\;.

(77)

Here, we use the subscripts
block-CS
and
CS
to denote that the variance arises from the symmetric classical shadow and the standard classical shadow applied within each irrep sector, respectively. The first term corresponds to the average shadow-induced variance within each sector
\lambda
, while the second term captures the classical variance associated with sampling over irrep labels. Using the fact that
{\rm Var}[\hat{o}_{\lambda}]_{\rm CS}\leq\norm{O_\lm}_{\rm CS}\leq d_{\max}^{2}\norm{O}_{\infty}^{2}
and
{\rm Var}_{\lambda}o_{\lambda}\leq\norm{O_\lm}^{2}_{\infty}\leq\norm{O}^{2}_{\infty}
together with standard shadow norm bounds, we obtain the following variance bounds:

{\rm Var}[\hat{o}]_{\rm{block-LC}}\leq(d_{\max}^{2}+1)\norm{O}^{2}_{\infty}

(78)

{\rm Var}[\hat{o}]_{\rm{block-GC}}\leq 3(d_{\max}^{2}+1)\norm{O}^{2}_{\infty}

(79)

with the notation
LC
for local Clifford and
GC
for global Clifford measurements on the dimension register. The result implies that the sample complexity of the symmetric shadow protocol is bounded by the largest irrep dimension of the group-equivariant operators, which is equivalently given by the largest irrep multiplicity of the underlying group representation.

D.2
Permutation-invariant classical shadow

We now focus on the case
G=S_{n}
and formalize the PI-CS framework. The most direct implementation uses quantum Schur transform (QST), denoted as
U_{\rm QST}
, which changes the basis from the computational basis into Schur basis.
Efficient quantum circuits implementing QST have already been proposed with the quantum time complexity of
\order{n \cdot\poly(\log\ep_{\rm QST}^{-1})}
where
\epsilon_{\rm QST}
quantifies the accumulated approximation error
6
;
7
;
41
;
5
.

Under this setting, following Eqs. (
78
) and (
79
), the variance of the estimated expectation values can be bounded as
57

{\rm Var}[\hat{o}]_{\rm QST-LC}\leq(n^{2}+2n+2)\norm{O}_{\infty}^{2},

(80)

and

{\rm Var}[\hat{o}]_{\rm QST-GC}\leq 3(n^{2}+2n+2)\norm{O}_{\infty}^{2}\,,

(81)

where we use
d_{\max}=n+1
for the Schur basis.
In particular, for the (normalized) symmetrized Pauli operators defined in Eq. (
28
), the spectral norm satisfies
\norm{O}_{\infty}\leq 1
by its construction.

Following the bounds of the variance, we obtain the following theorem for quantum sample complexity using both local and global Clifford measurements.

Theorem 4

(Quantum Sample Complexity of deep PI-CS)
.

Consider a general input state
\rho
and a collection of
M

S_{n}
-equivariant observables
O_{i}
, as well as parameters
\epsilon,\delta>0
. Then, with probability (at least)
1-\delta
, classical shadows of size

N\in\order{\log(\frac{M}{\dl})\frac{n^2}{\ep^2} \max_i \norm{O_i}^2_\infty}\;,

(82)

suffice to jointly estimate all
M
expectation values up to additive accuracy
\epsilon
.

Although this approach is conceptually straightforward, it requires substantial circuit overhead. In particular, implementing QST requires polynomial depth circuit and additional qubits, since ancillary registers are needed. Another alternative method that mitigates this quantum overhead is shallow permutation classical shadows which achieves quantum data acquisition using
\order{1}
circuit depth by avoiding the explicit implementation of the Schur transform.
However, this advantage comes at the cost of increased classical post processing. In particular, reconstructing components of the state projected onto irrep subspace becomes computationally expensive, while access to the quantity
\sum_{p_{\lambda}=1}^{d_{\lambda}}\rho_{\lambda}^{p_{\lambda}}
is essential for efficient classical simulation as highlighted in Eq. (
26
). This underlines the inherent trade-off between quantum resources (circuit depth and qubit count) and classical resources (post-processing).

Appendix E
Efficient PI-CS shadow for
S_{n}
-equivariant input state

Now, we consider the case where the initial state is also
S_{n}
-equivariant, i.e.,
\rho=\bigoplus_{\lambda}\mathds{1}_{m_{\lambda}}\otimes\rho_{\lambda}
with
\rho_{\lambda}
a
d_{\lambda}\times d_{\lambda}
sized matrix. In this case, we are allowed to use symmetrized PI-CS, which avoids implementing a QST. In this section, we summarize the symmetrized PI-CS protocol formulated entirely in Pauli basis and Schur basis. Reader may refer to Appendix B and C in Ref.
57
for further details as well as rigorous mathematical proofs of the method.

E.1
Symmetrized PI-CS protocol

In symmetrized PI-CS, we take the projective measurement ensemble
\mathcal{V}_{\rm symm-PI}
defined as:

\mathcal{V}_{\rm symm-PI}=\{V=W^{\otimes n}\;\lvert\;W\in\mathbb{SU}(2)\}

(83)

In practice, each run draws a single-qubit gate
W
from the Haar measure on
\mathbb{SU}(2)
by parameterizing
W
with a vector of Euler-angles
\bm{\theta}=[\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}]
as

W_{\bm{\theta}}=e^{-i\frac{\theta_{3}}{2}Z}e^{-i\frac{\theta_{2}}{2}Y}e^{-i\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}Z},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{with }\bm{\theta}\penalty\ \text{sampled from }\begin{cases}p(\theta_{1})=p(\theta_{3})=\frac{1}{2\pi},&\theta_{1},\theta_{3}\in[0,2\pi]\;,\\
p(\theta_{2})=\frac{\sin(\bt_2)}{2},&\theta_{2}\in[0,\pi]\;.\end{cases}

(84)

After applying
V=W^{\otimes n}
, the measurement is performed with a set of Hamming weight projectors, which consists of
n+1
projectors given by

\Pi_{h}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\\
{\rm HW}(x)=h\end{subarray}}\outerproduct{x}{x}=\frac{1}{h!(n-h)!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\sigma\left(\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes(n-h)}\outerproduct{1}{1}^{\otimes h}\right)\sigma^{\dagger}\;.

(85)

Then, the classical-shadow estimator for an observable
O
can be explicitly written as:

\hat{o}=\Tr[\hat{\rho}O]=\Tr[O\mathcal{M}^{-1}_{\rm symm-PI}\left((W^{\dagger}_{\bm{\theta}})^{\otimes n}\Pi_{h}W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\otimes n}\right)]\;.

(86)

E.2
Constructing classical shadow estimates

To construct the classical shadow estimates for symmetrized PI-CS, we evaluate the expectation value
\mathbb{E}_{V}[(V^{\dagger})^{\otimes 2}\tilde{\Pi}V^{\otimes 2}]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[(W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger})^{\otimes 2n}\tilde{\Pi}W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\otimes 2n}]
in Eq. (
62
).
We introduce an orthonormal symmetrized Pauli basis set which consists of all the symmetrized Pauli string
\mathcal{T}_{S_{n}}(P_{\bm{k}})
defined over
n
-qubits

B_{\rm Pauli}=\left\{B_{\bm{k}}=\mathcal{T}_{S_{n}}\left(P_{\bm{k}}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{dn!(n-k)!\bm{k}!}}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}R(\sigma)\left(X^{\otimes k_{X}}Y^{\otimes k_{Y}}Z^{\otimes k_{Z}}\mathds{1}^{\otimes n-k}\right)R^{\dagger}(\sigma)\right\}_{\bm{k}}\;,

(87)

where
d=2^{n}
denotes the Hilbert space dimension.
Here, we use a shorthanded notation
\bm{k}!=k_{X}!k_{Y}!k_{Z}!
and
P_{\bm{k}}
is defined as Eq. (
4
) with
k=\absolutevalue{\vec{k}}
. The basis spans
\mathrm{comm}(S_{n})
with dimension

d_{\rm PI}=|B_{\rm Pauli}|=\binom{n+1}{3}=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}{6}\;.

(88)

Expressed in this basis, the two-copy moment admits the expansion

\mathbb{E}_{W}\left[(W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger})^{\otimes 2n}\tilde{\Pi}W^{\otimes 2n}_{\bm{\theta}}\right]=\sum_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}c(\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime})B_{\bm{k}}\otimes B_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\,,

(89)

where
c(\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime})
builds a matrix
C
of size
d_{\rm PI}\times d_{\rm PI}
. This matrix
C
fully characterizes the symmetrized PI measurement channel
\mathcal{M}_{\rm symm-PI}
leading to the expression

\mathcal{M}_{\rm symm-PI}(\rho)=\sum_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}c(\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime})\Tr[\rho B_{\bm{k}}]B_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\;.

(90)

For a given linear operator
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, the action of the symmetrized PI measurement channel admits a compact vectorized representation,

|\mathcal{M}_{\rm symm-PI}(A)\rangle\!\rangle=C|A^{S_{n}}\rangle\!\rangle\;,

(91)

where
A^{S_{n}}=\mathcal{T}_{S_{n}}(A)
denotes the projection of
A
onto the symmetric subspace
{\rm comm}(S_{n})
. The vector
|A^{S_{n}}\rangle\!\rangle
is a
d_{\rm PI}
dimensional vector defined via its components in the symmetrized Pauli basis as
|A^{S_{n}}\rangle\!\rangle_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha\in[d_{\rm PI}]}\Tr[B_{\alpha}^{\dagger}A]|B_{\alpha}\rangle\!\rangle
. Here, we relabel the multi-index
\bm{k}
by a single index
\alpha\in[d_{\rm PI}]
for notational simplicity.

Since the measurement channel is supported on
{\rm comm}(S_{n})
, its (pseudo-)inverse
\mathcal{M}_{\rm symm-PI}^{-1}
is obtained by inverting the matrix
C
on this subspace. Explicitly, the inverse channel acts as
|\mathcal{M}_{\rm symm-PI}(A)\rangle\!\rangle=C^{-1}|A^{S_{n}}\rangle\!\rangle
.
Combining the expression with Eq. (
86
), the classical shadow estimator for the expectation value of an observable
O
can be written in vectorized form as:

\hat{o}=\Tr[\hat{\rho}O]=\langle\!\langle(W^{\dagger}_{\bm{\theta}})^{\otimes n}\Pi_{h}W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\otimes n}|C^{-1}|O\rangle\!\rangle

(92)

with the vector

|(W^{\dagger}_{\bm{\theta}})^{\otimes n}\Pi_{h}W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\otimes n}\rangle\!\rangle=\sum_{\bm{k}}\Tr[B_{\bm{k}}(W^{\dagger}_{\bm{\theta}})^{\otimes n}\Pi_{h}W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\otimes n}]|B_{\bm{k}}\rangle\!\rangle\;.

(93)

Therefore, evaluating the symmetrized PI-CS estimator reduces to two tasks: (i) computing matrix
C
that characterizes the measurement channel, and (ii) evaluating the entries of the measurement vector
|(W^{\dagger}_{\bm{\theta}})^{\otimes n}\Pi_{h}W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\otimes n}\rangle\!\rangle
in the symmetrized Pauli basis.

We begin by computing the matrix
C
. To this end, it is convenient to first express the Hamming weight projectors
\{\Pi_{h}\}
in the symmetrized Pauli basis. We introduce the shorthand notation=
B^{m}=B_{(0,0,m,n-m)}=\mathcal{T}_{S_{n}}(Z^{\otimes m}\mathds{1}^{\otimes(n-m)})
which denotes the normalized symmetrization of Pauli-
Z
operators acting on
m
qubits. In this basis, each Hamming-weight projector with weight
h
admits the form

\Pi_{h}=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\alpha(h,m)B^{m}\;.

(94)

Since
B^{m}
are orthonormal, the coefficients
\alpha(h,m)
can be evaluated as

\alpha(h,m)=\Tr[B^{m}\Pi_{h}]=d^{-\frac{1}{2}}\binom{n}{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}a(h,m),\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{with }a(h,m)=\sum_{l=0}^{h}\binom{m}{l}\binom{n-m}{h-l}(-1)^{l}\;.

(95)

We now evaluate the expression of
2n
-qubit operator
\widetilde{\Pi}
defined in Eq. (
62
). Substituting Eq. (
94
) into its definition yields

\tilde{\Pi}=\sum_{h=0}^{n}\left(\Pi_{h}\right)^{\otimes 2}=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=0}^{n}\tilde{\alpha}(m,m^{\prime})B^{m}\otimes B^{m^{\prime}},\qquad\tilde{\alpha}(m,m^{\prime})=\sum_{h}\alpha(h,m)\alpha(h,m^{\prime}).

(96)

Inserting Eq. (
96

\mathbb{E}_{W}\left[(W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger})^{\otimes 2n}\tilde{\Pi}W^{\otimes 2n}_{\bm{\theta}}\right]
given by Eq. (
89

c(\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\frac{(-1)^{\frac{\absolutevalue{k - k'}}{2}}}{d(k+k^{\prime}+1)\sqrt{\bm{k}!\bm{k^{\prime}}!(n-k)!(n-k^{\prime})!}}\cdot\frac{(\bm{k}+\bm{k^{\prime}})!}{\left(\frac{\bm{k}+\bm{k}^{\prime}}{2}\right)!}\cdot\frac{(2n-k-k^{\prime})!}{\left(\frac{2n-k-k^{\prime}}{2}\right)!}&\text{if }k_{\alpha}+k^{\prime}_{\alpha}\in 2\mathbb{N},\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\alpha\in\{X,Y,Z\}\,,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(97)

We again use the shorthand notation for multi-index factorials
\bm{k}!=k_{X}!\cdot k_{Y}!\cdot k_{Z}!
and denote by
k:=\absolutevalue{\vec{k}}=k_{X}+k_{Y}+k_{Z}
the total weight of the vector
\bm{k}
.
The parity constraint implies that
C
decomposes into 8 independent blocks, corresponding to the parity sectors of
(k_{X},k_{Y},k_{Z})
. Constructing
C
explicitly requires
\order{n^6}
operations.

Having determined
C
, we now compute the entries of the measurement vector
|(W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger})^{\otimes n}\Pi_{h}W_{\bm{\theta}}\rangle\!\rangle
on which the inverse matrix
C^{-1}
acts following Eq. (

). Using the expansion of
\Pi_{h}
in Eq. (

(see Eq. (

)) and single-qubit rotation

(c.f. Eq. (

)), we obtain

\displaystyle\Tr[B_{\bm{k}}(W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger})^{\otimes n}\Pi_{h}W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\otimes n}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\alpha(h,k)\Tr[B_{\bm{k}}(W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger})^{\otimes n}B^{m}W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\otimes n}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{n!}}{\sqrt{d\bm{k}!(n-k)!}}a(h,k)\cos(\bt_1)^{k_{X}}\sin(\bt_1)^{k_{Y}}\sin(\bt_2)^{k_{X}+k_{Y}}\cos(\bt_2)^{k_{Z}}\;.

(98)

Together with the inverse matrix
C^{-1}
, these expressions fully determining the symmetrized PI-CS estimator.

The main computational bottleneck of evaluating symmetrized PI-CS estimators is the inversion of the matrix
C
given in Eq. (
64
). By reordering the vector
\bm{k}
, we can reduce the matrix
C
into 8 blocks, thereby reducing the effective size of the matrices that must be handled. Nevertheless, a direct inversion of these blocks remains computationally expensive.
In practice, we therefore avoid explicitly computing
C^{-1}
, which costs
\order{d_{\rm PI}^3}\approx\order{n^9}
. Instead, we perform a one-time LU decomposition of
C
and subsequently solve the system of linear equations
C\mathinner{\left.\left|{x}\right\rangle\kern-1.72218pt\right\rangle}=|O\rangle\!\rangle
for each observable
O
of interest. The following theorem summarizes the above discussion:

Theorem 5

(Cost of post-processing symmetrized PI-CS in symmetrized Pauli basis)
.

Consider the symmetrized PI-CS applied on
S_{n}
-equivariant initial state. Under the assumption that matrix
C
is precomputed and its LU decomposition is evaluated, the cost of post-processing the symmetrized PI-CS scales as
N_{\rm C.T.}\in\order{n^6}
.

Finally, the following bound can be found for the observable estimator:

{\rm Var}[\hat{o}]\leq(2n+1)\norm{O}_{F}^{2}.

(99)

where
\norm{A}_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm of the matrix
A
, determining the quantum sample complexity of symmetrized PI-CS protocol.

E.3
Symmetrized PI-CS in Schur basis

One issue with the symmetrized PI-CS in the symmetrized Pauli basis explained in the section above is that we cannot obtain
\hat{o}_{\lambda}
, which is required in the classical simulation procedure explained in the main text. Therefore, it is needed to work in Schur basis.
In this section, we formulate the symmetrized PI-CS protocol expressed in the Schur basis. Up to this point, we have interpreted the dimension label
q_{\lambda}
as the Hamming weight associated with the symmetric component of the canonical Schur basis. Here, we introduce a new label
\tilde{q}_{\lambda}
, defined by

\tilde{q}_{\lambda}\equiv\tilde{q}_{\lambda(m)}=q_{\lambda}-\left(\frac{n}{2}-m\right)\;,

(100)

which satisfies
\tilde{q}_{\lambda}\in Q_{\lambda}
where
Q_{\lambda}
denotes the set of all admissible half-integer values of
\tilde{q}_{\lambda}
for the given irrep label
\lambda
as

Q_{\lambda}=\{-s_{\lambda},-s_{\lambda}+1,\dots,s_{\lambda}-1,s_{\lambda}\}\;,\qquad s_{\lambda}\equiv s_{\lambda(m)}=\frac{n}{2}-m\;,

(101)

with
|Q_{\lambda}|=d_{\lambda}=n-2m+1
. With this convention, the Schur basis states are relabeled as
\ket{\lm, p_\lm, q_\lm}\equiv\ket{\lm, p_\lm, \tilde{q}_\lm}
.

Using the relabeled Schur basis states, we begin by introducing an orthonormal PI operator Schur basis, defined as

B_{\rm Schur}=\left\{B_{\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}^{\prime}_{\lambda}}^{\lambda}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m_{\lambda}}}\sum_{p_{\lambda}=1}^{m_{\lambda}}\outerproduct{\lm, p_\lm, \tilde{q}_\lm}{ \lm, p_\lm, \tilde{q}'_\lm}\right\}_{\lambda,\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}^{\prime}_{\lambda}}\;.

(102)

To derive the explicit form of the classical shadow estimators, we now express Eq. (
85
) in terms of the Schur basis as

\Pi_{h}=\sum_{\lambda:\tilde{q}(h)\in Q_{\lambda}}B^{\lambda}_{\tilde{q}(h),\tilde{q}(h)}\sqrt{m_{\lambda}}\;,

(103)

where we define the notation
\tilde{q}(h)=h-\frac{n}{2}
to make explicit the dependence of the label
\tilde{q}
on the Hamming weight
h
. The condition
\lambda:\tilde{q}(h)\in Q_{\lambda}
indicates that the summation runs over irrep label
\lambda
for which the value
\tilde{q}(h)
belongs in the set
Q_{\lambda}
.
Having established this Schur-basis representation of
\Pi_{h}
, we now turn to constructing the associated
2n
-qubit operator
\widetilde{\Pi}
appearing in Eq. (
62
). Substituting Eq. (
103
) into the definition of
\widetilde{\Pi}
, we obtain

\widetilde{\Pi}=\sum_{h=0}^{n}(\Pi_{h})^{\otimes 2}=\sum_{\tilde{q}\in Q_{(n,0)}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda:\tilde{q}\in Q_{\lambda}\\
\nu:\tilde{q}\in Q_{\nu}\end{subarray}}\sqrt{m_{\lambda}m_{\nu}}\tilde{A}^{\lambda,\nu}_{(\tilde{q},\tilde{q}),(\tilde{q},\tilde{q})}\;,

(104)

where
Q_{(n,0)}
denotes the largest admissible set among the
Q_{\lambda}
, corresponding to the irrep
\lambda=(n,0)
with
s_{(n,0)}=\tfrac{n}{2}
. Here, we have introduced the operators
\tilde{A}^{\lambda,\nu}_{(\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}^{\prime}),(\tilde{q}_{\nu},\tilde{q}^{\prime}_{\nu})}
as

\tilde{A}^{\lambda,\nu}_{(\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}^{\prime}),(\tilde{q}_{\nu},\tilde{q}^{\prime}_{\nu})}=B^{\lambda}_{\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}^{\prime}}\otimes B^{\nu}_{\tilde{q}_{\nu},\tilde{q}_{\nu}^{\prime}}\;.

(105)

We next evaluate the average
\mathbb{E}_{W\sim\mathbb{SU}(2)}\left[(W^{\dagger})^{\otimes 2n}\tilde{\Pi}W^{\otimes 2n}\right]
, which corresponds to twirling
\widetilde{\Pi}
with respect to
2n
-fold tensor products
W^{2n}
. Equivalently, it implements the orthogonal projection of
\widetilde{\Pi}
onto the space of operators commuting with
W^{\otimes 2n}
for all
W\sim\mathbb{SU}(2)
. This commutant space can be spanned by an orthogonal set of
2n
-qubit Schur operator basis

B^{2n}=\left\{\widetilde{B}^{\mu}_{p_{\mu},p_{\mu}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{\mu}}}\sum_{\tilde{q}_{\mu}\in Q_{\lambda}}|\mu,p_{\mu},\tilde{q}_{\mu}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle\mu,p^{\prime}_{\mu},\tilde{q}_{\mu}|\right\}_{\mu,p_{\mu},p^{\prime}_{\mu}}\;.

(106)

Therefore, the average
\mathbb{E}_{W\sim\mathbb{SU}(2)}\left[(W^{\dagger})^{\otimes 2n}\tilde{\Pi}W^{\otimes 2n}\right]
can be expanded in terms of those basis as :

\mathbb{E}_{W\sim\mathbb{SU}(2)}\left[(W^{\dagger})^{\otimes 2n}\tilde{\Pi}W^{\otimes 2n}\right]=\sum_{p_{\mu},p^{\prime}_{\mu}}\Tr[\widetilde{B}^{\mu}_{p_{\mu},p_{\mu}^{\prime}}\widetilde{\Pi}]\widetilde{B}^{\mu}_{p_{\mu},p_{\mu}^{\prime}}\;.

(107)

We now introduce the coefficients
C^{\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\nu}}_{\lambda,\nu;\mu}
defined in terms of the standard Clebsch–Gordan coefficients
CG(j_{1},j_{2},J,m_{1},m_{2},M)
as

C^{\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\nu}}_{\lambda,\nu;\mu}=CG(s_{\lambda},s_{\nu},s_{\mu},\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\nu},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}+\tilde{q}_{\nu})\;.

(108)

This definition makes explicit the correspondence between the standard angular momentum quantum numbers
(j,m)
and the Schur basis related symbols
(s_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\lambda})
. Using these coefficients, we evaluate the overlap with respect to
\widetilde{B}^{\mu}_{p_{\mu},p_{\mu}^{\prime}}
and obtain the expansion

\mathbb{E}_{W\sim\mathbb{SU}(2)}\left[(W^{\dagger})^{\otimes 2n}\tilde{\Pi}W^{\otimes 2n}\right]=\sum_{\lambda,\nu}\sum_{\mu\doteq\lambda+\nu}\frac{\widetilde{\Pi}_{\mu,\lambda,\alpha}\sqrt{m_{\lambda}m_{\nu}}}{d_{\mu}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\nu},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}^{\prime},\tilde{q}_{\nu}^{\prime}\\
\tilde{q}_{\lambda}+\tilde{q}_{\nu}=\tilde{q}^{\prime}_{\lambda}+\tilde{q}^{\prime}_{\nu}\in Q_{\mu}\end{subarray}}C^{\tilde{q}_{\lambda}^{\prime},\tilde{q}_{\nu}^{\prime}}_{\lambda,\nu,\mu}C^{\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\nu}}_{\lambda,\nu;\mu}\widetilde{A}^{\lambda,\nu}_{(\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}^{\prime}_{\lambda}),(\tilde{q}_{\nu},\tilde{q}_{\nu}^{\prime})}\right)\;.

(109)

where the coefficient
\widetilde{\Pi}_{\mu,\lambda,\nu}
is given by projection of
\widetilde{\Pi}
on the operator
\tilde{B}^{\mu}_{p_{\mu}p_{\mu}}
as:

\widetilde{\Pi}_{\mu,\lambda,\nu}=\sqrt{m_{\mu}}\Tr[\widetilde{B}^{\mu}_{p_{\mu},p_{\mu}}\widetilde{\Pi}]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{q}:\\
\tilde{q}\in Q_{\lambda}\cap Q_{\nu}\\
2\tilde{q}\in Q_{\mu}\end{subarray}}(C^{\tilde{q},\tilde{q}}_{\lambda,\nu,\mu})^{2}\;.

(110)

In Eq. (
109
), the summation constraint
\mu\doteq\lambda+\alpha
indicates that the sum runs over those irrep
\mu
for which there exist
\tilde{q}_{\lambda}\in Q_{\lambda}
and
\tilde{q}_{\nu}\in Q_{\nu}
such that
\tilde{q}_{\lambda}+\tilde{q}_{\nu}\in Q_{\mu}
. This condition encodes the Clebsch–Gordan selection rules associated with angular momentum addition, and is equivalent to the triangle inequality between spin numbers
\absolutevalue{s_\lm- s_\nu}\leq s_{\mu}\leq s_{\lambda}+s_{\nu}
.

Inserting Eq. (
109
) to Eq. (
62
), we obtain the following expression for the measurement channel

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\rm symm-PI}(\rho)=\sum_{\nu}\sum_{\tilde{q}_{\nu},\tilde{q}_{\nu}^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}^{\prime}}c(\lambda,\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}^{\prime},\nu,\tilde{q}_{\nu},\tilde{q}_{\nu}^{\prime})\Tr[\rho B^{\lambda}_{\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}^{\prime}}]\right)B^{\nu}_{\tilde{q}_{\nu},\tilde{q}_{\nu}^{\prime}}

(111)

where the coefficient
c(\lambda,\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}^{\prime},\nu,\tilde{q}_{\nu},\tilde{q}_{\nu}^{\prime})
is defined as

c(\lambda,\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}^{\prime},\nu,\tilde{q}_{\nu},\tilde{q}_{\nu}^{\prime})=\mathds{1}_{[\tilde{q}_{\lambda}+\tilde{q}_{\nu}=\tilde{q}_{\lambda}^{\prime}+\tilde{q}_{\nu}^{\prime}]}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\\
\mu\doteq\lambda+\nu,\\
\tilde{q}_{\lambda}+\tilde{q}_{\nu}\in Q_{\mu}\end{subarray}}\frac{\widetilde{\Pi}_{\mu,\lambda,\nu}\sqrt{m_{\lambda}m_{\nu}}}{d_{\mu}}\left(C^{\tilde{q}_{\lambda}^{\prime},\tilde{q}_{\nu}^{\prime}}_{\lambda,\nu;\mu}C^{\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\nu}}_{\lambda,\nu;\mu}\right)\;.

(112)

Here,
\mathds{1}_{[\textrm{C}]}
denotes a function such that
\mathds{1}_{[\textrm{C}]}=1
if the condition
\rm C
is satisfied and
0
otherwise.

To further simplify the computation, we exploit the additional block structure of the measurement channel. To this end, we relabel the Schur operator basis
B^{\lambda}_{\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}^{\prime}}
as
B^{\lambda}_{\Delta_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}+\Delta_{\lambda}}
according to their shift
\Delta_{\lambda}=\tilde{q}_{\lambda}-\tilde{q}_{\lambda}^{\prime}
. Under this notation, the measurement channel
\mathcal{M}_{\rm symm-PI}
takes the form:

\mathcal{M}_{\rm symm-PI}(\rho)=\sum_{\nu}\sum_{\tilde{q}_{\nu},\Delta_{\nu}}\left(\sum_{\lambda}\sum_{\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\Delta_{\lambda}}\tilde{c}(B^{\lambda}_{\Delta_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}},B^{\nu}_{\Delta_{\nu},\tilde{q}_{\nu}})\Tr[\rho B^{\lambda}_{\Delta_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}}]\right)B^{\nu}_{\Delta_{\nu},\tilde{q}_{\nu}}\;,

(113)

with the coefficients defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{c}(B^{\lambda}_{\Delta_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}},B^{\nu}_{\Delta_{\nu},\tilde{q}_{\nu}})

\displaystyle\equiv c(\lambda,\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\lambda}+\Delta_{\lambda},\nu,\tilde{q}_{\nu},\tilde{q}_{\nu}+\Delta_{\nu})

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{[\Delta_{\lambda}=-\Delta_{\nu}]}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\\
\mu\doteq\lambda+\nu,\\
\tilde{q}_{\lambda}+\tilde{q}_{\nu}\in Q_{\mu}\end{subarray}}\frac{\widetilde{\Pi}_{\mu,\lambda,\nu}\sqrt{m_{\lambda}m_{\nu}}}{d_{\mu}}\left(C^{\tilde{q}_{\lambda}+\Delta_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\nu}+\Delta_{\nu}}_{\lambda,\nu;\mu}C^{\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}_{\nu}}_{\lambda,\nu;\mu}\right)\;.

(114)

By construction, the measurement channel has non-trivial contribution only when
\Delta_{\lambda}=-\Delta_{\nu}
. As a result, by reordering the basis operator according to the shift label
\Delta
, the matrix representation
C
of the measurement channel can be decomposed into a direct sum of independent blocks indexed by
(\Delta,-\Delta)
. As
\Delta\in\{-n,-n+1,\dots,n\}
, the channel decomposes into
2n+1
blocks, each acting on the subspace spanned by all basis operators satisfying
\Delta_{\lambda}=\Delta
. This block structure substantially reduces the computational complexity required for inverting
C
.

Finally, we compute the entries in the vector
|(W^{\dagger})^{\otimes n}\Pi_{h}W^{\otimes n}\rangle\!\rangle
on which we apply the inverse measurement channel with
W=W_{\bm{\theta}}
defined in Eq. (
84
). Recall that, under vectorization of operators, the adjoint action of an operator
A
on an arbitrary operator
B
,
B\mapsto A^{\dagger}BA
, is represented by a matrix
\bar{A}
acting on the vectorized form
|B\rangle\!\rangle
. We denote
\bar{Y}
and
\bar{Z}
to be the matrix form of the commutation action generated by
\sum_{i}Y_{i}
and
\sum_{i}Z_{i}
, respectively. With this notation, we obtain

|(W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger})^{\otimes n}\Pi_{h}W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\otimes n}\rangle\!\rangle=\exp\left(-i\frac{\theta_{3}}{2}\bar{Z}\right)\exp\left(-i\frac{\theta_{2}}{2}\bar{Y}\right)\exp\left(-i\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\bar{Z}\right)|\Pi_{h}\rangle\!\rangle\;,

(115)

where the entries of vector
|\Pi_{h}\rangle\!\rangle
can be evaluated using Eq. (
103
).

Combining all the results derived above, we can now compute the classical shadow estimator associated with each irrep. To this end, we define
\hat{o}_{\lambda}=\Tr[\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}O_{\lambda}]
where
\hat{\rho}_{\lambda}
denotes the estimator of
\rho_{\lambda}
, and rewrite Eq. (
86
) as a sum of
\hat{o}_{\lambda}
from each irrep
\lambda
as

\hat{o}=\Tr[\hat{\rho}O]=\sum_{\lambda}\Tr[\hat{\rho}(\mathds{1}_{m_{\lambda}}\otimes O_{\lambda})]=\sum_{\lambda}\langle\!\langle(W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\dagger})^{\otimes n}\Pi_{h}W_{\bm{\theta}}^{\otimes n}|C^{-1}|O_{\lambda}\rangle\!\rangle=:\sum_{\lambda}m_{\lambda}\hat{o}_{\lambda}

(116)

where the matrix
C
is block-diagonalized and the vector
|O_{\lambda}\rangle\!\rangle
has non-zero components only in the entries corresponding to the operator
B^{\lambda}_{\tilde{q}_{\lambda},\tilde{q}^{\prime}_{\lambda}}
. We emphasize that, although
C
is block-diagonal,
|O_{\lambda}\rangle\!\rangle
has support across multiple blocks of
C
, as each block is indexed solely by the shift values
(\Delta,-\Delta)
which is independent of
\lambda
.

As the above analysis shows, the computation of the classical shadow estimators requires evaluating a large number of Clebsch–Gordan coefficients to compute the CS estimators, which constitutes the main computational bottleneck of the protocol. Assuming that the cost of evaluating a single Clebsch–Gordan coefficient is
\order{1}
using a precomputed lookup table, the total computational cost scales at least as
\order{n^6}
, since the construction of
\mathcal{M}_{\rm symm-PI}
involves nested loops over the indices
\lambda
,
\nu
,
\mu
,
q_{\lambda}
,
q_{\nu}
and
\Delta
(see Eqs. (
113
) and (
114
)), each of which takes at most
\order{n}
distinct values. As a result, the procedure becomes computationally demanding for large numbers of qubits
n
. Without pre-computation, an additional overhead is incurred due to the repeated evaluation of the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients.

This cost can be amortized, since the measurement channel matrix
C
depends only on the measurement protocol and the number of qubits
n
, and is independent of the initial state. Hence, for a fixed
n
, the matrix
C
needs to be precomputed only once and can be reused across multiple CS simulations. In practice, this significantly reduces the per-simulation computational overhead and makes the approach feasible even for moderately large
n
.
