Abstract
. We present GlintMarkers , the first system to perform gaze-driven spatial perception using the inward-facing cameras on XR eyewear. Our key observation is that the cornea acts as a mirror that encodes both gaze direction and visual information about the environment in a small, low-contrast reflection. To extract spatial and semantic information from this reflection despite the camera’s limited pixel budget, we present a passive retroreflective marker design that concentrates reflected near-infrared light onto the cornea, producing bright glint patterns. We develop a custom Perspective-n-Point (PnP) estimation framework adapted to corneal imaging and perform orientation and distance estimation of tagged objects, as well as unique object identification.

1.
Introduction

“The eye embraces the beauty of the whole world.” - Leonardo da Vinci

Gaze has become an important input primitive on AR headsets (e.g. Apple Vision Pro
(
Apple 2026
)
) and XR glasses (Meta’s Orion
(
Meta 2026
)
) that increasingly integrate eye-facing cameras to support gaze-driven user interfaces. Gaze-driven interactions with the physical world have typically required the use of an additional outward facing camera to capture what the user is looking at.

Here, we ask:
Can we enable gaze-driven user interfaces with the physical world without an outward-facing camera?
Instead, we use the eye as a mirror that compresses the wide-angle visual richness of the world into a reflection visible at the cornea.

Figure 2
.
Example applications of
GlintMarkers

Our key observation is that images of the eye simultaneously provide dual visual capabilities:
first
the corneal reflection encodes spatial information that enables visual perception of the outside physical world (Fig.
1
),
second
these same images also capture gaze, revealing user’s visual attention.
Together, these two signals enable a new class of gaze-driven interactions with the physical environment (Fig.
2
):

Intent-aware object control.
By glancing at one light among several identical ones or a specific 3D printer in a makerspace, our system decodes the marker ID of the gazed object and routes commands to the correct target, resolving ambiguity that voice commands struggle with.

Proximity-aware assistance.
As a user approaches a gym machine, the system detects proximity, decodes its ID, and surfaces a tutorial without any explicit request. During furniture assembly, asking “Which is the right part?”, causes the system to leverage marker proximity and respond with the correct one.

Spatial state inference.
A quick glance at a cupboard reveals whether its door is open or closed based on the estimated pitch angle, and during furniture assembly the system can detect that a panel is being held at an incorrect orientation and alert the user before fastening.

The key technical challenge is that the entire wide-angle scene is compressed onto the small surface of the cornea, and the inward-facing camera captures this reflection with a limited pixel budget, making it difficult to recover fine-grained visual detail. This makes conventional computer vision algorithms and object tagging designs for object detection and pose estimation poorly suited for corneal imaging, demanding a new approach to spatial sensing. To address these challenges, we make three key technical contributions:

Retroreflective markers for spatial sensing in the cornea.
Conventional fiducial markers for spatial perception, such as QR codes and ArUco markers, require high visual contrast and deliberate camera positioning making them unsuitable for reliable decoding through low-contrast corneal reflections on XR eyewear.

We introduce a novel passive marker system consisting of miniature retroreflective patches that are designed to be decoded from corneal reflections. Retroreflective materials are unique as they are designed to concentrate the reflected optical energy back towards the source rather than spreading it in all directions, resulting in bright point reflections that manifest as sharp
glints
that stand out in the corneal reflections.

Our markers both serve as fiducial markers to estimate orientation and distance with respect to the eyewear and are able to encode a unique object ID. The markers require no power and can be used to mark objects of varying spatial scales ranging from desktop-sized devices such as coffee machines and laptops to larger appliances like refrigerators and cabinets.

Smart glasses for dual sensing of corneal reflections and gaze.
We build a lightweight, proof-of-concept eyewear prototype with a world-facing near-infrared LED to strobe the scene which causes retroreflective markers in the environment to be illuminated and produce glints in the corneal reflections. Our design includes an inward-facing, near-infrared camera facing the eye to measure corneal reflections and simultaneously track the user’s gaze. This design enables use even in dim indoor lighting environments.

Real-time spatial reconstruction framework.
We create a real-time geometric reconstruction framework to estimate orientation, and distance entirely from the 2D positions of glints visible in the corneal reflections using Perspective-n-Point (PnP) algorithms adapted to corneal reflections. We develop a one-time per-user calibration that accounts for individual differences in corneal curvature and eyewear fit. These spatial estimates, combined with gaze, augment AI agents with the spatial context needed to proactively and intelligently engage with the user and their environment.

Corneal reflections.
Corneal reflections have been studied as a source of indirect visual information about the environment. These reflections are difficult to decode, as they are low-contrast and reflect less than 1% of incident light
(
Nishino and Nayar 2004
)
, their convex geometry introduces distortions, and the texture of the iris contaminates the scene
(
Wang et al. 2008
)
, all of which vary between users due to physiological differences
(
Nitschke et al. 2013
)
.

Despite these challenges, prior works have developed techniques for a variety of visual understanding tasks using corneal reflection, including 3D scene reconstruction
(
Alzayer et al. 2024
)
, super-resolution
(
Nitschke and Nakazawa 2012
)
, lifelogging
(
Lander et al. 2016
)
, object recognition
(
Kaluarachchi et al. 2023
)
, visual place recognition
(
Ohshima et al. 2021
)
, and image registration
(
Nakazawa et al. 2016
)
. They have also been used part of video-oculography (VOG) eye-tracking systems that estimate gaze direction using corneal reflections as geometric landmarks for gaze estimation
(
George and Routray 2016
;
Wibirama et al. 2019
)
.

In contrast to these works,
GlintMarkers
differs in two ways. First, our work is focused on estimating two novel spatial primitives from corneal reflections, specifically 3-DoF orientation and distance estimation. This necessitates the design of a retroreflective marker scheme designed specifically for corneal reflections. Second, our system is designed to operate within the power and compute constraints of mobile AI glasses.

Passive fiducial markers.
Fiducial markers have been widely used for pose estimation and object identification. ARTag
(
Kato and Billinghurst 1999
)
, AprilTag
(
Wang and Olson 2016
;
Olson 2011
)
and ArUco
(
Kalaitzakis et al. 2021
)
are black-and-white 2D patterns that support 3D pose estimation and data encoding. These markers rely on high visual contrast and require a large surface area for reliable decoding at longer ranges which can be visually obtrusive. As a result, they are challenging to decode from low-contrast, low-resolution corneal reflections imaged from power-constrained mobile cameras.

Excitation-based markers have been developed to enable robust marker detection at longer ranges. InfraredTags
(
Dogan et al. 2022
)
, and BrightMarkers
(
Dogan et al. 2023
)
use infrared-reflecting and fluorescent materials that are excited by an ambient illumination source, with reflected signals captured by a camera. Operating in the NIR spectrum reduces optical interference, enabling detection at several meters using markers as small as one inch. RetroSphere
(
Balaji et al. 2023
)
leverages retroreflective spheres, which concentrate reflected light back toward the source, to achieve 6-DoF object tracking.

In contrast to these works,
GlintMarkers
introduces a retroreflective marker scheme that enables spatial sensing of distance and 3-DoF orientation even under the low-contrast and low-resolution constraints of corneal reflections. Our system further measures a user’s gaze and designs gaze-based interaction mechanisms to resolve which object among several the user intends to interact with.

3.1.
Smart glasses hardware design

We built a proof-of-concept eyewear prototype consisting of an eye-facing camera and a near-infrared (NIR) illuminator directed toward the scene (Fig.
5
). The prototype uses a 1 W, 850 nm NIR LED with a
120^{\circ}
viewing angle
(
LEDGUHON nd

. We selected 850 nm because commodity image sensors typically exhibit high sensitivity at this wavelength, while sensitivity decreases at longer NIR wavelengths.

To capture corneal reflections, we mounted a 4K RGB camera
(
AliExpress nd
)
on the lower rim of a commodity eyeglass frame, oriented upward toward the eye. We removed the camera’s built-in IR-cut filter and replaced it with a visible-light blocking filter
(
MidOpt nd
)

3.2.
Retroreflective marker design

GlintMarkers
are composed of multiple retroreflective patches which are designed to be visible within corneal reflections. Designing an effective marker requires balancing two competing concerns: individual patches must be large enough to appear as distinct, separable reflections in the corneal reflection, but the overall tag must remain small enough to attach to everyday objects. We first characterize how patch size and spacing affect detection performance across operating distances, then use these findings to inform our final tag design.

Conventional printed fiducial markers such as ArUco are not well suited to our setting. As shown in Fig.
6
, when a 20
\times
20 cm ArUco marker and a
GlintMarkers
marker are placed side by side and observed from 4 m, the ArUco marker is difficult to detect in the eye-facing camera. Eye-facing cameras typically operate in the near-infrared domain, whereas standard ArUco markers are designed for visible-light imaging and may not provide sufficiently reliable contrast under NIR illumination. In contrast, our retroreflective marker remains clearly visible under NIR lighting, motivating our use of marker designs optimized specifically for corneal-reflection sensing.

3.2.1.
Characterizing patch parameters

To characterize this tradeoff, we built an automated data collection platform (Fig.
3
) in which a marker board is mounted on a robotic arm that sweeps through a range of orientations: pitch within
\pm~30^{\circ}
, yaw within
\pm~45^{\circ}
, and roll within
\pm~45^{\circ}
, at approximately
20^{\circ}
/s.

We conducted an IRB-approved study (STUDY2026_00000012) with five participants, all studies
complied with relevant ethical regulations. The participants kept their head directed at the center of the board and fixated on it while the robotic arm performed an orientation sweep at distances of 2, 3, 4, and 5 m. Each sweep lasted 1 minute, yielding 900 eye images per distance.

Effect of patch size.
We assess the detection accuracy across three patch sizes (12, 16, 20 mm), where a marker is considered correctly detected if all individual patches are visible in the corneal reflections to a human annotator. Frames affected by blinks were excluded from analysis.

Fig.
4
shows that the 16 and 20 mm patch had high detection accuracies at 2 to 4 m from 99.1 to 100%, decreasing to 89.4 to 90.8% at 5 m. The detection accuracy of the 12 mm patch degraded sooner, with accuracy falling to 92.2% at 4 m.

These results show that all three patch sizes are suitable for interaction within 4 m. Since patch size has little effect on the overall tag footprint, we select 16 mm for subsequent evaluations.

Effect of patch spacing.
While tighter patch spacing allows tags to fit on smaller objects, their appearance in the corneal reflections can merge and become difficult to distinguish, reducing decoding reliability. Conversely, wider spacing improves patch separability but limits the range of objects the tag can be attached to. We evaluate three patch spacings (5, 10, and 15 cm) using 16 mm patches.

Because the camera sits below the eye, the corneal reflections compress the vertical axis more than the horizontal, so the effective patch spacing varies with orientation. To capture both axes in a single experiment, we arrange three patches in an equilateral triangle, which samples spacing at multiple angles simultaneously. We then score each frame using a Difference-of-Gaussians (DoG) blob detector, and consider a tag detectable if the three blobs are detected and are all within pairwise distances of 60 px.

Our results in Fig.
4
show that when using a patch spacing of 10 and 15 cm, detection accuracy remained high at 96.3 to 99.6% across 2—4 m. For the smallest patch spacing 5 cm, detection accuracy degraded at 4 m.

Based on these findings, we design two marker variants: a short- to mid-range marker using 12 mm patches with 5 cm spacing (2–3 m), and a longer-range marker using 16 mm patches with 10 cm spacing (up to 4 m).

Both variants use an
N\times N
grid layout (Fig.
7
), which serves a dual purpose of both orientation and distance estimation as well as ID encoding of any object it is attached to. We evaluate
3\times 3
and
4\times 4
configurations in our experiments.

Pose estimation.
We place retroreflective patches at all four corners to serve as fiducial anchors for PnP-based pose estimation. While three non-collinear points is the theoretical minimum needed to solve PnP, using four corners provides a more robust overdetermined system. To resolve rotational ambiguity, the top-right corner patch is larger than the others, establishing a canonical orientation. We use a 20 mm orientation patch for the short- to mid-range marker and a 30 mm orientation patch for the longer-range markers.

ID encoding.
With the corners reserved for pose estimation, the five remaining interior grid positions are available for identity encoding. The presence or absence of a retroreflective patch at each position acts as a binary bit, yielding 32 possible binary configurations. The same design principle can be extended to larger grids to support a greater number of uniquely identifiable objects.

More broadly,
GlintMarkers
markers are not limited to a grid-based layout. In principle, any constellation of more than four retroreflective patches can serve as a valid marker, provided that different constellations remain sufficiently distinguishable across the range of poses encountered in practice. In this paper, we adopt a grid-based layout for simplicity.

3.3.
Spatial reconstruction framework

We describe our spatial reconstruction framework in two phases (Fig.
8
).
First
, marker detection localizes glints in the eye image, clusters them into candidate markers, and identifies which marker the user is attending to along with its ID.
Second
, PnP estimation recovers the marker’s 3D pose and distance from the camera.

3.3.1.
Marker detection

Pupil localization.
We use DeepVOG
(
Yiu et al. 2019
)
, a lightweight convolutional neural network that outputs a probability map indicating which pixels belong to the pupil. We fit an ellipse to the largest connected component of this map. To handle blinks, we discard any frame where the maximum probability falls below 0.96, along with the two frames that follow (133 ms total). The pupil centroid serves two roles: it reduces the search space for subsequent glint detection, and we track it over time to infer gaze direction, which tells us which marker the user is attending to.

Glint localization.
We first run a Difference-of-Gaussians (DoG) blob detector
(
OpenCV 2026
)
to coarsely identify up to 40 glint candidates per frame.

Marker identification.
As multiple markers can appear in a frame, we perform spatial clustering on the detected patches using the DBSCAN algorithm
(
Schubert et al. 2017
)
with a distance threshold of 50 pixels to group the patches into clusters, each corresponding to one tag. We denote the marker being gazed at by the user as the one whose cluster centroid is closest to the pupil centroid.

3.3.2.
Pose Estimation and ID Decoding

Figure 9
.
Corneal reflections for markers at different orientations. Rotational shifts in the marker are reflected in the glint patterns and used by our PnP framework to estimate 3-DoF orientation. For evaluation, the test board has a 4

4 marker layout to assess performance across different tag sizes. ArUco markers on the same board provide ground-truth pose via a front-facing camera.

Given the selected glint cluster, we must determine which marker in a known library the user is looking at and recover its orientation and distance. A natural approach for grid-based markers would be to decode a binary pattern directly from the dot positions. However, this requires first establishing a precise grid alignment, which is fragile when one or two spurious glints are present in the cluster, and it ties the decoding method to a specific grid geometry. Instead, we adopt a
geometry-based library matching
approach: we attempt to fit a Perspective-n-Point (PnP) model to each candidate marker pattern in the library and accept the one with the lowest geometric error. This strategy is agnostic to the specific grid layout, naturally tolerates extra detections through cascading subset matching, and simultaneously yields the marker’s pose as a byproduct of the identification step.

Glint center refinement.
The DoG blob detector returns integer-pixel center estimates, but PnP accuracy is sensitive to image-point localization error. A naïve intensity-weighted centroid over a fixed window is unreliable when glints are closely spaced, because the tails of neighboring intensity profiles bleed into the window and bias the estimate. To isolate each glint, we compute a Voronoi partition seeded at all detected centers, so that each glint’s refinement window only includes pixels closer to it than to any neighbor. Within this masked region, we compute the sub-pixel center as an intensity-weighted centroid over a local patch (default half-window of 5 px).

Canonical ordering.
Before matching, we establish a canonical ordering of the detected glints so that they can be compared against library patterns. We identify the four corner glints via convex hull extraction. One corner, the
anchor patch
, is physically larger and produces a higher mean intensity (measured within a 4 px radius). We place the anchor first, the remaining corners in clockwise order, and interior dots sorted by their clockwise angular position relative to the cluster centroid. Each library pattern defines its object coordinates in the same canonical order.

PnP-Based Matching.
Because all retroreflective patches lie on a single plane, we use the Infinitesimal Plane-based Pose Estimation (IPPE) method
(
Collins and Bartoli 2014
)
. For each candidate library pattern with
k
dots, IPPE takes the
k
canonically ordered image points and the corresponding object-frame coordinates, and outputs a rotation matrix, a translation vector
\mathbf{t}
, and a reprojection RMSE. A match is accepted only if the RMSE falls below a threshold
\tau
(default 2.5 px). When multiple candidates share the same dot count, we select the one with the lowest RMSE. The pose recovered from the accepted match directly identifies the marker and yields its orientation and distance.

Reducing detection noise.
The number of glints
N
in a cluster may exceed the true marker’s patch count due to spurious reflections. We first attempt to match library markers with exactly
N
patches using all
N
detected glints. If no match falls below
\tau
, we enumerate all subsets of size
N{-}1
, re-apply the canonical ordering and matching for each subset, and accept the best match below
\tau
. If this also fails, we continue to
N{-}2
,
N{-}3
, and so on, down to a minimum dot count. In practice, the cluster selection step already isolates the target marker’s glints, so the number of spurious detections is typically small (0–2 extra dots), keeping the combinatorial cost manageable.

Tracking markers across time.
To avoid running the full detection and matching pipeline every frame, we enter a
tracking mode
after a successful identification with RMSE below
\tau
. The glint positions are propagated to subsequent frames using Lucas-Kanade (KLT) optical flow
(
Sharmin and Brad 2012
)
. We validate tracked points with a forward-backward consistency check: points are tracked forward then backward, and any point with a round-trip error exceeding 15 px is rejected. If all points pass, PnP is solved directly on the tracked positions without re-running detection or library matching. Tracking continues until any point is lost or the reprojection error exceeds the threshold, at which point the system falls back to full detection.

3.3.3.
From PnP to orientation and distance

A standard PnP solver recovers a full 6-DoF pose: 3-DoF rotation and a 3-DoF translation vector
(t_{x},t_{y},t_{z})
. In a conventional setting, the translation encodes the marker’s 3D position relative to the camera. In our setting, however, the eye camera observes a
virtual image
of the marker formed by reflection on the convex corneal surface. The position of this virtual image depends not only on the marker’s true location but also on the eye’s position within the camera frame, which depends on each user. As a result, the individual translation components do not correspond to a stable 3D position in the scene. The norm
\hat{d}=\|\mathbf{t}\|
, however, is a consistent proxy for the marker-to-user distance, related to the true distance by a per-user affine relationship that we recover during calibration. The rotation matrix, by contrast, is preserved by the mirror geometry and directly yields the marker’s 3-DoF orientation (roll, pitch, yaw).

One-time user calibration.
To account for per-user variations in eye geometry and eyewear fit, we perform a one-time calibration procedure to compute three per-user calibration parameters. The user fixates on a known marker at two or more known distances (e.g., 2 m and 3 m) with a frontal orientation for a few seconds each. Once computed, all three parameters persist across sessions for the same user and eyewear.

Gaze Offset.
The pupil center in the corneal reflection does not perfectly coincide with the true gaze direction due to individual differences in corneal geometry and glasses alignment. To correct for this, we project the marker center onto the undistorted eye image using the estimated PnP pose and compute the 2D offset
(\Delta x,\Delta y)
between this projected center and the detected pupil center. At runtime, we add this offset to the pupil center to obtain a corrected gaze estimate.

Distance.
The raw PnP distance
\hat{d}
deviates from the true distance
d
by a per-user affine relationship
\hat{d}\approx k\cdot d+b
. During calibration, the system accumulates
(\hat{d}_{i},d_{i})
pairs from successive frames at known fixation distances and fits
k
and
b
via ordinary least-squares regression. At runtime, the calibrated distance is
d=(\hat{d}-b)/k
.

Pitch.
The corneal surface introduces a nonlinear distortion in the reflected marker pattern that varies with gaze angle, particularly in the pitch (vertical) axis. This distortion causes a systematic bias in the estimated pitch angle that differs between upward and downward gaze. During calibration, we compute per-user correction constants for a dual-path pitch model determined from the known ground-truth pose at calibration. At runtime, we apply separate pitch offsets for positive and negative estimated pitch angles.

4.1.
Orientation and distance estimation

Here we evaluate our system’s ability to perform 3-DoF orientation and distance estimation of the retroreflective markers under varying distances, marker sizes, and gaze angles.

4.1.1.
Study design.

We create a board for evaluation that includes a 16-dot
GlintMarkers
layout (
4\times 4
grid, 10 cm patch spacing) placed at fixed distances and orientations from the participant. The board also has a
2\times 2
array of ArUco fiducial markers visible to the front-facing camera, providing ground-truth pose via standard ArUco PnP. Each participant completed six experiment sessions corresponding to three distances (2, 3, and 4 m) when facing the board
head-on
and
head-off
at a 20° angle. Each session lasted 60 seconds, yielding approximately 900 frames. We recruited 10 participants.

To study the effect of tag size on accuracy, we evaluate three subsets of the 16-dot grid, simulating markers of different physical footprints: a 4-dot subset spanning 36 cm, a 4-dot subset spanning 24 cm, and a 4-dot subset spanning 12 cm. Each subset uses only the four corner dots of the corresponding sub-grid. Because PnP accuracy generally improves with more input points, four corners represent a worst-case configuration for each tag span; markers with additional interior dots would yield equal or better accuracy. For each subset, we solve the planar PnP on just the selected dots and compare against the ArUco ground truth. We report orientation mean absolute error (MAE) in degrees and distance MAE in meters, averaged per user and then across users, with error bars showing the sample standard deviation across participants.

4.1.2.
Orientation accuracy

Fig.
10
shows the orientation MAE for the 24 cm tag span across distances in head-on and head-off conditions.

Head-on condition.

With the 24 cm tag, roll is consistently the most accurate axis, with MAE around 5° at all distances. Yaw degrades moderately with distance, rising from 8.5° at 2 m to 10.7° at 4 m. Pitch MAE is the highest axis, around 14–15° across all distances, reflecting the systematic bias from the off-axis camera placement.
The 36 cm tag yields slightly lower error overall (e.g. pitch 13.4° at 2 m; yaw 7.9°), while the 12 cm tag shows noticeable degradation at longer range, with pitch reaching 18.3° and yaw 14.0° at 4 m. Roll remains robust across all tag sizes, staying within 5°.

Head-off condition.

When the participant’s head is turned 20° away, all axes show elevated error.
For the 24 cm tag, roll increases to approximately 8–9°, pitch rises to 17–18°, and yaw reaches 14.0° at 2 m and 15.6° at 4 m.
Cross-user variability is also wider in the head-off condition, reflecting increased sensitivity to individual eye geometry and eyewear fit when gaze and head direction diverge.
The pattern across tag sizes is similar: the 36 cm tag achieves the lowest error (e.g. pitch 15.7° at 2 m), while the 12 cm tag degrades most at 4 m, with pitch reaching 20.5° and yaw 18.2°.

4.1.3.
Distance accuracy

The PnP translation vector yields a raw distance estimate that, after per-user affine calibration, provides a calibrated distance. For each user, we fit
\hat{d}=k\cdot d+b
using the first 10% of frames from each session (approximately 6 seconds) as calibration data and evaluate on the remaining 90%.
Fig.
11
shows the calibrated distance estimate versus the ArUco ground-truth distance for the 24 cm tag subset, pooling all participants across both head-on and head-off conditions. After per-user affine calibration, the estimates closely follow the identity line across the 2–4 m range, achieving an overall MAE of 0.144 m on the held-out frames. The scatter around the diagonal is relatively uniform across distances, indicating that the affine model captures the dominant source of per-user bias.

4.1.4.
Discussion

These results show that
GlintMarkers
can estimate marker orientation and distance with practically useful accuracy. Roll, which depends primarily on the in-plane rotation of the dot pattern, is robust across all conditions (
\sim
5°). Pitch, the axis most affected by the corneal convex-mirror distortion, benefits substantially from calibration. Distance estimation achieves sub-20 cm MAE over a 2–4 m range after a one-time per-user calibration. The tag size versus distance trade-off provides guidance for marker design: a 36 cm tag maintains single-digit yaw error out to 4 m, while a 12 cm tag is suitable at shorter distances (2–3 m) but degrades significantly at 4 m. The head-off condition confirms that the system remains functional when gaze and head direction are misaligned by 20°.

4.2.
Object identification

Figure 12
.
Corneal reflection with six markers simultaneously in view. GlintMarkers identifies the marker the user is gazing at and decodes its ID.

A core capability of
GlintMarkers
is identifying which tagged object a user is gazing at. This study evaluates the end-to-end object identification pipeline from glint detection through gaze-based cluster selection to PnP-based library matching under realistic multi-tag conditions where several markers are simultaneously visible.

4.2.1.
Study design.

Six distinct
GlintMarkers
were placed in front of the participant at 2 m distance with 40 cm gap from each other in a
2\times 3
grid. Each marker encodes a unique identity using a different retroreflective patch configuration on a shared
3\times 3
grid: three markers use 5 patches and three use 6 patches, assigned digits 1 through 6. Although only six markers were physically present, the system’s marker library contains all 32 geometrically distinct patterns achievable on the
3\times 3
grid. During identification, the cascading PnP matcher evaluates the detected glint cluster against all 32 library entries, making the classification task substantially harder than a closed six-class problem.

Each experimental session consisted of 3 complete sweeps through all 6 markers, yielding 18 trials per participant. In each trial, the system announced a target digit via text-to-speech, and the participant fixated on the corresponding marker for 5 seconds while the eye camera captured frames. The trial sequence within each sweep was randomized. Only frames captured 3 seconds after digit onset (allowing time for the participant to shift gaze) were included in the analysis. A frame was classified as correct if the predicted marker ID matched the spoken ground-truth digit.

These results demonstrate that
GlintMarkers
can reliably identify which of six simultaneously visible markers a user is gazing at, achieving over 96% accuracy with sub-second temporal integration, even when matched against a full 32-entry library. The high per-frame accuracy (95–99%) indicates that even individual frames carry sufficient information for object identification in most cases, while majority voting over a short window provides additional robustness for applications that can tolerate a fraction-of-a-second latency. The low confusion between markers confirms that the PnP-based matching criterion is highly discriminative, and that the gaze-based cluster selection successfully disambiguates the intended target from neighboring markers.

Discussion

Curved surfaces.
We tested whether
GlintMarkers
remain visible when attached to curved objects. Specifically, we placed markers on a curved chair back and on the side of an electric kettle. The user looked directly at the chair marker from 3 m and at the kettle marker from 2 m. As shown in Fig.
14
, the marker pattern remained visible in the corneal reflection in both cases, suggesting that moderate surface curvature is compatible with our approach. However, stronger curvature can place individual patches at extreme angles relative to the incident NIR illumination and viewing direction, thereby reducing the amount of light retroreflected back to the eye camera.

Dual-eye sensing.
Our current system uses a single eye camera, but extending it to binocular sensing could further improve robustness and accuracy. First, eyelashes often occlude part of the pupil region in one eye, which can hide a subset of glints or make them blurry; the other eye is unlikely to exhibit the same occlusion pattern, so combining both views can recover missing detections. Second, localized tear-film irregularities can blur portions of one corneal image as the eye dries between blinks, reducing glint localization accuracy. Third, spurious reflections from eyeglass lenses or nearby surfaces could be filtered through cross-validation: true retroreflective glints should appear in both eyes with geometrically consistent structure, whereas incidental artifacts are unlikely to do so. Fourth, the two eyes provide independent geometric measurements with slightly different viewing angles, which may help average out nonlinear distortions and reduce pose-estimation error. Fifth, binocular sensing also yields two independent distance estimates that can be fused to improve precision.

Laser dot projector.
We investigated whether the structured-light dot projector found in commodity depth sensors (e.g., iPhone LiDAR) could replace our dedicated NIR LED as the illumination source. In principle, the dense field of NIR dots emitted by such projectors could excite retroreflective patches without requiring a co-located LED on the eyewear. We tested a
3\times 3
marker grid with 5 cm inter-patch spacing using 16 mm patches, with the iPhone positioned approximately 20 cm from the marker. However, because the projector emits a sparse, pseudo-random dot pattern rather than a uniform flood, many patches received no dot at any given instant. Even when a dot did land on a patch, the intensity difference between a dot striking a retroreflective patch and one striking the surrounding non-reflective surface was insufficient to reliably distinguish marker dots from background dots in the corneal reflection (Fig.
15
). These two limitations, sparse spatial coverage and low on-patch versus off-patch contrast, suggest that structured-light projectors are not a practical substitute for flood illumination in our current design. A co-located NIR flood source remains preferable because it guarantees that every retroreflective patch is illuminated in every frame, producing the high-contrast glints that our detection pipeline relies on.

Infrared filament.
Our current markers use retroreflective patches attached to the object’s exterior surface. However, NIR-transmissive materials such as the 3D-printing filament used in InfraredTags
(
Dogan et al. 2022
)
appear opaque in visible light but pass NIR radiation. Retroreflective patches placed beneath such a shell would remain invisible to the naked eye yet still produce glints under NIR illumination, allowing manufacturers to embed
GlintMarkers
directly into product housings during fabrication rather than attaching them externally.
