Association between body composition and bone mineral density in community-dwelling older adults from Chile: a sex-stratified DXA-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Nicole Fritz-Silva, Cristian Mansilla-Antilef, María Angélica Castillo-Cerda, Carla Bittner-Hofmann, Carol Ule-Marín
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDecreased bone mineral density (BMD) remains one of the main determinants of frailty and fracture risk in older adults. Lean mass is an important contributor to bone health through mechanical loading and muscle–bone interactions. Nevertheless, evidence from Latin America, particularly from DXA-based studies incorporating sex-stratified analyses, remains limited. This study aimed to examine the association between body composition—emphasizing lean mass—and BMD in community-dwelling older adults from Chile.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in 274 community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 65 years from Puerto Montt, Chile (80% women). Anthropometric data were collected, and body composition (total lean mass, total fat mass, and regional fat distribution) was assessed via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). BMD was analyzed at the whole-body level and, in subsamples with complete data, at the femoral neck and lumbar spine (L3–L4). Spearman correlation analyses and multiple linear regression models adjusted for age, body mass index (BMI), sex, and regional fat distribution were performed.ResultsIn participants with complete whole-body BMD data (n = 274), total lean mass was moderately positively associated with whole-body BMD (ρ = 0.66; p < 0.001), with a stronger correlation in men (ρ = 0.62) than in women (ρ = 0.47). In the multivariable regression models, lean mass showed the strongest independent association with whole-body BMD, explaining 53.4% of the variance in Model 1 (adjusted R² = 0.534). The inclusion of sex and regional fat distribution modestly improved model performance (adjusted R² ≈ 0.56). Analyses of lumbar spine (L3-L4) and femoral neck BMD were conducted in smaller subsamples with available regional DXA data (n = 133 for each site), in which associations with body composition variables were generally weaker or nonsignificant. Adiposity measurements revealed weaker and site-dependent associations with BMD.ConclusionsLean mass showed the strongest independent association with whole-body BMD in community-dwelling older Chilean adults, with modest differences between women and men. These findings support the relevance of lean mass as an important correlate of bone health during aging and provide evidence from a Latin American population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07904-x.

Introduction

The age-related decline in bone mineral density (BMD) represents an epidemiological challenge of increasing relevance, as it contributes to the development of osteopenia, osteoporosis, and fragility fractures conditions associated with a high burden of morbidity, functional dependence, and mortality among older adults [1–3]. This issue is particularly critical in women, who experience accelerated bone loss after menopause owing to the decline in estrogen levels, significantly increasing fracture risk later in life [4, 5]. In the current context, accelerated population aging has been observed worldwide and is particularly pronounced in Latin America. Consequently, the prevalence of osteopenia, osteoporosis, and fragility fractures is expected to increase substantially, with important implications for health systems and the development of public policies targeting older adult populations [6–8].

The identification of BMD determinants is fundamental for guiding preventive strategies and clinical management. Bone mineral density in older adults is influenced by multiple interacting determinants, including physical activity levels, body weight status, hormonal changes, and body composition [9]. These factors operate within a complex musculoskeletal framework in which neuromuscular function, inflammatory processes, comorbidities, medication use, and endocrine regulation may jointly influence skeletal health during aging. Among the factors influencing bone health, the interaction between the muscular system and bone tissue has received increasing attention in scientific literature. DXA-derived lean mass has been consistently associated with BMD in observational studies, potentially reflecting the influence of mechanical loading induced by muscle contraction, neuromuscular regulation, and biochemical signaling mediated by myokines that influence bone remodeling [10–12]. Evidence from observational studies and systematic reviews indicates that higher lean mass is consistently associated with greater BMD and reduced fracture risk in older adults, suggesting that muscle mass is an important component of healthy musculoskeletal ageing [13–15]. Notably, however, lean mass estimated by DXA represents a composite measure of nonfat soft tissue and does not directly measure skeletal muscle mass.

In contrast, the role of adipose tissue in bone health is more complex and heterogeneous. Although total fat mass may have positive or neutral associations with BMD, emerging evidence suggests that regional adipose tissue distributions—particularly android adiposity—may have detrimental effects on bone, which are mediated by low-grade chronic inflammation, metabolic alterations, and insulin resistance [16–18]. Additionally, sex differences in these associations have been described, reinforcing the need to address the determinants of bone health from a stratified perspective [16, 17].

Despite the relevance of these findings, epidemiological evidence from Latin America remains limited. In Chile, one of the countries in the region experiencing the most rapid population aging, research examining the relationship between body composition and bone mineral density has been largely restricted to prevalence estimates or specific clinical populations [9, 19, 20]. In addition, relatively few studies have simultaneously assessed BMD and body composition via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), which is widely used as a clinical reference method for assessing BMD and as a commonly used method for estimating body composition in clinical and research settings [21–23]. Excess body weight is also highly prevalent among Chilean older adults, with national data indicating that nearly one quarter live with obesity and almost one third with overweight, reflecting a high burden of adiposity in this population group. Moreover, sex-stratified analyses remain underexplored in the regional literature despite well-documented biological, functional, and epidemiological differences between women and men in the process of musculoskeletal aging [3–5, 16].

Considering these gaps, the present study aimed to examine the associations between body composition, particularly lean mass, and BMD in community-dwelling older adults in Chile via DXA-based measurements and sex-stratified analyses. Whole-body BMD was selected as the primary outcome because it allows direct comparison with whole-body body composition parameters obtained from the same DXA whole-body scan and provides an integrated estimate of overall skeletal mineralization. Regional BMD measurements, including those of the lumbar spine and femoral neck, which are commonly used for the clinical diagnosis of osteoporosis, were analyzed as secondary outcomes when available. Existing studies have consistently reported positive associations between lean mass and BMD across different populations and age groups, suggesting that muscle mass may play a relevant role in skeletal health [15, 24–29]. We therefore hypothesized that lean mass would show the strongest independent association with whole-body BMD compared with adiposity indicators and that the magnitude of this association would differ between women and men.

Methods

Objective and study design

This cross-sectional study included community-dwelling older adults aged 65 years and older from Puerto Montt, Chile. The aim of this study was to examine the associations between anthropometric and body composition variables, with a particular emphasis on lean mass, and whole-body and regional bone mineral density (BMD) in community-dwelling older adults, as lean mass has been proposed as a key component of the muscle–bone interaction described in previous studies. Data collection was carried out at the facilities of the Universidad de Los Lagos.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The research protocol was approved by the Scientific Ethics Committee of the University of La Frontera (N.° 031_2024) and by the Ethics Committee of the University of Los Lagos (ORD. N.° 045/2024). All participants took part voluntarily and provided written informed consent. The data were analyzed anonymously, ensuring confidentiality, and used exclusively for scientific research. The study design and reporting followed the recommendations of the STROBE initiative for observational studies.

Participants

A total of 274 community-dwelling adults aged 65 years or older who were functionally independent were included in the study and recruited through official institutional channels of the University of Los Lagos and the public health care system. Recruitment followed a nonprobabilistic convenience sampling approach based on voluntary participation through community outreach activities and health promotion initiatives associated with the university and local primary health care networks. Individuals who expressed interest in participating were screened for eligibility by the research team. Functional independence was determined on the basis of self-reported ability to perform activities of daily living without assistance and the absence of medical conditions limiting participation in the assessment protocol. No restrictions were imposed by sex. No a priori sample size calculation was performed, as the study was based on an available community volunteer sample.

The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are presented in Supplementary Table S1. In brief, the participants were community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 65 years who could complete the DXA assessment protocol. Chronic diseases and medication use were not used as exclusion criteria to reflect the characteristics of community-dwelling older adults participating in community health promotion activities. The potential influence of these factors on BMD is acknowledged as a limitation of this study.

Because recruitment was community-based and participation was voluntary, the sample should be interpreted as a community volunteer sample rather than a probabilistic representation of the general older adult population. To assess potential selection bias associated with missing regional DXA data, anthropometric and body composition characteristics were compared between participants with complete and missing regional DXA measurements (Supplementary Table S3).

Data collection

Demographic variables

Age and sex were recorded. Age was treated as a continuous variable in the regression analyses. For descriptive purposes, participants were categorized into three age groups (65–69, 70–79, and ≥ 80 years) to explore age-related differences in BMD and body composition parameters.

Anthropometric variables

Body weight and height were measured via a calibrated digital scale (Omron HBF-514 C, Omron Healthcare Co., Kyoto, Japan) and a wall-mounted stadiometer (SECA, model 206, USA), respectively. The participants were assessed wearing light clothing and no shoes. Height was recorded with participants standing upright, with their head positioned in the Frankfort horizontal plane [30, 31]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as body weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). BMI categories were interpreted according to the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) criteria for older adults: underweight (< 22.9 kg/m²), normal weight (23.0–27.9 kg/m²), overweight (28.0–31.9 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 32.0 kg/m²) [32]. Information on lifestyle-related determinants of bone health, such as dietary intake and physical activity, was not systematically collected as part of the community screening protocol implemented during data collection.

Body composition and bone mineral density were determined via DXA

Total and regional body composition, as well as BMD, were assessed via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) with a whole-body clinical densitometer (Healthcare General Electric Company, ENCORE software version 18, USA). All measurements were performed by trained personnel in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations and standards of the International Society for Clinical Densitometry (ISCD) [21]. Daily quality control procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s guidelines using a calibration phantom to ensure measurement stability.

DXA whole-body scans provide measures of total and regional lean mass, total and regional fat mass, percentage of body fat (including android and gynoid distribution), and total and regional BMD. Regional BMD measurements at clinically relevant skeletal sites (lumbar spine and femoral neck) were obtained via standard DXA protocols. Although the ISCD recommends the use of L1–L4 for lumbar spine BMD assessment, in the present study, lumbar spine BMD was assessed at L3–L4, as these vertebrae provided the most complete and reliable measurements across participants, thereby maximizing data completeness and minimizing potential artifacts at adjacent vertebral levels.

Although site-specific BMD measurements (e.g., femoral neck or lumbar spine) are commonly used for the clinical diagnosis of osteoporosis, whole-body BMD provides an integrated assessment of skeletal mineralization and allows direct comparison with whole-body body composition parameters obtained from DXA. For this reason, total body BMD was selected as the primary analytical outcome, while site-specific analyses were conducted as complementary assessments. The bone status categories (normal BMD, osteopenia, and osteoporosis) reported in the descriptive analysis were based on DXA-derived T scores according to World Health Organization criteria: normal BMD (T score ≥ − 1.0), osteopenia (T score between − 1.0 and − 2.5), and osteoporosis (T score ≤ − 2.5) [33].

To ensure internal comparability across the whole-body and regional BMD analyses, a complete-case approach was applied, including only participants with valid body compositions and BMD data for each anatomical site evaluated. Missing regional DXA measurements were related primarily to incomplete regional scans or technical limitations during image acquisition. A complete-case analysis approach was used, and no imputation procedures were performed. Comparisons between participants with complete and missing regional DXA data revealed no significant differences in anthropometric or body composition variables, suggesting a low risk of selection bias (Supplementary Table S3).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted via R and Python with standard statistical libraries, including the lm package for regression models.

Normally distributed variables are presented as the means ± standard deviations, whereas nonnormally distributed variables are reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Sex-based comparisons within age groups were performed via Welch’s t test or the Mann‒Whitney U test, depending on the data distribution. Differences between age groups within the same sex were analyzed via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test or the Kruskal‒Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test, as appropriate. When the Kruskal‒Wallis test was applied, post hoc pairwise comparisons were adjusted via the Bonferroni correction to control for type I error.

Associations between demographic (age) and anthropometric variables (BMI), DXA-derived body composition parameters (total, lean mass, total fat mass, and regional fat percentages), and total and regional BMD were assessed via Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ), both in the total sample and stratified by sex, to explore potential sex-related differences in the associations. Spearman’s correlation was selected in preference to Pearson’s coefficient because of the nonnormal distribution of several variables. Owing to missing data at some anatomical sites, analyses were performed independently for three regions: total body BMD, the femoral neck, and the lumbar spine (L3-L4). The correlation strength was interpreted according to Danceys and Reidy’s criteria [34]: ρ = 0.2–0.4 (weak), ρ = 0.4–0.6 (moderate), ρ = 0.6–0.8 (strong), and ρ > 0.8 (very strong). Statistical significance for the correlation analyses was defined as p ≤ 0.05 after Bonferroni correction to account for multiple comparisons.

Multiple linear regression models were constructed to examine the associations between body composition variables and total body BMD. To avoid potential collinearity among adiposity-related variables, total body fat percentage and regional fat distribution variables were not included simultaneously in the same regression models. To explore potential sex differences in the association between lean mass and BMD, an additional interaction term (sex × total lean mass) was tested in the regression models. Sensitivity analyses were also conducted to evaluate the robustness of the estimates, including models excluding BMI and models estimated without stepwise variable selection. Bonferroni correction was not applied to the regression analyses. Model 1 included age, BMI, total lean mass (entered as an absolute DXA-derived value in kilograms), and total regional fat percentage. Model 2 additionally incorporated sex and regional fat distribution (android and gynoid fat percentages). Finally, Model 3 was obtained via a backward stepwise selection approach, retaining variables with p < 0.05. This procedure was used as an exploratory model selection strategy to assess the stability of the predictors identified in the predefined regression models. Importantly, the initial model specification was informed by theoretical and biological considerations regarding the muscle–bone relationship rather than relying solely on automated statistical selection.

Multicollinearity was assessed via variance inflation factors (VIFs), with values < 5 considered acceptable. For categorical variables, generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF) values were adjusted as GVIF^(1/(2×Df)). All the predictors included in the regression models presented VIF values below this threshold, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity; detailed values for each model are reported in Supplementary Table S4. Model assumptions were evaluated by inspection of residual and Q–Q plots to assess linearity, homoscedasticity, and normality of residuals. No substantial deviations from these assumptions were observed. Regression coefficients are reported as unstandardized β values to facilitate clinical interpretation of the estimated change in BMD per unit increase in lean mass. The results are presented as unstandardized regression coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals, and adjusted R² values. Given the exploratory nature of the analyses and the available sample size, the regression models assumed linear relationships between variables. In addition, a post hoc power analysis was conducted for the primary multivariable regression model examining total body BMD. Power was estimated using Cohen’s f² derived from the observed R² value, the final analytical sample size, the number of predictors included in the model, and an alpha level of 0.05. The observed effect size indicated statistical power > 0.99 for the primary analysis. Site-specific analyses for lumbar spine and femoral neck BMD were interpreted cautiously because of the smaller analytical subsamples.

Results

Sample characteristics

The study included 274 community-dwelling older adults with a mean age of 72.5 ± 6.9 years. The mean body weight and height were 70.1 ± 13.8 kg and 1.55 ± 0.09 m, respectively (see Table 1).

Women accounted for 80% of the sample (n = 218), whereas men represented 20% (n = 56). This imbalance reflects the voluntary nature of community-based recruitment and should be considered when interpreting sex-stratified analyses, particularly for men, owing to the smaller subsample size. Compared with men, women presented significantly lower body weights and heights (67.7 ± 13.6 kg vs. 79.8 ± 10.2 kg; and 1.52 ± 0.06 m vs. 1.65 ± 0.08 m, respectively; both p < 0.001). When stratified by age groups (65–69, 70–79, and ≥ 80 years), this pattern was consistent across all groups, except for body weight in the ≥ 80 years group, in which no significant differences were observed. No significant differences in anthropometric or body composition variables were detected between participants with complete and missing regional DXA data, suggesting a low likelihood of selection bias related to missing regional DXA measurements (Supplementary Table S3).

Anthropometric and body composition differences by sex and age group

Overall, 9.5% of the participants were underweight, 37.6% had a normal weight, 25.5% were overweight, and 27.4% were living with obesity. Overweight was more prevalent among women (35.7%) than men (22.9%), whereas obesity was slightly more common in men (28.4%) than in women (23.2%). With respect to bone status, 71.9% of the participants had normal BMD, 21.5% had osteopenia, and 3.3% had osteoporosis. For 3.3% of the participants, complete T score data were unavailable.

Across age groups, no significant differences were observed among men in terms of anthropometric variables, body composition, or regional fat distribution. In contrast, among women, body weight and total fat mass were significantly lower in the ≥ 80 years group than in the younger age groups (see Table 1).

Bone mineral density differences by sex and age group

The total body BMD was significantly greater in men than in women in the 65–69 years group (1.20 ± 0.12 vs. 1.02 ± 0.10 g/cm²; p ≤ 0.001) and 70–79 years group (1.20 ± 0.12 vs. 0.99 [0.92–1.06] g/cm²; p ≤ 0.001). No significant sex differences were detected in the ≥ 80 years group. Lumbar spine BMD (L3-L4) did not significantly differ between men and women or across male age groups. Among women, significant differences were observed between the 65–69 and 70–79 age groups, whereas no significant differences were found between the ≥ 80 years group and the other age groups (see Table 2).

Correlation analyses between body composition variables and BMD

Spearman correlation analyses for total body bone mineral density (BMD) were performed in the full study sample (n = 274). Site-specific analyses of femoral neck and lumbar spine (L3–L4) BMD were conducted in smaller subsamples with available regional DXA measurements (n = 133 for each site). Consequently, these analyses had lower statistical power, and the observed associations with body composition variables were generally weaker or nonsignificant than those observed for total body BMD.

Spearman correlation analyses revealed significant associations between body composition variables and total body BMD. Total body BMD was moderately to strongly positively correlated with total lean mass (ρ = 0.66; p < 0.001). After stratification by sex, this association remained moderate-to-strong in men (ρ = 0.62; p < 0.001) and was moderate in women (ρ = 0.47; p < 0.001). The detailed correlation results for the complete sample are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Moderate correlations were observed between total body BMD and total fat mass (ρ = 0.23; p = 0.005). In sex-stratified analyses, this association was present only in women (ρ = 0.34; p < 0.001). The gynoid fat percentage showed a moderate negative correlation with total body BMD (ρ = −0.25; p < 0.001), whereas in women, the android fat percentage showed a weak positive correlation (ρ = 0.25; p = 0.005).

Age demonstrated a weak negative correlation with total body BMD exclusively in women, with no significant associations observed in the overall sample or among men. These results are detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

Multivariate regression models

Multiple linear regression models consistently identified total lean mass as the most relevant independent predictor of total body bone mineral density. In Model 1, total lean mass was the only significant predictor (β = 0.014; SE = 0.001; p < 0.001), explaining 53.4% of the variance in BMD (adjusted R² = 0.534). When expressed per kilogram of lean mass, each additional kilogram of lean mass was associated with a higher total body BMD of approximately 0.014 g/cm² per kilogram of lean mass.

After the inclusion of sex and regional fat distribution in Model 2, the predictive capacity increased modestly (adjusted R² = 0.558). Male sex (β = 0.0690; p = 0.002) and android fat percentage (β = 0.00239; p = 0.028) were independently associated with higher BMD, whereas the coefficient for total lean mass remained stable and statistically significant. The gynoid fat percentage was not significantly negatively associated.

Model 3, which was derived through backward stepwise selection, retained total lean mass, sex, and android and gynoid fat percentages, had a similar explanatory capacity to Model 2 (adjusted R² = 0.559). The detailed model results are presented in Table 3.

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that lean mass is most strongly associated with BMD in community-dwelling older Chilean adults. Given the cross-sectional design, these results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships, and this pattern was consistent across both correlation analyses and multivariable regression models. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that lean mass is among the variables most strongly associated with BMD in older populations [14, 35]. However, DXA-derived lean mass represents a broader compartment that includes nonmuscle tissues and should therefore not be interpreted as a direct measure of skeletal muscle mass [14, 24–29].

Existing evidence suggests that the muscle–bone relationship is supported by multiple physiological mechanisms, including mechanical loading induced by muscle contraction, neuromuscular regulation, and endocrine signaling mediated by myokines that influence bone remodeling [10–12]. In this context, the association observed between lean mass and total body bone mineral density in the present sample is consistent with the concept of the ‘muscle–bone unit’, which describes the functional and biomechanical interaction between skeletal muscle and bone tissue, as supported by previous observational and interventional studies in older adults [10–12]. However, it should be noted that the present study assessed lean mass rather than direct measures of skeletal muscle mass.

An important finding of this study was the sex-related difference in the strength of the association between lean mass and BMD. In line with previous research, this association was stronger in men than in women, which may be explained by higher absolute levels of muscle mass, differences in skeletal loading patterns, and a lower relative influence of adiposity on bone metabolism in men [16, 18, 35]. Similar sex-related differences have been reported in observational studies across adult and older populations, where lean mass tends to be more strongly associated with BMD in men than in women [25, 27, 29]. In women, the association observed in the present study was moderate in magnitude, which may be influenced by postmenopausal estrogen decline, age-related changes in body composition, and the redistribution of adipose tissue that occurs with aging [4, 16]. These findings highlight the importance of considering sex-related biological differences when examining osteomuscular interactions during aging. However, the interaction term between sex and lean mass was not statistically significant, indicating no evidence of effect modification by sex in the multivariable models. In the present study, potential sex differences were therefore explored via interaction terms rather than fully stratified models to preserve statistical power given the smaller male subsample.

In terms of fat mass, the associations observed with BMD were generally weaker and more heterogeneous than those identified for lean mass and varied according to sex and the anatomical site evaluated. In some analyses, these associations were modest in magnitude and sensitive to correction for multiple comparisons, suggesting that the contribution of adiposity to BMD in this population may be limited and context dependent. Although total fat mass and several indicators of regional adiposity have been reported to be positively associated with total body BMD, particularly in women [27, 36, 37], these associations appear to be less consistent than those observed for lean mass. These findings should be interpreted in the context of the high prevalence of overweight and obesity in the Chilean older adult population, as documented in national epidemiological studies, where excess body weight represents a predominant condition in this age group [9, 18].

Previous studies have reported inconsistent associations between lean mass, fat mass, and bone mineral density, which may partly reflect methodological limitations in the estimation of lean mass via DXA-derived measures [23]. Recent evidence highlights the complex and context-dependent relationships between adiposity, lean mass, and bone health. Similarly, longitudinal evidence from middle-to-older adult populations has shown that changes in lean mass are stronger predictors of femoral BMD trajectories than changes in fat mass are, supporting the central role of muscle-related mechanical loading in skeletal maintenance.

In this context, the positive association between android adiposity and BMD observed in the multivariable models should be interpreted with caution, as it may partly reflect the mechanical loading effect of greater body mass and potential residual confounding rather than a direct protective effect of adipose tissue on bone. Previous studies have reported that, in populations with high adiposity, greater body weight may be associated with higher BMD values, even in the presence of unfavorable metabolic profiles and low-grade inflammatory processes that may negatively affect bone health over time [17, 28, 29]. These findings reinforce the need to interpret the relationship between adiposity and BMD with caution and within its epidemiological context, particularly in countries such as Chile, where overweight and obesity constitute a predominant profile among older adults [9]. In contrast, the consistency and magnitude of the association between lean mass and BMD, independent of adiposity level, further support the role of lean mass as a key correlate of bone health during aging.

Multiple linear regression models indicated that lean mass had the strongest independent association with total body BMD, explaining more than 50% of the variance in the statistical models. However, because both lean mass and whole-body BMD are partially influenced by overall body size, this relatively strong association may partly reflect shared size dependence between these variables rather than a direct physiological relationship. Comparable studies have also reported substantial contributions of lean mass to BMD variability, particularly when whole-body measurements are used, reflecting the strong biomechanical relationship between muscle-related loading and skeletal adaptation [27–29]. In contrast, the associations between lean mass and site-specific BMD measures (femoral neck and lumbar spine) were weaker than those observed for total body BMD, a pattern also described in previous studies, reflecting the influence of whole-body mass and loading effects. Similarly, the associations observed for femoral neck BMD with total fat mass and android adiposity may reflect the effect of body mass–related mechanical loading at weight-bearing skeletal sites [10, 17]. The inclusion of sex modestly improved model fit, which is consistent with known differences in bone and muscle physiology between men and women during aging [4, 38, 39]. Although android adiposity emerged as a significant predictor in some models, its contribution was smaller than that of lean mass, supporting the interpretation that lean mass is the primary correlate of total body BMD in this sample.

Among the main strengths of this study are the use of dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), which is widely used as a clinical reference method for the assessment of bone mineral density and is a commonly applied method for estimating body composition [21–23]; the relatively large sample size; and the sex-stratified analytical approach, which has rarely been explored in Latin American research [21–23]. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged.

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Additionally, the sample presented a marked sex imbalance, with women representing approximately 80% of participants, which may have limited the precision of estimates in male-specific analyses despite the use of sex-stratified approaches. Participants were recruited through voluntary community-based outreach activities, which may have resulted in a sample of relatively healthier and more health-conscious older adults, introducing potential selection bias. Missing data at some anatomical sites reduced the sample size for specific analyses; however, this was addressed through predefined and transparent analytical criteria. The complete-case analytical approach may also introduce selection bias if participants with incomplete DXA measurements differ systematically from those with complete data. A formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed, as the study was based on an available community sample. However, a post hoc power analysis conducted for the primary multivariable regression model indicated adequate statistical power for the analysis of total body BMD. Nevertheless, the regional BMD analyses should be interpreted cautiously because the smaller subsamples available for lumbar spine and femoral neck measurements may have reduced statistical power and precision for these secondary outcomes.

Another limitation of this study is the lack of information on several potential confounders that may influence the relationship between body composition and BMD. Additionally, DXA-derived lean mass is an indirect estimate that may be influenced by hydration status, adiposity levels, and other physiological factors, and DXA provides a two-dimensional estimate of bone mineral density that does not capture bone microarchitecture. Variables such as physical activity level, muscle strength, smoking status, alcohol consumption, comorbidities, medications affecting bone metabolism (including osteoporosis treatments), vitamin D or calcium supplementation, and hormonal status were not available in the dataset and therefore could not be included in the analytical models. These factors should be considered when interpreting the observed associations. Despite these limitations, the findings of this study contribute to the understanding of the association between body composition and bone health in a community-based sample of older adults in Chile. These results support the relevance of lean mass as a key correlate of bone health during aging and highlight the need for future longitudinal and interventional studies to evaluate whether strategies aimed at preserving or improving muscle mass and function are associated with bone health outcomes in older adults while accounting for sex differences and the Latin American epidemiological context.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate that lean mass has the strongest independent association with BMD in community-dwelling older adults in Chile, surpassing the relevance of age, BMI and indicators of adiposity.

The strength of this association differed between women and men, reinforcing the importance of incorporating sex-sensitive analytical approaches in the study of osteomuscular aging. In an epidemiological context characterized by a high prevalence of overweight and obesity, the consistency of the effect of lean mass, independent of adiposity level, highlights the central role of the muscular component in bone health.

These results highlight the potential relevance of strategies aimed at preserving or increasing muscle mass as part of approaches to maintain bone health in older adults. However, because this study did not directly evaluate muscle mass, muscle strength, or participation in muscle-strengthening activities, the results should be interpreted as observational associations rather than evidence supporting specific interventions. In addition, this study provides locally generated evidence from a community sample of older adults in Chile that may inform future research in Latin American settings, where demographic changes across recent years demand evidence-based public health responses. Future longitudinal studies will be necessary to further explore the causal trajectories between body composition and bone health.
