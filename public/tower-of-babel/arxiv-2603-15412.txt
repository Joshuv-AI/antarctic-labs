Local Urysohn Width: A Topological Complexity Measure for Classification
Xin Li

Abstract
We introduce local Urysohn width , a complexity measure for classification problems on metric spaces. Unlike VC dimension, fat-shattering dimension, and Rademacher complexity, which characterize the richness of hypothesis classes , Urysohn width characterizes the topological-geometric complexity of the classification problem itself : the minimum number of connected, diameter-bounded local experts needed to correctly classify all points within a margin-safe region. We prove four main results. First, a strict hierarchy theorem : for every integer w ≥ 1 w\geq 1 , there exists a classification problem on a connected compact metric space (a bouquet of circles with first Betti number β 1 = w \beta_{1}=w ) whose Urysohn width is exactly w w , establishing that topological complexity of the input space forces classifier complexity. Second, a topology × \times geometry scaling law : width scales as Ω ⁡ ( w ⋅ L / D 0 ) \Omega(w\cdot L/D_{0}) , where w w counts independent loops and L / D 0 L/D_{0} is the ratio of loop circumference to locality scale. Third, a two-way separation from VC dimension : there exist problem families where width grows unboundedly while VC dimension is bounded by a constant, and conversely, families where VC dimension grows unboundedly while width remains 1. Fourth, a sample complexity lower bound : any learner that must correctly classify all points in the safe region of a width- w w problem needs Ω ⁡ ( w ​ log ⁡ w ) \Omega(w\log w) samples, independent of VC dimension.

Our motivation stems from a
locality
-first view of classification: rather than selecting a single global hypothesis, one assembles a
library of local continuous separators
, each defined on a connected patch of controlled diameter at a fixed resolution scale
D_{0}
. Urysohn’s lemma
[
6
]
provides the guiding intuition: on normal spaces, disjoint closed sets can always be separated by a continuous function, so within any single margin-separated region, the
existence
of a separator is guaranteed. The nontrivial question is how many such local separators are needed to cover the entire safe region
[
7
]
. Without an explicit locality constraint, this viewpoint degenerates because a single global separator reduces every margin-separated problem to width 1. Enforcing locality (e.g., connected supports of bounded diameter) turns the qualitative separation guarantee into a quantitative complexity measure: the
local Urysohn width

\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}
. Defined as the minimum number of connected, diameter-bounded patches required to cover and correctly classify the margin-safe region (Fig.
1
c), local Urysohn width is sensitive to instance geometry and topology
[
8
]
and complements hypothesis-class measures precisely in the regime where global expressivity is not the bottleneck.

Related work.

Urysohn’s lemma
[
6
]
guarantees the existence of continuous separating functions on normal spaces but is non-constructive. Urysohn width originated in the 1920s in dimension theory
[
9
]
and was revitalized by Gromov in the 1980s–90s within scalar curvature and systolic geometry
[
10
]
. Most recently, Kumar and Sen showed that the macroscopic version of Gromov’s Urysohn width conjecture for scalar curvature is false in dimensions four and above
[
11
]
, and Berdnikov et al. analyzed the behavior of Urysohn width under connected sum operations
[
12
]
. The present work studies the
complexity-theoretic
consequences of requiring local separators and local Urysohn width as a property of the learning problem on the space. Our width measure is closest in spirit to covering numbers and packing numbers from metric entropy theory
[
13
]
, but incorporates both the topological structure of the space
[
14
]
and the correctness requirement of the classifier, which pure metric entropy does not.

2
The Urysohn Machine

We introduce the computational model that motivates Urysohn width. The complexity measure we study (Definition
3.3
) is self-contained, but the machine model explains why each design choice is natural and connects our results to a broader theory of geometric computation.

Informal Overview

A
Urysohn Machine
(
\mathcal{UM}
) is a model of computation whose primitive data structure is a
Metric Library
: a finite collection of local continuous classifiers, each defined on a bounded region of the input space. The machine processes a stream of inputs via three operations:

1.

Evaluate
(
\mathcal{E}
): apply the current local classifier to the input. Cost:
O(1)
.

2.

Detect
(
\mathcal{D}
): test whether the input lies within the support of any existing classifier. If not, raise an alarm.

3.

Construct
(
\mathcal{T}
): when the alarm fires, build a
new
local classifier for the novel region and add it to the library. Past classifiers are frozen (never modified).

The Evaluate–Detect–Construct cycle is the machine’s analog of the Turing Machine’s read–transition–write cycle, but operates on continuous spaces rather than discrete symbol strings.

Formal Definition

Definition 2.1

(Urysohn Machine)
.

A
Urysohn Machine
is a 7-tuple

\mathcal{UM}=\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{A},\tau,\delta,q_{0}\rangle

(1)

where:

•

\mathcal{X}
is a compact metrizable space (the
input space
).

•

\mathcal{B}
is a countable discrete set (the
concept space
; elements are class labels).

•

\mathcal{M}=\{(S_{i},U_{i},f_{i})\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{M}|}
is the
metric library
, a finite collection of
local Urysohn triples
, where each triple consists of:

–

S_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
: a
connected
subset with
\mathrm{diam}(S_{i})\leq D_{0}
(the
support
),

–

U_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
: a finite set of labels (the
target partition
),

–

f_{i}\colon S_{i}\to\Delta^{|U_{i}|-1}
: a continuous map (the
separating function
).

•

\mathcal{A}\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is the
alarm function
, computing prediction residue.

•

\tau>0
is the
tolerance threshold
.

•

\delta
is the
transition operator
, mapping (configuration, input) pairs to new configurations via the
\mathcal{E}
–
\mathcal{D}
–
\mathcal{T}
cycle.

•

q_{0}=(\mathcal{M}_{0},\bot)
is the
initial configuration
, with
\mathcal{M}_{0}=\emptyset
.

The
connectivity
and
diameter bound
on each support
S_{i}
are the defining structural constraints. They enforce that each expert is genuinely
local
: it classifies inputs in a single coherent region of bounded spatial extent. Without these constraints, Urysohn’s lemma
[
6
]
guarantees that a
single
continuous function on any metrizable space separates any two disjoint closed sets, collapsing the library to one entry for every problem.

Width as Library Size

The central question of this paper is:

What is the minimum number of local Urysohn triples (entries in the metric library) needed to classify a given problem?

This minimum count is what we formalize as
Urysohn width
. It is simultaneously:

•

a property of the
classification problem
(not the hypothesis class),

•

determined by the
topology and geometry
of the input space,

•

and a
resource measure
for the Urysohn Machine (analogous to the number of states in a finite automaton or the tape length of a bounded Turing Machine
[
15
]
).

We now make this precise by introducing formal definitions of local Urysohn width and studying its basic properties.

3
Local Urysohn Width

Formal Definitions

Throughout,
(\mathcal{X},d)
denotes a compact metric space
[
16
]
.

Definition 3.1

(Margin partition and safe region)
.

A
K

\gamma
problem
is a tuple
\mathcal{P}=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{K})
of closed sets
A_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
satisfying

d(A_{i},A_{j})\geq\gamma\quad\forall\,i\neq j.

(2)

The

\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P},\gamma):=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{\gamma/2},\qquad A_{k}^{\gamma/2}:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:d(x,A_{k})\leq\gamma/2\}.

(3)

Note that by the margin condition, the sets
A_{i}^{\gamma/2}
and
A_{j}^{\gamma/2}
are disjoint for
i\neq j
(Appendix
A
).

Definition 3.2

(Local Urysohn triple and
(\gamma,D_{0})
-covering)
.

Fix a
locality scale

D_{0}>0
. A
local Urysohn triple
is a tuple
(S,U,f)
as in Definition
2.1
:
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is
connected
with
\mathrm{diam}(S)\leq D_{0}
,
U=[K]
, and
f\colon S\to\Delta^{K-1}
is continuous.
A collection
\{(S_{i},U,f_{i})\}_{i=1}^{w}
is a
(\gamma,D_{0})
-Urysohn covering
of

if:

1.

(Coverage)

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{w}S_{i}\supseteq\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)
.

2.

(Local correctness)
For all
i
,
k
, and
x\in S_{i}\cap A_{k}^{\gamma/2}
:
\argmax f_{i}(x)=k
.

Definition 3.3

(Local Urysohn width)
.

\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P},\gamma):=\min\{w:\exists\text{ a }(\gamma,D_{0})\text{-Urysohn covering of }\mathcal{P}\text{ of size }w\}.

(4)

This is the minimum size of the metric library of any Urysohn Machine (Definition

) that correctly classifies all points in
\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)
.

Remark 3.4

(Why locality is essential)
.

Without the connectedness and diameter constraints, a single triple with
S=\mathcal{X}
and
f
given by Urysohn’s lemma would achieve
\mathrm{uw}=1
for every margin-
\gamma
problem on a metrizable space. The locality axiom
[
17
,
18
]
, requiring each expert to be connected and diameter-bounded, prevents this collapse and makes the width a structural measure of geometric-topological complexity (useful for continual learning generalization bounds
[
19
]
).

Basic Properties

Lemma 3.5

(Monotonicity in margin)
.

If
0<\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma
, then

\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P},\gamma^{\prime})\leq\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)
.

Proof.

Since
A_{k}^{\gamma^{\prime}/2}\subseteq A_{k}^{\gamma/2}
for each
k
, we have

\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P},\gamma^{\prime})\subseteq\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)
.
Any
(\gamma,D_{0})
-covering of the larger safe region
\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)
also covers the smaller safe region
\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P},\gamma^{\prime})
. Therefore, the minimum covering size for
\gamma^{\prime}
cannot exceed that for
\gamma
.
∎

Lemma 3.6

(Monotonicity under refinement)
.

If
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
refines
\mathcal{P}
(each class of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is contained in a class of
\mathcal{P}
) and both have margin
\gamma
, then
\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\gamma)\geq\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)
.

Proof.

Any
(\gamma,D_{0})
-covering correct for the refined labels is also correct for the coarser labels by composing with the refinement map.
∎

Lemma 3.7

(Additivity on separated disjoint unions)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{X}_{2}
be a disjoint union with
d(\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2})>D_{0}
. Then for any margin-
\gamma
problem
\mathcal{P}
on
\mathcal{X}
,

\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)=\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}|_{\mathcal{X}_{1}},\gamma)+\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}|_{\mathcal{X}_{2}},\gamma).

(5)

Proof.

(\leq)
Take optimal coverings on each component and take their union; the triples remain valid since they lie in disjoint regions.

(\geq)
If
S
is connected and
\mathrm{diam}(S)\leq D_{0}
, it cannot intersect both
\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{X}_{2}
: any two points
x_{1}\in S\cap\mathcal{X}_{1}
and
x_{2}\in S\cap\mathcal{X}_{2}
satisfy
d(x_{1},x_{2})\geq d(\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2})>D_{0}\geq\mathrm{diam}(S)
, contradicting
x_{1},x_{2}\in S
. Therefore, every triple lies entirely within a single component, and each component requires at least its own width.
∎

Hierarchy on Connected Spaces via
\beta_{1}

Construction: Bouquet of Circles

For each loop

Fix

. Define a

\mathcal{P}_{w}=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{w})
with

A_{j}:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}_{w}:d(x,p_{j})\leq\gamma/4\},\qquad j=1,\ldots,w.

These are disjoint closed balls around the antipodes. Since any path from

. With radii

(7)

confirming that

is a valid margin-

\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P}_{w},\gamma)=\bigcup_{j=1}^{w}A_{j}^{\gamma/2}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{w}\{x:d(x,p_{j})\leq 3\gamma/4\}.

(8)

Main Result

Theorem 4.1

(Connected-space hierarchy; tight width
=\beta_{1}
)
.

Assume the locality scale satisfies

\frac{3\gamma}{2}\leq D_{0}<\frac{L}{2}-\frac{3\gamma}{4}.

(9)

Then we have

\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}_{w},\gamma)=w
and in particular,
\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}_{w},\gamma)\geq\beta_{1}(\mathcal{X}_{w})
.

Proof.

Lower bound (
\geq w
).
Let
\{(S_{i},U,f_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
be a
(\gamma,D_{0})
-Urysohn covering of
\mathcal{P}_{w}
.
By coverage, for each
j\in[w]
there exists an index
i(j)
such that

S_{i(j)}\cap A_{j}^{\gamma/2}\neq\emptyset
.
Choose

x_{j}\in S_{i(j)}\cap A_{j}^{\gamma/2}
.
Since
A_{j}^{\gamma/2}
is the
(\gamma/2)
-neighborhood of the ball
A_{j}=B(p_{j},\gamma/4)
, we have

d(x_{j},p_{j})\leq\frac{3\gamma}{4}
.
Because
d(p_{j},v)=L/2
, the triangle inequality gives

d(x_{j},v)\geq d(p_{j},v)-d(x_{j},p_{j})\geq\frac{L}{2}-\frac{3\gamma}{4}.

(10)

Now let
y
be any point on a different loop
k\neq j
. In the bouquet metric, every path from
x_{j}
to
y
passes through the wedge point
v
, so

d(x_{j},y)\geq d(x_{j},v)\geq\frac{L}{2}-\frac{3\gamma}{4}>D_{0}
,
where the last inequality is exactly Eq. (
9
). Therefore, no set of diameter at most
D_{0}
containing
x_{j}
can contain any point on any other loop. In particular,
S_{i(j)}
cannot intersect
A_{k}^{\gamma/2}
for any
k\neq j
.
It follows that the indices
i(1),\dots,i(w)
are pairwise distinct: a single triple cannot serve two different safe regions. Therefore

m\geq w
.

Upper bound (
\leq w
).
For each
j
, let

S_{j}:=A_{j}^{\gamma/2}
.
Since
A_{j}=B(p_{j},\gamma/4)
and

\frac{3\gamma}{4}<\frac{L}{2}
,
the set
S_{j}
is precisely the geodesic ball of radius
3\gamma/4
about
p_{j}
inside loop

, forming a connected arc. Moreover,

\mathrm{diam}(S_{j})\leq 2\cdot\frac{3\gamma}{4}=\frac{3\gamma}{2}\leq D_{0}
.
Define

to be the constant map outputting label

. This map is continuous, and
S_{j}
intersects only the

-th safe region, so the local correctness condition is satisfied. We conclude that

\{(S_{j},U,f_{j})\}_{j=1}^{w}

is a valid
(\gamma,D_{0})
-Urysohn covering of size
w
.
Combining the lower and upper bounds gives

\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}_{w},\gamma)=w
.
∎

Remark 4.2

(Parameter compatibility and the role of scale)
.

The constraint Eq. (
9
) requires
3\gamma/2\leq D_{0}<L/2-3\gamma/4
, which is satisfiable if and only if
L>9\gamma/2
. Note that
\gamma
(the classification margin) and
L
(the loop circumference) are properties of the problem and the space respectively, they are given, not chosen. The locality scale
D_{0}
is a property of the computational model, and the constraint asks that it be simultaneously large enough to cover each safe ball (
D_{0}\geq 3\gamma/2
) and small enough that no single patch can reach across loops (
D_{0}<L/2-3\gamma/4
).
The condition
L>9\gamma/2
is therefore a statement about the
ratio of topological feature size to classification resolution
: the loops must be macroscopic relative to the margin. Note that this is not an artificial requirement - if
L\leq 9\gamma/2
, a single margin-scale patch can cover an entire loop, rendering the topology invisible at the classification resolution. In that regime, width
should
be 1, the topology does not matter because the features are below the resolution scale. The theorem says precisely that topology forces classifier complexity when, and only when, the topological features are large relative to the margin.

Remark 4.3

(Why this is a hierarchy theorem)
.

Theorem
4.1
also establishes a strict arbitrarily large hierarchy of width levels: for every integer
w\geq 1
, the problem
\mathcal{P}_{w}
has
\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}=w
, and the lower bound
\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}\geq w
implies that no
(\gamma,D_{0})
-covering of size
w-1
suffices. Therefore, width
1\subsetneq
width
2\subsetneq
width
3\subsetneq\cdots
forms a strictly increasing chain of problem classes, analogous to how the classical time hierarchy theorem produces a strict chain
\mathrm{DTIME}(t_{1})\subsetneq\mathrm{DTIME}(t_{2})\subsetneq\cdots
for increasing time bounds. The additional content beyond an existence statement is that the hierarchy is realized on
connected
spaces with the width controlled by a topological invariant (
\beta_{1}
) rather than artificial disconnection.

5
Topology
\times
Geometry Scaling Law

The hierarchy theorem gives
\mathrm{uw}=w
: one expert per loop. But this only accounts for the
topological
contribution to width. A practitioner with small-receptive-field classifiers operating on large loops would ask:
do I need more than one expert per loop
? The answer is yes, and the additional cost is
geometric
- it depends on the ratio of loop size
L
to locality scale
D_{0}
. This section shows that width factors into two independent contributions that multiply together: the number of topologically independent contexts (
\beta_{1}
) and the number of patches needed to cover each context at the given resolution (
L/D_{0}
). Neither factor alone determines the width; both are necessary.

Construction: Multiple Safe Regions per Loop

We now show that width has two independent sources:
topological complexity
(number of loops) and
geometric resolution
(patches per loop).

w,m\geq 1
. On each loop

equally spaced along the loop at arc-length spacing

where

(Scaling law with explicit constant)
.

Let

Then we have

.
Consequently, for sufficiently large

\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}_{w,m},\gamma)\geq\Omega\!\left(w\cdot\frac{L}{D_{0}}\right)

Proof.

Let
\{(S_{i},U,f_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}

-Urysohn covering.
For each safe region

.

Same loop.

Consecutive centers on the same loop are separated by arc length

.
Since each safe region has radius

\frac{L}{2m}-\frac{3\gamma}{2}
.
By Eq. (

.
It follows that a set of diameter at most
D_{0}
cannot intersect two distinct safe regions on the same loop.

Different loops.

Every center
p_{j,r}
lies on the semicircle opposite the wedge point, hence

d(p_{j,r},v)\geq\frac{L}{4}
.
Therefore every point
x_{j,r}\in A_{j,r}^{\gamma/2}
satisfies

d(x_{j,r},v)\geq\frac{L}{4}-\frac{3\gamma}{4}
.
Any path from
x_{j,r}
to a point on a different loop must pass through
v
, so every such point is at distance at least

\frac{L}{4}-\frac{3\gamma}{4}>D_{0}

from
x_{j,r}
by Eq. (
12
). Therefore, a set of diameter at most
D_{0}
cannot intersect safe regions on two different loops.
Combining the two cases, each of the
wm
safe regions requires a distinct patch. we conclude that

N\geq wm
,
leading to

\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}_{w,m},\gamma)\geq wm
.
∎

5.2
Connection to Metric-Topology Factorization

The factorization of a learning problem into metric and topological components has a rigorous mathematical basis
[
21
]
: any metrizable space can be embedded into a product of a closed subset and a zero-dimensional space, preserving completeness and large-scale structure. The metric-topology factorization (MTF) operationalizes this mathematical structure as an architectural principle for learning systems
[
22
]
, requiring that the metric (funnel) and topological (trap) subsystems be trained by independent objectives.
The scaling law
\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}\geq\Omega(\beta_{1}\cdot L/D_{0})
admits a natural interpretation through the lens of MTF: the principle that the complexity of a classification problem decomposes into a
topological
component and a
metric
component, and that these two components are governed by independent mechanisms.

The factorization.

Write the lower bound as

\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)\;\geq\;\underbrace{\beta_{1}(\mathcal{X})}_{\text{topological factor}}\;\times\;\underbrace{\left\lfloor c\cdot\frac{L}{D_{0}}\right\rfloor}_{\text{metric factor}}.

(13)

The topological factor counts the number of
independent classification contexts
, qualitatively distinct regions of the input space that cannot be reached from one another within the locality scale. The metric factor counts the number of
local experts per context

[
7
]
, how finely each region must be partitioned at the resolution
D_{0}
.
These two factors are independent: the topological factor depends on the global connectivity structure of
\mathcal{X}
(its fundamental group), while the metric factor depends on the local geometry (the ratio of feature size to resolution). Modifying the topology (adding or removing loops) changes the first factor without affecting the second; modifying the geometry (stretching or compressing loops) changes the second without affecting the first.

Implications for classifier design.

The factorization in Eq. (
13
) suggests a natural two-stage architecture for classification on topologically complex spaces
[
22
]
:

1.

Topological indexing (I-system).
Determine which topological context the input belongs to, i.e., which loop of the bouquet. This is a discrete routing decision that depends on the global structure of
\mathcal{X}
and is independent of the metric geometry within each loop. The cost scales with
\beta_{1}
.

2.

Metric classification (M-system).
Within the identified context, apply a local continuous separator to classify the input. This depends on the local geometry and the resolution
D_{0}
, with cost scaling as
L/D_{0}
per context.

The above metric-topology decomposition mirrors the structure of the Urysohn Machine (Definition
2.1
): the Detect operator
\mathcal{D}
performs topological indexing (routing the input to the correct triple in the metric library), while the Evaluate operator
\mathcal{E}
performs metric classification (applying the local separator). The scaling law Eq. (
13
) shows that neither component can be eliminated, both the routing cost (
\beta_{1}
contexts) and the per-context classification cost (
L/D_{0}
patches) are necessary.

Width as a product complexity measure.

Most complexity measures in learning theory are
scalar

[
5
]
: VC dimension, fat-shattering dimension, and Rademacher complexity each assign a single number to a hypothesis class. Eq. (
13
) reveals that Urysohn width has a richer,
multiplicative
structure. Two problems can have the same width for entirely different reasons: a space with many simple loops (
\beta_{1}
large,
L/D_{0}
small) and a space with few complex loops (
\beta_{1}
small,
L/D_{0}
large) can have identical width, yet they pose qualitatively different challenges for learning. The topological factor determines how many independent tasks the learner must discover; the metric factor determines how precisely each task must be solved. Distinguishing these two contributions is not possible with any single scalar measure but requires
structural decoupling
, which we will discuss later in Sec.
9
.

6
Separation from VC Dimension

The separation we prove in this section has a conceptual root in the Urysohn-Brouwer lemma (a.k.a. Tietze extension theorem)
[
8
]
: if
A
is a closed subset of a normal space
\mathcal{X}
and
f\colon A\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous, then
f
extends to a continuous
\hat{f}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. For classification, this means that any labeling that is locally correct on a closed safe region
always
extends to a globally defined continuous classifier. Expressiveness of the function class is therefore never the bottleneck, the class of all continuous functions on a metrizable space can represent any locally consistent labeling.
This observation exposes a fundamental limitation of VC dimension in settings where topology matters
[
23
]
. VC dimension measures how many labelings a hypothesis class can express. But Urysohn-Brouwer lemma guarantees that continuous functions can express
all
locally consistent labelings, so VC dimension either returns a trivially small value (for restricted local classes: constant functions have
\VC=0
) or a trivially large value (for the global class: all continuous functions shatter arbitrary finite sets). Neither answer captures the true difficulty.

The source of this failure is a conflict between
local geometry
and
global topology

[
24
]
. On each safe ball
A_{j}^{\gamma/2}
, the classification task is geometrically trivial, every point has the same label, so a constant function suffices. Tietze guarantees that this constant function extends to all of
\mathcal{X}
. But the extension from safe ball
A_{j}
has
no obligation
to be correct on safe ball
A_{k}
when
j\neq k
. On a connected space with nontrivial topology (e.g.,
\beta_{1}\geq 2
), the extensions from different safe balls must traverse the same shared structure, paths through the wedge point, in the bouquet, and there is no guarantee they remain compatible. The topology forces the extensions into conflict.
The resolution is that either (a) a single global classifier must implicitly know the entire topology to satisfy all local constraints simultaneously, or (b) separate local classifiers are maintained, one per topologically independent region. Urysohn width counts option (b); VC dimension is blind to the distinction.
We now make this precise by exhibiting separations in both directions.

Theorem 6.1

(VC dimension does not determine local Urysohn width)
.

There exist two families of margin-
\gamma
classification problems

\{\mathcal{P}_{w}^{(1)}\}_{w\geq 1}
and
\{\mathcal{P}_{n}^{(2)}(C)\}_{n\geq 1,\;C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}

such that:

(a)

Width can grow while a natural associated hypothesis class has only
O(w\log w)
VC dimension.

For the bouquet family
\mathcal{P}_{w}^{(1)}:=\mathcal{P}_{w}
from Theorem
4.1
,

\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}_{w}^{(1)},\gamma)=w
.
Moreover, the class
\mathcal{H}_{w}
of classifiers that are constant on each of the
w
designated safe arcs has

\VC(\mathcal{H}_{w})\leq w\log_{2}w
.

(b)

VC can grow while width remains
1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{n}
be the concept class of unions of
n
disjoint closed intervals in
[0,1]
, separated by gaps of length at least
\gamma
. For each
C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, let
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{(2)}(C)
be the induced margin-
\gamma
problem. Then

\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}_{n}^{(2)}(C),\gamma)=1

for
D_{0}\geq 1
, while

\VC(\mathcal{C}_{n})=2n=\Theta(n)
.

Proof.

Part (a).

Take
\mathcal{P}_{w}^{(1)}:=\mathcal{P}_{w}
from Theorem
4.1
. Then

\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}_{w}^{(1)},\gamma)=w
.
Now define
\mathcal{H}_{w}
to be the family of classifiers obtained by assigning an arbitrary label in
[w]
to each of the
w
designated safe arcs and extending by a default label outside these arcs. The number of such classifiers is at most
w^{w}
, so

\VC(\mathcal{H}_{w})\leq\log_{2}(w^{w})=w\log_{2}w
.

Part (b).

Let
\mathcal{X}_{n}=[0,1]
and
D_{0}\geq 1
. For any
C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, define the margin-
\gamma
problem
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{(2)}(C)
by

A^{+}=C,\qquad A^{-}=[0,1]\setminus\mathrm{Nbr}_{\gamma/2}(\partial C)
.
Then
A^{+}
and
A^{-}
are closed and separated by distance at least
\gamma
. Since
S=[0,1]
is connected and
\mathrm{diam}(S)\leq D_{0}
, Urysohn’s lemma gives a single continuous separator on
S
, leading to

\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}_{n}^{(2)}(C),\gamma)=1
.
On the other hand, the class
\mathcal{C}_{n}
of unions of
n
intervals has VC dimension
2n
(a classical result in
[
25
]
).
∎

Remark 6.2
.

The separation reveals a fundamental distinction: VC dimension measures the
expressive capacity
of a function class (how many labelings it can realize), while Urysohn width measures the
structural complexity
of a problem (how many geometrically distinct local classification tasks it contains). A problem with many simple, disjoint tasks has low VC but high width; a problem with one complex task has high VC but low width.

7
Sample Complexity Lower Bound via Width

The results so far establish that Urysohn width is a well-defined complexity measure independent of VC dimension. A natural question is whether width has
learning-theoretic
consequences -
does it impose costs that a learner must pay
? We show that it does: width creates a sample complexity bottleneck that is invisible to the VC dimension. Even when every local classification task is trivially simple (
\VC\leq 1
on each safe ball), the learner needs
\Omega(w\log w)
samples to
discover
that
w
separate tasks exist. This is not an expressiveness bottleneck but a
coverage
bottleneck
[
26
]
: the learner must collect enough samples to hit every topologically separated region at least once, a coupon-collector phenomenon
[
27
]
driven by the topology of the input space rather than the complexity of the hypothesis class.

Sampling model.

An unknown target problem
\mathcal{P}
is chosen from the bouquet family
\{\mathcal{P}_{w}\}
. The learner receives
n
i.i.d. labeled samples
(X_{i},Y_{i})
drawn from a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{w}
supported on
\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P}_{w},\gamma)
. The learner must output a classifier correct on
all
of
\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P}_{w},\gamma)
with probability at least
2/3
.

Theorem 7.1

(Sample lower bound from width)
.

Fix the label-permuted bouquet family
\mathfrak{F}_{w}
with
K=w
classes. Assume the sampling distribution
\mathcal{D}_{w}
satisfies

\frac{1}{c_{1}w}\;\leq\;\mathcal{D}_{w}(A_{j}^{\gamma/2})\;\leq\;\frac{c_{2}}{w}\qquad\text{for all }j\in[w],

(14)

for absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}\geq 1
. Then any learning algorithm that, with probability at least
2/3
over the random choice of
\sigma
and the
n
i.i.d. samples, outputs a classifier correct on all of
\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P}_{w}^{\sigma},\gamma)
must use

n=\Omega(w\log w)=\Omega\!\bigl(\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}_{w},\gamma)\cdot\log\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}_{w},\gamma)\bigr).

(15)

Proof.

If the learner receives no sample from some safe region
A_{j}^{\gamma/2}
, then the label
\sigma(j)
assigned to that region is never observed. Since
\sigma
is a uniformly random permutation of
[w]
, conditioned on the labels observed on all sampled regions, the missing label is uniformly distributed over the remaining unused labels. Therefore, on the event that at least one safe region is missed, any hypothesis has probability at most
1/2
of being correct on that missed region.
It follows that

\Pr[\text{success}]\leq\Pr[\text{all regions seen}]+\frac{1}{2}\,\Pr[\exists\,j\text{ missed}]
,
and success probability at least
2/3
implies

\Pr[\text{all regions seen}]\geq\frac{1}{3}
.
It therefore suffices to lower bound the number of samples needed so that all
w
safe regions are observed with probability at least
1/3
. Under Eq. (
14
), each region has probability mass at most
c_{2}/w
, so observing all regions is at least as hard as a coupon collector problem with coupon probabilities of order
1/w
. Standard coupon collector lower bounds imply that there exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that if

n\leq c\,w\log w
,
then

\Pr[\text{all regions seen}]<\frac{1}{3}

for all sufficiently large
w
. Therefore any learner succeeding with probability at least
2/3
must satisfy

n=\Omega(w\log w)
.
Since Theorem
4.1
gives
\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}_{w},\gamma)=w
, this is equivalently

n=\Omega\!\bigl(\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}_{w},\gamma)\cdot\log\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}_{w},\gamma)\bigr)
.
∎

Remark 7.2
.

The
\Omega(w\log w)
bound is the
coupon collector
phenomenon
[
27
]
: to observe at least one sample from each of
w
regions, each with probability mass
\Theta(1/w)
, requires
\Theta(w\log w)
draws. This lower bound is independent of VC dimension - indeed, the local classifiers in the bouquet family have VC dimension
\leq 1
(Theorem
6.1
(a)), while
\Omega(w\log w)
samples are needed purely because of the topological structure.

8
Toward a General Betti Lower Bound

The hierarchy theorem (Theorem
4.1
) establishes
\mathrm{uw}\geq\beta_{1}
for the bouquet family. A natural concern is whether this is an artifact of the construction or a general principle. If the result only holds on bouquets, it is a curiosity; if it holds on arbitrary spaces with nontrivial topology, it is a fundamental constraint on classifier design. Data manifolds arising in practice, natural images, protein configurations, robotic state spaces, are known to have nontrivial topology - e.g., the space of natural image patches has
\beta_{1}\geq 1
using persistent homology
[
28
]
. If
\mathrm{uw}\geq c\cdot\beta_{1}
holds generally, it means the topological complexity of real data manifolds imposes a provable lower bound on the number of local experts any classifier must maintain. We present evidence that the bouquet result is the tip of a deeper phenomenon: under a mild geometric condition (locality scale below the systole), every patch in a covering is homologically trivial, suggesting that the topological complexity of the input space fundamentally constrains classifier complexity. The full generalization remains a conjecture, but the systole lemma and proof roadmap show the path to a complete proof.

8.1
Systole Controls Patch Homotopy Type

Definition 8.1
.

The
systole
of a compact geodesic space
\mathcal{X}
is

\mathrm{sys}(\mathcal{X}):=\inf\{\text{length}(\ell):\ell\text{ is a non-contractible loop in }\mathcal{X}\}.

(16)

Lemma 8.2

(Local patches are null-homotopic under geodesic convexity)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a compact geodesic space. If
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is contained in a
geodesically convex
ball
B(p,r)
with
r<\mathrm{sys}(\mathcal{X})/4
, then
S
is contractible in
\mathcal{X}
, and in particular every loop in
S
is null-homotopic.

Proof.

Since
B(p,r)
is geodesically convex, any two points in
B(p,r)
are connected by a unique geodesic lying entirely in
B(p,r)
. In particular,
B(p,r)
is contractible (contract along geodesics to the center
p
). Since
S\subseteq B(p,r)
, every loop in
S
is a loop in
B(p,r)
, which is contractible. The radius bound
r<\mathrm{sys}(\mathcal{X})/4
ensures that
B(p,r)
is indeed geodesically convex: in a compact geodesic space, any ball of radius
<\mathrm{sys}(\mathcal{X})/4
is convex (a standard result in Riemannian geometry; see e.g. Bridson-Haefliger
[
29
]
).
∎

Remark 8.3
.

The earlier version of this lemma (which appeared in a preprint) claimed that the diameter bound
\mathrm{diam}(S)<\mathrm{sys}(\mathcal{X})/2
alone implies null-homotopy of loops in
S
. That claim is false: a loop in a connected set of small diameter can have arbitrarily large length (consider a spiral). The geodesic convexity condition is the correct replacement. For the bouquet construction (Theorem
4.1
), this subtlety does not arise because each patch is an arc on a single loop, which is automatically convex.

This lemma establishes the key structural fact: if each patch is contained in a geodesically convex ball of radius below
\mathrm{sys}(\mathcal{X})/4
, then every patch is contractible. Each patch “carries no topology.”

8.2
Betti Lower Bound Under Bounded Adjacency

Adding one structural assumption to the proof roadmap yields a complete theorem.

Definition 8.4

(Bounded nerve adjacency)
.

A local Urysohn covering
\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{w}\}
has
nerve adjacency at most
\Delta_{0}
if each patch
S_{i}
intersects at most
\Delta_{0}
other patches.

Theorem 8.5

(General Betti lower bound under bounded adjacency)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a compact geodesic space and
\mathcal{P}
a margin-
\gamma
problem on
\mathcal{X}
.
Let
\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{w}\}
be a
(\gamma,D_{0})
-Urysohn covering of
\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)
satisfying:

(i)

each
S_{i}
is contained in a geodesically convex ball of radius
<\mathrm{sys}(\mathcal{X})/4
;

(ii)

every nonempty finite intersection of the
S_{i}
is contractible;

(iii)

the inclusion
\iota:\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{X}
induces a map on first homology whose image contains
\beta_{1}(\mathcal{X})
linearly independent generators of
H_{1}(\mathcal{X};\mathbb{F})
;

(iv)

each patch
S_{i}
intersects at most
\Delta_{0}
other patches.

Then we have

w\geq\frac{2\,\beta_{1}(\mathcal{X})}{\Delta_{0}}
.
Consequently, if every
(\gamma,D_{0})
-Urysohn covering of
\mathcal{P}
satisfies
(i)
-
(iv)
, then we have a general Betti lower bound

\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)\geq\frac{2\,\beta_{1}(\mathcal{X})}{\Delta_{0}}
.

Proof.

By (i) and Lemma
8.2
, each
S_{i}
is contractible. By (ii) and the Nerve Lemma,

H_{*}(\mathcal{N};\mathbb{F})\cong H_{*}(\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P},\gamma);\mathbb{F})
,
where
\mathcal{N}
is the nerve of the covering. Hence

\beta_{1}(\mathcal{N})=\beta_{1}(\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P},\gamma))
.
By (iii),

\beta_{1}(\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P},\gamma))\geq\beta_{1}(\mathcal{X})
.
Let
G=\mathcal{N}^{(1)}
be the 1-skeleton of the nerve. Since adding higher-dimensional simplices cannot create new first homology classes,

\beta_{1}(\mathcal{N})\leq\beta_{1}(G)
.
Now
G
has
w
vertices, and by (iv) each vertex has degree at most
\Delta_{0}
, so by the handshake lemma

|E(G)|\leq\frac{\Delta_{0}w}{2}
.
Since

\beta_{1}(G)=|E(G)|-|V(G)|+c(G)\leq|E(G)|
,
we obtain

\beta_{1}(\mathcal{X})\leq\beta_{1}(\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P},\gamma))=\beta_{1}(\mathcal{N})\leq\beta_{1}(G)\leq|E(G)|\leq\frac{\Delta_{0}w}{2}
.
Rearranging gives

w\geq\frac{2\,\beta_{1}(\mathcal{X})}{\Delta_{0}}
.
∎

Remark 8.6

(When is bounded adjacency realistic?)
.

In covers by balls of radius
r
in a metric space with doubling constant
\lambda
, each ball intersects at most
\lambda^{O(1)}
others, so
\Delta_{0}=O(\lambda^{O(1)})
. For Riemannian manifolds with bounded sectional curvature,
\lambda
is bounded in terms of the curvature and dimension. Therefore, bounded adjacency holds automatically in the geometric settings most relevant to applications. The assumption fails only for pathological covers with highly overlapping patches, which are unlikely to arise as optimal coverings.

Example 8.7

(Genus-
g
surface)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\Sigma_{g}
be a closed orientable surface of genus
g
. Then
\beta_{1}(\Sigma_{g})=2g
. If the safe region contains representatives of the
2g
standard cycles and patches lie in convex charts with bounded adjacency
\Delta_{0}
, Theorem
8.5
gives
\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)\geq 4g/\Delta_{0}
.

Example 8.8

(Finite connected graph)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite connected graph with
|V|
vertices and
|E|
edges, viewed as a 1D CW-complex. Then
\beta_{1}(\mathcal{X})=|E|-|V|+1
. If patches are short enough to lie in contractible subtrees, Theorem
8.5
gives
\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}\geq 2(|E|-|V|+1)/\Delta_{0}
.

Remaining Conjecture: Without Bounded Adjacency

Conjecture 8.9

(Betti lower bound without bounded adjacency)
.

Under conditions (i)–(iii) of Theorem
8.5
alone (without bounded adjacency), one still has
\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)\geq c\cdot\beta_{1}(\mathcal{X})
for some
c>0
, possibly with
c
depending on the geometry of
\mathcal{X}
.

The difficulty is that without adjacency control, the nerve on
w
vertices can have up to
\binom{w}{2}
edges, giving only
\beta_{1}(\mathcal{N})\leq O(w^{2})
and hence
w\geq\Omega(\sqrt{\beta_{1}})
- a weaker bound. Resolving this conjecture likely requires either a geometric argument that optimal coverings automatically have bounded adjacency, or a fundamentally different proof technique that bypasses the nerve.

8.3
Higher-Dimensional Extension

The bouquet argument generalizes to all dimensions without new techniques.

Theorem 8.10

(Higher-dimensional wedge hierarchy)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{w}^{(k)}:=\bigvee_{j=1}^{w}S^{k}_{j}
be the wedge of
w
copies of the
k
-sphere (
k\geq 1
), each of radius
R
, with the shortest-path metric. Then
\beta_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{w}^{(k)})=w
and
\beta_{i}(\mathcal{X}_{w}^{(k)})=0
for
i\neq 0,k
.
Define a classification problem
\mathcal{P}_{w}^{(k)}
with one safe ball per sphere at the antipode of the wedge point. If

\frac{3\gamma}{2}\leq D_{0}<\pi R-\frac{3\gamma}{4}
,
then
\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P}_{w}^{(k)},\gamma)=w=\beta_{k}(\mathcal{X}_{w}^{(k)})
.

Proof.

The proof is identical to Theorem
4.1
. Let
p_{j}
be the antipode of the wedge point
v
on sphere
S^{k}_{j}
, and define
A_{j}:=\{x:d(x,p_{j})\leq\gamma/4\}
. Any connected set of diameter
\leq D_{0}
containing a point
x_{j}\in A_{j}^{\gamma/2}
satisfies
d(x_{j},v)\geq\pi R-3\gamma/4>D_{0}
. Since every path to a different sphere must pass through
v
, no such set can intersect another sphere. Hence
\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}\geq w
. The upper bound
\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}\leq w
follows from one cap per sphere.
∎

Remark 8.11
.

This shows that the elementary metric confinement argument, patches near one component’s antipode cannot reach other components through the wedge point, works in all dimensions. No cup products, Steenrod operations, or other higher algebraic-topological machinery is needed. The obstruction is metric (diameter bound) rather than cohomological.

Conjecture 8.12

(All-Betti lower bound)
.

Under appropriate hitting and good-cover conditions generalized to all degrees,

\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)\geq c\sum_{k\geq 1}\beta_{k}(\mathcal{X}).

(17)

For wedges of spheres of mixed dimensions, the sum is the correct count; for spaces where a single patch arrangement witnesses multiple homology groups simultaneously,
\max_{k\geq 1}\beta_{k}(\mathcal{X})
may be the tighter bound.

Theorem
4.1
is the first tight special case where all three conditions hold trivially: the bouquet has
\mathrm{sys}=L
, every loop passes through its safe ball (Loop-Hitting with independent generators), and the covering from the upper bound has pairwise disjoint patches (good cover with no intersections at all).

9
Discussion

Topology as a missing axis of complexity in machine learning.

The central message of this paper is that the
connectivity structure
of the input space is a source of classification complexity that existing measures do not see. VC dimension, fat-shattering dimension, and Rademacher complexity all answer the question “
how expressive must the classifier be
?” Urysohn width answers a different question: “
how many independent classification contexts does the problem contain
?” The Urysohn-Brouwer lemma suggests (heuristically) that the existence of a continuous classifier is rarely the bottleneck; any locally correct labeling extends globally
[
23
]
. What
is
the bottleneck is the topological structure that makes local solutions incompatible with one another. The hierarchy theorem (Theorem
4.1
) and the VC separation (Theorem
6.1
) together establish that this topological axis of complexity is real, nontrivial, and independent of classical measures. The
\Omega(w\log w)
sample lower bound (Theorem
7.1
) shows it has a concrete learning-theoretic cost: topology imposes a coverage burden that no amount of classifier sophistication can eliminate.

VC dimension as selection complexity; Urysohn width as coverage complexity.

VC dimension is the natural complexity measure for function
selection
, a combinatorial question native to the Turing paradigm
[
15
]
, where inputs are structureless elements of a set and hypothesis classes are collections of labelings. It asks:
given a pre-specified class of candidate classifiers, how many samples suffice to select a good one
? This question is well-posed and the answer (
VC dimension controls uniform convergence

[
1
]
) is a foundational result of statistical learning theory
[
25
]
. But it is a question about
enumeration over a discrete set of candidates
, which is inherently TM-native computation.
Urysohn width is the natural complexity measure for geometric
coverage
, a topological question native to the Urysohn Machine paradigm, where inputs live on metric spaces with continuity and connectivity structure. It asks: how many local experts are needed to cover the problem? This question has no meaning in a purely combinatorial framework - it requires the notions of connected support, bounded diameter, and continuous separation that the UM provides.
The two measures are complementary, not competing. VC dimension governs the cost of
choosing
a good classifier from a given class (selection). Urysohn width governs the cost of
assembling
enough local classifiers to cover the problem (coverage)
[
26
]
. The separation theorems in §
6
show that neither measure subsumes the other: there are problems where selection is trivial but coverage is hard (the bouquet family), and problems where coverage is trivial but selection is hard (unions of intervals on
[0,1]
). A complete complexity theory of classification requires both axes.

Metric-topology factorization as an architectural principle.

The scaling law
\mathrm{uw}\geq\Omega(\beta_{1}\cdot L/D_{0})
is not merely a quantitative bound; it is a
structural decomposition
of classifier complexity into two independent factors. The topological factor
\beta_{1}
counts contexts that are qualitatively distinct (they live on different loops and cannot be reached from one another within the locality scale). The metric factor
L/D_{0}
counts the resolution needed within each context. This factorization suggests that optimal classifiers for topologically complex spaces should mirror the decomposition: a
topological indexing
stage that routes inputs to the correct context, followed by a
metric classification
stage that solves the local task within that context
[
30
]
. The indexing stage is governed by the global connectivity of
\mathcal{X}
and is insensitive to local geometry; the classification stage is governed by local geometry and is insensitive to global topology. Any architecture that entangles these two functions, as standard end-to-end trained networks do, pays the cost of solving both simultaneously, without leveraging their independence.

Structural decoupling for continual learning.

In a non-stationary environment where new topological contexts arrive over time, the Urysohn width of the accumulated problem grows monotonically (by Lemma
3.6
). The metric-topology factorization implies that a continual learning system must maintain two structurally decoupled components to handle this growth without catastrophic interference
[
31
]
. The first component, the
indexing system
(I-system), must detect when the current input falls outside all known contexts (the alarm in Definition
2.1
) and route to the appropriate local expert. This component operates on the topological structure and must be
gradient-free
: if the routing function is trained end-to-end, gradients from new contexts corrupt routing decisions for old contexts (the routing drift problem in Mixture-of-Experts architectures
[
32
]
). The second component, the
metric system
(M-system), maintains one local expert per context, each frozen after construction. New contexts trigger the allocation of new experts; past experts are never modified. The width lower bound proves this modular architecture is not merely convenient but
necessary
: any system that maintains fewer than
\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)
experts cannot correctly classify all points in the safe region, regardless of how sophisticated each expert is. Structural decoupling, gradient-free indexing plus parameter-disjoint metric experts, is the minimal architecture that respects the factorization.

10
Conclusion

We introduced
local Urysohn width
, a complexity measure for classification problems on metric spaces that captures a form of difficulty invisible to standard hypothesis-class measures. Whereas VC dimension, fat-shattering dimension, and Rademacher complexity quantify the expressive richness of a function class, Urysohn width quantifies the
structural complexity of the problem itself
: the minimum number of connected, diameter-bounded local experts required to cover and correctly classify the margin-safe region.
Our main results establish that this notion is mathematically nontrivial and learning-theoretically meaningful. The hierarchy theorem (Theorem
4.1
) shows that topological complexity of the input space, measured by the first Betti number
\beta_{1}
, forces classifier complexity even on connected spaces. The topology
\times
geometry scaling law (Corollary
5.1
) reveals that width factorizes multiplicatively into a topological component (
\beta_{1}
) and a geometric resolution component (
L/D_{0}
), separating two independent sources of difficulty. The two-way separation from VC dimension (Theorem
6.1
) confirms that width measures something genuinely orthogonal to expressiveness. And the
\Omega(w\log w)
sample complexity lower bound (Theorem
7.1
) shows that width creates a coverage bottleneck in learning that no amount of classifier sophistication can eliminate.

These results provide the complexity-theoretic foundation for the
Urysohn Machine
(§
2
), a computational model in which classification is factored into topological indexing (the Detect operator
\mathcal{D}
, routing inputs to the correct local expert) and metric evaluation (the Evaluate operator
\mathcal{E}
, applying the local separator). The scaling law proves that both components are necessary - neither topological routing nor metric classification alone suffices.
Several important directions remain open. The general relationship between width and topological invariants beyond the bouquet family is unresolved; Conjecture
8.9
proposes an extension to arbitrary geodesic spaces under additional geometric assumptions. Whether
\beta_{k}
for
k\geq 2
provides further lower bounds on width in higher-dimensional settings is a natural next question. Most ambitiously, one would like to develop online algorithms that adaptively grow the Metric Library to match the unknown topological width of the data manifold - a theory of continual learning grounded in structural allocation rather than global parameter reuse.

Appendix A
Safe Region Conventions and Tie Handling

The main definitions use closed
\gamma/2
-neighborhoods for the safe region:

A_{k}^{\gamma/2}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:d(x,A_{k})\leq\gamma/2\}.

(18)

With the strict margin condition
d(A_{i},A_{j})>\gamma
(Definition
3.1
), these closed neighborhoods are disjoint:

Lemma A.1

(Disjointness of safe regions)
.

If
d(A_{i},A_{j})>\gamma
for
i\neq j
, then
A_{i}^{\gamma/2}\cap A_{j}^{\gamma/2}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
x\in A_{i}^{\gamma/2}\cap A_{j}^{\gamma/2}
. Then there exist
a_{i}\in A_{i}
and
a_{j}\in A_{j}
with
d(x,a_{i})\leq\gamma/2
and
d(x,a_{j})\leq\gamma/2
. By the triangle inequality,
d(a_{i},a_{j})\leq d(a_{i},x)+d(x,a_{j})\leq\gamma
. But
d(A_{i},A_{j})>\gamma
, so
d(a_{i},a_{j})>\gamma
- a contradiction.
∎

Remark A.2

(Why strict inequality)
.

An earlier version of this paper used the non-strict condition
d(A_{i},A_{j})\geq\gamma
. As the proof above shows, that allows the degenerate case
d(a_{i},a_{j})=\gamma
where the neighborhoods can touch at a single point. The strict condition
d(A_{i},A_{j})>\gamma
eliminates this edge case cleanly. All constructions in the paper (the bouquet family, the interval-union family) satisfy the strict condition with ample room: the bouquet has
d(A_{i},A_{j})\geq L-\gamma/2\gg\gamma
.

Remark A.3

(Alternative convention: open neighborhoods)
.

One could equivalently define the safe region using
open

\gamma/2
-neighborhoods
A_{k}^{\gamma/2,\circ}=\{x:d(x,A_{k})<\gamma/2\}
and retain the non-strict margin
d(A_{i},A_{j})\geq\gamma
. Under this convention, disjointness follows immediately: if
x\in A_{i}^{\gamma/2,\circ}\cap A_{j}^{\gamma/2,\circ}
, then
d(a_{i},a_{j})<\gamma
, contradicting
d(A_{i},A_{j})\geq\gamma
. The results of this paper hold under either convention. We use closed neighborhoods with strict margin for concreteness.

Appendix B
Clarification of Theorem
6.1
: VC Dimension Notions

Theorem
6.1
establishes that Urysohn width is not determined by VC dimension. We clarify the VC notion used in each direction and its relationship to the standard textbook definition.

Standard VC dimension (textbook).

Given a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
of functions
h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\{1,\ldots,K\}
, the VC dimension
\VC(\mathcal{H})
is the largest
m
such that there exist
m
points in
\mathcal{X}
that can be labeled in all
2^{m}
binary patterns by some
h\in\mathcal{H}
. This is a property of the class
\mathcal{H}
, independent of any particular target problem.

Part (a): patchwise-constant class.

In Theorem
6.1
(a), we define
\mathcal{H}_{w}
as the class of all classifiers that are patchwise-constant on at most
w
connected sets of diameter
\leq D_{0}
. This is a standard, problem-independent hypothesis class: it depends on the parameters
w
,
D_{0}
, and the metric space
\mathcal{X}
, but not on the labeling of any particular problem instance. Its VC dimension is
O(w\log w)
by a standard counting argument: each classifier is specified by a partition of the input space into
w
patches (a combinatorial choice) and
w
label assignments.

The separation in part (a) is therefore: the problem-independent class
\mathcal{H}_{w}
has
\VC(\mathcal{H}_{w})=O(w\log w)
, yet the Urysohn width of the bouquet family grows as
w\to\infty
. Note that
\VC(\mathcal{H}_{w})
grows with
w
- the point is that width grows because
more loops are added to the space
(a topological change), not because the per-instance hypothesis class becomes more expressive.

Proposition B.1

(Conservative restatement)
.

For every fixed integer
C\geq 1
, there exists a classification problem
\mathcal{P}
with
\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}(\mathcal{P},\gamma)>C
that is correctly solvable by a patchwise-constant classifier with VC dimension
O(C\log C)
. Conversely, there exists a problem family with
\mathrm{uw}_{D_{0}}=1
for all instances, but whose concept class has VC dimension
\Theta(n)\to\infty
.

This makes it explicit: VC dimension does not determine Urysohn width, and Urysohn width does not determine VC dimension.

Part (b): concept class.

In Theorem
6.1
(b), the VC dimension is that of the concept class
\mathcal{C}_{n}
of unions of
n
intervals - a standard object in learning theory
[
25
]
with
\VC(\mathcal{C}_{n})=2n
. Here we use the textbook definition without modification.

Appendix C
Explicit Target Family for Theorem
7.1

We spell out the label-permuted bouquet family and the indistinguishability argument in full detail.

Family parameterization.

Fix the bouquet space
\mathcal{X}_{w}=\bigvee_{j=1}^{w}S^{1}
and the
w
safe balls
A_{1}^{\gamma/2},\ldots,A_{w}^{\gamma/2}
as in Theorem
4.1
. The
label-permuted bouquet family
is

\mathfrak{F}_{w}=\{\mathcal{P}_{w}^{\sigma}:\sigma\in S_{w}\},

(19)

where
S_{w}
is the symmetric group on
[w]
and
\mathcal{P}_{w}^{\sigma}
is the problem in which safe ball
A_{j}^{\gamma/2}
receives label
\sigma(j)
. All
w!
problems share the same space
\mathcal{X}_{w}
and the same safe balls; they differ only in which label is assigned to which ball.

Sampling distribution.

The distribution
\mathcal{D}_{w}
is supported on
\mathrm{Safe}(\mathcal{P}_{w},\gamma)=\bigcup_{j=1}^{w}A_{j}^{\gamma/2}
, with
\mathcal{D}_{w}(A_{j}^{\gamma/2})\geq 1/(cw)
for all
j
and some constant
c\geq 1
. The distribution is the same for all
\sigma\in S_{w}
- only the labels change, not the input distribution.

Indistinguishability argument.

Suppose the learner receives
n
i.i.d. samples and misses safe ball
A_{j}^{\gamma/2}
(no sample lands in it). Conditioned on this event:

•

The learner has observed labels
\sigma(k)
for all
k\neq j
such that
A_{k}^{\gamma/2}
was hit.

•

The unknown label
\sigma(j)
is uniformly distributed over the labels
not
observed on other balls (since
\sigma
is a uniformly random permutation).

•

If at least
2
labels remain unassigned, the learner cannot determine
\sigma(j)
and has error probability
\geq 1/2
.

Coupon-collector calculation.

To ensure
\Pr[\exists\,j\text{ missed}]\leq 1/3
, we need
\Pr[j\text{ missed}]\leq 1/(3w)
for each
j
(by the union bound). Since
\Pr[j\text{ missed}]=(1-\mathcal{D}_{w}(A_{j}^{\gamma/2}))^{n}\geq(1-1/(cw))^{n}
, we require
(1-1/(cw))^{n}\leq 1/(3w)
. Using
\ln(1/(1-x))\leq 2x
for
x\in(0,1/2)
:

n\geq\frac{cw}{2}\ln(3w)=\Omega(w\log w).

(20)

This is the coupon-collector lower bound: collecting at least one sample from each of
w
regions, each with mass
\Theta(1/w)
, requires
\Theta(w\log w)
draws.

Appendix D
Geodesic Convexity Assumptions in Section 9

Lemma
8.2
assumes that each patch is contained in a geodesically convex ball. We clarify this assumption and list settings where it is known to hold.

Definition D.1

(Geodesically convex ball)
.

A ball
B(p,r)
in a geodesic space
\mathcal{X}
is
geodesically convex
if for any
x,y\in B(p,r)
, every minimizing geodesic from
x
to
y
lies entirely in
B(p,r)
.

Definition D.2

(Convexity radius)
.

The
convexity radius
of a compact geodesic space
\mathcal{X}
is

\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})=\sup\{r>0:B(p,r)\text{ is geodesically convex for all }p\in\mathcal{X}\}.

(21)

Remark D.3

(When is the convexity radius positive?)
.

The following settings guarantee
\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})>0
:

1.

Complete Riemannian manifolds with sectional curvature
\leq\kappa
.
By the Whitehead theorem (see Bridson–Haefliger
[
29
]
, Ch. II.1), the convexity radius satisfies
\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})\geq\min(\mathrm{inj}(\mathcal{X})/2,\pi/(2\sqrt{\kappa}))
for
\kappa>0
, and
\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})=\mathrm{inj}(\mathcal{X})/2
for
\kappa\leq 0
. Here
\mathrm{inj}(\mathcal{X})
is the injectivity radius.

2.

\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
spaces.
Geodesic metric spaces satisfying the
\mathrm{CAT}(\kappa)
comparison condition have
\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})\geq\pi/(2\sqrt{\kappa})
for
\kappa>0
and
\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})=\infty
for
\kappa\leq 0

[
29
]
.

3.

Graphs and 1D CW-complexes.
For the bouquet of circles, any arc of length
<L/2
is geodesically convex. The convexity radius is
L/2
, and the systole is
L
, so
\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})=\mathrm{sys}(\mathcal{X})/2
. This is why the bouquet construction needs no additional assumptions.

4.

Simplicial complexes with piecewise-flat metrics.
Convexity radius is positive and computable from the local geometry of the triangulation.

The sufficient condition for Lemma
8.2
is
D_{0}<2\,\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})
, ensuring each patch of diameter
\leq D_{0}
fits inside a geodesically convex ball. For the conjectural Betti lower bound (Conjecture
8.9
), the strongest setting is a Riemannian manifold with bounded curvature, where
\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})
is explicitly computable and the condition
D_{0}<2\,\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{X})\leq\mathrm{sys}(\mathcal{X})/2
can be verified.

Remark D.4

(What can go wrong without convexity)
.

Without geodesic convexity, a connected set of small diameter can contain long, non-contractible loops. For example, in a space with a thin “handle” of diameter
\epsilon
but length
\gg\epsilon
, a connected set of diameter
2\epsilon
could wrap around the handle. The geodesic convexity condition excludes such pathological sets by ensuring that small patches are topologically trivial.

Appendix F
Bibliographic Notes

We collect standard references for the classical results used in this paper.

Urysohn’s lemma.

Originally proved by Urysohn
[
6
]
. Standard modern treatments appear in Munkres,
Topology
(2nd ed., 2000), Theorem 33.1, and Willard,
General Topology
(1970), Theorem 15.8. The lemma states that on a normal topological space, disjoint closed sets can be separated by a continuous function into
[0,1]
.

Urysohn-Brouwer lemma or Tietze Extension Theorem.

Due to Tietze (1915), extended by Urysohn. States that a continuous real-valued function on a closed subset of a normal space extends to the entire space. Standard reference: Munkres,
Topology
, Theorem 35.1. We use this result only for heuristic motivation in Section
6
, not as a formal premise of any theorem.

Nerve Lemma.

Due to Leray (1945) and Borsuk (1948). States that if
\{U_{i}\}
is a good cover of a paracompact space (all finite intersections are contractible), then the nerve complex has the same homotopy type as the union
\bigcup_{i}U_{i}
. Standard reference: Hatcher,
Algebraic Topology
(2002), Corollary 4G.3. Used in the proof roadmap of Conjecture
8.9
.

Systole and convexity radius.

The systole of a Riemannian manifold is the length of the shortest non-contractible closed geodesic. The convexity radius is the largest radius for which all metric balls are geodesically convex. Standard reference: Bridson–Haefliger
[
29
]
, Chapters I.3 and II.1. Used in Lemma
8.2
and Appendix
D
.

VC dimension.

Introduced by Vapnik–Chervonenkis
[
1
]
. The connection to learnability was established by Blumer et al.
[
25
]
. We use VC dimension as a comparison measure in Section
6
and Appendix
B
.
