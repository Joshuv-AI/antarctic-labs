JetPrism: diagnosing convergence for generative simulation and inverse problems in nuclear physics
and Adam Szczepaniak

Abstract
High-fidelity Monte Carlo simulations and complex inverse problems, such as mapping smeared experimental observations to ground-truth states, are computationally intensive yet essential for robust data analysis. Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) offers a mathematically robust approach to accelerating these tasks, but we demonstrate its standard training loss is fundamentally misleading. In rigorous physics applications, CFM loss plateaus prematurely, serving as an unreliable indicator of true convergence and physical fidelity. To investigate this disconnect, we designed JetPrism, a configurable CFM framework acting as an efficient generative surrogate for evaluating unconditional generation and conditional detector unfolding. Using synthetic stress tests and a Jefferson Lab kinematic dataset ( γ ​ p → ρ 0 ​ p → π + ​ π − ​ p \gamma p\to\rho^{0}p\to\pi^{+}\pi^{-}p ) relevant to the forthcoming Electron-Ion Collider (EIC), we establish that physics-informed metrics continue to improve significantly long after the standard loss converges. Consequently, we propose a multi-metric evaluation protocol incorporating marginal and pairwise χ 2 \chi^{2} statistics, W 1 W_{1} distances, correlation matrix distances ( D corr D_{\mathrm{corr}} ), and nearest-neighbor distance ratios ( R NN R_{\mathrm{NN}} ). By demonstrating that domain-specific evaluations must supersede generic loss metrics, this work establishes JetPrism as a dependable generative surrogate that ensures precise statistical agreement with ground-truth data without memorizing the training set. While demonstrated in nuclear physics, this diagnostic framework is readily extensible to parameter generation and complex inverse problems across broad domains. Potential applications span medical imaging, astrophysics, semiconductor discovery, and quantitative finance, where high-fidelity simulation, rigorous inversion, and generative reliability are critical.

Introduction

Experimental nuclear and particle physics analyses require massive simulation datasets to populate phase space. While raw Monte Carlo generation is computationally inexpensive per event, the required volume imposes a significant computational burden. For Jefferson Lab experiments (e.g., CLAS12, GlueX), modeling complex particle transport, secondary interactions, and detector responses makes GEANT-based detector simulation a primary computational bottleneck.

Detector unfolding—recovering true event-level observables from smeared measurements—relies heavily on these detailed simulations to determine precise detector responses and acceptance corrections. The immense computational demands of this process motivate developing AI-based surrogate models that approximate traditional simulations at a fraction of the cost.

Deep generative models, particularly Conditional Flow Matching, offer stable, simulation-free training dynamics. However, we demonstrate that standard Conditional Flow Matching training loss is an unreliable convergence indicator for physics-faithful generation. We explore this diagnostic gap through JetPrism, a configurable Conditional Flow Matching framework for kinematic event generation and unfolding. While the name ‘JetPrism’ reflects the framework’s broader capability to model high-multiplicity jet environments, this work utilizes its architecture specifically for the generative modeling and unfolding of photoproduction kinematics. The central contributions of this work are:

1.

Convergence diagnostics and validation suite.
We identify the premature plateau of standard Conditional Flow Matching loss, which obscures ongoing physical refinement, and establish a rigorous multi-metric protocol (
\chi^{2}
,
W_{1}
,
D_{\mathrm{corr}}
, NFE,
R_{\mathrm{NN}}
) to accurately track true convergence, verify generative fidelity, and prevent data memorization.

2.

JetPrism Framework.
We introduce JetPrism, a configurable Conditional Flow Matching-based tool, and validate its capabilities for event generation and conditional detector unfolding on a realistic Jefferson Lab dataset (
\gamma p\to\rho^{0}p\to\pi^{+}\pi^{-}p
) relevant to the forthcoming Electron-Ion Collider.

3.

Synthetic stress tests.
We provide controlled 1D benchmarks (gaussian, high-cut, multi-peak, high-frequency, delta, uniform, and exponential) to isolate generative capabilities and diagnose topological failure modes prior to deployment on real physics data.

Methodology

3.1
Datasets and feature representation

MC-POM dataset.

The MC-POM (Monte Carlo Pomeron) dataset models exclusive photoproduction
\gamma p\rightarrow\rho^{0}p\rightarrow\pi^{+}\pi^{-}p
. We focus on forward kinematics with low momentum transfer (
|t|<1~\mathrm{GeV}^{2}
), where pomeron exchange dominates. In this regime, the P-wave
\rho(770)
resonance is prominent in the
M(\pi^{+}\pi^{-})
mass spectrum. We utilize a fixed dataset of 8,000,000 events.

Events initially consist of 24-dimensional vectors encoding the four-momenta of all involved particles (
p_{\gamma}^{\mu},p_{1}^{\mu},p_{2}^{\mu},\pi^{\pm}
) and derived variables (
t,M_{\pi\pi},\cos\theta,\phi
) evaluated in the
\pi^{+}\pi^{-}
helicity rest frame. To eliminate redundancy, we project the data into a 10-dimensional phase space. Excluding the recoil proton (fixed by four-momentum conservation) and extracting the spatial momenta
(p_{x},p_{y},p_{z})
of the remaining four particles yields 12 components. Dropping the identically zero
p_{y}
components of the incident photon and target proton results in the final 10 dimensions. Each feature was standardized to zero mean and unit variance, and subsequently scaled by a factor of 5.0. Empirically, this specific scaling factor yielded marginal performance improvements compared to baseline factors of 1.0 and 2.0. This deliberate mismatch with the unit-variance prior
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
separates source and target supports along
x_{t}=t\,x_{1}+(1{-}t)\,x_{0}
, amplifying the deterministic velocity signal and improving training stability. The inverse transform recovers physical units.

For detector unfolding, we simulate resolution effects via Gaussian momentum smearing on the
\pi^{\pm}
tracks, with widths proportional to
p_{i}^{2}
scaled by
\sigma_{\mathrm{smear}}\in\{0.5,1.0,2.0\}
. The recoil proton four-momentum is subsequently algebraically inferred (
p_{2}^{\mu}=p_{\gamma}^{\mu}+p_{1}^{\mu}-p_{\pi^{+}}^{\mu}-p_{\pi^{-}}^{\mu}
). Because pion smearing directly breaks energy-momentum conservation, this inferred state drops off the exact invariant mass shell. A primary advantage of JetPrism is its ability to ingest this physically ‘broken’ conditional data and learn the implicit constraints required to project it back onto the exact ground-truth manifold. We utilize the entire 8M-event dataset for training without a holdout split; at this scale, self-evaluation bias is negligible, whereas reserving events would unnecessarily degrade reference distribution fidelity. Generalization is explicitly monitored by evaluating continuous
R_{\mathrm{NN}}
against the complete training manifold, precluding dataset memorization.

Synthetic mock datasets.

To isolate modeling challenges, we construct a diverse suite of 1D synthetic benchmarks (gaussian, high-cut, multi-peak, high-frequency, delta, uniform, and exponential) across configuration presets. These controlled environments enable rigorous ablation studies of mode collapse and fine-grained resolution prior to real physics deployment. Detailed formulations and extended results are in Appendix
C
.

3.2
Conditional flow matching framework

Figure 1
:
An illustration of the Conditional Flow Matching generation process. The model learns to transform a simple base Gaussian distribution (left) to a complex three-peak mixed-Gaussian target distribution (right) by learning the intermediate velocity field (middle).

Flow Matching defines the objective of learning a time-dependent velocity field
v_{\theta}(x,t)
whose induced Ordinary Differential Equation transports samples from a simple prior
p_{0}=\mathcal{N}(0,I)
to the target data distribution
p_{1}
. Because the marginal Flow Matching objective is intractable, we employ Conditional Flow Matching (CFM)
[
19
]
. This approach conditions on individual data samples to construct simple probability paths, yielding a tractable regression loss that recovers the exact marginal velocity field.

Given a target data sample
x_{1}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}
and noise
x_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, Conditional Flow Matching constructs interpolated samples along a linear conditional path:

x_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1},\quad t\in[0,1].

(3.1)

The conditional vector field generating this path is the constant derivative:
u_{t}=\frac{dx_{t}}{dt}=x_{1}-x_{0}
. The Conditional Flow Matching loss then trains a neural network to regress against this target velocity:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,x_{0},x_{1}}\left[\|v_{\theta}(x_{t},t)-(x_{1}-x_{0})\|^{2}\right].

(3.2)

An intuitive visual representation of this learned velocity mapping, transporting samples from a base Gaussian noise state through intermediate trajectories toward a multi-modal deterministic target, is provided in Figure
1
.

We use two distinct network variants under this shared Conditional Flow Matching objective:

Unconditional generation network.

For generative simulation, we train an unconditional velocity network
v_{\theta}(x_{t},t)
that takes as input the current state
x_{t}
concatenated with a Fourier embedding of
t
, autonomously mapping pure Gaussian noise into the target physics distribution.

Conditional unfolding network.

For detector unfolding, we train a conditional velocity network
v_{\theta}(x_{t},t\mid c)
parameterized by the detector-level measurement
c
. The conditioning vector
c
is processed through a learned two-layer SiLU-activated Multi-Layer Perceptron and concatenated alongside the
x_{t}
state and time embedding. During inference, this condition
c
is held constant throughout the entire Ordinary Differential Equation integration, allowing the network to deterministically recover particle-level kinematics from localized, smeared observations.

Both variants utilize residual backbone architectures
[
15
]
with SiLU activations. At inference, generation is performed by integrating the learned Ordinary Differential Equation from
t=0
to
t=1
via an adaptive Dormand-Prince solver using deterministic paths, establishing a deterministic mapping between the prior and target distributions. Comprehensive hyperparameter configurations of both networks are provided in Appendix
A
.

3.3
Physics-informed metrics

To rigorously evaluate model performance, we monitor the following physics-informed metrics in addition to standard training metrics, such as loss, during the validation and testing phases:

1.

Marginals:
We report the
\chi^{2}
statistic and the Wasserstein-1 distance (
W_{1}
) between generated and true univariate distributions.

2.

Pairwise joints:
We report the 2D binned
\chi^{2}
statistic (
\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{2D}}
) over all feature pairs, testing whether the model captures bivariate dependencies beyond individual marginals.

3.

Global correlation structure:
We report the correlation matrix distance
D_{\mathrm{corr}}=\|\mathrm{corr}(\text{truth})-\mathrm{corr}(\text{gen})\|_{F}
, the Frobenius norm of the Pearson correlation-matrix difference, measuring the holistic reproduction of linear dependencies across all channels.

4.

Memorization:
We report the nearest-neighbor distance ratio
R_{\mathrm{NN}}=\bar{d}_{\mathrm{gen\to train}}/\bar{d}_{\mathrm{train\to train}}
, where
\bar{d}
denotes the mean
L^{2}
nearest-neighbor distance. A ratio
R_{\mathrm{NN}}\approx 1
indicates generalization, whereas
R_{\mathrm{NN}}\ll 1
flags memorization.

In tabular summaries, we present
\chi^{2}
,
W_{1}
,
\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{2D}}
,
D_{\mathrm{corr}}
, and
R_{\mathrm{NN}}
. For dynamic tracking (figures), we monitor training loss, Number of Function Evaluations (lower values indicate straighter flows for adaptive solvers),
W_{1}
, and
D_{\mathrm{corr}}
. Detailed mathematical formulations are in Appendix
B
.

Discussion and conclusion

For practitioners applying generative models to High-Energy Physics, our results underscore a critical training dynamic: the convergence of the standard Conditional Flow Matching velocity loss can be misleading and does not ensure the fidelity of physical observables. Because physical distributions require significantly longer training to stabilize, decoupled evaluation using domain-specific observables is essential. Accounting for this discrepancy, we demonstrated through JetPrism—validated on synthetic and JLab MC-POM datasets—that Conditional Flow Matching provides a robust, deterministic framework for capturing complex phase spaces and unfolding detector-level kinematics.

By learning flexible mappings between detector observables and particle truths, JetPrism offers an AI-driven, unbinned alternative to computationally expensive Monte Carlo simulations. This event-level approach reduces binning artifacts and facilitates exploring low cross-section topologies (e.g., near-threshold
J/\psi
photoproduction,
\phi
SDMEs, exotic hybrid mesons). This accelerated pipeline is highly advantageous for Electron-Ion Collider development, enabling rapid systematic iterations of detector configurations without repeated simulation campaigns.A detailed quantification of these computational throughput gains is provided in Appendix
F
.

JetPrism prioritizes modularity and reproducibility using a PyTorch Lightning
[
12
]
, Hydra
[
27
]
, and Weights & Biases
[
7
]
backend. We utilized standard Conditional Flow Matching, which achieves high-fidelity generation and unfolding across joint and marginal distributions without the high computational costs of calculating optimal pairings via Optimal Transport Conditional Flow Matching on large datasets.

Beyond nuclear physics, this methodology naturally extends to other scientific fields tackling ill-posed inverse problems and computationally intensive simulations
[
26
]
. For instance, in material science and molecular dynamics, simulating complex lattice dynamics, discovering new semiconductors, or modeling structural biology configurations requires generative models that strictly adhere to underlying physical laws. Similarly, in medical imaging, reconstructing diagnostic scans from noisy or sparse sensor data is an inverse problem closely mirroring detector unfolding. In astrophysics and cosmology, mapping smeared observational signals back to foundational theoretical parameters shares the same demand for calibrated surrogate models
[
18
]
. Across these domains, our multi-metric validation offers a reliable blueprint to ensure generative models satisfy strict physical constraints beyond minimizing generic probability divergences.

Having identified the absolute necessity of physics-informed convergence diagnostics in Conditional Flow Matching, future deployments of JetPrism will natively integrate GEANT-based detector simulations using its modular backend. Its lightweight architecture provides an ideal foundation for uncertainty quantification, architectural ablations, and benchmarking alternative paradigms. Building on nearest-neighbor evaluations, future pipelines will incorporate large tests for out-of-distribution generalization. Explicit physics-informed loss functions
[
5
,
4
,
23
]
will be integrated to eliminate invalid generations and improve unfolding precision.

Appendix B
Evaluation metrics

To rigorously assess the fidelity of the generated kinematic distributions against the ground truth, we utilize a structured set of evaluation metrics spanning marginal distributions, multivariate structures, and network memorization characteristics:

Marginals.

The quality of individual feature distributions is measured primarily using the
\chi^{2}
statistic and the Wasserstein-1 distance (
W_{1}
):

•

Mean
\chi^{2}
(val/chi2_mean):
Evaluated over 1D binned histograms of individual features. To prevent artifacts from outlier limits, exactly 50 uniform bins are dynamically bound between the minimum and maximum values of the true expected distribution. The generated histograms are normalized to match the total event count of the truth distribution before evaluation. Formally, for a single feature, the statistic is:

\chi^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{50}\frac{(O_{i}-E_{i})^{2}}{E_{i}}

(B.1)

where
O_{i}
is the normalized generated count and
E_{i}
is the expected true count in the
i
-th bin.

•

Mean Wasserstein distance (val/wasserstein_mean):
Measures the minimum mass-transport distance required to transform the generated 1D marginal distributions to the true distributions. To explicitly circumvent the manual binning limits of
\chi^{2}
, this metric evaluates the raw, unbinned 1D distributions via their cumulative distribution functions (CDFs)
F(x)
and
G(x)
:

W_{1}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|F(x)-G(x)|\,dx

(B.2)

Pairwise joints.

To evaluate bivariate dependencies between kinematic variables, we examine two-dimensional distributions:

•

Mean 2D
\chi^{2}
(val/chi2_2d_mean):
Extends the
\chi^{2}
formulation to two-dimensional cross-sections across all 45 unique pairwise feature combinations, returning the arithmetic mean. Each axis is divided into 20 uniform bins (yielding exactly 400 rectangular bins per pair), determined entirely by the ground-truth coordinate extremes. This rigorously tests whether the generator captures underlying multivariate dependencies beyond independent marginals.

Global correlation structure.

To verify that generated events accurately reproduce multi-dimensional kinematic constraints, we assess holistic structural fidelity using:

•

Correlation matrix distance
D_{\mathrm{corr}}
(val/correlation_distance):
A holistic measure of how effectively the network reconstructs global linear relationships. It is evaluated as the Frobenius norm of the difference between the sample Pearson correlation matrices:

D_{\mathrm{corr}}=\|\mathrm{corr}(\text{truth})-\mathrm{corr}(\text{gen})\|_{F}

(B.3)

Memorization.

We test for generative generalization using comparative
L^{2}
nearest-neighbor mappings:

•

Nearest-neighbor distance ratio
R_{\mathrm{NN}}
(nn/memorization_ratio):
Serves as our principal guard against model over-fitting. It calculates the ratio of the mean nearest-neighbor distance from generated samples to the training set (
\bar{d}_{\mathrm{gen\to train}}
) versus the mean nearest-neighbor distance natively found within the training set itself (
\bar{d}_{\mathrm{train\to train}}
):

R_{\mathrm{NN}}=\frac{\bar{d}_{\mathrm{gen\to train}}}{\bar{d}_{\mathrm{train\to train}}}

(B.4)

A ratio
R_{\mathrm{NN}}\approx 1
indicates high-fidelity generalization, whereas
R_{\mathrm{NN}}\ll 1
flags strict dataset memorization. Notably, for certain synthetic mock datasets, the native distance
\bar{d}_{\mathrm{train\to train}}
can be exceptionally small, causing
R_{\mathrm{NN}}
to appear abnormally large; this is an expected geometric artifact rather than an indication of model collapse.

Additional auxiliary scalars monitored behind the
R_{\mathrm{NN}}
evaluation include:

•

nn/D_gen_to_train_mean:
The arithmetic mean
L^{2}
Euclidean distance from generated events to their single closest element in the true training manifold.

•

nn/D_train_to_train_mean:
The native baseline mean
L^{2}
distance computed by matching points from a random sub-sample of the training set to the remainder of the training elements.

•

nn/D_gen_to_train_min
and
nn/D_train_to_train_min:
The corresponding absolute minimum scalar values computed for the distributions above.

Appendix F
Computational performance

To evaluate the computational efficiency of both training and inference, we benchmark the unconditional generation and conditional unfolding tasks on CPU and GPU hardware. Table
4
summarizes the training throughput (over 10 iterations with batch size 20,000) and inference throughput (over 5 repeated runs of 50,000 samples). The GPU evaluations utilize either a single NVIDIA Tesla V100-SXM2 or an NVIDIA A100-SXM4 GPU, while the CPU evaluation is performed on an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6230 Processor. The V100 GPU delivers a
\sim
25
\times
speedup over the CPU in both phases and tasks, and the A100 further accelerates this, reaching throughputs exceeding 880K samples/s during training. These significant performance gains enable the high-throughput generation characteristic of specialized fast simulation workflows.

While training throughput is comparable across both tasks, inference speed differs dramatically: conditional unfolding achieves substantially higher throughput than unconditional generation. On the A100, unfolding operates at
1.01\times 10^{5}
events/s compared to
3.70\times 10^{3}
events/s for unconditional generation (an
\sim
27
\times
speed disparity consistent across GPU hardware). This disparity arises primarily from the ODE solver tolerances: unconditional generation uses strict tolerances (
\texttt{atol}=\texttt{rtol}=10^{-7}
), whereas unfolding employs relaxed tolerances (
\texttt{atol}=\texttt{rtol}=10^{-3}
), requiring far fewer function evaluations per integration. Notably, the relaxed tolerances introduce no measurable degradation in unfolding fidelity (Table
1
), suggesting that the conservative generation tolerances could be substantially loosened to achieve comparable speedups without compromising distributional accuracy—a promising avenue for future optimization.
