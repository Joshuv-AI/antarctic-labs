The effect of increasing dietary Vitamin E and D doses on the performance of broilers and CLDN2 gene expression during experimental heat stress
Authors: Soudeh Shaabanzadeh Soufiabadi, Mohammad Chamani, Ali Asghar Sadeghi, Mehdi Aminafshar, Farhad Foroudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of increased doses of vitamins E and D on broiler performance, blood parameters, and intestinal CLDN2 gene expression under heat stress (HS). Four hundred broilers were allocated to five groups: a negative control (C−) receiving recommended vitamin levels without HS exposure, a positive control (C+) exposed to HS without additional vitamin supplementation, a group receiving a higher dose of vitamin D (6000 IU/kg) during HS (D6), a group receiving a higher dose of vitamin E (100 mg/kg) during HS (E100), and a group receiving a combination of higher doses of vitamins D and E during HS (D6E100). Broiler performance, blood parameters, and intestinal CLDN2 gene expression were assessed. The results showed that blood glucose, ALT, AST, and LDH levels were significantly reduced in the D6E100 group following HS exposure (p< 0.05). Administration of higher vitamin doses increased feed intake and reduced the feed conversion ratio (p< 0.05). Heat stress significantly upregulated intestinal CLDN2 gene expression, whereas supplementation with vitamins D and E significantly downregulated its expression. In conclusion, HS negatively affected broiler performance and increased intestinal CLDN2 gene expression. However, combined supplementation with higher doses of vitamins D and E mitigated these adverse effects, suggesting a potential strategy to enhance broiler resilience under HS conditions. Introductiona

The poultry industry is expanding globally in response to the growing demand for protein sources (Wasti et al., 2020). Improving poultry production capacity can provide substantial benefits. To enhance production efficiency, intensive genetic selection and increased metabolic rates are commonly used to meet rising demand. However, increased metabolic activity and selection for high performance have made poultry more susceptible to heat stress (HS) (Wasti et al., 2020).

Heat stress is among the most significant environmental stressors, particularly in tropical regions. It is strongly associated with various physiological disorders in poultry, including nervous and immune system dysfunctions (Lian et al., 2020; Siddiqui et al., 2020), oxidative stress (Akbarian et al., 2016), reduced feed intake (Goo et al., 2019), and a higher feed conversion ratio, ultimately leading to impaired production performance (Lin et al., 2006). Moreover, HS is a major factor contributing to intestinal barrier disruption through intestinal hypoxia. The intestinal epithelial barrier serves as a defense mechanism against antigens and pathogens in the gastrointestinal tract.

Several factors influence the functionality of the intestinal mucosal barrier. Abnormal CLDN2 gene expression has been reported to damage epithelial and endothelial cell integrity, leading to intestinal dysfunction (Patel et al., 2012). Intestinal inflammation can increase CLDN2 expression and contribute to the disruption of tight junction integrity (Liu et al., 2013).

Vitamin E (Puthpongsiriporn et al., 2001) and vitamin D (Oikeh, 2019) supplementation are commonly used strategies to mitigate the effects of HS in poultry, resulting in improved feed conversion efficiency. Vitamin D is well recognized for its anti-inflammatory properties, including the reduction of proinflammatory cytokines in chicken macrophages (Shojadoost et al., 2015). Similarly, vitamin E exhibits antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects (El-Senousey et al., 2018), enhances immune cell function, and stimulates antibody production in response to stressors (Shojadoost et al., 2015).

Dietary vitamin E supplementation has been reported to increase feed intake and body weight gain while improving antioxidant capacity and immune function in birds (Khan et al., 2011). Vitamin D receptors (VDRs) play an essential role in maintaining intestinal barrier homeostasis by preserving tight junction integrity. Conversely, vitamin D deficiency may impair the mucosal barrier, increasing susceptibility to mucosal damage and inflammatory bowel disease (Zhang et al., 2015).

Although previous studies have highlighted the beneficial roles of vitamins E and D in poultry under HS conditions, limited information exists regarding the effects of higher doses of these vitamins on intestinal CLDN2 gene expression and broiler performance. Therefore, the present study was conducted to evaluate the effects of vitamins D and E on performance, blood parameters, liver enzymes, and intestinal CLDN2 gene expression in broiler chickens under HS conditions.

Materials and methods

Bird density and housing conditions

The experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Islamic Azad University, Science and Research Branch, Tehran, Iran. The study was conducted from mid-July to late August 2018 at a poultry farm located in Hashtgerd city, Alborz province, Iran (35.94°N, 50.66°E, elevation 1175 m). The facility maintained controlled temperature and humidity using heaters, ventilation fans, and misting systems. Environmental conditions were monitored using a TES-1362 temperature and humidity meter (TES, Taiwan).

Due to the inclusion of a negative control group (C−), the experiment was conducted in two separate halls (with and without HS). Birds were housed in pens (1 × 1.5 m²) on straw bedding and had ad libitum access to feed and water. The vaccination program followed regional veterinary guidelines. Lighting intensity ranged from 25 to 30 lx during the first week and 10–15 lx thereafter. The photoperiod was 23 h light/1 h dark (L23:D1) during the first week and L20:D4 thereafter.

The initial rearing temperature was maintained at 32–33 °C with 50–60% relative humidity and was gradually reduced to 24 °C by day 14. From days 14 to 42, non-stressed birds were kept at 21–24 °C. Heat stress was induced by exposing birds to 32 °C for 6 h daily (09:00–15:00) from days 14 to 35. Relative humidity ranged between 60 and 70% from week two to the end of the experiment.

Nutritional requirements and treatments

The basal diet consisted of starter (1–21 days) and finisher (22–42 days) phases and was formulated according to ROSS 308 broiler nutrition guidelines. A total of 400 broilers were randomly assigned to five treatments in a completely randomized design (CRD), with four replicates per group and 20 birds per replicate.

The experimental groups included C− (negative control; basal diet without additional vitamins and no HS), C+ (positive control; HS without additional vitamins), D6 (6000 IU/kg vitamin D under HS), E100 (100 mg/kg vitamin E under HS), and D6E100 (combination of vitamin D and E under HS). Vitamins were uniformly mixed into the feed in crystalline (vitamin D) and powder (vitamin E) forms. Starter and finisher diets were provided during the respective growth phases (Table 1).

Growth performance

Body weight gain was calculated based on the difference in body weight over a given period, including adjustments for mortality. Feed intake was determined by subtracting remaining feed from total supplied feed and dividing by the number of birds. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated as feed intake divided by weight gain. Feed composition and amino acid profiles were analyzed by Evonik Degussa Company (Tehran, Iran).

CLDN2 gene expression

CLDN2 gene expression in jejunal samples was assessed using RT-qPCR. Samples (12 birds per treatment) were collected on days 14 (pre-HS) and 28 (during HS) and stored at −40 °C. RNA extraction was performed using a silica-based kit (Pars Tous Biotechnology, Iran). RNA quality and quantity were evaluated using agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. cDNA synthesis was performed using an Easy cDNA Synthesis Kit (Pars Tous Biotechnology, Iran). qPCR was conducted using RealQ Plus 2x Master Mix Green (Ampliqon, Denmark) on a Rotor-Gene 6000 system (Corbett Life Science, Australia). The thermal profile included initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation (95 °C, 15 s), annealing (60 °C, 30 s), and extension (72 °C, 30 s). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method, with β-actin as the reference gene (Table 2).

Blood parameters and liver enzymes

Blood samples were collected from the wing vein of four randomly selected birds per experimental unit between days 14 and 42. Samples were processed using heparinized and non-heparinized tubes. Serum was obtained by centrifugation at 600 g for 15 min and stored at −20 °C.

Serum concentrations of AST, ALT, LDH, and cholesterol (CHOL) were measured using a Hitachi 912 autoanalyzer (Hitachi, Japan) with commercial kits (Pars Azmoon, Tehran, Iran).

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the UNIVARIATE procedure and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Statistical analyses were performed using the GLM procedure in SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Means were compared using Duncan’s multiple range test, with significance set at p≤ 0.05.

Results

Growth performance

The effects of vitamin E, vitamin D, and their combination on broiler growth performance and feed conversion ratio are presented in Table 3. Group C+ showed significantly lower weight gain during HS (p< 0.05), whereas no significant differences were observed among treatments before HS (p> 0.05). During weeks three to five, which coincided precisely with the HS period, group D6E100 showed significantly higher weight gain than the other groups (p< 0.05). Moreover, HS significantly decreased broiler FI. The groups exposed to HS showed lower FI than the C− group (p< 0.05). Group C+ showed significantly lower feed intake at week five than the other groups. In contrast, the groups that received supplemental vitamin D or E, or their combination, did not differ significantly from the C− group. Group C+ had lower BW and FI and a higher FCR than the other groups (p< 0.05). Groups E100, D6, and D6E100 showed improved FCR compared with the C+ group during the experiment.

Liver enzymes and blood parameters

The effects of vitamins D3 and E on blood cholesterol and triglyceride concentrations in broilers are presented in Table 4. The results showed that blood cholesterol and triglyceride concentrations increased during HS. In the second week, blood cholesterol concentration in the C+ group increased nonsignificantly compared with the C− group. Blood cholesterol concentration significantly increased in the C+ group during the fourth and fifth weeks of the experiment, which coincided with the HS period. In contrast, blood cholesterol concentrations decreased as the effect of HS diminished. There was no statistically significant difference in blood cholesterol concentration among the C−, D6, E100, and D6E100 groups. In addition, blood triglyceride concentration increased during HS, whereas the D6, E100, and D6E100 groups showed lower triglyceride levels than the C+ group (p< 0.05).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate whether vitamins D and E can improve broiler performance under heat stress conditions by regulating intestinal CLDN2 gene expression. The results showed that broiler performance, including BW, FI, and FCR, was positively affected by increasing dietary doses of vitamins D and E under HS conditions. These results were consistent with those of previous studies (Cheng et al., 2019; Ghazi Harsini et al., 2012; Niu et al., 2009; Tawfeek et al., 2014).

Some studies have reported that increasing ambient temperature above 20 °C negatively affects feed consumption (Niu et al., 2009). They noted that increasing the temperature by 10 °C resulted in a 17% reduction in feed consumption. Moreover, consistent with the results of this study (Brannan et al., 2022), they observed that HS could decrease feed consumption and increase FCR. At the same time, they observed that HS did not affect total BW in birds. This may be due to reduced appetite in broilers as a consequence of HS. Heat increment is another limiting factor for increased feed consumption. Heat stress affects peripheral heat receptors, causing nerve impulses to be transmitted to the appetite center in the hypothalamus, resulting in appetite suppression and reduced food intake. Therefore, fewer nutrients are available for hormone production and enzymatic activities (Attia et al., 2017). Digestive enzyme activity, for instance, may be reduced, and nutrient digestion and absorption may subsequently be impaired. This may lead to an increase in birds' FCR and possibly weight loss (Ege et al., 2019).

In the present study, the groups receiving higher doses of vitamin E and vitamin D, as well as the mixture of these two vitamins, showed greater weight gain than the C+ group. Moreover, the vitamin E group showed the highest weight gain under HS conditions. Broiler FI increased, and FCR decreased. These results were consistent with those of previous studies (Attia et al., 2017; Rehman et al., 2018; Sahin et al., 2003). However, they contrast with other findings indicating that body weight and feed intake were not significantly affected by dietary supplementation with vitamin E (Niu et al., 2009). Heat stress has also been reported to increase plasma corticosterone concentration in broilers (Ghazi et al., 2015). In contrast, reduced hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis activity has been reported to reduce stress responses in birds (Mohammed et al., 2018).

Several mechanisms may explain the positive effects of vitamin E. First, increasing blood vitamin E concentration, as an enzymatic antioxidant, reduces serum ACTH concentration, resulting in a lower impact of HS on broilers (Sahin et al., 2003). Second, because of the antioxidant capacity of vitamin E supplements, vitamin E can inhibit ROS production and, consequently, HSP70 (heat shock protein 70) production, which can improve bird performance (Jang et al., 2014). Vitamin E has also been reported to increase growth hormone secretion, which may contribute to weight gain Rehman et al. (2018).

Heat stress in broiler chickens causes decreases in the blood mineral elements sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus. Decreases in these elements cause electrolyte imbalance in the body and, as a result, metabolic processes may not function as efficiently as they should. Calcium is one of the elements that plays a fundamental role in the absorption of other elements (Amiri et al., 2024). Previous studies have shown that vitamin D, during heat stress, acts on small-intestinal enterocytes, increases intestinal absorption of plasma calcium, and can increase the absorption of other elements by stimulating parathyroid hormone (Shojadoost et al., 2021). By improving elemental absorption during heat stress, electrolyte balance is maintained and, as a result, growth performance in birds is increased by improving metabolic processes.

The results of this study indicated that blood total protein concentrations were higher in the C+ group than in the C− and D groups during week five. However, blood albumin concentration in the C+ group was lower than in the other groups during HS (week 4 to 5). These results are consistent with those of previous studies (Attia et al., 2017; Sahin et al., 2003). Heat stress increases total protein, which may lead to increased lipid peroxidation and ROS production. Consequently, ROS generation triggers damage to various biomolecules, including proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids (Panda & Cherian, 2014). A possible explanation for albumin reduction during HS is a lower protein supply. In addition, feed consumption decreases during HS, which may be one of the primary reasons for reduced blood albumin levels (Rehman et al., 2018).

Glucocorticoids released to combat stress promote free radical production, leading to protein degradation, membrane lipid peroxidation, and decreased performance (Poorghasemi et al., 2024). Previous studies have shown that vitamin E can enhance the antioxidant system and prevent free radical production in poultry under heat stress conditions, thereby preventing detrimental effects on growth and body metabolism (Tahmasbi et al., 2012). Under stress conditions, changes in mitochondrial nutrient metabolism occur because of decreased oxidant defenses, which may contribute to increased production of reactive oxygen species (Tahmasbi et al., 2012).

The activity of antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase, has been shown to be associated with vitamin D levels (Poorghasemi et al., 2017). Cellular and animal studies show that vitamin D has a protective role against oxidative stress by altering gene expression and the synthesis of antioxidants, including catalase, superoxide dismutase, and glutathione peroxidase, as well as other oxidative stress factors, and may play an effective role in cellular redox pathways and improved bird performance (Yang et al., 2020).

Ultimately, serum albumin concentration decreases during HS, whereas the dietary supplements used in this experiment have antioxidant properties that help prevent an excessive reduction in albumin.

The levels of some enzymes in blood serum, such as ALT, AST, and LDH, can reflect tissue health (Senanayake et al., 2015). Heat stress significantly increased ALT and AST concentrations from weeks three to six, which coincided precisely with the HS period. This is consistent with the results of a previous study (Sahin et al., 2003). The authors showed that HS significantly increased AST and ALT levels in quail blood. In contrast, increasing vitamin E and D concentrations decreased the levels of these indicators in broiler blood. It appears that higher doses of vitamins D and E could mitigate the effects of HS on some organs. Heat stress causes peroxidation of lipid membranes in liver cells and increases the release of AST and ALT into the bloodstream (Huang et al., 2017). Consequently, any decrease in the effect of HS on the liver can lead to lower blood levels of liver enzymes. It has also been demonstrated that increasing dietary antioxidant concentration (Hu et al., 2019) reduced the negative effects of HS on the liver and the blood concentrations of ALT and AST. Consistent with these results, one study showed that long-term exposure to HS increased blood levels of ALS, AST, creatine kinase, and LDH in laying hens (Melesse et al., 2011). In the current study, HS increased blood ALT and AST concentrations in broilers. At the same time, dietary supplementation with vitamins E and D may help protect sensitive broiler tissues, including the liver and heart, from the unfavorable effects of HS.

Vitamin E has been shown to act independently as an antioxidant at the cellular level and can prevent liver damage by reducing oxidative stress, controlling the adverse effects of hyperlipidemia, and inhibiting inflammatory cytokine production (Jankowski et al., 2017). Studies have shown that vitamin D3 can prevent fat accumulation in the liver and, as a result, liver damage by inhibiting lipogenesis and enhancing fat oxidation. In fact, vitamin D3 can regulate lipid metabolism by increasing ATG16L1 gene expression and prevent liver damage and increased liver enzymes (Yin et al., 2012).

This study showed that blood glucose concentration increased during HS, with the C+ group having the highest concentration compared with the other groups. In contrast, dietary supplementation with vitamins D and E ameliorated the adverse effect of HS on blood glucose. These results are consistent with those of previous studies (Rehman et al., 2018; Tawfeek et al., 2014). Increasing ambient temperature has been shown to increase glucocorticoid concentrations as a consequence of increased adrenocorticotropic hormone levels. Because glucocorticoids stimulate gluconeogenesis in muscle tissue, any changes in blood glucocorticoid levels can affect blood glucose concentrations, especially under HS conditions.

Researchers have reported that vitamin E in broiler chicken diets caused a decrease in blood glucose during heat stress (Rafiee et al., 2016). They stated that this decrease was associated with an increase in adrenocorticotropin, a hormone that increases catabolic processes and glucose utilization (Rafiee et al., 2016).

Vitamin E can also help improve insulin action by inhibiting protein kinase C and reducing internal levels of diacylglycerol, which can reduce blood glucose during heat stress (Hosseini-Mansoub et al., 2010).

Animal studies show that vitamin D stimulates pancreatic beta cells to secrete insulin because vitamin D enhances the ability of calcium to influence insulin secretion. The association between vitamin D deficiency and insulin resistance may be mediated through inflammation, because vitamin D deficiency is associated with increased inflammatory markers. Vitamin D has been shown to improve glucose tolerance and reduce insulin resistance (Yang et al., 2019).

The results of this study showed that CLDN2 gene expression increased in the broiler jejunum during HS. In contrast, increasing dietary levels of vitamins E and D reduced CLDN2 gene expression. The claudin superfamily plays a crucial role in maintaining barrier function and preventing bacterial infections in the host (Krishna Rao & Samak, 2013). These results are consistent with those of a previous study (He et al., 2020), which demonstrated that HS increased the expression of certain CLDN gene families. Vitamin E has been reported to decrease CLDN2 gene expression through two different mechanisms. First, because vitamin E is a potent antioxidant in the redox system, it can inhibit the production or activation of free radicals. This can ultimately inhibit cytokine production and release (Lian et al., 2020). The second function of vitamin E in CLDN2 gene expression is not redox-dependent. It can regulate intracellular signaling pathways, such as by inhibiting protein kinase C activity or phosphatidylinositol kinase-3 activation (Domazetovic et al., 2020). Thus, vitamin E can inhibit inflammatory cytokine production through these two mechanisms and ultimately suppress CLDN2 gene expression (Goel et al., 2021).

Previous research indicates that 1,25(OH)2D3 can regulate CLDN2 protein levels. Researchers found that the effects of vitamin D on CLDN2 are mediated by the p-Stat-6 signal. In fact, vitamin D reduces CLDN2 levels by blocking Stat-6 phosphorylation (Domazetovic et al., 2020).

Heat stress can disturb the balance between ROS production and the antioxidant system in broilers. Increased ROS may then lead to increased expression of proinflammatory cytokines (Jankowski et al., 2017). These agents can negatively affect the intestinal barrier by altering tight junction function. Interleukin-6, for instance, can increase tight junction permeability by increasing CLDN2 gene expression (Suzuki et al., 2011). Moreover, increased CLDN2 gene expression due to inflammation may influence intestinal intracellular pathways. This can ultimately increase pathogen passage as well as water removal from different sections of the intestine. Thus, any reduction in CLDN2 gene expression should serve as a defense mechanism against pathogen entry and diarrhea. In the current study, vitamins E and D, as well as their combination, effectively decreased intestinal CLDN2 gene expression and diarrhea resulting from high ambient temperatures.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results showed that heat stress had unfavorable effects on the performance of broiler chickens. In contrast, increasing dietary concentrations of vitamin E and vitamin D improved the performance of heat-stressed broilers. Moreover, vitamin E and vitamin D supplementation significantly decreased intestinal CLDN2 gene expression in broilers during HS. Therefore, this intervention may produce healthier chicks during the rearing period under heat stress conditions. Finally, the results showed that dietary supplementation with vitamins D and E can enhance broiler performance, especially under high-temperature stress conditions.
