Poster 160. Assessing the Chronological Progression of Pain And Myofiber Damage in a Novel Exercise-Induced Muscle Injury Model
Authors: Alex Youn, Hiroyori Fusagawa, Sankalp Sharma, Hanzhi Zhang, Nesa Milan, Leila Hennessy, Sanvi Sharma, Mia Schraith, Jarret Weinrich, Xuhui Liu, Brian T Feeley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:In high-level competitive settings, athletes may be required to push the limits of their normal workload through continuous, repetitive training, thus resulting in exercise-induced muscle injury. Unlike severe muscle strains, which are often caused by acute, deleterious eccentric contractions, this type of muscle injury occurs when routine, non-harmful movements are repetitively performed without allowing for adequate rest, thus leading to a fatigued muscle. The chronological progression of exercise-induced muscle fatigue to injury remains unclear, and to date, no animal model has been proposed that accurately replicates this fatigued muscle condition that we consistently witness in humans. In this study, we propose a novel, injury-prone fatigued muscle model by implementing a series of continuous isometric contractions induced by neuromuscular electrical stimulation and trending both muscle strength and quality from baseline to injury, then back to full recovery.Methods:This study employed the use of 10-13-week-old, C57/BL6 mice (N = 9). To avoid the potential confounding variable of sex, only male mice were used. Mice underwent five daily sets of 100 isometric contractions (ISO) of the right plantar flexor muscles using electrical stimulation via surface electrodes for six consecutive days (Figure 1A). Each contraction was administered at 1ms pulses at 70Hz and 45V lasting for 0.35s. There was a 5-minute break in-between consecutive sets. Prior to each ISO protocol, weightbearing ratio and stride length were assessed using the Blackbox R4 device (Blackbox Bio). Additionally, in vivo torque analysis was conducted to measure maximum contractile force of both left and right plantar flexor muscles. On day 7, five of the nine mice were sacrificed, and bilateral plantar flexor muscles-gastrocnemius muscles and plantaris-were harvested, sectioned and stained with Evan's Blue Dye (EBD) to assess myofiber damage. The remaining underwent, exclusively, routine Blackbox and torque testing until full recovery at week 4, after which their plantar flexor muscles were similarly harvested and stained for histologic analysis. In a separate experiment, an additional cohort of mice (N = 4) underwent the same ISO protocol and sacrificed on day 4 to observe early-stage myofiber damage with EBD stain. Paired t-tests were used to measure differences in raw torque and histological differences in percent area of positive EBD signal. Statistical differences are reported as mean difference ± SEM, and significance was set at p < 0.05.Results:Maximum isometric torque measurements revealed a significant decline of right plantar flexor muscle strength from after two rounds of ISO, -4.10 mN-m ± 1.11 mN-m, p < 0.05, which persisted until full recovery at week 4, -0.98 nM-m ± 0.50, p = 0.14 (Figure 1B). Blackbox analysis similarly revealed significant decline in hindpaw weightbearing ratio beginning at day 2, indicating that the ISO-generated pain caused mice to bear more of their weight on the contralateral, uninjured leg (Figure 2A-B). Additionally, right hindpaw stride length significantly decreased-and also persisted-after four rounds of ISO, -0.91 cm ± 0.15, p < 0.05, exhibiting that the exercise-induced muscle damage generated not only pain but also limited functional mobility (Figure 2C). Histologically, there was significantly higher percent area of positive EBD signal in the ipsilateral versus contralateral side on day 7, 6.38% ± 2.24%, p < 0.05, with no difference observed either at the early stage timepoint of day 3, 3.16% ± 1.51%, p = 0.10, or at week 4, -0.09% ± 0.40%, p = 0.83, the latter suggesting full recovery from exercise-induced myofiber damage (Figure 3A-B).Conclusions:In this study, we developed a novel mouse model, in which repeated loading using ISO contractions induces severe muscle injury over time. Unlike overuse conditions, which are known to cause intramuscular fibrosis as a result of prolonged inflammatory responses from repetitive loading, this injury-prone muscle condition resulted in marked muscle damage and significant strength decline. Notably, full recovery was noted by week 4, in terms of behavioral measurements, in vivo torque testing, and muscle histology. Our findings demonstrate that even isometric contractions, which are generally considered safe, can induce muscle injury when performed without adequate rest. Future research using this model is needed to define the key molecular phenotypes of fatigued versus injured muscle, to compare male and sex differences in injury severity and progression, and to eventually trial therapeutic interventions for at-risk athletes inevitably exposed to repetitive, strenuous physical activity. In summary, to our knowledge, this is the first evidence of a model mapping out the chronological progression of exercise-induced muscle injury from baseline to a significantly injured state, back to full recovery. This model can serve as vehicle through which therapeutic interventions can be trialed, with the eventual goal of developing safe treatment options for injured athletes.
