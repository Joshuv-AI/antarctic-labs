Graphdiyne confined-membrane with intrinsic in-plane-pores for angstrom-scale gas sieving
Authors: Jingrui Fan, Xing Liu, Chunlei Wang, Huiling Chen, Guiping Yang, Yichang Pan, Guosheng Shi, Joseph S Francisco, Gaofeng Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGraphdiyne (GDY), an emerging carbon allotrope with inherent and well-defined in-plane nanopores, holds high potential as a molecular sieve membrane. However, experimentally validating its precision separation capability has been hindered by the challenges in fabricating continuous membranes with vertically accessible intraplane pores. Here, we develop a confinement-driven pore-registered synthesis strategy that enables the templated growth of GDY within the aligned meso-channels of covalent organic framework (COF), producing confined GDY nanomembranes. The resulting GDY⊕COF membrane exhibits high selectivity for separating 3-Angstrom gas molecules (H2, He) from larger molecules. Theoretical calculations reveal the selectivity originates from a synergy of strict size exclusion and an amplified electronic polarization effect within the unique GDY framework. As GDY represents an extensive family of graphyne materials with tuneable pore size and functions, this work not only addresses the challenge of small gas separation in graphitic membranes but also establishes a versatile strategy for developing advanced graphyne-family materials separation membranes.Subject terms: Synthesis and processing, Polymers, Polymers

Introduction

Low-dimensional graphitic materials such as graphene and carbon nanotubes have advanced membrane science by offering atomically smooth surfaces and molecular-sieving nanometre channels, demonstrating transformative potential in gas separation and desalination1–5. However, precise pore engineering and scalable fabrication remain fundamentally limited6–9. Conventional bottom-up synthesis methods, exemplified by chemical vapour deposition, often yield graphitic membranes with structural heterogeneity due to stochastic nucleation and defect-prone assembly processes. Simultaneously, the lack of angstrom-scale precision in pore engineering creates a critical trade-off between molecular selectivity and permeance10,11. For instance, carbon nanotubes exhibit inherent diameter variations, while graphene requires destructive post-synthesis etching to create permeation pathways, inevitably compromising structural integrity and pore uniformity7,11. This fundamental dichotomy, between the need for atomic-level precision in pore architecture and the constraints of top-down fabrication, motivates thermodynamically guided molecular assembly strategies.

Graphdiyne (GDY), a two-dimensional (2D) carbon allotrope with sp/sp2-hybridised alkadyne-phenyl conjugated frameworks, presents inherent advantages for membrane applications, including atomic thickness, uniform in-plane nanopores, high stability, and mild synthetic accessibility12–15. Unlike conventional graphitic analogues, GDY can be synthesized directly from molecular monomers via solvothermal coupling under moderate conditions, suggesting a viable pathway for bottom-up membrane construction akin to that of crystalline microporous membranes16,17. However, its practical implementation is constrained by high monomer reactivity and uncontrolled orientational growth, which impede the formation of continuous planar membranes18. Existing strategies to achieve flat GDY nanostructures, such as interfacial growth, oxidative exfoliation, and terminal functionalization, still face challenges similar to those encountered with graphene-based membranes, including multi-step processing and weak interlayer interactions18,19. Moreover, as a prototypical representative of the conjugated diyne family encompassing graphyne and graphyne-like materials, experimental validation of GDY in-plane pore separation performance will propel broad applications of this material family, featuring precisely tailorable pore dimensions and functionalities, across diverse separation scenarios5. Therefore, establishing a controlled and versatile method to fabricate high-quality GDY membranes is crucial for advancing this promising class of materials into high-performance separation applications. Concurrently, covalent organic frameworks (COFs) provide crystalline porous networks with programmable nano-scale pore, high stability, rich functional diversity, and proven membrane-forming ability20–26. Although their inherent pore sizes in nanometre typically exceed those required for gas sieving applications27–30, the combination of well-defined aligned meso-channels and readily modifiable internal surfaces presents a unique opportunity to leverage COFs as spatially confined reactors for templated GDY growth.

Herein, we develop a pore-registered synthesis strategy. We utilize an alkynyl-functionalized COF with aligned one-dimensional channels as a template to direct the spatially confined growth of GDY via covalent cross-linking between the terminal alkynes of the COF and the GDY precursor. This strategy ensures the registered formation of GDY nanomembranes anchored covalently within the COF channels, exposing the in-plane pores for molecular transport. The resultant COF-confined GDY nanomembrane achieves precise molecular discrimination for small gases, providing the evidence of GDY sieving capability. Theoretical calculations attribute the high selectivity primarily to strict size exclusion, complemented by an amplified electronic polarization effect that emerges as a unique attribute of the GDY matrix.

Results

Design and synthesis pore-registered GDY nanomembranes

The synthesis of pore-registered GDY nanomembranes employed COF channels as spatially aligned templates, implementing a photolithography-inspired registration strategy to ensure nanoscale precision in GDY pore alignment (Fig. 1). A BPTA-TAPB-COF membrane, synthesized from 2,5-bis(prop-2-yn-1-yloxy)terephthalaldehyde (BPTA) and 1,3,5-tris(4-aminophenyl) benzene (TAPB) on pre-activated porous ceramic support, served dual templating and anchoring functions through periodic alkynyl moieties at its structural nodes. GDY structure was obtained from hexaethynylbenzene (HEB) monomer. The aligned pores of COF spatially confined the lateral HEB polymerization, while COF-wall-anchored alkynyl groups enabled regioselective coupling via terminal acetylene activation. Copper-catalysed Glaser-Hay coupling in pyridine simultaneously grafted HEB onto these alkynyl sites and promoted self-polymerization, generating confined GDY nanomembranes (denoted as GDY⊕COF) with exposing in-plane pores (Fig. 1). This confinement mechanism with COF-wall/GDY hetero-coupling suppressed random GDY nucleation, yielding fixed nanomembranes with intrinsic in-plane pores. The pore-registered strategy thus delivers accessible GDY nano-membranes for validation of theoretically predicted separation performance.

The fabricated GDY⊕COF membranes on the interior surfaces of porous ceramic tubes exhibited uniform substrate coverage with a distinct colour shift from orange (pristine BPTA-TAPB-COF) to intense yellow upon GDY incorporation (Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Figs. 1–3). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) presented a defect-free, continuous GDY⊕COF membrane with a thickness of ~500 nm conformally deposited on the ceramic support (Fig. 2c, d, Supplementary Fig. 4). This thickness of COF layer was optimized to ensure low-defect and reliable parent membranes for the subsequent pore-registered synthesis. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) demonstrated ordered domain arrangements through distinct periodic ring patterns for parent BPTA-TAPB-COF (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 5). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) imaging resolved regular equilateral hexagonal frameworks with inscribed circle diameters of ~2.7 nm, which aligned closely with the theoretically predicted BPTA-TAPB-COF architecture (Fig. 2f). In comparison, the structural integrity remained preserved after pore-registered GDY integration, as indicated by the persistence of hexagonal boundaries (Fig. 2g, h), suggesting that GDY growth occurred predominantly via in-plane reactions within the COF matrix rather than through membrane surface deposition. Conversely, compared to the clear texture of GDY control sample (Fig. 2i), the microstructure of GDY within the GDY⊕COF membrane proved challenging to resolve the ultrathin nature of the GDY nanomembranes.

The structural formation of BPTA-TAPB-COF and GDY⊕COF was systematically verified through spectroscopic analyses. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy confirmed successful COF synthesis through the disappearance of the BPTA-specific C=O stretching band at ~1670 cm−1, while GDY⊕COF demonstrated consumption of acetylene groups via self-/hetero-coupling reactions with significant attenuation of the terminal alkyne vibration at ~2100 cm−1 (Supplementary Fig. 6)31,32. While this confirmed the consumption of acetylene groups via coupling reactions, it cannot spectroscopically distinguish between the self-coupling within the forming GDY network and the hetero-coupling between GDY and COF-anchored alkynyl groups. Therefore, an indirect strategy employing a nitrogen-tagged molecular probe was devised. Specifically, 4-(2-propynyloxy)-aniline, a molecule designed to mimic the reactive alkyne site of the COF linker (BPTA) while providing a nitrogen elemental tag, was reacted with a pre-formed GDY structure. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy measurement of the product revealed nitrogen incorporation, providing evidence for successful hetero-coupling (Supplementary Fig. 7). This element-specific validation confirmed that HEB monomers covalently anchor to the terminal alkynyl sites of COF during the confined synthesis, underpinning the integrated architecture of the GDY⊕COF.

Solid-state 13C NMR spectroscopy of GDY⊕COF exhibited characteristic signals at ~72 ppm and ~25 ppm, corresponding respectively to sp-hybridized carbons in the diyne linkages (-C≡C–C≡C-) and the oxidation-induced defects within the GDY framework (Supplementary Fig. 8)33. The unshifted signals of the BPTA-TAPB-COF confirmed the structural preservation of the host structure during GDY integration33. Raman analysis further affirmed the retention of the GDY sp-hybridized carbon network within GDY⊕COF, evident from conjugated diyne stretching modes at 2100–2200 cm−1 (Supplementary Fig. 9)32. GDY⊕COF displayed a modest degree of enhanced structural ordering, as evidenced by its reduced D and G band intensity ratio (ID/IG = 0.58 vs. 0.62 for parent COF and 0.81 for unconfined GDY), inversely correlating with defect reduction and graphitic perfection34. Layer quantification via the G/2D peak intensity analysis (2D ~ 2680 cm−1) revealed that GDY⊕COF contained fewer GDY layers (~10) compared to unconfined GDY sample (~50 layers)35, evidencing the spatial confinement effect imposed by the COF structure.

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) analyses of BPTA-TAPB-COF and GDY⊕COF confirmed crystalline structural preservation (Fig. 2j, Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). The BPTA-TAPB-COF displayed characteristic peaks at 2.86°, 5.64°, and 7.54°, corresponding to its AA-stacked configuration. Pawley refinement showed a good agreement with experimental pattern (Rwp 3.1% and Rp 2.4%). GDY⊕COF exhibited slightly reduced diffraction peak intensity relative to its parent, consistent with pore-filling effect which attenuates X-ray scattering contrast. Concurrently, all primary diffraction features of the COF host were retained. A slightly enhanced broad signal at 20–25° was observed, consistent with graphitic (π-π) stacking of the confined GDY domains. Together with the persistence of the host COF characteristic signals, this confirmed the integrity of the COF framework during GDY registering growth.

Synchrotron small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) profiles of BPTA-TAPB-COF and GDY⊕COF were nearly identical (Fig. 2k, l), displaying characteristic peaks at Q values of 2.04, 3.54, 4.10, and 5.43 nm−1 (Fig. 2m)36. The ratio of these peak positions (1:√3:√4:√7) was the hallmark of a 2D hexagonal architecture of BPTA-TAPB-COF, corresponding to the (10), (11), (20), and (21) reflections37. No visible difference was observed, suggesting preservation of crystallinity and structural integrity of framework after GDY growth. Wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS), however, revealed a new signal for GDY⊕COF at Q = 1.23 Å−1, corresponding to d-value to ~0.63 nm (Fig. 2n–q). It signified structural modification and pore size reduction consistent with successful GDY incorporation. The signal at ~1.23 Å−1 aligned with the diffraction signature of GDY (~1.27 Å−1), further corroborating the integration of GDY domains within the COF (Fig. 2q).

N2 physisorption analysis revealed that the parent BPTA-TAPB-COF exhibited a Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area of ~1418 m2 g−1 with unimodal mesopore distribution centred at 2.99 nm (Supplementary Fig. 12). GDY⊕COF showed a ~22% reduction in both surface area and pore volume, accompanied by a slight leftward shift in pore size distribution. These collectively demonstrated that GDY⊕COF maintains the structure integrity of the parent COF while achieving chemical integration of GDY through pore-registered growth mediated by host (COF)-guest (GDY) architecture.

Molecular sieving on GDY⊕COF membrane

The gas separation performance of the GDY⊕COF membrane was compared against its parent BPTA-TAPB-COF membrane through single-gas and mixed gas measurements (Supplementary Fig. 13, Supplementary Table 3). Under single gas method, the BPTA-TAPB-COF membrane exhibited high permeance (10−7–10−6 mol m−2 s−1 Pa−1) across all tested small gases (He, H2, CO2, N2, CH4, C2H4, C2H6, C3H6 and C3H8), consistent with low-resistance transport through its mesoporous straight channels (Fig. 3a, b). Ideal H2 selectivity ranged from 1.4 to 3.8, aligning closely with theoretical Knudsen values (black stars, Fig. 3b). H2 containing binary mixture separations further confirmed Knudsen-controlled transport, with H2 permeance remained at ~10−6 mol m−2 s−1 Pa−1, while heavier gases (CO2 to C3H8) exhibited permeance around 10−7 mol m−2 s−1 Pa−1, yielding separation factors of 2.8–4.4 (Fig. 3c).

Conversely, the GDY⊕COF membrane demonstrated clear molecular discrimination. The incorporation of the GDY structure introduced a more restrictive diffusion pathway, leading to a measurable reduction in the permeance of all gases, which indicates the enhanced molecular sieving function from GDY. Single-gas tests revealed permeance at ~10−7 mol m−2 s−1 Pa−1 (an order of magnitude lower than the parent COF membrane) for small molecules with kinetic diameter below 0.30 nm (He and H2), while molecules exceeding 0.33 nm experienced a drastic permeance reduction to ~10−10 mol m−2 s−1 Pa−1 (Fig. 3d, e). This established a clear size cut-off between 0.29 nm and 0.33 nm. Therefore, ideal H2 selectivity significantly surpassed the corresponding Knudsen selectivity (Fig. 3e). Helium similarly exhibited markedly enhanced ideal selectivity toward larger gases, consistent with the emergence of molecular sieving transport within GDY⊕COF membrane (Supplementary Fig. 14). Binary mixture separation further validated robust size sieving performance (Fig. 3f). The GDY⊕COF membrane exhibited H2 permeance (10⁻8 mol m−2 s−1 Pa−1) while suppressing the permeation of larger gases (CO2 to C3H8) to ~10⁻10 mol m−2 s−1 Pa−1, achieving high separation factors of H2 to large gases. The moderate reduction in exclusion versus single-gas measurements aligns with competitive transfer in mixed feeds38.

To verify the critical role of covalent anchoring in the pore-registered growth of GDY, control experiments were conducted using DMPDA-TAPB-COF membranes, a structural analogue lacking terminal alkynyl groups (Supplementary Fig. 15)39. Characterizations confirmed that DMPDA-TAPB-COF exhibited comparable framework properties to BPTA-TAPB-COF (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). Under identical GDY growth conditions applied to GDY⊕COF, the resulting membrane (denoted GDY@DMPDA-TAPB-COF) exhibited pore volume reduction, indicating successful GDY formation within the pores. However, single-gas permeation tests revealed Knudsen-dominated transport in both DMPDA-TAPB-COF membrane (ideal H2 selectivity to second gas 1.5–3.3) and GDY@DMPDA-TAPB-COF membrane (ideal H2 selectivity 1.8–6.1) (Fig. 3g). The performance contrast between GDY⊕COF and GDY@DMPDA-TAPB-COF clarified the distinct roles of each component of pore-registered synthesis. While the intrinsic pores of the GDY structure provide the ultimate sieving dimension, and the aligned meso-channels in COF supply necessary spatial confinement, the pivotal function of the interfacial covalent bond is to register the GDY growth within this template. This covalent anchoring orients the GDY network, ensuring its in-plane pores are aligned for transmembrane transport. In its absence, GDY forms disordered deposits without creating a continuous molecular sieve, resulting in Knudsen diffusion. Thus, the transition to precise sieving is a synergistic effect of the GDY inherent pore structure, the COF confining architecture, and, decisively, the covalent bond that integrates and orients them.

The GDY⊕COF membrane demonstrated high gas separation performance, achieving an optimal balance between hydrogen permeability and selectivity that surpasses microporous membranes such as metal-organic frameworks, zeolites, and porous organic polymers (Fig. 3h, Supplementary Table 4)40–42. Its performance exceeds the empirical upper bounds for polymeric membranes and microporous membranes. GDY⊕COF membrane further exhibited enhanced performance for H2/N2, H2/CH4, and H2/C2H4 separations compared to polymer membranes and graphitic membranes (Supplementary Figs. 18–20, Supplementary Tables 5–7).

The operational stability of the GDY⊕COF membrane was evaluated through pressure-dependent, humidity-resilient, temperature effect, and prolonged testing, using gas mixtures of H2-N2 and H2-CO2. Under feeding moderate pressure of equimolar H2-N2, the permeance of gases and H2/N2 separation factors remained stable with acceptable fluctuations (Supplementary Fig. 21). This reflects the structural integrity of the few-layered GDY structure, which may derive from its covalent diyne backbone and anchoring to the COF host. Similar to the supported membranes, on the other hand, the overall mechanical strength of the GDY⊕COF membrane derives from the ceramic support (Supplementary Fig. 22). Humidity tests revealed that the intrinsic hydrophobicity of the incorporated GDY enhanced the humidity resistance of GDY⊕COF membrane. This was evidenced by a supressed decline in H2 flux under both humid H₂ and humid H2-CO2 feeds (Supplementary Figs. 23–25). It presents a critical advantage over humidity-sensitive materials like GO-based membranes and ionic frameworks4,43,44. The membrane was also evaluated with an H2-N2 mixture feed under elevated temperatures up to 80 °C. A reversible increase in gas permeance and a modest decrease in H2/N2 separation factor were observed, consistent with thermally activated transport (Supplementary Fig. 26). The performance mostly recovered after cooling, along with thermogravimetric analysis confirming its stability (Supplementary Fig. 27). Continuous operation under H2-N2 mixture feed for 120 h exhibited sustained stability, with the H2 permeance and H2/N2 separation factors remaining robust overall, albeit showing a modest decline over time (Fig. 3i). Post-test characterization confirmed preserved structural integrity and morphology of membrane (Supplementary Fig. 28).

2D carbon materials with atomic-scale thickness, exemplified by graphene, have emerged as promising membrane separation platforms due to their ultrathin transport pathways45. However, current graphene-based membranes predominantly rely on post-synthetic pore generation within impermeable basal planes through ion bombardment and oxidative etching, which suffer from inconsistent pore uniformity, sub-nano dimensional inaccuracy, and limited scalability46,47. Additionally, laminated graphene-based membranes exhibit weak interlayer interactions and tortuous diffusion pathways, fundamentally constraining size-sieving precision48. In contrast, GDY, a structural analogue of graphitic materials, intrinsically integrates 2D framework with ordered in-plane nanopores. Critically, the bottom-up synthesis of GDY via solution-phase monomer coupling under mild conditions enables direct membrane formation, significantly advantaged over multi-step fabrication requiring harsh-condition-derived prefabricated components followed by complex assembly for graphitic membranes. This integration of GDY nanomembranes within COF matrix leverages this inherent pore regularity to achieve Å-level gas discrimination, validating its precise molecular sieving capabilities.

Separation behaviour of gases on GDY nanostructure

The molecular basis for selective gas permeation through the GDY⊕COF membrane was elucidated through combined density functional theory (DFT) calculations and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. DFT calculations revealed critical differences in equilibrium adsorption distances and adsorption energetics for different molecular species (He, H2, CO2, N2, CH4) on a single-layer GDY surface (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 29). Specifically, H2 and He exhibited weak physisorption, with equilibrium adsorption distances of 1.76 Å and 1.62 Å, respectively, and corresponding adsorption energies of 0.97 and 1.82 kcal mol−1 (Fig. 4b). In contrast, larger molecules such as N2, CO2, and CH4 demonstrated stronger adsorption energies of 2.55, 2.76, and 3.86 kcal mol−1. This trend suggests that interactions beyond simple van der Waals forces, such as those arising from orbital overlap with the sp²-sp hybridized carbon framework of GDY, contribute to the binding strength of these larger gases.

To probe the interaction nature, charge density difference and Bader charge analyses were conducted (Supplementary Fig. 30). This confirmed interfacial charge redistribution, evidence of an electronic polarization effect, with calculated net charge transfers (e.g., 0.003 e for H2, 0.011 e for N2). This minor magnitude indicated that polarization plays a secondary role. Although experimental diffraction indicated an effective GDY pore aperture of ~0.34 nm, which aligns with the observed size exclusion, computational modelling also suggested the geometric in-plane pores of GDY can accommodate a larger size of ~0.5 nm4. The sieving mechanism is best understood by considering dynamic steric interactions, such as van der Waals (vdW) repulsion, rather than static pore geometry alone. Further analysis of the vdW volume overlap between gas molecules and the GDY aperture revealed that small molecules (H2, He) show minimal vdW overlap and larger gases (CO2, N2, CH4) exhibit significant overlap. This direct spatial correlation establishes steric size exclusion, governed by the GDY pore, as the principal contribution for molecular discrimination.

The molecular transport dynamics were analysed using potential energy profiles along trajectories perpendicular to the GDY plane and through its in-plane pores (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 31). The results revealed a consistent trend where the energy barrier for all gas molecules increased as they approach the GDY plane, reaching a maximum within the confined space of the GDY in-plane pore. Among these gases, H2 and He encountered the lowest and similar energy barriers. In contrast, CH4 experienced the highest barrier, while CO2 and N2 were intermediate. Quantitative analysis of the normalized potential energy profiles, the energy barriers for H2 and He permeation were 1.04 and 2.51 kcal mol−1, respectively, less than half the hydrogen binding energy, indicating near-barrierless diffusion through the triangular GDY pores49. In comparison, CO₂ and N2, despite similar adsorption strengths (ΔE ~ 0.2 kcal·mol−1), exhibited markedly different permeation barriers (CO2 6.09 kcal mol−1 and N2 11.46 kcal mol−1) (Fig. 4d). This contrast stems from differential steric constraints and repulsion effects within the pore architecture. CH4 experienced a prohibitive energy barrier of 37.15 kcal mol−1, effectively preventing its permeation. This mechanistic hierarchy aligns with experimental gas flux measurements, confirming that molecular discrimination arises from synergistic size exclusion and electronic structure complementarity rather than kinetic diameter alone.

MD simulations of gas mixtures through mono-, bi- and tri-layer GDY models, constructed to represent AA, AB, and ABC stacking configurations, respectively, demonstrated layer-dependent molecular transfer behaviour for H2/CO2, H2/N2, and H2/CH4 systems (Fig. 4e, f, Supplementary Figs. 32–34). Monolayer GDY afforded high H2 permeation rates while strongly hindering CO2 transport and completely blocking N2 and CH4, consistent with the energy barriers derived from potential energy surface analysis (Fig. 4g). In bilayers, H2 permeation equilibrium was delayed due to interlayer slip effects narrowed the pore dimensions (Fig. 4h). Furthermore, tri-layer completely blocked permeation of all tested gases, highlighting the critical role of interlayer registry on molecular transport (Fig. 4i). A similar layer-dependent trend was observed for helium-based mixtures, where He flux remained significant in the monolayer, decreased in the bilayer, and was fully suppressed in the tri-layer system (Supplementary Figs. 35–37). Ideal gas permeability, derived from linear fitting of the initial permeation transients, further highlighted the role of layer number (Fig. 4j). Monolayer GDY exhibited high permeability for H2 and He (~10−1 mol m−2 s−1 Pa−1), which decreased by an order of magnitude in the bilayer model. In contrast, permeation of larger gases was effectively suppressed across all GDY models regardless of stacking order assumptions (AA, AB, or ABC), with CO2 displaying minimal permeability exclusively in monolayer case. The exponential decline in permeability with increasing layer count originates from cumulative π-orbital interactions that distort pore geometry and amplify molecular discrimination through synergistic size and electronic exclusion. These simulations indicate that molecular sieving is mainly facilitated by monolayer GDY and modulated by interlayer stacking in few-layer systems.

Discussion

In summary, this study established a pore-registered synthesis strategy that effectively resolves the persistent challenge of harnessing GDY intrinsic in-plane pores for molecular separation. By confining GDY growth within pre-engineered COF mesopores, we obtained GDY nanomembrane structure with accessible in-plane pores. The resulting GDY⊕COF membranes exhibited exceptional permselective performance for small molecules such as hydrogen and helium, providing the experimental confirmation of GDY in-plane-pore molecular sieving capability. Theoretical calculations further revealed that the high separation performance arises from size exclusion with electronic polarization effect. As GDY represents a prototypical member of the graphyne and graphyne-like material family with tuneable pore sizes and functions, and stability profiles, this methodology advanced the rational design of graphyne-based membranes for separation applications.

Methods

Membrane preparation

Porous α-Al2O3 tubes were firstly underwent amino-functionalization via (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES) grafting in toluene solution (0.7 mM) at 110 °C for 2 h under argon. BPTA-TAPB-COF layer was synthesized on the functionalized inner surface of support via solvothermal reaction, combining TAPB (28 mg) and BPTA (28.5 mg) in a 1:1 v/v butanol/o-dichlorobenzene mixture with 6 M acetic acid catalyst at 120 °C for 24 h. The resulting membranes were washed with THF and vacuum dried. The pore-registered GDY growth within COF membrane involved processes of solution-infiltration and confined coupling. Prior to synthesis, the tubular COF membrane was sealed with a stopper on one end, and its outer surface was wrapped with polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) tape. A pyridine solution of copper (II) acetate catalyst (5 mg mL−1) was introduced into the inner lumen of the membrane tube for 12 h. This infusion step was repeated three times, with vacuum drying at room temperature applied after each cycle. HEB monomer was then introduced into the catalyst-loaded membrane. A THF solution of HEB monomer (5 mg mL−1) was held within the membrane tube lumen for 1 h. During this step, both ends of the membrane were sealed, and the outer surface remained PTFE-wrapped and kept in the dark. The membrane was subsequently vacuum-dried in the dark at room temperature for 3 h. This monomer infiltration and drying procedure was performed three times. Finally, the membrane lumen was filled with a pyridine solution of HEB (5 mg mL−1). The Glaser-Hay coupling reaction was proceeded at 80 °C for 24 h. The resulting GDY⊕COF membrane was sequentially washed with THF/ethanol and dried under vacuum.

Gas permeation

Gas permeation measurements employed membrane modules assembled with GDY⊕COF membranes sealed by O-rings at both glazed ends of support. Dehydrated feed gas (single or binary mixture) was introduced into the lumen side of the tubular membrane and argon sweep gas was introduced to the permeate side of membrane. Permeate side flux was quantified after system stabilization using soap-bubble flowmeters, while permeate composition was analysed by gas chromatography. Additional method details were provided in Supplementary Information.
