Sum of consecutive powers as a perfect power
Angelos Koutsianas, Nikos Tzanakis

Abstract
. In this paper we study the equation x k + ( x + 1 ) k = y n , n ≥ 3 , x^{k}+(x+1)^{k}=y^{n},\quad n\geq 3, when k ≡ 2 ( mod 4 ) k\equiv 2\pmod{4} . We prove that the only solutions are for x = 0 , − 1 x=0,-1 when 6 ≤ k ≤ 100 6\leq k\leq 100 or for a k k with odd prime factors congruent to 3 ( mod 4 ) 3\pmod{4} . We use linear forms in logarithms, the modular method and the resolution of Thue equations.

1.
Introduction

A fundamental question in Diophantine equations is the determination of the sums of consecutive perfect powers that are a perfect power. For example, Euler
[
14
, art. 249]
showed the very beautiful relation

6^{3}=3^{3}+4^{3}+5^{3}.

In the last few decades, many mathematicians have tried to get similar to Euler’s above relation, with a variety of different techniques, by studying the equation

(1.1)

x^{k}+(x+1)^{k}+\cdots+(x+d-1)^{k}=y^{n},\quad n\geq 2,

for different values of
k
and
d
.

When
k=2
the cases
2\leq d\leq 10
have been solved by Cohn, Patel and Zhang
[
9
,
28
,
20
]
. The case
k=3
and
2\leq d\leq 50
has been resolved by many
[
26
,
8
,
24
,
28
,
4
]
. For
k\geq 4
less are known. The case
k=4
and
d=2
is due to the deep results of Ellenberg about the image of the Galois representations of
\mathbb{Q}
-curves over imaginary quadratic fields and the resolution of the generalized Fermat equation of signature
(2,4,n)

[
13
,
2
]
. For
k=4,5,6
and
d=3
we have the results of Zhang and Bennett-Patel-Siksek
[
28
,
3
]
. Finally, for higher values of
k
, the asymptotic theorem of Patel and Siksek states that equation (
1.1
) has no solutions for almost all
d\geq 2
when
k
is even
[
19
]
. We recommend the survey
[
10
]
for a detailed exposition of the known results about (
1.1
) and its generalizations.

Apart from the cases
k=2,3,4
there are no results for
d=2
. This is not a coincidence and has a very simple but deep explanation. For
d=2
the equation (
1.1
) is a special case of the generalized Fermat equation of signature
(r,r,p)
. From Darmon’s program
[
12
]
we know the existence of the obstructions
(0,1,1)
and
(1,0,1)
that correspond to non-singular Frey hyperelliptic curves with complex multiplication. We could isolate the two obstructions if there was an analogous to Darmon-Merel results
[
11
]
for curves of genus greater than
1
but this is far beyond the current technology. The same obstructions, and their difficulties, appear in our case for
x=0,-1
and
|y|=1
.

In this paper, we consider the case
d=2
and study the equation

(1.2)

x^{k}+(x+1)^{k}=y^{n},\quad n\geq 3,

for
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first attempt to solve (
1.1
) for
d=2
and big values of
k
. Even though the mentioned obstructions still exist, we succeed in overcoming all of the above difficulties by reducing the resolution of (
1.2
) to the resolution of a finite number of Lebesgue-Nagell equations that we can solve. The main result of the paper is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
. If
6\leq k\leq 100
, then the only
solutions of the Diophantine equation

(1.3)

x^{k}+(x+1)^{k}=y^{n},\quad n\geq 3,

result when
x\in\{-1,0\}
.

Note that, if
(x,y,n)
is a solution with
x\leq-2
, then

(|x|-1,y,n)
is also a solution, therefore we may assume that

x\geq 1
and, consequently,
y>1
and odd.
By changing the variable
x
(and leaving unaltered
y
) we can express the consecutive positive integers

x,x+1
as
(x-1)/2,(x+1)/2
, where
x
is an odd integer with
x>1
. Therefore, our equation becomes

(1.4)

\frac{(x-1)^{k}+(x+1)^{k}}{2}=2^{k-1}y^{n},\quad x,y\geq 3\text{ odd},\quad n\geq 3,

which is symmetric on the left-hand side with respect to
x
around
0
.

For the rest of the paper, we solve (
1.4
) instead of (
1.2
) for
n=4
or
n
is an odd prime. In the study of (
1.4
) we use linear forms in logarithms to get an upper bound
n_{0}
of
n
, the modular method to prove that there are no other solutions of (
1.4
) apart from those with
y=1
and the resolution of Thue equations when
n\leq 7
.

It is important to mention that the case
k=2
of (
1.4
) has been studied in
[
9
,
1
]
and we have the following result which we also use for
k\geq 6
.

Theorem 1.2
.

The only solutions of the Diophantine equation

(1.5)

x^{2}+1=2y^{n},\quad n\geq 3,

are
(|x|,y,n)=(1,1,n),~(239,13,4)
.

Theorem
1.2
and
Lemma
4.5
imply the
theorem below, whose proof is given immediately after the proof of
Lemma
4.5
.

Theorem 1.3
.

If
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
with all its odd prime factors
congruent to
3\pmod{4}
, then the solutions of the Diophantine
equation

(1.6)

x^{k}+(x+1)^{k}=y^{n},\quad n\geq 3,

are
(x,y,n,k)=(0,1,n,k),~(119,13,4,2),~(-120,13,4,2)
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
3
we recall the basic terminology and results about the modular method. In Section
4
we prove the key Lemma
4.2
which shows that (
1.4
) is reduced to a finite number of systems of two equations with the first one being a Lebesgue-Nagell equation. At the end of this section we also prove Theorem
1.3
. In Section
5
we show that, for fixed
n
, the resolution of (
1.4
) is reduced to the resolution of a finite number of Thue equations, which we solve for
n\leq 7
. In Section
6
we apply the modular method and prove that there are no solutions for
n\leq n_{0}
where
n_{0}
is given in Table
2
. Finally, in Section
7
we use linear forms in logarithms to prove that there are no solutions with
y>1
when
n>n_{0}
.

The computations were carried out on a machine running Ubuntu 24.04.4 with
an Intel Core i5-10400,
12
core CPUs at 2.90GHz and 24 GB RAM. For the
parallel computations we used the
10
cpus. The code and details how to
rerun the computations can be found in

https://github.com/akoutsianas/SumOfConsecutivePowers
.

For the remainder of the paper, we implicitly assume that

k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
.

3.
Modular Method

The proof of Theorem
1.1
for
k\geq 3
is based, to a
large extent, on the modular method. We use Frey-Hellegouarch curves, modularity, Galois representations and level lowering
[
21
,
27
,
25
,
6
,
5
,
7
]
. In this section, we briefly recall terminology and results about the modular method that we use in Section
6
.

Suppose
f
is a cuspidal newform of weight
2
, trivial character and level
N_{f}
with
q
-expansion

f=q+\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}a_{i}(f)q^{i}.

We denote by
K_{f}
the
eigenvalue field
of
f
and say that
f
is
irrational
if
[K_{f}:\mathbb{Q}]>1
and
rational
otherwise. For a rational prime
n
we denote by
\mathfrak{n}
a prime ideal of
K_{f}
above
n
. We associate to
f
a continuous, semisimple Galois representation

\rho_{f,\mathfrak{n}}:~\Gal(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow\GL_{2}(\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{n}),

that is unramified at all primes
\ell\nmid nN_{f}
and

\Tr(\rho_{f,\mathfrak{n}}(\Frob_{\ell}))\equiv a_{\ell}(f)\pmod{\mathfrak{n}}
,
where
\Frob_{\ell}
is a Frobenius element at
\ell
.
If
f
is rational we use the notation
\rho_{f,n}
and denote
E_{f}
an elliptic
curve over
\mathbb{Q}
corresponding to
f
due to modularity.

Suppose
E
is an elliptic curve over
\mathbb{Q}
with conductor
N_{E}
. For a prime

\ell\nmid N_{E}
, let
a_{\ell}(E)=\ell+1-\#\tilde{E}(\mathbb{F}_{\ell}):=a_{\ell}(\tilde{E})
, where
\tilde{E}
is the reduction of
E
at
\ell
. We also denote by
\rho_{E,n}
the Galois representation of
\Gal(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})
on the
n
-th torsion subgroup of
E
.

Proposition 3.1
.

With the above notation we assume:
\rho_{E,n}\simeq\rho_{f,\mathfrak{n}}
for a prime ideal
\mathfrak{n}\mid n
of
K_{f}
and
l
is a prime. Then, the following hold:

(i)

if
\ell\nmid nN_{E}N_{f}
, then
a_{\ell}(f)\equiv a_{\ell}(E)\pmod{\mathfrak{n}}
,

(ii)

if
\ell\nmid nN_{f}
and
\ell\|N_{E}
, then
\ell+1\equiv\pm a_{\ell}(f)\pmod{\mathfrak{n}}
.

If
f
, as above, is rational, then we have

(i)

if
\ell\nmid N_{E}N_{E_{f}}
, then
a_{\ell}(E_{f})\equiv a_{\ell}(E)\pmod{n}
,

(ii)

if
\ell\nmid N_{f}
and
\ell\|N_{E_{f}}
, then
\ell+1\equiv\pm a_{\ell}(f)\pmod{n}
,

where
E_{f}
is an associate to
f
elliptic curve over
\mathbb{Q}
due to modularity and
N_{E_{f}}=N_{f}
is the conductor of
E_{f}
.

The above proposition is standard (see
[
5
, Proposition 4.3]
or
[
22
, p. 203]
; in the case that
f
is rational, see
[
7
, Lemma 6.4]
or
[
15
, Proposition 3]
), and we apply it to bound
n
, when
a_{\ell}(f)-a_{\ell}(E)\neq 0
, using a
range of different primes
\ell
.

Remark 3.2
.

We recall that, when
f
is irrational, there are infinitely many primes
\ell

such that
a_{\ell}(f)\not\in\mathbb{Q}
, which implies that there are infinitely
many primes
\ell
for which
a_{\ell}(f)-a_{\ell}(E)\neq 0
. Hence, for
f

irrational Proposition
3.1
always succeeds.

4.
Preliminaries

Define
g_{k}(t)
by

(4.1)

g_{k}(t):=\frac{(t-1)^{k}+(t+1)^{k}}{2}.

The following is a key lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

We have
g_{k}(t)\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
and

g_{k}(t)=(t^{2}+1)f_{k}(t^{2}),

where
f_{k}(t)\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
and
\deg(f)=\frac{k}{2}-1
. In particular,

(4.2)

f_{k}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{(k/2-1)/2}2^{k/2-(2i+1)}\binom{k/2}{2i+1}(t+1)^{2i}t^{(k/2-(2i+1))/2}.

Proof.

The fact that
g_{k}(t)\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
is an immediate consequence of the definition of
g_{k}(t)
in (
4.1
). Furthermore, an elementary computation shows that
g_{k}(\sqrt{-1})=0
.

Since
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, we express
g_{k}(t)
as follows.

\displaystyle g_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left((t^{2}+1-2t)^{k/2}+(t^{2}+1+2t)^{k/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{k/2}\binom{k/2}{i}(t^{2}+1)^{i}2^{k/2-i}(1+(-1)^{k/2-i})t^{k/2-i}.

For even
i
we have
1+(-1)^{k/2-i}=0
, therefore,

\displaystyle g_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{(k/2-1)/2}\binom{k/2}{2i+1}(t^{2}+1)^{2i+1}2^{k/2-i}2t^{k/2-(2i+1)}

\displaystyle=(t^{2}+1)\sum_{i=0}^{(k/2-1)/2}2^{k/2-(2i+1)}\binom{k/2}{2i+1}(t^{2}+1)^{2i}t^{k/2-(2i+1)}.

Because
k/2
is odd we have
(k/2-(2i+1))/2\in\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

The following lemma will be used in Sections
6
and

7
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
k\geq 6
and suppose
(x,y,n)
is a solution of (
1.4
) with
n>2v_{p}(k)
for every prime
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
dividing
k
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of primes which divide
k/2
and are congruent to
1\pmod{4}
, if any such primes exist; otherwise set
\mathcal{P}=\emptyset
. Define
\mathcal{D}
to be the set consisting of
1
and the positive divisors of
k/2
whose prime divisors belong to
\mathcal{P}
(if
\mathcal{P}\neq\emptyset
). Then

(4.3)

\displaystyle x^{2}+1

\displaystyle=2\frac{d_{2}}{d_{1}}y_{1}^{n},

(4.4)

\displaystyle f_{k}(x^{2})

\displaystyle=2^{k-2}\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}}y_{2}^{n},

where
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathcal{D}
are relatively prime,
y_{1},y_{2}
are relatively prime positive odd integers and
y_{1}y_{2}=y
.

Proof.

By (
1.4
) and Lemma
4.1
we have
(x^{2}+1)f_{k}(x^{2})=2^{k-1}y^{n}
. Since
x
is odd,
v_{2}(x^{2}+1)=1
, therefore
(x^{2}+1,f_{k}(x^{2})):=2d
with
d
odd. If
d>1
, every prime
p
dividing
d
also divides
x^{2}+1
, therefore
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
. Also,

(4.5)

\frac{x^{2}+1}{2d}\cdot\frac{f_{k}(x^{2})}{2d}=2^{k-3}\frac{y^{n}}{d^{2}}.

From (
4.2
) with
t=x^{2}
we get
2d\mid 2^{k/2-1}(k/2)
, hence
d\mid(k/2)
; we have already seen that, if
d>1
, every prime divisor of
d
is
\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, therefore
d\in\mathcal{D}
. Moreover,
v_{p}(d)\leq v_{p}(k)<n/2
for every odd prime
p
, a fact that will be used below.

If
d=1
, then we put
d_{1}=d_{2}=1
and (
4.5
)
immediately implies the relations (
4.3
) and
(
4.4
) with appropriate
y_{1},y_{2}
as in
the announcement of the lemma.

We consider now the case
d>1
.
From the last displayed equation it is clear that
y^{n}/d^{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{0}\neq\emptyset
be the set of primes dividing
d

(
\mathcal{P}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
), and write

d=\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}p^{e_{p}}
for its canonical factorization.
Then
y=y^{\prime}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}p^{m_{p}}
where
m_{p}:=v_{p}(y)>0
and

(y^{\prime},\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}p)=1
. Consequently, by (
4.5
),

(4.6)

\frac{x^{2}+1}{2d}\cdot\frac{f_{k}(x^{2})}{2d}=2^{k-3}y^{\prime n}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}p^{nm_{p}-2e_{p}}=2^{k-3}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}p^{-2e_{p}}\cdot\left(y^{\prime}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}p^{m_{p}}\right)^{n},

Note that, for every
p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
, the
p
-exponent on the right-hand side is positive because
m_{p}>0
and
n>2v_{p}(d)=2e_{p}
. On the other hand, the two factors on the left-hand side are relatively prime, therefore, for every
p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
, precisely one of them has positive
p
-exponent. It follows that we can set
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\mathcal{P}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{P}_{2}
, where
\mathcal{P}_{1}
is the set of those primes
p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
with non-zero
p
-exponent in
(x^{2}+1)/(2d)
and, analogously,
\mathcal{P}_{2}
is the set of the primes
p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
whose
p
-exponent in

is non-zero. Note that
\mathcal{P}_{1}
or
\mathcal{P}_{2}
(but not both) may be empty. Now, from (
4.6
) it follows that

(4.7)

\frac{x^{2}+1}{2d}=\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}p^{-2e_{p}}\cdot\left(y^{\prime}_{1}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}p^{m_{p}}\right)^{n},\quad\frac{f_{k}(x^{2})}{2d}=2^{k-3}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}}p^{-2e_{p}}\cdot\left(y^{\prime}_{2}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}}p^{m_{p}}\right)^{n}

with
(y^{\prime}_{1},y^{\prime}_{2})=1
,
y^{\prime}_{1}
odd and
y^{\prime}_{1}y^{\prime}_{2}=y^{\prime}
.
Putting

y_{i}=y^{\prime}_{i}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{i}}p^{m_{p}},\quad i=1,2,

we see that
y_{1}
is odd and

y_{1}y_{2}=y^{\prime}_{1}y^{\prime}_{2}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}p^{m_{p}}=y^{\prime}\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}p^{m_{p}}=y
.
Then, by (

),

x^{2}+1=2d\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}p^{-2e_{p}}y_{1}^{n},\quad f_{k}(x^{2})=2^{k-3}d\prod_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}}p^{-2e_{p}}y_{1}^{n},

and, since

, it is
straightforward to see that

We complete the proof by setting

∎

Lemma 4.5

If in the assumptions and notations of Lemma

9.
Conclusion

The methods and analysis we use to get Theorem
1.1
is, in principle, applicable to any given value
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
with
k>100
. Therefore, it is quite natural to move our attention to the cases
k\not\equiv 2\pmod{4}
. The case
k\equiv 0\pmod{4}
is a consequence of the work of Bennett-Ellenberg-Ng about the generalized Fermat equation of signature
(2,4,n)
that we have already mention
[
13
,
2
]
. Therefore, the remaining case is when
k
is odd.

For
k
odd the situation is quite different. The key Lemma
4.2
no longer holds; however,
equation (
1.4
) can be written as

(9.1)

xf_{k}(x)=2^{k-1}y^{n},

where
f_{k}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. This implies that
x
is almost a perfect
n
-th power which is a very restricted property for
x
. Even the case
k=5
is a very challenging problem.
