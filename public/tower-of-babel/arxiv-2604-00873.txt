Transport-Geometric Formulation of Peak Statistics: Curvature-Conditioned Point Processes and Response Hierarchy Preprint: APS/123-QED
Tsutomu T. Takeuchi

Abstract
We develop a geometric formulation of peak statistics in cosmological density fields based on optimal transport and entropy. In this framework, the density field is treated as a probability measure, and its local structure is characterized by the Hessian of the log-density, which arises as the local response of an entropy functional in Wasserstein space. Peaks are thereby defined as positive-curvature stationary points, and their number density is expressed as a curvature-conditioned point process. In the linear Gaussian limit, the joint distribution of local variables closes in terms of a finite set of spectral moments, recovering the standard theory of peak statistics, known as BBKS. This clarifies that BBKS corresponds to a solvable limit of a more general structure combining probability distributions, curvature constraints, and geometric measure. The framework extends naturally beyond Gaussianity and linearity. Deviations from Gaussianity are incorporated as deformations of the joint distribution of curvature variables, while nonlinear structures are described through the curvature of the log-density. We further derive the two- and three-point peak statistics as curvature-conditioned n n -point measures, and show that the full hierarchy of peak statistics can be organized as response functions to long-wavelength background modes. In this formulation, the conventional peak bias appears as the lowest-order response coefficient, with higher-order correlations arising as its natural extensions. This work embeds peak theory into a unified geometric framework and provides a systematic basis for incorporating nonlinearity, non-Gaussianity, and higher-order statistics, with direct relevance for observational applications.



I
Introduction

The large-scale structure of the Universe is characterized by the growth of primordial density fluctuations and their statistical properties.
Among these, local maxima of the density field, namely peaks, are considered to play an important role as seeds of dark matter halos and galaxy formation, and their statistical properties have been studied for many years.
In this context,
2
(hereafter BBKS) is widely known as a classical theory that systematically provides peak statistics in a Gaussian random field.
BBKS derived, in closed form, the peak number density, the curvature distribution, and the peak bias based on the gradient and the Hessian of the density fluctuation field, and has since served as a fundamental reference point for peak theory
(
2
)
.
Furthermore, the relation between peak bias and halo formation has been extended in connection with the ideas of peak-background split and excursion set, and has become one of the foundations of structure formation theory
(
20
;
19
;
28
;
7
, e.g.,)
.

On the other hand, in recent theories of large-scale structure, the description of bias itself has undergone significant development.
In particular, integrated perturbation theory (iPT) based on Lagrangian perturbation theory provides a framework that treats arbitrary bias models in a unified manner
(
15
;
16
)
, and more recent studies have clarified that bias can be formulated not only as a function of density but also as a function of local tensor fields including its gradients and second derivatives
(
17
;
18
)
.
That is, the distribution of galaxies and halos can be expressed as a semi-local bias of the form

\displaystyle F=F(\delta,\partial_{i}\delta,\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\Phi,\dots)

(1)

and it has been shown that tidal tensors and shape information are essentially involved in the statistics.
Such developments indicate that cosmological statistics are being extended from scalar quantities to tensor quantities.

Nevertheless, even through developments since BBKS, the theoretical starting point of peak statistics still strongly depends on the assumptions of Gaussianity, linear theory, and smoothing at a fixed scale.
Under these assumptions, the joint distribution of local variables closes, and calculations can be carried out in a clear form.
On the other hand, it is not easy to treat nonlinear structures, non-Gaussian initial conditions, scale dependence, and
n
-point statistics in a unified manner.
Therefore, it is required to reconsider peak statistics at a level prior to these assumptions and to clarify which structures are essential and which parts are specific to the Gaussian limit.

This line of thought is different from simply adding correction terms to the BBKS theory.
Rather, what is required is to reposition peaks not as a given collection of density maxima, but within a more general geometric or measure-theoretic structure.
In fact, cosmological density fields are given observationally as a finite number of galaxies or halos, and their statistical description intrinsically involves the problem of finite samples and finite observational windows
(
31
)
.
Moreover, from a theoretical point of view, structure formation is not merely a change in field values, but has an aspect that should be understood as the spatial rearrangement of mass distributions.
This suggests that peak statistics can be regarded not only as an extremum problem in functional analysis, but also as a problem of measure deformation and local geometry.

Furthermore, it is important that such a reformulation is carried out on the same space of local variables as recent tensor bias theories.
That is, the set of local quantities
(\delta,\partial_{i}\delta,\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\delta)
serves as the fundamental variables characterizing bias in iPT and its extensions
(
18
)
.
In these approaches, these quantities are typically treated as arguments of a bias function, whereas in the present work they are regarded as random variables, with a measure structure introduced on this space.
This distinction leads to complementary descriptions on the same local tensor space, namely “bias as a function” and “peak statistics as a measure.”

From this perspective, optimal transport theory naturally emerges.
Optimal transport is a framework that measures the difference between two distributions not as a difference of function values but as a cost of mass rearrangement
(
21
;
10
;
34
;
35
)
, and provides a natural language to describe cosmological structure formation as a deformation of mass distributions.
In particular, once one enters the space of probability measures, the deformation of distributions is described geometrically by the Wasserstein distance, and the behavior of entropy functionals plays an important role
(
22
;
11
;
29
;
30
)
.
Although this structure has not been emphasized in conventional peak theory, it provides a powerful clue to embed the statistics of local extrema into a more general geometry.

Furthermore, in recent cosmology, the viewpoint of understanding structure formation itself as transport or deformation is becoming increasingly important.
For example, Lagrangian mass transport, the geometry of displacement fields, and statistical measures based on distances between distributions characterize structure from perspectives different from conventional correlation functions and power spectra
(
4
;
33
)
.
Within this trend, reconsidering peak statistics in connection with optimal transport is not merely a formal generalization, but has significance as an attempt to reorganize the foundations of structure formation theory.
To our knowledge, however, the classical theory of peak statistics, known as BBKS, has not been reformulated directly within the framework of optimal transport, Wasserstein geometry, or entropy-based curvature.
Existing developments of peak theory have primarily focused on extensions within the random-field and bias-based frameworks, while applications of optimal transport in cosmology have been directed mainly toward reconstruction and mass transport problems.
In this sense, a direct connection between peak statistics and transport geometry has remained largely unexplored.

The starting point of this study is to regard the density field as a probability measure and to characterize its local structure from the standpoint of entropy geometry.
From this viewpoint, the Hessian used to describe local extrema is reinterpreted not as a merely analytical auxiliary quantity, but as a local response to deformations of the distribution.
As a result, questions such as what a peak is, why the determinant of the Hessian appears, and why BBKS-type formulas close in the Gaussian limit are understood not as individual computational techniques but as consequences of a more general curvature statistics.
The aim of this paper is to make this perspective explicit and to reformulate peak statistics as a curvature-conditioned point process.

This reformulation has three advantages.
First, the position of the BBKS theory obtained in the Gaussian limit becomes clear.
That is, Gaussianity does not provide the essential structure, but is understood as a solvable condition that allows the general theory constructed in this paper to be evaluated explicitly.
Second, when nonlinearity and non-Gaussianity are introduced, it becomes possible to systematically identify what is preserved and what is deformed.
Third, it opens a path to discuss not only the peak number density, but also the two-point and three-point correlations of peaks, and even the hierarchy of responses to long-wavelength background fields, within a unified framework.
Therefore, this study does not aim to replace the BBKS theory, but to reposition it within a more general geometric theory.
In other words, the essential point of this study is to introduce a measure structure on local tensor variables rather than a bias function.

The organization of this paper is as follows.
In Sec.
II
, we reformulate the density field as a measure and introduce the curvature tensor from optimal transport and entropy geometry.
In Sec.
III
, we explicitly evaluate the statistics of the curvature-conditioned point process in the Gaussian limit and show how BBKS-type results emerge.
In Sec.
IV
, we organize the internal structure of peak statistics and clarify its geometric meaning.
In Sec.
V
, we discuss extensions to nonlinearity, non-Gaussianity, scale dependence, and finite-dimensional curvature while preserving this structure.
In Sec.
VI
, we formulate the two-point and three-point correlations of peaks as
n
-point statistics of the curvature-conditioned point process and interpret them in a unified manner as a hierarchy of response functions.
Finally, in Sec.
VII
, we summarize the results and discuss future theoretical and observational prospects.

II
Curvature Geometry of Cosmological Density Fields

II.1
Measure-Theoretic Description of the Density Field

The cosmological density field is usually described as a function
\rho(\bm{x})
on space
(
25
;
24
;
32
, e.g.,)
, but in this work we reinterpret it as a probability measure
(
33
, cf.)
.
In what follows, the spatial position vector is written as
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, while in measure-theoretic notation we use
{\rm d}^{3}x
for brevity.
That is, on a finite domain
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we define

\displaystyle\mu({\rm d}^{3}x)=\rho(\bm{x}){\rm d}^{3}x.

(2)

If necessary, by imposing the normalization

\displaystyle\int_{V}\rho(\bm{x}){\rm d}^{3}x=1,

(3)

\mu
can be regarded as a probability measure.

The significance of this reformulation lies in viewing structure formation not as a “change in field values,” but as a “rearrangement of mass distributions.”
In fact, galaxy distributions and dark matter distributions are observed as a finite number of particles or halos, and are therefore essentially given as point processes or finite measures
(
31
)
.
Thus, in order to describe the comparison and evolution of cosmological structures, it is natural to deal with differences between measures rather than differences of function values.
From this perspective, structure formation is described as the time evolution of a measure

\displaystyle\mu_{t}({\rm d}^{3}x)=\rho(t,\bm{x}){\rm d}^{3}x,

(4)

and the problem reduces to how to define a distance between two measures.

II.2
Optimal Transport and Distance Between Distributions

The simplest distance between measures is the
L^{p}
distance based on pointwise differences, but this does not reflect the viewpoint of mass rearrangement.
For example, if a mass distribution is spatially translated, the
L^{p}
distance takes a large value, whereas physically this is merely a translation and the structure is essentially identical.

A natural framework that resolves this problem is optimal transport.
In optimal transport, the distance is defined as the minimal cost required to move one distribution into another.
In Monge’s formulation, one considers a map
T
satisfying

\displaystyle\nu=T_{\#}\mu,

(5)

and minimizes

\displaystyle\int|\bm{x}-T(\bm{x})|^{2}\mu({\rm d}^{3}x)

(6)

(
21
)
.
Here
T_{\#}\mu
denotes the pushforward measure by the map
T
, defined for any measurable set
A
as

\displaystyle T_{\#}\mu(A)=\mu(T^{-1}(A)).

(7)

That is, the measure obtained by transporting mass originally at position
\bm{x}
to another position via the map
T
is given by
T_{\#}\mu
.

In general, since the existence of a solution is not guaranteed, one uses the relaxation due to Kantorovich
(
10
)
.
That is, introducing a coupling measure
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
,

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{2}\pi({\rm d}^{3}x\,{\rm d}^{3}y)

(8)

is defined.
Here
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of coupling measures whose marginals are
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively.
In this way,
W_{2}
becomes a distance on the measure space
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

(
35
)
.
Since this distance directly measures the cost of mass rearrangement, it is a natural quantity for describing structure formation.
In particular, it is important that global translations and local deformations can be distinguished.

II.3
Wasserstein Geometry and Geodesics

With the introduction of the distance
W_{2}
, the space of measures
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
acquires a geometric structure analogous to that of a Riemannian manifold
(
34
;
22
)
.
This space is called the Wasserstein space and is understood as a probability measure space endowed with the metric
W_{2}

(
35
)
.
Within this geometric structure, the shortest path connecting two distributions is defined as a geodesic.

This geodesic is constructed as a displacement interpolation, and the time evolution of the density
\rho(t,\bm{x})
is transported by a velocity field
\bm{v}(t,\bm{x})
.
Its time evolution is governed by the continuity equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\bm{v})=0

(9)

(
3
)
.
This represents mass conservation and shows that deformation of measures is described as a transport process.

Furthermore, due to the geometric structure of optimal transport, the velocity field is not arbitrary but can be written as the gradient of a scalar function
\phi
,

\displaystyle\bm{v}=\nabla\phi

(10)

(
35
;
22
)
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\nabla\phi)=0

(11)

holds, and the deformation of measures is realized as a potential flow.
As a result, structure formation is understood as mass transport driven by a potential.

From the above, cosmological structure formation can be regarded as a geometric motion in the space of measures.
The important point here is that structure formation is described not as mere growth of amplitudes, but as rearrangement of mass distributions.
This viewpoint provides the foundation for the curvature concept introduced later.

II.4
Entropy and Curvature of Measure Space

The geometric structure in Wasserstein space is characterized by variations of functionals on measures.
In particular, a fundamental role is played by the entropy functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\int\rho(\bm{x})\ln\rho(\bm{x})\,{\rm d}^{3}x

(12)

(
34
;
22
)
.
This functional quantifies the spread of a distribution, and by considering its variation along geodesics, one can describe geometric properties of the deformation of distributions.
In particular, the convexity of
\mathcal{E}
along Wasserstein geodesics corresponds to the curvature of the measure space
(
11
;
29
;
30
)
.
That is, curvature is defined as the response of entropy to deformations of the distribution.

In this sense, curvature appears not as a local tensor on a Riemannian manifold, but first as the rate of change of a functional on the space of measures.
Therefore, in order to construct the curvature geometry of cosmological density fields, it is necessary to translate this global concept into local quantities of the density field.
This point connects with recent perturbative frameworks in which local tensor fields are used as arguments of bias functions
(
18
)
, but in the present work the curvature tensor is introduced not from a functional expansion but as a local limit of entropy response.
Thus, the second-order tensor appearing here is not a variable of bias expansion, but a local quantity naturally derived from the geometry of measure space.

II.5
Curvature Tensor as a Local Limit

Since the entropy-based curvature described above is essentially a global quantity, it is necessary to consider its local limit in order to describe local structures.
For this purpose, we expand the density field
\rho(\bm{x})
around a point
\bm{x}_{0}
and consider a local quadratic approximation.

The Taylor expansion of the logarithmic density is written as

\displaystyle\ln\rho(\bm{x})=\ln\rho(\bm{x}_{0})+(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})_{i}\partial_{i}\ln\rho(\bm{x}_{0})+\frac{1}{2}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})_{i}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})_{j}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\ln\rho(\bm{x}_{0})+\cdots

(13)

In this expansion, the first derivative represents the local slope, and the second derivative represents the local curvature.
Therefore, we define the curvature tensor as the Hessian of the logarithmic density,

\displaystyle H_{ij}(\bm{x})=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\ln\rho(\bm{x}).

(14)

The negative sign is taken so that
H
becomes positive definite at a local maximum.

With this definition, curvature is introduced not as the second derivative of the density field itself, but as a local response to deformations of the distribution.
Thus, this tensor is interpreted as a local representation of entropy curvature in Wasserstein geometry.
Here, the fact that
\ln\rho
naturally appears in the local limit of entropy geometry will become important later when discussing nonlinearity.
Moreover, in recent tensor bias theories, decomposition of local second-order tensors into rotationally invariant quantities plays a central role
(
18
)
, whereas the distinctive feature of this work is to assign a curvature-geometric meaning to the space of local tensors.

II.6
Curvature Invariants and Positive Curvature Structure

The curvature tensor
H_{ij}
is a symmetric matrix, and the local geometry is characterized by its eigenvalue structure.
In particular, the trace and determinant are defined as

\displaystyle J_{1}=\mathrm{Tr}(H)

(15)

\displaystyle J_{3}=\det(H).

(16)

J_{1}
is interpreted as a measure of mean curvature, and
J_{3}
as a measure of local volume contraction.
Let
\mathrm{Sym}(3)
denote the vector space of real symmetric
3\times 3
matrices.
The curvature tensor is an element
H\in\mathrm{Sym}(3)
, and its local shape is characterized by eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
.

The positive curvature condition is equivalent to
H
being positive definite, and is defined as the positive curvature cone

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{+}=\{H\in\mathrm{Sym}(3)\mid\bm{v}^{\top}H\bm{v}>0\ \text{for all}\ \bm{v}\neq 0\}.

(17)

This definition is equivalent to the eigenvalue condition

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}>0.

(18)

Furthermore,
\mathcal{C}_{+}
is a convex set closed under positive scalar multiplication and forms a convex cone with the origin as its apex.
Thus, the peak condition is expressed as a geometric constraint that the curvature tensor belongs to this positive curvature cone.
In this sense, local maxima of the density distribution are understood not as merely analytic conditions, but as membership conditions in a specific region of curvature tensor space.

II.7
General Definition of Stationary Points and Peaks

In order to characterize the local structure of a distribution, it is necessary to consider both the gradient and curvature simultaneously.
Defining the gradient of the density field as

\displaystyle g_{i}(\bm{x})=\partial_{i}\ln\rho(\bm{x}),

(19)

stationary points are given by

\displaystyle g=0.

(20)

Furthermore, for such a point to be a local maximum, the curvature tensor must be positive definite,

\displaystyle H\in\mathcal{C}_{+}.

(21)

Therefore, a peak is defined as a point where the gradient vanishes and the curvature is positive in measure space.
This definition does not depend on a specific probability distribution and holds for general density fields.

Note that, given that the curvature tensor is defined as the Hessian of
\ln\rho
, the choice of
\partial_{i}\ln\rho
as the gradient is natural.
Moreover, since the location of stationary points coincides for
\partial_{i}\rho=0
and
\partial_{i}\ln\rho=0
, we adopt
g_{i}=\partial_{i}\ln\rho
as the fundamental variable in the general theory.
In this sense, the basic variables
(\rho,g,H)
in this work overlap with the set of local variables used in tensor bias theory, but their role here is not as arguments of a bias function, but as variables of a conditional measure.

II.8
Point Process Representation of Stationary Points

In order to connect the above definitions from local geometric conditions to statistical quantities, it is necessary to represent the set of peaks as a point process in space.
In the classical theory of extrema statistics due to Rice, the number density of stationary points or extrema is described using the Jacobian associated with the zeros of the gradient field
(
26
;
27
)
.
This idea was later introduced into cosmology for the statistics of maxima in Gaussian random fields, and was formulated in BBKS as the fundamental expression for peak statistics in three-dimensional fields
(
2
)
.
In this work, we derive this structure naturally from the geometry of the curvature tensor field.

Let
\bm{x}_{p}
denote the peak positions.
Then the corresponding point process is expressed as

\displaystyle n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x})=\sum_{p}\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p})

(22)

where the sum runs over all points satisfying the stationary condition
g(\bm{x}_{p})=0
and the positive curvature condition
H(\bm{x}_{p})\in\mathcal{C}_{+}
.

Expanding the gradient field
g_{i}(\bm{x})=\partial_{i}\ln\rho(\bm{x})
around a stationary point
\bm{x}_{p}
, we obtain

\displaystyle g_{i}(\bm{x})=g_{i}(\bm{x}_{p})+\partial_{j}g_{i}(\bm{x}_{p})(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p})_{j}+\cdots

(23)

At the stationary point,
g_{i}(\bm{x}_{p})=0
, and furthermore

\displaystyle\partial_{j}g_{i}(\bm{x}_{p})=\partial_{j}\partial_{i}\ln\rho(\bm{x}_{p})

(24)

holds.
On the other hand, the curvature tensor introduced in this work is given by

\displaystyle H_{ij}(\bm{x})=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\ln\rho(\bm{x})

(25)

Therefore, in the vicinity of a stationary point,

\displaystyle g_{i}(\bm{x})\simeq-H_{ij}(\bm{x}_{p})(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p})_{j}

(26)

This implies that the Jacobian of the mapping from the zero set of the gradient field to spatial coordinates is locally governed by
\det H
.

Using the change-of-variables formula for multivariate delta functions,

\displaystyle\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g(\bm{x}))=\sum_{p}\frac{\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p})}{\left|\det\left(\partial_{j}g_{i}\right)_{\bm{x}_{p}}\right|}

(27)

we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{p}\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{p})=\left|\det\left(\partial_{j}g_{i}\right)\right|\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g(\bm{x}))

(28)

(
1
;
2
, e.g.,)
.
To select only maxima, one imposes that the corresponding Hessian is negative definite.
In the present work, since we use
H_{ij}=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\ln\rho
, this condition is written as
H\in\mathcal{C}_{+}
.
Therefore, the peak point process is given by

\displaystyle n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x})=\left|\det\left(\partial_{j}g_{i}\right)\right|\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g(\bm{x}))\Theta(H(\bm{x}))

(29)

Furthermore, since

\displaystyle\partial_{j}g_{i}=-H_{ij}

(30)

we can write the expression in the most compact form

\displaystyle n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x})=|\det H(\bm{x})|\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g(\bm{x}))\Theta(H(\bm{x}))

(31)

Moreover, the locations of stationary points themselves coincide for
\partial_{i}\rho=0
and
\partial_{i}\ln\rho=0
.
Therefore, in the geometric formulation of this work, the essential Jacobian is
|\det H|
, which appears as the natural volume element when counting the zeros of the gradient field of the logarithmic density.
This shows that the determinant factor of the Hessian appearing in conventional extrema statistics functions here as the Jacobian of the curvature tensor of the logarithmic density.
In other words, counting peaks amounts to evaluating the measure pushed forward from the zero set of the gradient field through the local volume transformation induced by the curvature tensor.
In this sense,
|\det H|
is not merely a computational weight, but a geometric Jacobian that induces the point process.

II.9
Peak Statistics as a Curvature-Conditioned Point Process

Based on the above point process representation, the distribution of peaks is formulated as a conditional measure on the curvature tensor field.
Taking the expectation value of the point process, we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{\rm pk}=\left\langle|\det H|\,\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g)\,\Theta(H)\right\rangle

(32)

Here
g_{i}(\bm{x})=\partial_{i}\ln\rho(\bm{x})
is the gradient of the logarithmic density, and the locations of stationary points coincide with those obtained from
\partial_{i}\rho=0
.
Writing this expectation in terms of the joint distribution of local variables, we have

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{\rm pk}=\int{\rm d}\rho\,{\rm d}^{3}g\,{\rm d}H\,|\det H|\,p(\rho,g,H)\,\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g)\,\Theta(H)

(33)

Thus, when the gradient of the logarithmic density is taken as a fundamental variable, the geometric Jacobian is naturally extracted as
|\det H|
.

In what follows, in order to make the correspondence with curvature geometry as transparent as possible, we adopt this formulation based on the logarithmic density gradient as the canonical form.
Then the peak number density is given by

\displaystyle n_{\rm pk}=\int{\rm d}\rho\,{\rm d}^{3}g\,{\rm d}H\,|\det H|\,p(\rho,g,H)\,\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g)\,\Theta(H)

(34)

Here
p(\rho,g,H)
is the joint distribution of the density, the gradient of the logarithmic density, and the curvature tensor,
\Theta(H)
is the indicator function imposing the constraint of the positive curvature cone, and
|\det H|
represents the local volume element near a stationary point, appearing as a geometric weight.

This expression shows that peak statistics are not constructed as a quantity dependent on a specific random field, but rather as a general conditional measure defined on the curvature tensor field.
Thus, peaks are understood not as an analytic condition of density maxima, but as natural structures in curvature geometry.
Importantly, while this expression reduces to the BBKS peak statistics when a Gaussian distribution is assumed,
its derivation itself does not rely on Gaussianity
.
Therefore, the BBKS theory is positioned as a special case of this general curvature-conditioned point process.
In this sense, the fundamental object of this work is not a functional response coefficient, but the conditional measure defined on local tensor variables themselves.
This viewpoint will also be important in the later discussion of the hierarchy of response functions.

II.10
Connection to the Linear Regime and Density Fluctuations

Finally, we verify how this general theory connects to the conventional description in terms of density fluctuations.
Expanding the density field around the mean density
\bar{\rho}
,

\displaystyle\rho=\bar{\rho}(1+\delta)

(35)

In the linear regime where the density fluctuation is sufficiently small,

\displaystyle\ln\rho\simeq\delta

(36)

holds, and therefore

\displaystyle H_{ij}\simeq-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\delta

(37)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle g_{i}=\partial_{i}\ln\rho\simeq\partial_{i}\delta

(38)

so that in the linear Gaussian limit,

\displaystyle\eta_{i}=\partial_{i}\delta

(39)

and the peak condition reduces to the conventional gradient-zero condition and the sign condition on the Hessian.
Thus, the curvature tensor introduced in this section coincides, in the linear limit, with the second derivative of the density fluctuation field used in the conventional formulation.

III
Curvature-Conditioned Point Process in the Gaussian Limit

III.1
Linear Limit and Reduction to the Curvature Tensor

In Section
II
, peak statistics were formulated as a conditional measure on the curvature tensor field.
In this section, we introduce the linear regime and the assumption of a Gaussian random field into this general framework, and evaluate explicitly the statistical structure of the curvature tensor field.

We write the density field around the mean density
\bar{\rho}
as

\displaystyle\rho=\bar{\rho}(1+\delta)

(40)

and consider the regime where the density fluctuation
\delta
is sufficiently small.
In this limit,

\displaystyle\ln\rho\simeq\delta

(41)

holds, and the curvature tensor reduces to

\displaystyle H_{ij}\simeq-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\delta

(42)

In Section
II
, the peak point process was defined using the logarithmic density gradient

\displaystyle g_{i}=\partial_{i}\ln\rho

In the linear regime,

\displaystyle g_{i}\simeq\partial_{i}\delta

(43)

so we introduce the notation

\displaystyle\eta_{i}\equiv\partial_{i}\delta

(44)

Thus, in this section,
\eta_{i}
is adopted as the linear Gaussian limit of
g_{i}
used in the general formulation.
The peak locations are then given by

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\delta=0

(45)

This perspective of treating density, its gradient, and second derivatives as local variables runs parallel to recent Lagrangian bias theories based on local tensor fields, but here they are treated not as arguments of a bias function, but as jointly distributed random variables defining a curvature-conditioned point process.

Therefore, in the linear regime, the curvature-conditioned point process introduced in Section
II
reduces to a formulation described by the gradient-zero condition and the Hessian condition of the density fluctuation field.
In this section, we impose the assumption of a Gaussian random field on this formulation and evaluate the joint statistics of the local variables
(\delta,\eta_{i},H_{ij})
.

III.2
Gaussian Random Field and Statistics of the Curvature Tensor

Next, we assume that the density fluctuation
\delta(\bm{x})
is a Gaussian random field with zero mean.
In this case, the statistics are completely characterized by the power spectrum
P(k)

(
25
;
24
;
32
, e.g.,)
.
Using a Fourier expansion,

\displaystyle\delta(\bm{x})=\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\tilde{\delta}(\bm{k})e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}

(46)

and Gaussianity and isotropy imply

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\delta}(\bm{k})\tilde{\delta}(\bm{k}^{\prime})\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(\bm{k}+\bm{k}^{\prime})P(k)

(47)

An important property of Gaussian random fields is that fields generated by linear operators are also Gaussian.
Thus,

\displaystyle\eta_{i}=\partial_{i}\delta,\qquad H_{ij}=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\delta

and the set of local variables

\displaystyle(\delta,\eta_{i},H_{ij})

(48)

follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution.
Therefore, under the Gaussian assumption, the conditional measure defining the peak number density reduces to a finite-dimensional joint distribution of local variables.

Their covariances are given by

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{2}\rangle=\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}P(k)

(49)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{i}\delta\,\partial_{j}\delta\rangle=\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}k_{i}k_{j}P(k)

(50)

\displaystyle\langle H_{ij}H_{kl}\rangle=\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}k_{i}k_{j}k_{k}k_{l}P(k)

(51)

and, by isotropy,

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{i}\eta_{j}\rangle=\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{3}\delta_{ij}

(52)

\displaystyle\langle\delta H_{ij}\rangle=\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{3}\delta_{ij}

(53)

\displaystyle\langle H_{ij}H_{kl}\rangle=\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{15}\left(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\right)

(54)

where the spectral moments are defined as

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2}{\rm d}k}{2\pi^{2}}P(k)k^{2j}

(55)

Thus, the statistics of the curvature tensor are completely determined by
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
.
In particular, since the covariance of
H_{ij}
involves a
k^{4}
weighting, the curvature tensor is sensitive to high-wavenumber modes.
Furthermore, due to the isotropy of the Gaussian random field, the gradient
\eta_{i}
is statistically independent of the scalar variables
(\delta,H_{ij})
.
Therefore, in evaluating the peak number density, the gradient-zero condition
\eta=0
can be treated as an independent Gaussian factor.

III.3
Dimensionless Curvature Variables and Joint Statistics

Even in the Gaussian limit, the spatial distribution of peaks inherits, without modification, the general structure of the curvature-conditioned point process introduced in Section
II
.
What the assumption of a Gaussian random field affects is that the joint distribution
p(\delta,\eta,H)
appearing in the point process can be evaluated explicitly and written in a closed form.
Here
H
denotes the curvature tensor
H_{ij}
, and
\eta
is a shorthand notation representing the vector
\eta_{i}
.

For this purpose, we introduce the amplitude

\displaystyle\nu=\frac{\delta}{\sigma_{0}}

(56)

and the dimensionless curvature

\displaystyle x=-\frac{\nabla^{2}\delta}{\sigma_{2}}

(57)

Here
x
is a quantity corresponding to the trace of
H_{ij}
, and is the most fundamental scalar invariant of the curvature tensor.
In fact,

\displaystyle x=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(H)}{\sigma_{2}}

(58)

holds.
In this way, organizing a local second-order tensor in terms of its rotational invariants is a natural procedure when describing tensor statistics under rotational symmetry, and is also closely related to recent tensor bias theories.

By Gaussianity,
(\nu,x)
follows a bivariate Gaussian distribution.
In fact,

\displaystyle\langle\nu^{2}\rangle=1,\qquad\langle x^{2}\rangle=1,\qquad\langle\nu x\rangle=\gamma

(59)

where

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{\sigma_{0}\sigma_{2}}

(60)

is the correlation coefficient between the amplitude and the curvature.
Therefore, the joint distribution can be written as

\displaystyle p(\nu,x)\propto\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2(1-\gamma^{2})}\left(\nu^{2}-2\gamma\nu x+x^{2}\right)\right]

(61)

This distribution shows that, in a Gaussian field, the evaluation of peak statistics is reduced to the joint statistics of the amplitude
\nu
and the curvature invariant
x
.
In the subsequent evaluation of integrals, one combines this joint structure of
(\nu,x)
with the positive curvature condition imposed on the eigenvalues of the Hessian.

III.4
Peak Number Density: Measure Integration in Curvature Space and the Gaussian Limit

The mean peak number density is obtained by applying the linear limit to the curvature-conditioned point process (Section
II
), and can be written as

\displaystyle n_{\rm pk}(\nu)=\int{\rm d}H\,|\det H|\,p(\nu,H\,|\,\eta=0)\,\Theta(H)

(62)

Here
\Theta(H)
represents the constraint to the positive curvature cone
\mathcal{C}_{+}
, and
{\rm d}H
is the measure on the space of symmetric matrices
\mathrm{Sym}(3)
.
If necessary,

\displaystyle{\rm d}H=\prod_{i\leq j}{\rm d}H_{ij}

(63)

may be understood.

In order to evaluate this integral explicitly, we use the eigenvalue decomposition of the curvature tensor

\displaystyle H=O^{\top}\Lambda O

(64)

where
O\in\mathrm{O}(3)
is an orthogonal matrix, and
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues.
Under this change of variables, the measure is decomposed as

\displaystyle{\rm d}H=|\Delta(\lambda)|\,{\rm d}^{3}\lambda\,{\rm d}\mu(O)

(65)

Here
{\rm d}\mu(O)
is the invariant measure on the orthogonal group,
{\rm d}^{3}\lambda={\rm d}\lambda_{1}{\rm d}\lambda_{2}{\rm d}\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle\Delta(\lambda)=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{3})(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3})

(66)

is the Vandermonde-type Jacobian.

Due to the statistical isotropy of the Gaussian random field, the joint distribution
p(\nu,H)
is invariant under rotations and does not depend on the rotational degrees of freedom

Therefore, the integral over the rotational part separates as a constant factor.
As a result, the peak number density can be written as an integral over eigenvalue space,

\displaystyle n_{\rm pk}(\nu)\propto\int_{\lambda_{i}>0}{\rm d}^{3}\lambda\,|\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}|\,|\Delta(\lambda)|\,p(\nu,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})

(67)

where the integration domain
\lambda_{i}>0
corresponds to the positive curvature cone
\mathcal{C}_{+}
.
Equation (
67
) shows that the peak number density in a Gaussian random field is evaluated as a conditional integral over the eigenvalues of the curvature tensor.
Moreover, since the distribution
p(\nu,H)
is determined by the spectral moments
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
, Eq. (
67
) is reduced to a form that can be evaluated using these dimensionless quantities.
In this way, while the evaluation of statistical quantities using rotationally invariant representations of local tensors is common with recent tensor statistics theories, it is important in the present work that the final object remains a measure integral of a conditional point process.

Therefore, the problem of peak statistics in the Gaussian limit is reduced to the problem of integrating a finite-dimensional Gaussian measure of the curvature tensor field over eigenvalue space under the positive curvature condition.
The meaning of this integral structure and its correspondence with the BBKS theory will be discussed in detail in the next section.

IV
Structure of Peak Theory as Curvature Statistics

IV.1
Basic Decomposition of Peak Statistics

In Section
III

H_{ij}

In particular, Eqs. (
62

\text{curvature constraint}\\[4.0pt]
\times\\[4.0pt]
\text{geometric measure}\end{array}

Probability distribution

The first element refers to the joint probability distribution of the local variables
(\delta,\eta_{i},H_{ij})

Here

In the Gaussian limit, this distribution is given as a multivariate Gaussian distribution and is completely determined by the spectral moments

Curvature constraint

The second element is the geometric constraint defining peaks, expressed as the stationary condition
\eta=0
and the positive curvature condition
H\in\mathcal{C}_{+}

Here
\eta=0

Geometric measure

The third element is the geometric weight appearing when the measure on the symmetric matrix space

|\Delta(\lambda)|
in Eq. (
65

|\det H|

This decomposition shows that the essence of peak statistics is not merely the number of extrema, but a conditional geometric statistic on the curvature tensor field.
In particular,
|\det H|

\Theta(H)
ensures that the curvature tensor belongs to the positive curvature cone.
Therefore, a peak should be understood not as a local maximum of the density field itself, but as a locally selected geometric structure within the curvature tensor field.

IV.2
Geometric Meaning of Spectral Moments

In a Gaussian field, peak statistics are described in a closed form by the spectral moments
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
, but in the present framework these are not merely auxiliary quantities for computation.
Each of them should be understood as representing different geometric aspects of curvature statistics.

First,
\sigma_{0}^{2}
gives the variance of the density fluctuation amplitude and represents the typical scale of the “height” of local structures.
In contrast,
\sigma_{1}^{2}
is the variance of the gradient field and controls the roughness of the field, that is, the ease of forming stationary points.
Here the gradient field refers to
\eta_{i}=\partial_{i}\delta
, corresponding to
g_{i}
in the general notation.
Furthermore,
\sigma_{2}^{2}
is the variance of the second-derivative field and directly governs the strength of the curvature tensor
H_{ij}=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\delta
.
Therefore,
\sigma_{2}
is the most fundamental scale in curvature statistics.

The dimensionless quantity constructed from these,

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{\sigma_{0}\sigma_{2}}

(73)

represents the strength of the coupling between amplitude and curvature.
As appears in Eq. (
61
),
\gamma
controls the correlation between the height
\nu
and the dimensionless curvature
x
, and larger
\gamma
implies that higher peaks tend to be associated with stronger curvature.
Thus,
\gamma
is a geometric parameter that measures the statistical coupling between peak height and shape.

Moreover,

\displaystyle R_{\ast}=\sqrt{3}\,\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\sigma_{2}}

(74)

appears as the ratio of the gradient scale to the curvature scale and gives the typical spatial scale of peaks.
The appearance of this quantity in the prefactor of the peak number density shows that peak statistics are governed not simply by an amplitude threshold, but by the geometric scale of local shapes.
Therefore, the spectral moments and dimensionless quantities introduced in BBKS should not be regarded as arising merely from the general theory of Gaussian random fields, but rather as natural invariants characterizing the geometric structure of the curvature tensor field.

IV.3
Meaning of the Coupling Between Amplitude and Curvature

The core of peak statistics lies in the fact that the amplitude
\nu
and the curvature variable
x
are not independent.
The joint distribution (
61
) obtained in Section
III
makes explicit that peaks are defined not only by height but by the joint statistics with invariants of the curvature tensor.
Here
x
is the dimensionless curvature corresponding to the trace of the curvature tensor.
That is, as defined in Section
III
,

\displaystyle x=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(H)}{\sigma_{2}}

(75)

and
(\nu,x)
form the most basic local description combining “height” and “mean curvature”.
In conventional peak theory, peaks are often understood simply as points exceeding a threshold
\nu
, but in the present framework this understanding is extended to naturally include how the eigenvalue structure of the curvature tensor is coupled.
Even if two points have the same amplitude
\nu
, they represent different geometric objects if their curvature tensor eigenvalue structures differ.
Therefore, the essence of peak statistics lies not in amplitude thresholds, but in the joint distribution of amplitude and curvature.

Furthermore, imposing the positive curvature condition
\Theta(H)
means that the allowed region of curvature is determined not only by the trace
x
but by the full eigenvalue configuration.
Thus, the shape of peaks is specified not by a single scalar, but by a domain constraint in the symmetric matrix space
\mathrm{Sym}(3)
.
In this sense, peak statistics are not amplitude statistics, but conditional geometric statistics of invariants of the curvature tensor.

This viewpoint remains important when extending to non-Gaussian fields and nonlinear structures.
This is because even when Gaussianity is lost, the structure of the coupling between amplitude and curvature is preserved.
What changes is the explicit form of the distribution, not the geometric skeleton of the joint statistics.

IV.4
Eigenvalue Space and Curvature Selection

The fact that the peak number density is expressed as an integral in eigenvalue space most clearly reveals the geometry of the curvature tensor field.
Equation (
67
) shows that peak statistics are a measure integral in eigenvalue space.

Here
|\det H|
represents the local volume transformation rate from the gradient field to coordinate space near a stationary point.
As derived in Section
II

In the notation of this section, the gradient field is
\eta_{i}=\partial_{i}\delta
, corresponding to
g_{i}=\partial_{i}\ln\rho
in the general theory.
On the other hand, the Vandermonde-type Jacobian
|\Delta(\lambda)|
appearing in Eq. (
65
) is the measure factor arising from the change of variables from symmetric matrix space to eigenvalue space, and represents that eigenvalue degeneracies form a measure-zero set.

These two factors show that peak statistics are not merely probabilistic integrals, but are based on a “selected measure in curvature space”.
In particular, the restriction of the integration domain
\lambda_{i}>0
directly reflects that peaks are defined by the condition of belonging to the positive curvature cone
\mathcal{C}_{+}
.
Here the measure in eigenvalue space follows the notation of Section
III

\displaystyle{\rm d}^{3}\lambda={\rm d}\lambda_{1}{\rm d}\lambda_{2}{\rm d}\lambda_{3}

(76)

Therefore, the peak number density is understood as the result of selecting curvature tensors belonging to the positive curvature cone within the symmetric matrix space
\mathrm{Sym}(3)
and integrating their measure over eigenvalue space.
This interpretation positions the well-known BBKS peak number density formula as a concrete realization of the measure-theoretic structure of the curvature tensor field under a Gaussian distribution.

IV.5
Response-Theoretic Interpretation of Peak Bias

The response of peaks to long-wavelength modes can also be naturally understood from the viewpoint of curvature statistics.
Here
\delta_{L}
denotes the long-wavelength component of the density fluctuation field
\delta(\bm{x})
.
That is, for a separation scale
R_{L}

\displaystyle\delta_{L}(\bm{x})=\int{\rm d}^{3}y\,W_{L}(|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|)\,\delta(\bm{y})

representing a global background mode.

Since the peak number density is determined by the joint statistics of amplitude and curvature, the long-wavelength fluctuation
\delta_{L}
changes not only the amplitude but also the entire distribution of peaks through its coupling with local curvature.
Therefore, peak bias is defined as a response coefficient measuring how the local peak number density changes in the presence of this long-wavelength background field.
This viewpoint parallels the perspective emphasized in recent Lagrangian bias theory, where bias is understood as a response

The peak bias is defined as the response of the peak number density to the long-wavelength background field,

\displaystyle b_{\rm pk}=\dfrac{\partial\ln n_{\rm pk}}{\partial\delta_{L}}

Under the Gaussian limit, this response can be written as

\displaystyle b_{\rm pk}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}}\left[\nu-\gamma\langle x\rangle_{\rm pk}(\nu)\right]

(79)

where
\langle x\rangle_{\rm pk}(\nu)
is the mean curvature under the condition of fixed amplitude
\nu

As shown by this expression, the peak bias is essentially decomposed into two components:

\displaystyle b_{\rm pk}=\underbrace{\frac{\nu}{\sigma_{0}}}_{\text{amplitude response}}-\underbrace{\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{0}}\langle x\rangle_{\rm pk}(\nu)}_{\text{curvature response}}.

(80)

The first term is the response to the density amplitude itself and corresponds to conventional threshold selection.
The second term, in contrast, is the shape response mediated by the statistics of the curvature tensor, representing the effect that the local geometry of peaks is modulated by long-wavelength modes.
In this sense, peak bias is not merely an abundance response.
It measures how a point process conditioned on the curvature tensor field responds to a large-scale background field, and should be understood as the

This response-theoretic interpretation is closely related to the classical discussion of large-scale bias based on the peak-background split.
In particular,

In this respect, the present formulation shares the basic idea that long-wavelength backgrounds modify the number density of local structures.
However, the starting points of the two approaches are fundamentally different.
While

Therefore, the bias in this work is defined not as the response of a mass function, but as the response of the curvature-conditioned point process

This difference becomes clearer at the level of theoretical structure.
In

Thus, the bias here is not a simple abundance response, but a geometric response of a peak ensemble selected by the curvature tensor.
In this sense, while sharing the philosophy of recent response-based bias theories, this work extends the target to curvature-conditioned point processes.

From this, bias in peak theory should be understood not as a statistical correction term, but as part of a response theory based on curvature geometry.
This perspective is also useful when considering extensions of bias under non-Gaussian initial conditions and nonlinear evolution.

IV.6
Redefinition of the Role of BBKS

From the above discussion, the meaning of the BBKS theory is redefined in a broader context.
BBKS should not be regarded merely as an approximate formula for peaks in a Gaussian random field, but as a theory that explicitly evaluates a conditional measure on the curvature tensor field under the Gaussian assumption.
More concretely, the theoretical structure of BBKS can be decomposed into

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\text{probability distribution}\\[4.0pt]
\times\\[4.0pt]
\text{curvature constraint}\\[4.0pt]
\times\\[4.0pt]
\text{geometric measure}\end{array}

Among these, Gaussianity is responsible only for providing a closed-form probability distribution, while the curvature constraint and geometric measure remain valid for more general fields.
Therefore, in the present framework, BBKS is not the starting point but is positioned as the Gaussian limit of curvature statistics.
Through this repositioning, the classical results of BBKS are embedded within a larger framework of curvature geometry, clarifying both their domain of applicability and their limitations.
At the same time, in relation to recent theories dealing with the statistics of local tensor fields, the present work should be understood not as competing with them but as providing a complementary viewpoint based on conditional measures.

V
Extensions of Curvature Statistics Beyond Linear Gaussian Theory

V.1
Invariant and Deformed Structures of the Curvature-Conditioned Point Process

In the preceding sections, we have clarified that the internal structure of peak statistics can be decomposed into three elements: “probability distribution,” “curvature constraint,” and “geometric measure.”
The purpose of this section is to provide a theoretical organization of what remains invariant and what is deformed when the assumptions imposed in the BBKS theory are relaxed one by one, while preserving this three-element decomposition.
The important point here is that this extension is not merely the introduction of correction terms.
The essential point is that the structure of the curvature-conditioned point process introduced in Section
II
itself does not depend on Gaussianity or linearity, and that BBKS corresponds to a particular closed-form evaluation of it.

In this section, we extend the joint distribution
p_{\rm G}
used in the Gaussian limit to a general joint probability distribution
p
.
Here
p
denotes the general joint distribution of the local variables
(\rho,g,H)
, and reduces to
p_{\rm G}
in the Gaussian limit.

From this viewpoint, the invariant structure is clear.

1.

The condition for selecting stationary points is always given by the zero condition of the gradient field.

2.

The condition for selecting maxima is given by the geometric constraint that the curvature tensor belongs to the positive curvature cone.

3.

The factor
|\det H|
appearing as the measure factor of the point process is defined as the Jacobian of the local mapping from the gradient field to real space, and is therefore preserved independently of the specific form of the distribution.

Here, in this section, in accordance with the basic formulation of Section
II
, we use the gradient field

\displaystyle g_{i}=\partial_{i}\ln\rho

In the linear Gaussian limit, this coincides with

\displaystyle\eta_{i}=\partial_{i}\delta

used in Section
III
.
That is, in the nonlinear theory we use
g_{i}
, while in the linear Gaussian limit we use
\eta_{i}
, but in both cases they are gradient variables representing the stationary point condition.
What is deformed, on the other hand, is primarily the joint distribution.
In the Gaussian theory, this part is closed by a finite number of spectral moments and is concretized as a multivariate Gaussian distribution.
However, in general, the joint distribution contains higher-order cumulants, and the coupling between amplitude and curvature is deformed into a more complicated form.
Therefore, the extension treated in this section is not a replacement of the existing measure structure, but an extension of the family of distributions and scale dependence defined on top of it.
In this section, we first reconfirm the necessity of logarithmic density curvature from entropy geometry, then discuss the extension to general distributions, and finally organize scale dependence as a flow.

V.2
Natural Derivation of Log-Density Curvature and Nonlinear Structure

In previous studies, the logarithmic density function has often been regarded as a convenient generalization beyond linear theory, or as a phenomenological functional form reproducing observations and simulations.
However, as discussed in Section
II
, the curvature tensor based on the logarithmic density,

\displaystyle H_{ij}=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\ln\rho

(86)

is the quantity that first appears from entropy geometry based on optimal transport.
Localizing the second variation along geodesic directions of the entropy functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\int\rho(\bm{x})\ln\rho(\bm{x})\,{\rm d}^{3}x

(87)

for the probability measure
\mu({\rm d}^{3}x)=\rho(\bm{x})\,{\rm d}^{3}x
, the Hessian of the logarithmic density naturally appears.
That is,
H_{ij}
is not a substitute for the second derivative of the density field, but a quantity with the theoretical meaning of a local representation of the entropy response to deformations of the distribution.

To make this point explicit, decompose the density field around a local reference density
\rho_{0}
as

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{x})=\rho_{0}+\delta\rho(\bm{x})

(88)

and expand the logarithm to obtain

\displaystyle\ln\rho=\ln\rho_{0}+\frac{\delta\rho}{\rho_{0}}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\delta\rho}{\rho_{0}}\right)^{2}+\cdots

(89)

On the other hand, in cosmological linear theory, writing around the mean density
\bar{\rho}
as

\displaystyle\rho=\bar{\rho}(1+\delta)

(90)

we have

\displaystyle\ln\rho=\ln\bar{\rho}+\delta-\frac{1}{2}\delta^{2}+\frac{1}{3}\delta^{3}+\cdots

(91)

Therefore,

\displaystyle H_{ij}=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\delta+\partial_{i}(\delta\,\partial_{j}\delta)-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\!\left(\frac{1}{3}\delta^{3}\right)+\cdots

(92)

and in the linear limit it indeed reduces to

\displaystyle H_{ij}\simeq-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\delta

This relation does not mean that the logarithmic density curvature is an “extension” of linear theory, but rather that the conventional Hessian theory is the lowest-order approximation of the logarithmic density curvature.

In general,

\displaystyle\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\ln\rho=\frac{1}{\rho}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\rho-\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\partial_{i}\rho\,\partial_{j}\rho

(93)

so that

\displaystyle H_{ij}=-\frac{1}{\rho}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\rho+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\partial_{i}\rho\,\partial_{j}\rho

(94)

holds.
This expression explicitly shows what enters newly in the nonlinear regime.
The first term corresponds to the conventional density Hessian, while the second term is a positive-definite correction consisting of squared gradients, which becomes dominant in regions where the density gradient is large.
Therefore, around high-density cores and steep filamentary structures, this nonlinear term deforms the effective magnitude and anisotropy of curvature.

This also changes the meaning of the peak condition itself.
In linear theory, a peak is understood as a point where
-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\delta
is positive definite, whereas in nonlinear theory it is a point where

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\rho}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\rho+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\partial_{i}\rho\,\partial_{j}\rho

(95)

is positive definite.
That is, a nonlinear peak is selected not only as a local maximum of the density, but as a point where the local convergence of the density gradient is sufficiently strong.
In this sense, the logarithmic density curvature is a natural fundamental quantity for describing nonlinear concentrated structures such as halos, clusters, and filaments.

From the above, the first extension here is not a formal replacement of the Hessian.
It is the recovery of the nonlinear terms that were truncated in the linear approximation by returning the origin of curvature to entropy geometry.
Through this extension, peak statistics are connected from linear Gaussian theory to a more general nonlinear measure geometry.

V.3
Extension to General (Non-Gaussian) Distributions and Preserved Structure

Next, we consider the structure when Gaussianity is relaxed.
The essential reason why a closed form was obtained in Section

What is broken is that the joint distribution of local variables is no longer closed by a finite number of two-point statistics.

\gamma
, in a general distribution infinitely many independent higher-order coupling parameters appear.
The essence of the present discussion is not to “add correction terms to a Gaussian distribution,” but to “extend the joint distribution space of amplitude and curvature itself.”
In this sense, non-Gaussianity should be understood not as a distortion of amplitude statistics, but as a deformation of curvature geometry.

Furthermore, this structure allows both primordial non-Gaussianity and nonlinear gravitational evolution to be treated within the same framework.
The former appears as higher-order cumulants of the initial distribution, while the latter appears as effective higher-order cumulants generated through transport evolution.
In both cases, however, the basic form of the point process remains invariant, and only the joint distribution is deformed.
This is the strength of the present theory.

V.4
Scale Dependence and Flow of Curvature

In the BBKS theory, the smoothing scale is treated as a fixed external parameter.
In contrast, in the viewpoint of curvature statistics, the scale is an essential internal degree of freedom.
This is because the curvature tensor introduced in Section
II
is originally defined for a coarse-grained density field.

Using a smoothing kernel
W_{R}
,

\displaystyle\rho_{R}(\bm{x})=\int{\rm d}^{3}y\,W_{R}(|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|)\rho(\bm{y})

(99)

we define the logarithmic density curvature as

\displaystyle H_{ij}(\bm{x};R)=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\ln\rho_{R}(\bm{x})

(100)

In Section
III
, in linear theory this reduces to

\displaystyle H_{ij}(\bm{x};R)\simeq-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\delta_{R}(\bm{x})

(101)

and it was shown that the statistics are determined through the spectral moments

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}^{2}(R)=\int\frac{k^{2}{\rm d}k}{2\pi^{2}}P(k)\,k^{2j}W^{2}(kR)

(102)

However, in general, since taking the logarithm of the density and smoothing do not commute, scale dependence acquires a more essential meaning in the nonlinear regime.

The scale dependence of the peak number density is generally written as

\displaystyle n_{\rm pk}(R)=\int{\rm d}\rho_{R}\,{\rm d}^{3}g_{R}\,{\rm d}H_{R}\,|\det H_{R}|\,p_{R}(\rho_{R},g_{R},H_{R})\,\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g_{R})\,\Theta(H_{R})

(103)

Here the subscript
R
indicates that the definition of the local variables themselves depends on the coarse-graining scale.
Differentiating this expression with respect to
R
, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{R}n_{\rm pk}=\int{\rm d}\rho_{R}\,{\rm d}^{3}g_{R}\,{\rm d}H_{R}\,\partial_{R}\left[|\det H_{R}|\,p_{R}(\rho_{R},g_{R},H_{R})\,\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g_{R})\,\Theta(H_{R})\right]

(104)

Thus, there are three sources of the flow.
Namely, (1) the deformation of the distribution
p_{R}
, (2) the deformation of the curvature tensor
H_{R}
itself, and (3) the deformation of the region satisfying the curvature cone constraint.
In Gaussian linear theory, this reduces to the
R
dependence of the spectral moments, but in general it becomes a much richer flow.

From this viewpoint, the BBKS theory corresponds to a single “fixed section” in scale space.
It is a section at a particular
R
, where a closed evaluation is possible under the linear Gaussian limit.
In contrast, curvature statistics theory treats
R
as a flow parameter and describes how the classification of peaks, filaments, sheets, and voids is reorganized as the scale changes.
In this sense, the scale dependence of curvature is not a mere correction, but a geometric coarse-graining flow, and in essence has an RG-like structure.

Furthermore, this flow is also important in observational analysis.
In actual galaxy distributions and simulation data, both the number and shape of detected peaks change depending on the smoothing scale.
The present theory provides a basis for describing this change not as an ad hoc parameter dependence, but as a scale flow of the curvature-conditioned point process.
The core of this extension lies in understanding curvature statistics itself as a dynamical system in scale space, beyond fixed-scale theory.

V.5
Finite-Dimensional Curvature and Weighted Measures

In the discussion so far, we have used the curvature corresponding to Kullback–Leibler-type entropy.
In this case, the local representation of curvature is given by the Hessian of the logarithmic density,

\displaystyle H_{ij}=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\ln\rho

(105)

and describes the response to deformations of the distribution.
However, as discussed in Section
II
, entropy geometry on measure space also admits a finite-dimensional version, and an effective curvature based on Rényi-type entropy can be introduced.
Thus, the choice of entropy is not merely a formal difference, but should be understood as a geometric degree of freedom that controls which types of distributional deformations are emphasized.

When a finite dimension
N
is introduced, what naturally appears is a weighted measure with respect to the density,

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mu_{N}=\rho(\bm{x})^{N}\,{\rm d}^{3}x

(106)

At this point, from

\displaystyle\ln\rho^{N}=N\ln\rho

(107)

it follows that

\displaystyle-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\ln\rho^{N}=NH_{ij}

(108)

holds.
Therefore, the curvature based on finite-dimensional entropy can be understood as a weighted extension of the basic curvature tensor
H_{ij}
.

This weighted measure emphasizes the volume element
{\rm d}^{3}x
in high-density regions, and as
N
increases, the contribution of concentrated structures is selectively amplified.
Thus,
N
is interpreted as a geometric parameter that controls how strongly concentrated structures are observed in probability measure space.
The scalar effective curvature associated with this measure is given by

\displaystyle K_{\rm eff}^{(N)}=-\nabla^{2}\ln\rho^{N}=-N\nabla^{2}\ln\rho

(109)

That is, the finite-dimensional curvature is a quantity obtained by reweighting the logarithmic density curvature according to the degree of density concentration, and should be understood not as introducing a new geometric structure, but as modifying the sensitivity to the existing curvature structure.

In this case, the peak statistics are defined as

\displaystyle n_{\rm pk}^{(N)}=\int{\rm d}\mu_{N}\,|\det H|\,\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g)\,\Theta(H)

(110)

Here
g
provides the stationary point condition, and
\Theta(H)
represents the constraint to the positive curvature cone.
Therefore, the preserved structure is identical to the conventional case, and only the weighting of the measure changes.
In this sense, the introduction of finite-dimensional curvature should be understood as an operation that deforms the measure while preserving the three-element structure of the curvature-conditioned point process.
The effect of this weighting is evident from the expression

\displaystyle n_{\rm pk}^{(N)}=\int\rho(\bm{x})^{N}\,{\rm d}^{3}x\,|\det H|\,\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g)\,\Theta(H)

(111)

in which the contribution is amplified in high-density regions and suppressed in low-density regions.
Thus,
N
becomes a parameter that controls which types of structures are statistically emphasized.

This property can be understood in contrast with conventional Gaussian peak theory.
In the conventional framework, peak statistics are primarily characterized by the distributions of amplitude and curvature, whereas in the present framework, while preserving the same curvature-conditioned point process structure, selection depending on the degree of concentration becomes possible through the measure.
Therefore, this extension generalizes peak statistics into concentration-sensitive statistical quantities.
Furthermore, this weighted measure is also important in observational analyses based on finite samples.
In actual galaxy distributions and halo catalogs, the treatment of the statistical contribution from high-density regions significantly affects the properties of estimators.
Weighting by
\rho^{N}
can be interpreted as a natural geometric operation for stably extracting concentrated structures.
From the above, the introduction of finite-dimensional curvature extends curvature statistics from a mere classification of shapes to geometric statistics reflecting the degree of density concentration.

V.6
Theoretical Structure of Curvature Statistics and Its Solvable Limit

From the above discussion, the content of the extension introduced in this section becomes clear.
First, the logarithmic density curvature is not an ad hoc definition, but is naturally derived as the local limit of entropy geometry.
Second, non-Gaussianity deforms the distribution component within the three-element decomposition, while the skeleton consisting of the zero-gradient condition, curvature constraint, and Jacobian measure is preserved.
Third, scale dependence is not merely an auxiliary variable, but provides the flow of curvature statistics itself.
Fourth, by introducing finite-dimensional curvature, curvature statistics are extended into geometric statistics that selectively emphasize concentrated structures under the weighted measure
\mu_{N}
.

These results show that the essence of peak statistics does not depend on a specific probability distribution or approximation, but is supported by a universal structure as a curvature-conditioned point process.
Therefore, the BBKS theory is reinterpreted as a closed-form theory obtained under the special conditions

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\text{curvature-conditioned point process}\\[4.0pt]
+\\[4.0pt]
\text{linear limit}\\[4.0pt]
+\\[4.0pt]
\text{Gaussian closure}\\[4.0pt]
+\\[4.0pt]
\text{fixed scale}.\end{array}

The significance of this section lies in clarifying that even beyond this special limit, the skeleton of the theory itself is preserved.
That is, what is newly introduced by the extension are the specific forms of the distribution and scale dependence, not the fundamental structure based on curvature constraints and measure.
In this sense, BBKS is not an isolated classical theory, but should be understood as one solvable limit of a more general curvature statistics theory based on transport geometry and entropy curvature.

VI.1
n
-point Statistics as a Curvature-Conditioned Point Process

In the discussion so far, we have organized the structure of the curvature-conditioned point process mainly in terms of the one-point statistic, namely the peak number density.
However, the essence of a point process lies not in the mean number density itself, but rather in the correlations of peaks at different positions.
In particular, the two-point and three-point correlations are the most fundamental
n
-point statistics describing what kind of geometric configuration peaks take in space.

In the standpoint of the present paper, peaks are defined as those zeros of the logarithmic density gradient

\displaystyle g_{i}(\bm{x})=\partial_{i}\ln\rho(\bm{x})

(119)

for which the curvature tensor

\displaystyle H_{ij}(\bm{x})=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\ln\rho(\bm{x})

(120)

belongs to the positive curvature cone.
Accordingly, the point-process operator for peaks can be written as

\displaystyle n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x})=|\det H(\bm{x})|\,\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g(\bm{x}))\,\Theta(H(\bm{x}))

(121)

Here
g(\bm{x})
is a shorthand for the vector
g_{i}(\bm{x})
,
H(\bm{x})
is a shorthand for the symmetric matrix
H_{ij}(\bm{x})\in\mathrm{Sym}(3)
,
|\det H|
is the Jacobian of the local mapping from the gradient field to real space, and
\Theta(H)
represents the positive curvature constraint.

In this case, the one-point statistic is given by

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{\rm pk}=\langle n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x})\rangle

(122)

while the
n
-point statistics are naturally defined as expectation values of products of this operator.
That is, the two-point function is

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm pk}^{(2)}(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})=\left\langle n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x}_{1})\,n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x}_{2})\right\rangle

(123)

and the three-point function is

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm pk}^{(3)}(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\bm{x}_{3})=\left\langle n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x}_{1})\,n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x}_{2})\,n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x}_{3})\right\rangle

(124)

Assuming statistical isotropy and homogeneity, the two-point function depends only on the distance
r=|\bm{x}_{1}-\bm{x}_{2}|
, and the usual two-point correlation function is defined by

\displaystyle 1+\xi_{\rm pk}(r)=\frac{\rho_{\rm pk}^{(2)}(r)}{\bar{n}_{\rm pk}^{2}}

(125)

Similarly, the three-point statistic becomes a function of the three sides of the triangle,

\displaystyle r_{12}=|\bm{x}_{1}-\bm{x}_{2}|,\qquad r_{23}=|\bm{x}_{2}-\bm{x}_{3}|,\qquad r_{31}=|\bm{x}_{3}-\bm{x}_{1}|

(126)

and the connected three-point correlation function can be defined as

\displaystyle\zeta_{\rm pk}(r_{12},r_{23},r_{31})=\frac{\rho_{\rm pk}^{(3)}(r_{12},r_{23},r_{31})}{\bar{n}_{\rm pk}^{3}}-\sum_{\rm cyc}\xi_{\rm pk}(r_{ij})-1

(127)

What is important is that these quantities are not merely “correlations of peak positions.”
As can be seen from Eq. (
121
), the existence of a peak is given by the product of three elements: the zero-gradient condition, the curvature constraint, and the local volume element.
Therefore, the two-point and three-point correlations should also be understood as higher-order versions of these three elements.
That is, what is newly required in
n
-point statistics is the joint distribution extending over two or three points, while the basic structure of the curvature-conditioned point process itself is preserved as it is.

Such a structure of
n
-point statistics can also be understood as a natural extension of the framework of the BBKS theory.
In fact, in studies after BBKS, while preserving the formulation based on the same local variables (amplitude, gradient, and curvature tensor), extensions have mainly been developed in four directions.
Namely, (i) direct extension to
n
-point correlations, (ii) peak correlation theory as its refinement, (iii) non-Gaussian extension as a generalization of the distribution, and (iv) extension introducing scale structure.
What is important in these existing extensions is that none of them changes the definition of peaks itself, but rather extends the treatment of the joint distribution of local variables.
That is, the basic structure consisting of the zero-gradient condition, the curvature constraint, and the Jacobian factor remains invariant, and what is deformed is the explicit form of the distribution
p_{n}
and its scale dependence.

The
n
-point statistics of the curvature-conditioned point process introduced in this chapter provide a natural framework that uniformly contains these extensions.
That is, the essence of
n
-point statistics lies not in the differences among individual theoretical approximations, but in the decomposition

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\text{preserved geometric structure}\\[4.0pt]
+\\[4.0pt]
\text{extension of the joint distribution}\end{array}

From this viewpoint, the BBKS theory is repositioned as a closed-form evaluation in a particular limit, and the theory of the present paper makes explicit the general structure underlying it.

VI.2
General Structure of the Two-Point Correlation

We first consider the two-point correlation.
Let us abbreviate the local variables at positions
\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2}
as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{a}=(\rho_{a},g_{a},H_{a}),\qquad a=1,2

(131)

Then the two-point peak correlation can be written as

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm pk}^{(2)}(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})=\int{\rm d}\Gamma_{1}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{2}\,|\det H_{1}|\,|\det H_{2}|\,p_{2}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})\,\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g_{1})\,\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g_{2})\,\Theta(H_{1})\,\Theta(H_{2})

(132)

Here
p_{2}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})
is the joint distribution of the local variables at the two points.
Moreover,
{\rm d}\Gamma_{a}
represents the measure of the local variables at each point, and may be understood explicitly as

\displaystyle{\rm d}\Gamma_{a}={\rm d}\rho_{a}\,{\rm d}^{3}g_{a}\,{\rm d}H_{a}

(133)

This expression is the natural higher-order version of the general one-point formula.
The preserved structure is clear.
First, at each point there is the zero-gradient condition selecting stationary points.
Second, at each point there is the positive curvature constraint selecting peaks.
Third, at each point there is the Jacobian factor
|\det H|
.
What is newly required in higher-order statistics is only the joint distribution
p_{2}
connecting these elements.

This structure is precisely the lifting of the principle of “preserved structure and deformed structure” organized in Section
V
to the two-point statistics.
That is, in Gaussian theory
p_{2}
is concretized as a finite-dimensional Gaussian distribution, whereas in the general theory the explicit form of
p_{2}
is not fixed.
Therefore, Eq. (
132
) itself is the fundamental expression for the two-point peak correlation beyond BBKS.

Furthermore, assuming statistical homogeneity,
p_{2}
does not depend on the absolute positions of each point, but only on the separation vector

\displaystyle\bm{r}=\bm{x}_{2}-\bm{x}_{1}

(134)

Therefore,

\displaystyle p_{2}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})=p_{2}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2};\bm{r})

(135)

can be written, and under isotropy it becomes further a function of
r=|\bm{r}|
.
As a result, the problem of the two-point correlation is reduced to the problem of evaluating, as a function of the distance
r
, the joint distribution of the curvature-conditioned point process defined at two points.

This general structure directly corresponds to attempts to extend the BBKS framework to
n
-point statistics while preserving its structure
(
9
, e.g.,)
.
In particular, in studies that extend the local variables introduced in BBKS (amplitude, gradient, and curvature tensor) directly to two points and evaluate the two-point correlation function of peaks, an integral structure isomorphic to the present expression appears.
There, the joint distribution
p_{2}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2})
is concretized as a finite-dimensional Gaussian distribution and is evaluated explicitly as a tensor kernel constructed from the density correlation function and its spatial derivatives.
From this viewpoint, the existing theory of the two-point peak correlation corresponds to a concretization of the present expression under a particular assumed family of distributions for
p_{2}
.
That is, the
n
-point extensions after BBKS do not introduce a new structure, but are understood as attempts to evaluate Eq. (
132
) by choosing the explicit form of the joint distribution.
Therefore, the theoretical core of the two-point peak correlation lies not in the details of individual approximations, but in the decomposition

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{c}\text{geometric constraints}\\[2.0pt]
(\text{zero gradient, curvature, Jacobian})\end{array}\\[6.0pt]
+\\[4.0pt]
\text{joint distribution }p_{2}\end{array}

By making this decomposition explicit, the framework of the present paper positions the BBKS result and its extensions within a more general curvature-conditioned point process.

VI.3
Two-point correlation in the linear Gaussian limit

In the linear Gaussian limit, one has, as used in Section
III
,

\displaystyle\eta_{i}=\partial_{i}\delta,\qquad H_{ij}=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\delta

That is, in this limit, the logarithmic density gradient used in the general formulation,
g_{i}=\partial_{i}\ln\rho
, reduces to
\eta_{i}=\partial_{i}\delta
.
In this case, the two-point joint distribution is completely determined by a finite number of covariance functions.
The fundamental quantity is the density correlation

\displaystyle\xi(r)=\langle\delta(\bm{x})\delta(\bm{x}+\bm{r})\rangle,

(140)

and other covariances are obtained by spatial derivatives thereof.

For example, the two-point covariance of gradients is

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{i}(\bm{x})\eta_{j}(\bm{x}+\bm{r})\rangle=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\xi(r),

(141)

and the cross-correlation between the density and the curvature tensor is

\displaystyle\langle\delta(\bm{x})H_{ij}(\bm{x}+\bm{r})\rangle=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\xi(r).

(142)

Similarly, the correlation between curvature tensors is given by

\displaystyle\langle H_{ij}(\bm{x})H_{kl}(\bm{x}+\bm{r})\rangle=\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\xi(r)

(143)

This equation (
143
) is of central importance.
Just as in one-point statistics the variance of the curvature tensor is sensitive to
k^{4}P(k)
, in two-point statistics the curvature-tensor correlation is determined by the fourth-order derivatives of the correlation function.
Therefore, the peak two-point correlation is not merely a thresholded version of the density two-point correlation, but rather a quantity that probes the spatial correlation of the local compression structure of the underlying field.
This fact most clearly demonstrates that the present theory is grounded in curvature geometry, going beyond the BBKS-type picture of “the positional correlation of high peaks.”

Indeed, substituting the Gaussian finite-dimensional distribution into Eq. (
132
), the two-point peak correlation is not given solely by
\xi(r)
, but rather by a set of tensor kernels constructed from

\displaystyle\xi(r),\quad\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\xi(r),\quad\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\xi(r).

(144)

Thus, the essence of the peak two-point correlation lies in the correlation between “local compression centers,” rather than the correlation between “high-density points.”
The BBKS result should therefore be understood as a special case in which this general structure is explicitly evaluated under the Gaussian and linear assumptions.

The explicit evaluation in this Gaussian limit has been realized in studies that extend the BBKS methodology directly to two-point statistics.
In particular,
9
constructed a finite-dimensional Gaussian distribution composed of the density field and its first and second derivatives, and computed the two-point correlation function under the peak constraint.
In that approach, the joint distribution of local variables at two points is completely determined by the covariance function
\xi(r)
and its spatial derivatives, and the integral corresponding to Eq. (
132
) is explicitly evaluated.
Therefore, the general expression of the present paper reduces to these existing results when a Gaussian distribution is assumed.
Furthermore, the structure in the Gaussian limit has been refined in subsequent developments of peak correlation theory.
For example,
6
analyzed in detail the two-point correlation and bias structure of peaks, systematically clarifying how the coupling between amplitude and curvature appears through spatial derivative structures.
Within the present framework, these results correspond to specifying the joint distribution
p_{2}
as Gaussian and further expanding its long-wavelength response.

From the above, the peak two-point correlation in the linear Gaussian limit can be understood as

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{c}\text{general structure of}\\[2.0pt]
\text{curvature-conditioned point processes}\end{array}\\[6.0pt]
+\\[4.0pt]
\text{Gaussian joint distribution}\end{array}

Accordingly, existing extensions of BBKS and their refinements are unified in the present theory as special cases corresponding to particular choices of the distribution
p_{2}
.

VI.4
Interpretation of the two-point correlation from transport geometry

As discussed in Section
II
and Section
V
, the logarithmic density is related to the Jacobian of the transport map as

\displaystyle\ln\rho=\ln\rho_{0}-\ln\det\nabla T.

(149)

Therefore, the curvature tensor
H_{ij}
measures the spatial variation of the transport Jacobian.

From this viewpoint, the two-point correlation
\rho_{\rm pk}^{(2)}
measures how often “maxima of local volume compression” occur simultaneously at two positions.

If two points belong to the same global convergent flow, the spatial variation of the transport Jacobian is correlated, resulting in an enhancement of the peak two-point correlation.
Conversely, if the two points belong to independent flows, the correlation is suppressed.
In this sense, the peak two-point correlation is a quantity that observes the geometry of global transport flows through the correlation of local compression centers.

This interpretation provides a feature absent in the usual density two-point correlation.
While the density two-point correlation measures amplitude correlations of the field, the peak two-point correlation measures how that amplitude is realized in terms of local geometric structure.
Thus, the two are not competing statistics but complementary ones.
The former measures “how similar the density values are,” whereas the latter measures “how similar the local compression structures are.”

VI.5
General structure of the three-point correlation

Next, we consider the three-point correlation.
Let the local variables at positions
\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\bm{x}_{3}
be

\displaystyle\Gamma_{a}=(\rho_{a},g_{a},H_{a}),\qquad a=1,2,3.

(150)

Then the three-point peak function is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm pk}^{(3)}(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\bm{x}_{3})=\int{\rm d}\Gamma_{1}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{2}\,{\rm d}\Gamma_{3}\,\prod_{a=1}^{3}\left[|\det H_{a}|\,\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g_{a})\,\Theta(H_{a})\right]p_{3}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3})

(151)

where
{\rm d}\Gamma_{a}={\rm d}\rho_{a}\,{\rm d}^{3}g_{a}\,{\rm d}H_{a}
.

This expression is a natural generalization of the two-point correlation.
The preserved structure is the same: at each point, the zero-gradient condition, the positive-curvature constraint, and the Jacobian factor appear.
The only new ingredient is the three-point joint distribution
p_{3}
.
Under statistical homogeneity and isotropy, this distribution depends only on the triangle configuration, i.e., the three side lengths
r_{12},r_{23},r_{31}
.
Therefore, the three-point peak correlation can be understood as the probability that three local compression centers simultaneously occur under a given triangle configuration.

This structure is even more independent of the BBKS-type picture than in the two-point case.
This is because
in the three-point function, simple height selection is no longer sufficient, and the consistency of local geometry among the three points becomes directly relevant
.
Thus, the three-point peak correlation is a geometric statistic that measures how the curvature tensor field correlates over triangular configurations.

Such
n
-point structures can be connected to conventional perturbative frameworks by assuming specific forms of the distribution.
In particular, by employing Edgeworth-type or Gram–Charlier-type expansions around a Gaussian distribution, higher-order cumulants can be systematically incorporated into the joint distribution
p_{3}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3})
.
These methods are widely used in the analysis of
n
-point statistics and non-Gaussianity of density fields.
However, it is important to emphasize that such distributional expansions do not alter the structure of Eq. (
151
) itself.
That is, the geometric structure consisting of the zero-gradient condition, curvature constraint, and Jacobian factor remains invariant, and only the specific form of the joint distribution
p_{3}
changes.
In this sense, non-Gaussian extensions should be understood not as deformations of the structure, but as deformations of the distribution.

Therefore, also for the three-point correlation,

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\text{preserved geometric structure}\\[4.0pt]
+\\[4.0pt]
\text{joint distribution }p_{3}\end{array}

provides the fundamental decomposition.
From this viewpoint, conventional theories of
n
-point statistics can be unified as specific realizations of the general expressions of the present work under particular assumptions on the distribution.

VI.6
Three-point correlation in the linear Gaussian limit and its characteristics

When the underlying field
\delta
is Gaussian, the connected three-point function of the linear field itself vanishes.
However, since the peak point process is the nonlinear operator

\displaystyle n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x})=|\det H(\bm{x})|\,\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g(\bm{x}))\,\Theta(H(\bm{x})),

(155)

its three-point function does not automatically vanish even if the underlying field is Gaussian.
This is an important point.
That is, the peak three-point correlation is not necessarily a “direct indicator of primordial non-Gaussianity,” but first of all represents the geometric correlation of a point-process operator subject to nonlinear conditioning.

In the Gaussian limit,
p_{3}
itself is a finite-dimensional Gaussian distribution, but the integrand of Eq. (
151
) is strongly nonlinear.
Therefore, the connected three-point correlation can in general satisfy

\displaystyle\zeta_{\rm pk}^{(\mathrm{G})}\neq 0.

(156)

This fact shows that the present theory goes beyond the dichotomy of “Gaussian or non-Gaussian” and explicitly treats the nonlinearity of the point process itself.

Furthermore, when the underlying distribution deviates from Gaussianity, the distribution
p_{3}
itself contains irreducible three-body correlations.
In that case, the three-point peak correlation is decomposed as

\displaystyle\zeta_{\rm pk}=\zeta_{\rm pk}^{(\mathrm{cond})}+\Delta\zeta_{\rm pk}.

(157)

Here, the first term is the geometric contribution as a curvature-conditioned point process, and the second term represents the additional contribution arising from the non-Gaussian deformation of the joint distribution.
In this formulation, the second term is understood in a general sense as the deviation from the Gaussian reference distribution.
Through this decomposition, the three-point peak correlation provides a setting in which both the “nonlinearity of the point process” and the “non-Gaussianity of the underlying field” can be discussed separately.

This decomposition is a concrete realization, at the level of three-point statistics, of the basic principle that has repeatedly appeared in this paper:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\text{preserved structure}\\[4.0pt]
+\\[4.0pt]
\text{deformation of the distribution}\end{array}

That is,
\zeta_{\rm pk}^{(\mathrm{cond})}
is a universal contribution originating from the structure and does not depend on the specific form of the distribution.
By contrast,
\Delta\zeta_{\rm pk}
corresponds to the deviation of the joint distribution
p_{3}
from its Gaussian form and is characterized by the higher-order cumulants of the distribution.

From this viewpoint, many conventional treatments of non-Gaussian statistics may be understood as having focused primarily on the evaluation of
\Delta\zeta_{\rm pk}
.
For example, by using Edgeworth-type or Gram–Charlier-type expansions around a Gaussian distribution, it is possible to systematically introduce non-Gaussian corrections to
p_{3}
.
What is important in the framework of the present paper, however, is that all such corrections are included in the second term and are clearly separated from the geometric structure of the first term.

Therefore, in the physical interpretation of the three-point peak correlation, it is essential to
distinguish between the geometric correlation arising from the nonlinearity of the point-process operator and the distributional correlation arising from the non-Gaussianity of the underlying field
.
Through this separation, peak statistics are understood not merely as indicators of non-Gaussianity, but as geometric statistics that probe the spatial arrangement itself of local compression structures.

VI.7
Hierarchy of
n
-point statistics and its theoretical significance

From the above, it follows that both the peak two-point correlation and the peak three-point correlation can be formulated in a unified manner as higher-order correlations of a curvature-conditioned point process.
The general
n
-point function is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm pk}^{(n)}(\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{n})=\left\langle\prod_{a=1}^{n}n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x}_{a})\right\rangle,

(161)

and its general expression can be written as

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm pk}^{(n)}=\int\prod_{a=1}^{n}\left[{\rm d}\Gamma_{a}\,|\det H_{a}|\,\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g_{a})\,\Theta(H_{a})\right]p_{n}(\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{n}).

(162)

Therefore, even when one proceeds from one-point statistics to the two-point correlation and to the three-point correlation, the skeleton of the theory does not change at all.
What changes is only the order of the joint distribution
p_{n}
.

This means that the theory developed in this paper is not essentially confined to a “theory of peak number density.”
Rather, the present theory is a theory of
n
-point statistics of local compression structures arising from transport geometry.
In the BBKS line of discussion, the peak number density and the bias of high peaks were central, but from the standpoint of the present theory they are merely the first step of the
n
-point hierarchy.
The general structures of the two-point and three-point correlations obtained in this section provide the foundation for proceeding toward a broader statistical theory including primordial non-Gaussianity, nonlinear transport, finite-dimensional curvature, and scale flow.

An important point of this hierarchical structure is that the extension of
n
-point statistics is realized not by introducing a new geometric structure, but by repetition of the existing local constraints and measure structure.
That is, the basic elements—the zero-gradient condition, the curvature constraint, and the Jacobian factor—remain unchanged even at the
n
-point level, and the complexity of the theory is entirely concentrated in the joint distribution
p_{n}
.
In this sense, the hierarchy of
n
-point statistics should be understood as a two-layer structure consisting of “fixed structure” and “extension of the distribution.”

From this viewpoint, the theoretical developments since BBKS can essentially be organized as different directions concerning the treatment of
p_{n}
.
Direct extensions to the two-point and three-point correlations appear as an increase in the dimensionality of
p_{n}

(

.

Furthermore, in frameworks that unify excursion-set theory and peak theory, scale-dependent joint distributions

On the other hand, in multivariate extensions that introduce multiple random variables into the collapse condition, the joint distribution

In this way, both extensions in the scale direction and extensions in the variable direction can be understood in a unified manner as directions that enrich the structure of the joint distribution
p_{n}

Accordingly, the theoretical significance of

Within this framework, the one-point statistic, the two-point correlation, and the three-point correlation are not independent objects, but are understood in a unified way as different levels of the same structure.
The above results therefore reposition peak statistics from being merely statistics of extrema to a theory of

VI.8
Peak
n
-point statistics as response functions

From the discussion so far, the peak statistics are represented as a curvature-conditioned point process,

\displaystyle n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x})=|\det H(\bm{x})|\,\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g(\bm{x}))\,\Theta(H(\bm{x})),

(168)

and its one-point, two-point, and three-point statistics are defined as the expectation value of this operator and the expectation values of its higher-order products, respectively.
Pushing this structure one step further, all these
n
-point statistics can be understood in a unified manner as a hierarchy of response functions to a long-wavelength background field.

As already seen in Section
IV
, the response of the one-point statistic to a long-wavelength background field
\delta_{L}
is defined by

\displaystyle b_{\rm pk}=\dfrac{\partial\ln n_{\rm pk}}{\partial\delta_{L}},

(169)

and in the Gaussian limit is given as the sum of the amplitude response and the curvature response.
This quantity is the lowest-order response coefficient for the curvature-conditioned point process.

Extending the same idea to the two-point function, the response of the peak two-point correlation is defined as

\displaystyle R_{\rm pk}^{(2)}(r)=\dfrac{\partial\ln\rho_{\rm pk}^{(2)}(r)}{\partial\delta_{L}}

(170)

or equivalently as

\displaystyle R_{\xi}(r)=\dfrac{\partial\ln[1+\xi_{\rm pk}(r)]}{\partial\delta_{L}}.

(171)

This quantity is the response coefficient that measures how a background density fluctuation modulates the joint appearance probability of two local compression centers.
Whereas the one-point bias was the “response of the peak number density,” the issue here is the “response of the coupling between peaks.”

Similarly, for the three-point statistic one may define

\displaystyle R_{\rm pk}^{(3)}(r_{12},r_{23},r_{31})=\dfrac{\partial\ln\rho_{\rm pk}^{(3)}(r_{12},r_{23},r_{31})}{\partial\delta_{L}}.

(172)

This describes how the background field modulates the joint appearance probability of three local compression centers on a triangular configuration.
Therefore, the three-point response is not merely the magnitude of the three-point correlation, but a quantity that gives the susceptibility of the three-body geometry to the background field.

Generalizing these definitions, for the
n
-point peak statistic

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm pk}^{(n)}(\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{n})=\left\langle\prod_{a=1}^{n}n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x}_{a})\right\rangle,

(173)

its response to the long-wavelength background field may be defined as

\displaystyle R_{\rm pk}^{(n)}(\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{n})=\dfrac{\partial\ln\rho_{\rm pk}^{(n)}(\bm{x}_{1},\dots,\bm{x}_{n})}{\partial\delta_{L}}.

(174)

In this way, the entire peak statistics are reorganized as the response hierarchy

\displaystyle\left\{n_{\rm pk},\rho_{\rm pk}^{(2)},\rho_{\rm pk}^{(3)},\dots\right\}\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad\left\{R_{\rm pk}^{(1)},R_{\rm pk}^{(2)},R_{\rm pk}^{(3)},\dots\right\},

(175)

where
R_{\rm pk}^{(1)}\equiv b_{\rm pk}
.

The advantage of this response hierarchy is that each order of peak statistics is understood not as a mere collection of unrelated formulas, but as a sequence of susceptibilities to the background field.
In particular, although in the one-point statistic the decomposition into amplitude response and curvature response appeared, the same structure is preserved in higher-order statistics as well.
That is, in the two-point response and the three-point response, the background field does not merely change the amplitude, but deforms the joint distribution of the curvature tensors themselves.
As a result, the response coefficient generally takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\text{amplitude response}\\[4.0pt]
+\\[4.0pt]
\text{curvature response}\\[4.0pt]
+\\[4.0pt]
\text{response of the joint structure}\end{array}

Whereas in the one-point statistic the last term was trivial, in higher-order statistics this response of the joint structure plays an essential role.

In the linear Gaussian limit, these response functions can be evaluated explicitly as derivatives of finite-dimensional Gaussian measures.
For example, the two-point response is determined through the
\delta_{L}
dependence of the two-point joint distribution
p_{2}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2};r)
appearing in Eq. (
132
), and its leading terms are determined by

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\xi(r)}{\partial\delta_{L}},\qquad\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\delta_{L}}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\xi(r),\qquad\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\delta_{L}}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\xi(r).

(181)

Therefore, the two-point response is not a quantity that probes the density correlation itself, but rather one that measures how the correlation of local compression structures is modulated by the background field.
Similarly, in the three-point response, the background-field dependence of the three-point joint distribution
p_{3}
plays the central role, and the geometric susceptibility for each triangle configuration is thereby defined.

From this viewpoint, the position of BBKS becomes even clearer.
The peak number density, peak curvature distribution, and large-scale bias given by BBKS all correspond to the first few levels of this response hierarchy.
The characteristic feature of the present theory of curvature statistics, however, is that it makes explicit the underlying general structure as

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\text{curvature-conditioned point process}\\[2.0pt]
\downarrow\\[4.0pt]
\text{$n$-point statistics}\\[2.0pt]
\downarrow\\[4.0pt]
\text{response hierarchy}\end{array}

Therefore, the final form of the present theory lies in understanding peak statistics not as a collection of individual formulas, but as a theory of response functions for a curvature-selected point process.

This formulation in terms of response functions also provides a natural basis for future extensions.
Primordial non-Gaussianity appears as higher-order mixed terms in the response functions, while nonlinear transport deforms the response kernels through the effective curvature depending on the background field.
Moreover, the introduction of finite-dimensional curvature and weighted measures can be understood as degrees of freedom controlling which types of structure are emphasized in the response.
In this sense, the theory developed in the present paper not only reconstructs peak statistics on the basis of optimal transport and entropic curvature, but also provides a framework that reorganizes its entire
n
-point statistics as a hierarchy of response functions.

VII
Discussion and Outlook

In this work, we have reformulated the peak statistics of cosmological density fields within the framework of curvature statistics based on optimal transport and entropy geometry.
The starting point is to regard the density field not as a function but as a probability measure, and to describe its deformation as a transport in Wasserstein geometry.
In this formulation, the Hessian of the logarithmic density naturally appears as the local response of the entropy functional,

\displaystyle H_{ij}=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\ln\rho

which serves as the fundamental definition of the curvature tensor in this paper.

Based on this curvature tensor, peaks are no longer merely density maxima, but are geometric objects defined as points satisfying

\displaystyle g_{i}=0,\qquad H\in\mathcal{C}_{+}

where
g_{i}=\partial_{i}\ln\rho
is the logarithmic density gradient introduced in Section
II
, which reduces to
\eta_{i}=\partial_{i}\delta
used in Section
III
in the linear Gaussian limit.
Furthermore, the peak number density is given as a curvature-conditioned point process,

\displaystyle n_{\rm pk}=\int{\rm d}\rho\,{\rm d}^{3}g\,{\rm d}H\,|\det H|\,p(\rho,g,H)\,\delta_{\rm D}^{(3)}(g)\,\Theta(H)

As this expression shows, the essence of peak statistics lies in the combination of three elements,

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\text{probability distribution}\\[4.0pt]
\times\\[4.0pt]
\text{curvature constraint}\\[4.0pt]
\times\\[4.0pt]
\text{geometric measure}\end{array}

This general structure does not depend on Gaussianity.
The assumption of a Gaussian random field only serves to close the joint distribution
p(\rho,g,H)
in terms of a finite number of spectral moments.
Therefore, the BBKS theory is positioned as a special case in which explicit evaluation becomes possible under the linear Gaussian condition, within the general theory of curvature-conditioned point processes.

In this paper, we have further extended this framework to
n
-point statistics.
The two-point and three-point peak correlations are defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm pk}^{(2)}(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})

\displaystyle=\left\langle n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x}_{1})n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x}_{2})\right\rangle,

(192)

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm pk}^{(3)}(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\bm{x}_{3})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\prod_{a=1}^{3}n_{\rm pk}(\bm{x}_{a})\right\rangle

(193)

and are expressed as conditional integrals over the joint distribution of the curvature tensor field.
Through this structure, peak statistics are understood not as mere positional statistics, but as geometric statistics describing the spatial correlations of local compression structures.

The final outcome of this work is that these
n
-point statistics can be understood in a unified manner as a hierarchy of response functions.
Defining the response to a long-wavelength background field
\delta_{L}
as

\displaystyle R_{\rm pk}^{(n)}=\dfrac{\partial\ln\rho_{\rm pk}^{(n)}}{\partial\delta_{L}},

(194)

one obtains the response hierarchy

\displaystyle R_{\rm pk}^{(1)}=b_{\rm pk},\quad R_{\rm pk}^{(2)},\quad R_{\rm pk}^{(3)},\ \dots

(195)

Thus, the entire peak theory is reconstructed as a response-function theory of curvature-selected point processes.

Within this reconstruction, the position of the BBKS theory becomes clear.
Namely, BBKS corresponds to a closed-form solution obtained under the conditions

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\text{curvature-conditioned point process}\\[4.0pt]
+\\[4.0pt]
\text{linear limit}\\[4.0pt]
+\\[4.0pt]
\text{Gaussian closure}\\[4.0pt]
+\\[4.0pt]
\text{fixed scale}\end{array}

It therefore represents one slice of the general theory developed in this paper.
Accordingly, the present framework naturally allows extensions to general curvature statistics beyond the linear Gaussian regime:

1.

By defining peaks in terms of logarithmic density curvature, nonlinear structures are described in the same formalism.

2.

Non-Gaussianity is incorporated as a deformation of the joint distribution
p(\rho,g,H)
, while the three-element decomposition remains intact.

3.

By introducing a smoothing scale
R
, curvature statistics can be understood as flows in scale space.

Furthermore, by introducing the weighted measure

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mu_{N}=\rho(\bm{x})^{N}{\rm d}^{3}x

new statistical quantities based on finite-dimensional curvature can be defined.

As future directions, it is important to derive explicit evaluation formulas for non-Gaussian distributions.
In particular, it is essential to clarify how primordial non-Gaussianity and nonlinear gravitational evolution appear in the joint distribution of the curvature tensor and in the response-function hierarchy.
In this context, comparison with recent studies that provide systematic perturbative expansions of statistical quantities
(
18
, e.g.,)
is of interest.
While the framework of this paper is a geometric formulation that does not rely on perturbative expansion, the two approaches are complementary from the viewpoint of explicit expansion of joint distributions and evaluation of response functions, and their integration is expected to lead to further theoretical developments.
Moreover, by constructing explicit equations for scale flow, it becomes possible to describe structure formation as a dynamical system in curvature geometry.
Observationally, measurements of curvature statistics for galaxy and halo distributions constitute an important application.
In particular, formulations that incorporate finite-sample effects and observational window functions are intrinsically consistent with the measure-theoretic framework developed in this paper.

In conclusion, the present work reconstructs peak theory from a mere statistics of extrema into a geometric response theory based on optimal transport and entropic curvature.
This unified perspective interprets cosmological structure formation as the transport of probability measures and positions peak statistics as the response of local compression structures.
This framework connects statistical and geometric methods, and is expected to open new directions for both theoretical and observational studies.
