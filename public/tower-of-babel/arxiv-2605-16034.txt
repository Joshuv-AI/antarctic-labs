Walking Sudakov: From Cusp to Octagon Dedication: Dedicated to George Sterman on the occasion of his Octogintennial
Luis F. Alday, Elisabetta Armanini, Andrei V. Belitsky, Kelian Häring, Alexander Zhiboedov

Abstract
We study the Sudakov form factor and the four-point scattering amplitude on the Coulomb branch of planar 𝒩 = 4 {\cal N}=4 SYM as functions of the Coulomb-branch parameters and kinematic invariants. This setup provides a controlled probe of the interpolation between on- and off-shell regimes of infrared-sensitive quantities in gauge theories. We identify a novel scaling limit in which both observables exhibit double-logarithmic behavior governed by a walking anomalous dimension. As the mass scales are varied, this walking anomalous dimension interpolates between the cusp anomalous dimension of the on-shell regime and the octagon anomalous dimension of the off-shell regime. Based on the explicit two-loop result and the expected all-order structure, we propose an all-loop form for the walking anomalous dimension both for the form factor and for the four-point scattering amplitude. These all-loop expressions depend on new, presently unknown functions of the ’t Hooft coupling.

3.2
From the cusp to the octagon

To start with, let us recall the known high-energy limits of the Sudakov form factor, highlighting its exponentiation property
66
. The behavior of the form factor changes depending on which of the two masses is set to zero, with the remaining mass parameter small compared to
Q

For

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{F}_{2}=-\frac{\Gamma_{\rm cusp}(g)}{4}\log^{2}\frac{Q^{2}}{m^{2}}+\Gamma_{\text{coll}}(g)\log\frac{Q^{2}}{m^{2}}+R_{\rm on}(g)\,.

the appearance of the walking anomalous dimension.

The scattering amplitude
A
can be conveniently written as

\displaystyle A(s,t,m,M)

\displaystyle=g_{YM}^{2}\,\mathcal{M}(s,t,m,M,g)\,,

(76)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle=1+g^{2}\mathcal{M}^{(1)}+g^{4}\mathcal{M}^{(2)}+\dots\,.

In this setup, the amplitude is also a function of the mass of the external particles: the existence of this extra parameter is crucial to explore a new type of high-energy limit of the amplitude that we describe next.

4.2
From the cusp to the octagon

Let us first review known facts about the scattering amplitude on the Coulomb branch of
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM that will be useful for the discussion of the next sections.

We are interested in the high-energy limit of the amplitude as
both

s
and
t
are taken to be large. The simplest limit to consider is the one where we take the limit while keeping
m
and
M
non-zero and fixed.
In this case the amplitude exhibits the characteristic double-logarithmic behavior
4
;
3
;
31

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{M}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Gamma_{\text{cusp}}(g)}{2}\log s\log t+\mathcal{O}(\log s,\log t)\ .

(77)

In writing (
77
), it was important that
m
and
M
are nonzero and fixed as we take the limit. If we relax this condition, different behaviors are possible.

For example, a different double-logarithmic behavior emerges in the special case of
m=0
. In this case
32

\log\mathcal{M}=-\frac{\Gamma_{\text{oct}}(g)}{4}\log^{2}\left(\frac{s\,t}{M^{4}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}D_{0}(g)+\mathcal{O}(s^{-1},t^{-1})\,\ .

(78)

More generally, we can introduce a high-energy limit characterized by two exponents
(\eta_{s},\eta_{t})
, where as we take
s,t\to\infty
we keep
s^{\eta_{s}}/t^{\eta_{t}}
fixed. In particular,
\eta_{s}=\eta_{t}
corresponds to the familiar fixed-angle limit. In addition, as we go to high energies, we can scale the ratio of the internal mass
m
, and the external mass
M
. To characterize different behaviors we find it convenient to introduce the following dimensionless variables

\omega_{s}\equiv\frac{-s}{M^{2}}\,,\quad\omega_{t}\equiv\frac{-t}{M^{2}}\,,\quad y=\frac{m^{2}}{M^{2}}\ .

(79)

In terms of these dimensionless variables we consider a family of high-energy limits
\omega_{s},\omega_{t}\to\infty
with

\eta_{s}=-\frac{\log y}{\log\omega_{s}}\,,\quad\eta_{t}=-\frac{\log y}{\log\omega_{t}}\ ,

(80)

kept fixed.

In the next section, we will show that the walking anomalous dimension behavior found in the Sudakov form factor that interpolates between (
77
) and (
78
) generalizes to the
(m,M)
scattering amplitude on the Coulomb branch of
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM. We will present evidence that

\displaystyle\log\mathcal{M}=-\Gamma_{\text{walk}}(\eta_{s},\eta_{t},g)\log\omega_{s}\log\omega_{t}+\mathcal{O}(\log\omega_{s},\log\omega_{t})\,.

(81)

with
\Gamma_{\text{walk}}(\eta_{s},\eta_{t},g)
the walking anomalous dimension that interpolates between the cusp (as
\eta_{s},\eta_{t}\to 0
), and the octagon anomalous dimensions (as
\eta_{s},\eta_{t}\to 1
).

\Gamma_{\text{ansatz}}(\eta_{s},\eta_{t},g)=\tilde{\gamma}+\left(a_{1}\frac{\eta_{t}}{\eta_{s}}+a_{2}\frac{\eta_{s}}{\eta_{t}}\right)+\left(b_{1}\eta_{s}+b_{2}\eta_{t}\right)+c\,\eta_{s}\eta_{t}\,,

(110)

based on crossing symmetry and the fact that we expect at most double-logarithmic asymptotic behavior of the amplitude. We will focus on the case
\eta_{t}<\eta_{s}
, the complementary region can be obtained by crossing.

Region
0\leq\eta_{t}\leq\eta_{s}\leq 1

In this region we have to uplift the predicted all-loop expression for the
\eta_{s}=\eta_{t}
case of the Sudakov form factor

\Gamma_{\rm walk}(\eta,\eta,g)=2\left(\frac{\Gamma_{\rm cusp}}{4}+\gamma\,\eta+\left(\frac{\Gamma_{\rm oct}}{2}-\frac{\Gamma_{\rm cusp}}{4}-\gamma\right)\eta^{2}\right)\,,

(111)

to the
\eta_{s}\neq\eta_{t}
case

\Gamma_{\rm walk}(\eta_{s},\eta_{t},g)=2\left(\frac{\Gamma_{\rm cusp}}{4}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\,(\eta_{s}+\eta_{t})+\left(\frac{\Gamma_{\rm oct}}{2}-\frac{\Gamma_{\rm cusp}}{4}-\gamma\right)\eta_{s}\eta_{t}\right)\,.

(112)

In doing the uplift, we assumed that terms of the form
\eta_{s}/\eta_{t}
do not appear based on the expected double-logarithmic behavior in the limit
\eta_{s},\eta_{t}\to 0
.

Region
1\leq\eta_{t}\leq\eta_{s}

By matching
\Gamma_{\text{ansatz}}(\eta_{s},\eta_{t}=1,g)
to the behavior in the octagon region (
100
) for
\eta_{t}=1
we get

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{ansatz}}(\eta_{s},\eta_{t}=1,g)

\displaystyle=(\tilde{\gamma}+b_{2})+\frac{a_{1}}{\eta_{s}}+(a_{2}+b_{1}+c)\eta_{s}\equiv\frac{\Gamma_{\text{oct}}}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{2\eta_{s}}+\frac{\eta_{s}}{2}\right)\,,

(113)

which fixes three of the free parameters.

Region
0\leq\eta_{t}\leq 1\leq\eta_{s}

Finally, in the intermediate region
\Gamma_{\text{ansatz}}(\eta_{s}=1,\eta_{t},g)
can be matched with
\Gamma_{\text{walk}}(\eta_{s}=1,\eta_{t},g)
. This fixes two of the remaining free parameters, leaving two of them
\gamma,\tilde{\gamma}
unfixed.
The ansatz is then fixed to be

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{ansatz}}(\eta_{s},\eta_{t},g)=\left(\frac{\Gamma_{\text{cusp}}}{2}+\gamma\right)\eta_{s}(1-\eta_{t})+\frac{\Gamma_{\text{oct}}}{4}\frac{\eta_{t}}{\eta_{s}}(1+\eta_{s})^{2}+\tilde{\gamma}(\eta_{s}-1)(\eta_{t}-1)\,.

(114)

Region
-1\leq\eta_{t}\leq\eta_{s}\leq 0

For this region we have to make a different ansatz, valid for
\eta_{t},\eta_{s}\leq 0
, with
\Gamma_{\text{walk}}(\eta_{s},\eta_{t},g)\propto(1+\eta_{s})(1+\eta_{t})
.
This correctly vanishes at
\eta_{s}=\eta_{t}=-1
and, matching it with (
112
) at
\eta_{s}=\eta_{t}=0
, the proportionality coefficient is fixed to be
\Gamma_{\text{cusp}}/2
.

The all-loop prediction

The all-loop prediction for
\Gamma_{\text{walk}}(\eta_{s},\eta_{t},g)
in the
\eta_{t}<\eta_{s}
region is

\begin{cases}0,&\eta_{t}\leq\eta_{s}\leq 0,\,\,\eta_{t}\leq-1,\\
\frac{\Gamma_{\text{cusp}}(g)}{2}(1+\eta_{s})(1+\eta_{t}),&-1\leq\eta_{t}\leq\eta_{s}\leq 0,\\
\frac{\Gamma_{\text{cusp}}(g)}{2}+\gamma(g)(\eta_{s}+\eta_{t})+\left(\Gamma_{\text{oct}}(g)-\frac{\Gamma_{\text{cusp}}(g)}{2}-2\gamma(g)\right)\eta_{s}\eta_{t},&0\leq\eta_{t}\leq\eta_{s}\leq 1,\\[4.0pt]
\left(\frac{\Gamma_{\text{cusp}}(g)}{2}+\gamma(g)\right)\eta_{s}(1-\eta_{t})+\frac{\Gamma_{\text{oct}}(g)}{4}\frac{\eta_{t}}{\eta_{s}}(1+\eta_{s})^{2}+\tilde{\gamma}(g)(\eta_{s}-1)(\eta_{t}-1),&0\leq\eta_{t}\leq 1<\eta_{s},\\[8.0pt]
\frac{\Gamma_{\text{oct}}(g)}{2}\left(1+\dfrac{\eta_{t}}{2\eta_{s}}+\dfrac{\eta_{s}}{2\eta_{t}}\right),&1\leq\eta_{t}\leq\eta_{s}.\end{cases}

(115)

which reduces to (
109
) when using

\Gamma_{\text{cusp}}^{(1)}=4g^{2}\,,\quad\Gamma_{\text{oct}}^{(1)}=4g^{2}\,,\quad\gamma^{(1)}=2g^{2}\,,\quad\tilde{\gamma}^{(1)}=2g^{2}.

(116)

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we studied the interpolation of the Sudakov form factor and the four-point scattering amplitude on the Coulomb branch of
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM between the on-shell regime (internal mass
m\neq 0
, external mass
M=0
) controlled by the cusp anomalous dimension, and the off-shell regime (internal mass
m=0
, external mass
M\neq 0
) controlled by the octagon. To interpolate between the two we introduced a walking parameter, see Figure
1
and Figure
2
. To study observables with Euclidean virtuality
p_{i}^{2}=-M^{2}<0
, we effectively analytically continue the angles on
S^{5}
to imaginary values, see e.g.
45
;
46
;
63
. The corresponding walking anomalous dimension interpolates between the cusp anomalous dimension and the octagon. Our prediction for the form factor is given in (
69
), and for the four-point scattering amplitude in (
115
).

The results depend on four functions of the ’t Hooft coupling: the familiar

\Gamma_{\rm cusp}
,
\Gamma_{\rm oct}
, and two new functions
\gamma,\tilde{\gamma}
, which are not known at present. For the form factor, only

\gamma(g)
appears, and we calculated it up to two loops, see
(
67
). The four-point amplitude depends on an additional
unknown function
\tilde{\gamma}(g)
that we calculated up to one loop, see
(
116
).

It is well-known that the on- and off-shell regimes receive contributions from different pinch surfaces according to Libby-Sterman infrared power counting
79
. In other words, they are governed by different effective theories. The on-shell case receives leading contributions from the collinear and soft modes
k_{\rm s}\sim m
, while the off-shell case is driven instead by the collinear and ultrasoft ones
k_{\rm us}\sim M^{2}/Q
. The presence of the ultrasoft region threatens the potential applicability of perturbative techniques in QCD due to the coupling constant growth in the infrared. What we uncovered, however, is that the soft-collinear effective theory is valid over a very wide region of mass/virtuality parameter space as determined by the
y
-parameter. It determines the walking behavior of the form factor and four-leg amplitude. The ultrasoft-collinear theory, on the other hand, sets in only on the ‘shoulder’ of the asymptotic limit
y\ll 1/\omega
. This gives hope for future robust QCD applications relying on the factorization of soft-collinear modes from the hard remainder, but it shows an onset of nonperturbative physics as one increases off-shellness of partonic processes.

It is interesting to point out that the nature of collinear modes in the above two effective theories is quite different. Namely, in the soft-collinear case, even with non-vanishing masses and virtualities, factorization of full infrared observables into independent collinear functions is impossible without the use of supplemental regulators in addition to the conventional dimensional one. This is the consequence of the presence of well-known rapidity divergences. They are an artifact of the leading power expansion in the corresponding pinch regions. When these are mapped into the operator language, they are intimately related to the infinite length of light-like Wilson lines defining corresponding jet factors. Once proper regulators are introduced in the factorization formalism, they can be made well-defined
42
. On the other hand, for the ultrasoft-collinear theory, these do not arise at all since all Wilson lines are in fact cut off by the inverse off-shellness, making them finite
18
. Another distinguished feature of the ultrasoft-collinear theory is the lack of factorization of ultrasoft and collinear modes from each other. This is, however, hardly a limitation since there is no physical process where they can be measured separately. Thus, one can rely on the protected hard factorization to evaluate physical observables with both ultrasoft and collinear modes included in separate infrared factors.

Our results suggest several natural directions for further study.

•

Extending the form-factor analysis to three loops should be within reach
using the same techniques
20
. Similarly, the two-loop
analysis of the scattering amplitude should be accessible.

•

It would be very interesting to perform a strong-coupling analysis along
the lines of
4
;
5
. Such an analysis
faces an immediate puzzle: the strong-coupling limit of the octagon
anomalous dimension differs from that of the cusp anomalous dimension by
an additional factor of
\pi^{-1}
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm cusp}\Big|_{g\to\infty}=2g\,,\qquad\Gamma_{\rm oct}\Big|_{g\to\infty}=\frac{4g}{\pi}\,.

(117)

How does this factor arise from a classical string solution?

•

Both
\Gamma_{\rm cusp}(g)
and
\Gamma_{\rm oct}(g)
can be computed at finite ’t Hooft coupling using integrability of
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM. The cusp can be extracted by the so-called BES kernel
14
, while the octagon, first computed at finite coupling in
19
, can be computed from a deformation of the BES kernel. In particular, this deformation of the BES kernel, controlled by a parameter
\alpha
, was defined in
10
. Different values of
\alpha
capture different anomalous dimensions, for example the octagon for
\alpha=0
and the cusp for
\alpha=\pi/4
. This allows to define a tilted anomalous dimension
\Gamma_{\alpha}(g)
, which interpolates between different anomalous dimensions. It is natural to then try to connect the walking anomalous dimension with
\Gamma_{\alpha}(g)
, but the two are different and the connection is not obvious. In particular, the weak-coupling expansion of
\Gamma_{\alpha}(g)
contains higher powers of
\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha
as the loop order increases, while the walking anomalous dimension contains at most
\eta^{2}
terms. We checked that, matching one and two-loops and then using the conjectured relation between
\eta
and
\alpha
, the tilted cusp result does not reproduce, at three and higher loops, our prediction for the walking.

It is still an open question whether a connection of some sort between the two can be found and if the walking anomalous dimension can be computed using integrability of
\mathcal{N}=4
SYM.

•

In this work, we studied the observables in the Euclidean region,
including the spacelike external virtualities

p_{i}^{2}=-M^{2}<0
. From the perspective of
{\cal N}=4
SYM, this
involves analytically continuing the angles on
S^{5}
to imaginary
values. This analytic continuation has been considered previously,
see, for example,
45
;
46
;
63
.
A natural direction for future work is to extend our analysis to
timelike virtualities,
p_{i}^{2}>0
, where anomalous thresholds are expected
to play an important role
47
. It would also be very
interesting to generalize the finite-coupling bootstrap analysis of
Coulomb-branch scattering in
{\cal N}=4
SYM
2
to
nonzero external masses and virtualities.

•

High-energy hadron-hadron collisions with production of heavy vector bosons and/or Higgs at small transverse momentum are sensitive to the off-shellness of the initial-state partons that induce the hard process
33
;
35
;
104
. It would be interesting to construct factorization theorems for these cross sections and study their ‘walking’ to the collinear regime
36
, where colliding partons can be regarded as massless.

Appendix A
Detailed analysis of the regions of the one-loop amplitude for
s=t

In this appendix we present a detailed analysis of the different regions contributing to the double-logarithmic coefficient of the one-loop amplitude
\mathcal{M}^{(1)}(s,t)
in the double-scaling limit (
88
) for
s=t
. We consider separately the two cases
y<1
and
y>1
.

A.1
Case
y<1

In this case, equivalent to
\eta\geq 0
, the dispersion representation of the one-loop amplitude is

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}(s,t)=-2\,\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{1+2x\omega+2\sqrt{x\omega(1+x\omega)}}{1+4(x+1)y}\right)}{(1+4x/\omega)\sqrt{x+y}\sqrt{(x+1/\omega)}}\,.

(118)

The relevant scales for the integration variable
x
that determine the different behaviors of the integrand are

x\sim\omega\,,\quad x\sim\omega^{-1}\,,\quad x\sim y^{-1}\,,\quad x\sim y\,.

(119)

Let us analyze in detail what happens for
y>\omega^{-1}
, corresponding to the octagon-to-cusp transition and for
y<\omega^{-1}
, corresponding to the octagon shoulder.

A.1.1
Case
y>\omega^{-1}
: octagon-to-cusp transition

For
y>\omega^{-1}
we have the following ordering of the relevant scales

\omega^{-1}<y<y^{-1}<\omega\,,

(120)

and the integral can then be divided into the corresponding different regions. In each case we consider the proper simplification of the integrand, valid for that specific region, and identify the ones that give double logarithms.
Let us enumerate the different regions:

•

For
0<x<\omega^{-1}
:

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}(s,t)\Big|_{x<\omega^{-1}}=-2\,\int_{0}^{\omega^{-1}}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{1+2x\omega+2\sqrt{x\omega(1+x\omega)}}{1+4y}\right)}{\sqrt{y}\sqrt{(x+1/\omega)}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{y\omega}}\right)\,,

(121)

so this region does not contribute to the walking anomalous dimension.

•

For
\omega^{-1}<x<y
:

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}(s,t)\Big|_{\omega^{-1}<x<y}=-2\,\int_{\omega^{-1}}^{y}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{4x\omega}{1+4y}\right)}{\sqrt{x+y}\sqrt{x}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\log(\omega y)\right)\,,

(122)

and also this region is not relevant since it just gives single-logarithms.

•

The region
y<x<y^{-1}
gives

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}(s,t)\Big|_{y<x<y^{-1}}=-2\,\int_{y}^{y^{-1}}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{4x\omega}{1+4y}\right)}{x}=4\log y\log\omega+\mathcal{O}\left(\log(y)\right)\,,

(123)

and contributes to the expression of the walking anomalous dimension.

•

Also
y^{-1}<x<\omega

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}(s,t)\Big|_{y^{-1}<x<\omega}=-2\,\int_{y^{-1}}^{\omega}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{4x\omega}{4xy}\right)}{x}=-2\log^{2}\omega+2\log^{2}y\,,

(124)

contains double logarithms.

•

Finally
x>\omega
:

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}(s,t)\Big|_{x>\omega}=-2\,\int_{\omega}^{\infty}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{4x\omega}{4xy}\right)}{4x^{2}/\omega}=\mathcal{O}(\log(\omega/y))\,.

(125)

The expression of the walking anomalous dimension can be extracted from the sum of double logarithms, as presented in (
94
) in the main text.

A.1.2
Case
y<\omega^{-1}
: the octagon shoulder

On the octagon shoulder
y<\omega^{-1}
the relevant scale are ordered as follows

y<\omega^{-1}<\omega<y^{-1}\,.

(126)

As done before, we separate the integral into the different regions and approximate the integrand consistently.

•

The first region
0<x<y

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}(s,t)\Big|_{x<y}=-2\,\int_{0}^{y}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{1+2\sqrt{x\omega}}{1+4y}\right)}{\sqrt{y/\omega}}=\mathcal{O}\left(y\omega\right)\,,

(127)

does not contribute to the expression on the shoulder.

•

For
y<x<\omega^{-1}
:

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}(s,t)\Big|_{y<x<\omega^{-1}}=-2\,\int_{y}^{\omega^{-1}}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{1+2\sqrt{x\omega}}{1+4y}\right)}{\sqrt{x/\omega}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)\,.

(128)

•

The region
\omega^{-1}<x<\omega
is relevant, since it gives double logarithms

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}(s,t)\Big|_{\omega^{-1}<x<\omega}=-2\,\int_{\omega^{-1}}^{\omega}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{4x\omega}{1+4y}\right)}{x}=-4\log^{2}\omega+\mathcal{O}\left(y\right)\,.

(129)

•

The result for
\omega<x<y^{-1}

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}(s,t)\Big|_{\omega<x<y^{-1}}=-2\,\int_{\omega}^{y^{-1}}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{4x\omega}{1+4y}\right)}{4x^{2}/\omega}=\mathcal{O}(\log\omega)\,,

(130)

does not contribute.

•

Finally, for
x>y^{-1}
:

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}(s,t)\Big|_{x>y^{-1}}=-2\,\int_{y^{-1}}^{\infty}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{4x\omega}{4xy}\right)}{4x^{2}/\omega}=\mathcal{O}(y\omega\log(\omega/y))\,.

(131)

A.2
Case
y>1

To extract the interpolating anomalous dimension for
y>1
, corresponding to
\eta\leq 0
, the dispersion representation of the one-loop amplitude has to be simplified with
y\gg 1
. It reads

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}\Big|_{y\gg 1}=-2\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{(\sqrt{x\omega}+\sqrt{x\omega+4y(x+y)})^{2}}{4y(x+y)}\right)}{(1+4(x+y)/\omega)\sqrt{x+y}\sqrt{(x+4y(x+y)/\omega)}}\,,

(132)

with the relevant scales for the integration variable
x
given by

x\sim\omega\,,\quad x\sim\omega^{-1}\,,\quad x\sim y^{-1}\,,\quad x\sim y\,,

(133)

as before. We proceed as in the previous section by identifying the double logarithms in the case
1<y<\omega
, which describes the transition from the cusp to zero. The case of
y>\omega
is not analyzed since the result for the walking anomalous dimension is just
\Gamma_{\rm walk}^{(1)}(\eta\leq-1)=0
.

A.2.1
Case
1<y<\omega
:

The ordering of the relevant scales for the cusp-to-zero transition is

\omega^{-1}<y^{-1}<y<\omega\,.

(134)

Let us analyze in detail the different contributions.

•

The first region
0<x<\omega^{-1}
gives

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}\Big|_{x<\omega^{-1}}=-2\int_{0}^{\omega^{-1}}dx\,\frac{\log\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{x\omega}}{2y}\right)}{(1+4y/\omega)\sqrt{y}\,(2y/\sqrt{\omega})}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{y^{5/2}\sqrt{\omega}}\right)\,.

(135)

•

For the region
\omega^{-1}<x<y^{-1}

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}\Big|_{\omega^{-1}<x<y^{-1}}=-2\int_{\omega^{-1}}^{y^{-1}}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{(\sqrt{x\omega}+\sqrt{x\omega+4y^{2}})^{2}}{4y^{2}}\right)}{(1+4y/\omega)\sqrt{y}\sqrt{x+4y^{2}/\omega}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log\omega}{y}\right)\,,

(136)

and does not contribute.

•

The region
y^{-1}<x<y
gives

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}\Big|_{y^{-1}<x<y}=-2\int_{y^{-1}}^{y}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{(\sqrt{x\omega}+\sqrt{x\omega+4y^{2}})^{2}}{4y^{2}}\right)}{(1+4y/\omega)\sqrt{y}\sqrt{x+4y^{2}/\omega}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\log\omega,\log y\right)\,,

(137)

•

The region
y<x<\omega
contributes with a double logarithm:

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}\Big|_{y<x<\omega}=-2\int_{y}^{\omega}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{(\sqrt{x\omega}+\sqrt{x\omega+4xy})^{2}}{4xy}\right)}{\sqrt{x(x+4xy/\omega)}}=-2\log^{2}\left(\frac{\omega}{y}\right)+\mathcal{O}(y^{-1})\,.

(138)

•

Finally, for
\omega<x<\infty
:

\mathcal{M}^{(1)}\Big|_{\omega<x<\infty}=-2\int_{\omega}^{\infty}dx\,\frac{\log\left(\frac{\omega}{y}\right)}{4x^{2}/\omega}=\mathcal{O}\left(\log\frac{\omega}{y}\right)\,.

(139)

The only contribution to the interpolating anomalous dimension comes from the region
y<x<\omega
, as also reported in the main text (
97
).
