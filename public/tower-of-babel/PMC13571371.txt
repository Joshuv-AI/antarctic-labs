Rosenbaum Syndrome Successfully Treated With Left Bundle Branch Area Pacing

Intraventricular conduction abnormalities may reflect advanced involvement of the His-Purkinje conduction system and are associated with an increased risk of progression to high-degree atrioventricular (AV) block. The coexistence of right bundle branch block (RBBB) with alternating left anterior fascicular block (LAFB) and left posterior fascicular block (LPFB), also known as Rosenbaum syndrome,1 represents a serious and rare manifestation of infranodal conduction system disease. This condition is typically associated with progressive degenerative changes in the cardiac conduction system and is frequently linked to serious clinical outcomes, such as sudden cardiac death.

History of Presentation

A 45-year-old patient presented to his primary care physician because he had been experiencing mild shortness of breath during moderate physical activity for the past few months, with no other associated symptoms. The physical examination revealed no significant findings.

Past Medical History

He had a history of hypothyroidism, which was treated with hormone replacement therapy, and his father had a pacemaker for an unknown reason.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of dyspnea in a middle-aged man primarily includes pulmonary and cardiac conditions. The physical examination and medical history did not suggest pulmonary disease and his blood chemistry, complete blood count, and B-type natriuretic peptide levels were normal. A treadmill stress test was performed to rule out ischemia, with negative results. However, frequent premature ventricular contractions were observed during the test, so he was referred to the electrophysiology unit.

Investigation

A 24-hour Holter electrocardiogram (ECG), along with a surface ECG, was ordered to assess the arrhythmic burden of premature ventricular contractions. The main findings are shown in Figure 1. Because of the arrhythmic burden and the abnormalities detected on the 24-hour Holter ECG, cardiac imaging studies were performed, including transthoracic echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, to rule out structural heart disease; these studies revealed no abnormalities.

24-Hour Electrocardiogram

(A) Morning electrocardiogram. (B) The same day in the afternoon.

The patient's electrocardiographic tracings showed sinus rhythm, with a heart rate of 53 beats/min and a QRS duration of 132 ms. All beats were conducted with RBBB; however, there was a change in the QRS axis. Beats 1, 2, 3, 5, and 7 showed a QRS morphology of qRS in DI, qR in aVL, and rS in DII, DIII, and aVF, with an axis of −75°, consistent with LAFB. The fourth beat showed an even more extreme axis deviation to −105°, likely because of a more advanced degree of fascicular block. The sixth beat showed an abrupt change in the QRS axis and morphology: DI and aVL rS, DII and aVF qrs, and DIII qR. The QRS axis changed to +105°, consistent with left posterior fascicular block (LPFB) (Figure 1A).

The tracing in Figure 1B is from the same patient on the same day. The QRS axis alternates between LAFB (∗) and LPFB (arrow); RBBB is constantly present. The prolongation of the PR interval (⎴) is striking. This occurs during periods of posterior fascicular block, that is, when AV conduction is via the left anterior fascicle (Figure 2).

Based on the combined electrocardiographic and clinical findings, as well as the results of additional tests, a diagnosis of Rosenbaum syndrome was established. No reversible cause was identified.

Management

Given the high risk of developing complete AV block, it was decided to implant a dual-chamber pacemaker with a left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) protocol. Alternating bundle branch blocks or alternating fascicular blocks with fixed RBBB identified on surface ECG are conditions strongly associated with disease in the His-Purkinje system, which warrant pacing, even in asymptomatic patients, without resorting to invasive procedures such as an electrophysiological study.2 The pacemaker was implanted without complications and the patient was discharged (Figure 3). A beta-blocker was also started, which helped control premature ventricular contractions.

Left Bundle Branch Area Pacing

In the postprocedural electrocardiogram, the morphology and axis of the fascicular blocks have disappeared. The duration of the QRS complex has shortened.

Months later, during follow-up, the patient's dyspnea resolved, and he was in NYHA functional class I. When the device was interrogated, the ventricular pacing percentage was 97%, with a minimum heart rate threshold of 60 beats/min.

Discussion

In this work we present the case of a patient with a rare form of trifascicular block and an unusual clinical presentation. Given the findings described and the severity of the disease affecting his cardiac conduction system, it was decided to implant a pacemaker, in agreement with current guidelines.2 Because of the risk that this patient would develop pacemaker-induced cardiomyopathy, an LBBAP protocol was chosen. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of successful treatment of Rosenbaum syndrome using LBBAP (Visual Summary).

The association between RBBB and alternating block between the left anterior and left posterior fascicles was first described by Mauricio Rosenbaum et al1 in 1969. These authors described 4 cases with this electrocardiographic pattern and adverse outcomes, such as complete AV block and sudden death. Taken together, these intraventricular and atrioventricular blocks have been termed trifascicular block. This term may also encompass other intraventricular conduction disorders, such as RBBB alternating with left bundle branch block and left bundle branch block with first-degree AV block.

In Rosenbaum's work, in addition to alternating intraventricular block, varying degrees of AV block were described concurrently, as seen in this patient (Figures 1 and 2). The possibility of progressive involvement of the cardiac conduction system has been proposed, beginning in the left anterior fascicle and then in the left posterior fascicle. The most severe form of the disease occurs in the left anterior fascicle and manifests as prolonged AV conduction, resulting in complete AV block. It has been postulated that these idiopathic combinations of intraventricular and atrioventricular blocks are caused by progressive degeneration of the cardiac conduction system (progressive cardiac conduction disease), initially described by Lev3 and Lenègre.4 In some cases, a genetic influence can be documented.5 Our patient's father had a pacemaker for an unknown reason. Genetic testing, including testing for channelopathies, was not available for this patient or his father. Given the association of specific genes with progressive cardiac conduction disease and the importance of genetic counseling, screening of first-degree relatives, clinical follow-up, and therapeutic approach, these tests could be useful, particularly in individuals younger than 50 years, in accordance with the resources available for their performance.6

Any of the 3 forms of trifascicular block mentioned represents a severe conduction disturbance, with a high probability of progressing to advanced AV blocks and the need for pacing.2

According to current pacing guidelines,2 trifascicular block is an indication for pacemaker implantation. Traditionally, pacing due to bradyarrhythmias without left ventricular systolic dysfunction is performed conventionally from the right ventricle. However, there is concern regarding the dyssynchrony caused by pacing the heart from the right ventricle. A decline in systolic function has been observed when the pacing percentage exceeds 20%, particularly if there is preexisting structural or functional cardiac disease. In this relatively young 45-year-old patient, a high percentage of pacing was anticipated due to the electrocardiographic findings frequently observed in his 24-hour Holter ECG recording. Based on these findings, and given the likely decades of subsequent pacing, it was decided to implement a physiological pacing protocol rather than conventional pacing. During follow-up, telemetry of the implanted pacemaker was performed, documenting a pacing percentage of 97%. In recent years, LBBAP has emerged as an alternative to conventional pacing. This technique offers advantages over stimulation from the coronary sinus, such as a higher success rate, greater reproducibility, shorter radiation exposure time, and a shorter procedure duration. Likewise, differences are observed when comparing LBBAP with other forms of physiological pacing, such as His bundle pacing, which involves a higher pacing threshold, accelerated battery depletion, and electrode displacement.7

Pacing of the cardiac conduction system, as well as cardiac resynchronization via the coronary sinus, has stronger evidence in the setting of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. However, in the context of bradyarrhythmia with high pacing rates or painful left bundle branch block syndrome, physiological pacing appears to be a reasonable option with favorable results to date.8,9 In addition, during follow-up of these patients, no deterioration in systolic function has been demonstrated.10 In some cases of conduction system pacing in patients with narrow QRS complexes, the QRS may lengthen slightly following pacing. In our patient, the baseline rhythm had a widened QRS complex, which narrowed after pacing with LBBAP, suggesting improved interventricular synchrony following therapy. The results of ongoing clinical trials on conduction system pacing in patients with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction may provide further evidence regarding the use of this technique in daily practice. For now, observational evidence and biological plausibility suggest favorable outcomes.

Conclusions

Rosenbaum syndrome can pose a diagnostic challenge because of its intermittent nature and, in some cases, insidious onset, carrying a high risk of complete AV block and sudden death. LBBAP appears to be a suitable therapeutic option for this syndrome.
