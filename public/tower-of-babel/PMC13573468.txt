A two-stage deep learning model with segmentation-guided top-K slice selection for patient-level PAS prediction on MRI
Authors: Chengzhi Song, Shuiyan Chen, Wei Ye, Yan Guo, Yuting Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePlacenta accreta spectrum (PAS) can cause severe obstetric complications, and accurate preoperative assessment is critical. However, heterogeneous MRI manifestations and the high cost of fine placental annotations limit deep learning applications. This study developed and validated a two-stage segmentation-guided MRI framework for automated placental localization, ROI selection, and patient-level PAS prediction.MethodsA total of 170 third-trimester placental MRI cases were included in the internal cohort, comprising 87 PAS and 83 non-PAS cases. Data were split at the patient level into training, internal validation, and internal test sets. An external validation cohort of 54 patients, including 36 PAS and 18 non-PAS cases, was additionally used. A 2D U-Net-based segmentation model first generated placental probability maps for ROI extraction. Selected ROIs were then classified using a ResNet-18 model, and slice-level probabilities were aggregated into patient-level predictions. The operating threshold was determined using the Youden index on the internal validation set.ResultsFor patient-level PAS classification, the internal validation set achieved an AUC of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.68–0.97), and the Youden-derived operating threshold was 0.61. In the held-out internal test set, the model achieved an AUC of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.53–0.92). In the external validation cohort of 54 patients, including 36 PAS and 18 non-PAS cases, the model achieved an AUC of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.54–0.87), sensitivity of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.62–0.88), specificity of 0.56 (95% CI: 0.34–0.75), and accuracy of 0.70 (95% CI: 0.57–0.81).ConclusionThis two-stage segmentation-guided MRI framework achieved reliable placental segmentation and moderate external performance for patient-level PAS prediction. The model may serve as an auxiliary screening and risk stratification tool, but further prospective multicenter validation and optimization are required before clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02536-4.

Introduction

Placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) refers to a group of severe obstetric conditions characterized by abnormal adherence or invasion of placental villi into the myometrium, and in severe cases, extension beyond the uterine serosa [1, 2]. PAS is associated with a substantially increased risk of massive obstetric hemorrhage, blood transfusion, hysterectomy, adjacent organ injury, and other maternal complications [3]. With the increasing rate of cesarean delivery and the growing number of pregnancies with a scarred uterus, the clinical burden of PAS has continued to rise. Accurate preoperative assessment is therefore important for delivery planning, surgical strategy, blood product preparation, and multidisciplinary team coordination [4, 5].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is widely used as an adjunctive imaging modality for patients with suspected PAS, particularly when ultrasound findings are inconclusive or when posterior placentation, complex anatomy, or extrauterine extension needs further evaluation [6, 7]. MRI provides high soft-tissue contrast and multiplanar anatomical information, allowing visualization of placental heterogeneity, disruption of the placenta–uterus interface, intraplacental dark bands, abnormal vascularity, and uterine wall thinning [8]. However, these imaging signs are often focal and unevenly distributed across slices. In clinical practice, radiologists usually review the full MRI sequence, identify diagnostically informative slices, and then integrate representative signs to reach a patient-level judgment [9]. This characteristic makes automated PAS prediction challenging, because direct averaging or indiscriminate use of all slices may dilute diagnostically relevant information with numerous low-information slices [10].

Recent artificial intelligence approaches have shown potential for MRI-based obstetric image analysis, but several barriers remain for PAS prediction [11]. First, fine pixel-level annotation of the placenta is labor-intensive and requires expertise, limiting the availability of high-quality annotated datasets [12]. Second, placental MRI is inherently multi-slice and often multi-sequence, while sample sizes in PAS studies are usually limited. Directly training end-to-end three-dimensional models may therefore increase computational burden and overfitting risk, and may also reduce interpretability in practical deployment [13, 14]. Third, a model designed for patient-level PAS prediction should not only localize the placenta but also select slices that are most informative for diagnosis, while minimizing the influence of background tissue and redundant slices [15, 16].

To address these challenges, we developed a two-stage segmentation-guided MRI framework for PAS prediction. In the first stage, a 2D U-Net-based segmentation model was used to localize the placenta and generate placental probability maps from MRI slices. These probability maps were then used to guide ROI extraction and slice selection, thereby reducing background interference and improving the consistency of classification inputs. In the second stage, selected placental ROIs were classified using a ResNet-18-based model, and slice-level probabilities were aggregated into patient-level PAS risk predictions. Instead of relying on the entire sequence equally, this framework aims to approximate the clinical process of focusing on diagnostically informative slices while retaining a lightweight and interpretable design.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the feasibility and performance of this two-stage segmentation-guided framework for MRI-based PAS prediction. We assessed placental segmentation performance, patient-level classification performance, and the robustness of key design choices, including ROI selection and patient-level probability aggregation. In addition, we evaluated the model using internal testing and an external validation cohort to provide a more balanced assessment of its potential utility and limitations as an auxiliary PAS risk stratification tool.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was designed as a retrospective cohort analysis. Pregnant women who underwent placental MRI at the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University between 2020 and 2024 were consecutively screened. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age > 18 years; (2) diagnosis of placenta previa or clinical suspicion of placenta accreta spectrum (PAS); (3) availability of preoperative MRI examinations containing the target sequences for placental assessment; and (4) a definitive reference diagnosis of PAS or non-PAS based on clinical consensus, intraoperative findings, and/or histopathological assessment. The exclusion criteria were: (1) missing key clinical labels or incomplete imaging data; (2) incomplete MRI examination due to claustrophobia or other reasons; (3) severe fetal or maternal motion artifacts, susceptibility artifacts, or other image-quality limitations precluding evaluation; and (4) inadequate coverage of the placenta.

An independent external validation cohort of 54 patients, including 36 PAS cases and 18 non-PAS cases, was additionally collected from participating external institutions. MRI data and ground-truth labels

All placental MRI examinations were performed using a 1.5T GE Optima MR360 superconducting MRI scanner with an 8-channel phased-array body coil. Patients were scanned in the lateral decubitus position with feet-first entry. The imaging parameters were as follows: slice thickness, 6.0 mm; interslice gap, 1 mm; matrix size, 256 × 256; and field of view, 40 × 40 cm. The MRI protocol included FIESTA and SSFSE sequences. For FIESTA, the repetition time was 4.1 ms and the echo time was set to Min Full. For SSFSE, the repetition time was 2000 ms and the echo time was 100 ms.

The FIESTA sequence was used as the primary model input because of its rapid acquisition and favorable soft-tissue contrast for placental assessment [17]. Placental segmentation masks were manually delineated on the target MRI slices and reviewed by experienced imaging specialists. Patient-level PAS classification labels were determined according to the predefined reference standard based on clinical consensus, intraoperative findings, and/or pathological assessment [5].

Framework architecture

Stage 1: Placenta segmentation model

Annotation strategy

To minimize labeling burden under real-world constraints, pixel-wise placenta masks were available only for a subset of patients [18, 19]. Specifically, a randomly selected subset of 70 patients had at least one manually annotated slice. Annotations were performed by 2 readers with 5 years of experience, with adjudication by a senior reader when disagreements occurred.

Network architecture

Placenta segmentation was performed using a 2D U-Net–type model [15], which takes a single-slice grayscale image as input and outputs a binary placenta mask of the same spatial resolution. The model was trained on annotated slices.

Training details

The models were implemented in PyTorch v1.10 and trained on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU with 15 GB of memory, NVIDIA driver version 580, a 12-core Intel Xeon Gold 6271 CPU, 62 GB of system RAM, and 200 GB of storage. For the segmentation model, images were intensity-normalized to [0,1], and random horizontal flipping was used for data augmentation with a probability of 0.5. The segmentation model was optimized using AdamW with a learning rate of 1e-3 and a batch size of 8. No weight decay was used for the segmentation model. No learning rate scheduler was applied during segmentation model training; the learning rate was kept constant throughout training. For the PAS classification model, the cross-entropy loss was optimized using AdamW with a learning rate of 1e-3, weight decay of 1e-5, and batch size of 8.No learning rate scheduler was used; the learning rate was kept constant throughout training.

Stage 1 output: ROI generation and quality control

ROI extraction from predicted masks

Using the trained segmentation model, all slices for each patient were inferred to obtain placenta masks. For each slice, the placenta ROI was generated by cropping the image using the bounding box of the predicted placenta mask. To preserve contextual tissue around the placenta, the bounding box was expanded by a fixed margin of 10 pixels on each side. The same crop was applied to the mask, producing paired ROI image and ROI mask.

Stage 2: PAS classification model

Model architecture and initialization

For each patient, the selected Top-K ROI slices were resized to 256 × 256. Since the classification backbone expects 3-channel input, each single-channel ROI image was replicated across channels. We employed ResNet-18 as the PAS classification backbone, balancing representational capacity and computational cost for this relatively small medical dataset.

Training procedure

The classification objective was optimized with cross-entropy loss. We used the AdamW optimizer [20] with a learning rate of 1e-3, weight decay of 1e-5, and batch size of 8.

Sensitivity analysis and rationale for design choices

Sensitivity analyses showed that ROI margin and patient-level aggregation strategy affected classification performance. Under the evaluated settings, an ROI margin of 10 pixels achieved the highest validation AUC when K was fixed at 5. Aggregation strategy comparison showed that max aggregation achieved the highest validation AUC, although it may be more sensitive to single-slice outliers. The full sensitivity analysis is shown in Supplementary Fig. 3.

Patient-level probability fusion (max)

Patient-level PAS probability was calculated using max aggregation, taking the maximum slice-level probability among the Top-K slices. This approach prioritizes the slice most indicative of PAS, providing high sensitivity while mitigating the impact of single-slice noise.

Results

Patient population

A total of 170 patients were included in the internal cohort, comprising 87 patients with PAS and 83 patients without PAS. This study was designed as a retrospective cohort analysis. Pregnant women who delivered at the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University, a tertiary perinatal medical center, between 2020 and 2024 were screened. All included patients underwent placental MRI during hospitalization because placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) was suspected on ultrasound examination.

Placental regions were delineated by experienced clinicians and reviewed by a senior reader when necessary. The annotated data were randomly divided into training and test sets at the patient level. To visually assess segmentation performance, representative slices from the test set were selected, and the manually annotated placental contours and model-predicted contours were overlaid directly on the original MRI images (Fig. 2A).Qualitative visualization results showed that the placental contours predicted by the model were generally consistent with manual annotations in terms of morphology and position, and could stably cover the main placental region. Minor deviations were mainly concentrated in the placental edge, slender structures, or regions with unclear gray-scale transitions, which is consistent with the inherent imaging feature of blurred placenta-uterus interface boundaries. These findings indicate that the proposed model has good practical applicability in clinical scenarios. On average, each placental MRI consisted of approximately 12–15 axial slices covering the entire placenta from the fundus to the cervix. Slice thickness was 6 mm with a 1 mm interslice gap, providing full coverage of the placenta while maintaining sufficient resolution for segmentation and classification.

The training process of the 2D placental segmentation model is shown in Fig. 2B. With the increase of training epochs, the training loss (DiceCELoss) continuously decreased from approximately 1.8 to 0.45, while the Dice coefficient of the validation set increased rapidly and tended to stabilize at a plateau in the later stage (fluctuating in the range of approximately 0.78–0.85). This indicates that the model was fully converged without obvious performance collapse or severe degradation. The slight fluctuation of the validation Dice coefficient may be related to the small size of the test/validation samples, individual differences, and the diversity of placental morphologies. In the quantitative evaluation on the final validation set, the model achieved high and stable segmentation performance: the per-sample Dice coefficient was 0.84 ± 0.07 (global Dice coefficient: 0.84), and the Intersection over Union (IoU) was 0.73 ± 0.11 (global IoU: 0.73). Meanwhile, the model maintained good detection completeness (Recall: 0.87 ± 0.08). In summary, this 2D placental segmentation model can achieve reliable placental region segmentation, providing a stable preprocessing foundation for subsequent ROI generation and downstream PAS classification/risk prediction.

A total of 836 ROI slices were derived in this study, all of which met the effective mask threshold (mask_area ≥ 10 pixels, 836/836). Since a fixed number of Top-K (K = 5) ROI slices were retained per patient for subsequent modeling, the number of valid slices at the patient level showed a highly consistent distribution (mean ± SD: 4.95 ± 0.44; median (IQR): 5.0 (5.0–5.0)), indicating that this process had stable slice selection output on the full dataset.

Comparison of different aggregation strategies (full-slice mean [Mean], Top-K mean [TopKmean], Top-2 mean [Top2mean]) at the patient level revealed that the median area ratio of Top2mean was 0.10, which was higher than that of Mean and TopKmean (Fig. 3A). This indicates that under the premise of fixed K = 2, aggregation based on key slices with “higher placental ratio” can better highlight the information expression of the most salient placental slices, providing more representative inputs for subsequent patient-level prediction.

At the slice level, the ratio of placental mask area to ROI area (mask_area / ROI_area) was calculated to characterize the “salience” of placental components in the ROI. The results showed that the median (IQR) of this ratio was 0.09 (0.08–0.10), with an overall concentrated distribution and small dispersion, suggesting that the ROI cropping scale was relatively consistent among different patients and slices (Fig. 3B).

Classification model performance

The classification model achieved an AUC of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.68–0.97) on the internal validation set, with an accuracy of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.60–0.91), F1-score of 0.78, sensitivity of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.66–0.98), and specificity of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.39–0.86). On the held-out internal test set, the AUC was 0.75(95% CI: 0.53–0.92). In the external validation cohort, the model achieved an AUC of 0.72(95% CI: 0.54–0.87), sensitivity of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.62–0.88), specificity of 0.56 (95% CI: 0.34–0.75), and accuracy of 0.70 (95% CI: 0.57–0.81).

The patient-level classification threshold was determined using the Youden index on the internal validation set, resulting in a threshold of 0.61. This threshold provides a balanced trade-off between sensitivity and specificity, serving as a reference for patient-level classification decisions.

Discussion

Focusing on real-world clinical workflows, this study constructed a two-stage framework for patient-level Placenta Accreta Spectrum (PAS) risk prediction, which integrates placental segmentation, ROI generation, key slice selection, and patient-level probability fusion. Compared with direct end-to-end classification of full-sequence MRI slices, the core advantage of this framework lies in first localizing placenta-related regions through segmentation to reduce background interference, and then reducing the dilution effect of a large number of low-information slices on the model via key slice selection and patient-level probability fusion, thereby being more consistent with clinical image reading logic—clinicians usually make judgments based on a few typical slices [5, 6].

The ROI generation process yielded stable patient-level inputs: the patient-level ROI generation coverage rate in this cohort was 100%, with a total of 836 valid ROI slices generated, averaging approximately 5 key slices per patient. The area ratio of placental masks within the ROI showed an overall concentrated distribution, indicating consistent cropping scales and reducing the impact of “unstable cropping” on downstream classification. These statistical indicators not only serve as part of the method results but also provide evidence for data quality control, enhancing the credibility of the study [8, 15].

MRI manifestations of PAS exhibit significant spatial heterogeneity, and key signs are often prominent only in a few slices [5, 6]. Simple averaging of all slices is likely to “dilute” the signal with a large number of low-information slices. To address this, the study implemented Top-K key-slice selection (K = 5) and employed maximum aggregation for patient-level probability fusion. This approach prioritizes the slice most indicative of PAS, aligning with clinical decision-making by emphasizing diagnostically critical slices while mitigating the influence of less informative or noisy slices [19]. From a statistical perspective, the patient-level placental area ratios of the Top-K slices demonstrated high consistency, confirming that the selected slices reliably capture the most salient placental features and provide robust inputs for patient-level classification.

In the patient-level classification stage, this study adopted the lightweight and mature ResNet-18 [16] pre-trained parameters, and the output layer was replaced with a 2-classification layer to adapt to the task. Transfer learning can alleviate the overfitting risk under small-sample conditions while maintaining inference speed and deployment convenience [21]. The patient-level threshold was determined using the Youden index of the internal validation set only and was then fixed before being applied to the held-out internal test set and the external validation cohort.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size remains relatively small, and PAS represents a continuous pathological spectrum; while the binary classification (Non-PAS vs. PAS) allows for stable model training, it does not capture finer-grained grading or subtypes of PAS. Second, the two-stage framework is susceptible to error propagation, as segmentation inaccuracies may compromise ROI quality and adversely affect classification performance. Third, the study relies primarily on 2D modeling, which limits the exploitation of cross-slice continuity and three-dimensional structural information. Fourth, multi-center external validation is necessary to assess the generalizability of the framework across different scanners, imaging protocols, and clinical settings. Finally, the choices of Top-K and the aggregation method were empirical; although they align with clinical intuition, systematic evaluation on larger datasets—including approaches such as attention-based multiple-instance learning or 2.5D/3D fusion—is warranted to optimize slice selection and patient-level prediction.

Conclusion

This study proposed and implemented a preoperative assessment framework for Placenta Accreta Spectrum (PAS) that is closely aligned with real-world clinical workflows: first, placental segmentation was performed to generate standardized Regions of Interest (ROIs), followed by key slice selection and patient-level risk prediction. The ROI generation achieved stable patient-level coverage in this cohort, and the number of key slices and the distribution of area ratios were concentrated, indicating that the framework has good repeatability and quality controllability. By explicitly introducing key slice selection and patient-level probability fusion, the proposed method exhibits favorable interpretability and deployment potential in scenarios with scarce annotations and excessive full-sequence redundant information. In the future, it is still necessary to expand multi-center samples and develop more advanced cross-slice modeling and learnable aggregation strategies to further improve the model’s generalization ability and clinical risk stratification value.
