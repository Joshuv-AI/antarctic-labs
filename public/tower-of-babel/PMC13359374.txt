The Organic Phase Drives Metal Complex Dehydration at Liquid–Liquid Interfaces through Hydrophobic Interactions in Solvent Extraction
Authors: Noa Nakai, Shu-hei Urashima, Masayuki Watanabe, Takuya Sekikawa, Ryoji Kusaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dehydration is a critical step in transferring hydrated metal ions from the aqueous phase to the organic phase, typically facilitated by complexation with amphiphilic extractants. Here we reveal a distinct, hydrophobically driven dehydration mechanism at the liquid–liquid interface, in which the organic (oil) phase promotes metal complex dehydration through molecular interactions between extractant alkyl chains and the alkane solvent. In a representative Eu3+–di­(2-ethylhexyl)­phosphoric acid (HDEHP) extraction system, vibrational sum-frequency generation (VSFG) spectroscopy demonstrates that Eu in interfacial complexes becomes less hydrated in the presence of n-dodecane, correlating with enhanced ordering of the interfacial alkyl chains. These results establish that hydrophobic interactions organize the interfacial environment to drive metal dehydration, providing new mechanistic insight into the molecular origins of liquid–liquid extraction.

The transfer of metal ions across aqueous–organic interfaces is a fundamental process in numerous areas of chemistry and industry, particularly in liquid–liquid extraction systems used for metal separation, recovery, and purification. This process requires metal ions to overcome a substantial energetic barrier associated with dehydration, a step that is often rate-limiting and thus critically determines extraction efficiency. − In typical extraction systems, amphiphilic extractants (ligands) facilitate dehydration by coordinating with metal ions to shield their charge, while their long and bulky alkyl chains render the resulting complexes hydrophobic, thereby promoting their transfer into the organic phase. Because many extractants are surface-active, they preferentially reside at the oil–water interface, making this boundary a crucial site for extractant-mediated dehydration through complex formation.

Recent advances in interfacial characterization, including vibrational sum-frequency generation (VSFG) spectroscopy, X-ray and neutron scattering, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, have provided molecular-level insights into extractant-induced dehydration. − For example, in the extraction of lanthanides with organophosphorus acid extractants such as bis­(hexadecyl)­phosphoric acid (DHDP), a model extractant well suited for interfacial investigation, Schlossman and co-workers demonstrated that lanthanide–DHDP complexes can assemble into inverted bilayers at the dodecane–water interface, in which dehydrated lanthanide ions are enveloped by DHDP molecules. , Dutta and co-workers further investigated DHDP at the air–water interface and showed, by X-ray scattering and MD simulations, that heavy lanthanide ions interact with a DHDP monolayer to form inverted bilayers. For di­(2-ethylhexyl)­phosphoric acid (HDEHP), an extractant widely used in industrial rare-earth (lanthanide) separations, our group has shown using VSFG spectroscopy that partial dehydration of lanthanide ions occurs via coordination with DEHP (the deprotonated form of HDEHP) molecules at the air–water interface; a DEHP molecule coordinates to a lanthanide ion on the air side, while water molecules continue to hydrate the ion. , Subsequently, Srinivasan et al. and Cantu et al. confirmed this molecular picture for the lanthanide–DEHP system and provided further insights at the oil–water interface using MD simulations. Collectively, these studies highlight the central role of extractant-induced interfacial dehydration, particularly in lanthanide extraction systems employing organophosphorus acid extractants.

Whereas extractant-induced interfacial dehydration has been well recognized, here we demonstrate a previously unrecognized dehydration mechanism, in which the presence of the oil phase actively promotes dehydration within lanthanide–DEHP interfacial complexes. This behavior is demonstrated using a model system comprising Eu3+ and the HDEHP extractant, which are known to form Eu–DEHP complexes at the air–water interface , (Figure a). The hydration state of these interfacial complexes is monitored, in the presence and absence of the oil phase (n-dodecane), by probing the OH stretching vibrations of interfacial water using VSFG spectroscopy. This direct comparison, which has been lacking in previous studies, is critical for isolating the role of the oil phase. These findings highlight the role of the oil phase in interfacial dehydration, offering a new molecular perspective on metal ion transfer.

Before discussing interfacial water based on the VSFG spectra of the oil–water and air–water interfaces in the OH stretching region (Figure a), we first examined the nature of the interfacial complexes formed at the oil–water and air–water interfaces, which were prepared as described in the Supporting Information, using VSFG spectroscopy in the CH and POO– stretching regions. The CH and POO– spectra (Figure c,d and Figures S1 and S2) indicate that the POO– group coordinates to Eu3+ and that both interfaces are predominantly populated by 1:1 Eu–DEHP interfacial complexes (Figure c). Notably, these spectra reveal that the Eu–DEHP interfacial complexes remain stable at the oil–water interface as interfacial intermediates, exhibiting minimal transfer into the oil phase. This observation agrees with a recent MD simulation study showing that 1:1 lanthanide–DEHP complexes at the hexane–water interface reside at an energetic minimum and are unlikely to migrate into the oil phase because of a substantial energy barrier.

(b) VSFG spectrum of the air–pure water interface in the same spectral region. (c) VSFG spectra of the air–water (red) and oil–water (blue) interfaces in the CH and OH stretching regions. (d) VSFG spectra of the air–water (red) and oil–water (blue) interfaces in the POO stretching region.

The blue and red traces in Figure a show the VSFG spectra of the oil–water and air–water interfaces, respectively, in the OH stretching region (3150–3750 cm–1), which reflect the structure of interfacial water molecules. A dominant band is observed in both spectra above 3500 cm–1, with a maximum at approximately 3700 cm–1. Such high-wavenumber OH features (>3500 cm–1) have been reported previously at oil–water interfaces and are attributed to OH stretches that weakly interact with alkyl groups. Consistent with these assignments, the OH signal above 3500 cm–1 indicates that interfacial water molecules weakly interacting with the hydrophobic alkyl chains of n-dodecane and the extractant predominate at both interfaces. The comparable intensity of the >3500 cm–1 OH band between the oil–water and air–water interfaces further suggests that both interfaces are similarly populated by water molecules interacting with hydrophobic moieties.

In contrast, the VSFG spectrum of the oil–water interface (blue) shows much weaker intensity below 3500 cm–1, whereas the air–water interface (red) exhibits stronger intensity in this region. The lower-wavenumber OH stretches (<3500 cm–1) are generally attributed to hydrogen-bonded OH vibrations; under the present conditions, namely when the interfaces are populated with interfacial Eu–DEHP complexes, these hydrogen-bonded water molecules also hydrate the Eu3+ ions within the interfacial complex via their oxygen atoms. These OH groups of the hydrating water form hydrogen bonds with neighboring water molecules, with the hydrogens oriented toward the bulk aqueous phase. Given that both the oil–water and air–water interfaces are similarly populated with Eu–DEHP complexes, the OH signal below 3500 cm–1 at the oil–water interface primarily originates from water molecules hydrating the Eu3+ ions within these interfacial complexes. Therefore, the substantially weaker OH intensity in this region at the oil–water interface, compared with that at the air–water interface, indicates that the presence of the oil phase promotes dehydration of Eu within the interfacial complexes, as illustrated in Figure b.

The spectral features observed in the OH stretching region, when compared with those of the air–pure water interface (Figure b), further support the conclusion that hydrogen-bonded water molecules are scarce at the oil–water interface. The VSFG spectrum of the air–pure water interface exhibits a sharp band at ∼3710 cm–1, which can be attributed to “free” OH groups oriented toward the air, no hydrophobic water band around 3600 cm–1, and a significantly intense band below 3550 cm–1 arising from hydrogen-bonded OH groups. Comparison with the spectrum in Figure b clearly indicates that the molecular environments of water at the oil–water differ markedly from that at the air–pure water interface.

The promotion of Eu3+ dehydration within the interfacial complexes at the oil–water interface can be attributed to hydrophobic interactions between the alkyl chains of DEHP and the organic solvent molecules. The presence of the organic phase likely induces partial penetration of interfacial Eu–DEHP complexes toward the oil side (Figure b), as their alkyl chains interact favorably with the dodecane solvent. This interpretation is supported by previous studies. For instance, molecular dynamics simulations by Cantu et al. revealed that DEHP molecules and their complexes exhibit greater free energy stabilization at the water–hexane interface than at the water–octanol interface, indicating the existence of favorable interactions between the extractant alkyl chains and alkane solvent molecules. Similarly, Premadasa et al. reported that HDEHP molecules are more ordered and preferentially oriented at liquid–liquid interfaces than at air–water interfaces, providing further evidence for direct interactions between the extractant and organic solvent molecules.

Our CH stretching spectra (Figure c and Figure S1) also support this view. The CH3 stretching band observed at ∼2890 cm–1 at the oil–water interface is sharper, and the peak-area fraction (PAF = A CH3 /(A CH3 + A CH2 )) is larger than that at the air–water interface (the data were not quantitatively analyzed accurately because the peaks are not well resolved, particularly in the air–aqueous spectrum shown in Figure S1), suggesting that the alkyl chains of interfacial Eu–DEHP complexes adopt a more upright orientation and interact with the dodecane solvent. Taken together, these results indicate that hydrophobic interactions between the alkyl chains of interfacial Eu–DEHP complexes and the organic solvent play a key role in drawing the interfacial complexes slightly toward the oil phase, thereby expelling water molecules from the Eu3+ coordination sphere and promoting dehydration within the interfacial complexes.

In a very recent study, interfacial systems consisting of ammonium-terminated amines (octadecyl amine) and various anions were investigated in detail using VSFG and MD simulations to compare cases with and without an organic phase. The results clearly show that organic solvents (decane) interact with interfacial species. However, the intensity of the H-bonded OH stretching band is higher at the oil–water interface than at the air–water interface; this trend is opposite to our results, suggesting that the influence of the oil-phase solvent on interfacial species depends on the specific system, while interactions between oil-phase solvent molecules and interfacial species are a general phenomenon.

In another recent study, HDEHP systems at the air–aqueous and oil–aqueous interfaces in the absence of lanthanide ions were compared, and their OH spectra differed, indicating distinct interfacial water structures. The intensity appears to be lower at the oil–water interface than at the air–water interface, which is consistent with our results. The experiments were conducted at pH 1 (whereas the pH in our study is ∼4–5). Under these conditions, neutral HDEHP covers the interfaces, suggesting that even neutral HDEHP extractants, in the absence of lanthanide ions, are slightly displaced toward the oil phase and become dehydrated at the interface in the presence of oil solvent molecules.

The observed VSFG response in Figure a may include contributions from the electric field originating from interfacial charges. − In our system, the electric charge density arising from interfacial Eu–DEHP complexes can be considered similar at the oil–water and air–water interfaces shown in Figure a (see the Supporting Information for details of sample preparation and the comparable CH intensities in Figures S1 and c, which suggest a similar number of HDEHP molecules at both interfaces). Thus, the observed change in the VSFG response below 3500 cm–1 is likely due to a decrease in the level of hydrated water (i.e., dehydration of interfacial water).

All spectra presented in this paper were obtained using homodyne detection; therefore, it is difficult to uniquely isolate and subtract the contribution of the electric field. Furthermore, in the system studied here, interfacial complexes are present, and increasing the ionic strength by adding salt to extract the electric field contribution may induce side reactions such as dissociation of the interfacial complexes.

To estimate the magnitude of the Fresnel factor contributions at the air–water and oil–water interfaces in our experimental system, we conducted analogous experiments in a D2O system. As a result, the nonresonant signal was found to be comparable at the air–water and oil–water interfaces, indicating that the influence of the Fresnel factors is similar at both interfaces. This result demonstrates that the experimental results at the two interfaces can be directly compared without applying Fresnel factor corrections.

Furthermore, this result suggests that, at the oil–water interface prepared in this study, the dodecane phase is extremely thin, on the order of a molecular layer, and does not significantly affect optical responses such as refraction. Although the thickness of the oil phase in our system, as estimated in the Supporting Information, appears to be larger (125 μm), this value is calculated under the assumption that the oil and aqueous phases form uniform, flat, and meniscus-free layers within a cylindrical geometry; in reality, the oil phase is likely much thinner. The presence of dodecane at the interface is nevertheless confirmed by differences in the OH and CH spectra between the air–water and oil–water interfaces. The preparation method for the oil–water interface in our system is similar to that reported by Sun et al.

To further support our conclusions, we employed an HDEHP-containing air–aqueous interface without Eu as an experimentally accessible control to isolate the contribution of HDEHP in the absence of Eu complexation (Figure S3). We note that the HDEHP-containing dodecane–pure water interface could not be reliably prepared using the present experimental procedure (see the Supporting Information). In the absence of Eu ions, the OH stretch signal is very strong due to the orientation of interfacial water molecules induced by negatively charged DEHP– species. In contrast, when Eu is present (Figure a,c), the OH stretch signal becomes substantially weaker, consistent with the formation of Eu–DEHP interfacial complexes. This comparison suggests that the observed changes in the OH stretch region (Figure a) are attributed primarily to differences in the hydration state of Eu–DEHP complexes.

Finally, we note that the pH values of the aqueous phases at the air–aqueous and oil–aqueous interfaces investigated in this study are different (4.3 and 5.6, respectively (see the Supporting Information for details)). To assess whether the difference in the OH stretching band shown in Figure a could be attributed to this pH difference, we measured the VSFG spectrum of the air–aqueous interface at pH 5.6 (Figure S4). A strong OH stretching intensity below 3500 cm–1 was observed, consistent with that observed at pH 4.3 (Figure a, red spectrum). These results demonstrate that the differences in OH stretching intensity shown in Figure a cannot be attributed to the pH difference.

In summary, this study reveals a previously unrecognized interfacial dehydration mechanism operating at the oil–water boundary during rare-earth extraction with HDEHP. The OH stretching VSFG spectra provide clear spectroscopic evidence that the presence of the organic phase reduces the level of hydration of the interfacial Eu–DEHP complex, indicating oil-phase-driven interfacial dehydration. This conclusion is noteworthy because dehydration is more strongly promoted at the dodecane–water interface (∼50 mN/m), even though its effective hydrophobicity is lower than that of the air–water interface (∼72 mN/m). Because dehydration is a key step in ion transfer to the organic phase, these findings establish that the organic phase actively promotes this process at the interface, facilitating the migration of metal ions into the organic layer. We hypothesize that more efficient oil-phase-driven dehydration may facilitate faster extraction.

Traditionally, variations in the extraction efficiency have been interpreted primarily in terms of diffusion and interfacial reaction kinetics. These variations have also been linked to macroscopic factors, such as solvent properties, extractant solubility, complexation equilibria, solution conditions, viscosity, interfacial tension, interfacial area, dielectric constant, temperature, and hydrodynamic effects.

In contrast, this study demonstrates that molecular-level interactions at the interfacespecifically, hydrophobic interactions between the extractant alkyl chains and the organic solventplay a decisive role in promoting interfacial dehydration and facilitating ion transfer and are fundamentally linked to interfacial reactions. These findings provide a new molecular-level perspective that bridges traditional macroscopic interpretations with the microscopic mechanisms governing extraction efficiency and kinetics.
