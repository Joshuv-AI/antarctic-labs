SNAKE-71: wire-free direct aspiration thrombectomy using the Cereglide 71 catheter for acute large vessel occlusion stroke
Authors: Halil İbrahim Akçay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Direct aspiration has become a first-line strategy in mechanical thrombectomy (MT) for large vessel occlusion (LVO) stroke. Advances in large-bore aspiration catheters have enabled wire-free navigation techniques such as SNAKE; however, data on newer 0.071″ platforms remain limited. To evaluate the technical feasibility, angiographic efficacy, and clinical outcomes of the wire-free SNAKE technique using the Cereglide 71 aspiration catheter in acute anterior circulation LVO. Fourteen consecutive patients with acute anterior circulation LVO underwent MT using a direct aspiration-first strategy. The Cereglide 71 catheter was advanced without a microguidewire or microcatheter, following the SNAKE technique. Primary outcomes included successful reperfusion (TICI 2b–3) and first-pass effect (FPE). Secondary outcomes included procedural time, complications, NIHSS improvement, and 90-day modified Rankin Scale (mRS). Successful reperfusion was achieved in all patients (100%). First-pass effect occurred in 57.1% of cases. Mean procedural time was 12.1 ± 5.8 min with a median of one device pass. Median NIHSS improved from 19.5 at admission to 2.0 at 24 h. At 90 days, 78.6% of patients achieved functional independence (mRS 0–2). Distal embolization occurred in 42.9% of cases; however, final reperfusion was maintained in all patients. Wire-free SNAKE thrombectomy using the Cereglide 71 catheter is technically feasible and highly effective, achieving rapid reperfusion and favorable clinical outcomes. This approach represents a viable minimalist aspiration strategy in contemporary MT practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58034-8.

Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion (LVO) is a leading cause of disability and death worldwide1. Mechanical thrombectomy (MT) has evolved over the past decade to become the standard of care for acute LVO stroke and has demonstrated significantly better outcomes compared to intravenous thrombolysis2. Major clinical trials conducted in 2015 demonstrated the efficacy of MT, and subsequent guidelines published in North America and Europe formally recommended endovascular treatment for eligible LVO patients2,3. MT is now routinely performed in comprehensive stroke centers. Catheters are typically inserted into the cerebral circulation via the femoral artery, and the occlusive thrombus is physically removed using different techniques4.

Early MT approaches relied on stent retriever devices as primary tools, but direct aspiration techniques have come to the fore. The A Direct Aspiration First Pass Technique (ADAPT) is an aspiration-only revascularization approach, and prior evidence has reported clinical outcomes comparable to those achieved with stent retriever–based thrombectomy5. For example, the COMPASS randomized trial showed that aspiration-first treatment was not inferior to stent retrievers in 90-day functional outcomes and had similar safety profiles5. Although another study found no difference in outcomes between approaches, direct aspiration may offer advantages such as faster reperfusion and less endothelial damage in some cases6. These findings have solidified ADAPT as an accepted first-line strategy in MT, and the choice between aspiration, stent retriever, or combined techniques continues to be based on the patient’s condition and the operator’s assessment.

Advances in catheter technology have played a central role in the increasing success of thrombectomy using the aspiration technique. In particular, large-bore aspiration catheters (inner diameters ~ 0.068–0.072 inches) have been developed to provide significantly higher suction power and clot retention force compared to their smaller predecessors7. This higher aspiration efficiency translates into higher recanalization rates, particularly for large clot loads in proximal vessels7,8. Historically, aspiration was limited by the available catheter size and trackability—catheters needed a lumen wide enough to capture clots, while also being flexible enough to navigate tortuous cerebral arteries9. New-generation 0.071-inch catheters have filled this gap by combining wide lumens with advanced material designs for flexibility and support9. Manufacturers have optimized features such as variable stiffness profiles, inner liners, and hydrophilic coatings to increase accessibility without compromising lumen size10. As a result, modern aspiration devices can more easily access intracranial circulation and provide longer reach. This enables improved effective thrombectomy in terms of speed and safety.

The improved trackability of today’s large aspiration catheters has also led to innovations in technique. In particular, the Sofia Non-wire Advancement Technique (SNAKE) has been introduced as a wire-free navigation method for flexible aspiration catheters11. In this approach, a soft, torqueable aspiration catheter is gently advanced into the occluded vessel to locate and capture the clot, relying on the catheter’s flexible distal segment and suction power, without the need for a distal microguide wire or microcatheter11. This minimalist technique potentially simplifies the procedure by eliminating the need for a coaxial microcatheter. Clinical experience has reported SNAKE to be both safe and effective: in one series, intracranial placement of the catheter was achieved in over 80% of cases without arterial dissection11. A recent study also demonstrated that a SNAKE-based approach significantly shortened reperfusion times compared to traditional catheter navigation12. These findings underscore the rationale for non-wire aspiration—by leveraging the catheter’s navigational properties, it is possible to reduce procedural steps and recanalization time.

Amid these developments, Cereglide 71 (Cerenovus, USA) has emerged as a new 0.071-inch aspiration catheter designed to further advance stroke intervention. This new generation large-bore catheter features a tapered, variable-stiffness shaft (transitioning from a rigid proximal segment to a flexible hydrophilic distal segment) and a polytetrafluoroethylene inner liner9. This design was developed to provide “robust application” and “reliable trackability” through challenging anatomical structures without compromising lumen size9. Cereglide 71 is optimized for efficient clot aspiration and can also serve as a channel for stent retriever placement when needed9. The introduction of this device fills a significant gap in MT technology: achieving the aspiration power of a 0.071-inch catheter while maintaining excellent navigation and catheter integrity during multiple passes. Early clinical use has been promising, and initial reports indicate high revascularization success due to the catheter’s large inner diameter and enhanced flexibility9. In this context, our study was conducted to evaluate the performance of the Cereglide 71 aspiration catheter used in conjunction with the wire-free SNAKE technique for acute LVO stroke, to assess its technical efficacy and potential to facilitate thrombectomy procedures.

Materials and methods

Fourteen consecutive patients treated between March 2024 and December 2025 at our institution were analyzed.

No patients treated with the Cereglide 71 catheter using the wire-free SNAKE approach during the study period were excluded due to procedural failure, unsuccessful reperfusion, or unfavorable angiographic outcome.

All patients underwent non-contrast brain computed tomography (CT) and CT angiography (CTA) to confirm pre-circulatory LVO. Mechanical thrombectomy procedures were performed in the angiography suite under local anesthesia with conscious sedation when necessary, according to institutional practice. For each case, an 8 F short femoral sheath (Cordis, USA) was insterted via the common femoral artery. Through this sheath, a long 8 F guide catheter was advanced to the internal carotid artery—in our practice, either a 95 cm Cerebase DA distal access sheath (Cerenovus, USA) or a 90 cm AXS Infinity LS Plus long sheath (Stryker, USA) was used, depending on availability. Subsequently, a 0.071-inch Cereglide 71 aspiration catheter (Cerenovus, USA) was advanced using only the catheter’s own flexibility and trackability, without the use of a micro-guidewire or micro-catheter. When the catheter tip reached the thrombus, continuous aspiration was initiated using the Penumbra ENGINE aspiration pump (Penumbra Inc., Alameda, CA, USA) connected to the catheter hub. This guidewire-free approach, compatible with the SNAKE (Sofia Non-wire Advancement techniKe) technique, allowed the catheter to advance in a controlled manner under continuous negative pressure. The thrombus was removed by aspiration through the Cereglide 71 catheter. Representative procedural steps of the wire-free SNAKE technique are demonstrated in Supplementary Video 1. Reperfusion success was assessed by digital subtraction angiography after each pass, and additional passes were performed as needed until a Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (TICI) grade ≥2b flow was achieved or no further improvement was possible. All device names, sizes, and manufacturers were recorded for each procedure. In this series, a stent retriever was not used as the first-line device. In cases of distal embolization or incomplete distal reperfusion following primary aspiration, rescue combined thrombectomy using aspiration plus stent retriever was performed at the operator’s discretion. Neurological status was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) at admission, 24 h after the procedure, and at discharge. Functional outcome was assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at 90 days through outpatient evaluation or structured telephone follow-up. Procedure duration was defined as the time interval between common femoral artery puncture and final successful reperfusion (TICI ≥2b). In cases requiring rescue combined thrombectomy, the additional rescue maneuvers were included in the total procedural time calculation. The study was approved by the Niğde Ömer Halisdemir University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Protocol Number: 2025/114). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median values where appropriate, while categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Statistical analyses were performed using standard descriptive methods.

Results

Patient characteristics and initial clinical status

A total of 14 consecutive patients were included in the study. The mean age of the cohort was 66.6 ± 12.9, and males were slightly predominant (8 males, 6 females). The vascular risk profile was notable for the high prevalence of hypertension (71.4%), atrial fibrillation (57.1%), and hyperlipidemia (64.3%), indicating that the population was at significant cardioembolic and atherothrombotic risk. Diabetes was present in 14.3% of patients, while 28.6% reported smoking. No patient reported alcohol use, and obesity was rare (7.1%).

Baseline functional status was good in all cases. Premorbid mRS was 0 in 71.4% and 1 in 28.6%, reflecting an independent population prior to the index stroke event. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1.

At presentation, neurological deficit was severe, with a median NIHSS score of 19.5; however, baseline imaging showed relatively preserved brain tissue, as demonstrated by a mean ASPECTS score of 9.0 ± 0.9.

Procedure performance and technical outcomes

Intravenous thrombolysis was administered to 3 patients (21.4%) prior to endovascular treatment. Mechanical thrombectomy procedures were characterized by high technical efficacy, with a mean puncture-to-recanalization time of 12.1 ± 5.8 min and a median of 1 device pass (range 1–3).

Successful reperfusion (TICI 2b–3) was achieved in all patients (100%), highlighting a consistently high angiographic success rate. Importantly, first-pass effect (FPE) was achieved in 57.1% of cases, reflecting effective clot-device interaction and favorable procedural dynamics. Rescue combined therapy was required in 6 patients (42.9%).

Clinical evolution and functional outcomes

Early neurological recovery was marked and rapid. The median NIHSS score improved from 19.5 at admission to 2.0 at 24 h, demonstrating the significant benefits of early reperfusion. Neurological improvement continued until discharge, with a median NIHSS of 0.5.

At 90-day follow-up, 11 patients (78.6%) achieved good functional outcomes (mRS 0–2), demonstrating that high technical success translated into sustained clinical benefit.

Complications and procedural challenges

Procedure-related or post-procedural adverse events were observed, but these did not negate the overall clinical benefit. Intracranial hemorrhage or cerebral edema was observed in 4 patients (28.6%), while thrombus migration was documented in 6 patients (42.9%). Despite these events, reperfusion was successfully maintained, and favorable functional outcomes were achieved in most cases. Procedural and clinical outcomes are presented in Table 2.

Discussion

Main findings

In this single-center case series, we demonstrated that the wire-free aspiration navigation approach, commonly referred to as SNAKE (Sofia Non-wire Advancement Technique), can be successfully applied in patients with acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion using a large-bore aspiration catheter (Cereglide 71) with a diameter of 0.071 inches. Successful reperfusion (TICI 2b–3) was achieved in all patients, and a first-pass effect was observed in more than half of the cases. Procedure times were short, and rapid neurological recovery was observed, translating into a high rate of functional independence at 90 days.

In our series, the observed reperfusion and functional outcome rates appear numerically favorable compared with previously reported aspiration-based thrombectomy studies, although direct comparison is limited by the small sample size and single-center design13,14. The median procedure time was only ~ 12 min, highlighting the efficiency of this approach. Such rapid recanalization reflects the advantages of direct aspiration-first strategies, which have been shown to shorten procedure time compared to stent retriever-first methods5. The minimalist nature of the SNAKE approach may further reduce device exchanges and procedural steps, potentially contributing to faster reperfusion.

These results suggest that a simplified aspiration-first strategy using modern large-bore catheters can provide rapid and effective recanalization in selected patients. Importantly, these findings should be interpreted in the context of a small, carefully selected cohort treated at a single experienced center.

Feasibility of aspiration without wire using cereglide 71

The SNAKE approach was initially defined as a wire-free method using the Sofia aspiration catheter, utilizing catheter flexibility and aspiration force for intracranial navigation. Our experience demonstrates that this concept can also be applied to the Cereglide 71 catheter, which, thanks to its variable-stiffness shaft and flexible distal segment, allows consistent advancement to the occlusion site without requiring a distal microguide wire or microcatheter. Wire-free navigation may reduce procedural complexity and eliminate the need for distal microcatheter and guidewire manipulation, potentially shortening procedural time and minimizing vessel wall interaction. Cereglide 71 reliably reached the occlusion site by being easily tracked in the cervical and intracranial circulation without requiring a distal microguide wire or microcatheter. Notably, the first-pass success rate of 57% in our series reflects the high efficacy reported with the latest generation of aspiration catheters14. We encountered no arterial dissection or device-related vasospasm causing clinical problems. This is consistent with the original reports of the SNAKE (SOFIA wireless advancement) technique, which showed 100% successful catheter navigation and zero dissection in 187 cases11.

We encountered no device-related complications such as arterial dissection, vessel perforation, catheter fracture, or clinically significant vasospasm. These findings support the procedural safety of the wire-free navigation strategy when performed with modern flexible aspiration catheters.The observed first-pass effect rate is comparable to those reported with contemporary aspiration-based thrombectomy techniques and reflects the effective clot capture facilitated by the catheter’s large inner diameter. While these findings do not prove superiority, they support the technical feasibility of a wire-free aspiration strategy using Cereglide 71 in routine clinical practice.

Given the limited published literature on this new catheter, our series provides valuable evidence that the Snake technique can be safely and effectively performed with Cereglide 71. The high rate of favorable outcomes (mRS 0–2 78.6%) further supports the safety of this approach, as no serious complications occurred that would offset the benefits of reperfusion.

Role of distal embolization and proximal flow control

Despite excellent overall reperfusion outcomes, distal embolization was observed in a considerable proportion of cases. This rate appears higher than that reported in series using balloon guide catheters (BGC) with proximal flow-stop capability. At our institution, a BGC with an inner diameter compatible with a 0.071-inch aspiration catheter was not available; therefore, a long sheath that did not allow effective flow control during clot removal was used. This limitation may partly explain the relatively high distal embolization rate observed in our series.

Unlike a BGC, a long sheath cannot stop antegrade flow during clot retrieval. The literature strongly supports the benefit of BGC-induced flow cessation in reducing clot fragmentation. In a retrospective analysis, the use of a BGC with a traditional guidewire was associated with distal embolism in only ~ 7% of cases, whereas the use of a traditional guidewire alone was associated with distal embolism in 32% of cases13. Similarly, an in vitro thrombectomy study demonstrated that proximal flow control via BGC significantly reduced the release of large (> 1 mm) clot fragments (P < 0.01) and increased flow reversal to aspirate residuals15.

Consistent with these findings, the absence of proximal flow control in our series may have contributed to the observed embolic migration. Nevertheless, successful reperfusion was achieved in all patients, and favorable functional outcomes were maintained, suggesting that most distal emboli were clinically silent or effectively managed with additional aspiration passes or rescue techniques. Although distal embolization was relatively frequent, most events involved smaller distal branch territories and were not consistently associated with poor functional outcome. However, due to the limited sample size, the clinical impact of distal embolization could not be reliably evaluated through subgroup analysis.

Aspiration catheter size and clot burden considerations

A significant proportion of patients in this series had ICA terminus occlusions associated with large clot burden and increased technical complexity. Removing large, hard clots at the carotid terminus can be challenging. Encouragingly, advances in catheter technology are addressing this issue. While Cereglide 71 provides significant aspiration power, emerging evidence suggests that super-wide-bore aspiration catheters may further enhance clot retrieval and reduce fragmentation in such scenarios. The use of super-large lumen aspiration catheters (SLACs), with an inner diameter of approximately 0.080–0.088″, has been shown to increase thrombus retrieval and reduce the risk of clot fragmentation16. The use of larger-diameter aspiration systems or improved proximal flow control may reduce distal embolization, particularly in carotid terminus occlusions, and further increase first-pass success.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. The sample size is small, the design is retrospective, and there is no comparison group. Therefore, the findings cannot be generalized without caution. The small sample size and single-center retrospective design limit the external validity of the findings. Larger prospective multicenter studies are warranted. Additionally, the lack of a compliant balloon guide catheter limits the results regarding embolic protection and flow control. Finally, only anterior circulation occlusions were included, and the applicability of this approach to posterior circulation stroke cases is unknown.

These findings suggest that wire-free aspiration using the Cereglide 71 catheter is feasible in real-world clinical practice and may represent a practical alternative for selected cases of large vessel occlusion. However, larger multicenter studies with standardized use of proximal flow control and direct comparisons with conventional wire-guided or combined thrombectomy techniques are required to more definitively assess the safety and efficacy of this approach.

Conclusion

In selected patients with anterior circulation large vessel occlusion, the wire-free aspiration approach using the Cereglide 71 catheter is feasible and associated with rapid and effective reperfusion. Although distal embolization remains a concern in the absence of proximal flow control, overall clinical and angiographic outcomes are favorable. With further refinement and appropriate device selection, this technique may provide a useful addition to endovascular treatment strategies for acute ischemic stroke.
