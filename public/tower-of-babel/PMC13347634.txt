Evaluation of an Efficient Cogeneration Scheme and Reduction of Polluting Emissions for a Pilot-Scale Biodiesel Production Process
Authors: Miguel F. Altamirano-Muratalla, Raúl Hernández-Altamirano, Ignacio Carvajal-Mariscal, Violeta Y. Mena-Cervantes, Raúl Ramírez-Hernández, Luis Roberto Martínez-Castro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work reports the implementation of a biobased reciprocating engine cogeneration system (BRECS) for the operation of a pilot-scale biodiesel batch production plant based on the IPN-GBD-1000 (1000 L/batch) process, with the aim of improving energy efficiency and pollutant emission reduction on the biodiesel industrial process. The results indicate that the plant can operate under an efficient cogeneration scheme by implementing the use of a Green Hybrid Fuel (diesel-biodiesel blends) in a 30 kW capacity diesel-electric power generator, which supplies power to the heating and electromotive systems of the biodiesel production plant. Based on the annual electrical consumption of the IPN-GBD-1000 process (7669.38 kWh/year), that produces 100 batches per year, and the annual diesel fuel demand of the electric generator (2040.06 L/year), the proposed efficient cogeneration scheme was determined according to the NOM-017-CRE-2019 methodology. For this case, efficient cogeneration (ηmin > 5%) was reached by the use of B20 (80% diesel, 20% biodiesel), B25 (75% diesel, 25% biodiesel), and B30 (70% diesel, 30% biodiesel) blends, obtaining 10.8% (B20), 16.4% (B25), and 21.9% (B30) of primary energy savings due to Ultra-Low Sulfur Diesel (ULSD) consumption reduction. Additionally, polluting emissions (CO, NOx, and particulate matter) were reduced on most biodiesel blends. Smoke opacity decreased in B5 (−19.7%), B10 (−23.1%), B15 (−29.8%), B20 (−36.5%), B25 (−46.6%), and B30 (−53.3%), compared to the ULSD. At 100% load, NOx values remained stable (−2.64% maximum reduction for B5) and CO emissions decreased (−6.09% maximum reduction for B25). Therefore, BRECS implementation is viable for industrial scale biodiesel production, integrating biodiesel blends for pollutant emission reduction.

Introduction

Due to the current problem of climate change, faced worldwide, actions have been taken in Mexico to contribute to the reduction of polluting emissions and greenhouse gases (GHG) into the atmosphere for the year 2030, adopting the 22% reduction of national GHG emissions and a 31% reduction in the electric power sector, according to the baseline. , The transport and electric power sectors are the main emitters of GHG, contributing 47% of the country’s total emissions (734 million of tCO2e). Mexico has already reached a capacity to generate renewable electrical energy of 31% (geothermal 1.2%, nuclear 2.0%, solar 4.3%, wind 7.5%, and hydroelectric 16%), so the remaining 69% of the electricity generation comes from fossil fuels. For this reason, renewable fuels must be incorporated into conventional technologies for electric power generation, taking into account the technical challenges associated with implementation, the physicochemical characteristics of biofuels, feedstock supplies, and government incentives. − Likewise, regular power plants can utilize only 30% of the total fuel energy, resulting in energy losses. Consequently, cogeneration systems have been implemented to improve energy efficiency, converting 75–80% of the fuel energy into useful energy. ,

Cogeneration systems simultaneously produce two different forms of useful energy (electricity and heat, fuels, chemicals, oxygen, cooling, water, among others.) from a primary energy source, − commonly used in prime movers such as reciprocating engines, microturbines, and combustion turbines.

Recent works explore different multigeneration schemes, implementing different types of energy, such as solar energy (photovoltaic), biomass, or hydrogen, used for small-scale cogeneration systems and diverse applications (heating, fuel and energy production, food, and agriculture industry). − However, the applications of Biobased Fuels Cogeneration Systems (BFCS) are still limited, and the configuration must be determined by the characteristics of the particular process. ,,

Besides the Combined Cycle Power Plants (CCPP), reciprocating engines and emergency generators can be intended for cogeneration applications from 30 kW to 30 MW, commonly used in universities, hospitals, scientific laboratories, industrial facilities, and commercial and residential buildings, mainly using natural gas or other gaseous fuels (84% of the installed reciprocating engine cogeneration systems). In addition, the efficient cogeneration methodology helps to highlight the energy benefits of the cogeneration system by establishing criteria for energy efficiency, emphasizing the useful heat demand and primary energy savings. Furthermore, small-scale and microgeneration power plants that provide primary energy savings should be considered as efficient cogeneration.

Currently, in Mexico, efficient cogeneration technology represents 2322 MW (provided only by CCPP) of the national installed capacity, seeking to increase an additional 1338 MW for 2030. This shows a panorama for the implementation and development of Biobased Reciprocating Engine Cogeneration Systems (BRECS), either for small-scale applications or for the industrial sector, since Mexican regulation (NOM-017-CRE-2019) encourages the implementation of cogeneration systems in the productive process that produces residual fuels (Figure ), as well as the efficient cogeneration evaluation of the systems by the methodology and criteria (CRE-RES/003/2011).

Typically, the residual fuel cogeneration systems in Mexico are based on heavy fuel oil or natural gas, serving as petroleum refineries. However, this scheme of power generation could be applied to BFCS, along with its implementation in biofuel industrial production processes, where a fraction of the biofuel produced could be destined for electric energy production that simultaneously can be used to produce another biofuel batch. Specifically, BRECS uses a reciprocating engine for electric power generation; therefore, liquid and gaseous biofuels must be utilized as an energy feedstock, obtaining both electric power and calorific energy, from the exhaust gases, mainly for short-scale applications. Besides, primary fossil energy savings achieved through the substitution of renewable fuels for fossil fuels lead to the opportunity for BRECS to be considered an efficient cogeneration system.

Hence, the emerging industrial biodiesel production process seems attractive for BRECS small-scale implementation, using part of the biodiesel produced to replace fossil diesel on the reciprocating engine for power generation, fully or partially (diesel-biodiesel blends), also contributing to the reduction of GHG (CO, NOx) and polluting emissions into the atmosphere, such as particulate matter (PM). Furthermore, literature reveals that BRECS has not been implemented for industrial scale or evaluated for efficient cogeneration, either for heat recovery or primary energy savings. Mainly, works are focused in the potential implementation of more efficient cogeneration and trigeneration systems, using conventional fuels and improving the heat recovery, emission performance, and electrical operation. −

Particularly, the pilot-scale biodiesel production plant, utilizing IPN-GBD-1000 technology located on the CMPL, operates with electricity as its primary energy source. Besides the use of chemicals (alcohol, waste cooking oil (WCO), catalyst) for biodiesel production, the main source of GHG and pollutant emissions are related to the conventional electric power used for the heating and electromotive systems of the plant.

Thereby, this work aims to evaluate the implementation of a BRECS for the IPN-GBD-1000 plant, based on the use of Ultra-Low Sulfur Diesel (ULSD) and Green Hybrid Fuel (GHF) with B5, B10, B15, B20, B25, and B30 diesel-biodiesel blends (being B5 to B20 the most approved blends by engine manufactures), in order to determine the blends that operate under an efficient cogeneration scheme. On the other hand, the reduction of GHG and PM is associated with the environmental benefits of BRECS, compared to a conventional biodiesel production process.

Materials and Methods

IPN-GBD-1000 Pilot-Scale Biodiesel Production Plant

The IPN-GBD-1000 pilot-scale biodiesel plant (Figure ) produces 1000 L of biodiesel per batch, utilizing WCO as the feedstock for the transesterification reaction and electricity as the sole source of energy for the entire process. The process is registered and assures the biodiesel quality production.

IPN-GBD-1000 pilot-scale biodiesel production plant general scheme and BRECS electric power plant.

In the first stage, the WCO is filtered and transferred to the reactor with methanol (6:1 mol) and potassium hydroxide as the catalyzer at 1%/W, forming a mixture. Then, the reaction mixture is heated by an immersion heater and a hot water heating system, maintaining the reactor operating temperature between 50 and 60 °C and mixing at constant 500 rpm. Once the reaction is complete, the main product obtained is biodiesel, and glycerin is produced as a byproduct. The residual alcohol in the mixture is recovered using a vacuum system, which raised the temperature in the reactor to 65 °C. The purpose of this process is to evaporate the alcohol and then condense it using a shell-and-tube condenser.

Finally, the temperature in the reactor is reduced to approximately 40 °C by cooling the tower water, which separates the phases of glycerin and biodiesel. The glycerin is stored in tanks, and the biodiesel is purified by filtering it.

The entire process utilizes only electrical energy as a power source, both in the heating system (immersion heater) and in the electromotive system (electric motors and pumps). This electricity can be supplied by the grid or an electric generator installed as an emergency power plant (30 kW of capacity).

Green Hybrid Fuel

The biodiesel used for the blends was produced in the IPN-GBD-1000 plant, and ULSD was purchased from a commercial station. The GHF blends were prepared using two graduated cylinders of 1 L each: one for the ULSD and the other for biodiesel. Ten liters of hybrid fuel were formulated for every blend.

For example, the B5 blend used 0.5 L of biodiesel and 9.5 L of ULSD. In total, six blends of hybrid fuels were made (B5, B10, B15, B20, B25, and B30).

Experimental Procedure

For every experiment, GHF blends were charged into the electric generator and then tested at three different loads, 0% (0 kW), 50% (12 kW), and 100% (24 kW), in order to obtain the most representative tendency of the pollution emission reduction due to the use of a higher biodiesel percentage and because, in real operation conditions, the electrical consumption for the IPN-GBD-1000 biodiesel plant is close to these values.

Before the test, 10 L of hybrid fuel were weighted, allowing for the determination of fuel consumption. The first run was always at 0% of the load, allowing the engine to stabilize until the operating oil temperature (70 °C) and oil pressure (5.1 bar) were reached. This step takes about 10–15 min.

After the operating parameters were stable, the flue gas analyzer probe was placed through a slot in the gas exhaust tube. Every 60 s, a lecture on CO, NO, NO2, temperature, and gas flow rate was taken along for 10 min.

For the 50% and 100% loads, a pair of immersion heaters were used. Each unit required 12 kW of electrical power, which was turned on as needed to meet the load. Consequently, the operator placed the opacimeter in the chimney of the gas exhaust tube, and the data reported are for ULSD and B5 to B30 blends.

Finally, the fuel consumption was determined based on the final weight of the hybrid fuel used.

Efficient Cogeneration Methodology

In Figure , the authors present a general scheme for BRECS implemented in the IPN-GBD-1000 pilot-scale biodiesel production plant.

According to the methodology, the proposed system can be considered an efficient cogeneration if the ratio between annual primary energy savings and the fuel used to generate electrical energy is less than or equal to 5%, as the BRECS capacity is less than 0.5 MW. Nevertheless, the efficiency attributable to the production of electrical energy must be higher than 44%, according to the reference efficiency value. Polluting Emission Reduction

The polluting emission reductions for the different blends tested are shown in Figures and . NOx data could not be measured by the flue gas analyzer for the 0% load because the oxygen percentage level for the exhaust gases was high (17.9%). Values for 0 kW and 12 kW were unstable as a result of the engine’s lower efficiency and combustion performance. However, CO reduction is observed as the load and the biodiesel blend increased, obtaining maximum reductions for B20 at 0 kW (−45.1%), 12 kW (−38.5%), and B25 at 24 kW (−6.0%). A better combustion leads to CO reduction, associated with the load increase, though the higher value emission for B30 at the maximum load can be described as a result of energy loss due to the lower heating value of this blend, observing it as an increase of fuel consumption, as reported in Figure . For NOx, the values remained stable, similar to ULSD, differing from expected due to the higher temperatures in the engine combustion chamber, , but similar to the reduction reported by De Paulo et al. for the B30 blend, which achieved a 3.4% reduction at 12 kW in this work. Instead, for 100% load (24 kW), B5 obtained the maximum reduction of 2.6%, while the maximum increase was obtained for B15 (3.8%). Similar results for CO and NOx emissions decrease are reported by Gracz et al. and Zhang et al., for a similar load (12 kW load on this work) and different biodiesel feedstocks. The chemical properties (degree of unsaturation) of biodiesel from different vegetable oil sources might be the reason for a higher temperature and thermal NOx production as well as the presence of more oxygen in the biodiesel and the load variation. Reductions in medium loads can be attributable to the NOx increase. Most of NOx emission reduction can be treated with precombustion (additives, water injection method, and water-fuel emulsion) and postcombustion techniques (NOx adsorber catalyst and selective catalytic and noncatalytic reduction).

From the figure, it is clear that the use of biodiesel blends reduces smoke opacity as the biodiesel content increases. This reduction in GHF blends can be attributed to the decrease in the aromatic compound content from fossil ULSD (maximum 30% in Mexico). The maximum reduction was achieved by the B30 blend, which showed a smoke emission of 1.39% compared with the reference value of ULSD (2.98%), representing a 53.4% reduction in opacity. Conclusions

The efficient cogeneration scheme can be applied for B20 (η = 12.1%), B25 (η = 19.6%), and B30 (η = 28.1%) GHF blends for the IPN-GBD-1000 industrial pilot-scale process, obtaining 10.8% (B20), 16.4% (B25), and 21.9% (B30) of primary energy savings of fossil diesel fuels on the BRECS. Thereby, the proposed efficient cogeneration scheme represents an alternative for typical configurations of residual fuel cogeneration systems and shows the viability for biofuels to be implemented in electric power generation, particularly for emergency diesel generators, leading to sustainable systems that produce bioenergy along with polluting emission reduction and GHG mitigation, also, reducing the carbon footprint of the biodiesel produced, by avoiding conventional electrical power consumption from the grid and substituting a fraction of the ULSD directly for biodiesel, which might be technically challenging to implement on large-scale systems, but necessary for industrial processes where liquid fuels are produced and reciprocating engine technology is implemented for electric power generation, since energy is available with no need for transportation and reciprocating engines are adaptable. Nevertheless, thermal energy utilization from the gas exhaust gases has to be explored in order to improve the BRECS efficiency, but this work demonstrates that even heat power is not considered for the efficient cogeneration methodology, and fossil fuel reduction is sufficient to determine efficient cogeneration. Unlike conventional cogeneration systems, BRECS environmental benefits can be directly measured as GHG emissions and smoke opacity reductions, using electric power energy directly, avoiding large-scale transportation and losses. On the other hand, current renewable energy cogeneration systems are focused on buildings and public service sectors, and most of the research works proposed are laboratory scale or modeling systems; hence, the BRECS evaluated for this pilot scale biodiesel production process is relevant for the growing biofuel industry sector in Mexico.

The environmental benefits of BRECS are achieved through the use of GHF blends, which reduce the aromatic compound content of ULSD, resulting in lower smoke opacity emissions. Every blend implemented presented smoke opacity reductions B5 (−19.7%), B10 (−23.1%), B15 (−29.8%), B20 (−36.5%), B25 (−46.6%), and B30 (−53.3%), compared to the ULSD smoke opacity percentage emission reported, as well as the air pollution emissions of CO and NOx while fuel consumptions remained similar for ULSD and GHF blends. Aside from efficient cogeneration results, the B20, B25, and B30 GHF blends might be incorporated in residual fuel cogeneration schemes. These GHF blends must be tested in different operating engines in order to demonstrate that higher blends do not generate damage on the mechanical parts, also improving the physicochemical properties of the GHF blends by the use of additives and applying different methods for postcombustion emission reduction of GHG. Also, the GHG and smoke opacity emission measuring systems are simple to implement and the direct environmental impact of the system is determined efficiently, as well as most of the research works that approach the biodiesel blend pollutant emission reduction operate under controlled laboratory conditions.

Additionally, reductions of CO were observed for the B5, B10, B15, B20, B25, and B30 GHF blends at 0%, 50%, and 100% loads, except for the increase for B30 at 24 kW (13.2%). Hence, for this specific cogeneration scheme, the B25 GHF blend is viable to implement as it accomplishes efficient cogeneration and shows pollutant emission reductions in every case. Moreover, significant pollution and efficiency advantages were identified for the B30 blend, except for the aforementioned increase in CO emissions. So perhaps this GHF blend is an interesting alternative for an ad hoc postcombustion gas treatment in order to harness the maximum cogeneration efficiency and even carbon footprint benefits from the primary energy saving increases.

Thus, it is concluded that this small-scale BRECS integrates adaptability, energy efficiency, and ecological advantages compared to large-scale conventional and renewable energy cogeneration systems, representing an alternative for biofuel industrial processes where energy efficiency and pollutant emission reduction are required to be implemented for cleaner energy transitions. Nevertheless, Mexican national policy and biofuel regulation must be developed to encourage large-scale biodiesel production.
