Limiting distributions of triangle counts in linear preferential attachment models
Partha S. Dey, Grigory Terlov

Abstract
. We derive distributional approximations for the number of triangles in the linear preferential attachment model PAM n ​ ( m , δ ) \mathrm{PAM}_{n}(m,\delta) , where m ⩾ 2 m\geqslant 2 and δ > − m \delta>-m , with explicit rates of convergence. The limiting distribution undergoes a phase transition from Gaussian to another nontrivial distribution, which we characterize explicitly. The asymptotic behavior is governed by the interplay between the hidden random environment and the mean-field interaction effect. In particular, our analysis also yields a continuous phase transition in the expected number of triangles as δ \delta varies.



#1#

1.
Introduction and main results

In the study of random graphs and complex networks, the number of triangles plays a central role in quantifying local clustering and higher-order connectivity patterns. It provides insight into the structural properties and is closely tied to community formation and transitivity in real-world systems. For instance, the standard measure of clustering in a network, the clustering coefficient, is defined as the ratio of the number of triangles to the number of wedges (i.e., paths of length 2).

Preferential attachment models (PAM), introduced by Barabási and Albert in 1999
[
2
]
, are among the most studied classes of evolving random graphs. Their appeal lies in a simple “rich-get-richer” growth mechanism, where new vertices are more likely to attach to vertices of large degree. This generates scale-free degree sequences that typically exhibit power-law tails, consistent with observations across a range of networks, including citation graphs, the World Wide Web, and various social networks. At the same time, PAMs remain sufficiently tractable from the perspective of rigorous analysis, especially when the attachment function is linear.

In this paper, we study a variant of the linear preferential attachment model
\mathrm{PAM}_{n}(m,\delta)
, where
n\in\mathds{N}
denotes the number of vertices.
We refer the reader to
[
17
, Chapter 5]
for a systematic overview of the most common variants.
Since different variants of the model typically yield comparable large-scale (mean) behavior, the choice of the variant primarily affects which technical tools are available. On the other hand, it is not a priori clear that the same is true for distributional approximations. The variant considered in the present work is constructed recursively as follows:

Initial data:

m\in\mathds{N}
and
\delta>-m
.

\mathbf{n=2}
:
\mathrm{PAM}_{2}(m,\delta)
consists of two vertices
\{1,2\}
with
m
edges between them.

\mathbf{n=k}
: Given
\mathrm{PAM}_{k-1}(m,\delta)
, the
k
-th vertex is added with
m
edges
\{e_{k,\ell}\}_{\ell\in[m]}
. The edges connect sequentially to vertices of
\mathrm{PAM}_{k-1}(m,\delta)
with probabilities proportional to an affine function of the current degree, which is updated after each new connection; in other words, for
\ell\in[m-1]
and
v\in[k-1]
,

\pr(e_{k,\ell+1}\text{ connects to }v)=\frac{\deg^{(\ell)}_{k-1}(v)+\delta}{2m(k-2)+\ell+(k-1)\delta},

where
\deg_{k-1}^{(\ell)}(v)
denotes the degree of vertex
v\in\mathrm{PAM}_{k-1}(m,\delta)
after
\ell
edges from the
k
-th vertex have been connected to the graph.

The advantage of this variant is that it admits an exchangeable representation via a hidden environment given by a family of independent Beta-distributed random variables, see Section
2.1
.

For PAM

1.1.
Main results

Our first main result explains the nature of the phase transition in the mean behavior from Theorem
1.1
, by interpolating between different parameter regimes. To be precise, we consider a more general model constructed with the same steps as
\mathrm{PAM}_{n}
, but where the parameter
\delta
also depends on the current size of the system. Formal definition is given in Section
7
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
m\geqslant 2
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
\alpha>0
be fixed and
\delta_{k}:=2mc\,(\log k)^{-\alpha}
for
k\geqslant 2.

Let
T_{n}
be the number of triangles in
\mathrm{PAM}_{n}(m,(\delta_{k})_{k\in[n]})
. Then

\displaystyle\E T_{n}\cong\begin{cases}(\log n)^{1+2\alpha}&\text{ when }\alpha\in(0,1),c>0,\\
(\log n)^{3}&\text{ when }\alpha>1\text{ or }\alpha=1,c>-1,\\
(\log n)^{3}\log\log n&\text{ when }\alpha=1,c=-1,\\
(\log n)^{2-c}&\text{ when }\alpha=1,c<-1\\
(\log n)^{1+\alpha}e^{-\frac{c}{1-\alpha}(\log n)^{1-\alpha}}&\text{ when }\alpha\in(0,1),c<0.\end{cases}

In particular, fixing
\alpha=1
, the phase transition for
\E T_{n}
occurs at
c=-1
.

We believe that with techniques similar to ours, one can also show fluctuation phase transitions in the model. For example, in terms of fluctuations, fixing
\alpha=1
, the transition should occur at
c=1/2
, see open question
8.6
.

Our second main result establishes distributional limits for the number of triangles in PAM
(m,\delta)
and gives explicit bounds on the rates of convergence. This addresses a question raised in
[
10
, page 10]
.
Similarly to the behavior of the mean given by Theorem
1.1
, there are three different regimes depending on the sign of
\delta
. In the case when
\delta>0
, the limiting distribution is normal, and the analysis required for the proof is completely different from that in the remaining cases.

To measure the rate of convergence, we will use the
L^{1}
-Wasserstein distance
\textrm{d}_{\mathcal{W}}
. For two random variables
X
and
Y
, it is defined by

\textrm{d}_{\mathcal{W}}(X,Y):=\sup_{h:\,\mathrm{Lip}(h)\leqslant 1}\left|\E h(X)-\E h(Y)\right|.

Let
\psi_{1}\equiv 1
and
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i\geqslant 2}
be independent

\mathrm{Beta}(m+\delta,(2m+\delta)(i-1)-m)
random variables. Define the nonnegative sequence of real numbers
(b_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
by

(1)

\displaystyle b_{i}:=\begin{cases}1&i=1\\
\left\|\psi_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\prod_{t=2}^{i}\left\|1-\psi_{t}\right\|_{2}^{-2}&i\geqslant 2.\end{cases}

Observe that
(\psi_{i},b_{i})_{i\geqslant 2}
depend on the parameters
m,\delta
and define

(2)

\displaystyle s_{n}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\log n},&\text{if }\delta>0,\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{1}{4}(\log n)^{2},&\text{if }\delta=0,\\[4.30554pt]
\left({m}/{|\delta|}-1\right)n^{|\delta|/(2m+\delta)}\log n,&\text{if }\delta\in(-m,0).\end{cases}

For
\delta\leqslant 0
, we interpret
s_{n}
as the expected number of triangles that contain the starting vertex
1
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
m\geqslant 2
and
\delta>-m
be fixed. Let
T_{n}
be the number of triangles in
\mathrm{PAM}_{n}(m,\delta)
. We have

\var(T_{n})\cong s_{n}^{2}.

Moreover, there exists a constant
\gamma=\gamma(m,\delta)\in(0,\infty)
such that the following holds.

(1)

When
\delta>0
, we have

\displaystyle\textrm{d}_{\mathcal{W}}\left(\frac{T_{n}-\E T_{n}}{\gamma{s_{n}}},Z\right)\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log n}},

where
Z\sim\N(0,1)
.

(2)

When
\delta=0
, we have

\displaystyle\textrm{d}_{\mathcal{W}}\left(\frac{T_{n}-\E T_{n}}{\gamma{s_{n}}},\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}b_{i}\left(\frac{\psi_{i}^{2}}{\left\|\psi_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\prod_{j=i+1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\psi_{j})^{2}}{\left\|1-\psi_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}}-1\right)\right)\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log n}}.

(3)

When
\delta\in(-m,0)
, we have

\displaystyle\textrm{d}_{\mathcal{W}}\left(\frac{T_{n}}{\gamma s_{n}},\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}b_{i}\cdot\frac{\psi_{i}^{2}}{\left\|\psi_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\prod_{j=i+1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\psi_{j})^{2}}{\left\|1-\psi_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\right)\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log n}}.

We present our interpretation of the limiting distribution in parts
2
and
3
alongside simulations in Subsection
8.1
.

Remark 1.4
.

The value of the constant
\gamma
is given by

(3)

\gamma=\begin{cases}\frac{m}{\delta}\sqrt{\frac{(m-1)(m+\delta)(m+\delta+1)}{2m+\delta}}&\text{ if }\delta>0,\\
\qquad m^{2}(m-1)&\text{ if }\delta\leqslant 0.\end{cases}

Remark 1.5

(Optimality)
.

Since for
\delta>0
the rate of convergence in Theorem
1.3
is of order
1/\sqrt{\var(T_{n})}
, it is reasonable to expect it to be optimal, although it still requires establishing an anti-concentration result (or a local limit theorem). For
\delta\leqslant 0
, our rate of convergence is far from the reciprocal of the standard deviation, and so we do not yet have a strong enough understanding to conjecture the optimal rate. Establishing optimality in the former case and improving the convergence rate in the latter case would be an interesting research direction.

1.2.
Our techniques

Establishing limiting distribution results for evolving graphs is often more challenging than for their static counterparts. There are two primary reasons for that.

First, many common approaches for showing limiting distributions in discrete models seem to fail for preferential attachment models due to the global temporal dependence inherent in their evolution and the lack of exchangeability between edges. Thus, besides a few notable exceptions, the common techniques in this setting reduce to martingale convergence theorems and renewal-type arguments, both of which require careful control of relevant graph statistics. This is precisely the second primary reason for the difficulty of deriving distributional limits. The combinatorics get involved and require novel ideas even to derive the limiting behavior of the mean, e.g., see
[
5
,
8
,
10
]
. Naturally, tackling higher moments multiplies the complexity of the task.

It is of particular interest to expand applications of other techniques, such as Stein’s method, to non-static models. To prove distributional convergence via Stein’s method, one usually needs to either establish control on the change in the statistic of interest under a perturbation in the system via some sort of coupling or derive a quantified bound on the dependency between the random variables, see
[
13
,
6
]
.
However, in models such as
\mathrm{PAM}_{n}(m,\delta)
, any small perturbation such as resampling a single edge may cause a significant change in the later evolution of the graph, and there is no clear way to bound the “range” of dependence.
A recent successful example of such an application of this method in a non-static model is
[
4
]
, where the authors showed the central limit theorem for subgraph counts in the uniform attachment models by using the Stein–Chen method for Poisson approximation as an intermediate step.
In their case, the model shares many properties with
\mathrm{PAM}_{n}(m,\delta)
, except for the absence of bias in the attachment scheme.
It is plausible that their approach could be adapted to
\mathrm{PAM}_{n}(m,\delta)
when
\delta>0
; however, the computations became extremely involved, and we concluded that the size-biased coupling is not the most natural approach.

With these challenges in mind, our approach has several key ideas that we would like to highlight,

(1)

exchangeable description of the model,

(2)

centered-edge decomposition,

(3)

graphical computation,

(4)

applications of Stein’s method and
L_{2}
-bounds.

First, as we already mentioned, the sequential attachment variant of
\mathrm{PAM}_{n}(m,\delta)
admits an exchangeable description in terms of hidden weights introduced in
[
3
]
, see Subsection
2.1
. In particular, almost all of the analysis is carried out conditioned on the values of these weights.

•

Centered-edge decomposition.
Next, we work with the centered-edge decomposition of the subgraph counts. The idea of working with centered subgraph counts has been particularly useful for simplifying covariance computations in various settings; see, e.g.,
[
12
,
7
,
16
,
9
]
. Focusing on the number of triangles
T_{n}
in some random graph on
[n]
, we can rewrite it as follows

(4)

\displaystyle T_{n}-\E T_{n}=\widetilde{T}_{n}^{(0)}+\widetilde{T}_{n}^{(1)}+\widetilde{T}_{n}^{(2)}+\widetilde{T}_{n}^{(3)},

where, denoting by
\mathds{1}_{i\leftrightarrow j}
the indicator random variable that vertex
i
is connected to
j
and by
\overline{\mathds{1}_{i\leftrightarrow j}}
its centered version, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}_{n}^{(0)}:=\sum_{i<j<k}\pr(i\leftrightarrow j)\pr(i\leftrightarrow k)\pr(j\leftrightarrow k),

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}_{n}^{(1)}:=\sum_{i<j}\left(\sum_{k\neq i,j}\pr(i\leftrightarrow j)\pr(j\leftrightarrow k)\right)\overline{\mathds{1}_{i\leftrightarrow j}},

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}_{n}^{(2)}:=\sum_{i<j}\pr(i\leftrightarrow j)\sum_{k\neq i,j}\overline{\mathds{1}_{i\leftrightarrow k}}\cdot\overline{\mathds{1}_{j\leftrightarrow k}},

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}_{n}^{(3)}=\sum_{i<j<k}\overline{\mathds{1}_{i\leftrightarrow j}}\cdot\overline{\mathds{1}_{i\leftrightarrow k}}\cdot\overline{\mathds{1}_{j\leftrightarrow k}}.

Working with centered-edge counts has several advantages. Firstly, any two subgraphs that do not share any edges are automatically uncorrelated, which crucially simplifies the computation of the variances. Secondly, it highlights the competition between the terms
\widetilde{T}_{n}^{(0)},\widetilde{T}_{n}^{(1)},\widetilde{T}_{n}^{(2)},
and
\widetilde{T}_{n}^{(3)}
that otherwise might be overlooked. As we will see throughout this paper, depending on the parameter regime, different terms dominate, simplifying the analysis and providing intuition for the model’s behavior and possible phase transitions. For example, in the case
1
of Theorem
1.3
, the dominating term is
\widetilde{T}_{n}^{(3)}
while in the remaining cases it is
\widetilde{T}_{n}^{(0)}
. We interpret it as a competition between the mean-field interaction in the triangle formation and the effect of the hidden random environment. In particular, understanding the dominant term is key to establishing Theorem
1.2
.

In Appendix
A
, we illustrate the connection between the centered-edge decomposition and the classical Hoeffding decomposition in a different model of independent interest. Namely, we consider the random graph with immigrating vertices (RGIV), introduced by Aldous and Pittel in
[
1
]
. The analysis of this model is significantly less involved than that of PAM, while the model still exhibits nontrivial competition among terms in the centered-edge decomposition of triangle counts, or even edge counts. Hence, using similar ideas, we establish limiting distributions for these counts in various regimes.

•

Graphical computation.
To address the second challenge of unraveling combinatorics, we developed an intuitive book-keeping mechanism, which we call the graphical computation lemma, see Section
3
. While it is possible to do all of the computations by hand, in our opinion, it would take already involved computations to another level, making them extremely difficult to follow. On the other hand, many of the sums one has to compute share a similar structure and geometric interpretation. Therefore, the main idea of this tool is to convert the problem into a diagram reading, which will essentially evaluate the sum. In particular, with this lemma, one can quickly derive Theorem
1.1
in all three regimes of parameters, again, ignoring the constants that the original results carefully computed. It can also be used to derive analogous results for other subgraphs such as cycles and cliques, see Lemma
3.5
. Furthermore, by simplifying the computations in this way, we gain insight into the structure of the dominating terms. This led to much of the progress, even in results that technically do not explicitly rely on the graphical computation lemma, e.g., Theorem
1.2
. Finally, it also enabled us to give detailed predictions for the limiting distributions of cycles and cliques given in Open Questions
8.4
and
8.5
.

•

Stein’s method and
L_{2}
-techniques.
Finally, we approach case
1
of Theorem
1.3
in a different way than the other cases. When
\delta>0
, the mean-field interaction dominates the behavior, and we can apply Stein’s method of exchangeable pairs. In the remaining cases
2
and
3
, we reduce the problem to a sum of martingale differences and then carefully analyze the error terms in the
L_{2}
-norm.

2.1.
Urn graph and centered-edge decomposition

We briefly review the construction of the Pólya urn graph introduced in
[

\psi_{1}\equiv 1

be independent
\mathrm{Beta}(m+\delta,(2m+\delta)(i-1)-m)

\displaystyle\varphi_{j,n}:=\psi_{j}\prod_{t=j+1}^{n}(1-\psi_{t}),\quad S_{(k,n]}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\varphi_{j,n}=\prod_{t=k+1}^{n}(1-\psi_{t}),\quad I_{k}^{(n)}:=\left(S_{(k-1,n]},S_{(k,n]}\right].

Conditional on
\boldsymbol{\psi}:=(\psi_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}

\displaystyle\{U_{k,\ell}\}_{k\in[n],\ell\in[m]}\text{ be independent and uniformly distributed on }[0,S_{(k-1,n]}].

(9)

\displaystyle T_{n}^{(2)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i<j}\theta_{ij}\sum_{k\neq i,j}\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}=1}^{m}\overline{\omega}_{ik}^{[\ell_{1}]}\overline{\omega}_{jk}^{[\ell_{2}]},

Auxiliary quantities

Since our analysis becomes quite technical, we introduce several variables to simplify expressions and isolate statements that define their properties. Some of the computations below are similar to those from
[

First, it turns out to be more convenient to work with triangles indexed by their oldest vertex, hence we introduce the following quantities and write the sum from the conditional mean in (

\Delta_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}\Delta_{n,i}

where

(11)

\displaystyle\Delta_{n,i}:=\sum_{i<j<k\leqslant n}\theta_{ij}\theta_{ik}\theta_{jk}=\psi_{i}^{2}\cdot\sum_{i<j<k\leqslant n}\prod_{t=i+1,t\neq j}^{k-1}(1-\psi_{t})^{2}\cdot\psi_{j}(1-\psi_{j}),\quad i\geqslant 1.

Observe that

is the conditional expectation given
\boldsymbol{\psi}
of the number of triangles with
i
as the lowest labeled vertex.
For

such that

(12)

\displaystyle\mu_{i,j,k}

\displaystyle:=\E\theta_{ij}\theta_{ik}\theta_{jk}.

We have

\displaystyle\mu_{i,\cdot,\cdot}^{(n)}:=\E\Delta_{n,i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j<k\leqslant n}\E\psi_{i}^{2}\cdot\prod_{t=i+1}^{k-1}\E(1-\psi_{t})^{2}\cdot\frac{\E(\psi_{j}(1-\psi_{j}))}{\E(1-\psi_{j})^{2}}

(13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j<k\leqslant n}\frac{\E\psi_{i}^{2}}{\prod_{t=2}^{i}\E(1-\psi_{t})^{2}}\cdot\prod_{t=2}^{k-1}\E(1-\psi_{t})^{2}\cdot\frac{\E(\psi_{j}(1-\psi_{j}))}{\E(1-\psi_{j})^{2}}.

In particular,

Lemma 2.6

For

and
\delta>-m

\beta:=(m+\delta)/(2m+\delta)

\displaystyle\E\psi_{t}^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(m+\delta+k)}{\Gamma(m+\delta)}\cdot\frac{\Gamma((2m+\delta)t-2m)}{\Gamma((2m+\delta)t-2m+k)},

\displaystyle\E(1-\psi_{t})^{k}

3.
Graphical computation

We would like to reiterate that random variables
\theta_{ij}
’s are not independent; therefore, when computing the expectation of products, one needs to rewrite these terms in terms of corresponding products of
\psi_{\cdot}
or
(1-\psi_{\cdot})
and redo the computation similar to the one above. In this particular example, using Lemma
2.9
we get (assuming without loss of generality that
j<k
)

\displaystyle\E\theta_{ij}\theta_{ik}

\displaystyle=\E\psi_{i}^{2}\left(\prod_{t=i+1}^{j-1}\E(1-\psi_{t})^{2}\right)\prod_{t=j}^{k-1}\E(1-\psi_{t})\cong\frac{1}{i^{2}}\left(\frac{i}{j}\right)^{2\beta}\left(\frac{j}{k}\right)^{\beta}=i^{2\beta-2}j^{-\beta}k^{-\beta}.

To derive the expected values and the variances of various subgraph counts in PAM
(m,\delta)
, one needs to compute the sum of such expectations of products. In order to significantly simplify proofs, we introduce a “graphical computation” lemma (see Lemma
3.1
) as a convenient bookkeeping mechanism. To make the exact statement intuitive, we find it helpful to first consider the following computation, in which we derive the sum in a brute-force fashion.

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j<k}\E\theta_{ij}^{2}\theta_{ik}\theta_{jk}

\displaystyle=\E\psi_{i}^{3}\left(\prod_{t=i+1}^{j-1}(1-\psi_{t})^{3}\right)(1-\psi_{j})\psi_{j}\prod_{t=j}^{k-1}(1-\psi_{t})^{2}

(17)

\displaystyle[\text{by Lemma}~\ref{lem:powerk}]\quad

\displaystyle\cong\sum_{i<j<k}\frac{1}{i^{3}}\left(\frac{i}{j}\right)^{3\beta}\frac{1}{j}\left(\frac{j}{k}\right)^{2\beta}\cong\sum_{i<j<k}\frac{1}{i^{3-3\beta}j^{1+\beta}k^{2\beta}}.

We now would like sum first over
k
(which ranges from
j
till
n
), then over
j
(which ranges from
i
till
n
), and finally over
i
(which ranges from
1
till
n
). However it is clear that the answer will depend on the value of
\beta
as in each step we compare the power of the variable with
1
. Hence, we consider the following three cases.

Case of
\beta\in(1/2,1)
:
\displaystyle\sum_{i<j<k}\frac{1}{i^{3-3\beta}j^{1+\beta}k^{2\beta}}\cong\sum_{i<j}\frac{1}{i^{3-3\beta}j^{3\beta}}\cong\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{i^{2}}\cong 1.

Case of
\beta=1/2
:
\displaystyle\sum_{i<j<k}\frac{1}{i^{3-3\beta}j^{1+\beta}k^{2\beta}}=\sum_{i<j<k}\frac{1}{i^{\frac{3}{2}}j^{\frac{3}{2}}k}\cong\log n\sum_{i<j}\frac{1}{i^{\frac{3}{2}}j^{\frac{3}{2}}}\cong\log n.

Case of
\beta\in(0,1/2)
:
\displaystyle\sum_{i<j<k}\frac{1}{i^{3-3\beta}j^{1+\beta}k^{2\beta}}\cong\sum_{i<j}\frac{n^{1-2\beta}}{i^{3-3\beta}j^{1+\beta}}\cong\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{n^{1-2\beta}}{i^{3-2\beta}}\cong n^{1-2\beta}.

Therefore

(18)

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j<k}\E\theta_{ij}^{2}\theta_{ik}\theta_{jk}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\beta>1/2\\
\log n&\text{ if }\beta=1/2\\
n^{1-2\beta}&\text{ if }\beta<1/2\end{cases}.

Indeed, each time when we sum over a variable we compare its power, denote it by
\gamma
, with
1
(or with
-1
if it is in the numerator) and if
\gamma>1
it contributes a constant factor and
(\gamma-1)
to the power of the next variable, if
\gamma=0
it gives a
\log n
factor, while if
\gamma<1
it contributes
n^{\left|\gamma-1\right|}
factor. Therefore, we formalize this computation in the following general lemma.

Lemma 3.1

(Graphical computation)
.

For
m\geqslant 2
and
\delta>-m
let
\beta:=(m+\delta)/(2m+\delta)
.
Let
H=([k],E)
be a finite connected (undirected) multigraph that does not contain self-loops but might contain multi-edges. Then the expectation of the
H
-subgraph count in
\mathrm{PAM}_{n}(m,\delta)
is given by

(19)

\displaystyle\E\sum_{1\leqslant i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leqslant n}\prod_{(a,b)\in E\atop a>b}\theta_{i_{b}i_{a}}\cong F_{k}\biggl(d_{1}-\beta\ell_{1},d_{2}-\beta(\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}),\ldots,d_{k}-\beta(\ell_{k}-\ell_{k-1})\biggr),

where

•

d_{j}
is the in-degree of vertex
j
(i.e., the number of edges
(j,k)\in E
with
k>j
),

•

\ell_{j}
is the number of edges
(u,v)\in E
such that
v\leqslant j<u
,

•

F_{k}(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}):=\sum_{1\leqslant i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leqslant n}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}i_{j}^{a_{j}}\right)^{-1}
.

Moreover, the function
F_{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle F_{k}(

\displaystyle a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k})

\displaystyle\cong F_{t-1}(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{t-1})\cdot\begin{cases}n^{k-t+1-(a_{t}+a_{t+1}+\cdots+a_{k})}&\text{ if }a_{t}+a_{t+1}+\cdots+a_{k}<k-t+1\\
\log n&\text{ if }a_{t}+a_{t+1}+\cdots+a_{k}=k-t+1\end{cases},

where
t:=\max\{i\in[k]\mid a_{i}+a_{i+1}+\cdots+a_{k}\leqslant k-i+1\}
and

\displaystyle F_{k}(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k})\cong 1\text{ if no such $t$ exists.}

Note that the function
F_{k}
also depends on
n
, which we omit from the notation as we do with many quantities throughout the paper. Before delving into the proof, we present a few applications.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
m\geqslant 2
and
\delta>0
. Let
H=([k],E)
be a finite connected multigraph such that all vertices have degrees at least two. Then the expectation of the
H
-subgraph count in
\mathrm{PAM}_{n}(m,\delta)
is

(1)

\Theta(\log n)
if
H
is a cycle,

(2)

\Theta(1)
otherwise.

Proof.

Recalling the notations
\{(d_{i},\ell_{i})\}_{i\in[k]}
from Lemma
3.1
notice that

\displaystyle\ell_{i}-\ell_{i-1}=d_{\mathrm{in}}(i)-d_{\mathrm{out}}(i)=2d_{i}-\deg_{H}(i),

where
d_{\mathrm{in}}(i)=d_{i}
(resp.
d_{\mathrm{out}}(i)
) denotes the in-degree (resp. out-degree) in the orientation of edges of
H
from the endpoint with the higher index to the one with the lower one, and
\deg_{H}(i)
denotes the degree of
i
in
H
. Consider any
t>1
, then summing over
i
in
\{t,t+1,\ldots,k\}
we get

\displaystyle-\ell_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=t}^{k}2d_{i}-\deg_{H}(i)=2\left(\sum_{j=t}^{k}d_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\deg_{H}(i)\right).

In the next expression, we use the facts that
\deg_{H}(i)\geqslant 2
for all
i
, that
H
is connected (implying
\ell_{i}>0
for every
i\in[k-1]
), and that
\delta>0
, which in turn yields
\beta>1/2
. Using these observations, we derive that for all
t>1

\displaystyle\sum_{j=t}^{k}(d_{j}-\beta(\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}))=\sum_{j=t}^{k-1}d_{j}+\beta\ell_{t-1}

\displaystyle=(k-t+1)+\sum_{j=t}^{k-1}(d_{j}-1)+\beta\ell_{t-1}

\displaystyle\geqslant(k-t+1)+\sum_{j=t}^{k-1}\left(d_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\deg_{H}(i)\right)+\beta\ell_{t-1}

\displaystyle=(k-t+1)+\left(\beta-\frac{1}{2}\right)\ell_{t-1}>k-t+1.

Finally, the case when
t=1
is treated as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}(d_{j}-\beta(\ell_{j}-\ell_{j-1}))-k=\sum_{j=1}^{k}d_{j}-k=\left|E(H)\right|-\left|V(H)\right|,

which equals zero when
H
is a cycle and is strictly positive when
H
is any other connected multigraph with the lowest degree at least two.
The conclusion now follows from Lemma
3.1
.

\blacksquare

\displaystyle\quad+2\sum_{i=1\atop i<i^{\prime}}^{n-2}\sum_{k=i^{\prime}+1}^{n-1}\underbrace{\mu_{i,\cdot,\cdot}^{(n)}}_{s_{n}/i^{2-2\beta}}\underbrace{\mu_{i^{\prime},\cdot,\cdot}^{(n)}}_{s_{n}/(i^{\prime})^{2-2\beta}}\underbrace{\E(X_{i}Y_{i+1}\cdots Y_{i^{\prime}-1})\E(X_{i^{\prime}}Y_{i^{\prime}})\cdot\E(Y_{i^{\prime}+1}^{2}\cdots Y_{k-1}^{2})}_{\text{uniformly bounded}}\underbrace{\var(Y_{k})}_{1/k^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim s_{n}^{2},

where, with under-braces, we denote the orders of the respective terms.
The claim follows by multiplying with
\left\|\prod_{j=n}^{\infty}Y_{j}-1\right\|_{2}\cong n^{-1/2}
.

\blacksquare

\delta\in(-m,0]

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}r_{i,4}\right\|_{2}

7.
Phase transition in
\delta

Let
m\geqslant 2
be a fixed integer. Fix a sequence of real numbers
\boldsymbol{\delta}=(\delta_{t})_{t\geqslant 1}\in[-1,1]^{\mathds{N}}
. We modify the definition of the random network – the network is constructed recursively as follows.

\mathbf{n=1}
:
\mathrm{PAM}_{1}(m,\boldsymbol{\delta})
consists of a single vertex and no edges.

\mathbf{n=2}
:
\mathrm{PAM}_{2}(m,\boldsymbol{\delta})
consists of two vertices with
m
edges between them.

\mathbf{n=k}
: Given
\mathrm{PAM}_{k-1}(m,\boldsymbol{\delta})
, the
k
-th vertex is added with
m
edges
\{e_{k,\ell}\}_{\ell\in[m]}
. The edges connect sequentially to vertices of
\mathrm{PAM}_{k-1}(m,\boldsymbol{\delta})
with probabilities proportional to an affine function of the current degree, which is updated after each new connection; in other words, for
\ell\in[m-1]
and
v\in[k-1]
,

\pr(e_{k,\ell+1}\text{ connects to }v)=\frac{\deg^{(\ell)}_{k-1}(v)+\delta_{v}}{2m(k-2)+\ell+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\delta_{i}},

where
\deg_{k-1}^{(\ell)}(v)
denotes the degree of vertex
v\in\mathrm{PAM}_{k-1}(m,\boldsymbol{\delta})
after
\ell
edges from the
k
-th vertex have been connected to the graph.

The above graph process can also be described by the Pólya urn graph introduced in
[
3
]
with a little modification.
Let
\psi_{1}\equiv 1
and, for all
i\geqslant 2
, let
\psi_{i}
be independent
\mathrm{Beta}(m+\delta_{i},m(2i-3)+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\delta_{j})
random variables. Define

\displaystyle\varphi_{j,n}:=\psi_{j}\prod_{t=j+1}^{n}(1-\psi_{t}),\quad S_{(k,n]}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\varphi_{j,n}=\prod_{t=k+1}^{n}(1-\psi_{t}),\quad I_{k}^{(n)}:=\left(S_{(k-1,n]},S_{(k,n]}\right].

Conditional on
\boldsymbol{\psi}:=(\psi_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
, let

\displaystyle\{U_{k,\ell}\}_{k\in[n],\ell\in[m]}\text{ be independent and uniformly distributed on }[0,S_{(k-1,n]}].

For any
1\leqslant j<k\leqslant n
, place an edge from
k
to
j
for every
U_{k,\ell}
contained in
I_{j}^{(n)}
for some
\ell\in[m]
. The random graph constructed in such a manner has the same distribution as the sequential linear preferential attachment model
\mathrm{PAM}_{n}(m,\boldsymbol{\delta})
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

In particular, we have the conditional expectation given
\boldsymbol{\psi}
of the number of triangles with
i
as the lowest labeled vertex is
\E(T_{n,i}\mid\boldsymbol{\psi})=m^{2}(m-1)\Delta_{n,i}
for
i\geqslant 1
where

(35)

\displaystyle\Delta_{n,i}:=\psi_{i}^{2}\sum_{i<j<k\leqslant n}\prod_{t=i+1,t\neq j}^{k-1}(1-\psi_{t})^{2}\cdot\psi_{j}(1-\psi_{j})

with mean

(36)

\displaystyle S_{n}(i):=\E\Delta_{n,i}=\frac{\E\psi_{i}^{2}}{\prod_{t=2}^{i}\E(1-\psi_{t})^{2}}\sum_{i<j<k\leqslant n}\prod_{t=2}^{k-1}\E(1-\psi_{t})^{2}\cdot\frac{\E\psi_{j}(1-\psi_{j})}{\E(1-\psi_{j})^{2}}.

Define,
f_{1}=g_{1}=0,h_{1}=1
and for
n\geqslant 2

f_{n}:=1+\sum_{k=2}^{n-1}\prod_{t=2}^{k}\E(1-\psi_{t})^{2},\quad g_{n}:=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{\E\psi_{j}(1-\psi_{j})}{\E(1-\psi_{j})^{2}},\quad h_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\E\psi_{i}^{2}}{\Delta f_{i+1}},

so that we have with
\Delta f_{n}:=f_{n}-f_{n-1},

\Delta g_{n}:=g_{n}-g_{n-1},

\Delta h_{n}=h_{n}-h_{n-1}
,

\E\Delta_{n,i}=\sum_{i<j<k\leqslant n}\Delta h_{i}\cdot\Delta g_{j}\cdot\Delta f_{k}.

We fix
c\in\mathds{R}
, and
\alpha>0
. Define
\delta_{1}\equiv 0
and

\delta_{t}:=2mc\cdot(\log t)^{-\alpha}\text{ for }t\geqslant 2.

Thus, we have

\log\E(1-\psi_{t})^{2}=-\frac{2}{t}\cdot\frac{m+\delta_{t}}{2m+\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\delta_{i}}+O(1/t^{2})=-\frac{1}{t}-\frac{2\delta_{t}-\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\delta_{i}}{2mt+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\delta_{i}}+O(1/t^{2}),\quad t\geqslant 2.

Note that,
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}=\Delta_{n}\cdot(1+O(1/\log n))
,
\E\psi_{n}^{2}\cong n^{-2}
and
\Delta g_{n}\cong n^{-1}
.
In particular,

\displaystyle\log\Delta f_{n}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{t=2}^{n-1}\frac{1}{t}-\sum_{t=2}^{n-1}\frac{2\delta_{t}-\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\delta_{i}}{2mt+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\delta_{i}}+O(1)

\displaystyle=-\log n-c\int_{2}^{n}\frac{dx}{x(\log x)^{\alpha}}+O\left(1+\int_{2}^{n}\frac{dx}{x(\log x)^{1+\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\log n-c\int_{2}^{n}\frac{dx}{x(\log x)^{\alpha}}+O(1).

Case
\alpha>1
.

First, we study the case of
\alpha>1,c\in\mathds{R}
. We have
\Delta f_{n}\cong n^{-1}
and thus

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E\Delta_{n,i}\cong\sum_{1\leqslant i<j<k\leqslant n}\frac{1}{i}\cdot\frac{1}{j}\cdot\frac{1}{k}\cong(\log n)^{3}.

Moreover, we get
S_{n}(i)\cong{(\log n)^{2}}/{i}
for all
i
. This behavior is the same as the
\delta=0
case.

Case
\alpha=1
.

Next, we consider the
\alpha=1,c\neq 0
case. We have
\Delta f_{n}\cong n^{-1}(\log n)^{-c}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E\Delta_{n,i}\cong\sum_{1\leqslant i<j<k\leqslant n}\frac{(\log i)^{c}}{i}\cdot\frac{1}{j}\cdot\frac{1}{k(\log k)^{c}}.

Depending on whether
c>-1
,
c=-1
, or
c<-1
, we get different behavior for the sum. For
c>-1
, we get
(\log n)^{3}
; for
c=-1
, we get
(\log n)^{3}\log\log n
; and for
c<-1
, we get
(\log n)^{2-c}
.
Moreover, we have

S_{n}(i)\cong\begin{cases}\frac{(\log i)^{2}}{i},&c>2,\\
\frac{(\log i)^{2}}{i}\cdot(\log\log n-\log\log i_{0}),&c=2,\\
\frac{(\log i)^{c}}{i}\cdot(\log n)^{2-c},&c<2.\end{cases}

Case
\alpha<1
.

Finally, we fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
. We have

\Delta f_{n}\cong\frac{1}{n}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{1-\alpha}\cdot(\log n)^{1-\alpha}\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle S_{n}(i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j<k\leqslant n}\Delta h_{i}\cdot\Delta g_{j}\cdot\Delta f_{k}

\displaystyle\cong\sum_{i<j<k\leqslant n}\frac{1}{ijk}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c}{1-\alpha}\cdot(\log k)^{1-\alpha}+\frac{c}{1-\alpha}\cdot(\log i)^{1-\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\cong\sum_{i<k\leqslant n}\frac{\log(k/i)}{ik}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c}{1-\alpha}\cdot(\log k)^{1-\alpha}+\frac{c}{1-\alpha}\cdot(\log i)^{1-\alpha}\right)

First, we focus on the case
c>0
. We have

\displaystyle i\cdot S_{n}(i)

\displaystyle\cong\int_{i}^{n}\frac{\log(x/i)}{x}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{1-\alpha}((\log x)^{1-\alpha}-(\log i)^{1-\alpha})\right)\!dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\log(n/i)}u\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{c}{1-\alpha}((u+\log i)^{1-\alpha}-(\log i)^{1-\alpha})\right)\!du\cong(\log i)^{2\alpha}.

Here, we used the fact that for
b,u>0,\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have

\frac{u}{b^{\alpha}+u^{\alpha}}\leqslant\int_{0}^{u}(b+x)^{-\alpha}dx=\frac{(b+u)^{1-\alpha}-b^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\leqslant\frac{u}{b^{\alpha}}.

Thus, with
b=\log i\geqslant 1
, the integral satisfies

b^{2\alpha}\int_{0}^{(\log n-b)/b^{\alpha}}ue^{-cu}du\leqslant\int_{0}^{\log n-b}u\exp\left(-c\cdot\frac{(b+u)^{1-\alpha}-b^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\right)\!du\leqslant b^{2\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}u\exp\left(-\frac{cu}{1+u^{\alpha}}\right)\!du.

In particular, we get for
c>0
,

S_{n}(i)\cong\frac{1}{i}(\log i)^{2\alpha}

and

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E\Delta_{n,i}\cong(\log n)^{1+2\alpha}.

For
c<0
, we get

f_{n}\cong F(\log n)\cong(\log n)^{\alpha}\exp(-c/(1-\alpha)\cdot(\log n)^{1-\alpha})

where

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\exp(-c/(1-\alpha)y^{1-\alpha})dy

\displaystyle=\exp(-c/(1-\alpha)x^{1-\alpha})\int_{0}^{x}\exp(c/(1-\alpha)(x^{1-\alpha}-(x-u)^{1-\alpha}))du

\displaystyle\cong\exp(-c/(1-\alpha)x^{1-\alpha})\ (\log n)^{\alpha}\text{ when }x\gg 1

and

\displaystyle\E\Delta_{n,i}=\Delta h_{i}\cdot\sum_{i<j<k\leqslant n}\Delta g_{j}\cdot\Delta f_{k}

\displaystyle=\Delta h_{i}\cdot\sum_{i<j<n}\Delta g_{j}\cdot(f_{n}-f_{j})

\displaystyle=\Delta h_{i}\cdot\biggl(f_{n}\sum_{i<j<n}\Delta g_{j}-\sum_{i<j<n}f_{j}\Delta g_{j}\biggr)\cong\Delta h_{i}\cdot f_{n}\cdot\log n.

Note that,
\Delta h_{i}\cong\frac{1}{i}\exp(c/(1-\alpha)\cdot(\log i)^{1-\alpha})
is summable when
c<0
. Thus we get

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\E\Delta_{n,i}\cong f_{n}\cdot\log n\cong(\log n)^{1+\alpha}\exp\left(-c/(1-\alpha)\cdot(\log n)^{1-\alpha}\right).

This completes the proof.

\blacksquare

Corollary 7.1
.

As
n\to\infty
, the asymptotics of

are as follows.

•

Case
\alpha>1
.

S_{n}(i)\cong\frac{(\log n)^{2}}{i}
for all

.

•

Case
\alpha=1

S_{n}(i)\cong\begin{cases}\frac{(\log i)^{2}}{i},&c>2,\\
\frac{(\log i)^{2}}{i}\cdot(\log\log n-\log\log i),&c=2,\\

•

Case

\frac{1}{i}\exp\!\big(\frac{|c|}{1-\alpha}\cdot[(\log n)^{1-\alpha}-(\log i)^{1-\alpha}]\big),&c<0.\end{cases}

8.2.
Stability of results in other variants

As mentioned in the introduction, different variants of linear preferential attachment often yield comparable mean behavior across many statistics of interest. We already saw an example of this in Theorem

Appendix A
Edge and triangle counts in RGIV

In this Section, we briefly consider a different model, the random graph model with immigrating vertices, introduced by Aldous and Pittel in
[
1
]
, to highlight the use of the centered-edge decomposition and the competition between terms in the context of distributional convergence for small subgraph counts. In this model, the graph is also constructed in continuous time as follows.

Initial data:

\lambda:=\lambda(t):(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
,
G_{0}=\emptyset
.

Vertices:
The vertices arrive according to a Poisson process with rate
\lambda
.

Edges:
The edge arrival process between two vertices
i<j
already present in the graph is given by i.i.d. Poisson processes of rate
1/\lambda
.

We observe the graph at time
t
where
\lambda\to\infty,\lambda t\to\infty
.

A.1.
Edge count

Let
N_{t}
be the number of vertices at time
t
with
N_{t}\sim\poi(\lambda t).
Let
\mathcal{E}_{t}
be the number of edges at time
t
, respectively. Define

\theta:={t}/{\lambda},\qquad\mu_{t}:=\E\mathcal{E}_{t}\text{ and }\sigma_{t}^{2}=\var(\mathcal{E}_{t}).

Throughout the section, we assume that
\lambda\to\infty
. We will prove the following.

Theorem A.1

(Limit Theorems for the edge count)
.

The following convergences hold as
\lambda\to\infty
.

(i)

When
\lambda t^{3}\to c\in(0,\infty)
, we have

\dtv(\mathcal{E}_{t},\poi(c/6))\lesssim t/\lambda+t(t+\sqrt{\lambda t^{3}})+\left|\lambda t^{3}-c\right|.

(ii)

When
\lambda t^{3}\to\infty
and
t=O(\lambda)
, we have

\textrm{d}_{\mathcal{W}}\left((\mathcal{E}_{t}-\mu_{t})/\sigma_{t},\N(0,1)\right)\lesssim\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}}

where

\sigma_{t}^{2}=(1+o(1))\cdot\begin{cases}\frac{1}{6}\lambda t^{3}+\frac{2}{15}\lambda t^{5}&\text{ when }t\ll\lambda\\
C_{\theta}\cdot\lambda^{3}t^{3}&\text{ when }t\approx\lambda,\end{cases}

for a constant
C_{\theta}
depending on
\theta=t/\lambda
.

We provide a skeleton of the proof of Theorem
A.1
, which crucially depends on the decomposition of
\mathcal{E}_{t}
into a sum of uncorrelated random variables. Depending on the assumption on
t=t_{\lambda}
, only a few terms will dominate.
First, we note that conditionally on
N_{t}=n
, the arrival times are distributed as the order statistics of
n
many i.i.d.
\mathrm{Uniform}(0,t)
random variables. Let
\{\xi_{ij}\}_{i,j\geqslant 1}
be i.i.d.
\textrm{Exp}(1)
and
\{U_{i}\}_{i\geqslant 1}
be i.i.d.
\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
random variables, independent of each other and
N_{t}
.
The edge count process can be written as

\mathcal{E}_{t}=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N_{t}}\mathds{1}_{\{\lambda\xi_{ij}\leqslant\min(tU_{i},tU_{j})\}}.

Define
\omega_{ij}:=\mathds{1}_{\xi_{ij}\leqslant\theta(U_{i}\wedge U_{j})},1\leqslant i<j<\infty

so that

\mathcal{E}_{t}=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N_{t}}\omega_{ij}.

Define the functions

h_{\theta}(u,v):=\pr(\xi\leqslant\theta(u\wedge v))=1-e^{-\theta\min(u,v)}

and

\displaystyle H(\theta):=\E h_{\theta}(U_{1},U_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\theta^{2}}\bigl(1-\theta+\theta^{2}/2-e^{-\theta}\bigr)=\frac{\theta}{3}(1+o(1))\text{ when }\theta\ll 1.

Also, define the
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{F}_{t}
generated by
\{N_{t};U_{i},1\leqslant i\leqslant N_{t}\}
. Denote

h_{ij}:=h_{\theta}(U_{i},U_{j})\text{ and }\overline{\omega}_{ij}:=\mathds{1}_{\xi_{ij}\leqslant\theta(U_{i}\wedge U_{j})}-h_{ij},

Hence

\mathcal{M}_{t}:=\E(\mathcal{E}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N_{t}}h_{ij}.

Using the fact that
\E N_{t}(N_{t}-1)=\lambda^{2}t^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{t}=\E\mathcal{E}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}t^{2}H(t/\lambda)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{6}\lambda t^{3}\cdot(1+o(1))&\text{ when }\theta\ll 1,\\
\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}t^{2}H(\theta)&\text{ when }\theta\approx 1.\end{cases}

In this case, instead of considering the centered-edge decomposition for
\mathcal{M}_{t}
conditioned on
N_{t}
in the form of (
4
), we find it easier to work with the classical Hoeffding decomposition; nonetheless, it virtually accomplishes the same goal.
Since
\mathcal{M}_{t}\mid N_{t}
is a rank two U-statistic, and using Hoeffding’s decomposition with
g_{\theta}(u):=\E h_{\theta}(U_{1},u),
we can write

\mathcal{E}_{t}-\mu_{t}=S_{t}+L_{t}+D_{t}+R_{t,1}+R_{t,2}

where

(40)

\displaystyle S_{t}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N_{t}}\overline{\omega}_{ij},

(41)

\displaystyle L_{t}

\displaystyle:=(N_{t}-1)\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}\bigr(g_{\theta}(U_{i})-H(\theta)\bigl),

(42)

\displaystyle D_{t}

\displaystyle:=\lambda tH(\theta)\cdot(N_{t}-\lambda t),

(43)

\displaystyle R_{t,1}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N_{t}}\left(h_{\theta}(U_{i},U_{j})-g_{\theta}(U_{i})-g_{\theta}(U_{j})+H(\theta)\right),

(44)

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad R_{t,2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}H(\theta)((N_{t}-\lambda t)^{2}-N_{t}).

The terms
R_{t,1}
and
R_{t,2}
are
O_{P}(t^{2})
as

\displaystyle\E\var(R_{t,1}\mid N_{t})

\displaystyle=\E\binom{N_{t}}{2}\cdot\var(h_{\theta}(U_{1},U_{2})-g_{\theta}(U_{1})-g_{\theta}(U_{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}t^{2}\var(h_{\theta}(U_{1},U_{2})-g_{\theta}(U_{1})-g_{\theta}(U_{2}))\approx t^{4}

and

\E\left|R_{t,2}\right|\leqslant H(\theta)\lambda t\leqslant t^{2}

when
t=O(\lambda)
. Here, we used the fact that
h_{\theta}(u,v)=\theta(u\wedge v)+O(\theta^{2})
when
\theta\ll 1
.

Now, for the centered Bernoulli sum
S_{t}
, the conditional variance can be written as

\var(S_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N_{t}}(h_{2\theta}(U_{i},U_{j})-h_{\theta}(U_{i},U_{j})).

Taking expectation
gives

\displaystyle\E\var(S_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})

\displaystyle=\E\binom{N_{t}}{2}\cdot\bigl(H(2\theta)-H(\theta)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\E\frac{N_{t}(N_{t}-1)}{\lambda^{2}t^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}t^{2}\bigl(H(2\theta)-H(\theta)\bigr)=\E\frac{N_{t}(N_{t}-1)}{\lambda^{2}t^{2}}\frac{1}{\theta}\bigl(H(2\theta)-H(\theta)\bigr)\cdot\frac{1}{2}\lambda t^{3}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\E\var(L_{t}\mid N_{t})

\displaystyle=\var(g_{\theta}(U))\E N_{t}(N_{t}-1)^{2}=(1+o(1))\cdot\begin{cases}\frac{1}{45}\lambda t^{5}&\text{ when }t\ll\lambda,\\
\lambda^{3}t^{3}\cdot\var(g_{\theta}(U))&\text{ when }t\approx\lambda,\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\var(D_{t})=H(\theta)^{2}\lambda^{3}t^{3}=(1+o(1))\cdot\begin{cases}\frac{1}{9}\lambda t^{5}&\text{ when }t\ll\lambda,\\
\lambda^{3}t^{3}\cdot H(\theta)^{2}&\text{ when }t\approx\lambda.\end{cases}

For the limiting variance, what matters is whether
t\to\infty
or not. Under our assumption,
\lambda
is going to
\infty
, when
t=\Theta(1)
both
S_{t}
and
M_{t}
terms contribute.

When
t\ll 1
, by the Stein-Chen method of Poisson approximation for the sum of independent Bernoulli random variables, conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, we have

\dtv(\mathcal{E}_{t},\poi(M_{t}))\leqslant\max_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N_{t}}h_{\theta}(U_{i},U_{j})\leqslant\theta,

which is small since
t\ll 1\ll\lambda
.
Moreover, we have

\dtv(\poi(M_{t}),\poi(\mu_{t})))\leqslant\E\left|M_{t}-\mu_{t}\right|\leqslant\left\|M_{t}-\mu_{t}\right\|_{2}

which is bounded by
O(t^{2}+t\sqrt{\lambda t^{3}})
.

When
t=\Theta(1)
, the dominating terms are
S_{t},L_{t},D_{t}
which jointly converge to independent Gaussian after proper scaling. When
1\ll t=O(\lambda)
, the dominating terms are (
41
) and (
42
). The distributional limit follows from the Strong Law of Large Numbers, classical CLT for the sum of i.i.d. random variables, and the convergence of characteristic functions.

A.2.
Triangle count

Theorem A.2

(CLT for the triangle count)
.

Let
T_{t}
be the number of triangles at time
t
. The following convergences hold as
\lambda\to\infty
.

(i)

When
t\to c\in(0,\infty)
, we have

\dtv(T_{t},\poi(c^{6}/(2\cdot 3^{4})))\to 0.

(ii)

When
t\to\infty
and
t=O(\lambda)
, we have

\textrm{d}_{\mathcal{W}}\left((T_{t}-\E T_{t})/\sqrt{\var(T_{t})},\N(0,1)\right)\to 0

where

\var(T_{t})\cong t^{6}(1+t^{5}/\lambda).

Now, following similar steps to the number-of-edges analysis, we first analyze the triangle counts
T_{t}
conditioned on the
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, which is determined by the number of vertices and their arrival times.
We decompose
T_{t}
conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
as

T_{t}=\Delta_{t}+T_{t,1}+T_{t,2}+T_{t,3},

where

(45)

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j<k\leqslant N_{t}}h_{ij}h_{ik}h_{jk},

(46)

\displaystyle T_{t,1}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i<j\leqslant N_{t}}\left(\sum_{k\neq i,j}h_{ik}h_{jk}\right)\overline{\omega}_{ij},

(47)

\displaystyle T_{t,2}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i<j\leqslant N_{t}}h_{ij}\sum_{k\neq i,j}\overline{\omega}_{ik}\overline{\omega}_{jk},

(48)

\displaystyle\text{and }\quad T_{t,3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j<k\leqslant N_{t}}\overline{\omega}_{ij}\overline{\omega}_{ik}\overline{\omega}_{jk}.

Recall that,
h_{ij}=h_{\theta}(U_{i},U_{j})
. Clearly, we have

\E T_{t}=\E(h_{\theta}(U_{1},U_{2})h_{\theta}(U_{2},U_{3})h_{\theta}(U_{1},U_{3}))\cdot\E\binom{N_{t}}{3}\cong t^{6}

when
t=O(\lambda)
. It is straightforward to verify that

\displaystyle\E\var(T_{t,1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})

\displaystyle\cong\E\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N_{t}}h_{ij}\left(\sum_{k\neq i,j}h_{ik}h_{jk}\right)^{2}\leqslant\E(N_{t}^{4}h_{12}^{5})\cong t^{9}/\lambda,

\displaystyle\E\var(T_{t,2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})

\displaystyle\cong\E\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N_{t}}h_{ij}^{2}\left(\sum_{k\neq i,j}h_{ik}h_{jk}\right)\leqslant\E(N_{t}^{3}h_{12}^{4})\cong t^{7}/\lambda,\text{ and }

\displaystyle\E\var(T_{t,3}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})

\displaystyle\cong\E(N_{t}^{3}h_{12}h_{23}h_{13})\cong t^{6}.

Now, conditionally on
N_{t}
,
\Delta_{t}
is a rank three U-statistic with
\E\var(\Delta_{t}\mid N_{t})\cong\E N_{t}^{5}(t/\lambda)^{6}\cong t^{11}/\lambda.
Finally, we have
\var(\E(T_{t}\mid N_{t}))\cong(t/\lambda)^{6}\var(N_{t}(N_{t}-1)(N_{t}-2))\cong t^{11}/\lambda.

Thus, when
t\ll\lambda^{1/5}
,
T_{t,3}
dominates, and when
\lambda^{1/5}\ll t\lesssim\lambda
,
\Delta_{t}
dominates. Proof of the Poisson and Gaussian limit follows a similar approach to the edge-count case, using the classical Stein–Chen method for Poisson approximation, CLT for U-Statistics (see
[
11
]
), and CLT for a polynomial of Poisson random variable.
