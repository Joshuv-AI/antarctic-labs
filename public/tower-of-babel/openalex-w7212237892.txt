Abstract
Pot-still cachaça (cachaça de alambique) is a traditional Brazilian spirit produced by thousands of small regional
producers. Despite advances in process standardization and quality control, its penetration into the international
market remains limited. Aiming to help boost the global presence of pot still cachaça, this paper offers insights to
inform potential adjustments in applied research priorities and the expansion of quality criteria. A mixed-methods
approach—predominantly qualitative and based on the interpretation of representative cases—was employed,
emphasizing the selection and preparation of barrel woods as key factors in sensory identity. It is proposed that
sensory diversity, while celebrated domestically, acts as a barrier to international recognition, which prioritizes criteria
such as origin traceability, botanical species certification, and sensory predictability. In practice, meeting these
standards requires limiting the variety of woods used and strengthening the traceability chain—from harvesting in
native forests to wood management at the cooperage and usage procedures in producers' cellars. To overcome these
challenges, the study advocates for the selective use of native woods. Amburana cearensis is highlighted as a prime
example; this native species—widely distributed across Brazil and traditionally used—offers distinctive, pleasant
sensory attributes, making it a reliable choice for efforts to enhance sensory consistency and strengthen cachaça's
national identity.
Keywords: Pot-still cachaça; Wood maturation; Standardization; Amburana cearensis; Sensory predictability;
National cooperage.

1. Introduction
Cachaça de alambique (pot still-distilled cachaça) holds strong historical and cultural significance in Brazil, with roots
dating back to the early period of colonization (Cavalcante, 2011; Fajardo, 2024; Maia, 2025). It is produced in agricultural
settings by thousands of producers distributed across virtually the entire national territory (Sebrae; Inmetro, 2013; Brazil,
2020).
In recent decades, notable advances have been made in the standardisation of the beverage and its production processes
(Santiago et al., 2017; Brazil, 2022; Castro, 2023), driven by a more vigorous performance within the sector, aimed at both
improving quality and expanding market recognition (Ibrac, 2021). Several brands have won awards in national and
international tasting competitions, whilst also consolidating their presence in the domestic market (Stacanelli et al., 2023;
Sebrae; Inmetro, 2013).
Notwithstanding these advances, the international recognition of cachaça de alambique remains limited, focusing mainly on
"white cachaça" (not stored in contact with wood), which is produced by a limited number of manufacturers on an industrial
scale (Neutzling et al., 2015; Paiva et al., 2017; Brazil, 2020). This scenario has given rise to frustration among small
producers, who often perceive their beverage as richer and more sensorially differentiated, attributing special value to storage
in wood — particularly in native species — as a strategy for sensory enhancement and the expression of the Brazilianness of
the beverage (Catão et al., 2011; Alcarde et al., 2014).
In this context, the wide diversity of tropical woods available in the country has been perceived as a positive
differential, capable of enriching the aromatic and flavour profiles associated with Brazilian cachaça de alambique (Faria et al.,
2003; Alcarde et al., 2014; Santiago et al., 2021). However, the persistent barriers to broader international market insertion
suggest the need for a more critical assessment of this assumption (Pinheiro, 2025). Sensory richness alone does not
encompass other attributes that are equally — or even more — relevant to quality characterisation, such as the sensory
reproducibility (Serafim et al., 2013), technological consistency (Schoeninger et al., 2014; Borges et al., 2020), and product
safety assurance (Maia; Marinho, 2026).
Studies on Brazilian woods have highlighted the lack of reliable analytical markers for the certification of botanical species
intended for the storage of alcoholic beverages (Santiago et al., 2017; Silva et al., 2023). Furthermore, limited interaction
between cachaça de alambique producers and the timber and cooperage sectors has hindered consistent advances in the
optimisation and standardisation of barrel wood heat treatments, an essential element in establishing a stable and predictable
sensory identity (Maia et al., 2025, 2026).

2. Methodology
This study adopted a qualitative approach with limited systematization (Risemberg et al., 2026a; Pereira et al., 2018)
within a narrative literature review (Ogassawara et al., 2025; Fernandes et al., 2023), featuring a predominance of qualitative
analysis and positioned between documentary research and the interpretive analysis of representative cases. Primary sources
included databases and specialised platforms (Google Scholar, SciELO, Web of Science, Scopus, and ScienceDirect), as well
as institutional documents from regulatory bodies and entities linked to the standardisation of alcoholic beverages. The
selection prioritised studies addressing maturation in wooden barrels, the relevance of sensory predictability, and the role of the
production sector itself — particularly in beverages that, like cachaça de alambique, are crafted by a large number of
geographically dispersed regional producers. The analysis focused on factors influencing the consistent insertion of cachaça de
alambique into broader market contexts.

3. Results and Discussion
3.1 Quality Criteria
The evaluation of cachaça quality — as in other distilled beverages — encompasses several layers of perception,
which vary according to the origin and perspective of the observer. For inspection agencies, quality is understood primarily as
compliance with the limits established for certain physico-chemical parameters and the adoption of Good Manufacturing
Practices (Brazil, 2022).
Beginner or occasional consumers, in turn, tend to assess quality based on immediate sensory perceptions, valuing
attributes such as pleasant aroma and the absence of aggressive notes (Lockshin; Corsi, 2012; Kladou et al., 2024; Galdino et
al., 2025). Experienced tasters, by contrast, incorporate additional criteria into their evaluations, including aromatic complexity
and consistency across different batches (Lawless; Heymann, 2010).
Quality benchmarks also vary according to market scale. At the regional level, the cultural and territorial identity of
the beverage may take precedence over formal criteria of standardisation and traceability (Rosas; Ortega, 2018; Rivas et al.,
2020; Temerdashev et al., 2023).
This dynamic is also reflected in different ways across domestic and international markets. In the domestic context,
the wide sensory variability of cachaça de alambique is accepted and even valued as inherent to its small-scale production and
to the diversity of geographical origins and production styles. As a result, a single product category simultaneously serves
distinct consumer niches, ranging from more traditional segments strongly associated with cultural consumption habits to more
demanding publics, guided by criteria of sensory refinement, standardisation and traceability.
In international markets, by contrast, the beverage tends to be positioned within established categories of spirits, in
which sensory standardisation plays a central role in product valuation, as opposed to more subjective criteria of preference. In
this context, there is also a growing appreciation of attributes such as guaranteed origin, geographical identity and
sustainability (Trubek, 2008; Lockshin; Corsi, 2012; Maia, 2023).
Following this logic, when production is shared among a large number of small producers, quality criteria extend
beyond the benchmarks of individual brands. As a result, quality is increasingly framed as a collective attribute, sustained by
trust in sectoral organisation, internal monitoring mechanisms, and shared control systems. Thus, external recognition is not
built on isolated exceptions, but on the effective demonstration of integration, technical maturity, and governance across the

3.2 Alcoholic Beverages in the International Market
Since the last century, several beverages produced on a small scale by numerous producers have succeeded in
consolidating themselves in the international market. This achievement largely resulted from initiatives by the productive
sector itself, through deliberate strategic choices aimed at establishing recognisable sensory profiles coherent with
geographical and cultural references, supported by mechanisms of collective coordination (Piggott et al., 1989; Ceccarelli et
al., 2010; Russell; Stewart, 2014).
Russian vodka is often cited as a paradigm of extreme standardisation in the universe of traditional alcoholic
beverages. Originally produced domestically, with wide variability in raw materials and production practices, it gradually
incorporated rigorously defined technical procedures, culminating in its normative redefinition: a mixture of rectified ethyl
alcohol and water, with an alcohol content of around 40% (v/v), strict limits for impurities and congeners, and systematic use
of purification techniques. The intentional acceptance of reduced sensory diversity enabled the consolidation of a sensorially
neutral, highly predictable, and widely accepted beverage in the international market (Herlihy, 2012; Wiśniewska et al., 2015).
During the 19th century, Scotch whisky also went through a path of standardization. Unlike the search for sensory
neutrality in Russian vodka, systematization efforts at the time aimed to establish the foundations for distinct aromatic profiles
while preserving their complexity and consistency (Bower, 2016; Bower; Higgins, 2024). As it is a distillate aged in wood, the
definition of specific procedures for preparing barrels and the stipulation of minimum maturation times — today consolidated
by laws such as the SWR (2009) — were decisive in establishing reproducible, stable and easily identifiable references on the
market (Daiches, 1969; MacLean, 2009).
Tequila followed a similar trajectory. Produced until the beginning of the twentieth century in a heterogeneous
manner, without clear sensory distinctions associated with different raw materials and production practices, it began from the
1960s onwards to undergo rigorous systematisation and to be organized into well-defined commercial categories. According to
the Consejo Regulador del Tequila (2012), this structured categorization created the conditions for the consolidation of
recognisable sensory references, which are fundamental to the construction of authenticity, traceability, and trust in the
international market.
The histories of vodka, whisky, and tequila illustrate that the international consolidation of these beverages required
deliberate efforts by the productive sector to reduce initial technological heterogeneity and establish predictable and welldefined sensory signatures. This comparative trajectory is particularly instructive when contrasted with Asian spirits, such as
shōchū and baijiu, which, despite their broad cultural relevance, remain strongly anchored in regional and intense sensory
profiles, thereby limiting their wider acceptance in the global market (Daiches, 1969; Humphrey; Schmitz, 2001; Laudan,
2013).
3.3 Cachaça
Brazilian cachaça has been produced since the sixteenth century, during the sugarcane cycle that marked the early
phase of colonisation. It rapidly became popular, spreading across diverse agricultural environments and leading to
heterogeneous production processes with no consistent sensory identity for a prolonged period. Efforts to standardise cachaça
and organise its production chain began in the 1980s (Maia, 2024), while official recognition of cachaça as a typical and

exclusive Brazilian sugarcane spirit, with defined identity parameters, was consolidated in the early 2000s (Brazil, 2001,
2005). Since then, standards for identity, quality, and safety have been progressively refined.
A major development occurred in 2022 with the formal establishment of “cachaça de alambique” as a distinct
category, defined as being distilled exclusively in batch distillation using copper pot stills, from the fermented must of fresh
sugarcane juice (Brazil, 2022). This recognition allows a clear distinction between small-scale production in agricultural
settings, linked to historical traditions, cultural values, and ongoing efforts of refinement, and large-scale industrial production,
typically carried out in continuous column stills and often without wood maturation.
Although both categories refer to spirits obtained from fermented sugarcane must, cachaça de alambique exhibits
distinctive characteristics throughout the production process — from raw material and yeast selection to fermentation,
distillation, and maturation in wooden casks — resulting in a markedly differentiated chemical and sensory profile (Maia,
2023). Accordingly, the analyses and proposals presented in this article focus exclusively on cachaça de alambique.
With the support of government and academic institutions over recent decades, cachaça de alambique production has
been extensively refined and standardised up to the distillation stage. Subsequently, maturation — although decisive in shaping
the sensory identity of the beverage — has hitherto been conducted in a predominantly empirical manner, with the use of
multiple species of native woods and barrel preparation procedures often lacking consolidated technical criteria.
In fact, sensory consistency requires clear and reproducible markers. This principle is readily understood in light of
the historical trajectory of widely established spirits, which have opted for the practically exclusive use of oak vessels. The
sensory contribution of this wood, readily recognised by consumers, has come to be associated with references of safety,
predictability, and technical control. It is important to emphasise that this exclusivity did not arise from the intrinsic properties
of oak being immediately evident, but rather from a historical process built upon empirical observations, systematic
experimentation, and the consolidation of a vast body of scientific knowledge, with emphasis on the standardisation of
practices such as drying, toasting, and wood management, ranked among the most determining factors for global recognition
(Chatonnet; Boidron, 1988; Mosedale, 1995; Mosedale; Puech, 1998; Olszak, 2010).
Complementing this historical perspective, recent research provides robust chemical support, describing the
mechanisms of formation of phenolic, volatile, and aromatic compounds during maturation in oak, as well as their role in
sensory evolution and in shaping the final profile of fermented and distilled beverages (Waterhouse et al., 2024;
Courregelongue; Pons, 2024; Chanivet et al., 2024).
This situation contrasts with the current scenario for cachaça de alambique, whose sensory diversity, whilst culturally
valued in regional contexts, has yet to be translated into clear references of identity, safety, and traceability that can be readily
assimilated in foreign markets. International recognition of regional beverages and foods stems not only from sensory richness,
but also from the capacity of the productive sector to convert this diversity into understandable, auditable, and reliable
normative references (Barham, 2003).
In this context, whilst several cachaça brands have already received awards in national and international tasting
competitions, such evaluations should not be interpreted as proof of clearly recognisable and traceable sensory identity (Gil et
al., 2020). For this purpose, the careful selection of botanical species, combined with technically oriented barrel preparation,
may be more decisive for market recognition than the use of a wide diversity of woods that have not yet been sufficiently
studied
3.4 Chemical Characterisation of Native Woods
At the end of the twentieth century, when the systematic study of the diversity of Brazilian woods used in the storage
of cachaça began, the absence of specific technical references and the available analytical limitations naturally led to the

adoption of parameters already established for the characterisation of oak (Chatonnet et al., 1994). Considering that most
phenolic compounds derive from lignin — a structural component common to all woody species — it was reasonable to
assume that similar molecular markers could be used to describe different woods, and that their relative contents and
proportions would faithfully reflect the genetic particularities and sensory expression of each botanical species.
The accumulation of experimental results has confirmed that the phenolic compounds classically used for the
characterisation of oak are indeed present — albeit in variable proportions — in the chromatographic profiles of the native
woods analysed (Mosedale, 1995; Souza et al., 2025; Moreira, 2025). However, pronounced oscillations in the relative
proportions of these compounds demonstrated that the direct application of these parameters for the purpose of certifying the
identity of tropical woods is unfeasible. This limitation can be attributed to the conjunction of multiple factors.
First, although all lignin is composed of guaiacyl, syringyl, and p-hydroxyphenyl units, the relative proportions of
these units, as well as the nature of the bonds between them, vary widely between species and even between varieties of the
same species (Sarkanen; Ludwig, 1971; Fengel; Wegener, 1989; Boerjan et al., 2003; Santos et al., 2012). In the case of oak,
this variability is attenuated by the systematic use of cultivated forests and by the restricted and well-characterised number of
species and varieties employed (Chatonnet et al., 1994). In contrast, in Brazil, native woods used in cooperage are
predominantly sourced from natural forests, resulting in greater structural heterogeneity of lignin (Faria et al., 2003; Jahan;
Mun, 2009).
Additional determining factors include: (a) the empirical felling of wood intended for cooperage, without
standardisation regarding the tree age and growth conditions; (b) the absence of specific standards for natural drying, a stage in
which enzymatic reactions play a major role in lignin transformation; and (c) marked variability in cooperage procedures,
where heat treatments, in addition to being optional, lack established technical standards (Markovic, 2025).
More recent studies have adopted more refined comparative approaches, expanding the analytical scope to include
aromatic compounds characteristic of tropical woods, in addition to the classic lignin-derived phenolics (Santiago et al., 2017;
Bortoletto et al., 2021; Souza et al., 2025; Moreira, 2025). Nevertheless, chemical markers enabling unequivocal certification
of the identity of Brazilian woods — such as amburana, jequitibá, amendoim bravo, and castanheira brasileira — have not yet
been established, despite their widespread use in the maturation of cachaça.
Addressing this gap involves significant challenges, especially given that natural forests are distributed across a wide
diversity of geographic regions, subject to marked variability in edaphoclimatic conditions. Consequently, the characterisation
of native woods extends beyond the scope of isolated analytical studies and requires the integration of forest engineering
parameters — such as tree age, trunk diameter, basic density, and moisture content (Panshin; Zeeuw, 1981; Masson et al.,
1995) — together with wood technology criteria, including drying procedures, treatments, and thermal regimes applied in
lumber and cooperage (Fengel; Wegener, 2011).
3.5 Amburana spp.
Among the Brazilian woods used in cooperage practices, Amburana spp. occupies a unique position, as it is the only
one that simultaneously fulfils the following criteria: (a) it has a wide geographical distribution in Brazil, reinforcing its
territorial representativeness; (b) it is widely used in the storage of cachaça de alambique, being traditionally known and
appreciated in the domestic market; (c) it exhibits a characteristic odour that allows ready sensory identification at the
botanical species level; (d) it displays exclusivity as a species endowed with a specific chemical compound — coumarin —
that can be used as a marker to verify the authenticity of the wood (Alves et al., 2024). Figure 1 below shows the natural

attributes (such as clove and cinnamon), dried fruits, and subtle fruity nuances. Together, these features result in a smooth,
complex, and distinctive flavour profile, with an intense colour — reddish amber in new barrels and golden in older vats —
and a strong, characteristic aromatic bouquet (Cardello; Faria, 2000; Santiago et al., 2014).
Other woods currently used in Brazilian cooperage, in addition to not yet having a specific parameter for certification
of botanical identity, exhibit further weaknesses. Bálsamo, for example, constitutes a heterogeneous group of species
belonging to different genera and botanical families, which essentially share the exudation of aromatic resins (Lorenzi, 2014).
Jequitibá also has a wide distribution across Brazilian territory; however, this designation encompasses a heterogeneous set of
species that exhibit a limited sensory contribution and occur predominantly in mature forests, which are often subject to legal
restrictions. These factors limit its regular and traceable availability for cooperage purposes. Amendoim bravo (Pterogyne

In light of these considerations, a more selective focus on Amburana spp.—in both scientific research and cooperage
practices—could mark a significant milestone in establishing a clearer, traceable, and validated sensory identity for Brazilian
pot-still cachaça, thereby elevating its status and recognition in premium markets.

4. Conclusion
Integrating diverse production and cultural experiences can be pivotal to the commercial evolution of heritage spirits
like cachaça. Historically, Brazilian artisanal pot-still cachaça gained strength by attracting a diverse consumer base, which
fostered a wide range of styles and production approaches. However, the sector's maturation now calls for institutional and
collective efforts toward standardization — grounded in traceability and scientific rigor — to reinforce a sensory identity
linked to historical ties and cultural values. Within this evolutionary dynamic, gaining international market recognition
transcends mere commercial goals; it fosters collective and cooperative action across various levels of the production sector
and shapes its interactions with society at large. It is from this perspective that prioritizing native botanical species with strong
identity-defining value — such as Amburana cearensis — becomes crucial. In contrast to the widespread use of woods lacking
robust scientific study, opting for an emblematic native species—carefully and sustainably managed in the forest, mastered in
cooperage, and scientifically validated in the cellar—can establish a diverse, safe, and sophisticated sensory portfolio. This
strategy can elevate Brazil’s historical heritage and biodiversity into irreplaceable competitive advantages in the global market.
