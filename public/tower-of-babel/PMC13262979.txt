Analysis of Two New Flavonoid Glycosides From Zanha golungensis Hiern With COX Inhibition Activity: Structural Elucidation, DFT Calculations, In Vitro and Molecular Docking Assessment
Authors: Jean Zeuikena, Romeo Toko Feunaing, Alfred Ngenge Tamfu, Abel Joel Yaya Gbaweng, Celine Henoumont, Sophie Laurent, El Hassane Anouar, Emmanuel Talla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Zanha golungensis is used as herbal medicine for a variety of ailments. In this study, flavonoids, including two new kaempferide polyglycosides, namely, kaempferide‐3‐O‐[α‐l‐rhamnopyranosyl‐(1→2)‐α‐l‐rhamnopyranosyl‐(1→6)]‐β‐d‐glucopiranoside‐7‐O‐α‐l‐rhamnopiranoside (1) and kaempferide‐3‐O‐[4‐O‐acetyl‐α‐l‐rhamnopyranosyl‐(1→2)‐α‐l‐rhamnopyranosyl‐(1→6)]‐β‐d‐glucopiranoside‐7‐O‐α‐l‐rhamnopiranoside (2) alongside known compounds (−)‐epicathecin (3), procyanidin A2 (4) and procyanidin B2 (5), were isolated and characterized using ESI‐TOF‐MS and NMR. The compounds exhibited good cyclooxygenase‐1 (COX‐1) and cyclooxygenase‐2 (COX‐2) inhibitions with high percentage inhibitions at 20 µg/mL. Compound was most active with percentage inhibitions of 74.3 ± 1.2 and 78.6 ± 1.5 on COX‐1 and COX‐2, respectively, compared to Cy‐3‐coumSamb‐5‐Glc (standard) with percentage inhibitions of 68.3 ± 1.1 and 70.0 ± 1.4 on COX‐1 and COX‐2, respectively. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were used in predicting the electronic and molecular properties of isolated compounds, notably optimized geometries and molecular electrostatic potentials, which all correlated with highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO)–lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energies. Molecular docking predictions indicated favourable and thermodynamically stable complexes between the compounds and COX‐2 receptor sites revealed by binding affinities and negative binding energies. The results show the potential anti‐inflammatory, antipyretic and anticancer potential drug design of the isolated compounds.

Introduction

Inflammation occurs as the biological reaction of the body to tissue damage resulting from a range of detrimental stimuli, including physical trauma, microbial invasions and irritants and usually accompanied by biochemical mediators that can lead to diseases [1, 2]. This reaction involves secretion of pro‐inflammatory signalling molecules such as chemokines and cytokines to initiate tissue repair and eliminate damaged cells [3]. This also involves the release of inflammatory mediators, including prostaglandins (PGs), leukotrienes (LTs) and thromboxanes (TXs), and their production is accompanied by pain, heat, redness and swelling [4]. Factors like infections, poor diet, physical inactivity, stress, and industrial and environmental toxicants can increase chances of systemic chronic inflammation and cause diseases that results into disability and mortality [5]. Over the past three decades, the socioeconomic burden of inflammation‐mediated diseases such as ischaemic heart disease, stroke, cancer, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease, obesity, arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, psychiatric disorders, autoimmune and neurodegenerative conditions has increased significantly [6, 7]. Amongst the mediators of inflammations and related pathologies, cyclooxygenase (COX) plays a significant role.

COXs interfere in lipid signalling and are mediators of fever, pain, inflammation, cancer and cardiovascular diseases [8, 9]. The two isoforms of COXs, namely, cyclooxygenase‐1 (COX‐1) and cyclooxygenase‐2 (COX‐2), are distributed and controlled differently [10]. The two isozymes or homodimers of COXs (COX‐1 and COX‐2) are composed of COX and peroxidase active sites, catalyse prostanoid biosynthesis but have 576 and 581 amino acids, respectively [11, 12]. The primary source of prostanoids that support housekeeping processes is COX‐1, which is constitutively expressed in the majority of cells [13]. Conversely, COX‐2 is thought to be the most significant source of prostanoid production in inflammation and proliferative disorders like cancer as it is triggered by hormones, growth factors and inflammatory stimuli [14]. Arachidonic acid (AA) is converted by COX (COX‐1 and COX‐2) into hydroperoxy‐endoperoxide PG G2 and then by peroxidase activity to hydroxy‐endoperoxide PG H2, which is then further metabolized to PG, TXs and prostacyclins [2]. COXs are therefore responsible for the oxidative conversion of AA into various TXs and PGs, which interfere in many cellular and pathological processes [15]. COXs are involved in the formation of PGs which engender inflammation and pain; their inhibitors exhibit anti‐inflammatory and analgesic activities [16]. As they contribute to the synthesis of PGs COX enzymes are responsible for cell proliferation and inflammation, and their inhibitors can prevent the occurrence of colorectal cancer [17]. COXs are responsible for high production of PGs, which is a common feature in breast cancer, and the reduction of prostanoids reduces related inflammations [18]. Inflammations cause tissue destruction, fibrosis, angiogenesis and cancer progression at the site of inflammation [19]. COX inhibitors provide neuroprotection, alleviate neuronal cell death, tumours and neuroinflammation and slow cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disorders, including situations of brain injury, Alzheimer's disease, acute and chronic epilepsy [20, 21, 22, 23]. Despite the anti‐inflammatory, analgesic and antipyretic properties of existing COX inhibitors, as well as their capacity in relieving symptoms of numerous illnesses, they show side effects such as impairment of gastric mucosa and cardiovascular issues [17]. Most non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) from synthetic origin are used for the treatment of inflammation‐mediated disorders but usually present serious secondary effects like vomiting, nausea, hyperacidity and gastrointestinal ulcers [24]. Natural products being structurally diverse, natural products are attracting great attention of medicinal chemists as safer and alternative COX inhibitors which can serve in the development of anti‐inflammatory and cancer chemopreventive agents [25].

The Sapindaceae family comprises approximately 2000 identified species, distributed among about 150 genera [26, 27]. The genus Zanha is the rarest, containing only three species, including Zanha golungensis Hiern. Z. golungensis Hiern is a species found in tropical and temperate zones. Native to Africa, it is most prevalent in Central, West and Southern Africa. It is widely used in traditional medicine for treating wounds, abscesses, skin infections, fungal infections and muscle pain. It is also used as an antivenom for snakebites, as an antidote, as a fish poison, and for respiratory problems [28, 29]. Few biological studies have been conducted on the plant. Nevertheless, the studies carried out have shown the following activities: antibacterial, antioxidant [29, 30] and molluscicidal activities [31]. At the limit of our research, the phytochemical work carried out on the plants focused mainly on the root bark and led to the isolation of triterpenoids (zanhic acid, zanhic‐γ‐lactone acid [32], prosapogenins (zanhine prosapogenin and medicagine), saponins and oleanane‐type triterpenes) [33].

The ongoing search for alternatives to NSAIDs of therapeutic relevance from medicinal plants is a suitable remedy to inflammatory conditions and related diseases. COX, which is a rate‐limiting enzyme that converts AA into pro‐inflammatory PGs, is a possible target. This study therefore investigated the COX‐1 and COX‐2 inhibition of compounds isolated from Z. golungensis Hiern, which reflects their potential anti‐inflammatory, anticancer, antipyretic and analgesic properties. Computational studies involving molecular docking were sued to supplement in vitro assays and density functional theory (DFT) calculations to predict electronic, molecular and additional structural features of the compounds.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material

The plant Z. golungensis Hiern was collected from Banyo in the Adamawa Region of Cameroon. It was identified by the botanist Mr. Mounmemi Kpoumie Hubert, and a voucher specimen was prepared and deposited at the National Herbarium of Cameroon under the number 675175/HNC. The barks of the trunk were harvested and dried for the studies.

Extraction and Isolation

The tree barks were chopped into pieces, dried in shade and then ground to obtain 3 kg of dry plant powder. The 3 kg were subjected to extraction by maceration at room temperature using 10 L of hydroethanol solvent (80/20). After 72 h, the supernatant was decanted and filtered, and the solvent was removed by evaporation on a Rotavapor to afford the crude extract. This process was repeated three times, and the extracts were combined, giving 405.23 g. An amount of 400 g of the crude extract was dissolved in 1 L of distilled water and divided into two equal parts of 500 mL each, which were separately partitioned with n‐butanol and ethyl acetate. This process was exhaustively repeated and yielded 25.85 g of ethyl acetate fraction and 44.12 g of n‐butanol fraction.

An amount of 20 g of the n‐butanol fraction was used in preparing a slurry and subjected to column chromatography on silica gel and eluted with AcOEt/MeOH/H2O in the ratios (93/5/2; 85/10/5; 80/15/5) while collecting fractions of equal volumes of 100 mL. A total of 30 fractions were collected and grouped based on TLC. Fractions 16–29 were rechromatographed on silica gel column using the ternary systems AcOEt/MeOH/H2O (85/10/5) and AcOEt/MeOH/H2O (70/20/10) to afford 41 subfractions. The Subfractions 37–39 precipitated and were filtered and washed to afford Compound 1 (21 mg) (HUA 12). The Subfractions 1–36 were regrouped and successively rechromatographed on silica gel column using the isocratic ternary system AcOEt/MeOH/H2O (85/10/5) to afford Compound 2 (11 mg) (HUA 14).

An amount of 20 g of the ethyl acetate extract was used in preparing a slurry which was purified by column chromatography on a silica gel column using a successive gradient system of hexane: AcOEt (0%–100%) and AcOEt: MeOH (0%–100%). 185 fractions of 50 mL each were collected and grouped into 4 subfractions (A, B, C and D) on the basis of their TLC profiles. The TLC profiles in DCM/MeOH (85/15) indicated suitable separation for Subfractions B (98–106), C (107–114) and D (119–138). Purification by column chromatography of Subfractions B, C and D using isocratic system DCM/MeOH (85/15) afforded Compound 3 (HUA3, 7 mg), Compound 4 (HUA4, 8 mg) and Compound 5 (HUA6, 12 mg). Anti‐Inflammatory (COX Inhibition) Activity

The anti‐inflammatory potential of the compounds was evaluated using in vitro spectrophotometric measurement of COXs (COX‐1 and COX‐2) inhibition at a wavelength of 590 nm as described previously with some modifications [37, 38]. COX inhibitor screening assay kit (no. 701230) from Cayman Chemical Company (Ovine, Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, MI) with 250 µL capacity 96‐well plates were used as described elsewhere [39]. To 150 µL of the reaction mixture consisting of TRIS [tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane]‐HCl buffer (pH 8.0) and 20 µL of 20 µg/mL of the test compounds or reference compound which was Cy‐3‐coumSamb‐5‐Glc (cyanidin 3‐O‐coumaroyl‐sambubioside‐5‐O‐glucoside), were successively added 10 µL of hematin (0.1026 mmol) and 10 µL of COX (COX‐1 and COX‐2) at 1 mg/mL. After mixing and incubation for 10 min at 37°C, 20 µL of N,N,N′,N′‐tetramethyl‐p‐phenylenediamine (TMPD) were added at 24.35 mM, followed by 20 µL of AA (35 mmol) so as to initiate the reaction. The presence of oxidized TMPD was monitored at 590 nm. Changes in the absorbance were recorded for 3 min at 590 nm using a Spectro UV‐Vis Double PC 8 Auto Cell Model UVD‐3200 (LABOMED INC, LA, USA). The control experiment was without test sample. Each experiment was carried out in triplicate. The percentage of inhibition of COX activity was calculated by the following formula: %Cyclooxygenaseinhibition=ΔAcontrol−ΔAsampleΔAcontrol×100 where ΔA control is enzymatic activity in the absence of test sample and ΔA sample denotes enzymatic activity in the presence of compound or reference compound.

Theoretical Calculations

Geometry optimization and frequency calculations of the stable conformers of the isolated flavonoid derivatives (1–5) were carried out at the B3LYP/6‐31G(d) as implemented in the Gaussian 16W software [40]. All optimized geometries are found to be true minima with zero imaginary frequencies; that is, all vibrational frequencies are real. The electronic and molecular properties of the isolated flavonoid derivatives (1–5), like the ionization potential (IP), electronic affinity (EA), gap energy (ΔE) between the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), are determined through the application of Koopman's theorem IP=−EHOMO, EA=−ELUMO and Egap=ELUMO−EHOMO [41]. B3LYP is found to be a suitable method to reproduce the experimental data [42, 43]. More details related to scaled vibration modes, predicted excited states, calculated global properties and solvent models may be found in our previous study [44].

Molecular Docking Study

The binding affinities of the isolated flavonoid derivatives (1–5) into the binding site of COX‐2 were estimated using the AutoDock4 package [45]. X‐ray coordinates of COX‐2 and its original ligand indomethacin were downloaded from the RCSB data bank (PDB code 4COX) [46]. Water molecules were removed; polar hydrogen atoms and Kollman charge were added to the receptor structure using the automated tool in AutoDock Tools 4.2. Four active sites were identified on the basis of co‐crystallized receptor‐ligand complex structure. The re‐docking of the original ligand (i.e., indomethacin) into the receptor active sites led to RMSD value less than 1 Å. The 3D geometries of the isolated flavonoid derivatives (1–5) were minimized at Merck molecular force field 94 (MMFF94) level [47] and were saved as pdb files. Nonpolar hydrogens were merged, and rotatable bonds were defined for each docked ligand. Docking studies were performed by Lamarckian genetic algorithm, with 500 as the total number of runs for each binding site. In each respective run, a population of 150 individuals with 27,000 generations and 250,000 energy evaluations was employed. Operator weights for crossover, mutation and elitism were set to 0.8, 0.02 and 1, respectively. The binding site was defined using a grid of 40 × 40 × 40 points each with a grid spacing of 0.375 Å. The docking calculation has been carried out using an Intel Core i5‐3770 CPU @ 3.40 GHz workstation.

Statistical Analysis

Experiments were done in triplicate, and results were expressed as mean value ± standard deviation. The statistical differences between the test samples were analysed through one‐way ANOVA. The differences with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results and Discussion

Structural Elucidation of Compounds 1 and 2

Compound 2 was obtained in form of a reddish oil. On the high resolution electrospray mass spectrum with time of flight detector in positive mode (HR‐ESI‐TOF‐MS) of Compound 2, there was a pseudo‐molecular ion peak [M + Na]+ at m/z 965.2861 corresponding to the molecular formula C42H54O24Na. There were other important diagnostic peaks, including those at m/z 796 corresponding to [M + H‐Rha]+ representing the loss of a rhamnose unit, at m/z 635 corresponding to [M + H‐Rha‐ORha]+ representing the loss of two rhamnose units. The peak at m/z 592 corresponds to [M + H‐ORha‐RhaOAc]+ representing the loss of an acetylated rhamnose and non‐acetylated rhamnose units while the peak m/z 299 corresponds to [M + H‐Rha‐ORha‐RhaOAc‐Glc]+ and represents the loss of three rhamnose units alongside one glucose unit. The latter signal at m/z 299 for the loss of all four sugar moieties thus represents the aglycone which corresponds to kaempferide‐type flavonoid. Mass peaks for the rhamnose units were also observed at m/z 149, corresponding to [Rha + H]+. The various fragments put together suggest that Compound 2 is a kaempferide‐type tetra glycosylated flavonoid [48, 49, 50]. Anti‐Inflammatory Potential of Isolated Compounds

COX is an enzyme that mediates the synthesis of PGs from AA. Inhibitors of COXs are exploited for therapeutic applications in anti‐inflammatory, antipyretic and analgesic treatments. The isolated compounds were evaluated for their potential inhibition of COX‐1 and COX‐2, and the percentage inhibitions of COX‐1 and COX‐2 at 20 µg/mL evaluated and reported in Table 2. It is observed that all compounds inhibited COX‐2 to a greater extent than they did for COX‐1. The most active compound was Compound 2, with percentage inhibitions of 74.3 ± 1.2 and 78.6 ± 1.5 on COX‐1 and COX‐2, respectively, compared to the standard compound Cy‐3‐coumSamb‐5‐Glc which had percentage inhibitions of 68.3 ± 1.1 and 70.0 ± 1.4 on COX‐1 and COX‐2, respectively. Compounds 1, 2 and 4 were more active than the standard. Though the standard was more active than Compounds 3 and 5, their activities remained close and almost in the same range. The structural features of the compounds and that of the standard are closely related, and their COX‐1 and COX‐2 inhibitory activities reflect these structural resemblances.

COX‐1 is cytoprotective and responsible for PG production, whereas COX‐2 is induced by mitogens, cytokines and endotoxins and accounts for high PG amounts during inflammation, making them targets of anti‐inflammatory and anti‐cancer drug development [64]. The flavonoids described in this study exhibited good inhibitory potential of both COX‐1 and COX‐2. The compounds either showed activity comparable to that of the standard compound used or greater than the standard. Our results corroborate with previous findings, which described flavonoids showing activity greater than or comparable to the standards used in COX‐1 and COX‐2 inhibition assays [65]. Flavonoids have been described as being capable of modulating inflammatory processes by inhibiting COX‐1, COX‐2 enzymes, as well as the production of cytokines and chemokine [66]. TX and PGs synthesis from AA requires COX or PG‐endoperoxide synthase (PTGS) and the inhibitors of the latter clinically used in treating pains, inflammations and cancers [67]. The results of this study suggest that the compounds are COX inhibitors that can interfere in the catalytic conversion of AA into PGs and TXs and thereby prevent the onset of inflammatory diseases and cancers. The compounds could also modulate the synthesis of pro‐inflammatory biomolecules.

COXs are heme‐containing, membrane‐bound and bifunctional enzymes, and their inhibitors bind to their active sites, preventing them from binding AA [68]. Flavonoids are reducing cosubstrates for COXs with high affinity, which can bind to peroxidase active sites and donate electrons from hydroxyl groups of their B‐ring to hematin [13, 69]. Flavonoids with a catechol system are able to inhibit the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and inflammatory PG E2 simultaneously [66]. This explains the Cox inhibitory effects of Compounds 3, 4 and 5, which possess the catechol systems. Compounds 1 and 2 were glycosylated and possessed methoxy groups at C‐4′ which has been described as a favourable structural feature for COX‐2 mRNA inhibition and used in explaining the high anti‐inflammatory activity of flavonoids [70]. The presence of double bond between C2–C3 (C‐ring), hydroxyl groups at C5, C7, C3′ and C4′ positions of flavonoids is a general structural feature for exerting anti‐inflammatory and antidiabetic activity [71]. All the compounds possessed hydroxyl groups at C5 and C7, Compounds 3, 4 and 5 had hydroxyl groups at position C4′, whereas Compounds 1 and 2 contained methoxy groups at C4′. Though all compounds were sufficiently active, the enhanced COX inhibition and anti‐inflammatory potential could be attributed to the presence of C2–C3 double bond, methoxy substituents and sugar moieties. Flavonols have C2–C3 bond unsaturation and C4 carbonyl, which are responsible for suppressing COX‐2 activities [72]. Oxygenation of C4 could also account for higher activity of Compounds 1 and 2. COX‐2 is particularly central in inflammations and is associated with tumour growth, carcinogenesis, angiogenesis, immune evasion and cancer progression in various human cancers [73, 74]. This makes COX‐2 more attractive in drug development than COX‐1. The compounds exhibited greater inhibition on COX‐2 than COX‐1, indicating that they are selective COX‐2 inhibitors. COX‐2 inhibitors are significantly efficient compounds with anti‐inflammatory, anticancer, neuroprotective and cardioprotective properties [75, 76]. The lack of more information of the involvement of COX‐1 biology in human diseases makes it scarcely studied [77]. Because of its relevance, COX‐2 was used for molecular docking with the compounds.

DFT Results

DFT method is used in predicting the electronic and molecular properties of synthetic organic and natural compounds [78]. Quantum chemical analysis of electron affinity (EA), IP, LUMO–HOMO energy gap (E gap), electronegativity (χ), chemical hardness (η), chemical softness (S), electrophilicity index (ω), dipole moment (DM) and energy were derived and presented in Table 3. Electrostatic potential (ESP) is essential in drug design in order to optimize the electrostatic complementarity of the molecular surface with receptors. Herein, the molecular and electronic properties of the isolated phenolic derivatives (1–5) were calculated in order to get information about their estimated reactivity. The optimized geometries of Compounds 1–5 are given in Figure 3. The frontier molecule orbitals (FMOs) concern the two main orbitals involved in chemical reactions, which are the LUMO and the HOMO. This energy gap between them can be used to identify the molecules’ chemical reactivity, kinetic stability, polarizability, chemical hardness and softness, aromaticity and electronegativity [79]. The chemical stability of a molecule, indicated by the energy gap between HOMO and LUMO, is an important factor in determining the transition states and electrical transport capabilities of the compound. The favourable electrophilic and nucleophilic sites were identified by analysing their corresponding ESP (Figure 4) and FMO orbitals (Figure 5). The calculated molecular and electronic properties of the isolated flavonoid derivatives (1–4) are summarized in Table 3. The possibility of a compound interacting with other species and receptors is determined by these orbitals. The gap of the frontier molecular orbitals gives information about the kinetic stability and chemical reactivity of the compound. The HOMO and IP energies (IP = −E HOMO) indicate the capability of the isolated compounds to donate electrons. Precisely, the lower the IP values (the higher E HOMO), the stronger the ability of the isolated compound to donate electrons. The isolated phenolic compounds may be subdivided into two subclasses: flavonols (1–2) and flavan‐3‐ols (3–5). The effect of IP in each subclass is negligible. Indeed, the acylation of sugar moiety in Compound 2, results in a variation of IP less than 0.01 eV. Similarly, IP values of 3 and 4 flavan‐3‐ols vary by less than 0.03 eV (Table 3). In the other hand, the electron affinity (EA) indicates the capability of the compound to accept or donate electrons. The higher the EA (lower LUMO energy), the more the compound can accept electrons. EA values indicate that the flavonols (1–2) with higher EA may have the ability to accept electrons compared to flavan‐3‐ols (3–5) with EA values of less than 0.3 eV (Table 4). The energy gap (E gap), HOMO–LUMO energy difference, is one of the most important variables in rationalizing the reactivity and the essential properties of molecular systems. The lower the Egap, the higher the reactivity of the compound. However, a larger gap may imply a higher kinetic stability [78]. The flavonols (1–2) with gap energy (E gap) of 3.89 eV are more reactive than flavan‐3‐ols (3–5) with average gap energy of 5.73 eV (Compound 3), 5.43 eV (Compound 4), and 5.34 eV (Compound 5). The calculated electronic energies of 1–5 indicate that they are stable with electronic energies of −3246.30, −3398.96, −1031.35, −2060.30 and −2061.49 Hartree, respectively. Among 1–5, Compound 2 is found to be the most stable, with relative energies of 152.66, 2367.61, 1338.66 and 1337.47 Hartree to 1 and 3–5, respectively. The inflammatory tests show that the flavonols (1–2) have higher inhibitory potential towards COX‐1 and COX‐2 targets compared to the flavan‐3‐ols (3–5). Indeed, the higher inflammatory activity of the flavonols (1–2) compared to the flavan‐3‐ols (3–5) may be attributed in part to the higher reactivity of flavonols (1–2) compared to the flavan‐3‐ols (3–5) as revealed by DFT. The global hardness and softness may also indicate the effectiveness and the reactivity of the isolated phenolic compounds. The compounds that possess low hardness, or high softness, may have high reactivity and thus exhibit high inflammatory activity (COX inhibition). In accordance with calculated energy gap variations (Table 3), the flavonols (1–2) with a hardness of 3.97 eV are more reactive than flavan‐3‐ols (3–5) with an average hardness of 0.80 eV. The flavonols (1–2) with the lowest global hardness and highest softness compared to the flavan‐3‐ols (3–5) may also explain the higher inflammatory activity of the former compared to the latter subclass. Finally, the flavonols (1–2) with higher dipolar moments are more soluble in water and more polar compared to flavan‐3‐ols (3–5). The information about geometrical and electronic features of an excited molecule can be derived from its dipole moment in the short‐lived state [80]. The high dipole moment of Compounds 1 and 2 can be attributed to the presence of the carbonyl groups, which are strong electron‐withdrawing, especially when they are excited. The molecular and electronic properties of the compounds all correlated with the HOMO and LUMO energies and molecular properties. The molecular and electronic properties provide insights about molecular structures, electronic transitions and potential drug design of compounds [81].

Molecular Docking Results

The results of the anti‐inflammatory potential of the isolated phenolic derivatives (1–5) are given in Table 2. The flavonol glycosides (Compounds 1 and 2) exhibited greater activity compared to the flavan‐3‐ols (Compounds 3, 4 and 5). Certainly, the observed activity depends mainly on the structural features of the isolated flavonoid derivatives (1–5) and the presence or absence of some functional groups such as hydroxyl groups, double bonds and sugar moieties. To explain the observed anti‐inflammatory activities, molecular docking study is performed to determine thee binding modes of the isolated flavonoid derivatives (1–5) into the binding site COX‐2. Table 4 summarizes the calculated free binding energies, the number of hydrogen bonds, and the number of interactions in the complexes formed between the isolated flavonoid derivatives (1–5) and the active residues of COX‐2.

It is revealed that the compounds with the most negative binding energies exhibited highest percentage of COX inhibition and thus highest anti‐inflammatory potential. Molecular docking outputs reveal that the isolated flavonoid derivatives (1–5) may fit well into the binding site of COX‐2. They form stable complexes with their amino acids; negative bending energies range from −5.84 to −8.03 kcal/mol. The negative binding energy indicates that the inhibition process of the isolated flavonoid derivatives (1–5) towards COX‐2 may be considered spontaneous and thermodynamically favourable (Table 4). Compound 3 shows fewest interactions in terms of the number of hydrogen bonding, other types of interactions and number of residues that are involved in intermolecular interaction, which may explain its weakest binding energy and thus its higher instability in the binding site of COX‐2. The other flavonoids show closer binding affinities with free binding energy differences of less than 1.00 kcal/mol, five to seven hydrogen bonds and five to six amino acids that participate in the stability of the formed complexes. Another important observation is that the acylation of one hydroxyl group of sugar moiety in Compound 1 to form Compound 2 led to an increase in the inflammatory activity of Compound 2, and this was reflected by the binding energies of Compounds 1 and 2. Compounds 4 and 5 can be considered as structural derivatives, precisely dimers of Compound 3. Indeed, the reaction between two units of Compound 3 linking C4 of one to C8 of another unit yields Compound 5, whereas the reaction of two units of Compound 3 linking C2 and C4 of one to hydroxyl (OH) at C7 and C8, respectively, of another unit yields Compound 4. Thus, the increase in the number of OH groups may explain the high anti‐inflammatory of Compounds 4 and 5 compared to Compound 3. This was substantiated by the observed binding energies of Compounds 2, 4 and 5. One may also expect that Compound 5 may have higher activity than Compound 4 due to the presence of more hydroxyl groups in the latter, which may lead to the formation of more HBs with the amino acids of COX‐2.

Studies involving binding pockets and active sites of COXs are important as they show binding modes of various inhibitors and ease structural comparison amongst them in relation to the structural features [8]. The high COX‐2 inhibition and thus anti‐inflammatory effect of the isolated flavonoids necessitate molecular docking to predict the ligand–protein interactions and give structure‐activity insights. The flavonoids showed good binding interactions with COX‐2 revealed by the negative binding energies and number of hydrogen bonds. All flavonoids interacted with the Glu residue in COX receptor, and it was reported previously that Glu interacts with flavonoid ligands through hydrogen bonding and stabilizes them within the active site of COX [82]. Flavonoid derivatives bind with Lys of COX primarily by hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions usually formed with the oxygen atom in the hydroxyl or carbonyl groups [83]. The high COX inhibition of Compounds 1, 2, 4 and 5 may be enhanced thanks to their interactions with Lys. The most active of the flavonoids was Compound 1 and was the only compound to bind with the His residue of the COX receptor. His as well as Val and Ser play an important role at the entrance and exit route of COX active site. Histidine and tryptophan can change the electrostatic profile of the side pocket of COX‐2 and both residues are particularly crucial for the entrance and exit of some NSAIDs from the COX active site [84]. Interaction of Compound 1 with histidine and tryptophan could therefore be responsible for additional inhibition compared to the other compounds. The COX inhibitory potential of the flavonoid compounds from Z. golungensis as revealed by in vitro and molecular docking indicates their potential as selective COX‐2 inhibitors, which can find applications in treating inflammations, pains, cancers, diabetes, schizophrenia, ischaemic brain injury and neuroinflammatory diseases such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases.

Conclusion

COX exists as two isozymes (COX‐1 and COX‐2), which are relevant catalysts for the conversion of AA into PGs. Despite their physiological roles, COXs are at the onset of various illnesses, such as inflammations, cancers, diabetes, neurodegeneration and cardiovascular diseases. Pharmacological inhibitors of COX, such as NSAIDs, relief the symptoms of pains and inflammation but are often associated with side effects. Natural COX inhibitors are considered safer and attract research interests. In this study, flavonoid compounds from the medicinal plant Z. golungensis were evaluated for their COX‐1 and COX‐2 inhibitions. The compounds exhibited good COX inhibitions with tendency of COX‐2 selectivity. Structure‐activity relationship suggests that carbonyl function, double bonds and hydroxyl groups are favourable factors for COX inhibition. Molecular and electronic features of the compounds were elucidated using DFT calculations. Molecular docking was used to substantiate in vitro assays and revealed favourable binding interactions of the isolated flavonoid compounds with the receptor sites of COX‐2. Negative binding energies and number of H‐bonds formed indicated that the compounds can bind effectively to COX‐2, forming stable complexes and resulting in physiological effects. The flavonoid compounds could be used in the development of natural anti‐inflammatory drugs.
