The association between depressive symptoms and sleep quality in patients with multiple sclerosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Afshin Moradi, Asal Ebrahimian, Mahnaz Talebi, Amirreza Naseri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, immune-mediated disease often associated with neuropsychiatric complications, including depressive symptoms and sleep disturbances. Evidence regarding their association remains inconsistent; therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to investigate the correlation between depressive symptoms and sleep quality in patients with MS.MethodsA systematic search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus up to February 2, 2025. Cross-sectional and case-control studies which assessed sleep quality using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and depressive symptoms using validated instruments were included. Two independent reviewers conducted the study selection, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment. Pooled correlation coefficients (r) were calculated using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software (CMA).ResultsTwenty-one studies comprising 3,144 patients with MS were included. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the scales including Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS). Meta-analysis demonstrated a moderate positive correlation between PSQI and different scales of depressive symptoms (r = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.37–0.55, p < 0.01); however, heterogeneity was considerable (I² = 83.3%). The certainty of evidence was low considering the observational nature of the included studies and substantial inconsistency. Subgroup analysis of studies based on the scales of depressive symptoms showed lower heterogeneity.ConclusionThis review suggests a possible association between poor sleep quality and depressive symptoms in patients with MS; however, the findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the low certainty of evidence. Further well-designed prospective studies are needed to clarify the strength and direction of this relationship.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-05075-1.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a common immune-mediated disease which leads to inflammation of the white and gray matters of the central nervous system (CNS) affecting young adults with an increasing incidence worldwide [1, 2]. MS represents an escalating health challenge, particularly across developed nations. The global prevalence of MS is 23.9 cases per 100,000 and in 2021, it has been estimated that 1.8 million people lived with MS and more than 62,000 new cases diagnosed during 2021 alone [3, 4]. Factors such as genes, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection, and some well-defined environmental factors like ultraviolet B-light (UVB) or vitamin D deficiency can elevate the susceptibility to this multifactorial disease [5–7]. Some most common complications of MS include ataxia, visual problems, poor sleep quality, chronic pain, fatigue, depressive symptoms, and muscle spasticity [8–11].

As mentioned, depressive symptoms is one of the most common complications of MS which is characterized by persistent depressed mood, loss of interest in previous activities and hobbies, thoughts of death and physical and cognitive symptoms [12]. The higher prevalence of depressive symptoms among MS patients in comparison to the general population, can influence patients’ quality of life [13, 14]. In more severe cases, these symptoms may even be accompanied by suicidal ideation, highlighting the importance of early recognition and appropriate psychiatric support [15]. Insomnia and hypersomnia are also symptoms of depressive disorders [16].

Sleep quality depends mostly on satisfaction with all four aspects of sleep including: sleep efficacy, latency, duration and wake after sleep onset [17]. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) is one of the most widely used measures of sleep quality in clinical settings. This questionnaire is consisted of 19 questions by which quality of sleep is evaluated [18].

The burden of depressive symptoms and poor sleep quality in patients with MS was investigated in earlier research [19, 20]. A previous meta-analysis by Boeschoten et al., revealed an increased prevalence of depressive symptoms among MS patients [21]. Poor sleep quality was also found to be more common among MS patients compared to control groups [22]; however, this study did not examine the relationship between depressive symptoms and sleep quality. A review by Yasugaki et al. highlighted that sleep disturbances are consistently observed in patients and animal models of depressive disorders, with sleep perturbations and antidepressants exerting diverse and complex effects on sleep patterns [23]. The association between sleep and depressive symptoms in general population is well documented [24]. Also mediating effects of sleep quality on the relationship among family health [25], chronic pain [26] and depressive symptoms, highlights the need for dissecting this association. Studies have investigated the relationship between depressive symptoms and sleep quality in MS patients, yielding inconsistent findings; therefore, we aimed to systematically review this association, based on the clinical evidence.

Methods

This systematic review was performed based on the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology [27], and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement [28].

Eligibility criteria

Clinical studies of any observational design (cross-sectional, cohort, or case-control) investigating the association between sleep quality, based on PSQI and depressive symptoms based on the validated scales (Beck Depression Inventory [BDI], Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale [HADS-D], Hamilton Depression Rating Scale [HDRS], Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale [CES-D], Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale-Revised [CESD-R], Patient Health Questionnaire-9 [PHQ-9], Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale [SDS]), in patients with MS in any stage of disease and any phenotype, were included. Animal studies, studies in pediatric populations (defined as age < 18 years), conference abstracts, reviews, case reports, letters, and studies in any language except English and Persian were not included.

Search strategy

Databases were searched from inception to February 2, 2025, across the Medline via PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus. Details of search strategies are presented in Supplementary Material 1. After selecting the final articles, the reference list, citations, and studies investigated in related review articles were also checked for possible inclusions.

Study selection and data extraction

References were managed using EndNote 20 (Clarivate Analytics), and screening was performed using the Rayyan review tool (https://www.rayyan.ai) [29]. The results of the electronic search were imported into Rayyan and duplicated studies were removed. Two independent authors (A.M. and A.E.) screened the studies in two title/abstract and full-text stages. Data extraction was conducted by two authors (A.M. and A.E.) and double-checked by another author (A.N.). Any disagreements were resolved through another author’s opinion (M.T.). The data was extracted using an electronic table in Microsoft Word including the name of the first author and study publication year, setting of the study, study design, number of participants with MS, mean age, female ratio, disease phenotype, disease duration, disease severity, PSQI global score, percentage of poor sleepers (PSQI > 5), depressive symptom indexes and scores, and correlation reported between PSQI and depressive symptom.

Risk of bias assessment

The Risk of Bias (RoB) in the included studies was assessed according to their study design by using JBI critical appraisal tools [30]. The JBI checklists, assess various methodological aspects of each study including the appropriate defining of eligibility criteria, identifying, and dealing with confounding factors, valid and reliable measurement of exposures and outcomes, and appropriate statistical analysis. RoB assessments were conducted by two authors (A.M. and A.E.), and any disagreement between the researchers was resolved through discussion or by consulting a third author (A.N.). The certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach adapted for observational association studies. Evidence was evaluated across the domains of RoB, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

Quantitative synthesis

The primary effect measure was the correlation coefficient (r) between PSQI and depressive symptoms’ scores. The quantitative synthesis was conducted using the third version of Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (CMA3) with 95% confidence intervals and 0.05 level of significance for p-value. The software automatically applied Fisher’s z transformation (z = 0.5 × ln[(1 + r)/(1 − r)]) to each correlation coefficient before pooling, calculated the standard error as SE(z) = 1/√(n − 3), performed pooling in the z metric, and back-transformed the pooled estimate to a correlation coefficient using the inverse formula (r = (e^(2z) − 1)/(e^(2z) + 1. A random-effect model was used for all pooled analyses, chosen a priori because clinical and methodological heterogeneity was expected across studies (variations in MS phenotypes, disease duration, disability levels, and depressive symptom scales). The DerSimonian-Laird estimator was applied and heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. Publication bias was assessed using Begg and Mazumdar’s rank correlation test and Egger’s linear regression test. The included studies were stratified according to the depressive symptom assessment tools used and analyzed by subgroup.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1,072 citations were identified through the initial search. After eliminating duplicates, title/abstracts of 570 citations were screened. Following exclusion of 522 citations, 48 publications were deemed potentially eligible and were sought for retrieval. After full-text screening of 48 articles, 27 were excluded: 22 conference abstracts [31–52], three reviews and one study with unknown assessment of depressive symptoms [53–56], and one book [57]. Hence 21 articles were included in this systematic review (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics and results of individual studies

Table 1 Provides a summary of characteristics of all included studies. This review included 21 studies, which comprised 17 cross-sectional and 4 case-control studies. Studies were conducted in Europe (n = 8), North America (n = 5), Asia (n = 6), South America (n = 1) and one study did not report study setting. Studies reported data from a total of 3,144 MS patients with sample sizes ranging from 25 to 504. Across the total set of studies except one, the female gender was predominant within the study populations (n = 2,295 (73.0%)). The mean age ranging from 31.1 to 56.9 years. Fifteen studies reported the number of patients according to MS subtypes. A total of 1,677 patients were identified with relapsing remitting MS (RRMS), 166 with secondary progressive MS (SPMS), 36 with primary progressive MS (PPMS), 5 with progressive relapsing MS (PRMS), and 21 with clinically isolated syndrome (CIS). Notably, in one study reporting MS subtype, the subtype was unknown for 23% of the patients [58]. Disease duration was reported in 18 studies. Level of disability as measured with expanded disability status scale (EDSS) and patient determined disease steps (PDDS) was reported in 14 and one studies respectively. The mean of EDSS was ranging from 1.11 to 5.77. The mean PDDS score of one study was two. Mean of PSQI scores was reported in 16 studies in which scores ranged from 4.02 to 9.30. A PSQI score of greater than five, generally considered as the cut-off point for poor sleep quality, was reported in 12 studies showing that 792 (54.17%) patients were categorized as poor sleepers. For assessment of depressive symptoms, eight studies utilized the BDI, with reported mean depressive symptoms scores ranging from 5.7 to 14.2. Seven studies used the HADS, with mean scores varying between 4.3 and 8.5. HDRS was employed in two studies where, mean scores were 12.6 and 16.2. Other tools such as the PHQ-9, CESD-R, and SDS were less frequently used. Results of individual studies are presented in Supplementary Material 2.

Risk of bias in studies

All four case-control studies had a low RoB. Among the 15 cross sectional studies, 14 had low risk while, one study by Neau et al. (2012) [59] had moderate risk because the setting was not described and confounding factors were not identified. Overall, there was no study with high RoB and all studies were included (Table 2).

Studies using the beck depression inventory (BDI or BDI-II)

Seven studies used the BDI scale [60–67]. All but one reported significant positive correlation between BDI and PSQI scores, with correlation coefficients ranging from r = 0.35 to r = 0.60. One study also reported that BDI score significantly predicted PSQI score (B = 0.34, p = 0.004) [67]. Another study found that patients with depressive symptoms had significantly higher PSQI scores than those without depressive symptoms (10.98 vs. 6.40, p < 0.001) [61]. One study reported a non-significant positive correlation (r = 0.4255, p = 0.1625) [63]. Yeni et al. demonstrated that depressive symptoms directly and indirectly affected sleep quality through fatigue, with significant indirect effects on physical and mental health-related quality of life [60].

Studies using the hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS-D)

Eight studies used the HADS-D [58, 59, 68–73]. Seven of these reported significant positive correlations between HADS-D and PSQI scores (r values ranging from 0.36 to 0.55). One study reported a significant negative correlation (r = -0.359, p = 0.006) [58], which was the only negative correlation observed across all studies. Cederberg et al. found that depressive symptoms was a significant predictor of poorer sleep quality (B = 0.164, p = 0.001) [70]. Vitkova et al. reported that depressive symptoms increased the odds of poor sleep by 5.7 times (OR = 5.7, 95% CI: 1.6–20.2, p = 0.007) [73], with a stronger effect in women (OR = 4.1) than men (OR = 9.1), though not statistically significant. Akın et al. found that individuals with depressive symptoms were 9.82 times more likely to have sleep disorders (p = 0.018) [71].

Studies using the hamilton depression rating scale (HDRS)

Two studies used the HDRS [61, 74], both finding strong positive correlations with PSQI (r = 0.61 and r = 0.64). Aparicio-Castro et al. reported that the mean PSQI score was significantly higher in patients with depressive symptoms than those without (10.98 vs. 6.40, p < 0.01) [61]. Merlino et al. found that depressive symptoms were more common among poor sleepers than good sleepers (77.2% vs. 20.6%, p < 0.001) [74].

Studies using other depression scales (PHQ-9, CESD-R, SDS)

Four studies used the PHQ-9 [75, 76], CESD-R [77], and SDS [78]. Aljundi et al. found that total PSQI score was not significantly correlated with PHQ-9 (p > 0.05), but the PSQI subscale “Daily Disturbances” was significantly correlated (r = 0.55) [75]. Whibley et al. reported that poor sleep quality was linked to a 2.84-point increase in PHQ-9 score [76]. Lamis et al. found a moderate, statistically significant correlation between PSQI and CESD-R (r = 0.54, p < 0.001) [77]. Lobentanz et al. identified PSQI scores as an independent predictor of SDS scores (F = 33.97, p < 0.0001) [78].

Quantitative synthesis of correlation between PSQI and depression symptoms

The correlation between depressive symptoms and sleep quality as measured by PSQI, was reported in 14 studies. Overall, two studies using PHQ-9 and BDI found a non-significant positive correlation [63, 75]. Another study which used HADS, reported a significant negative correlation (r=-0.359, p-value = 0.006) [58]. In contrast other four studies using HADS, significant positive correlations (r values ranging from 0.34 to 0.55) were found [68–70, 72]. Similarly, six studies using BDI, reported significant positive correlations where correlation coefficients ranged from r = 0.35 to r = 0.599 [60–64, 67]. Two studies employing HDRS, both found significant strong positive correlations (r = 0.61 and r = 0.64) [61, 74]. Also, a study using CESD-R, found a significant positive correlation (r = 0.54) [77]. The meta-analysis of 14 studies, in which the correlation between PSQI and depressive symptoms scales have been reported, yielded a pooled effect size of 0.54 (95% CI: 0.49–0.58), with a statistically significant correlation (p-value < 0.01). Overall heterogeneity was high among with an I² value of 83.33% (Fig. 2.). Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s regression (p = 0.89), and Begg’s rank correlation test (p = 0.23), neither of which indicated significant asymmetry (Supplementary Material 3). To find the source of heterogeneity we conducted sub-group analysis based of depressive symptom index and results showed that heterogeneity among studies using BDI scale for measurement of depressive symptom was low (I²: 26.75%) (Fig. 2). In order to address the observed heterogeneity, another meta-analysis was conducted excluding the study by Motaharinezhad et al. [58], which reported a negative correlation. In this condition, the pooled effect size was 0.53 (95% CI: 0.48–0.57), with a statistically significant association (p-value < 0.01). The overall heterogeneity was still high (I²: 65.79%, p value < 0.01). No evidence of publication bias was found (Egger’s p = 0.35; Begg’s p = 0.38) (Supplementary Material 3). According to the GRADE approach, the certainty of evidence was rated as low, primarily due to the observational design of the included studies and substantial inconsistency across study findings.

Discussion

The association between depressive symptoms and sleep quality among patients with other chronic diseases is well established [79–81]. This systematic review was the first to investigate the association between depressive symptoms and sleep quality in patients with MS. In this study, 21 studies were included in most of which a significant association between depressive symptoms and sleep quality among MS patients was found. The pooled analysis of 14 studies demonstrated moderate-to-strong correlation between depressive symptoms and sleep quality while the heterogeneity among all studies was high. The correlation between sleep and depressive symptoms, indicates the importance of these two variables and should be considered together in patients with MS.

Out of 14 studies which were included in meta-analysis, 13 studies found positive correlation. However, one study by Motaharinezhad et al. [58], found a significant negative correlation. Patients receiving anti-depressants, hypnotic drugs, or any related drug that can influence mood and sleep were not excluded. The inclusion of these individuals may have confounded the observed association. Together, these methods explain why this study reported findings that differ from majority of included studies.

There are a variety of assessment tools for detection of depressive symptoms and evaluating its severity in MS patients including BDI, HADS, PHQ-9, and CES-D. However, the structure, psychometric properties, sensitivity and specificity of these scales differ considerably [82, 83]. The HDRS is a standard measure widely used for evaluating the severity of depressive symptoms, which requires clinician observation [84]. Two studies used HDRS scale and analysis showed a moderate correlation between HDRS and PSQI with low heterogeneity. The HADS is a reliable and valid self-assessment questionnaire used to identify and measure the severity of anxiety and depressive symptoms in non-psychiatric hospital patients, such as patients admitted to general medical or surgical wards without a primary psychiatric diagnosis [85, 86]. In this review, eight studies using HADS scale which all of them found significant association between HADS and PSQI. Subgroup analysis showed a significant moderate correlation between HADS and PSQI with high heterogeneity. BDI, a screening tool, is one of the most widely used instruments for detecting depressive symptoms [87]. Seven studies used BDI scale which analysis of six studies showed significant moderate correlation with low heterogeneity. PHQ-9 is a widely used self-report measure that assesses the severity of depressive symptoms [88]. Two study used PHQ-9 scale which showed moderate correlation between PHQ-9 and PSQI. the CES-D and its revised version, the CESD-R, are self-report questionnaires which are used for screening and measuring the severity of depressive symptoms in the general population [89]. One study reported moderate correlation between CESD-R and PSQI.

Furthermore, it is important to note that antidepressant medications themselves can significantly influence sleep patterns, either improving or worsening them depending on the drug type and dosage. While sedative antidepressants such as mirtazapine, trazodone, and doxepin (a tricyclic antidepressant [TCA]) can enhance sleep quality particularly in patients with comorbid insomnia, they may also cause excessive daytime sleepiness and reduced alertness during long-term use. Conversely, activating antidepressants like bupropion, fluoxetine (a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor [SSRI]) and venlafaxine (a serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor [SNRI]) may initially exacerbate sleep disturbances, especially insomnia, during acute treatment phases. Evidence from large meta-analyses involving more than 60,000 patients has shown that most antidepressants are associated with higher risks of either insomnia or somnolence compared to placebo, with fluvoxamine being most linked to somnolence and reboxetine most associated with insomnia. The dose–response relationship between antidepressants and sleep disturbances varies across medications, emphasizing the need for individualized dose adjustment and careful monitoring [90, 91]. In addition, the possible impacts of MS drugs on sleep-related outcomes are also suggested [92–96]. Therefore, when evaluating depressive symptoms and sleep quality in MS patients, it is essential to consider the potential confounding effects of antidepressant treatment on sleep outcomes.

The kynurenine pathway which represents a relation between neuroinflammation and some behavioral symptoms like depressive symptoms and sleep dysregulations. Kynurenine and quinolinic acid are metabolites of this pathway which have immunomodulatory and neurotoxic effects leading to worsening of mood and sleep [97, 98]. Studies on psychiatric and infectious disease populations have noted alterations in kynurenine metabolites in serum and CNS which highlight the central and systemic effects of this pathway [99, 100]. Although direct studies of the effect of this pathway on MS are limited, these findings may suggest a possible pathway by which depressive symptoms and worse sleep quality may be in association via neuroinflammation processes.

Lack of longitudinal studies prevents the establishment of causality; therefore, future prospective studies can potentially clarify the direction of the association between depressive symptoms and sleep quality. Another limitation of evidence was the high heterogeneity observed among studies. Despite subgroup analyzing based on depressive symptoms scales, heterogeneity was high in HADS. A protocol for this systematic review was not prospectively registered, which may introduce reporting bias. In addition, studies published in English or Persian were included in this systematic review, which was another limitation during the review process.

Conclusion

This systematic review suggests a moderate association between depressive symptoms and poor sleep quality in patients with MS; however, the strength of this finding should be interpreted with caution. The included studies were observational, methodological variability was substantial, and heterogeneity remained high across analyses, resulting in low-certainty evidence. Routine assessment of both sleep quality and depressive symptoms in MS patients may still be clinically relevant, but further high-quality prospective studies are required before firm conclusions regarding the magnitude or clinical implications of this association can be established.
