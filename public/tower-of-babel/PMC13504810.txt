Percutaneous versus subcutaneous titanium elastic nail tips: complication profiles by age group in pediatric forearm shaft fractures
Authors: Sabri Gökdere, Sadettin Çiftci, Mehmet Ertanıdır, Ali Güleç, Ebubekir Eravşar, Bahattin Kerem Aydın
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTitanium elastic nailing (TEN) is widely used for the treatment of unstable pediatric forearm fractures. Although subcutaneous nail tips remain the standard technique, superficial (exposed) nail tip placement has been described as an alternative strategy to facilitate implant removal. However, evidence regarding complication profiles associated with different TEN tip management techniques remains limited, particularly with respect to age-related differences.This study addresses three critical gaps: (1) comparative complication rates between superficial and subcutaneous TEN placement, (2) the influence of patient age on outcomes, and (3) identification of age thresholds for complication risk stratification.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 99 pediatric patients treated with TEN for forearm fractures at a tertiary care center (2011–2023). Patients were stratified into Group 1 (subcutaneous TEN, n = 48) and Group 2 (superficial TEN, n = 51). Inclusion criteria required ≥ 1 year follow-up post-removal. Primary outcomes included infection, re-fracture, nonunion, and implant removal time. Secondary outcomes assessed reduction adequacy and age-specific complication patterns. Advanced statistical analyses included ROC curve analysis to identify age cutoffs and logistic regression to evaluate age-technique interactions.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were identified between the groups regarding infection (p = 1.0), re-fracture/nonunion (p = 0.488), or overall complication rates (p = 0.271). However, subgroup analysis demonstrated that patients aged ≥ 9.8 years in the superficial TEN group showed increased risks of re-fracture/nonunion (OR = 5.826, 95% CI: 1.35–25.2, p = 0.01) and overall complications (OR = 3.158, 95% CI: 1.15–8.7, p = 0.02). Implant removal occurred significantly earlier in the superficial TEN group (1.93 vs. 5.43 months, p < 0.001), reflecting technical convenience rather than accelerated fracture healing. ROC analysis identified 9.8 years as a potential age-related threshold for increased complication risk in the superficial TEN group.ConclusionSuperficial TEN tip placement did not increase overall complication rates compared with subcutaneous placement in the overall cohort. However, older children treated with superficial TEN placement demonstrated higher complication risks. The identified 9.8-year threshold should be interpreted cautiously as an exploratory and hypothesis-generating reference value rather than a definitive surgical cutoff. Consideration of patient age and skeletal maturity may assist surgical decision-making regarding TEN tip management in pediatric forearm fractures.

Introduction

Forearm fractures are among the most common pediatric injuries, accounting for approximately 40% of all childhood fractures [1]. By the age of nine, 20% of children experience at least one fracture, with the distal forearm being the most frequent site [1, 2]. Proper management of these fractures is critical to restore full function and prevent long-term complications such as malunion or re-fracture [3]. While non-surgical treatment (e.g., closed reduction and casting) remains the gold standard for most cases, surgical intervention is indicated for unstable fractures, open injuries, or failures of conservative management [4, 5].

Titanium Elastic nailing (TEN) with subcutaneous nail tips and early mobilization has long been considered a standard surgical technique for unstable pediatric forearm fractures, with favorable clinical and functional outcomes reported in the literature [6].Titanium elastic nailing (TEN) has become increasingly popular because of its minimally invasive nature, preservation of fracture biology, maintenance of periosteal circulation, and satisfactory cosmetic results [4, 5, 7]. In addition, TEN fixation allows stable alignment with relatively low complication rates and facilitates later implant removal after fracture healing [7, 8].

Despite the widespread adoption of TEN, debates persist regarding alternative surgical techniques—particularly whether leaving the nail tips subcutaneous versus on the skin affects complication rates. While most surgeons advocate burying the tips beneath the skin to reduce infection risk or implant irritation, some suggest that leaving them superficial may simplify removal without increasing complications [8]. Additionally, the influence of patient age on complication risk—a key factor in pediatric fracture healing—has not been thoroughly investigated. Younger children have a higher remodeling potential; however, it remains unclear whether leaving TEN tips in a superficial position increases complication rates in this patient group [7–9].

This study aims to address these gaps by evaluating whether leaving TEN tips superficial increases complications (e.g., infection, implant migration, or nonunion) in pediatric forearm shaft fractures and whether age serves as a significant predictor of risk. We further seek to identify a potential cutoff age that stratifies patients into distinct risk groups. By clarifying these relationships, our findings will provide evidence-based guidance for surgical decision-making in pediatric forearm shaft fractures.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval was secured from the local ethics committee (decision number 2024/31), adhering to the Helsinki Declaration. The retrospective review encompassed pediatric patients treated with TEN for forearm fractures at Selçuk University Medical Faculty Hospital between 2011 and 2023.

This study included pediatric patients with acute forearm shaft fractures treated with titanium elastic nails (TEN) who had at least one year of follow-up after implant removal. Open fractures, pathological fractures, syndromic cases, and patients lost to follow-up were excluded. Treatment decisions were based on post-reduction radiographs obtained in the emergency department after closed reduction under sedoanalgesia with midazolam or ketamine. Conservative management was indicated for younger children when residual angulation was ≤ 20° in both planes and cortical apposition remained complete, whereas in older children, ≤ 10° angulation and ≥ 50% cortical contact were considered acceptable. Fractures exceeding these limits were managed surgically. Patients initially underwent attempted closed reduction under general anesthesia. Open reduction was performed for patients who could not be reduced closed. The nail diameter was selected according to TEN principles, corresponding to approximately 40% of the narrowest diameter of the medullary canal. In our cohort, nail diameters ranged from 2.5 to 3.5 mm. For the radius, the TEN was advanced proximally via the tubercle of Lister’s or 1–2 cm proximal to the radial styloid. For the ulna, the TEN was advanced distally via the insertion 1–2 cm distal to the tip of the ulna. Implant placement was left to the surgeon’s discretion. When the TEN tips were left outside, the nails were bent just above the skin level without additional rotation to prevent local pressure and skin irritation, and then slightly curved approximately 1 cm above. Patients were advised to apply a soft dressing of antiseptic solution to the base of the affected area every two days. Although TEN typically allows early mobilization, a short period of splint immobilization was preferred in our practice to improve patient comfort and soft-tissue recovery, particularly in younger or less compliant patients. After TEN fixation, patients were immobilized with a splint for 3 weeks to allow wound healing and prevent rotation, and mobilized after the third week postoperatively. Active and passive joint movements were permitted. Strength-based exercises were not allowed.

Fracture healing was confirmed radiographically when at least radio dense three cortices demonstrated continuous callus formation on both anteroposterior and lateral views (Fig. 1). Nonunion was defined as absence of progressive radiographic healing and persistent fracture line beyond 6 months after surgery.

Complications were categorized as major and minor complications.

Major complications included: Re-fracture,nonunion,deep infection/osteomyelitis

Minor complications included: Superficial skin infection, skin irritation, movement restriction at the end of the treatment protocol. Allinfections observed in the study were classified as superficial, defined as localized erythema or discharge at the nail entry site, and weresuccessfully treated with oral antibiotic therapy. No cases of deep soft-tissue infection, osteomyelitis, or infection requiring intravenousantibiotics or surgical intervention were encountered. Those with skin irritation were monitored with soft dressings.Cases of nonunionand re-fracture were treated with plate and screw osteosynthesis.

Statistical analysis was conducted using Python, employing Fisher’s Exact tests and Chi-square tests for categorical data to identifysignificant differences between the groups. Independent t-tests were used for continuous variables such as age. Receiver OperatingCharacteristic (ROC) curves and logistic regression analyses were utilized to explore potential age-related predictors for complications ornonunion. The age threshold of 9.8 years was identified post hoc using ROC curve analysis. Sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve(AUC), confidence intervals, and Youden index values were calculated. The significance threshold was set at a p-value of less than 0.05.

Results

The study assessed the impact of the placement of titanium elastic nails (TEN) superficial versus subcutaneous tissue in pediatric forearm fractures and investigated age-related differences in outcomes. A total of 99 pediatric patients met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed, with 48 patients in Group 1 (subcutaneous placement) and 51 in Group 2 (superficial placement). Overall, the cohort consisted predominantly of male patients (77%), and there were no statistically significant gender differences across the groups (p = 0.584).

No significant differences were found in the rates of re-fracture or nonunion between the two groups (Fisher’s Exact Test, p = 0.488). Similarly, infection rates did not differ significantly, with a p-value of 1.0, indicating uniformity in infection control regardless of TEN placement. Complication rates, encompassing skin irritation, restricted movement, and osteomyelitis, also showed no significant variance (Chi-square = 1.210, p = 0.271). The study further analyzed the reduction types but found no significant differences in the adequacy of fracture reduction between the groups (Chi-square = 0.461, p = 0.497).

Statistical tests indicated significant differences the superficial group had significantly shorter removal times compared to the subcutaneous group (Mann-Whitney U, p < 0.001). The mean removal time was 5.43 months for the subcutaneous group and 1.93 months for superficial group. Further Kruskal-Wallis tests confirmed these differences were statistically significant across different age-based subgroups, leading to targeted post hoc comparisons.

Age-based ROC analysis for the entire cohort did not identify a significant cutoff age that predicted complications or nonunion outcomes, with AUC values indicating non-significance for both re-fracture (AUC = 0.43, p = 0.06) and overall complications (AUC = 0.53, p = 0.14) (Table 1).

To determine whether the identified age cutoffs are clinically relevant, we stratified the patients into four distinct groups based on the age at treatment and the placement of titanium elastic nails (TEN). The groups were defined as follows: Group A (TEN subcutaneous and age < 9.8 years), Group B (TEN subcutaneous and age ≥ 9.8 years), Group C (TEN superficial and age < 9.8 years), and Group D (TEN superficial and age ≥ 9.8 years). This stratification allowed us to assess whether there was an interactive effect between the age of the patients and the placement of TEN on the risk of developing complications.

A logistic regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the risk of re-fracture, nonunion, or other complications across these groups. The analysis revealed significant findings, particularly in Group D, where TEN was left externally in older children. In this group, there was a marked increase in the risk of re-fracture and nonunion, with an odds ratio (OR) of approximately 5.826(CI: 1.35–25.2, p: 0.01), indicating that children in this group were nearly six times more likely to suffer from these complications compared to those in Groups A, B, and C, who had TEN placed subcutaneously or superficial at a younger age. Furthermore, the odds of experiencing general complications in Group D were also elevated, with an OR of 3.158(CI: 1.15–8.7, p: 0.02), suggesting a more than threefold increase in risk compared to younger children with superficial TEN placement and those with subcutaneous placement (Table 2).

Discussion

The primary advantage of TEN in pediatric forearm fractures is the ability to provide stable axial alignment while allowing early mobilization and safe implant removal after complete healing. Any modification of this technique, including differences in nail tip management, should therefore be carefully evaluated in this context. Our findings demonstrate that superficial TEN placement does not inherently increase overall complication rates compared to subcutaneous placement. This aligns with Dincer et al.‘s [8] conclusion that leaving TEN superficial carries no additional risk beyond cosmetic concerns, though our findings provide additional evidence supporting this observation.

Importantly, our findings should not be interpreted as a challenge to established TEN principles. Buried nail tips, dual-bone fixation, and early mobilization remain the standard and most widely accepted treatment methods for unstable pediatric forearm fractures [6]. The purpose of our study was to evaluate whether superficial TEN tip placement, which is occasionally used in selected clinical settings to facilitate implant removal, demonstrates an acceptable complication profile in specific patient populations.

The relatively high rates of open reduction and complications observed in our cohort should be carefully interpreted. As our institution functions as a tertiary referral center, a substantial proportion of the included cases consisted of unstable, severely displaced, or difficult-to-reduce fractures. This may have contributed to the higher rates of open reduction and overall complications compared with classical TEN reported in the literature. The absence of increased infection rates in the superficial TEN group is consistent with previous studies suggesting that careful soft-tissue management and regular dressing care may reduce infection-related concerns associated with implants [8, 10]. Furthermore, all infections observed in the present study were superficial and resolved completely with oral antibiotic therapy. No cases of deep infection or osteomyelitis were encountered.

Moreover, studies have reported that when adequate soft tissue protection is provided, superficial or deep infection rates do not increase, and research exists that supports the safety profile of superficial placement [10]. The absence of increased infection risk (p = 1.0) is particularly noteworthy given historical concerns about superficial placement and supports the growing body of evidence favoring this technically simpler approach in appropriate cases. However, our age-stratified analysis revealed a critical nuance- while safe in younger children, superficial TEN placement in patients ≥ 9.8 years was associated with significantly higher complication rates (OR = 5.826 for re-fracture/nonunion), suggesting that skeletal maturity dramatically impacts outcomes. Nevertheless, the identified 9.8-year threshold should not be interpreted as a definitive biological or surgical cutoff. Given the retrospective design, the limited number of adverse events, and the absence of skeletal age assessment, this threshold should instead be considered an exploratory and hypothesis-generating reference value. This age-dependent risk is consistent with well-documented changes in cortical bone geometry during late childhood, including rapid cortical thickening and reduced remodeling potential after age ten, which may reduce the tolerance for mechanically less stable constructs such as superficial nail tips [11]. This finding bridges the gap between previous conflicting reports, explaining why some studies [12] reported higher complication rates with TEN in older children while others [8] found no difference. An additional contributing factor may be sex-specific variability in skeletal maturation, as girls often reach cortical consolidation earlier than boys, potentially altering risk profiles within the same chronological age groups [13].

The age-dependent outcomes we observed likely reflect fundamental differences in bone biology. Younger children’s remarkable remodeling and healing capacity, well-documented in studies of single-bone fixation [7, 14, 15], appears to compensate for any biomechanical disadvantages of superficial TEN placement. In contrast, our data support Freese et al.‘s [12] recommendation to consider alternative fixation methods as patients approach skeletal maturity, though we identify a more precise age threshold (9.8 years) than previously described. This threshold closely corresponds with the transition periods in fracture epidemiology noted by previous authors [2, 16], suggesting an underlying biological basis rather than arbitrary age cutoff. The logistic regression results further strengthen this interpretation, showing that age and technique interact to determine risk rather than either factor acting alone. Similar interactions between biological maturation and fixation stability have been reported in adolescent forearm fractures, where older children display higher failure rates when relying on constructs that are sufficient in younger patients [17].

Our findings regarding surgical efficiency add important practical considerations to the literature. The significantly shorter removal time for superficial TEN (1.93 vs. 5.43 months, p < 0.001) confirms the logistical advantages first suggested by Baldwin et al. [18], while addressing their concern about secondary procedures. This difference reflected the technical ease of implant removal in superficial TEN constructs rather than accelerated fracture healing. The earlier implant removal observed in the superficial TEN group should not be interpreted as evidence of accelerated biological fracture healing.

This efficiency gain must be balanced against our finding that superficial placement becomes riskier with age, creating a clear decision-making framework: superficial TEN offers safe simplification for younger children but should be avoided in older patients. Whether earlier removal translates into meaningful functional or patient-reported benefits remains uncertain, as previous work has suggested that removal timing alone does not necessarily improve functional outcomes in children [19]. This nuanced approach resolves the apparent contradiction between studies advocating TEN’s efficiency [18, 20] and those cautioning about complications [12, 21] by demonstrating that both perspectives are valid but apply to different age groups.

While the present study provides clinically relevant observations regarding TEN tip management in pediatric forearm fractures, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-center design inherently limits control over potential confounding variables and introduces the possibility of selection bias. Treatment allocation was not randomized, and the decision regarding superficial versus subcutaneous TEN tip placement was based on surgeon preference, fracture characteristics, and intra operative judgment. Second, although the overall cohort size was moderate, the number of re-fracture and nonunion events was relatively small, which may limit the statistical robustness of subgroup analyses and ROC-derived threshold estimation. Therefore, the identified 9.8-year cutoff should be interpreted cautiously as an exploratory and hypothesis-generating reference value rather than a definitive biological or surgical threshold. Third, chronological age was used as the primary developmental parameter, whereas skeletal age and sex-specific maturation patterns were not evaluated and may have influenced healing potential and complications. In addition, standardized functional outcome measures, including range of motion, pronation–supination assessment, patient-reported outcomes, and return-to-activity evaluation, were not available. Radiographic parameters such as residual angulation and radial bowing were also not analyzed in detail. As prior multicenter work has demonstrated, even small residual deformities or imperfect reduction may lead to measurable pronation–supination limitation, highlighting the value of including detailed radiographic parameters in future studies [22]. Furthermore, the relatively high rates of open reduction and complications observed in our cohort may reflect the tertiary referral nature of our institution and the inclusion of more complex fracture patterns. Because surgically treated patients in our institution mainly consisted of fractures with failed initial reduction attempts or referral cases after unsuccessful manipulations, a selection bias toward more difficult-to-reduce fractures may have influenced the relatively high open reduction rate. Finally, earlier implant removal in the superficial TEN group may have acted as a potential confounding factor when interpreting complication profiles. Prospective multicenter studies with standardized treatment protocols and functional outcome assessment are needed to validate these findings.

In conclusion, our results support the consideration of age when selecting the TEN placement strategy, suggesting that an individualized approach may be preferable to a universally applied technique. In children younger than approximately 9.8 years, superficial TEN placement may offer a safe and practical alternative for fracture management and implant removal. For older children approaching skeletal maturity, subcutaneous placement or alternative fixation methods may be preferable. This framework reconciles previous conflicting literature while providing evidence-based guidance for surgical decision-making. The findings underscore that pediatric orthopedics cannot treat children as simply “small adults”- their evolving biology demands tailored solutions at each developmental stage.
