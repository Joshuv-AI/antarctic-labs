Prognostic value of MYD88/CXCR4 mutation allele fraction in Waldenström macroglobulinemia using a multiplex ddPCR assay
Authors: Eleni Thanou, Ioannis Kollias, Eleni Kalafati, Christos Vlachos, Nefeli Mavrianou‐Koutsoukou, Ourania Theologi, Maria Sakkou, Christine Liacos, Konstantina Taouxi, Alexandra Papadimou, Katerina Chrisostomidou, Agisilaos Papadopoulos, Maria Gavriatopoulou, Evangelos Terpos, Evi Lianidou, Meletios A. Dimopoulos, Efstathios Kastritis, Athina Markou, Tina Bagratuni
Published: 2026

Waldenström's macroglobulinemia (WM) is a low‐grade B‐cell lymphoproliferative disorder that is characterized by the expansion of clonal lymphoplasmacytic cells in the bone marrow (BM) that secrete monoclonal IgM immunoglobulin. 1 , 2 The major genetic features of WM include the presence of somatic mutations in MYD88 L265P in 90%–95% and CXCR4 in 30%–40% of patients, both of which significantly contribute to disease development and influence clinical presentation and patient outcomes, notably associated with poorer responses to BTK inhibitors. 3 , 4 Regarding CXCR4 mutations, approximately 50% occur in the S338 and/or R334 region, while the remainder frequently consist of deletions or insertions (indels) in the C‐terminal region of the gene. 3

Recent guidelines from the European Consortium of Waldenström's Macroglobulinemia (ECWM) 5 have suggested the use of allele‐specific quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ASqPCR) and digital droplet polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) on unselected BM samples, as well as Sanger or next‐generation sequencing (NGS) on selected BM CD19+ cells, as highly reproducible and sensitive techniques for the detection of MYD88 L265P mutation. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 On the other hand, mutational analyses of CXCR4 should be performed by Sanger sequencing or NGS on CD19+ enriched BM samples due to the fact that CXCR4 mutations are mainly subclonal. 3 , 11

Lately, the development of multiplex ddPCR assay has enabled the simultaneous detection and quantification of multiple target sequences within a single reaction, providing the advantage of using a small amount of DNA sample. 12 , 13 , 14 In this study, we developed and validated a highly specific, sensitive, cost‐effective, and reproducible multiplex (7‐plex) ddPCR assay for the simultaneous detection and quantification of the MYD88 L265P mutation and other three CXCR4 mutations (S338X C>A, S338X C>G, and R334X), together with their corresponding wild‐type (WT) in a single reaction (Tables S3–S6 and Figure S1A–C).

The clinical potential of this assay was assessed by analyzing BM tumor DNA (tDNA) from CD19+ cells in 173 newly diagnosed patients with IgM monoclonal gammopathies. Additionally, longitudinal samples from 37 patients were analyzed 6 months after ibrutinib therapy, using both tDNA and cell‐free DNA (cfDNA) samples. Detection sensitivity for MYD88 L265P was 5 × 10−4 in tDNA and 5 × 10−3 in cfDNA. For all CXCR4 mutations, the sensitivity was consistently 5 × 10−3 in both tDNA and cfDNA samples. Mutation allele frequency (MAF) values were calculated for each mutation to evaluate tumor burden and monitor the therapeutic response in these patients. Overall, 287 samples from 173 patients (Table S1) were analyzed in this study, of which 40 were IgM‐MGUS (23%), 27 were asymptomatic WM (aWM, 16%), and 106 patients were symptomatic WM (sWM, 61%).

Overall, the MYD88 L265P mutation was observed in 28 out of 40 IgM‐MGUS patients (68%), 24 out of 27 aWM patients (89%), and 96 out of 106 sWM patients (91%). CXCR4 S338X C>A mutation was observed in 16 out of 173 patients (9%), CXCR4 S338X C>G in 17 out of 173 patients (10%), whereas CXCR4 R334X C>T in 5 out of 173 patients (3%). In total, CXCR4 was mutated in 39 out of 173 patients (22%) and, as expected, co‐occurred with MYD88 L265P mutation. Focusing on the MAF of each mutation, the median for MYD88 L265P was 30.87% (range: 1.22%–97.17%), for CXCR4 S338X C>A was 15.69% (range: 0.97%–47.56%), for CXCR4 S338X C>G was 9.38% (range: 1.94%–46.43%), and for CXCR4 R334X C>T was 3.44% (range: 0.67%–15.3%). In line with previous studies from our group and others, 10 , 15 , 16 , 17 MYD88 L265P MAF was higher in sWM with a median 37.24% (range: 3.24%–97.17%) compared to aWM with a median of 19.29% (range: 2.04%–46.86%, P = 0.0001) and IgM‐MGUS patients with a median of 8.5% (range: 1.22%–37.74%, P < 0.0001) indicating its potential use for monitoring tumor burden in WM (Figures 1A and S2A,B). A cutoff point for MYD88 L265P MAF was established to differentiate between sWM and aWM patients (best threshold = 22.16%, F1 score confidence interval [CI]: 0.83–0.91) and between aWM and IgM‐MGUS patients (best threshold = 10.13%, F1 score CI: 0.65–0.85), to better separate the three disease groups. After adjustment for BM infiltration, MYD88 L265P MAF remained an independent predictor of disease state with significantly higher MAF being associated with increasing probability of sWM (P = 0.0066) and of aWM (P = 0.0132) compared to IgM‐MGUS. We observed higher MYD88 L265P MAF in 6 out of 40 IgM‐MGUS (15%) and in 11 out of 27 aWM patients (40%), and lower MAF in 9 out of 106 sWM patients (8%). For CXCR4, we observed higher prevalence of the tested mutations in sWM with MYD88 L265P (n = 30, 28%) and aWM patients (n = 6, 26%) compared to the IgM‐MGUS patients (n = 3, 7%, P = ns), and as expected in most cases (n = 34, 88%) median MAF was lower compared to the MYD88 L265P as these mutations occur at subclonal level. 11 , 18 Additionally, we observed that both CXCR4 S338X C>A and CXCR4 S338X C>G mutations were observed at similar rates in aWM (10% both) and sWM patients (14% both), with no significant differences in the MAF (Figure 1B). Cross‐detection of CXCR4 S338X C>A and CXCR4 S338X C>G mutations was observed in eight patients, where in each case, one of these mutations was subclonal compared to the other. Furthermore, a significant positive correlation of MYD88 and CXCR4 MAF was observed in patients harboring mutations in both genes (P < 0.0001, Figure 1C); however, CXCR4 MAF was not significantly correlated with BM infiltration possibly due to its subclonal nature (Figure S2C).

We then applied this assay on patient samples for which sequential sampling was available at diagnosis (T1, n = 37), 6‐month post initiation of therapy with ibrutinib (T2, n = 37). After a median follow‐up of 1.7 years (range: 0.58–4.08 years), 7 (19%) patients achieved very good partial response (VGPR); 17 (45%) achieved partial response (PR), 4 (11%) patients achieved minor response (MR), whereas 9 (25%) patients had stable disease (SD) or progressive disease (PD). Patients achieving PR or better after 6 months of therapy were defined as the responder group (RG), while patients with less than PR were defined as non‐responder group (NRG). Our cohort consisted of 24 patients in the RG and 13 patients in the NRG (Table S2). Patients lacking the MYD88 L265P mutation or with a MAF < 1% were excluded from the analysis. Initially, our analysis demonstrated that the median MYD88 L265P MAF at diagnosis was slightly higher in the RG compared to the NRG (39.59% vs. 28.82%, P = 0.94), possibly suggesting a trend toward an association of tumor burden with response to ibrutinib 19 (Figure 2A). Following treatment, MYD88 L265P MAF significantly decreased post‐ibrutinib in the RG (n = 20, P = 0.004) while in the NRG, MYD88 L265P MAF was slightly increased with the most cases having a stable MAF pre and post treatment (n = 8, P = 0.49, Figure 2B,C). These findings underscore the potential of MYD88 L265P MAF screening as a potential new tool for assessing treatment efficacy.

The changes during treatment were also assessed in the cfDNA of patients undergoing ibrutinib therapy. As above, 74 samples were collected at two distinct time points: at T1 and T2. In line with the tDNA results, MYD88 L265P MAF at diagnosis was slightly higher in the RG compared to the NRG (P = 0.219) (Figure 2D). Upon treatment, MYD88 L265P MAF in cfDNA was significantly decreased in RG (n = 19, P = 0.0076), while in the NRG MYD88 L265P MAF was slightly increased with most cases having a stable MAF pre and post treatment (n = 8, P = 0.6, Figure 2E,F).

Regarding CXCR4, 5 out of 37 (13%) patients carried the CXCR4 S338X C>G , of which 3 were classified as RG, while two as NRG (median MAF: 33.97%, range: 1%–46.15%). As with MYD88 L265P MAF, CXCR4 S338X C>G MAF in tDNA was reduced in the RG, while remained stable in the NRG (Figure 2G); however, due to the small number of cases, these could not reach statistical significance. In the cfDNA, though, CXCR4 mutations had a very low MAF in these patients, and monitoring these at diagnosis and post‐ibrutinib therapy was not possible.

In summary, using the highly sensitive and specific ddPCR assay, we successfully combined the simultaneous detection of highly mutated genomic events in WM. This approach enables rapid and cost‐effective screening, particularly in cases with limited starting material and cases where a BM biopsy is not feasible for genotyping. It could also be useful during disease monitoring allowing for early detection of disease progression and identification of resistance mechanisms to specific therapies without the need for repeated BM sampling. In this context, we applied this assay in patients at different stages of the disease as well as patients undergoing ibrutinib therapy. Our assay could classify different stages of the disease based on the MYD88 L265P MAF, although few overlaps were observed in each stage in relatively newly diagnosed patients (diagnosed within the last 4 years), which could potentially be associated with earlier progression, as previously supported by our group and others. 17 , 20 Furthermore, we were also able to evaluate ibrutinib responders based on the MYD88 L265P MAF in both tDNA and cfDNA samples, which could be used as a potential tool for monitoring patients. However, understanding the limitation of this approach in not detecting the frequently occurring CXCR4 deletions and insertions in the C‐terminal end of the gene, further work is required in designing an assay that will include these alterations too. In conclusion, further validation of these findings in a larger cohort will be required, including their potential use as predictors with other BTK or non‐BTK inhibitors.
