 ABSTRACT
 The Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT)
program under the Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy aims to
develop and qualify additively manufactured materials for nuclear applications.
One key challenge to this is the microstructural variability observed in the
additively manufactured products and their impact on the properties and
performance of the material in extreme environments. AMMT is using a
combination of high-throughput experimental and modeling techniques to
accelerate qualification. Conventionally, in-situ and ex-situ characterizations
and testing are performed to correlate different aspects of the additive
manufacturing process to the final product and its performance. However,
adopting a trial-and-error approach to experimentally evaluate the vast
range of process parameters required to capture microstructural variability is
cost-prohibitive. Modeling and simulation provide a comparatively inexpensive
way to understand and correlate the microstructural evolution to the processing
conditions. The modeling and simulation work-packages within the AMMT
program aims to use physics-based and machine learning models to develop a
digital twin for additive manufacturing that can correlate the process conditions
to the final product and establish a process-structure-property-performance
(PSPP) correlation.
 The melting and subsequent solidification that occurs during the additive
process is a complex phenomenon that requires multiscale multiphysics
analysis. This work package focuses on understanding the role of process
variabilities on the unique microstructural characteristics of additively
manufactured materials. Microstructural features at the subgrain level, such
as compositional micro-heterogeneity and dislocation cells, are of particular
interest here since they can influence the creep properties and radiation
performance. Idaho National Laboratory’s Multiphysics Object-Oriented
Simulation Environment (MOOSE), specifically the MOOSE Application
Library for Advanced Manufacturing UTilitiEs (MALAMUTE) software,
provides an ideal platform for developing the multiphysics multiscale model
to explore the intricacies of the microstructural evolution during the AM
processes within a single framework. Furthermore, given that such full-fidelity
simulations can be computationally intensive, reduced order models are
necessary to explore the PSPP space for additively manufactured materials in
an efficient, reliable, and cost-effective way.
 This work focuses on capturing the microstructural variabilities at the

 iii

subgrain level that are often missing in the part-scale models. In fiscal year
2025, we significantly advanced upon our work in the last fiscal year, in terms
of the predictive capabilities of the physics-based and ML models, by adding the
capabilities to capture subgrain-level micro-segregation during solidification
using phase-field model and to predict the time-dependent dynamics of the
AM process through the MOGPAR model. The alloy solidification model in
MOOSE incorporates the thermodynamic properties and free energy relevant
to 316 stainless steel. The model demonstrates the Cr and Ni segregation
that occurs during solidification, including that the rate of solidification. The
microstructural evolution model is connected to the process conditions via the
surrogate model developed in this work. This enables predictions of the final
microstructure in conjunctions with the manufacturing process.
 This work supports AMMT’s rapid qualification goals by laying the
foundation for an efficient and cost-effective model establishing the PSPP
correlation for AM. The generated microstructures and predicted micro-
segregation can be used by other work packages under AMMT to evaluate
the properties and environmental response of the material at the mesoscale.
Thus, this work helps to identify the key microstructural features at the
subgrain level that are significant in property and performance predictions of
additively manufactured components. This work will also provide inputs to the
large-scale process variability models to reevaluate and validate assumptions
and simplifications made in the part-scale models. Furthermore, through
active learning this work can help identify the data need from both modeling
and experimental sides for development of a robust digital twin for additive
manufacturing and accelerate the AMMT’s qualification efforts.

 Multiscale and Machine Learning Modeling for
 Process-informed Microstructure Prediction in
 Additively Manufactured Materials using MALAMUTE

 1. INTRODUCTION
 In recent years, additive manufacturing (AM) techniques have gained significant interest across
government agencies and nuclear industries due to their ability to produce complex structures in a shorter
time, facilitating faster fabrication. These techniques have the potential to change the paradigm of new
material and component designs for nuclear applications, such as producing new alloys and graded materials,
as well as innovative component designs for advanced nuclear reactors [1]. The Advanced Materials and
Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program under the Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy
aims to accelerate the development and qualification of additively manufactured materials for nuclear
applications. Several work packages under the program focus on how the additively manufactured products
perform under extreme thermal and irradiation conditions. However, an understanding of the development of
the initial microstructure is crucial to evaluate the performance of the material and eventually qualify it for
advanced nuclear reactor deployment.
 The key challenge that AM techniques face is controlling process conditions to obtain desired
microstructures and properties for targeted applications. AM also faces the issue of reproducibility due to
microstructural and property variation across, as well as within, builds. For example, depending on the
build direction, the texture of the additively manufactured sample could be very heterogeneous, leading to
extremely anisotropic strength and deformation behavior [2–4]. Compared to traditional manufacturing
techniques, qualifying additively manufactured component for extreme environments becomes challenging
due to such morphology variations from build to build and even within a single build [5]. This is especially
true for the development of novel materials for nuclear applications. It is therefore of utmost importance
to understand the microstructural evolution during the manufacturing process and optimize the process
parameters to design materials with the desired microstructures and properties.
 AM is a complex process involving the melting of powder particles and the eventual solidification to form a
complex grain structure. The rapid cooling rate causes unique changes in the microstructure and the layer-wise
manufacturing causes anisotropy in the build direction, thus changing the mechanical properties with respect
to wrought material. Hence, understanding the AM process and its impact on the microstructural evolution of
the material necessitates development of a multiscale multiphysics models, models that capture and correlate
the intricacies of the AM process and the corresponding microstructure formation. The interconnection
between different physics-based models is shown in Figure 1.

Figure 1. Schematic of the multiscale modeling components for establishing PSPP correlations for AM.

 Various experimental and computational efforts have been devoted to establishing a process-structure-
property (PSP) relation for various AM processes. For example, the effects of processing parameters on
solidification textures were evaluated, and an attempt was made to control texture during the electron beam
melting process [4]. However, adopting a trial-and-error approach to experimentally evaluate the process
parameters required for texture control would be costly and time-consuming. Modeling and simulation
provide a cost-effective way to understand and correlate microstructural behavior to processing conditions.
The melting and subsequent solidification that occurs during the AM process is a complex phenomenon
that requires multiscale multiphysics analysis. Several macroscopic studies have been done to capture the
heat and fluid flow during various AM processes as well as part-scale component modeling [6]. However,
the physics-based microstructural analysis of the material undergoing the process has been limited [7].
Additionally, the multiscale multiphysics models that model the AM process and microstructures themselves
can be computationally prohibitive to rapidly explore the vast PSP space and iterate toward optimal material
and component designs. As such, surrogate modeling and machine learning that can reduce the computational
expense of these multiscale multiphysics models have become popular alternatives.
 The Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) software developed at Idaho
National Laboratory (INL), specifically the MOOSE Application Library for Advanced Manufacturing
UTilitiEs (MALAMUTE) software, provides an ideal platform for developing the multiphysics multiscale
model to explore the intricacies of the microstructural evolution during the AM processes within a single
framework. This work package aims to develop a MOOSE-based multiscale model that can couple different
physical aspects of the AM process in a modular way. Here, we utilize and extend the existing capabilities of
MOOSE and MALAMUTE to capture different aspects of the AM process. The physics-based process models
demonstrates the solidification and associated chemical segregation in 316 stainless steel (SS), modeled as a
simplified Fe-Cr-Ni system. Furthermore, a machine learning (ML) framework is developed using MOOSE-
based Stochastic Tools Module (STM) to correlate the process parameters to the final microstructure through
a surrogate model representing the high fidelity process simulations[8]. This work package provides answers

 2

to several programmatic questions regarding the segregtaion behavior observed in additively manufactured
materials. It also delivers a computationally efficient way to connect the process and part-scale models to the
microstructural evolution model.
 This work uses a high-fidelity multiphysics process model as compared to most heat-equation-centric
models to evaluate the thermal history variation with various process conditions. We developed a latent
multi-output Gaussian-process autoregressive (L-MOGPAR) emulator for the coupled temperature and
melt-pool dynamics that yields calibrated uncertainty over space and time. We then pushed these predicted
thermal histories through a grand-potential-based phase-field model to quantify how process-level predictions
influence the solidification rate at the microstructure level. To our knowledge, recent AM machine learning
studies reported fast field predictors with limited UQ and rarely connected process-level machine learning
predictions to microstructure evolution; we closed this gap with an end-to-end pipeline.
 This report summarizes the efforts within fiscal year 2025 to quantify chemical micro-segregation around
the solidification fronts and to connect the microstructural model to realistic process conditions using a
reduced order model. The key highlights of the work are as follows:
• Multiphysics process model: A high-fidelity, arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian-based finite element method
  with free-surface Navier–Stokes, enthalpy-based phase change, radiation, and surface forces (recoil,
  capillarity), which enables an evaluation beyond heat-equation-only surrogates.
• Probabilistic process emulator: A latent multi-output Gaussian process (GP) autoregressive model for
  joint temperature and melt-pool fields with calibrated uncertainty and a Markov temporal structure.
• Phase-field based solidifcation model: A phase-field-based solidification model is developed for 316SS,
  capturing the solidification rate and associated chemical micro-segregation.
• Process-to-microstructure link: The first end-to-end demonstration (to our knowledge) of propagating
  machine learning predictions to a grand-potential phase-field model, which quantifies their impact on
  solidification metrics.
 The rest of the report is organized as follows. In Section 2, we describe the high-fidelity process model
predicting the melt pool dynamics with varying process conditions. In Section 4 we present the phase-field
model for capturing the solidification and associated micro-segregation in 316 SS. Section ?? presents the
machine learning models necessary for deriving the reduced order models for AM thermal conditions. Finally,
in Section 2 we demonstrate how different capabilities of MALAMUTE can be integrated to establish the
process to microstructure connections.

 2. HIGH-FIDELITY PROCESS MODEL
 To provide data for the machine learning models, either a high-fidelity model or detailed measurements
are essential for different process parameters. The process parameters significantly influence the temperature
and shape of the melt pool and subsequently the microstructure of the product itself. This parametric problem
can be challenging to describe, especially with limited access to measurement data. For this reason, a
comprehensive finite element numerical model has been developed in this work, incorporating multiphysics
phenomena that describe the heat transfer and flow in melt pools, together with the melt pool deformation.
This comprehensive model has been successfully applied to single pass laser melting simulations with different
model parameters, ensuring state estimation with sufficient accuracy.

 2.2. Pulsed Heat Source
 In laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) and laser welding, process settings typically use either continuous-
wave (CW) or pulsed-wave (PW) emission [13]. CW delivery favors stable, high-throughput energy input
that often promotes higher track stability at the cost of larger cumulative heat input and a higher propensity
for keyhole/overheating defects. On the other hand, PW delivery lowers average heat input and can sharpen
feature fidelity or thermal control in thin sections. The choice of power delivery mode influences melt
pool geometry, stability, and flow and therefore the local thermal histories that set solidification rates, grain
morphology, and microstructural features. These ultimately influence macroscopic properties such as strength,
fatigue, and anisotropy. Given prior comparative studies showing that power delivery mode measurably
alters melt pool dimensions and melting efficiency, we developed numerical models within MALAMUTE to
investigate the effect of power delivery mode on melt pool characteristics.

 2.3. Melt-pool Dynamics Prediction
 We considered a single-pass laser simulation on a 316L stainless steel plate (100 µm thick, 850 µm long).
The baseline thermal field predictions were obtained using a high-fidelity finite element multiphysics solver
implemented in the MOOSE framework, based on the formulation in Noble et al. [11]. The plate dimensions
were chosen because they would allow the melt pool to develop fully. The simulation setup is presented in
Figure 3.

Figure 3. Simulation results for the process input (74.77 W , 1.0 m/s, 125 µm) sample at 303 µs. The
velocity of the melt pool is visualized using unscaled arrows. The melt-pool deformation, which has a
Gaussian distribution, is also visible at the leading edge of the laser beam.

 This model showcases relevant phenomena for many AM techniques such as direct energy deposition or
LPBF. The model incorporates the temperature- and phase-dependent material properties of 316L stainless
steel, laser-material interaction via a Gaussian heat source, vapor pressure recoil, radiative heat losses, and
surface tension. The deformation of the melt-pool surface is simulated using an ALE approach involving
the fully coupled solution of the energy, momentum, and mass conservation equations. The full-field
spatiotemporal temperature evolution is obtained on the fly by treating the laser heat source as a time-
dependent heat flux boundary condition on the top surface of the domain. For a more detailed description of

 6

 the computational model, we refer the reader to Biswas et al. [15].
 The simulated temperature fields reveal classical melt-pool signatures consistent with LPBF processes,
 showing rapid thermal gradients near the melt-pool boundary, highly localized temperature peaks exceeding
 2500 K, and strong anisotropy along the laser travel direction. These features are comparable in scale and
 topology to the experimental and numerical results reported by Lamb et al. [16], who examined the melt-
 pool geometries and solidification behavior in Ti-6Al-4V under simulated AM conditions. The melt-pool
 elongation and boundary instability are captured well in our simulation outputs, especially under high laser
 power and low scan velocity conditions. Time-dependent snapshots from the simulation further exhibit
 melt-pool oscillations and convective front instability, which are particularly evident at intermediate laser
 velocities [17].
 The model serves as a data generator for the machine learning workflow. To investigate the solution
 manifold, three process parameters were selected: effective laser beam radius, effective laser power, and
 laser velocity. These parameters are known to vary significantly, both across different configurations of a
 single additive-manufacturing machine and between different machines. Figure 4 demonstrates the influence
 of process parameters on the peak flow velocity of the melt pool. The results show that increasing the laser
 power results in a larger melt pool with a higher maximum melt-pool temperature. Conversely, increasing
 scanning speed reduces the melt-pool size because the linear energy deposition is reduced. On the other
 hand, depending on the laser power, changing the effective laser radius can both increase and decrease the
 melt-pool size. In total 498 simulations were performed for different values of the process parameters using

dynamics and surface deformation effects in order to reduce computational complexity while still illustrating
the key differences between pulsed and continuous deposition.

with an effective radius of 70 µm, and the position of the laser centroid is shown in Figure 2. The maximum
simulated time was 240 µs, using adaptive time-stepping with time increments in the range of 10−9 − 10−8 s.
For the continuous energy deposition case, the laser scanning speed was fixed at 0.75 m
 s . The two-dimensional

 3. UNCERTAINTY-AWARE MACHINE LEARNING MODEL FOR
 MELT-POOL DYNAMICS
 In this section we provide the probabilistic machine learning for predicting the temperature and meltpool
dynamics using a latent multi-output Gaussian process auto-regressive (L-MOGPAR) model. The training
data set is generated using the arbitrary Lagragian-Eulerian (ALE) method with free-surface tracking in
the multiphysics object-oriented simulation environment (MOOSE) [9, 19], presented in Section 2.3. An
L-MOGPAR models the covariances between both the inputs (i.e., process parameters) and outputs (i.e.,
temperature fields) and captures time dependencies using a Markov property [20–22]. In addition to predicting
the dynamics, L-MOGPAR provides uncertainty estimates over the fields. Complimentarily, a UNet is
developed, which is adept at processing image data, to predict the temperature and meltpool dynamics in a
deterministic fashion. The performance of both the model to predict the melt pool geometry and temperature
is compared.

 3.1. Latent Multi-output Gaussian Process Auto-regressive Model
 A probabilistic nonlinear model, L-MOGPAR, was used to predict the underlying time-dependent behavior
of the AM process. The spatial covariance of the temperature field from the laser melt-pool simulations is high
dimensional, nonlinear, and time varying. Hence, we seek a low-dimensional representation (latent space)
of this large temperature field using principal component analysis (PCA) [23]. To keep the time-dependent
behavior of the AM process intact, the dimensionality reduction is performed on each time snapshot of
the temperature field for a given combination of process parameters. There are three controllable process
parameters: laser power, scanning speed, and laser beam radius, which are discussed further in Section 2.3.
Using PCA, the high-dimensional 51 × 162 spatial grid temperature field is first reduced in dimensionality
by identifying the orthogonal basis (eigenfields) that captures the dominant spatial patterns of temperature
variation. We retain the top r = 25 principal components at each time step, which explain approximately
99% of the variance in the training data. Each full temperature field is thus represented by a vector of
PCA coefficients, ζ t ∈ Rr . We employ a multi-output Gaussian process in the latent dimension (L-MOGP)
to model the relationship between process inputs and the PCA-reduced outputs, and extend it in time via
an autoregressive (AR) formulation. GPs provide a Bayesian non-parametric approach to regression, and
L-MOGPAR generalizes this to vector-valued outputs that vary in time by defining a joint covariance across
all output dimensions [20]. The principal component (PC) scores representing the spatial correlations of the

 3.2. CNN-based UNet
 A convolutional neural network (CNN)-based UNet was built to learn the time-evolving temperature field
from the AM process. The UNet is composed of an encoder-decoder-type architecture with additional skip
connections. The encoder extracts the small feature map from the input image by convolution and pooling
layers, and the decoder expands it to the original image size by convolution and upsampling layers. The input
vector contains the effective laser input parameters, and the feature map extracted from the inputs is then
passed through several upsampling and convolution layers to increase the spatial resolution and decrease the
number of channels. The encoder consists of five convolution blocks (ConvBlocks) with output channels
64, 128, 256, 512, and 1,024, each followed by max-pooling layers to downsample the feature maps. Each
ConvBlock includes two convolution layers with instance normalization [30] and rectified linear unit (ReLU)
[31] activation. The instance normalization layer is applied to each channel of the data, which corresponds
to normalizing the output data at each time step. Downsampling is implemented as strided convolutions
(motivated by representational bottleneck [32]), and upsampling is implemented as convolution transposed.
The bottleneck layer connects the encoder and decoder, doubling the number of channels from 1,024 to
2,048. To transmit high-resolution information in the input images, the network has skip connections between
each convolution layer of the encoder and the corresponding upsampling layer of the decoder. Each skip
connection simply concatenates all channels at the layer of the encoder with one of the decoders. The decoder
path includes upsampling layers and ConvBlocks, with output channels 1024, 512, 256, 128, and 64, and it
uses skip connections to concatenate corresponding encoder feature maps and retain high-resolution features.
The final output layer is a 2D convolution layer that reduces the number of channels to the number of discrete
time steps, producing the segmentation map, and bilinear interpolation ensures the output dimensions match
the original input size. A schematic of the UNet is shown in Figure 8.

 3.3. Machine Learning Melt-pool Predictions: UNet And L-MOGPAR
 To reduce the computational burden while preserving fidelity, two machine learning models were trained
with spatiotemporal temperature fields generated with the MOOSE multiphysics model discussed in the
previous section: a probabilistic L-MOGPAR model and a deterministic UNet model. In this section we
compare three aspects of these models’ simulation results: temperature field, melt-pool morphology, and
solidification. For all three comparisons, the UNet and L-MOGPAR predictions were consistent with those
of the MOOSE multiphysics model.

3.3.1. Temperature field
 Both the UNet and L-MOGPAR models successfully replicated the spatiotemporal evolution of the
melt-pool temperature fields compared to the MOOSE multiphysics simulations, though their accuracy and
output characteristics differed slightly. Figure 9 compares the temperature fields of the MOOSE multiphysics
model of a representative test data point to the UNet and L-MOGPAR predictions at three time steps: (1)
row 1 at t = 267.7µs, (2) row 2 at t = 444.4µs, and (3) row 3 at t = 800µs. The UNet’s predictions closely
match the MOOSE multiphysics model temperature fields, capturing the high-temperature melt-pool region
and its evolution. In contrast, the L-MOGPAR model produces relatively smoother mean predictions and
slightly diffuses the sharp thermal gradients. The primary discrepancy between the machine learning models
and the MOOSE multiphysics model is observed in the melt-pool area, particularly under high energy density
regimes. The performance of the machine learning models is evaluated at the center point of the domain at
(425, 49) µm with time in Figure 10a and along the center line at the top surface at t = 444.4 µs in Figure
10b. These figures highlight that the UNet model’s predictions for both melt-pool depth and length closely
align with those of the MOOSE multiphysics model, while the predictions of the L-MOGPAR model are not
much different than those of the UNet model.

3.3.3. Uncertainty quantification
 In addition to generating deterministic predictions of the temperature fields, quantifying the predictive
uncertainty of the model predictions to evaluate their reliability is also important. Uncertainty-aware models
can be very useful for optimization and control tasks, as they allow researchers to quantify confidence in
different scenarios and guarantee the robustness of the calculations [34]. L-MOGPAR inherently provides
uncertainty estimates over the mean predictions of the spatiotemporal temperatures. This means that the
model provides a position-resolved uncertainty for any mean value in the feature space, unlike the R2 value,
which only rates the performance accuracy of the GP model overall. Figure 14 shows the predicted standard
deviation (first row), absolute error in the prediction (second row), and mean predicted temperature field (third

 17

row) at three representative time steps for a test sample. The standard deviation maps reveal that L-MOGPAR
is less confident in regions where and at time steps when the melt pool is evolving rapidly—notably around
the melt-pool boundaries and during the peak heating period. This result makes sense because models struggle
to learn complex regimes involving nonlinear physics at the melt-pool front and solidification boundary.
The L-MOGPAR model reflects this complexity by assigning such regions a larger standard deviation.
Conversely, in regions far from the melt pool in the unheated/cooled substrate, the L-MOGPAR uncertainty
is low. Importantly, these uncertainty estimates correlate with the error distribution—where L-MOGPAR
shows large errors, it also reports high uncertainty. Whereas the computation of the error estimates requires
the ground truth solution, the standard deviations provided by L-MOGPAR does not require it. The UNet
model does not provide uncertainty as it outputs a single point estimate of the temperature field.

 Microstructural evolution during the AM process is a complex phenomenon encompassing different length
scales. At the grain level, the morphologies of the grains vary from build to build, as well as within the same
build, depending on the process conditions and build directions. At the subgrain level, various solidification
structures and dislocation cells with different shapes are formed owing to such process variations. Recently, a
MOOSE-based model of alloy solidification for polycrystals has been developed that can predict the geometry
of solidified structures based on the variation in the thermal gradient and cooling rate [35]. We extend the
model to incorporate 316 SS thermodynamics and properties to capture the micro-segregation that occurs
during solidification. We evaluate how process conditions influence the formation of various dislocation
cell structures within the subgrains of the additively manufactured material. This is aims to quantitatively
predict the subgrain features that are important for evaluating the properties and performance of the AM parts

 In this section, we present the solidification model and investigate the impact of accurately predicting the

 4.1. Phase-field Model For Solute Segregation During Solidification
 In this section, we present the phase-field model to capture the solidification and associated solute
segregation observed during the laser powder bed fusion AM method. Here, we utilize a grand-potential-
based multi-phase, multi-order parameter, multi-component phase-field model to capture the solidification
process. The model was initially developed and implemented in MOOSE to capture alloy solidification [36?
, 37]. Considering the non-equilibrium rapid solidification that occurs during the AM process, we extend the
model to incorporate the CALculation of PHAse Diagrams (CALPHAD) method to obtain the relevant phase
diagrams and the thermodynamic properties for 316 SS [38, 39]. The details about the model formulation and
its extension to connect to thermodynamic database, along with verification and preliminary demonstration,
are provided below.

4.2.1. Model verification and parameter selection

the growth of the planar interface. A simplified domain ranging from y = −20 to y = 20 and x = −1 to

The stability of the solid-liquid interface under equilibrium conditions was evaluated for T = Tm . The solute
concentration within the solid and liquid was set at the equilibrium condition to avoid any solute transport. In
the absence of any undercooling, the interface remains stable and maintains the equilibrium configuration, as
there is no driving force for the solidification to occur. Next, we consider the growth of the solid under an
isothermal cooling condition. With undercooling, when T < Tm , the planar interface moves at a steady pace
over time as the solid area fraction increases due to solidification. Additionally, the initial supersaturation of
solute is considered within the liquid to facilitate the solidification.
 Phase-field is a diffuse interface model and solidification characteristics highly depend on the interface
width and corresponding mesh discretizations. In FY 2024, the effect of the interface width on solidification
was studied. The interface width was varied from 200 nm to 80 nm and the steady-state growth rates for
the solids are compared. Based on this analysis, an interface with of 100 nm was deemed sufficient to
capture the appropriate solidification behavior in a computationally efficient manner. Reducing the interface
width further only increased the computational cost without adding significant improvement to the results.
Furthermore, the value of the Allen-Chan mobility, L, was varied to study the kinetics of solidification. From
this analysis, L = 1 was selected, beyond which the solidification rate remains unaffected. This ensures
that the solidification in the material is primarily driven by the thermal and solute diffusion, rather than the
interface kinetics. Additionally, it was demonstrated that the steady-state solute concentration does not vary

significantly with varying interface width and mobility. This indicates that the model is not influenced by
the choice of interface width or other model parameters and can capture the appropriate physical behavior.
Details about this analysis can be found in Ref. [43]. Furthermore, considering that the solute diffusivity in
solids is slower than liquid, the diffusivity of the overall of the system is defined as, D = hl DL + hs DS . For
computational simplification, we assume the same diffusivity values for both of the components and vary
them within the solid and liquid phases individually. Based on the parametric study done in Ref. [43], the
non-dimensionalized diffusivity values were set as 1.0 and 1 × 10−3 .

4.2.2. Solute segregation during solidification
 To demonstrate the solute segregation, we simulate the growth of a solid nuclei in undercooled liquid
field in two dimension (2D). For the first scenario, we set Tr = −2.0. At the initial conditions, the solute
concentrations within the solid is set at the nominal composition, while a supersaturation is introduced within
the liquid to facilitate solidification. The initial Cr and Ni concentration within the liquid are set at 0.2 atomic
fraction. The generated solidification microstructure and corresponding solute concentration is provided in
Figure 20. The corresponding solute tracking is presented in Figure 21. In this case, higher Ni segregation
is observed compared to Cr segregation. This is primarily due to the higher level of deviation from the
nominal composition at the initial condition. Note that although the solid demonstrates anisotropic growth, no
dendritic structure formation has been observed in this case. Inducing surface undulation or high magnitude
of interfacial noise is needed for dendrite formation. This will be explored in the future.

 4.3. Process To Microstructure Connections
 In this section, we present the process to microstructure connection via surrogate model. To this end, we
conduct microstructural simulations with the phase-field model presented in Section 4.1 in conjunction with
the processing model. In Section 4.3.1, we compare the effect of process conditions on solidification using
the temperature gradients and cooling rates obtained from the two surrogates, namely, L-MOGPAR and UNet,
with the predictions from the multiphysics simulation. To accomplish this, we used the MOOSE stochastic
tools module [44] to sample the temperature gradients and cooling rates obtained from the three models (i.e.,
L-MOGPAR, UNet, and multiphysics simulation). This module then launches phase-field simulations (using
the phase-field module of MOOSE) in parallel corresponding to the temperature inputs using the MultiApp
feature of the MOOSE [45].

 In order to study the impact of the L-MOGPAR and UNet temperature field predictions at the material
mesoscale, we performed phase-field simulations of solid formation due to the AM process. We performed
these simulations for all three temperature prediction methods (i.e., multiphysics simulation, L-MOGPAR,
and UNet) for three different input parameter sets, three time instances, and three locations around
the melt pool. The three process parameter sets are input 1 (138.97 µm, 1.41 m/s, 74.11 W), input 2
(138.85 µm, 1.02 m/s, 68.27 W), and input 3 (154.16 µm, 1.17 m/s, 72.86 W). The three time instances
used are 266.7 µs, 444.4 µs, and 800 µs. Figure 22 shows the melt-pool locations for the phase-field
simulations for the three time steps of interest. In total, we performed 81 phase-field simulations of solid
growth across the different prediction methods, time steps, spatial locations, and input parameters. The
goal was to compare the solidification dynamics corresponding to the temperature fields predicted by the
multiphysics simulation, L-MOGPAR, and UNet models. The phase-field solidification model itself was not
approximated in any fashion using machine learning. Rather, the inputs for evaluating the phase-field model,
such as the temperature gradients and cooling rate, were obtained from the three methods (i.e., multiphysics
simulation, L-MOGPAR, and UNet) to predict temperature fields.

 Previous researchers have explored using machine learning for the additive manufacturing of metals (for
e.g., see [47–49]). But the surrogate modeling of full-field temperature trajectories with CNN-based UNets
and GPs, and the performance of surrogate models for predicting solidification and microstructure formation,
have not been studied before. It is valuable to predict the thermal response of the AM substrate and its effect
on directional solidification, which impacts the final grain morphology of the AM materials. Analyzing
melt-pool dynamics is a challenging task considering its multiphysics and multiscale nature. The developed
coupled model in this work yielded detailed insight into the underlying mechanisms by simulating melt-pool
energy transport, deformation, and solidification. However, this came at the price of increased computational
resources. Other works neglected similar melt-pool resolution by considering a heat-conduction-only model
[50, 51], which can reduce the accuracy in energy gradient and temperature history that is essential for high-
fidelity solidification modeling. Therefore, in this work, we relied on data-driven workflows to accelerate
parametric studies.
 The L-MOGPAR and UNet surrogate models offer a computationally-efficient and accurate analysis of
the thermal histories for microstructure solidification evolution. From the results in Section 3.3, it can be
inferred that UNet yields lower errors across the domain compared to the L-MOGPAR model. UNet’s errors
are relatively lower and mostly localized near the dynamic melt-pool boundary, while the L-MOGPAR model
exhibits larger error magnitudes and a broader error region. This is expected, as the L-MOGPAR regression
assumes a smooth kernel in its covariance function, and this can smooth out localized extremes. UNet’s ability
to preserve fine spatial details is due to its encoder-decoder architecture with skip connections, which allows

 When discussing the trade-offs and considering which machine learning approach to deploy in production,
it is important to not only evaluate the model’s performance, as measured by standard metrics, but also
other properties, such as model interpretability [55] and robust UQ [56]. While deep learning models offer
exceptional performance, they are difficult to interpret [57]. On the other hand, L-MOGPAR leverages
the flexibility and interpretability of GPs while capturing structure across outputs [58, 59]. Additionaly,
the robust UQ of deep learning models such as UNet’s is often computationally expensive and can lead to
misrepresentations of predictive uncertainties [60]. On the other hand, the standard deviations inherently
obtained from the L-MOGPAR model provide a measure of uncertainty in the model’s prediction. When the
standard deviation is high, it indicates that the model is less confident about its prediction, which often leads
to regions where the absolute error of the mean prediction is also high. The spatial distribution of the model’s
standard deviation stems from the truncated PC components used in the multi-output GP and/or test data
points that are outliers compared to the training data. The improvement in the standard deviations decreases
and saturates with the number of PCs used to train the L-MOGPAR model, hence the standard deviation
contribution is likely only from the distance of a test data point from the training cloud. Upon training and
validating the L-MOGPAR against an increasing number of PCs, we concluded that 25 PCs are sufficient to
capture about 99% of the explained variance without compromising the training time of the surrogate. Hence,
by leveraging the uncertainty estimates, further experiments or simulations can be set up using active learning
to improve the confidence in the test predictions by reducing the epistemic uncertainty.
 Linking the machine learning models’ accuracy in predicting melt-pool dynamics, or the lack thereof, to
microstructural evolution is a unique aspect of this work. This enabled us to evaluate the influence of process
conditions on the formation of the solidification rate and subgrain morphologies in the additively manufactured
material. The melt-pool predictions of both the L-MOGPAR and UNet models resulted in highly satisfactory
solidification rates. Although the L-MOGPAR model has higher percentage errors for predictions under
certain process inputs and for certain spatiotemporal locations, on average either the L-MOGPAR or UNet
model is viable for the solidification simulations.

the conditions simulated. Figure 25a demonstrates the solidification via growth of a planar solid-liquid

reaches a steady-state growth rate after some time. The solidification rate for the three cases vary due to
variation in the cooling rate resulting from the variations in the process conditions. Rate of solidification
increases with increasing cooling rate. Figure 25b shows the position of the solid-liquid interface at simulation

combined effect of solute drag and solute rejection. Higher level of segregation values observed in these
simulations compared to the experimental observations. This could be attributed to the initial supersaturation
assumption in the liquid. Further analysis and model validation will be pursued in the future.

 5. CONCLUSION
 In conclusion, we investigated the microstructural evolution of 316 SS during the LPBF process in
connection to the process conditions using physics-based and machine learning models. we developed and
evaluated a probabilistic, multi-output Gaussian-process autoregressive (L-MOGPAR) surrogate for modeling
spatiotemporal temperature and melt-pool dynamics in laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), and contrasted it with
a fast UNet baseline. Both models were trained on the same high-fidelity Arbitrary Lagrangian Eulerian–finite
element method Navier–Stokes plus enthalpy data. We further propagated surrogate predictions to a grand-
potential phase-field model, enabling an end-to-end assessment of how process-level prediction accuracies
affect microstructure evolution. We demonstrated the solidification behavior and quantified associated
micro-segregation for different process conditions. Such segregation behavior is crucial for evaluating creep
and irradiation performance of the AM materials.
 While comparing the point accuracy of the surrogate models, UNet achieved higher coefficients of
determination on temperature and spatial gradients (RT2 = 0.986, R∂2x T = 0.918, R∂2y T = 0.938) than
L-MOGPAR (RT2 = 0.931, R∂2x T = 0.746, R∂2y T = 0.85). However, L-MOGPAR provides calibrated
predictive uncertainty over space and time, revealing where epistemic uncertainty is large (e.g., in high-
gradient regions near the melt-pool boundary) and where predictions are reliable. These spatially resolved
uncertainty estimates correlated well with the spatial errors. When the machine learning predictions of the
temperature and melt-pool dynamics were passed to the phase-field model on solidification, both models
satisfactorily captured the dendritic structure formation. The predictions propagated well to the chemical
microsregregation predicted at the solid-liquid interface of 316 SS. Quantitatively, the segregtaion values
obtained from the simulations were higher that what has been observed in experiments. More one-to-one
validation is needed to improve this further.
 The model predictions can be improved further by generalizing the physics choices (laser/path space,
boundary conditions, and selected surface forces) to broader process windows (e.g., multitrack/multilayer),
to add additional surface physics (e.g., thermocapillary shear) and incorporate experimental measurements.
Additional physics, process windows, and experimental measurements would further strengthen the viability
of uncertainty-informed machine learning models for advanced manufacturing (AM) process predictions.
Overall, our results indicate that risk-aware process decisions benefit from probabilistic surrogates: even when
a deterministic model is slightly more accurate on average, quantified uncertainty is pivotal for quantifying
robustness. The phase-field model can be easily extended to capture segregation on phase boundaries and
grain boundaries due to its multi-phase multi-order parameter nature.
 In summary, we present the computational framework necessary for capturing different aspects of the
AM process encompassing different length-scales and associated microstructural evolution at the subgrain
level. This effort provides a strong foundation for reaching the overall goal of establishing a reduced-order
process-structure-property-performance (PSPP) correlation for AM materials in a cost-effective way and
thus supporting AMMT’s rapid qualification goals. This work will also provide crucial physics-informed
information for the larger length scale process models developed by the AMMT team at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory. The generated models can also be used within an active learning framework to identify data need
for accelerated material development and qualification for AMMT program.
