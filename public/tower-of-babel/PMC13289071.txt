Analysis of bilateral development assistance for surgery in low- and middle-income countries: trends, challenges and opportunities
Authors: Simon Fabry, Gabriella Yael Hyman, Nardos Getachew Bulfeta, Desmond T Jumbam, John E Varallo, Kee B Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical care in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) remains underfunded despite representing a significant portion of the Global Burden of Disease. Understanding bilateral development assistance for surgery is crucial to address this disparity.MethodsWe conducted a narrative review and quantitative analysis of bilateral development assistance for surgery in LMICs from 2014 to 2022 using data from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development Creditor Reporting System. Surgical projects were identified through keyword search. We report funding trends, key actors, project classifications, implementation channels and regional distribution, and examine trends through thematic analysis of key characteristics of bilateral aid.ResultsBetween 2014 and 2022, US$2.73 billion was disbursed to surgical projects. Surgical funding decreased as a proportion of total bilateral development assistance for health (DAH), from 2.13% in 2014 to 1.32% in 2022. Key donors were Canada, the USA, Australia, Italy and Korea. Obstetrics and gynaecology received the most funding among surgical subspecialties. Non-governmental organisations were the primary implementation channels (51.4%), which could undermine public sector development by bypassing national health systems, perpetuating fragmentation and limiting governments’ capacity to sustain surgical services independently. Sub-Saharan Africa was the principal recipient region, securing 55.17% of disbursements.ConclusionsDespite increasing commitments, surgical care remains underfunded, with an overall 1.84% share of bilateral DAH. Contributing factors include limited expressed public sector demand and absorption capacity in recipient countries, donor disbursement practices tied to performance indicators and shifting donor priorities. Recipient countries can enhance funding utilisation and donor attraction by strengthening national surgical plans, improving health system capacity, engaging stakeholders collaboratively, advocating for balanced funding across specialties and exploring innovative financing mechanisms. Addressing these challenges is essential to optimising surgical care funding and improving health outcomes in LMICs.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Drawing from databases that track bilateral development assistance for health, we quantified bilateral aid to surgical care in LMICs from 2014 to 2022. Surgical care is underfunded as a proportion of total bilateral assistance to health. The landscape of bilateral donors which disburse to surgical care shows a distributed donor base compared with the handful of donors that dominate the bilateral aid to health portfolio. Notably, a subset of these donors allocated a substantial share of their bilateral aid portfolios to surgical care, suggesting differential prioritisation across the donor landscape.

Introduction

In global health discourse, certain disease areas frequently dominate the agenda—infectious diseases, reproductive, maternal, neonatal and child health (RMNCH), trauma, and non-communicable diseases (NCDs).1 Donors and international organisations design their strategies and allocate funding with these priorities in mind, principally guided by the objectives of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).2 3

It might be assumed that surgery largely falls under the NCDs umbrella,4 which account for an estimated 74% of deaths worldwide. This figure rises to 77% of the total and 86% of premature deaths in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).5 However, surgery transcends this single category and is vital for addressing a multitude of pressing global health challenges—from reducing high mortality rates in RMNCH to managing complications of infectious diseases to containing the escalating burden of injuries and trauma.6

In 2015, the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery reported that 5 billion people lack access to safe and affordable surgical and anaesthesia care, highlighting profound global disparities that disproportionately affect LMICs.7 Scholarly works have emphasised that surgical care is essential to achieving universal health coverage (UHC),8 and multilateral organisations such as the WHO have increasingly recognised the importance of surgical and anaesthesia care in various resolutions and frameworks,9 fueling the global surgery movement. Consequently, global health leaders have intensified efforts to bolster surgical capacities in LMICs, especially as UHC and donor priorities have become central to global health discussions.10 11

Despite the increasing acknowledgement of the importance of surgical care, securing funding for it remains a significant challenge.12 Donor reluctance to fund NCDs has been attributed to several factors: the absence of disease transmissibility, high intervention costs, inadequate infrastructure, and the complexity and delayed measurability of outcomes.13 These challenges are equally, if not more, pertinent to the field of surgery, which is often overlooked as an integral component of the continuum of care.14

The development of National Surgical Plans (NSPs) has been crucial in attracting funding for surgery15 16 by facilitating potential donor cooperation and integrating surgical care into broader national health policies.17 While multiple LMICs are in various stages of NSP development and implementation,1821 most of these efforts remain underfunded and require further support to become operational.12 To bolster donor attraction and ownership, the Pan-African Surgical Healthcare Forum has added the development of national health insurance schemes and improvements in infrastructure and equipment for surgical care as recommendations to their consensus statement. Additionally, participating ministries of health consented to establish a budget line for surgical and anaesthesia care, stating that NSPs alone would not be sufficient in achieving surgical system integration or attracting further funding.22

As part of a broader series on funding avenues for surgery, this study concentrates on bilateral aid. Bilateral development assistance for health (DAH) refers to financial and technical support provided directly by a donor country’s government to a recipient country via various implementation channels aimed at improving health outcomes and strengthening health systems.23 To fully address the challenges that recipient countries face when seeking bilateral assistance for surgery, it is necessary to understand the current landscape. Our study aims to equip LMICs with the knowledge and strategies needed to navigate this vital funding source to finance their NSPs. It presents evidence on characteristics, opportunities, and barriers of bilateral aid and quantifies bilateral assistance to surgical care over the past decade. Hence, our research question is as follows: Based on data from 2014 to 2022, what lessons can be learnt from the landscape, trends and patterns of bilateral assistance to LMICs for surgical care?

Methods

This study consists of quantitative analysis and narrative review of literature.

Data sources and preparation

We conducted a quantitative data analysis of DAH using two primary databases: the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) Financing Global Health Database24 and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) Creditor Reporting System (CRS) database.25 These two databases are publicly available, methodologically rigorous and transparent, and collect data from multiple sources to compile evidence on development assistance. The list of bilateral donors included in our analysis is listed in online supplemental appendix IA.

Data analysis was performed using R Studio statistical software R V.24.04.02.26

IHME Financing Global Health Database

The IHME database provided an overview of bilateral aid trends from 2014 to 2022, including data on donor sources, funding channels and recipient regions. The IHME employs various methodologies to impute missing data,27 and missing data in this dataset was <5%. After removing duplicates, we aggregated the data to focus exclusively on bilateral channels. Custom health focus areas relevant to surgical conditions were identified: NCDs (excluding mental health and tobacco control), maternal and child health (MCH) (including neonatal care and excluding vaccine programmes) and sector-wide approaches (SWAps, excluding pandemic preparedness programmes). The cleaning and aggregation of IHME data offered an overview of bilateral aid trends for the years 2014–2022 revealing important data on sources, funding channels and recipient regions. Additionally, for the years 2014–2022, we were able to extract bilateral aid data for the custom health focus areas relevant to our analysis.

OECD CRS database

The OECD CRS28 collects data from members of the Development Assistance Committee (DAC), non-DAC member states, multilateral organisations and philanthropy, allowing uniform comparison across organisations to address our main research question. It offers detailed project-level information, including project titles, descriptions, implementing entities and financial commitments and disbursements. The database is updated at least twice a year, in April and December to report provisional and completed values of the preceding year, respectively.29 The study used the June 2024 version, which was the most complete version for the latest year (2022) we reported, to avoid provisional data. Data preparation included merging data for the years 2014–2022 and excluding data with sector codes other than the health-related DAC sector codes (DAC3 codes: 120, 121, 122, 123 and 130). We excluded data not reported as bilateral and retained the ‘USD Disbursement Deflated’ column as our primary financial measure.

Identification of surgical projects

Data extraction of surgical projects involved a comprehensive keyword search using terms in English, French and Spanish that were widely associated with surgery, including, but not limited to, ‘cirugía’, ‘operación’, ‘quirúrgico’, ‘pediátrico’, ‘oncológico’, ‘oxígeno’, ‘sangre’, ‘anestesia’ and ‘anestesiología’. This method is adopted from the IHME method to comprehensively search the project descriptions in the CRS database, which is reported in various languages.27 Full keywords are detailed in online supplemental appendix IB.

Inclusion criteria encompassed projects with surgical aspects, surgical system integration initiatives, emergency, critical and operative care, surgical equipment and infrastructure, blood supply and transfusion services, digital health, telemedicine, perioperative services and capacity building (including non-specialist surgical training). Exclusion criteria included projects exclusively on infectious diseases without addressing surgical complications, non-surgical RMNCH, research in high-income countries, general public health initiatives without a surgical focus, pharmaceutical research and development, and projects without clear relevance to surgical care (see online supplemental appendix IC). The parent and child codes have been outlined in online supplemental appendix ID. Projects that have keywords that fit multiple surgical specialty categories were classified as ‘Multispecialty Programs’.

We quantified total bilateral aid for the study period, described trends in disbursements, key actors, preference of implementation channels, and regional distribution. All values are expressed in 2022 USD values. Full details of quantitative analysis are provided in online supplemental appendix IE.

Narrative review

To further assess the role of bilateral aid in surgical care and how those characteristics influence disbursement trends and other quantitative findings, we conducted a narrative review with thematic analysis. Literature search was performed on MEDLINE and Google Scholar using a combination of keywords (see online supplemental appendix II). The review focused on themes related to accessing bilateral assistance for surgery: facilitators, barriers and unique attributes in comparison to other funding channels. Findings are presented as comparative advantages and disadvantages to inform strategies for strengthening bilateral aid flows to surgical care in LMICs.

Title and abstract screening was performed independently by two authors (SF and GYH), with discrepancies resolved through consultation with NB. Eligibility criteria (table 1) guided the selection of titles and abstracts, after which full texts of qualifying articles were retrieved and reviewed. While surgical care is cross-cutting across various domains, for the majority of global health discourse over the past two decades, surgically treatable conditions such as cancer, cardiovascular and neurovascular diseases have been placed under the NCDs category per the Global Burden of Disease (GBD). NCDs were therefore in our inclusion criteria. The GBD category ‘Injuries’ was not included due to multiple humanitarian and disaster relief aid, which is distinct from development aid and was excluded from our quantitative analysis on the same basis. Once the final set of relevant documents was established, SF undertook inductive coding at the paragraph level in Microsoft Word to categorise content characterising governance, capacity and distinguishing features of bilateral aid. Emerging codes were then reviewed collaboratively by authors and consolidated into four overarching themes which formed the basis for synthesising the findings. Definitions of common terminologies used in the manuscript are summarised in table 2.

Results

Bilateral assistance for health 2014–2023

Trends in disbursements

In 2022, governments provided US$53.8 billion in DAH, constituting roughly 76% of all DAH recorded (US$71.1 billion). Governmental aid included US$20.3 billion in core contributions to multilateral organisations, primarily GAVI and the Global Fund, and an additional US$2.26 billion in core contributions to non-governmental organisations (NGOs). The remaining US$27.8 billion, about 39.1% of all DAH, was identified as bilateral aid. This represents a 109.8% increase in bilateral disbursements from 2014 (US$13.25 billion), where bilateral assistance represented a significantly lower 30.7% of total DAH (US$43.17 billion). Bilateral aid from 2014 to 2022 accounted for 41% of DAH.

Key actors

Bilateral aid is driven by several key donor countries; from 2014 to 2022, the USA was the largest contributor, providing over US$75.7 billion in DAH, approximately 51% of all bilateral assistance (US$147.8 billion). Germany contributed US$14.6 billion and the UK provided US$10.7 billion. Other significant donors included Japan (US$7.8 billion), France (US$5.5 billion) and Canada (US$5.6 billion). In total, more than 81% of all bilateral assistance was provided by these six donors. China, the only emerging donor included in the IHME database, ranked fifth with US$6.25 billion in disbursements.

Implementation channels

In 2022, a considerable portion, 36%, was directed at public sector entities, primarily recipient governments. Multilateral organisations channelled 28% bilateral aid for earmarked projects, while civil society received 16% for specified projects. The involvement of the private sector in the recipient country was evident, with 9%, and academic and research institutions with 5%. Lastly, 2% was allocated to public–private partnerships and networks.

Health focus areas

While the IHME Financing Global Health database offers Health Focus Areas (Parent Codes) and Program Areas (Child Codes) to estimate funding for specific diseases, surgery or surgical conditions are not tracked separately.

Analysis of custom aggregates, which exclude non-surgical areas, reveals moderate trends (figure 1). MCH funding fluctuated over the period, peaking at US$1.428 billion in 2019 before declining sharply to US$0.752 billion in 2021, with a slight recovery to US$0.994 billion in 2022. NCD funding showed a general upward trend, increasing from US$0.074 billion in 2014 to US$0.187 billion in 2022, despite some year-to-year variations. SWAps funding demonstrated the highest overall levels and relative stability, ranging from US$1.120 billion to US$1.749 billion, with projected funding of US$1.611 billion for 2022. In comparison, funding for infectious diseases rose steadily from US$10.19 billion in 2014 to US$25.03 billion in 2022.

Bilateral assistance for surgery, 2014–2022

In our analysis of OECD CRS data from 2014 to 2022, a total of 3153 surgical projects were identified, involving 39 bilateral donors (list included in the online supplemental appendix) and 129 recipient countries across 6 World Bank regions.

Trends in disbursements

Bilateral disbursement to surgical care totalled US$2.73 billion in the study period. Analysis revealed significant fluctuations in bilateral funding (see online supplemental appendix III, figure i) with annual values ranging from US$192 million to US$440 million. While overall health sector disbursements increased substantially, surgical project funding represented 1.84%, a declining fraction of total bilateral DAH, dropping from 2.1% in 2014 to 1.32% in 2022.

Key actors

Certain countries emerged as key donors in surgical funding (figure 2), and their contributions were analysed relative to their total bilateral DAH. Canada contributed US$596.1 million to surgical projects, representing 16.72% of its total bilateral DAH, with high yearly variability peaking at 39.12% in 2017. The United States provided US$582.5 million to surgical projects, with surgical funding representing 0.78% of its total bilateral DAH, ranging from 0.31% to 1.87% over the years. Australia’s disbursement to surgical projects was US$241.9 million, accounting for 8.6% of its total bilateral DAH. Italy’s total disbursement to surgical projects was US$207 million. Surgical funding accounted for 17.56% of Italy’s total bilateral DAH, peaking at 36.67% in 2019. Korea’s disbursement to surgical projects was US$191 million, accounting for 6.81% of its total bilateral DAH. The United Arab Emirates (UAE) disbursed US$97.7 million to surgical projects, with surgical funding constituting 9.37% of its total bilateral DAH.

Project classification and funding allocation

Subspecialties

The analysis of funding allocation to surgical subspecialties revealed distinct patterns. Obstetrics and gynaecology emerged as the most funded single specialty domain, with annual disbursements ranging between US$17.7 million and US$118.3 million. Ophthalmology received significant funding, often in the range of US$3.5–US$25.7 million per year. Orthopaedics and trauma care secured the third-largest share, receiving between US$2 and US$21 million annually, often associated with integrated primary healthcare initiatives or humanitarian projects in conflict or disaster areas. Paediatric surgery has gained increasing attention over the years, with disbursements rising from less than US$1 million in 2015 to more than US$10 million in recent years. Other surgical specialties, such as general surgery, neurosurgery, and urology, received smaller but steady allocations, typically under US$5 million annually.

Infrastructure and equipment

Funding for infrastructure and equipment received the highest allocations among all categories, ranging from US$99.8 million to US$222.3 million annually. This focus underscores the recognition of inadequate surgical facilities and medical equipment as major barriers to surgical care in LMICs30 and easily trackable and measurable outcomes for donors. Investments in infrastructure include constructing and equipping hospitals, upgrading operating rooms, and enhancing supply chain management systems.

Implementation channels

When examining the implementation channels for surgical funding within the study period, NGOs received the largest share at 51.4%, highlighting the significant role of NGOs in implementing surgical system development and capacity building in LMICs. Public Sector Entities received 31.72% of funding directly, facilitating direct support for national health systems. Universities and Research Institutions accounted for 5.42% of funding, reflecting contributions to academic collaborations, training, and research initiatives. Multilateral entities including the UN channelled only 6.83% of funds (figure 3).

OECD CRS: bilateral assistance for surgery: implementation channels, 2014–2022. CRS, Creditor Reporting System; NGOs, non-governmental organisations; OECD, Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development.

When compared with overall bilateral DAH in 2022, where 36% of bilateral aid was directed at public sector entities, 28% channelled through multilateral organisations, 16% to NGOs, 9% to the private sector and 5% to academic and research institutions,15 16 it is evident that surgical projects rely more heavily on NGOs and less on public sector entities and multilateral organisations.

Regional distribution

Securing 55.17% of disbursements, sub-Saharan Africa emerged as the primary recipient region for surgical funding, reflecting significant surgical needs and gaps in surgical care. Countries like Canada, the USA, Italy, and Korea concentrated substantial portions of their surgical funding in African nations, supporting projects ranging from MCH surgeries to capacity building in surgical training. East Asia and the Pacific region received 15% of funds particularly from donors like Australia, Germany, and Korea, focusing on improving maternal health services and establishing specialised medical centres (see online supplemental appendix III, figure ii).

Emerging donors

In the OECD database, we observed five emerging donors who disbursed a total of US$230.5 million in the study period, with disbursements ranging from US$5.88 million to US$66.6 million annually. The United Arab Emirates was the leading emerging donor with US$97.9 million. The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, with US$87.11 million, Kuwait with US$26.46 million, Qatar with US$14.39 million, and Turkiye with US$4.5 million, account for the rest of the disbursements. Just over 81% of disbursements were given in the form of grants, while the rest were loans administered directly to recipient governments. Grants were administered through NGOs 50% of the time, recipient governments in 21%, and multilateral and private entities in the rest. The UAE, Qatar, and Turkiye administered all financial contributions as grants. KSA disbursed 80% as grants and 20% as loans, whereas Kuwait disbursed 96% as loans to recipient governments.

Donors disbursed to 42 countries across 6 World Bank regions, with the Middle East and North Africa receiving the highest percentage of disbursements at 52.4%, matching the tendency for emerging donors to focus on their neighbours. Sub-Saharan Africa followed with 23.68%, and South Asia, 11.9% of funds. Disbursements to infrastructure and surgical equipment accounted for nearly 68% of disbursements, while Ophthalmology received the highest share of funds with US$29 million (13.5%) as a single specialty programme.

AidData: Global Chinese Development Finance

China does not report to the OECD on its global development cooperation. Existing literature indicates that DAH from China is usually provided in five categories: medical teams, construction of hospitals, donation of drugs and equipment, training of health personnel, and malaria control.31 The largest share of health aid is spent on medical teams and donated facilities. We explored Aid Data Global Chinese Development Finance32 for health projects funded from 2014 to 2022 to quantify funding to surgical care using the same keywords listed in the methodology.

The dataset included 2192 health-related projects with a total monetary value of US$5.24 billion (2022 Constant). About 88% of these funds were disbursed for humanitarian, medical mission, equipment donation and training activities. A total of US$601.3 million was disbursed in the form of loans and grants, out of which 51%, US$306.8 million, went to surgical projects. Disbursements went to support infrastructure development in Nepal, Turkiye, Senegal, and Sri Lanka to build operating rooms, oncology centres, various surgical specialties, and obstetric centres. Chinese state/private owned companies were the implementing agency for all grants, while the loan to Turkiye was a joint venture between the two governments (see online supplemental appendix III, table i).

Narrative review

The literature search resulted in 477 articles, of which 369 were excluded at the title and abstract stage due to duplication and ineligibility. The remaining 108 articles were reviewed in full text, and 85 were excluded. 23 articles were selected for inclusion, and a thematic analysis was conducted (see online supplemental appendix III, figure iii). The analysis drew on key themes that characterise bilateral aid, with particular description of the unique attributes that potentially facilitate or hinder bilateral support to surgical systems compared with other external assistance modalities such as multilateral assistance or philanthropy.

Comparative advantages

Accountability and alignment of health priorities

Bilateral aid offers a higher degree of direct accountability and program level execution than multilateral organizations, and donor heterogeneity can broaden the partnerships and approaches available to recepient countries compared to the narrower mandates of vertical funds which dominate the multilateral landscape, enhancing the alignment between donor and recipient health priorities and ultimately contributing to the relevance and impact of health programmes.33 34 This direct government-to-governemnt (G2G) relationship allows bilateral donors to work closely with recipient countries, enabling better monitoring, evaluation, and implementation of surgical care interventions.35 The strengthening of these relationships fosters trust and enables the tailoring of programmes to meet local needs. Several bilateral funding agencies ranked among the most harmonised donors in terms of aligning with recipient country priorities.36 This alignment was demonstrated by donors such as the United States Agency for International Development(USAID), which had integrated surgical care into broader health system strengthening strategies.12

Effective communication plays a pivotal role in maintaining these partnerships. Establishing open lines of communication between recipient countries and bilateral donors ensures transparency and fosters mutual trust. Regular updates on progress, challenges, and successes of programmes keep donors engaged and foster dialogue on shared goals and expectations, strengthening partnerships and aligning interests.35 37 Such direct interactions often sustained accross decades through longstanding diplomatic partnerships offer continuity of engagement with priority partner countries.38

Sustainability and multisector support

Bilateral aid relationships are often long-term in nature and are intertwined with broader diplomatic and economic partnerships. This strategic alignment increases the likelihood of sustained support for health and surgical programmes while creating avenues for assistance in other key sectors.39 Bilateral programmes frequently include substantial components of technical assistance and knowledge transfer,30 40 which are particularly beneficial in fields like surgical care, where capacity-building of specialised skills and access to advanced technologies are essential.41

Unlike private philanthropic donations, which are shaped by market volatility and donor-specific interests, bilateral aid is generally structured as a sustained strategic engagement, with donor governments targeting development in countries where their own economic, security, and migration interests are linked to recipient outcomes.38 This predictability provides recipient countries with the confidence needed to scale surgical initiatives and achieve sustainable health outcomes over time.33 36 With the rapid growth of South-South bilateral cooperation that is based on mutual economic development and regional integration,42 the role of bilateral aid to health and, thereby to surgical care, will continue to rise.

Comparative disadvantages

Political influence

Bilateral aid is not without challenges, with political influence being a significant factor in determining who receives aid and how it is allocated. Partner selection bias—where bilateral aid is driven by strategic or geopolitical interests rather than health needs—can result in a misalignment between funding priorities and the pressing health needs of recipient countries.43 44 Moreover, political motivations may dictate resource distribution, potentially leaving critical areas like surgical capacity unaddressed.44

Furthermore, diplomatic disruptions, such as political instability, conflicts, or leadership/government changes, can jeopardise bilateral aid programmes leading to the abrupt termination of aid.45 Recent coups in West African nations, including Mali (2020 and 2021), Guinea (2021), Burkina Faso (2022) and Niger (2023), have resulted in significant reductions or suspensions of bilateral aid, particularly from influential donors like France.4547 These interruptions disrupt health systems and affect infrastructure, leaving ongoing programmes underfunded and jeopardising their sustainability.48 On the donor side, frequent political changes may lead to shifting priorities, destabilising aid predictability.49 We are witnessing the impact this characteristic has in the changes observed in 2025.50

Systemic barriers

Another limitation of bilateral aid is its reliance on performance-based financing (PBF) mechanisms aimed at enhancing aid effectiveness. While PBF schemes improve accountability, they may not be suited to the realities of delivering surgical care under resource constraints.38 It can be difficult to accurately measure and reward performance indicators under such systems.34 Surgical care, in particular, requires advanced tracking of indicators such as surgical volume, complications, and adherence to safety protocols—all of which are difficult to reliably collect or analyse in LMICs.51 This mismatch can disadvantage surgical programmes, making it harder to secure or sustain bilateral funding.33

Finally, bilateral aid may create dependency and donor-driven projects run the risk of failing to sufficiently develop local systems, especially when external expertise is overly relied on.52 Without a focused effort on workforce training and health system strengthening, recipient countries may struggle to sustain progress after donor funding diminishes, emphasising the need to invest in domestic healthcare providers and infrastructure to ensure the long-term viability of surgical programmes.35

The preferential use of donor or international-based NGOs for the implementation of bilateral aid creates parallel systems in recipient countries, reducing efficiency and creating fragmentation in programmes.53

Discussion

The striking underfunding of surgical care

Despite the significant contribution of surgical conditions to the GBD, surgical care continues to receive a disproportionately small share of bilateral aid. While surgical conditions are estimated to account for approximately 11%54 to 28%–32%7 of the disease burden, our analysis reveals a stark contrast between this substantial need and the funding allocated to address it. Between 2014 and 2022, only US$2.73 billion was disbursed for surgical projects, representing a mere 1.84% of total bilateral DAH. In 2022, this accounted for 1.32% of bilateral DAH, while in comparison, infectious diseases received approximately US$120.74 billion (61.6%). This chronic underfunding of surgical care underscores a persistent imbalance in global health priorities. Possible reasons for this imbalance include:

Recipient absorption capacity: Many LMICs struggle to absorb increased funding due to critical limitations in infrastructure, human resources and administrative capacity. Infrastructural constraints were identified as a significant barrier to surgical care in Malawi, resulting in high elective surgery cancellation rates.55 Addressing these bottlenecks by strengthening health systems could, therefore, enhance absorption capacity and optimise the impact of donor funding.

The trend of emerging donors, especially China and India, has been more in kind (training, equipment/medication delivery, medical missions, humanitarian aid) than in cash41 56 which has made quantification of disbursement difficult. However, with the rising support from emerging donors,57 whose global health financing model differs from traditional bilateral aid,56 there is a potential for mutual investments that lead to long-term partnerships and offer sustainability and economic growth.

Donor priorities in surgical funding

Donor priorities heavily influence how bilateral funding is allocated to surgical care. For example, countries such as Canada and Italy allocated higher proportions of their total bilateral DAH (16.72% and 17.56%, respectively) to surgical projects, reflecting a strategic attention to the surgical domain.44 Canada demonstrated consistent investments in maternal and neonatal health and infrastructure projects, further reinforcing its prioritisation of surgical capacity-building. Meanwhile, countries such as the United States (0.78%) and the UK (0.81%) allocated relatively minor fractions of their bilateral DAH to surgery, instead broadly dispersing resources across various health sectors.44

The prioritisation of specific surgical subspecialties reflects both donor preferences and global health agendas. Funding for obstetrics and gynaecology aligns with the SDGs, particularly those targeting reductions in maternal, child and neonatal mortality.2 Similarly, substantial investments in ophthalmology likely stem from the cost-effectiveness of procedures such as cataract surgeries, which yield immediate and visible health improvements.58 Funding for orthopaedics and trauma care may mirror the growing burden of injuries in LMICs, alongside the need for robust systems to provide emergency trauma care in settings affected by conflict or disasters.59

However, lower allocations to specialties like general surgery, anaesthesia and perioperative care indicate foundational gaps in addressing the surgical needs of LMICs. To build holistic and sustainable surgical systems, balanced investments across all surgical specialties, including workforce development in anaesthesia, perioperative nursing and general surgical training, are essential.

Additionally, infrastructure development, which consistently receives substantial allocations, underscores an acknowledgement of inadequate facilities and supply chains as major barriers to expanding surgical care in LMICs.30 However, infrastructure funding must be paired with investments in human resources, including the training of healthcare workers and targeted recruitment.35 Without adequately trained professionals operating in these upgraded facilities, infrastructural investments risk falling short of their potential impact.

Implementation channels for surgical projects: preference of civil society

The analysis shows that NGOs are the dominant implementation channel for surgical funding, receiving 51.4% of disbursement allocations. This demonstrates their pivotal role in delivering surgical services and capacity-building programmes in LMICs. However, over-reliance on NGOs carries the potential to undermine public sector development by bypassing national health systems, perpetuating fragmentation, and limiting governments’ capacity to independently sustain surgical services.37

In comparison, 31.7% of funding was directed to public sector entities, suggesting an underutilisation of government-led mechanisms for healthcare delivery. Strengthening national capacity requires bilateral donors to channel more funding through public sector programmes to enhance institutional capacity and ensure ownership of health systems by recipient governments. Partnerships between NGOs and governments may serve as a viable model, combining the responsiveness and expertise of NGOs with the institutional stability and scalability of public health systems.35

Implications for recipient countries’ best practice

Based on the analysis above, recipient countries can adopt strategic approaches to address the persistent underfunding of surgical care, improve utilisation of donor commitments, and maximise the unique advantages of bilateral aid.

Strengthening NSPs and financial frameworks

NSPs offer an actionable pathway for embedding surgical care into broader national policy frameworks. These plans, when aligned with donor priorities, can attract targeted funding and facilitate sustainable integration of surgical services into UHC agendas.60 Research demonstrated the transformative economic and social value of surgical investments, emphasising their triple return on investment through improved health, economic benefits and strengthened systems.17 NSPs must also focus on domestic resource mobilisation and include transparent financial frameworks to address donor concerns over fungibility while fostering accountability.33

An illustrative model is the USAID-supported MOMENTUM project in Nigeria, which linked grassroots-level interventions to national health priorities through participatory planning.61 By involving local stakeholders in project design, this initiative directly addressed systemic bottlenecks such as gaps in infrastructure and workforce shortages while informing the development of Nigeria’s NSOANP.62 Despite its discontinuation, MOMENTUM remains a successful, replicable financing model for LMICs.

Improving absorption capacity

The ability of LMICs to translate financial commitments into tangible results depends on their absorption capacity. Weaknesses in areas such as workforce readiness, data reporting, and administrative systems often limit the effectiveness of PBF mechanisms.51 Investments in health systems strengthening, infrastructure, workforce training, and data management can close these gaps. Reducing reliance on external expertise and prioritising investments in local capacity as critical to fostering surgical system self-sufficiency.35

Stakeholder engagement and collaboration

Strong stakeholder engagement is critical for ensuring the relevance, ownership and sustainability of surgical initiatives. The MOMENTUM project highlighted how participatory design and leveraging NGO expertise in implementation, while maintaining government ownership, can effectively align objectives and resources.61 Collaboration among governments, NGOs and civil society creates a united approach to addressing gaps and scaling surgical services sustainably.

Balanced funding allocation

Recipient countries should work with bilateral donors to advocate for balanced funding across specialties. While measurable interventions, like maternal health services and cataract surgeries, tend to attract donor attention, holistic system strengthening requires equal attention to anaesthesia, perioperative care and general surgery. Gradual scaling of such areas fosters sustainable improvements and ensures comprehensive surgical care for future generations.39

Strengthening innovative financing mechanisms

There is a growing momentum around blended finance which reflects increasing political will among development funders to move beyond traditional aid modalities toward more sustainable financing architectures.59 OECD partners have begun reframing SDGs as investment opportunities, a shift that could meaningfully reshape the trajectory of bilateral aid. Complementary mechanisms such as debt-for-health swaps—already demonstrated in communicable disease responses including tuberculosis—offer additional pathways for leveraging bilateral partnerships in scaling surgical care in LMICs.63 64 Italy’s latest commitment to convert sovereign debt into investments in development sectors in African LMICs represents one concrete opportunity that regional stakeholders and programme implementers could strategically engage.65

The abrupt discontinuation of USAID in early 2025 highlights the inherent political nature of health and bilateral aid. The effects were immediate and far-reaching: longstanding US funding commitments were suspended overnight, creating deep uncertainty for development partners and recipients alike.66 Many European government donors have reassessed their commitments and, in several cases, have already announced significant reductions to their aid budgets, amplifying the ripple effect across the international development community.66 67

However, while Finland, France, Germany, the UK, and the USA reduced their investments in global health in 2025, contributions from other countries, including Canada, China and the United Arab Emirates, have stayed steady in 2025, while Japan, Australia and South Korea increased their DAH slightly from 2024.57 These countries are also found in our analysis to allocate a higher percentage of their development assistance to surgical care. Our analysis shows, especially in the years after 2020, disbursements to surgical care were distributed across multiple donors rather than being dominated by a few.25 This has significant implications that, despite declining overall bilateral development assistance from major donors, multiple bilateral partners continue to support LMIC health system strengthening.

Furthermore, the US 2025 America First Global Health Strategy affirms that global health foreign assistance will remain a key strategy for strengthening the US bilateral relationships with countries around the world.68 The strategy also addresses the plan to reduce use of third parties for implementation, and focus on direct government-to-government engagement with recipient countries, which would reduce parallel systems and inefficiencies in LMICs.68 With such shifting strategies that reframe traditional bilateral assistance, increasing opportunities in innovative mechanisms, and the increase in the number and capacity of emerging donors over the past decade, bilateral aid will continue to support development in LMICs, although with different dynamics.

Limitations

This study has several limitations due to potential gaps in the aid allocation data for surgery. First, our reliance on a keyword search within the OECD CRS database to identify surgical funding may have excluded relevant projects lacking specific keywords, potentially underestimating actual funding levels. Second, the data does not include contributions from emerging donors such as China, India and Brazil, which limits the comprehensiveness of our analysis. However, multiple non-DAC emerging donors such as the UAE, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, Turkiye report to OECD are included in our analysis. Third, by focusing on DAC sectors 120 to 130 related to health, we may have missed surgical funding categorised under other sectors, particularly 720 (Emergency Response) and 730 (Disaster Prevention and Preparedness). Non-English literature was excluded due to limiting non-English proficiency potentially excluding relevant literature. Future research should aim to include all relevant DAC sectors and incorporate data from emerging donors to provide a more complete picture of global surgical funding and identify potential avenues to foster multisector collaboration.

Conclusions

Bilateral aid plays a critical role in addressing the significant gaps within the surgical care systems of LMICs. Bilateral donor priorities have driven substantial, yet fractionally small, investments in key areas such as maternal health, neonatal care, trauma, infrastructure and high-impact subspecialties like ophthalmology. However, other foundational components of surgical systems—such as anaesthesia, critical and perioperative care and general surgery—continue to be underfunded, exposing critical imbalances in allocation that hinder long-term sustainability and scalability.

Strengthening capacity, aligning donor and recipient priorities, and engaging local stakeholders are essential to mitigating these gaps. Evidence shows how bilateral assistance can be a key driver for change: through participatory approaches, capacity building, and integrating evidence-based grassroots interventions into national policies can help overcome implementation challenges and enable sustainable system strengthening.

Future research should investigate the factors contributing to the commitment-disbursement gap and evaluate how to overcome these barriers through improved donor–recipient collaboration. Comparative studies examining the sustainability of NGO-driven versus public sector-led implementation models, as well as approaches to address allocation inequities across surgical subspecialties, will be key to improving the efficiency of bilateral aid.
