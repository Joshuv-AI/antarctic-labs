Homological algebra over non-unital rings and algebras, with applications to ( ∞ , 1 ) (\infty,1) -categories

Abstract
The article is developing homological algebra in modules over non-unital rings and algebras. The main application is the definition and study of (directed) homology of ( ∞ , 1 ) (\infty,1) -categories and of directed spaces, including relative homology and its exact sequence.

1
Introduction

The category of modules over rings is the archetype of the category in which to carry out homological algebra. Indeed this category is abelian, and all abelian categories can be represented as a concrete category of modules over some ring, by the Freyd-Mitchell embedding theorem
Freyd 1964
. This goes for modules over
unital rings
, and a natural question is to know whether this holds as well for modules over non-unital rings, for which there exists several versions, e.g. firm, closed and
s
-unital modules,
Quillen unpublished manuscript, 1996
;
Positselski 2023
;
Tominaga 1976
. Under tame assumptions, all those notions because equivalent and give rise to an abelian category, making them prime candidates for homological theories.

This question is not uniquely of an abstract nature. In numerous contexts, one would actually need a homology theory over non-unital rings, or even non-unital algebras.

For instance, in topological data analysis, persistence modules
Zomorodian and Carlsson 2005
;
Carlsson and Zomorodian 2007
;
Bubenik et al. 2015
are used to define persistence homology, and are, by Morita equivalence, the same as persistence modules over their path algebras
Assem et al. 2006
. In case the underlying filtration or multi-dimensional filtration has infinitely many strata, or in case the underlying category has an infinite number of objects, the corresponding path algebra has an infinite number of idempotents. In this case, the path algebra (and in particular its underlying ring) has no natural unit, and persistence modules are to be considered over a non-unital algebra (and underlying ring).

In directed topology
Fajstrup et al. 2016
;
Grandis 2009
, topological spaces under study are “directed”, meaning they have “preferred directions”, for instance a cone in the tangent space, if we are considering a manifold, or the canonical local coordinate system in a precubical set. A directed homology theory that accounts for directed features has been introduced in
Goubault 2024a
, as particular persistence modules over the path algebra of the underlying quiver of a precubical set, building on natural homology theories
Dubut et al. 2017
. Once again, if the precubical set has an infinite number of points, this path algebra has to be considered non-unital. This explains the limitation of the approach of
Goubault 2024a
which only considers finite precubical sets, and the impossibility to define a directed homology theory for more general directed spaces, let alone
(\infty,1)
-categories.

In this paper, we begin by recapping the somewhat scattered knowledge about modules over non-unital rings and adapt it to (bi-)modules over non-unital algebras. We then proceed to apply it to a (directed) homology theory for simplicially enriched categories, which constitute a model for
(\infty,1)
-categories in the case where the hom-simplicial-sets are Kan.
We prove that, even in this non-unital context, relative homology can be defined and corresponding exact sequences, proven. We make the conditions for these exact sequences to hold more explicit in the case of directed spaces, extending the approach of
Goubault 2024a
, at the end of the article.

Organization of the paper

We start in Section

by reviewing the scattered litterature about modules over non-unital rings and algebras. An important ingredient in understanding the properties of categories of modules over non-unital algebras is the unitalization functor, for algebras and modules, that we study in great detail in Section

In most applications we are envisioning, such as the ones in directed topology, there is a need for bimodules instead of (left or right) modules. In the classical unital case, there is no particular difficulty in going from (left or right) modules to bimodules since

4
On categories of closed, firm and s-unital modules

Take some unital commutative ring
R
. We can show that the category of modules over the
R
-algebra
R
as defined earlier (in Definition
4
) is not equivalent to the category of modules over the ring
R
in the usual sense (see introduction of
Quillen unpublished manuscript, 1996
).

This is one of the reasons why one might question whether
{}_{\mathbb{A}}\mathrm{Mod}
(or more generally
{}_{\mathbb{A}}\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathbb{B}}
) is a good category to do homological algebra in.

An article of Quillen
Quillen unpublished manuscript, 1996
answers this concern by defining three reasonable categories of modules over a nonunital ring or algebra, that are more natural in some ways and coincide under mild hypotheses. We will go over his approach after having defined the tensor product of modules over algebras.

4.1
Nonunital tensor product

Quillen defines the tensor product of modules over a rng using an arbitrarily chosen embedding of the rng into a ring. If that embedding is taken to be the canonical embedding given by unitalization, then this gives rise to the following construction.

Taking some
R
-algebra
\mathbb{A}
, a right
\mathbb{A}
-module
M
and a left
\mathbb{A}
-module
N
, we start by seeing
M
and
N
as modules
\tilde{M}
and
\tilde{N}
over the unitalization
U(\mathbb{A})
using the category isomorphism of the previous section. Then, we consider the tensor product

\tilde{M}\otimes_{U(\mathbb{A})}\tilde{N}

This amounts to taking the free abelian group generated by
M\times N
, and quotient it out by the subgroup generated by elements of the following form, for
m,m^{\prime}\in M
,
n,n^{\prime}\in N
and
(a,r)\in U(\mathbb{A})=\mathbb{A}\oplus R
:

•

(m+m^{\prime},n)-(m,n)-(m^{\prime},n)

•

(m,n+n^{\prime})-(m,n)-(m,n^{\prime})

•

(m.(a,r),n)-(m,(a,r).n)

Since
(m.(a,r),n)-(m,(a,r).n)=(m.a,n)-(m,n.a)+(m.r,n)-(m,r.n)
, we can equivalently replace elements of the form in the last point above, into elements of the following form:

•

(m.a,n)-(m,a.n)
for
a\in\mathbb{A}

•

(m.r,n)-(m,r.n)
for
r\in R

The canonical projection
M\times N\to M\otimes N
is, as usual, denoted by
\otimes
.

This gives us an expression of the universal property of the tensor product that does not reference the unitalization in any way.

Definition 8
.

Let
G
be a commutative group. A function
f:M\times N\to G
so that, for any
m,m^{\prime}\in M
,
n,n^{\prime}\in N
and
a\in\mathbb{A}
and
r\in R
:

1.

f(m+m^{\prime},n)=f(m,n)+f(m^{\prime},n)

2.

f(m,n+n^{\prime})=f(m,n)+f(m,n^{\prime})

3.

f(m.a,n)=f(m,a.n)

4.

f(m.r,n)=f(m,r.n)

is said to be a
\mathbb{A}-
balanced bi-additive function
.

This tensor product is clearly characterized by the following universal property (that does not appear to be stated in previous literature):

Proposition 5

(Universal property of the tensor product)
.

For every commutative group
G
and any
\mathbb{A}
-balanced bi-additive function
f:M\times N\to G
,
there exists a unique map
\overline{f}:M\otimes N\to G
such that

f(m,n)=\overline{f}(m\otimes n)

for any
m\in M
and
n\in N
.

Since this tensor product is defined in terms of the usual one, all of the usual properties of the unital tensor product carry over, including the extra structure when tensoring bimodules, associativity etc.

4.2
Categories of firm and closed modules

Let us consider an
R
-algebra
\mathbb{A}
(or a rng, since rngs are particular cases of
\mathbb{Z}
-algebras).

The first category we are considering is obtained by quotienting
{}_{\mathbb{A}}\mathrm{Mod}
as to identify with zero all ill-behaved objects. Consider the full subcategory of left
\mathbb{A}
-modules

verifying the following degeneracy property:

\exists n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\mathbb{A}^{n}M=0

This category is called the category of

. It is a Serre subcategory
Serre 1953
, which means that we can quotient it out to obtain an abelian category
\mathbf{{}_{\mathbb{A}}\mathcal{M}}
of non-degenerate modules. This is the most fundamental and well-behaved category of non-degenerate modules over non-unital algebras, but it is also not very explicit given because of the quotient operation.

The second category is the category of firm modules as defined below:

Definition 9

(Firm module)
.

The modules
M\in\;_{\mathbb{A}}\mathrm{Mod}
for which the natural map

\mu:\mathbb{A}\otimes M\to M,\quad a\otimes m\mapsto a.m

is an isomorphism
are said to be
firm
.

We will denote by
{}_{\mathbb{A}}\mathrm{FMod}
the full subcategory of
{}_{\mathbb{A}}\mathrm{Mod}
of left firm
\mathbb{A}
-modules.

Remark 7
.

Note that for arbitrary
\mathbb{A}
, the category of firm modules is not necessarily abelian
González-Férez and Marín 2007
.

The last category is defined dually to firm modules:

Definition 10

(Closed module)
.

The modules
M\in\;_{\mathbb{A}}\mathrm{Mod}
such that the natural map

\mu^{\prime}:M\to\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{A},M),\quad\mu^{\prime}(m)=(a\mapsto a.m)

is an isomorphism
are said to be
closed
. The category of closed modules is the full subcategory of
{}_{\mathbb{A}}\mathrm{Mod}
whose objects are closed modules.

However, those three categories are often the same as shown by the following theorem established by
Quillen unpublished manuscript, 1996
.

Definition 11
.

A rng
A
is said to be
idempotent
if
A^{2}=A
.
An algebra is idempotent when it is idempotent as a rng.

Theorem 6
.

If

is an idempotent

-algebra
then the category of firm modules, the category of closed modules, and the category of non-degenerate modules

are all equivalent abelian categories.

All of this obviously extends to right modules by duality.

In this article, we will only use idempotent rings so we will systematically use firm modules and bimodules for convenience.

4.4
Categories of
\mathbf{s}
-unital modules

The algebras we will be working over have some additional properties that will allow us to describe firm modules in a more convenient way
. A more detailed explanation of the most useful results can be found in

Tominaga 1976
;
Komatsu 1986
, here we only check that they hold under our definition of module over an algebra. All the following is folklore, that we prove only for completeness, as the literature about it is quite scattered.

Definition 14
.

A left (resp. right) module
M
over an
R
-algebra
\mathbb{A}

\mathbf{s}
-unital

\forall m\in M,\;\exists e\in\mathbb{A},\quad e.m=m

(resp.
m.e=m

s
-unital as a left (resp. right)
\mathbb{A}

A bimodule is

-unital.

Remark 10
.

Any left (or right)

-unital algebra is clearly an idempotent algebra.

Proposition 9
.

Any left (resp. right)
s
-unital module

over a left (resp. right)

Proof.

This is essentially the same proof as that given for rngs in

The main point is that, given some
m\in M
and

such that

does not depend on the particular choice of

Indeed, take

such that

e.m=m=e^{\prime}.m

By

such that

and

such that

f^{\prime}(e^{\prime}-fe^{\prime})=e^{\prime}-fe^{\prime}

Then, by setting

ge=fe+f^{\prime}e-f^{\prime}fe=e+f^{\prime}(e-fe)=e

ge^{\prime}=fe^{\prime}+f^{\prime}e^{\prime}-f^{\prime}fe^{\prime}=fe^{\prime}+e^{\prime}-fe^{\prime}=e^{\prime}

As such,

and

It is then clear that the function that associates to each

and thus

5
A convenient abelian category of bimodules

When considering unital algebras
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{B}

\mathbb{A}

\mathbb{B}
-bimodules are the same as unital
\mathbb{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{B}^{\text{op}}

\mathbb{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{B}^{\text{op}}
modules (as we will show), but we do not expect the converse to be true.

First of all, it is clear that
\mathbb{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{B}^{\text{op}}
can be given a
R\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}S^{op}
-algebra structure, when
\mathbb{A}
is a
R
-algebra, and
\mathbb{B}
is a
S
-algebra, by defining the product law by:

(a\otimes b)(a^{\prime}\otimes b^{\prime})=(aa^{\prime}\otimes b^{\prime}b)

and the bimodule law by:

(r\otimes s).a\otimes b=(r.a\otimes s.b)

Now, define a functor
\mathrm{Merge}
by the following:

-

To every

\mathbb{A}
-
\mathbb{B}
-bimodule
M
, for any
m\in M
, use the universal property of the tensor product to define a left
\mathbb{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{B}^{\text{op}}
-module law on
(M,+)
such that

(a\otimes b).m=a.m.b

for
a\in\mathbb{A},b\in\mathbb{B}

and

(r\otimes s).m=r.m.s

for
s\in S,r\in R

and associate the resulting
\mathbb{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{B}^{\text{op}}
-module
\mathrm{Merge}(M)
to the bimodule
M
.

-

To every
\mathbb{A}
-
\mathbb{B}
-bimodule morphism

, associate
f
itself, since then

f((a\otimes b).m)=f(a.m.b)=a.f(m).b=(a\otimes b).f(m)

and

f((r\otimes s).m)=f(r.m.s)=r.f(m).s=(r\otimes s).f(m)

.

This functor is faithful, but it fails to be full as soon as there exists a nonzero left
\mathbb{A}
-module

: the bimodule obtained by setting

\forall m\in M,\forall b\in\mathbb{B},\quad m.b=0

is sent to a
\mathbb{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{B}^{\text{op}}
-module such that
\lambda.v=0
for any vector
v
and any scalar
\lambda
.

Since we do not have a general category equivalence, obtaining an abelian category of bimodules is a bit more complicated than in the unital case. This is the category of
s
-unital bimodules we are defining now:

Definition 15

(
s
-unital bimodules)
.

If
\mathbb{A}
is an algebra over
R

\mathbb{B}
is an algebra over
S

\mathbb{A}

\mathbb{B}

.

The functor
\mathrm{Merge}

The category

Proposition 12
.

\,{}_{\mathbb{A}}\overline{\mathrm{Mod}}_{\mathbb{B}}

Proof.

Let
M
and

be

-unital
\mathbb{A}

\mathbb{B}
-bimodules.

Then they are

(a+a^{\prime}-a^{\prime}a).m=a.m+a^{\prime}.m-a^{\prime}.(am)=a.m=m

Since the canonical decomposition of any

∎

is a simplicial map.

A simplicially enriched category is said to be

if all the hom-sets are Kan complexes.

Fibrant simplicially enriched categories are of particular interest because they model

-categories,

. However, the machinery developed here applies to general simplicially enriched categories. We believe this construction could be extended to quasi-categories, but this would require complicated technical lemmas that are unnecessary for our purposes.

6.1
Defining the algebra of paths

We want to embed a category’s

Definition 18

6.4

Let us take a fixed

and
R

R^{\prime}
two fixed commutative rings.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}
and

be two simplicially enriched categories and

induces a functor

.

.

Proof.

We already explained how to define

Suppose that

Now let us prove

Since

6.5
Relative homology for

-categories

This subsection generalizes an earlier construction of directed topology for finite precubical sets

Overview

A crucial aspect of singular homology is that the chain complex

-category to that of a sub-

7.1
Directed spaces

There exists multiple notions of space with a direction of time, but one of them stands out for being particularly general and well-fitted for homology: directed spaces (see e.g.
Fajstrup et al. 2016
;
Grandis 2009
for all definitions in this section), which we present briefly in this section.

Definition 24

(Directed space,
Grandis 2009
)
.

A

is a pair

where
X
is a topological space and
dX
is a set of continuous paths in
X
, called the

of the space, such that;

(i)

Every constant path is a directed path

(ii)

Every increasing reparametrization of a directed path is a directed path i.e. for every directed path
\gamma\in dX
and for any function
\varphi:[0,1]\to[0,1]
which is continuous and increasing,
\gamma\circ\varphi\in dX

(iii)

For any directed paths
\gamma
and
\delta
so that
\gamma(1)=\delta(0)
, the concatenation
\gamma\cdot\delta
of
\gamma
and
\delta
is a directed path

One simple example of a directed space is the closed interval
[0,1]
with increasing function
[0,1]\to[0,1]
as directed paths. This space is the
directed unit interval
.

Morphisms of directed spaces are defined as follows.

Definition 25
.

Let
\vec{X}=(X,dX)
and
\vec{Y}=(Y,dY)
be two directed spaces. A continuous function

is called a

, or

\forall\gamma\in dX,\quad f\circ\gamma\in dY

A dimap from the directed unit interval to a directed space

is called a

\vec{X}
are exactly its directed paths.

Directed spaces together with dimaps (and composition of functions) form the category of directed spaces. An isomorphism in this category is called a
dihomeomorphism
.

In a given directed space, the set of paths from one point to another is endowed with the compact-open topology.

Definition 26
.

Let
\vec{X}=(X,dX)
be a directed space and

.

The set of all directed paths

so that

from

Given a directed space whose points model the states of a program and whose directed paths model all the possible sequences of state transitions, the execution traces should be understood as paths
up to bijective increasing reparametrization

Grandis 2009
in the sense that the speed with which we transition through the states does not matter.

The relation

“being the same up to bijective increasing reparametrization” defined by

is indeed an equivalence relation.

.

Now note that, if

\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\otimes m_{i}\otimes b_{i}\;\in\;R[\mathcal{K}_{\vec{X}}]\otimes C_{n}(\mathcal{K}_{\vec{A}})\otimes R^{\prime}[\mathcal{K}_{\vec{X}}]

is said to be in
reduced form
if

1.

For every
i\in I
,
a_{i}=\lambda_{i}u_{i}
where
\lambda_{i}\in R
and
u_{i}
is a trace that cannot be factored by a non-constant trace of
A
on the right,
i.e. if
u_{i}=tt^{\prime}
with
t^{\prime}
a trace in
A
, then
t^{\prime}
is a constant trace

2.

For every
i\in I
,
b_{i}=\mu_{i}v_{i}
where
\mu_{i}\in R^{\prime}
and
v_{i}
is a trace that cannot be factored by a non-constant trace of
A
on the left,
i.e. if
v_{i}=t^{\prime}t
with
t^{\prime}
a trace in
A
, then
t^{\prime}
is a constant trace

3.

For every
i\in I
,
m_{i}
is a
n
-simplex in the trace space of
\vec{A}

Reduced forms will allow us to understand better the kernel of the transfer map for relative pairs.

Proposition 24
.

If
(\vec{A},\vec{X})
is a relative pair then every element has a reduced form.

Proof.

Every element of
R[\mathcal{K}_{\vec{X}}]\otimes C_{n}(\mathcal{K}_{\vec{A}})\otimes R^{\prime}[\mathcal{K}_{\vec{X}}]
is a linear combination of elementary tensors, so we only have to prove the decomposition for elementary tensors.

By trilinearity of
(u,x,v)\mapsto u\otimes x\otimes v
, we can reduce the problem into finding a reduced form of
t\otimes m\otimes t^{\prime}
where
m
is a trace simplex of
\vec{A}
and
t
,
t^{\prime}
are traces in
\vec{X}
such that
t\otimes m\otimes t^{\prime}\neq 0
.

Let us take a path
\gamma\in dX
associated to a trace
t=[\gamma]
. Then
\gamma^{-1}(A)
is a closed interval.

Moreover,
t\otimes m\otimes t^{\prime}\neq 0
so
t.m.t^{\prime}\neq 0
. Indeed, if
t.m.t^{\prime}
was null, then we would have either
t.m=0
or
m.t^{\prime}=0
. Consider the first case for example: denoting by
c
the constant trace at the beginning endpoint of
m
, we would have
t.c=0
and thus:

t\otimes m\otimes t^{\prime}=t\otimes c.m\otimes t^{\prime}=t.c\otimes m\otimes t^{\prime}=0

which is absurd.

Thus,
\gamma(1)=m(\cdot)(0)\in A
. Since
\gamma^{-1}(A)
is closed, and by closeness of
A
, the connected component of
1
in
\gamma^{-1}(A)
is a compact interval, and we will call its smallest point
s_{0}
. Splitting
\gamma
at
s_{0}
gives two new traces
t_{1},t_{2}
such that:

•

t=t_{1}t_{2}

•

t_{2}
is a trace in
\vec{A}
(because
\vec{A}
is closed)

•

t_{1}
cannot be factored on the right by a non-constant trace of
\vec{A}

We can write in the same way
t^{\prime}=t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}
, where
t^{\prime}_{2}
cannot be factored in
\vec{A}
on the left and
t^{\prime}_{1}\in dA
. Then:

t\otimes m\otimes t^{\prime}=(t_{1}t_{2})\otimes m\otimes(t_{1}^{\prime}t_{2}^{\prime})=t_{1}\otimes(t_{2}.m.t_{1}^{\prime})\otimes t_{2}^{\prime}

∎

We will now establish the unicity of reduced forms, starting with elementary tensors in reduced form before extending the result.

Lemma 25
.

If
a\otimes m\otimes b
and
a^{\prime}\otimes m^{\prime}\otimes b^{\prime}
are reduced forms in
R[\mathcal{K}_{\vec{X}}]\otimes C_{n}(\mathcal{K}_{\vec{A}})\otimes R^{\prime}[\mathcal{K}_{\vec{X}}]
, then:

a.m.b=a^{\prime}.m^{\prime}.b^{\prime}\text{ if and only if }\begin{cases}a=a^{\prime}\\
m=m^{\prime}\\
b=b^{\prime}\end{cases}

Proof.

For every
s\in\Delta_{n}
, there exists
\sigma(s)
that represents
m(s)
, and two dipaths
\gamma,\delta
such that
a.m.b(s)
is represented by
(\gamma\cdot\sigma(s))\cdot\delta
,
with
[\gamma]=a
and
[\delta]=b
.

In the same way, there also exists a path
\sigma^{\prime}(s)
representing
m^{\prime}(s)
and two dipaths
\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime}
so that
a^{\prime}.m^{\prime}.b^{\prime}(s)
is represented by
(\gamma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}(s))\cdot\delta^{\prime}
, with
[\gamma^{\prime}]=a
and
[\delta^{\prime}]=b
.

Since
a.m.b(s)=a^{\prime}.m^{\prime}.b^{\prime}(s)
, there exists a continuous increasing bijective function

f_{s}:[0,1]\to[0,1]

such that

(\gamma\cdot\sigma(s))\cdot\delta=\left((\gamma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}(s))\cdot\delta^{\prime}\right)\circ f_{s}

However, as in the proof of the existence of reduced forms,

\displaystyle\inf\left\{t\;\vline\;\delta(t)\in A\right\}=\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{t\;\vline\;\left((\gamma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}(s))\cdot\delta^{\prime}\right)\circ f_{s}\in A\right\}

\displaystyle=f_{s}^{-1}\left(\inf\left\{t\;\vline\;\left((\gamma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}(s))\cdot\delta^{\prime}\right)\in A\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=f_{s}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)

so we necessarily have
f_{s}(\frac{1}{2})=\frac{1}{2}
, and thus
f_{s}
induces a bijective reparametrization of
\delta^{\prime}
into
\delta
, which proves that
b=b^{\prime}
.

The same reasoning gives
a=a^{\prime}
, and that the restriction of
f_{s}
to
[f_{s}^{-1}(\frac{1}{4}),f_{s}^{-1}(\frac{1}{2})]
sends
\sigma^{\prime}(s)
to
\sigma(s)
, thus
m(s)=m^{\prime}(s)
for all
s\in\Delta_{n}
and thus
m=m^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark 17
.

In the case of precubical sets, only the case
n=0
requires conditions on relative pairs, as the bimodules of
n
-chains are free for
n\geq 1

Goubault 2024a
. This idea generalizes to our case.

Note that the separatingness of
\vec{A}
in
\vec{X}
is only needed in the case where the simplexes in the trace spaces may be constant. As such, in the case where
n\geq 1
, normalizing our chain complexes (i.e. quotienting out the degenerate simplices) would allow us to weaken the hypotheses considerably, as only the first homology bimodule would require special treatment.

More precisely, we believe that, for
n\geq 1
,
R[\mathcal{K}_{\vec{X}}]\otimes C_{n}(\mathcal{K}_{\vec{A}})\otimes R^{\prime}[\mathcal{K}_{\vec{X}}]
is the direct sum of

1.
The bimodule generated by the non-constant traces, which is free.

2.
The bimodule generated by the constant traces, which is likely projective under the hypotheses above, and which can be killed by normalizing singular chains before taking the homology.

Proposition 26
.

If
(\vec{A},\vec{X})
is a relative pair then the transfer map is injective.

Proof.

Let
m\in\ker\iota
. It is possible to write
m
as a finite sum of the form:

\sum_{i\in I}(\lambda_{i}t_{i})\otimes m_{i}\otimes(\mu_{i}t^{\prime}_{i})

where
m_{i}
is a trace simplex of
\vec{A}
,
t,t^{\prime}
are traces in
\vec{X}
,
\lambda_{i}\in R
,
\mu_{i}\in R^{\prime}
, and

\iota(m)=\sum_{i\in I}(\lambda_{i}.t_{i}).m_{i}.(\mu_{i}.t^{\prime}_{i})=0

This last equality happens in
C_{n}(\mathcal{K}_{\vec{X}})
, which is a free
R
-
R^{\prime}
-bimodule over simplexes of the trace space, so this equality is equivalent to the conjunction of its projections on all simplexes of the trace space of
\vec{X}
. As such, take some
i_{0}\in I
and consider

J=\left\{i\in I\;|\;t_{i}.m_{i}.t^{\prime}_{i}=t_{i_{0}}.m_{i_{0}}.t^{\prime}_{i_{0}}\right\}

Then

\sum_{j\in J}\lambda_{j}.(t_{j}.m_{j}.t^{\prime}_{j}).\mu_{j}=0

But then, for every
j\in J
,
t_{j}\otimes m_{j}\otimes t^{\prime}_{j}
has a reduced form
a_{j}\otimes n_{j}\otimes b_{j}
and

a_{j}.n_{j}.b_{j}=t_{j}.m_{j}.t^{\prime}_{j}=t_{i_{0}}.m_{i_{0}}.t^{\prime}_{i_{0}}=a_{i_{0}}.n_{i_{0}}.b_{i_{0}}

so, according to the previous lemma,

\displaystyle a_{j}

\displaystyle=a_{i_{0}}

\displaystyle n_{j}

\displaystyle=n_{i_{0}}

\displaystyle b_{j}

\displaystyle=b_{i_{0}}

and thus

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in J}(\lambda_{j}.t_{j})\otimes m_{j}\otimes(\mu_{j}t^{\prime}_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J}\lambda_{j}.(t_{j}\otimes m_{j}\otimes t^{\prime}_{j}).\mu_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J}\lambda_{j}.(a_{j}\otimes n_{j}\otimes b_{j}).\mu_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J}\lambda_{j}.(a_{i_{0}}\otimes n_{i_{0}}\otimes b_{i_{0}}).\mu_{j}

Then, since
C_{n}(\mathcal{K}_{\vec{X}})
is a free
R
-
R^{\prime}
-bimodule with basis the trace simplices, the sub-

-
R^{\prime}
-bimodule

R^{\prime}
-bimodule with basis
(a_{i_{0}}.n_{i_{0}}.b_{i_{0}})
. As such, there exists a unique
R
-
R^{\prime}
-bimodule morphism

f:R.(a_{i_{0}}.n_{i_{0}}.b_{i_{0}}).R^{\prime}\to R.(a_{i_{0}}\otimes n_{i_{0}}\otimes b_{i_{0}}).R^{\prime},\quad a_{i_{0}}.n_{i_{0}}.b_{i_{0}}\mapsto a_{i_{0}}\otimes n_{i_{0}}\otimes b_{i_{0}}

and then

\sum_{j\in J}\lambda_{j}.(a_{i_{0}}\otimes n_{i_{0}}\otimes b_{i_{0}}).\mu_{j}=f\left(\sum_{j\in J}\lambda_{j}.(a_{i_{0}}.n_{i_{0}}.b_{i_{0}}).\mu_{j}\right)=f(0)=0

Hence, for any

\sum_{j\in I,\;t_{j}.m_{j}.t_{j}=t_{i_{0}}.m_{i_{0}}.t^{\prime}_{i_{0}}}(\lambda_{j}.t_{j})\otimes m_{j}\otimes(t^{\prime}_{j}.\mu_{j})=0
