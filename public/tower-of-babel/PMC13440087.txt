Cultural and linguistic correlates of autism in Jordan
Authors: Arwa Nasir, Amira Masri, Laeth Nasir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by altered social communication and restricted and repetitive behaviors. Social communication is deeply shaped by culture, which also influences what is considered abnormal or disordered behavior. Although nearly 80% of the world’s children live in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), most autism research—including studies on epidemiology, diagnosis, and interventions—originates from Western, high-income countries. Consequently, there is limited knowledge about autism within other cultural and sociodemographic contexts, creating a barrier to developing culturally and linguistically appropriate diagnostic and intervention strategies suitable for various cultural contexts. Using semi-structured interviews, we collected data from Jordanian parents of children 2–5 years of age with autism regarding their children’s autism symptoms. We also examined parents’ knowledge and attitudes about autism and their responses to the diagnosis, as these factors affect symptom perception and help-seeking behaviors. We found significant differences in Jordanian parents’ perception, interpretation and reaction to symptoms compared to those seen in parents from the Western populations that reflect the unique cultural values of the community. These included more concern about social interaction than language development, more tolerance to variance in children’s behavior, and a tendency to interpret symptoms in positive terms. Parents were reluctant to accept a diagnostic label as it carried social stigma and sense of loss of potential. We also found unique linguistic features that were used to describe the symptoms that have not been described before, including terms used for the conceptualization of autism as well as symptom descriptions. Our findings provide insights into the unique cultural and linguistic aspects of autism symptoms in this Arabic-speaking, Middle Eastern culture. These findings may inform future autism research and support the development and adaptation of diagnostic tools and interventions for children with autism in Jordan and other culturally similar populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04947-y.

Lay summary

We interviewed Jordanian parents of young children with autism to learn how they perceive and describe their children’s symptoms. Jordanian parents noticed and interpreted symptoms differently than parents in the United States, Reflecting their cultural values, they were more concerned about social difficulties than language delays, more accepting of behavioral differences, and often described symptoms positively. Many were hesitant to accept an autism diagnosis due to social stigma. We also identified unique Arabic words for describing autism that haven’t been documented before. These findings will help develop better diagnostic tools and treatments for children with autism in Jordan and similar communities.

Introduction

Autism is the one of the most common developmental disorders worldwide, with a prevalence of 1–2% [49, 54]. Although 70% of children with autism have average or above-average cognitive ability, they often experience significant challenges due to impaired social communication and restricted, repetitive behaviors [35]. These behavioral characteristics, as defined by DSM-5 criteria, reflect a Western perspective, as most autism research is conducted in Western countries [10, 15, 16]. Since social communication and interaction are heavily shaped by culture [32], significant cultural differences exist in many aspects of verbal and nonverbal communication that likely impact the presentation of autism in culturally diverse populations [30]. Additionally, most autism diagnostic instruments were normed in primarily white populations [31]. Because autism diagnosis relies on behavioral observation rather than biological markers, the interpretation of autism-associated behaviors may vary significantly across cultures [26, 46].

Screening and diagnostic tools, developed primarily in high-income countries, have advanced research in autism etiology, diagnosis, and intervention. Although cultural influences on autism are well acknowledged, most autism screening and diagnostic research is centered in high-income countries and focuses predominantly on high-income, white populations [27, 37]. This bias may restrict the applicability of research findings to other cultural and resource-limited settings [12]. The absence of culturally and linguistically adapted diagnostic instruments continues to hinder research and clinical care, particularly in low-resource settings [20, 44]. Rates of autism diagnosis and access to services vary widely between countries and within countries among different racial and socioeconomic groups [11, 28, 50]. In addition to diagnostic limitations, cultural perceptions of developmental differences affect autism recognition and intervention availability, with symptom expression and significance varying across cultures—a phenomenon known as “cultural relativism” [13]. The limited existing evidence suggests variability across cultures in autism presentations and family beliefs about autism diagnosis [17, 18, 20].

In Arab countries, cultural values significantly influence perceptions of autism. Jordanian society, for instance, is a collectivist and patriarchal culture where family loyalty is paramount. Children, particularly sons, are viewed as sources of social status, with parents often identified by their firstborn male child’s name [29]. Children’s social behavior and success reflect directly on their parents. Mothers, who are typically solely responsible for child-rearing, may be blamed for any problems the child may have, including health and academic challenges [43]. A small but growing body of research on autism is emerging from Arab countries showing autism to be one of the most identified developmental concerns among pediatric patients [1, 2, 4, 8, 43]. Studies exploring sociocultural aspects of autism have focused on caregiver experiences and outcomes showing the high social and economic burdens of this condition [3]. A cross-sectional study from Lebanon showed 83% of subjects did not endorse stigma around the diagnosis. This may indicate shifting attitudes towards the diagnosis in some Arab societies coinciding with improved knowledge about autism [53]. A few studies examined social and communication behavior of Arab individuals with autism describing educational interventions with this population [3].

The autism research gap in Arab countries particularly hinders diagnosis and management. While the biological and psychological features and core symptoms of autism may be similar across cultures, significant variations in the identification and interpretation of symptoms, as well as reactions to the diagnosis such as help seeking behaviors, are highly variable in various cultural contexts. Primary research exploring these issues can facilitate the development of diagnostic tools and interventions to improve the care of children with autism globally.

This study aimed to explore autism presentation within Jordanian society from the perspective of parents and examine family attitudes toward autism diagnoses in their children. We hoped to gain insight into the cultural factors that influence the presentation and perceptions of autism in Jordanian society as a microcosm of the Arab, Middle Eastern culture.

Methods

We used semi-structured qualitative interviews with parents of children 2 through 5 years of age who had been diagnosed with autism in the past 12 months, regarding the developmental and behavioral manifestations that their children exhibited in the period leading up to the diagnosis [25]. The interview also explored their understanding of the diagnosis and the impact that the diagnosis had on them. Our focus on younger children with autism stems from the critical need to improve early diagnosis during the period of maximum brain plasticity when interventions are most effective in improving outcomes. We chose to interview parents within one year of diagnosis to minimize the time lapse between symptom recognition and diagnosis, ensuring that parents could accurately recall the symptoms while having had sufficient time to reflect on the diagnostic process.

An interview guide was developed by the investigators for the purposes of this research. (supplement). Clarifying and probe questions were asked as necessary. We recruited parents from the child neurology clinic at a university hospital in Jordan that serves as the main referral site for children with developmental and behavioral conditions from many regions of the country.

Using purposive sampling, we recruited 10 families from the child neurology clinic who had a child diagnosed with autism within the past year. Data collection and analysis were done concurrently, in which the investigators conducted thematic analysis after each interview using grounded theory methodology. Through ongoing team discussion and consensus, thematic saturation was determined to have been reached after the tenth interview [33].

The research was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of Declaration of Helsinki, and the research protocol was approved by the University of Nebraska and the University of Jordan Institutional Review Boards. Informed consent was obtained from the participants, who were parents of children with autism diagnosed in the past year, following a discussion of the study protocol by the investigator, as stipulated by the IRB regulations.

The diagnosis of autism was made using the standard of care utilized at the Jordan University Hospital at the time of diagnosis, which included a parent interview and unstructured clinical observation by a child neurologist. In a separate part of this research, we administered a modified version of the ADOS-2 to all these children, and they all scored in the range of clinical diagnosis of autism. The modification of the ADOS-2 included administration in Arabic, the native language of both the investigator conducting interviews and the families.

The interviews with the parents focused on eliciting the behavioral and developmental features their children exhibited before the diagnosis, as well as the parents' interpretations and reactions to the symptoms, including their help-seeking behaviors. The interviews were recorded, transcribed in Arabic, and subsequently translated into English by the primary investigator.

Community involvement statement: Parents of children with autism were not involved in the design of the research.

Positionality statement

The authors of this research are natives to Jordan, where the study took place, and have extensive experience in working in the Jordan, the US and France. This gives us the advantage of the native understanding of the culture of our patients as well as the cross-cultural perspective. Our interest in this subject is the result of our clinical experience in working with children with autism both in Jordan and the US as well as previous research showing the huge need for autism research in Jordan. It also stems from our deep understanding of and compassion with the plight of the parents and children with autism who struggle to find answers and help their children.

Data analysis

We used NVivo-12 to organize data and manage the coding process. We used grounded theory methodology to analyze the data [19, 42].

Initial coding: each of the three authors read the transcripts independently and assigned codes on the hard copy of the transcript. This was followed by a group meeting in which the transcripts were read aloud, and codes were inductively generated to identify the social and psychological processes, actions and thoughts offered by the interviewees. The investigators identified 27 categories that were as close to the data as possible and often used participants’ words to denote the codes. This was followed by the intermediate coding phase where through an iterative process, core categories were identified that extracted the abstract concepts from the initial 27 codes. Three categories, or themes, represented the abstract concepts gleaned from the initial codes. The properties of each category detailed the characteristics that are common to the concepts included in the categories. These themes overlapped with themes reported by DeLeeuw [24] and we used insights from her conceptual framework to organize the data. Therefore, our final coding, consisted of a blend of inductive and deductive elements that helped in the formulation of our conclusions.

Results

The children’s ages ranged from 2–4 years. The ratio of males to females was 4:1. The interviews lasted from 38–72 min. The interviewees included both parents (4), mother and maternal uncle (1), father and maternal grandmother (1), mother alone (3), father alone (1). The parents usually provided most of the information, the other interviewees contributed to the discussion in varying degrees. The interviews were conducted by the primary investigator with each individual family separately. The primary investigator had no previous relationship with any of the families. She was introduced to each family by their treating physician who is one of the coinvestigators and who recruited the families for this research.

The discussion followed the interview guide, with clarifying and follow up questions used as determined by the interviewer.

We organized our data into three interrelated levels of impact of culture on autism symptoms (Fig. 1). The first theme revolved around symptom recognition and interpretation, which in the context of reporting parent interview data, were inextricable from each other. The second theme pertains to the parental understanding of autism as a neurodevelopmental condition and diagnostic category, including their beliefs about the causes of autism. This overlapped with symptom recognition and reporting as described by DeLeeuw in which parents interpret the symptoms through the filter of their preexisting understanding of autism [24]. Symptom recognition and interpretation

Broad symptoms of autism including deficits in social communication and speech delay were observed in all children. However, social interaction and relatedness to others caused significantly greater concern for the parents than speech delay.“First thing we noticed about him was the eye contact and the response, he would not respond (M).” Speech delay did not seem to cause as much alarm and was not recognized by the family as representing a significant problem, except when associated with social interaction deficits or atypical behaviors. “His older brother was delayed [talking], he did not talk for 4 years. But he did not have the behaviors like [this one]. This is what made us suspicious and prompted us to go see a neurologist (F).”

But he did not have the behaviors like [this one]. This is what made us suspicious and prompted us to go see a neurologist (F).”

Most parents provided anecdotes of speech delay that had occurred in siblings, relatives, or themselves, where the delay was outgrown with age. “I was late talking and my father was late talking. I used to talk using signs. But like him, until I was 3–4 years, I did not talk, and my father is the same (F).” Another parent stated: “My kids naturally are late talking (M).”

Some parents initially normalized or minimized the importance of speech delay and interpreted it as timidity, stubbornness, or due to lack of exposure to other children and adults. “His language is good, but he does not like to talk. Does not like to interact. sometimes I think he is shy, other times I feel, he is just like that” (M). Another parent stated: “…but up till now, when you call him, sometimes he responds and others not. I think it is stubbornness or whatever you call it. I think he is stubborn(M)”. Some parents interpreted speech delay as part of the normal variation between children, exhibiting a greater tolerance to the variability: “… some days he ignores me, but that is normal.(M)”.

In two children with advanced speech skills, the parents noted that the children used many words and phrases in classical Arabic. Classical Arabic, or formal Arabic differs from colloquial conversational Arabic typically used in everyday life, and almost never by children.

Developmental regression was particularly troubling for parents as it implied ongoing losses and challenged their narrative of a benign delay. “He stopped saying our names and his siblings’ names.” “It’s like he stopped talking. It is unbelievable that he used to talk and then stopped talking!” (M).

Repetitive behaviors, although frequently noted by parents, were often interpreted through a cultural and familial lenses that sometimes differed from traditional clinical interpretations. A father stated: “They tell me he walks on his tiptoes and flaps a lot, but no, I don’t think he has that. Not continuously, sometimes, very little, when he wants to run, he does like this.”. Another mother commented: “[Flapping] …is not a sign of autism. Flapping indicates that he wants to express himself and talk. It is a natural movement”.

The lack of understanding of social boundaries was also alarming to parents. One father stated: “He likes to shake hands with men. Even when he was very small. Sometimes he would get us in trouble, we would be walking in the street and a man is walking towards us, he leaves my hand and runs to shake his hand. It is embarrassing. Some people are not accepting of this. And it is not appropriate to start with. Sometimes he goes and hugs complete strangers. Just like that”.

Here too, behaviors indicative of a lack of understanding of social relationships, such as a lack of sharing, were often normalized by parents, reframing these behaviors as attributes of strength, independence, and assertiveness, especially for boys.

While a few parents noted emerging food pickiness, they did not endorse it as a significant problem. They reported that they were able to manage to get the child to eat a variety of food with the family. “He eats well. He eats pasta and rice and salad, normal. At first, he would refuse but then he would get on and eat with us” (M).

Some parents reported they were able to reduce specific behaviors through behavioral modification and occasionally punishment, especially when the behaviors were clearly socially unacceptable, such as touching their genitals. A mother stated: “He also had a habit of biting his finger. When he was off the diapers, he would bite his finger and grab his genitals. I spanked him once or twice and he stopped”.

Some parents described behaviors such as the child refusing to use an unfamiliar bathroom and found it perplexing. “He will not go here [at the hospital], he only goes to the bathroom at our house” (M). Delay in toilet training was a source of concern and distress by parents because Jordanian society places a high value on early toilet training and considers it an achievement for both the child and the mother. Parents also described unusually strong interest in certain toys such as cars or construction toys or police and rescue. “When we go to a toy store, his attention only goes to cars. No other toys” (F).

Some also described preference to certain TV or YouTube programs that the child wanted to watch over and over.

Linguistic considerations in symptom reporting

Parents used Arabic terms and metaphors to describe the symptoms and behaviors that they observed in their children. These terms differed significantly from the terms usually used by physicians in Jordan or that appear in translated autism screening tests.

Examples of these included the use of the expression يكسر عينه translating literally to”breaking his eye”, meaning breaking or averting the gaze and not maintaining eye contact. In general use, this term is used to indicate shyness or shame. It differs considerably from the Arabic translation of the term for eye contact customarily used in screening tests for autism and by many clinicians to describe eye contact: التواصل البصري which back translates to “visual connection” a term which is not used in day-to-day conversation and does not have an immediately recognizable meaning for most people. Some parents used the English term “eye contact” to describe the phenomenon, likely because of the lack of a familiar Arabic term to describe it.

Another example was the use of the word شرود, literally meaning “escape” or “flight” and usually used in association with شرود الذهن or “flight of the mind” or absent mindedness. In everyday conversation, this term carries positive connotations and suggests deep thought or preoccupation with important matters. Parents used this term to describe their child’s lack of engagement with or responsiveness to the social environment. Another word used repeatedly by parents was يفصل which means “disconnect” or “separate”, used by the parents in the context of failure to respond or communicate with the social environment. Other linguistic observations include the use of the term autism التوحد. The root word in Arabic is (واحد) meaning ‘one’ ‘singular’ or ‘alone’. The word for autism in Arabic is translated directly from English, and although the word exists in Arabic, meaning ‘the state of being alone’ it is quite obscure and not typically used in the context of an illness or disorder. “Spectrum”, was another term frequently mentioned by families and seemed to generate a lot of confusion. The Arabic term for "spectrum" is طيف, which translates to "shadow" or "ghost," unlike the English meaning of "range." Families interpreted this as implying that their child's condition was not "real" autism but merely a shadow or silhouette of it, which seemed to contribute to their preference for referring to the diagnosis of “autism spectrum” instead of just “autism”.

Additionally, the Arabic word for “delay”, whether referring to developmental delay, language delay, or simply tardiness, does not carry the same negative connotations as it does in Western cultures. A rhyming Arabic proverb states, كل تأخيرة فيها خيرة which translates to “In every delay, there is advantage.” This proverb is commonly used to save face for someone who arrives late or used to reframe a setback positively, reflecting a broader cultural belief that one can always catch up without shame. In the context of autism, parents appeared to apply this cultural framework, viewing symptoms as temporary and amenable to improvement rather than as part of a static diagnosis. Consequently, diagnosis of “speech delay” or “developmental delay” seemed to carry less stigma than a diagnosis of autism and appeared more acceptable to parents, possibly because these implied the possibility of eventual progress. While parents did not explicitly articulate this preference, it was evident from their persistent use of the word “delay” and avoidance of the word “autism” when referring to their children.

Terms that parents used spontaneously reflected everyday conversational language. Some of the words they used had undertones that revealed aspects of their own beliefs or feelings about autism.

Understanding of autism

Most parents indicated that they had never heard of autism before or had never known anyone with this diagnosis “in all our area, we have never had anything like that [autism];” another parent stated: “It is true, (that) I am educated but frankly, I did not know about autism before” (F). Many parents were perplexed by the term "autism" and expressed confusion about what it meant. Almost all the parents expressed some degree of skepticism about the diagnosis. This was especially marked among those who said that they had received conflicting messages from professionals, family, and friends “My understanding of autism is that they are children who may, depending on your luck, your child may grow up liking school or he may not like it. Your child may accept toilet training or not, your child’s case may worsen or improve. Your child may accept the doctor to touch him or not. Your child may reach the stage which they tell you is the most important, of being able to talk, so you can understand them. If he does not talk you may not be able to understand him and will have a hard time with him. Things like that” (F).

Reactions to the diagnosis

Confusion and skepticism

Virtually all the parents interviewed, including those who were more accepting of the diagnosis and were fully engaged in seeking interventions for the treatment of autism also expressed some degree of ambivalence and skepticism about the diagnosis. “I used to hear about autism. A difficult thing. Isolated and does not interact with anyone. That’s what I knew about autism. I mean, not normal human. But after what I learned on the net and from doctors and such, I feel it is a behavioral thing. Behavioral modification. Some doctors say autism is degrees, but some people say autism is autism. Others say it is a spectrum, a light version. Some say the light spectrum are mild behaviors that will disappear. The severe spectrum is when one does not know his father or mother and is not toilet trained. Like, a retarded person. That’s what one knows. But my son, his condition is good. He needs behavioral interventions, interactions with other kids. I feel he does not understand what goes on around him. That is all for him”. (F)

Many expressed hopes or expectations that the child would “get better”, “recover” or “return to normal.” Many stated that there were already signs pointing in that direction. Several of the parents would admit that the child’s behaviors were “not normal” and simultaneously state that he or she was a “normal” child. The fact that the child occasionally exhibited typical behavior and social interaction or said something that showed insight, confused the parents, and increased their skepticism about the diagnosis. Other factors that seemed to add to the confusion were the often-conflicting messages that the parents had received from different professionals who had previously evaluated their child. Diagnoses provided to some of the parents by different professionals included autism, autism spectrum, a psychiatric disorder, developmental delay, speech delay, a normal variation in development, or that the child was “normal”. This further confused the parents and decreased their trust in any professional diagnosis. Another factor that seemed to contribute to the skepticism among some parents was that their perception of autism was one of severe behavioral and developmental delay. Parents seemed to believe their child would exhibit more noticeable impairment if they had autism. While most recognized some behaviors as clearly atypical, they often stated that they felt that the condition would be more apparent. Given the high social stakes of having a child with a disability, parents often clung to any hope that these symptoms were insignificant. Several parents shared stories of friends, relatives, or acquaintances who had been diagnosed with developmental problems, only to later see the child develop normally.

Resistance to the diagnosis

Resistance to diagnosis was common and manifested differently in different families. Some families expressed a clear and open rejection of the diagnosis by at least one parent. Others vacillated between acceptance and skepticism, admitting there may be a problem, but not sure that it was autism. Several of the families implied that they felt that a supernatural cause was a more plausible explanation than the likelihood of autism. A common dynamic observed was one parent advocating for professional intervention while the other verbally insisted the child did not have autism, though not actively opposing the interventions.

Parents also commonly minimized the symptoms or tried to explain them away. They tended to interpret the behaviors in ways that decreased their significance (he flaps only when he is excited, and that is normal for children) (M). Often the behaviors were either reframed as normal or as favorable personality traits. For example, a lack of responsiveness was interpreted as being temperamental or stubborn, decreased interaction with strangers was interpreted as shyness and lack of interest in other children was considered to be a sign of maturity. Social isolation was interpreted as self-sufficiency.

Stigma

Stigma was often cited as a significant reason for parental reluctance to consider a diagnosis or seek professional help. Many parents admitted to hiding the diagnosis from extended family and others who didn’t need to know. “My sister-in-law lives downstairs from me, I did not tell her. My husband himself did not tell anyone. He even has a brother in America that he talks to, he did not tell him. He said: I don’t want to tell him, so he does not feel I am asking for money to treat her (M). Another mother said: “My family knows, but our relationship has changed, they are sorry for me, interaction with me is just to show compassion, how is she?”.

In contrast, one parent openly criticized the 'culture of shame' and stigma that, in her view, prevailed in her in-laws' family, leading them to resist the diagnosis and interventions “My husband’s family has a culture of shame, they do not talk about these things.”(M)

Loss, grief, guilt, and worry about the future

Although expressions of loss and grief among parents were not prevalent, some parents expressed loss and devastation. “Everything in life is one heap and this is another heap” (F). Several parents expressed their concerns about the child’s future. Prominent among these concerns were that the child would grow up to be conspicuously different and be rejected by family and society. The mother of one of the female patients expressed deep concern that her daughter with autism would be rejected by her extended family, and the implications of this should the mother pass away “Who do I leave her with [when I die]? my other daughters will be married in their homes with their husbands, won’t tolerate her. You know our culture. The boys’ wives will say: I don’t need a handicapped person. Plainly. They are going to reject her” (M).

Feelings of guilt about perceived parenting failures such as allowing long exposure to screens and neglecting to provide a sufficiently interactive social environment was expressed by many parents. “This is a failure on our part. We did not give him the attention. Frankly, only food, drink, sleep, clean. The emotional environment at home was not helpful for talking and communicating”(F).

Shock, distress, and emotional turmoil were evident in most of the families. Some parents described their initial reaction to the diagnosis as shock صدمة, or like having been struck by lightning انصعقنا. One father said “we were destroyed تدمرنا by the news, it was very upsetting”. Another mother said: “You see, when they first told me, I was shocked. I will not hide it. I cried. But when I think to myself, and read and cry, I say, my lord, forgive me, but you either cure her or take her to you”.

Adjustment

Parents and families also expressed themes of resilience and adaptation, largely influenced by the spirituality rooted in Jordan's Muslim culture and tradition, which is prevalent among both Muslims and Christians. Central to this belief system is the acceptance of "God's will" as a core tenet of faith, with any defiance of God's judgment seen as unacceptable and counter to the spiritual rewards promised to those who submit to divine will. This perspective fosters a dignified acceptance of misfortune, distinct from fatalism, as it does not imply a sense of helplessness or hopelessness. Instead, it reflects a belief in being aligned with a divine plan, even if the reasons behind it are not fully understood “What gives me comfort is that in these cases, the earlier you start the interventions, the more you abbreviate the problem, especially that this is not a disease, it is a condition that one must deal with. At least I can get him to be stable and functioning in the community, I mean, what else can I do, it is God’s will, and one must try” (M).

Discussion

Our findings align with previous research demonstrating that communities develop their own cultural interpretations of developmental disorders like autism. This study further illustrates how family and community attitudes toward autism shape parental responses to their child's condition, influencing both the individual reaction to diagnosis and subsequent help-seeking behaviors.

Cultural variation in symptom recognition

Although the children in our Jordanian sample displayed the core symptoms of autism as defined in the DSM-5, parents demonstrated markedly different interpretations of how these symptoms manifested compared to parents in Western studies [23, 34]. In high-income Western countries, speech delay is typically the primary concern prompting families to seek evaluation; in contrast, Jordanian parents in our sample were more concerned by low social engagement, such as failure to respond to their name or diminished interest in interacting with family members. This pattern has been documented previously in studies of autism in India [21] and Iran [47] and may be attributable to the centrality of social relatedness in collectivist cultures.

Repetitive motor stereotypies were frequently interpreted as bad habits or intentional misbehavior and were typically addressed through behavioral management strategies rather than recognized as potential indicators of a developmental condition. Consistent with prior research documenting cross-cultural variation in sensory symptom profiles [17], our study found fewer reported sensory symptoms overall, including reduced rates of picky eating. This may reflect cultural norms in which food is regarded as sacred and waste is discouraged, as well as comparatively limited availability of highly processed foods with intensified taste and texture. Cross-national variation in picky eating has been documented in a European study on food neophobia [45] and a study from Japan reported no evidence of picky eating among young children, an observation attributed to that culture's strict food-related social norms [52].

Societal understanding and stigma

Our findings also shed light on broader societal perceptions of autism, which were frequently characterized by confusion and ambivalence. Families appeared to lack an adequate framework for conceptualizing autism as a neurodevelopmental condition, and features such as developmental regression or the intermittent presence of age-appropriate behaviors served to deepen this uncertainty. These findings are consistent with studies from the region indicating generally high levels of diagnostic awareness alongside substantially lower levels of perceived knowledge about the condition [6, 7, 48].

Parents in our sample expressed significant distress and shock upon receiving their child's diagnosis and identified stigma as one of the considerable challenges they faced, consistent with a growing body of regional literature [5, 9, 22, 40, 53]. Research from the region consistently demonstrates that an autism diagnosis is associated with adverse effects on parental quality of life and substantial parenting-related challenges [2, 5, 22], with one study finding that a significant proportion of parents met diagnostic criteria for PTSD [40]. Although the present study focused on symptom recognition and identification rather than parental mental health outcomes specifically, the distress and stigma parents described were consistent and prominent themes.

Language, terminology, and explanatory models

The Arabic terminology used by both laypeople and professionals to describe autism, often derived through direct translation from English, appeared to contribute to parental confusion. This was, in part manifested by the use of more familiar Arabic terms and metaphors describing and explaining the symptoms that they encountered in their children. These expressions are often markedly different from the descriptions offered by professionals. They reveal the cultural understanding and frameworks used to contextualize and comprehend these symptoms. Many of these terms mirror the cultural communication style seen in many collective societies [51]. A notable tendency emerged to frame symptoms in positive or attenuating terms, a discursive strategy that appeared to function both to reduce stigma and to preserve the dignity of the child and the family.

While biomedical explanations such as autism being a developmental disorder were accepted by many parents that we interviewed—likely because this sample was seeking treatment at a tertiary medical center—supernatural causes were frequently suggested, either openly or indirectly. These beliefs appeared to reflect more than a lack of knowledge; rather, biomedical, and supernatural explanations seemed to coexist in parents’ understanding of autism.

Parents’ beliefs about autism were influenced by cultural, religious, and supernatural factors, with some seeking help from traditional healers. In an earlier study, Masri et al. found that about one-third of Jordanian parents of children with autism had pursued alternative treatments alongside medical care [41]. Other studies have shown that diverse populations, including well-educated Western groups, often combine seemingly conflicting beliefs to construct unique explanations for illness and misfortune [14, 38]. This has similarly been described in a study from Kenya that found that 31% of a study sample of health care workers believed autism is caused by preternatural and supernatural causes.

Despite this conceptual confusion, the language parents employed often demonstrated the adaptability of Arabic in describing novel or unfamiliar phenomena.

Information-seeking behavior

The internet was identified as a primary source of autism-related information for parents in this study, with many reporting reliance on YouTube videos featuring physicians and behavioral specialists [39]. This preference likely reflects both the accessibility of digital content and a cultural orientation toward oral and face-to-face modes of communication that video is better positioned to approximate than written materials. One Arabic-speaking content creator cited frequently by parents provided guidance on strategies such as increasing one-on-one interaction with the child, which families reported finding helpful and which aligns with evidence-based management recommendations.

The quality of available Arabic-language online resources, however, remains an empirical question warranting further investigation. A study from Germany found that German-language websites providing health information about autism were generally rated as low quality [36]. No comparable assessment of Arabic-language resources currently exists. Given the extent of internet use in Arabic-speaking populations and the cultural suitability of video-based formats, digital platforms represent a significant and underutilized opportunity for disseminating accurate, culturally appropriate autism education.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The focus on young children means that findings are not generalizable to older individuals with autism who may therefore exhibit a distinct symptom profile. Additionally, the clinical sample drawn from a specialty clinic at a major urban academic hospital likely overrepresents urban, professionally employed, and more highly educated families with perhaps better access to early diagnosis. Future studies incorporating rural and more socioeconomically diverse populations will be important and may yield additional relevant findings.

Conclusions and implications for future research

Parental recognition and interpretation of autism symptoms reflect the cultural values, biases, and social norms of this population, situated within an evolving societal understanding of autism spectrum disorder. The Arabic language and terminology employed by parents actively shape how autism is framed, in ways that may either facilitate or impede acceptance and understanding. Incorporating these linguistic and cultural dimensions into the development of diagnostic instruments and intervention strategies has the potential to meaningfully improve their validity and effectiveness in Arabic-speaking settings.

The present study focused primarily on symptom recognition and interpretation as expressions of cultural background and illness conceptualization. These findings provide a foundation for further research into parental perceptions of intervention approaches, with a view toward developing strategies that are adapted to this cultural context. Comparable studies conducted in other Arab and Middle Eastern settings, as well as across diverse cultural contexts globally, would facilitate cross-cultural comparison and support identification of the cultural factors most consequential for symptom recognition, interpretation, and timely diagnosis.
