CTE has multiple pathologic variants that might relate to different clinical symptom presentation
Authors: Fatima Tuz-Zahra, Eli Hallowell, Chris Tilton, Victor E Alvarez, Nurgul Aytan, Samantha Hawkins, Jesse Mez, Michael Alosco, Ann C McKee, Yorghos Tripodis, Thor D Stein, Jonathan D Cherry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease found in individuals with a history of repetitive head injury (RHI) received through playing contact sports. Currently, CTE can only be diagnosed after death through the identification of perivascular neuronal hyperphosphorylated tau (pTau) pathology, found at the depth of the cortical sulcus. Although the pathognomonic lesion is found among all cases, pTau deposition is patchy and heterogeneous among individuals. To determine whether the heterogeneity of CTE masks more subtle ordering or additional disease variants, we applied the unsupervised machine learning algorithm subtype and stage inference (SuStaIn) on fully quantitative pTau density data from 26 brain regions in 207 CTE cases and 75 control cases. SuStaIn identified three distinct pathological progression subtypes: 93 cases were classified as Subtype 1 (cortical predominant) and showed dense, rapidly progressing cortical pTau akin to the classically described CTE neuropathology; Subtype 2 (cortical sparing–hippocampal enhanced) had 105 cases that exhibited a reduced cortical pTau burden that progressed slower, but also had pronounced hippocampal involvement in a CTE-specific pattern; and Subtype 3 (copathology altered) consisted of 28 older cases with higher CTE stage, was enriched for comorbid pathologies (pTDP43, Aβ, hippocampal sclerosis, and arteriolosclerosis), and had enhanced pTau in regions highly related to those additional pathologies. Clinically, impaired performance in the Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ) correlated with pTau regional burden and severity only in cases with Subtype 1 pathology, suggesting the enhanced cortical pTau pathology was linked to greater functional decline. Overall, these findings help characterize the heterogeneity in CTE progression, validate key pathological variants, and will be crucial for refining diagnostic criteria and advancing in-life diagnosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00401-026-03045-y.Keywords: Chronic traumatic encephalopathy, Machine learning, Neurodegeneration, pTau

Introduction

Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease found in individuals with a history of repetitive head injury (RHI) [5, 21]. CTE has been primarily observed in athletes with exposure to contact sports, including American football, soccer, hockey, boxing, or rugby [1, 19, 21]. Currently, CTE can only be diagnosed after death. To that end, there are significant ongoing efforts to diagnose CTE while individuals are still alive. However, CTE is a clinically and pathologically heterogeneous disease, introducing complexity in understanding the clinical syndrome and identifying diagnostic biomarkers [12, 23].

The neuropathologic lesion that defines CTE is the perivascular accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau (pTau) at the depth of the cortical sulcus [5, 18]. Neurons contain the majority of pTau in CTE; however, astrocytic pTau can also be observed, typically in older individuals, and may reflect age related rather than RHI-driven pathology [7]. CTE is staged using the McKee criteria based on the location and severity of pTau [5]. Briefly, Stage I is characterized by 1–2 isolated foci of perivascular pTau in the cortex. Stage II includes multiple larger epicenters in the frontal, parietal, or temporal cortex. In Stage III, there is hippocampal and medial temporal lobe involvement. Stage IV is characterized by brain wide spread of pTau pathology. In subsequent studies, these criteria were found to correlate well with semi-quantitative pTau burden measurements across regions [3]. However, although this staging system captures general neuropathologic progression of the disease, the patchy nature of CTE can make staging challenging, particularly in early stages when pathology is less dense and can vary in its distribution across regions.

While the dorsolateral frontal cortex (DLFC) is traditionally one of the first regions a lesion can be identified, lesions can also be first observed in other cortical areas as well. Furthermore, adding to the complexity of heterogeneous expression of pTau in CTE, different variants of CTE have recently been reported. One such variant that was recently described neuropathologically is “Cortical Sparing” (CS), which has a sparse burden of cortical tau despite involvement of typically higher stage brain regions like the medial temporal lobe [2]. However, given how recently CS CTE has been described, it is not yet part of the current consensus criteria. It is unclear what might drive these regional or burden related variants, or their functional impact; however, such neuropathological categorizations are common in neurodegenerative diseases. Different pathologic protein trajectories and presentation variants have been identified in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Lewy body disease (LBD), and fronto-temporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) [17, 35, 36, 38]. Identifying distinct pathologic progressions of CTE might provide important explanations for the overall disease heterogeneity, allow clinicians and researchers to better categorize features, and allow for subtype-specific analyses.

Taken together, it is vital that we understand the full spectrum of pathologic subtypes present within CTE. To accomplish this, we leveraged a diverse set of comprehensively characterized brain donors with CTE from the Boston University UNITE brain bank. A challenge for assessing the question of neuropathologic progression in a postmortem cohort is the lack of longitudinal data, as each case is essentially a snapshot in time. However, recent computational techniques have been developed to aggregate neuropathologic cases over a spectrum of pathologic stages to infer progression patterns. One such approach is subtype and stage inference (SuStaIn), an unsupervised machine learning algorithm that identifies subtypes and groups with common predicted disease progressions [37]. SuStaIn has been used in past studies to model both clinical imaging data and neuropathology to identify novel disease expression profiles [17, 35–38]. This approach is ideal for investigation of different subtypes of CTE. Therefore, using a quantitative measurement of total pTau pathology from 26 different brain regions, we applied the SuStaIn algorithm to identify CTE predicted progression subtypes. We also investigated whether subtypes were associated with demographic, genetic or RHI exposure factors, as well as with distinct clinical symptoms. Overall, this work aimed to clarify the heterogeneity in neuropathologic features of CTE and provide a framework for improved classification of disease variants.

Methods

Donors

Brain donors were used from the Boston University Understanding Neurologic Injury and Traumatic Encephalopathy (UNITE) Brain Bank. Neuropathologic evaluations were performed blinded to clinical information and adjudicated by board-certified neuropathologists (T.D.S, V.A., A.C.M). Evaluations included Thal stage, CERAD score, and Braak stage, pTDP43, α-synuclein, hippocampal sclerosis, and arteriolosclerosis [6, 20, 24, 25, 34]. To focus on the changes specific to CTE type pTau pathology that might be independent of the contributions from other comorbid diseases factors, donors with neuropathologically diagnosed Alzheimer’s disease (AD), motor neuron disease (MND), fronto-temporal lobar degeneration (FTLD), or neocortical Lewy body disease (LBD) were excluded from the study using established criteria [5, 15, 16, 18, 20, 24, 25, 31]. Cases with a CERAD score > 1 were also excluded [24]. A total of 207 cases with neuropathologically confirmed CTE were included in the analytic cohort. CTE cases were classified as low (n = 92) or high (n = 115) stage CTE based on recent consensus criteria [5, 18]. Additionally, 75 control cases were also included. These control cases had a history of RHI exposure, but did not have a diagnostic CTE lesion or any other comorbid neuropathologic disease. All included cases were male. Controls were included in this study to act as an anchor for biomarker distribution and a baseline reference, or a “zero” point while setting SuStaIn parameters. Therefore, only cases that had either none or extremely limited pTau pathology were selected. Institutional review board approval was obtained from Boston University School of Medicine, VA Boston Healthcare System, and VA Bedford Healthcare System. Written informed consent was obtained from the donor’s next of kin. To assess clinical symptoms and athletic histories, donor’s next of kin completed online questionnaires and were interviewed as previously described elsewhere [22, 23, 33]. The degree and duration of contact sports exposure was captured as total “years of RHI” which represented the number of years playing any contact sport. Additionally, sports-specific metrics were also captured. For the current study, in addition to the total years of RHI, subanalyses focusing on “years of football” were used to evaluate sports-specific changes. Digital pathology

Tissue isolation and staining: For every donor, tissue blocks were harvested from the following 18 tissue regions: olfactory bulb, superior frontal cortex (Brodmann area (BA) 8,9), dorsolateral frontal cortex (BA 45,46), inferior frontal cortex (BA 10,11,12), motor cortex (BA 4,3,2,1), inferior parietal cortex (BA 39,40), anterior temporal cortex (BA 38), superior temporal cortex (BA 20,21,22), insular cortex, calcarine cortex (BA 17,18), hippocampus at the level of the lateral geniculate, amygdala with entorhinal cortex (BA 28), caudate–putamen–accumbens, globus pallidus–substantia innominate, thalamus with mammillary body, cerebellum with dentate nucleus, upper pons at the level of the locus coeruleus, and pons with the substantia nigra. Tissue was processed, stained for pTau (Invitrogen, Clone AT8, Cat# MN120), amyloid beta (BioLegend, Clone 4G8, Cat# 800,701), pTDP43 (Cosmobio, polyclonal, Cat# TIP-PTD-P07), α-synuclein (Millipore, polyclonal, Cat# AB5038P), LHE (Luxol Fast Blue Cat# 212,171,000, Thermo Scientific; Hematoxylin 1 Cat# 7221, Epredia; Eosin-Y Cat# 7111, Epredia), and Bielschowsky silver stain (Cat# 93–4718, STREM Catalog) in select regions, and digitized as previously described [23]. Slides were digitized using an Aperio GT450 digital slide scanner [11].

Image processing: To quantify the burden of pTau pathology, the AT8 stained digital images were imported into Indica Laboratory’s HALO for quantification. Images were then subjected to region annotation and pTau quantification. Using a HALO AI algorithm, gray matter was automatically annotated using previously described methods [11]. For non-cortical structures such as the hippocampus, amygdala, striatum, globus pallidus, thalamus, cerebellum, and brain stem, annotations to regions of interest were drawn by hand by an expert observer and verified by a neuropathologist. After segmentation, there were 26 total tissue regions that were identified and segmented for each case. Those regions were gray matter from the superior frontal cortex, dorsolateral frontal cortex, inferior frontal cortex, motor cortex, inferior parietal cortex, anterior temporal cortex, superior temporal cortex, insular cortex, calcarine cortex, hippocampal CA1, CA2&3, and CA4, amygdala, entorhinal cortex, caudate, putamen, nucleus accumbens, globus pallidus, substantia innominata, thalamus, mammillary body, cerebral dentate, olfactory bulb, substantia nigra, and the locus coeruleus. After regions were annotated, an AI tool to count the number of AT8-positive cells was then used to assess overall AT8 density across all regions as previously described [11]. The final AT8 count was then standardized to the area of the annotation for a final density measurement AT8+ cells/mm2. To examine differences in regional changes, all frontal cortex regions were averaged together to create a “Frontal” pTau burden variable similar to previous studies [4]. Additionally, all hippocampal regions were averaged together to create a “hippocampal” pTau burden variable. Frontal pTau was then divided by hippocampal tau to create a ratio to examine for possible cortical-sparing variants of CTE [2]. Values over 1 suggested more pTau in the frontal cortex, while values under 1 suggested more pTau in the hippocampus. Last, a final pTau burden measure was created by averaging all the cortical AT8 density measures together to create a “Mean Cortical Tau” variable, which was used to further examine cortical-sparing (CS) CTE changes.

SuStaIn analyses

AT8+ cell density preprocessing: A square-root transform was applied to reduce AT8+ cell density skewness. Next, values were z-score transformed using the mean and standard deviation of the controls, such that the control population had a mean 0 and standard deviation 1. Normalizing density values to controls ensures that score changes represented predicted disease progression rather than normal variation. To ensure numerical stability and model convergence, z-scores were re-scaled to the range 0–10. As each region could demonstrate highly different ranges of pTau based on disease specificity (i.e., DLFC is highly affected and has a larger range of values while the calcarine cortex does not), re-scaling to a 0–10 range for each density value allowed easier comparison within the SuStaIn algorithm. This method did not change in distribution within each regional density value. Average pTau densities for each region and pathological group are present in Supplemental Table 1.

Running SuStaIn: SuStaIn was implemented as previously described [38]. All donors had complete region data across all measures. Control cases were included to be an anchor point and act as a baseline where predicted pathology would start. Because the SuStaIn piecewise linear z-score model requires pre-specified z-score limits to define predicted disease progression stages, we determined region-specific thresholds using quantiles [30th, 60th, and 90th percentile]. Thresholds were estimated separately for each region to account for different scales of density scores. If a density value did not reach the next severity level, the corresponding z-score threshold was set to 0, indicating that no further thresholds were needed. The maximum z-score reached at the end of the progression was set as the 97th percentile of the cohort. Within the SuStaIn framework, biomarker score uncertainty was assumed to be Gaussian around each input data point.

Models with 1 to 6 subtypes were fitted. The optimal number of subtypes was determined by comparing model likelihoods across the different subtype solutions using internal cross-validation. This process involved varying tests to train data splits for each subtype, with model generalizability evaluated using the test set. The cross-validation information criterion (CVIC) was then considered for each of the 6 models as a way to compensate for the tendency of SuStaIn to overfit by splitting into more subtypes. The Markov Chain Monte Carlo likelihood trace was also monitored through progression reordering iterations to ensure stability during optimization. Based on these metrics, a 3-subtype solution was determined optimal. A Subtype 0 (control) was also included as a quality-control step to verify correct subtype calling of samples with minimal or no pathology.

Following model fitting, each case was assigned a probability of belonging to each subtype and stage based on the likelihood of the observed AT8+ cell density data. Using the built-in functions in SuStaIn, each donor was assigned to a stage and subtype by selecting the highest probability subtype and stage. While subtypes represented different patterns of pTau burden, stages represent incremental increases in disease along that course. Lower stages have minimal pathology in only a few scattered regions, while higher stages have more pathology in more regions. Greater deposition in a given region or the appearance of pathology in a new region will result in an increase of stage. All analyses were performed with python 3.11.5.

Genotyping

Genotyping for TMEM106B rs3173615 variant and APOE alleles was performed as previously described [11]. Briefly, DNA was extracted from fresh-frozen tissue using Promega Maxwell RCS Tissue DNA Kit (AS1610). Real-time PCR (rtPCR) was used to determine genotypes. Three genotypes were assayed for TMEM106B: GG, GC, CC. For APOE, rtPCR was performed to identify single-nucleotide polymorphisms for rs429358 and rs7412 to identify APOE genotypes.

Statistics

Chi-square statistical tests were used to determine enrichment of copathologies, genotype differences, type of sport played, CS CTE, and cases under/over a frontal/hippocampal pTau ratio of 0.1 between each SuStaIn subtype. Linear regression analyses were performed to measure the correlation between each SuStaIn stage (measure of predicted disease progression within each subtype) and AT8 density in respective regions. To measure the overall burden of AT8, the ratio of pTau between the frontal and hippocampal regions, overall mean cortical tau burden, and demographic variables, one-way ANOVAs with a Tukey post-hoc test were used. ANCOVA was used to control for age at death when assessing pTau regional ratios. For demographic measures, statistics were only calculated comparing the 3 subtypes against each other and not including the control subtype. A receiver operator characteristic curve was used to assess possible cutoff points for the frontal/hippocampal pTau ratio ability to predict Subtype 1 or 2. Youden’s index was used to determine optimal values. Finally, multiple linear regression analyses were performed to measure the correlation between SuStaIn stages and FAQ score. Age at death was included in the regression to account for age-related changes.

Results

SuStaIn identifies 3 distinct CTE subtypes

Using 282 donors as the input for SuStaIn modeling, 3 distinct CTE subtypes were predicted. We then assigned each case into its predicted subtype and examined demographic details to determine factors related to subtype (Table 2). The majority of subjects were assigned to Subtypes 1 and 2 (70.5% of all samples), while only 10% of subjects were grouped in Subtype 3. When comparing across subtype, donors in Subtypes 1 and 2 had a similar age at death (54.96 vs 58.30 years old), while donors in Subtype 3 were significantly older than both (74.07 years old, p < 0.001). Additionally, 82.1% of the donors in Subtype 3 were diagnosed with high-stage CTE compared to 49.4% of Subtypes 1 and 43.8% of Subtype 2. As a metric of quality control and a test of possible error in the model, donors were also assigned to a control Subtype 0 that represented cases with no disease pathology. While the majority of assigned Subtype 0 donors originally came from the control group as expected, 10 cases with CTE were also assigned to the Subtype 0. These donors had an average of 28.9 ± 7.9 years old, and all were CTE stage I with very sparse pTau pathology. This highlights that although the model was generally consistent, and could correctly identify CTE pathology, there could be a small degree of error to differentiate very sparse pathology from background levels.

Regarding contact sport related measures, subjects in Subtype 3 had a slightly older age of first exposure to football (p = 0.036). However, there were no significant differences in total years of all types of RHI exposure (p = 0.091) or specifically to American football (p = 0.285) (Table 2). Comorbid protein aggregation might contribute to subtype prediction

Although cases that met criteria for significant neurodegenerative diseases including AD, neocortical LBD, FTLD, or MND were excluded from the analyses, comorbid pathologies such as pTDP43, Aβ, and brainstem LBD are often found in CTE and were allowed [26, 32]. Therefore, we examined whether the distribution of comorbid pathology influenced subtype prediction. Overall, we observed that cases in Subtype 3 were more likely to have diffuse cortical amyloid pathology (Thal Stage 1) (Fig. 1a), pTDP43 pathology (Fig. 1b), hippocampal sclerosis (Fig. 1c), or arteriolosclerosis (Fig. 1d). Only 12 (4.3%) donors were found to have Lewy bodies in the brainstem. There was also no significant difference between subtype distribution (p = 0.292).

Subtypes 1 and 2 pTau pattern replicated recently described CTE neuropathology

The overall pTau distribution suggested that Subtype 1 represented a more cortical predominant disease while Subtype 2 represented a more medial temporal predominant disease. This closely mirrors a recently described study identifying a “cortical sparing” type of CTE [2]. To better investigate whether our SuStaIn subtypes were in line with past work, we directly compared frontal pTau burden to hippocampal pTau burden within each case. The frontal/hippocampal pTau ratio was significantly elevated in Subtype 1 compared to Subtype 2 (Fig. 5a,b). To investigate if age of death contributed to differences in pTau ratio, we performed an analysis of covariance test. While age at death did have a significant effect on the model (p = 0.001), the differences and direction of effect between Subtypes 1 and 2 were still observed (p = 0.019). There was no significant change to Subtype 3. We then examined if cases that have been identified as “cortical sparing” (CS)-type CTE by neuropathological criteria based on semi-quantitative pTau pathology assessment were enriched in any of our subtypes. Out of the 282 cases, 100 cases meet criteria for CS CTE. When looking at the distribution of the 100 cases, Subtype 2 contained 50% of all CS cases while Subtypes 1 and 3 represented 24% and 26%, respectively (Fig. 5c). Interestingly, even in cases that were not called CS CTE, the mean cortical pTau density was significantly lower in Subtype 2 compared to Subtype 1 (Fig. 5d). Conversely, the hippocampal burden was significantly elevated in Subtype 2 with or without CS CTE, compared to all other subtypes (Fig. 5e).

As the data suggested that Subtype 2 was likely related to CS CTE, we then chose to focus on further characterizing what additional specific pathologic features made Subtype 1 and Subtype 2 distinct. Subtype 3 was not examined for these analyses due to the smaller sample size and overall general similarities to Subtype 1. Since the frontal vs hippocampal pTau ratio was observed to have a stark contrast between Subtypes 1 and 2 (Fig. 5b), we performed a ROC curve to determine if a specific ratio could be used as a cutoff value to help differentiate between subtypes (Supplemental Table 2). Interestingly, the greatest specificity and sensitivity was achieved at approximately 0.1 ratio, or more simply, a tenfold increase in the hippocampal pTau relative to the frontal cortex pTau. Using 0.1 as a cutoff, we then investigated how well this score could divide high-stage CTE cases (Table 3). The 0.1 cutoff value significantly segregated subtypes (p < 0.001), suggesting that tenfold more pTau density in the hippocampus relative to the cortex could successfully identify cases in Subtype 2 in high-stage CTE with minimal error.

Different subtypes have different clinical symptom presentations

Finally, we wanted to examine if the difference in pTau distribution patterns might be related to clinical symptoms (Table 4). FAQ score, a measure of daily living activities, correlated with SuStaIn stages in subjects from Subtype 1 only. Although a non-significant trend was observed in Subtype 2, the findings suggest Subtype 1 had the strongest correlation between pathologic progression and FAQ-specific clinical symptoms. Age at death was included in all regression models to help rule out age as a cofactor. However, to further determine if the cognitive changes were associated with age, donors were binned into an over 50-year-old and under 50-year-old group, and reanalyzed for FAQ score correlation. Similar to the main analysis, only the Subtype 1 over 50-year-old group demonstrated a correlation between FAQ and SuStaIn Stage. Interestingly, a non-significant trend was also observed in the Subtype 1 under 50-year-old group as well, suggesting these cognitive features might better correlate to the specific Subtype 1 pathological features (i.e., cortical predominant pathology) as opposed to aging. No additional changes were observed when age of symptom onset was added to the model (Supplemental Table 3).

Discussion

Using unbiased data-driven computational modeling, we demonstrated that there are at least 3 distinct subtypes of CTE pathologic progression: Subtype 1 displayed a greater overall cortical pTau density and enhanced rate of progression. Thus, it could be referred to as a “cortical predominant” form of CTE; Subtype 2 had overall less cortical pTau and slower progression, akin to the previously described cortical-sparing variant of CTE. However, upon deeper characterization, Subtype 2 also had enhanced hippocampal pTau pathology relative to the other subtypes. The best predictor of Subtype 2 was found to be a ratio of 1:10 for frontal cortex pTau vs hippocampal pTau. While Subtype 2 was predominantly enriched with CS CTE cases, we chose to further clarify this variant by referring to it as “cortical-sparing–hippocampal-enhanced” CTE. Finally, while only accounting for ~ 10% of the cases, Subtype 3 displayed an enrichment of copathologies like TDP43 and Aβ. Interestingly, there was also enhanced pTau pathology and progression in areas where those copathologic features are commonly found, suggesting the concurrent pathology could possibly enhance local pTau deposition. Subtype 3 can be referred to as a “copathology altered” form of CTE. However, Subtype 3 was also enriched for older donors, so there is likely an age component driving this phenotype as well. Interestingly, when examining what factors might contribute to the subtypes difference, with the exception of Subtype 3 having a slightly older age of first exposure (which is difficult to contextualize given the much smaller sample size), athletic history or genetics variables were not different between each subtype. Finally, we demonstrated that the heterogeneous regional pTau differences might also correlate with clinical symptoms as well.

A key finding of the current study was the additional validation of CS CTE as a possible distinct progression pattern for CTE. Although not yet part of the CTE consensus criteria, recent work had identified a possible distinct form of high-stage CTE and defined CS CTE based on a qualitatively observed pattern [2]. Using semi-quantitative neuropathologist scoring, where no pTau pathology = 0, mild = 1, moderate = 2, and severe = 3, CS CTE was defined as cases with high-stage CTE with an average cortical pTau burden score less than 1. Here, we have identified a subset of cases characterized by a reduced cortical pTau burden, replicating those previous qualitative and semi-quantitative assessments with fully unbiased pTau quantitation and machine learning. The added sensitivity of fully quantitative assessments likely attributes to the findings that not all cases in Subtype 2 were called CS CTE by neuropathologic criteria. However, while most of the CS CTE cases were found in Subtype 2, a few could also be observed in the other subtypes. This suggests that even within CS CTE, there might be further variants. To that end, the largest driving feature for Subtype 2 was the hippocampal enhancement of pTau, even when compared among all CS CTE cases (Fig. 5e). To signify that Subtype 2 was a type of cortical-sparing CTE, but to also note the elevated hippocampal involvement, we chose to term this group ‘cortical-sparing–hippocampal-enhanced’ CTE. This distinction was chosen to avoid conflating all of Subtype 2 with other possible forms of CS CTE, such as those where there might be cortical sparing in high-stage CTE without elevated hippocampal pathology (i.e., the few other CS CTE cases found in Subtypes 1 and 3). The hippocampal involvement relative to the cortical burden was so pronounced that ROC curve analyses suggested a 1:10 ratio of frontal to hippocampal pathology could be a thresholding method to help discriminate and identify new cases in a clinical setting. Utilizing this threshold would allow labs that have access to quantitative image analyses tools, but not complex machine learning systems, to identify this subtype. However, this will need to be further validated and expanded in a separate cohort.

Another interpretation of the Subtype 2 phenotype is that the observed pathology represents the intersection of early CTE changes (affecting the cortex) and early AD-like changes (affecting the hippocampus) [6]. Although this is possible and would be challenging to untangle, many of these cases do not have amyloid, suggesting they are not AD related. Additionally, the early hippocampal changes could also be attributed to primary age-related tauopathy (PART). However, these cases are generally younger compared to when PART would typically be observed [9]. Additionally, the higher degree of hippocampal CA4 involvement also points towards more CTE-specific pTau patterns [10]. Therefore, it is more likely that Subtype 2 hippocampal-related changes were part of the CTE pathologic spectrum.

The observed difference in cortical pTau burden between Subtypes 1 and 2 also might relate to functional impairment as well. When assessing differences in cognitive symptoms with FAQ score, Subtype 1 demonstrated more severe impairments compared to Subtype 2, highlighting that the observed cortical burden differences have possible connections to overall functional impairment in activities of daily living, such as bill paying, remembering appointments, or traveling [28]. Future work using more memory-specific tests like the mini-mental state examination or Montreal cognitive assessment would be useful to determine if the enhanced MTL pathology better correlates with Subtype 2. Taken together, these findings further suggest that Subtype 1 and Subtype 2 might represent two distinct types of CTE, neuropathologically and clinically. However, a more nuanced, higher-powered study will be needed to untangle possible confounding factors that might drive this mechanism. Additionally, although APOE and TMEM106B did not show any enrichment, they may have small effects with a larger sample size, and other genetic risk factors may be involved as well.

The last subtype of interest was Subtype 3, which appeared to be a CTE variant characterized by the presence of comorbid pathology, a common feature in all neuropathologies [30]. As the initial aim of this study was to examine specific variants of CTE, we restricted our cohort to remove cases that carried a neuropathologic diagnosis of AD, FTLD, neocortical LBD, or MND in order to focus on the spectrum of CTE-specific pTau. However, this exclusion criteria did not remove cases that had some comorbid pathology, but not enough to meet a diagnostic threshold. Approximately 50% of CTE cases will have some degree of beta-amyloid deposits and ~ 43% have pTDP43 inclusions [26, 32]. Interestingly, SuStaIn appeared to identify these cases and grouped them into Subtype 3, which had a higher Thal score, more pTDP43, hippocampal sclerosis, and even a higher degree of arteriolosclerosis. As the only variable included in SuStaIn was pTau, it is revealing, and critically important, that the presence of copathology influenced pTau deposition patterns. This was highlighted in areas like the caudate and putamen, regions where pTDP43 pathology in ALS and FTLD can be commonly found, having higher pTau densities [29]. Elevated Aβ can also be found in these regions as well in CTE. Furthermore, two common neuronal projection pathways from the caudate and putamen are the globus pallidus and substantia nigra [13], both of which also demonstrated enhanced pTau in Subtype 3. While the mechanisms behind these increases are unclear, it is possible that regional changes like increased comorbid protein aggregates, age-related effects, or even RHI-driven proteasome dysfunction [27] might have enhanced the degree of neuroinflammation/neurodegeneration which has been shown to influence further pTau deposition [8]. Finally, while we did observe a correlation between SuStaIn stage and FAQ score, the lower Subtype 3 sample size prevented additional comparisons between specific neuropathologic comorbid features and clinical symptoms. Future studies that include a higher number of cases with diverse pathologic features will be needed to better assess the contribution of comorbid pathology to clinical symptom differences.

However, an alternative explanation for Subtype 3 could be attributed to the higher average age of the group. Since Subtype 3 was on average ~ 15 years older than Subtype 1 or 2, it is possible that this comorbid pathology phenotype could also be partly driven by age. Additionally, due to the lack of longitudinal data, it is also possible that donors in Subtypes 1 and 2 might converge on Subtype 3 given enough time. However, no matter the root cause, these results highlight that donors with copathology have a different profile of pTau deposition compared to donors that do not, which is a crucial factor when it comes to in-life biomarker and imaging studies. Overall, a follow-up study that includes a larger sample size of cases with diverse comorbid pathology will be needed to examine how these added features contribute to CTE variants.

There are several limitations associated with this type of study that should be carefully considered. First, donated brain samples do not represent the full spectrum of pathologic changes that might be observed in the general population. However, when possible, bias is taken into account and controlled for during analyses as highlighted in past work [14]. Next, SuStaIn is a computational algorithm that improves with larger sample sizes. Therefore, while the results are significant and in line with past observations, it is likely that larger sample sizes could help identify more subtle variants and improve some of the variations observed in the findings. Additionally, SuStaIn relies on probabilistic calculations for stage and subtype calculations limited to the samples available for analyses. Therefore, it is possible, and even likely, that there are more CTE subtypes that are not identified here. In addition to the larger sample sizes, additional studies that include all types of comorbid pathology are necessary to understand the full spectrum of disease phenotypes and clinical symptoms. Additionally, the AT8+ cell counting algorithm used in this study did not distinguish between neuronal and astrocytic pTau. As aging related tau astrogliopathy (ARTAG) can also be found in older cases with CTE [5, 7], future algorithms to distinguish between neuronal and astrocytic pTau will be needed to better identify cell type-specific changes. Furthermore, although SuStaIn was designed to predict progression profiles for neurodegeneration, the individual data points used in this study were derived from single timepoint for each case. Longitudinal imaging data obtained during life would be required to definitively identify true progression subtypes. Finally, given these observations come from a single brain bank, there will need to be validation using samples from additional centers to determine how consistent and replicable the overall findings might be.

Overall, using the machine learning tool SuStaIn, we have demonstrated that there are at least 3 different subtypes of CTE neuropathology, cortical predominant (classical CTE); cortical-sparing–hippocampal-enhanced CTE; and comorbid pathology altered CTE. These findings, using unbiased quantitative pTau measurements and unsupervised machine learning, provide additional validation on recently described CTE pathologic variants, and help better characterize the disease heterogeneity found in CTE. By improving and better categorizing CTE neuropathology, we expect to have better resolution to increase our ability to diagnose CTE in life and treat clinical symptoms. Future work with a more robust and diverse sample set will be critical to build off these initial findings and help further explore CTE pathogenesis.
