Development and validation of a diagnostic model for mild cognitive impairment in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and concomitant white matter hyperintensities
Authors: Bo Wang, Runzhou Pan, Qiuyue Yan, Yan Yao, Yan Wang, Xuechun Li, Meng Li, Yongcai Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo construct and validate a nomogram model that effectively discriminates mild cognitive impairment (MCI) from normal cognition in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and concomitant white matter hyperintensities (WMH).MethodsThis single‑center cross-sectional study included 1,175 consecutive T2DM patients with WMH from Cangzhou Central Hospital (January 2020 – December 2025), randomly assigned to a training cohort (n = 823) and a validation cohort (n = 352) in a 7:3 ratio. LASSO regression and multivariable logistic regression with four adjustment models were used to select associated variables. A nomogram was constructed and evaluated by receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC), calibration plots, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsEight independent associated variables were identified: C-peptide, HbA1c, mean amplitude of glycemic excursions (MAGE), coefficient of variation (CV), time in range (TIR), Fazekas scale, weight-adjusted waist index (WWI), and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR). The nomogram showed good discrimination in the training cohort [area under the curve (AUC) = 0.813, 95%CI 0.782–0.843] and validation cohort (AUC = 0.773, 95%CI 0.720–0.827). Calibration was excellent (training: intercept 0.00, slope 1.00; Hosmer-Lemeshow P = 0.317; validation: intercept 0.13, slope 0.90; P = 0.432). DCA confirmed clinical net benefit.ConclusionThis novel nomogram, incorporating glycemic variability, central obesity, insulin resistance, renal-vascular injury, and WMH burden, accurately discriminates the presence of MCI in T2DM patients with WMH, offering a practical tool for early risk stratification and intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05050-w.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, White matter hyperintensities, Mild cognitive impairment, Glycemic variability, Nomogram, Diagnostic model

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is an escalating global public health crisis. The number of affected individuals is projected to surge from 537 million in 2021 to 783 million by 2045, with T2DM accounting for over 90% of all diabetes cases [1]. While traditionally associated with macro- and microvascular complications, the deleterious effects of T2DM on the central nervous system are increasingly recognized. Epidemiological data indicate a 1.5‑ to 2‑fold increased risk of cognitive impairment in patients with T2DM. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI), a prodromal stage between physiological aging and dementia, is highly prevalent in this population. MCI not only severely impairs quality of life but also strongly predicts progression to Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias. Thus, early stratification and intervention for MCI in T2DM patients are critical to mitigate cognitive decline [2, 3]. The pathophysiology of diabetes-associated cognitive dysfunction (DACD) is multifactorial, involving insulin resistance (IR), chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and vascular injury [4, 5]. Among these, cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) is posited as a key pathological link bridging metabolic dysregulation and cognitive impairment [6, 7]. According to the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Vascular Cognitive Impairment in China (2024 Edition), cognitive impairment attributable to CSVD represents a major subtype of vascular cognitive impairment, characterized by neuroimaging hallmarks such as white matter hyperintensities (WMH) and lacunar infarcts [8]. WMH, the most ubiquitous manifestation of CSVD, are more prevalent in T2DM patients and significantly correlate with impairments in executive function, processing speed, and memory [9, 10].

Currently, existing diagnostic models for MCI in patients with T2DM mainly focus on traditional variables such as age, smoking, physical activity, social support, depression, living alone, glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), educational level, systolic blood pressure, and duration of T2DM [11–14]. These models neither deeply explore the pathophysiological mechanisms of DACD, nor has any study specifically constructed a diagnostic model for the special population of T2DM patients with concomitant WMH. To fill this gap, this study aimed to develop and validate a visual nomogram model for discriminating the presence of MCI in patients with T2DM and concomitant WMH, thereby providing evidence-based support for risk stratification and early intervention of DACD.

Methods

Study design and participants

In this single‑center cross-sectional study, a total of 1,175 consecutive patients with T2DM and concomitant WMH, treated at the Cangzhou Central Hospital from January 2020 to December 2025, were enrolled and randomly assigned to a training cohort (n = 823) or a validation cohort (n = 352) in a 7:3 ratio. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) Meeting the diagnostic criteria for T2DM as defined by the World Health Organization in 1999 [15]; (3) Presence of WMH on cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); (4) Availability of complete clinical data and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data; (5) Possessing assessable cognitive function evaluation results. The exclusion criteria comprised: (1) Type 1 diabetes mellitus, specific types of diabetes, or gestational diabetes mellitus; (2) Other neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, or frontotemporal dementia; (3) History of severe head trauma or intracranial surgery; (4) Intracranial space-occupying lesions, central nervous system infections, or demyelinating diseases; (5) Severe psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia, major depression); (6) Severe dysfunction of the heart, liver, or kidneys, or thyroid abnormalities, or malignant tumors; (7) Invalid or missing CGM data; (8) Contraindications for MRI examinations (such as the presence of a pacemaker, automatic defibrillator, or claustrophobia); (9) Incomplete clinical data.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Cangzhou Central Hospital (Ethics Approval No. 2025-006-01). All procedures performed in this study involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. As this study was retrospective in nature and all data were anonymized, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of Cangzhou Central Hospital.

Exposure variables

Basic clinical data were extracted from the electronic medical record system, including age, sex, C-peptide, HbA1c, total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], homocysteine (Hcy), C-reactive protein (CRP), creatinine (Cr), uric acid (UA), cystatin-C (Cys-C), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), albumin (ALB), total bilirubin (TBIL), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), hemoglobin (Hb), red blood cell distribution width (RDW), platelet count (PLT), fibrinogen (FIB), lymphocyte count (LYM), monocyte count (MONO), and neutrophil count (NEU), body mass index (BMI) = weight/height² (kg/m2), urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR, mg/mmol), and weight-adjusted waist circumference index (WWI) = waist circumference/√weight (cm/√kg), and years of diabetes duration. Smoking history (≥ 1 cigarette/day for ≥ 6 months), alcohol consumption (≥ 50 g/day for ≥ 6 months), years of education, coronary heart disease (prior myocardial infarction, angina, or revascularization), atrial fibrillation (by electrocardiogram/Holter), and stroke (ischemic/hemorrhagic) were defined according to standard criteria. According to the Clinical practice guideline for the management of hypertension in China, hypertension was defined as office systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg [16].

All patients underwent CGM within 48 h of admission for at least 72 h. For patients with multiple hospitalizations during the study period, only the CGM data from the first admission that met the inclusion criteria were used as baseline assessment. Repeated CGM measurements from subsequent hospitalizations were not included to avoid potential bias from disease progression or treatment changes. The 72-hour CGM period strictly represented the baseline hospitalization for each patient, initiated within 48 h of admission to ensure that the recorded glycemic variability reflected the patient’s usual metabolic status prior to any substantial in-hospital glucose management adjustments. The following glycemic parameters were assessed: HbA1c, mean amplitude of glycemic excursions (MAGE, calculated from fluctuations exceeding 1 SD), coefficient of variation (CV = SD/mean × 100%), mean of daily differences (MODD), time in range (TIR, 3.9–10.0 mmol/L), time above range of 10.0 mmol/L (TAR), time below range of 3.9 mmol/L (TBR).

The Fazekas scale is a visual rating system used to assess the severity of WMH on MRI (T2-weighted or FLAIR sequences). It consists of two subscores: Periventricular hyperintensities (PVH): 0 = none; 1 = caps or pencil‑thin lining; 2 = smooth halo; 3 = irregular lesions extending into the deep white matter. Deep WMH (DWMH): 0 = none; 1 = punctate foci; 2 = beginning confluent foci; 3 = large confluent areas. Overall WMH severity classification was based on the combination of PVH and DWMH subscores: Mild: PVH = 1 and/or DWMH = 1; Moderate: PVH = 2 and/or DWMH = 2; Severe: PVH = 3 and/or DWMH = 3 [17, 18].

Definition of MCI

Cognitive assessment was conducted using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). The MoCA is a standardized 30‑point screening instrument originally developed by Nasreddine et al. to assist frontline physicians in the detection of MCI [19]. The complete test takes approximately 10 min and assesses seven core cognitive domains, including visuospatial/executive function, naming, attention, language, abstraction, delayed recall (short‑term memory), and orientation, with a total possible score of 30 points. In validation studies, using a cutoff score of 26, the MoCA demonstrated high sensitivity (90%) and specificity (87%) for discriminating MCI from normal aging in the original cohort [19]. In Chinese populations, the Chinese version of the MoCA (MoCA-C) has been validated and showed 92% sensitivity and 85% specificity for MCI screening, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.930 (95%CI: 0.894–0.965) [20]. Based on evidence from Chinese populations, a MoCA score between 18 and 25 can be defined as MCI [21]. This study adopted this criterion for MCI diagnosis. Patients with a MoCA score of 26 or higher were classified as having normal cognition. All assessments were conducted by trained neuropsychologists in a quiet, well-lit room, with standard testing materials (pencil, printed MoCA forms) and instructions strictly following the official administration manual. The MoCA scores were recorded and subsequently used for group classification.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 30.0 and R 4.3.1 software. Continuous variables that were normally distributed were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), and comparisons between groups were performed using independent sample t-tests. For variables that did not follow a normal distribution, data were expressed as median (interquartile range) [Median (Q1, Q3)], and comparisons between groups were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were represented as frequency (percentage) [n (%)] and were compared using the χ² test. LASSO regression was used to select the optimal variables associated with MCI. To assess potential multicollinearity among the candidate variables selected by LASSO regression, we calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF) for each discriminator. A VIF > 5 was considered indicative of problematic collinearity. Additionally, we generated a correlation heatmap to examine the pairwise correlations among the selected variables. Subsequently, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to analyze the independent associations between each variable and MCI, with four adjustment strategies: Model I was unadjusted; Model II adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and years of education; Model III additionally adjusted for hypertension, atrial fibrillation, coronary heart disease, stroke history, smoking, alcohol consumption, and duration of diabetes based on Model II; and Model IV further adjusted for TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, Lp(a), Hcy, CRP, Cr, UA, CysC, ALT, AST, ALB, TBIL, ALP, Hb, RDW, PLT, FIB, LYM, MONO, and NEU based on Model III. A nomogram was developed based on the selected independent variables. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to assess model calibration. The discriminative ability of the model for identifying MCI was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, with the area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated. A calibration plot was generated to evaluate the agreement between discriminative ability of the nomogram and observed outcomes. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was employed to assess the clinical utility of the model. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients in the training and validation cohorts

A total of 1,428 consecutive patients with T2DM and concomitant WMH were initially identified from the electronic medical record system between January 2020 and December 2025. After excluding 253 patients due to incomplete clinical data (n = 139), invalid or missing CGM data (n = 72), or contraindications to MRI (n = 42), 1,175 patients were ultimately enrolled in this study. The eligible patients were then randomly allocated into a training cohort (n = 823) and a validation cohort (n = 352) in a 7:3 ratio. The majority of characteristics, including age, sex, diabetes duration, clinical history (hypertension, atrial fibrillation, coronary heart disease, stroke, smoking, and alcohol consumption), anthropometric indices (BMI, WWI), imaging severity (Fazekas scale), glycemic parameters (HbA1c, C-peptide, and various dynamic glucose metrics), or laboratory parameters (complete blood count, biochemistry, lipid profiles, liver and renal function, and fibrinogen levels) showed no statistically significant differences between the two groups (all p > 0.05). Minor statistical differences were noted only in years of education (median: 8.00 years in the training cohort vs. 7.00 years in the validation cohort, p = 0.018) and LYM count (median:2.10 × 109/L in the training cohort vs. 1.90 × 109/L in the validation cohort, p = 0.008). Furthermore, there were no significant differences in the MoCA scores and the prevalence of MCI between the two groups (both p>0.05) (Table 1).

Baseline characteristics of patients in the MCI group and non-MCI group in the training cohort

In the training cohort, clinical characteristics were compared between the MCI Group and non-MCI Group. Compared with the non-MCI group, the MCI group had a significantly higher WWI (9.60 ± 1.19 vs. 9.38 ± 0.99 cm/√kg, P = 0.006), a higher median Fazekas scale (4.00 vs. 3.00, P < 0.001), and a larger proportion of severe WMH (35.8% vs. 5.8%, P < 0.001). Regarding glucose-related parameters, the MCI group showed significantly higher median HbA1c (8.00% vs. 7.40%, P < 0.001), MAGE (4.52 vs. 3.96 mmol/L, P < 0.001), CV (36.85% vs. 32.31%, P < 0.001), and mean TAR (36.90% ± 10.51% vs. 34.14% ± 11.77%, P < 0.001), along with significantly lower mean TIR (60.44% ± 10.42% vs. 63.28% ± 11.74%, P < 0.001). For other metabolic and laboratory indices, the MCI group had significantly higher median C-peptide (2.76 vs. 2.36 ng/ml, P = 0.003) and UACR (22.31 vs. 19.58 mg/mmol, P < 0.001), lower median PLT (231.00 × 10⁹/L vs. 239.00 × 10⁹/L, P = 0.019), higher median TG (1.82 vs. 1.67 mmol/L, P = 0.049), and lower median HDL-C (1.10 vs. 1.17 mmol/L, P = 0.008). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in age, sex, history of hypertension, atrial fibrillation, coronary heart disease, stroke, diabetes duration, years of education, LYM, Hcy, TC, LDL-C, liver function, renal function, or FIB (all P > 0.05) (Table 2).

Screening for associated variables

A total of 44 candidate variables were included in the analysis: age, sex, C‑peptide, HbA1c, TC, TG, LDL‑C, HDL‑C, Lp(a), Hcy, CRP, Cr, UA, Cys‑C, ALT, AST, ALB, TBIL, ALP, Hb, RDW, PLT, FIB, LYM, MONO, NEU, BMI, UACR, WWI, years of diabetes duration, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, years of education, coronary heart disease, revascularization, atrial fibrillation, stroke, MAGE, CV, MODD, TIR, TAR, TBR, and the Fazekas scale. Ten‑fold cross‑validation was applied to determine the optimal penalty parameter λ for the model, and LASSO regression was then used to select associated variables with non‑zero coefficients. At the optimal λ value, nine variables associated with MCI were identified: WWI, Fazekas scale, HbA1c, MAGE, CV, TIR, C‑peptide, UACR, and CRP. All of these variables demonstrated significant discriminative ability in the model (Figs. 1 and 2). Multicollinearity assessment revealed that all VIF values were below 5 (C-peptide: 1.028, HbA1c: 1.023, MAGE: 1.029, CV: 1.020, TIR: 1.022, Fazekas scale: 1.034, WWI: 1.020, UACR: 1.016, CRP: 1.023), indicating no significant collinearity among the associated variables (Supplementary Table 1). The correlation heatmap further demonstrated that the absolute pairwise correlation coefficients among these variables were uniformly low (all |r| < 0.10), with the strongest correlation observed between MAGE and TIR (r = -0.09). These findings confirm that the included glycemic metrics capture distinct aspects of glucose homeostasis without substantial redundancy (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Logistic regression analysis of factors associated with MCI in T2DM patients and concomitant WMH

Logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the strength of associations between each associated variable and MCI. Univariate analysis identified C-peptide, HbA1c, MAGE, CV, TIR, Fazekas scale, WWI, and UACR as significant variables of MCI (all P < 0.01). In contrast, CRP did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.079). In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, four sequentially adjusted models were constructed. The results showed that all eight variables consistently retained statistical significance across all models (P < 0.01), confirming their robustness as independent variables of MCI. Specifically, the fully adjusted Model IV yielded the following odds ratios (OR) and 95% CI: C-peptide (OR = 1.37, 95% CI 1.18–1.59), HbA1c (OR = 1.52, 95% CI 1.32–1.74), MAGE (OR = 3.08, 95% CI 2.41–3.94), CV (OR = 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.04), TIR (OR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.97–0.99), Fazekas scale (OR = 1.61, 95% CI 1.42–1.82), WWI (OR = 1.22, 95% CI 1.06–1.41), and UACR (OR = 1.03, 95% CI 1.02–1.04) (Table 3).

Nomogram development and validation

Based on the independent associated variables identified through LASSO regression and multivariate logistic regression analysis, a nomogram model was developed to discriminate the presence of MCI in patients with T2DM and concomitant WMH. This nomogram includes eight associated variables: C-peptide, HbA1c, MAGE, CV, TIR, Fazekas scale, WWI, and UACR. Each variable corresponds to a specific score based on its value, with the total score calculated as the sum of the individual scores. This total score can then be extrapolated to the risk axis to determine the discriminated probability of an individual having MCI (Fig. 3).

Discriminative accuracy and net benefit of the nomogram

This study used ROC curve analysis to evaluate the diagnostic performance of various associated variables for MCI in patients with T2DM and concomitant WMH. The AUC and 95% CI for each variable were as follows: MAGE 0.705 (95% CI: 0.667–0.744), HbA1c 0.622 (95% CI: 0.581–0.664), C-peptide 0.562 (95% CI: 0.515–0.609), UACR 0.579 (95% CI: 0.536–0.621), Fazekas scale 0.653 (95% CI: 0.611–0.695), WWI 0.551 (95% CI: 0.508–0.594), CV 0.576 (95% CI: 0.534–0.617), and TIR 0.572 (95% CI: 0.532–0.612). All AUC values were statistically significant, with MAGE demonstrating the best discriminative performance, significantly outperforming the other variables (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4A). In the training cohort, the developed nomogram demonstrated strong discriminative accuracy, with AUC of 0.813 (95% CI: 0.782–0.843). In the validation cohort, the model achieved an AUC of 0.773 (95% CI: 0.720–0.827) (Fig. 4B).

The consistency between the discriminated probability of the model and the actual observed probability is evaluated through the calibration curve. In the training set, the calibration curve aligned well with the ideal diagonal, with a calibration intercept of 0.00 (95% CI: -0.17 to 0.17) and a slope of 1.00 (95% CI: 0.85–1.15), indicating excellent agreement between discriminated probabilities and observed outcomes. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated no significant difference between the model’s discriminated risk and the observed risk (χ² = 9.313, degrees of freedom = 8, P = 0.317) (Fig. 5A). In the validation set, the calibration curve also aligned well with the ideal diagonal, with a calibration intercept of 0.13 (95% CI: − 0.12 to 0.39) and a slope of 0.90 (95% CI: 0.67–1.12) (Fig. 5B). The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test also indicated no significant difference between the model’s discriminated risk and the observed risk (χ² = 8.018, degrees of freedom = 8, P = 0.432).

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to construct and validate a clinical diagnostic model to effectively discriminate the presence of MCI in patients with T2DM complicated by WMH. A nomogram model incorporating eight variables—C-peptide, HbA1c, MAGE, CV, TIR, Fazekas scale, WWI, and UACR—was successfully developed. This model demonstrated excellent discrimination and good calibration in both the training set (AUC = 0.813) and the internal validation set (AUC = 0.773), and its reliability and clinical practicability have been confirmed through calibration curves and DCA.

Previous studies have focused on developing diagnostic models for dementia or MCI in patients with T2DM [11, 13, 14, 22–25]. However, most of these models have focused on the general T2DM population and have not specifically targeted patients with WMH, a specific structural brain alteration. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first report to construct a diagnostic model for MCI in patients with T2DM and concomitant WMH. As the most common imaging marker of CSVD, the pathological basis of WMH includes demyelination, axonal injury, and gliosis, while their formation mechanisms involve chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, blood‑brain barrier dysfunction, and enlargement of perivascular spaces [26]. Recent studies have found that significant abnormal iron deposition exists within WMH regions, suggesting that iron clearance is impaired following microglial activation. This leads to the production of reactive oxygen species via the Fenton reaction, thereby exacerbating oxidative stress and neuroinflammation. Such iron overload may mediate impairments in attention and memory by modulating glymphatic system dysfunction, providing a theoretical basis for WMH‑related cognitive decline [27]. In the setting of T2DM, chronic hyperglycemia and oxidative stress may shift alternative splicing patterns of genes involved in glucose metabolism and vascular integrity toward pro-inflammatory and pro-atherosclerotic isoforms, thereby initiating molecular dysregulation that precedes MRI-detectable WMH [28]. Concurrently, hyperglycemia and IR accelerate WMH progression through endothelial dysfunction, enhanced oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses [8]. The resulting increase in WMH burden disrupts white matter fiber tract integrity and impairs frontal‑subcortical circuit connectivity, leading to deficits in executive function and processing speed, thus mediating T2DM‑associated cognitive decline [9]. In addition, several other interconnected mechanisms—including blood pressure variability and hypotensive episodes [29], arterial stiffness [30], age‑associated chronic inflammation [31], diabetes‑mediated vascular remodeling as a key determinant of arterial aging [32], and gender differences [33], that may modulate each of these pathways—likely contribute to the progression of WMH and the subsequent development of MCI in patients with T2DM. As a quantitative imaging indicator for assessing WMH, the Fazekas scale can reflect different pathophysiological mechanisms by distinguishing between two anatomical regions: periventricular and deep white matter. Periventricular hyperintensities involve demyelination and subependymal gliosis, whereas deep WMH more specifically represent chronic ischemic injury of cerebral small vessels [18, 34]. The results of this study showed a significant independent association between the Fazekas score and the risk of MCI (OR = 1.61, 95% CI: 1.42–1.82). Therefore, T2DM, WMH, and MCI are closely interrelated, and focusing on this specific population may help accurately identify individuals at high risk for MCI among patients with T2DM and concomitant WMH, thereby providing a basis for early clinical intervention.

Most previous diagnostic models for T2DM‑related cognitive impairment mainly incorporated conventional clinical variables such as education level, sex, age, blood pressure, BMI, and duration of diabetes, and paid little attention to the value of glycemic variability indicators in identifying MCI. In contrast, this study systematically incorporated, for the first time, parameters reflecting glycemic fluctuations, including MAGE, TIR, and CV, and found that glycemic variability indicators had significantly better discriminative ability for MCI than the traditional indicator HbA1c (OR for MAGE = 3.08, 95% CI: 2.41–3.94). This finding aligns with the recent academic perspective emphasizing the importance of glycemic stability. Cohort studies have confirmed that glycemic variability is closely associated with a significantly increased risk of dementia, with a stronger association than that of average glucose levels [35]. The present study further extended this association to the early stage of cognitive impairment, confirming that glycemic variability already plays an important role at the MCI stage. From a pathophysiological perspective, cognitive impairment induced by glycemic variability may involve multiple pathways. First, recurrent glycemic fluctuations trigger oxidative stress responses more strongly than sustained hyperglycemia, as manifested by mitochondrial dysfunction, activation of Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide Phosphate Hydrogen oxidase, and accumulated production of superoxide anions, leading to sustained damage to vascular endothelial cells and neuron [36]. Second, glycemic variability can induce inflammatory responses, resulting in significantly elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines. These cytokines can cross the damaged blood‑brain barrier, activate microglia, and initiate neuroinflammatory processes, ultimately causing synaptic dysfunction and neuronal injury [37]. Third, glycemic variability leads to endothelial dysfunction, including uncoupling of endothelial nitric oxide synthase, reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, and impaired vasodilation, which may promote the occurrence and progression of WMH and increase the risk of CSVD [38]. Furthermore, recent studies have revealed that the susceptibility of cognitive function to glycemic fluctuations is closely associated with levels of phosphorylated tau-181, suggesting that glycemic variability may contribute to tau pathology and participate in the neurodegenerative process [39].

C‑peptide is a polypeptide fragment secreted in equimolar amounts with insulin during the enzymatic cleavage of proinsulin. Under conditions of IR in T2DM, compensatory hypersecretion by pancreatic β‑cells leads to pathologically elevated circulating C‑peptide levels. In this study, C‑peptide levels were not only significantly higher in the MCI group than in the control group (2.76 vs. 2.36 ng/mL, P = 0.003), but were also incorporated into the nomogram model (OR = 1.37). Previous studies have shown that high C-peptide levels are closely related to CSVD and WMH [40, 41]. In the insulin‑resistant state of T2DM, increased lipid accumulation and vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation accelerate atherosclerosis and microvascular damage, while microvascular inflammation and neuroinflammation are intensified, thus worsening CSVD and the progression of WMH [40, 41]. Clinical studies have shown that high C‑peptide levels are closely associated with cortical thinning and cognitive decline [42]. Importantly, C‑peptide levels follow a biphasic trajectory across the natural history of T2DM. During the early‑to‑middle stages characterized by IR, pancreatic β‑cells compensate by hypersecreting insulin and C‑peptide, leading to normal or elevated circulating C‑peptide levels; only in advanced disease with long‑standing T2DM and progressive β‑cell failure do C‑peptide levels eventually decline [43]. The median diabetes duration in our study was approximately 9 years (interquartile range 6‑11 years), placing the majority of patients in the insulin‑resistant stage, in which elevated C‑peptide remains a valid marker of IR rather than β‑cell exhaustion. Furthermore, prospective studies among middle‑aged and older adults without diabetes have demonstrated that higher baseline C‑peptide levels independently predict faster cognitive decline and increased risk of dementia over 10‑18 years of follow‑up [44, 45]. These findings support the notion that C‑peptide elevation, as a surrogate of IR, contributes to cognitive impairment through mechanisms that are not entirely dependent on the presence of hyperglycemia. Of note, although C‑peptide exerts anti‑inflammatory and vascular protective effects in type 1 diabetes mellitus and early‑stage T2DM, its pro‑atherosclerotic, pro‑inflammatory, and neurotoxic effects predominate under the insulin‑resistant state of advanced T2DM, thereby making it a risk marker for MCI [46].

WWI is a novel obesity metric that demonstrates superior independent discriminative value (OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.06–1.41) compared to the traditional BMI. This finding underscores the critical role of central obesity and its associated metabolic disorders in CSVD and cognitive impairment. A large-scale longitudinal study based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study revealed a significant negative correlation between baseline WWI levels and cognitive function, with each unit increase in WWI linked to a 0.218-point decline in cognitive scores and an accelerated annual decline of 0.008 points [47]. Visceral adipose tissue functions not only as an energy reservoir but also as an active endocrine organ, secreting various adipokines such as leptin, adiponectin, and resistin. In central obesity, adipokine secretion is disrupted, resulting in leptin resistance and decreased adiponectin levels, which may impair hypothalamic appetite regulation, amplify inflammatory responses, and reduce insulin sensitivity [48]. Moreover, visceral fat accumulation fosters ectopic fat deposition, including infiltration around the liver, muscles, and vascular walls. This ectopic fat contributes to systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction through the release of free fatty acids and inflammatory mediators, thereby exacerbating CSVD [49].

UACR, as a sensitive indicator of early renal damage, demonstrated discriminative value for MCI in this study. This finding provides evidence for the consistency of target organ damage in diabetes from the perspective of the “kidney–brain axis.” Both the kidneys and brain possess extensive microvascular networks that are highly sensitive to hemodynamic changes and metabolic abnormalities. Microalbuminuria reflects systemic endothelial dysfunction, a pathological change that is also observed in cerebral microvessels [50]. A recent large-scale cross-sectional study based on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey confirmed that elevated UACR levels significantly correlate with an increased risk of cognitive impairment in older adults. Notably, higher UACR levels remain linked to cognitive decline even within the normal albuminuria range (UACR < 30 mg/g) [51]. Endothelial dysfunction in the state of diabetic kidney injury leads to impaired cerebrovascular autoregulation and reduced cerebral blood flow, while compromising blood-brain barrier integrity and allowing circulating neurotoxic substances to enter brain tissue more easily, thereby triggering neuroinflammatory responses. Additionally, renal impairment may result in complications such as the accumulation of uremic toxins, electrolyte disturbances, and anemia, all of which may further exacerbate cognitive impairment [52].

This study has important theoretical and clinical implications in the field of neuroendocrine interactions. Theoretically, our findings provide novel empirical support for the “metabolic‑vascular‑cognitive” axis hypothesis, which posits that diabetes‑related metabolic abnormalities including hyperglycemia, glycemic variability, and IR synergistically interact with vascular damage such as WMH to drive cognitive decline [53]. By constructing a comprehensive diagnostic model, we quantified the contribution of each factor and found that glycemic variability, particularly MAGE, conferred the highest risk for MCI. This suggests that glycemic fluctuations may induce oxidative stress and inflammatory damage to neurons and the blood‑brain barrier more directly and severely than sustained hyperglycemia [36], highlighting glycemic variability as an independent therapeutic target beyond average glucose control. Clinically, our nomogram model integrates readily available clinical, laboratory, and imaging indicators to provide individualized MCI risk assessments for patients with T2DM and concomitant WMH. Decision curve analysis confirmed its net benefit, offering clinicians a convenient tool to identify high-risk individuals who require priority cognitive screening and enhanced management. Furthermore, previous studies have confirmed that macroscopic brain morphological features—such as cortical surface area, thickness, and structural asymmetry—can serve as sensitive biomarkers that capture the brain’s heterogeneous adaptive responses to systemic stressors [54, 55]. Consequently, the integration of metabolic dysregulation markers with detailed structural imaging parameters in our model may enhance discriminative accuracy by capturing complementary dimensions of brain sensitivity and compensatory adaptation, which likely explains why our model outperforms models relying solely on traditional clinical variables.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the single‑center design may introduce selection bias and limit the external generalizability of the model. Therefore, multi‑center external validation across diverse populations is strongly recommended before widespread clinical implementation. Second, the cross‑sectional design precludes causal inferences. It remains unclear whether glycemic fluctuations precede cognitive decline or whether underlying neurodegenerative processes and shared metabolic dysregulation develop concurrently. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to establish the temporal sequence of these events. Third, although the Fazekas scale is practical for clinical settings, its assessment of WMH burden is relatively basic and subjective; modern neuroimaging frequently employs quantitative volumetric measurements or integrates other markers of CSVD, which are lacking in our model. Fourth, cognitive function was assessed only by the total MoCA score without domain‑specific subscores, preventing analysis of whether metabolic factors differentially affect specific cognitive domains such as executive function and processing speed. Fifth, detailed medication histories of glucose‑lowering drugs (types, doses, duration, adherence) and statins were not captured. These agents may act as unmeasured confounders through their neuroprotective or vasculoprotective effects, their direct impact on glycemic variability, and their influence on CSVD burden, thereby independently affecting both the risk of mild cognitive impairment and key discriminative variables.

Conclusion

In summary, this study systematically identified key risk factors for MCI in patients with T2DM complicated by WMH, including glycemic variability (represented by MAGE, CV, and TIR), chronic hyperglycemia (indicated by HbA1c), central obesity (indicated by the WWI), IR (marked by C‑peptide), renal‑vascular injury (reflected by the UACR), and white matter lesion burden (assessed by the Fazekas scale). Based on these eight variables, we successfully developed and internally validated a nomogram diagnostic model, which demonstrated good discrimination and calibration in both the training and validation cohorts, and decision curve analysis confirmed its clinical net benefit. Future studies should conduct prospective multicenter external validation to assess the generalizability of the model, and incorporate multimodal biological and imaging markers as well as dynamic variables to develop individualized, stratified, and longitudinal risk assessment tools.
