What influenced the use of embedded genetic expertise by non-genetic clinicians: a qualitative study using the diffusion of innovations theory and theoretical domains framework
Authors: Trang Thu Do, Belinda Dawson McClaren, Clara Gaff, Melissa Martyn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile employing change agents is a widely used strategy to support implementation of evidence-based practices in healthcare, the perspectives of the target users of this intervention are under-explored. Within a genomic care setting in Australia, we examined non-genetic clinicians’ views of and experience with embedded genetic counsellors employed as external change agents to support the adoption of genomics in clinical care.MethodsWe conducted qualitative interviews with 16 non-genetic healthcare professionals involved in different models of genomic care, all of which employed genetic counsellors as external change agents to provide embedded genetic expertise and support in non-genetic specialty clinics. The Diffusion of Innovations (DOI) theory and the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) were drawn upon to guide the study design and data analysis to explore the enablers/barriers to non-genetic clinicians’ use of genetic expertise and the wider implementation context of the change agent approach.ResultsThe TDF domain ‘Belief about Consequences’ was a positive influence on non-genetic specialists’ view of the embedded change agents, when they believed that the change agents positively impacted patient outcomes and the clinicians’ knowledge and practice of using genomics in routine care. Meanwhile, major barriers were related to ‘Environmental Context & Resources,’ e.g. time constraints and logistic hurdles of involving change agents. Perceived attributes related to ‘complexity’ and ‘observability’ of genetic expertise were critical factors influencing the decision to put the support to actual use, impacting the diffusion of the genomic innovation.ConclusionsOur study offers insights into behavioural influences and contextual conditions shaping clinicians’ decisions to interact with genetic counsellor change agents and incorporate (or not) their expertise into their routine care. Such understandings can inform future design and implementation of interventions that use change agents to support the adoption of innovations, such as genomics, effectively and sustainably.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s43058-026-01026-6.

Introduction

Genomic sequencing has transformed diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment, as well as disease prevention across many areas of medical care thanks to the vast amount of information it can provide in relation to a person’s current and future health [1–3]. The field of genomic medicine - the use of individual patient’s genotypic information in clinical care of their medical condition [4] - is fast evolving and, in the process, moving from specialist clinical genetic services into mainstream practice in a range of clinical specialties. With this rapid evolution of the role of genomic medicine in clinical practice, comes the need to introduce alternative models of care to provide timely access to genomic care – by which we refer to all elements of person-centred clinical practice that includes genomic testing [5].

Models of genomic care in which non-genetic clinicians manage genetic and genomic testing are gaining favour, providing earlier access to genomic testing and counselling for patients [6, 7]. Many of these ‘mainstream’ models of genomic care included embedding genetic counsellors (GCs) in specialty medical teams [8, 9]. In these models, embedded GCs contribute to multi-disciplinary team meetings, facilitate clinical decision-making, and provide holistic patient care from discussing consent to disclosing results [8–10]. These models offer a cost-effective strategy to facilitate integration of genetic and genomic testing into clinical care, enhancing access to genomic care, and promoting equity and patient outcomes [10–13]. Access to embedded genetic expertise also benefits non-genetic clinicians, with GCs supporting them to adapt their practice and incorporate genomics in routine care. GCs thus not only provide care as an embedded team member, they are also acting as ‘change agents,’ addressing the knowledge gaps and a lack of skill and capability-related barriers reported by non-genetic clinicians attempting to incorporate genomic testing in their routine practice [14, 15].

A change agent is defined as individual who influences clients’ innovation decisions in a direction deemed desirable by a change agency [16] and can be internal [17] or external to the implementation site [18, 19]. A change agent intervention has been widely used to enable the implementation of evidence-based practices in a complex system such as healthcare [17, 19, 20]. However, our knowledge of the enablers/barriers to the change agent intervention and its effectiveness in influencing target behaviours from the adopters’ perspectives remains quite limited. By focusing on clinician views and experience, our aim in this paper is to provide insights into what works best to change clinician behaviour regarding the use of genomics in routine clinical practice through introducing the role of change agents external to the practice sites.

Study context: Australian healthcare context and the change program

This implementation science-informed study was conducted as part of an overarching research study within a Change program which funded multiple change projects road-testing innovative, multidisciplinary models of care to support the use of genomics by non-genetic healthcare professionals in a public health system in Victoria, Australia since 2021 [21]. All of those models employed GC as external change agents to support clinicians and patients in diverse, real-world healthcare settings outside of clinical genetics services.

The healthcare system of Australia comprises both public and private sectors. Public hospital systems are managed by State/Territory governments and receive funding from the relevant State/Territory and federal governments. Citizens and permanent residents are covered within the public health system through Australia’s Medicare system, which also subsidises the cost of health services listed on the Medicare Benefits Schedule (MBS) [22]. The MBS includes an increasing number of genetic and genomic tests [23]. Although service volumes are increasing [24], test use remains low [25], reflecting a lack of capability and capacity of non-genetic health professionals in adopting genetic and genomic testing in routine care [14, 26].

With an explicit focus on building the capability of those within the system to lead the change needed to integrate genomics into the Victorian public health system, each of the change projects funded by the Change program targeted a different clinical service [21] and road-tested models of genomic care (Fig. 1). These include collaborative models - where genomic experts work within other clinical services, providing integrated care within another specialty and independent models – where clinicians manage the genomic care of a patient within the specialty where there are MBS rebates for genomic tests and have access to expert genomic advice as needed [27]. Each of the change projects was conducted at a single or multiple hospital sites and comprised different funded roles, as reported elsewhere [21]. All projects employed GCs embedded as change agents who provided immediate and in-clinic support to non-genetic clinicians and patients [28] and received separate interventions from the Change program to support them in their mainstream roles [29].

In earlier publications, we examined the views of change agents employed by the change projects [28] and the relational nature of implementation when change agents promoted the use of genomics among the clinician adopters [30]. This paper focuses exclusively on the non-genetic clinicians who interacted with the GC change agents and were the target adopters of the change agent intervention. Findings from our study could inform the development of effective strategies to help non-genetic clinicians overcome barriers to engaging with change agents. The change agent roles were funded through the Change program and as such embedded GC support was not guaranteed once the change projects finished. This allows us to also explore perceived need for ongoing access to embedded support. Our findings can thus inform interventions to maximise the use of change agent interventions and provide insights into sustainable use of innovations such as genomics in clinical care.

Methods

Theoretical frameworks

Our study is informed by Rogers’ Diffusion of Innovations (DOI) theory [16] which describes the social process of how an innovation spreads and proposes that certain attributes of an innovation, including its relative advantage, complexity, compatibility, observability, and trialability, can explain why it can spread more easily than others. The DOI theory recognises five successive stages of the innovation–decision process which begins with the two ‘mental stages’ [31]: the ‘knowledge’ stage – when an individual gains an understanding of how an innovation functions and the ‘persuasion stage’ – when an individual forms a favourable or unfavourable attitude towards the innovation. Three ‘behavioural stages’ then follow, when an individual adopts or rejects the innovation (‘Decision’ stage), puts the innovation to use (‘Implementation’ stage), and seeks reinforcement for this innovation-decision (‘Confirmation’ stage).

While the DOI theory can be used to explore the organisational context in which the innovation occurs, we also wish to understand what influences the adoption, non-adoption, or discontinuance of an innovation at an individual level. To do this, we draw upon, the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation and Behaviour (COM-B) model of behaviour change [32] and its associated determinant framework the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) [33], to complement the DOI theory. The COM-B model posits that behaviour is a result of one’s capability (C), opportunity (O) and motivation (M). The TDF maps to the COM-B, with its 14 domains providing greater granularity about influences on capability (e.g. knowledge, skills), opportunity (e.g. environmental context and resources) and motivation (e.g. beliefs about the intervention). The COM-B and TDF are commonly used to explore influences on behaviour in a wide range of settings, including healthcare [33, 34]. Genomic care is complex and has unique challenges that can influence health services’ responsiveness to change [35, 36]. Using DOI and the COM-B model and its associate determinant framework the TDF in a complementary way allows a more comprehensive understanding of the influences at multiple levels (e.g. individual and organisational) [37]. We can thus examine the barriers and enablers facing non-genetic health professionals throughout the innovation-decision process. At the same time, this combined use will enable the understanding of contextual influences on the desired behaviour of clinicians when it comes to utilising the support provide by the change agents embedded in their clinics.

Procedure

Clinicians were recruited for this study using a purposive sampling strategy, which we describe elsewhere [30]. The research team emailed clinicians who had been involved in the change project implementation or interacted with the change project teams to invite them to participate in the interviews. Participants took part in qualitative interviews which lasted for up to 40 min. Our interview guide was developed with questions related to the stages of the innovation-decision process [16] and COM-B [32] (Supplementary File 1). Specifically, we first asked questions related to their knowledge of and attitudes towards having GC change agents working in their specialty clinics (Knowledge and Persuasion stages). The interview then went on to inquire about participants’ use of the expertise and support provided by those GCs and the impacts it had on their practice and patients (Decision and Implementation stages). Given that the embedded support was no longer available when the change projects finished, final questions elicited the perceived need for the future role of GCs to support sustained use of genomics in clinical specialties (Confirmation stage).

Data analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim either by the first author TD or a professional transcription service provider. All identifiable information was removed before analysis. The first step of the analysis process started with identifying the target behaviour [33], that is the utilisation of change agents support among non-genetic specialists and how it influenced their use of genomics in routine care.1 We adopted a multistage analytical approach, combining inductive and deductive analysis and used NVivo software to manage data and facilitate analysis [38]. In the first stage, data from the interviews with non-genetic clinicians, including medical specialists and allied health workers were coded using inductive content analysis [39]. The first author who did all the interviews read all the notes and interview transcripts to become familiar with the data and then coded segments of five interview transcripts that illustrated perceptions of the change agent roles and experience with the embedded GC change agents in the specialist clinics across three major innovation-decision stages: (1) Knowledge and Persuasion, (2) Decision and Implementation, and (3) Confirmation. The chunks of data related to factors that facilitated or hindered target clinicians from utilising the embedded genetic counselling support were coded and categorised into distinct code labels. A deductive coding stage then followed, whereby we assigned multiple codes to the data selected in the first stage. Those codes were simultaneously mapped to the TDF domains, considering their relevance to the respective definitions [33, 40], adapted to the specific context of this study (see Supplementary File 2), and where data suggested, to the attributes of the embedded change agent support that aligned with the DOI theory to highlight where the DOI and TDF could overlap or complement each other.

A coding guide was developed at this stage and applied to code the remaining interview transcripts. This stage involved the senior author MM who checked the analysis of factors based on the TDF. The researchers met regularly to discuss emerging patterns and resolve any discrepancies in coding and interpretation until agreement was reached. We then synthesised and interpreted the findings against the theoretical propositions. We noted where there were differences in the perspectives of participants in collaborative versus independent models of genomic care. In the sections below, we present the findings related to the enablers/barriers to the use of change agent support and interacting with the GC change agents and integrating the genetic expertise offered by the change projects according to the TDF domains. The findings are organised by the three major stages of the decision-innovation process (see Supplementary File 3). Where data exist, different views between participants recruited from the collaborative versus those working in the independent models are noted in this section. We then discuss these findings in relation to the perceived attributes and other propositions in the DOI theory and what it means for the use of change agents to promote the adoption of innovations.

Findings

Participants

We recruited 16 participants for the interviews from 18 invitations sent. Those included 14 non-genetic medical specialists and 2 allied health professionals (Table 1) across haematology, cardiology, nephrology, transplantation, neurology, and neuropsychiatry. Seven of these clinicians were working within collaborative models and nine within independent models. Participants were all employed by public hospitals, with some also holding part-time roles in the private sector.

Knowledge and persuasion stages

Enablers

Five enablers were present at these stages, which were primarily centred around TDF domains related to ‘Knowledge,’ ‘Optimism,’ ‘Beliefs about consequences,’ ‘Social/professional role and identity,’ and ‘Environmental context & resources.’

Most participants recruited for the interviews were aware of the availability of genetic counselling support they could access throughout the duration of the change projects (TDF: Knowledge). In most cases, it was officially communicated to them in team meetings at the beginning of the GCs’ employment and/or through the introduction of the department heads or the change project leads who were their colleagues.

Participants in the independent and collaborative models generally held favourable attitude towards having GCs embedded in their clinics and were convinced that the in-clinic support was desirable and fit within the structure of the specialist departments. Most notably, they believed that having access to GCs in the specialist clinics would bring about clinical benefits, helping expedite patient access to genetic testing (TDF: Optimism & Belief about Consequences), ensuring continuity of care, promoting family-centred care, and improving the overall healthcare experiences for patients and families (TDF: Belief about consequences), as one participant stated:

How should we counsel the family or what’s the logistics around it, that gets a bit harder, and I think having the genetic counsellor is very useful. Often, we end up referring them [patients] to the complex genetic clinic anyway. But having that genetic counsellor there is also useful for the next steps. (NC003)

Interviewed clinicians across both models expressed good understanding of the purpose of employing GCs in the change projects, believing that it aligned with the priorities of their clinics and the specialists’ needs, helping address the existing barriers to providing genetic testing and counselling in the non-genetic specialty areas. Specifically, there was recognition of the employed GCs who possessed good knowledge of the specialist clinic, the specialty areas, and the delineation of GCs’ role (TDF: Social/Professional Role and Identity). Employing GCs to work in specialty clinics was believed to be compatible with the local context, as the embedment of genetic expertise helped address a lack of collaboration between genetics and non-genetics departments and a common lack of understanding among non-genetic medical specialists about the referral pathways and the work happening within clinical genetics, even when they were located in the same hospital (TDF: Social/Professional Role and Identity). Participants appreciated the immediate and accessible genetics expertise within their clinic (TDF: Environmental context & resources), instead of having such expertise only available at an external clinical genetics department:

I think it worked well when there was a genetic counsellor who was friendly, approachable around… and someone who was just comfortable to be within the unit and be around the space. (NL001)

Barriers

Our analysis revealed two major barriers at these earliest stages of the innovation-decision process reported by participants recruited from the collaborative models. Specifically, lack of promotion among colleagues in the same clinic played a key role in influencing participants’ initial feeling about the fit of genetic counselling support (TDF: Social influences). This was due to a limited number of patients who required genetics involvement in a specific specialty. For example, one shared their experience:

I was a bit apprehensive initially … because the doctor didn’t think it was appropriate for her [the genetic counsellor] to give it [the patients’ test results]. (TN001)

Meanwhile, some believed that the expertise of the genomic professional was not relevant for the patients they cared for (TDF: Social & Professional Role and Identity). This perception dissuaded them from using the genetic expertise offered by the employed GCs, limiting their interaction with the intervention (genomics) introduced by the change projects.

Decision and implementation stages

The utilisation of genetic counselling support varied among the participants we interviewed: some reported more regular and multifaceted engagement through co-consultations and daily informal conversations with the GCs, while others limited their interaction to referring their patients for in-clinic genetic counselling with the employed GCs, exchanging remote communication (via emails/phone calls) or multidisciplinary team meetings which were mostly held online (via Teams/Zoom). The different levels of interaction with the change agents impacted how participants adopted genomics in their practice as we illustrate below.

Enablers

Utilising the support from the GC change agents in the specialist clinics was perceived to contribute to addressing clinicians’ knowledge gaps and skill deficiencies and overall improve their capability to use genomics in patient care. The interviewed clinicians from both models reported having improved awareness of the utility of genomic testing in their practice (TDF: Knowledge). Interacting with GCs was recognised as a helpful channel for clinicians to acquire new knowledge and seek guidance (TDF: Social influences & Knowledge). Having access to genetic expertise in their clinics and throughout team meetings also prompted clinicians working within the collaborative models to consider testing for their patients earlier (TDF: Intentions).

Participants across both types of models particularly commented on how they improved their skills thanks to the support provided by the embedded GCs, whereby they learned how to counsel patients from observing GCs in co-consultations and became more confident communicating with patients about genomic testing and conducting consent discussion (TDF: Skills):

And I think that’s one of the best things because now I’m not afraid to talk to patients about what the testing involves, what the costs are, how it’s going to affect their insurance, but also what does it mean if they get a positive result or negative result or the VUS [a variant of uncertain significance] and how should they approach their family, and screening for their children. (NC003)

In addition, in both models under study participants highlighted the valued role of the change agents in facilitating the process of test ordering, when they could engage an embedded GC as part of the clinical team who helped with patient follow-up and test interpretation. This inclusion helped reduce the need to refer patients to a genetics service (TDF: Professional Role and Identify). Participants particularly acknowledged the role of the embedded GCs in providing support in interpreting test results, managing the implications for patients and family members, and providing post-test genetic counselling (TDF: Social/Professional Role and Identify). In complex situations, participants valued the access to genetic expertise provided by the change agents, as it helped them cope with the emotional burden associated with communicating with patients about genetics, given their lack of training and experience in the field (TDF: Social/Professional Role and Identify & Beliefs about Consequences). They also described the value of the embedded support in terms of fostering the rapport with patients as compared to having to refer patients to an external department (TDF: Belief about Consequences). Meanwhile, the immediate nature of the embedded support allowed participants to approach GCs within their clinics when they were faced with some practical issues related to the test ordering process (TDF: Environmental context & resources). Considering the benefits of having speedy access to genetic counselling support available within their clinics, the interviewed clinicians reported engaging GCs as part of their routine appointments (TDF: Behaviour Regulation) and improving their actual practice of using genomic test in clinical care (TDF: Belief about Capabilities), as a participant noted below:

[If] a new patient that had a strong clinical suspicion of being a genetic participant, than I’d call NAME [a genetic counsellor] and she was obliging enough to actually sit in the clinic with me. So, that was excellent in the way that it facilitated the investigations, like considerably faster than it normally would have been. (DC004)

Barriers

The barriers to accessing and utilising the support from GCs can be mainly categorised under five TDF domains. ‘Belief about Capabilities’ could be simultaneously regarded as an enabler (as described above) and a barrier to using the support, leading some participants to switch from adopting to discontinuing genetic support over the course of the change projects. Specifically, participants in independent models reported observing improvement in their knowledge about genomics and confidence with counselling because of working with the GCs in the early stage of the change projects. This was a positive change, resulting in ongoing use of genomics and discontinuing the need for change agent support, evident among participants working in the specialty areas with access to a rebate for genomic tests through the national MBS. Meanwhile, many participants mentioned the barriers related to the TDF domains of ‘Environmental Context & Resources’ when they considered the practicality of having GCs in co-consultations, leading some to disengage with the GCs. Specifically, time constraint was often mentioned as a dominant challenge in both models where clinicians could engage a GC in a co-consultation because it usually increased the length of the specialist appointments. Participants recruited from the collaborative models further spoke about the barrier regarding the logistics of setting up the co-consultations when a GC was present. Some were also concerned about the logistical difficulty for extending patients’ time to meet with the GCs either within the same or outside of the appointment which had been scheduled with the medical specialist (TDF: Belief about Consequences). They were also hesitant to involve GCs in co-consultations or introduce patients to the embedded GCs for separate conversations about genomic testing due to their concern related to finding the right time, being mindful that such conversations potentially caused anxiety for patients, especially when MBS rebates were not available for genomic test in those specialty areas (TDF: Social/Professional Role and Identify & Belief about Consequences). A lack of advanced planning for the clinic and not knowing if any genetics-related information was required for the patients they saw on their clinic days also limited their use of support from GCs, which was noted by those working in the independent models (TDF: Behaviour Regulation):

But the reality is like most people, I think just turn up to their clinic and then they see the patients that they’ve been allocated, so they might not have pre-identified those that might be suitable for [genomic] testing. (NC004)

Confirmation stage

The overarching Change Program supported individual change projects to articulate what sustainability might look like in each setting [29]. This included consideration of the long-term need for embedded GCs. Most clinicians in both the collaborative and independent models of care were enthusiastic about maintaining contact with the GCs beyond the change projects in some form. However, participants had different views about the longer-term need for genetic counselling expertise in non-genetic specialty clinics. Factors that influenced these views are discussed below as barriers and enablers to continuation of the embedded GC model.

Enablers

Participants in both collaborative and independent models who believed they still lacked capability (TDF: Beliefs about Capabilities) in counselling patients and managing genomic test results identified a need for continuing change agent support in the long term after the change projects finished and for integrating GCs into specialist clinics to provide routine care for patients. Genetic expertise was perceived as particularly important when: genomic test results indicated a positive variant or a variant of uncertain significance; patients required provision of reproductive information; or clinicians needed to manage the broader implications for patient families. Participants also identified the need for continuing genetic counselling support for their specialist colleagues and/or junior specialists who were less experienced in genomics. Participants from the collaborative models particularly emphasised the educational role they could see for GCs in their specialties to help keep them abreast of the evolving complexity of genomic testing, given the vital role of continuous learning for non-genetic health professionals to safely use genomics in their practices (TDF: Belief about Consequences).

Barriers

Participants who held firm beliefs in the benefits of having GCs continue working in their clinics reported significant barriers to funding those roles in the future (TDF: Environmental Context & Resources). This was especially the case when there were uncertainties in anticipating the workload for an embedded GC, which was raised by clinicians recruited from both types of models (TDF: Social and Professional Role & Identity). One shared this concern:

[W]ithin that clinic there’s only, there’d be one new and four reviews, so, there’s not a huge pool of patients [requiring genomic testing]. (DC004)

Where embedded GCs were desired, it was highlighted that they needed to have expertise in the specialty area: the value of generic genetic support was questioned (TDF: Social/Professional Role & Identity and Belief about Consequences). Other clinicians were not convinced about the need for the continuity of similar support in the future, particularly when change agents had fulfilled their role and upskilled non-genetic clinicians in genomics so they were capable of ordering and returning test results (TDF: Beliefs about Capabilities). This was particularly the case observed among participants in the independent model of genomic care where a MBS rebate had already been available for genomic tests. Some participants from those models also expressed concerns over the over-reliance of non-genetic specialists on GCs when it comes to practicing genomics if such embedded roles persisted in non-genetic departments (TDF: Belief about Consequences).

Alternative models to sustain genetic counselling support in specialty areas

Having support from GCs with expertise in the specialty area was envisaged as desirable in the long term, which was reiterated by participants in the independent models. Meanwhile, some participants from the collaborative models, in considering future service models against the wider organisational context and individual needs, recommended ongoing access to genetic counselling expertise via genomic multidisciplinary team meetings where non-genetic specialists could go to seek case-specific advice when ordering genomic testing and returning results (TDF: Social/Professional Role & identity):

I think perhaps maybe instead of doing, having a genetic counsellor all the time, is to have maybe like a, like a MDT clinic and then maybe the counsellor to be available less frequently than it is for this project, that might be more realistic (TC001).

Discussion

Use of change agents is a common strategy to support integrating new innovations in healthcare. In genomics, one model of facilitating the incorporation of genomic care outside of specialist genetic services is to embed GCs in specialty clinics to provide genetic expertise to support clinical decision-making and offer in-clinic genetic counselling to patients in non-genetic clinical specialties to enhance greater access to genomic medicine [6, 8]. This study offers crucial insights into the factors that influenced the use of the change agent support among non-genetic healthcare professionals, whose views and experiences are often missing from the literature examining the implementation of change agent interventions and/or alternative models of genomic care. Using the TDF to guide data analysis, we identified ‘Belief about Consequences’ as a notable factor that led non-genetic specialists to favour the role of embedded change agents. Specifically, having GCs in their clinics was recognised to improve patient outcomes through expediting patient access to genomic testing and counselling and ensuring continuity of care for patients and families. Participants engaged with the external change agents and utilised their support to improve their knowledge and practice of using genomics in their clinical care. Meanwhile, major barriers were related to the ‘Environmental Context & Resources,’ i.e. time constraints and the logistic hurdles of involving GCs in co-consultations or additional appointments with patients, as well as concerns over the consequences of raising anxiety for patients.

Considering the findings according to the DOI theory [16] offers a more nuanced understanding of the implementation context beyond the individual level (see Supplementary File 3). During the ‘Knowledge and Persuasion’ stages, participants interviewed in this study expressed favourable opinions towards the employment of GCs in specialty clinics based on the perceived attributes related to the ‘relative advantage’ and ‘compatibility’ of the support they could access through the introduction of the change agent intervention. They were convinced about the ‘relative advantage’ of embedded support over the expertise that could only be accessed through specialist clinical genetics departments. This support was deemed compatible to the local context, which was clearly evidenced by the perceived advantages of that support at different levels, from individual, inter-personal, to organisational levels. At the individual level, it aligned with the clinical team’s skillset and ways of working, designed to help upskill non-genetic specialists in genomics and overcome the challenges of using and accessing genomic testing. Interpersonally, the availability of in-clinic genomic support strengthened clinician-patient rapport. Participants also perceived the ‘compatibility’ of employing external change agents to work in the specialty clinics, believing that those roles contributed to addressing the pre-existing lack of connection between genetics and non-genetics departments. Our interviewees highlighted the significant role of social exchange with peers and senior colleagues throughout these early mental stages of implementing and diffusing innovations, echoing a common finding found in previous research in healthcare services [41, 42]. Formal validation and endorsement of the external change agents to the team at the clinic where they were embedded was shown to be an integral part in establishing early perceptions in favour of the change agent support. Meanwhile, some participants seemed more resistant to the embedded roles when there was a lack of buy-in among colleagues in the same clinic, which led those health professionals to limit their interaction with the employed GCs.

The perceived attributes related to ‘complexity’ and ‘observability’ of the change agent intervention were critical factors influencing the decision to put the support provided by GCs to actual use (‘Implementation’ stage). Specifically, the ‘observability’ of the support was highly regarded among the interviewees, many of whom described the experience of co-consulting patients with the GC change agents as an enabler. This finding corroborates the DOI’s theory’s proposition about the importance of change agents in speeding up the innovation-decision process by demonstrating a new idea in a social system [16]. For non-genetic specialists interviewed in our study, observing how a GC demonstrated communicating with patients about genomic testing and test implications for family members and relatives in a co-consultation helped them learn about discussing consent and counselling patients about genomic testing. On the other hand, for some participants in the collaborative models, the perceived ‘complexity’ of engaging with the embedded GCs in terms of additional appointment time or advanced planning required for co-consultations acted as barriers to utilising the embedded genetic expertise offered by the change projects. This led to ‘discontinuance’ after several occasions of engaging a GC in co-consultations. In this regard, exploring what influences discontinuance is useful in understanding future need for ongoing access to embedded support beyond the change projects.

Meanwhile, for others, discontinuance occurred when clinicians became more capable of using genomics, which was evident among participants recruited from the independent models. What the data suggest regarding this discontinuance could point to an improvement in the clinicians’ capability as a result of utilising the embedded support at earlier stages and that genomics had become routinised in their practice. Given that embedded GC support provided by GC change agents was not guaranteed once the change projects finished, the discontinuance should be considered as an intended outcome rather than a negative rejection of the change agent support among clinicians in independent models of genomic care in specialty areas where there is a MBS rebate for genomic tests. For the specialties implementing the collaborative models, such understanding is equally useful to inform future innovations seeking to introduce external change agents to support clinician behaviour change when genomic tests are made available through the MBS.

‘Re-invention’ was common when participants discussed the future role of GC change agents in specialist clinics in the long term when the change projects finished. Defined as the degree to which an innovation is adapted or modified by a user in the adoption and implementation stages, the concept of ‘re-invention’ is frequently examined in diffusion research in the ‘Implementation stage’ [16]. In our study, re-invention occurred when participants highlighted the need for modifying the embedment of external change agents in their clinics (the ‘Confirmation’ stage), while recognising the benefits of continuing genetic counselling support to assist non-genetic clinicians in navigating the complex nature of genomic testing and integrating genomics as part of routine care. They suggested having genetic expertise available at genomic multidisciplinary team meetings – somewhat similarly to what had been offered through the independent model [27] - or by having GCs with expert knowledge in the specialty area providing ad-hoc support. By imagining the future positioning of GCs in non-genetic specialty areas, participants become active adopters in the ‘Confirmation’ stage. They give meaning to the embedded change agent intervention – the innovation introduced by the change projects - by constructing a new idea to customise a future role that is adaptable to the local context characterised by uncertain workload anticipated for GCs’ involvement and funding instability facing public health services across Australia.

Linking our findings with the literature on ‘knowledge brokering’ [43–45], ‘change agents’ [18], and ‘champions’ [20, 46, 47] suggests a range of implementation strategies that could improve the impact of GC change agents as a strategy to support implementation of an innovation. Head of department support appears a critical factor for successful implementation [43]. Many of our participants reported awareness of the GC agents and understanding of their role following formal introduction by the head of department or project lead. Physical co-location was cited by our participants as supporting access to the change agents and has been reported previously as an enabling factor for ‘champion’ success [47]. At the level of personal strategies change agents can use to maximise success, the ability of GC change agents to contextualise genomics knowledge was valued by our participants and has been reported previously as an implementation strategy with good evidence of effectiveness [18, 45, 48].

Limitations and strengths

One of the limitations of this study was the deployment of a targeted recruitment strategy, whereby we only invited healthcare professionals with direct experience of the change projects to participate. This could have influenced some of the barriers during the first stages of ‘Knowledge’ and ‘Persuasion,’ because most participants were aware of the availability of change agent support by the time of the interviews. On the other hand, the clinicians interviewed in our study came from diverse specialist backgrounds and health services. This diversity ensured inclusion of a broad spectrum of views and professional experiences.

The strength of this paper is the deployment of an integrated approach based on multiple theoretical frameworks, which is recognised as complicated and yet valuable [37, 49, 50]. This study has showed how the DOI theory and TDF could be drawn upon in a complimentary way to develop the interview guide, coding, and interpret data through enabling a structured analysis of multilevel influences of an innovation adoption, including organisational and health professional factors [51, 52]. We demonstrate the benefits of complementing an analysis solely based on the DOI theory, as this theory tends to overlook the individual factors and is restricted in unpacking how an individual or a team behaves in a particular way in response to the introduction of change into the system. Mapping the barriers and enablers to the TDF domains allowed us to examine a range of behavioural influences including capability, motivation, and opportunity to understand why individuals adopt or resist the support provided by external change agents and explain the mechanism of behaviour change occurring at different stages. Meanwhile, the simultaneous use of DOI as the guiding theory expands the mere description of static barriers and facilitators and facilitates the elaboration of organisation-level domains in greater detail [37], allowing for a nuanced analysis of the dynamics between individuals and contextual conditions. The combined use of both the DOI theory and TDF therefore enables the identification of areas that influence the need for embedded change agent support and development of distinct implementation strategies to facilitate the introduction of innovations across different stages of the decision process.

Conclusion

This study is one of the first attempts to examine how clinicians perceived and made use of the change agent intervention provided through different models of genomic care to change behaviour of using genomics in non-genetic clinical specialties. Recently, there has been substantial literature looking into embedded models as promising avenues to tackle the growing demands for integrating genomics in routine clinical care [8–10]. However, little is known about the perceptions of those whose behaviour needs to change as the primary target for interventions in those genomics-mainstreaming models. By focusing on clinician views, this theory-informed study has offered valuable insights into what influences their use of change agent support available in their clinics and the social and organisational contexts within which they integrated that embedded support to improve their capability of using genomics in routine care. In illuminating the understandings and actions at and beyond the individual level, our study contributes granularity to the implementation literature, recognising the complex adaptive nature of healthcare work to change practices within unique contexts [50]. The clinicians’ insights and positive experiences with the support provided by the embedded GC change agents as demonstrated in our study offer important evidence to promote those models of mainstreaming genomics to other clinical specialty areas. Findings from our study can inform the design and implementation of new models of care as to what works to effectively and sustainably support clinician behaviour change.
