Characterization of the biosynthetic enzymes of the Morus alba benzofuran natural products
Authors: Xinshui Yu, Nianxin Guo, Teng Long, Yuling Zhu, Han Ke, Qi Ding, Lei Gao, Xiaoguang Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Moracins are a distinct class of benzofuran natural products, primarily found in Moraceae plants. Despite their varying biological activities and interesting chemical structures, little is known about their biosynthesis. Here, we elucidate the biosynthetic pathway of moracin M, a common intermediate in moracin biosynthesis. Using thermal proteome profiling, we identified a noncanonical laccase from Morus alba (MaLAC) that catalyzes an unprecedented oxidative cyclization of oxyresveratrol to moracin M. Distinct from other plant laccases, MaLAC is the first plant laccase shown to catalyze an intramolecular oxidative cyclization reaction in natural product biosynthesis. Mechanistic studies revealed a copper-mediated radical cyclization, providing guidance for engineering laccases to catalyze synthetically valuable intramolecular oxidative cyclization. The biosynthetic pathway for moracin M was heterologously constructed in Nicotiana benthamiana. This work not only provides important insights into moracin biosynthesis, laying the foundation for manufacturing bioactive moracins via metabolic engineering strategies, but also demonstrates the power of label-free chemoproteomics in elucidating unknown biosynthetic pathways of plant secondary metabolites.

INTRODUCTION

The benzofuran core structure is commonly found in bioactive natural products and pharmaceuticals (1–3). To date, more than 30 benzofurans or their derivatives have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration as drugs, including fruquintinib, vilazodone, and benzbromarone (Fig. 1A) (2, 4). Therefore, developing novel chemical catalysts and strategies for benzofuran synthesis has received considerable attention (1, 5). Meanwhile, bioactive benzofuran–type natural products are also produced by nature, mainly found in higher plants such as Asteraceae (6), Fabaceae (7) and Moraceae plants (8). Despite the fact that advances have been made in furanocoumarins biosynthesis (9–11), biosynthetic pathways of other benzofuran-type natural products in plants remain largely unexplored, hampering their efficient synthesis via metabolic engineering strategy.

Moracins and their derivatives are a representative family of benzofuran-type natural products characterized by a standard 2-arylbenzofuran skeleton (Fig. 1B) (3, 8). This family of natural products is rich in Moraceae plants and initially isolated as phytoalexins from diseased mulberry (12–14). In addition to their role as chemical weapons in response to biotic stress, moracins have been shown to exhibit various biological activities, including antibacterial (15, 16), antidiabetic (17, 18), antioxidant (19, 20), and anti-inflammatory (20, 21) effects. Biosynthetically, moracins originate from moracin M via diverse prenylation (22, 23) and oxidation (24) and can be further coupled with chalcones via an enzymatic intermolecular Diels-Alder reaction to increase their structural complexity (25, 26). Despite progress in the late-stage biosynthesis of moracins and their derivatives, the mechanism by which Moraceae plants assemble the core benzofuran skeleton of moracin M remains elusive (Fig. 1C).

In this study, we identified three missing enzymes in moracin M biosynthesis and successfully achieved the heterologous synthesis of moracin M in Nicotiana benthamiana. To uncover novel biosynthetic enzymes from crude plant extracts, we developed a label-free thermal proteome profiling (TPP) method. Leveraging this approach, we identified a laccase that catalyzes an intramolecular oxidative cyclization to produce moracin M, demonstrating the practicality of this chemical proteomic strategy for plant natural product biosynthesis studies. In addition, by combining density functional theory (DFT) calculations, molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and mutagenesis experiments, we elucidated the reaction mechanism that involves a distinct copper-mediated radical cyclization. This remarkable laccase could also facilitate the synthesis of other natural product–like benzofuran-containing molecules.

RESULTS

Molecular cloning and functional analysis of the missing enzymes for oxyresveratrol biosynthesis in Morus alba

In the proposed biosynthetic pathway of moracin M (27, 28), the first unknown step in moracin M (3) biosynthesis is the ortho-hydroxylation of p-coumaroyl coenzyme A (CoA) (1) (Fig. 2A). Notably, enzymes catalyzing the same reaction have been identified in Ipomoea batatas and Ruta graveolens, and both belong to 2-oxoglutarate–dependent oxygenases (2ODDs) (29–31). Therefore, we conducted phylogenetic analysis of all 2ODDs in M. alba, along with the functionally similar 2ODDs from other species (fig. S1). Five genes clustered within the same phylogenetic clade as the reported 2ODDs. Among them, CP050226.1-5.19, CP050228.1-231.38, and CP050228.1-213.55 exhibited closer evolutionary relationships to the ortho-hydroxylases of p-coumaroyl/feruloyl CoA (e.g., RgC2′H-1 and Ib2-1-1) than the ortho-hydroxylases of feruloyl CoA (e.g., Ib1-3) and therefore were selected as candidate genes for subsequent analysis (Fig. 2B). However, only CP050228.1-231.38 (renamed as MaC2′H1) was successfully cloned and then heterologously expressed in Escherichia coli (fig. S2). To test its enzymatic activity, we first in situ generated p-coumaroyl CoA (1) from p-coumaric acid (4) using At4CL, a 4-coumarate: CoA ligase from Arabidopsis thaliana (fig. S3) (32), and then added this enzyme and its cosubstrates for activity test. As expected, M. alba CoA 2′-hydroxylase 1 (MaC2′H1) catalyzed the formation of 2,4-dihydroxycinnamonyl CoA (5), which was subsequently hydrolyzed to 2,4-dihydroxycinnamic acid (6) by alkali treatment (Fig. 2C). In contrast, this enzyme showed no activity toward p-coumaric acid (4) (Fig. 2C), demonstrating the importance of the CoA moiety in substrate recognition of MaC2′H1.

The next biosynthetic step is the formation of oxyresveratrol (2) from 2,4-dihydroxycinnamonyl CoA (5), which is proposed to be catalyzed by stilbene synthases. Previous studies have reported that ultraviolet (UV) irradiation can induce the biosynthesis of benzofuran derivatives, including moracin N, and chalcomoracin (33). Accordingly, we subjected M. alba leaves to 1 hour of UVB radiation and harvested the leaf samples at different time points following UVB treatment. We then performed transcriptome analysis to identify stilbene synthases with expression patterns similar to those of MaC2′H1. Consequently, six genes were identified as the candidate genes for oxyresveratrol biosynthesis (Fig. 2D). Further phylogenetic analysis of these six genes suggested that CPCP050232.1-82.75 and CP050232.1-82.74 were potential chalcone synthases, while the other four genes might function as stilbene synthases (fig. S4). Therefore, we attempted to clone these four genes from M. alba cDNA; three were successfully cloned (CP050231.1-42.35, CP050231.39.21, and CP050231.1-39.8). To characterize the function of these three genes, we first transformed 2,4-dihydroxycinnamic acid (6) to its CoA ester (5) using At4CL. Then, the purified enzymes and malonyl-CoA were added to the reaction system after the total consumption of 6. The enzymatic assay showed that all three enzymes produced oxyresveratrol (Fig. 2E), indicating that they are involved in oxyresveratrol biosynthesis. Therefore, we designated CP050231.1-42.35, CP050231.39.21, and CP050231.1-39.8 as M. alba oxyresveratrol synthases 1 to 3 (MaORS1 to MaORS3), respectively. Notably, MaORS1 to MaORS3 show 95.8, 98.2, and 94.0% amino sequence identity with three stilbene synthases from Morus atropurpurea, MaSTS2, MaSTS3, and MaSTS1, respectively (34), indicating that MaORS1 to MaORS3 may also catalyze resveratrol formation from p-coumaroyl CoA (1) as MaSTS1 to MaSTS3. To probe the substrate promiscuity of MaORS1 to MaORS3, p-coumaroyl CoA (1) was in situ generated using At4CL and then incubated with MaORS1 to MaORS3. As expected, all MaORSs exhibited enzymatic activity to produce resveratrol (Fig. 2F).

Development of a TPP method for finding unknown plant natural product biosynthetic enzymes

After elucidating the biosynthetic pathway of oxyresveratrol in M. alba, the key unresolved step is identifying the enzyme catalyzing its intramolecular oxidative cyclization to moracin M. Despite the report of enzymes involved in the oxidative oligomerization of stilbenes (35–37), no enzyme has been reported to catalyze such an intramolecular reaction, precluding homology-based reverse genetics for target gene identification. Our group previously identified the intermolecular Diels-Alderase MaDA (M. alba Diels–Alderase) using a biosynthetic intermediate probe (BIP)–based chemoproteomic strategy (25, 38–40); however, BIP requires derivatization of intermediates, which is challenging for molecules lacking modifiable groups or sensitive to modification. TPP, a label-free approach that detects protein–small molecule interactions via thermal stability shifts (41–44), provides an alternative. Given dynamic enzyme-substrate/product interaction during catalysis (45), we hypothesize that TPP can identify uncharacterized plant biosynthetic enzymes using their substrates/products at the proteome level.

As a proof of concept, we first examined the thermal stability shift of plant enzymes heterologously expressed in E. coli using Western blot (fig. S5A). Given that the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway in A. thaliana has been extensively characterized (46) (fig. S5B), five enzymes in this pathway were selected for heterologous expression and thermal shift assays to validate our hypothesis. After confirming the enzymatic activities of each enzyme during cell lysis (fig. S6), Western blot assays were performed to analyze changes in thermal stability upon substrate/product binding. For O-methyltransferase 1 (OMT1), addition of substrate (quercetin) or product (isorhamnetin) increased thermal stability (fig. S5, C and D). Similarly, flavonol 3-O-glucosyltransferase (UGT78D2) showed elevated melting transition temperature (Tm) values with substrate (quercetin, +9.5°C) or product (quercetin-3-O-glucoside, +7.3°C; fig. S5, E and F). Among 2ODDs, flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H) exhibited increased thermal stability with substrate (dihydroquercetin, ΔTm = 3.7°C) or product (eriodictyol, ΔTm = 6.1°C; fig. S5, G and H), whereas flavonol synthase (FLS) showed no notable shifts with its substrate (dihydroquercetin) or product (quercetin; fig. S7A). Chalcone isomerase (CHI) also displayed no thermal stability changes via protein precipitation–based assays (fig. S7B), but fluorescence-based thermal shift assays detected Tm increases with substrates/products (table S1), indicating substrate/product interactions undetectable by precipitation methods (47).

In summary, among the five enzymes examined, the Tm values of OMT1, UGT78D2, and F3H shifted significantly toward higher temperatures upon incubation with their respective substrates and products. In contrast, FLS and CHI displayed no discernible changes in Tm values using Western blot (fig. S7). Despite certain limitations, these findings suggest that this method may be applicable to plant natural product biosynthesis.

Further validation of the application potential of TPP in searching for plant biosynthetic enzymes

Although thermal shifts of flavonoid biosynthetic enzymes overexpressed in E. coli were observed upon addition of their substrates or products, it is uncertain whether this phenomenon can be replicated in plant lysate, where target enzymes are present at lower abundance and are accompanied by more competing proteins. Given the strong phylogenetic relationship between broccoli and A. thaliana and the greater availability of sufficient quantities of cell lysate, we chose broccoli as a model plant to address this concern. In broccoli, a candidate OMT1 homologous protein can be found, which shows a high sequence identity (93%) and similarity (95%) with the OMT1 from A. thaliana (fig. S8). Therefore, the candidate OMT1 from broccoli was selected as a model to simulate the enzyme discovery process using the TPP strategy.

To verify the presence of OMT1 homologs in the broccoli lysate, we assessed OMT1 enzymatic activity by incubating the lysate with the substrate quercetin and S-adenosyl methionine (SAM). Although quercetin was consumed, no desired product was detected (Fig. 3A), possibly because of the presence of multiple enzymes with activities surpassing that of OMT1. To address this issue, we performed activity-based protein purification to enrich for OMT1 (Fig. 3B). Following preliminary separation by ammonium sulfate precipitation and size exclusion chromatography (SEC), we identified isorhamnetin production in the SEC fractions (Fig. 3A), demonstrating the presence of the target protein in the lysate. This partially purified crude enzyme (PPCE) then underwent TPP, in which we introduced the OMT1 substrate or product into the PPCE and subjected aliquots to heating at seven temperatures. After centrifugation, the soluble fractions were digested and analyzed through label-free quantitative proteomics, enabling the construction of thermal shift curves for each protein and the calculation of Tm values (Fig. 3B). Quantitative thermal stability data for nearly 2000 proteins across seven different temperatures were obtained from the substrate- and product-treated PPCE profiling. Melting points were determined for proteins that met the curve fitting quality criteria (43). Among the proteins with a determined melting point, four proteins, including OMT1 (UniProt ID: Q9FK25), were identified in both substrate- and product-based profiling (Fig. 3, C and D, and fig. S9A). Among them, OMT1 stands out for its most considerable ΔTm value (fig. S9). These results suggest that substrate- or product-based TPP holds great promise for identifying biosynthetic enzymes from plant cell lysis.

Identification of a noncanonical laccase that is responsible for the oxidative cyclization of oxyresveratrol to generate moracin M

After establishing the label-free chemoproteomics method for the discovery of plant biosynthetic enzymes, we envisioned using this approach to elucidating the unknown enzyme responsible for the oxidative cyclization of oxyresveratrol, which generates moracin M (30). To verify this proposal, we collected fresh mulberry leaves and subjected them to low-temperature solvent grinding, ammonium sulfate precipitation, and subsequent redissolution to yield a crude enzyme extract. Afterward, we added oxyresveratrol to the crude enzyme for the activity test. Fortunately, we detected the conversion of oxyresveratrol to moracin M in the crude enzyme (Fig. 4A), confirming that oxyresveratrol is the biosynthetic precursor of moracin M. An anion-exchange chromatography step was performed to further enrich for the target enzymes for TPP. After purification, the enzymatic activity increased markedly by nearly 30-fold (fig. S10). The active PPCE acquired through this process was then used to identify moracin M synthase using the TPP method. We quantified 1061 proteins in the TPP experiment, and 13 proteins exhibited noticeable changes in Tm (ΔTm < −1 or ΔTm > 1) across the two replicates (Fig. 4B and table S4). Among these identified proteins, only one is annotated as an oxidase, specifically laccase from M. alba XP_024020441.1 (MaLAC) (Fig. 4, C and D). Inhibition of MaLAC by a laccase inhibitor markedly reduced the production of moracin M in both the PPCE-catalyzed in vitro reaction (fig. S11A) and the in vivo transformation in the detached leaves of M. alba (fig. S11B), suggesting that MaLAC is the primary enzyme for the benzofuran formation in moracin M biosynthesis. Thus, MaLAC was chosen as the candidate gene for further biochemical characterization.

Given the advantages of operational simplicity and the short protein expression cycle in E. coli, we first recombinantly expressed MaLAC in E. coli to characterize its function (fig. S12A). However, upon adding oxyresveratrol to the reaction system, we were unable to detect any moracin M in the assays (fig. S12B). Considering that laccase from Zea mays had been actively expressed in Pichia pastoris (48), we also attempted to express the MaLAC in P. pastoris. Different from the soluble enzyme obtained from E. coli, the purified MaLAC protein from P. pastoris is highly glycosylated (fig. S13A) and shows enzymatic activity toward the conversion from oxyresveratrol to moracin M (Fig. 4A), indicating that the posttranslational modification, such as glycosylation, might be necessary for the enzymatic activity. The inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) results revealed that MaLAC, similar to other laccases, also binds to cupric ions (fig. S13B). Biochemical characterization of MaLAC showed that the optimal temperature was 30°C (fig. S13C) and the optimal pH value was 6.5 (fig. S13D). We also determined the steady-state kinetic parameters for MaLAC (Fig. 4E). The apparent Michaelis constant KM value of MaLAC for oxyresveratrol was calculated as 1.96 ± 0.25 μΜ with a kcat (turnover number)/KM value of 0.0061 ± 0.00094 μΜ−1 min−1.

With the noncanonical laccase MaLAC in hand, we performed in vivo transformation of p-coumaric acid (4) in N. benthamiana. Coexpression of At4CL with MaC2′H1, along with substrate coinjection, led to the detection of umbelliferone (7), which is the spontaneously cyclized derivative of intermediate 5. This result further supports the role of MaC2′H1 in catalyzing the ortho-hydroxylation of p-coumaroyl CoA. In addition, coexpression of MaORS1 with At4CL and MaC2′H1 in N. benthamiana yielded both oxyresveratrol (2) and resveratrol (8), consistent with the in vitro observations (Fig. 2, E and F). Last, when all three genes were coexpressed along with MaLAC and substrate coinjection, moracin M (3) was detected, confirming the functional involvement of the newly reported genes in the biosynthetic pathway of moracin M (Fig. 4F).

Mechanistic study of MaLAC

Plant laccases are members of the multicopper oxidase family and are typically involved in the oxidative oligomerization of phenylpropanoid to form lignin. To gain further insight into the mechanism underlying the uncommon intramolecular oxidative cyclization reaction, we performed a DFT study to evaluate the Gibbs free energy of all structures involved (Fig. 5A). The previous study revealed that the T1 site copper directly interacts with substrate (48), so we used a truncated T1 Cu(II) as an oxidant. The substrate was oxidized by T1 Cu(II) and deprotonated, generating the radical intermediate Int1, which is favored because of its low energy (−11.4 kcal/mol). Int1 can undergo an intramolecular cyclization reaction to generate radical intermediate Int2 via TS1, with a free energy barrier of 21.9 kcal/mol. Int2 can be further oxidized by T1 Cu(II) and deprotonated to generate the benzofuran skeleton, which is thermodynamically favored. From the DFT study, we found that the whole reaction can easily proceed both kinetically and thermodynamically. Therefore, the enzyme’s interaction with the substrate is critical for this enzymatic reaction.

A structure-activity relationship study was conducted between MaLAC and its substrates (Fig. 5, B and C). Compared to oxyresveratrol, compound 9, which lacks the B ring, completely lost activity, while compound 10, containing a phenyl ring without hydroxyl groups, retained 45% activity (Fig. 5C). Consistently, derivatives of 10 (i.e., 14 to 17) were also enzymatically active, and their cyclized products could be prepared in reasonable yields (see the Supplementary Materials). These results suggested that MaLAC prefers substrates with an electron-rich aromatic B ring. Meanwhile, the two hydroxyl groups in the A ring were found to be indispensable for the MaLAC-catalyzed oxidative cyclization reaction, as the removal or methylation of either of these groups completely abolished enzymatic activity (8, 11, 12, and 13) (Fig. 5, B and C). These results are consistent with our DFT calculations, which indicate that these hydroxyls are essential for T1-site copper-catalyzed O-radical formation, which initiates the intramolecular cyclization reaction.

To gain more insight into the catalytic mechanism of MaLAC, we built a homology model of MaLAC through the SWISS-MODEL workspace using the 1.80-Å crystal structure of Zea mays laccase 3 (ZmLAC3)-sinapyl alcohol complex [Protein Data Bank (PDB) ID: 6KLI] (45) as the template. The substrate was docked into the same binding pocket as in ZmLAC3, where the A ring of the substrate is positioned toward the catalytic T1-site Cu. Zoom-in analysis indicated that the side chains of five residues (Arg162, Ile344, Val442, Leu445, and Trp518) extend into the substrate-binding pocket and thus may interact with the substrate (Fig. 5D). Among them, Ile344, Val442, and Trp518 form a conserved hydrophobic patch in plant laccases (fig. S14A) (48). Each of the five residues was mutated to alanine to probe the role of these three residues in catalyzing this reaction. Enzymatic assays revealed a significant decrease in the activity of these three variants, particularly the Ile344→Ala and Val442→Ala variants, which completely lost their enzymatic activity (Fig. 5E), supporting the fact that the hydrophobic interactions between them and the substrate are important for enzymatic activity. Besides these residues, L445 was also identified as an essential hydrophobic residue in the substrate binding pocket since the L445E mutation completely abolished the catalytic activity of MaLAC, while the Leu445→Ala and Leu445→Val mutations retained 5.3 and 81% activity, respectively (Fig. 5E). In addition, the R162A mutant results in a 70% loss of enzymatic activity, which might be attributed to the important role of R162 in stabilizing substrate binding via forming a cation-π interaction with the B ring (fig. S14B). Together, we have identified the key residues that position the substrate close to the catalytic copper (T1 Cu). We propose that, due to strong interactions between the substrate and MaLAC, Int1 binding is stabilized within the pocket, thereby permitting intramolecular cyclization, followed by a second single-electron oxidation to yield a benzofuran ring from stilbene.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this work provides the first comprehensive elucidation of the biosynthetic pathway of moracin M by uncovering three previously missing enzymes, thereby bridging key knowledge gaps in the metabolic engineering of bioactive benzofuran–type natural products. Our integrated approach, combining homology-based screening and transcriptome analysis, identified the key ortho-hydroxylase MaC2H´1 and three functionally redundant stilbene synthases, MaORS1 to MaORS3, which collectively convert p-coumaroyl CoA into oxyresveratrol via the unstable intermediate 2,4-dihydroxycinnamoyl CoA. Notably, the most crucial challenge—and our central innovation—was the discovery of the enzyme catalyzing the oxidative cyclization of oxyresveratrol to form moracin M, a reaction with little prior biochemical precedent. By using innovative activity–based protein purification and label-free chemoproteomic strategies, we identified MaLAC, a noncanonical laccase, and characterized its novel catalytic mechanism and substrate versatility. These findings fundamentally expand the synthetic biology toolbox for benzofuran scaffold assembly. Ultimately, we successfully reconstructed the entire moracin M pathway in N. benthamiana, laying the foundation for the efficient production of moracins via synthetic biology.

Laccases (p-diphenol: dioxygen oxidoreductase, EC 1.10.3.2) are polyphenol oxidases characterized by the presence of one mononuclear copper (T1 Cu) site and one trinuclear copper site (49). Plant laccases play essential roles in physiological and biochemical processes, including lignin synthesis (50), wound healing (49), and polymerization of phenolic compounds (51). Typically, laccases catalyze one-electron oxidation of substrates to initiate intermolecular radical coupling reactions, while MaLAC catalyzes two-electron oxidation events to build new C─O bonds intramolecularly. Phylogenetically, MaLAC is highly related to two known plant laccases, Arabidopsis thaliana laccase-15 (AtLAC15) and ZmLAC3, which are involved in lignin synthesis (48) and the oxidative polymerization of flavonoids (51), respectively (fig. S15). Therefore, MaLAC might also catalyze intermolecular radical coupling reactions to form lignin or flavonoid polymers. The enzymatic assays showed that MaLAC exhibits similar enzymatic activity on the lignin monomers (i.e., coniferyl and sinapyl alcohols) and piceatannol as the typical plant laccase ZmLAC3 (fig. S16), suggesting that MaLAC may play similar roles in physiological and biochemical processes as ZmLAC3. In contrast, MaLAC mainly catalyzes intramolecular oxidative cyclization of oxyresveratrol to synthesize moracin M, while maintaining weak activity toward the oxidative dimerization of oxyresveratrol to form corresponding dimers, as observed for ZmLAC3 (fig. S16). Considering that moracins are exclusively found in Moraceae plants (3), MaLAC is probably a newly functionalized laccase during the evolution of Moraceae plants.

Although many methods are currently available for identifying missing biosynthetic enzymes for plant natural products (52, 53), the TPP method may be the most practical for finding this functionally distinct laccase from the mulberry tree. For example, transcriptome analysis is currently considered one of the most powerful methods to identify plant natural product biosynthetic enzymes (54–56). However, when using this method, we typically need to make an initial inference about the enzyme type of interest to narrow the candidate genes to a manageable number for functional characterization. In our case, even if an oxidase/dehydrogenase is believed to be involved in the oxidative reaction, it is difficult to determine which specific type of oxidative enzyme is engaged because there are no reference enzymes with similar functions. The TPP method, by contrast, does not require consideration of this issue in advance. Therefore, this method can be complementary to the discovery of functionally novel enzymes from an unexpected protein family. Although interactions between enzymes and their substrates or products occur, not all can be detected by the TPP method. Therefore, this method is subject to limitations. Some proteins lack a Tm value and do not aggregate or precipitate even upon denaturation (47), making them insensitive to the TPP method. Using CHI as an example, we detected interactions between CHI and its substrate or product using the fluorescence-based thermal shift assay (57) (table S1). However, this phenomenon could not be detected by protein precipitate–based thermal shift assay (fig. S7B).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

General

The primers used in this study are listed in table S1 and were synthesized by Xiang Hong Company. DNA fragments were amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using standard thermocycling protocols with TransStart FastPfu DNA polymerase from Transgene Biotech. The expression vector was assembled using the ClonExpress II One-Step Cloning Kit from Vazyme Biotech. DNA sequencing was conducted by RuiBiotech Company. Protein quantification was conducted using a NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Transcriptome analysis of M. alba

The fresh leaves of M. alba were treated with UVB irradiation for 1 hour using a protocol similar to that previously described (33) and then incubated in petri dishes with water. The leaves of M. alba were collected at different time points (0, 1, 4, 13, 24, and 48 hours) and submitted to RNA sequencing. The preliminary coding sequence (CDS) assemblies were further processed with tgicl. CDSs from all time points were combined, and redundant sequences with an identity greater than 85% were removed using CD-HIT v4.6.7, yielding the final transcript set. Protein translation and functional annotation were performed with TransDecoder v5.5.0 using the UniProt-SwissProt and Pfam_A.V32.hmm databases. The M. alba genome (ASM1206604v3) was downloaded from National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), yielding a fasta file. Expression of enzymes in E. coli

Full-length cDNAs of OMT1, F3H, UGT78D2, chalcone synthase (CHS), FLS, and At4CL were obtained from The Arabidopsis Information Resource database (http://arabidopsis.org) and then subcloned into a pET28a plasmid containing the 6× His tag. A reaction mixture that contained 50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.0), 5 mM adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP), 5 mM MgCl2, 200 μM p-coumaric acid, 4 mM CoA, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 20 μg of At4CL in a final volume of 100 μl was incubated at pH 7.0 and 30°C for 3 hours. Transient expression of candidate genes in N. benthamiana

Agrobacterium strains with recombinant plasmid containing candidate genes were grown in 15 ml of LB with rifampicin (25 μg/ml), gentamycin (50 μg/ml), and kanamycin (50 μg/ml) for 24 hours at 30°C. Subsequently, the cells were collected by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min and resuspended in 5 ml of induction buffer containing 10 mM MES (pH 5.7), 10 mM MgCl2, and 100 μM acetosyringone. The suspension was further diluted with induction buffer to an OD600 of 0.2 and incubated at room temperature for 3 hours. The strains were infiltrated into the underside of 4- to 5-week-old N. benthamiana leaves using a 1-ml needleless syringe. For each mixture, three leaves from different plants were used as biological replicates. After 3 days, 1 mM substrate in 10 mM MES (pH 5.7) and 10 mM MgCl2 buffer with 1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was infiltrated into the undersides of previously Agrobacterium-infiltrated leaves using a 1-ml needleless syringe. The leaves were harvested 1 day after substrate infiltration, then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and lyophilized using a freeze dryer for metabolite analysis.

Metabolite extraction for N. benthamiana transient expression experiments

The freeze-dried leaves from N. benthamiana transient expression experiments were homogenized using a cryogenic grinder (JXFSTPRP-CL, Shanghai Jing Xin) with one 3-mm-diameter stainless steel bead per sample, shaken at 60 Hz for 25 s. Methanol (50 μl/mg of freeze-dried leaves) was added, and the samples were sonicated at room temperature for 30 min; this process was repeated three times to extract metabolites. The extracts were then centrifuged at 15,800 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatants were filtered through 0.22-μm hydrophobic polytetrafluoroethylene syringe filters before analysis by high-resolution LC-MS. Metabolites were identified by comparing their retention times and mass fragments to those of standard compounds.

LC-MS analysis of metabolites from N. benthamiana transient expression experiments

Samples were analyzed by reversed-phase liquid chromatography on a Quadrupole-time-of-flight LC–tandem MS (MS/MS) System-Vion (Waters) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (50 mm in length, 2.1 mm in inner diameter, and 1.7 μm in particle size; Waters). The samples run at a flow rate of 0.3 ml/min and a temperature of 40°C. A gradient elution method was used, with a mixture of water (A) and MeCN (B). Elution began with 85% A and 15% B for the initial 2 min, then transitioned to 10% A and 90% B over 4 min, held at this gradient for 0.5 min, and lastly reached 15% B in 0.5 min. The MS data were collected in negative mode (electrospray ionization−) [parameters: mass range, 50 to 1000 mass/charge ratio (m/z); capillary, 2.5 kV; drying gas, 250°C; 600 l/hour; con gas flow, 50 l/hour; source temperature, 100°C; 500 ms per spectrum]. For MS/MS analysis, 25-eV collision energies were used for umbeliferone, 25-eV collision energies were used for resveratrol, 20-eV collision energies were used for oxyresveratrol, and 30-eV collision energies were used for moracin M.

Thermal shift assay with the E. coli lysates expressing the target enzymes

For the experimental group samples, 200 μg of lysates expressing the target protein and 50 μM substrates (or products) were added to a final volume of 50 μl. In the control group samples, the substrates (or products) were replaced with an equal volume of solvent (ensuring that the concentration of DMSO does not exceed 1%), while keeping the other components consistent. The samples were incubated at room temperature for 20 min and then heated them within an appropriate temperature range for 3 min. The centrifuge was set to 20,000g for 20 min, and the supernatant was collected for SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and Western blot analysis. The obtained results were processed using the ImageJ software.

Fluorescence-based thermal shift assay

The fluorescence-based thermal shift assay was conducted in a 96-well plate. The purified CHI was diluted to a final protein concentration of 0.5 mg/ml in a solution comprising enzyme buffer [10 mM tris and 50 mM NaCl (pH 8.0)] and 2.5× SYPRO Orange (a stock solution of 5000 × SYPRO Orange from Sigma-Aldrich ref. S5692) in a total volume of 25 μl. The samples were then subjected to thermal denaturation using a real-time PCR machine (Bio-Rad) with a temperature range of 25° to 95°C at a heating rate of 1°C/min. Protein unfolding was tracked by the rise in fluorescence emitted by the SYPRO Orange probe (57).

Preparation of the PPCE from broccoli for TPP

Sixty grams of fresh broccoli, 300 ml of 100 mM sodium phosphate at pH 7.5, 1 mM PMSF, and ~100 ml of ice were added to a homogenizer and ground them for 5 min, followed by sonication (2-s on and 2-s off at 40% power) for 20 min. The ground liquid was filtered through eight layers of gauze to collect the filtrate, and solid ammonium sulfate was then gradually added to achieve a final concentration of 40% saturation. The mixture was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 30 min, and the supernatant was collected. Solid ammonium sulfate was continuously added to the supernatant until the concentration reaches 75% saturation, followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 30 min to collect the protein precipitate. Subsequently, the protein precipitate was solubilized with 50 ml of 100 mM sodium phosphate at pH 7.5. The dissolved protein was filtered through a 0.22-μm filter and then separated on a Superdex 75 column, collecting 1.8 ml per tube. Quercetin and SAM are used as substrates to test the activity of OMT1. Fractions with higher activity were collected as PPCE for TPP experiments.

Preparation of the PPCE from M. alba leaves for TPP

A total of 120 g of fresh or frozen mulberry leaves, 800 ml of 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer at pH 7.0, 25 mM sodium ascorbate, 1 mM PMSF, and ~100 ml of ice were added to a homogenizer and ground for 5 min, followed by ultrasonication (2-s on and 2-s off at 40% power) for 20 min. The resulting mixture was filtered through eight layers of gauze to collect the filtrate. Solid ammonium sulfate was gradually added to the lysate until the concentration reaches 40% saturation. The mixture was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 30 min, and the supernatant was collected. Solid ammonium sulfate was continuously added to the supernatant until the concentration ranges between 75 and 100% saturation. The mixture was centrifuged again at 7000 rpm for 30 min, and the protein precipitate was collected. The protein precipitate was resuspended in 200 ml of 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer at pH 7.0. Dialysis was performed with 5 liters of 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer at pH 7.0. The mixture was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 30 min, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was passed through a Q column, and oxyresveratrol was used as the substrate to assess the target activity. Fractions with higher activity was collected as PPCE for TPP experiments.

TPP experiment with PPCE

For the experimental group examples, 50 μg of PPCE and 100 μM quercetin (or 50 isorhamnetin) were added for OMT1 identification or 200 μM oxyresvetrol for MaLAC identification to a 15-μl system. In the control group, an equal volume of solvent (ensuring that the concentration of DMSO did not exceed 1%) was added to replace the corresponding compound. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 20 min, followed by heating within an appropriate temperature range for 3 min. The sample was centrifuged at 20,000g for 20 min, and the supernatant was collected for in-solution enzymatic digestion and LC-MS/MS analysis.

In-solution digestion

A sixfold volume of cold acetone was incorporated into the sample for overnight precipitation at −20°C. The mixture was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was carefully aspirated using a pipette, followed by drying of the residual acetone. Subsequently, the protein was solubilized with 20 μl of 8 M urea in 100 mM tris-HCl at pH 8.5. After the precipitated protein is completely dissolved, tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine was added to a final concentration of 5 mM, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 to 30 min. Iodoacetamide was then added to a final concentration of 10 mM, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 to 30 min. The urea concentration was diluted to 2 M by adding 60 μl of 100 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.5). Trypsin activity was enhanced by adding calcium chloride (CaCl2) to a final concentration of 1 mM, and carbamylation was reduced by adding methylamine to a final concentration of 20 mM. Last, trypsin should be added at a mass ratio ranging from 20:1 to 100:1 and incubated at 37°C for 12 to 18 hours of enzyme digestion. The reaction was terminated with a final concentration of 5% (v/v) formic acid. Approximately 500 ng of peptide fragments was taken for MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS and data analysis

The peptides were separated by Thermo Fisher Scientific Easy-nLC 1200 system at a flow rate of 300 nl/min. Phase A consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid, while phase B comprised 80% MeCN with 0.1% formic acid. The separation gradient lasted 75 min: Phase A decreased from 100 to 94% within the first minute, then decreased further to 56% over the next 60 min, dropped to 0% over 5 min, and remained at 0% for 9 min.

The Thermo Fisher Scientific Q-Exactive Plus mass spectrometer is used to collect MS data. Data are acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode, capturing the 15 most abundant signals per cycle. Orbitrap mass analyzer with a mass resolution setting of 70,000. For secondary mass spectrum scans, the resolution is set to 17,500, with an AGC (Automatic Gain Control) target of 1 × 105 and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. The isolation window for secondary scans is 1.6 m/z, and the normalized collision energy is set to 28. Ions with charges of 1+, 8+, and greater than 8+ and those with indeterminate valences are excluded. The dynamic exclusion time is set at 40 s.

After obtaining the raw data, Proteome Discoverer (v2.4, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for protein identification and quantification. The MS/MS spectra were searched by Sequest HT. The A. thaliana database was downloaded from UniProt (www.uniprot.org) and used to identify OMT1. The Morus notabilis database was downloaded from NCBI (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and used to identify the moracin M synthase. Trypsin was selected as the protease, allowing up to two missed cleavages. Proteins were quantified by averaging the top three peptide MS1 areas, yielding raw protein abundances across samples. Subsequently, the abundance data were analyzed using the TPP package in R, following the reported method (43).

Expression and purification of MaLAC and its mutants in P. pastoris

The full-length cDNA of MaLAC encoded a protein comprising a signal peptide of 31 amino acids and a mature protein of 547 amino acids. The reaction mixture contained 200 μM oxyresveratrol or resveratrol, 20 mM sodium phosphate (pH 6.5), and either 60 μg of crude enzyme or 0.31 μg of purified MaLAC (or its mutants). To investigate the effect of pH and temperature on MaLAC activity, enzymatic reactions were conducted in various buffers: citric acid–sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.0 to 6.5), Na2HPO4-NaH2PO4 buffer (pH 6.0 to 8.0), and tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.0 to 9.0). Homology modeling and molecular docking

The homology model of MaLAC, excluding the signal peptide of 31 amino acids, was generated using SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/) with the crystal structure of ZmLAC3 complexed with sinapyl (PDB ID: 6KLI) as the template, which has a 56.51% sequence identity. Molecular docking of oxyresveratrol was performed using Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) 2022.02. Oxyresveratrol was placed into the binding pocket of MaLAC based on its alignment with ZmLAC3 (PDB ID: 6KLI). Molecular docking was performed using standard parameters, and the final output was in PDB format.

DFT calculation

Gaussian 16 was used for all DFT calculations. The geometric optimization and vibration analysis of the molecules were performed at the uB3LYP-D3(BJ) (58) level. The def2SVP (59) basis set was used. The transition states were confirmed by a single imaginary frequency, consistent with the reaction coordinate. At the same time, the Goodvibes 3.0.1 (60) software developed by the Paton laboratory was used to calculate the energy contributions of the vibrational and rotational parts of the free energy using a quasi-resonance approximation based on the vibration analysis results. The single-point energies of each intermediate and transition state were calculated at the uB3LYP-D3(BJ) level with the def2TZVP (59) basis set and the Solvation Model Based on Solute Electron Density (SMD) (61) to account for the effect of solvent water. The CREST 2.11 (62) software developed by the Grimme laboratory performed a conformational search, ensuring that the lowest energy was obtained for each intermediate or transition state.

MD simulation and molecular mechanics/poisson–boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) analysis

The protonation state of MaLAC in a neutral solution was checked by Propka3 (63). MD simulations in explicit water were performed using the pmemd module of the Amber20 and AmberTools21 (64) packages. For the protein scaffold, the ff19SB force field was used. OPC (“optimal” point charge) parameters were applied for water molecules. The parameter of all Cu atoms and the residue directly connected to Cu atoms was built by the MCPB.py module of the AmberTools21. The gaff parameters were applied for the ligand. The partial charge of substrate was set to fit the transition state’s electrostatic potential generated at HF/6-31G(d) level (65). The complex was immersed in a preequilibrated octahedral box with a 10-Å buffer of OPC water molecules, as implemented in Leap. All systems were neutralized by adding explicit counterions (Na+ or Cl−). A 10-Å cutoff was applied to Lennard-Jones and electrostatic interactions. A constraint with 500 kcal/mol per square angstrom was applied to maintain the distance between T1 Cu and the ligand. Three independent MD simulations were performed according to the following steps: (i) Minimization was performed with a maximum cycle of 10,000 and with the steepest descent algorithm for the first 5000 cycles. Positional restraints of 3.0 kcal/mol per square angstrom were applied to the protein. (ii) Minimization was performed with a maximum cycle of 10,000 and with the steepest descent algorithm for the first 5000 cycles. (iii) A 1-ns heating process was performed with a periodic boundary for constant volume with the SHAKE algorithm (all bonds containing hydrogen were constrained). Temperature was increased from 0 to 303 K using Langevin dynamics with a collision frequency of 2 ps−1. Positional restraints of 3.0 kcal/mol per square angstrom were applied on protein and ligands. (iv) A 0.5-ns equilibrium process was performed with a periodic boundary for a constant pressure of 1 atm and a constant temperature of 303 K to adjust the system’s density with 1-fs step size. (v) A 200-ns production was performed with a periodic boundary for a constant pressure of 1 atm and a temperature of 303 K with 1-fs step size. The MMPBSA.py module in AmberTools21 was used to calculate the binding energies of ligands and proteins from MD simulation trajectories.

Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry

Each protein sample was treated with 200 μl of nitric acid (MOS, Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor, grade) and 20 μl of hydrogen peroxide (MOS grade) and then incubated at 70°C for 1 to 5 hours. Afterward, the samples were diluted with double-distilled H2O, and their metal contents were quantified by ICP-MS using an Elan DRC-e spectrometer (PerkinElmer). Protein concentrations were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer.
