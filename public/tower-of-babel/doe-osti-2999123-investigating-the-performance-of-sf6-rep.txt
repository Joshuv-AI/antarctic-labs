ABSTRACT
High voltage switches are essential components in pulsed power systems, where consistent and
reliable performance is crucial—particularly as the field explores alternatives to SF₆ as an insulating
gas. This project examines the self-break voltage distributions of various gases, with a focus on the
low-voltage discharges observed in the lower tail of these distributions. Experimental results
revealed that the specific housing design influenced the self-break behavior. Among the tested gases,
air demonstrated a more favorable overall distribution compared to SF₆, albeit requiring higher
operating pressures. However, air also exhibited a greater likelihood of extremely low-voltage
dropouts, raising concerns about its suitability as a direct replacement for SF₆. Notably, all gases
tested showed a higher-than-expected probability of low-voltage events when considering the tail of
the distribution rather than the bulk behavior.

1. INTRODUCTION
 Current pulsed power facilities rely on sulfur hexafluoride (SF₆) as the insulating medium in
high-voltage switches due to its exceptional voltage hold-off capabilities. While SF₆ provides
favorable electrical properties, its use poses several challenges, including safety risks (e.g.,
asphyxiation in the event of a release), supply chain limitations, and evolving state and federal
regulations that may restrict its availability. As future pulsed power facilities are expected to
incorporate a greater number of switches, the demand for SF₆ is projected to increase significantly.
To address these challenges, research into alternative insulating gases is critical for developing
switches that are not only more reliable but also capable of enhanced switching performance.
Furthermore, the increased number of switches amplifies the risk of prefiring events, which can
compromise experimental shots and damage the machine. The primary objective of this
investigation is to evaluate the performance of various gases based on key metrics, including:
 • High dielectric strength—allows the gas to hold off a large voltage at as low of a pressure as
 possible to reduce the mechanical requirements of the switch.
 • Tight breakdown distribution—consistent breakdown voltage, which depends on the gas
 chemistry and gas-electrode interactions.
 • High conductivity and low inductance when broken down—this minimizes the gas’s effect
 on the overall circuit, approaching ideal switch behavior.
 • Non-toxic and non-toxic byproducts—this avoids leading to toxicity hazards when a leak
 occurs.
 • Non-reactive and non-reactive byproducts—this avoids material compatibility issues for the
 gas that could influence the larger system.
 • Voltage holdoff—this determines the pressure required to operate the switch at a given
 voltage.
 • Switch conditioning—how to best condition switches for a given operating condition.
 • Voltage distribution—this determines the probability of low dropouts in a self-break mode
 and the possibility of pre-fires or no-fires in a triggered mode.
 • Switch longevity—how do the various parameters vary over the lifetime of the switch, where
 a basic lifetime ranges from 500–2,500 shots or higher (rough lifetime of the Z Marx and
 MTG switches).

2. RESULTS
 The Switch-a-roo automated test stand was used to measure self-break voltage distributions
across various gases. The system incrementally increased voltage in 500 V steps every 0.5 seconds
until a discharge occurred. After each discharge, the gas was purged and replenished to the target
pressure. Tests were conducted with two energy configurations, adjusting capacitors between 20 and
40 nF to deliver approximately 200 to 400 J per discharge. All experiments utilized modified L3 T-
670 field distortion spark gap switches with C26000 cartridge brass electrodes, consistent with the
design of Z Marx trigger generators.
 Testing began with a “stairstep” sequence of 25 shots at eight pressures, ranging from
nominal 60 kV to 100 kV self-break conditions, followed by lifetime testing of up to 10,000 shots at
the nominal 100 kV self-break condition to evaluate long-term switch behavior. Stairstep data was
used to establish a linear Paschen fit for lifetime data, correcting for minor pressure fluctuations
during testing. Analysis focused on the percentage of self-break events rather than self-break voltage
to account for these variations.
 Initially, a T-508 switch was installed in Switch-a-roo but was excluded from further testing
due to breakdown limitations (~200 kV at 5 psig), which prevented operation at reasonable
pressures near Z Marx bank levels. Subsequent testing exclusively used T-670 switches with a 0.75
cm gap and elliptical profile midplane (part # J00043-002).
 Results were first analyzed on a switch-by-switch basis before combining data sets for gas-
by-gas comparisons. While each switch’s sequence and distribution were examined individually, the
analysis of outliers—particularly low dropouts—was reserved for aggregated gas comparisons across
switches to better capture trends, as individual switches may have limited outlier data.

2.1. SF₆ Switches
 Seven switches have been run with SF6. The first switch underwent a conditioning process
of 4 stairsteps of 25 shots each from 60 kV to 120 kV self-break voltages over 8 steps (2.5 to 20
psig). This was followed by 2 stairsteps of 200 shots each at nominal self-break voltages of 80, 100,
and 120 kV (7.5, 12.5, and 20 psig) to investigate the conditioned performance of the switch. This
was followed by another stairstep of 25 shots each from 60 kV to 120 kV to determine if the
nominal self-break voltages had deviated. Then 1000 shots were conducted at the nominal 100 kV
condition to observe variations in the breakdown voltage over time. A final stairstep of 25 shots
each from 60 kV to 120 kV was conducted to determine if the nominal self-break voltages had
deviated.
 The results are presented in Figure 1, which shows the breakdown voltage data. Figure 2
illustrates the percent of self-break, calculated using the full dataset for the switch. The median at
each pressure step was used to derive an expected linear Paschen self-break curve, enabling the
calculation of the percent of self-break for the switch. The box plot showing the distribution is the
standard one MatLab outputs with the box marking the 25%–75% region of the distribution, the red
line denoting the median, the whiskers noting the farthest points that are within 1.5 of the IQR of
the box, and all “outliers” being noted as individual points beyond the whiskers.

 Overall, the switch performed as expected, with higher standard deviations observed at
higher pressures. It is unclear whether conditioning occurred during the first 200 shots, as pressure
control was less precise due to Switch-a-roo settings. No significant changes in the means (<2%)
were observed during the subsequent two stairsteps or the final stairsteps after additional shots.
 In the literature, self-break distributions for gas switches have been modeled using Weibull
[1], three-parameter Weibull [2], [3], and normal distributions [4], [5]. QQ plots were generated for
these three distributions using data from 1,113 shots at the nominal 100 kV breakdown condition,
based on the percentage of self-break rather than breakdown voltage (to minimize the influence of
slight pressure variations at breakdown). The results show that none of these distributions fit the
data well (Figure 3). Notably, the three-parameter Weibull distribution converged to a standard two-
parameter Weibull.
 To further investigate, the top and bottom 5% of the data were excluded to remove
potential high and low tails that might not follow the core distribution. Even with this adjustment,

 A third SF₆ switch was tested at higher energy levels, using 40 nF capacitors instead of the
20 nF capacitors used previously. The test began with four stairsteps of 25 shots each, ranging from
60 kV to 100 kV self-break voltages over eight pressure steps (2 to 12.5 psig). This was followed by
a stairstep of 200 shots each at nominal self-break voltages of 70, 80, 90, and 100 kV (3.5, 6.5, 9.5,
and 12.5 psig).
 The initial stairsteps produced varying results (Figure 9 and Figure 10). The first and fourth
stairsteps showed significant variation in breakdown voltage, while the second and third stairsteps
exhibited comparatively little variation. This large variation persisted during the 200-shot stairstep
and continued throughout the subsequent testing, which totaled over 10,000 shots. Notably, the
percent of self-break was higher during the second day of testing (shots 201–706), but this trend did
not continue in subsequent days (Figure 11). After 5,000 shots, the variation in breakdown voltage
appeared to increase further (Figure 12). After completing all 10,494 shots, the housing interior was
observed to have a black discoloration (Figure 13). As with previous tests, the data does not fit any
of the tested distributions well. While a Weibull distribution might approximate the core data if
more of the tails were excluded, the results suggest the presence of at least two underlying
distributions in the data (Figure 14).

 The fourth SF₆ switch will be discussed alongside air switch #5, as it represents a
continuation of that switch using SF₆ instead of air for later shots. This comparison aims to
determine whether the switch maintained the tighter distribution observed with air or exhibited the
larger variance noted in SF₆ switch #3.
 The fifth SF₆ switch was tested for 4,140 shots (Figure 15 and Figure 16) at a nominal
breakdown voltage of 80 kV with higher energy (40 nF capacitors). This test was conducted to
evaluate whether the switch behaved differently when charged to a lower voltage compared to SF₆
switch #3. The spread in self-break voltages was smaller than that observed in SF₆ switch #3 under
the same capacitance but higher charge voltage, suggesting that the higher voltages in the prior
switch may have contributed to the larger spread in self-break voltages. However, several low
dropouts were still observed, including one at 58.5% of self-break, a value comparable to those seen
in SF₆ switch #3. Testing on this switch was stopped early due to updates being pushed to the
Switch-a-roo software. As with other SF₆ switches, no single distribution adequately fits the data
(Figure 17). Additionally, the data for this switch was fairly discretized due to the voltage stepping of
the Switch-a-roo system.

3. GAS ANALYSIS

3.1. Summary and Baselining
 To summarize the data, Table 1 compares various useful metrics across all switches. Instead
of relying solely on breakdown voltage for comparisons across switches and pressures, the percent
of self-break (%SB) is used. This metric is defined as:
 𝑉𝑏𝑟
                                      %𝑆𝐵 = 100          # (1)
 𝑉𝑆𝐵
where 𝑉𝑆𝐵 represents the Paschen-defined self-break voltage and 𝑉𝑏𝑟 is the breakdown voltage.
Determining 𝑉𝑆𝐵 is not straightforward, and several methods were evaluated (Figure 70) 1:
 1. Using literature data: This approach yielded poor fits for SF₆ and N₂ and slightly
 overestimated results for air. No literature data was available for the specific SF₆/N₂
 blend tested.
 2. Using a single stairstep for a given gas: This method was skewed, particularly for air,
 due to apparent break-in or conditioning effects that were not accounted for.
 3. Using all 25-shot stairsteps for a given gas: This method showed potential bias from
 conditioning effects or low dropouts (e.g., in N₂) and produced poor results at lower
 pressures.
 4. Using all data for a given gas or gas/capacitance combination: This approach was
 heavily influenced by the large number of data points at the nominal 100 kV condition,
 leading to skewed fits. For N₂ and SF₆/N₂, this method was identical to method #3 due to
 limited test conditions.
 5. Using all data for each switch independently: For switches with no pressure variation,
 data from a previous switch with matching gas and capacitance was used. This method
 was identical to method #4 for N₂ and SF₆/N₂ due to the same testing conditions.
 Given that housing variations were found to influence results (e.g., air switch #7), method #5
was selected for further analysis. Additionally, the median was determined to be a more reliable
metric than the mean, as the data distribution was skewed.

3.2. Bulk Distribution Fitting
There are several approaches to fitting multiple distributions to data, including:

 1. Fitting a mixture of two or more probability functions [6].
 2. Splitting the domain into regions, each modeled by different probability distributions [7].
 3. Fitting the data and applying a Pareto tail to the lower end of the distribution [8], [9].

 To improve the fit of the distribution to the bulk data, a MATLAB routine was developed to fit
the main distribution to the bulk of the data while applying a Pareto tail to the lower tail. The
probability at which the Pareto tail begins is treated as a parameter within the MATLAB routine.
QQ plots for each gas-capacitance combination are shown in the figures below, comparing fits using
a single distribution versus a distribution with a Pareto tail:

3.3. Distribution Tail Fitting
 To better analyze the low-probability tail, which describes the likelihood of pre-fires in
triggered systems, a distribution can be fitted to the data below a threshold value, while data above
this threshold is treated as censored. The censored data is assumed to have failed either due to the
same mechanism as the fitted tail or due to other factors. As a starting point, only data classified as
low outliers was selected for analysis. Outliers were defined as values greater than three median
absolute deviations below the median of the data. Alternative methods for identifying outliers (e.g.,
values >3 standard deviations below the mean or 1.5×IQR below the lower quartile) produced
similar results. The CDF of the outliers is shown in Figure 79.
These outliers were then fitted to four distributions:
 • Normal distribution
 • Generalized Pareto distribution
 • Two-parameter Weibull distribution
 • Three-parameter Weibull distribution
Data points higher than the outliers were treated as censored during the fitting process. The results
are presented in the following figures:
 • Figure 80: SF₆ with 20 nF capacitors
 • Figure 81: SF₆ with 40 nF capacitors
 • Figure 82: Air with 20 nF capacitors
 • Figure 83: Air with 40 nF capacitors
 • Figure 84: SF₆/N₂ with 20 nF capacitors
 • Figure 85: N₂ with 20 nF capacitors
 • Figure 86: N₂/O₂ with 40 nF capacitors

 This analysis attempts to estimate the likelihood of very low-probability dropouts if the tail
follows a single distribution. However, the analysis relies on several assumptions that may not hold
true, including:
 1. The outlier points originate from a single distribution.
 2. These points sufficiently represent the tail for fitting purposes.
 3. No other low-probability breakdown mechanisms contribute to voltages this low or lower
 (i.e., the outliers belong to a secondary tail distribution rather than the main “core”
 distribution).

 For the data sets analyzed, none of the distributions adequately describe the tail behavior. The
generalized Pareto distribution, commonly used for modeling distribution tails, provides the closest
fit but still fails to fully capture the tail in any of the data sets.

4. CONSLUSIONS
 High voltage gas switches were tested with various gases and energy depositions for
approximately 10,000 shots to evaluate their self-break voltage distributions over the lifetime of the
switches. Analysis revealed that fitting standard statistical distributions to the measured data
significantly underestimates the low-probability tail, which is critical for predicting pre-fires. This
finding highlights the importance of extended testing beyond a limited number of shots (e.g., 500–
1000 shots) to accurately capture the low-probability tail and assess the true likelihood of pre-fires.
Additionally, the housing was found to play a significant role in the self-break voltage distribution,
with older housings exhibiting a markedly higher spread in self-break voltages. However, housing
effects were not consistently tracked across all tests, introducing variability into the results.
 The study also uncovered inconclusive or conflicting observations regarding conditioning
effects. While no clear conditioning effect was observed over 10,000 shots, swapping housings with
the same electrodes resulted in distributions that did not align with previous tests using the same
housing (e.g., air switch #7, Figure 44 and Figure 45). This discrepancy suggests that factors beyond
conditioning may influence switch performance. Furthermore, the self-break distributions contained
numerous low dropouts that were not explained by the core distribution, yet these dropouts are
critical for scaling to larger systems, as they correspond to pre-fires. A deeper understanding of these
low dropouts is essential for evaluating the impact of gas selection on the performance of large
pulsed-power systems

 The analysis of arc resistance in spark gap switches revealed several key insights into the
behavior of the system and the impact of inductance estimation and waveform fitting. Resistance
calculations using raw data showed a slight increasing trend over time, which may be attributed to
measurement drift in voltage or current despite detrending efforts. However, when resistance was
computed using detrended, fitted decaying sine waves, variations within each half-period were
observed—initially decreasing and then increasing toward the latter portion. This behavior suggests
that the inductance may be slightly underestimated at the start of the half-period and overestimated
toward the end. Unlike the raw data analysis, minimal overall resistance trend was observed in the
fitted data.
 Additionally, the fitting process introduced discrepancies between the computed resistance
from fitted data and real data, requiring slightly different inductance values. This highlights the
sensitivity of resistance calculations to inductance estimation and the potential for corrections or
errors introduced during waveform fitting. The findings emphasize the importance of careful
alignment of starting indices and accurate determination of inductance to ensure reliable resistance
measurements. Future work should focus on refining the fitting process and exploring methods to
minimize measurement drift and fitting inaccuracies.
