Zero-shot detection of apical periodontitis on periapical radiographs using multimodal large language models: diagnostic accuracy and error patterns
Authors: Mehmet Adıgüzel, Furkan Özeken, Tuba Özeken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe present study evaluated the diagnostic performance of three general-purpose multimodal large language models (MLLMs)—Claude 4.5 Sonnet, GPT-5.2 Thinking, and Gemini 3.0 Pro—in detecting apical periodontitis on periapical radiographs.MethodsOne hundred twenty periapical radiographs were included (60 apical periodontitis–positive and 60 healthy), retrieved from routine clinical records at a university dental clinic. Teeth with root canal fillings and radiographs showing metallic artifacts were excluded to minimize superimposition in the apical region. Two experienced clinicians independently assessed all images to establish the reference standard based on two-dimensional periapical radiographic interpretation without CBCT confirmation. Disagreements were resolved by consensus, and interobserver agreement was high (Cohen’s κ = 0.88). The same coded images were subsequently evaluated by the three MLLMs in separate sessions using a standardized prompt, without any example or training images. Model outputs were restricted to binary “present/absent” responses. Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated. Differences among models were analyzed using Cochran’s Q test (α = 0.05), followed by Bonferroni-adjusted McNemar tests.ResultsGemini 3.0 Pro achieved the highest overall accuracy (71.67%). This performance was significantly superior to that of Claude 4.5 Sonnet (Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.0015). Claude 4.5 Sonnet demonstrated very high sensitivity (95.00%) but low specificity (13.33%), reflecting a strong false-positive tendency. In contrast, GPT-5.2 Thinking showed high specificity (98.33%) with markedly reduced sensitivity (20.00%). Gemini 3.0 Pro maintained high sensitivity (95.00%) while achieving moderate specificity (48.33%). Positive and negative predictive values (PPV/NPV) were 52.29%/72.73% for Claude, 92.31%/55.14% for GPT-5.2 Thinking, and 64.77%/90.62% for Gemini 3.0 Pro.ConclusionsUnder zero-shot conditions, the evaluated MLLMs did not demonstrate sufficient reliability to replace clinical judgment in the detection of apical periodontitis on periapical radiographs. Although diagnostic performance varied among models, none achieved a level of diagnostic performance sufficient for independent clinical use. At present, these systems appear more suitable as clinician-supervised decision-support tools. Further research is needed to improve diagnostic reliability and validate performance across diverse clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-09026-7.

Introduction

Apical periodontitis (AP) is an inflammatory disease of the periapical tissues that develops in response to microbial infection within the root canal system and is characterized by periapical bone resorption [1, 2]. In clinical practice, both diagnosis and treatment follow-up rely heavily on radiographic evaluation [3]. Periapical radiography remains the primary imaging modality due to its accessibility, low radiation dose, and high spatial resolution. However, its two-dimensional projection, anatomical superposition, and geometric distortion may reduce diagnostic sensitivity and contribute to variability between observers, particularly in the detection of early-stage lesions [4]. Consequently, even minor differences in radiographic interpretation may influence clinical decision-making and treatment planning. The radiographic presentation of AP may vary according to lesion size, cortical bone involvement, anatomical superimposition, and disease activity. Consequently, AP should not always be regarded as a strictly binary radiographic entity, and interpretation may involve varying degrees of diagnostic uncertainty, particularly in early or subtle lesions [4].

To address these diagnostic limitations, artificial intelligence (AI)–based image interpretation systems have increasingly been investigated in dental radiology [5]. Most existing studies have focused on task-specific convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures, such as U-Net, YOLO, and related models, trained on curated datasets for defined diagnostic tasks. When supported by sufficiently large and well-annotated datasets, these systems can achieve high diagnostic performance [6]. Nevertheless, concerns remain regarding their generalizability to routine clinical practice, as performance may vary depending on dataset composition, labeling standards, image quality, and inter-center differences [7]. Moreover, optimal performance is often observed when evaluation data resemble the training distribution, raising questions about external validity.

More recently, general-purpose multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have emerged with the capability to process visual and textual inputs simultaneously. Unlike task-specific CNN models, these systems can analyze image inputs without additional domain-specific training and are typically deployed in an off-the-shelf manner [8]. However, their internal decision mechanisms remain largely non-transparent, raising concerns about interpretability and clinical accountability [9]. Despite their growing adoption, evidence regarding their zero-shot diagnostic performance on dental radiographs remains limited. In particular, their tendencies toward false-positive and false-negative classifications have not been sufficiently characterized for clinical use [10]. Given that different types of errors may carry distinct implications for patient management, evaluating overall accuracy alone may be insufficient. In the context of endodontic decision-making, false-positive findings may lead to unnecessary intervention, whereas false-negative results may delay appropriate treatment [11]. Therefore, understanding model-specific error tendencies is clinically as important as overall diagnostic accuracy. Previous systematic reviews have reported that task-specific convolutional neural network (CNN)-based artificial intelligence systems developed for radiographic caries detection can demonstrate promising diagnostic performance, with AUC values reported as high as 0.98, although substantial heterogeneity in datasets and reference standards has been noted [12].

Unlike task-specific CNN models developed within controlled research environments, general-purpose multimodal large language models are already publicly accessible and increasingly used by clinicians without formal validation. This creates an urgent need to characterize their diagnostic behavior under real-world, off-the-shelf conditions before informal clinical adoption expands.

To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to directly compare off-the-shelf multimodal large language models for endodontic radiographic diagnosis under zero-shot conditions.

Methods

Dataset and image selection

This retrospective study was conducted on 120 digital periapical radiographs following approval by the institutional ethics committee (Approval No: 25/02/2026-22). Images were retrieved from routine clinical records archived at a university dental clinic and were acquired between January 2024 and December 2025. Periapical radiographs were acquired using a Best-X-DC intraoral X-ray unit (New Life Radiology, Italy) and a VistaScan phosphor plate imaging system (DÜRR Dental, Bietigheim-Bissingen, Germany). Radiographs of premolar and molar teeth were eligible for inclusion when the entire root apex and surrounding periapical region were visible, image sharpness and contrast were sufficient for interpretation, and no motion artifacts, severe superimposition, or other image defects that could interfere with assessment of the apical region were present. Teeth with root canal fillings, radiographs with insufficient image quality, images with pronounced artifacts or superimposition, and teeth with incomplete root development were excluded. Endodontically treated teeth were excluded because radiopaque filling materials may obscure the apical region and increase superimposition, potentially complicating interpretation and increasing false-positive classifications.

A total of 450 periapical radiographs were initially screened. Of these, 330 were excluded for not meeting the eligibility criteria (root canal–treated teeth, n = 245; insufficient image quality, n = 38; incomplete root development, n = 27; pronounced artifacts/superimposition, n = 20). The final dataset therefore comprised 120 radiographs and was balanced to include 60 apical periodontitis–positive and 60 healthy cases. All images were anonymized prior to analysis and assigned unique study codes (R001–R120).

Sample size calculation

An a priori sample size calculation (G*Power 3.1) based on the McNemar test indicated that 108 paired observations were required to detect a 15% difference in accuracy (p1 = 0.55, p2 = 0.70) with α = 0.05 and 80% power. Therefore, 120 radiographs were included.

Reference standard (Ground Truth) and image labeling.

The reference standard was established through independent evaluation of all radiographs by two experienced clinicians who were blinded to the model outputs. AP positivity was defined as the presence of a distinct periapical radiolucency in the apical region and/or loss of lamina dura continuity. At the same assessment session, AP-positive cases were also graded using the Periapical Index (PAI) scoring system based on periapical radiographic features [13]. For subgroup analysis, AP-positive cases were categorized as PAI = 3 (small/moderate lesions) or PAI ≥ 4 (well-defined radiolucent lesions). In cases of disagreement for either AP status or PAI category, the images were re-evaluated and a final decision was reached by consensus. Images were exported from the institutional clinical archive and saved in JPEG format for analysis. No image preprocessing, enhancement, contrast adjustment, cropping, resizing, or other image manipulation procedures were performed beyond anonymization. Interobserver agreement for AP status was assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ) coefficient and indicated a high level of agreement (κ = 0.88).

AI models and evaluation protocol

Three current general-purpose MLLMs were evaluated under off-the-shelf conditions in February 2026 using the standard user interface: Claude 4.5 Sonnet (Anthropic), GPT-5.2 Thinking (OpenAI), and Gemini 3.0 Pro (Google). Because model versions and service infrastructure may be updated over time, the performance metrics reported in this study reflect the off-the-shelf behavior of these systems as accessed in February 2026; the same images and prompt may yield different outputs at later time points.

For each model, all radiographs were submitted using a single standardized prompt without providing any example or training images, and model outputs were restricted to a binary decision (present/absent). The standardized prompt was as follows: “Is apical periodontitis (a periapical radiolucent area) present in the target tooth/teeth on this periapical radiograph? Respond only with ‘present’ or ‘absent’. If uncertain, provide the most likely clinical decision.” This directive was used to mirror the binary decision requirement in clinical practice and to standardize responses in ambiguous cases. Including an instruction to choose the most likely option in uncertain cases was intended to allow observation of model decision tendencies (threshold behavior). As is typical for periapical radiographs, some images included the target tooth together with adjacent teeth, and no visual markers were used to identify a specific tooth.

Each radiograph was evaluated once by each model; no repeat measurements were performed, to reflect real-world single-interaction usage. This approach was chosen to simulate typical clinical single-session use rather than controlled repeated laboratory testing. Accordingly, this study reports a single-time-point performance profile of the models in February 2026 rather than within-model repeatability under identical input and prompt conditions. The evaluation order of coded images was randomized in Microsoft Excel, and images were presented to each model in this randomized order in separate, new chat sessions without additional context.

All radiographs were anonymized prior to analysis, and no patient-identifiable information was included in the uploaded images or prompts. Only coded radiographic images were submitted to the evaluated AI systems. The study was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical approval and applicable data protection requirements.

In addition to conventional diagnostic performance metrics, model-specific error patterns were evaluated by examining the distribution of false-positive and false-negative classifications derived from the confusion matrices generated for each model. This analysis was performed to identify potential differences in diagnostic decision tendencies among the evaluated models.

This study was reported in accordance with the STARD-AI guidelines for diagnostic accuracy studies involving artificial intelligence.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Diagnostic performance metrics were calculated, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). In addition, agreement between each model and the reference standard was assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ) coefficient. 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using the Wilson method. Overall differences among models were assessed using Cochran’s Q test. Pairwise comparisons were conducted using the McNemar test with Bonferroni correction. ROC curve analysis was not performed because the evaluated models provided only forced binary outputs without calibrated probability scores. For subgroup analyses based on lesion severity (PAI categories), sensitivity values among models were compared using Cochran’s Q test. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Gemini 3.0 Pro achieved the highest accuracy (71.67%), with high sensitivity (95.00%) and moderate specificity (48.33%). Claude 4.5 Sonnet also showed high sensitivity (95.00%) but low specificity (13.33%). In contrast, GPT-5.2 Thinking demonstrated very high specificity (98.33%) and low sensitivity (20.00%). The positive and negative predictive values were 52.29% and 72.73% for Claude 4.5 Sonnet, 92.31% and 55.14% for GPT-5.2 Thinking, and 64.77% and 90.62% for Gemini 3.0 Pro, respectively (Table 1). Agreement analysis revealed differences among the evaluated models. Claude 4.5 Sonnet (κ = 0.08) and GPT-5.2 Thinking (κ = 0.18) demonstrated slight agreement with the reference standard, whereas Gemini 3.0 Pro showed moderate agreement (κ = 0.43). Analysis of model-specific error patterns revealed distinct diagnostic tendencies. Claude 4.5 Sonnet generated a high number of false-positive classifications (n = 52), whereas GPT-5.2 Thinking produced substantially more false-negative classifications (n = 48). Gemini 3.0 Pro demonstrated a comparatively more balanced distribution of false-positive (n = 31) and false-negative (n = 3) results. When AP-positive cases were stratified according to lesion severity using the PAI system as PAI = 3 (small/moderate; n = 27) and PAI ≥ 4 (well-defined; n = 33), both Claude 4.5 Sonnet and Gemini 3.0 Pro demonstrated high sensitivity in each subgroup (PAI = 3: 92.6%; PAI ≥ 4: 97.0%). Discussion

The clinical role of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) in dental radiology remains to be clearly defined. The present study provides a structured evaluation of the zero-shot diagnostic performance and error profiles of three off-the-shelf MLLMs for detecting apical periodontitis (AP) on periapical radiographs. Beyond differences in overall accuracy, marked variations were observed in the balance between sensitivity and specificity, highlighting that model outputs cannot be interpreted solely on the basis of global performance metrics. Rather, model-specific error tendencies must be considered within a clinically contextualized decision-support framework.

Distinct diagnostic behaviors were observed across models. Claude 4.5 Sonnet demonstrated very high sensitivity but extremely low specificity, indicating a strong tendency toward false-positive classifications. In contrast, GPT-5.2 Thinking showed very high specificity but markedly reduced sensitivity, reflecting a conservative tendency with increased false-negative results. In endodontic practice, false-positive classifications may result in unnecessary retreatment or surgical intervention, whereas false-negative results may delay essential therapy and increase the risk of lesion progression [11]. In routine dental practice, even small shifts in diagnostic thresholds may translate into substantial cumulative effects at population level, particularly in settings where AI outputs are informally consulted without structured validation. Gemini 3.0 Pro exhibited a comparatively more balanced sensitivity–specificity profile and achieved the highest overall accuracy. However, its moderate specificity indicates that clinically relevant false-positive findings remain present. These findings suggest that the models operate at different implicit decision thresholds, which may require calibration before clinical implementation. The mechanisms underlying these divergent error profiles remain uncertain because the internal architectures, training data, and alignment procedures of proprietary multimodal models are not publicly disclosed. Nevertheless, several plausible explanations may be considered. One possible explanation for the extremely low specificity observed with Claude 4.5 Sonnet is a tendency toward over-identification of pathology, whereby normal anatomical structures, trabecular bone patterns, or radiographic ambiguities may be interpreted as disease-related findings. Similarly, the low sensitivity observed with GPT-5.2 Thinking may be related to a more conservative decision strategy, potentially associated with uncertainty-handling mechanisms or higher implicit thresholds for assigning positive classifications. Importantly, the present findings do not allow differentiation between limitations in radiographic pattern recognition and operational factors related to model alignment or response policies. Gemini 3.0 Pro demonstrated a comparatively more balanced sensitivity–specificity profile, which may reflect differences in training data composition, vision–language integration, or optimization strategies. However, because these systems are proprietary, any explanation regarding the precise causes of performance differences remains speculative and requires further investigation. Because the internal decision processes of proprietary multimodal models remain non-transparent, the observed outputs should be interpreted as pattern-recognition behavior under zero-shot conditions rather than evidence of true diagnostic reasoning or clinical cognition.

The differing sensitivity–specificity patterns may reflect variation in how these models interpret radiographic ambiguity. False-positive tendencies may arise from misinterpretation of normal anatomical structures—such as trabecular bone patterns, lamina dura variations, or superimposed anatomical landmarks—as pathological radiolucencies [14]. Conversely, conservative thresholding may increase the risk of overlooking subtle or poorly defined lesions. Anatomical factors may also have contributed to diagnostic errors. Structures such as the maxillary sinus, mental foramen, and other normal radiographic landmarks can create radiolucent appearances that may mimic apical pathology [4]. Future studies may further investigate whether model performance varies across different tooth regions and anatomical locations. The results therefore suggest that zero-shot multimodal systems may rely more heavily on generalized visual reasoning rather than domain-specific radiographic pattern recognition [8, 10]. Calibration strategies—such as prevalence-aware threshold adjustment or risk-weighted decision policies depending on clinical use (e.g., triage versus treatment planning)—may be required before these systems can be responsibly integrated into endodontic workflows [15]. Future domain-specific adaptation strategies, including fine-tuning on annotated dental radiographic datasets, may improve calibration and reduce model-specific threshold biases. However, whether such approaches can fully overcome limitations associated with general-purpose multimodal architectures remains uncertain.

Subgroup analysis according to lesion severity (PAI categories) further supports this interpretation. Claude 4.5 Sonnet and Gemini 3.0 Pro maintained high sensitivity in both moderate (PAI = 3) and well-defined lesions (PAI ≥ 4), whereas GPT-5.2 Thinking demonstrated persistently low sensitivity across severity levels. These findings indicate that differences in overall performance were primarily driven by divergence in false-positive rates rather than lesion detection capability among AP-positive cases. Accordingly, assessment of multimodal models should extend beyond aggregate accuracy values and incorporate stratified analyses reflecting lesion severity and clinically relevant error types. Although lesion severity was stratified using PAI categories, other lesion-related factors such as lesion size, anatomical location, radiographic contrast, and cortical bone involvement may also influence radiographic interpretation and model performance. These factors were not assessed independently in the present study and may represent additional sources of variability in model performance.

The present study has several limitations. First, the dataset was derived from a single academic center, and the reference standard was established by consensus interpretation of two experienced clinicians based on two-dimensional periapical radiographs. Although three-dimensional imaging modalities such as CBCT may provide higher diagnostic sensitivity than periapical radiography, particularly for early or subtle lesions, CBCT confirmation was not available for the present dataset [3, 4]. Consequently, some lesions may have been misclassified when the reference standard was established using two-dimensional radiographic assessment alone. Accordingly, some model outputs categorized as false positives may represent lesions that were not detectable on conventional periapical radiographs. The inherent limitations of two-dimensional imaging, including anatomical superimposition and reduced sensitivity for early or cancellous bone lesions, may have affected both the establishment of the reference standard and the classifications generated by the evaluated models. In addition, the exclusion of endodontically treated teeth and radiographs with pronounced artifacts or superimposition improved methodological standardization but may have reduced clinical representativeness. Because AP is frequently evaluated under more complex radiographic conditions in routine endodontic practice, model performance observed in the present study may not fully reflect performance in everyday clinical settings. Nevertheless, the substantial false-positive tendencies observed in some models suggest that performance differences cannot be attributed solely to the reference standard used. Second, each radiograph was evaluated only once by each model, precluding assessment of within-model response stability under identical input conditions. Because publicly accessible MLLMs may generate different responses across separate sessions, the absence of repeated testing limits assessment of response consistency and reproducibility. Third, because the models were restricted to forced binary outputs without calibrated probability scores, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and area under the curve (AUC) estimation were not feasible. Consequently, threshold optimization and calibration assessment could not be performed. In addition, the use of a forced binary-response format may not fully reflect real-world clinical decision-making, in which diagnostic uncertainty itself may represent a valid outcome. By requiring the models to provide a “present” or “absent” decision and instructing them to select the most likely option when uncertain, the study may have influenced threshold behavior and error distributions. Therefore, some of the observed false-positive and false-negative tendencies may partially reflect the response constraints imposed by the study design rather than the intrinsic diagnostic capabilities of the models. Future studies should evaluate non-forced prompting strategies that allow explicit expression of diagnostic uncertainty. In addition, no explainability, localization, or saliency-based analyses were performed. Therefore, it remains unclear which image regions contributed most strongly to model outputs, limiting interpretation of the visual features underlying model decisions.

Another limitation is that the dataset was artificially balanced to include equal numbers of AP-positive and healthy cases. Because PPV and NPV are prevalence-dependent measures, the values reported in this study may differ substantially from those observed in routine clinical populations, where disease prevalence is typically lower [16]. Therefore, extrapolation of predictive metrics to real-world settings should be undertaken cautiously. External validation on independent multi-center datasets—including heterogeneous imaging systems, acquisition parameters, and prevalence distributions—is necessary before clinical translation can be considered. Differences in detector technology, image processing algorithms, and acquisition protocols may influence radiographic appearance and could potentially affect model performance across clinical settings.

Another important consideration relates to the evolving nature of publicly accessible foundation models. Because multimodal models are continuously updated without strict version locking in user interfaces, reproducibility of performance over time remains uncertain. This phenomenon, often referred to as model drift, represents a methodological challenge unique to general-purpose AI systems and underscores the need for time-stamped evaluation and transparent reporting of model access conditions [17].

Collectively, these findings emphasize that off-the-shelf MLLMs, when used under zero-shot conditions, should not be regarded as stand-alone diagnostic tools for apical periodontitis. Instead, their potential role may lie in supervised decision-support scenarios, provided that future research addresses calibration, stability, and external validation.

Conclusion

Off-the-shelf MLLMs do not currently demonstrate a level of reliability sufficient to replace clinical judgment in detecting apical periodontitis on periapical radiographs. Distinct error profiles were observed among the models: Claude 4.5 Sonnet showed a greater tendency toward false-positive classifications, whereas GPT-5.2 Thinking was more prone to missing true lesions. Although Gemini 3.0 Pro achieved the highest overall accuracy and exhibited a more balanced sensitivity–specificity profile, the findings support the use of these systems, at present, only as clinician-supervised decision-support tools. Further investigation of domain-specific adaptation strategies is warranted to enhance diagnostic performance. Because the present study evaluated radiographic interpretation rather than full clinical assessment, the findings should be interpreted within this specific context.
