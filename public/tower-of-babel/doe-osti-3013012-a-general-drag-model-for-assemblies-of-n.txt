Executive Summary

This project was conducted to improve predictive capability and reduce uncertainty in the modeling of
momentum exchange in fluid–solid multiphase systems by developing a general and accurate methodology
for determining the drag coefficients of non-spherical particles. While drag correlations for spherical
particles are well established, existing approaches for non-spherical particles, particularly for particle
assemblies, remain limited in accuracy and applicability across broad ranges of flow conditions and particle
characteristics. This work addressed these limitations through a combined computational fluid dynamics
(CFD), correlations, and artificial neural networks (ANN) based approach.
 High-fidelity numerical simulations were performed using a verified and validated in-house CFD code
to generate comprehensive datasets for both single non-spherical particles and assemblies of such particles.
These simulations covered wide ranges of Reynolds numbers, particle shapes and orientations, and solid
volume fractions. For single non-spherical particles, including ellipsoids and spherocylinders, accurate drag
coefficient expressions were derived for multiple flow orientations, and the key variables governing drag
behavior were identified and systematically characterized.
 Building on the single-particle results, extensive numerical studies were conducted for assemblies of
non-spherical particles, including both monodisperse and bidisperse systems, to quantify collective drag
behavior under a broad spectrum of conditions. In particular, assemblies of bidisperse particles composed
of spheres and ellipsoids were studied in detail, and regression-based correlations were developed to
describe the dependence of drag on particle shape, mixed ratio, and solids volume fraction. The resulting
datasets captured complex, nonlinear dependencies arising from particle geometry, inter-particle
interactions, and flow regime.
 Artificial intelligence methods were then employed to develop artificial neural network (ANN) models
capable of accurately representing the drag coefficients of single non-spherical particles. The ANN models
were trained and validated using the generated high-dimensional datasets and demonstrated strong
predictive capability across the full range of parameters investigated. The finalized ANN framework was
implemented using TensorFlow, providing a flexible and efficient platform for practical application.
 The primary outcomes of this work are a validated, ANN-based methodology for modeling drag in
systems containing non-spherical particles and regression-based correlations for particle assemblies,
applicable over wide ranges of flow conditions. These results provide a robust foundation for improving
the accuracy and reducing the uncertainty of multiphase flow simulations relevant to energy and industrial
applications. Additional benefits of the project include the development of a systematic, data-driven
framework for drag modeling and the training of undergraduate and graduate students in advanced
numerical simulation and machine-learning techniques. In particular, three graduate students contributed
directly to the development of ANN-based drag models as part of their thesis research.

1. Introduction
The goal of this project was to improve predictive capability and reduce uncertainty in the modeling of
momentum exchange in fluid–solid multiphase systems by developing a general and accurate methodology
for determining the hydrodynamic drag of non-spherical particles. While drag behavior for spherical
particles has been extensively studied and is relatively well characterized across a wide range of flow
conditions, realistic particulate systems encountered in energy and industrial processes frequently involve
particles that are non-spherical or irregular in shape. For such particles, existing drag models remain limited
in accuracy and applicability, particularly when extended to particle assemblies and dense flows.
 Early analytical investigations, beginning with Oseen’s work (1915) on spheroids, together with
subsequent experimental and theoretical studies on disks, spheroids, and cylinders, established foundational
understanding for selected non-spherical geometries. However, these studies addressed only limited classes
of particle shapes and orientations. For irregularly shaped particles, the available literature is sparse, and
attempts to correlate drag with characteristic particle measures such as equivalent diameters or fractal
dimensions have generally shown limited predictive capability. Classical and modern references on
particulate flows, spanning coarse, fine, and nano-particles, consistently emphasize the need for improved
and more general drag models for non-spherical particles, including particle agglomerates, clusters, and
assemblies.
 The determination of drag for non-spherical particles presents challenges that are fundamentally more
complex than those associated with spherical particles. Non-spherical particles lack point symmetry and
often require multiple geometric measures to characterize their size, surface area, and shape. In addition,
drag depends strongly on particle orientation relative to the flow. Although certain particle shapes exhibit
preferred orientations in uniform flows, turbulence, inter-particle interactions, and wall effects induce
continuous rotation, resulting in a wide distribution of orientations and corresponding drag forces.
Furthermore, the wakes formed behind non-spherical particles differ fundamentally from those of spheres
and may exhibit asymmetry even at low Reynolds numbers, further complicating the relationship between
flow conditions and drag.
 Additional complexity arises in assemblies of non-spherical particles, where the drag experienced by
an individual particle is influenced by neighboring particles and by flow within the interstitial regions.
Existing modeling approaches for particle assemblies have relied primarily on empirical correlations and
asymptotic analyses that introduce solids or fluid volume fraction as an additional parameter. Most of these
approaches ultimately originate from drag expressions for isolated spherical particles and extend them to
multi-particle systems through correction factors. As a result, their applicability to assemblies of non-
spherical particles is limited, and the associated uncertainty can be substantial when particle shape,
orientation, and inter-particle interactions play a dominant role.
 To address these limitations, this project pursued a comprehensive approach that combined high-fidelity
computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations with artificial intelligence (AI)–based modeling. The
work focused on generating validated, particle-resolved datasets for both single non-spherical particles and
assemblies of such particles and on using these datasets to develop general, data-driven drag models.
Specifically, the project objectives were to:
 Identify the governing variables and appropriate geometric descriptors for the drag of single non-
spherical particles.
 Determine accurate drag coefficients for isolated non-spherical particles, including ellipsoids and
spherocylinders, over wide ranges of Reynolds number, particle orientation, and material properties.

 5

 Conduct systematic numerical studies of assemblies of non-spherical particles, including monodisperse
and bidisperse systems, to quantify collective drag behavior across broad ranges of particle shape, size, and
volume fraction.
 Develop regression-based correlations and artificial neural network (ANN) models capable of
representing the complex, nonlinear dependence of drag on particle and flow parameters.
 Validate the resulting models and assess their applicability for improving drag representation in
multiphase flow simulations.
 Table 1 summarizes the project timeline, including the major tasks and milestones defined in the Project
Management Plan.
 The main body of this report focuses on the key technical outcomes of the project. The initial phase of
the work addressed the drag of single non-spherical particles, providing validated force data and physical
insight into the role of particle shape and orientation. Subsequent phases extended the analysis to assemblies
of non-spherical particles, capturing the effects of inter-particle interactions and volume fraction. The
resulting datasets were then used to develop and validate ANN-based drag models and regression
correlations. The report concludes with a summary of the major findings and a discussion of the broader
impact of this work on the modeling of fluid–solid multiphase systems relevant to DOE mission areas.

2. Numerical Methods and Code Development
As part of this project, the existing direct numerical simulation (DNS) framework developed by the
Principal Investigator and collaborators was further improved and extended to enable accurate, particle-
resolved simulations of assemblies of non-spherical particles. The code resolves particle–fluid interactions
at the particle scale and computes hydrodynamic forces and torques directly, without reliance on empirical
closure models.
 The fundamental premise of the DNS method is that solid particles within the flow field may be
represented by the system of forces they exert on the surrounding fluid. This is supported by the Immersed
Boundary (IB) theory, where the actual particles are substituted by a body force density function,
incorporated into the momentum equation of the fluid. The IB theory for single-phase fluid computations
was introduced by Peskin (1972) for the blood flow inside the heart. In this model the three-dimensional
fluid momentum equations were solved on a fixed Eulerian grid, while the immersed boundary that
represents the moving body was described by a Lagrangian grid. A significant advantage of this combined
Eulerian and Lagrangian approach is that the fixed fluid field does not need to be re-meshed after every
time step during the simulation. Feng and Michaelides (2004, 2005) were the first to use the Lattice
Boltzmann Method in combination with the IB method for the movement of groups of particles.

is approximately equal to the width of the strips.

3. Drag Coefficients of Single Non-Spherical Particles
For single non-spherical particles, several particle geometries were implemented in the particle-resolved
numerical framework developed in this project, including prolate and oblate ellipsoids, spherocylinders,
cubes, and short cylindrical (fiber-like) particles. These implementations enabled systematic investigations
of the effects of particle shape, aspect ratio, orientation, and Reynolds number on hydrodynamic forces.
 Detailed drag and lift studies were carried out for multiple particle shapes using a consistent numerical
methodology and analysis procedure. In this final report, results for a single spherocylindrical particle are
presented as a representative example. The spherocylinder was selected because it is a canonical non-
spherical geometry that captures key shape effects while providing a clear illustration of the numerical
approach, validation strategy, and force analysis used throughout the project.
 The same simulation framework, force evaluation methods, and post-processing procedures were
applied to all other non-spherical particle shapes studied in this work. Extended results for other shapes are
documented in graduate student theses completed as part of the project. By presenting one representative
case in detail and referencing the remaining studies, this report demonstrates the generality of the approach
while avoiding unnecessary repetition.

3.2 Convergence Study
 Based on our past experience on the use of the in-house DNS code, when tested with a uniform flow
over a sphere of diameter 𝐷, we found that for Re=O(1), a grid resolution of D/h=12 is sufficient, while
for Re=O(100) a grid resolution of D/h=20 will be needed. This practice has also been confirmed by other
researchers (Sanjeevi et al., 2017, 2018) who determined that the normalized drag coefficients (defined as
the numerical results divided by the accepted values at the same Re) have less than 1% error at resolutions:
D/h=8 for Re=0.1; D/h=20 for Re=100; and D/h=30 for Re=1000.

 Lift and torque coefficients were investigated with the same level of detail and numerical resolution as
the drag coefficients. More than one thousand simulation cases were analyzed in an effort to derive accurate
and reliable correlations. Despite extensive analysis, including examination of multiple functional forms,
parametric trends, and derivative behavior of the data, it was found that no explicit correlation could achieve
a level of accuracy and robustness comparable to that obtained for the drag coefficient. The inability to
obtain accurate explicit correlations reflects the strong nonlinearity and high dimensionality of lift and
torque behavior for spherocylinders, particularly with respect to particle orientation and flow conditions.
For these reasons, artificial neural network (ANN) models are better suited to represent lift and torque
coefficients. The ANN-based approach provides a more flexible and accurate framework for capturing these
complex dependencies and is therefore recommended for modeling lift and torque in applications involving
non-spherical particles.

4. Drag Coefficients of Assemblies of Non-Spherical Particles
 Extending the analysis from a single non-spherical particle to an assembly of particles constitutes one
of the central objectives of this project and introduces substantially greater physical and computational
complexity. In contrast to single-particle configurations, particle assemblies may be monodisperse or
polydisperse, and particles within the same suspension may differ in shape, size, and orientation. The
hydrodynamic drag experienced by each particle is strongly influenced by neighboring particles through
wake interactions, confinement effects, and flow through the interstitial regions, leading to collective
behavior that cannot be inferred directly from isolated-particle results.
 The investigation of particle assemblies presents several significant challenges. First, particle-resolved
simulations of dense or moderately dense assemblies of non-spherical particles are computationally
expensive, which limits the size of the datasets that can be generated within practical time constraints. This
limitation is particularly restrictive for the development of artificial neural network (ANN) models, which
typically require large and diverse datasets for effective training and validation. Second, achieving random
high solid volume fractions for assemblies of non-spherical particles remains a nontrivial problem. The
generation of densely packed configurations for non-spherical particles is itself an active area of research
and introduces additional uncertainty in both numerical setup and interpretation of results.
 In light of these challenges, the scope of the present study was intentionally focused on two
representative classes of particle assemblies. Specifically, detailed analyses were conducted for (i)
monodisperse assemblies of spherocylinders and (ii) bidisperse assemblies consisting of spherical particles
mixed with ellipsoidal particles. These configurations capture key features of shape-induced collective drag

 22

behavior while remaining computationally feasible. For these assembly studies, emphasis was placed on
developing regression-based correlations rather than ANN models, as the limited size of the available
datasets favored physically interpretable regression approaches over data-intensive machine-learning
techniques. The results presented in this section therefore represent a balance between physical generality
and computational practicality. They provide new quantitative insight into drag behavior in assemblies of
non-spherical particles and establish a foundation for future extensions to more complex, polydisperse
systems as computational capabilities and particle-packing methodologies continue to advance.
4.1 Problem Description and Simulation Setup
 To model an effectively infinite fluid–particle suspension, simulations were performed in a three-
dimensional periodic cubic domain. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all directions, allowing
particles to be randomly distributed within the domain while preventing overlap. Flow was driven by an
imposed pressure gradient in the 𝑧-direction. After the flow reached steady state, the volumetric flow rate
was computed and the superficial velocity 𝑈 was defined as the volumetric flow rate divided by the cross-
 𝜌𝑈𝐷𝑣
sectional area of the domain. The Reynolds number is defined as 𝑅𝑒 = 𝜇
 , where 𝜌 𝑎𝑛𝑑 𝜇 are fluid
density and dynamic viscosity, respectively. The choices of the values of these properties are not of any
consequence since all the results are expressed in dimensionless form. 𝐷𝑣 is the volume-equivalent diameter
of the particle and it is reduced to the diameter of the sphere in the case of a sphere.
 A major challenge in simulating assemblies of non-spherical particles or bidisperse particles is the
generation of random particle configurations without introducing preferential orientations. Many existing
packing algorithms for ellipsoids enforce alignment constraints to avoid particle overlap, which can lead to
anisotropic packing structures. Because the drag on non-spherical particles depends strongly on orientation,
such structured configurations can result in average drag forces that depend on the flow direction, thereby
obscuring the intrinsic drag behavior of the assembly. For example, very thin oblate particles (disks) can
achieve high solid volume fractions if preferentially oriented parallel to the faces of the computational
domain, but such arrangements violate the assumption of random orientation and require additional
orientation-dependent parameters.
 To avoid this issue, we adopted a different strategy. For monodisperse assemblies of non-spherical
particles, such as spherocylinders, a packing of identical spheres was first generated at a prescribed solid
volume fraction using a well-established sphere-packing algorithm (Skoge et al., 2006). Each sphere was
then replaced by a randomly oriented non-spherical particle that was fully enclosed within the original
spherical volume. In the case of spherocylinders, the particle dimensions were selected such that the
maximum extent of the spherocylinder did not exceed the diameter of the enclosing sphere. This procedure
guarantees the absence of particle overlap while allowing completely random particle orientations, thereby
avoiding packing-induced anisotropy.
 A similar approach was used for bidisperse assemblies consisting of spheres mixed with non-spherical
particles, such as ellipsoids. Starting from a densely packed monodisperse sphere configuration, selected
spheres were replaced by either smaller spheres or non-spherical particles that were co-centered with the
original spheres. As in the monodisperse case, the replacement particles were chosen to remain fully
enclosed within the volume of the original spheres, which permitted random orientations to be assigned
without introducing overlap. This strategy enabled systematic control of particle composition and solid
volume fraction while preserving randomness in particle orientation.
 To achieve the desired solid fraction, two primary methods can be used. The first approach involves
varying particle size while keeping the number of particles constant, meaning the number of particles
remains fixed while their size is adjusted to achieve different solid fractions. The advantage is that a large

 23

number of particles helps reduce statistical noise and fluctuations; however, at low solid fractions, the
particles become very small, requiring a finer background grid to maintain accuracy, which increases
computational cost. The second approach involves varying the number of particles while keeping the
particle size constant, where the particle size remains fixed, and the number of particles is adjusted to reach
different solid fractions. This method ensures a fine grid is not needed at low solid fractions, providing
consistent numerical accuracy across different cases. However, a key drawback is that the number of
particles can vary significantly between low and high solid fractions, potentially leading to inconsistencies,
particularly in bidisperse assemblies, where a small number of one particle type may not sufficiently balance
fluctuations, introducing noise into the system. Figure 19 illustrates two sphere assemblies at solid fractions
of 0.05 and 0.314, both generated using 100 particles of different radii.

In this section, the focus is on bidisperse systems composed of spherical particles and oblate ellipsoids,

correlation is developed for bidisperse assemblies of spheres and oblate ellipsoids. The correlation is
applicable for Reynolds numbers between 10 and 300, ellipsoid fractions ranging from 0 to 1, and solid
volume fractions between 0.05 and 0.314.

5. Artificial Neural Network (ANN) Modeling
The hydrodynamic forces acting on non-spherical particles depend on multiple interacting parameters,
including particle shape, orientation, and Reynolds number, leading to highly nonlinear behavior. While
particle-resolved simulations were performed for both single particles and particle assemblies in this
project, the computational cost associated with simulating dense assemblies of non-spherical particles
limited the size of the datasets available for data-driven modeling. As a result, the artificial neural network
(ANN) modeling effort focused primarily on individual non-spherical particles, for which sufficiently large
and comprehensive datasets could be generated.
Three graduate students developed ANN models as part of their thesis research. In each case, particle-
resolved direct numerical simulations were conducted to generate high-fidelity datasets, which were
subsequently used to train and validate the ANN models. These studies targeted different non-spherical
particle geometries and force components, including drag, lift, and torque, and collectively established a
consistent ANN modeling framework. In the following sections, the case of spherocylinder particles is
presented as a representative example to illustrate the typical procedures employed in the development,
training, and validation of the ANN models.
5.1 DNS Data Analysis
 A substantial database was generated from particle-resolved direct numerical simulations (DNS). The
detailed DNS methodology, boundary conditions, and validation for single non-spherical particles are
presented in Section 3. Briefly, the simulations considered a single spherocylindrical particle suspended in
a laminar flow, with systematic variations in particle geometry, orientation, and flow conditions. The
spherocylinder aspect ratio ranged from 1.0 to 6.0, the Reynolds number varied from 0.1 to 300, and the
angle of incidence relative to the flow direction spanned 0° to 90°. For each simulation, the resulting
hydrodynamic force and torque were quantified in terms of the dimensionless drag, lift, and torque
coefficients. In total, 1,219 DNS data points were generated. The distribution of the dataset across the
governing parameters is illustrated in Figures 38 and 39.
 Because the DNS outputs consist of continuous quantitative variables, artificial neural network
regression techniques were employed for modeling. An examination of the dataset revealed that the input
features corresponding to aspect ratio and angle of incidence are uniformly distributed, whereas the

Reynolds number and the resulting drag, lift, and torque coefficients exhibit strongly skewed,
approximately exponential distributions over wide ranges of values. This broad dynamic range and non-
uniform distribution motivated the use of data normalization and careful model training strategies to ensure
balanced learning across all parameter regimes.

regions of the dataset. To mitigate these effects, functional transformations and data scaling were applied
prior to ANN training.

 To evaluate model robustness and reduce sensitivity to data partitioning, K-fold cross-validation was
employed. Rather than performing a single random split of the dataset into training and testing subsets, the
full dataset was divided into 𝑘 equally sized folds. The ANN was trained 𝑘 times, each time using a different
fold for validation and the remaining 𝑘−1 folds for training. This procedure enables the model to be
evaluated over the entire dataset and improves generalization. Values of 𝑘 between 3 and 10 are commonly
used; the specific choice adopted here is discussed alongside the model results. A schematic illustration of
the K-fold cross-validation process is shown in Figure 41.

 Special care was taken to avoid data leakage during preprocessing (Russell and P. Norvig, 2016;
Goodfellow et al., 2016) All transformations and scaling operations were performed independently within
each fold, rather than prior to cross-validation. This ensures that statistical information from the validation
set does not influence the training process. The scaling parameters obtained from the selected optimal fold
were subsequently used for model evaluation and prediction.
 The ANN models developed in this work employ a supervised learning framework, as the DNS
simulations provide explicit labels for drag, lift, and torque coefficients corresponding to known input
features. Model performance was monitored to avoid both overfitting and underfitting. Overfitting occurs
when a model memorizes training data and performs poorly on unseen cases, while underfitting arises when
the model lacks sufficient complexity to capture the underlying relationships. Achieving an appropriate
balance required systematic tuning of model hyperparameters.
 Key hyperparameters adjusted during training include the learning rate, optimizer selection, batch size,
number of hidden layers, number of neurons per layer, regularization strength, and dropout rate. These
parameters were tuned to ensure stable convergence and robust predictive performance across the full
parameter space of the DNS dataset.
5.3,Baseline,Single,Layered,Neural,Network,Model,(SLNN),
Because no universal methodology exists for determining an optimal artificial neural network architecture
a priori, a structured, incremental approach was adopted. As a first step, a baseline single-layer neural
network (SLNN) was developed to establish reference performance and to assess the inherent learnability
of the DNS dataset. This baseline model serves as a benchmark for evaluating the benefits of increased
architectural complexity and systematic hyperparameter optimization presented in subsequent sections.
The SLNN was implemented using K-fold cross-validation with 𝑘=6, yielding approximately 80% of the
available data (978 samples) for training and 20% (196 samples) for testing in each fold. In addition, a
separate unobserved dataset consisting of 45 samples was reserved exclusively for final performance
evaluation. The SLNN architecture, illustrated in Figure 42, consists of three input nodes with linear
activation functions, a single hidden layer containing five neurons with ReLU activation, and an output
layer with three neurons corresponding to the drag, lift, and torque coefficients, each with linear activation.

 Figure 42. Baseline Single Layer Neural Network

To limit the dimensionality of the design space and avoid an exponential growth in training combinations,
several hyperparameters were held constant for the baseline study. The number of training epochs was set
to 1000, which lies within the typical range used for regression-based neural networks. Although large

The mean squared logarithmic error (MSLE) was selected as the loss function, as it provides improved
sensitivity to relative error across the wide dynamic range of the force coefficients and is well suited to the
right-skewed distributions present in the dataset.

The predictive capability of the optimized ANN model was assessed through direct comparison with
correlations established in the literature and Section 3 of this report. The comparisons will be reserved
strictly for the coefficient of drag since most of the established literature neglects lift and torque due to the
large uncertainty errors. The ANN model performed accurately when predicting the correlation data at
spherocylinder aspect ratio of 4. To examine the precision of the proposed ANN model, it was compared to
the mathematical equation proposed by Sanjeevi et al. (2018) and plotted in Figure 51. The correlation
coefficient of the ANN model and the correlation proposed by Sanjeevi et. al measured to 0.997.

model and the equation are almost identical.

5.6,Discussion
 The results presented in this section demonstrate that the optimized ANN model provides an accurate,
reliable, and generalizable representation of drag for spherocylinders over a wide range of Reynolds
numbers, aspect ratios, and orientations. The ANN predictions are fully consistent with established
correlations in the literature and with the analytical expressions developed in this project.
 Traditional correlation-based approaches for lift and torque have often been omitted or shown limited
accuracy due to their strong sensitivity to orientation and flow conditions. In contrast, the ANN framework
developed in this project successfully captures drag, lift, and torque simultaneously with comparable
accuracy, highlighting a key advantage of data-driven modeling for complex particle–fluid interactions.

 46

 The ANN model provides an efficient alternative to computationally expensive DNS or experimental
campaigns when repeated evaluations of hydrodynamic forces are required. Its application is particularly
attractive for scenarios involving non-spherical particles where explicit correlations are unavailable or
unreliable.
 Finally, the methodology established here provides a clear framework for future extensions. These
include expanding the parameter space to high Reynolds number turbulent flow regimes, extending the
ANN approach to additional particle shapes, and incorporating multi-particle interaction effects. The
successful use of ANN modeling in this study underscores its value as a complementary tool to physics-
based simulations in multiphase flow research.
6. Summary and Conclusions
This project was conducted to improve predictive capability and reduce uncertainty in the modeling of
momentum exchange in fluid–solid multiphase systems by developing a general, accurate, and data-driven
methodology for determining the hydrodynamic drag of non-spherical particles and assemblies of such
particles. While drag models for spherical particles are well established, existing approaches for non-
spherical particles are limited in accuracy and applicability, particularly for particle assemblies and realistic
flow conditions. The work reported here addressed these limitations through a comprehensive program
combining particle-resolved direct numerical simulations (DNS) with artificial intelligence (AI)–based
modeling.
 All proposed project objectives and tasks were successfully completed. High-fidelity DNS simulations
were performed for single non-spherical particles, including ellipsoids and spherocylinders, over wide
ranges of Reynolds number, particle aspect ratio, and orientation. These simulations provided validated
force and torque data and enabled systematic identification of the governing geometric and flow parameters
influencing drag. For representative particle shapes, excellent agreement was obtained with available
reference data, establishing confidence in the numerical framework and analysis procedures.
 Building on the single-particle results, the project extended the analysis to assemblies of non-spherical
particles, including both monodisperse and bidisperse systems. Particle-resolved simulations were
conducted over broad ranges of solids volume fraction, particle shape, and flow conditions to quantify
collective drag behavior arising from inter-particle interactions and confinement effects. Regression-based
correlations were developed for selected assembly configurations, and the resulting datasets revealed strong
nonlinear dependencies on particle geometry, mixture composition, orientation effects, and flow regime.
 To address the high dimensionality and complexity of drag behavior for non-spherical particles and
particle assemblies, artificial neural network (ANN) models were developed, trained, and validated using
the DNS-generated datasets. The ANN models demonstrated strong predictive capability across the full
range of parameters investigated and provided a flexible and extensible framework for representing drag
behavior where closed-form correlations are difficult to derive. The ANN framework was implemented
using TensorFlow and structured to support future extensions to additional particle shapes, mixtures, and
flow regimes.
 The primary outcomes of this project include a validated particle-resolved database for non-spherical
particle drag, new regression-based correlations for selected particle assemblies, and an ANN-based
methodology capable of significantly reducing uncertainty in drag prediction for complex particulate
systems. Together, these results provide a robust foundation for improving the accuracy and reliability of
multiphase flow simulations relevant to DOE mission areas, including energy conversion, particulate
transport, and industrial process modeling.

 47

 In addition to its technical contributions, the project had a meaningful educational and workforce
development impact. Graduate students were actively involved in all phases of the research, including
numerical simulation, non-spherical particle geometry implementation, data analysis, and artificial
intelligence model development. Several students developed their own AI modeling codes as part of their
thesis research, gaining interdisciplinary training in computational fluid dynamics, high-performance
computing, and machine learning.
 Overall, this project advances the state of the art in drag modeling for non-spherical particles, bridges
a critical gap between particle-resolved simulations and practical drag representations, and establishes a
scalable, data-driven framework for future research and application. The methodologies and results reported
herein provide lasting value for the modeling of realistic fluid–solid multiphase systems and support
improved prediction, optimization, and design of energy-related processes involving particulate flows.
