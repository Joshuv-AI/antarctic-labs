Physiological Characteristics of Scalp Skin With High Sebum Production and the Impacts of Shampoo Cleansing
Authors: Kuangyu Chen, Hiufung Lau, Sheldon Xu, Amy Yu, Steven Wang, Susan Biehle, Christina Gemmer, Charles Zhang, Rui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe scalp has a higher sebum content than other body areas, leading to issues such as itchiness, dandruff and discomfort. In individuals with scalp seborrhoea, the relationships between sebum levels, hydration, barrier function and self‐perceived scalp condition remain poorly understood.AimsThis study aimed to explore scalp sebum accumulation and associated physiological properties, as well as to evaluate the impact of a single shampoo cleansing efficacy on sebum levels.MethodsThis study involved an Asian population with self‐reported scalp oiliness and dandruff. Participants with elevated scalp sebum levels (>75 µg/cm2) completed a health questionnaire and underwent assessments of sebum, hydration, barrier function and lipid oxidative stress biomarkers. To compare physiological differences, measurements were also taken at the frontal hairline and nape. Furthermore, the effects of a single shampoo application on scalp sebum levels were evaluated.ResultsAt baseline, the scalp exhibited higher sebum levels, lower hydration and weaker barrier function compared to the frontal hairline and nape. Scalp hydration negatively correlated with sebum levels measured in scalp pores. Hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (HODE), an oxidative stress biomarker, positively correlated with scalp sebum. Interestingly, HODE levels also showed a positive correlation with participants' self‐reported dandruff and itchiness. After single wash with a shampoo containing 0.5% piroctone olamine (PO), surface and pore sebum levels were significantly reduced, as well as HODE levels.ConclusionThis study underscores the significance of sebum levels and composition in scalp health among Chinese individuals, emphasizing the need for shampoos that remove excess sebum while preserving the scalp barrier.Keywords: barrier function, dandruff, HODE (Hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid), hydration, itchiness, oxidative stress

Introduction

Human sebum is produced by sebaceous glands, which are part of the pilosebaceous units of the skin. These glands are found in all regions of the skin except the palmar and plantar surfaces, with the highest density in the scalp and facial areas [1, 2].

Sebum is a complex mixture of lipids, including triglycerides, wax esters, squalene, cholesterol, cholesterol esters and fatty acids [3]. Scalp sebum is an important component of the scalp barrier, playing a role in protecting the skin, preserving moisture and maintaining skin homeostasis [4]. However, excessive sebum levels are often associated with poor scalp health condition. People with oily dandruff showed increased expression of interleukin‐8 (IL‐8), and higher levels of kallikrein 5 (KLK5) in the stratum corneum (SC) than dry dandruff and healthy subjects [5]. Elevated sebum levels can promote the proliferation of Malassezia, leading to increased IL‐8 expression in keratinocytes. This upregulation results in neutrophilic infiltration, which is associated with various dermatoses [6]. Additionally, overactivity of KLK5 may cause abnormal desquamation, contributing to flaking [7]. Thus, a thorough understanding of scalp sebum's role is essential for individuals facing suboptimal scalp health.

Firstly, scalp sebum levels are influenced by demographics such as gender, age and ethnicity. Studies have found that the highest sebum levels in African American subjects, followed by Caucasian American, Japanese, Chinese, Thai, Caucasian European and Indian subjects [8]. Males generally have higher scalp sebum levels than females, as observed among African American and Japanese populations. In the Indian and Chinese population, individuals aged 18–35 exhibit higher sebum levels compared to those aged 45 to 65 [8]. Additionally, variations in the refatting kinetics of the scalp and forehead have been noted [9].

Secondly, scalp sebum metabolites play an important role in scalp health. Malassezia free fatty acid metabolites from sebum (represented by oleic acid) induced flaking in dandruff susceptible subjects [10]. Malassezia and environmental oxidative stress like UV exposure could convert linoleate to hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (HODE). 9‐HODE triggers the secretion of inflammatory cytokines and inhibits cell proliferation in epidermal keratinocytes through the G2A receptor (GPR132). 9‐HODE has also been reported to cause epidermal barrier disruption and is a mediator of chromonic photoaging [11, 12, 13, 14]. Recent studies have shown that Malassezia produces multiple bioactive oxygenated polyunsaturated fatty acids, known as oxylipins, which modulate the skin immune system [15]. For instance, Malassezia restricta is positively correlated with the oxylipin 9,10‐dihydroxy‐12Z‐octadecenoic acid (9,10‐DiHOME) on adult skin [16]. Co‐culturing Malassezia with keratinocytes demonstrated a link between 9,10‐DiHOME and pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐1β and IL‐6 production [16]. M. restricta also mediates squalene oxidation. Squalene monohydroperoxide and malondialdehyde (MDA) alter increased reconstructed human epidermis permeability and decreased structural maturation [17].

Previous studies have highlighted the crucial role of sebum in scalp health; however, the locational distribution of sebum abundance on the entire scalp and its differences from adjacent areas remain inadequately understood. In individuals with scalp seborrhoea, the relationships between sebum levels, oxidized lipids, hydration, barrier function and self‐assessed scalp condition are unclear. This study firstly aims to understand sebum distribution on scalp, and how the physiological properties of the scalp differ from nearby body areas. This was investigated via measuring surface sebum, pore sebum, oxidized lipids and other biophysical properties on different body sites. The second aim of this study is to understand how a prototype shampoo application affects scalp surface sebum, pore sebum and oxidized sebum level. Additionally, participants used the same washout shampoo for one week to minimize confounding factors in our physiological scalp assessments.

Materials and Methods

Clinical Study Design

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by Ethics Committee of Cosmetics Center, China Academy of Quality Inspection and Testing Sciences (Approval # S2024‐05‐008). All participants read and signed an informed consent before participating in the study. This study was conducted in May 2024 in Beijing, China. Overall clinical study design was illustrated in Figure 1A. Two shampoos were used for this study. The standard cosmetic shampoo, used for preconditioning, contained sodium laureth sulfate (SLES) as its primary surfactant and lacked sebum control or anti‐dandruff agents. It was purchased from the Chinese market and relabelled. The prototype shampoo, formulated to evaluate treatment effects, was manufactured at the Procter & Gamble Beijing Technology Innovation Centre and shipped to the clinical site. This prototype contains sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) and SLES as major surfactants, with 0.5% Piroctone Olamine (PO). A total of 35 healthy Chinese participants with self‐reported oily scalp, dandruff condition and regular hair wash frequency ≥ 3 times per week were enrolled in the one‐week preconditioning, during which a standard cosmetic shampoo was provided for hair wash. Measurements timeline.

The last hair wash (Day ‐1) during preconditioning was conducted at test facility to ensure a 24 h accumulation time for their next day qualification. At qualification (Day 0), sebum levels were measured using a Sebumeter at nine spaced out locations along three paralleled hair partings. This was for understanding sebum level distribution on scalp and assessing eligibility. Scalp sebum distribution at nine locations is shown in Figure 1B. To target a population with relatively high scalp sebum, those with an average sebum level between 75 and 160 µg/cm2 were enrolled.

To facilitate instrument measurement, enrolled participants had hair shaved at a 3 x 3 cm scalp area. As sebum measurement itself also removes sebum from the scalp, to ensure technical accuracy participants washed hair on site again and let sebum accumulate for another 24 h before Day 1 baseline measurements.

At Baseline (Day 1), scalp sebum levels, pore sebum levels, SC hydration, skin barrier function were measured at various locations including hair parting lines, the clipped scalp area, the forehead (frontal hairline) and the nape of the neck. Tape strips were also collected on scalp for biomarker analysis (Figure 1C). Participants washed hair on site with standard cosmetic shampoo and came back after 24 h on Day 2 for evaluating the efficacy of the prototype shampoo. On Day 2, after sebum had re‐accumulated, participants washed their hair on site with the prototype shampoo containing 0.5% PO. Sebum removal effectiveness was evaluated 30 min after hair drying, measured by Sebumeter and Sebufix. Tape strips were also collected at this point. The sebum level after cleansing was compared with the level before wash at Baseline (Day 1).

Measurements

All measurements were done after participants acclimated for 30 min in controlled temperature and humidity environment (Temperature = 21 ± 1°C, RH = 50 ± 10 %). Sebum quantification was conducted by Sebumeter SM 815 (Courage + Khazaka, Germany) [8]. Pore sebum quantification and visualization was performed by Sebufix F 16 tape mounted on VisioScan VC20 (Courage + Khazaka, Germany) [18]. Skin hydration and barrier function were measured by gpskin Barrier Pro (GPOWER Inc., Korea). Bio‐samples were collected by D‐squame discs (22 mm, CuDerm Corporation, USA) at scalp area in between parted hairs.

Scalp Condition Self‐Evaluation

Participants self‐evaluated their scalp conditions using a standard questionnaire, assessing the severity and frequency of aspects such as dandruff, dry scalp, itchy scalp, visible flakes, scalp irritation and product buildup over the past 24 h.

Bio‐Sample Analysis

At baseline and 30 min after prototype shampoo application, scalp SC tape strip samples were collected at the middle parting hairline. 3 consecutive tape strip samplings were performed at the same location following a standard protocol. The second and third tapes were combined for two HODE isoforms (13‐HODE and 9‐HODE) analytical quantification as reported previously [19]. Total HODE, representing the sum of 13‐HODE and 9‐HODE, was analysed for its correlation with scalp health and shampoo cleansing effects.

Statistical Method

For baseline detection, multiple measurement points were tested on each subject's head. A linear mixed model was employed for statistical analysis, with subject set as a random effect to account for individual differences, and measurement point set as a fixed effect to evaluate differences between distinct head positions. After significant fixed effects were detected, Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test was used for multiple comparison correction to control Type I error, ensuring the reliability of the results.

Paired sample t‐tests were employed to investigate differences across various locations and time points. Additionally, to examine the correlation between different measurements, linear regression was utilized to assess the presence of a linear relationship between variables. This was accomplished by evaluating the significance of the regression coefficient.

These findings suggest that scalp skin has higher sebum levels yet is less hydrated and has a weaker barrier function compared to nearby facial skin, indicating a potential link between excessive sebum production and compromised scalp health.

Correlation of HODE Levels With Scalp Sebum and Self‐Perceived Health

Hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (HODE), an oxidation product of linoleic acid, serves as a biomarker for scalp oxidative stress. HODE was extracted from the second and third layers of scalp tape strip samples. Both 13‐HODE and 9‐HODE were analysed in SC sample. Data indicate that total HODE levels showed significant positive correlation with both scalp surface and pore sebum levels (Figure 4A and B). Notably, HODE also showed a significant correlation with participants' self‐perceived scalp health, with higher HODE levels observed in those reporting dandruff and itchiness. (Figure 4C and D).

Discussion

Sebum levels exhibit considerable variation across different anatomical regions, with the highest concentrations observed in the T‐zone of the face and the scalp [4, 8, 20].This study simultaneously assesses sebum and skin physiological parameters on both the scalp and adjacent areas, revealing that the scalp is a uniquely distinct location characterized by elevated sebum levels, reduced hydration, and compromised barrier function compared to other body sites.

Our findings indicate a notable correlation between high scalp sebum levels and the prevalence of scalp health concerns among participants. Specifically, 69% of participants reported experiencing dandruff, while 56% noted scalp itch within 24 h post‐wash using a cosmetic washout shampoo in this study. This further suggests a link between sebaceous secretions and the manifestation of scalp disorders [5, 10, 21].

Moreover, our data highlights the elevated levels of hydroxyoctadecadienoic acids (HODE) in participants with self‐reported dandruff and scalp itch. HODE serves as a biomarker for oxidative stress within the SC and may instigate inflammatory responses [12, 13, 19]. Epidermis is rich in linoleic acid that exists as a free acid or is incorporated into acylceramide and phospholipid [22]. We observed a significant correlation between total HODE levels and scalp sebum measured by both Sebumeter and Sebufix, reinforcing the notion that scalp sebum contributes to the oxidative stress observed in this region. Interestingly, our analysis revealed that the levels of 13‐HODE were significantly higher than those of 9‐HODE in the scalp (Figure S1). Studies have shown that enzymatic oxidation by Malassezia produced higher level of 13‐HODE than 9‐HODE [16]. Malassezia yeasts are the most frequently detected and highly abundant eukaryotic habitants on scalp [23, 24]. These findings underscore the role of Malassezia in sebum metabolism.

The effect of a single cleansing cycle with an anti‐dandruff shampoo prototype corroborated previous studies that indicated a reduction in scalp sebum levels with increased wash frequency [25]. While sebum lipids can help maintain scalp moisture and barrier function, excessive pore sebum accumulation can negatively affect scalp hydration after 24 h (Figure 3). This inverse relationship between scalp sebum and scalp health warrants further investigation.

In summary, the complex interactions between scalp sebaceous secretions, lipid composition, the microbial communities and scalp health needs to be investigated in the future. There are several limitations in this study. Firstly, participants with dandruff concern were not further assessed for dandruff flaking severity using the Adherent Scalp Flaking Score (ASFS), which may introduce bias and affect the generalisability of the findings [26]. Secondly, skin microbiome and lipidome were not assessed. Skin microbiome elements like Malassezia metabolize sebum lipids and can produce oxylipins with diverse biological functions [16]. Future research should include more scalp physiological and biomarker measurements to better understand the complex relationship between sebum lipids and scalp health. Thirdly, before‐after design was used in study for preliminary learning and method feasibility. In the next step, paralleled design with a control group could be used to minimize confounding factors like mechanical cleansing effects.

Conclusion

This study highlights significant differences in sebum levels between the scalp and adjacent facial skin, with the scalp exhibiting lower hydration and higher TEWL, indicating compromised barrier function. HODE levels correlated positively with sebum levels and negatively with self‐perceived scalp health, particularly for those with dandruff and scalp itchiness conditions. The tested prototype shampoo effectively reduced sebum and HODE in SC. These findings underscore the complex interplay between sebum production, oxidative stress, and scalp health, highlighting the need for targeted sebum control in managing scalp disorders.
