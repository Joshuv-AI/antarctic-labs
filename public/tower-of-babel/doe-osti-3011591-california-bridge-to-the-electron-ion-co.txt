1. Executive Summary
This Final Technical Report summarizes the activities and outcomes of DOE Grant DE-SC0022358, a
traineeship program aimed at preparing students for future nuclear physics research at the Electron-Ion
Collider (EIC). The project was carried out as a partnership between the California EIC
Consortium—which includes campuses from the University of California (UC) and California State
University (CSU) systems, as well as UC-managed national laboratories—and the Cal-Bridge program, a
statewide academic network spanning UC, CSU, and California community-college campuses.

The primary objective of the program was to provide trainees with immersive research experiences within
a collaborative environment consisting of undergraduate and graduate students, postdoctoral researchers,
faculty members, and national-laboratory scientists working toward common scientific goals associated
with the future EIC. By leveraging existing academic networks across California, the program was able to
reach a broad population of students and provide structured pathways into nuclear physics research.

During the period of support, the program provided training and research opportunities for four students
from CSU and Cal Poly campuses. These trainees engaged in theoretical, computational, and
experimental research projects related to EIC physics, gained experience working in collaborative
research environments spanning universities and national laboratories, and participated actively in
professional meetings. Several participants subsequently advanced to graduate study in physics.

2. Recruitment Strategy and Program Structure
For the first cohort of the program, recruitment was conducted in coordination with the Cal-Bridge
program, which identifies and prepares undergraduate students from CSU campuses for research
engagement. Because the start of this project was out of phase with the main Cal-Bridge recruitment
calendar, many potential candidates had already committed to other research opportunities, limiting
flexibility in matching research interests with geographic proximity to UCLA.

To address this challenge, the program adopted a flexible hybrid research model that allowed both remote
and in-person participation while maintaining strong integration with UCLA-based research groups.
Using this approach, two students were recruited for the first cohort and were jointly supervised by
faculty at UCLA and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, with additional close mentorship provided
by postdoctoral researchers.

For the second cohort, recruitment benefited from increased familiarity with CSU campuses and faculty
networks. Direct outreach was made to colleagues at CSU campuses closer to UCLA, including CSU
Long Beach and CSU Northridge. This approach enabled improved matching of students based on
research interests and geographic proximity, facilitating deeper participation in group meetings and
consortium activities throughout the academic year.

3. Student Research Activities and Mentorship

The program supported two cohorts of trainees during different phases of the award. Each cohort was
embedded within the California EIC Consortium and benefited from close mentorship by faculty
members, postdoctoral researchers, and graduate students across UCLA, CSU campuses, and national
laboratories.

The first cohort, consisting of students from Cal Poly San Luis Obispo and CSU Dominguez Hills,
worked on complementary projects related to physics at the future Electron-Ion Collider. One project
focused on simulation studies of jet production at the EIC, while the other involved theoretical
calculations of particle production within the Color Glass Condensate framework, an effective field
theory describing gluon saturation at high energies. A hybrid working model was adopted to
accommodate geographic constraints while maintaining regular engagement with the UCLA-based
research groups. The students participated in recurring research meetings with postdoctoral and faculty
mentors and developed strong foundations in nuclear physics phenomenology, scientific computing, and
collaborative research.

The second cohort, consisting of students from CSU Long Beach and CSU Northridge, was recruited after
the program had established stronger connections with CSU campuses near UCLA. This allowed closer
alignment between student research interests and available mentorship. The two students pursued distinct
but complementary research directions. One project explored the application of machine-learning
techniques to high-energy nuclear physics, specifically the separation of up- and down-quark initiated jets
using jet substructure observables. Building on prior studies demonstrating the importance of jet charge,
this work investigated the incorporation of additional observables, such as charged-particle multiplicity, to
improve discriminative power. The second project focused on experimental nuclear physics studies of jets

and heavy-flavor production at the EIC, involving data analysis, interpretation, and participation in
experimental research discussions. One student produced promising preliminary results before leaving the
program early due to personal circumstances, while the other continued through the end of the award
period.

Across both cohorts, students benefited from a structured mentorship model that paired them with
postdoctoral researchers for day-to-day guidance while maintaining regular interaction with senior faculty
and national-laboratory scientists. Through these experiences, participants gained hands-on research
skills, exposure to collaborative research environments spanning universities and laboratories, and insight
into potential career pathways in nuclear physics.

4. Conferences and Professional Development
Program participants attended multiple professional meetings, including California EIC Consortium
Collaboration Meetings, the EIC User Group Early Career Workshop, and the APS Division of Nuclear
Physics meeting. These activities provided trainees with opportunities to present their work, interact with
a broad scientific community, and gain exposure to professional research environments in nuclear physics.

5. Educational Outcomes
The program contributed directly to student advancement into graduate education. Two participants were
admitted to Ph.D. programs in physics and astronomy at major research universities. Another participant
completed a master’s degree in physics during the award period and continued research through the end
of the program. These outcomes demonstrate the effectiveness of the program in preparing students for
advanced study and research in nuclear physics.

6. Summary
This traineeship program provided a comprehensive introduction to modern research in nuclear physics
and leveraged the California EIC Consortium to connect students with research environments spanning
universities and national laboratories. By coordinating efforts across UC and CSU campuses, the program
enabled broad access to research opportunities and fostered collaborative training experiences aligned
with the scientific goals of the future Electron-Ion Collider.

Participating students gained direct hands-on research experience, developed transferable computational
and analytical skills, engaged with the broader nuclear physics community, and obtained valuable insight
into graduate-level research and careers in STEM fields. Several participants advanced to graduate study,
underscoring the program’s success in preparing students for continued participation in nuclear physics
research.
