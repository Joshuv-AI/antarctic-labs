Developing quality improvement awareness, skills, and emerging leadership behaviors through clinical audits in postgraduate medical education
Authors: Lisanne Hut-Mossel, Eva De Felice, Gera Welker, Jaap Tulleken, Kees Ahaus, Reinold Gans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundQuality improvement (QI) is a crucial competency for medical residents navigating the complexities of modern healthcare systems. This study explores the impact of experiential learning, through clinical audits, on residents’ QI awareness, QI skills and emerging QI leadership behaviors within their clinical learning environment.MethodsA retrospective multiple embedded case study was conducted within two departments of a University Medical Centre in the Netherlands. We interviewed residents (n = 11), supervisors (n = 7), training program directors (n = 2), and a quality officer within two years after the clinical audit. Data analysis involved within and cross-department analysis to identify factors influencing the development of QI awareness, QI skills and emerging QI leadership behaviors in residents.ResultsResidents develop QI awareness and QI skills through increased experiential knowledge, by presenting audit data and inspiring colleagues to implement change, by being aware of shared QI responsibility, demonstrating emerging QI leadership behaviors legitimized by the clinical audit and its outcome, and by striving to complete the full clinical audit cycle, including implementation and re-evaluation. Challenges for the successful implementation of clinical audits include the following: (1) Recognizing the value of clinical audits by residents and shared responsibility at work for auditing and improving patient care, (2) Department leadership’s support in ensuring commitment from all healthcare professionals to clinical audits as a QI Instrument, (3) Faculty role modeling in line with the department leadership’s goal that quality improvement is an integral part of daily practice, and (4) Healthcare professionals’ commitment to fostering teamwork and collaborative learning.ConclusionsThis study shows that clinical audits can support residents in developing QI awareness and QI skills; however, this effectiveness was variable. Progression beyond data collection and analysis towards implementing improvements and demonstrating emerging QI leadership behaviors was limited and strongly shaped by the clinical learning environment. Effective development of these skills and behaviors therefore depends on a supportive environment in which medical staff values clinical audits, actively participates in the audit process, and embeds audits within departments’ clinical governance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09541-9.

Background

Postgraduate medical education (PGME) programs increasingly acknowledge the need to integrate quality improvement (QI) initiatives in the training of residents so that they are well prepared for today’s complex healthcare systems [1–3]. Research shows that when residents lead QI initiatives, it not only contributes to their professional development but also influences the extent to which they prioritize QI in their future practice [4–6]. The Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) and the CanMEDS model both highlight leadership as a core competency in residency training, underscoring the need for residents to engage in quality improvement as part of their roles [4, 7–10]. Despite this emphasis, systematic reviews have found that meaningful resident engagement in QI remains inconsistent and that evidence of sustained impact on clinical practice is limited [11–13]. Moreover, leadership development in PGME has relied predominantly on formal programs, with few studies examining how leadership skills develop through workplace-based activities [14]. Given their frontline experience, medical residents have significant potential to lead improvements in care quality [6, 15–17]. At the same time, residency training is challenging, as residents need to cope with new responsibilities, tasks, and expectations while also gaining confidence in their role as doctors and medical specialists [18–22].

In this study, we focus on residents who must complete a clinical audit as a local, department-based initiative within their postgraduate training. This provides a learning experience to improve awareness of clinical governance, routinizing QI in the daily work of residents and medical staff such that it directly impacts daily clinical care [23]. Clinical audits, which systematically evaluate daily clinical practice against commonly agreed-upon standards to enhance performance and prevent poor care, are part of a continuous quality improvement process [24–27]. In a clinical audit, the resident, as an auditor, collects and analyses data, evaluates delivered care against agreed-upon standards, identifies shortcomings and their causes, and contributes to the design and implementation of improvements within a specific clinical setting.

Nordquist et al. [28] emphasized that learning in the clinical learning environment is fundamental in shaping residents’ training [28]. Defined as the “social interaction, organizational cultures and structures and physical and virtual spaces that surround and shape respondents’ experiences, perceptions and learning”, this environment is pivotal for preparing residents for advanced clinical responsibilities following their postgraduate training [29]. An essential component of readiness for postgraduate clinical practice is incorporating QI as an integral aspect of daily clinical practice. The clinical learning environment can either support or hinder residents’ learning through its social, organizational, and educational features [28, 30, 31]. Furthermore, supportive PGME environments contribute to better patient care by positively influencing residents and favorably shaping their current and future medical practices [32, 33]. While a growing body of research has evaluated structured QI curricula in residency programs and formal leadership development programs, less is known about how the clinical learning environment shapes residents’ development of QI skills and emerging QI leadership behaviors, when QI is embedded in daily clinical practice through instruments such as clinical audits [11, 12, 34, 35]. To date, the literature on successful improvements of quality of care has focused primarily on the attributes of QI leadership of physicians who have already attained leadership positions, leaving a gap in our understanding of how residents acquire QI skills and begin to exhibit QI leadership behaviors during training [2, 8, 9, 36, 37]. Here, we focus on whether and how residents develop QI awareness, QI skills, and emerging QI leadership behaviors through the use of clinical audits as a training tool. A deeper understanding of how residents experience and build QI skills and emerging QI leadership behaviors through QI projects could inform the design of training programs that more effectively cultivate QI competence and prepare residents for future QI leadership roles. This study addresses these research gaps through the following research questions:

How do residents develop QI awareness, QI skills and emerging QI leadership behaviors during a clinical audit as part of their residency training?How does the clinical learning environment affect the development of residents’ QI awareness, QI skills and emerging QI leadership behaviors?

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective multiple embedded case study in which two departments were investigated at several levels of analysis [38]. In this study, the cases corresponded to two clinical departments, with their own, specific clinical learning environment where residents conducted clinical audits, embedded within one academic medical center. We selected two departments for our study because we believe that variations in the clinical learning environment and the clinical audit approach between these departments could influence residents’ QI awareness and their development as QI leaders. The descriptive case study design allowed for the opportunity to gather a detailed description of the specific clinical learning environment through consideration of multiple perspectives i.e. supervisors, residents, program directors and quality officers. We defined QI awareness as residents’ professional awareness, reflection, and ability to improve the quality of their daily clinical care [6, 39]. QI skills were defined as the practical competencies involved in conducting a clinical audit, including data collection and analysis, evaluating care against standards, and proposing improvement actions. Where residents engagement with clinical audits appeared to extend beyond these core QI skills, for instance through influencing colleagues, mobilizing stakeholders, or contributing to the implementation of changes at the team or department level, we considered these as potential emerging QI leadership behaviors [4, 40]. We used the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist (COREQ) to facilitate comprehensive reporting (see Supplementary material 1) [41].

Setting and participants

We conducted this study at the University Medical Center Groningen (UMCG) in the Departments of Internal Medicine and of Critical Care (Table 1). The training program directors of both departments are dedicated to developing QI leadership behaviors in their residents, incorporating clinical audits as a learning experience in their residency training.

Context of clinical audit in the Netherlands

In the Department of Internal Medicine, clinical audits have been part of residency training since 2013. Residents receive an introductory session with the training program director, who encourages the identification of improvement opportunities. Residents consult with relevant medical staff, who possess specific expertise in the topic of their clinical audit and present their findings and proposed improvement actions in end-of-day meetings. The implementation and follow-up of these improvement actions are overseen by a dedicated quality officer, as part of the ongoing audit cycle.

In the Department of Critical Care, clinical audits were introduced in 2015 as a mandatory part of training. Residents conduct these audits in pairs in coordination with an intensivist (EdF), presenting findings and proposed improvement actions during morning shift handovers. The audit cycle, including the implementation and evaluation of improvements, is coordinated with an intensivist (EdF). Both departments encourage residents to propose their own audit topic to enhance their engagement and impact on daily clinical care. For example, a resident’s observation that erysipelas and cellulitis do not result in a different choice of antibiotic prompted a clinical audit on adherence to antibiotic guidelines in such cases. Additionally, residents can refer to a list of topics identified as issues in incident reporting analyses, while ensuring possible follow-up on frequently reported incidents.

Residents for this study were purposively selected if they conducted a clinical audit and presented audit findings in the past two years to minimize recall bias. In both departments, all residents were required to conduct and present a clinical audit as a mandatory component of their training; the study sample therefore represents the general population of residents who completed this requirement, rather than a self-selected subgroup of more engaged trainees. The departments’ secretaries recruited residents using a standardized invitation email.

Data collection

The interview guide, developed by RG, KA, GW, and LH-M, was based on a realist review of audits (see Supplementary material 2) [40]. This realist review investigated the working mechanisms of audit programs by describing not only the audit and its outcome (O), but also the context (C) and the underlying mechanism (M) in a so-called CMO configuration (CMOc). The interview questions were based on these CMOcs, as our initial aim was to investigate the practical validity of the CMOcs. The interview guide evolved dynamically through periodical reflections [40].

During data collection, most recruited residents were still in residency training; those who had finished their residency training were contacted through updated contact information. Additionally, supervising medical staff members and the quality officer were invited to participate because of their role during the clinical audit. Also, both program directors were invited because of their role in clinical audit development and residents’ learning.

During the interviews, the respondents discussed their work context, the supervisor’s role and involvement, suggested improvements, and the implementation of the latter within clinical care. Supervisors were specifically asked about their supervising role and ownership of the suggested improvements. LH-M (a female PhD student and nurse, who completed training in qualitative research) collected data between April 2017 and May 2021 through individual face-to-face interviews at a venue preferred by the respondent. Owing to COVID-19 restrictions, some semi structured interviews were conducted online via video conference using Teams. Data collection and processing were extended because of respondents’ and authors’ increased commitments related to the pandemic. Informed consent was obtained from all the respondents, who received a written explanation of the research and could ask further questions. The background of the researchers was explained to the respondents prior to the interviews. No specific biases were discussed. LH-M had no prior relationships with the respondents, except for program directors. LH-M conducted interviews within two years of the completion of the clinical audit. We conducted interviews until we reached data saturation, that is, when no new data emerged about QI awareness, QI skills, emerging QI leadership behaviors or the learning environment.33 All the interviews were recorded, transcribed, pseudonymized, and returned to the respondents for verification. No fieldnotes were taken.

Data analysis

Detailed case descriptions were constructed, outlining the context of the department, the resident’s role during the audit, and the clinical audit’s outcomes. The interview transcripts were managed and coded in ATLAS.ti (version 9) [42]. First, we treated the departments independently, using a thematic analysis approach [38]. Following recommendations for the development of leadership curricula [14, 43], we used iterative coding on the basis of factors emerging from the data, existing QI literature and codes from the Medical Leadership Competency Framework (MLCF) [36, 40, 44]. This framework starts with personal qualities such as developing self-awareness and progressing to leadership that impacts the system, such as encouraging improvement of quality and safety of patient care [44]. We chose the MLCF because it focuses on competences that can be developed during PGME. Additionally, this framework aligns with our conceptualization of QI awareness, QI skills and QI leadership, as developing leadership begins with learning about oneself [44–48]. We used abductive coding to integrate new data insights with theoretical knowledge [49–51]. Categories were then reviewed and refined, with themes aligned with the MLCF’s five domains. GW and LH-M separately coded two interviews, creating and discussing new codes for emergent themes. Thematic analysis continued until ten interviews were analyzed, after which LH-M coded the remaining interviews.

Next, LH-M conducted a cross-case analysis to compare conditions within and between departments affecting the development of QI awareness, QI skills and leadership. Our approach was guided by Coles et al., whose realist review provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how contextual factors influence healthcare QI initiatives [34]. The authors identified four key domains - leadership, organizational characteristics, change agents (crucial for driving success within the clinical context), and multidisciplinary collaboration - which they further refined into eight specific contextual factors. We applied this framework to gain deeper insight into the challenges within the clinical learning environment. In this way, we were able to better understand how the clinical learning environment influences the development of QI awareness, QI skills and emerging QI leadership behavior. We summarized the data into narratives with themes and quotations to draw conclusions [38]. Themes were reviewed with GW, RG, and KA for accuracy, and preliminary findings were discussed with EdF and JT for additional feedback. The final insights incorporated these perspectives for a comprehensive analysis.

Three researchers (RG, EdF, and JT), as senior medical specialists and supervisors within one of the two departments, provided an in-depth ‘first circle’ perspective’. Their insights, rooted in the local sociocultural context, complemented the ‘second circle’ perspective of three other researchers (LH-M, GW, and KA), who challenged assumptions about the learning environment and collaborative practices. This reflection and discussion process met the practice of reflexivity requirements [52]. We employed three types of triangulation: (1) data source triangulation by interviewing residents, supervisors, training program directors, and a quality officer within two departments; (2) investigator triangulation with two researchers coding data; and (3) research group triangulation by involving an interprofessional team with varied perspectives in nursing (LH-M), implementation science and quality management (GW), healthcare management (KA), and medical and postgraduate education (EdF, JT, and RG). This approach integrated diverse types of expertise, enriching the analysis and discussions [53].

Results

In total, 21 respondents were interviewed, 11 residents, 7 supervisors, one quality officer and two training program directors overseeing PGME in each department. Five of the eleven (45%) residents were female. Four potential respondents declined or were unable to schedule an interview after three attempts. The average interview lasted 49 min, with three interviews (14%) conducted online.

The results are presented in two sections. The first section describes how residents develop QI awareness, QI skills and emerging QI leadership behavior, categorized by the MLCF domains (see Table 2) [44]. The second section describes four challenges for the successful implementation of clinical audits for QI awareness, QI skills and QI leadership behaviors in PGME, which align with established contextual factors influencing healthcare QI initiatives (see Table 3) [34]. The second section describes four challenges for the successful implementation of clinical audits in supporting QI awareness, QI skills and QI leadership behaviors in PGME, which align with established contextual factors influencing healthcare QI initiatives.

Development of residents’ QI awareness, QI skills and emerging QI leadership behaviors

Building residents’ QI awareness through increased experiential knowledge

Residents build QI awareness through increased experiential knowledge gained from conducting clinical audits. Both departments initially intended to encourage critical assessment of care, and, as a result, the audits increased residents’ awareness of improvement opportunities in their daily routines. This awareness often translated into a strong sense of urgency and a drive for improvement, especially when residents were knowledgeable and had an affinity with the audit topic. In contrast, some residents regarded the clinical audit primarily as a mandatory task in their training, lacking clear affinity with their clinical audit subject. Consequently, these residents invested less effort and did not experience the same increase in experiential knowledge. As a result, these residents not only failed to cultivate a sense of urgency but also had fewer opportunities to develop QI skills such as critically appraising care processes or formulating improvement actions.

Raising residents’ QI awareness and QI skills by presenting audit data and inspiring colleagues to implement change

Some residents indicated that the clinical audit positioned them as inspirational sources for other colleagues, even when residents themselves lacked motivation or affinity with the clinical audit’s subject. During their clinical audit presentations, residents challenge current daily practices using audit data. In doing so, residents practiced core QI skills: Interpreting audit data, translating findings into actionable insights, and communicating these to colleagues. Following these presentations, colleagues, including medical staff and other residents, were encouraged to critically examine their daily work and adapt their daily working routines. Residents observed a change in QI awareness in themselves as colleagues began to recognize their expertise and sought guidance on the audit subject.

Strengthening residents’ shared QI responsibility and demonstrating emerging QI leadership behaviors legitimized by the clinical audit and its outcome

After completing their clinical audit, several residents independently took on QI roles, feeling legitimized and empowered by the audit’s outcome. These residents demonstrated emerging QI leadership behaviors: They not only exhibited commitment to QI but also motivated and positively influenced medical staff and fellow residents. The QI skills residents had developed during the audit process, particularly their ability to present evidence-based findings, lent credibility to their efforts to engage colleagues. Some residents mobilized and engaged nurses in implementing changes, recognizing nurses’ crucial role in sustaining these changes in daily practice. This strategic involvement of stakeholders was seen by residents as essential for embedding changes into daily practice. Despite these efforts, most residents mentioned that they felt that they were merely passers-by rather than being recognized and fully integrated as members of the medical team. They felt they lacked the positional authority to influence others and drive the implementation of improvements - a constraint that limited the development of QI leadership behaviors beyond basic QI skills, particularly given the limited duration of their residency. This sentiment was echoed by supervisors and medical staff from both departments, who largely viewed residents as merely passers-by rather than full members of the care team.

Some residents demonstrate emerging QI leadership by striving to complete the full clinical audit cycle, including implementation and re-evaluation

Some residents demonstrated emerging QI leadership behaviors by taking proactive measures from the start to organize the implementation stages of their clinical audit, e.g. one resident disseminated the relevance of precise and systematic documentation in end-of-life care during interactions with junior doctors if the opportunity arose. However, some residents faced challenges completing the full audit cycle - particularly the implementation and re-evaluation stages - due to their residency ending, often implicitly relying on experienced supervisors for sustaining changes. This reliance underscores the importance of an experienced supervisor in supporting residents’ development of QI skills, particularly the ability to plan and execute improvement actions and to organize re-evaluation of outcomes. Residents indicated that the PDCA cycle of a clinical audit often outlasted their residency, and they rarely actively transferred ownership of the audit results to other team members. This issue was more pronounced when residents lacked affinity with their clinical audit topic or when they did not receive clear guidance or encouragement from their supervisors for defining improvement actions, underscoring that the formulation of improvement actions is a critical QI skill that requires deliberate supervision and mentorship to develop effectively.

Challenges for the successful implementation of clinical audits for the development of QI awareness,QI skills,and QI leadership in PGME

Challenge 1: Recognizing the value of clinical audits by residents and shared responsibility at work for auditing and improving patient care

First, it should be highlighted that only a minority of the residents in both departments recognized the impact of clinical audits, with few seeing clinical audits as a meaningful process to improve care quality. Most residents felt unsupported, felt no shared responsibility with other medical staff for their clinical audit process and expressed the mandatory character of the clinical audit as a “tick-box” exercise. In the Department of Critical Care, where audits were directed by a staff member, residents sometimes felt pressured rather than taking part in a collaborative effort to increase patient care.

Supervisors and program directors of both departments viewed clinical audits as valuable, manageable QI exercises, but residents perceived them as an additional burden. One resident described this period as: “exceptionally busy”. Notably, residents with prior research experience were able to transfer data collection and analysis skills to the audit context, suggesting that these QI skills, when already partially developed, facilitate both the execution of and engagement with clinical audits. This set of skills not only motivated them but also facilitated effective execution of the clinical audit and enabled more efficient workload management. This suggests that research- and data-oriented experience potentially benefits residents by helping them meet the demands of clinical audit activities and building confidence in data collection and analysis.

Challenge 2: Department leadership’s support in ensuring commitment from all healthcare professionals to clinical audits as a QI instrument

The second challenge relevant to the clinical learning environment involved the commitment of all relevant stakeholders to clinical audits as a QI instrument. The Internal Medicine Department held end-of-day meetings that sometimes conflict with other staff duties. Conversely, the Department of Critical Care scheduled presentations during the morning shift handover “allowing most of the medical staff to join”. However, within both departments, the timing of the clinical audit presentations overlapped with the nursing staff’s shift handover, resulting in minimal attendance by nursing team members.

Especially in the Internal Medicine Department, residents and supervisors noted the limited attendance during clinical audit presentations, with non-attending medical staff often lacking engagement with the audit subject prioritizing other (clinical) activities. Those who did attend were willing to review practices and often emphasized the importance of following up on improvements. Despite efforts to summarize audit outcomes in online news feeds, this rarely led to changes in daily practice.

Limited attendance by medical staff hindered residents’ motivation to share knowledge and implement changes, impacting their opportunities to practice QI skills such as presenting findings and proposing changes, and to develop emerging QI leadership behaviors. One resident noted that “the strongest nonbelievers in the clinical audit approach were absent during the presentations”. Both residents and supervisors noted that it was particularly challenging to engage skeptical or less willing staff, especially when audits focused on basic care aspects of clinical care such as nutrition or thrombosis prophylaxis. In those situations residents and supervisors noted that apparently not all staff members acknowledge a shared responsibility for quality-of-care issues beyond their specialty or clinical activities.

Challenge 3: Faculty role modeling in line with department leadership’s goal that quality improvement is an integral part of daily practice

Some residents strategically chose knowledgeable and committed supervisors, understanding that selecting a clinical opinion leader with strong interest in the subject was crucial for following up on recommended improvements. These supervisors, serving as QI leaders and role models, took charge when the audit topic aligned with their expertise, leading to follow-up actions and fostering incremental changes. Motivated by their supervisors’ dedication, residents actively organized follow-up activities, thereby practicing QI skills related to planning and coordinating improvement actions while contributing to meaningful patient care improvements. Some supervisors also used audit outcomes to persuade other staff to alter their daily practices.

Some residents felt “disappointment” when the perceived lack of staff support hindered the sustained follow-up of improvements after completing their residency training, given the feeling that the clinical audit had been “a waste of time”. This sentiment occurred regardless of whether the residents had chosen the audit topic themselves. Residents in the Department of Critical Care, mentioned feeling discouraged by a lack of perceived staff commitment to their audit when returning from a rotation in another department or returning for a fellowship. For example, a resident aiming to lead QI efforts saw her project falter due to shifting department priorities during her absence, leaving her with insufficient time and support to implement the proposed improvements.

Challenge 4: Healthcare professionals’ commitment to fostering teamwork and collaborative learning

A major challenge within the clinical learning environment was fostering effective teamwork and continuous learning. This involves healthcare professionals sharing responsibility for high-quality and safe patient care through close collaboration, with tasks assigned on the basis of their expertise. This approach creates a thriving environment for residents’ development of QI awareness and QI leadership. However, residents have struggled with promoting interprofessional teamwork during clinical audits because of the limited nurse involvement and competing clinical priorities of medical staff. The Department of Critical Care purposefully encouraged collaborative learning by having two residents work together on an audit to develop teamwork and leadership skills. Although this approach aimed to shift physicians from individualistic roles to collaborative leaders, residents from the Department of Critical Care merely divided tasks between the two of them, unaware of the broader goals, including the development of collaborative QI skills such as shared problem analysis and joint improvement planning. This suggests a gap in residents’ understanding of the intended collaborative approach.

Discussion

This study aims to deepen our understanding of how residents develop QI awareness and QI leadership while undertaking a clinical audit as part of their residency training. Our findings indicate that residents develop QI awareness and QI skills – particularly in data collection, analysis, and communicating findings to colleagues - through increased experiential knowledge and by presenting audit data that challenges current practices. In some cases, residents demonstrated emerging QI leadership behaviors - taking shared responsibility for care quality, mobilizing colleagues, and striving to complete the full audit cycle. However, these leadership behaviors were not consistently observed; most residents primarily developed QI awareness and QI skills rather than sustained QI leadership behaviors. However, the successful implementation of clinical audits in PGME faces significant challenges. These include recognizing the value of clinical audits by residents and shared responsibility at work for auditing and improving patient care, department leadership’s support in ensuring commitment from all healthcare professionals to clinical audits as a QI instrument, and faculty role modeling in line with department leadership’s goal that quality improvement is an integral part of daily practice. Additionally, teamwork and collaborative learning do not spontaneously arise when residents are paired for audit tasks.

Residents were more likely to develop and apply QI skills, such as formulating improvement actions and organizing follow-up demonstrate, when they received support from medical staff and when there was a shared, collective belief in the value of clinical audits. Nevertheless, some residents expressed frustration when audits did not result in improved care quality, thus lowering their motivation and drive for QI. Voogt et al. [53] and van de Voort [47] also stressed the importance of staff commitment to enact recommended changes to maintain residents’ faith in QI initiatives [48, 54].

The CanMEDS framework and the ACGME stress the importance of teamwork and adapting communication in interprofessional settings [4, 55]. Despite these guidelines, our data revealed significant gaps in interprofessional teamwork. Collaboration among residents during joint audits was limited, with tasks being split rather than shared. Collaborative learning with other healthcare professionals, such as nurses, was neglected hindering interprofessional teamwork and implementing change. Previous research has indicated that interprofessional leadership programs enhance collaboration and team-based care [43]. Moreover, effective QI leadership requires skills such as acknowledging others’ contributions, active listening, and collaboration [40, 56, 57]. Despite structuring PGME according to CanMEDS with attention to collaboration and teamwork, current medical training still largely focuses on the roles of medical expert and scholar. Both residency programs lack sufficient emphasis on developing the role of interprofessional collaborator and the importance of teamwork in achieving optimal clinical care. Addressing this gap is essential for enhancing clinical outcomes and improving quality improvement efforts. Strengthening the focus on interprofessional collaboration within training programs is therefore critical.

This study highlights the crucial role of the clinical learning environment in developing residents’ QI awareness, QI skills and emerging QI leadership behavior. The challenges identified in this study are linked to organizational culture and leadership. Effective QI initiatives rely on strong leadership and a supportive organizational culture, which includes motivation, open communication, and shared values. The interviews further emphasized the impact of the ‘hidden curriculum’, with the beliefs and attitudes of residents and staff toward QI activities impacting the outcome and implementation of QI initiatives [58, 59]. When staff visibly deprioritize audit activities, residents miss opportunities to practice QI skills in a supported setting, reinforcing the perception that these skills are peripheral to clinical training rather than integral to it. Therefore, fostering a positive organizational culture and addressing hidden curriculum factors are essential for the successful implementation of QI initiatives and the development of residents’ QI skills.

Our findings align with US-based research on resident QI engagement. Butler et al. reported that residents view QI as isolated and burdensome rather than integrated into clinical work - mirroring our finding that many residents perceived audits as a “tick-box” exercise [60]. Jones et al.‘s realist review identified trained local faculty and alignment between educational and clinical systems as key success factors, consistent with our Challenges 2 and 3 [35]. Koller et al. proposed practical strategies including meaningful project selection and faculty coaching - recommendations that closely parallel our practical implications [61]. While most of these studies evaluate structured QI curricula, our study examines how QI awareness and skills develop through a clinically embedded instrument (i.e. the clinical audit) capturing dimensions such as the hidden curriculum and interprofessional dynamics.

On the basis of the interviews, the current approach to mentoring residents during clinical audits appears insufficient. The data indicate a need for mentorship by QI-trained staff, particularly in supporting residents through the later stages of the audit cycle, such as translating findings into improvement actions and planning re-evaluation, where the demands on QI skills increase and residents most often encounter difficulties. Effective mentors not only teach QI concepts but also assist in planning follow-up actions, which is crucial for realizing residents’ QI leadership potential and can support lasting relationships beyond the formal completion of a rotation or residency training [15, 43, 62, 63]. Similarly, our findings are supported by the residents’ learning experiences, which highlight that residents view their supervisors as role models and replicate their behaviors [59]. Additionally, resistance from colleagues, especially influential peers, is a known barrier to improvement efforts [64–66]. To sustain QI outcomes and foster effective QI leadership, strengthening the mentor’s role and addressing the organizational culture in which healthcare professionals collectively commit to implementing suggested improvements are essential.

This study builds on Coles et al.’s framework for understanding how contextual factors influence healthcare QI initiatives [34]. In our research, this approach enabled us to move beyond viewing contextual factors as mere barriers to improvement. While existing studies often classify contextual factors as either barriers or facilitators, this binary perspective overlooks the dynamic and complex nature of real-world settings [67]. What hinders QI in one context might facilitate it in another, as these factors often represent the everyday conditions of practice [68]. By applying this framework, we were able to identify, understand, and analyze how these challenges differentially affect the development of QI awareness (which most residents achieved to some degree), QI skills (which developed unevenly depending on supervisor support and task engagement), and emerging QI leadership behaviors (which were observed only in a subset of residents). Additionally, using the MLCF framework helped us understand how QI leadership evolves from self-awareness to driving improvements in quality and patient safety. This approach not only enhances our understanding of contextual influences but also aids in developing practical implications for improving QI initiatives and leadership in healthcare settings. Currently, there is no consensus on the most effective components of QI in medical education, and few studies have included objective measures to assess curricular success [35]. Notably, challenges to resident engagement in QI identified in the literature include competing demands, didactics not connected with meaningful work, suboptimal and incomplete experiential learning, lack of clear accountability, lack of timely and relevant data, and lack of faculty coach and role model [61, 69]. In line with Sadowski et al.’s recommendation, we provide practical implications for addressing challenges in implementing clinical audits in PGME (see Table 4) [43].

Practical implications

Our findings point to four areas for practical improvement, summarized in (Table 4). First, clinical audits should be embedded within the organization’s quality governance structure, with faculty and nursing teams involved in selecting audit topics to align them with departmental priorities. Second, supervisors require training in QI coaching and mentoring to support in developing QI skills beyond data collection, specifically in analyzing findings against care standards, formulating actionable improvements, and planning re-evaluation. Third, to counter residents being viewed as ‘passers-by’, roles and expectations regarding the clinical audit should be explicitly defined at the start of each rotation, with relevant stakeholders engaged throughout the process to foster genuine collaboration rather than task division. Finally, QI activities should be integrated into residents’ workload planning and assessed through entrustable professional activities (EPAs) to ensure they serve their educational purpose.

Strengths and limitations

Our study focused on the development of informal and frontline QI leadership by performing a clinical audit during training. This choice aligns with recent literature on medical leadership and leadership theorists who distinguish between formal and informal medical leadership in professional contexts such as hospital care [36].

Our study has its limitations. First, we collected data in two departments within a single Dutch Academic Medical Center, which may limit the transferability of the findings. However, residency training in the Netherlands follows a competency-based framework that is used worldwide. Second, since we conducted this qualitative study after the completion of clinical audits, recall bias may be a concern. We minimized this risk by only selecting recent clinical audits and interviewing respondents within two years of their completion. Third, this study lacked the perspective of those on the receiving end of the clinical audit. Future studies should explore all team members’ experiences regarding QI awareness, QI skills, QI leadership, and collaborative learning. Finally, our study views emerging QI leadership behaviors as a surrogate for care quality but we did not measure healthcare quality outcomes. While our interviews provided insight into residents’ QI awareness, QI skills and emerging QI leadership development, future research should evaluate the relationship between residents’ QI awareness, skills and leadership, as part of a team effort, and healthcare quality.

Conclusions

This study shows that clinical audits can support residents in developing QI awareness and QI skills, though the extent of this development varied considerably: while most residents gained basic QI skills in data collection and analysis, fewer progressed to formulating and implementing improvement actions, and this progression was strongly shaped by the clinical learning environment. While some residents demonstrated emerging QI leadership behaviors, such as mobilizing colleagues and driving implementation of improvements, many regarded audits as a mandatory task with limited perceived value, and the completion of full audit cycles was frequently constrained by rotation length and insufficient staff engagement. The effective development of QI awareness and skills depends on a supportive learning environment in which medical staff values clinical audits, actively participates in the audit process, and embeds audits within departments’ clinical governance. Without these conditions, clinical audits risk being perceived as a tick-box exercise rather than a meaningful learning experience.
