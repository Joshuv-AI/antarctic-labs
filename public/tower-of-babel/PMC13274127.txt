Strategies for Implementing Nursing Activities for Chronic Pain Management in Primary Care: Integrated Findings from a Sequential Mixed-Methods Study
Authors: Andréanne Bernier, Marie-Eve Poitras, Marie-Dominique Poirier, Sylvie Beaudoin, Anaïs Lacasse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic pain (CP) affects one in five Canadians, representing a major public health issue. Its economic burden is estimated between CAD $38.3–$40.4 billion annually. Despite its prevalence, integration of nursing activities in CP management within primary care remains limited, indicating a need for strategies that facilitate effective implementation.AimDetermine implementation strategies to promote the integration of nursing activities in CP management in primary care, considering nursing practice realities and patient needs.MethodsA sequential explanatory mixed-methods study (QUAN→qual) was conducted in collaboration with two patient partners. A Delphi study captured prioritized nursing activities for CP management, while qualitative focus groups explored nurses’ barriers and facilitators in CP management. Findings were integrated through a joint display comparing quantitative and qualitative results following Fetters’ (2019) recommendations, in order to identify implementation strategies.ResultsFindings indicated a substantial gap between prioritized nursing activities for CP management and current practice, highlighting that nursing involvement remains at an early stage of implementation. Two key orientations emerge: (1) Strengthening nurses’ capacities to integrate CP-related activities by providing appropriate tools, training, and clinical guidance, (2) Mobilizing other healthcare system interest-holders (patients living with CP, healthcare providers, and managers) to support adoption of new nursing activities and create an environment conducive to change.ConclusionThe study underscores the importance of implementation strategies to foster the adoption of nursing activities in CP management. These strategies provide an implementation blueprint for maximizing nurses’ contribution to CP management in primary care through full deployment of their competencies.KEYWORDS: Chronic pain, management, treatment, nursing activities, nurse, primary care, mixed-methods

Introduction

Chronic pain (CP) represents a major public health issue in Canada, significantly affecting individuals’ quality of life and imposing a considerable burden on healthcare systems.1 It is estimated that up to one in five adults suffers from CP,2–5 leading to substantial personal, societal, and economic burdens (38–40 billion Canadian dollars [CAD] per year).6 Primary care settings often serve as the first point of contact for individuals with chronic diseases,7 highlighting the critical role of primary care providers in CP management.8

While evidence-based interventions for CP management exist, their implementation in primary care remains suboptima.6 Physician-centered models of care have often fallen short in effectively managing this multifaceted condition.9 Limitations such as time constraints, insufficient training in pain management, and a lack of interdisciplinary collaboration have hindered the delivery of comprehensive care.10,11 Consequently, there is an urgent need to explore innovative approaches that enhance the capacity of primary care to manage CP effectively.12

Integrating primary care nurses into CP management emerges as a promising strategy to address current challenges.13–15 Primary care nurses bring a holistic perspective to patient care, emphasizing patient education, self-management support, and coordination of services.16 Their involvement can facilitate more comprehensive assessments, personalized care plans, and ongoing monitoring, which are essential components in managing CP.17 Evidence suggests that nurse-led interventions in primary care can lead to improved patient outcomes, increased patient satisfaction, and more efficient use of healthcare resources.18,19 Despite growing recognition of the importance of interdisciplinary approaches in CP management, the specific contributions of primary care nurses remain underexplored in the literature, and their involvement in CP care remains limited.20–22 Although nurses express a willingness to contribute more actively, they report a lack of clinical guidance, insufficient training, and ambiguous role definitions as key obstacles that limit their ability to assume a substantive role in CP management.10,20 This gap underscores the need for research that clarifies the potential scope of nursing practice, concrete pain management nursing activities, and implementation strategies necessary to support practice.23–25

Implementation science offers a valuable framework for examining the conditions that enable or impede the uptake of innovations in healthcare.26 By focusing on strategies that foster the adoption and sustainability of evidence-based practices in real-world settings, it provides insight into the professional, organizational, and systemic factors that shape the evolution of nursing practice.27 Grounded in this perspective, the present integration of results from a mixed-methods study aimed to identify implementation strategies to support the integration of nursing activities in CP management, taking into account the realities of primary care nurses and the needs of patients living with CP, within the context of Family Medicine Groups in Quebec, Canada. Building upon previously published quantitative findings (Delphi study to establish and prioritize nursing activities for CP management in primary care)28 and qualitative findings (focus groups about barriers and facilitators to implementing these activities),29 this mixed-methods paper integrates quantitative and qualitative findings to generate novel insights, demonstrating how their combination yields a richer understanding than either method could alone (“1 + 1 = 3 effect”).

Method

Study design

We conducted this study using an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design (QUAN → qual),30 beginning with the collection and analysis of quantitative data and then using those results to inform a subsequent qualitative phase designed to complement and enrich the findings. Figure 1 provides a visual representation of this design, which included two phases: the quantitative phase focused on generating a list and prioritizing nursing activities for CP management in primary care through a Delphi study, and the qualitative phase allowed in-depth exploration of the barriers and facilitators to implementing these nursing activities.

Integration of Knowledge-to-Action (KTA) Phases33 and Sequential Explanatory Mixed-methods (MM) Study.

Integration in this mixed-methods study occurred at two points: 1) the connection between the quantitative and qualitative phases, where qualitative data collection and analysis were guided by quantitative findings; and 2) the comparison of the results of each phase at the end. Although each phase was conducted independently (i.e., data were collected, analyzed, and interpreted separately), the qualitative phase was intentionally informed by the quantitative findings to enrich the understanding of nursing practices and challenges in CP management, as suggested in the literature.31 The detailed methods and results of the individual phases, published elsewhere,28,29 are summarized here as foundational inputs to the integration process. In the present paper, we present new insights emerging from the integration of complementary results. The prioritized list of nursing activities (quantitative phase) and themes related to professional, organizational, and systemic factors influencing implementation (qualitative phase) together inform implementation strategies to promote the uptake of nursing activities in CP management. The manuscript was written in accordance with the Good Reporting of A Mixed-Methods Study (GRAMMS) checklist.32

Framework and approach

Our project was guided by the Knowledge-to-Action (KTA) framework,33 which includes seven phases for structuring the knowledge translation process: 1) Identify the problem and review knowledge; 2) Adapt knowledge to local context; 3) Assess barriers and facilitators to knowledge use; 4) Select, tailor, and implement interventions; 5) Monitor knowledge use; 6) Evaluate outcomes; and 7) Sustain knowledge use. In the overall mixed-methods study, we aligned our project with four key phases of the KTA framework.

Following a literature review13 (Phase 1: Identify the problem and review knowledge), we conducted a quantitative study to prioritize nursing activities for CP management through a Delphi survey28 (Phase 2: Adapting knowledge to the local context). Next, we conducted focus groups (qualitative study)29 to assess barriers and facilitators to implementing these activities (Phase 3: Assessed barriers and facilitators). Finally (this study), we integrated the quantitative and qualitative findings to develop tailored implementation strategies (Phase 4: Selecting, tailoring, and implementing interventions), thereby advancing our overall goal of facilitating the implementation of nursing activities in CP management in primary care.

Patient and public involvement

The aim of patient involvement was to enhance the relevance of the study, with the goal of improving the quality of care for individuals with CP. Two patient partners with long-term lived experience of CP were involved throughout all stages of the quantitative and qualitative components of the study, following best practice recommendations from the Initiative on Methods, Measurement, and Pain Assessment in Clinical Trials (IMMPACT) for patient engagement in pain research,34 with reporting guided by the Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public (GRIPP2-SF) reporting checklist.35 Their involvement contributed to refining research questions, informing data collection and analysis, and strengthening the interpretation and contextualization of findings, particularly regarding the applicability of implementation strategies in real-world primary care settings. Overall, patient involvement meaningfully influenced the study design, interpretation, and knowledge translation outputs.

Study populations and setting

Both primary care nurses and patients living with CP were included in the quantitative phase (Delphi study prioritizing nursing activities for CP management in primary care).28 However, the qualitative phase, designed to allow in-depth exploration of the barriers and facilitators to implementing these nursing activities, involved only primary care nurses, as they are the ones directly concerned by the integration of these activities into their clinical practice.29

Primary care nurses are registered nurses who typically hold a bachelor’s degree and provide a broad range of services for individuals across the lifespan, including the management of both acute and chronic health conditions.36 In Canada, primary care models vary widely.7 In the province of Quebec, interprofessional primary care practices are called ‘Family Medicine Groups’ (FMGs) [Groupes de médecine de famille (GMF)] and represent the predominant organizational structure, with 386 groups as of 2024.37,38 Family Medicine Groups typically include family physicians, registered nurses (primary care nurses), and other health and social services professionals (e.g., nurse practitioners, social workers, pharmacists, physiotherapists, psychologists), with team composition depending on available resources and provincial agreements.39 Care delivery and the implementation of professional roles are guided by a co-management structure involving local health network administrators and physician leaders.39

However, despite this interprofessional model, CP management within Family Medicine Groups remains largely physician-led,40 with primary care nurses playing a limited role.20 This situation is further compounded by the fact that approximately 78% of Quebec residents are not formally registered with a physician in a Family Medicine Group,41 potentially limiting access to coordinated, team-based CP care.

Nurses’ inclusion criteria for both phases of this study were as follows: 1) current provincial professional licensure, and 2) employment within a Family Medicine Group. The initial pool of primary care nurses for the quantitative phase was recruited between November 2023 and January 2024 through a multi-strategy, web-based approach.28 For the qualitative phase, a maximum variation sampling strategy was employed.29 Personalized invitations to participate in online focus groups (conducted between August and November 2024) were emailed to primary care nurses who had previously participated in the quantitative phase.

As for patients living with CP, inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) reporting persistent or recurring pain for over 3 months, as defined by the International Classification of Diseases [ICD-11],42 2) self-reported registration in an interprofessional primary care team, and 3) living in the province of Québec. Given the diversity of health conditions encountered by primary care nurses in Family Medicine Groups and the fact that CP is not systematically diagnosed, in this study the term CP refers to any condition in which pain persists for more than 3 months, regardless of whether it is primary (not attributable to another condition) or secondary (resulting from an underlying condition). Accordingly, the study focuses on the management of CP, regardless of its onset, context, or etiology. Patients were recruited from a other study conducted by the research team (pool of participants who had agreed to be recontacted).43

Ethics approval was obtained from the University of Quebec in Abitibi-Témiscamingue Ethics Committee. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before their involvement in both quantitative and qualitative phases.

Within this mixed-methods design, a brief overview of previously published findings from each phase is provided to orient the reader, while the Results section of this article focuses exclusively on the new integrated findings derived from the mixed-methods integration. Detailed methodological procedures, participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, and findings from the quantitative phase of the study have been published elsewhere,28 and findings from the qualitative phase have also been published separately.29

Quantitative phase of the mixed-methods study

To establish and prioritize nursing activities for CP management in primary care, we conducted a three-round, web-based Delphi survey44 involving two key interest-holder groups: nurses working in primary care practices (n = 48) and patients living with CP (n = 122). The Poitras et al.45 framework, developed by a member of our research team from a systematic review of primary care nursing activities for individuals with chronic conditions, categorizes activities into four domains: global assessment, care management, health promotion, and interprofessional collaboration. This framework guided the classification and interpretation of nursing activities in CP management. Forty-one activities reached the consensus threshold and were distributed across four domains.

Qualitative phase of the mixed-methods study

A descriptive interpretive approach46 was employed to explore perceived barriers and facilitators to implementing the nursing activities identified. Four online focus groups47 with 21 primary care nurses were conducted, guided by the Integrated-Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Services (i-PARIHS) framework.27 This framework emphasizes the dynamic interactions among three key components in the implementation of evidence-based practices: Innovation (the activities or intervention), Recipients (those involved or affected), and Context (the environment in which implementation occurs). The analysis revealed a range of professional, organizational, and systemic factors shaping implementation.

Innovation – integration of CP management into primary care nursing

The integration of CP management is challenged by the unclear distinction between acute and CP, discomfort with providing support for psychosocial aspects, and the absence of practical tools and guidance. How CP care could be structured and delivered remains unclear, creating tensions between biomedical and holistic approaches. Facilitators include an online toolkit with video capsules and flowcharts to support clinical judgment. Nurses identified overlaps with chronic disease care, which could help them apply existing skills. They also perceived added value in their role through patient education and continuity of care.

Recipients – characteristics of nursing staff and professional practice

Nurses expressed a desire for recognition and professional growth but reported limited CP-specific training and low awareness of available learning opportunities. Barriers included limited knowledge of multimodal strategies, lack of awareness of community resources for CP, and workload and scheduling constraints that made it difficult to address CP during consultations. Unclear role boundaries, reliance on physicians, and perceived high patient expectations contributed to role tensions. Despite this, nurses showed strong motivation to learn, openness to peer support, and appreciation for multimodal approaches. A virtual community of practice was seen as a potential enabler of sustained practice change.

Context – primary care settings and healthcare system

CP is not consistently prioritized, and nurses are often excluded from care planning decisions. Administrative complexity and medical gatekeeping were seen as barriers to nursing innovation. Some organizations have begun supporting CP-related pilot projects, aiding nursing role integration. System-level issues such as poor coordination and limited access to specialized services were identified as major challenges. Inter-organizational mentorship and collaboration with pain clinics were viewed as promising supports for long-term transformation.

Integration of the quantitative and qualitative phases (present study)

In line with the fourth phase of the KTA framework (Selecting, tailoring, and implementing interventions), quantitative and qualitative results were integrated using the four-stage process proposed by Fetters,48 enabling systematic comparison and interpretation within a unified analytic framework. Despite recent advances in mixed-methods research, the operationalization of integration at the analysis stage remains challenging.49 Fetters’ approach is widely cited, notably in health research,50–52 and provides practical, structured guidance for mixed-methods integration, including the use of joint displays.49

Rogers’ Diffusion of Innovations (DOI) theory,53 a recognized framework in implementation science,54 was used to map the adoption stages of nursing activities domain, providing a temporal lens to identify how strategies could influence uptake at both the individual and system levels, treating adoption as a dynamic process (Table 1). This temporal lens was particularly relevant because it enabled the team to prioritize and tailor implementation strategies according to the current stage of adoption in each nursing activity domain, ensuring that strategies targeted key points for advancing integration in primary care.

Rogers’ Diffusion of Innovations theory, Stages and Strategies.

Our implementation strategies were also structured using the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy,55 a comprehensive framework of 73 discrete implementation strategies that provides guidance for selecting, tailoring, and operationalizing interventions to improve implementation outcomes and also facilitates transparency and replicability. ERIC was used to systematically select, label, and classify each strategy, linking it to specific nursing activity domains and adoption stages.

The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings was conducted with consideration of the research team’s positionality to enhance reflexivity and interpretive rigor. The first author, a registered nurse with 15 years of primary care experience, led the process, supported by an interdisciplinary team including a nurse researcher with over 10 years of primary care research experience, a researcher with more than 15 years of experience in CP epidemiology and health services research, and two patient partners with over 20 years of lived experience with CP and primary and specialized care seeking. Rigor was enhanced through iterative discussion of joint displays, explicit comparison of quantitative and qualitative findings, investigator triangulation, and validation with patient partners.

Fetters’ approach Step 1: development of joint displays

Four joint display tables were created to visually align and compare findings from both phases (Supplementary Material 1 to 4). Each table represented one of the four nursing activity domains identified in the quantitative phase: global assessment, care management, health promotion, and interprofessional collaboration.45 Rows present barriers and facilitators identified in the qualitative phase, while columns reflect the three components of the i-PARIHS framework27 (Innovation, Recipients, Context).

Step 2: mapping factors influencing implementation

The joint displays were then used to link factors influencing the implementation of each nursing activity domain. ‘Innovation’ included factors enabling or hindering the operationalization of the domain; ‘Recipients’ reflected influences on nurses’ capacity and willingness to carry out its activities; and ‘Context’ indicated whether the structural conditions necessary for implementation were present. This mapping helped identify the key enablers and barriers to adopting each domain in practice.

Step 3: examining relationships between results

The relationships between quantitative and qualitative results were examined through comparison for each domain of nursing activities (Tables 2–5). This step allowed the qualitative findings to contextualize and explain the quantitative results, producing an integrated interpretation that goes beyond a descriptive listing of nursing activities and highlights their potential for successful implementation in primary care.

Following Fetters’ typology,48 relationships were classified as confirmation (factors already in place that support the adoption of nursing activities), complementarity (different factors that require coordination to facilitate adoption), expansion (levers that encourage the integration of new nursing activities), and discordance (opposing factors that hinder the adoption of nursing activities). For example, the comparison identified activities perceived as important but poorly implemented, and vice versa, with qualitative data clarifying the barriers and facilitators influencing adoption. Building on these relationships, targeted implementation strategies (Tables 2–5) were developed for each domain of nursing activities, based on the key stages of DOI theory53 (Table 1) and the ERIC taxonomy.55

Step 4: interpreting and reporting

This step focused on reporting the integrated findings and translating them into actionable insights capable of informing and guiding the implementation process in our study. First, guided by DOI theory,53 the overall analysis identified the current baseline of implementation, reflecting the general stage of adoption of CP-related nursing activities among primary care nurses.

Building on this baseline, the analysis then focused on structuring the forthcoming implementation process. Based on DOI theory,53 which highlights the interaction between individual adoption and organizational factors, the implementation process was framed around two overarching axes: nurses’ readiness to adopt CP-related practices and the organizational and professional context shaping implementation. Then, implementation strategies emerging from the four domains of care (Tables 2–5) were grouped according to conceptual similarity (e.g., training in CP management and training in CP self-management). As a result, a four-level hierarchical classification (draft of the implementation blueprint according to the ERIC taxonomy)55 was inductively developed to present the synthesis in an ordered and actionable manner: 1) broad axes representing the overall vision, 2) to objectives defining the intended purpose (why), 3) orientations describing the preferred pathways for action (how), and finally 4) implementation strategies specifying concrete actions to be undertaken.

Results

The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings highlighted the scope of implementation required for nursing activity domains in CP management within primary care. Three key findings emerged: (1) a major gap between prioritized and currently implemented activities, identifying nursing domains that require targeted strategies; (2) specific strategies addressing nurses’ knowledge and skills, organizational factors, and facilitators of the implementation process; and (3) two strategic axes to guide overall implementation, providing a structured implementation blueprint to align nursing practice with evidence-based priorities in CP management.

Finding 1: gap between prioritized and implemented activities

A key finding from the integrated analysis is the substantial gap between the 41 nursing activities identified in the Delphi study and their actual implementation in CP management within primary care. Most nurses reported limited experience or understanding of how to apply these activities, with minimal implementation across the four domains of care. Only a few nurses had initiated CP management activities, primarily in the assessment domain, and the majority of prioritized activities remain largely absent from routine practice.

When interpreted through the lens of DOI theory,53 the majority of primary care nurses appear to be at the knowledge stage, with limited awareness of CP management and insufficient information or training to be convinced of its relevance and utility. Even when primary care nurses (few) were exposed to validated assessment tools or multimodal approaches and showed some interest in CP management (persuasion stage), uptake remained variable and inconsistent. Progression to the implementation stages is further constrained by organizational barriers, including time pressures and limited resources, as well as hierarchical constraints with physicians.

Finding 2: identification of implementation strategies

Tables 2–5 present the identification of specific considerations for implementation and corresponding strategies (Steps 3 and 4) to support the implementation of prioritized activities across the four domains.

Global assessment (Table 2)

The findings suggest that nursing activities related to CP build upon established foundations in health assessment and well-developed nursing competencies. These activities, already observed in the management of other chronic conditions (e.g., reviewing medical records, using standardized assessment tools), suggest that the nursing scope could extend to include CP assessment. Findings also suggest that nurses’ biopsychosocial approach to care, combined with their close relationships with patients, appears to support ongoing pain assessment as part of routine follow-up for individuals living with chronic conditions. The analysis further highlights contextual factors that influence the operationalization of these activities. For example, in settings where care is primarily organized around a biomedical model, assessment tended to emphasize specific diagnoses while underrepresenting psychological and social dimensions of pain. Similarly, perceptions that CP is relatively uncommon in primary care may contribute to variable prioritization of assessment across settings. Findings emerging from the integrated results suggest that gradual introduction of assessment tasks (e.g., beginning with characterization of pain), use of validated instruments (such as the Brief Pain Inventory Interference scale),56 and training in CP could facilitate assessment activities. At the same time, difficulty distinguishing acute from CP, absence of nursing-specific tools in CP, restricted organizational recognition of CP, and time constraints were consistently observed as potential constraints.

The strategies (including training on multidimensional assessment, standardized assessment tools, clarification of nursing roles in evaluation, and workflow adjustments) aim to strengthen nurses’ abilities to accurately collect and interpret physical, psychosocial, and functional data, increase their confidence in identifying patient needs, and reduce ambiguity in assessment responsibilities.

Care management (Table 3)

The results indicate that nurses’ roles in organizing and coordinating care align with established practices in chronic disease management, such as tailoring treatment plans, monitoring patient progress, and facilitating continuity of care. Results suggest that nurses’ proximity to patients and professional identity grounded in support and education may provide a basis for contributing to the coordination of complex CP-care trajectories. The analysis further highlights how contextual conditions influence the operationalization of these activities. In organizations where access to specialized services is limited or knowledge of community resources is insufficient, nurses’ ability to assume broader responsibilities may be constrained according to our study. Findings emerging from the integrated results further suggest that potential facilitators include CP-specific follow-up tools, training in multimodal treatment strategies, and structured clinical guidelines. Organizational conditions, such as mentorship programs, pilot projects, and recognition of CP as a care priority, seemed to support leadership and expanded involvement. At the same time, the findings indicate constraints that shape implementation. Professional boundaries and systemic factors, such as uncertainty regarding nursing autonomy, reliance on physician-led decision-making, and limited mechanisms for innovation, were reported to influence feasibility. Additionally, high patient expectations emerged as a factor that may increase pressure on nurses and affect the extent to which expanded roles are operationalized.

The strategies (including training on multimodal pain management, structured care guides, delegation of nursing responsibilities, and mentorship networks) enhance nurses’ capacity to organize and prioritize interventions, foster confidence in managing complex patient trajectories, and clarify their coordination role within the healthcare team.

Health Promotion (Table 4)

The results indicate that nurses occupy a central role in patient education and support, particularly in fostering self-management strategies and encouraging healthy lifestyle behaviors. Results indicate that ongoing engagement with patients could support sustained motivation and autonomy in daily CP management, for example through discussions of pain triggers and guidance on treatment options. The analysis further highlights contextual factors influencing the operationalization of these activities. The availability of external supports, such as mental health services, was observed to be variable and sometimes limited across public and private settings, which may affect the extent to which health promotion activities are implemented. Findings emerging from the integrated results also suggest that targeted CP-specific training and enhanced awareness of local resources appear to facilitate nurses’ ability to personalize lifestyle advice and direct patients toward contextually appropriate solutions. At the same time, several constraints were evident. Patients’ expectations that prioritize biomedical or pharmacological solutions were frequently noted, potentially limiting alignment with health promotion strategies. Additionally, some nurses reported discomfort in addressing complex psychosocial dimensions of pain, particularly when time, training, or interdisciplinary support were insufficient.

The strategies (including training on lifestyle modification and self-management, and patient education materials) aim to increase nurses’ knowledge and skills in guiding behavior change, foster positive attitudes toward patient empowerment, and strengthen confidence in supporting patient self-management.

Interprofessional collaboration (Table 5)

The results indicate that nurses’ core values of empathy and attentive listening provide a foundation for establishing therapeutic alliances and building trust with patients, which appears to create opportunities for nurses to assume leadership roles within care teams, support patient involvement in decision-making, and influence care planning and coordination. The integration also highlights contextual factors shaping collaborative practices. Clarity of roles within interprofessional teams was observed to enhance mutual understanding and reduce potential tensions, facilitating more cohesive teamwork. Findings emerging from the integrated results also indicate that mechanisms such as shared clinical tools, structured collaboration processes (e.g., regular case discussions or interdisciplinary meetings), ongoing pilot projects and innovative approaches to pain management, appear to support nursing leadership, and effective interprofessional collaboration could facilitate nursing leadership and more effective interprofessional collaboration. At the same time, several constraints were perceived. Persistent perceptions that certain aspects of CP management are solely the responsibility of other healthcare providers may limit nursing involvement. Fragmented information systems were observed to impede coordination and communication, and organizational challenges (including restricted access to clinical decision-making and limited institutional support) were consistently associated with difficulties in fully integrating nursing activities into interprofessional collaboration.

The strategies (including role clarification, nurse champions, interprofessional education, collaborative digital tools, structured team meetings, and support for nursing-led pilot projects) aim to foster positive attitudes toward teamwork and shared decision-making, increase confidence in contributing to interprofessional care pathways, and promote sustainable integration of collaborative practices and leadership engagement within the healthcare system.

Finding 3: draft of implementation blueprint

The overarching synthesis of the integrated results provides new insights about the importance of a gradual, phased approach to support the evolving role of primary care nurses in CP management. The notable gap between current practices and the full range of desired CP-related nursing activities (Finding 1) suggests that expecting simultaneous implementation of all activities across all domains would be neither realistic nor sustainable (Finding 2).

Our integrated results suggest that successful implementation of broader nursing responsibilities requires two complementary strategic axes, as certain foundational elements are necessary to enable progressive adoption (temporal lens according to DOI theory).53 The first axis targets individual and team-level adoption, focusing on developing awareness, building clinical skills, and shaping attitudes to progress through the DOI stages of Knowledge, Persuasion, and Decision. The second axis emphasizes organizational and system-level adoption, enhancing organizational buy-in and creating a supportive structural environment that facilitates the DOI stages of Persuasion, Decision, and Confirmation, thereby reinforcing formal commitment and the sustainable integration of nursing practices.

Discussion

This study explored implementation strategies to promote the integration of nursing activities, taking into account nursing practice realities and patient needs, using a mixed-methods integration guided by established frameworks, thus going beyond what the quantitative and qualitative phases could reveal on their own. The quantitative phase employed a Delphi method to establish a prioritized list of nursing activities deemed essential by both nurses and patients living with CP. The qualitative phase, based on a descriptive interpretive approach, then explored the barriers and facilitators influencing the implementation of these activities from the nurses’ perspective. Ultimately, integrating these findings enabled the development of realistic, context-sensitive strategies to support the integration of nursing activities into primary care CP management. By aligning these strategies along two key axes (Capacity-building of nurses and Mobilizing patients, healthcare providers, and managers), this study offers a clear, actionable roadmap to guide practice transformation and enhance the contribution of nursing in the care of patients living with CP. The integrated analysis brings to light three essential considerations for effectively supporting the integration of primary care nurses for CP management: 1) the limited awareness of their role, 2) the importance of professional autonomy and institutional support as drivers of integration, and 3) the variability in how their role is enacted across clinical domains.

Limited awareness of the nursing role in CP management

Although the Delphi study indicated that nurses could engage in multiple CP management activities (n = 41),28 many nurses reported that they had never addressed CP explicitly as a clinical issue in our qualitative study. This finding highlights a persistently limited awareness of the nursing role in CP management, consistent with earlier observations in the Canadian context by Bergeron et al.20 and Lukewich et al.21 but suggesting that little progress has been made over the past decade. Our integrated analysis also suggests that significant role ambiguity among primary care nurses may contribute to challenges in CP management. Although chronic disease management, including CP, clearly falls within the nursing scope of practice,36 many participants reported uncertainty about what is expected of them in this area. This misalignment between expected (defined by policies and guidelines), enacted (actual practices), and perceived roles (nurses’ own understanding of their responsibilities)57 appears to undermine nurses’ confidence, motivation, and self-efficacy, limiting their engagement in CP care in our study.

Overall, our findings highlight the importance of first building foundational knowledge of CP through targeted training, which serves as a prerequisite for the adoption of nursing activities. However, evidence indicates that training alone is insufficient to achieve meaningful and sustained changes in the nursing role within primary care settings.58 Our integrated analysis further supports this, showing that role ambiguity and the complexity of nursing responsibilities in primary care require a combination of complementary implementation strategies addressing multiple dimensions of practice.

Systematic reviews suggest that the most effective strategies at both individual and team levels involve multiple approaches applied simultaneously, with training being most effective when it combines several components (e.g., educational sessions, instructional materials, and mentoring).59,60 Moreover, employing multiple training strategies appears to positively influence several dimensions of innovation adoption, including attitudes, intentions, knowledge, perceived behavioral control, perceived social norms, and skills.60 At the same time, evidence shows that studies are highly heterogeneous, making it difficult to identify an optimal combination of strategies.59 Accordingly, implementation science recommends using theory- or framework-informed approaches to guide the selection of complementary strategies across multiple levels of practice, enhancing the likelihood of successful and sustained implementation,54 as applied in our study.

Professional autonomy and institutional support as levers for integration

Our findings indicate that nurses’ professional autonomy in Family Medicine Groups is constrained not by formal scope of practice limitations, but by systemic and organizational factors that limit the integration of CP-related activities into routine care. Professional autonomy, defined as the ability to make independent clinical decisions and adapt care to patient needs, is a cornerstone of high-quality nursing practice.61,62 Whereas literature reviews often report scope and autonomy restrictions as the main barrier to primary care nurse role implementation,58 our study highlights a different dynamic: even when CP-related activities clearly fall within nurses’ scope,63 many perceived their role as task- or diagnosis-oriented, and their enactment often depends on physician guidance, reflecting entrenched hierarchies and a prevailing medical orientation in these interprofessional teams. Although Family Medicine Groups provide an interprofessional environment conducive to task-sharing and tailored patient follow-up,39 variations in team composition and organizational norms appear to shape how autonomy is exercised and how primary care nursing innovations are integrated into practice.64 These contextual factors are important to consider when interpreting our findings and their generalizability to other primary care models in Canada or internationally,7 given the diversity of primary care settings and team structures.

Our study also highlights that supporting nurses in fulfilling their role in CP care requires more than their individual initiative; it depends on organizational commitment and a supportive work environment. The literature indicates that successful implementation relies on strong organizational leadership, clear support from primary care managers, dedicated time for training and integration, and effective team-wide communication.65 These conditions were also reflected in our study as essential to fostering innovation and enabling nurses to expand their scope of practice in CP management.

At the organizational level, our study identified strategies aligned with the literature,66–68 including promoting a culture of continuous learning and actively involving nurses in care planning and decision-making. At a broader system level, strategies such as redesigning care pathways, collaborating with specialized pain clinics, and formalizing interprofessional collaboration protocols between public and private services appear critical to embedding CP nursing management into primary care and the wider healthcare system.

Taken together, these organizational and system-level strategies aim to address barriers that are particularly important for the implementation of the nurse role in primary care, namely an unfavorable implementation climate and professional isolation.58 These barriers largely arise from the decentralized nature of primary care teams, which necessitates intentional organizational structures and strategies to support nurses and interprofessional collaboration in implementing evidence-based practices.69

Implications for clinical practice and education

Our findings highlight several implications for nursing practice and education in the context of CP management. First, they can help primary care nurses and healthcare teams identify areas for improvement and opportunities for integration into routine practice. For instance, the study suggests that nurses could take a more autonomous role in assessing pain or educating patients about the physiology of CP. Strengthening nurses’ confidence and competence in these activities may serve as an effective entry point for broader involvement in CP care. Our findings also identify professional, organizational, and systemic barriers and suggest strategies that can be adapted and applied depending on the specific context of each primary care practice. This involves addressing rigid hierarchical structures when present and role boundaries, while promoting organizational cultures that value interprofessional collaboration and recognize nurses as clinical leaders. Ultimately, this study contributes to a clearer recognition of nurses as key players in CP management.

In terms of education, our findings underscore the need for targeted capacity-building initiatives. They provide a foundation for updating existing nursing curricula and continuing education programs that integrate the knowledge, skills, and attitudes required for effective CP management. For example, nurses should be trained to differentiate between acute and CP, use validated pain assessment tools (e.g., the Brief Pain Inventory Interference scale),56 and develop personalized care plans using SMART goals (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound).70 They should also be equipped to implement evidence-based interventions and engage in team-based care, for example by co-designing pain management strategies with physiotherapists or psychologists. Our findings further emphasize the importance of communication skills, such as empathetic listening and non-judgmental care, as essential for reducing the stigma often associated with CP. In addition, our findings highlight the need to strengthen training programs that support the integration of psychosocial dimensions into CP management, as nurses are currently not adequately equipped to address both the physical and psychosocial aspects of patient care.71–73 In our opinion, these competencies should be addressed both in a transversal manner across the curriculum and through dedicated mandatory courses. For nurses already in practice, accessible and flexible continuing education formats (e.g., online video modules, interactive quizzes, and in-person workshops) can help overcome time and resource constraints.74

Strengths and limitations

This study, based on a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design,30 demonstrated the value of combining quantitative and qualitative methods to examine key challenges related to professional role transformation in primary care nursing. A strength lies in the use of an innovative co-creation approach, grounded in the KTA framework,75 which actively involved nurses and patients living with CP. This participatory, bottom-up approach enhanced the relevance of the findings and empowered patients and nurses as key interest-holders in the change process. The study protocol also adhered to the Mixed-methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT),76 ensuring methodological rigor in each phase.

This study focused specifically on the perspectives of primary care nurses, as both the quantitative and qualitative phases included only nursing participants as providers. While insights from other primary care providers (e.g., physicians, administrators) could offer additional understanding of team dynamics and organizational factors affecting implementation, their inclusion was beyond the scope of the present study due to time and resource constraints. Future research should expand to such interest-holders.

Structuring the findings using the four nursing practice domains of the Poitras et al.45 framework (global assessment, care management, health promotion, interprofessional collaboration) facilitated the development of targeted implementation strategies considering the volume and complexity of data. However, this framework may oversimplify the inherently fluid and overlapping nature of nursing roles in clinical practice, where multiple responsibilities are often performed simultaneously. While the framework has not yet been widely adopted across nursing research, to our knowledge, it has informed important conceptual work on nursing scopes of practice.77

Finally, the study was conducted within the context of Quebec’s primary care system, specifically interprofessional Family Medicine Groups. These teams operate under funding and organizational structures (e.g., team composition, funding models, remuneration schemes, patient attachment rates, provider-to-population ratios)78 that may differ from other provinces or countries, influencing the organization and delivery of nursing care. Accordingly, the transferability of our findings to other primary care settings should be considered in light of these contextual differences, and adaptations may be required for practical implementation in regions with different primary care structures.

Conclusion

The findings from this study contribute to the limited but growing body of knowledge on CP management in primary care by identifying concrete nursing activities for CP management. By elucidating strategies to facilitate the integration of primary care nurses and what are the barriers, this research provides practical insights for healthcare providers, primary care practices, and decision-makers aiming to improve CP care through team-based models. Ultimately, this work seeks to support the design and implementation of effective, sustainable, and patient-centered strategies for managing CP within primary care settings.
