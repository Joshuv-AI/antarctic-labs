Leveraging primary healthcare institutions for the deployment of automated external defibrillators: a geospatial optimization analysis in Deyang, China
Authors: Sheng Liu, Chunyan Liao, Yi Yang, Yong Yang, Wei Jiang, Dingxiu He, Kaisen Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo analyze the spatial and epidemiological characteristics of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) in Deyang, China (2021–2023), quantify the supply–demand coverage gap in current automated external defibrillator (AED) placement, and evaluate a geospatial optimization strategy embedding AEDs in 24/7 accessible outdoor cabinets at primary healthcare institutions (PHCIs).MethodsWe integrated data on OHCA cases, existing AEDs, PHCIs, and demographics. Temporal variations were assessed via descriptive epidemiology, while spatial heterogeneity was characterized via Global/Local Moran’s I and Getis-Ord Gi* hotspot analysis. A network analysis model integrating the Gaussian Two-Step Floating Catchment Area (Ga2SFCA) method and the Maximal Covering Location Problem (MCLP) was constructed using PHCIs as candidate nodes based on road network distance. Optimization was simulated under two scenarios: complete redistribution and incremental expansion.ResultsThe study included 3273 OHCA cases. Significant temporal variations were observed, with peak incidence during morning hours and public holidays. Spatial autocorrelation indicated intensifying OHCA clustering from 2021 to 2023 (Moran’s I: 0.38–0.49, P < 0.01). Furthermore, spatial analysis revealed a stable ‘core-periphery’ structure, with Jingyang District identified as a consistent high disease burden hotspot. A marked supply–demand coverage gap was observed: residential areas accounted for 82.22% of cases but only 6.29% of AED deployments. Existing AEDs covered only 31.87% of historical OHCAs within a 500-m road network distance. The new Optimized Model for Network Analysis demonstrated that achieving >50% coverage required 100 additional AEDs under a complete redistribution strategy, versus 200 under an incremental expansion strategy.ConclusionThe distribution of OHCA incidence exhibits distinct epidemiological patterns, significant spatial clustering, and a marked disconnect from current AED placement. A geospatial optimization strategy leveraging the extensive network of PHCIs significantly enhances coverage efficiency. This “PHCI-embedded” model provides a cost-effective framework for establishing resilient cardiac arrest response systems in resource-constrained urban settings.Keywords: Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, Automated external defibrillator, Maximal covering location problem, Primary healthcare institutions, Resource allocation, Geographic information systems

Introduction

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) constitutes a major public health challenge in China, with an estimated annual incidence of 95.7 per 100,000 population, affecting approximately 750,000 individuals annually and yielding a survival rate of only 1.2%.1 Immediate cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and early defibrillation are crucial for saving lives,2, 3 as the probability of survival decreases by 7–10% for every minute of delay.4 However, pre-hospital emergency response times in China often exceed this critical window,5, 6 making Public Access Defibrillation (PAD) programs leveraging automated external defibrillators (AEDs) a core initiative to improve OHCA survival outcomes.

Despite national efforts, AED distribution in China suffers from insufficient density, inequitable allocation, and a geographic coverage gap between device locations and OHCA incidence.7, 8, 9 Low public awareness and reluctance to use AEDs further limit their utility.10 Existing strategies for AED placement focus on high-accessibility service outlets (e.g., convenience stores) or rely on widespread CPR training—though training saturation is slow and skill retention declines rapidly.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17

Primary Healthcare Institutions (PHCIs), including clinics and community/township health centers, are underutilized for AED deployment. Strategically located in residential communities, they offer geographic proximity to high disease burden hotspots and access to BLS-trained medical staff, though limited operating hours pose a barrier.11 This study aims to analyze the temporal and spatial distribution characteristics of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) in Deyang City, construct a novel geospatial resource allocation optimization model, and formulate a pragmatic automated external defibrillator (AED) deployment strategy centered on Primary Healthcare Institutions (PHCI). This approach addresses geographical accessibility issues (“coverage gaps”) and responder capability issues (“skill gaps”).

Methods

Study Area and data sources

This study leverages Deyang City, Sichuan Province, as a strategic case study. The jurisdiction, administering 5911 km2 and 3.454 million residents across six county-level divisions, presents a gradient of development from intense urbanization (Jingyang District) to rural, agriculture-based economies (e.g., Zhongjiang County). This intrinsic spatial heterogeneity establishes Deyang as an ideal microcosm to examine the optimization of limited emergency medical resources across varied socio-geographic contexts. Furthermore, its city-wide, three-tiered pre-hospital care network—integrating municipal, county, and network hospitals—provides a practical framework for studying the synergistic efficiency of AED placement within an integrated emergency response system.

Case data were obtained from the standardized pre-hospital database of the Deyang 120 Emergency Command Center (January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2023). OHCA locations were categorized based on the incident address recorded in the emergency dispatch system; “Residential Areas” were defined as private housing estates, dormitories, and rural residential compounds, distinct from public commercial zones or industrial parks. Data collection strictly followed the Utstein-style template,18 encompassing patient demographics, timestamps, geocoded incident locations, clinical diagnoses, and outcomes. From an initial 3368 records, we excluded cases due to trauma, drowning, or obvious non-cardiac causes (n = 86) and those with incomplete geospatial data (n = 9), yielding a final cohort of 3273 cases. AED location data and PHCI locations (clinics and community/township health centers) were verified via the local Red Cross Society and Amap API (current to December 2023). Existing public AED locations (n = 159) and PHCI locations (n = 825, comprising community/township health stations (n = 187) and clinics (n = 638)) were verified via the local Red Cross Society and Amap API as of December 2023. Geospatial Data: Fine-scale population distribution data were derived from the 7th National Population Census (2020).

This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of a tertiary hospital in Sichuan Province (Project number: 2024-03-067-K01). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Spatial units and geocoding

All study locations were geocoded using the Amap API (GCJ-02 coordinate system) and corrected to the WGS-84 coordinate system for analysis. In the spatial analysis phase, Primary Healthcare Institutions (PHCIs) were designated as fixed AED deployment nodes, and coverage rates were calculated based on road network distance of 100 m, 200 m, 300 m, 400 m, and 500 m. Precise coordinate points were utilized to calculate road network distance for both coverage assessment and optimization modeling. A route planning engine was employed to measure road network distance between OHCA incident locations, existing AED deployment sites, and PHCI candidate nodes. A comprehensive geodatabase was constructed in ArcGIS, integrating demand points (OHCA incidents), supply points (existing AEDs), candidate nodes (PHCIs), population distribution, and road network data. To address the limitation of PHCI operating hours, the model assumes that all AEDs deployed at PHCI candidate nodes are installed in publicly accessible outdoor cabinets (ensuring 24/7 availability), rather than being locked within the facility.

Epidemiological analysis

Temporal and demographic Analysis: Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the demographic characteristics (e.g., gender, age, occupation) and temporal attributes (year, month, season, time of day, day type (defined as Workdays, Weekends, or Statutory Holidays)) of OHCA cases, with calculations of proportions and incidence rates. Intergroup differences were assessed using the chi-square test (χ2 test), with a significance level set at α = 0.05. Temporal dynamics were evaluated using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 4.9.0.0) to quantify the trend in OHCA incidence over time by calculating the Annual Percent Change (APC). The 95% confidence interval of estimated APC was used to determine the statistical significance of the trend. Incidence Rate Ratios (IRR) were computed to quantify risk differences between Statutory holidays/weekends and workdays.

Spatial analysis: To determine whether OHCA incidents occurred randomly or exhibited systemic clustering, we first defined the administrative divisions of districts, counties and townships as the corresponding spatial units for statistical analysis. The Global Moran’s I was used to quantify the overall spatial autocorrelation across the entire city (index ranges from −1 to +1), where positive values indicate positive spatial autocorrelation (clustered distribution), negative values indicate negative spatial autocorrelation (dispersed distribution), and a value of 0 suggests a random spatial pattern. Significance (P < 0.05) was assessed via a Z-test). Subsequently, to pinpoint specific locations with high historical disease burden, we utilized Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) and the Getis-Ord Gi* statistic. Getis-Ord Gi* Hotspot Analysis was applied to identify statistically significant clusters of high values (hotspots) and low values (cold spots). The Gi* statistic was calculated for each spatial unit for significance testing. Areas with a confidence level ≥90% (P < 0.10) for high-value clustering were defined as high disease burden hotspots.

LISA stratified local spatial patterns into four distinct categories: (1) High-High clusters (hotspots), representing areas with high OHCA incidence surrounded by similarly high disease burden neighbors; (2) Low-Low clusters (cold spots), indicating consistent low-incidence zones; and spatial outliers including (3) High-Low (an isolated high disease burden location surrounded by low disease burden areas) and (4) Low-High (a low disease burden enclave within a high disease burden zone). Statistically significant (P < 0.05) High-High clusters were identified as core disease burden clusters for OHCA.

Geospatial assessment of current AED deployment

We evaluated the adequacy of the existing AED infrastructure through two dimensions:

Supply-Demand coverage gap: Chi-square tests compared the proportional distribution of OHCA incidents versus AED deployments across three location categories: residential areas, medical facilities, and public spaces.

Road network coverage: We calculated the proportion of historical out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) cases covered by at least one automated external defibrillator (AED) within specific road network distance. Alternative road network service distances of 400 m and 500 m were designated as primary benchmarks. Assuming a brisk walking speed of approximately 100 m per minute, these distances correspond to a 4-min and 5-min one-way trip, respectively, aligning with the critical therapeutic window for early defibrillation.4, 19, 20

Optimization modeling

We developed a three-stage geospatial optimization framework integrating the Gaussian Two-Step Floating Catchment Area (Ga2SFCA) method and the Maximal Covering Location Problem (MCLP).21, 22, 23

Stage 1 – Diagnostic assessment (Ga2SFCA): Recognizing that AEDs are non-congestible resources unlike clinical services, we adapted the Ga2SFCA model to quantify spatial accessibility intensity rather than usage competition. By utilizing a Gaussian kernel function, this method captures the critical time-sensitivity of emergency rescue, where service utility decays rapidly with distance. This step calculates a supply-to-demand ratio for each grid cell, identifying “accessibility depressions” where high OHCA density is met with insufficient distance-weighted coverage, thereby serving as a diagnostic baseline for subsequent optimization.

Stage 2 – Location optimization (MCLP): Based on the identified gaps, the MCLP model selected optimal locations from the candidate PHCI nodes to maximize the total number of OHCA demand points covered within a defined service road network distance (R) given a fixed number of devices (N).

Stage 3 – Integrating S2FCA (Ga2SFCA) and Maximal Covering Location Problem (MCLP) methods, we built a network analysis model using PHCIs as candidate nodes based on road network distance, and simulated two policy scenarios: (i) Complete Redistribution (optimizing all existing AED locations) and (ii) Incremental Expansion (adding new AEDs to fixed existing locations).

Results

Demographic characteristics

The majority of patients were male (61.01%, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 1). Individuals aged >80 years constituted the most numerous group (35.90%).

Temporal characteristics

No significant trend was observed in annual OHCA incidence (APC = −1.34%, P = 0.72) (Supplementary Table 2). However, significant monthly variation occurred (χ2 = 360.03, P < 0.001), peaking in January (percentage trends = 34.37%, P < 0.05) and reaching a nadir in September (percentage trends = −14.97%, P < 0.05). The morning period (06:00–11:59) represented the peak time for OHCA occurrence (35.62%). The proportion of OHCAs occurring on public holidays (8.40%) significantly exceeded the proportion of risk exposure time (3.47%), representing a 2.80-fold increased risk compared to workdays (Incidence Rate Ratio [IRR] = 2.80, 95% CI 2.48–3.17, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 3). Significant declining incidence trends were observed in the 21–30 (APC = −34.50%, P < 0.05) and 41–50 (APC = −6.59%, P < 0.05) age cohorts(Supplementary Table 1).

Spatial characteristics

Global Moran’s I was positive and significant across all years (2021: 0.3821; 2022: 0.4451; 2023: 0.4946; all P < 0.01), indicating strengthening spatial clustering (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 4). Local analysis identified Jingyang District as a stable high disease burden hotspot and Zhongjiang County as a low disease burden cold spot, forming a consistent “core-periphery” structure (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 5). Further hotspot analysis confirmed these patterns persisted throughout the study period (Fig. 3). Residences were the primary location of OHCA occurrence (82.22%) (Supplementary Table 5).

Current AED distribution and coverage

By the end of 2023, 159 public AEDs were deployed (4.6 per 100,000 population). A severe geospatial coverage gap was evident: residential areas accounted for 82.22% of OHCAs but only 6.29% of AEDs. It is important to note that while 52.83% of AEDs were located in “Medical Facilities” (Supplementary Table 5), these were predominantly situated in large tertiary hospitals, not the community/township-based PHCIs proposed in our optimization model. Consequently, the vast majority of residential communities served by PHCIs currently lack immediate AED access. Network-based analysis revealed that within a 500-m road network distance, only 31.87% of historical OHCAs were covered by at least one public AED (Table 1).

Optimization modeling results

To evaluate the pure benefit of spatial optimization, we first simulated the redistribution of the existing 159 AEDs without adding new devices. By relocating these 159 units to optimal PHCI nodes under the Complete Redistribution strategy, the OHCA coverage rate within a 500-m road network distance increased from the baseline of 31.87–45.62% (Table 2). This substantial efficiency gain demonstrates that strategically targeting locations with a high spatial density of OHCA events increases coverage, even without expanding total resources.

We then calculated the specific resource investments required to exceed a 50% coverage target within the same 500-m distance. The outcomes differed significantly between the two approaches. Under the Complete Redistribution strategy, achieving the >50% target required exactly 100 additional AEDs, bringing the total to 259 devices. Conversely, under the Incremental Expansion strategy—which retains all existing sub-optimal AED locations and restricts new deployments to PHCIs—achieving the same target required 200 additional AEDs (Total N = 359). This highlights that retaining inefficient baseline locations necessitates double the new resource investment to reach equivalent coverage goals.

Finally, we evaluated the model’s performance using an alternative, stricter service distance of 400 m. While baseline coverage rates naturally decreased under this shorter travel distance, the optimization models remained robust. Simulations confirmed that adding 250 new devices allowed both the Complete Redistribution and Incremental Expansion strategies to successfully recover and exceed the 50% coverage benchmark (Table 2).

Discussion

This study analyzed the epidemiological characteristics of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest in Deyang City, revealing a significant gap between the distribution of emergency resources and actual geographical demand. Through an integrated model combining the Gaussian Two-Step Floating Catchment Area Method (Ga2SFCA) and the Maximum Coverage Location Problem (MCLP), this study confirms that the current network of automated external defibrillators (AEDs), prioritizing deployment at high-traffic locations such as public spaces and tertiary medical institutions, leaves residential areas, which bear a high absolute burden of OHCA cases, inadequately protected, highlighting an urgent need for feasible strategies to guide allocation. The optimized model constructed demonstrates that, within resource-constrained urban environments, leveraging existing primary healthcare institutions as deployment nodes represents a cost-effective solution for enhancing device coverage efficiency.

The study reveals distinct temporal and spatial distribution patterns in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest incidents, offering direct guidance for emergency resource allocation. Temporally, risk increases during public holidays and morning rush hours, aligning with human circadian rhythms and population behavior patterns.24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29 This underscores the need for sustained emergency response capacity during non-standard working hours. Spatially, out-of-hospital cardiac arrests exhibit stable “core-periphery” clustering patterns, with Jingyang District emerging as a persistent high-incidence hotspot. Crucially, our three-year longitudinal analysis confirms that this hotspot structure is not transient but highly stable over time. This temporal and spatial stability is fundamental, as it validates the use of historical OHCA data as a reliable predictor for future fixed-location resource planning. This challenges the efficacy of traditional resource allocation models based on uniform distribution across administrative regions.30, 31, 32 Unlike commercial areas with fluctuating population density, these residential hotspots represent fixed demand sources for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest care, where current emergency resources provide significantly inadequate coverage. Similar to other international research,33, 34 this study’s model confirms that risk-oriented AED deployment significantly enhances coverage compared to existing patterns of AED allocation. A core finding is the substantial coverage gap between AED deployment sites and out-of-hospital cardiac arrest locations. Historical analysis reveals that over 82.22% of out-of-hospital cardiac arrests occur in residential areas, yet only 6.29% of AEDs are deployed in these zones, with the majority of existing AEDs concentrated in tertiary hospitals. While placing devices in high-traffic public venues like tertiary hospitals enhances visibility and holds some rationale, this study demonstrates that such a strategy fails to provide effective coverage for the vast majority of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest cases within communities. In historical out-of-hospital cardiac arrest cases, only 31.87% of incidents were covered within the 500-m road network distance of existing devices. By redistributing the existing 159 AEDs to optimal PHCI nodes, coverage significantly improved to 45.62% (Supplementary Table 7). Based on this spatial metric, the current deployment logic appears to prioritize high-traffic public venues over geographic proximity to absolute residential risk points. However, this interpretation warrants caution. While optimized PHCI locations increase spatial proximity to historical OHCAs,35 spatial coverage does not strictly equate to actual device usage. Existing AEDs situated in highly visible public locations (e.g., shopping malls, transit stations) may inherently benefit from a higher likelihood of bystander intervention compared to residential areas. Furthermore, proximity models do not exclude cases with obvious signs of death upon discovery, for whom AED placement would not alter clinical outcomes.

The optimisation model proposed in this study addresses this gap by deploying AEDs anchored to primary healthcare facilities. Primary healthcare facilities are inherently embedded within residential communities, offering geographical proximity advantages lacking in standalone commercial service points.30, 31, 36, 37 Furthermore, deploying devices through primary healthcare institutions can effectively address the widespread “skills gap” in areas with low bystander CPR rates. Additionally, associating AEDs with medical institutions enhances device credibility and public psychological safety, potentially alleviating operational concerns.38, 39 Equipping primary healthcare facilities with AEDs establishes a dual emergency response mechanism: during operational hours, medical staff trained in basic life support can be integrated into the emergency medical dispatch system.40, 41 The presence of professionally trained responders significantly increases the success rate of effective defibrillation compared to untrained bystanders.42, 43 This enables response to out-of-hospital cardiac arrest incidents in the vicinity, ensuring professional emergency capabilities. Furthermore, the temporal analysis findings served as the decisive factor for the system’s operational design. The identification of significant risk peaks during a portion of non-working hours (mornings and holidays) dictates that a standard indoor deployment would fail to cover critical incidence windows. Consequently, the installation of smart outdoor cabinets on the exterior walls of facilities is not merely a technical detail but a prerequisite mandated by the temporal epidemiology of OHCA.

From a planning perspective, this study confirms that optimizing device deployment locations yields greater resource efficiency than merely increasing device numbers. Simulation analysis indicates that, compared to incremental expansion, completely redeploying devices to primary healthcare facility nodes in high disease burden hotspots requires only a small number of additional devices to significantly enhance coverage. Specifically, achieving the >50% coverage target requires activating approximately 259 PHCI nodes, which represents only 31.4% of the total 825 available PHCIs in the city. This suggests that a highly effective network can be established without requiring universal installation across all facilities. This offers crucial insights for urban planners in middle-income regions: future public defibrillator deployment should shift from quantity-driven models to risk-priority geospatial allocation. Implementation should adopt a phased strategy, beginning with installing outdoor defibrillator cabinets at primary healthcare facilities within identified high-incidence clusters, followed by systematic training of primary healthcare personnel to serve as community/township first responders. Future studies incorporating real-time population heatmaps could further refine these models by transitioning from historical burden-based optimization to predictive spatial risk modeling.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, regarding the spatial optimization methodology, our model utilized the entire retrospective cohort of OHCA cases to maximize the size of the training dataset for location selection. Consequently, we forewent the opportunity to split the data into separate ‘training’ and ‘test’ sets for validation. Because the level of spatial coverage was assessed on the same dataset used to train the optimization algorithm, the model is susceptible to overfitting. Therefore, the observed optimized coverage of 45.62% likely overestimates the true coverage that can be expected for future OHCA incidents. While future OHCA events will likely follow a similar macroscopic spatial pattern, they will not occur in the exact historical locations our model sought to cover. Clinical benefits and the true predictive validity of this PHCI-embedded deployment strategy require rigorous validation through future prospective studies. Second, the research model assumes 100% operational readiness of AEDs within smart outdoor cabinets—an idealized usage scenario—without accounting for mechanical failures or vandalism. Furthermore, the model assumes immediate access; however, in practice, AEDs that are locked or require access codes can significantly delay defibrillation, highlighting the need for standardized, code-free ‘Smart Cabinets’. Third, the study treats all primary healthcare institutions as homogeneous deployment nodes, whereas actual variations exist in staffing levels and emergency response capabilities across different facilities. Fourthly, the analysis utilized static population data without accounting for daytime population mobility patterns; however, given that out-of-hospital cardiac arrests predominantly occur in residential areas, the impact of this factor on the findings is relatively limited.

Conclusion

The distribution of OHCA risk in Deyang City exhibits distinct epidemiological clustering and significant gaps in AED resource coverage. This study confirms that a geospatial optimization strategy deploying nodes based on the extensive Primary Healthcare Institutions substantially enhances AED coverage efficiency. This “PHCI-embedded”configuration model provides a viable framework for establishing resilient, cost-effective cardiac arrest emergency response systems in resource-constrained regions.
