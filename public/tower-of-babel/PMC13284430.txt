Rapidly progressive angiomatous epulis: How to differentiate a benign gingival lesion from malignancy?
Authors: Saad Bouchlarhem, Ayman Elmahjoubi, Mohammed Amine Rabhi, Achraf Amine Sbai, Drissia Benfadil, Azeddine Lachkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Angiomatous epulis, also known as vascular epulis or gingival pyogenic granuloma, is a benign reactive gingival lesion characterized by marked capillary proliferation. It typically develops in response to chronic local irritation, trauma, inflammatory stimuli, or hormonal influences. Clinically, it presents as a rapidly growing erythematous to violaceous gingival mass with a marked tendency to bleed, which may mimic malignant gingival tumors and raise diagnostic concern. Although the diagnosis is primarily based on clinicopathological correlation, imaging plays an important complementary role. Radiological evaluation, particularly computed tomography (CT), is useful to assess lesion extent, confirm its superficial localization, and exclude aggressive features such as bone erosion or deep tissue invasion. Imaging also contributes to the differential diagnosis by helping distinguish angiomatous epulis from other gingival lesions, including peripheral giant cell granuloma, peripheral ossifying fibroma, vascular malformations, and malignant tumors. We report the case of a 15-year-old patient presenting with a rapidly progressive gingival mass evolving over two months. Periapical radiography demonstrated preserved alveolar bone architecture, while CT imaging confirmed a well-circumscribed soft-tissue lesion confined to the gingiva, without cortical bone destruction, periodontal ligament alteration, or extension to adjacent structures. Given the rapid growth and bleeding tendency, imaging was essential to exclude malignancy and guide management. Complete surgical excision was performed, and histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of angiomatous epulis. This case highlights the added value of imaging in the diagnostic workup of vascular gingival lesions, particularly in differentiating benign entities from malignant conditions and in supporting appropriate therapeutic decision-making.

Introduction

Angiomatous epulis, also known as vascular epulis or gingival pyogenic granuloma, is a benign reactive hyperplastic lesion arising from the gingival tissues. Rather than representing a true neoplasm, it reflects an exaggerated tissue response to chronic local irritation, trauma, inflammatory stimuli, or hormonal influences. This lesion is commonly encountered in dental and maxillofacial practice and predominantly affects children, adolescents, and young adults, with a female predilection, particularly during periods of hormonal fluctuation such as puberty and pregnancy [1].

Clinically, angiomatous epulis typically presents as a rapidly growing, erythematous to violaceous gingival mass, which may be sessile or pedunculated and is often prone to spontaneous bleeding or ulceration. Although usually painless, its vascular appearance and sometimes rapid enlargement may mimic more aggressive or malignant gingival lesions, leading to diagnostic uncertainty. In this context, imaging plays a valuable complementary role by assessing lesion extent, confirming its superficial localization, and excluding underlying bone involvement or invasive features.

While imaging findings are often subtle or non-specific, radiological evaluation—particularly computed tomography (CT)—can provide important information regarding cortical bone integrity, periodontal structures, and possible deep extension. Moreover, imaging contributes to the differential diagnosis by helping distinguish angiomatous epulis from other gingival lesions such as peripheral giant cell granuloma, peripheral ossifying fibroma, vascular malformations, and malignant tumors, which may demonstrate bone destruction or infiltrative characteristics [2].

Management of angiomatous epulis remains primarily surgical and is based on complete excision combined with elimination of local etiological factors. Despite its benign nature, recurrence may occur, particularly in cases of incomplete removal or persistent irritation. Therefore, accurate diagnosis, supported by clinicopathological correlation and appropriate imaging assessment, is essential to guide treatment and ensure optimal outcomes [3].

Case presentation

We report the case of a 15-year-old female patient with no history of tobacco or alcohol use and no relevant personal or family medical history. She presented with a two-month history of a gingival mass that had developed insidiously and progressively increased in size (Fig. 1). The main complaint was recurrent bleeding, occurring spontaneously and exacerbated by minimal mechanical stimulation such as toothbrushing, mastication, or gentle contact. The patient did not report pain, purulent discharge, or preceding local trauma. On intraoral examination, an exophytic lesion was identified on the anterior maxillary gingiva, measuring approximately 3 cm at its greatest diameter, extending across the gingival margin and interdental papillae in relation to the central and lateral incisors. The mass appeared erythematous to violaceous, with a smooth to slightly lobulated surface and focal superficial friability. It bled readily on gentle probing, consistent with a highly vascular lesion. The base was predominantly sessile, and the lesion was well delineated from the adjacent mucosa. Palpation revealed a soft, compressible consistency without induration, and the lesion was non-tender. No clinical signs of deep tissue invasion were observed. The surrounding gingiva showed inflammatory changes consistent with poor oral hygiene, including gingival erythema and plaque accumulation, suggesting persistent local irritative factors. No cervical lymphadenopathy was detected, and the remainder of the ENT examination was unremarkable.

Routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood count and inflammatory markers, were within normal limits. Coagulation studies (prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, and platelet count) revealed no abnormalities, thereby excluding an underlying hematologic or hemostatic disorder.

Given the rapid growth and marked bleeding tendency of the lesion, imaging evaluation was performed to assess lesion extent and exclude underlying bone involvement or aggressive pathology. Discussion

Angiomatous epulis, also referred to as vascular epulis or gingival pyogenic granuloma, is a benign reactive lesion arising from the gingival tissues. The term epulis is descriptive and topographic, encompassing a heterogeneous group of localized gingival overgrowths rather than a single histopathological entity. Angiomatous epulis represents the vascular variant of these reactive lesions and is characterized by prominent capillary proliferation within an inflammatory stroma. Despite its benign nature, its rapid growth and marked bleeding tendency often raise clinical concern and necessitate thorough evaluation to exclude more aggressive or malignant conditions [4].

Angiomatous epulis can occur at any age but is most frequently observed in children, adolescents, and young adults, with a female predominance likely related to hormonal influences. The gingiva is the most commonly affected site, particularly the anterior maxillary region, as observed in our case [5].

The pathogenesis of angiomatous epulis is multifactorial and primarily reactive rather than neoplastic. Chronic local irritation from dental plaque, calculus, defective restorations, orthodontic appliances, or trauma plays a central role in triggering an exaggerated inflammatory response. This process promotes angiogenesis and proliferation of granulation tissue, further enhanced by hormonal factors such as estrogen and progesterone [6].

From a diagnostic perspective, angiomatous epulis is primarily identified through clinical and histopathological evaluation. However, imaging plays an important complementary role, particularly in cases with atypical presentation, rapid growth, or significant bleeding, where malignancy or deep extension must be excluded. Radiological assessment allows evaluation of lesion extent, underlying bone integrity, and adjacent structures, thereby contributing to diagnostic confidence.

Conventional radiography typically demonstrates preserved alveolar bone architecture. In some cases, mild pressure-induced bone remodeling or superficial cortical scalloping may be observed, but without aggressive features. Cross-sectional imaging, particularly computed tomography (CT), provides a more precise assessment of cortical bone integrity and soft-tissue extent. In the present case, CT confirmed a well-circumscribed soft-tissue lesion confined to the gingiva, without cortical bone destruction, periodontal ligament widening, or osseous invasion, supporting a benign superficial process.

Doppler ultrasound may represent a valuable first-line imaging modality for superficial gingival lesions. It allows non-invasive evaluation of vascularity and lesion depth, facilitating differentiation between hypervascular lesions such as angiomatous epulis and less vascular or fibrous entities. In addition, it may help guide further imaging or therapeutic planning, particularly in resource-limited settings.

From an imaging standpoint, the differential diagnosis of angiomatous epulis includes several benign and malignant conditions. Peripheral giant cell granuloma may demonstrate superficial bone resorption or “cupping” of the alveolar crest. Peripheral ossifying fibroma may exhibit focal calcifications or mineralized components. Hemangiomas and vascular malformations typically show marked vascularity and may be associated with phleboliths or characteristic flow patterns. In contrast, malignant gingival lesions, including squamous cell carcinoma or sarcoma, usually demonstrate aggressive imaging features such as ill-defined margins, cortical bone destruction, and infiltration of adjacent structures. In the present case, the absence of bone erosion, lack of infiltrative features, and well-defined superficial localization strongly argued against malignancy.

Histopathological examination remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis. It typically demonstrates lobular proliferation of capillary-sized vessels within an edematous fibrovascular stroma associated with inflammatory infiltrates. These findings, correlated with clinical and radiological features, allow for a confident diagnosis [7].

Management of angiomatous epulis is based on complete surgical excision combined with elimination of local etiological factors. Conventional scalpel excision remains the standard approach, although alternative techniques such as laser excision, electrosurgery, and sclerotherapy may be considered in selected cases due to their hemostatic advantages [8,9].

The prognosis is generally excellent following appropriate treatment. However, recurrence may occur, particularly in cases of incomplete excision or persistence of local irritants. Therefore, long-term follow-up is essential to ensure sustained favorable outcomes. Importantly, malignant transformation has not been reported [10].

Conclusion

Angiomatous epulis is a benign reactive vascular lesion of the gingiva that, despite its non-neoplastic nature, may exhibit rapid growth and a marked tendency to bleed, thereby mimicking more aggressive or malignant conditions. Accurate diagnosis relies on careful clinicopathological correlation, with histopathological examination remaining the gold standard.

In this context, imaging plays a crucial complementary role by confirming the superficial localization of the lesion, assessing underlying bone integrity, and excluding features suggestive of malignancy or deep extension. Radiological evaluation, particularly computed tomography, contributes significantly to the diagnostic workup and helps guide appropriate management.

Definitive treatment is based on complete surgical excision combined with elimination of local etiological factors. When appropriately managed, angiomatous epulis demonstrates an excellent prognosis with low recurrence rates and no risk of malignant transformation.

This case underscores the importance of integrating imaging into the diagnostic approach of vascular gingival lesions to improve diagnostic accuracy and optimize therapeutic decision-making.
