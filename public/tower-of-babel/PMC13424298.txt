The bacteroidal metabolite O-LysoPE facilitates hepatocyte-mediated immunosuppression in autoimmune hepatitis
Authors: Mengyi Xu, Kangkang Luo, Zhou Zhou, Junjie Li, Shiyao Zhang, Lingdong Kong, Longfeng Jiang, Yuxuan Zhang, Yu Li, Fuyu Xiao, Chuanlong Zhu, Qiang Xu, Hongwei Yi, Xudong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutoimmune hepatitis (AIH) is a chronic inflammatory liver disease typically managed with broad-spectrum immunosuppressants that carry significant systemic side effects and often provide incomplete efficacy. While gut-microbiota-derived metabolites are known to influence AIH progression, the specific microbial drivers that maintain hepatic immune homeostasis remain poorly defined. Here, we show that Bacteroides acidifaciens (BA) and its metabolite 1-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (O-LysoPE) are enriched in self-healing mouse models of hepatitis but markedly depleted in AIH patients. We demonstrate that O-LysoPE induces a ‘hepatocyte-driven active immunosuppression’ by targeting the Qa-1b (HLA-E): NKG2A immune checkpoint. Mechanistically, O-LysoPE selectively redirects the transcription factor Creb1 to the H2T23 promoter under inflammatory conditions, thereby upregulating hepatocytic Qa-1b expression. This elevation of Qa-1b engages the inhibitory receptor NKG2A on T cells, suppressing their overactivation and restoring a quiescent phenotype. Genetic disruption of H2T23 abrogates the hepatoprotective effects of O-LysoPE, confirming the central role of this metabolic-immune axis. Our findings reveal that the BA–O-LysoPE axis mobilizes the liver’s intrinsic self-rescue mechanisms to restore immune quiescence. This study establishes a robust biological framework for liver-specific, targeted immunotherapy in AIH, offering a precision alternative to current systemic immunosuppression.Subject terms: Molecular medicine, Inflammation

Introduction

Autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) is a chronic progressive autoimmune condition of the liver. It is characterised predominantly by the presence of autoantibodies, elevated levels of serum transaminase and immunoglobulin G, and histologic interface hepatitis1. Protracted AIH may lead to liver cirrhosis, liver cancer, liver transplantation, or death2. Currently, the standard treatment for AIH comprises the use of steroid immunosuppressants prednisolone or budesonide, with or without azathioprine; however, their long-term use is associated with adverse effects, which may lead to treatment interruption3. The current standard of therapy for AIH relies on corticosteroids, either alone or combined with azathioprine, which induces remission in approximately 65–80% of patients. However, up to one-third of patients exhibit incomplete or no response, and many require long-term or lifelong steroid therapy, often associated with substantial adverse effects. These limitations underscore the pressing need for alternative therapeutic strategies4–6. Although steroids constitute an essential part of induction therapy for AIH, the identification of a steroid-free disease maintenance regimen is important7. Shifting away from broad immunosuppression to specific immunoregulation by treat-to-target approaches will be the most effective and safe option for AIH.

The pathogenesis of AIH is thought to result from alterations in immune tolerance, genetic susceptibility and environmental factors, which collectively drive an overactivated CD4+ and CD8+ T cell-mediated attack on hepatocytes, causing necroinflammation and, in the chronic setting, progression to fibrosis8. The healthy liver is a solid organ with unique immunomodulatory functions involving local antigen-presenting cells (APCs) with tolerogenic capabilities9,10. Among hepatic tolerogenic APCs, hepatocyte is remarkable owing to their large size and large population in the liver11,12. Hepatocytes are considered a unique anatomical compartment, characterised by distinct mechanisms of tolerance induction focused on the restriction of effector T cell function. Under steady-state conditions, hepatocytes can induce T cell tolerance by clonal elimination or recruitment and proliferation of regulatory T (Treg) cells13. Therefore, evoking tolerogenic signals even on a small percentage of hepatocytes may be sufficient to trigger immune balance in AIH. The abundance of hepatocytes with tolerogenic capabilities facilitates interactions with overactivated T cells and allows the liver to function as a significant immunoregulatory control system for skewing hepatic, as well as extrahepatic, immune responses13. The principles governing hepatic tolerance can be studied to develop therapeutic options to alleviate autoimmunity in AIH.

AIH exhibits a relapsing–remitting disease course, in which episodes of immune-mediated liver injury may be followed by partial or spontaneous remission in some patients. This clinical feature suggests the presence of endogenous immune-regulatory and tissue-repair mechanisms during disease progression. In this context, concanavalin A (Con A)-induced hepatitis was employed, as it represents a classic T-cell-dependent model of acute hepatitis, which can well simulate the T-cell-driven immune attack stage in the pathogenesis of AIH14. This model recapitulates both the T cell-driven immune attack and the subsequent self-healing phase of hepatic inflammation. While Con A-induced hepatitis is acute and the absence of hallmark features, such as chronicity and autoantibody-associated pathology15,16. Human CYP2D6 adenovirus-based mouse model recapitulates key clinical features of AIH, including chronic immune-mediated liver injury, the breakdown of tolerance, autoantibody production and progressive fibrosis, thereby providing a more relevant platform for translational investigation17,18. Together, these two complementary models enabled us to investigate both immune injury and self-healing mechanisms in AIH.

Intestinal bacteria and their metabolites are recognised as environmental triggers that critically influence host immune responses and inflammatory states19–21. Here, we focus on the gut–liver axis to understand the connections among the gut microbiome, hepatic tolerance and autoimmunity in AIH. Con A-induced acute hepatitis model mice exhibited a remarkably strong self-healing ability within 48 h, which was significantly blocked by antibiotic mixture (ABX) treatment. Mechanistically, our data demonstrated that Bacteroides acidifaciens (BA) and its metabolite 1-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (O-LysoPE) facilitated the hepatocyte-mediated immunosuppression in AIH by targeting Pebp1 to promote the phosphorylation of Creb1. This process upregulated the nonclassical MHC molecule Qa-1b (HLA-E in humans, Qa-1b in mice)22, with BA abundance and the O-LysoPE concentration correlated with AIH in patients. Coincidentally, we discovered the dual regulatory role of O-LysoPE in mediating transcription following Creb1 phosphorylation according to distinct environmental conditions. Only in a high-inflammatory environment dose O-LysoPE selectively upregulated Creb1-induced Qa-1b transcription in hepatocytes, increasing its interaction with Nkg2a, a key inhibitory receptor on T cells, thereby contributing to immune suppression in Con A-induced acute hepatitis models. While under a high-fat environment, O-LysoPE exhibited a propensity for Creb1-controled lipid synthesis. This study focused solely on T-cell modulation and proposed a 'hepatocyte-driven active immunosuppression'. This process helps hepatocytes achieve effective 'self-healing' in a highly inflammatory environment, revealing a mechanism for liver function recovery and immune balance restoration.

Results

The gut microbiota drives the self-healing of Con A-induced acute hepatitis

Concanavalin A (Con A)-induced hepatitis is a classic T-cell-dependent model of acute liver injury, which can well simulate the T-cell-driven immune attack stage in the pathogenesis of AIH. Therefore, it is often used for mechanism research and exploration of new immune regulation therapies. Following Con A administration, the serum levels of AST and ALT markedly increased at 8 h. The serum levels of IgG, ASMA and ANA also showed an increasing trend (Supplementary Fig. 1c–g). We utilised a combination of antibiotics (ABX) to effectively deplete the native intestinal microbiota and facilitate the colonisation of donor-derived gut bacteria. After two weeks of ABX treatment, acute hepatitis was induced in the mice via tail vein injection of Con A (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Consistent with previous reports, serum AST and ALT levels, as well as hepatic Il6 and Tnfα expression, peaked between 4 and 8 h post-Con A treatment and subsequently returned to baseline by 48 h. In the ABX-treated group, spontaneous resolution of Con A-induced acute hepatitis was inhibited (Fig. 1a–d), accompanied by significantly reduced survival rates (Fig. 1e) and pronounced hepatic inflammatory cell infiltration as demonstrated by HE staining (Supplementary Fig. 1h). These findings suggest that the gut microbiota contributes to the spontaneous resolution of Con A-induced acute hepatitis models, a hypothesis further supported by observations of inhibited resolution following microbiota depletion via ABX.

Focusing on the 8-h post-Con A treatment timepoint as a critical juncture for spontaneous resolution. At this stage, mice begin transitioning from peak immune injury toward inflammation resolution (Fig. 1a–d). Consistently, our 16S sequencing data show a significant increase in beneficial bacterial taxa, particularly the Bacteroidetes phylum, at this time point (Fig. 1f). We conducted 16S rRNA sequencing of caecal content samples from wild-type (WT) and Con A-treated 8 h mice. Principal coordinate analysis revealed significant differences in microbial compositions between these groups (Supplementary Fig. 1i). Analysis of relative abundance highlighted the predominance of Bacteroidetes among the top nine key microbial operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at the phylum level in Con A-treated mice compared with WT controls (Fig. 1f). To investigate the effects of the changes in the related microbiome after the Con-A treatment. We used faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) to transplant the microbiota from Con A donor mice into recipient mice for 4 weeks prior to Con A challenge (Supplementary Fig. 1j). Compared with Con A-only mice, FMT-Con A recipient mice presented significantly lower serum AST and ALT levels and lower hepatic Il6 and Tnfα expression after Con A treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1k–n). In addition, we have included 16S rRNA sequencing data of the gut microbiota from both WT and FMT-Con A mice. The results demonstrate that, at 4 weeks post-FMT-Con A, the high-abundance bacterial phyla and species in the microbiota of FMT-Con A mice closely matched those found in the microbiota of Con A-treated mice at 8 h post-challenge (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 1o, p). These findings indicate that a reproducible and established Con A-associated microbial configuration had formed by week 4. Even after ABX-mediated elimination of the native microbiota, the FMT-Con A recipient mice exhibited ameliorated Con A-induced acute hepatitis, which significantly affected liver function or inflammatory cell infiltration, unlike the FMT-WT recipient mice did (Fig. 1g–n and Supplementary Fig. 1q). These findings underscore the intrinsic protective role of the gut microbiota, particularly those from Con A-treated mice, in conferring resistance to Con A-induced acute hepatitis.

Bacteroides acidifaciens (BA) and its related metabolite O-LysoPE are responsible for the self-healing process in AIH mice

Analysis of relative abundance highlighted the predominance of Bacteroidetes among the top nine key microbial OTUs at the phylum level in Con A-treated mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 1f). 16S rRNA sequencing further delineated significant microbial composition differences with notable enrichment of BA and Bacteroides caecimuris (BC) in the Con A group at the Bacteroidetes phylum (Fig. 2a–c). The mice were colonised with BA or BC, respectively according to the Supplementary Fig. 2a. We examined whether BC or BA, which was markedly different between the WT and Con A groups, conferred resistance to Con A-induced acute hepatitis in recipient mice (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) demonstrated that BA and BC were significantly more abundant in BA or BC recipient mice than in FMT-Con A recipients and were least abundant in the WT group, confirming effective microbiota transfer (Fig. 2d, e). BA recipient mice presented markedly reduced levels of serum AST, ALT, LDH and AKP following Con A treatment, as well as decreased hepatic Il6 and Tnfα expression and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, as shown by HE staining (Fig. 2f–l). Multiplex fluorescence staining for immune cell markers and apoptosis-related proteins revealed that BA significantly suppressed T cells while simultaneously alleviating hepatocellular damage (Fig. 2m and Supplementary Fig. 2c). Notably, BA recipient mice demonstrated significant resistance to Con A-induced acute hepatitis, akin to FMT-Con A recipients, whereas BC recipient mice did not exhibit comparable protection (Supplementary Fig. 2d–j). We conducted comparative genomic and functional annotation analyses of representative BA and BC genomes using eggNOG-mapper (COG/KO assignment). These analyses revealed substantial differences in genomic and metabolic capacity, especially in pathways related to lipid metabolism and cell membrane formation pathways related to phospholipid metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 2k). Analysis of faecal samples from patients with AIH and healthy controls revealed significantly lower levels of BA in patients with AIH, whereas BC levels remained unchanged (Fig. 2n, o). These results identify BA as a critical bacterium in the spontaneous resolution of Con A-induced acute hepatitis.

The gut microbiota engages in complex metabolic processes, producing metabolites that impact host physiology23–25. We hypothesised that gut microbiota-derived metabolites mediate protective effects in hepatitis. To identify key metabolites, we performed untargeted metabolomics on faecal and liver samples from Con A and FMT-Con A mice. Principal component analysis revealed distinct clustering of metabolites among the WT, Con A and FMT-Con A groups (Fig. 3a, b). Volcano plots indicated significant up- and downregulation of metabolites in faecal and liver samples from FMT-Con A or WT mice compared with those from Con A mice (Supplementary Fig. 3a–d). Venn analysis identified 121 and 92 common differentially abundant metabolites between the WT and Con A groups and between the Con A and FMT-Con A groups in the liver and faeces, respectively, with 45 and 54 showing consistent trends. Finally, we identified 5 differentially abundant metabolites with regression trends among the WT, Con A and FMT-Con A groups (Fig. 3c). The five differentially abundant metabolites were as follows: 1-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (O-LysoPE), His-Asn-Gly, N6, N6, O-tridemethylpuromycin-5′-phosphate, 2′-deoxyadenosine-5′-donophosphate and taurochenodesoxycholic acid (TCDCA) (Supplementary Fig. 3e–n). Two metabolites, O-LysoPE and TCDCA, which were selected by LC-MS assay, were consistent with the changing trend of disease progression (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Increasing O-LysoPE concentrations were associated with reduced hepatic inflammation and lower AST, ALT, Il-6 and Tnfα levels, suggesting that O-LysoPE promotes spontaneous resolution of Con A-induced acute hepatitis (Fig. 3d–h). Multiplex fluorescence staining for immune cell markers and apoptosis-related proteins revealed that O-LysoPE significantly suppressed T cell activation and simultaneously alleviated hepatocellular damage (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 3o). In addition, to provide quantitative evidence for T cell activation, we have now performed flow cytometry to determine the frequency of CD8+ CD69+ T cells in the liver. The results are consistent with our multiplex immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 3j, k and Supplementary Fig. 3p). In contrast, TCDCA did not exhibit significantly affect AIH (Supplementary Fig. 4c–f).

O-LysoPE mainly originates from BA catabolism, enters the portal vein upon absorption, and is subsequently transferred to the liver

Portal vein-enriched metabolites, from gut microbiota, are identified as regulators in liver functions26. In our study, further metabolomic analysis of faecal samples from BA recipient mice revealed significantly elevated O-LysoPE levels compared with those in Con A controls (Fig. 4a). We measured the levels of O-LysoPE in the culture environments and within BA and BC bacteria. Compared with BC, BA demonstrated superior O-LysoPE metabolism (Fig. 4b, c). Given the strong correlation between BA abundance and O-LysoPE levels and the latter’s protective role in AIH, we propose that BA may exert its hepatoprotective effects through O-LysoPE production.

To directly assess the transfer of BA-derived O-LysoPE into the host, we first administered purified O-LysoPE via oral gavage to ABX-treated mice and tracked its dynamics in cecal content, portal vein blood and liver over 0–48 h (Fig. 4d). O-LysoPE was detectable in the cecal content within 2 h, reaching a significant increase at 2 h and peaking at 4 h (Fig. 4e). In the portal vein, O-LysoPE levels rose significantly by 4 h and reached a peak shortly thereafter, whereas in the liver, peak O-LysoPE levels were observed between 4 and 8 h and gradually decline (Fig. 4f, g). Next, we examined BA mono-colonisation in ABX-treated mice (Fig. 4h–j). Compared with WT mice, ABX treatment markedly reduced BA abundance and significantly decreased O-LysoPE levels in cecal content, portal circulation and liver (Supplementary Fig. 5a–c). Following BA colonisation, O-LysoPE levels in the cecal content, portal vein and liver progressively increased over time, becoming detectable by day 3 and further rising at days 7–14 (Fig. 4k–m). In contrast, mice receiving heat-inactivated BA exhibited no significant increase in O-LysoPE level (Fig. 4k–m). Together, these data indicate that BA can produce O-LysoPE in the gut, which is subsequently transferred to the host and reaches the liver, supporting a functional role for BA-derived O-LysoPE in modulating hepatocytes. Consistently, in germ-free (GF) mice mono-colonised with BA, O-LysoPE levels began to rise by day 3 and increased further at days 7 and 14 in the cecum, portal circulation and liver (Fig. 4n–q). After colonisation of GF mice with BA, the increase in O-LysoPE detected in the portal vein and liver originates exclusively from this single bacterial source. These findings provide direct evidence that BA is sufficient to generate O-LysoPE in vivo and deliver it to the liver.

To determine whether O-LysoPE primarily originates from the gut microbiota or from the host, we performed qPCR analysis of several PLA2 isoforms (pla2g2d, pla2g4a and pla2g7) in liver and gut tissues from ABX-treated mice colonised with live BA and from mice receiving heat-inactivated BA. Our results showed no significant differences in the expression levels of these PLA2 isoforms between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 5d–i). We also performed qPCR analysis of major PLA2 isoforms in both intestinal and hepatic tissues from GF mice. Consistent with our observations in ABX-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 5j–o). These findings suggest that O-LysoPE mainly derives from microbiota, particularly BA, rather than from enhanced PLA2 activity in host tissues.

The abundance of BA and levels of O-LysoPE are reduced in AIH patients

Analysis of serum and faecal samples from patients with AIH and healthy controls revealed significantly lower levels of BA in patients with AIH, whereas BC levels remained unchanged (Fig. 2n, o). We detected a strong correlation between O-LysoPE levels and BA abundance, but no clear relationship with BC abundance (Fig. 4r). In our human cohort, patients under standard therapy exhibited largely normalised ALT and AST levels, rendering transaminases unreliable for assessing disease activity. Nevertheless, immune activity remained evident, with elevated ASMA, ANA and IgG levels (Supplementary Fig. 5p–r). We observed a trend toward correlation between serum ASMA levels and faecal O-LysoPE abundance. However, this did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Fig. 5s–u). Our data has indicated that both faecal and serum O-LysoPE levels are significantly reduced in AIH patients compared with healthy controls (Fig. 4s, t), supporting an association between O-LysoPE depletion and disease status.

Unlike mice, human AIH fails to spontaneously resolve, highlighting the potential of leveraging murine self-healing mechanisms for therapeutic development. This could be attributed to the low abundance of BA bacteria within the gut microbiota, resulting in inadequate production of O-LysoPE, which enters the liver. Consequently, this insufficiency fails to assist the liver in defending against attacks from hyperactive immune cells in AIH patients.

O-LysoPE to fuel the checkpoint Qa-1b: Nkg2a, facilitates hepatocytic immune tolerance, subsequently restoring overactivated T cells to the resting state

To investigate the genetic and signaling pathways influencing liver self-healing mechanisms, we conducted a transcriptomic analysis of liver tissues from three mouse cohorts. The objective of this study was to identify key genes and signaling pathways involved in the amelioration of AIH. We identified differentially expressed genes common to both the WT and Con A groups, as well as between the Con A and FMT-Con A groups. The focus was placed on genes whose expression trends were consistent across both the WT and FMT-Con A liver samples. Further analysis of key genes revealed regression trends in the WT, Con A and FMT-Con A groups (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database, we explored the metabolic pathways and functions associated with these differentially expressed genes. Hypergeometric testing revealed significant alterations in the natural killer (NK) cell-mediated cytotoxicity pathway and the critical gene Nkg2a (Fig. 5a). Nkg2a, an inhibitory receptor predominantly expressed on NK and T cells, forms a dimer with Cd94. This Nkg2a-Cd94 dimer interacts with the Qa-1b (HLA-E in humans) ligand on self-cells, resulting in significant inhibition of NK cell and T cell activity27–30 (Fig. 5b). Western blot (WB) analysis revealed that Cd94 was downregulated in Con A-treated mice, and its level was not restored in FMT-Con A or BA recipient mice (Fig. 5c). However, the content of Qa-1b and Nkg2a was significantly increased in the latter groups. In addition, transcriptomic and WB analyses indicated that Qa-1b was more abundant in the mouse liver than in other major organs. However, in the human tissue transcriptome database, HLA-E was not as prevalent in the liver as H2t23 was in the mouse liver (Fig. 5d, e). Notably, the gene corresponding to Qa-1b, H2t23, exhibited more pronounced and rapid changes than did Nkg2a. Additionally, H2t23 expression changes were notably suppressed following gut microbiota depletion with antibiotics (Fig. 5f, g). Qa-1b expression progressively increased with increasing O-LysoPE concentration. Qa-1b: Nkg2a binding was also increased (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Fig. 5b, c). These findings underscore the critical role of Qa-1b in the O-LysoPE-mediated self-healing mechanism in the liver. O-LysoPE, which originates from the gastrointestinal tract, enters the liver and modulates immune cell activity by enhancing the activity of the Qa-1b: Nkg2a pathway.

Upon Con A treatment, T cells in the liver become excessively activated, leading to the destruction of hepatic parenchymal cells. Single-cell sequencing data collected 24 h post-Con A treatment revealed that H2t23 expression in parenchymal cells remained significantly elevated (Fig. 5i, j), whereas the expression levels of Nkg2a and Cd94 in NK and T cells had normalised (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e). These findings suggest that T cells and NK cells in the liver are significantly suppressed 24 h after Con A induction, suggesting that the upregulation of H2t23 expression in hepatic parenchymal cells plays a critical role in inhibiting T cell activity. We hypothesise that O-LysoPE targets hepatic parenchymal cells and inhibits T cell activation through the interaction of hepatic Qa-1b with Nkg2a on T cells.

To test this hypothesis, primary hepatocytes (PHs) and primary lymphocytes (Lys-Sp (primary lymphocytes from the spleen); Lys-Ln (primary lymphocytes from the lymph nodes)) were isolated and preincubated with O-LysoPE before being cocultured with Con A to mimic the in vivo immune‒parenchymal cell interactions during Con A-induced acute hepatitis (Supplementary Fig. 7a). We assessed T cell activation by quantifying the expression of CD25+ and CD69+, key markers of T cell activation, using flow cytometry. The results indicated that T cells pretreated with O-LysoPE alone were significantly activated upon Con A stimulation, similar to those in the untreated O-LysoPE group. However, in the groups in which PHs were pretreated with O-LysoPE before Con A stimulation, T cell activation was markedly inhibited (Fig. 5k, l and Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). The levels of granzyme B, a key mediator of T cell cytotoxicity expressed by activated T cells31,32 were significantly reduced in the culture medium of primary hepatocyte–lymphocyte co-cultures when hepatocytes were pretreated with O-LysoPE (Supplementary Fig. 7d, e). In addition, the downregulation of the inflammatory cytokines Tnfα and Il6 in PHs was noted (Supplementary Fig. 7f–i). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining revealed that O-LysoPE pretreatment followed by Con A stimulation led to increased Qa-1b protein levels in PHs (Fig. 5m). The increased Qa-1b content in PHs also drove the increased Qa-1b: Nkg2a binding (Fig. 5m and Supplementary Fig. 7j). Notably, direct T cell activation with Con A, independent of the influence of PHs, was not significantly suppressed by O-LysoPE alone (Supplementary Fig. 8a). These results directly confirm that hepatocytes are the primary target of O-LysoPE and that O-LysoPE effectively attenuates T cell overactivation in the liver by the upregulation of Qa-1b expression in hepatocytes.

Therapeutic effects of O-LysoPE and BA in a chronic AIH model

To investigate the therapeutic potential of O-LysoPE and BA in chronic AIH, we employed a human CYP2D6 adenovirus-based mouse model. After the viral injection, O-LysoPE and BA were administered by oral gavage every 2 days. Blood samples were collected every 5 days for serum AST, ALT and IgG measurement to monitor disease progression and evaluate treatment effects (Fig. 6a).

During the initial 15-day acute phase, adenovirus-induced liver injury caused peak serum AST, ALT and IgG levels. Notably, BA abundance in the gut increased transiently during the acute phase but declined as the model transitioned to chronic inflammation, suggesting that sustained liver injury may not support long-term BA colonisation (Fig. 6b, c). Despite this, both O-LysoPE and BA treatment significantly reduced serum AST and ALT levels compared to the Ad-h-CYP2D6 group, indicating improved liver function (Fig. 6d, e). Additionally, IgG levels were significantly suppressed in the O-LysoPE and BA groups, reflecting reduced autoimmune activity (Fig. 6f).

Histological analysis at day 35 revealed significant reductions in hepatic inflammatory cell infiltration (HE staining) and fibrosis (Sirius Red staining) in the O-LysoPE and BA treatment groups compared to controls, suggesting therapeutic effects on both inflammation and fibrosis (Fig. 6g). Liver cytokine analysis showed a marked decrease in Il6 and Tnfa expression, indicating a reduction in inflammatory cytokine production (Fig. 6h, i). Furthermore, upregulation of H2t23 and Nkg2a suggests that O-LysoPE and BA modulate immune responses through hepatocyte-driven immunoregulation (Fig. 6j, k). These results demonstrate that O-LysoPE and BA significantly ameliorate liver damage in chronic AIH by improving liver function, reducing inflammation, alleviating fibrosis and promoting immune tolerance, offering a promising therapeutic approach for chronic AIH.

AIH alleviation by O-LysoPE is dependent on Qa-1b content in hepatocytes

The TBG promoter of adeno-associated virus serotype 8 (AAV8) enables long-term sustained expression in hepatocytes. We utilised an AAV8 viruses with a TBG promoter for sustained H2t23 overexpression (AAV8 oe-H2t23) and knockdown (AAV8 sh-H2t23) in hepatocytes (Fig. 7a). Six weeks postinjection, mice received O-LysoPE pretreatment followed by Con A treatment as per the outlined groupings. WB and qPCR confirmed the successful modulation of Qa-1b/H2t23 expression in liver tissues (Fig. 7b, c). Assessment of serum AST and ALT levels, along with hepatic Il6 and Tnfα gene expression, revealed exacerbation of Con A-induced acute hepatitis upon H2t23 knockdown (Fig. 7d–g), and the protective effect of O-LysoPE significantly diminished under these conditions. Conversely, H2t23 overexpression in hepatocytes conferred resistance to Con A-induced acute hepatitis. Histological examination revealed increased inflammatory infiltration in the liver following H2t23 knockdown, which was notably inhibited by O-LysoPE (Fig. 7h). Multiplex fluorescence staining of immune cells and apoptotic proteins revealed that H2t23 knockdown significantly increased activated T cell infiltration and strongly exacerbated hepatocyte damage (Fig. 7i and Supplementary Fig. 9a). Our results show that knockdown of H2t23 in hepatocytes markedly attenuated the protective effect of O-LysoPE, indicating that H2t23 is essential for O-LysoPE-mediated hepatocyte regulation.

Similarly, isolated PHs and lymphocytes were used to simulate the immune-parenchymal cell state after Con A treatment in vitro. Transfection of PHs with H2t23 overexpression (oe-H2t23) or H2t23 knockdown (sh-H2t23) plasmids followed by flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that H2t23 knockdown increased the T cell activation markers CD25+ and CD69+ (Fig. 7j, k and Supplementary Fig. 9b–e), whereas H2t23 overexpression reduced T cell activation under Con A stimulation. These in vivo and in vitro findings underscore Qa-1b as a critical target of O-LysoPE in the inhibition of excessive T cell activation in hepatocytes.

The selective transcriptional regulation of Creb1 by the H2t23 promoter is responsible for the promotion of self-healing by Qa-1b in O-LysoPE-induced AIH

Phosphatidylethanolamine serves as a specific ligand for phosphatidylethanolamine-binding proteins (Pebps). Among the Pebp family, Pebp1 is highly expressed in the liver. To evaluate the affinity of O-LysoPE for Pebp1, molecular docking analysis was performed. The optimal binding poses and interactions between O-LysoPE and Pebp1 were obtained using AutoDock Vina v.1.2.2, yielding a binding energy of −8.2 kcal/mol (Fig. 8a, b and Supplementary Table 1). This strong binding affinity, supported by the results of the cellular thermal shift assays (Fig. 8c, d), confirmed the stable interaction between O-LysoPE and Pebp1.

Based on full-length 16S rRNA sequences, we mapped the sequenced microbiome composition to a database and determined that the most highly enriched pathway associated with BA was the cAMP signaling pathway (Fig. 8e). Numerous studies have shown that Ser133 phosphorylated cAMP response element-binding protein 1 (Creb1) binds to the SXY region of the H2t23 promoter, upregulating Qa-1b expression30,33. We found that phospho-Creb1 (Ser133) was significantly elevated of O-LysoPE-treated mice (Fig. 8f). Consistent with previous reports that Pebp1 promotes Creb1 phosphorylation34, our coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays confirmed the interaction between Pebp1 and phospho-Creb1 (Ser133) and their role in enhancing phosphorylation (Fig. 8g). Under high-inflammatory conditions, O-LysoPE produced by BA binds to Pebp1 in hepatocytes, promoting Creb1 phosphorylation at Ser133, which increase H2t23 expression in hepatocytes (Fig. 8h).

Previous studies have indicated that LysoPE in hepatocytes induces lipid droplet formation and affects the expression of fatty acid biosynthesis-related genes, including Acaca, Fasn, Scd1 and Srebp135,36. Creb1 is also a key regulator of fatty acid synthesis genes37,38. However, our findings revealed that in both FMT-Con A recipient mice and BA recipient mice, fatty acid synthesis-related genes were not upregulated following Con A-induced liver inflammation (Supplementary Fig. 10a–c). In both in vitro and in vivo experiments, hepatic lipid synthesis was not increased by O-LysoPE in Con A-treated mice (Fig. 9a–c and Supplementary Fig. 10d, e). Surprisingly, direct O-LysoPE treatment of isolated PHs significantly upregulated the lipid synthesis genes Acaca, Fasn, Scd1 and Srebp1 (Fig. 9d), independent of coculture with Con A-activated lymphocytes, but did not affect H2t23 or Qa-1b expression levels (Fig. 9e, f and Supplementary Fig. 10f). Nile red staining confirmed increased lipid droplets in PHs directly treated with O-LysoPE (Supplementary Fig. 10g). Conversely, in PHs cocultured with Con A-activated lymphocytes, O-LysoPE treatment did not promote lipid droplet formation. These results indicate that O-LysoPE alone significantly upregulates Creb1-regulated lipid synthesis genes under physiological conditions without altering Qa-1b levels, whereas under inflammatory conditions, it promotes Creb1-mediated H2t23 transcription.

Using the JASPAR database, Creb1 binding sites were identified on the promoter sequences of lipid synthesis genes and H2t23 (Fig. 9g). The promoter sequences of lipid synthesis genes, such as Acaca, Fasn and Scd1, do not contain any Creb1 binding sites except for the proximal promoter sequence of Srebp1, which possesses a Creb1 binding site. The binding sites of Creb1 on the promoters of Srebp1 and H2t23 were found to be incongruous (Fig. 9h). Chip-qPCR confirmed that O-LysoPE stimulates fat generation through Creb1-Srebp1 under physiological conditions while promoting Creb1-H2t23 transcription under inflammatory conditions (Fig. 9i, j). Co-immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry (Co-IP-MS) of phospho-Creb1 under physiological (Ctl vs O-LysoPE) and inflammatory (Con A vs Con A + O-LysoPE) conditions identified distinct sets of differentially associated proteins in each context (32 proteins in the physiological comparison, 30 proteins in the inflammatory comparison). Only a single protein changed in both contexts, indicating largely non-overlapping interaction networks (Fig. 9k). After removing the single commonly changing protein, GO enrichment of the 29 inflammation-specific candidate interactors highlighted nucleotide-binding and chromatin-associated factors (including Arf4, Eef1a1, Hsp90ab1, Suclg1 and Tubb4b). Several of these proteins (for example, Hsp90ab1 and Tubb4b) are plausibly linked to chromatin remodelling or to recruitment/stabilisation of transcriptional complexes, and Phb2—also recovered in the screen—has been implicated in innate antiviral programmes, consistent with an inflammation-responsive interactome. By contrast, GO enrichment of the 31 physiological-condition candidate interactors implicated fatty-acid and lipid metabolic processes (notably Acsl1, Fabp5, Hsd17b4 and Cyp2d10). (Supplementary Fig. 10h and Supplementary Table 2)

These results support a model in which O-LysoPE-stimulated phospho-Creb1 engages different co-activator/co-regulator complexes depending on cellular context: under homoeostatic conditions, Creb1 associates with lipid-metabolism-linked partners and preferentially occupies the Srebp1 promoter to promote lipogenesis; under inflammatory conditions, Creb1 associates with chromatin-remodelling and nucleotide-binding factors and is retargeted to the H2t23 promoter, driving Qa-1b expression and hepatocyte-mediated suppression of T-cell activation. In short, O-LysoPE does not change Creb1 phosphorylation per se but appears to reprogramme the phospho-Creb1 interactome—altering chromatin accessibility and promoter selection—to switch Creb1 output from metabolic to immunoregulatory gene programmes.

Consequently, in AIH livers, O-LysoPE derived from the intestine can facilitate Creb1 binding to the promoter region of H2t23 in liver parenchymal cells under conditions of high inflammation. This promotes the transcription and expression of H2t23, enabling the liver to draw on the advantages and avoid disadvantages while inhibiting excessive T cell activation through Qa-1b. This mechanism allows the liver to balance immune tolerance and lipid metabolism while mitigating excessive T cell activation (Fig. 9l).

Discussion

The Con A-induced acute hepatitis model in mice is currently widely used as a significant immune-mediated liver injury animal model39. Using this model, we identified a self-healing mechanism in the liver that revealed protective pathways against T-cell-mediated injury. To extend these findings to chronic disease, we employed a human CYP2D6 adenovirus-based model of AIH, capturing tolerance breakdown, autoantibody production and fibrosis17,18. AIH shows a marked female predominance in humans40,41. Consistent with this feature and the higher sensitivity of female mice to Con A-induced hepatitis, we primarily used female mice to investigate. To address potential sex bias, we further validated our findings in male mice, which also exhibited self-healing after Con A treatment (Supplementary Fig. 11a–d). Importantly, both BA and its metabolite O-LysoPE retained protective effects in male mice (Supplementary Fig. 11e–k), consistent with females, suggesting that the identified microbiota-metabolite-hepatic immunoregulatory axis is broadly applicable across sexes.

Growing evidence supports an association between gut microbiota dysbiosis and AIH, highlighting the gut–liver axis as a potential modulator of hepatic immune homoeostasis42–47. The relationship between the gut microbiota and AIH is complicated and heterogeneous. In this study, we found that the self-healing effect in Con A-induced acute hepatitis mice was significantly blocked after gut microbiota depletion by ABX, highlighting the importance of the gut microbiota in the self-healing of AIH mice. Additionally, a significantly increased abundance of the BA strain was observed at 8 h after Con A stimulation, coinciding with a decline in liver injury markers and hepatic inflammatory cytokines. More importantly, FMT and BA strain colonisation suggested that BA promotes self-recovery of the liver in Con A-induced acute hepatitis. While BA and O-LysoPE appear central, other microbial taxa, metabolites, and host immunoregulatory pathways may also contribute and warrant further study.

Metabolomic analysis revealed that the related metabolite O-LysoPE produced by BA was enriched. LysoPE is metabolically produced by gram-negative bacteria in the gut microbiota and can be released into the external environment upon bacterial lysis48,49. Our findings revealed that in the Con A-induced acute hepatitis model in mice, O-LysoPE mainly upregulated Qa-1b in hepatocytes through the 'gut‒liver axis', in conjunction with Nkg2a, suppressing immune cell activation and thereby restoring immune balance in the liver. We confirmed that O-LysoPE had little direct effect on T-cell activation (Supplementary Fig. 8), which also indicated that liver parenchymal cells play a strong role in O-LysoPE-mediated hepatic immune tolerance. We leverage this self-healing phenotype to propose a hepatocyte-centred immunoregulatory mechanism, wherein gut-derived metabolites empower hepatocytes to actively suppress intrahepatic immune overactivation.

Our data indicate that increased abundance of the gut microbe BA and its derived metabolites are associated with modulation of hepatic immune responses. Notably, we observed a rapid expansion of BA following Con A challenge, which temporally coincided with changes in hepatic inflammation and metabolic parameters. These observations suggest that the increase in BA abundance may, at least in part, reflect a dynamic remodelling of the gut microbiota secondary to acute liver injury. Acute hepatic inflammation is known to perturb systemic and intestinal metabolic homoeostasis, including alterations in bile acid composition and oxidative stress, which may create a transient intestinal environment permissive for BA expansion.

Extensive prior studies have established a strong genetic association between the HLA locus and AIH, with HLA-DRB1 consistently identified as a major susceptibility gene in European and North American populations50,51. These observations were subsequently reinforced by large-scale genome-wide association analyses, which confirmed the HLA region (particularly HLA-B and HLA-DRB1) as the most significant genetic risk locus for AIH52. In this study, we identified a potential link between Qa-1b, the non-classical MHC molecule in hepatocytes, and immune regulation in AIH. Our results revealed that O-LysoPE specifically upregulated hepatocytic Qa-1b in AIH mice, promoting hepatocyte-induced tolerance and limiting immune cells via Nkg2a, the inhibitory receptor. These findings suggest that promoting the expression of HLA-E on hepatocytes and strengthening subsequent immune tolerance could serve as a crucial therapeutic approach for treating AIH, which may lead to longer-term alleviation of the disease. Furthermore, targeting hepatocytic Qa-1b with O-LysoPE or BA strains avoids the side effects of traditional nonspecific immunosuppressants, such as granulocyte deficiency and severe infections due to the over-suppression of the immune system53.

Moreover, O-LysoPE does not affect lipid synthesis-related genes in inflammatory liver environments and can overcome the dyslipidaemia caused by traditional glucocorticoids54. LysoPE has previously been implicated in hepatic lipid metabolism through the induction of lipid droplet formation and modulation of triglyceride profiles35. Our study uncovers an additional, context-dependent role for O-LysoPE in directing distinct Creb1 transcriptional programmes. Under homoeostatic conditions, O-LysoPE promotes the association of phospho-Creb1 with lipid metabolism–linked cofactors such as Acsl1, Fabp5, Hsd17b4 and Cyp2d10, thereby facilitating occupancy of the Srebp1 promoter and driving lipogenesis. By contrast, during inflammatory stress, O-LysoPE reshapes the phospho-Creb1 interactome toward chromatin- and nucleotide-binding proteins, including Hsp90ab1, Eef1a1, Suclg1, Tubb4b and Phb2, resulting in preferential engagement of the H2t23 promoter and induction of Qa-1b, a non-classical MHC molecule that attenuates T-cell activation. Notably, the interactomes under physiological and inflammatory states were almost entirely distinct, consistent with a model in which O-LysoPE does not alter Creb1 phosphorylation per se but reprogramme its co-activator landscape to switch Creb1 output from metabolic to immunoregulatory gene networks. The identification of candidate cofactors such as Hsp90ab1 and Phb2 provides a mechanistic entry point into this selective promoter engagement, and future studies integrating chromatin accessibility profiling and single-cell transcriptomics may further delineate how the epigenetic milieu governs Creb1-dependent transcriptional choice.

Previous clinical research has shown that, in patients with AIH, the abundances of Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus and Bacteroidetes in faecal samples were reduced42. Whether the absence of BA fully explains the lack of spontaneous remission in AIH patients remains unresolved. Reduced BA abundance may lower hepatic O-LysoPE availability and weaken hepatocyte-mediated immunoregulation, yet additional host or microbial factors are likely to constrain self-healing in patients. Our findings, therefore, position BA and its metabolite O-LysoPE as potential modulators of disease outcome, but we consider this a testable hypothesis that requires validation in clinical cohorts and mechanistic models. In this study, stool samples were obtained from AIH patients during treatment-induced remission, which limited our ability to capture microbial dynamics from diagnosis through remission. Future longitudinal studies including treatment-naive patients will be essential to delineate how BA and BC abundance evolve across different disease stages and under therapeutic interventions. Recent studies have shown that dietary inulin, a fermentable fibre, selectively enriches BA abundance in the gut and alleviates liver fibrosis55,56. When integrated with our findings, this suggests that practical dietary interventions, such as inulin supplementation, may provide a feasible strategy to enhance BA levels in AIH patients. Such an approach could offer a tractable means to restore immunoregulation and potentially ameliorate chronic AIH.

In summary, our findings highlight a previously underappreciated mode of liver self-regulation in AIH. The intestinal microbial metabolite O-LysoPE reaches the liver in acute hepatitis and selectively engages a hepatocyte-intrinsic immune checkpoint pathway, Qa-1b (HLA-E in humans)–NKG2a, thereby attenuating excessive T-cell activation. Hepatocytes as the primary target cells through which a microbiota-derived metabolite exerts immunoregulatory effects. Therefore, our study represents a paradigm shift in AIH treatment by moving beyond the conventional focus on T-cell modulation. Importantly, this mechanism avoids potential metabolic complications while opening avenues for developing liver-specific targeted immunotherapies for AIH.

Methods

Mice

BALB/c mice (Mus musculus, inbred strain, substrain not specified) were purchased from GemPharmatech. All experimental mice were maintained and bred under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. GF mice were obtained from GemPharmatech and housed in a dedicated GF facility. All mice were 6–8 weeks of age at the time of experimentation. Animals were maintained under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, with controlled ambient temperature (24 ± 2 °C) and relative humidity (40–60%). Sex was considered in the study design; female mice were primarily used due to the higher prevalence of AIH in females and increased susceptibility to Con A-induced hepatitis, and key findings were further validated in male mice. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Academies Press, 2011) and were approved by the Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing University (approval number: IACUC-2409005).

Human studies

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (approval number: 2024-SRFA-082). We obtained written informed consent from patients with AIH and healthy donors. Human faecal and blood samples were obtained and prepared under standard procedures at The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. Briefly, healthy donors were 20–40 years old and generally healthy based on self-reported data. Patient donors ranged in age from 10 to 70 years old (Supplementary Table 4). Faecal samples from two AIH patients failed to yield sufficient high-quality genomic DNA due to environmental factors affecting sample integrity. All the samples were collected and stored at −80 °C.

In our human cohort, all patients were under standard therapy and were clinically improving at the time of sampling; consequently, serum transaminase levels (ALT and AST) have largely normalised and were not reliable indicators of ongoing disease activity. However, immune activity was still measurable through diagnostic autoantibodies, including ASMA, ANA, as well as elevated IgG levels, which are key indicators for clinical decision-making in AIH.

Con A induced AIH model mice

Con A (Sigma-Aldrich, C2010) was dissolved in 0.01 mol/L PBS, the concentration was 2 mg/ml, and the dose of 10 mg/kg was injected once from the tail vein of mice. Meanwhile, WT control group was set up after the tail vein injection of PBS.

Chronic AIH model

To investigate the therapeutic potential of O-LysoPE and BA in a chronic AIH model, we employed a human CYP2D6 adenovirus-based mouse model. This human CYP2D6 adenovirus was designed, packaged and purified by Cyagen Biosciences Inc. BALB/c mice were injected with a total of 2 × 1010 PFU Ad-h-CYP2D6 (i.p. and i.v.)18 and were randomly assigned into four treatment groups: control, Ad-h-CYP2D6, Ad-h-CYP2D6 + O-LysoPE and Ad-h-CYP2D6 + BA. After the viral injection, O-LysoPE and BA were administered by oral gavage every 2 days. Blood samples were collected every 5 days for serum AST, ALT and IgG measurement to monitor disease progression and evaluate treatment effects.

Antibiotics-cleaned mice

The mice were administered a mixture of four different antibiotics (ABX) once a day for 2 consecutive weeks via continuous intragastric administration. The ABX consisted of ampicillin (1 g/L, Innochem), metronidazole (1 g/L, Macklin), neomycin (1 g/L, Shanghai Dibai Biotechnology) and vancomycin (0.5 g/L, Innochem)57,58.

FMT-Con A mice and FMT-WT recipient mice

The model group of AIH induced by Con A was injected into the tail vein of mice at a dose of 10 mg/kg. After Con A injection for 8 h, mice faeces were collected and frozen at 1 g/ tube at −80 °C. The WT control group was set up with PBS tail vein injection, and the faeces of WT mice were collected and frozen at 1 g/ tube −80 °C. Subsequently, add 800 μl of PBS to each tube and mix thoroughly. Centrifuge the samples at 1600 × g to obtain the supernatant. Administer 200 μl of the supernatant orally to each mouse daily for a duration of 4 weeks.

BA and BC recipient mice

Individual strains BC (Leibniz Institute DSMZ, DSM26085) and BA (Leibniz Institute DSMZ, DSM15896) were screened. SPF mice colonised with 109 CFU BA or BC. BC and BA bacteria were given intragastric administration for 4 weeks. Faecal samples were collected to detect whether the recipient mice of group BC and group BA were successfully colonised. WT control group of mice in the PBS group was set up.

O-LysoPE and TCDCA treatment in vivo

According to the content of O-LysoPE (Aladdin, O130377) in the blood of mice, we conducted tail-vein pre-administration, which was 0.1 mg/kg (physiological concentration), 0.5 and 1 mg/kg, respectively. O-LysoPE was pre-administered in the tail vein for 1 week, once every 2 days. The WT control group injected by PBS in the tail vein was set up.

We performed tail-vein preadministration of TCDCA according to the blood content of mice, which were 0.08 mg/kg (physiological concentration), 0.8 and 1.6 mg/kg, respectively. The tail vein of TCDCA (MCE, HY-N2027) was preadministered once every 2 days for 1 week. The WT control group injected by PBS in the tail vein was set up.

Adeno-associated viruses (AAV8)

We selected adeno-associated viruses (AAV8) containing the liver-specific promoter TBG to induce H2T23 overexpression (AAV8 oe-H2T23) and shRNA-mediated knockdown of H2T23 (AAV8 sh-H2T23). The construction of the AAV8 oe-H2T23 plasmid or the AAV8 sh-H2T23 plasmid for the expression of no-load plasmids targeting liver mice was designed, validated and synthesised by Tsingke Biotechnology, Inc. The shRNA sequence is presented in Supplementary Table 3, all oligonucleotides were commercially synthesised by Tsingke Biological Technology. Briefly, AAV8 oe-H2T23, AAV8 sh-H2T23, or the WT control was injected once into 4-week-old BALB/c mice at a dose of 5 × 1011 vg/mouse via the tail vein. The samples were collected 4 weeks later.

Cell culture

Mouse PHs were isolated from 8-week-old mice by using the collagenase IV perfusion method. Lymphocytes from the spleen and lymph nodes of the mice were isolated using the grinding method. Mouse PHs were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotics (penicillin and streptomycin). Mouse primary lymphocytes (Lys-Sp (primary lymphocytes from the spleen); Lys-Ln (primary lymphocytes from the lymph nodes)) were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics (penicillin and streptomycin). The cells were cultured in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Lymphocytes and PHs co-culture

After the primary lymphocyte density was adjusted to 2 × 106/mL, the cells were planted in 6-well plates according to the groups. PHs was also spread in 6-well plates after isolation, and the fresh medium was replaced after 6 h of wall attachment. Primary lymphocytes and PHs were pretreated with O-LysoPE 50 μg/ml for 12 h. The pre-treated primary lymphocytes were co-cultured with PHs and stimulated with 5 μg/mL Con A for 48 h. Lymphocyte surface proteins are surface-stained using specified fluorescently labelled flow antibodies. Finally, flow cytometry was used to detect the proportion of activated T cells.

Bacterial strains culture

BA and BC were cultured using Colombian blood AGAR medium (special peptone 23 g/L, cornstarch 1 g/L, sodium chloride 5 g/L, AGAR 15 g/L, defibrillated sheep blood 50 ml). The plates were anaerobically incubated (10% H2, 10% CO2 and 80% N2) at 37 °C for 48 h (AW300SG anaerobic workstations; Electrotek, England). BA and BC were scraped off and then suspended with PBS. The bacterial suspensions were centrifuged at 8100 × g for 5 min, washed with sterile PBS twice and resuspended in 1 ml sterile PBS containing 5 × 109 colony-forming units (CFUs). Each mouse was intragastrical treated with 200 μl every day for single bacterial colonisation.

Western blotting

For protein expression analysis, liver tissues were homogenised, and the cells were lysed in RIPA buffer. The protein concentration was quantified with a BCA protein quantification kit. Equal amounts of protein were loaded and separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were incubated overnight with appropriate primary antibodies. Bound antibodies were then visualised using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. WB results are shown as representative blots from two animals randomly selected from each group. The primary antibody: mouse monoclonal anti-beta-Tubulin (1:10000, Affinity, T0023), mouse monoclonal anti-Qa1b (1:1000, Abcam, ab72487), rabbit polyclonal anti-KLRC1(Nkg2a) (1: 1000, Abclonal, A1233), mouse monoclonal anti-CD94(H-3) (1:800, Santa Cruz, sc-390776), rabbit monoclonal anti-CREB (phospho S133) (1:500, Abcam, ab32096).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Takara, 9109) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentrations were determined by a Nanodrop analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Complementary DNA samples were synthesised with PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Accurate Biology, AG11706). Gene expression was analyzed with quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using SYBR premix (Accurate Biology, AG11701). Relative expression levels were determined using the comparative CT method to normalise target gene mRNA to internal control 36B4. Primer sequences used are listed in Supplementary Table 3, all oligonucleotides were commercially synthesised by Tsingke Biological Technology.

Relative quantification of BA and BC abundance in faecal samples

Faecal samples were collected from mice at the indicated time points, and total bacterial DNA was extracted using the Stool DNA kit (CwBio, CW2091S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR was performed using SYBR Green chemistry on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System. Species-specific primers were designed to amplify BA and BC (listed in Supplementary Table 3), while universal 16S rRNA primers were used as an internal reference to normalise for total bacterial DNA content.

The relative abundance of BA or BC in each sample was calculated using the ΔCt method: 2−ΔCt, (ΔCt = Ct target − Ct total), while fold-changes between groups were determined by the ΔΔCt method.

Serological analyses

Blood samples of mice were collected in non-heparinized tubes and centrifuged at 1600 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The contents of AST (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, C010-2-1), ALT (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, C009-2-1), LDH (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, A020-2-2) and AKP (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, A059-2-2) in serum were determined by spectrophotometry using commercial kits. The contents of IgG (Beyotime, PI480), ASMA (Enzyme-linked Biotechnology, Ml002066), ANA (ReagentGenie, MOFI01271), Tnf-α (Thermo Fisher Scientific, ECM008), Il-6 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, BMS603-2), Il-1β (Dakewe, 1210122) and granzyme B (Elabscience, E-EL-M0594) were qualified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits.

Blood samples of human from AIH patients and healthy individuals were collected in non-heparinized tubes and centrifuged at 1600 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The contents of AST and ALT in serum were determined by spectrophotometry using commercial kits. The contents of IgG (Abclonal, RK00393) and ASMA (Enzyme-linked Biotechnology, Ml025676), ANA (Enzyme-linked Biotechnology, Ml025818) in serum were quantified by ELISA kits.

Histological analysis

Tissues were isolated, fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution for 24 h in situ, processed for paraffin embedding and cut into 4 μm transverse sections for routine hematoxylin & eosin (HE) staining.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cells were cultured and expanded until 90–100% confluence in 10-cm dishes, with five experimental groups (one dish per group). For each dish, 700 μL of lysis buffer was added for protein extraction. Total protein was quantified to ensure an equal amount of 1000 μg per group. The total volume of each lysate was adjusted to 500 μL using lysis buffer. The 40 μL aliquot from each group was collected and mixed with loading buffer to serve as the input sample for WB analysis.

For immunoprecipitation, the remaining lysates (excluding the IgG control group) were incubated with primary antibodies at a 1:300 dilution. The IgG control group was treated with an equivalent amount of IgG antibody. The lysates were sealed with parafilm and incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. On the following day, 40 μL of protein A/G magnetic beads (MCE, HY-K0202) were added to each sample, followed by incubation at room temperature for 1 h with rotation. The supernatants were discarded, and the beads were retained. After washing, 50 μL of loading buffer was added to the beads, and the samples were boiled for 10 min to denature the proteins. The immunoprecipitated samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis. WB was subsequently performed to detect specific proteins, following standard protocols.

LC-MS detection

When treating liquid samples (serum, bacterial culture medium, bacterial lysate), the 50 μL sample was mixed with 150 μL precipitation reagent (methanol: isopropyl alcohol: formic acid v/v/v = 2:1:0.1%) in a vortex and centrifuged at 4 °C at 20,000 × g for 30 min. The supernatant 1:10 was diluted and analyzed. When handling solid samples (faeces: 30 mg samples in EP tube, to join the two small steel balls, placed in a freezer in −20 °C after 5 min, into the mill grinding; Add 600 μL isopropyl alcohol: acetonitrile (1:1) was extracted by ultrasound in ice water bath for 10 min, and left for 30 min at −20 °C. Centrifuge for 10 min (20,000 × g, 4 °C). The supernatant was diluted at 1:10 and analyzed. Sample on the ACQUITY CSH using a C18 chromatographic column separation, mobile phase A is ultrapure water (including 0.1 formic acid %), mobile phase of acetonitrile B (including 0.1 formic acid %). Injection volume 2 µl, flow rate 0.4 ml/min. The mass spectrometry was performed using the QTRAP6500 MS/MS spectrometer for positive and negative ion modes.

Co-immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry (Co-IP-MS)

Samples obtained through Co-IP. In-gel digestion protocol for proteomic analysis: protein bands were excised from SDS-PAGE gels, cut into small pieces, and rinsed twice with distilled water. The gel pieces were destained at 37 °C until colourless, then dehydrated with acetonitrile (ACN) and dried using a vacuum concentrator. Gel pieces were reduced with 25 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 55 °C for 45 min, followed by alkylation with 55 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) in the dark for 30 min. After rinsing and dehydration with ACN, the gel pieces were incubated with trypsin at 37 °C for 12–16 h. Peptides were extracted from the gel pieces using a buffer containing 50% ACN and 0.1% formic acid at 37 °C for 30 min. The extraction was repeated twice, and the combined supernatant was concentrated with a vacuum concentrator. The peptides were reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid and transferred to mass spectrometry vials for analysis.

Mass spectrometry analysis: peptides were desalted using C18 columns and analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) on a high-resolution mass spectrometer. Raw data were processed using appropriate software (MaxQuant) with peptide identification performed against a reference protein database (UniProt).

Bioinformatics analysis: identified proteins were filtered for high-confidence interactions based on false discovery rate thresholds (e.g. <1%) and quantified by label-free or labelled quantification methods.

ChIP-qPCR assays

ChIP assays were performed using BeyoChIP ChIP Assay Kits (Beyotime, P2080S) following the manufacturer’s protocols. PHs were cultured in 10 cm cell culture dish, and the amount of cell culture medium was 10 ml. Add a proper amount of formaldehyde, mix gently until the final concentration is 1%, then incubate at 37 °C for 10 min. The complexes were rotationally incubated with 30 μL ChIP-Grade Protein G Magnetic Beads for 2 h at 4 °C and then washed three times with wash buffer. Purified DNA was analyzed by qPCR with SYBR Green Master Mix. The relative enrichment values were calculated through normalisation of the results to the input values and are expressed relative to the values obtained with normal IgG. Molecular docking

To analyze the binding affinities and modes of interaction between the O-LysoPE and Pebp1, AutodockVina 1.2.2, a silico protein–ligand docking software, was employed. The molecular structure of O-LysoPE was retrieved from PubChem Compound (PubChem CID: 9547071). The 3D coordinates of Pebp1 (PDB ID, 6ENS; resolution, 1.30 Å) were downloaded from the PDB. For docking analysis, all protein and molecular files were converted into PDBQT format with all water molecules excluded and polar hydrogen atoms were added. The grid box was centred to cover the domain of each protein and to accommodate free molecular movement. The grid box was set to 30 Å × 30 Å × 30 Å, and the grid point distance was 0.05 nm. Molecular docking studies were performed by Autodock Vina 1.2.2 (http://autodock.scripps.edu/).

Immunofluorescence

Pre-treatment of cell samples: discard the medium and slowly add PBS to the cells for cleaning; then the cells were covered with 4% neutral formaldehyde fixative for 15 min. After removing the fixative, rinse with PBS buffer pre-cooled at 4 °C.

Pre-treatment of tissue section samples: the slices were roasted in the oven at 60 °C, and then the slices were taken out and soaked in xylene for 15 min. Then the slices were transferred to new xylene and soaked for 15 min. The slices were then hydrated. The slices were soaked in 100% ethanol, 95% ethanol, 90% ethanol, 70% ethanol and 50% alcohol for 2 min each time. Finally, the slices are lifted out and placed in distilled water. Remove the hydrated slices, soak them in preheated citric acid antigen repair solution, heat them in 100 °C for 30 min, remove them from the water bath, and let them cool to room temperature.

Dyeing procedure: the sample should be completely covered with 5% goat serum, and the sections should be placed in a wet box. The cell orifice plate can be directly sealed and placed at room temperature for 1 h; remove the sealing solution, directly add the primary antibody working solution prepared by PBS buffer onto the sample, the sample should be completely covered, the sections should be placed in a wet box, and the cell hole plate can be directly sealed and incubated at 4 °C overnight; The sample was placed at room temperature, rewarmed for 15 min, the antibody working liquid was removed, and washed with buffer PBS. The fluorescent secondary antibody working liquid corresponding to the primary antibody species was added to the sample. The sample should be completely covered, protected from light, and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Remove the secondary antibody working liquid and wash with buffered PBS. Add 1 µg/mL DAPI working liquid to the sample, avoid light, at room temperature, and incubate for 30 min. Remove the DAPI working liquid and wash with PBS. The cell pore plate can be directly added with anti-fluorescence attenuating tablet, and the image can be observed and collected under a fluorescence microscope. Tissue section drops were added with anti-fluorescence attenuation sealant, and then covered with a glass slide. The images were observed and collected under a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, LSM980). Metabolomics analysis

All cecal content and liver samples were collected and immediately stored at −80 °C. Metabolomic detection and analysis of the samples were conducted by Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The samples were first homogenised and centrifuged, after which the supernatants were collected and analyzed by an LC-MC system. The original data file acquisited by LC-MS was converted into mzML format by ProteoWizard software. Peak extraction, peak alignment and retention time correction were respectively performed by the XCMS program. The 'SVR' method was executed to correct the peak area. The peaks with a detection rate lower than 50 % in each group of samples were discarded. After that, metabolic identification information was obtained by searching the laboratory’s self-built database, integrated public database, AI database and metDNA.

The orthogonal projections to latent structures-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) method was used to obtain variable importance in projection (VIP) using R package MetaboAnalystR. We applied univariate analysis (t-test) to calculate the p-value. The metabolites with VIP ≥ 1 and p ≤ 0.05 and absolute Log2FC (|Log2FC| ≥ 1.0) were considered to be differential metabolites. Identified metabolites were annotated using the KEGG Compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/ compound/), and annotated metabolites were then mapped to the KEGG Pathway database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/ pathway.html). Metabolic pathways were considered statistically significant enrichment when the hypergeometric test’s p-value of the metabolic pathway ≤ 0.05. Heatmap was implemented to visualised differential metabolites by the pheatmap package of R (version 4.1.0).

16S rRNA sequencing

Cecal content samples were collected into 1.5 mL sterile cryogenic tubes and stored at −80 °C. The Total genomic DNA was extracted using the DNA Extraction Kit (MagPure Soil DNA LQ Kit, Magen, D6356-02) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The genome DNA was used as a template for qPCR amplification with the barcoded primers. For bacterial diversity analysis, V3-V4 variable regions of 16S rRNA genes was amplified with universal primers 343F and 798R. The library sequencing and data processing were conducted by OE biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). To evaluate differences in species diversity complexity, alpha and beta diversity were assessed using the R package of MicrobiotaProcess. The LEfSe analysis was employed to assess the differentially abundant taxa. Functional predictions of 16S rRNA gene sequences were performed using PICRUSt2 with the KEGG pathway database. Spearman correlation analysis between predicted KEGG pathway abundances and OTUs was conducted using the psych R package, with a correlation heatmap generated by R software.

High-throughput RNA sequencing

To identify candidate genes associated with FMT-driven improvements in AIH of the liver, liver samples were collected and immediately stored at −80 °C. Total RNA was isolated, and library construction and sequencing were conducted by APTBIO Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Raw reads were filtered using fastp and aligned to the mouse genome (GRCm38/mm10) with Hisat2. A count matrix was generated via featureCounts and analyzed for differential gene expression using the DESeq2 pipeline in R, with a significance threshold of P ≤ 0.05 and |Log2 (Fold change)| ≥ 2. KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted, considering pathways significantly enriched at P ≤ 0.05.

Statistics and reproducibility

All in vivo experiments were independently repeated at least three times with similar results. Representative immunoblots, immunofluorescence images, histological staining and microscopy images shown in the figures were reproduced in at least three biologically independent experiments with comparable results. For animal studies, 'n' represents biologically independent animals. For cell-based assays, experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Technical replicates within a single experiment are described in the corresponding figure legends where applicable. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups whenever possible. Investigators were not blinded during data acquisition and analysis unless otherwise stated.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed by using GraphPad Prism 8. All the results are expressed as the means ± standard deviations. The time series data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc correction. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Unless otherwise indicated, statistical analysis was performed using Student’s t test when only two groups were compared. Survival data were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method to estimate cumulative survival rates, and the log-rank test was used to compare survival curves between groups.
