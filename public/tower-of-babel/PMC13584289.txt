Ethical issues in caring for older persons in Sub-Saharan Africa: a scoping review
Authors: Abdulgafar Lekan Olawumi, Zainab Abdulkadir, Abdullahi Kabir Suleiman, Zainab Abdulazeez Umar, Bukar Alhaji Grema, Godpower Chinedu Michael, Abiso Abubakar Mohammed, Umar Faruk Abdullahi, Mahmud Baba Mahmud, Abdulrauf Segun Ibraheem, Amina Danladi Muhammad, Abba Badamasi, Ibrahim Danjummai Gezawa, Muktar Hassan Aliyu, Muhammad Musa Borodo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRapid population ageing in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is unfolding within fragile health systems, weak social protection, and limited geriatric and palliative services, creating ethical challenges in older adult care. This study critically reviews current literature and policy reports, synthesizes key themes, and proposes evidence-based recommendations for policy, practice, and research.MethodsThis scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology and reported following PRISMA-ScR guidelines. A comprehensive search of biomedical, ethics, social science, and grey literature databases was undertaken to identify studies published between 2000 and 2025 on ethical issues in the care of older adults in Sub-Saharan Africa. Eligible English-language sources were screened by predefined inclusion criteria, and data were charted and synthesized thematically to map key ethical issues, concepts, and evidence gaps.ResultsWe identified multiple ethical challenges that affect older adult care in SSA, including justice, equity, autonomy, dignity, vulnerability, abuse, and ageism. Healthcare-related concerns involve unmet needs, resource allocation dilemmas, limited palliative and end-of-life care, and age-biased clinical decisions. Socially, weakening family support systems create moral tensions for caregivers. Policy analyses emphasise rights-based, culturally sensitive, and equitable approaches, highlighting the need for integrated ethical, social, and systemic care strategies.ConclusionsAddressing ethical issues in older care in SSA requires legally enshrined protections, health-system investments, public campaigns to counter ageism, supportive decision-making frameworks that respect cultural contexts and individual rights and strengthened research and surveillance. A right-based, culturally sensitive approach focused on the voices of older persons is essential.RegistrationThe protocol for this scoping review was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) and is available at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/9AXVW.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12910-026-01539-8.Keywords: Ethics, Elder abuse, Older persons care, Resource allocation, Sub-Saharan Africa

Introduction

Population ageing is a global phenomenon with important implications for health systems, social structures, and ethical practice [1, 2]. In Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the proportion of older adults is increasing, albeit at a slower pace than in high-income countries, due to improvements in life expectancy and declining fertility rates [2, 3]. While the absolute number of older persons in SSA remains lower than in Europe or North America, their health and social care needs are growing, often in contexts of fragile healthcare systems, limited geriatric expertise, and constrained resources [1–4].

Ethical challenges in the care of older adults in SSA emerge as healthcare providers, families, and policymakers navigate issues related to access, quality, and fairness [4]. These ethical considerations are multifaceted. Older adults often face chronic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, and dementia, which require long-term patient-centered care [5]. Yet, health systems in SSA are often oriented toward acute care and maternal-child health, leaving older persons underserved. Social norms [1, 6], cultural expectations, and family dynamics further complicate the provision of ethical care. For example, autonomy and informed consent, foundational principles in Western bioethics, may conflict with communal decision-making practices prevalent in SSA [7, 8]. The ethical tension arises between respecting individual rights and accommodating cultural norms, requiring clinicians and policymakers to navigate delicate moral terrain [9]. In addition, despite growing scholarly attention to ageing in SSA, ethical issues remain dispersed across disciplines and inadequately synthesized [10, 11]. Existing reviews have largely focused on epidemiology, service delivery, or social support, with limited critical engagement with ethical dimensions [10–14]. A scoping review with critical analysis is therefore warranted to systematically map the available evidence, examine how ethical issues are framed, and identify gaps to inform research, policy, and practice [15, 16].

The objectives of this scoping review are to map the range of ethical issues related to the care of older persons in SSA, examine how ethical principles such as autonomy, justice, beneficence, and respect for dignity are applied in different care contexts, and critically analyse the influence of cultural, social, legal, and health-system factors on ethical decision-making. The review will also identify evidence gaps and propose priorities for ethical research, policy, and practice in aging care.

Materials and methods

Ethical frameworks relevant to geriatric care in SSA

Understanding ethical issues in older care requires grounding in bioethical theory. Several frameworks are particularly relevant:

Principlism

It is based on the four pillars of autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice, which provides a practical approach to ethical decision-making in healthcare [17].

Autonomy

Respecting older adults’ choices in healthcare decisions, including treatment refusal or advance directives, can conflict with familial authority in SSA contexts [7].

Beneficence and non-maleficence

Clinicians must balance interventions that promote well-being while minimizing harm, particularly when resource constraints limit available treatments [8, 9].

Justice

Ethical allocation of scarce resources, such as medications, hospital beds, or specialized geriatric care, requires transparent and culturally sensitive policies [9].

Virtue ethics

Emphasizing character traits and moral dispositions, virtue ethics highlights the clinician’s responsibility to act with compassion, empathy, and integrity [18]. This approach is particularly useful in SSA, where formal protocols may be limited, and ethical practice relies heavily on professional judgment and relational care.

Care ethics

Care ethics prioritizes relationships, interdependence, and responsiveness to the needs of vulnerable individuals [10]. Given the centrality of family and community in older care in SSA, care ethics provides a culturally resonant framework that complements principlism while addressing contextual realities [10, 11].

Public health ethics

Ethical frameworks in public health emphasize population-level considerations, resource allocation, and equity [9, 10]. In SSA, public health ethics is critical when addressing systemic challenges affecting older adults, such as access to vaccinations, chronic disease management, and social protection [8, 9].

Registration

The protocol for this scoping review was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) and is available at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/9AXVW.

Scope and approach

We employed a systematic and transparent search strategy across multiple databases to identify relevant literature according to the PRISMA-ScR guidelines [19, 20].

Search strategy

We performed a comprehensive literature search to identify relevant studies addressing ethical issues in caring for older persons in SSA. The review focused on older adults as the population of interest, ethical and moral concerns as the core concept, and healthcare and social care settings within SSA (East, West, Central, and Southern Africa) as the context. (Fig. 1) The primary question guiding the search was: What ethical issues arise in the care of older persons in SSA, and how are these issues addressed within healthcare and social care systems?

Sub-Saharan Africa in green shadings [1]

Electronic databases searched included PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science to capture biomedical and multidisciplinary literature. Given the regional focus, African-specific databases such as African Journals Online (AJOL), and African Index Medicus were also searched. We identified gray literature through targeted searches of reports and policy documents from the World Health Organization (WHO), United Nations agencies, HelpAge International, non-governmental organizations and relevant government sources.

In PubMed, the search strategy combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms to identify relevant literature on older adults. The first concept included terms such as “older adults,” “elderly,” “ageing,” “aging,” “older persons,” “older people,” “geriatric,” and “seniors,” as well as the MeSH term “aged.” The second concept focused on ethics-related issues and included the MeSH term “ethics,” along with keywords such as “ethic,” “bioethics,” “ethical issues,” “ethical challenges,” “ethical dilemmas,” and “ethical concerns.” It also incorporated related concepts including autonomy, dignity, consent, confidentiality, privacy, justice, beneficence, non-maleficence, ageism, neglect, abuse, discrimination, end-of-life care, and decision-making. The third step combined the first and second concepts using the Boolean operator AND to retrieve studies addressing both older adults and ethical issues. The fourth concept added a geographical filter to identify studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa, using terms such as “Africa South of the Sahara,” “sub-Saharan Africa,” “SSA,” “Africa,” and variations of “sub-Saharan African.” The fifth step combined the previous results with the sub-Saharan Africa filter using AND to limit the search to the target region. Finally, the search was restricted to studies involving humans, published in English, and within the publication years 2000 to 2025.

Detailed search strategies for all databases and grey literature sources are provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S6.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies included empirical qualitative and quantitative research, systematic and narrative reviews, ethical analyses, and policy papers that explicitly addressed ethical issues in the care of older adults in SSA. Studies were excluded if they focused exclusively on regions outside SSA, did not address ethical or moral dimensions of care, did not focus on older persons, or consisted solely of editorials without substantive ethical analysis. The search commenced in October 2025 to December 2025.

Study selection, data extraction and charting

Rayyan software was used for reference management, duplicate detection, and screening support. All duplicates identified by the software were manually verified and removed by the review team. No studies were automatically excluded; all eligibility decisions were made by reviewers based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Title/abstract and full-text screening were independently conducted by two reviewers (ALO and ZA). Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and where consensus was not reached, a third reviewer (ZAU) adjudicated. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented. Systematic reviews and other review articles were identified during full-text screening and handled separately to avoid duplication of primary evidence. These review types were not included as primary sources but were used for backward citation tracking to identify additional eligible primary studies. Where overlap occurred between reviews and primary studies, only the original primary studies were included in the synthesis. When both a review and its included primary study were retrieved, the primary study was prioritized for data extraction, while the review was retained for contextual reference only.

Data charting was performed using a standardized form developed in line with Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidance and study objectives. The form was pilot-tested on a subset of included studies by the two reviewers independently (AKS and AAM). Following comparison of extracted data, inconsistencies were discussed and the form was refined for clarity and completeness. For full charting, two reviewers independently extracted data from all included studies (ALO and UFA). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with arbitration by a third reviewer (MBM), when necessary. Extracted variables included author, year, country, study design, setting, population characteristics, and ethical issues in older persons’ care. Where data were missing or unclear, corresponding authors were contacted for clarification.

Synthesis of the results

A narrative synthesis approach was used to summarize the extracted evidence, as all included studies reported qualitative outcomes. Key findings were systematically identified, coded, and organized into meaningful categories to facilitate interpretation across studies. This process involved repeatedly reviewing the extracted data to ensure consistency and depth in capturing relevant concepts. An inductive thematic synthesis was then applied to the data. Similar concepts were grouped to form overarching themes reflecting ethical issues in the care of older persons, including autonomy, informed consent, dignity, confidentiality, access to care, equity, cultural considerations, and end-of-life decision-making. These themes were refined iteratively through continuous comparison of findings across studies. The synthesized findings were then presented in a structured narrative format, highlighting similarities, differences, and recurring patterns across contexts and study settings. This approach allowed for an integrated interpretation of the evidence without statistical pooling, providing a comprehensive thematic understanding of ethical issues in older persons’ care in SSA.

Result

As shown in Fig. 2, a total of 80 studies met the inclusion criteria for this scoping review, published between 2001 and 2025. The included studies varied in geographical scope and were classified as country-specific, subregional, continental, or international. Among the country-specific studies, Nigeria contributed the largest proportion of evidence (n = 18, 22.5%), followed by Ghana (n = 11, 13.75%), South Africa (n = 7, 8.75%), Uganda (n = 5, 6.25%), and Ethiopia (n = 4, 5.0%). Malawi, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Senegal, and Eswatini each contributed one study (1.25%). At the broader geographical level, 15 studies (18.75%) focused on SSA as a whole, while one study (1.25%) examined East Africa. Four studies (5.0%) addressed ageing ethics from a continental African perspective. In addition, 11 studies (13.75%) were classified as “Global” because they comprised global policy documents, ethics frameworks, conceptual papers, or review articles with worldwide applicability rather than being conducted within a specific country or region. Overall, the evidence base was dominated by studies from Nigeria and Ghana, with comparatively fewer contributions from other African countries.

The PRISMA-ScR flow diagram illustrates the study selection process in the scoping review, showing how studies were identified, screened, and assessed for inclusion or exclusion

The body of evidence comprised predominantly reviews, qualitative studies, cross-sectional studies, and policy reports focusing on older adults across SSA. Collectively, the findings reveal a wide range of ethical and moral concerns in the care of older adults, with recurrent themes of justice, equity, autonomy through consent and decision making, dignity, vulnerability, ageism, intergenerational responsibility, and culturally grounded ethical frameworks shaping care experiences.

Within healthcare contexts, we found that service delivery for older adults in Nigeria and across SSA face multiple ethical challenges, particularly in decision-making, consent, palliative and end-of-life care (Table 1). In addition, culturally mediated decision making, limited palliative infrastructure, and inadequate professional training frequently undermine ethically-sound care. Qualitative studies from Nigeria by Doobay-Persaud et al. (2023) and Udeh et al. (2025), and a systematic review by Gysels et al. (2011), showed that communication barriers, family-dominated decisions, and poor symptom control often compromise autonomy and dignity at the end of life [21–23]. Powell et al. (2014) further highlighted the absence of comprehensive palliative care policies, reinforcing systemic inequities in access to compassionate care [24]. Hospital- and clinic-based studies reveal broader ethical vulnerabilities. Olawumi et al. (2021) documented high morbidity and poor nutritional status among hospitalized older adults in northern Nigeria, raising concerns about neglect and distributive justice [5]. Odusanya et al. (2018) and Olanrewaju et al. (2020) identified ageism, financial barriers, and fragmented services as persistent constraints to equitable access [25, 26]. Knight et al. (2018) further demonstrated how older adults with HIV and non-communicable diseases (NCDs) navigate poorly coordinated services, highlighting failures in continuity and equity [27]. Ethical challenges in autonomy and informed decision-making are evident in consent, disclosure, and treatment prioritization. Ede et al. and Mussie et al. (2024) showed that cultural norms privileging family authority and clinician discretion limit individual autonomy, reflecting relational ethics, but risking marginalization [28, 29]. A report from Ethiopia by Tegegn et al. 2018 found that older patients value involvement in deprescribing, suggestive of tensions between clinical judgement, autonomy, and resource constraints [30]. In Uganda, Alupo et al. (2025) highlighted systemic and cultural barriers to anticipatory care based on low uptake of advance directives [31]. Access and stigma also remained critical concerns: Jolley et al. (2025) showed older adults with disabilities in Uganda navigate structural barriers to eye care [32], while Wandera et al. (2025) identified stigma as an ethical barrier in the HIV care cascade [33]. Together, these findings demonstrate that ethical challenges in healthcare delivery for older adults in SSA extend beyond clinical encounters, and reflect deeply embedded structural, cultural, and policy-level constraints.

In social care contexts across SSA, as shown in Table 2, ethical challenges for older adults arise amid changing family structures, migration, poverty, and weakening informal support systems [14]. Evidence from Ghana, Malawi, and other SSA settings indicate that family-based care, traditionally central to elder support, is increasingly strained Narrative reviews from Ghana by Agyemang (2025) and Dovie (2019), and studies in Democratic Republic of Congo, by Adei et al. (2025), and Bikouta et al. (2015), document strong moral expectations on families and informal caregivers alongside economic hardship, urbanisation, and inter-generational change, resulting in neglect, reduced autonomy, and lower quality of care [7, 34–36]. Hospital-based and community studies in Malawi [37], and South Africa [38, 39], highlight risks of malnutrition, abuse, and caregiver burden, reflecting both household- and system-level vulnerabilities [37–39]. Regional and cross-national analyses, including works by Okolie et al. (2025), Aboderin (2025), HelpAge International (2011), Adamek et al. (2022), and Gedfew et al. (2024), identify gaps in social protection, prevalence of elder abuse, and limited institutional safeguards, situating ethical concerns within broader social and policy environments [4, 40–43].

Social care ethics: family responsibility, elder abuse, neglect, and vulnerability

On the other hand, Jecker (2022) applied an inter-generational ethical perspective, framing moral obligations toward older adults within multi-generational and skipped-generation households, despite structural constraints [66]. Older adults’ lived experiences further illuminate these ethical challenges: qualitative studies from Uganda [32], Nigeria [49], and Ethiopia [67], reported perceptions of vulnerability, loss of autonomy, and culturally incongruent care. Ethics-of-care approaches, emphasizing relational responsibility, dignity, and context-sensitive care, are also described in studies from South Africa [44], and Ghana [45]. Collectively, the published evidence from Nigeria, Uganda, Ethiopia, Ghana, Malawi, and South Africa consistently link elder abuse and neglect to social inequality, caregiver stress, migration, food insecurity, and limited regulatory oversight [46–48, 50–65].

Global and regional policy frameworks

Global and regional policy instruments, including the WHO Global Strategy on Ageing and Health (2017), the WHO AFRO Framework for Healthy Ageing (2022), and the United Nations World Population Ageing Report (2023), promote rights-based, equitable, and context-sensitive approaches to ageing [2, 73, 74]. These frameworks prioritize dignity, autonomy, and equity while advocating integration of health and social care systems. However, evidence from Ghana, Senegal, Nigeria, and Eswatini revealed implementation gaps, with older adults reporting unmet basic needs, financial hardship, and unequal utilisation of healthcare services, despite policy commitments to universal health coverage [69–71, 76, 77].

Ethics of research, policy, and health systems

Expert reports and reviews by the National Research Council (NRC, 2006) [78], Prince et al. (2015) [79], Aboderin (2010; 2025) [3, 4], Brock and Wikler (2006) [9], and Childress et al. (2002) [10], emphasized justice, distributive fairness, and ethical resource allocation in ageing societies. These ethical concerns are reflected in empirical studies showing socioeconomic inequalities, frailty-related vulnerability, and inequitable service provision among older adults in Ghana, Rwanda, Kenya, and Nigeria [69, 72, 82, 83, 90]. Studies further documented structural barriers such as fragmented primary care, inadequate workforce training, and low preventive service uptake, including vaccination among caregivers and healthcare workers [77, 93].

Human rights, bioethics, and contextual moral frameworks

Comparative rights analyses by Doron et al. (2019) introduce the International Older Persons’ Human Rights Index as a tool for ethical monitoring of elder care systems [92]. The role of inter-generational obligations, family responsibility, and moral duties toward older adults are highlighted by conceptual and case-based work that advance culturally grounded African bioethics [13, 85]. These perspectives align with findings on ageism, workplace discrimination, and the moral consequences of poverty and multimorbidity in later life [68, 82]. Foundational biomedical ethics texts by Beauchamp et al. (2019) and Brock and Wikler (2006) also stress the core principles of autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice, that underpin ethical decision-making in geriatric practice across SSA [9, 17].

Discussion

This scoping review demonstrates that ethical challenges in the care of older adults in SSA are complex, interrelated, and deeply embedded in clinical, social, cultural, and structural contexts. Across the literature, five recurring ethical domains emerge: informed consent and decision-making capacity; end-of-life and palliative care; resource allocation and justice; elder abuse, neglect, and vulnerability; and cultural considerations under ethical pluralism. Together, these findings reveal systemic gaps that compromise autonomy, dignity, and equity for ageing populations in the region.

Autonomy, family decision-making, and ethical tensions in older adult care

A recurring theme across the reviewed studies was the central role of family members in healthcare decision-making for older adults in SSA. While this practice is often rooted in cultural norms that emphasize interdependence, collective responsibility, and respect for family authority, it raises important ethical questions regarding autonomy and informed consent. The predominance of family-centred decision-making should not be interpreted solely as a limitation of individual choice; rather, it reflects a relational understanding of personhood in which decisions are made within the context of family and community relationships. From a bioethical perspective, this finding highlights the tension between individual autonomy and relational autonomy. Conventional bioethics places strong emphasis on the individual’s right to make informed decisions about their own healthcare [9, 13, 17, 47, 72, 94]. In contrast, relational autonomy recognizes that individuals make choices within social networks and that family involvement may support rather than undermine autonomous decision-making. In many SSA settings, older adults may actively prefer family participation in healthcare decisions, viewing such involvement as an expression of care, solidarity, and respect rather than a restriction of their autonomy [13, 47, 72].

Nevertheless, family involvement can become ethically problematic when the wishes of family members override the expressed preferences of older adults or when assumptions are made that older persons lack decision-making capacity solely because of age, illness, or dependency. Such practices risk undermining respect for persons and may contribute to paternalistic forms of care [25, 88, 95]. The ethical challenge for healthcare professionals is therefore to distinguish between supportive family engagement and situations in which family influence compromises the older person’s ability to participate meaningfully in decisions affecting their health and wellbeing [7, 29, 66].

“Virtue ethics” demand that the clinicians working in culturally diverse contexts should strive to balance respect for cultural values with the ethical obligation to protect the rights and preferences of older adults. Shared decision-making approaches may provide a useful framework by enabling healthcare providers to involve families in discussions while ensuring that older persons are given opportunities to express their own values, preferences, and goals of care [47, 96, 97]. Where conflicts arise between family wishes and the preferences of competent older adults, healthcare professionals should prioritize the older person’s informed choices while facilitating respectful dialogue among all parties involved [88, 95].

These findings suggest that ethical frameworks for the care of older adults in SSA should extend beyond the exclusive application of Western individualistic conceptions of autonomy. Instead, they should incorporate culturally grounded notions of relational autonomy, which recognize the influential role of family and community in healthcare decision-making, while ensuring adequate safeguards against coercion, neglect, and the exclusion of older adults from decisions affecting their care [73, 91]. Given the central role of family and community in the care of older persons across SSA, “care ethics” offers a culturally relevant framework that complements principlism by emphasizing relationships, interdependence, and contextual realities in ethical decision-making [10, 11].

End-of-life care and palliative ethics

End-of-life care in SSA is characterized by substantial ethical challenges arising from weak health system capacity, including limited availability of palliative care services, poor access to essential analgesics, and shortages of trained healthcare professionals [22, 24]. These structural constraints often result in inadequate symptom control, delayed initiation of palliative care, and unmet physical and psychosocial needs among older adults. Consequently, healthcare providers may struggle to fulfil the ethical obligations of beneficence and non-maleficence, particularly when suffering cannot be adequately relieved because of resource limitations [21]. Beyond these structural barriers, end-of-life care presents important ethical tensions regarding decision-making, treatment goals, and the meaning of a good death. Clinicians are frequently required to determine whether continued life-prolonging interventions remain in the patient’s best interests or whether care should shift toward comfort-oriented approaches [98–100]. While beneficence may support interventions intended to prolong life, non-maleficence requires consideration of whether such treatments may impose additional suffering, diminish quality of life, or provide limited clinical benefit. Determining the appropriate balance between these ethical principles is particularly challenging in settings where prognostic uncertainty is common and institutional guidance is limited.

Evidence from Nigeria and other SSA settings suggests that clinicians’ assessments of appropriate end-of-life care may differ from the expectations of families, who often favour maximal treatment because of cultural beliefs surrounding hope, filial obligation, religious values, and the social significance of death [24, 101]. Although family involvement can provide important emotional and practical support, ethical concerns arise when family preferences overshadow the values and wishes of older adults themselves. This raises questions about whose interests should guide decision-making when the goals of clinicians, patients, and families diverge. In such circumstances, respect for persons requires that older adults, where capable, be given meaningful opportunities to express their preferences regarding treatment, symptom management, and end-of-life care.

The ethical significance of these issues extends beyond decisions about treatment withdrawal or continuation. Cultural norms emphasizing collective responsibility and family-centred decision-making may support relational forms of autonomy, yet they may also unintentionally marginalize the voice of the older person, particularly when advance care preferences have not been discussed [13, 36]. The limited uptake of advance care planning and the absence of clear legal and policy frameworks for surrogate decision-making in many SSA countries further increase the risk of ethically contested decisions [80, 88]. As a result, clinicians are often required to navigate complex situations without adequate institutional, ethical, or legal support.

The concept of a dignified death also warrants careful consideration. While dignity is frequently invoked in discussions of end-of-life care, understandings of what constitutes dignity may differ among patients, families, clinicians, and communities. For some individuals, dignity may involve comfort, symptom relief, and the avoidance of burdensome interventions, whereas for others it may be linked to prolonging life, maintaining spiritual practices, or fulfilling family obligations. Ethically sound end-of-life care therefore requires ongoing dialogue that acknowledges these differing perspectives while ensuring that the values and wellbeing of older adults remain central to decision-making.

Guidance from the WHO emphasizes the integration of palliative care into primary healthcare systems and promotes rights-based approaches that prioritize dignity, symptom relief, and culturally responsive family engagement [73, 74]. The findings of this review suggest that ethically grounded end-of-life care models in SSA should balance respect for cultural values with the protection of older adults’ rights, preferences, and wellbeing. Such approaches may help reconcile clinical judgment, family involvement, and the ethical imperative to support older persons in achieving a dignified and person-centred end-of-life experience.

Resource allocation and justice

Resource scarcity is a defining feature of many health systems in SSA and presents complex ethical challenges regarding the fair distribution of healthcare resources. Evidence from Nigeria and other SSA countries indicates that older adults frequently encounter barriers to accessing healthcare services, essential medicines, specialized geriatric care, and social support systems [72, 75]. These barriers disproportionately affect individuals living with multimorbidity, frailty, disability, and chronic illness, thereby increasing vulnerability among a population that often has substantial healthcare needs [5]. While such disparities are frequently attributed to limited resources, they also raise fundamental questions of distributive justice concerning how scarce healthcare resources ought to be allocated and whose needs should receive priority.

A central ethical tension concerns whether older adults should receive lower priority in resource-constrained health systems where maternal and child health, infectious diseases, and other pressing public health challenges compete for limited funding. From a utilitarian perspective, priority-setting may favour interventions that maximize overall population health gains, potentially directing resources toward younger populations with longer projected life expectancy [8–10]. However, such approaches risk systematically disadvantaging older adults and may inadvertently reinforce structural ageism by equating the value of healthcare interventions with age-based assumptions about social worth or future productivity. Ethical frameworks grounded in equity and human rights challenge this reasoning, arguing that all individuals possess equal moral worth regardless of age and that healthcare allocation should not discriminate solely on the basis of chronological age [7, 8].

The literature reviewed suggests that ageism may influence healthcare access through both explicit and implicit mechanisms, including exclusion from treatment opportunities, reduced referral to specialist services, and assumptions that health interventions for older adults are less beneficial or less deserving of investment [25, 26, 81]. Such practices raise important ethical concerns because they may transform resource scarcity into a justification for discrimination rather than a rationale for fair and transparent prioritization. An ethically defensible allocation framework should therefore distinguish between clinical factors that legitimately influence treatment decisions, such as prognosis and potential benefit, and age-based biases that unfairly limit access to care.

The ethical challenge is further complicated by the high prevalence of chronic diseases, disability, and long-term care needs among older adults. In settings where resources are insufficient to meet all demands, healthcare providers and policymakers must balance competing obligations to maximize benefits, reduce suffering, and protect vulnerable populations. Prioritarian approaches to distributive justice suggest that greater moral weight should be given to those who are worst off, including frail older adults who experience significant health and social disadvantages [7, 10]. From this perspective, vulnerability may justify increased attention and support rather than reduced priority. Conversely, approaches that focus exclusively on efficiency may overlook the cumulative disadvantages and unmet needs experienced by ageing populations.

These tensions are particularly evident in palliative and end-of-life care, where limited infrastructure, shortages of trained personnel, and competing service priorities often necessitate difficult decisions regarding the allocation of medicines, hospital beds, and supportive services [24, 100]. Ethical decision-making in such contexts requires more than clinical judgment; it demands transparent processes that balance efficiency, equity, and respect for human dignity. Failure to provide adequate palliative care may not only reflect resource limitations but may also raise questions about whether the suffering of older adults is being accorded sufficient moral importance within health systems.

The findings also raise broader questions regarding the obligations of governments toward ageing populations. As demographic transitions accelerate across SSA, health systems that continue to prioritize maternal-child health and communicable diseases without corresponding investment in geriatric services risk perpetuating intergenerational inequities. While competing health priorities are inevitable, principles of justice require policymakers to ensure that older adults are not systematically excluded from health planning, financing, and service delivery. Governments have an ethical responsibility to progressively develop age-inclusive policies, strengthen social protection systems, and invest in geriatric and palliative care services that enable older adults to achieve equitable health outcomes. Ultimately, resource allocation in SSA is not merely a technical or economic issue but an ethical one. Public health ethics emphasize population-level considerations, resource allocation, and equity [10]. Decisions about who receives care, which services are prioritized, and how resources are distributed reflect societal values regarding fairness, dignity, and the moral status of older persons. Ethical approaches to priority-setting should therefore promote transparency, equity, and accountability while ensuring that efforts to maximize population health do not inadvertently reproduce structural ageism or neglect the needs of older adults. Integrating distributive justice principles into policy and practice will be essential for building health systems that respond fairly to the growing health and social care needs of ageing populations in SSA.

Elder abuse, neglect, and vulnerability

Elder abuse and neglect in SSA represent profound ethical challenges that extend beyond individual acts of harm to encompass broader questions of justice, human rights, and societal responsibility. Older adults are often exposed to multiple forms of vulnerability arising from poverty, chronic illness, functional dependence, cognitive impairment, social isolation, and weak social protection systems [3, 41, 75, 84, 95]. These vulnerabilities may increase reliance on family members and informal caregivers, thereby heightening the risk of neglect, exploitation, and abuse when caregiving resources are limited or support systems are inadequate [12, 66, 75, 95].

From an ethical perspective, elder abuse constitutes a violation of fundamental principles of respect for persons, dignity, non-maleficence, and justice. Physical, emotional, financial, and social forms of abuse not only inflict direct harm but also undermine the intrinsic worth and autonomy of older adults. Ethical concerns are particularly acute when abuse remains hidden within family settings, where cultural expectations of filial responsibility may discourage reporting or external intervention [66, 95]. In such circumstances, tensions may arise between respecting family privacy and fulfilling the moral obligation to protect vulnerable individuals from harm.

The literature suggests that vulnerability in later life is not solely a consequence of individual dependency but is also shaped by broader structural factors, including poverty, inadequate healthcare access, age discrimination, and social exclusion [3, 41, 75]. These conditions raise important questions of distributive justice, particularly regarding the extent to which societies and governments are obligated to allocate resources to protect older adults and address inequalities that contribute to abuse and neglect. A justice-oriented perspective recognizes that older persons often face cumulative disadvantages across the course of life and may therefore require additional social and institutional support to achieve equitable health and well-being outcomes. Ethical tensions may also emerge when families experiencing financial hardship are unable to meet the care needs of older relatives. While resource limitations may help explain some instances of neglect, they do not eliminate the ethical responsibility to safeguard older persons from preventable harm. This distinction is important because it shifts attention from assigning individual blame to examining the societal conditions that constrain caregiving capacity. Consequently, responses to elder abuse should address both immediate protection needs and the structural determinants that perpetuate vulnerability.

The findings of this review highlight the need for a multi-level ethical response that integrates healthcare services, legal protections, social welfare programmes, and community-based support systems. Healthcare professionals have an ethical duty to recognize and respond to signs of abuse, while policymakers and communities share responsibility for creating environments that promote safety, dignity, and social inclusion for older adults. Such an approach reflects a commitment to justice, human rights, and the moral obligation to protect vulnerable populations while respecting the dignity and agency of older persons.

Cultural considerations and ethical pluralism

Sub-Saharan Africa encompasses a rich tapestry of cultural norms, family structures, and belief systems, all of which profoundly influence ethical perspectives and practices in the care of older adults [3, 75, 84]. Across all domains, cultural norms profoundly shape ethical practice in SSA. Care for older adults is embedded within family and communal structures, where collective responsibility often supersedes individual autonomy. Ethical pluralism: recognising the legitimacy of multiple moral frameworks, emerges as essential for reconciling biomedical ethics with local traditions [4, 14, 66]. Rather than viewing communal decision-making as inherently unethical, the evidence supports approaches that integrate relational autonomy with safeguards against marginalization [21–24, 96]. Culturally competent care requires policies and practices that support families while ensuring that older adults’ rights, dignity, and wellbeing remain central [102, 103]. Community engagement, intergenerational dialogue, and locally adapted interventions are consistently identified as critical to ethically coherent ageing care in SSA.

Research implications

Ethical ageing in SSA is constrained by limited empirical evidence on older populations, cultural influences on consent, and health system capacity [52, 56, 72, 97]. Priority research areas include epidemiological studies to guide ethical resource allocation and service planning in ageing populations, and studies of multimorbidity, cognitive impairment, and functional decline [50, 56, 102]. Research is also needed to adapt and validate decision-making capacity assessment tools appropriate to SSA contexts, accounting for language, literacy, and cultural norms [7, 25, 84]. Comparative studies on ethical frameworks for resource allocation can inform transparent and culturally acceptable prioritization approaches in constrained systems [9]. Further work on community-based palliative care models and elder abuse prevention strategies is essential to strengthen ethical standards and protective mechanisms [41, 51–53].

Clinical practice implications

At the clinical level, ethical care requires patient-centered, culturally responsive approaches. Clinicians should adopt decision-making models that respect individual autonomy while acknowledging communal decision-making traditions [17]. Transparent triage processes, early integration of palliative care, and culturally competent communication with families and communities are critical to ensuring ethical, dignified care for older adults in resource-limited settings [9, 22].

Limitation

This scoping review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the review was restricted to studies published in English, which may have introduced language bias and resulted in the exclusion of relevant literature published in other languages. Second, the available evidence was unevenly distributed across Sub-Saharan Africa, with a substantial proportion of the included studies originating from Nigeria. While these studies provided important insights into ethical issues affecting older adults, they may not fully reflect the diversity of sociocultural, legal, economic, and healthcare contexts across the region. Consequently, the findings should not be assumed to be uniformly applicable to all SSA countries, and further research from underrepresented regions is needed to strengthen the regional evidence base. Third, the evidence base was characterized by considerable heterogeneity, comprising empirical studies, policy documents, commentaries, conceptual papers, and ethical analyses. Although the inclusion of diverse sources is consistent with scoping review methodology and enabled a broad mapping of the literature, it may have influenced the synthesis by combining evidence generated using different methodological approaches and levels of rigor. As a result, some findings reflect conceptual or policy perspectives rather than empirical observations. Fourth, much of the available literature was primarily descriptive in nature, focusing on documenting ethical challenges such as healthcare inequities, elder abuse, family involvement in decision-making, and resource constraints. Comparatively few studies engaged in detailed normative or philosophical analyses of these issues. This limited the extent to which the review could draw robust ethical conclusions or critically evaluate competing ethical frameworks and theories. The ethical interpretations presented in this review should therefore be viewed as informed syntheses of the available evidence rather than definitive normative judgments. Finally, as with all scoping reviews, the objective was to map the breadth and nature of the existing literature rather than to assess study quality, determine causal relationships, or estimate effect sizes. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as providing an overview of the current state of knowledge rather than conclusive evidence regarding ethical practices or outcomes.

Despite these limitations, this review provides a comprehensive overview of ethical issues in the care of older adults in Sub-Saharan Africa, identifies important knowledge gaps, and highlights priority areas for future empirical, policy, and bioethical research.

Conclusion

In this scoping review, we found that multifaceted ethical challenges shape the care of older adults in SSA. Key concerns include informed consent, end-of-life care, equitable resource allocation, protection from abuse, and the need for culturally grounded ethical pluralism. Addressing these issues requires integrated approaches that align policy frameworks, professional ethics, family engagement, and cultural competence across healthcare and social care settings. We also found that ethical ageing in SSA depends on bridging clinical ethics, public health ethics, and social justice perspectives. Strengthening ethical care will therefore require rights-based policies, culturally responsive models of care, and shared accountability among families, communities, health systems, and the state to protect the dignity, rights, and well-being of older adults.
