Hybrid arch frozen elephant trunk reconstruction with a novel fenestrated endograft
Authors: Brandon Loshusan, Jill J Gelinas, François Dagenais, Michael WA Chu
Published: 2026

Preop: anomalous LVA and deep LSA. Postop: Novel Fenestrated FET with patent LSA stent.

Central Message

Redo surgery for residual thoracic aortic dissection with a deep left subclavian artery and anomalous left vertebral artery requires detailed perioperative planning and new reconstructive strategies.

See Commentary on page XXX.

When considering reoperative aortic arch repair, a deeply positioned left subclavian artery (LSA) is challenging to reach and often associated with prolonged circulatory arrest, increased risks of bleeding, and injury to the recurrent and phrenic nerves.1 The presence of an anomalous left vertebral artery (LVA) only complicates matters further.2

Clinical Summary

The patient provided informed consent for publication of their study data; institutional review board approval was waived. A 62-year-old man who underwent ascending aorta and hemiarch reconstruction in 2015 for acute aortic dissection presented with residual chronic dissection and a dilated 65-mm thoracic aorta. Preoperative computed tomography showed an anomalous LVA and deep LSA. The LSA measured 70 mm from the patient's skin and 20 mm from the left carotid origin. Given this information and anatomy, a novel frozen elephant trunk (FET; Cook Hybrid FET-FEN) and LSA stent graft system were chosen as the reconstructive strategy (Figures 1 and 2).

Surgical Technique

At operation, dense mediastinal adhesions were lysed. Cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated via an 8-mm Dacron side graft anastomosed to the right carotid artery for antegrade cerebral perfusion and direct aortic cannulation for lower-body perfusion, with percutaneous femoral venous cannulation. During cooling to 28 °C, the innominate and carotid arteries were debranched, the LVA transposed to the left carotid (for bilateral antegrade cerebral perfusion), and the ascending aorta crossclamped, achieving diastolic arrest with ostial cardioplegia. The aortic valve was preserved. The ascending aorta and proximal arch were resected to the level of zone 1. A 30- × 36- × 100-mm hybrid Cook FET-FEN graft was advanced over a femorally inserted extra-stiff wire and deployed into the descending thoracic aorta; an off-the-shelf 16- × 39-mm BeGraft aortic bridging stent (Bentley) was deployed through the integrated 10-mm fenestration over a wire and inflated with a balloon to 13 atm to revascularize the LSA, leaving approximately 10 mm of the BeGraft flared into the lumen of the FET graft. A 28-mm Gelweave Ante-Flo graft (TERUMO Vascutek) was anastomosed to the arch graft and lower-body reperfusion was initiated. The proximal end was sewn to the sinotubular junction and the separate head vessel graft was anastomosed to the Ante-Flo graft to complete epi-aortic branch reconstruction. After reperfusion, the patient was weaned off cardiopulmonary bypass on low-dose inotropes with preserved biventricular function. Postoperative computed tomography, fluoroscopy, and transesophageal echocardiography confirmed a well-expanded FET with a widely patent LSA stent and a competent aortic valve without any endoleaks. The patient was discharged uneventfully on postoperative day 8. At 6 months, the patient had an intact hybrid arch reconstruction and widely patent reimplanted LVA and LSA stent (Video 1).

Discussion

Management of the difficult LSA remains one of the most technically challenging aspects of aortic arch reconstruction. When the vessel originates deep within the mediastinum or posterior to the trachea, direct reimplantation prolongs circulatory arrest and increases bleeding risk, particularly in redo settings with dense adhesions.1 Extra-anatomic strategies such as carotid-subclavian bypass or transposition can provide reliable revascularization but add an additional procedure with graft-related morbidity and cannot be easily used in emergency presentations.1 Optimal management of these pathologies continues to evolve, and patient selection remains central.3 In elective cases in which the patient is a suitable candidate—reasonable carotid diameter, absence of obesity, and complex anatomy—we prefer preoperative carotid-subclavian transposition or bypass. In urgent and emergent presentations, however, preoperative extra-anatomic revascularization is often not feasible.

Alternative strategies have been increasingly explored to reduce these problems. Fenestrated and branched endograft technologies permit maintenance of LSA perfusion while simplifying distal arch reconstruction and decreasing deep mediastinal dissection.4 In the present case, given the deep LSA, aneurysmal arch, and adhesions from redo surgery, deployment of a fenestrated hybrid FET device with bridging stent graft revascularization of the LSA allowed secure perfusion, thereby avoiding a hazardous in situ anastomosis, enabling a controlled zone 1 reconstruction in a reoperative field. Patient selection for this strategy also warrants caution; those with challenging LSA anatomy—aneurysmal, calcified, tortuous, or low vertebral take-off—are unlikely to be ideal candidates for LSA stenting and should be considered for alternative reconstructive strategies.

The anomalous origin of the LVA added further complexity but had a simple solution. Failure to recognize and revascularize a dominant or isolated LVA may result in posterior circulation stroke or spinal cord injury, particularly when the LSA is excluded or covered during arch repair.2 Vertebral-carotid transposition has been demonstrated as a safe and effective alternative during thoracic aortic and arch procedures, preserving vertebrobasilar flow while combining naturally into arch debranching strategies.2 In this case, LVA transposition was performed during cooling, allowing cerebral protection without prolonging ischemic times.

This report illustrates the significance of careful preoperative imaging to identify challenging epi-aortic branch anatomy and underscores the versatility of contemporary hybrid FET platforms. Among the FET grafts available in Canada—the Thoraflex Hybrid (Terumo Aortic); E-vita Open Neo (Artivion), and Cook Hybrid (Cook Medical)—this device offers greater radial force as the result of its Z-stent design, which may make it a preferable choice for landing across aneurysms with an adequate distal landing zone (Figure E1). The incorporated fenestration may also provide a more expeditious means of reconstructing the LSA in patients presenting with acute aortic syndromes. This novel fenestrated FET graft may be an important tool in the aortic arch repair toolbox to help manage anatomically complex reoperative arch pathology and facilitate in situ LSA reconstruction with ease.5 Key limitations of this strategy include reliance on advanced hybrid endovascular technology, a hybrid operating room, and operator expertise, which may limit generalizability. There is also a lack of long-term durability data for fenestrated FET with bridging stents. Risks of endoleak, branch instability, or the need for reintervention remain incompletely defined in this setting. The previously mentioned anatomic constraints may also narrow patient candidacy. Finally, this is a single-case experience, and comparative data against conventional FET with extra-anatomic LSA revascularization are needed to determine which approach offers a more durable solution.

Conclusions

This case illustrates an innovative approach to addressing a complex aortic arch dissection in a patient with deep LSA and an anomalous LVA, using a novel fenestrated FET system.
