Associations among stress, anxiety, and depression in postgraduate dental students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Bakhtiyar Islamzade, Ikbal Leblebicioglu Kurtulus, Ozden Melis Durmaz Yilmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostgraduate dental education is a demanding process characterized by increased clinical responsibility and academic expectations. While psychological distress among undergraduate dental students has been widely studied, evidence regarding postgraduate populations remains limited. This study aimed to assess levels of stress, anxiety, and depression among postgraduate dental students and to examine their associations with sociodemographic factors.MethodsA cross-sectional observational study was conducted among 192 postgraduate dental students. Data were collected using a demographic questionnaire and the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21). Subscale scores were multiplied by two for comparability with standard severity classifications. Statistical analyses included Cronbach’s alpha, Pearson correlation, and linear regression. Multiple regression models were used to assess associations between psychological variables and sociodemographic factors. Group comparisons were performed using t-tests and one-way ANOVA with Scheffé post hoc tests.ResultsSignificant positive associations were observed among stress, anxiety, and depression (p < .001). In multiple regression analysis, stress (β = 0.543, p < .001) and anxiety (β = 0.317, p < .001) were significantly associated with depression, accounting for a substantial proportion of the observed variance (R² = 0.696). Participants with “normal” perceived financial status tended to show higher distress levels than those with “good” status, although this association did not reach statistical significance in multivariable analysis. No significant differences were found for training year, specialty, residence, or smoking status. The majority of participants were classified within the normal range across all DASS-21 subscales.ConclusionsStress, anxiety, and depression are closely interrelated among postgraduate dental trainees. Psychological distress appears to be associated with individual and perceived contextual factors; however, these associations were not consistently supported in the multivariable model and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Background

Dental education is widely regarded as an academically and clinically demanding process that places substantial psychological pressure on students [1, 2]. While much of the existing literature has focused on undergraduate cohorts, postgraduate dental students constitute a distinct population characterized by increased clinical autonomy, direct responsibility for patient care, academic productivity requirements, and specialization-related career pressures [3, 4]. These combined demands may create a unique psychological burden that differs qualitatively from undergraduate education, potentially influencing both mental well-being and professional performance outcomes [1, 2].

Psychological distress in dental education, particularly stress, anxiety, and depression, has received increasing attention in recent years [1, 3]. Within the framework of stress appraisal theory, stress arises when individuals perceive environmental demands as exceeding their adaptive capacity [5]. Previous studies have reported associations between prolonged stress and anxiety and depressive symptoms, which may contribute to emotional exhaustion and reduced academic performance [6]. These psychological states frequently co-occur and may interact in a mutually reinforcing manner, reflecting shared emotional and cognitive vulnerability mechanisms [7, 8]. Previous research has consistently reported high correlations among these constructs, particularly in health sciences populations; however, these relationships may also be influenced by conceptual and measurement overlap inherent in multi-dimensional instruments such as the DASS-21, which should be carefully considered when interpreting these associations [9, 10]. Understanding these interrelationships is particularly important in postgraduate training environments, where cumulative psychological burden may directly influence clinical performance and patient care quality.

Previous studies have highlighted elevated levels of psychological distress in health professions education, particularly during periods of disruption such as the COVID-19 pandemic; however, empirical evidence specifically addressing postgraduate dental trainees remains limited, with most studies emphasizing undergraduate populations or isolated psychological constructs [9, 11–13]. Furthermore, the role of sociodemographic factors such as gender, age, and perceived financial status in shaping psychological distress among postgraduate students remains inconclusive, with findings varying across cultural and institutional contexts [12, 14–17]. Therefore, there remains a need for integrated, multivariable analyses that specifically address this population, who experience distinct clinical and academic demands.

The primary aim of this study was to assess stress, anxiety, and depression levels among postgraduate dental students using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) and to examine the associations among these variables and quantify their interrelationships using regression-based modeling. Furthermore, the study evaluated whether these outcomes differed according to sociodemographic characteristics. Based on these aims, the following hypotheses were tested:

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted among postgraduate dental students at Erciyes University Faculty of Dentistry during the 2024–2025 academic year. All postgraduate students enrolled in the institution during the study period were invited to participate via an online survey. Although all eligible students were invited, participation was voluntary, which may introduce potential selection bias. A total of 192 students completed the questionnaire, corresponding to a response rate of 90% (192/213 students).

Participants were aged between 24 and 40 years. Inclusion criteria were current enrollment in a postgraduate dental program and provision of informed consent. Exclusion criteria included current use of psychiatric medication, interruption or discontinuation of postgraduate education, and incomplete questionnaire responses.

To minimize the risk of duplicate responses, the survey platform was configured to restrict multiple submissions from the same device/IP address. The sample size was considered adequate to represent the postgraduate student population of the institution, as a large proportion of eligible students participated in the study.

Data collection and ethics

Ethical approval was obtained from the Erciyes University Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 96681246). Data were collected between October and December 2024 using an anonymous online survey platform.

Before accessing the questionnaire, participants were provided with detailed information about the study and were required to provide electronic informed consent. Participation was voluntary, and data confidentiality was ensured.

Survey instruments

A researcher-designed questionnaire was used to collect demographic data, including age, gender, marital status, year of training, specialty programs (e.g., oral and maxillofacial surgery, orthodontics, prosthodontics, etc.), place of residence, smoking status, and perceived financial status.

Psychological distress was assessed using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale–21 (DASS-21), a widely used instrument that allows simultaneous assessment of stress, anxiety, and depression within a single tool [18, 19]. The Turkish version of the scale has been validated by Sarıçam [18]. Compared to instruments such as PHQ-9 and GAD-7, DASS-21 allows simultaneous assessment of stress, anxiety, and depression within a single tool, which was particularly suitable for the aims of this study.

The scale consists of 21 items divided into three subscales. Responses are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“Did not apply to me at all”) to 3 (“Applied to me very much or most of the time”). Subscale scores were calculated by summing the relevant items and multiplying by two, in accordance with the original DASS manual to ensure comparability with the DASS-42 severity classifications [19]. Higher scores indicate greater symptom severity.

Severity classifications were applied according to established cutoff values, and the distribution of participants across severity categories is presented in Table 1. Previously reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the Turkish version were 0.85 for depression, 0.80 for anxiety, and 0.77 for stress.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and psychological scores. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. Normality was evaluated using skewness and kurtosis values, with values between ± 2 considered acceptable. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine associations among stress, anxiety, and depression scores. To further explore these relationships, linear regression analyses were conducted. Given the cross-sectional design, regression results were interpreted as measures of statistical association rather than causal relationships. In addition to simple regression models, multiple linear regression analyses were performed to assess the associations between sociodemographic variables (gender, age group, marital status, financial status, training year, residence, smoking status, and specialty) and psychological outcomes. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values, with VIF values below 5 considered acceptable. Group comparisons were conducted using independent-samples t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The Scheffé post hoc test was selected due to unequal group sizes and its conservative approach to controlling Type I error. Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

Nearly half of the participants were in the 24–27 age group (49.5%). Most students were single (61.5%) and in their first or second year of postgraduate training (44.8%). The largest specialty group was Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery (18.2%). Detailed demographic characteristics are presented in Table 2.

In the multiple regression model, both stress (β = 0.543, p < .001) and anxiety (β = 0.317, p < .001) were significantly associated with depression scores. Standardized β coefficients were numerically higher for stress than for anxiety in relation to depression.

None of the sociodemographic variables showed statistically significant associations with depression (p > .05). Perceived financial status approached, but did not reach, statistical significance (p = .058).

The model accounted for 69.6% of the variance in depression scores (R² = 0.696, Adjusted R² = 0.680). Residual diagnostics indicated an approximate normal distribution.

The distribution of participants according to severity levels is presented in Table 1.

For depression, 60.9% of participants were classified as normal, 13.0% as mild, 16.7% as moderate, 7.3% as severe, and 2.1% as extremely severe.

For anxiety, 71.4% of participants were classified as normal, with smaller proportions observed in higher severity categories.

For stress, 62.5% of participants were classified as normal, with 15.1% classified as moderate and 6.3% as severe.

Discussion

This study examined the relationships among stress, anxiety, and depression in postgraduate dental students and explored how these psychological outcomes vary according to sociodemographic characteristics. Unlike undergraduate populations, postgraduate training involves increased clinical responsibility, academic productivity expectations, and career-related pressures, which may create a distinct psychological burden [3, 4]. The use of the DASS-21 allowed a simultaneous assessment of these closely related psychological constructs within a single validated framework, facilitating the interpretation of their co-occurrence patterns [18, 19].

A key finding of the present study is the statistically significant positive associations observed among stress, anxiety, and depression scores. These findings are consistent with previous literature in health sciences populations, where these psychological dimensions frequently co-occur [1, 10]. These findings may also reflect a broader underlying psychological distress construct, rather than fully distinct dimensions, given the conceptual and measurement overlap among DASS-21 subscales. However, these associations should be interpreted with considerable caution, as the relatively high correlation coefficients may partly reflect conceptual and measurement overlap inherent in multi-dimensional instruments such as the DASS-21 [18–20].

The regression analyses further demonstrated that stress and anxiety remained significantly associated with depression scores, even when considered within the same model. Despite the statistically significant associations observed, the relatively high R² value should be interpreted with considerable caution, as it is likely partly attributable to the inclusion of closely related subscales derived from the same instrument (DASS-21), which inherently share conceptual and measurement variance. In this context, the model may reflect a broader psychological distress construct rather than fully independent explanatory effects [4, 10, 11, 21]. Although VIF values did not indicate problematic multicollinearity, the high correlation between stress and anxiety suggests that their associations with depression should be interpreted with caution, as they may partly reflect a shared underlying psychological distress construct rather than fully distinct effects. Therefore, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of distinct explanatory effects, but rather as reflecting interconnected dimensions of psychological distress.

Regarding sociodemographic variables, gender emerged as a significant differentiating factor, with female students reporting higher levels of stress, anxiety, and depression compared to male students. This finding is consistent with previous studies suggesting that female students may experience greater psychological distress due to differences in coping strategies, emotional processing, and social expectations [12, 14, 17]. These findings may be interpreted within the framework of stress vulnerability and coping theory, which suggests that individual differences in emotional processing, social support, and adaptive coping mechanisms may influence psychological distress levels.

Age-related differences indicated that participants aged 28–30 years exhibited higher levels of distress, which may correspond to a period characterized by increased clinical responsibility, academic expectations, and career-related uncertainty [22–24]. Similarly, single participants reported higher levels of psychological distress, supporting the role of social support as a protective factor for mental well-being [14].

An important finding of the present study was the higher level of psychological distress among participants who perceived their financial status as “normal” compared to those reporting a “good” financial status. This finding may reflect the subjective nature of financial perception, where individuals in a “normal” category may experience greater uncertainty and perceived instability. During the study period, Türkiye experienced significant inflationary pressures and a marked increase in the cost of living, which directly impacted the purchasing power of fixed-income professionals, such as postgraduate dental residents. Therefore, students who perceive their income as ‘normal’—typically reflecting standard public salaries—may still experience high distress due to the widening gap between fixed earnings and rapidly rising expenses for housing, clinical materials, and basic needs. In economic contexts characterized by increasing living costs and financial pressure, such perceptions may contribute to elevated psychological distress among postgraduate students. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies highlighting the role of financial stress as a determinant of mental health outcomes [4, 10, 25]. Although several sociodemographic variables showed significant differences in univariate analyses, these effects did not persist in the multivariable model, indicating that their associations with psychological distress may be attenuated after adjustment for related variables and shared variance. These sociodemographic findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and context-dependent rather than definitive explanatory relationships.

In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed according to training year, specialty program, place of residence, or smoking status. This may suggest that individual-level and perceived contextual factors play a more prominent role than structural or academic variables in shaping psychological distress in postgraduate dental education. These findings are partially consistent with previous research, although inconsistencies across studies may be explained by differences in institutional structures and cultural contexts [3, 26].

In summary, the findings of this study indicate that stress, anxiety, and depression are closely interrelated among postgraduate dental trainees, with stress and anxiety showing high correlations with depressive symptoms. These results highlight the importance of considering psychological well-being as a multidimensional construct within postgraduate dental education.

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference regarding causality or temporal direction between variables. Second, as the study was conducted within a single institution, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other educational or cultural contexts, and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Third, the use of self-reported measures may introduce response bias, including social desirability bias, which may influence the accuracy of participants’ responses. In addition, because all predictors were derived from the same psychometric instrument (DASS-21), the regression model may partly reflect shared measurement variance, which may limit the interpretation of these associations as fully independent effects. Furthermore, the relatively high R² value may be partly influenced by conceptual overlap among DASS-21 subscales, and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Despite these limitations, this study contributes to the literature by providing an integrated analysis of psychological distress in a postgraduate dental population, combining both severity-based descriptive findings and multivariate analytical approaches. Future research should incorporate longitudinal and multicenter designs to better understand temporal dynamics and causal pathways, and should also explore protective factors such as resilience, social support, and adaptive coping strategies.

Conclusion

This study indicates that stress, anxiety, and depression are closely interrelated among postgraduate dental trainees, with stress and anxiety showing high correlations with depressive symptoms. Psychological distress in this population may be influenced by individual and perceived contextual factors; however, these associations were not consistently supported in the multivariable model. By focusing specifically on postgraduate dental trainees and integrating multiple psychological dimensions within a single analytical framework, this study contributes to the limited literature addressing psychological distress in postgraduate dental education. These findings suggest that psychological well-being should be considered an important component of postgraduate dental education. While causal inferences cannot be drawn, the observed patterns highlight the potential value of supportive institutional strategies aimed at promoting mental health. Within this context, dental education programs may benefit from implementing accessible psychological support services, incorporating stress management and resilience-focused interventions into training, and fostering supportive academic and clinical environments. Such approaches may contribute to improving both student well-being and professional development.
