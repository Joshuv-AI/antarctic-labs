Application of confocal laser endomicroscopy in the diagnosis of peripheral pulmonary lesions: a narrative review
Authors: Ziyun Wu, Chunxi Zhang, Fangfang Xie, Junxiang Chen, Haoran Ding, Jiayuan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveDespite continued advances in navigational bronchoscopy, the diagnosis of peripheral pulmonary lesions (PPLs) remains challenging. Although contemporary bronchoscopic techniques have improved lesion accessibility, important limitations persist in instrument position confirmation, and representative tissue sampling. Confocal laser endomicroscopy (CLE) is an emerging real-time microscopic imaging technique that enables in vivo visualization of tissue microarchitecture and cellular morphology during bronchoscopic procedures. Its imaging modalities mainly include autofluorescence imaging and exogenous contrast-enhanced imaging. This review aimed to summarize the CLE features of these two imaging modes in PPLs, with particular emphasis on the differences between normal and lesional tissue, in order to clarify the potential role of CLE in lesion confirmation and biopsy guidance.MethodsA literature search was performed using PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science from inception to December 2025 for studies evaluating CLE in PPLs. The following terms were used in searching: (endomicroscopy or microendoscopy or fibered confocal fluorescence microscopy or confocal laser microscopy) and (lung or pulmonary or respiratory or bronch* or airway). Studies were excluded if they were non-PPLs, animal-only, or duplicate publications.Key Content and FindingsAutofluorescence CLE primarily visualizes elastin-rich alveolar microstructures and can effectively distinguish normal lung tissue from abnormal lesional areas, although it remains difficult for differentiating benign from malignant lesions. In contrast, exogenous contrast-enhanced CLE provides clearer visualization of cellular morphology and tissue architecture, and may facilitate the detection and differentiation of lung cancer. In the diagnosis of PPLs, CLE has the potential clinical value of providing real-time lesion diagnosis and guiding sampling location. Future studies are needed to further refine and validate CLE interpretation criteria for common benign and malignant PPLs, and to determine its clinical value in transbronchial biopsy of PPLs through randomized controlled trials.ConclusionsCLE is a promising adjunctive bronchoscopic imaging technology with potential value in the evaluation of PPLs, particularly for lesion confirmation and biopsy guidance.

Introduction

Peripheral pulmonary lesions (PPLs) are defined as lesions located distal to the segmental bronchi, completely surrounded by lung parenchyma, and not directly visible during bronchoscopy (1). With the widespread adoption of computed tomography (CT) for lung cancer screening, the detection of PPLs has risen substantially (2-4). PPLs can arise from multiple etiologies. Lung cancer is a common malignant cause and remains the leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, whereas common benign causes include inflammation, tuberculosis, and so on. For patients with PPLs suspicious for malignancy or other etiologies, timely specimen acquisition and definitive diagnosis are essential for initiating appropriate therapy.

Transbronchial biopsy is the principal minimally invasive approach for sampling PPLs and is generally considered safe (5,6). However, despite advances in guided bronchoscopy techniques, such as virtual bronchoscopic navigation, electromagnetic navigation bronchoscopy, and radial endobronchial ultrasound (r-EBUS), the overall diagnostic yield for PPLs remains at only about 70%. Even with the recent emergence of robotic-assisted bronchoscopy (RAB), the diagnostic yield typically reaches around 80% (7-9). Recently, with the combined use of advanced intraoperative imaging techniques such as mobile cone-beam CT (CBCT), RAB has achieved a diagnostic yield of approximately 90% for PPLs (10,11). However, even if navigation appears technically successful, the biopsy specimens may still be nondiagnostic. Near miss of the target lesion is a major obstacle. Additionally, because of the lesion heterogeneity, the obtained specimen may not be representative even when the biopsy tool is positioned within the lesion.

As an emerging imaging technology, confocal laser endomicroscopy (CLE) may become an important adjunctive technique in transbronchial biopsy, as it enables high-resolution, real-time, cellular-level in vivo imaging and has been proposed as a potential noninvasive “optical biopsy” technique (12-14). CLE was initially applied to the diagnosis and surveillance of gastrointestinal diseases (15,16). CLE was applied in the respiratory system in 2007 and further extended into the fields of oral cavity, pharynx, and neurosurgery (17-20). In the diagnosis of PPLs, this technique can provide real-time lesion diagnosis and guide sampling location, thereby potentially improving both the diagnostic yield and safety of transbronchial biopsy. Although CLE has not yet been incorporated into routine clinical practice, its potential value in diagnostic assistance, biopsy guidance, real-time lesion localization, and intra-procedural assessment is increasingly recognized (14,21,22).

Therefore, this review aims to summarize the CLE imaging interpretation criteria for PPLs and to discuss the clinical value of CLE. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0545/rc).

Methods

We performed a structured literature search of PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science from database inception to December 2025 for studies evaluating CLE in PPLs. The following terms were used in searching: (endomicroscopy or microendoscopy or fibered confocal fluorescence microscopy or confocal laser microscopy) and (lung or pulmonary or respiratory or bronch* or airway). The search resulted in 2,834 articles. Studies were excluded if they were non-PPLs, animal-only, or duplicate publications. Conference abstracts that lacked sufficient methodological detail or image descriptions were also excluded. We retrieved 82 publications, which were reviewed in full, and 58 articles were included (Table 1).

Technical principle and application

During CLE imaging, a 488-nm laser precisely focused onto the predefined tissue plane to induce fluorescence from endogenous structures or exogenous fluorescein. Emission signals originating from the focal layer are isolated through confocal pinhole filtration, thereby excluding out-of-focus light and enabling acquisition of high-resolution, in vivo microscopic images (23,24).

In 2007, Thiberville et al. demonstrated that there is a widespread presence of endogenous autofluorescence within lung tissue, establishing the foundation for CLE applications in pulmonary diseases (17). To address the limited signal intensity and suboptimal structural contrast in certain regions, Fuchs et al. introduced exogenous fluorophores to enhance image quality (25). Reported contrast agents include fluorescein, acriflavine hydrochloride, and methylene blue (25-27). Fluorescein stains the extracellular matrix and blood vessels but not the cells, hence creating contrast between cells and the fluorescein-rich background (28). Whereas topical acriflavine hydrochloride binds to DNA/RNA and therefore specifically stains nuclei under CLE (29). In clinical practice, fluorescein has become the most widely used contrast agent in CLE imaging due to its high safety profile and low cost (30,31). In contrast, acriflavine hydrochloride carries potential mutagenic risk. Although methylene blue is superior to acriflavine hydrochloride in terms of safety, its application in CLE for PPLs remains limited in reported cases.

At present, CLE is broadly categorized by the mode of access into probe-based CLE (pCLE) and needle-based CLE (nCLE). For pCLE, CLE probes are delivered through the working channel of the bronchoscope to the target lesion. For nCLE, needle puncture is performed, and CLE probes are delivered through the needle. Bronchoscopists can select appropriate probes based on lesion characteristics. Using CLE probes from Mauna Kea Technologies (Paris, France) as an example, most PPLs can be assessed by pCLE using 1.4-mm AlveoFlex probe. For lesions located at more acutely angulated sites, the more flexible 0.85-mm AQ-Flex 19 probe can be used for pCLE. For lesions without a bronchus sign, the AQ-Flex 19 probe can be introduced through a needle to perform nCLE (Figure 1).

Autofluorescence-based CLE imaging: features and application

In the gastrointestinal tract, the application of CLE predominantly relies on exogenous fluorescent contrast agents (such as topically applied acridine-based dyes and intravenously administered fluorescein), enabling cellular-level visualization of the upper and lower gastrointestinal mucosa during routine endoscopic examinations (29). However, in 2007, Thiberville et al. reported that the respiratory system contains abundant endogenous autofluorophores, and spectral analysis demonstrated that the observed autofluorescence predominantly originated from elastin within the basement membrane (17). The group subsequently extended pCLE to the alveolar compartment, demonstrating that (particularly in nonsmokers) autofluorescence arises predominantly from elastin backbone within the alveolar walls, including perialveolar vascular structures. In active smokers, intensely fluorescent, tar-laden alveolar macrophages were additionally observed (32). Leveraging this intrinsic autofluorescence of the respiratory tract, early researchers primarily focused on autofluorescence-based CLE imaging (Table 2).

Autofluorescence-based CLE imaging in normal area

Salaün et al. evaluated 21 healthy subjects and found that normal pulmonary area exhibited uniformly distributed elastic fibers, preserved alveolar architecture, and no abnormal clusters of fluorescent cells (33). In a subsequent study, Wijmans et al. described normal pulmonary area on pCLE as slender alveolar fibers forming alveolar openings, with a high degree of concordance with histopathology (34). Wang et al. further demonstrated that normal regions in pCLE images show regular, intact alveolar units with trapezoid interconnections, thin and continuous elastic fibers, and in some cases, well-preserved microvasculature (35). Overall, these studies define the baseline CLE appearance of normal lung area, consistently characterized by orderly elastic fibers, intact alveolar architecture, and the absence of pathological features (Figure 2A). Taken together, these observations provide a “normal reference standard” against which CLE abnormalities associated with pulmonary lesions can be identified.

(B) Malignant disease: disordered elastin fibers with disrupted alveolar structure. (C) Benign disease: disorganized and accumulated elastin fibers. Scale bars: (A) 20 µm and (B,C) 50 µm. CLE, confocal laser endomicroscopy.

Autofluorescence-based CLE imaging in malignant diseases

Several studies have characterized autofluorescence-based CLE abnormalities in lung tissue surrounding neoplastic lesions. In an ex vivo pCLE assessment of lung cancer, three pCLE features were observed in the cancer surroundings: alveolar dystelectasis with thickening of alveolar walls, alveolar edema and a large number of macrophages (39). A similar prelesional pattern was reported in the study by Trindade et al., the CLE images manifested as alveolar wall thickening secondary to an increase in the elastin bundle diameter, alveolar edema, and a large number of macrophages in the alveolar space (36). Notably, although CLE can identify the cancer surroundings, a prospective study indicates that discrimination between perilesional tissue and truly normal regions remains less robust, achieving an accuracy of only 72.8% (35). This may be attributable to the fact that perilesional tissues often show indistinct boundaries and nonspecific features despite subtle pathological changes, which may lead to misclassification.

When imaging moves from the perilesional into the malignant area, autofluorescence-based CLE imaging more prominently characterized by disorganization, fragmentation, and disorderly accumulation of the elastin fibers (Figure 2B). Thiberville et al. first reported that pre-malignant lesions presented a disorganized fiber network on CLE images, representing an early sign of elastin scaffold disruption along the neoplastic development (17). In a cohort of 26 patients, Filner et al. found that malignant regions were characterized by a solid, indistinct, cloud-like appearance with loss of the normally organized fiber architecture, and these features differentiated malignant from normal regions with a sensitivity of 100% (41). Wellikoff et al. conducted a comparative study between histopathological findings and pCLE images in 25 patients with suspected malignant lesions. They found that pCLE changes in lung cancer regions exhibited mottled elastin or “black holes” appearance, septal studding and disorganization/fragmentation with increased friability, consistent with histologic evidence of elastin destruction (37). Building on histologic correlation, one study conducted by Wang et al. developed a pCLE atlas for malignant pulmonary lesions, categorizing patterns into three types: (I) “hyperplasia”, which is primarily marked by thickening of the alveolar septa and diffuse proliferation of elastic fibers; (II) “deformation”, which primarily manifests as curling of the alveolar walls, alveolar collapse, and adhesion of the alveolar walls; and (III) “destruction”, which manifests as severe destruction of the alveolar structure, including the rupture of elastic fibers, the accumulation of elastic protein in fragments, structural defects (the absence of elastic protein), and microvascular destruction. Importantly, as the malignancy grade of the tumor increased, the destruction of alveolar structures observed in CLE images became more pronounced. Furthermore, the proposed pCLE imaging features for malignant pulmonary lesions demonstrated good reproducibility and stability, with both inter-observer agreement and intra-observer reliability >0.8. The overall accuracy for differentiating lesional from non-lesional tissue reached 95.8% (35).

Building on this, the contrast between elastin and non-fluorescent tumor cell aggregates can be further exploited for tumor subtyping. Sorokina et al. performed a study involving 18 lobectomy specimens. In all nine adenocarcinoma specimens, CLE was characterized by a highly fluorescent stromal component containing dark hollows. In all six squamous cell carcinoma specimens, CLE revealed a stromal component appearing as ‘biparously’ branching, highly fluorescent fibers. In all small-cell carcinoma specimens, no stromal component was observed on CLE. Instead, the cellular component was dominant and exhibited a light scattering pattern (39).

For non-solid pulmonary lesions [mixed or pure ground-glass opacities (GGOs)], CLE has the potential to serve as a novel tool for localizing GGOs (44). In 2021, Viatgé et al. reported a case of GGO, the pCLE showed studding alveolar walls with an increased frequency of small alveolar entrance rings, septal fibers, and a rigid and dense acinar elastic network, which is similar to previous features described by Salaün et al. (33,40). Later, Kalifa et al. evaluated 36 patients with non-solid nodules and applied a subset of previously described CLE features, including a disorganized elastic network, an alveolar mouth diameter <200 µm, a rigid and dense acinar elastic network, and prominent septal fibers to localize GGOs. Using these criteria, they achieved high sensitivity for GGO localization (0.90), which was superior to that of r-EBUS (0.73), although specificity remained modest (0.25) (45). This modest specificity may be attributable to the limited penetration depth of autofluorescence-based CLE and to the overlap of imaging features between malignant and inflammatory tissues.

Autofluorescence-based CLE imaging in benign diseases

Autofluorescence-based CLE imaging has been less frequently applied to benign pulmonary diseases. Nevertheless, it can provide early, in vivo recognition of subtle microstructural abnormalities. For example, in pulmonary alveolar proteinosis, Danilevskaya et al. identified globular lipoproteinaceous sign on pCLE: a fluorescent flowing amorphous substance in the alveoli lumen sticking to conglomerates along with alveolar macrophages. This feature was confirmed as a characteristic feature and detectable even in lung segments that appeared normal on CT (43). Filner et al. found that in patients with Wegener’s granulomatosis, the normally organized fiber pattern on CLE was absent and was replaced by a solid, indistinct, cloud-like appearance (41). In patients with stage II-III sarcoidosis, Salaün et al. found a significantly higher frequency of bronchiolar cells and alveolar nodular fluorescent structures. The alveolar cells observed in sarcoidosis were uniformly <20 µm, suggesting that they were predominantly lymphocytes (33).

Autofluorescence-based CLE imaging: current limitations and prospects

In summary, CLE enables high-resolution, in vivo visualization of the microstructure of normal pulmonary regions as well as the microscopic structural alterations within diseased areas. Since 2007, researchers have progressively explored CLE features of normal lung tissue and various malignant and benign lesions. However, most of these studies were single-center investigations with small sample sizes, and the CLE features proposed have not been validated in large-scale clinical cohorts. In addition, autofluorescence-based CLE imaging primarily reflects the alterations in elastin and cannot directly depict cytological features within lesions. As a result, it could distinguish normal from disease tissue. However, differentiating benign from malignant lesions and identifying specific diseases remains challenging. For example, Filner et al. found that both Wegener’s granulomatosis and non-small cell lung carcinoma demonstrated a similar loss of autofluorescence on CLE imaging, characterized by a dense, indistinct, cloud-like pattern (41). Similarly, an observational study reported that in most parenchymal lung diseases, CLE architecture became unrecognizable, manifesting as nonspecific autofluorescence loss (46). Consistent with these findings, our center has observed that autofluorescence-based CLE has limited ability to differentiate malignant from benign diseases (Figure 2C).

Accordingly, Seth et al. suggested that further research should incorporate exogenous fluorophores to visualize the cellular architecture of pulmonary lesions and even combined with molecular markers. Such approaches could help compensate for inherent limitations of autofluorescence-based CLE imaging and may improve its capacity to discriminate between benign and malignant lesions (47).

CLE imaging based on exogenous contrast agent: features and application

In the clinical application of CLE, autofluorescence-based imaging primarily reveals the elastin architecture of lesions but yields limited insight into critical cellular-level features. In contrast, coupling CLE with exogenous contrast agent increases signal intensity and tissue contrast, enabling visualization of cytological details. In a small cohort study, Hassan et al. demonstrated that pCLE with exogenous contrast agent is safe and feasible (48). By enhancing microstructural and cytologic details, contrast-enhanced CLE advances the concept of real-time “optical biopsy”, thereby facilitating the identification of more reproducible morphologic patterns (47).

Contrast-enhanced CLE imaging in normal area

Early studies indicate that the use of exogenous contrast agents enables CLE to achieve cellular- and even subcellular-level analysis without associated adverse effects (25,26). However, most contrast-enhanced CLE studies have focused primarily on diseased tissue, while the characterization of normal tissue was comparatively limited. Fuchs et al. reported that after intravenous administration of fluorescein, the alveolar spaces in healthy subjects appeared densely populated with foam-like structures, which were subsequently identified as air bubbles (Figure 3A) (25). Contrast-enhanced CLE imaging in malignant diseases

With the assistance of exogenous contrast agents, CLE enables high-resolution, real-time visualization of microstructural differences between lesional and normal regions. Peritumoral abnormalities may precede overt malignant changes and provide clues for early identification. Fuchs et al. reported that, following acriflavine staining, inflamed peritumoral areas were characterized by an increased distance between nuclei surrounding the tumor, and the borderline between inflamed and neoplastic tissue was clearly delineated (26). In a proof-of-concept pilot study, Shulimzon et al. further showed that fluorescein highlighted the extracellular matrix and delineated vascular structures within tumor regions. Thus, pCLE could accurately differentiate vascularized tissue from necrotic or fibrotic areas, which may help optimize biopsy site selection and improve diagnostic accuracy (49).

For malignancy recognition, exogenous contrast agents substantially enhance CLE imaging quality and are associated with favorable diagnostic performance (50,51). A meta-analysis reported that pCLE yielded pooled sensitivity and specificity of 85% and 90%, respectively, with an overall accuracy of 87% for malignant diagnosis (52).

Acriflavine and fluorescein are commonly used agents in clinical application. Acriflavine provides strong contrast of cell nuclei. In a prospective study of 32 patients with suspected malignancy, Fuchs et al. reported that neoplastic cells appeared dark and exhibited large nuclei that were heterogeneous in size. These nuclei showed weaker acriflavine uptake and were less densely arranged than in normal mucosa (26). Furthermore, a wide and dark cytoplasm surrounding the heterogeneous nuclei was noted. Based on these distinctive morphological features, neoplastic changes could be predicted with high accuracy (sensitivity =96.0%, specificity =87.1%, accuracy =91.0%). To standardize interpretation, Takemura et al. proposed the CLE atypia (CLEA) classification, which quantified cellular density and nuclear size variation, and then categorized pCLE morphological findings into “benign”, “suspicious”, and “malignant”. Malignant features were defined as high cellular density along with enlarged nuclei, with a minor-axis length was at least twice that of the smallest nuclei within the same field of view. Using this diagnostic framework to detect malignancy, the authors reported a sensitivity of 91% and a specificity of 77%, highlighting the potential of CLE for rapid histological evaluation. Beyond recognition, contrast-enhanced CLE has also been evaluated for lesion localization and procedural guidance (53). However, because acriflavine is potentially mutagenic to DNA, its use for in vivo staining has become strictly restricted.

Fluorescein preferentially highlights the extracellular matrix and vascular structures, thereby providing contrast between cellular elements and the fluorescein-enriched background and facilitating discrimination of tissue and cellular architecture. In vivo, fluorescein is metabolized and is almost completely eliminated renally within 24 hours. However, intravenous administration of fluorescein sodium carries a risk of type I hypersensitivity reactions. Therefore, allergy testing is recommended before injection. In 2016, Wijmans et al. observed dark, pleomorphic cell clusters within the alveoli of patients with PPLs using nCLE. Histopathology subsequently confirmed these findings as adenocarcinoma cells (54). The appearance closely resembled the malignant lymph node patterns previously described by Benias et al. (55). Notably, Wijmans and colleagues later identified three nCLE features suggestive of malignancy: dark enlarged pleomorphic cells, dark cell clusters, and directional streaming. These criteria demonstrated substantial inter- and intra-observer agreement, supporting the reliability and reproducibility. Overall sensitivity and specificity for lung cancer detection were 0.90 and 0.89, respectively (38). Among these features, enlarged pleomorphic cells and dark clusters were highly consistent with malignant pulmonary lesions (Figure 3B). This finding has been further corroborated in subsequent studies (40,56,57). Beyond morphologic characterization, nCLE has also been evaluated for lesion localization and procedural guidance. A preliminary report by Su et al. evaluated nCLE combined with fluoroscopy for pulmonary lesions and showed that this approach effectively localized pulmonary lesions (58). Kramer et al. incorporated nCLE with r-EBUS and fluoroscopy in 26 patients with suspected lung cancer, achieving overall diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of 96% and 94%, respectively (59). Moreover, integrating nCLE with RAB has been shown to optimize biopsy sampling (60-62).

Contrast-enhanced CLE imaging in benign diseases

Compared to researches on malignancy, contrast-enhanced CLE evidence in benign diseases remains limited. Representative contrast-enhanced CLE features of benign disease are illustrated in Figure 3C, which mostly appeared as uniform-sized cells on CLE images. Sarcoidosis is a systemic granulomatous inflammation that may present with pulmonary lesions or lymphadenopathy. Wijmans et al. reported that CLE was limited in its ability to detect the morphological features of granulomas without the assistance of exogenous fluorescent agents (34). To address this limitation, Kramer et al. established nCLE criteria for granuloma identification using fluorescein, requiring the simultaneous presence of three features: increased brightness, well-defined margins, and homogeneous internal structure. Application of these criteria yield a granuloma detection accuracy of 91.7%, thereby assisting differentiation between granulomatous disease and malignancy (42). However, there were numerous false positives, resulting in a specificity of only 37.5%. These false-positive malignant videos can be attributed to the co-existing presence of multinucleated giant cells mimicking enlarged tumor cells. Additionally, compression of the granuloma on the surrounding inflammation causes overprojection of cells, thereby mimicking the nCLE malignancy criterion of “dark clumps”.

Contrast-enhanced CLE imaging: current limitations and prospects

In conclusion, with the aid of exogenous contrast agents, CLE enhances tissue contrast and enables real-time visualization at the cellular level. However, current contrast-enhanced CLE studies have primarily focused on defining imaging features of malignant lesions. CLE interpretation criteria for common benign diseases and for different malignant subtypes remain lacking and require further refinement, and the proposed criteria warrant prospective, multicenter validation. Future studies should broaden the inclusion of benign disease phenotypes and different malignant subtypes, and use lesion-matched histopathology as the reference standard to improve interpretability and interobserver consistency while reducing ambiguity introduced by CLE patterns that may mimic malignancy.

Discussion

Although several bronchoscopic navigational techniques have been developed to target PPLs, diagnostic performance remains variable and is strongly influenced by factors such as lesion size, bronchus sign, and whether r-EBUS demonstrates a concentric or eccentric pattern (63,64). In this context, CLE may be a promising intraprocedural microscopic confirmation tool that addresses two distinct questions after macroscopic navigation has been achieved.

First, CLE may help confirm whether the biopsy tool is actually within the target lesion. At present, most navigational bronchoscopy systems rely on preprocedural CT for pathway planning and intraprocedural guidance. Therefore, navigational accuracy can be affected by CT-to-body divergence (63). Although r-EBUS is commonly used as an adjunct to confirm the relationship between the ultrasound probe and the lesion, it cannot reflect the exact position of the biopsy tool at the moment of tissue acquisition. After the r-EBUS probe is withdrawn and the sampling tool is advanced, uncertainty may still remain as to whether the biopsy tool is actually within the lesion. CLE has the potential to directly visualize whether the biopsy tool is positioned within the lesion. For example, in the study by Manley et al. combining nCLE with RAB, after lesion localization by r-EBUS, the first needle pass was performed according to the planned trajectory. The CLE probe was then advanced beyond the needle tip to verify whether the needle tip lied within the lesion. Realtime nCLE suggested incorrect needle positioning (nCLE demonstrated airway/lung parenchyma) in 9/20 patients (45%), prompting repositioning. After repositioning, nCLE provided tool-in-lesion confirmation (nCLE showed dark enlarged pleomorphic cells, dark cell clusters, or directional streaming) in 19/20 patients, ultimately achieving a diagnostic yield of 80% (65).

Second, CLE may help optimize biopsy location within a lesion. Pulmonary lesions are often heterogeneous, and the tissue obtained may not be representative. In this context, CLE can provide real-time in vivo microscopic information before sampling, which may help the operator judge whether the current biopsy location appears more or less likely to yield informative tissue and thereby optimize biopsy location. Once CLE has identified the ideal biopsy site, fluoroscopy or CBCT can be used to mark the location before tissue sampling. This role is conceptually different from that of rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE). ROSE provides feedback after tissue has already been obtained, whereas CLE has the potential to provide in vivo imaging feedback before sampling (65).

Despite these potential roles, the current evidence base remains limited. First, most available studies are small, single-center investigations, and high-quality multicenter studies as well as randomized controlled trials demonstrating the clinical utility of CLE in practice are lacking. Second, there is a lack of standardized image classification systems for CLE, unlike the well-established Mainz or Miami criteria used in gastrointestinal applications. Current research has focused predominantly on malignant diseases, with relatively limited investigation of benign conditions. Furthermore, studies assessing the ability of CLE to differentiate malignant and benign subtypes remain scarce. Third, there is a lack of adequate validation. The CLE interpretation criteria proposed have not been thoroughly validated. On one hand, these criteria have not been broadly tested across common benign and malignant lesions. For example, the CLE study conducted by Wijmans et al. in PPLs only enrolled normal lung tissue as a negative control. On the other hand, multicenter external validation studies are still lacking.

For real-world adoption of CLE, several practical factors should be considered. First, training is an important factor. Studies have shown that, after short-term training, inter-observer agreement for CLE-based assessment of PPLs reaches a moderate-to-substantial level (0.63–0.78) (42,59), supporting the feasibility of broader clinical implementation. Second, in terms of procedural efficiency, CLE does not appear to add substantial time to the overall procedure. A recent retrospective study reported that the additional time required for nCLE was approximately 5 minutes, suggesting that integrating it into routine bronchoscopic workflows is feasible (56). Third, cost remains an important practical consideration. Although imported CLE systems and mini-probes are associated with relatively high equipment and per-procedure costs, the emergence of lower-cost Chinese CLE platforms may help reduce the economic burden and improve accessibility. Fourth, the choice and regulation of contrast agents also require careful consideration. Fluorescein sodium remains the most commonly used contrast agent. It has long been used safely in ophthalmic fluorescein angiography, and fluorescein-assisted CLE studies have likewise supported its safety profile. By contrast, acriflavine is potentially mutagenic and should be used with caution under strict regulatory constraints.

In the future, artificial intelligence (AI) and digital image analysis may serve as potential tools to make CLE interpretation more quantitative and intelligent. Early studies using automated segmentation and transfer learning approaches suggest that machine learning may help characterize or classify CLE images (66,67). However, before AI-assisted CLE can be considered clinically applicable, several prerequisites must be addressed, including rigorous external validation and improved algorithm interpretability. In parallel, molecularly targeted near-infrared nCLE has been proposed as an innovative proof-of-concept strategy for the real-time discrimination of tumor from non-tumor tissue during biopsy. Nevertheless, the current evidence base remains at an early stage (68,69). The additional clinical value of this technology relative to conventional CLE remains uncertain. Another emerging approach, dynamic cell imaging, evaluates biopsy specimens by integrating cellular morphology with metabolic activity-based signal mapping to generate a real-time “heat map” of cellular activity for immediate sample review. Studies have demonstrated its utility for ex vivo assessment of specimen quality during transbronchial biopsy (70). However, unlike CLE, it is not currently capable of providing comparable real-time in vivo assessment.

Conclusions

In conclusion, CLE shows promise primarily as an intraprocedural adjunct to navigational bronchoscopy, with potential value in helping confirm whether the biopsy tool is within the target lesion and in optimizing biopsy location in heterogeneous lesions. However, the current level of evidence remains insufficient, as most available studies are small-scale and single-center in design. Further high-quality clinical studies will help refine its optimal use in routine practice, including the development and improvement of CLE interpretation criteria for common benign and malignant PPLs, broad and reliable validation of these interpretation criteria, and randomized controlled trials evaluating its added value within existing diagnostic pathways, such as its impact on diagnostic yield. Such efforts will be important for fully establishing the clinical value of CLE in the application of PPLs.
