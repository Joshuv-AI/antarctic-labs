Quantum geometry of the non-Hermitian skin effect
Ken-Ichiro Imura, Kohei Kawabata

Abstract
The non-Hermitian skin effect is nonreciprocity-induced localization phenomena in which a macroscopic number of eigenstates accumulate anomalously at the boundary, accompanied by the extreme sensitivity to boundary conditions. Here, we develop a geometric characterization of the non-Hermitian skin effect. We demonstrate that the localization length scale associated with the skin effect is encoded in the quantum metric defined solely from right eigenstates, but not in the biorthogonal quantum metric. Moreover, we show that the quantum metrics exhibit the power-law divergences at gapless points that depend on the different boundary conditions. We also reveal that cusps of the generalized Brillouin zone in non-Bloch band theory are signaled by discontinuities in the quantum metrics. We illustrate these behavior using prototypical non-Hermitian models, such as the Hatano-Nelson model and the non-Hermitian, nonreciprocal Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model.

II
Quantum geometry in non-Hermitian systems

Quantum geometry characterizes how a quantum state varies in parameter space after removing the trivial dependence on its overall phase or normalization.
It provides a natural language for quantifying the sensitivity of eigenstates to external parameters and will serve as the basic tool in the following discussion.

II.1
Quantum metric tensor in Hermitian systems

We begin with a brief review of the Hermitian case
102
;
17
.
Let
H(\bm{\lambda})
be a Hermitian Hamiltonian that depends smoothly on a set of parameters
\bm{\lambda}=\left(\lambda_{\mu}\right)
, and let
\ket{u_{n}(\bm{\lambda})}
be a normalized eigenstate of an isolated band:

H(\bm{\lambda})\ket{u_{n}(\bm{\lambda})}=E_{n}(\bm{\lambda})\ket{u_{n}(\bm{\lambda})},\quad\braket{u_{n}(\bm{\lambda})|u_{n}(\bm{\lambda})}=1.

(1)

By introducing
\partial_{\mu}\coloneqq\partial/\partial\lambda_{\mu}
, the quantum geometric tensor (or equivalently, Fubini-Study metric) is defined as

\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{H}}\coloneqq\braket{\partial_{\mu}u_{n}|\left(1-\ket{u_{n}}\bra{u_{n}}\right)|\partial_{\nu}u_{n}}.

(2)

Its real symmetric part gives the quantum metric tensor,

g_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{H}}\coloneqq\mathrm{Re}\,\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{H}},

(3)

while its imaginary antisymmetric part yields the Berry curvature,

F_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{H}}\coloneqq-2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{H}}.

(4)

The quantum metric tensor is invariant under a local phase transformation

\ket{u_{n}(\bm{\lambda})}\to e^{\text{i}\varphi(\bm{\lambda})}\ket{u_{n}(\bm{\lambda})}
,
and measures the infinitesimal distance between nearby rays in Hilbert space.
In particular, for a one-dimensional parameter
\lambda
, the diagonal component
g_{\lambda\lambda}^{\mathrm{H}}
quantifies how rapidly the state changes as
\lambda
is varied.

II.2
Two quantum metric tensors in non-Hermitian systems

For a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian, right and left eigenstates are generally distinct
6
.
They are denoted by

H(\bm{\lambda})\ket{u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}(\bm{\lambda})}=E_{n}(\bm{\lambda})\ket{u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}(\bm{\lambda})},

(5)

and

\langle\!\langle u_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}(\bm{\lambda})|H(\bm{\lambda})=E_{n}(\bm{\lambda})\langle\!\langle u_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}(\bm{\lambda})|.

(6)

Because of this distinction, extension of Eq. (
2
) to non-Hermitian systems is not unique.
Two natural definitions are particularly useful for the present work, as introduced below.

The first one is defined solely from the right eigenstates.
Assuming the normalization

\braket{u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}(\bm{\lambda})|u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}(\bm{\lambda})}=1,

(7)

we introduce

\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{RR}}\coloneqq\braket{\partial_{\mu}u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}|\left(1-\ket{u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}}\bra{u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}}\right)|\partial_{\nu}u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}}.

(8)

This quantity is the direct analog of the Hermitian quantum geometric tensor constructed with the ordinary inner product.
For
\mu=\nu
, it is real and nonnegative.
Moreover, it is invariant under a local phase transformation of the normalized right eigenstate.

The second one is the biorthogonal quantum metric tensor, for which we impose

\langle\!\langle u_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}(\bm{\lambda})|u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}(\bm{\lambda})\rangle=1,

(9)

and define

\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}\coloneqq\langle\!\langle\partial_{\mu}u_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}|\left(1-\ket{u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}}\langle\!\langle u_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}|\right)\ket{\partial_{\nu}u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}}.

(10)

Unlike
\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{RR}}
, the tensor
\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}
is generally complex and need not be positive.
Its gauge ambiguity is broader:
it is invariant under the
\mathrm{GL}\left(1,\mathbb{C}\right)
transformation

\ket{u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}}\to e^{f(\bm{\lambda})}\ket{u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}},\qquad\langle\!\langle u_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}|\to e^{-f(\bm{\lambda})}\langle\!\langle u_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}|,

(11)

with an arbitrary complex function
f(\bm{\lambda})\in\mathbb{C}
,
which preserves the biorthonormality condition in Eq. (
9
).

In the Hermitian limit, where left and right eigenstates coincide, Eqs. (
8
) and (
10
) reduce to the same object in Eq. (
2
).
Away from the Hermitian limit, however, they characterize different aspects of non-Hermitian quantum geometry.
One of the central purposes of this work is to compare these two quantities and clarify which one captures the non-Hermitian skin effect.
While we may also introduce a quantum metric tensor solely in terms of left eigenstates, it should be essentially equivalent to the right-right metric tensor
\chi^{\rm RR}_{\mu\nu}
.

In the following, we mainly focus on diagonal components associated with a single parameter.
For brevity, we write

\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}(\lambda)\coloneqq\chi_{\lambda\lambda}^{\mathrm{RR}},\qquad\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}(\lambda)\coloneqq\chi_{\lambda\lambda}^{\mathrm{LR}}.

(12)

The relevant parameter will be the crystal momentum under the periodic boundary conditions, or the coordinate that parametrizes the generalized Brillouin zone under the open boundary conditions.

II.3
Plane-wave and internal contributions

For lattice systems, it is useful to distinguish between the geometry associated with the spatial profile of a wave function and that associated with internal degrees of freedom such as sublattice structure.
Suppose that the full right eigenstate factorizes as

\ket{\psi_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}(\bm{\lambda})}=\ket{\phi^{\mathrm{R}}(\bm{\lambda})}\otimes\ket{u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}(\bm{\lambda})},

(13)

where
\ket{\phi^{\mathrm{R}}}
represents the plane-wave part and
\ket{u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}}
the internal part.
Similarly, let the left eigenstate factorize as

\langle\!\langle\psi_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}(\bm{\lambda})|=\langle\!\langle\phi^{\mathrm{L}}(\bm{\lambda})|\otimes\langle\!\langle u_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}(\bm{\lambda})|,

(14)

with

\braket{\phi^{\mathrm{R}}|\phi^{\mathrm{R}}}=\langle\!\langle\phi^{\mathrm{L}}|\phi^{\mathrm{R}}\rangle=1.

(15)

Then, the quantum metric tensors decompose additively into plane-wave and internal contributions:

\displaystyle\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{RR}}

\displaystyle=\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{RR,\,pw}}+\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{RR,\,int}},

(16)

\displaystyle\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}

\displaystyle=\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{LR,\,pw}}+\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{LR,\,int}},

(17)

with

\displaystyle\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{RR,\,pw}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\braket{\partial_{\mu}\phi^{\mathrm{R}}|\left(1-\ket{\phi^{\mathrm{R}}}\bra{\phi^{\mathrm{R}}}\right)|\partial_{\nu}\phi^{\mathrm{R}}},

(18)

\displaystyle\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{RR,\,int}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\braket{\partial_{\mu}u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}|\left(1-\ket{u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}}\bra{u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}}\right)|\partial_{\nu}u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}},

(19)

\displaystyle\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{LR,\,pw}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\langle\!\langle\partial_{\mu}\phi^{\mathrm{L}}|\left(1-\ket{\phi^{\mathrm{R}}}\langle\!\langle\phi^{\mathrm{L}}|\right)\ket{\partial_{\nu}\phi^{\mathrm{R}}},

(20)

\displaystyle\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{LR,\,int}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\langle\!\langle\partial_{\mu}u_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}|\left(1-\ket{u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}}\langle\!\langle u_{n}^{\mathrm{L}}|\right)\ket{\partial_{\nu}u_{n}^{\mathrm{R}}}.

(21)

This distinction is especially relevant to the present problem.
The plane-wave contribution is already nontrivial even in models without internal degrees of freedom, and it is directly sensitive to real-space localization.
For an extended plane wave under periodic boundary conditions, it typically grows with the system size.
However, as shown in Sec.
III
, under the open boundary conditions in non-Hermitian systems, such a plane wave can become localized as a consequence of the non-Hermitian skin effect, and the plane-wave contribution to the quantum geometry can then be governed by the corresponding localization length scale.
Specifically, in Sec.
III
, we analyze a model without internal degrees of freedom and show that the metric defined from right eigenstates detects the localization length associated with the non-Hermitian skin effect.
By contrast, the internal contribution becomes essential in multiband models, where the quantum geometry probes the parameter dependence of the internal eigenstate and can become singular at band-touching points.
As we show in Secs.
IV
and
V
, in the generalized Brillouin-zone formulation, it can also diagnose nonanalytic structures such as cusps.
This framework allows us to compare the metric defined solely from right eigenstates with the biorthogonal one on equal footing throughout this work.

II.4
Symmetry

Symmetry of the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian
H\left(\bm{\lambda}\right)
constrains its eigenstates and associated quantum metrics.
Specifically, suppose that
H\left(\bm{\lambda}\right)
respects

\mathcal{T}H^{*}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right)\mathcal{T}^{-1}=H\left(-\bm{\lambda}\right),\quad\mathcal{T}\mathcal{T}^{*}=+1,

(22)

with a unitary matrix
\mathcal{T}
.
This symmetry can be interpreted as time-reversal symmetry when
\bm{\lambda}
represents momenta, or its generalization in non-Bloch band theory.
Notably, the models studied in this work—the Hatano-Nelson model and the non-Hermitian Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model—respect time-reversal symmetry in Eq. (
22
).
As a result of this symmetry, a right eigenstate
\ket{u_{n}^{\rm R}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right)}
in Eq. (
5
) satisfies

H\left(-\bm{\lambda}\right)\mathcal{T}\ket{u_{n}^{\rm R}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right)}^{*}=(E_{n}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right))^{*}\mathcal{T}\ket{u_{n}^{\rm R}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right)}^{*},

(23)

showing that
\mathcal{T}\ket{u_{n}^{\rm R}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right)}^{*}
is a right eigenstate of
H\left(-\bm{\lambda}\right)
with complex eigenenergy
(E_{n}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right))^{*}
.
Accordingly, we have

\ket{u_{n}^{\rm R}\left(-\bm{\lambda}\right)}=\mathcal{T}\ket{u_{n}^{\rm R}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right)}^{*}

(24)

under the appropriate choice of the gauge.
Similarly, a left eigenstate
|u_{n}^{\rm L}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right)\rangle\!\rangle
in Eq. (
6
) satisfies

H^{{\dagger}}\left(-\bm{\lambda}\right)\mathcal{T}|u_{n}^{\rm L}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right)\rangle\!\rangle^{*}=E_{n}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right)\mathcal{T}|u_{n}^{\rm L}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right)\rangle\!\rangle^{*},

(25)

leading to

|u_{n}^{\rm L}\left(-\bm{\lambda}\right)\rangle\!\rangle=\mathcal{T}|u_{n}^{\rm L}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right)\rangle\!\rangle^{*}.

(26)

Consequently, the quantum metric tensors defined in Eqs. (
8
) and (
9
) satisfy

\displaystyle(\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\rm RR}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right))^{*}=\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\rm RR}\left(-\bm{\lambda}\right),

(27)

\displaystyle(\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\rm LR}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right))^{*}=\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\rm LR}\left(-\bm{\lambda}\right).

(28)

It follows that the real parts of the quantum metrics are even functions of
\bm{\lambda}
, whereas the imaginary parts are odd.

III
Hatano-Nelson model

As a paradigmatic example that exhibits the non-Hermitian skin effect, we consider the Hatano-Nelson model
21
;
*Hatano-Nelson-97
.
We show that the quantum geometric tensor defined solely from right eigenstates detects the localization length scale of the skin effect, whereas the biorthogonal quantum geometric tensor constructed from both right and left eigenstates does not.

We study the single-particle Hamiltonian
H
on a chain of length
L
.
Under the periodic boundary conditions, we take

H_{\mathrm{PBC}}=\sum_{n=1}^{L}\left(\frac{J+\gamma}{2}\,\lvert n+1\rangle\langle n\rvert+\frac{J-\gamma}{2}\,\lvert n\rangle\langle n+1\rvert\right),

(29)

with
\lvert L+1\rangle\coloneqq\lvert 1\rangle
.
Here,
\ket{n}
’s (
n=1,2,\cdots,L
) denote the single-particle sites.
Under the open boundary conditions, we instead consider

H_{\mathrm{OBC}}=\sum_{n=1}^{L-1}\left(\frac{J+\gamma}{2}\,\lvert n+1\rangle\langle n\rvert+\frac{J-\gamma}{2}\,\lvert n\rangle\langle n+1\rvert\right).

(30)

Here,
J\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the amplitude of the reciprocal hopping while
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
characterizes the amplitude of the nonreciprocal hopping.

III.1
Periodic boundary conditions

Under the periodic boundary conditions, both right and left eigenstates are extended Bloch waves,

\lvert u(k)\rangle=\lvert u(k)\rangle\!\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{n=1}^{L}e^{\text{i}kn}\,\lvert n\rangle,\quad k\in[0,2\pi).

(31)

Since their derivative with respect to
k
is

\partial_{k}\lvert u(k)\rangle=\frac{\text{i}}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{n=1}^{L}n\,e^{\text{i}kn}\,\lvert n\rangle,

(32)

it follows that

\displaystyle\langle u(k)\rvert\partial_{k}u(k)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{i}}{L}\sum_{n=1}^{L}n\eqqcolon\text{i}\langle n\rangle,

(33)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{k}u(k)\rvert\partial_{k}u(k)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{n=1}^{L}n^{2}\eqqcolon\langle n^{2}\rangle.

(34)

The quantum metric then reads

\displaystyle\chi(k)

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{k}u(k)\rvert\bigl(1-\lvert u(k)\rangle\langle u(k)\rvert\bigr)\lvert\partial_{k}u(k)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle n^{2}\rangle-\langle n\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{L^{2}-1}{12}.

(35)

Thus, the quantum metric
\chi
gives the variance of the center of the Bloch wave and scales as
\chi\propto L^{2}
for large
L
, which reflects the delocalization of the eigenstate over the entire system.

III.2
Open boundary conditions

Under the open boundary conditions, the nonreciprocal hopping can be effectively removed by a nonunitary similarity transformation (i.e., imaginary gauge transformation in Ref.
21
;
*Hatano-Nelson-97
).
Introducing

V\coloneqq\operatorname{diag}\left(b,b^{2},\dots,b^{L}\right),\qquad b\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{J+\gamma}{J-\gamma}},

(36)

we have

V^{-1}H_{\mathrm{OBC}}V=\frac{\sqrt{J^{2}-\gamma^{2}}}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{L-1}\left(\lvert n+1\rangle\langle n\rvert+\lvert n\rangle\langle n+1\rvert\right).

(37)

This transformed Hamiltonian is Hermitian, and its eigenstates are extended. Consequently, the right and left eigenstates of the original non-Hermitian Hamiltonian, which are obtained by applying
V^{-1}
and
V
to the extended eigenstates, acquire exponential factors
b^{\pm n}
.
It is therefore natural to introduce the complex momentum

k+\text{i}g,\qquad g\coloneqq\log b=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{J+\gamma}{J-\gamma},

(38)

where
g
characterizes the skin localization and
1/\lvert g\rvert
plays the role of the localization length.

Accordingly, the right and left eigenstates are obtained as

\displaystyle\lvert u^{\rm R}(k,g)\rangle

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{n=1}^{L}e^{(\text{i}k+g)n}\,\lvert n\rangle,

(39)

\displaystyle\lvert u^{\rm L}(k,g)\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{n=1}^{L}e^{(\text{i}k-g)n}\,\lvert n\rangle.

(40)

These states satisfy the biorthogonal normalization condition

\langle\!\langle u^{\rm L}(k,g)|u^{\rm R}(k,g)\rangle=1,

(41)

although neither the right nor the left eigenstate is individually normalized in the usual sense (see below).
Notably, the states
\lvert u^{\rm R}(k,g)\rangle
and
\lvert u^{\rm L}(k,g)\rangle\!\rangle
in Eqs. (
39
) and (
40
) can be regarded as being parameterized by
k
and
g
.
We thus evaluate the quantum geometric tensor in the
\left(k,g\right)
parameter space, as detailed below.
It should also be noted that Eqs. (
39
) and (
40
) do not yield exact eigenstates of Eq. (
30
).
Nevertheless, they capture the leading contributions of the skin states,
in the same manner as the non-Bloch band theory
98
;
100
;
see Sec.
V
for further details.

III.2.1
Quantum geometric tensor from right eigenstates

We first consider the quantum geometric tensor defined solely from the right eigenstate:

\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{RR}}\coloneqq\langle\partial_{\mu}u^{\rm R}\rvert\bigl(1-\lvert u^{\rm R}\rangle\langle u^{\rm R}\rvert\bigr)\lvert\partial_{\nu}u^{\rm R}\rangle,\quad\mu,\nu\in\{k,g\}.

(42)

For this purpose, we normalize the right eigenstate by itself (i.e.,
\braket{u^{\rm R}|u^{\rm R}}=1
):

\lvert u^{\rm R}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z(g)}}\sum_{n=1}^{L}e^{(\text{i}k+g)n}\,\lvert n\rangle,

(43)

with

Z(g)\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{L}e^{2gn}=\frac{e^{2g}\bigl(e^{2gL}-1\bigr)}{e^{2g}-1}.

(44)

We then obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\lvert u^{\rm R}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{i}}{\sqrt{Z(g)}}\sum_{n=1}^{L}n\,e^{(\text{i}k+g)n}\,\lvert n\rangle,

(45)

\displaystyle\partial_{g}\lvert u^{\rm R}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z(g)}}\sum_{n=1}^{L}n\,e^{(\text{i}k+g)n}\,\lvert n\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{Z(g)^{3/2}}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{L}n\,e^{2gn}\right)\left(\sum_{n=1}^{L}e^{(\text{i}k+g)n}\,\lvert n\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=-\text{i}\,\partial_{k}\lvert u^{\rm R}\rangle-\langle n\rangle_{g}\lvert u^{\rm R}\rangle,

(46)

where we introduce

\langle f(n)\rangle_{g}\coloneqq\frac{1}{Z(g)}\sum_{n=1}^{L}e^{2gn}f(n).

(47)

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\langle n\rangle_{g}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{e^{2g}-1}+\frac{Le^{2gL}}{e^{2gL}-1},

(48)

\displaystyle\langle n^{2}\rangle_{g}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{e^{2g}-1}+\frac{2}{(e^{2g}-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{2Le^{2gL}}{(e^{2g}-1)(e^{2gL}-1)}+\frac{L^{2}e^{2gL}}{e^{2gL}-1}.

(49)

Using these expressions, we find

\displaystyle\chi_{kk}^{\mathrm{RR}}

\displaystyle=\langle n^{2}\rangle_{g}-\langle n\rangle_{g}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2g}}{(e^{2g}-1)^{2}}-\frac{L^{2}e^{2gL}}{(e^{2gL}-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[L\to\infty]{}\frac{1}{4\sinh^{2}g},

(50)

and

\chi_{gg}^{\mathrm{RR}}=\text{i}\chi_{kg}^{\mathrm{RR}}=-\text{i}\chi_{gk}^{\mathrm{RR}}=\chi_{kk}^{\mathrm{RR}}.

(51)

Hence, the quantum geometric tensor takes the compact form,

\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}=\chi_{kk}^{\mathrm{RR}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\text{i}\\
\text{i}&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\chi_{kk}^{\mathrm{RR}}\xrightarrow[L\to\infty]{}\frac{1}{4\sinh^{2}g}.

(52)

As in the case of the periodic boundary conditions, the quantum metric
\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}
describes the variance of the center of the right eigenstate with respect to the deformed distribution induced by the skin effect in Eq. (
47
).
Importantly, the localization length scale
g
due to the skin effect explicitly appears in
\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}
.
In this sense, the quantum metric defined solely from right eigenstates directly captures the non-Hermitian skin effect.

The corresponding Berry curvature in the complex-momentum space
\left(k,g\right)
is nonzero:

F_{kg}^{\mathrm{RR}}=-2\,\operatorname{Im}\chi_{kg}^{\mathrm{RR}}=2\chi_{kk}^{\mathrm{RR}}\xrightarrow[L\to\infty]{}\frac{1}{2\sinh^{2}g}.

(53)

Additionally, we have

F_{kg}^{\mathrm{RR}}=\partial_{k}\!\left(\text{i}\langle u^{\rm R}\rvert\partial_{g}u^{\rm R}\rangle\right)-\partial_{g}\!\left(\text{i}\langle u^{\rm R}\rvert\partial_{k}u^{\rm R}\rangle\right)=\partial_{g}\langle n\rangle_{g}.

(54)

Thus,
F_{kg}^{\mathrm{RR}}
measures the response of the center of the right eigenstate to the non-Hermitian deformation
g
, and it is therefore naturally tied to the emergence of the skin localization.

III.2.2
Biorthogonal quantum geometric tensor

We next consider the quantum geometric tensor constructed from both right and left eigenstates:

\chi_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{LR}}\coloneqq\langle\!\langle\partial_{\mu}u^{\rm L}\rvert\bigl(1-\lvert u^{\rm R}\rangle\langle\!\langle u^{\rm L}\rvert\bigr)\lvert\partial_{\nu}u^{\rm R}\rangle,\quad\mu,\nu\in\{k,g\}.

(55)

Since we have

\partial_{g}\lvert u^{\rm R}\rangle=-\text{i}\,\partial_{k}\lvert u^{\rm R}\rangle,\quad\partial_{g}\lvert u^{\rm L}\rangle\!\rangle=-\text{i}\,\partial_{k}\lvert u^{\rm L}\rangle\!\rangle,

(56)

we obtain

\displaystyle\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}

\displaystyle=\chi_{kk}^{\mathrm{LR}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\text{i}\\
\text{i}&1\end{pmatrix},

(57)

\displaystyle\chi_{kk}^{\mathrm{LR}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{n=1}^{L}n^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{L}\sum_{n=1}^{L}n\right)^{2}=\frac{L^{2}-1}{12}.

(58)

In contrast to the right-right tensor,
\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}
is completely independent of
g
.
This is because
\ket{u^{\rm R}}
and
|u^{\rm L}\rangle\!\rangle
are localized at opposite boundaries.
Therefore, the biorthogonal quantum metric does not detect the skin localization scale.

The corresponding biorthogonal Berry curvature also vanishes:

F_{kg}^{\mathrm{LR}}=\partial_{k}\!\left(\text{i}\langle\!\langle u^{\rm L}\rvert\partial_{g}u^{\rm R}\rangle\right)-\partial_{g}\!\left(\text{i}\langle\!\langle u^{\rm L}\rvert\partial_{k}u^{\rm R}\rangle\right)=0,

(59)

which is consistent with the absence of any
g
dependence in
\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}
. We also note that, unlike in the right-right construction, the relation

F_{kg}^{\mathrm{LR}}=-2\,\operatorname{Im}\chi_{kg}^{\mathrm{LR}}

(60)

does not generally hold.

In summary, the Hatano-Nelson model provides a simple and transparent setting in which two distinct notions of quantum geometry lead to qualitatively different conclusions.
The quantum geometric tensor defined solely from right eigenstates faithfully captures the localization length scale induced by the skin effect, whereas the biorthogonal quantum geometric tensor does not.
This contrast suggests that the physically relevant notion of quantum geometry in non-Hermitian systems depends crucially on how the underlying eigenstates are used to construct it.
In the presence of the skin effect, the plane-wave contributions should exhibit behavior similar to the Hatano-Nelson model even in generic non-Hermitian systems (see also Sec.
II.3
).
Thus, we hereafter focus on the internal contributions to the quantum metric in multiband non-Hermitian systems, especially the non-Hermitian Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model.

IV
Non-Hermitian Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model

Next, we consider a non-Hermitian, nonreciprocal extension of the Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model
77
.
This paradigmatic two-band model has played a central role in the study of the non-Hermitian skin effect and the restoration of the bulk-boundary correspondence
40
;
98
;
35
;
100
.
Here, we investigate the quantum metric
associated with its Bloch and non-Bloch eigenstates, and compare the metric constructed solely from right eigenstates with the biorthogonal metric defined from both right and left eigenstates.
We show that both quantities diverge as the energy gap closes, and that their critical singularities are qualitatively different.
Notably, as a consequence of the non-Hermitian skin effect, the gap-closing points depend on the different boundary conditions.
We demonstrate that the quantum metrics can identify the gap-closing points appropriate to each boundary conditions.
While we focus on the simple case in this section, we shortly study more generic cases where the generalized Brillouin zone exhibits singularities in Sec.
V
.

IV.1
Model

We consider the non-Hermitian Su-Schrieffer-Heeger Hamiltonian
40
;
98
;
35
;
100
, whose Bloch Hamiltonian is given as

H(k)=\begin{pmatrix}0&p(k)\\
q(k)&0\end{pmatrix},

(61)

with

\displaystyle p(k)

\displaystyle=t_{1}+\frac{\gamma}{2}+t_{2}e^{-\text{i}k},

(62)

\displaystyle q(k)

\displaystyle=t_{1}-\frac{\gamma}{2}+t_{2}e^{\text{i}k}.

(63)

While
t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
describe the Hermitian hopping amplitudes,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
describes the non-Hermitian, nonreciprocal hopping amplitude.
The corresponding eigenenergies are

\displaystyle E_{\pm}(k)

\displaystyle=\pm\sqrt{p(k)\,q(k)}

\displaystyle=\pm\sqrt{\left(t_{1}+t_{2}\cos k\right)^{2}+\left(t_{2}\sin k+\frac{\text{i}\gamma}{2}\right)^{2}}.

(64)

In what follows, we focus on the lower band,

E_{-}(k)=-\sqrt{p(k)\,q(k)}.

(65)

While Ref.
12
also investigated this model under the periodic boundary conditions, we here focus primarily on the open boundary conditions, highlighting the non-Hermitian skin effect.

It is convenient to introduce

z(k)\coloneqq\frac{p(k)}{q(k)}.

(66)

Then, a right eigenstate of the lower band is written as

\ket{u_{-}^{\mathrm{R}}(k)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|z(k)|}}\begin{pmatrix}-\sqrt{z(k)}\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(67)

where the square-root branch is chosen continuously away from singular points.
This state is normalized with respect to the usual inner product:

\braket{u_{-}^{\mathrm{R}}(k)|u_{-}^{\mathrm{R}}(k)}=1.

(68)

The corresponding left eigenstate is given as

\langle\!\langle u_{-}^{\mathrm{L}}(k)|=\frac{\sqrt{1+|z(k)|}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{z(k)}}&1\end{pmatrix},

(69)

which satisfies the biorthonormality condition

\langle\!\langle u_{-}^{\mathrm{L}}(k)\ket{u_{-}^{\mathrm{R}}(k)}=1.

(70)

IV.2
Quantum geometry

IV.2.1
Metric defined from right eigenstates

We first consider the quantum metric defined solely from the normalized right eigenstate:

\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}(k)=\braket{\partial_{k}u_{-}^{\mathrm{R}}(k)|\partial_{k}u_{-}^{\mathrm{R}}(k)}-\left|\braket{u_{-}^{\mathrm{R}}(k)|\partial_{k}u_{-}^{\mathrm{R}}(k)}\right|^{2}.

(71)

A straightforward calculation from Eq. (
67
) yields

\displaystyle\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\partial_{k}\sqrt{z(k)}\right|^{2}}{\left(1+|z(k)|\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|z(k)|}{4\left(1+|z(k)|\right)^{2}}\left|\partial_{k}\log z(k)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left|\dfrac{p(k)}{q(k)}\right|}{\left(1+\left|\dfrac{p(k)}{q(k)}\right|\right)^{2}}\left|\frac{\partial_{k}p(k)}{p(k)}-\frac{\partial_{k}q(k)}{q(k)}\right|^{2}.

(72)

This quantity is real and nonnegative, and it is thus a natural analog of the
Fubini-Study metric for wave functions in Hermitian systems.

IV.2.2
Biorthogonal metric

We next consider the biorthogonal metric defined from both right and left eigenstates:

\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}(k)=\langle\!\langle\partial_{k}u_{-}^{\mathrm{L}}(k)|\left(1-\ket{u_{-}^{\mathrm{R}}(k)}\langle\!\langle u_{-}^{\mathrm{L}}(k)|\right)\ket{\partial_{k}u_{-}^{\mathrm{R}}(k)}.

(73)

Using Eqs. (
67
) and (
69
), we find

\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}(k)=-\frac{1}{16}\left(\frac{\partial_{k}p(k)}{p(k)}-\frac{\partial_{k}q(k)}{q(k)}\right)^{2}.

(74)

Unlike
\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}
, the quantity
\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}
is generally complex
and is not positive definite.
Nevertheless, it is invariant under the

\mathrm{GL}(1,\mathbb{C})
gauge transformation

\ket{u_{-}^{\mathrm{R}}(k)}\to e^{f(k)}\ket{u_{-}^{\mathrm{R}}(k)},\quad\langle\!\langle u_{-}^{\mathrm{L}}(k)|\to e^{-f(k)}\langle\!\langle u_{-}^{\mathrm{L}}(k)|,

(75)

with an arbitrary complex function
f(k)\in\mathbb{C}
,
preserving the biorthonormality condition

\langle\!\langle u_{-}^{\mathrm{L}}(k)\ket{u_{-}^{\mathrm{R}}(k)}=1
.

IV.3
Periodic boundary conditions

Under the periodic boundary conditions, momentum is real (i.e.,
k\in\mathbb{R}
), and the quantum metrics are given directly by
Eqs. (
72
) and (
74
).
The metrics diverge for either
p(k^{*})=0
or
q(k^{*})=0
for some
k^{*}\in[0,2\pi)
.
Since the band energies are given by

E_{\pm}(k)=\pm\sqrt{p(k)q(k)}
, this condition is precisely the condition for
the energy gap to close.
Thus, both
\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}
and

\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}
detect the band-touching point, although they do so with
different singular behaviors, as shown below.

IV.3.1
Critical behavior of
\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}

Suppose that there exists
k^{*}\in[0,2\pi)
such that

p(k^{*})=0,\qquad q(k^{*})\neq 0.

(76)

In the generic case, we may expand

p(k)\simeq p^{\prime}(k^{*})(k-k^{*})\quad\left(p^{\prime}(k^{*})\neq 0\right).

(77)

Then, Eq. (
72
) gives

\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}(k)\simeq\frac{|p^{\prime}(k^{*})|}{4|q(k^{*})|}\frac{1}{|k-k^{*}|}.

(78)

Therefore, the right-right quantum metric diverges with a first-order pole in
the momentum deviation.
The same conclusion holds for
q(k^{*})=0
and
p(k^{*})\neq 0
, with the roles
of
p
and
q
interchanged.

IV.3.2
Critical behavior of
\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}

Under the same assumption
p(k^{*})=0
and
q(k^{*})\neq 0
, Eq. (
74
) yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}(k)

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{1}{16}\frac{1}{(k-k^{*})^{2}},

(79)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\,\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}(k)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{8}\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{q^{\prime}\left(k^{*}\right)}{q\left(k^{*}\right)}\right]\frac{1}{k-k^{*}}.

(80)

Notably, the power-law divergence of
\mathrm{Re}\,\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}
is independent of the detailed forms of
p(k)
and
q(k)
.
The biorthogonal metric exhibits a second-order pole, which is more
singular than the first-order divergence of
\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}
.
This distinction is one of the central observations of the present analysis:
although both notions of quantum geometry are sensitive to the gap closing,
the critical exponents of their singularities are different.

IV.4
Open boundary conditions

We next turn to the open boundary conditions.
In non-Hermitian systems, the
appropriate bulk description is formulated not necessarily on the ordinary Brillouin zone,
but on the generalized Brillouin zone, where momentum is complex valued.
According to the non-Bloch band theory
98
;
100
,
we have in the present model

\left|e^{\text{i}k}\right|=b\coloneqq\sqrt{\left|\frac{t_{1}-\gamma/2}{t_{1}+\gamma/2}\right|}.

(81)

We therefore introduce

\beta\coloneqq e^{\text{i}k}=be^{\text{i}\theta},\qquad\theta\in[0,2\pi),

(82)

and regard
\theta
as a natural momentum variable on the generalized Brillouin zone.
While
b
is a constant in the present case, it generally depends on
\theta
, as discussed in Sec.
V
.

In terms of
\beta
, the functions
p
and
q
become (for
t_{3}=0
)

\displaystyle p(\beta)

\displaystyle=t_{1}+\frac{\gamma}{2}+t_{2}\beta^{-1}=t_{1}+\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{t_{2}}{b}e^{-\text{i}\theta},

(83)

\displaystyle q(\beta)

\displaystyle=t_{1}-\frac{\gamma}{2}+t_{2}\beta=t_{1}-\frac{\gamma}{2}+t_{2}be^{\text{i}\theta}.

(84)

Then, we consider the quantum metrics under the open boundary conditions by replacing
k
with
\theta
and

p(k),q(k)
with
p(\beta),q(\beta)
:

\displaystyle\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\frac{\left|\dfrac{p(\beta)}{q(\beta)}\right|}{\left(1+\left|\dfrac{p(\beta)}{q(\beta)}\right|\right)^{2}}\left|\frac{\partial_{\theta}p(\beta)}{p(\beta)}-\frac{\partial_{\theta}q(\beta)}{q(\beta)}\right|^{2},

(85)

\displaystyle\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{16}\left(\frac{\partial_{\theta}p(\beta)}{p(\beta)}-\frac{\partial_{\theta}q(\beta)}{q(\beta)}\right)^{2}.

(86)

Note that
p(\beta)
and
q(\beta)
are now functions of
\theta
[see Eqs. (
83
) and (
84
)],
and that
\partial_{\theta}p(\beta)
and
\partial_{\theta}q(\beta)
should be understood as

\partial_{\theta}p(\beta)=\frac{dp}{d\beta}\frac{d\beta}{d\theta},\quad\partial_{\theta}q(\beta)=\frac{dq}{d\beta}\frac{d\beta}{d\theta}.

(87)

These metrics diverge when we have either
p(\beta^{*})=0
or
q(\beta^{*})=0
for some

\beta^{*}
on the generalized Brillouin zone. In the present two-band model,
this is precisely the condition for the band gap under the open boundary conditions to close, because the non-Bloch band energies are again given by

E_{\pm}(\beta)=\pm\sqrt{p(\beta)\,q(\beta)}.

(88)

Thus, the same statement holds as in the periodic boundary conditions:
the quantum metrics generally diverge at the gap-closing points under the open boundary conditions, but their critical singularities are different for the choice of
\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}
or
\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}
.

IV.5
Discussion

The above analysis shows that the divergence of the quantum metric generally corresponds to the closure of the band gap, although the relevant gap-closing point depends on the boundary conditions.
Under the periodic boundary conditions, the singularity appears when the ordinary Bloch Hamiltonian becomes gapless for a real momentum.
Under the open boundary conditions, by contrast, the singularity instead appears when the non-Bloch Hamiltonian becomes gapless for a complex momentum on the generalized Brillouin zone.
The gap closing does not, however, necessarily cause the divergence of the quantum metrics.
In particular, when
p
and
q
vanish simultaneously, the quantum metrics remain finite, as shown in Sec.
V.4
.
In passing, we note that Eqs. (
83
) and (
84
) realize precisely this situation.

An important consequence is that the right-right and left-right constructions encode
different aspects of non-Hermitian criticality.
The right-right metric

\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}
remains a real, nonnegative quantity and generally exhibits a first-order pole.
By contrast, the biorthogonal quantity
\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}
is generally complex and
develops a second-order pole.
In this sense, while both quantities detect the same gap-closing conditions, they do not characterize them in the same manner.

V
Non-Bloch band theory

We now consider the extent to which the results obtained in Sec.
IV
persist in more general non-Hermitian Su-Schrieffer-Heeger-type models.
For example, upon including additional
hopping terms, the trajectory of complex momentum, namely, the generalized Brillouin zone, can acquire a more
intricate structure, possibly with nonanalytic points.
In this section, we demonstrate that such singular features are also encoded in the quantum geometry.
Specifically, we show that cusps on the generalized Brillouin zone (see Fig.
2
) are manifested as discontinuities in the quantum geometry.

These cusps are not, however, directly related to a topological phase transition.
At most, they merely play a role in forming the band structure that makes such a topological phase transition possible.
Therefore, one cannot, for instance, infer the existence of a cusp merely from the occurrence of a topological phase transition.
How, then, can this cusp in turn be detected?
In this work, we propose that the quantum metric provides a useful diagnostic for identifying the cusp.

At these cusps, the generalized Brillouin zone switches from one branch of the solutions to the eigenvalue equation,
E^{2}=pq

100
.
In Fig.

\beta

This construction reproduces the bulk or continuum part of the complex spectrum under the open boundary conditions,
except for the zero-energy states.

In passing, we note that nonanalytic points on the generalized Brillouin zone are not cusps in the strict mathematical sense, although they are often referred to as cusps in the literature on non-Bloch band theory (see, for example, Ref.
100
).
More precisely, in the mathematics of algebraic curves, a cusp is defined as a singular point at which a moving point on the curve must reverse direction.
By contrast, a typical nonanalytic point on the generalized Brillouin zone consists of two different smooth local branches with different tangential directions, which is more properly classified as a node.
In other words, a cusp is characterized by a single singular local branch, whereas a node is characterized by two transverse local branches.
In this work, we find that a node on the generalized Brillouin zone gives rise to a discontinuity in the quantum metric.
Still, following the conventional terminology in the non-Bloch band theory literature, we here refer to such nonanalytic points on the generalized Brillouin zone as cusps.

\beta
on the generalized Brillouin zone determine the topological invariant of the non-Hermitian Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model
98
.
In Fig.
3
(a),
we show these trajectories of
p(\beta)
and
q(\beta)
for
t_{1}=1/10
, with the remaining parameters chosen as in Eq. (
92
).
These trajectories show that
both
p(\beta)
and
q(\beta)
wind around the origin
in the complex
p
- and
q
-planes
as
\beta
encircles the origin in the complex
\beta
-plane, namely, along the generalized Brillouin zone.
The difference between the winding numbers of
p(\beta)
and
q(\beta)

is in one-to-one correspondence
with the existence of a pair of zero-energy edge modes, thereby providing the generalized bulk-boundary correspondence.
Plugging the same values of
\beta
into Eqs. (
85
) and (
86
), we obtain the quantum metrics.
Notably, Eqs. (
85
) and (
86
) involve derivatives of
p(\beta)
and
q(\beta)
with respect to
\theta
,
which mean
\partial_{\theta}p\left(\beta\left(\theta\right)\right)
and
\partial_{\theta}q\left(\beta\left(\theta\right)\right)
[recall that
\beta
itself depends on
\theta
; see Eq. (
93
)].
In this manner, we calculate the quantum metrics
\chi^{\rm RR}\left(\theta\right)
and
\chi^{\rm LR}\left(\theta\right)
along the generalized Brillouin zone.

At cusps of the generalized Brillouin zone [see, for example, Fig.

these derivatives become discontinuous;
the derivative taken from one side of a cusp

Following the above procedure, we calculate
\chi^{\rm RR}(\theta)
and
\chi^{\rm LR}(\theta)
, and confirm such discontinuities, as shown in Fig.
4
.
Here,
\theta
-derivatives are taken numerically, using the data points for
\beta

To identify the cusps in the generalized Brillouin zone, we first replot

b(\theta)=|\beta(\theta)|
in Fig.

\theta^{\times}_{0}=\pi,\quad\theta^{\times}_{1}\approx 0.64,\quad\theta^{\times}_{2}\approx 5.64=2\pi-\theta^{\times}_{1}.

(94)

In Fig.

\chi^{\rm RR}

\theta

.
In Fig.

.
Note that, while
\chi^{\rm RR}

\chi^{\rm LR}

The real part (plots in blue)

Figures

the metrics

The absence of such discontinuities for

\theta=\theta^{\times}_{1,2}

originates from time-reversal symmetry.
Indeed, the generalized non-Hermitian Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model in Eq. (
89

\left(H\left(\beta\right)\right)^{*}=H\left(\beta^{*}\right).

(95)

Then, as demonstrated in Sec.

,
both

and
\mathrm{Re}\,\chi^{\rm LR}

\theta\to-\theta

\theta=\pi
(and also
\theta=0
).
By contrast,
\mathrm{Re}\,\chi^{\rm LR}
is antisymmetric under
\theta\to-\theta
and hence compatible with the discontinuity at
\theta=\pi
.

V.3
Cusps and a pair of gapless points

Let us now turn to the case of
t_{1}=6/5
,
indicated in Fig.
1
(a) and
corresponding to
Fig.
2
(b) and Fig.
3
(b).
For this choice of the parameters,
while the trajectory of
\beta(\theta)
shows cusps, the corresponding spectrum becomes gapless.
Indeed, the spectrum possesses a pair of gapless points on the generalized Brillouin zone [Fig.
5
(a)], which corresponds to a pair of zeros of
q(\beta)
.
Notably, the locations of the cusps do not generally coincide with those of the gapless points.
The cusps appear at
\theta=\theta^{\times}_{0,1,2}

with

\theta^{\times}_{0}=\pi,\quad\theta^{\times}_{1}\approx 1.92,\quad\theta^{\times}_{2}\approx 4.36=2\pi-\theta^{\times}_{1};

(96)

see Fig.
2
(b) and Fig.
5
(a).
On the other hand, the gapless points of the spectrum are located at

\theta^{*}_{1}\approx 1.71,\quad\theta^{*}_{2}\approx 4.57=2\pi-\theta^{*}_{1}.

(97)

These two gapless points coincide with a pair of zeros of
q(\beta)
.
In Fig.
3
(b), we show the
trajectories of
p(\beta)
(plotted in blue) and
q(\beta)
(plotted in orange)
on the generalized Brillouin zone.
They indicate that the trajectory of
q(\beta)
crosses itself at the origin,
implying the presence of a pair of zeros of
q(\beta)
.
In Fig.
6
, we present the singular behaviors of
\chi^{\rm RR}(\theta)
and
\chi^{\rm LR}(\theta)

at these cusps and gapless points.
As noted before,
\chi^{\rm RR}(\theta)
is always real,
and here shows discontinuities at
\theta=\theta_{1,2}^{\times}
and a cusp
at
\theta=\pi
,
while it diverges at the gapless points
\theta=\theta_{1,2}^{*}
.

Recall that
q(\beta)
vanishes at these gapless points,
i.e.,
q(\beta_{1})=q(\beta_{2})=0
,
where
\beta_{1,2}\coloneqq\beta(\theta_{1,2}^{*})
.
On the contrary,
p(\theta)=p|_{\beta=\beta(\theta)}

remains nonzero even at
these gapless points
\theta=\theta_{1,2}^{*}
.
At a gapless point where either
p
or
q
vanishes,
the quantum metrics
\chi^{\rm RR}(\theta)
and
\chi^{\rm LR}(\theta)
should diverge, while the critical behavior depends on the choice of the metric, as also discussed in Sec.
IV
.
Assuming that
q(\theta)=q|_{\beta=\beta(\theta)}
can be expanded around one of the zeros

q(\theta_{n}^{*})=0
(
n=1,2
) as

q(\theta)\simeq q^{\prime}(\theta_{n}^{*})(\theta-\theta_{n}^{*})+O(\theta-\theta_{n}^{*})^{2},

(98)

where

\displaystyle q^{\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\theta}\left[q|_{\beta=\beta(\theta)}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{b^{\prime}(\theta)}{b(\theta)}+\text{i}\right)\left.\frac{dq}{d\beta}\right|_{\beta=\beta(\theta)}\beta(\theta),

(99)

we obtain
from Eq. (
85
)
the asymptotic form of
\chi^{\rm RR}(\theta)
as

\chi^{\rm RR}(\theta)\simeq\frac{1}{4}\left|\frac{q^{\prime}(\theta^{*}_{n})}{p(\theta^{*}_{n})}\frac{1}{\theta-\theta^{*}_{n}}\right|.

(100)

Thus,

\chi^{\rm RR}(\theta)
diverges as
1/|\theta-\theta^{*}_{n}|

around
\theta=\theta^{*}_{n}
.
Similarly, from Eq. (
86
), we obtain the asymptotic behavior of
\chi^{\rm LR}(\theta)
.
The real part of
\chi^{\rm LR}(\theta)

around
\theta=\theta^{*}_{n}
obeys

\mathrm{Re}\,\chi^{\rm LR}(\theta)\simeq-\frac{1}{16}\frac{1}{(\theta-\theta^{*}_{n})^{2}},

(101)

and hence diverges as
-1/(\theta-\theta^{*}_{n})^{2}
.
By contrast, the imaginary part of
\chi^{\rm LR}(\theta)
follows

\mathrm{Im}\,\chi^{\rm LR}(\theta)\simeq\frac{1}{8}{\rm Im}\left[\frac{p^{\prime}(\theta^{*}_{n})}{p(\theta^{*}_{n})}\right]\frac{1}{\theta-\theta^{*}_{n}},

(102)

diverging as
1/(\theta-\theta^{*}_{n})
, unlike the real part.

In Fig.
7
,
we replot the divergent behavior of
\chi^{\rm RR}
and
\chi^{\rm LR}

shown in Fig.
6
,
with improved numerical precision using
12538

\beta

To evaluate
\chi^{\rm RR}
and
\chi^{\rm LR}
,
we compute numerical derivatives on the basis of these
\beta
-points.
Then, on top of the numerical data,
we superpose the asymptotic expressions in Eqs. (
100
), (

), and (

).
These asymptotic curves are shown as the solid curves,
whereas the numerical data are plotted with the markers “

These results confirm that the critical behavior at gapless points of the generalized Brillouin zone depends qualitatively on the type of the quantum metrics under consideration.
To further verify these power laws, we also present a log-log plot of

\chi(\theta)
’s around a gapless point

In this plot, the slope of the asymptotes determines the exponent

that controls the divergence of

\chi(\theta)\propto\frac{1}{|\theta-\theta_{1}^{*}|^{\alpha}}.

From the plots in Fig.

and

V.4
Cancellation of the divergence

While we have so far assumed that either
p
or
q
vanishes at a gapless point,
there are cases in which both
p
and
q
vanish simultaneously.
In view of the expressions of the quantum metrics in Eqs. (
85
) and (
86
),
one may expect the corresponding critical behavior to be modified.
In this section, we demonstrate that this is indeed the case, and that the quantum metrics can remain finite even at a gapless point for
p=q=0
.
Specifically, we consider the parameter choice,

t_{2}=1,\quad t_{3}=\frac{1}{5},\quad\gamma_{1}=\frac{4}{3},\quad\gamma_{2}=0.

(104)

For this choice of the parameters, the energy gap closes at
t_{1}=13/10
, separating the topologically nontrivial and trivial gapped phases;
see Fig.
9
.

In Fig.
10
(a),
we find that
both
p(\theta)
and
q(\theta)
vanish at the gap-closing point
\theta=\theta^{*}=\pi
[i.e.,
p(\theta^{*})=q(\theta^{*})=0
].
Around this simultaneous zero,
we expand both
p(\theta)=p|_{\beta=\beta(\theta)}
and
q(\theta)=q|_{\beta=\beta(\theta)}
as

\displaystyle p(\theta)

\displaystyle=p^{\prime}(\theta^{*})(\theta-\theta^{*})+\frac{p^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{*})}{2}(\theta-\theta^{*})^{2}+O(\theta-\theta^{*})^{3},

(105)

\displaystyle q(\theta)

\displaystyle=q^{\prime}(\theta^{*})(\theta-\theta^{*})+\frac{q^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{*})}{2}(\theta-\theta^{*})^{2}+O(\theta-\theta^{*})^{3},

(106)

with

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(\theta)\coloneqq\frac{d}{d\theta}\left[p|_{\beta=\beta(\theta)}\right]

\displaystyle,~~q^{\prime}(\theta)\coloneqq\frac{d}{d\theta}\left[q|_{\beta=\beta(\theta)}\right];

(107)

\displaystyle p^{\prime\prime}(\theta)\coloneqq\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}\left[p|_{\beta=\beta(\theta)}\right]

\displaystyle,~~q^{\prime\prime}(\theta)\coloneqq\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}\left[q|_{\beta=\beta(\theta)}\right].

(108)

Although
q(\theta)
looks almost quadratic in Fig.
10
(a),
this is merely a superficial feature due to a large difference in magnitude between

p^{\prime}(\theta^{*})
and
q^{\prime}(\theta^{*})
.
Recall that in Eqs. (
85
) and (
86
),
the origin of the divergence is the combination

\frac{\partial_{\theta}p(\beta)}{p(\beta)}-\frac{\partial_{\theta}q(\beta)}{q(\beta)}=\frac{p^{\prime}(\theta)}{p(\theta)}-\frac{q^{\prime}(\theta)}{q(\theta)}.

(109)

Around
\theta=\theta^{*}
, these terms behave as

\displaystyle\frac{p^{\prime}(\theta)}{p(\theta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\theta-\theta^{*}}+\frac{p^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{*})}{2p^{\prime}(\theta^{*})}+O(\theta-\theta^{*}),

(110)

\displaystyle\frac{q^{\prime}(\theta)}{q(\theta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\theta-\theta^{*}}+\frac{q^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{*})}{2q^{\prime}(\theta^{*})}+O(\theta-\theta^{*}).

(111)

This shows that the leading-order, divergent contributions of order
{1/(\theta-\theta^{*})}
in Eq. (
109
) cancel exactly.
As a result, Eq. (
109
) converges to a constant:

\frac{\partial_{\theta}p(\beta)}{p(\beta)}-\frac{\partial_{\theta}q(\beta)}{q(\beta)}=\frac{p^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{*})}{2p^{\prime}(\theta^{*})}-\frac{q^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{*})}{2q^{\prime}(\theta^{*})}+O(\theta-\theta^{*}).

(112)

Accordingly, the asymptotic behaviors of the quantum metrics are obtained as

\displaystyle\chi^{\rm RR}(\theta)

\displaystyle\simeq\cfrac{\left|\cfrac{p^{\prime}(\theta^{*})}{q^{\prime}(\theta^{*})}\right|}{16\left(1+\left|\cfrac{p^{\prime}(\theta^{*})}{q^{\prime}(\theta^{*})}\right|\right)^{2}}\left|\frac{p^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{*})}{p^{\prime}(\theta^{*})}-\frac{q^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{*})}{q^{\prime}(\theta^{*})}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle={\rm const.}

(113)

and

\chi^{\rm LR}(\theta)\simeq-\frac{1}{64}\left(\frac{p^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{*})}{p^{\prime}(\theta^{*})}-\frac{q^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{*})}{q^{\prime}(\theta^{*})}\right)^{2}={\rm const.}

(114)

Figures
10
(b) and (c) show the behavior of

\chi^{\rm RR}
and
\chi^{\rm LR}

for
t_{1}=13/10
.
The quantum metric
\chi^{\rm RR}(\theta)
defined solely from right eigenstates exhibits no divergence at
\theta=\pi
.
As approaching the gapless point
\theta\rightarrow\pi
from either side,

\chi^{\rm RR}(\theta)
converges to a finite value consistent with
Eq. (
113
).
Similarly, the biorthogonal quantum metric
\chi^{\rm LR}(\theta)
does not show the divergence at
\theta=\pi
.

VI
Concluding remarks

In this work, we have highlighted various quantum geometrical aspects of the non-Hermitian skin effect.
Primarily, the skin effect manifests itself in the tail/plane-wave part of the wave function, as discussed in Sec.
III
.
Taking the Hatano-Nelson model as a prototypical example,
we have shown that the quantum metric
\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}
in Eq. (
8
)
defined solely from right eigenstates captures the skin effect.
By contrast, the biorthogonal quantum metric
\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}
in Eq. (
10
)
defined from both right and left eigenstates
is unaffected,
reproducing essentially the result under the periodic boundary conditions.
The difference between right and left eigenstates is also relevant to the Anderson transitions in non-Hermitian systems;
see, for example, Ref.
23
.
It is worthwhile to revisit such transitions from the perspective of quantum geometry.

The sensitivity of non-Hermitian systems to boundary conditions
becomes even more pronounced in models with internal degrees of freedom,
as illustrated by the non-Hermitian, non-reciprocal Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model in Secs.
IV
and
V
.
In the Hermitian case,
the appearance of zero-energy edge states under the open boundary conditions
is attributed to nontrivial topology of the eigenstates under the periodic boundary conditions (i.e., bulk-boundary correspondence).
In non-Hermitian systems, however,
both the spectrum and eigenstates are extremely sensitive to boundary conditions,
and this correspondence superficially breaks down.
To overcome this difficulty, we have employed the recipe of non-Bloch band theory
98
;
100
.
We have demonstrated that
the obtained expressions for the quantum metric, as in Eqs. (
72
) and (
74
)
for the periodic boundary conditions,
and Eqs. (
85
) and (
86
)
for the open boundary conditions,
has a structure compatible with this extremely sensitivity to the boundary conditions.

We have further focused on the open boundary conditions,
under which the generalized Brillouin zone plays a crucial role.
We have shown that nonanalytic points on the generalized Brillouin zone give rise to discontinuities in the quantum metrics.
Since the skin effect changes the entire structure of the spectrum and eigenstates
when the boundary conditions are switched from periodic to open,
both
\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}
and
\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}
are sensitive to various singularities on the generalized Brillouin zone.
As implied in the expressions in Eqs. (
85
) and (
86
),
both
\chi^{\mathrm{RR}}
and
\chi^{\mathrm{LR}}

diverge at an isolated zero of
p=p(\beta)
or
q=q(\beta)
,
which typically arises in the gapless case
studied in Sec.
V.3
.
On the other hand, at the gap closing where both
p
and
q
vanish,
the divergent contributions to the quantum metrics associated with the two zeros
can cancel,
resulting in regular behavior of
\chi
’s, as discussed in Sec.
V.4
.

Beyond the prototypical examples considered in this work, it is important to study how the present quantum geometric picture extends to more general non-Hermitian systems.
Generic non-Hermitian systems can host both localized skin modes and delocalized modes within the same spectrum (see, for example, Ref.
74
), and the resulting quantum geometry should reflect the coexistence of two qualitatively distinct contributions:
the former yields a finite contribution associated with the skin-localization length, whereas the latter gives a growing contribution characteristic of extended states (see also Sec.
III
).
Moreover, while this work has been restricted to one-dimensional systems, higher-dimensional non-Hermitian systems exhibit a much richer variety of skin effects
97
;
43
;
37
;
101
;
56
;
30
;
16
;
106
;
78
;
83
;
105
.
It is thus worthwhile to investigate how such higher-dimensional boundary localization phenomena are encoded in quantum geometry.
Furthermore, since quantum geometry remains a meaningful framework also in the presence of disorder and many-body interactions, extending the present approach to disordered and interacting non-Hermitian systems should also be worth investigating.

Finally, the quantum metric plays a significant role in various response and transport phenomena of Hermitian systems
102
;
17
.
The non-Hermitian quantum metrics studied in this work may also leave observable signatures in physical phenomena.
Specifically, it is significant to find natural physical observables that detect nonanalytic points of the generalized Brillouin zone, which are a feature unique to non-Hermitian systems, so that such singularities become experimentally accessible.
More broadly, it merits further investigation to elucidate which observables provide the most direct probes of quantum geometric effects in non-Hermitian systems.
