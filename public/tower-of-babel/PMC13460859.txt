Early metastatic BRAFV600E mutation melanoma without known primary lesion: a case report
Authors: Hao Wang, Yu-Wei Xu, Xi-Long Pan, Hong-Hong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Malignant melanoma (MM) has a poor prognosis and is rare in China.Case presentation:A 23-year-old man presented with an initial complaint of a mass under the left armpit without any symptoms. Multiple movable, merged, peanut-sized masses were found behind the left ear and a single peanut-sized mass in the left anterior chest and under the left armpit. These masses were medium-high in hardness upon palpation and were non-tender. Multiple black-brown flat moles, with diameters ranging from 2 to 4 mm, were observed on the anterior chest and abdomen; however, they did not meet any of the ABCD early diagnostic criteria for MM. Ultrasound revealed lymphadenopathy in the left neck and under the left armpit, with the mass under the left armpit subsequently excised. Histopathological results showed epithelioid cells with loosely arranged nuclei, melanin granules in the cytoplasm, and the following immunohistochemical markers: S-100 (+), HMB (partially+), Melan-A (+), SOX10 (+), P53 (locally+), and BRAFV600E mutation (+). Positron emission computed tomography (PET/CT) images revealed tumor metastases in multiple organs. After initiating treatment with Dabrafenib and Trametinib, the masses became smaller and fewer, with the one behind the left ear completely disappearing.Discussion:It is rare to diagnose such an early metastatic BRAFV600E mutation MM without a known primary lesion in such a young man in China. PET/CT is effective for detecting MM locations, providing evidence for staging, evaluating and monitoring treatment effectiveness. Multidisciplinary diagnostic methods should be the best choice to confirm the diagnosis of MM.Conclusion:Dabrafenib and Trametinib can be used as a standard of care for advanced BRAFV600E melanoma.

Introduction

Malignant melanoma (MM) is an extremely invasive and easily metastasizing tumor transformed from melanocytes[1–5]. MM can occur in many organs and tissues, with common occurrences in the skin and oral and nasal mucosa near the skin, and about 10% of cases occurring in the digestive tract, brain, reproductive mucosa, lens, iris, and choroid[1,2,6]. However, it is rare to diagnose MM with early metastasis without knowing the primary lesion[2,6]; for example, no significant lesion in the skin may be observed in young people whose moles have not met the ABCD method (A for asymmetry, B for border irregularity, C for color variegation, and D for diameter of 6 mm or more) proposed by Friedman et al[7,8], which is regarded as a useful tool for the early diagnosis of MM. This method has been expanded by Hazen et al[9] to the ABCDEF criteria (E for evolutionary change and F for funny-looking lesions), which help improve its diagnostic sensitivity. Historically a rare disease, the global MM incidence has been increasing across populations over time for several decades[3,4,10,11]. In 2020, about 325 000 persons (174 000 men and 151 000 women) were diagnosed with MM, and approximately 57 000 persons (32 000 men and 25 000 women) died of the disease worldwide. Additionally, 79.7% of new cases were older than 50 years [4]. MM is more common in men than women based on the incidence across world regions, except in Eastern and Western Africa, as well as Northern Europe and Melanesia, where MM incidence rates in women exceed those in men[4]. The highest MM incidence rates for both men (42/100 000 person-years) and women (31/100 000 person-years) are in Australia/New Zealand, while the lowest incidence rates are in most regions of Africa and Asia, with less than 1/100 000 person-years (except for Middle and Southern Africa, as well as Western Asia)[4].

The MM incidence in China is lower than the global average, but its incidence has been increasing recently, with more than 20 000 new cases diagnosed every year[2]. The etiology of MM is not completely understood. Exposure to UV radiation is the most significant risk factor for MM[4,5]. Age, gender, ethnicity, geographic location, outdoor activity, the habit of using sun-protection products, and a supportive environment, such as access to shade or wearing a hat, as well as injury and genetic factors, are also associated with MM incidence[3,4].

The highest MM mortality is 4 per 100 000 person-years in men and 2 per 100 000 person-years in women in Australia/New Zealand, whereas rates in most other regions are much lower, ranging from 0.2 to 1.0 per 100 000 person-years. However, the global share of MM deaths is disproportionately high in Asia and Africa compared to other world regions[4]. Despite being largely preventable, MM is the most lethal skin cancer due to its easy metastasis[3,4]. The 5-year survival rate of stage IV patients is only 4.6%[3].

The prognosis of MM can be improved by early diagnosis[5,6,12]. Visual aids can help screen for MM. For example, dermoscopy can have a sensitivity of 61%, and skin confocal technology can reach a sensitivity of 100%. AI-assisted diagnosis can provide a more accurate diagnosis of MM. With the development of genomics, BRAFV600E and NRAS mutations have been used in the clinical diagnosis of MM. There is still a lack of highly specific and sensitive biomarkers for its early diagnosis. We believe multidisciplinary diagnostic methods should be the best choice to confirm the diagnosis of MM for now. Therefore, we used a high-risk-factor questionnaire (HRFQ), histopathological diagnosis with immunohistochemical markers, gene mutation testing, and positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) together to confirm the diagnosis for a rare case of primary MM.

This case report has been presented in line with the SCARE checklist[13].

Patient information

A 23-year-old man presented to the surgery department at our hospital with an initial complaint, asking the surgeon to remove a mass under his left armpit on 13 November 2023. He did not experience tenderness or any other symptoms, though he had noticed movable masses, about the size of a soybean but with a texture not as hard as soybeans, behind his left ear and in the anterior chest 1 year ago. He felt the masses were getting bigger and harder over time. Recently, another larger mass appeared under his left armpit, which bothered him. According to the HRFQ data, he claimed he did not have any high-risk factors for MM, such as a history of exposure to UV radiation, long-term exposure to sunlight, or a family history of melanoma. He was a nonsmoker with no family history of cancer or any other diseases. Additionally, he mentioned that his mother was a full-time electric welder during her pregnancy with him, which indicated that he had long-term fetal exposure to the intensely harsh light produced by electric welding.

Clinical findings

Physical examination revealed multiple movable, merged peanut-sized masses behind the left ear and a single peanut-sized mass in the left anterior chest and under the left armpit, with a medium-high hardness to the touch but no tenderness. Multiple black-brown flat moles, with diameters ranging from 2 to 4 mm, were observed on the anterior chest and abdomen; however, they did not meet any of the ABCD criteria for the early diagnosis of MM. His body mass index was 23.88 kg/m2.

Laboratory testing showed a serum ferritin level of 450.35 µg/mL (reference range for adult men: 30–400 µg/mL) and a plasma fibrinogen level of 4.18 g/L (reference range for adult men: 2.5–4.0 g/L), with normal blood cell counts, biochemistry, and other tumor biomarkers. B-mode ultrasonography found lymphadenectasis in the left neck and under the left armpit, polyps in the gallbladder, nephrolith with hydronephrosis in the right kidney, crystallization in the left kidney, and prostatic calcification. Electrocardiography revealed ventricular premature beats with T-wave changes.

Pathohistological examination of the lymph node under the left armpit from an excisional biopsy based on hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining observed under a microscope at 10× (Fig. 1A), 20× (Fig. 1B), and 40× (Fig. 1C, arrows pointing to three typical MM cells) showed the following: epithelioid cells with obvious nuclei loosely arranged like a nest; melanin granules visible in the cytoplasm of some cells; and mitotic figures with prominent nucleoli and irregular nuclear shapes easily observed in some cells. Immunohistochemical biomarker tests found Ki-67 (40%+), S-100 (+), HMB (partially+), Melan-A (+), SOX10 (+), and P53 (locally+). Both the HE slides and immunohistochemical results suggested the diagnosis of MM. A gene mutation test showed that there was a BRAF mutation located in Exon 15: p.V600E (c.T1799A) in the lymph node tissue under the left armpit. There were about 70% MM tumor cells in the detected sample of lymph node tissue, and there were more than 2000 tumor cells in each slide. The Stratagene Mx3000P was used to detect the BRAF mutation using PCR-ARMS testing kits provided by Xiamen Ed Biopharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd.

In order to confirm the MM locations, an 18F-FDG PET/CT scan was performed on 25 November 2023. In Figure 2, image A showed there were many areas related to the changes after the surgery of MM: (1) a slightly high 18F-FDG metabolism in a flaky shape with an obscure boundary (SUVmax of 3.32) observed in the fat diastema under the left armpit, (2) multiple dense nodules with clear boundaries and obvious high 18F-FDG metabolism (SUVmax of 29.20) found in the fat diastema at the level of Cervical 1 and Thoracic 3, behind the left first rib, in the fat diastema of the left front chest, in the muscles of the right front chest, in the right abdomen at the level of Lumbar 2 near the lower edge, in the fat diastema of the front abdomen at the level of Lumbar 3 and right lower abdomen, in the gluteus minimus, etc., (3) multiple small nodules with slightly increased density and slightly high 18F-FDG metabolism (SUVmax of 3.89) found in the muscles of the left upper limb and in the fat of the right front chest, the left front and rear chest before the sternum, in the right front lower abdomen, in the upper inside of the right femur, and in the middle and lower front of the left femur, (4) lymph node metastases found in multiple lymph nodes behind the left ear, in area II, and in areas I and II of the right neck with obvious high 18F-FDG metabolism (SUVmax of 21.77), and (5) small nodes with clear boundaries and slightly high 18F-FDG metabolism (SUVmax of 5.94) found in the front sacrum, which were considered reactive hyperplasia of lymph nodes. The PET/CT images indicated that the tumors had already spread into many organs (this MM was at an advanced stage). Intervention

According to the request of the patient, surgery was performed to remove the lymph node mass under the left armpit on 15 November 2023. Considering his MM had already spread to many organs, as indicated by the PET/CT image A before treatment, he was prescribed Dabrafenib (150 mg bid, i.e., 300 mg/day) and Trametinib (2 mg qd, i.e., 2 mg/day) immediately after being discharged from our hospital. He has been consulting with an internal oncologist at another hospital every 3 months, as per the most recent follow-up on 22 June 2026.

Follow-up and outcomes

Based on the results of the pathohistological examination, immunohistochemical marker tests, a gene mutation test, PET/CT imaging, and the effective treatment, the patient was confirmed to be diagnosed with primary advanced BRAFV600E mutation MM, but without a known primary lesion. The moles had not met any of the ABCD early diagnosis criteria for MM. No changes, such as diameter and color of the moles, were observed during the follow-up period. The mass behind the left ear disappeared within a few weeks after taking Dabrafenib and Trametinib, according to self-examination, which was also confirmed by the FAPI PET/CT images B and C in Figure 2 performed on 9 January 2025, about 1 year after treatment. In addition, the masses were getting smaller and fewer when comparing images B and C with image A in Figure 2. According to the last active follow-up on 22 June 2026, the total survival time for this case, estimated from the initial finding of movable masses behind the left ear and in the anterior chest about 1 year before visiting our hospital, was approximately 55 months. He returned to work at the same job as before without demonstrating any side effects from the active immunotherapy and targeted therapy.

Discussions

MM is historically a rare disease in China[2–6]. This case is even much rarer based on its characteristics:

(1) The patient is Chinese, which genetically has a low risk of MM, but his MM shows early metastases into lymph nodes without a known primary lesion, such as no obvious lesion in the skin or mucosa. This is much different from most Chinese patients, who generally have the typical MM seen in the skin and tend to have MM in the limb skin (plantar, toe, finger end, and under nails) and mucosa in the nose, mouth, and upper and lower digestive tract[2,3,5,6].

(2) His age at diagnosis is 23 years old, which is much younger than the common incidence age of older than 50 years for MM, based on the GLOBOCAN 2020 database[4].

(3) The patient has no history of exposure to any high-risk factors for MM, except that he had long-term fetal exposure to the strongly harsh light produced by electrical welding, which fetal exposure is very rare but is the highly likely risk factor that may be associated with his MM diagnosed at such a young age without any family history.

(4) Initial complaint for the young man to see the doctor is the mass under the left armpit, not because of the multiple movable lymphadenectases behind the left ear and in the left anterior chest with a touch feeling like medium-high hardness, which he confirmed did not bother him at all; and he confirmed he did not have any history of cancer, MM, or any symptoms like tenderness or other uncomfortable complaints.

(5) This MM case only has multiple sporadic black-brown little flat moles (about 2–4 mm in diameter) on the anterior chest and abdomen, which moles have not met any of the ABCD early diagnosis criteria for MM, but the cancer cells in this case have already metastasized into many organs, which indicates this is very aggressive MM.

(6) The immunohistochemical biomarker tests show Ki-67 (40%+), S-100 (+), HMB (partially+), Melan-A (+), SOX10 (+), and P53 (locally+).

(7) A BRAF mutation is found in Exon 15: p.V600E (c.T1799A), which provides evidence for him to qualify for the treatment of targeted therapy and immunotherapy.

(8) The PET/CT results clearly show the MM locations in his body, providing additional evidence for the confirmation of this diagnosis of advanced MM and this advanced BRAFV600E mutation MM with early metastasis, having a very good response to Dabrafenib and Trametinib.

(9) This patient has already survived about 55 months without MM progression, which is longer than the median overall survival time of 13.6 months[11] and 15.9 months[14].

Early diagnosis is especially important for MM because of its poor prognosis. The early diagnosis is mainly based on the history of the disease and a body skin check for now. Researchers are trying to use AI, non-invasive radioscopy (visual-assisted diagnostic equipment), or other tools to help increase the early diagnosis of MM, but both the rates of misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis are high[3] due to the nature of the large skin area on the human body, complicated clinical symptoms and signs, lack of specificity, and the need for confirmation through pathological examinations. The results of pathohistology and cytology show diversity, especially in cases lacking pigment. To confirm MM locations, this case involved a PET/CT after surgery, which provided images clearly showing tumor locations and important evidence for staging, evaluating, and monitoring treatment effectiveness. Thus, PET/CT can help minimize the rates of misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis and eventually increase the MM survival rate.

We believe the multidisciplinary diagnostic methods, which combine AI-assisted histopathological diagnosis with dermoscopy, skin confocal technology, immunohistochemical markers, gene mutation tests, and PET/CT, should be the ideally innovative technique for the confirmation of MM diagnosis. In this young case, the combination of HRFQ, histopathological diagnosis with immunohistochemical markers, a gene mutation test, and PET/CT was used to confirm the diagnosis, which is still a good option for the current diagnosis of MM. Therefore, multidisciplinary diagnostic methods were suggested for the confirmation of MM diagnosis.

This MM shows a good response to the combined treatment of Dabrafenib, which is based on the immune-modulating effects targeting the BRAF mutation in MM, and Trametinib, which is an MEK inhibitor. Based on the latest follow-up, the patient has been taking Dabrafenib and Trametinib without showing any side effects; the mass behind the left ear had already disappeared within a few weeks after starting Dabrafenib and Trametinib; and some masses had disappeared while others were obviously shrinking, as evidenced by comparing the PET/CT images from 9 January 2025 after treatment to those from 25 November 2023 before treatment and surgery. All of this indicates that Dabrafenib and Trametinib are effective for this advanced MM with a BRAFV600E mutation, Ki-67 (40%+), S-100 (+), HMB (partially+), Melan-A (+), SOX10 (+), and P53 (locally+), even though no primary lesion was confirmed, which should be informative and educational for clinicians. Our findings confirm the reported results[10,11,14–17].

Limitations

MM is so rare in China. The etiology behind the increasing incidence of MM in the Chinese population is uncertain. No practical health education for the prevention of MM recurrence can be provided to this young man. His parents have no history of MM, which does not help determine whether the BRAFV600E mutation found in Exon 15 of his tumor is inheritable or not.

Conclusion

The combination of Dabrafenib and Trametinib is effective in treating advanced BRAFV600E mutation MM without confirmation of the primary lesion in the skin and mucosa. It suggests that this combination therapy can be used as a standard of care for advanced BRAFV600E mutation MM. PET/CT is effective for detecting MM locations, providing precise evidence for staging, evaluating, and monitoring treatment effectiveness. Multidisciplinary methods can be used for the confirmation of MM diagnosis, which helps minimize misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis rates, ultimately improving the MM survival rate.
