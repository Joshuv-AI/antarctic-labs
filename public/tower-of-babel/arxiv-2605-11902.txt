The Future of Scholarly Blogs: Scholarly Bloggers’ Perspectives on Long-Term Preservation
Catharina Ochsner, Heinz Pampel

Abstract




Abstract

Scholarly blogs have become an important venue for scholarly communication, yet they remain insufficiently integrated into digital research and information infrastructures, which places their long-term preservation and citability at risk. This study investigates what challenges German scholarly bloggers perceive concerning blog preservation and what requirements they articulate for a sustainable information infrastructure. Drawing on Star and Ruhleder’s (
56
) dimensions of information infrastructure as a theoretical lens, we conducted and qualitatively analyzed 13 semi-structured interviews with scholarly bloggers. The analysis reveals three connected themes. First, bloggers perceive a structural deficit in institutional responsibility and support: the long-term preservation of blogs is not systematically assumed by libraries, universities, or platforms, while bloggers are not sufficiently supported by their affiliated institutions. Second, bloggers articulate heterogeneous requirements like persistent identifiers, structured metadata, technical interoperability, and organizational sustainability. Third, governance preferences are characterized by distrust toward commercial and public infrastructures, compounded by concerns about geopolitical dependencies on non-European platforms. These findings demonstrate that no single centralized infrastructure can adequately address the diverse and context-dependent needs of bloggers. We argue for a decentralized information infrastructure for scholarly blogs and offer concrete recommendations for information infrastructure facilities, platform providers, bloggers and research performing organizations.

Keywords: Open Science; Scholarly Communication; Scholarly Blogs

Introduction

Within the broad landscape of scientific information dissemination, scholarly blogs are a distinct type of scholarly publication, that facilitate the communication of research both within scientific communities and to wider audiences
(
39
)
. Blogs are publicly accessible webpages on which authors publish diary-style content in reverse chronological order
(
42
)
. Blogs first emerged in the late 1990s
(
49
)
and gained prominence with the emergence of the Web 2.0 in the 2000’s
(
22
)
in which participatory digital environments are characterized by increasingly user-generated content
(
42
)
. Scholarly blogs are written by scholars or concerned with scholarly topics
(
37
)
and serve as venues through which research and research-related topics are circulated across disciplinary and institutional boundaries
(
39
)
. Scholars are often also blogging on Microblogs, which differentiate themselves from conventional blogs by disseminating information in more frequent posting intervals
(
38
)
and shorter content
(
48
)
. Therefore, blog posts on scholarly research are a lot more time and energy intensive
(
55
)
. Blogs have furthermore been found to be a channel for information dissemination in the LIS community, used by both LIS researchers and librarians
(
2
)
. Compared to traditional research outputs such as journal articles, conference proceedings, or books, blogs are accessible and low-cost to both scholars and readers and enable fast, informal, and open scholarly communication
(
7
;
23
)
. Whereas traditional scholarly output is routinely preserved for future scholars, students, and readers
(
4
)
, this is not yet true for scholarly blogs, which are still at risk of information loss
(
44
)
.

Existing research on the long term preservation of scholarly has been of quantitative nature and has described the extent to which scholarly blogs are already integrated into digital research and information infrastructures, meaning the shared distribution of social, organizational, and technical systems and activities that enable and support research practices
(
5
)
. Findings show, that while individual bloggers, libraries, and blogging platforms have made efforts to make scholarly blogs accessible and citable as scholarly output, there are no comprehensive best practices that ensure their long-term preservation. This lack of preservation leaves open questions as to what challenges scholarly bloggers face in integrating their blogs into digital research and information infrastructure, what a successful integration could look like and how an infrastructure could be governed
(
44
)
. To address this gap, we conducted 13 semi-structured interviews with German scholarly bloggers to identify their challenges and requirements in integration scholarly blogs into digital research and information infrastructures. This study is guided by the following research questions:

RQ1:
What challenges do scholarly bloggers identify in ensuring the preservation of their blogs?

RQ2:
Considering these challenges, according to scholarly bloggers…

RQ 2.1.
… what blog content should be preserved by an information infrastructure?

RQ 2.2.
… what are scholarly bloggers’ requirements for an information infrastructure?

RQ 2.3.
… how should an information infrastructure for scholarly blogs be governed?

This study contributes to the research on scholarly communication, infrastructure studies and digital preservation by providing empirical insights into the requirements of scholarly bloggers for the long-term preservation of their blogs. Furthermore, we examine the perceived roles and responsibilities of relevant stakeholders, including academic institutions, libraries, blogging platforms, and repositories. The findings are of particular relevance to experts working in information infrastructures, scholarly bloggers, and research performing and funding organizations. Following this introduction this study proceeds with an outline of the theoretical background, using Star and Ruhleder’s (
56
) dimensions of information infrastructure as a theoretical lens. We then present a description of methods, and the findings, followed by a discussion that answers the research questions and articulates the broader conceptual and practical implications of our results for the continued advancement of information infrastructures.

Preserving Scholarly Blogs

Digital preservation guarantees the long-term access to digital information, which has emerged as the primary source of the scholarly record, with libraries and archives playing a primary role in ensuring its access
(
4
;
41
)
. Digital preservation can be enacted through web archiving
(
36
)
, which plays a significant role in safeguarding cultural heritage and knowledge
(
34
)
. Preservation is always concerned with what content can and should be saved, since storage, hardware, and software need to be maintained
(
4
;
41
)
. In a prior study we collected a sample of 866 scholarly blogs and found that the blogs represented in that sample are not yet comprehensively integrated into digital research and infrastructures
(
44

. However, individual bloggers, blogging platforms and information infrastructure facilities are already engaging in efforts to make blogs long-term accessible and citable
(

. We discuss the most prevalent stakeholders below.

Stakeholders in Blog Preservation

Libraries

Libraries and archives are viewed as responsible for the access, preservation and curation of scholarship and research
(
4
)
. Especially academic libraries are tasked with providing access to research outputs for the students and researchers of their parent institutions
(
9
)
. Some libraries already include blogs in their catalogs. The German National Library (DNB), for example, has collected online publications since 2006. However, a comprehensive acquisition of websites through autopsy is not feasible due to limited financial resources. Access to harvested online publications and blogs is generally restricted to the DNB’s reading rooms because required licenses are lacking
(
58
)
. Long-term digital archiving in the DNB is conducted using the
koala
preservation system, which stores digital objects and their associated metadata and subsequently integrates them into the libraries catalog
(
13
)
. The DNB does not distinguish between scholarly and non-scholarly blogs during cataloging. As of August 2024, 289 scholarly blogs were indexed in the DNB
(
45
)
. Additionally, the DNB assigns International Standard Serial Numbers (ISSNs), a widely used Persistent Identifier (PID) for serial publications, to blogs listed in its catalog
(
12
;
44
)
.

Digital Archives and Libraries

Digital libraries are enhanced information storage and retrieval systems that store data and metadata. They enable the creation, search, and use of information and are created and maintained by communities for their community
(
4
)
. This makes digital libraries a combination of research, demonstration, and production systems
(
3
)
. A prominent method for implementing web archiving is the Internet Archive, which aims to create a digital library of web pages
(
33
;
34
)
. A concrete effort for the preservation of blogs has been made through Rogue Scholar. Rogue Scholar defines itself as a Diamond Open Access infrastructure that provides a dedicated digital archive for science blogs
(
26
)
. The service is based on the open-source repository software InvenioRDM
(
23
)
. As of November 2025, Rogue Scholar had archived 185 blogs
(
50
)
. The infrastructure preserves blog content on it’s website and assigns Digital Object Identifiers (DOIs) to blog posts. A DOI is a unique, persistent digital identifier for an object that enables reliable access to the object
(
15
)
. They are assigned via Crossref and DataCite and come with the automated retrieval and import of metadata
(
31
)
. All blogs archived by Rogue Scholar are licensed under a Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY)
(
51
)
. Rogue Scholar offers full-text search across all archived posts, thus improving discoverability
(
24
;
25
)
. The service also subscribes to the Internet Archive’s “Archive-It” program, enabling systematic long-term archiving of all participating blogs
(
52
)
. In November 2025, Rogue Scholar began the process of becoming a German non-profit organization to establish a more sustainable governance structure. Membership will not be required for archiving, but it will come with additional benefits
(
27
)
.

Method

Data Collection

Semi-structured Interviews

To systematically investigate the research questions, we adopted an exploratory qualitative design based on semi-structured interviews. Semi-structured interviews are widely used in qualitative research and are particularly suitable when the aim is to explore specific phenomena while maintaining flexibility in the interaction with participants
(
60
)
. We developed an interview guide to provide structure, while allowing for adaptation of phrasing and question order according to the individual interview context. Each interview began with general questions about the participant’s background, blogging activities, and blogging history, followed by more specific questions related to infrastructure. The interview guide intentionally did not define the terms “information infrastructure” or “preservation”. Instead, participants were encouraged to articulate their own interpretations of these concepts. This approach aligns with qualitative inquiry that seeks to foreground participants’ perspectives.

Sampling and Participants

We followed a sampling strategy aimed at capturing heterogeneity among German scholarly bloggers in order to explore a broad range of experiences until theoretical saturation was reached
(
60
)
. We sought diversity in gender identity, disciplinary background, institutional affiliation, and blogging platform. To identify potential participants, we used the dataset by Ochsner et al. (
43
) that provides a list of 866 German scholarly blogs. Since the majority of blogs in this dataset (77.48%) originate from the Humanities and Social Sciences, which also reflects broader trends in scholarly blogging
(
57
)

(
29

Pre-tests and Conducting the Interview

To assess the clarity and feasibility of the interview guide, we conducted two pre-tests with one female and one male participant, both from the Humanities and Social Sciences. The pre-tests lasted 42 and 31 minutes (average: 36.5 minutes) and indicated that participants struggled to describe specific long-term preservation practices when asked overly broad questions. As a result, we refined the guide by removing overly general questions and incorporating concrete scenarios and examples to support comprehension. Participants were contacted via e-mail and received detailed information about the study’s purpose, procedures, and data protection measures. Before the interview, participants were informed about their rights and provided written consent for participation and for the recording and processing of audio and screen data. Interviews were conducted in German between January 30 and March 26, 2025. Twelve interviews were conducted and recorded via Zoom
1
1

1

https://zoom.us/de/join
and one via the open-source platform BigBlueButton
2
2

2

https://bigbluebutton.org/
, according to participant preferences. Interviews lasted between 35 and 51 minutes (average: 44 minutes). Audio recordings excluded greetings, introductory study information, explanations of participant rights, and closing remarks, as these sections contained no analytically relevant material. To ensure anonymity, neither the audio recordings nor the transcripts have been made publicly available.

Data analysis

All interviews were transcribed using the GDPR-compliant software Amberscript
3
3

3

https://www.amberscript.com/de/
. Participants were informed in advance about the use of Amberscript and consented accordingly. After automated transcription, each transcript was reviewed by the authors to ensure accuracy and completeness. Repetitions were omitted only if they did not contribute additional meaning. The transcription was conducted in German and all quotations used in this article were translated into English by the authors. Data analysis followed the qualitative content analysis approach described by Kuckartz and Rädiker (
35
). To code the interviews, we used MAXQDA 24, a qualitative research software widely used in Library and Information Science (LIS) research
(
40
)
. Anonymized transcripts were imported into MAXQDA and coded using a structured coding system. Our coding frame consisted of deductive codes derived from the interview guide and inductive codes that emerged directly from the data (e.g., data loss, participants’ prior knowledge, issues of trust). All codes were formulated in English to ensure conceptual consistency. The coding frame was organized hierarchically across multiple levels. All interviews were coded by the first author. The code and reviewed transcripts were reviewed by the second author.

Findings

Challenges in Blog Preservation

Participants reported a range of personal challenges that shape their ability to preserve their blogs. Several participants indicated limited knowledge of long-term preservation, preservation practices, and the integration of blogs into information infrastructures (P6 - P8, P11). Technical expertise, particularly its absence, was sometimes described as a barrier that hindered engagement with preservation-related tasks (P2, P4, P8). P4 expressed difficulties in preparing metadata in a manner that would support interoperability and reuse. Participants also raised concerns related to data security. Two participants (P2, P7) reported uncertainty about how data security considerations apply to their blogs, while others highlighted specific issues such as rights management of images or comments (P1, P4, P6). Participants stated that it would not be feasible to ask every person involved in each blog post to consent to having the content preserved, especially concerning comments. P7 also stated that they worry about not complying with their university’s policies. Many participants (P1, P7, P9, P12, P13) reported that they lack the time both to blog and to acquire the knowledge needed to integrate their blogs into information infrastructures, as well as to engage in preservation-related efforts (P7, P12, P13). One blogger illustrated this challenge by noting:
“Editorial work. We do all of this on the side. And that is a problem for many blogs, that they lack stable funding to actually do editorial work, which is a form of unpaid labor”
(P10, pos. 47-48). Two participants (P5, P8) explained that a lack of institutional support also contributes to not having enough time for blogging or blog-related topics. P2 stated that they worry about not having a tenured position and therefore not being able to work on the blog and maintain it in the long term. In addition to the challenges participants already experienced concerning blog preservation, they also also anticipated several possible infrastructural challenges concerning the the long-term preservation of scholarly blogs. P1 highlighted the increasing complexity of maintaining interconnected blog environments, noting:

The more interconnected you want it to be, the more difficult it becomes, because blogs were intended to respond to other blogs. Then you have to consider what kind of data structures we actually store the blogs in, in order for them to be comprehensive and meaningful. (P1, pos. 37)

They further elaborated:
“That means you would actually have to preserve this blog ecosystem in its entirety, and that’s pretty difficult”
(P1, pos. 35). Another participant emphasized the temporal fragility of digital materials:
“But you also have to think a little bit long-term: what will happen in 50 years? And 50 years is almost an eternity for digital storage media”
(P5, pos. 65). Many participants (P1, P3, P6, P8, P13) additionally acknowledged that not all content can be preserved and that determining which materials are sufficiently relevant constitutes a significant archival challenge. Some participants (P4, P8) remarked that interoperability could be a potential challenge. One participant said:
“I mean, in my daily work, I am so hindered by our IT department in my work, in what I can and cannot do”
(P8, pos. 73). Participants (P3, P4, P6) also identified versioning as a likely challenge and emphasized the need for different versions of a blog post to remain visible within any preservation infrastructure. Related to this, some participants (P3, P6) expressed broader concerns about quality control for blogs and blog posts that might be incorporated into such an infrastructure.

Requirements for an Information Infrastructure

Requirement 1: Content-related Aspects

Although participants expressed a general desire for their blogs to remain accessible in the long term, they articulated differing views on which types of content should be preserved. Some participants regarded all materials as worthy of preservation, as illustrated by P13:
“As long as there is enough storage space, I would save everything”
(P13, pos. 55). At the same time, P13 acknowledged the limitations of preservation infrastructures, noting:
“That’s an archival problem, what has to be sorted out”
(P13, pos. 55). Other participants (P3, P6, P8) similarly emphasized that not all blog posts are sufficiently relevant to warrant preservation. As one participant noted:
“So, first of all, I would like to raise the question of whether it is really necessary to save everything?”
(P1, pos. 35). Several participants expressed a desire for bloggers to have agency in determining which materials should be preserved:
“If you could control that individually, that wouldn’t be bad, or at least let the bloggers decide”
(P11, pos. 41). When asked what kind of content should be saved bloggers named texts (P2, P5, P7, P9 - P13), pictures (P2, P3, P6, P7, P9, P11, P13), comments (P2 - P4, P6, P9 - P13), layout elements (P1, P3, P6, P11), audio files (P7), and references or links (P7, P9 - P11). Conversely, participants identified several types of content as not relevant for preservation, including posts announcing calls for papers, outdated posts, or posts not related to scientific output. One blogger explicitly stated that images were not important to them (P3), while another claimed that the layout was not significant to them (P7).

Some bloggers also distinguished between preserving blogs for the purpose of safeguarding their content and preserving blogs as potential research objects. According to participants, the latter would necessitate capturing the broader blogosphere at the network and citation level, rather than focusing solely on individual posts. One participant articulated this distinction as follows:

If it’s just a matter of understanding the content, blog posts are sufficient. But if you want to analyze blogs as a media phenomenon or as a socio-technical construct, that’s not enough. Then you need to have the context. You actually need the form of presentation, because you have to be able to understand how people received or could have received these things at the time. So it [preservation] really depends very much on what you presuppose as future usage intentions. (P1, pos. 39)

Requirement 2: Technical and Organizational Aspects

Most participants did not propose concrete technical and organizational requirements and instead articulated broad ideas about what long-term preservation should entail. One participant emphasized several key expectations:

It’s always been crucial that things are reliably available in the long-term, that URLs are stable and available in the long-term, and that things can be cited, especially when they are cited in print formats. If you cite a link that can no longer be found a year later, the whole thing somehow loses its meaning. This means that there has always been an expectation that an institution would simply maintain stability in this regard. (P2, pos. 46)

P11 emphasized the importance of the provision of DOIs for blog posts. With respect to functional requirements for a preservation infrastructure, P6 said that they want it to be searchable by date and that the operators of this infrastructure provide editorial services such as updating posts that are not up to date, organizing peer reviews, making sure bloggers are institutionally affiliated and sorting out content that is not relevant. P7 stated it was important to them that content is not changed retrospectively. They also expressed a desire for transparency regarding the conditions under which content would be made accessible in the long term, emphasizing the need to trust that these conditions would be upheld. Furthermore, they wished for guidance on technical knowledge, such as data handling and storage, IT literacy, and licensing and correctly using images. Another participant emphasized the importance of metadata and metadata literacy. They went on to say:
“It [Metadata] affects workflows and means more steps. At the same time scholarly blogs that cannot acquire this knowledge should be provided with the structures and perhaps tools and automation, to make it as easy as possible for them.”
(P4, pos. 86). One participant, expressing concerns about the long-term stability of digital content, suggested producing physical copies of blogs as an additional preservation strategy. As they explained:

We’ll have to see how storage media develops over the next few decades. How long it will remain stored, or whether people will actually sit down and print out my blog, for example. Bind the whole thing, put two or three versions of it somewhere, just so that it physically exists. That seems to me to be a rather important point with texts like these, that you have to get away from the digital a little bit. (P5, pos. 52-53)

Requirement 3: Governance Aspects

A prominent concern for several participants was the question of governance. Participants reflected on which types of institutions they considered sufficiently trustworthy to take responsibility for preserving blogs. P13 argued that this responsibility should lie exclusively with government-funded institutions, stating:
“For me things like cultural heritage are not tasks of private institutions”
(P13, pos. 53). P13 explained that they did not trust private actors such as Google as a company or Google services. P13 also went on to express concerns over cyber attacks on their blog or an infrastructure that preserves blogs. Other participants expressed concerns about government institutions. For example, P7 reported not trusting libraries to ensure long-term preservation, whereas P6 expressed a high level of trust in libraries. A different participant described the precarity of the situation as follows:

I think we need a mix of different players involved. Because one thing is clear from the current political situation, especially when you look at the US right now, with the Trump administration and Elon Musk. Social media and internet platforms are finding themselves in a strange situation where there are deliberate attempts to censor scientific language. We need to protect ourselves from that and that means it actually has to have a very strong base in the scientific community itself. If I were to apply that to the situation in Germany, I would say that, to a certain extent, it has to be the German Research Foundation. But I’m also seeing in the US how the NSF
4
4

4

U.S. National Science Foundation
is being accessed and regulated. […] I also believe that we should not rely on national solutions in this area, but international ones, so that no single government can encroach on such infrastructures. (P3, pos. 43)

Several participants supported these concern about broader political developments. P10 noted that the political situation in the United States worried them and that they feared similar dynamics emerging in Germany. P12 further described hesitations about blogging on controversial or politically sensitive topics. At the same time, some participants identified a countervailing potential in scholarly blogs to resist anti-science developments. As one participant articulated:

We need to become politically resilient with all these digital platforms and blogs. I no longer consider academic freedom to be guaranteed. I think we need to equip ourselves somehow to develop resilience in this area. And I believe that blogs will play a very important role here, blogs that may not even be hosted on your own national platform, but somewhere internationally. (P3, pos. 43)

Participants that had concrete ideas or wishes concerning governance, discussed the following stakeholders that were previously introduced in Chapter 2, as institutions that could preserve scholarly blogs:

Libraries

Some participants (P5 - P7, P13) identified libraries as key stakeholders responsible for ensuring the long-term preservation of their blogs, mentioning institutions such as the DNB (P5, P13) or university libraries (P7). Two participants expressed a desire for their blog posts to be automatically captured either by the DNB as part of a mandatory deposit process (P10, P13) or by their university library (P9).
“At the latest when a blog is assigned an ISSN, the library should normally also preserve this publication in the long-term, i.e., the National Library”
(P13, pos. 47). The participant further suggested:
“I would think that it’s a task for the German National Library that would then accordingly need positions and require a reorganization maybe toward digital publications”
(P13, pos. 49). Furthermore, participants also mentioned several additional stakeholders they considered relevant for securing scholarly blogs, including Fachinformationsdienste (specialist information services, P12), subject-specific repositories (P4), and Europeana or other international or European solutions (P3, P11, P13).

Digital Archives and Libraries

Some bloggers (P1, P2, P4, P5, P10, P11) praised the Internet Archive for its efforts in archiving online content. Some participants (P2, P10, P11) also viewed the Internet Archive as a potential solution—or at least an additional safeguard—when used alongside other information infrastructure services. As one participant noted:
“Yes, I think the Internet Archive for example is an admirable and incredibly important thing but now it is almost an emergency solution, since for a long time we did not have anything better”
(P1, pos. 45). At the same time, this participant acknowledged limitations of the IA, pointing out that the archive is not easily searchable and that images are often missing. They added:
“In the end it’s a community or institutional solution that needs to be implemented”
(P1, pos. 37). Another participant added:
“With something like the Internet Archive, I wouldn’t feel safe. Especially not if I have anything to do with gender or postcolonialism. Since it’s through foundations based in the USA, the risk would be too great for me”
(P13, pos. 51). Another participant raised concerns about the sustainability of online archives more broadly:
“The problem with online archives is that they basically have the same disadvantage as the [blogging] platforms themselves, namely that they can disappear sometime, and then the contents are gone as well”
(P5, pos. 63). P4 referred to the scholarly blog archive Rogue Scholar, praising the high quality of its support while noting that such intensive support is feasible only because of the archive’s current size. Participants also proposed conceptual ideas, such as a scholarly archival cloud (P1) or the use of Wikis or Wikidata due to their international reach (P3).

Blogging Platforms

Many participants, particularly those blogging on the scholarly platform de.hypotheses, reported relying on their platform to ensure the long-term preservation of their blogs. As one participant noted:
“So I relied on Hypotheses for that [digital preservation]”
(P13, pos. 35). Another participant similarly explained their lack of engagement with long-term preservation measures:
“So, the honest answer is that I rely a little bit on Hypotheses”
(P9, pos. 49). This view was shared by an additional Hypotheses user (P7). This also matches the answer of one participant that used the scholarly blogging platform SciLogs who also assumed that the publishing house already ensured the long-term preservation of blogs. The participant blogging on ScienceBlogs stated that they expect the publishing house behind the platform to make sure blog content is not lost but would also want an independent government funded institution to be involved.

Bloggers

Expectations toward responsibility were closely tied to the perceived abilities for long-term stability. Participants critiqued the limits of individual responsibility due to time constraints, employment precarity, and a lack of technical expertise. P2 expressed a wish for person-independent infrastructures, so that the survival of a blog does not depend on bloggers. As they explained:

Even with this simple WordPress system, the question was always: who will take care of continuously installing updates to the system, and who will ensure that the site remains accessible and reasonably stable after the next update, just as it is now online? And I don’t think there’s a patent solution for that. So, it can’t be people. I myself am on a fixed-term contract. I simply don’t know whether I will still be working in research in ten years’ time. And if I’m no longer doing that, it’s very unlikely that I’ll still be the one providing updates. And that’s why, if we want the content to be permanently accessible, we are actually dependent on infrastructures that are independent of individuals. (P2, pos. 60)

Other participants recognized that preservation cannot be delegated entirely to external institutions but remains partially dependent on the efforts of bloggers’. Participants described themselves as responsible for preparing content for preservation by applying licenses and structuring metadata.

Discussion

Challenges in Blog Preservation: Structural Conditions and the Individual Burden

A central challenge that emerged from the data is bloggers’ limited technical knowledge, which restricts their capacity to implement preservation measures. The lack of knowledge also extends to uncertainty about data handling, storage, rights management, and metadata. The latter, being an especially notable finding, since appropriate metadata can increase the discoverability of documents
(
4
)
. Considering Star and Ruhleder’s (
56
) dimensions of infrastructure, the use of an infrastructure is something that does initially need to be learned. However, infrastructure users would need to be able to familiarize themselves with an infrastructure regardless or their technical background
(
56
)
. Challenges concerning literacy were sometimes accompanied by concerns about non-compliance on local institutional policies or local and global legal requirements. Such anxieties reduce bloggers’ willingness to engage proactively with long-term preservation. Infrastructures would therefore need to be able to be interoperable with local and global technical systems and ensure the embodiment of standards locally and globally
(
5
;
56
)
. The challenges discussed above reveal the need for targeted training programs that equip bloggers with technical, organizational, and legal competencies relevant to preservation workflows. Training and resources could be embedded in existing information infrastructure services, such as academic libraries and repositories, archives, or offered directly through blogging platforms such as de.hypotheses.

Bloggers lack of ability to ensure preservation is also connected to staff turnover due to tenured contracts and the fragility of personal hosting arrangements. Participants felt that blogging remains insufficiently acknowledged within their institutions. Many participants described blogging as extra work layered onto existing responsibilities, limiting their ability to maintain or preserve their blogs. This suggests that a lack of institutional support for bloggers directly influences bloggers preservation efforts. To address the disconnect between blogging activities and institutional support, we argue for increased institutional support such as the formal recognition of blogging as a scholarly activity, the institutional hosting of blogs on secure servers, technical and managerial support via libraries or Open Science offices, and the inclusion of blogs in institutional repositories. Together, these measures would strengthen the position of blogs within the scholarly communication ecosystem and support their long-term preservation.

No differences on bloggers challenges were observed across disciplinary backgrounds. This suggests that challenges related to preserving scholarly blogs are shaped more strongly by infrastructural conditions than by disciplinary cultures. For example, tenured researchers emphasized the fragility of preservation practices that rely on individual responsibility that are influenced by employment precarity, limited time resources, and uncertainty about continued participation in academia. Some senior scholars, by contrast, framed challenges in preservation as a question of governance and political resilience. All in all, the challenges identified by participants demonstrate the interplay of social, organizational and technical activities and systems
(
56
;
5
)
. Limited knowledge, time constraints, and dependence on institutional support do not merely represent individual barriers but reflect structural conditions under which scholarly communication takes place. The findings show that preservation is constrained less by the absence of technical solutions than by the coordination of distributed socio-technical relations.

Requirements for an Information Infrastructure: Scope, Control, and Standards

Requirement 1: Content-related Aspects

Participants requirements related to the preservation of relevant blog content were diverse. While some participants wished to save everything, conditional only on sufficient storage space, others argued for a more selective approach. Participants identified various content types such as audio, comments, images, links, layouts, texts, and videos, as potentially relevant. The data suggests no difference of what content was deemed preservation worthy across disciplines. However, the data indicates the need for nuanced criteria for which content merits preservation, considering that the preservation of content types such as links and comments is impacted by technical and legal constraints. Participants also expressed concerns about potential information overload, which could strain storage systems and complicate retrieval and analysis. The strong desire for bloggers to retain control over their own content, as described in prior work
(
46
)
, suggests the adoption of an opt-in approach to ensure that decisions about content preservation remain with bloggers.

Requirement 2: Organizational and Technical Aspects

Key technical and organizational needs included stable PIDs, metadata support, interoperability, and transparency regarding the conditions under which content is preserved. PIDs and metadata require coordination across bloggers, blogging platforms and infrastructure institutions. These requirements confirm once again the need for an infrastructure to incorporate standards that enable it to interoperate transparently with other tools and systems
(
56
)
. Considering the individual and structural challenges such as a lack of technical literacy and institutional support, an infrastructure for the long-term preservation of scholarly blogs is not only constituted by technical systems and organizational activities but also by practices through which scholars learn to engage with them. An information infrastructure for scholarly blogs must therefore align technical standardization with scholarly conventions of practice
(
56
)
, by considering challenges concerning literacy and time and support bloggers by offering technical and organizational support. All in all, technical systems and organizational and social activities need to come together to ensure an infrastructure’s functionality
(
5
)
.

Libraries

Participants viewed libraries, especially academic and national libraries, to be well positioned to assume responsibility for the long-term preservation of scholarly blogs because preservation, curation, and the provision of stable access to knowledge have traditionally been core elements of libraries institutional mission

4

Since specialist information services and repositories are often affiliated with academic libraries or research performing institutions and already fulfill central functions in cooperative scientific information systems, they hold strong potential for integrating scholarly blogs. Repositories already provide persistent identifiers, quality-controlled metadata, and long-term citability for scientific content, which could be extended to include blogs. The acquisition of scholarly blogs in multiple repositories (e.g., through the institutional repository of the blogger’s institution or academic library and a subject specific repository) would ensure the preservation of blog content by multiple stakeholders, reducing the risk of information loss.

Digital Archives and Libraries

While some participants praised the Internet Archive, others expressed concerns about its safety and stability. In May 2024, the Internet Archive experienced a major cyberattack, resulting in restricted access to the Wayback Machine and inconsistent services
(

Therefore, while the Internet Archive makes valuable contributions to the preservation of web archiving, some participants agreed that it can not be solely responsible for safeguarding scholarly blogs. The preservation of scholarly blogs by the Internet Archive in addition to libraries and repositories would however ensure decentralization independently of government funded institutions. The preservation of individual blogposts in the Internet Archive also requires little technical knowledge and could be ensured by bloggers themselves. Similarly, bloggers can easily register their blog with Rogue Scholar and ensure PIDs for blogposts and the archival of posts on Rogue Scholar. One participant proposed to use Wikidata, a free and collaborative repository that gathers structured data to support Wikipedia, Wikimedia Commons, other wikis in the Wikimedia movement, and their users

Blogging Platforms

Participants blogging on de.hypotheses expressed that their platform should be responsible for long-term preservation. Participants using SciLogs and ScienceBlogs also wished their platforms would preserve blogs, but acknowledged that this is not realistic given their commercial nature. In contrast, de.hypotheses, as a publicly funded project, already supports discoverability and citability by assigning DOIs, requesting ISSNs from the DNB, and listing blogs in the OpenEdition catalog, functioning as an intermediary between bloggers and information infrastructure facilities

. We propose similar efforts for other existing scholarly blog platforms such as SciLogs and ScienceBlogs.

Bloggers

The findings demonstrate the long-term preservation of scholarly blogs is shaped not only by institutional arrangements but also by the ongoing practices of bloggers themselves. Bloggers are both contributors to preservation and infrastructure users who depend on technical and organizational support. Overall, most participants conceptualized long-term preservation as a distributed process in which bloggers, platforms, and institutional stakeholders contribute.

In summary, the findings indicate that the preservation of scholarly blogs cannot be addressed through a single institutional or technical solution but instead requires an infrastructural configuration capable of accommodating diverse positional, organizational, and political systems and activities. When asked about their requirements for an information infrastructure for scholarly blogs, most participants named or indirectly described already existing infrastructures, aimed at preserving the scholarly record. This proposition is also driven by concerns about the vulnerability of single institutions to cyberattacks, financial, and political instability. A decentralized approach distributes risk and ensures that no single actor holds exclusive responsibility for or control over scholarly blogs.

Limitations and Future Research

This study was limited by two factors. First, some bloggers we reached out to who declined being part of this study expressed that they did not think they could contribute to the topic of long-term preservation, since they did not think they were educated enough on the topic. Therefore, some of the participants were bloggers who already had prior knowledge about how scholarly blogs fit into digital information and research infrastructures. However, the data revealed that even bloggers with prior experience on the topic still did not feel knowledgeable enough about possible preservation strategies. Second, both authors are involved in a project focused on the long-term preservation of scholarly blogs and therefore bring a pre-existing interest in ensuring their preservation. This positionality may have shaped the framing of the research questions and interpretation of the data. Accordingly, reflexive practices were employed throughout the analysis to mitigate potential bias. While this study contributed to the existing research with novel findings, these findings could be expanded on by future research. Since this study was concerned with bloggers’ requirements for information infrastructures, future research could investigate good-practice solutions in information infrastructure facilities. Moreover, future research can build on this study by proposing a concrete practical implementation of a decentralized information infrastructure tasked with the preservation for scholarly blogs. Lastly, future research could investigate the long-term preservation of other types of scholarly outputs, such as microblogs, newsletters or podcasts.

Conclusion

The long-term preservation of scholarly blogs remains insufficiently ensured. To address this gap, we conducted 13 interviews with German scholarly bloggers to identify the requirements they consider essential for an information infrastructure capable of supporting long-term blog preservation. Specifically, we asked bloggers about the challenges they encounter, the types of content they deem preservation worthy, their technical and organizational requirements, and the stakeholders they consider responsible for preserving scholarly blogs. The results indicate that bloggers face substantial challenges, consisting of limited technical expertise, a lack of institutional recognition and support, and ambiguity regarding responsibility for long-term preservation. At the same time, participants articulated clear expectations for infrastructures that are trustworthy, transparent, and sustainable, while also respecting bloggers’ autonomy and intellectual property. The analysis shows that expectations regarding long-term preservation are shaped less by disciplinary background than by participants’ positions within academic and infrastructural arrangements. The requirements articulated by bloggers frame preservation not as a singular but as a distributed task. The German scholarly blogosphere encompasses a heterogeneous set of stakeholders, with preservation efforts already being distributed among bloggers, blogging platforms, and information infrastructure facilities. It is unlikely that any single organization can be created to assume responsibility for comprehensive preservation efforts. No single stakeholder is perceived as capable of guaranteeing long-term preservation alone. Our findings therefore suggest building on an already existing decentralized information infrastructure. Such an infrastructure would distribute responsibility across multiple stakeholders, mitigate risks associated with political volatility, censorship, cyberattacks, and platform instability, and provide flexible pathways for preservation. Moreover, the findings show that scholarly blogs are meaningful components of scholarly communication, and cultural heritage whose long-term preservation should not depend on individual labor alone. Building a distributed, interoperable, and community-supported infrastructure would not only secure long-term access to scholarly blogs but also strengthen their visibility, citability, and legitimacy within the broader scholarly communication ecosystem.
