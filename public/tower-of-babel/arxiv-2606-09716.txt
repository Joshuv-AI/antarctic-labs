Optomechanically controlled response amplification for enhanced quantum sensing
Javid Naikoo

Abstract
We show that strongly amplified dynamical responses in cavity optomechanical systems can be harnessed for enhanced quantum sensing. By tuning the optomechanical interaction to a regime of enhanced susceptibility, weak perturbations produce disproportionately large changes in the system response, leading to substantially improved estimation precision. Using Gaussian estimation theory, we demonstrate that the quantum Fisher information exhibits a divergent scaling as the perturbation strength decreases, implying a corresponding suppression of the estimation error. We further show that heterodyne detection of the output cavity field yields the classical Fisher information with the same asymptotic scaling as the quantum Fisher information, demonstrating that the enhanced sensitivity is accessible with a standard measurement protocol. These results identify amplified optomechanical dynamics as a controllable resource for quantum enhanced sensing and metrology.



I
Introduction

The pursuit of increasingly precise measurements has long been a driving force in the development of physics, enabling stringent tests of fundamental principles and the detection of exceedingly weak physical effects. Quantum sensing has emerged as a prominent application of quantum technologies, exploiting resources such as superposition and entanglement, and other nonclassical effects to achieve measurement sensitivities that surpass classical limits
Giovannetti et al. 2004
;
Degen et al. 2017
;
Demkowicz-Dobrzański et al. 2012
;
Chaves et al. 2013
. At the same time, there is growing interest in understanding how quantum mechanics manifests at larger scales, as demonstrated by experiments showing coherence in systems ranging from superconducting circuits to matter-wave interference with complex molecules
Andrews et al. 1997
;
Friedman et al. 2000
;
Eibenberger et al. 2013
;
Fein et al. 2019
. These developments have motivated the exploration of mesoscopic platforms that not only bridge the quantum-to-classical transition but also serve as functional devices for precision measurement and quantum-enhanced technologies.

Among such platforms, cavity optomechanical systems have emerged as a leading candidate for studying macroscopic quantum behavior
Aspelmeyer et al. 2014
. In these systems, a mechanical resonator couples to an optical cavity field via radiation pressure. The mechanical displacement shifts the cavity resonance frequency, while the intracavity field exerts a back-action force on the mechanical element. This mutual interaction enables highly sensitive optical readout of mechanical motion as well as precise optical control of mechanical dynamics
Verhagen et al. 2012a
;
Chen 2013
;
Aspelmeyer et al. 2014
;
Bowen and Milburn 2015
. Continuous experimental progress has enabled cooling of mechanical resonators close to their quantum ground state, thereby accessing regimes where
quantum fluctuations
play a central role in their dynamics
Chan et al. 2011a
;
Teufel et al. 2011a
;
Peterson et al. 2016
.

Beyond their fundamental interest, optomechanical systems constitute powerful platforms for precision force and displacement sensing
Aspelmeyer et al. 2014
. Their high susceptibility to external perturbations enables the detection of extremely weak forces, with applications ranging from precision inertial sensing and gravimetry to searches for faint signals such as ultralight dark matter
Mow-Lowry et al. 2008
;
Abbott et al. 2009
;
Matsumoto et al. 2019
;
Pierce et al. 2018
. Significant efforts have therefore been devoted to improving their sensing capabilities through advanced measurement and control techniques. Recent studies have demonstrated enhanced performance using photon-counting-based inference protocols
Clark et al. 2022
, Kerr-assisted optomechanical architectures
Cheng et al. 2025
, and entanglement-enhanced sensing schemes
Li et al. 2026
.

Of particular relevance here, optomechanical systems exhibit nonlinear dynamical regimes associated with sharp transitions between distinct states. Owing to the radiation-pressure interaction, these systems can exhibit bistability, self-sustained oscillations, limit cycles, and chaotic dynamics
Ludwig et al. 2008
;
Lörch et al. 2014
;
Bakemeier et al. 2015
;
Zhu et al. 2023
. Such phenomena are often accompanied by transitions between distinct dynamical regimes, where the response of the system becomes exceptionally sensitive to small parameter variations. The resulting enhancement of susceptibility near transition points is closely related to critical phenomena in nonequilibrium systems and provides a natural mechanism for amplifying weak perturbations
Wang et al. 2016
;
Jäger et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2021
;
Xiong et al. 2023
. Motivated by these observations, criticality-enhanced sensing in optomechanical platforms has recently been proposed
Tang et al. 2023
. By operating in the vicinity of dynamical critical points, such schemes harness the amplified response associated with critical behavior to facilitate the detection of weak perturbations.

In this work, we investigate quantum-enhanced sensing in a lossy cavity optomechanical system, accounting for dissipation arising from both intrinsic system–environment coupling and the probing channels. To characterize the achievable precision, we consider Gaussian probe states and employ Gaussian estimation theory, with the Fisher information serving as the central figure of merit. This approach allows us to relate the ultimate estimation precision to the Green’s function of the driven optomechanical system. We show that the optomechanical coupling can induce pronounced response amplification associated with singular behavior of the Green’s function near dynamical transitions, leading to enhanced scaling of the achievable precision.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we introduce the cavity optomechanical system and develop its linearized dynamical description, along with the corresponding input–output framework for the relevant observables. In Sec.
III
, we analyze the sensitivity of the system to external perturbations, formulating the estimation problem in terms of the Green’s function and associated statistical quantities. Section
IV
then demonstrates how optomechanical coupling can be used to engineer singular response, leading to enhanced sensing performance and nontrivial scaling of estimation precision. We conclude in Sec.
V
.

Cavity Optomechanical Model and Linearized Dynamics

We consider a cavity optomechanical system consisting of a single optical mode coupled to a mechanical oscillator, as schematically shown in Fig.
1

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mathrm{cav}}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\omega_{\mathrm{M}}\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}-g_{0}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}(\hat{b}^{\dagger}+\hat{b})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{i}\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{L}}\,(e^{-\mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{L}}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}-e^{\mathrm{i}\,\omega_{\mathrm{L}}}\hat{a}),

(1)

where
\hat{a}
(
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
) and
\hat{b}
(
\hat{b}^{\dagger}
) denote the annihilation (creation) operators for the optical cavity mode and the mechanical oscillator, respectively. The parameters
\omega_{\mathrm{cav}}
and
\omega_{\mathrm{M}}
represent the bare resonance frequencies of the optical and mechanical modes. The term proportional to
g_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
describes the radiation-pressure interaction, whereby the photon number
\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
couples to the mechanical position quadrature
(\hat{b}^{\dagger}+\hat{b})
. The final term corresponds to coherent driving of the cavity by an external laser field of amplitude
\epsilon_{\mathrm{L}}

\omega_{\mathrm{L}}

Before proceeding further, it is convenient to separate the coherent dynamics from the quantum fluctuations. We decompose the cavity and mechanical operators as

\delta\hat{a}\equiv\hat{a}-\alpha,\quad\delta\hat{b}\equiv\hat{b}-\beta,

(2)

where
\alpha\!=\!\langle\hat{a}\rangle
and
\beta\!=\!\langle\hat{b}\rangle
denote the corresponding expectation values. The operators
\delta\hat{a}
and
\delta\hat{b}
thus describe fluctuations about the mean fields and capture the quantum noise of the system. In the regime of strong driving, the dynamics is dominated by the mean fields
\alpha
and
\beta

For notational simplicity, we omit the
\delta
symbol henceforth and relabel the fluctuation operators
\delta\hat{a}
and
\delta\hat{b}
as
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
; it is understood throughout that these operators represent deviations from their respective mean values.

II.1
Quantum Langevin equations

In practice, both optical and mechanical modes interact with environmental reservoirs, leading to photon loss from the cavity and phonon damping in the mechanical oscillator. Incorporating these dissipative processes leads to the quantum Langevin equations governing the dynamics of the fluctuation operators
Gardiner and Zoller 2010
. These equations read (see App.
A
for details)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{a}

\displaystyle\!=\!\left(\mathrm{i}\,\Delta-\gamma_{a}\right)\hat{a}+\mathrm{i}\,g(\hat{b}^{\dagger}+\hat{b})+\sqrt{\kappa_{a}}\hat{F}_{a}^{\mathrm{in}}+\sqrt{\eta_{a}}\hat{L}_{a}^{\mathrm{in}},

(3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{b}

\displaystyle\!=\!\left(-\mathrm{i}\,\omega_{b}-\gamma_{b}\right)\hat{b}+\mathrm{i}\,g(\hat{a}^{\dagger}+\hat{a})+\sqrt{\kappa_{b}}\hat{F}_{b}^{\mathrm{in}}+\sqrt{\eta_{b}}\hat{L}_{b}^{\mathrm{in}},

(4)

Here,
\Delta\!=\!\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}-2g_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\operatorname{Re}(\beta)
denotes the effective cavity detuning, where
\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\!=\!\omega_{\mathrm{L}}-\omega_{\mathrm{cav}}
is the bare detuning between the driving laser frequency and the cavity resonance frequency. The quantity
g\!=\!g_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}|\alpha|
represents the linearized optomechanical coupling strength, with
\alpha
and
\beta
introduced in Eq. (
2
).

The system couples to external probe fields and internal reservoirs through two distinct channels. The external channels, characterized by the rates
\kappa_{\ell}
, inject probe fields into the system via the input operators
\hat{F}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}
, while the intrinsic dissipation channels, with rates
\eta_{\ell}
, give rise to environmental noise described by
\hat{L}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{in}}
. These processes jointly account for both driven fluctuations and irreversible coupling to internal baths. The combined effect of these channels determines the total damping of each mode. In particular, for
\ell=a,b
, the overall damping rate is given by
\gamma_{\ell}=(\kappa_{\ell}+\eta_{\ell})/2
. Consequently, the system dynamics incorporates both coherent optomechanical interactions and dissipative fluctuations arising from external driving and intrinsic losses.

To streamline the notation and make the structure of the coupled equations more transparent, it is convenient to collect the operators into column vectors. We therefore define

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle\!:=\!\begin{pmatrix}\hat{a}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\hat{b}\end{pmatrix},\qquad~\hat{\bm{a}}^{\dagger}\!:=\!\begin{pmatrix}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\hat{b}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\neq(\hat{a}^{\dagger}~~\hat{b}^{\dagger}),

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}

\displaystyle\!:=\!\begin{pmatrix}\hat{F}_{a}^{\mathrm{in}}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\hat{F}_{b}^{\mathrm{in}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\hat{\bm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}}\!:=\!\begin{pmatrix}\hat{L}_{a}^{\mathrm{in}}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\hat{L}_{b}^{\mathrm{in}}\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

Here
\hat{\bm{a}}
groups together the fluctuation operators of the optical and mechanical modes, while
\hat{\bm{a}}^{\dagger}
denotes the corresponding vector of creation operators written in the same column form. The distinction from the row vector
(\hat{a}^{\dagger}~~\hat{b}^{\dagger})
will be important when writing the equations in matrix form. Similarly,
\hat{\bm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}
and
\hat{\bm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}}
collect the probe and bath input fields, respectively. In terms of these vectors, the coupled quantum Langevin Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
) can be written in a compact matrix form,

\partial_{t}\hat{\bm{a}}\!=\!-\mathrm{i}\,\textbf{H}\,\hat{\bm{a}}+\textbf{K}_{\mathrm{om}}\,\hat{\bm{a}}^{\dagger}+\textbf{K}_{\mathrm{probe}}\,\hat{\bm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}+\textbf{K}_{\mathrm{bath}}\,\hat{\bm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}},

(6)

which makes explicit the different contributions to the dynamics. The first term describes the coherent evolution governed by an effective non-Hermitian dynamical matrix
H
, the second term captures the parametric coupling between operators and their Hermitian conjugates arising from the linearized optomechanical interaction, while the remaining terms account for the probe and environmental noise. The matrices appearing in Eq. (
6
) are given by

H

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\Delta-\mathrm{i}\,\gamma_{a}&-g\\
-g&\omega_{b}-\mathrm{i}\,\gamma_{b}\end{pmatrix},\quad\textbf{K}_{\rm om}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathrm{i}\,g\\
\mathrm{i}\,g&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\textbf{K}_{\rm probe}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\kappa_{a}}&0\\
0&\sqrt{\kappa_{b}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\textbf{K}_{\rm bath}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\eta_{a}}&0\\
0&\sqrt{\eta_{b}}\end{pmatrix}.

(7)

Here,
H
encodes both the coherent detuned dynamics and the damping of the two modes,
\textbf{K}_{\rm om}
represents the linearized optomechanical interaction, and the matrices
\textbf{K}_{\rm probe}
and
\textbf{K}_{\rm bath}
specify the coupling strengths to the probe and loss channels, respectively. In this form, the equations provide a convenient starting point for analyzing the system response in both time and frequency domains.

II.2
Quadrature representation and output field statistics

In order to analyze and quantify the measurement sensitivity, it is convenient to reformulate the dynamics in terms of field quadratures. This expresses the bosonic modes in terms of canonical Hermitian operators corresponding to field amplitudes and provides a natural framework for treating Gaussian states and their noise properties.

In the Fourier domain we define the generalized position and momentum quadratures associated with the field operator
\hat{\bm{a}}[\omega]
as
1
1

1

For a vector of bosonic operators
\hat{\hat{\bm{a}}}(t)\!=\!\big(\hat{a}(t),\hat{b}(t),\ldots\big)^{\rm T}
, we define the Fourier transform as
\hat{\hat{\bm{a}}}[\omega]\!:=\!\mathcal{F}_{\omega}[\hat{\hat{\bm{a}}}]\!=\!\int dt\,e^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\hat{\hat{\bm{a}}}(t)
. The corresponding quadratures in frequency space are
\hat{\bm{q}}[\omega]\!=\!\hat{\hat{\bm{a}}}[\omega]+(\hat{\hat{\bm{a}}}[\omega])^{\dagger}
and
\hat{\bm{p}}[\omega]\!=\!-\mathrm{i}\big(\hat{\hat{\bm{a}}}[\omega]-(\hat{\hat{\bm{a}}}[\omega])^{\dagger}\big)
, where the Hermitian conjugation acts entry-wise. Importantly, one has
\big(\hat{\hat{\bm{a}}}[\omega]\big)^{\dagger}\!=\!\hat{\hat{\bm{a}}}^{\dagger}[-\omega]
.

\hat{\bm{q}}[\omega]\!=\!\hat{\bm{a}}[\omega]+(\hat{\bm{a}}[\omega])^{\dagger},\quad\hat{\bm{p}}[\omega]\!=\!-\mathrm{i}\bigl(\hat{\bm{a}}[\omega]-(\hat{\bm{a}}[\omega])^{\dagger}\bigr).

(8)

These operators are Hermitian and correspond to the amplitude and phase quadratures of the field modes. In this representation the fluctuations of the optical field and their correlations can be conveniently expressed in terms of covariance matrices.

We now rewrite the dynamical equations in terms of these quadratures. Taking the Fourier transform of Eq. (
6
) together with its Hermitian conjugate and using the definitions in Eq. (
8
), we obtain a set of coupled equations for the quadrature components (see App.
B
). It is convenient to express these equations using the canonical symplectic structure, encoded in the matrix
{\mathsf{\Omega}}\!=\![\textbf{0},\textbf{I};-\textbf{I},\textbf{0}]
, with
0
and
I
denoting the
2\times 2
null and identity matrices, respectively. This matrix plays the role of the imaginary unit in quadrature space, satisfying
{\mathsf{\Omega}}^{2}\!=\!{\mathsf{I}}

Ferraro et al. 2005
. In matrix form, the equations read

\displaystyle\omega\,{\mathsf{\Omega}}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\bm{q}}[\omega]\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\hat{\bm{p}}[\omega]\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle={\mathsf{H}}\,{\mathsf{\Omega}}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\bm{q}}[\omega]\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\hat{\bm{p}}[\omega]\end{pmatrix}+{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm om}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\bm{q}}[-\omega]\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\hat{\bm{p}}[-\omega]\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle+{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm probe}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\bm{q}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\omega]\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\hat{\bm{p}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\omega]\end{pmatrix}+{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm bath}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\bm{q}}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\omega]\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\hat{\bm{p}}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\omega]\end{pmatrix}.

(9)

The matrices appearing above admit the following block-structured representation

\displaystyle{\mathsf{H}}\!

\displaystyle=\!\mathcal{R}[\textbf{H}],\quad{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm om}\!=\!\mathcal{T}[\textbf{K}_{\rm om}],\quad{\mathsf{K}}_{\bullet}\!=\!\mathrm{diag}(\textbf{K}_{\bullet},\textbf{K}_{\bullet}),

(10)

where
\bullet
labels the external channels, corresponding to the probe and bath contributions. The mappings
\mathcal{R}[\cdot]
and
\mathcal{T}[\cdot]
are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}[X]

\displaystyle\!:=\!\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}(X)&-\operatorname{Im}(X)\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\operatorname{Im}(X)&\quad\operatorname{Re}(X)\end{pmatrix},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}[X]

\displaystyle\!:=\!\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}(X)&\quad\operatorname{Im}(X)\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\operatorname{Im}(X)&-\operatorname{Re}(X)\end{pmatrix}.

(12)

The first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
9
) describes the intrinsic dynamics of the system, while the second term couples the positive and negative frequency components due to the optomechanical interaction. The remaining terms account for the input fluctuations originating from the probe and the inaccessible channels.

For notational convenience, we assemble all system and input quadratures into vector form,

{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}[\pm\omega]\!:=\!\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\bm{q}}[\pm\omega]\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\hat{\bm{p}}[\pm\omega]\end{pmatrix},~{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}^{\mathrm{in/out}}_{\ast}[\omega]\!:=\!\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\bm{q}}_{\ast}^{\mathrm{in/out}}[\omega]\vskip 2.84526pt\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\hat{\bm{p}}_{\ast}^{\mathrm{in/out}}[\omega]\end{pmatrix},~\ast=\mathrm{F/L},

(13)

where F/L pertains to the probe/inaccessible channel. In terms of these variables, the linear system in Eq. (
9
) can be formally solved in the frequency domain. The resulting solution can be expressed as

\displaystyle{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}[\omega]

\displaystyle\!=\!{\mathsf{G}}[\omega]\,{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{probe}}\,{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\omega]+{\mathsf{G}}[\omega]\,{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{bath}}\,{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\omega]

\displaystyle\quad-{\mathsf{G}}[\omega]\,{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathrm{op}}\,{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}[-\omega]-{\mathsf{G}}[\omega]\,{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathrm{ob}}\,{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}}[-\omega],

(14)

where the optomechanical-probe and optomechanical-bath transfer matrices are defined as

\displaystyle{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\!:=\!{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{om}}\,\Omega^{-1}(\omega{\mathsf{I}}+{\mathsf{H}})^{-1}{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{probe}},

(15)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathrm{ob}}

\displaystyle\!:=\!{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{om}}\,\Omega^{-1}(\omega{\mathsf{I}}+{\mathsf{H}})^{-1}{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{bath}},

(16)

and

{\mathsf{G}}[\omega]\!:=\!\left[(\omega\,{\mathsf{I}}-{\mathsf{H}})\,\Omega-{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm om}\,\Omega\,(\omega\,{\mathsf{I}}+{\mathsf{H}})^{-1}{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm om}\right]^{-1},

(17)

acts as the frequency-domain Green’s function of the system. It fully characterizes the linear response of the quadratures to external probe and noise sources. Here,
{\mathsf{I}}
denotes the
4\times 4
identity matrix.

To connect the internal dynamics with experimentally measurable quantities, we now relate the intracavity quadratures to the outgoing probe field through the standard input–output formalism
Gardiner and Collett 1985
,

{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{out}}[\omega]={\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\omega]-{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{probe}}\,{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}[\omega].

(18)

Substituting the intracavity solution given in Eq. (
II.2
) into Eq. (
18
) expresses the output field entirely in terms of the system Green’s function

\displaystyle{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{out}}[\omega]\!

\displaystyle=\!\left({\mathsf{I}}-\kappa\,{\mathsf{G}}[\omega]\right)\,{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\omega]-\sqrt{\kappa}\,{\mathsf{G}}[\omega]\,{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{bath}}\,{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\omega]

\displaystyle\quad-\sqrt{\kappa}\,{\mathsf{G}}[\omega]\,{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathrm{op}}\,{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}[-\omega]-\sqrt{\kappa}\,{\mathsf{G}}[\omega]\,{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathrm{ob}}\,{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}}[-\omega],

(19)

thereby making explicit how the internal response is transferred to observable probe signals. Here we have assumed
\kappa\!:=\!\kappa_{a}\!=\!\kappa_{b}
the equal coupling strength of the probe fields to the photon and phonon cavity modes, respectively, such that
{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{probe}}=\kappa\,{\mathsf{I}}
i.e. the photon and phonon field will suffer equal loss due to interaction with the probe fields.

We next turn to a statistical description of the output field in terms of its first and second moments. Denoting amplitude expectation values by
{\mathsf{S}}\!:=\!\langle{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}\rangle
and the symmetric covariance matrix
{\mathsf{V}}\!:=\!\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\{{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{j},{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{k}\}\rangle-\langle{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{j}\rangle\langle{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{k}\rangle
. Since the quadrature vectors introduced above describe fluctuations, all first moments vanish; in particular

{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{out}}[\omega]=0.

(20)

The fluctuation properties are therefore encoded entirely in the covariance matrix of the output quadratures and is given by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{out}}[\omega]

\displaystyle\!=\!({\mathsf{I}}-\kappa\,{\mathsf{G}}[\omega])\,{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F_{+}}}^{\mathrm{in}}({\mathsf{I}}-\kappa\,{\mathsf{G}}[\omega])^{\rm T}

\displaystyle\quad+\kappa\,{\mathsf{G}}[\omega]\,\Xi\,{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{L_{\pm},F_{-}}}^{\mathrm{in}}\Xi^{\rm T}{\mathsf{G}}[\omega]^{\rm T}.

(21)

Here,
{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F_{\pm}}}^{\mathrm{in}}\!=\!{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\pm\omega]
and
{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{L{\pm},F_{-}}}^{\mathrm{in}}\!=\!{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\omega]\oplus{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}[-\omega]\oplus{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}}[-\omega]

with the first term in Eq. (
21
) describing the transformation of probe fluctuations by the system response, while the second term accounts for additional noise entering through the bath and the negative-frequency sector via the
4\times 12
optomechanical coupling matrix
\Xi:=({\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{bath}}|{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathrm{op}}|{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathrm{ob}})
.

With these observable output quantities established, we are now in position to examine how an external perturbation modifies them through its effect on the Green’s function, as developed in the following section.

III
Sensitivity analysis

III.1
Perturbation of system parameters

We now consider the system’s response to a weak external perturbation characterized by a small parameter
\theta
. The perturbation is implemented directly at the level of the dynamical matrix
H
given in Eq. (
II.1
) as follows

\textbf{H}\rightarrow\textbf{H}-\theta\,\textbf{n},

(22)

where
n
encodes the structure of the perturbation across the system degrees of freedom, specifying which entries (and therefore which physical parameters of
H
) are affected. In this sense,
n
acts as a selection or weighting operator that identifies the subspace, modes, or coupling channels to which the perturbation is applied, thereby determining how the external influence is distributed within the system.

In quadrature space, we note that the counterpart
{\mathsf{H}}
of
H
(see Eq. (
10
)) is modified as

{\mathsf{H}}\;\rightarrow\;{\mathsf{H}}-\theta\,{\mathsf{n}},

(23)

where
{\mathsf{n}}
denotes the real representation of the perturbation matrix in the doubled Fourier-space basis, explicitly given by

{\mathsf{n}}=\mathcal{R}[\textbf{n}],

(24)

with
\mathcal{R}
defined in Eq. (
11
). This construction preserves the action of the original complex perturbation matrix
n
when expressed in the real-valued block form adopted in Fourier space.

As a consequence of this shift in the dynamical matrix, the Green’s function introduced in Eq. (
17
) is modified into the perturbed resolvent

{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}=-\,\Omega\Big[{\mathsf{H}}_{-}+\theta\,{\mathsf{n}}+{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm om}^{\prime}(\theta\,{\mathsf{n}}-{\mathsf{H}}_{+})^{-1}{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm om}^{\prime}\Big]^{-1},

(25)

where we have dropped the explicit
\omega
dependence and introduced the shorthand notations

{\mathsf{H}}_{\pm}:=\omega\,{\mathsf{I}}\pm{\mathsf{H}},\quad{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm om}^{\prime}:={\mathsf{K}}_{\rm om}\,\Omega.

(26)

The Eq. (
25
) explicitly captures how the external perturbation alters the system response in frequency space through both the direct modification of
{\mathsf{H}}
and its indirect effect on the coupling-mediated term.
At this stage, the structure of the problem becomes particularly transparent: the perturbation enters not only directly through the term
\theta\,{\mathsf{n}}
, but also indirectly through the inverse resolvent in the coupled sector.

To proceed analytically, we assume that
{\mathsf{H}}_{+}
is invertible and that
\theta
is sufficiently small to justify a perturbative expansion. We therefore expand the resolvent using a Neumann series. To first order in
\theta
, one finds

(\theta\,{\mathsf{n}}-{\mathsf{H}}_{+})^{-1}=-{\mathsf{H}}_{+}^{-1}-\theta\,{\mathsf{H}}_{+}^{-1}{\mathsf{n}}{\mathsf{H}}_{+}^{-1}+\mathcal{O}(\theta^{2}).

(27)

Substituting this expansion back into Eq. (
25
) yields

\displaystyle{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}\approx

\displaystyle-\Omega\left[{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}+\theta\,{\mathsf{n}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\right]^{-1},

(28)

where the effective dynamical generator and the perturbation matrix are respectively given as

\displaystyle{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle\!:=\!{\mathsf{H}}_{-}+{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm om}^{\prime}{\mathsf{H}}_{+}^{-1}{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm om}^{\prime},

(29)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{n}}_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle\!:=\!{\mathsf{n}}+{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm om}^{\prime}{\mathsf{H}}_{+}^{-1}{\mathsf{n}}{\mathsf{H}}_{+}^{-1}{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm om}^{\prime}.

(30)

These expressions reveals a clear physical picture. In the absence of optomechanical coupling i.e.,
{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{om}}\!=\!0
, the perturbation
\theta\,{\mathsf{n}}
would act directly and locally within the system. However, the presence of the coupling matrix
{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm om}
qualitatively changes this behavior: the perturbation is now “dressed” by virtual excursions into the complementary
(\omega{\mathsf{I}}+{\mathsf{H}})
sector. As a result, the system does not only respond to the bare perturbation, but also to an induced, nonlocal contribution mediated by the resolvent
{\mathsf{H}}_{+}^{-1}
.

Since the Green’s function now explicitly depends on the perturbation parameter
\theta
, all output quantities derived from it inherit the same dependence. In particular, the output covariance matrix given in Eq. (
21
) becomes
\theta
-dependent and are accordingly denoted by
{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F,\theta}}^{\mathrm{out}}
(dropping the
\omega
dependence) as

\displaystyle{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F,\theta}}^{\mathrm{out}}

\displaystyle\!=\!({\mathsf{I}}-\kappa\,{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta})\,{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F_{+}}}^{\mathrm{in}}\,({\mathsf{I}}-\kappa\,{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta})^{{\rm T}}

\displaystyle\quad\!+\!\kappa\,{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}\,\Xi\,{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{L_{\pm},F_{-}}}^{\mathrm{in}}\Xi^{{\rm T}}{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}^{{\rm T}}.

(31)

Thus, the external perturbation influences the internal dynamical response encoded in
{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}
, and propagates directly into the measurable output observables.

III.2
Gaussian estimation and the role of Green’s function

In light of Eq. (
20
), the probe field exiting the system is fully specified by Eq. (
31
). The parameter
\theta
may then be estimated within Gaussian estimation theory, with the Fisher information quantifying the estimation precision.

III.2.1
Fisher Information and precision bounds in Gaussian systems

Consider a family of quantum states
\rho(\theta)
depending smoothly on an unknown parameter
\theta
. A measurement performed on the system produces an outcome
\xi
distributed according to the conditional probability density
p(\lambda|\theta)
. The amount of information about
\theta
that can be extracted from the measurement outcomes is quantified by the classical Fisher information (CFI)
Paris 2009
,

F(\theta)=\int d\lambda\,p(\lambda|\theta)\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln p(\lambda|\theta)\right]^{2}.

(32)

The CFI determines the precision attainable for a given measurement strategy. Optimising over all physically allowed measurements leads to the quantum Fisher information (QFI), which depends solely on
\rho_{\theta}
and is defined as

\mathcal{F}(\theta)=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\rho(\theta)L_{\theta}^{2}\right],

(33)

where
L_{\theta}
denotes the symmetric logarithmic derivative (SLD), implicitly determined by

\frac{\partial\rho(\theta)}{\partial\theta}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\rho(\theta)L_{\theta}+L_{\theta}\rho(\theta)\Bigr).

(34)

Suppose that
\nu
independent copies of the state are measured and used to construct an unbiased estimator
\tilde{\theta}
of the true parameter value. Its mean-squared error,

\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{\theta})\!=\!\mathbb{E}_{p(\lambda|\theta)}\!\left[(\tilde{\theta}-\theta)^{2}\right],

(35)

where the expectation value is defined as
\mathbb{E}_{p(\lambda|\theta)}[\bullet]\!:=\!\int d\lambda\,p(\lambda|\theta)\,\bullet
. In the quantum case, this classical average is naturally replaced by the trace over the quantum state,
\mathbb{E}_{p(\lambda|\theta)}[\bullet]\;\longrightarrow\;\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\rho_{\theta}\,\bullet\right]
. In this setting, the variance is constrained by the hierarchy

\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{\theta})\geq\frac{1}{\nu F(\theta)}\geq\frac{1}{\nu\mathcal{F}(\theta)}.

(36)

The first inequality is the classical Cramér–Rao bound associated with a fixed measurement strategy, whereas the second follows from the fact that no measurement can provide more information than allowed by the QFI. It is therefore convenient to introduce the classical and quantum precision benchmarks

\delta_{\mathrm{C}}^{2}:=\frac{1}{F(\theta)},\qquad\delta_{\mathrm{Q}}^{2}:=\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}(\theta)},

(37)

so that every unbiased estimation protocol obeys

\nu\,\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{\theta})\geq\delta_{\mathrm{C}}^{2}\geq\delta_{\mathrm{Q}}^{2}.

(38)

The quantity
\delta_{\mathrm{Q}}^{2}
represents the ultimate sensitivity limit achievable in principle, while
\delta_{\mathrm{C}}^{2}
characterises the best precision attainable with a specific measurement scheme.

For Gaussian states, the QFI can be expressed entirely in terms of the first and second moments of the quadrature operators. Let
{\mathsf{S}}
and
{\mathsf{V}}
denote the mean quadrature vector and covariance matrix, respectively. The QFI corresponding to parameter
\theta
is then given by
Jiang 2014

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\theta}=\big(\partial_{\theta}{\mathsf{S}}\big)^{\rm T}{\mathsf{V}}^{-1}\big(\partial_{\theta}{\mathsf{S}}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\left({\mathsf{V}}^{-1}\partial_{\theta}{\mathsf{V}}\right)^{2}\right].

(39)

The above expression naturally separates the contributions originating from the displacement of the Gaussian state in phase space and from the changes in its noise properties encoded in the covariance matrix.

For a specific measurement protocol producing Gaussian-distributed outcomes, the corresponding CFI also assumes an analogous structure. In particular, for measurement statistics of the form

\displaystyle p(\bm{x}|\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}\det{\mathsf{C}}(\theta)}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{x}-\bar{\bm{x}}(\theta)\right)^{\rm T}{\mathsf{C}}(\theta)^{-1}\left(\bm{x}-\bar{\bm{x}}(\theta)\right)\right],

(40)

where
\bar{\bm{x}}(\theta)
and
{\mathsf{C}}(\theta)
denote the mean vector and covariance matrix of the measured observables, respectively, the CFI is given by
Kay 1993

\displaystyle F_{\theta}=\big(\partial_{\theta}\bar{\bm{x}}\big)^{\rm T}{\mathsf{C}}^{-1}\big(\partial_{\theta}\bar{\bm{x}}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\left({\mathsf{C}}^{-1}\partial_{\theta}{\mathsf{C}}\right)^{2}\right].

(41)

As in the quantum case, the two terms separately account for sensitivity in the mean and in the covariance of the measurement outcomes.

III.2.2
Reduction to fluctuation and dependence on Green’s function

In the present formulation, the fundamental operators are decomposed into coherent amplitudes and fluctuation operators as introduced in Eq. (
2
), and all physically relevant observables are expressed in terms of the latter, implying that the mean quadrature vector vanishes identically (cf. Eq. (
20
)). Consequently, the first-moment contribution in Eq. (
39
) does not contribute, and both QFI and CFI are fully determined by second-order correlations. In particular, in terms of
{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F,\theta}}^{\mathrm{out}}
from Eq. (
31
), the QFI reduces to
2
2

2

Although
\hat{F}_{b}
is an auxiliary mode, one may project onto the measurable optical mode
\hat{F}_{a}
and compute the Fisher information using only the corresponding reduced covariance matrix. Since the scaling is governed entirely by the underlying response function itself [see Eqs. (
44
) and (
47
)], excluding
\hat{F}_{b}
does not modify the scaling behavior of the Fisher information—the primary focus of this work—and affects only the associated prefactors.

\mathcal{F}_{\theta}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\left(\left({\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F,\theta}}^{\mathrm{out}}\right)^{-1}\partial_{\theta}{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F,\theta}}^{\mathrm{out}}\right)^{2}\right].

(42)

Further insight into the scaling of
\mathcal{F}_{\theta}
follows from the structure of the response function in Eq. (
28
). In particular, the output covariance inherits its leading dependence from the Green’s function, such that
{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F,\theta}}^{\mathrm{out}}\sim{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}^{2}
, while its parameter derivative scales as
\partial_{\theta}{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F,\theta}}^{\mathrm{out}}\sim{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}\,\partial_{\theta}{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}
. Using the identity
\partial_{\theta}{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}\!=\!-\kappa\,{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}\,{\mathsf{n}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\,{\mathsf{\Omega}}\,{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}
,
it follows that

\big({\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F,\theta}}^{\mathrm{out}}\big)^{-1}\partial_{\theta}{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F,\theta}}^{\mathrm{out}}\sim{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta},

(43)

and hence from Eq. (
42
), we note that

\mathcal{F}_{\theta}\sim\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}^{2}\right],

(44)

demonstrating that the achievable sensitivity is governed by the square of system Green’s function.

The classical measurement strategy considered in this work is the heterodyne detection of the probe output field, in which both quadratures are measured simultaneously, resulting in an effective addition of vacuum noise (see Sec.
IV.1
for details). The corresponding measured amplitude and covariance matrix entering Eq. (
41
) is
Weedbrook et al. 2012

{\mathsf{x}}_{\mathrm{F,\theta}}^{\mathrm{out}}\!=\!{\mathsf{S}}_{\mathrm{F,\theta}}^{\mathrm{out}},\qquad{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathrm{F,\theta}}\!=\!{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F,\theta}}^{\mathrm{out}}+{\mathsf{I}}.

(45)

Owing to the same reasoning of vanishing first moments, we write

F_{\theta}\!=\!\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[\left(\left({\mathsf{C}}_{\mathrm{F,\theta}}^{\mathrm{out}}\right)^{-1}\partial_{\theta}{\mathsf{C}}_{\mathrm{F,\theta}}^{\mathrm{out}}\right)^{2}\right].

(46)

Applying the same Green’s-function scaling arguments to the CFI, one obtains

F_{\theta}\sim\mathrm{Tr}\!\left[{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}^{2}\right].

(47)

In summary, both classical and quantum precision bounds are entirely determined by the output covariance matrix, whose structure is governed by the system response function
{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}
. This response function therefore controls how the parameter
\theta
is imprinted onto the measured output.

III.3
Role of singular effective dynamical generator

To make the role of the Green’s function explicit, we first consider the regime in which the effective dynamical generator
{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}
appearing in the Green’s function in Eq. (
28
) is invertible, i.e.

\det{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\neq 0.

(48)

In this case, the inverse entering the Green’s function admits the Neumann expansion

\left({\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}+\theta\,{\mathsf{n}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\right)^{-1}={\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(-\theta\,{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}\,{\mathsf{n}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\right)^{k}.

(49)

Taking the limit
\theta\to 0
term by term, all contributions with
k\geq 1
vanish, and only the leading term survives, yielding

\lim_{\theta\to 0}\left({\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}+\theta\,{\mathsf{n}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\right)^{-1}={\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1}.

(50)

Since the Green’s function
{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}
is constructed from this inverse and depends on it continuously, it follows that

\lim_{\theta\to 0}{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}=-\,{\mathsf{\Omega}}\,{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1},

(51)

Consequently, all output observables derived from
{\mathsf{G}}_{\theta}
become independent of
\theta
in this limit. In particular, the output amplitude and covariance matrix reduce to their unperturbed forms, implying that no residual sensitivity to
\theta
remains at the level of the measured statistics. As a result, the QFI associated with estimation of
\theta
is constant in this regime, reflecting the absence of any observable dependence on the perturbation parameter in the
\theta\to 0
limit.

We now turn to the complementary regime in which the effective dynamical generator becomes singular,

\det{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}=0.

(52)

In contrast to the regular case discussed above, the inverse entering the Green’s function is no longer well-defined at
\theta=0
, and the perturbation plays a crucial role in regularizing the resolvent. In this situation, the inverse admits the so called Sain–Massey expansion
Sain and Massey 1969
;
Zhou 2003
,

\left({\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}+\theta\,{\mathsf{n}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\right)^{-1}\!=\theta^{-s}\left({\mathsf{X}}_{0}+\theta\,{\mathsf{X}}_{1}+\cdots\right),\quad{\mathsf{X}}_{0}\!\neq\!0.

(53)

The exponent
s
characterizes the leading divergence of the inverse and is determined by the degeneracy structure of
{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}
at
\theta=0
, i.e., by the dimensionality and coupling structure of its null space. Physically, it quantifies how strongly the resolvent amplifies perturbations near the singular point, with larger
s
corresponding to stronger sensitivity to the regularizing parameter
\theta
. The matrices
{\mathsf{X}}_{0},{\mathsf{X}}_{1},\ldots
describe successive corrections to this dominant divergent contribution. They are fully determined by the structure of
{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
{\mathsf{n}}_{\mathrm{eff}}
and can, in principle, be obtained systematically from the resolvent expansion. In this sense, they act as generalized residues that encode both the leading divergence and the subleading response of the Green’s function. Importantly, no further assumptions beyond the existence of the expansion are required: once the singular structure is fixed, both the pole order and all coefficients are uniquely determined by the underlying dynamical generator. For detailed construction of Sain-Massey expansion, see Appendix of
Naikoo et al. 2023
.

This non-analytic dependence on
\theta
is consequently transferred to the Green’s function, leading to a strongly enhanced sensitivity to the perturbation. As a consequence, the QFI exhibits a divergent scaling for small
\theta

Naikoo et al. 2023
;
Naikoo et al. 2025
,

\mathcal{F}_{\theta}\approx\theta^{-s}\left(A+\order{\theta}\right),\quad A\neq 0,

(54)

where the coefficient
A
is given by

\displaystyle A\!:=\!\operatorname{Tr}\left[\Bigl({\mathsf{\Omega}}({\mathsf{X}}_{0}^{{\rm T}})^{-1}{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}^{-1}\,{\mathsf{n}}\,{\mathsf{X}}_{0}\,{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}\,{\mathsf{X}}_{0}^{{\rm T}}\,{\mathsf{\Omega}}\Bigr)+\Bigl({\mathsf{\Omega}}\,{\mathsf{X}}_{0}\,{\mathsf{n}}\,{\mathsf{\Omega}}\Bigr)^{{\rm T}}\right]^{2},

(55)

with
{\mathsf{Y}}_{0}=({\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\,{\mathsf{\Omega}}+\kappa\,{\mathsf{I}})\,{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F_{+}}}^{\mathrm{in}}\,({\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\,{\mathsf{\Omega}}+\kappa\,{\mathsf{I}})^{\rm T}+\kappa\,\Xi\,{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{L_{\pm},F_{-}}}\Xi^{\rm T}
, where
{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F_{+}}}^{\mathrm{in}}
,
{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{L_{\pm},F_{-}}}
, and
\Xi
appear in Eq. (
21
). Thus, in sharp contrast to the non-singular case—where the response becomes insensitive to
\theta
in the limit
\theta\to 0
—the singular regime leads to a divergent QFI, reflecting a strongly enhanced sensitivity of the system to the perturbation.

IV
Optomechanically Controlled Singularity and Sensing Advantage

Having established that a divergent scaling of the QFI arises only when the effective dynamical generator
{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}
is singular, we now demonstrate how this condition can be physically realized and controlled in an optomechanical setting. In particular, we show that the optomechanical interaction provides a tunable mechanism to access the singular regime, thereby enabling enhanced sensing performance.

The explicit form of the dynamical matrix
{\mathsf{H}}
entering the definition of
{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}
in Eq. (
29
) is given by

{\mathsf{H}}=\begin{pmatrix}\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}-2g_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\operatorname{Re}(\beta)&-g_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}|\alpha|&\gamma_{a}&0\\
-g_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}|\alpha|&\omega_{\mathrm{M}}&0&\gamma_{b}\\
-\gamma_{a}&0&\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}-2g_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\operatorname{Re}(\beta)&-g_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}|\alpha|\\

(56)

where
\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
,
g_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
, and
\gamma_{a(b)}
were introduced in Eqs. (
3
)–(
4

denotes the absolute value of

from Eq. (
2
).
As discussed in Sec.
III.3
, the emergence of a divergent response is associated with the singularity of the effective generator. For convenience, we restate the singularity condition introduced in Eq. (
52
) of Sec.

in terms of the structure of
{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}

\det\!\left({\mathsf{H}}_{-}+{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{om}}^{\prime}{\mathsf{H}}_{+}^{-1}{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{om}}^{\prime}\right)=0,

(57)

defined in Eq. (
10

{\mathsf{H}}_{\pm}

26

To quantify the sensing performance, we consider the estimation of a real parameter
\theta
that perturbs both the cavity detuning
\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
and the mechanical frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{M}}

\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\rightarrow\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}+\theta,\qquad\omega_{\mathrm{M}}\rightarrow\omega_{\mathrm{M}}+\theta.

in the matrix

\widetilde{{\mathsf{n}}}_{\mathrm{eff}}:={\mathsf{n}}_{\mathrm{eff}}({\mathsf{n}}={\mathsf{I}})={\mathsf{I}}-{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm om}^{\prime}({\mathsf{H}}_{+}^{-1})^{2}{\mathsf{K}}_{\rm om}^{\prime}.

(59)

For the present choice of perturbation, the Green’s function Eq. (
28

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathsf{G}}}_{\theta}\approx

\displaystyle-\Omega\left[{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathrm{eff}}+\theta\,\widetilde{{\mathsf{n}}}_{\mathrm{eff}}\right]^{-1}.

(60)

Equation (
59

{\mathsf{I}}

into a generally non-diagonal effective perturbation that influences other system parameters.

IV.0.1
Sensing in non-singular regime :
{\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{om}}={\mathsf{0}}

\theta

In contrast to the nonsingular case, optomechanical coupling enables access to a regime in which the system response becomes highly sensitive to perturbations. This enhanced sensitivity thus arises as a direct consequence of the optomechanically induced singular structure. The comparison highlights the central role of the coupling strength: while the absence of optomechanical coupling precludes the existence of any parameter choice that reaches the singular regime, increasing the coupling makes such singular operating points accessible, where enhanced sensitivity and divergent precision can be achieved.

These findings indicate that the enhanced sensitivity in the generic system considered in this work is intrinsically tied to the optomechanically induced singularity of the effective dynamical generator. Taken together, our analysis demonstrates that optomechanical interaction constitutes a key resource for engineering singular response, enabling substantial improvements in sensing performance.

IV.1
Experimental relevance of heterodyne detection in optomechanical systems

In cavity optomechanical experiments, the mechanical displacement is ultimately inferred from the phase and amplitude fluctuations imprinted on the outgoing optical field. A widely used detection strategy for accessing both quadratures simultaneously is the heterodyne detection.

In a typical implementation, illustrated schematically in Fig.
3
, the output field from the optomechanical cavity is first combined with vacuum at a balanced 50:50 beam splitter, generating two output ports that are subsequently analyzed through independent balanced homodyne measurements
Walton et al. 2021
. Each output is mixed with a strong local oscillator on a second beam splitter and detected via a pair of photodiodes, thereby enabling separate measurements of the two conjugate field quadratures. Together, the dual homodyne measurements realize a heterodyne detection scheme that simultaneously reconstructs both quadratures of the optical field. Consequently, heterodyne detection provides access to the full Gaussian statistics of the output field, at the expense of an additional unit of vacuum noise [cf. Eq. (
45
)] arising from the vacuum mode introduced at the initial beam splitter
Ferraro et al. 2005
;
Weedbrook et al. 2012
.

In optomechanical systems, heterodyne detection and related dual-homodyne measurement schemes have become standard tools for probing mechanical motion and extracting the full Gaussian statistics of the output field. Such techniques have played a central role in experiments on ground-state cooling and sideband thermometry
Teufel et al. 2011a
;
Clark et al. 2017
, including recent demonstrations of high-purity room-temperature quantum optomechanics
Dania et al. 2025
. Heterodyne measurements have furthermore been extensively employed for the observation and interpretation of motional sideband asymmetry and quantum noise signatures in cavity optomechanical systems
Børkje 2016
, as well as for high-precision displacement sensing and the recovery of quantum correlations in optomechanical spectra
Monteiro and Lang 2017
. These measurement protocols are now routinely implemented across a broad range of optomechanical platforms, including membrane-in-the-middle and photonic-crystal cavity systems
Aspelmeyer et al. 2014
;
Safavi-Naeini et al. 2013
.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we developed a theoretical framework for analyzing parameter estimation in optomechanical systems based on a frequency-domain Green’s function formalism combined with Gaussian quantum estimation theory. Within this framework, perturbations acting on the system degrees of freedom are encoded in the dynamical response and become accessible through measurements on the output cavity field. This approach provides a direct connection between the response properties of the driven optomechanical system and the achievable precision in parameter estimation.

Our analysis shows that the optomechanical interaction fundamentally modifies the structure of the system Green’s function and enables operation in the vicinity of an effectively singular configuration. Perturbations that shift the system toward or away from this configuration produce disproportionately large variations in the response function and, consequently, in the output field statistics. As a result, the information carried by the output field about the perturbation parameter is strongly amplified. This amplification manifests itself in a divergent scaling of the QFI and the corresponding estimation precision. Furthermore, the CFI associated with heterodyne detection exhibits the same asymptotic scaling as the quantum counterpart, demonstrating that this experimentally accessible measurement scheme captures the full scaling advantage provided by the amplified response.

Away from the regime of strong optomechanical coupling, the response no longer exhibits the near-singular behavior responsible for the enhanced sensitivity. In particular, for vanishing optomechanical coupling the Green’s function remains regular and analytic in the perturbation parameter, leading to a saturation of both the QFI and CFI.

These findings demonstrate that optomechanical interactions provide a controllable mechanism for accessing regimes of enhanced dynamical susceptibility, in which weak perturbations induce amplified changes in the output field and consequently yield improved parameter-estimation precision.

{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\omega]\\
{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}[-\omega]\\
{\hat{{\mathsf{S}}}}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}}[-\omega]\end{pmatrix}.

(107)

The scattering matrix
\mathcal{S}[\omega]
therefore characterizes the complete linear transformation between the incoming and outgoing quadrature operators. Its upper blocks, relevant for our calculation are given by

\mathcal{S}[\omega]=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}{\mathsf{I}}-\kappa\,{\mathsf{G}}[\omega]&-\sqrt{\kappa}\,{\mathsf{G}}[\omega]\,\Xi\\
\hline\cr\dots&\dots\end{array}\right),

(108)

where

\Xi_{4\times 12}:=\left({\mathsf{K}}_{\mathrm{bath}}~|~{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathrm{op}}~|~{\mathsf{T}}_{\mathrm{ob}}\right).

(109)

The covariance matrix of the output fields follows directly from the linearity of the scattering relation. The covariance matrix transforms according to

{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathrm{Total}}[\omega]=\mathcal{S}[\omega]\,{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{Total}}[\omega]\,\mathcal{S}[\omega]^{\mathrm{T}},

(110)

which is the standard transformation rule for Gaussian states under linear bosonic operations. The total input covariance matrix takes a block-diagonal form,

{\mathsf{V}}^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{Total}}[\omega]=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F_{+}}}^{\mathrm{in}}&0\\
0&{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{L_{\pm},F_{-}}}^{\mathrm{in}}\end{pmatrix},

(111)

consistent with the independent preparation of the probe and bath inputs. The matrix
{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F_{+}}}^{\mathrm{in}}\!:=\!{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\omega]
and the lower block is given by

{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{L_{\pm},F_{-}}}^{\mathrm{in}}:={\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\omega]\oplus{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}[-\omega]\oplus{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}}[-\omega].

(112)

Using the block structure of
\mathcal{S}[\omega]
, one obtains the covariance matrix of the outgoing probe fields as

\displaystyle{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{out}}[\omega]

\displaystyle=({\mathsf{I}}-\kappa\,{\mathsf{G}}[\omega])\,{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F_{+}}}^{\mathrm{in}}\,({\mathsf{I}}-\kappa\,{\mathsf{G}}[\omega])^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle\quad+\kappa\,{\mathsf{G}}[\omega]\,\Xi\,{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{L_{\pm},F_{-}}}^{\mathrm{in}}\Xi^{\mathrm{T}}\,{\mathsf{G}}[\omega]^{\mathrm{T}}.

(113)

This expression corresponds to Eq. (
21
) of the main text. The first term describes the transformation of the incoming probe fluctuations through the cavity response, while the second term accounts for the additional noise contribution originating from the bath and the interaction-induced coupling to the negative-frequency sector. The probe and bath inputs are assumed to be thermal states characterized by covariance matrices

{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\pm\omega]=(2n_{\pm\omega}+1)\,{\mathsf{I}},\quad{\mathsf{V}}_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{in}}[\pm\omega]=(2m_{\pm\omega}+1)\,{\mathsf{I}},

(114)

with occupation numbers
n_{\pm\omega}
and
m_{\pm\omega}
, respectively. Finally, since the system is probed exclusively with positive-frequency fields, the negative-frequency input probe is taken to be in the vacuum state, implying

n_{-\omega}=0.

(115)

Under this assumption, all nontrivial correlations in the output fields arise from the positive-frequency probe fluctuations together with the frequency-mixing processes induced by the optomechanical interaction.
