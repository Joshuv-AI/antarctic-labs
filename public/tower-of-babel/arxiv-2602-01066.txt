Simple and Robust Quality Disclosure: The Power of Quantile Partition
Shipra Agrawal, Yiding Feng, Wei Tang

Abstract
Quality information on online platforms is often conveyed through simple, percentile-based badges and tiers that remain stable across different market environments. Motivated by this empirical evidence, we study robust quality disclosure in a market where a platform commits to a public disclosure policy mapping the seller’s product quality into a signal, and the seller subsequently sets a downstream monopoly price. Buyers have heterogeneous private types and valuations that are linear in quality. We evaluate a disclosure policy via a minimax competitive ratio: its worst-case revenue relative to the Bayesian-optimal disclosure-and-pricing benchmark, uniformly over all prior quality distributions, type distributions, and admissible valuations. Our main results provide a sharp theoretical justification for quantile-partition disclosure. For K K -quantile partition policies, we fully characterize the robust optimum: the optimal worst-case ratio is pinned down by a one-dimensional fixed-point equation and the optimal thresholds follow a backward recursion. We also give an explicit formula for the robust ratio of any quantile partition as a simple “max-over-bins” expression, which explains why the robust-optimal partition allocates finer resolution to upper quantiles and yields tight guarantees such as 1 + 1 K 1+\frac{1}{K} for uniform percentile buckets. In contrast, we show a robustness limit for finite-signal monotone (quality-threshold) partitions, which cannot beat a factor- 2 2 approximation. Technically, our analysis reduces the robust quality disclosure to a robust disclosure design program by establishing a tight functional characterization of all feasible indirect revenue functions.

Introduction

Information about product quality is central to consumer purchase decisions.
BrightLocal’s 2025 Local Consumer Review Survey finds that only 4% of consumers say they never read online business reviews.

In the modern online markets, consumers are inundated with quality-related signals, ranging from platform-generated badges such as “verified purchase,” “top rated,” or “recommended,” to star ratings, written reviews, expert and influencer endorsements, and third-party consumer reports.
These disclosures have become so pervasive that many online platforms now actively mediate quality information as an integral part of the consumers’ shopping experience.

Yet, despite their importance, the signals used in practice are often deliberately simple and remarkably robust across diverse market environments.
Upwork, for instance, assigns percentile-based talent badges to freelancers, such as “Top Rated” (top 10%), “Top Rated Plus” (top 3%), and “Expert-Vetted” (top 1%), and deploys the same badge system across a highly heterogeneous marketplace spanning many skill categories (e.g., software development, graphic design, writing)

(

Such simple and robust disclosure policies have clear several practical advantages: they are easy to implement at scale, readily interpretable to consumers, and require limited coordination between platforms and sellers.

Because quality disclosure directly shapes consumer beliefs, it also influences demand, matching efficiency, and ultimately trade outcomes.
Although the study of quality disclosure dates back to the seminal contributions by
Akerlof 1978
;
Grossman 1981
;
Milgrom 1981

much of this literature evaluates disclosure rules under fairly detailed knowledge about the market environment.
In practice, however, platforms often operate across many categories, regions, and submarkets, and cannot reasonably rely on fine-grained knowledge of either the quality distribution or the demand side (e.g., buyers’ preferences).
Motivated by this perspective, we focus on the following two fundamental practical questions:

When simplicity and robustness are imposed as constraints, what is the best disclosure rule?
How close can such simple and robust disclosure rules come to the revenue performance of fully optimal disclosure that is tailored to the precise market environment?

To formalize this robustness agenda, we introduce a robust quality disclosure framework in which the platform commits to a simple disclosure policy and we evaluate its performance by its worst-case approximation to the optimal revenue across admissible environments.
In our setting, an online platform (or “market designer”) mediates information about a seller’s product quality.
The product has an intrinsic quality level, and the platform commits to a quality-disclosure policy that maps the realized quality into a public signal observed by all buyers. After a signal is realized, the seller posts a signal-contingent price, potentially a different posted price for different signals, which is chosen by maximizing the expected revenue given the buyers’ type distribution and the posterior induced by the signal.
Buyers are heterogeneous: each buyer has a private type capturing their willingness to pay, and their valuation depends on both their type and the (unknown) product quality.
Throughout, we focus on
linear
valuation functions, where a buyer’s value is linear in product quality.

Upon observing the signal, a buyer forms a posterior belief about quality and purchases the product if and only if the posted price does not exceed their (posterior) expected valuation.
The seller’s expected revenue is then the posted price times the induced demand under that signal, aggregated over signals and quality realizations.
Our goal is to understand how effectively simple disclosure policies can approximate the performance of optimal ones, uniformly across admissible market environments.

1.1
Our Contributions and Techniques

A robust quality disclosure framework.

As our first contribution,
we introduce and study the robust quality disclosure problem:
the platform seeks a simple disclosure policy from a prescribed class
\mathcal{X}

that performs well uniformly across all admissible market primitives, namely, all prior quality distributions, all consumer type distributions, and all admissible valuation functions. The class
\mathcal{X}
serves as our modeling device for “simplicity” along two dimensions:

•

Detail-freeness –
We let
\mathcal{X}
to encode requirements that the platform’s disclosure rule not rely on fine details of the demand side.
3
3

3

Our detail-freeness requirement on the policy class
\mathcal{X}
echoes the “Wilson doctrine” in mechanism design
(
Wilson 1985
)
, which advocates mechanism-design prescriptions that are as detail-free as possible.

•

Structural simplicity – We restrict
\mathcal{X}
to simple policies that are widely used in practice, such as quantile or monotone partitions.

Performance is measured by the
minimax competitive ratio
, defined as the worst-case ratio between the optimal Bayesian revenue (achievable when the market designer can tailor disclosure policy using full knowledge of the quality distribution, the consumers’ valuation function, and their type distribution) and the revenue generated by the chosen disclosure policy coupled with optimal downstream pricing (optimized for that same valuation function and type distribution) (see
Definition

2.2
).
This formulation captures the practical reality that platforms must design disclosure mechanisms under limited knowledge about both the supply side (quality) and the demand side (preferences), yet still hope to ensure strong performance guarantees in the worst case.

Optimal robust quantile-partition policy.

Our first set of results identifies a natural and practically relevant family of “simple” disclosure rules, i.e.,
K
-quantile
partition policies
where
K
is the number of partitional intervals.
Here, a disclosure policy is a rule that takes a prior quality distribution as input and outputs a signaling scheme, a mapping from product quality realizations to probabilistic signals observed by buyers.
A
K
-quantile partition disclosure policy
fixes quantile thresholds
0=Q_{0}\leq Q_{1}\leq\cdots\leq Q_{K}=1
and, for every prior quality distribution, pools qualities whose
prior quantiles
fall in the same interval
[Q_{r-1},Q_{r}]
into one public signal.
Within this class, we fully characterize the
robustly optimal
policy: the optimal worst-case
competitive ratio
\Gamma_{K}^{*}
is uniquely determined as the solution to a scalar fixed-point equation involving a
K
-fold composition of a specific function, and the corresponding optimal thresholds are derived by a simple backward recursion (see
Theorem

3.1
).

Our characterization yields a sharp
quantitative message: this ratio
\Gamma_{K}^{*}

decreases with
K
, starting at 2 for
K=1
(i.e., no information) and converging to
1
at a rate of
\Gamma_{K}^{*}=1+\Theta(1/K)
(see
Proposition

3.2
).
Notably, our results show that even with just
K=5
signals, the optimal policy guarantees at least
91.71\%
of the Bayesian optimal revenue uniformly across all prior quality distributions, consumer type distributions, and linear valuation functions.
Taken together, these results offer a formal theoretical justification for quantile-partition disclosure rules (e.g., “top 10%, 5%, 1%” labels) that are widely adopted on real-world platforms: despite their simplicity, they provably approximate the Bayesian-optimal revenue uniformly over market environments.

Robustness of general
K
-quantile partition.

Our second set of results provides an explicit, easy-to-use performance formula for any
K
-quantile partition policy.
In particular, the robust competitive ratio depends only on the threshold profile
\mathbf{Q}=(Q_{0},Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{K})
via a remarkably simple “max-over-bins” expression of the form (see
Theorem

3.4
):

\displaystyle\max_{r\in[K]}~\left(1+\frac{Q_{r}-Q_{r-1}}{\left(1+\sqrt{1-Q_{r}}\right)^{2}}\right)~,

which identifies exactly where the worst case comes from: an adversarial instance can concentrate all the revenue loss on a single quantile partition bin, and the overall guarantee is therefore governed by the interval that maximizes the derived expression.
The formula also clarifies why the optimal policy allocates quantile partitions unevenly: to minimize the worst-case ratio, it must equalize the above expression across all bins, which necessarily makes the quantile intervals progressively narrower (i.e., finer discrimination) in the upper quantiles and coarser intervals in the lower quantiles (see
Proposition

3.3
).
This characterization provides a clean performance guarantee for the kind of percentile-tier disclosures used in practice, it also shows that even the most basic uniform percentile buckets (e.g.,
10\%,20\%,\ldots
) achieve a ratio
1+\frac{1}{K}
exactly, which is tight asymptotically w.r.t.
K
(see
Corollary

3.5
).

Robustness limit of quality-threshold partitions.

We also examine alternative “simple” policies such as those based on finite-signal monotone partitions on quality space.
In contrast to quantile partitions, we show that no such finite-signal monotone-partition policy can guarantee better than a factor-
2
approximation, regardless of how the quality thresholds are chosen (see
Theorem

C.1
).
This underscores that the quantile-based disclosure policy, i.e., pooling by prior ranks rather than raw quality levels, is what enables robust improvement with finitely many signals, by adapting the partition to the prior quality distribution while still remaining simple and interpretable.

Our techniques.

Our analysis proceeds by building a two-step reduction that decouples the economic modeling in our problem from the worst-case optimization.
First, a key observation with the affine structure of consumers’ valuations is that a signal affects the revenue only through the
posterior mean
of quality it induces.
Conditional on a posterior mean
x
, the revenue under the optimal downstream pricing against a population of heterogeneous buyers can be summarized by an
indirect revenue
function
h(x)
, defined as the seller’s optimal posted-price revenue when all
buyers share the belief that expected quality equals
x
. We show that every such
h
generated by
any linear valuation function and type distribution is non-negative, non-decreasing, and convex.
This reduces the original economic worst-case optimization to bounding, uniformly over priors
F
and feasible indirect utilities
h
, the worst-case ratio
\mathbb{E}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]
computed under the prior quality distribution to
\mathbb{E}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]
computed under the distribution of posterior means induced by the disclosure policy.

Building on this observation, we introduce a general minimax framework, i.e.,
robust disclosure design
(see
Definition

4.1
), that abstracts away underlying economic primitives: the designer selects a disclosure policy from a prescribed class while optimizing against the worst case over both (i) the prior and (ii) a convex functional class of all feasible indirect utilities.
In this way, the economics of our quality-disclosure model is encoded entirely in the functional constraints imposed on the indirect utility functions
h
; once these constraints are in place, the remaining task becomes a purely geometric optimization problem over a convex set of functions.
A central technical step is then to establish a tight connection between this abstract program and the original robust quality disclosure program: for
K
-quantile partitions,
solving the robust disclosure design program yields bounds that are not only upper bounds but also exactly achievable in the original economic setting.

Formally, robust disclosure design is a relaxation of the robust quality disclosure problem, because it replaces the set of indirect revenue functions induced by admissible valuation and type distributions with a larger convex functional space.
Perhaps surprisingly, we show that this relaxation is nevertheless tight once we impose a natural linear lower bound satisfied by all induced indirect revenue functions
(i.e.,
h(x)\geq x\cdot h(1)
).
Intuitively, this inequality captures a minimal “monotone scaling” property of revenue with respect to expected quality, and it turns out to be the only additional structure needed to pin down the worst case.
This reduction, translating our economic quality-disclosure model into functional constraints on the induced indirect revenue function, is the central tool behind our tractable, exact characterization.

The remaining task then reduces to solve the robust disclosure design problem (see
Theorem

4.2
), which we use the following two tools.
First, after normalizing by scale (e.g.,
setting
h(1)=1
), we prove an
extreme-point reduction
: every feasible indirect function
h
admits an integral representation as a mixture of hinge functions
h_{c}(x)=\max\{c,h\}
, and the worst-case ratio is attained at such an extreme point, which significantly reduces the inner optimization over functions to an optimization over a single parameter
c\in[0,1]
that parameterizes the hinge functions (see
Lemma

4.5
anad
Lemma

4.6
).
Second, exploiting the piecewise-linear structure of extremal function
h_{c}
, we show that for a fixed quantile partition, only the unique bin (i.e., the quantile partition interval) containing the cutoff
c
can generate the relevant loss compared to the benchmark, leading directly to the closed-form max-over-bins expression above.
We then show that optimizing the thresholds becomes a balancing argument: minimax optimality requires equalizing the bin-wise worst-case terms, which yields the backward recursion and the scalar equation characterizing
\Gamma_{K}^{*}
.
Finally, we establish the tightness for the original robust quality disclosure problem by explicitly constructing model primitives (including the prior quality distribution, a valuation function and a type distribution) that implement the hinge-shaped indirect utilities and match the upper bound
(see
Lemma

4.3
).

1.2
Related Work

Quality disclosure.

The study of quality disclosure in markets with uncertain product quality has a long history in information economics,
dating back to the seminal work of the

Akerlof 1978
’s
(
Akerlof 1978
)
“lemons” model,
which shows how quality uncertainty and asymmetric information can generate adverse selection.
Subsequent work by
Grossman 1981
;
Milgrom 1981
studies the implications of mandatory or voluntary disclosure under unraveling, and shows that when the sender cannot commit to a disclosure rule, strategic incentives can lead to full disclosure as the unique equilibrium outcome.
Later work then studies the settings where the seller/sender can commit to a disclosure policy before observing private information
(
Rayo and Segal 2010
;
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
)

and shows that partial disclosure can arise in equilibrium.
Our work joins the later strand of where the seller commits to a quality disclosure policy (and then sets signal-contingent monopoly prices).

More recently, a growing literature has examined disclosure policies that interact with pricing and broader market or mechanism design primitives.
Notable examples include the public disclosure in second-price auctions with probabilistic goods
(
Bro Miltersen and Sheffet 2012
;
Emek et al. 2014
)
,
the sale of hard information
(
Ali et al. 2022
)
,
and platform-mediated pricing for disclosure services
(
Gan and Li 2024
)
.
From a mechanism design perspective,
Rayo and Segal 2010
analyze optimal disclosure in a monopolistic setting with endogenous signaling and demonstrate that partial disclosure can be revenue-maximizing.

Li and Shi 2017
show that when a seller can disclose type-dependent additional information about the buyer’s valuation distribution, discriminatory disclosure may strictly outperform full disclosure in terms of revenue.

Wei and Green 2024
;
Guo et al. 2025
further study joint design of transfers/prices and information, highlighting the role of information discrimination and “price-experiment” menus.

Li et al. 2025
study vertical differentiation where a (literal or metaphorical) designer chooses a public signal about product quality before firms set prices.
Their model can be viewed as an extension of our Bayesian optimal signaling-and-pricing benchmark to a competitive environment with multiple sellers.

Our work focuses signal-contingent pricing, wherein the seller posts a (possibly different) price for each realized signal, which also aligns with prior works
(
Rayo and Segal 2010
;
Li et al. 2025
)
.
This framework abstracts away from general transfers, sequential screening, or type-contingent “experiments,” and instead focuses on a practically prevalent design space, namely, the combination of public disclosure and posted pricing.
Within this setting, we aim to understand how well simple disclosure policies can perform in a robust manner, uniformly across all prior quality distributions and consumer preferences (including valuation functions and type distributions).

Robust information design.

There has been a growing interest in designing information policies that are robust to uncertainty about model primitives.
A prominent strand of this literature studies robustness to unknown receiver utilities, often framed in terms of regret minimization or online learning in persuasion settings
(e.g.,
Castiglioni et al. 2020
;
Castiglioni et al. 2021
;
Zu et al. 2024
;
Feng et al. 2022
;
Agrawal et al. 2023
).
Other works consider robustness to uncertainty about receiver types (see, e.g.,
Babichenko et al. 2022
;
Feng et al. 2024
), or to ambiguity in the receiver’s prior belief, typically by optimizing against the worst-case prior within a given set (see, e.g.,
Dworczak and Pavan 2022
;
Kosterina 2022
;
Lin and Li 2025
).

Our paper contributes to this robustness agenda with focusing on a market-oriented setting of quality disclosure coupled with pricing.
Crucially, our notion of robustness is simultaneously uniform over all prior quality distributions (i.e., the prior distributions), all admissible consumer valuation functions (i.e., receiver utilities), and all type distributions. As a result, our notion subsumes and unifies several dimensions of uncertainty that are typically treated in isolation in much of the existing literature.
This makes our robustness criterion notably stronger and more comprehensive than those in prior work, which usually fix all but one source of model uncertainty.

Simple v.s. optimal information/mechanism design.

Our focus on approximation guarantees for simple disclosure policies parallels the broader “simple v.s. optimal” paradigm in mechanism design, which asks how much performance is lost when restricting to simple and interpretable mechanisms (see, e.g.,
Chawla et al. 2010
;
Hartline and Lucier 2010
;
Alaei et al. 2019
;
Feng and Jin 2024
, and the surveys of
Hartline and Roughgarden 2009
;
Chawla and Sivan 2014
;
Roughgarden 2015
for an overview).
A related line of work has recently developed in information design, studying when simple or structured signaling schemes can approximate optimal persuasion outcomes (see, e.g.,
Gradwohl et al. 2021
;
Mensch 2021
;
Bergemann et al. 2022a
;
Kolotilin et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Cai et al. 2025
).

A major focus of this paper is on quantile-based simple disclosure rules (e.g., percentile tiers / quantile partitions), which have also appeared in previous works under different contexts.

Strack and Yang 2024
, for example, study privacy-preserving signaling and propose a canonical disclosure that reveals only the rank of the underlying information; they show that any privacy-preserving disclosure can be obtained by garbling an appropriately reordered quantile signal, which in general is not a finite partitional policy.

Bergemann et al. 2022b
show that in a classic second-price auction, the revenue-maximizing disclosure rule takes an explicit quantile-threshold form, fully separating low values while pooling sufficiently high values.
Finally, our interest in partitional disclosure more broadly connects to work on monotone categorisations of quality
(
Onuchic and Ray 2023
)
and to the broader information-design literature studying when monotone/partitional structures are optimal (see, e.g.,
Dworczak and Martini 2019
;
Kolotilin et al. 2025
).
Unlike these works, our work emphasize robust approximation guarantees for simple quantile partitions rather than environment-specific optimality.

-Quantile Partition

Theorem 3.4

(Robust competitive ratio)
.

For any
K\in\mathbb{N}
and quantile threshold profile

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{V},\\

The above characterization reveals a significant simplification in the structure of the robust quality disclosure problem. Although the original minimax program
\textsc{Rqd}[\mathcal{X}_{K}]
involves optimization over all linear valuation functions, product quality distributions, and consumer type distributions, the worst-case competitive ratio for any fixed
K
-Quantile Partition

\mathbf{Q}
in a remarkably simple way. Specifically, the robust competitive ratio is given by the maximum over
K
explicit expressions. Each expression, indexed by

, corresponds to a candidate worst-case instance (whose precise form will become clearer in our analysis) and whose revenue gap is entirely determined by the upper quantile threshold
Q_{r}
and the quantile margin
Q_{r}-Q_{r-1}
. Moreover, the specific functional form
{(Q_{r}-Q_{r-1})}/{(1+\sqrt{1-Q_{r}})^{2}}

4.1
Robust Disclosure Design Framework

The robust quality disclosure problem, as formulated in program

⁢
Rqd

[
X
]

, involves optimizing over disclosure policies that output signaling schemes without knowledge of the consumer’s preferences, while permitting downstream pricing to fully adapt to those preferences. A key observation is that, under the assumption that the consumer’s valuation function is affine in product quality (
1
), the seller’s expected revenue depends on the signaling scheme only through the
posterior means
of quality it induces. Consequently, the seller’s expected revenue can be expressed as the expectation of a certain convex
indirect utility function

h:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where
h(x)
denotes the maximum revenue attainable when all consumers share a common belief that the product’s expected quality is
x
.
With slight abuse of notation, we use
\Phi(F)
interchangeably to denote both the signaling scheme induced by prior quality distribution
F
and the corresponding distribution over posterior means. Accordingly, we write
x\sim\Phi(F)
to indicate that
x
is a posterior mean drawn from the distribution induced by the signaling scheme
\Phi(F)
under prior
F
.

The shape of the seller’s indirect utility function
h
is determined by the consumer’s valuation function
v
and the type distribution
D
.
Due to the convexity of
h
, the seller’s optimal revenue is achieved by using full-information signaling scheme (see
Corollary

3.6
), and it equals to the expectation of this indirect utility function
h
under the prior quality distribution
F
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim F}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]
.
This observation leads to a crucial simplification, once
h
is fixed, the robust competitive ratio of any disclosure policy
\Phi
reduces to the ratio

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim F}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\Phi(F)}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]}~.

This insight motivates the following general abstraction: the
robust disclosure design
framework, which studies, for a given class
\mathcal{H}
of indirect utility functions, how well a simple disclosure policy
\Phi
performs under the worst-case prior distribution and worst-case indirect utility function
h\in\mathcal{H}
.
Notably, this framework operates entirely at the level of induced posterior distributions and does not require reference to the underlying economic primitives such as valuation functions or type distributions.

(Robust disclosure design)

\displaystyle\inf_{\Phi\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in\mathcal{H}\\
F\in\Delta([0,1])\end{subarray}}~\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim F}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\Phi(F)}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]}~,

(
\textsc{Rdd}[\mathcal{X},\mathcal{H}]
)

x\sim\Phi(F)
denotes the posterior mean induced by the signaling scheme
\Phi(F)
under prior

A disclosure policy

[

Motivated by program

For any price
p\geq 0
, define the demand events

\displaystyle A_{i}(p)\triangleq\left\{\theta:v(\theta,x_{i})\geq p\right\},\quad i\in\{1,2\}~.

The pointwise inequality implies
A_{1}(p)\subseteq A_{2}(p)
, and thus

\displaystyle p\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\theta\sim D}\!\left[{A_{1}(p)}\right]\leq p\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\theta\sim D}\!\left[{A_{2}(p)}\right]~.

Taking the supremum over price
p\geq 0
yields

h_{v,D}(x_{1})\leq h_{v,D}(x_{2})
,
establishing that
h_{v,D}
is non-decreasing.

Convexity.

By
1
, the valuation function
v(\theta,x)
is affine in quality (i.e., in
x
) for each fixed type
\theta
, and non-decreasing in type
\theta
for each fixed quality. This monotonicity ensures that, for any posterior mean
x
, the optimal posted price corresponds to a cutoff type: there exists some
\theta\in[0,1]
such that all types
\theta^{\dagger}\geq\theta
purchase the product, while all types
\theta^{\dagger}<\theta
do not. Consequently, the optimal posted-price revenue function can be written as

\displaystyle h_{v,D}(x)=\sup_{\theta\in[0,1]}\left(1-D(\theta)\right)\cdot v(\theta,x)~.

For each fixed
\theta
, define the auxiliary function

\displaystyle f_{\theta}(x)\triangleq\left(1-D(\theta)\right)\cdot v(\theta,x)~.

Since
v(\theta,\cdot)
is affine in
x
and
1-D(\theta)
is a non-negative constant with respect to
x
, the function
f_{\theta}(\cdot)
is affine (and therefore convex) on
[0,1]
. Finally, note that function
h_{v,D}
is the pointwise supremum of the family
\{f_{\theta}:\theta\in[0,1]\}
. Since the supremum of any collection of convex functions is convex, it guarantees that function
h_{v,D}
is convex on
[0,1]
.

Linear lower bound.

Building on the previous analysis, recall that for each fixed
\theta
, the auxiliary function
f_{\theta}(x)
is affine in
x
. Therefore,

\displaystyle f_{\theta}(x)=(1-x)\cdot f_{\theta}(0)+x\cdot f_{\theta}(1)\geq x\cdot f_{\theta}(1),

where the inequality follows from the non-negativity of the valuation function, which implies
f_{\theta}(0)\geq 0
.
Taking the supremum over
\theta\in[0,1]
on both sides yields

\displaystyle h_{v,D}(x)\geq x\cdot h_{v,D}(1),

as desired.
∎

Given the necessary conditions established in
Lemma

4.1
, it is natural to consider the robust disclosure design program
\textsc{Rdd}[\mathcal{X}_{K},\mathcal{H}]
with a functional space
\mathcal{H}
consisting of indirect utility functions that satisfy these conditions (see
Figure

2(a)
for an illustration). The following theorem characterizes both the optimal robust competitive ratio and the corresponding optimal disclosure policy for this program. Its formal proof is given in
Section

4.4
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Fix any integer
K\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathcal{X}_{K}
denote the class of all
K
-Quantile Partition
policies, and let
\mathcal{H}
be the space of all non-negative, non-decreasing, convex functions
h:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfying
h(x)\geq x\cdot h(1)
for all
x\in[0,1]
. Then:

(i)

The optimal robust competitive ratio of program
\textsc{Rdd}[\mathcal{X}_{K},\mathcal{H}]
is
\Gamma_{K}^{*}
(as defined in
Theorem

3.1
), and the optimal robust
K
-Quantile Partition
policy is the one specified by the quantile threshold profile in Eqn. (
1
) of
Theorem

3.1
.

(ii)

For every
K
-Quantile Partition
policy
\Phi
with quantile thresholds
\mathbf{Q}=(Q_{0},\dots,Q_{K})
, its robust competitive ratio in program
\textsc{Rdd}[\mathcal{X}_{K},\mathcal{H}]
is given by

\displaystyle\Gamma(\Phi\mid\mathcal{H})=\max_{r\in[K]}\left(1+\frac{Q_{r}-Q_{r-1}}{\left(1+\sqrt{1-Q_{r}}\right)^{2}}\right).

We begin by observing that
Theorem

4.2
is the natural counterpart of
Theorems

3.1
and
3.4
, but formulated within the robust disclosure design framework
\textsc{Rdd}[\mathcal{X}_{K},\mathcal{H}]
rather than the original robust quality disclosure problem
\textsc{Rqd}[\mathcal{X}_{K}]
. By the necessary conditions established in
Lemma

4.1
, the inner supremum in
\textsc{Rdd}[\mathcal{X}_{K},\mathcal{H}]
is a relaxation of that in
\textsc{Rqd}[\mathcal{X}_{K}]
, and thus the robust competitive ratio under
\textsc{Rdd}[\mathcal{X}_{K},\mathcal{H}]
provides an upper bound for
\textsc{Rqd}[\mathcal{X}_{K}]
.

Crucially, although these conditions are only necessary (not sufficient), they are precisely strong enough to ensure that this relaxation is
tight
: the upper bound obtained via
\textsc{Rdd}[\mathcal{X}_{K},\mathcal{H}]
matches the true robust competitive ratio of
\textsc{Rqd}[\mathcal{X}_{K}]
. In other words,
Lemma

4.1
identifies the “right” relaxation over the space of indirect utility functions—one that preserves the robust competitive ratio exactly.

This tightness is not automatic. In general, when applying the robust disclosure design framework to a specific economic setting (e.g., downstream pricing in our model), the choice of structural conditions on the indirect utility function space is critical. As we illustrate in
Appendix

B
, there exists another natural necessary condition satisfied by the optimal posted-price revenue function
h_{v,D}
that, if used in place of those in
Lemma

4.1
, leads to a strictly looser upper bound, i.e., it fails to recover the tight characterization in
Theorem

4.2
(see
Figure

3
for the illustration of this natural necessary condition and
Proposition

B.2
for the formal statements).

Given this tight reduction, the proofs of
Theorems

3.1
and
3.4
follow a clean two-step structure: we invoke
Theorem

4.2
to obtain the matching upper bound, and complement it with a matching lower bound, which is established in
Lemma

4.3
below.

Lemma 4.3

(Matching lower bound)
.

Fix any
K\in\mathbb{N}
and a quantile threshold profile
\mathbf{Q}=(Q_{0},Q_{1},\dots,Q_{K})
satisfying
0=Q_{0}\leq Q_{1}\leq\dots\leq Q_{K}=1
. Let
\Phi
be the
K
-Quantile Partition
parameterized by
\mathbf{Q}
, and let

\displaystyle r^{\dagger}\in\argmax_{r\in[K]}\left(1+\frac{Q_{r}-Q_{r-1}}{\left(1+\sqrt{1-Q_{r}}\right)^{2}}\right).

Then the following hold:

•

If
r^{\dagger}\in[K-1]
, there exist a linear valuation function
v
, a consumer type distribution
D\in\Delta([0,1])
, and a product quality distribution
F\in\Delta([0,1])
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\textsc{Opt}_{v,F,D}}{\textsc{Rev}_{v,F,D}(\Phi(F))}=1+\frac{Q_{r^{\dagger}}-Q_{r^{\dagger}-1}}{\left(1+\sqrt{1-Q_{r^{\dagger}}}\right)^{2}}~.

•

If
r^{\dagger}=K
, then for every
\varepsilon\in(0,Q_{K}-Q_{K-1})
, there exist a linear valuation function
v
,
D\in\Delta([0,1])
, and
F\in\Delta([0,1])
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\textsc{Opt}_{v,F,D}}{\textsc{Rev}_{v,F,D}(\Phi(F))}>1+(Q_{K}-Q_{K-1})-\varepsilon~.

Here,
\textsc{Opt}_{v,F,D}
denotes the seller’s expected revenue under the Bayesian optimal signaling and pricing scheme, and
\textsc{Rev}_{v,F,D}(\Phi(F))
denotes the expected revenue under the signaling scheme
\Phi(F)
induced by
\Phi
, given consumer valuation
v
, type distribution
D
, and quality distribution
F
.

The proof of
Lemma

4.3
relies on the following unified construction of the consumer’s valuation function and type distribution.

Example 4.3

(Consumer preference
(v,D)
with
h_{v,D}(x)=\max\{c,x\}
)
.

Fix any
c\in(0,1)
. Let consumer type distribution
D
be a Bernoulli distribution supported on
\{0,1\}
with mean
c
, and define the linear valuation function

\displaystyle v(\theta,\omega)\triangleq\theta(1-\omega)+\omega,\quad\text{for all }(\theta,\omega)\in[0,1]^{2}.

For this pair
(v,D)
, the optimal posted-price revenue function is given by

\displaystyle h_{v,D}(x)=\max\{c,x\},\quad\forall\,x\in[0,1].

Proof of
Lemma

4.3
.

We prove the two cases separately.

Case 1: index
r^{\dagger}\in[K-1]
.
In this case,
define auxiliary notations

\displaystyle s_{r^{\dagger}}\triangleq\frac{\sqrt{1-Q_{r^{\dagger}}}}{1+\sqrt{1-Q_{r^{\dagger}}}}\in(0,1),\qquad\bar{\omega}_{r^{\dagger}}\triangleq(1-Q_{r^{\dagger}})+(Q_{r^{\dagger}}-Q_{r^{\dagger}-1})\cdot s_{r^{\dagger}}\in(0,1).

Consider a Bernoulli quality distribution
F
supported on
\{0,1\}
with mean
\bar{\omega}_{r^{\dagger}}
.

Let
u\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
and define
x=F^{-1}(u)
. Since quality distribution
F
places mass
1-\bar{\omega}_{r^{\dagger}}
at
0
and mass
\bar{\omega}_{r^{\dagger}}
at
1
, there exists a threshold

\displaystyle u_{0}\triangleq 1-\bar{\omega}_{r^{\dagger}}

such that
x=0
for
u\in(0,u_{0}]
and
x=1
for
u\in(u_{0},1]
.
We claim that
u_{0}\in(Q_{r^{\dagger}-1},Q_{r^{\dagger}}]
. To see this, note that

\displaystyle u_{0}=1-\bar{\omega}_{r^{\dagger}}=1-\bigl[(1-Q_{r^{\dagger}})+(Q_{r^{\dagger}}-Q_{r^{\dagger}-1})s_{r^{\dagger}}\bigr]=Q_{r^{\dagger}}-(Q_{r^{\dagger}}-Q_{r^{\dagger}-1})s_{r^{\dagger}}.

Since
s_{r^{\dagger}}\in(0,1)
, it follows that

Q_{r^{\dagger}-1}<u_{0}<Q_{r^{\dagger}}
,
as required.

Let auxiliary notation
m_{r}
denote the posterior mean given signal
r\in[K]
under signaling scheme
\Phi(F)
induced from prior
F
. Since
u_{0}\in(Q_{r^{\dagger}-1},Q_{r^{\dagger}})
, we have:
(i) For
r<r^{\dagger}
, the entire quantile interval
(Q_{i-1},Q_{i}]
lies in
(0,u_{0}]
, so
x=0
almost surely and
m_{r}=0
.
(ii) For
r>r^{\dagger}
, the entire quantile interval lies in
(u_{0},1]
, so
x=1
almost surely and
m_{r}=1
.
(iii) In quantile interval indexed by
r^{\dagger}
, the subinterval
(u_{0},Q_{r^{\dagger}}]
has length
(Q_{r^{\dagger}}-Q_{r^{\dagger}-1})s_{r^{\dagger}}
, which is a fraction
s_{r^{\dagger}}
of the total bin mass. Hence,
m_{r^{\dagger}}=s_{r^{\dagger}}
.
Thus, the induced posterior mean distribution is

\displaystyle\Phi(F)=Q_{r^{\dagger}-1}\cdot\delta_{0}+(Q_{r^{\dagger}}-Q_{r^{\dagger}-1})\cdot\delta_{s_{r^{\dagger}}}+(1-Q_{r^{\dagger}})\cdot\delta_{1}.

Now choose
(v,D)
as in
Example

4.3
with
c=s_{r^{\dagger}}
. Then

\displaystyle h_{v,D}(x)=\max\{s_{r^{\dagger}},x\},\quad\forall\,x\in[0,1].

The seller’s revenue under
\Phi(F)
is

\displaystyle\textsc{Rev}_{v,F,D}(\Phi(F))=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\Phi(F)}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]

\displaystyle=Q_{r^{\dagger}-1}\cdot s_{r^{\dagger}}+(Q_{r^{\dagger}}-Q_{r^{\dagger}-1})\cdot s_{r^{\dagger}}+(1-Q_{r^{\dagger}})\cdot 1

\displaystyle=Q_{r^{\dagger}}s_{r^{\dagger}}+(1-Q_{r^{\dagger}}).

The Bayesian optimal revenue (under full-information signaling scheme) is

\displaystyle\textsc{Opt}_{v,F,D}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim F}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]=(1-\bar{\omega}_{r^{\dagger}})\cdot s_{r^{\dagger}}+\bar{\omega}_{r^{\dagger}}\cdot 1=s_{r^{\dagger}}+\bar{\omega}_{r^{\dagger}}(1-s_{r^{\dagger}})

\displaystyle=\textsc{Rev}_{v,F,D}(\Phi(F))+(Q_{r^{\dagger}}-Q_{r^{\dagger}-1})s_{r^{\dagger}}(1-s_{r^{\dagger}}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\textsc{Opt}_{v,F,D}}{\textsc{Rev}_{v,F,D}(\Phi(F))}=1+\frac{(Q_{r^{\dagger}}-Q_{r^{\dagger}-1})s_{r^{\dagger}}(1-s_{r^{\dagger}})}{Q_{r^{\dagger}}s_{r^{\dagger}}+(1-Q_{r^{\dagger}})}.

Substituting
s_{r^{\dagger}}=\frac{\sqrt{1-Q_{r^{\dagger}}}}{1+\sqrt{1-Q_{r^{\dagger}}}}
and simplifying, one verifies that the fraction equals

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{r^{\dagger}}-Q_{r^{\dagger}-1}}{(1+\sqrt{1-Q_{r^{\dagger}}})^{2}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{\textsc{Opt}_{v,F,D}}{\textsc{Rev}_{v,F,D}(\Phi(F))}=1+\frac{Q_{r^{\dagger}}-Q_{r^{\dagger}-1}}{(1+\sqrt{1-Q_{r^{\dagger}}})^{2}},

as claimed.

Case 2: index
r^{\dagger}=K
.

In this case, the maximizing term corresponds to the last quantile interval
[Q_{K-1},1]
, with length
Q_{K}-Q_{K-1}=1-Q_{K-1}
.
Fix any
\varepsilon\in\bigl(0,1-Q_{K-1}\bigr)
. Consider a three-point quality distribution
F_{\varepsilon}
supported on
\{0,t,1\}
for some
t\in(0,1)
(to be chosen later), with

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{F_{\varepsilon}}\!\left[{0}\right]=1-\varepsilon,\quad\mathbb{P}_{F_{\varepsilon}}\!\left[{t}\right]=\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\quad\mathbb{P}_{F_{\varepsilon}}\!\left[{1}\right]=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Since
1-\varepsilon\geq Q_{K-1}
, the first
K-1
quantile intervals are fully contained in the atom at
0
, so their posterior means are
0
. The last quantile interval, of mass
1-Q_{K-1}
, contains:
(i) mass
(1-\varepsilon)-Q_{K-1}=(1-Q_{K-1})-\varepsilon
at
0
,
(ii) mass
\varepsilon/2
at
t
,
(iii) mass
\varepsilon/2
at
1
.
Thus, the posterior mean in the last quantile interval is

\displaystyle m_{K}=\frac{0\cdot((1-Q_{K-1})-\varepsilon)+t\cdot(\varepsilon/2)+1\cdot(\varepsilon/2)}{1-Q_{K-1}}=\frac{\varepsilon(1+t)}{2(1-Q_{K-1})}.

Choose
t\in(0,1)
such that
m_{K}<t
. Now apply
Example

4.3
with
c=m_{K}
, yielding
h(x)=\max\{m_{K},x\}
for all
x\in[0,1]
. By construction,
the revenue under
\Phi(F_{\varepsilon})
is

\displaystyle\textsc{Rev}_{v,F_{\varepsilon},D}(\Phi(F_{\varepsilon}))=Q_{K}\cdot m_{K}+(1-Q_{K-1})\cdot m_{K}=m_{K}.

The Bayesian optimal revenue (under full-information signaling scheme) is

\displaystyle\textsc{Opt}_{v,F_{\varepsilon},D}

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)\cdot m_{K}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdot t+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdot 1=m_{K}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(1+t-2m_{K})

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}m_{K}+m_{K}(1-Q_{K-1})-\varepsilon m_{K}=m_{K}(1+(1-Q_{K-1})-\varepsilon)

where equality (a) holds since
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(1+t)=m_{K}(1-Q_{K-1})
(implied by the definition of
m_{K}
).
Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\textsc{Opt}_{v,F_{\varepsilon},D}}{\textsc{Rev}_{v,F_{\varepsilon},D}(\Phi(F))}=1+(1-Q_{K-1})-\varepsilon=1+(Q_{K}-Q_{K-1})-\varepsilon,

as required.
Combining the two cases finishes the proof of
Lemma

4.3
.
∎

We conclude this section by proving
Theorems

3.1
and
3.4
.

Proof of
Theorems

and

.

By

, the indirect utility function

induced by any linear valuation function

and consumer type distribution

belongs to the function class

defined in

. Consequently, the robust quality disclosure problem

is a restriction of the robust disclosure design problem

, and thus

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{V}\\

for every

-Quantile Partition

\Phi
.

Applying

, we obtain an upper bound on the robust competitive ratio of any such policy, and in particular, the optimal robust competitive ratio of

is at most
\Gamma_{K}^{*}
, achieved by the quantile threshold profile

defined in Eqn.(

).

To show this bound is tight, we invoke

, which constructs, for the policy

, explicit primitives

4.3
Solving
\textsc{RDD}[\mathcal{X}_{K},\mathcal{H}]
: Key Steps and Technical Lemmas

We now outline the main steps in solving the robust disclosure design problem
\textsc{Rdd}[\mathcal{X}_{K},\mathcal{H}]
defined in
Theorem

4.2
. The analysis leverages convex-analytic tools and extremal constructions to derive a closed-form expression for the robust competitive ratio of any
K
-Quantile Partition
.

Step 1: Normalization via
h(1)=1
(
Lemma

4.4
).

The objective in
\textsc{Rdd}[\mathcal{X}_{K},\mathcal{H}]
is invariant under positive scaling of the indirect utility function
h
. Hence, without loss of generality, we may restrict attention to the normalized class
\mathcal{H}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathcal{H}_{1}\triangleq\{h\in\mathcal{H}:h(1)=1\}
.

Lemma 4.4

(Scale normalization)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the space of all non-negative, non-decreasing, convex functions
h:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfying
h(x)\geq x\cdot h(1)
for all
x\in[0,1]
, and define the normalized subclass

\mathcal{H}_{1}\triangleq\{h\in\mathcal{H}:h(1)=1\}
.
Then, for any distributions
F,G\in\Delta([0,1])
,

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim F}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim G}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]}=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{1}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim F}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim G}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]}.

Step 2: Reduction to extremal functions (
Lemmas

4.5
and
4.6
).

The set
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is convex and compact under the topology of pointwise convergence. Its extreme points admit a simple and explicit characterization (see
Figure

2(b)
for an illustration).

Lemma 4.5

(Extremal representation)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
denote the functional space defined in
Lemma

4.4
.
For every function
h\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
, there exists a unique probability measure
\nu_{h}\in\Delta([0,1])
such that

\displaystyle h(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\max\{c,x\}\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu_{h}(c),\quad\forall\,x\in[0,1].

Consequently, the set of extreme points of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is given by
\textsc{ext}(\mathcal{H}_{1})=\left\{h_{c}(\cdot)\triangleq\max\{c,\cdot\}:c\in[0,1]\right\}
.

Although the objective in program
\textsc{Rdd}[\mathcal{X}_{K},\mathcal{H}]
is not linear in
h
, the supremum over
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is attained at an extreme point, as shown below.

Lemma 4.6

(Reduction to extremal functions)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
be as defined in
Lemma

4.4
. For any distributions
F,G\in\Delta([0,1])
,

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{1}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim F}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim G}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]}=\sup_{c\in[0,1]}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim F}\!\left[{\max\{c,x\}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim G}\!\left[{\max\{c,x\}}\right]}.

Step 3: Closed-form expression for
\Gamma(\Phi\mid\mathcal{H})
(Part (ii) of
Theorem

4.2
).

For any fixed
c\in[0,1]
, consider the extremal function
h_{c}(x)\triangleq\max\{c,x\}
. Its piecewise-linear structure ensures that, for any quantile threshold profile
\mathbf{Q}=(Q_{0},Q_{1},\dots,Q_{K})
, only the unique quantile interval
[Q_{r-1},Q_{r}]
containing
F(c)
contributes a Jensen gap (i.e., a loss in the objective of program
\textsc{Rdd}[\mathcal{X}_{K},\mathcal{H}]
). All other intervals yield identical contributions to the numerator and denominator of the ratio in the objective. Consequently, the worst-case competitive ratio reduces to a one-dimensional optimization over the position of
c
within its interval, which admits the closed-form solution stated in part (ii) of
Theorem

4.2
.

Step 4: Optimizing over quantile threshold profiles (Part (i) of
Theorem

4.2
).

Minimizing the expression in part (ii) of
Theorem

4.2
over all valid quantile threshold profiles
\mathbf{Q}
yields the optimal robust disclosure policy described in part (i) of the same theorem. The optimum is attained when the competitive ratio is equalized across all quantile intervals, which leads to the backward recursion and boundary condition stated in the theorem.

4.4
Proofs in the Analysis of
\textsc{RDD}[\mathcal{X}_{K},\mathcal{H}]

In this section, we include all the proofs of the technical lemmas and then establish
Theorem

4.2
.

See
4.4

Proof of
Lemma

4.4
.

Fix an arbitrary function
h\in\mathcal{H}
. If
h(1)=0
, then since
h
is non-negative, non-decreasing, and satisfies
h(x)\geq x\cdot h(1)=0
, it follows that
h(x)=0
for all
x\in[0,1]
. Such a function contributes zero to both numerator and denominator, and thus does not affect the supremum.
Now suppose
h(1)>0
, and define the normalized function

\displaystyle h^{\dagger}(x)\triangleq\frac{h(x)}{h(1)},\quad x\in[0,1].

Since
h
is non-negative, non-decreasing, and convex, so is
h^{\dagger}
. Moreover, for all
x\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle h^{\dagger}(x)=\frac{h(x)}{h(1)}\geq\frac{x\cdot h(1)}{h(1)}=x=x\cdot h^{\dagger}(1),

where we used the defining property
h(x)\geq x\cdot h(1)
. Hence
h^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{H}
and
h^{\dagger}(1)=1
, so
h^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
.
Furthermore, the scaling cancels in the ratio:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim F}\!\left[{h^{\dagger}(x)}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim G}\!\left[{h^{\dagger}(x)}\right]}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim F}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]/h(1)}{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim G}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]/h(1)}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim F}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim G}\!\left[{h(x)}\right]}.

Since every
h\in\mathcal{H}
with
h(1)>0
corresponds to a
h^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
yielding the same objective value, and functions with
h(1)=0
are irrelevant to the supremum, the two suprema coincide. This completes the proof.
∎

We provide the following auxiliary lemma, which characterizes several structural properties of functions in the normalized indirect utility space
\mathcal{H}_{1}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
denote the functional space defined in
Lemma

4.4
. For any function
h\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
:

(i)

x\leq h(x)\leq 1
for all
x\in[0,1]
;

(ii)

the right derivative
h^{\prime}_{+}(x)
exists for all
x\in[0,1)
, is non-decreasing, and satisfies
0\leq h^{\prime}_{+}(x)\leq 1
;

(iii)

h
is absolutely continuous, and for all
x\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle h(x)-h(0)=\int_{0}^{x}h^{\prime}_{+}(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t.

Proof of
Lemma

4.7
.

We prove the three claims in order.

Part (i).
Since
h\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
, it satisfies
h(x)\geq x\cdot h(1)=x
for all
x\in[0,1]
. Moreover, because

Appendix A
Relaxing the Quality Linearity in Valuation Function

In this section, we discuss extensions of some of our results under a relaxation of the quality-linearity assumption in the valuation function (
1
).

Assumption 2

(Constant marginal rate of substitution (CMRS))
.

The valuation function
v(\theta,\omega)
is increasing in quality
\omega\in[0,1]
for every fixed type
\theta\in[0,1]
, and non-decreasing in type
\theta
for every fixed quality
\omega\in[0,1]
, and it satisfies that for any
\theta\in[0,1]
, and any
\omega^{\dagger},\omega^{\ddagger}\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\frac{\left.\frac{\partial v(\theta,\omega)}{\partial\theta}\right|_{\theta,\omega^{\dagger}}}{\left.\frac{\partial v(\theta,\omega)}{\partial\theta}\right|_{\theta,\omega^{\ddagger}}}=\frac{\tau\left(\omega^{\dagger}\right)}{\tau\left(\omega^{\ddagger}\right)}~,

where
\tau(\cdot):[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is some function that only depends on the quality.

Intuitively, this condition says that the relative effect of a higher type on valuation at two different qualities is pinned down solely by those qualities, and does not depend on the level of
\theta
. Equivalently, type acts as a “common shifter” of marginal willingness-to-pay across qualities, with the quality-dependent scaling captured
u(\cdot)
.
Many valuation functions including
v(\theta,\omega)=\theta+\omega
,
v(\theta,\omega)=\theta\cdot\omega

and
v(\theta,\omega)=\theta\cdot\omega+\omega

We denote by
\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{cmrs}}
the set of valuation functions that satisfy
2
.

2
allows the valuation function in
\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{cmrs}}
to depend on the quality in a non-linear way.
However, we note that
2
is not a strict relaxation of the
1
.
Consider the following valuation function

.
Clearly this function satisfies
1
but does not satisfy the above
2
.

Proposition A.1
.

Fix any valuation function
v\in\mathcal{V}_{\textsc{cmrs}}
, consumer type distribution
D\in\Delta([0,1])
, and product quality distribution

achieves the optimal revenue, i.e.,

\displaystyle\textsc{Rev}(\phi^{\textsc{{FI}}})=\textsc{Opt}~.

\displaystyle\textsc{Rev}(\phi^{\textsc{{NI}}})\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\textsc{Opt}~.

\mathcal{X}_{K}^{\dagger}
.

In contrast to
Theorem

3.1
for
K
-Quantile Partition
,
Theorem

C.1
reveals that the robust quality disclosure problem under
K
-Quality Partition
is degenerate: all such policies are equally effective (or ineffective), yielding a robust competitive ratio of exactly
2
regardless of the number of partitions
K
.

By comparison, the optimal robust competitive ratio under
K
-Quantile Partition
improves with
K
, converging to
1
at a rate of
\Theta(1/K)
(
Proposition

3.2
). This stark contrast highlights that the
K
-Quantile Partition
policies studied in the main text are not only theoretically richer but also practically more efficient for robust quality disclosure.

Proof of
Theorem

C.1
.

Invoking
Corollary

3.6
, since every monotone partition signaling scheme is 2-approximation under any consumer valuation function, type distribution and product quality distribution, the robust competitive ratio of any
K
-Quality Partition

\Phi
is at most 2. It remains to show a matching lower bound.

Let us fix any
K\in\mathbb{N}
, and an pair of
(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\xi})
, and consider a
K
-Quality Partition

\Phi
that is parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
Fix any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and define the index

\displaystyle j\triangleq\min\{r\in[K]:\omega_{r}>0\}

Such
j
exists because
\omega_{K}=1>0
, so the open interval
(0,\omega_{j})
is nonempty.

We consider a quality distribution
F_{\varepsilon}
supported on
\{\varepsilon^{2}\cdot\frac{\omega_{j}}{2},\frac{\omega_{j}}{2}\}
with the mass

\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon},\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}
, respectively.
By definition, the mean of this quality distribution is given by

\displaystyle\bar{\omega}=\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\varepsilon^{2}\cdot\frac{\omega_{j}}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{\omega_{j}}{2}=\varepsilon\cdot\frac{\omega_{j}}{2}~.

Note that by construction, we have
\mathsf{supp}(F_{\varepsilon})\subset(0,\omega_{j})
.
Thus, the
K
-Quality Partition

\Phi
parameterized with
\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\xi}
with the input
F_{\varepsilon}
essentially outputs a no-information signaling scheme.
Namely, we have
\Phi(F_{\varepsilon})=\delta_{(\bar{\omega})}
.
Now consider a valuation distribution
v
and the type distribution
D_{\varepsilon}
defined in
Example

4.3
with
c\leftarrow\bar{\omega}
.
Then the induced optimal posted-price revenue function
h_{v,D_{\varepsilon}}(x)=\max\{x,\bar{\omega}\}
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\textsc{Opt}_{v,F_{\varepsilon},D_{\varepsilon}}}{\textsc{Rev}_{v,F_{\varepsilon},D_{\varepsilon}}(\Phi(F_{\varepsilon}))}=\frac{\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\frac{\omega_{j}}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{\omega_{j}}{2}}{\varepsilon\cdot\frac{\omega_{j}}{2}}=\frac{2}{1+\varepsilon}~.

We thus finish the proof.
∎

Appendix D
Missing Proofs

Lemma D.1

(Monotonicity of
\Lambda_{K}(\Gamma)
)
.

Fix any
K\in\mathbb{N}
. Function
\Lambda_{K}(\Gamma)
defined in
Theorem

3.1
is continuous and strictly increasing in
\Gamma\in(1,\infty)
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{K}(1)=0~,\quad\text{and}\quad\lim\limits_{\Gamma\to\infty}\Lambda_{K}(\Gamma)=\infty~.

Proof.

Define auxiliary function
\varphi(z)\triangleq(1+\sqrt{z})^{2}
.
For each fixed
z\in[0,1]
, the mapping
\Gamma\mapsto\lambda_{\Gamma}(z)=z+(\Gamma-1)\varphi(z)
is continuous and strictly increasing on
(1,\infty)
because
\varphi(z)\geq 1>0
.
Thus, for each fixed
\Gamma
, the
K
-fold composition
\lambda_{\Gamma}^{\circ K}(0)
is also continuous in
\Gamma
, and the strict monotonicity is preserved under composition:
if
\Gamma_{2}>\Gamma_{1}>1
, then
\lambda_{\Gamma_{2}}(z)>\lambda_{\Gamma_{1}}(z)
for all
z
, hence
\lambda_{\Gamma_{2}}^{\circ K}(0)>\lambda_{\Gamma_{1}}^{\circ K}(0)
, i.e., function
\Gamma\mapsto\Lambda_{K}(\Gamma)
is strictly increasing.
At
\Gamma=1
, we have that
\lambda_{1}(z)=z
, so we have
\Lambda_{K}(1)=0
.
For
\Gamma>1
and
z\geq 0
, we have that
\varphi(z)\geq 1
, hence

\displaystyle\lambda_{\Gamma}(z)=z+(\Gamma-1)\varphi(z)\geq z+(\Gamma-1)~.

Iterating from
0
yields
\Lambda_{K}(\Gamma)=\lambda_{\Gamma}^{\circ K}(0)\geq K\cdot(\Gamma-1)
, which approaches to
\infty
as
\Gamma\to\infty
.
This completes the proof as desired.
∎

Lemma D.2

(Feasibility equivalence)
.

Fix any
\Gamma>1
. There exists a
K
-Quantile Partition

\Phi
parameterized by
\mathbf{Q}
such that
\Gamma(\Phi\mid\mathcal{H})\leq\Gamma
if and only if
\Lambda_{K}(\Gamma)\geq 1
.

Proof.

Fix any
\Gamma>1
.
Suppose there exists
K
-Quantile Partition

\Phi
parameterized by
\mathbf{Q}
such that
\Gamma(\Phi\mid\mathcal{H})\leq\Gamma
.
Define auxiliary function
\varphi(z)\triangleq(1+\sqrt{z})^{2}
.
From Part (ii) of
Theorem

4.2
, we know that
\Gamma(\Phi\mid\mathcal{H})\leq\Gamma
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\Gamma(\Phi\mid\mathcal{H})=\max\nolimits_{r\in[K]}\left(1+\frac{Q_{r}-Q_{r-1}}{(1+\sqrt{1-Q_{r}})^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Gamma

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle 1+\frac{Q_{r}-Q_{r-1}}{\varphi(1-Q_{r})}

\displaystyle\leq\Gamma\quad\text{for all }r\in[K]~,

(7)

The
\Rightarrow
direction.

We first prove the
\Rightarrow
direction.
From Eqn. (
7
), we know

\displaystyle 1-Q_{r-1}\leq 1-Q_{r}+(\Gamma-1)\varphi(1-Q_{r})=\lambda_{\Gamma}(1-Q_{r}),\quad r\in[K]~.

Note that function
\lambda_{\Gamma}(z)=z+(\Gamma-1)\cdot(1+\sqrt{z})^{2}=z+(\Gamma-1)\cdot\varphi(z)
is increasing,
iterating yields

\displaystyle 1-Q_{0}\leq\lambda_{\Gamma}(1-Q_{1})\leq\lambda_{\Gamma}(\lambda_{\Gamma}(1-Q_{2}))\leq\lambda_{\Gamma}(\lambda_{\Gamma}(\lambda_{\Gamma}(1-Q_{3})))\leq\cdots\leq\Lambda_{K}(\Gamma)~.

and thus
\Lambda_{K}(\Gamma)\geq 1
as desired.

The
\Leftarrow
direction.

Suppose
\Lambda_{K}(\Gamma)\geq 1
.
Define a sequence
(s_{r})_{r\in[0:K]}
by the tight recursion

s_{K}\triangleq 0~,\quad s_{r-1}\triangleq\lambda_{\Gamma}(s_{r})=s_{r}+(\Gamma-1)\varphi(s_{r})~,\quad r=K,K-1,\dots,1~.

(8)

Then by definition, we have
s_{0}=\Lambda_{K}(\Gamma)\geq 1
.
Using the sequence
(s_{r})_{r\in[0:K]}
, we next construct a valid quantile threshold profile
\mathbf{Q}=(Q_{0},Q_{1},\dots,Q_{K})
.
Let

Q_{r}\triangleq 1-\frac{s_{r}}{s_{0}},\qquad r\in[0:K]~.

(9)

Then
Q_{0}=0
and
Q_{K}=1-s_{K}/s_{0}=1
.
Also we have
s_{r-1}\geq s_{r}
because
\Gamma>1
implies
\lambda_{\Gamma}(z)>z
,
hence we have
Q_{r-1}\leq Q_{r}
.
Therefore,
\mathbf{Q}
is a valid quantile threshold profile.

Consider
K
-Quantile Partition
parameterized by quantile threshold profile
\mathbf{Q}
constructed above.
Now it remains to verify
\Gamma(\Phi\mid\mathcal{H})\leq\Gamma
. By construction, for each
r\in[K]
,

\displaystyle s_{r-1}-s_{r}=(\Gamma-1)\varphi(s_{r})

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle Q_{r}-Q_{r-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{s_{r-1}-s_{r}}{s_{0}}=\frac{(\Gamma-1)\varphi(s_{r})}{s_{0}}\leq(\Gamma-1)\varphi\left(\frac{s_{r}}{s_{0}}\right)=(\Gamma-1)\varphi(1-Q_{r})~,

where we have used the observation that the function
\varphi(z)=(1+\sqrt{z})^{2}
satisfies
for every
\alpha\in[0,1]
and every
z\in[0,1]
, we have
\varphi(\alpha\cdot z)\ \geq\ \alpha\cdot\varphi(z)
.

Thus all the inequalities in Eqn. (
7
) holds, and thus we have
\Gamma(\Phi\mid\mathcal{H})\leq\Gamma
. This completes the proof of
Lemma

D.2
as desired.
∎
