Development of patient-reported experience and patient-reported outcome measurement tool for abortion care in Ethiopia: a mixed-method study
Authors: Negash Wakgari, Stuart J. Watson, Gizachew A. Tessema, Delayehu Bekele, Zoe Bradfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMeasuring patient-reported experiences and outcomes of abortion care would enhance access to person-centred and rights-based abortion care. Assessment requires a reliable measurement approach to improve healthcare outcomes and service quality. Worldwide, there are no patient-reported measures to assess abortion care experiences and outcomes from the perspectives of the women accessing this service. This study developed a Patient-Reported Experience Measure (PREM) and Patient-Reported Outcome Measure (PROM) tool for women receiving abortion care services in Ethiopia.MethodsA sequential, exploratory mixed-methods study was conducted to develop the measures through standard tool development approaches. First, item pools were identified via a systematic review exploring experiences and current measures of outcomes of abortion care in sub-Saharan countries, and a qualitative study exploring the lived experience of abortion care pathways in Ethiopia. Second, item pools for experiences and outcomes of abortion care were assessed by a panel of experts in Ethiopia during two modified Delphi rounds. Items were removed if expert consensus or the item-level content validity index (I-CVI) was not achieved (I-CVI < 0.70). Finally, cognitive interviews were conducted with women to assess the relevance of measurement, interpretation, judgment formation, information recall, and response mapping. Qualitative data were analysed using inductive thematic analysis. Quantitative data were analysed descriptively using frequencies and percentages.ResultsOverall, 72 items were included in the first round of the modified Delphi study, while 30 items remained in the final tools. Twenty-one items for the PREM and nine items for the PROM were retained. During the second Delphi round, I-CVI ranged between 0.55 and 1. During cognitive interviews, women reported that item instructions, wording, response options, and the recall period were clear and comprehensible. Women also described that the developed tool was relevant, brief, and covered important experiences and outcomes of abortion care.ConclusionsThe PREM and PROM tool was developed to assess experiences and outcomes for Ethiopians who access abortion care services. Researchers and healthcare providers can use the new measurements to monitor service quality, and policymakers can use the tool’s results to improve women’s health. Future research should undertake psychometric validation and assess potential applications worldwide.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12978-026-02393-y.

Background

Unsafe abortion is the fifth most common cause of maternal deaths worldwide, contributing to significant morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Poor outcomes are attributed to restrictive abortion policies and abortion related stigma, challenging reproductive health rights and service quality, causing women to seek unsafe abortion [3, 4]. Access to quality abortion services remains challenging worldwide due to legal, structural, and sociocultural barriers [5–7]. Limited availability of trained providers, cost constraints, stigma, and lack of awareness about service availability are barriers to accessing abortion services [8–10]. The strategy for ending preventable maternal deaths is grounded in ensuring quality health service provision, which can be met by offering woman-centred abortion services and strengthening healthcare systems to respond to the needs of women [5, 11–14].

Quality abortion care is determined not only by ensuring safe outcomes but also by ensuring a respectful and dignified experience [15, 16]. Ensuring a woman’s autonomy, informed decision making, and well-being throughout the process is essential for comprehensive, safe and high-quality reproductive healthcare [17, 18]. However, women encounter negative experiences such as long waiting times to receive care, stigma and abuse, a lack of autonomous decision-making, anxiety, and worry about future pregnancy after abortion [3, 19–25]. Measuring experiences and outcomes of abortion care from women’s perspectives enhances access to person-centred and rights-based abortion care, resulting in better healthcare outcomes and service quality [15, 16].

Patient-Reported Experience Measures (PREMs) and Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) tools are available to measure patient-reported experiences and outcomes of care. PREMs collect patient opinions about their service experiences directly from the patient and provide insights into the patient-centeredness of service delivery [26, 27]. PROMs enable patients to report on their health status and outcomes in response to the care they received [28, 29]. There are such measurements for infectious and chronic diseases, and for some services in maternity care, but they have not yet been developed to measure abortion care experiences and outcomes globally or tailored to Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) or Ethiopia [30–39].

In regions such as SSA countries, where healthcare system constraints and sociocultural norms shape experiences and outcomes of abortion care, context-specific measurement is necessary [13, 40]. The lowest levels of satisfaction with healthcare are reported in this region globally, underscoring the importance of incorporating patient perspectives into quality improvement efforts [41]. Abortion policy in Ethiopia permitted pregnancy termination, either to save a woman’s life, to maintain physical and mental health, for pregnancy due to rape or incest and for foetal impairments [26, 27]. Even in legally permissible conditions, sociocultural barriers, stigma, judgment, and inconsistencies in the interpretation of abortion policies continue to impede access to quality abortion services. These intersecting social, legal, and health system factors underscore the importance of a context-specific measurement tool for abortion services [28–30].

Evidence indicates that existing maternal healthcare-focused tools have poor measurement performance and fail to prioritise women’s needs during instrument development [33, 42, 43]. The lack of a patient co-designed and reliable maternity PREM and PROM tool, and the absence of abortion care-tailored PREM and PROM are documented [33, 42]. The absence of a validated and contextually appropriate PREM and PROM for abortion care limits the ability of healthcare systems to systematically evaluate quality of care from women’s perspectives [16, 17]. An abortion-specific PREM and PROM tool enables a more comprehensive assessment of service quality by capturing both experiences and care-outcome dimensions directly from the women. Using such a tool for abortion care aligns with a global call for person-centred reproductive health services and improves health outcomes [1, 44].

PREM and PROM tools are necessary for improving clinical care in a way that is aligned with women’s expectations of experiences and outcomes of abortion care. Reporting and benchmarking quality metrics within service, between services and at a national level support strategic and standardised approaches to improving healthcare quality and safety. Studies promote the development of these tools based on demand from women to improve the quality of care and maternal health outcomes [44–53]. This measurement is useful for policymakers, researchers, and service providers in measuring service quality from women’s perspectives, enabling necessary improvements. Given the identified gap, this study documents the development of a PREM and a PROM tool for abortion care for use in Ethiopia.

Methods

Design and setting

A sequential, exploratory mixed-methods study design [54, 55] was employed to develop a PREM and PROM tool for abortion care, guided by COSMIN (COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments) international measure development standards [56]. First, we conducted a systematic review exploring the experiences and outcomes of abortion care and its existing measurement in SSA; this was followed by a qualitative study interviewing women exploring the lived experience of the abortion care pathway in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. This first phase informed the initial item pool for the Delphi study. Next, two rounds of a modified Delphi study were conducted among experts in Addis Ababa. Then, cognitive interviews [57, 58] were undertaken to assess whether the resulting tool is understandable to respondents in Addis Ababa health facilities (Fig. 1).

Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia, is the most densely populated city in the country, with a population exceeding 5 million [60]. The city has 14 public hospitals, 98 public health centres, 40 privately owned hospitals and about 759 private clinics. Ethiopia has a three-tier healthcare system with primary, secondary, and tertiary levels of care. Primary healthcare focuses on preventive care, provides maternal health services including abortion care, and treats common illnesses. Secondary and tertiary levels provide inpatient and ambulatory services and a more extensive range of specialised medical and surgical services, including abortion services [61, 62].

Procedure

Phase 1: development of the first draft of the PREM and PROM tool/item generation

Systematic review and qualitative findings were synthesised to form item pools with potential domains to support analysis and meaning-making in the Delphi study [54, 63] (Supplementary file 1).

Phase 2: modified Delphi methodology for item development

Delphi methodology was used as it is a well-established, iterative technique for achieving consensus among experts, particularly in areas where empirical evidence is limited [64, 65]. This method is appropriate for measurement development, as it allows for structured item refinement through multiple rounds of anonymous feedback, minimising the influence of dominant individuals and enabling experts to contribute equally. Furthermore, given the sensitive and context-dependent nature of abortion services and the need to incorporate multidisciplinary experts, the Delphi methodology was suitable for this study [66–68].

Experts in the Delphi methodology were tasked with reviewing, rating, and refining the draft items generated from earlier phases of the study. Panel experts were required to judge items for relevance, clarity, cultural appropriateness, and comprehensiveness. They were also asked to add new items, remove or suggest modifications. Experts also provided quantitative ratings and qualitative feedback, which informed iterative revisions of the tool. This process ensured that the new measurements are both contextually appropriate and content valid.

Sample size and population

Methodological guides encourage consideration of logistical issues, the sensitivity and complexity of the phenomenon, the homogeneity of the experts, and data management capabilities to determine the sample size for Delphi studies [67, 69]. We included twenty experts in the study. An expert can represent the content of interest in science or practice [67, 70, 71]. In the context of this study, experts were women who had sought abortion, abortion care providers, policymakers, and managers of women’s health services [69]. Women who sought induced abortion care, including those who were coming for follow-up appointments after induced abortion care services, were eligible to participate in the Delphi study. Expertise may be derived not only from professional or academic knowledge but also from lived experience and direct engagement with the phenomenon under investigation. As this study aimed to develop measurements for experiences and outcomes of abortion care, the perspectives of women with abortion care experience as experts were considered essential to ensure the relevance, comprehensiveness, and person-centredness of the tool items. Abortion care providers with a minimum of five years of experience in abortion service provision and offering abortion care during the study period were eligible as experts. The balance between different perspectives was kept by considering institutional affiliation and including heterogeneous experts [72–74]. Accordingly, the panel included ten women with abortion experience, five abortion care providers, and five policymakers or women’s health service managers, to ensure a balance of lived experience, clinical and policy perspectives.

Recruitment and data collection process

Purposive sampling techniques were used to recruit experts. Experts were recruited from St Paul’s Hospital, Arada Maternal and Child Health Centre, and the Ethiopian Ministry of Health. The lead researcher contacted eligible experts, provided the participant with an information form in Amharic (the local language), and informed them about the study. Experts wishing to participate in the Delphi rounds provided written informed consent. Paper-based and email-based techniques were adapted to administer draft tools and collect data, as necessary [75, 76]. Participants completed either the English or Amharic versions of the draft PREM and PROM tool, based on their preference, to collect feedback from experts in both Delphi rounds.

We used two rounds of anonymous modified Delphi methodology [68]. The Delphi methodology involved iterative rounds of feedback and consensus-building among experts. Evidence indicates that anonymous Delphi study rounds have advantages over group meetings, including improved response rates, reduced bias, and social pressure that encourages candid responses [66, 67].

First round-Delphi study

Experts were invited to record observations or reflections, edit, and add items in a comment box alongside each item of the draft tool, if any. Experts were requested to review proposed items and categories within domains and to add suggestions for new items on the content of abortion care experiences and outcomes. They were also asked to discuss each item’s usability and rate each item’s relevance to the measured domains using a four-point Likert response scale, wherein the scale 1 = the item is not relevant, could exclude, 2 = the item is somewhat relevant, 3 = the item is quite relevant, and 4 = the item is very relevant, must include. Expert responses 1 or 2 were assessed as the item not being relevant, and responses 3 and 4 were assessed as the item being assessed by the expert as relevant. The experts’ consensus for inclusion, exclusion, and modification of each item was 70% agreement [77]. Although there is no consistent definition for consensus [69, 78], experts were asked to rate the items’ relevance and clarity in each Delphi round. Item consensus was then measured as the percentage of experts who endorsed the item as relevant or not relevant. Items with ≥ 70% of experts rating them as quite/very relevant (3 or 4) were retained; those with < 70% were removed. Items with low CVI but considered conceptually important were revised and carried forward into the second round, where the consensus level was fully applied. In both rounds of the Delphi methodology, experts did not see others’ responses, which reduced social desirability and hierarchy bias. The research team analysed, discussed, and summarised the first round.

Second round-Delphi study

Experts who participated in the first round were invited to the second round. The revised tool was sent to experts to review and indicate any confusion, or double-barrelled questions, and share their views on the proposed categories’ relevance and clarity. The relevance and clarity of each item were assessed on a four-point scale, ranging from 1 (not relevant) to 4 (very relevant).

The Content Validity Index (CVI) is a mechanism to assess the degree to which the items of a tool are related to the purpose of the tool and represent the content domain it is intended to measure [79]. CVI was computed, and the third draft of the tool was developed. Both the Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) and the Scale Content Validity Index (S-CVI) were computed. The I-CVI indicates how well experts consider individual items on a scale or assessment as relevant. It is calculated by dividing the number of experts who rate an item as relevant or very relevant by the total number of experts. The I-CVI value ranges from 0 to 1. If the I-CVI is greater than 0.79, the item is considered relevant. If it falls between 0.70 and 0.79, the item requires revisions, while items scoring less than 0.7 are omitted [80]. The S-CVI indicates the average content validity score across all items on a scale and is calculated using the Universal Agreement (S-CVI/UA) and Average methods (S-CVI/Ave). The S-CVI/UA is calculated as the number of items on a scale that all experts rated as relevant or very relevant divided by the total number of items. The average CVI (S-CVI/Ave) is calculated as the total I-CVI score of all the items divided by the total number of items [81, 82].

Phase 3: cognitive interview

To ensure the developed measurement tool was consistent and understood by the target population, cognitive interviews were conducted. Cognitive interviewing is a technique to assess whether items are understandable to the target population. It is an effective method for enhancing the validity of the measurements, as demonstrated through refining items and strengthening the tool’s reliability [83]. Additionally, by prioritising patients’ needs while developing the PREM and PROM tool, healthcare systems can better assess and improve the quality of care provided, ultimately leading to more woman-centred and effective healthcare services [42, 84].

Study population and recruitment process

A purposive sampling method was used to select three hospitals, one Sexual and Reproductive Health clinic, and one Maternal Child Health centre. Then, purposive sampling techniques were used through maximum variant sampling methods to include twenty women who sought care for induced abortions in the selected health facilities during the study period [85]. The researchers individually contacted women who received induced abortion services, providing a participant information form, and raising awareness of the study. Women who agreed to be interviewed provided written informed consent, and interviews were conducted in a private and comfortable room.

Data collection and cognitive interview techniques

The final draft of the PREM and PROM tool, supported by standardised concurrent think-aloud techniques, supported the conduct of interviews [57, 58]. We assessed whether women could understand and answer each question by examining the question-and-answer process. The cognitive interview focused on four cognitive tasks required to answer a tool: interpretation, recall of information, judgment of formation, and response mapping. Cognitive interview probing questions on item instructions, recall period, item stem, response options, content coverage, format, and length to complete the questionnaire were used to guide the interview [86].

Two MSc-qualified midwives (one female and one male), trained and experienced in data collection, conducted the cognitive interviews under the supervision of the lead researcher. Interviews were conducted in Amharic (the local language). The lead researcher provided a one-day orientation on the aim of the study, data collection tools, and procedures to the Midwife data collectors. A digital voice recorder was used to record the respondents’ voices with consent.

Methodological quality assessments

We used nine evaluation criteria recommended by Nasa et al. [67] to conduct and ensure the rigour of the methodological quality of Delphi studies in healthcare services [67]. Guideline on Conducting and REporting DElphi Studies (CREDES) was adopted to conduct and report Delphi study findings [87, 88] and provided as a supplementary file (Supplementary file 2). Similarly, the Cognitive Interviewing Reporting Framework (CIRF) was used to conduct and report cognitive interview findings [89]. The CIRF checklist is provided as a supplementary file (Supplementary file 3).

Data analysis

Quantitative data were managed in STATA software version 18. Descriptive analysis results were presented using frequencies and percentages in texts and tables. The qualitative data were analysed using inductive thematic analysis [90]. Interview recordings were transcribed and analysed using a thematic approach focusing on comprehension issues, problematic wording, and response process.

Results

The first phase of the study generated 72 items for use in the Delphi round one. Five domains were created for the PREM comprising 53 items for the first round Delphi study: Domain I: Autonomy, dignity, and respect; Domain II: Communication and counselling; Domain III: Access and supportive care; Domain IV: Waiting time to obtain abortion services; and Domain V: Privacy and confidentiality. For the PROM, a single dimension comprising nineteen items was created for the first round of the Delphi study. See supplementary file 1 for details of domains and items created at this stage.

Modified Delphi study

Twenty experts participated in two rounds of the Delphi study. The response rate was 100% in each round. An iterative summary of changes to the PREM and PROM tool during the Delphi study rounds is provided as a supplementary file (Supplementary file 4).

Delphi study first round

Items that were deemed redundant by experts due to content similarity were removed. Most (80%) of the experts reached consensus and agreed to keep four domains at the conclusion of this round. PREM domains were reduced from five to four, with waiting time to obtain abortion care and access and supportive care domains combined. A minimum of 70% and a maximum of 90% consensus was reached, which guided the removal and retention of items in this round. The number of items was reduced from 72 to 40 in this first round. Thirty-two items were identified as potentially redundant due to content similarity or did not measure the construct and were rejected. However, consensus was not attained for some items; for example, “Did you feel greeted respectfully and with kindness?”, “Did health professionals give you adequate information on the results of the procedures?”, and ”Did health professionals discuss follow-up care with you (scheduled follow-up visits)?” These items were kept for the second round, with minor edits suggested by experts considered. Experts added six new items, and 14 items were edited, rewritten, or re-ordered. Overall, 40 items from the PREM and PROM tools, across four domains, were developed in this round.

Patient-reported experience measure

Domain I: autonomy, dignity, and respect

Fifteen items were included in this domain for the first round of the Delphi study. Nine items, deemed similar in content to other items, were rejected from the domain. Hence, six items remained for the second round of the Delphi study.

Domain II: communication and counselling

Ten items were initially included in this domain for the Delphi study’s first round. Experts added one item and removed six items from this domain. Finally, four items moved across to this domain from domain III, resulting in nine items for the second round of the Delphi study.

Domain III: access and supportive care

Eighteen items were included in this domain for the first round of the Delphi study. The experts identified the four items listed here as domain II questions and suggested moving these items. Accordingly, four items moved to domain II as experts felt they addressed communication and counselling, while eight items were removed, as suggested by experts. The health experts included one new item. One item moved from Domain IV. Therefore, eight items remained for the second round Delphi study.

Domain IV: waiting time to obtain abortion services

Six items were included in this domain for the first round of the Delphi study. Experts removed five items and suggested merging this domain with domain III. Thus, this domain was merged with the access and supportive care domain. Experts felt that waiting time was an important component of perceived access and supportive care, rather than a standalone domain.

Domain V: Privacy and confidentiality

Four items were included in this domain for the first round of the Delphi study. One item was removed, and one item was added by the experts. Hence, four items remained for the second round Delphi study.

Patient-reported outcome measure

Nineteen items were included in the PROM for the first round of the Delphi study. The experts suggested removing nine items and adding three new items to the domain. Thirteen items remained for the second round Delphi study. Overall, after the first Delphi round, the number of PREM items was reduced from 53 to 27, and the number of PROM items was reduced from 19 to 13, resulting in a total of 40 items.

Delphi study second round

Overall, this round reduced the number of items from 40 to 30. PREM domains were reduced from four to three, with domain II, communication and counselling, and domain III, access and supportive care, merged and retitled counselling and access to care. Overall, I-CVI ranged from 0.55 to 1. Ten items scored less than 0.7 on the I-CVI and were eliminated in this round. The number of PREM items reduced from 27 to 21, and the number of PROM items reduced from 13 to 9. I-CVI for the remaining 30 items was 1. The S-CVI-universal agreement approach demonstrated moderate validity (0.75), while average methods reported strong validity 0.91. The CVI computed findings are presented in full as a supplementary file (Supplementary file 4).

Patient-reported experience measure

Domain I: autonomy, dignity, and respect

Six items were included in this domain for the second round of the Delphi study. The I-CVI was 1 for all items in this domain, and all items were retained for the cognitive interview.

Domain II: counselling and access to care

Communication and counselling (Domain II) and Access and supportive care (Domain III) merged as counselling and access to care. Experts felt that communication and counselling constructs overlapped with access and supportive care in how women perceived their abortion care experience. Of the nine items included in the second round Delphi study, three scored less than 0.7 of CVI (i.e., all 0.65) from the communication and counselling domain and were rejected; these items were: “Did the Health professionals give you periodic updates on the status and progress of your abortion care?”, “Did health professionals instruct you on post-abortion complications?” and “Did health professionals discuss follow-up care with you (scheduled follow-up visits)?”

In the access and supportive care domain, of eight items, two scored below 0.7 on the I-CVI (0.65) and were rejected. These two items are: “Did you receive postabortion family planning counselling that met your needs?” and “Did health professionals insist you use family planning after abortion?” Hence, twelve items were included in this domain for the cognitive interview.

Domain III: privacy and confidentiality

Of the four items included in the second-round Delphi study, one scored 0.65 on the CVI and was rejected. This item was “Do you feel there was sufficient privacy in the clinic for women seeking abortion care?”

Patient-reported outcome measure

Four items scored less than 0.7 (ranging from 0.55 to 0.65) and were rejected. These items were: “Did you feel the procedure was successful?” “Did you feel guilty and ashamed about your abortion?” “Did you feel selfish and worried that you lost faith?”, and “Did you feel downhearted and miserable with the abortion care you received?” Items that scored less than 0.7 were criticised by experts as sensitive questions that could retraumatise clients. For example, one expert commented, “This question does not take the moral values of a woman into consideration. The question itself seems selfish?” Although these items captured important emotional dimensions of abortion experiences, experts considered them potentially retraumatising and misaligned with a supportive, non-judgemental care model. We therefore removed them at this stage, noting this as a potential trade-off between comprehensiveness and emotional safety. CVI scores ranged from 0.8 to 1. Therefore, nine items remained for the cognitive interview. Generally, following the second round and CVI computation, the final pool entering cognitive interviews comprised 21 PREM items across three domains and 9 PROM items.

Themes

Three themes were identified to organise findings: i) Item’s instructions and stem; ii) response options and recall period; iii) content coverage, format, and length. Developed PREM and PROM items, added as a supplementary file (Supplementary file 5).

Theme 1: item’s instructions and stem

The directions, how to respond to each item, and the intended meaning of the items from the patient’s perspective were addressed under this theme. Item instructions were clear and easy for participants to follow. Summarised by participant, “item’s instructions are clear and easily understandable…I think it is nice instructions” (P003). Supported by P004: “There is no ambiguity of the item’s instruction… I am comfortable with the item instruction. It is clear and directive.” The perceived meaning of the items was described as in line with the intended meaning of the tools and was found to be easily understandable to the target population. For instance, one participant stated the perceived meaning of the question [Did you feel that you were welcomed when you first came to the health facility? ] as follows… “this asks how I was welcomed and accepted by providers… asks if I [was] received in a good manner in the healthcare facility.” Similarly, the question [Did you feel you provided pain relief that met your needs? ] is explained as follows: Did the healthcare provider give you anti-pain medication to control pain related to the procedure? (P001). Overall, participants found the instructions clear and item stems understandable, suggesting strong face validity of the tool.

Theme 2: response options and recall period

The clarity and appropriateness of the response options and the recall period were explained under this theme. Participants had no difficulty recalling information or selecting response options. They agreed with the response options, reporting them as clear and appropriate to the listed items. Available response options enabled respondents to choose from a wide range, as evidenced by the interviewees. “Response options gave a wide range of response options for respondents. Therefore, it is appropriate and inclusive” (P011). However, one participant felt the response options would be reduced, saying, “I think three response options are enough…. better to categorise into satisfied, neutral, and not satisfied.” Response formats and recall periods were generally well accepted, although one woman suggested simplifying the response categories.

Theme 3: content coverage, format, and length

This theme discussed whether all key areas of abortion care experiences and outcomes were included in the tool, how the items were structured and the overall length. Descriptions indicated the tool covered important experiences and outcomes of abortion care for the target population. A participant (P010) responded as follows: “I don’t think that there are other abortion care experiences and outcomes that are not covered in this questionnaire.” This was also supported by another participant (P006), describing, “I have no [additional] question to add. There could be variation among service receivers. But for me, the questions are appropriate, and I gave proper responses.” However, some respondents reported that some of their concerns were not incorporated into the items. For instance, a participant said that: “The questionnaire did not address whether my abortion was induced or spontaneous onset…” (P008). Respondents reported the time needed to complete the PREM and PROM as reasonable and appropriate. Participants explained their experience with the time it took them to complete the tools: It is okay… does not take more than 25 min (P002). Most women felt the tool comprehensively captured key aspects of abortion care, though a minority noted that distinctions between induced and spontaneous abortions were not explicitly requested.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to develop a PREM and PROM tool for measuring abortion care services experiences and outcomes in Ethiopia. Using international measurement standards, a measurement tool was developed to assess experiences and outcomes of abortion care from patients’ perspectives. Twenty-one items for the PREM and nine for the PROM were developed through rigorous and standard tool development phases [54, 63].

I-CVI for the new measurements showed strong agreement among experts on the item’s relevance to the intended purpose [91]. The CVI of the entire scale indicates high validity (S-CVI/Ave: 0.91). This indicates that the average I-CVI score across all items and experts was 91%, indicating that a significant proportion of experts agreed on the relevance of each item. On the other hand, the CVI of the entire scale using the universal agreement approach was moderate (S-CVI/UA 0.75), implying that the experts rated 75% of the items as very relevant or relevant. Moderate validity scores might be due to the S-CVI/UA’s sensitivity to sample size; as the number of experts involved increases, the likelihood of a low S-CVI increases. This is because, in universal agreement, a score of one is given when all experts agree; otherwise, a score of zero is given [92, 93]. Overall, the CVI of the new measurement is high [81] and could be used by providers, health managers, and policymakers in auditing abortion care experiences and outcomes in the study setting. Moreover, a rigorous method used to develop the new measurement can confirm the reliability and validity of the newly developed PREM and PROM tool, as evidenced in previous studies [33, 42, 55, 81], enhancing its potential applicability in clinical settings.

To our knowledge, the newly developed PREM and PROM tools are among the first tools to monitor abortion care experiences and outcomes worldwide. As we used a systematic review finding conducted in SSA to develop the first iteration of the items for use in the Delphi study, and some commonalities of socio-economic and healthcare systems in the region [40, 41], the tool could be implemented more broadly throughout SSA with appropriate contextual adaptation and psychometric evaluation. To translate the measurement into practice, healthcare authorities and professional associations need to facilitate tool implementation by making the tool available and creating awareness.

Given the recognised utility of PREMs and PROMs to benchmark and improve clinical safety and quality [44], this tool can provide a standardised and evidence-based approach to capture experiences and outcomes of abortion care from the perspectives of women using services. It can be used by healthcare providers, researchers, and program managers to improve the quality of care, and policymakers can use the tool’s results to improve women’s health. In addition, the new measurement may serve as a baseline for researchers and stakeholders interested in the development of the PREM and PROM tools to improve the quality of abortion care in other settings. While this measurement was developed in Ethiopia, its methodological rigour suggests it may be applicable in similar settings with appropriate contextual adaptation and psychometric validation.

The PREM and PROM tool development process was rigorous [54, 55, 63]. It included a systematic review, a qualitative study, a Delphi study, and a cognitive interview aligned with international measurement standards. The measurement was developed in collaboration with experts such as abortion service providers, policymakers, maternal health managers, and women who sought abortion care. In addition, the new tool measures what it intends to measure, as assessed by face validity, and has adequate content validity. Whilst this study established content and face validity through the modified Delphi process and cognitive interviews, we recommend that future studies conduct a more comprehensive psychometric evaluation of construct validity, reliability, and responsiveness before routine implementation.

Strengths and limitations

First, tool development was undertaken in accordance with international standards, ensuring rigour, transparency, and reproducibility. A comprehensive description of the study setting and context enables readers to assess the transferability of the findings to their own setting. This measurement tool should also be validated across diverse populations and in other healthcare systems. Second, efforts were made to include a diverse panel of experts in the Delphi methodology; the panel sample size was discrete and may not fully capture all possible expert perspectives. Third, cognitive interview findings supported strong face validity and comprehensibility. It is possible that this study may not reflect the full diversity of literacy levels, languages, and cultural interpretations across Ethiopia. Furthermore, as this study focused primarily on content and face validity, other important psychometric properties, such as construct validity, reliability, and responsiveness, were not assessed and are recommended for future research.

Conclusions

This study developed contextually relevant and content-valid measurements to assess abortion care experiences and outcomes from the perspectives of women in Ethiopia. Through a rigorous multi-phased process grounded in international measurement standards, 30 items: 21 PREM items across three domains (autonomy, dignity and respect; counselling and access to care; and privacy and confidentiality) and 9 PROM items were developed and validated. The measurement is relevant to abortion providers, health service managers, policymakers, and researchers, as it offers a structured, standardised approach to capturing experiences and outcomes of abortion care from women’s perspectives. These measurements may support future efforts to monitor and improve person-centred abortion care following further psychometric evaluation and implementation testing. Future research should focus on further psychometric validation, including construct validity, reliability, and responsiveness, as well as implementation studies to assess feasibility, scalability, and impact across diverse contexts.
