hUC-MSC-exosomes deliver Let-7b/7f-5p to dermal papilla cells for a multi-target synergistic treatment of androgenetic alopecia
Authors: Qimei Chen, Xueer Wang, Yarui Zhang, Fengting Liang, Hao Zhang, Chenhui Guo, Mianbo Huang, Jingjing Yang, Xuan Wang, Yong Miao, Lin Zhang, Min Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExosomes play pivotal roles in immunomodulation, tissue regeneration, and the treatment of diseases. However, their specific mechanisms of action remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (hUC-MSC-exosomes) in androgenic alopecia (AGA). We established a modified AGA mouse model and found that hUC-MSC-Exosomes could be taken up by dermal papilla cells (DPCs), promoted the transition of hair follicles from telogen to anagen, ameliorated hair follicle miniaturization, and enhanced hair regeneration and thickening.ResultsCytological experiments revealed that exosomes inhibited aging and apoptosis of DPCs, promoted their proliferation and migration, increased alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels, stimulated the secretion of paracrine cytokines, and reduced the expression level of the androgen receptor (AR). Exosomal miRNA sequencing analysis revealed that the top 10 miRNAs were primarily from the Let-7 family. GO and Reactome functional enrichment analyses and dual-luciferase reporter experiments revealed that hUC-MSC-exosomes delivered Let-7b-5p to target the deubiquitinating enzyme USP12, thereby inhibiting AR deubiquitination and promoting AR ubiquitination and degradation. This process further activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and suppresses the TGF-β/Smad pathway.ConclusionsIn summary, this study provides insights into the underlying mechanisms of action of hUC-MSC-exosomes in AGA, indicating their therapeutic potential. In-depth analysis revealed that Let-7b-5p simultaneously targets DKK3 to activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in multiple dimensions. Moreover, hUC-MSC-exosomes delivered Let-7f-5p to target Smad2, synergizing with Let-7b to inhibit the TGF-β/Smad pathway and suppress DHT-induced DPC apoptosis. Further exploratory clinical trial showed that hUC-MSC-exosomes increased hair density and average hair diameter in patients with AGA.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04752-0.

Background

Androgenic alopecia (AGA) is the most common cause of hair loss worldwide, with male-pattern androgenetic alopecia (mpAGA) being the predominant form in men. mpAGA is a genetically predisposed condition, which is distinct from female-pattern alopecia [1]. The core pathogenesis of AGA involves an overreaction of the androgen receptor (AR) to dihydrotestosterone (DHT), where genetic factors increase androgen sensitivity in specific hair follicles, leading to progressive follicular degeneration [2]. AGA is characterized by the progressive miniaturization of hair follicles and follicular cycle disturbances, and its clinical manifestations include a receding hairline and progressive alopecia in the frontal and parietal regions.

DHT is a key mediator of AGA pathogenesis. After binding to AR in DPCs, DHT induces AR activation and nuclear translocation, thereby regulating gene expression [3]. Elevated DHT levels activate the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction in DPCs, which accelerates aging, apoptosis, and cell cycle arrest [4]. Additionally, DHT can promote the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in hair follicle cells and induce excessive production of inflammatory factors (e.g., Tumor Necrosis Factor-α, Interleukin 1β, and Interleukin 6) by macrophages, which disrupts the microenvironment of DPCs, exacerbates hair follicle microinflammation, and subsequently inhibits the proliferation and function of DPCs [5]. Thus, DHT can damage and result in the dysfunction of DPCs in AGA. Damaged and dysfunctional DPCs lead to abnormalities in pathways such as ADRB2/SHH, which results in an inability to activate dormant hair follicle stem cells [6], ultimately leading to the loss of the capacity to induce hair follicle stem cells. Furthermore, damaged and dysfunctional DPCs significantly reduce the expression of key regulatory genes (e.g., Wnt5a) and other hair-inducing genes, thereby disrupting the hair follicle cycle transition and hair regeneration process [7–9]. Moreover, a reduction in the number of DPCs and their apoptosis impairs their ability to regulate the proliferation and differentiation of hair matrix cells, resulting in fine, soft hair with slow growth. Dysfunctional and damaged DPCs also disrupt intercellular communication within hair follicles, leading to hair follicle miniaturization and atrophy [10, 11]. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of therapies targeting DPCs for AGA treatment.

Currently, finasteride and minoxidil are the FDA-approved and widely used agents for the treatment of AGA; however, they exhibit variable therapeutic effects and are often associated with side effects, such as sexual dysfunction and skin irritation. Therefore, exploring alternative interventions for the prevention and treatment of alopecia has become a popular research area. As nanoscale extracellular vesicles, exosomes possess inherent nanoscale characteristics (30–150 nm, low immunogenicity, and the ability to carry various bioactive molecules) that align closely with nanobiotechnology strategies in the field of hair regeneration. Their nanoscale dimensions facilitate targeted delivery to hair follicles, their low immunogenicity makes them suitable for long-term treatment, and their endogenous load of multi-component signaling molecules enables synergistic regulation of multiple targets. Studies have shown that DPC-derived exosomes (DPC-Exos) can upregulate the expression of downstream Wnt signaling genes and promote the proliferation of hair follicle stem cells by modulating the protein content of proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) [12]. Additionally, adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell exosomes (AMSC-Exos) locally activate DPCs to promote hair regeneration without significant adverse effects [13]. Furthermore, the injection of foreskin-derived mesenchymal stem cell exosomes (FSMSC-Exos) increased scalp hair density in patients without causing severe adverse reactions [14]. These findings indicate that exosomes, as “signal carriers,” can regulate the biological activity of DPCs through paracrine mechanisms; however, their effects are highly dependent on the source cells and microenvironment [15]. Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSC) alleviate DHT-induced disruption of the hair follicle growth cycle by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway to promote hair regeneration. However, it remains unclear which miRNAs in exosomes play the primary role, or whether the target sites of these miRNAs are single or multidimensional.Therefore, in this study, we investigated the specific therapeutic effects in AGA mice and the underlying signaling mechanisms. Subsequently, through exploratory clinical trial, we investigated their therapeutic efficacy in patients with AGA to provide a research foundation for the clinical application of exosomes.

Methods

Isolation and culture of hUC-MSCs cells and extraction of hUC-MSC-exosomes

Primary hUC-MSCs were cultured in a Grade A + B cleanroom environment using a GMP-grade, animal-free, recombinant protein-free serum-free medium (NC0106, Yocon, China) and its additive (GMP-grade, NC0106.S, Yocon, China) according to the established experimental protocols. The characterization of hUC-MSCs, including morphological analysis of cells from P1 to P10 generations, has been described in a previous study [16]. Fifth-generation (P5) cells were cultured in 10 cm culture dishes and T255 flasks. After 48 h of incubation, when the cell density reached 95%, the supernatant was collected and stored at − 80 °C for future use.

Exosomes were extracted by ultracentrifugation at the Precision Medicine Center of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University. The exosomes were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C in ultra-low temperature freezers. Exosomes were characterized according to the standards listed in Supplementary Table 1 (see Additional file 1), and samples were retained for testing. Specific extraction procedures, characterization methods, fluorescence labeling, and detection are detailed in the Supplementary Materials and Methods (see Additional file 1).

Experimental animals

Twenty-four SPF-grade 7-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Guangzhou RuiGe Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (SCXK (Yue) 2023-0059, approved on June 11, 2023). The mice underwent one week of acclimatization feeding and housing at the Animal Experiment Department of Southern Medical University (SYXK (Yue) 2021 − 0167, approved on December 9, 2021). The housing environment was maintained at a temperature of (24 ± 2) °C, relative humidity of (50 ± 10)%, and a 12 h light-dark circadian cycle. Each mouse was individually placed in a plastic cage, fed standard animal feed, and provided free access to water and feed. All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Guangzhou, China) of the Animal Welfare and Ethics Management Committee of Southern Medical University. All animal experiments were performed according to the ARRIVE (Animal Research: In Vivo Experiment Report) guidelines.

Dht-induced mouse AGA model and grouping

DHT (40 mg) was purchased from Beijing Solarbio Technology Co., Ltd., and dissolved in 0.2 mL of anhydrous ethanol (Guangzhou Chemical Reagent Factory) to achieve a concentration of 200 mg/mL. Subsequently, 4 mL of corn oil (Aladdin) was added to obtain a final concentration of 10 mg/mL. Mice with pink dorsal coloration and hair follicles in the telogen phase were selected. DHT (1 mg/day) was administered via intraperitoneal injection for four consecutive days. Anesthesia was then induced by intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA; w/v; 0.01 mL/g body weight). Dorsal hair shafts and roots were removed using honeywax depilatory cream (Mayllie, Guangzhou, China). The day marked as day 0 for treatment was followed by continued intraperitoneal DHT injections for 2 days, followed by a 1-day pause (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 1b) to establish AGA in the mice.

The 24 mice were randomly divided into four groups: (1) Normal control group (Normal), which received an intraperitoneal injection of ethanol and corn oil without AGA modeling, and PBS injected via dorsal microneedling (Compuvo Co., Ltd.); (2) AGA model group (AGA + PBS), which received an intraperitoneal injection of DHT (1 mg/day) and 200 µl PBS injected via dorsal microneedling; (3) Exosome therapy group (AGA+Exos), which received an intraperitoneal injection of DHT (1 mg/day) and 200 µl hUC-MSC-exosomes (5 × 108 Parcide) via dorsal microneedling every 3 days; and (4) minoxidil therapy group (AGA+Mino), which received an intraperitoneal injection of DHT (1 mg/day), followed by AGA modeling, and daily topical application of 200 µl minoxidil (Shanxi Zhendong Anxin Biopharmaceutical Co., Ltd.) to the back.

The mice were intraperitoneally anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium on days 0, 10, 14, 18, and 22. Skin color was recorded using a digital camera (Sony, Japan), and the skin color and hair were observed under high magnification using a dermatoscope (Dermoscan, Germany). Melanin content in the dorsal skin was measured using a skin physiometer (TPM, Germany). Data on dorsal hair coverage and skin melanin were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measures using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Tissue sampling and specimen preparation

Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA; w/v; 0.01 mL/g body mass) on days 0, 10, 14, 18, and 22. Skin from the dorsal region was harvested, and tissue blocks were divided into four portions: one was placed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Guangzhou Jibes Bio-Technology Co., Ltd.) for paraffin embedding, sectioning, H&E staining, and immunohistochemistry; one was placed in 4% paraformaldehyde and dehydrated with 30% sucrose for frozen tissue sectioning; one was used for photography with a stereomicroscope (Ningbo Shunyu Instrument Co., Ltd.); and the remaining portion was ground using a cryogenic grinder (Guangzhou Luoka Sequencing Instrument Co., Ltd.) to extract tissue proteins.

Observation of hair regeneration and hairballs

Using tweezers, newly grown hairs within a 0.5 cm diameter circular area were weighed and placed on a slide. The hair was straightened and examined under a stereomicroscope. Hair length and diameter were measured using LitoDigital software. Briefly, 0.5 cm×0.5 cm dorsal skin tissue was placed flat on the stage, hair ball formation was observed under a stereomicroscope, and the hair ball diameter was measured using LitoDigital. The hairballs were counted. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software. Additionally, multiple comparison analyses were performed for hair length/diameter, hair ball diameter, and hair follicle density. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

H&E and ALP staining and detection

Tissues were fixed with 4% polyformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with conventional hematoxylin and eosin. Five fields were randomly selected from three samples in each group to measure dermal thickness and hair follicle length.

Frozen Sect. (8 μm thick) of mouse skin tissue were selected, fixed with 4% polyformaldehyde, and stained with 5-Bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl phosphate p-toluidine salt/Nitrotetrazolium Blue chloride (BCIP/NBT) working solution (C3206, Shanghai Biyuntian Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Images were captured under a microscope, and five randomly selected fields were observed for ALP staining of the hair papilla. Additionally, mouse skin tissue was lysed with 1 mL of tissue lysis buffer without inhibitors (P0013J; Shanghai Biyuntian Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). The lysate was centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and 50 µL of the supernatant was incubated with 50 µL of nitrobenzene phosphate ester (pNPP) liquid chromogenic substrate (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 30 min. ALP activity was measured using a microplate reader at 405 nm. The above data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software with one-way ANOVA and multiple comparison analysis, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, and tissue TUNEL apoptosis staining

Paraffin-embedded skin tissue Sect. (5 μm thick) were prepared, dewaxed, and rehydrated. Endogenous peroxidase was inactivated using 3% H2O2, followed by antigen retrieval using citrate solution (Wuhan Boside Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). The sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies against AR (ab133273,1:500, Abogen (Shanghai) Trading Co., Ltd.) and USP12 (PS10708S,1:200, Abmart) at 4 °C. Subsequently, a secondary antibody (PV-6001; Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and DAB chromogenic solution (ZLI-9017; Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were used to stain the nuclei. Sections were mounted on neutral resin slides and observed under a microscope for positive staining. AR and USP12 expression in the hair papilla region was analyzed to determine the positivity rate (AR/USP12-positive cells/total cells).

Additionally, 8 μm thick frozen sections of mouse skin tissue were processed with antigen retrieval solution (P0090, Shanghai Biyuntian Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) for antigen retrieval. This was followed by blocking with 5% BSA and 10% goat serum (S9070, Beijing Solubio Technology Co., Ltd.). The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies against Ki67 (ab15580, 1:500, Abogen (Shanghai) Trading Co., Ltd.) and β-catenin (ab32572, 1:200, Abogen (Shanghai) Trading Co., Ltd.) at 4 °C in the dark overnight. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies against rabbit red fluorescent protein (RGAR002, 1:200, Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and rabbit green fluorescent protein (VJ315878, 1:200, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at room temperature. DAPI working solution was added, and the slides were incubated in the dark. Finally, an anti-fluorescence decay-sealing reagent was added, and the images were observed and photographed using a fluorescence microscope. Five randomly selected fields were analyzed to calculate the positive cell rates of Ki67 and β-catenin in the pilosebaceous units.

TUNEL staining of mouse skin tissue sections was performed according to the protocol for TUNEL Reagent (A113-01/02/03, Nanjing Novozymes Biotech Co., Ltd.). Images were observed and photographed under a forward fluorescence microscope, and five randomly selected fields were analyzed to determine the TUNEL-positive cell rate in the pilosebaceous units. Data were processed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software, with one-way ANOVA and multiple comparison analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Cultivation of Human DPCs

Immortalized DPCs were purchased from Wain Bio (Beijing, China). The collection of human cells was approved by the ethics committee, and informed consent was obtained from the donors [17]. All cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS (10091148, Gibco, USA) and 4.5 g/L glucose (11965118, Gibco, USA) at 37 °C in a constant temperature incubator with 5% CO2. After starvation, the cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated in basic DMEM for 4–6 h. Subsequent cell-level experiments are described in the Supplementary Materials and Methods (see Additional file 1).

Extraction of tissue and cellular proteins and Western blotting

Proteins were extracted from mouse skin tissue samples using a tissue grinder with tissue lysis buffer (containing phosphatase inhibitors). After grinding, the mixture was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected as the tissue supernatant for determining the BCA protein concentration. The samples were stored at − 80 °C. Total proteins from cellular tissues were extracted using cell lysis buffer (P0013, Shanghai Biotek Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and cryopreserved at − 80 °C. The extracted proteins were subjected to BCA protein concentration assay, with 30 µg of protein per sample loaded for isoelectric focusing electrophoresis to separate proteins based on molecular weight. The gel was then subjected to continuous-flow membrane transfers. After transfection, the samples were sealed with QuickBlock blocking solution on a shaker at room temperature. Subsequently, the following primary antibodies were diluted in the primary antibody dilution buffer and added to the sections: Wnt10b (TA383331S, 1:500, OriGene), β-catenin (ab32572, 1:1000, Abcam), DKK3 (10365-1-AP, 1:500, Proteintech), AR (ab273500, 1:1000, Abcam), Bax (T40051, 1:1000, Abmart), USP12 (12608-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), UBE2J1 (27890-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), NEDD4 (83112-1-RR, 1:1000, Proteintech), TGFβRI (Pa5-14959, 1:1000, Invitrogen), Smad2 (5339, 1:1000, Cell Signaling), p-Smad2 (8828, 1:1000, Cell Signaling), GAPDH (RM2002, 1:2000, Beijing RIKO), β-actin (RM2001, 1:2000, Beijing RIKO). The samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was then washed with Tris-buffered saline (TBST) containing Tween-20. Depending on the target protein, the samples were incubated with the corresponding horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h, followed by washing with TBST. The ECL luminescent solution was then added to the membrane, incubated, and automatically exposed using a Tanon 5200CE fully automated chemiluminescence instrument. The gray values of each protein band were statistically analyzed using ImageJ software, and the relative gray values were adjusted based on the minimum gray value of each band. Statistical protein expression charts for each group were generated from three independent WB experiments. One-way ANOVA and multiple comparison analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Small RNA-Seq of hUC-MSC-exosomes

Exosomes isolated by high-speed centrifugation were subjected to small RNA sequencing by Xiamen Life Interconnection Technology Co., Ltd. using the PE150 sequencing protocol. The quality of the sequencing library was evaluated using FASTQC. Fastp was used to trim N-base ends, filter q20, and remove adapters. Using the Ensembl release 102 Homo sapiens genome as a reference, the Rfam library was aligned with the bowtie short sequence alignment tool to remove ncRNAs, such as rRNA and tRNA. The miRDeep2 tool was employed for the quantitative analysis of small RNAs, with the Top10 miRNAs selected for functional enrichment analysis of predicted and experimentally validated target genes (Gene Ontology, KEGG, DO, and Reactome enrichment analyses).

Dual luciferase reporter

Based on target sites predicted by TargetScan, wild-type and mutant gene fragments of synthetic nucleic acid sequences were designed. Through total gene synthesis, the wild-type and mutant gene fragments were positioned downstream of the luciferase-encoding gene (Luc), specifically at the 3’, to construct a dual luciferase/wild-type reporter plasmid (referred to as the WT plasmid) and a dual luciferase/mutant reporter plasmid (referred to as the MUT plasmid).Mutations were introduced using known sequences to synthesize mutant vectors of the Let-7b-5p and Let-7f-5p seed sequences and the binding site regions of DKK3, USP12, and Smad2. The mutations were confirmed by sequencing.

In the luciferase reporter gene experiment, 50 ng of psiCHECK2 target plasmid DNA and 50 nM of the target siRNA were co-transfected into HEK 293T cells inoculated in 48-well plates using Lipofectamine 2000 liposomes. After 24 h of transfection, the cell lysate (Shanghai Biotek Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was collected and transferred to a white opaque 96-well plate (3362; Corning (Shanghai) Management Co., Ltd.). Sea urchin luciferase and firefly luciferase activities were measured using a Dual-Lumi II dual-luciferase reporter gene detection kit (RG089S, Shanghai Biotek Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and a multifunctional cell imaging microplate reader (BioTek, Cytation5), with relative light units (RLU) used to quantify the values. Using the luciferase activity of fireflies as an internal standard, the luciferase activity of the sea kidneys was normalized to obtain relative luciferase activity. Lower values indicate stronger interactions between the reporter plasmids and exogenous substances.

Exploratory clinical trial design and efficacy evaluation criteria

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University, and informed consent was obtained from all participants (Approval no.: NFEC-2024-323). Between June 2024 and September 2024, patients diagnosed with androgenetic alopecia were enrolled based on the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Androgenetic Alopecia (2019) and other relevant criteria [18]. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) voluntary signing of informed consent; (2) age 18–50 years; and (3) diagnosis of androgenetic alopecia by a physician. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) psychological disorders; (2) pregnancy or lactation, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, malignancy, thyroid dysfunction, infectious diseases; coagulation disorders; history of drug allergies; anticoagulant therapy; history of hair transplantation; and history of hair loss treatment within the past 6 months (e.g., minoxidil, finasteride, low-level laser therapy); and (3) any acute or chronic medical or laboratory abnormalities that may increase study participation risk. Withdrawal criteria were as follows: All reasons for participant withdrawal must be documented, including, but not limited to, the following: (1) participant withdrawal of informed consent; (2) sponsor termination of the study; (3) serious adverse events preventing continued participation; (4) major protocol violations/deviations; (5) pregnancy; (6) poor compliance; (7) loss to follow-up/death; and (8) other. Informed Consent: Prior to signing the informed consent form, the participant must have fully read and understood the relevant content and been given sufficient time to consider and ask questions. After signing and dating the form, participants received a copy of the informed consent document. Participants who did not provide informed consent and sign the informed consent form were not enrolled in the trial.

This study employed a combination of metal microneedle puncture and topical application of hUC-MSC-exosomes for treatment. A total of 22 patients with androgenetic alopecia were enrolled. Each patient received hUC-MSC-exosome treatment twice monthly at months 1, 2, and 3 (six sessions in total). Neither the patients nor the researchers evaluating the outcomes were aware of the group assignment or treatment details, except for the study designer. Prior to the treatment period, target sites were randomly selected within the patients’ hair loss areas and marked using a tattoo needle. Hair photographs of the target areas were captured using a 4 K ultra-high-definition trichoscope and dermatoscope (HXSK-III; HONSKIN Medical, China). Trichoscopic examinations and macroscopic photography were conducted at two time points: month 0 (baseline) and month 3 (three months of treatment). At study completion, paired macroscopic photographs from Month 0 and Month 3 were independently evaluated by three physicians unaware of the treatment information using a 7-point scale: -3 (significant worsening), -2 (moderate worsening), -1 (mild worsening), 0 (no change), 1 (mild improvement), 2 (moderate improvement), and 3 (significant improvement). Dermoscope findings were analyzed by the instrument’s built-in software: within an 8 mm diameter circular target area, the software automatically counted the total hair count and calculated hair density; simultaneously, it measured hair shaft diameter within 1 cm above the follicle to calculate the mean hair diameter. Statistical analysis

Results

Successful establishment of the AGA model in mice using DHT dissolved in anhydrous ethanol and corn oil

During the transition of the dorsal hair follicles of the mice from the telogen to anagen phase, the skin color gradually changed from pink to pinkish-white, grayish-white, grayish-black, and black (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 1a). We selected 8-week-old mice with pink dorsal skin as the experimental subjects. According to the literature [19], DHT is poorly soluble in corn oil. Therefore, we first dissolved DHT in anhydrous ethanol, diluted it with corn oil to the required concentration, and established an AGA mouse model following the protocol shown in Supplementary Fig. 1b (see Additional file 1).

The skin of mice in both groups turned pink after depilation (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 1c), indicating that their dorsal hair follicles were in the telogen phase. Honey paraffin depilatory cream was used to remove the hair shaft, following which the root induced the dorsal hair follicles to transition uniformly from the telogen to the anagen phase [20]. By day 10 post-depilation, the skin color of the mice in the normal group had turned grayish black, indicating that their dorsal hair follicles had entered the anagen phase. From days 14 to 22, a large amount of regenerating hair was observed, and the depilated areas on the backs of the mice were completely covered with hair. In contrast, the mice in the AGA model group exhibited a predominantly pinkish-white skin color from days 10 to 14 post-depilation, with only a few areas appearing grayish-white, suggesting that most of their dorsal skin follicles remained in telogen. By day 22 post-depilation, approximately 50% of the dorsal skin showed hair regeneration (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 1e), and the melanin content in the dorsal skin of AGA model mice was significantly lower than that of mice in the normal group (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 1d). Compared to normal group mice, AGA model mice had a lower average hair bulb diameter (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 1f and g) and lower hair follicle density per unit area (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 1h) on day 10 post-depilation. By day 22, the average hair diameter of the AGA model mice was also lower (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 1f and i). H&E staining revealed that the hair bulbs in the normal group extended deeply into the dermis, whereas in the AGA model group, hair follicles remained confined to the dermis (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 1k). Further analysis of dermal thickness and follicle length revealed significantly lower values in the AGA model group than in the control group (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 1l and m). ELISA results indicated markedly higher DHT concentrations in the skin of AGA-model mice (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 1j). Immunohistochemical and Western Blot (WB) analyses revealed significantly higher proportions of AR-positive cells in hair papillae (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 1n and o) and elevated relative AR expression levels in skin tissue (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 1p and q) in the AGA model group than in the control group.

Androgenetic alopecia is a chronic condition characterized by progressive shrinkage of hair follicles (follicular miniaturization) and disruption of the hair growth cycle. The results indicated that the use of anhydrous ethanol and corn oil to dissolve DHT, followed by intraperitoneal injection to induce an AGA model in mice, significantly increased the DHT concentration and AR expression level in skin tissues. The mice some hair follicles remain in the telogen phase, accompanied by reduced follicle density, follicle miniaturization, and a fine, soft hair phenotype. These characteristics to some extent mimic the follicle miniaturization and fine, soft hair phenotype observed in human AGA; however, as an acute model of hair growth cycle delay, it still has limitations regarding the disruption of the hair growth cycle in human AGA. This suggests that the model can serve as a reference for studying AGA in terms of DHT-induced follicular miniaturization and fine hair phenotypes.

Characterization of hUC-MSC-exosomes and DPCs uptake assay

Using isolated, cultured, and characterized hUC-MSCs [16], exosomes were separated from the supernatant of P5 hUC-MSC cultures by ultracentrifugation (Fig. 1a), followed by morphological, particle size, and marker protein analyses. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed that hUC-MSC-exosomes exhibited round, double-membrane vesicular structures (Fig. 1b). Nanoparticle tracking analysis revealed that the average particle size of the hUC-MSC-exosomes was 167.4 nm, with a concentration of 5.3E + 10 particles/mL (Fig. 1c). WB and nanoparticle flow cytometry (NFC) assays showed that hUC-MSC-exosomes were positive for the membrane protein surface markers CD9, CD63, and CD81, but negative for endoplasmic reticulum (calnexin) [21] (Fig. 1d and e). These results indicate that the hUC-MSC-exosomes extracted by ultracentrifugation possessed intact vesicular structures with an average particle diameter of less than 200 nm, meeting the quality control standards for exosomes.

Prior to initiating exosome efficacy testing, we employed upright fluorescence microscopy to assess the retention of PKH26-labeled hUC-MSC-exosomes within hair follicle tissue 24 h after subcutaneous injection via microneedles. These results demonstrated the presence of PKH26-labeled exosome particles within the papilla of mouse hair follicles (Fig. 1f). Subsequently, at the cellular level, co-culture of hUC-MSC-exosomes with DPCs for 24 h revealed PKH26-labeled exosomes within the DPCs, indicating that hUC-MSC-exosomes could be taken up by DPCs, thereby exerting their effects (Fig. 1g).

hUC-MSC-exosomes promoted the hair follicle transition from telogen to anagen and hair regeneration in AGA mice

An experimental design was established using the following groups to investigate the effect of exosomes on hair regeneration in AGA mice: normal, AGA (AGA + PBS), exosome-treated AGA (AGA+Exos), and minoxidil-treated AGA (AGA+Mino) (Fig. 2a). The results showed that by day 10 post-depilation, the dorsal skin of mice in the AGA+Exos and AGA+Mino groups turned grayish-black, similar to that of the normal group (Fig. 2b). Skin physiological instrument measurements revealed that after exosome and minoxidil treatment, the melanin content in the skin of mice significantly increased, with the AGA+Exos group showing higher melanin levels than the AGA+Mino group on day 14 (Fig. 2c). The hair coverage areas on the dorsal skin of the AGA+Exos and AGA+Mino groups were significantly larger than those of the AGA group and were similar to those of the normal group (Fig. 2d). By day 10, the number of hair follicles entering the anagen phase per unit area in the AGA + PBS group was significantly lower than that in the normal group. After exosome treatment, the number of hair follicles in the anagen phase in the AGA+Exos group showed no difference compared to the normal or minoxidil group (Fig. 2e and f). The average diameter of the largest hair follicles in the AGA + PBS group was smaller than that in the control group, indicating a miniaturized state of hair follicles. However, after exosome treatment, the hair follicle size in the AGA+Exos group was similar to that in the normal group, and the average diameter of the hair follicles was significantly higher than that in the minoxidil group (Fig. 2e and g), suggesting that exosomes significantly promoted the transition of hair follicles in AGA mice from the telogen to the anagen phase. Regenerated hair in a circular area (diameter, 0.5 cm) was harvested from each mouse. The hair weight of AGA mice was significantly lower than that of the normal group, whereas the hair weight of mice treated with exosomes significantly increased, showing no significant difference compared with the normal or minoxidil groups (Fig. 2k). The hair length and average diameter of AGA mice treated with exosomes were longer and thicker, respectively, than those of the AGA + PBS group, with no significant differences compared to normal mice or the minoxidil-treated group (Fig. 2h–j). These results suggest that hUC-MSC-exosomes can significantly promote hair regeneration in AGA mice and markedly improve hair quality in AGA mice.

hUC-MSC-exosomes improved hair follicle miniaturization in AGA mice and regulated the hair follicle induction capacity, proliferation, and apoptosis of DPCs

We observed morphological structural changes to investigate the specific effects of exosomes on hair follicles in AGA mice. On day 10, hair follicles in the control group entered the anagen phase (anagen III-VI) [22], with hair bulbs reaching the subcutaneous tissue. In the AGA group, hair follicles were in the telogen and early anagen phases (anagen I-VI) and were located within the dermis. After exosome treatment, the overall hair follicle structure resembled that of the normal and minoxidil groups, with hair follicles in the anagen phase (anagen III-IV) reaching the subcutaneous tissue. By day 18, hair follicles in the normal group reached the mature anagen phase, with some beginning to enter the telogen phase. In the AGA group, hair follicles were smaller, and some were in the telogen phase. Hair follicles in the minoxidil group were similar to those in the normal group; however, no significant telogen phase follicles were observed in the exosome group (Fig. 3a and b). By day 22, although some hair follicles in the AGA group reached the subcutaneous tissue, the hair bulbs were smaller. In the normal and minoxidil groups, most follicles entered the telogen phase, whereas in the exosome group, most hair follicles entered the early telogen phase (Fig. 3a and c). Statistical analysis of hair follicle length, hair bulb diameter, and dermal thickness on day 10 revealed that hair follicle length and hair bulb diameter were shorter in the AGA group (Fig. 3d and e). The dermis of the mouse skin was significantly thinner. After exosome treatment, the hair bulb diameter increased and was higher than that in the normal group, similar to that in the minoxidil group. The dermal thickness was similar to that of the normal group but higher than that of the minoxidil group (Fig. 3h). Further observation of the number and structure of hair follicles through cross-sectional sections revealed a significant decrease in hair follicle density in the AGA group on day 10, whereas hair follicle density increased markedly after exosome treatment (Fig. 3f and g). The inner root sheath (IRS) is a critical component of hair follicles that influences their function and quality. The hair follicle/IRS (HF/IRS) indirectly reflects the health status of hair follicles or the regenerative capacity of hair [23]. In the AGA group, the HF/IRS ratio decreased, whereas it increased after exosome treatment, as evidenced by the straight stem of the hair shaft exceeding the thickness of the inner root sheath in the cross-section (Fig. 3f and k). These results suggest that hUC-MSC-exosomes improved the miniaturization of hair follicles in AGA mice and promoted hair follicle growth.

hUC-MSC-exosomes deliver let-7b-5p targeting USP12 to inhibit AR deubiquitination, thereby promoting AR ubiquitination degradation, which activates Wnt signaling while suppressing TGFβR1 signaling

In AGA, the primary mechanism of action of DHT on DPCs involves the binding of DHT to AR, which induces dysfunction and damage, including aging, apoptosis, cycle arrest, oxidative stress, and abnormalities in the key signaling pathways. These mechanisms collectively lead to hair follicle miniaturization, growth cycle disruption, and hair loss [3, 4, 28]. Therefore, we investigated whether hUC-MSC-exosomes regulate AR expression. In the AGA model group, AR expression in the hair papilla was significantly higher than that in the normal group (Fig. 6a and b). At the cellular level, WB analysis also revealed that AR expression in DPCs treated with DHT was significantly upregulated, whereas hUC-MSC-exosome treatment significantly downregulated AR expression in hair papillae (Fig. 6c and d). Subsequently, we investigated the regulatory effects of Let-7a, Let-7b, Let-7c, Let-7f, and Let-7i on DHT-induced AR expression and found that Let-7b and Let-7f significantly reduced AR expression (Fig. 6e and f). However, whether the Let-7b and Let-7f regulate AR expression in DPCs remains unknown. The top 10 miRNA-predicted target genes (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Table 2) showed no predicted target gene relationship between AR and Let-7b or Let-7f, and TargetScan indicated no target sites for AR with Let-7b or Let-7f, suggesting that they may not directly regulate AR mRNA degradation and translation inhibition.

GO functional enrichment analysis of the top 10 experimentally validated miRNA target genes revealed enrichment of ubiquitin-dependent proteasome-mediated protein catabolism (Fig. 6g). Ubiquitination and deubiquitination play key positive and negative regulatory roles, respectively, in stabilizing AR levels. Deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) are negative regulators of the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), which specifically removes ubiquitin tags from substrate proteins to prevent their recognition and degradation by proteasomes [29]. AR-specific ubiquitin proteasomes primarily consist of E2 and E3 ubiquitin ligases, whereas deubiquitinating enzymes mainly include: USP26 (ubiquitin-specific protease 26), USP14 (ubiquitin-specific protease 14), USP11 (ubiquitin-specific protease 11), and USP12 (ubiquitin-specific protease 12) [30–33]. WB analysis revealed a significant increase in the deubiquitinase USP12 and a significant decrease in the E2 ubiquitin ligase UBE2J1 and E3 ubiquitin ligase NEDD4 in DHT-treated DPCs. In contrast, after exosome treatment, the deubiquitinase USP12 was significantly reduced, whereas the E2 ubiquitin ligase UBE2J1 and E3 ubiquitin ligase NEDD4 were significantly increased in DPCs (Fig. 6h and i). Concurrently, immunohistochemical analysis of mouse tissue sections revealed markedly elevated USP12 expression within the hair follicle papillae of the AGA model group compared to that in the control group, whereas the exosome-treated group exhibited a significant reduction in USP12 expression within the hair follicle papillae (Figure S7a and b).

Among the top 10 miRNA-predicted target genes (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Table 2), Let-7b exhibited a predicted target relationship with USP12 (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Table 3). hUC-MSC-exosomes delivering Let-7b-5p can also promote the proliferation, migration, and secretory capacity of DPCs by targeting DKK3 to dual regulate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

GO functional enrichment analysis of the predicted target genes of the top 10 miRNAs revealed significant enrichment of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway (Fig. 7a). Wnt10b and β-catenin are important positive regulators of the Wnt signaling pathway in the regulation of hair growth. Immunohistochemical detection showed that the expression of β-catenin in DPCs was significantly reduced after DHT treatment, whereas exosome treatment significantly increased the expression of β-catenin in DPCs (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 8a and b). DKKs are negative regulators of the Wnt signaling pathway, which can inhibit Wnt ligands by binding to LRP5/6, thereby suppressing the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [34]. WB analysis revealed that the expression of Wnt10b and β-catenin in DPCs was significantly reduced after DHT treatment, whereas that of DKK3 was significantly upregulated. The expression of Wnt10b and β-catenin in DPCs was significantly increased after treatment with hUC-MSC-exosomes and Let-7b (Fig. 7b and c), whereas that of DKK3 was significantly decreased (Fig. 7b and d). However, the expression of DKK3 in DPCs was not significantly reduced after treatment with Let-7a, Let-7c, Let-7i, or Let-7f (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 8c and d). These results suggest that Let-7b may activate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway by affecting DKK3, which is a negative regulator. The top 10 miRNA-predicted target genes (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Table 2) revealed a predicted relationship between DKK3 and Let-7b (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Table 3). TargetScan also indicated the presence of a target site for DKK3 using Let-7b (Fig. 7f). Subsequently, a Let-7b plasmid was constructed based on the predicted target site to validate this relationship using a dual-luciferase reporter assay. The results demonstrated that Let-7b and DKK3 are mutual targets (Fig. 7e), suggesting that Let-7b-5p may target DKK3 to regulate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. We then treated DPCs with the pcDNA3.1(+)–DKK3 overexpression plasmid (oe-DKK3) and found that, compared to the other groups, Wnt10b expression was significantly reduced in the Let-7b + oe-DKK3 group (Figure S8e and f).

DPCs were treated with a oe-DKK3 to investigate whether DKK3 also mediates Let-7b regulation of DPCs proliferation, migration, and secretory capacity. Edu proliferation assays and CCK-8 experiments revealed that compared to the pcDNA + Let-7b group, the Let-7b + oe-DKK3 group inhibited DPCs proliferation (Fig. 7g and h; see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 8g). Transwell assays and Wound-healing demonstrated markedly reduced migration capacity in the Let-7b + oe-DKK3 group (Fig. 7j and i; see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 8h and i) and significantly decreased secretion of VEGF, HGF, and IGF-1 (Fig. 7k–m). These findings indicate that DKK3 overexpression suppressed the proliferative effects of Let-7b-5p in DPCs. Collectively, these results suggest that Let-7b-5p also directly targets DKK3, a multi-target regulator of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby promoting DPCs proliferation, migration, and secretory capacity (Fig. 7n).

hUC-MSC-exosome-mediated delivery of Let-7f-5p, synergizing with Let-7b-5p inhibits DHT-induced apoptosis of DPCs by targeting Smad2 to modulate the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway

Functional enrichment analysis of the top 10 miRNA experimentally validated target genes using Reactome (Fig. 8a) revealed enrichment in the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway and apoptosis. In AGA, high AR expression often activates the TGF-β/Smad pathway through paracrine signaling, promoting the apoptosis and aging of DPCs [3]. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate whether the TGF-β/Smad pathway participates in regulating DPC apoptosis via exosomes. WB analysis revealed that in DPCs, exosomes and Let-7f significantly reduced DHT-induced expression levels of TGFβR1, p-Smad2, and Smad2 (Fig. 8b and c), whereas other miRNAs (Let-7a, Let-7b, Let-7c, and Let-7i) did not modulate DHT-induced expression levels of p-Smad2 and Smad (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Fig. 9a and b). The top 10 miRNA predicted target genes (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Table 2) revealed a predicted target gene relationship between Let-7f and Smad2 (see Additional file 1: Supplementary Table 3). TargetScan indicated that Let-7f had a target site for Smad2 (Fig. 8d). Based on the predicted target sites, a Let-7f plasmid was constructed for a dual-luciferase reporter assay to validate this relationship. The results showed that Let-7f and Smad2 were mutual targets (Fig. 8e), suggesting that Let-7f may target Smad2 to regulate the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway and inhibit DHT-induced apoptosis of DPCs. Subsequently, DPCs were treated with the pcDNA3.1(+)-Smad2 overexpression plasmid (oe-Smad2). We found that, compared with other groups, the Let-7f + oe-Smad2 group exhibited significantly increased expression of p-Smad2, as well as increased expression of Bax, a functional downstream effector of TGF-β/Smad signaling (Figure S9c and d), and a significant increase in the number of apoptotic cells (Fig. 8f and g). These results suggest that Let-7f-5p targets Smad2 and acts synergistically with Let-7b-5p to regulate the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, thereby reducing DHT-induced apoptosis in DPCs (Fig. 8h).

Discussion

AGA is a common hair disorder primarily caused by the complex interaction of genetic and environmental factors, leading to hair follicle miniaturization, hair thinning, and hair loss, which significantly impacts patients’ psychological and social quality of life [28]. This study investigated the therapeutic effects and mechanisms of hUC-MSC-exosomes in serum-free cultures for AGA treatment. The results demonstrated that hUC-MSC-exosome administration via microneedles and topical application significantly improved hair density and diameter in patients with AGA. In animal studies, it was found that it can be taken up by DPCs; by delivering Let-7b, it promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of the AR, while Let-7b targets DKK3 to activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway through multiple targets, delivering Let-7f to target Smad2, which synergizes with Let-7b to inhibit the TGF-β/Smad pathway. This multi-target synergistic action improves hair regrowth and hair quality in AGA.

Enriched the mechanisms of exosome therapy for AGA—Multi-target synergistic effects

To date, nearly 12 clinical studies, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and non-randomized prospective studies, have shown that exosomes derived from adipose tissue, placenta, hair follicles, bone marrow, and foreskin mesenchymal stem cells significantly improve hair density and diameter with no severe adverse reactions, indicating that exosomes represent a novel, safe, nanoscale, cell-free therapeutic approach [35, 37]. Current research has primarily focused on identifying the regulatory effects of exosomes on individual signaling pathways in hair follicles [38], but it remains unclear how the miRNAs delivered by exosomes target specific molecules and how they synergistically regulate abnormal signaling molecules associated with AGA through multiple targets. Our study also found that exosomes derived from hUC-MSCs significantly enhanced hair density and quality in patients with AGA, with no adverse reactions reported. However, the specific mechanisms of action of exosomes derived from different sources remain unclear.

AR is primarily expressed in DPCs in alopecia-affected regions in patients with AGA. The expression and activity of AR proteins play pivotal roles in AGA progression. After binding to DHT, AR is activated and promotes nuclear translocation, leading to DPC damage, triggering hair follicle miniaturization, and arresting the hair growth cycle [3]. Although the interaction between exosomes and AR plays a central role in cancer progression (e.g., prostate cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, and liver cancer), numerous studies have found that exosomes carry miRNAs (e.g., miR-224-5p, miR-146a-5p, and miR-92a-2-5p) that target AR signaling and promote tumor resistance, metastasis, and androgen-independent transformation [39–41]. However, research on the regulation of AR expression and metabolism by exosomes in AGA remains limited. Our study found that hUC-MSC-exosomes significantly reduced AR levels in the hair papillae of AGA hair follicles in vivo and in DHT-induced DPCs in vitro. However, their miRNAs did not directly modulate AR transcriptional or translational levels. Furthermore, exosomes targeted the deubiquitinating enzyme USP12 via Let-7b-5p, thereby reducing USP12 expression, increasing AR ubiquitination, and promoting AR ubiquitin-mediated degradation, which subsequently reduced AR expression in DPCs. Even in the presence of DHT, reduced AR levels significantly attenuated DHT-induced damage to DPCs, thereby addressing DHT-induced AGA. Currently, there are no drugs targeting AR degradation for the clinical treatment of AGA. Our innovative discovery expands our understanding of AR degradation pathways and adds a new dimension to the mechanisms underlying exosome therapy.

We found that hUC-MSC-exosomes significantly activated the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, enhancing the proliferation, migration, ALP, and ability to secrete paracrine factors (such as VEGF, HGF, and IGF-1) of DPCs. The activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway by exosomes is not only achieved through Let-7b-5p targeting the deubiquitinase USP12, thereby reducing the level of AR in DPCs and alleviating the inhibitory effect of AR overexpression on the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [42], but also through Let-7b-5p targeting the negative regulator of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, DKK3, by reducing DKK3 expression and thus activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. The Wnt/β-catenin pathway plays a core regulatory role in AGA, providing a molecular basis for hair regeneration by driving hair follicle cycle transition, enhancing the function of hair follicle stem cells, and synergizing the secretion of trophic factors [43]. Other studies have also found that MSC-derived exosomes can resist DHT-induced apoptosis of DPCs and promote the hair follicle cycle [44–46]. Some studies have also demonstrated that exosomes activate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [45–51]. However, most studies have not elucidated which components (miRNAs or proteins) in exosomes activate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and through which mechanisms [52, 53]. Only one study has found that miR-22-5p contained in DPC-Exos can regulate the proliferation of hair follicle stem cells (HFSCs) and hair growth by targeting the Wnt signaling pathway, which mediates the translocation of β-catenin via the transcription factor LEF1 [54]. Exosomes from DPCs deliver miR-218-5p to target the negative regulator SFRP2, thereby activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and promoting hair growth. Our findings demonstrate that Let-7b-5p delivered via exosomes can regulate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway through multiple targets.

We also found that hUC-MSC-exosomes significantly inhibited the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, thereby suppressing DHT-induced DPC apoptosis. Exosome-mediated inhibition of the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway occurs not only by targeting the deubiquitinase USP12 via Let-7b-5p, which reduces AR levels in DPCs and consequently decreases TGF-β/Smad expression induced by high AR levels [54, 55], but also by directly targeting Smad2 via Let-7f-5p to inhibit the TGF-β/Smad pathway. The TGF-β/Smad pathway plays an inhibitory role in AGA, where its activation by DHT leads to apoptosis, senescence, and cycle arrest of DPC, as well as exacerbated inflammation and hair loss [56]. Additionally, studies have found that miR-122-5p, miR-574-3p, and miR-125a-5p derived from adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs) target the TGF-β1/Smad2 signaling pathway to treat AGA [57], whereas miR-129-2-3p derived from DPC exosomes influences the growth and development of hair follicles by targeting the TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathway. Our study further suggests that exosome-mediated delivery of Let-7b/7-5p exerts a synergistic regulatory effect on TGF-β/Smad signaling.

Strengths and limitations

The core innovation of this study lies in its systematic elucidation of the signaling network mechanism underlying the therapeutic effects of miRNAs in hUC-MSC-Exosomes against AGA: it demonstrates that Let-7b-5p mediates the ubiquitination and degradation of AR by targeting USP12, while simultaneously activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway through multi-target regulation of DKK3; Let-7b-5p and Let-7f-5p, which targets Smad2, synergistically inhibit the TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathway. Although previous studies on hUC-MSC-exosomes have identified the Let-7b family and the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, they have not elucidated how miRNAs negatively regulate specific signaling molecules to activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [46]. This study provides a more detailed mechanistic analysis, enriching the potential of hUC-MSC-exosomes as multi-pathway, multi-target ynergistic regulators. Although this study provides innovative insights into the mechanisms, significant limitations remain. The study found that the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is inhibited in AGA patients, while the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway is elevated. It also found that inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin signaling leads to upregulation of TGF-β signaling, and inhibition of TGF-β signaling leads to upregulation of Wnt/β-catenin; there may be crosstalk between these two important signaling pathways [58]; however, our study did not explore this crosstalk in depth. Second, we found that exosomes regulate the expression of the E2 ubiquitin ligase UBE2J1 and the E3 ubiquitin ligase NEDD4; however, their specific regulatory mechanisms remain unknown.

During the study, we monitored the physiological status of the participants’ scalps, including scalp sebum, moisture content, barrier function (before microneedle application), and pH. No adverse effects were observed for these physiological parameters, and the participants reported no subjective adverse reactions related to their scalp or hair. However, this clinical trial has several areas that need improvement. First, the sample size was relatively small. Second, although telephone follow-up revealed no significant increase in hair loss or premature graying three months after treatment cessation, we did not collect the corresponding indicators or conduct long-term follow-up. Therefore, this was only a exploratory clinical trial, and more large-scale clinical trials with larger sample sizes are required.

Future research directions

In this study, P5 hUC-MSCs were cultured in serum-free medium with defined components to obtain exosomes. Although we previously analyzed the proliferation density limit of cells from generations P0 to P10 and conducted mass spectrometry analysis of the secretome, we did not perform a differential analysis of exosomes derived from different generations. Future studies are required to standardize exosomes obtained using this culture method. Additionally, a systematic evaluation of the long-term safety of hUC-MSC-derived exosomes is required. It is also essential to investigate whether other exosome components (proteins and peptides) play a role. Furthermore, the development surface-engineered modifications that target hair follicles, as well as highly efficient delivery systems or sustained-release systems, to reduce the frequency of injections or enable topical application should be explored. Ultimately, these studies will provide a molecular basis and clinical evidence for hUC-MSC exosome-based therapies, facilitating the transition from mechanistic exploration to translational application.

Conclusions

we obtained exosomes from P5 hUC-MSCs cultured in a well-defined GMP-grade serum-free medium. Using a modified AGA mouse model and DPCs, we demonstrated that hUC-MSC-exosomes promote AR ubiquitination and degradation by delivering Let-7b-5p to target the deubiquitinating enzyme USP12, thereby inhibiting AR deubiquitination. Simultaneously, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is activated through multidimensional targeting of DKK3 to enhance proliferation, migration, ALP, and paracrine function in human papilloma cells. Furthermore, hUC-MSC-exosomes delivered Let-7f-5p to target Smad2, synergistically inhibiting the TGF-β/Smad pathway with Let-7b-5p to suppress DHT-induced apoptosis in human hair follicular cells. Exploratory clinical trial studies have demonstrated that hUC-MSC-exosomes increase hair density and diameter in patients with AGA. This study elucidates the multidimensional synergistic signaling molecular mechanisms of exosomes in AGA treatment, providing a research foundation for their therapeutic applications.
